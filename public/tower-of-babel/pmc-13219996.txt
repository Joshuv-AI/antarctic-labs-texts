Cortical Support in Unstable Intertrochanteric Fracture without Fixation of Posteromedial Fragment
Authors: Ki-Tae Park, Dong-Hoon Lee, Kyung-Hoi Koo, Jung-Wee Park, Young-Kyun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIt is well known that the posteromedial (PM) buttress plays an important biomechanical role in ensuring stability in unstable intertrochanteric (INT) fractures. However, the necessity for the fixation of PM fragments remains controversial. This study aims to assess surgical outcomes, including fracture site collapse and fixation failure, when PM fragments are not fixed in patients who are treated with intramedullary nailing for unstable INT fractures with large PM fragments.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 658 hip fracture surgeries performed at a tertiary referral hospital between September 2011 and August 2021. The patients who had unstable INT fractures without fixation of large PM fragments and who were followed up for a minimum of 6 months were included. In 157 patients (157 hips), surgical outcomes, such as fracture site collapse, fixation failure, and posttraumatic osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH), were assessed. The patients with an intramedullary reduction on lateral radiographs were classified as the non-cortical support (non-CS) group, and patients with extramedullary or anatomical reduction were classified as the CS group.ResultsAmong 157 patients (157 hips), fracture site collapse occurred in 64 patients (40.8%), fixation failure in 5 (3.2%), and posttraumatic ONFH in 2 (1.3%). Reoperation, such as conversion to hip arthroplasty, was performed in 4 patients (2.5%). In the comparative analysis between the CS and non-CS groups, fracture site collapse occurred significantly more frequently in the non-CS group (48.7%) compared to the CS group (32.9%) (p = 0.044). There was no significant difference in fixation failure, posttraumatic ONFH, and reoperation (p > 0.05) between the groups.ConclusionsIn unstable INT fractures without fixation of the PM fragment, the incidence of fixation failure was 3.2% and significantly associated with inadequate reduction, especially when CS was absent. The surgeon should pay attention to achieving optimal CS with appropriate reduction status of the fracture, which is critical in determining the surgical outcome.

Intertrochanteric (INT) fractures are commonly observed in elderly populations with osteoporosis and show a high risk of morbidity and mortality.12) The stability of these fractures is determined by the tendency of the fracture to collapse, even after proper reduction and fixation have been achieved.3)

INT fractures with large posteromedial (PM) fragments have been considered unstable fractures, because of the loss of medial cortical support (CS), which plays a critical role in stress distribution and fracture stabilization.45) Loss of medial CS has been associated with higher reoperation rates and unfavorable surgical outcomes.36) A biomechanical study also highlighted the importance of restoring medial CS, emphasizing its role in achieving stability and preventing postoperative complications, such as fracture site collapse and implant failure.7)

Traditionally, it is well known that the integrity of the PM wall of the femoral INT plays an important biomechanical role in ensuring the stability of the proximal femur.8) The loss of PM support is considered to be an important risk factor for femoral neck shortening, fixation failure, and even reoperations.6) Therefore, several techniques to reduce and fix the PM fragments have been introduced to prevent fracture site collapse and fixation failure.910) However, reduction and fixation of the PM fragment is relatively challenging and time-consuming,11) because it additionally requires a larger incision, more soft-tissue dissection, and more blood loss.49) Additionally, there was no consensus regarding the fixation of the PM fragment, and no previous study has evaluated outcomes in a specific fracture pattern, unstable INT fracture with large PM fragments.

Therefore, the purposes of this study were to evaluate surgical outcomes of unstable INT fractures with large PM fragments when the large PM fragments are not fixed and to compare the outcomes according to CS or not.

METHODS

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (IRB No. B-2312-873-108), which waived patients’ informed consent due to the retrospective nature of this study.

Study Population

We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of 658 hip fracture surgeries performed at a tertiary referral hospital between September 2011 and August 2021. The inclusion criteria were patients (1) who had unstable INT fractures with large PM fragments (AO Foundation/Orthopaedic Trauma Association [AO/OTA] 31A2) and (2) who were followed up for a minimum of 6 months. To identify unstable INT fractures with large PM fragments, we defined the large PM fragments as those greater than 20 mm with compromised medial calcar.1213) Fractures in which the fracture line extended below the subtrochanteric area or exited out the lateral cortex of the greater trochanter were all excluded (Fig. 1). After radiographic evaluation, 182 fractures in 182 patients met the definition of an unstable INT with large PM fragments. Of the 182 patients, 25 (13.7%) died before 6 months or were followed up for less than 6 months. Thus, 157 patients (157 hips) who survived and were followed up for more than 6 months postoperatively were analyzed. The mean follow-up was 18.4 months (range, 6–125 months). There were 55 men and 102 women and the mean age at the time of fracture was 80.3 years (range, 39–99 years) (Table 1). The mean operative time was 46.3 minutes (range, 35–80 minutes).

Surgical Technique

In all patients, closed reduction using an image intensifier was performed on the fracture table. In traction, the affected leg was abducted, adducted, and rotated to reduce the fracture. A reduction was considered as acceptable if the neck-shaft angle was reduced to less than 5° and displacement at the fracture site was less than 4 mm compared to the uninjured side.14) In all operations, we did not fix the large PM fragments. All operations were performed by using the Proximal Femoral Nail Antirotation-II System (PFNA-II, Synthes). In all cases, we used a 200-mm- or 240-mm-long implant to achieve fracture stability according to the diameter of the intramedullary (IM) canal. The manufacturer’s instructions for the operative technique were followed. After the insertion of the nail, the guide pin for the helical blade was inserted into the femoral head center. The blade was placed in the middle-inferior 1 / 3 of the femoral neck on the anteroposterior (AP) view and in the middle 1 / 2 on the lateral view. The tip of the helical blade was placed into the subchondral area of the femoral head. A distal locking screw was inserted to prevent rotation of the nail within the femur.

After surgery, a tolerable range of motion of the hip was immediately permitted, and wheelchair ambulation was started at 2 or 3 days postoperatively. Patients walked with protected weight-bearing and use of assistive devices (wheelchair, walker, crutches, or cane) 3 to 10 days after the operation. As their walking ability improved, their assistive devices were changed appropriately by a physical therapist. After discharge, the patient was followed up regularly at 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months postoperatively and annually thereafter.14) When a patient had not returned on regularly scheduled visits, the patient or their family was contacted by telephone.

Postoperative radiographs were assessed in both AP and lateral radiographs to evaluate the reduction status. The reduction status was determined in each radiograph according to the location of the proximal fragment relative to the distal fragment into 3 categories: anatomical, extramedullary, and intramedullary reduction.15) Recently, some studies emphasized that intramedullary reduction in the lateral radiograph is a predictor for loss of CS and a risk factor of fixation failure.1617) Thus, a cross table lateral radiograph of the patients was obtained immediately postoperatively, and the patients who had an intramedullary reduction on lateral radiographs were categorized as the non-CS group, while patients with extramedullary or anatomical reduction for fractures in the lateral radiographs were classified as the CS group.18)

Demographic data including age, sex, body mass index, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score,19) and length of hospital stay were compared between both groups. Surgical parameters including operation time, estimated blood loss, and tip to apex distance (TAD) length of nail were also compared between both groups. The TAD was measured after correcting for magnification using the known implant dimensions.20)

Outcome Measures

The primary outcomes were fracture site collapse and fixation failure. The fracture site collapse was determined by a sliding distance of the blade of more than 5 mm, which was measured between the lateral cortex and end of the blade from immediate postoperative to the latest follow-up radiographs (Fig. 2).2122) The sliding distance of the blade was adjusted by the magnification ratio of known implant dimensions.23) Fixation failure was defined as a fracture requiring reoperation due to a cut-out, and position change of blade within the femoral head. Bone union was assessed by the criteria suggested by Corrales et al.24) Posttraumatic osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) was also evaluated.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using Student t-tests or Mann-Whitney U-tests for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS version 16.0 (IBM Corp.).

RESULTS

Among a total of 157 patients without fixation of large PM fragments, fracture site collapse was observed in 64 patients (40.8%) and fixation failure in 5 patients (3.2%). Among 5 patients with fixation failure, 3 patients underwent bipolar hemiarthroplasty, and 2 patients refused reoperations (Fig. 3). Except for 5 patients with fixation failure due to nonunion, bone union was achieved in 152 patients at the last follow-up (96.8%). Posttraumatic ONFH was observed in 2 patients (1.3%). Among them, 1 patient was treated with hip arthroplasty, and the other patient refused reoperation. Overall, reoperations, such as conversion to hip arthroplasty, were performed in 4 patients (2.5%) (Table 1).

Based on postoperative radiographs, 79 patients (50.3%) were categorized into the CS group (Table 2). There was no significant difference in age, sex, body mass index, and ASA score in demographics between the 2 groups (p > 0.05). Also, there was no significant difference in intraoperative data, such as operation time and blood loss (p > 0.05). However, the non-CS group had a significantly higher rate of fracture site collapse (48.7%) compared to the CS group (32.9%) (p = 0.044). Fixation failure was observed in both CS and non-CS groups, occurring in 2 patients (2.5%) and 3 patients (3.8%), respectively, which was not statistically significantly different (p = 0.681). Posttraumatic ONFH was observed in 1 patient (1.3%) in the CS group and 1 patient (1.3%) in the non-CS group (p = 1.000). Overall, the reoperation rate was similar in both groups (p = 1.000) (Table 2).

In the univariate analysis, female sex (odds ratio [OR], 2.400; 95% CI, 1.226–4.698; p = 0.011) and intramedullary reduction on the lateral radiograph (OR, 1.937; 95% CI, 1.015–3.695; p = 0.045) were significantly associated with fracture site collapse. However, in the multivariate analysis, only female sex remained a significant risk factor (OR, 2.602; 95% CI, 1.229–5.509; p = 0.012).

DISCUSSION

In unstable INT fractures with large PM fragments, the risk of fracture site collapse was prevalent (40.8%) when the large PM was not fixed. However, the rate of fixation failure requiring reoperation was low (3.2%, 5 / 157) even without fixation of large PM fragments. The patients without proper CS were significantly associated with fracture site collapse. In this study, the collapse of the fracture site was prevalent, as shown in 64 patients (40.8%), which means that INT fractures with large PM fragments were unstable. When the PM fragment is not fixed, the lack of medial CS may lead to greater sliding of the fracture due to uncontrolled compression postoperatively.25) Previous studies highlighted the role of PM fragments in preventing fracture site collapse in unstable INT fractures by providing a medial CS.826) However, these studies did not account for the reduction status, which could influence surgical outcomes.

Although our study included only 1 fracture configuration—unstable intertrochanteric fractures with large PM fragments—and the follow-up duration showed no significant difference between 2 groups (p = 0.060), the distribution of implant lengths was significantly different (p = 0.011) (Table 2). This difference could be a potential confounding factor. However, previous studies have consistently reported that implant length does not affect surgical and clinical outcome differences,2728) suggesting that its influence in the present study may be limited.

Recent clinical studies have reported that CS, provided through proper reduction of the fracture, played a significant role in decreasing fracture site collapse.1182129303132) Additionally, a cadaveric study on unstable INT fractures with PM comminution demonstrated that the biomechanical properties, such as axial stiffness and load to failure, were superior in the CS group compared to the non-CS group.33) However, these studies did not differentiate between the configuration of unstable INT fractures and included heterogeneous fractures. To the best of our knowledge, there have been no studies evaluating the surgical outcomes of specific configurations of unstable INT fractures with large PM defects according to the reduction status (CS vs. non-CS).

The rate of fixation failure ranged from 0 to 12.9% in previous studies using IM nailing for INT fractures.6182129303132) The fixation failure observed in our study (3.2%) was comparable, or more favorable than, reported rates, even though the large PM fragment was not fixed. Although the collapse of the fracture site was prevalent (40.8%) in INT with large PM fragments, most fractures achieved union at the last follow-up (96.8%). This suggests that the intraoperative achievement of CS and IM nailing could provide sufficient stability for unstable fractures without fixation of the large PM fragment.12)

The lower failure rate could be explained by not only the effect of the anteromedial CS but also the biomechanical benefits of IM nailing.34) Compared to extramedullary fixation devices, such as the dynamic hip screw, IM fixation devices are positioned more medially from the axis of weight bearing, which means that they have a shorter moment arm.35) The IM fixation devices also provide a buttress effect on the proximal fragment.36) As a result, they are less prone to sliding of the lag screw or blade, leading to lower rates of screw cut-out and fixation failure.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective study with a small number of patients, particularly the non-CS group. Subsequently, statistical power may be decreased for comparison of surgical outcomes, such as fixation failure, and the potential type II error cannot be excluded. In addition, because only 5 fixation failure cases occurred, the number of cases was insufficient for a reliable multivariable logistic regression analysis, which limited the identification of risk factors for fixation failure. Also, there was a risk of selection bias as 25 patients (13.7%) died or were lost to follow-up within 6 months, and we used IM nailing alone to manage unstable INT fractures with PM fragments, using only 1 model (PFNA-II). Second, we did not have a group of patients who underwent fixation of the PM fragments as a control group. Further investigations with larger numbers and comparisons are warranted.

Despite these limitations, we demonstrated favorable surgical results of unstable INT fractures with large PM fragments even without fixation of the PM fragment. The surgeon should pay attention to achieving CS of the fracture site, which may affect the surgical outcomes. In conclusion, in unstable INT fractures without fixation of the PM fragment, the incidence of fixation failure was 3.2% and significantly associated with inadequate reduction, especially when CS was absent. The surgeon should pay attention to achieving optimal CS with appropriate reduction status of the fracture, which is critical in determining the surgical outcome.
