Association between blood lead levels and body composition parameters in middle-aged pre- and postmenopausal women
Authors: Feng Xu, Yan Zhang, Zhongxin Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLead (Pb) exposure is a global health concern, with recognized adverse effects on skeletal health. However, its relationship with body composition across menopausal stages remains unclear. This study aimed to examine the associations between blood lead levels (BLL) and key body composition parameters—lumbar bone mineral density (BMD), appendicular lean mass index (ALMI), and appendicular fat mass index (AFMI)—in middle-aged women.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, we analysed data from 1,522 women aged 40–59 years who participated in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2011–2018. We employed weighted multivariable linear regression models to examine the associations between log-transformed BLL (LnBLL) and body composition parameters, adjusting for potential confounders.ResultsPostmenopausal women exhibited significantly higher BLL compared to premenopausal women, along with lower lumbar BMD and ALMI. LnBLL was significantly and inversely associated with lumbar BMD (β = -0.02; 95% CI: -0.03 to -0.01), ALMI (β = -0.39; 95% CI: -0.49 to -0.29), and AFMI (β = -0.61; 95% CI: -0.78 to -0.45). Subgroup analyses revealed that the inverse association between LnBLL and lumbar BMD was statistically significant in premenopausal women (β = -0.03; 95% CI: -0.05 to -0.01), but not in postmenopausal women (β = -0.01; 95% CI: -0.03 to 0.01). In contrast, LnBLL exhibited stronger inverse associations with both ALMI and AFMI among postmenopausal women (ALMI: β = -0.46; 95% CI: -0.60 to -0.32; AFMI: β = -0.65; 95% CI: -0.87 to -0.43).ConclusionsOur findings show complex, menopause-dependent relationships between BLL and body composition parameters in middle-aged women. These results highlight the critical importance of considering menopausal status in future Pb toxicity studies.Keywords: Lead exposure, Body composition, Bone mineral density, Lean mass, Fat mass

Introduction

Lead (Pb) exposure remains a critical public health concern, with well-documented adverse effects across multiple physiological systems, particularly among vulnerable populations such as children and pregnant women [1, 2]. The skeleton serves as the primary reservoir for Pb, storing approximately 90–95% of the total body burden in adults [3]. Due to its prolonged half-life in bone tissue, accumulated Pb can be mobilized during periods of increased bone turnover, such as menopause, leading to endogenous exposure [4]. Although evidence has linked elevated blood lead levels (BLL) to reduced bone mineral density (BMD) and increased fracture risk [5, 6]—with mechanisms such as disruption of calcium homeostasis, oxidative stress, and impaired osteoblast function proposed [7, 8]—epidemiological findings on this association remain limited and inconsistent [9].

Furthermore, emerging research suggests that Pb may also influence soft tissue composition, including muscle and fat [10–13]. However, evidence regarding these associations remains limited, particularly in women undergoing the menopausal transition—a critical period characterized by a marked decline in estrogen levels and alterations in body composition that underscore the need to examine their interaction with BLL [14, 15]. This physiological shift highlights the need to examine how BLL interacts with body composition parameters during this critical period.

To address these gaps, this study aimed to comprehensively investigate the associations between BLL and key body composition parameters—lumbar BMD, appendicular lean mass index (ALMI), and appendicular fat mass index (AFMI)—in a large, nationally representative sample of middle-aged women. This analysis seeks to elucidate potential differences in the associations of Pb exposure with body composition by menopausal status, providing observational insights that may inform future longitudinal research and targeted public health strategies.

Methods

Study design and population

We leveraged data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a comprehensive program employing a complex, stratified, multistage probability sampling design to assess the health and nutritional status of the United States population. Our analysis encompassed four NHANES cycles from 2011 to 2018. The NHANES protocols were approved by the Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics, with all participants providing informed consent. As this study utilized publicly available data, no additional institutional review board approval was required.

Across the four survey cycles (2011–2012, n = 9,756; 2013–2014, n = 10,175; 2015–2016, n = 9,971; 2017–2018, n = 9,254), a total of 39,156 individuals were surveyed. Our study comprised women aged 40–59 years at the time of the survey (n = 3,908). We applied stringent exclusion criteria: participants were excluded if they lacked measurements for BLL (n = 1,240), lumbar BMD (n = 645), or ALMI/AFMI (n = 243); had a history of cancer (n = 132), as cancer and its treatments can significantly affect body composition and menopausal status; or had unclear menstrual status (n = 126). The final analytical sample consisted of 1,522 participants.

Study variables

The primary exposure variable, BLL, was quantified in whole blood specimens using inductively-coupled plasma mass spectrometry at the Division of Laboratory Sciences, National Center for Environmental Health [16]. BLL values below the limit of detection (LOD) were imputed as LOD divided by the square root of 2 [17]. To approximate a normal distribution, BLL data underwent natural log-transformation (Ln).

Outcome variables encompassed various aspects of body composition parameters, assessed via dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry whole-body scans using Hologic Discovery model A densitometers and analyzed with APEX software [18]. We examined lumbar BMD (g/cm2), ALMI (appendicular lean mass [kg] divided by height squared [m²]), and AFMI (appendicular fat mass [kg] divided by height squared [m²]). For women, the sarcopenia cut-off point for low muscle mass is an ALMI < 5.5 kg/m² [19].

Potential confounding variables incorporated into multivariable models included age, race, educational level, marital status, menstrual status, history of hypertension, history of diabetes, body mass index (BMI), moderate recreational activities, total protein, blood urea nitrogen, serum uric acid, and serum calcium. Detailed variable descriptions are available on the NHANES website (www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/).

Statistical analyses

All analyses followed NHANES guidelines and incorporated appropriate survey weights to account for the complex sampling design. Weighted linear regression and weighted χ² tests were utilized to evaluate group differences for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. The relationship between BLL and body composition parameters was assessed using progressively adjusted weighted multivariable linear regression models. These included: model 1 (unadjusted); model 2 (adjusted for age and race); and model 3 (fully adjusted for all covariates). For lumbar BMD, BMI was included as a covariate in the fully adjusted model. In contrast, for ALMI and AFMI, BMI was excluded from the fully adjusted models to mitigate the risk of over-adjustment and collider bias arising from compositional overlap. To examine potential effect modifications, we conducted subgroup analyses stratified by the following pre-determined variables, selected a priori based on their established relevance in the literature and biological plausibility: menstrual status, race/ethnicity, educational level, marital status, moderate recreational activities, history of hypertension, history of diabetes, and BMI category (< 25, 25–30, > 30 kg/m²). To explore non-linear relationships between BLL and body composition parameters, stratified by menstrual status, we employed a combination of smooth curve fits and generalized additive models. A two-piecewise linear regression model was applied for the objective identification of inflection points marking a shift in the relationship. All analyses were performed using R (http://www.R-project.org, version 3.4.3) and EmpowerStats (http://www.empowerstats.com, version 2.0), with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

Our analysis encompassed 1,522 participants, with their weighted characteristics stratified by menstrual status (Table 1). Postmenopausal women exhibited distinct demographic and health profiles compared to premenopausal women. They were significantly older, less educated, and demonstrated a higher prevalence of hypertension and diabetes. Notably, postmenopausal women had elevated BLL (0.06 vs. 0.04 µmol/L, P < 0.001), serum uric acid, blood urea nitrogen and serum calcium. Conversely, they displayed lower lumbar BMD (0.99 vs. 1.08 g/cm², P < 0.001) and ALMI (6.83 vs. 7.04 kg/m², P < 0.001).

Subgroup analyses revealed associations between LnBLL and body composition parameters across diverse demographic and health factors (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). Lumbar BMD exhibited a significant negative association with LnBLL mainly in premenopausal women (β = -0.03, 95%CI: -0.05 to -0.01), individuals with diabetes, and those with a BMI > 30 kg/m² (Fig. 2). In contrast, LnBLL demonstrated stronger negative associations with both ALMI and AFMI among postmenopausal women (ALMI: β = -0.46; 95% CI: -0.60 to -0.32; AFMI: β = -0.65; 95% CI: -0.87 to -0.43) compared to premenopausal women. These associations were also more pronounced in participants with hypertension and those with a BMI > 30 kg/m², but were not statistically significant among never-married women (Figs. 3 and 4).

Discussion

Our study provides evidence for the complex relationship between BLL and body composition parameters in middle-aged women, a population uniquely vulnerable to Pb toxicity. The findings reveal significant differences in the associations between Pb and body composition parameters according to menopausal status, indicating that menopausal status should be considered as a key stratification variable in future epidemiological studies examining lead toxicity.

Consistent with previous research [20, 21], our data demonstrated elevated BLL in postmenopausal women compared to premenopausal women. This observation is consistent with the hypothesis that menopause-related hormonal changes could facilitate the mobilization of Pb from bone stores into the bloodstream, potentially contributing to higher systemic Pb levels. The inverse relationship between BMD and BLL, previously reported in adults over 50 years [22] and young females [23], is particularly pronounced in premenopausal women in our study. This association likely stems from the intricate interplay between Pb toxicity and estrogen-mediated bone metabolism. Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge that age-related physiological changes—such as the gradual decline in renal function affecting lead excretion, or the cumulative burden of environmental exposures over a lifetime—may also contribute to variations in BLL and BMD, and these processes are inherently difficult to fully disentangle from menopausal status in a cross-sectional design.

In vitro and animal studies suggest that Pb may influence BMD through several potential mechanisms, such as alterations in bone turnover, changes in bone mineralization, and modulation of osteoclast activity [24, 25]. The inverse associations of LnBLL with ALMI and AFMI were not statistically significant among never-married women, indicating that marital status may serve as an effect modifier in these relationships. While the mechanisms underlying this observation are not fully understood, marital status may serve as a proxy for psychosocial stress, social support, health behaviors, or socioeconomic factors that influence nutrition, physical activity, and access to healthcare [26–28], all of which can modulate body composition and potentially interact with Pb toxicity. Furthermore, the observed non-linear relationships between BLL, lumbar BMD, ALMI and AFMI in premenopausal women potentially reflect threshold effects or adaptive responses at varying exposure levels.

The impact of Pb on muscle function involves several interconnected pathways, primarily oxidative stress, alterations in calcium signaling, and direct cellular damage [29, 30]. While direct evidence linking BLL to muscle mass is limited, Pb poisoning has been shown to decrease blood levels of calcium and zinc [31], potentially affecting muscle function and repair processes indirectly. Moreover, the potentially causal effect of BLL on reduced renal function suggests that chronic Pb exposure could impair muscle function through systemic toxicity [32].

Experimental evidence indicated that combined Pb exposure and a high-fat diet synergistically alters metabolic parameters and bone quality [33], while epidemiological studies have reported positive associations between BLL and cholesterol levels [34, 35]. These findings suggested that Pb exposure could indirectly affect body composition parameters through its impact on metabolic pathways [36], underscoring the intricate interplay between environmental toxins and biological systems in shaping body composition parameters.

The observed differences in the associations between Pb and body composition parameters according to menstrual status highlight the potential importance of considering menopausal status in future studies aimed at risk assessment. However, several limitations of our study warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences, necessitating longitudinal studies to establish temporal relationships between Pb exposure and changes in body composition parameters. Second, while we adjusted for several confounding factors, other potential confounders, such hormone replacement therapy, cannot be ruled out. Third, the use of self-reported diagnoses for diabetes and hypertension may introduce recall bias or misclassification, which could affect the accuracy of our adjusted models. Fourth, the binary categorization of menopausal status overlooks important transitional gradients, such as perimenopause, time since menopause, and hormone replacement therapy use, which may clarify the stratified associations observed and should be addressed in future studies to better characterize Pb–body composition dynamics across the menopausal continuum. Last, the choice of adjustment for appendicular mass indices (e.g., by height², BMI, or weight) may influence the observed associations, as different adjustments capture distinct physiological aspects of body composition [37]. Future studies could explore the relationships between environmental exposures and alternative appendicular mass indices to further elucidate these interactions.

Conclusion

Our findings showed complex, menopause-dependent relationships between BLL and body composition parameters in middle-aged women. These results highlighted the importance of considering menopausal status in the design of future studies on Pb toxicity. Further longitudinal research is warranted to elucidate the causal mechanisms underlying these associations and to inform targeted interventions for mitigating the health risks associated with Pb exposure in women across different life stages.
