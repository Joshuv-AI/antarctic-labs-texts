The cellular ecosystem of skeletal muscle regeneration: molecular mechanisms, pathological disorders, and potential therapeutic strategies
Authors: Jiahuan Gong, Hongyi Xu, Xinlei Yao, Yutong Wei, Xia Li, Yuntian Shen, Bingqian Chen, Hualin Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Skeletal muscle regeneration is a highly coordinated physiological process. It relies on the intricate collaboration of a complex cellular ecosystem. This ecosystem includes muscle stem cells, immune cells, stromal cells, vascular cells, neural cells, and the extracellular matrix. Research has recently expanded beyond focusing solely on satellite cells. It now delves into the multi-level regulatory networks within this ecosystem. These networks encompass key signaling pathways, such as Wnt/β-catenin, TGF-β, Hippo/YAP, and AMPK. They also include epigenetic regulation, cellular metabolic reprogramming, and extracellular vesicle-mediated intercellular communication. However, under pathological conditions, this regenerative program is severely impaired. This leads to failed repair, fibrosis, and fatty infiltration, ultimately resulting in loss of muscle function. This review aims to systematically outline recent advances in the field of skeletal muscle regeneration. First, from the perspective of the “cellular ecosystem,” we will elaborate on the dynamic behaviors and regulatory mechanisms of various cell types during regeneration. Second, we will dissect the core mechanisms underlying regenerative failures in various pathological states. Third, we will comprehensively evaluate the most promising current intervention strategies. Finally, considering the limitations of current research, we will provide future perspectives. This review aims to systematically integrate existing knowledge and provide a clear roadmap for future research, ultimately offering a robust theoretical foundation and innovative insights for the development of clinical treatments targeting skeletal muscle regenerative disorders.

Introduction

Skeletal muscle, the largest tissue organ in the human body, performs essential physiological functions including movement, posture maintenance, energy metabolism, and endocrine activity. It is critical for overall health and functional independence [1–3]. Skeletal muscle exhibits remarkable regenerative capacity, which relies on the precise coordination of multiple cell types and dynamic regulation of multi-level molecular networks. Under normal physiological conditions, muscle regeneration is initiated by activating satellite cells—muscle stem cells located beneath the basal lamina of muscle fibers. This triggers a highly coordinated regenerative program that repairs daily wear and minor injuries [4, 5]. However, under pathophysiological conditions such as acute trauma (e.g., volumetric muscle loss), degenerative diseases (e.g., Duchenne muscular dystrophy), metabolic disorders (e.g., diabetes and obesity), and aging (e.g., sarcopenia), the regenerative capacity of skeletal muscle is severely impaired. This leads to failed repair, fibrotic scarring, or pathological fat infiltration, ultimately resulting in loss of muscle function, reduced mobility, and diminished quality of life [6–9]. Sarcopenia affects hundreds of millions of older adults worldwide, while hereditary muscular dystrophies pose serious threats to the health of children and adolescents [10]. Therefore, elucidating the cellular and molecular mechanisms of skeletal muscle regeneration is of great scientific importance for understanding the fundamental biology of tissue repair and regeneration. It also holds urgent clinical and practical significance for developing effective therapies for regenerative disorders, addressing public health challenges posed by an aging population, and improving patient outcomes.

Recent years have witnessed significant advances in skeletal muscle regeneration research. The scope has expanded from early satellite cell behavior to a complex ecosystem encompassing immune cells, stromal cells, the extracellular matrix, vascular and neural innervation, and systemic factors [5]. At the cellular level, satellite cells are established as the core executors of regeneration. Their entire process—including quiescence, activation, proliferation, differentiation, and self-renewal—is finely regulated. This involves key transcription factors such as Pax7 and MyoD, epigenetic regulators like CHD4 and HDAC11, signaling pathways including Wnt/β-catenin, TGF-β, Hippo, and AMPK, as well as cellular metabolism such as iron homeostasis [11–14]. Importantly, the research perspective has moved beyond satellite cells themselves. It is now widely recognized that precise immune cell regulation is a prerequisite for successful regeneration. Macrophages orchestrate the entire inflammatory process, from initiation to resolution, through a timely phenotypic switch from pro-inflammatory M1 to reparative M2 states. This transition is centrally regulated by efferocytosis and immunometabolic reprogramming involving factors like MCUb and AMPKα1 [15, 16]. Meanwhile, regulatory T cells bridge immune responses and myogenic repair. They modulate macrophage polarization and directly act on satellite cells by secreting growth factors such as amphiregulin [17, 18]. Additionally, stromal cells, particularly fibro/adipogenic progenitors (FAPs), are key builders and regulators of the regenerative microenvironment. The fate of FAPs—whether supporting regeneration or leading to fibrosis/adipogenesis—is strictly controlled by multiple signaling pathways. These include Hedgehog, AMPK, Hippo/YAP, TGF-β/SMAD, and PPARγ. Their heterogeneity and dynamic behavior have been revealed through single-cell sequencing and other technologies [19–22]. Emerging areas, such as extracellular vesicle research, indicate that these nanoscale vesicles serve as critical intercellular messengers. They mediate systemic regulation, such as the rejuvenating effects of young blood, and directly influence satellite cell function by delivering functional cargo like miRNAs (e.g., miR-210-3p and miR-145-5p) and proteins [7, 23, 24]. Regarding intervention strategies, cell therapies (e.g., mesenchymal stem cells and their exosomes), biomaterials and tissue engineering (e.g., biomimetic ECM scaffolds, conductive hydrogels), molecular drug interventions (e.g., targeting key signaling pathways and modulating immune responses), as well as physical and nutritional therapies, all show great potential [14, 25–27]. In summary, skeletal muscle regeneration research has evolved from a single-cell focus to a multidimensional analysis of complex ecosystems, opening new avenues for understanding mechanisms and developing precise therapeutic strategies.

Despite these achievements, the field still faces numerous challenges and shortcomings. First, our understanding of the complex regulatory network governing skeletal muscle regeneration remains fragmented. The dynamic crosstalk among different cell types—such as satellite cells, macrophages, Treg cells, FAPs, and endothelial cells—during spatiotemporal progression of regeneration is not fully mapped. Moreover, how these interactions become dysregulated under pathological conditions lacks systematic integration. Second, on the mechanistic level, the precise upstream and downstream regulatory networks of newly identified molecules—such as non-coding RNAs and epigenetic regulators—and their cell type-specific functions remain unclear. For instance, the specific signaling axes through which iron metabolism disorders affect regeneration via ferroptosis or ferritinophagy are not fully elucidated. Furthermore, most current interventional strategies are still in the preclinical stage. Cell therapies face challenges such as low transplantation efficiency, poor survival, and potential tumorigenic risks. Biomaterials require further optimization in terms of biocompatibility, degradation rate, and long-term guidance of tissue regeneration. Drug interventions demand improved targeting specificity, reduced off-target effects, and the development of combinatorial strategies tailored to complex pathological microenvironments. Finally, translating basic research breakthroughs into safe and effective clinical treatments remains a long and challenging journey. Therefore, there is an urgent need for a review that systematically synthesizes existing knowledge, clarifies current bottlenecks, and provides a forward-looking perspective on future directions.

Building upon ecosystem perspective, the scope of this review is threefold. First, we will systematically dissect the molecular and cellular mechanisms within the regenerative ecosystem, emphasizing the dynamic crosstalk between myogenic, immune, stromal, vascular and neural components. Second, we will analyze how this ecosystem breaks down in major pathological conditions—including aging, metabolic disease, chronic wasting disorders, and severe trauma—linking specific pathway dysregulations to functional outcomes. Finally, we will evaluate current and emerging intervention strategies—from cell therapy and biomaterials to pharmacological and physical modalities—assessing their potential to restore the regenerative niche. It is worth noting that this review uniquely synthesizes these recent high-resolution discoveries into a cohesive “cellular ecosystem” framework. We will specifically focus on integrating findings from single-cell omics that redefine cellular heterogeneity, newly elucidated intercellular crosstalk networks, and their collective dysfunction in pathologies. By integrating these facets, this review aims to provide a cohesive framework that bridges basic mechanisms with translational applications, offering clear directions for future research aimed at overcoming skeletal muscle regenerative failure.

Scope and aims

Given the complexity and rapid evolution of the field, this review seeks to provide a comprehensive synthesis of skeletal muscle regeneration through the lens of a “cellular ecosystem.” To this end, it is structured around three primary objectives: First, to systematically delineate the molecular and cellular mechanisms within the regenerative ecosystem. We will elaborate on the dynamic behaviors and regulatory networks of satellite cells, immune cells (e.g., macrophages, Tregs), stromal cells (FAPs), and the neurovascular unit, highlighting key intercellular communication via extracellular vesicles and the extracellular matrix. Second, to dissect the mechanistic basis of regenerative failure. We will analyze how this ecosystem is disrupted in major pathological contexts—including aging, metabolic diseases, chronic wasting conditions, and severe trauma—linking specific pathway dysregulations to impaired functional outcomes. Third, to critically evaluate current and emerging therapeutic strategies. We will assess interventions ranging from cell therapy and biomaterials to pharmacological and physical modalities, analyzing their potential to restore the regenerative niche and discussing translational challenges. By integrating these aspects, this review aims to establish a cohesive framework that bridges fundamental biology with clinical translation, offering both a deepened understanding of muscle regeneration and clear guidance for future research aimed at overcoming regenerative disorders.

Molecular and cellular mechanisms of muscle regeneration

Skeletal muscle is a highly specialized tissue in the human body. It not only enables movement and posture maintenance but also performs critical functions such as metabolic regulation. Upon injury, skeletal muscle exhibits remarkable regenerative capacity. This complex and well-ordered repair process cannot be accomplished by a single cell type. Instead, it relies on a dynamic ecosystem formed by diverse cell populations and their intricate interactions. The core executors of this system are muscle stem cells, known as satellite cells. These cells reside beneath the basal lamina of muscle fibers and maintain tissue homeostasis in a quiescent state. Following damage, quiescent satellite cells become activated. They then proliferate and differentiate, ultimately fusing to form new muscle fibers [28]. However, satellite cell behavior is not autonomous. It is tightly regulated by the surrounding microenvironment. This includes infiltrating immune cells, such as macrophages and regulatory T cells, as well as tissue-resident interstitial cells like FAPs. Together with the extracellular matrix, blood vessels, and nerves, they constitute a complex regulatory network [29]. Moreover, recent studies have revealed that extracellular vesicles act as key intercellular communication carriers. By transferring bioactive molecules—including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids—they play an essential role in coordinating long-distance cell-to-cell crosstalk [7]. Therefore, deeply dissecting this multi-level, multicellular collaborative network is fundamentally important (Fig. 1). It is crucial for fully understanding skeletal muscle regeneration mechanisms and for developing regenerative medicine strategies targeting diseases such as sarcopenia and muscle atrophy.

Satellite cell regulatory networks

Skeletal muscle regeneration is a highly coordinated biological process. Its core executors are satellite cells, which are muscle-specific stem cells. Following injury, quiescent satellite cells become activated. They then enter the cell cycle and proliferate. Subsequently, these cells differentiate and fuse to form new myofibers. These events are tightly regulated by various intrinsic and extrinsic factors. Key regulators include transcription factors, signaling pathways, and epigenetic modifications [11]. Understanding satellite cell biology is crucial for developing muscle regeneration therapies.

Activation and proliferation of satellite cells

Activation of satellite cells initiates skeletal muscle regeneration. Quiescent satellite cells typically express the paired box transcription factor Pax7, which is essential for maintaining their stem cell properties and homeostasis. Under injury signals—such as inflammatory factors and mechanical stress—the quiescent state is disrupted, leading to activation. Once activated, satellite cells begin expressing myogenic regulatory factors (MRFs), including MyoD; this marks their transition into myogenic progenitor cells (myoblasts) and triggers proliferation. Studies indicate that this process is tightly coordinated by multiple signaling pathways and molecular clocks. For instance, molecular clock genes form functional circadian oscillations in satellite cells, regulating cell cycle progression and myogenic programs to influence activation and myogenic progression [30]. Another key regulator is FOS, which is rapidly and transiently induced after injury. FOS activates a set of pro-regenerative target genes, promoting cell cycle entry and proliferative expansion to establish a basis for effective muscle regeneration; without FOS, satellite cells exhibit severe impairment in initiating these critical stem cell functions [11]. Additionally, autocrine motility factor (Autotaxin, Atx) and its product lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) signal through the LPAR1 receptor, and this pathway is indispensable for satellite cell differentiation and in vivo muscle repair [31]. In summary, satellite cell activation is a precisely regulated process involving synergistic actions of diverse factors and pathways, and deeper insights into its molecular mechanisms will provide a crucial theoretical foundation for skeletal muscle regeneration research.

Differentiation and fusion of satellite cells

Following proliferation, myogenic progenitor cells exit the cell cycle and begin upregulating late myogenic regulatory factors, including myogenin. This leads to their gradual differentiation and fusion into multinucleated myotubes, which ultimately mature into muscle fibers. The process is tightly coordinated by epigenetic mechanisms and signaling pathways. Chromatin remodeling complexes play a critical role in ensuring the correct fate transition of satellite cells. For example, chromodomain helicase DNA-binding protein 4 (CHD4) is a core component of the nucleosome remodeling and deacetylase (NuRD) repressor complex. It is essential for maintaining satellite cell lineage fidelity, and conditional deletion of Chd4 in satellite cells causes lineage identity confusion and impairs muscle regeneration after injury [12]. CHD4 primarily maintains satellite cell identity during regeneration by suppressing non-myogenic lineage gene expression. Another epigenetic regulator, histone deacetylase 11 (HDAC11), promotes premature satellite cell differentiation when deficient. This accelerates skeletal muscle regeneration and is accompanied by macrophage phenotype changes, further optimizing the regenerative microenvironment [32]. Thus, epigenetic regulators such as CHD4 and HDAC11 finely tune satellite cell differentiation timing and lineage specificity through distinct mechanisms, ensuring efficient and accurate muscle regeneration. These findings not only deepen our understanding of skeletal muscle repair mechanisms but also provide potential directions for regenerative medicine strategies targeting epigenetic regulation.

Iron metabolism homeostasis is essential for cellular differentiation processes. Research indicates that specific knockout of transferrin receptor 1 (Tfr1) in satellite cells irreversibly depletes the satellite cell reservoir and triggers cell-autonomous proliferation and differentiation impairments. The mechanism involves intracellular labile iron accumulation, enhanced lipid synthesis, and lipid peroxidation, ultimately activating ferroptosis and exacerbating muscle regeneration deficits [33]. Conversely, iron deficiency induces ferritinophagy, which promotes RNF20 degradation. This subsequently negatively regulates histone H2B monoubiquitination and suppresses the expression of key myogenic markers such as MyoD and MyoG, ultimately hindering myogenic differentiation and regeneration [34]. In summary, both iron overload and deficiency disrupt myocyte differentiation through distinct molecular pathways, underscoring the importance of iron homeostasis for muscle regeneration and revealing potential therapeutic targets for related diseases.

Signaling pathways governing satellite cell fate

Multiple evolutionarily conserved signaling pathways collectively form the core network regulating satellite cell fate (Fig. 2, Left). Among these, the Wnt/β-catenin, TGF-β, Hippo, and AMPK pathways play critical roles. For example, CLIC5 activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway by interacting with biglycan, thereby promoting myoblast differentiation and skeletal muscle regeneration. Its deficiency significantly impairs muscle repair capacity [14]. Meanwhile, the tRNA-derived small RNA 5’tiRNA-Gly-CCC regulates TGF-β signaling by targeting Tgfbr1 after injury, influencing inflammatory response and satellite cell activation [35]. On the other hand, knocking down the Hippo pathway component SAV1 in muscle fibers induces YAP nuclear translocation. This accelerates perfusion recovery in ischemic muscle and promotes proliferation of Pax7+ satellite cells and endothelial cells [36]. Furthermore, CREG1 prevents ubiquitination and degradation of AMPKα1 by binding to C-CBL. Its deficiency impairs satellite cell differentiation and muscle regeneration [13]. Collectively, these studies highlight the essential roles of diverse signaling pathways in coordinating satellite cell behavior.

As core executors of skeletal muscle regeneration, satellite cells are meticulously regulated in their quiescence, activation, proliferation, and differentiation. This regulation involves multi-layered molecular networks, including transcription factors, epigenetic modifications, key signaling pathways, and cellular metabolic states. Furthermore, interactions between satellite cells and components of the microenvironment—such as immune cells and interstitial progenitor cells—collectively form a dynamic and coordinated regenerative ecosystem. Understanding these regulatory mechanisms and intercellular communication not only helps reveal the essence of muscle regeneration, but also provides a theoretical foundation for developing novel therapies for regenerative disorders. Thus, deciphering the functional integration of this complex regulatory network holds significant scientific and clinical relevance.

Precise regulation of immune cells

Skeletal muscle regeneration is a highly coordinated and complex process. It relies not only on the activation and differentiation of muscle stem cells, specifically satellite cells, but also on precise regulation by immune cells. Traditionally, inflammation has been viewed as a harmful response. However, in the repair process after skeletal muscle injury, the inflammatory response serves as an essential prerequisite for tissue recovery [5]. Immune cells, particularly macrophages and regulatory T cells, play critical roles. Macrophages undergo a temporal phenotypic switch from pro-inflammatory M1 to reparative M2 types, thereby driving inflammation initiation and resolution. Regulatory T cells (Treg) modulate macrophage polarization and directly support satellite cell functions. The synergy among these immune cells is finely regulated by mechanisms such as efferocytosis and metabolic reprogramming, ensuring smooth tissue repair [15]. In-depth understanding of these mechanisms establishes a foundation for treating muscle injury-related diseases.

Macrophages

Macrophages play a central role in skeletal muscle regeneration. Their functions exhibit distinct temporal dynamics and high plasticity. Following injury, macrophages rapidly infiltrate the damaged tissue and drive the early inflammatory response, thereby establishing a foundation for subsequent repair. This precisely timed regulation is essential for successful muscle regeneration. Initially, macrophages predominantly exhibit a classical pro-inflammatory (M1) phenotype. They clear necrotic debris and secrete inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-1β; this inflammatory milieu is crucial for activating and proliferating satellite cells. Subsequently, macrophages undergo a critical phenotypic switch from M1 to an alternatively activated (M2) phenotype. M2 macrophages display anti-inflammatory properties and secrete factors like IL-10 and TGF-β, which not only resolve inflammation but also directly support satellite cell differentiation, myoblast fusion, and nascent myotube maturation [15]. Thus, timely initiation and resolution of inflammation are central to effective regeneration, rather than simply suppressing the inflammatory response.

One key driver of the transition from M1 to M2 phenotype is efferocytosis, the process by which macrophages clear apoptotic cells and necrotic debris. This is not merely physical cleanup but a critical biological signaling event. When macrophages phagocytose cells such as apoptotic neutrophils, intrinsic signals are triggered, polarizing them toward a repair phenotype [15]. Research indicates that efferocytosis integrates inflammation resolution, cellular metabolic reprogramming, and interactions among multiple signaling pathways [15]. For instance, scavenger receptor BI-mediated phagocytosis is essential for promoting phenotypic switching and muscle regeneration [37]. Moreover, phosphatidylserine and its receptors not only mediate efferocytosis but also regulate myoblast fusion [38]. Together, these mechanisms form a molecular bridge for phenotypic switching.

The phenotype and function of macrophages are closely linked to their metabolic state, a field known as immunometabolism. In skeletal muscle regeneration, metabolic reprogramming enables precise control over macrophage function. This process is exemplified by the role of MCUb, a regulatory subunit of the mitochondrial calcium uniporter. MCUb abundance influences macrophage metabolism and inflammatory status. Loss of MCUb prevents the transition to an anti-inflammatory phenotype. It promotes glycolysis, accompanied by accumulation of TCA cycle intermediates and HIF-1α stabilization. Consequently, this sustains the pro-inflammatory program, leading to satellite cell pool depletion and delayed regeneration [16]. Additionally, metabolic sensors such as AMPKα1 and PPARγ act as core regulators of macrophage inflammatory status and tissue repair [39]. Thus, metabolic reprogramming provides an intrinsic rhythm that governs macrophage function during regeneration.

Regulatory T cells

In addition to macrophages, regulatory T cells (Tregs) also play an indispensable role in regulating skeletal muscle regeneration. They rapidly infiltrate damaged muscle tissue and support the regeneration process through multiple mechanisms. A key function of Tregs is to modulate macrophage polarization, promoting the timely transition from pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to reparative M2 phenotype. Studies have shown that depleting Tregs shortly after injury leads to increased interferon-γ (IFN-γ) levels, resulting in macrophage dysfunction, enhanced inflammation, and fibrosis, ultimately impairing muscle repair [17]. Thus, by suppressing IFN-γ production, Tregs effectively regulate macrophage recruitment and phenotypic differentiation, thereby maintaining a stable regenerative microenvironment. This highlights the central role of Tregs during the inflammatory transition phase.

Furthermore, the functions of Treg cells extend beyond immune regulation. They act directly on muscle satellite cells, promoting their proliferation and differentiation. Specifically, Treg cells secrete growth factors such as amphiregulin, which directly activate satellite cells, thereby enhancing muscle growth and repair [18]. During aging, the decline in muscle repair capacity is partly due to reduced Treg cell numbers. Exogenous IL-33 supplementation can expand the Treg population, subsequently improving muscle regeneration in aged mice [18]. These findings not only highlight the therapeutic potential of Treg cells in age-related muscle regeneration disorders, but also underscore their critical bridging role between the immune system and myogenic progenitor cells.

Immune cell diversity and crosstalk

Skeletal muscle regeneration is a complex process coordinated by multiple cell types. It is far from being dominated by a single cell population. Beyond the long-studied macrophages and regulatory T cells (Tregs), other immune cells like neutrophils actively participate. Neutrophils are among the first immune responders to arrive at the injury site. They play a dual and context-dependent role, initially promoting inflammation and tissue cleanup but potentially exacerbating damage if their activity is dysregulated [40]. Key functions include phagocytosis, respiratory burst, degranulation, and the release of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which can influence the local microenvironment [40]. Their rapid and efficient clearance mechanisms are crucial for preventing excessive tissue damage. Conversely, their timely resolution is equally critical; the persistent infiltration of neutrophils beyond the acute phase, as observed in degenerative volumetric muscle loss (VML) injuries, impairs muscle stem cell function and promotes fibrosis [41]. The transition from acute inflammation to repair involves intricate crosstalk; for instance, natural killer (NK) cells can help resolve neutrophilia by inducing apoptosis, and the chemokine CXCL16 is critical for regulating neutrophil and macrophage infiltration to support effective regeneration [41, 42]. Neutrophils also directly and indirectly influence myogenic cells. They are a major source of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF), which stimulates myoblast proliferation [43]. Furthermore, neutrophils highly express the IL-17 signaling pathway in inflamed muscle, and targeting this pathway reduces neutrophil activity and pro-inflammatory cytokines, highlighting a potential therapeutic node [44]. Moreover, satellite cells can activate and proliferate even with notably reduced neutrophil infiltration, and that their interaction with myeloid cells in vivo is often transient, suggesting a shift from contact-dependent to cytokine-mediated regulation during the repair process [45]. Recent single-cell RNA sequencing studies have further revealed up to 25 distinct cell populations and subpopulations during regeneration. The discovery of a myoblast subpopulation highly expressing immune-related genes is particularly notable. This suggests deeper and more direct interactions between myogenic cells and the immune system [46]. These advances highlight the remarkable spatiotemporal dynamics in gene expression, cellular composition, and intercellular communication during muscle regeneration. Consequently, they are advancing our understanding of the regenerative microenvironment to a new level.

In summary, skeletal muscle regeneration is not a solo performance by satellite cells. Instead, it resembles a meticulously orchestrated “cellular social event” directed by immune cells. Macrophages, through their switch from a pro-inflammatory to an anti-inflammatory phenotype, direct the entire process from necrotic tissue clearance to regeneration. This transition is finely regulated by efferocytosis and metabolic reprogramming. Meanwhile, Tregs act as higher-level regulators. They not only control macrophage polarization but also communicate directly with satellite cells. This ensures a seamless transition from the inflammatory response to the repair phase. Together, these intricate interactions form a highly coordinated and robust regulatory network for regeneration. Delving into the molecular mechanisms of immune cells in this context reveals fundamental principles of tissue repair. It also provides new perspectives for developing therapeutic strategies. With the application of cutting-edge technologies like single-cell multi-omics and spatial transcriptomics, future research will likely uncover finer, yet undefined details within this network. This includes the dynamic behaviors of specific immune cell subsets under pathological conditions. Although this review does not directly address therapeutic interventions, a deep understanding of these fundamental biological mechanisms provides a solid foundation for novel immunotherapies. These could target muscle atrophic diseases, aging-related sarcopenia, and severe muscle injuries.

The critical role of FAPs

FAPs represent a crucial group of mesenchymal stromal cells in skeletal muscle. They act as guardians of tissue homeostasis and coordinators of the regeneration process. Precise regulation of their functional state is essential for skeletal muscle health. Under physiological conditions, FAPs sense microenvironmental signals and secrete various cytokines. This provides necessary support for muscle stem cell activation and differentiation, thereby ensuring effective tissue repair. However, in persistent injury or pathological states, FAPs exhibit imbalanced fate decisions. These include excessive proliferation or abnormal differentiation into adipocytes and fibroblasts. This imbalance is central to muscle fibrosis and fat infiltration. Such pathology is widespread in various diseases, including Duchenne muscular dystrophy and age-related sarcopenia [19, 46]. Therefore, a deep exploration of FAPs’ biological characteristics and regulatory networks is critical. It is key to understanding skeletal muscle regeneration mechanisms and developing targeted therapeutic strategies.

FAPs: dual roles in regeneration

In healthy skeletal muscle, FAPs remain in a quiescent state. They continuously sense and integrate signals from the muscle microenvironment to maintain tissue homeostasis. Although FAPs do not directly participate in myofiber formation, they support muscle satellite cell proliferation and differentiation through paracrine mechanisms. This aids in the repair of minor injuries [19]. Meanwhile, their fibrogenic and adipogenic differentiation potential is strictly suppressed, ensuring their role remains supportive and confined. Upon acute muscle injury, homeostasis is disrupted, and FAPs rapidly activate and expand. Their numbers peak early after injury, primarily to establish a temporary microenvironment conducive to regeneration. By secreting cytokines such as interleukin-6, FAPs directly promote muscle satellite cell proliferation, thereby laying the foundation for effective tissue repair [47]. However, FAP accumulation acts as a double-edged sword. If not cleared or regulated timely, FAPs overcome differentiation restrictions and further mature into adipocytes and fibroblasts. This ultimately leads to pathological fat infiltration and fibrosis in muscle tissue—a common feature in wasting muscle diseases like Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) [19]. In DMD, intrinsic defects in muscle stem cells (MuSCs), such as the loss of the transcription factor YY1, can lead to excessive secretion of the chemokine CCL5. This aberrant signaling recruits macrophages which, in turn, produce elevated levels of Transforming Growth Factor-β1 (TGFβ1), directly hindering the timely clearance of FAPs and leading to their pathological accumulation [48]. Therefore, precise regulation of FAP fate is not only central to understanding skeletal muscle regeneration but also a critical strategy for preventing and treating related pathologies.

Key pathways governing FAPs fate

The fate decision of FAPs—whether they maintain supportive functions or commit to fibrosis/adipogenesis—is precisely regulated by multiple signaling pathways (Fig. 2, Right).

The Hedgehog signaling pathway serves as a key regulator of fibro/adipogenic progenitor (FAP) fate. Its ligand, Desert Hedgehog (DHH), plays a critical role after muscle injury. This signaling acts as an effective “adipogenic brake” and potently suppresses FAP differentiation into adipocytes [49]. Further studies reveal that Gli1 labels a subset of FAPs with high Hedgehog signaling activity. These Gli1 + FAPs demonstrate stronger clonogenic capacity and reduced adipogenic tendency in injured environments. Enhancing Hedgehog signaling not only promotes muscle regeneration but also markedly decreases intramuscular fat accumulation, thereby highlighting its dual benefits in tissue repair and metabolic balance [50]. However, persistent or excessive Hedgehog signaling activation may drive FAPs toward fibroblast differentiation, ultimately leading to extensive fibrosis. This indicates that precise regulation of the pathway is essential for maintaining tissue homeostasis [49]. Thus, a deeper understanding of its homeostatic mechanisms will offer potential therapeutic avenues to suppress fibrosis and promote functional muscle regeneration.

The AMPK signaling pathway plays a crucial role in enhancing skeletal muscle regeneration through exercise. This effect is partly mediated by AMPK activation, which induces senescence in FAPs. During normal regeneration, exercise-induced muscle damage promotes FAPs senescence. This senescent state is essential for establishing a pro-regenerative inflammatory environment. Moreover, via the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), FAPs secrete multiple factors that effectively support muscle satellite cell-mediated regeneration. However, in models of chronic inflammatory myopathy, FAPs fail to undergo normal senescence and exhibit anti-apoptotic properties. This leads to their pathological accumulation in muscle tissue and exacerbates functional decline. Notably, combining exercise with pharmacological AMPK activators can restore the senescence program in FAPs, thereby improving muscle regeneration and functional recovery [20]. Furthermore, recent studies reveal that oxidative stress-mediated FAPs senescence is a key post-injury event. Melatonin can mitigate this senescence by activating the AMPK pathway, restoring the ability of FAPs to support myogenesis [51]. Together, these findings highlight the central role of AMPK signaling in regulating FAPs fate and muscle regeneration, offering new targets and strategies for treating muscle degenerative disorders.

The MST1/2-Hippo/YAP signaling pathway involves mammalian Ste20-like kinase 1/2 (MST1/2), which are core components of the Hippo pathway. These kinases are essential for regulating the apoptosis and differentiation of FAPs. Specific knockout of MST1/2 in FAPs suppresses their apoptosis and reduces IL-6 secretion. Consequently, this impairs muscle satellite cell function and overall regeneration. Meanwhile, loss of MST1/2 causes nuclear accumulation of its downstream effector, Yes-associated protein (YAP). Nuclear YAP co-localizes with Smad2/3, and they synergistically promote FAP differentiation into myofibroblasts. This ultimately leads to excessive collagen deposition and muscle fibrosis. Furthermore, MST1/2 inhibition activates the WNT/β-catenin pathway through YAP/TAZ, thereby suppressing adipogenic differentiation of FAPs [21]. Thus, MST1/2 serves as a key molecular switch that balances FAP apoptosis and differentiation, and its dysregulation may be a critical driver of muscle fibrosis.

The TGF-β/SMAD signaling pathway plays a crucial role not only in regulating the fibrotic process of FAPs but also significantly influences their adipogenic differentiation. In a glycerol-induced porcine muscle injury model, single-cell RNA sequencing analysis further revealed that this pathway acts as a key switch determining the differentiation fate of FAPs. Activation of TGF-β signaling enhances the phosphorylation of SMAD2/3, thereby suppressing adipogenic differentiation of FAPs. Conversely, inhibition of this pathway upregulates the expression of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), which in turn promotes adipogenesis [22]. This mechanism provides a molecular explanation for the tendency of FAPs to drive tissue fibrosis rather than fat infiltration in pathological microenvironments. Furthermore, it suggests potential therapeutic targets for the treatment of fibrotic diseases.

The PPARγ signaling pathway serves as a master regulator of adipogenesis. Its mediated adipogenic response is indispensable in skeletal muscle regeneration. Systemic deletion of PPARγ in mice completely abolishes ectopic fat formation after muscle injury. However, more notably, muscle regeneration is severely impaired in these animals. This impairment manifests as insufficient expansion of muscle satellite cells and hindered myogenesis. These findings indicate that PPARγ not only controls adipogenesis but also directly regulates muscle stem cell function. Therefore, it constitutes an essential molecular foundation for effective muscle repair [52]. Consequently, PPARγ coordinates adipogenic and myogenic processes. It profoundly influences the biological properties of FAPs and their crosstalk with myogenesis. This highlights the complex interplay between metabolism and cell fate determination during tissue regeneration.

FAPs: cellular diversity and dynamics

The application of single-cell RNA sequencing has significantly advanced our understanding of FAP heterogeneity. Studies reveal that FAPs are not a homogeneous population but consist of distinct subpopulations with diverse gene expression profiles and functional potentials [46, 53]. For example, in a glycerol-induced muscle injury model, scRNA-seq identified six distinct subpopulations within the fibroblast/FAP cluster. These included subpopulations expressing canonical FAP markers (e.g., Pdgfra, Ly6a), myofibroblast markers (e.g., Myl9, Tagln), activated FAP markers (e.g., Osr1), and tenocyte markers (e.g., Scx, Tnmd), highlighting a spectrum of stromal cell states during regeneration [54]. Moreover, recent spatial transcriptomic analysis of regenerating aged human skeletal muscle has provided an unprecedented spatial resolution of FAP dynamics. This approach confirmed the temporal expansion of FAPs post-injury and, crucially, revealed their close spatial co-localization with infiltrating monocytes/macrophages at the injury site. This spatiotemporal association suggests a direct, niche-specific interplay between these cell types during human muscle repair [55]. Beyond the previously mentioned Gli1+ subset, FAPs exhibit remarkable spatiotemporal dynamics during regeneration. Their population composition, gene expression patterns, and interactions with other cells continuously evolve over time [46]. Pseudotime trajectory analysis of these subpopulations placed proliferative cells at the origin, with trajectories leading toward distinct termini corresponding to early adipogenic, later adipogenic, and myofibrogenic fates, illustrating the dynamic and potentially divergent differentiation paths of FAPs following injury [54]. Importantly, the C3-expression and secretory function of human FAPs appears to be state-dependent, being high in freshly isolated cells but downregulated upon culture, highlighting the dynamic regulation of their immunomodulatory role based on microenvironmental cues [55]. This heterogeneity and dynamic behavior enable FAPs to precisely respond to microenvironmental cues and perform diverse functions—ranging from early pro-inflammatory support to later pro-regenerative and matrix-remodeling activities.

FAP-immune cell interaction

The functions of FAPs are profoundly regulated by immune cells, particularly macrophages, and they play a dynamic role in muscle regeneration. During the inflammatory phase of regeneration, M1 macrophages clear excess FAPs by producing pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF, thereby preventing their over-accumulation. As regeneration progresses, macrophages polarize toward the M2 phenotype, and their secreted factors then support the supportive functions of FAPs. However, in aging or chronic disease states, this immune regulation becomes disrupted, and the phenotypic switch of macrophages is impaired. This leads to improper clearance and dysfunction of FAPs, ultimately impairing muscle regeneration [56, 57]. The fine balance between immune cells and FAPs is crucial for maintaining tissue repair, and any dysregulation may provoke long-term pathological consequences. Therefore, further exploration of the underlying molecular mechanisms is necessary to guide therapeutic strategies.

In skeletal muscle regeneration, FAPs are not only potential negative factors but also active builders and regulators of the regenerative microenvironment. Their fate is precisely regulated by multiple signaling pathways, including Hedgehog, AMPK, MST1/2-Hippo/YAP, TGF-β, and PPARγ. These pathways collectively form a complex network that determines whether regeneration leads to functional recovery or pathological fibrosis and adipogenesis. Moreover, advances in single-cell technologies have further revealed the heterogeneity and dynamic transitions of FAP populations, thereby deepening our understanding of their functional diversity. Future research should continue to dissect the details of these regulatory networks and explore the specific functions of different FAP subpopulations under particular physiological and pathological conditions. This will open new avenues for understanding and ultimately targeting muscular diseases. By integrating multi-omics data and functional experiments, we can more precisely uncover the roles of FAPs in diseases and advance the development of personalized medicine.

Extracellular vesicles

The role of extracellular vesicles (EVs) as critical mediators of intercellular communication in skeletal muscle regeneration has gained increasing attention in recent years. EVs are membrane-bound vesicles released by cells. They carry bioactive molecules such as proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. These vesicles can transmit signals over long distances via the circulatory system, thereby playing a central role in skeletal muscle regeneration. Studies show that EVs derived from young individuals possess pro-regenerative capacity. For instance, in heterochronic blood exchange models, circulating factors in young blood restore youthful features in aged tissues. This rejuvenating effect is significantly attenuated upon the removal of EVs [7]. Further experiments confirm that EVs from young animals can improve the bioenergetic state of aged cells and enhance skeletal muscle regeneration. Conversely, aging alters the heterogeneity of EV subpopulations and impairs their beneficial effects on recipient cells [7]. Thus, circulating EVs are key mediators of systemic rejuvenation, and their role in maintaining tissue homeostasis should not be overlooked.

EVs regulate gene expression and downstream signaling pathways in target cells through their cargo of functional proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, particularly microRNAs. This regulation maintains skeletal muscle homeostasis and promotes repair. Regarding nucleic acids, aging alters the nucleic acid fingerprint of circulating EVs, accompanied by decreased levels of the pro-longevity protein α-Klotho transcripts. Injecting young EVs improves muscle regeneration in a Klotho mRNA-dependent manner [7]. Furthermore, multiple studies focus on the role of miRNAs within EVs. For example, exosomes from hypoxia-preconditioned bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells regulate the KLF7 gene via miR-210-3p and activate the PI3K/AKT pathway, promoting satellite cell activation [23]. Exosomes from muscle-derived progenitor cells carry miR-140-5p, which targets Pax7 to regulate the cell cycle and enhance cell proliferation [58]. miR-126-5p in bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes stabilizes MyoD protein by inhibiting FBXO32, thereby promoting myogenic differentiation [59]. Meanwhile, miR-145-5p in exosomes from human fetal cartilage-derived progenitor cells promotes myogenic marker expression by activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [24]. Beyond nucleic acids, EVs are also enriched with various signaling proteins, including myogenic factors like insulin-like growth factor and hepatocyte growth factor [60]. Secretome studies of adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells indicate that EVs and soluble molecules act synergistically to promote muscle regeneration [61]. The diversity and synergy of these molecular cargoes constitute the molecular basis for EV-mediated regulation of skeletal muscle regeneration.

EVs activate multiple downstream signaling pathways via their cargo molecules, coordinating various stages of skeletal muscle regeneration. For instance, the PI3K/AKT pathway is regulated by the miR-210-3p/KLF7 axis, influencing satellite cell function [23]. The Wnt/β-catenin pathway can be activated by miR-145-5p, promoting myogenesis [24]. miR-126-5p stabilizes MyoD protein by inhibiting FBXO32, driving myogenic differentiation [59]. The miR-140-5p/Pax7 axis regulates satellite cell proliferation and fate determination [58]. In summary, EVs act as long-distance messengers. They deliver specific nucleic acids and protein cargoes to precisely regulate gene expression in recipient cells, particularly muscle satellite cells. This process activates key signaling pathways and coordinates multiple steps in muscle regeneration, including satellite cell activation, proliferation, differentiation, and tissue microenvironment remodeling. However, factors like aging alter EV composition and function, impairing their pro-regenerative capacity. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the molecular regulatory mechanisms of EVs will not only elucidate the intrinsic principles of skeletal muscle regeneration but also provide a theoretical basis for developing related regenerative therapeutic strategies.

Extracellular matrix and vascular innervation

Skeletal muscle regeneration is a complex process involving multiple cells and factors. Functional recovery relies not only on myogenic cells but also on the coordinated reconstruction of the extracellular matrix (ECM), vascular system, and neural network. These three components form the core regulatory network for regeneration. The ECM plays a dual role, serving as both a physical scaffold and a key signaling source. After muscle injury, ECM remodeling precisely coordinates satellite cell activation, proliferation, differentiation, and fusion. For instance, the residual basement membrane, particularly its laminin and type IV collagen networks, provides a spatial framework for satellite cell migration and myotube fusion [62]. In severe injuries like volumetric muscle loss, native ECM structure is disrupted. Decellularized ECM bioscaffolds or biomimetic materials can mimic its structure and biomechanical properties, promoting host cell migration, new myofiber formation, and vascular network construction [26, 63]. Thus, the ECM acts as an essential structural and signaling platform, and its dynamic remodeling is fundamental to successful muscle repair.

Angiogenesis

Angiogenesis is equally critical during regeneration, supplying blood to metabolically active tissues and supporting energy needs and waste removal. It not only delivers nutrients to new myofibers but also promotes satellite cell activation and differentiation via paracrine signaling. Angiogenesis is viewed as a coordinated multicellular process within a dynamic tissue ecosystem. Endothelial cells (ECs) are the central effectors, guided by both classic angiogenic pathways (e.g., VEGF/VEGFR2, FGF, PDGF) and neuronal guidance cues (e.g., Ephrins/Ephs, Semaphorins, Netrins). These cues direct EC migration and plexus patterning [64, 65]. Notably, ECs themselves are functionally heterogeneous; specific subpopulations, such as metabolically primed ATF3/4+ ECs enriched in oxidative muscle regions, exhibit a higher intrinsic angiogenic potential, which is crucial for exercise-induced vascular expansion [66]. Furthermore, perivascular stromal populations, including pericytes and fibroblasts, are indispensable partners. Pericytes stabilize new vessels and modulate EC behavior through direct contact and paracrine signals [67, 68]. This cellular ecosystem is dynamically regulated by mechanical forces. Depending on whether the primary stimulus is intraluminal shear stress or abluminal mechanical stretch, angiogenesis proceeds via distinct morphological patterns (longitudinal splitting vs. abluminal sprouting), involving differential activation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and temporal expression of VEGF [69]. Crucially, the integrity of this ecosystem dictates the regenerative sequence. In models of volumetric muscle loss where native tissue architecture and guidance cues are completely removed, angiogenesis can precede and pave the way for subsequent myogenesis, indicating that vascular regeneration is a pioneering event that establishes a necessary scaffold for muscle fiber ingrowth [70]. Angiogenesis is important for muscle Regeneration. Critically, during regeneration, a tight coupling exists between myogenesis and angiogenesis. Endothelial cells and myogenic progenitor cells (MPCs) interact bidirectionally, with both cell types secreting factors like Apelin, Oncostatin M, and Periostin to mutually promote vascular formation and muscle repair [71]. This interdependence is rooted in their shared developmental lineage and extends beyond paracrine signaling. Satellite cells (MuSCs) themselves express a subset of canonical endothelial genes, and the endothelial-type VEGFA-FLT1-AKT1 signaling axis functions within MuSCs to critically promote their survival during regeneration, blurring the functional boundary between these cell types [72]. It reported that in volumetric muscle loss models, glycosaminoglycan-based hydrogels enhance angiogenesis and accelerate muscle repair [26]. Conversely, lipocalin-2 deficiency impairs angiogenesis and hinders regeneration, highlighting its necessity in vascular network formation [73].

Molecularly, angiogenesis is tightly regulated by a balance of stimulatory and inhibitory signals within this ecosystem. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) plays a central role, with its upregulated expression post-injury promoting endothelial cell proliferation and migration via VEGFR2 activation. Muscle fiber-derived VEGF is essential for initiating angiogenesis in response to various stimuli, including exercise and mechanical overload [74]. However, VEGF activity is spatially and temporally constrained by anti-angiogenic factors like thrombospondin-1 (TSP-1), which is transcriptionally regulated by FoxO1 in ECs and determines the extent of capillary growth [65]. The process also involves specific receptors that translate metabolic signals into angiogenic programs, such as the lactate receptor HCAR1 found on perivascular cells, which mediates exercise-induced VEGF expression and angiogenesis in response to increased lactate [75]. Nitric oxide (NO), particularly from eNOS, serves as a critical upstream signal for stress-induced angiogenesis [76]. Other factors like fibroblast growth factor (FGF) and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) synergistically enhance angiogenesis [77]. Moreover, the regulation of oxidative stress within the niche is a pivotal component; scavenging excess reactive oxygen species (ROS) can alleviate endothelial cell dysfunction and create a microenvironment conducive to both angiogenesis and myoblast differentiation [78]. Moreover, ECM remodeling provides a structural basis for angiogenesis, as collagen and fibronectin guide endothelial cell migration and lumen formation through integrin signaling [79]. Together, these mechanisms form a coordinated network, ensuring spatiotemporal precision in angiogenesis and establishing the necessary nutritional and signaling foundation for regeneration.

Neural components

Innervation is indispensable for functional recovery, primarily through neuromuscular junction (NMJ) reconstruction to convert electrical signals into mechanical contractions. During regeneration, motor neuron axons must extend accurately to new myofibers and induce acetylcholine receptor clustering. This precise targeting is influenced by long-range signals from the target organ itself; for instance, muscle-derived extracellular vesicles have been shown to significantly influence the anatomical accuracy of motor neuron regeneration [80]. Engineered muscle constructs have been shown to promote NMJ formation and reinnervation, improving functional recovery [81]. Molecules like Sema3A regulate axonal guidance in NMJ development, and their dysregulation leads to innervation defects [82]. The Agrin-LRP4-MuSK pathway is central to NMJ formation, and its upregulation facilitates acetylcholine receptor clustering and synaptic maturity [83]. Within the regenerative niche, Schwann cells (SCs) play multifaceted and indispensable roles. Following nerve injury, denervated SCs undergo phenotypic reprogramming: they downregulate myelin-associated genes, proliferate, elongate, and line the endometrial tubes to physically guide and trophically support regenerating axons through the upregulated expression of growth-associated genes and neurotrophic factors [84]. The neurotrophic factors such as BDNF and NGF support neuronal survival and axonal growth via Trk receptors, while extracellular vesicles may deliver these factors to coordinate nerve-muscle communication [85]. Moreover, A specialized subset of SCs, the non-myelinating perisynaptic Schwann cells (PSCs) at the NMJ, are critical for local synaptic remodeling. Upon denervation, PSCs extend processes that bridge between innervated and denervated endplates, actively guiding axonal sprouts to reinnervate neighboring muscle fibers, which contributes to the characteristic “clumping” of reinnervated motor units [84]. Notably, vascular and neural regeneration are closely coupled. New vessels provide physical pathways and chemotactic signals for axonal growth, and neural-derived factors like VEGF and neuregulin directly stimulate angiogenesis. Intriguingly, Schwann cells, the myelinating glia of peripheral nerves, also intersect with the angiogenic niche. They can secrete VEGF and other trophic factors, potentially coupling nerve repair with revascularization, and their processes may physically guide both regenerating axons and capillary sprouts [65]. Macrophages bridge these processes, with M2-type cells releasing VEGF and BDNF to simultaneously promote angiogenesis and reinnervation [86]. The ECM integrates these signals as a dynamic platform, creating a microenvironment conducive to vascular-neural synergy. Therefore, the coordinated interplay between reinnervation and angiogenesis is essential for complete structural and functional restoration of skeletal muscle.

Therefore, the coordinated interplay between reinnervation and angiogenesis, mediated by a multicellular consortium that includes endothelial cells, guiding SCs, restorative macrophages, and muscle-derived signals, is essential for complete structural and functional restoration of skeletal muscle. In this ecosystem, SCs not only guide axons and supporting neurons but also potentially interact with the emerging vascular network to synchronize neurovascular repair.

Mechanisms of impaired muscle regeneration

Skeletal muscle regeneration is a highly coordinated biological process. It relies on the precise regulation of satellite cells and their dynamic interactions with the niche to maintain tissue homeostasis and repair damage. However, various pathological and physiological challenges can disrupt this process. These include aging, metabolic diseases, chronic wasting conditions, severe trauma, and exposure to environmental pollutants (Fig. 3). These factors impair regenerative capacity by affecting both cell-intrinsic functions and the external microenvironment. Key alterations include epigenetic changes, mitochondrial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, and immune cell abnormalities. Consequently, this leads to the loss of muscle function and fibrosis [4, 56, 87]. Understanding these complex mechanisms is crucial. It is essential for developing targeted, multi-dimensional intervention strategies to restore muscle health and improve clinical outcomes.

Age-related muscle regeneration decline

Skeletal muscle possesses a robust intrinsic regenerative capacity; however, this finely tuned mechanism undergoes systemic decline during aging. In elderly individuals, satellite cells not only decrease in number but also exhibit functional impairments, including delayed activation, reduced proliferative potential, and diminished differentiation efficiency. At the molecular level, aging triggers alterations in the epigenetic landscape, such as aberrant histone modifications and disrupted DNA methylation patterns, which directly impact the expression of myogenic regulators like MyoD and MyoG. Concurrently, mitochondrial dysfunction leads to excessive reactive oxygen species accumulation, aggravating cellular damage and promoting the release of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype. This phenotype contains abundant pro-inflammatory factors, such as IL-6 and TNF-α, collectively undermining satellite cell function [87, 88]. Concurrently, aged MuSCs display a heightened susceptibility to apoptosis. When faced with energy stress or pro-apoptotic signals, aged cells are more likely to initiate apoptosis over the pro-survival autophagy pathway, leading to increased cell death upon activation. A central molecular driver of this dual dysfunction is the age-related dampening of AMPK activity. In aging, diminished AMPK signaling leads to hypophosphorylation of p27Kip1. This hypophosphorylation disrupts pro-autophagic and anti-apoptotic functions, tipping the cell fate balance in aged MuSCs away from self-renewal and towards cell death and senescence [89]. Furthermore, the aged muscle microenvironment displays a chronic low-grade inflammatory state, termed inflammaging, characterized by weakened macrophage responsiveness and a predisposition of FAPs to differentiate into adipose and fibrous tissues. Coupled with impaired angiogenesis, these changes collectively create an environment hostile to regeneration [56, 87]. Systemic factors also play a critical role, as circulating youth factors—such as extracellular vesicles—decline, while aging-associated toxins like advanced glycation end products accumulate, further exacerbating regenerative decline at a systemic level [7]. Recent single-cell transcriptome studies have uncovered that shifts in stem cell states are closely linked to aging hallmarks in aged muscle, offering novel insights into regeneration impairment [87]. Therefore, addressing aging-associated regeneration deficits requires multi-target strategies that enhance the intrinsic functions of satellite cells, remodel their microenvironment, and modulate systemic factors. Future research should prioritize developing aging-specific interventions to restore muscle health.

Regenerative impairment in metabolic disease

Metabolic diseases, such as diabetes and its comorbidities, severely impair skeletal muscle regeneration. This involves extensive cellular and molecular alterations. Under hyperglycemic and insulin-resistant conditions, satellite cell function is compromised. Their activation and differentiation capacities are suppressed. This is partly due to dysregulated insulin/IGF-1 signaling, which reduces Akt/mTOR pathway activity. Consequently, protein synthesis and cell proliferation are affected. Concurrently, dysregulated inflammation is observed, marked by increased M1 macrophage polarization and elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β and TNF-α. These changes exacerbate oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby impeding effective repair [6]. Specifically, a reduction in the antioxidant enzyme SOD1 leads to superoxide overproduction in diabetic muscle, which amplifies inflammation, fibrosis, and severely delays regeneration after injury [90]. Chronic hyperglycemia and the accumulation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) directly promote excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition. The canonical Wnt signaling pathway, often deregulated in diabetes, interacts with pro-fibrotic mediators like TGF-β and CTGF, driving fibroblast activation and pathological collagen accumulation, thereby hindering functional tissue repair [91]. Concurrently, the fate and function of Fibro-Adipogenic Progenitors (FAPs) are critically dysregulated. In a diabetic or obese state, FAPs exhibit aberrant behavior, contributing to fibro-fatty infiltration rather than supportive regeneration [92]. This pathological FAP differentiation is significantly driven by the chronic overactivation of TGF-β1 signaling, a central pathway in fibrotic diseases [93]. Vascular complications also play a critical role. Hyperglycemia-induced attenuation of VEGF signaling impairs angiogenesis, restricting nutrient and oxygen supply and further weakening regenerative capacity. Moreover, the immune microenvironment in diabetic muscle undergoes significant changes. These include persistent pro-inflammatory macrophages and delayed neutrophil clearance, which disrupt the precise immune timing required for regeneration [94]. Notably, common antidiabetic drugs like canagliflozin may impair mitochondrial function by downregulating leucyl-tRNA synthetase 2 expression, thereby hindering regeneration [95]. Although specific treatments for diabetic regenerative impairment are currently lacking, emerging studies suggest that modulating signaling pathways or utilizing biomaterials may offer new opportunities. This highlights a promising direction for developing targeted therapies and underscores the need for further mechanistic studies to elucidate underlying factors, such as non-coding RNAs.

Impaired regeneration in chronic wasting diseases

In chronic wasting diseases such as cancer cachexia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and chronic kidney disease, skeletal muscle regeneration is severely impaired. This condition is primarily characterized by persistent catabolism and inflammation. Systemic inflammatory factors, including TNF-α and IL-6, are elevated in these diseases. They activate the NF-κB signaling pathway, leading to protein degradation and muscle wasting. Concurrently, they disrupt the precise timing and signals required for immune cell function, preventing the initiation or completion of regeneration [4]. Alterations in intercellular communication are also critical. Studies in animal models show that the normal network among satellite cells, FAPs, immune cells, and endothelial cells is disrupted. This disruption suppresses the regenerative program while abnormally activating fibrotic and adipogenic programs. For example, transforming growth factor-β signaling drives excessive differentiation of FAPs into fibroblasts, promoting scar formation [4]. It is particularly notable that macrophages in these environments exhibit impaired metabolic reprogramming. This affects their ability to switch to a repair phenotype, thus failing to effectively support muscle stem cell function [56]. Furthermore, T cell dysfunction, especially alterations in the number and function of regulatory T cells, further exacerbates the imbalance in the regenerative microenvironment [96]. Therefore, addressing regenerative failure in chronic diseases requires a focus on immune-muscle stem cell interactions. Restoring immune cell function and coordinated intercellular communication could be promising therapeutic strategies. Targeting inflammatory pathways may consequently improve patient outcomes.

Volumetric muscle loss resulting from severe trauma

Severe trauma can cause volumetric muscle loss (VML), which refers to the traumatic absence of a substantial mass of muscle tissue. This extent of damage exceeds the intrinsic regenerative capacity of skeletal muscle and presents unique therapeutic challenges. In such injuries, the basal lamina and extracellular matrix (ECM) scaffolds are disrupted. Consequently, the structural foundation for regeneration is lost. Satellite cells lack the necessary guidance for directed migration, proliferation, and fusion [63]. Concurrently, extensive vascular and nerve impairment compromises nutrient supply and innervation to nascent tissue. This impedes tissue maturation and functional recovery. Notably, the disruption of myogenesis-angiogenesis coupling further diminishes regenerative potential [7]. In the absence of effective regeneration, FAPs and other fibroblasts become hyperactivated. These cells overproduce ECM components, leading to dense scar tissue formation. Such fibrosis not only blocks muscle regeneration but also causes joint contractures and functional deficits [4]. Recently, tissue engineering strategies have demonstrated promising potential. For example, a decellularized adipose extracellular matrix bioscaffold perfused with adipose-derived stem cells and myoblasts can promote functional muscle regeneration and enhance angiogenesis [63]. Therefore, integrated approaches combining biomaterials, cell therapy, and physical stimulation may offer new hope for patients. This multimodal strategy constitutes a forefront in muscle regeneration research, though further optimization is essential for clinical translation.

Negative impacts of environmental pollutants and drugs

Environmental pollutants and certain drug side effects pose threats to muscle regeneration by interfering with normal cellular physiological processes. For example, exposure to polystyrene microplastics induces excessive intracellular reactive oxygen species production. It inhibits myogenic differentiation by suppressing p38 MAPK phosphorylation and promotes adipogenic differentiation through increased NF-κB expression, thereby disrupting skeletal muscle regeneration in mice [97]. In terms of pharmaceuticals, besides anti-diabetic drugs, some chemotherapeutic agents may exert toxic effects on muscle stem cells, impairing long-term muscle health [4]. Notably, immunomodulatory drugs such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs can interfere with early inflammatory signals during regeneration, altering the regenerative process [98]. However, emerging research shows that nanomaterials like MXene nanoparticles can create a favorable microenvironment for regeneration by modulating oxidative stress and macrophage polarization, offering new strategies to counteract environmental toxin damage [9]. Although the specific molecular mechanisms of these factors are not fully understood, further studies are needed to clarify their pathways; developing targeted protective strategies will help mitigate the adverse effects of external factors on muscle regeneration.

Integrated challenges and future strategies

Impaired skeletal muscle regeneration often results from complex interactions among multiple cell types, signaling pathways, and systemic factors. At the cellular level, regenerative failure is rooted in intrinsic dysfunction of satellite cells, imbalanced differentiation of FAPs, dysregulated immune cell activity, and inadequate vascular support [4, 56]. Molecular mechanisms involve interconnected processes such as dysregulated epigenetic control, mitochondrial dysfunction, aberrant inflammatory signaling, and metabolic disturbances; these collectively form a molecular network that impedes regeneration [6, 87]. Notably, the dysregulation of iron metabolism homeostasis has emerged as a pivotal node within this network. The age-related regeneration decline is driven by chronic, low-grade inflammation, which epigenetically silences anti-ferroptosis genes. Specifically, inflammatory signals downregulate the histone methyltransferase Kmt5a, leading to a loss of the H4K20me1 mark and consequently promoting ferroptosis in aged MuSCs [99]. Moreover, research indicates that Tfr1 expression is significantly reduced in the skeletal muscle and satellite cells of aged mice [33]. This ferroptotic pathway is a critical convergence point in various myopathies. For instance, in facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy (FSHD), toxic DUX4 expression induces pathological iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation, activating ferroptosis; conversely, iron supplementation or the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 improves muscle function [100]. The antioxidant transcription factor NRF2, a key regulator of ferroptosis defense (e.g., via GPX4), is also crucial for mitigating oxidative stress and supporting regeneration in dystrophic contexts [101]. Conversely, iron deficiency induces ferritinophagy, which promotes RNF20 degradation, ultimately hindering myogenic differentiation and regeneration [34]. The impact of iron dysregulation extends to its microenvironment. For example, crosstalk between FAPs and MuSCs involves the IL-6/HO-1 axis, where IL-6 signaling downregulates HO-1 in MuSCs. Reduced HO-1 alleviates iron-dependent oxidative stress and ferritinophagy, thereby creating a permissive environment for myogenic differentiation [102]. Furthermore, other iron-related proteins like Lipocalin-2 (LCN2), upregulated after injury, can inhibit myogenesis by promoting ferroptosis [103]. Iron metabolism thus acts as a key sensor and effector node for pathological perturbations, serving as a central mechanistic link between diverse disease states and failed regeneration.

The significant synergies exist among different challenges. For example, aging exacerbates the adverse effects of diabetes on regeneration, while chronic inflammation serves as a common pathway linking multiple pathological states [4, 56]. To address these complexities, future therapeutic strategies should adopt multi-dimensional approaches. These may include targeting muscle stem cells, remodeling the microenvironment, modulating systemic factors, and developing novel biomaterials. The elucidated role of iron metabolism highlights specific therapeutic avenues, such as modulating the inflammation-epigenetics-ferroptosis axis, correcting specific iron deficiencies or overloads, or targeting downstream effectors like LCN2/ACOD1 or the NRF2 pathway. At the same time, emerging methods based on single-cell technologies and artificial intelligence will help elucidate the underlying mechanisms [87]. Thus, although skeletal muscle regeneration faces multiple obstacles, advances in mechanistic understanding and technology are driving the development of innovative treatments. This progress holds promising prospects for restoring muscle function and improving patients’ quality of life.

Potential interventions and therapeutic strategies

Skeletal muscle injury and degeneration present significant clinical challenges. The core issue lies in the insufficient regenerative capacity of damaged muscle. This is particularly evident during aging or under pathological conditions, where the function of endogenous muscle stem cells markedly declines [104]. Conventional rehabilitation therapies offer limited effectiveness. Consequently, the field of regenerative medicine is actively exploring novel intervention strategies. Recent advances in understanding the molecular mechanisms of muscle regeneration, combined with rapid progress in biomaterials and engineering technologies, are fostering a new paradigm. Current therapeutic approaches are increasingly multi-modal and synergistic. These strategies no longer focus on a single pathway. Instead, they integrate cell therapies, bioactive materials, molecular pharmaceuticals, physical interventions, and nutritional or metabolic modulation (Fig. 4). They aim to synergistically promote structural and functional muscle recovery. This is achieved by supplementing repair cells, constructing a regenerative microenvironment, regulating key signaling pathways, and optimizing systemic conditions [5]. Cell therapy

The repair of skeletal muscle injury and degenerative diseases represents a major challenge in regenerative medicine. The intrinsic regenerative capacity of muscle is often limited during aging or under pathological conditions. Consequently, researchers are actively developing stem cell-based therapeutic strategies. These approaches primarily fall into two categories. First, endogenous muscle stem cells can be activated via chemical or genetic methods. Second, exogenous stem cells or their derivatives may be transplanted to replenish the regenerative cell pool. This section will separately examine advances in targeting endogenous muscle stem cells and transplanting exogenous stem cells. It will also review their underlying molecular mechanisms and future application prospects.

Targeting endogenous muscle stem cells

Skeletal muscle regeneration primarily depends on the function of muscle satellite cells (MuSCs). However, under aging or disease conditions, the activation and regenerative capacity of satellite cells decline significantly. Thus, targeting and enhancing the regenerative potential of endogenous satellite cells has become an important therapeutic strategy [5]. Recent studies show that specific small molecule cocktails can selectively expand Pax7+ myogenic stem cells both in vitro and in vivo. For example, in mouse muscle injury models, localized sustained release of these molecules effectively activates and expands resident satellite cells, thereby promoting the repair of muscle structure and function [25]. On the other hand, modulating key signaling pathways has been proven critical for muscle regeneration. For instance, inhibiting the Hippo signaling component Salvador promotes nuclear translocation of its effector YAP. In ischemia and cardiotoxin-induced muscle injury models, this significantly accelerates blood flow recovery, enhances angiogenesis and satellite cell proliferation, and consequently improves muscle regeneration [36]. Furthermore, FOS acts as a key driver of early satellite cell activation. It guides stem cells into the cell cycle and promotes proliferation by regulating target genes such as ART1, which is essential for efficient muscle regeneration [11]. Notably, in metabolic disorders like diabetes, a high-glucose environment impairs satellite cell function and delays regeneration, suggesting metabolic intervention as a potential strategy to enhance endogenous repair [6]. These studies not only reveal the molecular basis of satellite cell activation but also offer new targets for regenerative therapies against muscle degenerative diseases. In summary, targeting endogenous muscle stem cells through multi-dimensional approaches—including chemical induction, signaling pathway modulation, and key transcription factor intervention—shows broad clinical potential. Future research should focus on optimizing the translation of these strategies to provide more efficient and safe regenerative medicine solutions for patients with muscle injuries and degenerative conditions.

Transplantation of exogenous stem cells

When endogenous regenerative capacity is insufficient, transplanting exogenous stem cells becomes a viable strategy. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), distinct from muscle satellite cells in developmental origin, are particularly promising not as alternative myogenic progenitors but due to their accessibility and potent paracrine/immunomodulatory actions. The primary therapeutic mechanism of MSCs in muscle repair is attributed to their secretion of various trophic factors and cytokines. This secretory profile secretion modulates the immune microenvironment, promotes angiogenesis, and suppresses fibrosis [105]. Furthermore, simple cell injections often suffer from low survival and retention rates. Combining MSCs with biomaterials, such as alginate cryogels, provides a protective microenvironment and enables sustained release of growth factors like IGF-1 and VEGF. This approach significantly amplifies their paracrine effects, effectively promoting new muscle fiber formation and functional recovery in volumetric muscle defect models [106]. Many beneficial effects of MSCs are mediated by their secreted extracellular vesicles, particularly exosomes. These exosomes carry proteins, mRNAs, and miRNAs, mimicking parental cell functions. For instance, exosomes from hypoxic preconditioned bone marrow MSCs deliver miR-210-3p, which targets the KLF7 gene and activates the PI3K/AKT pathway. This process markedly promotes muscle satellite cell proliferation and differentiation, effectively restoring muscle function in aged sarcopenia models [23]. Similarly, exosomes secreted during myogenic differentiation of human fetal cartilage-derived progenitor cells are enriched in miR-145-5p. By activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, these exosomes robustly stimulate myogenesis both in vitro and in sarcopenia rat models [24]. As a cell-free therapeutic strategy, exosomes circumvent limitations associated with direct cell transplantation. They also exhibit strong clinical potential, offering novel molecular insights and therapeutic perspectives for muscle regeneration. However, some studies have preliminarily shown that they possess myogenic potential. MSCs derived from bone marrow, adipose tissue, and even human urine have demonstrated myogenic potential in vitro [107, 108]. To improve efficacy, preconditioning MSCs with agents like interleukin-4 or stromal cell-derived factor-1 had a beneficial effect in the regeneration process after their transplantation into injured muscles, potentially through cell fusion [109]. Together, these strategies advance stem cell-based therapies toward more efficient and controllable muscle regeneration.

C2C12, a widely utilized immortalized mouse myoblast cell line, has served as a fundamental in vitro model for advancing skeletal muscle tissue engineering strategies. C2C12 cells are easy to culture, grow rapidly, can differentiate stably into multinucleated myotubes, and possess high standardization characteristics. Research showed that aligned electrospun polycaprolactone-gelatin scaffolds combined with mechanical stimulation were shown to enhance C2C12 myotube width, nuclear fusion, and contractility [110]. Similarly, electroactive nanohybrid hydrogels incorporating reduced graphene oxide significantly promoted C2C12 myoblast adhesion and myogenic differentiation by providing conductive cues similar to native tissue [111]. Furthermore, conductive 3D-printed scaffolds doped with carbon materials from biomass enhanced C2C12 myotube formation under electrical stimulation [112], and bioinks incorporating MXene nanoparticles within 3D bioprinted constructs encouraged C2C12 differentiation without additional myogenic agents [113]. These examples underscore the critical role of C2C12 cells as a versatile and accessible platform for screening the biocompatibility, topographical guidance, electrical conductivity, and biochemical efficacy of innovative biomaterials designed to support myogenesis. However, significant disadvantages limit their translational relevance. As an immortalized murine cell line, C2C12 cells do not fully recapitulate the biology of primary human satellite cells. They may exhibit genetic and epigenetic alterations from prolonged culture, possess different immunogenic profiles, and lack the niche-responsive behaviors essential for in vivo regeneration and clinical application.

Biomaterials and tissue engineering

For volumetric muscle loss, biomaterial scaffolds provide essential structural support and guide aligned cell organization for tissue regeneration. In smart scaffolds and hydrogels, controlling physical and chemical properties is crucial. The scaffold’s microstructure, stiffness, and bioactivity directly influence cellular behavior. For instance, aligned polycaprolactone/gelatin nanofibrous scaffolds fabricated via electrospinning guide myotube orientation and growth along a specific direction, thereby mimicking the anisotropic structure of native muscle. When combined with mechanical stimulation in bioreactors, these scaffolds further enhance myotube width, nuclear fusion, and contractility [110]. These advances demonstrate that precise modulation of scaffold properties establishes a strong foundation for optimizing muscle regeneration strategies and shows great potential for clinical translation.

Injectable hydrogels have gained clinical interest due to their minimally invasive nature. For example, glycosaminoglycan hydrogels based on thiolated hyaluronic acid and chondroitin sulfate allow tuning of biophysical properties by adjusting thiol substitution degrees. These hydrogels support myoblast differentiation and angiogenesis. In a mouse VML model, they promoted Pax7+ satellite cell migration, nascent myofiber formation, and re-innervation while minimizing fibrosis [26]. Conductive materials also offer significant benefits. Since skeletal muscle is electroactive, incorporating reduced graphene oxide into gelatin-based microcryogels creates a conductive microenvironment. This not only protects transplanted muscle stem cells but also enhances C2C12 myogenic differentiation in vitro. In VML models, it significantly improved muscle regeneration by reducing collagen deposition and inflammation [114]. These innovative materials enhance therapeutic outcomes and offer new possibilities for minimally invasive interventions and functional recovery.

Moreover, biomimetic extracellular matrix hydrogels derived from decellularized muscle matrix retain native bioactive components. Modification with phenolic groups such as catechol or pyrogallol enhances mechanical properties and adhesiveness. After injection into a mouse muscle atrophy model, these hydrogels effectively induced tissue rejuvenation, promoted muscle protein synthesis, and restored function [115]. Modular tissue construction further addresses the synergy between muscle and vascular regeneration. For instance, co-injecting PLGA porous microspheres loaded with C2C12 myoblasts (“micro-muscles”) and PEG hollow microrods loaded with human umbilical vein endothelial cells (“micro-vessels”) improved in situ muscle regeneration and remodeling in a mouse VML model, highlighting the potential of minimally invasive therapy [116]. These integrated approaches not only advance tissue engineering techniques but also provide versatile solutions for future clinical applications.

Molecular and pharmacological interventions

Skeletal muscle regeneration is a complex process involving multiple cellular and molecular events. Dysfunctions in this process are closely linked to various diseases, including muscle injury and age-related sarcopenia. In recent years, researchers have aimed to develop precise strategies for enhancing skeletal muscle regeneration. These efforts focus on three core approaches: finely regulating key signaling pathways, targeting inflammation and immune responses, and repurposing existing drugs through drug repositioning. By modulating critical pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin, AMPK, and Hippo [14, 36], adjusting immune responses like macrophage polarization [27], or reusing available compounds including sartans [117], investigators are progressively revealing novel mechanisms and targets for muscle repair and regeneration. This work establishes a solid theoretical basis for future development of efficient and specific clinical interventions.

Modulating key signaling pathways

Targeting specific molecular pathways represents another key strategy for precisely promoting skeletal muscle regeneration. For instance, the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway plays a central role in regulating myogenic differentiation and regeneration. Studies confirm that CLIC5 activates the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway by interacting with its binding partner biglycan (BGN), thereby promoting myoblast differentiation and skeletal muscle regeneration. Conversely, CLIC5 deficiency significantly impairs muscle regenerative capacity [14]. Similarly, precise regulation of the TGF-β signaling pathway is critical for regeneration. Recent research reveals that the tRNA-derived small RNA 5’tiRNA-Gly-CCC modulates the TGF-β pathway by targeting Tgfbr1, influencing inflammatory responses and myoblast differentiation, and ultimately regulating skeletal muscle regeneration [118]. In terms of metabolic regulation, obesity-induced suppression of AMPK activity leads to satellite cell dysfunction and impaired regeneration. Studies demonstrate that treatment with the AMPK-specific agonist AICAR restores muscle regeneration in obese mice. However, satellite cell-specific knockout of AMPKα1 abolishes this beneficial effect, confirming the central role of AMPKα1 in obesity-associated regenerative deficits [119]. Further research shows that CREG1 influences muscle regeneration by regulating the C-CBL E3 ubiquitin ligase-mediated degradation of AMPKα1, providing new insights into the upstream regulation of the AMPK pathway [13]. On another front, modulation of the Hippo signaling pathway also demonstrates significant regenerative potential. Research finds that AAV9-mediated knockdown of Sav1 inhibits the Hippo pathway, promoting nuclear translocation of the effector YAP. This enhances angiogenesis and satellite cell proliferation in ischemic muscle, significantly improving regenerative outcomes [36]. Regarding negative regulators, ARHGEF3 negatively regulates skeletal muscle regeneration through its GEF activity. ARHGEF3 knockout mice exhibit enhanced regeneration and strength recovery after injury, an effect dependent on autophagy activation. In aging models, ARHGEF3 deletion restores autophagy levels and ameliorates muscle weakness [120]. These studies not only reveal the multifaceted regulatory mechanisms of key signaling nodes—such as Wnt/β-catenin, TGF-β, Hippo, and AMPK—in muscle regeneration but also highlight the complexity of cross-talk between different pathways. With advances in technologies like single-cell transcriptomics, future research will enable more precise analysis of the spatiotemporal dynamics of these pathways within specific cellular subpopulations. This progress will provide new directions and targets for developing precise molecular therapies for skeletal muscle regeneration disorders.

Targeting inflammation and immunity

Inflammation plays a dual role in tissue regeneration. Thus, a timely transition from pro-inflammatory to anti-inflammatory phenotypes is critical for successful regeneration. Macrophage polarization is a key strategy to improve regenerative outcomes. For example, hepatocyte growth factor suppresses M1 markers and promotes M2 marker expression via the CaMKKβ/AMPKα pathway, thereby driving macrophages toward the M2 phenotype and accelerating muscle regeneration [27]. Gold nanoparticles, owing to their excellent biocompatibility and immunomodulatory capacity, have also been shown to promote M2 polarization and optimize the muscle regenerative microenvironment [121]. Moreover, low-intensity pulsed ultrasound upregulates FZD5 and enhances β-catenin nuclear translocation, activating the WNT pathway. This induces a shift from M1 to M2 macrophage polarization and significantly enhances skeletal muscle regeneration. Depleting macrophages abolishes this therapeutic effect [122]. Collectively, these strategies demonstrate that precise regulation of macrophage polarization is an effective approach to improving tissue regeneration quality.

Beyond macrophage polarization, targeting specific inflammatory factors and the complement system also plays an important role in promoting muscle regeneration. In a severe muscular dystrophy mouse model, anti–IL-6 receptor antibody reduced fibrosis and increased regenerating myofiber area by inhibiting STAT3 phosphorylation, thereby promoting skeletal muscle regeneration, although its benefits in diaphragm and heart muscle were limited [123]. Regulatory T cells are central in controlling tissue repair and limiting fibrosis. Enhancing their activity through exercise or pharmaceuticals may serve as a potential immunomodulatory strategy for treating muscle injury and muscular dystrophy [124]. Complement C3a signaling promotes monocyte recruitment and infiltration into injury sites, where they polarize into M2 macrophages, thereby supporting muscle repair. Deficiency in C3 or the C3a receptor impairs regeneration and reduces immune cell infiltration [125]. These studies provide a strong rationale for developing targeted interventions against inflammatory responses.

Drug repurposing and rediscovery

Drug repurposing and rediscovery show great promise in treating traumatic muscle injury. Sartans, as angiotensin II receptor blockers, inhibit TGF-β1 activation by antagonizing AT1R. Preclinical studies indicate their potential to reduce collagen deposition and promote muscle regeneration, offering a new direction for anti-fibrotic therapy [117]. This finding suggests that such classic antihypertensive drugs may have broader clinical applications in modulating muscle fibrosis. Furthermore, the farnesyltransferase inhibitor lonafarnib—originally developed for cancer and recently FDA-approved for Hutchinson–Gilford progeria syndrome—has emerged as a promising repurposed candidate against glucocorticoid-induced muscle atrophy. Studies demonstrate that lonafarnib counteracts dexamethasone-induced atrophy in C2C12 myotubes, Caenorhabditis elegans, and rat models by enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis, upregulating UCP3 and ANGPTL4, and activating anabolic pathways such as mTOR/S6 ribosomal protein signaling [126]. In addition, important progress has been made in prostaglandin E2 research. By chemically conjugating it to collagen to form a sustained-release matrix, its half-life is significantly prolonged. In a limb ischemia model, this matrix promoted angiogenesis and activated MyoD1-mediated muscle stem cells, effectively improving neovascularization and skeletal muscle regeneration [127]. This sustained-release technology offers valuable insights for the design of bioactive molecule delivery systems.

Physical therapy

In the field of skeletal muscle regeneration, physical therapy is gaining increasing attention as a non-invasive and highly promising strategy. Photobiomodulation therapy (PBMT), also known as low-level laser therapy, exerts reparative effects by influencing mitochondrial function, modulating inflammatory responses, and promoting satellite cell activation and angiogenesis [128]. Although existing clinical outcomes are heterogeneous, standardizing key parameters—such as wavelength and energy density—combined with real-time biofeedback and AI-driven personalized protocols, may significantly enhance the therapeutic value of PBMT in muscle regeneration. Meanwhile, exercise, as the most physiological promoter of regeneration, has been shown to induce senescence in FAPs, thereby establishing a pro-regenerative inflammatory microenvironment [20]. Notably, in chronic inflammatory myopathy models, exercise alone may be insufficient to induce FAPs senescence; however, combining exercise with AMPK activators can reverse apoptosis resistance and improve muscle function and regeneration, highlighting the potential benefits of combined interventions. Furthermore, recent studies have revealed that tRNA-derived small RNAs (tsRNAs) are significantly enriched during the inflammatory response after muscle injury. Among them, 5’tiRNA-Gly-CCC regulates inflammation and myogenic differentiation via the TGF-β signaling pathway, thereby promoting skeletal muscle regeneration [35]. In addition, the cellular differentiation regulator CREG1 plays a critical role in muscle injury repair by stabilizing AMPKα1 signaling, and its deficiency impairs regeneration [13]. In summary, physical interventions such as PBMT and regular exercise not only multi-targetedly regulate the regeneration process at the mechanistic level but also show great potential in technical integration and personalized protocol design. With the standardization of relevant parameters and deeper integration of novel assistive technologies, physical therapy is expected to offer safer and more effective clinical solutions for muscle regeneration disorders. Future research should further incorporate multi-omics technologies and precision medicine concepts to elucidate the synergistic effects of different interventions, thereby advancing muscle regeneration therapy from empirical application toward evidence-based personalized treatment.

Nutritional and metabolic interventions

Nutritional status profoundly influences skeletal muscle regeneration. Specific nutrients and supplements play key roles by modulating the inflammatory immune microenvironment, apoptosis, and myogenesis. Among fatty acids, omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids—such as those derived from flaxseed—and their lipid mediators (e.g., resolvins) reduce apoptosis in muscular dystrophy models. They protect myogenesis and promote regeneration by regulating macrophage polarization and suppressing excessive inflammation [129, 130]. Other nutritional supplements also show potential in modulating regeneration. For instance, cannabidiol demonstrated limited effects on muscle damage markers and performance recovery within 24–48 h after resistance exercise. However, by 72 h, it exhibited mild benefits in reducing muscle damage and improving squat performance recovery [131]. Additionally, platelet lysate enriched with multiple growth factors promoted angiogenesis and myogenesis in diabetic rats and equine muscle injury models. It accelerated functional recovery and enhanced regeneration quality [132, 133].

Beyond specific nutrients, dietary regimens such as caloric restriction (CR) or dietary restriction (DR) have emerged as potent modulators of skeletal muscle health and regenerative capacity. CR/DR, characterized by reduced calorie intake without malnutrition, extends lifespan and ameliorates age-related pathologies. In the context of skeletal muscle, even short-term CR enhances the function of muscle satellite cells (MuSCs), increasing their availability and activity in both young and aged animals. This enhancement is associated with increased mitochondrial abundance and the induction of conserved metabolic and longevity regulators [134]. At the molecular level, CR promotes a more youthful phosphoproteomic signature in aged muscle, suppressing known “pro-aging” signaling pathways such as Protein Kinase A (PKA) [135]. Metabolomic analyses further reveal that DR attenuates sarcopenia in progeroid mice not by preserving mass, but by improving muscle quality. It reduces oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory mediators while upregulating anti-inflammatory metabolites and energy production pathways (e.g., glycolysis, TCA cycle), collectively contributing to maintained grip strength and improved motor function despite lower body weight [136]. These findings highlight a paradigm where CR/DR optimizes the systemic and local muscle microenvironment, enhancing stem cell function and tissue resilience, which is crucial for recovery from injury. However, the relationship between longevity interventions and muscle mass maintenance presents a paradox, as pathways like reduced IGF-1/mTOR signaling that promote longevity may simultaneously limit anabolic growth, underscoring the need for balanced nutritional strategies [137].

Notably, muscle regeneration is impaired in various pathological conditions. Diabetes significantly hampers regenerative capacity through hyperglycemia and insulin resistance. The underlying mechanisms involve muscle stem cell dysfunction and dysregulated inflammatory signaling [6]. Aging also reduces muscle stem cell numbers and function, thereby weakening regenerative potential [104]. Collectively, these studies indicate that nutritional interventions can optimize the regenerative microenvironment as an adjuvant strategy. They may also compensate for molecular defects in regenerative disorders via multi-target regulation, offering novel therapeutic approaches for muscle-related diseases and injury repair. Therefore, future research should integrate nutritional interventions with cell therapy and biomaterials. Developing personalized nutritional regimens for specific pathological microenvironments could maximize structural and functional recovery of skeletal muscle. Further exploration into how nutritional factors interact with intra- and extracellular signaling networks in muscle stem cells will uncover deeper regulatory mechanisms and advance translational applications in muscle regeneration.

Gene therapy and targeting non-coding RNAs

Gene therapy is regarded as a fundamental strategy for correcting the underlying genetic defects in hereditary muscle disorders, such as Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD). Simultaneously, the regulation of non-coding RNAs is emerging as a promising therapeutic direction. Under various pathological conditions, including diabetes and aging, the level of high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) is abnormally elevated. This upregulates miR-342-5p, which suppresses the synthesis of the key myogenic regulator Pax-7, ultimately impairing muscle regeneration. Studies indicate that inhibiting HMGB1 not only restores Pax-7 expression but also significantly improves muscle regeneration capacity. These findings suggest that targeting the miRNA-HMGB1 axis may offer a viable approach to mitigating muscle atrophy and regeneration deficits [138]. Moreover, other non-coding RNAs, such as miR-206 and miR-1, play essential regulatory roles in muscle differentiation and repair, further expanding the potential of RNA-targeted therapies [139]. Therefore, combining gene correction with RNA modulation strategies could enable multi-target interventions, offering more comprehensive and sustained therapeutic outcomes for hereditary myopathies. Future studies should focus on validating the synergistic effects and safety of such approaches in preclinical models.

Summary and outlook

Skeletal muscle regeneration is a sophisticated biological process, orchestrated by a dynamic cellular ecosystem involving satellite cells, immune cells, FAPs, and the neurovascular unit, all coordinated through complex molecular and signaling networks. Significant research advances have elucidated key mechanisms, from canonical pathways (Wnt/β-catenin, TGF-β, Hippo/YAP, AMPK) to emerging areas like epigenetic regulation, metabolic reprogramming, and extracellular vesicle-mediated communication. However, our understanding remains fragmented, particularly regarding the spatiotemporal dynamics of intercellular crosstalk and the hierarchy of regulatory networks in vivo. Under pathological conditions like aging, diabetes, obesity, severe trauma, or neuromuscular diseases, this process is severely impaired. Cellular functions are compromised, and microenvironmental homeostasis is disrupted, leading to markedly reduced regenerative capacity. It is worth noting that these pathological conditions often share common underlying features, such as mitochondrial dysfunction, persistent epigenetic changes, and persistent inflammation that cannot be alleviated. However, they exhibit distinct characteristics in terms of disease manifestations, which requires targeted investigation and treatment approaches. For instance, in diabetes, hyperglycemia and insulin resistance impair satellite cell function and disrupt macrophage polarization, thereby delaying regeneration. In aged muscle, FAPs exhibit a greater tendency to differentiate into adipose and fibrous tissue instead of supporting myogenesis, further weakening repair. Additionally, chronic inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and epigenetic alterations are recognized as critical factors contributing to regenerative failure.

Addressing these challenges requires focus on several frontiers (Fig. 5). Future research must move beyond descriptive cataloging to achieve mechanistic, predictive, and therapeutic depth. First, leveraging high-resolution technologies is important. Single-cell multi-omics and spatial transcriptomics will be essential to systematically deconvolute the cellular heterogeneity, dynamic state transitions, and interaction networks within the muscle ecosystem. A key goal is identifying the drivers of detrimental cell state transitions during aging or disease and using genetic and pharmacological perturbations to validate their functional necessity. Integrating these datasets with advanced computational modeling and artificial intelligence can predict network behavior, identify novel therapeutic targets, and stratify patient populations based on molecular signatures. Second, targeted therapies designed to “reset” the diseased microenvironment are needed. This includes developing smart biomaterials or nanomedicines. These could provide on-demand release of specific factors (e.g., HGF, MANF) or modulate specific immune cell subsets (e.g., pro-regenerative macrophages, regulatory T cells). Furthermore, engineered extracellular vesicles with customized cargo (miRNAs, proteins, genome-editing tools) represent a promising cell-free strategy for precise intercellular reprogramming. Third, given the limitations of monotherapies for complex regeneration disorders, future strategies will likely combine multiple approaches. Combination therapies may include cell therapy (e.g., iMPCs), bioactive scaffolds providing physical and biochemical cues, gene editing (to correct mutations), and physical treatments (e.g., mechanical stimulation). The key will be to define synergistic combinations and optimal temporal sequences for administration. Finally, strengthening translational research is essential. Promising preclinical findings must be advanced toward clinical application. This requires optimizing cell expansion and delivery methods, systematically evaluating the long-term safety and efficacy of biomaterials, and rigorously testing new therapies in clinical trials. Furthermore, future clinical trial designs should incorporate deep molecular phenotyping of patients to enable biomarker-driven enrollment and response assessment, moving towards true precision medicine. Ethical considerations regarding genetic interventions, cell source, and long-term outcomes must also be proactively addressed.

In conclusion, the field of skeletal muscle regeneration is at a pivotal juncture. Ongoing research is building a solid foundation, but the path forward requires a paradigm shift towards integrative analysis, intelligent therapeutic design, and patient-centric translation. The increasing convergence of bioengineering, immunometabolism, epigenetics, and data science promises transformative advances. By elucidating the fundamental principles of the muscle ecosystem and leveraging cutting-edge technologies, we can anticipate the development of innovative therapies that not only achieve structural repair but, more importantly, restore full muscle function and significantly improve patients’ quality of life. The ultimate goal is a future where regenerative medicine provides effective, personalized solutions for a wide spectrum of muscle disorders.
