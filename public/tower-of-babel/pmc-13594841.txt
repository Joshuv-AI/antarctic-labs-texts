Kikuchi-Fujimoto-Like Renal Involvement in Autoimmune Disease
Authors: Nicole K. Andeen, Tiffany Caza, Sadhika Sood, Anila Abraham Kurien, Jacob Dougherty, Vanderlene L. Kung, Jennifer Dunlap, Rupali S. Avasare, Atul Deodhar, Megan L. Troxell, L Nicolas Cossey, Christopher Larsen, Josephine Ambruzs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionKikuchi-Fujimoto Disease (KFD) is a rare, self-limiting disorder primarily involving lymph nodes and characterized by necrotizing lymphohistiocytic inflammation with prominent apoptotic debris. Extranodal involvement has been described, but KF-like necroinflammation has only rarely been documented in kidney biopsies.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed data from 3 institutions over 20 years and identified 9 patients with native kidney biopsies showing KF-like interstitial necroinflammation and compared the clinical and pathologic features with 8 patients with systemic KFD and kidney biopsies.ResultsA total of 8 of 9 patients with KF-like necroinflammation had previously diagnosed or suspected evolving systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE); none had systemic KFD. The most prominent finding in kidney biopsies was an atypical necroinflammatory interstitial infiltrate composed of histiocytes and lymphocytes with areas of brisk apoptosis, karyorrhectic debris, mitotic activity, and occasional large, atypical nuclei. The majority had limited or no glomerular involvement, and when present, this usually consisted of focal, mild active or chronic lesions with modest immune complex deposition. In comparison, in the 8 patients with systemic KFD, kidney biopsy findings were attributable to lupus nephritis (LN) in 4 of 5 patients with SLE, including 3 with membranous LN. No patient with systemic KFD had KF-like renal interstitial necroinflammation.ConclusionsAlthough lymphohistiocytic inflammation with prominent karyorrhexis most commonly manifests as a necrotizing lymphadenitis or dermatitis in both KFD and SLE, our study reveals that this distinctive pattern may also be recognized as an unusual interstitial nephritis, most commonly associated with SLE and seen in biopsies with limited glomerular involvement.

KFD, or KF histiocytic necrotizing lymphadenitis, most commonly affects younger patients of Asian descent and is characterized by tender cervical lymphadenopathy and fever, as well as rash, cytopenias or leukocytosis, arthritis, fatigue, and/or hepatosplenomegaly in a minority of cases.1 KFD may mimic, be triggered by, or evolve into other entities, particularly SLE, infection (especially Epstein–Barr virus [EBV], HHV6 and 8, HIV, parvovirus B19, tuberculosis, Bartonella, and others), and lymphomas.1 Histologically, lymph nodes in patients with KFD show patchy necrosis, histiocytic and T-cell rich inflammation with scant B-cells, abundant karyorrhectic debris, and scattered immunoblasts, without associated neutrophilic or eosinophilic infiltrate. SLE lymphadenitis may show a greater degree of polyclonal plasma cell infiltrate but otherwise be histologically indistinguishable from KFD.2,3 The pathogenesis of KFD and SLE lymphadenitis and the immunologic mechanisms of the characteristic inflammation are unknown. Extranodal involvement has been described in KFD and most commonly involves the skin, with skin biopsies showing a similar pattern of lymphohistiocytic inflammation with karyorrhectic debris as seen in involved lymph nodes4, 5, 6, 7; skin biopsies from patients with cutaneous lupus can have a similar KF-like inflammatory pattern.8

However, Kikuchi-Fujimoto-like renal involvement with necrotizing lymphohistiocytic inflammation has only rarely been documented in kidney biopsies, limited to 4 cases in abstract form9,10 without other published reports to our knowledge. Herein, we report 9 patients from 3 institutions with native kidney biopsies showing KF-like interstitial necroinflammation and compare the clinical and pathologic features with those of 8 patients with systemic KFD who also underwent kidney biopsy.

Methods

Kidney biopsy records and associated clinical data from 2006 to 2026 at 3 institutions (Arkana Laboratories, Renopath Center for Renal and Urological Pathology in Chennai, India, and Oregon Health & Science University) were searched for (1) terms including Kikuchi-like, necroinflammatory, necrotizing lymphadenitis, atypical hematopoietic infiltrate, and (2) patients with previously diagnosed KFD and available kidney biopsy. Immunofluorescence (IF) was performed on all biopsies (17 with frozen IF, 1 with paraffin IF only due to lack of tissue submitted for frozen) and electron microscopy was performed on 14 biopsies (1 had no tissue available for electron microscopy). Immunohistochemical and special staining to further characterize the interstitial infiltrate and/or exclude a viral or infectious process were performed in 5 of 9 KF-like renal necroinflammation cases. Not all clinical variables were available for each patient; for variables with missing data, the denominator is provided.

Results

Patients With KF-Like Renal Necroinflammation on Kidney Biopsy

Clinical Presentation

In terms of ethnicity, there was a predominance of African American patients (4/9), followed by Hispanic (2/9), Native American, Asian, and unknown (1 each). Overall, 7 patients had a prior diagnosis of SLE), including 1 SLE/mixed connective tissue disease overlap, 1 SLE/rheumatoid arthritis/psoriatic arthritis overlap, and 1 with possible additional hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Only 2 did not have a rheumatologic diagnosis documented at the time of kidney biopsy, although 1 of these had suspected evolving lupus. Systemic symptoms at the time of kidney biopsy included cervical (in 5/7 patients with available data) and/or generalized (4/7) lymphadenopathy, fever (8/9), weight changes (loss in 3, gain in 2), arthralgias (3/5), and rash (4/7); 1 patient had seizure activity. Laboratory evaluation revealed a positive antinuclear antibody (7/7), antidouble stranded DNA antibody (5/5), anti-Smith antibody (3/3), low C3 and C4 (6/6), as well as anemia (6/9), thrombocytopenia (6/8), leukopenia (6/8), hypoalbuminemia (7/7), and transaminitis (8/8). Kidney biopsies were generally performed for proteinuria (7/9 with subnephrotic range proteinuria; 2/9 with nephrotic syndrome) and microscopic hematuria (6/9), with preserved renal function (2/8 with acute kidney injury).

Pathologic Findings

The most prominent finding in the kidney biopsies (Table 2) was an atypical necroinflammatory interstitial infiltrate (Figures 1 and 2), generally composed of histiocytes and lymphocytes, some with large, atypical nuclei. Foci of brisk apoptosis and karyorrhectic debris were present, as were zones of mitotic activity and small areas of geographic necrosis without fibrin deposition. Immunohistochemistry performed in 5 of 9 biopsies showed that the infiltrate consisted predominantly of histiocytes and monocytic lineage cells (CD33, CD68 positive), including a subset that coexpressed myeloperoxidase, and/or CD8+ T-cells; a high Ki-67 proliferative rate was present. This infiltrate usually had patchy, nondiffuse cortical involvement and could also be observed in peritubular capillaries, but generally not in glomeruli. EBV by in situ hybridization was negative in all tested cases (5/5), including in the patient with high plasma EBV DNA. Cytomegalovirus testing was performed on one case and was negative. No fungal or acid-fast organisms were identified. Glomerular light microscopic findings were comparatively limited in most cases and ranged from no glomerular abnormalities (4/9) to mild mesangial expansion (1/9) in the majority. A total of 4 cases had focal active or chronic lesions which were limited in extent (< 20% of glomeruli), consisting of focal intracapillary hyaline pseudothrombi or subendothelial deposits (2/9), focal endocapillary hypercellularity and karyorrhexis with fibrocellular crescents (1/9) and/or focal fibrous crescents (1/9). No case had cellular crescents, diffusely active International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society (ISN/RPS) class IV LN, a membranoproliferative pattern, thrombotic microangiopathy, or a substantial component of membranous LN (class V).

IF microscopy revealed no deposits or a “tissue antinuclear antibody”-only pattern in a third of cases (3/9). Cases with immune deposits were mesangial-predominant (5/6) and showed “full house” staining (5/6) with extraglomerular deposits (tubular basement membrane: 2, peritubular capillary: 1). One patient with a type III cryoglobulin had only paraffin IF available which showed IgM-dominant deposits with focal intracapillary plugs (Case 9). No cases had monotypic deposits. Electron microscopy revealed diffuse podocyte foot process effacement in 2 patients with nephrotic syndrome, consistent with lupus podocytopathy (Cases 1, 5).

Overall kidney biopsy diagnoses were atypical interstitial necroinflammation (9/9) without glomerular findings in 3 patients, and with associated focal LN in 3 patients (ISN/RPS class III), lupus podocytopathy in 2 patients, minimal mesangial LN in 2 patients (ISN/RPS class I), and/or rare intracapillary IgM deposits suspicious for cryoglobulinemic or other focal immune complex glomerulonephritis.

Treatment and Follow-up

All patients with available treatment data were treated with immunosuppressive agents (7/7), including steroids,7 hydroxychloroquine,4 and/or mycophenolate.3 A total of 3 patients underwent repeat kidney biopsy, revealing the following: (1) mesangial proliferative LN with associated chronic injury (ISN/RPS class III) and without interstitial infiltrates (at 1 year), (2) class V LN with focal aggregates of interstitial histiocytes (at 3 years), and (3) class IV and V LN without interstitial infiltrates (at 10 years). A patient with suspected evolving SLE and an atypical hematopoietic infiltrate on kidney biopsy (Case 9) had a lymph node biopsy negative for neoplasm or KF-like necroinflammation, a skin biopsy showing leukocytoclastic vasculitis with IgM and IgG deposits without KF-like inflammation, and a type III cryoglobulin identified weeks after biopsy; she was treated as lupus with stable renal findings but worsening systemic symptoms at 6 months.

Kidney Biopsy Findings From Patients With Systemic KFD

Clinical Presentation

A total of 8 patients (Cases 10–17) with a diagnosis of systemic KFD who had kidney biopsies were included for comparison (Tables 1 and 3). These patients had a median age of 31 years (range, 23–63 years), a 1:1 female-to-male ratio, and were African American (2/8), South Indian (2/8), Hispanic (1/8), White (1/8) or unknown race or ethnicity (2/8). KFD diagnosis was recent in 7 patients and preceded kidney biopsy by 8 years in 1 patient. Of these, 5 patients had a concurrent diagnosis of SLE, including 1 with SLE/mixed connective tissue disease/Sjögren syndrome/sarcoidosis; 1 had hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, and the 2 remaining patients had no prior rheumatologic diagnosis. All patients had a lymph node biopsy showing necrotizing histiocytic lymphadenitis characteristic of KFD. Discussion

In this clinicopathologic cohort, we examine a distinctive but rare finding in kidney biopsies—tubulointerstitial involvement by KF-like necroinflammation and cell turnover. We found that these cases were derived largely from patients with previously diagnosed or suspected lupus and suggest that many of them represent lupus-associated interstitial necroinflammation, a novel form of interstitial nephritis. Although taken in the setting of clinically and serologically active lupus with other systemic symptoms, these kidney biopsies were relatively distinct from the glomerular and immune-complex-mediated injury common in LN. Specifically, the majority had limited or no glomerular involvement, and when present, this usually consisted of focal, mild active or chronic lesions with modest immune complex deposition, or podocytopathy. Our findings expand the renal manifestations observed in LN as well as the differential for necrotizing lymphohistiocytic inflammation on kidney biopsy and raise questions about the involved mechanisms and significance of KF-like renal necroinflammation. Beyond the unusual histology, we did not identify other consistent, clinically distinct features in patients within this cohort with regard to presentation or outcome, albeit with a small sample size and limited follow-up data.

An interstitial necroinflammatory infiltrate with high apoptotic and mitotic activity and large nuclei without a prominent glomerulonephritis can raise concern for infection, vasculitis, hematopoietic malignancy, septic emboli from endocarditis, or potentially an unusual infarct, autoimmune phenomenon, or hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, the latter of which can also have LN and focal cortical necrosis.11 In settings of limited tissue, KF-like necroinflammation can often be highlighted by CD8 and myeloperoxidase,12,13 but an expanded immunohistochemical staining panel may be required to exclude a malignant hematopoietic infiltrate or infection depending on the morphology and clinical history. Although 1 patient with lupus also had active EBV, no infectious agent was detected in kidney biopsy tissue in this or any other case, and no patient was subsequently found to have an underlying hematopoietic malignancy or infectious driver of the kidney inflammation.

KFD may overlap with autoimmune diseases including SLE, antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, Sjögren syndrome, and systemic sclerosis.14 Although not the primary focus of this investigation, the 8 patients reported herein represent the largest series of patients with KFD who underwent kidney biopsy. Previously reported kidney biopsy findings in patients with KFD include PLA2R-negative membranous nephropathy,15 ISN/RPS class I LN,16 class IV proliferative LN,17 and class V LN18 In patients with KFD without kidney biopsy, acute kidney injury19 and acute kidney injury in the setting of atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome20 have also been reported. In our cohort, biopsy was generally prompted by proteinuria. LN was present in 4 of 8 patients with KFD undergoing kidney biopsy, with 3 of these showing class V LN, including 2 cases of Exostosin 1 positive membranous LN. Although this represents a small subset of patients with KFD, it adds data to the connection between membranous nephropathy and KFD, particularly in patients with SLE.

None of the patients with systemic KFD had KF-like necroinflammation on kidney biopsy, suggesting that although the unique pattern of inflammation is shared, other factors may determine the degree of organ involvement and clinical manifestations. The strength of this conclusion is somewhat limited by the lack of histologic evaluation of other organs (lymph nodes, skin) in 8 of 9 patients with KF-like necroinflammation on kidney biopsy, all of whom also had a constellation of systemic features common in both KFD and active SLE.21,22 Case reports and small series highlighting parallel histology of lymphohistiocytic inflammation with karyorrhectic debris in both lymph nodes and skin in patients with KFD and SLE4, 5, 6, 7, 8, as well as 1 previously reported patient with systemic KFD and necrotizing lymphohistiocytic inflammation in both kidney and lymph node biopsies10, together suggest that this pattern of inflammation is shared by KFD and SLE and may be seen in multiple organs. However, larger studies of patients with untreated SLE and lymphadenopathy suggest that Langerhans cell infiltration—rather than KF-like necroinflammation—may be the more prevalent immunologic process.23

Conclusion

In conclusion, although lymphohistiocytic inflammation with prominent karyorrhexis most commonly manifests as a necrotizing lymphadenitis or dermatitis in both KFD and SLE, our study reveals that this distinctive pattern may also be recognized as an unusual interstitial nephritis. It is most commonly associated with SLE and is present without the usual type or extent of glomerular inflammation or immune complex deposition that typically characterizes kidney biopsies in active SLE.
