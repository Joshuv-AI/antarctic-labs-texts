Computational investigation of biochanin a targeting DEPTOR in Alzheimer’s disease with in vitro cellular validation of neuroprotective activity
Authors: Vishnu Malakar, Dhritiman Roy, Neeru Dugar, Pratap Chand Mali, Chandi C Malakar, Mukesh Gautam, Nitesh Kumar Poddar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTrifolium pratense (TP) L. contains active phytoconstituents such as biochanin A are known to prevent oxidative stress–induced neuronal death in Alzheimer’s disease (AD). This study explored the molecular mechanism of TP phytoconstituents in AD therapy by network pharmacology and computational modelling. Common targets were analyzed via protein–protein interaction (PPI) in STRING and Cytoscape, followed by Gene Ontology (GO). Based on topological parameters DEPTOR (DEP domain–containing mTOR-interacting protein) was selected as a key target. Subsequently, molecular docking, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, MM-PBSA free energy calculations, and DFT analyses were performed. It revealed that Biochanin A exhibited the highest MM/PBSA energy (− 150.04 kJ/mol) compared to the standard drug (− 24.01 kJ/mol) and formed stable interactions with key residues (Lys131 and Arg138). Moreover, the currently available therapy targeting the cholinesterase enzyme-related pathways is inadequate, as it displays only symptomatic treatment and fails to address the underlying neurodegenerative molecular mechanisms related to AD. In addition to computational analyses, in vitro validation was performed to confirm the neuroprotective effect of biochanin A. Using the MTT assay, increasing concentrations of Aβ₁–₄₂ (0.625–20 µM) were shown to reduce SH-SY5Y cell viability in a dose-dependent manner, with 10 µM Aβ₁–₄₂ causing ~ 48% reduction. Pretreatment with biochanin A (6.25–100 µM) significantly improved cell survival, with 100 µM restoring viability to ~ 82% of control levels (P < 0.01), indicating a dose-dependent neuroprotective effect without marked cytotoxicity below 100 µM. Overall, this integrative study reveals that biochanin A may target DEPTOR and stabilize mTOR regulatory interactions, suggesting a promising computationally supported and experimentally validated neuroprotective mechanism for Alzheimer’s disease therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47343-7.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, Network Pharmacology, DEPTOR, Molecular simulation, MM-PBSASubject terms: Biochemistry, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery, Neuroscience

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is an autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disease characterized by cognitive deficits and memory impairment1. The active pathological biomarkers of AD are the aggregation of amyloid plaque, neurofibrillary tangles (NFT), neuronal inflammation, astrogliosis, and synaptic dysfunction2. The major significant changes in microglial and astrocyte function in AD lead to the production of pro-inflammatory mediators and other markers which involved in the immune and inflammatory activity. Currently, Donepezil, Rivastigmine, Galantamine, and amantadine are Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved drugs that mitigate symptomatic relief in AD patients3. Recently, the FDA approved anti-amyloid monoclonal antibody drugs, such as aducanumab, lecanemab, and donanemab, based on surrogate biomarkers of reduced Aβ aggregation in AD patients4. Subsequently, urgent need to identify the potent target mechanism via suitable pathways and treatment strategies. In the current era, natural plants and their phytoconstituents have gained more attention. The research advancement in this field has evaluated that the phytoconstituents (P. constituents) isolated from herbs of the traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) database (https://tcmsp-e.com/tcmsp.php) display potential therapeutic effects against AD and may be associated with lower side effects5. Trifolium pratense L. (TP) plant synonym name is red clover, which has also been used as a medicine and dietary supplement at a fixed dose6. The traditional uses include fever, heart disease, inflammatory symptoms, and neurodegenerative disease7. Due to their active phytoconstituents (P. constituents) containing polyphenolic active moiety, such as formononetin, genistein, and biochanin A (BCA), that act as an antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, phytoestrogen, and neuroprotective effects8. For instance, TP active P. constituents ameliorate cognitive decline, function and mental health improvement in AD patients. Wei L et al. reported that Pratensein acts as a neuroprotective agent against amyloid-beta (Aβ1−42) induced cognitive impairment in an animal model. The results in reduction of inflammatory mediators, including malondialdehyde, nitrous oxide, interleukin-1beta, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, also reduce the Aβ peptide aggregation by exhibiting the Aβ-associated gene expression, such as amyloid precursor protein (APP), beta-secretase (BACE), and neprilysin (NEP)9. Network pharmacology (NP) and in-silico approach integrated the drug-targets-disease relationship for the evaluation of potent targets and novel therapeutic approaches on the basis of protein-ligand stability and functional properties10. It has also been extensively incorporated in the TCM research field to identify potential targets and their molecular pathways underlying higher therapeutic efficacy of drugs11. Moreover, the use of NP to identify the active targets of BCA in AD therapy and their associated molecular signalling pathways in the Wistar Albino AD rat model10. Hou et al. analyzed that BCA reduced the cognitive impairment, inhibited Aβ aggregation, and BACE1 level within the hippocampus of APP mice by reducing mitochondrial function and abnormal mitochondrial autophagy12. Davies et al. reported that the Aβ1−42 peptide-treated SH-SY5Y cell line causes a reduction of DAPTOR (DEP domain-containing mTOR-interacting protein) expression and remarkable upregulation of mTOR gene expression13. Caccamo et al. revealed that aggregation of Aβ leads to hyperactivity of the mTOR pathway via PRAS40 phosphorylation regulation, while RSV enhances the non-amyloidogenic pathway cleavage of APP, which enhances Aβ clearance and regulation of neuronal death14. Detailed investigation has revealed that the DEPTOR’s ability to potentially regulate mTOR homeostasis by enhancing DEPTOR levels that inhibit mTOR hyperactivity can be detected in postmortem AD brains and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs)-based neuronal death in AD patients. Consequently, by enhancing autophagic reflux, regulating proteotoxicity, and reducing insulin or insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), it mitigated the signalling deficits associated with sporadic AD pathogenesis13. While cholinesterase inhibitors such as donepezil and rivastigmine display symptomatic relief without changing disease pathogenesis2. Unlike DEPTOR-mediated strategies, it may exert disease-altering effects by targeting multiple AD biomarkers, such as autophagy impairment, rather than by exerting anticholinergic effects. Based on preclinical results, it can be stated that the mTOR expression inhibition improves the Aβ-induced synaptic plasticity and hyperphosphorylation of tau protein13. The main goal of this study is to identify and explore the TP active P. constituents BCA targeting DEPTOR for AD treatment by NP, in-silico approach, and Aβ1−42 induced Wistar albino rat AD model.

Materials and methods

Computational studies

Chemical candidates of TP and their associated targets

All TP-related P. constituents with AD property were collected from the literature and various natural databases. A total number of 35 molecular structures were examined by using the Schrodinger suite QikProp model 2025-415. Based on molecular weight between 250 and 500 g/mol, without sugar moiety, hydrogen bond donor (HBD) and acceptor (HBA) 0–2, and 0–8, blood-brain-barrier (BBB) penetration > 0.3, and central nervous system (CNS) ≥ 0. Further, the active P. constituents were downloaded from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) to identify the molecular structures by compound name in the entry system15. TP active compounds-associated targets with a cut-off probability value > 0 were selected by the Swiss Target Prediction database (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/)16.

Prediction of AD-related targets

AD-related targets were retrieved from different databases, including GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/)17, DisGeNET (https://disgenet.com/), and GeDiPNET (https://gedipnet.bicnirrh.res.in/), that display extensively user-friendly, insightful information in annotated human genes, and automatically incorporate gene-centric data, such as genomics, proteomics, metabolomics, clinical, and functional information. Finally, from different database targets were incorporated and labelled as AD-related potential targets18.

Network construction and protein-protein interaction analysis

STRING database (https://string-db.org/) integrates to analyze all known and predicted connections within targets, such as physical and functional associations19. In this research article, a database was used for the prediction of the association between TPP. constituents-targets and AD-related targets, which were examined as potential therapeutic targets. The potent target and its protein-protein interaction (PPI) network were designed based on the following parameters: species (Homo sapiens) and reliability (≥ 0.4 medium confidence). A 0.4 cut-off score indicates a medium confidence value while analyzing PPIs. This specific value was chosen due to its ability to normalize the specificity as well as sensitivity, although acts as a dependable threshold value for the determination of potential PPIs. Proteins corresponding to higher scores or equal to 0.4 are integrated within the PPI network. Furthermore, the protein network was designed by Cytoscape v3.8.0 software to analyze nodes topological parameters within the network, such as degree, closeness, and betweenness value. The degree denotes the total number of networks within network serve directly with the node and represents node importance in the network. Similarly, closeness and betweenness determine the importance in the network depending on the nodes’ interaction ability, or serve as a bridge for detailed information between connected nodes.

Functional enrichment and pathways analysis

All potent therapeutic proteins were integrated into Gene Ontology (GO) as well as Reactome pathway database identification by the Cytoscape plug-in ClueGO V2.5.4 to analyze the associated molecular signaling and associated GO nomenclature, such as BP (biological process), CC (cellular component), and MF (molecular function) types20. The signalling pathways and GO nomenclature with a standard cut-off threshold value p < 0.05 were investigated and retained. Furthermore, ShinyGO (https://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/) was integrated into the analysis of GO and Reactome pathways database, results plotted in a bar plot of pathways and GO in a bubble plot. The TP active P. constituents and their potential targets-related pathways were analyzed by Cytoscape v3.8 software21.

Density functional theory calculation

Density Functional Theory (DFT) analysis is a modern computational technique that determines the nature and electronic configuration of potential compounds by applying the basic parameters, including B3LYP/6-311G**, in the Gaussian 16 software for Frontier Molecular Orbital analysis. The potential compound optimized geometry energy was calculated through DFT methods and their FMO energies, such as HOMO (highest occupied molecular orbital) and LUMO (lowest unoccupied molecular orbital), electronic configuration, and dipole moments. The DFT method aims to identify potential compound biological activity through electronic configuration methods11.

Preparation of ligands

A total No. of 24 active P. constituents of TP were downloaded from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) in SDF 3D format, further converted into Protein Data Bank (PDB) format by using Open Babel software. Subsequently, the SDF file was transformed into. pdbqt employing the AutoDock tool22.

Preparation of protein and active site prediction

The DEPTOR (PDB ID:7DKL) protein sequence was downloaded from the RCSB protein database (https://www.rcsb.org/structure/7DKL). The protein was imported and prepared using AutoDock Tools Software. The protein preparation procedure involves deletion of unwanted water molecules, heteroatoms, and unwanted catalyst or co-crystal, subsequently incorporating polar hydrogen atoms, Kollman charges, solvation energies, and saved into .pdbqt format for molecular docking11. Furthermore, the prepared protein was visualized using Discovery Studio Visualizer software, which permits the analysis of active binding sites. To analyze the binding site using Discovery Studio Visualizer, the receptor-ligand interaction section using “Define and Edit Binding Site” left-side panel parameter was used to define the binding pocket. The binding site coordinates were X, Y, and Z (16.83 Å, -8.53 Å, and − 28.95 Å), and active residues were Arg53, 55, and 138, Lys58 and Lys131, respectively23.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was performed to identify ligand-protein complex binding affinity by AutoDock Vina Tools, and their active amino acid interaction was visualized by using Biovia Discovery tools. Both protein and ligands were saved in the same format (.pdbqt) with a grid box size confined based on active amino acid residues, including Arg53, Arg54, Lys58, Ala79, Lys87, Leu88, Lys91, Lys131, and Arg13824. The docked complex was predicted based on binding affinity and their residues interaction17.

Molecular dynamics studies

Based on the highest docking score and standard amino acid interaction with the active P. constituents were selected for molecular dynamics simulation by using the CHARMM 36 force field of GROMACS software 2025.3 version at 1000 ns for gaining deeper insight, such as protein-ligand complex stability and flexibility over time. The Swiss Param online server is used to generate a ligand topology file25. The native protein topology file was further created through the CHARMM27 force field along with the water charge model. Subsequently, a protein-ligand complex. The Ro file was modified by adding protein and ligand. gro file. Further, a 1.0 nm triclinic box size was generated in the presence of a boundary situation, along with ions (Na+ and Cl−), which were selected for complex neutralization. Initially, for system energy minimization, 50,000 steps were carried out under NVT and NTP with 300 K temperature and 1 atmospheric pressure for 100 ps. The simulation trajectory analysis: gmx_RMSD, RMSF26, Rg, SASA, H-bonds, and total energy were analyzed7.

Principal component analysis (PCA) calculation

PCA was calculated by using a molecular simulation trajectory file, which calculates the protein-ligand complex orientation, intrinsic activity, and translation coordinates. However, PCA abrupt trajectory motion and other complex trajectory motion were created on two axes, PC1 vs. PC2 of PCA analysis27. The gmx_sham, covar, and anaeig were used to create the principal component result data. Subsequently, a graph was plotted between two different eigenvectors (ev1 vs. ev2), which illustrates two different complex orientations11.

Molecular mechanics poisson–boltzmann surface area (MMPBSA) calculation

To determine the binding energy between protein-ligand complexes, which is a crucial step in molecular simulation and thermodynamic evaluation, was performed via the gmx_MMPBSA file consolidated in the GROMACS 2025.3 software. The MM/PBSA was mainly identified with molecular simulation studies, which determine the different binding energy (ΔGbind) of protein-ligand complexes11.

In vitro cellular study

The human neuroblastoma cell line SH-SY5Y used in this work was obtained from the National Centre for Cell Science (NCCS), Pune, India. Cells were cultured in a medium composed of an equal ratio (1:1) of Nutrient Mixture Ham’s F12 and DMEM/F12. The growth medium was enriched with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) to provide essential nutrients and 1% penicillin–streptomycin to prevent microbial contamination. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with an atmosphere of 5% CO₂ and 95% air to ensure optimal cell growth and viability.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay. SH-SY5Y cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁴ cells per well and allowed to adhere for 12 h at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. After attachment, the medium was replaced, and cells were pretreated with biochanin-A (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 1 h before exposure to amyloid-β₁₋₄₂ (Aβ₁₋₄₂) (GenScript, China). Amyloid-β₁₋₄₂ (Aβ₁₋₄₂) peptide was first dissolved in hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP), aliquoted, and dried under vacuum. The peptide film was reconstituted in DMSO and diluted with culture medium to the desired concentration immediately before use. Following a 48-hour incubation, the medium was discarded and 20 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to each well28. The plates were then incubated for 4 h at 37 °C to allow formazan crystal formation. The supernatant was carefully removed, and the crystals were dissolved in 150 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Multiskan™ FC; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean ± SD from at least three independent cellular experiments. Statistical comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test in GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA), and values of p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Biochanin a protected SH-SY5Y cells against amyloid-β₁₋₄₂-induced cytotoxicity

To establish the optimal concentration of amyloid-β₁–₄₂ (Aβ₁–₄₂) for inducing cytotoxicity, SH-SY5Y cells were treated with increasing concentrations of Aβ₁–₄₂ (0.625, 1.25, 2.5, 5, 10, and 20 µM) for 48 h, and cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay (Fig. 9A and D). Aβ₁–₄₂ reduced cell viability in a dose-dependent manner, with a significant decline to 52.1 ± 4.6% of control observed at 10 µM (P < 0.01). Therefore, 10 µM Aβ₁–₄₂ was selected as the working concentration for subsequent experiments. To evaluate both the cytotoxicity and cytoprotective potential of biochanin A, SH-SY5Y cells were first exposed to various concentrations of biochanin A (6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, 100, and 200) alone for 48 h. Biochanin A up to 100 µM did not significantly affect cell viability, whereas concentrations at 200 µM produced a minute reduction in viability, indicating mild cytotoxicity at higher doses (Fig. 9B and E). For the protection assay, cells were pretreated with biochanin A (6.25–100 µM) for 1 h before exposure to 10 µM Aβ₁–₄₂ for 48 h. As shown in Fig. 9C and F, Aβ₁–₄₂ alone decreased cell viability to 51.8 ± 4.3% of the control (P < 0.01). Pretreatment with biochanin A (12.5–100 µM) significantly improved cell viability in a dose-dependent manner, with 100 µM biochanin A restoring cell survival to ~ 82.45 ± 7.9% of control levels (P < 0.01). These findings indicate that biochanin A confers a dose-dependent neuroprotective effect against Aβ₁–Aβ₄₂-induced cytotoxicity, with no notable intrinsic toxicity observed at concentrations below 100 µM.

Conclusion

Our research focused on network pharmacology, molecular docking, molecular dynamics (over 1000 ns), principal component analysis, Molecular Mechanics Poisson–Boltzmann Surface Area (MM-PBSA), and density functional theory (DFT) to investigate Trifolium pratense–derived phytoconstituents and their potential targets for Alzheimer’s disease (AD) therapy through key molecular pathways. Network pharmacology identified five major targets: DEPTOR, ESR1, BCL2, STAT3, and TNF-α. Based on higher degree, closeness, and betweenness values, DEPTOR was selected for further studies as a potential modulator in AD treatment. Molecular docking revealed that biochanin A displayed the highest binding affinity and interacted with key residues (Lys131 and Arg138) compared with the standard drug. Subsequent molecular dynamics simulations, PCA, DFT, and MM-PBSA analyses confirmed the stability and strong binding of the biochanin A–A-DEPTOR complex over 1000 ns. Furthermore, in vitro validation using the MTT assay demonstrated that biochanin A protected SH-SY5Y cells against Aβ₁–₄₂–induced cytotoxicity, significantly restoring cell viability at 100 µM, thereby confirming its neuroprotective potential. Overall, DEPTOR emerged as a promising therapeutic target, and biochanin A represents a potential lead compound that provides both computational and experimental evidence supporting its role in AD management and the rational design of new neuroprotective agents.
