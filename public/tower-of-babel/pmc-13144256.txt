Commentary: the TSP-1-CD47-integrin α4β1 axis—deciphering pathogenic mechanisms and therapeutic horizons in rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Jie Guo, Feng-yi Mai, Xin-yu Li, Yu Liu, Jing-rong Liang, Xing-long Li, Chen-guang Li
Published: 2026

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA), a chronic systemic inflammatory disorder affecting 0.5–1% of the global population, remains a major clinical challenge due to its complex pathogenesis and limited curative therapies [1]. The persistent infiltration of immune cells into synovial tissue, leading to chronic inflammation, pannus formation, and joint destruction, is a hallmark of the disease [2, 3]. While previous studies have implicated diverse immune molecules and signaling pathways in rheumatoid arthritis progression, the precise mechanisms governing T cell infiltration, a key driver of synovial inflammation, remain incompletely elucidated. Hu et al.’s recent study published in Frontiers in Immunology addresses this critical gap by identifying the TSP-1-CD47-integrin α4β1 axis as a central regulator of T cell infiltration and synovial inflammation in RA, offering new insights into disease pathogenesis and potential therapeutic targets [4].

Results and discussion

A rigorous multilevel approach validates the central role of CD47

CD47, also known as integrin-associated protein, is a 50 kDa glycosylated cell surface glycoprotein of the immunoglobulin superfamily, characterized by an extracellular IgV domain, five transmembrane regions, and a short cytoplasmic tail. It is ubiquitously expressed across nearly all somatic cells, including erythrocytes, immune cells, and epithelial cells, with variable expression levels under physiological and pathological conditions [5]. The core biological function of CD47 lies in its role as an immune checkpoint. By interacting with signal regulatory protein α (SIRPα) on phagocytes, it delivers a “don’t eat me” signal that inhibits phagocytosis, maintaining self-recognition and immune homeostasis. Beyond this, CD47 regulates immune cell activation: high CD47 expression in NK cells, CD8⁺ T cells, and B cells enhances antiviral responses, cytotoxicity, and humoral immunity respectively. It also mediates inhibitory signaling via thrombospondin-1, impacting cell proliferation, adhesion, and migration [6, 7].

One of the most compelling strengths of Hu et al.’s work is its comprehensive, multilevel experimental design, which integrates clinical data analysis, genetic manipulation, cellular biology, and molecular signaling studies. The authors begin by leveraging large-scale GEO database resources, analyzing 230 synovial tissue samples and 1238 peripheral blood samples from RA patients. This clinical evidence reveals significant overexpression of CD47, its ligand TSP-1, and multiple integrin subunits (including α4, αL, and β1) in RA patients compared to healthy controls, establishing a strong correlation between these molecules and disease status [4]. Such translational relevance is critical, as it bridges the gap between preclinical research and human disease, ensuring that subsequent mechanistic findings are clinically meaningful. To dissect the functional role of CD47, the authors employ CRISPR/Cas9 technology to generate Cd47 knockout rats, a rigorous genetic tool that avoids the off-target effects associated with pharmacological inhibition. In collagen-induced arthritis (CIA), a well-established preclinical model for rheumatoid arthritis (RA), Cd47 deficiency significantly alleviates disease severity, as evidenced by reduced joint swelling, decreased arthritis scores, attenuated synovial hyperplasia, and diminished bone erosion. Notably, the authors demonstrate that Cd47 knockout specifically reduces T cell infiltration into synovial tissue without affecting B cells, macrophages, or neutrophils, highlighting the cell type specific role of CD47 in RA pathogenesis. This specificity is a key insight, as it suggests that targeting CD47 may modulate pathogenic T cell responses without inducing broad immunosuppression— a major limitation of current RA therapies such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or corticosteroids.

Mechanistic insights: the TSP-1-CD47-integrin α4β1 trimolecular axis

Hu et al.’s study excels in unraveling the molecular mechanisms underlying CD47-mediated T cell infiltration. By means of a series of well-orchestrated experiments, the authors establish a trimolecular model wherein TSP-1 (expressed on vascular endothelial cells in inflamed synovium) interacts with CD47 on T cells, activating integrin α4β1. This activation induces a conformational change in integrin α4β1 from a folded, low-affinity state to an extended, high-affinity state, enhancing its binding to VCAM-1 on endothelial cells and promoting T cell transendothelial migration into synovial tissue [4]. The authors confirm this interaction using laser confocal microscopy and co-immunoprecipitation, demonstrating direct physical association between CD47 and integrin α4β1 on T cells. Furthermore, the study identifies PKA and Src kinases as key downstream mediators of CD47-induced integrin α4β1 activation. Inhibiting these kinases, either pharmacologically or genetically, blocks integrin α4β1 phosphorylation and activation, thereby reducing T cell adhesion and migration. Importantly, the authors show that this signaling pathway is Gi protein-independent, distinguishing it from CD47-mediated integrin activation in other cell types such as sickle red blood cells. This cell-type-specific signaling divergence underscores the complexity of CD47 biology and highlights the need for context-specific therapeutic strategies.

Therapeutic implications and translational potential

The translational value of Hu et al.’s findings is a major highlight of the study. The authors demonstrate that local injection of CD47 antibodies, PKA inhibitors, or Src inhibitors significantly alleviates CIA symptoms in wild-type rats, mirroring the effects of Cd47 knockout [4]. This preclinical evidence strongly supports the potential of targeting the TSP-1-CD47-integrin α4β1 axis for RA therapy. Notably, current RA treatments, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors, are effective in only a subset of patients and are associated with increased infection risk due to their broad immunosuppressive effects. In contrast, targeting CD47 or its downstream signaling pathways offers a more selective approach, focusing on pathogenic T cell infiltration without disrupting other immune cell populations. Another notable translational advantage is the identification of TSP-1-derived peptide 4N1K as a key mediator of CD47-integrin α4β1 activation. The authors show that 4N1K enhances T cell adhesion and migration in a CD47-dependent manner, while a control peptide (4KGG) has no effect [4]. This finding suggests that blocking the TSP-1-CD47 interaction via anti-CD47 antibodies or peptide antagonists could be a viable therapeutic strategy. Additionally, since PKA and Src kinases are well-characterized drug targets with existing small-molecule inhibitors, repurposing these compounds for RA therapy may accelerate clinical development.

Limitations and future directions

While Hu et al.’s study provides a landmark mechanistic framework for understanding T cell-mediated synovial inflammation in rheumatoid arthritis, several limitations and unaddressed questions highlight avenues for future investigation.

Limitations of global Cd47 knockout models and the need for cell-type-specific targeting

A key technical constraint of the current study is the use of global Cd47 knockout (KO) rats generated via CRISPR/Cas9. Although functional assays confirm loss of CD47 activity, residual Cd47 mRNA and truncated peptide fragments persist in KO animals [4]. This residual expression raises concerns about potential off-target or partial functional effects, as CD47 is ubiquitously expressed across hematopoietic and non-hematopoietic cells [5]. Global KO cannot distinguish whether CD47’s pro-inflammatory role is intrinsic to T cells, vascular endothelial cells, or other synovial-resident cells. For instance, Vallejo et al. [8] previously showed that FLS-derived TSP-1 interacts with T cell CD47 to promote T cell activation, but the current study does not dissect cell-autonomous vs. non-cell-autonomous contributions of CD47. Employ conditional Cd47 knockout models using Cre-LoxP technology to target CD47 specifically in T cells or vascular endothelial cells. This approach will validate whether T cell-intrinsic CD47 is the primary mediator of synovial infiltration, as suggested by the selective reduction in T cells in global KO rats [4]. Conditional KO models have emerged as a pivotal tool for dissecting cell-type-specific functions in RA. A notable illustration is the work by Nakae et al., who leveraged IL-17-Cre transgenic mice to delineate the distinct contributions of Th17 cells to the pathogenesis of collagen-induced arthritis [9] .

Incomplete exploration of integrin crosstalk and synergistic pathways

Hu et al. focus exclusively on integrin α4β1 as the downstream effector of CD47-TSP-1 signaling, but their GEO data analysis reveals overexpression of multiple integrin subunits (αL, αM, β2, β3) in RA synovial tissues [4]. While the study excludes direct interaction between CD47 and integrin αLβ2, it overlooks potential synergistic crosstalk between integrin α4β1 and other integrins or adhesion molecules in T cell transendothelial migration. Notably, Chan et al. [10] reported that engagement of α4β1 integrin with vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) triggers αLβ2 integrin/intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1)-dependent adhesion in human T cells, a signaling pathway that is pivotal for leukocyte extravasation into inflamed tissues. Furthermore, integrin αVβ3, which is also overexpressed in the synovial tissue of rheumatoid arthritis patients, has been demonstrated to interact with TSP-1 in melanoma cells, thereby regulating cell migration [11]. This finding raises important questions regarding the potential cooperative role of αVβ3 with α4β1 integrin in the pathogenesis of RA. Future studies should investigate the synergistic interactions between integrin α4β1 and αLβ2/αVβ3 using blocking antibodies or siRNA-mediated knockdown in Jurkat cells and primary T cells isolated from patients with rheumatoid arthritis. Functional assays, including adhesion assays and Transwell migration assays, ought to be employed to determine whether the co-inhibition of α4β1 and αLβ2 elicits additive or synergistic reductions in synovial infiltration.

Off-target effects and therapeutic delivery challenges for CD47-targeted therapies

Hu et al. propose CD47 antibodies and PKA/Src inhibitors as potential RA therapeutics, but they underestimate the risk of off-target effects due to CD47’s pleiotropic functions. CD47 is widely expressed on various cell types, and its interaction with SIRP-α provides a “don’t eat me” signal that regulates phagocytosis [12]. Blocking CD47 could enhance macrophage-mediated phagocytosis of healthy cells, leading to anemia or thrombocytopenia—side effects observed in clinical trials of CD47 antibodies for cancer therapy [6]. While local injection (e.g., intra-articular) reduced systemic toxicity in the CIA model [4], this delivery route is impractical for widespread joint involvement in RA. Additionally, PKA and Src kinases are ubiquitous signaling molecules—non-specific inhibition may disrupt normal immune function or tissue homeostasis [13]. Future studies should develop T cell-specific CD47 inhibitors (e.g., antibody-drug conjugates targeting CD47 and CD3, or peptide antagonists that bind CD47 only in the context of T cell integrins). Leclair et al. [14] showed that TSP-1-derived peptides can exert CD47-dependent or -independent effects, suggesting that peptide engineering could enhance T cell selectivity. Alternatively, exploring combination therapeutic strategies to mitigate off-target effects: combine low-dose CD47 antibodies with existing RA therapeutics (e.g., TNF inhibitors or JAK inhibitors) to attain synergistic efficacy at reduced concentrations.

Limited exploration of non-T cell immune populations and synovial microenvironment interactions

Hu et al.’s study focuses on T cell infiltration but overlooks CD47’s potential role in other immune cells implicated in RA, such as macrophages and neutrophils. Macrophages are major producers of TNF-α and IL-6 in synovial tissue [15], and CD47-SIRP-α signaling regulates macrophage phagocytosis [5]. While Hu et al. report no change in macrophage/neutrophil numbers in Cd47 KO rats, they do not assess macrophage activation status or cytokine secretion. Additionally, the synovial microenvironment includes FLS, which produce TSP-1 and VCAM-1 and interact with T cells to promote inflammation [8]. The current study does not address whether CD47 is expressed on FLS or contributes to FLS-T cell crosstalk. Future studies should investigate CD47 expression on RA FLS and macrophages of synovial tissue biopsies. Functional assays should test whether FLS-derived CD47 regulates T cell adhesion or cytokine production, as shown for TSP-1. Evaluate the impact of CD47 inhibition on macrophage polarization (M1 vs. M2) in vitro and in vivo. For example, treat RA synovial macrophages with CD47 antibodies and measure changes in TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-10 expression, as M1/M2 imbalance is a key driver of synovial inflammation [16].

Conclusion

Hu et al.’s study represents a landmark contribution to RA research, providing a comprehensive mechanistic understanding of the TSP-1-CD47-integrin α4β1 axis in regulating T cell infiltration and synovial inflammation. By integrating clinical data, genetic models, and molecular signaling analyses, the authors establish CD47 as a key therapeutic target for RA, offering a more selective alternative to current immunosuppressive therapies. The identification of PKA and Src kinases as downstream mediators further expands the therapeutic pipeline, providing multiple points of intervention within this pathway. As the field moves forward, translating these findings into clinical practice will require addressing potential off-target effects, optimizing drug delivery, and validating these mechanisms in human clinical trials. Additionally, combining CD47-targeted therapies with existing treatments (such as TNF inhibitors or JAK inhibitors) may enhance efficacy for refractory patients. Overall, Hu et al.’s work not only advances our understanding of RA pathogenesis but also paves the way for the development of novel, precision-based therapies that improve patient outcomes while minimizing adverse effects. This study serves as a model for translational immunology research, demonstrating how rigorous preclinical studies can bridge basic science and clinical application to address unmet medical needs.
