Ultrasound-assisted deep eutectic solvent extraction of green, high-efficient, and high-bioactive polysaccharides from Glycyrrhiza uralensis
Authors: Yan Sun, Shandang Shi, Rui Tang, Zhuang Meng, Haitao Shen, Lingling Chen, Fei Wang, Hongbin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Glycyrrhiza uralensis Fisch., commonly known as licorice, is a perennial herb of the genus Glycyrrhiza within the subfamily Papilionoideae of the family Leguminosae. As among the most widely used herbs in traditional Chinese medicine, G. uralensis displays a broad spectrum of pharmacological benefits, with documented activities including spleen tonification, heat clearance, detoxification, phlegm resolution, cough relief, spasm alleviation, and the modulation of coadministered herbs [1], [2]. In addition to its medicinal uses, extracts of this plant are widely incorporated into cosmetic formulations, food additives, and natural preservatives [3], [4]. The therapeutic effects of this plant are largely attributed to its bioactive constituents, particularly polysaccharides, flavonoids, and coumarins [5]. Among these components, polysaccharides have drawn increasing interest from food, materials science, and cosmetic sectors owing to their favorable safety profile, low toxicity, and numerous biological functions. A recent investigation revealed an acidic polysaccharide from G. uralensis with a molecular weight of 26.4 kDa that exerted notable immunomodulatory effects by regulating dendritic cell function through the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway [6]. Several subsequent studies have further demonstrated that G. uralensis polysaccharides possess lipid-lowering, antioxidant, and antitumor properties, underscoring their considerable promise as functional ingredients for food and pharmaceutical applications [7], [8].

Efficient extraction of polysaccharides represents a critical step toward their downstream application. Existing studies on GPs have largely relied on a single extraction technique, such as hot water extraction (HWE), ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE), or subcritical water extraction (SWE) [9], [10], [11]. Each of these strategies, however, has intrinsic limitations. HWE is time-consuming and remains inefficient for the recovery of intracellular polysaccharides, whereas SWE requires expensive high-pressure equipment. Although UAE effectively disrupts cell walls, it tends to induce chain fragmentation, which compromises the structural integrity and biological activity of the products. The lack of systematic comparisons among combined extraction strategies has restricted the current understanding of how processing conditions govern the structure–activity relationships of GPs [12].

Deep eutectic solvents (DESs) constitute a class of supramolecular systems formed through self-assembly between a hydrogen bond acceptor (HBA) and a hydrogen bond donor (HBD). Owing to their excellent physicochemical features, including thermal stability, low volatility, and tunable polarity, DESs enable efficient disruption of plant matrices with minimal damage to structural integrity and align well with the principles of green chemistry [13]. The integration of DESs with UAE further allows for solvent recovery and reuse, thereby substantially reducing both solvent consumption and energy demand, which together support the application of this strategy at the industrial scale. The resulting ultrasound-assisted DES extraction (UDE) generates synergistic effects that accelerate extraction kinetics and modulate the properties of the recovered bioactive compounds [14], [15].

In the present work, polysaccharides obtained using water or DES as the extraction solvent were systematically compared under a series of processing modes, including heat conduction, alkaline treatment, enzymatic hydrolysis, microwave irradiation, and ultrasonication. On the basis of these comparisons, a novel, efficient, and environmentally friendly method for the extraction of polysaccharides from G. uralensis roots was established. Through a systematic evaluation of physicochemical properties, structural features, and biological activities, UDE was identified as the most effective strategy for GP extraction. These findings serve as a valuable reference for the efficient preparation of GPs and lay a solid foundation for their industrial-scale application.

Materials and methods

Reagents and analytical instruments

Methanol, trifluoroacetic acid, potassium dihydrogen phosphate, and sodium acetate were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Dextran standards (purity ≥ 99%), monosaccharide standards, and dexamethasone (purity ≥ 97%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Preparation, characterization and screening of DES

G. uralensis plants were cultivated in an experimental field, and the roots were collected for further use. Four DESs, namely, choline chloride (Ch)/oxalic acid, Ch/ethylene glycol, Ch/isopropyl alcohol, and Ch/urea, were prepared by heating according to previously reported methods [16], [17], [18], [19]. An identical HBA/HBD molar ratio of 1:3 was applied to every formulation so that the identity of the HBD remained the only variable throughout the screening. Ultrapure water at 30% (w/w) was then added to each system to lower viscosity and promote mass transfer. The pH, density, viscosity, and electrical conductivity of each DES were measured with a portable pH meter, a densitometer, a viscometer, and a precalibrated conductivity meter, respectively. G. uralensis root powder was mixed with each of the four DESs, and extraction proceeded in a KQ-500DE ultrasonic bath at 250 W and 39 °C for 30 min.

Comparative extraction of GP by eight methods

Eight methods for GP extraction were systematically compared. For each method, the milled plant powder was dispersed in the corresponding solvent at a defined liquid-to-solid ratio (LSR), and the mixture was processed under the operating conditions specific to each technique. The supernatants were then collected by centrifugation and concentrated under reduced pressure, after which the polysaccharides were precipitated with 70% ethanol, dialyzed against ultrapure water (MWCO 3500 Da), and lyophilized. Optimization of UDE by RSM and ANN modeling

Single-factor experiments were first conducted to establish the operational window for five extraction parameters, namely, the DES water content, extraction time, ultrasonication power, temperature, and LSR. On the basis of the preliminary screening results, a five-factor three-level Box–Behnken design was established, in which 46 experimental runs were performed and the polysaccharide yield was selected as the response variable. The experimental data were fitted to a second-order polynomial model with Design-Expert software, and the statistical significance of the model was assessed through analysis of variance. In parallel, an artificial neural network (ANN) model was developed to identify nonlinear relationships in the dataset. The network was trained using a backpropagation algorithm with the same 46-run BBD dataset used for response surface methodology (RSM) modeling. The predictive performance was evaluated using R2, RMSE, and mean absolute relative deviation, and validation experiments were performed under the predicted optimum conditions. The detailed data are provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2.

Comparative structural characterization of GP

Chemical composition detection

Total sugar was quantified by the phenol–sulfuric acid procedure, protein content by a BCA kit with microplate detection, and uronic acid content by the sulfuric acid-carbazole colorimetric method [20], [21].

Surface characterization by Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

Lyophilized GP samples were sputter-coated with gold and imaged on a SU8020 SEM at 500 × magnification under an accelerating voltage of 1 kV (Hitachi, Japan).

Molecular weight determination

The Mw of each fraction was determined by high-performance size-exclusion chromatography coupled with multiangle laser light scattering and refractive index detection (HPSEC-MALLS-RI). Every polysaccharide solution was passed through a 0.22-µm membrane before injection, and the elution profiles were processed with the accompanying software. Mw and Mn were obtained for each resolved peak, and the polydispersity of a peak was expressed as its Mw/Mn ratio. The mass fraction carried by each peak and the total mass recovery of each sample were calculated from the injected mass together with the refractive index response.

Monosaccharide composition determination

Polysaccharide samples were hydrolyzed in 2 M trifluoroacetic acid at 110 °C for 6 h, after which the hydrolysates were dried under nitrogen and redissolved in ultrapure water. Then, the composition was determined on a Thermo ICS-6000 instrument.

UV and FT-IR spectroscopy determination

UV absorption spectra were recorded from 200 to 600 nm to assess the levels of coextracted protein and nucleic acid impurities. FT-IR spectra were collected from 4000 to 400 cm−1 on KBr pellets to identify the principal functional groups of the polysaccharide backbone.

Congo red determination

The conformational properties of GPs were evaluated using the Congo Red method [22]. Polysaccharide‒Congo red complexes were prepared at NaOH concentrations ranging from 0 to 0.5 M, and the maximum absorption wavelength (λmax) was monitored on a spectrophotometer.

Thermal stability determination

Thermal stability was evaluated through thermogravimetric analysis using an STA 449F3 instrument (Netzsch, Selb, Germany) under a nitrogen atmosphere from 50 to 750 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. Differential scanning calorimetry was performed under the same atmosphere to record endothermic and exothermic transitions, with onset, peak, and end-set temperatures, together with the associated enthalpy changes, extracted from the resulting curves.

Functional properties of GP samples

Aqueous solubility and storage stability

Solubility was assessed by stirring 100 mg of each lyophilized fraction in 10 mL of ultrapure water at 25 °C for 30 min, followed by centrifugation and gravimetric quantification of the soluble fraction. Storage stability was assessed by monitoring the visual appearance of 1% (w/v) GP solutions at ambient temperature, 4 °C, and −20 °C for 7, 14, and 21 days, respectively. Homogeneity and color consistency were recorded photographically at each time point.

pH, zeta potential and particle size

The zeta potential and hydrodynamic particle size of each GP solution (1 mg/mL) were measured on a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 at 25 °C, in which each sample was equilibrated for 2 min prior to data acquisition [23]. The pH of each GP solution (1 mg/mL) was recorded with a calibrated pH meter at ambient temperature.

Rheological behavior

Steady-state flow curves were obtained from 0.1 to 1000 s−1 on a stress-controlled rheometer fitted with a parallel-plate geometry, and small-amplitude oscillatory measurements were performed within the linear viscoelastic regime from 0.1 to 100 rad/s. The storage modulus G' and the loss modulus G“ were recorded as a function of angular frequency.

DFT calculations of DES-saccharide interactions

Quantum chemical analysis was performed at the density functional theory level with the B3LYP hybrid functional and the 6-311G(d,p) basis set in the Gaussian 16 package. Geometry optimization, electrostatic potential mapping, and frontier molecular orbital analysis were performed for the complexes formed between Ch-EG (or water) and the three representative monosaccharides (glucose, galactose, and arabinose). Interaction energies were corrected for basis set superposition error using the counterpoise method, while noncovalent interactions were visualized through an independent gradient model based on Hirshfeld partition (IGMH) analysis using Multiwfn in combination with VMD. Isosurface plots were generated at an isovalue of 0.005 a.u.

DES recycling studies

After each extraction cycle, the DES phase was separated from the solid residue by centrifugation. Polysaccharides were precipitated from the supernatant with 70% ethanol and collected by a second centrifugation step. The remaining liquid, which contained the DES together with ethanol and co-extracted impurities, was concentrated on a rotary evaporator under reduced pressure until ethanol no longer distilled over. Ultrapure water was added to the concentrate to restore a water content of 31% (w/w), and the regenerated solvent was applied directly to the next run under the optimized UDE conditions. Five successive cycles were completed, and the polysaccharide yield was recorded for each.

Antioxidant activity determination

The in vitro antioxidant potential of GP was examined through radical scavenging assays and reducing power determination. Scavenging activities against DPPH, ABTS, hydroxyl, and superoxide anion radicals were measured according to established protocols, and the detailed experimental procedures are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Cell Culture and anti-inflammatory evaluation

BEAS-2B cell culture

Human bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS-2B), sourced from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures, were maintained in complete RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin under 5% CO2 at 37 °C (FBS; BI, XP Biomed Ltd., Shanghai, China). The cells were passaged at 80% confluence and used at passages 5 to 15.

Cytotoxicity and establishment of the IL-13 model

Cytotoxicity was assessed by a CCK-8 assay after 24 h of exposure to each GP fraction at 5 to 160 µg/mL. The IL-13 model was established by treating BEAS-2B cells with recombinant human IL-13 (10 ng/mL) for 12, 24, or 48 h, and NO production guided the choice of stimulation time. Dexamethasone at 5 µM served as the positive control. For the anti-inflammatory assay, cells were first stimulated with IL-13 and then exposed to UDE at three concentrations or to dexamethasone for 3, 6, and 12 h, after which NO production was measured.

Determination of oxidative stress

SOD, GSH-Px, and CAT activities, as well as MDA levels, were quantified using commercial assay kits according to the manufacturer's instructions (Elabscience, China).

qRT-PCR detection

Total RNA was extracted with the TRIzol reagent and reverse transcribed to cDNA using a commercial kit. Quantitative PCR was performed on a real-time PCR system with SYBR Green chemistry, in which the transcript levels of STAT6, MYD88, IL-5, CCL26, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and VEGFC were normalized against those of actin using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The primer sequences used are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Data processing and statistical analysis

For visual clarity and conciseness in the figures and text, polysaccharide samples obtained using the eight extraction methods are referred to by their methods (abbreviated as HWE, DAE, AAE, EAE, MDE, MAE, UDE, and UAE) throughout this work. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results are reported as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical comparisons among groups were performed through one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's post hoc test in SPSS 27.0, with p < 0.05 indicating a statistically significant difference. Mantel test correlations among physicochemical and bioactive parameters were calculated in R with the linkET package, and data visualization was performed in Origin 2024 along with GraphPad Prism 9.0.

Results and discussion

Screening and physicochemical characterization of DESs

Rational solvent design is fundamental to efficient and sustainable polysaccharide extraction, as the physicochemical properties of the extraction medium directly govern mass-transfer kinetics, solute selectivity, and the structural preservation of target macromolecules. DESs have been suggested to outperform conventional aqueous solvents in terms of extraction yield, purity, and structural integrity [24], [25]. To identify a DES suitable for the recovery of G. uralensis polysaccharides, four Ch-based formulations were systematically screened, and their physicochemical parameters are summarized in Table 1. The four candidate solvents covered a broad pH window from 0.52 ± 0.05 in Ch-OA to 8.56 ± 0.12 in Ch-Urea, with intermediate values of 4.85 ± 0.06 and 6.20 ± 0.12 for Ch-EG and Ch-IPA, respectively. The viscosity varied across nearly two orders of magnitude, ranging from 7 ± 1.00 mPa·s in Ch-IPA to 543 ± 2.00 mPa·s in Ch-OA. A strictly parallel pattern was discernible in electrical conductivity, which reached 650 ± 1.73 mS/cm in Ch-OA but declined to 17.80 ± 0.95 mS/cm in Ch-Urea. These results indicate that the identity of the HBD is the principal determinant of the physicochemical environment within the DES system. The strongly acidic medium of Ch-OA (pH 0.52), together with its very high viscosity (543 mPa·s), most likely promoted partial hydrolysis of glycosidic linkages while restricting solvent penetration into the plant tissue. This finding is consistent with that of a reported work in which highly viscous acidic DESs were shown to limit diffusion efficiency and compromise polysaccharide integrity [26]. The suboptimal performance of Ch-Urea, despite its moderate viscosity of 56 mPa·s, can be attributed to the low polarity of urea, which weakens the affinity of the solvent toward hydrophilic polysaccharide chains. By contrast, Ch-EG had low viscosity (25 mPa·s) and moderate conductivity (38.5 mS/cm), and these features together favored efficient solvent penetration and rapid intracellular mass transfer. The higher polarity of ethylene glycol also matched the intrinsic hydrophilicity of polysaccharides, while its mildly acidic pH (4.85) was sufficient to solubilize pectic polysaccharides without inducing glycosidic bond cleavage [27], [28].

The 1:3 stoichiometry shared by the four candidates follows published screenings of Ch-EG, in which biomacromolecule recovery rose and then fell as the proportion of ethylene glycol increased and peaked at this value. Once association between HBA and HBD approaches saturation, a further gain in the donor fraction raises viscosity and lowers fluidity, which hinders dissolution of the target solutes. Because the molar ratio shapes polarity, hydrogen-bonding capacity, and viscosity in a nonlinear and matrix-dependent way, this variable was held constant so that the contribution of the HBD could be isolated [29], [30]. Ch-EG (ChCl:ethylene glycol = 1:3 supplemented with 30% water) was accordingly selected for all subsequent experiments.

Optimization of UDE conditions through RSM and ANN modeling

Reliable optimization of extraction parameters is essential for translating laboratory-scale performance into reproducible industrial yields. The combined use of RSM and ANN modeling has emerged as a strong approach for understanding both linear and nonlinear interfactor relationships that characterize polysaccharide extraction systems [31], [32]. To exploit this methodological complementarity, a five-factor Box–Behnken design with three levels was constructed on the basis of the results of preliminary single-factor screening (Supplementary Fig. S1). A total of 46 experimental runs were performed, and the resulting response data were fitted to the following second-order polynomial expression (Supplementary Table S5):Yield (%) = 18.1–1.23*A + 0.6113*B-0.0881*C + 0.3787*D + 0.4862*E + 1.45*AB-0.08*AC-0.395*AD + 0.8175*AE + 0.02*BC + 0.3025*BD-0.9125*BE + 0.44*CD-0.9475*CE-0.4475*DE-4.11*A2-5.35*B2-3.4*C2-2.46*D2-2.15*E2

Factors A to E represent the DES water content, extraction time, ultrasonication power, temperature, and LSR, respectively. The regression model indicated highly significant predictive ability (p < 0.0001), with an F value of 17.81, while the lack-of-fit test was statistically nonsignificant (p = 0.0558, F = 4.32). The coefficient of determination reached R2 = 0.9344, indicating that 93.44% of the response variability was successfully captured (Supplementary Table S6). As shown in the three-dimensional response surfaces along with the corresponding two-dimensional contour projections (Fig. 2A), pronounced curvature and clearly elliptical patterns were observed, both of which are diagnostic of strong pairwise interactions. The most prominent interaction arose between DES water content and extraction time (coefficient = 1.45), and water content also carried the second largest negative quadratic coefficient (A2 = −4.11). This dual role agrees with the behavior of hydrophilic eutectic media, in which hydration to 20% to 30% (w/w) lowers viscosity by two to five times and thereby favors mass transfer, whereas larger water fractions weaken the hydrogen bond network and diminish the recovery of plant metabolites [33]. These results highlight the complementary strengths of the two modeling paradigms. The RSM provides a transparent quadratic representation of factor effects and clearly identifies significant interactions, whereas the ANN, through its hierarchical architecture, captures higher-order nonlinearities arising from the cooperative behavior of ultrasound, DES, temperature, and LSR in an integrated extraction system. This observation is in line with recent reports showing that hybrid RSM–ANN strategies consistently outperform either method used independently for polysaccharide recovery from complex botanical matrices [34]. The convergence of the two models toward closely overlapping parameter windows further reinforces the robustness of the identified optimum and reduces the overfitting risk inherent in single-model optimization. The ANN-derived combination of 31% water, 49 min, 400 W, 50 °C, and 40 mL/g was therefore adopted as the optimal UDE protocol for all further investigations.

Comparative analysis of GP structure, composition, and properties

Extraction yield and chemical composition

Elevated temperature, alkaline media, enzymatic catalysis, and DESs can mobilize polysaccharides from plant cell walls, and as a consequence, both the yield and chemical composition of the resulting extracts are strongly dictated by the underlying extraction mechanism, which in turn governs the bioactivity of the recovered macromolecules [35]. To clarify which strategy most effectively disrupted the cell wall matrix of G. uralensis without compromising chain integrity, the eight extraction methods were subjected to a systematic comparative evaluation.

As shown in Table 3, the extraction yields followed the order of UDE (18.56% ± 0.74%) > DAE (14.33% ± 0.80%) > AAE (14.16% ± 1.08%) > UAE (11.74% ± 0.31%) > HWE (10.84% ± 0.89%) > MDE (8.28% ± 0.44%) > EAE (5.83% ± 0.06%) > MAE (4.76% ± 0.43%). Compared with their aqueous counterparts (UAE, HWE, and MAE), the three DES-based protocols (UDE, DAE, and MDE) consistently afforded higher polysaccharide yields, highlighting the intrinsic mass-transfer advantage of the eutectic medium. A recent side-by-side comparison of enzymatic, ultrasonic and DES-based extraction from a cereal matrix likewise showed that the choice of technique markedly changes both the polysaccharide yield and the profile of coextracted metabolites [36]. Notably, UDE simultaneously resulted in the highest total polysaccharide content (67.46% ± 2.48%) and the highest uronic acid content (30.68% ± 1.84%), whereas AAE, despite affording only a moderate yield (14.16%), resulted in a comparably elevated uronic acid content of 23.82% ± 1.99%. As each extraction method disrupts the cell wall through a chemically or physically distinct mechanism, the observed yield ranking can be interpreted in mechanistic terms. The superior performance of UDE results from the synergy of two complementary effects: the porous supramolecular network assembled through cooperative hydrogen bonding within the DES facilitates solute infiltration into the intracellular space, and concurrent ultrasonic cavitation disrupts the cell wall architecture and accelerates outward mass transfer [37]. The relatively high yield of AAE might result from the alkaline cleavage of rhamnogalacturonan I (RG-I) pectin via de-esterification and β-elimination, which together release pectic polysaccharides from their wall-bound state [38]. By contrast, the lower yields of MAE and MDE are consistent with the localized overheating that accompanies microwave irradiation, as this overheating drives thermal degradation of pectic polysaccharides and is especially detrimental to uronic acid residues. Given that uronic acid content strongly influences polysaccharide bioactivity, this thermal effect is an important consideration for food and pharmaceutical applications.

SEM analysis

The microstructure of polysaccharide extracts represents the mechanism of cell wall disruption and influences solubility, bioavailability, and downstream functional performance. Accordingly, SEM analysis was employed to visualize how each extraction method shaped the surface architecture of the GP samples. All eight GP samples displayed irregular lamellar structures (Fig. 3), but the surface morphology varied with the extraction method. Polysaccharides obtained using aqueous solvents (HWE, EAE, MAE, and UAE) generally showed densely packed, smooth, and layered surfaces, indicating a strongly cross-linked and aggregated state. HWE retained a relatively intact surface with only minor microcracks, whereas AAE produced fractured surfaces that contained both smooth and rough regions. The DES-extracted samples (DAE, MDE, and UDE) exhibited densely packed yet porous structures, and among them, UDE displayed the most extensively fragmented and porous architecture, with numerous small particles, cavities, and rough surfaces distributed throughout the field of view.Fig. 3Polysaccharide structural feature analysis by SEM. These morphological differences are linked to the underlying extraction mechanisms. The limited surface disruption observed with HWE is also observed with thermal extraction, which mainly depends on enhanced molecular diffusion and results in slight mechanical disruption of the cell wall [39]. The fractured surfaces of AAE are a result of the alkaline cleavage of ester linkages, whereas the sponge-like architecture of UDE and MDE reflects the synergistic action of physical cavitation or microwave forces and DES chemistry, where both wall breakage and exposure of hydrophilic functional groups occur [40]. This porous architecture exposes additional reactive groups and is expected to enhance solubility and bioactivity, in agreement with reports that UDE yields polysaccharides with a more porous surface morphology and improved functional performance [41].

Mw determination

Congo red analysis

Congo red serves as a sensitive molecular probe for detecting triple-helical conformations in polysaccharide chains. After binding to polysaccharide chains, it produces a characteristic bathochromic (red) shift in the maximum absorption wavelength (λmax) under mild alkaline conditions, whereas disruption of the helical architecture unwinds the polysaccharide chains and reverses the spectral shift [56]. As illustrated in Fig. 6C, polysaccharides obtained by HWE, DAE, AAE, EAE, MDE, UDE, and UAE all exhibited pronounced Congo red binding accompanied by significant redshifts under alkaline conditions, indicating that the triple-helical conformation was largely preserved under these seven extraction methods. By contrast, MAE-derived polysaccharides showed no appreciable bathochromic shift, suggesting that the triple-helical structure was disrupted during microwave-assisted extraction. The marked decrease in λmax for MAE polysaccharides further revealed that rapid localized microwave heating disrupts intermolecular hydrogen bonding and destabilizes the ordered helical arrangement. UDE retained a clear and monotonic redshift, demonstrating that the synergistic combination of ultrasonic cavitation with DES not only improved the extraction efficiency but also preserved the higher-order conformation of the polysaccharides.

Thermal stability analysis

Thermal stability is vital in determining the applications of polysaccharides, since processes such as microencapsulation, spray drying, and emulsion stabilization expose the molecules to elevated temperatures. All eight GP samples displayed an endothermic transition between 88 and 105 °C (Fig. 6D), which corresponded to the loss of free and bound water. A second exothermic peak appeared between 210 and 310 °C, with peak temperatures of 215.3, 303.0, 255.2, 267.6, 273.4, 250.4, 306.4, and 298.5 °C for HWE, DAE, AAE, EAE, MDE, MAE, UDE, and UAE, respectively, with associated enthalpy changes of 86.5, 83.2, 95.4, 93.7, 92.2, 1.9, 101.8, and 104.3 J/g, respectively. DES-assisted methods (DAE, UDE, and MDE) resulted in smoother DTG profiles with sharper peaks, whereas MAE resulted in the lowest thermal stability, with an enthalpy change of only 1.9 J/g. Among all eight fractions, UDE had the highest initial decomposition temperature of 306.4 °C. On the basis of thermal performance, the thermal stability of the eight fractions ranked in the order UDE > DAE > UAE > MDE > EAE > AAE > MAE > HWE.

The sharp DTG peaks of the DES-assisted fractions reflect a unimodal Mw distribution and a higher degree of structural homogeneity, both of which increase the activation energy required for thermal decomposition. The low enthalpy change of MAE confirmed that rapid microwave heating substantially degraded the polysaccharide chains, whereas the comparable enthalpy change of MDE (92.2 J/g) showed that the DES protected the structure during microwave-assisted extraction [57]. The outstanding thermal stability of UDE probably arises from its lower Mw combined with a high GalA content, which together support a tightly packed and conformationally ordered molecular architecture. This reading agrees with reports that uronic acid-rich polysaccharides gain thermal stability through cooperative hydrogen bonding among carboxyl groups [58], [59]. Such performance makes UDE well suited to thermally demanding uses such as microencapsulation and emulsion stabilization.

Functional analysis of GP samples

Analysis of solubility and stability during storage

Aqueous solubility together with storage stability govern the practical utility of polysaccharide extracts as functional food ingredients or pharmaceutical excipients, since changes in color, turbidity, or homogeneity directly affect product quality and shelf life [60]. The high solubility of UDE is supported by its porous microstructure, high GalA content, and lower Mw, which together promote water‒polysaccharide interactions and limit chain entanglement. To assess the long-term stability of the eight GP fractions, the appearance of each solution was monitored at ambient temperature, 4 °C, and − 20 °C for 7, 14, and 21 days (Fig. 6E). At ambient temperature and 4 °C, all eight GP solutions remained homogeneous and free of visible precipitation or phase separation throughout the 21-day period. Slight yellowing was observed in the HWE and MDE fractions after 14 days, which was likely caused by the gradual oxidation of coextracted phenolic residues in these solutions. By contrast, the UDE, UAE, and DAE fractions retained their initial clarity and color under both storage conditions. pH, zeta potential and particle size analysis

The zeta potentials of all eight samples were negative, as expected for natural polysaccharides that bear carboxyl groups (Table 5). Fractions recovered with microwave or ultrasonic assistance carried appreciably more negative surface charge (MAE – 20.34 mV; MDE – 19.17 mV; UDE – 16.00 mV; UAE – 15.57 mV) than those obtained by conventional heating (HWE – 10.14 mV; DAE – 10.70 mV), which indicates that cavitation and microwave irradiation opened the cell wall more thoroughly and exposed additional anionic sites. Since carboxyl dissociation depends on the acidity of the medium, the pH of every sample was also determined. The eight values spanned 4.53 ± 0.07 to 7.70 ± 0.11 and ranked as MDE > EAE > UAE > MAE > HWE > DAE > AAE > UDE, an order that inverted the uronic acid ranking without exception (Spearman ρ = −1.000; Pearson r = −0.95, p < 0.001).

UDE, which held the highest uronic acid content (30.68%), gave the most acidic dispersion (pH 4.53). This acidity arises from proton release by galacturonic acid carboxyl groups once the sample is redispersed in deionized water [62]. A higher carboxylate density normally favors a more negative zeta potential, yet UDE measured only −16.00 mV. The apparent contradiction is attributed to the self-generated acidity: as charge density along the chain increases, the apparent pKa rises, and the counterions released at the same time compress the electrical double layer and thereby attenuate the measured potential [63]. Near neutrality, ionization becomes more complete and weakly acidic biopolymers acquire markedly more negative zeta potentials [64]. MDE and MAE, which lay closer to neutrality at pH 7.70 and 6.83, accordingly reached higher absolute values despite carrying far fewer acidic residues.

Particle size differed markedly among the methods and decreased in the order HWE > DAE > EAE > AAE > UAE > UDE > MDE > MAE, from 903.1 ± 92.30 nm to 258.8 ± 4.10 nm (Table 5). HWE and DAE gave the coarsest particles, consistent with thermal extraction that relies on molecular diffusion rather than on physical disruption of intermolecular interactions, an effect reinforced by their elevated coextracted protein content [65], [66]. Microwave irradiation produced the smallest particles in MAE and MDE by breaking up polysaccharide clusters, whereas the modest size of AAE presumably arose from alkaline cleavage of hydrogen bonds and ionic interactions between chains [67]. Cavitation-induced shear held UAE and UDE near 300 nm, close to the values of MAE and MDE.

The magnitude of the UDE zeta potential (16.0 mV) lies below the ± 30 mV threshold conventionally linked to purely electrostatic stabilisation, so repulsion between charged surfaces alone cannot account for the behavior of this system [68]. The hydrophilic RG-I side chains rich in Ara and Gal supply an additional steric and hydration barrier, and this electrosteric mechanism, together with the compact hydrodynamic diameter, keeps the particles apart [69]. The homogeneous appearance that the UDE solution retained throughout 21 days of storage supports this interpretation (Fig. 6E). UDE thus united the richest uronic acid content with a fine and lasting dispersion, a profile well suited to biopharmaceutical use, where colloidal stability and nanoscale dimensions are prerequisites for efficient drug delivery and improved bioavailability [70].

Analysis of Rheological properties

Rheological behavior reveals the molecular conformation, intermolecular interactions, and processability of polysaccharide solutions, which together determine performance in applications such as food gels, drug delivery systems, and biomaterials. Steady-state flow curves along with the dynamic viscoelastic properties of the eight GP fractions were measured at 1.0% (w/v). All eight GP solutions exhibited clear pseudoplastic behavior across the shear rate range (Fig. 7A–D), with the apparent viscosity decreasing by two to three orders of magnitude as the shear rate increased. At the highest shear rate, all eight fractions converged to similar viscosities of 2.0 to 4.3 mPa·s, with UDE showing the lowest terminal viscosity of 2.04 mPa·s. Such rheological data point to fundamentally different chain organizations across the eight extracts. The pronounced shear thinning observed in all samples characterizes the progressive disruption and shear-induced alignment of random-coil chains together with intermolecular entanglements. The exceptionally high η0.1 of HWE is consistent with its high Mw of 188.80 × 104 Da, since zero-shear viscosity scales with Mw through the power-law relationship η0 ∝ Mwα (α ≈ 3.4 for entangled polymer solutions). The convergence at high shear rate together with the low terminal viscosity of UDE indicates efficient chain alignment, a property that benefits processing in spray drying, injection molding, and coating. The elastic dominance of G' over G“ across all fractions confirms that the GP solutions behave as weak gels.

The high G′ of UDE, despite its lower Mw and low η0.1, indicates an elastic gel network built on dense intermolecular junction zones rather than on chain entanglements. Steady-shear viscosity at a low shear rate is governed by chain length and entanglement density, whereas the dynamic elastic modulus represents the capacity of the system to store elastic energy under small-amplitude oscillation. This capacity is determined by the density and strength of intermolecular junctions, including hydrogen bonds, electrostatic interactions among carboxyl groups, and ordered conformational elements. UDE combines a lower Mw with a wide and continuous chain length range, the highest GalA content, the most diverse monosaccharide composition, and a well-preserved triple-helical conformation, which support a strong elastic network with relatively few entanglements. This interpretation is consistent with those of studies showing that uronic acid-rich pectic polysaccharides form robust gel networks through cooperative hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions among carboxylate groups [71], [72]. The combination of a high elastic modulus with low flow resistance in UDE indicates a structurally complex, pectin-enriched, and conformationally ordered molecular architecture, which makes it a versatile biopolymer for food, pharmaceutical, and biomaterial applications.

Analysis of the DES extraction mechanism

These data confirm that Ch-EG forms multipoint hydrogen bonds with the hydroxyl and carboxyl groups of monosaccharides and builds a cooperative solvation environment far stronger than water alone provides. The larger interaction energies and BSSE corrections of the DES systems further indicate more extensive intermolecular contact. Molecular dynamics simulations of polysaccharides dissolved in a choline chloride and ethylene glycol medium support this picture, since the choline component dominated the electrostatic contribution while the glycol acted as a secondary solvating agent [74]. Greater electrostatic complementarity between the DES and the monosaccharides likewise favors stronger noncovalent binding, which promotes disruption of polysaccharide-polysaccharide associations in the cell wall [75]. The marked preference of Ch-EG for galactose is consistent with the high galactose and GalA contents of UDE and supplies a molecular basis for the preferential extraction of pectic polysaccharides.

Frontier molecular orbital analysis

A smaller HOMO‒LUMO energy gap indicates greater chemical reactivity of an intermolecular complex, which enables more facile electron transfer and stronger noncovalent interactions. The HOMO–LUMO energy gaps for the DES and monosaccharide complexes (7.24 eV for DES-Glc, 8.00 eV for DES-Gal, and 7.75 eV for DES-Ara) were consistently smaller than those for the corresponding water complexes (8.00 eV for Water-Glc, 8.14 eV for Water-Gal, and 9.32 eV for Water-Ara) (Fig. 8C1–D6). The narrower HOMO–LUMO gaps in the DES complexes indicate greater chemical reactivity and more favorable charge transfer between DES and saccharide units. These electronic features are fully consistent with the interaction energy and ESP results and help explain why the multipoint hydrogen bonding among Ch, ethylene glycol, and saccharide hydroxyl groups creates a cooperative solvation environment that disrupts polysaccharide–polysaccharide associations more effectively than the simple hydrogen-bonding network of water does. These findings are consistent with those of computational investigations showing that DESs with strong hydrogen bond donor capacity preferentially solvate polysaccharide hydroxyl networks [76].

Noncovalent interaction analysis via IGMH mapping

Complementary IGMH mapping was subsequently employed to visualize and assess the spatial distribution along with the relative magnitude of intermolecular forces (Fig. 8E). Within the IGMH isosurface representations, the blue, green, and red regions denote hydrogen bonding interactions, van der Waals contacts, and steric repulsion effects, respectively. The IGMH isosurface maps of DES-monosaccharide complexes displayed extensive blue–green regions, indicating the coexistence of strong hydrogen bonds with widespread van der Waals interactions. By contrast, the water–monosaccharide complexes exhibited substantially smaller interaction surfaces, reflecting more limited noncovalent binding capacity. Quantitative comparison of electron density values at critical bond points confirmed consistently higher values in the DES – monosaccharide complexes. The corresponding scatter plots of the sign(λ2)ρ function against the reduced density gradient further supported the conclusion that the DES systems displayed larger populations of interaction points in the attractive region (Fig. 8F).

These IGMH results confirm that Ch-EG engages each saccharide unit through both strong hydrogen bonding and extensive van der Waals interactions. The larger attractive interaction surfaces of the DES systems indicate broader and more cooperative solvation, in agreement with the computational evidence supporting the use of DESs as tailored solvents for biomolecule extraction [77]. Together, the results of the geometry, energy, frontier orbital, and IGMH analyses provide a coherent molecular rationale for the experimentally superior polysaccharide extraction performance of the Ch-EG system.

Recycling performance of DESs

The ability to recover and reuse DESs reduces solvent consumption and aligns with green chemistry principles, which position recyclability as a prerequisite for industrial adoption. The extraction yield remained stable over the first three cycles (18.56% ± 0.74% vs. 16.83% ± 0.64% in cycles 1 and 3, respectively), but dropped to 12.30% ± 0.67% and 10.18% ± 0.53% in the fourth and fifth cycles (Supplementary Fig. S2). Stability over three cycles shows that the Ch-EG system retains effective extraction performance under repeated use, which supports its practical applicability. Recent studies have demonstrated that no compositional change occurs in hydrophilic choline chloride‑based solvents even under prolonged sonication at elevated temperature [33], confirming the resistance of the eutectic medium to the applied cavitation field.

The decline from the fourth cycle onward was most likely caused by gradual accumulation of residual oligosaccharides, pigments, and phenolic impurities in the recycled solvent, where competition for hydrogen-bonding sites lowered extraction efficiency, together with partial loss of choline chloride or ethylene glycol during evaporation. However, a clear limitation of this study is the absence of compositional characterization (viscosity, HBA/HBD ratio) for the recycled DES, leaving these proposed mechanisms unverified. Future work will track these parameters over successive cycles and incorporate an adsorptive purification step, aiming to identify the primary cause of performance decay and enhance operational durability.

Even after four reuse cycles, the yield remained at a practically useful level (12.30%). Together with the low toxicity and biodegradability of Ch‑EG, these results underline the environmental sustainability and industrial scalability of the UDE strategy [78].

Antioxidant activity determination

Elevated ROS levels contribute to numerous inflammatory and metabolic disorders [79], [80], and the capacity of plant polysaccharides to scavenge ROS is regarded as one of their most important bioactivities. Accordingly, we assessed this capacity in the eight polysaccharide samples, all of which exhibited dose‑dependent radical scavenging activity and concentration‑dependent reducing power (Fig. 9B–F). UDE showed the strongest scavenging activity for DPPH· and ·OH radicals among the eight fractions. The scavenging rates of UDE at 2 and 4 mg/mL were equal to or exceeded that of vitamin C (VC), with a DPPH· scavenging activity of 95.02% ± 1.00% with 2 mg/mL UDE and 94.64% ± 1.38% with 2 mg/mL VC and a ·OH scavenging activity of 97.01% ± 0.21% with 4 mg/mL UDE and 96.05% ± 0.62% with 4 mg/mL VC, respectively. Compared with the other methods, HWE resulted in consistently lower scavenging activity at the same concentrations. In the ABTS assay, the scavenging rate of UDE at 8 mg/mL (100.3%) markedly exceeded those of MAE, AAE, MDE, and UAE. With respect to superoxide anion radical scavenging, UDE achieved 70.2% inhibition, which was the highest among the eight fractions. The strong antioxidant performance of UDE is supported by its lower Mw, broad and continuous chain length range, and high contents of GalA, Rha, Gal, and Ara. A lower Mw results in the exposure of additional hydroxyl groups for radical scavenging, whereas uronic acid- and rhamnose-rich side chains contribute to metal chelation and electron donation. The reduced antioxidant capacity of HWE is correlated with its high Mw and with the partial degradation of thermolabile coextracted antioxidants under prolonged heat exposure [81], [82]. These results suggest that compared with conventional methods, the UDE method couples the ultrasonic effects and the hydrogen-bonding properties of DESs to promote controlled polysaccharide chain cleavage and structural modification, enhancing the antioxidant activity of the extracts. These enhanced antioxidant properties position UDE-derived extracts as promising ingredients for functional foods, nutraceuticals, and antioxidant formulations, highlighting their potential for developing standardized, high-efficacy plant-based health products.

Anti-inflammatory activity of UDE in IL-13-stimulated BEAS-2B cells

Type 2 airway inflammation is the central driver of allergic asthma, with IL-13 acting as a key cytokine that activates the STAT6 pathway and triggers the production of CCL26 along with downstream proinflammatory mediators [83]. With the aim of translating the structural and antioxidant advantages of UDE into a biomedically relevant context, an IL-13-stimulated BEAS-2B human bronchial epithelial cell model was used to evaluate the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities of the extract.

Cytotoxicity and dose‒response evaluation of UDE

Initial cytotoxicity screening revealed that all eight GP fractions maintained cell viability at greater than 95% at 10 µg/mL, confirming the absence of detectable cytotoxicity (Fig. 10A). UDE gave the highest cell viability (117.73% ± 2.85%), and no significant reduction in viability was observed at concentrations up to 160 µg/mL, as determined through dose‒response analysis (Fig. 10B). These results indicate that UDE is biocompatible across a wide concentration range. Inhibition of IL-13-induced nitric oxide production by UDE

Prior to evaluating the anti-inflammatory activity of UDE, an IL-13-stimulated BEAS-2B cell model was established. Given that NO production captures the degree of IL-13-induced inflammatory activation through iNOS-mediated signaling [84], NO levels were monitored at multiple time points to determine the optimal stimulation duration. The optimal duration of IL-13 stimulation was determined by treating BEAS-2B human bronchial epithelial cells with IL-13 and measuring NO production at 12, 24, and 48 h posttreatment (Fig. 10C). IL-13 increased NO levels at all time points compared with the normal controls. As NO concentrations at 24 h (5.82 ± 0.02 µM/mg) and 48 h (5.81 ± 0.16 µM/mg) were similar, the 24 h time point was selected for subsequent experiments. UDE at 5, 10, and 20 µg/mL then attenuated NO production in a dose-dependent manner relative to the IL-13 model group, and the strongest inhibition appeared after 6 h of treatment (Fig. 10D). Dexamethasone at 5 µM, applied on the same schedule, served as the positive control and lowered NO release to a similar degree at 6 h.

Dose-dependent NO suppression by UDE indicates effective inhibition of the early iNOS upregulation that drives type 2 inflammation. The strongest effect was observed at 6 h, which aligns with the kinetics of iNOS expression and is consistent with reports that polysaccharides act on the early phase of cytokine-induced inflammatory signaling in bronchial epithelial cells [85]. The comparable activity of UDE at 20 µg/mL to that of dexamethasone highlights the therapeutic potential of polysaccharide-based anti-inflammatory agents.

Restoration of antioxidant enzyme activities and reduction of oxidative stress

IL-13 activates the JAK/STAT6 signaling pathway, which transcriptionally upregulates NADPH oxidase expression and drives excess ROS production; moreover, it suppresses the expression and activity of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD and CAT, thereby disrupting the oxidant–antioxidant balance and inducing oxidative stress [86]. UDE treatment attenuated IL-13-induced oxidative stress in BEAS-2B cells (Fig. 10E). Compared with the control treatment, IL-13 stimulation suppressed SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px activities and promoted a 2.35-fold increase in the MDA content. UDE at 5, 10, and 20 µg/mL progressively reduced these effects caused by IL-13, and 20 µg/mL UDE restored the activity of antioxidant enzymes and the MDA content to levels similar to those of the untreated control. These data confirm that UDE restores the intracellular antioxidant defense system and limits lipid peroxidation under type 2 inflammatory stimulation.

Transcriptional suppression of type 2 inflammatory mediators via the STAT6/MYD88 axis

STAT6 and MYD88 are central transcription factors involved in type 2 airway inflammation, whereas CCL26 (eotaxin-3) is a potent eosinophil chemoattractant that signals through CCR3 [87]. qRT‒PCR analysis revealed the transcriptional regulation of key inflammatory mediators by UDE (Fig. 10F). IL-13 stimulation markedly upregulated the transcription of STAT6, MYD88, IL-5, CCL26, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and VEGFC in BEAS-2B cells, confirming the successful establishment of a type 2 inflammatory phenotype. Among these mediators, CCL26 expression was the most strongly induced by IL-13 treatment. Treatment with 5, 10, and 20 µg/mL UDE significantly suppressed the expression of all eight genes induced by IL-13 in a dose-dependent manner.

These findings suggest that UDE strongly suppresses the transcription of type 2 inflammatory mediators, most likely through coordinated inhibition of the STAT6/MYD88 signaling axis. STAT6 is canonically activated by IL-13 and IL-4 via JAK kinases and serves as a master regulator of Th2-driven airway inflammation. The downregulation of STAT6 by UDE implies interference with upstream receptor-mediated signaling. The concurrent suppression of the expression of MYD88, a key adaptor protein in the Toll-like receptor (TLR) and IL-1 receptor signaling pathways, further suggests that UDE may attenuate both innate and adaptive inflammatory cascades. A significant reduction in CCL26 expression is particularly important for asthma, allergic rhinitis, and chronic rhinosinusitis, as CCL26–CCR3 signaling is a primary driver of the recruitment of eosinophils to the airway epithelium, a hallmark of type 2 airway inflammation [88]. In addition, the downregulation of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β suggests that UDE synchronously dampens classical proinflammatory NF-κB-associated cytokine responses, thereby exerting a dual modulatory effect on both type 2 and type 1 inflammatory pathways. The inhibition of VEGFC further reveals a potential role in attenuating IL-13-driven lymphangiogenesis and vascular remodeling in the airway. Overall, the dose-dependent transcriptional suppression of these eight mediators demonstrated that UDE targets multiple nodes within the type 2 inflammatory network rather than a single pathway, thereby contributing to its broad anti-inflammatory efficacy in vitro.

Correlation analysis of the eight GP samples

Correlation analysis through the Mantel test was performed to clarify the structure–activity relationships across the eight GP fractions and to provide a quantitative basis for the molecular features that drive the superior bioactivity of UDE. The four radical scavenging activities showed strong mutual associations, indicating a shared mechanistic basis (Fig. 11A). Rha was strongly positively correlated with Gal and Ara (p < 0.001), and these three monosaccharides together with GalA formed a close cluster, in agreement with the characteristic composition of pectic polysaccharide domains. Glc was negatively associated with Rha, Ara, and Gal (p < 0.001), in accordance with the structural separation between glucan-type and pectin-type fractions. These correlations could explain the superior antioxidant performance of UDE at the molecular level. The high GalA content; diverse monosaccharide composition enriched in Rha, Gal, and Ara; lower Mw; and ordered helical conformation of UDE create a molecular architecture that is conducive to radical scavenging [90]. The negative correlation between Mw and antioxidants indicates that controlled chain cleavage by ultrasound, combined with the selective extraction of pectic polysaccharides by DESs, is essential for generating extracts with strong bioactivity. The Ch-EG system interacts with polysaccharide hydroxyl groups through multivalent hydrogen bonds and extensive van der Waals interactions and forms a cooperative solvation shell that replaces the native hydrogen-bonding network of the cell wall matrix (Fig. 11B). By providing additional hydrogen-bonding sites through their carboxyl groups, pectin enable a dual-interaction mechanism that enhances polysaccharide solubilization and selectively enriches uronic acid-rich fractions. The structure–activity framework therefore supports the conclusion that UDE preferentially recovers uronic acid-rich polysaccharides of moderate chain length, yielding extracts with excellent structural, functional, and biological performance.

Conclusion

In this study, we established a green, high-efficient, and scalable UDE-mediated platform for extracting highly bioactive G. uralensis polysaccharides. The ultrasound-mediated recyclable and biocompatible Ch-EG DES system can selectively modulate the polysaccharide architecture toward lower molecular weight, higher galacturonic acid content, and smaller particle size. This tunable approach can be extended to the polysaccharide extraction from other plants. Functionally, the UDE-extracted polysaccharides suppressed IL-13-induced inflammation via the STAT6/MYD88 axis, identifying them as potential candidates for the treatment of type 2 inflammatory disorders. Future studies could focus on validating the in vivo efficacy and dose–response of GPs efficacy and dose–response, compositional monitoring of the recycled DES to define its practical service life, clarification of the therapeutic exploitation of these structural advantages, and integration of green extraction design with polysaccharide-based functional foods and therapeutics.
