Intraoperative continuous noninvasive hemoglobin monitoring in patients undergoing thoracic surgery
Authors: Mona Mohamed Mogahed, Taha Saad Alnoamani, Mohamed Shafik Elkahwagy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aims to assess the agreement between noninvasive hemoglobin (SpHb) and invasive hemoglobin (InvHb) monitoring in guiding blood transfusions during thoracic procedures.MethodsThis study was not interventional but assessed the agreement between SpHb and InvHb monitoring in guiding blood transfusions during thoracic procedures. Data were collected from 80 patients ≥ 18 years older who were candidates for thoracic surgeries. Continuous SpHb monitoring was conducted. Simultaneous (InvHb) samples were collected for validation at predefined perioperative intervals: preoperative baseline, immediately after induction, 1, 2, 3, and 4 h after incision, at the end of surgery, and in the recovery room. Agreement was assessed using Bland–Altman analysis, and correlation was evaluated using Pearson coefficients. The primary outcome measure was the correlation between SpHb and InvHb.ResultsThere was a significant positive correlation between SpHb and InvHb at preoperative period, immediately after induction of anesthesia, 1 h, 2 h, 3 h, 4 h after start of surgery, at the end of surgery and after recovery of the patient (Pearson’s r = 0.949772, 0.940262, 0.915292, 0.981438, 0.989623, 0.984838, 0.965235 and 0.933264). Bland–Altman analysis between SpHb and InvHb demonstrated low bias with good limits of agreement at the same interval points [Mean bias (limits of agreement): 0.56 (-0.3 to 1.4), 0.54 (-0.38 to 1.5), 0.67 (-0.37 to 1.7), 0.52 (-0.14 to 1.8), 0.48 (0.12 to 1.1), 0.6 (-0.001 to 1.2) g/dl].ConclusionIn patients undergoing thoracic surgery, there is a strong correlation and acceptable agreement between SpHb monitoring using Masimo pulse co-oximetry and invasive hemoglobin measurements.Keywords: Thoracic, Surgery, Blood loss, Hemoglobin, Oximetry

Background

Lung surgeries are considered moderate to high-risk, with significant potential for blood loss depending on pathology and surgical type, accurate assessment of hemoglobin concentration is essential during thoracic surgery [1]. Advances in medical technology have reduced invasiveness in both diagnostic and therapeutic procedures, with hemoglobin monitoring remaining a key test for assessing oxygen delivery [2]. Traditional invasive measurements, though accurate, are costly, time-consuming, and expose patients to biohazards, delaying care through phlebotomy and laboratory processing [3].

Recently, non-invasive hemoglobin monitoring has gained popularity, offering continuous, real-time assessment of hemoglobin concentration [2–4]. This approach helps prevent complications from over- or under-transfusion [2]. Devices developed by Masimo Corporation, such as the Radical-7 Pulse CO-Oximeter, use multi-wavelength light (500–1,300 nm) to calculate hemoglobin concentration via proprietary algorithms [5, 6]. By 2008, the technology expanded to measure total hemoglobin, with Radical-7 for bedside monitoring and Pronto-7 for portable spot checks [7]. Ongoing software and probe improvements aim to enhance accuracy, using fingertip sensors similar to pulse oximeters.

Non-invasive hemoglobin monitoring has demonstrated utility across diverse clinical settings [8–12] Although correlation between the two methods is often strong, correlation alone does not establish interchangeability. Bland–Altman analysis is required to determine whether measurement differences fall within clinically acceptable limits. This study is the first to assess the agreement between SpHb and InvHb in patients undergoing thoracic surgery and examines whether SpHb provides clinically acceptable accuracy for perioperative monitoring.

Methods

Study design and setting

After obtaining Institutional Review Board approval (A01890) by the Institutional Review Board of Ministry of Health, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia and clinical trial registration NCT07135492.

Participants

Eighty adult patients scheduled for elective thoracic surgery at King Fahd General Hospital Jeddah between July 2024 and July 2025 were included.

Inclusion criteria

Patients aged 18 years and older who were subjected to open thoracic surgery the length of surgery was ≥ 4 h were included to compare simultaneously collected SpHb values and InvHb obtained at predefined perioperative intervals: preoperative baseline, immediately after induction, 1, 2, 3, and 4 h after incision, at the end of surgery, and in the recovery room.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with known dysrhythmia, intracardiac shunts, significant coagulopathy, left or right ventricular dysfunction, significant hepatic dysfunction (ALT and AST > 3 times of the normal) and significant renal disorders (creatinine clearance < 40 mL/min and/or serum creatinine > 1.5 mg/dL). All patients were monitored using ASA standard monitors, along with invasive arterial and/or central venous monitoring as part of their standard care.

Demographic data (age, gender, ASA physical status) and surgical variables (type of surgery, duration of the procedure, estimated blood loss, total volume of IV fluids administered, and total volume of packed red blood cells transfused) were recorded.

Monitoring and measurements

For noninvasive hemoglobin monitoring, Rainbow adult ReSposable™ sensors (rev E) were attached to the ring or middle fingertip of each subject and connected to a Radical-7 Pulse CO-Oximeter (SET version 7601; Masimo Corporation, Irvine, CA). The sensors were covered with black plastic shields to prevent optical interference.

The Pulse CO-Oximeter was linked to a laptop running automated data collection software (PhysioLog; Masimo Corporation) for the noninvasive recording of hemoglobin (SpHb) and signal quality indicator (SIQ), as well as to log the exact time of each blood draw for comparison with SpHb. The Radical-7 had a sampling frequency of 62.5 Hz.

During each surgery, blood samples were collected at preoperative period, immediately after induction of anesthesia, 1 h after start of surgery, 2 h after start of surgery, 3 h after start of surgery, 4 h after start of surgery, at the end of surgery and after recovery of the patient (or more frequently if clinically indicated) by withdrawing 2 mL of blood from an indwelling arterial catheter into heparinized blood gas syringes. The samples were analyzed for total hemoglobin (tHb) using a satellite blood analyzer (Gem Premier 4000).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). The mean and standard deviation (SD) presented quantitative variables and compared using student t-test. Qualitative variables were presented as number and percentage. Pearson correlation was done to check the relationship between two numerical variables. To evaluate agreement amongst approaches over the range of hemoglobin levels, modified Bland-Altman graphs with limits of agreement (defined as bias ± SD) precision (standard deviation, SD) were drawn. For the SpHb accuracy analysis, the Bland-Altman test limits of agreement (LOA). The bounds of agreement represent the range within which 95% of the differences between the two measurement methods are expected to fall. (LOA = bias ± 1.96 SD). A clinically acceptable difference was defined as ± 1 g/dL, based on published transfusion-threshold literature. P value less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

The sample size was calculated to detect a statistically significant correlation between noninvasive hemoglobin (SpHb) and invasive hemoglobin (Inv Hb) measurements across multiple intraoperative time points. An expected correlation coefficient of approximately 0.4 between SpHb and Inv Hb was assumed. The minimum required sample size was calculated to be 47 patients using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7) for a two-tailed Pearson correlation test, assuming an effect size of 0.4, α = 0.05, and power = 0.80. To enhance statistical robustness and account for potential exclusions, a larger sample of 80 patients was targeted and successfully analyzed.

Discussion

Advancements in technology allow clinicians to monitor hemoglobin levels continuously and without invasive methods [11]. Continuous and precise monitoring of blood loss during extended clinical procedures presents significant challenges for doctors. Intraoperative changes in SpHb reflected corresponding changes in invasive hemoglobin, supporting its usefulness for trend monitoring [13]. Noninvasive hemoglobin monitors provide real-time hemoglobin values without blood sampling, allowing continuous assessment of a key determinant of oxygen delivery. During anesthesia, hemoglobin levels can be monitored alongside vital signs like blood pressure and heart rate. This functionality helps clinicians identify concealed bleeding and prepare for necessary transfusions. Significant blood loss during surgery can severely affect oxygen delivery, underscoring the importance of such monitoring [14].

This study evaluated the accuracy and clinical applicability of continuous noninvasive hemoglobin monitoring during thoracic surgery. Although transfusion requirements in thoracic surgery are generally low, continuous SpHb monitoring may assist in early detection of hemoglobin decline during periods of physiological instability, guiding the timing of confirmatory laboratory testing rather than serving as a standalone transfusion trigger. The findings demonstrate that SpHb provides reliable trend information with strong correlation and acceptable agreement compared to invasive hemoglobin measurements. While correlation alone cannot determine interchangeability, Bland–Altman analysis showed low bias and clinically reasonable limits of agreement.

Several studies examined the relationship and accuracy of continuous non-invasive hemoglobin measurement techniques, as well as the performance of spot-check hemoglobin devices. The LOA in this study were similar to or narrower than those reported in previous studies. Although LOA occasionally exceeded ± 1 g/dL, the bias remained small, suggesting that SpHb is reliable for trend monitoring, though not a substitute for laboratory testing when precise hemoglobin values are required. The largest prospective study on trauma patients involved 525 individuals and 1,350 spot-check hemoglobin readings [3]. Results demonstrated a strong correlation (R² = 0.77) between spot-check and invasive hemoglobin measurements, maintaining reliability even in cases of bleeding, hypotension, or anemia (hemoglobin < 8.0 g/dL; R² = 0.71). However, in 14% of cases, spot-check measurements were not feasible due to issues like nail polish, soot, tar on fingers, and interference from radiological devices [3]. In another study, Shah et al. [15] compared spot-check hemoglobin results with venous blood samples analyzed in a laboratory. From a cohort of 440 outpatients, they noted minimal bias with a standard deviation of − 0.1 ± 1.1 g/dL. The findings indicated that spot-check non-invasive hemoglobin testing offers accuracy comparable to invasive methods, thus presenting an opportunity to enhance patient care efficiency. Adel et al. [16] validated our findings, highlighting a significant correlation between SpHb and Invasive Hb. Notably, even in cases of poor perfusion and low hemoglobin levels among patients undergoing major orthopedic surgeries with anticipated substantial blood loss, SpHb demonstrated a favorable mean bias of 0.01 g/dl. Gamal et al. [17] studied trauma patients with hemoglobin levels below 8 g/dL. Their findings indicated limits of agreement between − 0.56 g/dL and 0.79 g/dL, accompanied by a mean bias of 0.12 g/dL for these patients. Nakamori E et al. [18] demonstrated that postoperative SpHb monitoring can help prevent transient, undetected anemia and unnecessary allogeneic blood transfusions in recovery rooms. However, the authors were unable to conclusively determine its effectiveness in preventing anemia on the first postoperative day. Panda et al. [19] illustrated that SpHb can be effectively utilized in clinical settings, providing Invasive Hb values comparable to those seen in neonates through to young adults. Raikhel et al. [20] observed that patients involved in outpatient research demonstrated an SpHb bias of − 0.5 g/dL, with limits of agreement ranging from − 2.5 to 1.5 g/dL compared to Invasive Hb. The study concluded that SpHb provides additional advantages, such as improved patient comfort, enhanced safety, and reduced complexity for healthcare providers by minimizing risks of blood-spill contamination and needle-stick injuries. Miller et al. [21] conducted a study on 20 individuals undergoing prone spine surgery under GA, confirming a strong correlation between SpHb and InvHb values. However, their findings also highlighted that SpHb measurements in certain patients might not meet clinical reliability standards. This may be attributed to advancements in technology, with newer versions of sensors and devices being developed. As a result, further insights into the relationship between perfusion, acute Hb changes, and the effectiveness of this noninvasive sensor could be achieved. Dermaco et al. [22] included 13 patients, which is smaller than our sample of 80 participants they reported findings that contrasted with ours, indicating that SpHb failed to accurately measure blood loss during cesarean section (CS). Their study showed poor precision and the absence of trends when compared to Invasive Hb. Moreover, Murphy et al. [23] observed a significant bias in SpHb compared to Invasive Hb, reporting a mean bias of + 1.64 and limits of agreement ranging from − 1.03 to 4.31. The bias in SpHb increased as average hemoglobin levels rose, which may be attributed to the differences in the studied population, specifically dark-skinned critically ill patients. Additionally, Skelton et al. [24] demonstrated that the co-oximeter exhibited lower reliability than the laboratory reference measurements and was less sensitive in comparison to the HemoCue cuvette system. Gayat et al. [25] found that larger bias when hemoglobin monitoring devices from manufacturers other than Masimo were used, which may contribute to variability in accuracy. In contrast, our study used the Masimo Radical-7, allowing more direct comparison with studies employing the same technology. Butwick et al. [26] Studied 50 elective CS patients monitored over a 48-hour period. They reported a mean bias of 0.14 g/dL (95% CI: − 0.18 to 0.46), with LOA ranging from − 0.9 to 3.33, − 2.35 to 2.56, and − 0.55 to 3.27 g/dL, respectively. This discrepancy may be attributed to the earlier timing of their study in 2012, during which subsequent advancements introduced modifications in devices and novel sensor versions. Beleta et al. [27] concluded in their study that continuous SpHb Masimo pulse co-oximetry provides a clinically reliable Hb measurement compared to Invasive Hb, even in patients undergoing CS with low hemoglobin levels. In another study for Lamhaut et al. [28] involving patients undergoing potentially hemorrhagic major urologic surgery, the accuracy of the Radical-7 was compared to that of the HemoCue (cHb). The Radical-7 showed a lower correlation coefficient and wider limits of agreement (LOA) compared to the cHb. Moreover, the percentage of outliers (absolute bias > 1 g/dL) was significantly higher for the Radical-7 than for the cHb (46% vs. 16%, P < 0.05). As a result, the authors concluded that SpHb monitoring with the Radical-7 tends to underestimate hemoglobin concentration compared to the cHb during hemorrhagic surgeries. Overall, noninvasive continuous hemoglobin monitoring is less accurate in patients experiencing bleeding compared to healthy individuals. On the other hand, Kapoor et al. [29], who investigated 30 patients undergoing GA pituitary surgery, reported a weak correlation between Invasive Hb and SpHb, contrary to our findings. This discrepancy could be attributed to their smaller sample size, which may lack the sensitivity to detect minor differences. Also, Applegate et al. [30] assessed the performance of the Radical-7 device during gynecologic, urologic, and general abdominal surgeries, where the average estimated blood loss was 760 mL. In their study of 91 surgical patients, a weak correlation was observed between SpHb and tHb measurements. Furthermore, in 48.6% of the paired measurements, SpHb deviated by more than 1 g/dL from tHb. The authors identified several factors influencing bias: it was more pronounced in patients with blood loss exceeding 1,000 mL, hemoglobin levels below 9.0 g/dL, or those requiring intraoperative transfusion. However, the bias diminished during deep anesthesia. The increased bias in patients needing transfusion presents a challenge to the reliability of SpHb monitoring for transfusion decision-making.

Conclusion

Pulse CO-Oximetry enables continuous, noninvasive monitoring of hemoglobin levels, demonstrating a strong positive correlation with the standard invasive hemoglobin measurement during thoracic surgery. By offering faster insights into hemoglobin status than periodic blood sample analyses, this technology holds promise for perioperative optimization of blood management during thoracic surgery.
