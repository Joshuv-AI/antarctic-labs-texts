Impact of Siponimod on Clinical and Radiological Parameters of Secondary Progressive Multiple Sclerosis: A Real-World Prospective Study
Authors: Konstantina Stavrogianni, Dimitrios K. Kitsos, Vasileios Giannopapas, Maria-Ioanna Stefanou, Niki Christouli, Vassiliki Smyrni, Athanasios K. Chasiotis, Alexandra Akrivaki, Evangelia Dimitriadou, Maria Chondrogianni, Georgios Tsivgoulis, Sotirios Giannopoulos
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background and PurposeSecondary progressive multiple sclerosis (SPMS) presents with a challenging clinical phenotype, and siponimod has a potential to treat the active clinical phenotype of this disease. This single-center longitudinal study aimed to determine the therapeutic effects of siponimod in patients with active SPMS over 12 months.MethodsThe clinical and radiological parameters of 50 patients with active SPMS treated using siponimod were assessed at baseline and after a 1-year follow-up period using the annual relapse rate (ARR), the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS), the occurrence of gadolinium-enhanced lesion (GdE+), the Modified Fatigue Impact Scale (MFIS), and the Symbol Digit Modalities Test. The urine bladder postvoid residual (PVR) volume was also measured in a subcohort of 39 participants. Participants with an EDSS score ≥5.0 at baseline were finally assessed separately in prespecified subgroup analyses.ResultsThere were significant reductions in ARR (p<0.001), GdE+ (p<0.001), and MFIS score (p=0.001) during the follow-up period. The progression of physical and cognitive disabilities remained stable (p>0.05). The PVR-volume analysis revealed a significant decrease in urine bladder PVR volume (p<0.001). These observations were consistent for the subgroup with EDSS score ≥5.0.ConclusionsSiponimod demonstrated efficacy in reducing ARR, GdE+, fatigue levels, and PVR volume, while maintaining stability in the cognitive and physical disability statuses of patients with SPMS. Similar findings were documented in the subgroup with EDSS score ≥5.0.

INTRODUCTION

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic inflammatory autoimmune disease characterized by concurrent demyelination and neurodegeneration that affects the central nervous system (CNS).1 It has a heterogeneous clinical course, with patients experiencing clinical relapses, known as relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS). Many patients with MS transition to secondary progressive MS (SPMS),2345 which poses a significant challenge due to the variability in disability progression,6 often arising at the time of diagnosis, which irreversibly impacts the quality of life for patients. The transition to SPMS represents a crucial stage that typically occurs across 10–20 years. During this phase, approximately two-thirds of patients with MS experience progression marked by a gradual decline in inflammatory acute symptoms that manifests as a reduced annual relapse rate (ARR) and concurrent significant progression of disability, which is unrelated to the acute clinical relapses.567 However, around 30% of patients with MS may still encounter relapses concurrent with disability progression.57

The treatments for SPMS are still inadequate despite the progress made in understanding the immunity aspects of progressive MS.8 Unlike RRMS, where immunomodulatory therapies have proven effective in reducing the ARR, identifying effective treatments for SPMS remains a substantial challenge.9 The unsatisfactory effect of disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) to address disability progression in RRMS has therefore prompted the search for innovative solutions. Siponimod has received attention for its distinctive mechanism of action, and it holds promise in modulating the inflammatory cascade associated with MS progression that would address both the inflammatory and degenerative aspects of SPMS.10 The randomized-controlled EXPAND (EXploring the efficacy and safety of siponimod in PAtients with secoNDary progressive multiple sclerosis) clinical trial was a double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 3 study that was critical in securing approval to use siponimod to treat active SPMS.12

Siponimod functions as a modulator of the next-generation sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) receptor modulator that targets specific S1P receptor subtypes, primarily S1P1 and S1P5. Siponimod binds to these receptors and regulates lymphocyte circulation, preventing their migration from lymph nodes into the bloodstream and subsequently into the CNS.11 The modulatory effects of siponimod aim to restrain the inflammatory response linked to MS, resulting in reduced ARR and the occurrence of gadolinium-enhanced lesion (GdE+), and potentially affecting the rate of disability progression. Siponimod may also exert neuroprotective effects, further highlighting its significance in treating active SPMS.12

Regner-Nelke et al.13 conducted a retrospective, observational multicenter study that aimed to determine the efficacy and safety of siponimod in patients with active SPMS. They hypothesized that participants in the EXPAND study may have exhibited differences in individual and disease-specific characteristics relative to the real-world population. This situation may reduce the applicability of clinical trial results to standard clinical practices.13 We therefore attempted to determine the effectiveness of treatment in real-world cohorts of patients with active SPMS, and to provide clinicians and patients with more comprehensive information that would enable them to understand what to anticipate when considering siponimod as a treatment option. The motivation for exploring the effects of siponimod on active SPMS therefore arose from the pressing demand for therapeutic options that specifically address the unique challenges presented in this disease phase. While siponimod has the potential to slow disability progression, a more-focused investigation is the safest approach to understand its precise impact on active SPMS progression.

In this context, the primary objective of this prospective single-center study was to bridge the knowledge gap in the effects of siponimod by obtaining high-quality real-world data. Our primary goal was to determine how the treatment affects disease activity and to understand its possible influence on core symptoms and overall functionality by evaluating key clinical and radiological parameters over a 1-year period in a cohort of patients with active SPMS. We also aimed to provide a preliminary description of patients with active SPMS whose profiles align most effectively with the treatment by closely assessing and comparing how they respond to siponimod treatment after 1-year of follow-up.

METHODS

Study design

This study employed a prospective longitudinal design to compare clinical characteristics (disability status, fatigue, cognitive ability, bladder function, and ARR) and radiological characteristics (GdE+) of patients with SPMS after initiating iponimod treatment over 1 year from onset. No control group was included due to the focus of the study.

Participants

This longitudinal study recruited participants from the Multiple Sclerosis Outpatient Clinic of the Second Department of Neurology, Attikon University Hospital, a tertiary-care MS treatment center.

The minimum sample size required for the study was determined by a priori power analysis conducted using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7).14 The analysis was based on the t-test, and the parameters included a two-tailed test, a moderate effect size (d) of 0.6, an alpha error probability (a) of 0.05, and a desired statistical power (β) of 0.95. The results indicated that a sample of at least 39 participants was required. The final sample was adjusted to 47 participants to account for a potential dropout rate of 20%. The sample size used (n=50) was therefore deemed sufficient.

All enrolled patients began siponimod treatment between December 2022 and January 2023. Patients aged 18–60 years with a confirmed diagnosis of active SPMS were included.15 The exclusion criteria were confirmed cardiovascular disease, history of malignancy or cerebrovascular incidents, medical history of other neurological diseases (e.g., epilepsy) or autoimmune disorders with CNS manifestations (e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus), or declining to sign an informed-consent form.

The administration protocol for siponimod involved a titration phase to achieve the daily maintenance dose of 2 mg. Patients began with a 0.25-mg dose on each of the first 2 days, followed by 0.5 mg on day 3, 0.75 mg on day 4, 1.25 mg on day 5, and finally the maintenance dose of 2 mg on day 6. This dose was consistently administered at approximately the same time each day, with or without food. No participant missed a dose within the first 6 days of the titration phase, and maintenance treatment was not interrupted to avoid the need to restart treatment. The dose was also calculated based on each patient genotype according to the CYP2C9 gene of the P450 cythochrome.16 All participants belonged to one of the following genotypes: CYP2C9*1/*1, CYP2C9*1/*2, or CYP2C9*2/*2. The mean time to siponimod induction was 6–12 weeks, depending on the lymphocyte count and the recovery time.2

Data were collected during the initial assessment and after the 1-year follow-up. The data collection phase extended from December 2022 to January 2024. The collected data comprised basic demographic (age and sex) and clinical characteristics, and disease-related and disability-progression measures. Most of the 50 patients with active SPMS had a previous history of receiving DMT (Table 1). All participants provided informed consent, which also confirmed their voluntary participation in the study. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Attikon University Hospital (EBΔ185/15/03/22). The research adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.17

Outcome measures

The clinical and neuroimaging parameters that served as outcome measures were disability progression according to the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS),18 information processing speed according to the Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT),19 and fatigue according to the Modified Fatigue Impact Scale (MFIS),20 ARR, and GdE+.

The postvoid residual (PVR) volume was measured in milliliters among a subgroup of patients as part of an evaluation aimed at assessing the function of the lower urinary tract, and corresponded to the volume of urine that remained in the bladder after voiding.21

Finally, another subgroup comprising patients with EDSS score ≥5.0 presented progressive difficulty in and/or the need for support when walking.22 The same clinical and radiological parameters were analyzed separately in this subgroup. This stratification allowed the evaluation of the effects of siponimod in patients with both moderately and strongly progressed disability, thereby providing insights into its impact across different disability levels.

All parameters were identified twice during the initiation visit and at the 1-year follow-up.

Statistical analyses

ARR was treated as a categorical dichotomous variable (presence or absence) and was described using absolute and relative frequencies, as was GdE+. Due to the minimal variation in GdE+ (few patients had two lesions at baseline), differences between baseline and follow-up were analyzed as a binary dichotomy (presence or absence). Mean±standard-deviation values of their EDSS, SDMT, and MFIS scores were calculated and used in the primary analysis. Patients were divided into two groups according to EDSS scores: ≤4.5 and ≥5.0. We did not use three groups due to only a few patients having EDSS scores <2.5, with most having scores of 3.0–4.5; these scores were therefore grouped together.

In the analysis of the entire cohort, separate paired-samples t-tests were applied to the three continuous variables: EDSS, SDMT, and MFIS scores. Data normality and the absence of outliers were ensured and confirmed by inspecting Q-Q plots for all continuous baseline and follow-up measurements across the entire sample and in the subgroups. ARR and GdE+ were evaluated using McNemar’s chi-square test, which identified differences in the categorical variables between the initial phase and the follow-up period. Changes over time were assessed among the subcohort of patients with available PVR data using paired-samples t-tests. Finally, targeted statistical analyses were applied to a subgroup of patients with EDSS score ≥5.0. McNemar’s test was also applied to the binary variables (ARR and GdE+). We opted for the paired-samples t-test for EDSS, SDMT, and MFIS scores. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 28.0.1.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).23

RESULTS

Demographic characteristics

This prospective study enrolled 50 patients with active SPMS who were undergoing siponimod treatment. The age at baseline was 43.7±8.1 years (range 29–59 years), with a slight male predominance (n=29, 58%). The duration from symptom onset to baseline evaluation was 12.30±2.56 years (range 7–17 years). Regarding treatments, 8 patients (16%) had received none at their initial visit, and 30 (60%) had received first-line treatment:24 20 (40%) had received injectable DMT (interferon beta 1a/b or glatiramer acetate) and 10 (20%) had received an oral first-line treatment regimen (teriflunomide or dimethyl fumarate). Finally, 12 patients (24%) had received natalizumab or fingolimod (Table 1).

Before initiating siponimod treatment, 20 patients (40%) had not experienced a relapse, while 30 (60%) had, while the numbers at the follow-up were 44 (88%) and 6 (12%), respectively. Prior to initiating siponimod, 31 (62%) patients presented new GdE+ at baseline, while 19 (38%) did not. Among them, 28 had one new GdE+ and 3 had two; the numbers at the follow-up were 45 (90%) and 5 (10%), respectively (Table 2).

Long-term outcomes

Applying the paired-samples t-test to the entire sample (n=50) revealed a significant reduction in fatigue according to MFIS scores, from 45.86±3.89 at baseline to 44.10±3.95 the end of the follow-up period [t(49)=3.48, p=0.001], with a moderate effect size indicated by a Cohen’s d of 0.49. No significant change during the follow-up period was observed in the EDSS score [t(49)=0.56, p=0.228, d=0.08] or SDMT score [t(49)=0.32, p=0.375, d=0.05]. These results suggest a stabilization in physical disability progression, with baseline and follow-up EDSS scores of 4.45±1.09 and 4.41±1.22, respectively. Similarly, there was a lack of significant changes in information processing speed, with baseline and follow-up SDMT scores of 36.68±3.72 and 36.54±3.18, respectively. McNemar’s chi-square test showed significant changes in ARR (χ2=22.04, p<0.001) and GdE+ (χ2=20.83, p<0.001) over the 1-year follow-up period. ARR decreased significantly (p<0.001) from 60% at baseline to 12% at the end of follow-up, as did GdE+ (p<0.001), from 62% to 10% (Table 2).

In the subcohort of patients (n=39) with available PVR data, a paired-samples t-test revealed a significant improvement in bladder functionality, with the residual urine volume decreasing from 71.03±18.52 mL at baseline to 46.89±29.25 mL at follow-up, with a large effect size [t(37)=7.16, p<0.001, d=1.16] (Table 2).

For the subgroup of patients (n=19) with EDSS score ≥5.0, the paired-samples t-test indicated a significant reduction in fatigue according to MFIS scores, from 48.47±3.57 at baseline to 46.42±3.88 at follow-up, with a moderate-to-large effect size (t=2.69, p=0.007, d=0.74). Additionally, there was a significant difference in bladder functionality, with the measured PVR volume improving from baseline (78.89±17.28 mL) to follow-up (60.89±33.10 mL), showing a moderate-to-large effect size (t=3.24, p=0.005, d=0.62). There was no significant difference in the EDSS score (t=0.29, p=0.386, d=0.07) or SDMT score (t=-0.45, p=0.331, d=-0.10), suggesting stabilization of the progression of physical disability from baseline (5.61±0.52) to the end of the follow-up (5.58±0.65), and also in the information processing speed (from 34.95±3.06 to 35.26±1.85). McNemar’s chi-square test for this subgroup yielded significant changes in ARR (χ2=4.18, p=0.031), and GdE+ (χ2=13.08, p<0.001) over the 1-year follow-up period, with ARR decreasing from 36.8% to 5.3% and complete resolution of GdE+ from baseline (78.9%) to the end of follow-up (0.0%) (Table 2).

Safety outcomes

Regarding the safety profile of siponimod, 39 patients (78%) presented with grade 3 lymphopenia or lower, and 12 patients (24%) presented with elevated values of alanine transaminase in liver function tests. Based on the EXPAND study, none of these quite-common side effects were a reason for medication discontinuation.2

DISCUSSION

This study revealed the siponimod produced positive outcomes across clinical and radiological parameters over a 1-year follow-up period in 50 patients with active SPMS. Specifically, there was a significant reduction in fatigue, as measured by the MFIS, and substantial decreases in both ARR and GdE+. Additionally, in a subcohort of 39 patients with available data, significant improvements in bladder functionality were indicated by reductions in the PVR volume. The EDSS and SDMT scores did not change over time, indicating stability of disability progression and information processing speed. These results were consistent for the entire sample of patients as well as for the subgroup of patients with EDSS score ≥5.0. This subset analysis was conducted to ascertain if the observed changes were applicable to patients with active SPMS independent of their disability severity. Collectively, these findings emphasize the promising role of siponimod in addressing both the inflammatory and degenerative aspects of active SPMS, offering hope for improved management in this challenging phase of MS.

Siponimod is currently the only approved therapy for active SPMS.12 This is based on findings from the EXPAND study, in which siponimod treatment improved ARR,2 mirroring the outcomes of the present study. The effectiveness of siponimod is reinforced by previous research involving RRMS patients that found decreases in both ARR and brain lesions on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI),25 in line with the present observations. The consistent reduction in ARR and GdE+ across different MS stages may be attributable to the ability of siponimod to modulate the inflammatory cascade that is implicated in MS progression. This alignment across various disease stages highlights the potential of siponimod to address not only relapse-related aspects but also broader inflammatory components, which contribute to its efficacy in both RRMS and SPMS. Evidence from the EXPAND study also supports the use of siponimod in a typical SPMS population, with even greater benefits in early or active SPMS as defined by relapses or MRI activity.214

The current study employed the GdE+ as a radiological marker for disease activity. In general, compared with new or enlarged T2-weighted lesions, GdE+ is easier to detect and less likely to be affected by technical factors, such as changes in the positions where scans are performed.26 Additionally, since new T2-weighted lesions can be small or located in regions affected by confluent lesions, they might only be detectable visually after they have been identified as a new GdE+.2728 This is corroborated by the existing inclusion criteria based on the EXPAND study,2 which did not have any radiological prerequisites and included participants with specific disability levels at the time of selection.29 For the aforementioned reasons, we attempted to follow the clinical indications for siponimod2 and introduced variables that would best describe patients with active SPMS, and did not use enlargement of T2-weighted lesions as a variable for assessing disability progression.29

Nevertheless, insights from the study of Regner-Nelke et al.13 introduce a more-subtle and holistic perspective. Their research involved a patient cohort undergoing siponimod treatment that displayed new T2-weighted lesions after 12 months, indicative of the subclinical progression of inflammatory disease. Those authors raised the possibility that in some patients with SPMS, persistent inflammatory disease activity may endure despite the administration of siponimod treatment, especially in patients transitioning from highly effective DMTs.13 Those authors consequently concluded that the decision to switch from highly active DMTs to siponimod requires careful consideration and mandates vigilant monitoring during the washout period, so that any signs of inflammatory activity can be detected promptly.13

In the present study we divided patients based on their EDSS scores into two groups: ≤4.5 and ≥5.0. This classification reflects that EDSS scores up to 4.5 typically indicate better ambulatory ability, whereas scores of 5.0 and above suggest significant impairment in ambulatory function.16 This approach is consistent with the methods used in the EXPAND study2 and the study of Regner-Nelke et al.13 The EXPAND study found that siponimod significantly reduced the risk of disability progression.2 This observed efficacy was supported by analyses incorporating novel motor integration measures, including ambulation and brainstem/pyramidal functions, and collateral subscales of the EDSS.30 Regner-Nelke et al.13 partially replicated these results in a subgroup of 41 patients with active SPMS, where a delay in disability progression was also evident. The very similar findings of these studies, including our own, suggest that siponimod can consistently slow the progression of disability.1330 In our study, the results for the EDSS scores were consistent across both groups (i.e., ≤4.5 and ≥5.0). This delay in the progression of physical disability can be attributed to the multifaceted mechanism of action of siponimod, which not only addresses the inflammatory aspects of MS,10 but also potentially exerts neuroprotective effects through the suppression of proinflammatory pathways.3031 This dual action contributes to the efficacy of siponimod in preserving neurological function in patients with SPMS.

Efforts to protect against the neurodegenerative effects of MS are a primary focus in the development of novel MS therapies. The widely utilized EDSS serves as a primary tool for documenting the progression of physical disability; however, its emphasis on motor changes and the ambulation status neglects other critical facets of MS progression, such as increasing fatigue and cognitive decline. These aspects are only addressed in the cerebral-function subscale of the EDSS.32 With this in mind, the current study further examined the outcomes of the MFIS and SDMT with the aim of providing a more-holistic perspective on the ability of siponimod to address various dimensions of MS progression. The findings of our study are consistent with the outcomes obtained in the open-label extension phase of the EXPAND study, revealing sustained clinical achievement without any decline in the SDMT score.33 Similarly, the study of Regner-Nelke et al.13 specifically assessed cognitive abilities related to information processing speed using the Paced Auditory Serial Addition Test and the SDMT, and demonstrated stability in these cognitive measures throughout the 12-month follow-up period. This concordance underlines the importance of considering both cognitive and fatigue dimensions when evaluating MS progression beyond the conventional metrics employed in clinical trials.

A novel aspect of this study that has been absent in previous investigations of siponimod treatment is monitoring of the PVR volume. The PVR volume is not universally measured in clinical practice, but it is significant due to its association with the disease progression observed in patients with MS.21 Currently, the primary parameters used to assess urinary bladder dysfunction in MS include patient questionnaires, a urinary bladder diary, and PVR-volume evaluation. These parameters are critical predictors of disability progression related to urinary bladder dysfunction in patients with MS.34 Individuals with greater disability and progressive MS phenotypes are more prone to exhibit pathological urodynamic findings, highlighting the significance of monitoring such parameters.21 The association between lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) and a progressive disease course makes measurements of the PVR volume particularly important. In addition, higher EDSS scores are correlated with more self-reported LUTS, emphasizing a connection between the presence of LUTS and increased disability. This relationship between a larger PVR volume and disease severity emphasizes the need for comprehensive clinical evaluations that encompass both neurological and micturition disorders.21

This study aimed to provide insights into the profile of patients with active SPMS who appear to benefit from siponimod treatment. The observed positive outcomes, including significant reductions in ARR, GdE+, and fatigue, improved bladder function, and stabilization of cognitive and physical functionality, indicate the beneficial effects of siponimod during a very specific timeframe of the disease where declining inflammatory pathophysiology coexists with characteristic degenerative mechanisms of innate immunity. In addition, the findings of this study suggest that siponimod offers benefits not only in patients with moderate disability but also in those in more-advanced stages of SPMS where activity may be less evident. In such cases, the efficacy of siponimod results from its effects on a wide spectrum of clinical and radiological parameters.

Several study limitations should be acknowledged. The relatively small sample and the absence of a control group may have hindered the ability to detect causal relationships between siponimod treatment and the observed outcomes. Additionally, the relatively short 1-year follow-up period might not have captured the long-term effects of siponimod. Finally, the absence of PVR-volume measurements for the entire cohort might have introduced biases into specific analyses. Despite these limitations, the study has provided new insights into the understanding of the impact of siponimod on active SPMS. The inclusion of PVR-volume monitoring—an underexplored aspect in previous studies—has provided a unique perspective on the treatment effects on lower urinary tract function. Larger multicenter studies with diverse populations and longer follow-up durations could enhance the generalizability of the present findings and provide more-comprehensive assessments of the effects of siponimod. Future studies could also consider performing comprehensive assessments that go beyond traditional measures, by incorporating additional patient-reported symptoms and functional evaluations.

In conclusion, this study has highlighted the potential benefits of siponimod treatment in patients with active SPMS. A cohort of 50 patients with active SPMS exhibited significant reductions in ARR and GdE+ over a 1-year follow-up period, supporting the efficacy of siponimod in modulating the inflammatory cascade implicated in MS progression. The study also found significant reductions in the MFIS score that reflected a noteworthy alleviation of fatigue. Moreover, the study introduced a novel dimension by including PVR-volume measurements, and demonstrated improvements in bladder functionality across patients, including those with higher disability scores. These findings contribute to the evolving landscape of MS therapeutics and underline the importance of exploring novel outcome measures in future research focused on SPMS.
