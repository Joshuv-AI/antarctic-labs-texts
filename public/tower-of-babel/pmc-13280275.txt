Prior SGLT2 Inhibitor and Metformin Use and Risk of Long COVID in Type 2 Diabetes: A Nationwide Population-Based Cohort Study
Authors: Jinghao Nicholas Ngiam, Enoch Xueheng Loy, Matthew Chung Yi Koh, Calvin J. Chiew, Russell Jingxian Li, Su Chi Lim, E Shyong Tai, Yong Mong Bee, Wai Leng Chow, David Chien Boon Lye, Yew Woon Chia, Mark Yan Yee Chan, Derek J. Hausenloy, Kelvin Bryan Tan, Liang En Wee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPatients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) are at increased risk of post-acute sequelae after COVID-19 (PASC). Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) and metformin may have systemic benefits beyond glycemic control. We evaluated the impact of prior SGLT2i and metformin use on the risk of post-acute COVID-19 complications.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, population-based cohort study using national healthcare claims databases, from July 1, 2021 to February 28, 2023. Cohorts were stratified based on SGLT2i or metformin, against patients had not received these medications. Overlap weighting was applied to adjust for baseline differences in demographics, vaccination status, comorbidities, and prior healthcare utilization. Competing-risks regression models were used to assess differences in the risk of long-COVID outcomes between 31 and 300 days post-infection.ResultsAmong 71,698 patients with T2DM, 22,501 (31.4%) had prior SGLT2i use, and 66,792 (93.1%) had prior metformin use. Compared with non-SGLT2i users, patients treated with SGLT2i had a significantly lower risk of neurological sequelae (aHR = 0.60 [0.45–0.81]), particularly memory and cognitive impairment (aHR = 0.63 [0.41–0.98]). SGLT2i was also associated with a reduced risk of post-acute symptoms (aHR = 0.87 [0.77–0.99]). Metformin use was associated with significantly lower risk of composite post-acute outcomes (aHR = 0.80 [0.68–0.96]) and post-acute symptoms (aHR = 0.77 [0.63–0.93]). Amongst patients on metformin, the addition of SGLT2i further lowered the risk of neurological sequelae (aHR = 0.81 [0.71–0.93]) and composite symptoms (aHR 0.87 [0.76–0.99]).ConclusionSGLT2i and metformin use were associated with a lower risk of PASC and post-acute symptoms. There may be additional protective benefits when both agents are used concurrently.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-026-01362-z.

Introduction

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic has left a significant burden of post-acute sequelae, collectively termed long COVID, affecting multiple organ systems, including cardiovascular, neurological, respiratory, and metabolic pathways [1–3]. Patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) are at an increased risk of developing long COVID. A systematic review by Harding et al. found that 44% of the included studies identified diabetes as a significant risk factor for post-acute sequelae and post-acute symptoms after COVID-19 (PASC) [4]. A cohort study by Nørgård et al. demonstrated that patients with type 2 diabetes who were hospitalized with COVID-19 had an increased risk of post-acute hospitalization for respiratory diseases and infections, as well as a higher risk of death [5]. Given the growing recognition of PASC as a major public health concern, there is an urgent need to identify therapeutic strategies that may mitigate this risk [6, 7]. Specific to T2DM, the antidiabetic medications, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) and metformin, have been hypothesized to provide benefits beyond glycemic control [8–10]. SGLT2i have demonstrated cardiovascular and renal protective effects [11, 12]. Additionally, emerging evidence suggests they may attenuate inflammation and oxidative stress, potentially modifying the course of PASC [13–15]. Metformin, on the other hand, possesses pleiotropic properties, including anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and antiviral effects [16–20].

Recent studies have suggested that early metformin use may reduce the incidence of long COVID, although its effects on specific organ-system sequelae remain to be fully elucidated [21]. The COVID-OUT trial demonstrated that early metformin use was associated with a reduced incidence of long COVID, with a hazard ratio of 0.59, indicating a potential protective effect [21]. On the other hand, SGLT2i have been shown to reduce systemic inflammation, mitigate endothelial dysfunction, and modulate immune responses, which may be particularly relevant in preventing long-term complications following SARS-CoV-2 infection [13, 15, 22, 23]. While prior studies have suggested that metformin may reduce the risk of long COVID, and SGLT2i may improve COVID-19 outcomes, data on their effects on organ-system-specific PASC remain limited [24, 25]. In addition, the potential combined or additive effects of these commonly co-prescribed agents have not been well characterized. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the association between prior metformin and SGLT2 inhibitor use and the risk of multi-system PASC in a large national cohort, and to assess whether concurrent use confers additional benefit.

Methods

Study Population, Study Period, and Data Sources

The national COVID-19 database, containing records of all positive SARS-CoV-2 test results (polymerase-chain-reaction, PCR or rapid-antigen-test, RAT) reported by healthcare providers to the local Ministry of Health (MOH) was integrated with the National Diabetes Database, the nationwide registry for diabetes in Singapore, to construct a retrospective, population-based cohort of adults with T2DM who had been prescribed antidiabetic medications and subsequently diagnosed with an index episode of SARS-CoV-2 infection over the study period of July 1, 2021 to February 28, 2023. These databases had been previously utilized to examine long-term diabetes risk post-COVID-19 [24]. COVID-19 vaccination status was obtained from the National Immunization Registry. Patients were included if they had a confirmed COVID-19 infection and had a prior history of T2DM with documented use of antidiabetic medications in the 12 months before their SARS-CoV-2 infection. To ensure a well-defined cohort, patients were excluded if they were younger than 18 years, had received non-mRNA COVID-19 vaccinations, had no recorded prescription of antidiabetic medication in the past year, had chronic renal disease, died within 30 days of infection, or were reinfected within 300 days of their initial COVID-19 diagnosis, similar to prior studies done in this population [26–30].

The cohort was stratified into four medication exposure groups based on the use of metformin, SGLT2i, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors (DPP-4i), or sulfonylureas in the year preceding COVID-19 diagnosis. For each medication group, a control cohort consisted of individuals who had been prescribed any other antidiabetic medication but not the specific drug of interest. This approach allowed for direct comparisons of long-term outcomes between users and non-users of each medication class. We focused on comparisons in patients with and without prior metformin use, as well as patients with and without SGLT2i use.

Cohort Construction

The construction of the study cohort is outlined in Fig. 1. Overlap weighting was applied to balance differences in baseline characteristics, ensuring comparability between the exposed and non-exposed groups for each medication. This method adjusted for key demographic and clinical factors, including age, sex, ethnicity, socioeconomic status (based on housing type), Charlson’s Comorbidity Index (CCI), prior healthcare utilization, vaccination status at the time of infection, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), HbA1c, other chronic medication use (metformin, SGLT2i, sulfonylureas, insulin, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, statins, anticoagulants, aspirin) and receipt of acute COVID-19 treatments such as antivirals or monoclonal antibodies. Pre-Specified Outcomes

The primary outcomes were post-acute sequelae and post-acute symptoms occurring between 31 and 300 days after the initial COVID-19 diagnosis. These outcomes were identified using International Classification of Diseases Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes recorded in the national healthcare claims database. For clarity, we defined acute COVID-19 as the period within 30 days of SARS-CoV-2 diagnosis. Post-acute outcomes were defined as those occurring between 31 and 300 days after infection, consistent with prior literature on PASC. Post-acute sequelae refer to new incident diagnosis-based conditions identified using ICD codes, while post-acute symptoms refer to symptom-based clinical presentations similarly captured. The definitions of these conditions were adapted from prior research on PASC, and are fully listed the Supplementary Material (Supplementary tables 24 and 25) [26–30]. The national government-administered medical-savings scheme (Medisave) and medical insurance scheme (Medishield) can be claimed against for inpatient care treatment at public and private healthcare providers; participation in Medisave/Medishield is compulsory [31]. This enabled comprehensive capture of new-incident sequelae across different healthcare settings. The following categories of multi-system long-COVID sequelae were assessed (i) cardiovascular outcomes: dysrhythmias, ischemic heart disease (IHD), heart failure, inflammatory heart disease, thrombotic disorders, and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE); (ii) neurological outcomes: cerebrovascular events, cognitive impairment, memory deficits, peripheral neuropathies, episodic disorders, movement disorders, and sensory deficits; (iii) psychiatric outcomes: depression, anxiety, and other mood disorders; (iv) respiratory outcomes: asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and interstitial lung disease (ILD); (v) autoimmune outcomes: composite of autoimmune and inflammatory connective tissue diseases; (vi) dermatological and gastrointestinal outcomes; (vii) post-acute symptoms: cardiovascular signs and symptoms, respiratory signs and symptoms, headache, musculoskeletal pain/stiffness, abdominal and pelvic pain, generalized pain, loss of smell or taste, memory and cognitive impairment, fatigue and malaise.

Covariates and Confounding Adjustment

To account for potential confounders, demographic and clinical variables were extracted from national databases. These included age, sex, ethnicity, socioeconomic status and comorbidity burden, measured using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). COVID-19 vaccination status was classified as boosted (≥ three doses of mRNA vaccine), and those who were unboosted (< 3 doses of vaccine). Information on renal function, HbA1c levels, and prior healthcare utilization, including emergency department visits and hospitalizations in the year before infection were included to adjust for baseline health status. We accounted for the use of other medications, including renin-angiotensin system inhibitors (ACE inhibitors/ARBs), statins, aspirin, and other antidiabetic agents. Given that some patients received antivirals (nirmatrelvir/ritonavir, molnupiravir, remdesivir) or monoclonal antibodies during the acute COVID-19 phase, these treatments were adjusted for in the statistical models.

Statistical Analysis

The risk of developing post-acute sequelae and symptoms was estimated using competing-risks regression models. For each outcome, a sub-cohort was constructed, excluding individuals with a history of the studied condition in the five years prior to infection. Baseline characteristics were compared between medication exposure groups and their respective controls, with standardized mean differences (SMD) used to assess balance. An SMD of < 0.1 was considered indicative of adequate covariate balance. Overlap weighting was applied to adjust for residual differences between groups, ensuring that comparisons reflected treatment effects rather than baseline imbalances. Adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) were estimated using competing-risks regression models, incorporating overlap weights to account for differences in follow-up time and censoring due to death or loss to follow-up.

Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate potential effect modification by key demographic and clinical characteristics. Analyses were stratified by age (< 60 versus ≥ 60 years), sex, vaccination status (boosted versus unboosted), and glycemic control [categorized as good (HbA1c < 6.5%) versus poor HbA1c control (HbA1c > 6.5%)]. Given potential temporal differences in the risk of PASC, an additional subgroup analysis was conducted in individuals infected during Omicron predominance (from Jan 7, 2022 onwards), as well as in non-hospitalized patients to assess whether findings were consistent in milder cases. For each subgroup, associations with composite outcomes (any PASC, system-specific diagnoses, and post-acute symptoms) were examined. To evaluate the additive effect of SGLT2i to metformin, we examined the subgroup of patients who had been on metformin, and compared those with or without concurrent SGLT2i use.

To further evaluate the robustness of our findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses. We employed a negative outcome control analysis by examining the incidence of newly diagnosed leukemia/lymphoma, an outcome not expected to be influenced by antidiabetic medication use, to assess potential residual confounding. Additionally, we conducted an alternative time-restricted sensitivity analysis, limiting follow-up to 90–300 days post-infection to assess whether associations persisted when excluding the immediate post-acute phase. Finally, we also conducted similar analyses with the other antidiabetic medications, namely, comparing the primary outcome in patients on DPP-4i and those without, as well as patients on sulfonylureas and those without. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to visualize the risk trajectories for neurological outcomes in SGLT2 inhibitor users versus non-users, with 95% confidence intervals shaded to illustrate uncertainty. A 95% confidence interval excluding one was considered statistically significant, and p values < 0.01 were interpreted as robust in the context of multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA version 16.0.

Ethics Approval

This study was conducted as part of national public health surveillance under the Singapore Infectious Diseases Act, and separate ethics approval by an Institutional Review Board (IRB) was not required. This study was performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments.

Results

A total of 71,698 individuals with T2DM who had a documented COVID-19 infection between July 1, 2021 and February 28, 2023 were included. The study population was stratified based on exposure to different oral antidiabetic medications, specifically metformin (n = 66,792) and SGLT2i (n = 22,501). The comparator groups for each of these were those that had T2DM but were not on these agents (Fig. 1). The baseline characteristics were generally well-balanced between groups after weighting (Tables 1 and 2). Patients on prior metformin and those without metformin had a similar median age (~ 60 years), sex distribution (49% female in both groups), and Charlson Comorbidity Index. Prior hospitalization and COVID-19 severity were also comparable. Vaccination rates were high across both groups (~ 80% boosted) (Table 1). Likewise, SGLT2i users and non-users showed no significant differences in age distribution, sex distribution (~ 45% female in both groups), glycemic control and Charlson Comorbidity Index. COVID-19 hospitalization rates were similar after weighting (Table 2). In the primary outcome analysis, metformin use was associated with a lower risk of composite outcomes (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] = 0.80, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.68–0.96, p = 0.0149) and composite symptoms (aHR = 0.77, 95% CI 0.63–0.93, p = 0.0084) (Table 3). SGLT2i were associated with a reduced risk of neurological outcomes (aHR = 0.60, 95% CI 0.45–0.81, p = 0.0006), primarily driven by a protective effect against memory and cognitive impairment. SGLT2i were also linked to lower risks of composite symptoms (aHR = 0.87, 95% CI 0.77–0.99, p = 0.0350) and fatigue and malaise (aHR = 0.63, 95% CI 0.41–0.98, p = 0.0389) (Table 4, Fig. 2). In the subgroup of patients who had been on metformin (n = 66,792), 20,668 (30.9%) were concurrently on SGLT2i, while the remainder were not. Those on both SGLT2i and metformin had a lower risk of neurological sequelae (aHR = 0.81 [0.71–0.93], p = 0.003) and composite symptoms (aHR 0.87 [0.76–0.99], p = 0.038) compared with patients on metformin alone (Table 5). On further subgroup analyses, amongst individuals aged greater than 60 years, metformin was still protective against composite outcomes and symptoms, and was additionally protective against neurological outcomes (aHR = 0.75, 95% CI 0.60–0.93, p = 0.0091). SGLT2i in the older age group was also protective for neurological outcomes (p = 0.0002), composite symptoms (p = 0.0079), and fatigue and malaise (p = 0.0435). In males, metformin remained significant for composite outcomes (p = 0.0497) and was additionally protective against memory and cognitive impairment (aHR = 0.11, 95% CI 0.02–0.57, p = 0.0086). SGLT2i were protective against neurological outcomes in both sexes. Among boosted individuals, metformin retained protective effects against composite outcomes (aHR = 0.76, 95% CI 0.63–0.93, p = 0.0063) and symptoms (aHR = 0.71, 95% CI 0.57–0.88, p = 0.0015). Additionally, metformin was protective against cardiovascular symptoms (aHR = 0.69, 95% CI 0.49–0.98, p = 0.0397). SGLT2i in the boosted subgroup remained protective against neurological outcomes (aHR = 0.79, 95% CI 0.69–0.91, p = 0.001) and composite symptoms (aHR = 0.84, 95% CI 0.73–0.96, p = 0.0121) (Supplementary Material).

In the subgroup of individuals with well-controlled T2DM, metformin was protective against composite symptoms (aHR = 0.59, 95% CI 0.43–0.80, p = 0.0008). SGLT2i in this subgroup was also protective against neurological outcomes (p = 0.0012) and were additionally protective against respiratory symptoms (aHR = 0.60, 95% CI 0.38–0.95, p = 0.0276). When analyses were limited to the Omicron-predominant period, metformin retained protective effects against composite outcomes (p = 0.0435) and composite symptoms (p = 0.0037), while SGLT2i continued to show a protective effect against neurological outcomes (p = 0.0008). In non-hospitalized patients, metformin conferred protection against composite outcomes (p = 0.0169), psychiatric outcomes (p = 0.0497), composite symptoms (p = 0.001), and memory and cognitive impairment (p = 0.0201). SGLT2i were protective against neurological outcomes (p = 0.0041) and composite symptoms (p = 0.0313) (Supplementary Material).

A negative control outcome analysis was conducted using leukemia/lymphoma as a presumed unrelated outcome. No significant associations were observed between any diabetes medications and leukemia/lymphoma, mitigating concerns about residual confounding. On subsequent sensitivity analysis using a 90- to 300-day post-infection window (instead of 31–300 days previously), SGLT2i were still protective against neurological sequelae (p = 0.0014) and memory and cognitive impairment (p = 0.0017). In this time period, metformin remained protective against composite outcomes (p = 0.0391) and composite symptoms (p = 0.0436). When the analyses for primary outcomes were conducted comparing patients on DDP-4i and those without, as well as patients on sulfonylurea and those without, we did not observe any significant differences in PASC (Supplementary Material).

Discussion

Our findings build on existing evidence suggesting a protective role of metformin in long COVID and emerging data on SGLT2i in COVID-19 outcomes [24, 25]. In addition to confirming these associations in a large, nationally representative cohort, a key novel finding in this study was demonstrating a potential additive effect when both agents had been used concurrently.

Firstly, we found that metformin was associated with a lower risk of composite PASC and post-acute symptoms. This aligns with findings from the COVID-OUT trial, which demonstrated that early metformin use significantly reduced the incidence of long COVID [21]. However, the findings of COVID-OUT may be different from those with prevalent metformin use, as the initiation of metformin at the time of COVID-19 diagnosis may be limited by a slower onset of action [23]. Given its well-documented pleiotropic properties, metformin’s protective effects are likely mediated through multiple pathways [19, 32, 33]. It possesses anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties, reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines and mitochondrial dysfunction, both of which are implicated in the pathophysiology of long COVID [18, 34, 35]. Additionally, metformin has been hypothesized to exert antiviral effects against SARS-CoV-2, potentially reducing viral persistence and post-acute complications [36]. This antiviral effect against SARS-CoV-2 has additionally been demonstrated in a randomized controlled trial [37]. There is additionally a speculative role that this drug plays in modulating gut microbiota and metabolic pathways, which may also contribute to mitigating PASC [38, 39]. These may be important to study to future prospective and mechanistic studies.

Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that SGLT2i may confer benefits in COVID-19 beyond glycemic control. Observational studies have reported improved clinical outcomes, including reduced severity and mortality, among patients receiving SGLT2i, while recent analyses have also suggested a potential role in reducing the risk of PASC [40, 41]. In our study, we found that SGLT2i use was linked to reduced neurological sequelae, particularly in terms of memory and cognitive impairment. Our findings are consistent with these observations and extend prior work by demonstrating associations with reduced risk of specific PASC in a large, nationally representative cohort of patients with T2DM. This may in part be explained mechanistically by the role of SGLT2i in mitigating systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction [42–44]. Further possible explanations for the neuroprotective effects of SGLT2i may include enhancing ketone metabolism, improving cerebral blood flow, and modulating oxidative stress [45–49]. Additionally, their effects on cardiovascular and renal health could indirectly contribute to a lower burden of PASC and post-acute symptoms, particularly in vulnerable populations with metabolic comorbidities [50, 51].

As COVID-19 continues to remain endemic with sporadic outbreaks in various settings globally, the burden of PASC remains a significant concern, particularly in patients with T2DM [52, 53]. Therefore, understanding the impact of various antidiabetic drugs on PASC could further help guide the choice of these agents in managing patients with T2DM. Further research is needed to clarify the mechanisms by which metformin and SGLT2i exert their protective effects against PASC. Prospective randomized studies should evaluate whether the timing and duration of therapy influence outcomes. Future research should also explore whether these findings extend to non-diabetic populations and assess the impact of other glucose-lowering agents on PASC and post-acute symptoms.

Limitations

Several limitations exist in this nationwide cohort study. The use of SGLT2i was not randomly assigned, and treatment decisions were made at the discretion of managing physicians, which may have introduced selection bias due to unmeasured confounders. While overlap weighting had been applied to balance baseline characteristics, residual confounding from factors such as socioeconomic status, differences in health-seeking behavior and baseline health status could still remain, and could thus influence the likelihood of detecting certain outcomes. As such, our observations should be interpreted cautiously and warrant further validation in prospective studies. Additionally, data on body mass index, adherence to medications and measures of glycemic control beyond HbA1c levels were not available in national databases and could not be adjusted for. The study relied on national healthcare claims data to identify PASC, which may have a degree of under-reporting, particularly for patients with milder symptoms who did not seek medical attention or did not receive a formal diagnosis. As our definitions were based on diagnostic codes, misclassification bias was possible, particularly for complex conditions such as cognitive impairment or autoimmune disorders. Furthermore, while we adjusted for prior healthcare utilization, differences in care-seeking patterns among patients prescribed different antidiabetic medications may have affected the observed outcomes.

Another important limitation is the heterogeneity in the timing of SGLT2i initiation and duration of use before COVID-19 infection, which was not standardized in this study. While we stratified patients based on documented use within the preceding year, we could not account for variations in adherence or discontinuation that may have influenced PASC. Similarly, concurrent use of other antidiabetic medications was common in the cohort, making it challenging to fully isolate the effects of SGLT2i from potential additive or synergistic effects when drugs were used in combination [10, 54, 55]. Finally, the study was conducted in Singapore, with high healthcare access, and the majority of the population was vaccinated with mRNA vaccines during the study period [56, 57. The generalizability of these findings to other settings with different vaccination strategies, healthcare systems, or racial and ethnic compositions may be limited. Future studies incorporating alternative data sources, such as primary care records and patient-reported outcomes, would help to further validate these findings and refine our understanding of the long-term impact of metformin and SGLT2i on PASC.

Conclusions

In patients with T2DM, we found that the prior use of SGLT2i was associated with a lower risk of post-acute neurological sequelae and post-acute symptoms, particularly cognitive impairment and fatigue following an acute episode of SARS-CoV-2 infection. Metformin use was associated with reduced composite PASC overall and symptoms. There also appeared to be an additive protective effect for patients on both agents, compared with metformin alone. These findings suggest that SGLT2i and metformin may offer different protective effects against certain PASC, though the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Future prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings, with a focus on exploring the underlying mechanisms and longer-term clinical implications.
