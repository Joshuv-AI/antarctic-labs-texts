Nanotextured light modulation for flexible OLEDs with 370% enhanced EQE and angular color stability
Authors: Young Rok Kim, Ajay Nimbalkar, Min Chul Suh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTop-emitting microcavity OLEDs (TEOLEDs) exhibit excellent color purity but suffer from severe angular color shifts. To overcome this, we introduce a nanotextured light modulation strategy using a nanoporous film (NPF) and an index-matched optically clear resin (OCR) for encapsulation. The nanotextured NPF enhances light outcoupling while suppressing cavity-induced angular dependence. As a result, the external quantum efficiency (EQE) is enhanced by 370% (from 8.5 to 31.6%), and angular color shift (Δu′v′) is reduced by 65.2% (from 0.046 to 0.016). By introducing a nanostructure-based light modulation technology, we have achieved a breakthrough in microcavity OLED performance, simultaneously improving both outcoupling and angular stability. Applied to flexible TEOLEDs, the device exhibits stable optical and mechanical performance under bending. This scalable and effective light modulation design enables highly efficient and color-stable flexible OLED displays for next-generation wearable applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-28107-1.Keywords: Microcavity OLEDs, Top-emission OLEDs, Light extraction, Photonics, Viewing angle dependence, Flexible OLEDSubject terms: Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Optics and photonics

Introduction

Active-Matrix Organic Light Emitting Diode (AMOLED) displays have emerged as a leading technology for modern portable and large-area devices, owing to their thin form factor, high contrast ratio, fast response time, and mechanical flexibility. Among various AMOLED configurations, top-emitting AMOLEDs (TEOLEDs) are particularly advantageous for high-resolution applications, as they achieve a higher aperture ratio by directing emission away from the substrate—independent of the underlying backplane routing1–6. However, TEOLEDs are inherently subject to optical interference effects, as a Fabry–Pérot-type microcavity is formed between the reflective bottom electrode and the semi-transparent top electrode7–9. This cavity selectively enhances certain wavelengths through resonant amplification, thereby improving color purity and narrowing the emission spectrum. Despite these advantages, the microcavity structure presents a critical drawback: a strong angular dependence of the emission spectrum caused by variations in optical path length with viewing angle. This results in undesirable spectral shifts most notably, a blue shift at oblique viewing angles10–15. Such angular color instability is especially problematic for next-generation displays, including foldable, rollable, stretchable, and large-area formats, where off-axis visibility is crucial to user experience16–20.

To address these limitations, nanoporous films (NPFs) have been explored as a strategy to redistribute emitted light over a broader angular range21–26. The internal nanopores of NPFs scatter waveguided modes and partially out-couple trapped light, thereby promoting Lambertian-like emission and mitigating angular color shifts. However, conventional NPFs fabricated via mist-assisted spin coating processes often exhibit non-ideal surface morphologies—specifically, irregular, concave hemispherical voids on their top surface that cause substantial diffuse back-scattering27–30.

To accurately evaluate the performance of a NPF, it’s more appropriate to apply it within a TEOLED structure. When the NPF is used on a Bottom-Emitting OLED (BEOLED), the film is exposed to the external environment, making it vulnerable to degradation from moisture and oxygen. Additionally, the significant refractive index mismatch between the air-filled nanopores and the glass substrate can cause light loss, which counteracts the film’s intended light extraction benefits.

In contrast, a TEOLED structure allows the NPF to be properly protected from the environment. By filling the nanopores with a material that has a matching refractive index, the reflection and light loss are minimized, allowing the full scattering effect of the NPF to be realized. Therefore, the true efficiency-boosting effect of the NPF can only be accurately assessed in a protected structure like a TEOLED, which minimizes external vulnerabilities and refractive index-related losses22.

These surface irregularities effectively lower the average refractive index of the NPF surface towards that of air (n = 1), resulting in a pronounced refractive index mismatch with the encapsulation glass (n = 1.5). A comparison between the Reference 2 device and Device 2 clearly highlights the unique effect of the nanoporous structure. The corrected EQE for Reference 2 was only 11.3%, whereas Device 2 achieved a high 31.6%. This significant difference indicates that simple refractive index matching, as in Reference 2, cannot substitute for the strong light scattering provided by the internal voids of the NPF. When placed adjacent to the glass lid, such NPFs induce multiple internal reflections, leading to haze formation, reduced forward light extraction, and over 25% efficiency loss22.

In this work, we propose a structurally engineered optical strategy in which the NPF is not formed directly on the OLED, but rather on the inner surface of the encapsulation glass. A UV-curable index-matching optically clear resin (OCR, n = 1.56) is subsequently applied to fill the concave surface voids of the NPF, effectively planarizing the surface morphology and minimizing interfacial scattering, while preserving the angular light redistribution enabled by the internal pores31–36. This inverted lamination design demonstrates substantial improvements in both angular color stability and device efficiency. The proposed architecture is compatible with mass production processes and readily scalable to flexible display formats. These results provide a viable strategy for resolving the intrinsic trade-off between spectral precision and angular performance in microcavity-based OLEDs.

As shown in Fig. 2a, the process began by spin coating the NPF onto the inner surface of a glass lid. We used a solution of cellulose acetate butyrate (CAB) in chloroform to form a porous matrix. During this mist-assisted spin coating process, a stream of water vapor was introduced onto the rotating CAB solution. This mist-assisted process created a network of nanoscopic voids throughout the solidified polymer film. To achieve a target film thickness of approximately 1 μm, the polymer concentration and spin rate were optimized to ~ 8 wt% and ~ 2000 rpm, respectively. Subsequently, an index-matching, UV-curable OCR was spin-coated over the NPF. The resin filled the voids on the NPF surface and, after UV curing, resulted in a planarized surface suitable for device lamination.

To precisely understand the observed enhancement in the device performance, blue shift in peak wavelength and the broadening of the FWHM in NPF based TEOLEDs, a comprehensive Q-factor analysis was performed. The Q-factor, fundamentally a measure of a resonant system’s energy Decay, provides critical insight into the spectral purity and lifetime of optical modes within a device; a higher Q-factor typically correlates with a sharper, more defined emission peak. This analysis was performed using 3D FDTD theoretical calculations, a strong computational method that enables the accurate simulation of light propagation and interaction within complex nanostructures. By meticulously analyzing the resonance Decay characteristics within the TEOLED structures, we were able to precisely extract the Q-factor values. These quantitative insights are crucial for deciphering the underlying nanophotonic mechanisms responsible for the observed modifications in the emitted light’s spectral properties.

The Reference 1 device exhibits a rapid Decay in resonance with lower intensity, indicating reduced light confinement within the TEOLED and consequently, lower device efficiency. In contrast, Fig. 5b shows that the Reference 2 device achieves higher resonance Decay and intensity. This enhancement is attributed to the presence of an OCR layer, which significantly improves light resonance within the TEOLED, thereby boosting device performance.

Similarly, Fig. 5d and f display the resonance plots for the fabricated NPF based TEOLEDs. Devices 1 and 2 demonstrate a comparable resonance Decay but with higher intensity. This is due to the random distribution of pores and air voids within the NPF, which induces strong light scattering. This scattering reduces the coherence of light within the effective cavity, leading to a much broader emission spectrum and, consequently, a lower Q-factor. Furthermore, the scattering phenomenon causes photons to deviate and escape, directly reducing their effective lifetime within the NPF resonator, which further contributes to a lower Q-factor.

Figure 5c and f present the Q-factor values obtained from both simulation and calculation. Figure 5c displays the simulated Q-factor values are 23.0 for Reference 1, 17.0 for Reference 2, 21.1 for the NPF TEOLED without OCR layer, and 38.3 for the NPF TEOLED with OCR layer. Concurrently, Fig. 5f illustrates the calculated Q-factor values are 24 for Reference 1, 24 for Reference 2, 20.3 for Device 1, and 17.6 for Device 2.

While the Q-factor values from both methods exhibit a similar trend, notable discrepancies are observed, particularly for Device 2. Specifically, the simulated Q-factor value at 530 nm is approximately 38, whereas the calculated Q-factor value at the same wavelength is approximately 17. This difference arises because the calculated Q-factor value is dependent on the device’s peak FWHM, as detailed in the supporting information. The simulated Q-factor analysis, on the other hand, relies on low-Q cavity values, where the electromagnetic fields Decay completely from the simulation within a reasonable FDTD simulation time. In such cases, the quality factor can be accurately determined from the Fourier transform of the field by identifying the resonance frequencies of the signal and measuring the FWHM of the resonant peaks.

These comprehensive simulation and calculation results, particularly the Q-factor analysis and resonance Decay plots, provide a cohesive explanation for the blue shift in peak wavelength and the broadening of the FWHM observed in our NPF-based TEOLEDs. They demonstrate how the altered light confinement and increased scattering within the NPF structures directly influence the spectral characteristics of the emitted light.

Discussion

This study presents a significant achievement in light extraction technology: the development of highly efficient NPF based TEOLEDs. By successfully addressing challenges related to low efficiency and poor angular independence as a result, the external quantum efficiency (EQE) is enhanced by 370% (from 8.5 to 31.6%), and angular color shift (Δu′v′) is reduced by 65.2% (from 0.046 to 0.016). Experimental results from fabricated devices, corroborated by spectroradiometer measurements, clearly demonstrate substantial improvements in EQE, CE, and PE. Comparing the high-performing fabricated Reference 1 device to the NPF-based TEOLED Device 2, we observe impressive enhancements: EQE increased from 8.5% to 31.6%, CE from 146.6 cd A−1 to 161.1 cd A−1, and PE from 125.1 lmW−1 to 141.7 lmW−1. The statement regarding the lowering of the effective refractive index is based on the presence of concave, cavity-like features on the NPF surface, which are initially filled with air prior to OCR application. This surface structure can be regarded as a composite medium of cellulose acetate butyrate (n = 1.48) and air (n = 1.0), resulting in an effective refractive index closer to that of air. This enhanced refractive index mismatch with the encapsulation glass (n = 1.5) contributes to the observed optical effects. Device 2, fabricating an innovative “NPF with OCR” approach, dramatically boosts overall performance. This approach leads to a 64.4% (Du′v′) and a 14.3% increase in current efficiency. Our research further demonstrates that flexible OLEDs are integrated with NPFs, and an OCR layer exhibit comparable characteristics to Device 2. The NPFs specifically enhanced the color coordinates and viewing angle independence of the flexible device, a critical advancement for future wearable applications. The synergistic combination of TEOLED architecture with the porous surface and air-trapped voids within the NPF significantly enhances device performance, a phenomenon further supported by optical simulations.

The meticulous optimization of key parameters enabled the realization of a pronounced microcavity effect, which not only improves efficiency but also facilitates the fine-tuning of the emission spectrum. The observed dependence of EL performance on cavity length underscores the intricate design considerations. The NPF with OCR layer crucially contributes to light extraction, a phenomenon consistently supported by 3D FDTD simulations, further amplifying the EL properties of the devices. The EL properties of the fabricated NPF devices exhibit extreme changes in angle independence and color coordinate shift characteristics. The changes in peak wavelength and FWHM were thoroughly investigated using Q-factor analysis and the resonance Decay of the cavity in the TEOLED, all aided by theoretical FDTD studies.

From an industrial perspective, the fabrication cost for a 25 cm² Nanoporous Polymer Film (NPF) is estimated to be less than $0.1. This extremely low raw material cost, driven by the use of common chemicals like Cellulose Acetate Butyrate and Tetrahydrofuran, highlights the technology’s strong potential for high scalability and industrial viability.

This cost advantage is a critical factor for the commercial competitiveness of NPF-based TEOLEDs, especially when compared to the expensive and complex lithography or specialized material processes used in other light extraction technologies. This suggests that NPF technology not only offers superior performance but also possesses a significant economic benefit, paving the way for large-scale, cost-effective production.

This work represents a remarkably successful approach for light extraction in green strong microcavity TEOLEDs. This phenomenon holds significant potential for adoption in designing next-generation commercial wearable OLED applications. Furthermore, this study not only addresses challenges associated with light extraction in OLEDs but also offers invaluable insights into the factors influencing overall device performance. Consequently, it lays a robust foundation for future advancements in material systems for stretchable organic light-emitting diodes, presenting unprecedented possibilities in the evolution of wearable display technology.

Methods

Optical simulation analysis

Mode analysis of ETL and HTL thickness against mode contribution as well as the power dissipation spectrum of the normalized in-plane wavevector versus wavelength was conducted with pristine microcavity TEOLEDs. Furthermore, optical power fraction simulation was executed by sweeping the emission wavelength from 380 to 700 nm by 1 nm steps with a TEOLED device. The designed OLED structure is identical to Reference 1 and Reference 2, which is further used for fabricating the NPF based TEOLED. The SEM raw data were applied in the simulation to demonstrate the emission related to the wavelength of NPF. (SETFOS) The outcoupled radiances of the fabricated Reference A and Reference B are demonstrated by simulation of ETL and HTL thickness variation (SETFOS).

FDTD simulations

The finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) method, a well-established and powerful tool for solving Maxwell’s equations, was employed to study the optical properties of our microstructures and nanostructures. For these simulations, we used a micro-sized simulation window. We directly imported the complex geometries of the porous surface and voids, derived from scanning electron microscopy (SEM) data of the nanoporous film (NPF) substrate. This ensured that our simulations captured all essential three-dimensional features of the NPF.

Numerical simulations were performed using commercial FDTD software (Lumerical Solutions Inc.). The FDTD analysis involved placing emitting dipoles of various orientations—along the x, y, and z directions—within the emitting layer. The z-direction, or azimuthal direction, was set perpendicular to the substrate surface. In our simulated data, we utilized full-wave FDTD modeling for both the porous surface and elliptical void structures, with diameters ranging from 200 to 500 nm, consistent with our SEM observations. Crucially, the refractive indices of all materials used in these simulations were based on experimentally determined parameters obtained from ellipsometer measurements, ensuring high fidelity to real-world conditions.

Characterization of OLED

J–V–L of OLED was measured by J–V–L system with spectroradiometer (CS-2000, Minolta). Information of EQE, current efficiency, power efficiency, EL spectra, and the color coordinates were all measured using the system with a spectroradiometer (CS-2000, Minolta) with power supply Keithley 2400. luminous intensity and Lambertian correction factors were measured by JALS purchased from JooAm Co.

Surface morphology and roughness

The surface of the deposited layers was analyzed using a UHR FE-SEM, S-4700, Hitachi, at the Kyung Hee University (KHU)Seoul, Korea, with an accelerating voltage of 10 kV.

Laser-induced confocal microscopy (LICM)

Carl Zeiss LSM800 confocal microscope, located at Kyung Hee University (Seoul, Korea), was employed for the laser-induced confocal microscopy (LICM) analysis. High-definition differential interference contrast (DIC) images were also obtained using this instrument.

Optical microscopy (OM)

OM analysis of the devices was conducted using an OM (Olympus BX51M Metallurgical Microscope, Lamp: U-LH100HG, U-RFL-T (Power Supply), KHU, Korea) to analyze the surface morphologies of the samples.

Haze analysis

The optical haze of NPFs was measured using haze meter (NDH-5000, Nippon Denshoku Industries).
