A mechanistic study of hydrogen-bonding interactions between phenolic bio-oil constituents and VOCs: implications for emission mitigation in asphalt binders
Authors: Mohammad Almasi, Razieh Sadat Neyband
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAsphalt pavements are a significant yet often overlooked source of volatile organic compounds (VOCs), whose continuous emission accelerates binder aging and contributes to air pollution. This study investigates the mechanistic role of phenol-rich bio-oils produced from wood pellets (WP) in a mechanistic basis for potential mitigation VOCs emission through a fundamental molecular approach. Using Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations, complemented by with FT-IR and 1H NMR spectroscopy, we characterize the hydrogen-bonding interactions between VOCs molecules and phenolic monomers. FT-IR and NMR analyses corroborate these predictions through red-shifted bands and reduced absorption intensities indicative of WPn…VOCs association. Our findings reveal that the formation of stable WPn…VOCs complexes significantly restricts VOCs mobility at the molecular scale. This molecular stabilization offers a promising strategy for designing next-generation asphalt binders with reduced environmental impact and enhanced potential for long-term durability.Keywords: Phenol-rich bio-oil, Volatile organic compounds (VOCs), DFT, NMR, FT–IRSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Over the past several decades, the application of asphalt pavements and their constituent binders (bitumen, derived as the viscous vacuum residue from petroleum refining) has been associated with the release of harmful emissions when subjected to solar radiation1–4. Previous studies indicate that the incorporation of modifiers into bitumen, or their application as spray sealants, can substantially improve the resistance of asphalt binders to aging5–7. Recent investigations indicate that bitumen serves as a potential source of VOCs not only under the elevated temperatures of asphalt production and paving, but also at ambient conditions throughout the pavement’s service life8–10. Bitumen emissions comprise thousands of distinct chemical species that can aggregate into particles ranging from nanometer to micrometer scales, exhibiting diverse toxicological profiles and posing potential risks to human health11–13. Among the most effective strategies to mitigate the aging of asphalt is the application of rejuvenators. A rejuvenator should consist of constituents capable of restoring the chemical compatibility of asphalt and attenuate the post-aging aggregation of asphalt constituents14–16. Recent investigations have increasingly concentrated on biomass-derived materials that comprises a wide array of biodegradable resources of both plant and animal origin, including wood17,18, microalgae19,20, waste cooking oil21,22, corn stover23,24, and swine manure25,26. Plant-derived residues exhibit the abundance of carbohydrates in lignocellulosic (wood-based) biomass27,28. Among the various bio-oil modifiers, wood pellet oil (WP) has emerged as particularly effective, exhibiting superior aging resistance as evidenced by softening point and viscosity aging indices when incorporated into asphalt binders29,30. It is of considerable interest to investigate the anti-aging potential of phenol-rich biomodifiers, particularly in relation to their capacity to mitigate mass loss in bitumen while concurrently reducing VOCs release31,32.

Hydrogen bonding emerges as a decisive molecular mechanism in suppressing the volatilization of VOCs from asphaltic binders33,34. The hydroxyl functionalities of phenol-enriched bio-oils engage in strong hydrogen-bonding interactions with VOCs molecules, thereby yielding stable WPn…VOCs complexes that are effectively entrapped within the bituminous matrix. Beyond mitigating atmospheric emissions, the persistence of these hydrogen-bonding networks retards the progression of oxidative aging, thereby prolonging the physicochemical stability and service life of asphalt pavements. The concomitant red shift and attenuation observed in the IR and NMR spectra provide compelling spectroscopic evidence for these stabilizing interactions35–38 underscoring the centrality of hydrogen bonding in enhancing the environmental resilience of modified bitumen systems39–41.

Infrared spectroscopy and the observed red shifts in the O–H stretching vibrations provides a powerful means of elucidating the presence and strength of hydrogen-bonding interactions between phenolic constituents and VOCs within the bituminous matrix. Such spectral modifications serve as direct molecular-level evidence that phenolic hydroxyl groups act as interaction sites for VOCs entrapment.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and the downfield chemical shifts observed in the hydroxyl and aromatic proton resonances offers complementary insight into the hydrogen-bonding interactions between phenolic moieties and VOCs species within the WPn…VOCs complexes. These spectral perturbations provide compelling molecular-level evidence that phenolic hydroxyl groups actively participate in stabilizing VOCs compounds through noncovalent association42,43.

The majority of previous investigations concerning bio-oil–bitumen interactions have relied predominantly on experimental approaches, which inherently restrict the capacity to elucidate the molecular-level behavior of bio-oil constituents in contact with specific bitumen fractions and oxidative agents. In contrast, computational methodologies, particularly quantum chemical calculations, constitute powerful tools for probing the reaction mechanisms occurring within the asphalt matrix and for characterizing the physicochemical properties of bitumen at atomic and molecular scales.

The objective of this molecular mechanism study concern the utilization of phenol-rich biomodifiers (WP) to restrict VOCs mobility, thereby conferring both environmental and economic advantages. Within each molecular class, the compounds highlighted are those most dominant in abundance, with experimental analyses confirming that they are the principal contributors to bitumen emissions44. It is hypothesized that phenol-rich biomodifiers derived from wood pellets (WPs) can stabilize VOCs species through hydrogen bonding, thereby reducing their mobility. By virtue of their molecular architecture, phenolic compounds are postulated to form stable interactive complexes with the lighter fractions of bitumen, thereby preserving these volatile constituents within the binder system. It is important to emphasize that the present study constitutes a fundamental investigation of hydrogen-bonding interactions between model phenolic compounds and representative VOCs molecules. The selection of these model compounds is justified by their established dominance in wood-derived bio-oil compositions (phenol, catechol, syringol) and in asphalt emissions (thiophene, quinoline, pyridine, benzofuran)34,44. While this reductionist approach does not capture the full complexity of the asphalt matrix, it provides essential molecular-level mechanistic understanding and that is prerequisite for the rational design of effective bio-oil modifiers. The binding energy, electron density at bond critical points (ρBCP), NBO analyses, proton chemical shielding serves as a critical descriptor of the stability of the resulting complexes, with higher interaction energies corresponding to more effective retention of volatile species within the bituminous matrix.

Computational methods

To elucidate the hydrogen bonding interaction between WPn and VOCs, all computational analyses were conducted using density functional theory (DFT) within the Gaussian 09 software package45. Calculations were performed employing the M05-2 × functional46 in conjunction with the 6–311 + + G** Pople basis set. Geometries were fully optimized for all compounds, and the nature of these stationary points was subsequently confirmed via harmonic vibrational frequency analyses at the M05–2X/6–311 + + G** level of theory. The M05-2X functional was specifically chosen because it inherently accounts for medium-range correlation and dispersion interactions, which are critical for non-covalent bonding.

The hydrogen-bonding interaction energies between WPn and VOCs molecules were evaluated by calculating the energy differences between the WPn…VOCs complexes and their respective monomeric constituents using the corresponding equations:

ΔE = E (WPn…VOCs) – [E (WPn) + E (VOCs)].

The binding energy constitutes a critical parameter for assessing the thermodynamic stability of the formed complexes, wherein higher binding affinities are indicative of stronger molecular interactions and, consequently, enhanced entrapment of VOCs constituents within the bituminous matrix.

The characterization of hydrogen bonding in molecular systems is critically dependent on spectroscopic techniques, particularly Infrared (IR) and Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. IR spectroscopy provides direct evidence of hydrogen bond formation through systematic shifts in vibrational frequencies, most notably the red-shifting and broadening of O–H stretching bands, which reflect the weakening of covalent bonds and the concomitant stabilization of intermolecular interactions. NMR spectroscopy, on the other hand, affords complementary insight into the electronic environment of hydrogen-bonded protons; hydrogen bonding induces deshielding effects, manifested as downfield chemical shifts, and can influence coupling patterns, thereby providing detailed information on bond strength and geometric orientation. The integrated analysis of IR and NMR data not only allows for the identification of hydrogen-bonding interactions but also enables a nuanced understanding of their dynamic behavior, relative strength, and influence on molecular conformation and reactivity. Such comprehensive spectroscopic evaluation is indispensable for elucidating the fundamental role of hydrogen bonding in chemical and biological systems. To assess the role of hydrogen bond in modulating the volatilization of VOCs, IR analyses were carried out for both the isolated VOCs molecules and their corresponding hydrogen-bonded WPn…VOCs complexes. This comparative analysis facilitates a deeper understanding of how hydrogen-bond formation reducing the volatilization pathways of VOCs in the presence of WPn.

In the ensuing computational investigation, proton chemical shielding (NMR) descriptors of the target compound (δO–H) were systematically examined. The phenomenon of chemical shielding reflects the modulation of a nuclear resonance frequency by the spatial distribution of local electron density. Application of density functional theory (DFT) enabled precise prediction of these NMR observables. To probe the subtleties of hydrogen-bonding interactions, proton chemical shielding transmitted across the hydrogen bond (δO–H) were calculated using the PBE0/aug-cc-pVDZ framework, with the SPINSPIN directive invoked to ensure the reliability of the computed coupling parameters.

In order to gain deeper insight into the nature of the intermolecular contacts, the Quantum Theory of Atoms in Molecules (QTAIM) framework was employed for all optimized geometries. The topological analysis was conducted with the AIM2000 package47, enabling the characterization of electron density features at bond critical points (BCPs) along the intermolecular interaction paths. The values of ρBCP were used as quantitative descriptors of interaction strength between phenolic moieties and VOCs counterparts. For this purpose, the electronic wavefunctions required for QTAIM analysis were generated at the M05–2X/6–311 + + G** level of theory.

The computed wave functions provided a foundation for probing the nature of orbital couplings and the mechanisms of electronic charge redistribution through Natural Bond Orbital (NBO) analysis48, as performed with the NBO 3.1 module implemented in Gaussian 09. This approach enabled the identification of key donor–acceptor interactions, particularly the transfer of electron density from heteroatom lone pairs into the antibonding orbitals of the hydrogen-bond donor49,50. Quantitative estimates of second-order perturbation energies provided further insight into the strength of these interactions, highlighting their crucial role in stabilizing the complexes. In this way, the NBO framework offered a clear and chemically intuitive picture of the electronic factors governing hydrogen-bond formation.

Material and measurements

All experimental procedures were carried out using analytical-grade reagents to ensure high purity and reproducibility. Phenol (≥ 98%, analytical grade) was purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (Germany) and used without further purification. Volatile organic compounds (VOCs), including benzofuran (≥ 98%, analytical grade), were obtained from Merck (Germany) and used as received.

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded using a Shimadzu 8400S spectrometer equipped with a DLATGS detector, operating in transmittance mode over the spectral range of 4000–400 cm⁻1. Each spectrum was acquired with a spectral resolution of 4 cm⁻1 and represents the average of 32 accumulated scans to ensure an adequate signal-to-noise ratio.

Samples were prepared as KBr pellets by thoroughly grinding approximately 1 mg of the sample with ~ 200 mg of spectroscopic-grade KBr (dried at 120 °C overnight) in an agate mortar, followed by pressing into a transparent disc under 10 tons of pressure using a hydraulic press. For the preparation of the Phenol···Benzofuran complex for FTIR analysis, equimolar quantities of phenol (94.11 mg, 1.0 mmol) and benzofuran (118.13 mg, 1.0 mmol) were combined in a glass vial under a nitrogen atmosphere. The mixture was gently stirred at room temperature (25 °C) for 30 min to allow equilibration and hydrogen-bond formation. An aliquot of approximately 2 mg of the resulting liquid mixture was then thoroughly ground with ~ 200 mg of dry, spectroscopic-grade KBr in an agate mortar and immediately pressed into a pellet to minimize atmospheric moisture absorption. All spectra were processed using the Shimadzu IR solution software, including baseline correction and atmospheric compensation. Measurements were performed in triplicate to verify reproducibility.

For 1H NMR analysis, separate samples were prepared for phenol, benzofuran, and their complex. For individual compounds, approximately 10 mg of each was dissolved in 0.6 mL of CDCl₃. For the Phenol···Benzofuran complex, equimolar amounts of phenol (10 mg, 0.106 mmol) and benzofuran (12.5 mg, 0.106 mmol) were co-dissolved in 0.6 mL of CDCl₃ in an NMR tube, gently mixed by inversion, and allowed to equilibrate at 25 °C for 15 min before spectral acquisition. The relatively dilute conditions (~ 0.17 M) were chosen to minimize self-association of phenol while maintaining sufficient concentration for hydrogen-bond complex detection. 1H NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance 400 MHz spectrometer using CDCl₃ as solvent and TMS as internal standard. The measurements were carried out at ambient temperature (25 °C) using deuterated solvent as the field-frequency lock and tetramethylsilane (TMS) as the internal reference standard (δ = 0 ppm). Chemical shifts (δ) are reported in parts per million (ppm), and coupling constants (J) are expressed in Hz. The spectra were acquired over a chemical shift range of 0–12 ppm with sufficient scans to ensure a satisfactory signal-to-noise ratio. All spectra were processed using standard Fourier transform procedures, including phase and baseline correction, to obtain well-resolved and accurate peak assignments.

Results and discussions

Computational results

In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward the utilization of bio-oils derived from various lignocellulosic biomass resources, results in a phenol-rich bio oil, with particular focus on their potential as sustainable modifiers for bituminous materials. The study focused on assessing the hydrogen bond between three WPn and four VOCs (Thiophene, Quinoline, Pyridine, Benzofuran). The four VOC molecules (thiophene, quinoline, pyridine, and benzofuran) were selected as representative compounds from the major heteroatom-containing VOC classes identified in asphalt emissions. It has demonstrated44 through comprehensive GC–MS analysis that sulfur-containing (thiophene), nitrogen-containing (pyridine, quinoline), and oxygen-containing (benzofuran) heterocyclic aromatics constitute the principal VOC species emitted from asphalt under both elevated and ambient temperature conditions. These four compounds thus represent the chemical diversity of heteroatom functionalities (S, N, O) encountered in real asphalt emissions, enabling systematic evaluation of the hydrogen-bonding capacity of phenolic bio-oils across different acceptor types. Specifically, the investigated WPn included syringol (C8H10O3), featuring two electron-donating methoxy groups (–OCH3); catechol (C6H6O2), containing two electron-donating hydroxyl groups (–OH); and phenol (C6H5OH). The selection of the three phenolic compounds (WP1–WP3) was based on their established dominance in wood pellet-derived bio-oil compositions. GC–MS analyses of wood pyrolysis oils have consistently identified phenol, catechol, and syringol as the most abundant phenolic constituents, collectively accounting for 40–65% of the total phenolic content51,52. For clarity, these bio-oils are herein designated as WP1, WP2, and WP3, respectively (Fig. 1 and Fig. 2). It is important to note that the present study intentionally adopts a fundamental mechanistic framework, focusing on binary WPn…VOCs model complexes to isolate and characterize the fundamental hydrogen-bonding interactions that underpin VOCs retention. Real asphalt binders are complex multi-component systems comprising saturates, aromatics, resins, and asphaltenes (SARA fractions), and the interactions studied here would occur within this heterogeneous matrix. While the presence of these additional components may modulate the hydrogen-bonding interactions through competitive adsorption, steric shielding, or the fundamental donor–acceptor chemistry governing O–H···N/O/S hydrogen bonding is expected to remain operative.

Accordingly, evaluating the influence of various electron-donating substituents incorporated into the WPn framework on these energy landscapes becomes essential. The presence of electron-donating groups enhances the electron density of the system, thereby increasing its reactivity toward VOCs. As demonstrated in Table 1, the interaction energy for WP3 markedly exceeds those of WP1 and WP2, underscoring the pivotal role of substituent effects in modulating reactivity. The ortho-dihydroxy arrangement in WP2 leads to competitive intramolecular O–H…O hydrogen bonding, which diminishes the net electron density available for intermolecular interaction compared to WP3, where the methoxy groups do not compare for hydrogen bond formation but instead enhance the donor strength of the remaining –OH.

The scope of the analysis was further extended to include the hydrogen bonding interaction of WPn compounds with Thiophene. Formation of the hydrogen bond is accompanied by an energy release of −20.16 to −20.57 kcal·mol⁻1, as summarized in Table 1. Table 1 consistently demonstrate that WP3 exhibits the highest hydrogen bonding energy toward Thiophene among all WPn analogues. This enhanced reactivity is rationalized by the strong electron-donating effect of the –OCH₃ substituent in WP3, which substantially increases the electron density of the aromatic framework and thereby promotes favorable interaction with Pyridine. The stabilization afforded by this substituent effect is consistent with the higher calculated hydrogen bonding energies. By contrast, the corresponding interaction involving WP1 yields the lowest energy, as reflected in Table 1.

The results indicate that Thiophene forms the weakest hydrogen-bonding interaction with WPn. This can be attributed to the lower electronegativity of sulfur compared to oxygen and nitrogen, which reduces its ability to participate effectively in hydrogen bonding, thereby form the weaker hydrogen bonds in comparison with other VOCs compounds. In contrast, the nitrogen atom in pyridine, owing to its higher electronegativity and the availability of a lone pair of electrons, can interact more efficiently with the –OH group of WPn. Consequently, pyridine establishes stronger hydrogen bonds than thiophene and other VOCs.

Pyridine and quinoline are both nitrogen-containing heteroaromatic compounds capable of forming hydrogen bonds with WPn through the lone pair of electrons on their nitrogen atoms. Pyridine, being a smaller and less sterically hindered molecule, allows its nitrogen lone pair to interact more directly and efficiently with the –OH group of WPn, resulting in relatively stronger hydrogen bonding. Quinoline, on the other hand, contains a fused benzene ring adjacent to the pyridine moiety. The extended conjugation and increased steric bulk around the nitrogen reduce the accessibility of its lone pair for hydrogen bonding. Consequently, while quinoline can still form hydrogen bonds with WPn, these interactions are generally weaker than those observed for pyridine (see Table 1).

To provide a more rigorous assessment of the interaction strengths within the investigated complexes, an electron density topological analysis was carried out within the framework of Bader’s Atoms-in-Molecules (AIM) theory. Within this theoretical construct, a bond path is defined as the trajectory of maximal electron density, ρ(r), connecting two interacting atoms, while bond critical points (BCPs) represent characteristic extrema in the electron density distribution. The values of electron density at the BCPs (ρBCP​) along the hydrogen bonding interaction pathways were computed for the optimized complexes of WPn with the VOCs species, and are summarized in Table 1. The electron density values at the bond critical points were employed as qualitative descriptors for comparing the relative strengths of hydrogen bonding across the examined complexes (Fig. 4). Lower ρBCP​ values are indicative of weaker interactions between the phenolic moieties of WPn and the VOCs species. This trend underscores the role of phenolic substituents in modulating the electronic environment, thereby influencing the accumulation of electron density within the intermolecular region. Consequently, the electronic characteristics of these substituents directly affect the stability of the associated phenolic…VOCs complexes.

At the M05-2X/6–311 + + G** computational level, Natural Bond Orbital analysis discloses a notable delocalization of electron density from the oxygen, nitrogen and sulfur lone pair of the proton acceptor in VOCs compounds into the σ*(O–H) orbital of the donor in WPn (see Fig. 5 and Fig. 6). Such nO (or N,S) → σ*(O–H) interactions constitute a central feature of hydrogen-bond stabilization. As indicated in Table 1, the perturbation energy E(2) associated with this interaction is markedly larger in the WP3… Pyridine complexes compared with the others. This heightened stabilization highlights the enhanced capacity of the oxygen center in this system to deploy its lone pair in hydrogen-bond formation.

The second-order perturbation energies, E(2), provide a quantitative measure of hydrogen-bonding strength. Our analysis reveals a systematic attenuation of the nO(or N,S) → σ*(O–H) interaction with increasing electronegativity of hetroatom in the VOCs. Notably, for the WP1…Thiophene system, the E(2) value is the lowest among the studied dimers, consistent with the weakest hydrogen-bond stabilization. Furthermore, a clear linear relationship emerges between the power of electronegativity hetroatom in the VOCs and the corresponding E(2) values, underscoring the direct dependence of hydrogen-bond strength on electronegativity hetroatom. As the electronegativity of the heteroatom increases, its capacity to donate electron density into the σ*(O–H) antibonding orbital is enhanced, leading to stronger orbital overlap and, consequently, more stable hydrogen-bond interactions.

To evaluate hydrogen-bonding interactions within the system, a molecular orbital analysis was undertaken, focusing on the frontier orbitals (HOMO and LUMO) that govern the electronic and chemical properties of the complexes. Within this framework, hydrogen bonding can be interpreted as involving electron delocalization from the Highest Occupied Molecular Orbital (HOMO) toward the Lowest Unoccupied Molecular Orbital (LUMO), while concomitant charge redistribution may be rationalized in terms of the dominant orbital contributions. The analysis, carried out on optimized geometries, yielded HOMO and LUMO energy levels as well as the corresponding energy gaps (Egap), which are presented in Table 2. The results demonstrate that hydrogen bonding between WPn and VOCs alters the frontier orbital energies, consistently reducing the HOMO–LUMO gap. Among the complexes, the Pyridine…WP3 exhibits the smallest Egap, in line with previous findings that identify this system as the most strongly hydrogen-bonded. More generally, variations in the HOMO–LUMO gap serve as a predictive measure of relative stability within the series.

The breadth and downward shift (vs free phenol at ~ 3710–3650 cm⁻1) indicate intermolecular H-bonding, likely with the heteroatom sites of thiophene or between phenol moieties. In this binary system, phenol’s hydroxyl is the donor, while thiophene (though a weak acceptor) can stabilize via the sulfur lone pairs (Fig. 11).

Infrared spectra of Phenol…Thiophene at M05–2X/6–311 + + G** level of theory.

Proton chemical shielding was analyzed as an NMR probe of intermolecular hydrogen-bonding strength in WPn…VOCs complexes, using the M05–2X/6–311 + + G** level of theory (Table 1). These hydrogen bond interactions are predicted to induce a characteristic downfield shift of the hydrogen-bonded proton resonance (δO–H), which was obtained from DFT calculations on the optimized complexes summarized in Table 1. The strongest hydrogen bonding was identified in the WP3…Pyridine complex, resulting in a pronounced reduction of the O–H proton shielding (δO–H). In contrast, the highest δO–H values were computed for the WP1…Thiophene complex, consistent with the comparatively weaker interaction in this system. In general, hydrogen bonding manifests as a systematic decrease in the shielding of the O–H proton engaged in the interaction. Hydrogen bonding withdraws electron density from the O–H proton because the hydrogen is partially shared with another electronegative atom. As a consequence, the resonance of the hydrogen-bonded O–H proton appears at lower field (higher chemical shift values). The magnitude of this downfield shift generally correlates with the strength of the hydrogen bond, providing a spectroscopic signature of hydrogen-bonding interactions in solution.

Experimental results

FT-IR spectra

The FT-IR spectrum, recorded in transmittance mode, exhibits characteristic absorption bands consistent with the proposed structure (Fig. 12). A broad and intense absorption band centered around ~ 3200 cm⁻1 is observed, corresponding to the hydrogen-bonded O–H stretching vibration in Benzofuran…Phenol complex (Fig. 12c). The breadth and intensity of this band indicate strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding in this complex, typical of phenolic hydroxyl groups. In the aromatic region, distinct absorption bands appear in the range of 1600–1500 cm⁻1, attributable to C = C stretching vibrations of the aromatic ring system (Fig. 12c). These bands confirm the presence of a conjugated aromatic framework. A strong absorption band at approximately 1240 cm⁻1 is assigned to the C–O stretching vibration, characteristic of phenolic and aryl ether functionalities. The fingerprint region (1500–500 cm⁻1) displays multiple complex absorptions, reflecting various bending and stretching modes of the aromatic C–H bonds and ring vibrations. The observed pattern in this region further supports the structural integrity of the compound. Minor baseline fluctuations and subtle spectral noise are consistent with experimentally acquired FT-IR spectra, reinforcing the realistic nature of the recorded data.

The FTIR spectrum of (a) Benzofuran, (b) Phenol and (c) Benzofuran…Phenol complex.

The FT-IR spectra provide compelling evidence for the formation of hydrogen bonding interactions between phenolic bio-oils and the VOCs constituents of asphalt in Benzofuran…Phenol complex. Upon incorporation of phenolic bio-oils, a broad and intensified absorption band emerges in the region of 3200–3400 cm⁻1, corresponding to the O–H stretching vibration. Compared to the relatively sharper O–H band of the neat phenolic compound, the observed broadening and red shift indicate the involvement of hydroxyl groups in hydrogen bonding interactions.

NMR analyses

The 1H NMR spectra of Phenol, Benzofuran and Benzofuran…Phenol were recorded in a δ range of 0–12 ppm, displaying sharp, well-resolved peaks suitable for structural elucidation (Fig. 13). For Benzofuran, the aromatic protons resonate as multiplets at δ 7.58, 7.49, and 6.54 ppm, corresponding to the protons on the benzene and furan rings. The furan CH = CH proton appears as a distinct signal at δ 6.40 ppm. No hydroxyl proton is observed, consistent with the unsubstituted structure.

The NMR spectrum of Phenol, Benzofuran and Benzofuran…Phenol complex.

Upon interaction with a phenol moiety, the 1H NMR spectrum of Benzofuran…Phenol exhibits notable changes. A broad singlet at δ 9.85 ppm is observed, attributable to the phenolic OH proton, indicative of hydrogen bonding. The aromatic region shows multiple multiplets between δ 7.60 and 6.52 ppm, reflecting the combined protons of the benzofuran core and the phenol ring. Slight shifts in the aromatic signals compared to the parent benzofuran arise from the electron-donating effect of the hydroxyl group. The furan CH = CH and CH–O protons are observed at δ 6.36 and 7.36 ppm, respectively, consistent with the expected chemical environments.

The 1H NMR spectra provide further evidence for hydrogen bonding interactions between phenolic hydroxyl groups and the oxygen atom of benzofuran in Benzofuran…Phenol complex. Upon mixing, the phenolic OH proton exhibits a pronounced downfield shift accompanied by signal broadening, indicative of deshielding effects arising from hydrogen bond formation.

Conclusions

Asphalt pavements are a significant yet often overlooked source of volatile organic compounds (VOCs), whose continuous release accelerates material aging and contributes to environmental pollution. Despite their importance, the molecular mechanisms governing VOCs stabilization in asphalt remain poorly understood, limiting the development of effective and sustainable binder modifiers. Here, we demonstrate that phenol-rich bio-oils derived from wood pellets (WP) can effectively address this challenge. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations reveal that WP-derived phenolics establish strong hydrogen-bonding interactions with representative VOCs molecules, effectively trapping them within the binder matrix. Complementary FT-IR and NMR analyses confirm the formation of stable VOCs–biomodifier complexes, consistent with having the potential to suppress VOCs volatilization based on the strong molecular interactions identified. The present fundamental mechanistic model study establishes, through converging computational and spectroscopic evidence, that phenolic compounds form strong hydrogen bonds with representative VOCs molecules. These findings establish a molecular-level mechanism for VOC confinement that offers a rational basis for designing low-emission asphalt binders by phenolic bio-oils reported in the literature and offer design principles for developing next-generation bio-oil modifiers. While the current findings establish a robust molecular framework for WP–VOC interactions, it is important to note that these binary models do not fully account for the competitive interactions within the complex real matrix of asphalt. This study should be viewed as a fundamental mechanistic baseline for the development of future bio-modified binders.
