Determinants of cultural significance in consumed wild mammals: a case study from the Lacandon Forest, Mexico
Authors: Yasminda García del Valle, Eduardo J Naranjo, Felipe Ruan-Soto, María Silvia Sánchez-Cortés, Arturo Carrillo-Reyes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWild mammals have constituted a widely used source of protein for most of the world’s cultures. Although there is no consensus on why some mammal species are more important for people than others, evidence provided by the discipline of ethnozoology suggests that distinct cultures value species differently, probably based on motivations and interests relevant for each group. In this paper we analyze the factors involved in the valuation of wild mammals consumed by two culturally distinct groups (Lacandon and Mestizo) in the Lacandon Forest, Chiapas, Mexico. We test the hypothesis that, due to different historical processes of each group, the Lacandones will prefer a larger number of species, favoring reasons such as consumption frequency, knowledge transmission, and capture techniques, whereas the Mestizos will favor other factors such as the commercial value of selected species.MethodsWe interviewed 189 adult residents (87 lacandones and 102 mestizos) of the study area to obtain seven indicators and two indices to assess the relative significance of the reasons why they value mammals: TaI = Taste indicator; HuI = Hunting indicator; CoI = Consumption indicator; KTI = Knowledge transmission indicator; HFI = Healthy food indicator; CoVI = Commercial value indicator; and CaTI = Capture technique indicator. After that, we interviewed 40 residents per community selected randomly. A Cultural Significance Index (CSI) was proposed and compared to the Mention Index (MI). CSI values were used to run cluster analyses and principal component analyses. A correlation analysis was also run to test for potential relationships between the specific MI values and each indicator.ResultsEight consumed mammal ethnotaxa were recorded in the study area; six en Lacandon communities and five in Mestizo communities. Spotted paca (Cuniculus paca), white-tailed deer (Odocoileus virginianus), and collared peccary (Pecari tajacu) were the most frequently used species for food. Consumption and commercial value were the most relevant indicators in consumed mammal valuation by Lacandon and Mestizo interviewees. Spotted paca was the most highly rated specie in the CSI, followed by white-tailed deer, among both Lacandones and Mestizos. The only differences between the populations were for the KTI, with higher values among Lacandones, and the CaTI, with higher values among Mestizos.ConclusionsLacandones and Mestizos do not experience marked differences in the reasons they value mammals or in the composition of the most important species. Methodologically, the seven indicators suggested by the interviewees and the two indices applied in this study were useful to perform a comprehensive assessment of the reasons why the residents of the Lacandon Forest value wild mammals.Keywords: Consumed mammals, Ecological ethnozoology, Ethnobiology, Indicators of cultural significance

Background

Wildlife-human interactions give rise to a series of complex relationships that shape different aspects of daily life. A fundamental dimension of these relationships is utilitarian; these utilitarian dynamics acquire cultural characteristics over time and are influenced by historical processes that both humans and wild animals experience in relation to one another [1, 2]. Wild mammals have constituted a widely used source of protein for most of the world’s cultures [3]. Although there is no consensus on why some mammal species are more important for people than others, evidence provided by the discipline of ethnozoology suggests that distinct cultures value species differently based on motivations and interests relevant to each group [4–6]. Several authors have pointed out that particular mammal species are considered more important for human consumption than others, producing species lists ranked by order of cultural importance [3, 7]. Those authors suggest that specific mammalian importance value depends on each cultural group’s criteria. However, until now these criteria have been poorly evaluated quantitatively.

This situation is challenging in two ways. From a methodological point of view, there is a need for the development of quantitative and qualitative tools for evaluating specific cultural significance. From an ethnographic perspective, there is an interest to explore if the assessment patterns for mammals differ across cultural groups coexisting in one region.

Methodologically, the quantitative ethnobiology school has proposed different indices to identify the cultural significance of species [8]. Indicators such as the mention frequency of each species in interviews have been used for their relative ease and efficacy [9]. Even though these proposals have significantly contributed to the theoretical and methodological advance of ethnobiology, they have two main limitations. First, indicators such as the mention frequency and consensuses among informants [9–11] give little or no information on the reasons why some species are better regarded by users than others [8]. Second, the so-called cultural significance indices [12, 13] and those describing use values [14, 15] among others, explore the reasons for cultural ranking using few indicators selected from an etic perspective in which the researcher subjectively selects their own indicators according to their experience, regardless of local criteria. The composed indices [8, 16] previously used in ethnobotanical studies have emerged as tools allowing the understanding and analysis of the reasons why people assign differential values to their resources on the basis of multiple indicators.

In the case of mammals, Pinto Marroquín et al. [17] proposed an index to assess cultural significance of two primate species in a Nature Reserve in eastern Mexico. They developed a composed index based on 11 cultural variables or sub-indices: Order of mention frequency, Medicinal significance, Significance as companion animal, Commercial significance, Food significance, Touristic significance, Significance in cosmovision, Emotional significance, Interest in conservation, Ecological significance, and Perceived abundance. This framework enabled the authors to determine which variables were most relevant in shaping the valuation of these primates.

Garibay-Orijel et al. [8] suggested that indicator selection should be a process built from an emic perspective (i.e., from the local users’ point of view). Accordingly, we believe that indicators used to understand the most important aspects in fauna valuation should be those that hold meaning for local hunters and consumers of wildlife. Toward this end, qualitative approaches are crucial. By following these approaches (through semi-structured and informal interviews arising from a base of participant observation) it is possible to amass data that allows for better understanding of these valuations and that we can revisit later through quantitative and representative exercises.

From an ethnographic perspective, it has been documented that Indigenous communities of the Americas value wildlife species based on different criteria than those of modern western societies [14]. For many indigenous cultures, worldviews (cosmovisiones; i.e., the particular ways to explain the world and the elements within) play relevant roles, subconsciously influencing their choice of valuable species [18]. García del Valle et al. [3] found no significant differences in the numbers and identities of mammal species regarded as important by Lacandon and Mestizo interviewees, two groups with different traditions coexisting in the Lacandon Forest. On the one hand, Lacandons are an Indigenous group that are thought to have inhabited the present-day Lancandon Forest for at least 300 years; many ethnobiological studies have confirmed that the Lacandons have ample traditional ecological knowledge of local resources. On the other hand, Mestizos arrived in the region within the last 60 years, having come from areas with different environmental conditions and having learned how to take advantage of the resources in their new territory [3]. The most frequently consumed mammals were essentially the same in both cases: spotted paca, peccaries, white-tailed deer and nine-banded armadillo (Cuniculus paca, Tayassuidae, Odocoileus virginianus, and Dasypus novencinctus). However, the question remains as to whether Lacandons and Mestizos value the mammal species they consume for the same reasons, or whether each group—shaped by its own tradition and history—relies on different variables for such valuation.

In this paper, we propose the use of a novel Cultural Significance Index (CSI) for consumed wild mammals derived from seven indicators regarded as significant by Lacandon and Mestizo residents of four communities of the Lacandon Forest, Chiapas, Mexico. This index was used to assess the cultural significance of mammals and to compare the relative weight of the indicators involved for each species. The usefulness of this index compared to mention frequency is also discussed. We hypothesize that Lacandons and Mestizos differ in variables they consider relevant when assessing the mammal species they consume. In this sense, we expect that, due to their different historical processes, the Lacandons would have a preference for a greater diversity of species, favoring reasons such as consumption frequency, knowledge transmision, and capture techniques, while the Mestizos would favor reasons such as the commercial value of selected species.

Methods

The study was conducted in four communities of the Lacandon Forest, Chiapas, in southern Mexico (Fig. 1). This region has an elevation gradient from 100 to 900 masl, a tropical climate with mean annual temperatures of 23–27 °C, and precipitations over 2,500 mm per year. The predominant vegetation types originally were tropical rainforest in the lowlands and montane forest in the highlands [19]. Two of the communities visited were ejido Playón de la Gloria (pop. size = 209) and ejido Reforma Agraria (pop. size = 145), both with Mestizo inhabitants. The territories of both communities contain rainforest fragments of different sizes and ages surrounded by pasturelands for livestock production and farming plots. However, there is some continuity in the vegetation cover since both are adjacent to the Montes Azules Biosphere Reserve (MABR). Although the founders of ejido Reforma Agraria were originally Chinanteco Indians from Oaxaca state, most of the current residents consider themselves Mestizo, as is the case in Playón de la Gloria. Most residents of both ejidos are cattle ranchers and farmers, and a few work as providers of tourism services [19, 20]. The Lacandon Maya communities of Naha (pop. size = 198) and Metzabok (pop. size = 96) lie within federal protected areas created in 1998. Their territories are covered by fragments of rainforest, cloud forest, and pine forest interspersed with secondary vegetation and small farming areas. It is important to note that in both communities, the forest cover—while relatively continuous (just over 3400 ha in Naha and 2900 ha in Metzabok)—is completely isolated by pastures dedicated to cattle breeding in neighboring Tseltal ejidos (communities). Residents of both communities are mainly seasonal farmers (they also raise backyard poultry but do not have livestock) and providers of tourism services [20]. In both the Lacandon and Mestizo communities, the National Commission of Natural Protected Areas (CONANP) has a strong presence; they carry out different activities related to community organization, dissemination, production, and biodiversity monitoring with the local population.

Communities of study in the Lacandon Forest, Chiapas, Mexico

Fieldwork was conducted from January to December 2015 with previous permission granted by local and federal authorities. Using an inductive ethnographic approach [21], we selected as many adult residents as possible to be interviewed in the four communities. We applied semi-structured interviews with 189 adult residents (55 in Nahá, 32 in Metzabok, 52 in Playón de la Gloria, and 50 in Reforma Agraria) who were selected through a snowball sampling technique [21] to gather qualitative information about their reasons to value consumed mammal species. This information was analyzed by a constant comparison of categories to identify patterns (other results derived from these qualitative interviews can be found in García del Valle et al.) [3, 22]. Through these semi-structured interviews we identified and selected seven indicators that interviewees regarded as the most relevant to value the cultural significance of consumed mammals, which later were used to create a CSI. The indicators selected were: (1) Taste (TaI), suggested whether the species tasted good or not; (2) Hunting (HuI), valued the effort needed to hunt or catch the animal; (3) Consumption (CoI), quantified the consumption frequency of the species (e.g., twice or more a year, once a year, every five years); (4) Knowledge Transmission (KTI), told us if species consumption was a traditional practice that had remained for generations or decades within the community; (5) Healthy Food (HFI), indicated if wild meat was regarded as clean and safe for human consumption provided it was free of hormones, chemicals, and endoparasites; (6) Commercial Value (CoVI), showed if wild meat was locally or regionally sold; and (7) Capture Technique (CaTI), quantified the techniques employed for hunting or capturing mammals (Table 1).

After the semi-structured interviews, structured interviews divided into three segments were conducted with 40 residents per community. These participants were selected randomly from a list of inhabitants. Due to the small population size of the communities, some interviewees were repeats, having been interviewed previously for the earlier exercise. The first segment was designed to gather sociodemographic data such as gender, ethnicity, age, religion, education, and origin of the interviewees. In the second segment, we compiled free lists of mammals consumed by interviewees and their families. The last segment included multiple-choice questions with similar weight to assess each of the seven indicators using either three (0, 5, 10), or four (0, 3.3, 6.6, 10) possible scores corresponding to the available answers (see Table 1). Mean values from all interviews for each indicator and consumed species were calculated to build a Cultural Significance Index (CSI) using the equation: TaI + HuI + CoI + KTI + HFI + CoVI + CaTI. This equation was based on the composed indices previously developed by Pieroni [16] and Garibay-Orijel et al. [8] for ethnobotanical studies. In addition, we estimated a Mention Index (MI) using the frequency of mentions for each species divided by the total number of residents interviewed during the study (N = 160).

CSI values were used to run cluster analyses (model UPGMA) and principal component analyses (PCA) using the NTSYS package (Numerical Taxonomy and Multivariate Analysis System package, version 2.11). These analyses were applied for each species and indicator to assess both their importance for human consumption and the main reasons involved in that consumption across communities. A correlation analysis was also run to test for potential relationships between the specific MI values and each indicator. Finally, both indices (CSI and MI) were compared among species, communities, and cultural groups to identify their effectiveness to assess the cultural importance of mammals consumed in the study area.

Results

Eight ethnotaxa (local taxa) of consumed mammals with 10 or more mentions in the interviews were recorded in the four communities (Table 2). Five of those ethnotaxa corresponded to a single species: spotted paca (Cuniculus paca), nine-banded armadillo (Dasypus novemcinctus), Central American agouti (Dasyprocta punctata), white-nosed coati (Nasua narica), and raccoon (Procyon lotor). The remaining three ethnotaxa corresponded to multiple species belonging to three families: Cervidae, including red brocket deer (Mazama temama) and white-tailed deer (Odocoileus virginianus); Tayassuidae, including collared peccary (Pecari tajacu) and white-lipped peccary (Tayassu pecari); and Sciuridae, comprising grey squirrel (Sciurus aureogaster), brown squirrel (S. deppei), and flying squirrel (Glaucomys volans). All ethnotaxa were present in the Lacandon Forest (Table 3). Six of the eight ethnotaxa mentioned above were recorded in the Lacandon communities (Naha and Metzabok), while five were registered in the Mestizo communities (Playon de la Gloria and Reforma Agraria). The raccoon was mentioned only by interviewees of the latter two communities, while the Central American agouti and the white-nosed coati were cited only by Lacandon residents (Tables 4 and 5).

Taste indicator (TaI)

Interviewees distinguished mammals as the most important wildlife group for meat supply, and the spotted paca was at the top of the list, being regarded as the only taxon with “excellent taste” (TaI = 9.5) in the four communities combined. The raccoon was the only species referred as “tasteless” (TaI = 6.4), while the remaining taxa were considered “good taste” animals (TaI = 7.1–8.9). The spotted paca was the species with the best taste in both Lacandon and Mestizo communities, followed by deer, peccaries and armadillos.

Hunting indicator (HuI)

Eighty-five of all interviewees considered that no mammal species was easy to hunt or capture. This was mainly due to the nocturnal and shy habits of most consumed mammals, which makes them difficult to find. The raccoon, nine-banded armadillo, spotted paca, white-nosed coati, Central American agouti, rabbits, squirrels, and peccaries were regarded as difficult to hunt (HuI = 5.1–7.27). Deer were classified as the most difficult mammals to hunt in the study area (HuI = 4.15). This was reported in all four communities.

Consumption indicator (CoI)

The most frequently hunted mammal in the study area was the spotted paca, which on average was consumed more than twice a year per interviewee (CoI = 7.54). The other seven ethnotaxa were consumed at least once a year (6.41–7.33). Among the Lacandon respondents, the spotted paca was the only species used more than twice a year (7.71), while the remaining five ethnotaxa were used at least once a year (6.51–7.49). For Mestizo residents, the same five ethnotaxa were consumed at least once a year, while the raccoon and the nine-banded armadillo were used over twice a year (> 7.50).

Knowledge transmission indicator (KTI)

Values of KTI over 7.50 for the eight ethnotaxa considered in this analysis suggest that their use as important sources of protein is a practice that has been transmitted between at least two or three generations. Nevertheless, KTI values for the eight ethnotaxa were generally higher among the Lacandon (> 9.0) compared to the Mestizo (7.50–7.84), with the spotted paca again the species with the highest average.

Healthy food indicator (HFI)

The mean value of HFI (7.5) across communities showed that all eight mammal ethnotaxa analyzed were conceived as suppliers of clean meat for safe consumption, assuming that it did not contain harmful chemical substances or parasites. For both Mestizo and Lacandon interviewees, the meat produced in farms was considered unhealthy. The highest HFI values corresponded to the spotted paca and deer.

Commercial value indicator (CoVI)

Lacandon and Mestizo respondents pointed out that there was not a stable market for wild meat. Wild meat sale is illegal in Mexico and many people in the study area are aware of this. However, when hunters had the opportunity, some of them sold the meat of paca, deer, peccaries, agouties and armadillos at low prices, obtaining CoVI values between 3.33 and 6.07. Species such as the white-nosed coati and raccoon had no commercial value in the study area and were hunted only for household consumption.

Capture technique indicator (CaTI)

Different techniques for capturing mammals were described by interviewees of the four communities, including searching with dogs, diurnal and nocturnal walking with machetes with or without dogs, specific trapping, and most frequently, the use of rifles and shotguns. Pacas, peccaries, armadillos, and agouties were hunted using three to five techniques (CaTI > 3.33). The other species were captured using only one or two techniques (< 3.33). Lacandon hunters killed pacas, agouties, and peccaries using three to five techniques, while Mestizo hunters took pacas, peccaries and armadillos using more techniques than those used for the rest of the consumed mammals.

Mention index (MI)

Pacas (MI = 9.35), deer (9.29), peccaries (7.87), and armadillos (6.29) obtained by far the highest values of the Mention Index. These species were the same in Lacandon and Mestizo communities. In our survey, 50% of the interviewees mentioned these species, while the other mammals were mentioned by less than 26% of respondents.

Cultural significance index (CSI)

Once all indicators estimated in our study were integrated, we obtained a CSI for each ethnotaxon. We found that for all communities combined, CSI varied from 44.36 for peccaries to 50.49 for pacas (Table 2). After pacas, the most culturally important species were agouties (46.94) and armadillos (46.65). For Lacandons, the most important species were the paca (50.49), deer (47.48), and peccaries (46.39), while for Mestizos, the highest values were assigned to the paca (49.89), deer (47.80), and armadillos (48.82). Correlation analyses applied to MI and each indicator showed that the strongest associations were between MI and TaI, CoVI, HFI, and CaTI. In contrast, HuI, CoI and KTI were negatively correlated with MI. The most frequently mentioned mammals had lower values for the indicators HuI, CoI, and KTI (Table 6). Phenograms obtained through similarity analyses allowed to identify groups A (paca and deer), and B (the remaining six ethnotaxa) for all communities combined (Fig. 2). The composition of group A was the same for both Lacandon and Mestizo respondents. Within group B, the most similar species were peccaries and squirrels.

Principal component analyses (Fig. 3) suggest that component 1 explained 46.9% of variation, segregating deer, pacas, agoutis, peccaries, and squirrels from the rest of the species (coatis, raccoons, and armadillos). The indicators with the heaviest values in this segregation were TaI, Hui, and CoVI. Principal component 2 explained 24.3% of variation, separating deer from all other species. In this case, the main indicators were CaTI, KTI, and HuI. For the Lacandon communities, component 1 explained 46.6% of variation, where pacas and deer were set apart from the other consumed mammals. The primary indicators in this case were HFI, CoVI, and TaI. Component 2 explained 25.8% of variation separating pacas, agoutis, armadillos, and coatis form peccaries and deer. The most important indicators of component 2 were CaTI, HuI, and KTI. For Mestizo communities, component 1 explained 62.2% of variation and was composed by peccaries, pacas, and deer. The heaviest indicators for this component were CoVI, CoI, and HUI. Component 2 accounted for 28.3% of variation and included deer, being HFI and HuI the most important indicators. Pooling all data, we found that principal component 1 explained 92.5% of total variation, segregating TaI, HFI, KTI, and CoI from the other indicators (Fig. 4).

Our results suggest that the spotted paca is by far the most important consumed mammal in the Lacandon forest, obtaining the highest values of Cultural Significance (CSI) and Mention (MI) among interviewees in the four communities.

Discussion

Working with two populations that have experienced two very different and distinct historical processes, a substantial difference between the Lacandon and Mestizo populations was expected; however, this was not the case. While the Lacandons identified one more edible species than the Mestizos, essentially the richness and composition of their diets was the same.

The most frequently mentioned mammals in the four communities of study were basically the same species reported as most consumed in other localities of the Lacandon Forest [6, 23], and in other areas across the Neotropics [7, 24–27]. Using different methods and analyses, our results and previous studies coincide in concluding that pacas, deer, peccaries, armadillos, and agoutis are the primary mammals hunted in an opportunistic way in the study area. Our results support the previous assumption that the paca was the most important consumed mammal in the four communities visited, obtaining the highest values of MI and CSI. Among the reasons explaining this result are the excellent taste and quality of paca meat, its high hunting frequency, a greater diversity of capture methods employed on this mammal, and its high commercial value in the Lacandon Forest. Pacas may weigh up to 7 kg, and their high quality as food sources has been demonstrated in previous studies throughout its distribution range [27–32].

Deer were the second most important group of mammals consumed in our study area. They had low values for the indicators related to ease of capture due to the higher effort and costs (e.g., more people and firearms) needed for hunting them. There is abundant literature on the great importance of the white-tailed deer as a source of animal protein, hides, hunting practices, traditions, and rituals in diverse cultures across North and Central America, and in Mexico in particular [23, 33–35]. This mammal is still one of the preferred targets for subsistence and sport hunters in Mexico, where there are many wildlife management units (UMA for its name in Spanish) [36–38]. The white-tailed deer was regarded as difficult to find and hunt by interviewees, probably because of its low density and limited distribution in the Lacandon Forest. This species moves efficiently through openings and corridors between forest fragments, secondary vegetation, and farmlands [39].

On the contrary, the nine-banded armadillo was the most easily captured mammal in the study area, probably because it may adapt well lo live in disturbed forests, farmlands and secondary vegetation, coexisting with people in rural areas. It is common to find armadillo tracks along dirt roads and trails in such areas, where farmers spot and hunt this mammal whenever possible [40]. The collared peccary is one of the consumed species with less commercial value because it is hunted whenever possible by both Lacandon and Mestizo residents. This has been documented in previous studies in the Lacandon Forest and other regions of Mexico [e.g. 41]. In our study area, there have been relatively few confirmed records of wild mammal trade, mainly hides and meat of paca and deer, and occasionally young spider monkeys as pets [23, 40, 41]. However, more detailed studies in the region are needed on this particular topic.

In general terms, the indicators for which all analyzed species had high values were HFI, TKI, TaI, and CoI. These were the same indicators segregated by the PCA analyses for the species with the highest cultural importance values (Fig. 4). Such indicators fit well in the traditional patterns of wildlife use by many rural residents across Latin America, which are linked to the selective consumption of the species with the best taste. This knowledge has been transmitted through generations over long time periods [7, 26, 42, 43].

We did not detect significant differences between Lacandon and Mestizo interviewees in reference to the species involved with the indicators TaI, HuI, KTI, HFI, CoVI, and CaTI. Both cultural groups essentially valued the same species, which agrees with the results of previous studies conducted in Mexico and other Latin American countries [26, 44–46]. Although they are two peoples with different cultural traditions, their perceptions of what constitutes healthy food and its flavor do not vary. This is perhaps because these perceptions are based on the characteristics of the species themselves and, to a lesser extent, on cultural differences in taste [22, 25, 37]. The absence of differences for the frequency of consumption (CoI) and the commercial value (CoVI) in both Lacandon and Mestizo communities may be explained by the strong and permanent presence of both governmental and civil society conservation agencies for almost half a century [19, 22, 44]; even the commercialization of these species is considered a crime by current Mexican laws. Noss et al. [47] suggested that quality of life and the nutritional condition of many rural communities would be severely affected if they did not have access to the wildlife resources available in their territories.

The only differences between indicators in the Lacandon and Mestizo communities were related to knowledge transmission (KTI) and hunting techniques (HFI). We estimated higher values for the Lacandon than for the Mestizo respondents in the knowledge transmission indicator for all species. The residents of Naha and Metzabok have maintained many of their traditional practices and knowledge about wildlife [48], which explains the high values obtained for that indicator. On the other hand, Mestizos had higher values for HFI. Both Playón de la Gloria and Reforma Agraria are made up of migrants from other regions of Mexico and Guatemala. Because of this, many of their practices are a mix of traditions created in different contexts. As such, it follows that they would have a larger variety of techniques, strategies, and hunting tools, such as different firearms, and the use of dogs or traps of complex design. In contrast, the Lacandons are a group that has remained relatively isolated from other traditions for at least 200 years, maintaining their own techniques such as the use of different types of arrows specific for each animal.

In methodological terms, ethnozoology worldwide has been remarkably prolific in understanding the relationships between humans and animals from various perspectives, including the ways in which fauna are named and classified, the organisms used in healing, their symbolic role, and the management and processes of domestication—all from more qualitative perspectives [49]. On the other hand, there is still a need for collaborative research that uncovers the emic perspectives of ethnobiological studies. While quantitative ethnozoology has delved into the study of extraction rates and some indices that value fauna [17, 49], it needs to continue research with different methodologies that allow for a better understanding of the mechanisms underlying the differential valuations of fauna.

The mention frequencies and their correspondent indices (MI) seem to be efficient indicators when a list of the culturally relevant species in a region is available [11]. Through these free lists, it becomes feasible to gather relevant social information for wildlife conservation within a relatively short period of time. In our case, the list of consumed mammals was very similar to that obtained by several authors in different localities of the Lacandon Forest and other Neotropical study sites, even in relatively the same order of importance found in this survey. We acknowledge that the calculation of the CSI used in this study is somewhat demanding in time and effort, particularly if the interviewees consume a high number of mammal species. Nonetheless, this index is more comprehensive and inclusive than the Mention Index (MI) and proved helpful to assess the weight of each indicator selected in valuing the cultural significance of wildlife species, constituting an important quantitative tool to identify and explore the reasons behind local hunting preferences [8].

With the exception of the paca, our results show differences in the rankings of the consumed mammal species depending on their MI and CSI (Table 2). This may be because, although the composed indices are applied to quantify cultural importance based on a series of indicators [16], the assessments will always be subject to limited numbers of indicators under consideration. Even though we tried to include as many reasonable indicators suggested by interviewees as possible in our indices, it is likely that some factors could have been involuntarily excluded. An example of this exclusion might have been certain cultural practices dependent on the worldview of the interviewees [18]. Moreover, in this study we assigned the same weight to each indicator used, which perhaps does not necessarily reflect the real value of the variables involved among respondents through a longer time scale. The mention frequency, although less comprehensive than a composed index (i.e., CSI), is not subject to these problems, since free lists allow for a more precise recovery of the most significant elements in a cultural domain [50]. Finally, once the cultural drivers for using wildlife species are understood, it will be possible to improve management and conservation plans and actions to increase their success and benefits for both the animal populations and their users. The urgency to design and implement effective management actions for wildlife conservation is well recognized within the scientific community. However, traditional ecological knowledge for that purpose has been largely neglected in many areas around the world. Because of that, it is of utmost importance that wildlife management and conservation strategies consider local interests and knowledge through a serious and responsible commitment to the benefit of both rural communities and the animal populations they use.

Conclusions

In general terms, Lacandons and Mestizos do not exhibit marked differences in the reasons why they value mammals or even in the composition of the most important species. In methodological terms, the seven indicators suggested by the interviewees and the two indices applied in this study were useful to perform a comprehensive assessment of the reasons why the residents of the Lacandon Forest value wild mammals. However, we recognize that there might be additional variables involved in the cultural significance of mammals that should be included in further ethnozoological studies.

In terms of the animal species, the spotted paca is by far the most important consumed mammal in the Lacandon forest. The primary reasons explaining this were the excellent taste and quality of paca meat, its high hunting frequency, a greater diversity of capture methods, and its high commercial value. Other important mammal species providing food for residents of the study area were the white-tailed deer, red-brocket deer, collared peccary, white-lipped peccary, nine-banded armadillo, Central American agouti, and white-nosed coati. These species had high consumption and commercial value indicators estimated in our survey.

Finally, we consider that conserving the most valued species and their habitats in the study area may be a strategy to improve the food security of many poor families in the Lacandon Forest. This strategy needs further involvement of local, regional, and federal authorities with rural communities and academic institutions in order to develop management plans for the sustainable use of the species with the highest cultural importance.
