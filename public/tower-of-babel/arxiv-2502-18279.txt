Near-Optimal Approximations for Bayesian Inference in Function Space
Veit D. Wild veit.wild@stats.ox.ac.uk

Abstract
We propose a scalable inference algorithm for Bayes posteriors defined on a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS). Given a likelihood function and a Gaussian random element representing the prior, the corresponding Bayes posterior measure Π B \Pi_{\text{B}} can be obtained as the stationary distribution of an RKHS-valued Langevin diffusion. We approximate the infinite-dimensional Langevin diffusion via a projection onto the first M M components of the Kosambi–Karhunen–Loève expansion. Exploiting the thus obtained approximate posterior for these M M components, we perform inference for Π B \Pi_{\text{B}} by relying on the law of total probability and a sufficiency assumption. The resulting method scales as O ⁡ ( M 3 + J ​ M 2 ) O(M^{3}+JM^{2}) , where J J is the number of samples produced from the posterior measure Π B \Pi_{\text{B}} . Interestingly, the algorithm recovers the posterior arising from the sparse variational Gaussian process (SVGP) ( Titsias, 2009 ) as a special case—owed to the fact that the sufficiency assumption underlies both methods. However, whereas the SVGP is parametrically constrained to be a Gaussian process, our method is based on a non-parametric variational family 𝒫 ⁡ ( ℝ M ) \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{M}) consisting of all probability measures on ℝ M \mathbb{R}^{M} . As a result, our method is provably close to the optimal M M -dimensional variational approximation of the Bayes posterior Π B \Pi_{\text{B}} in 𝒫 ⁡ ( ℝ M ) \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{M}) for convex and Lipschitz continuous negative log likelihoods, and coincides with SVGP for the special case of a Gaussian error likelihood.

Introduction

Bayesian inference in function spaces crucially relies on the construction of a prior distributions on an infinite-dimensional function space.
Gaussian processes (GPs) are the standard tool deployed for this purpose, and have proven a valuable framework for principled uncertainty quantification over the past few decades
(
Rasmussen, 2003
)
.
A notable shortcoming of GPs is their cubic computational cost with respect to data size, and their intractability for the case of non-Gaussian likelihoods.
Numerous approaches have been proposed to overcome these challenges
(
Gneiting, 2002
;
Quiñonero-Candela and Rasmussen, 2005
;
Chalupka et al., 2013
;
Wilson and Nickisch, 2015
;
Gardner et al., 2018
;
Wang et al., 2019
;
Liu et al., 2020
, see e.g.)
.
Amongst them, sparse variational Gaussian processes (SVGPs)
(
Titsias, 2009
)
arguably are considered to be the gold standard for scalable GP approximations.
SVGPs rely on the introduction of inducing features that are assumed to follow a Gaussian distribution with learnable variational parameters, and which can be interpreted as an approximate low-dimensional summary of the posterior GP.
To learn these variational parameters, one then typically performs gradient based maximisation of the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO)
(
Hensman et al., 2013
;
Hensman et al., 2015a
, see e.g.)
.

While SVGPs often perform surprisingly well in practice, their approximation quality is inadequate whenever the Gaussianity imposed upon the inducing points is too restrictive.
In the hope of overcoming this shortcoming, this paper explores the possibility of performing Bayesian inference by using a version of gradient descent
directly
in the space of probability measures—rather than on variational parameters indexing the family of approximating measures
(
Wild et al., 2023
, see)
.
We achieve this in three steps:

1.

In
Section

3
, we derive the required Wasserstein Gradient Flow (WGF)
(
Otto, 2001
;
Ambrosio et al., 2005
)
as a natural analogue for gradient descent over the space of probability measures;

2.

We augment the resulting infinite-dimensional diffusion via an
M
-dimensional projection operation in
Section

4
to make it computationally tractable;

3.

We study the consequences of this projection in
Section

5
, and find that relative to the theoretically optimal approximation (see
Theorem

1
), the proposed algorithm is close in Kullback-Leibler divergence (
Theorem

2
), with a discrepancy that vanishes at least as
\mathcal{O}(M^{-1})
, and even as
\mathcal{O}(\exp(-M))
for certain special cases of interest (
Lemma

3
).

Before we state the results in full detail,
Section

2
elaborates on the setting studied in this paper, and
Section

2.3
provides a high-level summary of our results.

2
Bayesian Inference in Function Space

Throughout, we will be concerned with Bayesian inference in a function space.
To this end, we have to assign a prior distribution over an unknown function
f
.
This function
f
acts as the infinite-dimensional parameter of the likelihood function
p(y_{1:N}|f)
which we use to describe the distribution of
N
observations
y_{1:N}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

2.1
Computation for GP Regression

Generally speaking, Bayesian inference in function spaces is computationally challenging.
As a result, the most widely studied special case of this setting is Gaussian process (GP) regression
(
Rasmussen, 2003
)
, which leads to comparatively tractable inference algorithms.
As indicated by its name, GP regression is characterised by placing a GP prior over the function
f
.
Most commonly, the likelihood in GP regression is also Gaussian, resulting in the log likelihood

\displaystyle\log p(y_{1:N}|f)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log\mathcal{N}\big(y_{n};f(x_{n}),\sigma^{2}\big).

Here,
\sigma^{2}>0
denotes the variance in the observation noise,
x_{1:N}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\in\mathcal{X}^{N}
are known input features, and
\mathcal{N}(y;\mu,\sigma^{2})
denotes a Gaussian density with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
evaluated at
y
.
Given the GP prior on
f
and a likelihood such as the above, GP regression finds the Bayes posterior over
f
given
y_{1:N}
.

For the special case of Gaussian likelihoods, the Bayes posterior is available in closed form, and computable via basic linear algebra operations.
While everything would seem to be fine in this setting, there is an important complication: even in this best case scenario, the computational burden of obtaining the exact GP posterior is of order
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
, and thus prohibitive for even moderately large sample sizes.
As a result, the Bayes posterior will almost always have to be approximated in practice—even if closed forms are available.
Arguably the most common way of doing this is via sparse variational GPs (SVGPs) first proposed by
Titsias (2009)
.
SVGPs provide a low-level stochastic summary of the GP using so-called
inducing features
.
These features are assumed to follow a Gaussian distribution with finite-dimensional variational parameters that are typically learnt via gradient-based variational inference techniques
(
Hensman et al., 2013
;
Hensman et al., 2015a
, see e.g.)
.
While SVGPs can perform well in practice, they also often lead to subpar performance.
One of the main reason is that the Gaussianity assumed for the inducing features is too restrictive, and leads to poor approximations.
More generally speaking,
SVGPs do not provide universal guarantees for their approximation quality, and are really only appropriate if the exact posterior is itself a Gaussian process or close to it.

2.2
Beyond GPs and SVGPs

GP regression and its sparse variational approximations are computationally viable but restrictive.
In this paper, we aim at building a more general framework for function space inference.
To this end, we apply the Wasserstein Gradient Flow (WGF) for a variational reformulation of Bayesian inference (see
Section

3.2
), resulting in an infinite-dimensional version of gradient descent over the space of probability measures on the desired function space
H
.
Like SVGP, this is a variational method. But unlike SVGP, it is
not
parametrically constrained to be a Gaussian process.
As a result, one may hope that the resulting algorithms can provide superior approximation quality—a suspicion we will verify in
Section

5
.
Applying the WGF will enforce an important distinction to a GP-centred view on function space inference: to construct it, we require explicit knowledge of the function space
H
over which the prior and posterior measures are defined.
1
1

1

The necessary calculations exploit various concepts from infinite-dimensional analysis such as Fréchet derivatives or the integration by parts for Gaussian measures on Hilbert spaces (cf. Appendix
B
), and thus necessitate precise understanding of the underlying function space
H
.

This is in stark contrast to Bayesian inference schemes with GPs, which only rely on evaluating
f
at a finite set of points
(
Rasmussen, 2003
)
, and therefore are agnostic to the exact function space
H
which draws from the GP realise in.
Consequently, the family of distributions we consider throughout will be Gaussian Random Elements
(
Van Neerven, 2008
, see)
: unlike for GPs,
the function space on which draws from a Gaussian Random Element (GRE) realise are explicitly part of their definition.

While GREs and GPs are different families of
measures over function spaces, they have important connections—and we use these to link a sub-class of GREs to GPs in
Section

3.5.2
.
Importantly, our inference algorithm does
not
rely on this connection: it can be employed for Bayesian computation of a class of GP posteriors, but is applicable to more general GRE posteriors
beyond
GPs.
Thus, we mainly provide the connection to GPs to make prior specification easier and posterior inferences more interpretable.
Additionally, this will
allow us a natural way for comparing our (more general) approach with existing (more narrowly defined) function space inference algorithms such as SVGPs.
This special case is particularly interesting
because it leads to a set of optimality results for our method in
Section

5
.
In the language of SVGPs, the optimality results show that amongst all possible posteriors that condition on the chosen inducing features, our procedure targets the one that is closest to the exact posterior in Kullback-Leibler divergence up to a vanishingly small error.

2.3
Contributions

The paper is organised in three sections, each of which relates to three interrelated contributions:
Section

3
studies the WGF, which is used in
Section

4
to derive a new tractable algorithm for function space inference that we study in
Section

5
.
We provide a high-level overview of each of these below.

•

First, we derive the
WGF
for the optimisation-centric reformulation of Bayesian
function space inference
, which is discussed in
Section

3
.
To implement the WGF numerically and allow for a practicable implementation, we require certain Fréchet derivatives to exist, which—perhaps surprisingly—implies that the function space
H
in which draws from the posterior are realised is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
H_{k}
associated to some kernel
k
.
At first glance, this may seem to suggest that it would be natural to specify priors and posteriors to be GPs associated to the kernel
k
, but this is a misconception: Driscoll’s Theorem
(
Driscoll, 1973
)
shows that samples from GPs associated to
k
will realise
outside
of
H_{k}
with probability one, whenever
H_{k}
is infinite-dimensional
(
Steinwart, 2024
, see also)
.
Therefore, we consider GREs in
H_{k}
instead—a class of priors in function spaces that have traditionally been underexplored for Bayesian inference; albeit with notable exceptions
(
Stuart, 2010
;
Wild and Wynne, 2022
;
Wild et al., 2023
)
.
With this in place, we finally obtain a diffusion on
H_{k}
whose stationary distribution is the targeted Bayes posterior
\Pi_{\text{B}}
.

•

Second, we perform
Bayesian computation by projection
based on the infinite-dimensional Langevin stochastic differential equation (SDE) that implements the dynamics arising from the WGF.
In particular, as elements of
H_{k}
can generally not be represented numerically without approximation,
Section

4
provides a projected
M
-dimensional representation of this infinite-dimensional diffusion and uses it to construct an inference algorithm that scales as
\mathcal{O}(M^{3})
.
Specifically, we orthogonally project onto the first
M
coefficients of the Kosambi-Karhunen-Loève expansion of our function, which corresponds to representing the infinite-dimensional object through an
M
-dimensional subspace.
While various other subspaces might be considered,
orthogonal projection is not only a natural choice, but also turns out to result in near-optimal posterior approximations (see
Section

5
).
We call the resulting inference algorithm Projected Langevin Sampling (PLS). PLS consists of first simulating from this projected finite-dimensional diffusion on
\mathbb{R}^{M}
, and then probabilistically mapping back
into the infinite-dimensional
H_{k}
via the law of total probability.

•

Third, we derive
theoretical guarantees
of PLS,
and
shed light on its
relationship with SVGPs
in
Section

5
.
To start with, we can show that PLS provides near-optimal variational approximations.
Next—and despite being a sampling algorithm—PLS is also a variational inference scheme.
Notably, PLS approximately samples from a nonparametric variational posterior that is strictly more expressive than the corresponding parametrically constrained Gaussian variational posterior of SVGPs.
This allows PLS to target a strictly better posterior approximation than SVGPs, except for the special case of Gaussian likelihoods, for which PLS and SVGPs target the same (Gaussian) approximation.
Unlike SVGPs, PLS can also be used for arbitrary likelihoods without requiring that their integrals with respect to variationally parameterised Gaussian measures are tractable or easy to approximate.

3
Wasserstein Gradient Flow (WGF) for Functional Inference

The first step in building our new inference algorithm is the application of the Wasserstein Gradient Flow (WGF) to an optimisation problem that fully characterises a functional Bayes posterior.
To this end, we first define prerequisite notations and concepts in
Section

3.1
, and the optimisation problem to which the WGF is applied in
Section

3.2
.
Next, we draw instructive parallels between the WGF and gradient descent on measure spaces in
Section

3.3
, and state the SDE obtained as a limiting case of this gradient descent scheme in
Section

3.4
.
While all developments to this point apply for function space inference on arbitrary Hilbert spaces
H
,
Section

3.5
explains why computational feasibility demands that one should choose an RKHS
H=H_{k}
based on a kernel
k
, and explains how to specify GRE priors on such spaces.

3.1
Preliminaries & Notations

On a technical level, a GRE
F
is simply a random variable with realisations on a function space
H
.
What makes GREs attractive is their Gaussianity: they are fully specified
through a mean function
m\in H
and a covariance operator
C:H\to H
(a positive, self-adjoint, trace-class operator).
Similarly to commonly employed notation for GPs, we thus write
F\sim\mathcal{N}(m,C)
to indicate that
F
is a GRE with mean function
m
and covariance operator
C:H\to H
(cf. Appendix
A
for a brief introduction to GREs in Hilbert spaces).

By definition, it is clear that GREs have a finite second moment. Further, and as we prove in Appendix
C
, Bayes posteriors depending on GRE priors are also guaranteed to have finite second moment.
To rigorously define the larger set of all measures with finite second moment on
H
, we let
\mathcal{B}(H)
be the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
H
, and then define the set of Borel probability measures on
H
with finite second moment as

\mathcal{P}_{2}(H):=\left\{\mu:\mathcal{B}(H)\to[0,1]\,\big|\;\mu(H)=1,\,\int_{H}||u||^{2}\,d\mu(u)<\infty\right\}.

For the application of the WGF, this set of measures is crucial: we can metrize it with the 2-Wasserstein metric distance, which for
\Pi,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(H)
is defined as

\displaystyle W_{2}(\Pi,Q)^{2}:=\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(\Pi,Q)}\int_{H\times H}||h-h^{\prime}||^{2}\,d\gamma(h,h^{\prime}),

where
\mathcal{C}(\Pi,Q)\subset\mathcal{P}_{2}(H\times H)
is the set of all probability measures on
\mathcal{B}(H\times H)
whose marginals are
\Pi
and
Q
, so that
\gamma(A\times H)=\Pi(A)
and
\gamma(H\times B)=Q(B)
for all
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(H)

(cf.
Ambrosio et al., 2005
, Chapter 6)
.

3.2
Optimisation-centric perspectives on Bayesian inference

It is well-known that the Bayes posterior is the solution to a particular optimisation problem, both for the parametric and the non-parametric case
(e.g., Theorem 1 in
Knoblauch et al., 2019
;
Wild et al., 2022
, Appendix A.1)
.
Our WGF-based algorithmic perspective builds on this.
In particular, the WGF implements a form of gradient descent on the objective underlying this optimisation problem.
To state the objective under study, let
\Pi:=\mathcal{N}(0,C)
be a GRE prior on the function space
H
, and
\ell(f):=-\log p(y_{1:N}|f)
an arbitrary negative log likelihood function.
2
2

2

Here,
p(y_{1:N}|f)
is called a likelihood function if
(y_{1:N},f)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times H\to p(y_{1:N}|f)\in[0,\infty)
is product-measurable, and if
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}p(y_{1:N}|f)\,d\mu(y_{1:N})=1
for all fixed
f\in H
, and for
\mu
a base measure on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
—such as the counting measure or Lebesgue measure.

With this, one obtains the Bayes posterior
\Pi_{\text{B}}
as

\displaystyle\Pi_{\text{B}}=\argmin_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(H)}\underbrace{\int\ell(f)\,dQ(f)+\operatorname{KL}(Q,\Pi)}_{=:L(Q)},

(1)

where

\operatorname{KL}(Q,\Pi)
denotes the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the measures
Q
and
\Pi
.
3
3

3

This optimisation problem is more commonly stated over the space of
all
Borel probability measures on
H
, often denoted
\mathcal{P}(H)
.
As we show in
Appendix

C
however, the GRE prior guarantees that even over this larger space, the solution always lies in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(H)
.

This optimisation-centric perspective has many methodological and theoretical uses.
For example, it shows that conventional variational inference relates to full Bayesian inference like constrained to unconstrained optimisation
(
Knoblauch et al., 2019
, see Theorem 2 in )
.
To see this, simply note that conventional variational inference consists in choosing a family of measures
\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathcal{P}(H)
that has a finite-dimensional Euclidean parameterisation, and then computing the variational Bayes posterior as
\widehat{Q}=\argmin_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}L(Q)
through stochastic gradient descent
(
Titsias and Lázaro-Gredilla, 2014
, e.g.)
or other optimisation techniques.
In the specific context of Gaussian processes (GPs), such variational Bayesian approaches were pioneered by
Titsias (2009)
, further developed in
Matthews et al. (2016)
, and adapted to Gaussian Random Elements (GREs) in Banach spaces by
Wild and Wynne (2022)
.

The current paper also develops variationally Bayesian methodology, but refrains from the conventional approach: instead of choosing a parameterised family of distributions
\mathcal{Q}
and performing gradient descent on its finite-dimensional parameters, gradient descent is performed
directly
on the infinite-dimensional space
\mathcal{P}_{2}(H)
.
Unlike parameterised families
\mathcal{Q}
, this allows for nonparametric variational posteriors, and results in much greater flexibility.
On a technical level, our methodology amounts to a computationally efficient implementation of the WGF for
L(Q)
, and transfers the insights of
Wild et al. (2023)
to inference on measures over functions
f\in H
.
While we do not explore the case where the Kullback-Leibler divergence in (
1
) is replaced with other regularisers, this setting was explored for finite-dimensional parameter spaces in
Wild et al. (2023)
, and the results therein could be extended to function spaces.

3.3
Gradient descent in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(H)

The intuition behind our application of the WGF is to use the functional
Q\mapsto L(Q)
in (
1
) to find the minimiser
\Pi_{\text{B}}
by performing a type of gradient descent over
\mathcal{P}_{2}(H)
.
In particular and as outlined in Chapter 2 of
Ambrosio et al. (2005)
, we can pick a starting point
Q_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(H)
, and then iteratively update according to

\displaystyle Q_{k+1}:=\argmin_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(H)}\big\{L(Q)+\frac{1}{2\eta}W_{2}(Q,Q_{k})^{2}\big\},

for
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\eta>0
a sufficiently small step-size.
4
4

4

This is similar to gradient descent in Euclidean spaces: for a fixed and sufficiently small
\eta>0
and a function
\ell
to be minimised, we can rewrite the
(k+1)
-th iterate of ordinary gradient descent as
\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}-\eta\nabla\ell(\theta_{k})=\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}\left\{\ell(\theta)+\frac{1}{2\eta}\|\theta-\theta_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\right\}
.

If
L
is sufficiently regular and we let
\eta\to 0
, the continuously indexed family
\big(Q_{\lfloor t/\eta\rfloor}\big)_{t\geq 0}
converges to the limit
\big(Q(t)\big)_{t\geq 0}\subset\mathcal{P}_{2}(H)
with
Q(0)=Q_{0}

(
Ambrosio et al., 2005
, Chapter 11.1.3)
.
This limiting evolution of measures
\big(Q(t)\big)_{t\geq 0}
is called the Wasserstein gradient flow (WGF) for
L
starting at
Q_{0}
.
It has obvious parallels with gradient descent, and is directly interpretable as its infinite-dimensional analogue on
\mathcal{P}_{2}(H)
: at every infinitesimally small step forward in time
t
,
Q(t)
moves in the direction amounting to the biggest decrease in the value for
Q\mapsto L(Q)
, and we would hope that the optimum is recovered in the limit so that
\Pi_{\text{B}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}Q(t)
.

3.4
Following the WGF via the Langevin SDE in Hilbert space

Following Theorem 8.3.1 in
Ambrosio et al. (2005)
, the WGF
\big(Q(t)\big)_{t>0}
for functional
Q\mapsto L(Q)
in (
1
) satisfies for all test functions
5
5

5

More precisely, the relevant class of test functions are the smooth cylindrical functions, denoted
\text{Cyl}\big((H\times[0,T]\big)

(
Ambrosio et al., 2005
, Definition 5.1.11)
. By definition,
\varphi\in\text{Cyl}\big((H\times[0,T]\big)
if and only if there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
and orthonormal vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}\in H
such that
\varphi(f)=\psi\big(\langle f,v_{1}\rangle,\ldots,\langle f,v_{N}\rangle,t\big)
where
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function with compact support.

\varphi:[0,T]\times H\to\mathbb{R}
the equation

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{H}\partial_{t}\varphi(t,f)-\big\langle\nabla_{W}L[Q(t)](f),D\varphi(t,f)\big\rangle_{H}\,dQ(t)(f)\,dt=0,

(2)

where
D\varphi(t,f)
denotes the Fréchet derivative of
\varphi
with respect to
f
, and
\nabla_{W}L[Q]:H\to H
is the so-called
Wasserstein gradient
of
L
evaluated at
Q\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(H)
.
Obtaining an explicit form for this Wasserstein gradient is essential in deriving the dynamics governing
(Q(t))_{t\geq 0}
, and we derive it in
Theorem

7
(cf.
Appendix

B
) via the set of equations given by the characterisation of
(Q(t))_{t\geq 0}
in (
2
).
In so doing, we find that for (
1
), we get

\displaystyle\nabla_{W}L[Q](f)=D\ell(f)+D(\log q)(f)=D\ell(f)+\frac{Dq(f)}{q(f)},

(3)

where
q:=dQ/d\Pi:H\to\mathbb{R}
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
Q
with respect to the prior measure
\Pi
.
Plugging (
3
) into (
2
), we can identify the law of a stochastic process

\big(F(t)\big)_{t>0}
with
F(t)\in H
for which
F(t)\sim Q(t)
for all
t\geq 0
(cf. Theorem
6
in Appendix
B
).
In particular, we find that
\big(F(t)\big)_{t>0}
is the solution to the infinite-dimensional version of the Langevin Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE) given by

\displaystyle dF(t)=-\left(D\ell\big(F(t)\big)+C^{-1}F(t)\right)dt+\sqrt{2}dW(t).

(4)

Here,
\big(W(t)\big)_{t\geq 0}
is a cylindrical Brownian motion in
H
and
C^{-1}:\text{Im}(C)\subset H\to H
is the inverse of
C
.
6
6

6

The operator
C^{-1}
is unbounded and given by
C^{-1}f=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}^{-1}\langle f,e_{n}\rangle e_{n}
where
\{\lambda_{n},e_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
are the eigenvalue-eigenvector pairs obtained from the spectral decomposition of the self-adjoint trace class covariance operator
C
.

This SDE was first introduced in
Hairer et al. (2005)
, and the existence, uniqueness and ergodic properties of its solution are discussed in
Hairer et al. (2007)
.

Hairer et al. (2011)
provides a more accessible treatment on the subject, and
Da Prato and Zabczyk (2014)
provides a more general discussion of SDEs in Hilbert spaces.

For our purposes, we can thankfully gloss over many of the technical aspects of dealing with this infinite-dimensional SDE.
Instead, our main reason for introducing (
4
) will be its use as the driving engine for our methodology.
To this end, it is instructive to note the parallels with the finite-dimensional case:
if we consider (
1
) for
H=\mathbb{R}^{J}
, we recover a Bayes posterior defined on a finite-dimensional parameter
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
.
Writing the associated likelihood function as
p(y_{1:N}|\theta)
, and the corresponding Gaussian prior density as
p(\theta)=\mathcal{N}(\theta;0,\Sigma)
, the resulting WGF—now defined on
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{J})
—yields a measure evolution that corresponds to the well-known finite-dimensional Langevin SDE

\displaystyle d\theta(t)=-\left(\nabla\ell_{N}\big(\theta(t)\big)+\Sigma^{-1}\theta(t)\right)dt+\sqrt{2}d\beta(t),

(5)

where
\ell_{N}(\theta):=-\log p(y_{1:N}|\theta)
and
\big(\beta(t)\big)_{t\geq 0}
is the standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{J}

(
Jordan et al., 1998
;
Otto, 2001
)
.
This finite-dimensional Langevin SDE is the basis of many classical algorithms for Bayesian computation since its stationary distribution for
\theta(t)
as
t\to\infty
recovers the Bayes posterior
(
Roberts and Tweedie, 1996
;
Welling and Teh, 2011
, see e.g.)
.
In other words, and exactly as for the infinite-dimensional case, one way of motivating
why
this diffusion recovers the Bayes posterior is by interpreting it as the natural analogue of gradient descent on (
1
) for the special case of
H=\mathbb{R}^{J}
.

Just as (
5
) is the inspiration for many seminal algorithms for computing Bayes posteriors over finite-dimensional parameters, (
4
) can be used to generate samples from the posterior measure
\Pi_{\text{B}}
that solves the optimisation problem (
1
) with infinite-dimensional function spaces
H
.
However, the infinite dimensionality of
H
introduces an additional complication: the Langevin SDE in (
4
) generally cannot be represented on a computer.
Fortunately, we can overcome this with parsimonious numerical approximations (cf. Section
4
).

3.5
Choosing Function Space
H
and Prior
\Pi

So far, our developments are valid for likelihoods and GREs on general Hilbert spaces.
In particular, the Langevin SDE in (
4
) in principle may produce posterior inferences over general function spaces
H
as long as they are Hilbert spaces.
As we will explain next however, the seemingly innocuous requirement that
\ell
be Fréchet differentiable will necessitate the assumption that
H
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) for virtually all likelihood functions of practical interest (cf. Appendix
A.1
for basic properties of an RKHS).

3.5.1
The Inevitability of the RKHS

Implementing the Langevin SDE in (
4
) requires calculation of the Wasserstein gradient in (
3
), and therefore of the Fréchet derivative
D\ell(f)
of
\ell:H\to\mathbb{R}
.
For simplicity, consider negative log-likelihood functions which for a cost function
c:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
can be written as

\displaystyle\ell(f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}c\big(y_{n},f(x_{n})\big)+\text{ constant},

(6)

where
c\big(y_{n},f(x_{n})\big)
measures the discrepancy between an observation
y_{n}
and the prediction
f(x_{n})
for
y_{n}
, and where the constant does not depend on
f
.
For example, the Gaussian likelihood
corresponds to
c(y_{n},f(x_{n}))=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\big(y_{n}-f(x_{n})\big)^{2}
.
Another example is the Bernoulli likelihood for binary classification, which is

\displaystyle p(y_{n}|f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi\big(f(x_{n})\big)^{y_{n}}\cdot\Big(1-\phi\big(f(x_{n})\big)\Big)^{1-y_{n}}.

(7)

In this case,
y_{n}\in\{0,1\}
, so that the associated negative log likelihood
\ell(f)
is of the form (
6
) with
c(y_{n},f(x_{n}))=-y_{n}\log\phi(f(x_{n}))-(1-y_{n})\log(1-\phi(f(x_{n})))
.
Here,

\phi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
is a mapping that transforms latent functional outputs into probabilities such as the the logistic function
\phi_{\operatorname{logistic}}(f(x_{n}))=\big(1+\exp(-f(x_{n}))\big)^{-1}
.

For any negative log likelihood of form (
6
), we can apply the chain rule and obtain the corresponding Fréchet derivative as

\displaystyle D\ell(f)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\partial_{2}c)\big(y_{n},f(x_{n})\big)D(s_{n})(f),

(8)

where we have written
s_{n}(f):=f(x_{n})
as the point-wise evaluation functional for
f\in H
at
x_{n}
,
\partial_{2}c
as the derivative of
c
with respect to its second component, and
D(s_{n})
as the Fréchet derivative of
s_{n}
.
Inspecting the expression, it is now clear that we need to assume that all
s_{n}
are Fréchet differentiable and therefore continuous.
However, demanding continuity of all pointwise evaluation functionals
s_{n}(f)
for all
f\in H
is in fact equivalent to
H
being an RKHS
(
Berlinet and Thomas-Agnan, 2004
, Theorem 1)
.

In summary, even when attention is restricted to the simple sub-class of likelihood functions based on pointwise evaluations of
f
as in (
6
), the Wasserstein gradient in (
3
) needed to evolve the infinite-dimensional Langevin SDE in (
4
) will
only
have the Fréchet derivatives necessary for implementing it numerically if
H
is an RKHS.
Throughout the remainder of the paper, we therefore take
H
to be an RKHS
H_{k}
associated with the reproducing kernel function
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Thanks to the widely celebrated reproducing property of RKHS functions, this implies that
s_{n}(f)=\langle f,k_{n}\rangle
for
k_{n}=k(x_{n},\cdot)\in H_{k}
, so that the required Fréchet derivative in (
8
) is
D(s_{n})=k_{n}
, and

D\ell(f)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\partial_{2}c)\big(y_{n},f(x_{n})\big)k_{n}.

3.5.2
Covariance Operators and Gaussian processes

While choosing GRE priors may seem like an intractably difficult proposition, one can draw clear analogies and parallels to the design of Gaussian process (GP) priors.
We will leverage these connections to design our GRE priors in the current paper.
To this end, note that
the GRE prior
\Pi:=\mathcal{N}(0,C)
is specified by a covariance operator
C:H_{k}\to H_{k}
that encodes prior knowledge about the unknown function.
In this paper, we specify

through a kernel function

\displaystyle Cf

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int k(\cdot,x^{\prime})\,f(x^{\prime})\,d\nu(x^{\prime})

(9)

for all
f\in H_{k}

In Appendix

under mild assumptions on

and

Every GRE

This connection is practically useful, since it is well-understood how to specify interpretable GP function priors

Therefore, one can specify interpretable GRE priors via the GP induced by

The correspondence between

The easiest way to do this—and the approach we use in our experiments of

In practice, this has the immediate effect of making us operate on an

A natural first step for approximating the intractable diffusion

\displaystyle F(x)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\langle F,e_{m}\rangle e_{m}(x),

\displaystyle\operatorname{Proj}[F]:=\sum_{m=1}^{M}F^{m}e_{m}.

(11)

Denoting

Since

10

4.2
Finite-Dimensional Projection of the WGF in Hilbert space

From a practical standpoint, the projection in (
11

M
-dimensional SDE
(F^{1:M}(t))_{t\geq 0}
instead of
(F(t))_{t\geq 0}
.
As we will show in the remainder, the limiting distribution of this projected SDE allows us to exactly sample from the Bayes posterior
\Pi_{\text{B}}
when

, or to tractably approximate it with provable guarantees when
M\ll N
(cf.
Theorem

).
Intriguingly, the resulting posterior approximation coincides with SVGPs for Gaussian likelihoods (cf.
Lemma

4
), but is
not
constrained to a Gaussian variational family—and thus is generally non-Gaussian for non-Gaussian likelihoods.

To power this approximation algorithm, we first study the time evolution for
F^{1:M}(t)=(F^{1}(t),F^{2}(t),\dots F^{M}(t))^{\top}
with
F^{m}(t):=\langle F(t),e_{m}\rangle\in\mathbb{R}
.
Using Itó’s Rule, we find

\displaystyle dF^{m}(t)=-\Big(\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\partial_{2}c)\big(y_{n},F(t)(x_{n})\big)e_{m}(x)+\frac{F^{m}(t)}{\lambda_{m}}\Big)dt+\sqrt{2}dB^{m}(t),

(12)

where

are stochastically independent Brownian motions in
\mathbb{R}
.
While this diffusion is simpler than (
4

M
largest eigenvalue-eigenfunction pairs
\{{\lambda}_{m},{e}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
would still not suffice to make it tractable due to its dependence on
F(t)
, for which we do not have an exact representation.
To rectify this, we therefore replace
F(t)
with its

\text{Proj}[F(t)]
. This yields the new SDE

\displaystyle d\widetilde{F}^{m}(t)=-\Big(\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\partial_{2}c)\big(y_{n},{\text{Proj}}[F(t)](x_{n})\big)e_{m}(x)+\frac{\widetilde{F}^{m}(t)}{\lambda_{m}}\Big)dt+\sqrt{2}dB^{m}(t),

(13)

where
{\text{Proj}}[F(t)]=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\widetilde{F}^{m}(t)e_{m}
and
\widetilde{F}_{m}=\langle F(t),e_{m}\rangle
.
Further, the limiting measure of this diffusion is defined as
\Pi_{\infty}^{1:M}
so that
\Pi_{\infty}^{1:M}\sim\lim_{t\to\infty}\widetilde{F}^{1:M}(t)

Even this approximation is not computationally feasible however: since the exact values of
\{\lambda_{m},e_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
are unknown in practice,
\text{Proj}[F(t)]
cannot be computed exactly and needs to be estimated.
To this end, we use the Nyström method to estimate the
M
largest eigenvalue-eigenfunction pairs
\{\widehat{\lambda}_{m},\widehat{e}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
(cf. Appendix
G
).
Given
\{\widehat{\lambda}_{m},\widehat{e}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
, a natural estimate for

then follows from the

\displaystyle\widehat{\text{Proj}}[F(t)]

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}\underbrace{\langle F(t),\widehat{e}_{m}\rangle}_{=:\widehat{F}^{m}(t)}\widehat{e}_{m}

(14)

and its evaluations
\widehat{\text{Proj}}[F(t)](x_{n})=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\widehat{F}^{m}(t)\widehat{e}_{m}(x_{n})
.
Substituting this into (
13

whose components evolve as

\displaystyle d\widehat{F}^{m}(t)=-\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\partial_{2}c)\Big(y_{n},\widehat{\text{Proj}}[F_{t}](x_{n})\Big)\widehat{e}_{m}(x)+\frac{\widehat{F}^{m}(t)}{\widehat{\lambda}_{m}}\right)dt+\sqrt{2}dB^{m}(t).

4.3
Posteriors on Infinite-Dimensional Spaces via Projection

Having developed the tractable projected diffusion
(\widehat{F}^{1:M}(t))_{t\geq 0}
, we now seek to use this projection for approximation of the Bayes posterior
\Pi_{\text{B}}
.
To this end, first note that we can characterise the Bayes posterior via
\Pi_{\text{B}}\in\mathcal{P}(H_{k})
given by
\Pi_{\text{B}}\sim\lim_{t\to\infty}F(t)
.
It might thus appear reasonable to expect that the limiting distribution
\widehat{\Pi}^{1:M}_{\infty}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{M})
given by
\widehat{\Pi}^{1:M}_{\infty}\sim\lim_{t\to\infty}\widehat{F}^{1:M}(t)
evolved via (
15
) can approximate an
M
-dimensional summary of
\Pi_{\text{B}}
.
Specifically, we might expect the limiting distribution of
\widehat{\text{Proj}}[F(t)]
in (
11
) constructed using components
\widehat{F}^{m}
sampled from
\widehat{\Pi}^{1:M}_{\infty}
to follow a distribution that is roughly equal to
\Pi_{\text{B}}
.
But a construction like this would restrict posterior uncertainty to an
M
-dimensional subspace of
H_{k}
, and lead to miscalibrated representations of uncertainty.
In particular, if we wanted to quantify the posterior uncertainty of functions in
H_{k}
that are far outside of the
M
-dimensional subspace given by
\text{span}\{\widehat{e}_{m}:1\leq m\leq M\}
, their uncertainty would degenerate to zero.
Clearly, this is undesirable: these are
precisely
the types of functions in
H_{k}
for which we have the highest degree of uncertainty.

Fortunately, we can construct a much smarter approximation that rectifies this issue and which we illustrate in
Figure

3
.
The key insight is that to provide uncertainty on the remaining dimensions of
H_{k}
,
we have to push the finite-dimensional randomness from
\widehat{\Pi}^{1:M}_{\infty}
into the infinite-dimensional space
H_{k}
.
The algorithm we propose implements this pushforward approximately, using the same logic that underlies SVGPs: In particular, we consider for a particular choice of
\tau\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{M})
the measure

\displaystyle\widehat{\Pi}_{\tau}:=\int\mathbb{P}\big(F\in\cdot\,|\,\widehat{F}^{1:M}=u\big)\,d\tau(u),

(16)

which can be understood as a member of the non-parametric variational family given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{M}:=\left\{\widehat{\Pi}_{\tau}:\tau\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{M})\right\}\subset\mathcal{P}_{2}(H_{k}).

(17)

While a classic variational method would seek to find an optimal value of
\tau
through some optimisation routine, our approach is different.
Rather than attempting to solve a difficult optimisation problem like this over a non-parametric variational family, we instead
hand-select
a
\tau
which we know how to sample from relatively cheaply, and which we hypothesise provides a nearly-optimal variational approximation—a hypothesis that we prove to be true in
Section

5
.
In particular, we advocate for choosing

\tau=\widehat{\Pi}_{\infty}^{1:M},

which is the limiting measure of (
15
), and motivate why this is sensible.
The next section provides this motivation from an intuitive and practical standpoint, while
Section

5
does so from a theoretical vantage point.

4.4
Approximate Inference via Sufficiency

Before explaining our approximation, we have to introduce a number of relevant objects.
First, we define

\widehat{\Pi}^{1:M}_{\text{B}}=\widehat{\phi}^{1:M}\#\Pi_{\text{B}}
, where
\#
denotes the pushforward operator, and
\widehat{\phi}^{1:M}(f):=\langle f,\widehat{e}^{1:M}\rangle=(\langle f,\widehat{e}_{1}\rangle,\ldots,\langle f,\widehat{e}_{M}\rangle)^{\top}
denotes the function
\widehat{\phi}^{1:M}:H_{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
that maps
f\in H_{k}
into the
M
largest
estimated
components
\widehat{F}^{1},\widehat{F}^{2},\dots\widehat{F}^{M}
used in (
11
).
In other words,
\widehat{\Pi}^{1:M}_{\text{B}}
is really the Bayes posterior corresponding to the conditional distribution given by
\widehat{F}^{1:M}\mid y_{1:N}
, so that
\widehat{\Pi}^{1:M}_{\text{B}}(A^{\prime})=\mathbb{P}(\widehat{F}^{1:M}\in A^{\prime}\mid y_{1:N})
for all measurable subsets
A^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{M}
.
Since our ultimate goal is to approximate the predictive distribution of
F(x_{1:N_{*}}^{*})\mid y_{1:N}
for
F\mid y_{1:N}\sim\Pi_{\text{B}}
and new test points
x_{1:N_{*}}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}^{N_{*}}
, we additionally define the point-wise evaluation functional
\operatorname{eval}[x^{*}_{1:N_{*}}](f):=f(x^{*}_{1:{N_{*}}})
for
f\in H_{k}
.
As the law of total probability implies that

\Pi_{\text{B}}(B)=\int\mathbb{P}(F\in B\;|\>y_{1:N},\widehat{F}^{1:M}=u)d\widehat{\Pi}^{1:M}_{\text{B}}(u)
for all
B\in\mathcal{B}(H_{k})
, we thus obtain for all measurable
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{N_{*}}
the exact target predictive distribution as

\displaystyle\big(\operatorname{eval}[x^{*}_{1:N_{*}}]\#\Pi_{\text{B}}\big)(A)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M}}\underbrace{\mathbb{P}(F(x^{*}_{1:N_{*}})\in A\mid y_{1:N},{\widehat{F}}^{1:M}=u)}_{\text{(I)}}\;\;d\underbrace{\widehat{\Pi}^{1:M}_{\text{B}}(u)}_{\text{(II)}},

(18)

and Connections to Previous Methods

So far, the motivation for our algorithm was guided heuristically through two approximations (I) and (II) given by (

In the remainder, we demonstrate that these approximations are sound, and that the resulting method can be rigorously justified.

To do so, we first study the theoretically optimal approximation

for the class of approximations mapping the first

Perhaps surprisingly, we obtain an exact expression for this theoretically optimal approximation

The result is an explicit bound on this difference in terms of the eigenvalues

5.2
Characterising Optimal Approximations

In Theorem
2
, we will show that

To define this class, we re-interpret
\Pi_{\infty}^{1:M}
in (

To make this logic explicit, we define for each

\displaystyle{\Pi}_{\tau}:=\int\mathbb{P}\big(F\in\cdot\,|\,F^{1:M}=u\big)\,d\tau(u).

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{M}:=\left\{{\Pi}_{\tau}:\tau\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{M})\right\}\subset\mathcal{P}_{2}(H_{k}).

Notice also that as previously remarked upon in the discussion of (

More generally and perhaps more surprisingly, we can even give a closed form for the optimal variational approximation to

in

The next result derives this optimal variational approximation.

Theorem 1
.

For the optimal variational approximation
\Pi^{*}_{M}

\displaystyle\Pi^{*}_{M}:=\argmin_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{M}}\operatorname{KL}(Q,\Pi_{\text{B}}),

\Pi^{*}_{M}={\Pi}_{\tau^{*}}
with probability measure
\tau^{*}(u)\propto\exp\left(-V^{*}(u)\right)

\displaystyle V^{*}(u):=-\log\frac{d\tau^{*}}{du}(u)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\Big[\ell_{N}\big(\mu_{u}(x_{1:N})+\sqrt{\Sigma(x_{1:N})}\xi\big)\Big]+u^{T}\frac{1}{2}\Lambda_{M}^{-1}u

\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{M})
,
\Lambda_{M}:=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{M})

\displaystyle\mu_{u}(x_{1:N})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle u^{T}e^{1:M}(x_{1:N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

(27)

\displaystyle\Sigma(x_{1:N})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle r(x_{1:N},x_{1:N})-e^{1:M}(x_{1:N})^{T}\Lambda_{M}e^{1:M}(x_{1:N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},

where
e^{1:M}(x_{1:N}):=\big(e^{m}(x_{n})\big)_{m,n=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}

Intriguingly, for the special case of Gaussian likelihood functions,

This allows us to relate our findings to SVGPs, which variationally approximate GP posteriors using a version of (

In light of this, an implication of Theorem

In contrast, outside of the Gaussian likelihood setting, the Gaussianity enforced upon

5.3
Optimal Approximations and PLS

Having established the optimal approximation
\Pi^{*}_{M}
over (
25
) in Theorem
1
, our next result shows that the approximation
\Pi_{\infty}
targeted by PLS is provably close to
\Pi^{*}_{M}
under standard regularity assumptions on the negative log likelihood
\ell_{N}
.
To do this, we first derive the explicit form for the limiting measure
\Pi^{1:M}_{\infty}
featuring in
\Pi_{\infty}
as per (
23
).
In particular, we can show (cf. Appendix
J
) that
\Pi^{1:M}_{\infty}(u)\propto\exp\left(-V_{\infty}(u)\right)
for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and

\displaystyle V_{\infty}(u)

\displaystyle:=\ell_{N}\big(\mu_{u}(x_{1:N})\big)+\frac{1}{2}u^{T}\Lambda_{M}^{-1}u,

where we use
\mu_{u}(x_{1:N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
as defined in (
27
).
To arrive at this result, simply recognise that the SDE in (
15
) is a finite-dimensional Langevin diffusion, and that
V_{\infty}
is the potential associated to this diffusion.

In a similar vein,
V^{*}
defined in Theorem
1
can be interpreted as the potential of a Langevin diffusion with stationary distribution
\tau^{*}
.
This raises an immediate question: given that
\tau^{*}
parameterises the optimal variational measure
\Pi^{*}_{M}
, what stops us from constructing a conventional Langevin sampler based directly on
V^{*}
?
The answer is simple:
V^{*}
depends on an intractable
M
-dimensional integral.
In contrast,
V_{\infty}
is tractable, so that constructing PLS as the Langevin diffusion that draws approximate samples from
\tau_{\infty}
is computationally feasible.

While
V_{\infty}
and
V^{*}
are very different in terms of the computational effort required to evaluate them, there is reason to hope that their numerical differences are relatively small.
Specifically, a close inspection reveals that
V_{\infty}
is a simple approximation to
V^{*}
: instead of evaluating the intractable integral by averaging over
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{M})
as in
V^{*}
, the potential
V_{\infty}
uses a somewhat coarse approximation, and evaluates the integrand only at the mode for
\xi=0
.
While crude, this is not a bad approximation strategy for sufficiently large
M
.
In fact, the next result quantifies the resulting error between
\Pi_{\infty}
and
\Pi^{*}_{M}
in KL divergence, and shows it to be negligible whenever the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{m}\}_{m>M}
decay sufficiently quickly and the negative log likelihood is both Lipschitz continuous and convex.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that for some
\kappa>0
,
\ell_{N}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
\kappa

Then for any fixed
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathcal{X}
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}\big({\Pi}_{\infty},\Pi^{*}_{M}\big)\leq\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}\mathrm{tr}\big[\Sigma(x_{1:N})\big]=\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}\sum_{m=M+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(e_{m}(x_{n}))^{2}.

(28)

Further, if
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathcal{X}
are independently and identically distributed according to
\nu
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x_{1:N}}\Big[\operatorname{KL}\big(\Pi_{\infty},\Pi^{*}_{M}\big)\Big]\leq\frac{N\kappa^{2}}{2}\cdot R_{M}

(29)

for the remainder term
R_{M}=\sum_{m=M+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}^{2}
.

If the remainder term
R_{M}
in (
29
) decays sufficiently fast, the above result provides a strong performance guarantee.
Fortunately, the next result shows that
R_{M}
does indeed decay at sufficient speeds even under no further assumption—and the decay is substantially faster at the expense of a variety of mild assumptions. Its proof is in Appendix

L.2
.

Lemma 3
.

For the remainder term
R_{M}=\sum_{m=M+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}^{2}
in (
29
), it holds that

•

R_{M}=\mathcal{O}(M^{-1})
regardless of
k
and
\nu
;

•

R_{M}=\mathcal{O}(\exp(-cM))
for Gaussian kernels and
\nu
being a Gaussian measure or a measure with compact support;

•

R_{M}=\mathcal{O}(M^{-3(l+1)})
for Matérn kernels of order
l+1/2
and
\nu
being a uniform measure with compact support.

According to the asymptotic decay of the remainder
R_{M}
derived in Lemma
3
, a constant, non-vanishing error persists in (
29
) for the most general case.
However, for commonly chosen smoother kernels
k
and
\nu
endowed with mild regularity conditions, the message is far more hopeful: for these situations,
M=\mathcal{O}(\log(N)^{1+\epsilon})
and
M=\mathcal{O}(N^{1/(3l+3)+\epsilon})
are sufficient to ensure that this error vanishes for any arbitrarily small
\epsilon>0
as
N\to\infty
.
Since Gaussian and Matérn kernels are often the default choice for various kernel-based methods in machine learning, we can therefore expect substantial computational savings without deterioration in approximation quality for a large class of practically relevant settings.

The above two results show that under Lipschitz continuity and convexity for
\ell_{N}
, the measure
\Pi_{\infty}
targeted by PLS is therefore not only computationally efficient, but also guaranteed to be very close to the optimum
\Pi^{*}_{M}
.
Note that these conditions are satisfied in a variety of settings of interest, for instance for the binary classification losses with logistic link functions as in (
7

For a comprehensive overview of other losses satisfying these conditions, see
Steinwart and Christmann (2008)

It is also important to note that assuming Lipschitz continuity and convexity for
\ell_{N}
is sufficient, but definitely not necessary.
In line with this, we find that empirically,
\Pi_{\infty}
continues to be an excellent posterior approximation even when these conditions are violated.
Lipschitz continuity and convexity should therefore be understood as technical requirements that are likely far too strict.
In fact, the negative log Gaussian likelihood

\Pi_{\infty}
can be justified without the prerequisite regularity conditions on

.
Specifically, even though the Gaussian likelihood is not Lipschitz continuous,
\Pi_{\infty}
is in fact

to
\Pi^{*}_{M}
.

Lemma 4

If
\ell_{N}\big(f(x_{1:N})\big)=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}||y_{1:N}-f(x_{1:N})||^{2}

Multimodal Regression with presence of an unknown shift.

While the previous examples served to illustrate quantitative and qualitative differences in the inferences between PLS and SVGP, we now give an illustration that demonstrates the capability of our method to efficiently sample from complex and exotic posterior distributions with relative ease.
In particular, we will show that as long as
c
and
\partial_{2}c
as defined in Section
3.5.1
are tractable, then PLS can be applied without any further model-specific adjustments.
To do this, we posit the following relationship between the observations
y_{n}
and the input features
x_{n}
, given for
n=1,2,\dots N
by

\displaystyle y_{n}=f(x_{n})+s\cdot b+\epsilon_{n},

where
s\in\mathbb{R}
is a shift parameter of known magnitude,
b\sim\operatorname{Ber}(\alpha)
is a Bernoulli random variable with success probability
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and
\epsilon_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
represents Gaussian noise.
Including a Bernoulli random variable that ’switches on’ the shift results in a multimodal likelihood model, as the unknown function
f
can only be identified up to the constant shift
s
(for details, see Appendix

).
This model is related to the classical fixed effects approach, a class of models popular in econometrics
(
Wooldridge, 2010
)
.
Intuitively, it describes a setting where we are uncertain whether a measurements
y_{n}
has been shifted by a constant
s
prior to observation.
As shown in Figure
6
, PLS accurately captures the uncertainty we have about whether or not our data points were shifted prior to observation by generating posterior samples that correspond to both scenarios.
As a result, it produces a bimodal distribution whose modes are apart by a distance of almost exactly
s
.

7
Conclusion

To summarise, we derived the Wasserstein gradient flow for Bayesian inference with a Gaussian random element prior distribution, and for arbitrary likelihood functions.
We demonstrated that the WGF can be efficiently implemented by a projection of the Langevin equation from a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) into Euclidean spaces, which led to a new method we call projected Langevin sampling (PLS).
Our results show that the posterior targeted by this projected sampler coincides with the optimal
M
-dimensional posterior approximation for Gaussian likelihoods.
Beyond that, we showed that it is the first method to be provably close to the optimal
M
-dimensional posterior approximation for non-Gaussian likelihoods under mild convexity and Lipschitz assumptions.
We concluded by exploring the performance of
PLS on several regression and classification tasks, compared it to Sparse variational GPs (SVGPs), and provided demonstrations for some of its capabilities for inference in multi-modal posteriors that are impossible to achieve with SVGPs.

Throughout this development, we made a key observation that motivated much of the paper’s focus: in Section
3.5
, we discovered that in order to implement a WGF-powered functional inference scheme, the functions of interest
needed
to lie in an RKHS.
It is worth reflecting upon this: we did not choose RKHS spaces
directly
.
Rather, they emerged as a consequence of
assuming that the relevant function space is a Hilbert space, and that the negative log likelihood is Fréchet differentiable.
Although it is not obvious how these assumptions could be relaxed while ensuring that the WGF derived in Section
3.4
can be tractably evolved on a computer, it raises the fundamental question if weaker assumptions could be found to generalise our algorithm to functional inference such as more general Hilbert spaces or Banach spaces.
While we believe that these questions could establish an exciting new frontier for functional inference, the underlying mathematics would have to fundamentally depart from the results we derived here.

Turning attention to the methodological gains made in our paper, it is noteworthy that PLS can compete with SVGPs along several dimensions.
On top of its ability to make substantive computational savings by being run in parallel, one advantage of our approach seemed to be the ability to accommodate multi-modal posterior distributions.
While our illustrations provided evidence that PLS can in principle perform multi-modal inference in fortuitous circumstances, it is worth noting that PLS is essentially a Langevin sampler—a class of algorithms well-known to struggle with multi-modal inference.
An important future direction of research on using the WGF to derive functional inference algorithms would be the derivation of a different class of algorithms that is more naturally suited to multi-modal posteriors.
Doing so would require a fundamentally new approach:
the theoretical guarantees we derived for PLS depended on convexity of the negative log likelihood function—but multi-modality emerges mostly when they are non-identifiable and non-convex.
Though the multi-modal setting would require a completely different mathematical toolkit from the one developed in the current paper and is beyond the scope of what we studied here, it poses intriguing questions for further research into functional inference based on WGFs.

Appendix A
Technical background

A.1
Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces

Let
\mathcal{X}
be non-empty set and
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R})
be the set containing all functions
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. A Hilbert space
\big(H,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\big)
with
H\subset\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R})
is called Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS) if and only if the pointwise evaluation functionals
\pi_{x}:H\to\mathbb{R}
,
f\mapsto f(x)
are continuous for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
A function
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is called kernel, if the kernel matrix
k(x_{1:N},x_{1:N}):=\big(k(x_{n},x_{n^{\prime}})\big)_{n,n^{\prime}=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is positive semi-definite for all
x_{1:N}\in\mathcal{X}^{N}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

Moore-Aronszajn theorem
(
Aronszajn, 1950
)
states that, for every RKHS
H
, there exists a kernel
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
with the property that
k(x,\cdot)\in H
and
\pi_{x}(f)=\big\langle f,k(x,\cdot)\big\rangle
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. The functions
k(x,\cdot):\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
are called canonical feature maps and the property
f(x)=\langle f,k(x,\cdot)\rangle
is called reproducing property.

Conversely, for any kernel
k
, we can construct a Hilbert space
H\subset\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R})
as closure of
H_{0}
defined as

\displaystyle H_{0}:=\left\{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\alpha_{n}k(x_{n},\cdot)\,|\,\,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{R},\,x_{n}\in\mathcal{X},\,N\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

(30)

with respect to norm induced by the inner product
\langle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\alpha_{n}k(x_{n},\cdot),\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{n}k(\widehat{x}_{n},\cdot)\rangle:=\sum_{n,n^{\prime}=1}^{N}\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}k(x_{n},\widehat{x}_{n^{\prime}})
for all
\alpha_{n},\beta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
,
x_{n},\widehat{x}_{n}\in\mathcal{X}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
. The Hilbert space constructed in this way is an RKHS with kernel
k

(
Wendland, 2004
, Theorem 10.10)
.

Additional details about RKHS can be found in
Berlinet and Thomas-Agnan (2004)
,
Wendland (2004)
and
Steinwart and Christmann (2008)
. Furthermore
Schölkopf and Smola (2002)
and
Hofmann et al. (2008)
describe how the theory of RKHS can be used in the context of machine learning.

A.2
Gaussian Random Elements and Gaussian Measures

Let
\big(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
be the underlying physical probability space and
\big(H,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\big)
a (separable) Hilbert space.

The measurable mapping
F:\Omega\to H
is called Gaussian Random Element (GRE) in
H
if
\langle F,h\rangle:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a Gaussian random variable for all
h\in H

16
16

16

In this context a Gaussian random variable
X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,0)
is defined as Dirac measure at
\mu

(
Van Neerven, 2008
, Definition 4.1)
. If
F
is a GRE then
\mathbb{E}\big[\|F\|^{2}\big]<\infty

(
Van Neerven, 2008
, Theorem 4.3)
and we can define the mean element
\mathbb{E}[F]\in H
and the covariance operator
\mathbb{C}[F]:H\to H
via

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[F]

\displaystyle:=\int F(\omega)\,d\mathbb{P}(\omega)

(31)

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[F]h

\displaystyle:=\int F(\omega)\langle F,h\rangle\,d\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\langle m,h\rangle m

(32)

for
g\in H
, where the integrals are understood as Bochner integrals. By standard rules for Bochner integrals, one obtains
\langle\mathbb{E}[F],h\rangle=\mathbb{E}\big[\langle F,h\rangle\big]
,
\langle\mathbb{C}[F]h,g\rangle=\mathbb{C}\big[\langle F,h\rangle,\langle F,g\rangle\big]
and consequently
\langle F,h\rangle\sim\mathcal{N}\big(\langle\mathbb{E}[F],h\rangle,\langle\mathbb{C}[F]h,h\rangle\big)
.
The covariance operator
\mathbb{C}[F]:H\to H
is a positive, symmetric, trace class operator
(
Da Prato and Zabczyk, 2014
, Proposition 2.16)
.
Conversely, for a given mean element
m\in H
and given positive, symmetric, trace class operator
C:H\to H
there exits a GRE
F:\Omega\to H
such that
\mathbb{E}[F]=m
and
\mathbb{C}[F]=C

(
Da Prato and Zabczyk, 2014
, Proposition 2.18)
. We write
F\sim\mathcal{N}(m,C)
for a GRE with mean element
m
and covariance operator
C
.

A probability measure
P
defined on the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(H)
is called Gaussian measure if and only if the push-forward measure
T\#P:\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\to[0,1],B\mapsto T\#P(B):=P\big(T^{-1}(B)\big)
is a Gaussian measure on
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
for all bounded, linear functionals
T:H\to\mathbb{R}
. Notice that by Riesz representation theorem every bounded, linear functional
T
is of the form
T=\langle\cdot,h\rangle
for a
h\in H
. Hence, by definition, if
F:\Omega\to H
is a GRE then the push-forward measure
F\#\mathbb{P}
is a GM. Consequently, the statements about GREs outlined above carry over to GMs mutatis mutandis.

Bogachev (1998)
is the authoritative source on Gaussian measures and discusses their properties on general Fréchet spaces. Gaussian measures on Banach and Hilbert spaces are described in
Da Prato and Zabczyk (2014)
.
Van Neerven (2008)
discusses Banach space valued Gaussian random elements.

Appendix B
Wasserstein gradient flow in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(H)

Let
\big(H,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\big)
be a separable Hilbert space and

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{2}(H):=\left\{\mu:\mathcal{B}(H)\to[0,1]\,\big|\;\mu(H)=1,\,\int_{H}||u||^{2}\,d\mu(u)<\infty\right\}

(33)

be the space of Borel probability measures on
\mathcal{B}(H)

\mathcal{B}(H)
denotes the Borel
\sigma

H

\displaystyle L^{2}(Q)

\displaystyle:=\left\{f:H\to\mathbb{R}\,\big|\,\int\big(f(u)\big)^{2}\,dQ(u)<\infty\right\}

(34)

\displaystyle L^{2}(Q;H)

\displaystyle:=\left\{f:H\to H\,\big|\,\int\langle f(u),f(u)\rangle\,dQ(u)<\infty\right\}

(35)

We are interested in calculating the Wasserstein gradient flow for

\displaystyle L(Q)=\int_{H}\ell(u)\,dQ(u)+\operatorname{KL}(Q,\Pi),

(36)

Figalli and Glaudo (2021, Definition 4.2.2)

Definition 5
.

Let

\phi\in L^{2}(Q;H)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}L\big(Q(t)\big)=\int\langle\phi(u),v(u)\rangle\,dQ(u)

(38)

\big(Q(t)\big)_{t\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon)}\subset\mathcal{P}_{2}(H)

Q(0)=Q

at

\nabla_{W}L[Q]:H\to H

at
Q

We will now discuss how to construct a curve
\big(Q(t)\big)_{t\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon)}\subset\mathcal{P}_{2}(H)
at
Q
with tangent vector
v
. Let
u(t;u_{0})\in H
be the solution to the ODE

\displaystyle u(0)

\displaystyle=u_{0}

(39)

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=v\big(u(t)\big)

(40)

for
t\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon)
with given initial value

. We assume that
v\in L^{2}(Q;H)
is sufficiently regular so that a solution exits for small enough
\epsilon>0
. Then
\big(Q(t)\big)_{t\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon)}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(H)
with
Q(t):=u(t;\cdot)\#Q
is a smooth curve in

with tangent vector

at

.

Indeed, it suffices to show that (

) holds for all curves constructed via (

) and (

)

.

Theorem 6
.

The Wasserstein gradient for

\displaystyle\nabla_{W}L[Q](u)=D\ell(u)+D\log(dQ/d\Pi)(u)

(41)

for all
u\in H
and

whenever

Proof.

Let
\big(Q(t)\big)
be the curve constructed in (

) and (

).We denote by

the Radon-Nikodym derivative of

\Pi

.

We start the proof by deriving an equation for the time evolution of

.
The Hilbert space version of the Fokker-Planck equation (FPE)
(

The moments of the posterior measure

Our Bayesian model consist of:

•

A prior measure
\Pi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(H)

•

A likelihood function

\displaystyle\frac{d\Pi_{\text{B}}}{d\Pi}(f)=\frac{p(y|f)}{p(y)},

Theorem 8
.

The Bayesian posterior satisfies
\Pi_{\text{B}}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(H)
for
\gamma
-almost every

.

Proof.

Let
\big(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
be an (sufficiently large) underlying probability space and

,

two measurable mappings such that

Let

. Put differently, a kernel matrix based on
r
would be extremely uninformative. This is why we choose
\nu=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{n}}
or an approximation based on sub-samples of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
in all our experiments.

Appendix F
Langevin SDE in ONB representation

The time evolution of the SDE in
H
is given as (cf. Theorem
7
)

\displaystyle dF(t)=-\left(D\ell\big(F(t)\big)+C^{-1}F(t)\right)dt+\sqrt{2}dW(t)

(109)

for
t\in(0,T]
and
F(0)=F_{0}
with
F_{0}\in H
given. Define now
F^{m}(t):=\langle F(t),e_{m}\rangle
where
\{\lambda_{m},e_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
is the spectral decomposition of the covariance operator
C
in (
9
). Define

\phi:H_{k}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\phi(f):=\langle f,e_{m}\rangle
and by linearity it’s Fréchet derivatives are given as
D\phi(f)=e_{m}
and
D^{2}\phi=0
and consequently by Ito’s rule
(
Da Prato and Zabczyk, 2014
, Chapter 4.4)
we obtain

\displaystyle dF^{m}(t)

\displaystyle=d\phi\big(F(t)\big)

(110)

\displaystyle=-\langle D\ell\big(F(t)\big)+C^{-1}F(t),e_{m}\rangle dt+\sqrt{2}d\langle W(t),e_{m}\rangle

(111)

\displaystyle=-\langle D\ell\big(F(t)\big),e_{m}\rangle dt-\langle C^{-1}F(t),e_{m}\rangle dt+\sqrt{2}d\langle W(t),e_{m}\rangle.

(112)

Recall, that our loss
\ell
is of the form
(
6
)
and we therefore can simplify further as

\displaystyle dF^{m}(t)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\partial_{2}c)\big(y_{n},F_{t}(x_{n})\big)\langle k(x_{n},\cdot),e_{m}\rangle dt-\langle F(t),C^{-1}e_{m}\rangle dt+\sqrt{2}d\langle W(t),e_{m}\rangle

(113)

\displaystyle=-\Big(\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\partial_{2}c)(y_{n},F_{t}(x_{n}))e_{m}(x_{n})+\frac{F^{m}(t)}{\lambda_{m}}\Big)dt+\sqrt{2}dB^{m}(t),

(114)

where we used the reproducing property and that
C^{-1}
is self adjoint. Note that
B^{m}(t):=\langle W(t),e_{m}\rangle

(m=1,\ldots,M)
are stochastically independent Brownian motions by standard properties of the cylindrical Wiener process
(
Da Prato and Zabczyk, 2014
, Chapter 4.1.2)
.

Appendix G
The Nyström method

The Nyström method
(
Williams and Seeger, 2001
)
is designed to approximate eigenvalue-eigenfunction pairs
\{\lambda_{n},b_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of the kernel integral operator
T_{k}:\mathcal{L}_{2}(\nu;\mathbb{R})\to\mathcal{L}_{2}(\nu;\mathbb{R})
defined as

\displaystyle T_{k}f:=\int k(\cdot,x^{\prime})f(x^{\prime})\,d\nu(x^{\prime})

(115)

which we introduced in Appendix
D
. For
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
independent samples from
\nu
, the Nyström method calculates the spectral decomposition of
\frac{1}{N}k(x_{1:N},x_{1:N}):=\frac{1}{N}\big(k(x_{n},x_{n^{\prime}})\big)_{n,n^{\prime}=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}k(x_{1:N},x_{1:N})=V\widehat{\Lambda}V^{T}

(116)

where
V=(v_{1}|\ldots|v_{N})
and
\widehat{\Lambda}:=\text{diag}(\widehat{\lambda}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\lambda}_{N})
. Then

\widehat{b}_{n}(x):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}\widehat{\lambda}_{n}}v_{n}^{T}k(X,x):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}\widehat{\lambda}_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}v_{nj}k(x_{j},x)

approximates
b_{n}(x)
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{m}
approximates
\lambda_{m}
.

However,
C
has the same eigenvalues as
T_{k}
and the eigenfunctions are related
(
Steinwart and Christmann, 2008
, Theorem 4.51)
with

e_{n}=\sqrt{\lambda}_{n}b_{n}.

We can therefore use

\displaystyle\widehat{e}_{n}(x):=\sqrt{\widehat{\lambda}_{n}}\widehat{b}_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N\widehat{\lambda}_{n}}}v_{n}^{T}k(X,x)

(117)

as approximation for
e_{n}
.

In many cases, using all available data
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
would be computationally prohibitive since the spectral decomposition in (
116
) costs
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
. However, for typical kernels, such as the squared exponential, the eigenvalues decay very rapidly and only the first few eigenvalues contribute meaningfully to explain the data. We can therefore chose a subsample
z_{1},\ldots,z_{M}
of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
and apply the Nyström method for
z_{1:M}:=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{M})
where
M\ll N

which leads to
\mathcal{O}(M^{3})
costs. We describe a heuristic in Appendix
N
for choosing
z_{1:M}
.

Appendix H
Sufficiency of Nyström projections

Lemma 13
.

Let
F\sim\mathcal{N}(0,C)
be a GRE and
Y_{1:N}\sim\gamma
be two random mappings such that (cf. Appendix
C
for a construction)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y_{1:N}\in A|F=f)=\int_{A}p(y_{1:N}|f)\,d\mu(y_{1:N}).

(118)

We assume that the negative log-likelihood is of the form

\displaystyle-\log p(y_{1:N}|f)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}c(y_{n},f(x_{n}))+\text{ const}.

(119)

We define
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
as
\psi\big(f(x_{1:N})\big):=\exp\left(-\sum_{n=1}^{N}c(y_{n},f(x_{n}))-\text{const}\right)
.
Then it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(F(x^{*})\in B\,|\,y_{1:N},\widehat{F}^{1:N}\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(F(x^{*})\in B\,|\,\widehat{F}^{1:N}\big)

(120)

for all
x^{*}\in\mathcal{X}^{N_{*}}
and Borel sets
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{N_{*}}
. Furthermore, if
\nu=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{n}}
, then we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\Pi}_{\infty}^{1:N}={\Pi}_{\infty}^{1:N}=\widehat{\Pi}_{\operatorname{B}}^{1:N}={\Pi}_{\operatorname{B}}^{1:N}=\frac{1}{Z}\int\exp(-\widehat{V}_{\infty}(u))\,du

(121)

where
Z:=\int\exp(-\widehat{V}_{\infty}(u))\,du
and
\widehat{V}_{\infty}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
given as

\displaystyle\widehat{V}_{\infty}(u)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}c\big(y_{n},\sum_{m=1}^{N}\widehat{e}_{m}(x_{n})u_{m}\big)+\frac{1}{2}u^{T}\widehat{\Lambda}^{-1}u.

(122)

with
\widehat{\Lambda}
as defined in Appendix
G
.

Proof.

Recall that
\widehat{F}^{1:N}:=\big(\langle F,\widehat{e}_{1}\rangle,\ldots,\langle F,\widehat{e}_{N}\rangle\big)^{T}
with

\displaystyle\widehat{e}_{n}(x)=\widehat{v}_{n}^{T}k_{X}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\widehat{v}_{ni}k(x,x_{i}),

(123)

where
\widehat{v}_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N\widehat{\lambda}_{n}}}v_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
(cf. Appendix
G
). We now want to show that the orthogonal projection
\widehat{\operatorname{Proj}}[f]=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\langle f,\widehat{e}_{n}\rangle\widehat{e}_{n}
is exact for all data points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
which were used in the Nyström method, i.e
\widehat{\operatorname{Proj}}[f](x_{n})=f(x_{n})
for all
n=1,\ldots,N
. To this end, we define
k_{X}(\cdot)=\big(k(x_{1},\cdot),\ldots,k(x_{N},\cdot)\big)^{T}
and calculate

\displaystyle\widehat{\operatorname{Proj}}[f](x_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle f,\widehat{e}_{i}\rangle\widehat{e}_{i}(x_{n})

(124)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\widehat{v}_{i}^{T}f(x_{1:N})\widehat{v}_{i}^{T}k_{X}(x_{n})

(125)

\displaystyle=f(x_{1:N})^{T}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\widehat{v}_{i}\widehat{v}_{i}^{T}\Big)k_{X}(x_{n})

(126)

\displaystyle=f(x_{1:N})^{T}\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}v_{i}v_{i}^{T}\Big)k_{X}(x_{n})

(127)

\displaystyle=f(x_{1:N})^{T}k(x_{1:N},x_{1:N})^{-1}k_{X}(x_{n})

(128)

\displaystyle=f(x_{n}),

(129)

for all
n=1,\ldots,N
. This can equivalently be expressed as

\displaystyle f(x_{1:N})=\widehat{\operatorname{Proj}}[f](x_{1:N})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\underbrace{\langle f,\widehat{e}_{i}\rangle}_{=:\widehat{f}^{i}}\widehat{e}_{i}(x_{1:N})

(130)

for all
f\in H_{k}
. Consequently, we have

\displaystyle p(y_{1:N}|f)=\psi\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\widehat{f}^{i}\widehat{e}_{i}(x_{1:N})\Big)

(131)

and conclude that the conditional density of
Y_{1:N}
given
F=f
is a measurable function of
\widehat{f}^{1:N}
. We now write
p(f(x^{*})|\widehat{f}^{1:N},y_{1:N})
for the pdf of
F(x^{*})
given (
\widehat{F}^{1:N},Y_{1:N})=(\widehat{f}^{1:N},y_{1:N})
and similarly for other marginal and conditional distributions involved and obtain

\displaystyle p\big(f(x^{*})|\widehat{f}^{1:N},y\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{p\big(f(x^{*}),\widehat{f}^{1:N},y_{1:N}\big)}{p\big(\widehat{f}^{1:N},y\big)}

(132)

\displaystyle=\frac{p\big(y_{1:N}|f(x^{*}),\widehat{f}^{1:N}\big)p\big(f(x^{*})|\widehat{f}^{1:N}\big)p\big(\widehat{f}^{1:N}\big)}{p\big(y_{1:N}|\widehat{f}^{1:N}\big)p\big(\widehat{f}^{1:N}\big)}.

(133)

Here, we notice that
p\big(y_{1:N}|f(x^{*}),\widehat{f}^{1:N}\big)=p(y_{1:N}|\widehat{f}^{1:N})
because
p(y_{1:N}|f)
is a measurable function of
\widehat{f}^{1:N}
and consequently we obtain

\displaystyle p\big(f(x^{*})|\widehat{f}^{1:N},y_{1:N}\big)=p(f(x^{*})|\widehat{f}^{1:N})

(134)

which shows (
120
). It remains to show (
121
). However,
analogous to what we do in in Appendix
J
, it is easy to show that (
15
) is a Langevin diffusion with potential

\displaystyle\widehat{V}_{\infty}(u)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}c\big(y_{n},\sum_{m=1}^{N}\widehat{e}_{m}(x_{n})u_{m}\big)+\frac{1}{2}u^{T}\widehat{\Lambda}^{-1}u.

(135)

Consequently, we immediately infer that

\widehat{\Pi}_{\infty}^{1:N}\propto\exp(-\widehat{V}_{\infty})
which proves the second equality in (
121
). Furthermore, it follow from (
131
) that

\displaystyle-\log p(y|\widehat{f}^{1:N})

\displaystyle=-\log\psi\big(\sum_{m=1}^{N}\widehat{f}^{m}\widehat{e}_{m}(x_{1:N})\big)

(136)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}c\big(y_{n},\sum_{m=1}^{N}\widehat{e}_{m}(x_{n})\widehat{f}^{m}\big)

(137)

and for the prior we know that
\widehat{F}^{1:N}\sim\mathcal{N}\Big(0,\big(\langle C\widehat{e}_{m},\widehat{e}_{m^{\prime}}\rangle\big)_{m,m^{\prime}=1}^{N}\Big)
by definition of a GRE. By definition,
v_{m}
is the eigenvector of
\frac{1}{N}k(x_{1:N},x_{1:N})
with eigenvalue
\widehat{\lambda}_{m}
and further
r(x_{1:N},x_{1:N})=\frac{1}{N}k(x_{1:N},x_{1:N})^{2}
due to
\nu=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{n}}

which leads to

\displaystyle\langle C\widehat{e}_{m},\widehat{e}_{m^{\prime}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\widehat{v}_{m}^{T}r(x_{1:N},x_{1:N})\widehat{v}_{m^{\prime}}

(138)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\widehat{v}_{m}^{T}\big(k(x_{1:N},x_{1:N})\big)^{2}\widehat{v}_{m^{\prime}}

(139)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\widehat{\lambda}_{m}\widehat{\lambda}_{m^{\prime}}}}v^{T}_{m}\big(\frac{1}{N}k(x_{1:N},x_{1:N})\big)^{2}v_{m^{\prime}}

(140)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\lambda}_{m}\delta_{m,m^{\prime}},

(141)

where
\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}
denotes the Kronecker delta. As a result, we have by Bayes theorem

\displaystyle p_{\widehat{F}^{1:N}|Y_{1:N}}(u|y_{1:N})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\Big(\log p(y_{1:N}|\widehat{F}^{1:N}=u)+\log p_{\widehat{F}^{1:N}}(u)\Big)

(142)

\displaystyle=\exp\Big(-\sum_{n=1}^{N}c\big(y_{n},\sum_{m=1}^{N}\widehat{e}_{m}(x_{n})u_{m}\big)-\frac{1}{2}u^{T}\widehat{\Lambda}^{-1}u\Big)

(143)

which proves the claim.

Appendix I
Matheron’s Rule for Gaussian Random Elements

Matheron’s rule
(
Journel and Huijbregts, 1976
;
Wilson et al., 2020
)
is a simple trick to sample a conditional Gaussian. Let
\big(U,V\big)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)
with

\displaystyle\mu=\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{U}\\
\mu_{V}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{UU}&\Sigma_{UV}\\
\Sigma_{VU}&\Sigma_{VV}\end{pmatrix}

(144)

Then
U|V=v\sim\widetilde{U}+\Sigma_{UV}\Sigma_{VV}^{-1}\big(v-\widetilde{V}\big)
where
\big(\widetilde{V},\widetilde{U}\big)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)
is independent from
(U,V)
. In other words: We can transform a sample from the joint distribution of
(U,V)
into a sample of the conditional distribution
U|V=v
.

We want to use Matheron’s rule to generate samples from
F(X^{*})|{F}^{1:M}=u
where
X^{*}\in\mathcal{X}^{N_{*}}
is a set of input locations. Since
F\sim\mathcal{N}(0,C)
is a GRE, we know that
\big(F(X^{*}),{F}^{1:M}\big)
is jointly Gaussian with mean zero and covariance matrix

\displaystyle R:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{C}\big[F(X^{*}),F(X^{*})\big]&\mathbb{C}\big[F(X^{*}),F^{1:M}\big]\\
\mathbb{C}\big[F^{1:M},F(X^{*})\big]&\mathbb{C}\big[F^{1:M},F^{1:M}\big]\end{pmatrix}.

(145)

We calculate the relevant covariance matrices as

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\big[F(X^{*}),F(X^{*})\big]=r(X^{*},X^{*})

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\big[F^{1:M},F^{1:M}\big]=\Lambda

(146)

where
\Lambda=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{M})
and
r
is the kernel defined in (
10
). Further, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\big[F^{m},F(x_{n}^{*})\big]=\langle Ce_{m},k_{x_{n}^{*}}(\cdot)\rangle=\lambda_{m}\langle e_{m},k_{x_{n}^{*}}(\cdot)\rangle=\lambda_{m}e_{m}(x_{n}^{*})

(147)

for all
m=1,\ldots,M
,
n=1,\ldots,N^{*}
and consequently

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\big[F^{1:M},F(X^{*})\big]=\Lambda e^{1:M}(X^{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N_{*}},

(148)

where
(e^{1:M}(X^{*}))_{m,n}:=e_{m}(x_{n}^{*})
. Matheron’s rule therefore takes the form

\displaystyle F(X^{*})|F^{1:M}=v\sim U+e^{1:M}(X^{*})^{T}(v-V)

(149)

for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
with
(U,V)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R)
. Naturally, we do not have access to
e^{1:M}(X^{*})
,
\Lambda
and
R
. We therefore use the corresponding approximations

\displaystyle\widehat{R}:=\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{r}(X^{*},X^{*})&\widehat{e}^{1:M}(X^{*})^{T}\widehat{\Lambda}\\
\widehat{\Lambda}\widehat{e}^{1:M}(X^{*})&\widehat{\Lambda}\end{pmatrix},

(150)

where
\widehat{\Lambda}
and
(\widehat{e}^{1:M}(X^{*}))_{m,n}:=\widehat{e}_{m}(x_{n}^{*})
(
m=1,\ldots,M
,
n=1,\ldots,N_{*}
)
are obtained from the Nyström approximation based on the samples
Z:=z_{1:M}
(cf. Appendix
G
). The kernel matrix is approximated via

\displaystyle\widehat{r}(X^{*},X^{*}):=\frac{1}{N_{*}+M}k(X^{*},\widehat{X})k(\widehat{X},X^{*})

(151)

with
\widehat{X}:=(X^{*},Z)\in\mathcal{X}^{N_{*}+M}
. Notice that inclusion of
X^{*}
in
\widehat{X}
leads to
\widehat{r}(X^{*},X^{*})
having full rank
N^{*}
.

Appendix J
Asymptotic Analysis of Projected Langevin Sampling

Lemma 14
.

Let
F\sim\mathcal{N}(0,C)
be a GRE and assume that
-\log p(y|f)=\ell_{N}(f(x_{1:N}))
for a function
\ell_{N}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
. Let further
\left(\widetilde{F}^{1:M}(t)\right)_{t\geq 0}
be the solution to the SDE whose components are given as

\displaystyle d\widetilde{F}^{m}(t)=-\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\partial_{n}\ell_{N})\Big({\operatorname{Proj}}[F(t)](x_{1:N})\Big){e}_{m}(x_{n})+\frac{\widetilde{F}^{m}(t)}{\lambda_{m}}\right)dt+\sqrt{2}dB^{m}(t),

(152)

where
\operatorname{Proj}[F(t)]=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\widetilde{F}^{m}(t)e_{m}
. Notice, that for
\ell_{N}\big(f(x_{1:N})\big)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}c\big(y_{n},f(x_{n})\big)
we recover (
15
). We want to show that

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}^{1:M}(t)\overset{\mathcal{D}}{\longrightarrow}\Pi^{1:M}_{\infty}

(153)

for
t\to\infty
where
\Pi^{1:M}_{\infty}
has the potential

\displaystyle V_{\infty}(u)

\displaystyle=\ell_{N}\big(\mu_{u}(x_{1:N})\big)+\frac{1}{2}u^{T}{\Lambda_{M}}^{-1}u+\text{ const.}

(154)

for
u\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
where
\mu_{u}(x_{1:N})=u^{T}e^{1:M}(x_{1:N})
and
\Lambda_{M}=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{M})
.

Proof.

Let
V_{\infty}
be the potential above and note that, by the chain-rule, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{m}V_{\infty}(u)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\partial_{n}\ell_{N}\big(\mu_{u}(x_{1:N})\big)e_{m}(x_{n})+\frac{u_{m}}{{\lambda}_{m}}

(155)

where
\partial_{m}
denotes the derivative with respect to
m
-th coordinate. Let further
\nabla V_{\infty}=(\partial_{1}V_{\infty},\ldots,\partial_{M}V_{\infty})^{T}
be the gradient of
V_{\infty}
. We now immediately recognise (
152
) as Langevin diffusion for the potential
V
, since it holds that

\displaystyle d\widetilde{F}^{m}(t)=-\partial_{m}V\big(\widetilde{F}^{1:M}(t)\big)+\sqrt{2}dB^{m}(t).

(156)

It is well established
(
Roberts and Tweedie, 1996
)
that—under mild assumption on
V
—the limiting distribution of (
156
) is given as
\Pi^{1:M}_{\infty}
.
∎

Appendix K
Optimal Variational Approximation

We prove a slightly more general result. Indeed, we can derive the optimal variational distribution for arbitrary inducing features
U
with
U:=\big(\langle F,h_{m}\rangle\big)_{m=1}^{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
for set of functions
\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{M}\}\subset H_{k}
. Theorem
1
follows for the choice
h_{m}=e_{m}
,
m=1,\ldots,M
. Define now for
\tau\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
the measure

\displaystyle Q_{\tau}(A):=\int\mathbb{P}\big(F\in A\,|\,U=u\big)\,d\tau(u),

(157)

for
A\in\mathcal{B}(H_{k})
and the set
\mathcal{Q}_{M}:=\{Q_{\tau}\,:\,\tau\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{M})\}\subset\mathcal{P}_{2}(H_{k})
. We can find a closed form expression for the best posterior approximation in
\mathcal{Q}_{M}
.

K.1
Proof of
Theorem

1

Below, we derive
Theorem

15
below without relying on the decomposition into eigenvalues and eigenvectors, since doing so will allow us to more easily draw upon the result in subsequent analysis.
The required steps to translate the results of
Theorem

15
into the more intuitively appealing form stated in
Theorem

1
are trivial.

Theorem 15
.

Define
\Pi^{*}_{M}
as

\displaystyle\Pi^{*}_{M}:=\argmin_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{M}}\operatorname{KL}(Q,\Pi_{\text{B}})

(158)

where
\Pi_{\text{B}}
is the Bayesian posterior. Then
\Pi^{*}_{M}
satisfies (
157
) with
\tau^{*}\propto\exp(-V^{*}(u))
with

\displaystyle V^{*}(u):=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\Big[\ell_{N}\big(\mu_{u}(x_{1:N})+\sqrt{\Sigma(x_{1:N})}\xi\big)\Big]+\frac{1}{2}\Big(\langle Ch,h\rangle\Big)^{-1}

(159)

for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
. Here,
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{M})
, and we define

\displaystyle\mu_{u}(x_{1:N})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{C}\big[F(x_{1:N}),U]\mathbb{C}[U,U]^{-1}u

(160)

\displaystyle\Sigma(x_{1:N})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{C}\big[F(x_{1:N}),F(x_{1:N})]-\mathbb{C}\big[F(x_{1:N}),U]\mathbb{C}[U,U]^{-1}\mathbb{C}\big[U,F(x_{1:N})]

(161)

\displaystyle\langle Ch,h\rangle

\displaystyle:=\big(\langle Ch_{m},h_{m^{\prime}}\rangle\big)_{m,m^{\prime}=1}^{M}

(162)

for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
. Notice that by standard properties of the GRE these covariance terms can be expressed in terms of the covariance operator
C
.

Proof.

Let
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{M}
be of the form (
157
) with probability measure
\tau
. By Theorem 4 in
Wild and Wynne (2022)
, we know that

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(Q,\Pi_{\text{B}})=\mathbb{E}_{Q}\Big[\ell_{N}(f(x_{1:N}))\Big]+\operatorname{KL}(\tau,\Pi_{U})+\log p(y)

(163)

where
\Pi_{U}
is the prior law of
U
given as
\mathcal{N}(0,\langle Ch,h\rangle)
. Furthermore, we know that
F(X)|U
is Gaussian for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{M}
by definition
\mathcal{Q}_{M}
. We therefore know by standard properties of Gaussians that

\displaystyle F(x_{1:N})|U=u\sim\mu_{u}(x_{1:N})+\sqrt{\Sigma(x_{1:N})}\xi

(164)

for fixed
u\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and a
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{M})
. We can therefore condition on
U=u
and use the tower property of expected values to obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(Q,\Pi_{\text{B}})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\Big[\ell_{N}\big(\mu_{u}(x_{1:N})+\sqrt{\Sigma(x_{1:N})}\xi\big)\Big]\,d\tau(u)+\operatorname{KL}(\tau,\Pi_{U})+\log p(y).

(165)

We now define
L(\tau):=\int\phi(u)\,d\tau+\operatorname{KL}(\tau,\Pi_{U})
with
\phi(u):=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\Big[\ell_{N}\big(\mu_{u}(x_{1:N})+\sqrt{\Sigma(x_{1:N})}\xi\big)\Big]
,
u\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and
\tau\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{M})
. From the above calculations, it then immediately follows that

\displaystyle\min_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\operatorname{KL}(Q,\Pi_{\text{B}})=\min_{\tau}L(\tau)+\log p(y).

(166)

However, the global minimiser of
L
is well-known and given as
\tau^{*}\propto\exp(-V^{*}(u))

(
Knoblauch et al., 2019
, Theorem 1)
with potential

\displaystyle V^{*}(u)=\phi(u)-\log\frac{d\Pi_{U}}{du}(u)=\phi(u)+\frac{1}{2}\Big(\langle Ch,h\rangle\Big)^{-1}.

(167)

This proves our claim.
∎

Appendix L
Optimality of Projected Langevin Sampling

Let
\{\lambda_{n},e_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be the eigenvalue-eigenfunction pairs of the covariance operator
C
with
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\ldots
and define
F^{m}:=\langle F,e_{m}\rangle
,
m=1,\ldots,M
and
F^{1:M}:=\big(F^{1},\ldots,F^{M}\big)^{T}
. We simulate according to the PLS algorithm and obtain
\Pi^{1:M}_{\infty}
with potential
V_{\infty}
as derived in Appendix
J
. The PLS approximation to the posterior is defined as

\displaystyle\Pi_{\infty}(A):=\int\mathbb{P}(F\in A\mid F^{1:M}=u)\,d\Pi^{1:M}_{\infty}(u)

(168)

for
A\in\mathcal{B}(H_{k})
. By construction, clearly
\Pi_{\infty}\in\mathcal{Q}_{M}
and so it is natural to compare
\Pi_{\infty}
to the optimal measure
\Pi^{*}_{M}
of Theorem
15
.

L.1
Proof of
Theorem

2

Proof.

Let
\Pi
the Gaussian prior measure. Define the map
\Phi:H_{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
as
\Phi(f):=\langle f,e^{1:M}\rangle:=\big(\langle f,e_{m}\rangle\big)_{m=1}^{M}
. The by construction we have

\displaystyle\Phi\#\Pi_{\infty}=\Pi^{1:M}_{\infty}

and

\displaystyle\Phi\#\Pi^{*}_{M}=\tau^{*}.

(169)

Furthermore
\Pi_{\infty}\in\mathcal{Q}
means that
\Pi_{\infty}
is dominated by the prior measure
\Pi
and of the form
(
Matthews et al., 2016
;
Wild et al., 2023
)

\displaystyle\frac{d\Pi_{\infty}}{d\Pi}(f)=\frac{d(\Phi\#\widehat{\Pi})}{d(\Phi\#\Pi)}\big(\phi(f)\big)=\frac{d\Pi^{1:M}_{\infty}}{d(\mathcal{N}(0,\Lambda_{M}))}\big(\phi(f)\big)

(170)

and similarly for
\Pi^{*}_{M}

\displaystyle\frac{d\Pi^{*}_{M}}{d\Pi}(f)=\frac{d(\Phi\#\Pi^{*}_{M})}{d(\Phi\#\Pi)}\big(\phi(f)\big)=\frac{d\tau^{*}}{d(\mathcal{N}(0,\Lambda_{M}))}\big(\phi(f)\big)

(171)

for all
f\in H_{k}
. Here
\Lambda_{M}:=\text{diag}\big(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{M})
, Consequently, we have by standard rules for Radon-Nikodym derivatives that

\displaystyle\frac{d{\Pi}_{\infty}}{{d\Pi^{*}_{M}}}(f)=\frac{d\Pi^{1:M}_{\infty}}{d\tau^{*}}(\phi(f))

(172)

for all
f\in H_{k}
. We can now calculate the KL-divergence as

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(\Pi_{\infty},\Pi^{*}_{M})

\displaystyle=\int_{H}\log\Big(\frac{d\Pi_{\infty}}{d\Pi^{*}_{M}}\Big)(f)\,d\Pi_{\infty}(f)

(173)

\displaystyle=\int_{H}\log\Big(\frac{d\Pi^{1:M}_{\infty}}{d\tau^{*}}\Big)(\phi(f))\,d\Pi_{\infty}(f)

(174)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M}}\log\frac{d\Pi^{1:M}_{\infty}}{d\tau^{*}}(u)\,d\tau_{\infty}(u)

(175)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{KL}(\Pi^{1:M}_{\infty},\tau^{*})

(176)

where we applied the change of measure formula. It remains to find an upper bound for the KL-divergence between the two probability measures
\Pi^{1:M}_{\infty},\tau^{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{M})
.

By Theorem
15
, we know that
\tau^{*}
has potential given as

\displaystyle V^{*}(u)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\Big[\ell_{N}\big(\mu_{u}(x_{1:N})+\sqrt{\Sigma(x_{1:N})}\xi\big)\Big]+\frac{1}{2}\Big(\langle Ch,h\rangle\Big)^{-1}

(177)

where
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{M})
. For
h_{m}=e_{m}
the mean vector and covariance matrix can be calculated as

\displaystyle\mu_{u}(x_{1:N})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{C}\big[F(x_{1:N}),U]\mathbb{C}[U,U]^{-1}u

(178)

\displaystyle=\big\langle Ck_{x_{1:N}}(\cdot),e^{1:M}\rangle\big(\langle Ce^{1:M},e^{1:M}\rangle\big)^{-1}u

(179)

\displaystyle=e^{1:M}(x_{1:N})^{T}\Lambda_{M}(\Lambda_{M})^{-1}u

(180)

\displaystyle=e^{1:M}(x_{1:N})^{T}u

(181)

and further

\displaystyle\Sigma(x_{1:N})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{C}\big[F(x_{1:N}),F(x_{1:N})]-\mathbb{C}\big[F(x_{1:N}),U]\mathbb{C}[U,U]^{-1}\mathbb{C}\big[U,F(x_{1:N})]

(182)

\displaystyle=r(x_{1:N},x_{1:N})-e^{1:M}(x_{1:N})^{T}\Lambda_{M}e^{1:M}(x_{1:N})

(183)

where
e^{1:M}(x_{1:N}):=\big(e^{m}(x_{n})\big)_{m,n=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
.

Further, the potential of
\Pi^{1:M}_{\infty}
is given as (cf. Appendix
J
)

\displaystyle V_{\infty}(u)

\displaystyle=\ell_{N}(\mu_{u}(x_{1:N}))+\frac{1}{2}u^{T}\Lambda_{M}^{-1}u

(184)

where the second equality follows from the definition of
\ell_{N}
and the calculations for
\mu_{u}(X)
. Due to the convexity of
\ell_{N}
, we obtain from Jensen’s inequality

\displaystyle V^{*}(u)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\Big[\ell_{N}\big(\mu_{u}(x_{1:N})+\sqrt{\Sigma(x_{1:N})}\xi\big)\Big]+\frac{1}{2}u^{T}\Lambda_{M}^{-1}u

(185)

\displaystyle\geq\ell_{N}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\big[\mu_{u}(x_{1:N})+\sqrt{\Sigma(x_{1:N})}\xi\big]\Big)+\frac{1}{2}u^{T}\Lambda_{M}^{-1}u

(186)

\displaystyle=V_{\infty}(u).

(187)

By Lemma 3 in
Dalalyan (2017)
this implies that

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(\Pi^{1:M}_{\infty},\tau^{*})\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{U}\Big[|V^{*}(U)-V_{\infty}(U)|^{2}\Big]

(188)

where
U\sim\Pi^{1:M}_{\infty}
. We now calculate

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U}\Big[|V^{*}(U)-V_{\infty}(U)|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\Big[|\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\big[\ell_{N}(\mu_{U}(x_{1:N}))-\ell_{N}(\mu_{U}(x_{1:N})+\sqrt{\Sigma}\xi)\big]|^{2}\Big]

(189)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{U}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\Big[|\ell_{N}(\mu_{U}(x_{1:N}))-\ell_{N}(\mu_{U}(x_{1:N})+\sqrt{\Sigma}\xi)|^{2}\Big]

(190)

\displaystyle\leq\kappa^{2}\mathbb{E}_{U}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\Big[||\sqrt{\Sigma}(x_{1:N})\xi)||^{2}\Big]

(191)

\displaystyle=\kappa^{2}\mathbb{E}\big[||\sqrt{\Sigma}(x_{1:N})\xi)||^{2}\big]

(192)

\displaystyle=\kappa^{2}\text{tr}[\Sigma(x_{1:N})],

(193)

where the first inequality is due to Jensen’s inequality and the second inequality uses the Lipschitz continuity of
\ell_{N}
.

We combine the results to obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}\big(\Pi_{\infty},\Pi^{*}_{M}\big)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{KL}(\Pi^{1:M}_{\infty},\tau^{*})\leq\frac{1}{2}\kappa^{2}\text{tr}[\Sigma(x_{1:N})].

(194)

Furthermore, the trace can be simplified to

\displaystyle\text{tr}[\Sigma(x_{1:N})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}r(x_{n},x_{n})-\sum_{n=1}^{M}\lambda_{m}\text{tr}[e_{m}(x_{1:N})^{T}e_{m}(x_{1:N})]

(195)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}r(x_{n},x_{n})-\sum_{m=1}^{M}\lambda_{m}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(e_{m}(x_{n}))^{2}.

(196)

Furthermore note that

\displaystyle r(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\langle Ck_{x}(\cdot),k_{x^{\prime}}(\cdot)\rangle

(197)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}\langle k_{x},e_{m}\rangle\langle k_{x^{\prime}},e_{m}\rangle

(198)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}e_{m}(x)e_{m}(x^{\prime})

(199)

for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
. We plug this expression back into (
196
) and obtain

\displaystyle\text{tr}[\Sigma(x_{1:N})]=\sum_{m=M+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(e_{m}(x_{n}))^{2}.

(200)

Furthermore we know that for
X_{n}\sim\nu
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[(e_{m}(X_{n}))^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\int(e_{m}(s))^{2}\,d\nu(s)

(201)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{m},

(202)

due to the isometric isomorphism
S
introduced in Appendix
D
. See also
Steinwart and Christmann (2008, Theorem 4.51)
. We combine all calculations and obtain for fixed
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
the inequality

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}\big(\Pi_{\infty},\Pi^{*}_{M}\big)\leq\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}\sum_{m=M+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(e_{m}(x_{n}))^{2}

(203)

and further for
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}\sim\nu

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{1:N}}\Big[\operatorname{KL}\big(\Pi_{\infty},\Pi^{*}_{M}\big)\Big]\leq\frac{N\kappa^{2}}{2}\sum_{m=M+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}^{2}

(204)

which proves the claim.
∎

The assumptions that
\ell_{N}
is Lipschitz continuous and convex is indeed often satisfied in practice. For example, the binary classification loss with logistic link function introduced in Section
3.5.1
is both convex and Lipschitz continuous
(
Steinwart and Christmann, 2008
)
. Unfortunately, the squared loss does not satisfy Lipschitz continuity (even though it is convex), but in this case the PLS measure
\Pi_{\infty}
actually coincides with
{\Pi}^{*}_{M}
.

Proof of Lemma
3

Proof.

First, consider the Gaussian kernel.
Following the findings of
Ritter et al. (1995)
summarised in
Table

2
and by virtue of the big-
\mathcal{O}
definition, we can always find
M
so that for all
m\geq M
,
\lambda_{m}^{2}\leq\lambda_{m}\leq\exp\{-(\alpha/D)x\}
.
Further, we can always choose
M
large enough so that the function
x\mapsto\exp\{-(\alpha/D)x\}
is monotonically decreasing for all
x\geq M
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{m=M+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{M}^{\infty}\exp\{-(\alpha/D)x\}dx=\frac{D}{\alpha}\exp\{-(\alpha/D)M\}.

Using similar arguments, in the case of Matérn kernels with uniformly supported distributions on intervals, we can choose
M
so that for all
m\geq M
, it also holds that we have
\lambda_{m}^{2}\leq m^{-4(l+1)}\left(\log(m)^{2(D-1)(l+1)}\right)^{2}\leq m^{-4l-3}
.
Since
x\mapsto x^{-4l-3}
is monotonically decreasing, it therefore holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{m=M+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{M}^{\infty}x^{-4l-3}dx=\frac{1}{3(l+3)}M^{-3(l+1)}.

For the general case, we follow the same arguments after noting that
\lambda_{m}=o(m^{-1})
due to the definition of trace-class operators with spectral decomposition, for which both
\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}<\infty
and
\lambda_{m}\geq 0
, so that
\lambda_{m}=o(m^{-1})
.
Hence,
\lambda_{m}^{2}=o(m^{-2})
so that for some
M
and all
m\geq M
it holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{m=M+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{M}^{\infty}x^{-2}dx=M^{-1},

which concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma

Proof.

According to Theorem

the potential of
\tau^{*}
is given as (
h_{m}=e_{m}
)

\displaystyle V^{*}(u)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\Big[\ell_{N}\big(\mu_{u}(x_{1:N})+\sqrt{\Sigma(x_{1:N})}\xi\big)\Big]+\frac{1}{2}u^{T}\Big(\langle Ch,h\rangle\Big)^{-1}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[||Y_{1:N}-\mu_{u}(x_{1:N})+\sqrt{\Sigma(x_{1:N})}\xi||^{2}\right]+\frac{1}{2}u^{T}\Lambda_{M}^{-1}u

where

\displaystyle Y_{1:N}-\mu_{u}(x_{1:N})+\sqrt{\Sigma(x_{1:N})}\xi\sim\mathcal{N}\big(y_{1:N}-\mu_{u}(x_{1:N}),\Sigma(x_{1:N})\big)

and so by Equation 378 in
Petersen et al. (2008)

\displaystyle V^{*}(u)

\displaystyle=\text{tr}[\Sigma(x_{1:N})]+\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}||Y_{1:N}-\mu_{u}(x_{1:N})||^{2}+u^{T}\Lambda_{M}^{-1}u

(209)

(210)

\displaystyle=V_{\infty}(u)+\text{ const.},

(211)

where the last equality follows from

which we calculated in (

). We therefore conclude that

and

∎

Appendix M
Projected Langevin Sampling for other inducing functions

The idea of PLS can be generalised to inducing functions other than
\widehat{e}^{1:M}
. To this end, let now
P:H_{k}\to H_{M}
be the orthogonal projection onto
H_{M}:=\text{span}\big(\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{M}\}\big)\subset H_{k}
,
h=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{M})
given as

\displaystyle Pf=h(\cdot)^{T}\langle h,h\rangle^{-1}\langle f,h\rangle.,

(212)

where
(\langle h,h\rangle)_{m,m^{\prime}}=\langle h_{m},h_{m^{\prime}}\rangle
and
(\langle f,h\rangle)_{m}=\langle f,h_{m}\rangle
for all
m=1,\ldots,M
. We first prove that
P
is indeed given by expression (
212
).

Lemma 16
.

The orthogonal operator
P:H_{k}\to H_{M}
is given by expression (
212
).

Proof.

By definition of the orthogonal projection
P
onto
H_{M}
satisfies

\displaystyle\|f-P(f)\|_{k}=\argmin_{g\in H_{M}}\|f-g\|_{k},

(213)

where
\|\cdot\|_{k}
is the norm induced by the RKHS inner product. Every element in
g\in H_{M}
can, by definition, be written as

\displaystyle g(x)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_{m}h_{m}(x)

(214)

for
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{M})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
. Exploiting this fact leads to an finite-dimensional optimisation problem for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
with solution

\displaystyle\alpha^{*}=\langle h,h\rangle^{-1}\langle f,h\rangle.

(215)

and hence
P(f)=h(\cdot)^{T}\alpha^{*}
.
∎

The next step is to derive an evolution equation for the inducing features
U:=(U_{1},\ldots,U_{M})^{T}
with
U_{m}:=\langle F,h_{m}\rangle
(
m=1,\ldots,M)
from the projected Langevin SDE which is given as

\displaystyle dF(t)=-\left(D\ell\big(P(F(t))\big)+C^{-1}F(t)\right)dt+\sqrt{2}dW(t).

(216)

Theorem 17
.

Define
U(t):=\langle F(t),h_{m}\rangle
then
U(t)
satisfies the SDE in
\mathbb{R}^{M}
given as

\displaystyle dU(t)=

\displaystyle-h(X)\big(\partial_{2}c\big)\big(Y,P[F_{t}](X)\big)dt-\langle C^{-1}F(t),h\rangle dt+\sqrt{2\langle h,h\rangle}dB(t).

Here, we denote
h(X):=\big(h_{m}(x_{n})\big)_{m,n=1}^{M,N}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
,

\big(\partial_{2}(Y,P[F_{t}](X))\big)_{n}:=\partial_{2}c(y_{n},(P[F_{t}](X))_{n})
,

P[F_{t}](X)=h(X)^{T}\langle h,h\rangle^{-1}U(t)
,

(\langle C^{-1}F(t),h\rangle)_{m}=\langle C^{-1}F(t),h_{m}\rangle)
for
m=1,\ldots,M
,
n=1,\ldots,N
. Furthermore
B(t)
is a standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{M}
and
\sqrt{\langle h,h\rangle}
the square-root of the matrix
\langle h,h\rangle
.

Proof.

We apply Ito’s Rule to (
216
)
(
Da Prato and Zabczyk, 2014
, Chapter 4.4)
with
\phi:H\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
defined as
\phi(f):=\langle f,h\rangle
which gives the dynamics

\displaystyle dU(t)

\displaystyle=d\phi\big(F(t)\big)

(217)

\displaystyle=-\left(\Big\langle D\ell\big(P[F(t)]\big),h\Big\rangle+\big\langle C^{-1}F(t),h\big\rangle\right)dt+\sqrt{2\langle h,h\rangle}dB(t).

(218)

For a loss
\ell
of the form (
6
), we can simplify further by applying the chain-rule for Fréchet derivatives

\displaystyle\Big\langle D\ell\big(P[F(t)]\big),h\Big\rangle=h(X)^{T}\big(\partial_{2}c\big)\big(Y,(P[F_{t}])(X)\big)

(219)

which concludes the proof.
∎

The SDE for
U(t)
in Theorem (
17
) can not be simulated, since we do not have access to
\langle C^{-1}F(t),h\rangle
. However, for the specific choice
h_{m}=k(z_{m},\cdot)
where
Z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{M})
are samples from
\nu
, we can find an approximation for this term.

Lemma 18
.

Let
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}
be iid samples from
\nu
,
h=k_{Z}(\cdot)
and let
C
be the covariance operator defined in (
9
). Then

\displaystyle\langle C^{-1}f,k_{Z}\rangle\approx Mk(Z,Z)^{-1}f(Z)

(220)

for all
f\in H_{k}
.

Proof.

Let now
h_{m}=k(z_{m},\cdot)
and hence
h=k_{Z}(\cdot)
. By reproducing property we have

\displaystyle\langle C^{-1}f,h\rangle=C^{-1}f(Z).

(221)

We now use a standard Monte Carlo approach by assuming that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\delta_{z_{m}}\approx\nu.

(222)

Consequently, we approximate
C:H\to H
via for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
via

\displaystyle Cf(x)

\displaystyle=\int k(x,x^{\prime})f(x^{\prime})\,d\nu(x^{\prime})

(223)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}k(x,z_{m})f(z_{m})

(224)

\displaystyle=:\frac{1}{M}k(x,Z)f(Z)

(225)

which leads to
Cf(Z)\approx\frac{1}{M}k(Z,Z)f(Z)
and ultimately to

\displaystyle(C^{-1}f)(Z)\approx Mk(Z,Z)^{-1}f(Z).

(226)

This concludes the proof.
∎

The combination of Theorem
17
and Lemma
18
leads to a (approximate) SDE for
U(t)
given as

\displaystyle dU(t)=

\displaystyle-k(Z,X)\big(\partial_{2}c\big)\big(Y,k(X,Z)k(Z,Z)^{-1}U(t)\big)dt

\displaystyle-Mk(Z,Z)^{-1}U(t)dt+\sqrt{2k(Z,Z)}dB(t)

(227)

which can be simulated in
\mathbb{R}^{M}
. For large
t>0
we hope that
U(t)\approx U|Y
. Furthermore, by Matheron’s Rule, we can transform the samples from
U
into posterior samples (cf. Appendix
I
). The covariance matrices are given as

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}(\langle F,k_{x^{*}}\rangle,\langle F,k_{x^{*}}\rangle)=r(x^{*},x^{*}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}(\langle F,k_{x^{*}}\rangle,U)=r(x^{*},Z)

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[U,U]=r(Z,Z),

(228)

since
G(x):=\langle F,k(x,\cdot)\rangle
is a GP with kernel
r
(cf. Lemma
11
).

Furthermore, we can also obtain the asymptotic distribution of the SDE
U(t)
in closed form, since it is a preconditioned Langevin equation.

Theorem 19
.

Let
\big(U(t)\big)_{t\geq 0}
be the solution to
the SDE in (
227
) with
U(0)=U_{0}
for a given initial value
U_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
. Then

U(t)\overset{\mathcal{D}}{\longrightarrow}\widehat{Q}_{U}

for
t\to\infty
where
\widehat{Q}_{U}(du)=\widehat{q}_{U}(u)du
with

\displaystyle\widehat{q}_{U}(u)\propto\exp\left(-\sum_{n=1}^{N}c\big(y_{n},k_{Z}(x_{n})^{T}k(Z,Z)^{-1}u\big)-\frac{M}{2}u^{T}k(Z,Z)^{-2}u\right),

(229)

for
u\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
.

Proof.

First, define
A:=k(Z,Z)\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
. Then the SDE in (
227
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle dU(t)=-A\Big(A^{-1}k_{Z}(X)\partial_{2}\big(Y,k_{Z}(x_{n})^{T}A^{-1}U(t)\big)+MA^{-1}k(Z,Z)^{-1}U(t)\Big)dt+\sqrt{2A}d\beta(t),

(230)

Further, we define the potential
V:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle V(u)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}c(y_{n},k_{Z}(x_{n})^{T}A^{-1}u)+\frac{M}{2}u^{T}A^{-2}u

(231)

and calculate the gradient
\nabla V
as

\displaystyle\nabla V(u)=A^{-1}k(Z,X)(\partial_{2}c)(Y,k(X,Z)A^{-1}u)+MA^{-2}u.

(232)

The SDE (
227
) can therefore be rewritten as

\displaystyle dU(t)=-A\nabla V\big(U(t)\big)dt+\sqrt{2A}d\beta(t)

(233)

which is the preconditioned Langevin diffusion with potential
V
. It is known
(
Bhattacharya and Jiang, 2023
)
that

\displaystyle U(t)\overset{\mathcal{D}}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{\kappa}\exp(-V(u))

(234)

for
t\to\infty
where
\kappa:=\int\exp(-V(u))\,du
is the normalising constant.
∎

Appendix N
Implementation Details

N.1
Hyperparameter selection

For fair comparison, we shared the same hyperparameters for
r
across PLS and SVGP. We used an ARD kernel for
k
when constructing
r
, tuning the hyperparameters of
k
. For data sets with
N\leq 2000
observations (or
N\leq 1000
for classification), we learned the hyperparameters of
k
by maximising the exact marginal log-likelihood of a GP with
k
and a mean zero prior. For a data set with more than
2000
( or
1000
for classification) observations we use the following heuristic taken from
Lin et al. (2024)
:

1.

Randomly select a centroid uniformely at random the training data.

2.

Select a subset of size
2000
(or
1000
for classification) with the smallest Euclidean distance to the centroid.

3.

Learn kernel hyperparmeters on the data through regression GP marginal likelihood with kernel
k
and mean zero prior for this subset.

Repeat the above procedure
10
(or
5
for classification) and average the learned hyperparameters

The inducing points
z_{1},\dots z_{M}\in\mathcal{X}
used for the Nyström method in Appendix
G
were selected following the greedy variance selection method in
Burt et al. (2020)
and
Chen et al. (2018)
.

Projected Langevin Sampling Algorithm

Let
\eta>0
be the step-size and

be the the end time of our simulation. Define now

where
\widehat{F}^{1:M}(t)
is the solution to the SDE (
152
) and
t_{i}:=i\eta
for
i=0,\ldots,I
with
I=\lfloor T/\eta\rfloor
and further

\displaystyle\widehat{U}(0)\sim Q_{0}

(235)

\displaystyle\widehat{U}(t_{i+1}):=\widehat{U}(t_{i})-\eta\widehat{e}^{1:M}(X)\big(\partial_{2}c\big)\big(Y,\widehat{e}^{1:M}(X)^{T}\widehat{U}(t_{i})\big)-\eta\widehat{\Lambda}^{-1}\widehat{U}(t_{i})+\sqrt{2\eta}\xi_{i},

(236)

where
\eta>0
is the step size and
\{\xi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{I}
are i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{M})
. Here, we define

\displaystyle(\partial_{2}c)(Y,\widehat{Y})

\displaystyle:=\Big(\partial_{2}c(y_{n},\widehat{y}_{n})\Big)_{n=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

\displaystyle\widehat{e}^{1:M}(X):=\Big(\widehat{e}^{m}(x_{n})\Big)_{m,n=1}^{M,N}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}

(237)

for all
Y,\widehat{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
X=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\in\mathcal{X}^{N}
.

Notice that (
236
) is the Euler-Maruyama discretisation of the SDE (
152
) and therefore
\widehat{U}(t_{i})\approx U(t_{i})=\widehat{F}^{1:M}(t_{i})
for small enough
\eta
.

N.3
Likelihood functions

Regression

As discussed in Section
3.5
the Gaussian likelihood
p(y|f)=\mathcal{N}(f(X),\sigma^{2}I_{N})
corresponds to the choice
c:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with
c(y,\widehat{y})=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(y-\widehat{y})^{2}
and consequently

\displaystyle\partial_{2}c(y,\widehat{y})=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}(\widehat{y}-y).

(238)

Classification

In binary classification, we assume a Bernoulli data, i.e.

\displaystyle Y_{n}|F=f\sim\text{Bernoulli}\big(\phi(f(x_{n})\big).

(239)

This gives rise to the cost
c:\{0,1\}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with

\displaystyle c(y,\widehat{y})=-\log p(y|f)=-y\log\phi(\widehat{y})-(1-y)\log\big(1-\phi(\widehat{y})\big),

(240)

where
\widehat{y}=f(x)
. For our experiments we use the logistic function
\phi(\widehat{y})=\big(1+\exp(-\widehat{y}))^{-1}
. Due to the well-known property
\phi^{\prime}(\widehat{y})=\phi(\widehat{y})\big(1-\phi(\widehat{y})\big)
we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{2}c(y,\widehat{y})=-y\big(1-\phi(\widehat{y})\big)+(1-y)\phi(\widehat{y})

(241)

for
y\in\{0,1\}
,
\widehat{y}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Poisson Model

We choose a Poisson regression model where we assume

\displaystyle Y_{n}|F=f\sim\text{Poisson}\big((f(x_{n})^{2}\big).

(242)

This gives rise to the cost

\displaystyle c(y_{n},f(x_{n}))

\displaystyle=-2y_{n}\log|f(x_{n})|+\big(f(x_{n})\big)2

(243)

\displaystyle\partial_{2}c(y_{n},f(x_{n}))

\displaystyle=-2y_{n}\frac{\text{sign}(f(x_{n}))}{|f(x_{n})|}+2f(x_{n})

(244)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2y_{n}}{f(x_{n})}+2f(x_{n})

(245)

for
y_{n}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
f(x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}
.

Multimodal Regression with unknown shift

Assume that the data
Y
is generated as

Y_{n}|F=f\sim f(x_{n})+Z\cdot c+\sigma\epsilon

where
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
Z\sim B(\alpha)
,
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
\sigma>0
. Essentially, there is a latent variable
Z
which is unbobserved that cases an offset
c
. This leads to a mixture likelihood for
Y
given as

p(y_{n}|f(x_{n}))=\alpha\mathcal{N}(y_{n}|f(x_{n})+c,\sigma^{2})+(1-\alpha)\mathcal{N}(y_{n}|f(x_{n}),\sigma^{2})

Consequently, we obtain the cost

c(y_{n},f(x_{n}))=-\log p(y_{n}|f(x_{n}))=-\log\Big(\alpha\mathcal{N}(y_{n}|f(x_{n})+c,\sigma^{2})+(1-\alpha)\mathcal{N}(y_{n}|f(x_{n}),\sigma^{2})\Big).

and the partial derrivative with respect to second component is

\displaystyle\partial_{2}c(y_{n},f(x_{n}))=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma^{3}p(y_{n}|f(x_{n}))}\Big(\alpha(y_{n}-f(x_{n})-c)\exp\Big(-\frac{(y_{n}-f(x_{n})-c)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle+(1-\alpha)(y_{n}-f(x_{n}))\exp\Big(-\frac{(y_{n}-f(x_{n}))^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Big)\Big).

N.4
Time and Space Complexity

This section discusses the time and space complexity requirements of producing
J\in\mathbb{N}
posterior samples from
F|Y
with our method.

Training

We calculate the spectral decomposition of
\frac{1}{M}k(z_{1:M},z_{1:M})
and store the result which can be done in
\mathcal{O}(M^{3})
. The update in (
236
) requires only matrix multiplications which are each dominated by
\mathcal{O}(NM)
.

The total costs therefore are
\mathcal{O}\big(M^{3}+JNM\big)
. These costs could be reduced further by batch-approximations of the gradient. However, the data set considered in this paper serve illustration purposes only and a batch-approximation was not required.

Prediction

Let
x_{1:N_{*}}\in\mathcal{X}^{N_{*}}
be a set of input points for which we want to generate posterior samples, i.e.
F_{j}(x_{1:N_{*}})\approx F(x_{1:N_{*}})|y_{1:N}
. Generating one sample in (
246
) requires jointly sampling
\big(G_{j}(X^{*}),\langle G_{j},h\rangle\big)
from a multivariate Gaussian which is
\mathcal{O}\big((N_{*}+M)^{3}\big)
for calculating the Cholesky (or the spectral) decomposition once and then
\mathcal{O}\big((N_{*}+M)^{2}\big)
for generating samples. The matrix multiplication is
\mathcal{O}(N_{*}M)
. Hence we pay
\mathcal{O}\big((N_{*}+M)^{3}\big)
upfront for the Cholesky decomposition and then
\mathcal{O}\big(J(N_{*}+M)^{2})
.

For special kernels, this could be further improved by using the exploiting the ideas discussed in
Wilson et al. (2020)
.

Space Complexity

Space complexity in our implementation is
\mathcal{O}(NM+M^{2})
to store the
k(z_{1:M},x_{1:N})
and
k(z_{1:M},z_{1:M})
matrix.
