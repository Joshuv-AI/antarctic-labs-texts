Prevalence and determinants of burnout among anaesthetists in Switzerland
Authors: Greta Gasciauskaite, Julia Braun, Mélanie Suppan, Christoph B Nöthiger, Georges L Savoldelli, Mia Gisselbaek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDThe increasing prevalence of burnout among healthcare professionals has an impact on providers’ well being, the quality of patient care and the long-term sustainability of healthcare systems.OBJECTIVESThe primary objectives of this study were to assess the prevalence of burnout among anaesthetists in the German-, French- and Italian-speaking regions of Switzerland and to identify key risk factors that could serve as targets for prevention.DESIGNCross-sectional survey study.SETTINGThirty-three anaesthesia departments across Switzerland.PARTICIPANTSTrainees and board-certified anaesthetists.METHODSAn online questionnaire was sent to participants. Burnout was assessed with the Maslach Burnout Inventory, and the questionnaire also included items related to workplace and personal risk factors.RESULTSAcross all analysed regions of Switzerland 245/454 (54 (95% confidence interval (CI), 49 to 59)% of respondents were at high risk of burnout, and 69/454 (15 (95% CI, 12 to 19)% met the criteria for burnout syndrome. The prevalence of high burnout risk and burnout syndrome was highest in the German-speaking region, with 190/302 (63 (95% CI, 57 to 68)% of anaesthetists at high risk and 60/302 (20 (95% CI, 16 to 25)% meeting the criteria for burnout syndrome. Logistic regression analysis showed moderate evidence of a sex difference, with male anaesthetists having nearly twice the odds of experiencing burnout syndrome compared with female anaesthetists. The odds of burnout syndrome were significantly lower in regions outside of the German-speaking part of Switzerland.CONCLUSIONAnaesthetists in Switzerland experience a significant prevalence of burnout, which varies across linguistic-cultural regions, with the highest prevalence in the German-speaking cohort. Males are at greater risk of experiencing burnout.

Introduction

In recent years, healthcare workers have increasingly reported substantial mental health burdens related to workplace demands and resource shortages.1–3 Key contributing factors include staff shortages, inadequate working conditions and high stress levels. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that between January 2020 and April 2022, at least a quarter of healthcare workers experienced symptoms of anxiety, depression and burnout, with no significant improvements since 2022.4,5

Burnout is a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been effectively managed. It is characterised by emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation or cynicism and reduced professional accomplishment, and is classified in the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) as an occupational phenomenon.6,7 Among medical personnel, burnout is associated with physical and mental health problems, cognitive impairment, reduced job satisfaction and performance, increased medical errors and higher staff turnover.8–10

Anaesthetists work in high-intensity clinical settings and are exposed to considerable occupational stress. Accordingly, burnout prevalence ranges from 10% to 41%, with rates up to 55% among those working in intensive care units.11,12 A recent study in German-speaking Switzerland reported a burnout prevalence of 18% among anaesthetists,13 while a study from Latin Switzerland found a prevalence of 10% among anaesthesia residents and junior anaesthetists.14 However, evidence regarding regional differences across the country remains limited. Given the linguistic, cultural and organisational diversity of Switzerland, pooling data across regions allows a more comprehensive assessment of burnout burden and associated risk factors.

To better understand the situation among anaesthetists across Switzerland's linguistic-cultural regions, we conducted a multicentre cross-sectional study. The primary objectives were to assess regional prevalence and identify key risk factors as potential targets for prevention. Ultimately, this study aims to raise awareness of burnout syndrome in the anaesthesia profession and contribute to the development of effective prevention strategies.

Methods

Ethics

The Cantonal Ethics Committee of Geneva (Commission Cantonale d’Ethique de la Recherche sur l’être humain (CCER), Geneva, Switzerland) approved the study protocol and issued a declaration of nonjurisdiction (Basec No. Req-2024-01152) on 22 October 2024. Participants were informed of the scientific objectives of the survey and assured that all responses would remain anonymous and not linked to any identifiable data. Consent was implied by the participants’ actions of opening, completing and submitting the survey. The study adhered to the Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS) guidelines (Supplemental Digital Content (SDC) 1).

Study design

This multicentre study is a pooled data analysis of two separate cross-sectional studies conducted at anaesthesia training hospitals in the German-, French- and Italian-speaking regions of Switzerland, using an online questionnaire.13,14 Initially, two separate research teams were established, one for the German-speaking region and one for the French- and Italian-speaking regions. Data collection was conducted separately in the two regions. In the German-speaking region, data were collected between April and May 2023. In the French- and Italian-speaking regions, data were collected between June and November 2023. In both cases, heads of anaesthesia departments were contacted by phone and E-Mail and asked for institutional approval before the survey link was distributed. Following approval, eligible anaesthesia personnel were invited via E-Mail to participate voluntarily and anonymously. Contact was made with the heads of 41 anaesthesia departments in training hospitals in the German-speaking region, 9 in the French-speaking region and 2 in the Italian-speaking region, representing all the eligible hospitals. After receiving approval, anaesthesia care providers were invited via E-Mail to voluntarily participate, with confidentiality assured (SDC 2). In the German-speaking region, department heads, chief physicians, staff anaesthetists, residents and nurses were invited to participate in the study, whereas in the French- and Italian-speaking regions, only residents and staff anaesthetists were invited. Since the collected data varied in terms of the population, only residents and staff anaesthetists from all regions analysed were included in the data investigation in this study.

The survey link was distributed to training hospitals across categories A1, A2, B and C to assess potential differences, while ensuring the anonymity of individual centres. These categories are defined by the Swiss Institute for Postgraduate and Continuing Education in Medicine (SIWF/ISFM).15 They differ in training duration, scope and procedure complexity, with A1 encompassing the broadest range of competencies. A detailed description of the differences between the categories is provided in SDC 3. The translation of the original surveys sent to the German-, French- and Italian-speaking parts of Switzerland, before removing the questions on risk factors that do not match in both surveys, is provided in SDC 4.

As this study aimed to survey the entire population of anaesthesia physicians working in academic hospital in Switzerland, no formal sample size calculation was conducted.

Survey questionnaire

Both research groups assessed burnout using the validated Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI), a widely used tool for evaluating burnout among healthcare professionals.16–18 The MBI includes 22 items covering three burnout dimensions: emotional exhaustion (9 items), depersonalisation (5 items) and personal accomplishment (8 items). Each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale, from 0 (never) to 6 (every day). A high risk of burnout was defined as scores of 30 or higher on emotional exhaustion and/or 12 or higher on depersonalisation. According to the WHO and Maslach et al., burnout syndrome is characterised by high scores in emotional exhaustion (≥30) and depersonalisation (≥12), coupled with a low score (≤33) in personal accomplishment, which we used in our data analysis.7,19,20 The French and German validated versions of the tool were used in the different populations. Italian speaking individuals in the Swiss system have completed part of their medical studies in French-speaking universities (Lausanne, Geneva).

Participants were asked to provide demographic information, including age, sex, professional position, and years of experience in anaesthesia (SDC 2). In the German-speaking region, the questionnaire was administered via SurveyMonkey (SurveyMonkey Inc., San Mateo, CA, USA). In the French- and Italian-speaking regions, data collection and anonymisation were managed using the Joomla 3.10 content management system (Open Source Matters, New York, NY, USA), secured by Akeeba Admin Tools 6.1 (Akeeba Ltd., Nicosia, Cyprus) and RSFirewall 3.10 (RSJoomla, Constanta, Romania). The online interface, usability and technical functionality were pretested by five professional colleagues. Responses from this pretesting phase were excluded from the analysis. Total estimated survey time was of 10 min in both populations. Before pooling, both datasets were reviewed and harmonised. Variables with equivalent conceptual meaning (e.g., age, sex, professional position, hospital category, MBI subscale scores) were recoded to ensure consistent categorisation and reference groups. Variables that were not identically assessed in both surveys were excluded from the pooled analysis. Burnout definitions were applied uniformly across datasets according to established MBI cut-offs.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as medians [interquartile ranges] or as mean ± SD for continuous data, and as number (percentage) for categorical data. The prevalence of burnout risk or symptoms was calculated as a proportion, along with the corresponding 95% confidence interval, for each language region. Due to the small sample size in the Italian-speaking region, we present the prevalence of burnout dimensions, high risk of burnout, and burnout syndrome for the French- and Italian-speaking groups together. To investigate factors influencing the occurrence of burnout syndrome, a logistic regression model was applied. Covariates were chosen based on expert knowledge and included hospital category (reference: A1), work position (reference: resident, 1st–2nd year), age, sex (reference: female) and language region (reference: German). The regression model was limited to variables that were assessed identically across both surveys. Important occupational and organisational factors, such as workload, night shift frequency, job control, work-life balance and institutional support, were not included in the pooled model because these variables were not uniformly available across datasets. An interaction term between sex and language region was included to investigate if there is a sex difference in different parts of the country. As the number of missing values was very low, no imputation methods were applied, and missing values were reported where applicable. Generalised variance inflation factors were calculated for each of the variables included in the model to assess the presence of multicollinearity. The Hosmer–Lemeshow-test was used to assess goodness-of-fit. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Of the 52 anaesthesia training hospitals contacted nationwide, 63% (33) participated in the online survey, with participation rates of 54% (22/41) in German-speaking, 100% (9/9) in French-speaking, and 100% (2/2) in Italian-speaking regions of Switzerland. In the German-speaking cohort, the response rate among the anaesthesia providers contacted, including department heads, chief physicians, staff anaesthetists, residents and nurses, was 42% (688/1630). In the French-speaking cohort, which included only residents and staff anaesthetists, it was 49% (146/297), and in the Italian-speaking cohort, also limited to residents and staff anaesthetists, 29% (6/21) (Fig. 1). Discussion

Our findings indicate substantial regional variation in burnout risk among Swiss anaesthetists, suggesting that structural and cultural factors within training systems and workplace environments may significantly influence physician well being.21 Regional differences in participation rates may reflect cultural differences; lower response rates may be influenced by greater stigma and fear of professional or financial repercussions, potentially also having an impact on the mental health status across regions. Additionally, in our study, male anaesthetists showed a trend towards higher odds of burnout syndrome compared with female anaesthetists. However, given the cross-sectional design and the possibility of residual confounding, this finding should be interpreted cautiously.

Despite improvements in working conditions over time, the fundamental culture of medicine remains highly demanding.22 From medical studies onwards, it is expected that physicians embody resilience, prioritise work over personal well being, hesitate to seek help, and be invincible. The so-called “invincibility myth” persists, likely shaping professional expectations and potentially discouraging self-care.23 Physicians are often “very successful“ at hiding their own illnesses and face high expectations from patients and the public, as they face pressure to excel.24 Shifts toward competency-based training aim to improve physician preparedness, but they may also introduce additional pressures, as medical students and physicians feel the need to “tick all the boxes” and meet unrealistic standard.25

The overall rates of burnout among anaesthetists observed in our study are high and align with the findings of Afonso et al. in 2021 in the USA, where 59% of participants were at high risk of burnout and 14% met the criteria for burnout.18 The reasons are multiple: anaesthetists work under intense pressure, with limited control over their time schedules and unpredictable workload;26 anaesthesiology also presents challenges, including night shifts, sleep disruption, long working hours and the constant demands of high-stakes decision-making – factors that deplete emotional resilience.13,27 Levels of impostor phenomenon have also been found high within anaesthesiology, as it is a known risk factor for burnout.14,28,29 The physical and emotional isolation of working in the operating room further limits access to emotional support, exacerbating exhaustion.18,30

Addressing burnout in anaesthesiology is crucial due to its strong correlation with diminished care quality, lowered productivity, substance abuse and suicidal ideation.31 Given their access to addictive drugs, it is paramount to provide anaesthetists with sufficient well being support and tools, as emphasised in the World Medical Association's statement on physician well being.24 This support is essential not only for individual practitioners but also for maintaining high standards of patient care and safety in anaesthesiology practice.

While the exact reasons for the observed regional variation in burnout risk are not fully understood, several factors may contribute to this difference. First, the anaesthesiology residency program in Switzerland is regionally divided, with different structures in the German-speaking and French-Italian regions. The training in the German-speaking region is flexible, resembling that of Germany, where residents design their own training path by applying to different hospitals for required rotations.32 In contrast, the French-Italian region follows a more structured program organised around a network of training centres and residents accepted into the programme are guaranteed rotations for their five years of training. The structured program, as well as joint training weeks for residents, may foster a sense of belonging that could be protective.33,34 Moreover, admission to anaesthesiology in the French-Italian region requires 2 years of prior experience in internal medicine, intensive care, or surgery. Prior experience in another medical specialisation could foster higher interdisciplinary skills and resilience, which may ease the transition into a new field. Furthermore, cultural differences in approaches to work-life balance may exist between the regions, although specific data on regional differences, such as part-time work acceptance or full-time expectations, are not yet available.35 Nevertheless, the German-speaking region of Switzerland is known for its strong emphasis on efficiency, productivity and professional dedication.36 These expectations may create an environment where stress is normalised and seeking support is discouraged. Additionally, in the German-speaking region of Switzerland, physicians often regard a long-term staff anaesthetist position as a professional achievement, even without academic engagement, with clinical practice frequently taking precedence over scholarly activities. In contrast, in the French-speaking region, physicians may perceive this career path as less fulfilling, potentially motivating them to pursue greater personal and professional accomplishment, either by transitioning to private practice or advancing within academia. Cultural differences may indicate potential avenues for targeted interventions that could positively influence the burnout landscape. Nevertheless, these approaches require further empirical validation.

Sex differences in burnout risk have been reported in the literature, with evidence suggesting that males and females may experience burnout through different behavioural and psychosocial pathways.37 Social role theory proposes that socialisation influences how individuals express psychological distress, with females being more likely to report symptoms of emotional exhaustion due to social expectations favouring emotional expressiveness. In contrast, males may be more inclined toward depersonalisation and emotional withdrawal.38 Consequently, burnout in males may be under-recognised because of sex-related behavioural norms and societal expectations. While sex representation data specific to Swiss anaesthetists are not publicly available, the higher rates of burnout among males is particularly concerning as research shows that they are less likely to seek help when facing psychological stressors,39–41 especially from professionals.42,43 It is therefore possible that female anaesthetists in Switzerland are more inclined to seek support at earlier stages, potentially buffering them against the full development of burnout. Moreover, the intersection of sex and cultural context, particularly in the more conservative German-speaking regions, may further influence how stressors at work are reported.44,45 On the other hand, female anaesthetists are more inclined to suffer from impostor phenomenon, which has been linked to poor mental health ouctomes.14,29 Likewise, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis showed that the suicide rate was higher among female doctors than among their male counterparts.46 Importantly, this observed association may be influenced by unmeasured confounding factors, such as differences in workload distribution, leadership responsibilities, part-time employment rates, family obligations, or coping styles. As these variables were not comprehensively assessed in the present study, the observed sex difference cannot be interpreted as causal. Rather, it should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Further research into sex dynamics within the medical workforce is warranted to better understand these trends.

Several interventions have been proposed to mitigate burnout, with some studies reporting reductions in burnout prevalence of approximately 10%.47 Both individual-focused and organisational strategies, including mindfulness-based approaches, stress management programmes, peer support and working time regulation, may be beneficial. Given the ongoing risks of depression, substance use disorders and suicide in anaesthesiology, supportive work environments that facilitate access to mental health resources without stigma, financial or professional barriers are essential. Healthcare institutions and leadership play a key role in promoting a culture in which seeking psychological support is viewed as a professional and acceptable response to occupational stress.

Qualitative studies exploring the lived experiences of anaesthetists across different linguistic-cultural regions could provide deeper insights into the specific stressors, coping mechanisms and sex differences in different settings.48 Such insights could guide the design of targeted interventions aimed at reducing burnout and preventing further workforce attrition among anaesthetists.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, due to the cross-sectional design of this study, causal relationships cannot be established. Second, while MBI is a validated tool, it remains a self-reported measure and may be subject to responder bias.49 Considering the potential impact of responder bias, the response rate observed in this study was notably higher than those reported in other burnout studies, although it remained relatively modest.18,50 Third, regional differences in response rates may limit the representativeness of the findings. Additionally, due to the comparatively small sample size of the Italian subgroup, the statistical power of the corresponding subgroup analyses is limited. Furthermore, the study focused on predefined covariates and did not account for certain factors, such as personality traits or disabilities, which could influence burnout risk. Moreover, due to the initial involvement of separate research teams for the German-speaking and French- and Italian-speaking regions, the initial study populations differed. In the German-speaking region, department heads, chief physicians, staff anaesthetists, residents and nurses were invited to participate, whereas only residents and staff anaesthetists were invited in the French- and Italian-speaking regions. Consequently, only data from residents and staff anaesthetists across all regions were included in the analysis. This initial heterogeneity in the study populations may have introduced selection bias and may limit the comparability between regions. Additionally, the survey was conducted over different time periods (April–May vs. June–November 2023), raising the question of whether seasonal variations might have influenced burnout responses. Moreover, as data collection took place in 2023, lingering effects of the postpandemic context may also have affected burnout levels, for example through ongoing structural changes in the workplace, increased workload, or delayed psychosocial consequences of the COVID-19 pandemic. Furthermore, the lack of use of Regex rules in data processing may have led to inconsistencies in responses. Moreover, since interns and anaesthetists frequently change hospitals, some participants may have been invited in one centre but provided responses from a different institution, potentially affecting the accuracy of institutional-level analysis. This discrepancy is reflected in the demographics, where a participant from the Italian-speaking region reported working in an A1 training centre, despite the absence of such centres in that region. While a significant number of training hospitals took part, particularly in the French- and Italian-speaking regions, participation from institutions was lower in the German-speaking region (54%), which could affect the study's representativeness. Centres that did not participate may differ systematically in terms of organisational culture, workload or well being initiatives. Furthermore, as the study only included anaesthetists working in training hospitals, it does not capture those working exclusively in private practice or in nonaccredited institutions. These factors may introduce selection bias and limit the generalisability of the findings to the entire population of Swiss anaesthetists.

Burnout is a multifactorial condition influenced by numerous workplace and individual factors. Although we adjusted for selected demographic and professional variables, important determinants such as workload intensity, number of night shifts, perceived job control, work-life balance, institutional support and organisational culture were not included in the pooled regression analysis. These factors may act as confounders and could partly explain the observed regional and sex differences. Their omission introduces the possibility of residual confounding, and the reported associations should therefore be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, although sex was included as a covariate in the regression model, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and the study was not specifically powered to detect sex differences. Moreover, cultural and linguistic differences between regions could also influence how burnout symptoms are perceived and reported. Finally, while measures were taken to minimise biases, self-reported surveys remain susceptible to information and recall biases.51

Conclusion

The rising prevalence of burnout among healthcare professionals has significant implications for provider well being, the quality of patient care and the long-term sustainability of healthcare systems. In Switzerland, anaesthetists experience significant levels of burnout, with prevalence varying across linguistic and cultural regions, particularly higher in the German-speaking cohort. Male anaesthetists showed higher burnout rates, suggesting sex-specific factors in stress management and help-seeking behaviours. The country's linguistic and cultural diversity provides valuable insights into potential mitigation strategies, highlighting the need for region-specific approaches to address and reduce burnout in healthcare settings.
