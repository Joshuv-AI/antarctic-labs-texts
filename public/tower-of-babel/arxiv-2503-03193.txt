Saturation of 0-1 Matrices
Andrew Brahms, Alan Duan, Jesse Geneson, Jacob Greene

Abstract
A 0-1 matrix M M contains a 0-1 matrix P P if M M has a submatrix P ′ P^{\prime} which can be turned into P P by changing some of the ones to zeroes. Matrix M M is P P - saturated if M M does not contain P P , but any matrix M ′ M^{\prime} derived from M M by changing a zero to a one must contain P P . The saturation function sat ⁡ ( n , P ) \operatorname{sat}(n,P) is defined as the minimum number of ones of an n × n n\times n P P -saturated 0-1 matrix. Fulek and Keszegh showed that each pattern P P has sat ⁡ ( n , P ) = O ⁡ ( 1 ) \operatorname{sat}(n,P)=O(1) or sat ⁡ ( n , P ) = Θ ⁡ ( n ) \operatorname{sat}(n,P)=\Theta(n) . This leads to the natural problem of classifying forbidden 0-1 matrices according to whether they have linear or bounded saturation functions. Some progress has been made on this problem: multiple infinite families of matrices with bounded saturation function and other families with linear saturation function have been identified. We answer this question for all patterns with at most four ones, as well as several specific patterns with more ones, including multiple new infinite families. We also consider the effects of certain matrix operations, including the Kronecker product and insertion of empty rows and columns. Additionally, we consider the simpler case of fixing one dimension, extending results of (Fulek and Keszegh, 2021) and (Berendsohn, 2021). We also generalize some results to d d -dimensional saturation. Keywords: 0-1 matrices, pattern avoidance, saturation functions, semisaturation functions, multidimensional matrices

Introduction

All matrices in this paper are 0-1 matrices. The
weight
of a matrix
P
is the number of 1-entries in
P
. A 0-1 matrix
M

contains

P
if some submatrix
P^{\prime}
of
M
can be turned into
P
by changing some number of ones to zeroes. Otherwise we say that
M

avoids

P
.

A well-studied combinatorial problem is the
extremal function

\operatorname{ex}(m,n,P)
, which is the maximum weight of an
m\times n
matrix
M
which avoids
P
; particular interest has centered around the square case
\operatorname{ex}(n,P)=\operatorname{ex}(n,n,P)
. The impetus for this study originated with analyzing the time complexity of an algorithm for finding the shortest rectilinear path between two points in a grid with obstacles
[
27
]
. This analysis was done by Bienstock and Györi
[
4
]
, who showed that the complexity was bounded by
\operatorname{ex}(n,P)
for certain sparse forbidden patterns
P
.

The most well-known use of the extremal function so far has been for proving the Stanley-Wilf conjecture, which gave a bound on the number of permutations of length
n
avoiding a given forbidden permutation
\pi
. Klazar
[
24
]
reduced this to a problem about
\operatorname{ex}(n,P)
, which was resolved by Marcus and Tardos
[
26
]
.

Recently, interest has also grown around a new function for pattern avoidance in 0-1 matrices, which is our main object of study. We say that a matrix
M
is
P
-saturated
if
M
does
not
contain
P
, but changing any zero in
M
to a one introduces a copy of
P
; that is to say, for all matrices
M^{\prime}
derived from
M
by changing any zero in
M
to a one,
M^{\prime}
contains
P
.

Definition 1.1

(Saturation Function)
.

The
saturation function
\operatorname{sat}(m,n,P)
is the minimum weight of an
m\times n

P
-saturated 0-1 matrix; we write
\operatorname{sat}(n,n,P)=\operatorname{sat}(n,P)
.

Note that the extremal function
\operatorname{ex}(n,P)
, on the other hand, is the
maximum
weight of an
n\times n

P
-saturated 0-1 matrix.

The saturation function, along with other related questions, was first studied by Brualdi and Cao in
[
5
]
, although it was Fulek and Keszegh
[
10
]
who later introduced the terminology of
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)
. Brualdi and Cao found certain classes of matrices for which
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)
and
\operatorname{ex}(n,P)
coincide. This work was continued by Fulek and Keszegh, whose paper answers some of the questions asked by Brualdi and Cao. However, though these early results suggested that
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)
and
\operatorname{ex}(n,P)
might exhibit similar behavior, Fulek and Keszegh found a marked difference.

Fulek and Keszegh found that the saturation function exhibits a certain dichotomy in its asymptotic behavior: all patterns have saturation function either bounded or (asymptotically) linear in
n
. Fulek and Keszegh identified multiple infinite classes of matrices with linear saturation function, and later work by Geneson
[
15
]
and Berendsohn
[
2
,
3
]
has identified numerous other such classes, as well as other infinite classes of matrices with bounded saturation function. In particular, the asymptotic behavior of the saturation function of permutation matrices was completely characterized in
[
3
]
. However, the full classification for all 0-1 matrices remains incomplete. Our paper contributes to this classification, as well as resolving some other related questions about saturation.

In Section
2
, we briefly describe some results about the extremal function, before discussing in depth the past results that we will need on saturation. In Section

, we present several new results to develop the theory of
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P)
and
\operatorname{sat}(m,n_{0},P)
for fixed

,

. In particular, Fulek and Keszegh asked whether there exists a finite algorithm to determine whether

is bounded; we answer an analogue of this question for

in the affirmative. Also, we fully characterize semisaturation with one dimension fixed, we offer a corrected proof for a result of Fulek and Keszegh relating
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P)
and
\operatorname{sat}(m_{1},n,P)
when
m_{1}>m_{0}
, and we generalize a result of Berendsohn relating
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P)
and
\operatorname{sat}(m,n_{0},P)
to
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)
to a wider class of patterns

.

In Sections
4

-dimensional 0-1 matrix with bounded saturation function for

; we generalize a result of Geneson and Tsai
[
22
]
to the case of fixing certain dimensions while the others go to infinity; and we derive a sharp upper bound on the value of the

-dimensional saturation function over all forbidden

-dimensional 0-1 matrices of dimensions

.

In Section

2
Past Results

We will represent 0-1 matrices using a
\bullet
to represent a one, an empty space or a
\cdot
to represent a zero, and a
\ast
to represent either. In line with this notation, we call a row or column
empty
if it contains no ones.

2.1
The Extremal Function

There has been much work in determining the growth rate of the extremal function for many classes of matrices. Note that the extremal function is always
O(n^{2})
since there are
n^{2}
total entries in an
n\times n
matrix. On the other hand,
\operatorname{ex}(n,P)
is always
\Omega(n)
if
P
is not
1\times 1
or an all-zeroes matrix. If
Q
contains
P
, then clearly we have
\operatorname{ex}(n,P)\leq\operatorname{ex}(n,Q)
. Rotating a forbidden pattern
P
ninety degrees or reflecting it vertically or horizontally does not change the value of
\operatorname{ex}(n,P)
. A number of operations have been found which only change the extremal function by at most a constant factor (see, e.g.,
[
29
,
23
,
28
]
).

There are many examples of matrices
P
with
\operatorname{ex}(n,P)=\Theta(n)
. For example, any all-ones matrix
P
with a single row or a single column of length
k
has
\operatorname{ex}(n,P)=(k-1)n
. As mentioned in the introduction, Marcus and Tardos
[
26
]
proved that every permutation matrix
P
has
\operatorname{ex}(n,P)=O(n)
, thereby proving the Stanley-Wilf conjecture. Geneson
[
13
]
strengthened the result by showing that every 0-1 matrix obtained by doubling the columns of a permutation matrix has linear extremal function. Fulek found a new family of 0-1 matrices with linear extremal function
[
9
]
using a reduction to bar visibility graphs. Another family of 0-1 matrices with linear extremal functions was found in
[
18
]
using a reduction to bar visibility hypergraphs.

Growth rates strictly between
\Theta(n)
and
\Theta(n^{2})
are also possible, including patterns with
\Theta(n\log n)
which were used in the proof of the
O(n\log n)
upper bound on the maximum number of unit distances in a convex
n
-gon
[
11
]
. Keszegh used a connection between forbidden 0-1 matrices and forbidden sequences to show that every 0-1 matrix
P
with a single one in every column has
\operatorname{ex}(n,P)=O(n2^{\alpha(n)^{t}})
for some
t
, where
\alpha(n)
is the incredibly slow-growing inverse Ackermann function
[
23
]
. Among the 0-1 matrices with at most four ones, the greatest order of magnitude of the extremal function is
\Theta(n^{3/2})
, attained by a
2\times 2
all-ones matrix. More generally, the extremal function of a
j\times k
all-ones matrix is
O(n^{2-1/k})
using an argument analogous to the Kővári-Sós-Turán theorem.

In order to characterize forbidden 0-1 matrices with linear extremal functions, one may investigate an equivalent problem of determining the
minimal nonlinear 0-1 matrices
, which are 0-1 matrices that have superlinear extremal functions, but all patterns strictly contained in them have extremal functions that are at most linear. Keszegh posed the problem of whether there exist infinitely many minimal nonlinear 0-1 matrices
[
23
]
, which was answered affirmatively by Geneson
[
13
]
using a non-constructive argument. A number of properties of minimally nonlinear 0-1 matrices were identified in
[
7
,
21
]
, including bounds on the ratio of their dimensions and on the number of ones in a minimally non-linear 0-1 matrix with respect to the number of rows.

Besides identifying the minimally nonlinear 0-1 matrices, another method has been applied to the problem of characterizing the forbidden 0-1 matrices with linear extremal functions. In particular, several operations have been found which can be applied to forbidden 0-1 matrices with linear extremal functions to produce new forbidden 0-1 matrices which still have linear extremal functions
[
29
,
23
]
. In other words, these operations preserve linearity of the extremal function. One such linearity-preserving operation is adding rows or columns of all zeroes. In this paper, we investigate the effect of operations on saturation functions, including adding empty columns. We also note that a number of the aforementioned results have been extended to multidimensional 0-1 matrices (see, e.g.,
[
25
,
19
,
14
,
15
,
20
,
22
,
17
]
).

2.2
The Saturation Function

Brualdi and Cao
[
5
]
were the first to obtain results about the saturation function. In particular, they found forbidden 0-1 matrices for which the extremal function and the saturation function coincide.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
5
]
)
.

Let
I_{k}
be the identity matrix, and
J_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}&\bullet&\\
&&\bullet\\
\bullet&&\end{pmatrix}
. Then for all
n
, we have
\operatorname{sat}(n,I_{k})=\operatorname{ex}(n,I_{k})
, and
\operatorname{sat}(n,J_{3})=\operatorname{ex}(n,J_{3})
.

While this result might seem to indicate that the saturation function would often behave similarly to the extremal function, Fulek and Keszegh
[
10
]
showed that it differs markedly. While the extremal function
\operatorname{ex}(n,P)
exhibits a variety of different growth rates, Fulek and Keszegh demonstrated that the saturation function exhibits much simpler, dichotomous, asymptotic behavior. In particular, they proved the following result.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
10
]
)
.

For any pattern
P
, either
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)=\Theta(n)
or
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)=O(1)
. Additionally, for
m_{0}
,
n_{0}
fixed,
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P)
is either
\Theta(n)
or
O(1)
, and
\operatorname{sat}(m,n_{0},P)
is either
\Theta(m)
or
O(1)
.

The following is also asserted as part of this result:

Claim 2.3

(
[
10
]
)
.

If
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P)=O(1)
for some
m_{0}
, then for each
m_{1}>m_{0}
, also
\operatorname{sat}(m_{1},n,P)=O(1)
.

The proof in
[
10
]
of Claim
2.3
of the theorem is flawed. Their strategy relies on duplicating empty rows of an
m_{0}\times n

P
-saturating matrix, and such empty rows may not exist, as in Example
3.3
. However, the statement is true; we offer a corrected proof in section
3
.

Theorem
2.2
leads to a natural problem: characterize all
P
for which the saturation function is linear (i.e.
\Theta(n)
), and all
P
for which is it bounded (i.e.
O(1)
). Some progress has been made on this problem, which we discuss here, but the full classification has not yet been completed.

The first contributions to the classification of patterns by saturation function were from Fulek and Keszegh. Firstly, they introduced the
semisaturation function

\operatorname{ssat}(n,P)
, defined as follows.

Definition 2.4

(Semisaturation)
.

A matrix
M
is said to be
semisaturating
for a pattern
P
if changing any entry
0
in
M
to a
1
introduces a
new
copy of
P
; here
M
is allowed to contain a copy of
P
already, which differs from the definition of saturation. The
semisaturation function

\operatorname{ssat}(n,P)
is defined as the minimum possible weight of any
n\times n
matrix that is semisaturating for
P
.

Clearly, if a matrix is saturating, then it is semisaturating; thus we always have
\operatorname{ssat}(n,P)\leq\operatorname{sat}(n,P)
. Fulek and Keszegh classified the growth of the semisaturation function completely as follows.

Theorem 2.5

(
[
10
]
)
.

For any pattern
P
, we have
\operatorname{ssat}(n,P)=O(1)
if and only if all of the following hold:

1.

In both the first and last columns of
P
, there is a
1
entry which is the only
1
entry in its row.

2.

In both the first and last rows of
P
, there is a
1
entry which is the only
1
entry in its column.

3.

There is a
1
entry which is the only
1
entry in its row and column.

Otherwise,
\operatorname{ssat}(n,P)=\Theta(n)
.

As
\operatorname{ssat}(n,P)
is a lower bound for
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)
, this result also identifies a large class of patterns with
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)=\Theta(n)
. Seen differently, this result implies a necessary (but not sufficient) condition on
P
for
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)
to be bounded.

Fulek and Keszegh also found another class of matrices with linear saturation function. Call a matrix
M

decomposable
if
M
can be written as a block matrix
M=\begin{pmatrix}A&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&B\end{pmatrix}
or
M=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}&A\\
B&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}
for some matrices
A
and
B
that are both not all-
0
matrices, where
\mathbf{0}
represents an all-zeroes matrix of any size.

Theorem 2.6

(
[
10
]
)
.

Let
P
be any decomposable pattern. Then
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)=\Theta(n)
.

Besides identifying several classes of matrices with linear saturation functions, Fulek and Keszegh also introduced a method for demonstrating that a pattern has
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)=O(1)
, namely by constructing a so-called
witness
matrix. This construction has since been used by Geneson in
[
16
]
and Berendsohn in
[
2
]
and
[
3
]
to identify examples of patterns with bounded saturation function. We adopt the terminology of Berendsohn.

Consider a pattern
P
with no empty rows or columns. For a matrix
W
, call a row or column
i
of
W

expandable
with respect to
P
if
i
is empty and changing any entry of
i
to a
1
introduces a copy of
P
. A matrix
W
is a
witness
for
P
if
W
does not contain
P
and
W
has both an expandable row and an expandable column with respect to
P
.

W
is an
explicit witness
if
W
has an empty row and column and
W
is saturating for
P
. Clearly all explicit witnesses are witnesses, and any witness can be used to construct an explicit witness by adding ones until it is impossible to do so without producing a copy of
P
. A
horizontal (vertical) witness
for
P
is a matrix which does not contain
P
, and which has an expandable column (row).

We can generalize the notion of witnesses to patterns which contain empty rows and columns as follows.

Definition 2.7

(Witness)
.

A matrix
W
is a
horizontal (vertical) witness
for a pattern
P
containing no more than
k
consecutive empty columns (rows) if
W
does not contain
P
and
W
has
k
consecutive expandable columns (rows). We call
W
a
witness
for
P
if
W
is both a horizontal and a vertical witness.

These definitions allow us to formulate the following result, which Berendsohn
[
2
]
stated for patterns without empty rows or columns, but which is also clear in the general case.

Theorem 2.8

(
[
2
,
10
]
)
.

Let
P
be any pattern. Then
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)=O(1)
if and only if there exists a matrix
W
that is a witness for
P
. Similarly,
\operatorname{sat}(m,n_{0},P)=O(1)
if and only if there exists a vertical witness for
P
, and
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P)=O(1)
if and only if there exists a horizontal witness for
P

For certain matrices, the following result allows us to restrict our attention to horizontal and vertical witnesses.

Theorem 2.9

(
[
2
]
)
.

Let
P
be an indecomposable pattern with no empty rows or columns, with only one
1
entry in the last row and only one
1
entry in the last column. Then
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)=O(1)
if and only if both
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P)=O(1)
and
\operatorname{sat}(m,n_{0},P)=O(1)
, or equivalently, there exist both a horizontal witness
W_{H}
and a vertical witness

for

.

We note that this remains a necessary, but not sufficient, condition for boundedness in the general case. Clearly any witness is also both a horizontal and vertical witness, so the forward implication holds in general; however, see Example
9.7
for a matrix with linear saturation function despite having horizontal and vertical witnesses. In this paper, we prove several new results about saturation with a fixed dimension.

Fulek and Keszegh were able to construct a witness for one
5\times 5
permutation matrix, showing that it has bounded saturation function. They conjectured that other permutation matrices with bounded saturation function exist, and asked for other examples. Their conjecture was resolved in
[
16
]
, where Geneson constructed an infinite class of so-called
ordinary
permutation matrices, showing that all ordinary matrices have bounded saturation function and almost all permutation matrices are ordinary.

Geneson’s result has been subsumed by
[
3
]
, which completed the classification of saturation for permutation matrices as follows. Recall from Theorem
2.6
that all so-called
decomposable
matrices have linear saturation function. Berendsohn
[
3
]
showed that, if we restrict to permutation matrices, then the converse holds as well.

Theorem 2.10

(
[
3
]
)
.

Let
P
be a permutation matrix. Then
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)=\Theta(n)
if and only if
P
is decomposable.

We also note that the notion of saturation and semisaturation has been extended to multidimensional 0-1 matrices
[
30
,
22
]
. Containment and avoidance for
d
-dimensional 0-1 matrices are defined the same as in the
2
-dimensional case. We say that a
d
-dimensional 0-1 matrix

is

-saturated if
A
avoids

and any matrix
A^{\prime}
obtained from
A
by changing a zero to a one must contain
P
. The saturation function
\operatorname{sat}(n,P,d)
is defined to be the minimum possible number of ones in a
P
-saturated
d
-dimensional 0-1 matrix with all dimensions of length
n
. Note that
\operatorname{sat}(n,P,d)
is undefined for all

sufficiently large if
P
has no ones, and otherwise
\operatorname{sat}(n,P,d)
is defined for all

.

We say that a
d
-dimensional 0-1 matrix

is
P
-semisaturated if any matrix
A^{\prime}
obtained from
A
by changing a zero to a one must contain a new copy of
P
. As with the
2
-dimensional case, note that this differs from the definition of saturation since it allows the matrix

to contain
P
. The semisaturation function
\operatorname{ssat}(n,P,d)
is defined to be the minimum possible number of ones in a
P
-semisaturated
d
-dimensional 0-1 matrix with all dimensions of length

. As with
2
-dimensional saturation and semisaturation functions, one can also consider non-square matrices

. In particular, we define
\operatorname{sat}(n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{d},P,d)
and
\operatorname{ssat}(n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{d},P,d)
analogously. It was shown in
[

]
that the semisaturation function
\operatorname{ssat}(n,P,d)
is always of the form
\Theta(n^{r})
for some integer

. It is an open problem to determine if the same is true for the
d
-dimensional saturation function, though the same paper showed that the

-dimensional saturation function must be
\Omega(n)
if it is not

.

In this paper, we prove some new results about saturation in multidimensional 0-1 matrices. We prove a sharp upper bound on the effect of adding a new
(d-1)
-dimensional cross section to the beginning or end a forbidden
d
-dimensional 0-1 matrix

\operatorname{sat}(n,P,d)
. In particular, we show that this bound is exact in the case when we add an all-zero
(d-1)
-dimensional cross section to the beginning or end a forbidden
d
-dimensional 0-1 matrix

. We use this to obtain a sharp upper bound on the maximum possible value of the saturation function
\operatorname{sat}(n,P,d)
. We also find the first example of a

-dimensional 0-1 matrix with bounded saturation function for any

.

\mathbf{0}

to a

, we let

in

. The embedding that takes

. There is also a submatrix

of

. The submatrix

, but a

We first develop the simpler case of
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P)

Theorem

\operatorname{ssat}(n,P)
to be bounded. We state a similar condition characterizing when
\operatorname{ssat}(m_{0},n,P)=O(1)
.

Proposition 3.1
.

For a pattern
P
and sufficiently large
m_{0}
, the semisaturation function
\operatorname{ssat}(m_{0},n,P)=O(1)
if and only if the top and bottom rows of
P
each contain a
1
entry which is the only
1
entry in its column.

Proof.

Let
P
have dimensions
k\times\ell
. Suppose that the first and last rows of
P
both contain a
1
entry that is the only
1
in its column. We will call these
1
entries
special

1
entries. Then for
m_{0}\geq 2(k-1)
, consider the
m_{0}\times n
matrix
M
such that its first and last
\ell-1
columns are all
1
s, with
0
s elsewhere. That is,

M=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{1}_{m_{0}\times(\ell-1)}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{1}_{m_{0}\times(\ell-1)}\end{pmatrix}

where
\mathbf{1}_{m_{0}\times(\ell-1)}
is the
m_{0}\times(\ell-1)
matrix of all
1
s.
For this matrix, if we change a
0
that is not in the last
k-1
rows into a
1
, then the new
1
entry plays the role of the special
1
entry in the first row of
P
in a new copy of the pattern in
M
. Similarly, if the
0
is not in the first
k-1
rows of
M
, then the new
1
entry assumes the role of the special
1
entry in the last row of
P
in some new copy of
P
in
M
. Since the weight of
M
is
2m_{0}(\ell-1)
,
\operatorname{ssat}(m_{0},n,P)\leq 2m_{0}(\ell-1)=O(1)
.

Now suppose that
P
does not have a special
1
entry in either its first or last row, and let
M
be an
m_{0}\times n
matrix that is semisaturated for
P
. Then, every column of
M
would have to contain at least one
1
entry. If there was an empty column, then changing a
0
in that column into a
1
in the first row would not create a new copy of
P
because the corresponding
1
entry in
P
would have to be special, which is impossible. Therefore, there are at least
n

1
entries in
M
, so
\operatorname{ssat}(m_{0},n,P)=\Omega(n)
. Since
\operatorname{ssat}(m_{0},n,P)\leq\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P)=O(n)
, we conclude
\operatorname{ssat}(m_{0},n,P)=\Theta(n)
.
∎

3.1
A proof for bounded saturation functions

The statement of Theorem
2.2
as originally published in
[
10
]
contained the following assertion. We note that the proof given in
[
10
]
fails to fully cover this statement, and provide a corrected proof.

Theorem 3.2

(
[
10
]
)
.

If
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P)=O(1)
for some
m_{0}
, then for each
m_{1}>m_{0}
, also
\operatorname{sat}(m_{1},n,P)=O(1)
.

The proof given in
[
10
]
of this fact proceeds by finding empty rows in a
P
-saturating
m_{0}\times n
matrix and multiplying these empty rows to find a
P
-saturating
m_{1}\times n
matrix of the same weight; it follows that if the former weight is bounded, the latter must also be. However, this strategy relies on the original
P
-saturating matrix having an empty row. This cannot work in general.

Example 3.3
.

The pattern

P=\begin{pmatrix}\bullet&\bullet&&\bullet\\
\bullet&&\bullet&\bullet\end{pmatrix}

has no
1
entries which are alone in their row; thus no horizontal witness for
P
can possibly have an empty row. Thus the strategy of Fulek and Keszegh, which relies on empty rows, would certainly fail on
P
; but by Proposition
9.10
, we will see that
P
does have a horizontal witness.

We give a corrected proof of Theorem
3.2
, using the similar idea of multiplying the rows of
P
.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Suppose for some
k\times l
pattern
P
, the function
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P)=O(1)
. Then for some
n
, we have an
m_{0}\times n
horizontal witness
W=(w_{ij})
for
P
, with an expandable column
j
. In particular, when we change the entry
w_{m_{0}j}
to a
1
, we introduce a copy
P_{m_{0}}
of
P
in
W
. Let the number of
1
entries in the bottom row of
P
be
b
. Then the bottom row of
P_{m_{0}}
contains
1
entries
w_{m_{0}j_{1}},\dots,w_{m_{0}j_{a}},\dots,w_{m_{0}j_{b}}
, where
j_{a}=j
is the expandable column.

Define the matrix
W^{\prime}
by appending to
W
a row with
b-1
one entries located in the columns
j_{1},\dots,\widehat{j_{a}},\dots,j_{b}
(the notation meaning that the column
j_{a}=j
is omitted). If
W^{\prime}
contains a copy of
P
, then since
W
avoids
P
, this copy must contain the bottom row of
W^{\prime}
, but this row has only
b-1<b
one entries, so certainly
W^{\prime}
avoids
P
. Furthermore, if we change the entry of
W^{\prime}
in the bottom row and column
j
to a
1
, we obtain a copy of
P
as follows: considering
P_{m_{0}}
as above, replace the bottom row of
P_{m_{0}}
with the bottom row of
W^{\prime}
. By construction this has
1
entries in all the same columns, and so it is a copy. Then column
j
remains expandable.

We have seen that
W^{\prime}
is an
(m_{0}+1)\times n
horizontal witness for
P
. Repeating this argument inductively gives an
m_{1}\times n
horizontal witness for any integer
m_{1}>m_{0}
, which is enough to imply the claim.
∎

Fulek and Keszegh asked in
[
10
]
whether a finite algorithm exists to determine whether a pattern
P
has bounded
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)
. As they note, this is equivalent to asking whether there exists a computable function
f(k,l)
such that, for a
k\times l
pattern
P
, there exists a witness for
P
of dimensions at most
f(k,l)\times f(k,l)
. We resolve a version of this question for
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P)
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
P
be any
k\times l
pattern with no empty columns. If
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P)=O(1)
, then there is a horizontal witness for
P
with at most
(l-1)m_{0}+1
columns, and
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P)\leq(l-1)m_{0}^{2}
.

Proof.

That the latter claim follows from the former is clear; we show the former. We know we have some horizontal witness
W
for
P
, with an expandable column. Clearly, if a column
j
is contained in none of the patterns
P_{i}
in
W
, then
j
may be deleted and
W
will remain a witness. We can therefore assume that every column in
W
is in some
P_{i}
. The bound follows by seeing that there are
m_{0}
patterns
P_{i}
, each of which has
l
columns, and one of these columns is the expandable column, which is the same for every
P_{i}
.
∎

This bound is in terms of
m_{0}
; however, we fail to find a bound independent of
m_{0}
. Such a function would resolve the question of Fulek and Keszegh. Since, for most patterns discussed here and in Section
9
, we have either found a fairly small witness or shown that none exists, it seems likely that such a bound may exist.

3.2
A generalization of Theorem
2.9

Theorem
2.9
allows us to glue together a horizontal and vertical witness for certain matrices, reducing the study of saturation for square matrices to the case with one dimension fixed. We extend the result in Theorem
2.9
to apply to a class of patterns that do not have only one
1
entry in their last row and column.

Definition 3.5

(Strong Indecomposability)
.

We define an indecomposable pattern
P
to be strongly indecomposable if there do not exist matrices
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
,
P_{3}
, and
P_{4}
, at most one of which is empty, for which
P
can be represented as

\displaystyle P=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}&P_{1}&\mathbf{0}\\
P_{2}&\mathbf{0}&P_{3}\\
\mathbf{0}&P_{4}&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}.

We present a stronger necessary and sufficient condition for strongly indecomposable matrices to have bounded saturation function.

Theorem 3.6
.

For a given strongly indecomposable pattern
P
,
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)=O(1)
if and only if there exists a vertical witness
W_{V}
and a horizontal witness
W_{H}
corresponding to
P
, each such that at least one zero in the expandable row (column) corresponds to a
1
in
P
that is alone in its column (row).

Proof.

Firstly, we show that this condition is necessary for a bounded saturation function. If there does not exist a vertical witness
W_{V}
such that at least one zero in the expandable row corresponds to a
1
in
P
that is alone in its column, then every zero in an empty row in a saturating matrix for
P
must correspond to a
1
in
P
that is not alone in its column. Thus for every zero in the empty row, there must exist another
1
in its column. Thus no matrix that is saturating for
P
can contain both an empty row and an empty column, and the function
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)
cannot be bounded as
n
grows. A similar argument holds for
W_{H}
.

Second, we show that this condition is sufficient for a bounded saturation function by constructing a witness
W
for
P
given both a vertical witness
W_{V}
and a horizontal witness
W_{H}
with the property outlined. For
W_{H}
, if row
n
contains a zero which corresponds to a
1
in
P
alone in its row, then we can take the matrix
A
to be the submatrix of
W_{H}
consisting of the first
n
rows, and matrix
D
to be the submatrix of
W_{H}
consisting of the remaining rows. Similarly, if column
m
of
W_{V}
contains a zero corresponding to a
1
in
P
alone in its column, then we define
B
to be the submatrix of
W_{V}
consisting of the first
m
columns, and
C
the submatrix of
W_{V}
consisting of the remaining rows.

Let

\displaystyle W=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}&A&\mathbf{0}\\
B&\mathbf{0}&C\\
\mathbf{0}&D&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}.

We seek to prove that
W
is a witness for
P
. This is equivalent to the following two claims: That
W
contains both an expandable empty row and an expandable empty column, and that
W
does not contain
P
as a submatrix.

It is trivially true that
W
contains both an expandable empty row and an expandable empty column. Appending any number of empty rows to
W_{H}
directly below row
n
will preserve the condition of the expandable empty column because all of the zeroes that already existed in the empty column will still have the property that changing them into a one will create a copy of
P
. Meanwhile, all of the zeroes created immediately below row
n
will form copies of
P
with the same ones as the zero in row
n
. Similar logic applies for
W_{V}
’s expandable empty row.

Appending columns, empty or not, cannot transform a matrix with an expandable empty column into a matrix without one, and appending rows cannot transform a matrix with an expandable empty row into a matrix without one, so the process of transforming
W_{H}
into
W
, first by appending empty rows underneath row
n
and then by appending columns to the left and right, will not break the condition of there being an expandable empty column. Similarly, the process of transforming
W_{V}
into
W
will not break the condition of there being an expandable empty row.

It therefore suffices to show that
W
does not contain any copies of
P
. For this, we proceed with a proof by contradiction.

Assume that
P
is contained within
W
. Any nontrivial pattern
W^{\prime}
that is contained within
W
must be of one of the following forms:

1.

W^{\prime}
contains ones in only one of
\{A,B,C,D\}
.

2.

W^{\prime}
contains ones in
A
and
D
or
B
and
C
.

3.

W^{\prime}
contains ones in
A
and
B
,
A
and
C
,
B
and
D
, or
C
and
D
.

4.

W^{\prime}
contains ones in any three of
\{A,B,C,D\}
.

5.

W^{\prime}
contains ones in all of
\{A,B,C,D\}
.

If
W^{\prime}
is of the first or second form, then
W^{\prime}
cannot be
P
because, by definition,
W_{V}
and
W_{H}
do not contain
P
.

If
W^{\prime}
is of the third form, then we assume that it contains ones in
A
and
B
, then proceed with identical reasoning for the other three cases. Its ones can be split into two subsets,
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, with
S_{1}
corresponding to the ones in
A
and
S_{2}
corresponding to the ones in
B
. Because every row in
A
is above every row in
B
, and because every column in
A
is to the right of every column of
B
, every one in
S_{1}
is above and to the right of every one in
S_{2}
, and
W^{\prime}
is not indecomposable by definition. Since
P
is indecomposable,
W^{\prime}\neq P
.

If
W^{\prime}
is of the fourth form, for example if its ones belong to
\{A,B,C\}
, we, like in the previous case, split its ones into three subsets,
S_{1}
,
S_{2}
, and
S_{3}
, belonging to
A
,
B
, and
C
, respectively. All of the ones in
S_{1}
are above all of the ones in
S_{2}
and
S_{3}
. All of the ones in
S_{2}
are to the left of
S_{1}
and
S_{3}
. All of the ones in
S_{3}
are to the right of all of the ones in
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
. Therefore,
W^{\prime}
is of the form

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}&P_{1}&\mathbf{0}\\
P_{2}&\mathbf{0}&P_{3}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Since
P
is strongly indecomposable,
W^{\prime}\neq P
.

Finally, if
W^{\prime}
is of the fifth form, by the same logic,
W^{\prime}
is of the form

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}&P_{1}&\mathbf{0}\\
P_{2}&\mathbf{0}&P_{3}\\
\mathbf{0}&P_{4}&\mathbf{0}\\
\end{pmatrix},

and
W^{\prime}\neq P
.

Thus
P
is not contained within
W
, and
W
is a witness for
P
.

Therefore, this condition is both necessary and sufficient for matrices of this form.
∎

4
Inserting rows and columns

In this section, we show several results on the effect of inserting columns and rows into a pattern. In the case when the columns and rows are empty, we obtain an exact result.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
P
be any pattern, and let
P^{\prime}
be any pattern derived from
P
by adding a single column before the first column of
P
. Then
\operatorname{sat}(m,n,P^{\prime})\leq m+\operatorname{sat}(m,n-1,P)
.

Proof.

Let
M
be an
m\times(n-1)
matrix that is saturating for
P
. Consider the matrix
M^{\prime}
derived from
M
by adding a column of all
1
entries before
M
. We claim that
M^{\prime}
is saturating for
P^{\prime}
. First, we prove by contradiction that
M^{\prime}
avoids
P^{\prime}
. Suppose for contradiction that
M^{\prime}
contains
P^{\prime}
. Removing the leftmost column of
P^{\prime}
, we obtain a copy of
P
in
M^{\prime}
; furthermore this copy of
P
does not include the first column of
M^{\prime}
, so
M
also contains a copy of
P
, which is a contradiction.

To see that
M^{\prime}
is saturating, we now show that adding any
1
to
M^{\prime}
introduces a copy of
P^{\prime}
. Since the first column of
M^{\prime}
is all
1
, any
1
entry must be added outside the first column. Then this
1
entry is in
M
, so it is part of a copy of
P
in
M
; this copy of
P
together with the first column of
M^{\prime}
forms a copy of
P^{\prime}
in
M^{\prime}
as desired. Thus
\operatorname{sat}(m,n,P^{\prime})\leq m+\operatorname{sat}(m,n-1,P)
.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
P
be any pattern, and let the pattern
P^{\prime}
be derived from
P
by adding an empty column before the first column of
P
. Then
\operatorname{sat}(m,n,P^{\prime})=m+\operatorname{sat}(m,n-1,P)
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.1
, we have
\operatorname{sat}(m,n,P^{\prime})\leq m+\operatorname{sat}(m,n-1,P)
. For the other direction, now let
M
be any
m\times n
matrix saturating for
P^{\prime}
. If the first column of
M
has any
0
entries, then changing them to a
1
cannot introduce a copy of
P^{\prime}
, since the first column of
P^{\prime}
is empty. Thus the first column of
M
must have all ones. We claim that the matrix
M^{\prime}
derived from
M
by deleting the first column is saturating for
P
. Clearly if
M^{\prime}
contains a copy of
P
, then this copy of
P
with the first column of
M
added forms a copy of
P^{\prime}
in
M
, so
M^{\prime}
cannot contain a copy of
P
. If a
0
entry in
M^{\prime}
is changed to a
1
, we know that this creates a copy of
P^{\prime}
in
M
; then this also introduces a copy of
P
in
M
which cannot include the first column, which is a copy of
P
in
M^{\prime}
. Thus,
M^{\prime}
is saturating for
P
, so
\operatorname{sat}(m,n-1,P)\leq\operatorname{sat}(m,n,P^{\prime})-m
. Combining the two directions of inequality, the result follows.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

There exist patterns
P,P^{\prime}
with
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)=O(1)
and
\operatorname{sat}(n,P^{\prime})=\Theta(n)
for which
P^{\prime}
is obtained from
P
by adding an empty column.

Proof.

Let
P
be any pattern with
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)=O(1)
, e.g., almost any permutation matrix, and let
P^{\prime}
be obtained from
P
by adding an empty column on the left. Then,
\operatorname{sat}(n,P^{\prime})=\Theta(n)
by Corollary
4.2
.
∎

We begin with an easy statement, and in Propositions
4.5
and
4.6
we investigate its converse.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
P
be a pattern and
P^{\prime}
be obtained from
P
by adding empty rows and columns. If
\operatorname{sat}(m,n,P^{\prime})=O(1)
, then
\operatorname{sat}(m,n,P)=O(1)
as well.

Proof.

Let
W^{\prime}
be a witness for
P^{\prime}
. Since
W^{\prime}
does not contain
P^{\prime}
, it does not contain
P
either. Since changing each entry in an expandable column of
W^{\prime}
induces a copy of
P^{\prime}
in the resulting matrix and
P^{\prime}
contains
P
, we also obtain a copy of
P
in the resulting matrix. Therefore,
W^{\prime}
is also a witness for
P
and
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P)=O(1)
.
∎

It remains unknown whether there exist patterns
P
,
P^{\prime}
with
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)=O(1)
and
\operatorname{sat}(n,P^{\prime})=\Theta(n)
, for which
P^{\prime}
is derived from
P
by adding an empty
interior
column, i.e., between the first and last columns of
P
. We can, however, show the following for
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P)
.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
P
be a pattern without empty columns, and let
P^{\prime}
be derived from
P
by adding empty columns. Then
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P^{\prime})=O(1)
if
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P)=O(1)
.

Proof.

Assume first that
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P)=O(1)
. From Theorem
2.8
we know there exists a horizontal witness
W=\begin{pmatrix}a_{ij}\end{pmatrix}
for
P
; let
W
be
m\times n
. It suffices to give a horizontal witness for
P^{\prime}
. Suppose
P^{\prime}
contains up to
k
consecutive empty columns. Construct the
m\times((2k+1)(n-1)+1)
matrix
W^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{ij}\end{pmatrix}
by inserting
2k
empty columns between each of the columns of
W
; that is, let

b_{ij^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}a_{ij}&j^{\prime}=1+(2k+1)(j-1)\\
0&\text{else}\end{cases}.

Clearly
W^{\prime}
does not contain a copy of
P^{\prime}
, as otherwise
W
would contain
P
. Let the
j
th column of
W
be expandable. Let
j^{\prime}=(2k+1)(j-1)+1
. We firstly see that the
j^{\prime}
th column of
W^{\prime}
is expandable; if we flip
b_{ij^{\prime}}
to a
1
, then because
W
is a witness, we introduce a copy of
P
which is contained entirely within the nonempty columns of
W^{\prime}
. Then as there are
2k
empty columns between each nonempty column of
W^{\prime}
, we are able to introduce enough empty columns into this copy of
P
to find a copy of
P^{\prime}
in
W^{\prime}
.

We can now show further that the
j^{\prime}-k
th through
j^{\prime}+k
th columns of
W^{\prime}
are all expandable. Clearly these are all empty. Consider the matrix
W^{\prime\prime}
derived by adding a
1
at
b_{ij^{\prime\prime}}
, where
j^{\prime\prime}\in[j^{\prime}-k,j^{\prime}+k]
. We know the
j^{\prime}
th column is expandable, so in particular, when we flip
b_{ij^{\prime}}
to a
1
, we introduce a copy
P^{\prime}_{0}
of
P^{\prime}
.

We modify
P^{\prime}_{0}
to create a copy of
P^{\prime}
with a 1 entry at
b_{ij^{\prime\prime}}
rather than
b_{ij^{\prime}}
. Clearly the entry
b_{ij^{\prime}}
is the only
1
entry in its column, as is
b_{ij^{\prime\prime}}
, and they lie in the same row. Furthermore, we have only at most
k
empty columns in
P^{\prime}_{0}
between column
j^{\prime}
and another nonempty column, and we know column
j^{\prime\prime}
also has at least
k
empty columns between itself and another nonempty column. Suppose there are
k_{1}
empty columns to the left of
j^{\prime}
in
P^{\prime}
, and
k_{2}
to the right; then we can create
P^{\prime}_{1}
by taking the same columns and rows as
P^{\prime}_{0}
, except that column
j^{\prime}
is replaced by
j^{\prime\prime}
, and these
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
empty columns are replaced by
k_{1}
empty columns immediately to the left and
k_{2}
empty columns immediately to the right of column
j^{\prime\prime}
. We observe that
P^{\prime}_{1}
is a copy of
P^{\prime}
in
W^{\prime}
. Thus we may safely construct a copy of
P^{\prime}
including
b_{ij^{\prime\prime}}
.

Therefore in particular the column
j^{\prime\prime}
is expandable. Then
W^{\prime}
contains
2k+1>k
consecutive expandable columns, confirming the other condition for
W^{\prime}
to be a horizontal witness for
P^{\prime}
. Thus
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P^{\prime})=O(1)
.
∎

The corresponding statement of Proposition
4.5
for adding empty rows is not as clear in general; we require certain assumptions on the witness for
P
.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
P
be a pattern without empty rows and
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P)=O(1)
, and let
W
be a horizontal witness for
P
containing an expandable column
j
such that each
w_{ij}
is the only
1
entry in its row of the submatrix
P_{i}
of
W_{i}
. Let
P^{\prime}
be derived from
P
by adding empty rows in the interior (i.e. strictly between the first and last row). Then
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P^{\prime})=O(1)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
P^{\prime}
is derived by adding up to
k
consecutive empty rows. Let
W^{\prime}
be derived from
W
by adding
2k-1
consecutive empty rows between each row of
W
. We have an embedding
\psi
of
W
into
W^{\prime}
. It is clear that if
W^{\prime}
contains
P^{\prime}
, then
W
contains
P
. We claim that column
j
is still expandable. Consider any row
i^{\prime}
of
W^{\prime}
. Let
\psi(i)
be the closest row of
W^{\prime}
to

which corresponds to a row of
W
; without loss of generality suppose that
\psi(i)\leq i^{\prime}
. Then
\psi(P_{i})
can be extended to a copy
P^{\prime}_{\psi(i)}
of
P^{\prime}
by adding to it empty rows. This copy
P^{\prime}_{\psi(i)}
has at most
k
empty rows consecutively after the row
\psi(i)
. We know
i^{\prime}-\psi(i)\leq k-1
, so there are at least
k
empty rows in

between
i^{\prime}
and
\psi(i+1)
, the next row corresponding to a row of

. Thus, we may build
P^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}
from
P^{\prime}_{\psi(i)}
by replacing the row
\psi(i)
with
i^{\prime}
, and the up to

consecutive empty rows after
\psi(i)
with the consecutive empty rows immediately after
i^{\prime}
. This gives a copy
P^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}
in
W^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}
, so the column

is expandable, so
W^{\prime}
is a horizontal witness for
P^{\prime}
.
∎

We can combine these results to see the following.

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
P
be a pattern with bounded saturation function satisfying the hypotheses of
2.9
such that each
1
entry
p_{ij}
is the only
1
entry in row
i
if and only if it is the only
1
entry in column
j
. Let the pattern
P^{\prime}
be derived from
P
by adding empty rows and columns in the interior (i.e., strictly between the first and last row and column). Then
\operatorname{sat}(n,P^{\prime})=O(1)
.

Proof.

Because
P
satisfies the hypotheses of
2.9
, also
P^{\prime}
must; thus it suffices to give horizontal and vertical witnesses for
P^{\prime}
. We know there exists a horizontal witness for
P
; furthermore, by our condition on

, this witness must satisfy the hypotheses of
4.6
. Thus, if
P^{\prime\prime}
is derived from
P
by adding empty rows, we can find a horizontal witness for
P^{\prime\prime}
. Then by
4.5
we can find a horizontal witness for
P^{\prime}
. Similarly, there exists a vertical witness for
P^{\prime}
, which completes the proof.
∎

The above result covers in particular all permutation matrices
P
. More generally, we ask whether the hypotheses are strictly necessary; in particular, we make the following conjecture, which would widely generalize the results of this section.

Conjecture 4.8
.

Let
P
be any pattern, and let
P^{\prime}
be derived from
P
by adding empty rows and columns in the interior. Then,
\operatorname{sat}(n,P^{\prime})=O(\operatorname{sat}(n,P))
and
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P^{\prime})=O(\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P))
.

This would reduce the study of the asymptotic behavior of the saturation function to the case with no empty interior rows or columns.

5
The Kronecker Product

For two matrices
P=\begin{pmatrix}p_{i,j}\end{pmatrix}
,
Q=\begin{pmatrix}q_{i,j}\end{pmatrix}
, with dimensions
k\times l
,
m\times n
respectively, we consider the saturation of the
km\times ln
Kronecker product
P\otimes Q=\begin{pmatrix}r_{i,j}\end{pmatrix}
, defined by

r_{i,j}=p_{a,c}q_{b,d},

where

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle=m(a-1)+b

\displaystyle j

\displaystyle=n(c-1)+d.

We show the following relationship for the semisaturation function.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
P=\begin{pmatrix}p_{i,j}\end{pmatrix}
,
Q=\begin{pmatrix}q_{i,j}\end{pmatrix}
be any patterns, where
P
is
k\times l
,
Q
is
m\times n
. Then the semisaturation function
\operatorname{ssat}(n,P\otimes Q)=O(1)
if and only if both
\operatorname{ssat}(n,P)=O(1)
and
\operatorname{ssat}(n,Q)=O(1)
. Similarly,
\operatorname{ssat}(m_{0},n,P\otimes Q)=O(1)
if and only if both
\operatorname{ssat}(m_{0},n,P)=O(1)
and
\operatorname{ssat}(m_{0},n,Q)=O(1)
.

Proof.

For the forward direction, suppose that
\operatorname{ssat}(n,P\otimes Q)=O(1)
. Then by Theorem
2.5
, there exists a
1
entry
r_{i,1}
in the first column of
P\otimes Q
which is the only
1
entry in its row. Write
i=m(a-1)+b
; then by definition of the Kronecker product,
r_{i,1}=p_{a,1}q_{b,1}
. Thus we must have
p_{a,1}=q_{b,1}=1
. Suppose for some
c>1
that we have
p_{a,c}=1
. Then letting
j=n(c-1)+1
, we have
r_{i,j}=p_{a,c}q_{b,1}=1
, which contradicts the fact that
r_{i,1}
was the only
1
entry in its row in
P\otimes Q
. Thus
p_{a,1}
is the only
1
entry in its row in
P
. Similarly we see that
q_{b,1}
is the only
1
entry in its row in
Q
. Thus both
P
and
Q
have a
1
entry in the first column which is the only
1
entry in its first row. Similar reasoning shows that both
P
and
Q
have

(i)

a
1
entry in the last column which is the only entry in its row,

(ii)

a
1
entry in the first row which is the only entry in its column, and

(iii)

a
1
entry in the last row which is the only entry in its column.

To show
P
and
Q
have bounded semisaturation function, by Theorem
2.5
, it remains to show that both contain a
1
entry which is the only
1
in its row and column. We know that
P\otimes Q
contains such a
1
entry
r_{i,j}
. We write
i=m(a-1)+b
,
j=n(c-1)+d
. Then
r_{i,j}=p_{a,c}q_{b,d}
. Then
p_{a,c}=q_{b,d}=1
. Suppose for some
a^{\prime}\neq a
that the entry
p_{a^{\prime},c}=1
. Then letting
i^{\prime}=m(a^{\prime}-1)+b\neq i
, we see
r_{i^{\prime},j}=1
, contradicting the fact that
r_{i,j}
is the only
1
entry in its column. Thus
p_{a,c}
is the only
1
entry its column (and similarly in its row) in
P
. Similarly also
q_{b,d}
is the only
1
entry in its column and row in
Q
.

Thus
P
and
Q
both have bounded semisaturation function, completing the forward direction of the proof.

For the reverse implication, suppose
P
and
Q
both have bounded semisaturation function. Then we have
1
entries
p_{a,1}
,
q_{b,1}
in
P
and
Q
respectively which are the only
1
entries in rows
a
and
b
respectively. Taking
i=m(a-1)+b
, it is easy to see that the entry
r_{i,1}
in
P\otimes Q
is a
1
entry which is the only
1
in its row; we can symmetrically check the other conditions of Theorem
2.5
, except for the final condition, which we check separately. We have
1
entries
p_{a,c}
,
q_{b,d}
which are the only
1
entries in their respective rows and columns in
P
and
Q
; then taking
i=m(a-1)+b
,
j=n(c-1)+d
, it is again easy to see that the entry
r_{i,j}
in
P\otimes Q
is the only
1
entry in its row and column. Thus
\operatorname{ssat}(n,P\otimes Q)=O(1)
.

We can use very similar reasoning by Proposition
3.1
to prove the version for
\operatorname{ssat}(m_{0},n,\cdot)
.
∎

We have the following immediate corollary.

Corollary 5.2
.

For any patterns
P
and
Q
,
\operatorname{ssat}(n,P\otimes Q)=O(1)
if and only if
\operatorname{ssat}(n,Q\otimes P)=O(1)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\operatorname{ssat}(n,P\otimes Q)=O(1)
. Then by Theorem
5.1
, we have
\operatorname{ssat}(n,P)=O(1)
and
\operatorname{ssat}(n,Q)=O(1)
, so again by Theorem
5.1
, we have
\operatorname{ssat}(n,Q\otimes P)=O(1)
.
∎

We note that both Theorem
5.1
and Corollary
5.2
fail when analogous statements are made for saturation, as illustrated by the following example.

Example 5.3
.

Let

P=\begin{pmatrix}&\bullet&&\\
&&&\bullet\\
\bullet&&&\\
&&\bullet&\end{pmatrix},\>Q=\begin{pmatrix}\bullet&\\
&\bullet\end{pmatrix}.

Then
\operatorname{sat}(n,P\otimes Q)=O(1)
, while
\operatorname{sat}(n,Q)=\Theta(n)
. Furthermore,
\operatorname{sat}(n,Q\otimes P)=\Theta(n)
.

All of the above follow from Theorem
2.10
. This demonstrates the failure of Corollary
5.2
and of the forward direction of Theorem
5.1
when analogous statements are made for saturation. However, we fail to resolve the reverse implication of Theorem
5.1
when analogous statements are made for saturation. Based on the known case for permutation matrices, we formulate the following conjecture.

Conjecture 5.4
.

If
P
and
Q
are patterns with
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)=O(1)
and
\operatorname{ssat}(n,Q)=O(1)
, then
\operatorname{sat}(n,P\otimes Q)=O(1)
.

A special case of Proposition
4.5
comes from considering the Kronecker product

, or more generally any Kronecker product of

with a

matrix containing exactly one
1
entry. This operation is equivalent to adding empty columns to

. This yields the following observation.

Observation 5.5
.

Proposition

implies a version of Conjecture

for

in the case of a

8
Linear Program

Now, we present a computational approach that models saturation using a binary integer linear program.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
P
be an
m\times n
pattern with weight
w(P)
. Let
\mathcal{P}
be the set of all sets of indices which form a copy of
P
in the
m\times n
all-ones matrix
\mathbf{1}_{m\times n}
and
A_{i,j}=\{X\in\mathcal{P}:(i,j)\in X\}
be all copies of
P
containing
(i,j)
. Notice that
\lvert P^{\prime}\rvert=w(P)
for all
P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
. Define variables
x_{i,j}
for
1\leq i\leq m
and
1\leq j\leq n
and
y_{P^{\prime}}
for each
P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
. For notational convenience, set
f(P^{\prime})=\sum_{(i,j)\in P^{\prime}}x_{i,j}
. Suppose
x_{i,j}
and
y_{P^{\prime}}
satisfy

\displaystyle x_{i,j}

\displaystyle\in\{0,1\}

\displaystyle\forall(i,j)\in[m]\times[n]

(1)

\displaystyle y_{P^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\in\{0,1\}

\displaystyle\forall P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}

(2)

\displaystyle f(P^{\prime})

\displaystyle<w(P)

\displaystyle\forall P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}

(3)

\displaystyle(w(P)-1)y_{P^{\prime}}-f(P^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq 0

\displaystyle\forall P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}

(4)

\displaystyle y_{P^{\prime}}-f(P^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq-w(P)+2

\displaystyle\forall P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}

(5)

\displaystyle\sum_{P^{\prime}\in A_{i,j}}y_{P^{\prime}}

\displaystyle>0

\displaystyle\forall(i,j)\in[m]\times[n]

(6)

\displaystyle x_{i,j}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\forall(i,j)\in[m]\times[n],A_{i,j}=\varnothing.

(7)

Then
\min\sum_{i,j}x_{i,j}=\operatorname{sat}(m,n,P)
.

Proof.

We claim that if we have a matrix
M
with
m_{ij}=x_{i,j}
, then
M
is saturating for
P
and
y_{P^{\prime}}=1
if and only if
m_{i,j}=1
for exactly
w(P)-1
pairs
(i,j)\in P^{\prime}
for all
P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
. This suffices because
\operatorname{sat}(m,n,P)
is defined to be the minimum possible weight of such a saturating matrix
M
.

Any saturating matrix
M
must not contain
P
. This is satisfied by equation (
3
) because it states that for every possible copy
P^{\prime}
of
P
, less than
w(P)
of those entries are actually
1
in
M
.

Fix some
P^{\prime}
. The condition that
y_{P^{\prime}}=1
if and only if all but one entry of
P^{\prime}
is in
M
is satisfied through equations (
4
) and (
5
). Let
f(P^{\prime})=\sum_{(i,j)\in P^{\prime}}x_{i,j}
, the number of entries of
P^{\prime}
present in
M
. If
y_{P^{\prime}}=1
, then (
4
) implies that
f(P^{\prime})\geq w(P)-1
while (
5
) implies
w(P)-1\geq f(P^{\prime})
, so
f(P^{\prime})=w(P)-1
as claimed. Now if
y_{P}^{\prime}=0
, then (
4
) and (
5
) become

and

, which implies that less than
w(P)-1
entries of

are present in

. This proves the condition on

.

Finally, we show that changing any

to a

in

introduces a copy of
P
. If some entry of
M
isn’t part of any

, then it must be
1
or else changing that entry to

wouldn’t introduce a copy of

. This is encoded in (

that contain

. This means that changing the

will introduce a copy of

because then some

would have

entries in

, meaning

contains

.
∎

Assume a matrix

10
Discussion

In this paper, we proved many results about saturation and semisaturation for 0-1 matrices. In particular, we determined the effects of a number of basic operations on the saturation and semisaturation functions, including adding empty columns and rows, and performing Kronecker products. We showed a number of results about saturation functions and semisaturation functions with one fixed dimension, extending several results from the case where both dimensions have the same length. We introduced witness graphs, which we used to determine the saturation functions of some previously unresolved patterns. We also found a linear program for computing the saturation function, and we used it to compute the saturation function for small values of
n
and make some more general conjectures. Finally, we determined the saturation functions of many small, previously unresolved patterns up to a constant factor. In particular, we completed the classification of the saturation functions of
4\times 4
matrices, along with many others.

In Theorem
3.6
, we characterized the strongly indecomposable matrices with bounded saturation functions. However, there still exist matrices that are not strongly indecomposable for which it is possible to construct a witness using the horizontal and vertical witnesses in a similar fashion to the proof of Theorem
3.6
, such as for
Q_{4}
in Proposition
9.2
. The problem of categorizing such non-strongly indecomposable matrices remains open.

Additionally, while we have proven by construction in Example
9.7
that there exist matrices with linear saturation function despite also having vertical and horizontal witnesses, the manner by which it was determined that these matrices do indeed have linear saturation functions was by leveraging the fact that they have linear semisaturation functions. This prompts the natural question of whether or not there exists a matrix that has a constant semisaturation function and vertical and horizontal witnesses, but linear saturation function. In other words, can the condition of strong indecomposability and the conditions on the witnesses in Theorem
3.6
be weakened to imply that the semisaturation function is bounded? This question also remains open.

While Theorem
6.1
gives a
3
-dimensional pattern with
\operatorname{sat}(m_{1},m_{2},n;P)=O(1)
, there are still many questions for multidimensional saturation unanswered. There are no known examples of
3
-dimensional patterns with
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)=O(1)
or
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)=\Theta(n)
. It is not even known if, for every multidimensional pattern
P
, there exists an integer
k
such that
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)=\Theta(n^{k})
.

The argument in Theorem
7.11
seems generalizable to most
2\times n
matrices. Indeed, it suggests a general strategy of studying patterns of arbitrary size, by analyzing possible witness graphs for them to efficiently search for witnesses.

In Section
8
, we presented a linear program for computing the saturation functions of arbitrary 0-1 matrices. We used the linear program to evaluate the saturation functions of some forbidden 0-1 matrices for small values of
m
and
n
. Based on the results, we made a conjecture on the exact value of the saturation function for some triangular matrices. It would be interesting to compute the exact value of the saturation function for other forbidden 0-1 matrices, using linear programs or other computational methods. Similar methods could also be applied to the problem of computing the exact value of the saturation function of forbidden sequences
[
1
]
.

The witness construction in Proposition
9.10
involves starting with a simple pattern and witness, and then inserting additional rows to the pattern and correspondingly multiplying the rows of the witness. This closely resembles the approach used in several of our results around adding empty columns and rows, for example Proposition
4.5
. It seems likely that a generalization of this construction may be more widely applicable to other patterns, with other constraints on the rows or columns added. In particular, a construction of this kind might be useful in addressing at least parts of Conjectures
5.4
and
4.8
, noting that Proposition
4.5
already covers a special case of the former and is closely related to the latter.

Lastly, we mention the smallest patterns with still unknown asymptotic saturation functions. Several
3\times 4
patterns have unclassified
\operatorname{sat}(m_{0},n,P)
, and several
5\times 4
patterns have unclassified
\operatorname{sat}(n,P)
.
