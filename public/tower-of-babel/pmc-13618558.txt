A Guide for the Inclusion of Sex and Gender Considerations in Clinical Practice Guidelines
Authors: Tania King, Jennifer Ervin, Marya Yenita Sitohang, Kim Dalziel, Michelle Tew, Cara Tannenbaum, Humaira Maheen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDespite increasing understanding of sex and gender as important determinants of health, there has been a lack of consideration regarding sex, gender, and other intersectional factors in clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) to date, and little support for guideline developers to address these shortcomings. The development of a guide for guideline developers to facilitate the systematic uptake of sex and gender considerations may enhance the uptake and consistency of sex and gender considerations in CPGs. Informed by a comprehensive programme of work that included an umbrella review of the global literature, extensive expert consultation and input from individuals with living experience, the resultant guide provides practical step‐by‐step strategies for CPG developers to systematically integrate sex and gender considerations throughout the guideline development process. Aligning with key global guideline development steps, this guide addresses an important clinical need to translate sex and gender‐specific evidence into tailored clinical action, where relevant, and provides recommended pathways to improve guideline quality and equity. The integration of sex and gender into CPGs is a global imperative, and whilst acknowledging that guidelines are developed and disseminated differently across different countries and contexts, this guide has international relevance.

Background

This guide supports the systematic integration of sex and gender considerations throughout guideline development. Recommendations align with key global guideline development steps while flexibly accounting for local context and setting.

Sex and gender intersect with other important determinants of health and health equity, including age, sexual orientation, ethnicity, disability, socioeconomic position, and geographic location [1]. This guide does not replace comprehensive equity guidance related to these other determinants; guideline developers should use this guide in conjunction with other equity resources for comprehensive consideration of health equity [2, 3].

Sex and gender matter for clinical practice guidelines because biomedical research has historically relied on male cells, male animal models and male humans as the default, with females systematically under‐represented in preclinical research through to clinical trials and data analysis [4, 5, 6]. Significant gaps persist, and sex and gender considerations remain inadequately incorporated into CPGs internationally [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Incorporating sex and gender considerations enhances guideline quality, appropriateness and translation. Guidelines that do not account for evidence on sex and gender differences may provide recommendations that are less effective for some populations, potentially contributing to misdiagnosis, adverse treatment outcomes and the perpetuation of physical and mental health inequities [12, 13]. Integrating sex‐ and gender‐based analyses and considerations throughout guideline processes is essential to ensuring equitable, evidence‐informed, and inclusive healthcare. The evidence gap on sex‐related outcomes has reduced in recent years; however, gender considerations in clinical outcomes remain less common. While both sex and gender warrant consideration in CPGs, gender considerations may be limited by data gaps, and in many cases, there will be insufficient data, particularly for diverse gender‐populations. For many conditions, guideline developers may therefore find that prioritising sex‐based considerations represents a practical and evidence‐supported first step. More comprehensive attention to gender considerations should be incorporated as evidence, methodological approaches, and resources develop.

Evidence on sex and gender differences in health is constantly evolving, concomitant with growing societal understanding and awareness of these constructs. This underscores the ongoing need for regular re‐evaluation and updating to ensure that recommendations remain reflective of current knowledge and best practice.

Methods

Recommendations were developed through a multi‐component methodology including expert and lived experience consultation, participatory research, and two literature reviews, with full methodological details provided in the Supplementary Materials. In‐depth qualitative interviews were conducted with 31 participants drawn from diverse backgrounds, disciplines, and sex and gender perspectives, including individuals with lived experience of gender and sexual diversity and sex‐specific health conditions. Two international literature reviews were conducted: an umbrella review [20] synthesising existing research on the inclusion of sex and gender in clinical practice guidelines, and a targeted analysis of best‐practice guidelines identified through that review. Guide development drew on findings from these reviews and interview findings alongside established frameworks for health equity in guideline development, including the GIN‐McMaster Guideline Development health equity considerations [21], the Cochrane Equity Methods Group (PROGRESS‐Plus) [22, 23], the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) equity guidelines [24], and the Sex and Gender Equity in Research (SAGER) guidelines [12, 25]. Australian guidelines were also used, including the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC)'s Guideline Standards [26], Guidelines for Guidelines: Equity [3], and Statement on Gender, Variations of Sex Characteristics and Sexual Orientation in Health and Medical Research [18]. Feedback on the draft guide was obtained through a staged consultation process involving health research funders, clinicians, guideline developers, and lived experience consultants. Ethics approval was obtained from the University of Melbourne Human Research Ethics Committee (#2025‐33040‐68447‐3).

Structuring the recommendations against steps in this way is intended to enable developers to systematically integrate sex and gender considerations throughout the guideline development process. The prompts and reflective questions throughout the guide are intended to support consideration and discussion and are not prescriptive. Sex and gender consideration should be integrated across the development process alongside other core methodological considerations (similar to certainty of evidence and equity assessment). Guideline developers should apply those most relevant to their specific condition, target population, and context. To enable adaptation to different countries and contexts, particularly those where resources are limited or where political pressures restrict the translation of gender‐related scientific evidence to practice, the recommendations have been flexibly labelled as core (C) (minimum requirements for all guidelines); high‐impact (HI) (priority where resources permit); and context‐specific (CS) (recommended where relevant to the condition, population, or guideline scope). Supplementary resource materials accompanying this guide include case studies, practical ‘how‐to’ examples for each stage of guideline development, as well as a sex and gender considerations checklist aligned to each step.

Given that sex and gender are distinct constructs, this distinction is reflected throughout the recommendations that follow. Where a recommendation refers specifically to ‘gender’, ‘sex’ or ‘sex and gender’ together, this is intentional. Where a recommendation addresses both sex and gender, this is stated explicitly. Guideline developers are encouraged to read each recommendation in light of these distinctions and, where the primary focus is on one construct, to consider whether the other may also warrant attention in their specific context.

Step 1. Establishing Guideline Development Group and Advisory Panels

Core recommendation: Ensure diverse and inclusive gender representation in the guideline development group

The composition of guideline committees is crucial for developing inclusive CPGs [30]. Meaningful engagement with women and gender‐diverse populations, and their representation on guideline panels brings essential perspectives that shape clinical questions, scope definition, outcome prioritisation, interpretation of evidence and recommendations. Without this diversity, panels may inadvertently frame guidelines around default assumptions that do not reflect the experiences or needs of all patient populations, embedding bias from the earliest stages of development, and ultimately compromising care quality and patient outcomes [18, 26, 31].

Guideline development processes should: Aim for women to hold at least 40% of positions; where current representation is lower, document barriers and develop a plan to increase representation. (C)Include individuals with disciplinary expertise in sex and gender (e.g., a sex and gender champion [32]) on guideline development and advisory panels. (C)Where relevant to health condition or population that is the focus of the guideline, engage people with lived experience of sexual and gender diversity [7, 8] so that guideline development is informed by diverse perspectives. For fields where representation of women is low, guideline development groups could ensure representation through inclusion as lived experience experts. (CS)

Depending on the state of the science in the field under review, additional searches may be warranted. For example, using terms such as ‘male’ and ‘female,’ ‘women’ and ‘men’ or searches targeting single‐sex studies or gender‐related issues. A sex and gender expert within the guideline development group may help in determining when and how complementary searches should be developed. Validated search strategies, such as those developed by Song et al., [36] are suggested as a starting point to improve identification of sex‐specific diagnostic and treatment evidence and better inform recommendations across different clinical questions [34].

Evidence Synthesis and Subgroup Analysis

Where data permit, and where there is evidence or biological plausibility for sex or gender differences: Conduct or extract sex‐ or gender‐disaggregated analyses for clinical presentation, diagnostic accuracy, treatment efficacy, adverse effects, toxicity, and safety outcomes. (C)When data permit, and biological plausibility exists for conducting sex or gender subgroup analyses, consider the following: ∘Assess whether sex and gender‐specific subgroup analyses were pre‐specified in the original studies. Although post‐hoc analyses may be under‐powered to detect statistical differences, they may still illustrate important trends requiring further investigation.2 (C)∘Recognise that exploratory post‐hoc subgroup analyses can have an increased risk of spurious findings due to multiple comparisons. If analyses are not pre‐specified, frame them as exploratory and treat them as hypothesis‐generating (as opposed to hypothesis‐confirming). (C)∘Evaluate whether sample sizes are adequate. Small samples may produce imprecise estimates with wide confidence intervals; however, reporting them may prompt further study or future meta‐analyses. (HI)∘Consider the influence of publication bias: examine whether subgroup analyses were planned but not reported, as null or non‐significant findings by sex or gender are less likely to be published. Step 5. Structuring the Guideline

Core recommendation: Include a glossary of sex and gender terms and use terms consistently

While attention to sex and gender in CPGs is improving, sex and gender terms remain inadequately used in many guidelines, and when terms are included, they may be used inconsistently or incorrectly [7, 39]. Omitted or incorrect terminology reduces the accuracy, clarity and applicability of CPG recommendations, potentially leading to confusion in clinical implementation [26]. The inclusion of a glossary of key sex and gender terms relevant to your guideline ensures clarity and minimises confusion.

Guidelines should: Define key terms explicitly: include definitions of sex and gender, and additional terms where appropriate and relevant to the guideline scope. (C) Ensure accurate and consistent application of terms: avoid using “sex” and “gender” terms interchangeably. When evidence or clinical considerations relate to both biological and social factors, specify both (e.g., sex and gender considerations in pain management). (C) Specify the framework being applied: where possible utilise a gender‐spectrum based framework [18]. If applying a binary framework, clarify why binary categories are being used and acknowledge this limitation, as well as the populations for whom the recommendations may not apply. If addressing transgender and gender diversity, clarify the terminology used and ensure consistency. (C) Consider organ‐based or function‐based criteria: in some clinical contexts, it may also be relevant to make or qualify recommendations based on the presence or absence of specific organs, anatomy or physiological functions (e.g., “people with a cervix,” and “people with a prostate”) to ensure that the health needs of transgender individuals are met. (CS)Note that this is an evolving and contested area of practice with differing perspectives. Terminology choices in this area involve trade‐offs and careful consideration: language intended to be inclusive of some groups may feel reductive or diminishing to others. There is no universally accepted approach. Guideline developers are strongly encouraged to consult relevant patient and public groups—including women, transgender, and non‐binary individuals—to identify preferred terminology for their specific context. When using organ‐based or function‐based language, explain the rationale clearly and ensure the approach enhances, rather than obscures clarity for both clinicians and patients. Balance precision and inclusivity with practical applicability in clinical settings. (CS) Acknowledge complexity and limitations: recognise that sex and gender are distinct but inter‐related concepts that both influence health. Step 6. Undertaking Public Consultation

Core recommendation: Seek feedback, drawing on a range of sex and gender perspectives

Research evidence may not fully capture the perspectives and experiences of different sex and gender populations relevant to the guide. Meaningful engagement with diverse populations is one strategy to advance equity, ensuring that guidelines reflect the values, preferences and lived experiences of all people that the guidelines are intended to serve. When undertaking public or patient consultation, ensure that diverse sex and gender perspectives are actively sought and represented. This assists in the detection of unintended consequences or disparities, improves the acceptability and feasibility of recommendations, enhances clarity and communication, and assists in addressing implementation barriers [41]. Consultation on sex and gender considerations should be embedded within broader public consultation and engagement processes that are already standard practice in guideline development, rather than as a separate activity.

Final Comments

The seven steps outlined here provide a framework for integrating sex and gender considerations throughout CPG development. While not every step will be equally relevant to every guideline, systematic attention to sex and gender helps ensure that recommendations are appropriate, effective, and equitable across diverse populations.
