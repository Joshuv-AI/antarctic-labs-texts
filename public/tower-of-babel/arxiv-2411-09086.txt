Manas Bhatnagar and Robin Young

Abstract
. We present a modified Front Tracking (mFT) scheme for hyperbolic systems of conservation laws in one space dimension, in which we allow arbitrarily large nonlinear waves. We build the scheme by introducing and solving a “generalized Riemann Problem”, which yields exact solutions for finite times. This allows us to treat the states adjacent to all waves exactly, and approximate compressive simple waves in addition to rarefactions, contacts and shocks. In particular, we require exact expression of the various wave curves and avoid the use of Taylor expansions. After construction of the scheme, under reasonable assumptions, we show that the mFT approximations converge to a weak* solution of the system. This essentially reduces existence of solutions with large amplitude data to obtaining uniform bounds on the total variation of the approximations. We then apply the scheme to the Euler equations of gas dynamics, for which we exactly solve the generalized Riemann Problem and define the scheme for both 3x3 and 2x2 systems, and prove the equivalence of Eulerian and Lagrangian frames. For the p p -system, modeling isentropic gas dynamics in a Lagrangian frame, we show that there is no finite accumulation of interaction times. This means that the last remaining obstacle to global existence of large data, large amplitude solutions is the construction of a decreasing Glimm potential.



1.
Introduction

We consider
N\times N
systems of hyperbolic conservation
laws in one space variable, namely

(1.1)

q(u)_{t}+f(u)_{x}=0,\qquad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,T),

up to fixed time
T>0
(which may be infinite), with Cauchy data

(1.2)

u(\cdot,0)=u^{o}(\cdot)\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}).

Here

u=u(x,t)\in\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the
state
, and
q

and
f:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
are
C^{2}
functions giving the (vectors
of)
conserved quantities
, and corresponding
fluxes
,
respectively. As is well known, analysis of hyperbolic systems is
complicated by the formation of nonlinear
shock waves
, which
are discontinuities in the function
u(\cdot,t)
, and their subsequent
propagation
[
15
,
30
,
31
]
.

There is now a well established and largely complete theory of small

BV
solutions to (
1.1
) going back to Glimm’s foundational
existence theorem, including existence, uniqueness and well-posedness
results
[
22
,
45
,
52
,
7
,
8
,
6
,
10
]
. For some systems,
including the protoypical case of gas dynamics, the theory extends to
solutions having large total variation but small
oscillation
[
23
,
4
,
41
,
42
,
46
,
47
]
, while recent results
include the existence of periodic solutions
[
48
]
.

Many modern approaches are based on Front Tracking (FT)
approximations, which generate piecewise constant approximations and
in turn provide a simplified but effective setting for the nonlinear
analysis
[
17
,
2
,
24
,
7
,
16
]
. However, current FT
theory relies on a small
BV
assumption and is unsatisfactory for
physically realistic systems, because shock waves are inherently very
strong nonlinear phenomena. Ideally, mathematical models should allow
for large amplitude waves and solutions, which are ruled out by the
essential limitation
\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{\infty}<\epsilon
made in most FT
approaches to (
1.1
).

Our goal in this paper is to define a modified FT (mFT) scheme for
general systems (
1.1
) which is appropriate for waves and
solutions of arbitrarily large finite amplitude and locally finite
variation. We also show that, under an
a priori
assumption of
a finite
BV
bound, together with some mild technical and checkable
assumptions on the structure of the system, the mFT approximations
converge to a weak* solution of (
1.1
). In the forthcoming
paper
[
5
]
, we will apply this mFT scheme to prove the global
existence of large amplitude, large
BV
weak* solutions to the

p
-system of isentropic gas dynamics by obtaining the assumed global

BV
bound of the approximate solutions.

In developing and implementing the mFT scheme, we strictly apply the
following guiding principles:

•

For all nonlinear waves, adjacent states defining
all
waves
must be treated
exactly
;

•

To correctly resolve interactions of strong waves,

compressions
must be treated
explicitly
;

•

For finite times, the
number of waves
in the
approximation must remain
finite
;

•

For general systems, the scheme should be as
flexible
as
possible.

In particular, adherence to these principles allows us to avoid the
use of non-physical waves, which are necessary for most legacy
implementations of FT for systems with
N\geq 3
equations.

We assume that system (
1.1
) is
strictly hyperbolic
, so
that the generalized eigenvalue problem generated by the Jacobian
matrices of
q
and
f
, namely

Df(u)\,r_{i}(u)=\lambda_{i}(u)\,Dq(u)\,r_{i}(u),

has a full set of real eigenvalues

\lambda_{1}(u)<\lambda_{2}(u)<\ldots<\lambda_{N}(u),\qquad u\in\mathcal{U}.

This implies that the corresponding eigenvectors,

r_{1}(u),\ldots,r_{N}(u)
, are linearly independent for each

u\in\mathcal{U}
. In addition, we assume that each field is either

genuinely nonlinear (GNL)
or
linearly degenerate
,

r_{k}(u)\cdot\nabla\lambda_{k}(u)>0,\quad\text{or}\quad r_{k}(u)\cdot\nabla\lambda_{k}(u)=0,

for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
, respectively.

Generally, physically relevant systems are also
symmetrizable
,
which means that for some choice of state variable
u\in\mathcal{U}
, both

Dq
and
Df
are symmetric, and the system has a natural convex
entropy
[
21
,
32
,
44
]
. If this holds,
Dq
has a Cholesky
factorization,

Dq(u)=\sqrt{Dq(u)}^{\intercal}\sqrt{Dq(u)},

and the generalized eigenvectors
\{r_{k}\}
satisfying (
3.5
)
form an orthonormal frame relative to
Dq
,

(1.3)

r_{j}^{\intercal}(u)\,Dq(u)\,r_{k}(u)=\delta_{jk}=\big(\sqrt{Dq(u)}\,r_{j}(u)\big)^{\intercal}\big(\sqrt{Dq(u)}\,r_{k}(u)\big),

uniformly for
u\in\mathcal{U}
. Existence of a convex entropy provides a
nonlocal condition for choosing admissible shocks
[
32
,
50
,
21
,
37
,
34
]
, although for this paper we use a natural
extension of Lax’s characteristic condition
[
30
]
. Convex
entropies also form the basis of the method of compensated
compactness, which yields
L^{\infty}
solutions of some
systems
[
20
,
33
,
11
]
. However, these come with little
quantitative information and we prefer the local wave-based approach
which yields the detailed local structure of solutions
[
18
]
.

The
Riemann problem
is the basis of most analyses of solutions
of conservation laws in one space dimension, being both experimentally
realizable in one dimension and respecting the scaling invariance of
(
1.1
). Solutions to the Riemann problem also describe the
asymptotic structure of solutions with compactly supported data for
large times, and so can in some sense be regarded as “universal”.
However, using Riemann solutions solely as a basis for analysis of
interactions is risky because they cannot adequately capture the fine
structure of solutions at “intermediate” times, at which the
variation of the solution may grow, say

\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{BV}>C\,\max\big\{\|u^{o}\|_{BV},\;\|u(\cdot,\infty)\|_{BV}\big\},\qquad t=O(1).

As a way of understanding interactions of large waves and coping with
this intermediate growth, we introduce a
generalized Riemann
problem
(gRP), which admits compressions as well as rarefactions
and shocks, and captures some of the intermediate growth of nonlinear
interacting solutions. The gRP data is realizable
a
posteriori
, and we do not consider the solution globally, but only
for some finite time until secondary interaction effects are
manifested. This allows us to treat states exactly and incorporate
compressions, at least for short times. More importantly, use of gRP
solutions naturally introduces the
time
that nonlinear
interactions take to complete, effectively “slowing down” multiple
interactions and allowing us to bound the number of waves in our
approximate solutions.

Because we are treating general systems (
1.1
), we

assume
that the gRP is globally and uniquely solvable, although
this can be readily checked for well known systems such isentropic
and full gas dynamics and the nonlinear elastic string
[
45
,
56
]
.
We also
assume
that
BV
bounds are known; of course obtaining
such
BV
bounds is well known to be the main obstacle in proofs of
existence and regularity of solutions. Under some other technical
assumptions on the structure of the system, we then prove that the mFT
scheme we are defining converges to a weak* solution of system
(
1.1
).

Use of weak* solutions, introduced in
[
39
,
40
]
, provides an
effective method for defining a
residual
for approximate
solutions, and which are easily calculated for piecewise constant
approximations. The residual is defined as a measure and can be
manipulated using the corresponding calculus. Also, the residual can
be defined for any bounded function
u(x,t)
, and use of the norm on
the space of measures allows us to describe how far any bounded
function is from being a solution.

We build the piecewise constant mFT scheme by discretizing the gRP,
subject to the following constraints. First, we insist that the
states adjacent to any wave must be
exact
, so there is no
choice in assigning states and we never solve approximate Riemann
problems. However, to distinguish between shocks and simple waves, we
assign to each wave a non-negative
“virtual width”
. This width
chooses whether or not a shock or simple wave should be used in
solving the gRP; a wave is a shock if and only if it is both
compressive and has zero width, and it is simple whenever its width is
positive. Linearly degenerate waves are always given zero width.

Because states are assumed exact and gRPs are solved exactly, we have
no flexibility in assigning wave strengths or adjacent states, but we
can control the scheme by appropriately choosing the speed of waves
and their virtual widths. We are working with general systems, so we
introduce several modifications of widths and speeds in order to keep
the residual small and the number of waves finite.

Strong rarefactions cannot be sufficiently well approximated by a
small number jump discontinuities, and so we introduce a method of
splitting strong rarefactions into several smaller waves, called

“multi-rarefactions”
, which keeps the residual under control.
However, we must also respect upper and lower bounds for the strengths
of split rarefactions, in order to avoid too many waves. A more
immediate problem for general systems of size
N\geq 3
is to control
the number of waves: there are several known examples in which waves
can accumulate so that infinitely many waves are generated in finite
time. We control this problem by introducing
“composite
waves”
, in which weak waves, known as passengers, can
“piggyback” on stronger waves, so that the total number of waves in
the mFT approximation remains controlled
unless
some sort of
blowup occurs.

Definition of the scheme leads to a sequence of “interaction times”

0=t_{0}<t_{1}<t_{2}<\dots<t_{i}<\dots,

and between each pair of interaction times, waves are represented by
jump discontinuities with fixed wavespeed. We represent these by a

“wave sequence”

\Gamma^{t}=\big\{\gamma_{j}\;:\;j=1,\dots,n^{t}\big\},\qquad t\in(t_{i},t_{i+1}),

where each wave
\gamma_{j}
carries its own information, such as strength

\zeta_{j}
, family, adjacent states, speed, width, etc.
Using this notation, the mFT scheme can be regarded as a natural
extension of the “Method of Reorderings”, developed by the second
author in
[
52
,
46
]
.

We present a method of initializing the scheme, which generates the
wave sequence
\Gamma^{0+}
from
BV
initial data (
1.2
), as well
as a reconstruction method, which extrapolates a given wave sequence

\Gamma^{t}
to generate a
BV
approximation of the solution at time

t
. This reconstructed solution is not piecewise constant, but
provides profiles to the simple waves, consistent with the solution of
the gRP.

Our first theorem shows that the scheme can be defined inductively for
all interaction times
t_{i}
, and the corresponding approximation
remains in
BV
. Moreover the piecewise constant approximation is in
the correct class for the residual to be well-defined.

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem
6.1
)
.

For each
\epsilon>0
and
i\in\mathbb{N}
, the wave sequence

\Gamma^{t_{i}+}
has bounded variation, that is

\mathcal{V}\big(\Gamma^{t_{i}+}\big):=\sum|\zeta_{j}|<\infty.

Moreover, for each
i
, the corresponding piecewise constant
approximation

U^{\epsilon}\in W^{1,\infty}_{*}\big(0,t_{i+1};BV_{loc},M_{loc}\big),

lives in the correct function space
\mathcal{Z}
given by (
2.7
),
so that

f(U^{\epsilon})\in L^{\infty}_{*}(0,t_{i+1};BV_{loc})\qquad\text{and}\qquad q(U^{\epsilon})\in W^{1,\infty}_{*}(0,t_{i+1};BV_{loc},M_{loc}).

While we state the theorem for
BV
solutions, our results also
hold in the wider class of
H^{-b}
solutions,
b>1/2
. We expect
that this wider class will extend to more space dimensions, for which
the class
BV
is unstable to evolution by (
1.1
)
[
43
]
.

Although the scheme can be defined for all interaction times
t_{i}
,
these interaction times may accumulate at some finite time
t_{\infty}
,
possibly due to some form of blowup or simply because the number of
waves becomes infinite due to self-similarity. If this happens, we
say we have an
accumulation of interaction times
. We introduce
a checkable condition, which we call the “
\alpha
-ray condition”, which
is weaker than the requirement that the approximation
U^{\epsilon}(x,t)
be

BV
along rays, and which effectively characterizes the conditions
under which the scheme cannot be continued.

Theorem 1.2

(Theorem
6.3
)
.

For fixed
\epsilon>0
, suppose that the mFT approximation
U^{\epsilon}

satisfies the
\alpha
-ray condition for all
(x,t)
, with

t\leq t_{\infty}
. Then there is no accumulation of interaction times
and the scheme can be continued for all times, that is

t_{\infty}=\infty
.

Finally, we turn to convergence of the scheme. For this we

assume
uniform
BV
bounds on the approximation for time

t\leq T
. We also introduce a technical condition that the “weight
of heavy passengers”
W_{H}
be small: this can be interpreted as an
assumption that the different wave families effectively separate so
that comparably sized waves of different families do not continuously
interact with each other. Under these conditions, we show that the
mFT scheme for general systems converges to a weak* solution as

\epsilon\to 0^{+}
.

Theorem 1.3

(Theorem
7.3
)
.

Suppose the mFT scheme is defined for
t\leq T
with the uniform
variation bound

\mathcal{V}(\Gamma^{t})<V,\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq t\leq T.

Suppose also that
W_{H}(\Gamma,\epsilon)=o(1)
as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
, uniformly
for
t\in[0,T]
. Then some subsequence of the corresponding
approximations
U^{\epsilon}
converge as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
, and the limit is a
weak* solution of the system (
1.1
).

Since our scheme is defined for large amplitude waves, these theorems
show that in order to prove a large amplitude
BV
existence theorem
for a general nonlinear hyperbolic system, it suffices to obtain
uniform
BV
bounds. In particular, the authors expect that checking
the
\alpha
-ray and heavy passenger conditions will be essential steps
in deriving
BV
bounds for such systems.

The most important and well-known system of conservation laws is the
inviscid Euler equations of compressible gas dynamics. As an
application, we give a detailed description of the mFT for that
system. Because the system consists of three families and the middle
(contact) family is linearly degenerate, we do not expect self-similar
patterns to develop, so we choose
not to use
composite waves in
the scheme. We derive exact expressions for the wave curves and for
the residual of each wave. We then show that the mFT schemes and
their convergence properties are consistent between the spatial Euler
frame and the material Lagrangian frame, so it is sufficient to
consider only the Lagrangian frame, in which the wavespeeds are
simplified. Finally, we restrict to the
p
-system of isentropic gas
dynamics, and we show that if a (nonlocal) Glimm potential
\mathcal{P}
for
the total variation can be found, then the scheme is defined for all
times.

Theorem 1.4

(Theorem
9.6
)
.

Fix
\epsilon>0
and for some
T>0
, suppose that a potential
\mathcal{P}
can
be found for the
p
-system, such that

\Delta\,\mathcal{P}\leq 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Delta(\mathcal{V}+\mathcal{P})\leq 0,

for interactions occuring up to time
t<T
. Then the scheme can be
defined beyond time
T
; that is, there is some
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
the
k
-th interaction time satisfies
t_{k}>T
.

This theorem rules out wave patterns in which the variation becomes
unbounded, such as those in examples of
[
3
]
and
[
9
]
.
Together with our earlier theorems, the theorem implies that if such a
potential can be found for large but finite amplitudes and variations,
then existence of
BV
weak* solutions is implied for times

t\in[0,T]
. In the forthcoming paper
[
5
]
, the authors
construct such a Glimm potential for a large class of initial data,
provided the vacuum is avoided for
t<T
. We remark that this
potential is nonlocal, so is not inconsistent with
[
14
]
.
As a corollary, assuming the existence of the potential, we obtain
existence of solutions and convergence of the scheme to a weak*
solution
with a rate

\epsilon^{2e_{s}}
as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
, uniformly for

t\leq T
.

The paper is laid out as follows: in Section
2
, we recall
the notion of weak* solution to (
1.1
) and describe the
residual of piecewise constant approximations. In
Section
3
we recall the Riemann problem and define and
solve the generalized Riemann problem (gRP). Then in
Section
4
we define the modified Front Tracking (mFT)
approximations. In Section
5
we describe the
initialization of the scheme and reconstruction of solutions using the
gRP. In Section
6
, we show that the scheme can be
defined indefinitely, and in Section
7
we prove
convergence of a subsequence of the mFT approximations to a weak*
solution, assuming
a priori

BV
bounds. Finally in
Section
8
we specialize to the case of the Euler equations
of compressible gas dynamics, and in Section
9
we treat
the isentropic case, and show that the example of
[
9
]
cannot
be realized.

2.
Weak* Solutions

We begin by recalling the notion of weak* solution, introduced in

[
39
,
40
]
. This is a modification (practically slight, but
philosophically significant) of the well-known notion of weak
solution
[
30
,
45
,
16
]
. Recall that
u=u(x,t)
is a weak
solution to the Cauchy problem (
1.1
), (
1.2
) if it is a
bounded distributional solution; that is,

u(x,t)\in L^{\infty}\big(\mathbb{R}\times[0,T)\big)
is a weak solution
if

(2.1)

\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}q\big(u({\cancel{x}},{\cancel{t}})\big)\,\psi_{t}({\cancel{x}},{\cancel{t}})+f\big(u({\cancel{x}},{\cancel{t}})\big)\,\psi_{x}({\cancel{x}},{\cancel{t}})\;d{\cancel{x}}\;d{\cancel{t}}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{o}({\cancel{x}})\,\psi({\cancel{x}},0)\;d{\cancel{x}}=0,

for all compactly supported smooth test functions

\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R})
.

In contrast, weak* solutions are defined based on the observation that
the action (
2.1
) of
u
on test functions is
linear
, so
that solutions should be correctly interpreted as living in the dual
of the Banach space of test functions. With this interpretation, the
PDE system (
1.1
) can be re-interpreted as an ODE in the Banach
space
X^{*}
, which is the dual of the space of test functions
X
,
namely

(2.2)

q(u)^{\prime}+\mathbb{D}f(u)=0,\qquad u(0)=u^{o},

in which
\mathbb{D}
is the spatial distributional derivative, and

{}^{\prime}=\frac{d}{dt}
is the Gelfand-weak derivative of a Banach space
valued function
[0,T)\to X^{*}
. This ODE can be integrated directly
to get the equivalent condition

(2.3)

q\big(u(\cdot,t)\big)-q\big(u^{o}\big)+\star\!\!\!\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{D}f\big(u(\cdot,{\cancel{t}})\big)\;d{\cancel{t}}=0,

in
X^{*}
, for all
t\in[0,T)
. Here
\star\!\!\!\int
is the
Gelfand
integral
, defined by the condition

\Big\langle\star\!\!\!\int_{E}g({\cancel{t}})\;d{\cancel{t}},\varphi\Big\rangle=\int_{E}\big\langle g({\cancel{t}}),\varphi\big\rangle\;d{\cancel{t}}\quad\text{for all}\quad\varphi\in X,

where
E\subset[0,T)
is Borel. Note that the integral on the right
is just a Lebesgue integral, and this definition immediately yields an
interchange of limits (or Fubini theorem). Thus (
2.3
) can be
written as

(2.4)

\left\langle q\big(u(\cdot,t)\big),\varphi\right\rangle-\left\langle q\big(u^{o}\big),\varphi\right\rangle-\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle f\big(u(\cdot,{\cancel{t}})\big),\frac{d\varphi}{dx}\right\rangle\;d{\cancel{t}}=0,

for all
t\in[0,T)
and test functions
\varphi\in X
, where we have
integrated by parts inside the integral. For this definition to make
sense, note that
q(u)
must take values in
X^{*}
and
f(u)
takes
values in
{X^{\dagger}}^{*}
, where
X^{\dagger}
are derivatives of functions in

X
.

In
[
39
]
, it is shown that when restricting to locally bounded

BV_{loc}
solutions, weak and weak* solutions are equivalent. Due to
their flexibility in choice of test functions, and the ability to use
calculus in the space of measures, in this paper we consider only
weak* solutions.

More precisely, we define
BV
weak* solutions of (
1.1
) as
follows. Note that for fixed compact set

K=K_{\mathcal{U}}\subset\mathcal{U}
,
BV(K)
is the dual of a space, which we
write as
BV(K)=\big(C^{-1}(K)\big)^{*}
, although care must be taken
in defining the separable space
C^{-1}(K)
; see
[
1
]
.
This notation is consistent with the fact that derivatives of
BV

functions are Radon measures, which are in turn the dual of continuous
functions. Since the compact set
K
is arbitrary, and all our
approximations are constant outside a compact set, we drop explicit
mention of
K
and write

BV_{loc}=(C^{-1}_{0})^{*}\quad\text{and}\quad M_{loc}=(C^{0}_{0})^{*}.

Here the functions and corresponding measures are implicitly taken to
have values in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with the Euclidean norm; this is no
restriction because the target space is finite dimensional.
Throughout the paper, we use the convention that
BV
functions are
the average of their one-sided limits,

(2.5)

u(x)=\frac{u(x+)+u(x-)}{2},\quad\text{for all}\quad x.

Recalling that
f
and
q
are
C^{2}
, we require that
u\in BV_{loc}
,
so that both
q\circ u
and
f\circ u
are also in

BV_{loc}\subset M_{loc}
. Next, since
\mathbb{D}f\in M_{loc}
, we
check (
2.3
) in
M_{loc}
, so we apply continuous test
functions. As in
[
39
,
40
]
, we let

(2.6)

\begin{gathered}L^{p}_{*}(0,T;BV_{loc})=L^{p}_{*}\big(0,T;(C^{-1}_{0})^{*}\big)\quad\text{and}\quad\\
W^{1,p}_{*}(0,T;BV_{loc},M_{loc})=W^{1,p}_{*}\big(0,T;(C^{-1}_{0})^{*},(C^{0}_{0})^{*}\big)\end{gathered}

denote the Gelfand
p
-integrable and absolutely continuous functions,
respectively. Here, functions in
W^{1,p}_{*}(0,T;{X^{\dagger}}^{*},X^{*})
take
their values in
{X^{\dagger}}^{*}(=BV_{loc})
but are Gelfand differentiable in
the larger space
X^{*}(=M_{loc})
. Note that
C^{0}_{0}
is separable as
required, and it suffices to take
p=\infty
, because we are assuming
global
BV
bounds.

Definition 2.1
.

The function

(2.7)

u\in\mathcal{Z}:=f^{-1}\big(L^{\infty}_{*}(0,T;BV_{loc})\big)\cap q^{-1}\big(W^{1,\infty}_{*}(0,T;BV_{loc},M_{loc})\big)

is a BV-weak* solution to (
1.1
) if (
2.2
) holds in

M_{loc}
for a.e.
t\in[0,T)
, or equivalently, if (
2.3
)
holds in
M_{loc}
for every
t\in[0,T)
, that is (
2.4
)
holds for all continuous test functions
\varphi
.

Here
u
can be taken to be a norm-measurable representative, and
since the integral is absolutely continuous, (
2.3
) holds at
all times; see
[
39
,
40
]
for details. Note that
\mathcal{Z}
is

not
a Banach space, but as the intersection of preimages of
Banach spaces, is a complete metric space with a naturally endowed
metric.

We can similarly define weak* solutions in Sobolev spaces
H^{-b}
,

b\in\mathbb{R}
. In this case we require that (
2.3
) holds in

H^{-b}=(H^{b})^{*}
, or equivalently that (
2.4
) holds for all

\varphi\in H^{b}
. Making sense of this requires that

f\circ u\in H^{1-b}=(H^{b-1})^{*}
is Gelfand integrable, and that

q\circ u
lives in
H^{1-b}
and is absolutely continuous in

H^{-b}
. Thus a Gelfand
p
-integrable
H^{-b}
weak* solution is a
function

(2.8)

u\in\mathcal{Z}^{-b,p}:=f^{-1}\big(L^{p}_{*}(0,T;H^{1-b})\big)\cap q^{-1}\big(W^{1,p}_{*}(0,T;H^{1-b},H^{-b})\big)

for which (
2.3
) holds in
H^{-b}
.

2.1.
Residual of Front Tracking Approximations

A
BV
weak* solution satisfies (
2.2
) or (
2.3
) as an
equality in
M_{loc}
. Although this can be checked by considering
(
2.4
) for any test function
\varphi\in C^{0}_{0}
, it is easier
to simply check (
2.2
) in the space
M_{loc}
of measures.
That is, because
f\circ u
is also
BV_{loc}
, the weak derivative

\mathbb{D}f(u)
can be directly regarded as a measure. Indeed, for any
function
u\in\mathcal{Z}
, where
\mathcal{Z}
is the metric space given by
(
2.7
), the right hand side of (
2.2
) makes sense, and
so we can define a residual on
\mathcal{Z}
.

Definition 2.2
.

For any
u\in\mathcal{Z}
, the
residual
of
u
is defined to be the
time-dependent measure

(2.9)

\mathcal{R}(u)(t):=q\big(u(\cdot,t)\big)^{\prime}+\mathbb{D}f\big(u(\cdot,t)\big)\in M_{loc}.

Similarly, if
u\in\mathcal{Z}^{-b,p}
, we define the residual

\mathcal{R}(u)\in L^{p}_{*}(0,T;H^{-b})
in the same way.

This ability to define the residual is a powerful distinction between
weak and weak* solutions: indeed, the residual can in principle be
calculated for any candidate
u\in\mathcal{Z}
, and it is clear that
u
is
a weak* solution if and only if the residual
\mathcal{R}(u)
vanishes for
almost every
t\in[0,T)
. Moreover, in this context we can use the
calculus of measures to simplify and understand the residual because
we have a well understood topology to deal with. In particular, in
this paper we are developing an approximation scheme for which the
residuals are bounded by the discretization parameter, and so it is
unsurprising that some subsequence of these will converge to a weak*
solution.

Front Tracking approximations are piecewise constant functions that
are built to approximate solutions of (
1.1
), and the class of
piecewise constant functions is particularly well suited to the
calculation of residuals as measures. In particular, in a FT
approximation, each discontinuity, which represents a wave or part
thereof, propagates with a given, fixed speed for almost all times.

Thus suppose we are given some
U\in\mathcal{Z}
which is piecewise constant
and absolutely continuous for
t
in some open interval
(t_{a},t_{b})
.
That is, say
U(\cdot,t)
has
m
jumps, each located at

\xi_{i}(t)
. For
t\in(t_{a},t_{b})
, we can represent
U
as

U(x,t)=\begin{cases}U_{0}(t),&x<\xi_{1}(t),\\
U_{i}(t),&\xi_{i}(t)<x<\xi_{i+1}(t),\\
U_{m}(t),&\xi_{m}(t)<x,\end{cases}

and each of
U_{i}
and
\xi_{i}
are differentiable on
(t_{a},t_{b})
.
For each
i
, we define the jump

[U]_{i}(t):=U\big(\xi_{i}(t)+,t\big)-U\big(\xi_{i}(t)-,t\big)=U_{i}(t)-U_{i-1}(t).

If we let
H
denote the Heaviside function, using the convention that
H(0)=1/2
, we can rewrite

(2.10)

U(\cdot,t)=U_{0}(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}[U]_{i}(t)\,H\big(x-\xi_{i}(t)\big),\qquad t\in(t_{a},t_{b}).

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose that
U\in\mathcal{Z}
is piecewise constant and has the form
(
2.10
), with each state
U_{i}(t)=U_{i}
constant for

t\in(t_{a},t_{b})
. Then for such
t
, the residual has the form

(2.11)

\mathcal{R}(U)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\big([f]_{i}-\xi_{i}^{\prime}(t)\,[q]_{i}\big)\,\delta_{\xi_{i}}\in M_{loc},

where
[q]_{i}
and
[f]_{i}
are the jumps in
q(u)
and
f(u)
at

\xi_{i}(t)
, respectively,

(2.12)

[q]_{i}(t):=q(U_{i})-q(U_{i-1}),\quad\text{and}\quad[f]_{i}(t):=f(U_{i})-f(U_{i-1}).

Proof.

Since
U(\cdot,t)
is
BV
, we can simply plug this in to
(
2.9
) and evaluate it as a measure. It follows immediately
that

\displaystyle q\big(U(\cdot,t)\big)

\displaystyle=q\big(U_{0}(t)\big)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}[q]_{i}(t)\,H\big(x-\xi_{i}(t)\big),

\displaystyle f\big(U(\cdot,t)\big)

\displaystyle=f\big(U_{0}(t)\big)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}[f]_{i}(t)\,H\big(x-\xi_{i}(t)\big),

where the jumps in
q
and
f
are given by (
2.12
).
Plugging these into (
2.9
) immediately yields

(2.13)

\mathcal{R}(U)(t)=B(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\big([f]_{i}-\xi_{i}^{\prime}\,[q]_{i}\big)\,\delta_{\xi_{i}}\in M_{loc},

where
\delta_{\xi}
is the Dirac mass, and
B(t)
is the bounded
function

B(t):=q\big(U_{0}(t)\big)^{\prime}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}[q]_{i}^{\prime}(t)\,H\big(x-\xi_{i}(t)\big).

If each state
U_{i}
is constant for
t\in(t_{a},t_{b})
, it follows that

B(t)
vanishes identically there.
∎

Note that when we work in the space
\mathcal{Z}^{-b,p}
, the Lemma follows
as above, the only difference being that we should choose
b
large
enough that the Dirac mass
\delta\in H^{-b}
, namely
b>1/2
, and in
that case we can again take
p=\infty
.

3.
The Riemann Problem and Extensions

We briefly recall the solution of the Riemann problem
[
30
,
45
]
.
As is well known, this is the Cauchy problem in which the Cauchy data
consists of two constant states, namely

u^{o}(x)=\begin{cases}u_{L},&x<0,\\
u_{R},&x>0.\end{cases}

Note that this data respects the scale invariance of system
(
1.1
). In one space dimension, the solution of the Riemann
problem has two major applications: it represents the asymptotic limit
of general solutions with fixed states
u(\pm\infty,t)
, and it serves
as the main building block of more general solutions, including FT
approximations.

For strictly hyperbolic systems, the solution of the Riemann problem
is given by
N+1
constant states separated by
elementary
centered waves
. For genuinely nonlinear (GNL) fields, these
elementary waves are either
shocks
or centered

rarefactions
, while for linearly degenerate fields, they are

contact discontinuities
. For fields that are not either
genuinely nonlinear or linearly degenerate, centered waves are
composites of rarefactions and (degenerate)
contact shocks
, but
for ease of discussion we will not consider such families here.

We begin by describing a single shock or contact, which is a sharp
discontinuity separating two constant states. These take the form
(
2.10
) with a single jump, so have the form

u=\begin{cases}u_{-},&x<\sigma\,t,\\
u_{+},&x>\sigma\,t,\end{cases}

where the discontinuity propagates along the ray
x=\sigma\,t
, which
is forced by self-similarity. If this is to be an exact solution of
the system, the residual must vanish, so by Lemma
2.3
,

(3.1)

[f]=\sigma\,[q],\quad\text{or}\quad f(u_{+})-f(u_{-})=\sigma\,\big(q(u_{+})-q(u_{-})\big),

which is the
Rankine-Hugoniot jump condition
. Note that this
is
N
equations in
2N+1
variables, so for one state, say
u_{-}

fixed, it gives a one-parameter family of solutions
(u_{+},\sigma)
,
which describes a curve in state space.

This can be clarified by writing the Hugoniot condition as a
generalized eigenvalue problem, as follows. We set

[u]:=u_{+}-u_{-},\quad\overline{u}=\frac{u_{-}+u_{+}}{2},\quad\text{and}\quad u(\eta):=\overline{u}+\eta\,[u],

and integrate along the segment joining
u_{-}
and
u_{+}
, namely

\big\{u(\eta)\;:\;\eta\in(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})\big\}
, to get

\displaystyle f(u_{+})-f(u_{-})

\displaystyle=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}Df\big(u({\cancel{\eta}})\big)\;d{\cancel{\eta}}\;[u]=:\langle Df\rangle\,[u],

\displaystyle q(u_{+})-q(u_{-})

\displaystyle=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}Dq\big(u({\cancel{\eta}})\big)\;d{\cancel{\eta}}\;[u]=:\langle Dq\rangle\,[u],

so that
\langle Df\rangle
and
\langle Dq\rangle
are averaged Jacobian matrices. The
Hugoniot condition is thus simply a generalized eigenvalue problem,
namely

\langle Df\rangle\,[u]=\sigma\,\langle Dq\rangle\,[u],

in which the matrices
\langle Df\rangle
and
\langle Dq\rangle
are nonlinear functions
of
\overline{u}
and
[u]
. In general we expect
N
different shock or
contact families, each of which will yield a one-parameter family of
solutions for each reference state, which could be either of
u_{-}
,

u_{+}
or
\overline{u}
. We will refer to these as shock or contact curves
according to whether the family is genuinely nonlinear or linearly
degenerate.

For genuinely nonlinear (GNL) families, the
Lax entropy condition
is
used to rule out inadmissible discontinuities: this states that for a

k
-shock, the left and right
k
-characteristics must impinge on the
shock, namely

(3.2)

\lambda_{k}(u_{-})>\sigma(u_{-},u_{+})>\lambda_{k}(u_{+}),\quad\text{while also}\quad\lambda_{k-1}(u_{-})<\sigma(u_{-},u_{+})<\lambda_{k+1}(u_{+}),

and this in turn rules out one half of the Hugoniot locus. We will
denote the admissible half of the
k
-shock curve by

\mathcal{S}_{k}(u_{-}):=\big\{u_{+}\;;\;[f]=\sigma_{k}\,[q],\;\lambda_{k}(u_{-})>\sigma_{k}>\lambda_{k}(u_{+})\big\},

and we will write

(3.3)

u_{+}\in\mathcal{S}_{k}(u_{-})\quad\text{and}\quad u_{-}\in\mathcal{S}_{k}^{\intercal}(u_{+}),

if left state
u_{-}
can be joined to right state
u_{+}
by an
admissible
k
-shock.

For strong shocks, we assume the following
extension

of Lax’s entropy condition. Given any
u_{m}\in\mathcal{U}
and

k<k^{\prime}
, we require that

(3.4)

\sigma_{k}(u_{\ell},u_{m})<\sigma_{k^{\prime}}(u_{m},u_{r}),

for any
u_{\ell}\in\mathcal{S}_{k}^{\intercal}(u_{m})
, and
u_{r}\in\mathcal{S}_{k^{\prime}}(u_{m})
.
Unlike Lax’s condition, this compares shock speeds across

different
shock curves. To the authors’ knowledge, this
extension comparing shock speeds of different families has not been
previously formulated, and should extend consistently to more general
entropy conditions such as those given by
[
50
,
37
,
34
]
.

We next describe
simple waves
, which are
C^{1}
solutions
of (
1.1
), and which include the centered rarefaction waves. A
simple wave is a solution
u(x,t)
of (
1.1
) which has a
one-dimensional image in state space, and can thus be written

u(x,t)=U\big(\zeta(x,t)\big),\quad\text{where}\quad\zeta:\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}\times[0,T)\to\mathbb{R},\quad\text{and}\quad U:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{U},

see
[
28
,
45
,
51
]
. Plugging this in to (
1.1
) or
(
2.2
), and using classical differentiability, we immediately
get

Dq(U)\,\frac{dU}{d\zeta}\,\zeta_{t}+Df(U)\,\frac{dU}{d\zeta}\,\zeta_{x}=0.

This PDE system can immediately be broken down into three simpler
problems, namely a generalized eigenvalue problem,

(3.5)

Df(U)\,r_{k}(U)=\lambda_{k}(U)\,Dq(U)\,r_{k}(U),

an autonomous ODE,

(3.6)

\frac{dU}{d\zeta}=r_{k}(U),

and a scalar transport equation,

\zeta_{t}+\lambda_{k}\big(U(\zeta)\big)\,\zeta_{x}=0.

These are solved in the order in which they appear: namely, first, the
eigenvalue problem is solved for all
U\in\mathcal{U}
; next, the
(appropriately normalized) eigenvectors are integrated to get
N

simple wave curves
, parameterized by
\zeta
; and finally, the
transport equation is solved as a scalar hyperbolic PDE. Assuming
that the wave curves
U(\zeta)
have been described, we solve by
characteristics: that is, set

\frac{dx}{dt}=\lambda_{k}\big(U(\zeta)\big),\quad\text{so that}\quad\frac{d\zeta}{dt}=0,

so that
\zeta
is constant and the characteristics are straight
lines,

(3.7)

x=x_{0}+\lambda_{k}\big(U(\zeta)\big)\,(t-t_{0}).

This solution is determined by Cauchy data

\left\{\big(x_{0}(\zeta),t_{0}(\zeta)\big),U(\zeta)\right\}
, and extends
to the largest neighborhood of the plane for which different
characteristics do not focus or intersect. For any fixed value of

t
, the
profile
of the simple wave is the shape of the wave,
which we can write as
\big(x(\zeta),U(\zeta)\big)
, with
x=x(\zeta)

given by (
3.7
).

The simple wave is
centered
if all characteristics
(
3.7
) focus at a single
center

(x_{c},t_{c})
, and in
this case the simple wave consists of one half of the characteristic
cone through the center,

x-x_{c}=\lambda_{k}\big(U(\zeta)\big)\,(t-t_{c}).

Here
U(\zeta)
parameterizes the wave curve connecting states
u_{-}

and
u_{+}
, and the centered simple wave is a rarefaction if

\lambda_{k}(u_{-})<\lambda_{k}(u_{+})
, in which case the upper cone
t>t_{c}

is used, while if
\lambda_{k}(u_{-})>\lambda_{k}(u_{+})
the wave is a

centered compression
and the lower cone
t<t_{c}
is used.

We now describe the
simple wave curves
in more detail. The
generalized eigenvalue problem yields
N
eigenvector fields
r_{k}(U)

defined throughout
\mathcal{U}
, and each such (smoothly varying) field is
integrated as the autonomous ODE (
3.6
). For each
k
, this
generates a flow on
\mathcal{U}
, and each orbit of this flow is a simple
wave curve. We denote the
k
-wave curve through
u_{0}
by

(3.8)

\mathcal{W}_{k}(u_{0})=\Big\{u(\zeta):u(\zeta)=W_{k}(u_{0},\zeta):=u_{0}+\int_{0}^{\zeta}r_{k}(u({\cancel{\zeta}}))\;d{\cancel{\zeta}}\Big\},

Because the ODE is autonomous, the functions
W_{k}
have the local
group property

(3.9)

W_{k}(u_{0},z_{1}+z_{2})=W_{k}\big(W_{k}(u_{0},z_{1}),z_{2}\big),

as long as these expressions make sense, and in particular, the wave
curves are disjoint,

\mathcal{W}_{k}(u_{1})=\mathcal{W}_{k}(u_{0})\quad\text{whenever}\quad u_{1}\in\mathcal{W}_{k}(u_{0}).

If the
k
-th field is genuinely nonlinear (GNL), for each fixed left state

u_{-}
, the wave curve
\mathcal{W}_{k}(u_{-})
consists of two distinct pieces,
namely the
rarefaction curve
of right states
u_{+}
that can be
reached by a
k
-rarefaction, for which

\lambda_{k}(u_{-})<\lambda_{k}(u_{+})
, and the
compression curve
of
those
u_{+}
that can be connected by a
k
-compression, and for which

\lambda_{k}(u_{+})<\lambda_{k}(u_{-})
. By convention,

r_{k}\cdot\nabla\lambda_{k}>0
, so that the rarefaction curve corresponds
to positive parameter
\zeta>0
, while compressions correspond to

\zeta<0
. This in turn is consistent with the description of the

k
-shock curve above.

We put these curves together to describe the
elementary centered
wave curves
: the
k
-th such curve is the union of the
k
-shock
curve and the
k
-rarefaction curve,

(3.10)

\mathcal{E}_{k}(u_{-}):=\mathcal{S}_{k}(u_{-})\cup\big\{W_{k}(u_{-},\zeta)\;;\;\zeta\geq 0\big\},

and this consists of all right states
u_{+}
that can be reached from
left state
u_{-}
by an elementary centered
k
-wave. Recall that for
a linearly degenerate family, the Hugoniot locus
\mathcal{S}_{k}
and simple
wave curves
\mathcal{W}_{k}
coincide, and the corresponding wavespeed

\lambda_{k}
is constant on that curve
[
30
,
45
]
.

We now describe the solution of the Riemann problem, which is well
known
[
30
,
45
,
16
]
. Setting
u_{0}:=u_{L}
, we inductively take

(3.11)

u_{k}:=E_{k}(u_{k-1},\zeta_{k})\in\mathcal{E}_{k}(u_{k-1}),

where
E_{k}(u_{-},\zeta)
is a consistent parameterization of

\mathcal{E}_{k}(u_{-})
. This yields a map
u_{N}
, given by

u_{N}(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\dots,\zeta_{N})=E_{N}\Big(E_{N-1}\big(\dots,E_{1}(u_{0},\zeta_{1}),\dots,\zeta_{N-1}\big),\zeta_{N}\Big)\in\mathcal{U},

defined on some maximal set
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
containing
u_{L}
, and on
which the map is invertible. We now solve the equation

u_{N}(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\dots,\zeta_{N})=u_{R},

to get the strengths of the individual waves, and generate the Riemann
solution by connecting the intermediate states by the corresponding
centered elementary waves.

Existence and uniqueness of small amplitude solutions of the Riemann
problem was shown by Lax for general systems
[
30
]
, and global
solutions have been found for physically interesting systems

[
45
,
56
]
, but because we are working with a general system,
existence (and uniqueness) of solutions of the Riemann problem is an

assumption
. We will specify our main assumption more precisely
after generalizing the Riemann problem below.

3.1.
Generalized Riemann Problem (gRP)

In our development of the modified Front Tracking scheme, we wish to
account for the widths of simple waves and understand the consequences
thereof for general interactions. In order to consistently take this
into account, we will incorporate the explicit use of compressions
into the scheme. We begin with the observation that providing a given
width to a GNL simple wave gives a well-defined way of centering the
wave.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume the
k
-th family is genuinely nonlinear, and suppose we are
given states
u_{-}
and
u_{+}\in\mathcal{W}_{k}(u_{-})
, or equivalently
u_{+}

and
u_{-}\in\mathcal{W}_{k}(u_{+})
. Suppose also we are given a positive
initial width
w
. Then there is a unique profile
u^{o}=u^{o}(x)
,
with

u^{o}(x)=u_{-},\ x<0,\qquad u^{o}(x)=u_{+},\ x>w,

and a unique center
(x_{c},t_{c})
, such that the Cauchy problem

u(x,0)=u^{o}(x)
has a solution initially consisting of a simple
centered
k
-wave. If
t_{c}<0
, this simple wave is a rarefaction
and is defined for all
t\in(t_{c},\infty)
, while if
t_{c}>0
, the
simple wave is a compression for
t\in(-\infty,t_{c})
, and can be
continued as a translated Riemann solution for all times
t\geq t_{c}
.
Moreover, the
k
-th wavespeed
\lambda_{k}(u^{o})
is a monotonic
function of
x
, increasing for a rarefaction and decreasing for a
compression.

The position
(x_{c},t_{c})
of the center scales linearly with width

w
, and the profile of the data scales similarly: that is, if

u^{o}(x;w)
denotes the profile determined by
w>0
, then for any

\alpha>0
,

u^{o}(x;\alpha\,w)=u^{o}(x/\alpha;w).

Proof.

As functions of
t
, the left and right edges of the wave are

x_{-}(t)=\lambda_{k}(u_{-})\,t\quad\text{and}\quad x_{+}(t)=w+\lambda_{k}(u_{+})\,t,

respectively. These intersect at the point

t_{c}:=\frac{w}{\lambda_{k}(u_{-})-\lambda_{k}(u_{+})},\qquad x_{c}:=\lambda_{k}(u_{-})\,t_{c}=w+\lambda_{k}(u_{+})\,t_{c},

which is the center of the simple wave. Linear dependence of the
center on width
w
is evident. Genuine nonlinearity implies that

t_{c}
is finite and nonzero. Since
w>0
, we have
t_{c}<0
if and
only if
\lambda_{k}(u_{+})>\lambda_{k}(u_{-})
, corresponding to a
rarefaction, and
t_{c}<0
if and only if

\lambda_{k}(u_{+})<\lambda_{k}(u_{-})
, corresponding to a compression.

For clarity suppose that the wave is a compression, and parameterize

\mathcal{W}_{k}(u_{-})
by
\zeta\in[\zeta_{+},0]
, where

u_{+}=W_{k}(u_{-},\zeta_{+})
with
\zeta_{+}<0
as in (
3.8
). We choose
the profile of
u^{o}
so that all characteristics in the simple wave
focus at
(x_{c},t_{c})
, so we choose
x^{o}(\zeta)
by setting

x_{c}-x^{o}(\zeta)=\lambda_{k}\big(u(\zeta)\big)\,t_{c},\quad\text{and}\quad u^{o}\big(x^{o}(\zeta)\big):=u(\zeta),

for
\zeta\in[\zeta_{+},0]
. Since

\frac{d}{d\zeta}\lambda_{k}=\nabla\lambda_{k}\cdot\frac{du}{d\zeta}=r_{k}\cdot\nabla\lambda_{k}>0,

uniqueness and monotonicity of the profile follow by the implicit
function theorem.

By construction, the characteristics intersect only at
(x_{c},t_{c})
, so
the compression is defined for all
t<t_{c}
. Moreover, the solution
at time
t_{c}
is exactly
u_{-}
for
x<x_{c}
,
u_{+}
for
x>x_{c}
, which
is Riemann data translated to the focus
(x_{c},t_{c})
, and so
extends to a Riemann solution for all
t\geq t_{c}
. The case of a
rarefaction is similar, but we do not attempt to extend the solution
backwards beyond
t_{c}<0
.
∎

In the case of a compression, we refer to the time
t_{c}
in the above
lemma as the
time of collapse
of the compression; at that time
the compression will generally form a shock in the
k
-th family, with
associated waves in other families being reflected as a consequence of
nonlinearity.

Linearity in the width
w
is a consequence of scale invariance of the
PDE: this is most evident if we use translation invariance to shift
the center of the simple wave to the origin. For a rarefaction, this
is the ‘usual’ centered rarefaction, while for a compression the
profile is chosen so that the wave focuses exactly at the origin and
the solution continued as a Riemann solution.

Recall that in our solution to the Riemann problem, we used three
types of waves, namely shocks and contacts, which have no width, and
rarefactions centered at the origin, which also have no width at

t=0
. We then pieced these waves together in increasing order of
wavespeed to get the Riemann solution for
t\geq 0
.

Following Lemma
3.1
, we can enlarge the class of waves we
use by adding centered simple waves of a given initial width. We can
again piece these together in increasing order of wavespeed, to get a
solution for some positive time
t_{\#}
, which is
beyond
the
earliest time of collapse of any compressions. This time
t_{\#}
is
the first time of interaction of any reflected wave from a collapsed
compression with any other wave in the solution.

Definition 3.2
.

The
generalized Riemann problem
(gRP) is the (implicitly
defined) initial value problem determined by data consisting of two
states
u_{L}
and
u_{R}
together with
N
nonnegative initial
widths
\{w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{N}\}
, such that
w_{k}=0
for every linearly
degenerate family
k
. This problem is solved by piecing together,
in increasing order of wavespeed,
either
elementary centered
waves given by (
3.10
), (
3.11
) in case
w_{k}=0
,
or

centered simple waves of initial width
w_{k}>0
. The profile of
centered waves is determined by Lemma
3.1
, and the
waves are adjacent and non-overlapping at time
t=0
, so that the
data satisfies
u^{o}(x)=u_{L}
for
x<0
,
u^{o}(x)=u_{R}
for

x>\sum w_{k}
.

More precisely, to solve the gRP, we again set
u_{0}:=u_{L}
, and
referring to (
3.8
) and (
3.10
), inductively set

(3.12)

u_{k}:=\begin{cases}E_{k}(u_{k-1},\zeta_{k})\in\mathcal{E}_{k}(u_{k-1}),&w_{k}=0,\\
W_{k}(u_{k-1},\zeta_{k})\in\mathcal{W}_{k}(u_{k-1}),&w_{k}>0,\end{cases}

and again solve
u_{N}=u_{R}
. Once we have found the intermediate
states
\{u_{k}\}
, we resolve any simple waves with
w_{k}>0
into a
centered wave with initial profile given by Lemma
3.1
;
any shocks, contacts or origin centered rarefactions have zero width.

beyond the first collapse

). This gRP solution is shown in the left panel of

Because we are working with an abstract system, we do not prove

Assumption 3.3
.

The generalized Riemann problem is globally uniquely solvable in

\mathcal{U}

u_{R}\in\mathcal{U}
and initial

Note that the branch chosen in (

or not

Our construction of solutions to the gRP follows Lax’s solution of the

are inductively defined.
We provide elementary conditions which are sufficient for

.

For a strictly hyperbolic system, assume Lax’s extended entropy

For generic systems, we expect the conditions of this lemma to be

Proof.

Given gRP data

3.2.
Discretized Riemann Solution (dgRS)

We cannot apply the gRP directly to generate an FT approximation,
because the simple waves have continuous profiles. On the other hand,
the gRP solution is an exact solution, so is not piecewise constant,
and so we discretize to get a piecewise constant approximation
thereof. We will refer to this as the discretized (generalized)
Riemann solution (dgRS).

The data for the dgRS is the same as that of the gRP, namely left and
right states
u_{L}
and
u_{R}
, together with widths
\{w_{k}\}

satisfying
w_{k}\geq 0
and
w_{k}=0
for linearly degenerate
k
. Here we
regard the widths
w_{k}
as “virtual widths” of the waves, which will
in turn be represented by piecewise constant approximations. That is,
in the discretization, we do not give waves actual width, but treat
them as jumps, while using these virtual widths as a bookkeeping
device to track whether a discretized wave is simple or not.

Figure 1.
Generalized (left) and discretized (right) Riemann
solutions

Given gRP data, we again define intermediate states
u_{k}
as in
(
3.12
), and solve the equation
u_{N}=u_{R}
exactly. However,
instead of then giving each simple
k
-wave a profile with initial
width
w_{k}
, we approximate the wave by (one or more) jumps having no
actual width but with initial virtual width
w_{k}
. We then place each
of these jumps at the origin and propagate them as jumps with
wavespeed
s_{k}
which approximates the actual wavespeed

\lambda_{k}\big(u(\zeta)\big)
or
\sigma(u_{k-1},u_{k})
, according as
whether the wave is simple or a shock. This discretized solution thus
resembles an actual Riemann solution, with all waves emerging from the
origin, but allows for compressions and discretizes centered
rarefactions. By introducing and using virtual widths, we have
essentially introduced “centered compressions” into the approximate
solution. As in solutions of the gRP, the dgRS is defined only for
short times, although again it can be continued beyond the earliest
collapse time of compressions, to say
t_{\#}>t_{c}
. However, for our
purposes it will be sufficient to continue only up to
t_{c}
and to
treat the collapse as an explicit interaction. The dgRS is
illustrated in the right panel of Figure
1
: the shaded
areas represent the shifted individual simple waves, while the dark
lines are are the actual discontinuities in the dgRS.

The dgRS is not an actual solution of the system, so does not
have vanishing residual. However, because the solution is piecewise
constant, we can calculate the residual as in Lemma
2.3
, to
give

\mathcal{R}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big([f]_{j}-s_{j}\,[q]_{j}\big)\,\delta_{s_{j}\,t},

valid for
0\leq t\leq t_{c}
, where

[f]_{j}:=f(u_{j})-f(u_{j-1})\quad\text{and}\quad[q]_{j}:=q(u_{j})-q(u_{j-1}).

In fact, since a weak* solution corresponds to vanishing residual, but
our construction develops approximations to the solution, we can
modify the residual slightly by applying a
preconditioner
,
provided that this is uniformly bounded and invertible, as follows.
For each jump
[u]_{j}
, we pick some
N\times N
preconditioning matrix

A_{j}
, and define the
preconditioned residual

(3.16)

\mathcal{R}^{P}:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{j}\,\big([f]_{j}-s_{j}\,[q]_{j}\big)\,\delta_{s_{j}\,t}.

Since
A_{j}
is uniformly bounded and invertible, it is clear that
there is some constant
K
such that

\frac{1}{K}\,\|\mathcal{R}\|_{M}\leq\|\mathcal{R}^{P}\|_{M}\leq K\,\|\mathcal{R}\|_{M},

so convergence of the approximation to a solution is equivalent
for either the unconditioned or preconditioned residuals.

For moderate simple waves, the dgRS may have a large residual, because
a moderate simple wave cannot be well approximated by a single jump.
We thus choose to further discretize
rarefactions
in the dgRS,
using the group property (
3.9
) of simple waves. There is no way
to further discretize compressions, as all waves in the dgRS emanate
from the origin. In particular, in asking that the residual be small,
so that our approximations are close to solutions,
requires

that all compressions in the approximation be sufficiently weak; if
compressions become too strong, they are immediately replaced by
shocks.

We describe how to further discretize a moderate
k
-rarefaction, for
which

u_{k}=W_{k}(u_{k-1},\zeta_{k}),

and
\zeta_{k}>0
is relatively large. If we want a maximum rarefaction
strength of
\eta
, say, we choose

M_{k}=M(\zeta_{k})\geq\lceil\zeta_{k}/\eta\rceil,\qquad M_{k}\in\mathbb{N},

and choose
M_{k}
strengths
\zeta_{k}^{(m)}
satisfying

(3.17)

\sum_{i=1}^{M_{k}}\zeta_{k}^{(i)}=\zeta_{k},\qquad 0<\zeta_{k}^{(i)}\leq\eta,\ i=1,\dots,M_{k}.

Then we set

u_{k}^{(0)}:=u_{k-1},\qquad u_{k}^{(i)}:=W_{k}\big(u_{k}^{(i-1)},\zeta_{k}^{(i)}\big),

for
i=1,\dots,M_{k}
, so that
\big\{\lambda_{k}(u_{k}^{(i)})\big\}
is
monotonically increasing, and
u_{k}^{(M_{k})}=u_{k}
, by the group property
(
3.9
). We now choose an approximate speed

s_{k}^{(i)}\in\big(\lambda_{k}(u_{k}^{(i-1)}),\lambda_{k}(u_{k}^{(i)})\big)
, and
approximate the part of the centered rarefaction between
u_{k}^{(i-1)}

and
u_{k}^{(i)}
by a jump with speed
s_{k}^{(i)}
. We summarize the
foregoing in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

Given any gRP data
\big\{u_{L},u_{R},w_{1},\dots,w_{N}\big\}

which has the form

u_{k}^{(i)},&s_{k}^{(i)}\,t<x<s_{k}^{(i+1)},\\[3.0pt]

u_{1}^{(0)}=u_{0}=u_{L},\quad u_{k-1}^{(M_{k-1})}=u_{k}^{(0)}=u_{k-1},\quad u_{N}^{(M_{N})}=u_{N}=u_{R},

and in which

unless

\zeta_{k}^{(i)}

The preconditioned residual of the dgRS approximation is

where we have set

[f]_{k}^{(i)}:=f(u_{k}^{(i)})-f(u_{k}^{(i-1)})\quad\text{and}\quad[q]_{k}^{(i)}:=q(u_{k}^{(i)})-q(u_{k}^{(i-1)}),

and where

is the preconditioner of the wave

.

be any uniformly bounded matrix with bounded inverse. It turns out
that there is a natural preconditioner in the most important case that
the system (

) is symmetric, which corresponds to most
physical systems for which energy is conserved. Henceforth we assume
that the preconditioner
A
is implicitly given and drop the
superscript
P

Proof.

Existence and uniqueness of intermediate states
u_{k}
and positivity
of
t_{\#}
is a result of Assumption
3.3
. If the
k
-wave
is a shock, contact, compression or weak rarefaction, we have

M_{k}=1
, and if it is a moderate rarefaction,
\zeta_{k}>\eta
, then we
discretize the corresponding wave as above, with smaller wave

.
∎

4.
Modified Front Tracking (mFT)

Having developed the dgRS as a piecewise constant approximation of the
gRP solution, we now combine these to build piecewise constant
approximations of more general solutions. As in other FT
applications, we glue different dgRSs together with common adjacent
states. Effectively, we pose a series of non-overlapping gRPs,
resolve these to get a solution until two nonlinear waves interact,
and then try to repose the gRPs to resolve the interactions. Because
the system is highly nonlinear and wave interactions cannot be easily
resolved, we instead use dgRSs, which keep us in the class of
piecewise constant solutions. It is then easy to decide when waves
interact, and because we remain in the piecewise constant class, we
can resolve these interactions using more dgRSs.

There are several questions that need to be resolved to carry this
out: first, we must choose wavespeeds for the approximate waves;
second, we must decide exactly which dgRS to pose in order to resolve
interactions; and third, we must endeavour to keep the number of waves
finite and avoid accumulation of interaction times, so that the scheme
can be continued for long times. In particular, the last requirement,
that of keeping the number of waves finite and avoiding accumulation
of interaction times, requires some technical modifications of the
scheme. Note that we must also consistently include dependence on the
discretization parameters.

4.1.
Bookkeeping

At any given time, the modified FT scheme has a finite number of
waves, each of which is a single jump discontinuity propagating at a
given speed and with several other data of interest. Following the
method of reorderings, developed in
[
52
]
, we define a

wave sequence
which names each wave and each associated piece
of data. We find this to be an efficient bookkeeping tool for
tracking the position, speed, strength and other data associated to
each wave.

For a given time
t
, we thus define the wave sequence
\Gamma
to be
a finite sequence of waves,

\Gamma^{t}=\big\{\gamma^{t}_{j}\;;\;j=1,\dots,n^{t}\big\},

in which
n^{t}
is the number of waves in the sequence. The time
t

is usually implicitly understood, and when this is the case we will
drop the superscript
t
and write

\Gamma=\big\{\gamma_{j}\;:\;j=1,\dots,n\big\}
. Each wave in the
sequence carries its own data, and we can write it as an ordered
tuple, such as

(4.1)

\gamma_{j}=\big(\zeta_{j},k_{j},u_{j-1},u_{j},s_{j},w_{j}(t),x^{c}_{j},t^{c}_{j},t^{!}_{j},\accentset{\mbox{\Large.}}{w}_{j},A_{j}\big);

these components are, respectively: wave strength
\zeta_{j}
, family

k_{j}\in 1,\dots,N
, left state
u_{j-1}
, right state
u_{j}
, wavespeed

s_{j}
, virtual width
w_{j}(t)\geq 0
, virtual center or origin

(x^{c}_{j},t^{c}_{j})
, interaction time
t^{!}_{j}
, rate of expansion

\accentset{\mbox{\Large.}}{w}_{j}
, and preconditioner
A_{j}
. Several of these data are
related, for example

\accentset{\mbox{\Large.}}{w}_{j}=\lambda_{k_{j}}(u_{j})-\lambda_{k_{j}}(u_{j-1}),\quad\text{or}\quad u_{j}=W_{k_{j}}(u_{j-1},\zeta_{j}),

for a simple wave or contact. We can also store other information as
convenient or necessary, such as time of origin of the wave. We can
similarly calculate other quantities, such as the
position

x^{p}_{j}(t)
of the wave
\gamma_{j}
at time
t
, namely

x^{p}_{j}(t)=x^{c}_{j}+s_{j}\,(t-t^{c}_{j}).

We note that the origin and center of a simple wave are generally
distinct, because the wave is generated by a nonlinear
interaction. For a rarefaction, the center occurs earlier than the
origin, while for a compression, the center is in the future.
However, both points lie on the wave trajectory
x^{p}(t)
, which
strictly speaking applies only from the origin to the next interaction
time of the simple wave. By a slight abuse of notation, we record
only the center, and implicitly take
t
in the appropriate interval.
For consistency, if the wave is a shock or contact, we let
(x^{c},t^{c})

denote the origin of the wave.

In this wave sequence notation, the (preconditioned)
residual

of the wave sequence is defined as above, and we get an expression
similar to (
2.11
) and (
3.16
), namely

(4.2)

\mathcal{R}(\Gamma)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{j}\,\big([f]_{j}-s_{j}\,[q]_{j})\,\delta_{x^{p}_{j}(t)},

with
[f]_{j}=f(u_{j})-f(u_{j-1})
and
[q]_{j}=q(u_{j})-q(u_{j-1})
.
Another functional of great interest is the
variation
of the
wave sequence, defined by

(4.3)

\mathcal{V}(\Gamma):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\zeta_{j}|,

and which is equivalent to the
BV
norm, as well as other norms in
which the Heaviside function is bounded, such as
H^{b}
for
b<1/2
.

Note that at an interaction, the number of waves and the corresponding
indices of noninteracting waves will generally change, so care must be
taken in associating waves between different interaction times. In the
implementation of the scheme, the wave sequence and corresponding

state sequence

u:=\big\{u_{j}\;:\;j=0,\dots n\big\},

are stored as linked lists, which combines well with changing numbers
of waves. By allowing adjacent waves to occupy the same physical
point
(x,t)
when an interaction occurs, this notation extends to
interaction times as well, so for a discretized system, we have a
well-defined wave sequence for all times
t\in[0,T)
.

Recall that one of our guiding principles is to treat states

exactly
, so that the total variation of approximations is
always accurately given, while other features of waves may be
approximate. Thus in our description (
4.1
), some data,
particularly those data that are defined by states, must be exact,
while others may be approximated. We have no choice in specifying any
of
\zeta_{j}
,
k_{j}
,
u_{j-1}
,
u_{j}
or
\accentset{\mbox{\Large.}}{w}_{j}
, while we may vary
speed
s_{j}
and width
w_{j}
, and choose preconditioner
A_{j}
, as
needed for consistency and to bound the number of waves and/or
residual; on the other hand
(x^{c}_{j},t^{c}_{j})
and
t^{!}_{j}
are calculated
quantities, so are not directly adjustable. We conclude with the
important observation that
we are able to modify the speed

s_{j}
, virtual width
w_{j}
, and preconditioner
A_{j}
of each wave as
needed, but we cannot adjust the other parameters in (
4.1
).

We will use the following notation for the (various) discretization
parameters: first, we let
\epsilon
denote the basic discretization
parameter, so we are interested in the limit
\epsilon\to 0+
. We then
introduce various exponents
e_{\square}
to denote the different
thresholds which we use. For example, we will refer to the maximum
(absolute) compression or rarefaction strengths of an individual wave
as
{\epsilon^{e_{c}}}
and
{\epsilon^{e_{r}}}
, respectively. We will then establish
convergence by taking
\epsilon\to 0
, while keeping the various exponents

e_{\square}
finite.

4.2.
Choice of Wavespeed

A shock or contact is a single jump with wavespeed given by
(
3.1
), and so if this exact speed is chosen, the corresponding
approximate wave contributes nothing to the residual. It is thus
natural to use the exact wavespeed
s=\sigma(u_{-},u_{+})
for these
waves.

For simple waves, we observe that each individual wave contributes a
single term to the residual in (
4.2
), namely

R\,\delta_{x^{(}t)}:=A\,\big([f]-s\,[q]\big)\,\delta_{x^{(}t)},

in which the left and right states are
u_{\mp}
. Since Dirac measures
supported at different points are independent, by the triangle
inequality each wave contributes its Euclidean norm

\big|A\,[f]-s\,A\,[q]\big|
to the residual (since

\|\delta\|=1
). It thus makes sense to try to minimize this norm in
choosing the wavespeed
s
.

We define the
“vector residual”

R:=A\,\big([f]-s\,[q]\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
of each wave, and use least
squares to find
s
that minimizes this residual: that is, we minimize

(4.4)

\big(A\,([f]-s\,[q])\big)^{2}=\widehat{[f]}\cdot\widehat{[f]}-2\,s\,\widehat{[f]}\cdot\widehat{[q]}+s^{2}\,\widehat{[q]}\cdot\widehat{[q]},

where we have set

\widehat{[q]}:=A[q]\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{[f]}:=A[f].

Note that this least square minimization gives
s=\sigma(u_{-},u_{+})
and

R=0
if the wave is a shock or contact, so this is a consistent
choice for all waves.

Lemma 4.1
.

For
u_{+}=W_{k}(u_{-},\pm\zeta)

corresponding vector residual are

(4.5)

respectively, where

\widehat{[q]}:=A[q],\qquad\widehat{[f]}:=A[f],\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Pi_{\widehat{[q]}^{\perp}}:=I-\frac{\widehat{[q]}\,\widehat{[q]}^{\intercal}}{\widehat{[q]}^{\intercal}\widehat{[q]}}

\widehat{[q]}

(4.6)

\displaystyle\big|R(s)\big|

\widehat{[q]}

\widehat{[f]}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

4.3.
Interactions

Different waves generally propagate with different speeds, and so
adjacent waves may meet, at which point a nonlinear interaction
occurs. Because waves are represented by jump discontinuities, they
meet at a single point, and we will continue the solution beyond that
point by placing a dgRS at that point. The data for the dgRS consists
of left and right states, which are exact and given, together with
outgoing virtual wave widths, which implicitly determine the type of
wave and virtual center of a simple wave. If the wave is not simple,
we take the interaction point as the wave origin. Because we are
regarding the virtual width as a bookkeeping device, which
nevertheless affects future wave patterns, we are able to choose the
virtual widths of outgoing waves. We will do so in such a way as to
maximize consistency and avoid inadmissible overlaps of simple waves.

To be precise, consider the waves
\gamma_{j-1}
and
\gamma_{j}
.
Their trajectories are given by

(4.12)

x^{p}_{j-1}(t)=x^{c}_{j-1}+s_{j-1}\,(t-t^{c}_{j-1})\quad\text{and}\quad x^{p}_{j}(t)=x^{c}_{j}+s_{j}\,(t-t^{c}_{j}),

4.4.
Accelerated Collapse of Compressions

We have observed above that if the strength of a single simple wave is
too large, then so to will the residual be too large, and the
approximation insufficiently accurate. We can impose this upon
initialization, but moderate simple waves could appear as a result of
some nonlinear interactions. If the large simple wave is a
rarefaction, we will split it into smaller pieces using the group
property (
3.9
) as described below, but if the wave is a
compression, this is not an option, as all waves emanate from the
point of interaction.

In this case, suppose that some interaction produces a moderate
compression, say in the GNL
k
-th family. That is, having solved the
dgRS with given virtual widths including
w^{\prime}_{k}>0
, the
k
-th
resultant wave
\gamma^{\prime}_{k}
is a compression whose strength is beyond
some threshold, say
\zeta_{k}^{\prime}<-{\epsilon^{e_{c}}}
. This means that this
k
-th
compression is too strong, and hence the residual too large. In this
case, we will
force
the
k
-wave to instead be a shock, by
adjusting the virtual width of the emergent
k
-wave to zero: that is,
we reset
w^{\prime}_{k}=0
and re-evaluate the dgRS with this adjusted width
data.

Use of compressions eventually leads to an increase in the number of
waves, because when the compression collapses, it generates waves in
other families. To avoid excessive increase in the number of waves,
we therefore
also
collapse very weak compressions that arise as
a result of interactions into shocks, immediately. Thus if a
compression satisfies
either

\zeta^{\prime}_{k}<-{\epsilon^{e_{c}}},\qquad\text{or}\qquad-{\epsilon^{e_{w}}}<\zeta^{\prime}_{k}<0,

then we immediately collapse it into a shock.

Because the character of the solution will change, since a compression
has been replaced by a shock, we will need to re-check that no new
emergent compressions are outside the interval
[-{\epsilon^{e_{c}}},-{\epsilon^{e_{w}}}]
.
This in turn means that we could in principle need to repeat this step
(that is solve the dgRS) up to
N-1
times. During this process, once
any emerging virtual width has been set to zero, we
do not

allow it to again become non-zero: this guarantees that eventually all
compressions emerging from the interaction have strength

\zeta\in[-{\epsilon^{e_{c}}},-{\epsilon^{e_{w}}}]
. Note also, however, that this could in
principle break uniqueness of the resolution of this interaction: if
two or more emerging waves (of different families) are moderate
compressions, there may be some choice as to which ones should be forced
to become shocks.

4.5.
Avoidance of Overlaps

We wish to avoid overlap of adjacent waves of the same family
anywhere in the approximation. Such an overlap is nonphysical because
it indicates that characteristics would have overlapped previously, so
a shock should have formed earlier. We enforce this condition
inductively: first upon initialization, and then by restricting
virtual wave widths as necessary when resolving interactions.

We use the same notation as above: that is, we assume waves

\gamma_{j-1}
and
\gamma_{j}
interact at the space-time point

(x^{!},t^{!})
calculated in (
4.13
). If the edgemost emerging waves
are simple, and of the same family of the adjacent waves, then their
combined widths must be chosen small enough at time

that they do
not overlap. Thus we must also consider the adjacent waves

\gamma_{j-2}
and
\gamma_{j+1}
. We will consider only

\gamma_{j+1}

\gamma_{j-2}
being essentially the same.

Thus suppose that
\gamma_{j+1}

interaction, is a
k_{j+1}
-simple wave. The

\displaystyle x^{p}_{j+1}(t)

\displaystyle=x^{c}_{j+1}+s_{k_{j+1}}\,(t-t^{c}_{j+1}),\quad\text{and}\quad

\displaystyle x^{e-}_{j+1}(t)

\displaystyle=x^{c}_{j+1}+\lambda_{k_{j+1}}(u_{j})\,(t-t^{c}_{j+1}),

respectively. We check for overlap if the rightmost wave emerging
from the interaction is also a

-simple wave. The interaction

\gamma_{j+1}

\gamma_{j+1}
is not simple, so is a shock or

This reduction of virtual width of

4.6.
Multi-rarefactions

As with compressions, it is possible that after a nonlinear
interaction, say crossing a strong shock of a different family, a
rarefaction of positive width and large strength emerges. In this
case again the residual could grow, and an adjustment must be made so
that the approximation remains near a solution, that is so that the
residual remains small.

The solution is to split the moderate rarefaction that emerges from the
interaction into smaller rarefactions, as in Lemma
3.5
.
However, we cannot do this exactly as in that lemma, because the
virtual center of the rarefaction to be split,
(x^{c},t^{c})

differs from
the interaction point
(x^{!},t^{!})
, from which each
wave must emerge.

To resolve this issue, we first locate the virtual center
(x^{c},t^{c})

and actual “background” characteristics of the moderate rarefaction
to be split. We then split this centered rarefaction into smaller

“split waves”
as in Lemma

, which are still
centered at
(x^{c},t^{c})
, with virtual center satisfying
t^{c}<t^{!}
. We
calculate the corresponding speeds, widths and positions of these
split waves using the virtual center
(x^{c},t^{c})

could be propagated as is, we would have reduced the wave strengths
and residual without any change to the approximation. However, we
cannot do this directly because all waves must emanate from the
interaction point
(x^{!},t^{!})
, which satisfies
t^{!}>t^{c}
. Instead,
keeping the states exact as usual, we place each of these smaller

(x^{!},t^{!})

minimum, but only until the time that the wavespeeds are corrected.

calculated from
(x^{c},t^{c})
. When the split waves reach the correct
position, they are reassigned the correct wave speeds, and the
multi-rarefaction reverts to several individual virtually centered
rarefactions.

4.7.
Composite Waves

Because we are treating a general
N\times N
system (
1.1
),
the number of waves can in principle become unbounded in finite time.
This is particularly evident for
N\geq 4
, or even
N=3
for general
systems. In particular, there are known examples in which, if all
waves are discretized, infinitely many waves and interactions
accumulate around a single finite point. Three such pictures of wave
patterns are shown in Figure
5
. The left-most picture
can be realized exactly as waves in a linearly degenerate
(geometrically) nonlinear elastic string
[
56
]
, which we expect
does not blow up despite infinitely many interactions. The middle
pattern occurs in the example of the solution of a non-physical system
in which the amplitude blows up in finite time
[
26
]
. The third
pattern, which we expect is non-realizable in a strictly hyperbolic
system, shows a wave pattern in which there are finitely many waves at
any one time, but which nevertheless has a finite accumulation of
interaction points.

Figure 5.

It follows that to continue our mFT approximation for sufficiently
large times, we must find a way to limit the number of waves in the
scheme. However, we also wish to avoid non-physical waves and to keep
states exact throughout the scheme. We can achieve this consistently
because we are allowed to adjust the speeds (with a residual penalty)
and virtual widths (which affects future wave patterns only), while
preserving exact states and wave strengths.

Our assumption that the system is globally strictly hyperbolic has two
important consequences for us: first, there is a natural ordering of
the wave families by increasing (actual) wavespeed; and second, the
vectors spanning waves of different families are always independent.
The upshot of this is that in our scheme, in which waves are
represented by jump discontinuities, we can
superpose
waves of
different families, by giving them the same initial position and
wavespeed.

Specifically, suppose a dgRS has waves
\zeta_{k}
as in
Lemma
3.5
, so each state
u_{k}
is exactly connected to

u_{k-1}
as in (
3.12
). Then we can allow two or more consecutive
waves to be superposed, say by setting
s_{k-i}=s_{k}=s_{k+1}=:s
, and
the approximation still has the piecewise constant form (
2.10
).
In this case, we would have left and right states

u_{\ell}:=u\big(\xi(t)-,t\big)=u_{k-i-1}\quad\text{and}\quad u_{r}:=u\big(\xi(t)+,t)=u_{k+1},\qquad\xi^{\prime}(t)=s,

and although
u_{\ell}
and
u_{r}
are not connected by a single wave, we
do have

(4.26)

u_{r}=\widetilde{W}_{k+1}\big(\widetilde{W}_{k}\big(\dots\widetilde{W}_{k-i}(u_{\ell},\zeta_{k-i})..,\zeta_{k}\big),\zeta_{k+1}\big),

where each

is either

or

, given by (

(
3.10
), respectively. Note that this description is forced on us
by the ordering of waves, as long as we ensure that the speeds
s_{k}

are non-decreasing in

. Also, note that we can combine waves in
this way
only if
they emanate from the same point, that is as a
result of a wave interaction. We call such a superposition of jumps a

composite wave
. We do not give the composite wave a single
strength, but rather treat it as several superposed waves (of
consecutive families) each having their own strengths.

We identify and name the
base wave
or
carrier wave
of a
composite wave to be that wave having largest strength; this need not
itself be a weak wave. Since all other components are small, we
regard the base wave as dominant, and use the virtual width of the
base wave as that of the composite. In this way, the smaller
components “piggyback” onto the carrier; we will call these

passengers
.

It remains to specify precisely when to use composite waves, which
consecutive waves should be included in a composite wave, and what
speed to assign to the composite wave. We must use composite waves
often enough that interactions do not accumulate, but we wish to also
control the total residual. Since we are effectively using an
incorrect wavespeed for several of the different components, according
to (
4.6
), the strength of any such component must be small if we
are to control the residual.

After resolving an interaction by solving the dgRS, and carrying out
the checks above, including splitting into multi-rarefactions, we
check the strength of each emerging wave
\gamma_{j}
. If the wave has
strength
at least

{\epsilon^{e_{p}}}
,
|\zeta_{j}|\geq{\epsilon^{e_{p}}}
, then that wave

may not
be a passenger, so it must be a base or carrier wave.
Thus any passenger is necessarily weaker than
{\epsilon^{e_{p}}}
.

If there are any passengers in the emerging waves, each such passenger
must be associated with a carrier which emerges fron the same point.
There are three possibilities for each passenger
\gamma_{j}
: first, there
are base waves both to the left and right of
\gamma_{j}
; second, there is
a base wave either to the left or right of
\gamma_{j}
emerging from the
interaction; and third, there are
no base waves
emerging from
the interaction. In the first case, we choose the
stronger
of
the two base waves to be the carrier, and in the second case there is
no choice to make. In the third case, there are no “strong” wave
candidates for the carrier; in this case we choose as the carrier the

largest
of the waves emerging from the interaction point. In
cases in which two or more candidates have the same strength, we
merely enforce consistency by not allowing wave to cross, so that

all
waves between a passenger and its carrier are passengers
with the
same
carrier. This is illustrated in

4.8.
Speed of Composite Waves

In all cases, the composite wave inherits the width of the base wave.
It remains to choose the speed of the composite wave: to do so, we
check the relative strengths of the carrier wave
\gamma_{b}
and its
passengers
\gamma_{j}
,
b_{j}=b
. Fix a constant
Q=Q(\epsilon)<1
to be chosen
later. We use this

to distinguish between
“light”
and

“heavy”
passengers according as whether

(4.27)

|\zeta_{j}|\leq Q(\epsilon)\,|\zeta_{b_{j}}|,\quad\text{or}\quad Q(\epsilon)\,|\zeta_{b_{j}}|<|\zeta_{j}|\leq|\zeta_{b_{j}}|,

respectively. Thus a passenger is heavy if its strength is comparable
to that of its carrier, and light otherwise. We call the composite
wave “unbalanced” if all passengers in the wave are light, and
“balanced” if at least one of the passengers is heavy, and we choose
the composite wave speeds accordingly. We write
Q(\epsilon)
rather than

{\epsilon^{e_{q}}}
because we do not require a rate for
Q
: all we’ll require is
that
Q(\epsilon)=o(1)
as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
, so it suffices to take say

Q(\epsilon)=1/|\log\epsilon|
.

If a composite wave is unbalanced, so that all of its passengers are
light, then we choose the speed of the composite wave to be the least
square speed of the carrier as in Lemma
4.1
. Thus the
base wave is essentially unaffected by the passengers, while the
passengers inherit the speed and width of the base wave.

On the other hand, if the composite wave is balanced, the carrier does
not dominate and the heavy passengers have an effect on the wave
speed. In this case, we choose the composite wave using least squares
again, but now using all parts of the composite wave. That is, we
treat the composite wave as a single entity and compute the least
squared speed of the total, namely

\text{minimize }\big(\widehat{[f]}-s\,\widehat{[q]}\big)^{2}:=\Big(\sum_{k^{\prime}}A_{k^{\prime}}([f]_{k^{\prime}}-s\,\sum_{k^{\prime}}[q]_{k^{\prime}})\Big)^{2}.

In this case we cannot use
Lemma
4.1
and we need to recalculate the residual.

For definiteness, suppose that the composite wave is given by
(
4.26
). Then each individual wave satisfies (

),
namely

(4.28)

[f]_{k^{\prime}}=\lambda_{k^{\prime}}(\overline{u}_{k^{\prime}})\,[q]_{k^{\prime}}+O(|\zeta_{k^{\prime}}|^{3}),\qquad\overline{u}_{k^{\prime}}:=\widetilde{W}_{k^{\prime}}\Big(u_{k^{\prime}-1},\frac{\zeta_{k^{\prime}}}{2}\Big),

for

[f]=\sum_{k^{\prime}}[f]_{k^{\prime}}:=f(u_{k+1})-f(u_{k-i-1}),\qquad[q]=\sum_{k^{\prime}}[q]_{k^{\prime}}:=q(u_{k+1})-q(u_{k-i-1}).

and so we set

(4.29)

\epsilon>0

At this stage the gRP solution on each subinterval

Lemma 5.1
.

The procedure given above generates a wave sequence such that no

The piecewise constant function

such that the variation

\mathcal{V}(\Gamma^{0+})=\|\widehat{\vphantom{\rule{14.0pt}{7.0pt}}\smash{\widehat{u}}}^{o}\|_{BV},\quad\text{satisfies}\quad\frac{1}{C}\,\mathcal{V}(\Gamma^{0+})\leq\|u^{o}\|_{BV}\leq C\,\mathcal{V}(\Gamma^{0+}),

, and
\mathcal{V}(\Gamma)
is given by (

Moreover, there is a finite, positive time

Proof.

Since
\widehat{u}^{o}

5.1.
Reconstruction

The mFT approximation is evolved by repeated adjustment of the wave
sequence
\Gamma^{t}
via interactions as described above. However,
given the wave sequence at time
t
, the question arises as to how to
map this wave sequence back into a
BV
function
u(\cdot,t)
which is
an approximate solution of the system (
1.1
). Typically this
reconstruction occurs only at a discrete set of times, so we regard

t
and
\Gamma^{t}
as fixed and given.

We begin by locating all discontinuities, which are shocks and
contacts, and are identified by
w_{j}(t)=0
. These yield the function

u^{d}(x,t):=\sum_{j^{\prime}}\big(u_{j^{\prime}}-u_{j^{\prime}-1}\big)\,H\big(x-x^{p}_{j^{\prime}}(t)\big),

where
j^{\prime}
runs over shocks and contacts, and
H
is the Heaviside
function. This accounts for all discontinuities of the reconstructed
solution.

In order to reconstruct the continuous part of the solution, we treat
the simple waves in each GNL family separately. Consider the
k
-th
family, which is GNL, and extract the subsequence of all those simple
waves for which
k_{j}=k
. We will provide a profile for each of these,
but before we do so we must make sure that none of the waves overlap.
If there is some overlap of
k
-waves, including shocks, we reduce the
widths of the overlapping simple waves as in Section
4.5

above. In doing so, we make sure that the reduced widths remain
non-zero: in particular, we would typically reduce widths as little as
possible, so that
k
-waves may touch but not overlap: that is, after
reduction,

(5.4)

x^{e+}_{j^{\prime}}(t)\leq x^{e-}_{j}(t),

where
e\pm
refer to the right and left edges of the wave, and

\gamma_{j^{\prime}}
and
\gamma_{j}
are
consecutive

k
-waves, so
k_{i}\neq k

for all
j^{\prime}<i<j
.

Once the wave widths have been reduced so that (
5.4
) holds and
no consecutive
k
-waves overlap, we fill in the profile of the simple
waves as in Lemma
3.1
above; according to that lemma,
this profile is unique for each simple wave
\gamma_{j}
, and

u_{j}=W_{k}(u_{j-1},\zeta_{j})
. Moreover, we can parameterize this part of
the simple wave as

u(x)=W_{k}\big(u_{j-1},z(x)\big),\qquad\text{with}\qquad z\big(x^{e-}_{j}\big)=0,\qquad z\big(x^{e-}_{j}\big)=\zeta_{j},

where
z(x)
determines the profile of the centered wave. Now define
the “smoothed Heaviside function” for the simple wave
\gamma_{j}
to be

\Phi_{j}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x\leq x^{e-}_{j},\\
W_{k}\big(u_{j-1},z(x)\big)-u_{j-1},&x^{e-}_{j}\leq x\leq x^{e+}_{j},\\
u_{j}-u_{j-1},&x\geq x^{e+}_{j},\end{cases}

and define the continuous profile function for the simple
k
waves by

u^{c}_{k}(x,t):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,k_{j}=k\\
\gamma_{j}\text{simple}\end{subarray}}\Phi_{j}(x).

Having obtained the profile
u^{c}_{k}
for each GNL family, we now
combine these together with the discontinuous part, to obtain the
reconstructed function

U(x,t)=u_{0}+u^{d}(x,t)+\sum_{k\ \text{GNL}}u^{c}_{k}(x,t).

Lemma 5.2
.

There is a constant
C^{\prime}>0
such that the reconstruction
U(x,t)
has
variation equivalent to that of the wave sequence
\Gamma^{t}
, that
is

\frac{1}{C^{\prime}}\,\big\|U(\cdot,t)\big\|_{BV}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big|\zeta_{j}\big|\leq C^{\prime}\,\big\|U(\cdot,t)\big\|_{BV}.

Moreover, each separate profile of the functions
u^{c}_{k}(x,t)

is part of an exact gRP solution, so, if waves of other families are
ignored, can be extended to an interval
(t-\tau,t+\tau)
for which
no residual is generated.

We note that passengers may be in the wrong place because wavespeeds
have been changed, but these are weak and since
t
is fixed we cannot
adjust their locations.

In this reconstruction we have used the virtual width of waves to
provide approximate simple waves profiles, which would, locally and in
the absence of waves of other families, be exact solutions of the
system. That is, by replacing simple jump approximations with
centered profiles, we locally reduce the residual to zero away from
overlapping waves. However the presence of other families makes this
effect difficult to quantify exactly.

Proof.

Since each contact and shock appears in
u^{d}
, and there are no
simple waves in a linearly degenerate family, we can write

\displaystyle U(x,t)

\displaystyle=u_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}g_{k}(x,t),\quad\text{where}\quad

\displaystyle g_{k}(x,t)

\displaystyle:=u^{c}_{k}(x,t)+\sum_{j^{\prime},\ k_{j^{\prime}}=k}\big(u_{j^{\prime}}-u_{j^{\prime}-1}\big)\,H\big(x-x^{p}_{j^{\prime}}(t)\big)\Big),

where again
j^{\prime}
refers to waves of virtual width
w_{j^{\prime}}=0
.
Here each summand
g_{k}(x,t)
corresponds to all waves
\gamma_{j}
in the

k
-th family,
k_{j}=k
. Since the eigenvectors are uniformly
independent and bounded on the compact set
K_{\mathcal{U}}
, there is a
single constant
C^{\prime}
such that

\frac{1}{C^{\prime}}\,\sum_{j,\ k_{j}=k}|\zeta_{j}|\leq\big\|g_{k}(x,t)\big\|_{BV}\leq C^{\prime}\,\sum_{j,\ k_{j}=k}|\zeta_{j}|,

for each
k=1,\dots,N
. Now adding over
k
, the result follows.
∎

It follows from Lemmas
5.1
and
5.2
, as well as
the convergence results below, that in order to prove the existence of

BV_{loc}
weak* solutions, it is both necessary and sufficient to
obtain a Glimm-like bound for the variation of wave sequences,

\mathcal{V}(\Gamma^{t})\leq G\big(\mathcal{V}(\Gamma^{0})\big),

for some nonlinear, nonlocal function

. In our scheme, this
statement is independent of the amplitudes of waves.

6.
Continuation of the Scheme

We now show that the mFT scheme can be continued for arbitrary times.
For fixed
\epsilon>0
, we define the scheme inductively as described above.
This yields a sequence

(6.1)

0<t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq\dots\leq t_{i}\leq\dots,

of
interaction times
of the mFT approximation. For each

t\in[0,T]
, this yields the wave sequence
\Gamma^{t}
encoding the
various data as in (
4.1
), and which in turn generates the
piecewise constant approximation

(6.2)

U^{\epsilon}(x,t):=u_{0}+\sum_{j}[u]_{j}\,H\big(x-x^{p}_{j}(t)\big),\qquad[u]_{j}:=u_{j}-u_{j-1},

and where
x^{p}_{j}(t)
is the position of the wave at time
t
, given by
(
4.12
). Here it is implicit that the sum is only over waves that
are actually in the sequence
\Gamma^{t}
at time
t
.

We first show that the approximation
U^{\epsilon}
is in the right space.

Theorem 6.1
.

For each
\epsilon>0
and
i\in\mathbb{N}
, the wave sequence
\Gamma^{t_{i}+}

has bounded variation, that is

\mathcal{V}\big(\Gamma^{t_{i}+}\big)<\infty
. Moreover, the approximation

U^{\epsilon}\in W^{1,\infty}_{*}\big(0,t_{i+1};BV_{loc},M_{loc}\big),

and for
t<t_{i+1}
, satisfies the bounds

(6.3)

\big\|U^{\epsilon}{}^{\prime}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{M_{loc}}\leq\Lambda\,\big\|U^{\epsilon}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{BV_{loc}}\leq C\,\mathcal{V}(\Gamma^{t}),

where
\Lambda
is a bound on the absolute wavespeed, and
C
is a
constant depending only on the system. Moreover
U^{\epsilon}
is in
the space
\mathcal{Z}
given by (
2.7
), namely

U^{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{Z}:=f^{-1}\big(L^{\infty}_{*}(0,t_{i+1};BV_{loc})\big)\cap q^{-1}\big(W^{1,\infty}_{*}(0,t_{i+1};BV_{loc},M_{loc})\big).

Proof.

After initialization there are a finite number of waves, and the
first interaction time is
t_{1}>0
. In particular, the initial
variation
\mathcal{V}(\Gamma^{0+})
is finite. Since each interaction
yields a finite number of waves of finite strength, it follows by
induction that after any finite number of interactions, both the
number of waves and variation remain finite.

For any
t\in(t_{i},t_{i+1})
, it is clear that

\big\|U^{\epsilon}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{BV_{loc}}\leq\sum_{j}\big|[u]_{j}\big|,

where we have used the triangle inequality in the case of composite
waves. Differentiating in time, we get

U^{\epsilon}{}^{\prime}(\cdot,t)=\sum_{j}[u]_{j}\,s_{j}\,\delta_{x_{p}(t)},

and since different Dirac masses have independent supports and

\|\delta\|=1
, we get

\big\|U^{\epsilon}{}^{\prime}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{M_{loc}}\leq\Lambda\,\sum_{x\in X^{t}_{d}}\Big|\sum_{j,\;x_{j}^{p}=x}[u]_{j}\Big|\leq\Lambda\,\big\|U^{\epsilon}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{BV_{loc}},

where
X^{t}_{d}
denotes the (finite) set of jumps at
t
, and each

s_{j}
satisfies
|s_{j}|\leq\Lambda
. Now, treating composite waves as
sums of individual waves
\gamma_{j}
, by (
3.12
), we have

u_{j}=E_{k_{j}}(u_{j-1},\zeta_{j})\quad\text{or}\quad u_{j}=W_{k_{j}}(u_{j-1},\zeta_{j}),\quad\text{so}\quad\big|[u]_{j}\big|\leq C\,|\zeta_{j}|,

for some constant
C
determined by the compact set in which we
solve the dgRS. Now adding gives

\sum_{j}\big|[u]_{j}\big|\leq C\,\sum_{j}|\zeta_{j}|=C\,\mathcal{V}(\Gamma^{t}),

which yields the bound (
6.3
).

Next, we observe that, because states are treated exactly, and
interactions in the mFT take place only at a single point, at which
incident waves meet and/or reflected waves emerge, the function

U^{\epsilon}
is continuous in
M_{loc}
at each point of interaction

t_{i}
(noting that all sums are finite because there are only
finitely many waves). It follows that

U^{\epsilon}:[0,t_{i+1}]\to M_{loc}
is (strongly) Lipschitz with constant

\max_{i^{\prime}\leq i}\mathcal{V}(\Gamma^{t_{i^{\prime}+}})
, so that

U^{\epsilon}\in W^{1,\infty}_{*}\big(0,t_{i+1};BV_{loc},M_{loc}\big)=W^{1,\infty}\big(0,t_{i+1};BV_{loc},M_{loc}\big).

Finally, since both
f
and
g
are
C^{1}
on
\mathcal{U}
, we can carry
out the same argument for

\displaystyle f\circ U^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=f(u_{0})+\sum_{j}[f]_{j}\,H\big(x-x^{p}_{j}(t)\big)\quad\text{and}\quad

\displaystyle q\circ U^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=q(u_{0})+\sum_{j}[q]_{j}\,H\big(x-x^{p}_{j}(t)\big),

which implies that
U^{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{Z}
given by (
2.7
).
∎

We now define

(6.4)

t_{\infty}=t_{\infty}(\epsilon):=\sup_{i}\{t_{i}\}.

If
t_{\infty}(\epsilon)=\infty
, then the scheme can be defined for all
times; if not, we say that there is an
accumulation of
interaction times
at
t_{\infty}<\infty
.

We claim that if interactions accumulate at
t_{\infty}<\infty
, then
some sort of blowup must be occurring, such as total variation blowup,

\mathcal{V}(\Gamma^{t})\to\infty
as
t\to t_{\infty}^{-}
, or failure of the

“
\alpha
-ray condition”
which we introduce below. Verifying
this condition for general systems will require a careful analysis of
wave interactions and self-similar wave patterns. In Section

9
below, we show that in the important prototypical case
of the
p
-system modeling isentropic gas dynamics, there is no
accumulation of interaction times, so the scheme can indeed be defined
for all
t>0
.

6.1.
Trajectories

Denoting the set of
all
interaction times as in (
6.1
),
we define the set of
trajectories
as follows. Each trajectory
is a
time-like
set of waves
\{\rho_{m}\}
, one from each wave
sequence
\Gamma^{t_{m}+}
, which generates a continuous path. We write

(6.5)

\rho_{m}=\gamma_{j_{m}}\in\Gamma^{t_{m}+},\quad\text{each}\quad m,

where
j_{m}
is the index of
\rho_{m}
in
\Gamma^{t_{m}+}
, so that using
(
4.1
),
\rho_{m}
has family
k_{j_{m}}
, strength
\zeta_{j_{m}}
, and
speed
s_{j_{m}}
, etc., for times
t\in(t_{m},t_{m+1})
. Continuity
requires that the position given by (
4.12
) satisfy

x^{p}_{j_{m}}(t_{m}+)=x^{p}_{j_{m-1}}(t_{m}-)\quad\text{each}\quad m,

so that the path of each trajectory is continuous and piecewise
linear, so Lipschitz. Note that if the
m
-th interaction takes place
away from this trajectory, we will have

k_{j_{m}}=k_{j_{m-1}},\quad\zeta_{j_{m}}=\zeta_{j_{m-1}},\quad\text{and}\quad s_{j_{m}}=s_{j_{m-1}},

as well as other common properties, although the index will generally
change,
j_{m}\neq j_{m-1}
. The interaction at
(x_{m},t_{m})
is

nontrivial
if either of the states adjacent to
\rho_{m}
changes
at
t_{m}
,

u_{j_{m}-1}\neq u_{j_{m-1}-1}\quad\text{or}\quad u_{j_{m}}\neq u_{j_{m-1}},

which also implies that at least some other properties change.

If there is a finite accumulation of interaction times,

t_{*}:=t_{\infty}=\lim_{i}t_{i}<\infty,

then, since trajectories are uniformly Lipschitz and contained in a
compact set by finite speed of propagation, there must be at least one
limit point
x_{*}:=\lim x_{j_{m}}
for some subsequence
j_{m}
. In
particular, there must be at least one
accumulating trajectory
,
which is a trajectory
\{\rho_{m}\}
such that

x_{m}:=x^{p}_{j_{m}}(t_{m})\to x_{*}\quad\text{as}\quad m\to\infty,

with infinitely many non-trivial interactions along the trajectory.
Our goal is to rule out the existence of such accumulating
trajectories, so that the mFT scheme can be defined for all time, for
finite discretization parameter
\epsilon>0
.

Fix a point
(x_{*},t_{*})
and speed
\alpha
. Consider the trace of the
approximation on the ray with speed
\alpha
and ending at
(x_{*},t_{*})
,
that is

V_{\alpha}(t):=U^{\epsilon}\big(x_{*}+\alpha\,(t-t_{*}),t\big),\qquad t\in[0,t_{*}),

where we have used (
2.5
) for
U^{\epsilon}\in BV
. Because

U^{\epsilon}
is piecewise constant, we can write
V_{\alpha}
as a sum of
Heavisides,

(6.6)

\displaystyle V_{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j}[v]_{j}\,H(t-t_{j}),\quad 0\leq t<t_{*},\quad\text{where}\quad

\displaystyle[v]_{j}

\displaystyle:=U^{\epsilon}\big(x_{*}+\alpha\,(t_{j}-t_{*}),t_{j}+\big)-U^{\epsilon}\big(x_{*}+\alpha\,(t_{j}-t_{*}),t_{j}-\big),

where now there could be infinitely many jumps, and we take
H(0)=1/2
.

Definition 6.2
.

The approximation
U^{\epsilon}
satisfies the
\alpha
-ray
condition
at
(x_{*},t_{*})
, if the limit

V_{\alpha}:=\lim_{t\to t_{*}^{-}}V_{\alpha}(t)

exists, uniformly for
\alpha
in compact intervals.

Note that this condition implicitly assumes that the scheme has been
defined up to time
t_{*}-
.

Theorem 6.3
.

For fixed
\epsilon>0
, suppose that the mFT approximation
U^{\epsilon}

satisfies the
\alpha
-ray condition for all
(x,t)
, with

t\leq t_{\infty}
. Then there are no accumulating trajectories and
the scheme can be continued for all times, that is

t_{\infty}=\infty
.

Proof.

Suppose
\{\rho_{m}\}
is an accumulating trajectory, and let the
accumulation point be

(x_{\infty},t_{\infty}):=\lim(x_{m},t_{m}),

which exists because the trajectory is Lipschitz. Denote the
minimum and maximum possible wavespeeds by

\underline{\lambda}:=\min_{\mathcal{U}}\lambda_{1}(u)
and

\overline{\lambda}:=\max_{\mathcal{U}}\lambda_{N}(u)
, respectively. By the
\alpha
-ray
condition, there exists some
K\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
k\geq K
,
and all
\alpha\in[\underline{\lambda},\overline{\lambda}]
, we have

\big|U^{\epsilon}\big(x_{\infty}+\alpha\,(t_{k}-t_{\infty}),t_{k}\big)-V_{\alpha}\big|<\frac{1}{2}\,{\epsilon^{e_{p}}},

where
{\epsilon^{e_{p}}}
is the maximum strength of any passenger. Now
consider the space-time triangle

(6.7)

D_{\Delta}:=\Big\{(x,t)\;:\;t_{K}\leq t\leq t_{\infty},\ x_{\infty}-\overline{\lambda}t\leq x\leq x_{\infty}-\underline{\lambda}t\Big\}.

It follows by the triangle inequality that all waves in this
triangle are
either
weaker than
{\epsilon^{e_{p}}}
, and so by
construction are lonely weak waves,
or
lie on a
ray running into the accumulation point
(x_{\infty},t_{\infty})
.

Waves that lie on rays into
(x_{\infty},t_{\infty})
cannot interact
before
t=t_{\infty}
, so will not interact inside
D_{\Delta}
. It
follows that any interaction inside
D_{\Delta}
must involve at least
one lonely weak wave. However, any interaction that results in a
lonely weak wave cannot increase the number of waves. Thus
because there are only finitely many waves at time
t_{K}
, and in
particular entering
D_{\Delta}
, there can be only finitely many
interactions inside
D_{\Delta}
, contradicting our assumption that

(x_{\infty},t_{\infty})
is an accumulation point.
∎

Together with Theorem
6.1
, this theorem implies that the mFT
approximations can be defined for all time, and moreover the variation
of these approximations remains finite for all time. However, we have
no control over the size of the variation as
\epsilon\to 0
, and indeed,
this can become infinite in the limit in the known examples of
solutions which blow up. This is analogous to the use of Euler’s
method in approximating the ODE

\displaystyle y^{\prime}

\displaystyle=y^{2},\qquad y(0)=1,\quad\text{namely}\quad

\displaystyle y_{k+1}

\displaystyle=y_{k}+h\,y_{k}^{2},\qquad y_{0}=1,

for which the approximation
y_{k}
is defined and finite for any
h>0
,
even though the ODE solution exists only for
t<1
.

As an immediate corollary, we have the following

characterization of blowup
of mFT approximations for general
systems:

Corollary 6.4
.

If the total variation of the mFT approximation blows up in finite time,

\big\|U^{\epsilon}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{BV}\to\infty\quad\text{as}\quad t\to t_{\infty},

then the
\alpha
-ray condition fails at some point
(x_{\infty},t_{\infty})
.

This corollary indicates that
BV
blowup generally occurs only in
cases similar to known blowup results, such as the focussing blowup
due to infinitely many interactions as depicted in Figure

5
,
[
26
,
27
]
, or uniform amplitude blowup in
which multiple separated interactions drive the solution out of any
compact set
[
57
,
60
,
26
,
3
]
.

7.
Convergence of the Scheme

In defining the scheme for a general system, we have thus far
introduced several exponents
e_{\square}
related to the discretization
parameter
\epsilon
. We now impose conditions relating these which will
allow us to establish convergence of the scheme, assuming that the
total variation
\mathcal{V}(\Gamma^{t})
is uniformly bounded for
t\leq T
.

The exponents and their corresponding small parameters we have
introduced are:

•

Rarefaction
e_{r}
: the maximum rarefaction strength is
{\epsilon^{e_{r}}}
;

•

Compression
e_{c}
: the maximum (absolute) compression strength
is
{\epsilon^{e_{c}}}
;

•

Weak
e_{w}
: the minimum (absolute) compression strength is

{\epsilon^{e_{w}}}
;

•

Simple
e_{s}:=\min\{e_{r},e_{c}\}
: maximum strength of a simple wave
is
{\epsilon^{e_{s}}}
;

•

Passenger
e_{p}
: the maximum passenger strength is
{\epsilon^{e_{p}}}
;

•

Jump
e_{d}
: the minimum discrete jump in initialization is
{\epsilon^{e_{d}}}
;

•

Initial
e_{i}
: the maximum initial
BV
error is
{\epsilon^{e_{i}}}
.

We have also yet to choose the parameter
Q(\epsilon)
which distinguishes
light passengers from heavy ones, and the parameter
\kappa<1/3
which
gives the relative minimum strength of a split rarefaction. There are
several constraints on these indices which are required for
consistency of the construction. For example, since we do not want
multi-rarefactions to be passengers, we must have

{\epsilon^{e_{p}}}<\kappa\,{\epsilon^{e_{r}}},\quad\text{so that}\quad e_{r}<e_{p}.

7.1.
Residual Calculations

Recall that upon initialization, all waves are either shocks or
contacts, compressions or rarefactions, and initially, there are no
multi-rarefactions and no composite waves. Also recall that the
residual
\mathcal{R}(\Gamma)
of a wave sequence is the measure given by
(
4.2
). We will examine convergence of the mFT via this
measure: that is, the scheme converges provided
\mathcal{R}(\Gamma)\to 0

as
\epsilon\to 0
.

Lemma 7.1
.

If the initial wave sequence
\Gamma^{0+}
has variation

\mathcal{V}(\Gamma^{0+})=:V^{0}
, then up to the first
interaction time, the residual satisfies

\big\|\mathcal{R}(\Gamma^{t})\big\|_{M}\leq O(1)\,V^{0}\,\epsilon^{2e_{s}},\qquad 0<t<t^{!}_{*},

where
e_{s}:=\min\{e_{r},e_{c}\}
,
t^{!}_{*}
is given in
Lemma
5.1
, and
O(1)
depends only on the system.

Proof.

Each initial wave is given the least squared wave speed, and
Lemma
4.1
applies. Moreover, the residual vanishes for
shocks and contacts having the correct (least square) speed. It
follows that these contribute nothing to the residual, whereas each
simple wave of strength
\zeta
contributes
O(|\zeta|^{3})
. Since all
simple waves have strength
|\zeta|<{\epsilon^{e_{s}}}
, we have

\big\|\mathcal{R}\big(\Gamma^{0+}\big)\big\|_{M}\leq\sum_{j}\big|R(\zeta_{j})\big|\leq O(1)\,\sum_{j}|\zeta_{j}|^{3}\leq O(1)\,\epsilon^{2e_{s}}\,\sum_{j}|\zeta_{j}|\leq O(1)\,V^{0}\,\epsilon^{2e_{s}},

where the sum is over initial simple waves and we have used
(
4.9
) of Lemma
4.1
.
∎

Our goal is to bound the residual after interactions in the same way:
try to get a bound of order
\zeta^{1+\ell}
for each individual wave,
and add. However, because we have used composite waves and
multi-rarefactions, more care must be taken. In particular, in a
composite wave with a heavy passenger, we cannot get a uniform
superlinear bound for the residual. We must thus make an assumption
about the number of such heavy passengers. Recall that the passenger

\gamma_{h}
with carrier
\gamma_{b_{h}}
is heavy if the ratio

|\zeta_{h}/\zeta_{b_{h}}|>Q
, see (
4.27
).

We are assuming uniformity in
t
, so it suffices to consider the
residual of a single wave sequence
\Gamma
. Recall from (
4.1
)
that
\Gamma
is indexed by
j=1,\dots,n
, and partition this set of
indices into

B_{L}\cup B_{H}\cup P,

where
P
is the set of passengers,
B_{H}
the set of bases or carriers

\gamma_{b}
with at least one heavy passenger
\gamma_{h}
, and
B_{L}
those of
bases with no heavy passengers. To each
j\in P
is associated a
carrier
b_{j}\in B_{L}\cup B_{H}
, while for each
j\in B_{L}\cup B_{H}
, we
have
b_{j}=j
.

Recall that the quantity
Q(\epsilon)
is used to distinguish light
passengers from heavy in (
4.27
). If a passenger
\gamma_{h}\in P
with
carrier
\gamma_{b}\in B_{H}
is heavy, then their strengths satisfy

Q(\epsilon)\,|\zeta_{b}|<|\zeta_{h}|\leq\min\big\{|\zeta_{b}|,\,{\epsilon^{e_{p}}}\big\}.

Define the
“weight of heavy passengers”
by

(7.1)

W_{H}(\Gamma,\epsilon):=\sum_{b\in B_{H}}\sqrt{\sum_{b_{h}=b}\zeta_{h}^{2}},

where
h
runs over heavy passengers only, and interpret this as
follows. If this sum is large, it indicates that there are many
interactions in which (weak) waves of different families, and hence
different wavespeeds, but comparable strength, do not separate. This
indicates a strong nonlinear connection between different familes and
is likely a result of nonlinear resonances such as those described
in
[
51
,
55
,
53
]
. We expect that such resonances could be
understood in the context of WNGO approximations
[
38
,
25
,
29
]
. In general, we expect systems with several GNL families and
compactly supported data to separate after some transient
interactions, due to their distinct wave speeds
[
28
,
36
]
,
in which case (
7.1
) should be much smaller after the families
separate. Similarly, we expect that when shocks are present, they are
usually strong, and will therefore have light passengers only, so not
contribute to this term. In any event, these issues need to be
addressed in order to obtain
BV
bounds which are necessary for the
proof of convergence, and are likely highly system dependent.

Lemma 7.2
.

For fixed
t
, the residual of the wave sequence
\Gamma=\Gamma^{t}

satisfies the estimate

(7.2)

\big\|\mathcal{R}(\Gamma)\big\|_{M}\leq\mathcal{V}(\Gamma^{t})\,\Big(K_{s}\,\epsilon^{2e_{s}}+K_{p}\,Q(\epsilon)\Big)+K_{hp}\,W_{H}(\Gamma,\epsilon),

where
K_{\square}
are constants depending only on the system via a
compact region of state space
\mathcal{U}
.

Proof.

From (
4.2
), the residual of the wave sequence can be written
as

\mathcal{R}(\Gamma)=\sum_{b\in B_{L}\cup B_{H}}\sum_{b_{j}=b}A_{j}\big([f]_{j}-s_{b}\,[q]_{j}\big)\,\delta_{x^{p}_{b}},

where
A_{j}
is the preconditioner, since each passenger inherits the
speed of the carrier. Since the Dirac mass has
\|\delta\|_{M}=1
,
this yields

(7.3)

\big\|\mathcal{R}(\Gamma)\big\|_{M}\leq\sum_{b\in B_{L}}\Big|\sum_{b_{l}=b}A_{l}\big([f]_{l}-s_{b}\,[q]_{l}\big)\Big|+\sum_{b\in B_{H}}\Big|\sum_{b_{h}=b}A_{h}\big([f]_{h}-s_{b}\,[q]_{h}\big)\Big|,

and we treat the two sums separately. Here we have separated
only composite waves, which are still treated as combined groupings.

The first term in (
7.3
) handles all bases carrying light
passengers. In this case, the carrier itself has a small residual,
and the passengers can be controlled by the carrier’s strength. For
any
b\in B_{L}
, we have

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{b_{l}=b}A_{l}\big([f]_{l}-s_{b}\,[q]_{l}\big)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\big|A_{b}([f]_{b}-s_{b}\,[q]_{b})\big|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\in P\\
b_{l}=b\end{subarray}}\big|A_{l}([f]_{l}-s_{b}\,[q]_{l})\big|

\displaystyle\leq R(\gamma_{b})+(N-1)\,\Big|\big(\lambda_{l}(\overline{u}_{l})-s_{b}\big)\,A_{l}[q]_{l}+O(|\zeta_{l}|^{3})\Big|,

where
R(\gamma_{b})
is the residual of the carrier and we have used
(
4.7
). The sum is estimated because each carrier has at most
one passenger from each family. Since these passengers are light,
we use (
4.27
) to write

(N-1)\,\Big|\big(\lambda_{l}(\overline{u}_{l})-s_{b}\big)\,A_{l}[q]_{l}+O(|\zeta_{l}|^{3})\Big|\leq K_{lp}\,|\zeta_{l}|\leq K_{lp}\,|\zeta_{b}|\,Q(\epsilon),

where
K_{lp}
depends only on the system and state space
\mathcal{U}
.

Since
\gamma_{b}
carries only weak passengers, its residual is
calculated with either the least squared or multi-rarefaction wave
speed. If the wave is a shock or contact, its residual is zero,
while if it is simple, then by (
4.9
) its residual is

R(\gamma_{b})=O(|\zeta_{b}|^{3})\leq K_{b}\,|\zeta_{b}|\,\epsilon^{2e_{s}}.

On the other hand, if
\gamma_{b}
is part of a multi-rarefaction

\widehat{\gamma}_{b}
, then we treat all parts of the same multi-rarefaction
together, and use Lemma
4.2
to describe its residual.
According to (
4.24
), the (combined) multi-rarefaction
residual is estimated by

\big\|R(\widehat{\gamma}_{b})\big\|\leq\sum_{i}\zeta^{(i)}\,K_{mr}\,\overline{m}^{2}\,\epsilon^{2e_{r}}=:\widehat{\zeta}_{b}\,K_{mr}\,\overline{m}^{2}\,\epsilon^{2e_{r}},

where each multi-rarefaction consists of
at most

2\,\overline{m}+1

pieces. Since each simple wave incident in an interaction has
strength at most
{\epsilon^{e_{s}}}
, and the total variation is assumed
bounded, the maximum size of any newly generated rarefaction is

C\,{\epsilon^{e_{s}}}
, for some constant
C
depending only on the variation
bound and compact region of state space. On the other hand, any
rarefaction is split into
2\,m+1
multi-rarefactions of size at
least
\kappa\,{\epsilon^{e_{r}}}
, which yields the estimate

2\,\overline{m}+1\leq\frac{C\,{\epsilon^{e_{s}}}}{\kappa\,{\epsilon^{e_{r}}}},\qquad\text{so that}\qquad\overline{m}^{2}\,\epsilon^{2e_{r}}\leq\frac{C^{2}}{4\,\kappa^{2}}\,\epsilon^{2e_{s}}.

Combining these, we estimate the first sum in (
7.3
) as

(7.4)

\sum_{b\in B_{L}}\Big|\sum_{b_{l}=b}A_{l}\big([f]_{l}-s_{b}\,[q]_{l}\big)\Big|\leq\sum_{b\in B_{L}}|\zeta_{b}|\,\big(K_{s}\,\epsilon^{2e_{s}}+K_{lp}\,Q(\epsilon)\Big),

where
K_{s}:=\max\big\{K_{b},C^{2}/4\kappa^{2}\big\}
.

We now estimate the second sum in (
7.3
), in which each base

\gamma_{b}
carries at least one heavy passenger
\gamma_{h}
. For such a
composite wave
\widehat{\gamma}_{b}
, we use Lemma
4.3
to write

R_{*}(\widehat{\gamma}_{b})\leq K_{hp}\,\sqrt{\sum_{b_{j}=b}\zeta_{j}^{2}}\leq K_{hp}\,\sqrt{\sum_{b_{l}=b}\zeta_{l}^{2}}+K_{hp}\,\sqrt{\sum_{b_{h}=b}\zeta_{h}^{2}},

where we have split the sum up into light (
l
) and heavy
(
h
) passengers. For the light passengers, we estimate the first
sum by

\sqrt{\sum_{b_{l}=b}\zeta_{l}^{2}}\leq|\zeta_{b}|\,\sqrt{N-2}\,Q(\epsilon),

while the term for the heavy passengers is left as is.
Combining these with (
7.4
) in (
7.3
) yields

\displaystyle\big\|\mathcal{R}(\Gamma)\big\|_{M}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{b\in B_{L}}|\zeta_{b}|\,\big(K_{s}\,\epsilon^{2e_{s}}+K_{lp}\,Q(\epsilon)\Big)

\displaystyle}{}{\displaystyle+K_{hp}\,\sum_{b\in B_{H}}\Big(|\zeta_{b}|\,\sqrt{N-2}\,Q(\epsilon)+\sqrt{\sum_{b_{h}=b}\zeta_{h}^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{b}|\zeta_{b}|\,\big(K_{s}\,\epsilon^{2e_{s}}+K_{p}\,Q(\epsilon)\Big)+K_{hp}\,\sum_{b\in B_{H}}\sqrt{\sum_{b_{h}=b}\zeta_{h}^{2}}\,,

where
K_{p}:=\max\big\{K_{lp},\,\sqrt{N-1}\,K_{hp}\big\}
, and this
immediately implies (
7.2
).
∎

7.2.
Convergence

We have defined the mFT scheme by essentially tracking and modifying
the wave sequences
\Gamma^{t}
. Lemma
7.2
provides a bound
for the residual of
\Gamma^{t}
, uniform in
t
. Our goal is to obtain
existence of a solution via the mFT scheme, as follows. First use the
wave sequences to generate the corresponding piecewise constant
approximations
U^{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{Z}
given in (
6.2
) and
Theorem
6.1
. Next, we use compactness to extract a
convergent subsequence and limit
\widehat{u}
, and finally, we use the
residual calculation above to show that the limit is a solution. In
order to apply compactness, we
assume
that the variation is
uniformly bounded.

Theorem 7.3
.

Suppose the mFT scheme is defined for
t\leq T
with the uniform
variation bound

\mathcal{V}(\Gamma^{t})<V,\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq t\leq T.

Suppose also that
Q(\epsilon)
can be chosen so that

(7.5)

Q(\epsilon)=o(1)\qquad\text{and}\qquad W_{H}(\Gamma,\epsilon)=o(1)\qquad\text{as}\qquad\epsilon\to 0^{+},

uniformly for
t\in[0,T]
. Then some subsequence of the
corresponding approximations
U^{\epsilon}
converges as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
, and the
limit is a weak* solution of the system (
1.1
).

In particular, referring to (
7.1
), and if
Q(\epsilon)=O({\epsilon^{e_{q}}})
,
then (
7.5
) holds if say

\#\big\{B_{H}\big\}\,\epsilon^{e_{p}-e_{q}}=o(1),\quad\text{as}\quad\epsilon\to 0^{+}.

Proof.

By Theorem
6.3
, the wave sequence
\Gamma^{t}
is
defined for all
t\in[0,T]
, and by Theorem
6.1
, the
corresponding approximations
U^{\epsilon}
live in the space
\mathcal{Z}

given by (
2.7
). Since
\mathcal{V}(\Gamma)
is bounded uniformly
in
t
and
\epsilon
, by (
6.3
), so are both
U^{\epsilon}
and
\widehat{U}^{\epsilon}{}^{\prime}
,

\big\|U^{\epsilon}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{BV_{loc}}\leq\frac{C}{\Lambda}\,\mathcal{V}(\Gamma),\quad\text{and}\quad\big\|U^{\epsilon}{}^{\prime}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{M_{loc}}\leq C\,\mathcal{V}(\Gamma^{t}),

or in other words

\displaystyle U^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}_{*}(0,T;BV_{loc})=\big(L^{1}(0,T;C_{0}^{-1})\big)^{*},\quad\text{and}\quad

\displaystyle U^{\epsilon}{}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}_{*}(0,T;M_{loc})=\big(L^{1}(0,T;C_{0}^{0})\big)^{*},

where we have used (
2.6
).

It follows by the Banach-Alaoglu theorem that there is some sequence

\{\epsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
, with
\epsilon_{i}\to 0^{+}
monotonically, and
limit
U
such that

U^{\epsilon_{i}}\to U\in L^{\infty}_{*}(0,T;BV_{loc})

as
i\to\infty
, where the convergence is in the dual topology of
the space
C_{0}^{-1}
of test functions. Since
q
and

f:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
are
C^{2}
, it follows that

\Big\|f\big(U^{\epsilon_{i}}(\cdot,t)\big)-f\big(U(\cdot,t)\big)\Big\|_{BV}\leq\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}}|Df(u)|\,\big\|U^{\epsilon_{i}}(\cdot,t)-U(\cdot,t)\big\|_{BV}\to 0,

uniformly in
t
, and similarly for
q
, and so also

\displaystyle f\circ U^{\epsilon_{i}}

\displaystyle\to f\circ U\in L^{\infty}_{*}(0,T;BV_{loc}),\quad\text{and}\quad

\displaystyle q\circ U^{\epsilon_{i}}

\displaystyle\to q\circ U\in L^{\infty}_{*}(0,T;BV_{loc}).

Next, for each
\epsilon>0
, and for each
t\in[0,T]
, the function

q\circ U^{\epsilon}(\cdot,t)
is piecewise constant and
BV
, so its
(Gelfand) weak derivative
(q\circ U^{\epsilon})^{\prime}
is a measure of bounded
variation,

(q\circ U^{\epsilon})^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}_{*}(0,T;M_{loc}),\quad\text{with}\quad\big\|(q\circ U^{\epsilon})^{\prime}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{M_{loc}}\leq\big\|q\circ U^{\epsilon}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{BV_{loc}},

uniformly for
t\in[0,T]
. Again using (
2.6
) and applying
Banach-Alaoglu, there is some subsequence, again denoted
\epsilon_{i}
, and
time varying measure
\mu:[0,T]\to M_{loc}
such that

(q\circ U^{\epsilon_{i}})^{\prime}\to\mu\in L^{\infty}_{*}(0,T;M_{loc}),

as
i\to\infty
. Now for space and time test function

\phi=\phi(x)\in C^{0}(K)
and
\psi=\psi(t)\in C^{1}([0,T])
,
respectively, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\big\langle\mu(t),\phi\big\rangle\,\psi(t)\;dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\lim_{i}\big\langle(q\circ U^{\epsilon_{i}})^{\prime}(t),\phi\big\rangle\,\psi(t)\;dt

\displaystyle=\lim_{i}\int_{0}^{T}\big\langle(q\circ U^{\epsilon_{i}})^{\prime}(t),\phi\big\rangle\,\psi(t)\;dt

\displaystyle=-\lim_{i}\int_{0}^{T}\big\langle q\circ U^{\epsilon_{i}}(t),\phi\big\rangle\,\psi^{\prime}(t)\;dt

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}\lim_{i}\big\langle q\circ U^{\epsilon_{i}}(t),\phi\big\rangle\,\psi^{\prime}(t)\;dt

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}\big\langle q\circ U(t),\phi\big\rangle\,\psi^{\prime}(t)\;dt,

where we have used dominated convergence and the Gelfand weak
derivative. This implies

\mu^{\prime}=q\circ U\in L^{\infty}_{*}(0,T;M_{loc}),

and so in particular, by (
2.7
)

U^{\epsilon}\to U\in f^{-1}\big(L^{\infty}_{*}(0,T;BV_{loc})\big)\cap q^{-1}\big(W^{1,\infty}_{*}(0,T;BV_{loc},M_{loc})\big)=\mathcal{Z}.

Thus the limit
U
of mFT approximations lives in the space
\mathcal{Z}
,
and it remains to show that
U
is a weak* solution.
Lemma
7.2
provides the estimate (
7.2
), so by our
assumptions (
7.5
) and continuity of the residual
(
2.9
) we have

\mathcal{R}(U)(t)=\lim_{i}\mathcal{R}(\Gamma^{t})=0,

so
U
is a weak* solution and the proof is complete. Note that the
use of preconditioners
A
does not affect this convergence, because
we assumed that
A
is uniformly bounded with uniformly bounded
inverse.
∎

Here we have chosen to use Banach-Alaoglu rather than Helly’s
Selection Principle because of its wider range of applicability: in
particular, the proof applies unchanged to the alternative space

\mathcal{Z}^{-b,\infty}
defined in (
2.8
), provided we choose
b

large enough that the Dirac mass
\delta\in H^{-b}
, that is for

b>1/2
.

Corollary 7.4
.

Under the same assumptions as Theorem
7.3
, and for any

b>1/2
, a subsequence of the mFT approximations converges to an

H^{-b}
weak* solution of system (
1.1
).

7.3.
General Approximation of
BV
Solutions

We now argue that, given a well-behaved
BV
solution
u(x,t)
of
system (
1.1
), we expect the approximation
U^{\epsilon}(x,t)

generated by the mFT scheme, to accurately resemble the actual
solution
u(x,t)
, for appropriately small values of
\epsilon>0
. Since we
are considering large amplitude, large
BV
solutions of a general
system, for which existence and regularity theorems are not yet
available, there is little hope of proving anything rigorous in this
context, so our comments are necessarily speculative.

So suppose that we are given a solution
u(x,t)
of
(
1.1
), such that
\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{BV}<\infty
uniformly for

t\in[0,T]
, and which takes on initial data
u^{o}(x)
and appropriate
boundary values. For given
\epsilon
, we use this initial data and the
same boundary conditions to generate the mFT approximation

U^{\epsilon}(x,t)
. Assuming the solution is unique, we then ask the
question:
under what conditions will the mFT approximation

not
converge to the actual solution
u(x,t)
?

Recall that in order to keep the number of waves finite, the mFT
approximation uses carrier and passenger waves, as described in
Section
4.7
. In doing so, by construction, we are assured
that
U^{\epsilon}
satisfies the
\alpha
-ray condition for all
t\leq T
. The
assumption of uniform
BV
bounds together with
Corollary
6.4
implies that interaction times do not
accumulate for
t\in[0,T]
. As a result, we expect an analog of the

\alpha
-ray condition to continue to hold in the limit as
\epsilon\to 0
. We
thus introduce the following condition that we expect solutions which
are limits of mFT approximations to satisfy.

Definition 7.5
.

We say that a function
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;BV_{loc})
satisfies the

strong
\alpha
-ray condition
, if for almost every
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

and
(x_{*},t_{*})
, the limit

\lim_{t\nearrow t_{*}}\big[u\big]\big(x_{*}-\alpha\,(t-t_{*}),t\big)\quad\text{exists},

where
[u](x,t):=u(x+,t)-u(x-,t)
is the jump in
u
, which exists
for a.e.
(x,t)
because
u
is uniformly
BV
.

This implicitly assumes that the
\alpha
-ray condition applies to
U^{\epsilon}

uniformly in
\epsilon
. In particular, this condition holds if (the
restriction of)
u
is
BV
on time-like line segments.

We now assume we are given the known
BV
solution
u(x,t)
of
(
1.1
), and that the approximation
U^{\epsilon}(x,t)
has been
generated as described above. We further assume that appropriate
entropy conditions hold for both the solution and approximation, and
that the corresponding entropy conditions are enough to guarantee

uniqueness
and
stability
of solutions, in some norm.

Now suppose that the mFT approximations
U^{\epsilon}
, which are assumed to
be uniformly in
BV
,
fail to converge
to the actual solution

u
, as
\epsilon\to 0
. We wish to understand what conditions prevent such
convergence. We argue as follows. By Helly’s selection theorem or
Banach-Alaoglu, we can extract a subsequence
U^{\epsilon_{i}}
which
converges to a
BV
limit

\widehat{U}=\lim_{i\to\infty}U^{\epsilon_{i}}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;BV_{loc}).

By construction, for each
\epsilon>0
, the approximation
U^{\epsilon}
satisfies
the (strong)
\alpha
-ray condition, and we assume that the given
solution
u
also satisfies it. This assumption effectively excludes
wave patterns similar to those of Figure
5
, in which the
accumulating interacting waves do not vanish in the limit, in which
case some form of blowup should occur.

According to Theorem
7.3
, because we are assuming
a
priori

BV
bounds, the only way in which the limit
\widehat{U}
is

not
a solution, is if (
7.5
) fails at some minimal
positive time
t=t_{\sharp}\in(0,T]
. This means that given any
function
Q(\epsilon)\to 0
, say including
Q(\epsilon)=1/\log|\log\epsilon|
, there
exists some
\delta=\delta(Q)
, and a subsequence
\epsilon_{i_{k}}\to 0
,
such that

W_{H}(\Gamma^{t_{\sharp}},\epsilon_{i_{k}})\geq\delta,\qquad\text{uniformly in }k.

Here the wave sequence
\Gamma^{t_{\sharp}}
implicitly depends on
discretization parameter
\epsilon_{i_{k}}
.

Using (
7.1
), we can estimate the number of heavy bases, as

\delta\leq W_{H}(\Gamma^{t_{\sharp}},\epsilon_{i_{k}})\leq\#\{B_{H}\}\,\sqrt{N-1}\,{\epsilon^{e_{p}}},\qquad\text{so that}\qquad\#\{B_{H}\}\geq\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{N-1}}\,\epsilon^{-e_{p}},

since there is at most one passenger from each family distinct from
that of the base. Moreover, taking the uniform bound for the total
variation to be
V_{0}
, we must also have

\#\{B_{H}\}\leq\frac{V_{0}}{Q(\epsilon)\,{\epsilon^{e_{p}}}},\qquad\text{since}\qquad|\zeta_{b}|\geq Q(\epsilon)\,{\epsilon^{e_{p}}}

for each heavy base
\gamma_{b}
. We therefore have both upper and lower
bounds for
\#\{B_{H}\}
of almost the same order, namely

\frac{\delta\big(Q(\epsilon)\big)}{\sqrt{N-1}}\,\epsilon^{-e_{p}}\leq\#\{B_{H}\}\leq\frac{V_{0}}{Q(\epsilon)}\,\epsilon^{-e_{p}},

and the function
Q(\epsilon)\to 0
as slowly as we like. At the same time,
each heavy base
\gamma_{b}
has a corresponding heavy passenger, say

\gamma_{j}
, for which

(7.6)

Q(\epsilon)\,|\zeta_{b}|<|\zeta_{j}|\leq\min\big\{|\zeta_{b}|,{\epsilon^{e_{p}}}\big\},

so the waves have comparable strength, while all waves between
\gamma_{j}

and
\gamma_{b}
also have strength at most
|\zeta_{b}|
.

By the Pigeonhole Principle, there must be (at least) two distinct
families, say
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
, and
O(\epsilon^{-e_{p}})
interactions, in
which the emerging wave strengths
\zeta_{b_{i}}
are comparable and of
size (
7.6
), while all emerging waves between them have size at
most
{\epsilon^{e_{p}}}
. In this sense, the wave families
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
fail
to separate, in that the corresponding wave strengths are strongly
correlated rather than independent, and we say that they are

“strongly coupled by nonlinear interactions”
. This is in
contrast to local small amplitude interactions in hyperbolic systems,
in which different families in compactly supported solutions generally
separate after a short time and propagate largely independently
afterwards, as described in
[
28
]
and
[
36
]
.

We can express this failure of separation by referring to the
classical Glimm interaction estimates
[
22
,
52
]
. Recall that these
give accurate descriptions of the effects of nonlinear interactions of
small amplitude waves. We can describe the estimates in the current
context as follows: suppose that (composite) waves
\alpha
and
\beta

span states
u_{\ell}
,
u_{m}
and
u_{r}
, respectively. Using the
notation of (
3.15
), we write

u_{m}-u_{\ell}=\sum\alpha_{i}\,\overline{r}^{\alpha}_{i},\qquad\text{and}\qquad u_{r}-u_{m}=\sum\beta_{i}\,\overline{r}^{\beta}_{i},

in which the composite wave
\alpha
has components of strength
\alpha_{i}

in each family, and the superscripts indicate that the
\overline{r}^{\alpha}_{i}
,
as given by (
3.13
), vary with different waves. In resolving the
interaction, we similarly write

u_{r}-u_{\ell}=\sum\zeta_{i}\,\overline{r}^{\zeta}_{i},

and resolve the
\zeta_{i}
’s in terms of the
\alpha_{i}
’s and
\beta_{i}
’s.
Glimm’s estimate states that if the incident waves are weak, we have

\zeta_{i}=\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}+\sum_{j>k}\alpha_{j}\,\beta_{k}\,\Lambda^{jk}_{i}+O_{3},

in which the
\Lambda
’s are second derivative Lie bracket

interaction coefficients
, and
O_{3}
is cubic in incident wave
strength, see
[
22
,
52
,
51
]
.

It follows that if a heavy passenger and carrier emerge from this
interaction,
|\zeta_{b_{1}}|\approx|\zeta_{b_{2}}|
, then either these were
present in the incident waves, or they arise as nonlinear effects of
the interaction of other waves of moderate strength. If they were
previously present then
\#\{B_{H}\}
does not increase across the
interaction. On the other hand, there are only fintiely many waves of
moderate strength, so these should not interact very often. In either
case, we do not expect
\#\{B_{H}\}
to grow too large for generic
systems. In any event, a deeper analysis of the interaction structure
will be essential in deriving
BV
bounds, and accurate estimates for

\#\{B_{H}\}
will likely emerge from any such derivation.

Conjecture 7.6
.

Suppose that the hyperbolic system (
1.1
) has a unique
BV

entropy weak* solution
u
, which is stable in an appropriate
topology. Further, suppose that
u
satisfies the strong
\alpha
-ray
condition
7.5
, and that a “weak separation condition”
holds for the system. Then the mFT approximation
U^{\epsilon}
generated
with the same initial data will converge to the actual solution
u

of the nonlinear system.

Note that we have not specified the “weak separation condition”
beyond the heuristic given above. Our conclusion is necessarily
“soft”, because little is known analytically about solutions to
nonlinear hyperbolic problems, particularly those of large amplitude.
Indeed, obtaining existence, uniqueness and/or stability in that
regime would constitute a major advance in the field.

8.
Application to Gas Dynamics

We briefly recall the Euler equations for compressible gas dynamics.
We work primarily in a Lagrangian or material frame, in which the
equations and wavespeeds are simplified. The equations are

(8.1)

(-v)_{t}+u_{x}=0,\qquad u_{t}+p_{x}=0,\qquad\big(\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}\,u^{2}+e\big)_{t}+(u\,p)_{x}=0,

expressing continuity, Newton’s law, and conservation of energy,
respectively. The equations are closed by an equation of state, say

e=e(p,s)
, satisfying the Second Law of Thermodynamics. Here
u
is
the velocity, and the thermodynamics variables are specific volume

v
, pressure
p
, specific internal energy
e
, specific enthalpy

h
, specific entropy
s
, and temperature
\theta
. We use
p
and

s
as the primary thermodynamic variables, so that the equation of
state is given by the enthalpy
h=h(p,s)
, and the Second Law is

dh=v\,dp+\theta\,ds,\qquad h:=e+p\,v,

so also

v=\frac{\partial h}{\partial p}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\theta=\frac{\partial h}{\partial s}.

The system is of the form (
1.1
), if we denote

(8.2)

w:=\begin{pmatrix}p\\
u\\
s\end{pmatrix},\qquad q(w):=\begin{pmatrix}-v\\
u\\
\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}\,u^{2}+h-p\,v\end{pmatrix},\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(w):=\begin{pmatrix}u\\
p\\
u\,p\end{pmatrix},

and being a physical system it is symmetrizable; see below.

8.1.
Generalized Riemann Problem and mFT Scheme

For smooth solutions the system can be written in quasilinear form,

Dq(w)\,w_{t}+Df(w)\,w_{x}=0,

where

(8.3)

Dq(w)=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{\partial v}{\partial p}&0&-\frac{\partial v}{\partial s}\\
0&1&0\\
-p\,\frac{\partial v}{\partial p}&u&\theta-p\,\frac{\partial v}{\partial s}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\text{and}\qquad Df(w)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
u&p&0\end{pmatrix},

in which we have used
e=h-p\,v
. We can now easily find the
generalized eigenvalues (
3.5
): after two elementary row
operations, this is equivalent to

\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\,r_{k}(w)=\lambda_{k}(w)\,\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{c^{2}}&0&-\frac{\partial v}{\partial s}\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&\theta\end{pmatrix}\,r_{k}(w),

where we have defined the
acoustic impedance

c=c(p,s)
by

(8.4)

c^{2}=-\frac{\partial p}{\partial v}=-1\Big/\frac{\partial v}{\partial p}.

We can now read off the generalized eigenvalues and vectors, namely

(8.5)

\displaystyle\lambda_{-}(w):=-c(p,s),

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}(w):=0,\quad

\displaystyle\lambda_{+}(w):=c(p,s),

\displaystyle r_{-}(w):=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\
1/c\\
0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle r_{0}(w):=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
1\end{pmatrix},\quad

\displaystyle r_{+}(w):=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1/c\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

Thus, as is well known, the system is strictly hyperbolic as long as

c(p,s)>0
, and the forward and backward waves are GNL while

\frac{dc}{dp}>0
. The eigenvectors are easily integrated to get the
simple wave curves: the forward and backward wave curves are

(8.6)

u_{r}-u_{l}=\pm\int_{p_{l}}^{p_{r}}\frac{1}{c({\cancel{p}},s)}\;d{\cancel{p}},\qquad s_{r}=s_{l},

respectively, and the (stationary) entropy jumps are

p_{r}=p_{l},\qquad u_{r}=u_{l}.

To describe the shocks and contacts, we solve the jump conditions
(
3.1
), which are

[u]=\sigma\,[-v],\qquad[p]=\sigma\,[u],\qquad[u\,p]=\sigma\,\big[\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}\,u^{2}+e\big].

Eliminating
[u]
in the first two equations gives

(8.7)

\sigma=\pm\sqrt{\frac{[p]}{[-v]}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad[u]=\pm\sqrt{[p]\,[-v]},

while the third equation becomes

\overline{u}\,[p]+[u]\,\overline{p}=\sigma\,\big(\overline{u}\,[u]+[e]\big),\qquad\text{or}\qquad\sigma\,\big([e]+\overline{p}\,[v]\big)=0,

where
\overline{p}:=\frac{p_{l}+p_{r}}{2}
, and we regard this as an equation for
the entropy jump across the shock. If
\sigma=0
, then also

[p]=[u]=0
, which is again an entropy jump, while if
\sigma\neq 0

we rewrite this as

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=[e]+\overline{p}\,[v]=[h]-\overline{v}\,[p],\qquad\text{or}\qquad

\displaystyle h(p_{r},s_{r})

\displaystyle-h(p_{l},s_{l})=\frac{v(p_{r},s_{r})+v(p_{l},s_{l})}{2}\,(p_{r}-p_{l}),

which is an equation for
s_{r}
, say, and coupled with (
8.7
)
is the equation defining the shock curve.

The global existence and uniqueness of solutions to the Riemann
problem is well known; extension to the generalized Riemann problem
follows readily.

Lemma 8.1
.

The generalized Riemann problem for the Euler equations
(
8.1
) is globally uniquely solvable, although possibly with
a vacuum. A vacuum occurs if and only if the condition

(8.8)

u_{r}-u_{l}\geq\int_{0}^{p_{l}}\frac{1}{c({\cancel{p}},s_{l})}\;d{\cancel{p}}+\int_{0}^{p_{r}}\frac{1}{c({\cancel{p}},s_{r})}\;d{\cancel{p}}

holds.

Proof.

The proof is a direct application of Lemma
3.4
. We
wish to connect states

\{w_{l}=:w_{0},w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}:=w_{r}\},

respectively by (arbitrarily large) backward, stationary and and
forward waves, and we must check that the differences

[u]_{k}:=u_{k}-u_{k-1}
form an independent set.

If we interpret the integral in (
8.6
) as an average by setting

\Big\langle\frac{1}{c}\Big\rangle_{lr}:=\frac{1}{p_{r}-p_{l}}\int_{p_{l}}^{p_{r}}\frac{1}{c({\cancel{p}},s)}\;d{\cancel{p}}=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\frac{1}{c({\cancel{p}},s)}\;d{\cancel{\eta}}>0,\qquad{\cancel{p}}:=\overline{p}+{\cancel{\eta}}\,(p_{r}-p_{l}),

then both forward and backward simple wave curves can be written as

(8.9)

u_{r}-u_{l}=\Big\langle\frac{1}{c}\Big\rangle_{ab}\,(p_{a}-p_{b}),\qquad s_{r}-s_{l}=0,

where subscripts
a
and
b
denote the
ahead
and

behind
states, respectively.

Similarly, recalling that the entropy condition is that the pressure
be higher behind a shock, we write the shock curve (
8.7
)
as

(8.10)

u_{r}-u_{l}=\frac{1}{|\sigma|}\,(p_{a}-p_{b}),\qquad|\sigma|=\sqrt{\frac{[p]}{[-v]}},\qquad p_{b}>p_{a},

in which the entropy is regarded as implicitly solved.

Combining the waves together in order, we can then write

(8.11)

w_{1}-w_{0}=(p_{0}-p_{1})\,\overline{r}_{-},\qquad w_{2}-w_{1}=(s_{2}-s_{1})\,\overline{r}_{0},\qquad w_{3}-w_{3}=(p_{3}-p_{2})\,\overline{r}_{+},

where we have set

(8.12)

\overline{r}_{-}:=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\
1/{\{c\}}\\
{[s]}/{[p]}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\overline{r}_{0}:=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\overline{r}_{+}:=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1/{\{c\}}\\
{[s]}/{[p]}\end{pmatrix},

and where

\frac{1}{\{c\}}:=\Big\langle\frac{1}{c}\Big\rangle\quad\text{or}\quad\{c\}:=|\sigma|,

for simple waves or shocks, respectively, and
[s]=0
for simple waves.

Since these vectors are clearly independent, by
Lemma
3.4
, the gRP has a unique solution whenever
w_{r}

can be reached from
w_{l}
. The pressure
p
has the range
p>0
,
with boundary
p=0
describing the vacuum, at which the system loses
hyperbolicity,
c(0)=0
. Since any value of
s
can be reached by a
contact, we need to check the possible range of velocity differences

u_{r}-u_{l}
, so we consider (
8.7
) and (
8.6
). For a
shock wave, we have
p_{b}>p_{a}
, and we have

u_{r}-u_{l}=\pm\sqrt{(p_{b}-p_{a})\,(v_{a}-v_{b})},

which is unbounded if either
p_{b}\to\infty
or
p_{a}\to 0
, because
this implies
v_{a}\to\infty
. Thus the shock curves are unbounded.

For simple waves, if the wave is compressive,
p_{b}>p_{a}
,
(
8.9
) is again unbounded as
p_{b}\to\infty
. However, for a
rarefaction,
p_{a}>p_{b}
and we must consider the limit
p_{b}\to 0
.
If the integral (
8.6
) is unbounded, then again the
rarefaction curve is unbounded and the gRP can always be solved with
the available waves. However, if the integral (
8.6
) is

bounded
, then so is the velocity variation
u_{r}-u_{l}
. In
this case the range of allowable velocities is precisely those for
which (
8.8
) fails.

If the vacuum condition (
8.8
) holds, then we cannot solve the
generalized Riemann problem with these waves because of the vacuum.
However, we can still solve the gRP as a weak* solution, by
introducing the vacuum as a new type of wave which is manifest as a
Dirac mass in the specific volume
v
; see
[
40
,
54
,
35
]
for details.
∎

Having solved the gRP, globally away from the vacuum, it follows that
the mFT scheme can be defined for gas dynamics. However, because
there are only three wave families, the middle one of which is
linearly degenerate, wave patterns such as those in
Figure
5
cannot be drawn, and so we define the mFT
scheme
without the use of composite waves
. In this case we
define the mFT as in Section
4
, following the adjustments
in the same order but not joining any waves into composites.
According to Theorem
7.3
, the mFT approximations will
converge to a weak* solution as long as they are defined, uniformly in
discretization parameter
\epsilon
, provided the total variation

\mathcal{V}(\Gamma)
remains uniformly bounded.

Thus,
assuming
a uniform time independent variation bound, we
will obtain convergence of the scheme to a weak* solution, and thus
global existence, provided we show that there is no finite time
accumulation of interactions. Below we show that in the
2\times 2
system
of isentropic gas dynamics, no such accumulation can occur while the
variation is uniformly bounded. We expect that similarly there is no
such accumulation for the full
3\times 3
system, because there are no
points of self-similarity due to linear degeneracy of the (middle)
stationary contact field.

8.2.
Equivalence of Material and Spatial Frames

System (
8.1
), (
8.2
) are written in a
material or Lagrangian frame, and it is known that under weak
conditions, this is equivalent to the system written in a spatial, or
Eulerian frame. If
y
denotes the spatial variable, then the
equations are

(8.13)

\displaystyle\rho_{t}

\displaystyle+(\rho\,u)_{y}=0,

\displaystyle(\rho\,u)_{t}

\displaystyle+\big(\rho\,u^{2}+p\big)_{y}=0,

\displaystyle\big(\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}\,\rho\,u^{2}+\rho\,e\big)_{t}

\displaystyle+\big(\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}\,\rho\,u^{3}+\rho\,u\,e+u\,p\big)_{y}=0,

representing conservation of mass, momentum and energy, respectively.
Here
\rho:=1/v
is the density of the gas, and all other variables
are the same, but regarded as functions of
(y,t)
rather than

(x,t)
.

The transformation
y\mapsto x
from spatial to material coordinates
is given by

(8.14)

x:=\int^{y}\rho({\cancel{y}},t)\;d{\cancel{y}},\qquad\text{so that}\qquad\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}=\rho(y,t).

This in turn can be inverted as

(8.15)

\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}=\frac{1}{\rho(y,t)}=v(x,t),\qquad\text{so also}\qquad y=\int^{x}v({\cancel{x}},t)\;d{\cancel{x}}.

This gives a bi-Lipschitz map as long as
v
and
\rho
both remain
integrable, and this is enough to show equivalence of weak solutions
of (
8.1
) and (
8.13
)
[
49
]
. Moreover, this can be
extended up to the vacuum
\rho=0
if we consistently allow
v
to be
a Dirac mass and we consider weak* solutions, see
[
40
]
.

It follows immediately that solutions of the Riemann problem are
equivalent in the Lagrangian and Eulerian frames. In particular, the
profiles of any centered rarefaction are consistent for positive
times. Therefore the profiles of centered compressions must also be
consistent because compressions are backward rarefactions. Thus the
gRP solutions are also consistent across the frames, and the dgRS and
mFT schemes must also be consistent.

Because the Lagrangian to Eulerian transformation is nonlinear, this
consistency of solutions must be reflected in the calculation of
wavespeeds in the Eulerian frame. Here we carry out the various
calculations and check that the nonlinear wavespeeds in the two frames
are indeed consistent.

Lemma 8.2
.

The simple and shock wavespeeds in Eulerian and Lagrangian
frames are related by

\lambda^{E}_{i}(w)=u+v\,\lambda^{L}_{i}(w),\qquad\sigma^{E}_{i}(w)=\overline{u}+\overline{v}\,\sigma^{L}_{i}(w),

respectively, while the states
w
across the waves are identical,
and these are consistently related by the maps (
8.15
) and
(
8.14
).

Proof.

We use the state variable
w
defined in (
8.2
), so that
(
8.13
) has the form (
1.1
) for

(8.16)

q^{E}(w):=\begin{pmatrix}\rho\\
\rho\,u\\
\rho\,(\frac{1}{2}\,u^{2}+h)-p\end{pmatrix},\qquad f^{E}(w):=\begin{pmatrix}\rho\,u\\
\rho\,u^{2}+p\\
\rho\,u\,(\frac{1}{2}\,u^{2}+h)\end{pmatrix},

where we have used

\rho\,e=\rho\,(h-p\,v)=\rho\,h-p,\qquad h=h(p,s).

Differentiating, we have

(\rho\,h)_{p}=\rho_{p}\,h+\rho\,h_{p}=h\,\rho_{p}+1,\qquad(\rho\,h)_{s}=\rho_{s}\,h+\rho\,h_{s}=h\,\rho_{s}+\rho\,\theta,

and we calculate

Dq^{E}(w)=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{p}&0&\rho_{s}\\[3.0pt]
\rho_{p}\,u&\rho&\rho_{s}\,u\\[3.0pt]
\rho_{p}\,(\frac{1}{2}\,u^{2}+h)&\rho\,u&\rho_{s}\,(\frac{1}{2}\,u^{2}+h)+\rho\,\theta\end{pmatrix},

and

Df^{E}(w)=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{p}\,u&\rho&\rho_{s}\,u\\[3.0pt]
\rho_{p}\,u^{2}+1&2\,\rho\,u&\rho_{s}\,u^{2}\\[3.0pt]
\rho_{p}\,u\,(\frac{1}{2}\,u^{2}+h)+u&\frac{3}{2}\,\rho\,u^{2}+\rho\,h&\rho_{s}\,u\,(\frac{1}{2}\,u^{2}+h)+\rho\,\theta\,u\end{pmatrix}.

After applying elementary row operations and simplifying, these can
be written as

Dq^{E}(w)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
u&1&0\\
\frac{1}{2}\,u^{2}+h&u&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{p}&0&\rho_{s}\\
0&\rho&0\\
0&0&\rho\,\theta\end{pmatrix},

and

Df^{E}(w)=u\,Dq^{E}(w)+\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
u&1&0\\
\frac{1}{2}\,u^{2}+h&u&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&\rho&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},

so that the generalized eigenvalue problem (
3.5
) becomes

\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{p}&0&\rho_{s}\\
0&\rho&0\\
0&0&\rho\,\theta\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\,\begin{pmatrix}0&\rho&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\,r^{E}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\rho/\rho_{p}&0\\
1/\rho&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\,r^{E}=\big(\lambda^{E}-u\big)\,r^{E}.

We now use (
8.4
) to write

\rho_{p}=\Big(\frac{1}{v}\Big)_{p}=\frac{-1}{v^{2}}\,v_{p}=\frac{1}{v^{2}\,c^{2}}=\frac{\rho^{2}}{c^{2}}=:\frac{1}{a^{2}},

where we have defined the
speed of sound
by

(8.17)

a(p,s):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho_{p}(p,s)}}=\sqrt{\frac{\partial p}{\partial\rho}}=\frac{c}{\rho}=c\,v.

It now follows easily that the eigensystem in Eulerian coordinates
is

(8.18)

\displaystyle\lambda^{E}_{-}(w):=u-a(p,s),

\displaystyle\lambda^{E}_{0}(w):=u,\quad

\displaystyle\lambda^{E}_{+}(w):=u+a(p,s),

\displaystyle r_{-}(w):=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\

\displaystyle r_{0}(w):=\begin{pmatrix}0\\

We similarly compute the shock curves in an Eulerian frame.
The jump conditions (

[\rho\,u]=\sigma^{E}\,[\rho],\qquad[\rho\,u^{2}+p]=\sigma^{E}\,[\rho\,u],\qquad\big[\rho\,u\,\big(\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}\,u^{2}+h\big)\big]=\sigma^{E}\,[\rho\,\big(\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}\,u^{2}+h\big)-p\big],

Following

[m]=0,\qquad[m\,u+p]=0,\qquad\big[m(\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}\,u^{2}+h)\big]+\sigma^{E}\,[p]=0,

which represent conservation of mass, momentum and energy across the

[u]=[b]=[m\,v]=m\,[v].

We now write the energy conservation as

\displaystyle m\,\overline{u}\,[u]+m\,[h]+\sigma^{E}\,[p]

\displaystyle=m\,[h]-(\overline{u}-\sigma^{E})\,[p]

\displaystyle=m\,[h]-\overline{b}\,[p]=m\,\big([h]+\overline{v}\,[p]\big)=0.

Comparing these with (

[u]^{2}=[p]\,[-v]\qquad\text{and}\qquad[h]=\overline{v}\,[p],

while we must have

\sigma^{E}=\overline{u}-m\,\overline{v}=\overline{u}+\overline{v}\,\sigma^{L}.

Now suppose that a trajectory in material coordinates is described

\frac{dx}{dt}=a^{L}.

\displaystyle\frac{dy}{dt}

\displaystyle=\int^{x}\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}({\cancel{x}},t)\;d{\cancel{x}}+v(x,t)\,\frac{dx}{dt}

\displaystyle=u(x,t)+v(x,t)\,a^{L}=:a^{E}.

On the other hand, if

has a jump along the trajectory, we

(8.21)

a^{E}:=\overline{u}+\overline{v}\,a^{L},

where
\overline{\square}
is the average of left and right states. This is

∎

As a corollary, we obtain equivalence of the mFT schemes defined in
either Lagrangian or Eulerian coordinates.

Lemma 8.3
.

The mFT schemes defined using either the Lagrangian or Eulerian
frames are equivalent. That is, the maps (
8.15
) and
(
8.14
) respect both the mFT scheme and the limits thereof.

Although we prove this lemma only for gas dynamics, evidently the same
result will hold for any more complicated system for which both
Lagrangian and Eulerian frames of reference can be consistently

Proof.

We check that the residuals of the two schemes are consistent,
although they are not identical. According to (
2.11
), it
suffices to show that the residuals of a single jump in either frame
are (uniformly) related. We thus suppose that we have a jump in the
state
w
, propagating with speeds
a^{L}
and
a^{E}
in the Lagrangian
and Eulerian frames, respectively. Following
[
49

, it is
convenient to introduce a new (degenerate) equation for the identity

) and
(
8.16

\widehat{q}^{L}(w):=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-v\\

\frac{1}{2}\,u^{2}+h-p\,v\end{pmatrix},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{f}^{L}(w):=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
u\\
p\\
u\,p\end{pmatrix},

and by setting

(8.22)

\widehat{q}^{E}(w):=\rho\,\widehat{q}^{L}(w),\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{f}^{E}(w):=\widehat{f}^{L}(w)+u\,\widehat{q}^{E}(w).

It follows that the systems (

\widehat{q}(w)_{t}+\widehat{f}(w)_{z}=0,\qquad\text{for}\qquad\big(\widehat{q},\widehat{f},z\big):=\big(\widehat{q}^{L},\widehat{f}^{L},x\big)\quad\text{and}\quad\big(\widehat{q},\widehat{f},z\big):=\big(\widehat{q}^{E},\widehat{f}^{E},y\big),

respectively, where the first, respectively second, equation is

Now consider the corresponding (vector) residuals

\widehat{R}^{L}:=\big[\widehat{f}^{L}\big]-a^{L}\,\big[\widehat{q}^{L}\big]\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{R}^{E}:=\big[\widehat{f}^{E}\big]-a^{E}\,\big[\widehat{q}^{E}\big],

\widehat{q}^{L}=v\,\widehat{q}^{E}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{f}^{L}=\widehat{f}^{E}-u\,\widehat{q}^{E},

and calculate

\displaystyle\widehat{R}^{L}-\widehat{R}^{E}

\displaystyle=-\big[u\,\widehat{q}^{E}\big]-a^{L}\,\big[v\,\widehat{q}^{E}\big]+a^{E}\,\big[\widehat{q}^{E}\big]

\displaystyle=\big(a^{E}-\overline{u}-a^{L}\,\overline{v}\big)\,\big[\widehat{q}^{E}\big]-\overline{\widehat{q}^{E}}\,\big([u]+a^{L}\,[v]\big).

This can be written as

\Big(I_{4}+\overline{\widehat{q}^{E}}\,{\scriptstyle{\begin{pmatrix}0\\

so that the residuals are equivalent whenever

and in particular, when using (

∎

8.3.
Preconditioning and Residual

We now introduce a preconditioner and calculate the corresponding
residual for a simple wave. Thus suppose we consider a discontinuity
approximating a simple wave in the Lagrangian frame. We use
(
8.2
) and denote the wavespeed of the discontinuity by
a
. Then
the unconditioned residual is

(8.24)

[f]-a\,[q]=\begin{pmatrix}[u]-a\,[-v]\\
{}[p]-a\,[u]\\
-a\,\big[\frac{1}{2}\,u^{2}+h-p\,v\big]\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
\overline{p}&\overline{u}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}[u]-a\,[-v]\\
{}[p]-a\,[u]\\
a\,\big(\overline{v}\,[p]-[h]\big)\end{pmatrix},

where, as usual,
[\square]
and
\overline{\square}
are the (exact) jump
and average values of a state, respectively. Next, we have

\frac{\partial h}{\partial p}=v,\qquad\frac{\partial v}{\partial p}=\frac{-1}{c^{2}},

so integrating, we get

(8.25)

[h]=\big\langle v\big\rangle\,[p]\qquad\text{and}\qquad[-v]=\Big\langle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\Big\rangle\,[p],

where we have defined

(8.26)

\big\langle g\big\rangle:=\frac{1}{p_{r}-p_{l}}\int_{p_{l}}^{p_{r}}g({\cancel{p}},s)\;d{\cancel{p}}=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}g({\cancel{p}},s)\;d{\cancel{\eta}},\qquad{\cancel{p}}:=\overline{p}+{\cancel{\eta}}\,(p_{r}-p_{l}),

so that
\big\langle g\big\rangle
is an integral average of
g

across the wave. By (
8.9
) we have

[u]=\pm\big\langle\frac{1}{c}\big\rangle\,[p]
, and using this together
with (
8.25
) in (

), we get, after simplifying,

\frac{1}{[p]}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
-\overline{p}&-\overline{u}&1\end{pmatrix}\big([f]-a\,[q]\big)=\begin{pmatrix}\pm\big\langle\frac{1}{c}\big\rangle\\
1\\
0\end{pmatrix}-a\,\Big\langle\frac{1}{c}\Big\rangle\begin{pmatrix}\Big\langle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\Big\rangle\Big/\Big\langle\frac{1}{c}\Big\rangle\\
\pm 1\\
\big\langle\overline{v}-v\big\rangle/\Big\langle\frac{1}{c}\Big\rangle\end{pmatrix}.

We now choose the preconditioner and vector residual to be

A:=\begin{pmatrix}\Big\langle\frac{1}{c}\Big\rangle\Big/\Big\langle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\Big\rangle&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
-\overline{p}&-\overline{u}&1\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad R:=A\,\big([f]-a\,[q]\big),

and we then have

\frac{1}{[p]}\,R=\begin{pmatrix}\pm\beta\\
1\\
0\end{pmatrix}-a\,\Big\langle\frac{1}{c}\Big\rangle\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\pm 1\\
\big\langle\overline{v}-v\big\rangle/\Big\langle\frac{1}{c}\Big\rangle\end{pmatrix},\qquad\text{where}\qquad\beta:=\Big\langle\frac{1}{c}\Big\rangle^{2}\Big/\Big\langle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\Big\rangle.

Lemma 8.4
.

The (preconditioned) least squared wavespeed
a_{*}
and the ratio

\beta
for a simple wave satisfy

a_{*}=\pm\frac{\beta+1}{2\big\langle 1/c\big>}+O\big(\big|[p]\big|^{2}\big),\qquad\text{and}\qquad\beta=1+O\big(\big|[p]\big|^{2}\big),

respectively, and the corresponding residual satisfies

R_{*}=[p]\,\frac{\beta-1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\pm 1\\
-1\\
0\end{pmatrix}+O\big(\big|[p]\big|^{3}\big)=O\big(\big|[p]\big|^{3}\big).

Proof.

The least square problem (
8.27
) regarding

a\,\Big\langle\frac{1}{c}\Big\rangle
as the parameter is easily solved, namely

a_{*}\,\Big\langle\frac{1}{c}\Big\rangle=\pm\frac{\beta+1}{2+\big(\langle\overline{v}-v\rangle/\big\langle\frac{1}{c}\big\rangle\big)^{2}},

which in turn yields

R_{*}=\frac{[p]}{2+\big(\langle\overline{v}-v\rangle/\big\langle\frac{1}{c}\big\rangle\big)^{2}}\left[(\beta-1)\begin{pmatrix}\pm 1\\
-1\\
0\end{pmatrix}+\frac{\langle\overline{v}-v\rangle}{\langle 1/c\rangle}\begin{pmatrix}\pm\beta\,\frac{\langle\overline{v}-v\rangle}{\langle 1/c\rangle}\\
\frac{\langle\overline{v}-v\rangle}{\langle 1/c\rangle}\\
\pm(\beta+1)\end{pmatrix}\right].

We now use Taylor’s expansion in (
8.26
) to evaluate

\displaystyle\langle g\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\Big(g(\overline{p},s)+{\cancel{\eta}}\,[p]\,\frac{\partial g}{\partial p}(\overline{p},s)+O\big({\cancel{\eta}}^{2}\,[p]^{2}\big)\Big)\;d{\cancel{\eta}}

\displaystyle=g(\overline{p},s)+O\big([p]^{2}\big),

for any
C^{2}
function

, since entropy

is constant on a
simple wave. It now follows from (

\beta=\frac{\Big(1/{c(\overline{p},s)}+O\big([p]^{2}\big)\Big)^{2}}{1/{c^{2}(\overline{p},s)}+O\big([p]^{2}\big)}=1+O\big([p]^{2}\big).

Similarly using Taylor expansions,

\displaystyle\overline{v}

\displaystyle=v(\overline{p},s)+\frac{v\big(\overline{p}+\frac{1}{2}\,[p]\big)-v(\overline{p},s)}{2}-\frac{v(\overline{p},s)-v\big(\overline{p}-\frac{1}{2}\,[p]\big)}{2}

\displaystyle=v(\overline{p},s)+\Big(\frac{1}{4}\,[p]\,\frac{\partial v}{\partial p}(\overline{p},s)+O\big([p]^{2}\big)\Big)-\Big(\frac{1}{4}\,[p]\,\frac{\partial v}{\partial p}(\overline{p},s)+O\big([p]^{2}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=v(\overline{p},s)+O\big([p]^{2}\big)=\langle v\rangle+O\big([p]^{2}\big).

It follows that
\langle\overline{v}-v\rangle=O\big([p]^{2}\big)
, and the claims
follow by direct substitution.
∎

We now further restrict to the

\big(-v(p)\big)_{t}+u_{x}=0,\qquad u_{t}+p_{x}=0,

described by

, while we similarly drop the last component of

and

. The acoustic impedance is again given by
(

), but is a function of the pressure only, namely

The simple wave and shock curves are reduced versions of (
8.11
),
(
8.12
), and we can describe both the forward and backward waves
with the same equation by writing

(9.2)

w_{r}-w_{l}=(p_{a}-p_{b})\,\overline{r}_{\pm},\qquad\overline{r}_{\pm}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mp 1\\

where the
\pm
refer to the direction of propagation and the
subscripts
a
and

refer to the states

and

the wave, respectively, and again

\{c\}:=1\Big/\Big\langle\frac{1}{c}\Big\rangle\quad\text{or}\quad\{c\}:=|\sigma|=1\Big/\sqrt{\Big\langle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\Big\rangle},

for simple waves or shocks, respectively, where we have used

.

9.1.
Wave Curves, Strengths and Residuals

We can rewrite (
9.2
) as the scalar equation

u_{r}-u_{l}=\frac{1}{\{c\}}\,(p_{a}-p_{b}).

and for simple waves, we can in turn write this as

(9.3)

u_{r}-u_{l}=\Big\langle\frac{1}{c}\Big\rangle\,(p_{a}-p_{b})=:z(p_{a})-z(p_{b}),

where
z=z(p)
is the
Riemann coordinate
, defined by

(9.4)

z(p):=\int_{0}^{p}\frac{1}{c({\cancel{p}})}\;d{\cancel{p}}.

The well-known fact that simple waves are characterized by constant
values of the Riemann invariants
z\mp u
follows immediately.

Because all simple waves satisfy (
9.3
), a natural choice of
(signed) wave strength is

(9.5)

\zeta:=\frac{1}{2}\,\big(z(p_{a})-z(p_{b})+u_{r}-u_{l}\big),

which is also the change in (opposite) Riemann invariant. The wave is
expansive if
p_{a}>p_{b}
, or equivalently
\zeta>0
, and compressive
otherwise; this is consistent with Lax’s condition that the pressure
should be greater behind a shock. Having defined the strength of an
individual wave, the variation of a wave sequence
\Gamma
consisting
of any number
n
of waves
\gamma_{j}
satisfying (
4.1
), is given by
(
4.3
), namely

\mathcal{V}(\Gamma):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big|\zeta_{j}\big|,

again consistent with other treatments such as
[
23
,
19
,
45
]
.

Having described the wave curves and defined the wave strengths, we
record the following lemma, which describes the effects of pairwise
interactions of waves. It is well known that this holds
asymptotically for small amplitude waves, by Taylor expansions.
However, because we are treating interactions exactly by using simple
waves (that is compressions) for reflections rather than
(asymptotically small) shocks, the estimates become exact and extend
directly to waves of
arbitrary
amplitudes. Because there are
only two families, there are only two types of pairwise interactions:
either two waves of different families cross, or waves of the same
family merge; when waves merge, at least one of the incident waves
must be a shock.

Lemma 9.1
.

If any wave crosses an opposite simple wave, its strength is left
unchanged. If any wave crosses an opposite shock, its (absolute)
strength is increased. When two waves of the same family merge or a
compression collapses, the wave strengths in that family combine
linearly. If both incident waves are compressive or a compression
collapses, an opposite rarefaction is reflected, while if one
incident wave is a rarefaction, an opposite compression is
generated. For a fixed shock, the strength of the reflected simple
wave is a strictly monotone function of the strength of the other
(merging) wave. Similarly, if a compression collapses, the strength
of the reflected rarefaction is strictly monotone in the strength of
the collapsed wave.

Proof.

The proof follows because, apart from the crossing of two shocks or
simultaneous collapse of compressions,
all
wave interactions
result in at least one emergent simple wave. The corresponding
Riemann invariant is constant along that wave, and defines the
strength of both incident and emerging waves, which is then
unchanged. The fact that strengths increase following crossing of a
shock is a consequence of monotonicity of
c(p)
, which is genuine
nonlinearity. Similarly the monotonicity of the reflected simple
wave from a merge or compression collapse follows from monotonicity
of
c(p)
. We omit the details of the proof and exact estimates,
which can be found in the preprint
[
58
]
and our forthcoming
paper
[
5
]
.
∎

We note that there is an interplay between wave strengths, defined via
Riemann invariants, and residuals. As we have seen, wave strengths
(and Riemann invariants) combine linearly for waves in the same family
and are particularly well suited to simple waves, allowing a clean
treatment of multiple interactions. On the other hand, shocks, which
are nonlinear and strengthen crossing simple waves, are characterized by
vanishing residual because the jump conditions are satisfied exactly.

For the isentropic system, Lemma
8.4
simplifies to give the

exact
wavespeed and (preconditioned) residual of a jump
approximation as

a_{*}=\pm\frac{\beta+1}{2\,\langle 1/c\rangle}\qquad\text{and}\qquad R_{*}=[p]\,\frac{\beta-1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\pm 1\\
-1\end{pmatrix},

respectively, where
\beta
is given by (
8.27
). In particular,
the residuals are multiples of constant orthogonal vectors, so can be
regarded as scalars,

\big|R_{*}\big|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\big|[p]\,(\beta-1)\big|.

It follows that when splitting up a
monotone
simple wave into
smaller pieces, the split residual can be calculated as the sum of the
(scalar) residuals of the smaller waves,

\big|R_{sum}\big|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\Big|\sum_{i}[p]_{i}\,(\beta_{i}-1)\Big|,

because the signs of each piece are the same.

Lemma 9.2
.

If simple waves are approximated using least square wave speeds,
then whenever
any
monotone simple wave is split into smaller waves,
the absolute total residual decreases.

Although we do not split compressions in the mFT scheme, we do split
them when initializing. This result is essentially a statement of
convexity: monotonicity of the wavespeed is implicit by our treatment
of rarefactions and compressions as different simple waves which are
never merged, and integrals of monotone functions are convex.

Proof.

Denote the
signed scalar residual
of a jump approximating a
simple wave between pressures
p_{a}
ahead of the wave and
p_{b}

behind the wave as

R_{*}(p_{a},p_{b}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,[p]\,(\beta-1),\qquad\text{where}\qquad[p]:=p_{b}-p_{a},

and
\beta
is given by (
8.27
). Using (
8.26
), we write

[p]\,\beta=[p]\,\Big\langle\frac{1}{c}\Big\rangle^{2}\Big/\Big\langle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\Big\rangle=\frac{I_{1}(p_{a},p_{b})^{2}}{I_{2}(p_{a},p_{b})},

where we have defined the integral

I_{k}(p_{a},p_{b}):=\int_{p_{a}}^{p_{b}}\frac{d{\cancel{p}}}{c({\cancel{p}})^{k}},\qquad k=1,\;2,

so the residual is expressed as

\sqrt{2}\,R_{*}(p_{a},p_{b})=\frac{I_{1}(p_{a},p_{b})^{2}}{I_{2}(p_{a},p_{b})}+p_{a}-p_{b}.

Now suppose that the simple wave is approximated by two jumps, each
given the least squared speed, and denote the newly introduced
middle pressure by
p_{m}
, where
p_{m}
is any pressure between
p_{a}

and
p_{b}
, and with corresponding velocity given by (
8.6
).
Then the combined signed scalar residual of the split pair is

R_{*}(p_{a},p_{m})+R_{*}(p_{m},p_{b})
. The difference in residuals after
splitting is then

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\,\Delta R_{*}

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{2}\,\big(R_{*}(p_{a},p_{m})+R_{*}(p_{m},p_{b})-R_{*}(p_{a},p_{b})\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{I_{1}(p_{a},p_{m})^{2}}{I_{2}(p_{a},p_{m})}+\frac{I_{1}(p_{m},p_{b})^{2}}{I_{2}(p_{m},p_{b})}-\frac{\big(I_{1}(p_{a},p_{m})+I_{1}(p_{m},p_{b})\big)^{2}}{I_{2}(p_{a},p_{m})+I_{2}(p_{m},p_{b})},

and after simplifying this becomes

\sqrt{2}\,\Delta R_{*}=\frac{\big(I_{1}(p_{a},p_{m})\,I_{2}(p_{m},p_{b})-I_{1}(p_{m},p_{b})\,I_{2}(p_{a},p_{m})\big)^{2}}{I_{2}(p_{a},p_{m})\,I_{2}(p_{m},p_{b})\,\big(I_{2}(p_{a},p_{m})+I_{2}(p_{m},p_{b})\big)}.

It now follows that
\Delta R_{*}
has the sign of
p_{b}-p_{a}
, which is
also that of
p_{b}-p_{m}
and
p_{m}-p_{a}
. On the other hand, the sign
of
R_{*}(p_{a},p_{b})
is that of
(p_{b}-p_{a})\,(\beta-1)
, and similarly
for
R_{*}(p_{a},p_{m})
and
R_{*}(p_{m},p_{b})
. Using (
8.26
) again,
we have

\beta=\Big\langle\frac{1}{c}\Big\rangle^{2}\Big/\Big\langle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\Big\rangle=\Big(\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\;\frac{1}{c({\cancel{p}})}d{\cancel{\eta}}\Big)^{2}\Big/\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\Big(\frac{1}{c({\cancel{p}})}\Big)^{2}\;d{\cancel{\eta}}<1,

by Jensen’s inequality. Thus
\Delta R_{*}
and
R_{*}(p_{a},p_{b})
have
opposite signs, which implies

\big|R_{*}(p_{a},p_{m})+R_{*}(p_{m},p_{b})\big|<\big|R_{*}(p_{a},p_{b})\big|,

whenever
p_{m}
is between
p_{a}
and
p_{b}
. A simple induction now
shows that any further splits will further reduce the (absolute)
residual.
∎

9.2.
No Finite Accumulation of Interaction Times

Finally, we show that for the
p
-system (
9.1
), because there
are only two families of waves, there is no finite accumulation of
interaction times. The essential idea is that except for a finite
number of compression collapses or pairwise interactions, which
necessarily involve at least one strong wave,
all

interactions consist of two waves in and two waves out. This in turn
implies that the number of waves remains finite for all intermediate
times, and the interaction times can then accumulate only at

t=\infty
.

Proof that there is no accumulation of interactions requires that we
have some control on the total variation of approximate solutions, so
in this context we
assume
such bounds, which will be derived
in
[
5
]
. Specifically, we assume the existence of a (nonlocal)
Glimm potential
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)\geq 0
, defined for the sequence

\Gamma=\Gamma^{t}
, such that the Glimm functional

\mathcal{G}:=\mathcal{V}+\mathcal{P}
for the total variation is nonincreasing
across
every
pairwise interaction. Because it is defined on a
wave sequence, the functional
\mathcal{P}
is defined for the mFT
approximations for each finite discretization parameter
\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
.

Assumption 9.3
.

There is a nonlocal, non-negative Glimm potential

\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}(\Gamma^{t})
such that both
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{V}+\mathcal{P}

are decreasing,

(9.6)

\Delta\,\mathcal{P}\leq 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Delta(\mathcal{V}+\mathcal{P})\leq 0,

across
any
pairwise interaction, and such that

\mathcal{V}(\Gamma^{0})+\mathcal{P}(\Gamma^{0})
is bounded. That is, if
\Gamma^{\prime}

is the wave sequence that arises after interaction of any pair of
waves in
\Gamma
, then we have both

\mathcal{P}(\Gamma^{\prime})\leq\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{V}(\Gamma^{\prime})+\mathcal{P}(\Gamma^{\prime})\leq\mathcal{V}(\Gamma)+\mathcal{P}(\Gamma).

Here we are treating the discretization parameter
\epsilon>0
as fixed, so
we do not require uniformity in
\epsilon
in the assumption or in
Theorem

Lemma 9.4
.

Suppose that Assumption
9.3
holds. Then there exists some
finite

N=N\big(\mathcal{P}(\Gamma^{0}),\epsilon\big),

such that there are at most
N
waves in the mFT approximation for
any time
t
for which the mFT approximation is defined.

Proof.

Since
\epsilon>0
, there are only finitely many waves in the initial wave
sequence
\Gamma^{0+}
. Without loss of generality, we assume each
interaction in the mFT is either the collapse of a compression or a
pairwise interaction. That is, there are at most two incident waves
in any interaction.

Next, there can be more than two waves leaving an interaction only
if one of those is a rarefaction which has been split after the
interaction. Recall that a rarefaction is split if its strength
after interaction satisfies
\zeta>{\epsilon^{e_{r}}}
. If both waves leaving the
interaction are rarefactions, then by Lemma
9.1
, the
incident waves are also crossing rarefactions, and so their
strengths are unchanged, and in particular they will not be split
after the interaction.

It follows that the number of waves in the mFT approximation can
increase only if the emerging waves consist of a shock of one family
and a rarefaction of the other. There are three such interactions
at which the number of waves increases:

(a)

collapse of a compression: 1 wave in, 2 or more out;

(b)

shock and compressive wave merge: 2 waves in, 3 or more out;

(c)

rarefaction crosses a (strong) shock: 2 waves in, 3 or more out.

We show that each of these can occur only a finite number of times.

In case (a), a compression of strength
\zeta
collapses: according to
Lemma
9.1
, a shock of strength
\zeta
is transmitted and
a rarefaction, say of strength
\mu(\zeta)>0
, is reflected. By
construction, all compressions have (absolute) strength at least

{\epsilon^{e_{w}}}
, and so by monotonicity,

\Delta\mathcal{V}=\mu(\zeta)\geq\mu\big({\epsilon^{e_{w}}}\big),\qquad\text{so also}\qquad\Delta\mathcal{P}\leq-\mu\big({\epsilon^{e_{w}}}\big).

Since
\mathcal{P}(\Gamma^{0})
is finite and
\mathcal{P}
is decreasing, there
can be no more than

N_{(a)}:=\frac{\mathcal{P}(\Gamma^{0})}{\mu\big({\epsilon^{e_{w}}}\big)}

such interactions.

For case (b), because two waves merge to produce a reflected
rarefaction of strength
{\epsilon^{e_{r}}}
, at least one incident wave is a
shock and the other is compressive, either a shock or simple wave.
We
overcount
by considering the number of such interactions
in which a rarefaction of strength at least
{\epsilon^{e_{r}}}/2
is reflected.
Again by Lemma
9.1
, the shock strengths add linearly,
so as above we get

\Delta\mathcal{V}\geq\frac{1}{2}\,{\epsilon^{e_{r}}},\qquad\Delta\mathcal{P}\leq-\frac{1}{2}\,{\epsilon^{e_{r}}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad N_{(b)}:=\frac{2\,\mathcal{P}(\Gamma^{0})}{{\epsilon^{e_{r}}}},

where
N_{(b)}
counts the number of these interactions.

In case (c), rather than counting only the interactions across which
the number of rarefactions increases by a single rarefaction
attaining strength
|\zeta|>{\epsilon^{e_{r}}}
across a single shock, we count
the number of rarefactions that increase their strength from less than

\frac{4}{5}\,{\epsilon^{e_{r}}}
, say, to more than
{\epsilon^{e_{r}}}
after crossing several
shocks, and increasing its strength each time. Since shocks have no
change in wavestrength after crossing a rarefaction, the change in
variation across these cumulative interactions (excluding
interactions of other “far away” waves) is

\Delta\mathcal{V}\geq\frac{1}{5}\,{\epsilon^{e_{r}}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Delta\mathcal{P}\leq-\frac{1}{5}\,{\epsilon^{e_{r}}},

so that

N_{(c)}:=\frac{5\,\mathcal{P}(\Gamma^{0})}{{\epsilon^{e_{r}}}}.

Note that any
new
rarefaction whose strength is greater than

\frac{4}{5}\,{\epsilon^{e_{r}}}
, so is not included in this count, has previously
been counted in the waves of step (b) above.

In each case above, the emerging rarefaction is split into a number
of smaller rarefactions. According to our construction, each such
split rarefaction has strength
at least

\kappa\,{\epsilon^{e_{r}}}
, for

\kappa>0
. On the other hand, because the total variation is
bounded, the total increase of strength is uniformly bounded, and
the total number of new waves generated in each such interaction is
bounded by some constant
M
, which may depend on
\epsilon
and
\kappa
.
Since the only interactions in which the number of waves can
increase are enumerated above, it follows that the the total number
of new waves generated throughout the scheme is at most

\big(N_{(a)}+N_{(b)}+N_{(c)}\big)\,M<\infty.

It follows that the total number of waves in the mFT approximation
is bounded, and the proof is complete.
∎

9.3.
Breakdown of Periodic Wave Patterns

As an application of the mFT scheme, we briefly analyze the periodic
wave pattern introduced in
[
9
]
. This example was introduced
to demonstrate the difficulty of obtaining
BV
bounds for the

p
-system and provides an enlightening example of a piecewise
approximation in which the total variation can in principle become
unbounded in finite time. The authors first find a closed cyclic set
of states which are connected by a series of simple and shock waves.
They then describe a pattern of interactions under which this cycle of
states is visited periodically, provided the widths and speeds of the
various waves are carefully controlled. By then perturbing this
picture, they are able to produce an apparent piecewise constant
approximation in which the total variation blows up.

We develop the periodic cycle in state space, and show that such a
periodic cycle requires a certain minimum strength of simple waves.
Given such a cycle, we then consider the mFT approximation to data
which in principle generates the periodic pattern. In particular, we
show that the periodicity of the characteristic pattern cannot be
sustained as the discretization parameter
\epsilon\to 0
. The periodic wave
pattern breaks down because our requirement that the residual vanish
in the limit means that the large simple waves must be finely
discretized, which implies that interactions including simple waves
are spread out over time and space. This in turn prevents the
accumulation of wave interactions, perturbation of which yields blowup
of the variation in
[
9
]
. Instead, in the mFT approximation,
the simple waves cause the pattern to spread out rapidly, so that
decay of waves due to cancellation between shocks and rarefactions of
the same family eventually becomes the dominant feature of the
solution.

which shows apparent periodicity cannot be drawn
without a very large residual. We state this as a corollary.

Corollary 9.9
.

Wave patterns which are a perturbation of that drawn in Figure

10
, and which perturb to solutions having infinite
variation in finite time, are inconsistent with the mFT scheme.

The critical strength
\zeta_{*}
is large relative to a fixed ahead state,
say
z_{0}
. For the prototypical case of a
\gamma
-law gas,
\zeta_{*}
scales
directly with
z_{0}
, and for a physically reasonable value of
\gamma
,
say
\gamma\approx 1.4
, we have
\zeta_{*}(z_{0})\gtrapprox 4\,z_{0}
. This in
turn implies that the Mach number of each shock as drawn in
Figure
10
is of the order of
M\gtrapprox 30
, so these
shocks are extremely strong. The (combined) simple waves in the
solution must have strengths of the same order, and it is then clear
that these cannot be reasonably approximated by a small number of jump
discontinuities.

Figure 14.
FT simulation of cyclic data

We conclude that although the periodic cycle of states can be
generated, and initial data based on the corresponding wave pattern
will generate solutions in which the total variation grows, the actual
solution will not generate a localized wave pattern in which
interactions concentrate. Rather, the interaction of many weak simple
waves takes up both time and space, and the corresponding solutions
will spread out rapidly, with corresponding decay due to cancellation
between shocks and rarefactions. As an illustration, in
Figure
14
, we show a “front plot” of the waves
generated by our implementation of the mFT code, including
compressions, simulating the cyclic Cauchy data described above: the
right panel is a magnified piece of the left panel. Here red and blue
waves are compressive, with the darker, thicker lines being shocks,
while the lighter thinner lines are compressions, while orange and
magenta lines are rarefactions. The spread of the pattern begins
around
t\approx 0.002
, at which the rightmost forward shock and
rarefaction meet and interact; after this the wave pattern spreads out
rapidly in space, and we expect no accumulation of interaction times,
while there are clear indications of oscillatory behavior in both
families. Finally we note that the (dimensionless) wave speeds in
this picture are very large: the leading forward shock has speed on
the order of
30/0.004\approx 7500
, while the leading edge of the
outside rarefaction is of the order
40/0.0004\approx 10^{5}
; this is
consistent with the very large Mach numbers mentioned above.
