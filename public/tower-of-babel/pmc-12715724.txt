Cortical beta power modulation is asymmetric and reduced during movement execution and inhibition in Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Roohollah J Deligani, Enrico Opri, Benjamin B Risk, Felicia Goldstein, Stewart A Factor, Lenora A Higginbotham, Nicole C Swann, Svjetlana Miocinovic
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInability to initiate movements is a cardinal feature of Parkinson’s disease (PD), but inhibitory motor control is also impaired. Cortical electrophysiologic spectral patterns of these processes are still poorly understood. We investigated cortical electrophysiology during movement planning, execution and proactive inhibition in patients with PD. We hypothesized that in the healthy state, proactive inhibition is accompanied by increased beta band activity in the right frontal cortex, and that this process is impaired in PD patients. We recorded 64-channel EEG in 20 moderately advanced PD patients, off and on levodopa, and 20 age-matched healthy controls during cued Go and Go/NoGo tasks and performed spectral time-frequency analysis. PD patients exhibited prolonged response times, more errors and altered cortical activity patterns compared to controls. Levodopa improved movement execution in a simple Go task, but impaired proactive inhibition in a more complex Go/NoGo task. Spectral EEG analysis during movement planning and execution (Go task) showed reduced beta power modulation in PD patients particularly in the more impaired hemisphere, off and on levodopa. When proactive inhibition was engaged (Go/NoGo task), beta power over the right lateral frontal cortex was higher (i.e. decreased less compared to Go task) in healthy controls than in PD patients on medications. In conclusion, PD patients exhibit decreased and asymmetric beta power modulation over the sensorimotor cortex during movement. Proactive inhibition is associated with elevated beta activity over the right lateral frontal cortex in healthy controls, and this pattern is attenuated in PD patients in conjunction with a behavioral inhibitory deficit.Keywords: Response inhibition, Proactive inhibition, Lateralization, Go/no-go task, Impulsivity

Introduction

Increased oscillatory power in the beta frequency range (13–30 Hz) is a well-known feature of parkinsonian state, consistently demonstrated in the motor regions of the basal ganglia in dopamine-depleted state and suppressed by levodopa or deep brain stimulation (Kuhn et al., 2006; Ray et al., 2008; Eusebio et al., 2009; Little and Brown, 2014). However, in the cortex, resting-state beta power is not elevated in patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD) despite strong connections between the basal ganglia and cortex (de Hemptinne et al., 2013; Swann et al., 2015; Stegemoller et al., 2016; Geraedts et al., 2018). Modulation of beta power during movement is a well-known physiologic phenomenon throughout the motor network including the cortex (Pfurtscheller, 1981; Courtemanche et al., 2003). Cortical beta desynchronization during movement is observed in patients with PD, but the degree of reduction may be altered compared to controls (Pfurtscheller et al., 1998; Swann et al., 2011; Heinrichs-Graham et al., 2014; Rowland et al., 2015; Meissner et al., 2018) suggesting that the inability to appropriately modulate beta power interferes with normal planning and execution of movements. However, comparably fewer studies compared PD patients to healthy controls, or tested levodopa effect on cortical activity in the same cohort (Karekal et al., 2022).

Beta activity is not only involved in motor initiation but also response inhibition, and patients with PD additionally have difficulty inhibiting movements (Gauggel et al., 2004; Manza et al., 2017). Patients commonly develop difficulties with executive function even in early disease (Muslimovic et al., 2005). One of the key components of executive control is the ability to cancel or inhibit responses (Miyake et al., 2000). In PD patients, the failure of inhibitory motor control may manifest as an inability to stop festinating gait or jumping out of a chair and losing balance. Beyond the failure of stopping or inhibiting motor responses, patients are also prone to impulsivity and compulsions, leading to behaviors such as overeating or gambling (Weintraub et al., 2012). While both behavioral and motor inhibition is thought to involve the prefrontal cortex and basal ganglia, it is unknown if these are overlapping or parallel networks (Aron et al., 2016).

Inhibitory motor control has a proactive and reactive component. Proactive inhibition refers to preparatory mechanisms that facilitate action inhibition (i.e. enables a person to act with restraint or caution), while reactive inhibition is a sudden stopping process of an initiated action triggered by an external stimulus (Liebrand et al., 2017). Studies of reactive inhibition suggest that inferior frontal cortex provides an emergency stopping signal to the basal ganglia through the subthalamic nucleus via the hyperdirect pathway, bypassing the striatum and the indirect pathway (Aron, 2011; Swann et al., 2011). This process may be mediated by beta band activity as supported by observations of increased oscillatory beta power over the right inferior prefrontal cortex during reactive stopping (Swann et al., 2009; Swann et al., 2011; Chen et al., 2020). Prior work on proactive inhibition suggests that it may also involve beta activity and may recruit the same brain areas as reactive stopping (Swann et al., 2013; Muralidharan et al., 2019). In PD, electrophysiologic studies detailing underlying cortical mechanisms for proactive control are lacking, and it is unknown how PD treatments affect this activity.

The purpose of this study was to investigate cortical electrophysiology during movement execution and inhibition in PD. We characterized EEG activity in patients with moderately advanced PD, off and on levodopa, and compared their behavioral responses and cortical activity to a cohort of age-matched healthy controls. We compared a movement task requiring proactive inhibition (cued Go/NoGo) against a simpler Go task to disassociate the effect of movement and the effect of inhibition on cortical activity. We hypothesized that cortical beta modulation is impaired in PD during movement execution and that it normalizes with levodopa. We further hypothesized that in the healthy state, proactive inhibition is accompanied by increased beta band activity in the right prefrontal cortex, and that this process is impaired in PD patients particularly when on levodopa and impulsivity may be more pronounced.

Methods

Subject selection

Patients with PD selected to undergo DBS surgery were recruited from the Emory Movement Disorders Clinic. They were part of a larger longitudinal study, but only preoperative results are presented here. Age- and sex-matched healthy controls were recruited from the local community. Exclusion criteria for healthy controls were the presence of a neurologic or psychiatric disorder, or use of medications primarily used to treat neuropsychiatric conditions.

All procedures were performed in compliance with relevant laws and institutional guidelines and have been approved by the Institutional Review Board of Emory University (protocol IRB00091680). The privacy rights of human subjects have been observed and all participants provided written informed consent.

Experimental protocol

Cognitive evaluation

Patients underwent detailed neurocognitive testing during their clinical workup for DBS surgery, and none had dementia. Healthy controls were tested using the same battery, administered by a psycho-metrist and scored by a clinical neuropsychologist. Healthy controls who were below expected normative scores were excluded from the study (N = 6). Global cognition was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) test. A composite executive domain score calculation and impulsivity scales are described in Supplementary Methods.

EEG acquisition and behavioral tasks

EEG was recorded using a 64-channel BioSemi ActiveTwo system (Amsterdam, Netherlands) at 2048 Hz sampling rate. Subjects performed two behavioral tasks, a Go task and a Go/NoGo task, while seated in front of a screen and holding a button in each hand (Fig. 1). Tasks were administered using custom scripts in MATLAB R2021b (Mathworks, Natick, MA) and Psychtoolbox-3 (Kleiner et al., 2007). A fixation signal (a central white 1 × 1 cm square) appeared at the onset of a trial, followed after 500 ms by a Cue signal (yellow or pink 2.5 × 2.5 cm square to the right or left of the central square) which indicated to the subject to plan either a right or left button press. After another 500 ms delay, a Go signal was issued by the central square changing to the same color as the flanking square (Isoda and Hikosaka, 2007). For the Go/NoGo task, in 25 % of random trials, a NoGo signal was indicated by the central square changing to a different color. Subjects had 1.5 s to make (or withhold) a response. A fixed Cue-to-Go delay and a fixed task order (Go task always first) were used to habituate subjects to responding and make subsequent response inhibition more challenging. To synchronize on-screen trial events with EEG signals, a photodiode was affixed to the bottom corner of screen and recorded with Biosemi. For the Go task, 120 trials were administered (4 blocks of 30 trials) after an initial 12-trial practice block (except two patients performed 90 trials and four patients performed 150 trials). For the Go/NoGo task, 240 trials were administered (8 blocks of 30 trials) after an initial practice block (two patients performed 180 trials).

Patients performed both tasks first OFF (~12 h) and then ON levodopa (45–60 min after a regular morning dose of levodopa; levodopa equivalent daily dose listed in Table S1) during one 3-h morning visit. Healthy controls underwent the same protocol with two recording sessions in one visit and a break in between (no medications were administered). Disease severity was assessed with Movement Disorders Society Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) part III, scored by a movement disorders neurologist and performed within 15 min of EEG recordings.

Analysis

Behavioral analysis

We calculated the following behavioral metrics (definitions detailed in Supplementary Methods): median response time, percent premature errors (responding before or up to 100 ms after Go signal), percent commission errors (responding on NoGo trials), percent wrong button errors (responding with wrong hand) and percent omission errors (not responding on Go trial).

EEG signal analysis

Signal analysis is detailed in Supplementary Methods. Briefly, data were processed using custom scripts in Matlab and EEGlab toolkit (Delorme and Makeig, 2004). Channels were referenced to average montage, filtered from 1 to 100 Hz and downsampled to 1000 Hz. Independent Component Analysis was used to remove eye and muscle artifacts (Jung et al., 2000) (number of rejected components was ~2 for each subject/task). Next, data were epoched into trials. Noisy trials were removed using automated functions in EEGlab. The average number of trials per subject used for EEG analysis after removing incorrect and noisy trials was 96 for Go and 202 for Go/NoGo (153 Go, 49 NoGo) tasks (~%16 of all trials removed). Trials were aligned to investigate relevant behavior so time zero was defined as the response time for movement execution analysis or as the Go/NoGo signal for movement inhibition analysis. Time-frequency spectrograms were generated using Morlet wavelets (Cohen, 2014). Spectrograms were averaged (for each subject and task) and baseline corrected (period between Fixation and Cue). Finally, channel spectrograms were averaged by region of interest: C3/4, CP3/4 for left/right sensorimotor cortex; F7/8, F5/6, FC5/6 for left/right lateral frontal cortex (Schmajuk et al., 2006).

Statistical analysis

Cognitive and behavioral data were not normally distributed (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test) so non-parametric paired and unpaired statistical tests (Wilcoxon signed rank and Wilcoxon rank sum, respectively) were used for comparison. Demographics were compared using paired Student’s t-test, Chi-Squared test, and Fisher Exact test. Correlations between behavioral, cognitive and clinical scores were investigated with Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. P-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The primary statistical analysis was comparison of EEG power changes in time-frequency spectrograms over predefined regions of interest for which we used non-parametric pixel-wise permutation testing and cluster correction detailed in Supplementary Methods (Cohen, 2014). For additional exploratory statistical comparison of topographic maps of EEG power, we used the same permutation testing (each channel treated as a pixel). Since EEG channels are not contiguous and defining clusters based on neighboring channels can be subjective, we used false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons in topographic maps (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995).

The data and materials necessary to reproduce the findings reported in this manuscript will be made available at https://osf.io/gd24w after publication.

Results

Subject characteristics

The study included 20 patients with moderately advanced PD before DBS surgery and 20 matched healthy controls based on age, sex, education and handedness. The controls had significantly higher MoCA and composite executive scores compared to patients (Tables 1, S1, S2).

Behavioral results

In the Go task, patients responded significantly faster ON medication (median 225 ms; interquartile range (IQR) = 118) compared to OFF medication state (271 ms; IQR = 61) (Fig. 1C). Healthy controls performed similarly between their first and second session (1st:222 ms; IQR = 84; 2nd:216 ms; IQR = 73). Patients had longer response times compared to controls when OFF medications, but there was no difference when ON medications. In the OFF-medication state, response times had a positive correlation with MDS-UPDRS motor exam scores (Fig. 1E).

In the Go/NoGo task, response times were comparable across medication conditions (PD OFF:389 ms; IQR = 127; PD ON:372 ms; IQR = 108), but patients OFF medication were significantly slower compared to controls (1st:335 ms; IQR = 89; 2nd:329 ms; IQR = 86; Fig. 1D). Motor disease severity did not correlate with the response times (Fig. 1E). In the medication ON state, patients had more commission errors (responding to NoGo signal) compared to medication OFF state (PD OFF: 3.3 %; IQR = 8.3; PD ON: 5 %; IQR = 12.5; Fig. 1F). For healthy controls, there was no difference in commission errors between sessions.

Response times for both cohorts were significantly slower in the Go/NoGo task compared to Go task regardless of medication state or session suggesting that proactive inhibition was employed during the Go/NoGo task. Response times and error rates (Figs. S1) did not correlate with MoCA scores indicating that general cognitive status did not affect task performance. Commission errors did not correlate with composite executive function scores or impulsivity scales (not shown). Specifically, no significant correlations were found between the QUIP score and commission errors or frontal beta power. Only 4 patients had a non-zero QUIP score (Table S1, S2), which limits the ability to detect a possible correlation.

EEG time-frequency results

Spectral power changes in the Go task

To investigate spectral changes during movement execution, we focused on the Go task and averaged EEG spectrograms with time zero defined by the button press. As expected, both alpha (8–13 Hz) and beta (13–30 Hz) power decreased in both cohorts during movement planning (Cue to Go period), movement initiation/execution (Go to Response period) and around the response compared to baseline (Fixation period) over the sensorimotor cortex. The difference was statistically significant in the beta band over the right sensorimotor cortex when comparing controls to patients OFF medication (|z| > 1.96, cluster corrected) (Fig. 2A). When patients were ON medication, the difference was smaller and not statistically significant after cluster correction (Fig. 2A). The findings were similar for left sensorimotor cortex but not statistically significant after cluster correction (Fig. S2).

To investigate beta power changes over the entire scalp, we averaged beta power around the response time (−100 ms before to 100 ms after the response) for each channel (Fig. 2B). In both medication OFF and ON conditions, the difference between patients and controls was the most pronounced over the midfrontal and right sensorimotor channels where controls reduced beta power more than the patients (difference z-score reaching −3 but no channels were significant after FDR correction).

To investigate the effect of laterality on beta power, we separately analyzed right-hand and left-hand responses (Fig. S3). In patients, there was a pronounced asymmetry in hemispheric responses compared to controls who modulated both hemispheres, so we did further analysis separating trials based on more versus less impaired hand (based on PD severity). Beta power decreased less in the more impaired hemisphere, and when trials were executed with the less-impaired hand, the more-impaired hemisphere was minimally modulated. In contrast, when the more-impaired hand was active, the less impaired (ipsilateral) hemisphere still displayed marked desynchronization (no differences significant after FDR correction; Fig. S4). This analysis was similar after excluding 3 left-handed controls to remove the possible effect of hand dominance (not shown).

There were no statistically significant spectral power differences when directly comparing patients OFF and ON medication or healthy controls between their two sessions (Figs. S5, S6).

Spectral power changes in the Go trials of the Go/NoGo task versus Go task

To investigate spectral power changes underlying proactive inhibition, we compared Go trials of the Go/NoGo task versus Go trials of the Go task with time zero defined by the Go signal. Specifically, we analyzed subtracted spectrograms to remove spectral changes due to movement planning/initiation (present in both tasks) and to reveal changes due to engagement of proactive inhibition (present in Go/NoGo task). Based on our hypothesis, we focused primary analysis on the lateral frontal regions. In healthy controls, beta power decrease during movement planning (Cue to Go period) was less pronounced in Go/NoGo compared to Go task which was statistically significant (|z| > 1.96, cluster-corrected with significant cluster outlined in black, Fig. 3). In other words, beta power was relatively higher over the right lateral frontal cortex during movement planning in the Go/NoGo compared to Go task. This difference was not observed over the left lateral frontal channels (Fig. S7). In patients, there was no difference in power between the tasks during movement planning regardless of medication state (Fig. 3).

Spectral power changes in the NoGo trials versus Go trials of the Go/NoGo task

Finally, we compared Go/NoGo trials where movement was executed (correct Go) with trials where movement was inhibited (correct NoGo). Since the number of NoGo trials administered was much smaller, we randomly selected an equal number of Go trials for each subject to include in this analysis. In channels over the right lateral frontal cortex region of interest, there was no difference in alpha/beta power reduction during movement planning and initiation regardless of the movement being executed or inhibited, in patients and controls (Fig. 5). There was more alpha/beta power increase (rebound) after movement execution compared to trials with no movement (statistically significant only for patients OFF medication). There was increased theta band (4–8 Hz) power approximately 250 ms after the NoGo signal which was the most pronounced in controls and patients OFF medications (not significant in either cohort after cluster correction).

Discussion

We studied spectral EEG patterns during movement execution and proactive inhibition in moderately advanced PD patients off and on levodopa and compared them to healthy controls. Our findings revealed that PD patients exhibited longer response times and altered cortical activity patterns. Levodopa improved movement execution in a simple motor Go task, but impaired response inhibition in a more complex Go/NoGo task without altering response times. Spectral EEG analysis during movement execution showed reduced beta power modulation in PD patients compared to controls, suggesting impaired sensorimotor cortex dynamics particularly in the more impaired hemisphere. During proactive inhibition, beta power over the right lateral frontal cortex was higher (meaning less decreased during Go/NoGo than Go task) in healthy controls compared to PD patients suggesting a potential mechanism for impaired proactive inhibition in PD. Levodopa did not have a direct effect on cortical oscillatory activity despite behavioral changes.

The effect of disease state and levodopa on movement execution

A recent systematic review of studies comparing EEG activity between PD and healthy controls concluded that while there is some evidence for increased low (2–8 Hz) frequency activity at rest and alterations during movement in PD, most studies were small with methodological deficiencies (Shirahige et al., 2020). In our study, we show there is a reduction in beta power modulation in PD patients compared to controls consistent with several smaller prior reports (Degardin et al., 2009; Stegemoller et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2019). A similar reduction of cortical beta power modulation during movement has been demonstrated for single-unit activity in parkinsonian non-human primates (Hendrix et al., 2018). However, two studies have reported exaggerated movement-related oscillatory changes in PD so these effects may be related to type of movement or restricted to focal areas of the sensorimotor cortex accessible with intracranial electrodes (Rowland et al., 2015; Chung et al., 2018). The reduction in beta power modulation was previously observed in healthy controls when their reaction times were experimentally slowed (Little et al., 2019; Leriche et al., 2022). Given that we observed similar cortical activity reduction in medication-ON state when patients performed similarly to controls, we posit that these differences are likely due to pathophysiologic PD changes rather than executed response times. Our results also indicate pronounced hemispheric asymmetry in beta power modulation in PD, similar to a prior study (Tamas et al., 2003). This is in contrast to healthy controls where beta power decreased in both hemispheres regardless of active hand (although more so in the contralateral hemisphere as expected (Pfurtscheller, 1981; Heinrichs-Graham et al., 2016)). However, it should be noted that these exploratory topographic findings did not survive statistical FDR correction and are presented as preliminary observations.

We observed that levodopa improves response times in the Go task but not in the Go/NoGo task. The preparatory inhibition in the Go/NoGo task was associated with a strategic slowing. This led to slower response times (compared to Go task) and a probable ceiling effect with respect to dopaminergic modulation. The Go task on the other hand was associated with quick responses such that motor deficits associated with dopamine depletion (and subsequent reversal with therapy) were apparent. Interestingly, despite behavioral changes, we did not observe differences in cortical activity when directly comparing off and on medication states even though we purposely conducted both evaluations during the same visit to keep EEG recording conditions the same. This would indicate that most electrophysiologic activity changes that occur with levodopa are at the subcortical level as commonly observed with intracranial depth recordings (Mathiopoulou et al., 2024) and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies (Maillet et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2024). Additionally, any possible cortical effects may be small and variable between the subjects, so our study may have lacked statistical power to detect them despite paired comparisons.

The effect of disease state and levodopa on response inhibition

Reactive inhibition, typically studied with a Stop Signal task, is impaired in PD (Logan et al., 1984; Gauggel et al., 2004; Manza et al., 2017; Verbruggen et al., 2019) and it modulates activity in the prefrontal regions and the basal ganglia (Aron, 2011; Mirabella, 2014). Specifically, increased beta activity over the right inferior frontal cortex during stopping suggests that it provides an emergency ‘brake’ signal to the basal ganglia via the cortico-STN hyperdirect pathway (Swann et al., 2009; Swann et al., 2011; Chen et al., 2020). In this study, we focused on less understood proactive inhibition where the subject must inhibit a planned action (Isoda and Hikosaka, 2007; Aron, 2011). With our EEG analysis, we showed that proactive inhibition of planned responses was accompanied by higher beta band activity (compared to Go task) only in healthy controls and specifically over the right and not the left lateral frontal cortex. Similar difference in beta power between the tasks was also observed over the bilateral sensorimotor cortices (before the Go cue) suggesting that healthy subjects started desynchronizing beta power later in the Go/NoGo task (when response was uncertain and response times were slower). The question remains whether this delay is driven by the observed changes in the right prefrontal cortex signaling the need for behavioral restraint, and how the basal ganglia may be involved. Patients on the other hand, did not show a similar beta modulation difference between the tasks even though they also responded slower during the Go/NoGo compared to Go task. This suggests that higher beta power in the right frontal region of heathy controls was not simply due to differences in response times between the tasks. In healthy subjects, movement cancellation is accompanied by beta power increases over prefrontal areas (Wessel, 2020). A similar pattern was observed in fMRI studies in healthy controls where regions related to inhibition were strongly lateralized to the right hemisphere (Simmonds et al., 2008; Jamadar et al., 2013). Our study adds support for right hemisphere dominance in proactive inhibition and demonstrates impaired beta power modulation in PD patients in this region. Additional studies are needed that can record activity simultaneously in different cortical and subcortical regions to investigate network communication and direction of information flow.

A meta-analysis suggested that levodopa could correct response inhibition impairment in early PD; however, few studies utilized within-subject design so the effect of levodopa on individual patient performance is not well understood (Manza et al., 2017). While PD patients are known to make significantly more commission errors than controls (Cooper et al., 1994; Manza et al., 2017; Mirabella et al., 2017), we explicitly demonstrate that this is not due to presence of the disease but is rather a negative effect of levodopa. Dopaminergic medications are thought to have an inverted U-shaped effect on executive function; thus, performance may worsen in early patients but improve in more advanced disease (Kulisevsky, 2000; Mirabella et al., 2024). Proactive inhibition has been shown to worsen with levodopa in patients with earlier age at disease onset supporting the dopamine overdose hypothesis (Kubler et al., 2019; Waldthaler et al., 2021). In our moderately advanced patients with relatively early age at disease onset (50 ± 8 years), proactive inhibition worsened with levodopa on a group level (Fig. 1F) and individually it improved in only one patient. Importantly, levodopa worsened proactive inhibition without improving response times in the Go/NoGo task suggesting a direct negative effect on cognition beyond simply speeding up responsiveness. A possible alternative mechanism to explain why levodopa worsens proactive inhibition can be because levodopa is taken in doses that cause short bursts of dopamine rather than a steady level. This pulsed stimulation can destabilize brain networks that connect the frontal cortex and regions of the basal ganglia. Such instability has been linked to impulsive behavior in PD pointing to network-level dysregulation rather than simple over-dosing of the striatum and cortex (Simoni et al., 2020).

In a recent study, patients on levodopa performed worse on a proactive inhibition antisaccade task compared to controls and did not exhibit increased beta activity during preparatory period in the right prefrontal cortex similar to our findings (Waldthaler et al., 2022). In a study of reactive inhibition where patients improved with levodopa, there was an increased beta band power over right frontal cortex for successful stopping (George et al., 2013). Our study did not show EEG differences in the prefrontal cortex when directly comparing off and on levodopa conditions despite significant behavioral effects (similar to the lack of effect on sensorimotor activity discussed earlier). We also note that the absence of a significant correlation between frontal beta power and commission errors may be related to the limited sensitivity of our behavioral measures, as commission errors were relatively few. In addition, variability among patients in disease severity, medication response, and individual task strategy could further reduce the strength of such associations. Nonetheless, we now have converging evidence from electrophysiologic studies indicating that insufficient beta power level in the right lateral prefrontal cortex of PD subjects is associated with impaired proactive inhibitory control. This is consistent with imaging study showing that levodopa decreased activity of the right inferior frontal cortex during reactive inhibition (Cerasa et al., 2015). Overall, these findings suggest that both reactive and proactive inhibition rely on similar circuits.

Relationship between movement initiation and inhibition

Difficulty with initiating movement is a defining feature of PD and involves excessive inhibition in the motor circuit traditionally thought to be due to imbalance between indirect (‘inhibitory’) and direct (‘excitatory’) pathways (Alexander et al., 1990). It has been suggested that impairment in initiating movements in PD might be related to abnormal proactive inhibitory control (Criaud et al., 2016). In other words, patients may be locked into maintaining proactive inhibition over voluntary movements even when the situation does not require action restraint. Increased fMRI signal within the proactive inhibitory network has been demonstrated in PD patients compared to healthy subjects that was also predictive of delayed movement (Criaud et al., 2016). PD patients can initiate movement more quickly if given sufficient preparatory time (presumably to reduce proactive inhibitory control) but will be abnormally slow without a cue (Favre et al., 2013). In our study, levodopa reduced proactive inhibitory control and improved movement initiation in the Go task. We propose that beta power may act as a brake that gets released during movement planning throughout the cortex but inhibitory tone is maintained in the right lateral frontal region during uncertainty. It is therefore possible that prefrontal inhibitory mechanisms may be involved in release of inhibition necessary for movement but when inhibitory tone is too low there will be an impulsive movement.

Limitations

We acknowledge several limitations in our study. First, we used fixed testing order so that medication off condition was always performed first. This was to ensure comparable EEG recording conditions and minimize the number of visits. We attempted to mitigate the order confound by having the healthy controls undergo exactly the same protocol with two repeated sessions so that we could assess for any learning or fatigue effects. The healthy cohort showed no behavioral or EEG differences between sessions. This makes it less likely that the observed levodopa effect in patients was caused by the task order alone, but cannot be completely ruled out as patients could fatigue faster or learn task strategies differently than controls. Second, none of our patients had clinical diagnosis of impulse control disorder which may be why we did not see a direct electrophysiologic change with levodopa (known to worsen impulsiveness). Nonetheless, patients showed impaired inhibition on the experimental task indicating a subclinical level of impulsivity. Third, our PD cohort consisted of patients evaluated for DBS, representing moderately advanced PD. Therefore, the findings may not generalize to earlier disease stages or to patients not considered for surgical treatment.

Conclusions

In summary, our study reveals that PD is marked by intertwined deficits in motor execution and proactive inhibitory control. The findings indicate that patients exhibit slower response times, increased error rates (in medication on state), and attenuated beta power modulation (particularly over sensorimotor and right frontal regions) compared to healthy controls. While levodopa improved the speed of movement initiation under certain conditions, it also impaired the neural dynamics underlying response inhibition, highlighting the complex and sometimes opposing effects of dopaminergic therapy. These electrophysiological insights not only underscore the potential of beta oscillatory activity in the right frontal cortex as a biomarker for executive dysfunction in PD but also point to the need for more targeted therapeutic strategies that address both motor and cognitive aspects of the disease.
