ChatGPT improves usability, effectiveness, scalability, interpretability and accessibility, in early diagnosis of metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease
Authors: Xiaoying Zhou, Zhipeng Gao, Han Ma, Jiahui Hu, Chengfu Xu, Zhe Shen, Miaomiao Tan, Chaohui Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) is one of the most common chronic liver diseases. We aimed to conduct a pilot study to evaluate the potential utility of ChatGPT in early diagnosis of MAFLD.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed data from participants undergoing annual health examinations at the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine in 2022. Questionnaires, laboratory tests, physical examinations, and liver ultrasonography were conducted. We built MAFLD-GPT by leveraging the GPT-3.5-turbo model provided by OpenAI. Zero-shot and few-shot learning approaches were used to identify patterns within the MAFLD data samples to construct an MAFLD model. We compared MAFLD-GPT with Machine Learning Technique (MAFLD-ML).ResultsIn total, 7,571 participants were included in the study, with 123 cases containing non-numeric data. MAFLD-GPT generates predictions using prompt engineering with zero- or few-shot training samples, whereas machine learning methods require massive training data. MAFLD-GPT demonstrated superior performance in recall and F1-score compared to traditional ML models, highlighting its potential for MAFLD diagnosis. The initial in-context examples (3-shot) brought the largest performance gain (F1 score = 0.724) in MAFLD-GPT, which outperformed SVM (the best performance achieved by traditional ML algorithms) by 6.1%. In addition, the evaluation results of the MAFLD-GPT model on 123 feature-missing samples demonstrated that MAFLD-GPT achieved promising scalability on non-numeric features. Importantly, MAFLD-GPT has the potential to be accessible to the public and to present its results in a clear and understandable manner.ConclusionsMAFLD-GPT surpasses the MAFLD-ML model in terms of usability, effectiveness, scalability, interpretability and accessibility, demonstrating its tremendous potential for early diagnosis of MAFLD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04442-5.Keywords: Metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease, Chat-GPT, Machine learning technique, In-context learning

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) ranks among the most prevalent chronic liver conditions on a global scale and has evolved into a notable issue of public health significance [1, 2]. It develops in the context of an unhealthy metabolic environment, most notably overweight or obesity, and is associated with disturbances in glucose homeostasis and lipid handling [3]. Therefore, early noninvasive detection is crucial, as it facilitates enhanced prevention and effective management of FLD associated with metabolic dysfunction.

In 2020, a group of international experts introduced a new term, metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), replacing the previous nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [4, 5]. This terminology offers a more precise delineation of the pathophysiology of liver diseases and their linked metabolic irregularities. MAFLD is defined as a condition characterized by liver fat accumulation in the presence of at least one of the following three metabolic conditions: overweight/obesity, T2DM, or at least two of the seven metabolic risk abnormalities.

Computer-aided decision-making systems for NAFLD have received considerable attention [6, 7]. Initially, a variety of comprehensive evaluation indexes are employed to assess NAFLD. For example, the Hepatic Steatosis Index (HSI) is based on alanine aminotransferase (ALT), Body Mass Index (BMI), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), sex, and diabetes history [8]. The ZJU index is a novel model to predicting NAFLD in the Chinese population [9]. All these indexes were derived through conventional statistical methodologies. The process is typically initiated with a logistic regression model, followed by an assessment to determine the degree of alignment between the data and the proposed model. Recently, in the field of computer science, machine learning (ML), which involves the extraction of specific features from vast datasets, represents a contemporary approach for analyzing large and complex data sets [6, 10–12]. We have used state-of-the-art ML algorithms to develop models to classify subjects with or without NAFLD in our previous study [12]. However, ML algorithms require large datasets, and they may encounter limitations when dealing with smaller datasets or data that contains missing information, posing great challenges in constructing accurate models.

Driven by promising advancements in artificial intelligence techniques, particularly in general-purpose models like ChatGPT, significant progress has been made towards utilizing such data-rich platforms effectively for healthcare [13–15]. ChatGPT is a large language model (LLM) built on a Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT-3.5 turbo), fine-tuned with human supervision and reinforcement. LLMs are trained on ultra-large-scale corpora and can support a wide range of natural language processing tasks with prompt engineering, and can be invoked to perform specific tasks without requiring additional training processes or hard coding. The ChatGPT has shown promising performance in accountant exams, medical licensing exams, and healthcare [16–18], demonstrating its great potential to generate accurate textual responses to complex input criteria.

Inspired by the promising performance of ChatGPT in understanding and solving complex tasks, in this study, we aimed to explore a pilot for the possible utility of ChatGPT in MAFLD decision-making.

Methods.

Methods

Data collection and preparation

Subjects

We retrospectively analyzed data from 9,901 participants who undergoing annual health examination in the First Affiliated Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University, China in 2022. All participant data were anonymized during collection and analysis. After excluding participants with incomplete information, 7,571 participants were included in the present cross-sectional study, with 123 cases containing non-numeric data, thus a total of 7,448 cases were enrolled. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University and was in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration. All data were retrospectively obtained from patients’ medical record. Before analysis, the dataset underwent a de-identification process, in which all direct personal identifiers (e.g., names, identification numbers, contact information) were removed, and indirect identifiers were replaced with unique study codes to ensure confidentiality. All methods were performed in accordance with the approved guidelines.

Data split

After removing 123 non-numeric data samples, we randomly split our collected dataset (7,448 cases) into two chunks: the training dataset and the testing dataset. In particular, 90% of the data samples were used for training and validation, and 10% were used for testing.

Clinical examination and diagnosis of MAFLD

Clinical and laboratory assessments were performed according to our previously established protocols [12], including standardized measurements of anthropometrics, blood pressure, and fasting biochemical markers. Participants abstained from exercise for 24 h before assessment. For all participants, blood was collected following an overnight fast to ensure fasting measurements. Blood pressure was measured by standard methods, and height and weight were recorded to calculate BMI for classifying overweight and obesity. Fasting serum triglycerides were measured by an enzymatic colorimetric GPO-PAP assay (glycerol-blanked) on an automated analyzer. MAFLD was diagnosed using ultrasound based on criteria from the Chinese Liver Disease Association [19]. MAFLD was defined as the presence of hepatic steatosis with 1 or more of the following: [1] Metabolic syndrome [2], type 2 diabetes; or [3] at least 1 metabolic abnormalities, (a) waist circumference ≥ 90 cm in men and ≥ 85 cm women or body mass index ≥ 24 kg/m2; (b) fasting glucose ≥ 6.1mmol/L or HbA1c ≥ 5.7%;c. triglycerides ≥ 1.7mmol/L or specific drug treatment; d. high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ≤ 1.0mmol/L in men and ≤ 1.3mmol/L in women or specific drug treatment; e. blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg or specific drug treatment.

GPT approach (MAFLD-GPT)

Given a patient and their clinical features, we proposed an automated approach, named MAFLD-GPT, which incorporates the LLMs and in-context few-shot learning to predict whether the patient has fatty liver disease or not. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to adapts the ChatGPT to assist in MAFLD predictions (Fig. 1MAFLD-GPT workflow).

MAFLD-GPT workflow

Feature design

In this step, our study included factors related to MAFLD, including a total of 16 metabolic-related features. These features are likely to be crucial indicators used in the analysis of the relationships between MAFLD and other factors. They included Age, Sex, Diastolic Blood Pressure (mmHg), Waist Circumference (cm), body mass index (BMI) (kg/m2),γ-glutamyltransferase (GGT), Direct bilirubin (µmol/L), Indirect bilirubin (µmol/L), Serum uric acid (µmol/L), triglyceride (mmol/L), HDL cholesterol (mmol/L), LDL cholesterol (mmol/L), Fasting plasma glucose (mmol/L), Hemoglobin (g/L), platelet count (*109/L), and serum HbA1c (%). All numeric features were standardized, and non-numeric categorical or textual features were directly formatted into natural-language statements for input to the MAFLD-GPT (which would be introduced later).

Specifically, MAFLD-GPT was instructed: “I want you to act as an AI fatty liver disease classifier.”, as shown in Table 1. This role instruction constrained responses to the MAFLD domain while deferring the specific task objective and output specifications to the subsequent Task Description section.

Prompt engineering – task description

After assigning the role of AI fatty liver disease expert to MAFLD-GPT, we also described the input/output restriction and format restriction in the prompt. Regarding the input restriction, we first described our task of predicting fatty liver disease, i.e., “I will provide you with detailed information of a patient, including medical measurements represented as ‘feature: value’ pairs. Your task is to analyze the detailed information and determine whether the patient has fatty liver disease or not.” The format restriction indicates that the inputs of MAFLD-GPT should be provided as feature: value pairs and wrapped with backticks. In terms of the output restriction, MAFLD-GPT should only reply with the prediction results without any explanations, i.e., “you should only output yes or no.” The prompt details are shown in Table 1.

In-context learning (training of MAFLD-GPT)

With the increasing ability of ChatGPT, in-context learning has been widely adopted which refers to inference-time conditioning via prompts without weight updates. It usually implemented as zero-shot learning and few-shot learning. Few-shot learning refers to in-context learning that includes a small number of labeled exemplars while zero-shot learning includes none. In this study, we explored the effectiveness of zero-shot learning and few-shot learning for MAFLD predictions respectively [14, 20].

Regarding zero-shot learning, it requires no training data at all; it only leverages the prior knowledge and logic reasoning of LLMs from ultra-large-scale training resources. We combined the role designation and task description to make our zero-shot learning prompt. Few-shot learning is utilized to augment the context with a few examples of desired inputs and outputs, which is widely used to elicit knowledge and logical reasoning from LLM to perform unseen tasks. For example, if two examples of inputs and outputs are provided to MAFLD-GPT, it is referred to as 2-shot learning. In this work, to determine the optimal number of examples for few-shot learning, we randomly selected 1–5 samples each time to construct our few-shot learning prompt for evaluation.

Few-shot examples were drawn from the training set using stratified random sampling to preserve the class distribution of the dataset. The dataset was first divided into positive and negative strata (MAFLD and non-MAFLD patients), and samples were drawn proportionally to maintain the same overall class balance as the training data. For each evaluation round, 746 test cases were used, and for each case, a distinct subset of few-shot examples was randomly selected. Depending on the few-shot configuration, one to five examples were sampled per test case while preserving the same positive-to-negative prevalence. This procedure ensured that the overall sampling across all evaluations reflected the real-world prevalence of MAFLD, while the specific examples used for each test case varied between rounds, promoting both fairness and diversity in the few-shot learning evaluation.

Prompt design and optimization

Finally, the prompt template used in MAFLD-GPT is structured as follows: [Role Designation] [Task Description] [Few-shot Learning Examples] [Query Example]. Specifically, [Role Designation] defines the expert role assigned to ChatGPT, ensuring its responses focus on MAFLD-related medical knowledge. [Task Description] specifies the study task, where the model receives patient information in the form of < feature: value > pairs and outputs a binary decision (“Yes” or “No”). [Few-shot Learning Examples] are representative samples drawn from the ML training dataset, designed to activate the model’s domain understanding and logical reasoning. Finally, [Query Example] refers to the current test case input to MAFLD-GPT, for which the model predicts “Yes” (presence of fatty liver disease) or “No” (absence of fatty liver disease). Empirical results indicated that prompts explicitly specifying the expert role (e.g., “AI fatter liver disease classifier”) and employing few-shot examples yielded the most stable and accurate responses. Adopting a structured and consistent < feature: value > input format facilitated improved interpretability and reduced response ambiguity. Finally, responses containing ambiguous expressions (e.g., “most likely yes” or “probably no”) were normalized to binary outputs (“Yes” or “No”) to ensure compatibility with downstream evaluation metrics. We illustrated a 3-shot learning example prompt in Table 1 as a reference.

All model interactions were implemented through the OpenAI application programming interface (API) (https://platform.openai.com/docs/introduction), which enables programmatic access to large language models such as ChatGPT. The GPT-3.5-turbo model (March 2023 release) was employed for all MAFLD-GPT experiments. To ensure transparency and reproducibility, the API configuration was fixed across all experiments. The following parameters were applied: temperature = 0, max tokens set to 512, and default values for top-p, frequency penalty, and presence penalty. All other parameters were maintained at their default values.

Predicting results (testing of MAFLD-GPT)

The training dataset only applies to ML methods. For a fair comparison, MAFLD-GPT and baselines use the exactly the same testing dataset to report the final performance.

Each case was evaluated in three independent rounds (with the same resampled few-shot examples). The final prediction was determined by majority voting, which aggregates multiple predictions for the same case and selects the result with the highest vote count, reducing variance from prompt sampling. Performance was then summarized on the majority-vote labels by recall, precision, accuracy, F1-score.

To further demonstrate the robustness of our approach, MAFLD-GPT was tested in two methods: (i). each test case in three rounds with independent resampled few-shot examples; (ii). with same prompt in different time twice. Also, the same 10-fold cross-validation splits used for traditional ML models (which would be introduced later) were applied to evaluate MAFLD-GPT which will introduced later. For each fold, the few-shot examples are drawn exclusively from the training portion, and the model is tested on the corresponding held-out fold.

Subgroup analysis

We assessed model generalizability via prespecified subgroup analyses by sex (female vs. male) and age (≤ 50 vs. >50 years). For the age subgroup analysis, age is a recognized risk factor for MAFLD progression; however, current clinical guidelines do not define a specific age cutoff for screening or diagnosis. In our study, we selected 50 years as the cut-off point since The 2024 joint EASL–EASD–EASO clinical practice guidelines highlight men aged >50 years and postmenopausal women as populations at higher risk for progressive fibrosis in MASLD/MAFLD [21]. For each subgroup, predictions were obtained using the majority voting as introduced before (three runs with same few-shot examples; final prediction by majority voting). Performance metrics included accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score.

Traditional machine learning technique (MAFLD-ML)

To verify the effectiveness of our proposed MAFLD-GPT, we compared our MAFLD-GPT with several commonly used ML techniques, which can only deal with numeric features. In particular, we choose the following ML-based baselines: Logistic Regression, Decision Trees, SVM, and Random Forest. Each data sample had 16 MAFLD-relevant numeric features. Specific settings of ML methods followed as our previous study [12]. Notably, the ML models were trained and evaluated only on the numeric dataset, while MAFLD-GPT was assessed both on the numeric dataset and separately on the 123 cases containing non-numeric textual features. It is also worth mentioning that for a fair comparison, for both ML methods and MAFLD-GPT, all numeric features were not standardized prior to training and no specific technology was performed to handle the class imbalance problem.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS software (version 13.0; SPSS, Chicago, IL) and R software (version 4.4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess whether continuous data were normally distributed. Continuous variables were expressed as the mean and standard deviation (SD) or the median and interquartile range and were compared using the Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test. The chi-squared test was used for the comparison of categorical variables. Stepwise logistic regression analysis (Backward: Wald; Entry: 0.05, Removal: 0.10) was used to evaluate the risk factors for MAFLD. Intra-group consistency across three repeated tests was evaluated using Flesis kappa and Cohen kappa test. The diagnostic performance was assessed using precision, recall, accuracy and F1-score. For paired comparisons of diagnostic methods on the same cohort, McNemar’s test was prespecified for accuracy and recall, while precision and F1-scores were compared using bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations). For independent subgroup analyses (e.g., gender, age), accuracy was tested with Fisher’s exact test, precision and recall with the two-proportion z-test, and F1-scores by bootstrap. P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Effectiveness of performance of MAFLD-GPT

Effectiveness of machine-learning algorisms

To establish a reference for evaluating MAFLD-GPT, we assessed the predictive performance of conventional ML models. As shown in Table 4, the results reveal notable variability across methods. Logistic regression achieved a balanced performance with a precision of 0.677, recall of 0.559, and an overall accuracy of 0.751, an F1-score of 0.613. The decision tree model, although simple and interpretable, showed the weakest performance across metrics (precision = 0.570, recall = 0.616, accuracy = 0.701, F1 = 0.592), suggesting limited generalization capacity. More advanced classifiers demonstrated modest improvements. SVM achieved a precision of 0.693, recall of 0.635, accuracy of 0.772, and the highest F1-score among the baseline models (0.663), highlighting its advantage in handling non-linear decision boundaries. Similarly, Random Forest exhibited competitive performance, achieving the highest accuracy (0.775) and precision (0.719), though its recall (0.593) was slightly lower, yielding an F1-score of 0.650. This trade-off between precision and recall provides a useful benchmark for evaluating MAFLD-GPT’s sensitivity-oriented performance.

Comparison of machine-learning algorisms and MAFLD-GPT

When compared with conventional ML algorithms, MAFLD-GPT demonstrated competitive and even more predictive performance. When provided with few-shot examples (k = 3), MAFLD-GPT achieved the highest recall (0.837) across all methods, surpassing traditional ML models and highlighting its strength in identifying true positive cases. This improvement in sensitivity translated into the overall best F1-score (0.724), outperforming both ensemble methods such as random forest (0.650) and kernel-based classifiers like SVM (0.663). However, Random Forest demonstrated the highest precision (0.719), suggesting a more conservative prediction pattern. Accuracy remained comparable across approaches, with random forest (0.775) was similar to MAFLD-GPT, but the latter demonstrated a more balanced trade-off between precision and recall. Notably, for the MAFLD prediction task, the recall metric is more important than the precision metric. This is because, regarding our research goal, we care more about the sensitivity of our model in detecting MAFLD. In other words, considering MAFLD as the potential bad cases, as an assistant tool for doctors, our main goal is to reduce the amount of false negative cases because we hope to not miss any potential MAFLD patients. Collectively, these findings underscored that MAFLD-GPT not only matched the performance of established ML approaches but also provided notable advantages in high recall and F1-score, especially under few-shot learning conditions.

Robustness of MAFLD-GPT

We first assessed the robustness of MAFLD-GPT (3shot) by performing three independent runs, in which the few-shot examples were independently resampled for each round. Fleiss’s kappa was measured for overall agreement among repeated 3 runs. As shown in Table 5, Supplemental Tables 4 and Fig. 3, kappa values ranged from 0.79 to 0.95 across 1- to 5-shot examples, all with highly significant z-tests (p < 0.001). According to Landis and Koch’s benchmarks, the agreement was substantial (kappa ≈ 0.786–0.793 for 1- and 2-shot) and improved to almost perfect (kappa ≥ 0.80 for 3-, 4-, and 5-shot). Notably, the 3-shot (kappa = 0.887, 95% CI: 0.865–0.910) and 4-shot (kappa = 0.905, 95% CI: 0.884–0.926) conditions demonstrated the most stable predictions, indicating high reproducibility under these prompt configurations. We further performed pairwise Cohen’s kappa analyses between independent runs for each shot condition (Fig. 4). Consistently, kappa values demonstrated at least substantial agreement (kappa > 0.70, p < 0.001) and frequently reached almost perfect levels (kappa > 0.90). For instance, under the 3-shot setting, pairwise kappas were 0.84–0.96, suggesting that predictions from different runs were highly concordant with each other and with the reference labels. In contrast, lower kappas were observed in 1- and 2-shot comparisons, reflecting increased variability when fewer examples were provided.

Subgroup analysis

The subgroup analysis demonstrated that MAFLD-GPT performance could be varies across gender and age as shown in supplemental Fig. 2 and supplemental Fig. 3. By gender, accuracy was significantly higher in females than males (0.842 vs. 0.726, p < 0.001). In contrast, males exhibited superior recall (0.889 vs. 0.678, p < 0.05) and F1-score (0.749 vs. 0.638, p < 0.05). These results suggested that the model tended toward greater sensitivity in males while adopting a more conservative classification pattern in females. With respect to age, patients aged ≤ 50 years achieved significantly higher accuracy than those > 50 years (0.816 vs. 0.729, p < 0.01), whereas differences in recall and F1-score did not reach statistical significance.

Discussion

Recent studies have explored the application of artificial intelligence (AI) in diagnosing liver diseases, primarily focusing on machine learning models applied to structured clinical data or deep learning models for imaging data [22]. While these approaches have demonstrated promising predictive performance, they often have notable limitations. First, many models rely exclusively on structured numeric data, excluding potentially informative textual or categorical features [23]. Also, interpretability is often limited, providing clinicians with predictions but little insight into the underlying reasoning [24]. In addition, models are typically trained and evaluated on single-center datasets, reducing generalizability. Large language models (LLMs) like ChatGPT offer a potential solution to these challenges [25]. By integrating numeric and textual patient data, generating natural-language rationales, and leveraging few-shot learning, MAFLD-GPT can provide both accurate predictions and interpretable explanations, addressing key gaps in prior AI-based approaches to early MAFLD diagnosis. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to apply few-shot learning within a ChatGPT-based diagnostic framework for MAFLD and to compare its performance with traditional machine learning algorithms.

Leveraging the GPT-3.5-turbo model provided by OpenAI, we built a large language model (MAFLD-GPT) tailored to the specific domain of MAFLD based on the ChatGPT model. The MAFLD-GPT model demonstrated many distinctive advantages compared with MAFLD-ML in the early detection of MAFLD.

To be specific, firstly, MAFLD-GPT exhibited practical usability. Compared to ML methods which require extensive (thousands of) training data and time-consuming labeling processes, MAFLD-GPT only requires the natural language of MAFLD diagnose descriptions, which can be invoked by providing no training data at all, known as zero-shot learning or only providing a few (1–5 examples in our method) representative examples, known as few-shot learning. Previous work have reported that LLM’s performance steadily improves with the number of examples in the context, showing benefits from in-context few-shot learning. We scaled the number of examples in few-shot learning up to five, and the experimental results showed that 3-shot examples achieved the best performance, which is similar to the existing studies settings that LLM usually requires 3–8 examples to outperform other state-of-the-art models [14, 26].

Secondly, our results indicated that the diagnostic performance of MAFLD-GPT exceeded MAFLD-ML. While zero-shot learning showed promising performances in training tasks by leveraging prior knowledge from training resources, it remained challenging to apply MAFLD-GPT directly to unseen tasks. To overcome this challenge, few-shot learning was utilized in our work to augment the context with a few representative examples of desired inputs and outputs. Our results showed that 3-shot learning bring the largest absolute performance gain, which outperformed SVM (the best performance achieved by traditional ML algorithms) by 6.1% in F1-score. While Random Forest obtained slightly higher precision, MAFLD-GPT achieved superior recall and F1-score, confirming its potential for sensitive early screening of MAFLD. These findings suggested that incorporating few-shot examples improved overall performance. However, MAFLD-GPT could not always benefit from increasing k, which may be because too many examples can bring high-level noise to MAFLD-GPT and hinder performance improvement.

Additionally, in our study, we were intrigued to find that MAFLD-GPT showed superior performance when dealing with non-numeric features as compared to the standard dataset, highlighting its exceptional scalability. ChatGPT’s flexible input handling allows it to effectively manage missing feature values and multimodal feature samples. It can easily modify features, making it highly scalable. ML models have strict requirements for model inputs, typically handling only numerical features. They struggle with missing feature values and multimodal feature samples, such as diagnostic advice in text format, leading to poor scalability.

Fourthly, MAFLD-GPT provides interpretability of results. The prompt-based input of ChatGPT enables the large model to understand the semantic information of each feature dimension. Last but not least, MAFLD-GPT is relatively accessible, as it could be operated without specialized technical expertise, making it a potentially practical tool for broader use.

Our study had several limitations. A key limitation was that the reference standard of our study for MAFLD diagnosis was ultrasound rather than liver biopsy. Although biopsy remains the gold standard, it is invasive, costly, and impractical for large-scale population research, whereas ultrasound is widely available, routinely used in clinical and epidemiological settings, and therefore meets both ethical and practical considerations. We recognized that reliance on an imperfect standard can introduce uncertainty; to mitigate this, all methods—including MAFLD-GPT and the ML baselines—were trained and evaluated on the same ultrasound-labeled dataset to ensure a fair, like-for-like comparison. Under these identical diagnostic conditions, MAFLD-GPT consistently outperformed the baselines in 10-fold cross-validation, supporting the robustness of our approach. Besides, ChatGPT-based learning relied on a limited number of training samples (zero-shot and few-shot), which raises concerns about robustness. To address this, we randomly selected training samples three times and averaged the results, and further tested reproducibility across three different evaluation time points. Finally, although our findings demonstrated the feasibility of LLM-based approaches for MAFLD diagnosis, future studies should include multimodal validation, for example by integrating biochemical markers and biopsy-confirmed subsets where feasible, and conduct multi-center evaluations across different ethnic groups and geographic regions to strengthen generalizability and clinical credibility. We also acknowledged that more ML baselines can be incorporated in this study to evaluate their performance. However, the comparison between MAFLD-GPT and traditional ML methods illustrated how an off-the-shelf LLM with only 3 in-context examples performs relative to traditional ML trained on thousands of cases.

Beyond these methodological constraints, limitations intrinsic to large language models should also be acknowledged. ChatGPT and similar LLMs are trained primarily on general textual data rather than structured clinical datasets; consequently, they may generate contextually plausible but factually incorrect outputs (so-called “hallucinations”). In addition, their internal decision processes are opaque, limiting interpretability and accountability in medical contexts. MAFLD-GPT should therefore be viewed as a proof-of-concept rather than a deployable diagnostic system. Extensive external validation, domain-specific fine-tuning on curated clinical corpora, regulatory review, and ethical oversight will be essential before any potential clinical translation. Future work should also integrate multimodal validation, including biochemical indices and biopsy-confirmed subsets where feasible, and conduct multi-center studies across diverse populations to improve generalizability and clinical credibility.

In our study, we provided the proof-of-concept of MAFLD-GPT when showed competitive performance compared to conventional ML models, with strengths in few-shot prompting, tolerance to missing features, and model-side explanations. These findings suggested that MAFLD-GPT could potentially serve as a research tool for MAFLD risk assessment and triage in human-in-the-loop settings, although further validation and local host are required before clinical application.
