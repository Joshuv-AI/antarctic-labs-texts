Circulating immunometabolic markers associated with sarcopenia and muscle strength at high altitude: An integrated multi-omics study
Authors: Xingli Liu, Heng Li, Wanying Zuo, Gang Wang, Jianxun Jiang, Zhonglin Li, Xiao He, Wu Wang, Yan Yue, Yang Zhang, Ling Wang, Liang Lyu, Xuancheng Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Sarcopenia, Muscle strength, High altitude, Immunometabolism, Serum biomarkers, Metabolic stress

Introduction

Sarcopenia is a major age-related condition associated with reduced muscle strength, functional decline, and adverse health outcomes in aging populations [1]. With an estimated 50 million cases globally, projected to reach 500 million by 2050, sarcopenia poses a significant economic burden on aging populations [2]. Current management mainly relies on exercise and nutritional support, while effective pharmacological options remain limited [3]. A better understanding of the biological processes associated with sarcopenia is therefore needed.

The etiology of sarcopenia is multifactorial, involving neurogenic factors related to motor neuron loss, endocrine changes due to reduced hormone expression, muscle motor unit loss, and sedentary behavior-induced changes in nutrition and lifestyle [[4], [5], [6]]. While the exact pathogenesis remains unclear, research has focused on the roles of hormones, inflammation, and protein homeostasis loss in its development [7,8]. Additionally, the prevalence of sarcopenia is significantly higher in high-altitude (conventionally defined as >1500 m [9]) regions due to hypoxic effects on skeletal muscle. Our previous study suggested that altitude-related differences in muscle phenotype may exist among older adults, highlighting the need for further investigation into the molecular characteristics of sarcopenia in this setting [10].

This study performed a combined analysis of transcriptomics, proteomics and metabolomics on blood samples from middle-aged and elderly sarcopenia patients and controls in high-altitude regions. Through this multi-omics approach. We aimed to characterize circulating immunometabolic profiles associated with sarcopenia and to identify candidate circulating markers related to muscle strength in this population.

Methods

Patients

We have established a prospective sarcopenia cohort of middle-aged and elderly individuals in Kunming (2000 m), Yunnan previously [10], which has been registered with the China Clinical Trial Registration Center (No.: ChiCTR2100052153). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First People’s Hospital of Yunnan Province (Approval No.: KHLL2021-KY056) and have therefore been performed in accordance with the ethical standards laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. During the third-year follow-up of this cohort, 60 sarcopenia patients (30 males and 30 females) and 60 non-sarcopenia controls were enrolled for blood sample collection and subsequent multi-omics analysis.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Sarcopenia group: individuals aged >50 years who have resided locally for more than 10 years, with sarcopenia diagnosed according to 2019 Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia: 2019 Consensus Update on Sarcopenia Diagnosis and Treatment (AWGS2019) [1]. (2) Control group: non-sarcopenia controls were matched to the sarcopenia group by 1:1 sex matching, age (±1 year), and, where possible, matched for the prevalence of metabolic diseases (including hypertension, diabetes, and osteoporosis). Given that 70% of sarcopenia patients in this study had concurrent osteopenia or osteoporosis (n = 42), and since sarcopenia and osteoporosis often co-occur in elderly populations, the control group was further divided into osteoporosis-positive (n = 35) and osteoporosis-negative (Normal) subgroups (n = 25) for the multi-omics analysis. Exclusion criteria included: presence of metal implants; inability to move independently (e.g., hemiplegia, recent fractures, long-term bedridden); patients with tumors or hematological disorders; congenital or hereditary muscle atrophy-related diseases; acute or unresolved chronic infections, such as COVID-19 or tuberculosis; blood samples that did not meet testing standards (e.g., contamination, hemolysis).

Baseline data collected included age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, and history of metabolic disease. Additionally, information was gathered on sarcopenia-related questionnaire responses, muscle mass measurements, muscle strength, and physical function assessments. Muscle mass was evaluated using Appendicular Skeletal Muscle Mass Index (ASMI), determined through whole-body fat composition scans via Lunar iDual-energy X-Ray Absorptiometry (DXA) equipment (GE, USA). Muscle strength was assessed by measuring handgrip strength (HGS) of the dominant hand using a Jamar hydraulic dynamometer; each participant was tested twice, with the maximum HGS recorded. Physical function was evaluated using the SARC-F questionnaire (Strength, Assistance in walking, Rising from a chair, Climbing stairs, Falls) and the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), which includes balance, 4-meter gait speed, and five-time chair-stand tests. According to the AWGS2019, sarcopenia was defined as ASMI < 7.0 kg/m² for men and <5.4 kg/m² for women by DXA, along with either HGS < 28.0 kg for men and <18.0 kg for women and/or an SPPB score <9.

Blood collection

For each participant, 10 mL of whole blood was collected from the cubital vein. Of this, 5 mL was placed in EDTA anticoagulant tubes, divided into three aliquots, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for 15 min, and stored at −80 °C. The other 5 mL was centrifuged at 3000 g for 10 min at 4 °C, with the supernatant divided into three aliquots, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for 15 min, and stored at −80 °C.

Transcriptome RNA-seq analysis

Total RNA was extracted with the phenol/chloroform method, and RNA quality and quantity were assessed via an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer. A cDNA library was constructed using the M-MLV reverse transcriptase kit (Takara, Japan) and sequenced on an Illumina platform. Raw data were cleaned to obtain high-quality reads (clean data) for bioinformatics analysis (Table S1). Data processing and cleaning were performed in R, with counts data converted to FPKM for further analysis. Differential analysis used the “limma” R package. To account for multiple testing, P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate. Features with |logFC| > 0.58 and Padj < 0.05 were considered statistically significant, this FDR correction procedure was applied to each omics layer included in the differential analysis.

Weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA) was conducted to identify gene modules associated with MetS. The top 50% of genes with the highest median absolute deviation (MAD) were selected, and ineligible data were filtered. A “soft” threshold power (β) was applied to calculate adjacency for co-expression similarity. Using a topological overlap matrix (TOM) and dynamic tree cutting with a minimum module size of 100, genes were grouped into modules. Module gene dissimilarity was calculated, and average linkage hierarchical clustering was performed to visualize the results. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using the “clusterProfiler” R package, with gene sets obtained from the Msigdb website (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb). Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) data were retrieved from the STRING database (https://string-db.org) using the “STRINGdb” R package. A PPI network was constructed, where nodes represent proteins and edges indicate interactions between them.

Proteomic profiling

Total proteins were extracted using the ProteoMiner kit (BioRad, USA). Protein quality was assessed using the Bradford protein assay kit (Beyotime, CN). After protein digestion, data independent acquisition liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry was performed using the Vanquish™ Neo UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher, USA), with data acquired on an Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, USA). Protein identification, quantification, and differential protein analysis were conducted using the DIANN software, based on the homo_sapiens_uniprot_2019.01.18.fasta (169,389 sequences) protein database.

Differential analysis was conducted using the “limma” R package, with a Padj< 0.05 considered statistically significant. Proteome composition was visualized using Proteomap (https://proteomaps.net), focusing on protein functionality. Proteins were classified according to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, with functionally related proteins grouped together. Protein abundance was represented by polygon area, weighted by protein size, to highlight highly expressed proteins. The final visualization displayed protein functional enrichment.

Untargeted metabolomics profiling

100 μL of the sample was mixed with 1:4 volume of extraction solvent, sonicated for 10 min in an ice water bath, and incubated at −40 °C for 1 h. The sample was then centrifuged at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for analysis. Target compounds were separated using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system (Nexera HPLC, Japan), followed by primary and secondary mass spectrometry data acquisition using a high-resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). After data acquisition, ProteoWizard software converted the raw data to mzXML format. Peak recognition, extraction, alignment, and integration were performed using an in-house R package (based on XCMS). Compound annotation was performed by matching with the BiotreeDB (V2.1) secondary mass spectrometry database, with a Cutoff score of 0.3. Differential analysis of the metabolomics data was carried out using the “limma” R package, with Padj < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serum levels of human CD14 (JL20037, Jonlnbio, China) and Leucine-rich alpha-2-glycoprotein (LRG1) (E-EL-H6277, Elabscience, China) were quantified using specific commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturers' protocols. The optical density of each well was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Measurement of serum Fc-gamma receptor II-a (FCGR2A) concentration was not performed due to the unavailability of the commercial assay kit.

Cell culture and treatments

C2C12 cells were purchased from Procell (CL-0044, China). They were cultured in DMEM with 1% penicillin/streptomycin and 10% fetal bovine serum (BI, USA) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. When the density of cells reached 80%–90%, the cells were switched to DMEM-high glucose with 2% horse serum (Gibco, USA) for differentiation. The Myotubes were assessed at 72, 96, and 120 h after differentiation to determine the optimal time point. Cells were treated with D-(+)-Galactose (D-gal) (Servicebio, China) at concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 g/L during differentiation, and senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining kit (Servicebio, China) was performed after myotube formation to identify the optimal D-gal concentration. Subsequently, cells were divided into two groups: the sarcopenia model group was treated with D-gal during differentiation, and the negative control (NC) group was treated with DMSO.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from C2C12 cells using the standard TRIzol method according to the manufacturer's instructions. M-MLV RTase Assay Kit (Takara, Japan) was used to transcribe the RNA to cDNA reversely. qRT-PCR was carried out using a SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ Kit (Takara, Japan) on ABI Prism 7900 real-time PCR System (ThermoFisher, USA), The primers are listed in Table S2. The relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the ΔΔCt method, which were normalized to GAPDH expression.

Western blot (WB)

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in tris-buffered saline containing 0.05% Tween-20 (TBST) for 2 h at room temperature, followed by primary antibody incubations overnight at 4 °C, including β-actin, CD14, FCGR2A, and LRG1, the details of the antibodies are shown in the Table S3. After three washes with TBST, the membranes were then incubated with the horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h. After three washes with TBST, an enhanced ECL kit (Meilunbio, China) was used for detection.

Animals

Male senescence-accelerated mice prone-8 (SAMP8) and SAM resistant-1 (SAMR1) mice (n = 6 per group), aged 10–12 weeks, were purchased from Wesun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Mice were fed a standard diet for over 7 months. All experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Commitee of Kunming University of Science and Technology (No. KUST2025086) and followed the guidelines provided by the National Institutes of Health. Mice were euthanized at 8 months.

Histological analysis

Gastrocnemius (GA) and tibialis anterior (TA) muscles from mice were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Transverse sections (5 μm thick) were obtained at the maximum cross-section of the muscles and embedded in paraffin, followed by H&E (Servicebio, China) and immunofluorescence staining.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized and hydrated with xylene and gradient concentration of ethanol (100%, 95%, 85%, and 70%). Then, antigen retrieval was achieved by boiling the sections in 10 mM sodium citrate for 10 min in a microwave oven. The sections werethen washed with PBS three times and treated with 3% H2O2-methanol for 15 min. Immunostaining was performed by incubation with antibody against LAMA1 (1:100) (PC4973S, abmart, China), FCGR2A (1:100) (15625-1-AP, Proteintech, China), CD14 (1:100) (GB11254, Servicebio, China), LRG1 (1:100) (13224-1-AP, Proteintech, China) for 2 h. The sections were subsequently incubated with fluorescently-labeled secondary antibody for 50 min at room temperature, DAPI was added to counterstain nuclei. After 3 PBS washed, an autofluorescence quencher was applied for 5 min. Sections were mounted with anti - fade medium and imaged by a scanner.

Statistical analysis

Baseline data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics 27.0 (IBM, USA). Categorical data were expressed as percentages and compared between groups using the chi-squared test. Continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), with comparisons between two-groups performed using the t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, and two-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Correlations analysis were assessed using Pearson’s test for normally distributed data and Spearman’s rank test for non-normally distributed data, as appropriate. Bioinformatics analyses and data visualization were conducted using R 4.3.1 (R Foundation, Austria). Group comparisons were assessed using t-tests or rank-sum tests for two groups, and the Kruskal-Wallis test for multiple groups. Correlation analyses employed Pearson correlation and Mantel tests. The gray values of staining and WB bands were quantified using ImageJ (v1.53k, NIH, USA). The cross-sectional area (CSA) of individual muscle fibers at the maximal muscle cross-section was also measured with ImageJ, and the mean fiber CSA was calculated as the total summed area of all measured fibers divided by the fiber count. All experimental data were analyzed and visualized with GraphPad Prism (v9.3.0, GraphPad Software, USA). A threshold of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. There were no significant differences between the sarcopenia group and the two control groups in age, height, history of drinking or tea consumption, hypertension, diabetes, or surgical history (P > 0.05). Patients in the sarcopenia group had significantly lower weight, BMI, and waist circumference than those in both control groups, and their ASMI was significantly lower than that in the positive control group and among female participants in the negative control group (P < 0.05). Although HGS was lower in both men and women in the sarcopenia group, a statistically significant reduction was observed only in women (P < 0.05). Additionally, fracture history and smoking history showed significant differences between the sarcopenia group and the negative control groups (P < 0.05). However, no significant differences were noted in SARC-F or SPPB scores among the groups.

Transcriptomics profiling

Transcriptomic analysis revealed that, compared to the positive control group, the sarcopenia group exhibited 93 upregulated and 75 downregulated genes; relative to the negative control group, there were 20 upregulated and 184 downregulated genes (Fig. S1a-b). GSEA enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes indicated that, compared to the positive control group, sarcopenia patients showed evidence of chronic inflammation, with activation of the IL6-JAK-STAT3 and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)α- nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathways, while muscle-associated signaling pathways, including the cytoskeleton in muscle cells and motor proteins, were suppressed (Fig. S1c). Compared to the negative control group, sarcopenia patients exhibited widespread inhibition of inflammation-related pathways, such as the NF-κB and IL2-STAT5 pathways, as well as autophagy, along with metabolic pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis (Fig. S1d). Additionally, the TGF-β pathway and muscle-associated signaling pathways, including the cytoskeleton in muscle cells and motor proteins, were suppressed in sarcopenia patients (Fig. S2a-b).

Using WGCNA analysis across the three groups to identify gene modules, we found that the brown module was most significantly associated with both osteoporosis and sarcopenia groups (Fig. 1a). This module was selected for further analysis. KEGG enrichment of genes within this module highlighted enrichment in pathways such as NF-κB signaling and cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, while Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed associations with cytokine receptors, Toll-like receptor (TLR) binding, and immune-related functions (Fig. 1b-c). We intersected genes from the WGCNA-associated module with differentially expressed genes for sarcopenia and osteoporosis, identifying a set of 56 genes. These genes were then analyzed using a PPI network, resulting in an interaction network of 119 proteins (Fig. 1d). KEGG enrichment analysis of the PPI network genes revealed associations with pathways including NF-κB signaling, TLR signaling, cytoskeleton regulation in muscle cells, and apoptosis. GO enrichment analysis also indicated relevance to immune functions, such as cytokine and chemokine binding (Fig. 1e-f).

Proteomics profiling

Analysis of differentially expressed proteins showed that, compared to the positive control group, the sarcopenia group had 141 upregulated and 47 downregulated proteins, while compared to the negative control group, there were 160 upregulated and 31 downregulated proteins (Fig. 2a-b). Enrichment analysis of these differentially expressed proteins using the ProteoMap website revealed that, relative to the positive control, sarcopenia primarily enriched pathways such as the p38 mitogen activated protein kinase (MAPK), the phosphatidylinositide 3-kinases (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt) pathway, oxidative phosphorylation, and lipid and steroid metabolism (Fig. 2c). Compared to the normal group, enrichment was observed in MAPK, NF-κB, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR), and oxidative phosphorylation pathways (Fig. 2d). Subsequently, intersecting differentially expressed proteins with PPI network proteins identified three key proteins: P12318 (FCGR2A), P08571 (CD14) and P02750 (LRG1) (Fig. 2e-f). All three were significantly upregulated in the sarcopenia group, and their corresponding genes—FCGR2A, CD14 and LRG1—also showed significant elevation in sarcopenia patients (P < 0.05).

Untargeted metabolomics profiling

Under differential metabolite analysis, sarcopenia showed 40 upregulated and 36 downregulated metabolites compared to the positive control, and 26 upregulated and 40 downregulated metabolites compared to the negative control, primarily including sphingolipids (SP), glycerophospholipids (GP), amino acids, fatty acyls, glycerolipids, aromatic compounds, sterol lipids, glucosylsphingoshine and their derivatives (Fig. S3a-b). Among these, three metabolites—phinganine, phytosphingosine, and arginyl-alanine—exhibited consistent trends across both groups. We then conducted a correlation analysis between these differential metabolites and the three key genes identified previously. The analysis revealed significant correlations: the metabolite most strongly correlated with LRG1 was glycerol 1-(5-hydroxydodecanoate), a glycerolipid (Fig. S3c); with FCGR2A, it was ACar(10:1), classified as choline acetylcarnitine (Fig. S3d); and with CD14, it was phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) (10:0/26:4), a GP (Fig. S3e). Further details are provided in Table S4-6.

Biomarkers were upregulated in clinical and experimental Sarcopenia

Serum concentrations of CD14 and LRG1 were measured in all patients using ELISA. FCGR2A was not assessed due to the lack of a suitable assay kit. Correlation analysis between serum biomarker levels and clinical characteristics of patients with sarcopenia and controls is summarized in Table S7. CD14 serum concentration showed a weak positive correlation with ASMI in male patients (rs = 0.337), a weak negative correlation with waistline in female patients (r=-0.377), and a weak positive correlation with HGS in males (rs = 0.452). However, only the association with HGS in males remained statistically significant (P < 0.05). LRG1 level was weakly positively correlated with HGS in male patients (r = 0.45), and this correlation was statistically significant (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3a). Comparative analysis of serum biomarker levels between sarcopenia patients and sex-matched controls revealed that both LRG1 and CD14 concentrations were elevated in male and female patient groups compared to controls (P < 0.05). Subsequent Tukey’s multiple comparisons test showed no statistically significant intergroup differences, indicating a lack of interaction effect between sex and the significant upregulation of the two (Fig. 3b-c).

A cellular model of sarcopenia was established by inducing myotube differentiation in C2C12 cells treated with D-gal and horse serum. SA-β-gal staining indicated that treatment with 20 g/L D-gal for 120 h yielded the optimal myotube senescence phenotype (Fig. 3d), which was subsequently used as the sarcopenia model. Expression validation of biomarkers in the cellular model showed that mRNA levels of FCGR2A, CD14, and LRG1 were significantly elevated in the sarcopenia group compared with NC (P < 0.05) by qRT‑PCR (Fig. 3e). However, WB analysis revealed that only LRG1 protein expression was significantly upregulated in the sarcopenia group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3f). To further verify differences in biomarker expression, an in vivo sarcopenia experiment was conducted using 8-month-old SAMP8 and SAMR1 mice. H&E staining confirmed that the CSA of GA and TA muscles was significantly reduced in SAMP8 mice compared with the SAMR1 group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4a). Meanwhile, IF staining showed that LAMA1 expression was significantly downregulated in both muscles of SAMP8 mice (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4b–c). Subsequent IF staining of biomarkers indicated that the expression levels of CD14, FCGR2A, and LRG1 were significantly increased in the GA and TA muscles of the SAMP8 group compared with the SAMR1 controls (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4b–c), consistent with our bioinformatics analysis.

Discussion

In this study, integrated multi-omics analysis showed that sarcopenia in middle-aged and older adults living at high altitude was characterized by distinct circulating immunometabolic profiles. Transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic findings consistently indicated dysregulation of inflammation-, immunity-, and metabolism-related pathways. Among the identified molecules, LRG1, FCGR2A, and CD14 emerged as key markers associated with sarcopenia, while CD14 and LRG1 were also associated with muscle strength. These findings broaden current understanding of the molecular features of sarcopenia in high-altitude populations.

Sarcopenia involves multiple molecular mechanisms that disrupt muscle physiology, with hormone-regulated and inflammation-induced imbalances in protein homeostasis and mitochondrial dysfunction playing crucial roles in muscle atrophy [8,11,12]. Skeletal muscle, the body’s primary amino acid reservoir, maintains a critical balance between protein synthesis and degradation [13]. Protein degradation in muscle is primarily through the MAPK and NF-κB pathways. MAPK activation induces the E3 ligases muscle RING-finger protein-1 (MuRF-1) and muscle atrophy F-Box (MAFbx), which promote proteolysis. Similarly, NF-κB signaling—activated by inflammation or oxidative stress—upregulates MuRF-1 and MAFbx [14]. NF-κB also binds to the myostatin promoter, enhancing the expression of this potent negative regulator of muscle mass, which further amplifies protein breakdown through cross-talk with multiple signaling pathways [[15], [16], [17]]. Our Transcriptomics profiling revealed activation of inflammatory pathways (TNFα-NF-κB) and suppression of muscle structure-related pathways compared to controls. Correspondingly, proteomics profiling identified enrichment of differential proteins in PPAR, MAPK, NF-κB, PI3K-Akt, and cytokine–receptor interaction pathways. WGCNA and PPI analyses further connected these changes to NF-κB signaling, TLR activity, and cytoskeletal pathways, consistent with previously reported mechanisms of muscle atrophy. Together, our multi-omics results suggest that sarcopenia progression is closely linked to coordinated inflammatory activation and structural impairment in muscle, likely driving protein homeostasis disruption through pathways such as NF-κB and MAPK. In the context of previous low-altitude studies, our high-altitude immunometabolic findings appear to both overlap with and extend reported molecular features of sarcopenia. Proteomic studies of low-altitude (30 m) sarcopenia have primarily implicated focal adhesion, mitochondrial dysfunction, apoptosis, regeneration, and antioxidant responses, particularly reduced mitochondrial energy metabolism proteins [18]. A large three-country cohort (<340 m) further reported suppressed mitochondrial proteostasis and PGC-1α/ERRα signaling, leading to impaired respiratory complex activity, reduced mitochondrial abundance, and muscle NAD+ depletion [19]. Similarly, a recent multi-omics study of osteosarcopenia in Fuzhou (80 m) identified MYH7 and NCAM1 and highlighted redox balance, cellular metabolism, immune responses, osteoclast differentiation, and NF-κB signaling [20]. The overlap between these low-altitude findings and our results, especially regarding immune-inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB signaling and osteoclast differentiation, suggests possible shared molecular features of sarcopenia. Nevertheless, without a matched low-altitude control group, these comparisons remain literature-based and hypothesis-generating.

High-altitude exposure is a recognized risk factor for sarcopenia. Recent large cohort study indicated that long-term residence above 1500 m accelerates biological aging and increases the risk of age-related conditions include sarcopenia and low ASMI [21]. A separate Chinese multi-ethnic cohort further linked high-altitude exposure to reduced bone mineral density, especially in older adults [22]. This is largely due to hypobaric hypoxia-induced oxidative stress, which disrupts cellular signaling, and promotes protein damage, thereby contributing to accelerated aging [23]. Through multi-omics profiling, we identified three upregulated proteins with substantial research potential. Serum protein quantification confirmed aberrantly elevated levels of LRG1 and CD14 in sarcopenia patients. A significant positive correlation was observed between both biomarkers and HGS in males, suggesting their potential specificity for reflecting sarcopenia severity rather than general systemic inflammation. However, the lack of a significant sex interaction further suggests a consistent elevation pattern across genders. The observed correlation patterns further suggest that these candidates may reflect immune-metabolic profiles associated with disease status. However, the modest strength of some associations indicates that their biomarker role remains preliminary. Cellular experiments verified the upregulation of LRG1, FCGR2A, and CD14 at the gene level and of LRG1 at the protein level. In vivo, 8-month-old SAMP8 model mice exhibited significant reductions in muscle mass and impaired myofiber stability, consistent with previous reports [24,25]. These results collectively validate the marked upregulation of these proteins in sarcopenia.

LRG1, a leucine-rich repeat protein, is implicated in angiogenesis [26] and has been associated with various conditions including cancer, aging [27], and metabolic disorders [28]. Although its role in sarcopenia remains underexplored, emerging evidence indicates that LRG1 predominantly functions through the TGF-β pathway [29]. Upon LRG1 induction, TGF-β signaling activates upstream mediators such as RhoA, Ras, MKKs, MEKs, and TAK1, which in turn stimulate the MAPK and PI3K-Akt cascades [28,30]. Based on our findings, elevated LRG1 may disrupt skeletal muscle protein homeostasis and promote inflammation and apoptosis in sarcopenia via TGF-β–mediated activation of MAPK and PI3K-Akt signaling, warranting further investigation. FCGR2A, an activating receptor within the Fcγ receptor family, is widely expressed on immune cells. It recognizes the Fc region of immunoglobulins to mediate phagocytosis and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, playing a significant role in autoimmune diseases and immunodeficiencies [31]. FCGR2A activation promotes the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and mediators, underscoring its importance in inflammatory responses [32]. The upregulation of FCGR2A observed in our study may therefore contribute to the inflammatory pathology associated with sarcopenia. CD14 functions as a co-receptor for TLR-4 and facilitates pro-inflammatory responses by binding bacterial lipopolysaccharide to activate TLR-4 signaling [33]. Mechanistically, CD14 mediates TLR-4 signaling through two distinct pathways: it promotes pro-inflammatory cytokine production via the MyD88-dependent pathway, and induces type I interferon expression through the TRIF-dependent pathway. Both pathways converge on the activation of NF-κB and interferon regulatory factors, leading to enhanced transcription of inflammatory mediators [34,35]. Our results align with these findings. While the cooperative role of these three proteins in metabolic diseases remains unexplored, a biomarker panel comprising apolipoprotein A-IV, CD14, and LRG1 has been reported to aid in distinguishing diabetes from early pancreatic cancer [36]. Our findings suggest that CD14 and LRG1 may interact in sarcopenia, potentially reducing muscle protein synthesis through mechanisms involving insulin resistance. Moreover, as all three proteins participate in pro-inflammatory signaling, they may collectively contribute to the progression of sarcopenia via inflammatory pathways—a possibility that warrants further investigation.

SP, bioactive lipids that influence mitochondrial function and protein homeostasis, contribute to muscle aging [37]. GP and phospholipids are similarly implicated in age-related skeletal muscle decline, with specific sphingomyelins, phosphatidylcholine (PC), and phosphatidylinositol (PI) identified as potential sarcopenia biomarkers [38]. In line with this, our study found sphinganine and phytosphingosine significantly elevated in sarcopenia, highlighting their promise as novel biomarkers. Altered PI levels in our results align with reported deficiencies in the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in sarcopenia, which can disrupt PI metabolism [39]. Further differential metabolites were linked to amino acid metabolism, inflammation, oxidative stress, and lipid metabolism. In line with our findings, a metabolomic study of sarcopenia patients in Taipei (30 m) identified metabolites including PI 32:1 and PI 34:3 as potential targetable biomarkers. These metabolites are linked to the PI3K/AKT/mTOR and ERK/MAPK pathways, as well as amino acid metabolism, molecular transport, inflammatory response, free radical scavenging, and lipid metabolism [40]. Moving a step further, a study of male sarcopenia in Seoul (80 m) found that ceramides could reflect low muscle mass and have harmful cellular effects on myogenesis and cell viability, indicating that SP accumulation plays a direct role in the etiology of sarcopenia, acting as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target [41]. Multi-omics integration identified strong correlations between the three upregulated genes and specific metabolites, most prominently for CD14. CD14 is known to recognize externalized PI phosphates to clear apoptotic cells, maintaining tissue homeostasis [34] and to bind oxidized phospholipids, promoting endocytosis and inflammasome-driven inflammation [42]. The significant correlation between CD14 and specific PC/PE species in our data suggests it may influence sarcopenia progression through these lipid-mediated inflammatory and clearance pathways.

Although our findings highlight inflammatory and immunity-related pathways, sarcopenia is a multifactorial process that also involves impaired myoblast differentiation, satellite cell dysfunction, reduced regenerative capacity, and microenvironmental remodeling. The lineage plasticity and non-myogenic trans-differentiation of muscle-resident progenitors may contribute to adipogenesis, fibrosis, and impaired muscle regeneration [43]. Moreover, muscle repair depends on coordinated vascular integration and extracellular vesicle-mediated communication among myogenic, immune, endothelial, and stromal cells, which may influence inflammation, metabolic adaptation, angiogenesis, and regenerative signaling [44,45]. Thus, our results should be interpreted within this broader regenerative and tissue-remodeling context rather than as evidence of inflammation-driven sarcopenia alone. From a translational perspective, effective interventions may require multi-component strategies integrating inflammation control with tissue remodeling and functional restoration [46]. Then, the D-gal-induced C2C12 model, while useful for studying oxidative stress and senescence-like changes in myogenic cells, cannot fully recapitulate human sarcopenia because it lacks immune, vascular, neural, stromal, endocrine, mechanical, and systemic metabolic components. In addition, This study was conducted at a single high-altitude location without a low-altitude comparator, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting sarcopenia in this high-altitude population rather than altitude-specific or high-altitude-induced sarcopenia. Therefore, these findings should be regarded as exploratory mechanistic evidence and require further validation in primary human muscle cells, co-culture or organoid systems, animal models, and independent clinical cohorts.

Conclusions

In summary, this study provides an integrated multi-omics analysis of sarcopenia in middle-aged and older adults living at high altitude. LRG1, FCGR2A, and CD14 were identified as key molecules associated with circulating immunometabolic profiles in sarcopenia, and CD14 was additionally linked to metabolic pathways. These findings provide exploratory evidence on the molecular characteristics of sarcopenia in a high-altitude population and highlight potential candidate circulating markers that warrant further validation in larger cohorts.
