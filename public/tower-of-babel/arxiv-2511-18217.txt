Open problems on Steiner trees and maximal distance minimizers
Yana Teplitskaya

Abstract
In this work, I collect and discuss a series of open questions in one-dimensional geometric optimization in Euclidean spaces. The focus is on two classes of problems: maximal distance minimizers and Steiner trees. Maximal distance minimizers concern finding a connected set of minimal length whose closed r r -neighborhood covers a given compact set, whereas Steiner trees aim to find a minimal-length set connecting a prescribed set of points. For both problems, I briefly summarize known results and highlight the remaining open questions. While some questions can be approached with elementary methods, others remain highly challenging.

2
Steiner trees

For a given compact set
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
find a closed set
{\mathcal{S}t}
with minimal length (one-dimensional Hausdorff measure
{\mathcal{H}}^{1}
) such that
{\mathcal{S}t}\cup\mathcal{A}
is connected.
We will call such a solution
{\mathcal{S}t}

Steiner set
. If
{\mathcal{S}t}
is connected (which is the case if
\mathcal{A}
is finite or totally disconnected) we call it Steiner tree. In 2013, Paolini and Stepanov showed the existence of a solution, see
[
26
]
. If a solution
{\mathcal{S}t}
has finite length, then it is compact, has no loops, and consists of countably many connected components.
The edges of the locally finite graph
{\mathcal{S}t}\setminus\mathcal{A}
are straight line segments.
The maximal degree (in graph-theoretic sense) of a vertex is at most
3
. Moreover, only terminals can have degree
1
or
2
, all the other vertices have degree
3
and are called
Steiner
(branching) points. The angle between any two adjacent edges of a
{\mathcal{S}t}
is at least
2\pi/3
, in particular
{\mathcal{S}t}
in a neighborhood of a branching point is a regular tripod (all three angles are equal to
2\pi/3
) and hence are coplanar. If every terminal has degree 1 then
{\mathcal{S}t}
is called
full
.

2.1
Uniqueness and existence

Question 2.1
.

Does there exist a finite set of terminals in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 3
with two different Steiner trees
{\mathcal{S}t}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{S}t}_{2}
such that the directions of all segments incident to terminals are the same (in other words trees coincide in a neighborhood of terminals)?

In the plane K. Oblakov has proved that it is impossible
[
23
]
.

Question 2.2
.

Is a solution to the Steiner tree problem unique for a general input?

We
[
1
]
provided the positive answer for finite planar input.
Moreover we proved that in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
the set of
n
-point ambiguous configurations has the
Hausdorff dimension at most
2n-1
(where a configuration is called
ambiguous
if there are several Steiner trees for it). This particularly means that
n
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of ambiguous
n
-point configurations is
0
, id est a general planar
n
-input has a unique Steiner tree.

Question 2.3
.

Is it possible to find a bounded set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that the problem:
“find
\mathcal{S}
such that
{\mathcal{H}}^{1}(\mathcal{S})
is minimal among all sets for which
\mathcal{S}\cup A
is connected”
does not have a solution?

This question was asked by E. Paolini, who is interested whether the assumption that
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is closed is necessary for the problem to be solvable.

2.2
Gilbert–Pollak conjecture on the Steiner ratio

A Minimum spanning tree (MST)
is a minimal connection of a given set
A
of points in the plane by segments with endpoints in
A
.

The Steiner ratio
is the infimum of the ratio of the total length of Steiner tree to the minimum spanning tree for a finite set of points in the Euclidean plane.

Question 2.4
.

What is the Steiner ratio in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
?

This is the most well-known open problem on Steiner tree, so called
Gilbert–Pollak conjecture on Steiner ratio
, id est whether the planar Steiner ratio is reached on an equilateral triangle (and so is equal to
\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}=0.866\ldots
). This conjecture is still open (as of 2025 year), see
[
21
]
. Graham
[
19
]
offered 1000$ for the proof (in his work, the reader can find the main information about the problem and attempts to solve it). The best proven bound is about
0.842
(1985,
[
11
]
).

In higher dimensions the problem becomes even more difficult (but cheaper): 500$ prize is offered for a conjecture in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, higher dimensions are priceless.

As Ronald L. Graham and Frank K. Hwang showed in 1976 in any Euclidean space, the Steiner ratio is at most
\sqrt{3}
, see
[
20
]
.
It is also known that for any metric space, the Steiner ratio is at most two.

The ratio for the vertices of a regular simplex, including the tetrahedron, is better than the planar value, but it is not optimal. D. Smith and J. MacGregor Smith constructed in
[
28
]
so called
d
-sausage configurations which provide the best known and conjecturally optimal value of the Steiner ratio in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
. For
d=3
this value is approximately
0.784
.

A natural way to investigate the connection is to consider the case of a finite set

2.5
Large number of terminals and asymptotical behavior of Steiner trees

Random geometric structures have been extensively studied
[
30
]

[
27
]
. However, to the best of my knowledge, the Steiner tree problem (STP) has not yet been explored in a random setting. The closest related topics that have been investigated include minimum spanning trees
[
16
]
, the rectilinear STP
[
4
]
and the traveling salesperson problem
[
3
]

[
14
]
. It is known that the asymptotics of the expected length of a solution for
N

\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
\beta(d)N^{\frac{d-1}{d}}
and the constants
\beta(d)
are distinct for different problems.

Question 2.9
.

Does the length of a Steiner tree for a random
N
-point input in
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
have asymptotics
\beta N^{\frac{d-1}{d}}
, where
\beta
is a constant depending on
\Omega
and the random model?

It seems highly ambitious to expect a complete answer to Question
2.9
with an explicit
\beta
. Firstly, corresponding results are not yet known even for the better-studied problems mentioned earlier. Secondly, there is still a lack of comprehensive understanding of the Steiner tree problem for
d>2
. Therefore, one may focus on establishing strong upper and lower bounds on the length of a random Steiner tree.

Question 2.10
.

Can one obtain the bounds of form
\beta_{1}N^{\frac{d-1}{d}}\leq\mathbb{E}\mathcal{H}^{1}({\mathcal{S}t})\leq\beta_{2}N^{\frac{d-1}{d}}
that distinguish the Steiner tree problem from the other mentioned problems (the minimum spanning tree, the rectilinear Steiner tree and the traveling salesperson problems)? What are good upper and lower bounds for the Steiner ratio in a random setup?

The next question is the result of personal conversations between G. Buttazzo, D. Cherkashin and E. Paolini.

Consider a planar connected compact set
\Omega
.
Let
L_{\Omega}(n)
be the maximal possible length of a Steiner tree for
n
-point set
\mathcal{A}\subset\Omega
.
It is well-known that
L_{\Omega}(n)
grows like

. Although this is not written explicitly in the literature, standard methods should imply
the existence of limit

l=\lim\sqrt{\frac{L_{\Omega}(n)}{\mathcal{H}^{2}(\Omega)}}.

The (asymptotically) worst known set
[
10

12
]
is obtained by the intersection of the hexagonal lattice with
\Omega

l\geq\left(\frac{4}{3}\right)^{1/4}=1.07\dots.

This bound seems to be sharp.

To prove the reverse inequality E. Paolini suggested to prove that one can always connect
n
points in a rectangle

\sqrt{3}\times(n-1)
by a set of length at most
\sqrt{3}(n-1)+o(1)
.
This bound is assumed to be achieved on the set of points with coordinates
(2i,0)
and
(2i+1,\sqrt{3})

Therefore we arrive at the following question.

Question 2.11
.

Is it true that one can always connect
n
points in a rectangle

\sqrt{3}\times(n-1)
by a set of length at most
\sqrt{3}(n-1)+o(1)

The same problems are also open for minimum spanning trees instead of Steiner trees. The extremal sets are conjectured to be the same. The asymptotic ratio of these extremal sets for Steiner and minimum spanning trees is
\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
which coincides with the one in Gilbert–Pollak conjecture.

The last question of this paragraph was stated in our work
[
6
]
.

Question 2.12
.

It is known, see
[
17
]
, that the total number of full Steiner topologies (the underlying abstract graphs) with
n

\frac{(2n-4)!}{2^{n-2}(n-2)!}.

How many of them may simultaneously give a shortest solution to the Steiner problem?

In all known examples the number is at most exponential in
n
while the number of topologies has a factorial order of growth. Note that the answer may depend heavily on the dimension.
