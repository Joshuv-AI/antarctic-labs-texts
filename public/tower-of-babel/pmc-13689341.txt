Very early onset inflammatory bowel disease in Jordan: a single-center case series of clinical, genetic, and therapeutic features
Authors: Eyad Altamimi, Hala Almomani, Ayah Alsmadi, Nicole Issi, Maria Shurman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVery early onset inflammatory bowel disease (VEOIBD), defined as onset before six years of age, is increasingly recognized as a distinct entity often linked to monogenic etiologies. Data from the Middle East remain limited.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective case series of ten pediatric patients diagnosed with VEOIBD at King Abdullah University Hospital, Jordan, between July 2017 and July 2024. Clinical, endoscopic, histopathologic, genetic, treatment, and outcome data were extracted and analyzed descriptively.ResultsThe cohort comprised ten patients (six males, four females). Median age at symptom onset was 12 months (range 3–60), with median diagnosis at 34 months (range 10–72). Diarrhea (100%), failure to thrive (60%), and recurrent infections (40%) were predominant presenting features. Endoscopy revealed mucosal ulceration in seven patients (70%), with non-caseating granulomas in three (30%) and focal active colitis in three (30%). Genetic testing (n = 8) identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in six patients and variants of uncertain significance (VUS) in two; the genes implicated were MEFV (n = 3), CYBB (n = 2; both VUS), G6PC3, GUCY2C, NPC1, NOD2, RIPK1, and LRBA. Three patients carried variants in two genes. In one pair — a homozygous pathogenic NPC1 frameshift variant with a heterozygous NOD2 VUS — a composite digenic mechanism has been proposed as a candidate contributor to the unusually severe VEOIBD phenotype [21]. Therapies included infliximab (n = 4), mesalazine, azathioprine, budesonide, intravenous immunoglobulin, elemental diet, and colchicine. Three patients were referred for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT); one died of infection-related multi-organ failure. The patient with a homozygous LRBA VUS lacked access to targeted therapy with abatacept or sirolimus.ConclusionsVEOIBD in Jordan is characterized by early presentation, genetic heterogeneity including co-occurring variants of uncertain significance, and poor response to conventional therapy. Limited access to targeted agents and HSCT significantly impacts outcomes. Early genomic testing, multidisciplinary management, and improved therapeutic access are essential for better prognosis.Keywords: Very early onset inflammatory bowel disease, Pathogenic variants, Consanguinity, MEFV, NOD2, NPC1, Digenic, Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation

Background

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), encompassing Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, is a chronic, relapsing-remitting inflammatory disorder of the gastrointestinal tract. Its pathogenesis is multifactorial, arising from a complex interplay between genetic susceptibility, environmental triggers, and gut microbiome dysbiosis. A distinct subset of IBD, however, challenges this conventional paradigm. Very early onset inflammatory bowel disease (VEOIBD), defined by disease onset before six years of age, is increasingly recognized as a unique entity that often exhibits a more severe phenotype and a higher likelihood of an underlying monogenic defect [1, 2]. The global incidence of VEOIBD is rising, with some studies reporting annual increases of up to 7%, underscoring its growing clinical relevance [3].

Unlike IBD in older children and adults, which is typically polygenic, VEOIBD is frequently driven by high-penetrance single-gene pathogenic variants [4]. These variants often produce inborn errors of immunity (IEI) that disrupt critical pathways governing intestinal epithelial barrier integrity, immune regulation, and microbial clearance [5, 6]. Over 70 monogenic defects have been causally linked to VEOIBD to date, including defects in interleukin-10 (IL-10) signaling (IL10RA, IL10RB, IL10), phagocyte oxidative burst (CYBB, CYBA, NCF1, NCF2), regulatory T-cell function (FOXP3, LRBA, CTLA4), and epithelial barrier maintenance [7]. Clinical presentations range from isolated intestinal inflammation to complex multisystem syndromes [3, 8]. In addition to these established monogenic causes, rare composite digenic mechanisms — whereby pathogenic or potentially pathogenic variants in two distinct genes act synergistically to aggravate disease severity — have been proposed as an underrecognized contributor to atypical, treatment-refractory VEOIBD phenotypes [9].

Management of VEOIBD presents unique challenges. Conventional therapies — corticosteroids, immunomodulators, and anti-TNF biologics — often show limited efficacy or high primary non-response in this population [10, 11]. Genetic diagnosis can directly inform targeted therapies, such as abatacept for CTLA-4 or LRBA deficiency, or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) for severe immunodeficiencies including IL-10 receptor deficiency and chronic granulomatous disease (CGD) [12, 13].

Data on VEOIBD from the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region remain sparse. Populations in this region exhibit high rates of consanguineous marriage, which increases the prevalence of autosomal recessive monogenic disorders [14]. This study characterizes the clinical, genetic, and therapeutic features of VEOIBD in a Jordanian single-center cohort.

Methods

Study design and setting

This is a descriptive single-center retrospective case series. All children diagnosed with VEOIBD and followed at the Department of Pediatric Gastroenterology, King Abdullah University Hospital (KAUH), Irbid, Jordan — a tertiary referral center serving northern Jordan — were reviewed. The study period spanned 1 July 2017 to 31 July 2024.

Patient selection and inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were: (1) age at IBD diagnosis less than six years; (2) confirmed IBD diagnosis based on the Porto criteria and ESPGHAN guidelines [15]; and (3) at least one documented follow-up visit at KAUH. All enrolled patients fulfilled the Porto/ESPGHAN endoscopic and histopathologic criteria for IBD.

Genetic testing and variant interpretation

Genomic DNA extracted from EDTA-anticoagulated peripheral blood underwent exome capture using the xGen Exome Research Panel v2 (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA, USA) and sequencing on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), generating 10,269,855,782 bases aligned to the GRCh37 reference genome and the revised Cambridge Reference Sequence (rCRS) for the mitochondrial genome, with 155.96× mean depth-of-coverage across the captured region (approximately 99.3% of the RefSeq protein-coding sequence) and ≥ 20× coverage at 99.00% of targeted bases, consistent with high-quality whole exome sequencing data. Variant calling identified 69,937 single nucleotide variants and 11,511 small insertions/deletions; data analysis and interpretation were performed using the proprietary EVIDENCE v5.5 platform (3 billion Inc.), which integrates a GATK v4.4.0 best-practices pipeline for SNV/INDEL detection, Manta v1.6.0 and an internally developed tool (3bCNV v2.1) for copy number variant and aneuploidy detection, Mutect2 v4.4.0 for mitochondrial heteroplasmic variant calling, ExpansionHunter v5.0.0 for repeat expansion detection, MELT v2.2.2 for mobile element insertion detection, and AutoMap v1.3 for detection of regions of homozygosity, with variant annotation performed using Variant Effect Predictor v112.0 (Ensembl). Variants were classified according to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) joint consensus guidelines [16], interpreted in the context of each patient’s phenotype, family history, and prior test results; only clinically significant variants were reported, and low-quality variant calls were confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

Statistical analysis

Given the small sample size, analyses were descriptive. Continuous variables are reported as medians with ranges; categorical variables as frequencies and percentages.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Jordan University of Science and Technology (Approval Number 20230094). The requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective, anonymized nature of the data, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Patient demographics and clinical presentation

Ten patients (six males, four females) met the inclusion criteria. Median age at symptom onset was 12 months (range 3–60), with six patients (60%) presenting during infancy (< 12 months). Median age at diagnosis was 34 months (range 10–72), a median diagnostic delay of 10 months (range 0–48) [17]. Parental consanguinity was reported in five families (50%). Median follow-up duration was 3.5 years (range 3–8).

All patients (100%) presented with chronic diarrhea. Failure to thrive was observed in six (60%), recurrent or severe infections in four (40%), and developmental or neurological concerns in three (30%). Perianal disease was a presenting feature in three (30%). Extraintestinal manifestations included arthritis (n = 2), neutropenia (n = 2), oral ulcers (n = 2), and pancreatitis (n = 1). Endoscopic and histologic findings

All patients underwent ileocolonoscopy with biopsies; upper endoscopy was performed selectively. The most common macroscopic finding was mucosal ulceration (seven patients, 70%). Additional findings included a “leopard-skin” colonic appearance (n = 2), ileocecal valve distortion (n = 1), pancolitis with terminal ileitis (n = 1), and rectosigmoid ulceration (n = 1). Histology demonstrated chronic active colitis in all patients, non-caseating granulomas in three (30%), and focal active colitis in three (30%).

Genetic findings

Genetic testing was performed in eight of ten patients (80%); Patients 2 and 8 were not tested (one declined, one lost to follow-up prior to testing). Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were identified in six of the eight tested patients (75%), involving G6PC3 (likely pathogenic), RIPK1, NPC1, and MEFV (pathogenic variants). In the remaining two tested patients, only a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) was identified — a hemizygous CYBB variant in one and a homozygous LRBA variant in the other. The co-occurring CYBB, GUCY2C, and NOD2 variants were also classified as VUS. Complete variant-level data — genomic coordinate, transcript, cDNA and protein change, type, zygosity, inheritance, ACMG class, and OMIM phenotype — are reported in Table 2, and each patient’s genotype is cross-referenced in Table 1. Homozygous autosomal-recessive variants (G6PC3, RIPK1, NPC1, LRBA) occurred predominantly in offspring of consanguineous parents, consistent with the demographic context; the MEFV, GUCY2C, and NOD2 variants were heterozygous, and the CYBB variants were hemizygous.

Treatment and clinical outcomes

Treatment was heterogeneous and often multimodal. First-line therapies included mesalazine (n = 4) and corticosteroids (n = 2). Exclusive elemental diet was used as bridging therapy in one infant (Patient 5). Four patients (40%) received infliximab; two required dose escalation and interval shortening for loss of response, and one (Patient 7) was poorly adherent and subsequently died. Patient 9 received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and Patient 5 (MEFV variant) showed clinical improvement on colchicine.

Three patients (Patients 4, 6, and 9) were referred to HSCT centers. Patient 9 carried a homozygous pathogenic RIPK1 variant, for which allogeneic HSCT is the established curative therapy; Patients 4 and 6 carried CYBB variants with a CGD-like, treatment-refractory intestinal phenotype, for which HSCT is considered in severe or refractory disease, contingent in these cases on biochemical confirmation of CGD. Patient 9 underwent two unsuccessful HSCT attempts and is maintained on budesonide, mesalazine, and IVIG in clinical remission. Patient 10, with the homozygous LRBA VUS, experienced recurrent hospitalizations; targeted therapy with Abatacept (CTLA-4–Ig) and mTOR inhibition with sirolimus are recognized options in LRBA deficiency [13], but neither was accessible owing to cost and lack of local regulatory approval.

At the end of follow-up, Patient 7 had died of multi-organ failure secondary to disseminated infection, and Patient 8 was lost to follow-up. Treatments and outcomes are summarized in Table 3.

Discussion

This single-center case series characterizes VEOIBD in a Jordanian cohort, demonstrating early, severe disease with a heterogeneous genetic landscape. The median onset of 12 months, with the majority of patients presenting in infancy, places this among the youngest-reported VEOIBD cohorts internationally and aligns with the aggressive, monogenic-enriched clinical course described in the literature [2, 4]. The diagnostic delay of nearly one year underscores the nonspecific nature of early disease presentation in this age group [17].

Parental consanguinity (50%) plausibly shapes the observed genetic architecture by increasing the likelihood of autosomal recessive (AR) disorders [14]; accordingly, all homozygous AR variants in this cohort (G6PC3, RIPK1, NPC1, LRBA) clustered in offspring of consanguineous parents. Clinical red flags — recurrent infections, failure to thrive, perianal disease, and neutropenia — strongly suggested an underlying IEI [5, 18]. The combination of severe neutropenia and recurrent bacterial infections in Patient 1 was consistent with G6PC3 deficiency, a cause of congenital neutropenia with IBD-like intestinal manifestations [19].

The genetic spectrum identified here differs substantially from Western cohorts. No IL-10 pathway defect was identified, despite IL-10 signaling defects being the most frequently reported monogenic cause of VEOIBD globally [7, 12]. The most prevalent variants were in MEFV, reflecting the high carrier frequency of MEFV mutations in Jordanian and broader Middle Eastern populations [20]. Although MEFV is classically associated with autoinflammatory disease, its variants may serve as disease modifiers in IBD [21], and the colchicine response observed in Patient 5 is consistent with such a role, although it does not establish causation.

Co-occurring variants and the digenic hypothesis

Three patients carried variants in two genes (MEFV/CYBB, NPC1/NOD2, and MEFV/GUCY2C). In each pair, the second variant (CYBB, NOD2, and GUCY2C, respectively) is classified as a VUS, and none has undergone functional characterization or segregation analysis. Robustly establishing a digenic or oligogenic contribution to VEOIBD requires functional assays, segregation analysis in additional family members, and independent replication — none of which are available in this series. These co-occurring variants are therefore presented as a hypothesis-generating observation rather than an established disease mechanism, with the exception of the NPC1/NOD2 pairing discussed below, for which a specific mechanistic rationale supported by external evidence has been advanced [9, 22].

Specific gene–disease considerations

CYBB

Both CYBB variants are hemizygous VUS. A CYBB VUS cannot be equated with CGD without functional confirmation; the dihydrorhodamine (DHR) neutrophil oxidative-burst assay is required and was inconclusive in the present cases. The “leopard-skin” colonic appearance and granulomatous histology observed in these two patients are recognized CGD-associated patterns and are suggestive, but not diagnostic, of the disorder.

NPC1 and NOD2

Patient 7 carried a homozygous pathogenic NPC1 frameshift variant (p.Gln119ValfsTer8), causative of Niemann–Pick disease type C (NPC), together with a heterozygous NOD2 missense variant (p.Pro397Leu) classified as a VUS. NPC1 bi-allelic loss-of-function causes a neurovisceral lysosomal storage disorder that can present with cholestasis and gastrointestinal involvement and requires biochemical confirmation (Filipin staining or plasma oxysterols). Pathogenic NPC1 variants have independently been shown to impair autophagosome-mediated bacterial clearance, resulting in sustained pro-inflammatory stimulation [22], while heterozygous NPC1 variants have separately been reported as Crohn’s disease susceptibility alleles [22]. The co-occurring NOD2 p.Pro397Leu variant, located within the NOD-binding domain, is a VUS; although NOD2 is the prototypical Crohn’s disease susceptibility gene, whose variants drive NF-κB hyperactivation and excess release of pro-inflammatory mediators, a VUS classification alone does not establish a contributory role [9]. Based on these converging mechanistic data, it has been proposed that the co-occurrence of pathogenic NPC1 and NOD2 variants may exert a composite digenic effect, with impaired bacterial clearance and NF-κB hyperactivation acting synergistically to amplify immune activation beyond either variant alone, potentially accelerating granuloma formation and producing an earlier-onset, more aggressive Crohn’s disease-like intestinal phenotype than is typically observed with either defect in isolation [9, 22]. This composite digenic hypothesis requires confirmation through functional and segregation studies; the NPC1/NOD2 combination is best regarded as hypothesis-generating, and warrants a low threshold for digenic screening in atypical, treatment-refractory VEOIBD.

GUCY2C

GUCY2C encodes the intestinal guanylate cyclase-C receptor; activating variants increase intracellular cGMP, driving CFTR-mediated chloride hypersecretion and causing familial GUCY2C diarrhea syndrome (FGDS), which carries an increased susceptibility to inflammatory bowel disease and obstructive small-bowel complications [23, 24]. The GUCY2C p.Pro17Thr variant identified in Patient 5 is a VUS; its contribution to the secretory diarrhea observed is therefore biologically plausible but unproven.

LRBA

The LRBA variant is a homozygous missense VUS; the systemic diagnosis of LRBA deficiency therefore remains molecular and provisional pending protein-level confirmation. Homozygous pathogenic LRBA variants typically produce a CVID-like antibody deficiency with autoimmunity; Patient 10 had low IgA levels with IgG subclass deficiency, a humoral phenotype consistent with this diagnosis. LRBA deficiency is amenable to Abatacept and to mTOR inhibition with Sirolimus [13]; access to both was limited in the present setting.

Therapy, anti-TNF exposure in CGD, and access barriers

Therapeutic outcomes were generally poor, consistent with the limited efficacy of anti-TNF agents in monogenic VEOIBD, where blocking TNF-α alone does not address underlying defects in regulatory T-cell function or microbial clearance [10, 11]. Anti-TNF therapy in CGD-associated colitis warrants particular caution; it has been associated with severe and sometimes fatal infections, and its risk–benefit balance remains unsettled. This concern is directly relevant to the present cohort, in which infliximab was not administered to two CYBB patients whose CGD status was not biochemically confirmed. This experience reinforces the need to confirm a phagocyte defect biochemically before exposing such patients to anti-TNF agents.

Access to precision therapies and HSCT was a major barrier throughout this cohort. Three patients were HSCT candidates, the only curative option for conditions such as CGD, RIPK1 deficiency, and LRBA deficiency, yet access was severely limited regionally [2, 25].

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include detailed longitudinal clinical and genetic characterization of a well-phenotyped cohort from an underrepresented region, with follow-up extending to eight years. Limitations include the small sample size and retrospective design. Although genetic testing was uniform (whole exome sequencing in all tested patients), several variants are of uncertain significance and functional validation, the gold standard for confirming pathogenicity was not performed. Disorder-specific confirmatory testing (DHR for CGD, Filipin staining or plasma oxysterols for NPC1, LRBA protein expression by flow cytometry) was not uniformly available, leaving several systemic diagnoses molecular and provisional.

Conclusions

This is the first detailed characterization of VEOIBD in Jordan, revealing extremely early onset, a severe phenotype, and a distinct genetic architecture shaped by high consanguinity. The high prevalence of monogenic defects, the co-occurrence of variants of uncertain significance in some patients including the candidate NPC1/NOD2 composite digenic mechanism [9, 22], and the poor response to conventional therapy underscore the importance of early, comprehensive genomic testing accompanied by disorder-specific functional confirmation. The contrast between potentially curative interventions and poor outcomes attributable to limited access highlights a profound health disparity. Improving outcomes will require regional registries, integration of next-generation sequencing into routine diagnostic pathways, advocacy for access to targeted therapies, and regional HSCT capability for non-malignant conditions.
