Functional characterization of the oligopeptide transporter operon oppABCDF-1 in Escherichia coli Nissle 1917
Authors: Peijun Yu, Ruxue Sun, Yufei Huang, Yunpeng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe ABC-type oligopeptide transporter (Opp system), composed of five subunits (OppA-F), plays an essential role in microbial fitness and survival by mediating the uptake of short peptides. Paradoxically, although the probiotic strain Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) harbors multiple Opp systems, it exhibits a defect in the import of oligopeptides comprising three or more amino acids, a limitation that may compromise its ability to colonize the intestinal tract. This functional paradox underscores the need for further investigation into the substrate specificity and physiological roles of Opp systems in EcN.ResultsGenomic analysis identified four putative Opp systems in EcN, which exhibited low mutual homology (< 41% amino acid identity across all OppA-OppF components), indicating potential functional divergence. Deletion or overexpression of one of the operons, oppABCDF-1 (ECOLIN_07295–07315) did not alter EcN growth under standard or oligopeptide-supplemented conditions. Untargeted metabolomics further demonstrated that this operon was dispensable for oligopeptide uptake. Notably, metabolic profiling revealed significantly impaired uptake of three metabolites in the oppABCDF-1 deletion mutant, along with a modest reduction in the export of putrescine, austinoneol, and leucinic acid. These findings suggest that this Opp system may function as a bidirectional transporter with differential import and export activities.ConclusionThese findings indicate that the oppABCDF-1 operon encoded by ECOLIN_07295–07315 in EcN is dispensable for oligopeptide assimilation, but may contribute to the transport of specific metabolites, offering new insights into the functional diversification of bacterial Opp systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05279-y.Keywords: Escherichia coli Nissle 1917, ABC-type oligopeptide transporter, Genome editing, Metabolic profiling, Functional diversification

Background

Oligopeptide transporters play a vital role in microbial survival and ecological fitness. These systems, which include ATP-binding cassette (ABC) importers like the Opp system and proton-dependent transporters such as the POT family, enable the uptake of short peptides (typically comprising 2–5 amino acids) with broad substrate specificity [1–4]. Under nutrient scarcity, they supply essential carbon, nitrogen, and amino acids, thereby supporting fundamental metabolic needs. Moreover, oligopeptide transporters contribute to diverse physiological processes, including quorum sensing [5], virulence regulation [6, 7], stress responses [8], and biofilm formation [9]. By enhancing peptide acquisition, they improve microbial competitiveness in complex habitats such as the gut, ultimately influencing colonization success [10, 11]. The multifunctional nature of these transporters highlights their significance in both pathogenic and probiotic lifestyles [12].

As a well-conserved ABC-type oligopeptide transporter in bacteria, the OppABCDF system consists of five subunits: OppA (a periplasmic substrate-binding protein), OppB and OppC (transmembrane permeases), and OppD and OppF (ATP-binding and hydrolysis components). This system enables the import of short peptides under nutrient limitation, promoting bacterial growth and metabolic flexibility [13–16]. In various pathogens, the Opp system also participates in antibiotic resistance [17], virulence [18], and cell-cell communication [4]. Although genetic and structural studies have advanced our understanding of Opp complexes, the full range of physiological substrates transported by OppABCDF across different bacteria remains poorly defined and necessitates further investigation [4, 19–21].

Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) is a nonpathogenic probiotic bacterium known for its beneficial effects on host health and has been extensively engineered for therapeutic applications [22–27]. However, the clinical potential of engineered EcN is often constrained by its limited intestinal colonization ability [28–30]. Previous research has shown that, while EcN can efficiently import dipeptides, it lacks the capacity to transport longer oligopeptides (≥ 3 amino acids) [10]. Significantly, introducing a heterologous tripeptide transporter has been demonstrated to enhance both its peptide competitiveness and gut colonization, underscoring the critical link between oligopeptide uptake and ecological fitness [10]. Given EcN’s inherent deficiency in importing larger peptides and the central role of the OppABCDF system in oligopeptide transport in bacteria, elucidating the specific function and substrate profile of OppABCDF in EcN becomes imperative.

In this study, genomic analysis identified four putative OppABCDF systems in EcN. To functionally characterize one of these loci (ECOLIN_07295–07315), we generated an oppABCDF-1 deletion mutant (EcNΔOpp-1) using CRISPR-Cas9-mediated genome editing. Complementing this, we introduced an overexpression plasmid into both wild-type and mutant backgrounds to generate the EcN(pOpp-1) and EcNΔOpp-1(pOpp-1) strains, respectively. Growth assays revealed that neither deletion nor overexpression of oppABCDF-1 affected bacterial growth in LB medium or oligopeptide-supplemented LB medium (LB+Oligo), suggesting that this system does not function as a conventional oligopeptide transporter in EcN. Untargeted metabolomics further demonstrated that OppABCDF-1 likely facilitates the import of three specific metabolites, (5R,6R)-3-(2-acetamidoethylsulfanyl)-6-ethyl-7-oxo-1-azabicyclo[3.2.0]hept-2-ene-2-carboxylic acid, N-(2-formyl-3-chlorophenyl)anthranilic acid, and Ala-Phe-OH, while potentially contributing to the export of putrescine, austinoneol, and leucinic acid. These findings point to a distinctive role for OppABCDF-1 in the transport of non-canonical substrates.

Materials and methods

Homology analysis of each component between the four Opp operons in EcN

Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) was sourced from Pharma-Zentrale GmbH (Germany). The amino acid sequences of each component (OppA, OppB, OppC, OppD, and OppF) from the four predicted oligopeptide transport (Opp) operons in EcN (ECOLIN_07295–07315, ECOLIN_07565–07585, ECOLIN_19575–19595, and ECOLIN_22855–22875) were retrieved. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using the Clustal Omega online tool (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/msa/clustalo) with default parameters. Percentage identity was subsequently calculated to evaluate the degree of homology between corresponding components across the four Opp systems.

Construction of the oppABCDF-1 deletion mutant EcN△Opp

To generate a complete deletion of the oppABCDF-1 operon in the Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) genome, CRISPR-Cas9-mediated genome editing was performed following an established protocol [31]. Specifically, the sgRNA containing the 20-nt guide RNA sequence (CGGCGAACGTACTTTACCAC) targeting the coding region of the oppABCDF-1 operon and two homologous arms spanning 500 bp upstream (LHA) and downstream (RHA) of the oppABCDF-1 operon were PCR‑amplified and then assembled by overlap extension PCR [32] to generate the sgRNA‑LHA‑RHA fragment. The fragment and the plasmid pTargetF [31] were double-digested with restriction enzymes SpeI and PstI, and then ligated to generate the recombinant editing plasmid pTargetF-Opp-1. The successful deletion of the oppABCDF-1 operon in the mutant strain EcN△Opp-1 was verified by PCR-based genotypic analysis. The resulting PCR-amplified fragment was subsequently sequenced to confirm deletion of the operon. The primers used for plasmid construction are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Overexpressing OppABCDF-1 in EcN and EcN△Opp

To investigate the function of the oppABCDF-1 operon in Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN), the operon (5,587 bp) was overexpressed under the control of the Pbio promoter, derived from the CAC1360-1362 locus of Clostridium acetobutylicum. The Pbio promoter was amplified by PCR from the genomic DNA of C. acetobutylicum using primers Pbio-s and Pbio-a. Both the amplified Pbio fragment and the pET-28a vector were double-digested with restriction enzymes BamHI and XhoI and ligated to construct the intermediate plasmid pET-28a-Pbio. The full-length oppABCDF-1 operon was PCR-amplified from the EcN genome using primers oppABCDF-1-s and oppABCDF-1-a. This fragment was then digested with XhoI and ligated into the XhoI-linearized pET-28a-Pbio vector, resulting in the final expression plasmid pET-28a-Pbio-oppABCDF-1. The plasmids pET-28a-Pbio and pET-28a-Pbio-oppABCDF-1 were subsequently electroporated into both EcN and EcN△Opp-1, generating the recombinant strains EcN(pET), EcN(pOpp-1), EcN△Opp-1(pET), and EcN△Opp-1(pOpp-1). The primers used for plasmid construction are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Assessing the growth impact of OppABCDF-1 on EcN

To evaluate the effect of the oppABCDF-1 operon on the growth of EcN, strains EcN(pET), EcN(pOpp-1), EcN△Opp-1(pET), and EcN△Opp-1(pOpp-1) were initially cultured in LB medium supplemented with 50 µg/mL kanamycin at 37 °C until the OD600 reached 1.0. The cultures were diluted 20‑fold into fresh LB medium with kanamycin (50 µg/mL), either containing an oligopeptide mixture (18 g/L casein acid hydrolysate (Solarbio, C8221) and 8.2 g/L casein enzymatic hydrolysate (Solarbio, C8210)) (LB+Oligo) or without (LB) as previously reported [10]. Growth was monitored by measuring the OD600 at 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 h after inoculation.

Untargeted metabolomic analysis

Bacterial strains EcN(pET), EcN(pOpp-1), EcN△Opp-1(pET), and EcN△Opp-1(pOpp-1) were cultured in LB+Oligo medium for 6 h. The resulting culture supernatants were collected by centrifugation and subjected to untargeted metabolomic analysis following a previously described method [33]. Because the OD600 values across the four strains in LB + Oligo medium did not differ significantly at the time of sampling, the metabolomics data were not normalized to cell density. Uninoculated LB+Oligo medium served as the negative control (Con). Each group contains three replicates for the untargeted metabolomic analysis. The relative abundance of each metabolite was calculated and used to assess its variation across different experimental conditions. Metabolites that accumulated in the EcNΔOpp-1(pET) group are potential substrates for import via the OppABCDF-1 transporter, while those less abundant in the EcNΔOpp-1(pET) group may represent metabolites exported by OppABCDF-1.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis data are presented as mean ± SEM. The statistical significance between more than three groups is analyzed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s post‑hoc test for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.1.2).

Results

Genome-wide identification and sequence analysis of oligopeptide transport (Opp) systems in EcN

Through genome-wide bioinformatic analysis, we identified four putative oligopeptide transport (Opp) systems in EcN, encoded by the loci ECOLIN_07295–07315, ECOLIN_07565–07585, ECOLIN_19575–19595, and ECOLIN_22855–22875 (Fig. 1A). Sequence similarity analysis of the individual Opp components (OppA, B, C, D, and F) revealed low amino acid sequence identity across the four systems. Pairwise comparisons showed that core components exhibited less than 48% identity: OppA shared less than 36% identity, OppB less than 39%, OppC less than 41%, OppD less than 46%, and OppF less than 48% (Fig. 1B). These findings suggest that the four Opp systems in EcN are phylogenetically divergent and may possess distinct functional roles.

The operon structures are depicted with arrows indicating the coding sequences of oppA, oppB, oppC, oppD, and oppF, along with their predicted functions. B Sequence similarity analysis of the five Opp protein components across the four identified Opp systems. Pairwise amino acid identity percentages for OppA, OppB, OppC, OppD, and OppF are shown, with values below 48% for all core components, suggesting potential functional divergence among the four systems

Generation and growth characterization of an oppABCDF-1 deletion mutant in EcN

To investigate the functional role of the oppABCDF-1 operon, we generated an in-frame deletion mutant in EcN using CRISPR-Cas9-mediated genome editing, resulting in the strain EcNΔOpp-1 (Fig. 2A). The deletion was verified by PCR amplification (Fig. 2B). For functional complementation and overexpression analyses, the oppABCDF-1 operon was cloned into an expression vector and introduced into both wild-type EcN and the EcNΔOpp-1 mutant, generating EcN(pOpp-1) and EcN△Opp-1(pOpp-1)respectively. Strains harboring the empty vector pET-28a-Pbio (EcN(pET) and EcN△Opp-1(pET)) served as controls. Growth of all four strains was assessed in LB medium with or without oligopeptide supplementation (LB+Oligo) (Fig. 2C). As shown in Fig. 2D, neither deletion nor overexpression of oppABCDF-1 significantly affected bacterial growth under either condition, indicating that this operon is dispensable for conventional oligopeptide transport in EcN.

Untargeted metabolomics reveals substrate specificity of the OppABCDF-1 transporter in EcN

Given that OppABCDF-1 appeared dispensable for conventional oligopeptide transport (Fig. 2D), we performed untargeted metabolomic analysis to comprehensively compare the metabolic profiles of the four engineered strains and to elucidate the substrate specificity of this transporter. Supernatants from cultures grown in LB+Oligo medium were collected from all strains (Fig. 2C), with uninoculated LB+Oligo medium serving as a control (Con). A Venn diagram revealed that 2,692 metabolites were shared across all five groups, while 8 metabolites were unique to the Con group and none were exclusive to any of the bacterial strains (EcN(pET), EcN△Opp-1(pET), EcN△Opp-1(pOpp-1), and EcN(pOpp-1)) (Fig. 3A). The 15 differentially occurring metabolites were presented in Supplementary Table 2. Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) further resolved distinct clustering among the five experimental groups. Notably, the metabolic profile of EcN△Opp-1(pET) diverged markedly from that of EcN(pET), whereas the complemented strain EcN△Opp-1(pOpp-1) clustered closely with EcN(pET), indicating that reintroduction of the oppABCDF-1 operon restored the metabolic phenotype of EcNΔOpp-1 (Fig. 3B).

Discussion

The present study reveals a non-canonical physiological function for the Opp system encoded by the locus ECOLIN_07295–07315 in EcN. Despite its annotation as an oligopeptide permease, growth assays and untargeted metabolomic analyses demonstrated that this OppABCDF-1 transporter is dispensable for oligopeptide uptake, indicating the presence of functional redundancy within the oligopeptide transport network in EcN. It is plausible that alternative peptide uptake systems, such as other Opp-like permeases or dipeptide/tripeptide transporters, compensate for the loss of OppABCDF-1 under the tested conditions. Corroborating this notion, metabolic profiling further indicated its involvement in the transport of specific non-peptide metabolites. These findings support the emerging view that bacterial Opp systems have undergone functional diversification, extending beyond their classical role in peptide assimilation [34–38].

In Fig. 2D, all four strains were cultured in LB medium either supplemented with an oligopeptide mixture (LB + Oligo) or left unsupplemented (LB) to compare their growth performance. Notably, all strains exhibited markedly enhanced growth in LB + Oligo compared to LB alone. The LB + Oligo medium contains not only oligopeptides (≥ 3 amino acid residues) but also considerable amounts of free amino acids and dipeptides, as all hydrolysates are derived from partial casein digestion. These smaller nitrogenous compounds can be readily imported and metabolized by EcN (Fig. 3D-E), which possesses intact amino acid and dipeptide transport systems yet lacks dedicated oligopeptide uptake pathways (Fig. 4A-D). Therefore, the enhanced growth of all four strains in LB + Oligo relative to LB alone likely reflects the increased bioavailability of free amino acids and dipeptides rather than the utilization of longer oligopeptides.

Although deletion or overexpression of the oppABCDF-1 operon exerted no detectable effect on bacterial growth or conventional oligopeptide transport, metabolomic analysis revealed that this system facilitates the uptake of three distinct metabolites and promotes the export of three metabolites, including putrescine (Figs. 3, 4 and 5). Notably, among the metabolites identified, putrescine appears to be the most biologically meaningful candidate, as its physiological functions have been far more extensively characterized than those of the other metabolites. As a member of the polyamine family, putrescine plays a pivotal role in gut homeostasis through its dynamic interactions with the intestinal microbiota [39–41]. Accumulating evidence underscores the pleiotropic functions of putrescine in intestinal physiology. For instance, putrescine serves as a growth factor in the gut and can be utilized as an immediate energy source following conversion to succinate [42, 43]. Beyond its metabolic role, putrescine participates in vitamin D-mediated development of the intestinal villus mucosa and functions as a positive regulator of group 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3s), thereby contributing to mucosal integrity and innate immune responses [44, 45]. Given the beneficial properties of putrescine described above, its elevated abundance in the supernatants of all four EcN‑derived engineered strains suggests that putrescine secretion may serve as a potential mechanism contributing to the probiotic properties of EcN (Fig. 5B). This observation aligns with our previous finding that exposure to a fecal metabolite-containing environment enhances putrescine production in EcN [10]. Notably, the reduced putrescine abundance in the Opp-deficient strain (EcN△Opp-1(pET)) implicates that OppABCDF-1 is partially involved in putrescine export, thereby identifying a novel function for this transporter in mediating non-canonical substrate flux with potential ecological relevance (Fig. 5B). The residual putrescine secretion observed in the EcN△Opp-1(pET) mutant indicates the existence of Opp‑independent export routes. This finding highlights an important caveat: while OppABCDF-1 is capable of transporting multiple metabolites, it does not act as the exclusive conduit for any given compound, including putrescine. These observations underscore the functional redundancy and synergistic interplay among distinct transport systems in bacteria.

Conclusion

This study addresses the functional paradox of Opp systems in the probiotic strain Escherichia coli Nissle 1917, which is inherently deficient in canonical oligopeptide uptake. We demonstrate that the operon oppABCDF-1 is dispensable for growth on oligopeptides and plays no essential role in their assimilation. Instead, functional metabolomics revealed its involvement in the transport of specific metabolites. These findings indicate that this operon has functionally diverged from a conventional peptide transporter to act as a differential import/export system for metabolites. Such functional specialization provides important insights into the evolutionary adaptation of ABC transporters in bacterial ecology. A limitation of this study is that the functions of the remaining three Opp systems in EcN have yet to be characterized. Given their low sequence homology to the system investigated here, it is plausible that they possess distinct and non-redundant substrate specificities. Future studies should focus on delineating the physiological roles of these uncharacterized systems and validating the transport of the identified substrates.
