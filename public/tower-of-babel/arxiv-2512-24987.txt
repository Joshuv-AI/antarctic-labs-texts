Abstract
Axial algebras of Monster type are a class of non-associative algebras which generalise the Griess algebra, whose automorphism group is the largest sporadic simple group, the Monster. The 2 2 -generated algebras, which are the building blocks from which all algebras in this class can be constructed, have recently been classified by Yabe; Franchi and Mainardis; and Franchi, Mainardis and M c Inroy. There are twelve infinite families of examples as well as the exceptional Highwater algebra and its cover, however their properties are not well understood. In this paper, we detail the properties of each of these families, describing their ideals and quotients, subalgebras and idempotents in all characteristics. We also describe all exceptional isomorphisms between them. We give new bases for several of the algebras which better exhibit their axial features and provide code for others to work with them.

Introduction

Axial algebras are a class of commutative non-associative algebras introduced by Hall, Rehren and Shpectorov
[
15
]
. One of the main motivations for their introduction was to axiomatise some properties found in the
196,884
-dimensional Griess algebra which was used to define the largest sporadic simple group, the Monster. However, almost all Jordan algebras are also examples of axial algebras. Recently examples have been found in some unexpected areas, outside algebra and group theory, such as Hsiang algebras in the theory of non-linear PDEs
[
27
]
and algebras of vector flows on manifolds
[
9
]
.

Many classes of algebra are defined by the elements having to satisfy some identities, for example Lie algebras satisfying the Jacobi identity. However, axial algebras are different. An axial algebra
A
is defined to be generated by a distinguished set of elements
X
, called
axes
, whose multiplication is partially controlled by a so-called fusion law
\mathcal{F}
. For graded fusion laws, we get the key feature of axial algebras, which is that we can associate an automorphism
\tau_{a}
to every axis
a
. These generate a distinguished subgroup
\Miy A
of
\Aut A
called the
Miyamoto group
. For the motivating example of the Griess algebras, its Miyamoto group is indeed the Monster.

Axes are semisimple idempotents
a
whose eigenspaces
A_{\lambda}(a)
multiply according to a
fusion law

\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{F},\star)
. That is,
A_{\lambda}(a)A_{\mu}(a)=A_{\lambda\star\mu}(a)
, where
\lambda\star\mu
is a set of eigenvalues. One of the key goals of axial algebras is to classify the algebras with a given fusion law
\mathcal{F}
and which groups occur as Miyamoto groups.

The smallest interesting fusion law is the Jordan fusion law
\mathcal{J}(\eta)
, which has three eigenvalues
1
,
0
and
\eta
(see Table
1
). It is well known that idempotents in a Jordan algebra have the so-called Peirce decomposition which gives the fusion law
\mathcal{J}(\frac{1}{2})
. Matsuo algebras
M_{\eta}(G,D)
, which are defined from a
3
-transposition group
G=(G,D)
, are another important family of examples which have fusion law
\mathcal{J}(\eta)
. Axial algebras of Jordan type
\eta\neq\frac{1}{2}
have been fully classified and the
2
-generated algebras have been classified for
\eta=\frac{1}{2}
too
[
16
]
.

The other fusion law of most interest is the Monster fusion law
\mathcal{M}(\alpha,\beta)
which has four eigenvalues,
1
,
0
,
\alpha
and
\beta
. Indeed, the motivating example of the Griess algebra has fusion law
\mathcal{M}(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{32})
. Many examples of axial algebras of Monster type
(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{32})
have been constructed, but far fewer of type
(\alpha,\beta)
are known. Of those which are known, the most important examples are the
2
-generated algebras – those which can be generated by two axes. These are the smallest examples and are the building blocks from which all other Monster type algebras can be constructed. The theoretical background for constructing a completion of a so-called shape is described in
[
23
]
and there are two different algorithms with implementations by Pfieffer and Whybrow
[
25
]
and by McInroy and Shpectorov
[
21
]
to achieve this.

The
2
-generated axial algebras of Monster type
(\alpha,\beta)
have been classified (under a symmetry assumption on the generating axes) and comprise several

2
-parameter families of examples and also the exceptional Highwater algebra and its cover.

was described in a different way as a split spin factor algebra that the number of axes and idempotents were known

In this paper, we detail the properties of the twelve families of Monster type algebras in all characteristics (except two where the fusion law is not interesting). We also provide code in

We give new bases for six of the twelve algebras which better exhibit their axial structure. In particular, this makes their axial subalgebras clear in the new basis and for

and

Interestingly, we also find that two of the algebras are isomorphic as algebras, but not as axial algebras. If

-generated algebra having a symmetry between

Since quotients of
2
-generated axial algebras of Monster type are also

-generated axial algebras of Monster type (or the

-dimensional

), it is important to classify all these. Table

Since axes are idempotents, it is natural to ask what other idempotents there are in our algebras and whether any of these also have Monster type, or have some other interesting fusion law. For the Norton-Sakuma algebras (

-generated axial algebras of Monster type

. The families

and

It remains to identify the idempotents for any exceptional values of the parameters, we will allow us to identify the automorphism group in all cases. We expect generically the Miyamoto group to be the full automorphism group except for

Definition 2.2

Let

If, in addition,

Where the fusion law is understood from context, we will just call

Let

Note that a Frobenius form is necessarily symmetric.

2.2
Gradings and the Miyamoto group and axets

We are most interested in algebras which have a naturally associated automorphism group. This comes from a grading of the fusion law. Following
[
7
]
, we define these via homomorphisms of fusion laws.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
(\mathcal{F}_{1},\star_{1})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{2},\star_{2})
, be two fusion laws. A
homomorphism
of fusion laws is a map
\xi\colon\mathcal{F}_{1}\to\mathcal{F}_{2}
such that

\xi(\lambda\star_{1}\mu)=\xi(\lambda)\star_{2}\xi(\mu)

For every abelian group
T
, we get a fusion law
(T,\star_{T})
by defining
s\star_{T}t=\{st\}
. We say that a fusion law
\mathcal{F}
is
T
-graded
if there is a homomorphism from
\mathcal{F}
to
(T,\star_{T})
. Looking back at the Monster and Jordan type fusion laws in Table
1
, we see that they are both
C_{2}
-graded.

Using this, we can now define an automorphism of the algebra. We do this just for a
C_{2}
-graded fusion law, which is what we are mostly concerned with in this paper. For a more general definition, see
[
18
]
. Let
\xi\colon\mathcal{F}\to C_{2}
be the grading map. Since an axis
a
is semi-simple,
A
decomposes as
A=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\mathcal{F}}A_{\lambda}
and so we can define
A_{\varepsilon}:=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\mathcal{F},\xi(\lambda)=\varepsilon}A_{\lambda}(a)
, the sum of the eigenspace which are
\varepsilon
-graded. Now define
\tau_{a}\colon A\to A
by

x\mapsto\begin{cases}x&\text{if }x\in A_{+}\\
-x&\text{if }x\in A_{-}\end{cases}

Since multiplication of eigenspaces obeys the fusion law, this is an automorphism that we call a
Miyamoto involution
.

Definition 2.6
.

For an
\mathcal{F}
-axial algebra
A=(A,X)
with a
C_{2}
-graded fusion law, we define the
Miyamoto group
as the group

\Miy(A)=\Miy(A,X)=\langle\tau_{a}:a\in X\rangle

The Miyamoto group is subgroup of the automorphism group, which is often all, or almost all of
\Aut A
.

Given a set
Y
of axes, we define the
closure

\overline{Y}=Y^{\Miy(A,Y)}
of
Y
. By
[
18
]
,
\Miy(A,\overline{Y})=\Miy(A,Y)
and so we say a set
Y
of axes is
closed
if
\overline{Y}=Y
. Hence we may always enlarge a set of axes so that it is closed.

Given a (closed) set of axes
X
, the Miyamoto group acts by permuting the elements of
X
. In
[
23
]
, M
c
Inroy and Shpectorov define an
axet
which roughly speaking is the set of axes
X
with a map
\tau_{a}\colon X\to G:=\Sym(X)
satisfying some natural conditions. For our purposes, we may just think of the set of axes together with the Miyamoto map as being the axet.

In this paper, we concentrate on symmetric
2
-generated axial algebras. By
[
23
, Theorem 1.1]
, every such algebra has axet
X:=X(n)
, where
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
. The axet
X=X(n)
has
n
elements which correspond to an
n
-gon and
\tau_{x}
, for
x\in X
, corresponds to the reflection of the
n
-gon through the vertex
x
. The Miyamoto group
\Miy(X)=\langle\tau_{x}:x\in X\rangle
for a
2
-generated axet/algebra is a dihedral group.

As such, the subaxets of
X(n)
are
X(k)
, where
k\mid n
. For each subaxet
Y=X(k)
of
X=X(n)
,
\langle\!\langle Y\rangle\!\rangle
is an axial subalgebra of
A
. Note that, due to other multiplications in the algebra
A
, this may or may not be proper.

2.3
2
-generated algebras of Jordan type

In this paper, we will encounter the
2
-generated axial algebras of Jordan type which were classified by Hall, Rehren and Shpectorov
[
15
]
. The axes here have three distinct eigenvalues

,

and

and satisfy the fusion law given in Table

[

]
, or

There are two algebras which occur for all values of
\eta
:
2\mathrm{B}
and
3C(\eta)
. The algebra
2\mathrm{B}\cong\mathbb{F}^{2}
is generated by two axes

and

. The algebra

has a basis of axes

and the multiplication is given by

. This algebra is simple unless

and for

, there is a

.

For

be a

-dimensional vector space with a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form

with gram matrix
\begin{pmatrix}2&\delta\\

. If

There are two additional exceptions

and its cover
\widehat{S}(2)^{\circ}

\widehat{S}(2)^{\circ}
has basis

, where

and

. We then define
S(2)^{\circ}=\widehat{S}(2)^{\circ}/\Ann(\widehat{S}(2)^{\circ})
. The algebras

,
\widehat{S}(2)^{\circ}
and

usually have infinitely many axes, but can have finitely many for certain values of

3
Theory and techniques

In this paper, we have two main goals, classifying all ideals and quotients, and finding all idempotents.

3.1
Ideals

The theory of ideals in axial algebras was developed in
[
18
]
. Since ideals are invariant under multiplication by axes, they decompose into the direct sum of eigenspaces. So if
I\unlhd A
and
a
is an axis, then

I=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\mathcal{F}}I_{\lambda}

where
I_{\lambda}:=I\cap A_{\lambda}(a)
.

Proposition 3.1
.

[
18
, Corollary 3.11]

Any ideal
I
in an axial algebra is invariant under the action of the Miyamoto group
\Miy(A)
.

In
[
18
]
, ideals are split into two types, those which contain axes, and those which do not.

Proposition 3.2
.

[
18
, Lemmas 4.14 and 4.17]

Let
A
be a primitive axial algebra which has a Frobenius form. Suppose that
I\unlhd A
which contains an axis
a
. If
b
is an axis such that
(a,b)\neq 0
, then
b\in I
.

For our situation, this gives an easy corollary by combining the above two propositions.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
A=\langle\!\langle a,b\rangle\!\rangle
be a
2
-generated primitive axial algebra with a
C_{2}
-graded fusion law. If either

(1)

there is one orbit of axes under
\Miy(A)
, or

(2)

there are two orbits of axes under
\Miy(A)
and there exists
c\in a^{\Miy(A)}
and
d\in b^{\Miy(A)}
such that
(c,d)\neq 0

then there are no proper ideals containing an axis from the generating set. ∎

We use this corollary to show that almost all algebras in this paper do not have proper ideals which contain axes.

We now turn to ideals which do not contain any axes. The
radical

R(A,X)
is the unique largest ideal that does not contain any axes in
X
.

Theorem 3.4
.

[
18
, Theorem 4.9]

Let
(A,X)
be an axial algebra which admits a Frobenius form. Then the radical
A^{\perp}
of the Frobenius form equals the radical
R(A,X)
of the algebra if and only if
(a,a)\neq 0
for all

.

Since all the
2
-generated algebras have a Frobenius form, we can use the above theorem to easily find the radical
R
. We then make heavy use of the fact that ideals are
\Miy(A)
-invariant and representation theory to decompose

into irreducible
\Miy(A)
-modules. We now recall the following.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
M
be a
G
-module and
M=\bigoplus_{i\in I}M_{i}
be an isotypic decomposition of

, where each

is the direct sum of isomorphic irreducible modules. If

, then

.

This allows us to find all subideals of

.

We note that we must pay careful attention to the characteristic of the field (and other parameters) in two places particularly. Firstly, in finding the radical. Instead of just checking for when the determinant of the Gram matrix
M
of the Frobenius form is zero, we instead find the characteristic polynomial
\chi(M)\in\mathbb{F}[t]
. We can then analyse when

has a factor

and how the multiplicity

and

. Secondly, when

divides the order of the Miyamoto group, the module structure of

3.2
Idempotents

In general, finding idempotents in an arbitrary algebra is hard. One naïve approach and sometimes the only one is to use Gröbner bases. Suppose that
A
is an algebra over a field
\mathbb{F}
with basis
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
. By extending the field to a polynomial ring
P:=\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, we can write a general element of the algebra as
x\in A
as
x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}a_{i}
, where the coefficients are the unknowns. If
x^{2}=x
, then we get
n
quadratic equations
f_{i}
in the
n
unknowns
x_{i}
. We can form the ideal
I=(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
. Any idempotents correspond to simultaneous solutions of the polynomials
f_{i}
; in other words, elements in the variety
\mathbf{V}(I)
. The variety is finite (and so there are only finitely many idempotents) over an algebraically closed field if and only if the ideal is
0
-dimensional. If
\dim I=0
, we can use Magma’s
Variety
function to find the variety.

A variety
V
is the set of simultaneous solutions of a set of polynomial equations
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
. So given a variety
V
, we can also form the ideal
\mathbf{I}(V)=(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
. For any variety
V
,
\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{I}(V))=V
. However, for an ideal
I
over an algebraically closed field,
\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{V}(I))=\sqrt{I}
, the radical of
I
. Hence, if
I=\sqrt{I}
, then the functors
\mathbf{V}
and
\mathbf{I}
are inverses of one another. A variety
V
is called
irreducible
if it cannot be written as the union
V_{1}\cup V_{2}
of two varieties
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
. A variety
V
is irreducible if and only if the corresponding ideal
\mathbf{I}(V)
is prime. So a decomposition
V=V_{1}\cup\dots\cup V_{k}
into irreducible varieties corresponds to a radical decomposition
\mathbf{I}(V)=I=I_{1}\cap\dots\cap I_{k}
where
I_{i}
are prime ideals corresponding to the irreducible varieties
V_{i}
.

So to find all idempotents, we can form the ideal
I
coming from the relations given by
x^{2}=x
. It is more efficient and quicker computationally to use Magma’s
RadicalDecomposition
function to first decompose
I=I_{1}\cap\dots\cap I_{k}
as the intersection of prime ideals
I
and then to find the variety associated to each
I_{i}
using
Variety
than it is to try to find the variety
\mathbf{V}(I)
directly. Note, sometimes the ideal
I
decomposes into some
0
-dimensional and some higher dimensional prime ideals. In some cases, we are able to describe the idempotents corresponding to a higher-dimensional ideal.

Even doing this, for algebras over function fields
\mathbb{F}(\alpha)
for example, this is too slow for dimension
6
and above. We also have two tricks which we employ which speed things up considerably.

For the first, note that over a function field
\mathbb{F}(\alpha)
, all our algebras have an identity. In this case, if
x
is an idempotent, then
\mathbbm{1}-x
is also an idempotent. Set
y=x-\frac{1}{2}\mathbbm{1}
. Then
x
is an idempotent if and only if
y^{2}=(x-\frac{1}{2}\mathbbm{1})^{2}=x^{2}-x+\frac{1}{4}\mathbbm{1}=\frac{1}{4}\mathbbm{1}
. So, we can instead search for
y
such that
y^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\mathbbm{1}
. This has the advantage that the polynomials are more symmetric and so the Gröbner basis calculation in finding the radical decomposition and variety is quicker. More importantly, we can use the Frobenius form to get another relation;
(y,y)=(y^{2},\mathbbm{1})=\frac{1}{4}(\mathbbm{1},\mathbbm{1})
. Since the identity can be found easily (it is a linear requirement), this easily gives another relation for the ideal.

We list orbits of idempotents under the action of
G:=\langle\Miy(A),{\tau_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\rangle\leq\Aut(A)
. If
u
is an idempotent in an orbit of size less than
|G|
, then it has a non-trivial stabiliser
G_{u}
. If
g\in G_{u}
, then
ug=u
. But as
g
acts non-trivially on
A
, this gives linear relations on the coefficients of
u
and so greatly speeds up the Gröbner basis calculation. So we proceed by finding the non-trivial conjugacy classes in
G
and for each representative
g
, forming the ideal
I
including these linear relations. This allows us to find all idempotents not in orbits of size
|G|
.

For the largest two algebras,

and
6\mathrm{J}(2\beta,\beta)
, using the group action trick is the only way finding idempotents is possible and so in these cases, we can only find idempotents with orbit size not equal to
