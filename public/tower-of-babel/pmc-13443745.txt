Uneven mosquito control knowledge, attitudes, and practices in Malaysia shape sustainable dengue prevention across urban and rural settings
Authors: Zulfadli Mahfodz, Rahmat Dapari, Siti Aekbal Salleh, Siti Nazrina Camalxaman, Marzani Mohammad Yusop, Nazri Che Dom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDengue remains a persistent public health challenge in Malaysia, where rapid urbanization, ecological transformation, and behavioral gaps sustain Aedes mosquito proliferation despite decades of control programs. Understanding geographic and urban–rural disparities in community knowledge and preventive practices is essential for developing sustainable, evidence-based strategies. This cross-sectional study, conducted from February to May 2024, involved 866 respondents from 12 states in Peninsular Malaysia representing diverse urban and rural populations. Using a validated Knowledge, Attitudes and Practices (KAP) questionnaire, composite Knowledge, Practice, and Overall KAP indices were computed on a 0–100% scale. State-level differences were examined using one-way ANOVA with post-hoc tests, and urban–rural disparities were evaluated using Welch’s t-tests and Mann–Whitney U tests. Significant heterogeneity was found across states for all indices. Knowledge scores varied widely (F(11,854) = 8.42, p < 0.001, η²=0.10), with Selangor and Perak scoring highest (≥ 80%) and Kedah and Pulau Pinang lowest (< 60%). Practices lagged behind knowledge (F(11,854) = 6.17, p < 0.001, η²=0.07), with Johor showing the strongest knowledge–behavior alignment. Urban respondents exhibited higher knowledge in Johor (+ 0.34, 95% CI: 0.21–0.47) and Pahang (+ 0.29, 95% CI: 0.14–0.44), whereas rural communities in Selangor scored slightly higher (–0.13, 95% CI: − 0.24 to − 0.01). The findings reveal persistent state-level and urban–rural inequalities in mosquito control literacy and behavior. Introduction

Mosquito-borne diseases, particularly dengue, represent one of the most urgent challenges for sustainable cities and societies in tropical and subtropical regions1,2. Globally, dengue incidence has increased more than eightfold in the past two decades, now placing nearly half of the world’s population at risk3. In Malaysia, dengue is hyperendemic, with recurrent outbreaks causing significant morbidity, mortality, and economic burden4. Despite decades of intensive vector control programs including fogging, larviciding, and environmental sanitation sustained reductions in Aedes mosquito populations remain elusive5. This persistent failure highlights the limitations of reactive, top–down interventions and emphasizes the need for proactive, community-driven prevention strategies embedded within sustainable urban and rural systems6.

A critical barrier is the long-recognized knowledge–practice gap, where communities may possess strong awareness of dengue risks yet fail to consistently translate this knowledge into preventive action. Studies in Southeast Asia repeatedly show that while individuals understand mosquito breeding sites and transmission pathways, preventive behaviors such as container cleaning, larval control, and waste management are inconsistently practiced7–9. Contributing factors include behavioral fatigue, socio-economic pressures, and reliance on government-led fogging operations10. Such gaps undermine the sustainability of vector control and hinder resilience building in cities and communities. In Malaysia, most dengue KAP studies have been conducted at the district or city level, limiting their generalizability and obscuring broader socio-ecological disparities9,12. Yet the burden of dengue is spatially uneven: highly urbanized states such as Selangor and Kuala Lumpur consistently report the highest case numbers, while rural states such as Kelantan and Terengganu remain vulnerable due to weaker infrastructure and limited community engagement13. These disparities are shaped by rapid urbanization, high housing density, migration patterns, and differential access to health services and environmental management systems14. Understanding these variations is essential not only for disease prevention but also for advancing sustainable development goals related to health (SDG 3), sustainable cities and communities (SDG 11), and climate action (SDG 13).

Against this backdrop, the present study was designed to examine state-level and urban–rural variation in community knowledge and practices on mosquito control across Peninsular Malaysia. Specifically, it aimed to: (i) analyze sociodemographic determinants of mosquito control behaviors; (ii) compare knowledge and practices across states; (iii) evaluate the extent of the knowledge–practice gap; and (iv) assess urban–rural differences in mosquito control behaviors. By generating comparative evidence across diverse socio-ecological settings, this study extends beyond a single-country perspective to contribute to broader epidemiological and behavioral understanding of dengue prevention in rapidly urbanizing regions. The findings provide transferable insights into how contextual factors shape the translation of knowledge into preventive practices, offering implications for designing scalable, community-centered interventions in other dengue-endemic settings globally.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This investigation employed a community-based cross-sectional design to assess state-level and urban–rural variations in knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) toward mosquito control in Peninsular Malaysia15. Data collection was conducted between February to May 2024 across multiple states selected to reflect both geographic and sociodemographic heterogeneity. The cross-sectional design was chosen as it allows for efficient capture of population-level patterns of awareness and behavior at a single point in time, providing baseline information for targeted intervention strategies.

States were purposively selected using two criteria: (i) dengue endemicity, based on surveillance reports from the Ministry of Health Malaysia, and (ii) demographic heterogeneity, ensuring inclusion of both highly urbanized states (e.g., Selangor, Kuala Lumpur, Penang) and predominantly rural states (e.g., Kelantan, Terengganu, Pahang). This approach ensured representation of populations with different risk exposures, health system access, and socio-ecological contexts that influence mosquito breeding and control practices. Within each selected state, sampling was designed to capture both urban and rural populations. Districts were first stratified by urbanization level using official classifications from the Department of Statistics Malaysia. Subsequently, communities were selected within each stratum, ensuring balanced recruitment across diverse settlement types including high-density metropolitan areas, semi-urban townships, and traditional villages. This stratification was critical to account for the urban–rural divide, as previous studies have shown differences in larval control practices, perception of mosquito risk, and reliance on adult mosquito interventions between these settings. The study setting therefore spanned a wide ecological and socio-economic spectrum, ranging from densely populated apartment complexes in Kuala Lumpur to coastal fishing villages in Terengganu. Such diversity enhances the external validity of the findings, allowing them to inform both national-level policy and state-tailored dengue prevention strategies.

The spatial distribution of the study coverage across Peninsular Malaysia is illustrated in Fig. 1. Randomly selected districts within each state are represented as polygon layers, highlighting the geographic scope of the sampling framework. These districts were selected to capture variability in urbanization levels, demographic characteristics, and dengue risk contexts across the peninsula. The selected areas spanned northern (e.g., Kangar, Alor Setar), central (e.g., Kinta, Hulu Selangor, Petaling), eastern (e.g., Kota Bharu, Kuala Terengganu, Kuantan), and southern regions (e.g., Melaka Tengah, Johor Bahru), ensuring broad spatial representation. This spatial design supports the study’s objective of capturing heterogeneous community knowledge and practices across diverse socio-ecological settings, thereby enhancing the external validity and generalizability of the findings.

Study population and sampling

A total of 866 respondents were recruited using a stratified multistage sampling design. In the first stage, states were stratified by urbanization level (urban, semi-urban, rural). Within each state, districts were randomly selected, and households were approached using systematic random sampling. Eligible participants were adults aged ≥ 18 years who had resided in the area for at least six months. Only one respondent per household was included to avoid clustering.

Data collection instrument

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire that was adapted from validated dengue KAP (Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices) surveys previously employed in Southeast Asia, and subsequently tailored to the Malaysian sociocultural and epidemiological context15. The adaptation process included consultation with entomologists, public health practitioners, and social scientists to ensure relevance to the local dengue control environment. The final instrument was prepared in both Malay and English, with careful attention to semantic and conceptual equivalence.

The questionnaire comprised four domains. First, sociodemographic characteristics were captured, including age, sex, ethnicity, education, occupation, household size, type of residence, and duration of stay at the current location, allowing analysis of social determinants of mosquito control behaviors. Second, knowledge indicators focused on perceived risk of mosquito-borne diseases and the ability to correctly identify Aedes larvae, representing essential cognitive determinants of vector control. Third, preventive practices were assessed through items measuring the frequency of cleaning water storage containers, adoption of protective measures (e.g., insecticide use, repellents, mosquito nets), and active control against both larvae (source reduction, larviciding) and adult mosquitoes (fogging, spraying). Responses were recorded using standardized frequency scales to enable quantification. Fourth, household environment characteristics included observations and self-reports on water storage practices, waste management, and potential breeding habitats, providing ecological context for reported behaviors.

To ensure clarity and cultural appropriateness, the draft questionnaire was pretested in a pilot study involving 30 participants from both urban and rural settings. Feedback was used to refine question wording, reduce ambiguity, and ensure comprehension across different literacy levels. The revised instrument demonstrated strong internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha exceeding 0.80 for both knowledge and practice domains, consistent with reliability thresholds recommended in behavioral health research. Administration of the questionnaire was carried out by trained field enumerators using face-to-face interviews, which minimized missing data and allowed clarification when necessary. The structured format and standardized training reduced interviewer bias and enhanced comparability of responses across diverse settings. The full questionnaire used in this study is provided as Supplementary Material (Supplementary File S1).

Measurement of KAP indices

Composite indices were developed to quantify KAP levels across states. Knowledge Index: Derived from responses to perceived risk of mosquito-borne diseases and ability to identify mosquito larvae, standardized to a 0–100% scale. Practice Index: Constructed from reported frequencies of preventive behaviors, including container cleaning, larval source management, and use of adult mosquito control measures (mosquito nets, insecticide spraying, fogging, repellents). Responses on a 5-point Likert scale were normalized and averaged, then converted to a 0–100% scale. Overall KAP Index: The mean of Knowledge and Practice indices, categorized as Good (≥ 80%), Moderate (60–79%), and Poor (< 60%).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize respondents. State-level differences in sociodemographic characteristics were visualized using heatmaps. Variation in knowledge and practices was assessed using chi-square tests of independence, with adjusted residuals indicating over- or under-representation. One-way ANOVA with post-hoc Tukey’s tests was employed to assess differences in Knowledge, Practice, and Overall KAP indices across states. Effect sizes were expressed as eta-squared (η²). To evaluate urban–rural disparities, independent samples Welch’s t-tests were performed where assumptions of normality and sample size were met; Mann–Whitney U tests were used otherwise. Forest plots summarized mean differences (Urban – Rural) with 95% confidence intervals, where values above zero indicated higher urban scores and values below zero indicated higher rural scores. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the [insert Institutional Review Board/Ethics Committee] (Approval No. REC/07/2023 (ST/MR/183)). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Participation was entirely voluntary, and respondents were assured of anonymity and confidentiality throughout the study. No identifying information was collected, and all data were analyzed in aggregate form to protect individual privacy. The study was conducted in full compliance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and adhered to all relevant national ethical guidelines for research involving human participants.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of respondents

A total of 866 respondents participated in the survey (Table 1). The majority were female (75.8%), with males accounting for 24.2%. Most respondents resided in urban areas (63.6%) compared to rural settings (36.4%). Age distribution showed a predominance of young adults aged 18–30 years (57.9%), followed by 31–40 years (16.1%), 41–50 years (13.6%), 51–60 years (10.0%), and > 61 years (2.4%). The population was overwhelmingly Malay (96.4%), with smaller proportions of Chinese (2.5%), Indian (0.7%), and others (0.4%). In terms of housing type, nearly half of the respondents lived in terrace houses (44.7%), followed by flats/apartments (15.9%), bungalows (13.4%), semi-detached (11.1%), and others (10.1%). Most participants reported living in the same area for more than three years (73.0%), and more than half lived in households of five or more members (51.0%).

State-level sociodemographic variation

Figure 2 highlights marked sociodemographic diversity across Malaysian states. While females constituted the majority of respondents in nearly all states, Negeri Sembilan showed the highest proportion of males (48.9%) (Fig. 2a). Residence patterns were strongly polarized, with Wilayah Persekutuan entirely urban, contrasted by Kelantan (95.9%) and Terengganu (97.9%) as predominantly rural populations (Fig. 2b). Age structure also varied: Selangor, Perak, and Johor were dominated by younger adults (18–30 years), whereas older age groups were more prevalent in Kelantan and Terengganu (Fig. 2c). Ethnicity was overwhelmingly Malay across states, although minority Chinese representation reached 25% in Melaka and 11% in Pulau Pinang (Fig. 2d). Duration of residence was longest in Kelantan, Terengganu, and Perlis, where more than 90% had lived in the same area for over three years, suggesting stable communities (Fig. 2e). Housing profiles showed strong state-specific trends: terrace houses were dominant in Selangor and Perak, bungalows more common in Kelantan, and “other” house types prevalent in Melaka and Kedah (Fig. 2f). Household size was particularly large in rural states, with more than 70% of respondents in Kelantan and Terengganu reporting ≥ 5 household members (Fig. 2g). A clear urban–rural divide was observed, with Wilayah Persekutuan representing highly urbanized, smaller-household demographics, while Kelantan and Terengganu reflected stable, larger rural household patterns likely to influence exposure risk and mosquito control practices.

State-level variation in knowledge and practices on mosquito control

Figure 3 highlights pronounced heterogeneity in knowledge and preventive practices across Malaysian states. Perceived risk of mosquito-borne diseases was generally high; however, some states were significantly overrepresented, reflecting heightened awareness likely associated with recent outbreak exposure, whereas others were underrepresented, indicating complacency despite ongoing endemicity (Fig. 3A). Preventive measures, such as the weekly or biweekly use of mosquito control tools and consistent cleaning of water-filled containers, showed inconsistent patterns between states (Fig. 3B–C). Certain states reported strong adherence to these measures, but others were underrepresented, revealing critical gaps in routine household-level practices that could sustain breeding sites.

Geographic patterns were further evident in larval and adult mosquito control. Rural states were more likely to report active larval control, while urban states exhibited stronger emphasis on adult mosquito control strategies, such as fogging and insecticide use (Fig. 3D–E). This reflects an urban–rural divide shaped by differences in ecological contexts and intervention access. Although the majority of respondents were able to identify mosquito larvae, significant underrepresentation in some states pointed to uneven knowledge distribution, highlighting a knowledge–practice mismatch that may hinder effective community-based prevention (Fig. 2F). Collectively, these findings indicate that dengue-related behaviors are not uniform nationwide but are shaped by local contexts, underscoring the need for state-specific and area-tailored strategies emphasizing larval control and container management in urban areas while strengthening adult mosquito control interventions in rural communities.

Composite knowledge, practice, and overall KAP indices

Significant state-level differences were observed across all composite indices, with moderate effect sizes for knowledge (η²=0.10), practice (η²=0.07), and overall KAP (η²=0.09). Figure 4 presents the state-level composite Knowledge, Practice, and Overall KAP indices, alongside ANOVA results. Significant heterogeneity was observed across states for all three indices. The Knowledge index showed the strongest variation (F(11,854) = 8.42, p < 0.001, η²=0.10), with Selangor and Perak achieving the highest scores (≥ 80%, “Good”), while Kedah and Pulau Pinang fell below 60% (“Poor”). Post-hoc analysis confirmed that Selangor performed significantly better than Kedah and Pulau Pinang. The Practice index also differed significantly across states (F(11,854) = 6.17, p < 0.001, η²=0.07), though overall scores were lower than for knowledge. Johor stood out with the highest practice score (~ 78%), significantly outperforming Kelantan and Perlis, both of which were among the weakest performers (< 50%). The Overall KAP index, which integrated knowledge and practice, revealed a similar pattern (F(11,854) = 7.56, p < 0.001, η²=0.09), with Perak leading and significantly surpassing Terengganu and Kedah. A consistent knowledge–practice gap was observed across states, where higher knowledge scores did not uniformly correspond to higher practice levels. These findings underscore the need for state-specific strategies for example, reinforcing behavioral interventions in states with high knowledge but weak practices, and improving community awareness in states with uniformly poor KAP levels such as Kedah.

Urban–rural differences in knowledge and practice indices

The forest plot (Fig. 5) summarizes urban–rural differences in Knowledge and Practice indices across Malaysian states. For the Knowledge index, urban respondents generally scored higher than rural counterparts, with substantial gaps observed in Johor (mean difference = + 0.34, 95% CI: 0.21–0.47) and Pahang (+ 0.29, 95% CI: 0.14–0.44). In contrast, Selangor showed the opposite trend, with rural residents scoring higher than urban participants (–0.13, 95% CI: − 0.24 to − 0.01). Other states demonstrated smaller or non-significant differences, with confidence intervals overlapping zero. For the Practice index, urban–rural differences were less pronounced. Most states exhibited overlapping confidence intervals, indicating no significant disparities between urban and rural groups. Notable exceptions were observed in Melaka, where urban respondents practiced more preventive measures than rural participants, although the small rural sample size warrants cautious interpretation. Urban–rural differences were more pronounced for knowledge than for practice, with several states showing significant differences in knowledge scores but overlapping confidence intervals for practice.

Discussion

This study highlights important heterogeneities in community knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding mosquito control across Malaysian states, with implications for building sustainable and resilient urban and rural communities. Dengue remains a pressing public health challenge in Malaysia, and its persistence is closely tied to rapid urbanization, ecological transformation, and socio-behavioral dynamics. By examining state-level and urban–rural variations, this study provides critical insights into how socio-ecological contexts shape prevention behaviors and how these insights can be integrated into sustainable city planning and community health strategies. These findings align with established behavioral and socio-ecological frameworks, where knowledge alone is insufficient to drive sustained behavioral change without supportive environmental and structural conditions. From an epidemiological perspective, this reflects the interaction between individual-level cognition and contextual determinants, including housing conditions, infrastructure, and community-level practices.

State-level disparities reflect uneven distribution of health literacy and preventive action across Malaysia. Selangor and Perak reported higher knowledge levels, reflecting more intensive health campaigns and urban concentration of resources. Yet, these gains in awareness did not consistently translate into sustainable household-level practices, underscoring the persistent knowledge–practice gap. The knowledge–practice gap observed across states is consistent with behavioral health models, where awareness does not necessarily translate into action due to barriers such as environmental constraints, risk perception, and behavioral fatigue16. In contrast, Johor demonstrated a more balanced translation of knowledge into practice, while states such as Kedah and Pulau Pinang performed poorly across both domains. These findings mirror patterns in other rapidly urbanizing Asian contexts, where communities remain informed but preventive behaviors falter due to reliance on reactive state-led interventions17. From a sustainability perspective, these patterns demonstrate the need to integrate behavioral reinforcement mechanisms within public health systems, ensuring that knowledge is not only transmitted but embedded into daily routines and supported by urban design that minimizes breeding sites.

Urban–rural comparisons revealed structural inequities. Urban respondents in Johor and Pahang exhibited higher knowledge, reflecting better access to education and risk communication, while rural communities in Selangor unexpectedly showed higher awareness, likely due to localized outbreak experiences. Practices, however, were less variable, was observed structural and ecological factors such as water storage, housing density, and waste management shape preventive behavior as much as knowledge itself. These insights resonate with the concept of socio-ecological resilience, where community behaviors are not only influenced by awareness but also by infrastructural support and environmental design18. Sustainable mosquito control therefore requires urban systems that integrate waste management, water infrastructure, and green space planning with active community engagement19–21.

The knowledge–practice gap observed across states has direct relevance for sustainable urban development. Traditional health education campaigns may raise awareness but do not guarantee behavioral change without supportive urban infrastructure. Consistent with studies in Thailand and Vietnam, this work reinforces that sustainable dengue control must be multisectoral, combining education with environmental management, social mobilization, and technological innovation22,23. The integration of digital health solutions such as mobile apps for vector surveillance or GIS-based hotspot alerts could translate awareness into timely community action25,26. Similarly, participatory approaches such as COMBI (Communication for Behavioral Impact) and community-led vector surveillance align with sustainable development principles by empowering local populations and fostering collective responsibility24.

Policy implications are significant. First, interventions should be state-tailored, addressing high knowledge but low practice states (e.g., Selangor) with behavioral reinforcement, while intensifying awareness and ecological interventions in low-KAP states (e.g., Kedah). Second, bridging the urban–rural divide requires rethinking dengue control as part of broader sustainability planning, integrating housing design, waste management, and water systems that reduce Aedes breeding potential. Finally, embedding dengue prevention within the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 3: Good Health and Well-being, SDG 11: Sustainable Cities and Communities, and SDG 13: Climate Action) situates community engagement on mosquito control as an essential component of urban resilience and planetary health. Importantly, the patterns observed in this study are not unique to Malaysia but reflect broader challenges in translating knowledge into sustained preventive behavior in dengue-endemic regions globally, particularly in settings undergoing rapid urbanization and environmental change.

Several potential sources of bias should be considered. First, the stratified multistage sampling approach, while enhancing representativeness, may introduce clustering effects at the district or community level that were not explicitly modelled in the analysis. Second, the reliance on self-reported data may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias, particularly in reporting preventive practices. Third, variations in sample distribution across states and rural areas may influence the precision of subgroup comparisons. Limitations of this study include its cross-sectional design, which captures only a single time point, and reliance on self-reported practices, which may be subject to recall and social desirability bias. Small sample sizes in certain rural areas, such as Melaka, further limited subgroup precision. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings, as they may influence the magnitude of observed differences. However, the broad geographic coverage and inclusion of diverse socio-ecological contexts support the overall generalizability of the findings to similar dengue-endemic and rapidly urbanizing settings. Nevertheless, the large sample, state coverage, and rigorous statistical analysis strengthen the robustness of the findings. Future directions should emphasize longitudinal research to track sustainability of behavioral change and evaluate the impact of outbreak cycles on practices. Integrating smart city approaches, including IoT-enabled mosquito surveillance and community reporting systems, could transform reactive dengue control into proactive, sustainable management. Intersectoral collaboration linking public health, urban planning, environmental management, and community governance remains pivotal to reducing mosquito-borne disease risk while advancing the broader agenda of sustainable cities and resilient societies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that community knowledge and practices on mosquito control in Malaysia are highly variable across states and between urban and rural populations. While awareness of dengue is generally high, preventive practices remain inconsistent, revealing a persistent knowledge–practice gap. Johor showed stronger translation of knowledge into practice, whereas Selangor and Perak exhibited high knowledge but weaker preventive behaviors, and several states, including Kedah and Pulau Pinang, showed uniformly low scores. Urban communities tended to report higher knowledge, while practice differences were less pronounced. These findings underscore the need for state-tailored, context-specific interventions that strengthen behavioral change strategies in knowledge-rich but practice-poor settings and enhance community awareness where both knowledge and practice are low.

Beyond the Malaysian context, these findings highlight broader challenges in translating knowledge into sustained preventive behavior in dengue-endemic regions globally, particularly in rapidly urbanizing settings. Strengthening integrated, community-centered, and scalable intervention strategies is essential for improving the effectiveness and sustainability of dengue control efforts worldwide.
