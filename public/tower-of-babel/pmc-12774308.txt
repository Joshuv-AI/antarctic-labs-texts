Untangling late-onset first-episode mania: A case series
Authors: Harsh Khandelwal, Sanyam Tyagi, Harmanpreet Kaur, Vishal Verma, Naresh Nebhinani, Navratan Suthar, Pratibha Gehlawat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Late-onset bipolar disorder (LOB) presents unique diagnostic and management challenges as various symptom overlaps with neurocognitive disorders, medical comorbidities, and age-related physiological changes.Cases:We report four elderly patients presenting with first-episode mania, highlighting clinical trajectories, comorbidities, and treatment responses. Three patients had metabolic comorbidities, while two had family history of psychiatric illness. Neuroimaging revealed small vessel ischemic changes in two cases, suggesting a vascular contribution. All patients required inpatient care and responded well to mood stabilizers and antipsychotics.Conclusion:Further research is warranted to optimize management strategies for this understudied subset of bipolar disorder.

INTRODUCTION

Late onset bipolar disorder (LOB) presents unique set of challenges in diagnosis and management within the realm of psychiatric disorders. Classically, bipolar disorder has been associated with an early onset, typically manifesting in late adolescence or early adulthood. However, increasing recognition of bipolar disorder occurring later in life has emerged, necessitating a deeper understanding of its clinical presentations, correlates, and management.

LOB refers to the onset of bipolar symptoms after the age of 50, as proposed by the International Society of Bipolar Disorders (ISBD).[1] However, no universally agreed-upon definition regarding the exact age threshold exists. LOB seldom presents as a first episode of mania in the elderly. This subtype of bipolar disorder poses a significant diagnostic difficulty, as symptoms overlap with other psychiatric conditions commonly seen in older adults, such as neurocognitive disorders. Further complications arise from medical comorbidities and age-related changes in physiology, making diagnosis and management challenging.

Despite the growing recognition, there remains a paucity of literature, especially from India, specifically addressing clinical characteristics, correlates, and optimal treatment of late onset mania.

Here we present four cases of first episode mania in old age, depicting varied presentations, clinical trajectories, comorbidities, treatment responses, and longitudinal outcomes to enhance our understanding.

CASE DETAILS

Case 1

A 63-year-old male, married farmer (8th grade education) from an extended Hindu family of upper middle socio-economic status, with no significant family history of mental illness, and no comorbid medical conditions presented with an illness of 4 months, beginning after being defrauded by his cousin, with complaints of decreased sleep, irritability, verbal aggression, wandering, over-religiosity, inappropriate social behavior, grandiose, and persecutory delusions leading to decreased work efficiency and frequent altercation with family members. He was brought by his son and admitted to the Psychiatry ward under section 89 of the Mental Health Care Act 2017 and was diagnosed with Bipolar type I disorder, current episode manic, with psychotic symptoms. On Mental Status Examination, patient exhibited increased rate, amount, and volume of speech, irritable affect and delusions of grandiosity and persecution in thought content, with Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) score of 35. Treatment included olanzapine 5 mg, which was increased to 20 mg, but later cross-tapered with risperidone due to no improvement, along with divalproex sodium 1000 mg, zolpidem 12.5 mg CR (for sleep), and IM haloperidol 5 mg and promethazine 50 mg (SOS for agitation).

This resulted in significant improvement, with the YMRS score decreasing to four within two weeks. At 5-month follow-up, patient showed complete improvement and medication compliance.

Case 2

A 68-year-old married female from a Hindu nuclear family of upper middle socio-economic status, studied till 3rd class, homemaker, no significant family history, with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus and Hypertension, and tobacco dependence, presented with 2-month symptoms with subacute onset and features of irritability, decreased need for sleep, restlessness, over talkativeness, Overspending, increased self-esteem, and aggressive behavior. At presentation and subsequent admission to our center due to difficulty in managing at home, her body mass index (BMI) was 20.3 kg/m². Mental Status Examination showed increased psychomotor activity, irritable affect, elevated self-esteem, impaired judgment, and Grade 1 insight. She was diagnosed with Bipolar I Disorder, current episode mania, Nicotine Dependence, Metabolic Syndrome, and Vitamin D insufficiency. YMRS was 31, and the Fagerstrom Test for Nicotine Dependence (FTND) score was 3.

She was treated with aripiprazole, gradually hiked up to 15 mg, vitamin D3, nicotine lozenges, and benzodiazepines. Psycho-education and sleep hygiene were taught. She was discharged after 2 weeks with YMRS 2. MRI Brain conducted showed small vessel ischemic changes and cerebral atrophy. She was stable at follow-up 2 years post-discharge.

Case 3

A 66 years married male, belonging to Hindu nuclear family of upper socio-economic status, with family history of psychotic illness in two siblings and depression in two other siblings, with medical history of Hypertension and Diabetes, presented to OPD with illness of 2 months duration, with sub-acute onset of symptoms characterized by irritable mood, increased energy, elevated self-esteem, increased goal-directed activity, decreased need for sleep, overspending, over talkativeness, overgrooming, overfamiliarity, disinhibition, verbal, and physical aggression, with history of nicotine dependence. On Mental status examination, there was increased psychomotor activity and irritable affect, increased tone, and self-esteem, arousable but ill-sustained attention, impaired judgement, and absent insight. He was started on divalproex 500 mg BD, haloperidol 5 mg, and tab trihexyphenidyl (THP) 2 mg. However, due to development of extrapyramidal symptoms, it was cross-tapered with tab quetiapine and gradually increased to 400 mg, while divalproex was increased to 1500 mg. Antidiabetic and antihypertensive medications were continued after consultation with endocrinology department. MRI of the Brain was normal. The patient has maintained well for 3 years with no further episodes on divalproex 1500 mg and quetiapine 200 mg.

Case 4

A 63-year-old married male, lawyer with family history of late-onset mental illness (possible dementia in father) and 30-year history of hypertension, presented with 4-month subacute onset of illness. Symptoms included irritable mood, increased self-esteem, over-talkativeness, goal-directed activity, grandiose ideas, reduced sleep, increased appetite, sexual indiscretions, aggression, and overspending

On examination, BMI was 26.3 kg/m², BP 138/88 mmHg. Mental status examination revealed increased speech, irritable affect, persecutory delusions, and Grade 1 insight. YMRS score was 12. Treatment included divalproex 1500 mg, olanzapine 10 mg BD, and clonazepam up to 6 mg/day. Telmisartan 40 mg was initiated for hypertension, and previous MRI Brain findings of small vessel ischemic changes led to the prescription of aspirin 75 mg and atorvastatin 40 mg by Neurology. Acute agitation was managed with haloperidol and promethazine IM injections. Discharged after 2 weeks with YMRS 4, he remained compliant but developed depressive symptoms within a month. Trials of lurasidone, quetiapine, and olanzapine-fluoxetine failed. Escitalopram 20 mg with divalproex 2000 mg showed gradual improvement over 10 months, aided by behavioral activation. He was stable at follow-up 2 years post-discharge.

DISCUSSION

LOB disorder is less common compared to early-onset bipolar disorder, with about 10% of all bipolar disorder cases beginning after age of 50.[1]

Gender differences have not been explored in late-onset variant of BD. However, this subset of individuals is more likely be married and less qualified, which may be consistent with expected trends for that generation.[2] LOB tends to have distinct characteristics compared to early-onset bipolar disorder (EOB). As per world literature, in older adults’ symptoms of mania, or hypomania are less pronounced. Depressive and cognitive symptoms are more common, with aggression, insomnia, impulsivity, and self-neglect posing significant risks.[3] According to Cassano et al.,[4] manic symptoms in LOB are generally fewer and milder than those in younger patients. Sajatovic[5] added that LOB patients are more likely to exhibit irritable affect, treatment resistance, and higher mortality. Soni et al.[6] reported that early-onset BPAD had more episodes, stronger family history, and higher irritability, while LOB exhibited more elevated mood in contrast all four of our cases presented with first-episode irritable mania with prominent aggression, requiring inpatient management, given the severity of symptoms.

Grover et al.[7] studied LOB in Indian patients and found fewer, but longer depressive episodes, more frequent manic episodes, less psychosis, higher brain insult rates, and significant family history, challenging the belief that LOB is nonfamilial. Treatment patterns in papers included mood stabilizers and antipsychotics that are seen in all cases here with significantly more trials following a depressive episode and treatment with divalproex and escitalopram combination proving effective in Case 4 for maintenance. Medical comorbidities were seen in 3 of 4 cases reported here, especially cardiovascular and metabolic disorders, which syncs with the available literature and should be prioritized in management, including screening for safety through investigations before starting antipsychotics, choosing cardiac and metabolically safe antipsychotics, and subsequent regular monitoring.[8] Family history of psychiatric illness was noted in 2 out of the 4 cases; however, only one was suggestive of depressive illness. Depp and Jeste[3] noted that LOB is associated with lower familial rate of bipolar disorder and higher medical and neurological comorbidity.

In older patients, it is essential to identify and rule out other medical causes of late onset of bipolarity, which include secondary mania, dementia, especially Frontotemporal Dementia, and delirium. Several medical conditions, such as hyperthyroidism, brain tumors, and traumatic brain injury, can be direct or indirect causes for LOB, so comprehensive workup, including thorough neurological examination, blood investigation, and neuroimaging, must be conducted to rule out organicity first, as performed in our cases.[9] Takahashi et al.[10] noted that late-onset mania patients had more severe bilateral deep white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) compared to early-onset patients that is seen in two of our cases suggesting a neurovascular pathology as basis of late-onset mania although both cases did not develop dementia syndrome at long term follow-up of 2 years, suggesting that these Cases are late-onset BPAD rather than earlier manifestation of organic disorders.

Given their distinct sociodemographic and clinical profiles, this case series underscores the need for tailored clinical approaches and vigilant monitoring for late-onset mania patients. Further longitudinal and prospective studies are warranted from Indian subcontinent for exploring genetics, neurobiological underpinnings, safe, and effective treatment alternatives for this subtype of bipolar disorder patients.
