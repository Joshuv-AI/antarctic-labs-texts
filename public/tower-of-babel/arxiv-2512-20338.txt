Up-down chains and scaling limits: application to permuton- and graphon-valued diffusions
Valentin Féray, Kelvin Rivera-Lopez

Abstract
An up-down chain is a Markov chain in which each transition is a two-step process that moves up to a larger object and then back down to an object of the original size. The first goal of this paper is to present a general framework for analyzing these chains and computing their scaling limits. This approach unifies much of the existing literature while extending it in several directions. These include explicit conditions for constructing integrable up-down chains and convergence results for families of intertwined processes. The latter contribute to the method of intertwiners of Borodin and Olshanski. The second goal is to highlight a notable application of this framework to the settings of permutations and graphs. Here, we identify some integrable up-down chains and construct their scaling limits, a family of permuton- and graphon-valued Feller diffusions. Both the up-down chains and the limiting diffusions exhibit ergodicity, diagonalizable semigroups, and explicit expressions for the maximal separation distance to stationarity. For the diffusions, the stationary measures are the recursive separable permutons and recursive cographons recently introduced by the authors, and the separation distances turn out to be related to the Dedekind eta function.



Keywords—
up-down chains, intertwining, scaling limits, Feller diffusions, mixing time, permutons, graphons

Permutation and graph dynamics.

To our knowledge, the permutation- and graph-valued
up-down chains we consider have not yet appeared in the literature.
However, the deterministic duplication operations are standard:
the permutations/graphs that can be obtained from the permutation/graph of size
1

by these operations are known as separable permutations/cographs
and are well-studied objects
(see the references given in
[
BBF+18
]

and
[
BBF+22
]
respectively).
Thus, using these duplication operations to build
up-down chains on permutations and graphs seems natural.

In another direction, let us point out that
there is a large existing literature on graph dynamics and graphons,
but it seems that simple dynamics on finite graphs leading to nontrivial dynamics on the space
of graphons are uncommon.
Indeed, due to an averaging effect, the limiting dynamic is often deterministic,
and can sometimes be analyzed through the
differential equation method
;
see the seminal paper of Wormald
[
Wor95
]
for general principles
or the recent paper
[
GHvS23
]

for a large class of random graph evolutions (defined via local edge replacements)
that lead to deterministic dynamics on graphons in the limit.
In contrast, our model, consisting of local vertex replacements,
still has a random behavior at the level of graphons.
It would be interesting to study
a mix of vertex and edge replacements
and to understand the phase transition between deterministic and random dynamics
in the limit, but this is out of the scope of this article.

Background on Feller Processes

This section provides the necessary theory of Feller processes that we will use.
For a simpler presentation, we have reformulated the results we reference into our specific context.
The discussion on separation distance contains new results, which we believe are of independent interest.

Kernels and transition operators

Let
(E,\mathcal{E})
and
(F,\mathcal{F})
be measurable spaces.
A
probability kernel from
E
to
F
is a function
\mu\colon E\times\mathcal{F}\to[0,\infty)
such that

(i)

for every
B\in\mathcal{F}
, the map
\mu(\,\cdot\,,B)
is measurable, and

(ii)

for every
x\in E
, the map
\mu(x,\,\cdot\,)
is a probability measure on
F
.

We will refer to these objects simply as kernels.
Kernels can be viewed as generalized transition matrices: given
x\in E
, we can transition to
F
by sampling an object according to
\mu(x,\,\cdot\,)
.
In fact, when the spaces
E
and
F
are discrete, we can regard a kernel as an
E\times F
matrix whose entries are given by
\mu(x,y)\hskip 1.0pt\raisebox{0.4pt}{:}\hskip-4.0pt=\mu(x,\{y\})
.

The operations of transition matrices can be generalized to operations involving kernels.
For example, a kernel
\mu
from
E
to
F
can act on a probability measure
\lambda
on
E
to obtain a probability measure on
F
. This measure is given by

(\lambda\mu)(B)=\int_{E}\lambda(dx)\mu(x,B),\qquad B\in\mathcal{F}.

Similarly, we can take the product of
\mu
and a kernel
\nu
from
F
to
G
to obtain a kernel from
E
to
G
.
This kernel is given by

(\mu\nu)(x,C)=\int_{Y}\mu(x,dy)\nu(y,C),\qquad x\in E,\,C\in\mathcal{G}.

The kernel
\mu
can also act on functions, similar to how a transition matrix can act on column vectors.
This action takes the form of a linear operator
T_{\mu}\colon\mathcal{M}_{b}(F)\to\mathcal{M}_{b}(E)
defined by

(T_{\mu}f)(x)=\int_{F}f(y)\mu(x,dy)=\mathbb{E}_{x}[f(Y)],

where, in the last expression, the random variable
Y
has distribution
\mu(x,\cdot)
.
This operator is the
transition operator
associated with
\mu
, and it can be viewed as a dual object to
\mu
.
The kernel
\mu
can be recovered from its transition operator
T_{\mu}
by the formula

\mu(x,B)=(T_{\mu}\mathbbm{1}_{B})(x).

(4)

The properties of
\mu
make
T_{\mu}
a positive, contractive, and conservative operator.
Recall that an operator
V
is
positive
if
Vf\geq 0
whenever
f\geq 0
,
contractive
if its operator norm is at most 1, and
conservative
if
V\,1=1
. Finally, we note that transition operators are often restricted to subspaces of their domain.
For example, whenever
F
is a compact topological space, it is common to restrict
T_{\mu}
to the space
C(F)

of continuous functions on
F
.

Markov processes, Feller semigroups, and generators

Let
(E,\mathcal{E})
be a measurable space and let
Y
be a continuous-time time-homogeneous Markov process in
E
.
Recall that
Y
is often described by a family of kernels
\{\mu_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
that specifies the conditional distributions

\mu_{t}(Y(s),B)=\mathbb{P}(Y(s+t)\in B\,|\,Y(s)),\qquad s,t\geq 0,\,B\in\mathcal{E}.

An alternative description of
Y
is given by the associated transition operators
\{T(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
, which specify the conditional expectations

\displaystyle(T(t)f)(x)=\int_{E}f(z)\mu_{t}(x,dz)=\mathbb{E}_{x}[f(Y(t))],\qquad f\in\mathcal{M}_{b}(E),\,x\in E,\,t\geq 0.

The family
\{T(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is called the
transition semigroup
of
Y
.
This language indicates that these operators are associated with a Markov process and form an
operator semigroup
: they are bounded, defined on a Banach space, start from
T(0)=I
, and satisfy the semigroup identity
10
10

10

This relation can be seen as dual to the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation.

T(t+s)=T(t)T(s),\qquad s,t\geq 0.

Conversely, the theory of Feller processes provides conditions for an operator semigroup to
be the transition semigroup of a Markov process exhibiting some regularity.
For simplicity, let
E
be a compact metric space.
A
Feller semigroup
on
C(E)
is an operator semigroup
\{T(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
of positive, contractive, conservative
11
11

11

This condition is sometimes left out.
operators that satisfy the following regularity conditions, known as the
Feller properties
:

(i)

T(t)C(E)\subseteq C(E)
for
t\geq 0
, and

(ii)

(T(t)f)(x)\to f(x)
as
t\to 0
for
f\in C(E)
and
x\in E
.

Theorem 2.1

(Chapter 4 Theorem 2.7 in
[
EK05
]
)
.

Let
\{T(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
be a Feller semigroup on
C(E)
.
For every Borel probability measure
\nu
on
E
, there exists a Markov process with initial distribution
\nu
, transition semigroup
\{T(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
, and sample paths in the Skorokhod space
D([0,\infty),E)
.

A Markov process associated with a Feller semigroup is called a
Feller process
.
Interestingly, this process can be described by a single operator, called the
generator
of its semigroup.
The generator of a Feller semigroup
\{T(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
on
C(E)
is the operator defined by the limit

Af=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{T(t)f-f}{t},

wherever it exists.
This domain is usually not

but is always a dense subspace of

(see
[

).
Moreover, the constant function
1
lies in the domain and
A\,1=0
since
T(t)1=1
.

In general, handling a generator on its full domain can be challenging.
As a result, a generator is typically only specified on a suitable subspace of its domain, chosen so that the full generator can be obtained from this restriction.
The choice of such a subspace is informed by the fact that generators are
closed

12

12

On the other hand, generators are usually unbounded.

operators – that is, if
A
is a generator with domain

then its

\{(f,Af):f\in\mathcal{D}\}

will be closed in

under the product topology.
A representative subspace can therefore be found in a

, a subspace

for which the graph of

is dense in the graph of

.

Proposition 2.2

(Proposition 19.9 in
[
Kal02
]
)
.

Let
A
be the generator of a Feller semigroup and
\mathcal{D}
be its domain.
Then every dense, invariant subspace

is a core for

.

A notable class of Feller processes are the
pseudo-Poisson processes
.
These are continuous-time variants of discrete-time chains that admit a simple construction.
Indeed, let
Y
be a discrete-time Markov chain on a finite set
E
,
T
the associated transition operator, and

with rate

Then the composition
X(t)\hskip 1.0pt\raisebox{0.4pt}{:}\hskip-4.0pt=Y(N[0,t])
is a pseudo-Poisson process whose generator is the bounded operator

and whose semigroup is given by

Convergence theorems

Let
E
be a compact metric space, to be viewed as the ambient space.
For

be a metric space,

In simple terms, they say that this convergence can be obtained from the convergence of semigroups or the convergence of generators.
For the precise statements, we need the following notion of convergence.
A sequence
\{f_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
with
f_{n}\in C(E_{n})
converges to

(and we write

)
if it is asymptotically close to the sequence of projections
\pi_{n}f=f\circ\gamma_{n}
– that is, if

\|f_{n}-\pi_{n}f\|_{C(E_{n})}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Theorem 2.3

(Chapter 4, Theorem 2.12 in

Let

Suppose that the initial distributions of
\{\gamma_{n}(Y_{n}(0))\}
converge, say to

, and that
\{\varepsilon_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is a positive sequence converging to zero such that

T_{n}^{\lfloor t/\varepsilon_{n}\rfloor}\pi_{n}f\to T(t)f,\qquad\text{ for all }f\in C(E),\,t\geq 0.

Then there exists a Markov process

with initial distribution
\nu
, transition semigroup
\{T(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
, and sample paths in the Skorokhod space

such that the convergence

\big(\gamma_{n}(Y_{n}(\lfloor t/\varepsilon_{n}\rfloor))\big)_{t\geq 0}\Longrightarrow\big(Y(t)\big)_{t\geq 0}

holds in distribution in

.

Theorem 2.4

(Chapter 1, Theorems 6.1, 6.5 in
[
EK05
]
)
.

Let

Separation Distance

Let
P
and
Q
be probability distributions on the same finite set
Z
.
Then their separation distance is defined as

\sep(P,Q)\hskip 1.0pt\raisebox{0.4pt}{:}\hskip-4.0pt=\max_{z\in Z:Q(z)\neq 0}\bigg(1-\frac{P(z)}{Q(z)}\bigg).

The separation distance satisfies all of the metric axioms except symmetry and takes its values in
[0,1]
.
As the following result shows, it also admits a dual description.
Recall that
C_{+}(E)
denotes the set of continuous functions from a topological space
E
to
(0,\infty)
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
P
and
Q
be probability distributions on the same finite set
Z
.
Then

\sep(P,Q)=\sup_{f\in C_{+}(Z)}\bigg(1-\frac{\int_{Z}fdP}{\int_{Z}fdQ}\bigg).

Proof.

It will suffice to show that

m\hskip 1.0pt\raisebox{0.4pt}{:}\hskip-4.0pt=\min_{z\in Z:Q(z)\neq 0}\frac{P(z)}{Q(z)}=\inf_{f\in C_{+}(Z)}\frac{\int_{Z}fdP}{\int_{Z}fdQ}.

The definition of
m
gives us an inequality in one direction:

\frac{\int_{Z}fdP}{\int_{Z}fdQ}=\frac{\sum_{z\in Z}f(z)P(z)}{\sum_{z\in Z}f(z)Q(z)}\geq\frac{\sum_{z\in Z}f(z)(Q(z)m)}{\sum_{z\in Z}f(z)Q(z)}=m,\qquad f\in C_{+}(Z).

The reverse inequality can be obtained by using
\mathbbm{1}_{z}+\varepsilon\mathbbm{1}_{Z}\in C_{+}(Z)
to approximate
\mathbbm{1}_{z}
:

\inf_{f\in C_{+}(Z)}\frac{\int_{Z}fdP}{\int_{Z}fdQ}\leq\frac{\int_{Z}\mathbbm{1}_{z}+\varepsilon\mathbbm{1}_{Z}\,dP}{\int_{Z}\mathbbm{1}_{z}+\varepsilon\mathbbm{1}_{Z}\,dQ}\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0^{+}]{}\frac{\int_{Z}\mathbbm{1}_{z}\,dP}{\int_{Z}\mathbbm{1}_{z}\,dQ}=\frac{P(z)}{Q(z)},\qquad z\in Z,\,Q(z)\neq 0.\qed

This dual description naturally extends to richer spaces
13
13

13

For an alternative extension of separation distance beyond finite spaces (using the essential supremum of the Radon-Nikodym derivative), see
[
ACPM24
]
.
: for probability distributions
P
and
Q
on a compact topological space
Z
, we define

\sep(P,Q)\hskip 1.0pt\raisebox{0.4pt}{:}\hskip-4.0pt=\sup_{f\in C_{+}(Z)}\bigg(1-\frac{\int_{Z}fdP}{\int_{Z}fdQ}\bigg).

We note that this quantity continues to lie in
[0,1]
and will satisfy the metric axioms (except symmetry) when
Z
is a separable metric space.

In the context of Markov chains, it is typical to let
Q
be the unique stationary distribution of the Markov chain and let
P
be the distribution of the Markov chain after a certain number of steps.
The separation distance then depends on the initial distribution of the chain and on time.
Following
[
AD87
]
, it is standard here to maximize over all initial positions in the state space and let the separation distance be a function of time.
More precisely, for a Markov chain
Y
with unique stationary distribution
\nu
, transition matrix
p
, and finite state space
E
, one considers

\Delta(m)=\max_{y\in E}\,\sep(p^{m}(y,\,\cdot\,),\nu)=\max_{y,y^{\prime}\in E,\,\nu(y^{\prime})\neq 0}\Big(1-\frac{p^{m}(y,y^{\prime})}{\nu(y^{\prime})}\Big),\qquad m\geq 0.

We call this the separation distance of
Y
.
Similarly, we define the separation distance of a Markov process
Y
with unique stationary distribution
\nu
, transition semigroup
\{T(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
, and compact topological state space
E
as

\Delta(t)=\sup_{x\in E}\sep(\Law_{x}(Y(t)),\nu)=\sup_{x\in E,\,f\in C_{+}(E)}\bigg(1-\frac{(T(t)f)(x)}{\int_{E}fd\nu}\bigg),\qquad t\geq 0,

where
\Law_{x}(Y(t))
is the distribution of the Markov process
Y

at time
t
, initiated at
x
.

A simple property of the separation distance is the following inequality:

T(t)g\geq\big(1-\Delta(t)\big){\textstyle\int_{E}g\,d\nu},\qquad g\in C(E),\,g\geq 0.

(5)

To see this, note that it holds by definition for positive
g
and extends to nonnegative
g
by using

{g+\varepsilon\mathbbm{1}_{Z}\in C_{+}(E)}
to approximate
g
as before.
The following result establishes a few more properties.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
Y
be a Markov process with unique stationary distribution
\nu
,
transition semigroup
\{T(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
,
and compact topological state space
E
.
Then its separation distance
\Delta(t)

(i)

is a nonincreasing function of
t
,
and

(ii)

is submultiplicative – that is,
\Delta(t+s)\leq\Delta(t)\Delta(s)
for
s,t\geq 0
.

Proof.

We begin with the second claim.
To this end, fix
s,t\geq 0
.
Taking
f\in C_{+}(E)
, an application of the inequality (
5
) implies that the following function is nonnegative:

g=T(s)f-(1-\Delta(s)){\textstyle\int_{E}fd\nu}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{E}.

Applying (
5
) now to
g
, we must have that

T(t)g\geq\big(1-\Delta(t)\big){\textstyle\int_{E}g\,d\nu}
.
Let us simplify the left-hand side here using the semigroup identity
T(t+s)=T(t)T(s)
and the conservativity of
T(t)

T(t)g=T(t)T(s)f-(1-\Delta(s)){\textstyle\int_{E}fd\nu}\cdot T(t)\mathbbm{1}_{E}=T(t+s)f-(1-\Delta(s)){\textstyle\int_{E}fd\nu}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{E}.

Now we handle the right-hand side using the stationarity of

{\textstyle\int_{E}g\,d\nu}={\textstyle\int_{E}T(s)fd\nu}-(1-\Delta(s)){\textstyle\int_{E}fd\nu}\cdot{\textstyle\int_{E}\mathbbm{1}_{E}d\nu}={\textstyle\int_{E}fd\nu}-(1-\Delta(s)){\textstyle\int_{E}fd\nu}=\Delta(s){\textstyle\int_{E}fd\nu}.

Our inequality thus becomes

T(t+s)f-(1-\Delta(s)){\textstyle\int_{E}fd\nu}\geq\big(1-\Delta(t)\big)\Delta(s){\textstyle\int_{E}fd\nu},

which rearranges to

1-\frac{T(t+s)f}{\textstyle\int_{E}fd\nu}\leq\Delta(t)\Delta(s).

Recalling that
f\in C_{+}(E)
was arbitrary gives us that
\Delta(t+s)\leq\Delta(t)\Delta(s)
, which proves the second claim.
The first claim then follows from the fact that separation distances take values in
[0,1]
.

\left(T_{n}^{\lfloor t/\varepsilon_{n}\rfloor}\pi_{n}f\right)(x_{n})\geq\big(1-\Delta_{n}(\lfloor t/\varepsilon_{n}\rfloor)\big)\,{\textstyle\int_{E_{n}}\pi_{n}fd\nu_{n}},\quad n\geq 1.

By assumption,

For the constants

The density functions and the triangular descriptions

In what follows, it will be useful to consider elements from arbitrary state spaces.
For this, we define

\mathbb{S}=\sqcup_{n\geq 0}\mathbb{S}_{n}

and reserve the symbols
r

s

for elements of this set.
We will also need to consider elements from certain state spaces.
To do this, we let
|s|
denote the index of the state space that
s

For example,
|s|\geq 1
specifies that
s\in\sqcup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{S}_{n}
and
|s|=k

s\in\mathbb{S}_{k}

We will use this notation freely, generalizing it in a natural way.

Let

\displaystyle\begin{split}p^{\uparrow}_{n}(s,u)&=(U_{n}\mathbbm{1}_{u})(s),\hskip 116.6563pt|u|-1=|s|=n\geq 0,\\

d_{s}(u)=\begin{cases}p^{\downarrow}_{|u|,|s|}(u,s),&|u|\geq|s|,\\
0,&\text{else}.\end{cases}

(12)

Proposition 3.5

Let

The following identities hold:

(i)

(d_{s})_{n}=\sum_{|u|=k}d_{s}(u)(d_{u})_{n},

(ii)

.

The first identity above implies that the state spaces of our Markov chains induce a filtration on each

Proof.

For the first identity, we evaluate the right-hand side at

\displaystyle\sum_{|u|=k}d_{s}(u)(d_{u})_{n}(r)=\sum_{|u|=k}p^{\downarrow}_{k,|s|}(u,s)p^{\downarrow}_{n,k}(r,u)=(p^{\downarrow}_{n,k}p^{\downarrow}_{k,|s|})(r,s)=p^{\downarrow}_{n,|s|}(r,s)=d_{s}(r).

Proposition 3.7

Let

\displaystyle T_{n}(d_{s})_{n}

\displaystyle=(1-\omega_{|s|,n})(d_{s})_{n}+\omega_{|s|,n}\sum_{|r|=|s|-1}(d_{r})_{n}\,p^{\uparrow}_{|r|}(r,s),\qquad|s|\leq n.

When

Combining (

\displaystyle T_{n}(d_{s})_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|r|=n+1}p^{\uparrow}_{n}(\,\cdot\,,r)\sum_{|u|=n}p^{\downarrow}_{n+1}(r,u)(d_{s})_{n}(u)=\sum_{|r|=n+1}\sum_{|u|=n}p^{\uparrow}_{n}(\,\cdot\,,r)p^{\downarrow}_{n+1,n}(r,u)p^{\downarrow}_{n,|s|}(u,s)

The diagonal descriptions

The next step in our analysis is to obtain diagonal descriptions of the transition operators.
For this, we will need the constants

Lemma 3.9
.

The following identity holds:

\sum_{k=i}^{j}\eta_{i,k}\eta^{*}_{k,j}=\mathbbm{1}(i=j),\qquad 0\leq i\leq j.

Interpreting this as a matrix identity reveals that

Proof.

The

\eta_{i,i}=\eta^{*}_{i,i}=1

Let then

Consider the Lagrange interpolating polynomial for the points

This polynomial must be
L(x)\equiv\eta^{*}_{i,j}
, since it interpolates the points and its degree is less than the number of points.
Using the Lagrange basis expansion, we can write this as

\eta^{*}_{i,j}\equiv\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\eta^{*}_{i,j}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=i\\
m\neq k\end{subarray}}^{j-1}\frac{c_{m}-x}{c_{m}-c_{k}}.

Evaluating now at

\displaystyle\eta^{*}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\prod_{m=i}^{j-1}\frac{c_{m}}{c_{m}-c_{i-1}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=i\\

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\prod_{m=i}^{k}\frac{c_{m}}{c_{m-1}-c_{k}}\prod_{m=k+1}^{j-1}\frac{c_{m}}{c_{m}-c_{k}}=-\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\eta_{i,k+1}\eta^{*}_{k+1,j}=-\sum_{k=i+1}^{j}\eta_{i,k}\eta^{*}_{k,j}.

Recalling that
\eta_{i,i}=1
concludes the proof.

\displaystyle=e^{-tc_{n}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(tc_{n})^{m}}{m!}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\bigg(1-\frac{c_{i}}{c_{n}}\bigg)^{m}\prod_{0\leq j\leq n-1\atop j\neq i}\frac{c_{j}}{c_{j}-c_{i}}

\displaystyle=e^{-tc_{n}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}e^{tc_{n}(1-\frac{c_{i}}{c_{n}})}\prod_{0\leq j\leq n-1\atop j\neq i}\frac{c_{j}}{c_{j}-c_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}e^{-tc_{i}}\prod_{0\leq j\leq n-1\atop j\neq i}\frac{c_{j}}{c_{j}-c_{i}}.\qed

We note that the above formulas lead to asymptotic estimates whenever the rates are sufficiently well-behaved.
An example is given in the following result.

Proposition 3.21
.

Suppose that
\sum_{n\geq 0}\tfrac{1}{c_{n}}<\infty
and that
\{c_{n+1}-c_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is an unbounded, nondecreasing sequence.
Then we have the limits

\lim_{n\to\infty}\Delta_{n}(\lfloor c_{n}t\rfloor)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Delta_{n}^{*}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}e^{-tc_{i}}\prod_{j=0\atop j\neq i}^{\infty}\frac{c_{j}}{c_{j}-c_{i}},\qquad t>0.

Moreover,
for
n\geq 0
we have the asymptotic description

\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}e^{-tc_{i}}\prod_{j=0\atop j\neq i}^{\infty}\frac{c_{j}}{c_{j}-c_{i}}=o(e^{-tc_{n}}),\qquad t\to\infty

(23)

Proof.

Fix
t>0
. Let us address the first limit (the second limit can be established similarly).
Using
Theorem

3.20
, the first limit above can be written as

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}(i<n)\bigg(1-\frac{c_{i}}{c_{n}}\bigg)^{\lfloor c_{n}t\rfloor}\prod_{j=0\atop j\neq i}^{n-1}\frac{c_{j}}{c_{j}-c_{i}}\longrightarrow\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}e^{-tc_{i}}\prod_{j=0\atop j\neq i}^{\infty}\frac{c_{j}}{c_{j}-c_{i}}.

(24)

We will establish this using the dominated convergence theorem.
It should be clear that the summands on the right are the limits of those on the left.
Therefore, it only remains to show that the summands on the left are uniformly bounded in
n

by a summable sequence of
i
.

The first two factors can be handled by the inequality
c_{i}<c_{n}
and some standard estimates:

0\leq{\mathbbm{1}(i<n)}\bigg(1-\frac{c_{i}}{c_{n}}\bigg)^{\lfloor c_{n}t\rfloor}\leq\left(e^{-c_{i}/c_{n}}\right)^{\lfloor c_{n}t\rfloor}\leq\left(e^{-c_{i}/c_{n}}\right)^{c_{n}t-1}\leq e^{1-c_{i}t}.

(25)

Next, we consider the product of the
c_{j}/(c_{j}-c_{i})
for
j<i
.
Here, we use the inequality
c_{j}<c_{i}
, the monotonicity of
\{c_{k+1}-c_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
, and the summability of
\{\frac{1}{c_{k}}\}_{k\geq 0}
:

0\leq\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{c_{j}}{c_{i}-c_{j}}\leq\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{c_{j}}{c_{i-j}-c_{0}}=\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{c_{j}}{c_{j}-c_{0}}=\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(1+\frac{c_{0}}{c_{j}-c_{0}}\right)\leq\exp\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{0}}{c_{j}-c_{0}}\bigg)<\infty.

(26)

The remaining terms are the product of the
c_{j}/(c_{j}-c_{i})
for
j>i
.
We claim that

\displaystyle 0\leq\prod_{j=i+1}^{{n-1}}\frac{c_{j}}{c_{j}-c_{i}}\leq\prod_{j=i+1}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac{c_{i}}{c_{j}-c_{i}}\right)\leq\prod_{j=i+1}^{\infty}e^{\frac{c_{i}}{c_{j}-c_{i}}}=\exp\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{i}}{c_{j+i}-c_{i}}\bigg)=\exp(o(c_{i})).

(27)

Since
c_{j}>c_{i}
whenever
j>i
, we only need to justify the limit

\lim_{i\to+\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{c_{j+i}-c_{i}}=0.

(28)

For this, we shall apply the dominated convergence theorem.
The termwise limits are zero since, by hypothesis,
\{c_{k+1}-c_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is unbounded.
To identify a summable bound, we use the monotonicity of
\{c_{k+1}-c_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
and the summability of
\{\frac{1}{c_{k}}\}_{k\geq 0}
:

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{c_{j+i}-c_{i}}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{c_{j}-c_{0}}<\infty.

This establishes (
28
) and thus (
27
).
Combining
Eqs.

25
,
26
and
27
then yields our final bound:

\mathbbm{1}(i<n)\,\bigg(1-\frac{c_{i}}{c_{n}}\bigg)^{\lfloor c_{n}t\rfloor}\,\prod_{j=0\atop j\neq i}^{n-1}\frac{c_{j}}{c_{j}-c_{i}}\leq\exp\big(1-c_{i}t+O(1)+o(c_{i})\big)=e^{-c_{i}(t-o(1))}.

We remark that this bound is indeed independent of
n
.
Moreover, since the

grow at least linearly (

is nondecreasing), this bound is summable in

for any fixed

This verifies that the dominated convergence theorem applies, establishing the limit in
(

).

For the final claim in the proposition, we must show that

\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}e^{-t(c_{i}-c_{n})}\prod_{j=0\atop j\neq i}^{\infty}\frac{c_{j}}{c_{j}-c_{i}}\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}0,\qquad n\geq 0.

Once again, we apply the dominated convergence theorem.
Since
c_{i}>c_{n}
for
i>n
, the termwise limits are each zero.
It thus only remains to identify a suitable bound.
Here, we can reuse the estimates in (

).
We see that

For fixed
n
, this upper bound is independent of
t
and summable in

.
Thus, the dominated convergence theorem applies and (

) is proved.
∎

The scaling limit

In this section, we establish the convergence of our chains to a Feller process.
Our approach continues to be based on the analysis of transition operators.

The limiting space and the extension of functions

We now assume, in addition to

–
(A5)

,
a map

for every

|u|=n\end{subarray}}\frac{(e^{tA_{n}}(f)_{n})(u)}{[h_{\varnothing}]f}\leq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in H^{+}_{n}\\
x\in E\end{subarray}}\frac{(\mathcal{T}(t)f^{o})(x)}{[h_{\varnothing}^{o}]f^{o}}.

We proceed by establishing the reverse inequality.
Since
E
is compact, the points
\{\iota(r_{m})\}_{m\geq 1}
have a subsequential limit point, say
\iota(r_{m_{k}})\to y
.
Using the identities in (
37
), (
36
), and
(S2)
, we find that

\displaystyle k_{n}(y,r_{n})=\lim_{k\to\infty}p^{\downarrow}_{m_{k},n}(r_{m_{k}},r_{n})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbbm{1}(m_{k}\geq n)=1,

and as a result,

(K_{n}g)(y)=g(r_{n})

for any
g
in
C(\mathbb{S}_{n})
.
In particular (recall (
45
)),

(\mathcal{T}(t)f^{o})(y)=(K_{n}e^{tA_{n}}(f)_{n})(y)=(e^{tA_{n}}(f)_{n})(r_{n}),\qquad f\in H_{n}^{+}.

Together with
4.12
(i)
,
3.15
(v)
and
Lemma

3.22
, this implies that

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in H^{+}_{n}\\
x\in E\end{subarray}}\frac{(\mathcal{T}(t)f^{o})(x)}{[h_{\varnothing}^{o}]f^{o}}\leq\inf_{f\in H^{+}_{n}}\frac{(\mathcal{T}(t)f^{o})(y)}{[h_{\varnothing}]f}=\inf_{f\in H^{+}_{n}}\frac{(e^{tA_{n}}(f)_{n})(r_{n})}{\int_{\mathbb{S}_{n}}(f)_{n}dM_{n}}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in C_{+}(\mathbb{S}_{n})\end{subarray}}\frac{(e^{tA_{n}}g)(r_{n})}{\int_{\mathbb{S}_{n}}g\,dM_{n}}.

The desired inequality, and the first claim, now follows from (
30
).

Moving on to the second claim, first observe that, since
K_{n}
is a positive operator,
(
40
) gives us the containment

\{f^{o}\colon f\in H^{+}_{n}\}\subseteq C_{+}(E).

Recalling the first claim and
4.14
43
, we can then write

\Delta_{n}^{*}(t)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in E,\\
f\in H^{+}_{n}\end{subarray}}\bigg(1-\frac{(\mathcal{T}(t)f^{o})(x)}{\int_{E}f^{o}dM}\bigg)\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in E,\\
f\in C_{+}(E)\end{subarray}}\bigg(1-\frac{(\mathcal{T}(t)f)(x)}{\int_{E}fdM}\bigg)=\Delta_{F}(t),\qquad t\geq 0,\,n\geq 0.

On the other hand,
4.3
and
Theorem

2.7
supply us with the inequality

\Delta_{F}(t)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\Delta_{n}^{*}(t),\qquad t\geq 0.

We must therefore have the equality
\Delta_{F}(t)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Delta_{n}^{*}(t)
for any
t\geq 0
.
The monotonicity of this convergence follows from
3.23
.
∎

In this section, we discuss how several up-down chains in the literature fit into our framework.
This will recover many of the main results in

,

Before the main discussion, let us state a lemma that will simplify checking the commutation relation
(C)
.
In words, this result says that it is enough to check
(C)
on the off-diagonal entries – the relation for the diagonal entries will follow automatically.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\{p^{\uparrow}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
and
\{p^{\downarrow}_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be transition matrices, respectively from

\mathbb{S}_{n}
to
\mathbb{S}_{n+1}
and from
\mathbb{S}_{n}
to
\mathbb{S}_{n-1}
.
Assume that for each
n
, there exist
\beta_{n}
in
(0,1)
such that

(p^{\uparrow}_{n}p^{\downarrow}_{n+1})(r,s)=\beta_{n}(p^{\downarrow}_{n}p^{\uparrow}_{n-1})(r,s),\qquad r\neq s.

(47)

Then Assumption
(C)
holds.

Proof.

We need to check that, for all
s
in
\mathbb{S}_{n}
,

(p^{\uparrow}_{n}p^{\downarrow}_{n+1})(s,s)=\beta_{n}(p^{\downarrow}_{n}p^{\uparrow}_{n-1})(s,s)+(1-\beta_{n}).

This follows immediately from (
47
) and the identities

(p^{\uparrow}_{n}p^{\downarrow}_{n+1})(s,s)=1-\sum_{u\neq s}(p^{\uparrow}_{n}p^{\downarrow}_{n+1})(s,u),\qquad(p^{\downarrow}_{n}p^{\uparrow}_{n-1})(s,s)=1-\sum_{u\neq s}(p^{\downarrow}_{n}p^{\uparrow}_{n-1})(s,u).\qed

Borodin–Olshanski chains on partitions

For
n\geq 1
, let
\mathbb{S}_{n}
be the set of integer partitions of
n
(equivalently, Young diagrams with
n
boxes).
The number of standard Young tableaux of shape
\lambda
will be denoted by
\dim(\lambda)
.
We write
\lambda\nearrow\rho
if a partition
\rho
can be obtained from a partition
\lambda
by adding a single box.
If this new box
\rho\backslash\lambda
lies in the
i
-th row and
j
-th column of the diagram of
\rho
, we set
c(\rho\backslash\lambda)=j-i
(this is known as the
content
of the box).

Let
\lambda\,\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
and
\rho\,\in\mathbb{S}_{n+1}
.
The down-steps considered in
[
BO09
]
are given by the formula

p^{\downarrow}_{n+1}(\rho,\lambda)=\begin{cases}\frac{\dim(\lambda)}{\dim(\rho)},&\lambda\nearrow\rho,\\
0,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

The up-steps are given by

p^{\uparrow}_{n}(\lambda,\rho)=\begin{cases}\frac{(z+c(\rho\backslash\lambda))(z^{\prime}+c(\rho\backslash\lambda))}{zz^{\prime}+n}\ \frac{\dim(\rho)}{(n+1)\,\dim(\lambda)},&\lambda\nearrow\rho,\\
0,&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
z
and
z^{\prime}
are two complex parameters chosen
19
19

19

Concretely, they should either be complex conjugates or lie in an interval contained in
\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Z}
.
so that the above quantities are nonnegative and
zz^{\prime}>0
.
In the following result, we show that these transition kernels satisfy a commutation relation (a variant in which the operators are normalized differently can be found in
[
Ful09
, Lemma 5.1]
).

Proposition 5.2
.

The above matrices
(p^{\uparrow}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(p^{\downarrow}_{n})_{n\geq 2}
satisfy Assumption
(C)

with parameter
\beta_{n}=\frac{zz^{\prime}+n-1}{zz^{\prime}+n}\frac{n}{n+1}
(for
n\geq 1
).

Proof.

From
Lemma

5.1
, it suffices to check that for
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
,
\lambda\neq\mu
, we have

(p^{\uparrow}_{n}p^{\downarrow}_{n+1})(\lambda,\mu)=\frac{zz^{\prime}+n-1}{zz^{\prime}+n}\frac{n}{n+1}(p^{\downarrow}_{n}p^{\uparrow}_{n-1})(\lambda,\mu).

(48)

If there are two boxes or more that are in
\lambda
but not in
\mu
, then both sides are equal to
0
and the equality holds trivially.
We can therefore assume that there is exactly one box that is in
\lambda
but not in
\mu
.
Let
\rho=\lambda\cup\mu
and
\tau=\lambda\cap\mu
, where
\lambda
and
\mu
should be viewed as sets of boxes.
Then
\rho
and
\tau
are Young diagrams of size
n+1
and
n-1
, respectively, and

(p^{\uparrow}_{n}p^{\downarrow}_{n+1})(\lambda,\mu)=p^{\uparrow}_{n}(\lambda,\rho)p^{\downarrow}_{n+1}(\rho,\mu)=\frac{(z+c(\rho\backslash\lambda))(z^{\prime}+c(\rho\backslash\lambda))}{zz^{\prime}+n}\frac{\dim(\rho)}{(n+1)\,\dim(\lambda)}\frac{\dim(\mu)}{\dim(\rho)}.

On the other hand,

(p^{\downarrow}_{n}p^{\uparrow}_{n-1})(\lambda,\mu)=p^{\downarrow}_{n}(\lambda,\tau)p^{\uparrow}_{n-1}(\tau,\mu)=\frac{\dim(\tau)}{\dim(\lambda)}\frac{(z+c(\mu\backslash\tau))(z^{\prime}+c(\mu\backslash\tau))}{zz^{\prime}+n-1}\frac{\dim(\mu)}{n\,\dim(\tau)}.

Observing that
\rho\backslash\lambda=\mu\backslash\tau
establishes (
48
) and concludes the proof.
∎

To address Assumption
(A0)
, we simply observe that
\mathbb{S}_{1}
is a singleton.
Notice that this involves a shift of index since the state spaces in this example are indexed by
n\geq 1
, not
n\geq 0
.
It then follows from
3.1
that our chains satisfy the hypotheses
(A0)
–
(A5)
with
c_{n}=(n+1)(zz^{\prime}+n)
.

We now discuss the hypotheses
(S1)
and
(S2)
, which are needed for the results on separation distance.
Under the shift of index,
(S1)
requires two elements
r_{n}
and
s_{n}
in
\mathbb{S}_{n}
that are at distance
n-1
from each other.
This condition is satisfied by taking
r_{n}=(n)
, a one-row diagram, and
s_{n}=(1^{n})
, a one-column diagram:

s_{n}
can be obtained from
r_{n}
by
n-1
up-down steps that create a new part and remove from the largest part each time, and this is the fastest way to obtain
s_{n}
from
r_{n}
since each up-down step can increase the number of parts by at most
1
.
The condition
M_{n}(s_{n})>0
is vacuous here because
M_{n}
has full support (this is a consequence of (
19
) or of its explicit expression given in
[
BO09
]
).
The assumption
(S2)
is clearly satisfied.
The additional hypotheses of
3.21
are also satisfied – namely, the rates satisfy
\sum_{n\geq 1}\tfrac{1}{c_{n}}<\infty
and
\{c_{n+1}-c_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is an unbounded, nondecreasing sequence.

Finally, we discuss our analytic hypotheses.
Again, we follow the work in
[
BO09
]
.
For the limit space, we take the so-called Thoma simplex, the subspace
\Omega\subset[0,1]^{\infty}\times[0,1]^{\infty}
consisting of pairs
(\bm{a},\bm{b})
, where
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
are infinite nonincreasing sequences of
real numbers
such that
\sum(a_{i}+b_{i})\leq 1
.
The topology on
\Omega
is that of pointwise convergence, under which it is a compact metric space.
A partition
\lambda\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
can be seen as an element of
\Omega
via its
modified Frobenius coordinates
– namely
\iota(\lambda)=\frac{1}{n}(\bm{a},\bm{b})
, where

a_{i}=\max\big(\lambda_{i}-i+\tfrac{1}{2},0\big),\qquad b_{i}=\max\big(\lambda^{\prime}_{i}-i+\tfrac{1}{2},0\big).

(49)

We can check that this injection satisfies
(L1)
, i.e. any point
(\bm{a},\bm{b})
in
\Omega
can be approximate by a sequence
of the form
\iota(\lambda_{n})
: indeed, one can construct
\lambda_{n}
so that, for each fixed
i
and
n
sufficiently large,
its
i
-th row (resp. column) has length
\lfloor a_{i}n\rfloor
(resp.
\lfloor b_{i}n\rfloor
) if
a_{i}\neq 0
(resp.
b_{i}\neq 0
), and length
o(n)
if
a_{i}=0
(resp.
b_{i}=0
).

We proceed by considering the density functions.
It can be verified that they are given by

d_{\mu}(\lambda)=\begin{cases}\dim(\lambda/\mu)\tfrac{\dim(\mu)}{\dim(\lambda)},&\mu\subseteq\lambda,\\
0,&\text{else},\end{cases}

(50)

where
\dim(\lambda/\mu)
is the number of standard Young tableaux of skew shape
\lambda/\mu
.
We will also need to consider the action of a power sum symmetric function on a partition – this is given by

p_{k}(\lambda)=\sum_{i\geq 1}a_{i}^{k}+(-1)^{k-1}\sum_{i\geq 1}b_{i}^{k},\qquad k\geq 1,\,\lambda\in\mathbb{S},

(51)

where
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
are defined by (
49
).
This definition can be extended to
F(\lambda)
for any symmetric function
F
, by requiring
that
F\mapsto F(\lambda)
is an algebra morphism.
With this definition, one can prove (see
[
BO09
, eq. (4.1)]
) that, for any
\mu\vdash k
, there exists
a symmetric function
FS_{\mu}
, called Frobenius–Schur function, such that

FS_{\mu}(\lambda)=|\lambda|(|\lambda|-1)\cdots(|\lambda|-k+1)\frac{\dim(\lambda/\mu)}{\dim(\lambda)}

(52)

Moreover
FS_{\mu}
is an inhomogeneous function of degree
\mu
,
and its top degree component is the standard Schur function
s_{\mu}
.

We note that formula (
51
) can be used to define
p_{k}(\omega)

for any
k\geq 2
and any point
\omega
of the Thoma simplex. By convention
p_{1}(\omega)=1
for all
\omega
. Once again, we extend this definition to any
F(\omega)
, where
F
is a symmetric function, by requiring that
F\mapsto F(\omega)
is an algebra morphism.
Then we set
d_{\mu}^{o}(\omega)\hskip 1.0pt\raisebox{0.4pt}{:}\hskip-4.0pt=\dim(\mu)\,s_{\mu}(\omega)
.
We claim that this family of functions satisfies

(L2)

and
(L3)
.

The assumption
(L2)
follows from Stone-Weierstrass theorem (recall that
\Omega
is compact).
The span of
\{d_{\mu}^{o},\mu\in\mathbb{S}\}

is the set of functions
\{\omega\mapsto F(\omega)\}
, where
F
runs over the algebra of symmetric functions. It is thus a subalgebra of
C(\Omega)
.
Moreover, it contains the constant function
\omega\mapsto p_{1}(\omega)=1

and separates point.
Indeed,
p_{k}(\omega)
can be interpreted as the
k-1
-st moment of the discrete probability measure on
[0,1]
, giving weight
a_{i}
to
a_{i}
, weight
b_{i}
to
-b_{i}
, and the remaining weight
1-\sum_{i}a_{i}-\sum_{i}b_{i}
to
0
.
Since bounded probability measures are determined by their moments,

\omega
is determined by the values of
(p_{k}(\omega))_{k\geq 1}
, as claimed.
We conclude that the span of
\{d_{\mu}^{o},\mu\in\mathbb{S}\}
is dense in
C(\Omega)
,
showing
(L2)
.

For the remaining assumption
(L3)
,
we observe that

|p_{k}(\lambda)|\leq\Big(\sum\nolimits_{i}a_{i}+\sum\nolimits_{i}b_{i}\Big)^{k}\leq n^{k}

for any diagram
\lambda
of size
n
.
This implies that, for any symmetric function
F
,
one has the bound
F(\lambda)=O(|\lambda|^{\deg(F)})
uniformly in
\lambda
(the constant in the
O
symbol however depends on
F
). Since the top-homogeneous part of
FS_{\mu}
is
s_{\mu}
, we get

\big|FS_{\mu}(\lambda)-s_{\mu}(\lambda)\big|=O(|\lambda|^{|\mu|-1})

Dividing by
|\lambda|(|\lambda|-1)\cdots(|\lambda|-|\mu|+1)=|\lambda|^{|\mu|}(1+O(|\lambda|^{-1}))
, and using (
52
), we have

\left|\frac{\dim(\lambda/\mu)}{\dim(\lambda)}-\frac{s_{\mu}(\lambda)}{|\lambda|^{|\mu|}}\right|=O(|\lambda|^{-1}).

But

\frac{s_{\mu}(\lambda)}{|\lambda|^{|\mu|}}=s_{\mu}(\iota(\lambda))=\frac{1}{\dim(\mu)}d_{\mu}^{o}(\iota(\lambda)),

while
\frac{\dim(\lambda/\mu)}{\dim(\lambda)}=\frac{1}{\dim(\mu)}d_{\mu}(\lambda)
(see (
50
)).
Since all error terms are uniform in
\lambda
, this proves
(L3)
.

Therefore, all results of
Section

3
apply and we recover the results from
[
Ful09
]
on the separation distance of this up-down chain.

To sum up, all hypotheses

,
(C)

(S1)
-

and

Olshanski’s generalization through Jack polynomials

The chain in the previous section can be generalized, introducing a parameter
\theta

linked to Jack polynomials. We follow here the paper
[
Ols10
]

and use its notation.

In particular, if
\lambda
is a partition and
\theta
a positive real number,

P^{\theta}_{\lambda}
denotes the Jack symmetric function indexed by
\lambda
of parameter
\theta
. It is a homogeneous symmetric function of degree
|\lambda|
and they form a basis of the symmetric function algebra.
The Pieri rule states that, for any partition
\mu
,

P^{\theta}_{\mu}\,p_{1}=\sum_{\lambda}\kappa^{\theta}(\mu,\lambda)P^{\theta}_{\lambda},

where the sum runs over partitions
\lambda
obtained by adding one box to
\mu
(i.e.
\mu\nearrow\lambda
) and
\kappa^{\theta}(\mu,\lambda)
are some combinatorial quantities whose precise definition is irrelevant here.
Using the coefficients
\kappa^{\theta}(\mu,\lambda)
, we can introduce a
\theta
-deformation of dimensions as follows: fixing
\mu
, we define
\dim^{\theta}(\lambda/\mu)
recursively on
\lambda
by

\dim^{\theta}(\lambda/\mu)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\lambda\not\supseteq\mu;\\
1&\text{ if }\lambda=\mu;\\
\sum_{\tau\nearrow\lambda}\dim^{\theta}(\tau/\mu)\kappa^{\theta}(\tau,\lambda)&\text{ if }\lambda\supsetneq\mu.\end{cases}

We simply write
\dim^{\theta}(\lambda)
for
\dim^{\theta}(\lambda/\varnothing)
,
where
\varnothing
is the empty partition. For
\theta=1
, this coincides with the usual
dimension of (skew) Young diagrams.
Still following Olshanski
[
Ols10
]
, we can now define a down transition matrix:
for
\rho\vdash n+1
and
\lambda\vdash n
,

p^{\downarrow,\theta}_{n+1}(\rho,\lambda)=\begin{cases}\frac{\dim^{\theta}(\lambda)\kappa^{\theta}(\tau,\lambda)}{\dim^{\theta}(\rho)}&\text{ if }\lambda\nearrow\rho,\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(53)

which will

Given a Young diagram
\rho

, but height
\theta

These are called the (
\theta

Composition chains arising from the Chinese restaurant process

In the up-down chains considered in
[
RLR23
]
, the state space
\mathbb{S}_{n}
consists of integer compositions of
n
.
A composition of
n\geq 1
is a tuple
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{k})
of positive integers that sum to
n
.
It will be helpful to think of these objects in terms of their associated box diagrams.
The diagram for a composition
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
contains
n
boxes arranged into columns so that there are
\sigma_{j}
boxes in the
j

\sigma

The down-steps considered in these chains delete a box uniformly at random from a composition.
If this box lied below other boxes, those boxes are shifted down so that the resulting diagram still describes a composition.

The up-steps in these chains add a box to a composition and depend on two parameters
(\alpha,\theta)
satisfying
\theta\geq 0
,
0\leq\alpha<1
, and
\alpha+\theta>0

•

create a one-box column to the left of the first column with probability
\frac{\theta}{n+\theta}
,
and

•

create a one-box column immediately to the right of a given column with probability
\frac{\alpha}{n+\theta}
.

These up-steps are inspired by an ordered variant of the Chinese restaurant process introduced in
[
PW09
]
.
The resulting up-down chains can thus be viewed as ordered variants of the chains in
Section

5.3
.

Up-down chains arising from Kerov’s operators

The paper
[
Pet13
]
does not focus on a specific example of up-down chains but is concerned with a certain class of up-down chains that follow a general construction.
The starting point for this construction is a branching graph, a graph
\mathbb{G}=\bigsqcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{G}_{n}
in which

•

each
\mathbb{G}_{n}
is finite,

•

\mathbb{G}_{0}
is a single vertex, denoted by
\varnothing
,

•

each edge is directed and moves from some
\mathbb{G}_{n}
to
\mathbb{G}_{n+1}
,

•

each vertex has an outgoing edge,
and

•

each vertex has an incoming edge (except for
\varnothing
).

The notation
\mu\nearrow\lambda
or
\lambda\searrow\mu
is used to denote that there is an edge from
\mu
to
\lambda
.

The graph
\mathbb{G}
is to be equipped with an edge multiplicity function
\kappa
that assigns a positive number
\kappa(\mu,\lambda)
to the edge from
\mu
to
\lambda
.
This function induces a weight on each path in
\mathbb{G}
, given by the product of the multiplicities of its edges.
These weights then lead to the relative combinatorial dimension
\dim(\mu,\lambda)
, defined as the sum of the weights of all of the paths from
\mu
to
\lambda
. The special case
\mu=\varnothing
results in the combinatorial dimension
\dim\lambda\hskip 1.0pt\raisebox{0.4pt}{:}\hskip-4.0pt=\dim(\varnothing,\lambda)

, respectively.
Summing over all of the equiprobable cases, we obtain

(p^{\uparrow}_{n}p^{\downarrow}_{n+1})(\sigma,\tau)=\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}\frac{1}{n(n+1)}\left(p\mathbbm{1}\big(\tau=\sigma_{\nearrow i}^{\setminus j}\big)+(1-p)\mathbbm{1}\big(\tau=\sigma_{\searrow i}^{\setminus j}\big)\right).

We will compute
(p^{\downarrow}_{n}p^{\uparrow}_{n-1})(\sigma,\tau)
similarly.
A down-up step from
\sigma
involves
removing a uniformly random point in
\sigma

and inflating a uniformly random point in the resulting permutation.
The point that we inflate can immediately be viewed as a point in
\sigma
that is different from the point that we remove.
Let us consider the case when
(i,\sigma(i))
and
(j,\sigma(j))
are the distinct points in
\sigma
that the down-up step will inflate and remove, respectively.
Observe that the final permutation is not affected by the order in which these operations occur.
In particular, its distribution is the same as it was in the above up-down case.
We can therefore proceed as before, modifying only the probability of choosing each pair
(i,j)
, to obtain

{(p^{\downarrow}_{n}p^{\uparrow}_{n-1})(\sigma,\tau)}=\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\left(p\mathbbm{1}\big(\tau=\sigma_{\nearrow i}^{\setminus j}\big)+(1-p)\mathbbm{1}\big(\tau=\sigma_{\searrow i}^{\setminus j}\big)\right).

Comparing the two identities above, we find that the commutation relation holds on the off-diagonal.
Applying
Lemma

5.1
then establishes (
67
), and the second claim follows immediately from
3.1
.
Notice that the rates

c_{n}=n(n+1)

indeed satisfy
\frac{c_{n-1}}{c_{n}}=\beta_{n}=\frac{n-1}{n+1}
.
∎

6.3.2
Asymptotics of pattern densities

We now consider

Here, we are primarily interested in pattern densities, which play an important role.
The estimate provided by our theory is as follows:

\bigg|\mathbb{E}\left[d_{\pi}\big(X_{n}(m)\big)\right]-M_{|\pi|}(\pi)\bigg|\leq B_{\pi}\Big(1-\tfrac{j(j-1)}{n(n+1)}\Big)^{m},\qquad n\geq|\pi|,\,m\geq 0,

We remark that the parameter

For this, we introduce some terminology.
Two elements which are consecutive both in position

Replacing these two elements by a single one leads to a smaller permutation – this operation will be referred to as

This is the reverse of the inflation operation used in the up-step.
Finally, we introduce the

An example is given in Figure

A permutation is called

Separable permutations are standard objects in the literature

Lemma 6.3

If

Proof.

The claims regarding the stationary distributions follow immediately from the description in (

To address the other claims, let us recall that the

can be found in

where

Since

When

Therefore, in this case,

Let us suppose that

(\varphi^{X_{0},\varepsilon},\varphi^{Y_{0},\varepsilon})_{\#}(\mu)
. Since
\varphi^{X_{0},\varepsilon}
and
\varphi^{Y_{0},\varepsilon}
are increasing functions, the map
(\varphi^{X_{0},\varepsilon},\varphi^{Y_{0},\varepsilon})
does not change the relative order of the coordinates,
and the probability that
(X_{1},Y_{1})
, …,
(X_{k},Y_{k})
form the pattern
\pi
is the same as for
i.i.d. random variable of law
\mu
.
Hence the probability that
M=0
and
(X_{1},Y_{1})
, …,
(X_{k},Y_{k})
form the pattern
\pi

is given by
(1-\varepsilon)^{k}d_{\pi}^{o}(\mu)
.

We now consider the case
M=m
(
1\leq m\leq k
) and
B=\nearrow
.
This happens with probability
p\binom{k}{m}(1-\varepsilon)^{k-m}\varepsilon^{m}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
(X_{1},Y_{1})
, …,
(X_{k-m},Y_{k-m})
have
law
(\varphi^{X_{0},\varepsilon},\varphi^{Y_{0},\varepsilon})_{\#}(\mu)
and
(X_{k-m+1},Y_{k-m+1})
, …,
(X_{k},Y_{k})

have law
\delta^{(\varepsilon),B}_{(X_{0},Y_{0})}
. By construction,
these last points are consecutive in values and in positions in the sample
(X_{1},Y_{1})
,…,
(X_{k},Y_{k})
,
and are in increasing order (since
B=\nearrow
).
Moreover they have the same place as
(X_{0},Y_{0})
in the sample
(X_{0},Y_{0})
,
(X_{1},Y_{1})
, …,
(X_{m-k},Y_{m-k})
.
Hence
(X_{1},Y_{1})
, …,
(X_{k},Y_{k})
form the pattern
\pi
if and only if,
for some
(\tau,i)
in
\mathcal{I}_{m}(\pi)
, the points

(X_{0},Y_{0})
,
(X_{1},Y_{1})
, …,
(X_{m-k},Y_{m-k})
form the pattern
\tau
and
(X_{0},Y_{0})
is the

i
-th point from the left in this sample.
For a given
(\tau,i)
, the latter happens with probability
\frac{d_{\tau}^{o}(\mu)}{k-m+1}
.
Summing over all
(\tau,i)
in
\mathcal{I}_{m}(\pi)
, we get that the probability that
M=m
,
B=\nearrow
and

(X_{1},Y_{1})
, …,
(X_{k},Y_{k})
form the pattern
\pi
is

p\binom{k}{m}\frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{k-m}\varepsilon^{m}}{k-m+1}\left(\sum_{(\tau,i)\in\mathcal{I}_{m}(\pi)}d_{\tau}^{o}(\mu)\right).

The case
B=\searrow
is similar, replacing
p
by
1-p
and
\mathcal{I}_{m}(\pi)
by
\mathcal{D}_{m}(\pi)
.
Summing the various contributions, we get the formula in the lemma.
∎

Proof of

.

Eq.

72
implies that the space
\mathcal{H}=\linearSpan\{d_{\pi}\}_{\pi\in\mathbb{S}}
is invariant
by the generator
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}

(Assumption
(L2)
),

2.2
tells us that
\mathcal{H}
is a core for
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}
.
On the other hand, we know from

, item
(iv)

that
\mathcal{H}
is a core for the generator
\mathcal{A}
of
F
.

Let
\pi
be a pattern of size
k
. Our next goal is to prove that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}d^{o}_{\pi}=\mathcal{A}d^{o}_{\pi}.

(73)

The quantity

appearing in

•

The constant term in (

•

Let us look at linear terms in

) gives

In the sums, only the summands corresponding to

•

We now consider the quadratic terms in

in the right-hand side of (

).

A graph example

Subgraph densities and graphons

In this section, for
n\geq 1
,
we let
\mathcal{G}_{n}
be the set of unlabelled simple undirected graphs on
n
vertices.
If
G
is a graph with vertex set
V
and
I
a subset of
V
,
we let
G[I]
be the induced subgraph of
G
on
I
,
that is the graph with vertex set
I
containing the edges of
G

with both extremities in
I
.
A special case of interest is when
I=V\setminus\{v\}
for some vertex
v
:
then
G[I]
is simply obtained by erasing the vertex
v
and all incident edges.

Erasing a uniform random vertex
v
in a graph
G
defines a kernel
p^{\downarrow}

from
\mathcal{G}_{n}
to
\mathcal{G}_{n-1}
.
With this definition, if
H
and
G
are graphs of size
k
and
n
respectively, we have

d_{H}(G)=p^{\downarrow}_{n,k}(G,H)=\frac{\occ(H,G)}{\binom{n}{k}},\qquad|V_{G}|=n\geq k=|V_{H}|,

where
\occ(H,G)
is the number of subsets
I
of
V_{G}
such that
G[I]
is isomorphic
to
H
. The quantity
d_{H}(G)
is usually referred to as the
induced
subgraph density
of
H
in
G
.

These (induced) subgraph densities play a central role in the theory of graph limits,
also known as graphons.
A
graphon
is a symmetric function from
[0,1]^{2}
to
[0,1]
.
A graph
G
can be seen as a graphon
W_{G}
by considering its rescaled adjacency matrices, i.e.

W_{G}(x,y)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if $\{\lceil nx\rceil,\lceil ny\rceil\}$ is an edge of $G$;}\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

The function
W_{G}
depends on the labeling of the vertices of
G
by numbers from
1

to
n
, but we usually consider graphons up to some equivalence relation.
Convergence of graphons is defined via a pseudo-metric, call box distance,
whose definition is irrelevant here.
It turns out that induced subgraph densities can be extended to the space of graphons
and that the convergence for the box metric is equivalent to convergence of all subgraph densities.
A detailed introduction to graphon theory can be found in the book of Lovász
[
Lov12
]
.
In particular Assumptions
(L1)
–
(L3)

are well-known to hold for the space of graphons.

Up-kernel, commutation and consequences

As for permutations, we also define an up-kernel
p^{\uparrow}_{n}
from
\mathcal{G}_{n}
to
\mathcal{G}_{n+1}
,
where
p^{\uparrow}_{n}(G,G^{\prime})
is the probability of obtaining
G^{\prime}
starting from
G
by
choosing a uniform random vertex of
G
,
creating a copy of it with the same neighbourhood
and connecting the selected vertex and its copy with probability
1-p
.
Then we have the following commutation relation, whose proof is identical to that of
6.1
.

Proposition 7.1
.

The above kernels satisfy the following commutation relations: for
n\geq 2

p^{\uparrow}_{n}p^{\downarrow}_{n+1}=\frac{n-1}{n+1}\,p^{\downarrow}_{n}p^{\uparrow}_{n-1}+\frac{2}{n+1}\,\delta_{n},

(74)

where
\delta_{n}
denotes the identity kernel on
\mathcal{G}_{n}
.
Consequently, the up-down transition operator
T_{n}

satisfies Assumptions
(A1)
–
(A5)
, with

c_{n}=n(n+1)
(for
n\geq 1
).

The results announced in the introduction (the scaling limit of the up-down chains, the continuity of the limit in
Theorem

1.3
,
the exact formula and asymptotics estimates for the separation distance in
1.4
) follow immediately.

Remark 7.2
.

Consider the map
I_{n}:\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to\mathcal{G}_{n}
associating with a permutation
\sigma

its inversion graph
G(\sigma)
(recall that
G(\sigma)
has vertex-set
\{1,\dots,n\}
)
and contains the edge
\{i,j\}
if
\{i,j\}
is an inversion of
\sigma
, i.e. if
(i-j)(\sigma(i)-\sigma(j))<0
.
As mentioned in the introduction, we can easily check that the up and down transition operators on graphs and permutations
are compatible with the inversion graph map in the following sense: for
n\geq 1
,

p^{\uparrow}_{\mathcal{G},n}\circ I_{n}=I_{n+1}\circ p^{\uparrow}_{\mathfrak{S},n},\quad p^{\downarrow}_{\mathcal{G},n+1}\circ I_{n+1}=I_{n}\circ p^{\downarrow}_{\mathfrak{S},n+1},

where we added subscripts
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathfrak{S}
to distinguish the kernels on graphs and permutations.
Hence it is not surprising that both
(p^{\uparrow}_{\mathfrak{S}},p^{\downarrow}_{\mathfrak{S}})
and
(p^{\uparrow}_{\mathcal{G}},p^{\downarrow}_{\mathcal{G}})
satisfy
the same commutation relation (with the same coefficients).
However, none of
6.1
or
7.1
implies the other,
since
I_{n}
is neither surjective, nor injective.
Also, the graph part
of
Theorem

1.3
does not imply the permutation part,
and vice-versa.
