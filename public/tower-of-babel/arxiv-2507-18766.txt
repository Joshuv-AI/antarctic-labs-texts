Variational principles on the space of Lorenz curves: Gradient structures and isometries inspired by Wasserstein geometry Thanks: The author received support from the NDSEG fellowship.
David W. Cohen

Abstract
. We motivate and derive novel Riemannian gradient structures on the space of Lorenz curves, which preserve infinite-dimensional variational principles inherited from Fokker-Planck equations via the lens of Wasserstein geometry and its variants. We also prove isometry results between corresponding formal manifolds of probability measures and Lorenz curves, which suggest meaningful metrics on the space of Lorenz curves when an underlying kinetic premise is present. In so doing, elegant variational principles are imbued upon highly nonlinear and nonlocal integro-differential evolution equations resulting from a recently derived variable transformation of McKean-Vlasov Fokker-Planck equations.

For a positive probability density
\rho

\mathbb{R}^{+}

Lorenz curve

\left(\int_{0}^{x}\,dy\,\rho(y),\int_{0}^{x}\,dy\,\rho(y)y\right)

By the monotonicity of the cumulative distribution function (cdf) for a positive probability density, the Lorenz curve

Thus for an equation of motion for a wealth distribution, there is an intrinsic time-dependent evolution equation of the associated Lorenz curves. Such a transform was derived for the first time in

Consider the McKean-Vlasov Fokker-Planck equation

(1.1)

\frac{\partial\rho(x,t)}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[\Sigma[x,t,\rho(x,t)]\rho(x,t)\right]+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\left[D[x,t,\rho(x,t)]\rho(x,t)\right],

where
\Sigma:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathcal{P}_{+,ac}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the drift coefficient and
D:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathcal{P}_{+,ac}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the diffusion coefficient. It was shown that the transformed dynamics of the Lorenz curve of
\rho(x,t)
, denoted
\mathcal{L}:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, obey

(1.2)

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(f,t)}{\partial t}=-\frac{\widetilde{D}[f,t,\mathcal{L}]}{\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}(f,t)}{\partial f^{2}}}+\int_{0}^{f}\,dg\,\widetilde{\Sigma}[g,t,\mathcal{L}],

where

\widetilde{\Sigma}[f,t,\mathcal{L}]\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\Sigma\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(f,t)}{\partial f},t,\left(\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}(f,t)}{\partial f^{2}}\right)^{-1}\right]

and

\widetilde{D}[f,t,\mathcal{L}]\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=D\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(f,t)}{\partial f},t,\left(\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}(f,t)}{\partial f^{2}}\right)^{-1}\right].

The equations governing the evolution in the space of Lorenz curves are nonlinear, nonlocal, and integro-differential even when the associated dynamics in the space of probability measures are linear, local, and only differential. It then appears folly to transform an evolutionary system into a facially worse set of variables. Below we set out the motivation in spite of this appearance.

1.1.
Motivation for pursuing the transformation

The application of techniques from kinetic theory and mean-field theory to idealized, stochastic economic models has yielded exciting results over the past three decades – for example, see
[
2
,
8
,
11
]
and the citations within. It has become known that a broad class of econophysics models exhibit dynamics that asymptotically limit to the state of total oligarchy
[
3
,
5
]
. That is, a state in which almost all of the probability mass is concentrated ever closer to zero wealth and a vanishingly small amount of probability mass (colloquially, the oligarch) is sent off to unimaginably large wealth.

These solutions look roughly like

\rho_{\epsilon}(w)=\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}\delta(w-\epsilon)+\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}\delta\left(w-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)

as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
.

A primary difficulty of this critical phenomenon is that the oligarch object

(1.3)

``\Xi(w)=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}\delta\left(w-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)"

does not fit cleanly within the classical theory of weak solution of PDE via Sobolev-Schwartz theory. This hinders the analysis of the evolution equations for the distributions of wealth.

Studying the evolution of the Lorenz curve, rather than the population distribution over wealth space
\mathbb{R}^{+}
, may alleviate these difficulties; since, in the Lorenz curve framework a partial or total oligarchy becomes a simple jump discontinuity at
f=1
. Therefore there is promise for understanding the solutions to the evolution equations of the Lorenz curves using the classical theory of weak solutions.

Therefore, while the evolution equations of
eq.

1.2
do not appear
prima facie
to be more pleasant, our motivation is to avoid dealing with the “oligarch distribution” of
eq.

1.3
represented as population density in wealth space.

In spite of the Lorenz equations’ apparent nastiness, our main results provide novel differential geometric understandings of the space of Lorenz curves that imbue infinite-dimensional variational principles to the
\mathcal{L}
-dynamics associated to common gradient flows in the space of probability measures.

Namely, we show that formally viewing the space of Lorenz curves as an infinite-dimensional Riemannian manifold preserves gradient structures arising from
2
-Wasserstein theory and its variants. The variational structure of Fokker-Planck equations is not lost in the intrinsic Lorenz dynamics, and a curve of steepest ascent in the space of probability measures remains so once transformed to the space of Lorenz curves when viewed using a “compatible” geometry, considered here for the first time.

Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation

The space of positive probability distributions defined over a set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
that are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure will be denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{+,ac}\left(\Omega\right).
The subset of these with finite second moment is
\mathcal{P}_{2,+,ac}\left(\Omega\right)
.

The Lorenz curve of
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{+,ac}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
is
\mathbf{L}[\rho]
. Therefore
\mathbf{L}[\rho]
is a map from
[0,1]
to
\mathbb{R}
. Given a Lorenz curve,
\mathcal{L}
, the positive probability density
\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{L}]\in\mathcal{P}_{+,ac}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
satisfies
\left(\mathbf{L}\circ\mathbf{P}\right)[\mathcal{L}]=\mathcal{L}
. The cumulative distribution function (cdf) of a density
\rho
will be denoted by
C[\rho]
and the inverse cdf by
G[\rho].

Where
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is an interval,
\mathfrak{L}\left(I\right)=\left\{\mathbf{L}[\rho]\,:\,\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{+,ac}\left(I\right)\right\}
. That is,
\mathfrak{L}\left(I\right)
is the set of Lorenz curves over the base space
I
, an interval of
\mathbb{R}
.

When there is a formal infinite-dimensional Riemannian manifold structure over some subset of
\mathcal{P}_{+,ac}\left(I\right)
, such as
W_{2}(I)
, and we are defining a
compatible
2
2

2

This notion is made clear later.
metric structure over the set
\mathfrak{L}\left(I\right)
, we will refer to resulting infinite-dimensional Riemannian manifold of Lorenz curves by, for example,
\mathfrak{L}_{W_{2}}
. Often the subset
I
of
\mathbb{R}
will be omitted for notational clarity. The four considered here are:
\mathfrak{L}_{W_{2}}
,
\mathfrak{L}_{W_{2,M}}
,
\mathfrak{L}_{{\mathcal{C}_{\rho}}}
, and
\mathfrak{L}_{{\mathcal{C}_{D}}}
.

Let
\rho(x,t):\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
be such that for each
t
,
\rho(\cdot,t)\in\mathcal{P}_{+,ac}\left(\Omega\right).
That is,
\rho(\cdot,t)
is a time-parameterized curve through the space of positive probability distributions. Whenever there is a time-parameterized curve through a space of functions, we let
\rho_{t}(x)=\rho(x,t).

The subscript
t
is not a time derivative.

The shorthand for a time derivative will be a centered dot above a time-dependent quantity, such as
\dot{\rho}_{t}(x).

For a functional
F:\mathcal{P}_{+,ac}\left(I\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we always use
\widetilde{F}:\mathfrak{L}\left(I\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
to denote the functional such that

F[\rho]=\left(\widetilde{F}\circ\mathbf{L}\right)[\rho]

\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{+,ac}\left(I\right)

\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{+,ac}\left(I\right)

\widetilde{F}

\mathbf{L}[\rho]\in\mathfrak{L}\left(I\right).

Riemannian structures will be endowed upon the spaces of probability measures and Lorenz curves. Let
\mathcal{M}
be a manifold and
T_{u}\mathcal{M}
be the tangent space at
u\in\mathcal{M}
then for

, the Riemannian metric tensor is expressed as
\langle h_{1},h_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{M},u}.

The burdensome writing of the manifold in question as a subscript to the inner product is necessary in this paper as there will be several variations of similar spaces of functions. This will help us keep track. If there are two broadly similar Riemannian spaces
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\beta}
, which are “variations on a theme,” we may denote the metric tensors by
\langle\,,\,\rangle_{\mathcal{M},\alpha,u}
and
\langle\,,\,\rangle_{\mathcal{M},\beta,u}
to avoid multiple levels of subscripts.

The canonical
L^{2}(\Omega)
inner product will be indicated by
(h_{1},h_{2})
.

For a functional
F:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the gradient of
F
at
\hat{u}_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
is

\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\left(F\circ u\right)(t)=\left\langle\text{grad}_{\mathcal{M}}F[\hat{u}_{o}],\frac{\partial u_{t}}{\partial t}\bigg|_{t=0}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{M},\hat{u}_{0}}

for all smooth curves
u_{t}:[-T,T]\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
passing through
\hat{u}_{0}
at
t=0
.

When the manifold is a space of functions over a base space
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we can define the canonical differential, the Fréchet derivative of a functional when it exists, to be

\left(\frac{\delta F}{\delta u_{t}}[u_{0}],\frac{\partial u_{t}}{\partial t}\bigg|_{t=0}\right)=\int_{\Omega}\,dx\,\left(\frac{\delta F}{\delta u_{t}}[u_{0}](x)\right)\left(\frac{\partial u_{t}}{\partial t}\bigg|_{t=0}(x)\right).

3.
2-Wasserstein gradient flows

The use of 2-Wasserstein,
W_{2}
, theory and optimal transport to view evolution equations as the gradient flows of meaningful energy functionals began around the turn of the twenty-first century with the two papers
[
10
,
16
]
. For a broader review of the theory of optimal transport and its many applications, see
[
1
,
9
]
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a measurable, convex set with smooth boundary.
W_{2}(\Omega)
is a metric space and a formal infinite-dimensional Riemannian manifold over the set

\mathcal{P}_{2,+,ac}\left(\Omega\right)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\left\{\rho\in\mathcal{P}\left(\Omega\right)\,:\,\text{a.c. w.r.t. Lebesgue and }\int_{\Omega}\,dx\,\rho(x)|x|^{2}<\infty\right\}.

The metric tensor at
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{2,+,ac}\left(\Omega\right)
is

(3.1)

\left\langle h_{1},h_{2}\right\rangle_{W_{2},\rho}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\int_{\Omega}\,dx\,\rho(x)\nabla\psi_{1}\cdot\nabla\psi_{2}\text{ where for }i=1,2\,\begin{cases}\text{div}(\rho\nabla\psi_{i})=-h_{i}&\text{in }\Omega\\
\frac{\partial\psi_{i}}{\partial\nu}=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where the tangent vectors
h_{i}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are measurable and mean zero, in the sense that
\int_{\Omega}\,dx\,h_{i}(x)=0.

The metric tensor,
\left\langle\,,\,\right\rangle_{W_{2},\rho}
, induces a gradient structure such that if
F:\mathcal{P}_{2,+,ac}\left(\Omega\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is smooth then

\text{grad}_{W_{2},\rho}F[\rho]=-\text{div}\left(\rho\nabla\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho}\right).

Following
[
12
]
, we define the Onsager operator,
\mathbb{K}_{\text{Otto},\rho}:T^{*}_{\rho}W_{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow T_{\rho}W_{2}(\Omega)
to be

(3.2)

\mathbb{K}_{\text{Otto},\rho}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=-\text{div}(\rho\nabla[\cdot]).

The Onsager operator is a nice way to present the gradient structure of manifolds such as these.

Consider a Fokker-Planck equation

(3.3)

\frac{\partial\rho_{t}}{\partial t}=\text{grad}_{W_{2},\rho_{t}}F[\rho_{t}]

generated by the 2-Wasserstein gradient flow of a functional
F:\mathcal{P}_{2,+,ac}\left(I\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

with the metric structure of

induced by the metric tensor in the space of Lorenz curve generates the same time-evolution as the dynamics given by carrying out the

variable transformation of
eq.

3.3

Said another way, a metric tensor on the space of Lorenz curves will be considered compatible if the connection between the lower left and right equations in

We quickly review key results from

that are used throughout this paper. The review of those previous results will be done using the notation of the present paper.

Let
\rho_{t}(x)
be a smooth time-parameterized curve in
\mathcal{P}_{+,ac}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
. The cumulative distribution function (cdf) of the density
\rho_{t}(x)
is
C[\rho_{t}](x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\,dy\,\rho_{t}(y)
and its inverse is
G[\rho_{t}](f)
for
f\in[0,1]
. For all
x\in\mathbb{R},

\left(G[\rho_{t}]\circ C[\rho_{t}]\right)(x)=x.

An integral over
x\in\mathbb{R}
can be transformed into an integral over the cdf variable
f\in[0,1]
by way of the variable transformation
f:=C[\rho_{t}](x)
such that

df=\rho_{t}(x)dx\quad\text{and}\quad dx=\frac{1}{\left(\rho_{t}\circ G[\rho_{t}]\right)(f)}df.

In this manner integrals transform as

(A.1)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,dx\,\rho_{t}(x)Q(x,t)=\int_{0}^{1}\,df\,Q(G[\rho_{t}](f),t)

and

(A.2)

\int_{0}^{1}\,df\,R(f,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,dx\,\rho_{t}(x)R(C[\rho_{t}](x),t).

Moreover differentials transform as

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}=\left(\rho_{t}\circ G[\rho_{t}]\right)(f)\frac{\partial}{\partial f}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial f}=\frac{1}{\rho_{t}(x)}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}.

These relations will be repeatedly used through the derivations in the main body of the paper.

The Lorenz curve of a positive probability density
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{+,ac}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
is defined as
\mathcal{L}(f)=(L\circ G[\rho])(f)
where
L(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\,dy\,y\rho(y).
We will denote this by
\mathbf{L}[\rho]=\mathcal{L}
, and the density associated to a Lorenz curve is
\mathbf{P}[\mathcal{L}].

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a Lorenz curve and
\rho,
the associated density. The spatial derivative of a Lorenz curve in the cdf variable

(A.3)

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(f)}{\partial f}=G[\rho](f).

This is easily seen by transforming the differential as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(f)}{\partial f}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{1}{\rho(x)}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}L(x)\right]_{x=G[\rho](f)}

\displaystyle=x|_{G[\rho](f)}.

Informally this indicates that the spatial variable
x
may be replaced by
\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{df}
, with the appropriately transformed arguments.

The second spatial derivative of a Lorenz curve is the reciprocal of the density,

(A.4)

\frac{d^{2}\mathcal{L}(f)}{d{f}^{2}}\equiv\frac{dG[\rho](f)}{df}=\frac{1}{\left(\rho\circ G[\rho]\right)(f)}.

This follows from the inverse function theorem, the positivity of
\rho

This implies the formal replacement of
\rho
with

in time and re-arranging to obtain
