Asymptotics of Fredholm determinant solutions of the noncommutative Painlevé II equation
Jia-Hao Du, Shuai-Xia Xu, Yu-Qiu Zhao

Abstract




Abstract

In this paper, we study the asymptotic behavior of a family of pole-free solutions to the noncommutative Painlevé II equation. These particular solutions can be expressed in terms of the Fredholm determinant of the matrix version of the classical Airy operator, which are analogous to the Hastings-McLeod solution and the Ablowitz-Segur solution of the classical Painlevé II equation.
Using the Riemann-Hilbert approach,
we derive the asymptotics of the Fredholm determinant and the associated particular solutions
\beta(\vec{s})
to the noncommutative Painlevé II equation in the regime
\vec{s}=\left(s+\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{-s}},s-\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{-s}}\right)
with
\tau\geq 0
and
s\to-\infty
. The solutions depend on a two by two Hermitian matrix with eigenvalues in the interval
(-1,1)
.
The asymptotics are expressed in terms of one parameter family of special solutions of the classical Painlevé V equation. Furthermore, we derive the asymptotics, including the connection formulas, for this one parameter family of solutions of the Painlevé V equation both as
ix\to-\infty
and
x\to 0
.

Keywords and phrases:

Asymptotic analysis; Fredholm determinants; noncommutative Painlevé II equation; Painlevé V equation; Riemann-Hilbert problems; Deift-Zhou method.

1
Introduction and statement of results

In random matrix theory, a fundamental problem is to determine the limiting distributions of the eigenvalues of large random matrices.
Many studies have revealed the universality
[
2
,
13
,
14
,
16
,
19
,
20
,
25
]
of these limiting distributions, among which the Tracy-Widom distribution functions play important roles. Consider Dyson’s
\beta
-ensemble with
\beta>0

[
25
]
, which
can be defined as a Coulomb gas of
N
charged particles in the space of one dimension. The locations of these
N
particles
\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<...<\lambda_{N}
have the following probability density function

p(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{N})d\lambda_{1}\cdots d\lambda_{N}=\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}|\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}|^{\beta}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\sum_{j=1}^{N}V(\lambda_{j})}d\lambda_{1}\cdots d\lambda_{N},

(1.1)

where
Z_{N}
is a normalization constant, and the external field
V(\lambda)
is assumed to be Gaussian, namely,
V(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}
. For
\beta=1,~2,~4
, the
\beta
-ensembles are known as Gaussian orthogonal, Gaussian unitary and Gaussian symplectic ensembles, respectively.
For
\beta>0
, the Tracy-Widom distribution functions
F_{\beta}(t)
describe the edge fluctuations of these eigenvalues in the large
N
limit
[
25
]

F_{\beta}(t)=\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\mathrm{Prob}\left((\lambda_{N}-\sqrt{2N})2^{\frac{1}{2}}N^{\frac{1}{6}}\leq t\right).

(1.2)

For
\beta=1,~2,~4
, the distribution functions
F_{\beta}(t)
become the classical Tracy-Widom distributions
[
37
]
. They can be represented explicitly both as the Fredholm determinant of the Airy kernel and integral of the Hastings-McLeod solution of the Painlevé II equation.
Taking
\beta=2
for example, we denote by
\mathbb{K}_{\mathrm{Ai},t}
the trace operator acting on
L^{2}((t,\infty),\mathbb{R})
with the Airy kernel

K_{Airy}(x,y)=\frac{{\rm Ai}(x){\rm Ai}^{\prime}(y)-{\rm Ai}^{\prime}(x){\rm Ai}(y)}{x-y},

(1.3)

where
{\rm Ai}(z)
is the Airy function
[
33
]
,

{\rm Ai}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{i}{3}s^{3}-izs}ds.

(1.4)

The Tracy-Widom distribution
F_{2}(t)
can be expressed by the Fredholm determinant of the Airy kernel

F_{2}(t)=\det(Id-\mathbb{K}_{\mathrm{Ai},t}).

(1.5)

It is remarkable that the Fredholm determinant admits the following integral representation; cf.

[
37
]
,

\det(Id-\mathbb{K}_{\mathrm{Ai},t})=\mathrm{exp}\left(-\int_{t}^{\infty}(s-t)u^{2}(s)ds\right),

(1.6)

where
u(t)
is the Hastings-McLeod solution
[
26
]
of the second Painlevé equation

u^{\prime\prime}=tu+2u^{3},

(1.7)

determined by the boundary condition

\begin{split}u(t)\sim\begin{cases}{\rm Ai}(t),&t\to+\infty,\\
\sqrt{-\frac{t}{2}},&t\to-\infty.\end{cases}\end{split}

(1.8)

These representations allow one to derive the following asymptotic expansion as
t\to-\infty

[
37
]

\frac{d}{dt}\ln F_{2}(t)=\frac{t^{2}}{4}-\frac{1}{8t}+\mathcal{O}((-t)^{-4}),~~~t\to-\infty.

(1.9)

Furthermore, we can obtain the asymptotic expansion of

F_{2}(t)
as
t\to-\infty
and the explicit expression of the constant term
[
17
,
22
]
.

Now consider a thinned process for the Gaussian unitary ensemble, which is obtained by removing each eigenvalue independently with the probability

1-\gamma
,
\gamma\in[0,1]
; see
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
. Then, the distribution function for the largest remaining eigenvalue
\lambda_{max}
in this thinned process is given by

F_{2}(t;\gamma)=\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\mathrm{Prob}\left((\lambda_{max}-\sqrt{2N})2^{\frac{1}{2}}N^{\frac{1}{6}}\leq t\right).

(1.10)

Similar to
F_{2}(t)
, the above distribution admits both Fredholm determinant representation and integral expression
[
7
]
:

F_{2}(t;\gamma)=\det(Id-\gamma\mathbb{K}_{\mathrm{Ai},t})=\mathrm{exp}\left(-\int_{t}^{\infty}(s-t)u^{2}(s;\gamma)ds\right),

(1.11)

whereas the Hastings-McLeod solution of the Painlevé II equation is replaced by the Ablowitz-Segur solution with the following asymptotic behavior
[
1
,
23
,
33
]
:

u(t;\gamma)\sim\begin{cases}\sqrt{\gamma}{\rm Ai}(t),&t\to+\infty,\\
(-t)^{-\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{2\rho}\cos\left(\frac{2}{3}(-t)^{\frac{3}{2}}-\rho\ln(8(-t)^{\frac{3}{2}})+\frac{\pi}{4}-\mathop{\rm arg}\nolimits\Gamma(-i\rho)\right),&t\to-\infty,\end{cases}

(1.12)

where
\rho=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln(1-\gamma)
with
\gamma\in[0,1)
.
Substituting (
1.12
) into the expression of
F_{2}(t;\gamma)
implies that as
t\to-\infty

[
3
,
7
,
11
]

\frac{d}{dt}\ln F_{2}(t;\gamma)=2\rho\sqrt{-t}+\frac{3\rho^{2}}{2t}-\frac{\rho}{2t}\sin(2\phi(t,\rho))+\mathcal{O}((-t)^{-\frac{7}{4}}),~\gamma\in[0,1),

(1.13)

where

\phi(t,\rho)=\frac{2}{3}(-t)^{\frac{3}{2}}-\rho\ln(8(-t)^{\frac{3}{2}})+\frac{\pi}{4}-\mathop{\rm arg}\nolimits\Gamma\left(-i\rho\right).

(1.14)

In
[
5
]
, Bertola and Cafasso
studied a matrix version of the Airy-convolution operator
Ai_{\vec{s}}
acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{r})
:

\begin{split}(Ai_{\vec{s}}\vec{\boldsymbol{f}})(x):=&\int_{R_{+}}\textbf{Ai}(x+y;\vec{s})\vec{\boldsymbol{f}}(y)dy,\\
\textbf{Ai}(x;\vec{s}):=&\int_{\gamma_{+}}\mathrm{e}^{\theta(\mu)}C\mathrm{e}^{\theta(\mu)}\mathrm{e}^{ix\mu}\frac{d\mu}{2\pi}=\left(c_{jk}{\rm Ai}(x+s_{j}+s_{k})\right)_{j,k=1}^{r},\\
\theta(\mu):=&\frac{i\mu^{3}}{6}I_{r}+\mathrm{diag}(s_{1},s_{2},...,s_{r})i\mu,\end{split}

(1.15)

where
\vec{\boldsymbol{f}}=(f_{1},...,f_{r})^{T}
,
\vec{s}=(s_{1},...,s_{r})
, the matrix
I_{r}
is a
r\times r
identity matrix,
C=\left(c_{jk}\right)_{j,k=1}^{r}
is a
r\times r
Hermitian matrix and the contour
\gamma_{+}
is a contour contained in the upper half plane and extending to infinity along the directions
\mathop{\rm arg}\nolimits z=\frac{\pi}{2}\pm\frac{\pi}{3}
. For
r=2
, we can choose a unitary matrix
U
such that

C=U\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&0\\
0&\lambda_{2}\end{pmatrix}U^{-1},

(1.16)

where
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are the eigenvalues of the Hermitian matrix
C
with
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in[-1,1]
.
The unitary matrix can be written as

U=\begin{pmatrix}\omega&\sqrt{1-|\omega|^{2}}\\
-\sqrt{1-|\omega|^{2}}&\overline{\omega}\end{pmatrix},

(1.17)

where each column corresponds to the eigenvector of
C
. The parameter
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
,
|\omega|\leq 1
and
\overline{\omega}
denotes the complex conjugation of
\omega
.

In
[
5
]
, Bertola and Cafasso
showed that the square of the matrix Airy-convolution operator (
1.15
) is totally positive and thus defines a determinantal point process, if
C
is a Hermitian matrix with eigenvalues in
[-1,1]
. The corresponding Fredholm determinant then gives the gap probability for this process. Furthermore, they showed that the Fredholm determinant (
1.18
) is related to a family of particular solutions for the noncommutative Painlevé II equation

\det(Id-Ai_{\vec{s}}^{2})=\mathrm{exp}\left(-4\int_{s}^{\infty}(t-s)Tr(\beta^{2}(t+\vec{\eta}))dt\right),

(1.18)

where

\vec{\eta}=(\eta_{1},...,\eta_{r}):=(s_{1}-s,...,s_{r}-s),~s=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}s_{j}
. Here, the
r\times r
matrix-valued functions
\beta(\vec{s})=\beta(\vec{s};C)
represents a family of solutions to the following noncommutative Painlevé II (PII for short) equation
[
5
,
6
]
:

D^{2}\beta(\vec{s})=4\left(\textbf{s}\beta(\vec{s})+\beta(\vec{s})\textbf{s}\right)+8\beta(\vec{s})^{3},~\textbf{s}:=\mathrm{diag}(s_{1},...,s_{r}),~D:=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{i}},

(1.19)

which first appeared in
[
34
]
. If
s=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}s_{j}\to+\infty
and
|s_{j}-s|\leq m,~j=1,2,...,r
are kept fixed, then
\beta(\vec{s})
satisfy the asymptotic behavior

(\beta)_{kl}(\vec{s})\sim-c_{kl}{\rm Ai}(s_{k}+s_{l}),~~s\to+\infty.

(1.20)

Additionally, if
C
is Hermitian then
\beta(\vec{s})
is pole-free for all
\vec{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
if and only if the eigenvalues of
C
are in
[-1,1]
; see
[
5
, Theorem 5.3]
. When
r=1
, the determiant in (
1.18
) reduces to the celebrated Tracy-Widom distribution (
1.6
). For general
r\geq 2
, the determinant in (
1.18
) is the noncommutative analogue of the Tracy-Widom distribution and the corresponding special solutions
\beta(\vec{s})
to the noncommutative PII equation are the noncommutative analogue of the classical Hastings-McLeod solution and Ablowitz-Segur solution.
We denote

D{H}_{II}(\vec{s})=-2i\beta(\vec{s})^{2}.

(1.21)

If
r=1
,
{H}_{II}(\vec{s})
is the Hamiltonian for the classical PII equation (
1.7
). For general
r\geq 2
, we can regard
{H}_{II}(\vec{s})
as the Hamiltonian for the noncommutative PII equation
\beta(\vec{s})
. Since
C
is Hermitian, we then have the following symmetry relations for
\beta(\vec{s})
and
{H}_{II}(\vec{s})
; cf.
[
5
]
,

\beta(\vec{s})=\beta(\vec{s})^{*},~{H}_{II}(\vec{s})=-{H}_{II}(\vec{s})^{*},

(1.22)

where
\beta(\vec{s})^{*}
denotes the Hermitian conjugate of
\beta(\vec{s})
.

It is remarkable that the Tracy-Widom distribution functions
F_{\beta}(t)
defined in (
1.2
) with the even values of parameter
\beta
admit integral expression in terms of the eigenvalues of a particular solution of the noncommutative PII equation; see
[
36
, Eq. (3.29)]
,
[
35
]
and
[
29
, Eq. (1.21)]
. We mention that the eigenvalues of the noncommutative PII equation satisfy the corresponding second Calogero-Painlevé system as shown in
[
6
]
. Recently, Its and Prokhorov
[
29
]

studied the tail asymptotics of the
\beta=6
Tracy-Widom distribution and the associated solution of the noncommutative PII equation
by using the Riemann-Hilbert problem representation for the noncommutative PII equation discovered in
[
5
,
6
]
. It should be pointed out that the Stokes multipliers of the Riemann-Hilbert problem considered in
[
29
]
differ from those in our case.

In the present work, we study the asymptotics of the Fredholm determinant (
1.18
) and the related special solutions of the noncommutative PII equation (
1.19
) for the first nontrivial case
r=2
. In the regime
\vec{s}=\left(s+\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{-s}},s-\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{-s}}\right)
with
\tau\geq 0
and
s\to-\infty
, we show that the asymptotics of the Fredholm determinant (
1.18
) and the associated solutions to the noncommutative PII equation can be expressed in terms of a family of special solutions of the classical Painlevé V (PV for short) equation. Furthermore, we derive the asymptotics of this one parameter family of solutions of the PV equation both as
ix\to-\infty
and
x\to 0
. For general
r>2
, it is expected that the asymptotics of the Fredholm determinant (
1.18
) would be described by the noncommutative PV equation; see Remark
11
below for the discussion. We will leave this problem for future investigation. It is noted that the asymptotics of the noncommutative PII equation is also considered recently in
[
32
]
under a different asymptotic regime than the one addressed in our study.

1.1
Asymptotics of one parameter family of solutions of the PV equation

To state our main results, we need to introduce the PV equation. Let
u(x),v(x)
and
y(x)
satisfy the following nonlinear ODEs

x\frac{du}{dx}=-xu-2v(u-1)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(u-1)[(\theta_{0}-\theta_{1}+\theta_{\infty})u-(3\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}+\theta_{\infty})],

(1.23)

x\frac{dv}{dx}=uv[v+\frac{1}{2}(\theta_{0}-\theta_{1}+\theta_{\infty})]-\frac{1}{u}(v+\theta_{0})[v+\frac{1}{2}(\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}+\theta_{\infty})],

(1.24)

x\frac{d\ln y}{dx}=-2v-\theta_{0}+u[v+\frac{1}{2}(\theta_{0}-\theta_{1}+\theta_{\infty})]+\frac{1}{u}[v+\frac{1}{2}(\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}+\theta_{\infty})].

(1.25)

These equations are the compatibility condition, namely
\Phi_{\lambda x}=\Phi_{x\lambda}
, of the following Lax pair for the
2\times 2
matrix-valued function
\Phi(\lambda,x)

[
23
,
24
,
38
]
:

\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\lambda}=L\Phi,~~~L(\lambda,x)=-\frac{x}{2}\sigma_{3}+\frac{L_{1}(x)}{\lambda}+\frac{L_{2}(x)}{\lambda-1},

(1.26)

and

\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x}=U\Phi,~~~U(\lambda,x)=-\frac{\lambda}{2}\sigma_{3}+B_{0}(x),

(1.27)

with

L_{1}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}v+\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}&-y(v+\theta_{0})\\
\frac{v}{y}&-(v+\frac{\theta_{0}}{2})\end{pmatrix},

(1.28)

L_{2}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}-v-\frac{1}{2}(\theta_{0}+\theta_{\infty})&yu(v+\frac{1}{2}(\theta_{0}-\theta_{1}+\theta_{\infty}))\\
-\frac{1}{yu}(v+\frac{1}{2}(\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}+\theta_{\infty}))&v+\frac{1}{2}(\theta_{0}+\theta_{\infty})\end{pmatrix},

(1.29)

B_{0}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{y}{x}[v+\theta_{0}-u(v+\frac{1}{2}(\theta_{0}-\theta_{1}+\theta_{\infty}))]\\
-\frac{1}{xy}[v-\frac{1}{u}(v+\frac{1}{2}(\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}+\theta_{\infty}))]&0\end{pmatrix}.

(1.30)

Using equation (
1.24
) to eliminate
v(x)
in (
1.23
), it follows that
u(x)
satisfies the PV equation

\frac{d^{2}u}{dx^{2}}=\left(\frac{1}{2u}+\frac{1}{u-1}\right)\left(\frac{du}{dx}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{x}\frac{du}{dx}+\frac{(u-1)^{2}}{8x^{2}}\left((\theta_{0}-\theta_{1}+\theta_{\infty})^{2}u-\frac{(\theta_{0}-\theta_{1}-\theta_{\infty})^{2}}{u}\right)\\
-\frac{(1-\theta_{0}-\theta_{1})u}{x}-\frac{u(u+1)}{2(u-1)}.

(1.31)

Moreover, we have the Hamiltonian for the PV equation
[
30
]

H_{V}(x)=\frac{1}{x}\left(v-\frac{1}{u}(v+\frac{\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}+\theta_{\infty}}{2})\right)\left(v+\theta_{0}-u(v+\frac{\theta_{0}-\theta_{1}+\theta_{\infty}}{2})\right)-v-\frac{\theta_{0}+\theta_{\infty}}{2}.

(1.32)

For our application, we have

\theta_{0}=-\theta_{1}=-2\alpha\in i\mathbb{R},~\theta_{\infty}=0.

(1.33)

For later use, we define the following function
\hat{u}(x)
, which is related to the PV equation

\hat{u}(x)=a_{0}b_{1}y\left(\frac{v-2\alpha}{v}-u\right),

(1.34)

where
a_{0}(x),~b_{1}(x)
are defined in (
2.15
).

We prove the following existence and asymptotics for one parameter family of solutions of the PV equation.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\alpha\in i\mathbb{R}
,
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
and
|\omega|\leq 1
. There exists a
\omega
-family of pole-free solutions on the imaginary axis to the Painlevé V system (
1.23
)-(
1.25
), which satisfies the following asymptotic behavior

\displaystyle u(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-x+4\mu\ln(-ix)}\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha-\mu)}{\Gamma(1+\mu-\alpha)}\frac{\Gamma(1-\alpha-\mu)}{\Gamma(1+\alpha+\mu)}+\mathcal{O}(x^{-1}),~ix\to-\infty,

(1.35)

\displaystyle v(x)=\alpha+\mu+\frac{2(\mu^{2}-\alpha^{2})}{x}(2\sin^{2}{d(x)}-1)+\mathcal{O}(x^{-2}),~ix\to-\infty,

(1.36)

\displaystyle y(x)=\frac{\sqrt{1-|\omega|^{2}}}{\overline{\omega}}\mathrm{e}^{\pi i\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mu\ln(-ix)}\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha+\mu)}{\Gamma(1+\alpha-\mu)}+\mathcal{O}(x^{-1}),~\omega\neq 0,~ix\to-\infty,

(1.37)

\displaystyle\hat{u}(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-2\alpha\ln(-ix)+2i\mathop{\rm arg}\nolimits\Gamma(2\alpha)+i\mathop{\rm arg}\nolimits\Gamma(\mu-\alpha)-i\mathop{\rm arg}\nolimits\Gamma(\mu+\alpha)}\Big(

\displaystyle\cos d(x)-\frac{\mu}{\alpha}i\sin d(x)\Big)

(1.38)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}(x^{-1}),~ix\to-\infty,

\displaystyle u(x)=1+\mathcal{O}(x),~x\to 0,

(1.39)

\displaystyle v(x)=2(1-|\omega|^{2})\alpha+\mathcal{O}(x),~x\to 0,

(1.40)

\displaystyle y(x)=\frac{\sqrt{1-|\omega|^{2}}}{\overline{\omega}}+\mathcal{O}(x),~\omega\neq 0,~x\to 0,

(1.41)

\displaystyle\hat{u}(x)=1+\mathcal{O}(x),~x\to 0,

(1.42)

where

d(x)=-\frac{ix}{2}+2\mu i\ln(-ix)+\mathop{\rm arg}\nolimits{\Gamma(\mu-\alpha)}-\mathop{\rm arg}\nolimits{\Gamma(-\alpha-\mu)},

(1.43)

and the connection formula between
\mu
and
\omega
is given by

\mu=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\ln\left((1-|\omega|^{2})\mathrm{e}^{2\pi i\alpha}+|\omega|^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi i\alpha}\right).

(1.44)

Moreover, the associated Hamiltonian
H_{V}(x)
is pole-free for
x\in i\mathbb{R}
and has the following asymptotic behavior

H_{V}(x)=-\mu-\frac{2}{x}(\alpha^{2}-\mu^{2})+\mathcal{O}(x^{-2}),~ix\to-\infty,

(1.45)

and

H_{V}(x)=(2|\omega|^{2}-1)\alpha+\mathcal{O}(x),~x\to 0.

(1.46)

Particularly, the functions
v(x)
and
H_{V}(x)
are purely imaginary on
i\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 1
.

We have the symmetry relations for this family of solutions to the PV equation

u(-x)=u(x)^{-1},~v(-x)=-v(x),~y(-x)=\mathrm{e}^{x}y(x)u(x);

see Remark
9
. Thus,
the asymptotic expansions of the solutions as
ix\to+\infty
can be obtained using these symmetry relations and Theorem
1
.

Remark 2
.

If
\alpha=0
, we have
\hat{u}(x)=|\omega|^{2}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{x}{2}}+(1-|\omega|^{2}){e}^{-\frac{x}{2}}
; see Remark
8
. This result is consistent with the asymptotic expansion (
1.38
) when taking the limit
\alpha\to 0
.

1.2
Asymptotics of the Fredholm determinant and the noncommutative PII equation

We will derive the asymptotics of the Fredholm determinant (
1.18
) and the associated particular solutions to the noncommutative PII equation (
1.19
)
as the variables tend to negative infinity at certain related speed. The asymptotics are expressed in terms of the one parameter family of special solutions of the classical PV equation with the properties given in Theorem
1
. The results are sated in the following theorems.

Theorem 2
.

Let
Ai_{\vec{s}}
denote the matrix version of the Airy-convolution operator acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})
as defined in (
1.15
), where
\vec{s}=\left(s+\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{-s}},s-\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{-s}}\right)
with
\tau\geq 0
and
s<0
. The eigenvalues of the Hermitian matrix
C
in (
1.16
), namely
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
, are in
(-1,1)
, and the eigenvectors of
C
involve a parameter
\omega
,
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
and
|\omega|\leq 1
as given in (
1.17
). Then, as
s\to-\infty
, we have

\partial_{s}\ln\det(Id-Ai^{2}_{\vec{s}})={4\sqrt{2}}(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})\sqrt{-s}+\frac{3}{2s}(\rho_{1}^{2}+\rho_{2}^{2})-\frac{1}{2s}\Bigg(K(\tau)\left(\rho_{1}\cos(d_{1}(s,\tau))+\rho_{2}\cos(d_{2}(s,\tau))\right)\\
\\
+2(K(\tau)-1)\sqrt{\rho_{1}\rho_{2}}\cos\left(\frac{d_{1}(s,\tau)+d_{2}(s,\tau)}{2}\right)-8\sqrt{2}\tau iH_{V}(4\sqrt{2}\tau i)\Bigg)+\mathcal{O}\left((-s)^{-\frac{7}{4}}\right),

(1.47)

where

d_{j}(s,\tau)=\frac{8}{3}\sqrt{2}(-s)^{\frac{3}{2}}-{2\rho_{j}}\ln(2^{\frac{9}{2}}(-s)^{\frac{3}{2}})-2\mathop{\rm arg}\nolimits\Gamma\left(-i{\rho_{j}}\right)+2(-1)^{j}\mathop{\rm arg}\nolimits\hat{u}(4\sqrt{2}\tau i),

(1.48)

K(\tau)=1+\frac{\sqrt{2}\tau i}{\alpha^{2}}\left(H_{V}(4\sqrt{2}\tau i)-\alpha+v(4\sqrt{2}\tau i)\right),~\alpha\neq 0,~K(\tau)=1,~\alpha=0,

(1.49)

and

\alpha=\frac{1}{4\pi i}\ln\left(\frac{1-\lambda_{1}^{2}}{1-\lambda_{2}^{2}}\right),~\rho_{j}=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln(1-\lambda_{j}^{2}),~~~j=1,2.

(1.50)

Here
v(x)
is the solution of the PV system (
1.23
)-(
1.25
) and
H_{V}(x)
is the associated Hamiltonian with the properties specified in Theorem
1
and
\hat{u}(x)
is defined by the relation (
1.34
).

Remark 3
.

If
C^{2}
is a diagonal matrix, then from Remarks
8
and
10
below, we have

v(4\sqrt{2}\tau i)=0,~\hat{u}(4\sqrt{2}\tau i)=\mathrm{e}^{2\sqrt{2}\tau i},~H_{V}(4\sqrt{2}\tau i)=\alpha,~K(\tau)=1.

(1.51)

Thus, the asymptotic expansion (
1.47
)
becomes the following simpler form

\partial_{s}\ln\det(Id-Ai^{2}_{\vec{s}})={4\sqrt{2}}(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})\sqrt{-s}+\frac{3}{2s}(\rho_{1}^{2}+\rho_{2}^{2})-\frac{1}{2s}\Big(\rho_{1}\cos(d_{1}(s,\tau))\\
+\rho_{2}\cos(d_{2}(s,\tau)))-8\sqrt{2}\alpha\tau i\Big)+\mathcal{O}\left((-s)^{-\frac{7}{4}}\right).

2.3

We will prove the existence of solution to the model RH problem for

and

Lemma 1
.

For
\alpha\in i\mathbb{R}
and
x\in i\mathbb{R}
, we suppose that
\hat{\Phi}(\lambda)
satisfies the same jump condition (
2.8

(2.26)

Then, we have
\hat{\Phi}(\lambda)=0
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}.

Proof.

To normalize the behavior as

\tilde{\Phi}(\lambda)=\hat{\Phi}(\lambda)\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}\lambda x\sigma_{3}}.

(2.27)

\tilde{\Phi}_{+}(\lambda)=\tilde{\Phi}_{-}(\lambda)\tilde{J}(\lambda),~\lambda\in(0,1),

where

\tilde{J}(\lambda)=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}\lambda x\sigma_{3}}U\mathrm{e}^{2\pi i\alpha\sigma_{3}}U^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}\lambda x\sigma_{3}}.

\mathbb{C}\backslash[0,1]

\tilde{\Phi}(\lambda)=\mathcal{O}(1)\lambda^{-\alpha\sigma_{3}},~\lambda\to 0,

\tilde{\Phi}(\lambda)=\mathcal{O}(1)(\lambda-1)^{\alpha\sigma_{3}},~\lambda\to 1.

(2.32)

\Phi(\lambda)

\hat{J}(x)=\begin{cases}(\tilde{J}^{-1}(x))^{*},~&x\in(0,1),\\

-\overline{b_{0}y}&\overline{a_{0}y\frac{v-2\alpha}{v}}\end{pmatrix},

(2.38)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&b_{1}yu\\
\frac{a_{1}}{yu}\frac{v}{v-2\alpha}&b_{1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\overline{b_{1}}&-\overline{\frac{a_{1}}{yu}\frac{v}{v-2\alpha}}\\
-\overline{b_{1}yu}&\overline{a_{1}}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.39)

So we have

\displaystyle\frac{|y(x)|^{2}}{v(x)}(v(x)-2\alpha)=-1,

(2.40)

\displaystyle\frac{v(x)}{v(x)-2\alpha}=-|y(x)|^{2}|u(x)|^{2}.

(2.41)

Then the condition
\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(v(x))=0
can be deduced from (
2.40
), since
\alpha
is a pure imaginary number. Combining (
2.40
) and (
2.41
), we see that
|u(x)|^{2}=1
. This, together with (
2.10
), (
2.17
) and (
2.18
), leads to the conclusion that
\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(H_{V}(x))=0
for
x\in i\mathbb{R}
. This completes the proof.
∎
