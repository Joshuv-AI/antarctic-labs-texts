Strong yet ductile heat-resistant aluminum alloy by additive manufacturing
Authors: Gan Li, Yuhe Huang, Chunlu Zhao, Xi He, Shuo Wang, Qiyang Tan, Ying Li, Fucong Lyu, Guanghui Feng, Junhua Luan, Wanqian Hu, Zhenmin Li, Xinggang Li, Yanjin Xu, Yuansong Zeng, Zhiqiang Li, Xinping Mao, Ming-Xing Zhang, Lehua Qi, Qiang Zhu, Jian Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aluminum (Al) alloys are pivotal structural materials, indispensable for advanced energy-saving solutions and lightweight technologies. However, the limited heat resistance and low critical strength of the present commercial Al alloys at elevated temperatures (300–400 °C) have constrained their broader applications. Here, we present a facile strategy to additively manufacture strong yet ductile heat-resistant Al alloys using laser powder bed fusion (PBF-LB). By embedding heat-resistant multicomponent intermetallic nanophases (HMINPs) at the solidified cell boundaries, the as-printed alloy forms thermally stable cellular structures containing a high-volume fraction (~14 vol%) of HMINPs. Without any additional post-treatment, our as-printed Al alloy exhibits an average room-temperature tensile strength of 582 MPa, combined with a tensile strength of 114 MPa and exceptional creep resistance at 400 °C. The partial solid-state amorphization of the HMINPs during tensile straining at 300–400 °C creates a nano-dual-phase glass–crystal structure, providing an additional toughening mechanism. This HMINP strategy and PBF-LB’s freeform manufacturing capability enable large-scale industrial use of our high-performance Al alloy, holding great promise for advancing energy efficiency, carbon neutrality, and sustainable manufacturing.

Introduction

Metal additive manufacturing (AM), or three-dimensional (3D) printing, has emerged as an innovative net-shape forming technology that enables the rapid fabrication of complex components directly from computer-aided design models, holding appeal for a diverse range of applications1–3. During AM, such as laser powder bed fusion (PBF-LB), the microscale melting pools (MPs), intrinsic steep temperature gradient (106–107 °C/m), and rapid cooling rate coupled with the extreme thermal cycles give rise to distinctive, far-from-equilibrium microstructures3,4. Among these, solidification-induced cellular structures are a characteristic feature that is rarely attainable through conventional casting or welding. Such cellular microstructures have now been reported across a wide range of alloy systems produced via PBF-LB, including Ti alloys2, steels4,5, Al alloys6, Ni-based superalloys7, and high-entropy alloys8. The cell walls or boundaries often exhibit pronounced compositional segregation from the matrix, allowing the introduction of precipitate skeleton architecture characterized by the secondary phases embedded in the cell boundaries to enhance the mechanical performance5,6,9. This architectural design accommodates substantial dislocation activity during deformation, offering a means to tailor work hardening behavior and achieve high strength4,9.

Al alloys are the most common lightweight alloys, and have been widely used in many critical fields due to their high specific strength and the abundance of natural resources10–12. However, a significant drawback of these lightweight Al alloys is their low heat resistance at elevated temperatures (300–400 °C), which limits their use in advanced engineering applications that require high service temperatures13,14. Conventional high-strength heat-resistant Al alloys, such as cast A201 and wrought 2618 Al alloys, suffer from issues such as rapid coarsening of strengthening precipitates driven by the extensive thermal diffusion and insufficient volume fraction of these precipitates, ultimately reducing load-bearing capacity at elevated temperatures15. Although certain Al alloys can incorporate precipitates with a large volume fraction, such as θ’-Al2Cu and η’-MgZn2, these precipitates typically comprise solutes with high-solubility and fast-diffusion rates, and therefore lack sufficient thermal stability15,16. In contrast, precipitates with high thermal stability, such as Al3Sc and Al3Zr, are composed of slow-diffusing solutes, making it difficult to achieve an adequate volume fraction for effective strengthening17,18. Consequently, the dissimilarity in solute diffusivities hinders the formation of nanoprecipitates with both high stability and a high-volume fraction, which are crucial for developing heat-resistant Al alloys. Recent advances highlight strategies for attaining high-temperature strength in Al alloys by stabilizing networks of eutectic precipitates during PBF-LB. Examples include thermally stable nanoscale Al-La eutectic cellular networks19 and Cu/Mn-stabilized networks of Al₆Fe phase20. Furthermore, Ren et al. enhanced thermal stability by dispersing spherical amorphous precipitates (~20 nm in size) within the cell interiors and along cell boundaries21.

Here, we propose a facile and green strategy to develop advanced heat-resistant Al alloy by importing a highly thermally stable cellular structure via PBF-LB (Fig. 1a). Our approach is based on creating a controlled distribution of high-density, strong and heat-resistant multicomponent intermetallic nanophases (HMINPs) at the cell boundaries, which provides effective strengthening in Al alloys (Fig. 1b). By actively tailoring precipitation behavior during PBF-LB, we utilize the major impurities in recycled Al alloys, including high-solubility Si and slow-diffusing transition elements (Mn, Ni, Fe), to form a high-volume fraction of α-type Al(FeMnNi)Si HMINPs. This stabilizes the nanoprecipitates through solute segregation and blocking reverse interface diffusion16,22. Combining the thermal stability derived from the multicomponent complex nature23 with the additional dislocation storage capacity offered by the heterogeneous cellular structures10, our conceptual design extends thermal stability to higher service temperatures while maintaining superior mechanical performance. Moreover, the limited addition of the readily abundant Fe, Mn, Ni elements, together with a high Si content, helps preserve the lightweight nature of the alloy. Results and discussion

Thermally stable microstructures

We printed the heat-resistant Al alloys via PBF-LB using gas-atomized powders with a chemical composition of Al-7.44Si-2.34Fe-1.79Mn-1.12Ni (wt%) (Supplementary Fig. 1a–d). Through careful optimization of the processing parameters, the as-printed samples achieved near-full density with a relative density of up to 99.99%, demonstrating excellent printability, as verified by the micro-focus computed tomography (micro-CT) results (Supplementary Fig. 1e, f). Side-view cross-sectional analysis revealed a bi-modal grain size distribution: columnar grains spanning the MPs alongside fine equiaxed grains adjacent to the MP boundaries (Supplementary Fig. 2a). The average grain size was 12.5 μm, with no significant crystallographic texture (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). Representative cellular structures in the as-printed condition are shown in Fig. 1c, d. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (Fig. 1c) displayed MPs consisting of fine cells measuring 300–400 nm. The cell size transitioned from fine cells within the MPs to relatively coarser cells near the MP boundaries, reflecting local variations in thermal history because of the overlaps of adjacent MPs during the layer-by-layer deposition3,4. The transmission electron microscopy energy-dispersive spectroscopy (TEM-EDS) mapping (Fig. 1d) illustrated that the cellular structure forms a co-continuous framework of HMINP networks and Al dendritic cells, where interconnected Al cells are encircled by the HMINPs that constitute the cell boundaries. The network skeleton width was approximately 60 nm, and the volume fraction of HMINPs was estimated to be about 14%. It is worth noting that the cell boundaries were present both within the grain interior and between adjacent grains (Supplementary Fig. 2d).

In addition to the HMINPs, Si nanoparticles were also observed, and some were located near the HMINPs (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 3a). The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern (Fig. 1e) indicated that the HMINPs possessed a crystal structure akin to the α-type Al(M)Si phases (where M represents transition elements such as Mn, Fe, Cr, etc.)24. Notably, these α-type phases, exemplified by the Al(Mn,Mo)Si phase25 in cast Al alloy and α-Al(FeMn)Si phase26 in 3D-printed Al alloy, play a critical role in high-temperature strengthening. We subsequently employed 3D atom probe tomography (APT) to further analyze the typical elemental distribution of HMINPs within grain interiors and at grain boundaries. As illustrated in Fig. 1f, noticeable elemental partitioning is evident between the HMINPs at different locations and the Al matrix, with Si nanoparticles also detected adjacent to the HMINPs. Despite minor composition undulations within the HMINPs, Al and Si are significantly enriched, reaching average concentrations of ~60 at % and 20 at %, respectively. Enrichment of Fe (~10 at%), Mn (~5 at%), and Ni (~4 at %) was also confirmed (Supplementary Fig. 3b–g and Supplementary Table 1). The atomic structure of the present HMINPs was further resolved by the scanning TEM with aberration corrector (AC-STEM) technique. As displayed in Fig. 1g, the high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) and corresponding fast Fourier transformation (FFT) patterns revealed a semi-coherent interface between the superlattice-structured HMINPs and the Al matrix. Atomic-scale STEM images and EDS mapping along various zone axes indicate that these HMINPs adopt a Pm3 cubic crystal structure with a lattice constant of 1.256 nm, wherein the transition elements are readily interchangeable (Supplementary Fig. 4a–e). Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) curves of the as-printed Al alloy suggest that these HMINPs form initially during solidification (Supplementary Fig. 4f, g), leading us to identify them as the quinary α-Al(FeMnNi)Si phases.

We then performed in situ TEM heating experiments (Supplementary Fig. 5a) to directly visualize the microstructure evolution during the short-time heating process of our Al alloy at various temperatures (200, 300, and 400 °C). The microstructure remained largely unchanging at temperatures below 300 °C, while there were subtle changes in the Al grains at 400 °C. The coarsening behavior of the HMINPs was investigated by subjecting bulk specimens (our alloy and AlSi10Mg for comparison) to thermal exposure at 400 °C for durations of 1–168 h. It is evident that after 168 h of exposure, the MP boundaries remain distinct and the HMINPs exhibit no significant coarsening or dissolving (Supplementary Fig. 5b). In contrast, in the AlSi10Mg sample, the Si network is disrupted, and the Si particles exhibit pronounced coarsening. This is significantly vital as the rapid coarsening or dissolution of nanoprecipitates in Al alloys often eventuates in accelerated failures at elevated temperatures15–17. Therefore, the excellent heat resistance of the cell structure formed by the continuous HMINPs in our Al alloy is expected to promote the thermal stability of our heat-resistant Al alloys at high temperatures.

Exceptional mechanical properties and commercial uses

To evaluate the mechanical performance of the newly developed Al alloy with highly thermally stable microstructure, a comprehensive series of tensile and creep tests was conducted across a range of temperatures (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 6). Figure 2a presents the representative tensile engineering stress–strain curves for the as-printed alloy tested in both transverse and longitudinal directions from 25 to 400 °C. Overall, tensile stress decreases while elongation increases with rising temperature. Remarkably, our heat-resistant Al alloy effectively minimizes the pronounced mechanical anisotropy typically seen in most Al alloys fabricated by PBF-LB. At 25 °C, it exhibits a yield strength (σY) of ~440 MPa, an ultimate tensile strength (σUTS) of ~582 MPa, and an elongation (EL) of ~7.2% along both directions. This excellent strength-ductility balance is comparable to that of the strongest wrought 7xxx series Al alloys and surpasses most Al alloys printed by PBF-LB27–33. Even at elevated temperatures, where most Al alloys suffer from premature failure due to limited heat resistance, our alloy retains high performance. For example, it maintains a high σUTS of ~263 MPa at 300 °C and ~114 MPa even at 400 °C. Compared with previously reported high-strength Al alloys fabricated by PBF-LB and conventional routes, our as-printed Al alloy shows clearly superior mechanical properties at both room and elevated temperatures (Fig. 2b)14,16,27–37. Tensile creep tests performed at 400 °C under stresses of 25–40 MPa to reveal the alloy’s strong resistance to time-dependent deformation (Fig. 2c). In comparison with previously reported Al alloys16,38–41, the heat-resistant Al alloy exhibited low steady-state creep rates, demonstrating superior creep resistance (Fig. 2d). While certain alloys (e.g., cast Al–Cu–Mg–Ag–Sc16) may achieve higher creep resistance, they often rely on costly elements such as Ag and Sc. In contrast, the present alloy delivers robust performance over a wide temperature range without requiring post heat treatment. Beyond its superb mechanical responses, the present Al alloy offers a distinct lightweight nature advantage over other heat-resistant Al alloys that often have densities exceeding 3 g/cm3. This inherent characteristic enables the printing of components with exceptional specific strength and thermo-mechanical stability (Supplementary Fig. 7a), supporting the development of high-performance components for demanding thermo-mechanical environments. To date, our advanced Al alloy has now moved beyond research into commercialization, with adoption in sectors such as marine engineering and aerospace systems. Herein, we present three exemplary components with intricate structures that can be disclosed, which demonstrate high surface quality and geometric precision (Supplementary Fig. 7b, c). These industrial applications highlight the transformative potential of integrating our high-performance Al alloy with near-net-shape AM. This organic fusion can drive innovative designs and enable the precise fabrication of advanced lightweight components with structure-function integration for diverse application scenarios.

Deformation mechanisms

To elucidate the origins underlying the exceptional heat-resistant properties exhibited in our alloy, we conducted an exhaustive analysis of the deformation microstructures ranging from microscopic to atomic scales. We captured the microstructure near the fracture region after creep rupture under 400 °C and 25 MPa tensile stress to analyze the evolution of defects and HMINPs. Under such extreme conditions, the specimen achieved a high creep life of ~330 h. The MP boundaries near the fracture remained intact and clearly discernible, and the fracture was observed to propagate along them, with the majority of creep-induced cavities located adjacent to them (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Despite some evident coarsening under stress and long-term thermal exposure, the HMINPs retained sizes ranging from tens to a few hundred nanometers (Supplementary Fig. 8b, c), demonstrating robust thermal stability. Similarly, the nanoprecipitate network also remained intact following tensile testing at 400 °C (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). Tensile deformation was primarily associated with typical dislocation slips (Supplementary Fig. 9c, d), and the accumulation of dislocations around the precipitates suggested a robust dislocation-precipitate interaction. Furthermore, post-fracture analysis revealed a significant rise in kernel average misorientation values within the tested specimen (Supplementary Fig. 9e–k), highlighting the efficacy of geometrically necessary dislocations in accommodating deformation5.

Intriguingly, amorphization zones were identified near the crack tips in the samples fractured during tensile testing at 400 °C (Fig. 3a). A similar phenomenon was observed in specimens tested at 300 °C but was scarcely discernible at lower temperatures. Figure 3b elucidates the coexistence of a large crystalline region, containing HMINPs and Si particles, with an adjacent amorphous area. The interface between the amorphous zone and Al matrix appears remarkably straight, as outlined by the dotted lines in Fig. 3b. This distinct geometric relationship is further illustrated in Fig. 3c, a finding rarely reported in the literature. Figure 3d unveils rod-like and globular supra-nano-sized particles (smaller than 5 nm) uniformly dispersed within the amorphous phase, thereby constituting a nano-dual-phase glass-crystal structure termed an “amorphous + supra-nano precipitate” composite. HAADF-STEM analysis (Fig. 3e) validates that these precipitates in the amorphous region correspond to L12-ordered γ’-(Ni,Fe)3Al phases, which are known for enhancing toughness and thermal stability in Ni-based or Co-based superalloys42. This nano-dual-phase glass-crystal architectural configuration, previously unreported in bulk Al alloys, highlights the exceptional attributes of our material. APT examination of the amorphous zone indicated a chemical composition in close congruence with that of the HMINPs (Supplementary Fig. 10a–e), suggesting the amorphous region originated from the partial transformation of HMINPs. Such amorphous phase transitions are often reported in high-entropy systems due to the nature of the inherent atomic-scale complexity and local chemical disorder in multicomponent alloying milieu43–45. APT results also indicate pronounced enrichment of Al, Ni, and Fe within the supra-nano precipitates in the amorphous region (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 10f), supporting the interpretation based on the TEM image in Fig. 3e. Notably, while amorphous-based materials tend to crystallize over time46, subsequent in situ heating TEM tests (Supplementary Fig. 10g) and deformation microstructure analysis (Supplementary Fig. 9b) confirmed that these HMINPs-derived amorphous areas remain stable at 400 °C, signifying their high thermal resistance.Fig. 3Deformation microstructures of the heat-resistant Al alloy after tensile testing at 400 °C.a Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image near the fracture surface showing amorphization zones with a distinctive shape. b Representative high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) image of the amorphization zone and corresponding energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping, showing the coexistence of a crystalline region comprising HMINPs and Si particles with the surrounding amorphous phase in a specific arrangement. c TEM image showing a specific orientation relationship between the Al matrix and the amorphous phase along the [001]Al direction. d Uniform distribution of supra-nano precipitates with sizes below 5 nm in the amorphous phase. e High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image confirming that the nanoprecipitates within the amorphous phase displayed the same crystalline structure as the L12-type phase that is widely observed in Ni-based superalloys. f Atom probe tomography (APT) analysis showing the growth of Ni/Fe-rich supra-nano phase in the amorphous region, forming a nano-dual-phase glass-crystal structure.

As the tensile temperatures previously noted (300–400 °C) remained beneath the melting threshold of the Al alloy, the crystalline-to-amorphous phase transformation (i.e., order-to-disorder transition) is triggered by solid-state amorphization. This transformation destabilizes the crystalline lattice and yields a disordered framework, which is markedly distinct from the typical liquid-to-solid amorphous transformation observed during rapid cooling43. Besides, the absence of amorphous zones within the as-printed sample (Fig. 1) and throughout in situ heating procedures (Supplementary Fig. 5a) indicates that the structural disorder leading to the crystalline-to-amorphous phase transformation is mainly driven by extensive strain concentration during tensile deformation47. The activation energy barrier for amorphization, signifying the energetic cost per unit volume necessary for this transformation, often exhibits a temperature-dependent profile48. As plastic deformation intensifies, the energy of the grain boundaries escalates, and elevated temperatures empower atom activation, enabling them to deviate from their equilibrium positions. Consequently, this enhances the activation energy and accelerates the amorphization process. This elucidates the observation of amorphization solely during tensile testing within the 300–400 °C range.

To deepen the understanding of the deformation mechanisms responsible for the exceptional strength-ductility combination in our Al alloy at 300–400 °C, it is essential to distinguish the respective roles of dislocation-mediated plasticity and strain-induced structural disorder. Three tensile samples (designated as S1, S2, and S3 hereafter) were prepared from one [110] orientated grain (Fig. 4a), exhibiting remarkable tensile ductility at 400 °C with an elongation exceeding 26%, roughly twice that observed in bulk samples. In the in situ TEM tensile experiments, deformation was primarily accommodated by dislocation nucleation, motion, and pile-up, as well as their interactions with individual precipitates and interfaces. Regions with relatively lower local precipitate density facilitated direct visualization of these dislocation processes by reducing excessive strain localization, enabling clearer identification of crack initiation pathways at elevated temperatures. Specifically, crack initiation was observed in S1 at a strain of ~17.5%, with cracks becoming evident in S2 and S3 at a strain of ~22.5%. A meticulous examination of S1 revealed that the crack (pink arrow) originated along high-density nanoprecipitates (Si + HMINPs, confirmed by TEM-EDS analysis) and subsequently propagated along these precipitates (Fig. 4b). This interfacial cracking arose from substantial stress concentrations caused by dislocation accumulation near these precipitates, as indicated by white arrows. This failure mode aligns with the fracture mechanism at the interface between the θ′ precipitates and the α-Al matrix, as reported in a previous study49. In contrast, crack initiation and propagation predominantly occurred within the Al matrix in S2 and S3. In S2, crack initiation and growth transpired rapidly within the Al matrix (Fig. 4c). The crack propagation was accompanied by accumulation of dislocations in S3 (Fig. 4d). Further deformation revealed that cracks propagated along grain boundaries (Fig. 4e), known as weak points during high-temperature deformation. Notably, no fracture or significant coarsening of HMINPs was observed during these high-temperature in situ TEM tensile tests, suggesting their exceptional thermal stability and ductility in accommodating plastic deformation. However, no crystalline-to-amorphous transition occurred during the TEM tensile tests at 400 °C. Owing to the presence of free surfaces and reduced triaxial constraint in thin TEM samples, the defect energy density required to trigger crystalline-to-amorphous transformation could not be sufficiently accumulated, and no amorphization was therefore observed during in situ tensile loading at 400 °C. We finally investigated the bulk alloys that were deformed at 300–400 °C (within a strain range of 5–9%) experienced sustained strain accumulation under higher constraint, leading to reproducible solid-state amorphization of a portion of the HMINPs, as evidenced by TEM and APT analyses (Supplementary Fig. 11). These amorphous phases underwent substantial deformation during the final stage of tension. By scrutinizing the microstructural attributes of both submicron and bulk samples after deformation, we can succinctly rationalize the high-temperature tensile deformation process of bulk Al alloys as follows: as deformation energy accumulates and the density of defects (dislocations) increases during tensile stress, elastic deformation transitions into the formation and motion of dislocations. Ultimately, in the realm of high-strain (and/or high-strain rates) conditions, a partial solid-state amorphization of ductile HMINPs emerges, giving rise to unique nano-dual-phase glass-crystal structures that help harmonize deformation until failure45,48.

The amorphization process serves as a potent mechanism for alleviating substantial deviatoric stresses that accumulate at the junctions of defects or transformation packets44,45. The formation of an amorphous region induced by deformation provides an auxiliary pathway for energy dissipation and halts crack propagation during tensile tests, thereby offering an effective toughening mechanism to maintain exceptional mechanical properties45,47,48. The absence of crystal defects and dislocation glide pathways, which are prevalent in crystalline materials, renders the amorphous phase resilient to both tensile and creep deformation50,51. This attribute has been explored in crystalline/amorphous nanolaminates52, magnetron sputtered supra-nano-dual-phase glass-crystal Mg50 and Al53 alloys, all of which demonstrate synergetic ultrahigh strength and resistance to crack initiation. Furthermore, the continuous generation-movement-annihilation of dislocations between the amorphous phase and grains (e.g., Al matrix and supra-nano γ’ phase in the present Al alloy) and the flow behavior of nano-sized amorphous phase can further increase plasticity, as evidenced in our previous nanostructured Al alloys53. Consequently, the present nano-dual-phase glass-crystal structure induced by the solid-state amorphization introduces an extrinsic toughening mechanism during tensile deformation at high temperatures (300–400 °C)44,50, aligning with the prolonged elongation observed after peak strength (within a strain below 5%) in the tensile curves. Although the detailed mechanisms behind this process are not yet fully understood, future investigations utilizing molecular dynamics simulations could offer valuable insights into the formation of both the amorphous phase and the supra-nano-sized particles within it44,54.

In summary, we developed a facile and economically viable strategy to additively manufacture strong yet ductile heat-resistant Al alloys, leveraging the common impurity elements (Si, Fe, Ni, and Mn) in recyclable Al alloys. By embedding HMINPs at the solidified cell boundaries, the resultant as-printed alloy boasts thermally stable cellular structures with a remarkably high-volume fraction (~14 vol%). These unique structures confer exceptional mechanical properties across a broad temperature range of 25–400 °C. Notably, the as-printed alloy, without any supplementary treatment, exhibits a harmonious blend of high tensile strength and ductility at ambient temperature, while also demonstrating exceptional tensile and creep resistance at elevated temperatures. Intriguingly, the solid-state amorphization of a portion of HMINPs during straining at 300–400 °C emerges as an alternative toughening mechanism, ensuring the sustained exceptional mechanical performance. Furthermore, the commercialization of this Al alloy underscores its capacity to offer an excellent combination of affordable, lightweight components with intricate geometric shapes for diverse applications. This work opens up a new avenue for developing advanced materials with exceptional properties, enabling transformative applications across multiple industries.

Methods

Powder preparation

The raw powders for PBF-LB were prepared through the supersonic gas atomization (GA) process, using a 50 kg cast ingot. The chemical composition of the raw powders (Al-7.44Si-2.34Fe-1.79Mn-1.12Ni, wt%) and as-printed samples (Al-7.67Si-2.48Fe-1.83Mn-1.27Ni, wt%) was determined using inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectroscopy. The content of Si and alloying elements (Fe, Mn, Ni) increased slightly after printing, which is caused by the preferential evaporation of Al due to its relatively low melting point55. The shape of the powders is highly spherical, with a few tiny satellites on the surface, which is a common inherent defect during the GA process56. The particle size distribution of the powders was determined using the Malvern Mastersizer 3000, which indicated D10, D50, and D90 values of 22.5, 40.2, and 56.6 μm, respectively. Laser reflectivity of the raw powders was measured using diffuse reflectance spectroscopy on a UV–visible–near-infrared SolidSpec-3700 spectrophotometer with a spectral resolution of 1 nm. The laser reflectivity of the powders at a laser wavelength of 1070 nm was 33.7 ± 0.1%, which was lower than that of other Al powders, such as AlSi10Mg, 7075 Al, and 7075 Al alloys55, facilitating the enhancement of the efficiency of the MPs and saving energy.

Laser powder bed fusion additive manufacturing

The PBF-LB process of Al alloys was conducted on a commercial SLM Solutions 280 system under an argon atmosphere, with the oxygen level well controlled below 0.1 vol%. The printing process was optimized with the following parameters: a laser power of 370 W, a scan speed of 1300 mm/s, a hatch spacing of 0.18 mm, a layer thickness of 0.03 mm, and a platform temperature of 25 °C (without preheating). A standard alternating x/y raster scan pattern with a 67° angle between adjacent layers was employed as the scanning strategy. The 10 × 10 × 10 mm3 cubic samples were prepared for densification and microstructural characterization, and cylinder samples with a length of 76 mm and a diameter of 17 mm were printed for tensile testing.

Densification behavior

The internal defects of the as-printed samples were examined using a GE Phoenix micro-focus CT (micro-CT) system operated at 190 kV and 20 μA. A small cubic specimen (~3 × 3 × 3 mm³) was extracted from the center of the larger as-printed cubes using electro-discharge machining for high-resolution imaging. During data acquisition, the sample was rotated from 0° to 360° with a step size of 0.2°, and an exposure time of 500 ms per projection was used with a spatial resolution of 2024 × 2024 pixels, achieving a voxel size of ~3.5 μm. The raw data were reconstructed into a 3D data field using VGSTUDIO Max 3.2 software to reveal internal defect characteristics and quantify the overall relative density. Although the use of a smaller sample is necessary for high-resolution imaging, it may not fully reflect the distribution of extremely large or rare defects. However, considering the high relative density (>99.9%) and process stability, the measured volume provides a reliable assessment of the bulk material.

Microstructural characterizations

MPs in the Al alloy were observed using a Zeiss Axio Observer 3 optical microscope (OM). For SEM, the as-printed samples were prepared using standard metallographic procedures without chemical etching. TEM specimens were prepared by using twin-jet electropolishing on a Struers TenuPol-5 system, followed by ion milling with a Gatan PIPS 695. Microstructural and chemical composition analyses were conducted using an FEI Quanta 450 SEM, a Talos TEM, and an FEI Titan Themis G2 TEM, each equipped with EDS. In situ heating experiments were performed in a spherical-aberration-corrected Titan G2 environmental TEM, where samples were heated from room temperature to target temperatures and held for 5 min to monitor microstructural evolution and thermal stability. Electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD) was performed on an FEI Scios Dual-Beam FIB/SEM equipped with an EDAX Hikari high-speed detector. Data collection was carried out at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV with a step size of 0.3 μm. For pattern collection, a 4 × 4 binning mode was utilized, providing a good balance between spatial resolution and acquisition speed. The indexing was based on the Hough transform method. Data processing and orientation reconstruction were performed using TSL OIM Analysis 8 software. To ensure data integrity, a Confidence Index (CI) cutoff of 0.1 was applied. Following the cutoff, a standard Grain Dilation cleanup procedure (minimum grain size of 5 pixels and a grain tolerance angle of 5°) was employed to remove minor indexing artifacts. All reported data meet these quality thresholds, ensuring the reliability of the orientation measurements.

The volume fraction of the multicomponent intermetallic nanophase was remeasured using image analysis of the area fractions by employing a commercial software package ImageJ (original ImageJ57), Over one hundred particles were included to determine an average value. Phase identification was performed via XRD (Rigaku SmartLab instrument) with Cu-Kα radiation at a scan speed of 2°/min. DSC experiments were performed on a NETZSCH 404 F3 Pegasus system under an argon flow of 50 mL/min. As-printed samples (~20 mg) were heated from 25 to 900 °C at 10 °C/min, held for 10 min, and cooled to 25 °C at the same rate. Needle-shaped samples for 3D APT (CAMECA LEAP 5000X HR) were prepared using the lift-out method and annular milling in an FEI Scios Dual-Beam system in the APT unit. The samples were analyzed in laser mode with a specimen temperature of 60 K, a laser energy of 30 pJ, a pulse rate of 200 kHz, and a detection rate of 0.5%.

Tensile and creep tests

Tensile tests were conducted on both the transverse and longitudinal sections of the as-printed samples. Round tensile specimens with a gauge length of 25 mm and a diameter of 6.25 mm were machined from the printed cylinders using electro-discharge machining (EDM). Tensile testing was performed on an MTS E45.105 machine in accordance with ASTM E8 (room temperature, 25 °C) and ASTM E21 standards (elevated temperatures: 100, 200, 300, and 400 °C). An initial strain rate of 0.005 min−1 was applied, and the rate was subsequently increased to 0.05 min−1 at 0.8% strain for the elevated temperature tests to reduce the total test time, following the E21 protocol. Three repeats were tested for each condition, and yield strength was determined by the 0.2% offset method.

Tensile creep tests were carried out on the longitudinal sections of the as-printed samples. Standard creep specimens (GB/T 2039–2012) with a gauge length of 25 mm and a diameter of 5 mm were extracted via EDM. Prior to testing, the samples were heated from ambient temperature to 400 °C at 15 °C/min and held for 1 h. Tensile creep experiments were then performed at stresses of 25, 30, 32, and 40 MPa using an electronic high-temperature creep testing machine RDL10058.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. All data were included in the analyses, and no data points were excluded. Sample sizes for mechanical and microstructural tests were chosen based on standard practice in materials science to ensure reliable measurements. Each test (e.g., laser reflectivity of the raw powders and tensile tests at different temperatures) was repeated three times to confirm reproducibility.
