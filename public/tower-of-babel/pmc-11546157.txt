Lithium extraction from geothermal brine using γ-MnO2: A case study for Tuzla geothermal power plant
Authors: Seyra Toprak, Çınar Öncel, Sinan Yılmaz, Alper Baba, Gonca Aksoy Koç, Mustafa M Demir
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGeothermal brines contain high concentrations of ions and form a source of various valuable elements. The isolation of the elements from their water systems is a great challenge when the gradual depletion of ores in mining is considered. Attempts have been made for a long time to isolate valuable elements from aqueous mixtures prepared in the laboratory. However, those studies might not reflect the complexity of natural systems and might yield results that deviate significantly from the performance in real field systems. In this study, sorption is used to extract lithium ions from a representative field, Tuzla Geothermal Power Plant (TGPP) Turkey, using a mini-pilot reactor introduced to the reinjection well of the plant. Electrolytic manganese dioxide (γ-MnO2), a relatively inexpensive material widely used as the cathode material in lithium-ion batteries, was employed as a sorbent material for lithium. The sorption/desorption performance of the novel γ-MnO2 was investigated under various conditions. Sorption is performed at 360K and 2 bars. The maximum sorption performance was obtained at 1 h in Tuzla GPP. The desorption experiments were performed in acidic solutions. The concentration of Li+ in the desorption solution was found to be 25 mg/L on average when 10 g of γ-MnO2 was dispersed into 30 mL of the acidic aqueous solution. The first desorption solution was used consecutively for collecting more Li+ ions through the desorption of fresh brine-treated powder samples (cumulative desorption). By repeating this process four times consecutively, 230 mg/L of Li+ was obtained in the desorption solution. Moreover, the reusability of the γ-MnO2 sorbent was examined. The sorbent powder showed almost 40% performance efficiency compared to virgin powder under the conditions employed in this study. The use of electrolytic γ-MnO2 sorbent for lithium adsorption was found to be a promising process for practical use in the separation of lithium from geothermal brines.Keywords: Lithium extraction, Geothermal brine, Adsorption technology, Electrolytic manganese dioxide

Introduction

Lithium is the lightest and 25th most abundant metal. It has many attractive physical and chemical properties, such as a high electrochemical potential of 3.04 V and a high specific heat capacity (3.489 J/g∙mol at 20 °C) [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Therefore, in recent years, lithium compounds have been extensively used in a variety of industries, including the glass and ceramics industry, lubricating greases, metallurgical industries, chemicals/pharmaceuticals, rubbers, and most notably lithium-ion batteries [3,5,6]. The global demand for lithium in the production of lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) has grown exponentially in recent years and is expected to increase further due to the widespread use of rechargeable lithium-ion batteries in the growing portable electronics market and especially in electric vehicles [[7], [8], [9], [10]].

Lithium can be obtained from various natural sources classified as solid and liquid sources. The former consist of mineral ores, clays, and recycled waste lithium-ion batteries, while the latter ones are mainly salt-lake brine, geothermal brine, and seawater [11]. Lithium extraction currently relies on conventional sources like hard-rock ores (mainly spodumene) and salt lake brines. However, the growing demand is driving interest in the use of unconventional resources like geothermal brines and seawater.[[12], [13]]The global identified resource for lithium is estimated by the U.S. Geological Survey to be approximately 98 million tons (Mt). The majority of these resources are found in Bolivia (21 Mt), the United States (9.1 Mt), Argentina (19 Mt), Chile (9.8 Mt), Australia (7.3 Mt), and China (5.1 Mt). The estimated amount of global lithium reserves is approximately 26 Mt [14]. Although the extraction of lithium from lithium-bearing ores such as spodumene is currently the dominant source for extraction, it poses significant challenges in terms of energy consumption and carbon dioxide emissions. The acid leaching commonly used in this method requires significant resources and produces a large amount of waste, namely lithium slag [13,15]. Improper disposal of lithium slag from spodumene processing not only occupies a significant land area, but also leads to environmental pollution if not managed effectively, as leaching of sulphatic compounds from discharged lithium slag poses serious health risks [16,17]. Uptake of valuable elements from aqueous systems is preferred due to being already dissolved in an aqueous solution. The recovery of lithium from brine is much more economical, environmentally friendly, and easier to process than recovering lithium from hard rock mining [7]. The current method of extracting lithium from salt lake brines is the lime-soda evaporation process, which relies on solar evaporation in large open-air tanks, resulting in increased lithium concentrations of the brine. However, the traditional evaporation technique also has significant disadvantages: it is a time-consuming process, that consumes high amounts of water and produces large amounts of waste [18]. That is why the development of an effective method for extracting lithium from unconventional sources such as geothermal brines gains great importance and interest [19].

Although brines are important sources of lithium, they also contain various other ions, making it challenging to extract lithium from brine sources selectively. Typically, brines are rich in Na+ and they also contain significant amounts of Mg2+ and Ca2+. Lithium must be isolated from other coexisting ions to recover high-purity lithium products from brines. Li+ and Mg2+ are more difficult to separate than other ions because they have comparable chemical properties and ionic hydration radii [1,20,21]. For comparison, lithium has an ionic radius of 0.79 Å, and magnesium has an ionic radius of 0.72 Å [22]. Nevertheless, it is reported that the production of lithium salts from geothermal brines has the potential to be cost-effective and represents a critical lithium resource [[7], [23]].A geothermal power plant in the Salton Sea, USA, is the best known example of demonstrating the potential of geothermal brine as a significant source of lithium. This plant recovers and recycles around 15,000 tons of lithium salt annually, highlighting the feasibility of cost-effective lithium production from geothermal brines [7,24]. Türkiye is positioned in the Alpine-Himalayan folds, and owing to its favorable geological conditions, the country possesses rich geothermal resources [25]. The Tuzla geothermal field on Türkiye's Biga Peninsula is important since it contributes to the energy generation of the country and has a comparatively high lithium concentration of roughly 25 mg/L. For example, the Ömer-Gecek region, one of the most critical geothermal areas of Afyonkarahisar, has a lithium concentration of approximately 3.5 mg/L [25]. The Cumalı well in the Izmir Seferihisar Geothermal field contains around 13.4 mg/L [26]. The facility is also the 30th largest geothermal power plant in Türkiye in terms of energy capacity (7.5 MW). The system employs a flow rate of 48 tons of steam and 693 tons of superheated water per hour. The underground geothermal fluid cooled from its initial state to a temperature range of 80–95 °C after circulating through the power plant [[27], [28], [29]].

Numerous methods have been investigated to extract lithium from brines, including co-precipitation, solvent extraction, membrane processes, sorption, and ion exchange [30,31]. Among these methods, the sorption method stands out as one of the most promising techniques for recovering lithium from brines by virtue of its high selectivity and eco-friendly features [32]. The most widely used inorganic Li+ sorbents include aluminum-based adsorbents, clay minerals, carbon materials, and manganese oxide-based sorbents [33,34]. Among the manganese-oxide based adsorbents, spinel manganese oxides are potential lithium ion-sieve sorbents for separating lithium from brine solutions with high selectivity and adsorption capacity, low toxicity, and good chemical stability [35,36]. Various lithium ion sieves, including λ-MnO2, MnO2∙0.3H2O, and MnO2∙0.5H2O, have been widely developed in the past several years, and the adsorption capacities of manganese-oxide type lithium ion-sieves (LIS) have an average in the range of 17–50 mg/g [35,[37], [38], [39]]. Lithium adsorption and desorption in LIS, notably LMOs, are characterized by a reversible ion exchange process. It is believed that Li+ intercalation and deintercalation in LMO-type LIS occur via an ion exchange mechanism that allows protons to replace lithium ions in solids entirely [40].

There are several crystallographic polymorphs of manganese oxides (MnO2), including α-, β-, R-, γ-, δ-, and λ-polymorphs of MnO2. These polymorphs differ from one another in terms of their structural characteristics. Due to the way [MnO6] octahedra are linked together by corner or edge-sharing, tunnels or interlayers with various sizes of gaps occur [41]. Highly attracted to lithium ions, the λ-MnO2 polymorph has drawn significant research interest for its potential as a lithium adsorbent. This particular form of manganese oxide is created by removing lithium ions from a precursor material (LiMn2O4) through acid treatment. This process essentially leaves regular vacancies the size of lithium ions within the λ-MnO2 structure [42]. Thanks to its particular pore size of about 0.7 Å, the resulting spinel-type ion-sieve is highly selective for capturing lithium ions [22]. The crystal structure of electrolytic manganese oxide (EMD) is intimately associated with the ε-, β- and γ-polymorphs of MnO2. γ-MnO2 is known to be one of the best materials for battery applications and can be synthesized both chemically and electrochemically [43]. The structure of γ-MnO2 is thought to be an irregular intergrowth of both ramsdellite (R-MnO2) and pyrolusite (β-MnO2) [44]. Electrolytic γ-MnO2 has not been studied for Li + adsorption from brines before. However, its use as a cathode material in lithium-ion batteries has been extensively studied, and the intercalation of Li+ ions onto the γ-MnO2 structure has been investigated in these studies. In the study of Minakshi et al. [45], the electrochemical discharge of γ-MnO2 in aqueous LiOH media was studied, and the intercalation mechanism of lithium was investigated. It was observed that rectangular tunnels in the γ-MnO2 structure accept lithium ions (Li+) of comparable size to Mn4+, followed by Coulombic interaction. Thus, the structure is stable after the intercalation/extraction of lithium ions. Following lithium-ion intercalation into the tunnel vacancies, the subsequent desorption step is required to reverse the process by displacing the adsorbed Li+ ions from the γ-MnO2 structure with protons (H+).

In this study, the potential of electrolytic γ-MnO2, which is traditionally used as a cathode material in lithium-ion batteries and is also attractive due to being quite cost-effective, is investigated for lithium extraction from geothermal brine. The adsorption properties of γ-MnO2 for lithium are largely unexplored. Moreover, this study explores in-situ tests carried out in the field, going beyond the limitations of conventional laboratory testing. This novel approach addresses a critical gap in the field, as in-situ studies are rarely employed in lithium adsorption research. The characterization of γ-MnO2 powder was carried out using XRD, XRF, SEM, DLS and BET methods. Afterwards, batch adsorption experiments were carried out on a lab scale using a synthetic lithium solution for the analytical characterization of the sorbent powder. The field experiment was performed in a representative Tuzla Geothermal Power Plant (TGPP) to investigate the sorption performance of γ-MnO2 powder in field conditions. The desorption performance of the brine-treated powder was studied in an acidic environment. Li+ is isolated from geothermal brine and enriched in the desorption solution.

Experimental section

Materials

The γ-MnO2 powder employed as the sorbent material for the lithium adsorption study was purchased from China Changsha Easchem Co., Ltd. Lithium chloride (LiCl) (anhydrous, analytical grade), was supplied from Loba Chemie (Mumbai, India), and used without further purification. For the desorption experiments, Hydrochloric Acid (HCl) ACS reagent 37% was supplied from ISOLAB (Wertheim, Germany). Sodium Hydroxide (NaOH) (pellets, puriss grade) was supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). For the preparation of buffer solutions, ammonia solution (%25 w/w) and NH4Cl (ammonium chloride) powder of 99 % purity were supplied from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany)

Morphological and surface characterization of the γ-MnO2

An X-ray diffractometer (XRD), the Philips X'Pert Pro (Eindhoven, Netherlands), was used to determine the crystalline structure of the sorbent powder and the phases it contains, with a copper anode as an X-ray source, a generator voltage of 45 kV, a tube current of 40 mA, and a Cu-Kα wavelength of 1.5406 Å. The sample was scanned between the 2θ angles of 10°–80°, and the scanning rate was 0.08° per second. The elemental composition of the sorbent powder was determined by using Energy Dispersive X-ray Fluorescence (EDXRF) spectrometry, SPECTRO IQ II (Kleve, Germany). The powder was analyzed at a voltage of 25 kV and 50 kV and 0.5 with 1.0 mA current for 50 s of duration. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), FEI Quanta 250 Feg (Oregon, USA), was used to analyze the morphology of the sorbent. For the particle size and zeta potential measurements, Malvern dynamic light scattering (DLS) Nano-ZS instrument (Worcestershire, UK) was used. The specific surface area, pore volume, and average pore diameter of γ-MnO2 powder were measured by The Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method using the nitrogen gas (N2) adsorption-desorption at 77 K. For zeta potential measurements, 10 mg of γ-MnO2 was dispersed in 100 mL of 1 mmol/L NaCl solution, the pH was adjusted to values ranging from 2 to 10 with 0.1 M HCl and NaOH, and the suspension was allowed to settle for 24 h before measurement.

Batch adsorption experiments

For the analytical characterization of the powder, batch adsorption experiments were performed using synthetic LiCl solutions with various Li+ concentrations. Batch adsorption experiments were carried out by mixing 60–600 mg of γ-MnO2 powder with 30 mL solutions of certain Li+ concentrations in 50 mL beakers to investigate the effect of different sorbent dosages and initial Li+ concentrations. Experiments were conducted at room temperature with constant stirring for 24 h. The mixture obtained after the adsorption experiment was taken and centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 30 min. The remaining solid was isolated from the decantate, then the lithium-ion concentration in the decantate was measured by the flame photometry method. Sherwood 360 Flame photometer (Cambridge, UK) was used for the analysis. The concentrations of Li+ in the solutions were first determined by creating a calibration curve using standard solutions with known Li+ ion concentrations.

Sample preparations LiCl solutions in the concentration range of 25–200 mg/L were prepared by dissolving LiCl in deionized water. For the pH adjustments, 1M of HCl and 2M of NaOH solutions were prepared from %37 HCl solution and NaOH pellets. The buffer solutions were prepared using 1 mol/L NH3 and 1 mol/L NH4Cl solutions. The pH value of the buffer solution was adjusted using different molar ratios of NH3/NH4Cl.

Field experiments (sorption)

Field experiments were performed in a representative field Tuzla Geothermal Power Plant (TGPP), and the concentration of several existing ions taken from the retention pond of the TGPP is given in Table 1. The additional physico-chemical characteristics of the Tuzla geothermal brine, including the electrical conductivity, pH and alkalinity are provided in Table S1. The Mg/Li ratio in geothermal brine has been observed to be approximately 4, which is below the critical level known to cause potential problems for extraction [[47], [48]]. However, the geothermal brine also contains a significant amount of Na+. It is predicted that Na+ may compete with Li+ for adsorption sites, thereby potentially hindering lithium recovery efficiency. A mini-pilot system was installed in the reactor near the reinjection well of the plant. It is considered to be the most convenient placement of the system since energy harvesting is already done before the lithium uptake process. The γ-MnO2 powder was placed inside the reactor. Textile filters, made out of polyester fibers, are used to place the sorbent powder inside. The filters have a high filtering capacity, providing minimum powder leaks and resistance to temperature and oxidation. An adhesive epoxy resin resistant to temperatures in the range of -55 °C–125 °C with good flexibility was applied to the coverage of the filter to keep the sorbent powder inside it, and prevent any powder release from the textile filter under the brine flow pressure. A total of 60 filters filled with γ-MnO2 powder were adjusted to the reactor's geometry and placed inside the reactor.

Desorption

A series of experiments were conducted to investigate the desorption behavior of brine-treated γ-MnO2 in an acidic solution. Initially, the brine-treated γ-MnO2 powder was scraped out of the filters and dried at RT for several days. Then 0.5–10 g of brine-treated sorbent powder was put into 30 mL of diluted hydrochloric acid (1.0 M HCl) and mixed continuously for 3h–48h. The idea is to replace Li+ ions placed in the vacancies of the γ-MnO2 structure with H+ (protons). Then, the obtained mixture was taken and centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 30 min. The remaining solid was isolated from the decantate, and the Li+ concentration in the decantate was measured by the flame photometry method. The study investigated how three key factors – sorption time in the field, brine-treated sorbent amount, and desorption solution pH – influence lithium desorption from field-treated sorbents. Both cumulative desorption for enriching Li + concentration in the desorption solution and reusability testing of the sorbent were conducted.

Cumulative desorption. First, 10 g of sorbent powder treated with geothermal brine was continuously stirred in 30 mL of 1.0 M HCl at RT for 24 h. Afterwards, the desorption solution, from which a certain amount of lithium was desorbed, was separated by centrifugation. Then, 10 g of fresh sorbent powder treated with the geothermal field was added to the desorption solution, and the same procedure was repeated. This procedure was repeated four times and the lithium concentration was measured by flame photometry at each step.

Reusability of the sorbent. To test the reusability of the powder, the desorption-emptied sorbent powder was used again in the field study at Tuzla GPP. Afterwards, the brine-treated powder, which came back from the field, was stirred at room temperature in 1.0 M HCl for 24 h and 48 h, respectively. The fresh brine-treated powder was compared with reused powder in terms of the amounts of lithium released by desorption.

Results and discussion

Morphological and surface characterization of the γ-MnO2

XRD analysis of γ-MnO2sorbent.Fig. 2a presents the XRD pattern of the γ-MnO2 before and after treatment with geothermal brine in the field. The reflections corresponding to the samples before and after treatment with geothermal brine are similar. This result indicates that Li + sorption does not affect the structure of the sorbent. The reflections were located at 2θ angles of 22.40°, 37.31°, 42.74°, and 56.47° for the sorbent before being treated with brine, and it was observed that after the brine treatment, the reflections shifted slightly to lower angles of 22.24°, 37.14° and 42.62° and 56.28°. The lattice spacing (d) corresponding to the given diffraction peaks was calculated as 3.97 Å, 2.41 Å, 2.12 Å, and 1.63 Å for the powder as is. After treatment with brine, the calculated spacing values for the sorbent were 4.0 Å, 2.42 Å, 2.12 Å, and 1.64 Å. This can be interpreted as the lattice expansion caused by lithium ions entering the tunnel structure after sorption, causing a slight decrease in 2θ angles and an increase in the lattice spacing(d). The reflections are indexed to the (001) plane of the ramsdellite (R-MnO2) phase and (100), (101), (102), and (110) planes of the akhtenskite (ε-MnO2) phase respectively. The multiphase XRD model of the γ-MnO2 is in agreement with the study reported by Kim et al. [50], which observed ε-MnO2 as an independent phase present in a conventional EMD material. The stability of the crystal structure infers the topotactic exchange of lithium and protons in the crystal lattice of the sorbent.

It was observed that the amount of Na+ in the sorbent increased after the sorption process. This is an expected result, considering the high Na+ content in the geothermal brine. Upon treatment with brine, Na+ most probably adsorbs on the surface of the powder. The same applies to other elements present in the brine, such as Sr2+, Cl−, and Ca2+. Their adsorption on the powder surface causes the elements' relative proportions to vary according to the powder's total weight, as revealed in Table 2. Furthermore, after desorption, the content of elements other than Mn has partially decreased. However, some adsorbed ions might have remained attached to the powder's surface through electrostatic forces, preventing their complete recovery after a single desorption process. The desorption process is controlled by a diffusion energy barrier, limiting the release of ions.

SEM images. The morphology of the γ-MnO2 before and after the brine treatment is shown in Fig. 2b and c, respectively. The powder material appears to be composed of almost spherical primary particles with submicron diameters, which are packed in the form of large aggregates. No remarkable physical change was observed in the morphology of the γ-MnO2 following the sorption process. It was observed that the powder was resistant to both the temperature and salinity of brine, indicating MnO2 is a stable oxide material in terms of structure and morphology.

Zeta potential and particle size distribution. The zeta potential was measured in the pH range of 2–10, and the results are shown in Fig. 2d. Surface charge of the powder was negative beyond a pH of ∼3.5. Hydrolysis may take place for the surface hydroxyl groups that are inevitably present on metal oxide surfaces [52]. As the pH increases, the negative charge becomes increasingly dominant. The increase in the negative charge of the powder surface increases the attractive force between the positively charged lithium ion and the surface, thus achieving higher sorption efficiency. The zeta potential results of this study is consistent with the literature reports. For example, Zhang et al. [53] compared surface zeta potentials of the varied MnO2 polymorphs, including α-, β-, γ-, δ-, and λ-MnO2 as a function of solution pH and observed that due to the continual deprotonation effect of surface hydroxyl groups, the zeta potential values of all sorbents decreased. The point of zero charge (pzc) of γ-MnO2 was determined as 3.5 which is compatible with the range of PZC values of manganese oxides, which are given as 2.0–4.5 in the literature [54]. The particle size distribution of γ-MnO2 nanoparticles was measured by the Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) method, as given in Fig. 2e. The average diameter of the particles was found at 50 nm, and the tailing of the distribution extended to 400 nm. Individual particles measured by scattering are uniform in diameter and there is no particle population larger than 400 nm. Not surprisingly, the size of the particles observed in SEM is larger than the values obtained from scattering since the microscopy is imaged from aggregated dry powder.

BET analysis. The surface characterization of the γ-MnO2 powder was conducted using the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method with N₂ adsorption-desorption at 77 K. The measured specific surface area was 33.7 m2/g, the total pore volume was 0.065 cm³/g, and the average pore diameter was 76.9 Å (7.69 nm). These findings suggest that the γ-MnO2 possesses well-developed mesopores (2–50 nm range) [55], potentially favorable for mass transport and lithium-ion accessibility within its structure.

Batch adsorption experiments

Effect of sorbent amount. The sorbent dose is another critical parameter in adsorption studies since it indicates the material's ability to adsorb metal ions for a particular initial sorbent concentration [56]. Fig. 3 illustrates the experimental data regarding the effect of sorbent dosage on the percentage removal of lithium ions from solution, and the figure also includes the sorbate uptake (mg/g) at various doses of the sorbent. The results revealed that increasing the sorbent dosage from 2 g/L to 20 g/L increased the adsorption percentage from 17% to 98%. The increase in the percentage removal could be attributed to an increase in the surface area, and the accompanying increase in the quantity of active sites that are present on the surface of γ-MnO2. Although most lithium ends up in the tunnels, surface area matters for the initial uptake rate. A larger surface area provides more entry points for lithium ions to come into contact with the γ-MnO2, facilitating faster diffusion towards the tunnel openings. On the other hand, it was observed that the sorbate uptake decreased with increasing sorbent dose. This can be attributed to the decrease in effective loading, which refers to the amount of lithium ions adsorbed per unit sorbent mass, since the lithium amount is constant while the sorbent amount increases. It results in more active sites of adsorbent remaining unsaturated. Another reason could be as follows: with more adsorbent particles present, they may start to aggregate, reducing the total surface area available for adsorption. Additionally, diffusion of Li ions through the solution to reach the active sites on the inner particles can become slower, further limiting the overall adsorption rate. With an increase in adsorbent weight, this aggregation becomes more significant [57]. The extent of sorption was 6.2 mg/g when the sorbent concentration was 3 g/L. This value is lower than the values reported for λ–MnO2 in the literature, which lie between 15 and 50 mg/g for various conditions [58]. Although the obtained adsorption capacity seems lower, it is very important to consider that in our case, a different MnO2 phase was used with various process conditions. While λ–MnO2 exhibits superior lithium uptake performance through the ion sieving effect, our focus is on commercially available γ-MnO2. Being commercially available, relatively low cost, and ability to be supplied on a large scale within a few days make it a practical candidate for the field applications. This point contrasts with laboratory-synthesized materials, which are often limited in quantity and can only be used for laboratory experiments. While conventional lithium manganese oxides boast dedicated vacancies specific for lithium-ion insertion, γ-MnO2, typically studied for its electrochemical activity in batteries, might be expected to exhibit lower adsorption performance. Despite this specific advantage in lithium ion-sieve MnO2, γ-MnO2 can still capture lithium ions. Its rectangular tunnels are suitable for accommodating lithium ions of similar size to the Mn4+ cations present, facilitating lithium uptake through a combination of size compatibility and Coulombic interaction.

Effect of solution pH and initial Li+ concentration. The effect of solution pH on the extent of Li+ uptake is given in Fig. 4a. The data shows a remarkable increase in ion sorption as the pH value of the solution increases. The uptake of lithium ions by the γ-MnO2 sorbent was found to be distinctly higher in a basic medium, as the surface sites were more negatively charged at higher pH levels. Consequently, as the electrostatic interaction between the positively charged Li+ and the sorbent surface became stronger, the surface exhibited a larger affinity for sequestration of the Li+ ions [7]. This result is also compatible with studies in the literature on the effect of pH on extent of sorption. Park et al. [59] studied the change of lithium sorption in the pH range of 2–10 and observed that the extent of sorption increased as the pH increased and reached the highest sorption value of 6–7 mg/g at pH 10. Another example from the literature, Yang et al. [60] also studied lithium sorption of λ-MnO2 in the pH range of 4–11 and observed that the extent of sorption is decreased below 4 mg/g under acidic conditions. The sorption extent on λ-MnO2 increased sharply as the pH increased and reached a maximum of above 24 mg/g at pH>10 indicating that the alkaline medium favored lithium sorption. The increase in the sorption amount is due to the electrostatic interactions associated with the deprotonation effect of the surface hydroxyl group as a result of increasing the pH, which is valid for manganese oxides and is interpreted in the same way for γ-MnO2 [61]. The variation of the extent of sorption as a function of initial Li+ concentration is given in Fig. 4b. The uptake is found to increase as the Li+ concentration increases. The extent of removal reached a value of 6.4 mg/g when the ionic concentration was 200 mg/L. It is proposed that boundary layer diffusion and intraparticle diffusion are the two stages that control the sorption process. By increasing the initial Li+ concentration, the surface of the adsorbent becomes in contact with more Li+ ions, which results in the diffusion of more Li+ through the boundary layer in a unit time [62]. In other words, the surface of the sorbent may not have been fully bound with the sorbates at the beginning of the sorption process, whereas at the optimum initial lithium concentration, all surfaces of the active site may have been filled with adsorbates [63]. In addition, increasing the ionic concentration at the boundary of a porous solid with a liquid solution enhances the intraparticle and surface diffusion of the sorbate entities, thus contributing to increasing the ionic uptake by the solid.

Desorption experiments after field study

Effect of sorption time in Tuzla GPP. Sorption time was systematically investigated in the field for durations ranging from 1 to 24 h. The sorbent powder was subsequently put into an acidic solution, under lab conditions, in order to desorb Li+ ions. Fig. 6a shows the concentration of the desorbed lithium ions, after various sorption times. The maximum concentration of desorbed Li+ ions is achieved when the sorption time is shorter. As the sorption time is extended, the concentration of the desorbed Li+ ions released decreases. This could be attributed to diffusion effects. As the sorption time increases, the ions will have better chances for intraparticle and surface diffusion inside the solid matrix, and as such, their desorption becomes harder. On the contrary, shorter sorption times will fix the Li+ ions on the outermost sorption sites, from which desorption is relatively easier. Another potential reason for this is that the quality of the sorbent could be reduced by the blocking of lithium adsorption sites by other coexisting ions, resulting in low desorption performance. In addition, the time-dependent lithium adsorption performance of MnO2 was evaluated in the batch adsorption studies carried out in the laboratory and the sorption reached equilibrium and maximum adsorption performance after 24 h. This finding is consistent with the studies in the literature [65]. The difference in the maximum adsorption performance obtained in a short time in the field study may be due to the fact that the field experiment was carried out in a non-equilibrium system with continuous flow, not in an equilibrium system where better interaction of the sorbent material with high volume of brine in the reinjection process.

Effect of brine-treated γ-MnO2amount and pH of the desorption medium. The concentration of desorbed Li+ in the decantate solutions obtained from various amounts of Li-loaded γ-MnO2 is given in Fig. 6b. As the amount of sorbent increased, the amount of Li+ released into the solution increased, and the concentration of Li+ reached to a maximum of approximately 26 mg/L when 10 g of γ-MnO2 was used. This is an expected result; as the amount of Li-loaded sorbent increases, more lithium ions can be replaced with hydronium ions, resulting in more Li+ ions being released into the acidic environment. As shown in Fig. S1, the acidic environment enhanced the desorption process, and 19.6 mg/L lithium was desorbed when the pH was highly acidic. This is because when the pH of the solution is lower, there is more H+ in the medium, and the desorption mechanism of lithium becomes controlled by the ion exchange mechanism with H+ ions in the solution.

Cumulative desorption and Li + enrichment. The brine is quite complex and rich in terms of various elements. After all of the isolation efforts, the concentration of Li+ in the desorption solution was 25 mg/L on average, in single batch trials. This level of concentration is considered to be low. Cumulative desorption was performed in order to increase the yield of desorbed Li+. The experiments were performed by contacting the same desorption solution to fresh Li-loaded sorbent powders in a subsequent manner. The results are given in Fig. 7a. At the end of the 4th cycle, the Li+ concentration increased from 27 mg/L to 230 mg/L. The higher the Li+ concentration of the solution obtained after desorption, the easier it is to precipitate and extract lithium from the solution. Li+ ions can be precipitated as Li2CO3 or AlLiO2. For example, Zhu et al. [66] suggested that increasing the lithium concentration enhanced the lithium recovery rate considerably, especially when the lithium concentration was in the 5–20 g/L range.

Reusability of γ-MnO2sorbent. The repetitive usage of the γ-MnO2 sorbent material is worth considering from an economic point of view. The sorption performance of the γ-MnO2 powder is re-examined in the field after desorption in an acidic solution. As stated above, γ-MnO2 contains vacancies in the crystal structure, which is assumed to be filled with Li+ ions. The desorption process makes these vacancies empty again by releasing the lithium back out, and the remaining vacancies may be suitable for the second round of sorption. By performing a second sorption cycle following desorption, the study investigates the effectiveness of the sorbent regeneration process and its potential for repeated lithium capture. Fig. 7b shows the Li+ concentration released by the fresh brine-treated powder and the reused powder after the second use, with desorption times of 24 h and 48 h, respectively. The figure further explores the influence of brine-treated sorbent amount, ranging from 2 to 10 g, on the desorption behavior. The concentration was 9.4 mg/L after 24 h of desorption, although the fresh powder yields 25 mg/L. It slightly increases to 11.1 mg/L when the desorption time is extended to 48 h. Thus, the reuse of the powder shows nearly 40% performance compared to virgin powder under the employed conditions.

Conclusions

The sorption performance of relatively inexpensive electrolytic manganese dioxide (γ-MnO2) compared to λ-MnO2 was investigated for the isolation of lithium ions from the geothermal brine of the Tuzla Geothermal Power Plant (TGPP). A mini-pilot reactor is employed using commercial γ-MnO2. Brine-treated sorbent powder is subjected to desorption in an acidic medium. From the analytical characterization of the sorbent, the optimum working conditions were determined as pH 12, adsorbent concentration of 3 g/L, and initial Li+ concentration of 200 mg/L. The highest extent of Li+ sorption was obtained as 6.4 mg/g. The Langmuir isotherm model shows that the maximum monolayer capacity of Li+ sorption was 9.74 mg/g. Following field experiments at the Tuzla GPP, desorption of Li+ ions into a 30 ml acidic solution yielded 25 mg/L of Li+ when 10 g of γ-MnO2 sorbent was used. The maximum sorption performance was achieved at 1 h of adsorption in the field, showing that the shorter sorption time is favorable. Cumulative desorption experiments were conducted to enrich the lithium concentration of the solution. The lithium concentration was enriched to over 230 mg/L after four desorption cycles. This concentration level could be further enriched by increasing the number of cycles. Due to the economic importance, reusability studies of sorbent powder were carried out, and 40% performance was observed for reused sorbent compared to the fresh sorbent under the conditions applied in this study. Based on the obtained results of the performed experiments, and by also considering the cheaper price and wide availability, γ-MnO2 sorbent was found as a promising candidate for a new approach of Li+ ion sorption, and for the practical use in the separation of Li+ ions from geothermal brines.
