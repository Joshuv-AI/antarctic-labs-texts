Lifestyle interventions for obesity prevention among adolescents in Pacific Islands Countries and Territories: a narrative review of outcomes and sociocultural, spatial and temporal dimensions
Authors: Olivier Galy, Marie-Jeanne Urvoy, Solène Bertrand, Corinne Caillaud, Jacquie Bay, Stephane Frayon, Guillaume Wattelez, Margaret Allman-Farinelli, David Raubenheimer, Rachel Novotny, Anthony Okely
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis narrative review synthesizes peer-reviewed studies of diet and/or physical activity lifestyle interventions for obesity prevention among adolescents in Pacific Island Countries and Territories. Nine databases were searched from inception to June 2024 for English and French publications. Of 2068 records identified, nine studies from Fiji, Tonga, New Caledonia, and French Polynesia met inclusion criteria. Outcomes extracted included anthropometric indicators of body fatness, physical activity, and diet, alongside information on how interventions addressed sociocultural (communities and cultural practices), spatial (settings and rurality), and temporal (dose, duration, seasonality) dimensions. Among adolescents aged 11.8–15.4 years, intervention effects on anthropometric indicators of body fatness were mixed and generally small. Assessment of physical activity and diet was often incomplete, limiting interpretation. Future research should use robust outcome measures and participatory, locally led designs that integrate sociocultural context and align with local policy.Keywords: Intervention, Physical activity, Diet, Overweight, Health, Children, Melanesia, Polynesia, Co-design, Youth

Introduction

During the last two decades, rapid socio–economic transition and health crises such as the COVID-19 pandemic, have occurred alongside increasing levels of childhood overweight and obesity in the 22 Pacific Islands Countries and Territories (PICT's includes the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands, Federated States of Micronesia, Guam, Kiribati, Nauru, Palau, Republic of Marshall Islands, American Samoa, Cook Islands, French Polynesia, Fiji, New Caledonia, Niue, Solomon Islands, Papua New Guinea, Pitcairn, Samoa, Tuvalu, Tokelau, Tonga, Vanuatu, Wallis and Futuna).1,2 Colonisation of cultures and more recently westernisation have negatively influenced lifestyles (i.e.: physical activity and diet), and fueled the obesity pandemic, and, as a consequence, impacted the health and well-being of PICT populations.3

Structural and demographic features of PICTs further shape this transition. Very small land mass alongside economic and structural constraints and reliance on imported foods that are common across PICTS, influence food security and the prevalence of obesity.4,5 Today, eight (Niue, Cook Islands, Nauru, Tonga, Tokelau, Tuvalu, American Samoa, Palau) of the 22 PICTs are among the 10 countries with the highest prevalence of child overweight and obesity globally. PICTs represent over 10 million inhabitants with a median age of 24 years.1 Among this group, 3.5 million adolescents under the age of 15 are at risk of developing obesity, which in turn increases the likelihood of their offspring developing obesity.2

Adolescence is a period of rapid psychological, social and biological development and maturation associated with the transition from childhood to adulthood.6 During this period, increasing agency allows adolescents to experiment with and develop behaviour patterns, including those influencing healthy lifestyle habits. Political, economic, educational, technological, and religious realities shape adolescent's environments and recent changes to these factors are influencing adolescents lifestyle in PICT.7 To date, published literature offers a limited view on Pacific adolescent's lifestyle, with little alignment of data on physical activity (PA), diet, and anthropometric indicators of fatness according to different socio-cultural environments, contexts and time periods.

While there are commonalities within PICTs, there are also important genetic, cultural, and socio-ecological differences that influence health and wellbeing3 and these can be considered as specific drivers of adolescent obesity. Here, sociocultural refers to norms and practices shaping health behaviours (e.g., family, school, community and religious contexts, and media influences).3,8, 9, 10, 11 If sociocultural factors can explain, to a large degree, differences in the prevalence of obesity between populations, they might also be points of leverage to address obesity.

The spatial dimension plays an important role in the 165,250,000 km2 covered by Pacific Ocean (approximately one third of the total surface area of the Earth) and in the 800,000 km2 covered by Pacific Island region. Regional collaboration to align broad policy directions and share evidence across the Pacific region may be beneficial, while intervention development and evaluation must remain locally adapted. It is important to recognise that across the diverse cultural contexts of the region, interactions among multiple knowledge systems, inclusive of science, religion, indigenous, local and traditional knowledges, influence the development of policies that are socially acceptable and offer the potential for positive outcomes. Common within island cultures is a unique sense of the relationship between land and sea, and concepts of diet, physical activity and identity typically extend into the ocean.12 Moreover, each island encompasses different spaces, including urban, peri-urban, rural and tribal areas. Adolescents living in a predominantly tribal/indigenous environment may follow more traditional Pacific lifestyles, while those living in higher-density urban areas may have adopted a more “Western way of life”.10,13

Daily activities for adolescents in rural or tribal areas have a unique temporal dimension influenced with the relationship of daily life to the environment of land and sea. Weekday routines are commonly structured by school attendance, which typically includes physical education and sports, where available. As a result, schooling provides a shared framework of activities for adolescents across tribal, rural, and urban settings, since curricula and attendance schedules (days and hours) are generally standardized at the national level. For example, in Polynesia and New Caledonia, adolescent schooling follows similar teaching methods and programmes, including physical education, that are broadly aligned with those used in mainland France. These arrangements apply to adolescents across the territory, including those living in isolated or tribal communities. The traditional Pacific lifestyle is characterised by a strong emphasis on community activities (traditional and religious duties, festivals, and ceremonies) and subsistence activities (gardening, fishing, and hunting).14, 15, 16 Adolescents are involved in such activities at a relatively early age. In PICTs, daily schedules (i.e., temporal dimension) align with solar time, especially in rural and tribal areas, with days starting and ending earlier than in cities of other Pacific countries (i.e., Australia or New Zealand). Seasonal changes and risks such as heat, floods and winds also strongly influence lifestyle activities.

Lifestyle interventions for children in non-Pacific Island contexts such as Australia, Europe and the USA report contrasting results regarding their effects on obesity.17, 18, 19 To positively impact lifestyle behavior, especially in the Pacific region, social support, supportive food environments, motivation and knowledge are crucial during adolescence.13 For children and adolescents in Pacific Island settings, evidence from individual studies suggests that drivers for engagement in PA and healthy eating include health knowledge, self-directed opportunities to be active and to access healthy foods, and competence in a range of types of PA.20 Systematic reviews of Western studies have suggested that school-based interventions involving families or communities can be beneficial.17,19 To promote sustainability, school-based health promotion interventions may be codesigned with educators to enable integration within the core functions of curriculum and pastoral care and adaptability to meet contextual needs.21,22 However, the extent to which published PICT interventions report these components, measure outcomes consistently and describe sociocultural, spatial and temporal context has not been synthesised. Moreover, the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic (around two years of closing borders and repeated lockdowns)23 may have disrupted food systems, schooling and opportunities for physical activity in PICT's, potentially influencing lifestyle transitions over a short period of time.

This narrative review synthesises evidence on lifestyle interventions designed to promote PA and healthy eating among adolescents in PICTs. We summarise reported outcomes for anthropometric indices of body fatness, physical activity and diet, and describe how sociocultural, spatial, and temporal dimensions were considered. The review also identifies priority areas for future research in PICTs. The relationship between lifestyle interventions and obesity is examined because it is a topic of high interest in the current literature; however, this review does not intend to cover every determinant of adolescent obesity. Instead, it focuses on physical activity and diet within specific sociocultural, spatial and temporal dimensions of obesity risk among adolescents living in PICTs.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

Nine electronic databases were searched: PsycINFO, CINAHL, EMBASE, EBSCO/AGRICOLA, PROQUEST (includes ERIC), Web of Science, MEDLINE/PUBMED- MeSH, Scopus, SPORTDiscus). The search was conducted from database inception up to and including June 2024. Searches were restricted to English and French publications and to adolescents aged 10–15 years. Eligible studies evaluated lifestyle interventions targeting diet and/or physical activity and reporting at least one relevant outcome (anthropometric indices of body fatness, physical activity and/or diet). Where available, we extracted participant sex as reported by the original studies; gender identity was not reported in the included studies. Search terms are detailed in Appendix A. We did not search grey literature (conference abstracts, theses, programme reports) because these sources often provide insufficient methodological detail for risk-of-bias assessment and are not consistently peer-reviewed.

Letters to the editors, commentaries and review articles were excluded from this review. Published studies must have been conducted within the 22 Pacific Islands Countries and Territories as defined by the Pacific Community.1 The geography of this vast region and PICTs can be seen in the first paper of this series.24 The primary outcome was overweight and/or obesity assessed by BMI category, standardised BMI score (BMI z-score) or other anthropometric indicators of fatness when available (e.g., body fat percentage, waist circumference, hip-to-waist ratio, or skin folds).

The PICO (population, intervention, intervention/exposure, comparator/control, and outcome) format25 was used to identify inclusion criteria which are described below.

Population

The population group included adolescents aged 10–15 years. We selected this age range to focus on early adolescence (typically 10–15 years) while capturing studies implemented in junior secondary school years in PICTs; studies were eligible if the target range fell within 10–15 years even when mean age at baseline extended slightly above 15 years (e.g., 15.4 years).

Intervention (exposure)

All included studies evaluated lifestyle interventions targeting diet and/or physical activity (PA). Interventions were eligible if they aimed to prevent overweight/obesity through behavioural, environmental, educational and/or community components addressing diet, PA, or both.

Comparator

Eligible study designs included (i) interventions with a concurrent comparison group (e.g., usual practice, no intervention, wait-list or alternative site), and/or (ii) pre–post designs with baseline and follow-up measures within the same participants.

Outcomes (indicators)

Primary outcomes were anthropometric indices of body fatness measures related to overweight/obesity (e.g., BMI, BMI-for-age z-score, waist circumference, waist-to-height ratio, skinfolds, body fat percentage) and behavioural outcomes (dietary intake/patterns and physical activity). In addition, we extracted descriptive information on three context dimensions when reported: (1) sociocultural dimension (who was involved and which cultural norms/practices were leveraged, e.g., families, churches, community leaders, traditional activities, local food production); (2) spatial dimension (delivery setting and geographic context, e.g., school/community, rurality/urbanicity, island context); and (3) temporal dimension (dose, duration and follow-up period, and any relevant seasonality or timing constraints, e.g., extreme weather).

The systematic search for this narrative review was done with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Checklist and Flow Diagram were used to guide the screening process (Fig. 1).26 After eliminating duplicates, a three-stage process was conducted in Covidence: (i) title/abstract screening, (ii) full-text screening, and (iii) reference list checking. Citation information, study design, population, location/setting, intervention components and outcomes were extracted. We also extracted participant age, sex and ethnicity as reported by the original studies; ethnicity categories were recorded using the original study definitions and are interpreted as sociocultural constructs, not biological traits.

Flow diagram of the review process.

Publication identification, data extraction

All references identified through the search strategy were downloaded into the reference management software Zotero and duplicate references were removed. References were then imported into Covidence for screening. Two reviewers initially screened references for potential inclusion based on title and abstract. Reviewers were not blinded to study author or journal. A third reviewer mediated any disagreements until consensus was reached. We obtained the full texts of studies deemed potentially eligible for inclusion, and two reviewers assessed the full texts for eligibility against the inclusion criteria. Those studies deemed as ineligible were recorded with reasons for ineligibility in the 'Characteristics of excluded studies' table. Any disagreements were resolved by consulting a third reviewer as necessary. Regarding any papers for which detail to determine the eligibility were lacking, we contacted the lead author.

Data extraction and management

Two reviewers extracted data onto templates developed for this review. In case of disagreement, a third reviewer mediated consensus.

Risk of bias (quality) assessment in included studies

The risk of bias for each included study was assessed independently by two reviewers using the ROBINS-I tool for non-randomised studies of interventions.27 Because all included evaluations used non-randomised or quasi-experimental designs. We assessed bias due to confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported result, and summarised judgments following ROBINS-I guidance.

The settings for these studies in adolescents (11.8–15.4 years) were primarily in schools, with some extending to communities and families.

Study design and population

The included studies were predominantly school-based and used quasi-experimental designs, with some programmes extending to families and communities. Two interventions were conducted in French Polynesia (RASA project).28,29 The intervention groups consisted of 240 and 226 adolescents, respectively. Swinburn et al.,30 Fotu et al.,31,32 Kremer et al.,33 and Schultz et al.34 conducted the Pacific Obesity Prevention in Communities project (OPIC) an international and multisite lifestyle intervention project across Fiji, Tonga, New Zealand, and Australia. While Swinburn et al.,30 as well as Schultz et al.,34 included New Zealand and Australia in their publication (paper concept and paper detailing experience post interventions), these countries were not considered in this review, as per the eligibility criteria previously described. These studies involved large sample sizes, with Swinburn et al.30 including 1500 Indo-Fijians and Fijians, and Fotu et al.32 involving 2868 adolescents. Waqa et al.35 also contributed to the OPIC project, focusing on schools and churches in Fiji, with a sample size of 18,000 participants. A French version of digital physical activity international program called “iEngage”36 was implemented in New Caledonia, involving secondary school students aged 11–14 years.

Intervention duration

Participant exposure to the interventions ranged from four weeks (iEngage, New Caledonia)36 to five years (Healthy Youth Healthy Communities, Fiji).35 Most interventions were multi-year, including OPIC-related programmes implemented over approximately three years in Fiji and Tonga.30, 31, 32, 33, 34

Group differences

Across studies, intervention groups received additional health promotion components compared with comparison groups (or compared with their own baseline), such as nutrition education, changes to school/community food environments, and/or additional physical activity opportunities (Tables 1 and 2).

Social, spatial and temporal dimensions of interventions

The interventions described in Table 2 were examined through sociocultural, spatial and temporal dimensions. The socio-cultural dimension commonly involved schools, families, churches and community organisations, and in some cases incorporated culturally salient practices (e.g., traditional canoe/kayak, community gardening and local food production).28,30, 31, 32, 33, 34,36 RASA studies in French Polynesia used traditional activities and local food production with participation from community members.28,29 OPIC-related studies in Tonga and Fiji involved schools, churches and community organisations, including adolescent 'champions' for change, and the Fiji process evaluation emphasised community capacity building, knowledge, skills and leadership.32,35,37 The New Caledonia iEngage intervention used digital delivery with role models such as local athletes and spiritual leaders.36 However, explicit reporting of formal participatory research or co-design processes was uncommon; most studies described community engagement or contextual adaptation without detailing decision-making processes.

Regarding the spatial dimension, interventions were delivered in diverse settings (school, community and/or family) and across rural, peri-urban and urban contexts.28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33,35 RASA studies considered contrasted school environments, while OPIC-related interventions intentionally included rural, peri-urban and urban sites.29, 30, 31, 32, 33,35 Nevertheless, few studies reported outcome analyses stratified by setting, rurality or school environment, limiting inference about whether intervention effects differed by spatial context.

For the temporal dimension, intervention exposure ranged from four weeks (iEngage, New Caledonia)36 to approximately five years (Healthy Youth Healthy Communities, Fiji),35 with most OPIC-related programmes implemented over two to three years.30, 31, 32,34,37 Some interventions increased scheduled physical education by two to 4 h per week or added structured activity sessions, whereas iEngage retained the existing physical education timetable and added digital education and activity tracking.28,29,36

Outcomes of interventions

Reported outcomes (Table 3) covered anthropometric indices of body fatness, physical activity and diet. Anthropometric outcomes were most consistently reported, whereas physical activity and diet outcomes were heterogeneous and often relied on self-report or were not reported. Because studies used different metrics and assessment schedules, findings are presented descriptively rather than pooled as an overall estimate of intervention effectiveness.

Anthropometry and body fatness

Across studies, changes in BMI and/or body fatness were mixed and generally small, with several evaluations reporting baseline differences between intervention and comparison groups (Table 3). In French Polynesia (RASA project; mean age ∼13–14 years), BMI changed minimally over follow-up (24.9–24.8 kg/m2 in the intervention group) while the comparison group increased from 21.5 to 22.5 kg/m2; statistical significance of change was not consistently reported and baseline group differences limit causal interpretation.29 In Tonga (Ma'alahi Youth Project; mean age 14.4–15.2 years) and Fiji (OPIC-related studies; mean age 15.2–15.4 years), BMI increased over time in both intervention and comparison groups,29, 30, 31,33,35 and where fat mass was measured changes were small.31,33 The short-duration iEngage intervention in New Caledonia (mean age 11.9 years) reported baseline anthropometry but did not report pre–post anthropometric change.36 Across the evidence base, anthropometric methods and follow-up schedules varied (e.g., BMI, %FM, waist-to-height ratio and different post-intervention/follow-up time points), limiting direct comparison between studies.

Physical activity

Physical activity outcomes were inconsistently reported. In French Polynesia, RASA interventions increased scheduled physical education by two to 4 h per week and added traditional activities, but did not report individual-level pre–post PA outcomes.28,29 In Tonga (Ma'alahi Youth Project), self-reported active transport (walking/cycling to/from school) and after–school activity decreased over follow-up in the intervention group, alongside increased lunchtime inactivity.31 In New Caledonia, the 4-week iEngage digital intervention used accelerometry/activity tracking and reported an 11% increase in steps per day (to 11,140 steps/day) and improved step consistency post-intervention.36

Dietary outcomes

Dietary outcomes were largely self-reported and were not consistently available across studies (Table 3). Dietary components, when reported, included nutrition education, changes to school/community food environments, food provision, promotion of fruit, vegetables and local/traditional foods, and discouragement of sugar-sweetened beverages, take-away foods and snack foods.28,35 In Tonga, Ma'alahi participants reported increased frequency of bringing lunch from home, which may reflect reduced purchasing of energy-dense snacks from shops or takeaway outlets; however, the nutritional quality of lunchboxes was not reported and several unfavourable changes were also observed (reduced breakfast consumption, increased sugary drinks and some snack foods after school).31 In Fiji, Healthy Youth Healthy Communities reported small changes in some food access indicators (e.g., lower reported access to snack foods after school for both groups) but also a higher proportion of the intervention group reporting <1 serving of vegetables per day at school at follow-up.32,35 RASA interventions described promotion of local/traditional foods but did not provide individual pre–post dietary intake data.28,29

Discussion

This review reported the impact of nine lifestyle intervention studies in adolescents conducted between 2007 and 2023 in four PICTs: French Polynesia, Fiji, Tonga and New Caledonia. Overall, reported changes in anthropometric indicators of fatness, body composition indicators and behaviours (PA and diet) were generally small and heterogeneous statistical or clinical significance was infrequently reported, limiting conclusions about intervention effectiveness.28, 29, 30, 31,35, 36, 37 Rather than identifying definitive ‘effective’ components, we summarise how interventions incorporated sociocultural, spatial and temporal dimensions and highlight evidence gaps that should be addressed in future study designs in PICT contexts.

A primary observation is the small number of publications reporting on interventions in the Pacific Islands (Fig. 1), with only nine papers meeting our inclusion criteria. All studies came from three national or international and/or multi-country projects: OPIC, RASA, iEngage.28,30,36 In the Pacific region (including Asia and Pacific countries), a recent systematic review of studies in low- and middle-income countries from the WHO Western Pacific Region observed only eight school-based interventions in the Pacific region.38 These low numbers of interventions in their review or in this one is discordant with the burden associated with the obesity pandemic and its increasing impacts over recent decades. International policies have been identified and the importance of developing, understanding and strengthening this area of research in adolescents living in PICTs is a priority.39 This review therefore provides an overview of the limited published evidence and identifies methodological and reporting gaps (e.g., outcome standardisation and transparent reporting of statistical/clinical significance) that currently constrain inference about effectiveness.

The socio-cultural dimension can be considered one of the three key aspects of lifestyle interventions in PICTs. Across the included studies, educators, families, community organisations and spiritual/traditional leaders were frequently involved in intervention governance and delivery.28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33,35,36 However, given the small and inconsistent outcome changes and the lack of analyses by intervention component, these descriptions should be interpreted as context and implementation features rather than as demonstrated drivers of effectiveness.28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33,35,36 This is highly relevant to PICTs because socio-cultural norms place strong importance on interactions among these actors during school hours, before and after school, and on weekends, when they are often involved in governance and service delivery. Even in rural or tribal areas, school attendance structures adolescents’ daily routines, and school food and activity environments can shape exposures to sugar-sweetened beverages and energy-dense snacks as well as opportunities for movement.9,14 Melanesian as well as Polynesian populations maintain a traditional Pacific tribal lifestyle, especially in rural areas, which are characterised by traditional family farming, fishing, and cultural activities that draw on Indigenous knowledge and customs (e.g., house construction, weddings, and mourning)15 and generally require vigorous daily physical activity.14 Small-scale family farming dominates in the organisation of the ancestral system and is one of the pillars of the traditional cultural practices that contribute directly and indirectly to the sustainability of communities living on customary lands.14 Family farming contributes directly to the economy (by ensuring food security and selling on local markets), socialisation (strengthening social and family ties through daily donations of plant production and mutual aid in the fields) and culture (maintaining customary traditions and donations) of the families within tribes.40 These sociocultural leverages in successful interventions (Table 2) focused on community capacity building as a key component.35

Regarding spatial dimension, lifestyle interventions were delivered in different settings (i.e., tribal, rural, semi-urban or urban areas), families, communities and school environments that influence dietary intake and opportunities for physical activity for adolescents.11,14,20 RASA studies considered contrasted school environments, while OPIC-related interventions intentionally included rural, peri-urban and urban sites.29, 30, 31, 32, 33,35 Nevertheless, few studies reported outcome analyses stratified by setting, rurality or school environment, limiting inference about whether intervention effects differed by spatial context.

The temporal dimension showed a strong variation based on intervention duration per se, ranging from four weeks with follow-up to five years with follow-up. Short-duration interventions (e.g., four weeks) may detect proximal behavioural changes, but are unlikely to yield meaningful anthropometric indicators of fatness or body composition changes; they should therefore be interpreted cautiously for obesity prevention outcomes.36,38 Within intervention programs, the frequency, timing and duration of activities during the week were considered in different ways among studies. With an increase of two to 4 h per week of traditional physical education in school timetables, Gatti et al.28 and Pana et al.29 observed increases of PA during the intervention, while Galy et al.36 observed an increase in PA through the program in spite of keeping similar physical education in school timetables during the whole intervention. Galy et al.36 concluded that an integrative approach in the sports sciences, combining user-centered and community-based education and digital technologies (wearable trackers) to build health-related skills and change PA behaviors, was promising in the PICTs. However, the evidence base remains limited and largely descriptive, with few studies reporting statistical or public-health relevance of observed changes. Meanwhile, the OPIC group noted that the 2–3 year intervention period sometimes contributed to a decrease in engagement by adolescents and or leaders (teachers, families, community leaders). This was often associated with other priorities in participating communities such as ceremonies, policy decisions, climate-related challenges, etc. This leads to another aspect of the temporal dimension of interventions that are the change in seasons affecting outdoors PA and school schedules with periods of school time and school holidays that alternate during the year. All studies agreed that summer temperatures, floods and cyclonic periods with repeated weather depressions strongly affect interventions related to both nutrition and PA.30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35 In addition to seasonal and climate-related disruptions, COVID-19 policy responses (including school closures and restrictions) can affect intervention delivery, participant engagement and follow-up in the region, and should be explicitly documented as part of the temporal context.23 Climate change was not a focus of this review and was not assessed as an intervention outcome. This suggests that future studies should document weather, school closures and other climate-related disruptions as part of the temporal and spatial context of intervention delivery, while avoiding causal claims about climate change unless specifically measured.

Impacts of lifestyle interventions on anthropometric indicators of fatness, PA and diet

Anthropometric indicators of fatness and body composition are measurable in school settings and can be used to track changes over time and examine comparable outcomes of lifestyle interventions contributing to address the obesity pandemic. When comparator groups are available, growth and puberty related changes should occur in both groups; however, variability in pubertal timing and measurement schedules can still dilute between-group differences, particularly when effects are small. However, it is important to remember that anthropometric indicators of fatness alone does not define health, and that to be safe and sustainable, changes in body composition should be gradual.41 School-based interventions in generally healthy adolescent populations are unlikely to result in observable changes in anthropometric indicators of fatness via pre-post assessments. Consistent with this, the studies included in this review generally reported limited or heterogeneous changes in BMI, %FM, or related anthropometric outcomes.28, 29, 30,32,33 Because adolescence is characterised by rapid changes in height, lean mass, and overall body composition, anthropometric outcomes are relatively distal endpoints and may be insensitive to short-to medium-term intervention effects.41 Interpretation is further complicated by heterogeneity in outcome selection, measurement protocols, and follow-up timing across studies, which reduces comparability and may obscure intervention effects.28, 29, 30,32,33 These findings indicate that anthropometric indicators should be interpreted alongside behavioural and contextual outcomes rather than as the sole marker of intervention success in adolescent populations.

Physical activity was a common target across interventions, yet the evidence remains difficult to interpret because PA was measured inconsistently and was often assessed using self-report rather than objective tools.28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33,35,36,42, 43, 44 Given that adolescent PA is multidimensional and includes frequency, duration, intensity, and context, differences in metrics and instruments substantially limit cross study comparison and weaken inference regarding health significance relative to recommended MVPA levels.45 Only one included study used objective monitoring, illustrating both the feasibility of wearable-based assessment and the scarcity of such methods in this literature.36 Overall, the limited use of objective PA measurement and the heterogeneity of PA metrics restrict the ability to compare interventions and to judge whether reported changes are likely to be meaningful for adolescent health outcomes.28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33,35,36,42, 43, 44 Future evaluations would benefit from standardised, objective measures and clearer reporting of PA dose, intensity, and timing.

Diet-related interventions were similarly heterogeneous and spanned environmental, educational, and food provision strategies across school and community settings.30,34,38,39 However, most studies assessed nutrition using self-reported behavioural questionnaires rather than direct dietary intake measures, limiting precision and increasing susceptibility to recall and social desirability bias.31,33 The overall pattern suggests that discrete behaviours such as lunch practices or snack purchasing may be more amenable to change than broader dietary quality, but the current evidence is insufficient to conclude that these interventions produced consistent improvements in overall diet.9,13,31 Future Pacific research would benefit from more robust dietary assessment methods, including digital tools linked to country-specific food composition databases, to improve the detection of meaningful change in both population and individual intakes.20,46

Future directions for lifestyle interventions in PICTs

A successful framework for future lifestyle interventions in PICTs would consider sociocultural, spatial and temporal dimensions of the intervention, evaluated by standardised, objective methods and metrics (for anthropometric indicators of fatness, PA and diet) and co-designed by a transdisciplinary community of researchers and research users including representation from health, education, community leadership and young people in each context. Indeed, providing reliable indicators in lifestyle interventions is driven by standardised methods and metrics, including objective measures when possible and transportable tools, using autonomous and digitalised tools. While measures can be standardised, it is important to emphasise that interventions must be contextualised. Regarding anthropometric indicators of fatness, we suggest the use of waist-to-height ratio and BMI cut off (i.e.,: that combine height, weight, age and sex) methods that are highly reliable, relatively easy to implement, and have strong adherence in Oceanian adolescents.47,48 These two methods and metrics have been recommended by The Lancet Diabetes and Endocrinology Commission.49 For PA, accelerometry that enable measures of sedentary, light-, moderate- and vigorous-intensity PA continuously are highly accurate and appropriate for use with Oceanian adolescents.14,20,42, 43, 44 Dietary patterns and their changes via FFQ and/or iRecall24 tools are reliable in adolescents11,49 and offer a large panel of educational support that can be shared by intergovernmental institutions present in the Pacific region.50 These tools and metrics are easy to use, highly scalable and adaptable to different environments in PICTs. In addition, implementation should consider socio-cultural, spatial and temporal dimensions for each of the sites of research. To connect these dimensions, methods, and metrics and the interest of participants in PICTs, an educational research co-design approach in PICTs schools is promising and has been identified as a key aspect of the health promotion in PICTs. Indeed, ‘research co-design’ can be defined as “the meaningful involvement of research users during the study planning phase of a research project, where ‘meaningful involvement’ is taken to refer to participation in an explicitly described, defined and auditable role or task necessary to the planning and/or conduct of health research.51 Here, the ‘research users’ are the adolescents, teachers, educators, parents, community leaders, and other people or groups (other than researchers themselves) that have an interest in the results of the intervention research and that encapsulate sociocultural, spatial and temporal dimensions of it. An example in PICTs research, includes research developed by Peralta et al.52 in Vanuatu using participation research to co-design a lifestyle intervention with and for adolescents. Importantly this approach aims at engaging in co-design with adolescents to identify research priorities that matter to them. It addresses the lack of participatory research in the Pacific Islands. By combining participatory research, co-design and standardised methods and metrics, future research will be able to identify and address needs while capturing robust data informing future design or policies. Future studies should pre-specify primary outcomes, include appropriate comparator groups and follow-up, and report both statistical significance and measures of clinical/public-health relevance to enable assessment of effectiveness beyond small changes.28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33,35,36 Alongside school-based co-designed approaches, culturally grounded, community driven initiatives,53 broader policy and food-system levers and Pacific-driven community-led initiatives may be needed to achieve population-level impact; these complementary approaches warrant rigorous evaluation in PICT contexts.12,30,50,51

Limitations

There are limitations to this review that should be highlighted. First, despite the comprehensive scope of this narrative review, grey literature was excluded due to potential ethical and quality concerns. Additionally, existing interventions in PICTs that can be considered as lifestyle interventions, but not published or considered as grey literature were excluded. Secondly, the small number of eligible studies and substantial heterogeneity in design, duration, populations, and outcome metrics prevented quantitative synthesis and limited comparability across interventions.28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33,35,36 Third, outcomes for PA and diet were often self-reported, and statistical/clinical significance was inconsistently reported, restricting conclusions about effectiveness and about which components or contextual dimensions may drive change.28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33,35,36 Fourth, because interventions rarely reported results stratified by setting or by sex/gender, we could not examine whether spatial context or sex modified intervention outcomes, which should be addressed in future work. Finally, the reliance on published literature and the exclusion of grey literature may have contributed to publication bias and may have missed local or community programmes that are implemented but not formally evaluated or published.

Conclusion

This review identified nine published lifestyle intervention evaluations among adolescents in PICTs, all conducted in French Polynesia, Fiji, Tonga or New Caledonia despite searching across 22 PICTs. Reported effects on anthropometric indices of body fatness, physical activity and diet were mixed and generally small, with limited reporting of behavioural outcomes and contextual moderators. Future work should prioritise locally led and adequately resourced co-designed interventions that integrate sociocultural, spatial and temporal context, include robust and comparable outcome measurement, and are paired with broader policy and systems actions capable of addressing the strong drivers of obesity risk in PICT settings.
