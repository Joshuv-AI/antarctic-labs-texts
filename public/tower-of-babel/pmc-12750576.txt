Advanced nursing practice in New Zealand through the lens of postgraduate nursing students: a focused ethnography study
Authors: Reen Skaria, Jed Montayre
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Background

Advanced nursing practice (ANP) has been linked to attaining and applying advanced knowledge in specialised nursing fields, with autonomy being an important feature of the role where nurses are able to independently assess, diagnose, manage disease conditions and engage in complex problem solving. Advanced nursing practice is a field of nursing that extends and expands the boundaries of nursing’s scope of practice, contributes to nursing knowledge and promotes advancement of the profession [1]. On the other hand, an advanced practice nurse is a registered nurse who has acquired an expert knowledge base, complex decision-making skills and clinical competency through additional education [2]. These advanced skills enable them to navigate complex healthcare scenarios effectively. The advanced nursing practice role emerged in response to a shortage of doctors and the growing demand for clinicians capable of working independently [3]. The first nurse practitioner program was established in 1965 in the United States [4]. In contrast, the first nurse practitioner program in New Zealand was formally recognized in 2001, introducing a new scope of practice regulated by the New Zealand Nursing Council [5, 6].

The terms “advanced nursing practice” (ANP) and “advanced practice nursing” (APN) are often used interchangeably and nurses in these roles may have diverse titles, skill sets, educational backgrounds, and regulatory requirements [7]. This variability can sometimes lead to confusion regarding specific responsibilities and expectations within these roles. According to the New Zealand Nurses Organisation [8], advanced nursing practice roles in New Zealand include nurse practitioner and clinical nurse specialist and are primarily aimed at improving the health outcomes of all New Zealanders. This perspective is supported by a national survey conducted by Carryer et al. [9], which found that nurse practitioner and clinical nurse specialist were the only roles to be defined as advanced practice nursing roles. However, Jacobs and Boddy [5] emphasise the inclusion of nurse prescribing and describe advanced nursing practice as an expanded and extended expert nursing practice, underpinned by a Master’s degree. Interestingly, Holloway [10] took it one step further by mentioning that advanced nursing practice is more than extended practice and it differs from extended-task roles in its scope and sphere of influence and in its application of advanced nursing knowledge.

In New Zealand, the registered nurse scope encompasses a range of skill levels, including general nursing, specialty clinical nurse roles, and the advanced practice role of clinical nurse specialist [8, 11]. A specialty refers to the area of practice, while a specialist denotes the level of practice where a nurse is authorised to work with advanced expertise [10, 11]. Advanced practice, on the other hand, is described as a continuum of clinical expertise that is not limited to the nurse practitioner role [10]. Significant effort has been dedicated to creating a structured framework for specialist/advanced nursing practice [10, 11]; however, there remains considerable ambiguity in the use of nursing titles to describe senior or advanced practice nursing roles in New Zealand [9]. This ambiguity can create challenges in career progression and role recognition for advanced practice nurses.

It has been noted that advanced nursing practice roles involves providing professional, patient centred, holistic care [12]; building effective communication and therapeutic relationships [13]; promoting ongoing patient safety initiatives [14]; comprehensive assessment, early detection of complications, improving quality of life [15] and having highest level of practical wisdom incorporating problem solving and critical thinking approach [16]. There is an emphasis on advanced nursing practice roles to meet population health needs, deliver culturally safe care and promote equity within the workforce and New Zealand community [8, 17, 18]. Conversely, there has been concern that advanced nurse practitioners are perceived as a cost-effective alternative to junior doctors, and this confusion surrounding role identity has been identified as a threat to the development of advanced nursing practice [13, 19–21]. Further research is required to clearly define the roles of advanced nurse practitioners. Additionally, there has been limited exploration of the perspectives of New Zealand registered nurses, particularly those in the early stages of their careers or those who are just beginning their nursing journey and seeking to advance their knowledge and skills. In this study, postgraduate nursing students were registered nurses who had completed a Bachelor of Nursing degree and were undertaking further education through a Postgraduate Certificate, Diploma, and in some cases, progression toward a Master’s degree. A notable gap exists in the literature regarding the views of New Zealand registered nurses on the meaning of advanced nursing practice and the role of advanced nurse practitioners.

Aims

The aim of this study is to explore postgraduate nursing students’ understanding of advanced nursing practice and their perspectives on the role of advanced nurse practitioners in New Zealand.

Methods

Design

A focused ethnography approach was utilised in the study, which involved 15 interviews of nurses undertaking their postgraduate studies. Focused ethnography was used as it is an appropriate methodological approach to understand participants’ perspectives of the professional and cultural outlook of advanced nursing practice. The approach provides a unique methodological framework for understanding nurses’ views and professional culture in the context of advanced practice, which might have implications for their professional biases within the context of advanced nursing. This qualitative approach allowed for an in-depth exploration of different perspectives. To mitigate bias and subjectivity, reflective notes were written for each interview and were checked by co-researchers. The domains of the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research (COREQ) were utilised in reporting the methods and results of this study [22].

Participants

Fifteen individuals participated in the study. Purposive sampling was undertaken, which was based on inclusion and exclusion criteria to recruit participants who have the requisite background to address the aim of the study on advanced nursing practice perspectives [23]. Participants were selected according to the inclusion criteria: (1) a registered nurse of New Zealand (2) currently enrolled in a post-graduate degree programme and (3) provides direct care to patients, families and communities. This selection ensured that participants had relevant experience and educational grounding in nursing.

Ethics

The Human Research Ethics Committee of the Southern Institute of Technology approved this study (reference no. 2024/69), which was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants prior to the online videoconference interviews. The participants returned the signed consent forms online. Pseudonyms were used in reporting the findings to maintain confidentiality.

Recruitment and data collection

Following ethical approval, an invitation with a flyer and information about the study were sent to postgraduate nursing students of Southern Institute of Technology. Those who received the information posted in a Learning Management System (LMS) Blackboard were directed to make contact with a researcher expressing their interest to participate. The researcher allocated time to contact interested potential participants and checked for their eligibility against the selection criteria. Of the 18 people who met the inclusion criteria and who indicated interest in participating, 15 consented to be interviewed. A key feature of focussed ethnography is interviewing with or without an observation component [24]. This study utilised individual interviewing as a method of data collection and as a focused ethnographic study field immersion was unnecessary. For the purpose of this study, a tailored semi-structured interview guide was developed (See Supplementary File ). A semi-structured interview guide was developed from the literature review. All interviews were conducted in English which lasted 45–60 min. All raw interview data were concurrently transcribed verbatim were read, re-read and then coded independently by two members of the research team.

Data analysis

A thematic analysis was conducted using an inductive, data-driven approach influenced by naturalistic inquiry to uncover recurring themes [25]. This analytical method offered a “user-friendly and theoretically adaptable approach” [25, 26] through a systematic procedure. The analysis revealed patterns in the interview data concerning participants’ experiences, opinions, and viewpoints [26]. Two researchers independently carried out the technical analysis process. They contributed by reaching a consensus on the translated data. In line with the Braun and Clarke framework [25], the analysis commenced with transcribing the data, followed by thorough reading of the transcripts, identifying notable data features (coding), and recognizing themes. The process began with identifying language patterns and words used by participants (semantic content) and their meanings, progressing to uncovering underlying constructs (latent content) expressed by participants. The authors then examined preliminary themes, engaging in discussions to refine the coding and analysis until the prominent patterns across and within transcripts were identified and agreed upon.

Results

Two themes were identified from the fifteen interviews, which focussed on participants understanding and definition of advanced nursing practice and how their perceptions of advanced nursing practice in NZ. The participants shared their personal views based on their direct experiences in working with nurses practicing in advanced levels such as nurse consultants and nurse practitioners.

Defining advanced nursing practice (ANP)

The participants viewed advanced nursing practice as the acquisition of more advanced, research-based knowledge that updates their understanding of nursing practice. They acknowledged that the nursing field has evolved significantly since they completed their initial training many years back and that pursuing postgraduate nursing education will help them update their knowledge and improve their practice. The perception of advanced practice is strongly linked to undertaking additional education in nursing (postgraduate level).Advanced nursing practice, for me, involves acquiring more advanced knowledge, in most cases through studying postgraduate courses. I completed my nursing training in 2008, and the nursing has changed a lot since then.

Two participants viewed advanced nursing practice as an expansion of the traditional nursing role, allowing for more specialized care and greater autonomy or independence beyond the usual scope of general nursing practice.Advanced nursing practice for me is… expanding the nursing role outside of the usual general nursing practice that we all do kind of after graduating and doing our registration.Meaning you’ll be able to do more specialized care to be able to be more autonomous or independent.

The majority of the participants mentioned about specific skills that advanced practice nursing should acquire such as the use of advanced technology and conducting advanced health assessment. There is also some acknowledgment of being a coach and educating nursing students and new nurses, as well as promoting policies and clinical guidelines.Advanced practice nursing is about keeping up with the newest technology, knowing how to use the latest equipment, and understanding new medications and supporting nurses.

Perspectives about the advanced nursing practice (ANP) role in New Zealand

Participants expressed that advanced practice in New Zealand can be ambiguous and would benefit from more structure. For example, nurse coaches and nurse educators in the ward often lack clear organisation and may not function at an advanced level.Some advanced roles are not well-structured, and sometimes nurses in these posts are not functioning at an advanced level and apart from the roles of Nurse Practitioners and Nurse Prescribers, the structure of the other roles is unclear.

Some advanced roles are not well-structured, and sometimes nurses in these posts are not functioning at an advanced level and apart from the roles of Nurse Practitioners and Nurse Prescribers, the structure of the other roles is unclear.

Another participant emphasized the importance of implementing new clinical guidelines and policies as part of the advanced practice nursing role. They mentioned that advanced practice in New Zealand might require nurses to be comfortable with policy and guideline development in clinical practice.I have seen advanced practice nursing’s effectively communicating new policies as part of their role, which probably is required of them.

I have seen advanced practice nursing’s effectively communicating new policies as part of their role, which probably is required of them.

However, there were also some ambivalent responses in pursuing an advanced practice nursing career path in New Zealand due to the lack of career options and uncertainty of employment after completion of postgraduate studies. One participant expressed their worries about pursuing advanced practice nursing due to the anticipated challenges associated with job seeking after graduation.While I’m interested in advanced practice nursing, the uncertainty about employment opportunities after graduation makes me hesitant to commit to this path. It feels risky to pursue advanced practice nursing since the career paths aren’t very clear.

Discussion

This study presented the findings from online interviews exploring the views of postgraduate nursing students on advanced nursing practice in New Zealand. The qualitative analysis revealed two main themes: “What is advanced nursing practice?” and “What are the perspectives of postgraduate nursing students on the advanced nursing practice role in New Zealand?” These issues are interrelated, but for the sake of clarity, they have been considered separately in this discussion. This discussion reiterates key results and relates these to the findings in the previous literature. These findings are explained and integrated into the following sections.

What is advanced nursing practice (ANP)?

In the current study, the participants indicated that advanced nursing practice encompasses autonomous and independent practice, as well as engaging in complex decision-making. In a meta-synthesis research study examining nurse practitioners’ experiences concerning their autonomy and roles within various ambulatory care settings, nurse practitioner autonomy was defined as the ability to engage in independent decision-making, allowing them to select the most appropriate patient care delivery method based on their knowledge, skills, critical thinking, and judgment within their scope of practice, emphasising independent practice and self-empowerment [27]. This finding aligns with the results of the current study with participants’ view of advanced nursing practice as being more autonomous in the job and being able to make independent decisions. Furthermore, there is evidence in the literature suggesting that there is a direct relationship between autonomy and self-confidence and a lack of autonomy hinders nurse practitioners’ opportunities to build confidence and self-esteem [27–29]; autonomy allows nurse practitioners to practice to the maximum extent of their advanced education [30]; and autonomy is a developmental achievement based on professional competence and self-reliance, which are essential for effective decision-making [31].

Interestingly, the participants in the current study described advanced nursing practice as being characterised by the integration and application of expert, up-to-date knowledge across various aspects of healthcare. This included, but was not limited to, maintaining current knowledge of pharmacology, demonstrating competency with advanced equipment, and exhibiting proficiency in emerging technologies. Various authors agree that advanced nursing practice is multifaceted and emphasises the importance of continuous high-quality education, including engagement with peer-reviewed journals and participation in professional conferences [32]; utilisation of remote monitoring, integration of artificial intelligence (AI) to enhance diagnostic accuracy, and maintaining up-to-date knowledge of advanced technologies [33]. Notably, a knowledge needs assessment study conducted on nurse practitioners’ transitioning to practice identified knowledge gaps in the area of pharmacology and medication management, radiologic diagnostics and interpretation, laboratory diagnostics and interpretation, career development, licensure and billing [34]. However, Hande et al. [35] sought to remedy this situation by providing professional development and workplace support.

Additionally, research participants in this study indicated that advanced nursing practice involves conducting thorough patient assessments, making accurate diagnoses, interpreting clinical findings, managing care efficiently, prioritising through triage, and applying advanced decision-making in complex clinical situations. This finding aligns with previous studies indicating that advanced clinical practitioners operate in highly autonomous roles, involving the assessment, diagnosis, and management of acutely unwell patients with complex health needs, thus require comprehensive physical assessment skills [36] and must stay current with the diagnosis and management of various disease conditions to excel as advanced nurse practitioners [32]. Interestingly, Htay and Whitehead [37] advanced this notion by suggesting that integrating advanced nurse practitioners into various clinical fields is not only feasible but required to alleviate the burden on physicians and reduce delays in diagnosis and treatment, as they provide care that is non-inferior compared to doctor-led care. However, Oates [36] added some challenges to maintaining the currency of physical assessment skills of community-based advanced clinical practitioners due to lack of opportunities for certain physical assessment skill rehearsal or clinical supervision. According to Wright et al. [2] this can be rectified by providing continuing education.

What are the perspectives of postgraduate nursing students on the advanced nursing practice role in New Zealand?

According to the postgraduate nursing students in the current study, advanced nursing practice is a specialised domain that provides expert care through a specialist role. A literature review described specialised nurses as independent practitioners with additional training in their chosen field of practice and identified nurse practitioners, clinical nurse specialists, nurse anaesthetists and nurse educators as advanced nurse practitioners [38]. Participants in the current study expressed confusion regarding the roles within advanced nursing practice. Notably, Carryer et al. [9] conducted a study to identify advanced practice roles within nursing titles in New Zealand. Their findings revealed that nurse practitioners achieved a Direct Care score significantly exceeding the advanced practice nursing threshold for that domain. Additionally, diabetes nurse specialists and specialty clinical nurses were the only other roles to consistently score within advanced practice nursing thresholds across all domains. The study concluded that nurse practitioners and clinical nurse specialist were the only roles to be defined as advanced practice nursing roles in New Zealand. According to Dowling et al. [39] the diverse range of titles and roles associated with advanced nursing practice leads to confusion and the underutilisation of nurses’ skill sets globally. A potential solution to this issue involves recognising and addressing the associated challenges by establishing role clarity, providing comprehensive job descriptions, and ensuring deliberate guidance from accrediting authorities [9, 40].

Participants in the current study identified education and leadership as essential aspects of advanced nursing practice roles as these specialist roles focus on educating and coaching nursing students and new staff, as well as effectively communicating new policies. While it has been established that advanced nurse practitioners demonstrate ability to initiate responses and lead teams effectively during crises [41], findings from the current study indicate that they also contribute significantly to improving patient outcomes and care quality. It can also be argued, as Glarcher et al. [14] posit, that advanced nurse practitioners play a pivotal role in guiding multidisciplinary teams, supporting patients and their families, educating nursing staff, and identifying and addressing systemic safety gaps to enhance patient safety.

National Health Service (NHS) Scotland introduced the concept of the four pillars of advanced practice: Clinical Practice, Leadership, Education and Research [42]. Interestingly, the participants in the current study who discussed advanced nursing practice roles addressed all the core pillars, with the exception of research. A national online survey of registered nurses and nurse practitioners conducted in New Zealand to identify advanced practice roles within nursing titles employed in New Zealand showed relatively low levels of activity in the Research and Professional Leadership domains for the senior clinical roles of nurse practitioner and clinical nurse specialist [9]. They further established that senior nurse clinicians do not, or are not able to, engage in Professional Leadership and Research activities to the same extent as they engage in Direct Care. This implies two things: (a) given the existing staff shortages and increased workload, the current expectations for advanced nurse practitioners to engage in research and other professional activities may be too high; and (b) there may be insufficient research support and significant time constraints, limiting the ability of advanced nursing practitioners to participate in research activities. Expanding on this, McConkey et al. [43] identified several barriers that hinder advanced nurse practitioners’ engagement in research activities such as: lack of time, insufficient research training, inadequate research support, limited funding, and restrictive policies and regulations. Although the Doctor of Nursing Practice (DNP) remains a relatively new field requiring further investigation, initial evidence indicates that DNP-prepared nurses are increasingly assuming leadership roles and they are unique in their ability to understand evidence-based practice and effectively translate evidence into practice settings [44]. This highlights the importance of the DNP pathway in fostering a research-oriented mindset among advanced nurse practitioners, thereby enhancing their impact on healthcare delivery and policy development.

Conclusion

Advanced nursing practice is a multifaceted concept that encompasses autonomous practice, complex decision-making, expert up-to-date knowledge across various aspects of healthcare, comprehensive patient assessments, and efficient care management. Postgraduate nursing students identified advanced nursing practice as a distinct field providing specialist care but highlighted confusion regarding the structure and clarity of roles within advanced nursing practice in New Zealand. While they recognized the pillars of Clinical Practice, Leadership, and Education, Research was largely overlooked. Consistent with previous findings, advanced nurse practitioners in New Zealand show limited involvement in research and leadership. Emerging evidence indicates that doctor of nursing practice prepared nurses, with their focus on evidence-based practice, are uniquely equipped to undertake leadership and research roles. This study highlights the need to address structural and systemic challenges in advanced nursing practice roles to enable their full potential in enhancing healthcare quality and patient outcomes.
