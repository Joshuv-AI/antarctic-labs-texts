Hydrothermal duck-feather keratin as a reagent-free bioreductant and capping matrix for bioactive silver nanoparticles
Authors: Mustapha Salisu Muhammad, Mohd Hafiz Dzarfan Othman, Mohd Hafiz Puteh, Mustafa Kamal, Abdul Halim Mohd Yusof, Nik Ahmad Nizam Nik Malek, Roziana Kamaludin, Yusuf Olabode Raji, Siti Maryam Jasman, Ojo Samuel, C. M. Liew, A. P. Parvin, Nurul Huda, Tutuk Djoko Kusworo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The sustainable production of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) from renewable biowaste would reduce environmental burden and expand green nanotechnology applications. This study reports a hydrothermal extraction route that avoids external chemical reductants, thereby enabling duck-feather keratin to function intrinsically as both a reducing and capping agent in the synthesis of stable, bioactive AgNPs. Extraction was verified using the Lowry assay and SDS–PAGE, confirming preservation of protein content needed for metal coordination. One-factor-at-a-time optimization identified pH 11, 70 °C, 30 mL extract per 1 mM Ag⁺ reaction, and a 24 h duration as optimal conditions, producing uniform spherical nanoparticles having an average (11 nm) with excellent dispersion and long-term optical stability. Characterization by UV–Vis, FTIR, XRD, and SEM–EDX confirmed Ag⁺ reduction, keratin capping, and crystalline face-centered cubic Ag formation. TGA–DTA showed improved thermal stability, while BET surface area increased from 1.55 to 6.32 m2·g⁻1 after nanoparticle incorporation, indicating enhanced mesoporosity. The synthesized duck-feather keratin silver nanoparticles (DFKSN) demonstrated strong antioxidant activity and potent antibacterial performance, with DDT, MIC, and MBC assays confirming both bacteriostatic and bactericidal effects against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. The nanoparticles also promoted cytocompatibility in human skin fibroblasts cell (HSF1184) at a dose of 3.0 mg.mL−1. These findings highlight hydrothermally processed keratin as a scalable, waste-valorizing route for sustainable and eco-friendly nanomaterial production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-026-04591-w.

Introduction

Global poultry production generates millions of tons of feather waste annually, creating environmental and public-health concerns when improperly managed [1, 2]. Feathers are composed predominantly of β-keratin (~ 91%), with minor fractions of water and lipids [3–5]. Despite being renewable and biodegradable, feather biomass is largely downcycled into low-value products such as feed or fertilizer, underutilizing its structural and functional potential [6, 7]. Owing to its biocompatibility and chemically active functional groups, keratin has attracted increasing interest for applications in biomaterials, tissue scaffolds, and environmental remediation [8, 9].

Hydrothermal extraction has emerged as an effective method for recovering soluble keratin through controlled disulfide bond cleavage under subcritical water conditions [10, 11]. This approach enables high-yield protein recovery while preserving functional groups capable of metal coordination and redox activity [5, 12]. Such properties make keratin-derived peptides promising candidates for nanoparticle synthesis.

Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) are widely studied for their size-dependent physicochemical properties and broad antimicrobial activity [13–16]. However, conventional synthesis often relies on toxic reductants and organic solvents, raising environmental and safety concerns [17]. These limitations have driven the development of biogenic synthesis strategies that utilize plant extracts, microorganisms, or biomolecules as reducing and stabilizing agents [18, 19]. Among these, proteins are particularly attractive templates due to their ability to coordinate metal ions, mediate electron transfer, and stabilize nanoparticle growth through surface passivation [20, 21].

Keratin is especially suitable for this purpose because of its cysteine-rich structure. Cleavage of disulfide bonds generates thiol groups capable of coordinating Ag⁺ ions and suppressing nanoparticle aggregation [22–26]. Additional amino and carboxyl groups contribute to stabilization via electrostatic and hydrogen-bond interactions [27, 28], while its amphiphilic nature supports steric protection of nanostructures [29].

Although keratin-mediated AgNP synthesis has been reported, many studies employ chemically assisted extraction methods that may alter functional residues and compromise biopolymer integrity [30–33]. Moreover, previous reports often focus primarily on nanoparticle synthesis or antibacterial screening, with limited evaluation of cytocompatibility and integrated biological performance. While duck feathers have been recognized as an abundant keratin source [34–37], the use of hydrothermally extracted duck-feather keratin for AgNP synthesis remains insufficiently explored.

Herein, we report a reagent-free hydrothermal strategy for extracting duck-feather keratin and employing it as an intrinsic bioreductant and stabilizing matrix for AgNP fabrication. Synthesis parameters were systematically optimized, followed by multi-scale physicochemical characterization and long-term colloidal stability assessment. Biological evaluation included antioxidant activity, antibacterial testing (DDT and MIC/MBC), and fibroblast cytocompatibility, providing a balanced assessment of antimicrobial efficacy and biocompatibility. This work establishes hydrothermally derived duck-feather keratin as a viable biomolecular platform for green silver nanoparticle synthesis.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Silver nitrate (AgNO₃, ≥ 99.9%) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥ 98%) were purchased from Merck (Germany). Mueller–Hinton broth (MHB), Mueller–Hinton agar (MHA), Luria–Bertani broth (LB), and bovine serum albumin (BSA) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) and streptomycin–penicillin (PenStrep) were obtained from Gibco (USA). n-Hexane (analytical grade) was supplied by Vchem Sdn Bhd (Malaysia). Bacterial strains were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), and normal human skin fibroblast cells (HSF 1184) were obtained from the European Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (ECACC). Duck feathers were collected from freshly processed poultry waste at a local slaughterhouse in Sokoto, Nigeria. Ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ·cm, Milli-Q system) was used for all preparations. All chemicals were of analytical grade and used as received.

Hydrothermal extraction

Keratin was extracted via a hydrothermal method adapted from green extraction protocols. Duck feathers were washed with mild detergent and distilled water, to remove surface contaminants, oven-dried at 60 °C for 12 h, and mechanically fragmented to increase surface area. The material was dispersed in ultrapure water (solid-to-liquid ratio 1:20 w/v) and stirred for 3 h before transfer to a 500 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave. Hydrothermal treatment was conducted at 180 °C for 60 min under autogenous pressure to promote disulfide bond cleavage and partial keratin hydrolysis. After cooling, the suspension was oven-dried at 60 °C to constant weight, ground, and sieved to obtain duck feather keratin extract (DFKE). For nanoparticle synthesis, DFKE was dispersed in water (5% w/v), heated at 80 °C for 15 min with stirring, filtered, and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 20 min. The supernatant was sterile-filtered (0.22 µm) and stored at 4 °C for up to 14 days. Protein content and molecular weight (Lowry, SDS–PAGE)

Protein concentration of the keratin extract was determined using the Lowry assay with bovine serum albumin (BSA, 0.5 mg·mL⁻1) as the standard [38]. Absorbance was measured at 750 nm. Results are reported as mean ± SD (n = 3). Molecular weight distribution was analyzed by SDS–PAGE under reducing conditions [39]. Gels were stained and imaged using a gel documentation system, and band sizes were estimated by comparison with a prestained protein ladder.

Dispersion behavior

Dispersion behavior was evaluated in Bijou bottles containing equal volumes (3 mL each) of water and n-hexane. Samples were added and phase distribution recorded photographically at 0 min, 30 min after agitation, and after 24 h of static settling.

Biosynthesis and optimization of duck feather keratin silver nanoparticles (DFKSN)

DFKE was used as both reducing and stabilizing agent for Ag⁺ ions. A freshly prepared 1 mM AgNO₃ solution was mixed with the extract under varying conditions of pH (7–12), temperature (30–80 °C), extract volume (10–50 mL), and reaction time (12–48 h) using a one-factor-at-a-time optimization approach. pH was adjusted with 0.1 M NaOH, and reactions were conducted under continuous magnetic stirring.

Nanoparticle formation was monitored by the appearance of a brown coloration and confirmed by UV–Vis spectroscopy (350–750 nm). Under optimized conditions (pH 11, 70 °C, 30 mL extract, 24 h), the suspension was centrifuged (10,000 rpm, 30 min), washed three times with ultrapure water, and dried at 60 °C to obtain DFKSN powder. Samples were stored in airtight containers at room temperature (Fig. 1c, d). The colloidal stability of the synthesized silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) was evaluated over 8 months using UV–visible spectrophotometry.

Physicochemical characterization

UV–Vis

UV–Vis spectra were recorded in the range of 350–750 nm using a calibrated spectrophotometer. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) bands were monitored to confirm silver nanoparticle formation.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis

Hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index (PDI) were measured using a Litesizer DLS 500 (Anton Paar) at 25 °C. Samples were centrifuged (10,000 × g, 10 min), filtered (0.22 µm), and diluted with deionized water (1:10) prior to analysis. Measurements were performed in triplicate, and results are reported as mean ± SD.

Scanning electron microscopy and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM–EDX)

Surface morphology and elemental composition were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). Samples were drop-cast onto carbon-coated aluminum stubs, air-dried, and sputter-coated with gold prior to imaging. SEM analysis was performed at 20 kV under high vacuum. Elemental composition and mapping were obtained using an integrated EDX detector to confirm the presence and distribution of constituent elements.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

TEM analysis was performed by drop-casting nanoparticle dispersion onto carbon-coated copper grids followed by air drying at room temperature. Imaging was conducted using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Talos L120C G2 TEM operated at appropriate accelerating voltage. Particle size distribution was determined from TEM micrographs.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

Crystalline structure and phase composition were analyzed using a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer (Cu Kα radiation, λ = 1.5406 Å) operated at 40 kV and 40 mA. Diffraction patterns were recorded over a 2θ range of 20–80° with a step size of 0.02°. Phases were identified by comparison with standard reference data (ICDD database).

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR analysis was performed using a PerkinElmer Spectrum Two spectrometer. Dried samples were mixed with spectroscopic-grade KBr (1:100) and pressed into pellets prior to measurement. Spectra were recorded in the 4000–400 cm⁻1 range at 4 cm⁻1 resolution with 32 scans per sample. Functional group changes before and after nanoparticle synthesis were evaluated to assess biomolecular involvement in reduction and stabilization.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential thermogravimetric analysis (DTG)

Thermal stability and composition were evaluated using a TGA Q500 system. Approximately 5–10 mg of dried sample was heated from 30 to 800 °C at 10 °C min⁻1 under nitrogen flow (20 mL min⁻1). Weight loss profiles and corresponding DTG curves were used to determine decomposition temperatures and mass loss behavior.

Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area

Surface area and porosity were determined by nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms using a Micromeritics TriStar II analyzer at 77 K. Prior to analysis, samples were degassed at 120 °C under vacuum for 6 h. Isotherms were recorded over a relative pressure (P/P₀) range of 0.01–0.99. Specific surface area was calculated using the BET method (P/P₀ = 0.05–0.30), while pore volume and size distribution were derived from the desorption branch using the BJH model.

Zeta potential

Surface charge was measured using a Litesizer 500 analyzer (Anton Paar, Austria) at 25 °C. Samples were diluted with deionized water, ultrasonicated for 5 min, and adjusted to pH 7.0 ± 0.1 prior to analysis. Measurements were performed in a folded capillary cell in triplicate. Zeta potential values were determined from electrophoretic mobility using the Smoluchowski model and reported as mean ± SD.

Antioxidative assay using DPPH radical-scavenging assay

Antioxidant activity was evaluated using the DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) radical-scavenging assay. Antibacterial activity

Disk diffusion technique (DDT)

Antibacterial activity was assessed using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method in accordance with CLSI guidelines [41, 42]. The following bacterial strains were used: Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 6538), methicillin-resistant S. aureus (ATCC 43300), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ATCC 15442), and Escherichia coli (ATCC 11229). Overnight cultures were adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard (~ 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU·mL⁻1) and spread onto Mueller–Hinton agar plates. Sterile filter paper discs were loaded with 200 µL of DFKSN (2 mg·mL⁻1), AgNO₃ (2 mg·mL⁻1), or DFKE (2 mg·mL⁻1). Streptomycin (100 µg·mL⁻1) served as the positive control. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and zones of inhibition were measured in cm. Experiments were performed in triplicate and reported as mean ± SD.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

MIC was determined using a resazurin-based broth microdilution assay in sterile 96-well microplates. DFKSN and silver nitrate (SN) stock solutions were prepared and serially diluted two-fold in Mueller–Hinton broth to final concentrations of 3–0.012 mg·mL⁻1 to enable accurate determination of antibacterial activity across the tested concentration range based on previously reported antimicrobial studies [43, 44]. Overnight bacterial cultures were adjusted to approximately 5 × 105 CFU mL⁻1, and 100 µL of bacterial suspension was combined with 100 µL of each test dilution (total volume 200 µL per well). Bacteria in broth without test sample served as growth controls, while broth-only wells served as sterility controls. Plates were sealed and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, 30 µL of 0.015% (w/v) resazurin solution was added and incubated for an additional 2–4 h. A blue-to-pink color change indicated bacterial metabolic activity. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration showing no color change, indicating growth inhibition.

Minimum bacterial concentration (MBC)

MBC was determined by aseptically plating 10 µL aliquots from MIC wells showing no visible growth onto Mueller–Hinton agar plates. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The MBC was defined as the lowest concentration at which no bacterial colonies were observed, indicating complete bactericidal effect.

Cytotoxicity assessment

Cytotoxicity of DFKSN was evaluated using an MTT assay with normal human skin fibroblast cells (HSF 1184). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO₂. At 80–90% confluence, cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and detached using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA, followed by resuspension in complete medium.

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1.0 × 104 cells per well in 100 µL medium, and allowed to attach overnight. DFKSN suspensions (0.75, 1.5, and 3.0 mg·mL⁻1) prepared in complete DMEM were added and incubated for 20 h. Untreated cells served as the control. After exposure, 10 µL of MTT stock solution (5 mg·mL⁻1 in PBS) was added to each well to obtain a final MTT concentration of 0.5 mg·mL⁻1, followed by incubation for 3–4 h at 37 °C. The medium was carefully removed, and 100 µL dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to dissolve the formed formazan crystals.

Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability (%) was calculated relative to untreated controls [45]. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Results and discussion

Efficiency of duck feather keratin extract

Extraction and protein content of duck feather keratin extract

Hydrothermal treatment of duck-feather powder produced a clear keratin-rich liquor, indicating effective disruption of the feather matrix and release of soluble keratin fragments. Under subcritical water conditions, cleavage of disulfide linkages and partial hydrolysis of the β-keratin network facilitate conversion of the insoluble structure into soluble peptides, consistent with prior reports on hydrothermal keratin recovery [5]. The resulting DFKE was homogeneous and readily dispersible in water, suggesting controlled hydrolysis without severe degradation.

Protein recovery was confirmed by the Lowry assay. DFKE produced the characteristic violet–blue coloration upon reaction with Lowry reagents, indicating the presence of soluble protein. The measured protein concentration was 2.43 mg·mL⁻1, comparable to values reported for poultry-feather keratin hydrolysates, including 0.95 mg·mL⁻1 [46], and 2.76 mg·mL⁻1 [47]. These results indicate efficient disruption of the disulfide-stabilized keratin matrix and substantial protein solubilization. Although the Lowry assay is non-specific, the predominance of β-keratin in duck feathers, together with SDS–PAGE profiles, characteristic amide I–III FTIR bands, and sulfur detection via EDX, supports attribution of the recovered fraction to keratin-derived peptides. These findings support DFKE as the principal biopolymer responsible for Ag⁺ reduction and nanoparticle stabilization.

Molecular Weigh determination

SDS–PAGE analysis was conducted to determine the molecular-weight distribution of the duck-feather keratin hydrolysate. As shown in (Figure 2), Lane A (BSA standard) exhibited a distinct band at approximately 66 kDa corresponding to monomeric molecular weight of BSA [48]. In contrast, Lane B (DFKE) displayed a band in the low molecular-weight region ~10 kDa, indicating substantial fragmentation of the native keratin structure during hydrothermal extraction. This molecular-weight distribution is in line with previous reports on hydrothermally treated keratin, where peptide fragments typically fall within the 4–12 kDa range under optimized conditions [5]. Similar molecular weights around 10 kDa have also been reported for duck-feather keratin hydrolysates [36], and generally fall within the broader 3–11 kDa range reported for keratin-derived peptides [30]. Dispersion stability

The dispersion behavior of DFKE was evaluated in a biphasic hexane–water system to assess its interfacial properties prior to nanoparticle synthesis (Fig. 3). Under static conditions, two immiscible phases were observed (hexane upper phase, aqueous lower phase), indicating no spontaneous mixing. After 30 min of agitation, turbidity appeared in both phases, suggesting migration of keratin molecules across the oil–water interface. Following overnight equilibration, the aqueous phase partially clarified, while a persistent interfacial layer and minor sedimentation were observed. This behavior indicates partial adsorption of keratin at the oil–water interface and limited aggregation of unstabilized fragments. Similar interfacial activity has been reported for protein-based biocolloids, reflecting the amphiphilic nature of keratin molecules [49]. These observations demonstrate that DFKE possesses inherent interfacial activity and dispersion capability, supporting its role as a natural stabilizing and dispersing agent during silver nanoparticle synthesis.Fig. 3Interfacial dispersion behavior of DFKE in a hexane–water biphasic system under static, shaken (30 min), and equilibrated (24 h) conditions

Optimization of silver nanoparticle synthesis

A one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) approach was used to evaluate the effects of DFKE pH, reaction temperature, extract volume, and reaction time on silver nanoparticle formation. Each parameter was varied independently while the remaining variables were kept constant. Optimization was assessed using UV–Vis spectroscopy by monitoring the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) band characteristic of silver nanoparticles. Sharper and more intense SPR peaks centered at 420–425 nm indicated efficient nanoparticle formation and improved colloidal stability.

Effect of pH

Reaction pH significantly influenced the reduction and stabilization of silver nanoparticles (Fig. 4a). At near-neutral conditions (pH 7–8), weak and broad surface plasmon resonance (SPR) bands were observed, indicating limited Ag⁺ reduction and heterogeneous particle formation. Increasing alkalinity enhanced nanoparticle formation, likely due to deprotonation of keratin functional groups (–NH₂, –COOH, and –SH), which improves their reducing and capping ability. The optimal condition was observed at pH 11, where a sharp SPR peak at ~ 420 nm indicated efficient nanoparticle formation and improved dispersion stability. Similar pH-dependent trends have been reported in keratin- and protein-mediated AgNP synthesis, with optimal formation generally occurring under alkaline conditions [50, 51]. Effect of temperature

Reaction temperature significantly influenced the reduction kinetics and nucleation of silver nanoparticles (Fig. 4b). At lower temperatures (30–40 °C), weak SPR intensities indicated incomplete Ag⁺ reduction and limited nanoparticle formation. Increasing temperature enhanced the reaction rate, producing sharper and more intense SPR peaks characteristic of improved nanoparticle formation. Optimal synthesis occurred at 70 °C, where a distinct SPR peak at ~ 425 nm indicated uniform particle growth and good colloidal stability. Similar temperature ranges (60–70 °C) have been reported as optimal for biomolecule-mediated AgNP synthesis [53]. At 80 °C, slight peak broadening was observed, suggesting partial keratin denaturation and reduced stabilization efficiency, leading to minor aggregation. Comparable behaviour has been reported in protein-mediated nanoparticle systems at elevated temperatures [54].

Effect of volume

The volume of DFKE significantly influenced nanoparticle size and colloidal stability (Fig. 4c). An extract volume of 30 mL produced the most favourable conditions, yielding a narrow SPR peak and the smallest hydrodynamic diameter (78.7 nm) with the lowest PDI (20.2%), indicating relatively uniform nanoparticle dispersion. DLS measurements across the tested volumes ranged from 78.7 to 130.0 nm, with PDI values between 20.2 and 27.1%, suggesting a moderately polydisperse system (Fig. 4e; Table 1). Lower PDI values generally reflect improved size uniformity and colloidal stability [55]. At lower extract volumes (10–20 mL), larger particle sizes (108.1–122.7 nm) were observed, indicating insufficient capping under higher metal-to-extract ratios [58, 59]. Increasing the extract volume beyond the optimum (40 mL) resulted in larger hydrodynamic diameters (130.0 nm) and higher PDI values, suggesting increased aggregation and heterogeneity. A slight decrease in size at 50 mL likely reflects dilution effects or partial redispersion. Overall, the results indicate that 30 mL DFKE provides the optimal extract-to-metal ratio, ensuring efficient reduction and adequate surface stabilization of silver nanoparticles.

Effect of time

Reaction time influenced the extent of Ag⁺ reduction and nanoparticle formation (Fig. 4d). The SPR peak intensity increased from 12 to 24 h, indicating progressive nanoparticle formation during this period. Beyond 24 h, no significant increase in absorbance was observed, suggesting that the reaction had reached completion. The SPR peak position remained nearly constant throughout the tested times, indicating stable particle size and morphology without evidence of secondary growth or aggregation. Similar behaviour has been reported in biomolecule-mediated AgNP synthesis, where extending reaction time beyond an optimal window provides little improvement in nanoparticle yield [60]. Accordingly, 24 h was selected as the optimal reaction time for DFKSN synthesis.

Optical properties and long-term colloidal stability

Formation of DFKSN was confirmed by the appearance of a dark brown dispersion and a distinct SPR band at ~ 423 nm, characteristic of silver nanoparticles (Fig. 5a). The presence of a single, narrow SPR peak indicates relatively uniform particle formation with minimal aggregation, whereas non-optimized conditions produced broader or shifted bands, reflecting less controlled nucleation. During synthesis, the initially alkaline reaction mixture gradually shifted toward near-neutral pH, a common phenomenon in green nanoparticle synthesis resulting from proton exchange during Ag⁺ reduction [61]. Long-term stability was evaluated over eight months at room temperature. The SPR peak position and bandwidth remained essentially unchanged (Fig. 5b), indicating preserved particle size and dispersion. A slight increase in peak intensity was observed (Fig. 5c) without any spectral shift, suggesting stable nanoparticle concentration and morphology. Such stability exceeds the 3–6 month storage typically reported for protein-stabilized AgNPs [62–64]. The extended stability is attributed to strong interactions between keratin functional groups (–SH, –NH₂, –COOH) and the nanoparticle surface, forming a protective steric and electrostatic capping layer. Similar stabilization behaviour has been reported for keratin-mediated AgNP systems [65–67]. [63–65]. Therefore, UV–Vis analysis confirms that the optimized synthesis conditions (pH 11, 70 °C, 30 mL DFKE, 24 h) produce highly stable DFKSN.

Functional group interactions and crystallinity

FTIR: DFKE vs DFKSN

FTIR spectra of DFKE and DFKSN (Fig. 6a) exhibit characteristic absorption bands associated with keratin proteins and reveal changes after nanoparticle formation. Both samples show broad bands at 3200–3450 cm⁻1, attributed to N–H and hydrogen-bonded O–H stretching (amide A), typical of peptide structures [68, 69]. Peaks at 2930 and 2860 cm⁻1 correspond to asymmetric and symmetric C–H stretching of aliphatic groups [63, 70, 71]. The characteristic protein bands amide I (1650 cm⁻1), amide II (1550 cm⁻1), and amide III (1237 cm⁻1) are present in both DFKE and DFKSN, indicating that the keratin peptide backbone remains largely preserved after hydrothermal extraction and nanoparticle formation [30, 72–75].Fig. 6Structural characterization of duck-feather keratin extract (DFKE) and keratin-mediated silver nanoparticles (DFKSN): a FTIR spectra showing characteristic protein bands and functional group interactions involved in nanoparticle formation; b XRD patterns of DFKE and DFKSN, confirming the formation of crystalline AgNPs with a face-centered cubic (fcc) structure

Structural characterization of duck-feather keratin extract (DFKE) and keratin-mediated silver nanoparticles (DFKSN): a FTIR spectra showing characteristic protein bands and functional group interactions involved in nanoparticle formation; b XRD patterns of DFKE and DFKSN, confirming the formation of crystalline AgNPs with a face-centered cubic (fcc) structure

However, slight shifts and reduced intensities in the amide II and III bands of DFKSN suggest interactions between peptide functional groups and the nanoparticle surface [76]. Changes were also observed in the low-wavenumber region related to sulfur-containing groups. The band near 625 cm⁻1, associated with C–S stretching, showed variation after nanoparticle formation, suggesting involvement of cysteine residues in Ag–S interactions during nanoparticle nucleation [72]. Additionally, a weak band at 1389 cm⁻1 in DFKSN may be attributed to residual nitrate species or modified carboxylate vibrations [64]. From this data, FTIR results indicate that keratin functional groups including thiol, amino, and carboxylate groups participate in the reduction and stabilization of AgNPs, forming a protein corona that stabilizes the metallic nanoparticles, consistent with previously reported protein-mediated synthesis mechanisms [77].

XRD: DFKE vs DFKSN

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of DFKE and DFKSN demonstrate structural changes associated with nanoparticle formation (Fig. 6b). The DFKE diffractogram shows broad reflections at 2θ ≈ 8.66°, 14.1°, 20.2°, 23.2°, and 26.6°, characteristic of the semi-crystalline structure of keratin proteins containing mixed secondary structures, with β-sheet contributions typically dominant in feather keratin [78–80].

Following in situ reduction of Ag⁺ ions, the DFKSN pattern displays distinct diffraction peaks at 2θ = 38.1°, 44.3°, 64.5°, 77.5°, and 81.6°, corresponding to the (111), (200), (220), (311), and (222) planes of face-centered cubic (fcc) silver (JCPDS No. 04-0783) [81, 82]. The intense (111) reflection at 38.1° confirms the formation of crystalline metallic silver nanoparticles. Simultaneously, the broad DFKE reflections become less prominent in the composite pattern, likely due to the dominance of the crystalline AgNP signals and partial rearrangement of the keratin matrix during nanoparticle formation. These results confirm the successful formation of crystalline AgNPs stabilized within the keratin framework, while the protein matrix acts as a capping agent without disrupting the intrinsic silver crystal structure.

Morphology and elemental composition of DFKE vs DFKSN

SEM and EDX analyses reveal clear morphological and compositional differences between DFKE and DFKSN. SEM images of DFKE (Fig. 7a₁) show irregular sheet-like and fibrous fragments with rough surfaces and porous debris, reflecting the disrupted macrofibrillar structure of keratin after hydrothermal extraction. In contrast, SEM images of DFKSN (Fig. 7a₂) display densely distributed, predominantly spherical nanoparticles dispersed across the keratin matrix, indicating successful nucleation and deposition of AgNPs. EDX spectra and elemental maps (Fig. 7b₂) show strong Ag signals (~ 3 keV) alongside keratin-derived elements (C, N, O, and S). Minor peaks of K, Mg, and Ca likely originate from residual salts or trace elements within the protein matrix. The co-localization of Ag with keratin elements suggests that nanoparticles are embedded and stabilized within the biopolymer framework [84–86].

TEM analysis further confirms the nanoscale morphology of DFKSN. Low-magnification TEM images (Fig. 7c₁) reveal well-dispersed, quasi-spherical nanoparticles with minimal aggregation. Particle size analysis (Fig. 7c₂) shows an average diameter of ~ 11 nm, indicating uniform nanoscale particles suitable for bioactive applications [87–89]. High-resolution TEM (Fig. 7c3) displays lattice fringes with an interplanar spacing of ~ 0.24 nm, corresponding to the (111) plane of face-centered cubic silver (JCPDS No. 04-0783) [90, 91]. The corresponding SAED pattern (Fig. 7c4) exhibits diffraction rings indexed to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) planes, confirming crystalline AgNP formation. Notably, the larger particle sizes observed by DLS compared with TEM are attributed to the contribution of the keratin corona and solvation layer surrounding nanoparticles in suspension [56].

Thermal and surface characteristics

Thermal stability (TGA–DTA) of DFKE vs DFKSN

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and derivative thermogravimetry (DTG) reveal clear differences in the thermal behaviours of DFKE and DFKSN (Fig. 8a, b). DFKE exhibits a typical three-stage degradation profile. The first stage (~ 8% mass loss) occurs near 98 °C and corresponds to the removal of bound moisture. The second stage, representing the main degradation event, occurs between 200 and 400 °C with ~ 70% mass loss and a DTG maximum at 320 °C, associated with decomposition of the keratin peptide backbone and disruption of its α-helix and β-sheet structures. Similar thermal behaviour has been reported for avian keratin materials [92–95]. Beyond this stage, gradual mass loss continues up to 900 °C, leaving a final residue of approximately 18–20 wt%, attributed to char and non-volatile inorganic components.Fig. 8Thermal and surface characteristics of DFKE and DFKSN: a TGA and; b DTG curves; c nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms (BET), and; d comparison of surface area, pore volume, and pore diameter

Thermal and surface characteristics of DFKE and DFKSN: a TGA and; b DTG curves; c nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms (BET), and; d comparison of surface area, pore volume, and pore diameter

In contrast, DFKSN shows improved thermal stability. The initial mass loss decreases to ~ 5% and shifts slightly to ~ 105 °C. The primary degradation stage occurs between 200 and 350 °C with a substantially lower DTG intensity, indicating a reduced decomposition rate of the keratin matrix. This behaviour suggests that incorporation of AgNPs restricts polymer-chain mobility and introduces stabilizing interactions such as Ag–S and Ag–O coordination. Similar metal-induced stabilization has been observed in keratin-based nanocomposites [85, 96, 97]. Additionally, DFKSN exhibits a weak DTG shoulder between 450 and 550 °C, likely associated with secondary decomposition of the keratin–silver composite structure. A key difference is the significantly higher residual mass, with DFKSN retaining approximately 40–45 wt% at 900 °C, reflecting the presence of non-volatile metallic silver after combustion of the organic matrix. These results confirm successful incorporation of AgNPs and demonstrate enhanced thermal stability of the keratin matrix.

Surface area and porosity (BET) of DFKE vs DFKSN

Nitrogen adsorption–desorption analysis (Fig. 8c) shows that both DFKE and DFKSN exhibit Type IV isotherms with H3-type hysteresis loops, characteristic of mesoporous materials with slit-like pores (Thommes et al., 2015). DFKSN shows substantially higher nitrogen uptake (5.45 cm3 g⁻1 STP) than DFKE (1.37 cm3 g⁻1 STP), indicating increased accessible adsorption sites after AgNP incorporation.

BET analysis (Fig. 8d) confirms this enhancement. The specific surface area increased from 1.554 to 6.318 m2 g⁻1, while the total pore volume increased from 0.002847 to 0.011460 cm3 g⁻1 following nanoparticle formation. The average pore diameter remained within the mesoporous range (≈5.77–5.80 nm). These results suggest that AgNP incorporation partially disrupts the keratin matrix, generating additional voids and a more accessible pore network. Similar increases in surface area and porosity have been reported for keratin-based nanocomposites and other biopolymer–nanoparticle systems [98–100]. The enhanced mesoporosity of DFKSN is therefore expected to improve adsorption capacity and facilitate catalytic and antimicrobial interactions by increasing the exposure of active sites.

Surface charge and colloidal stability of DFKSN

Zeta-potential measurements of DFKSN exhibited consistently negative values ranging from –18.5 to –22.1 mV (mean ± SD; Fig. 9), indicating moderate electrostatic repulsion between nanoparticles. The negative surface charge arises from deprotonated carboxylate and thiolate groups present in the keratin corona adsorbed on the Ag surface [101]. Although zeta potentials in this range are generally classified as moderately stable [55], the protein-based capping layer provides additional steric stabilization that enhances colloidal dispersion [102]. This combined electrostatic and steric stabilization mechanism explains the good dispersion stability observed in the UV–Vis and DLS analyses. Biological activity of DFKSN

Silver nanoparticles are known to exert multi-target antimicrobial effects, including membrane disruption, protein inactivation, and oxidative stress-mediated damage to bacterial DNA [105–107]. The keratin matrix acts mainly as a stabilizing and dispersing agent that prevents nanoparticle aggregation and modulates Ag⁺ release, thereby improving silver bioavailability and antibacterial efficacy [101, 108, 109].

The improved MIC performance of DFKSN is attributed to the nanoparticle formulation, where keratin-capped AgNPs act as reservoirs for controlled Ag⁺ release. This sustained release mechanism maintains effective antimicrobial ion concentrations and prolongs bactericidal activity compared with the rapid but short-lived ion release from AgNO₃ solutions [110–113]. In addition, interactions between AgNPs and bacterial membranes may promote reactive oxygen species generation and disruption of cellular metabolism [114–116].

MBC determination

Minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) results (Fig. 10d; Table 3) further confirm the enhanced bactericidal activity of DFKSN compared with silver nitrate (SN). DFKSN achieved complete bacterial killing at 0.375 mg.mL⁻1 for Pseudomonas aeruginosa and 0.75 mg.mL⁻1 for both Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus, representing approximately a two-fold improvement relative to SN. For methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, both DFKSN and SN exhibited the same MBC value (3.0 mg.mL⁻1), indicating reduced susceptibility of this strain.

The improved bactericidal performance of DFKSN is attributed to the nanoparticle formulation, which increases the effective interaction between silver species and bacterial cells through sustained Ag⁺ release and nanoparticle-cell contact. The comparatively higher MBC observed for MRSA likely reflects its well-documented resistance mechanisms, including a highly cross-linked peptidoglycan cell wall and enhanced oxidative stress defense systems that reduce silver penetration and toxicity [107, 117–119].

Cytocompatibility

Cytocompatibility of DFKSN was evaluated using an MTT assay after 24 h exposure to increasing concentrations (Fig. 11). A biphasic response was observed. At the lowest concentration (0.75 mg.mL⁻1), fibroblast viability decreased to 52.65% relative to the untreated control (100%), indicating measurable cytotoxicity. In contrast, higher concentrations showed markedly improved viability, reaching 90.25% at 1.5 mg.mL⁻1 and 106.27% at 3.0 mg.mL⁻1. The observed concentration-dependent response may reflect a hormetic effect, where low nanoparticle exposure induces transient oxidative or metabolic stress, while higher concentrations stabilized by protein coatings promote adaptive cellular responses and metabolic recovery [120]. Keratin likely contributes to this cytocompatibility through its cysteine-rich composition and amphiphilic structure, which can reduce nanoparticle-induced oxidative stress and improve cell–material interactions [121–123]. Keratin-based biomaterials are widely reported to support fibroblast adhesion, proliferation, and tissue regeneration due to their structural similarity to extracellular matrix components [72, 85, 124]. The keratin matrix likely contributes to this cytocompatibility by providing a stabilizing interface around the AgNPs and moderating silver ion release. Similar cytocompatible behavior has been reported for keratin–AgNP composites that support fibroblast proliferation through controlled silver release and protein-mediated cytoprotection [67, 76]. These results therefore indicate that DFKSN exhibits good cytocompatibility at moderate to high concentrations, supporting its potential use in biological and biomedical applications.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that hydrothermally extracted duck-feather keratin can serve as an effective dual-function biomolecular platform for the reduction and stabilization of silver nanoparticles without the use of external chemical reductants. Controlled reaction conditions enabled the generation of structurally stable nanocomposites characterized by nanoscale crystallinity, preserved colloidal integrity, and enhanced structural organization following nanoparticle integration. Physicochemical analyses confirmed the active participation of keratin functional groups in nanoparticle nucleation and surface passivation, supporting both structural stability and biological functionality. The resulting synthesized DFKSN exhibited enhanced antioxidant activity, broad-spectrum antibacterial efficacy, while maintaining favorable cytocompatibility under the tested conditions. These findings establish poultry-derived keratin as a viable biomolecular template for sustainable nanocomposite fabrication with potential utility in antimicrobial surface-related applications. However, further evaluation of long-term stability, in vivo biocompatibility, and safety profiles is required before clinical or biomedical translation can be considered.
