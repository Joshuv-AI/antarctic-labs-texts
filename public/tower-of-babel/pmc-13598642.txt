The Gut Microbiota–NLRP3 Inflammasome Axis in Chronic Heart Failure: Mechanisms Across Heart Failure Phenotypes and Therapeutic Perspectives
Authors: Yaxuan Guo, Tenghui Tian, Lihong Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic heart failure (CHF) patients often suffer from intestinal microbiome dysbiosis and persistent systemic inflammation, forming a vicious cycle that current therapies struggle to effectively disrupt. The NLRP3 inflammasome, a key component of the innate immune system, is abnormally activated in CHF and closely associated with myocardial fibrosis, ventricular remodeling, and deteriorating cardiac function. Recent advances in the “gut-heart axis” theory have revealed widespread gut microbiota imbalance and intestinal barrier dysfunction in CHF patients, with microbial-derived metabolites influencing cardiovascular homeostasis through multiple pathways. However, the mechanistic interplay between the NLRP3 inflammasome and gut microbiota in CHF, subtype-specific regulatory networks across different forms of heart failure, and the translational potential of interventions targeting this axis remain incompletely integrated. This narrative review aims to systematically summarize the tripartite axis of “gut microbiota–NLRP3 inflammasome–chronic heart failure.” Literature was retrieved from PubMed, Web of Science, and the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) databases between 2017 and 2026, including reviews, original research, and preclinical studies. Most available evidence comes from basic science and animal experiments, resulting in significant heterogeneity; clinical studies are limited and predominantly observational association analyses, leading to overall low-level evidence. Moreover, most therapeutic data—such as drug inhibition, dietary, and microbiome-based interventions—are derived from preclinical models, with insufficient large-scale, multicenter prospective intervention trials to confirm their efficacy and safety, necessitating further validation in humans. By summarizing the structure, function, and activation mechanisms of the NLRP3 inflammasome, analyzing the regulatory roles of gut microbiota and their metabolites in CHF, outlining the characteristics of this axis across different heart failure subtypes and reviewing emerging therapeutic strategies targeting NLRP3, this review seeks to address existing knowledge gaps and provide theoretical foundations and new research directions for precision prevention and treatment of heart failure.Keywords: NLRP3 Inflammasome, heart failure, gut microbiota, mechanism, treatments

Introduction

Chronic heart failure (CHF) is a leading cause of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality worldwide. CHF is classified based on left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) into heart failure with reduced EF (HFrEF; EF ≤40%), heart failure with mildly reduced EF (HFmrEF; EF 41–49%), and heart failure with preserved EF (HFpEF; EF ≥50%). Patients commonly exhibit gut microbial dysbiosis, endotoxemia, persistent systemic inflammation, and oxidative stress. Even with advanced therapies like heart transplants and left ventricular assist devices, endotoxemia and intestinal dysbiosis remain and are extremely difficult to reverse.1 Symptom-modifying therapies, that have been available for decades, fail to meaningfully restore the microbial homeostasis of the gut and resolve the chronic inflammation of the body. The unmet need for targeted gut microbiome and NLRP3 inflammasome therapies is urgent.2–4

The NLRP3 inflammasome is an intracellular multiprotein complex of innate immunity that, upon activation, can drive caspase-1-mediated IL-1β/IL-18 maturation and pyroptosis.5–7 Its activation has been associated with myocardial fibrosis, ventricular remodeling, and worsening cardiac function in heart failure.8–10 Importantly, most of this evidence derives from experimental models: pharmacological inhibition (eg, MCC950) or genetic ablation of NLRP3 attenuates myocardial injury and improves cardiac function in preclinical heart failure models,9–11whereas human data remain largely observational and correlative. We focus on NLRP3 rather than other inflammatory pathways because it can integrate microbial and metabolic danger signals and lies upstream of key pro-inflammatory cytokines and pyroptotic cell death, making it a plausible molecular hub linking the gut microbiota to cardiac inflammation.

Gut microbiota-derived metabolites and immune signals can regulate cardiac function. In CHF, dysbiosis is characterized by reduced microbial diversity, loss of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria, and expansion of potential pathogens, often together with impaired intestinal barrier integrity.1,3,12 Experimental studies have shown that Lipopolysaccharide activates the NLRP3 complex through the TLR4/NF-κB pathway, thereby inducing myocardial inflammation;13,14 trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) is associated with endothelial dysfunction and myocardial fibrosis;14,15 and dysregulation of the tryptophan–aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) pathway has been linked to adverse cardiac remodeling.16 Fecal microbiota transplantation in animals can partly replicate or reverse heart failure phenotypes, supporting a functional role of the microbiota.16,17 However, how these pathways converge on NLRP3 in patients with different CHF subtypes remains unclear.

Current knowledge gaps include the lack of a systematic framework integrating gut microbiota and NLRP3 signaling in CHF, uncertainty about subtype-specific differences across HFrEF/HFmrEF/HFpEF, and the predominantly preclinical nature of therapeutic evidence—pharmacological NLRP3 inhibitors, dietary, and microbial interventions—requiring validation in prospective human studies.9,12,18

This review aims to summarize the structure and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, analyze the regulatory roles of the gut microbiota and its metabolites in CHF, discuss subtype-specific aspects of this axis, and evaluate emerging therapeutic strategies targeting NLRP3 and/or the microbiota. By identifying key evidence gaps to provide a theoretical basis for the precise prevention and treatment of heart failure.

Overview of NLRP3 Inflammasome

The NLRP3 inflammasome is an intracellular polymeric protein complex, which is a key component of the innate immune response and plays a central role in sensing various physiological stimuli and danger signals. Its abnormal activation has been confirmed to be closely related to myocardial fibrosis, ventricular remodeling, and deterioration of cardiac function in heart failure. This chapter briefly introduces its core structure, main activation patterns, and regulatory nodes, with the focus being to lay the foundation for subsequent discussions on how the gut microbiota and their metabolites affect NLRP3 and thereby participate in the progression of CHF.

Structure and Components

The NLRP3 inflammasome is assembled from three core proteins: the NLRP3 protein, the adaptor protein ASC (apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD), and the effector protease caspase-1.19–21 The NLRP3 protein contains three functional domains: an N-terminal PYD domain, a central NACHT domain (with ATPase activity responsible for oligomerization), and a C-terminal LRR domain (involved in ligand sensing and autoinhibition),20,22 but recent studies have revealed that it has both positive and negative regulatory effects on the activation of specific agonists, indicating that its function may be more complex than simple ligand sensing or self-inhibition.23 ASC recruits pro-caspase-1 through its CARD (caspase activation and recruitment domain) domain to form a functional complex.21 Caspase-1 is the final effector protease responsible for cutting downstream substrates.24 In addition, NEK7 (NIMA-related kinase 7) acts as a scaffold protein and directly binds to the LRR domain of NLRP3 to promote the assembly of NLRP3-NEK7-ASC21 (Figure 1).

Major Activation Modes and Regulatory Highlights

Principal Activation Pathways

① Classical signaling pathways usually require two separate signals: Signal 1 (priming) is mediated by Toll-like receptors (TLRs) or cytokine receptors, activating NF-κB to upregulate transcription of NLRP3, pro-IL-1β, and pro-IL-18, accompanied by post-translational modifications (eg, deubiquitination) that relieve NLRP3 autoinhibition.21,25,26 For instance, Pellino 2 promotes NLRP3 ubiquitination through its FHA and RING domains to enhance its activation,27 while USP22 promotes autophagy-dependent NLRP3 degradation by stabilizing ATG5 to negatively regulate.28 Signal 2 (activation) is triggered by diverse danger signals, including ion fluxes (eg, K⁺ efflux, Ca2⁺ influx), lysosomal rupture (releasing cathepsin B), mitochondrial dysfunction (ROS production or mtDNA release), and PAMPs/DAMPs (eg, LPS, urate crystals)19,29–34. These signals promote NLRP3 oligomerization, followed by ASC recruitment through PYD-PYD interactions, leading to caspase-1 activation.19,21 Active caspase-1 cleaves pro-IL-1β/pro-IL-18 to mature cytokines and cleaves Gasdermin D (GSDMD) to induce pyroptosis.19,30 The ASC protein can aggregate into large cytoplasmic spots (ASC speck) during the activation process, which is traditionally regarded as a marker of inflammasome activation. However, recent studies have shown that this spot may not be necessary for all NLRP3 activation, and it is more likely to be a mechanism for amplifying inflammatory signals under weak stimulation, while the activation of caspase-1 may be spatially and temporally separated from the ASC spots.35 Therefore, the assembly and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome is a complex process involving precise structural rearrangement, multi-step signal integration, and strict temporal and spatial control. Its abnormal activation is closely related to the occurrence and development of various chronic inflammatory diseases, including cardiovascular diseases.36,37

② Non-canonical pathway: Involves direct activation of caspase-4/5 (human) or caspase-11 (mouse) by intracellular LPS, independently of upstream NLRP3 signals. This pathway directly cleaves GSDMD, causing pyroptosis and K⁺ efflux, which can in turn activate the canonical NLRP3 inflammasome, amplifying the inflammatory response.38 Additionally, it has been found that the orphan nuclear receptor Nur77 can act as an intracellular LPS sensor, cooperating with mitochondrial DNA and LPS to activate the non-classical NLRP3 inflammasome.23 The activation of the non-classical pathway is not limited to Gram-negative bacteria; human macrophages can also activate this pathway through the caspase-4/5 pathway dependent on NLRP11 during infection with Gram-positive bacteria such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis.39 Therefore, the non-classical pathway is an important defense mechanism for the body to resist specific intracellular pathogens, but its excessive activation can also lead to severe tissue damage.

③ Alternative pathway: Identified in human monocytes, where a single TLR ligand (eg, LPS) can induce caspase-1-dependent IL-1β maturation and release without requiring K⁺ efflux or typical pyroptosis.40 Although this pathway does not trigger typical pyroptosis, the formation of Gasdermin D channels may still exist at low levels and promote IL-1β release.40 Mechanistically, the activation of alternative pathways is finely regulated. For example, heat-inactivated Gram-negative bacteria can act as a single-step stimulator to trigger this limited NLRP3 inflammasome response in human monocytes, and its intrinsic activation limitation is associated with the negative regulation of caspase-8 by the short isoform of cFLIPs induced after NF-κB activation.41 In addition, V-ATPase inhibition exacerbates LPS-induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation in human monocytes, but this effect is independent of lysosomal acidification, suggesting that V-ATPase may play a regulatory role in alternative pathways beyond the maintenance of lysosomal pH.42 The above multiple mechanisms indicate that the NLRP3 inflammasome can flexibly select activation strategies according to different cell types and stimulation signals, thus playing a key role in pathological processes such as infectious and non-infectious cardiovascular inflammation43 (Figure 2).

Gut Microbiota: The Potential Regulator of Heart Failure

Overview and Functions of the Gut Microbiota

The gut microbiota is composed of bacteria, fungi, and viruses, and it colonizes the human intestinal tract and participates in physiological processes such as nutrient metabolism, immune regulation, and barrier maintenance.15,50 Its composition and stability are crucial for the host’s health. At the phylum level, the Firmicutes phylum and the Bacteroidetes phylum are the two core dominant bacterial groups that constitute a healthy gut microbiota,51 and they are the basis for maintaining the intestinal microecological balance. The diversity of the microbiota is usually measured by α diversity (such as the Chao1 index and Shannon index), which is a key indicator for assessing intestinal health. Studies have shown that higher gut microbiota diversity is associated with better health conditions. For example, in healthy individuals, higher α diversity is positively correlated with better cognitive functions (such as project recognition memory)52 and is associated with a more favorable lipid profile (such as higher levels of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol) and a lower risk of obesity.53 On the contrary, various disease states such as ulcerative colitis, asthma, sepsis, Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, and major depression are associated with significantly reduced diversity.54–58 This loss of diversity disrupts the stability and function of the microbiota. The core physiological functions of the healthy gut microbiota are the foundation for generating extensive benefits for the host. Its metabolites (such as short-chain fatty acids, trimethylamine N-oxide, lipopolysaccharide, and bile acids) affect cardiovascular homeostasis through the gut-brain axis.59,60 Among them, short-chain fatty acids are produced by the fermentation of dietary fibers, regulating host energy metabolism and immune balance; trimethylamine N-oxide originates from choline and carnitine and is converted by the microbiota to trimethylamine (TMA) and is oxidized by the liver to generate, having a pro-atherosclerotic effect.12,61 Secondary bile acids (such as deoxycholic acid) are generated by the modification of primary bile acids by the microbiota and participate in inflammation and metabolic regulation.62,63

Gut Microbiota Imbalance and Systemic Inflammation

Patients with Heart Failure Generally Have Intestinal Flora Imbalance

Multiple studies have confirmed that compared with healthy control groups, the β-diversity of the intestinal microbiota in patients with CHF shows significant changes.64 Specifically, beneficial bacteria (such as lactobacilli, Bacteroides, and Bifidobacterium) decrease, while potential pathogenic bacteria (such as Escherichia coli) increase, and microbial diversity significantly decreases.3,4,65 A systematic review on the Asian population pointed out that the abundance of key bacterial genera producing butyrate (Rothia, Coprococcus, Prevotella, and Bacteroides) decreased in individuals at risk of CHF, while the abundances of Prevotella, Klebsiella, Streptococcus, and Enterococcus increased.66 Another systematic review also confirmed that Streptococcus, Escherichia/Shigella, Veillonella, and Enterobacter genera increased in CHF.64 This “dysregulation pattern of this kind”—the reduction of butyrate-producing bacteria and the increase of pro-inflammatory or potentially harmful bacteria—constitutes the core feature of CHF-related intestinal microbiota dysbiosis.67 Moreover, abnormal expansion of specific bacterial genera is also closely related to CHF. Studies have found that Gram-negative bacteria, specifically Salmonella, expanded in the intestines of CHF patients and was associated with impaired cardiac function.68

The Specific Intestinal Flora Imbalance Pattern Is Positively Correlated with the Severity of CHF

The reduction in the diversity index of the gut microbiota (such as the Shannon diversity index) is associated with a more severe state of CHF.69 Specifically, patients with higher levels of citrullinated histone H3 (CitH3) in the circulation have lower gut microbiota diversity, and this is significantly correlated with high NT-proBNP levels.69 Although some studies have constructed a microbiota imbalance index without finding a direct correlation between it and LVEF or NT-proBNP levels,70 other evidence indicates that the increase in the abundance of the Segatella genus is positively correlated with the reduction in LVEF in patients with CHF.68 Additionally, the circulating level of the intestinal microbiota metabolite imidopyracetic acid (ImP) is elevated in patients with chronic heart failure, and it is related to the presence, cause, and severity of heart failure, providing metabolic-level evidence for the association between microbiota imbalance and disease severity.71 These associations suggest that the dysregulation of the gut microbiota is not a static phenomenon but may evolve dynamically as the CHF condition worsens, and it may also change in response to treatment interventions.72

Gastrointestinal Flora Imbalance Is Closely Related to the Impairment of Intestinal Barrier Function

Venous congestion in the internal organs can lead to intestinal wall edema, increased intestinal permeability, and the translocation of bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and its metabolites to the circulatory system.13,59,73 For instance, LPS activates the Toll-like receptor signaling pathway (especially TLR4) and triggers inflammatory pathways such as nuclear factor κB (NF-κB), generating a large amount of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as TNF-α and IL-6) and causing a chronic low-grade systemic inflammatory state.68,74 This inflammation not only directly damages myocardial cells, promotes fibrosis and adverse remodeling, but also further aggravates intestinal barrier dysfunction, forming a vicious cycle that promotes the progression of CHF.75,76 Clinical studies have shown that in patients with advanced CHF, the levels of serum LPS and inflammatory markers (such as sCD14, IL-6, TNF-α) are elevated, and endotoxemia and systemic inflammation are exacerbated.1,13,65 Animal experiments have also confirmed that dysbiosis through the LPS-TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway upregulates myocardial inflammatory factors (IL-1β, TNF-α) and is associated with myocardial fibrosis and cardiac dysfunction13,73 (Figure 4).

Key Intestinal Microbial Metabolites

Short-Chain Fatty Acids (SCFAs)

SCFAs (such as butyric acid, propionic acid, etc.) are produced by the fermentation of dietary fibers by the gut microbiota, and they regulate downstream signaling pathways by activating G protein-coupled receptors (GPR41, GPR43, GPR109A) and inhibiting histone deacetylases (HDACs).77,78 At the same time, as HDAC inhibitors, SCFAs can affect gene transcription through epigenetic regulation and thereby regulate immune responses and inflammatory processes.79 This dual mechanism makes SCFAs a key bridge connecting the gut microbiota and the host’s cardiovascular health. In patients with CHF, the reduction of SCFAs-producing bacteria (such as Faecalibacterium, Roseburia) leads to a decrease in SCFAs levels.12,80 SCFAs (especially butyric acid) have anti-inflammatory and myocardial protective effects, and can inhibit the assembly and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in immune cells such as macrophages and dendritic cells.81 Butyric acid can inhibit ferroptosis (reduce the expression of iron accumulation-related protein LCN2) and alleviate age-related heart failure,82 and regulate the TGF-β1/MAPK pathway to improve myocardial fibrosis.80 In animal models, supplementing sodium butyrate or sodium propionate can alleviate inflammation and improve cardiac function.83

Trimethylamine N-Oxide (TMAO)

TMA is produced by the metabolism of choline/carnitine by the gut microbiota and is oxidized by liver enzymes. The generation potential and serum levels of TMAO in patients with heart failure have increased.12,61,65 TMAO can accelerate the progression of heart failure by promoting endothelial dysfunction, myocardial fibrosis, and systemic inflammation, and can be used as a biomarker for the prognosis of heart failure.12,60,61 Dietary intervention (limiting choline/carnitine intake) or microbial enzyme inhibitors can reduce TMAO levels.12

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)

The outer membrane components of Gram-negative bacteria, intestinal barrier damage can cause LPS to enter the blood and activate signaling pathways, upregulating myocardial inflammatory factors such as IL-1β and TNF-α, thereby inducing myocardial inflammation and contractile dysfunction.13,73 It can strongly activate the myeloid differentiation factor 88 (MyD88)/nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway by binding to TLR4, providing an initiating signal for the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and upregulating the expression of components such as NLRP3 and pro-IL-1β;84 it can also directly activate caspase-4/5/11 (in mice, caspase-11) through a non-classical pathway, cleaving GSDMD and promoting the assembly and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome.85 In animal models of heart failure, serum LPS levels are negatively correlated with left ventricular function, and TLR4 inhibitors (TAK-242) can reverse the deterioration of cardiac function.13

Bile Acids (BAs)

The gut microbiota (such as Clostridium species, Megasphaera family) convert primary bile acids into secondary bile acids (such as deoxycholic acid and cholic acid) through bile salt hydrolase (BSH) and 7α-dehydrogenase.86–88 Secondary bile acids can activate the farnesoid X receptor (FXR) and G protein-coupled bile acid receptor 1 (TGR5/GPBAR1) in host cells to regulate host metabolism, inflammatory response, and immunity.86,89 However, it should be noted that hydrophobic bile acids have cardiotoxicity, while hydrophilic bile acids (such as chenodeoxycholic acid) may have protective effects.62 In patients with heart failure, the metabolism of primary bile acids is disordered, and specific bacterial group-related bile acid metabolites (such as β-MCA) are associated with left ventricular diastolic dysfunction.63,90 Therefore, studies have shown that regulating bile acid metabolism may improve cardiac function.62,63

Phenylacetylglutamine (PAGIn)

PAGIn is a metabolite produced by the intestinal microbiota from the metabolism of dietary phenylalanine. It has been confirmed to be closely related to the risk of cardiovascular diseases in recent years.67,91 A large amount of clinical and preclinical research evidence indicates that PAGln can significantly enhance platelet activity and thrombogenic potential. For example, the plasma PAGln level is significantly increased in patients with coronary artery disease who have stent restenosis or hyperplasia, and the abundance of related synthesis genes in the microbiota also increases.92 The plasma PAGln level is also associated with the progression of atrial fibrillation, and its pro-thrombotic mechanism may involve activating platelet adrenergic receptors and enhancing aggregation responses.93,94 Given that CHF patients often have a hypercoagulable state, PAGln is regarded as an important pathological link connecting microbiota imbalance and high thrombotic risk.67 Additionally, in patients with type 2 diabetes and ischemic stroke, the PAGln level is significantly increased and is positively correlated with the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs); animal experiments have confirmed that exogenous administration of PAGln can promote the formation of NETs, exacerbate systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, and aggravate cerebral infarction damage.95 The plasma PAGln level also changes in patients with Parkinson’s disease and mild cognitive impairment, suggesting potential diagnostic value.96 It is still unclear whether PAGln directly regulates the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, but given its ability to exacerbate neuroinflammation and systemic inflammation,95,97 it is speculated that PAGln may indirectly activate or synergistically enhance the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome through receptor signaling or oxidative stress, thereby participating in myocardial inflammation and remodeling in CHF—a hypothesis that awaits experimental verification (Figure 5).

COVID-19 as an Accelerator of Gut Microbiota–NLRP3 Axis Dysregulation in CHF

The COVID-19 pandemic has imposed a substantial burden on patients with pre-existing heart failure. Systematic review evidence indicates that COVID-19 is associated with sustained cardiovascular burden in individuals with HF, underscoring the importance of long-term surveillance.98 Patients with pre-existing HF are particularly vulnerable to adverse outcomes following COVID-19, with increased rates of heart failure hospitalization, new-onset atrial fibrillation, and mortality.99

Mechanistically, the interaction between COVID-19 and CHF may be partially mediated by the gut microbiota–NLRP3 axis. Hoel et al demonstrated that COVID-19 patients with cardiac involvement exhibit elevated markers of gut leakage and inflammasome activation, suggestive of a potential gut–heart axis in COVID-19.100 Specifically, COVID-19 patients had elevated plasma levels of LPS-binding protein (LBP) and CCL25, suggesting impaired gut barrier function and accentuated gut homing of T cells. Levels of LBP were twice as high at baseline in patients with elevated cardiac markers compared with those without and remained elevated during hospitalization. LBP was associated with higher NT-pro-BNP levels, whereas IL-18, IL-18BP, and IL-1Ra were associated with higher troponin levels.100 These observations suggest that COVID-19 may act as an environmental trigger that exacerbates pre-existing gut dysbiosis and NLRP3 activation in CHF patients, though direct evidence for this cascade requires further investigation.

The Association Mechanism Between NLRP3 Inflammasome, Gut Microbiota, and Different Types of Chronic Heart Failure

NLRP3 in Heart Failure with Reduced Ejection Fraction (HFrEF) and the Intestinal Microbiota

NLRP3 Involvement in Myocardial Fibrosis and Ventricular Remodeling

The intestinal flora imbalance in patients with HFrEF is characterized by abnormal abundances of specific phyla (such as increased abundance of Bacteroidetes and Spirochaetes, and decreased abundance of Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria) and decreased diversity.3,13 This imbalance leads to impaired intestinal barrier function and promotes the entry of microbial products such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) into the bloodstream.13,73 Circulating LPS is associated with the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome through the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway (manifested as increased expression of TLR4, P-IKBα/IKBα, and P-p65/p65).13 The activated NLRP3 inflammasome promotes the release of pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β and IL-18 and is associated with myocardial inflammation response, disordered myocardial fiber arrangement, and lymphocyte infiltration, ultimately correlating with myocardial fibrosis and ventricular remodeling.7,13,101 In animal models, the use of the TLR4 inhibitor TAK-242 can reverse these pathological changes, improve left ventricular ejection fraction, and reduce brain natriuretic peptide levels.13

Increased Intestinal Permeability and the Effect of Circulating LPS

In patients with HFrEF and in simulated animals, there is a widespread increase in intestinal epithelial permeability and intestinal barrier dysfunction,3,73 which leads to the translocation of intestinal bacteria and their metabolites into the circulatory system.13,73 Elevated serum LPS levels are directly correlated with significantly increased levels of pro-inflammatory factors.13 Circulating LPS, as an important PAMP, can activate the TLR4 receptor, initiating downstream NF-κB signaling, providing the necessary first signal to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome and exacerbate systemic inflammation and myocardial damage.13

Relevant Clinical and Animal Research Evidence

Clinical studies have shown that in patients with chronic heart failure, as their cardiac function deteriorates, the diversity of intestinal microorganisms decreases, and the levels of endotoxemia, inflammation, and oxidative stress increase, with the severity being particularly significant in patients with grade IV cardiac function.1 Animal experiments have confirmed that rats in the HErEF model induced by myocardial ischemia-reperfusion have abnormal intestinal flora composition, and this is related to the deterioration of cardiac structure and function.13,102 Intervention studies have demonstrated that targeting the TLR4/NF-κB pathway (such as TAK-242) can effectively inhibit NLRP3 inflammation, improve microbial dysbiosis, and protect cardiac function.13 Additionally, spontaneous hypertensive heart failure rats already exhibit abnormal β diversity and metabolic pathways in the intestinal flora before the onset of heart failure, suggesting that microbial dysbiosis may be an early event in HFrEF.102

It should be noted that the above mechanistic insights are predominantly derived from rodent models, which may not fully recapitulate the pathophysiology of human CHF due to species differences in immune regulation, gut microbiota composition, and cardiac physiology.

NLRP3 in Heart Failure with Preserved Ejection Fraction (HFpEF) and Gut Microbiota

Systemic Inflammation, Metabolic Syndrome, and NLRP3 Activation

HFpEF is closely related to systemic inflammation and metabolic syndrome.10,11 Complications such as obesity and diabetes drive persistent low-level inflammation in adipose tissue, liver, and skeletal muscle, generating a large amount of inflammatory cytokines and adipokines that are associated with NLRP3 activation. This systemic inflammation driven by metabolic disorders is the core of HFpEF pathogenesis.103,104 Preclinical models have shown that HFpEF models induced by high-fat diet in animals present complications such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, and hypertension, as well as a systemic pro-inflammatory state.11 This chronic low-level inflammation not only affects the circulatory system but also acts on the heart. For example, in HFpEF patients and animal models, the thickness of epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) increases and is associated with the occurrence and development of the disease, and can produce inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-1β, which through the “EAT-myocardium axis” exacerbate myocardial inflammation and autophagy.105 Additionally, factors such as aging and a high-fat diet, which are “multiple blows,” activate the NLRP3 inflammasome and NF-κB signaling pathway through pattern recognition receptors such as TLR4 to maintain the myocardial inflammatory state, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and abnormal calcium handling, ultimately promoting myocardial hypertrophy, dysfunction, and extracellular matrix accumulation, forming the metabolic cardiomyopathy (MC) phenotype and gradually developing into HFpEF.103 Clinical observational studies have also shown that inflammatory markers and cardiac metabolic characteristics in HFpEF patients are significantly elevated.103 Therefore, targeting these inflammatory mediators, especially the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway, is a potential strategy for intervening in the pathological process of HFpEF.

The Endothelial and Myocardial Effects of Intestinal Metabolites (Such as TMAO)

The metabolites of the gut microbiota, especially TMAO, play a crucial role in HFpEF.59,61 They can activate NLRP3 in cardiomyocytes and coronary microvascular endothelial cells, promoting cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, stiffness, and endothelial dysfunction, and are the core hub of diastolic dysfunction in HFpEF.106 In metabolic type HFpEF of the heart, comorbidities such as obesity, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes can cause dysbiosis of the gut microbiota, resulting in a reduction of beneficial bacteria that produce short-chain fatty acids and an increase in harmful products such as TMAO.106 Elevated TMAO levels not only reflect damage to the intestinal barrier and endothelial function but also exacerbate myocardial fibrosis and diastolic dysfunction.106 Although the specific mechanism by which TMAO activates NLRP3 has not been detailed in the references, other studies have confirmed that TMAO can activate the NLRP3 inflammasome through multiple pathways (such as inducing endoplasmic reticulum stress and reactive oxygen species production), promoting the maturation and release of IL-1β and IL-18, and damaging myocardial function. In HFpEF animal models, inhibition of NLRP3 can improve left ventricular diastolic function, reduce myocardial hypertrophy and fibrosis.10,11 Additionally, other metabolites of the gut microbiota, such as indole-3-propionic acid (IPA), are at lower levels in the plasma and cardiac tissues of HFpEF mice and patients. Supplementing IPA can protect diastolic function by improving the salvage pathway of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) and enhancing the expression of SIRT3, confirming the direct regulatory effect of microbiota metabolites on cardiac function.107 Therefore, targeting the “gut-heart axis,” regulating the balance of the microbiota through diet, probiotics, or drug intervention, reducing harmful products such as TMAO, and increasing beneficial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids and IPA, are potential therapeutic directions for improving inflammation and metabolic disorders in HFpEF.106,107

The Role of NLRP3 in Myocardial Stiffness and Microvascular Disorders

The animal simulation directly confirmed that the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome is involved in the core pathological mechanism of HFpEF. In the mouse model of HFpEF induced by a high-fat diet combined with angiotensin II, the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome significantly increased the level of IL-18 and caused myocardial hypertrophy, myocardial fibrosis (reduced by 32.5%), as well as macrophage infiltration in the myocardium and visceral adipose tissue,11 leading to left ventricular diastolic dysfunction (manifested as abnormal global longitudinal strain and reverse peak longitudinal strain) and myocardial stiffness.10,11 Specific inhibition of NLRP3 (such as using MCC950) could significantly reduce the level of IL-18 (−47.3%), alleviate myocardial hypertrophy (reduced myocardial cell size by 19.5%), and fibrosis, reduce macrophage infiltration, and improve exercise tolerance and glucose tolerance.10,11 Moreover, NLRP3 activation may participate in the myocardial stiffness of HFpEF by promoting microvascular endothelial inflammation and functional disorder, which exacerbates myocardial perfusion abnormalities.10

It should also be noted that although HFpEF animal models (such as high-fat diet/L-NAME mice) can recapitulate some key phenotypes, significant species differences exist between rodents and humans in cardiac physiology, immune regulation, and gut microbiota composition. Moreover, current models lack simulation of common comorbidities such as atrial fibrillation, limiting their translational value.

Discussion on the Mixed Mechanisms of Heart Failure with Mildly Reduced Ejection Fraction (HFmrEF) — A Hypothesis-Generating Framework

HFmrEF (LVEF 41%-49%) is not an independent pathological entity. It is more likely to be a dynamic transitional state between HFpEF and HFrEF, or an intermediate state of HFrEF after treatment.108 A community retrospective cohort study found that among patients initially diagnosed with HFmrEF, 26.9% had LVEF drop to ≤40% during follow-up, and 44.8% had LVEF improve to ≥50%.109 Another retrospective study divided HFmrEF patients into three groups based on LVEF trajectories: stable LVEF, increase (≥10%), and decrease (≥10%). It was found that the risk of all-cause mortality or cardiovascular hospitalization composite endpoint in patients with increased LVEF was significantly lower than that of patients with stable LVEF, while the risk was similar to that of HFrEF patients in patients with decreased LVEF.110 These findings strongly support the evolving characteristics of HFmrEF. The clinical phenotypic heterogeneity of HFmrEF is significant: ischemic causes are more common in HFrEF and HFmrEF, while hypertensive and valvular causes are more common in HFpEF;111 a study found that HFmrEF patients share most phenotypic characteristics with HFpEF patients in terms of microvascular damage and fibrosis degree, but the prevalence of occult ischemic heart disease is similar to that of HFrEF patients.112 This characteristic also manifests in inflammatory-related indicators. For example, the thickness of epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) is in the order of HFpEF > control group > HFmrEF > HFrEF, and a thicker EAT is associated with poorer left ventricular and left atrial function in HFpEF, while the opposite is true in HFrEF and HFmrEF.113 Currently, no direct experimental or clinical evidence specifically links the gut microbiota–NLRP3 axis to HFmrEF pathogenesis.114 The following discussion is therefore hypothesis-generating rather than evidence-based, intended to outline potential directions for future investigation. Based on its transitional state characteristics, it can be speculated that the activation level of inflammation and the pattern of intestinal microbiota dysregulation may change with the evolution of LVEF: Those progressing from HFpEF may retain more metabolic inflammatory features, while those improving from HFrEF may retain stronger ischemic/neurohormonal activation-related inflammatory imprints. Intestinal microbiota dysregulation, increased TMAO, reduced SCFAs, and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome may jointly participate in myocardial remodeling and functional impairment of HFmrEF, but its unique mechanism still needs to be elucidated (Table 1).

New Strategies for Heart Failure Treatment Targeting NLRP3 and the Intestinal Microbiota

Pharmacological Inhibition of the NLRP3 Inflammasome

Preclinical Evidence of Specific Inhibitors

The specific NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 shows significant efficacy in various heart failure models. In female aged mice with HFpEF induced by angiotensin II, the plasma IL-18 level decreased by 47% after 4 weeks of MCC950 treatment, improving left ventricular function (global longitudinal strain increased by 3.9%, P < 0.01; reverse peak longitudinal strain increased by 46.8%, P < 0.05), while reducing myocardial hypertrophy (myocyte size decreased by 19.5%, P < 0.01) and fibrosis (reduced by 32.5%, P < 0.05), and reducing macrophage infiltration in myocardial and visceral adipose tissue.11 In the HFpEF “double-hit” mouse model, MCC950 improved exercise tolerance, glucose tolerance, left ventricular diastolic function, and myocardial remodeling, and alleviated pulmonary hypertension and remodeling, and its mechanism is related to the inhibition of the NLRP3-IL-1β pathway and its downstream inflammatory cascade.10 Additionally, in the myocardial infarction model, Tamibarotene was confirmed to reduce myocardial fibrosis and atrial fibrillation susceptibility by binding to the NACHT domain of NLRP3 (Phe410 and Ile417), inhibiting its ATPase activity and composition assembly, and alleviating myocardial injury and improving cardiac function.9 In the aging-related atrial fibrillation model, MCC950 can reduce myocardial fibrosis and atrial fibrillation susceptibility.17

Of note, despite its robust preclinical efficacy, the clinical translation of MCC950 has been hindered by hepatotoxicity observed in Phase II trials, largely attributed to its furan ring structure. This safety concern underscores the critical gap between preclinical promise and clinical applicability, and has prompted the development of next-generation NLRP3 inhibitors with improved safety profiles.

Oral administration of the NLRP3 inhibitor OLT1177 (Dapansutrile) can directly inhibit the ATPase activity of NLRP3, blocking the maturation and release of IL-1β and IL-18.115 This drug has been proven effective in a phase II clinical trial for acute gout attacks and is currently being investigated for clinical studies on acute decompensated heart failure. It is expected to inhibit the activation of NLRP3 inflammasomes in the myocardium through oral administration, thereby alleviating inflammatory damage and cell pyroptosis during the acute phase.

The new generation of small molecule inhibitors (such as CY-09) have also demonstrated potential in preclinical studies. These compounds are typically designed to directly bind to the NACHT domain of the NLRP3 protein, by inhibiting the ATPase activity and oligomerization process of this domain, thereby blocking the formation of the inflammasome at the upstream level.115 Preclinical study data indicate that CY-09 can effectively alleviate inflammation and improve pathological remodeling in cardiovascular disease models. Due to its high specificity, it is expected to reduce off-target effects and open up new directions for chronic heart failure treatment drugs.

The Exploration of Anti-Inflammatory Drugs in Heart Failure

Current studies have shown that strategies targeting downstream factors of NLRP3 (such as IL-1β) may have potential for treating heart failure. Although the main evidence for canakinumab (an IL-1β monoclonal antibody) comes from atherosclerosis, given the crucial role of the NLRP3/IL-1β pathway in heart failure, this strategy is considered to have potential application value.7,30,116 Although the direct research evidence of colchicine in heart failure is not explicitly stated in the text, it has anti-inflammatory effects. Inhibiting the NLRP3 pathway itself or its effector cytokines is also considered a potential strategy for preventing heart failure.7,8,30,48 Emerging evidence indicates that sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (such as empagliflozin) not only reduce sodium excretion but also decrease the activation level of NLRP3 inflammasomes in myocardial cells and macrophages, and reduce the production of IL-1β and IL-18, which may be one of the important anti-inflammatory mechanisms of cardiovascular benefits.116 In HFpEF, chronic inflammation reduces the damage to cardiac diastolic function by reducing nitric oxide, while SGLT2 inhibitors show potential for improving prognosis through anti-inflammatory effects such as inhibiting the NLRP3 pathway.117 It is also regarded as a promising repositioned drug, with its mechanism of action covering anti-inflammatory, cardiac, and kidney protection and other multiple effects.118 These findings emphasize the importance of targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway in the treatment of heart failure and provide strong support for using existing drugs to develop new anti-inflammatory strategies.116

Potential Differences in Therapeutic Effects for Different Types of Heart Failure

The current preclinical evidence suggests that NLRP3 inhibitors may be effective in various types of heart failure. However, most current studies have focused on the HFpEF model. In HFpEF, MCC950 can improve systemic inflammation, myocardial hypertrophy, myocardial fibrosis, and ventricular/vascular function.10,11 In HFrEF-related models (such as myocardial infarction, angiotensin II–induced cardiomyopathy), the deletion of the NLRP3 gene or pharmacological inhibition (such as Tamibarotene) can also alleviate myocardial damage, inflammation, myocardial fibrosis, and improve cardiac function.9,119 However, the specific research evidence for HFmrEF is not directly provided in the text.

Diet-Based Regulation of the Gut Microbiota

High-Fiber/Mediterranean Diet Benefits on SCFAs and Gut Microbiota

Patients with heart failure generally have a reduction in SCFAs-producing bacteria. High-fiber or Mediterranean dietary patterns can promote the growth of SCFA-producing bacteria such as Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae.12,15 SCFAs (especially butyric acid) have anti-inflammatory, maintenance of intestinal barrier integrity, and inhibition of NLRP3 inflammasome activation effects.15,120,121 Post-Roux-en-Y reconstruction surgery observation found that promoting the colonization of butyric acid-producing bacteria can reduce the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in colon tissue and alleviate intestinal inflammation.122 In animal models, supplementing with high-fiber diets or specific prebiotics (such as yeast β-glucan) can regulate the intestinal microbiota, increase SCFAs, improve the intestinal barrier, and alleviate cardiac inflammation and fibrosis, providing experimental evidence for dietary intervention in the management of chronic heart failure.123,124

Limiting Choline/Carnitine Intake to Reduce TMAO

Limiting foods rich in choline and leucine-containing carnitine, such as red meat and egg yolks, can reduce the production of TMA in the gut microbiota at the source, thereby lowering TMAO produced by liver oxidation.125 Clinical trials have shown that direct use of choline analog antagonists (such as 3,3-dimethyl-1-butanol, DMB) or non-absorbable antibiotics (such as rifaximin) can effectively reduce plasma TMAO levels.125 TMAO has been proven to be an independent risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, and its pathogenic mechanism involves activating the NLRP3 inflammasome, promoting platelet aggregation, and exacerbating endothelial dysfunction.126 However, the long-term cardiovascular outcomes and safety of these interventions still need to be verified.

Calorie Restriction and Intermittent Feeding

Calorie restriction and intermittent fasting have shown potential in improving cardiac function in obesity-related heart failure, especially in animal models of HFpEF.127 Studies have demonstrated that in obese mice induced by a high-fat diet, calorie restriction or ketogenic diets can produce ketone bodies such as β-hydroxybutyric acid, which can effectively inhibit the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome.128 Moreover, intermittent fasting may also improve metabolic disorders and cardiac function through regulating bile acid metabolism and enhancing autophagy, etc.127 These studies provide important preclinical evidence for the adjunctive treatment of HFpEF.

The Applicability of Dietary Intervention for Different Types of Heart Failure

Dietary intervention (such as high-fiber intake, restriction of choline/carnitine) is a fundamental and low-risk strategy, theoretically applicable to all types of heart failure patients, aiming to improve intestinal flora imbalance, reduce pro-inflammatory/pro-atherosclerotic metabolites, and increase beneficial metabolites.4,12,15 However, direct comparative research evidence for optimizing dietary patterns for different types of heart failure is still insufficient.

Functional Foods and Bioactive Components

Beyond macronutrient modulation, emerging evidence suggests that bioactive components from functional foods may exert cardioprotective effects through the gut microbiota–NLRP3 axis.

Fatty acid-derived functional components: Floating wheat (Fu Xiao Mai), used in traditional Chinese medicine for CHF, contains linoleic acid (LA) and α-linolenic acid (ALA) as major bioactive constituents. Li et al demonstrated that LA and ALA alleviate CHF through a dual mechanism: an AQP1-dependent reduction in myocardial edema (with binding energies of −8.532 kcal/mol and −8.835 kcal/mol, respectively) and AQP1-independent anti-inflammatory and gut microbiota-modulating effects, as evidenced by reduced levels of inflammatory markers (TNF-α, NO, VEGF, VCAM-1) and altered gut microbiota composition.129

Polyphenol-derived functional components: Dietary polyphenols, including flavonoids and phenolic acids, represent another important category of functional food components. The gut microbiota serves as a critical biochemical intermediary that transforms dietary polyphenols into bioactive metabolites, including urolithins, phenyl-γ-valerolactones, protocatechuic acid, and short-chain fatty acids, which exhibit enhanced bioavailability and potent inflammasome-modulating properties.130 These compounds suppress NLRP3 activation through multiple converging mechanisms, including inhibition of NF-κB-dependent priming, mitochondrial quality control via mitophagy, Nrf2-mediated antioxidant responses, and HDAC inhibition. This axis functions as an integrated, self-regulated network in which dysbiosis primes NLRP3 and depletes protective metabolites, while polyphenol biotransformation by specific taxa interrupts this feed-forward loop at multiple nodes, restoring homeostasis.

These findings highlight the potential of integrating functional food components—particularly those rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids and polyphenols—into dietary strategies for CHF management. However, most current evidence derives from preclinical studies, and direct clinical evidence in CHF patients remains limited, warranting prospective human investigations.

Based on Direct Intervention Strategies Involving Microorganisms

Probiotics, Prebiotics, and Synbiotics

① Probiotics: Numerous studies have confirmed that supplementing with probiotics (such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species) may increase the abundance of beneficial bacteria and restore the balance of the microbiota.131,132 For instance, probiotics can produce antimicrobial peptides (such as bacteriocins) to inhibit or kill pathogenic bacteria, correct dysbiosis;132 specific probiotic strains (such as HMPM18123) can regulate the microbiota, improve barrier function, and inhibit the TLR4-NF-κB-NLRP3 pathway to alleviate inflammation.133 In the field of heart failure, traditional Chinese medicine (such as compound preparations) can also exert the effect of stabilizing the disease and improving cardiac function by regulating the intestinal microbiota.50

② Prebiotics and Synbiotics: Prebiotics (such as inulin and fructooligosaccharides) and synbiotics (probiotics + prebiotics) are another important strategy for targeting and regulating the intestinal microbiota. Prebiotics can selectively stimulate the growth of beneficial intestinal bacteria (such as bifidobacteria and lactobacilli).134 Synbiotics are the combined form of probiotics and prebiotics, and may produce more significant and stable health benefits.135,136 In cardiovascular and metabolic diseases, one of the core mechanisms is to promote the production of SCFAs (such as acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid), which exert anti-inflammatory, maintaining intestinal barrier integrity, and regulating immune metabolism effects.137 Animal studies have provided strong evidence for this mechanism. For instance, in obese rat models, the polysaccharides from Fu brick tea, as a type of prebiotic, can increase SCFAs and activate the GPR signaling pathway, thereby improving obesity, hyperlipidemia, and inflammatory conditions.138 Another study showed that supplementing mice with prebiotic fructooligosaccharides and enzymes derived from Aspergillus could regulate the intestinal microbiota, increase the levels of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) in the intestines and brain, and that certain genera related to the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), such as Akkermansia, were positively correlated with GABA levels.134 Synbiotics (baby bifidobacteria and low-mannitol) could alleviate colonic inflammation in ulcerative colitis mouse models, inhibit the expression of NLRP3 inflammasome, and were more effective than single components in enhancing intestinal tight junction proteins.139 Similarly, in a mouse model of liver fibrosis, synbiotics could effectively alleviate liver fibrosis by remodeling the intestinal microbiota, enhancing the intestinal barrier, reducing LPS translocation, and inhibiting the LPS/NLRP3/GSDMD cell pyroptosis pathway.140 These studies suggest that prebiotics and synbiotics may have positive effects on CHF by possibly inhibiting myocardial fibrosis and improving cardiac remodeling, but further verification is needed.

The Potential Applications of Fecal Microbiota Transplantation (FMT)

FMT shows potential in restoring the balance of intestinal flora. In an aging-related atrial fibrillation model, young donor FMT can restore intestinal structure, inhibit the activity of atrial NLRP3 inflammasome, and reduce the susceptibility to atrial fibrillation.17 In a hyperuricemia model, healthy donor FMT can alleviate renal damage.141 Although there are no direct studies on patients with heart failure, based on the role of FMT in correcting microbial imbalance and improving inflammation and fibrosis, FMT is considered a potential treatment strategy for heart failure.4,12,141,142

The Application of Microbial Enzyme Inhibitors

Enzyme inhibitors targeting harmful metabolites produced by the gut microbiota (such as 3,3-dimethyl-1-butanol, DMB, which inhibits the generation of TMA/TMAO) are an emerging field. Although not specifically mentioned in the text, regulating the gut microbiota and its metabolites (including microbial enzyme inhibitors) has been reviewed as a potential therapeutic strategy for heart failure.12

The Multi-Target Potential of Combined Treatment Strategies

Given the close interaction between NLRP3 inflammasome activation and intestinal flora imbalance in heart failure (such as the activation of NLRP3 by flora imbalance, and NLRP3 activation affecting the intestinal barrier and flora homeostasis),13,143,144 combined targeting of NLRP3 and intestinal flora may produce synergistic or additive effects, providing a more comprehensive multi-targeted treatment. For example, in ulcerative colitis models, ginsenoside Rg3145 and FSIEVs131 can simultaneously inhibit NLRP3 activation and regulate the intestinal flora, thereby improving inflammation and metabolic abnormalities. Similarly, in the model of intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 can also regulate the composition of the flora (increasing Bacteroides, Bifidobacterium, etc),146 although it is not a heart failure model, its ability to regulate the intestinal flora suggests the possibility of combining with flora intervention. Theoretically, combining NLRP3-specific inhibitors with dietary regulation, probiotics/probiotics or FMT can more effectively simultaneously block the core inflammatory pathways and correct the intestinal ecological balance, providing new therapies for different types of heart failure, especially for patients with significant systemic inflammation and intestinal flora disorders (Table 2).

It should be re-emphasized that the above combination strategies are currently at the stage of theoretical inference and preclinical exploration. Translation from laboratory to clinical practice still requires systematic mechanistic validation and rigorous human studies, including assessment of whether combination regimens are superior to monotherapy, optimal timing and sequencing, and long-term safety profiles.

Conclusions and Future Prospects

This review systematically examines the interaction between gut dysbiosis and NLRP3 inflammasome activation in chronic heart failure. Current evidence suggests that in HFrEF, intestinal barrier damage leading to LPS translocation is associated with NLRP3 activation via the TLR4/NF-κB pathway, potentially contributing to myocardial fibrosis and remodeling; in HFpEF, metabolites such as TMAO are associated with NLRP3 activation through oxidative stress pathways, potentially mediating myocardial stiffness and microvascular lesions; in HFmrEF, a mixed transitional mechanism is suggested. However, the evidence quality is markedly heterogeneous—most mechanistic evidence derives from animal experiments, clinical studies are predominantly observational, and therapeutic strategies remain at preclinical stages. Prospective human studies integrating microbiome, metabolome, and inflammasome detection are urgently needed before any strategy targeting this axis can be recommended for routine clinical management.

Future research should focus on: (1) verifying the direct agonistic effect of microbial metabolites on NLRP3—PAGln, TMAO, and specific secondary bile acids are statistically associated with NLRP3 activation, but whether they are direct molecular agonists requires clarification through molecular docking, surface plasmon resonance, and gene knockout models; (2) elucidating the dynamic evolution of this axis in HFmrEF—as a transitional state between HFrEF and HFpEF, the activation pattern may shift with LVEF changes, necessitating longitudinal cohort studies tracking multi-omics alterations; (3) evaluating synergistic effects of combination therapies (NLRP3 inhibitors + microbial interventions)—given the positive feedback loop between NLRP3 activation and dysbiosis, combined targeting may yield synergy, but systematic assessment in animal models and early trials is required; (4) developing biomarkers for patient stratification and efficacy prediction—multi-omics integration may identify microbial–metabolite–inflammatory signatures predicting therapeutic response; (5) addressing emerging factors including COVID-19 and functional foods—COVID-19 may exacerbate pre-existing dysbiosis and NLRP3 activation in CHF patients, while functional food components (linoleic acid, α-linolenic acid, polyphenols) show cardioprotective potential through this axis in preclinical studies, yet their clinical efficacy and safety require RCT validation.

Notably, the vast majority of mechanistic insights derive from rodent models. Due to significant species differences in immune regulation, gut microbiota composition, and cardiac physiology, animal findings cannot be directly extrapolated to human CHF patients. Translating candidate interventions from bench to bedside—with systematic evaluation of pharmacological feasibility, target specificity, and safety—remains a core challenge.

In summary, the gut microbiota–NLRP3 inflammasome axis offers a new perspective for understanding heart failure pathogenesis and developing therapeutic strategies. However, translation from laboratory to clinical practice requires systematic mechanistic validation and rigorous human studies. Future research should prioritize well-designed prospective cohort studies and randomized controlled trials to clarify causality, subtype-specificity, and therapeutic value, ultimately facilitating precision prevention and treatment strategies for heart failure.
