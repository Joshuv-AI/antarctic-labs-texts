External auditory canal osteoma with coexisting canal wall cholesteatoma: a case report and review of literature
Authors: Deviprasad Dosemane, Deepa Adiga, Meera Niranjan Khadilkar, Nithya Chandy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThough rare, benign lesions that usually do not necessitate active intervention, osteomas of the external auditory canal, can be troublesome when they start to produce mass effect and severe obstruction to the external auditory canal. Even more insidious is the rarer phenomenon of canal wall cholesteatomas, which have been known to cause significant canal erosion and if left unchecked and spontaneous destruction of the adjacent anatomical structures, including the facial nerve, temporomandibular joint, sigmoid sinus, and skull base. The occurrence of both these exceptionally uncommon findings in the same canal is an unusual finding and may point to an undetermined interplay in pathogenesis.Case presentationHere, we describe one such case in a 39-year-old gentleman hailing from South India who presented with complaint of unilateral ear block and decreased hearing and how it was managed.ConclusionsWhile osteomas are usually incidental findings in asymptomatic patients, the potential for mass effect and canal obstruction must be kept in mind and patients must be evaluated for development of the more sinister cholesteatoma in the deeper canal.

Background

Often encountered in otolaryngology practice, cholesteatomas are three-dimensional (3D) cystic sacs of keratin debris usually found within middle ear cavity and mastoid with associated periostitis and bony erosions. However, in rare cases, with an incidence of around 1–5 per 1000 patients [1], such lesions have been found to arise from the external auditory canal (EAC). While EAC cholesteatomas (EACC) can be sparked by entrapment of squamous epithelium following trauma or surgery, or the congenital presence of such cell nests due to arrested development of canal, these cases are often accompanied by severe canal stenosis [2, 3]. Further, it has been suggested that EACC is caused by faulty epithelial migration resulting in local buildup of squamous epithelium which may grow into cholesteatoma, as lesser than typical epithelial migratory rate has been observed in epithelium of inferior canal wall in such patients [4].

Osteomas are rare, benign, noninvasive bony tumors with a predilection to the EAC, facial bones, mastoid cortex, and mandible [5]. EAC is the most affected area, with an incidence of 0.05% of total otologic surgeries [6]. While largely indolent lesions are usually incidental findings, they can result in noteworthy symptoms due to canal obstruction and mass effect [7].

The association of osteoma with EAC cholesteatoma is uncommon; limited cases have been reported. Here, we illustrate one such case involving a 39-year-old man.

Case presentation

A 39-year-old man hailing from South India presented with 2-year history of right-sided ear block and decreased hearing, both of which were insidious in onset and progressive. There was occasional, mild, blood-tinged discharge noted only on usage of Q-tips. He gradually started to develop pain, which was relieved with painkillers; however, tenderness of the canal on using Q-tips persisted. For a few months prior to presentation, the patient also reported the development of hyperacusis. Local examination revealed presence of granulation tissue filling the whole circumference of outer right EAC, obscuring the canal (Fig. 1A). Sticky, thick, mucopurulent discharge was noted coating the tissue. Deeper structures and the tympanic membrane could not be visualized. The left ear and the rest of the otolaryngologic examination were unremarkable.Fig. 1A Endoscopic image of granulation tissue in right external auditory canal. High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) with 0.6 mm cuts showed extensive pneumatization of the right mastoid. Solitary unilateral bony outgrowth of anterior EAC wall into the canal was noted on right side, which was discernably pedunculated, suggestive of (s/o) osteoma. A soft tissue lesion was noted just medial to this growth, filling the canal and extending up to the tympanic membrane (TM); although, the drum itself was visualized separate to it. Middle and inner ear structures and the left ear were normal. Pure tone audiometry demonstrated moderate conductive hearing loss on right with an airbone gap of 41.7 dB and normal hearing on left.

The patient underwent endoscopic excision under general anesthesia (GA). Stay sutures were applied at the 7 o’clock and 11 o’clock position to allow for adequate visualization of canal structures. Under 00 endoscopic guidance, granulations were cleared after appropriate infiltration to reveal a solitary, white, bony, pedunculated lesion with stalk arising from anterior wall of right EAC at the bony–cartilaginous junction (Fig. 1B). The lesion was curetted and removed, which allowed visualization of keratin debris and cholesteatoma in deeper canal, arising from floor and posterior wall and abutting the tympanic membrane (Fig. 2), which were also cleared. The tympanic membrane was found to be intact (Fig. 3). Discussion

Based on clinical history and radiological findings, a preoperative diagnosis of osteoma of right EAC was made in this patient, which was verified by intraoperative and histopathological findings; however, the coexisting canal wall cholesteatoma was an unexpected finding given its rarity.

Osteomas are an uncommon, idiopathic, slow-growing, benign neoplasia of unknown etiology found in the cranial bones, which epitomize a localized hamartomatous proliferation of a typically dense, while otherwise normal, intramembranous bone [8]. Usual sites of temporal bone osteomas are EAC and mastoid cortex [8], along tympanosquamous or tympanomastoid sutures, adjacent to bony–cartilaginous junction [9]. Epidemiologically, osteomas occur most commonly in the thirties of life and is two to three times more preponderant in males than in females [10]. Inflammation and long-standing irritation have been postulated to be causative factors; however, definitive data in support of these suspicions are lacking [11], and the condition remains idiopathic. Osteomas are often misdiagnosed as exostoses, which are also a benign condition, characterized by multiple, bilateral hyperostotic outgrowths from the bony external canal [12] and usually caused by repeated cold-water immersion, triggering a periosteal reaction leading to temporal bone growth [13].

The first description of EAC cholesteatomas was given by Toynbee in 1850, who described a pearly white shining mass in posterior surface of EAC, which he labeled as “molluscum contagiosum of external auditory meatus” [14]. In 1874, Wreden illustrated the presence of accumulated keratinaceous material in a lamellar arrangement, which he termed “keratosis obturans”, differentiating it from ceruminosis obturans or impacted wax [15]. Throughout the late 1800s, any collection of keratin in the canal was grouped under this umbrella heading, until Schofield introduced a new terminology in 1893, viz., “cholesteatoma of EAC,” which he ascribed to insect bite [16]. However, the two terms continued to be used interchangeably for any buildup of desquamated keratin plugs in the bony EAC. It was in 1980 that Piepergerdes et al. first elucidated the difference; they described keratosis obturans as presenting with hearing loss and acute, excruciating pain secondary to accretion of big plugs of desquamated keratin in EAC, which may cause local erosion of the canal, while EACC presented with otorrhea and chronic, low-intensity pain due to squamous invasion into a localized region of periosteitis in EAC wall [17].

Various authors have attempted to explain the etiopathogenesis of EACC, leading to the development of different classification systems (Table 1). Holt described his cases as elderly patients with similar findings of a hard wax cap hiding an area of squamous debris, bony erosion, and granulation tissue [3]. He attributed the etiology as an “epithelial abnormality.” This theory is further supported by a report which had been published by Makino and Amatsu in 1986 [4], which found lowered rates of epithelial migration in inferior canal walls of ears with EACC than normal, (average of 112 µm/day as opposed to 142 µm/day). However, more recent studies published in the 2000s and 2010s appear to disprove this explanation, with the onus shifting from deficiency of migratory capacity of the epithelium to recurrent microtrauma to the canal skin (for example, from usage of Q-Tips) along with smoking leading to microangiopathy and keratin deposition [18–20]. Nevertheless, there remains a dearth of conclusive evidence to satisfactorily explain the true pathogenesis; as Owen et al. explained, it cannot be definitively concluded whether usage of Q-Tips led to EACC or if irritation from EACC resulted in Q-tip usage [21].

In our patient, it seems safe to postulate that the occurrence of osteoma led to narrowing of the canal, as well as disruption of the normal histological integrity of the canal wall, which caused a hindrance to the normal migratory pattern, and chronic inflammation of the canal and subsequent proliferation of granulation tissue, further aggravating the impediment. The accumulation of desquamated keratin debris and persistent inflammation, along with microtrauma due to earbud usage, could all have led to the development of canal wall cholesteatoma.

Due to the largely indolent nature of canal osteomas, patients are usually asymptomatic, and the diagnosis remains on the basis of incidental findings. As such, conservative management with intervallic observation is a rational approach, while surgical intervention is necessitated by large obstructive bony canal lesions causing conductive hearing loss or recurrent infections or those where such complications are expected. Excision may be possible via an external approach through EAC with negligible complications and excellent prognosis [11].

In the case of canal cholesteatomas, Naim et al. [22] proposed management strategies based on his classification system. Removal of keratin debris and local application of cortisone and salicylate was recommended for stage I EACC, while Tos suggested placement of a gauze dampened with hydrocortisone and antibiotic ointment for a week for similar lesions [23], with the procedure being repeated every third month. For stage II and III lesions, Naim suggested surgical excision, with clear margin. In stage III with bony canal defects, saucerization may be required to obtain smoother area, followed by occlusion with soft tissue graft, temporalis fascia, or cartilage of ipsilateral ear. Stage IV lesions require radical surgical removal of infected tissues. Postoperative reconstruction of EAC defect can be done with a split skin graft, as advocated by Garin et al. [24] or defect obliteration with an inferiorly based subcutis-muscle periosteum flap harvested along the posterior edge of the incision, as recommended by Tos [23].

In our patient, given the lesion was still in early stage II, with no destruction of EAC, simple keratin debris removal was carried out, with excision of cholesteatoma sac. A meatoplasty was not required. Postoperative recovery was good, with improvement in hearing and decrease in itching, pain, and sensation of ear block. He has been kept on follow-up, and no recurrence has been observed to date.

Conclusion

While osteomas are usually incidental findings in asymptomatic patients, the potential for mass effect and canal obstruction must be kept in mind, and patients must be evaluated for development of the more sinister cholesteatoma in the deeper canal.
