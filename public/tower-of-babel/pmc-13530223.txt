AI-driven dual-mode phase and label-free fluorescence imaging platform using a single-shot gabor hologram
Authors: Seonghwan Park, Jaeseong Lee, Jaewoo Park, Inkyu Moon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Simultaneous acquisition of quantitative phase and fluorescence information is essential for comprehensive cellular analysis, as these complementary modalities provide structural and biochemical insights, respectively. However, conventional dual-mode imaging systems require fluorescent labeling, complex optical architectures, and multiple acquisition channels, which limit scalability, increase cost, and hinder deployment in low-resource or high-throughput settings. Here, we present an AI-driven dual-mode phase and label-free fluorescence imaging platform using a single-shot Gabor hologram. The proposed framework enables simultaneous reconstruction of quantitative phase images and virtual fluorescence channels from a single low-cost holographic measurement, eliminating the need for fluorescent staining, multi-shot acquisition, or multimodal optical hardware. To achieve this, we introduce a one-sided unsupervised diffusion model that learns a unidirectional mapping from Gabor holograms to dual-mode outputs without requiring paired training data or cycle-consistency constraints. The model integrates contrastive learning–based hologram synthesis with hologram-conditioned denoising diffusion to ensure high structural fidelity and robust cross-modal reconstruction. Ground-truth phase and fluorescence images acquired from a conventional dual-mode optical system are used only during training, while inference relies exclusively on a single-shot Gabor hologram captured with a minimal optical configuration. Experimental validation across multiple cancer cell lines and organelle-specific fluorescence channels demonstrates that the proposed platform accurately recovers cellular morphology and subcellular distributions, achieving an FID of 57.74, SSIM of 0.76, PSNR of 26.89 dB, and LPIPS of 0.12, and further generalizes to unseen conditions including higher magnification, different cell type, low-illumination, and defocused acquisitions. These results support quantitative analysis, cell-type discrimination, and drug-response assessment, establishing a scalable and cost-effective platform that paves the way toward compact, high-content label-free imaging solutions.

Introduction

Simultaneous access to structural and biochemical information is fundamental for comprehensive cellular analysis. Fluorescence microscopy has long served as a cornerstone for visualizing organelle-specific and molecular processes with high specificity, supporting a wide range of applications in cell biology, drug discovery, and disease diagnostics1–6. Despite its strengths, fluorescence imaging typically requires exogenous labeling, involves complex sample preparation, and suffers from photobleaching and phototoxicity, which can perturb native cellular states and limit long-term or high-throughput observation.

Quantitative phase imaging provides a complementary, label-free modality that captures intrinsic optical properties of biological specimens, enabling measurement of optical path difference, dry mass distribution, and morphological dynamics7–14. Among various phase imaging techniques, digital holographic microscopy offers unique advantages by encoding amplitude and phase information into a single interference pattern, allowing numerical reconstruction without mechanical scanning. In particular, Gabor in-line holography features an exceptionally simple optical configuration, making it attractive for low-cost and scalable imaging. However, in-line holograms inherently suffer from twin-image artifacts and limited contrast, which complicate reliable phase recovery and restrict their direct use for advanced multimodal analysis.

To overcome the limitations of single-modality imaging, dual-mode approaches that combine phase and fluorescence measurements have been actively explored15–17. By jointly analyzing biophysical morphology from phase images and biochemical specificity from fluorescence channels, dual-mode imaging enables richer phenotypic characterization than either modality alone. Nevertheless, conventional dual-mode systems rely on elaborate optical architectures, multiple light sources, synchronized detectors, and fluorescent labeling, resulting in increased system complexity, cost, and limited scalability. These constraints pose significant barriers to widespread adoption in compact or high-throughput imaging platforms.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have opened new possibilities for computational imaging by learning complex mappings between optical measurements and desired image representations. Deep learning techniques have been successfully applied to holographic phase retrieval18–22, virtual staining and fluorescence prediction23–25, autofocus and aberration correction26, and automated cellular analysis27–31. In particular, AI-based methods have demonstrated the ability to suppress twin-image artifacts32, enhance signal-to-noise ratio33, and perform end-to-end holographic reconstruction30. Despite these advances, existing approaches typically focus on a single imaging modality or require paired datasets, supervised training, or cycle-consistent architectures34–42, which increase computational complexity and limit practical applicability. Notably, the simultaneous generation of quantitative phase and fluorescence information directly from a single-shot Gabor hologram has not yet been demonstrated.

In this work, we present an AI-driven dual-mode phase and label-free fluorescence imaging platform using a single-shot Gabor hologram. The proposed approach enables simultaneous reconstruction of quantitative phase images and virtual fluorescence channels from a single holographic measurement, eliminating the need for fluorescent labeling, multi-shot acquisition, or multimodal optical hardware. The platform is built upon a one-sided unsupervised diffusion model that learns a unidirectional mapping from holograms to dual-mode outputs without enforcing cycle-consistency or requiring paired training data. By integrating contrastive learning-based hologram synthesis with hologram-conditioned denoising diffusion, the model achieves high structural fidelity and robust cross-modal reconstruction from minimal optical input.

The phase imaging pathway is based on digital holography and supports both Gabor in-line and off-axis acquisition modes. A motorized shutter controls the reference beam, enabling single-shot Gabor hologram capture when the reference is blocked and off-axis hologram acquisition when a weakly angled reference beam is introduced. Both modes share the same microscope objective and relay optics, ensuring spatial correspondence between phase and fluorescence measurements. Fluorescence images are acquired through an epifluorescence imaging pathway that is coaxially aligned with the holographic optics. Wavelength-specific excitation and emission filters enable multi-channel fluorescence imaging of subcellular structures, including the nuclei, mitochondria, and membrane. A high-sensitivity CMOS camera records fluorescence signals, while hardware triggering synchronizes holographic and fluorescence acquisitions to ensure temporal consistency. Pixel-level spatial registration between the two modalities is achieved through calibration using fluorescent microspheres. Because capturing the single-shot Gabor hologram requires the reference beam to be momentarily blocked, however, it is recorded in a separate exposure rather than simultaneously with these co-registered phase and fluorescence targets and is therefore not directly paired with them.

Quantitative phase images used as ground truth are reconstructed from the acquired holograms using standard digital holography procedures. The recorded interference patterns are Fourier-transformed, and spatial-frequency filtering is applied to isolate the desired diffraction order. The filtered field is numerically back-propagated to the object plane to recover the complex wavefront, from which the phase distribution is extracted. This dual-mode optical imaging platform provides accurate, fully co-registered phase and fluorescence measurements that serve as reliable ground-truth targets for training the proposed one-sided unsupervised diffusion model. After training, the dual-mode optical hardware is no longer required: the AI-driven imaging platform reconstructs both quantitative phase and label-free fluorescence images directly from a single-shot Gabor hologram, demonstrating that complex multimodal optical acquisition can be effectively replaced by computational reconstruction.

One-sided unsupervised diffusion framework for dual-mode image reconstruction

To generate high-fidelity phase and fluorescence images from a single low-cost Gabor hologram, we propose a novel one-sided unsupervised diffusion framework that eliminates cycle-consistency constraints fundamental to traditional methods like CycleGAN. Our framework operates with two distinct datasets: dual-mode images (comprising phase and fluorescence images) and Gabor holograms, which are acquired separately without requiring paired correspondence. This unpaired setting arises naturally because the Gabor hologram is recorded under a different acquisition condition than the dual-mode targets, whereas the phase and fluorescence channels within each dual-mode image remain co-registered. The architecture decomposes the reconstruction pipeline into two sequential processes (Fig. 3): first, hologram synthesis from dual-mode images using contrastive learning, and second, dual-mode image reconstruction from generated holograms through conditional diffusion. Figure 3a visualizes the hologram synthesis pathway, where dual-mode images are transformed into synthetic Gabor holograms through patch-level contrastive learning and adversarial supervision. Figure 3b illustrates the subsequent denoising diffusion process, in which noisy intermediate samples are progressively refined into high-fidelity dual-mode images conditioned on the hologram input. In the first stage, generator Gh learns to create realistic Gabor holograms from dual-mode images, guided by contrastive loss and adversarial supervision. The second stage employs generator Gd to reconstruct clean dual-mode images from these synthetic holograms using a diffusion-based denoising process. This unidirectional framework significantly reduces computational complexity while achieving superior reconstruction quality compared to bidirectional cycle-consistent approaches.Fig. 3Overview of the training architecture for the proposed one-sided unsupervised diffusion framework.a Hologram synthesis stage, where synthetic Gabor holograms are generated from dual-mode images using a hologram generator Gh, guided by patch-level contrastive loss and adversarial supervision through a discriminator Dh. Our framework eliminates the need for cycle-consistency constraints that are fundamental to traditional unsupervised domain translation methods, because the inverse mapping from reconstructed images to holograms is neither physically meaningful nor required for inference, while still achieving robust cross-modal learning through a novel combination of adversarial, contrastive, and diffusion-based objectives. This one-sided formulation is particularly appropriate for holography because the hologram-to-dual-mode mapping is inherently asymmetric; no physically meaningful inverse mapping exists to reconstruct a hologram from fluorescence and phase images, making cycle-consistency both unnecessary and restrictive. Unlike conventional approaches that require bidirectional mappings and computational overhead, our one-sided architecture learns unidirectional transformations without enforcing cycle-consistency. This design philosophy significantly reduces model complexity, decreases the parameter count, and accelerates training convergence while maintaining high-fidelity reconstruction quality. The elimination of cycle-consistency is compensated through patch-level contrastive learning, while the inherent structural preservation capabilities of diffusion models enable precise dual-mode image generation. These two mechanisms together ensure semantic consistency and domain-specific feature alignment without requiring explicit bidirectional supervision.

While the training process utilizes dual-mode images containing both phase and fluorescence information to learn cross-modal relationships, the inference phase operates exclusively with a single hologram captured from a cost-effective Gabor configuration. This single-shot approach eliminates the need for complex optical configurations, expensive fluorescent labeling, or multiple image acquisitions, substantially reducing the instrumentation cost and optical complexity of dual-mode cellular imaging. The trained model has learned to extract sufficient information from the interference patterns within a single low-cost Gabor hologram to reconstruct comprehensive dual-mode outputs, effectively transforming a simple holographic acquisition into sophisticated multi-modal cellular imaging.

Discussion

This work introduces an AI-driven dual-mode phase and label-free fluorescence imaging platform that transforms a single-shot Gabor hologram into rich multimodal information through computational reconstruction. By integrating holographic imaging with a one-sided unsupervised diffusion framework, the proposed approach demonstrates that complex dual-mode optical measurements can be effectively replaced by an AI-based inference pipeline operating on a minimal optical input.

A key aspect of the proposed platform is the clear separation between the training architecture and the inference process, as illustrated in Fig. 3 and Fig. 4, respectively. During training (Fig. 3), a dual-stage framework is employed to address the absence of paired hologram–dual-mode data. The hologram synthesis stage bridges the unpaired domains by generating realistic Gabor holograms from dual-mode images using contrastive and adversarial objectives, while the subsequent diffusion-based reconstruction stage learns a robust mapping from holograms to quantitative phase and fluorescence images. Importantly, this dual-stage structure is used solely to facilitate effective learning and is not required during deployment.

During inference (Fig. 4), the platform operates in a markedly simplified manner. Only a single-shot Gabor hologram is required as input, and the trained reconstruction generator iteratively refines noisy latent representations through a hologram-conditioned reverse diffusion process. This clear decoupling of training complexity from inference simplicity is central to the practicality of the proposed platform, enabling high-quality dual-mode reconstruction without fluorescent labeling, multi-shot acquisition, or multimodal optical hardware.

The adoption of a one-sided unsupervised formulation is particularly well suited for holographic imaging. Unlike natural image translation tasks, the inverse mapping from reconstructed phase or fluorescence images back to holograms lacks physical meaning and is unnecessary for downstream applications. By eliminating cycle-consistency constraints, the proposed framework avoids redundant optimization paths and focuses learning capacity on the physically meaningful direction—from hologram to image—resulting in reduced computational complexity and improved reconstruction fidelity.

Another important consideration is the choice of an unsupervised learning objective for the hologram-to-dual-mode reconstruction task. In our system, “fully co-registered” refers to the alignment between phase and fluorescence images obtained simultaneously, whereas the Gabor hologram used as input is acquired under a different condition and is not directly paired with these targets. Therefore, a fully supervised, pixel-wise mapping from Gabor holograms to phase and fluorescence images is not straightforwardly defined. This design decision reflects the broader deployment scenarios targeted by the proposed platform rather than the specific conditions of any particular experimental setup. In practice, obtaining strictly pixel-aligned hologram and dual-mode references for every new cell line, organelle marker, magnification, or optical configuration is time-consuming, requires careful mechanical and temporal co-registration, and is often infeasible for live, motile, or light-sensitive samples. By formulating the reconstruction task in an unsupervised manner, the framework removes the need for pixel-level correspondence between holograms and dual-mode references, allowing it to be extended to datasets collected from different experimental setups, imaging hardware, or biological contexts in which such correspondence cannot be guaranteed. This flexibility is consistent with the robustness observed in our generalization experiments on unseen cell types, illumination levels, and defocus conditions, and positions the proposed platform as a scalable solution for label-free dual-mode imaging beyond any single experimental configuration.

The use of a diffusion-based reconstruction strategy further enhances the robustness of the platform. Diffusion models are inherently effective at preserving fine structural details through progressive denoising, which is critical for accurately recovering quantitative phase information and subcellular fluorescence patterns. Compared to single-step generative approaches, the iterative refinement enabled by diffusion allows the model to better exploit holographic priors encoded in the interference pattern, leading to improved structural consistency and reduced artifacts in the reconstructed outputs.

Beyond technical performance, the proposed platform has important implications for practical bioimaging applications. By replacing complex multimodal optics, fluorescent labeling, and multi-shot acquisition with a single in-line holographic measurement, the approach substantially lowers the optical and experimental barrier to dual-mode imaging. In practice, a low-cost Gabor-based optical setup combined with a GPU-equipped system is sufficient to rapidly reconstruct both quantitative phase and fluorescence images from a single-shot hologram (89.47 ms per image). The ability to generate label-free fluorescence channels from a single holographic measurement enables longitudinal studies without photobleaching or phototoxicity and supports scalable cellular phenotyping, drug-response analysis, and quantitative morphology assessment.

While this study focuses on phase and fluorescence modalities, the underlying framework is general and may be extended to additional imaging contrasts or functional channels. Future work may explore adaptation to other holographic configurations, incorporation of temporal dynamics for live-cell imaging, or integration with downstream analytical models for automated interpretation. Overall, the proposed AI-driven imaging platform demonstrates how the synergy between holography and diffusion-based learning can redefine the role of optical hardware, shifting complexity from physical acquisition to computational inference.

Conclusions

In this work, we presented an AI-driven dual-mode phase and label-free fluorescence imaging platform using a single-shot Gabor hologram. By leveraging a one-sided unsupervised diffusion framework, the proposed approach enables simultaneous reconstruction of quantitative phase and virtual fluorescence images from a single holographic measurement, eliminating the need for fluorescent labeling, multi-shot acquisition, or multimodal optical hardware during inference.

A central contribution of this study is the clear decoupling between training complexity and inference simplicity. While a conventional dual-mode optical imaging platform is used during training to acquire ground-truth phase and fluorescence data, the deployed system operates exclusively on single-shot Gabor holograms captured using a simplified in-line holographic configuration. This separation allows the proposed platform to retain high reconstruction fidelity while maintaining a minimal optical footprint at inference.

The one-sided formulation adopted in this work is particularly well suited for holographic imaging, where the inverse mapping from reconstructed images to holograms is neither physically meaningful nor required. By avoiding cycle-consistency constraints and integrating contrastive learning with diffusion-based reconstruction, the framework focuses learning capacity on the physically relevant transformation from holograms to images. The diffusion-based inference process further enhances structural fidelity by progressively refining noisy representations, enabling accurate recovery of cellular morphology and subcellular fluorescence distributions.

Beyond technical performance, the proposed platform offers a scalable and cost-effective solution for dual-mode cellular imaging. The ability to generate label-free fluorescence channels from a single holographic acquisition opens new opportunities for longitudinal and high-throughput studies without photobleaching or phototoxicity. More broadly, this work demonstrates how AI-driven computational reconstruction can shift complexity from optical hardware to data-driven inference, redefining the design paradigm of multimodal bioimaging systems.

Future extensions of this platform may include adaptation to additional imaging contrasts, incorporation of temporal dynamics for live-cell analysis, and integration with automated downstream analytics. Overall, the proposed AI-driven dual-mode imaging platform establishes a foundation for compact, flexible, and information-rich holographic imaging, advancing the convergence of optical sensing and artificial intelligence.

Materials and methods

Ground-truth dual-mode optical imaging system

The dual-mode optical imaging system was equipped with a Leica 20× microscope objective with numerical aperture 0.55, providing a pixel size of 0.2849 μm for optimal cellular resolution. Coherent illumination was supplied by a red diode laser operating at 666 nm wavelength, ensuring stable interference patterns for holographic reconstruction. For holographic imaging, detection was accomplished using a CMOS camera (Basler acA1920-155). For fluorescence imaging, a separate CMOS camera (Hamamatsu C13440-20CU) was employed for high-sensitivity detection, while excitation was provided by a CoolLED pE4000 system with precise wavelength control for multi-channel fluorescence acquisition. All acquisitions were performed in a controlled laboratory environment with temperature stabilization to minimize thermal drift and mechanical vibrations, ensuring reproducible and high-quality imaging across both modalities.

Sample preparation

For this study, we utilized three distinct human cancer cell lines to evaluate the performance and applicability of the proposed AI-driven dual-mode phase and label-free fluorescence imaging platform: SNU-475 (liver cancer), T-24 (bladder cancer), and NCI-H1299 (non-small cell lung cancer). Each cell line was cultured under standard tissue culture conditions at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Cells were seeded onto glass-bottom imaging dishes to ensure high-quality imaging across modalities. To investigate drug-induced cellular responses, each cell line was divided into five experimental conditions: a control group and four drug-treated groups. The pharmacological agents used were FCCP (10 μM), Rotenone (500 nM), Staurosporine (100 nM), and Blebbistatin (50 μM), each known to target distinct intracellular pathways including mitochondrial function, oxidative phosphorylation, apoptosis, and actomyosin contractility, respectively.

Cells were stained with three types of organelle-specific fluorescent markers to obtain fluorescence-based subcellular labels. Nuclei were labeled using NucBlue® Live reagent (Hoechst 33342), which binds DNA and emits a blue fluorescence under DAPI excitation. The plasma membrane was visualized with CellMask™ Plasma Membrane Stains, producing green fluorescence to outline the cell periphery. Mitochondria were stained using MitoTracker™ dyes, which accumulate in active mitochondria and emit red fluorescence. This three-channel fluorescence labeling strategy enabled us to capture precise spatial distributions of the nuclei, membrane, and mitochondria, which were later used as reference targets for virtual fluorescence image generation. All staining procedures were performed following manufacturer protocols, ensuring minimal phototoxicity and optimal signal intensity.

Datasets and preprocessing

Our dataset comprises holographic and fluorescence microscopy images of three cancer cell lines (lung, liver, and bladder cancer cells) under various drug treatment conditions. Four different drugs (Staurosporine, Blebbistatin, Rotenone, and FCCP) were individually applied to each cell type, resulting in 15 experimental conditions, including untreated controls. For each condition, 50 co-registered dual-mode acquisitions (each consisting of a quantitative phase image and its spatially aligned fluorescence image) were obtained, yielding a total dataset of 750 samples; the corresponding Gabor holograms, captured under a different acquisition condition, are not directly paired with these targets. The data was partitioned with 10 images per condition reserved for testing (150 total), and the remaining 40 images allocated for training (600 total). To enhance model robustness and address limited sample size, data augmentation was performed on the training set through 90°, 180°, and 270° rotations, effectively quadrupling the training data. Original images captured at 900×900 resolution were downsampled to 256×256 using bilinear interpolation for computational efficiency during model training. The dataset consists of single-channel Gabor holograms and phase images, while fluorescence images contain three channels representing cellular compartments: blue for nuclei, red for mitochondria, and green for cell membranes, enabling comprehensive subcellular analysis.

Evaluation metrics

To evaluate image translation performance in terms of output fidelity and computational efficiency, we employed perceptual, pixel-wise, and structural metrics. We computed Fréchet Inception Distance (FID)48 and Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS)49 scores to assess perceptual similarity between generated and reference images. FID quantifies the distance between real and generated image distributions in pre-trained Inception network feature space, with lower values indicating higher similarity. LPIPS measures perceptual differences using deep neural network embeddings, providing human-aligned image quality assessment. Additionally, we calculated Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) and Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM)50 to evaluate pixel-level and structural fidelity, respectively. PSNR measures the logarithmic ratio between maximum pixel intensity and mean squared error, while SSIM evaluates local pixel intensity patterns corresponding to visual perception. To assess model complexity and real-time deployment suitability, we compared total trainable parameters and Multiply-Accumulate Operations (MACs) for each architecture, reflecting computational and memory requirements for biosensor platform integration scalability.

Experimental setup

All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA Quadro RTX 6000 GPU for both training and testing phases. We employed the Adam optimizer with distinct learning rates for different network components: 0.00015 for generators and 0.0001 for discriminators, ensuring stable adversarial training dynamics. The optimizer momentum parameters (beta values) were set to 0.5 and 0.999 for all networks to balance convergence speed and stability. Learning rate scheduling was implemented using Cosine Annealing to progressively reduce learning rates and improve convergence quality. For the diffusion model training in the second stage, we configured the forward process noise schedule with beta values ranging from a minimum of 0.05 to a maximum of 1.0, providing appropriate noise injection across timesteps. The total number of diffusion timesteps T was set to 4, representing a significant reduction compared to conventional diffusion models while maintaining reconstruction quality. Training was performed end-to-end across both stages, and all hyperparameters were selected based on established references to achieve high performance across various image translation benchmarks.
