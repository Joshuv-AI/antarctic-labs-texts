Molecular subtyping and POLE mutation spectrum in endometrial carcinoma
Authors: Megha Uppin, Derin Mary Thomas, Rajshekhar Shantappa, Meher Lakshmi Konatam, Ranganath Ratnagiri, Kaushik Puranum, Khalid Razzak, Shantveer G. Uppin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesEndometrial carcinoma is the most common gynaecological malignancy and an emerging global health concern with limited molecular data from Indian cohorts. Our study investigated the molecular subtypes and POLE gene mutation spectrum in the South Indian cohort.MethodsMolecular subtyping was carried out using ProMisE classification, categorising patients into four molecular subgroups. This involved immunohistochemistry for mismatch repair proteins, p53abn, and Sanger sequencing for POLE mutations. POLE variants were further classified, and the pathogenicity was evaluated using in silico tools.ResultsHistopathology presented endometrioid carcinoma in 84.9% (n= 45) cases in our study. Molecular classification revealed POLE ultra-mutated (3.8%, n=2), MMR-deficient (18.9%, n=10), p53-abnormal (17.0%, n=9), and NSMP (37.7%, n=20) subgroups. POLE gene sequencing identified 11 exonuclease domain variants, including the recurrent endometrial carcinoma hotspot V411L (3.8% ,n=2) and ten non-hotspot variants such as T323I, T457M, T466I, V460M, L306P, P441S, A465V, T290I, E321K, and T278A (22.6%, n=12). Among these, six variants T466I, V460M, L306P, P441S, T290I, and E321K were unique in our cohort, and V460M, P441S, T290I, and E321K were predicted to have oncogenic potential. Clinically, p53abn tumours showed an aggressive phenotype with higher rates of advanced-stage disease and chemotherapy use (55.6%, n=5) compared with POLE-mutated (n=0), Mismatch repair-deficient (MMRd) (37.5%, n=3), and no specific molecular profile (NSMP) tumours (10.0%, n=2).Interpretation and conclusionsThis study demonstrates that integrating molecular profiling with histopathology to develop clinically applicable assays strengthens endometrial carcinoma classification and improves routine prognostic assessment.

Endometrial carcinoma pathogenesis is multifactorial, involving genetic predisposition, hormonal imbalance, and environmental influences.1 It is categorised into two subtypes based on histological and molecular features. Type I endometrial carcinoma (∼80%) is endometrioid morphology driven by unrestricted oestrogen stimulation and shows low-grade glandular differentiation. The type II tumours are (∼10%) high-grade serous and clear cell carcinomas with aggressive clinical behaviour and poorer prognosis, accounting for 40% of mortality.2

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) classifies endometrial carcinoma into four subgroups (1) POLE ultra-mutated tumours with pathogenic exonuclease domain mutations in the POLE gene, (2) Mismatch repair-deficient (MMRd)-tumours showing hypermutation due to inactivation of DNA mismatch repair proteins (3) p53-abnormal (p53abn) tumours with high copy number alterations and serous-like features (4) No specific molecular profile (NSMP) a heterogeneous group lacking the alterations of the other three subtypes. These subgroups are classified using the Proactive Molecular Risk Classifier for Endometrial Cancer (ProMisE).3,4 ProMisE is a practical, low-cost TCGA-based classification system for endometrial carcinoma. ProMisE integrates targeted sequencing to detect POLE mutations and immunohistochemistry (IHC) for p53 and MMR (MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, and MSH6). Furthermore, the World Health Organization (WHO) 5th edition incorporates molecular and histomorphological criteria from the European Society of Gynecological Oncology (ESGO) and the European Society of Pathology (ESP), which highlights the significance of molecular subtyping in the routine endometrial carcinoma diagnostics.5

Globally, the POLE ultra-mutated subgroup occurs in 10% of endometrial carcinomas, but the data on the prevalence and molecular subtyping in Indian patients remain limited. We aimed to use the ProMisE classifier at our centre and attempted to explore the POLE exonuclease domain mutation in our cohort, including the identification of reported/unique variants and predicting their structural and functional properties through in silico analysis.

Methods

This study was undertaken by the department of Pathology, Nizams Institute of Medical Sciences, Hyderabad, India. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee. Informed consent was waived as it was a retrospective study.

Patient selection and clinical characteristics

This study includes a retrospective cohort of 53 endometrial carcinoma patients between March 2019 and January 2025. Patients who underwent preoperative chemotherapy /other treatments before surgery/had poor quality slides and poor formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) blocks, were excluded. Diagnoses were independently reviewed by two qualified pathologists, and FFPE blocks with ≥50% tumour content were selected for molecular categorisation. The tumours were graded based on the WHO criteria, and tumour staging was performed according to the 2023 International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) guidelines.6 Demographics, intraoperative data, and surgical reports were obtained from hospital charts. Staging workup was done by CECT chest and abdomen or PET CT whole body. Staging was done using TNM and FIGO classification. Progression free survival was defined as the time between diagnosis and progression or death due to any cause. Overall survival was defined as the time between diagnosis and death due to any cause.

Molecular subtyping

All the patients underwent molecular subtyping and were classified into four molecular subgroups using the ProMisE classification, as mentioned earlier, defined by TCGA.3

POLE ultra-mutated exonuclease domain mutations

To analyse POLE ultra-mutated tumours, DNA was extracted from FFPE tumour tissues using the TRUPCR FFPE DNA extraction kit (3B Black Bio Biotech, India) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 5 µm FFPE sections were micro-dissected and used for DNA extraction. DNA was quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, USA), and the A260/280 ratio was recorded. PCR amplification of the POLE exonuclease domain (exons 9–14) was performed using previously published primers.7 (Supplementary Table). Sequencing was limited to these exons because pathogenic POLE proofreading domain mutations occur in this region, with 90% clustering in exons 9-13 as reported in large cohort PORTEC and TCGA studies.8 PCR reactions were carried out in a 25 μL volume using standard cycling conditions- initial denaturation at 95°C for 8 min, followed by denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, annealing for 30 s, extension at 72°C for 1 min, final extension at 72°C for 10 min. PCR products were confirmed on 1.5% agarose gel. Bidirectional sequencing was performed on an automated DNA sequencer (ABI PRISM 310 genetic analyser) with ABI PRISM Big Dye Terminator Cycle Sequencing Ready Reaction Kit (Applied Bio systems, USA). Sequence analysis was done using FinchTV v1.4 by comparison with the wild-type reference sequence. Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) 2017 guidelines was used for the interpretation of sequence variants9 and the pathogenicity of each variant was noted. Mutations P286R, V411L, S297F, A456P, and S459F were classified as pathogenic hotspot POLE mutations, while all others were categorised as non-hotspot variants.10

Mismatch repair (MMR) protein

MMR analysis was performed using tissue microarrays (TMAs) constructed from primary tumour samples as per previously reported protocols.11 IHC was performed using the following monoclonal antibodies: MLH1 (clone M1, Ventana), MSH2(G219-1129, Ventana), MSH6 (SP93, Ventana), PMS2 (A-16/4, Ventana). TMA slides were scanned using the 3D histech, Panoramic 250 scanner and digital images were analysed, and findings were recorded manually. MMRd was defined as a complete loss of nuclear staining in tumour cells for one or more of the MMR proteins (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2). All IHC staining was performed using the polymer horseradish peroxidase (HRP) technique on a fully automated immunostainer (Ventana Benchmark GX, Tucson, USA).

p53 abn

p53 was used as a surrogate marker for the copy-number high molecular subgroup indicative of Tp53 mutations. IHC was performed on 4 µm sections of FFPE tissue mounted on positively charged glass slides, using the p53 antibody (Cell Marque, RTU) on an automated Bench Mark Ultra platform (Ventana Medical Systems, Tucson, AZ, USA). p53 expression was interpreted as either positive or negative in accordance with established scoring guidelines.12

No specific molecular profile

In this study, endometrial carcinomas lacking POLE ultra-mutated, MMRd, p53 abn were classified under the NSMP.

In-silico analysis of POLE exonuclease domain variants

To assess the functional significance of the identified POLE variants, seven widely used in silico prediction tools were used. Mutation Taster ( http://www.mutationtaster.org/ ),13 AlphaMissense,14 SIFT (sorting intolerant from tolerant)15 ( https://sift.bii.a-star.edu.sg/), PolyPhen-2 (polymorphism phenotyping v2),16 PROVEAN (protein variation effect analyser)17 ( http://provean.jcvi.org/index.php ), which classifies mutations as deleterious (score < –2.5) or neutral (score > –2.5) and PhD-SNP ( https://snps.biofold.org/phd-snp/pages/PhD-SNP ).18 These computational tools collectively enable to evaluate the functional significance of all detected variants. Further to understand the structural and functional impact (if any) of the identified POLE non-hotspot mutations, 2 in silico tools were used, such as Project HOPE ( http://www.cmbi.ru.nl/hope/)19 and DynaMut2 ( https://biosig.lab.uq.edu.au/dynamut2/ ).20 CScape ( https://cscape.biocompute.org.uk ) was used to predict the oncogenic potential of somatic point mutations in coding and non-coding regions. It assigns quantitative scores to classify variants as high-confidence oncogenic, malignant, or benign, with reported accuracies of ∼92% for coding and ∼76% for non-coding regions.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were summarised as mean or median with range for continuous variables and as counts with percentages for categorical variables. Comparisons across the four molecular subtypes (POLE mutated, MMRd, p53 abn, and NSMP) were performed to assess associations with clinicopathological features, treatment modalities, and survival outcomes. The Kruskal-Wallis test was employed for comparing continuous variables (age and follow up) across molecular subgroups, while Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables (disease stage, histological subtype, surgical procedure, adjuvant therapy requirements, and high-risk features). High-risk features included advanced stage (III/IV), lymph node involvement (N1), metastatic disease (M1), or mortality during the follow up. All tests were two-tailed, with P<0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical and pathological characteristics

The mean age at diagnosis was 56 y (range: 35–79 y). The tumour size ranged from 0.5 to 9.5 cm, with a mean of 4.32cm. Histopathologic subtypes included endometrioid carcinoma (45/53), serous carcinoma (3/53), clear cell carcinoma (2/53), and mixed carcinoma (1/53). In surgical management, hysterectomy with pelvic lymph node dissection (PLND) was the most common procedure (26/53), followed by total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (19/53). The diagnosis was made on endometrial biopsy in eight patients (8). Lympho-vascular invasion (LVSI) and lymph node metastasis were also observed (4/53) in patients.

Molecular subtyping

Figure 1 represents the classification of the cohort into molecular subgroups based on the ProMisE system. The POLE hotspot–mutated subgroup comprised 2/53 of cases, all with endometrioid histology and early-stage. The MMRd subgroup (10/53) presented endometrioid histology and one clear cell carcinoma. About 6/53 underwent total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy. The p53abn subgroup (6/53) included endometrioid and (2/53) serous carcinomas. The NSMP subgroup (20/53) was predominantly with endometrioid histology with one single serous carcinoma ( Fig. 2). Combined molecular alterations such as POLE+p53 was seen in one case and MMRd+p53abn in 2 cases. Representative histopathological images of molecular subtyping are presented in Supplementary Figures 1-4.

Supplementary Figure 6 illustrates the schematic and three-dimensional structures of the POLE variants in both wild-type and mutant forms predicted using the Project HOPE tool. Each amino acid possesses a specific size, charge, and hydrophobicity value. The substitution of a wild-type residue with a mutant residue results in differences in these properties, which may alter protein structure and function. Furthermore, analysis on the structural and functional impact of the non-hotspot mutations analysed by Dynamut revealed eight variants were predicted to be destabilising: T323I (–0.28 kcal/mol), T457M (–0.08 kcal/mol), T466I (–0.53 kcal/mol), V460M (–0.52 kcal/mol), L306P (–1.95 kcal/mol), P441S (–0.25 kcal/mol), T290I (-0.07kcal/mol) and T278A (–0.46 kcal/mol). Two variants were predicted to be stabilising, such as A465V (0.31 kcal/mol) and E321K (0.14 kcal/mol). Supplementary Figure 7 represents the structural and functional impact of POLE non-hotspot variants predicted using DynaMut2. According to the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) classification, all non-hotspot variants in this study were classified in the Variant of Unknown Significance (VUS) category, where CScape predictions suggested oncogenic potential for the majority of the variants except for T466I and L306P.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that ProMisE-based molecular classification is practical and clinically useful in endometrial carcinoma. Molecular subtyping revealed NSMP as the most common group, followed by MMRd and p53-abnormal tumours, with a small subset of POLE-mutated cases. The identification of rare/unreported POLE exonuclease domain variants expands the known POLE mutational landscape and suggests opulation-specific molecular diversity in endometrial carcinoma.

The distribution of ProMisE molecular subgroups in our cohort was comparable to reports from other Indian studies (POLE: 6.3%, MMRd: 22.9%, p53 mutation/Copy number high: 25.0%, NSMP: 41.7%)21 and was comparable to global datasets including the TCGA (POLE: 7.0%, MMRd: 28.0%, Copy number high: 26.0%, NSMP: 39.0%) and the ProMisE classifier3 (POLE: 9.0%, MMRd: 29.0%, Copy number high: 18.0%, NSMP: 45.0%). Multiple classifier subgroups with a reported frequency of 3–6% were similarly observed in our cohort, including the mixed POLE+p53abn, MMRd+p53abn, and POLE non-hotspot mutations.8,22

The reported frequency of POLE ultramutated tumours in the literature ranges from 7-15%. The lower frequency (3.8%, n=2) of this group in our study may be due to differences in the clinicopathological profile of our patients. Previous reports have demonstrated a higher prevalence of POLE mutations in high-grade endometrioid carcinomas and in mixed histology23 whereas the majority of our patients had grade 1 endometrioid carcinomas. Imboden et al24 (Swedish cohort) (2019) report 58% of the POLE-mutated cases having grade 1 and 2 histology and a significantly better outcome in the POLE group. McConechy et al25 (2016) emphasise the geographic and population-specific variability, with 62% being high-grade endometroid carcinoma in the POLE group with excellent prognosis. All these studies convey the importance of doing POLE mutations irrespective of histologic grading for patient stratification and prognostic benefit, as is highlighted in our cohort. Younger patient age is a common observation in all these studies, and our cohort demonstrated the same trend. A recent study from a similar South Indian endometrial carcinoma cohort (Kuriakose et al26 2024) reported a high prevalence of non-pathogenic POLE variants, detecting 34 sequence variants, of which 10 were classified as VUS.26 The exon 13 variant P441S was reported in the study and also in our cohort, indicating population-specific recurrence in both studies. Across all studies, recurrent hotspot mutations such as P286R and V411L were observed as the most commonly reported variants.8,24,25 In addition, non-hotspot variants such as A456P, S297P/F and M295R were also recurrently detected across multiple cohorts.

The hotspot variant spectrum identified in our cohort has been previously reported in the COSMIC database as pathogenic at lower frequencies in other populations. The T323I variant observed in our study has previously been reported as T323A (COSM6911534). The POLE non-hotspot mutation T457M was observed recurrently in the COSMIC endometrial carcinoma databases.27 Our analysis of non-hotspot mutations revealed known endometrial carcinoma exonuclease domain pathogenic driver variants previously reported in the literature, including T466I, V460M, A465V, and T278A. In addition, three amino acid positions harboured alternative substitutions previously described: T323I (reported as T323P/A), L306P (reported as L306F), and P441S (reported as P441L)28 were observed in the cohort. Six previously unreported POLE variants, such as T466I, V460M, L306P, P441S, T290I, and E321K, were identified in our cohort. In silico prediction using CScape suggested a potential oncogenic role for four variants (V460M, P441S, T290I, and E321K). These unique POLE variants observed in the study, evaluated using in silico pathogenicity prediction tools, provided preliminary assessments of their potential functional impact. It is worth noting that, as per ACMG–AMP guidelines, these findings constitute supporting rather than stand-alone strong evidence and do not replace experimental functional validation to understand their definitive pathogenic classification.29 The presence of these unique variants in the Indian population suggests population-specific variants warranting further validation in larger Indian cohorts. The limited sample size of the present study could have influenced the detection of POLE-mutated high-grade tumours.

Our study, inclusive of the South Indian patient population from a single tertiary care centre, validates the integration of clinicopathological parameters with ProMisE-based molecular classification for endometrial carcinoma. It represents all four ProMisE molecular subtypes with internal comparison of clinical behaviour. Beyond identifying hotspot mutations, the study provides an in-depth analysis of non-hotspot POLE variants, including exon distribution, in silico pathogenicity prediction, ACMG classification, and structural stability assessment using Project HOPE and DynaMut2. We contribute to the concept of population-based molecular diversity. A single centre cohort with a small sample size (particularly for the POLE mutated cases), underrepresentation of the higher grades, reliance on in silico tools, and limited follow up period are some of the limitations of our study. We plan to extend this work to a larger cohort with greater histologic diversity and longer follow up.

The study emphasises the incorporation of molecular classification into routine diagnostic workflows for standardised reporting, beyond conventional histology and grade. It supports emerging policies of treatment de-escalation in POLE-ultramutated endometrial carcinomas. These policy measures would enable the evidence-based use of POLE-guided treatment decisions, including immunotherapy in routine clinical care.30 The non-hotspot POLE variants require a clear mention of VUS with a note for functional validation. Similarly, it highlights the early identification of aggressive p53-abnormal tumours for adjuvant therapy and high-risk treatment pathways.
