An Updated Checklist and Narrative Review of Helminth Parasites of Terrestrial and Semiaquatic Carnivorous Mammals in Uzbekistan
Authors: Alisher Safarov, Sunnat Okkiyev, Angela Monica Ionică, Mourad Ben Said
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHelminth parasites are critical components of biodiversity and influence the health of both wild and domestic carnivorous mammals. Despite their ecological and public health importance, comprehensive data on helminth diversity in Uzbekistan remain fragmented. This study delivers a thorough and updated synthesis of helminth parasites infecting 15 species of terrestrial and semi‐aquatic carnivorous mammals in Uzbekistan.MethodsThis study was designed as an updated checklist and narrative review rather than a formal systematic review or meta‐analysis. A total of 120 documents published between 1926 and 2024 were reviewed, including 61 journal articles, 54 national conference abstracts and 5 PhD theses written in Russian, Uzbek and English. Data were obtained from PubMed, ResearchGate, Google Scholar and local archival sources. Parasite and host nomenclatures were updated using internationally recognized taxonomic databases and recent parasitological references.ResultsThe review identified 74 helminth species, including 9 trematodes, 24 cestodes, 5 acanthocephalans and 36 nematodes, recorded from 15 carnivorous mammal species belonging to the families Canidae, Felidae and Mustelidae. Among these parasites, 37 species (50.0%) were retained as having zoonotic relevance after reassessment, although their public health importance varies from well‐established zoonoses to occasional or accidental human infections. Research efforts were strongly biased toward domestic dogs, jackals and foxes, while several host taxa and geographic regions remain poorly investigated.ConclusionThis review highlights the rich but unevenly documented helminth diversity of carnivorous mammals in Uzbekistan and provides a taxonomically updated baseline for future regional studies. Persistent gaps in host coverage, geographic sampling, molecular identification and zoonotic risk assessment limit current understanding. Future research should prioritize representative sampling, inclusion of poorly studied carnivore taxa, molecular and non‐invasive diagnostic approaches and integrated One Health surveillance to better assess parasite biodiversity, wildlife health and public health risks in Uzbekistan and Central Asia.Keywords: carnivores, Central Asia, diversity, helminths, Uzbekistan, wildlife

Introduction

The conservation and sustainable use of the environment and biological resources during Uzbekistan's rapid economic and social development is a crucial task and a priority of state policy (Kashkarov and Metropolskaya 2021).

Uzbekistan's rich fauna is largely attributed to its geographical location, which allows for the migration of animal species from Northern Europe, Central Asia, India and Africa. The region's natural and climatic conditions, along with its varied landscape, further contribute to the diversity of animal life. Most areas of Uzbekistan are home to specific animals, so each area is often unique in its fauna. Some animals living in Uzbekistan are especially significant because of their decreasing population, and in some cases, there is a risk of extinction (Academy of Sciences of the Republic of Uzbekistan 2019).

The fauna of Uzbekistan comprises 107 mammal species, 5 amphibians, 62 reptiles and 474 birds (Kashkarov et al. 2020). The Red Data Book of Uzbekistan (Conservation of Animals) lists 30 mammal species, 52 bird species, 21 reptile species, 18 fish species, 3 annelid worms, 15 mollusc species and 67 arthropod species (Academy of Sciences of the Republic of Uzbekistan 2019). Among the animal species, Carnivora are known for their diverse ecological adaptations. They inhabit various landscapes, thriving both on land and in aquatic environments, and play crucial roles in Uzbekistan's biological diversity (Deak et al. 2022; Safarov, Nasreen, et al. 2023; Safarov 2024). In Uzbekistan's ecosystems, 24 carnivorous mammals belonging to the families Canidae, Ursidae, Mustelidae, Hyaenidae and Felidae live (Shernazarov et al. 2006), which are at risk of several ecto‐ and endoparasites (Safarov 2024).

The initial studies of the helminth fauna of predatory mammals in Uzbekistan were conducted by the renowned Russian zoologist Professor A. P. Fedchenko, who led a scientific expedition from 1868 to 1871 (Safarov 2024). Current information about the helminth fauna of Uzbekistan, especially of predatory mammals, however, is closely associated with Academician K.I. Skrjabin and his school. Professor A. M. Petrov (1926) began examining the helminth fauna of various predatory mammal groups in Uzbekistan. In his work on the fauna of parasitic worms of domestic carnivores in Turkestan, he reported several helminth species found in dogs and cats, with some material collected from Uzbekistan (Petrov 1926).

Systematic studies of the helminth fauna in domestic and wild carnivores of Uzbekistan have been ongoing since 1950, and the findings from these studies have been published (Irgashev 1958; Irgashev and Sadykov 1967; Sultanov et al. 1969, 1971, 1975; Muminov 1976; Muminov et al. 1986; Safarov et al. 2018; Safarov 2020; Safarov et al. 2021; Berdibayev 2021; Safarov et al. 2022, 2023, 2024).

However, the majority of the existing literature is in Uzbek and Russian languages, or the documents are not indexed in international databases, making their access limited for the international scientific community. In the literature published from 1926 to 1980, very few animals were studied, making it difficult to draw a proper conclusion regarding their parasitic fauna. Also, the research work was carried out was based exclusively on classical methods.

The most recent research work was published by Safarov et al. (2023), where the authors provided information on the distribution and epidemiology of helminths in carnivorous mammals of Uzbekistan. However, detailed information on the taxonomic status and parasite–host relationships of each species, as well as the regions of Uzbekistan in which they are distributed, was not provided. The authors identified a total of 71 species of helminths in representatives of 3 families of carnivorous mammals of Uzbekistan (Canidae, Felidae and Mustelidae). No data were provided on species of zoonotic significance. Also, outdated taxon names were used for some species (Multiceps multiceps, M. serialis, M. skrjabini, Rictularia affinis, R. cahirensis and Diphyllobothrium latum). In addition, some listed species are not included in international databases and are not accepted by the international community (Troglostrongylus badanini, Oxynema numidica, Vigisospirura skrjabini). These cases have always been the subject of discussion among foreign researchers.

Considering these issues and the lack of comprehensive information on the species composition and taxonomic status of helminths in carnivorous mammals in Uzbekistan, this article aims to provide an updated overview of their diversity, known hosts, geographical distribution and zoonotic potential. The taxonomic status of species is also regulated. Gaps in different aspects of knowledge are also discussed.

Materials and Methods

Data Sources and Literature Search

This study was designed as an updated checklist and narrative review rather than a formal systematic review or meta‐analysis. Data were compiled from published journal articles, conference abstracts, PhD theses, governmental reports, local archival documents, personal communications with relevant governmental organizations, and the authors' own research data. The reviewed literature covered the period from 1926 to 2024 and included documents written in Russian, Uzbek and English. Literature searches were conducted in March 2025 using three electronic databases: PubMed, ResearchGate and Google Scholar. The search strategy combined the terms ‘Uzbek’, ‘Uzbekistan’, ‘parasite’, ‘helminth’, ‘helminth fauna’ and ‘Carnivora’ with the Latin names of carnivorous mammal species recorded in Uzbekistan. Searches were performed in English, Russian and Uzbek to maximize the recovery of regional and internationally indexed literature. Because much of the historical parasitological literature from Uzbekistan is not indexed in international databases, electronic searches were supplemented by archival and local sources. Additional documents were collected from the Central Library of the Academy of Sciences of the Republic of Uzbekistan, the Samarkand State University of Veterinary Medicine, Livestock and Biotechnology and the Veterinary Research Institute. Based on these collected records, the taxonomic status of helminth parasites infecting carnivorous mammals in Uzbekistan was reviewed and updated.

Search Strategy and Inclusion Criteria

The search strategy combined the keywords ‘Uzbek’ and ‘Uzbekistan’ in English, Russian and Uzbek with parasite‐related terms, including ‘parasite’, ‘helminth’, ‘helminth fauna’ and ‘Carnivora’. These terms were further combined with the Latin binomials of all carnivorous mammal species recorded in Uzbekistan, following Kashkarov et al. (2020). The targeted hosts included 15 carnivorous mammal species, comprising 14 terrestrial species and one semi‐aquatic species: Canis aureus, C. lupus, Vulpes corsac, V. vulpes, C. lupus familiaris, Martes foina, Neogale vison, Meles meles, Mustela nivalis, Felis chaus, F. lybica, F. catus, Lutra lutra, Lynx lynx and Vormela peregusna. Among these species, L. lutra, L. lynx and V. peregusna are listed in the Red Data Book of Uzbekistan. In addition, L. lutra is classified as ‘Near Threatened A2c’, whereas V. peregusna is classified as ‘Vulnerable A2c’ according to the IUCN Red List. Documents were included if they reported original or reviewed data on helminth parasites infecting the targeted carnivorous mammals within the geographic boundaries of Uzbekistan and were published between 1926 and 2024. Eligible document types included peer‐reviewed journal articles, conference abstracts, doctoral theses, governmental reports, archival documents and verified unpublished records. Studies were excluded if they lacked primary parasitological data, did not concern carnivorous mammals, were conducted outside Uzbekistan, or provided parasite records that could not be linked to a defined host or locality. Duplicate records were identified by comparing titles, authors, year of publication, host species, parasite species and geographic locality. When the same parasite–host–locality record was reported in more than one source, the most complete and/or peer‐reviewed source was retained, while duplicate information was not counted twice in the synthesis.

Data Extraction and Synthesis

A total of 120 documents were reviewed, including 61 journal articles, 54 national conference abstracts and 5 PhD theses. The language distribution was as follows: 68 documents in Russian, 36 in Uzbek and 16 in English. These documents were retrieved from Google Scholar (31), PubMed (7), ResearchGate (9) and the Central Library of the Academy of Sciences of the Republic of Uzbekistan (73). Data on parasite species, definitive hosts and precise geographic localities within Uzbekistan were systematically extracted and compiled. Unpublished records and personal communications were included only when they could be linked to verifiable institutional sources, archival documents or expert‐confirmed records. Such data were used cautiously and were not prioritized over peer‐reviewed publications when conflicting information was present. The compiled parasite fauna encompasses a broad diversity of taxa among Trematoda, Cestoda, Acanthocephala and Nematoda, representing infections in both domestic and wild carnivorous mammals. These data allowed for detailed ecological and epidemiological analyses.

Taxonomic Validation and Data Quality

Parasite and host nomenclatures were verified and standardized according to internationally recognized references, including the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF, https://www.gbif.org), Fauna Europaea (https://fauna‐eu.org), the World Register of Marine Species (WoRMS, https://www.marinespecies.org/) and updated classifications, catalogues and indexes of valid genera (Vieria et al. 2008; Nakao et al. 2013; Mathison and Sapp 2021; Hodda 2022). The names of helminth species reported from carnivorous mammals in Uzbekistan between 1926 and 2024 were revised according to currently accepted nomenclature, with historical synonymies and outdated taxonomic combinations updated where appropriate. Because the reviewed records covered a long period and were published in different languages and document types, methodological quality was carefully assessed. Priority was given to peer‐reviewed publications, while conference abstracts, theses, archival documents and verified unpublished data were included cautiously to fill gaps for poorly studied parasite–host associations. When conflicting information was encountered, peer‐reviewed and taxonomically updated sources were prioritized. It should be noted that, to the best of our knowledge, no reference specimens from the historical records were deposited in scientific collections or museums. Therefore, older records based exclusively on morphology were interpreted cautiously, especially for taxa known to include cryptic species or historically problematic synonymies. The multilingual search approach helped reduce language bias and allowed the inclusion of regional literature that is often inaccessible to the broader international scientific community.

Determining the Zoonotic Characteristics of Species

The zoonotic status of helminth species recorded in carnivorous mammals of Uzbekistan between 1926 and 2024 was assessed using information from the World Health Organization (WHO), the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH) and recent peer‐reviewed publications. A species was considered zoonotic only when published evidence of natural human infection was available. To avoid overinterpretation, zoonotic species were classified into three categories according to the strength of evidence and public health relevance: confirmed zoonotic species, for parasites with well‐documented and epidemiologically recognized human infections, occasional zoonotic species, for parasites reported only sporadically or accidentally in humans and potential zoonotic species, for parasites with limited evidence, rare spillover events or uncertain epidemiological importance. When evidence was insufficient or controversial, the species was not considered a confirmed zoonosis.

Results

Overview of Helminth Diversity

A total of 74 helminth species were identified in wild and domestic carnivorous mammals across Uzbekistan, summarizing nearly a century of research efforts (Table 1). These parasites comprise 9 species of Trematodes, 24 Cestodes, 5 Acanthocephalans and 36 Nematodes, affecting hosts from five major families: Canidae, Felidae and Mustelidae. While helminth records are documented throughout the country, research efforts have been disproportionately concentrated on domestic dogs, jackals and foxes, as reflected by the higher number of studies for these hosts. Table 1 provides a consolidated overview of all helminth species detected, their corresponding hosts, geographic distribution by administrative region and zoonotic status. This summary highlights both the broad parasite diversity and the uneven representation among host taxa and regions. Geospatial analysis, visualized in Figure 1, demonstrates that helminth parasite records are not evenly distributed nationwide. Most confirmed localities are clustered in well‐studied Central and Northern provinces, with some regions lacking data, potentially revealing true knowledge gaps or reflecting limitations in historical and recent sampling. The areas shaded in brown on Figure 1 denote administrative regions where at least one helminth species has been confirmed in carnivorous mammal hosts. Collectively, these data underscore the rich but incompletely mapped diversity of helminth fauna in Uzbekistan's carnivores, and reveal both substantial progress and persistent gaps in our current knowledge base. These patterns justify the need for expanded, geographically comprehensive surveillance and integrative studies, including molecular diagnostics, to achieve a more complete understanding of the helminth community and its implications for animal and public health.

Parasite Inventory

The following inventory details the full spectrum of helminth parasites identified in wild and domestic carnivorous mammals of Uzbekistan over the course of nearly a century of research. Compiled from both historical and recent sources, this list integrates morphological and available molecular data, offering an up‐to‐date reference for the region's helminth fauna. For each parasite species, we present taxonomic classification, definitive host records and precise geographic localities when available. This comprehensive listing is intended to serve as a baseline for future epidemiological studies, molecular investigations and regional biodiversity assessments.

Discussion

Host Coverage and Geographical Gaps

This review provides the first comprehensive synthesis of helminth parasites recorded in wild and domestic carnivorous mammals in Uzbekistan, documenting a total of 74 species. These parasites have been reported from members of the Canidae, Felidae and Mustelidae families (Table 1). However, the available data reveal a pronounced imbalance in research effort among host species. Domestic dogs, jackals and red foxes account for the majority of studies, with 28, 16 and 9 publications, respectively, whereas several carnivorous species remain poorly investigated or completely unstudied. This uneven coverage limits a comprehensive understanding of helminth biodiversity and host–parasite associations across the entire carnivore community of Uzbekistan.

Historically, knowledge of helminths infecting carnivorous mammals in Uzbekistan was based on the pioneering work of Petrov (1926); Irgashev (1956); Delyanova (1958); Matchanov (1959); Muminov (1963; 1976); Sadykov (1967) and Taryannikov (1983). Until 2018, only six (25.0%) carnivorous mammal species had been examined for helminth infections. More recent investigations have substantially expanded this knowledge base, increasing the number of studied species to 15 (62.5%) by 2024 (A. S. Berdibaev 2021; Safarov, Khan, et al. 2023; Safarov, Akramova, Turgunov, et al. 2023). This progress reflects growing recognition of the ecological and epidemiological importance of parasite diversity in carnivorous mammals. Despite these advances, major knowledge gaps remain. In particular, no comprehensive information is currently available for members of the Ursidae and Hyaenidae families, which together comprise nine (37.5%) carnivorous mammal species occurring in Uzbekistan. These poorly studied hosts may harbour unique parasite assemblages with potential implications for wildlife conservation, ecosystem functioning and zoonotic disease transmission. In addition to taxonomic gaps, parasite records remain geographically uneven. Most available data originate from a limited number of surveyed regions, suggesting that the observed distribution patterns may partly reflect sampling intensity rather than the true distribution of helminth species. Therefore, future research should prioritize systematic and geographically representative surveys, including the examination of opportunistically collected carcasses (e.g., road‐killed or naturally deceased animals), through collaboration between wildlife authorities and parasitological research institutions. Such efforts would substantially improve our understanding of helminth diversity, host range and geographic distribution in Uzbekistan.

Regional Comparison With Neighbouring Countries

Uzbekistan shares borders with Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Afghanistan and Turkmenistan, making regional comparisons important for understanding the diversity and distribution of helminths infecting carnivorous mammals in Central Asia. However, analysis of the available literature indicates that comprehensive inventories of helminth fauna remain scarce throughout the region. Most studies have focused on a single host species or a limited group of carnivores rather than providing broad assessments of parasite diversity (Ziadinov et al. 2010; Abdybekova and Torgerson 2012; Lyudmila et al. 2020; Akibekov et al. 2023). Kazakhstan represents the best‐studied neighbouring country. Lyudmila et al. (2020) reported 11 helminth taxa infecting wolves, red foxes and corsac foxes, while Abdybekova (2014) identified seven helminth taxa in corsac foxes and Abdybekova and Torgerson (2012) reported nine taxa in wolves. Additional studies documented parasite species not yet recorded in Uzbekistan, including Taenia krabbei and Trichinella nativa in wolves (Uakhit et al. 2022). Furthermore, Berkinbay et al. (2024) identified 17 helminth taxa in brown bears (Ursus arctos), a host species whose helminth fauna remains unstudied in Uzbekistan.

In Kyrgyzstan, available investigations have primarily focused on Echinococcus granulosus infections in dogs (Abdybekova et al. 2015). Information from Turkmenistan is largely derived from the governmental report ‘Eradication of Dracunculiasis in Turkmenistan’, which documented 140 helminth species in wild animals, including 52 species reported from carnivores and 19 species infecting humans. The same report highlighted the historical importance of Dracunculus medinensis infections in both dogs and humans (Berdyeva 1998). In contrast, very limited information is available regarding helminths of carnivorous mammals in Tajikistan and Afghanistan. This available evidence suggests that knowledge of helminth diversity in carnivorous mammals remains fragmented across Central Asia. Consequently, the checklist presented here not only updates the situation in Uzbekistan but also provides an important regional reference framework for future comparative and transboundary studies on parasite biodiversity, wildlife health and zoonotic disease emergence.

Taxonomic Limitations and the Need for Molecular Tools

One of the major challenges identified during this review concerns the taxonomic inconsistencies present in the historical literature. Several helminth species have been reported under outdated names or synonymized taxa, creating confusion and complicating comparisons among studies. For example, E. multilocularis was referred to as Alveococcus multilocularis in some earlier publications (Sadykov 1967; A. S. Berdibaev 2021). Similarly, T. multiceps and T. skrjabini were historically reported as M. multiceps and M. skrjabini, respectively (Irgashev 1956; Delyanova 1958; Matchanov 1959; Safarov et al. 2018; A. S. Berdibaev 2021). To address these inconsistencies, all parasite names reported in the reviewed literature were revised and standardized according to currently accepted taxonomic nomenclature.

Another important limitation is that most helminth records from Uzbekistan were established exclusively through morphological identification. Although morphology remains an essential diagnostic tool, reliance on morphological characters alone may lead to misidentifications and may fail to detect cryptic diversity. This issue is particularly relevant in groups that have undergone substantial taxonomic revision in recent years. For example, revision of the genus Taenia led to the resurrection and recognition of the genera Hydatigera and Versteria (Nakao et al. 2013). Likewise, the name E. granulosus is currently restricted to E. granulosus sensu stricto, whereas other members of the E. granulosus sensu lato complex require accurate molecular discrimination (Thompson 2020).

The integration of molecular methods into future surveys is therefore strongly recommended. Combining morphological and molecular approaches would improve species delimitation and facilitate the identification of morphologically similar or cryptic taxa, including members of the E. granulosus sensu lato complex (Thompson 2017), Spirocerca vulpes (Rojas et al. 2018), H. taeniaeformis sensu lato (Lavikainen et al. 2016) and Gongylonema nepalensis (Setsuda et al. 2020). Such integrative approaches would significantly enhance the reliability of parasite inventories and improve our understanding of host–parasite relationships in Central Asian ecosystems.

Recent advances in molecular ecology also offer new opportunities for non‐invasive wildlife parasitology. Molecular analysis of faecal samples has become an increasingly valuable tool for parasite detection and host assignment, allowing the investigation of parasite communities without capturing or disturbing wild animals. This approach has demonstrated high accuracy for host species identification and represents a promising avenue for future studies on wild and protected carnivores in Uzbekistan (Rojas et al. 2024).

Zoonotic Significance and One Health Relevance

Among the 74 helminth species recorded, 37 (50.0%) were retained as having zoonotic relevance after reassessment, with several species, including E. granulosus, Dipylidium caninum, Toxocara canis and Dirofilaria repens, being of major epidemiological importance (Sazmand et al. 2019; Sazmand et al. 2020; Hosseini et al. 2022; Safarov et al. 2022). However, the public health significance of these species is not equivalent. Some parasites, such as E. granulosus, E. multilocularis, T. canis, T. cati, D. repens and D. caninum, represent well‐established zoonoses, whereas others should be regarded as occasional or accidental zoonotic infections because human cases are rare, sporadic or dependent on specific exposure pathways. This distinction is important to avoid overestimating the public health impact of all recorded helminth species. The high proportion of zoonotic helminths identified in carnivorous mammals highlights the close links between wildlife health, domestic animal health and human health. Recent reports of human infections caused by the zoonotic filarioid D. repens in Uzbekistan further emphasize the need for continuous surveillance and preventive measures (Safarov, Ionică, et al. 2023). Similarly, although echinococcosis agents have been detected in a broad range of definitive and intermediate hosts, reliable epidemiological information on human echinococcosis remains limited or insufficiently accessible. This lack of accurate data may contribute to underestimation of the public health burden associated with these parasites.

The growing interface between humans, domestic animals and wildlife in Central Asia increases opportunities for parasite transmission and reinforces the relevance of a One Health framework. Because many of the helminths identified in carnivorous mammals are also capable of infecting humans, integrated surveillance programs should involve veterinarians, wildlife biologists, parasitologists and public health authorities. Such collaboration would facilitate early detection of emerging zoonotic threats and improve understanding of transmission pathways between wildlife reservoirs, domestic animals and human populations. Public awareness also represents a critical component of helminth control. Educational programs promoting hygiene measures, responsible pet ownership, regular veterinary care and appropriate deworming practices could substantially reduce transmission risks. In parallel, surveillance programs targeting both domestic and wild carnivores should be strengthened to monitor changes in parasite distribution and prevalence. Integrating these findings into public health policies would contribute to more effective prevention and control strategies for zoonotic helminth infections in Uzbekistan and neighbouring regions.

Conclusion

This review provides the first comprehensive and taxonomically updated synthesis of helminth parasites recorded in carnivorous mammals in Uzbekistan, documenting 74 species across the Canidae, Felidae and Mustelidae families. The findings highlight substantial progress in parasitological research over recent decades while also revealing important knowledge gaps regarding understudied host species, geographic coverage, parasite taxonomy and transmission ecology. A considerable proportion of the recorded helminths possess zoonotic relevance, emphasizing the importance of integrated surveillance at the wildlife–domestic animal–human interface. At the same time, helminths should be recognized not only as agents of disease but also as essential components of ecosystem biodiversity and functioning. Future investigations should prioritize geographically representative sampling, the inclusion of poorly studied carnivore taxa, and the integration of molecular and non‐invasive diagnostic approaches to improve taxonomic resolution and parasite detection. Strengthening multidisciplinary collaborations within a One Health framework will be critical for advancing our understanding of helminth diversity, assessing zoonotic risks, supporting wildlife conservation and improving public health preparedness in Uzbekistan and across Central Asia.
