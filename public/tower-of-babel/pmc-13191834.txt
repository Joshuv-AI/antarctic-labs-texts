A life course perspective on evolving hypertension and diabetes risk among older Indians
Authors: Aditi Chakraborty, Suryakant Yadav
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe growing burden of non-communicable diseases in ageing populations demands urgent public health attention. Hypertension and diabetes, as chronic conditions, frequently co-exist and pose serious public health challenges due to their contribution to cardiovascular morbidity, premature mortality and escalating healthcare costs.MethodsThis study uses Longitudinal Ageing Study in India (LASI, 2017-18), a nationally representative survey using a rigorous multi-stage sampling design across rural and urban areas. Of the 73,396 individuals surveyed, 66,606 were aged 45 years and above. After merging individual and biomarker datasets, the final analytical sample comprised 60,643 adults. The data are used to estimate age at onset of hypertension and diabetes, followed by age-specific patterns of reported age at diagnosis, prevalence, and associated risk factors among adults aged 45 years and above. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and modified Poisson regression were applied.ResultsThe probability of remaining undiagnosed declines with age, more rapidly for hypertension than diabetes, indicating earlier reported age at diagnosis and a greater cumulative burden to cardiovascular risk and underscoring the importance of early and sustained blood pressure screening and control. Urban residents experienced earlier reported age at diagnosis and a steeper decline in the undiagnosed proportion of both hypertension and diabetes, highlighting the impact of lifestyle transition and stronger preventive services in urban settings. Women were more vulnerable to hypertension, and men were slightly more susceptible to diabetes, supporting sex sensitive risk assessment and prevention. Hypertension prevalence increased steadily with age, while reported age at diagnosis was more frequent among the oldest, and diabetes prevalence peaked in the age-group 65–74, with the highest concentration of reported age at diagnosis in the early age-group 55–64, highlighting midlife as a critical window for metabolic screening to prevent long-term complications. Family history, obesity, and comorbidity emerged as strong predictors across all age groups. At the same time, urban residence, sex, wealth, caste, and behavioural factors showed age- and disease-specific effects, supporting targeted risk-based screening.ConclusionThe findings highlight the need for life course-oriented, socio-demographically responsive strategies, particularly in urban and other socio-economically vulnerable populations, to prevent and manage the rising clinical and public health burden of hypertension and diabetes in India’s ageing population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27284-6.

Introduction

Background

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) such as hypertension and diabetes are among the most pressing public health challenges of our time, contributing substantially to cardiovascular morbidity, premature mortality, and increasing healthcare expenditures worldwide [1–5]. With ageing populations, the prevalence and trend of these chronic conditions are on the rise, highlighting the need to understand the numerous factors that contribute to their development across different stages of life [6–8]. This study examines the epidemiology of hypertension and diabetes among older adults, situating the investigation within a life course framework to understand age-related variations in disease progression. Our research builds on a robust body of literature that documents the significant influence of socio-demographic factors, behavioural patterns, and biological markers on the incidence and prevalence of chronic diseases [9–11]. Moreover, the diverse historical, economic, and healthcare experiences of different age cohorts imply that unique life-long exposures and contextual influences may shape the risk profiles and disease trajectories of these groups [12].

Theoretical framework

Our investigation is grounded in the Natural History Model of Chronic Disease Progression, which posits that chronic conditions develop gradually along a continuum from early, often asymptomatic, biological changes to the eventual appearance of clinically evident disease [13, 14]. In this framework, individuals in the younger and middle age group, although they may not yet exhibit overt symptoms of hypertension and diabetes, are likely in the early, subclinical stages of these conditions. Over time, persistent exposure to adverse factors such as unhealthy diets, physical inactivity, and socioeconomic hardships may initiate subtle pathophysiological changes [15]. These changes, though initially undetectable, accumulate progressively and ultimately give rise to overt clinical disease as individuals age.

It is important to acknowledge that, while the Natural History Model provides the core basis for understanding disease progression, additional phenomena also shape the observed patterns. Selective survival or nature’s selection, for instance, implies that individuals who are most vulnerable to the adverse effects of risk factors may develop disease earlier or succumb to competing health issues, thereby altering the composition of older age cohorts [16, 17]. Similarly, competing risks, where the presence of other chronic conditions may delay or mask the diagnosis of a disease, can influence incidence patterns [18, 19]. Moreover, cohort effects remind us that each age group has been shaped by distinct historical, social, and healthcare contexts, which may modify the impact of long-term exposure [20].

Taken together, these considerations support the notion that individuals in the younger and middle-aged are on a trajectory toward developing hypertension and diabetes similar to that observed in older cohorts. Although they appear healthier at present, their risk factor profile suggests that, in the absence of intervention, their health outcomes will likely converge with those seen in later life [12]. This integrated theoretical perspective underscores the critical need for early and sustained preventive measures to interrupt the progression from subclinical changes to overt disease, ultimately reducing the long-term burden of these chronic conditions.

Rationale and objectives of the study

The escalating burden of non-communicable diseases, such as hypertension and diabetes, is a critical public health challenge worldwide [21–24]. It is not only essential to assess the current prevalence of these conditions but also to understand the trajectories that lead to their clinical appearance over time [12]. Although our study is cross-sectional, it uses age-specific patterns of reported age at diagnosis to approximate the timing of disease recognition across age groups and to offer insights into potential longitudinal trends. While studies have documented risk factors for hypertension and diabetes independently, few nationally representative analyses have compared their age-at-onset patterns within a n unified life-course framework in India. By jointly examining timing patterns, comorbidity interplay, and age-specific risk structures, this study provides evidence on when and for whom interventions may yield maximum benefit. We posit that individual in their early years of life, despite showing few evident symptoms, may already be undergoing early, subclinical changes that influence them to develop hypertension and diabetes as they age. Recognising these early changes is crucial, as it provides an opportunity to intervene before the full burden of disease becomes apparent.

In this context, our study is driven by three interrelated objectives that collectively emphasise the early origins of hypertension and diabetes and the timely need for intervention. First, we aim to characterise the patterns of disease diagnosis and progression by utilising Kaplan-Meier curves based on self-reported age at diagnosis to examine age-specific patterns and the distribution of disease recognition following the initial emergence of these conditions. This approach is intended to reveal the latent, subclinical period during which early physiological changes occur. Second, we will outline the age-specific reported age at diagnosis and prevalence of hypertension and diabetes among individuals aged 45 years and above, categorising the sample into four distinct age groups (45–54, 55–64, 65–74, and 75+). This objective is designed to quantify the current burden of these chronic conditions and to identify any age-related patterns in their distribution. Third, we seek to identify and quantify the associations between key socio-demographic, behavioural, and biological risk factors and the reported age at diagnosis of hypertension and diabetes across these age groups. By doing so, we aim to determine the extent to which factors such as socioeconomic status, lifestyle behaviours, and biological and familial predispositions contribute to the age-specific diagnosis rates of these diseases at different age points.

Finally, by employing the Natural History Model of Chronic Disease Progression as our interpretative framework, we seek to contextualise how early exposures observed in midlife predict future health outcomes. Collectively, these objectives aim to provide a comprehensive understanding of the current burden and trajectory of hypertension and diabetes. The findings from our study aim to provide policymakers and healthcare providers with actionable insights, highlighting critical intervention points that could mitigate the long-term societal and economic impacts of these chronic diseases. Through this integrated approach, we hope to advance the evidence base for early, sustained public health interventions that alter the natural progression of disease before it culminates in overt clinical manifestations.

Data and methods

Data source

This study uses wave I data from the Longitudinal Ageing Study in India (LASI), a survey conducted by the International Institute for Population Sciences (IIPS) and the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health that collects information on ageing, health, and well-being among older Indians. LASI employs a multistage stratified area probability cluster sampling design, targeting individuals aged 45 and above, along with their spouses, irrespective of age. The data are gathered via face-to-face interviews, physical measurements, and biomarker assessments, covering demographics, chronic diseases, cognitive and mental health, social support, healthcare utilisation, and socio-economic factors.

Sample description

This study draws on LASI data collected using a rigorous multi-stage sampling design across rural and urban settings in India. Initially, 73,396 participants were surveyed across 36 states and union territories, and 66,606 individuals aged 45 years and above were identified. As the study is conducted on both individual and biomarker characteristics, the biomarker file has to be merged with the individual file. After merging, a final analytic sample of 60,643 observations is obtained, providing a robust foundation for our analysis.

Review of LASI instruments

Operationalisation of dependent variables

The LASI survey collects a wide range of information on various chronic and non-communicable diseases from individuals aged 45 years and above, along with their spouses, irrespective of age. The survey collected information on chronic diseases through the question: “Has any health professional ever diagnosed you with the following chronic conditions or diseases? (yes/no)”. These questions are listed in the section HT002-HT010 of its questionnaire. Specific questions on Diabetes and Hypertension are collected under questions HT002 and HT003, which include the following sub-questions-.

HT002a. Who first diagnosed you with high blood pressure or hypertension? (1. A doctor (MBBS degree, 2. Ayurvedic/Unani/Homoeopathic/Siddha, 3. Others).

HT002b. When were you first diagnosed with high blood pressure or hypertension? (Year OR Age)

HT002c. To control your blood pressure or hypertension, are you currently taking any medication? (Yes/No)

HT002d. In order to control your blood pressure, are you under salt or other diet restrictions? (Yes/ No)

HT003a. Who first diagnosed you with diabetes or high blood sugar? (1. A doctor (MBBS degree), 2. Ayurvedic/Unani/Homeopathic/Siddha, 3. Others)

HT003b. When were you first diagnosed with diabetes or high blood sugar? (Year OR Age)

HT003c. In order to treat or control your diabetes or high blood sugar, are you currently taking medicine that you swallow? (Yes/No)

HT003d. Are you currently using insulin shots/injections? (Yes/No)

HT003e. In order to control your diabetes, are you following a special diet? (Yes/No)

Based on the responses to the questions HT002 and HT002a, we constructed the dependent variable “hypertension”. Similarly, responses from the questions HT003 and HT003a were used to formulate the dependent variable “Diabetes”. Further, responses to questions HT002b and HT003b are used to create a variable representing the onset of diseases (Hypertension/Diabetes).

Operationalisation of independent variables

The survey also collects comprehensive information on various dimensions that may influence the health of the older population, including socio-economic, metabolic, biological, familial, and behavioural aspects. The socio-economic aspects are derived from a series of items that ask respondents to report their place of residence, religion, and caste or tribal affiliation. Households’ economic status is determined by calculating the monthly per capita consumption expenditure based on the total food and non-food consumption and household size. Marital status is assessed by asking about their current status. At the same time, employment history is documented by asking respondents whether they have ever worked for at least 3 months, their current employment status, and the reason for ceasing work. Additionally, living arrangements are evaluated by collecting information on household members and their relationships to the household head.

Biological and familial factors encompass both the individual’s sex and their family history of disease. The sex of each respondent is recorded either by direct observation or by asking them to specify whether they are male or female. In addition, family history is assessed by inquiring about the medical histories of immediate family members. Respondents are asked whether their father, mother, siblings, children or grandchildren have ever been diagnosed with hypertension or diabetes. Further, metabolic risk factors include body mass index (BMI) and the presence of comorbid diabetes or hypertension, which are used to assess the risk of developing disease conditions. BMI is calculated from the individual’s height and weight measurements using the WHO formula (weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters). The information on diabetes and hypertension is already provided in the independent variable section.

Behavioural aspects are derived from self-reported information on alcohol and tobacco consumption, physical activity, and sleep quality. Respondents provide details on their consumption habits by answering questions such as whether they have ever consumed alcohol or used tobacco, the age at which they first did so, and the frequency and type of consumption over the past three months. In addition, physical activity is assessed through inquiries about participation in vigorous and moderate activities, including sports, as well as yoga, meditation, asana, and pranayama, for which respondents specify both frequency and duration. Moreover, sleep problems are evaluated by capturing information on difficulties falling asleep, waking during the night, early morning awakenings, the extent of unrest during the day, and the frequency of daytime naps. Together, these items offer a comprehensive measure of socio-economic, biological, familial, metabolic, and behavioural risk factors that may influence the onset and progression of chronic diseases.

Data analysis procedure

Survival analysis

Kaplan-Meier curves were constructed using self-reported age at diagnosis to characterise age-specific patterns of hypertension and diabetes. This approach estimates the distribution of reported age at diagnosis, specifically the proportion of individuals who remain undiagnosed up to a given age, while effectively handling the censored data from individuals who did not report a diagnosis at the time of the survey. The Kaplan Meier method generates curves that visually represent the cumulative pattern of disease recognition across successive ages. Widely used in epidemiological research to describe time-related patterns, this approach allows the reconstruction of age-at-diagnosis distribution from retrospective data. In doing so, it provides insights into the timing of disease recognition and the transition from an undiagnosed to a diagnosed state across the life course. This reflects the period during which early exposures may contribute to the subsequent identification of hypertension and diabetes in later life.

Modified Poisson regression models were employed to estimate risk ratios, which is interpreted as prevalence ratios for hypertension and diabetes across the entire study population in relation to various socio-economic, biological and familial, metabolic, and behavioural risk factors. Additionally, separate Modified Poisson models were conducted to calculate risk ratios for reported diagnosis timing for these factors within different age-groups. This dual approach enables us to identify the factors that are associated with a higher overall burden (prevalence) of these diseases, as well as those linked to an increased risk of age of diagnosis of diseases at different age groups.

However, the critical distinction between the two regression models lies in the estimation of the standard errors. Although Poisson regression is conventionally applied to count data, the modified approach has been increasingly adopted to obtain direct estimate of risk ratios adjusting the standard errors, ensuring they are not underestimated or overestimated when the outcome variable is binary. In this study, we adopted the modified Poisson regression, as described by [25], to obtain consistent and valid estimates of prevalence and diagnosis ratios. This approach adjusts for the potential over or underestimation of standard errors that may occur when applying traditional Poisson model to binary data.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis is being conducted to ensure the robustness of the regression results by comparing the estimates from survey adjusted Poisson regression models (using svy prefix with linearized standard errors) against those derived from Modified Poisson model using robust (sandwiched) variance estimators without the svy prefix. The findings revealed that survey-adjusted Poisson models produced larger standard errors and wider confidence intervals reducing the significance for key predictors. Due to this inflated standard errors and wider confidence interval, the primary analysis relies on the modified Poisson approach with robust error variance, as recommended by [25].

To further validate the regression models, multicollinearity among the independent variables was assessed using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). All variables had VIF values less than 5 which in acceptable range, except for living arrangement, which showed VIF exceeding 10. To examine its influence, the models were re-estimated with and without this variable. The results remain substantively unchanged in both the models. Given the variable’s conceptual relevance as a predictor in the context of chronic disease risk, it was retained in the final model [26–29].

Additionally, missing data on key covariates such as working status, alcohol and tobacco consumption, body mass index, family history of hypertension and sleep problem were addressed using multiple imputation via chained equations, thereby preserving sample size and minimizing potential bias due to missing observations. This comprehensive result obtained from imputed datasets and estimated strategies strengthen our conclusion regarding the risk factors for hypertension and diabetes.

Results

Survival curves for hypertension and diabetes

The survival curves of diabetes and hypertension presented in Fig. 1 shows a decline in the cumulative probability of remaining free from diabetes and hypertension with age. The decline begins earlier and is steeper for hypertension in comparison to diabetes, indicating an earlier onset and lower probability of remaining free from hypertension with age. By the end of the observation period the probability of remaining free from hypertension drops to less than 0.3(30%), whereas for diabetes it remains around 0.65(65%) indicating a higher burden of hypertension in the adult life course.

Further, Fig. 2 depicts survival curve for hypertension and diabetes stratified by sex and sector. For hypertension, urban female exhibited the earliest onset at around age 30 and steepest decline with survival probability reaching 20% at the end of the survey. Urban male followed closely, though their decline began slightly later at around age 40, reaching a survival probability of around 30% at the end of the survey. In comparison, rural males and females demonstrated a delayed onset and slower progression with females being more vulnerable than male. In the case of diabetes, urban residents again exhibited earlier onset and more pronounced decline in the survival curves compared to their rural counterparts. However, gender differences were minimal, although men showed a slightly steeper decline survival probability in urban areas after age 70. These findings clearly identify urban rural disparities, with urban residents being more vulnerable to both the diseases. Additionally, the result highlights a greater susceptibility to hypertension among females, whereas males exhibit slightly higher vulnerability to diabetes.

Age-specific reported age at diagnosis rate and age- specific prevalence of diabetes across four broad age-groups

For hypertension, prevalence rises steadily with age, from 19.08% in the 45–54 age group to 34.01% among those aged 75 years and above. The proportion of diagnoses also shows an increase, peaking from 10.99% in older adulthood to 14.7% in the oldest age group after remaining relatively stable in middle age (Table 1). Urban residents have consistently shown both higher prevalence and diagnosis rate for hypertension compared to the rural residents, with prevalence rising from 24.26% (45–54 years) to 49.29% (65–74 years) in comparison to 16.62% in the age-group 45–54 to 28.41% in the age-group 75 years and above among rural residents. Diagnosis rate, on the other hand, peaked in the 55–64 age group (17.18%) in the urban area and in the 75 + age group (14.08%) in the rural area. Muslims, Christians, and individuals categorised as “Others” have a higher burden and onset of hypertension compared to Hindus across all age groups, except for Christians, who have a lower diagnosed rate and prevalence rate in the early years. Schedule tribes remain the least affected across all age groups. In contrast, individuals with no caste/tribe affiliation have the highest diagnosed rate and prevalence of hypertension, and these rates rise consistently from 23.48% (prevalence) and 13.44% (diagnosed rate) in the early years to 40.89% (prevalence) and 16.06% (diagnosed rate) in the later years. Hypertension prevalence and diagnosed rate also increase consistently across economic quintiles, peaking at 43.32% among the richest elderly. Those currently unmarried have consistently higher rates than those married. Also, those who are currently working in all age groups have a lower diagnosis rate and prevalence of hypertension in comparison to those who either never worked or worked in the past and are currently not working. Individuals who are living alone have a higher prevalence and diagnosed rate of hypertension in the early years (45–54) and later years (75years and above) in comparison to those living with a spouse or others. Still, the difference is very small in the Middle Ages. Women are notably more affected than men across all ages, with prevalence and diagnosis rates rising from 22.22% to 12.37% in younger women to 39.54% and 17.46% in older women, compared with 28.2% and 11.81% in older men. Individuals having a family history of hypertension consistently exhibit higher prevalence and diagnosed rate of hypertension across all age groups. A clear gradient is observed with BMI; obese individuals have the highest prevalence, constantly rising from 36.75% in the 45–54 age group to 73.65% in the 75 and above age group, while the diagnosed rate among obese people is higher in the early years (45–64 years). In comparison, overweight individuals have a higher diagnosed rate in the later years. People with diabetes also report substantially higher hypertension rates than non-diabetics, with prevalence exceeding 70% by the oldest age groups. Inactive individuals and those reporting sleep problems show consistently higher prevalence and diagnosis across all age groups.

The prevalence of diabetes increases from 8.02% in the 45–54 age group to a peak of 16.05% in the 65–74 age group, before declining to 11.38% among those aged 75 years and above. Diagnosed rate follows a similar trend, rising from 4.89% in the youngest group to a peak of 6.13% in the 55–64 age group, then gradually declining in older ages (Table 2). Urban population exhibits substantially higher prevalence and diagnosed rates than their rural counterparts throughout all the age groups, with the prevalence peaking at 30.30% in urban individuals age 65–74 years and diagnosis peaking at 9.58% in the age-group 55–64 years, compared to prevalence of 9.91% and diagnosis rate of 4.73% in rural residents in the same age-group. Among religious groups, Christians record a higher prevalence, especially in the later years, with prevalence reaching 22.36% by age 75 years and above. Socially disadvantaged groups such as STs and SCs report lower prevalence and diagnosis across all age groups. Diabetes burden increases progressively across the economic ladder, with prevalence peaking at 25.16% among the richest individuals aged 65–75 years and diagnosis peaking at 9.19% among the richest individuals aged 55–65 years. Unlike hypertension, currently married individuals show marginally higher diabetes rates across most age groups. However, consistent with hypertension, individuals who are currently working report a lower diagnosis rate and prevalence than those who have never worked or have stopped working. Women show slightly lower diabetes rates than men across most age groups, in contrast to hypertension. Individuals with a family history of diabetes experience considerably higher diagnosis rates and prevalence across all age groups. A strong positive gradient is again evident with BMI, where obese individuals show peak prevalence of 40.42% in the age-group 65–74 years and peak diagnosis rate of 12.57% in the age-group 55–64 years, compared to prevalence of 4.06% and 13.66% and diagnosis rate of 2.03% and 4.90% among the underweight and normal weight individuals. Individuals with co-existing hypertension report significantly elevated diabetes rates across all age groups. Unlike results for hypertension, behavioural factors do not show a significant difference in diabetic rates across all age groups.

In conclusion, while the prevalence of hypertension increases with age, its diagnosis pattern displays a fluctuating pattern. The diagnosed rate of hypertension rises in the age group 55–64 years, slightly tapering off in the next age group (65–74), then increasing again in the oldest group (75 + years). In contrast, diabetes prevalence generally increases with age but declines slightly in the oldest age group. At the same time, the diagnosed rate rises in the age group (55–64) and then tapers off in the older ages. These distinct trajectories reflect differences in the timing and accumulation of risk for the two diseases across the life course. Additionally, while traditional behavioural risk factors such as tobacco and alcohol use were expected to elevate risk, the observed patterns show counterintuitive results, likely due to underreporting or survival bias and reverse causation, warranting cautious interpretations.

Discussion

Summary of results

The study examined the cumulative probability of remaining free of hypertension and diabetes using a Kaplan-Meier survival curve. The results highlight that the probability of remaining free from hypertension declines more rapidly with advancing age than for diabetes, with fewer than 30 per cent of adults remaining hypertension-free by the end of the observed period versus approximately 65% for diabetes. Further, the stratified Kaplan-Meier curves show earlier onset and a steeper decline among urban women and men than among their rural counterparts. The age-specific estimates showed that hypertension prevalence rose steadily from 19.8% in the age group 45–54 to 34.01% at age group 75 years and above, with diagnosis peaking at 14.70% in the oldest cohort. However, diabetes prevalence increased from 8.02% at ages 45–54 to 16.05% at 65–74 before declining by age 75 years and above, and the diagnosed rate peaked at 6.13% in those aged 55–64. Modified Poisson models revealed family history as the strongest predictor for both diseases. Along with this, obesity and overweight also conferred substantially elevated risk that peaked in the early years and remained significant in the later years. Hypertension as a comorbidity nearly doubled the diabetes risk, and diabetes, being a comorbid condition, similarly increased hypertension risk, the effect of which diminishes over the life stages for hypertension diagnosis and remains consistent for diabetes diagnosis. Urban residence was associated with a higher prevalence of both diseases. For the diagnosed rate, urban residence was not significantly associated with hypertension risk at ages 45–54 but became significantly protective from age 55 onwards, whereas for diabetes, urban residence increased risk through ages 45–74 before losing significance after 75. Higher wealth quintiles predict increased hypertension risk across all ages, whereas, in contrast, for diabetes, the influence of wealth was significant in younger ages and attenuated and became insignificant in the later years. Past but not currently employed predict a higher rate of diagnosed hypertension in older adults and mid-old group, but not among the oldest groups, and were linked to increased diabetes risk in the age group 75 years and above. OBC and other social group affiliations raised the rate of hypertension diagnosis from 45 years to 75 years, while it does not exhibit much significance with the rate of diabetes diagnosis. Physical inactivity becomes an increasingly strong predictor of hypertension, and sleep problems predict hypertension only until age 65, whereas neither factor achieves significance for diabetes. Female sex strongly predicts hypertension until age 55 and protects against diabetes until age 75, with neither effect significant thereafter. Unmarried status raises hypertension risk, while remaining non-significant for diabetes across all ages. Other factors such as religious affiliation, living arrangement and alcohol use remain insignificant across the life stages for both diseases.

Discussion of results

The study aimed to understand the life-course dynamics of hypertension and diabetes and to contextualise how early exposures observed in mid-life serve as predictors of future health outcomes, employing the Natural History Model of Chronic Disease Progression as our interpretative framework [13, 14].

The Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that hypertension-free survival declines more sharply and earlier than diabetes free survival, with fewer than 30% of adults remaining hypertension-free and roughly 65% being diabetes free at the end of the follow-up. This pattern aligns with the Natural History Model’s subclinical phase, indicating that vascular stress may lead to hypertension before metabolic irregularities lead to diabetes and suggests a primordial prevention for hypertension [30, 31]. Stratified curves further highlighted urban-rural and gender disparities: urban women showed the earliest onset and the steepest survival decline, followed by urban men, whereas rural residents showed delayed progression and higher survival probabilities, which may reflect differential exposure to lifestyle risks and the selective survival of healthier individuals in rural settings [16, 17, 32, 33].

Age-stratified prevalence and diagnosed rate of diabetes and hypertension disclose a unique life course trajectory with a steady increase in hypertension burden in the late age, versus a midlife peak and late life decline for diabetes. The hypertension pattern aligns with the cumulative burden theory, in which risk factors accumulate over time, increasing burden at older ages [34–36]. In contrast, the diabetes trajectories reflect competing risk, in which mortality from other fatal causes reduces the “at-risk” population for the onset of diabetes at a later age, and cohort effects, since older cohorts experienced different nutritional, environmental, and medical conditions than the present-day older adult population [18, 37, 38].

The results from Modified Poisson regression models indicate that a family history of hypertension or diabetes is the strongest predictor of developing this condition in the early years, with its effect lessening in later years but remaining significant. This occurs due to biological and familial predisposition and shared environments within families [39, 40]. Overweight and obesity conferred the highest risk in midlife, nearly tripling diabetes risk and more than doubling hypertension risk. In midlife, this stems from peak visceral fat accumulation driving insulin resistance, increased sodium retention, blood volume expansion, endothelial dysfunction and inflammation, causing high blood pressure, whereas in older ages, shifts in fat distribution, age -related lean-mass loss, and selective survival of healthier individual weakens the observed association [17, 41–45]. Bidirectional comorbidity effects with diabetes doubling hypertension risk, and vice versa, point to shared pathophysiological pathways and the value of integrated cardiometabolic screening [46–48]—socio-economic and behavioural influences vary by disease and age. Urban residence raised overall prevalence [49, 50], but became protective against hypertension diagnosis after mid-life (55 years), possibly reflecting greater health awareness and timely lifestyle modification among older urbanites [51]. Wealth gradients in hypertension persisted across all age bands, whereas for diabetes they were confined to early seniors (45-65years) [52]. Past but not current employment status and unmarried status elevated hypertension risk at younger age groups, suggesting the impact of psychosocial stressors and loss of daily structure [53–55]. Behavioural factors also predicted hypertension, the effect of physical inactivity growing with age, likely due to cumulative weight gain. At the same time, the severity of sleep problems attenuated, possibly reflecting adaptive coping over time [56–58]. Tobacco also becomes a significant predictor of hypertension after age 65, highlighting the cumulative harm of long-term nicotine exposure [59, 60].

Taking into account that each birth cohort experiences different nutritional, environmental, and medical exposures and that those most vulnerable may die earlier, our findings highlight the need for age-tailored prevention [17, 20]. Early identification of high-risk individuals based on family history, adiposity, and socio-economic status and doing the necessary interventions could interrupt the progression from silent pathology to overt diseases, ultimately reducing the long-term burden of hypertension and diabetes [12].

Limitation of the study

This study has several limitations. First, it relies entirely on participants’ recall of doctor-diagnosed hypertension and diabetes, introducing the potential of recall error and reporting bias. Second, using a single cross-sectional round excludes causal inference, raises the possibility of reverse causality, and cannot capture true time-to-event dynamics. Third, in the absence of biomarker measures (e.g., fasting glucose or HbA1c) for diabetes in LASI, and to maintain consistency across both diseases, we based all analyses on self-reported information, which is likely to underestimate the true prevalence and diagnosis of the diseases by missing undiagnosed or subclinical cases. Fourth, Regional variation in healthcare access and diagnostic coverage may influence reported age at diagnosis. States with stronger health systems may show earlier detection rather than higher true disease occurrence. Finally, despite adjusting for a broad range of covariates, there may still be unmeasured influences from factors such as dietary patterns, stress levels, environmental exposures and access to health care facilities, which could influence both risk factors and disease outcome.

Conclusion

The study confirms that hypertension and diabetes follow distinct yet overlapping life course paths among Indian older adults and elderly aged 45 years and above, with hypertension emerging earlier and rapidly accumulating with age in comparison to diabetes, as evidenced by steeper and earlier declines in hypertension-free life. Age-specific prevalence and diagnosis patterns further reveal that hypertension burden rises steadily with advanced age, whereas diabetes peaks in the mid-60s before tapering off, reflecting both cumulative vascular damage and competing mortality risks. Key drivers for both diseases include family history of disease, excess body weight, and the presence of one condition that increases the risk of the other, highlighting shared biological pathways. Socio-economic and behavioural factors, such as urban living, higher income, past work status, physical inactivity, sleep disturbances, and marital status, exert age- and disease-specific influences. Interpreted through the Natural History Model of Chronic Disease Progression, this finding demonstrates how subclinical exposures in midlife translate into overt disease and how cohort-specific backgrounds and selective survival shape late-life risk. To alter these trajectories, public health strategies should focus on early identification of high-risk individuals, weight management, integrated screening for cardiometabolic conditions, and age-tailored interventions that address social and lifestyle vulnerabilities, thereby delaying or preventing the onset of hypertension and diabetes and reducing their long-term burden.
