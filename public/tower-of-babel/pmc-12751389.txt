Laparoscopic transabdominal preperitoneal (TAPP) repair for an irreducible indirect inguinal hernia containing left ovary, dysplastic fallopian tube, and partial rudimentary uterine horn: a case report
Authors: Lang Wang, Fu Zeng, Xianglin Zhu, Quanhui Li, Yinlu Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndirect inguinal hernia is rare in adult females and is usually attributable to congenital anatomical anomalies. It poses a high risk of incarceration and strangulation, risking ovarian ischemia, necrosis, or compromised fertility. Early diagnosis and timely intervention are therefore essential.Case presentationA 39-year-old woman presented with a left inguinal mass of 20 years’ duration, recently enlarging with intermittent discomfort. Physical examination revealed a firm, non-reducible left inguinal mass with a positive cough impulse. Radiological examination (ultrasonography and computed tomography) demonstrated probable left ovarian herniation, with the left adnexa absent from its anatomical location. The patient underwent a one-stage laparoscopic transabdominal preperitoneal (TAPP) mesh hernia repair, resection of the rudimentary uterine horn, excision of the dysplastic fallopian tube, and oophoropexy, with an uneventful postoperative recovery.Conclusions This rare case highlights the diagnostic limitations of routine examinations in detecting female inguinal hernias associated with occult reproductive tract anomalies. Background

Inguinal hernias represent the most prevalent form of ventral hernia, accounting for approximately 70–75% of all abdominal wall herniation cases [1]. This condition is characterized by the protrusion of intraperitoneal viscera through inguinal anatomical defects toward the body surface. Based on the hernial sac’s pathway of protrusion, inguinal hernias are classified into indirect and direct subtypes [1].

Due to the high mobility of the greater omentum and small bowel, these structures most frequently constitute the hernial contents; occasionally, the sigmoid colon, cecum, appendix, ovary, and fallopian tubes may also herniate [2]. Inguinal hernia incidence demonstrates marked gender disparity [3], with lifetime prevalence rates of approximately 27–43% in males compared to only 3–6% in females [4–8]. Nevertheless, female patients comprise roughly 10% of all inguinal hernia cases [9]– [10].

In adult females, inguinal herniation involving the ovary (with or without fallopian tube) represents a rare clinical entity, accounting for less than 3% of all inguinal hernia cases [11], with occurrence predominantly associated with congenital anatomical abnormalities [12]– [13]. The clinical presentation typically manifests as reducible or irreducible inguinal masses, potentially accompanied by localized tenderness [14]. Ultrasonography serves as the primary imaging modality for such cases, demonstrating high sensitivity and specificity in identifying ovarian tissue within the hernial sac, while Doppler assessment of vascular flow patterns proves crucial for excluding ovarian torsion [15]. When further anatomical delineation is required or evaluation for concurrent congenital anomalies is indicated, computed tomography(CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provide essential complementary diagnostic information, offering precise visualization of hernial contents and their anatomical relationships with surrounding structures, thereby facilitating optimal surgical planning [16]– [17].

Given the propensity of ovarian hernias to develop incarceration, strangulation, and subsequent ischemic necrosis, prompt diagnosis and emergent evaluation are paramount, as this encompasses not only the prevention of acute complications but also directly impacts the preservation of reproductive function [18]– [19]. Surgical intervention remains the primary therapeutic modality, with early operative management being crucial for safeguarding fertility potential and preventing severe complications such as ovarian necrosis [14].

Given the rarity of ovarian inguinal hernias in adult women and the associated diagnostic and surgical challenges, this report presents a case of left-sided irreducible indirect inguinal hernia successfully treated via laparoscopic transabdominal preperitoneal repair (TAPP), with hernial contents comprising the ovary, hypoplastic fallopian tube, and partial rudimentary uterine horn. By synthesizing the clinical management experience of this case, we aim to provide clinical guidance for such rare and complex pathological conditions. This case report was conducted and reported in accordance with the criteria outlined in the Consensus Surgical Case Report (SCARE) guidelines [20].

Case presentation

A 39-year-old woman presented with a left inguinal mass of 20 years’ duration, recently enlarging with intermittent discomfort. The patient reports that the mass initially measured approximately the size of a date, demonstrating enlargement during ambulation and standing, with partial reduction when supine, though never achieving complete resolution. No prior discomfort was experienced, and no treatment was pursued. During cesarean Sect. 17 years prior, “absent left adnexa” was documented (confirmed via surgical record review). Over the past year, the patient perceived slight enlargement of the mass, experiencing local impact sensation during coughing or straining, accompanied by intermittent mild pain. No fever, nausea, vomiting, or other associated symptoms were present, prompting the current clinical consultation.

Past medical history: Previous two cesarean deliveries (17 years and 9 years prior, respectively). No chronic factors associated with increased intra-abdominal pressure, including chronic constipation, recurrent cough, urinary retention, or heavy manual labor. Obstetric and menstrual history: Gravida 2, para 2 (one son, one daughter), regular menstrual cycles without dysmenorrhea. Physical examination: In standing position, a palpable mass measuring approximately 3.0 cm × 3.0 cm × 2.0 cm was observed in the left inguinal region, firm in consistency, well-demarcated borders, mild tenderness, limited mobility, positive cough impulse, and irreducible when supine. Right inguinal region demonstrated no abnormalities. Abdomen was soft and non-tender without rebound tenderness or guarding, with normal bowel sounds.

Bilateral inguinal ultrasonography revealed poor visualization of the left ovary and a heterogeneous echogenic mass measuring approximately 3.2 × 3.2 × 1.7 cm in the left inguinal region. The mass contained multiple cystic components, with the largest measuring 1.2 × 0.8 cm, and demonstrated communication with the abdominal cavity through a hypoechoic tract approximately 0.4 cm in width. Color Doppler flow imaging demonstrated punctate and linear vascular signals within the mass (Fig. 1A, B), suggesting possible ovarian hernia. The right inguinal region appeared normal. Subsequent transabdominal and transvaginal gynecologic ultrasonography showed an anteverted uterus measuring 5.6 × 4.5 × 3.8 cm with regular morphology, homogeneous myometrial echogenicity, and endometrial thickness of 0.9 cm. The left ovary remained poorly visualized (Fig. 1C, D). To characterize the herniated contents and delineate surrounding anatomical relationships, contrast-enhanced pelvic CT was performed based on clinical requirements and economic considerations. CT imaging demonstrated widening of the left inguinal canal with herniation of intra-abdominal soft tissue structures. Post-contrast enhancement revealed vascular structures and enhancing soft tissue measuring approximately 3.2 cm, with indistinct left adnexal visualization, consistent with inguinal ovarian hernia (Fig. 1E, F). Following gynecological consultation, specialized gynecological examination was performed: external genitalia appeared normal, vaginal canal was patent, uterus was anteverted and laterally displaced to the right without tenderness, right adnexa was palpable and unremarkable, left adnexa were not clearly palpable; secondary sexual characteristics demonstrated normal development. The gynecological assessment suggests a high probability of left-sided irreducible indirect inguinal hernia (the hernia contains ovary), with no current evidence of ovarian torsion or necrosis. The department concurs with our proposed surgical intervention and recommends concurrent intraoperative exploration; if adnexal herniation is confirmed, simultaneous ligamentous shortening procedure may be performed to reduce postoperative torsion risk, contingent upon intraoperative findings.

Based on the comprehensive evaluation of the aforementioned clinical evidence, the preliminary diagnosis indicates a left-sided irreducible indirect inguinal hernia containing ovarian tissue. Given that the patient has no absolute surgical contraindications, and that we have thoroughly informed the patient and her family about all possible intraoperative scenarios and corresponding management plans (including, if necessary, oophorectomy, salpingectomy, or partial hysterectomy), the patient and her family have provided informed consent with full understanding and have signed all relevant surgical consent forms. Therefore, laparoscopic exploration and repair of the left-sided incarcerated indirect inguinal hernia is planned. Intraoperative assessment of the adnexal structures and reduction of ovarian tissue will be performed concurrently to prevent potential incarceration or torsion complications.

Intraoperative and postoperative conditions

Following general anesthesia administration, the patient was positioned in reverse Trendelenburg position (head elevated 10°−15°), with bilateral upper extremities placed alongside the torso. The primary surgeon positioned on the patient’s right side, with the assistant at the cephalic end, and the laparoscopic monitor positioned at the foot of the bed facing the operator. A Veress needle was inserted 1 cm superior to the umbilicus to establish pneumoperitoneum maintained at 12 mmHg. Subsequently, a 10 mm trocar was introduced as the camera port, and a 30° laparoscope was inserted for preliminary exploration. Under direct laparoscopic visualization, 5-mm trocars were placed bilaterally at the lateral borders of the rectus abdominis muscles at the umbilical level as surgical ports.

Intraperitoneal examination revealed a 2 cm peritoneal defect lateral to the inferior epigastric artery in the left inguinal region. The uterus was displaced to the right, with normal right adnexal structures and absence of left adnexal structures in their anatomical position. A elongated cord-like structure was palpated in the preperitoneal pelvic region, extending from the left uterine cornu to the aforementioned inguinal defect. The comprehensive diagnosis was left-sided irreducible indirect inguinal hernia with probable left adnexal contents. No right-sided inguinal defect was identified, and external palpation revealed no occult hernia. The decision was made to perform laparoscopic TAPP repair of the left irreducible indirect inguinal hernia. Due to concurrent reproductive system anatomical anomalies, intraoperative gynecological consultation was requested for collaborative management.

Following peritoneal incision, the retropubic space of Retzius and lateral space of Bogros were dissected lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels, with complete mobilization of the hernia sac and release of the internal inguinal ring. With external assistance, the hernial contents were successfully reduced and identified upon exploration as a morphologically normal left ovary, a rudimentary fallopian tube, and a cord-like structure connected to the uterine horn. Gynecologic consultation confirmed the diagnosis of left irreducible indirect inguinal hernia containing the left ovary, rudimentary fallopian tube, and portion of rudimentary uterine horn, with no evidence of torsion, ischemia, or necrosis. Given the absence of physiological function in the rudimentary fallopian tube and uterine horn, and the potential increased risk of future adnexal torsion if retained, following comprehensive discussion with the patient’s family and obtaining informed consent, the decision was made to proceed with TAPP repair of the left irreducible indirect inguinal hernia with concurrent laparoscopic excision of the left rudimentary uterine horn, rudimentary fallopian tube removal, and oophoropexy.

The surgical procedure continued with complete mobilization and dissection of the hernia sac, followed by ligation and division of the round ligament of the uterus. Further dissection was performed to develop the space of Retzius extending to the pectineal ligament, with expansion of the space of Bogros to the anterior superior iliac spine, creating adequate space for mesh placement. A 10 cm × 15 cm mesh was trimmed and positioned in the preperitoneal space, covering the myopectineal orifice region. The peritoneum was closed using continuous 3 − 0 absorbable barbed suture. The gynecologic specialist subsequently mobilized and completely excised the left rudimentary uterine horn and hypoplastic fallopian tube, with the specimen placed in an extraction bag for histopathological examination. The peritoneum was again closed with 3 − 0 absorbable barbed suture, and the left ovary was fixed to the lateral pelvic sidewall peritoneum. Following meticulous hemostasis of the operative field, a pelvic drain was inserted through the left 5 mm trocar site (Fig. 2 and Video S1). The operation was completed successfully with a total operative time of 86 min and estimated blood loss of approximately 5 mL.

Discussion

Female reproductive system structures (such as ovaries and fallopian tubes) as inguinal hernia contents represent rare clinical entities, with most documented cases occurring in pediatric patients with histories of prematurity or congenital anomalies (frequently accompanied by reproductive tract malformations) [21]– [22]. Adult female cases are uncommon, with etiology primarily attributed to embryological developmental aberrations, including incomplete obliteration of the Canal of Nuck and Müllerian duct developmental disorders (such as Mayer-Rokitansky-Küster-Hauser syndrome) [23]– [24], potentially presenting with concomitant genitourinary and skeletal system malformations [13, 24]. Despite their rarity, these hernias necessitate urgent assessment and intervention due to the risk of incarceration or strangulation, which may result in ovarian ischemic necrosis or infertility [25]. Clinical manifestations are typically nonspecific, characterized by post-exertional dull aching or dragging sensation in the inguinal region, with physical examination revealing tender palpable masses [26]– [27]. Differential diagnosis must encompass common inguinal hernias, lymphadenopathy, and lipomas [27]– [28].

Ultrasonography serves as the primary imaging modality, playing a pivotal role in real-time assessment of adnexal vascularity and exclusion of torsion or strangulation [29]. CT and MRI provide precise characterization of hernial contents and their anatomical relationships with surrounding structures, particularly facilitating identification of concurrent uterine anomalies; MRI demonstrates unique diagnostic value in delineating myometrial, endometrial, and ovarian architecture, offering significant complementary diagnostic information for complex cases [30]– [31].

Surgical intervention represents the gold standard for treating inguinal hernias, aimed at preventing serious complications including torsion, strangulation, and subsequent ovarian ischemic necrosis and secondary infertility [14]. The selection of surgical technique adheres to principles consistent with conventional inguinal hernia repair, requiring comprehensive evaluation of patient age, reproductive requirements, comorbidities, hernia contents, defect dimensions, and surgeon expertise [1]. Available techniques encompass: (1) open procedures (including Bassini and Lichtenstein repairs) [13, 32]; (2) minimally invasive laparoscopic approaches (such as TAPP repair and totally extraperitoneal repair [TEP]) [33–35]; and (3) robot-assisted repair (providing enhanced operative dexterity and three-dimensional visualization) [36]. Laparoscopic and robotic modalities demonstrate particular utility in evaluating and managing complex cases or reproductive-age patients with inguinal hernias, owing to advantages including reduced morbidity, comprehensive exploratory capability, and facilitation of concurrent management of intra-abdominal pathology (such as the reproductive tract anomalies observed in this case) [1]. Ultimate surgical approach selection necessitates individualized assessment.

This case report describes an adult female presenting with a left-sided irreducible inguinal hernia containing a rare combination of ovarian tissue, a hypoplastic fallopian tube, and a rudimentary uterine horn. This anatomical variant was not fully characterized on preoperative imaging studies or gynecological examination, consistent with published literature indicating that such anomalies are predominantly diagnosed intraoperatively [37]. Given the non-functional nature of the elongated hypoplastic fallopian tube and rudimentary uterine horn, coupled with their associated torsion risk, while the ovary demonstrated normal morphology and vascularization, we performed a single-stage laparoscopic procedure: TAPP repair of the left irreducible inguinal hernia with concurrent excision of the rudimentary uterine horn, resection of the hypoplastic fallopian tube, and oophoropexy. This surgical approach simultaneously addressed the hernial anatomical defect and eliminated potential acute abdominal complications, particularly addressing the reproductive and endocrine preservation requirements essential for patients of childbearing age.

The core insights from this case are as follows: (1) Preoperative diagnosis of complex hernias with concurrent cryptic reproductive tract malformations presents significant diagnostic challenges. Even with combined ultrasonography, CT, and multidisciplinary consultation, conventional imaging modalities demonstrate limited sensitivity for detecting subtle uterine and fallopian tube abnormalities. MRI offers superior visualization of myometrial architecture, endometrial structure, and ovarian morphology, warranting consideration as supplemental imaging in complex cases. (2) For reproductive-age patients without evidence of acute incarceration or torsion, laparoscopic TAPP repair represents a preferred surgical approach. This technique enables direct visualization of hernial contents, precise identification and concurrent management of associated reproductive system anomalies, thereby eliminating the morbidity of staged procedures while preserving ovarian function and reproductive potential. Comprehensive exploration should be performed intraoperatively to exclude additional occult hernias. (3) Given the potential association with multisystem congenital anomalies, comprehensive evaluation is indicated, including chromosomal analysis, renal ultrasonography, and spinal imaging studies to exclude coexistent abnormalities. (4) Postoperative surveillance remains paramount, encompassing monitoring for inguinal hernia repair-related complications including recurrence and chronic pain syndrome. Regular clinical examination and ultrasonographic assessment should monitor bilateral ovarian morphology and function, with particular attention to the affected side. Considering patient age and potential disease impact on ovarian reserve, hormonal evaluation including follicle-stimulating hormone and anti-Müllerian hormone levels may be warranted to assess reproductive capacity and endocrine status. For patients with reproductive intentions, consultation with reproductive endocrinology specialists is recommended for comprehensive fertility risk assessment and counseling.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates the exceptional rarity of irreducible inguinal hernia in adult females containing functional ovarian tissue, dysplastic fallopian tube, and rudimentary uterine horn, where preoperative diagnosis remains challenging due to conventional imaging limitations in detecting occult reproductive anomalies. Through single-stage laparoscopic TAPP, we successfully achieved hernia repair with concurrent excision of the rudimentary uterine horn, resection of the hypoplastic fallopian tube, and oophoropexy. This multidisciplinary integrated approach eliminated secondary surgical morbidity while providing dual benefits of anatomical restoration and fertility preservation, establishing a minimally invasive paradigm for complex irreducible inguinal hernias in reproductive-age women. Given the frequent association with multisystem developmental disorders, comprehensive evaluation including chromosomal analysis, genitourinary, and spinal screening is recommended. In future clinical practice, heightened vigilance for such presentations in female inguinal irreducible masses is warranted, with laparoscopic techniques representing an optimal primary strategy for non-acutely ischemic cases due to their combined diagnostic and therapeutic capabilities.
