Complexity Within Complexity
Authors: Taishi Yonetsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: drug-eluting stents, optical coherence tomography, percutaneous coronary intervention

Intravascular imaging has evolved from an adjunctive tool to a central component of contemporary complex percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Previous clinical studies have demonstrated superior outcomes with imaging-guided PCI, using predominantly intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) or optical coherence tomography (OCT) guidance, particularly in anatomically challenging settings such as long lesions, bifurcation lesions, or chronic total occlusion.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 Reflecting this accumulating evidence, current guidelines now position OCT-guided PCI alongside IVUS-guided PCI as a recommended strategy. However, an important clinical question remains unsettled: among the many anatomically challenging lesions encountered in daily practice, where does OCT provide the greatest incremental value over angiographic guidance alone? In this issue of JACC: Asia, Roh et al8 extend the Optical Coherence Tomography versus Conventional Guidance for Complex Percutaneous Coronary Intervention (OCCUPI) experience by examining whether lesion length and vessel size modify the benefit of OCT-guided PCI.8 Their analysis suggests a pragmatic answer: the value of OCT appears greatest when lesions are long and vessels are small.

This post hoc analysis examined 1,604 patients with complex coronary lesions enrolled in the randomized OCCUPI trial2 in which a complex lesion was defined by the presence of at least 1 of the following features: lesion length >28 mm, reference vessel diameter ≤2.5 mm, acute coronary syndrome, chronic total occlusion, or bifurcation lesion. In this background of anatomically and clinically complex PCI, patients were further stratified according to the median quantitative coronary angiography–derived lesion length (32 mm) and reference vessel diameter (2.8 mm). The main finding was straightforward and clinically relevant; OCT-guided PCI was associated with lower 1-year major adverse cardiac events in lesions ≥32 mm and in vessels <2.8 mm, with the most pronounced benefit observed in lesions that had both features. In that subgroup, OCT guidance nearly halved the risk of major adverse cardiac events compared with angiographic guidance, and the treatment effect was driven largely by a reduction in ischemia-driven target-vessel revascularization. At the same time, the direction of effect favored OCT across all anatomic subgroups, even though some of them did not reach statistical significance.

The message is not that OCT should be reserved exclusively for long lesions or small vessels. Rather, these data suggest that OCT yields increasing clinical leverage as anatomic complexity intensifies. This point is important because OCCUPI did not include simple lesions, but instead enrolled patients undergoing complex PCI; therefore, this post hoc analysis evaluated varying degrees of complexity within a population already restricted to complex lesions. Lesion length and vessel diameter therefore function here not as binary labels but as markers of technical difficulties, which are surrogates for the likelihood of underexpansion, geographic miss, incomplete lesion preparation, or edge-related complications.

Previous observational studies have complicated the interpretation of suboptimal stent deployment despite imaging-guided PCI. A small minimal stent area (MSA) on intravascular imaging has consistently been associated with a higher risk of adverse events, particularly target-vessel myocardial infarction and repeat revascularization. This observation has often been interpreted as evidence that achieving a larger MSA through more aggressive post-dilatation is a key procedural goal. However, an alternative and more skeptical interpretation has persisted which says that lesions with persistently small MSA despite imaging guidance may simply represent anatomically unfavorable subsets, such as heavily calcified or otherwise resistant plaques, diffuse disease, or intrinsically small vessels, in which even imaging-guided optimization cannot meaningfully change the mechanical result or subsequent prognosis. From this perspective, small MSA may be recognized less as a modifiable procedural target than as a marker of lesions destined for poor outcomes regardless of imaging use. In this context, the study from Roh et al8 is particularly important because it directly challenges this pessimistic view. Their findings indicate that intravascular imaging is not irrelevant for lesions predisposed to underexpansion; rather, its clinical benefit may be most evident precisely in these highly complex anatomies. The value of imaging guidance, therefore, may reside not only in maximizing final stent area, but also in mitigating risk in lesions for which optimal expansion is intrinsically difficult to achieve.

One notable finding of this study is the apparent paradox at its center. The subgroup deriving the greater benefit from OCT, longer or smaller lesions, also had the lower rate of OCT-defined procedural optimization. At first glance, this seems counterintuitive. If optimal procedural results were achieved less frequently, why was the clinical benefit greater? The most plausible explanation is that the value of OCT in these lesions may lie not solely in achieving prespecified optimization criteria after stent implantation but also in informing procedural strategy before stent deployment. Specifically, pre-interventional assessment of plaque morphology and vessel structure may facilitate more appropriate lesion preparation, more rational device selection, and more accurate balloon and stent sizing, such that satisfactory stent expansion may already be achieved before any additional optimization is performed.

This mechanistic interpretation has practical resonance. Long lesions and small vessels represent precisely the scenarios in which angiography is least reliable as a stand-alone guide. Diffuse disease obscures true lesion extent, vessel tapering complicates sizing, and modest absolute losses in lumen area carry greater clinical consequences in small-caliber arteries. In such cases, the superior spatial resolution of OCT may be uniquely useful, not simply because it measures more, but because it changes operator behavior in ways that meaningfully affect downstream outcomes. The reduction in repeat revascularization found in this analysis supports that view.

At the same time, the present findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. This was a post hoc analysis, and the subgroup cut points were derived from medians within the trial rather than prespecified clinical thresholds. Nevertheless, the absence of significant interaction testing reminds us that the data are more consistent with a gradient of benefit than with a binary boundary at 32 mm or 2.8 mm. The spline analyses support a continuum in which the estimated advantage of OCT increases as lesions become longer and vessels smaller. That is a more clinically credible interpretation than a dichotomous one, and it is probably the right one. Another issue that warrants consideration in randomized comparisons of intravascular imaging–guided vs angiography-guided PCI is that the observed treatment effect may depend not only on the quality of imaging guidance but also on the quality of the angiographic control strategy. Much of the randomized evidence favoring intravascular imaging has emerged from East Asia, where intravascular imaging is more deeply embedded in the practice of complex PCI and operator familiarity with image interpretation is correspondingly high. Although this experience is clearly a strength, it also raises an important methodological question: when imaging is routinely used in daily clinical practice for complex PCI, differences between these trial arms may reflect not only the efficacy of imaging itself, but also the quality of contemporary standard of angiography-guided PCI. In this regard, the recently reported IVUS-CHIP9 and OPTIMAL10 trials conducted in Europe, where intravascular imaging is less integrated in routine practice, are noteworthy because neither demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in the clinical primary endpoint with routine IVUS guidance. These observations suggest that, if image-guided PCI is to be standardized more broadly, equal attention must also be paid to defining and standardizing best-practice angiography-guided PCI. Beyond that, the future of imaging-guided PCI may lie not simply in demonstrating that imaging is beneficial, but in defining which imaging modality is best suited to which anatomy. OCCUPI and the present analysis are valuable in this regard because they help delineate a lesion subset in which OCT appears particularly compelling. The next step, therefore, is not merely larger trials, but more discriminating ones—studies designed to prospectively test anatomy-guided imaging strategies, integrate lesion-level physiology and plaque morphology, and clarify whether the principal value of imaging lies in achieving numeric optimization or in avoiding major procedural failure. Until such data are available, Roh et al8 provide a practical and clinically relevant message: when PCI becomes most challenging, OCT may become most useful.
