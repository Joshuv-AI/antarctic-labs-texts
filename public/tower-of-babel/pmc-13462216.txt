Contemporary National Inpatient Trends in appendectomy utilization and operative approach in the United States, 2016–2022
Authors: Sepehr Seifi, Ali Seifi, Tavis Kotzur, Nicholas Arabion Taylor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundContemporary national data on inpatient appendectomy practice remain limited. We evaluated recent trends in appendectomy utilization and operative approach among patients hospitalized with appendicitis and examined patient- and hospital-level factors associated with operative management and inpatient outcomes.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cross-sectional study of the National Inpatient Sample from 2016 to 2022. Hospitalized patients with appendectomy procedures were identified using ICD-10 codes. Operative approach was categorized as open, laparoscopic, or robotic. Weighted analyses and multivariable regression evaluated utilization, approach, length of stay, and adverse discharge.ResultsAmong 1,035,444 appendicitis hospitalizations, appendectomy utilization declined from 88.8% in 2016 to 82.2% in 2022 (p < 0.001). Among 889,289 appendectomy cases, laparoscopic appendectomy was performed in 90.2% overall and increased to 92.3% by 2022, while open appendectomy declined from 12.4% to 5.9%. Robotic appendectomy increased from 80 to 1900 during study period, (OR 47.02, 95% CI 23.72–93.22). Hispanic patients had slightly higher odds of appendectomy (OR 1.02, 95% CI 1.02–1.03), whereas Black patients (OR 0.94, 95% CI 0.93–0.95) and patients aged ≥65 years (OR 0.85, 95% CI 0.83–0.86) had lower odds. Rural hospitals had lower odds of laparoscopic (OR 0.92, 95% CI 0.91–0.92) and robotic (OR 0.03, 95% CI 0.02–0.03) approaches. Black race and ECI ≥5 were associated with longer length of stay, while older age, Black race, Medicare insurance, and ECI ≥5 were associated with adverse discharge.ConclusionsAppendectomy utilization declined while laparoscopy remained dominant, open surgery decreased, and robotic use increased but remained uncommon. These findings provide a national descriptive framework for future clinically granular studies of appendicitis management.Keywords: Appendectomy, Appendicitis, Laparoscopic, Robotic surgery, National Inpatient Sample, Trends, Disparities

Introduction

Appendectomy is a common and generally well-tolerated procedure performed most commonly for the treatment of acute appendicitis [1]. Historically, surgical appendectomy has been the definitive standard of care. Over the past two decades, however, the clinical landscape has shifted considerably. Improvements in Computed Tomography (CT) imaging have led to a reduction in unnecessary appendectomies [2], [3]. Currently, laparoscopic appendectomy is considered the gold standard for the treatment of appendicitis due to reduced wound complications and shorter hospital stay compared to open surgery [4]. Laparoscopic appendectomy, introduced in the 1980s, has become the dominant technique, increasing from 43.3% of all appendectomies in 2004 to approximately 75% by 2011 based on prior National Inpatient Sample (NIS) analyses [5]. In the recent years, robotic surgery has been introduced as a safe alternative method. Its use among surgeons is increasing, though it can be costly and with a learning curve, its role in acute care surgery has been debated [6]. Nationally, robotic techniques in emergency general surgery increased significantly during 2008 to 2020, though robotic appendectomy remains far less common than laparoscopic approaches and carries substantially higher costs [7]. Additionally, conservative management with intravenous antibiotics has emerged as a safe alternative, potentially changing overall trends [8], [9], [10].

Although prior studies have described earlier trends in laparoscopic appendectomy and evolving interest in nonoperative management, national inpatient data through 2022 remain limited. The primary objective of this study was to describe national trends in appendectomy utilization and operative approach; open, laparoscopic, and robotic, among patients hospitalized with appendicitis from 2016 through 2022 in the United States. The secondary objective was to identify patient and hospital level factors associated with appendectomy utilization, operative approach, hospital length of stay, and adverse discharge. By combining temporal trend analysis with adjusted modeling, this study provides an updated national overview of appendectomy practice patterns and outcome variation among hospitalized patients with appendicitis in the United States.

Methods

This cross-sectional, retrospective study analyzed temporal trends and predictors of appendicitis management using data from the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) database from 2016 to 2022. We selected 2016 as the first study year because it was the first complete NIS calendar year coded entirely using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10). The study ended in 2022 because it was the most recent NIS year available when the data were acquired and the analytic protocol and analyses were completed. Appendicitis inpatient discharges were identified by a primary diagnosis (DX1) of acute appendicitis using ICD-10-CM K35 codes. Appendectomy procedures were identified from any listed ICD-10-PCS procedure field. Open and minimally invasive approaches were classified according to the approach specified in the appendectomy procedure code. A discharge was categorized as robotic only when an appendectomy procedure code and an ICD-10-PCS robotic-assistance code from the 8E0W*CZ family were both present during the same hospitalization. Thus, the robotic-assistance code supplemented rather than replaced the appendectomy procedure code. The complete diagnosis and procedure code lists are provided in Supplementary Table S1.The study was reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines [11]. The NIS is the largest publicly available all-payer inpatient healthcare database in the United States and contains a 20% stratified sample of inpatient hospital discharges from community hospitals across the country [12]. Rehabilitation and long-term acute care hospitals are not included in the database. Separate subgroup analyses were performed for all appendectomy cases, laparoscopic appendectomy cases, and robotic appendectomy cases. Temporal trends in appendicitis hospitalization and appendectomy utilization were evaluated over the study period.

Demographic and hospital-level variables included age, sex, race/ethnicity, primary payer, household income quartile by ZIP code, Elixhauser comorbidity Index (ECI) burden, and hospital teaching and rural versus urban status. ECI is a comprehensive risk-adjustment tool used to measure the burden of pre-existing medical conditions in patient populations. Adverse discharge was defined as any discharge other than routine discharge to home or self-care, including transfer to skilled nursing facilities, intermediate care facilities, or hospice care. Length of stay was evaluated as a continuous outcome. Because the NIS does not contain patient identifiers, this study was deemed exempt from full Institutional Review Board review by the University of Texas Health Science Center at San Antonio (HSC20150408N).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies with percentages, while continuous variables are reported as means with standard deviations (SD). Comparisons between groups were performed using Chi-square tests for categorical variables and Student's t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables, as appropriate based on data distribution. Temporal trends across study years were also assessed.

Multivariable regression analyses were performed to evaluate factors associated with: (1) odds of undergoing appendectomy, (2) odds of laparoscopic appendectomy, (3) odds of robotic appendectomy, (4) hospital length of stay, and (5) adverse discharge disposition.

Logistic regression models were used for binary outcomes, and regression estimates are reported as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For hospital length of stay, regression coefficients were reported as percent changes with corresponding 95% CIs. Covariates included age category, sex, race/ethnicity, insurance status, household income quartile, Elixhauser comorbidity category, and hospital teaching and rural versus urban status. Additional analyses evaluated differences in utilization of laparoscopic and robotic appendectomy across hospital types, including urban teaching, urban non-teaching, and rural hospitals.

All analyses incorporated NIS discharge weights to generate national estimates. Statistical analyses were performed using R Foundation for Statistical Computing software (version 4.2.0; Vienna, Austria). A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Appendicitis hospitalizations and appendectomy utilization

During 2016–2022, there were 1,035,444 weighted inpatient discharges with a primary diagnosis of appendicitis. Annual appendicitis inpatient discharges decreased from 169,365 in 2016 to 130,705 in 2022, while the appendicitis hospitalization rate declined from 47.7 to 39.9 per 10,000 total inpatient discharges (p < 0.001; Fig. 1A). Appendectomy cohort and operative approach

Of the 1,035,444 appendicitis inpatient discharges, 889,289 (85.9%) underwent appendectomy. Appendectomy utilization declined from 88.8% in 2016 to 82.2% in 2022 (p < 0.001; Table 1 and Fig. 1B). The appendectomy cohort had a mean age of 36.16 years (SD 21.90); 55% were male, 56% were White, 46% had private insurance, and 70% were treated at urban teaching hospitals. Mean hospital length of stay was 2.86 days (SD 3.12). Complete annual characteristics of the appendectomy cohort are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Among appendectomy discharges, 801,710 (90.2%) were laparoscopic, 82,155 (9.2%) were open, and 5425 (0.6%) were robotic. Laparoscopic use increased from 87.5% in 2016 to 92.3% in 2022, open appendectomy declined from 12.4% to 5.9%, and robotic appendectomy increased from fewer than 0.1% to 1.8% (all p < 0.001; Table 1 and Fig. 1B).

Factors associated with appendectomy

On multivariable analysis, Hispanic patients (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.02; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.02–1.03) and patients in the lowest household-income quartile (aOR, 1.01; 95% CI, 1.00–1.02) had slightly higher odds of undergoing appendectomy. Patients aged 65 years or older (aOR, 0.85; 95% CI, 0.83–0.86), Black patients (aOR, 0.94; 95% CI, 0.93–0.95), Medicare beneficiaries (aOR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.96–0.98), and patients treated at urban teaching hospitals (aOR, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.92–0.93) had lower odds. Compared with 2016, the adjusted odds of appendectomy were lower in 2021 and 2022 (Fig. 2). Additional adjusted comparisons according to hospital setting are presented in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Laparoscopic appendectomy

Laparoscopic appendectomy accounted for 801,710 (90.2%) operative discharges and increased from 87.5% in 2016 to 92.3% in 2022. Adjusted odds of a laparoscopic approach increased progressively during the study period. Rural hospitals had lower odds of laparoscopic appendectomy (aOR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.91–0.92), whereas urban teaching hospitals had slightly higher odds (aOR, 1.01; 95% CI, 1.01–1.02). Older age and Black race were associated with modestly lower odds of laparoscopy, whereas residence in the highest household-income quartile was associated with higher odds. Complete annual characteristics and adjusted estimates are provided in Supplementary Table S4 and Supplementary Fig. S4.

Robotic appendectomy

A total of 5425 weighted discharges underwent robotic appendectomy. Use increased from 80 weighted discharges, representing fewer than 0.1% of operative cases, in 2016 to 1900 discharges, representing 1.8%, in 2022 (p < 0.001; Fig. 1). Patients undergoing robotic appendectomy had a mean age of 45.62 years (SD 18.25); 52% were female, 61% were White, 50% had private insurance, and 71% were treated at urban teaching hospitals. Mean total hospital charges increased from $74,337.75 in 2016 to $99,726.89 in 2022 (p = 0.021). Length of stay and adverse discharge

Mean hospital length of stay was 2.86 days (SD 3.12) and did not change significantly during the study period (p = 0.058; Table 1). In adjusted analysis, patients aged 18–39 years had shorter hospital stays than patients younger than 18 years, whereas Black race and increasing comorbidity burden were associated with longer stays (Supplementary Fig. S2). Older age, Black race, Medicare coverage, and greater comorbidity burden were also associated with higher odds of adverse discharge (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Discussion

In this national analysis of appendicitis inpatient discharges, appendectomy utilization declined between 2016 and 2022, while laparoscopic surgery became increasingly predominant and open appendectomy decreased. Robotic appendectomy also increased during the study period, although it continued to represent a small proportion of all procedures. We also observed differences in operative management and inpatient outcomes according to patient demographics, comorbidity burden, insurance status, and hospital characteristics. The importance of this study lies not only in describing changes in operative approach but also in identifying a contemporary national decline in appendectomy utilization among appendicitis inpatient discharges. Although the NIS cannot establish the reasons for this decline, documenting this pattern provides a foundation for future clinically granular studies examining the possible contributions of nonoperative treatment, disease severity, diagnostic imaging, patient preference, outpatient management, and hospital-level practice patterns. Overall, these findings provide a contemporary overview of changing inpatient appendectomy practice in the United States.

Declining appendectomy rates

The appendectomy rate among hospitalized patients with appendicitis declined from 88.8% in 2016 to 82.2% in 2022. This finding documents a measurable change in inpatient operative utilization over a recent national period. Several clinically plausible contributors may explain this pattern, including greater use of antibiotics in selected patients, improved diagnostic imaging, changing thresholds for admission or surgery, and pandemic-era shifts in hospital practice.

Nonoperative management has received increasing attention over the past two decades, particularly for selected patients with uncomplicated appendicitis. Prior studies and clinical trials have evaluated antibiotics as an alternative to immediate appendectomy in this population [10], [13]. Patient preference may also play a role, as some patients with uncomplicated appendicitis may favor an initial nonoperative approach when clinically appropriate [14]. However, administrative inpatient data cannot determine how often antibiotics were used as primary therapy or whether individual patients were considered appropriate candidates for nonoperative treatment.

Improved diagnostic imaging may also contribute to changes in operative utilization. Wider use and better availability of CT and other imaging modalities may reduce diagnostic uncertainty, identify alternative diagnoses, and decrease unnecessary appendectomy [15], [16], [17]. In one tertiary-center study, the negative appendectomy rate decreased from 23.0% to 1.7% as preoperative CT use increased from 1% to 97.5% [3]. These data support the broader concept that diagnostic practice can influence operative rates, although imaging findings and negative appendectomy status cannot be directly evaluated in NIS.

The lower odds of appendectomy in 2021 and 2022 may also reflect pandemic-era practice patterns. Prior studies have reported that COVID-19-related resource constraints, changes in emergency surgical workflows, and increased use of nonoperative management influenced appendicitis care during the pandemic [18]. In our study, however, the decline in appendectomy utilization began before 2020, suggesting that the pattern was not solely a pandemic-related effect. This observation is also consistent with broader epidemiologic reports describing declining appendicitis incidence in some populations over recent decades [15], [16]. Because the NIS cannot distinguish the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic from other concurrent changes in appendicitis management, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive temporal associations.

Taken together, these findings identify an important area for future investigation. NIS can define the timing, scale, and national distribution of declining inpatient appendectomy utilization, but it cannot determine the patient-level reasons for this change. Prospective studies or clinically granular datasets are needed to evaluate whether this trend is driven by nonoperative management, diagnostic imaging, disease severity, outpatient treatment, patient preference, or hospital-level practice patterns. In this way, the present analysis provides a national roadmap for unanswered questions in appendicitis management.

Open surgery decline and laparoscopic dominance

Among patients who underwent appendectomy, laparoscopic surgery remained the standard operative approach throughout the study period. Open appendectomy declined from 12.4% of operative cases in 2016 to 5.9% in 2022, while laparoscopic appendectomy accounted for more than 90% of operative cases overall. This pattern is consistent with prior national analyses demonstrating a long-term shift from open to laparoscopic appendectomy, with laparoscopic utilization increasing from 43.3% in 2004 to 75% by 2011 in earlier NIS studies [5]. By 2022, laparoscopic appendectomy represented 92.3% of operative cases in our cohort, indicating that it has become the predominant surgical approach for appendicitis in contemporary inpatient practice. This observation is also consistent with prior evidence showing that laparoscopic appendectomy is associated with lower wound infection rates, shorter hospital stays, and faster postoperative recovery compared with open surgery [4].

Because laparoscopic appendectomy was already widely adopted before the start of the present study period, our findings should be interpreted as documenting the continued maturation of this transition rather than identifying a new change in surgical practice. When analysis was limited to operative cases, the odds of undergoing a laparoscopic approach increased modestly over time, suggesting continued preference for minimally invasive surgery among patients requiring appendectomy. We also observed lower odds of laparoscopic appendectomy in rural hospitals, indicating ongoing variation in access to minimally invasive surgical care. Similar geographic differences have been reported previously, with rural hospitals facing challenges related to surgical workforce availability, infrastructure, and access to advanced operative technologies [19]. However, because NIS does not capture surgeon-level resources, referral patterns, or hospital capabilities, the specific reasons for these differences cannot be determined from the present analysis.

Increasing use of robotic appendectomy

Robotic appendectomy increased during the study period, rising from 80 weighted cases in 2016 to 1900 in 2022. Although the relative increase was large, robotic appendectomy remained uncommon, representing only 0.6% of all appendectomy cases across the study period. This distinction is important. The data suggest increasing adoption of robotic platforms for appendectomy, particularly in urban hospitals, but they do not establish that robotic appendectomy is replacing laparoscopic appendectomy on a clinically meaningful scale, a pattern that is consistent with broader national trends in robotic emergency general surgery [7]. The higher hospital charges associated with robotic cases also warrant caution when interpreting this trend, consistent with prior studies demonstrating greater costs associated with robotic compared with laparoscopic appendectomy [6], especially because NIS does not include operative time, surgeon experience, supply cost, patient preference, postoperative pain scores, or long-term outcomes.

Disparities in appendectomy care and inpatient outcomes

We observed differences in appendectomy utilization, length of stay, adverse discharge, and operative approach across race, payer, income, comorbidity burden, and hospital setting. These findings are consistent with prior studies showing unequal access and outcomes in appendicitis care [20], [21], [22], [23], [24]. However, the mechanisms behind these differences cannot be determined from NIS. Factors such as delayed presentation, language barriers, insurance status, outpatient access, hospital resources, disease severity, and discharge support may contribute, but they are not measured directly in this dataset. Therefore, these results should be interpreted as signals of variation in care and outcomes rather than proof of specific causes.

In our analysis, racial and socioeconomic differences were observed across several measures of care, including operative utilization, length of stay, and discharge disposition. Similar patterns have been reported previously, with studies demonstrating disparities in appendicitis diagnosis, disease severity at presentation, and postoperative outcomes among minority populations [21], [23], [24]. Although the present study cannot determine the reasons for these differences, the persistence of these associations in a contemporary national sample suggests that variation in appendicitis care remains an important area for further investigation.

The associations with length of stay and adverse discharge also support the value of using national inpatient data to identify areas where more detailed clinical study is needed. Older age, higher comorbidity burden, payer status, and race were associated with less favorable inpatient outcomes after appendectomy. These findings do not establish the underlying causes of prolonged hospitalization or nonroutine discharge, but they help identify patient groups and hospital contexts where future studies could examine disease severity, timing of presentation, discharge resources, and access to follow-up care.

Taken together, these findings help define where appendectomy practice is changing at the national level and where important clinical details remain unknown. By identifying trends in operative utilization, surgical approach, robotic adoption, charges, and patient- and hospital-level variation, this study provides an epidemiologic framework for future work. More detailed clinical studies are needed to determine how disease severity, imaging findings, nonoperative treatment, outpatient care, surgeon decision-making, and hospital resources contribute to these observed patterns.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective administrative design permits identification of associations but does not support causal inference. Second, the NIS captures inpatient discharges rather than individual patients or all episodes of appendicitis care; therefore, outpatient, emergency department–only, and nonhospital care are not represented. Third, clinically important variables, including imaging findings, appendicitis severity, perforation, abscess characteristics, operative details, antibiotic treatment, and reasons for nonoperative management were unavailable. Fourth, the study period includes the COVID-19 pandemic, and the available data do not permit the effects of the pandemic to be separated from other concurrent changes in practice. Fifth, diagnosis and procedure classification depended on administrative coding and may be affected by coding error or incomplete documentation. Robotic classification required an additional robotic-assistance code during the same hospitalization; omission or miscoding of this code could have resulted in underestimation of robotic utilization. Sixth, the study ended in 2022 because this was the most recent NIS year available when the data were acquired and the analyses were completed; trends after 2022 are therefore not represented. Finally, robotic appendectomy remained uncommon, particularly during the early study years, and estimates for this subgroup should be interpreted cautiously. Despite these limitations, the large national sample provides a useful description of contemporary inpatient appendectomy practice in the United States.

Conclusion

In a nationally representative inpatient sample from 2016 to 2022, appendectomy utilization among hospitalized patients with appendicitis declined modestly in the United States. Among patients who underwent surgery, laparoscopic appendectomy remained the dominant approach, open appendectomy continued to decline, and robotic appendectomy increased from a very low baseline but remained uncommon. Differences in operative utilization, operative approach, length of stay, and discharge disposition were observed across race, payer, income, comorbidity burden, and hospital setting. Because NIS does not capture antibiotic use, imaging findings, appendicitis severity, patient preference, outpatient management, surgeon decision-making, or long-term outcomes, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive inpatient associations rather than causal explanations for changing appendicitis care. Future studies using clinically granular datasets are needed to clarify the reasons for these trends and their implications for access, outcomes, and cost.
