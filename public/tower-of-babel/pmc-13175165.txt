Caregivers for people in their last year of life
Authors: Matthew Yau, Erin Gallagher, Jennifer Rose-Smith
Published: 2026

Clinicians should recognize and respond to escalating caregiver burden in the care recipient’s last year of life

As Canada’s population ages, unpaid caregivers face increasing burden. Costs and care needs rise sharply within the care recipient’s last 12 months of life.1,2 When clinicians anticipate death within 12 months, particularly in the setting of functional decline, escalating symptoms, or recurrent hospitalizations, caregiver burden should be assessed.3

Caregivers should be screened for burnout

Clinicians should screen caregivers for high caregiver burden at first contact and after major care transitions (e.g., hospital discharge, symptom crises). The Zarit Burden Interview–4 asks how often caregivers lack time for themselves, feel stressed balancing care, feel strained around the care recipient, and feel uncertain about what to do.4 A score of 8 or greater signals further support is required.

Trusted, condition-specific support should be offered early

Supporting caregivers starts with assessing safety, recognizing their essential role, validating concerns, and offering concrete resources by connecting them to condition-specific societies and peer support (Appendix 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.252034/tab-related-content). For caregivers who feel they cannot step away, clinicians should consider respite referral and social prescription of blocks of time without direct caregiving responsibilities. Federal financial supports are available, including the Canada Caregiver Credit (tax credit for caregivers supporting family members at any prognosis) and Compassionate Care Benefits (temporary income replacement through Employment Insurance for employed caregivers providing end-of-life care) (Appendix 1).

Advance care planning can reduce decisional burden for caregivers

Advanced care planning improves caregiver satisfaction and mental health.5 Clinicians can initiate such planning by exploring the patient’s awareness of their illness with questions such as, “What do you know about your condition?” and then continuing with an advanced care planning toolkit (Appendix 1).

Support for caregivers should not end with the patient’s death6

Clinicians should connect grieving caregivers to resources such as hospices, funeral homes, religious or cultural institutions, and free online platforms like the Canadian Virtual Hospice (https://www.virtualhospice.ca), which hosts modules for practical grief support, as well as age-appropriate resources for children.
