Mechanistic insights into micro and nanosilicon for environmental stress mitigation in plant
Authors: Lipsa Leena Panigrahi, Gyana Ranjan Rout
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOver the past two decades, the persistent escalation in global temperatures has emerged as a critical driver of ecological instability, exerting profound consequences on agricultural productivity and sustainability. Various environmental stressors, including biomedical contaminants, collectively impair plant growth, development, and yield potential. Numerous adaptive strategies have been explored to mitigate these stresses and enhance plant resilience. Among these, silicon has gained increasing recognition as a quasi-essential element capable of alleviating abiotic and biotic stress through multifaceted mechanisms. Additionally, Si synergistically interacts with micronutrients and plant growth regulators (PGRs) to promote metabolic efficiency and physiological robustness. Recent advancements highlight the pivotal role of silicon nanoparticles (SiNPs) in enhancing plant growth, nutrient uptake, and stress resilience. SiNPs surpass bulk forms by improving biomass and limiting heavy metal translocation. Mechanistically, they regulate antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, POD, APX), modulate transporter genes and signalling pathways, and influence hormonal cross-talk with ABA, auxin, and ethylene, collectively strengthening plant defence systems. This review highlighted the response of micro- and nano-silicon to regulating key metabolic pathways involved in stress resilience. This review uniquely synthesizes emerging evidence comparing micro- and nano-silicon, emphasizing their distinct roles in modulating antioxidant defence, nutrient signalling, and heavy metal detoxification under environmental stress.Keywords: Abiotic stress, Alleviate, Biotic stress, Growth & metabolism, Productivity, Si transporter gene

Introduction

Climate change is an overwhelming phenomenon that introduces novel risks and resource fluctuations, further exacerbating existing vulnerabilities, particularly in light of the current shortcomings of international climate stabilizing frameworks. The growing recognition of the inevitability of climate change has reinvigorated research into adaptive strategies and mitigation frameworks aimed at enhancing ecological and agricultural resilience. Faced with climate perturbations, species must acclimate, adapt, move, or die. Specifically, plant species are very complex and immobile. Over 30,000 genes and numerous proteins undergo over 200 identified post-translational modifications, helping plants withstand anthropogenic and non-anthropogenic stresses. Non-anthropogenic factors such as drought, cold, flooding, submergence, heavy metals, salinity, osmotic stress, and temperature significantly impact plant ecosystems. Similarly, anthropogenic factors, such as pests, pathogen, and weeds, also considerably impact plant growth and sustainability. Globally, abiotic stresses such as drought, salinity, and extreme temperatures account for over 50 to 60% of annual crop losses, highlighting the urgent need for effective and sustainable mitigation strategies like Si supplementation (Fig. 1) [1].Moreover, various other biotic impacts often lead to inadequate vegetative growth, crop yields, or even the death of plants if they exceed tolerance limits. Plants respond to stress primarily by releasing stress-communicating molecules to trigger stress-defensive mechanisms. Additionally, this defense mechanism consists of critical proteins, signal transduction pathways, and regulatory pathways controlled by the expression of many stress-tolerant gene [1]. Numerous methods have been adopted to manipulate the metabolic and molecular pathways involved in plant responses and acclimation to distinct stresses, among which Si application is exceptional in its features.

Si is the second most abundant element on Earth and is commonly found in nearly all soils. Research on Si accelerated a century ago and is now recognized as a valuable fertilizer for crop production. It enhances growth and development by protecting plants against biotic and abiotic stresses [2, 3]. Si primarily exists in nature as silicate or aluminosilicate minerals embedded within the lithospheric matrix. It has been reported that under stress conditions, Si significantly enhances photosynthetic efficiency, reduces malondialdehyde content in leaves, and positively influences the nitrate supply in foliage.

Additionally, Si modulates leaf proline levels and influences soluble carbohydrate profiles within root tissues, contributing to osmotic adjustment. For instance, Sienkiewicz-Cholewa et al. reported that Si (1.0–1.5 mM) application in the form of silicic acid (H4SiO4) improved the growth of wheat seedlings under drought stress [4]. Si application exhibited immensely ameliorative consequences on the photosynthetic responses of sugarcane under water stress, thereby making Si the most widely reported beneficial non-essential element for plant growth [5–7]. Additionally, Si transport and accumulation have been characterized in diverse plant species such as rice (Oryzasativa) [8], maize (Zea mays) [9], barley (Hordeumvulgare) [10], wheat (Triticumaestivum) [11], horsetail (Equisetum arvense) [12], pumpkin (Cucurbitamoschata) [13], sorghum (Sorghum bicolor), and soybean (Glycine max) [14] indicating its systemic mobility across taxa. Some researchers observed that the soluble forms of Si are available in plants resistant to different environmental stresses [15–18]. A substantial body of literature affirms the role of Si in alleviating stress-induced physiological damage in plants [16, 18]. Some potential mechanisms include the rate of photosynthesis, alteration in stomatal conductance, enriched water use efficiency, and increased structural reinforcement associated with stress comfort in higher plants [19–21]. Other mechanisms include increased water potential and water content, oxidative stress reduction [22, 23], amendment of mineral accumulation [24], and diversification of phytohormone concentrations (Fig. 1). Liang et al. reported that Si alleviates salinity stress by minimizing oxidative damage [25]. Reactive oxygen species (e.g., O2−, H2O2) are derivatives of oxygen that are combative and lead to oxidative damage to the cells. The production of these molecules accelerates when plants are exposed to stress. Similarly,VanBockhavenetal. reported the significance of photorespiration and oxidative metabolism in Si-induced stress tolerance [26].The photorespiration, antioxidative enzymes, and the expression of critical photorespiratory genes have enhanced abiotic stress tolerance. However, minimal information is available on the potential roles of Si in plant metabolic processes, plant stress interaction with other nutrients, and the role of nano Si. The present review emphasizes the knowledge of Si application in micro and nano form and its underlying mechanisms to mitigate different environmental stresses in plants and discusses the experimental evidence for the same.

Abiotic and biotic stress combinations in plants: implications for growth and survival

Plant stresses can be broadly classified into three groups depending on the number of interacting factors: single, multiple, and combined. Single stress occurs when a single factor regulates the plant growth and development. Two or more stresses arise at different periods with no overlap in the multiple stress category. In contrast, the combined stresses involve the simultaneous occurrence of multiple stress factors with at least some degree of overlap between them. For instance, heat and drought often co-occur during summers, leading to combined abiotic stress. Similarly, simultaneous bacterial and fungal pathogen infections represent a case of combined biotic stress. A notable example is the brown apical necrosis of Juglansregia (walnut), which is caused by a combination of fungal pathogens (Fusarium spp., Alternaria spp., Cladosporium spp., Colletotrichum spp., and Phomopsis spp.), and a bacterium, Xanthomonasarboricola [27]. For example, drought predisposes Sorghum bicolor(sorghum) to infection by Macrophominaphaseolina [28]. Plants are sometimes exposed to “repetitive” episodes of single or multiple stresses interspersed by long or short recovery periods. Various spells of hot days at different phenological stages of plants are examples of repetitive stresses. Simultaneous or sequential stress combinations are expected to intensify under climate change, posing an even greater threat to global crop yield. These interactions can amplify their adverse effects and lead to a significant reduction in crop productivity. One of the most evident detrimental stress combinations is the simultaneous occurrence of drought and heat stress [29]. Salinity, combined with heat stress, also severely threatens plant survival in arid and semi-arid regions. Similarly, cold and drought stress combination reduces the productivity of Vitisvinifera (grapes) in continental climate regions such as North China [30]. The Mediterranean region imparts combined cold and high light stress to the growing plants, affecting their growth [31].Furthermore, Triticumaestivum (winter wheat) exhibits frost hardiness due to the combined effects of ozone and cold stress [32]. Similarly, yields of Oryza sativa (rice) and Cicerarietinum (chickpea) are significantly reduced by salinity stress combined with ozone stress [33]. Sequential or simultaneous biotic stresses also adversely affect plant growth in a manner quite similar to that of different abiotic stress combinations. Several diseases often arise from combined infection of fungi, bacteria, and viruses rather than by individual pathogens. Table 1 includes various biotic stress combinations and their impact on plants. Plants frequently encounter biotic and abiotic stresses where environmental factors influence plants disease development, popularly known as the “disease triangle”. The interplay between these stresses often occurs at the plant interface and within the surrounding environment. These interactions determine the magnitude of their impact on crop response. For example, a concurrent heat wave during a drought period will magnify the drought conditions, leading to a severe decline in crop yields. Similarly, drought and heat stress synergistically affect plant physiology, causing a greater negative impact on crop yield. Likewise, concurrent drought and weed stress further lower the water availability for the crops and hampers the crop yield.

Si and plants: current knowledge

Si is a metalloid belonging to Group 14 of the Periodic Table. It has two allotropic forms: a brown amorphous powder and a dark grey crystal. It has a specific gravity of 2.42 and a high melting point of 1420 °C [34]. Si exhibits three oxidation states (− 4, + 2, and + 4), with + 4 being the most prevalent. It occurs in three naturally occurring isotopes (28Si, 29Si, and 30Si), with 28Si being the most prominent, occupying 92% of the total mass [35]. Si is the most ubiquitous element in the Earth ‘s crust, in over 370 rock forms [6]. In soil, it is present mainly as silica (silicon dioxide: SiO2), silica gel (a highly porous structure capable of adsorbing 40% of its weight in water from the saturated vapor), and silicate (a salt of silicic acid, H2SiO). Soil Si concentrations typically range from 23 to 35% wt. with an average value of 28.8%. This abundance and versatility of Si in soil highlights its critical role in plant nutrition and environmental interactions, forming the basis for its significance in soil–plant systems.

Si uptake and transportation mechanisms under stress

Si uptake and accumulation vary significantly due to specific protein transporters in the plant ‘s root system and the Si chemical composition in the soil. Si is absorbed by the roots in the form of mono silicic acid [Si(OH)4], which is water- soluble at pH < 9 and at concentrations below 2 mM.After, absorption by the roots, it is transported to the shootsthrough the transpiration process, where it gets polymerized to colloidal silica gel (SiO2·nH2O) (Fig. 2).

Molecular transport mechanism

In rice (Oryzasativa), silicic acid is transported rapidly across the cell membrane compared towater, indicating an active transport mechanism [20]. The Casparian strip blocks the apoplastic pathway, thereby facilitatingthe symplastic route. The first Si transporter protein, Lsi1, was identified using the mutagenesis approach by Ma et al.,followedbynumerous subsequent studies that later revealed detailed molecular mechanisms involved in this process [21]. Lsi1, a member of the aquaporin subfamily nodulin 26-like intrinsic proteins (NIPs), is an influx transporter for silicic acid. All aquaporins comprise six alpha- helix transmembrane domains (H1–H6) linked together by five loops (LA – LE). The conserved motif is contained within loops LB and LE, which form the NPA domain. The two constrictions in the pore of the aquaporin play a significant role in determining the solute specificity and transport kinetics.Lsi1, which transports uncharged silicic acid in particular, belongs to the NIP III group, comprising a unique selectivity filter:Gly (G), Ser (S), Gly (G), and Arg (R) [22].Subsequently, Ma et.al., identified Lsi2, a proton gradient, thereby acting as an efflux transporter actively pumping Si out of the cells using the same mutagenesis approach [23]. Being an active transporter, Lsi2has a homology and mechanism completely different from Lsi1. Lsi2 is an anion transporter coupled with a proton antiport, having 9–12 transmembrane domains [23]. Lsi1 mediates the influx of silicic acid into root cells, whereas Lsi2 actively exports it toward the xylem, enabling directional transport to aerial tissues. The molecular mechanisms involved in the uptake of Si by the roots of plants have been beautifully elucidated with the identification of the Lsi1 and Lsi2 transporters. For instance, Si is taken up by the rice (Oryzasativa) in the form of mono silicic acid via the Lsi1 present on the distal side of the exodermis, which is then pumped out in a de-protonated form in aerenchyma by Lsi2 at the proximal side of the cell. Silicic acid then passes through the apoplast of aerenchyma. The casparian strip blocks the apoplastic pathway in the endodermis, which directs Si to be transported via the symplastic pathway through Lsi1 and then Lsi2 to the xylem. The silicic acid moves upwards to the shoot via the transpiration stream, where Lsi6 (an influx transporter, homolog of Lsi1) unloads Si from the xylem and facilitates its transport to the different aerial parts of the plant.

The expression of the Si transporter genes is modulated under various stresses. For instance, Swain and Rout reported that Lsi1 and Lsi2 genes are up-regulatedunder salt stress [24]. Si helps to reduce gene expression under salinity stress. Similarly, the relative expression level of Lsi1 under salinity stress was up-regulated by 607% in Cv.Badami and 690% in Cv.Ghanteswari. Mitani et al., observed down-regulation of Lsi2 in maize and barley after the seventh day of Si treatment, with high genotypic changes in the expression level due to post-translational modifications [25]. Thus, variation in the Lsi2 expression level is caused by post-translational modifications, which significantly explain the genotypic variation based on Si accumulation. The role of Si in plants and its expression under different abiotic stresses at the transcriptomic level has been reported widely. Si application enhances plant resilience to drought by reducing hydrogen peroxide accumulation and up-regulating the antioxidant enzymes [23]. Jadhao et al., demonstrated that Si induces the synergistic defence mechanism by considerably accelerating the transcript level of Si transporter genes (EcLsi1, EcLsi2, and EcLsi6), and hormone-regulating genes (EcSAM, EcPAL, and EcLOX) at 72 h of pestinfestation [26]. The study by Song et al., [28] demonstrated thatSi supplementation plays a crucial role in enhancing the expression of genes associated with photosynthesis and stress tolerance in rice, particularly under zinc-induced stress [27]. Si treatment led to increased transcription of PsbY (Os08g02630), a key polyprotein component of Photosystem II (PSII), which is implicated in enhancing manganese-binding capacity and water oxidation—processes essential for optimizing PSII efficiency and improving electron transport. Additionally, genes associated with the light-harvesting complex, such as Os03g57120 and Os09g26810, were up-regulated, indicating improved light energy capture. Si also induced the expression of several transcription factors (TFs), such as DREB2A, NAC5, OsRDCP1, OsCMO, and OsRAB16b, which are central to stress signal transduction and gene regulation under abiotic stress [28] (Table 2). The activation of DREB2A, for instance, promotes osmotic stress resistance via an ABA-independent pathway, while NAC5 contributes to redox regulation and cellular protection by preventing lipid peroxidation and limiting hydrogen peroxide accumulation [28]. The up-regulation of OsNAC5 also enhances the expression of stress-inducible genes like LEA3, conferring improved tolerance to dehydration and oxidative stress. Furthermore, the expression of OsRAB16b, a gene from the LEA (Late Embryogenesis Abundant) family, was elevated, contributing to cellular stabilization and stress adaptation across both vegetative and reproductive tissues. Collectively, these transcriptional modulations underscore the pivotal role of Si in fortifying rice plants against abiotic stresses by enhancing photosynthetic efficiency and triggering a robust defence response [28].

Stress-responsive gene expression in rice under Si treatment

Si accumulation in plants ranges from 0.1 to 10% on a dry-weight basis [23]. Bryophyta, Lycopsida, and Equisetopsidaexhibit higher accumulation of Si compared to most of the angiosperms [23]. Among angiosperms,the families Poaceae and Cyperaceae, accumulate higher Si levels, while other angiosperms families namely, Commelinaceae, Urticaceae, and Cucurbitaceae, have been reported to accumulate intermediate levels of Si [23]. Si excluders comprise Brassicaceae and Solanaceae which have been reported to poorly accumulate Si. Up to 10% of Si can be accumulated in rice (Poaceae), making it a model species to study its biological roles [23, 33]. Although monocots were previously considered stronger Si accumulators than dicots, this distinction has been challenged by later studies.

In the same line,PGRs are the first-line modulators in plant growth, development, and stress tolerance (Fig. 3). PGRs potentially regulate stress primarily via antioxidant metabolism, ROS scavenging, and induction of gene expression. Si is considered a phyto-beneficial element as it interacts with endogenous phytohormones and influences their synthesis, accumulation, and signalling to help plants combat adversities. For instance, it is reported that Si mediates plant growth and development by adjusting endogenous levels of auxins. Similarly, gibberellins work synergistically with Si for plant stress protection and development. Si up-regulates the production of endogenous bioactive GAs in response to stress. Si is a useful element that strengthens jasmonic acid (JA)-dependent defensive mechanisms for plant tolerance against abiotic stress. JA production helps prevent functional membranes from drying out during osmotic stress. Si build up decreases JA synthesis and modifies signal transmission involved in membrane protection. Si also gives the membranes mechanical strength during osmotic stress by reducing electrolyte leakage and lipid peroxidation. Recently, rice seedlings benefited from the combination application of Si and methyl jasmonate (MeJA) in terms of genetic stability and vegetative development.

Silicon-mediated stress responses are regulated through interactions with multiple phytohormones and signalling cascades (Ca2⁺, SA, ABA, and JA). Mechanism of silicon-mediated stress tolerance

Si transportation in plants under stress

Abiotic stresses, like salinity, significantly affect carbohydrate accumulation in different plant species. The concentration of sucrose increasesunder salinity stress [55]. The antioxidant variation in different responses of genotypes has been reported. The addition of Si mitigatesauto toxicity due to the involvement of 3-phenylpropionic acid during seed germination and enhanced activities of α-amylase and β-amylase. The foliar application of Si enriches soluble sugars and sucrose in leaves, alleviating the drastic effects of shading stress. The external application of Si to rice plants underneath prolonged shade helps them to partially recover from shade response phenotypes such as reduced chlorophyll, reduced tiller number, and increased height. Cooke and Carey studied the role of Si under water and salinity stresses [34]. They observed that adding Si to unstressed plants did not consistently impact water movement. Two mechanisms are involved in Si transportation, i.e. mechanisms associated with transpiration, and mechanisms independent of transpiration. Passive transport of Sioccurs through transpiration stream. The second mechanism states that Si transport is directed into specific plant parts through transporters and involves active Si uptake as silicic acid through transporters in roots. Dabravolski and Isayenkov reviewed Si ‘s physiological and molecular mechanisms in salt stress. Si improves the physiological processes, including antioxidant response, photosynthetic efficiency, production of osmoprotectants, and localization of Si transporters [35]. Saja-Garbarzet et al., observed the differences in Si accumulation between the leaf and petiole, which affect the water power conductivity in the plant of Brassica napus. They observed that the physiological regulation of water balance through Si might be associated with the mechanism of overcoming the obstacles blocking water uptake by the plant.Various sources of Si (silica gel, potassium silicate, and algae rich in Si) have been tested against the growth of sugar beet under water stress [36]. They observed that potassium silicate was most capable of enhancing chlorophyll content and growth attributes compared with silica gel, algae rich in Si under water stress. It has been reported that Si shows favourable results on plant growth and development unequalled by any other non-essential nutrient, emphasizing the unrecognized status of this universal element (Table 3) [37].

Mitigation of abiotic stress through Si

Si and salt stress

Lamsaadia et al., investigated the impact of Si seed priming on Trigonellafoenum-graecum with (0—60 mM CaSiO3) against salinity stress (100 and 150mMNaCl) [38]. They noted that Si priming was particularly effective in promotinga higher germination rate under salt stress and was very influential in the reduction of oxidative stress. Based on an experiment conducted on natural conditions found that Si-primed seeds significantly enhanced biomass, yield attributes, nutritional quality, and antioxidant activity under salinity stress compared to non-primed plants [38]. Swain and Rout studied the effect of Si under salinity stress on rice genotypes. They classified genotypes as a low, medium, and high Si accumulators of rice [24]. The accumulation of Si was higher in Oryzasativa with shoot dry weight (74.6%), and therefore was declared a high Si- accumulator [24].

Similarly, a significant increase in root and shoot dry weight under salt stress with Si treatment has been observed in tomatoand chilli. Salinity-induced reductions in the photosynthetic pigment levels in plant systems are mitigated by Si. Swain and Rout [55] emphasized that Si protects against the salinity-induced damage in rice crops by reducing the effect of oxidative, ionic, and osmotic stress.

Si and water stress

Si absorption by roots from the nutrient solution is facilitated by aquaporin channels like nodulin 26-like intrinsic proteins. The transport of Si is mediated via AQP-like proteins, assisting in cell water intake. Zhu et al., noted that the upregulation of Si-transporting aquaporin has been observed in cucumber [39]. Saja-Garbarz et al., observed differences in the Si assemblage between the leaf and petiole of Brassica napus, which may have affected the hydraulic conductivity. They reported that the water balance regulation might be interrelated with the mechanism of overcoming barriers hindering water uptake by the plant. The indications suggest this element ‘s role in improving plant water uptake. Prisa [61] noted that Si application improved the resistance of cells to mechanical stresses, reduced water loss through transpiration, and reduced salt stress. Manivannan et al.,observed that the external application of Si depended on genotypic specificity [40].

Si and drought stress

El-Beltagi et al., reported that during the vegetative phase of maize plants under drought stress conditions, the foliar application of either Si (4.0 mmol) or Putrescine (Put) (0.5 mmol) or in combination did not show any significant advantages in growth. During the reproductive phase under drought stress, the green pigments, leaf area, and relative water contents are reduced. They also noticed that during drought stress, the production of malondialdehyde content, H2O2, and electrolyte leakage was significantly reduced because of the combined effect of Si and Put. However, drought stress increased the activities of catalase, superoxide dismutase, peroxidase, ascorbate peroxidase, and accumulation of osmolyte content (proline and soluble protein). Under drought stress conditions, the foliar application of Si + Put significantly increased cob length (22.70%), number of grains per cob (12.77%), and 100-grain weight (18.30%). They enhanced the maize grain yield by about 10.29% using a combination of Si and Put (Fig. 4).

Mitigation of biotic stress through Si

Biotic stressors such as fungal pathogens, insect herbivores, nematodes, bacteria, and viruses significantly compromise plant health and yield. Silicon (Si) has emerged as an effective agent in enhancing plant resistance against a wide array of these biotic challenges through multiple, interrelated mechanismsincluding physical fortification, metabolic reprogramming, and hormonal cross-talk.

Physical and structural defence

One of the primary modes through which Si confers resistance is by enhancing physical defences. Si deposition in plant tissues, primarily as amorphous silica or phytoliths (SiO₂), strengthens cell walls and creates a mechanical barrier that hinders pathogen penetration and herbivore feeding. As reported by Cristofano et.al., Si functions as a bio-stimulant, promoting tissue hardening and acting as a physical impediment to microbial invasion [55]. This structural reinforcement reduces the vulnerability of epidermal cells and can delay or block pathogen entry, particularly in the case of necrotrophic fungi and chewing insects [55].

Biochemical and phytohormonaldefence

Si also contributes to biochemical resistance by modulating the plant ‘s oxidative and enzymatic responses. It enhances the activities of key defence-related enzymes and modulates levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which act as signalling molecules in plant defence. Moreover, Si influences phytohormone-mediated pathways—including salicylic acid (SA), jasmonic acid (JA), and ethylene (ET)—each of which governs responses to specific pathogen lifestyles. For instance, Ye et al., demonstrated that Si-enhanced resistance to herbivores is predominantly JA-dependent [56]. In contrast, Vivancos et al. reported that although SA-dependent genes were up-regulated following Si treatment, the SA pathway itself was not essential for Si-mediated resistance to biotrophic pathogens [57].

Molecular interference with pathogen strategy

Intriguingly, Si may also disrupt pathogen attempts to suppress plant immunity. Van Bockhaven et al., proposed that Si deposition in the apoplast hinders effector molecules from reaching their intracellular targets, thereby sustaining an uninterrupted defense response [58]. This interference extends to the pathogen ‘s manipulation of ethylene signaling, a common tactic used by necrotrophs to subvert host defenses. Supporting this, experimental data from finger millet revealed that calcium silicate treatment reduced leaf blast severity and pink stem borer infestation, coinciding with increased levels of secondary metabolites, phenolics, and defense-associated proteins [58].

In sum, Si acts as a multifaceted modulator of biotic stress tolerance by reinforcing structural barriers, activating enzymatic defenses, priming hormonal signaling networks, and thwarting pathogen suppression strategies. These cumulative effects suggest that Si applicationparticularly in the form of nano-silicon formulationsholds promise as a sustainable, non-toxic strategy for integrated pest and disease management in crops. Si plays an intricate but crucial role in helping plants resist biotic stress through the multiple phytohormone signaling pathways. The activation of defense phytohormone signaling occurs via recognition of conserved molecular patterns, which vary depending on the pathogen- and herbivore-associated molecular patterns. Vivancos et al. hypothesized that the deposition of Si in the plant apoplast might interfere with biotrophic pathogen effectors reaching their target sites, thereby precluding the pathogen from hampering the defense response [57] (Fig. 5). Thus, Si potentially overcomes plant defense suppression, allowing a full defense response to biotic threats and reduces oxidative damage to the plant by enhancing antioxidant metabolism and/or ROS-mediated defense responses [44]. Fauteux et al. reported that defense genes are up-regulated, and genes linked to primary metabolites are down-regulated during pathogen attacks on plant systems [59]. However, Si application maintained the expression of these down-regulated genes ensuring sustained metabolic functions [59].

Role of nanoscale-Si in alleviating stress

Nanoscale silicon (SiNPs) has emerged as a promising tool for mitigating plant stress and enhancing agricultural productivity. Compared to bulk or other metal (loid) nanoparticles, SiNPs offer distinct physicochemical advantages, including a high surface-to-volume ratio, tunable pore structure, and ease of surface modification, which enable efficient delivery and improved plant responses [60]. While SiNPs enhance growth and stress tolerance, their molecular mechanisms—particularly gene regulation, signalling, and interactions with rhizospheremicrobiota—remain underexplored. Although green synthesis approaches provide eco-friendly alternatives, challenges such as low yield, variable morphology, and poor reproducibility persist. Moreover, their potential phytotoxicity at high concentrations highlights the need for careful evaluation before large-scale application. SiNPs can be synthesized using physical, chemical, or biological methods. Common chemical approaches include the sol–gel method, microemulsion, and chemical vapor deposition, which allow precise control over particle size and shape. Physical methods, such as laser ablation and ball milling, are less commonly used but are effective for bulk production [61]. Green synthesis, an emerging eco-friendly approach, utilizes plant extracts, agricultural waste, or microbial cultures to produce SiNPs in a cost-effective and non-toxic manner. Each method influences the morphology, surface properties, and functionality of the resulting nanoparticles [61]. Machine-learning (ML) offers a powerful tool for accelerating nanoparticle synthesis by predicting outcomes and optimizing experimental conditions [62]. It enables the fabrication of nanoparticles with controlled properties—such as size, shape, and yield—more efficiently and cost-effectively [63]. ML algorithms like linear regression, decision trees, support vector machines, and neural networks are used for prediction tasks, while Bayesian optimization and SNOBFIT assist in planning experiments [63]. Although ML has been applied to synthesize various nanoparticles, its use in SiNPs synthesis, particularly via green biological methods, remains unexplored. Integrating ML with biological synthesis could enhance reproducibility, yield, and customization of SiNPs, supporting sustainable agricultural applications.

SiNPs are typically stored in powder or colloidal forms and are characterized by their shape, size, mass, and crystallinity [64]. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) is used for particles larger than 100 nm, while Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) is preferred for those smaller than 100 nm. Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) is commonly employed to determine particle size distribution. Particle mass can be calculated using known size and density, often measured by Small Angle X-ray Scattering (SAXS) or Differential Centrifugal Sedimentation (DCS), while techniques like capillary resonators offer direct single-particle mass measurements. Elemental composition is analyzed using X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS), especially to detect residual impurities, and crystallinity is assessed as a crucial structural parameter [64]. SiNPs exhibit Mie resonance of electric and magnetic dipoles within the visible light range, particularly noticeable in particles sized 100–200 nm [65]. This is typically analysed by placing individual nanoparticles on glass substrates and capturing dark field images. Their tuneable Mie scattering properties can produce vivid red–green–blue (RGB) colors [65]. Ultraviolet–Visible (UV–Vis) spectroscopy is used to assess optical properties and colloidal stability, which depend on size, shape, concentration, aggregation, and refractive index. Fourier-Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy provides information on bond strength, functional groups, and molecular interactions. When particle size is reduced to the nanoscale, quantum confinement leads to tuneable photoluminescence, with quantum yields reaching up to 60%. A blue shift in photoluminescence occurs with decreasing size. Small Angle Neutron Scattering (SANS) further provides insights into particle size and internal surface area, useful for analysing porous or chemically modified nanoparticles [65].

SiNP ‘s unique physiochemical property makes it highly promising for applications in stress mitigation, seed priming, and plant growth improvement (Fig. 6).Shoukat et al.,used nano-fertilizers (nano-Zn/nano-Si) on maize under salt stress conditions [46]. They found a significant development of metabolic profiles, including sugar metabolism, and improved nutrient uptake levels in maize. Nano-fertilizers are most effective in reducing sodium accretion under salinity conditions and enhancing salt stress sufferance. Under a variety of stress conditions, plants generate natural mineralized nano-materials for proper growth and development. With the advancement in science, the priming of seeds with nanoparticles has revolutionized the agricultural field. Seeds coated with Si can help provide tolerance to different stresses in the plant [66]. Suriyaprabha et al., reported that in maize, the addition of Si enhanced organic compounds such as proteins, chlorophyll, and phenols as well as plant dry weight [67]. Increased seed germination, mean germination time, seed germination index, seed vigor index, seedling fresh and dry weight were observed in a study conducted with tomatoes by Siddiqui et al., [68]. In the same line of work, adding nSiO2 in the nutrient media improved seed germination and early seedling growth of lentil plants under salinity stress [69]. In basil (Ocimumbasilicum), nSiO2 alleviated the effects of salinity stress and increased leaf dry and fresh weights, chlorophyll content, and proline content [70]. The application of nSiO2 on Viciafaba improved germination characteristics when exposed to salinity stress. Thus, it can be concluded that nSiO2 application in Viciafaba can increase the relative water content, plant height, and fresh and dry weights under salinity stress conditions [71].

Role of silica nanoparticles in abiotic and biotic stress elimination

Nano-silicon can also be used to alleviate seed germination and plant growth inhibition due to the salt stress and improve photosynthetic quantum in tomatoes [72]. Almutairi reported that withnano-silicon application in tomatoes under salt stress, four salt-stress- up-regulated key stress-responsive genes included AREB, TAS14, NCED3, and CRK1 while down-regulated genes included RBOH1, APX2, MAPK2, ERF5, MAPK3, and DDF2.Nanoparticles (nSiO2) and nano-molybdenum (nMo)) are commonly used as engineered oxide nanoparticles. The effect of nSiO2 and nMo particles were studied on rice germination [73]. Uptake of both nanoparticles were observed in the rice seedlings. Nano-Mo particles caused the arrest of root growth and elongation in rice, whereas nSiO2 had no toxic effect on rice. However, nSiO2 enhanced the root length and volume, and dry matter weight of the shoot and root of rice [73]. Applying specific nanoparticles on plants directly might have positive and negative effects on plant growth and development. Potassium silicate alone or in combination with SA can provide tolerance to plants against salinity stress [45]. The direct and indirect role of nSiO2 in improving plant growth under different stressconditions makes it a sustainable alternative to harmful fertilizers (Table 4) [74].

Accumulation of nano-silicon in different parts of plants

Studies on SiO₂ nanoparticles (NPs) interactions with plants often use cell suspensions with or without cell walls. The plant cell wall, with pore sizes of 5–20 nm, serves as the primary barrier to NP entry. However, larger SiO₂NPs (e.g., 48 nm and 202 nm) have been observed to accumulate in rice suspension cells, though the exact entry mechanism remains unclear. Once inside, NPs may be internalized via endocytosis, penetrate the plasma membrane directly, or localize in the cytoplasm or organelles such as the nucleus, vacuoles, and chloroplasts. SiO₂NPs may also release silicic acid before or after cell entry, further complicating studies on their cellular accumulation and transport mechanisms. Seed priming with SiO₂NPs can enhance metabolic activities, improve germination rates, and increase plant mechanical stability by thickening the silicon-cellulose layer in the epidermis [75]. Higher Si concentrations in shoots and roots have been observed with increasing SiO₂NP exposure during seed soaking, though the source of detected Si (NPs or released silicic acid) remains unclear. SiO₂NPs can rupture seed coats, allowing NP entry, similar to scarification. SiO2NPs may also enter seeds through imbibed water and be transported from roots to buds during growth, as demonstrated by SEM imaging. Further studies are needed to investigate potential accumulation in fruits.

Plant leaves uptake SiO₂NPs through trichomes, stomata, and potentially hydathodes. Trichomes, glandular and non-glandular appendages of the epidermis, can accumulate NPs, with glandular types having a secretory role that may assist in detoxification. Stomata are the primary confirmed entry route for SiO₂NPs, enabling their adsorption onto the spongy mesophyll layer [76]. Hydathodes, though unexplored for SiO₂NPs, have been shown to facilitate the entry of other NPs like CeO₂. Wounds and crevices on leaves may also provide entry points for NP absorption.Root uptake occurs via adsorption on root hairs and through wounds caused by environmental stress. Thin and permeable root hair walls make them more amenable to NP absorption compared to other plant structures.

Anatomical differences affecting NP accumulation

Distinct anatomical features in monocotyledon and dicotyledonous leaves influence SiO₂NP uptake and distribution:

Stomata density and structure: Maize (monocotyledon) has dumbbell-shaped stomata with a lower density (34.3 ± 4.6 mm⁻2) compared to cotton (dicotyledon) with kidney-shaped stomata (258.4 ± 32.2 mm⁻2).

Mesophyll cell arrangement: Tightly packed mesophyll cells in maize leave minimal extracellular air spaces, whereas the spongy mesophyll in cotton leaves creates large air spaces, facilitating better NP penetration. SiO₂NPs (18 nm) penetrated cotton leaves but not maize.

Toxicity of silicon nanoparticles and its cross-talks with other nutrients (Fe, Zn, and P)homeostasis under stress

While silicon nanoparticles (SiNPs) are increasingly explored for their stress-mitigating properties in plants, emerging evidence underscores the importance of evaluating their potential phytotoxicity. Recent reviews highlight that SiNPs may induce oxidative stress, disrupt redox balance, and alter gene expression if applied at inappropriate dosages or physicochemical profiles [77]. Moreover, the environmental and ecological safety of SiNPs remains underexplored. It ‘s been noted that nanoparticles, depending on size, surface chemistry, and shape, can evoke cytotoxic effects such as mitochondrial dysfunction, lysosomal destabilization, and DNA damage in non-plant systems, pointing to possible broader ecosystem concerns [78]. These findings underscore the need for disciplined investigation into dose thresholds, nanoparticle design, and systematic environmental impact assessments to ensure that the benefits of SiNPs in agriculture are not overshadowed by unintended toxic effects in plant or surrounding systems.

Beyond toxicity, SiNPs appear to influence nutrient homeostasis, particularly involving Fe, Zn, and P nutrients that often display complex interactions. Although direct studies of SiNPs mediating Fe–Zn–P cross-talk remain limited, insights from bulk-Si research offer clues: silicon supplementation has been shown to mitigate cadmium-induced nutrient imbalance by promoting uptake and utilization of essential minerals including Zn, Fe, and P, thereby alleviating toxicity and improving plant growth [79, 80]. Separately, plant nutrition studies emphasize that P, Zn, and Fe homeostasis are tightly intertwinedphosphorus deficiency can lead to Zn overaccumulation, and imbalances between Fe and Zn are well-documented in plant tissues [81]. By analogy, SiNPs might modulate these nutrient dynamics under stress by influencing transporter expression, chelation processes, or root exudate profiles to rebalance nutrient uptake, especially in environments with elevated heavy metal or salinity pressures. Nevertheless, targeted experiments are needed to validate these interactions and clarify how SiNPs reshape micronutrient equilibrium in stressed plants.

Interaction of Si with other metal ions under stress

Si plays a crucial role in mitigating ion imbalances and alleviating plant stress. Under high salinity conditions, it reduces the accumulation of Na + ions, allowing tolerance and recovery from salt-induced damage. Besides Na + , other ionsare also strongly affected by salinity stress, with their uptake greatly reduced. Several studies have established a positive correlation between Si levels and enhanced K + uptake, thereby helping in maintaining the ion stability under salinity stress. Some researchers noted a co-relationship of Na + ion and Si in spinach, tomato, and maize [88, 89]. Swain and Rout observed a substantial absorption of sodium ions in the shoot of rice seedlings under sodium chloride treatment compared to the non-saline situation [38].

Plants also accumulateSi to increase their protection and counter the entry of toxic ions through the root apoplast. Saja-Garbarz et al., observed that Si absorption in the root was verylimitedbut improved relative water content in the leaves compared to the controlplants [45]. They found that the accumulation of BnPIP1, BnPIP2-1–7, and BnTIP1 aquaporins in oilseed roots decreased under drought compared to irrigated plants [45]. Si augmentation in oilseed rape plants helps improve metabolic activity in the roots under drought conditions. They noticed that Si enhances water balance in oilseed rape plants and is recreated by tonoplastaquaporins. The inclusion of Si with other plant nutrients assists in enhancing plant resistance to different environmental stresses. The Mechanical Resistance Hypothesis (MRH) assumes that Si stored in the epidermal cells safeguards plants against insects and herbivores by forming a mechanical barrier that impedes their feeding habits and survival [89]. It has also been reported that Si regulates the protection mechanism by waving secondary products, including toxins, inducing the signal transduction route, and exciting the herbivore-induced plant volatiles. Plants ‘volatiles help repress pest growth. Si is one of the beneficial and inducing resistances against stresses, improved nutrient balance, depletion of heavy metal toxicity, and photosynthetic activity.

Role of Si on membrane stability indices under stress

The cell ‘s electrolyte leakage potential (ELP), level of membrane permeability, oxidative stress, and lipid peroxidation (LPO) are considered cellular damage indicators under salinity stress. ELP monitored the amount of membrane impairment and morality in a cell, and salt stress directly accelerated the cell ‘s damage by enhanced ELP. Supplementation of Si helps to reduce membrane damage due to solute leakage in cells. Si treatment in saline solution significantly reduced ELP levels from 92.92 to 66.99% in rice genotype ‘Ghanteswari ‘ and from 95.34 to 91.93% in ‘Badami ‘. So, the dominant Si accumulating genotype showed a higher reduction in ELP than the low- accumulating one. Under stress and control, the rice genotypes exhibited a decrease in cell membrane damage. The inequality in ELP leads to severe lipid peroxidation and salt stress, resulting in the overproduction of ROS (Reactive Oxygen Species) and damage to DNA, protein, and lipids, as reported byManivannan et al,. [40, 89].Thus, mitigating LPO and regulating antioxidant activity is essential. El-Beltagi et al. ,assessed the possible mechanism of Si and Put in mitigating the harmful effects of drought on maize [47]. They observed that the integrated approach of Si and Put increased the accumulation of osmolyte contents. The combined foliar application of Si and Put has considerably improved the yield contributing attributes under drought stress. They concluded that an integrated foliar application at the reproductive phase effectively increases the crop yield in drought-prone areas [47].

Role of Si onantioxidant activity under stress

Stress generally reduces soluble protein levels and antioxidant enzyme activity in plants. Under natural conditions, plants constantly generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) as by-products of photosynthesis and respiration in organelles such as chloroplasts, mitochondria, and peroxisomes. To maintain cellular redox homeostasis, plants employ two major ROS detoxification systems: enzymatic antioxidantsincluding superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX); and non-enzymatic antioxidants, such as ascorbate, glutathione, carotenoids, and α-tocopherol [90]. SOD serves as the first line of defence, converting superoxide radicals (O₂⁻) into hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂). It exists in multiple isoforms based on metal cofactors (Cu/Zn-SOD, Mn-SOD, Fe-SOD) and is localizedin the chloroplasts, cytosol, and mitochondria. CAT, located mainly in peroxisomes, decomposes H₂O₂ into water and oxygen. APX also breaks down H₂O₂ and is found in multiple cellular compartments—chloroplasts (sAPX, tAPX), mitochondria (mitAPX), cytosol (cAPX), and peroxisomes (mAPX)—reflecting its diverse role in ROS scavenging [90].

In addition to enzymatic detoxification, non-enzymatic antioxidants also play a crucial role. Ascorbate is a key water-soluble antioxidant that directly neutralizes ROS and also regenerates other antioxidants. Glutathione aids in ROS scavenging, supports enzyme thiol-group protection, and regulates stress-responsive gene expression. Carotenoids and α-tocopherol are lipid-soluble antioxidants that prevent membrane lipid peroxidation by scavenging singlet oxygen and other radicals [90].

Ashraf and Harris noted substantial differences in the preventive mechanism against reactive oxygen species in various plant species underneath salt tolerance [48]. Dionisio-sese and Tobita studied the SOD and POD activity in rice under salt tolerance and obtained variable responses. They observed that Si-induced variability exists in ROS reduction for major antioxidant enzyme activity (SOD, CAT, POD) [91]. Si supplementation has been widely shown to reduce oxidative damage by enhancing the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, APX, CAT, andPOD under drought stress in various crops including wheat, sunflower, tomato, and chickpea. In rice, Sipretreatment up-regulated drought-responsive genes such as OsRDCP1 (a RING domain-containing protein), OsCMO (choline monooxygenase), and OsRAB16b (a dehydrin), indicating its role in boosting stress tolerance at both enzymatic and molecular levels [92]. Khan and Panda reported that there is a positive correlation of SOD, POD, CAT, and GR with salt tolerance in rice genotypes [93]. The salt-sensitive genotypes exhibited a decrease in superoxide dismutase activities, as reported by Zhu et al. [89].

Si treatment underneath saline and non-saline situations diminished the improved level of CAT. In the salt-tolerant genotype, the POD activity was decreased under stress. Thus, Si significantly impacts abiotic stresses by modulating antioxidant compounds and ROS response. In general, the levels of ROS are maintained under homeostasis through antioxidant molecules. Supplementation of Si improves plant salt stress tolerance across its regulatory gene expression, hormonal equilibrium, physiological responses, and signallingpathways. One of the hypotheses supported that plants use Si as a structural ingredient to increase their vigor and severity and protect them from pathogens. It is an alternative to lignin biosynthesis and up-regulation.

SiNP–apoplast interactions and stress regulation

Silicon nanoparticles (SiNPs) reshape plant stress responses by interacting with apoplastic proteins and forming dynamic protein coronas [94]. Large SiNPs, which cannot enter the symplast, primarily act in the apoplast by binding key proteins and regulating processes such as cell wall remodelling, hormone and ROS signalling, and pathogen defense. Their interactions with apoplastic enzymes, PR proteins, and signalling peptides affect redox balance, salicylic acid synthesis, and stress responses [94]. By adsorbing antioxidant enzymes, SiNPs raise ROS levels, indirectly enhancing intracellular redox buffering and SA-dependent defenses, whereas smaller particles may cause oxidative stress. SiNPs also appear to influence heavy metal transport and systemic immunity through redox-sensitive transporters and protein interactions [94]. A key hypothesis is that SiNPs increase apoplastic ROS levels by binding and limiting antioxidant enzymes, which in turn enhances redox buffering and activates salicylic acid (SA)–mediated defences. While larger SiNPs remain extracellular, smaller ones may enter cells and disrupt antioxidant balance, heightening oxidative stress [96]. SiNPs may also alter metal transporter activity through redox regulation and broadly influence physiological functions via transcriptional networks. However, little is known about how protein coronas shift under different environmental conditions or their long-term, cross-generational effects [94].

Role of Si on soil microbiota

The rhizosphere is a vital soil zone influenced by plant root exudates and microbial communities, comprising archaea, viruses, fungi, bacteria, and eukaryotic microorganisms. Approximately 1011 microbial cells per gram of root inhabit this region, contributing to plant health and nutrient cycling. Beneficial microbes such as phosphate-solubilizing bacteria (PSB), nitrogen-fixing microbes, and silicate-solubilizing bacteria (SSB) enhance soil fertility and detoxify pollutants. Si-NP applications have been shown to increase PSB populations (3.8 × 104 CFU g⁻1) without affecting SSB, and to improve maize (Z.mays) growth by enhancing microbial biomass and nutrient availability. They also enrich bacterial genera such as Paenibacillus (16% higher abundance) and fungal genera like Chaetomium, promoting nitrogen fixation and plant growth. Foliar exposure to SiO₂NPs significantly influences rhizosphere metabolite profiles, increasing sugars, fatty acids, and organic acids, which contribute to carbon and nitrogen cycling. In Escherichia coli K12 TG1, genetically modified with cloned luxCDABE genes from Photobacteriumleiognathi 54D10, SiO₂NPs showed no observable toxicity, with experimental doses categorized as only slightly toxic. However, a marked contrast was observed in the earthworm Eiseniafetida, which exhibited significant biochemical responses. Specifically, changes in antioxidant enzyme activity and protein content indicated oxidative stress, likely resulting from disturbances in intestinal microbial communities following nanoparticle exposure.

In the plant model Triticumaestivum (wheat), initial exposure to SiO₂NPs induced mild depressive effects on growth, yet by the experiment ‘s conclusion, notable stimulation of both shoot and root development was recorded [95]. Soil microbial responses revealed a decline in oligotrophic bacteria and microscopic fungi populations upon exposure to SiO₂NPs [95]. Interestingly, the shifts in microbial community structure and function were more strongly influenced by the presence of earthworms or plants than by the nanoparticles alone. Furthermore, a consistent reduction in the activity of soil enzymesparticularly hydrolases and oxidoreductaseswas observed across all treatments [95].In another study SiO₂ NPs enhance plant resistance to soil-borne diseases such as Fusarium root rot by strengthening cell walls, activating defense enzymes, and stimulating antimicrobial compounds [96]. The study revealed that SiO₂ NPs increased the diversity of rhizosphere microorganisms, enriched beneficial taxa (e.g., Lysobacter, Sphingobium), and reduced pathogen-associated bacteria (Pseudomonas, Microbacterium), thereby suppressing synergistic pathogen–microbe interactions [96]. These shifts were accompanied by enhanced amino acid metabolism. Additionally, SiO₂ NPs altered root exudate composition by increasing organic acids, lipids, phenylpropanoids, and flavonoids (e.g., naringenin, sinapyl alcohol), reinforces the cell walls and improved antifungal defense. Hormonal regulation, activates SAR and antioxidant enzyme activity (APX, CAT, POD), reducing oxidative damage.Overall, SiO₂ NPs improved Astragalus resistance to Fusarium root rot through physical reinforcement, SA-dependent signaling, antioxidant responses, and beneficial rhizosphereremodelling [96]. Although promising for sustainable disease management, further field studies are needed to validate their long-term environmental and agricultural impacts.Collectively, these findings underscore the potential utility of Eiseniafetida and Triticumaestivum as reliable bioindicators for evaluating the ecological impact of SiO₂NPs. They also emphasize the importance of long-term, field-based investigations to fully understand the implications of SiO₂NP exposure on biotic components, microbial ecosystems, and nutrient cycling processes in terrestrial environments.

However, oversaturation of NPs, including mixtures of Cu, Ag, and Si, has been linked to reduced microbial enzymatic activities and altered microbial community structure. Applying Si-NPs highlights the importance of soil metabolomics for evaluating microbial metabolic status and soil health. Further research is essential to optimize Si-NP usage for sustainable crop productivity without adverse ecological impacts.

Role of Si in disease resistance and as pesticide carrier in plant protection

Si contributes to plant defence through both direct and indirect mechanisms. Directly, Si is deposited as a polymerized layer beneath the cuticle and within the cell walls of epidermal cells, forming a physical barrier that limits pathogen penetration [97]. This structural reinforcement reduces the number and size of disease lesions and enhances the mechanical strength of plant tissues, thereby providing an effective deterrent against pathogen ingress [97]. Indirectly, Si enhances the plant ‘s innate immune responses. Microscopic studies have revealed that in Si-treated cucumber plants challenged with Podosphaeraxanthii, numerous leaf cells exhibited rapid accumulation of phenolic compounds—an indicator of activated defense pathways. This suggests that Si acts as a modulator, priming the plant ‘s defense system and enabling a more rapid and robust biochemical response upon pathogen attack [98]. Thus, Si not only strengthens physical structures but also boosts physiological responses to stress, contributing comprehensively to disease resistance.

SiNPs are emerging as smart carriers for pesticides due to their porous structure, tunable surface chemistry, and biocompatibility [60]. Compared with direct spraying, SiNP-based delivery enhances pesticide uptake, prolongs field persistence, reduces residues, and enables controlled release [60].Indoxacarb-loaded SiNPs outperformed commercial formulations against Plutellaxylostella [99]. Similarly, pectin-coated SiNPs carrying prochloraz improved systemic movement and antifungal activity in rice [100]. Functionalized carriers (e.g., α-cyclodextrin, copper, chitosan) enhanced stability and translocation of avermectin and azoxystrobin [101].Recent advances allow pH-, temperature-, or enzyme-responsive release, aligning pesticide activity with plant or pest physiology. SiNP carriers reduce pesticide residues in edible parts, as shown for prochloraz and spirotetramat in cucumber [102]. Additionally, SiNPs can adsorb or catalyze the degradation of organophosphates and phenols, highlighting potential roles in environmental remediation.

Conclusion and future prospect

Silicon (Si) is increasingly recognized as a phyto-beneficial element that mitigates the adverse effects of biotic and abiotic stressors while promoting plant growth and development. Applying Si in forms such as calcium silicate or silicic acid has enhanced seed germination, improved vegetative biomass, and stimulated physiological processes including photosynthetic efficiency and chlorophyll synthesis. Si-mediated seed priming has demonstrated significant potential in ameliorating salinity-induced stress by reducing oxidative damage, increasing dry matter accumulation, and modulating pigment systems I and II. This is often accompanied by a decline in stress biomarkers such as hydrogen peroxide and malondialdehyde (MDA), indicating enhanced oxidative stress tolerance. Beyond physiological benefits, recent studies have highlighted the involvement of Si in activating plant defence mechanisms, modulating gene expression, and influencing regulatory pathways under stress conditions. Si accumulation is also genotype-dependent; for instance, differential uptake among rice cultivars has been correlated with varying degrees of resistance to abiotic stresses such as salinity, drought, and cold and enhanced defense against biotic agents. A comprehensive understanding of Si ‘s mode of action must integrate its influence on plant physiological traits and its interactions with stress-response pathways. The regulatory impact of Si on gene expression—particularly the up-regulation and down-regulation of key signalling and metabolic networks—requires deeper investigation through multi-omics approaches, including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics. These insights will be instrumental in designing crop improvement programs to enhance stress resilience across diverse agro ecosystems. The advent of nano-silicon (SiNPs) formulations has opened new avenues for enhancing Si bioavailability and targeted delivery under diverse environmental conditions. SiNPs have already shown significant promise as nano-fertilizers, improving salt tolerance, nutrient absorption, and water-use efficiency, while also limiting the uptake and translocation of toxic metals. Importantly, SiNPs reduce oxidative stress and promote safer food production in contaminated soils, making them particularly relevant to sustainable agriculture. However, despite these advances, critical gaps remain. A clearer understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying SiNP action is needed to explain how nanoparticles interact with cellular signalling cascades and stress-gene networks. Optimizing nano-formulations tailored to specific crop species, including modifications of particle size, surface chemistry, and delivery systems, will further maximize their agronomic efficacy. Equally important is the assessment of long-term ecological safety, as the persistence and interactions of SiNPs in soil–plant–microbe systems are not yet fully understood. Looking ahead, the successful integration of Si and SiNPs into agricultural practice will depend not only on scientific innovation but also on knowledge transfer. Farmer awareness, extension activities, and capacity-building initiatives are vital to ensuring safe, effective, and widespread adoption. Embedding Si-based technologies into sustainable agricultural frameworks—such as precision nutrient management and stress-resilient cropping systems—holds immense potential for addressing the intertwined challenges of food security, climate resilience, and agroecological sustainability.
