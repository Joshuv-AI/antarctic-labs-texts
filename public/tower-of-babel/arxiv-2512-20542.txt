Reciprocity For Dedekind Sums via Conical Zeta Values
Yerko Torres-Nova

Abstract
. We study reciprocity formulas for Dedekind sums associated with absolutely continuous functions, extending the classical Dedekind-Rademacher reciprocity formula. In particular, we treat the case of periodic Bernoulli functions. Our approach generalizes an integral method and uses Fourier analysis to show that the reciprocity for polynomial-type functions admits a geometric interpretation in terms of conical zeta values.



Keywords:

Dedekind Sums, Reciprocity, Conical Zeta Values, Bernoulli Polynomials.

MSC2020:

11F20, 11B68, 11M32

1.
Introduction

Given positive integers
\nu_{0},\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
, the classical Dedekind sum is defined as

S(\nu_{1},\nu_{2}|\nu_{0}):=\sum_{i=1}^{\nu_{0}-1}b_{1}\left(\frac{i\nu_{1}}{\nu_{0}}\right)b_{1}\left(\frac{i\nu_{2}}{\nu_{0}}\right),

where
b_{1}(x)
is the
first periodic Bernoulli function
, i.e.,

b_{1}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}},\quad b_{1}(x)=\{x\}-1/2,

and
\{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor
denotes the fractional part of
x
. The Dedekind-Rademacher reciprocity theorem
[
14
,
13
]
asserts that if
\nu_{0},\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
are pairwise coprime positive integers, then

(1)

{\mathcal{R}}_{b_{1}}(\bm{\nu}):=S(\nu_{1},\nu_{2}|\nu_{0})+S(\nu_{0},\nu_{2}|\nu_{1})+S(\nu_{1},\nu_{0}|\nu_{2})=\frac{\nu_{0}^{2}+\nu_{1}^{2}+\nu_{2}^{2}}{12\nu_{0}\nu_{1}\nu_{2}}-\frac{1}{4}.

This reciprocity law is fundamental in many areas and provides techniques that link arithmetic, geometry, and topology (see, for instance,
[
11
]
). Due to its significance, numerous approaches have been developed to study its nature and to derive generalizations in various directions
[
6
,
17
]
. For our purposes, a key example arises by replacing the function
b_{1}(x)
with higher-degree periodic Bernoulli functions,

b_{q}(x):=B_{q}(\{x\})

where
B_{q}(x)
denotes the
q
-th Bernoulli polynomial
[
2
,
10
]
. In this paper, we study the reciprocity phenomenon from a general perspective. Our main objects of study are the
generalized Dedekind sums
defined by

S_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{\nu_{k}-1}\prod_{j\neq k}f_{j}\left(\frac{i\nu_{j}}{\nu_{k}}\right),\quad k=0,\ldots,r,

for a vector of distinct nonzero positive integers
\bm{\nu}=(\nu_{0},\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{r})
pairwise coprime, and
\mathbf{f}=(f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{r})\in{\mathcal{S}}_{1}^{r+1}
where

{\mathcal{S}}_{1}:=\left\{f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{C}}:\begin{array}[]{c}\text{$1$-periodic},\\
\text{discontinuities at most in }{\mathbb{Z}},\\
\text{absolutely continuous on }(0,1).\end{array}\right\}.

In
Section

2
we review the basic facts on integration, Dedekind sums, and conical zeta values. In
Section

3
, we introduce our reciprocity symbol
{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\nu})
. Using integration by parts, we obtain an
integral reciprocity formula
. As was observed in
[
13
]
, this gives
Equation

1
in a clean way. The integral reciprocity formula can be reformulated in terms of Fourier analysis. Since every function in
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
admits a Fourier series, we derive a
Fourier reciprocity formula
, which connects the theory to polyhedral geometry. We end the section by applying this Fourier reciprocity to the case where the functions
f_{j}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
arise from polynomials of degree
q_{j}\geq 1
(
Section

3.3
). In this setting, reciprocity is closely related to a class of polyhedral zeta values
\zeta_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}
defined on the lattice
\bm{\nu}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{\perp}
of integer vectors orthogonal to
\bm{\nu}
, where
\mathbf{q}=(q_{0},q_{1},\ldots,q_{r})
is a vector of positive integers. These values provide an explicit reciprocity formula for the Dedekind sums

S_{b_{\mathbf{q}}}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k}):=S_{(b_{q_{0}}\ldots b_{q_{r}})}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})

coming from the periodic Bernoulli functions (
Equation

12
). In
Section

4
, we study a two-step decomposition of
\zeta_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}
. First, we establish convergence by comparison with another type of zeta values, denoted
\zeta_{\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}
. The key point is that
\zeta_{\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}
converges for all
\mathbf{q}
and controls the first decomposition. Next, to simplify the situation, we decompose according to the integer orthants of
{\mathbb{R}}^{r+1}
, which leads to a description in terms of conical zeta values
[
3
,
9
,
15
]
. This enables us to use desingularization of the cones associated to
\bm{\nu}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{\perp}
and express the reciprocity formula in terms of conical zeta values of unimodular cones. In
Section

5
, we specialize to the case
r=2
, and use the well-known Hirzebruch-Jung algorithm
[
7
, Ch.10]
, also known as the reversed Euclidean algorithm, to resolve planar clones. This algorithm is used to derive a reciprocity formula for the Dedekind sums
S_{b_{\mathbf{q}}}
from conical zeta values associated with unimodular cones for each
\mathbf{q}
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Riemann-Stieltjes Integration

Let
f:[a,b]\to{\mathbb{C}}
be a function, and
P=\{t_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}:a=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n}=b\}
a
partition
of the interval
[a,b]
. The
variation
of
f
over
P
is the quantity,

V(f,P):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|f(t_{i})-f(t_{i-1})|.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{a}^{b}
be the set of all partitions of
[a,b]
. We say that
f
is of
bounded variation
on
[a,b]
if

V_{a}^{b}f=\sup_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{a}^{b}}V(f,P)<\infty.

There is a well-known classification: a function
f
is of bounded variation if and only if
\mbox{Re}(f)
and
\mbox{Im}(f)
can be written as the difference between two increasing functions. As a consequence, functions of bounded variation are differentiable almost everywhere and have at most countably many discontinuities. Thus, we have well-defined two-sided limits,

f(x_{0}^{+}):=\lim_{x\to x_{0}^{+}}f(x),\quad f(x_{0}^{-}):=\lim_{x\to x_{0}^{-}}f(x).

The relevance of functions of bounded variation is that they establish a criterion for guaranteeing the existence of the Riemann-Stieltjes integral and the integration by parts formula. Explicitly, set
|P|:=\max\{|t_{i}-t_{i-1}|:i=1,\ldots,n\}
for any
P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{a}^{b}
. If
f,g:[a,b]\to{\mathbb{C}}
are functions of bounded variation and have no common discontinuities, then the limits

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dg(x)=\lim_{|P|\to 0}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})(g(t_{i})-g(t_{i-1})),

\int_{a}^{b}g(x)df(x)=\lim_{|P|\to 0}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(x_{i})(f(t_{i})-f(t_{i-1})),

exists for any choice of points
x_{i}\in[t_{i-i},t_{i}]
of a partition
P=\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{a}^{b}
. The following two theorems are well-known (see, for instance,
[
5
, Ch. 6]
).

Theorem 2.1

(Integration by Parts)
.

Let
f,g,h:[a,b]\to{\mathbb{C}}
be functions of bounded variation with no common discontinuities. Then,

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)d(g(x)h(x))=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)g(x)dh(x)+\int_{a}^{b}f(x)h(x)dg(x).

∎

As a corollary, for
f(x)=1
we get the classical integration by parts formula:

g(x)h(x)\big|_{a}^{b}=g(b)h(b)-g(a)h(a)=\int_{a}^{b}g(x)dh(x)+\int_{a}^{b}h(x)dg(x).

We say that
f
is
absolutely continuous on an interval
I
if for all
\varepsilon>0
there exists a
\delta>0
such that for any collection of disjoint subintervals
(x_{1},y_{1}),...,(x_{k},y_{k})\subset I
we have

\sum_{j=1}^{k}|x_{j}-y_{j}|<\delta\implies\sum_{j=1}^{k}|f(x_{j})-f(y_{j})|<\varepsilon.

It is known that an absolutely continuous function is of bounded variation. For a function
f:[a,b]\to{\mathbb{C}}
, let
D(f)
be the set of discontinuities of
f
in
(a,b)
. Assume
D(f)
finite, we say that
f
is
pairwise absolutely continuous on
I
if
f
is absolutely continuous on each connected component of
I\setminus D(f)
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
f,g:[a,b]\to{\mathbb{R}}
be functions of bounded variation. If
D(f)\cap D(g)=\emptyset
,
D(f)
and
D(g)
are finite, and
g
is pairwise absolutely continuous on
(a,b)
, then

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dg(x)=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)g^{\prime}(x)dx+\sum_{x\in D(g)}f(x)(g(x^{+})-g(x^{-})),

where
g^{\prime}
is the derivative of
g
.

∎

2.2.
Dedekind Sums

Recall that a function
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is
periodic
if there exists a real number
\rho>0
such that

f(x+\rho)=f(x),\quad\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}.

In this case, we say that
f
is
\rho
-periodic and has
period

\rho
. A periodic function
f
is completely determined by its values in
[0,\rho)
. The open interval
(0,\rho)
is called the
fundamental domain
of
f
. We are interested in the following set of periodic functions defined by,

{\mathcal{S}}_{\rho}:=\left\{f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{C}}:\begin{array}[]{c}\text{$\rho$-periodic},\\
\text{discontinuities at most in }\rho{\mathbb{Z}},\\
\text{absolutely continuous on }(0,\rho).\end{array}\right\}.

For any function
f\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\rho}
and any positive integer
\nu
we have the identity

(2)

\int_{0}^{\rho}f(\nu x)dx=\int_{0}^{\rho}f(x)dx.

To normalize the situation, we fix all results in the sections that follow for functions
f\in{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
. Indeed, we have a bijection
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}\to{\mathcal{S}}_{\rho}
given by
f(x)\mapsto g(x):=f(\rho^{-1}x)
.

Example 2.3
.

The following functions are elements of
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
.

(1)

The normalized sine and cosine functions
\mbox{Sin}(x):=\sin(2\pi x)
and
\mbox{Cos}(x):=\cos(2\pi x)
. In particular, they have no discontinuities.

(2)

The
fractional part
given by
\{x\}:=x-\lfloor x\rfloor
. Moreover, every function
f\in{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
relates to the fractional part function by
f(\{x\})=f(x).

(3)

Let
F:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
be any absolutely continuous function. The function given by
f(x)=F(\{x\}),

belongs to
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
. If
F
is a polynomial, then we call
f
a
periodic polynomial function
.

∎

Example 2.4
.

For
\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}
, each
{\mathcal{S}}_{\nu}
has a
canonical element
given
by the
residue mod
\nu
function
defined as

\{x\}_{\nu}=x-\nu\left\lfloor\frac{x}{\nu}\right\rfloor,

for any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. This function has period
\nu
with discontinuities at
\nu{\mathbb{Z}}
, and is determined completely in the interval
[0,\nu)
. An important property is that

(3)

\frac{\{x\}_{\nu}}{\nu}=\left\{\frac{x}{\nu}\right\}.

∎

Definition 2.5
.

For
r\geq 1
, let
\mathbf{f}=(f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{r})
be a vector of functions in
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
. The
Dedekind sums
associated with this vector are defined as

S_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k}):=\sum_{i=1}^{\nu_{k}-1}\prod_{j\neq k}f_{j}\left(\frac{i\nu_{j}}{\nu_{k}}\right),

for any vector
\bm{\nu}=(\nu_{0},\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{r})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r+1}
of pairwise distinct and pairwise coprime positive integers. When
f_{j}=f
for all
j
, we simply denote
S_{f}
instead of
S_{(f,\ldots,f)}
. For
r=0
, we set by convention
S_{f_{0}}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{0})=\nu_{0}-1
.

From
Example

2.4
we obtain

S_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})=S_{\mathbf{f}}(\{\bm{\nu}\}_{\nu_{k}}|\nu_{k}),

where
\{\bm{\nu}\}_{\nu_{k}}=(\{\nu_{0}\}_{\nu_{k}},\ldots,\hat{\nu_{k}},\ldots,\{\nu_{r}\}_{\nu_{k}}).
Thus, for each fixed
\nu_{k}
, we may restrict us to integers
0<\nu_{j}<\nu_{k}
for
j\neq k
.

Example 2.6
.

Important sums of general interest are the Dedekind sums associated with the fractional part

S_{\{x\}}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k}):=\sum_{i=1}^{\nu_{k}-1}\prod_{j\neq k}\left\{\frac{i\nu_{j}}{\nu_{k}}\right\}.

Using
Equation

3
, these sums satisfy the identity

\nu_{k}^{r}S_{\{x\}}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{\nu_{k}-1}\prod_{j\neq k}\left\{i\nu_{j}\right\}_{\nu_{k}}.

2.3.
Conical Zeta Values

Let
C={\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\mathbf{v}_{1}+\ldots+{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\mathbf{v}_{s}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{r}
be a polyhedral rational cone. For any
\mathbf{q}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{r}
we define its
conical zeta value
as

\zeta(\mathbf{q},C):=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in C\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}}\frac{1}{\mathbf{n}^{\mathbf{q}}},\quad\frac{1}{\mathbf{n}^{\mathbf{q}}}:=\prod_{j=1}^{r}n_{j}^{-q_{j}},

where by convention
0^{0}=1
and
0^{-q_{j}}=0
when
q_{j}\geq 1
. The
reduced conical zeta value
is defined by

\zeta^{*}(\mathbf{q},C):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{n}\in C\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}\\
\gcd(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})=1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\mathbf{n}^{\mathbf{q}}}.

Both forms are related by

(4)

\zeta(\mathbf{q},C)=\zeta(|\mathbf{q}|)\zeta^{*}(\mathbf{q},C)

where we assume
|\mathbf{q}|:=q_{1}+\ldots+q_{r}>1
.

Remark 2.7
.

For a
1
-dimensional cone
C={\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\mathbf{v}
generated by a primitive integer vector
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r})
we get

\zeta(\mathbf{q},C)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\prod_{j=1}^{r}\frac{1}{(v_{j}n)^{q_{j}}}=\zeta(|\mathbf{q}|)\prod_{j=1}^{r}v_{j}^{-q_{j}},

for
\mathbf{q}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r}
.

Example 2.8
.

For the standard positive cone
C={\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\ldots+{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq}\mathbf{e}_{r}
generated by the canonical basis
\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{r}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{r}
, we have explicitly

\zeta(\mathbf{q},C)=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r}}\frac{1}{n_{1}^{q_{1}}\ldots n_{r}^{q_{r}}}=\sum_{n_{1}\geq 1}\frac{1}{n_{1}^{q_{1}}}\ldots\sum_{n_{r}\geq 1}\frac{1}{n_{r}^{q_{r}}}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\zeta(q_{j}),

when
q_{j}>1
for all
j
. We can write

\zeta^{*}(\mathbf{q},C)=\frac{1}{\zeta(|\mathbf{q}|)}\prod_{j=1}^{r}\zeta(q_{j}).

3.
General Reciprocity Formulas

3.1.
The Integral Reciprocity

For a vector of functions
\mathbf{f}=(f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}),f_{j}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
define,

\Delta f_{j}:=f_{j}(1^{-})-f_{j}(0^{+})=f_{j}(i^{-})-f_{j}(i^{+}),

for each
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}
by periodicity, and

\Delta\mathbf{f}:=\left(\prod_{j=0}^{r}f_{j}(1^{-})-\prod_{j=0}^{r}f_{j}(0^{+})\right).

The
reciprocity of
\mathbf{f}
over any
\bm{\nu}=(\nu_{0},\ldots,\nu_{r})
is defined as

{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\nu}):=\sum_{k=0}^{r}S_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})\Delta f_{k}

where we reserve the notation
{\mathcal{R}}_{f}(\bm{\nu})
for the case
\mathbf{f}=(f,\ldots,f)
. Note that if the functions
f_{j}
are continuous for all
j
, then
{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\nu})=0
. The following is what we refer to as the
integral reciprocity theorem
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\bm{\nu}=(\nu_{0},\ldots,\nu_{r})
be any vector of pairwise distinct and pairwise coprime positive integers. We have,

{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\nu})=-\Delta\mathbf{f}+\sum_{k=0}^{r}\nu_{k}\int_{0}^{1}f_{k}^{\prime}(\nu_{k}x)\prod_{j\neq k}f_{j}(\nu_{j}x)dx.

Proof.

Fix a small
\varepsilon>0
, and define

I_{\varepsilon}:=\int_{\varepsilon}^{1-\varepsilon}d\left(\prod_{j=0}^{r}f_{j}(\nu_{j}x)\right)=\prod_{j=0}^{r}f_{j}(\nu_{j}(1-\varepsilon))-\prod_{j=0}^{r}f_{j}(\nu_{j}\varepsilon).

So, observe that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}I_{\varepsilon}=\left(\prod_{j=0}^{r}f_{j}(1^{-})-\prod_{j=0}^{r}f_{j}(0^{+})\right)=\Delta\mathbf{f}.

On the other hand, since each
f_{j}
is of bounded variation in
[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
and the functions
f_{j}(\nu_{j}x)
have no common discontinuities, we can use integration by parts (
Theorem

2.1
) to get,

I_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\int_{\varepsilon}^{1-\varepsilon}\prod_{j\neq k}f_{j}(\nu_{j}x)df_{k}(\nu_{k}x).

The discontinuities of
f_{k}(\nu_{k}x)
are of the form
i/\nu_{k}
for
0\leq i\leq\nu_{k}
, so
f_{k}(\nu_{k}x)
is pairwise absolutely continuous on
(0,1)
. Using
Theorem

2.2
, we obtain,

\int_{\varepsilon}^{1-\varepsilon}\prod_{j\neq k}f_{j}(\nu_{j}x)df_{k}(\nu_{k}x)=\nu_{k}\int_{\varepsilon}^{1-\varepsilon}f_{k}^{\prime}(\nu_{k}x)\prod_{j\neq k}f_{j}(\nu_{j}x)dx-\sum_{i=1}^{\nu_{k}-1}\prod_{j\neq k}f_{j}\left(\nu_{j}\frac{i}{\nu_{k}}\right)(f_{k}(\nu_{k}^{-})-f_{k}(\nu_{k}^{+})).

Using that
(f_{k}(\nu_{k}^{-})-f_{k}(\nu_{k}^{+}))=\Delta f_{k}
, and taking
\varepsilon\to 0
the theorem follows.

∎.

Example 3.2
.

(1)

The interesting case is when
\Delta f_{j}=1
for all
j
. For example, the functions
f_{j}(x)=\{x\}^{q_{j}}
given a collection of integers
q_{j}\geq 1
. In this case,
f(0^{+})=0
and
f(1^{-})=1
, so
\Delta\mathbf{f}=1
and we have,

{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathbf{f}}(\nu)=\sum_{k=0}^{r}S_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})=1+\sum_{k=0}^{r}q_{k}\nu_{k}\int_{0}^{1}\{\nu_{k}x\}^{q_{k}-1}\prod_{j\neq k}\{\nu_{j}x\}^{q_{k}}dx.

(2)

Another important case is when
f_{j}=b_{1}
for all
j=0,\ldots,r
. We have
f(0^{+})=-1/2
and
f(1^{-})=1/2
,
\Delta b_{1}=1
. Also,
b_{1}^{\prime}(x)=1
, so the previous integral reciprocity formula applies as

(5)

{\mathcal{R}}_{b_{1}(\nu)}=\sum_{k=0}^{r}S_{b_{1}}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})=\frac{(-1)^{r+1}-1}{2^{r+1}}+\sum_{k=0}^{r}\nu_{k}\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{j\neq k}b_{1}(\nu_{j}x)dx.

Indeed, for
r=2
, we can provide a proof of Dedekind-Rademacher’s reciprocity, as observed in
[
13
, 2.D]
. The key result in the proof is the following identity

\int_{0}^{1}b_{1}(mx)b_{1}(nx)dx=\frac{\gcd(m,n)}{12mn},\quad\forall m,n\in{\mathbb{N}},

due to J. Franel in
[
8
]
, from where Rademarcher’s reciprocity follows directly. In his honour, a
Franel integral
is any value of the form

\int_{0}^{1}b_{1}(\nu_{0}x)\ldots b_{1}(\nu_{r}x)dx,

for any vector of distinct positive integers
(\nu_{0},\ldots,\nu_{r})
.

(3)

For a collection
a_{0},\ldots,a_{r}\in(0,1)
consider
f_{j}(x)=\{x\}-a_{j}
, then we have
\Delta f_{j}=1
, and,

\Delta\mathbf{f}=\prod_{j=0}^{r}(1-a_{j})-(-1)^{r}\prod_{j=0}^{r}a_{j}.

For
r=2
, we can obtain a parametric extension of Dedekind-Rademacher’s formula. Indeed, using Franel’s identity and
Equation

2
we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(\{\nu_{j}x\}-a_{j})(\{\nu_{k}x\}-a_{k})dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}(b_{1}(\nu_{j}x)+1/2-a_{j})(b_{1}(\nu_{k}x)+1/2-a_{k})dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12\nu_{j}\nu_{k}}+(1/2-a_{j})(1/2-a_{k}).

So, our reciprocity formula is

(6)

{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\nu})=-1+\sum_{k=0}^{2}a_{k}-\sum_{j<k}a_{j}a_{k}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j<k\\
l\neq j,k\end{subarray}}\nu_{l}(1/2-a_{j})(1/2-a_{k})+\frac{\nu_{0}^{2}+\nu_{1}^{2}+\nu_{2}^{2}}{12\nu_{0}\nu_{1}\nu_{2}}.

In particular, when
f_{j}(x)=f(x)=\{x\}-a
, for a fixed
0<a<1
we get

{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\nu})=\frac{(\nu_{0}^{2}+\nu_{1}^{2}+\nu_{2}^{2})}{12\nu_{0}\nu_{1}\nu_{2}}+\left(\frac{1}{2}-a\right)^{2}(\nu_{0}+\nu_{1}+\nu_{2})-(1-3a+3a^{2}).

3.2.
Fourier Approach

In
[
4
]
, the authors use the Fourier expansion of the function
b_{1}
to solve a conjecture of McIntosh
[
12
]
about Franel integrals of order
r=3
. We aim to apply these ideas to study our Dedekind sums. The main reason is that functions of bounded variation always admit a Fourier expansion. Explicitly, for a collection of functions
f_{0},\ldots,f_{r}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
, we can always assume an expansion

{\mathcal{F}}{f_{j}}(x)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}c_{j,n}e(nx),\quad c_{j,n}=c_{n}(f_{j}):=\int_{0}^{1}f_{j}(x)e(-nx)dx,

where
e(x):=\exp(2\pi\iota x)
, and
\iota
is the imaginary unit with
\iota^{2}=-1
. These expansions are characterized by
f_{j}(x)={\mathcal{F}}_{f_{j}}(x)
for any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus{\mathbb{Z}}
and extending for all
\nu\in{\mathbb{Z}}
by

(7)

{\mathcal{F}}{f_{j}}(\nu)=\frac{f_{j}(\nu^{+})+f_{j}(\nu^{-})}{2},

known as
principal value
of the series. For any
\mathbf{n}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r+1}
denote

c_{k,\mathbf{n}}:=\prod_{j\neq k}c_{j,n_{j}}.

In what follows, for any pair of vectors
\mathbf{m}=(m_{0},\ldots,m_{r}),\mathbf{n}=(n_{0},\ldots,n_{r})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r+1}
we have the inner product

\langle\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n}\rangle=m_{0}n_{0}+\ldots+m_{r}n_{r},

defining the orthogonal sets

\mathbf{m}^{\perp}=\{\mathbf{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r+1}:\langle\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n}\rangle=0\}.

The following subsets will be relevant,

\mathbf{m}^{\perp}_{\Lambda}:=\mathbf{m}^{\perp}\cap\Lambda^{r+1},

for any discrete subset
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}
. The previous can be applied to the quantities

I(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k}):=\int_{0}^{1}f_{k}^{\prime}(\nu_{k}x)\prod_{j\neq k}f_{j}(\nu_{j}x)dx,

by interchanging the Fourier expansion with the integral without concern about convergence.

Corollary 3.3

(Fourier Reciprocity)
.

If
f_{k}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
for all
k
, we have

{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\nu})=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\Delta f_{k}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}}\nu_{k}c_{k,\mathbf{n}}-\Delta\mathbf{f}.

Proof.

Since
f_{k}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
, there exists a Fourier expansion

{\mathcal{F}}f_{k}^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}c^{\prime}_{k,n}e(nx).

So, we have explicitly

{\mathcal{F}}f^{\prime}_{k}(\nu_{k}x)\prod_{j\neq k}{\mathcal{F}}f_{j}(\nu_{j}x)=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r+1}}c^{\prime}_{k,n_{k}}\prod_{j\neq k}c_{j,n_{j}}e\left(\langle\bm{\nu},\mathbf{n}\rangle x\right),

converging point-wise and uniform for any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus{\mathbb{Z}}
, so integrals can be interchanged. Recall that

\int_{0}^{1}e\left(\langle\bm{\nu},\mathbf{n}\rangle x\right)dx=\left\{\begin{matrix}1,&\langle\bm{\nu},\mathbf{n}\rangle=0,\\
0,&\langle\bm{\nu},\mathbf{n}\rangle\neq 0,\end{matrix}\right.

so we can compute directly,

I(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}}c^{\prime}_{k,n_{k}}\prod_{j\neq k}c_{j,n_{j}}.

Therefore, integral reciprocity can be rewritten as follows,

(8)

{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\nu})=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\Delta f_{k}S_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})=-\Delta\mathbf{f}+I(\nu),

where

I(\bm{\nu}):=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\nu_{k}I(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{\perp}}\nu_{k}c^{\prime}_{k,n_{k}}\prod_{j\neq k}c_{j,n_{j}}.

Observe that
c_{k,0}^{\prime}=\Delta f_{k}
, and for any
n\neq 0

\displaystyle c_{k,n}=\int_{0}^{1}f_{k}(x)e(-nx)dx

\displaystyle=\left.-\frac{f_{k}(x)e(-nx)}{2\pi\iota n}\right|_{x=0}^{x=1}+\frac{1}{2\pi\iota n}\int_{0}^{1}f_{k}^{\prime}(x)e(-nx)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\iota n}\left(c^{\prime}_{k,n}-\Delta f_{k}\right).

So
c_{k,n}^{\prime}=(2\pi\iota n)c_{k,n}+\Delta f_{k}
for all
n
, and now we can rewrite,

I(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})=\Delta f_{k}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}}\prod_{j\neq k}c_{j,n_{j}}+2\pi\iota\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\\
n_{j}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\}\end{subarray}}n_{k}\prod_{j=0}^{r}c_{j,n_{j}}.

Summing over
k=0,\ldots,r
, observe that in terms of principal values we have,

2\pi\iota\sum_{k=0}^{r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\\
n_{j}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\}\end{subarray}}\nu_{k}n_{k}\prod_{j=0}^{r}c_{n_{j}}=2\pi\iota\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\\
n_{j}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\}\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=0}^{r}c_{n_{j}}\sum_{k=0}^{r}\nu_{k}n_{k}=0.

So, we get the identity

(9)

I(\bm{\nu})=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\Delta f_{k}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}}c_{k,\mathbf{n}},

from where the result follows.

∎

Remark 3.4
.

Important particular cases of the previous result are the following.

(1)

Assume that some of the
f_{j}^{\prime}s
in
\mathbf{f}
are continuous in
{\mathbb{R}}
, i.e.,
\Delta f_{j}=0
. Let us denote
{\mathcal{C}}=\{j:f_{j}\text{ is continuous}\}
, so reciprocity looks like

{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\nu})=\sum_{k\not\in{\mathcal{C}}}S_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})\Delta f_{k}=\sum_{k\not\in{\mathcal{C}}}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}}\nu_{k}\Delta f_{k}c_{k,\mathbf{n}}-\prod_{k\in{\mathcal{C}}}f_{k}(0)\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{f}},

where
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}
is the vector of discontinuous functions of
\mathbf{f}
. In particular, if
f_{k}(x)=\{x\}-a
for any
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
f_{j}
continuous for all
j\neq k
, then

{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\nu})=S_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})=\nu_{k}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}}c_{k,\mathbf{n}}-\prod_{j\neq k}f_{j}(0).

(2)

For any
f\in{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
no constant with
f^{\prime}\in L^{1}([0,1])
and
f(0^{+})\neq f(1^{-})
, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{r}S_{f}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}}\nu_{k}c_{\mathbf{n}}-\frac{\Delta\mathbf{f}}{\Delta f},

where explicitly

\frac{\Delta\mathbf{f}}{\Delta f}=\sum_{k=0}^{r}f(1^{-})^{k}f(0^{+})^{r-k}.

The previous case can be extended to the case
f(0^{+})=f(1^{-})
by assuming that
f
is continuous at
x=0
and that the shifted functions
f_{a}(x):=f(x+a)
satisfies
\Delta f_{a}\neq 0
for all
a
in some neighborhood
(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\setminus\{0\}
with
\lim_{a\to 0}\Delta f_{a}=0
. So, the previous argument works for all
f_{a}
, and in the limit
a\to 0
we get
\Delta\mathbf{f}/\Delta f=-(r+1)f(0)^{r+1}
.

Let us check the previous to get some formulas for well-known test functions.

Example 3.5
.

(1)

For the exponential
e(x)=\exp(2\pi\iota x),

we have
c_{1}=1,
and
c_{n}=0
for all
n\neq 1
. So, we get

\overline{\nu}_{k}:=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}}\nu_{k}c_{k,\mathbf{n}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\nu_{k}&\text{if }\nu_{k}|\sum_{j\neq k}\nu_{j},\\
0&\text{if }\nu_{k}\nmid\sum_{j\neq k}\nu_{j}.\end{array}\right.

This allows us to write
S_{e}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})=\overline{\nu}_{k}-1.

(2)

For the normalized cosine function
\mbox{Cos}(x)=\cos(2\pi x)
we have,

\mbox{Cos}(x)=\frac{e(x)+e(-x)}{2},

thus,
c_{1}=c_{-1}=1/2
, and
c_{n}=0
when
|n|\neq 1
. Reciprocity implies,

S_{\mbox{Cos}(x)}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})=\frac{\nu_{k}}{2^{r}}\beta_{k}-1,

where
\beta_{k}
is the cardinality of the set

U_{k}(\bm{\nu})=\left\{\mathbf{u}\in\{-1,1\}^{r}:\nu_{k}\big|\langle\mathbf{u},\bm{\nu}^{k}\rangle\right\},

with
\bm{\nu}^{k}=(\nu_{0},\ldots,\nu_{k-1},\nu_{k+1},\ldots,\nu_{r})
.

(3)

For the normalized sine function

\mbox{Sin}(x)=\sin(2\pi x)=\frac{e(x)-e(-x)}{2\iota},

we have
c_{1}=1/2\iota
,
c_{-1}=-1/2\iota
, and
c_{n}=0
for
|n|\neq 1
. Any
\mathbf{u}\in U_{k}(\bm{\nu})
has a
sign
denoted as
\mbox{sgn}(\mathbf{u})
and defined by the product of its coordinates. So,

S_{\mbox{Sin}(x)}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})=\frac{\nu_{k}}{(2\iota)^{r}}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in U_{k}(\bm{\nu})}\mbox{sgn}(\mathbf{u}).

Since,
-\mathbf{u}\in U_{k}(\bm{\nu})
and
\mbox{sgn}(-\mathbf{u})=(-1)^{r}\mbox{sgn}(\mathbf{u})
we can define

\overline{\beta}_{k}=\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\tilde{U}}\mbox{sgn}(\mathbf{u}),

where
\tilde{U}
is the quotient of
U_{k}(\bm{\nu})
by the antipodal map
\mathbf{u}\mapsto-\mathbf{u}
. Now, we can rewrite

S_{\mbox{Sin}(x)}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})=\frac{1+(-1)^{r}}{(2\iota)^{r}}\overline{\beta}_{k}\nu_{k},

observing a vanishing when
r
is odd.

∎

3.3.
Reciprocity for Polynomial Dedekind Sums

For polynomials
F_{0},\ldots,F_{r}\in{\mathbb{C}}[x]
of degree
q_{j}\geq 1
consider its associated periodic function
f_{j}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
defined by
f_{j}(x):=F_{j}(\{x\})
. Since the study of the Dedekind sums
S_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})
is reduced to the Dedekind sums of a basis for
{\mathbb{C}}[x]
. Then, as a useful basis for
{\mathbb{C}}[x]
we use the Bernoulli polynomials
B_{q}(x)
defined by the generating function

\frac{te^{xt}}{e^{t}-1}=\sum_{q\geq 0}B_{q}(x)\frac{t^{q}}{q!}.

In
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
, we have the
periodic
q
-th Bernoulli polynomials

b_{q}(x)=B_{q}(\{x\})
, and their Fourier coefficients are computed as

(10)

c_{n}(b_{q})=\int_{0}^{1}B_{q}(x)e(-nx)dx=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}c_{0}(b_{0})=1,\quad c_{n}(b_{0})=0,\,n\neq 0,&\\[8.53581pt]
c_{0}(b_{q})=0,\quad c_{n}(b_{q})=-\dfrac{q!}{(2\pi\iota n)^{q}},&n\neq 0,\,q>0.\end{array}\right.

The relation with the power functions is

x^{q}=\frac{1}{q+1}\sum_{s=0}^{q}\binom{q+1}{s}B_{s}(x).

For any
\mathbf{q}=(q_{0},\ldots,q_{r})\in({\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})^{r+1}
we will denote
S_{\mathbf{q}}
and
S_{b_{\mathbf{q}}}
for the Dedekind sums associated to
S_{(\{x\}^{q_{0}},\ldots,\{x\}^{q_{r}})}
and
S_{(b_{q_{0}},\ldots,b_{q_{r}})}
respectively. From the previous relation, it is not too difficult to check that

\Delta\{x\}^{q_{k}}S_{\mathbf{q}}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})=\prod_{j=0}^{r}\frac{1}{q_{j}+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{s}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r+1}\\
0\leq s_{j}\leq q_{j}\end{subarray}}A_{\mathbf{s}}\Delta b_{s_{k}}S_{b_{\mathbf{s}}}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k}),\quad A_{\mathbf{s}}:=\prod_{j=0}^{r}\binom{q_{j}+1}{s_{j}}.

In this way, if
{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathbf{q}}(\bm{\nu})
denotes the reciprocity of the sums
S_{\mathbf{q}}
, then in terms of reciprocity we have,

(11)

{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathbf{q}}(\bm{\nu})=\prod_{j=0}^{r}\frac{1}{q_{j}+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{s}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r+1}\\
0\leq s_{j}\leq q_{j}\end{subarray}}A_{\mathbf{s}}{\mathcal{R}}_{b_{\mathbf{s}}}(\bm{\nu}).

Since the periodic Bernoulli functions are continuous for
q>1
, we have

\Delta b_{q}=\left\{\begin{matrix}1&q=1,\\
0&q\neq 1,\end{matrix}\right.

and thus,

{\mathcal{R}}_{b_{\mathbf{q}}}(\bm{\nu})=\sum_{k:q_{k}=1}S_{b_{\mathbf{q}}}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k}).

In particular, this reduces the index set of the right-side sum in
Equation

11
into the set
\{\mathbf{s}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r+1}:0\leq s_{j}\leq q_{j},\exists s_{j}=1\}
. We adopt the notation

\mathbf{q}_{k}=(q_{0},\ldots,q_{k-1},0,q_{k+1},\ldots,q_{r}),\quad I_{\mathbf{q},k}:=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{(2\pi\iota)^{|\mathbf{q}_{k}|}}\prod_{j\neq k}q_{j}!,

\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{q}}:=\#\{q_{j}\in\mathbf{q}:q_{j}=1\},\quad|\overline{\mathbf{q}}|=|\mathbf{q}|-1,\quad I_{\overline{\mathbf{q}}}=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{(2\pi\iota)^{|\overline{\mathbf{q}}|}}\prod_{q_{j}>1}q_{j}!.

From
Equation

10
, for any
k=0,\ldots,r
and any
\mathbf{n}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r+1}
we have explicitly

c_{k,\mathbf{n}}=\prod_{j\neq k}c_{n_{j}}(b_{q_{j}})=I_{\mathbf{q},k}\frac{1}{\mathbf{n}^{\mathbf{q}_{k}}},

which motivates the definition of the following quantity

\zeta_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}:=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}}\frac{1}{\mathbf{n}^{\mathbf{q}_{k}}},

called as a
multiple zeta value
. Moreover, observe that
I_{\mathbf{q},k}=I_{\overline{\mathbf{q}}}
for all
k
such that
q_{k}=1
. From (1) in
Remark

3.4
, we summarize the previous discussion into the following.

Corollary 3.6

(Reciprocity for Bernoulli Polynomials)
.

For any integer positive vector
\mathbf{q}
, we have

(12)

{\mathcal{R}}_{b_{\mathbf{q}}}(\bm{\nu})=I_{\overline{\mathbf{q}}}\sum_{k:q_{k}=1}\nu_{k}\zeta_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}-\frac{1-(-1)^{\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{q}}}}{2^{\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{q}}}}\prod_{q_{j}\neq 1}B_{q_{j}}.

∎

Since the sums
\zeta_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}
come from a Fourier analysis approach, in case of not being absolutely convergent, their value is considered as its principal value. So, if
|\mathbf{q}_{k}|
is odd, then the antipodal map
\mathbf{n}\mapsto-\mathbf{n}
gives,

\zeta_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\cap\{n_{k}\geq 0\}}\frac{1}{\mathbf{n}^{\mathbf{q}_{k}}}+(-1)^{|\mathbf{q}_{k}|}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\cap\{n_{k}\geq 0\}}\frac{1}{\mathbf{n}^{\mathbf{q}_{k}}}=0.

The condition
|\mathbf{q}_{k}|
being odd for all
k=0,\ldots,r
implies that
r
and all the
q_{j}
are odd. And since
B_{q}=0
for all odd integers
q>2
, as a direct consequence, the following holds.

Corollary 3.7
.

If
\mathbf{q}=(q_{0},\ldots,q_{r})
satisfy
q_{k}>1
for all
k
or
|q|_{k}
is odd for all
k=0,\ldots,r
, then
{\mathcal{R}}_{b_{\mathbf{q}}}(\bm{\nu})=0.

∎

4.
Zeta Values and Polyhedra

From the previous section, the reciprocity
{\mathcal{R}}_{b_{\mathbf{q}}}
for
r=1
is nontrivial when we assume
\mathbf{q}=(1,q)
where
q
is a positive integer. Then, we have explicitly

{\mathcal{R}}_{b_{\mathbf{q}}}(\nu_{0},\nu_{1})=-\frac{\nu_{0}q!}{(2\pi\iota)^{q}}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\nu^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}}\frac{1}{n_{1}^{q}}-\delta(q)\frac{\nu_{1}}{(2\pi\iota)}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\nu^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}}\frac{1}{n_{0}}-B(q),

where

\delta(q)=\left\{\begin{matrix}1&q=1,\\
0&q>1,\end{matrix}\right.\quad B(q)=\left\{\begin{matrix}0&q=1,\\
B_{q}&q>1.\end{matrix}\right.

The set

\nu^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}=\{(n_{0},n_{1}):\nu_{0}n_{0}+\nu_{1}n_{1}=0\},

have well-known solutions given by
(n_{0},n_{1})=n(\nu_{1},-\nu_{0})
for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. So, reciprocity becomes

{\mathcal{R}}_{b_{\mathbf{q}}}(\nu_{0},\nu_{1})=-\frac{(-1)^{q}q!}{\nu_{0}^{q-1}(2\pi\iota)^{q}}\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\frac{1}{n^{q}}-\delta(q)\frac{1}{(2\pi\iota)}\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\frac{1}{n}-B(q).

Recall that

\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\frac{1}{n^{q}}=\left\{\begin{matrix}0&q\text{ odd},\\
2\zeta(q)&q\text{ even},\end{matrix}\right.

where

\zeta(q):=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\frac{1}{n^{q}},

is the Riemann zeta function. The remarkable identity

\zeta(q)=(-1)^{\frac{q}{2}+1}\frac{B_{q}(2\pi)^{q}}{2q!},

holds for all
q
even. Therefore, reciprocity for
r=1
is

(13)

{\mathcal{R}}_{b_{\mathbf{q}}}(\nu_{0},\nu_{1})=\left\{\begin{matrix}-B(q)&q\text{ odd},\\[5.0pt]
\dfrac{(-1)^{1+\frac{q}{2}}q!}{\nu_{0}^{q-1}2^{q-1}\pi^{q}}\zeta(q)-B(q)&q\text{ even}.\end{matrix}\right.=\left\{\begin{matrix}-B(q)&q\text{ odd},\\[5.0pt]
B_{q}\left(\dfrac{1}{\nu_{0}^{q-1}}-1\right)&q\text{ even}.\end{matrix}\right.

In what follows, we treat the case
r\geq 2
for the multiple zeta values
\zeta_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}
. This will lead us into a reciprocity formula for
r=2
in the next section.

4.1.
The Multiple zeta values.

Denote

\bm{\nu}^{k,\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}:=\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\cap\{n_{k}=0\}\cong(\bm{\nu}^{k})^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}},

where
\bm{\nu}^{k}=(\nu_{0},\ldots,\nu_{k-1},\nu_{k+1},\ldots,\nu_{r})
, thus we can decompose

\zeta_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}=Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}+Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}
with,

Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}:=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}}}\frac{1}{\mathbf{n}^{\mathbf{q}_{k}}},\quad Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}:=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}^{k,\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}}\frac{1}{\mathbf{n}^{\mathbf{q}_{k}}}.

Observe that in both cases their principal values are zero for
|\mathbf{q}_{k}|
odd, so we assume this value is an even number in the rest of the section. Unfortunately, the values
Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}
do not converge on their own if
q_{j}=1
for some
j\neq k
. However, the right side of
Corollary

3.6
tells us the right way of how these sums agree with convergence. For this purpose, define the following multiple zeta value

\zeta_{\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}:=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}}}\frac{1}{\mathbf{n}^{\mathbf{q}}}.

Theorem 4.1
.

The sums
\zeta_{\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}
converge absolutely for all
\mathbf{q}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r+1}
. Furthermore,

Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}=\zeta_{\bm{\nu^{k}},\bm{q}^{k}},

\sum_{k:q_{k}=1}\nu_{k}Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}=-\sum_{k:q_{k}>1}\nu_{k}\zeta_{\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{e}_{k}}.

Proof.

For any
\mathbf{q}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r+1}
, we have

\displaystyle|\zeta_{\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}}}\left|\frac{1}{\mathbf{n}^{\mathbf{q}}}\right|=\sum_{n_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}}\frac{1}{|n_{k}|^{q_{k}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(n_{j})_{j\neq k}\in({\mathbb{Z}}^{\times})^{r}\\
\sum_{j\neq k}\nu_{j}n_{j}=-\nu_{k}n_{k}\end{subarray}}\prod_{j\neq k}\frac{1}{|n_{j}|^{q_{j}}}

\displaystyle=\nu_{l}^{-q_{l}}\sum_{n_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}}\frac{1}{|n_{k}|^{q_{k}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(n_{j})_{j\neq k,l}\in({\mathbb{Z}}^{\times})^{r-1}\\
\sum_{j\neq l}\nu_{j}n_{j}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|\sum_{j\neq l}\nu_{j}n_{j}|^{q_{l}}}\prod_{j\neq k,l}\frac{1}{|n_{j}|^{q_{j}}},

for some
l\neq k
. For any
l^{\prime}\neq k,l
, we denote
M_{l^{\prime}}=\sum_{j\neq l^{\prime},l}\nu_{j}n_{j}
and differentiate between three cases:

(1)

If
|\nu_{l^{\prime}}n_{l^{\prime}}|\leq|M_{l^{\prime}}|/2
, then

\left|\sum_{j\neq l}\nu_{j}n_{j}\right|=|\nu_{l^{\prime}}n_{l^{\prime}}+M_{l^{\prime}}|\geq\left|M_{l^{\prime}}\right|-|\nu_{l^{\prime}}n_{l^{\prime}}|\geq\frac{|M_{l^{\prime}}|}{2}.

So, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{l^{\prime}}\neq 0\\
|\nu_{l^{\prime}}n_{l^{\prime}}|\leq|M_{l^{\prime}}|/2\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|\sum_{j\neq l}\nu_{j}n_{j}|^{q_{l}}|n_{l^{\prime}}|^{q_{l^{\prime}}}}\leq\frac{2^{q_{l}}}{|M_{l^{\prime}}|^{q_{l}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{l^{\prime}}\neq 0\\
|\nu_{l^{\prime}}n_{l^{\prime}}|\leq|M_{l^{\prime}}|/2\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|n_{l^{\prime}}|^{q_{l^{\prime}}}}=\left\{\begin{matrix}O(\log(|M_{l^{\prime}}|)|M_{l^{\prime}}|^{-q_{l}})&q_{l^{\prime}}=1,\\
O(|M_{l^{\prime}}|^{-q_{l}})&q_{l^{\prime}}>1.\end{matrix}\right.

(2)

If
|M_{l^{\prime}}|/2<|\nu_{l^{\prime}}n_{l^{\prime}}|<2|M_{l^{\prime}}|
, then

||\nu_{l^{\prime}}n_{l^{\prime}}|-|M_{l^{\prime}}||\leq|M_{l^{\prime}}|.

So, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{l^{\prime}}\neq 0\\
|M_{l^{\prime}}|/2<|\nu_{l^{\prime}}n_{l^{\prime}}|<2|M_{l^{\prime}}|\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|\sum_{j\neq l}\nu_{j}n_{j}|^{q_{l}}|n_{l^{\prime}}|^{q_{l^{\prime}}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{q_{l^{\prime}}}}{|M_{l^{\prime}}|^{q_{l^{\prime}}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{l^{\prime}}\neq 0\\
|M_{l^{\prime}}|/2<|\nu_{l^{\prime}}n_{l^{\prime}}|<2|M_{l^{\prime}}|\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|\nu_{l^{\prime}}n_{j}+M_{l^{\prime}}|^{q_{l}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{q_{l^{\prime}}}}{|M_{l^{\prime}}|^{q_{l^{\prime}}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{l^{\prime}}\neq 0\\
0<|k|<|M_{l^{\prime}}|\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|k|^{q_{l}}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{matrix}O(\log(|M_{l^{\prime}}|)|M_{l^{\prime}}|^{-q_{l^{\prime}}})&q_{l}=1,\\
O(|M_{l^{\prime}}|^{-q_{l^{\prime}}})&q_{l}>1.\end{matrix}\right.

(3)

If
|\nu_{l^{\prime}}n_{l^{\prime}}|\geq 2|M_{l^{\prime}}|
, then

\left|\sum_{j\neq l}\nu_{j}n_{j}\right|=|\nu_{l^{\prime}}n_{l^{\prime}}+M_{l^{\prime}}|\geq|\nu_{l^{\prime}}n_{l^{\prime}}|-\left|M_{l^{\prime}}\right|\geq\frac{|\nu_{l^{\prime}}n_{l^{\prime}}|}{2}.

So, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{l^{\prime}}\neq 0\\
|\nu_{l^{\prime}}n_{l^{\prime}}|\geq 2|M_{l^{\prime}}|\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|\sum_{j\neq l}\nu_{j}n_{j}|^{q_{l}}|n_{l^{\prime}}|^{q_{l^{\prime}}}}\leq\frac{2^{q_{l}}}{\nu_{l^{\prime}}^{q_{l^{\prime}}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{l^{\prime}}\neq 0\\
|\nu_{l^{\prime}}n_{l^{\prime}}|\geq 2|M_{l^{\prime}}|\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|n_{l^{\prime}}|^{q_{l^{\prime}}+q_{l}}}\leq\frac{2^{q_{l}+1}}{\nu_{l^{\prime}}^{q_{l^{\prime}}}}\zeta(q_{l^{\prime}}+q_{l})<\infty.

Setting
q_{ll^{\prime}}=\min\{q_{l},q_{l^{\prime}}\}
, the prove of the convergence of
|\zeta_{\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}|
is reduce to check the convergence of

\sum_{n_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}}\frac{1}{|n_{k}|^{q_{k}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(n_{j})_{j\neq k,l,l^{\prime}}\in({\mathbb{Z}}^{\times})^{r-2}\\
\sum_{j\neq l,l^{\prime}}\nu_{j}n_{j}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|M_{l^{\prime}}|^{q_{ll^{\prime}}+\varepsilon}}\prod_{j\neq k,l,l^{\prime}}\frac{1}{|n_{j}|^{q_{j}}},

for every
\varepsilon>0
by the fact that we always can compare
\log(x)\sim x^{\varepsilon}
. This process can be done analogously for a
l^{\prime\prime}\neq k,l,l^{\prime}
with a
M_{l^{\prime}l^{\prime\prime}}=\sum_{j\neq l,l^{\prime},l^{\prime\prime}}\nu_{j}n_{j}
. We end reducing the convergence of
|\zeta_{\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}|
into the convergence of

\sum_{n_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}}\frac{1}{|n_{k}|^{q_{k}}|\nu_{k}n_{k}|^{\min\{q_{j}:j\neq k\}+\varepsilon}}=2\nu_{k}^{-\min\{q_{j}:j\neq k\}-\varepsilon}\zeta(q_{k}+\min\{q_{j}:j\neq k\}+\varepsilon).

Since,
q_{k}+q_{k^{\prime}}>1
for all
0\leq k,k^{\prime}\leq r
the convergence is assured. The identity for
Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}
is direct by definition. For the last identity, without loss of generality, let us assume that
q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{s}>1
and
q_{k}=1
for
k>s
. Now we can write,

\sum_{k:q_{k}=1}\nu_{k}Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}}}\sum_{k=s+1}^{r}\frac{\nu_{k}}{\mathbf{n}^{\mathbf{q}_{k}}}=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}}}\frac{1}{\mathbf{n}^{\mathbf{q}}}\sum_{k=s+1}^{r}\nu_{k}n_{k}.

Since
\langle\bm{\nu},\mathbf{n}\rangle=0
, the sum is modified into

\sum_{k:q_{k}=1}\nu_{k}\zeta_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}=-\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}}}\frac{1}{\mathbf{n}^{\mathbf{q}}}\sum_{k=1}^{s}\nu_{k}n_{k}=-\sum_{k:q_{k}>1}\nu_{k}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}}}\frac{1}{\mathbf{n}^{\mathbf{q}}}=-\sum_{k:q_{k}>1}\nu_{k}\zeta_{\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{e}_{k}}.

∎

Remark 4.2
.

(1)

The previous discussion says that in the case
\mathbf{q}=(1,\ldots,1)
we must have

\sum_{k=0}^{r}\nu_{k}Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}=0.

Thus, we get explicitly

(14)

\sum_{k=0}^{r}\nu_{k}\zeta_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\nu_{k}Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}.

(2)

For all
\mathbf{q}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r+1}
we have

Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}=(-1)^{|q_{k}|}\prod_{j\neq k}\frac{(2\pi\iota)^{q_{j}}}{q_{j}!}I(k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}),

where

I(k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}):=\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{j\neq k}b_{q_{j}}\left(\nu_{j}x\right)dx.

Moreover,
Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}
is a rational multiple of
(2\pi)^{|\mathbf{q}_{k}|}
. This follows directly from the proof of
Corollary

3.3
when we assume
q_{k}=1
. Since the
b_{q_{j}}
are defined from rational polynomials, the integrals
I(k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q})
must be rational numbers.

The following reduction will be useful for explicit computations in the next section. Let us translate the situation into the first orthant
{\mathbb{N}}^{r+1}
. For each
\mathbf{u}=(u_{0},u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})\in\{-1,1\}^{r+1}
the Hadamard product with
\bm{\nu}
is just
\mathbf{u}\bm{\nu}:=(u_{0}\nu_{0},\ldots,u_{r}\nu_{r}).
We have a bijection,

\bigsqcup_{\mathbf{u}\in\{-1,1\}^{r+1}}\mathbf{u}\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}\to\bm{\nu}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}}^{\perp},\quad(n_{0},\ldots,n_{r})\in(\mathbf{u}\bm{\nu})^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}\mapsto\mathbf{u}\mathbf{n}=(u_{0}n_{0},\ldots,u_{r}n_{r}).

Thus, we get

Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}=\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\{-1,1\}^{r+1}}\sigma_{k}(\mathbf{u})Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}},\quad Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}}=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbf{u}\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}}\frac{1}{\mathbf{n}^{\mathbf{q}_{k}}},

where
\sigma_{k}(\mathbf{u})=\prod_{j\neq k}u_{j}^{q_{j}}
. The same can be done for
Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}
, however using the identification
\bm{\nu}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{k,\perp}\cong(\bm{\nu}^{k})^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}}
we can rewrite,

Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\{-1,1\}^{r+1}}\sigma_{k}(\mathbf{u})Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}},\quad Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}}=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in(\mathbf{u}\bm{\nu}^{k})^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}}\frac{1}{\mathbf{n}^{\mathbf{q}_{k}}},

where the
2^{-1}
comes from avoiding repeated sums since the variable
n_{k}
is avoided. Indeed, it is possible to remove the
2^{-1}
factor redefining the sum over the
\mathbf{u}\in\{-1,1\}^{r}
. As above, the cases
q_{j}=1
make the sums
Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}}
diverge. However, we can combine them as in
Theorem

4.1
to establish convergence. Therefore, in what follows, we treat them as a single value without regard to convergence. When
q_{j}>1
for all
j
, we get the following estimates.

Proposition 4.3
.

If
\nu_{l}=\min\{\nu_{j}:j\neq k\}
, then the following holds,

Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}}\leq\prod_{j\neq k}\zeta(q_{k}),\quad Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}}\leq 2\nu_{l}^{q_{l}}\prod_{j\neq k}\zeta(q_{k}),

for all
\mathbf{q}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{>1}^{r+1}
.

Proof.

We have

Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}}=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in(\mathbf{u}\bm{\nu}^{k})^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}}\prod_{j\neq k}\frac{1}{n_{j}^{q_{j}}}\leq\sum_{(n_{j})_{j\neq k}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r}}\prod_{j\neq k}\frac{1}{n_{j}^{q_{j}}}=\prod_{j\neq k}\zeta(q_{j}).

For
\mathbf{n}=(n_{0},\ldots,n_{r})\in(\mathbf{u}\nu)^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}
choose an
l\neq k
and write
u_{l}\nu_{l}n_{l}=-\sum_{j\neq l}u_{j}\nu_{j}n_{j}
. Then, we get

Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}}=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in(\mathbf{u}\nu)^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}}\frac{1}{\mathbf{n}^{\mathbf{q}_{k}}}=(-u_{l}\nu_{l})^{q_{l}}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in(\mathbf{u}\nu)^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}}\left(\frac{1}{\sum_{j\neq l}u_{j}\nu_{j}n_{j}}\right)^{q_{l}}\prod_{j\neq k,l}\frac{1}{n_{j}^{q_{j}}}.

Then, we have

Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}}\leq\nu_{l}^{q_{l}}\sum_{(n_{j})_{j\neq k}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r}}\left|\frac{1}{\sum_{j\neq l}u_{j}\nu_{j}n_{j}}\right|^{q_{l}}\prod_{j\neq k,l}\frac{1}{n_{j}^{q_{j}}}.

For any fixed
\mathbf{n}=(n_{j})_{j\neq k,l}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r-1}
, the correspondence

n_{k}\in{\mathbb{N}}\mapsto\sum_{j\neq l}u_{j}\nu_{j}n_{j}\in{\mathbb{Z}}

is injective, so we can estimate

\displaystyle Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle\leq\nu_{l}^{q_{l}}\sum_{(n_{j})_{j\neq k,l}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r-1}}\prod_{j\neq k,l}\frac{1}{n_{j}^{q_{j}}}\sum_{n_{k}\geq 1}\left|\frac{1}{\sum_{j\neq l}u_{j}\nu_{j}n_{j}}\right|^{q_{l}}

\displaystyle\leq\nu_{l}^{q_{l}}\sum_{(n_{j})_{j\neq k,l}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r-1}}\prod_{j\neq k,l}\frac{1}{n_{j}^{q_{j}}}\sum_{n_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}}\frac{1}{|n_{k}|^{q_{l}}}

\displaystyle=2\nu_{l}^{q_{l}}\zeta(q_{l})\sum_{(n_{j})_{j\neq k,l}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r-1}}\prod_{j\neq k,l}\frac{1}{n_{j}^{q_{j}}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\nu_{l}^{q_{l}}\prod_{j\neq k}\zeta(q_{j}).

Choosing
\nu_{l}
as the minimum of the
\nu_{j}
with
j\neq k
, the result follows.

∎

As a direct corollary, summing all contributions of
\mathbf{u}\in\{-1,1\}^{r+1}
yields the following.

Corollary 4.4
.

If
\nu_{l}=\min\{\nu_{j}:j\neq k\}
, then the following estimate holds,

|\zeta_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}|\leq 2^{r}(1+4\nu_{l}^{q_{l}})\prod_{j\neq k}\zeta(q_{j}),

for all
\mathbf{q}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{>1}^{r+1}
.

Proof.

Indeed, we have
|\zeta_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}|\leq|Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}|+|Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}|
with

|Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}|\leq\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\{-1,1\}^{r+1}}Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}}\leq 2^{r+2}\nu_{l}^{q_{l}}\prod_{j\neq k}\zeta(q_{j}),

|Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\{-1,1\}^{r+1}}Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}}\leq 2^{r}\prod_{j\neq k}\zeta(q_{j}),

from where the result follows.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

Some general properties related to the previous sums are as follows. We have

(15)

Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},(1,\ldots,1)}=Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},(1,\ldots,1)}=Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},(-1,\ldots,-1)}=Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},(-1,\ldots,-1)}=0.

Moreover, the symmetry by the antipodal map
\mathbf{u}\to-\mathbf{u}
gives

(16)

Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},-\mathbf{u}}=Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}},\quad Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},-\mathbf{u}}=Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}}.

The first identity follows directly from the fact that the equations
\langle\mathbf{u}\bm{\nu},\mathbf{n}\rangle=0
and
\langle\mathbf{u}\bm{\nu}^{k},\mathbf{n}\rangle=0
have no nonzero solutions of the same sign, i.e.,

\mathbf{u}\bm{\nu}^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}
and
(\mathbf{u}\bm{\nu}^{k})^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}},
are empty for
\mathbf{u}=\pm(1,\ldots,1)
. The second one follows from

(\mathbf{u}\nu)^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}=\{\mathbf{n}:\langle n,\mathbf{u}\nu\rangle=0\}=\{\mathbf{n}:\langle n,-\mathbf{u}\nu\rangle=0\}=(-\mathbf{u}\nu)^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}.

4.2.
Desingularization Algorithms for Polyhedra

Coming back to the sums
Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}}
and
Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}}
, we know they depend on the sets

(\mathbf{u}\nu)^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}
and
(\mathbf{u}\nu)^{k,\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}
which are the nonnegative solutions to linear systems. By Gordan’s lemma
[
1
,
7
]
, these are normal affine semigroups with a finite set of
minimal generators, called a Hilbert basis. As a consequence,
(\mathbf{u}\nu)^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}
and

(\mathbf{u}\nu)^{k,\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}
are sets of positive integer points of polyhedral cones. Hence, we may assume that
(\mathbf{u}\nu)^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}=C_{\mathbf{u}}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{r+1}
and
(\mathbf{u}\nu)^{k,\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}=C_{\mathbf{u}}^{k}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{r+1}
where
C_{\mathbf{u}}
and
C^{k}_{\mathbf{u}}
are polyhedral cones with,

\dim C_{\mathbf{u}}^{k}=\dim C_{\mathbf{u}}-1=r-1,

whose generators lie in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{r+1}
. For computational purposes, Hilbert bases are not completely satisfactory
since they do not generate the semigroups freely, i.e., representations are not unique, and computing a Hilbert basis becomes increasingly difficult as the dimension
r
grows. We can avoid this problem by refining the cones
C_{\mathbf{u}}
and
C^{k}_{\mathbf{u}}
into unimodular cones, i.e., by taking a
desingularization of the cones
. Concretely, there exist finite collections of simplicial cones

C_{\mathbf{u},0},C_{\mathbf{u},1},\ldots,C_{\mathbf{u},s}\subset C_{\mathbf{u}},\quad C_{\mathbf{u},0}^{k},C_{\mathbf{u},1}^{k},\ldots,C_{\mathbf{u},s_{k}}^{k}\subset C_{\mathbf{u}}^{k},

whose unions cover the larger cones and which are all of multiplicity one, that is, the primitive generators of each subcone form a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
of the lattice of integer points in its linear span. These subdivisions define fans
\Sigma_{\mathbf{u}}
and
\Sigma_{\mathbf{u},k}
, respectively. Denote by
\Sigma^{(d)}_{\mathbf{u}}
and
\Sigma^{(d)}_{\mathbf{u},k}
the set of
d
-dimensional cones of each fan. We denote their elements by
F
, and we refer to them as faces of the fans. Denote as
F_{{\mathbb{N}}}
the set of positive integer points of each face in the relative interior of
F
. Then, we have

(17)

(\mathbf{u}\nu)^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}=\bigsqcup_{d=1}^{r}\bigsqcup_{F\in\Sigma^{(d)}_{\mathbf{u}}}F_{{\mathbb{N}}},\quad(\mathbf{u}\nu)^{k,\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}=\bigsqcup_{d=1}^{r-1}\bigsqcup_{F\in\Sigma^{(d)}_{\mathbf{u},k}}F_{{\mathbb{N}}}.

Therefore, the previous discussion is summarized as follows.

Lemma 4.6
.

Under the previous hypothesis we have

Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}}=\sum_{d=1}^{r}\sum_{F\in\Sigma^{(d)}_{\mathbf{u}}}\zeta(\mathbf{q}_{k},F),\quad Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}}=\sum_{d=1}^{r-1}\sum_{F\in\Sigma^{(d)}_{\mathbf{u},k}}\zeta(\mathbf{q}_{k},F).

∎

This statement is known as an
open subdivision relation

[
9
, Lemma 2.7]
.

5.
The Case of Dimension
2
.

In what follows, we will work the case
r=2
using the previous results. From
Remark

4.5
we know that,

Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}}=Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},-\mathbf{u}},\quad Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}}=Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},-\mathbf{u}}

Y_{k,\bm{\nu},(1,1,1),\mathbf{u}}=Z_{k,\bm{\nu},(1,1,1),\mathbf{u}}=0,

for
\mathbf{u}\in\{-1,1\}^{3}
.
Therefore, our computations are reduced to the vectors

\mathbf{u}_{0}=(1,-1,-1),\mathbf{u}_{1}=(1,-1,1),\mathbf{u}_{2}=(1,1,-1),

with

Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}=\sum_{l=0}^{2}\sigma_{k,l}Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}_{l}},\quad Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}=2\sum_{l=0}^{2}\sigma_{k,l}Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}_{l}},\quad\sigma_{k,l}:=\sigma_{k}(\mathbf{u}_{l}).

Since we are assuming
|\mathbf{q}_{k}|
even, observe that
\sigma_{k,k}=1
. As a first step, observe that we have the following cone descriptions,

(\mathbf{u}_{0}\bm{\nu})^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}=C(\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2})\cap{\mathbb{N}}^{3},

(\mathbf{u}_{1}\bm{\nu})^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}=C(\mathbf{v}_{2},\mathbf{v}_{0})\cap{\mathbb{N}}^{3},

(\mathbf{u}_{2}\bm{\nu})^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}=C(\mathbf{v}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{1})\cap{\mathbb{N}}^{3},

where

\mathbf{v}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\nu_{2}\\
\nu_{1}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{v}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\nu_{2}\\
0\\
\nu_{0}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{v}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}\nu_{1}\\
\nu_{0}\\
0\end{pmatrix},

following the orientation of the vectors. In this case, we can compute directly

\bm{\nu}^{k,\perp}={\mathbb{Z}}\mathbf{u}_{l}\mathbf{v}_{k},\quad l\neq k,

so, analog to
Remark

2.7
we get the following.

Lemma 5.1
.

We have

Z_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}=\dfrac{\zeta(|\mathbf{q}_{k}|)}{\mathbf{v_{k}}^{\mathbf{q}_{k}}}\sum_{l\neq k}\sigma_{k,l}.

∎

For the value
Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}_{l}}
we can give a geometric interpretation using the
Hirzebruch-Jung algorithm
for plane cones (for instance, see
[
7
, Ch. 10]
or
[
16
, Sec. 2.4]
). For any pair
(m_{0},m_{1})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
with
m_{0}>m_{1}
, there is a sequence of pairs

[m_{0}/m_{1}]:=\left\{(m_{i},\overline{m}_{i}):\begin{matrix}m_{0}>m_{1}>\ldots>m_{s}=1>m_{s+1}=0\\
\overline{m}_{0}=0<\overline{m}_{1}=1<\ldots<\overline{m}_{s}<\overline{m}_{s+1}=m_{0}\end{matrix}\right\},

defined recursively by

m_{1}\overline{m}_{s}\equiv 1\mod m_{0}

m_{i+1}=m_{i}k_{i}-m_{i-1},\quad\overline{m}_{i+1}=\overline{m}_{i}k_{i}-\overline{m}_{i-1}

where the
k_{i}\geq 2
are positive integers. We have
(\mathbf{u}_{l}\bm{\nu})^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}=C(\mathbf{v}_{l^{\prime}},\mathbf{v}_{l^{\prime\prime}})\cap{\mathbb{N}}^{3}

where the cone
C(\mathbf{v}_{l^{\prime}},\mathbf{v}_{l^{\prime\prime}})
have multiplicity
\nu_{l}
for each
l
. The desingularization algorithm is initialized as follows. Let
0<\epsilon_{l}<\nu_{l}
the unique integer such that

\frac{\mathbf{v}_{l^{\prime}}\epsilon_{l}+\mathbf{v}_{l^{\prime\prime}}}{\nu_{l}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{3}.

Consider the sequences of vectors
(m_{l,i},\overline{m}_{l,i})\in[v_{l}/\epsilon_{l}]
with
i=0,\ldots,s_{l}+1
. Thus, the semi-groups
(\mathbf{u}_{l}\bm{\nu})^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}
are generated minimally by
\{\mathbf{v}_{0,l},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{s_{l}+1,l}\}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{3}
defined recursively by

\mathbf{v}_{i,l}=\frac{1}{v_{l}}\left(\mathbf{v}_{l^{\prime}}m_{i,l}+\mathbf{v}_{l^{\prime\prime}}\overline{m}_{i,l}\right).

For each
l=0,1,2
denote by
\Sigma
the fan defined by the
2
-cones

C_{l,i}=C(\mathbf{v}_{l,i},\mathbf{v}_{l,i+1}).

Note that
\Sigma^{(1)}
is simply the set of ray cones defined by each
\mathbf{v}_{l,i}
. Then, we have the following.

Lemma 5.2
.

If we denote,

{\mathcal{Q}}_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}:=\sum_{l=0}^{2}\sigma_{k,l}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{s_{l}}\frac{1}{\mathbf{v}_{l,i}^{\mathbf{q}_{k}}}+\sum_{i=0}^{s_{l}}\zeta^{*}(\mathbf{q}_{k},C_{l,i})\right),

then we have,

\sum_{k:q_{k}=1}Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}=2\zeta(|\overline{\mathbf{q}}|)\sum_{k:q_{k}=1}{\mathcal{Q}}_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}.

Proof.

Assume that the nonzero coordinates of
\mathbf{q}_{k}
are
q_{k^{\prime}},q_{k^{\prime\prime}}
. Explicitly we have

Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}_{l}}=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in(\mathbf{u}_{l}\nu)^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}}\frac{1}{n_{j}^{q_{k^{\prime}}}n_{j^{\prime}}^{q_{k^{\prime\prime}}}}.

By the previous discussion the sets
(\mathbf{u}_{l}\nu)^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}
can be described as a union

(\mathbf{u}_{l}\nu)^{\perp}_{{\mathbb{N}}}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{s_{l}}C_{l,i}\cap{\mathbb{N}}^{3}.

Since each cone

C_{l,i}
has multiplicity one, from
Lemma

4.6
we have

Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}_{l}}=\sum_{i=0}^{s_{l}}\zeta(\mathbf{q}_{k},C_{l,i})+\sum_{i=1}^{s_{l}}\zeta(\mathbf{q}_{k},\mathbf{v}_{l,i}).

From
Remark

2.7
with the fact that
\zeta(\mathbf{q}_{k},C_{l,i})=\zeta(|\mathbf{q}_{k}|)\zeta^{*}(\mathbf{q}_{k},C_{l,i})
we obtain

Y_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u}_{l}}=\zeta(|\mathbf{q}_{k}|)\left(\sum_{i=0}^{s_{l}}\zeta^{*}(\mathbf{q}_{k},C_{l,i})+\sum_{i=1}^{s_{l}}\dfrac{1}{\mathbf{v}_{l,i}^{\mathbf{q}_{k}}}\right).

Therefore, we put all together with
Theorem

4.1
to assure convergence and replacing
|\mathbf{q}_{k}|=|\overline{\mathbf{q}}|
for all
k
such that
q_{k}=1
.

∎

Corollary 5.3
.

For all
\mathbf{q}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{3}
reciprocity for Bernoulli polynomials is

{\mathcal{R}}_{b_{\mathbf{q}}}(\bm{\nu})=-\frac{B_{|\overline{\mathbf{q}}|}}{|\overline{\mathbf{q}}|!}\prod_{q_{k}>1}q_{j}!\sum_{k:q_{k}=1}\nu_{k}\left(\frac{1}{\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\mathbf{q}_{k}}}\sum_{k\neq l}\frac{\sigma_{k,l}}{2}+{\mathcal{Q}}_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}\right)-\frac{1-(-1)^{\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{q}}}}{2^{\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{q}}}}\prod_{q_{k}>1}B_{q_{k}}.

Proof.

From
Corollary

3.6
we know that

{\mathcal{R}}_{b_{\mathbf{q}}}(\bm{\nu})=I_{\overline{\mathbf{q}}}\sum_{k:q_{k}=1}\nu_{k}\zeta_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}-\frac{1-(-1)^{\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{q}}}}{2^{\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{q}}}}\prod_{q_{k}>1}B_{q_{k}},\quad I_{\mathbf{q}}:=\frac{(-1)^{|\overline{\mathbf{q}}|/2}}{(2\pi)^{|\overline{\mathbf{q}}|}}\prod_{q_{k}>1}q_{j}!,

for
|\overline{\mathbf{q}}|
even. The previous two lemmas give

\sum_{k:q_{k}=1}\zeta_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}=2\zeta(|\overline{\mathbf{q}}|)\sum_{k:q_{k}=1}\left(\frac{1}{\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\mathbf{q}_{k}}}\sum_{k\neq l}\frac{\sigma_{k,l}}{2}+{\mathcal{Q}}_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}\right).

and the identity

\zeta(|\overline{\mathbf{q}}|)=(-1)^{\frac{|\overline{\mathbf{q}}|}{2}+1}\frac{B_{|\overline{\mathbf{q}}|}(2\pi)^{|\overline{\mathbf{q}}|}}{2|\overline{\mathbf{q}}|!},

we get the desired identity.

∎

Remark 5.4
.

In the case
\mathbf{q}
satisfying
q_{k}=1
and
q_{k^{\prime}}=q_{k^{\prime\prime}}=q
for
q\geq 1
we have
\sigma_{k,l}=(-1)^{q}
for all
k\neq l
. Then, the previous identity reduces to

{\mathcal{R}}_{b_{\mathbf{q}}}(\bm{\nu})=S_{b_{\mathbf{q}}}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})=-\frac{B_{2q}q!^{2}\nu_{k}}{(2q)!}\left(\frac{(-1)^{q}}{\nu_{k^{\prime}}^{q}\nu_{k^{\prime\prime}}^{q}}+{\mathcal{Q}}_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}\right)-B_{q}^{2},

for
q>1
. For
q=1
we know that
\sum_{k=0}^{2}\nu_{k}{\mathcal{Q}}_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}=0
, so our expression simplyfies to

{\mathcal{R}}_{b_{\mathbf{q}}}(\bm{\nu})=\frac{\nu_{0}^{2}+\nu_{1}^{2}+\nu_{2}^{2}}{12\nu_{0}\nu_{1}\nu_{2}}-\frac{1}{4}

which is just Dedekind-Rademacher’s reciprocity. In the case
q>1
, observe that the identity for
S_{b_{\mathbf{q}}}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})
can be summed for each
k=0,1,2
. Therefore, denoting
{\mathcal{Q}}_{\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}=\sum_{k=0}^{2}\nu_{k}{\mathcal{Q}}_{k,\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}
, we have an analogue to Dedekind-Rademacher’s reciprocity as follows

\sum_{k=0}^{2}S_{b_{\mathbf{q}}}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})=-\frac{B_{2q}q!^{2}}{(2q)!}\left(\frac{\nu_{0}^{q+1}+\nu_{1}^{q+1}+\nu_{2}^{q+1}}{\nu_{0}^{q}\nu_{1}^{q}\nu_{2}^{q}}(-1)^{q}+{\mathcal{Q}}_{\bm{\nu},\mathbf{q}}\right)-3B_{q}^{2}.

This shows how the study of reciprocities

\sum_{k=0}^{r}S_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k}),

where
\mathbf{f}
is composed of continuous functions in
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
, is reduced to the study of
{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\nu})=S_{\mathbf{f}}(\bm{\nu}|\nu_{k})
replacing
f_{k}
by
b_{1}
.
