Seismic fragility assessment of continuous RC rigid-frame bridges with irregular geometry: insights and recommendations
Authors: Seyed Hadi Rashedi, Alireza Rahai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a detailed seismic fragility and collapse performance assessment of geometrically irregular continuous reinforced concrete rigid-frame (CRCR) bridges based on a representative bridge in Iran. A regular configuration was first established as the reference, after which a systematic set of bridge models was generated by varying the span-length ratio, pier-height ratio, and pier skew angle to investigate their individual and combined effects on seismic response. Detailed three-dimensional nonlinear finite element models were developed in OpenSees and analyzed under a comprehensive set of far-field and near-field earthquake ground motions. Seismic fragility curves were constructed based on pier drift demand corresponding to four damage states ranging from minor damage to collapse, defined by the attainment of the complete damage state. The results indicate that geometric irregularities significantly amplify seismic vulnerability, particularly at higher damage states. Across a range of span-length ratios, bridges with pronounced pier-height irregularity exhibit up to 50–60% higher probabilities of exceeding severe damage at the same intensity level compared to the regular configuration, while the median seismic intensity associated with collapse is reduced by approximately 55% in highly irregular cases. Among the investigated parameters, pier-height irregularity was identified as the most influential factor governing fragility and collapse behavior, followed by pier skewness, whereas span-length irregularity showed a comparatively smaller effect. The resulting fragility functions and collapse metrics provide quantitative insight into the seismic performance of CRCR bridges and support fragility-based assessment and performance-based seismic design in earthquake-prone regions.Keywords: Bridge structure, Seismic performance assessment, Irregularity, Nonlinear structural analysis, Seismic fragility analysisSubject terms: Engineering, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Bridges are vital parts of transportation systems. In regions exposed to seismic hazards, the structural integrity of bridges takes on added significance, as their failure during an earthquake can have catastrophic outcomes. Among the various bridge types, continuous reinforced concrete rigid-frame (CRCR) bridges stand out due to their prevalence and importance in many transportation structures1. The performance of these bridges, which have continuous decks and are supported by piers made of reinforced concrete (RC), is well-known for their ability to handle heavy loads and to function effectively over long spans. However, they are not protected against the destructive forces of earthquakes, and their vulnerability to seismic activity increases as irregularities in geometry and structural details are added. Irregularities in bridges can take diverse forms, and these may encompass variations in span lengths, pier heights, or other geometric parameters, in addition to deviations in reinforcement detailing. They may be intentional or a result of design constraints, construction obstacles, or even the modification of existing bridges. Regardless of their origin, these irregularities can drastically impact the seismic response of bridges. In recent years, researchers have explored the significance of evaluating the vulnerability of bridge systems by considering a variety of factors. Analytical responses are examined in bridge components such as piers, abutments, and superstructures. In addition, consideration is given to the development of fragility curves for different bridge classes and their seismic performance levels. In the following, some of the studies and research that have been carried out on the vulnerability and fragility of these bridges have been presented2.

Seismic fragility analysis is significant in bridge design as it helps assess the vulnerability of bridge structures to earthquakes3. A performance-based seismic fragility assessment of bridges takes into account seismic wave characteristics, bridge structure specifics, potential damage mechanisms, performance criteria, and uncertainties related to seismic events and structural responses. It provides valuable information about the probability of different levels of damage or failure that a bridge may experience under various earthquake intensities4–9. The earthquakes that occurred in 1989 at Loma Prieta, 1994 at Northridge, and 1995 at Kobe marked a significant turning point in the exploration of fragility. A number of researchers collaborated to develop empirical fragility curves that were calculated by the actual damage caused by these three earthquakes10,11. In the field of seismic fragility analysis for bridge structures, several methodologies have been developed to evaluate vulnerability. In general, these approaches can be categorized into three main groups: ones based on expert opinion12, ones based on empirical evidence3,13, and others based on analytical methodologies14,15. Among various methodologies, analytical fragility curve methods have gained widespread acceptance7. In a study by Mosleh et al.16, they conducted a seismic performance analysis on a multi-span bridge using SAP2000 software17. They developed fragility curves from near-field and far-field ground motions to assess damage probabilities, suggesting future research should include additional structural elements for comprehensive fragility curves. Zhang et al.18 focus on steel fiber-reinforced concrete bridge piers, offering insights into damage analysis, structural response under seismic conditions, and probabilistic seismic demand calculations. They provide valuable fragility curves for damage probabilities. Stefanidou et al.19 emphasized seismic vulnerability assessment with a focus on soil-structure interaction, encompassing steps like finite element modeling and limit state thresholds. Pang and Wu20 underscore the importance of assessing the entire bridge system, creating fragility curves for components, and highlighting uncertainty in ground motion. Ren et al.21 and Zhao et al.22 investigated seismic fragility and vulnerability analysis methodology, utilizing cloud approaches, nonlinear time-history analysis, and OpenSees software23,24. They emphasize the consideration of uncertainty in structural parameters. These resources are essential for researchers and engineers working on seismic bridge design and reinforcement in susceptible regions. Zhang et al.25 introduced a method for seismic fragility analysis of bridge piers, employing moment-based techniques to simulate seismic accelerations, calculate structural response moments, and determine failure probabilities through fourth-moment reliability indices. Fragility curves are generated for various PGA values and damage levels, underscoring the role of design response spectrum parameters.

In other recent studies, researchers focused on a seismic fragility analysis of various CRCR bridges, covering structural damage and seismic performance under different conditions and offering findings and design recommendations1. The researchers observed that the traveling wave effect significantly impacts the fragility of long-span CRCR bridges in mountainous areas, with low piers having a higher likelihood of damage than high piers26. Additionally, they analyzed the combined effects of actual site conditions and collisions on the fragility of high-pier, long-span CRCR bridges, which was closely tied to the pier heights in the bridge structure27. Also, using a probabilistic framework, including seismic hazards and fragility curves, the studies assess the structural response and quantify the expected annual losses of rigid-frame bridges28. A substantial body of research has explored the seismic damage mechanisms and vulnerability of CRCR bridges exposed to marine environmental conditions29,30. Jiang et al.31 considered factors such as pile-soil interaction and hydrodynamics in their analysis. Liang et al.32 explored time-varying seismic fragility during a bridge’s service period. Zhang et al.33 emphasized the importance of fuzzy damage criteria and conducted a case study on a Chinese coastal bridge. Liang et al.34 also introduced optimized analysis methods for assessing bridge collapse resistance and robustness, with a specific focus on a four-span offshore CRCR bridge, addressing durability damage and bond-slip effects. Cui et al.35 examined seismic fragility analysis for high-pier CRCR bridges in deep-water areas, addressing hydrodynamic pressure impact and damage states with a focus on aiding design and assessment. These studies collectively stress the significance of various factors in assessing the seismic performance and vulnerability of marine bridges. Furthermore, several aspects that have the potential to exert influence on CRCR bridges, such as material, seismic excitations, long-period seismic waves, etc., were also given proper consideration36–41.

For irregular bridges with varying pier heights and different structural configurations, fragility curves help in understanding the specific vulnerabilities of different components of the bridge1. They provide insights into the likelihood of damage occurrence and the severity of damage for different parts of the bridge under seismic loading. Irregularity in bridge structure, often subject to intense and highly damaging near-fault ground motions, results in a significant reduction in the shear and compressive capacities of their piers, especially when located in near-fault zones, as demonstrated by studies such as Wilson et al.42, Jeon et al.43, Theodoulidis et al.44, Deshan et al.45, and Wei et al.46. In order to investigate the effects of irregularities in the structure of CRCR bridges, some studies have been conducted. Akbari47 examined the seismic fragility of irregular CRCR bridges by varying pier height as a design parameter and highlighted the role of damage and limit states in ensuring seismic safety. The demand-capacity ratio approach reveals that the shorter piers on the irregular bridge are severely damaged during a high-intensity earthquake, while the higher piers maintain elastic. Dong et al.48 presented a theoretical model for assessing seismic vulnerability in irregular CRCR bridges, highlighting bridge piers’ susceptibility to earthquake damage. Chen et al.49 discussed seismic vulnerability and fragility analysis in long-span irregular CRCR bridge structures and emphasize variable seismic damage probabilities among structural components, underscoring the importance of evaluating systemic seismic performance. In the following, Shan et al.50 discussed seismic fragility analysis of non-circular irregular CRCR bridge piers in China, emphasizing unique challenges, proposing a methodology, considering ground motion angles, and providing fragility curves. Overall, these studies contribute to a comprehensive understanding of the effects of irregularities in CRCR bridge seismic vulnerability assessment and design improvement and provide necessary guidance to increase the seismic resilience of these bridges and configurations in earthquake-prone areas.

While a substantial body of research has addressed the seismic vulnerability and fragility of CRCR bridges, the majority of existing studies have examined geometric irregularities independently. In real bridge systems, differences in span length, pier height, and pier skew angle often occur simultaneously and interact, producing coupled seismic response characteristics and localized damage patterns that cannot be captured by analyses focusing on a single parameter. More specifically, the combined effects of pronounced span-length asymmetry, pier-height irregularity, and skewed pier arrangements on the seismic fragility of CRCR bridges remain insufficiently explored. This limitation reduces the reliability of seismic performance prediction and post-earthquake damage assessment for irregular CRCR bridges in seismically active regions, which the present study addresses through the adoption of a comprehensive probabilistic fragility assessment framework.

This study systematically evaluates the seismic vulnerability and fragility of geometrically irregular CRCR bridges using a comprehensive numerical framework. Span-length variation, pier-height non-uniformity, and pier skewness are explicitly incorporated, both separately and in combined scenarios, to account for their coupled influence on seismic response behavior. Detailed three-dimensional nonlinear finite element models are developed in OpenSees23, and seismic demands are evaluated using nonlinear time-history (NTHAs) and incremental dynamic analyses (IDAs) under a set of recorded earthquake ground motions selected in accordance with FEMA P-69551 recommendations, representing both near-field (NF) and far-field (FF) excitation characteristics. Bridge pier drift demand is adopted as the damage index, and four damage states—minor, moderate, severe, and complete—are defined based on established performance criteria52. Seismic fragility curves are subsequently derived within a probabilistic framework that accounts for uncertainties associated with ground motion variability and nonlinear structural response. The resulting fragility functions enable a quantitative comparison of different irregular bridge configurations and identify geometric irregularities that most significantly increase the probability of pier- and system-level failure, providing valuable insight for seismic performance evaluation and design of irregular CRCR bridges.

Methodology

This study adopts a numerical simulation–based framework to evaluate the seismic fragility of CRCR bridges with irregular geometry, using a real bridge in Iran as the case study. A reference bridge with regular geometry was first selected to represent typical CRCR bridges in the national roadway network, and a three-dimensional finite element model (FEM) was developed in OpenSees version 3.2.224, in which material nonlinearity, geometric nonlinearity, and the seismic response of the primary structural components were explicitly considered. Using the reference configuration as a starting point, geometric non-uniformities were introduced by developing multiple irregular bridge models with systematic variations in span-length ratios, pier-height ratios, and pier skew angles. NTHAs were subsequently performed for all configurations using ground motion records consistent with the regional seismic hazard, from which key engineering demand parameters were obtained to evaluate structural performance. Seismic fragility curves were then derived by linking selected ground motion intensity measures with the probability of exceeding predefined damage states, thereby allowing a quantitative evaluation of how geometric irregularities influence the seismic vulnerability of CRCR bridges.

The selection of geometric irregularity parameters and their associated ranges was guided by prior numerical investigations and by national and international design standards, including Iranian bridge design guidelines published by the MRT-RITC53, as well as Caltrans54 and AASHTO55. Vertical irregularity is typically defined using the pier height ratio, expressed as the height of the tallest pier divided by that of the shortest, where ratios of approximately 1.3–1.5 are generally regarded as irregular, and values ranging from 1.5 to 3.0 are frequently documented in analytical studies and actual bridge designs56,57. Longitudinal irregularity is commonly quantified using the span length ratio, which represents the proportion between the shortest and the longest spans. Span length ratios lower than about 0.7–0.8 indicate irregular configurations, whereas ratios between 0.5 and 0.7 are commonly encountered in bridge practice in Iran as well as internationally. The presence of pier or deck skew angles represents a major contributor to plan irregularity. In common classifications, skew angles between 15° and 30° are treated as small, angles in the range of 30°–45° are considered moderate and are often linked to pronounced dynamic effects, while angles exceeding 45° are categorized as large, reflecting severe skew conditions58. Based on available research and data from constructed bridges, the parameter ranges most frequently associated with irregular bridge configurations include pier height ratios of 1.5–3.0, span length ratios of 0.5–0.7, and skew angles in the range of 30°–45°.

In this study, these established parameter ranges were employed to systematically evaluate the influence of geometric irregularities on seismic behavior. The bridge configurations were adopted, with minor adjustments, from a previous numerical investigation on CRCR bridges59, ensuring both realistic representation and comparability with earlier research. Based on this approach, the irregular bridge models were developed by varying three principal geometric parameters, including the span length ratio (L/l), the pier height ratio (h2 /h1), and the pier skew angle. The discrete parameter settings adopted in the study are documented in Table 3, where the span length ratio is evaluated at three levels, the pier height ratio at four levels, and the skew angle across five levels. These values cover regular, moderately irregular, and severely irregular configurations observed in practice.

Through the systematic combination of the parameter values presented in Table 3, the complete set of bridge models was generated. As shown in Table 4, the reference bridge (BM0) is clearly identified, while the remaining models correspond to various irregular configurations derived from this baseline model. It is important to note that to ensure realistic structural behavior and a logical progression of plastic hinge formation across different configurations, all irregular bridge models (BM1 to BM60) were systematically redesigned based on their modified geometries. While the primary objective of this research is not to perform detailed design, but rather to investigate the pure effect of geometric irregularities, applying the exact same pier cross-sections and reinforcement details to configurations with significantly different pier heights or span lengths would not reflect practical design reality and could lead to unrealistic comparative results. Therefore, to achieve meaningful and comparable seismic performance, each bridge model was initially redesigned using CSiBridge software60 in accordance with relevant design codes. This process ensured that for every variation in pier height, span length, or skew angle, the piers possessed appropriate cross-sectional dimensions and reinforcement ratios to withstand the anticipated gravity and seismic demands. Consequently, the subsequent nonlinear analyses in OpenSees24 were performed on consistently designed models, allowing the observed differences in seismic response and fragility to be reliably attributed to the geometric irregularities themselves, rather than to inconsistencies in member capacity.

Numerical modeling

The seismic behavior of the bridge system was investigated using fully three-dimensional nonlinear finite element models developed in OpenSees24. This modeling framework inherently captures the coupled longitudinal, transverse, and torsional responses induced by geometric irregularities, including variations in pier height and pier skewness. Recorded earthquake ground motions were applied to the three-dimensional bridge models, and the seismic response was evaluated through NTHAs and IDAs, allowing the interaction effects among different response components to be explicitly represented.

To evaluate the seismic performance and vulnerability of the bridge piers, IDA was conducted using a selected suite of earthquake ground motion records. Each ground motion record was incrementally scaled to higher intensity levels in order to track the structural response from the elastic range through pronounced nonlinear deformation. Pier displacement demand, expressed in terms of drift ratio, was extracted from the NTHAs and adopted as the engineering demand parameter for damage assessment. Based on these results, seismic fragility curves were derived to quantify the probability of exceeding predefined damage states as a function of seismic intensity. The resulting fragility functions provide a consistent basis for comparing different bridge configurations and for evaluating the influence of geometric irregularities on the seismic response and performance of the bridge system.

Finite element–based numerical model

In this study, a detailed finite element model of the bridge was developed, utilizing nonlinear force-based Beam–Column Fiber elements to simulate the piers. This formulation satisfies equilibrium exactly and captures distributed plasticity along the member height without relying on predefined plastic hinge lengths, thereby offering a more realistic representation of seismic damage progression compared to lumped plasticity models. To ensure accuracy while maintaining computational efficiency, each pier was discretized into five elements based on a convergence study, with five Gauss-Lobatto integration points per element providing critical sampling at the ends where moments are highest. This refined meshing strategy, combined with the fiber section approach, allows the inelastic behavior and curvature distribution to develop naturally based on moment-gradients and material response (Fig. 5).

Structural FEM of the studied bridge.

The pier–girder connection was modeled using Rigid Link Beam elements to represent a fully rigid, non-deformable joint. This modeling strategy ensures consistent transfer of axial forces, shear forces, and bending moments among structural components, while preventing artificial flexibility at component interfaces. An elastic behavior was assumed for the bridge deck in all analyses, given that the investigation primarily concentrates on the seismic performance and vulnerability of the piers. This assumption aligns with previous studies indicating that, for comparable bridge systems, the global seismic response is primarily controlled by pier behavior, although deck inelasticity may add to energy dissipation under intense ground motions. Concrete material behavior was represented using the Concrete0223 constitutive model to simulate both confined and unconfined concrete fibers61,62, whereas the longitudinal reinforcement was modeled using the Steel0223 material model. These constitutive models were chosen because of their established ability to capture stiffness degradation, strength deterioration, and hysteretic response under cyclic loading.

Ground motion selection

For the seismic performance evaluation carried out in this study, NTHA and IDA were conducted using recorded earthquake ground motions, which were chosen based on the selection criteria specified in FEMA P-69551. The selected dataset includes both far-field (FF) and near-field (NF) ground motions in order to capture a wide range of earthquake characteristics, including variations in magnitude, source-to-site distance, site conditions, and frequency content, as well as potential near-fault pulse (NFP) effects that may significantly influence nonlinear structural response.

A total set of ground motion records was adopted. For each earthquake event, the two recorded horizontal components were retained and, where required, rotated to align with the bridge longitudinal and transverse axes. The resulting longitudinal and transverse acceleration time histories were then applied simultaneously as bidirectional uniform support excitations in the nonlinear analyses. For record characterization and scaling purposes, an equivalent scalar horizontal intensity was computed using the square-root-of-sum-of-squares (SRSS) combination of the two components. For the NTHAs, the selected records were scaled in accordance with ASCE 7–1663. A single scale factor was determined for each record based on an equivalent horizontal response spectrum obtained by combining the two horizontal components using an SRSS-based definition. The scaling was performed such that the mean equivalent horizontal spectrum of at least 11 scaled records meets or exceeds the target design spectrum of the code over the period range of 0.2 T to 2.0 T (where T is the period of the main mode). The obtained scale factor was then applied identically to both horizontal components, which were subsequently imposed simultaneously along the longitudinal and transverse directions of the bridge. The compatibility between the scaled ground motion spectra and the target design spectrum within this period range is illustrated in Fig. 6, where the mean spectrum of the scaled records closely follows the target spectrum and the individual spectra collectively envelope it, confirming compliance with code-based spectral matching requirements.

Target design response spectrum and response spectra of scaled ground motions.

In this study, the nonlinear analyses explicitly accounted for concurrent two-component horizontal input motions; this procedure was consistently adopted for conducting IDA across all bridge models investigated. For the IDA, the same set of NF and FF ground motions was employed following the FEMA P-69551 procedure. The earthquake intensity was parameterized using the first-mode spectral acceleration, Sa (T1), where T1 denotes the fundamental period of the bridge system obtained from the three-dimensional modal analysis of the corresponding model, and each record was incrementally scaled to multiple intensity levels. At each IDA step, the same scale factor was applied to both horizontal components, which were then imposed simultaneously along the longitudinal and transverse directions of the bridge. The corresponding structural responses were traced until the onset of severe nonlinear behavior or numerical instability, enabling construction of IDA curves describing the progression of response from elastic behavior to highly nonlinear and near-collapse states.

Fragility curve

Seismic fragility analysis was employed to quantify the probability of damage in the studied bridge structures under increasing earthquake intensity levels. Fragility curves provide a probabilistic framework to evaluate structural vulnerability by expressing the likelihood that the structural demand (D) exceeds its capacity (C) for a given ground motion intensity measure (IM)64. where Pf denotes the probability of reaching or exceeding a specified damage state conditioned on the seismic intensity measure.

In performance-based seismic assessment, fragility curves, limit states, and damage indices are closely interrelated. Limit states represent discrete levels of structural performance degradation and are commonly classified as slight, moderate, extensive, and complete damage. These states correspond to progressively increasing levels of physical damage and loss of functionality in bridge components and the overall structural system.

In RC bridge structures, various engineering demand parameters have been employed as damage indices, including lateral displacement, drift ratio, curvature ductility, rotational ductility, and energy dissipation capacity65,66. Among these, displacement- and drift-based indices are widely adopted due to their clear physical interpretation and strong correlation with observed damage in bridge piers. Numerous experimental and numerical studies have demonstrated that RC bridge piers govern the global seismic response, making pier-level demand parameters particularly suitable for fragility assessment14,52,67–69.

In this study, pier drift ratio was adopted as the engineering demand parameter (EDP) for fragility assessment. For each pier, drift ratios in the longitudinal and transverse directions were computed from the relative pier-top to pier-base displacements normalized by the effective pier height. Under simultaneous two-component horizontal excitation, the directional drift ratios were combined at each time step using the SRSS measure, and the peak SRSS drift ratio was extracted for each pier. The governing EDP for a given analysis was defined as the maximum of these peak values across all piers. Damage state thresholds were established using the median SRSS drift-ratio capacities reported by Mackie et al52. for the corresponding limit states. These limits are characterized by a median drift ratio and an associated lognormal standard deviation and are summarized in Table 6.

The material parameters for concrete and reinforcing steel were defined using experimentally obtained data from Zong et al71., and the scaled specimen was formulated under similitude theory so that its geometry, material behavior, and dynamic characteristics matched those of prototype CRCR bridges. Within this modeling context, comparative evaluations were performed between test results and finite element analysis predictions, examining modal characteristics as well as peak displacement and acceleration responses.

Under seismic loading, bridge piers are expected to exhibit nonlinear elasto-plastic behavior, particularly under moderate to strong ground motions. To capture this response, the piers were modeled using nonlinear beam-column fiber elements, which allow for the explicit representation of distributed plasticity along the member length through fiber-section discretization. This modeling strategy allows the representation of stiffness loss and energy dissipation mechanisms typically observed in RC under cyclic loading. In the CRCR bridge model, the pier–girder joints were modeled using Rigid Link Beam elements to ensure proper transmission of internal forces and bending moments without introducing unintended flexibility at the interface.

The concrete constitutive response was represented using the uniaxial Concrete02 material model23,72, which accounts for nonlinear behavior in compression together with linear softening in tension. Numerical results

This section presents an evaluation of the seismic response characteristics of the studied CRCR bridge models based on NTHAs and IDAs. The numerical results provide a foundation for quantifying the evolution of pier drift demand under increasing seismic intensity and for establishing a reference response against which the effects of geometric irregularities are examined in subsequent sections.

The seismic response of the reference bridge with regular geometry (BM0) is first investigated to establish a baseline for comparison. The IDA results shown in Fig. 11 illustrate the relationship between pier drift demand and the first-mode spectral acceleration, Sa (T1). At low intensity levels, the structural response remains predominantly linear, exhibiting limited dispersion among ground motion records. As the seismic intensity increases, stiffness degradation and material nonlinearity progressively develop, leading to a noticeable increase in response variability associated with record-to-record uncertainty. At higher intensity levels, a rapid escalation in pier drift demand is observed, indicating the development of extensive nonlinear deformation and the onset of severe damage or collapse mechanisms.

IDA curves of the case study CRCR bridge (BM0).

The relatively smooth and stable trend of the IDA curves for BM0 reflects a well-balanced distribution of stiffness and strength in the regular bridge configuration. This response behavior provides a reliable benchmark for subsequent comparisons and enables a clear identification of demand amplification and fragility shifts induced by geometric irregularities in the following analyses.

Effect of span length and pier height ratios

Variations in span length ratio modify the longitudinal stiffness distribution and dynamic characteristics of CRCR bridges, which in turn influence seismic force demand, deformation patterns, and damage concentration in the piers. Similarly, non-uniformity in pier height introduces significant stiffness and strength irregularities in CRCR bridge systems, leading to pronounced differences in seismic demand distribution and damage localization among individual piers. The numerical results presented in this section quantify the effects of span-length asymmetry and pier-height irregularity on pier drift demand and seismic fragility under increasing earthquake intensity, with emphasis on comparative response trends among regular and irregular bridge configurations.

The IDA results shown in Figs. 12, 13 and 14 demonstrate that, for all investigated configurations, the initial portion of the IDA curves exhibits an approximately linear trend corresponding to the predominantly elastic response of the bridge system. In this elastic range, the initial slope of the IDA curves is primarily governed by the global stiffness of the structure, such that stiffer bridge configurations exhibit steeper slopes. Bridges with uniform pier heights (h2/h1 = 1.0) consistently show higher initial stiffness, whereas increasing pier-height ratios result in flatter initial IDA branches, reflecting reduced elastic stiffness and increased flexibility.

As illustrated in Fig. 12 for bridges with pronounced span-length asymmetry (L/l = 0.5), increasing the pier height ratio from 1.0 to 3.0 leads to a substantial amplification of pier drift demand at comparable seismic intensity levels. At moderate intensity levels, the median pier drift demand for bridges with h2/h1 = 3.0 is approximately 1.5 to 2.0 times that of the regular-height configuration, indicating strong stiffness concentration and deformation localization in the shorter pier. At higher intensity levels, this effect becomes more pronounced, with collapse occurring at spectral acceleration levels that are approximately 45–55% lower than those associated with bridges with uniform pier heights.

Similar response trends are observed in Fig. 13 for bridges with moderate span-length irregularity (L/l = 0.7). Although the overall seismic demand is slightly reduced compared to the L/l = 0.5 configurations, increasing the pier-height ratio still results in a marked reduction in seismic capacity. For example, bridges with h2/h1 = 3.0 exhibit collapse-level spectral accelerations that are on the order of 55% lower than those of bridges with uniform pier heights, confirming that vertical irregularity remains the dominant factor governing seismic vulnerability even when span-length asymmetry is less severe.

In contrast, Fig. 14 shows that for bridges with uniform span lengths (L/l = 1.0), the detrimental effects of pier-height irregularity are partially mitigated in the elastic and early nonlinear response ranges. Nevertheless, as seismic intensity increases, significant amplification of pier drift demand is still observed for configurations with large pier-height ratios. This indicates that pier-height irregularity alone can substantially degrade seismic performance, even in the absence of longitudinal geometric irregularity, particularly at intensity levels associated with severe damage and collapse.

Across all span-length configurations, increasing seismic intensity leads to a noticeable increase in dispersion of the IDA curves, reflecting enhanced record-to-record variability. This effect is especially pronounced under NF ground motions, where pulse-like characteristics induce abrupt increases in pier drift demand and accelerate the transition from moderate damage to collapse. Compared with FF records, NF excitations consistently produce higher drift demands at equivalent intensity measures, with the most severe amplification observed in bridges with large pier-height ratios and short effective stiffness paths. Overall, the results presented in Figs. 12, 13 and 14 indicate that while span-length irregularity influences the longitudinal distribution of stiffness and seismic demand, pier-height irregularity is the governing parameter controlling drift amplification, damage concentration, and collapse capacity. The combined effects of reduced elastic stiffness, increased nonlinear deformation demand, and the amplified response induced by NF ground motions lead to a pronounced increase in seismic vulnerability for CRCR bridges with highly non-uniform pier heights.

Effect of skew angle of piers

Pier skewness alters the load transfer mechanism and induces coupled longitudinal–transverse–torsional responses in CRCR bridges, which can significantly influence seismic demand distribution and damage progression. The IDA results presented in Fig. 15 illustrate the effect of pier skew angle on pier drift demand for bridge configurations with different pier-height ratios under increasing seismic intensity.

IDA curves of the: (a) BM4 (); (b) BM9 (); (c) BM14 (); and (d) BM19 ().

For all skewed configurations, the initial segments of the IDA curves exhibit an approximately linear trend corresponding to the elastic response of the bridge system. In this range, the initial slope of the IDA curves is primarily controlled by the effective lateral stiffness, which is reduced as the skew angle increases due to torsional deformation and reduced alignment between inertia forces and principal stiffness directions. Consequently, bridges with larger skew angles show slightly flatter initial IDA branches compared to non-skewed configurations, indicating reduced elastic stiffness.

As seismic intensity increases, the influence of skew angle becomes more pronounced. As shown in Fig. 15, increasing the skew angle to leads to a systematic amplification of pier drift demand across all pier-height ratios. At comparable intensity levels, skewed configurations exhibit median drift demands approximately 20–30% higher than their non-skewed counterparts, with the amplification becoming more significant in bridges with larger pier-height ratios. This behavior reflects the combined effects of torsional response, uneven force distribution among piers, and localized demand concentration in the shorter pier. At higher intensity levels, skewed bridges experience an earlier onset of severe nonlinear behavior and collapse compared to non-skewed configurations. For bridges with pronounced pier-height irregularity (h2/h1 = 3.0), the collapse-level spectral acceleration under skewed conditions is reduced by approximately 25–35%, indicating a notable decrease in seismic capacity attributable to skew-induced torsional effects.

In addition, NF ground motions further exacerbate the adverse impact of pier skewness. Pulse-like NF records produce abrupt increases in pier drift demand, particularly in skewed configurations, accelerating the transition from moderate damage to collapse. Compared with FF excitations, NF motions consistently result in higher drift demands at equivalent intensity measures, highlighting the increased vulnerability of skewed CRCR bridges subjected to near-fault seismic excitation. The results presented in Fig. 15 demonstrate that pier skewness plays a significant role in amplifying seismic demand and reducing collapse capacity through the introduction of torsional response components and stiffness eccentricity. Although skew angle alone exerts a less pronounced influence than pier-height irregularity, its interaction with vertical irregularity and NF ground motions leads to a substantial increase in seismic vulnerability. These findings highlight the necessity of explicitly incorporating skew effects in the seismic assessment and design of CRCR bridges.

This behavior reflects increased sensitivity to ground motion characteristics and is strongly influenced by the inclusion of NF and pulse-like ground motions in the record set. Discussion

The results of this study provide a comprehensive understanding of how geometric irregularities influence the seismic response, fragility, and collapse safety of CRCR bridges. By systematically varying span-length ratio, pier-height ratio, and pier skew angle, and by evaluating both deterministic response measures and probabilistic performance indicators, the analysis reveals the governing mechanisms through which geometric irregularities amplify seismic vulnerability, particularly at collapse-related limit states.

A primary finding indicates that pier-height irregularity consistently emerges as the dominant contributor to seismic vulnerability, irrespective of span configuration. While span-length asymmetry modifies the longitudinal stiffness distribution and dynamic characteristics of the bridge system, vertical non-uniformity directly governs deformation localization by concentrating stiffness and strength demands in the shorter pier. This phenomenon arises from the significant stiffness disparity between adjacent piers, where the shorter, considerably stiffer pier attracts a disproportionately higher share of the seismic lateral force. Consequently, this results in deformation concentration in the short pier, which experiences much higher curvature ductility demand and earlier plastic hinging compared to the taller, more flexible pier. This mechanism is reflected in both the IDA results, where an increased pier-height ratio leads to flatter elastic slopes and earlier nonlinear escalation, and in the fragility analysis, where Severe and Complete damage states exhibit pronounced leftward shifts. Ultimately, the acceleration of damage localization hastens stiffness degradation and strength deterioration in the short pier, leading to earlier collapse; this explains why increasing the height ratio consistently reduces seismic capacity, highlighting the critical role of pier-height irregularity in controlling post-yield behavior and stability.

Span-length asymmetry functions as a vulnerability amplifier by exacerbating the adverse effects of vertical irregularity, fundamentally altering the longitudinal mass distribution and stiffness continuity of the superstructure. When spans are unequal, the dynamic characteristics of adjacent spans differ, leading to out-of-phase vibrations during seismic excitation; this asynchronous movement imposes additional deformation demands on the connecting piers, effectively amplifying drift demands and modifying the distribution of inertial forces. Consequently, bridges with pronounced longitudinal asymmetry exhibit lower effective stiffness and greater demand redistribution among piers, which accelerates damage progression when combined with height non-uniformity. This interaction is evident in fragility comparisons, where bridges with a span ratio of 0.5 show substantially higher probabilities of exceeding Severe and Complete damage states at a given intensity level than those with more regular spans. Conversely, the results demonstrate that maintaining longitudinal regularity provides a stabilizing effect, significantly improving collapse resistance even in the presence of pier-height irregularity. This finding underscores the importance of considering longitudinal stiffness balance as a key design and assessment parameter for CRCR bridges, as the asynchronous span motion compounds the deformation concentration caused by stiffness disparity.

Pier skewness introduces an additional layer of complexity by creating geometric eccentricity between the center of mass and the center of stiffness, thereby inducing a coupled translational–torsional response and reducing effective lateral stiffness. Under lateral excitation, this eccentricity generates a response where lateral displacement is accompanied by superstructure rotation, increasing deformation demands on exterior piers and causing uneven force distribution among piers. As the skew angle increases, the torsional stiffness contribution becomes more significant, resulting in longer periods and amplifying drift demands. Although skewness alone has a secondary effect compared to pier-height irregularity, its interaction with longitudinal and vertical irregularities leads to a disproportionate increase in seismic vulnerability. Consequently, skewed configurations consistently exhibit higher probabilities of exceeding collapse-related damage states and lower collapse safety margins than their non-skewed counterparts at comparable geometric ratios. This behavior reflects the acceleration of nonlinear deformation and instability due to uneven force redistribution and torsional demand concentration, particularly in systems already suffering from stiffness irregularity.

The inclusion of NF and pulse-like ground motions plays a critical role in shaping fragility and collapse trends. NF excitations amplify deformation demands and broaden the transition regions of fragility curves, particularly for geometrically irregular bridges. This study provides a comparative assessment of NF versus FF effects, demonstrating that NF excitations reduce the median collapse intensity by 20–50% compared to FF records. Notably, the response gap between NF and FF records narrows as geometric irregularities intensify, indicating that highly irregular bridges become increasingly sensitive to record-to-record variability, which has important implications for probabilistic performance assessment and risk-informed decision-making.

From an engineering perspective, the findings of this study have direct implications for the seismic design and assessment of CRCR bridges. The results suggest that limiting pier-height irregularity should be a primary objective, particularly in bridges with unavoidable span-length asymmetry or skewness. When geometric irregularities cannot be eliminated, preserving longitudinal regularity and mitigating torsional response can significantly enhance collapse safety. Mechanistically, pier height disparity creates stiffness concentration that forces the shorter pier to attract a disproportionate share of seismic demand, while span length asymmetry induces asynchronous vibrations that amplify deformation on the overstressed pier. Furthermore, pier skewness introduces geometric eccentricity, generating coupled translational-torsional response that unevenly distributes forces. This combination of mechanisms explains why configurations with combined irregularities exhibit disproportionately higher vulnerability, highlighting the limitations of assessment approaches focused solely on lower damage levels and underscoring the need for collapse-oriented evaluation frameworks.

Conclusions

This study investigated the seismic performance of irregular CRCR bridges through detailed numerical modeling, NTHAs, IDAs, fragility assessment, and collapse safety evaluation. A systematic set of bridge configurations was developed by varying span-length ratio, pier-height ratio, and pier skew angle to quantify the influence of geometric irregularities on structural response, damage progression, and collapse-related performance. Limitations

Despite the comprehensive analytical framework adopted in this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. The bridge deck was modeled as elastic, consistent with the observed dominance of pier response in governing global seismic behavior; however, nonlinear deck response may influence seismic performance in other bridge typologies or detailing scenarios. Soil–structure interaction effects were not considered, and all bridge models were assumed to have fixed-base boundary conditions, which may affect the estimated dynamic characteristics and collapse capacity, particularly for soft soil sites. In addition, only horizontal ground-motion components were included, while vertical ground motions—which may be significant for near-fault earthquakes—were not explicitly considered. Finally, the results are based on a specific CRCR bridge typology and selected ranges of geometric parameters, and caution should be exercised when generalizing the findings to other bridge systems.

Recommendations

Based on the findings of this study, several recommendations can be made for seismic design, assessment, and future research. From a design and assessment perspective, limiting pier-height irregularity should be considered a primary objective, as vertical non-uniformity was identified as the dominant driver of seismic vulnerability. Where geometric irregularities are unavoidable, maintaining longitudinal regularity and minimizing span-length disparity can significantly enhance collapse resistance. For bridges with notable skewness, torsional effects should be explicitly accounted for through improved detailing, supplemental damping, or seismic isolation strategies. For existing bridges exhibiting severe geometric irregularities, retrofit measures such as pier strengthening, jacketing of shorter piers, and energy dissipation devices are recommended. Future research should extend the present work by incorporating nonlinear deck behavior, soil–structure interaction, vertical ground-motion components, and experimental validation to further improve fragility-based and collapse-oriented seismic performance assessment of irregular CRCR bridges.
