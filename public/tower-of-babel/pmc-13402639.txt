Foliar silicon enhances drought resilience and productivity in buckwheat by stabilizing photosynthetic performance
Authors: Md. Intesaful Haque, Bedabrata Saha, Radosław Juszczak, Anshu Rastogi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Drought stress is a major constraint to buckwheat (Fagopyrum esculentum Moench) productivity, primarily by impairing photosynthesis, growth, and reproductive output. This study investigated foliar-applied silicon (Si; 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mM) in mitigating drought stress. Drought reduced growth traits and seed yield, with decreased CO2 assimilation, electron transport rate (ETR), Photosystem II efficiency, and relative water content (RWC), while electrolyte leakage (EL) increased. The OJIP transient showed reduced maximum fluorescence (Fm), indicating photoinhibition. Additionally, the difference kinetics (ΔW) analysis revealed over-reduction of the plastoquinone (PQ) pool, restricted QA-QB electron transport, and partial PSI acceptor limitations under drought. Foliar Si, particularly at 1.0 mM, alleviated these effects by maintaining PSII function, stabilizing ETR, reducing PQ over-reduction, and improving light use. Si enhanced water relations by lowering EL and sustaining RWC, supporting cellular stability. Consequently, 1.0 mM Si restored biomass and seed yield to levels comparable to well-watered plants, whereas 0.5 and 2.0 mM Si provided partial mitigation. Following re-irrigation, most parameters partially or fully recovered, highlighting buckwheat resilience. The results indicate that Si acts as a physiological modulator integrating photochemical protection, water retention, and growth stabilization, with optimal concentration crucial for maximizing drought resilience and productivity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53286-w.

Introduction

Climate change, characterized by rising temperatures, altered precipitation patterns, and an increasing frequency of extreme weather events, has emerged as a major constraint on global agricultural productivity, causing substantial direct and indirect economic losses1–4. Sudden drought events affect crop yields for approximately 55 million people globally, causing annual losses of roughly US$5.4 billion5. Recent climatic extremes highlight the severity of this challenge, with multiple instances of unprecedented droughts in Europe, including the exceptionally dry summer of 20226. The combination of prolonged heatwaves and exceptional water scarcity resulted in widespread agricultural losses, including an estimated 10% reduction in cereal production across the European Union during both 2022 and 20237.

Buckwheat (Fagopyrum esculentum Moench), a fast-growing annual species of the Polygonaceae family, is cultivated worldwide for its nutritionally rich pseudocereal grains and its multifunctional role as a summer cover crop. Owing to its short growth cycle (10-12 weeks), rapid establishment, and efficient utilization of limited soil nutrients, buckwheat is well suited for cultivation on marginal lands where conventional cereals often fail8–10. In addition, buckwheat flowers are highly attractive to pollinators, which contributes positively to seed setting and yield stability11. In many regions of Eastern Europe and Asia, buckwheat remains an economically and ecologically important crop supporting rural livelihoods and diversified farming systems, serving as a bridge between agricultural production and environmental sustainability12. Studies have shown that buckwheat grown as a full-season crop develops a higher leaf area index, produces more flower clusters, forms better-developed seeds, and achieves up to 42% higher yield compared to buckwheat cultivated as a stubble crop13.

The buckwheat genotype “Smuga” is a short-season, fast-maturing Polish cultivar of common buckwheat, registered in 2019, developed for temperate regions with limited growing periods, and characterized by high yield potential, enhanced lodging resistance, and greater drought tolerance compared to other Polish varieties such as Kora and Panda14. Its reported responsiveness to foliar nutrient inputs makes “buckwheat” a suitable model for evaluating strategies aimed at improving stress resilience through external applications15.

Despite these advantages, drought stress remains a major constraint to buckwheat productivity16,17, as it induces osmotic imbalance and disrupts membrane stability, increasing metabolite leakage and potentially causing cell death18. Additionally, water deficits reduce soil moisture availability, lowering leaf water potential and relative water content (RWC)19,20.

Drought induces abscisic acid (ABA) production, causes stomatal closure to conserve water, thereby restricting CO2 diffusion into the leaf and, together with non-stomatal limitations, suppressing net photosynthetic rate, and ultimately reducing carbon availability for growth21. Declines in chlorophyll fluorescence (OJIP transient curve), photosystem II efficiency (ΦPSII), electron transport rate (ETR), and chlorophyll stability are commonly observed under drought, reflecting damage to the photosynthetic machinery and increased susceptibility to oxidative stress14. At the cellular level, dehydration disrupts the metabolic homeostasis, ultimately reducing biomass accumulation and reproductive development22. Buckwheat is particularly sensitive to water deficits during the flowering, a stage that accounts for most of its total water demand, when moisture limitation leads to poor pollination, reduced seed setting, and sharp yield declines23. Drought stress compromise stomatal regulation, enhance oxidative damage, accelerate leaf senescence, and weaken the plant’s capacity to maintain physiological stability, resulting in lower grain quality, and significant economic losses21,24,25.

Silicon (Si), although not classified as an essential plant nutrient, is the second most abundant element in the Earth’s crust and has been widely recognized for its beneficial role in enhancing plant tolerance to abiotic stresses26,27. Si accumulation strengthens cell walls, enhances root development, and improves water uptake, while its rapid absorption through the leaf cuticle, stomata, and trichomes allows Si to quickly support physiological mechanisms that mitigate stress28. Under drought stress, Si enhances plant water regulation by stimulating ABA accumulation and controlled stomatal closure to limit transpirational water loss, while Si application further improve leaf pigment content, photosynthetic performance, stomatal function, hydraulic conductance, transpiration stability, and leaf turgor, thereby strengthening overall drought tolerance29–32. In addition, recent studies suggest that auxin plays a role in coordinating growth and stress adaptation under drought, highlighting the importance of hormonal crosstalk in regulating plant responses. In this context, the beneficial effects of Si may be associated with modulation of hormone-mediated pathways, including ABA and auxin signaling33. Recent studies indicate that silicon-mediated drought tolerance is closely associated with hormonal crosstalk, particularly involving ABA and auxin signaling pathways that regulate stomatal behavior and stress adaptation34,35. Moreover, Si modulates antioxidant defence responses by reducing oxidative damage to cellular membranes and chloroplasts, thereby protects the photosynthetic machinery36. In addition, Si has been reported in tomato to maintain the integrity of thylakoid membranes, protect photosystem II activity, and sustain higher photochemical efficiency by stabilizing chloroplast ultrastructure and reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation37. These coordinated effects contribute to improved biomass production, physiological resilience, and yield performance under drought stress38.

Despite growing evidence for the beneficial role of Si in enhancing drought tolerance, the concentration-dependent effectiveness of foliar-applied Si and its influence on photosynthetic performance, biomass accumulation, and yield recovery during progressive drought and post-stress rehydration remain insufficiently understood. This knowledge gap is particularly relevant for buckwheat, a short-season crop often cultivated under rainfed and marginal conditions where water availability is highly variable. Therefore, the present study is aimed to evaluate the effects of different foliar Si concentrations on plant physiology, growth, and yield parameters of buckwheat under controlled drought stress and subsequent recovery. By integrating physiological, morphological, and yield-related responses, this study tests the following hypotheses: (H1) foliar Si improves drought tolerance in a non-linear, concentration-dependent manner; (H2) Si-mediated protection operates through combined effects on photochemistry (PSII stability) and water relations and (H3) an optimal Si concentration (1.0 mM) enables physiological and yield recovery comparable to well-watered conditions while minimizing potential negative effects under non-stress conditions.

Material and method

Plant material and growth conditions

Seeds of Fagopyrum esculentum Moench (cv. ‘Smuga’; supplier: MHR Kraków, Poland; www.mhr.com.pl, Logistic Sp. z o.o.) were rinsed under tap water and surface-sterilized in 1% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite (NaClO) for 5 min, followed by three rinses with deionized water (3 × 50 mL). Seeds were placed on Petri dishes lined with sterile paper towels moistened with 5 mL sterile water and incubated in darkness at 25 ± 1 °C for 72 h until radicle emergence (seed soaking and germination: 27-30 June 2025). Germinated seedlings were transplanted into pots of approximately 1 L volume (height 150 mm, diameter 125 mm) filled with a peat-based substrate (200 g weight), which, due to its low bulk density, fully occupied the container. After initial establishment seedlings were transferred to semi-controlled glasshouse conditions under natural daylight, with an average day/night temperature of 30/18 °C and relative humidity of 65-75%. Seedlings were thinned to one uniform plant per pot at 10 Days after Sowing (10 DAS).

Experimental design and treatments

Uniform plants were selected 15 DAS and assigned to eight treatment groups, each consisting of five biological replicates (n = 5), where each replicate represented an individual plant. Plants were spatially randomized to minimize positional effects. Two irrigation regimes were established (i) a well-watered control, maintained at 80-90% field capacity (FC), and (ii) a drought treatment, maintained at 30% FC by daily gravimetric monitoring and rewatering to a predetermined target weight. FC (Wwet) was determined by saturating the substrate and allowing excess water to drain freely, while Wdry represents the dry weight of the substrate. Target pot weights (Wtarget) corresponding to 80–90% (well-watered) and 30% (drought) substrate moisture were calculated as: Wtarget = Wdry + f × (Wwet − Wdry), where f = 0.80–0.90 for well-watered conditions and f = 0.30 for drought. Pots were weighed daily and water was added to maintain the desired moisture level.

Drought stress was initiated at 23 days after sowing by withholding irrigation and maintained until 40 DAS, resulting in a total drought duration of 17 days, after which drought-stressed plants were re-irrigated to field capacity for recovery.

Si was supplied as sodium silicate (Na₂SiO₃·9H₂O; Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) and applied as a foliar spray at three concentrations (0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mM) for both irrigation regimes. This resulted in the following treatments: well-watered (WW), drought-stressed (DS), WW + 0.5 mM Si, WW + 1.0 mM Si, WW + 2.0 mM Si, DS + 0.5 mM Si, DS + 1.0 mM Si, and DS + 2.0 mM Si. Si solutions were freshly prepared and applied twice during early vegetative growth, with the first application coinciding with the initiation of drought stress (21 DAS) and the second application administered one week later (28 DAS). Each plant received approximately 10 mL of spray solution using a hand-held sprayer, applied between 09:00 and 10:00 h until uniform wetting of the leaf surface. No surfactant was added. Plants in the non-Si control treatments received distilled water sprays.

All plants were grown under semi-controlled glasshouse conditions throughout the experiment. Measurements were taken at four time points: 25 DAS during early drought stress, 32 DAS (29 July 2025) during progressive drought stress, 39 DAS at peak or prolong drought stress, and 48 DAS during the recovery phase, nine days after re-irrigation. After recovery, plants were maintained under normal irrigation conditions until maturity and were harvested at 112 DAS (17 October 2025) for biomass and yield determination (Fig. 1). Measurement of plant growth and leaf morphological traits

Plant height, stem diameter, and leaf traits were measured weekly at four time points for all plants under both control and drought treatments. Plant height was recorded from the soil surface to the apex of the main stem using a ruler or measuring tape, while stem diameter was measured 1 cm above the soil surface with a digital Vernier caliper. Leaf area was assessed from the top, third fully expanded leaf using images captured against a white background with a scale and analyzed with ImageJ39, and leaf number per plant was recorded at each sampling. Phenotypic changes were documented weekly using standardized digital photographs taken at a fixed distance and angle under uniform lighting.

Electrolyte leakage (EL) and relative water content (RWC) were assessed after two weeks of growth under stress conditions. Gas exchange and chlorophyll fluorescence measurements

Gas-exchange parameters were measured using a GFS-3000 portable photosynthesis system (WALZ GmbH, Germany). Measurements were conducted on fully expanded leaves between 11:00 AM to 14:00 PM to avoid diurnal variability. The chamber conditions were standardized using a photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) of 1000 µmol m⁻2 s⁻1, supplied by the integrated LED light source (3057-FL, Walz, Germany). The reference CO2 concentration was maintained at 400 ppm, relative humidity was controlled using a water vapor concentration of 17,000 ppm, and the flow rate was set to 750 µmol s-1. Leaf temperature was allowed to equilibrate to ambient conditions and remained stable throughout each measurement. For each plant, gas-exchange parameters, including net photosynthetic rate (A), stomatal conductance (gs), intercellular CO2 concentration (Ci), and electron transport rate (ETR), were recorded after the leaf reached steady state. Immediately following gas-exchange measurement, pulse-amplitude-modulated chlorophyll fluorescence was recorded from the same leaf area using the integrated fluorescence module. All measurements were completed within 3 h to minimize environmental variation.

Chlorophyll a fluorescence is a widely used technique for evaluating plant responses to abiotic stress, based on the measurement of light re-emitted by chlorophyll-a during photosynthesis. In this study, fluorescence was measured using a FluorPen (PSI, Czech Republic) following the manufacturer’s standard (OJIP) protocols. Measurements were taken four times at weekly intervals; fluorescence was recorded between 11:00 AM and 12:00 PM to maintain consistent diurnal conditions. For the dark-adapted protocol (OJIP), leaves were dark-adapted for 20 minutes using a dark clip before measurement. For each plant, fluorescence was measured on two leaves, and the mean value was used for analysis. Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters, including F0, and Fm, were measured, and fast fluorescence kinetics (OJIP transients) were analyzed based on the O–J–I–P phases. Normalized fluorescence was calculated as (Ft/F0), and relative variable fluorescence as Vt = (Ft – F0) / (Fm – F0) for construction of relative OJIP curves. OJIP phase increments (ΔWOJ, ΔWJI, and ΔWIP) were derived according to standard PSI analysis procedures42,43. Phase-specific analysis was performed using normalized variable fluorescence transients, where ΔWOJ = (Ft − F0)/(Fj − F0), ΔWJI = (Ft – Fj)/(Fi – Fj), ΔWIP = (Ft – Fi)/(Fp – Fi), describing the kinetics of the O–J, J–I, and I–P phases, respectively. The parameter ΔW was calculated as the difference between initial and final normalized variable fluorescence values for each OJIP phase, expressed as ΔW = (W initial - W final) (Supplementary Table 1)44.

Statistical analysis

Data were tested for normality and homogeneity of variance prior to analysis. The effects of irrigation regime (well-watered vs. drought), silicon concentration (Si- 0, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mM), and their interaction were evaluated using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). In addition, for each variable, differences among individual treatment combinations were assessed using one-way ANOVA, in which each irrigation × Si combination was treated as an independent level. This approach allowed direct comparison among all experimental variants within a single model. When significant effects were detected (p < 0.05), post hoc multiple comparisons were performed using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test. Results are presented as mean ± standard error (SE). Superscript letters derived from Tukey’s test indicate statistically significant differences among treatment combinations. For each trait, F-values and corresponding p-values from the two-way ANOVA are reported in the figures.

Pearson correlation analysis was performed using data from the prolonged drought stage to evaluate relationships among physiological, growth, and yield parameters. Correlation coefficients (r) and their significance (p-values) were calculated using pairwise complete observations. Significance levels are discussed as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001. The analysis was performed using pooled data across all treatments (n = 5).

All statistical analyses were performed using (RStudio version 4.5.2 and jamovi version 2.7.6). Data are reported as mean ± standard error (SE).

Results

The primary aim of this study was to determine an effective foliar Si doses for drought stress amelioration in buckwheat. Drought stress occurs when water availability is insufficient to meet the physiological demand in plant, resulting in impaired parameters of photosystem II (PSII) efficiency, altered gas exchange, and overall growth inhibition. Here we aimed to evaluate the potential protective role of Si foliar spray in enhancing plant resilience to prolonged drought conditions.

Effects of drought and silicon on growth traits (stem diameter, plant height, and leaf area)

Stem diameter and plant height responded differently to drought stress and Si application over time. At the first measurement, stem diameter and plant height were comparable across all treatments, indicating uniform growth and no early effects of drought or Si application (Fig. 2a, e). From the second measurement onward, drought stress significantly reduced stem diameter and progressively limited plant height compared with well-watered plants (Fig. 2b-c, f-g). Under well-watered conditions, Si application had no significant effect on either trait. Under drought stress, however, 1.0 mM Si significantly alleviated the reduction in stem diameter, whereas 0.5 mM Si showed limited effectiveness and 2.0 mM Si produced an intermediate response (Fig. 2b-c). Similarly, Si-treated drought-stressed plants maintained greater plant height than untreated drought-stressed plants during the stress period (Fig. 2f-g). During the recovery phase, stem diameter in drought-stressed plants remained significantly lower than in well-watered plants; however, 1.0 mM Si fully restored stem diameter to levels comparable with well-watered plants, while 0.5 and 2.0 mM Si resulted in partial recovery (Fig. 2d). The initial measurement depicted no significant differences in leaf area among treatments, including well-watered and drought-stressed plants (Fig. 2i). This lack of variation reflects the short duration of water withdrawal (2 days), during which sufficient soil moisture was still available and visible leaf wilting had not yet developed. With the progression of drought stress, leaf started wilting and the leaf area was markedly reduced in drought-stressed plants, whereas well-watered plants maintained stable leaf expansion. Under drought conditions, application of 1.0 mM Si effectively mitigated leaf area reduction, while 0.5 and 2.0 mM Si had limited effect (Fig. 2j). Whereas, under prolonged drought conditions, leaf area remained consistently higher in well-watered plants, including those receiving Si, compared with drought-stressed plants. Drought-stressed plants sprayed with Si maintained higher leaf area than drought-stressed plants without Si, with increase of approximately 10%, 21%, and 19% for 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mM Si, respectively (Fig. 2k).

Si application under well-watered conditions did not significantly affect gs. Under drought stress, 1.0 mM Si maintained numerically higher gs. ETR did not vary significantly among treatments at the 25 DAS, indicating no early effect of drought or Si application on photosynthetic electron transport (Fig. 4m). At the 32 DAS measurement, drought stress significantly reduced ETR compared to well-watered control. At 32 DAS, drought stress markedly altered the OJIP curve, with the strongest reduction observed at the P step (Fm) compared to well-watered plants, reflecting impaired maximum PSII fluorescence. Discussion

This study aims to evaluate the potential of foliar-Silicon application to mitigate drought stress in buckwheat, in the process deciphering key growth parameters, photosynthetic performance, and chlorophyll fluorescence dynamics. Drought stress, as expected, led to significant reductions in plant growth, photosynthetic efficiency, and membrane integrity (Fig. 10). The differential responses observed across Si concentrations suggest a dose-dependent effect. Our findings indicate that the lower concentration (0.5 mM) may have been insufficient to effectively trigger protective physiological and biochemical responses45,46, whereas 1.0 mM appeared optimal for stress mitigation. In contrast, the reduced effectiveness observed at 2.0 mM indicates a potential inhibitory effect at higher concentrations. Stem diameter is highly sensitive to plant water status, and its reduction under drought reflects impaired radial growth caused by loss of turgor and restricted cell wall extensibility47. Application of 1.0 mM Si effectively mitigated this decline and supported full recovery after re-irrigation, indicating improved structural stability under stress (Fig. 2b–c). This response is consistent with the role of Si in reinforcing vascular tissues and cell walls through silica deposition, thereby maintaining membrane integrity and facilitating water and nutrient transport during dehydration48. Similar increases in stem diameter and drought tolerance following Si application have been reported in coffee, where enhanced structural support reduced tissue collapse under water deficit49. In contrast, following re-irrigation, stem diameter showed limited recovery in drought-stressed plants, indicating that radial growth is constrained after water deficit due to sustained effects on cambial activity and cell expansion50. However, 1.0 mM Si effectively restored stem diameter to well-watered levels (Fig. 2d), suggesting that optimal Si supply enhances structural recovery by supporting tissue integrity and water transport, whereas lower or higher doses were less effective51.

Plant height was similarly reduced under drought, reflecting suppressed elongation growth commonly associated with reduced cell division and expansion50. Si application partially alleviated this reduction during the stress period, likely through improved water status and sustaining carbon assimilation. However, the limited effect of Si on post-stress height recovery suggests that vertical growth is more sensitive to cumulative drought duration and less reversible than radial growth, indicating that Si primarily supports growth maintenance during active stress rather than recovery52. Following re-irrigation, plant height increased in all drought-stressed plants but remained significantly lower than in well-watered plants, indicating incomplete recovery (Fig. 2h). The similar responses across Si treatments suggest that height recovery was primarily driven by water availability, whereas Si mainly mitigated growth reduction during active drought rather than promoting post-stress elongation50.

Leaf area was highly sensitive to drought, consistent with dehydration-induced growth inhibition and accelerated senescence53. Si application, particularly at 1.0 mM, substantially preserved leaf area under drought, indicating delayed senescence and improved leaf hydration, as evidenced by reduced wilting compared with drought-stressed plants without Si (Supplementary Fig. S3-S5). This effect is likely mediated through Si-enhanced ABA-regulated stomatal regulation, which limits excessive transpiration and reduces leaf water loss, thereby maintaining leaf structural integrity under stress. The concentration-dependent response further indicates that optimal Si levels enhance drought tolerance without adversely affecting growth under well-watered conditions54.

Electrolyte leakage and relative water content measured at peak drought stress provided clear evidence of drought-induced cellular damage and impaired plant water status. The marked increase in EL% under drought reflects loss of membrane integrity, a common consequence of dehydration-induced oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation31, while the concurrent decline in RWC indicates reduced tissue hydration and turgor, together constraining physiological function under water deficit. Foliar Si application, particularly at 1.0 mM, effectively mitigated these detrimental effects by reducing EL% and maintaining higher RWC, thereby preserving cellular integrity and water balance. This protective response is consistent with previous reports, suggesting that Si enhances membrane stability, reinforces cell wall structure, stabilizes membrane lipids, and mitigates oxidative damage, collectively improving cellular water retention under stress32,52,]55. Si facilitates photoprotection and membrane stabilization in buckwheat under drought by modulating the antioxidant defense system to scavenge ROS, thereby mitigating lipid peroxidation and preserving cellular integrity56. The partial protection observed at 2.0 mM and the weak response at 0.5 mM indicate a concentration-dependent effect, highlighting the importance of optimal Si levels for effective drought mitigation. Mechanistically, Si also enhances ABA-mediated stomatal regulation, promoting timely stomatal closure and reducing transpiration, which further contributes to the maintenance of tissue hydration and RWC14,54,57. The close association between reduced EL% and improved RWC emphasise the critical role of Si in preserving cellular integrity, water status, and physiological function, thereby enhancing overall drought tolerance in plants (Fig. 3).

In the initial drought, gas exchange measurements showed no significant differences in CO2 assimilation, gs, Ci, or ETR, indicating that early drought did not severely affect stomatal function (Fig. 3a-d). Drought stress substantially impaired photosynthetic performance in buckwheat, as evidenced by declines in CO2 assimilation, gs, and ETR. As drought progressed into the second and third weeks, gs declined sharply in drought-stressed plants, reflecting stomatal closure to reduce water loss. This response aligns with reports in wheat, soybean, and maize, where increasing drought severity consistently reduces gs through stress-induced stomatal regulation52,]52,58,59. Si application mitigated this decline in a concentration-dependent manner. During the second measurement, 1.0 mM Si maintained gs nearly 200% higher than drought-stressed plants, whereas 0.5 and 2.0 mM Si showed only modest increases (~8% and ~59% higher than drought, respectively). By the third measurement, gs further declined across all treatments; however, 1.0 mM Si consistently preserved substantially higher gs, remaining ~248% above drought, while 0.5 and 2.0 mM Si exhibited comparatively smaller improvements (~62% and ~25% above drought, respectively). Although gs in Si-treated plants remained below well-watered levels, these results indicate that Si partially sustains stomatal function under progressive drought, with 1.0 mM Si showing the strongest and most consistent protective effect (Supplementary Fig. S1). This response is likely mediated through ABA-regulated stomatal control and improved leaf hydration, contributing to enhanced water-use efficiency and photosynthetic potential55.

In the second measurement, CO2 assimilation declined markedly in drought-stressed plants and those treated with 0.5 mM Si, both exhibiting similarly low rates, while well-watered plants maintained higher assimilation. In contrast, plants treated with 1.0 mM Si showed a pronounced enhancement in A, coinciding with the substantially higher gs observed. This trend persisted into the third measurement, where 1.0 mM Si maintained gs higher than drought, supporting continued CO2 diffusion into the leaf and sustained carbon fixation despite progressive water limitation. The enhanced stomatal opening under 1.0 mM Si treatment allowed partial CO2 uptake under drought conditions, supporting photosynthetic activity14,55,60.

Mechanistically, this effect may be associated with Si-induced improvements in stomatal regulation and is consistent with possible involvement of ABA-mediated pathways reported in previous studies, leading to optimal stomatal aperture under moderate drought stress61. Additionally, Si may improve leaf water retention and membrane stability, thereby preventing dehydration-induced damage to the photosynthetic apparatus and preserving photosynthetic efficiency60,62. In contrast, the relatively lower gs observed in 0.5 and 2.0 mM Si treatments, despite slight improvements over drought alone, likely limited internal CO2 availability and constrained assimilation rates. Overall, these findings underscore a clear concentration-dependent role of Si, with 1.0 mM Si being the most effective concentration for sustaining stomatal conductance and photosynthetic carbon fixation under progressive drought stress63.

In the first measurement, internal CO2 showed no significant differences, indicating minimal stress. By the second measurement, Ci decreased in all drought-stressed plants, reflecting restricted CO2 diffusion due to stomatal closure. In the third measurement, Ci increased in drought plants while significantly lower values were observed in 1.0 mM Si treatments under drought, reflecting the inverse relationship between assimilation and Ci. Under severe drought, photosynthetic capacity, rather than stomatal opening, becomes limiting, causing CO2 accumulation in the intercellular spaces. In contrast, 1.0 mM Si maintained lower Ci by partially sustaining assimilation through improved stomatal function and leaf hydration, enabling more efficient CO2 fixation despite stress14,55.

Drought reduced electron transport by restricting electron flow, increasing excitation pressure, and lowering PSII efficiency (Fv/Fm, PIabs), reflecting impaired photochemistry and potential photoinhibition64. Si-treated plants, particularly at 1.0 mM, maintained significantly higher ETR in the second measurement, indicating more efficient use of light energy for photochemistry. By the third measurement, ETR in Si-treated plants remained higher than drought-stressed plants, but differences were not statistically significant, suggesting that prolonged stress limits the protective effect of Si. Overall, Si likely stabilizes chloroplast structure, preserves PSII function, and reduces oxidative stress, supporting sustained photosynthetic performance under drought37,65.

Following re-irrigation, A, gs, Ci, and ETR recovered across all treatments, reaching levels comparable to well-watered plants. This notable recovery indicates that photosynthetic and stomatal functions are highly reversible once water availability is reinstated. The recovery reflects effective rehydration, restoration of stomatal conductance, and repair of the photosynthetic apparatus, even in previously drought-stressed plants, highlighting the resilience of plant physiological processes under transient stress66,67.

Insights from OJIP fluorescence, including Fv/Fm, PIabs, ΦEo, and ΦDo, provide a mechanistic understanding of PSII energy flow. Drought markedly reduced the P-step (Fm), Fv/Fm, PIabs, and ΦEo, indicating impaired excitation, restricted electron transport, and diminished energy conservation60. These declines were accompanied by increased qN, F0, and ΦDo, reflecting enhanced photoprotective heat dissipation and damage to PSII reaction centers. Double-normalized OJIP transients (Vt) further confirmed these acceptor-side limitations. Elevated Vt values during the I-P phase in drought-stressed plants indicate an over-reduced intersystem electron transport chain and limited PSI electron sink capacity. Conversely, Si-treated plants (1.0 mM) showed a reduced I-P rise and improved difference kinetics (ΔWOJ​, ΔWJI​, ΔWIP​). By facilitating efficient QA−​ reoxidation and coordinating electron flow from PSII to PSI, optimal Si supplementation (1.0 mM) preserves photosynthetic structural integrity and minimizes energy loss, whereas the partial recovery observed at 0.5 and 2.0 mM highlights the concentration-dependent nature of Si in enhancing drought tolerance14. Si stabilizes photosynthetic electron transport under water deficit by protecting the D1 protein repair cycle in Photosystem II and the PsaA/PsaB core subunits in Photosystem I, while maintaining the structural integrity of the Oxygen-Evolving Complex (OEC) and the fluidity of the thylakoid membrane60,68,69.

However, despite re-irrigation, the photosynthetic apparatus of drought-stressed plants exhibited incomplete recovery. Normalized OJIP transients (Ft/F0) revealed a persistently lower P-step (Fm) in untreated drought-stressed plants and those receiving 0.5 mM Si, indicating sustained impairment of PSII reaction centers. This suggests that although re-watering restores gas exchange and growth processes, structural or photochemical damage to PSII-likely induced by drought-associated oxidative stress-may be only partially reversible or require extended recovery periods. In contrast, plants supplied with 1.0 mM Si maintained higher Fm values, indicating improved preservation of PSII integrity during drought and enabling a more evident recovery of photochemical capacity upon re-irrigation14,60.

Drought stress markedly reduced fresh and dry biomass and seed yield, consistent with the high sensitivity of vegetative growth and reproductive processes to water deficit70. Restricted cell expansion, suppressed metabolic activity, and drought-induced stomatal closure limited CO2 influx, reducing gs, A, and overall carbon assimilation, thereby constraining biomass production and yield formation32. Similar drought-driven yield losses have been widely attributed to impaired pollination, reduced grain filling, and disrupted assimilate partitioning, leading to decreases in both seed number and individual seed weight23.

Foliar Si application mitigated these drought-induced effects, with 1.0 mM Si providing the improvement in shoot biomass and yield components. Under prolonged drought, Si-treated plants particularly at 1.0 mM maintained a greater number of leaves and flower pods, as visually illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S3-S5, which translated into higher shoot biomass, increased seed number, and enhanced yield11. These responses indicate that Si enhances productivity under drought primarily by preserving physiological function rather than directly stimulating growth. Comparable yield recovery under drought has been reported in wheat and maize, where Si improved shoot carbon status, water-use efficiency, gas exchange, and antioxidant protection, resulting in higher seed weight and number23,54.

The partial restoration of fresh and dry biomass at 1.0 mM Si further suggests improved water-use efficiency and sustained photosynthetically derived carbon supply during drought. This aligns with evidence that Si stabilizes photosynthetic membranes, preserves electron transport, and limits oxidative damage, thereby supporting continued biomass accumulation under water deficit. In contrast, the weaker responses at 0.5 and 2.0 mM Si highlight the importance of an optimal Si concentration for effective drought mitigation62.

Reproductive performance was particularly sensitive to drought, as reflected by reductions in seed number and seed dry weight, likely due to restricted carbohydrate availability during flowering and seed filling. Application of 1.0 mM Si restored total seed yield and individual seed weight to levels comparable with well-watered plants, indicating that Si-mediated protection extends beyond vegetative tissues to reproductive sinks by sustaining carbon assimilation and assimilate partitioning. Although this study does not address the balance between vegetative growth and reproductive allocation, the stress period encompassed early reproductive development, and previous reports in maize and rice suggest that Si application may confer greater ameliorative effects during reproductive phases than during vegetative growth.71,72. Similar Si-driven improvements in reproductive traits have been linked to enhanced water status, reduced lipid peroxidation, and coordinated growth responses in stress sensitive genotypes, including buckwheat14.

Differences among Si concentrations further highlight trade-offs between seed number and seed size. At 0.5 mM Si, the tendency toward increased seed number coupled with reduced individual seed weight suggests incomplete compensation of source–sink balance, whereas 1.0 mM Si optimized both assimilate supply and allocation, supporting balanced seed development73. The intermediate response at 2.0 mM may reflect diminishing returns or altered nutrient interactions at higher Si levels. The study on impact of Si in rainfed buckwheat system is limited, however Si application, including nano-Si, generally boosts plant growth and resilience by positively restructuring rhizospheric microbial communities and improving soil health74. Correlation analysis revealed a strong linkage between plant water status, photosynthetic efficiency, and yield, with Si treatments reinforcing these relationships under drought. Positive associations of A, gs, and ETR with growth and yield highlight the central role of coordinated carbon assimilation and photochemistry. In contrast, electrolyte leakage showed strong negative relationships, indicating membrane damage as a key constraint, while the negative correlation of intercellular Ci suggests reduced carbon use efficiency. The negative correlation of ΦD₀ suggests increased energy dissipation as heat under stress conditions, reflecting reduced photochemical efficiency and enhanced photoprotective responses. Collectively, these findings indicate that Si-mediated maintenance of water balance and photochemical integrity is critical for sustaining productivity under drought14.

Collectively, these findings, together with previous reports showing that Si enhances physiological protection, carbon sequestration, and stress resilience, support the hypothesis that optimal Si supplementation improves yield under drought by integrating physiological, and biochemical adjustments rather than direct growth stimulation62,75.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that foliar silicon application enhances drought tolerance in buckwheat through coordinated physiological and photochemical regulation rather than direct growth stimulation. Drought-induced reductions in growth, photosynthesis, biomass accumulation, and seed yield were consistently mitigated by Si, with 1.0 mM emerging as the most effective concentration across both vegetative and reproductive stages.

Mechanistically, Si-mediated drought resilience was associated with improved plant water relations and cellular stability, as reflected by the preservation of stem diameter, plant height, and leaf area under water deficit. At the photosynthetic level, Si stabilized PSII function, improved QA-QB electron transfer, moderated plastoquinone pool redox status, and partially alleviated acceptor-side limitations at PSI. Together, these effects enhanced electron transport efficiency, optimized light energy utilization, and reduced the risk of photoinhibition during drought stress.

The maintenance of photosynthetic performance and CO2 assimilation in Si-treated plants supported greater biomass accumulation and more efficient carbon allocation to reproductive sinks, resulting in higher seed number, seed weight, and overall yield under drought, particularly at 1.0 mM Si. The concentration-dependent responses observed across traits emphasize the importance of optimizing Si supply, as both lower and higher concentrations provided only partial protection. Following re-irrigation, clear recovery of gas exchange (A, gs, Ci), ETR, plant height, leaf area, and biomass was observed, especially in plants treated with 1.0 mM Si. In contrast, stem diameter and PSII maximum fluorescence (Fm) remained partially reduced in untreated drought and low-Si plants, indicating slower recovery of structural and photochemical components.

Overall, these findings reinforce the emerging view of silicon as an active physiological modulator that integrates physiological protection with dynamic regulation of photosynthetic electron transport, water-use efficiency, and yield formation under drought. Foliar Si application at an optimal concentration therefore represents a promising strategy to enhance drought resilience and productivity in buckwheat and potentially other drought-sensitive crops.
