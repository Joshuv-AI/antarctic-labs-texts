Study on causes of death and influencing factors in hemodialysis patients with End-Stage renal disease
Authors: Jing Huang, Yanchun Li, Shen Shen, Ling Yu, Qianmei Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMortality remains high among patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) on maintenance hemodialysis despite advances in dialysis technology. This study aimed to investigate the causes of death and identify factors associated with survival in a Chinese hemodialysis population.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective cohort of deceased maintenance hemodialysis patients. Eligible patients had received thrice-weekly hemodialysis for ≥ 3 months prior to death; peritoneal dialysis, transplant during follow-up, and incomplete records were excluded. Subgroup analyses compared survival by age (< 60, 60–70, 70–80, > 80 years), dialysis duration (< 1, 1–5, 5–10, > 10 years), and sex using Kaplan-Meier curves with log-rank tests. Cox models assessed factors associated with all-cause mortality; logistic regression evaluated correlates of cardiovascular death.ResultsA total of 194 patients were included. The mean age was 71.35 ± 13.05 years, with males accounting for 60.8%. Leading causes of death were cardiovascular (29.9%), cerebrovascular (19.6%), and infection (16.5%). Female sex and longer dialysis vintage showed better survival (log-rank p = 0.048 and p < 0.0001, respectively). The 60–70 years age group demonstrated the best survival profile (p = 0.023). Among patients with documented comorbidity data, hypertension was present in 73/73 (100%) and diabetes mellitus in 82/82 (100%). Higher LDL-C and pre-dialysis hyperkalemia were associated with cardiovascular death (OR 1.37, 95% CI 1.10–1.72, p = 0.005 and OR 2.19, 95% CI 1.08–4.45, p = 0.031, respectively), while lower albumin and higher CRP were associated with infection-related death (p = 0.037 and p = 0.024, respectively).ConclusionsCardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases remain the leading causes of death in hemodialysis patients. Sex, age, serum albumin, LDL-C, and inflammatory markers were associated with survival outcomes. Understanding these associations may help develop targeted interventions to improve survival in hemodialysis patients.

Introduction

End-stage renal disease (ESRD) represents a global health challenge with increasing prevalence worldwide [1]. Despite technological advancements in dialysis procedures and improvements in patient care, mortality rates among maintenance hemodialysis patients remain substantially higher than in the general population, with a 5-year survival rate typically below 50% in most countries [2].

Hemodialysis patients experience a complex pathophysiological state characterized by uremic toxin accumulation, chronic inflammation, mineral and bone disorders, and accelerated atherosclerosis [3]. These factors, combined with frequent comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus and hypertension, create a high-risk profile for premature death [4]. Previous studies have consistently identified cardiovascular disease as the leading cause of mortality in this population, accounting for approximately 40–50% of all deaths [5, 6]. Other significant causes include infection, cerebrovascular events, malignancy, and withdrawal from dialysis [7].

The high mortality rate observed in hemodialysis patients has prompted extensive research into potential risk factors. Traditional cardiovascular risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia contribute significantly to the mortality burden [8]. However, dialysis patients also face unique risk factors including anemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism, vascular calcification, malnutrition, and chronic inflammation [9]. The interaction between these traditional and non-traditional risk factors creates a complex mortality risk profile that remains incompletely understood [10].

In China, the rapid economic development and aging population have led to a significant increase in ESRD prevalence over recent decades. The Chinese dialysis population may differ from Western populations in terms of underlying etiologies of kidney disease, comorbidity patterns, and treatment approaches. The prevalence of stroke in both the general and dialysis populations in China is significantly higher than in Western countries, while the prevalence of coronary heart disease is relatively lower [11, 12]. These differences may influence mortality patterns and risk factor profiles, highlighting the need for China-specific research.

The present study aims to comprehensively analyze the causes of death and identify factors influencing survival in a cohort of Chinese maintenance hemodialysis patients. By examining the relationships between demographic characteristics, clinical parameters, laboratory data, and mortality outcomes, we seek to enhance our understanding of the determinants of survival in this high-risk population.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a single-center retrospective cohort of deceased maintenance hemodialysis patients. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of our institution, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the analysis of deceased patients only, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

Eligible patients had received thrice-weekly hemodialysis for ≥ 3 months prior to death; peritoneal dialysis, transplant during follow-up, and incomplete records were excluded. Patients were dialyzed using bicarbonate dialysate and synthetic dialyzer membranes. All patients received standard-of-care per the Chinese Guideline for Hemodialysis [13], including anemia management, mineral bone disorder treatment, and cardiovascular risk reduction.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data were extracted from the electronic medical records system of our institution. The collected information included age, gender, primary kidney disease, comorbidities (diabetes mellitus and hypertension), dialysis duration, and laboratory parameters. Laboratory data included: (1) Nutrition and liver function: serum albumin, ALT, AST, ALP, total bilirubin; (2) Hematology: hemoglobin, white blood cell count, absolute neutrophil count, platelets; (3) Electrolytes and acid-base: pre-dialysis potassium, sodium, carbon dioxide combining power (CO₂CP); (4) Urea kinetics: pre- and post-dialysis urea, Kt/V, urea reduction ratio (URR); (5) Mineral bone metabolism: calcium, phosphorus, calcium-phosphorus product (CaP), PTH; (6) Lipid profile: total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein (LDL-C); (7) Iron metabolism: serum iron (SI), total iron binding capacity (TIBC), transferrin saturation (TSAT), ferritin; (8) Inflammation: C-reactive protein (CRP). All parameters were measured within three months before death. The latest available laboratory values before death were used for analysis.

Primary kidney diseases were classified according to the International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision (ICD-10) coding system and categorized into the following groups: glomerulonephritis, diabetic nephropathy, hypertensive nephropathy, polycystic kidney disease, tubulointerstitial nephritis, and others (including unknown etiology).

Dialysis duration was calculated from the date of hemodialysis initiation to the date of death and categorized into four groups: <1 year, 1–5 years, 5–10 years, and > 10 years. Age was similarly categorized into four groups: <60 years, 60–70 years, 70–80 years, and > 80 years. Age and dialysis-vintage cutoffs were prespecified based on common clinical categories and the sample distribution to explore potential non-linear associations; alternative cut-points were not assessed and warrant future sensitivity analyses.

The causes of death were determined based on our hospital quality control center database and electronic medical records from outpatient and inpatient departments. Two nephrologists independently reviewed the records to assign the primary cause of death, and disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third nephrologist. The causes of death were categorized into six main groups: cardiovascular disease (including myocardial infarction, heart failure, arrhythmia, cardiac arrest, and sudden cardiac death), cerebrovascular disease (including ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke), infection (including pneumonia, sepsis, and other infectious complications), tumor (malignancy-related deaths), others (including gastrointestinal bleeding, trauma, and accidents), and unspecified (when the exact cause could not be determined with certainty).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the baseline characteristics of the study population. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR) depending on the normality of distribution. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

For comparative analyses between groups (e.g., different causes of death), continuous variables were compared using independent t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Continuous variables such as LDL-C, potassium, WBC, CRP, ALT, and CO₂CP were analyzed using quartile trend tests. Missing values < 5% were handled using multiple imputation methods.

Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method, with survival time calculated from the initiation of hemodialysis to the date of death. Comparisons between survival curves were performed using log-rank tests. Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify factors associated with mortality. Variables with p < 0.1 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals.

Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with specific causes of death, particularly cardiovascular mortality. Odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.0.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and SPSS software (version 25.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Baseline characteristics of the study population (N = 194)

The mean dialysis duration was 7.33 ± 5.81 years, with 32.1% of patients having received hemodialysis for more than 10 years, 30.1% for 5–10 years, 34.2% for 1–5 years, and 3.6% for less than 1 year.

The most common primary kidney diseases were diabetic nephropathy (28.5%), glomerulonephritis (21.0%), and hypertensive nephropathy (14.5%). Other causes included polycystic kidney disease (3.2%), tubulointerstitial nephritis (1.6%), and unknown etiology (10.2%). Among patients with documented comorbidity data, hypertension was present in 73/73 (100%) and diabetes mellitus in 82/82 (100%). As absence of documentation does not exclude the condition, cohort-wide prevalence is not inferred; potential information bias is acknowledged in the Limitations.

The mean serum albumin level was 35.81 ± 5.28 g/L. Mean hemoglobin level was 99.7 ± 15.4 g/L. Mean calcium level was 2.24 ± 0.28 mmol/L, and mean phosphorus level was 1.85 ± 0.63 mmol/L, resulting in a mean calcium-phosphorus product of 4.14 ± 1.48 mmol²/L². Median parathyroid hormone level was 334.5 pg/mL (IQR: 178.2–542.8.2.8 pg/mL). The mean Kt/V was 1.38 ± 0.32, and the mean urea reduction ratio was 67.4 ± 8.2%. Mean ALT was 18.5 ± 12.3 U/L, and mean AST was 21.7 ± 15.6 U/L. Mean WBC was 7.2 ± 2.8 × 10⁹/L, and mean neutrophil count was 5.1 ± 2.4 × 10⁹/L. Mean platelet count was 187.5 ± 78.4 × 10⁹/L. Mean sodium was 138.2 ± 3.5 mmol/L, mean potassium was 4.8 ± 0.9 mmol/L, and mean CO₂CP was 22.7 ± 3.6 mmol/L. Mean TC was 4.2 ± 1.0 mmol/L, mean TG was 1.8 ± 1.2 mmol/L, mean HDL-C was 1.1 ± 0.3 mmol/L, and mean LDL-C was 3.0 ± 0.9 mmol/L. Mean SI was 12.3 ± 5.7 µmol/L, mean TIBC was 47.2 ± 12.8 µmol/L, and mean TSAT was 26.3 ± 13.2%.

Comorbidity counts reflect patients with available records (hypertension n = 73; diabetes n = 82); missing entries indicate unavailable documentation.

Characteristics of patients by cause of death

Patients who died from cardiovascular disease were slightly younger (69.24 ± 14.37 years) compared to those who died from other causes (72.24 ± 12.40 years), although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.143). The gender distribution showed a higher proportion of males in the cardiovascular death group (65.5%) compared to the overall cohort (60.8%). Interestingly, serum albumin levels were significantly higher in patients who died from cardiovascular causes (37.65 ± 4.88 g/L) compared to those who died from other causes (35.06 ± 5.28 g/L, p = 0.023). LDL-C levels were higher in the cardiovascular death group (3.46 ± 0.78 mmol/L) compared to other causes (2.88 ± 0.82 mmol/L, p = 0.012). The incidence of pre-dialysis hyperkalemia (> 5.5 mmol/L) was higher in the cardiovascular death group compared to other causes (31% vs. 17%, p = 0.028). No significant differences were observed in hemoglobin levels (101.2 ± 14.8 vs. 99.1 ± 15.6 g/L, p = 0.372), calcium-phosphorus product (4.22 ± 1.53 vs. 4.10 ± 1.46 mmol²/L², p = 0.605), PTH levels (median 341.2 vs. 332.6 pg/mL, p = 0.782), or Kt/V (1.40 ± 0.33 vs. 1.37 ± 0.31, p = 0.547) between the cardiovascular death group and other causes.

Patients who died from cerebrovascular disease had a mean age of 69.87 ± 11.82 years, which was not significantly different from other causes (p = 0.438). This group had a higher proportion of females (55.3%) compared to the overall cohort (39.2%). Their mean dialysis duration was 8.53 ± 7.47 years, which was longer than the overall mean, though not statistically significant (p = 0.158). Serum albumin levels were higher (37.43 ± 5.34 g/L) compared to those who died from other causes, but the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.169). Hemoglobin levels (97.5 ± 16.2 vs. 100.2 ± 15.2 g/L, p = 0.321), calcium-phosphorus product (4.05 ± 1.41 vs. 4.16 ± 1.50 mmol²/L², p = 0.668), and dialysis adequacy parameters (Kt/V: 1.41 ± 0.34 vs. 1.37 ± 0.31, p = 0.488) showed no significant differences between groups.

Patients who died from infection were older (73.97 ± 12.78 years) compared to those who died from other causes (70.83 ± 13.08 years), though the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.214). This group had a higher proportion of males (71.9%) and a shorter mean dialysis duration (5.90 ± 4.26 years) compared to the overall cohort. Notably, patients who died from infection had significantly lower serum albumin levels (33.38 ± 4.78 g/L) compared to those who died from other causes (36.30 ± 5.27 g/L, p = 0.037). C-reactive protein levels were higher in the infection death group (median 18.4 vs. 7.3 mg/L, p = 0.024), as were WBC counts (9.3 ± 3.5 vs. 6.7 ± 2.5 × 10⁹/L, p = 0.003) and neutrophil counts (7.4 ± 3.2 vs. 4.6 ± 2.0 × 10⁹/L, p < 0.001). Other parameters including hemoglobin (97.1 ± 16.8 vs. 100.3 ± 15.1 g/L, p = 0.295), calcium-phosphorus product (3.98 ± 1.38 vs. 4.18 ± 1.50 mmol²/L², p = 0.493), and ferritin levels (median 412.6 vs. 382.1 ng/mL, p = 0.357) showed no significant differences.

Analysis of additional laboratory parameters

We examined the relationship between various laboratory parameters and mortality outcomes. No significant associations were found between hemoglobin levels and specific causes of death. The mean hemoglobin levels were similar across cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and infection-related death groups (101.2 ± 14.8, 97.5 ± 16.2, and 97.1 ± 16.8 g/L, respectively, p = 0.389).

Similarly, mineral bone disorder parameters including calcium, phosphorus, calcium-phosphorus product, and PTH showed no significant association with specific causes of mortality. The calcium-phosphorus product was 4.22 ± 1.53, 4.05 ± 1.41, and 3.98 ± 1.38 mmol²/L² for cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and infection-related deaths, respectively (p = 0.724).

Dialysis adequacy parameters, including Kt/V and urea reduction ratio, were not significantly associated with specific mortality causes. The mean Kt/V values were 1.40 ± 0.33, 1.41 ± 0.34, and 1.35 ± 0.29 for cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and infection-related deaths, respectively (p = 0.653). Similarly, the urea reduction ratios were 67.8 ± 8.5%, 68.2 ± 7.9%, and 66.3 ± 8.7% for the three groups, respectively (p = 0.587).

Iron metabolism parameters including serum iron, ferritin, and transferrin saturation showed no significant associations with specific causes of death, with the exception of slightly higher C-reactive protein levels observed in the infection-related death group.

Survival analysis by gender revealed significant differences in survival outcomes between male and female patients (p = 0.048), as illustrated in Fig. 3. Female patients demonstrated better survival compared to male patients throughout the follow-up period. Figure 4 presents the survival curves stratified by age groups. Significant differences were observed across the four age groups (p = 0.023). Risk factors for mortality

Cox proportional hazards model

Cox regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with all-cause mortality. In the univariate analysis, age alone was not significantly associated with mortality (hazard ratio [HR] 1.004, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.992–1.016, p = 0.488). However, when adjusted for gender, dialysis duration, and serum albumin in a multivariate model limited to patients with complete data (n = 102), none of the variables reached statistical significance, suggesting complex interactions between these factors.

Additional laboratory parameters, including hemoglobin, calcium-phosphorus product, PTH, ferritin, and Kt/V, were also evaluated in the Cox model. None of these parameters showed a significant association with all-cause mortality in either univariate or multivariate analyses. The hazard ratios for these parameters were: hemoglobin (HR 0.993, 95% CI 0.981–1.006, p = 0.312), calcium-phosphorus product (HR 1.048, 95% CI 0.896–1.227, p = 0.558), PTH (HR 1.000, 95% CI 0.999–1.001, p = 0.782), ferritin (HR 1.000, 95% CI 0.999–1.001, p = 0.867), and Kt/V (HR 0.852, 95% CI 0.441–1.647, p = 0.634).

Logistic regression for cardiovascular mortality

Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with cardiovascular mortality. In the univariate analysis, age was not significantly associated with cardiovascular death (odds ratio [OR] 0.982, 95% CI 0.961–1.005, p = 0.144). However, in the multivariate model adjusted for gender, dialysis duration, and serum albumin, higher serum albumin levels were significantly associated with increased odds of cardiovascular death (OR 1.105, 95% CI 1.012–1.206, p = 0.026). LDL-C was independently associated with cardiovascular death (OR 1.37, 95% CI 1.10–1.72, p = 0.005) in the multivariate model. Pre-dialysis hyperkalemia (> 5.5 mmol/L) was also associated with increased risk of cardiovascular death (OR 2.19, 95% CI 1.08–4.45, p = 0.031). These findings suggest that better nutritional status, as indicated by higher albumin levels, elevated LDL-C, and hyperkalemia may be associated with greater risk of cardiovascular mortality in this population.

Additional laboratory parameters were also evaluated for their association with cardiovascular mortality. Hemoglobin levels (OR 1.009, 95% CI 0.988–1.031, p = 0.370), calcium-phosphorus product (OR 1.057, 95% CI 0.864–1.293, p = 0.592), PTH (OR 1.000, 95% CI 0.999–1.001, p = 0.776), and Kt/V (OR 1.369, 95% CI 0.490–3.828, p = 0.549) showed no significant association with cardiovascular mortality in either univariate or multivariate analyses.

Discussion

This study provides comprehensive insights into the causes of death and factors influencing survival in a cohort of Chinese hemodialysis patients. Our findings confirm cardiovascular disease as the leading cause of death, accounting for approximately 30% of all mortality, followed by cerebrovascular disease and infection. We identified several factors associated with survival outcomes, including gender, age, and serum albumin levels.

The distribution of death causes in our study aligns with previous reports from both Western and Asian populations, though with some notable differences. The proportion of cardiovascular deaths (~ 30%) was lower than many Western cohorts (~ 40–50%), whereas cerebrovascular deaths (~ 20%) were relatively higher, consistent with patterns reported in Chinese populations [11, 12]. This pattern may reflect the distinct cardiovascular risk profile of Chinese ESRD patients. The prevalence of stroke in both the general and dialysis populations in China (approximately 2.1%) is significantly higher than in the United States (approximately 1.0%), while the prevalence of coronary heart disease is relatively lower [14, 15]. These differences are associated with high-salt diet, lower LDL-C levels, and differences in body habitus between Chinese and Western populations.

The observed female survival advantage aligns with prior studies in dialysis populations [16, 17]. This female survival advantage may be attributed to multiple factors, including hormonal differences affecting cardiovascular risk, disparities in treatment adherence, differences in dialysis adequacy due to body size variations, and potentially better preservation of residual renal function in female patients [18]. The attenuation of this survival advantage after prolonged dialysis duration suggests that the protective factors may diminish over time, possibly due to the cumulative burden of dialysis-related complications [19].

Our finding regarding the association between age and survival revealed an unexpected pattern, with the 60–70 years age group demonstrating the best survival outcomes. This contrasts with the typical linear relationship between advancing age and mortality risk observed in the general population. The poorer survival in the youngest age group (< 60 years) may reflect a selection bias, where younger patients reaching ESRD might have more aggressive kidney disease, greater genetic predisposition to serious comorbidities, or poorer treatment adherence [20]. Alternatively, this could indicate suboptimal management of younger patients, who might receive less aggressive interventions due to assumptions about their resilience. This finding warrants further investigation and highlights the importance of avoiding age-based assumptions in clinical decision-making [21]. Patients under 60 years in our center had greater interdialytic weight gain (3.9 ± 1.2 kg) and higher incidence of hyperkalemia (29%) compared to those ≥ 60 years, suggesting that insufficient dietary/fluid management and work-social burden may lead to excessive fluid and potassium intake, potentially resulting in heart failure or sudden death.

The relationship between dialysis duration and survival demonstrated a strongly positive association, with patients on dialysis for longer periods showing significantly better outcomes. This likely represents a substantial survivor bias, where patients who survive longer on dialysis represent a selected subpopulation with inherently better health characteristics or greater resilience to the challenges of ESRD and its treatment [22]. This observation reinforces the importance of considering time-dependent variables and selection effects when interpreting survival data in dialysis populations [23].

The paradoxical association between higher serum albumin levels and increased cardiovascular mortality is a particularly intriguing finding of our study. While hypoalbuminemia has consistently been identified as a risk factor for all-cause mortality in dialysis patients, our observation suggests a more complex relationship with cause-specific mortality [24]. Higher albumin levels, indicating better nutritional status, might be associated with different metabolic consequences in long-term dialysis patients, including alterations in lipid metabolism, increased calcification, or changes in inflammatory pathways that could specifically affect cardiovascular risk [25]. Alternatively, this finding might reflect confounding by other unmeasured factors or treatment differences between patients with different nutritional profiles [26].

In our multivariate model, LDL-C was independently associated with cardiovascular death (OR 1.37, 95% CI 1.10–1.72, p = 0.005), consistent with patterns observed in the general population. CRP, WBC, and CO₂CP were significantly associated with infection/acidosis-related death, suggesting that inflammation and acid-base imbalance remain important modifiable risk factors.

Conversely, the significantly lower albumin levels observed in patients who died from infection highlight the well-established relationship between malnutrition and immune dysfunction. Hypoalbuminemia may reflect both poor nutritional status and chronic inflammation, both of which can compromise immune function and increase susceptibility to severe infections [27]. This finding underscores the importance of nutritional interventions and careful monitoring of infection risk in hypoalbuminemic dialysis patients [28].

The lack of significant associations between various laboratory parameters (hemoglobin, calcium-phosphorus product, PTH, Kt/V) and mortality outcomes in our study is noteworthy. While anemia, mineral bone disorders, and dialysis inadequacy have been linked to adverse outcomes in previous studies, our findings suggest that these factors may not be the primary determinants of mortality in our patient population. This could reflect the relatively adequate management of these parameters in our center, with most patients having hemoglobin levels, mineral bone parameters, and dialysis adequacy measures within or close to recommended ranges. Among deceased patients, 63% had Hb ≥ 110 g/L, 58% had PTH ≤ 300 pg/mL, 72% had CaP < 4.4 mmol²/L², and 78% had Kt/V ≥ 1.2, indicating that our center generally managed traditional parameters adequately. The remaining mortality could be attributed to uncontrollable or unmonitored risk factors.

Our study has several strengths, including the comprehensive assessment of causes of death, the extended follow-up period capturing long-term dialysis outcomes, and the detailed analysis of various factors influencing survival. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective design introduces potential information bias, particularly in the determination of causes of death. The single-center nature of the study limits its generalizability to broader dialysis populations. The analysis of only deceased patients introduces a selection bias and precludes direct comparison with survivors. Given the retrospective observational design and inclusion of deceased patients only, our analyses identify associations rather than causation; residual confounding is likely. Additionally, we lacked data on several potentially important variables, including vascular access types and detailed information on medications. Alternative age and dialysis-vintage cut-points were not assessed and warrant future sensitivity analyses.

These limitations notwithstanding, our findings have important clinical implications. The high burden of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular mortality highlights the need for aggressive cardiovascular risk management in hemodialysis patients, potentially tailored to the specific risk profile of Chinese patients. The observed gender disparity in survival suggests that gender-specific approaches to dialysis care might be warranted. The complex relationship between age and survival outcomes calls for individualized assessment rather than age-based clinical decision-making. Finally, the opposing associations of serum albumin with cardiovascular and infectious mortality underscore the multifaceted role of nutritional status in determining outcomes in this population.

Future research should focus on prospective studies with comprehensive data collection to better elucidate the complex interplay between demographic, clinical, and laboratory factors in determining mortality risk. Studies incorporating detailed information on dialysis prescription, medication use, and biomarkers of inflammation and oxidative stress could provide deeper insights into the mechanisms underlying the observed associations. Intervention studies targeting modifiable risk factors, particularly those addressing the high cardiovascular mortality burden, are urgently needed to improve outcomes in this high-risk population.

Taken together, these findings highlight cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease—along with infection—as the dominant causes of death, and underscore the clinical relevance of sex, age, serum albumin, LDL-C, and inflammatory markers when tailoring management strategies for hemodialysis patients.

Conclusion

In summary, cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events remain the leading causes of mortality in this cohort, and sex, age, serum albumin level, LDL-C, and systemic inflammation are associated with survival outcomes and may warrant targeted risk management. This comprehensive analysis of causes of death and survival determinants in Chinese hemodialysis patients confirms the predominance of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular mortality and identifies several factors associated with survival outcomes. The complex patterns observed, including the gender advantage for females, the non-linear relationship between age and survival, and the paradoxical associations of serum albumin with different causes of death, highlight the multifactorial nature of mortality risk in this population. While several laboratory parameters including hemoglobin, calcium-phosphorus product, PTH, and dialysis adequacy metrics were comprehensively assessed, they did not demonstrate significant associations with mortality outcomes in our cohort. These findings underscore the importance of individualized risk assessment and targeted interventions addressing modifiable risk factors to improve outcomes in patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis.
