Evaluation of current practices and communication surrounding sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP): A survey analysis of healthcare practitioners
Authors: Katherine C Nickels, Carly Katterman, Kelly Gwin, Carla Schad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo characterize healthcare providers' awareness and education regarding SUDEP and their approaches to SUDEP counseling.MethodsA voluntary, observational 14‐question survey was administered at the 2024 American Epilepsy Society Annual Meeting to healthcare providers who treat patients with epilepsy. The survey was designed to assess SUDEP awareness, clinical training, counseling patterns, and application of SUDEP guidelines.ResultsA total of 114 responses were analyzed. Most respondents were epileptologists or neurologists and completed residency or fellowship between 2010 and 2024. A substantial portion of respondents (41%) reported that SUDEP training was not provided during their residency or fellowship. Nearly all respondents were aware of SUDEP risk in epilepsy patients, and 61% reported losing a patient to SUDEP. Despite endorsement by two professional medical associations, 26% of respondents were unaware of the 2017 AAN/AES SUDEP Practice Guidelines. Although almost all respondents reported counseling patients and families about SUDEP risk, only 52% counseled all patients in accordance with the 2017 guidelines, typically at or after the second visit, if at all. Respondents most frequently selected uncontrolled seizures as the top risk factor for SUDEP, followed by >3 generalized tonic–clonic seizures (GTCS), reflecting a discrepancy with guideline recommendations identifying GTCS as the primary risk factor. However, respondents prioritized ‘targeting GTCS’ similarly to ‘seizure freedom’ as a mitigation strategy. Most respondents rated SUDEP counseling practices negatively (needs improvement or unsatisfactory), were divided on preferred education/training delivery, and reported not using available counseling resources (PAME, EF, CURE, etc.).SignificanceThe survey responses demonstrate modifiable gaps between SUDEP counseling recommendations and clinical practice. Improving residency/fellowship training, awareness of the 2017 AAN/AES SUDEP Practice Guidelines, and use of available counseling resources may help align care with established recommendations.Plain Language SummarySUDEP, or sudden unexpected death in epilepsy, is when a person with epilepsy dies suddenly and without another clear cause. This survey of epilepsy care providers asked about SUDEP awareness and how providers discuss SUDEP risk with patients and caregivers. Formal SUDEP education is inconsistent during training, and 26% were unaware of guidelines that help providers discuss SUDEP risk. Counseling is recommended for all patients and caregivers, but just over half of providers do so. Many also did not realize that generalized tonic–clonic seizures are the main risk factor. Increased SUDEP education and improved guideline awareness may improve SUDEP counseling.Keywords: counseling, mortality, sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP), survey

INTRODUCTION

Premature all‐cause mortality risk is elevated in patients with epilepsy compared to the general population, 1 with median standardized mortality ratios (SMRs) ranging between 1.3 and 8.7 in adults 2 , 3 and between 3.1 and 22.4 in the pediatric population (<18 years of age). 2 , 3 In adults, elevated mortality risk is attributed to poor seizure control. Achieving freedom from seizures by using antiseizure medications (ASMs) can reduce this risk in adult patients. 2 , 4 , 5 In patients <18 years of age, elevated mortality risk is primarily driven by underlying conditions rather than seizures. 6 However, epilepsy‐related deaths are not absent from the pediatric population. 7 , 8 , 9

The most common epilepsy‐related causes of death are related to uncontrolled seizures and include sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP), status epilepticus (SE), and seizure‐related accidents and drownings. 2 , 4 , 10 Among these, SUDEP is the leading cause of seizure‐related death 10 , 11 and the second leading neurological cause of potential years of life lost. 12 The incidence of SUDEP varies across studies due to differences in study populations, definitions of SUDEP, and methods of determining and reporting causes of death. 13 Population‐based studies estimate that the annual incidence rate of SUDEP in adults ranges from 1.16 to 1.24 cases per 1000 person‐years, 5 , 8 , 12 with similar rates in the pediatric population. 8 , 9

The leading risk factor for SUDEP is the presence and frequency of uncontrolled generalized tonic–clonic seizures (GTCS), although other risk factors, such as lack of seizure freedom and nocturnal seizures, also contribute to risk. 5 , 14 , 15 Improvement in seizure control is recommended by the 2017 American Academy of Neurology and American Epilepsy Society (AAN/AES) practice guidelines to reduce the risk of SUDEP. The AAN/AES guidelines advise healthcare providers (HCPs) to inform their patients that seizure freedom, specifically from GTCS, has a strong association with decreased SUDEP risk. 5 Note that for consistency with the 2017 guidelines, “GTCS” is used here to refer to bilateral convulsive seizures regardless of whether the onset was generalized or focal.

In addition to GTCS management, HCPs are also advised to provide all patients and caregivers with information about SUDEP risk and how regular ASM adherence substantially reduces SUDEP risk. 4 , 5 , 16 Free online resources from organizations such as CURE Epilepsy (CURE), the Epilepsy Foundation (EF), and Partners Against Mortality in Epilepsy (PAME) are available for HCPs and patients to aid in these conversations. 17 , 18 , 19 Despite guidelines and resources available to HCPs regarding SUDEP education for patients, patients and caregivers report receiving inadequate information and counseling about SUDEP. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 Counseling in this setting is considered providing patients and caregivers with timely and accurate information to increase knowledge and facilitate informed decision‐making.

Understanding HCPs' backgrounds and current perspectives regarding SUDEP counseling and risk management is important for patient management and reducing the risk of SUDEP. We surveyed HCPs who treat patients with epilepsy to identify gaps in SUDEP awareness, clinical training/education, counseling patterns, and application of SUDEP guidelines. The results of this survey provide unique insight into SUDEP‐specific training in residency and fellowship over time as well as overall awareness of SUDEP guidelines and translation of that awareness into clinical practice.

METHODS

Survey design and administration

A voluntary, observational 14‐question survey with two adjunctive prompts, based on prior responses, was designed by one of the authors (CS) and evaluated by PAME for clarity and readability. The survey was conducted in‐person by a medical communications agency (MCI; Atlanta, GA) in December 2024 at an epilepsy‐specific meeting, namely, the 78th Annual Meeting of the American Epilepsy Society in Los Angeles, CA. The location of the survey was selected to maximize efficiency and to minimize burden on the respondents. The survey was created with simple, multiple‐choice questions (50% were structured as “Yes/No” questions) to eliminate free text responses and help categorize answers to identify trends. Results from the survey were analyzed by the same medical communications agency that administered the survey. The target population was HCPs practicing in the United States (US) or internationally who treat patients with epilepsy and was designed to assess SUDEP awareness, clinical training, counseling patterns, application of the current SUDEP guidelines, and preferred formats for education/training.

RESULTS

Characteristics of survey respondents

A total of 114 responses were recorded from HCPs who treat patients with epilepsy, including patients in the pediatric population. The survey questions and their responses are reported in Table 1. Adult and pediatric epileptologists accounted for 71% (36% and 35%, respectively) of respondents, while the remainder was divided between adult and child neurologists, advanced practice providers (APPs), and “other” specialties. Approximately two‐thirds (63%) of respondents practiced at National Association of Epilepsy Centers (NAEC) Level 4 epilepsy centers; responses also included NAEC Level 3 epilepsy centers, academic centers, regional hospitals, private practices, or “other” practices. Most respondents completed residency or fellowship between 2010 and 2024 (68%); a minority completed clinical training between 1980 and 2009 (32%). Most respondents (75%) practiced in the US, whereas 25% practiced internationally in 13 different countries.

SUDEP awareness among survey respondents

When asked if they received any SUDEP training in residency and/or fellowship, a substantial proportion of respondents (41%) reported that SUDEP training was not provided, although almost all (97%) were aware of SUDEP risk in epilepsy patients (Table 1). Among respondents who completed residency and/or fellowship in 2020–2024, 75% received formal clinical SUDEP training, a metric that increased every decade from the 1980s through the 2010s (Figure 1). The percentages of trainees receiving SUDEP education during residency/fellowship for the 5 years before (2011–2016) and after (2018–2022) the publication of the 2017 AAN/AES practice guidelines were 56% and 79%, respectively.

Nearly two‐thirds of respondents (61%) reported having lost a patient due to SUDEP (Table 1). Despite being aware of SUDEP risk in epilepsy patients and experiencing patient loss, 26% of respondents were not aware of the 2017 AAN/AES SUDEP Practice Guidelines, even among the subgroup of respondents working in NAEC Level 3 and 4 epilepsy centers (26%).

SUDEP counseling practices

Nearly all respondents (97%) reported counseling patients or families on SUDEP risk; however, only 52% reported counseling all epilepsy patients on SUDEP risk (Table 1). The timing of SUDEP risk counseling varied. Some HCPs provide counseling at the first visit (42%), but the majority counsel after the first visit (54%); 5% of respondents indicated they never counsel their patients about SUDEP. Respondents' opinions regarding the top risk factor for SUDEP were also varied. The most frequently selected top risk factor was uncontrolled seizures, followed by more than 3 GTCS (the term used in the survey) per year and nocturnal seizures. Respondents indicated that they most prioritized targeting GTCS and seizure freedom for SUDEP mitigation.

SUDEP counseling preferences

Most respondents indicated that counseling provided to patients and families around SUDEP risk needs improvement (49%) or is unsatisfactory (9%) (Table 1). Preferences on how to receive continuing SUDEP education and training were varied. Meeting symposiums and online education were the most common preferences, but in‐office/hospital educational programs, printed materials (brochures), and peer‐reviewed publications were also frequently cited. When asked if the respondents and their clinical staff use freely available resources for patient and caregiver education, such as PAME, EF, or CURE, when counseling patients and families on SUDEP risk, a striking proportion (42%) indicated that they did not.

DISCUSSION

This survey assessed HCPs' SUDEP awareness, clinical training, counseling patterns, and application of SUDEP guidelines. The results reveal that a substantial proportion (41%) completed residency and/or fellowships without receiving SUDEP training. This finding is consistent with a 2021 survey of neurology trainees in which similar proportions of trainees in the US (44%) and internationally (41%) reported not receiving SUDEP education. 26 The absence of residency‐ and fellowship‐level training among a considerable portion of HCPs treating patients with epilepsy reveals a widespread gap in medical curricula and highlights a deficit in SUDEP awareness, even though this gap is improving. When the year of residency and/or fellowship completion is separated by decade, formal clinical SUDEP training increased every decade from the 1980s (0%) through 2019 and up to 2024 (75%, the year of the survey; see Figure 1). This trend may be explained, at least in part, by the fact that SUDEP did not have a widely accepted definition or standardized terminology prior to the late 1990s, 27 which aligns with our data. Additionally, there is a stark difference in formal SUDEP training in the 5 years before (56%, 2011–2016) and after (79%, 2018–2022) the publication of the 2017 AAN/AES practice guidelines.

Practice guidelines were released by the AAN and the AES in 2017, recommending HCPs inform all patients and caregivers of pediatric patients about the risk of SUDEP. 5 The guidelines emphasize that seizure freedom, particularly freedom from GTCS, is strongly associated with a reduced risk of SUDEP. Although nearly all respondents (97%) were aware of SUDEP risk in epilepsy patients, only 74% of respondents were aware of these guidelines. Lack of awareness about the guidelines was even present among respondents working in NAEC Level 3 and 4 epilepsy centers (26%). This finding demonstrates the clear gap between awareness of SUDEP and awareness of guidelines to guide best practice. Potential reasons for this gap may include dissemination barriers or perception that the guidelines are outdated, as it has been nearly a decade since their publication. However, at the time of acceptance of this publication, the AAN has provided a draft protocol and solicited public comment for a focused update to the 2017 SUDEP guidelines, suggesting that updated recommendations may be available soon.

Almost all HCPs were aware of SUDEP risk in patients, and a higher proportion of respondents (61%) had lost a patient due to SUDEP than reported in similar surveys (31–56%). 28 , 29 , 30 The findings of our survey were not unexpected, as it was conducted at a professional meeting focused on epilepsy.

In addition to evaluating the state of SUDEP awareness, this survey assessed counseling practices related to SUDEP. While nearly all HCPs indicated that they counsel patients and families about SUDEP risk, only 52% indicated that they counsel all epilepsy patients. This proportion is higher than previous studies (2%–20%) but is still woefully short of providing all epilepsy patients with SUDEP counseling. 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 Even though SUDEP is most commonly associated with frequent GTCS and nonadherence to ASMs, an analysis of the North American SUDEP registry concluded that SUDEP affects patients with a range of epilepsy types regardless of ASM‐responsiveness, 34 emphasizing the importance of SUDEP education for HCPs and the need for education and counseling for all patients with epilepsy.

Additionally, withholding information about SUDEP stands in sharp contrast to patient and caregiver counseling preferences as described by previous studies and surveys, 21 , 23 , 24 revealing a gap between providers and patients. The patients who participated in these studies and surveys indicated that they want to know about SUDEP and that they want to know early in their diagnosis, preferably via face‐to‐face communication. Similarly, nearly all caregivers from these reports wanted to discuss SUDEP with their HCP and valued the discussions; however, most currently received information from other sources, such as general online sources (Google or Facebook), nonprofits, or other epilepsy caregivers. 22

The timing of these discussions varied among the respondents. Some providers reported counseling at the first visit (42%), with the majority indicating that counseling occurs during subsequent visits (53%) and 5% never providing counseling. The percentage of providers who provide counseling during the first visit is in line with previously published surveys (11–52%). 29 , 30 Beyond visit timing, some studies have examined optimal timing of SUDEP discussions based on social factors, disease course, and medication adherence. One survey reported that a greater proportion of HCPs preferred building a trusting relationship with patients before counseling about SUDEP (49%) or initiating counseling when patients have poor medication adherence or an unhealthy lifestyle (31%) compared to discussing it at the first visit (8%). 28 Another study reported that, of those surveyed, the majority of respondents (65%) reported deferring counseling until the patient failed standard treatments. 29

Respondents of this survey prioritized achieving seizure freedom and targeting GTCS in nearly equal amounts (37% and 40%, respectively), but respondents also considered nocturnal seizures to be a major risk factor (16%). This finding aligns with the respondents' opinions that uncontrolled seizures and >3 GTCS/year are the top risk factors for SUDEP. The literature identifies GTCS as the primary risk factor for SUDEP, but uncontrolled and nocturnal seizures are commonly mentioned alongside GTCS as modifiable risk factors. 30 , 35

Counseling preferences were also evaluated in this survey. HCPs were asked to assess the quality of SUDEP counseling provided to patients and families. A minority of responders rated the quality of counseling as good, very good, or excellent (42%). This finding aligns with another report, in which 34%–35% of survey respondents reported a positive overall counseling experience. 30 These results suggest there may be an unmet need or a lack of awareness of resources available to guide SUDEP counseling.

In this survey, responses for SUDEP education/training preferences varied. Meeting symposium and online education were the top responses, but other methods of material dissemination were not negligible, indicating the need for multiple lines of dissemination.

Resources for patients, caregivers, and HCPs are available from organizations such as PAME (pameonline.org), EF (epilepsy.com), and CURE (cureepilepsy.org). They provide education and support to HCPs and patients on SUDEP and risk mitigation. At the time of publication, these organizations offer a range of resources, including a clinician toolkit and a fact sheet for families (PAME) 19 ; brochures, webinars, and a 24/7 helpline (EF) 18 ; and education about ongoing developments in SUDEP research (CURE Epilepsy). 17 Despite these exhaustive resources, many respondents reported not using them.

This survey study has potential selection and self‐report (social desirability) biases as limitations. These include a non‐probabilistic recruitment strategy (i.e., non‐random recruitment) based on convenience sampling: attendees at AES, an epilepsy‐specific meeting, may have had a greater interest in SUDEP than a broader population of HCPs treating people with epilepsy, resulting in selection bias. Additionally, the survey was conducted in person, which could have influenced respondents to over‐report or record what they perceived to be the “correct” answer.

This study also has some considerable strengths. To the best of our knowledge, this is only the second survey to explicitly assess HCP SUDEP‐specific clinical training, an underexplored metric that is valuable for understanding gaps in epilepsy care. A study by Nascimento et al. surveyed neurology trainees in the US and internationally about their familiarity with the SUDEP term, definition, and risk factors; strategies to prevent SUDEP; and prior formal educational exposure to SUDEP and grouped results by postgraduate year. 26 While the Nascimento paper explores SUDEP‐specific education during training, it represents a snapshot of one cohort. Our study examines the pattern of SUDEP training over time and provides historical context for the gaps in SUDEP awareness also revealed by this survey. Another strength is that it highlights the lack of awareness of AAN/AES recommendations. To the best of our knowledge, this is the only survey to examine HCP awareness of the SUDEP guidelines and to demonstrate that unfamiliarity with the guidelines exists, even within specialized epilepsy centers. Lastly, this survey demonstrates the underutilization of readily available free public resources, which can help guide discussions about SUDEP.

CONCLUSIONS

Progress in SUDEP awareness is encouraging (i.e., increasing awareness in more recent graduates), but there is still much that needs to be done to ensure that patients, families, and caregivers receive the type of counseling and information about SUDEP they need and desire. Patients and caregivers express that they prefer to learn about SUDEP from a trusted provider and value those in‐person conversations. Counseling can help manage stress and medication adherence to aid in preventing the evolution of non‐convulsive seizures, whether bilateral or focal, to GTCS.

The results from this survey demonstrate the current state of SUDEP awareness and identify opportunities for improvement in patient‐provider communication about SUDEP risk. The survey responses expose modifiable gaps between SUDEP counseling recommendations and clinical practice. Improving residency/fellowship training and increasing awareness of the 2017 AAN/AES SUDEP Practice Guidelines and use of available counseling resources may help align care with established recommendations. We suggest that including more rigorous SUDEP education and training, based on review of the current practice guideline, in formal resident and fellow curricula would be beneficial. These measures could include instruction based on current understanding of SUDEP pathophysiology and risk factors. Additionally, trainees could be afforded the opportunity to practice discussing SUDEP and receive objective feedback through Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) or other simulation center formats.

SUDEP training can also be better integrated into routine clinical practice. Discussion of SUDEP cases during morbidity and mortality presentations can be used to identify opportunities for care improvement. Creation and implementation of practice‐specific guidelines for SUDEP education that are followed by all providers within a practice, as well as ensuring that attending staff are modeling appropriate SUDEP discussions with patients and families to their trainees, would help provide consistency of care to these patients.

Lastly, we note that the planned focused update to the 2017 guidelines is expected to provide current information on SUDEP incidence rates and risk factors and should be a valuable resource for HCPs, patients, families, and caregivers.
