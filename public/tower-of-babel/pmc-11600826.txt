Exploring the interplay of living arrangements, social support, and depression among older adults in rural northwest China
Authors: Qiufeng Gao, Cuiyao Lei, Xiaofei Wei, Lanxi Peng, Xintong Wang, Ai Yue, Yaojiang Shi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the intensification of aging, depression among the older adults has become a significant global health challenge, especially in developing countries where the problem is particularly severe. This study aims to explore the association between living arrangements and depression, with a specific focus on examining the mediating role of social support in rural northwest China.MethodsData were collected from 501 individuals aged 60 and above in rural Shaanxi Province, northwest China. Living arrangements were assessed based on co-residing family members. Depression was measured using the Geriatric Depression Scale-30, and social support was evaluated with the Social Support Rating Scale. Ordinary Least Squares Regression, Quantile Regression, Generalized Linear Regression, and Ordered Regression analyzed the association between living arrangements and depression. Hierarchical multiple linear regression examined the mediating role of social support. A heterogeneity analysis by gender and socio-economic status was also conducted.ResultsThe depression rates were 51.84% for non-empty nests, 67.92% for individuals living alone, and 46.80% for individuals only living with a spouse. The regression analysis did not reveal a significant difference in depressive between empty nests compared to non-empty nests. However, older adults living alone exhibited a higher susceptibility to depression symptoms (β = 2.594, P < 0.05). The mediating analysis showed that empty nesters living alone experience higher rates of depression due to receiving less social support compared to those living with a spouse or in non-empty nest arrangements, particularly concerning subjective social support (β = 0.481, P < 0.05). Heterogeneity analysis identified specific vulnerable groups among older adults, emphasizing the higher susceptibility to depressive symptoms among those living alone, especially among male older adults or those from non-poor families.ConclusionsOur findings emphasize a significant prevalence of depression among older adults in rural areas, particularly highlighting the vulnerability of those living alone. This underscores the urgent need for government attention to address the mental health challenges faced by the older adults. Future interventions should focus on improving social support networks to mitigate the impact of living alone on depression symptoms among this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20737-w.

Background

Depression has emerged as a predominant mental health concern, significantly contributing to the overall disease burden among the older adults, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1]. The incidence of depressive symptoms among older adults is notably higher in LMICs than in high-income countries, attributed to limited access to health resources and lower socio-economic status [2]. Recent research in LMICs reveals men (25%) and women (35%) exhibit depression screening rates that surpass double those observed in the United States [3]. Currently, the overall incidence of depression among the elderly in developing countries is 40.78% [4], and the incidence rate in rural areas of many developing countries far exceeds this average [5], and some have reached 63.5% [6] or even 81.34% [7]. This not only diminishes the quality of life for older adults, impacting both physical and mental health, but also correlates with increased mortality rates [8, 9].

As a middle-income country, China is experiencing a significant demographic shift characterized by a population aging. This shift has heightened the risk of depression among its older citizens, particularly in rural areas. Extensive studies consistently underscore a high prevalence of depressive symptoms in China, with rural older adults being particularly vulnerable. For instance, a nationwide study disclosed a substantial 30.72% prevalence of depressive symptoms among older adults in 2018, notably higher in rural areas (34.02% vs. 21.50%) [10]. In rural communities, the growing of “empty nesters”—a term referring to older adults living either alone or only with a spouse, without the companionship of children or other family members—raises additional concerns [11], By 2020, this living arrangement had become prevalent among 45.40% of older adults in rural China, with 22.42% of these being empty-nest individuals living alone [12]. This trend is largely attributed to the declining fertility rate, shrinking family sizes and heightened dynamics of urban-rural migration in recent decades [13]. Living arrangements, as a critical structural element of social support, influence the mental health of older adults and may contribute to depression. According to family systems theory, the interdependence and interactions among family members shape individual mental health outcomes [14, 15]. While cohabitation can provide essential social support that helps protect against depression [16, 17], it can also introduce stressors like interpersonal conflicts and caregiving burdens, potentially exacerbating depressive symptoms [18–20].

Numerous studies have investigated the associations between living arrangements and depression among older adults, but findings from rural China are inconsistent. Evidence suggests that the impact of living arrangements on depression varies across societies and cultures [21–24]. In Confucian Asian societies, where traditional familial roles are emphasized, living arrangements may significantly affect depressive symptoms, especially in rural areas with limited social ties [11, 25]. In rural China, some empirical evidence highlights a shift in living arrangements from co-residing with family members to more self-reliant structures, influencing depression among older adults [13], while others find no significant differences between empty-nests and non-empty nest households [26–28]. This inconsistency may partly arise from the heterogeneity within the empty-nest group, particularly regarding spousal companionship. Both theoretical and empirical findings suggest that empty-nesters living alone may face systematically different mental health challenges and support resources compared to those living with a spouse, potentially increasing their vulnerability to depression [29, 30]. To determine whether all empty nesters are equally at risk or if those living alone are particularly vulnerable, our analysis will not only compare older adults in non-empty nests with those in empty nests but also further distinguish between those who live alone and those who live with a spouse. In addition, the impact of older adults’ living arrangements on their depression may vary by gender and income, particularly in Asian societies with strong gender roles and in resource-limited rural areas, which should be further explored. Older women in empty-nest households may be less affected by the geographical isolation of empty-nest living due to their tendency to maintain active social networks [31]. Conversely, from low-income families may rely more on family support and face a higher risk of depression when not co-residing with children [17, 32].

The importance of social support in mitigating depression among older adults is well-established [33–35]. Although a few studies have empirically examined the mediating effects of social support in the association between living arrangements and depression in rural China, these studies often lack a comprehensive analysis and fail to capture the full complexity of this relationship. Social support, defined as the perception of help or support from one’s social network, plays a crucial aspect in the mental health of older adults [36]. Living arrangements closely intertwine with social support [37], as living with family members facilitates mutual support [38]. Varying access to social support emerges as a crucial factor contributing to differences in depression among different living arrangements [17, 29, 39]. The significant relationship between social support and depression underscores its potential predictive role among older adults [40], as those lacking support are more susceptible to depression [41, 42]. While existing literature indicates that older adults living in empty-nest often receive less social support, adversely affecting their mental well-being and exacerbating levels of depression [37, 43], there has been limited exploration of the mediating role of social support in this context of Asian countries [38, 44, 45].

Specifically, research that focuses exclusively on rural China—where family support systems are often strained—remains limited [45–47]. Social support encompasses various sources, including intergenerational family members, friends, and government assistance [48]. While empty-nest living arrangements might reduce support from children, increased support from friends could potentially mitigate this decline in overall social support. However, most studies have concentrated on specific sources without systematically assessing the entire social support network [49, 50]. Furthermore, when examining different dimensions of social support (e.g., objective support, subjective support), research has identified varied roles in the relationship between living arrangements and subjective evaluation outcomes of older adults [33, 37, 44]. However, these studies predominantly focus on outcomes like life satisfaction and psychological well-being, with insufficient attention to their impact on depression. In the context of depression, research has often limited its analysis to a single dimension of social support, failing to differentiate the effects of various types of support [51, 52]. In rural China, it remains unclear which type of social support has the most significant mediating effect on depression, underscoring the necessity for further research. To address this gap, this study will conduct a comprehensive examination of the mediating role of social support, analyzing it from different sources and categorizing it into three dimensions: subjective support, objective support, and utilization of social support.

The primary aim of this study was to investigate the impact of living arrangements on depression and explore whether social support can mediate this relationship among older adults in rural northwest China. We hypothesized that older adults living in empty nests have similar depression prevalence compared to those in non-empty nests. However, we expected that empty-nesters living alone would have higher levels of depression compared to empty-nesters living only with a spouse or others. Additionally, we hypothesized that lower levels of social support mediate the relationship between living alone and increased depression among older adults. The secondary aim of this study was to examine potential gender and socio-economic differences in how living arrangements impact depression among older adults. We hypothesized that the effect of living arrangements on depression will differ by gender, with living alone having a more pronounced impact on depression among men compared to women. Additionally, we hypothesized that socio-economic status will influence this relationship, with older adults from poor families who live alone experiencing a higher prevalence of depression than those from non-poor families.

Methods

Study design and participants

The data used in this study was collected from rural Shaanxi province that located in northwest of China in 2021. In the survey year of 2021, the rural population of Shaanxi province reached 14.37 million, comprising 36.37% of the total population [53]. This percentage is higher than the national average rural level of 35.28%. By the end of 2021, the elderly population over 60 years old in Shaanxi Province accounted for 19.58%, which was higher than the national average of 18.94%. In addition, the per capita disposable income of rural residents of Shaanxi province (14,745 RMB) was lower than the national average (18,931 RMB) in 2021 [54].

In order to conduct the study, a multi-layer random sampling approach was employed within three designated prefectures. The sampling process involved the following steps. Firstly, 16 counties were randomly selected from a total of 27 counties within the three prefectures. After excluding the urban township that located the county within each sample county, random selection was employed to choose townships and villages. Subsequently, households were randomly selected within each village, with a focus on individuals aged 60 or older. During the survey, all available older adults residing in these households were included in the study. The initial sample consisted of 527 older adults from 402 households across 167 villages. However, due to cognitive impairments, deaf-mute conditions, other disabilities, and missing information, the final sample comprised 501 older adults from 386 families, yielding a response rate of 95.07%. Trained enumerators conducted face-to-face interviews with each sampled older adult as well as their primary caregivers.

Besides, it should be noted that the survey of social support was not conducted in all prefectures and thus, the investigation pertaining to social support included a smaller sample size of only 311 older adults. The study was approved by “Ethics Committees of Xi’an Jiaotong University in China (2020 − 1239) and Xi’an Gaoxin Hospital in China” (2019-KY005). Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Measures

Depressive symptoms (dependent variable)

The assessment of depressive symptoms was conducted using the Geriatric Depression Scale-30 (GDS-30), developed by Brink et al. in 1982 [55, 56]. The older adult was asked to reflect on their feeling during the previous week and respond to simple yes or no questions. The total score ranged from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating higher levels of depressive symptoms. Besides, based on the total score, the levels of depressive symptoms were also categorized into three groups: normal (0–10 score), mild (11–20 score), moderate to severe (21–30 score) [57]. The GDS-30, recognized as the most widely used and validated tool for assessing depressive symptoms in older adults, demonstrated excellent internal consistency in this study, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.897 [58]. Compared to other generic measures of depression, the GDS was more sensitive to detecting the presence and severity of depression in older adults [2].

Living arrangements (independent variable)

Living arrangements were assessed by inquiring about the number of family members residing in the same household during the preceding six months. Based on this information, participants were classified into two main categories: “empty nest” (older adults living alone or only with a spouse) and “non-empty nest” (those living with children, grandchildren, or other relatives). Within the “empty nest” category, we further distinguished between: [1] “Empty Nest - Living Alone” (those living without any other family members) and [2] “Empty Nest - Living with a Spouse” (those living only with a spouse). This classification allowed for a comparison of depression and social support across three groups: non-empty nest, empty nest - living alone, and empty nest - living with a spouse.

Social support (mediating variable)

Social support was assessed using a 10-item questionnaire adopted from the Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS) (Appendix Table 1) [59]. SSRS had comprehensively assessed the presence and engagement of material, direct assistance, social networks, group relationships, as well as the stability and availability of relationships in various domains, including family, marriage, friends, colleagues, informal groups, and temporary social communication. The total SSRS score ranged from 12 to 66 scores, with higher scores indicating a higher level of social support [59, 60]. The scale evaluated three dimensions of social support: subjective support (8–32 scores), objective support (1–22 scores), and utilization of support (3–12 scores). Subjective support referred to the emotional perception of support, while objective support included that the actual support received, such as instrumental support and involvement in social networks. Utilization of support measured the active utilization of available support resources. Higher scores in each dimension indicate stronger social support, and the total score serves as an overall measure of the individual’s current total social support status. The SSRS has been extensively used in Chinese populations and has demonstrated good reliability [61]. The Cronbach’s α was 0.667 in our sample, indicating that the scale has acceptable internal consistency.

Covariates

The survey included specific questions about the background characteristics, physical health, and health behaviors of older adults. The sociodemographic covariates assessed include the older adults’ age (60–69, 70–79, ≥ 80), gender (male or female), and educational levels (illiterate, primary school, middle school, high school or above), and whether the individual had the new rural cooperative medical insurance (NCMS), and whether the household received minimum living standard guarantee payments from the government (i.e., an indicator of poor families). Information of physical health was collected through self-reported health status (very bad, bad, fair, good, very good) and whether having chronic diseases. In terms of health behaviors, participants were asked about their smoking status (never, former, current). drinking status (never, former, current) and physical activity (yes or no).

Statistical analysis

This study employed Stata 17.0 to analyze the influence of living arrangements on depressive symptoms and SPSS 27.0 to explore the mediating effects of social support in this relationship. Initially, differences in older adults’ characteristics and depressive symptoms were examined using the ANOVA test for categorical variables and the t-test for continuous variables. Due to a significant violation of the normality assumption for continuous variables, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was also employed to compare depression scores between different groups (chi-square categorical variables and rank sum test for continuous variables). Secondly, to investigate the associations between living arrangements and depressive symptoms, Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) models with robust standard errors were conducted. These models utilized a dummy variable representing empty-nest living, and subsequently, three living arrangements (i.e., none-empty nest, living alone, only living with a spouse) were included. To enhance robustness, additional analyses were performed using Quantile Regression Models (QRM), Generalized Linear Models (GLM), and Ordered Regression Models (ORM) to evaluate these associations comprehensively. Thirdly, to assess the mediating effect of social support, hierarchical multiple linear regression methods were employed to verify the correlation between living arrangements, social support, and depressive symptoms. The process involved assessing the total effect of the independent variable (X) on the dependent variable (Y), calculating the regression coefficient of X on the mediator variable (M), and evaluating the effect of X on Y after controlling for the mediator variable. The conditions for mediating effects included a significant total effect, a significant influence of X on M, and consideration of complete or partial mediation based on the significance of the effect coefficient of X on Y after controlling for the mediator variable. The bootstrap method and percentages were adopted to further test the indirect effect of older adults’ living arrangements on their depression through social support, following the approach provided by Song et al. (2021) [62]. In all the regression models, we used fixed effects at the county level to control for unobserved heterogeneity between counties and to reduce omitted variable bias. Clustering was applied at the township level to account for correlations among elderly individuals within each township. The significance for all tests was set at 0.05, a standard threshold for balancing Type I error risk and statistical power, commonly adopted in empirical research.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Table 1 presents the sociodemographic characteristics, physical health and health behaviors of the sampled older adults based on their living arrangements. Of the participants, 48.90% lived in non-empty nest families, 10.58% lived alone and 40.52% only lived with a spouse. The average age of the entire population was around 69.67 years, with females accounting for 52.10% of the sample. In general, 42.12% reported no formal education. Almost half of the participants (48.70%) self-reported poor health conditions, and around 60% had chronic diseases. Additionally, 38.52% were former or current smokers, while 38.12% reported being former or current drinkers. Moreover, three-fourths of the participants (75.65%) did not engage in any physical exercise during the preceding two weeks. Furthermore, a higher proportion of older adults living alone were physically active (33.96%).

Table 2 displays the distribution of depressive symptoms and social support among the entire population and older adults with different types of living arrangements. The mean ± standard deviation of the GDS-30 score was 11.79 ± 7.11, with a depressive prevalence of 51.50% among the participants. The degree of depression symptoms ranged from mild (38.32%), moderate to severe (13.18%). While nonsignificant differences were found among non-empty nest and empty nest families, older adults living alone exhibited significantly higher levels of depression (P < 0.05), with a prevalence of 67.92%. In contrast, the proportion of older adults only living with a spouse showing depression was the lowest (46.80%), followed by the non-empty nest group (51.84%). Similar patterns were observed in social support among different living arrangements. Significantly lower levels of social support were noted among older adults living alone, including scores in total, subjective, and objective support (P < 0.01). The mean ± standard deviation of social support total scores was 35.35 ± 7.49 for older adults living in non-empty nest, 30.00 ± 7.35 for older adults living alone, and 36.78 ± 7.07 for older adults only living with a spouse.

The relationship between living arrangements and depression

Table 3 reports the impact of living arrangements on depression among older adults. OLS regression analysis reveals no significant association between empty-nest living arrangements and depression scores, even after adjusting for various control variables. Further analysis, which included subdividing empty-nest arrangements, shows no significant differences between individuals in non-empty nest arrangements and those only living with a spouse (P > 0.05). However, older adults living alone exhibited significantly higher depression scores compared to those in non-empty nest arrangements (β = 2.594, 95% CI: 0.837, 4.351). Further robustness checks of QRM, GLM and ORM all consistently confirm these findings, with living alone being the only arrangement significantly influencing depression, while no significant effects were found for empty-nest arrangements.

The mediating analysis of the social support between living arrangements and depression

In this study, living arrangement serves as an independent variable (X), with social support serving as the mediating variable (M), and depression score as the dependent variable (Y). According to the mediation model analysis results (Table 4), in comparison to non-empty nest living arrangements, the path by which living arrangement influences depressive tendencies through social support is significant only among older adults who live alone. Older adults who living alone may receive less social support than others, and then leading to higher levels of depressive symptoms. However, among the empty nest older adults who live with a spouse, the results in Table 5 indicate that social support may not be the potential pathway to explain how their such living arrangement may affect their depression level. Additionally, Bootstrap tests were conducted to verify the mediating effects of categorical variables (Table 5). Under the living alone arrangement: (1) The indirect effect of total social support is 0.736 (95% CI: 0.145, 1.606), comprising 27.40% (β/(α＋β)) of the total effect. (2) When looking into different dimensions, the indirect effect of subjective social support is 0.481 (95% CI: 0.015, 1.214), comprising 17.91% of the total effect; while the mediating role of the objective support and utilization of social support is not significant. Therefore, empty nesters who live alone may have higher level of depression due to the lower level of subjective support. When examining the sources of subjective social support, results in the Appendix Table 2 indicate that the significant differences of subjective support among older adults living alone were mainly derived from lower levels of family support, especially from spouse and children.

This suggests that the negative impact of living alone on depression is more pronounced among male older adults or those from non-poor families compared to their counterparts.

Discussion

While the influence of living arrangements on depression among older adults in rural China has been previously documented, few studies have comprehensively evaluated the mediating role of social support in the relationship between living arrangements and depression among older adults in rural China. This study addressed this gap by providing a detailed examination of how social support mediates the connection between living arrangements and depression. The main findings are as follows: Although no significant differences were observed between non-empty nest and empty nest configurations, living alone emerged as a pivotal factor linked to a heightened risk of depression. After systematically assessing the potential social support available, our analysis explored and confirmed the mediating role of social support in the relationship between living arrangements and depression. Additionally, we identified distinct effects across various dimensions of social support, with subjective support holding greater significance. This comprehensive exploration provides valuable insights into the complex dynamics shaping mental health outcomes related to living arrangements among older adults.

Living arrangements and depression prevalence

Our findings reveal a high prevalence of depression symptoms among older adults in rural China, with living arrangements significantly impacting depression [30, 63, 64]. Approximately 51.50% of the sampled older adults were identified as depressed, with a notably higher prevalence of 67.92% among those living alone. This high rate of depression and the elevated pattern of higher figure among older adults living alone are consistent with previous studies conducted in rural areas of China [11, 13, 65, 66]. The analysis indicates that while living in empty nest is not significantly associated with increased depression, living alone is found to be a significant risk factor. Although the absence of significant differences between non-empty nesters and empty-nesters diverges from our initial expectations, this result aligns with findings from other studies in rural China and other Asian countries that also reported non-significant differences [63, 67, 68]. According to “family conflict theory”, living with adult children can create negative tensions or increase caregiving responsibilities, potentially diminishing the benefits of multi-generational family interactions [14, 15]. Moreover, in migratory societies, rural elderly often assume significant caregiving roles for grandchildren, as leaving children behind is common among migrant workers [48, 69]. Among the empty-nest population, those living alone are at a notably higher risk of depression compared to those living with a spouse, aligning with previous research [31, 70]. In rural areas, where community and government support are often limited, older adults rely heavily on family support. The lack of companionship from spouses and adult children can result in a significant loss of essential social support. This highlights the unique challenges faced by older individuals living alone and suggests that those only living with a spouse may experience similar benefits as those living with both a spouse and adult children [30].

Mediating role of social support

Consistent with previous findings, our study confirms the crucial mediating role of social support among older adults in rural China [71, 72]. Specifically, living arrangements have both direct and indirect effects (through social support) on depression among older adults. We observe that compared to older adults living in non-empty nest arrangements or only with a spouse, those living alone had the lowest levels of social support and, consequently, the highest level of depression. These findings support previous studies suggesting that living with family members fosters more interpersonal communication, closer social relationships and higher social support [17]. Aligning with earlier research, our results indicate that rural older adults who receive more support demonstrate better mental health outcomes [66, 73], underscoring the inverse relationship between social support and depression [17, 41]. Additionally, mediation analysis has also found similar results, showing that older adults living with others are more likely to experience mutual support, consequently, have a lower risk of depression [17]. In summary, our results suggest that older adults living alone receive less social support and are more susceptible to depression compared to those living with family members [44].

Dimensions of social support

Moreover, our study offers a unique perspective by confirming the nuanced impact of different dimensions of social support on the association between living arrangements and depression among older adults. Compared to objective and utilization of social support, subjective support plays a stronger mediating role among older adults in rural China. The non-significant differences in the utilization of support suggest that the active and effective use of available support resources is similar across different living arrangements [74], indicating that this dimension does not serve as a mediator. Our findings, consistent with a meta-analysis, indicate that older adults living alone experience reduced levels of subjective support, which in turn increases their risk of depression compared with the non-empty nest group [51]. This aligns with research emphasizing the psychological benefits of emotional expression and communication in reducing depression risk among older adults in rural areas [75, 76]. Subjective social support, closely linked to individual perceptions, holds greater significance. Studies indicate that perceived support, as a psychological reality influencing behavior and development, surpasses the impact of objective support [59, 77]. In essence, our results suggest that the emotional needs of older adults living alone in rural areas take precedence over other needs, especially in the context of accelerated urbanization and the heightened prevalence of social isolation [51]. Interestingly and importantly, our study underscores that in rural areas, spouses and children serve as the primary sources of subjective social support, consistent with the family systems theory and highlighting the critical role of family in supporting older adults [48].

Heterogeneity analysis of socio-economic status and gender

Additionally, our analysis of heterogeneity highlights variations in the impact of living arrangements on depression based on socio-economic status and gender among older adults. Contrary to previous findings, older adults living alone in non-poor families exhibite a higher likelihood of depression. This finding can be interpreted through two dimensions. From a psychological perspective, older adults from economically disadvantaged backgrounds may develop greater increased stress resistance and resilience over time, leading to overall health improved [78]. This enhanced resilience might make them less sensitive to change in living arrangements. Conversely, in impoverished rural areas, economic hardships may have a more significant impact on depression, rendering changes in living arrangements relatively less influential. The vulnerability observed in elderly men living alone underscores the significance of social networks, particularly spousal support, in mitigating the risk of depression [22]. Men, especially during midlife, often heavily depend on their spouses for household chores and childcare. Therefore, the loss of a spouse not only brings emotional distress but also exacerbates the burden of domestic tasks due to the absence of robust social networks [79]. In contrast, women are more likely to participate in social activities, such as square dancing, and maintain active social networks with friends and family. This engagement provides them with additional social support, which may help to alleviate the impact of living arrangements on depression [80].

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, the sample is exclusively drawn from rural areas in northwest China, and therefore, the prevalence of depression found in this study may not be fully representative of rural older adults nationally. Additionally, the cross-sectional nature of the gathered data limits our ability to establish causal relationships between living arrangements and depressive symptoms. Potential endogeneity issues cannot be entirely ruled out. Lastly, the small sample size may affect the robustness of the observed relationships. To address these challenges, we have performed sensitivity analyses to assess the reliability of our results. Despite these efforts, the limitations inherent to a small sample size may still impact the precision and generalizability of the findings. Future research employing longitudinal data and larger, more diverse samples are warranted to enhance the generalizability and causal inference of our findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study delves into the intricate relationship between living arrangements, social support, and depression among older adults in rural China. We highlight the significant role of living alone in contributing to a heightened risk of depression, underscoring the unique challenges faced by older individuals without companionship. The confirmed mediating role of social support unveils distinct effects, with subjective support emerging as a particularly influential factor. This emphasizes the importance of emotional well-being and interpersonal connections in mitigating depression risk among rural older adults. This nuanced comprehension informs the development of tailored strategies to address the specific needs of older adults living alone in rural China, ultimately promoting improved mental health outcomes in this demographic. Firstly, to mitigate the adverse effects of empty-nest and living alone situations, it is essential to enhance social support through targeted community and government initiatives. Implement programs that foster interaction among older adults and establish regular outreach efforts to provide ongoing emotional and practical assistance. Secondly, to strengthen subjective social support, it is recommended to implement comprehensive digital literacy programs for older adults. Specifically, develop and provide training sessions to teach them how to use technology effectively to stay connected with family and friends. This approach will help reduce the effects of physical isolation by facilitating regular, meaningful interactions and maintaining essential social connections.
