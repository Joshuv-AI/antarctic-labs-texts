Sharp bounds for the growth and distortion of the analytic part of convex harmonic functions
María J. Martín

Abstract
. We obtain the sharp upper and lower bounds for the growth and distortion of the analytic parts h h of orientation-preserving harmonic mappings f = h + g ¯ f=h+\overline{g} (normalized in the standard way) that map the unit disk onto a convex domain.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
31A05, 30C45, 30C75

1.
Introduction

A locally univalent, orientation-preserving, complex-valued harmonic function
f=h+\overline{g}
in the unit disk
{\mathbb{D}}
, normalized by the conditions
h(0)=g(0)=1-h^{\prime}(0)=0
is said to belong to the class
\mathcal{K}_{H}
if
f({\mathbb{D}})
is a convex domain. If, in addition,
g^{\prime}(0)=0
, the function
f
belongs to the class
\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
. Notice that both functions
h
and
g
in the decomposition
f=h+\overline{g}
are holomorphic in the unit disk whenever
f
is a harmonic function.

The obvious fact that any analytic function is harmonic, shows that the class
\mathcal{K}
, which consists of those analytic functions
\varphi
in
{\mathbb{D}}
such that
\varphi(0)=1-\varphi^{\prime}(0)=0
, is contained in
\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
.

It is well known (see
[
6
, Thm. 2.2.8]
, for instance) that the growth and distortion of a given
\varphi\in\mathcal{K}
are controlled, respectively, by the estimates

(1)

\frac{|z|}{1+|z|}\leq|\varphi(z)|\leq\frac{|z|}{1-|z|}\,,\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}\,,

and

(2)

\frac{1}{(1+|z|)^{2}}\leq|\varphi^{\prime}(z)|\leq\frac{1}{(1-|z|)^{2}}\,,\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}\,.

In particular, if we let
|z|\to 1
in the lower bound in (
1
), we conclude that the Euclidean disk of center
0
and radius
1/2
is contained in the range of any function
\varphi\in\mathcal{K}
. The value
1/2
is sharp, as will be justified below.

Both (
1
) and (
2
) are sharp: equality in any of the inequalities mentioned is obtained, for a given
z\in{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{0\}
by considering the function
\ell
, defined by

(3)

\ell(z)=\frac{z}{1-z}\,,\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}\,,

or some of its rotations,
\ell_{\lambda}
, where, for
|\lambda|=1
and
z
in the unit disk,
\ell_{\lambda}(z)=\overline{\lambda}\ell(\lambda z)
.

The function
\ell
maps the unit disk onto the half-plane
\mathbb{H}=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\colon{\rm Re\,}z>-1/2\}
. Therefore, no disk centered at the origin and with radius
r>1/2
is contained in the range of
\ell
.

The class
\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
is much wider that its analytic counterpart
\mathcal{K}
and there is a number of difficult problems that remain unresolved in relation with functions in this class. For instance, no sharp bounds for the growth of a given
f\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
are known. More concretely, we mention that it is not difficult to check (see
[
5
, p. 100]
) that if
f\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
and
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
, then

(4)

\frac{|z|}{(1+|z|)^{2}}\leq|f(z)|\leq\frac{|z|}{(1-|z|)^{2}}\,.

Nevertheless, as pointed out in
[
5
, p. 100]
, the lower bound in (
4
) is far from being sharp. The upper bound is known to be correct with respect to the order of growth since

\limsup_{r\to 1}\ (1-r)^{2}\sup_{|z|=r}|L(z)|\geq\frac{1}{2}\,,

where
L=H+\overline{G}\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
is the
half-plane harmonic mapping
defined by

(5)

L(z)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(\ell(z)+k(z)\right)+\left(\overline{\ell(z)-k(z)}\right)\right]\,,\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}\,.

In (
5
),
\ell
is given by (
3
) and
k
is the
Koebe function

(6)

k(z)=\frac{z}{(1-z)^{2}}\,,\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}\,.

Notice that the
analytic part

H
of
L
equals to

(7)

H(z)=\frac{2z-z^{2}}{2(1-z)^{2}}\,,\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}\,.

We again refer the reader to
[
5
, p. 100]
, where the author states that it seems likely that the sharp bounds for the growth of functions in the class
\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
are attained by the function
L
. This is a problem that is still open and will not be solved in this paper. However, as a perhaps first step towards a solution, we obtain the sharp bounds for the growth and distortion of the analytic part
h
of functions
f=h+\overline{g}
in the class
\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
, a result which seems to be interesting by itself and whose proof seems to have eluded the experts in the area so far. Here is the specific statement.

Theorem 1
.

Let
f=h+\overline{g}\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
. Then, the growth and distortion of the analytic part
h
of
f
are subject, respectively, to the bounds

(8)

\frac{2|z|+|z|^{2}}{2(1+|z|)^{2}}\leq|h(z)|\leq\frac{2|z|-|z|^{2}}{2(1-|z|)^{2}}\,,\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}\,,

and

(9)

\frac{1}{(1+|z|)^{3}}\leq|h^{\prime}(z)|\leq\frac{1}{(1-|z|)^{3}}\,,\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}\,.

The estimates are sharp: equality holds in any of the inequalities if and only if
f
equals the harmonic half-plane mapping (
5
) or some of its rotations
L_{\lambda}
given by
L_{\lambda}(z)=\overline{\lambda}L(\lambda z)
, where
|\lambda|=1
. That is, if and only if
h
equals the function
H
in (
7
) or some of its rotations.

As a consequence of our theorem, if we let
|z|\to 1
in the lower bound in (
8
), we conclude that the Euclidean disk of center
0
and radius
3/8
is contained in the range of every function
h
which is the analytic part of a function
f\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
. The value
3/8
is sharp, since the function
H
given by (
7
) satisfies that no Euclidean disk centered at the origin with radius
r>3/8
is contained in
H({\mathbb{D}})
.

2.
Background

In this section we review some results that will be used in the proof of Theorem
1
.

2.1.
Growth and distortion of functions in the class
\mathcal{S}

The class
\mathcal{S}
consists on univalent (
i.e.
one-to-one) analytic functions
\varphi
in the unit disk normalized by the conditions
\varphi(0)=1-\varphi^{\prime}(0)=0
. The following result is well known. We refer the reader to
[
4
, Thms. 2.5, 2.6]
,
[
6
, Thm. 2.2.7]
, or
[
9
, Thm. 1.6]
for a proof.

Theorem A
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}
and let
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
. Then,

\frac{|z|}{(1+|z|)^{2}}\leq|\varphi(z)|\leq\frac{|z|}{(1-|z|)^{2}}

and

\frac{1-|z|}{(1+|z|)^{3}}\leq|\varphi^{\prime}(z)|\leq\frac{1+|z|}{(1-|z|)^{3}}\,.

Equality in any of the inequalities mentioned is obtained, for a given
z\in{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{0\}
, by the Koebe function
k
in (
6
) or some of its rotations .

Another relevant theorem that must be mentioned is due to Bieberbach
[
1
,
2
]
(see also
[
4
, Thm. 2.2]
).

Theorem B
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}
. Then,
|\varphi^{\prime\prime}(0)|\leq 4
. Moreover,
|\varphi^{\prime\prime}(0)|=4
if and only if
\varphi
equals a rotation of the Koebe function
k
.

2.2.
Functions in the Carathéodory class

Let
p
be an analytic function in the unit disk with
p(0)=1
. If
{\rm Re\,}p(z)\geq 0
for
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
, we say that
p
belongs to the
Carathéodory class
, denoted by
\mathcal{P}
.

Any
p\in\mathcal{P}
can be represented as

p=\frac{1+\delta}{1-\delta}

for some analytic function
\delta
in the unit disk such that
\delta(0)=0
and
\delta({\mathbb{D}})\subset{\mathbb{D}}
(see
[
6
, p. 28]
). As a direct consequence of the Schwarz lemma, we have that if
p\in\mathcal{P}
,

(10)

\frac{1-|z|}{1+|z|}\leq|p(z)|\leq\frac{1+|z|}{1-|z|}\,,\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}\,.

The inequalities in (
10
) are sharp, as rotations of the functions
p(z)=(1+z)/(1-z)
, which belong to
\mathcal{P}
, show.

The following lemma will be used in Section
3
to prove Theorem
1
. The argument used in the proof can be found in
[
10
]
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
q
be an analytic function in the unit disk with
q(0)=1
. Suppose that there exists
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
{\rm Re\,}\left(e^{i\alpha}q(z)\right)\geq 0
for
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
. Then, there exists a function
\delta
, analytic in the unit disk, with
\delta(0)=0
and
\delta({\mathbb{D}})\subset{\mathbb{D}}
such that

(11)

q(z)=\frac{1+e^{-2i\alpha}\delta(z)}{1-\delta(z)}\,,\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}\,.

Proof.

Since
{\rm Re\,}\left(e^{i\alpha}q(z)\right)>0
and
q(0)=1
, we have that
\cos\alpha>0
. Let

Q(z)=\frac{e^{i\alpha}q(z)-i\sin\alpha}{\cos\alpha}\,,\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}\,.

Then,
Q
is analytic in the unit disk,
Q(0)=1
, and
{\rm Re\,}Q(z)>0
for all
z
in the unit disk. That is,
Q\in\mathcal{P}
. Therefore, there exists a function
\delta
as in the statement of the theorem such that
Q=(1+\delta)/(1-\delta)
. This gives (
11
) and ends the proof of the lemma.

\Box

2.3.
Functions convex in some direction

A domain
\Omega
in the complex plane is said to be
convex in the direction

t\in[0,\pi)
if the intersection of
\Omega
with any line parallel to the line through
0
and
e^{it}
is an interval or empty. Note that a convex domain is a domain convex in every direction. The orientation-preserving complex-valued function
f
in the unit disk
{\mathbb{D}}
is said to be convex in the
t
direction if
f({\mathbb{D}})
is convex in the direction
t
. Since every domain convex in some direction is a close-to-convex domain, any locally univalent analytic function convex in some direction is univalent (see, for instance,
[
8
]
and the references therein).

2.4.
Some fundamental results on convex harmonic mappings

The following beautiful theorem was proved in
[
3
]
. It shows a relation between functions in
\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
and the classes of functions convex in some direction defined in Section
2.3
and the class
\mathcal{S}
introduced in Section
2.1
.

Theorem C
.

Let
f=h+\overline{g}
be a locally univalent, orientation-preserving harmonic function with
h(0)=g(0)=1-h^{\prime}(0)=g^{\prime}(0)=0
. Then,
f\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
if and only if for all
\theta\in[0,\pi)
the analytic function
h-e^{2i\theta}g
is convex in the direction
\theta
. In particular, if
f=h+\overline{g}\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
, then for all
|\varepsilon|=1
the analytic functions
h-\varepsilon g
belong to the class
\mathcal{S}
.

Another result from
[
3
]
is the next one (
cf.

[
3
, Lem. 5.11]
).

Theorem D
.

Let
f=h+\overline{g}\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
. Then, there exist
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that for all
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
,

{\rm Re\,}\left(e^{i(\alpha+\beta)}\left(h^{\prime}(z)+e^{-2i\alpha}g^{\prime}(z)\right)\left(1-e^{-2i\beta}z^{2}\right)\right)\geq 0\,.

Regarding the Taylor coefficients of the analytic part
h
and the anti-analytic part
g
of functions
f=h+\overline{g}
in
\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
, we recall the result in the following theorem, whose proof can be found in
[
3
]
or in
[
5
, p. 50]
.

Theorem E
.

Let
f=h+\overline{g}\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
. Assume that the analytic functions
h
and
g
have Taylor series expansions

h(z)=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}\quad\text{and}\quad g(z)=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}b_{n}z^{n}\,,\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}\,,

respectively. Then, for
n=2,3,\ldots
,

|a_{n}|\leq\frac{n+1}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad|b_{n}|\leq\frac{n-1}{2}\,.

Equality holds for the function
L
defined in (
5
).

The
dilatation
of a locally-univalent, orientation-preserving harmonic function
f=h+\overline{g}
in
{\mathbb{D}}
is the analytic function in the unit disk defined by
\omega=g^{\prime}/h^{\prime}
. The fact that this function is analytic in the unit disk follows from the fact that de Jacobian
J_{f}=|h^{\prime}|^{2}-|g^{\prime}|^{2}
of any locally univalent orientation-preserving harmonic mapping
f=h+\overline{g}
is positive in
{\mathbb{D}}
(see
[
7
]
). Indeed this condition implies that not only
\omega
is holomorphic in the unit disk but also that
\omega({\mathbb{D}})\subset{\mathbb{D}}
. It is clear that if
f\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
has dilatation
\omega
, then
\omega(0)=0
.

To finish this section, we mention a transformation that preserves the class
\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
. It is related to rotations, which have been already mentioned. Concretely, as pointed out above, given
f=h+\overline{g}\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
and given
|\lambda|=1
, the
rotation

f_{\lambda}
of
f
is defined, for
z
in the unit disk, by
f_{\lambda}(z)=\overline{\lambda}f(\lambda z)
. It is easy to check that
f_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
for all
|\lambda|=1
.

Rotations are particular cases of holomorphic automorphisms of the unit disk. Other examples are the functions
\varphi_{a}
, for
a\in{\mathbb{D}}
, given by

\varphi_{a}(z)=\frac{a-z}{1-\overline{a}z}\,,\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}\,.

It is not difficult to check that if
f=h+\overline{g}\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
and
a\in{\mathbb{D}}
, the function

f_{a}=h_{a}+\overline{g_{a}}=\frac{f\circ\varphi_{a}-f(a)}{h^{\prime}(a)(1-|a|^{2})}\in\mathcal{K}_{H}\,.

Since the dilatation
\widehat{\omega_{a}}=g^{\prime}_{a}/h^{\prime}_{a}=\mu_{a}(\omega\circ\varphi_{a})
, where
\omega
is the dilatation of
f
and
\mu_{a}=h^{\prime}(a)/\overline{h^{\prime}(a)}
, does not necessarily fix the origin, one further transformation is needed to get a function in
\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
. Concretely, given a function
f=h+\overline{g}
in this class with dilatation
\omega
, the new function

(12)

F_{a}=\frac{f_{a}-\overline{\widehat{\omega_{a}}(0)f_{a}}}{1-|\omega_{a}(0)|^{2}}\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}\,.

We refer the reader to
[
5
, p. 79]
for a proof. A straightforward calculation shows that if we write the function
F_{a}
in (
12
) as
F_{a}=H_{a}+\overline{G}_{a}
, then

(13)

H_{a}(z)=\frac{\left(h\left(\varphi_{a}(z)\right)-h(a)\right)-\overline{\omega(a)}\left(g\left(\varphi_{a}(z)\right)-g(a)\right)}{h^{\prime}(a)(1-|a|^{2})(1-|\omega(a)|^{2})}

and

G_{a}(z)=\frac{\left(g\left(\varphi_{a}(z)\right)-h(a)\right)-\omega(a)\left(h\left(\varphi_{a}(z)\right)-h(a)\right)}{\overline{h^{\prime}(a)}(1-|a|^{2})(1-|\omega(a)|^{2})}\,,\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}\,.

The dilatation
\omega_{a}
of
F_{a}
is, for
z
in the unit disk,

(14)

\omega_{a}(z)=-\mu_{a}(\varphi_{\omega(a)}\circ\omega\circ\varphi_{a}(z))=-\frac{h^{\prime}(a)}{\overline{h^{\prime}(a)}}\frac{\omega(a)-\omega(\varphi_{a}(z))}{1-\overline{\omega(a)}\omega(\varphi_{a}(z))}\,.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1

Proof.

Let
f=h+\overline{g}\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
have dilatation
\omega
. We start by proving that the inequality

|h^{\prime}(z)|\leq\frac{1}{(1-|z|)^{3}}

holds for
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
.

To do so, let us fix the real values
\alpha
and
\beta
provided by Theorem
D
so that

{\rm Re\,}\left(e^{i(\alpha+\beta)}\left(h^{\prime}(z)+e^{-2i\alpha}g^{\prime}(z)\right)\left(1-e^{-2i\beta}z^{2}\right)\right)\geq 0\,.

A direct application of Lemma
1
shows that there exists an analytic function
\delta
in the unit disk with
\delta(0)=0
and
\delta({\mathbb{D}})\subset{\mathbb{D}}
such that for
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
,

h^{\prime}(z)+e^{-2i\alpha}g^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1+e^{-2i(\alpha+\beta)}\delta(z)}{1-\delta(z)}\frac{1}{1-e^{-2i\beta}z^{2}}\,.

As a consequence of the triangle inequality and de Schwarz lemma we have, from the previous identity, that for some
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

(15)

|h^{\prime}(z)+e^{-2i\alpha}g^{\prime}(z)|\leq\frac{1+|z|}{1-|z|}\frac{1}{1-|z|^{2}}=\frac{1}{(1-|z|)^{2}}\,.

On the other hand, by Theorem
C
, the function
h-e^{-2i\alpha}g
belongs to the class
\mathcal{S}
. Therefore, by Theorem
A
,

(16)

|h^{\prime}(z)-e^{-2i\alpha}g^{\prime}(z)|\leq\frac{1+|z|}{(1-|z|)^{3}}\,.

Hence, using again the triangle inequality, jointly with the estimates (
15
) and (
16
), we get

|h^{\prime}(z)|=\frac{1}{2}|h^{\prime}(z)+e^{-2i\alpha}g^{\prime}(z)+h^{\prime}(z)-e^{-2i\alpha}g^{\prime}(z)|\leq\frac{1}{(1-|z|)^{3}}\,.

This proves the upper bound in (
9
). The sharpness of the bound will be deferred to the end of the proof. However, it may be convenient to stress at this point that if for some
z\in{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{0\}
we have
|h^{\prime}(z)|=1/(1-|z|)^{3}
, then there must exist
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that equality in (
16
) holds, which gives that for that value of
\alpha
the function
h-e^{-2i\alpha}g
is a rotation of the Koebe function (
6
), according to Theorem
A
.

To prove that the upper bound in (
8
) is satisfied, we argue as usual, by using the bound for
|h^{\prime}|
as follows. Let us fix
z=re^{i\theta}\in{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{0\}
, so that
|z|=r\in(0,1)
, and observe that

h(z)=\int_{0}^{r}h^{\prime}(\rho e^{i\theta})\,e^{i\theta}\,d\rho\,,

since
h(0)=0
. Therefore,

|h(z)|\leq\int_{0}^{r}|h^{\prime}(\rho e^{i\theta})|\,|d\rho|\leq\int_{0}^{r}\frac{1}{(1-|\rho|)^{3}}\,|d\rho|=\frac{2r-r^{2}}{2(1-r^{2})}\,.

The previous estimate proves the upper bound in (
8
). Equality holds for some point
z
in the unit disk different from zero if equality holds for the bound of
|h^{\prime}|
for all points in the segment joining the origin with
z
. Therefore, as above, we conclude that if equality holds for such
z
, then
h-e^{-2i\alpha}g
is a rotation of the Koebe function (
6
) for some
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Let us now prove the lower bound in (
9
): given
f=h+\overline{g}\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
and given
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
, the inequality

|h^{\prime}(z)|\geq\frac{1}{(1+|z|)^{3}}

holds.

To do so, let
f=h+\overline{g}\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
and let
a\in{\mathbb{D}}
. Consider the function
F_{a}=H_{a}+\overline{G}_{a}
as in (
12
). Since
F_{a}\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
, we can argue as in (
15
) to get a real value
\alpha
such that for all
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
,

|H_{a}^{\prime}(z)+e^{-2i\alpha}G_{a}^{\prime}(z)|\leq\frac{1}{(1-|z|)^{2}}\,.

In particular, if we set
z=a
, we have

(17)

|H_{a}^{\prime}(a)+e^{-2i\alpha}G_{a}^{\prime}(a)|=|H_{a}^{\prime}(a)|\cdot|1+e^{-2i\alpha}\omega_{a}(a)|\leq\frac{1}{(1-|a|)^{2}}\,.

Here,
\omega_{a}
is the dilatation of
F_{a}
given by (
14
), which satisfies
|\omega_{a}(a)|=|\omega(a)|
, since the dilatation
\omega
of any function in
\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
fixes the origin. Therefore, an application of the triangle inequality gives, from (
17
),

(18)

|H_{a}^{\prime}(a)|(1-|\omega(a)|)\leq|H_{a}^{\prime}(a)|\cdot|1+e^{-2i\alpha}\omega_{a}(a)|\leq\frac{1}{(1-|a|)^{2}}\,.

Using the formula for
H_{a}
in (
13
), it is a straightforward calculation to show that, since
h^{\prime}(0)=1
,
g^{\prime}(0)=0
,
\varphi_{a}(a)=0
, and
|\varphi^{\prime}(a)|=1/(1-|a|^{2})
,

(19)

|H_{a}^{\prime}(a)|=\frac{1}{|h^{\prime}(a)|(1-|a|^{2})^{2}(1-|\omega(a)|^{2})}\,.

Therefore, setting (
19
) in (
18
), we conclude that for any
a
in the unit disk,

\displaystyle\frac{1-|\omega(a)|}{|h^{\prime}(a)|(1-|a|^{2})^{2}(1-|\omega(a)|^{2})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|h^{\prime}(a)|(1-|a|^{2})^{2}(1+|\omega(a)|)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-|a|)^{2}}\,.

This is equivalent to

(20)

\displaystyle|h^{\prime}(a)|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{(1-|a|)^{2}}{(1-|a|^{2})^{2}(1+|\omega(a)|)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1+|a|)^{2}(1+|\omega(a)|))}\geq\frac{1}{(1+|a|)^{3}}\,,

since
\omega
is an analytic function in the unit disk that satisfies the hypotheses of ht Schwarz lemma and therefore, the inequality
|\omega(a)|\leq|a|
holds. This proves the lower bound in (
9
).

For
0<r<1
, let
m_{r}=\min_{|z|=r}|h(z)|
. Since
h
is in the class
\mathcal{S}
, by Theorem
C
, we get that
m_{r}>0
and by the definition of
m_{r}
we have that the Euclidean disk centered at the origin and with radius
m_{r}
is contained in the set
S=\{h(z)\colon|z|\leq r\}
. Moreover, by the continuity of
h
in
{\mathbb{D}}
, there must exists a point
z_{0}
of modulus
r
such that
|h(z_{0})|=m_{r}
, so that the segment
s
which joints
0
and
h(z_{0})
is contained in
S
. Let
C
be the pre-image under
h
of the segment
s
. Using again that
h
is univalent in the unit disk, we have that
C
is an simple analytic curve which joins
0
and
h(z_{0})
. We then have, since
h^{\prime}(\zeta)\,d\zeta
has constant argument for
\zeta\in C
, that using that the lower bound in (
9
) holds,

(21)

\displaystyle|h(z_{0})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\int_{C}h^{\prime}(\zeta))\,d\zeta\right|=\int_{C}|h^{\prime}(\zeta))|\,|d\zeta|\geq\int_{0}^{r}\frac{1}{(1+\rho)^{3}}\,d\rho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2r+r^{2}}{2(1+r)^{2}}\,.

This shows that if
|z|=r
, then

|h(z)|\geq|h(z_{0})|\geq\frac{2r+r^{2}}{2(1+r)^{2}}\,,

which proves the lower bound in (
8
).

Sharpness

To finish the proof of Theorem
1
, we are to show that equality in any of the inequalities in either (
8
) or (
9
) holds for some
z\in{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{0\}
for a function
f\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
, then
f
is a rotation of the function
L
defined in (
5
). To do so, we will use the following proposition. We have not been able to find any explicit reference of this result, so that we include a proof for the sake of completeness. Notice that the function
L
in (
5
) satisfies the condition
H-G=k
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
f=h+\overline{g}\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
. If there exists a real number
\mu
such that
h-e^{-2i\mu}g
equals a rotation of the Koebe function
k
in (
6
), then
f
is a rotation of the half-plane harmonic mapping
L
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda=e^{it}
,
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, be such that
h(z)-e^{-2i\mu}g(z)=e^{-it}k(e^{it}z)
. The rotation
f_{\mu}=h_{\mu}+\overline{g_{\mu}}
of
f
, defined by
f_{\mu}(z)=e^{i\mu}f(e^{-i\mu}z)
satisfies

h_{\mu}^{\prime}(z)-g_{\mu}^{\prime}(z)=h^{\prime}(e^{-i\mu}z)-e^{-2i\mu}g^{\prime}(e^{-i\mu}z)=k^{\prime}(e^{i(t-\mu)}z)\,.

This implies that
h_{\mu}-g_{\mu}
equals a rotation of the Koebe function. Concretely,
h_{\mu}(z)-g_{\mu}(z)=e^{-i(\mu-t)}k(e^{i(t-\mu)}z)
,
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
. Since
f_{\mu}
is a rotation of a function in
\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
, it belongs to
\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
as well. Hence, according to Theorem
C
,
h_{\mu}-g_{\mu}
must be convex in the
0
direction.

The Koebe function
k
maps the unit disk onto the complex plane minus the slit along the negative real axes from
-\infty
to
-1/4
. Therefore,
k
is convex only in the horizontal direction (that is, only in the
0
direction) and hence, the rotation
k_{\lambda}
of the function
k
for
\lambda=e^{i\theta}
, given by
k_{\lambda}(z)=e^{i\theta}k(e^{-i\theta}z)
, is convex only in the
\theta
direction for
\theta\in[0,\pi)
, and only in the
2\pi-\theta
direction for
\theta\in[\pi,2\pi)
. This gives only two possibilities: either
h_{\mu}-g_{\mu}=k
or
h_{\mu}-g_{\mu}=\widehat{k}
, where
\widehat{k}(z)=-k(-z)
. The latter case can be reduced to the former by applying a new rotation to the function
f_{\mu}
. Concretely,
\widehat{f_{\mu}}=-f_{\mu}(-z)
. Therefore, we may assume, without loss of generality, that if a given function satisfies the hypotheses in the theorem, then some of its rotations that we again denote by
f=h+\overline{g}
in order not to burden the notation, satisfies
h-g=k
. The proof of the proposition will be completed if we check that any such function must be the function
L
.

To check that any convex harmonic mapping
f=h+\overline{g}
in
\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
with
h-g=k
is the harmonic half-plane mapping, we proceed as follows.

Recall that
h-g\in\mathcal{S}
. Hence, by the Bieberbach Theorem
B
,

|h^{\prime\prime}(0)-g^{\prime\prime}(0)|\leq 4\,.

On the other hand, we have, by Theorem
E
, that
|h^{\prime\prime}(0)|\leq 3
and
|g^{\prime\prime}(0)|\leq 1
. Therefore, we obtain

4=k^{\prime\prime}(0)={\rm Re\,}k^{\prime\prime}(0)={\rm Re\,}\left(h^{\prime\prime}(0)-g^{\prime\prime}(0)\right)\leq|h^{\prime\prime}(0)|+|g^{\prime\prime}(0)|\leq 4\,.

That is, in particular,
{\rm Re\,}g^{\prime\prime}(0)=-1
.

The dilatation
\omega
of
f
is given by
\omega(z)=g^{\prime}(z)/h^{\prime}(z)
for
z
in the unit disk. The conditions
\omega(0)=0
and
\omega({\mathbb{D}})\subset{\mathbb{D}}
are satisfied. Therefore, by the Schwarz lemma,
|\omega^{\prime}(0)|\leq 1
, with equality if and only if
\omega(z)=\lambda z
for some
\lambda=1
. It is easy to check that
\omega^{\prime}(0)=g^{\prime\prime}(0)
. Hence,
\omega(z)=-z
.

The solution of the system of equations
h-g=k
and
g^{\prime}(z)=-zh^{\prime}(z)
,
h(0)=g(0)=0
, is the function
L
. This ends the proof.

\Box

Let us now finish the proof of Theorem
1
. As pointed out above, if a given function
f=h+\overline{g}\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
satisfies that

|h(z)|=\frac{2|z|-|z|^{2}}{2(1-|z|)^{2}}

for some
z\in{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{0\}
, then, necessarily,
|h^{\prime}(z)|=1/(1-|z|)^{3}
. In order that this latter condition to hold, the inequality in (
16
) must be satisfied at
z
. According to Theorem
A
,
h-e^{i\theta}g
is a rotation of the Koebe function. Therefore, by Theorem
2
,
f
is a rotation of the half-plane mapping
L
and hence,
h
is a rotation of the function
H
as in (
7
).

Regarding the sharpness of the lower bounds in (
8
) and (
9
), according to (
21
), we have that if the lower bound in (
8
) is attained by some
a\in{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{0\}
, then so is the lower bound in (
9
) for the same
a
. Hence, it only remains to analyze the case of equality in the lower bound of (
9
).

Assume then that for some
a\in{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{0\}
, the analytic part
h
of a function
f=h+\overline{g}\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
satisfies

|h^{\prime}(a)|=\frac{1}{(1+|a|)^{3}}\,.

In this case, equality must hold in (
20
). So that
\omega
is a rotation and we can write
\omega(z)=\lambda z
for some
|\lambda|=1
.

In this case, the analytic function
h-\nu g
, where
\nu=\overline{\lambda}\overline{a}/|a|
belongs to the class
\mathcal{S}
and satisfies that

|h^{\prime}(a)-\nu g^{\prime}(a)|=|h^{\prime}(a)||1-\nu\omega(a)|=\frac{1-|a|}{(1+|a|)^{3}}\,.

Therefore, by Theorem
A
,
h-\nu g
is a rotation of the Koebe function. By Theorem
2
,
f
is a rotation of the half-plane mapping
L
and hence,
h
is a rotation of the function
H
as in (
7
). This completes the proof of Theorem
1
.

\Box

Further remarks

A careful reader may have noticed some direct consequences of both Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
that we now list.

The first one is that not any function in the class
\mathcal{S}
is allowed to be the analytic part of a function
f=h+\overline{g}\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
, since the bounds for the growth and distortion for the function
h
given by Theorem
1
do not reach the values of the bounds for functions in the class
\mathcal{S}
provided by Theorem
A
. In particular, the Koebe function (
6
) cannot be the analytic part of a function
f\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
, a result that also follows from Theorem
E
.

More generally, the arguments used to prove Theorem
2
show that, indeed, if for some
a
in the unit disk a function
f=h+\overline{g}\in\mathcal{K}_{H}^{0}
satisfies that
h+ag
is a rotation of the Koebe function, then
|a|=1
and
f
is a rotation of the harmonic half-plane mapping.
