A machine learning assisted truncated adaptive EWMA chart for robust process monitoring
Authors: Abdullah A. Zaagan, Hassan M. Aljohani, Hamad Zogan, Muhammad Nabi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The classical memory-type control charts, Adaptive EWMA (AE) and Truncated Adaptive EWMA (TAE) are chart types whose analytical properties are traditionally derived under normality assumptions, although they can be applied to non-normal data and operate on truncating the impact of extreme values. However, truncation-based methods can possibly fail to respond appropriately to genuine changes in the process. The research is based on a new Hybrid Machine Learning-assisted Truncated Adaptive EWMA (ML-TAE) chart which involves the use of anomaly scores associated with the machine learning models that adjustively weight each new observation before it is added to the EWMA statistic. Monte Carlo method is usedto evaluate the suggested chart in range of scenarios of contamination and varying shifts. According to the given results, the ML-TAE chart shows a consistent better performance in the stability of Average Run Length (ARL) as it keeps the rates of false-alarm lower and allows detecting a moderate shift much faster than the classical models. The validity of the practical use of the proposed method is shown by a real life case study as well regarding contaminated process environment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33073-9.

Introduction

Outliers are data points that significantly deviate from the majority of observations, appearing unusually small or large relative to the rest of the data. While some outliers arise from explainable sources such as instrument malfunction, faulty measurements, or data entry errors and should be corrected or removed, others may represent meaningful information about abnormal system behavior, process instability, or impending failures1,2. Their detection and analysis are therefore critical for improving process reliability and ensuring timely intervention. Outliers are often identified through residuals that are unusually large in absolute value, typically exceeding four or more standard deviations from the mean. Control charts remain one of the most powerful and widely applied tools in Statistical Process Control (SPC). They find application both in manufacturing and service industries to observe process behavior, and to indicate loss of statistical control, thus making it possible to identify possible problems with quality early. Memory-type control charts, e.g. the exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) chart with Roberts3 and the cumulative sum (CUSUM) chart with Page4, is most effective due to its greater sensitivity to minor and intermediate shifts. Such changes, however minimal, can add up in the course of time to bring about an entire loss of finances or even a collapse in the course of the processes. This has reportedly led memory-type charts to be used in a variety of other fields such as healthcare, fisheries, image and signal processing and public health surveillance5–7. The presence of outliers, however, can severely degrade the performance of traditional control charts by inflating false-alarm rates. To address this challenge, researchers have proposed robust charting methods that employ resistant estimator of process location and scale, particularly when subgroup data are available8–12. Hawkins2, for instance, proposed a winsorized CUSUM chart to reduce the influence of extreme observations on monitoring performance. Nevertheless, there have been relatively lesser studies on strong charts to be used in individual observations even though they are pertinent in most contemporary applications. More recent research has further developed the robust process monitoring field with the development of control charts that work efficiently in the deviation of models, and in the presence of data contamination. To give an example13, developed an efficient and strong CUSUM chart of mean monitoring that has been applied in chemical process control, and14,15 came up with adaptive monitoring methods that do not deteriorate accuracy when there is uncertainty in the model assumptions16 also applied high breakdown point estimators to increase the stability of the process on contaminated healthcare data, which helps to understand the further demand of powerful statistical instruments in the real world. On the same note14,15, designed a nonparametric Hampel score-function-adaptive EWMA chart, which is useful in alleviating non-normality of manufacturing processes. The latter contributions provide emphasis on the growing interest in integrating strength and flexibility in control chart design, which is consistent with the driving force behind the current ML-TAE framework.

Adaptive control charts have been adapted to adjust automatically to changes in sensitivity in situations where the degree of change in the process is not known. The grounds of adaptive designs were laid by Lucas and Crosier17 who suggested that standardized observations above and below ± 4.0 may be viewed as possible outliers. Expanding on this concept, Capizzi and Masarotto18 suggested AE. chart, a dynamically-smoothed version that is sensitive to a variety of shift sizes. These concepts have been extended later, with adaptive CUSUM procedures suggested by19,20, and an adaptive-parameter-free EWMA (EAE) chart suggested by21, which is even more efficient at detection. The22 have recently created the truncated normal-based EWMA (TE) chart which is stronger as it employs truncated values rather than extreme ones to rationalize the run-length distribution. Nonetheless, preliminary experiments suggest, that charts, such as the EAE, are sensitive to contamination and the false-alarm level rises tremendously when there are outliers. This promotes development of more powerful strategies which are sensitive but not greatly influenced with radical values.

Over the past years, the use of machine learning (ML) in Statistical Process Control (SPC) has increasingly been addressed due to the ability of machine learning to describe nonlinear trends, detect nuanced variations, and deal with high-dimensional or noisy process data. Various ML algorithms have been integrated to improve in different areas of the performance of control charts. As an example, the Support Vector Regression (SVR) has been used to predict changes in means and dynamically adjust control limits23. Similarly, the group techniques like the Random Forests and Gradient Boosting have been applied in the classification of process states and enhanced fault-detection in the complex industrial systems. Autoencoders and Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) have demonstrated special potential in the study of nonlinear structures and the reconstruction of normal patterns of the processes, thus allowing detecting abnormal deviations at an early stage24. Moreover, the unsupervised models such as the Isolation Forest and the One-Class SVM have been found useful in anomaly detection and have provided adaptive measures to limit the effect of data contamination to monitoring performance. Even though these hybrid SPC-ML models have achieved significant reductions in false alarms and improvement in responsiveness to medium-speed process changes, the vast majority of them are relying on fixed thresholds, or offline training. This reduces their flexibility to change as conditions of the processes change with time. Also, though models using deep learning (e.g. Autoencoder) can be more informative, they are less interpretable and are often not reliable without using large datasets. Thus it is evident that there is a necessity to come up with a solution that will be able to combine strength against outliers, adaptive responsiveness and computational efficiency to online monitoring.

As a solution to these difficulties, the current study suggests a control chart of Machine Learning-Assisted Truncated Adaptive EWMA (ML-TAE). The approach uses scores of anomaly which are the results of the unsupervised ML algorithms to keep changing the weight attributed to new process observations. By this combination, the proposed chart achieves a good balance between stability and sensitivity that allows it to have a reliable detection performance when data is contaminated.Based on such developments the current study proposes a ML –TAE control chart. The chart incorporates ML obtained anomaly scores which continuously tune the weights of the observations before inclusion in the EWMA statistic. This approach aims to find an excellent compromise between robustness and responsiveness by minimizing the contribution of extreme changes and maintaining the ability to discern the true process changes. The efficiency of the offered ML-TAE chart is strictly checked by means of the Monte Carlo simulation studies and one practical example of the IDA, which implies the demonstration of its practical interest. Methodology of ML-TAE control chart

This section presents the formulation and operational structure of the proposed Machine Learning–Assisted Truncated Adaptive EWMA (ML-TAE) control chart. We propose a ML- TAE chart for monitoring the mean of a normal process in the presence of outliers. The method enhances the robustness of memory-type charts in contaminated environments by integrating anomaly-score weighting, adaptive truncation, and adaptive smoothing. The goal is to protect the monitoring statistic from the influence of extreme values while preserving sensitivity to genuine process shifts.Although this truncation method does offer certain defense against contamination, it can have two important shortcomings: (i) failing to respond to the actual process changes where the corresponding observations are truncated and (ii) responding to harmless fluctuations that do not reflect actual process change.

Conceptual motivation

Classical types of EWMA charts are usually formulated under normality when obtaining control limits, although they do not assume normal data, hence, are very sensitive to extreme outlier values. As contamination develops, a single outlier will inflate the EWMA statistic at a high rate leading to excessive false alarms. The classic truncation-based methods like the TAE chart22 and Haq, 2024 constrain the size of the extreme values, but might erroneously smother the process changes in case the truncation is overly fierce. The ML-TAE model overcomes these weaknesses through the application of machine-learned anomaly scores, which are used to determine the validity of every incoming observation. The weighting of each new value based on the probability of it being an outlier is used in place of magnitude based truncation. The resulting monitoring statistic obtained through this soft weighting, then adaptive truncation and adaptive smoothing is robustly and responsively sensitive. Simulation design and validation

The effectiveness of the proposed ML-TAE chart was assessed through Monte Carlo simulations. The control limit, h = 0.41730, was chosen to achieve an in-control ARL₀ of roughly 500 under nominal conditions, based on preliminary trials. For every combination of process mean shift (δ) and contamination proportion (P), 10,000 independent simulation runs were performed to ensure reliable estimates. The stability of the ARL results was confirmed through convergence checks, and fixed random seeds were applied to allow reproducibility. Out-of-control ARLs (ARL₁) were calculated for δ values ranging from 0.1 to 6.0 across different contamination levels, and the corresponding standard deviations of the run lengths (SDRLs) were computed to measure variability.The anomaly-score component of the ML-TAE chart was generated using an unsupervised Isolation Forest model. The anomaly score used in our study was the Isolation Forest anomaly score generated by the isotree package (Liu et al., 2008). This score ranges between 0 and 1, with higher values indicating more anomalous observations. It was chosen because Isolation Forest is widely recognized for its ability to isolate outliers efficiently in high-noise environments, and its score integrates naturally with the weighting framework of the ML-TAE chart. The model was trained using the Phase-I in-control standardized data. Our nominal 5% false-alarm threshold of 100 trees, sub-sampling of 256 observations per tree, and a contamination parameter range of [0.01 0.05] was used to arrive at a nominal 5% false-alarm.The anomaly level was adopted as the 95 th percentile of the in-control score. All anomaly scores were converted to probabilities through an empirical distribution estimated from Phase-I data. The probability was then transformed into a time-varying weight that directly affected the EWMA update. After defining the ML-TAE statistic, ARL and SDRL values were computed using Monte Carlo simulation over 10,000 independent replications until a signal occurred.All simulated observations were generated sequentially using the following rules:

Phase-I (training data): Drawn from a standard normal distribution representing an in-control process without contamination.

Phase-II (monitoring data): i.Regular observations followed a normal distribution with the same standard deviation but with a shifted mean to represent the specified out-of-control condition.ii.With a small predefined probability, an observation was replaced by an outlier. Outliers were defined as extreme values generated uniformly from a specified interval (“outlier range”), representing large unexpected deviations that contaminate the process.The outlier probability P specifies the chance that any individual observation is contaminated. In our simulations, for instance, if we set P=0.1, meaning that on average, 10% of observations are outliers. Because the monitoring sequence stops once the chart signals, the actual number of outliers per run is random and may differ from 10%.

An in-control ARL of 500 (false-alarm rate ≈ 0.002) was chosen because this level is widely used in recent robust and adaptive SPC studies10. These studies demonstrate that higher in-control ARL values are especially appropriate in contaminated or heavy-tailed environments, where conventional EWMA or CUSUM charts may generate excessive false alarms. First, for small and moderate shifts (δ ≤ 2.0), both charts behave almost identically. The ARL values in Tables 3, 4 and 5 show only minor differences between ML-TAE and TAE, and the curves in Fig. 1 also follow a very similar trajectory. This reveals that the anomaly-based weighting in ML-TAE does not impair its ability to track gradual departures from the target mean. Second, for larger shifts (δ ≥ 2.5), the classical TAE chart clearly detects shifts more quickly. Across all contamination levels, the ARL values for TAE are consistently smaller than those of ML-TAE, as shown in Table 5. This behaviour is also visible in Fig. 1 where the TAE curves drop more rapidly as the shift magnitude increases. This difference arises because ML-TAE down-weights extreme observations, some of which correspond to genuine process changes when the shift is large. As a result, ML-TAE becomes more conservative while the classical TAE chart responds more promptly. Third, despite being slightly slower for detecting large shifts, the ML-TAE chart demonstrates stronger robustness when contamination is present. As the proportion of outliers increases, the ARL patterns in Tables 5 show that ML-TAE maintains more stable performance compared to TAE. This stability is also visible in Fig. 1 where the ML-TAE curves fluctuate less across different contamination levels. This confirms that the weighting mechanism effectively mitigates the influence of isolated extreme values.

In summary, Fig. 1 and Table 5 collectively show that ML-TAE and TAE perform similarly for small and moderate shifts, while the classical TAE chart is more efficient for detecting larger shifts. The ML-TAE chart, however, provides enhanced robustness under contamination and produces more consistent ARL behaviour when data irregularities are present. Thus, ML-TAE is preferable in applications where data contamination is expected, whereas TAE remains advantageous when rapid detection of substantial shifts is the primary objective.

Application

To demonstrate the practical performance of the proposed ML-TAE control chart, we applied it to the real dataset originally analyzed by9. The Phase I dataset (provided in the Table A1 of Appendix A) was first standardized to follow a standard normal distribution to eliminate location and scale effects. Using this standardized data, Phase I estimates of the process mean and dispersion were obtained, and the corresponding control limits were constructed for the proposed chart.

Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 display the Phase II behavior of the proposed chart under different process conditions. Fig. 3 and 4 show the chart’s performance under a small mean shift of δ = 0.25 at contamination probabilities of P = 0.05 and P = 0.10, respectively. Conclusion

This study introduces the ML-TAE control chart for the normal data, a robust and adaptive monitoring tool that combines the strengths of truncated EWMA charts and machine learning–based anomaly detection. By effectively incorporating anomaly scores as adaptive weights, the proposed chart minimizes the impact of outliers and maintains sensitivity to meaningful process shifts. The results of simulations confirmed that the ML-TAE chart outperforms the classical AE and TAE charts. Offering superior ARL properties for a wide range of shift magnitudes and contamination levels, the real-data application further demonstrated the practical relevance and effectiveness of the approach within real-world industrial contexts.

Extensions may be developed based on the proposed approach in the following directions: multivariate process monitoring, online learning for dynamic anomaly detection, or applications in high-dimensional data streams. Such developments hold further potential to strengthen the role of data-driven quality control within modern smart manufacturing systems. The computational cost of the proposed ML-TAE chart is moderate. In general, both anomaly detection models, such as Isolation Forest or Autoencoder, are trained only once in Phase I data and are updated in real time using lightweight scoring operations. Thus, the approach is feasible for online process monitoring or for implementation in an industrial IoT environment. In future studies, the framework can also be generalized to a multivariate or non-Gaussian setting by incorporating multivariate anomaly detectors or robust nonparametric transformations, widening its applicability to complex manufacturing and service processes.
