Mid-term outcomes of modified colonna capsular arthroplasty in adolescent and young adult patient with unilateral hip dysplasia dislocation
Authors: Chuan Li, Xianghong Zhang, Hongxuan Chen, Ziyi Wu, Fuqiang Gao, Cheng Huang, Tang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHip preservation remains a significant surgical challenge in adolescent and young adult patient with unilateral developmental dysplasia of hip (DDH). We compared the clinical and radiographic results of modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty in unilateral DDH patients.MethodsThe records of 19 consecutive patients (19 hips) who underwent modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty between 2017 and 2019 were retrospectively reviewed. There were 3 males and 16 females, with a mean age of 19.1 years at the time of surgery. We used the modified Harris Hip Score (mHHS), Visual analogue scale (VAS) scores and limb-length discrepancy (LLD) to evaluate the clinical outcomes. X-ray was used to measure the femoral anteversion angle, lateral center-edge angle and acetabular coverage.ResultsThe median follow-up time was 70.7 months. The average operative time and intraoperative blood loss were 80.5 ± 5.0 (range 75 to 95) minutes and 189.7 ± 15.3 (range 165 to 210) mL, respectively. The average mHHS improved from 58.6 ± 3.6 (range 50.5 to 62.5) preoperatively to 86.9 ± 2.1 (range 82.5 to 90.5) at the final follow-up (p < 0.05). The mean femoral anteversion angle had decreased from 40.8° ± 2.4° (range 36.5° to 44.6°) preoperatively to 15.2° ± 1.8° (range 12.2° to 18.8°) at the final follow-up with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.05). The average LLD decreased significantly from 28.3 ± 4.6 mm preoperatively to 10.8 ± 2.4 mm at the final follow-up, respectively. One patient was converted to total hip arthroplasty at 6 years postoperatively. However, there was no significant difference in VAS between preoperative and the final follow-up. According to the curative effect evaluation standard score of DDH, the excellent and good rate was 73.7%. Furthermore, the lateral center-edge angle and the acetabular coverage showed significant improvement in all hips after operation.ConclusionsThe modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty demonstrates favorable mid-term outcomes for adolescent and young adult patients with unilateral DDH. This procedure effectively improves hip function and radiographic parameters. Nevertheless, further studies with longer follow-up are need to determine the long-term efficacy.Keywords: Developmental dysplasia of the hip, Modified colonna capsular arthroplasty, Unilateral dislocation, Hip preservation

Background

Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) refers to a spectrum of congenital and developmental disorders, characterized by structural abnormalities in hip joint development that result in spatial and temporal instability of the hip [1]. As one of the most prevalent hip disorders in pediatric and adolescent populations, DDH presents with femoral head displacement, acetabular malformation, and reduced congruence between the femoral head and acetabulum during growth, potentially affecting long-term hip function [1–3]. DDH is also considered to be a major contributor to early-onset hip osteoarthritis in young populations [3]. The pathogenesis of DDH involves congenital acetabular dysplasia, resulting in insufficient femoral head coverage and impaired femoral-acetabular matching. These structural deficiencies lead to persistent biomechanical abnormalities, which in turn induce secondary femoral developmental malformations. The progressive femoral deformities further aggravate acetabular deformities, forming a vicious circle of joint degeneration [4, 5]. The progression of DDH may result in femoral head subluxation or complete dislocation over time. If left untreated, DDH can progress to severe hip osteoarthritis and significantly affect pelvic and spinal alignment in both the sagittal and coronal planes [5, 6].

Currently, the annual incidence of DDH in China ranges from 0.1% to 0.3%, and early intervention is key to optimizing treatment outcomes [7–9]. For infants younger than 6 months, conservative management typically yields satisfactory outcomes. However, in patients older than 8 years, hip-preserving surgery is primarily intended to delay the eventual need for joint replacement rather than provide a definitive solution [4, 7]. The primary goal of DDH treatment is to achieve anatomical restoration of the hip through concentric reduction of the femoral head within the acetabulum, and to address acetabular and femoral abnormalities to maintain optimal femoral-acetabular congruence. This comprehensive approach aims to prevent long-term complications, including scoliosis and avascular necrosis of the femoral head in adolescents [10, 11]. Capsular arthroplasty, first proposed by Codivilla and later named by Colonna, is a hip preservation surgery for DDH treatment [10, 12]. The literature review indicated that patient’s outcomes following Colonna capsular arthroplasty vary across different studies. For example, Chung et al. followed a cohort of patients treated with Colonna capsular arthroplasty and reported the results of a 17-year follow-up, which showed that the excellent and good rate of the Harris Hip Score was over 50% [13]. However, postoperative complications such as femoral head necrosis and joint stiffness have significantly affected patients’ satisfaction [14]. These complications are mainly attributed to the insufficient preservation of femoral head blood supply and inadequate correction of femoral deformities during surgery [14].

Long-term femoral head dislocation results in insufficient stress stimulation to the acetabulum, leading to the characteristic ‘triangular acetabulum’ deformity and subsequent femoral acetabular mismatch [3, 8, 15]. Furthermore, traditional surgical approaches, including acetabular arthroplasty and periacetabular osteotomy, often fail to achieve satisfactory concentric reduction [2, 11]. In 2012, Ganz et al. based on the anatomy of the blood supply of the femoral head, proposed the modified Codivilla-Hey Groves-Colonna capsular arthroplasty (referred to as modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty for short), a one-stage surgery consists of surgical hip dislocation and capsular arthroplasty [10]. Against this backdrop, the modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty has emerged as a feasible palliative surgical intervention for the management of unilateral DDH in adolescent and young adult patients. Initial clinical evidence from Ganz et al. demonstrated that favorable outcomes could be achieved with the modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty [16]. However, their study was constrained by both a small sample size and a short follow-up duration.

This study retrospectively analyzed adolescent and young adult patients with unilateral DDH who underwent modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty in our hospital since 2017, and observed postoperative clinical and imaging indicators. The purposes of this study include: (1) to analyze the long-term clinical efficacy and safety of modified Colonna arthroplasty in the treatment of adolescent and young adult patients with unilateral DDH; (2) to establish the optimal indications and application criteria for this surgical technique.

Patients and methods

Patients

A retrospective study was conducted on patients who underwent modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty between January 1, 2017 and December 31, 2019. This study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Second Xiangya hospital, Central south university, and written informed consent was obtained from the participants or their parents/legal guardians of those under 16 years of age (LYEC2024-0296). The Tönnis classification system was employed to assess the dysplastic hip. For this study, the inclusion criteria were defined as follows: (1) a confirmed diagnosis of unilateral DDH with complete hip dislocation; (2) an age range of 10 to 26 years; (3) preserved femoral head morphology, intact articular cartilage, and maintained joint capsule integrity (with a minimum thickness of > 1.5 mm); (4) sufficient acetabular bone stock to accommodate the femoral head without causing impingement. Additionally, the exclusion criteria included: (Ⅰ) a history of previous ipsilateral hip surgery or concurrent neuromuscular disorders; (Ⅱ) active systemic infection or localized hip infection; (Ⅲ) significant spinal scoliosis, or lower extremity deformities that affect gait; (Ⅳ) incomplete medical records; (Ⅴ) a follow-up duration of less than 3 months. Ultimately, this retrospective study enrolled 19 patients (19 hips) who met the inclusion criteria, with no patient lost to follow-up. Surgical technique

Surgical hip dislocation

Following successful induction of general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the standard lateral decubitus position. After adequate preparation of the surgical site and draping, a longitudinal incision of approximately 13 cm was made along the axis of the femoral shaft, with the greater trochanter apex as the center. This surgical approach allows for adequate exposure of the insertion sites of the vastus lateralis and gluteus medius muscles. Greater trochanter osteotomy was performed with preservation of the tendinous attachments, and the osteotomy thickness was maintained between 1.0 and 1.5 cm. After complete exposure of the joint capsule and its acetabular attachments, a longitudinal capsulotomy was made along the anterior surface of the capsule. The joint capsule was then meticulously dissected from its acetabular attachments, subsequent controlled hip flexion and external rotation maneuvers allowed visualization of both the acetabular floor and the true acetabulum [17, 18].

Handing acetabulum and femur

To optimize acetabular exposure, hypertrophic soft tissues within the acetabular cavity were meticulously debrided. Guided by preoperative radiographic measurements, sequential acetabular reaming was performed until the optimal fit with the femoral head size was achieved [19]. The articular capsule was first released from its peripheral attachments, then carefully retracted and secured around the femoral head. Special care was taken to avoid placing sutures in the weight-bearing zone of the femoral head, preserving its structural integrity [5, 10, 16]. Following capsular reconstruction, the hip was then reduced and its stability was assessed through a full range of motion. Proximal femoral osteotomy, incorporating both shortening and derotation components, was then performed according to the preoperative surgical plan, with intraoperative adjustments made based on the achieved hip reduction and stability assessment [20]. In this study, we performed femoral osteotomy at the subtrochanteric level. Furthermore, the shortening amount was determined by measuring the bone overlap distance at the femoral osteotomy, with the femoral head reduced and the knee in full extension, and the maximum shortening achieved was 3 cm. Subsequently, either a six-hole straight locking plate (Changzhou Nanxiang Medical Device Co., Ltd., China) or a pediatric hip plate (Changzhou Nanxiang Medical Device Co., Ltd., China) was routinely used for fixation. A C-arm system was routinely utilized for fluoroscopy evaluation during the operation, with pelvic anteroposterior and femoral axial images acquired to assess femoral-acetabular containment [21]. If the femoral head was found to have insufficiently coverage intraoperatively, the acetabular floor needed to be invaginated based on the required coverage [22]. Following fixation of the greater trochanteric, the surgical wound was closed in layers using antibacterial Vicryl Plus suture (Johnson, USA). Postoperatively, immobilization was achieved with a bilateral lower limb plaster cast, which maintained the affected hip in 30° of abduction and neutral rotation.

Postoperative management

No drainage tube was routinely used for the surgical incision, and intermittent ice compression was applied to the incision for the initial 12 postoperative hours. Antimicrobial prophylaxis with a second-generation cephalosporin was administered intravenously, initiated at least 30 min before skin incision and continued routinely provided with 24 h postoperatively. Mechanical prophylaxis against venous thromboembolism was routinely administrated with the assistance of rehabilitation therapists. The hip spica cast was maintained for 6 weeks. After cast removal, a structured rehabilitation program was initiated under the guidance of physical therapists, focusing on gluteus medius strengthening and lower extremity muscle re-education. Partial weight-bearing ambulation with crutches was implemented from 3 to 6 months postoperatively, followed by progressive weight-bearing as tolerated, based on radiographic and clinical evaluations.

Clinical and radiographic assessment

All patients were evaluated clinically and radiographically every month within 3 months after the operation, 6 months and 1 year after operation, and then annually during the follow-up period. Clinical assessment was focused on patients’ demographics, previous management, operative duration, and intraoperative blood loss. We applied the visual analogue scale (VAS) and the modified Harris Hip Score (mHHS) for the clinical and functional assessment, with results further graded based on mHHS scores [23, 24]. Furthermore, hip function was assessed using the curative effect evaluation standard score of DDH [25]. Radiographically, standing anteroposterior radiograph, 45° oblique radiographs, full-length radiograph of both lower limbs, computerized tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of hip were usually applied. We measured and calculated the lateral center-edge angle, acetabular coverage, femoral anteversion, and lower limbs length discrepancy. To ensure objective assessment and minimize interpretation bias, two independent authors retrospectively and blindly reviewed the radiological outcomes. Any discrepancies in their evaluations were resolved through discussion and consensus with two additional senior surgeons.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 19.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was applied to evaluate whether the data followed a normal distribution. The non-normal distribution data were described as medians (IQR, Q1-Q3), while normal distribution data were described as mean ± standard deviation. The enumeration data were described as count and rate or percent. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test or t-test was used to analyze difference between preoperative measurements and the last follow-up values of parameters. We consider it statistically significant when the p value was less than 0.05 for all tests.

Results

All patients in this cohort were followed up, with a median followed-up duration of 70.7 months (range 60 to 82 months). The mean operative duration and intraoperative blood loss were 80.5 min (range 75 to 95 min) and 189.7 mL (range 165 to 210 mL), respectively. Femoral shortening of 2 to 2.5 cm was done. At the final follow-up, no surgical site infections, neurological complications, deep vein thrombosis, or osteotomy nonunion were observed. One patient (5.3%) developed transient hip stiffness at 2 months postoperatively, which resolved completely after a 2-month structured rehabilitation program. One patient developed avascular necrosis of femoral head at 2 years postoperatively and underwent conservative treatment with regular follow-up. Additionally, 1 patient (5.3%) required conversion to total hip arthroplasty (THA) at 6 years postoperatively due to progressive osteoarthritis of the affected hip. All patients exhibited significant clinical improvement, as demonstrated by representative cases (Fig. 1 and Fig. 2). The mHHS was substantial enhanced, increasing from a preoperative mean of 58.6 points (range 50.5 to 62.5 points) to 86.9 points (range 82.5 to 90.5 points) at the final follow-up (p < 0.05). Based on the mHHS classification, outcomes were excellent in 1 hip (5.3%) and good in 18 hips (94.7%). The mean limb-length discrepancy (LLD) decreased significantly from 28.3 mm (range 20 to 35 mm) preoperatively to 10.8 mm (range 6 to 15 mm) at the final follow-up (p < 0.05). The mean femoral anteversion angle decreased from 40.8° (range 36.5° to 44.6°) preoperatively to 15.2° (range 12.2° to 18.8°) at the final follow-up with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.05). The mean postoperative lateral center-edge angle and acetabular coverage rate were 30.2° (range 26.8° to 36.3°) and 76% (range 70% to 81%), respectively. Based on the curative effect evaluation standard score of DDH [25], the mean score at the final follow-up was 21.7 points (range 13.7 to 26.5 points), including 1 excellent case (5.3%), 13 good cases (68.4%), 3 fair cases (15.8%), and 2 poor cases (10.5%). The overall success rate (excellent and good rate) was 73.7% (14 out of 19 cases).

Discussion

While DDH typically responds well to treatment in infant and young children, achieving satisfactory outcomes has become increasingly challenging in patients over 8 years of age and adolescents under 25 years old [1–3]. In these older patient groups, the management of hip dislocation still poses significant clinical challenges. Since its introduction first proposed by Codivilla and later named by Colonna in 1932, capsular arthroplasty has been used to treat complete hip dislocation in children age 3 to 8 years and has demonstrated favorable early efficacy [16]. The capsular arthroplasty involves anatomically repositioning the femoral head into the true acetabulum. This surgery is achieved by wrapping the femoral head with the joint capsule, promoting adhesion and fibrocartilage formation between the capsule and the acetabular cancellous bone, and ultimately forming a neoarticular space between the femoral head and the capsular tissue [26]. However, the traditional capsular arthroplasty (usually named Colonna capsular arthroplasty) is associated with a relatively high complication rate, including early avascular necrosis of the femoral head, joint stiffness, re-dislocation, and others [10, 16]. These complications may arise from an inadequate understanding of the femoral head’s vascular anatomy, failure to correct excessive femoral anteversion intraoperatively, and prolonged postoperative immobilization of the hip in abduction and internal rotation using a hip spica cast, factors that can elevate intra-articular pressure and impair femoral head perfusion. Recent advancements in DDH screening, particularly the widespread adoption of ultrasound diagnostic, have significantly reduced the incidence of delayed hip dislocation [27]. Concurrently, improvements in THA techniques, including enhanced implant durability and expanded indication, have increased the use of THA in younger patient populations [28]. These developments mentioned above have gradually reduced the use of traditional capsular arthroplasty.

Building on an in-depth understanding of the femoral head’s vascular anatomy, Ganz and his colleagues pioneered a modified approach of the traditional capsular arthroplasty, that named as the modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty [16]. This innovative technique integrates surgical hip dislocation to optimize joint exposure and enable precise correction of femoral deformities, guided by detailed preoperative imaging analysis. In their seminal 2012 study, Ganz et al. reported the outcomes of this modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty in 9 adolescent patients with unilateral hip dislocation. The study demonstrated a mean mHHS of 84 point at a mean follow-up of 7.5 years, indicating clinically satisfactory results [16]. Subsequently, Professor Zhang’s team successfully applied this modified technique for older pediatrics patients and adolescents in China, achieving comparable favorable outcomes [10]. Luo et al. conducted a clinical study involving 25 adolescent patients with DDH who underwent the modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty, reporting satisfactory hip reduction and joint function in all cases at a mean follow-up of 13.4 months [29]. These findings collectively suggest that the Ganz-modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty represents a viable treatment option for unilateral hip dislocation in older pediatric and adolescent populations. However, the current literature indicates that there were few clinical studies evaluating the application of modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty in adolescent patients with DDH, and its long-term clinical outcomes remain uncertain, which require further investigation through larger-scale studies and longer follow-up periods.

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed 19 adolescent and young adult patients with unilateral DDH who underwent modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty. The study specifically focused on unilateral DDH cases, as they have a more pronounced impact on daily function compared to bilateral cases. The median follow-up duration was 70.7 ± 6.6 months (from 60 to 84 months), and the mHHS at the final follow-up was 86.9 ± 2.1 pointes (from 82.5 to 90.5), which was significantly improved compared to preoperative scores (p < 0.05). According to the congenital hip dislocation evaluation criteria, the excellent and good rate was 73.7% (14 of 19 cases). Additionally, radiographic parameters showed significant improvement, with substantial correction of femoral anteversion and limb-length discrepancy, and outcomes comparable to those reported by Ganz et al. [16]. Our findings confirmed that modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty effectively preserves the femoral head blood supply, restores the normal femoral anatomy through precise osteotomy, and maintains both hip function and joint stability.

Although early diagnosis and staged treatment have improved prognosis, DDH screening practices remain inconsistent across different regions of China. Disparities in healthcare resources have also contributed to a high incidence of DDH in older children and adolescents [30]. Patients with unilateral DDH and associated LLD are particularity prone to developing secondary complications, including scoliosis, chronic low back pain, and progressive hip osteoarthritis, often requiring eventual conversion to THA [28, 31]. However, the THA in adolescents presents significant challenges, as their higher activity levels accelerate prosthetic wear, reducing implant longevity [28]. Consequently, these young patients frequently face the prospect of multiple revision surgeries throughout their lifetime. Each subsequent revision procedure increases surgical complexity and is associated with higher risks of prosthetic dislocation, aseptic loosening, and periprosthetic joint infection. Therefore, hip-preserving surgery is essential for delaying or avoiding THA in adolescent and young adult patients with unilateral DDH.

Our results demonstrated that the modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty is a feasible hip-preserving strategy for DDH patients with completed hip dislocation. However, careful patient selection is crucial, as this surgical approach is not universally applicable to all DDH cases. Comprehensive preoperative assessment, including detailed physical examination and advanced imaging studies, is essential for surgical planning and patient stratification. MRI plays a pivotal role in evaluating the femoral head morphology and assessing joint capsule integrity. The key selection criteria include: (1) preserved femoral head sphericity without significant flattening or extensive cartilage loss; (2) intact articular cartilage with minimal degenerative changes; and (3) a robust joint capsule with thickness exceeding 1.5 mm to ensure complete femoral head coverage. These anatomical prerequisites are critical for the formation of a new joint space between the femoral head and the joint capsule postoperatively. Furthermore, excessive femoral anteversion is a key pathological anatomical factor leading to hip instability. Therefore, it is necessary to perform a comprehensive CT-based three-dimensional limb alignment analysis preoperatively to determine the optimal derotation angle for surgical correction [32]. Surgical indications for derotation osteotomy typically include femoral anteversion exceeding 35°, with the correction target guided by both the contralateral femoral anteversion angle and intraoperative reduction stability. The recommended postoperative femoral anteversion should be maintained below 20° to ensure optimal hip biomechanics. Preoperative planning must also include detailed assessment of bilateral femoral neck-shaft angles and lower limb length measurements, which serve as critical parameters for intraoperative adjustments of varus correction and femoral shortening osteotomy.

Proficiency in surgical hip dislocation techniques is essential for surgeons performing the modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty, as it enables optimal preservation of femoral head vascularity and minimizes the risk of avascular necrosis. Intraoperatively, a 2.0 mm Kirschner wire was utilized to mark the planned derotation angle prior to femoral osteotomy, and the acetabular preparation requires careful selection of reamer size based on femoral head dimensions. Typically, the smallest reamer should be 2 mm smaller than the femoral head diameter to clear the hyperplastic soft tissue in the acetabulum and expose the acetabulum horseshoe fossa. The final reamer size should accommodate both the femoral head diameter and twice the measured capsular thickness to ensure proper joint reconstruction [10]. Intraoperative adjustments of derotation angle, varus correction, femoral shortening, and acetabular preparation should be guided by dynamic assessment of hip stability following reduction. Early postoperative immobilization with an abduction neural plaster helps maintain hip stability and allows time for capsule adhesion to the acetabulum. In our series, two cases developed postoperative hip stiffness due to early weight-bearing and poor joint capsule quality after operation. These findings underscore the regular follow-up and individualized postoperative rehabilitation exercise program are essential, and the rehabilitation should be conducted under the guidance and supervision of surgeons.

Claudication and hip pain often lead to significant impairment and disability. Number of patients with DDH experience these two symptoms for an extended period prior to intervention. Moreover, the relationship between pain, insomnia, and anxiety has emerged as a key focus in limb pain research. In addition, claudication and hip pain impact DDH patients’ work, educational potential, and extensive social interactions; for some, this impact even further disrupted key developmental milestones, including missing university, being excluded from typical young-person social activities, and struggling to form peer bonds. Although Colonna arthroplasty requires prolonged rehabilitation and hospitalization, which easily result in extended school absence, findings from this study indicate that patients can walk with crutches and return to school 3 months after surgery. Furthermore, their claudication and hip pain are well corrected following the procedure. In China, scheduling Colonna arthroplasty surgery during winter or summer vacations can further reduce the duration of patients’ home-based school absence. However, the specific impact of modified Colonna arthroplasty on the social and psychological issues of DDH patients still requires further in-depth investigation.

There are some limitations in this study. First, as a retrospective analysis with a relatively small sample size and no control group, the findings may be subject to selection bias and have limited generalizability. Second, the impact of periarticular soft tissue integrity and muscular balance on postoperative outcomes was not systematically evaluated. Third, the determination of optimal femoral derotation angles remains largely empirical, lacking standardized objective criteria. Fourth, we did not incorporate systematic psychological assessments or patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) targeting quality of life, social participation, and psychological well-being into the study design. This omission means we cannot comprehensively quantify the socio-psychological burden imposed by the surgery or evaluate changes in patients’ mental health status before and after treatment. Finally, the absence of uniform rehabilitation protocols in earlier cases may have contributed to variable functional outcomes and potentially higher complication rates.

Conclusion

The modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty represents an effective surgical intervention for adolescent and young adult patients with unilateral DDH, yielding favorable function and radiographic outcomes. Nevertheless, this technically demanding procedure requires advanced surgical expertise, comprehensive preoperative planning, and meticulous intraoperative execution to optimize outcomes and minimize complications. When appropriately indicated and performed, the modified Colonna capsular arthroplasty serves as a valuable hip-preservation strategy, offering adolescent and young adult DDH patients the potential to maintain native joint function and delay or avoid the need for THA.
