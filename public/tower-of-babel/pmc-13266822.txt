Application and Evaluation of a NOAA GFS-Driven Air Quality Model Using CMAQv5.4 and High-Resolution Emissions: FIREX-AQ 2019
Authors: Kiarash Farzad, Yang Zhang, Daniel Tong, Siqi Ma, Youhua Tang, Jianping Huang, Patrick C. Campbell, Havala O. T. Pye, Benjamin Murphy, Fanglin Yang, Barry Baker, Daiwen Kang, Bill Hutzell, Golam Sarwar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The National Air Quality Forecast Capability (NAQFC) provides numerical forecasting guidance of air quality up to 72 hr ahead over the United States. In this study, we evaluate the changes in its prediction skills by updating the Community Multiscale Air Quality (CMAQ) modeling system from version 5.3.1 to 5.4 and utilizing a high-resolution 1-km anthropogenic emission data set. The baseline and several sensitivity simulations with emission and science updates in CMAQv5.4 were conducted and evaluated. The results show improved prediction performance among model configurations over the contiguous United States (CONUS) in terms of correlation coefficient, normalized mean bias (NMB), and normalized mean error for fine particulate matter (PM2.5) with NMBs changing from −12.1% to minimum of −6.4% and maximum daily 8 hr average O3 (MDA8 O3) with an NMB changing from 4.6% to best of 0.6%. Evaluation over CONUS and along the FIREX-AQ field campaign flight paths suggests that current NAQFC predictions can be improved by using high-resolution anthropogenic emissions, adjustments to wildfire emissions, and newer versions of CMAQ. This work facilitates future developments of NAQFC by providing detailed information on the factors that contribute to model biases.

Introduction

Air pollution is a costly issue that leads to serious health problems and ranks as the third leading cause of death among health risk factors (Azimi & Rahman, 2024). Individual air pollutants pose a threat to our health through various mechanisms. For instance, fine particulate matter (PM2.5) is associated with cardiovascular diseases (Hayes et al., 2020), and ozone exacerbates asthma (Kim et al., 2020). Efforts have been made to reduce air pollution by establishing attainment goals and air quality standards through various agencies worldwide, including the United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and the World Health Organization (WHO). During extreme events, special precautions are typically recommended for sensitive populations, and these efforts rely on accurate predictions. Hence, up-to-date models with improved accuracy in air quality forecasting are critical for improving human health and reducing associated costs.

Air quality predictions are subject to errors in inputs, configurations, and representations of air quality models, such as anthropogenic and natural emissions, as well as the representation of transport, chemical transformation, deposition, and other physical processes. Air quality models have been deployed for real-time air quality forecasting (RT-AQF) since the 1990s (X. Zhang et al., 2012; Y. Zhang et al., 2012). As described by X. Zhang et al. (2012) and Y. Zhang et al. (2012), the first- and second-generation models were mainly based on statistical models. The third and fourth-generation models were 3-D numerical models that incorporated atmospheric transport, physics, and chemistry, utilizing a deterministic approach. In a joint effort between the EPA and the U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) (Kang et al., 2005), the initial National Air Quality Forecast Capability (NAQFC) was developed as the national air quality forecast guidance in response to the Congressional direction (H.R. Energy Policy Act of 2002 Senate Amendment S. 517, SA1383, Forecasts and Warnings).

While advancements in air quality forecasting are important progress, continuous improvement requires high-quality observational data to better evaluate and refine models. Field campaigns provide detailed chemical and meteorological data that are not available from typical ground-based monitoring networks, thus enabling a better understanding of the drivers of air pollution and related extreme events (e.g., wildfires). By providing information for a broader suite of chemical species and environmental conditions, field campaigns are ideally suited to support the development, evaluation, and improvement of 3-D air quality models. The 2019 Fire Influence on Regional to Global Environments and Air Quality (FIREX-AQ 2019) study (Warneke et al., 2023) provided data for wildland and prescribed fires across the U.S. and has been used by the NAQFC and the Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) model coupled with the Community Multiscale Air Quality (CMAQ) modeling system (WRF-CMAQ) to assess the impact of different meteorological drivers of the models on predictions during the campaign (R. Kumar et al., 2021; Y. Tang et al., 2022). In parallel, Wiggins et al. (2021) utilized FIREX-AQ data to evaluate two emission estimation methods, that is, bottom-up and top-down. Multiple studies have introduced parameterizations of U.S. wildfire and prescribed fire emission ratios and emission factors (Fiddler et al., 2024; Gkatzelis et al., 2024; Travis et al., 2023; Wiggins et al., 2021). Pye et al. (2024) used FIREX-AQ to constrain the abundance of reactive organic carbon emissions in western US wildfires. GEOS-Chem was used to analyze and better understand the emissions of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) from wildfires during FIREX-AQ (Jin et al., 2023; Liao et al., 2019).

Complementing these emission and air quality studies, researchers have also examined fire behavior and plume dynamics. WRF-Fire was used to compare heat output with satellite observations, outperforming a persistence forecast in predicting daily burned area and fire radiative power (Turney et al., 2023). WRF coupled with the Spread FIRE model (WRF-SFIRE) was used to simulate plume dynamics, confirming that the coupled system accurately captures plume evolution (Liu et al., 2019). Ye et al. (2021) demonstrated that improving emission temporal resolution and lowering plume-injection heights led to better performance in 12 forecast models during the Williams Flats fire, highlighting that these factors are critical for accurate wildfire pollutant predictions (Ye et al., 2021).

Kumar et al. (2020, 2022) and Pouyaei et al. (2023) showed that aerosol concentrations and optical properties simulated by WRF coupled with Chemistry (WRF-Chem) align well with ground-based and airborne measurements, validating its use for wildfire smoke assessments (A. Kumar et al., 2020, 2022; Pouyaei et al., 2023). Other models used for this purpose include the Global Environmental Multiscale—Modeling Air-quality and CHemistry (GEM-MACH) (Makar et al., 2021). The Goddard Earth Observing System Composition Forecast (GEOS-CF) by Johnson et al. (2021) was used to assess the impact of fire over western Canada (Johnson et al., 2021), and attempts have been made to understand the chemical mechanism that differentiates wildfire from non-wildfire organic matter formation (June et al., 2022). FIREX-AQ campaign is also paving the way for the new generation of air quality modeling systems, including NOAA’s Rapid-Refresh Forecasting System (RRFS) (Ahmadov et al., 2022; Romero Alvarez et al., 2022), and Multi-Scale Infrastructure for Chemistry and Aerosol version 0 (MUSICAv0) (W. Tang et al., 2023).

The objective of this paper is to inform the development of NOAA’s operational Unified Forecast System-Air Quality Model (UFS-AQM) through the systematic testing and evaluation of various configurations within the offline-coupled NAQFC system. This work contributes a unique analysis by leveraging two key advancements not seen in prior applications or in other models reviewed in the literature: we utilize an upgraded version of the chemistry driver and integrate high-resolution (1-km) anthropogenic emissions.

Eleven sensitivity simulations utilizing different anthropogenic/biogenic emission sets with CMAQ versions 5.3.1 and 5.4 were performed to target significant emission changes and model science upgrades. Through operational, diagnostic, and mechanistic evaluation of the NAQFC, this work provides useful information to NOAA’s forecasting systems, highlighting issues for further investigation and potential improvements.

Model System and Evaluation Protocols

Model Description

The meteorological inputs used in this work are derived from the NOAA/National Centers for Environmental Prediction’s (NCEP) operational global forecasting system, which is based on Global Forecast System (GFS) version 16 with Finite-Volume Cubed-Sphere (FV3) dynamical core (Campbell et al., 2022; Y. Tang et al., 2022). GFSv16 features numerous updated physical schemes and a newly introduced parameterization for sub-grid-scale nonstationary gravity-wave drag compared to its previous version (i.e., GFSv15). CMAQ is the chemical core of the NAQFC and handles transport of primary emitted, secondary chemical products, trace gas emissions, photolysis, chemical interactions (gas and aerosol), and wet/dry deposition.

Anthropogenic emissions are derived from the Neighborhood Emission Mapping Operation (NEMO), a high-resolution data set developed by George Mason University, which is produced at hourly intervals based on the EPA’s National Emission Inventory (NEI) (Ma & Tong, 2022). Fine-scale spatial allocation is achieved by distributing emission sources using 108 spatial surrogates, which represent the portion of a source in each 1-km grid cell. Gaseous and particulate pollutants are speciated into model species for the Carbon Bond 6 chemical mechanism with AERO7, and all sources are grouped into 10 sectors. NEMO provides the first 1-km mapping of anthropogenic air emissions over the CONUS, enabling new applications such as fine-scale air quality modeling and air pollution exposure assessment (Ma & Tong, 2022). No adjustments or predictions are applied to baseline anthropogenic emissions (except the scenarios that include emission adjustments).

Biomass burning emissions, including wildfires, are estimated based on the daily Blended Global Biomass Burning Emissions Products (GBBEPx V3) (X. Zhang et al., 2012, 2014). To generate CMAQ-compatible input files, a wildfire emission preprocessor transforms GBBEPx data by applying daily scaling factors alongside diurnal and speciation profiles. These profiles, characterized by peak daytime emissions and reduced nighttime activity, are derived from established US EPA frameworks (Baker et al., 2016; Tang et al., 2022). This daily data set includes global biomass-burning emissions, as measured by the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectrometer (MODIS) on the National Aeronautics and Space Administration’s (NASA) Terra and Aqua satellites, and the Visible Infrared Imaging Spectrometer (VIIRS) on the Suomi National Polar-orbiting Partnership (SNPP) and Joint Polar-orbiting Satellite System 1 (JPSS-1) satellites. Although the newer version of NAQFC uses the Regional ABI-VIIRS Emissions (RAVE) data set for this purpose, we retained the original data set, and the processed emissions were retrieved from previous work (Campbell et al., 2022; W. Li et al., 2025; Y. Li et al., 2021). For biogenic emissions, we have utilized the in-line module in CMAQv5.4, specifically the Biogenic Emissions Inventory System (BEIS) version 4.0 (Bash et al., 2016), which is informed by the Biogenic Emissions Landuse Data set version 5 (BELD5). For ocean emissions, the Sea Spray module in CMAQ simulates particle emissions from wave breaking and bubble bursts (Gantt et al., 2015). For windblown dust, the FENGSHA model was incorporated into CMAQv5.4 from the NOAA-Environmental Modeling Center (EMC) AQM v6.1.5 (Dong et al., 2016a).

The simulation domain is set over the CONUS with a spatial resolution of 12-km during FIREX-AQ, July-August 2019, which contains extensive observational data, including mobile and stationary ground-based measurements, aircraft sampling, and satellite imaging to support the evaluation of emissions of gases and aerosols and the chemical transformation effects of the emissions (Warneke et al., 2023). Figure 1 shows the simulation domain, NOAA climate regions for evaluation purposes (NOAA, 2022), and all the flight paths of the NASA DC-8 aircraft used from the FIREX-AQ 2019 data set over the most extensive fire in July and August 2019, including three major fires: the Williams Flats fire, the Snow Creek fire, and the Horsefly fire, all in early August.

Model Configuration

Table 1 shows the configurations for the two different CMAQ versions. The NAQFC package used in the current work is based on CMAQv5.3.1, with NOAA customizations that include an inline soil-moisture approximation, an additional inline plume-rise calculation scheme, inline FENGSHA wind-blown dust emissions, and several other changes. Additional CMAQv5.4 in-house modification only included the wind-blown dust module that was previously excluded in NOAA’s version. The simulation period, domain, resolution, meteorology, chemical initial and boundary conditions (ICONs/BCONs), wildfire emissions, aqueous-phase chemistry, and the land surface module remain unchanged between the two CMAQ configurations. The operational NAQFC output provides 72 hr of prediction, whereas we have simulated only the first 24 hr for evaluation and investigation purposes.

Simulation Setups

Table 2 and Table S10 in Supporting Information S1 summarize the main and supporting baseline and sensitivity simulation setups. BASE1 is the baseline simulation from the NOAA team (Y. Tang et al., 2022) and is based on CMAQv5.3.1. CMAQv5.3.1 (NAQFC) modifications by NOAA, which include two levels of soil-moisture approximation (note that moisture approximation is not available from the meteorological input), an additional fire plume-rise scheme, the FENGSHA windblown dust scheme, 90% reduced fugitive dust in rain and snow, and changes to output file customization. In BASE2, the anthropogenic emissions are updated to NEMO, with the 1-km resolution input re-gridded to 12-km emissions. The emission interpolation and reprojection process results in a ~1.5% domain-wide decrease in emissions for most species. The BASE2.1 simulation is designed to update BASE2 as closely as possible to CMAQv5.4 by creating a slightly modified CMAQv5.3.1 that adds the Surface Tiled Aerosol and Gaseous Exchange (STAGE) dry-deposition module. The only significant difference is the inclusion of the dry deposition module. Some modifications were made to the wildfire plume-rise to enable CMAQv5.3.1 with STAGE to read the wildfire emissions and function properly. All sensitivity simulations are based on CMAQv5.4, and each sensitivity test modifies a significant scientific module of CMAQv5.4, as shown in Table 2. Wind-blown dust is not simulated in any BASE simulations using CMAQv5.3.1 (NAQFC) (BASE1, 2, and 2.1) but is simulated with FENGSHA using CMAQv5.4 (SENS1 to 6). The wildfire plume rise in CMAQv5.4 does not require any modifications, as it is compatible with the available wildfire emissions. Several sensitivity simulations (denoted as SENS#.#) were designed to assess subtle adjustments. CMAQv5.4 updated the in-line biogenic emission module, BEIS4. Compared with BEIS3.61, BEIS4 produces 30% more isoprene due to a correction to the canopy light model (US EPA, 2024b). SENS3 updates the gas-phase mechanism from CB6r3 to CB6r5, incorporating changes based on a Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) assessment (Burkholder et al., 2015). The newer mechanism has updated reaction rate coefficients, 41 rate constants, and 6 photolysis rates (G. Yarwood et al., 2020). The adjustments in SENS5 and SENS6 were chosen based on the SENS1.1 and SENS1.2 simulations for Isoprene (ISOP) and wildfire Primary Organic Aerosol (POA), and the NMB of other sensitivity tests for other species adjustments.

Observation Data Sets and Evaluation Protocols

Two primary observational data sets, the Air Quality System (AQS) (US EPA, 2024a) and NASA DC-8 FIREX-AQ observations (Warneke et al., 2023), were used to evaluate the model in this work. The flight data are divided into identified non-fire and fire events using the “smoke_flag” flag, which is available in the data set. Unlike the work of Tang et al. (2022) (Y. Tang et al., 2022), which utilized AIRNOW, this study employs the AQS data set that became available during the study period. Chemical species and the number of sites/observations are provided in Table S1 in Supporting Information S1.

The evaluation protocol assesses model performance across various spatial and temporal domains using several distinct approaches: operational evaluation to determine if the model meets specific performance goals, diagnostic evaluation to investigate the underlying processes and identify strengths or weaknesses, and sensitivity analysis to test the model’s response to changes in inputs. For performance statistics used in the operational evaluations, a suite of metrics is used, including the correlation coefficient (r), mean bias (MB), gross error (GE), root mean square error (RMSE), normalized mean bias (NMB), normalized mean error (NME), and index of agreement (IOA) (Yahya et al., 2016; Yu et al., 2006; Y. Zhang et al., 2006, 2009). Table S2 in Supporting Information S1 contains the evaluation criteria based on previous studies, and the formulae are provided in Table S3 in Supporting Information S1.

Results and Discussion

Evaluation With Routine Monitoring Networks

CONUS-Scale Model Performance

The meteorological performance is available in the work by Tang et al. (Y. Tang et al., 2022), and our evaluation of hourly meteorology against DC-8 (Tables S6 and S7 in Supporting Information S1) and METAR (Table S9 in Supporting Information S1) is consistent with Tang et al. (2022). Table 3 shows PM2.5 performance for all simulations. BASE2, with high-resolution anthropogenic emissions, shows lower simulated PM2.5 than BASE1. Emission differences are not uniquely distributed across states (Figure S1 in Supporting Information S1). The lower emissions for some species in BASE2 (Table S4 in Supporting Information S1) exacerbate the underprediction of PM2.5, part of the reduction in emissions is due to the re-gridding and re-projecting process to our 12-km resolution domain. Explicit species within PM2.5 are less biased than unspeciated PM2.5, which accounts for up to 21% of simulated PM2.5. Unspeciated PM2.5 predictions are degraded in BASE2 compared to BASE1, as indicated by a 0.1 μg m−3 higher MB (see Table S5 in Supporting Information S1). PM2.5 speciation is provided in Figure S2 in Supporting Information S1. BASE2.1, compared to BASE2, improves PM2.5 NMB by 6.1% as a result of the dry deposition module update. The difference is in the assumptions in particle deposition velocities in a few land use categories (e.g., forest (tree cover in Figure S3 in Supporting Information S1)) (Appel et al., 2021), which impacts PM2.5_OC (PM2.5 Organic Carbon, Figure S3 in Supporting Information S1).

Emissions of isoprene and PM2.5 impact on model performance. SENS1 results in more isoprene than the previous base (isoprene NMB of −34.6% in SENS1 compared to −46.8% in BASE2.1) and less PM2.5_OC (NMB of −1.8% compared to −4.3%), a decrease in the unspeciated PM2.5 further results in an overall reduction in total PM2.5 (NMB at −15.8%) (Figure S4 in Supporting Information S1). SENS1.1 doubles isoprene emissions compared to SENS1, which increases PM2.5 by a small amount and improves all statistical parameters, yet at the cost of a considerable change in NMB for isoprene from an underprediction of −34.6% to an overprediction of 57.6% and an increase in NME from 55.6% to 81.7% over CONUS. SENS1.2, compared to SENS1, is an attempt to assess how an increase in wildfire POA emissions will affect the wildfire areas (Figure S6 in Supporting Information S1). This change increases PM2.5 over CONUS by a small amount and increases the NMB by 0.7%. Additional sensitivity simulations, in which the wildfire POA is multiplied by 2, 3, 5, and 7, show reduced underprediction of PM2.5_OC; however, this also decreases accuracy. Pye et al. (2024) found that the PM2.5 emissions for western US wildfires were likely underestimated by a factor of 3. In the SENS2 simulation, lightning NOx emissions have a small impact on O3 and a minimal impact on PM2.5, compared to SENS1 (Figure S7 in Supporting Information S1). The change from SENS2 to SENS3 reduces the underprediction marginally, primarily due to an increase in NMBs for PM2.5_SO42−, unspecified PM2.5, PM2.5_OC, and PM2.5_NH4+ (~35%, 24%, 24%, and 14%, respectively). With SENS4, the dry deposition module in CMAQv5.4 is updated to follow the work of Emmerson et al. (2020), resulting in an increased dry deposition velocity for accumulation mode particles, which leads to reduced PM2.5 over forests. SENS4, compared to SENS3, exhibits a reduction in simulated PM2.5, an increase in r, and an increase in errors. To further support ongoing development in NAQFC, SENS4.1 was conducted to show the impact of turning off the ammonia bidirectional flux (ABFLUX) for in-line deposition velocities. Domain-wide, PM2.5_NH4+ is reduced, and simultaneously PM2.5_NO3 are reduced, both contributing to lower PM2.5 predictions, reduced R and NMB, and increased NME, as shown in Table S5 and Figure S10 in Supporting Information S1. SENS5 outperforms SENS3 in all statistical metrics for PM2.5. SENS6 further improves PM2.5 statistics by adjusting anthropogenic emissions. The result over CONUS shows a reduction in accuracy (RMSE & NME) while enhancing the MB.

Figure 2 shows time-series plots of the daily average PM2.5 and several of its components, averaged over the CONUS. The persistent underprediction of total PM2.5 was visible across all simulations throughout August. Updating emissions in BASE2 results in lower PM2.5 than in BASE1, which can be partially mitigated through STAGE dry deposition in BASE2.1, while maintaining the higher r of BASE2. The underprediction of PM2.5 elemental carbon (PM2.5_EC) could be due to errors in emissions, deposition, or transport, since the species is only primary. Simulations at the NEMO native resolution (i.e., 1-km) in the future may improve PM2.5_EC performance, as source contributions can be better resolved at this resolution. The EC component in SENS6 performs better than all other simulations (Table S5 in Supporting Information S1), suggesting that the systematic underprediction of 12-km EC emissions and/or the limitations of the 12 km grid in capturing the spatial distribution of EC. The addition of lightning NOx (SENS2) results in essentially similar PM2.5 predictions as the simulation without it (SENS1). Compared to BASE2.1, SENS1 yields lower PM2.5 Organic Carbon (PM2.5_OC) predictions; however, PM2.5_OC with higher concentrations exhibits minimal MB and reduced accuracy. Compared with SENS4.1, PM2.5_NH4+ in SENS4 highlights the importance of including ABFLUX, which is recommended for future generations of online NAQFC, as it reduces bias and improves accuracy for this species. Across all simulations, PM2.5_SO42− predictions show much less day-to-day variability than the observations, mainly due to the averaging effect, where single station variations show better compatibility, and partially indicate possible inaccuracy of SO2 emissions, meteorology (e.g., precipitation or planetary boundary layer), and/or chemistry (e.g., the gas-phase oxidation of SO2). The average concentration of PM2.5_NO3− is well captured by the model, which may indicate uncertainties in the emission inputs, as the model fails to capture the variation and fluctuations in PM2.5_NO3− production. Compared with other simulations, SENS6, which directly increases anthropogenic emissions, shows the highest concentrations across all PM2.5 species, except for OC. However, the increases do not align with the observed temporal variability, resulting in reduced accuracy, as noted in the previous section.

NEMO emission shows higher accuracy for gaseous species across the CONUS and improved O3 predictions (higher r, lower RMSE and NME, and better MB and NMB), whereas other SENS tests do not improve O3 predictions. As shown in Table S4 in Supporting Information S1, NEMO exhibits lower NOx emissions but higher total anthropogenic VOC emissions. Lower NOx emissions result in a domain-wide O3 reduction across most areas of the CONUS, as regional O3 formation is NOx-limited (Table S4 and Figure S1 in Supporting Information S1). With the increase in isoprene and the decrease in domain-wide CO following the CMAQ upgrade (SENS1), reduced OH availability lowers O3, further improving O3 prediction accuracy and bringing MB and NMB close to zero. Compared to BASE2, BASE2.1 increases NMB from 1.3% to 1.4% due to changes in the dry deposition module (Figure S3 in Supporting Information S1). SENS1, compared to BASE2.1, further increases O3 by 0.2 ppbv at stations and 0.48 ppbv domain-wide (Figure S4 in Supporting Information S1). For SENS1.1 with the addition of isoprene (i.e., VOCs), OH is reduced domain-wide, yet O3 shows variability domain-wide with a net positive increase (see Figure S5 in Supporting Information S1). SENS2, compared to SENS1, includes lightning NO emissions and indicates where accuracy is reduced, and errors increase. The gas-phase mechanism upgrade in SENS3 results in increased surface O3 concentrations, primarily due to updated rate constants in CB6r5. Impacts of changing the gas-phase chemistry mechanism from CB6r3 to CB6r5 on monthly average NOx and O3 concentrations during August 2019 are shown in Figure S8 in Supporting Information S1. Upgrading the dry deposition module in SENS4 has a minimal impact on surface O3 statistics, which are within the margin of error of the reported values in Table 4. SENS5 and 6 both increase domain-wide O3, decrease the r, and increase NME. The increase in NOx is the primary driver of the increase in O3 in SENS6.

BASE1 better captures peak O3 concentrations on days when these concentrations are underpredicted by other simulations (Figure S13 in Supporting Information S1). BASE1 has the lowest nocturnal O3 predictions among the simulations because of the highest NO emissions, which facilitate titration. On select days, predicted O3 in BASE1 is lower than in observations, but BASE1 overall has the best nocturnal performance. During the day, BASE1, with its higher NO emissions, produces some of the highest O3 predictions. BASE2/2.1/SENS1/1.1 all show comparable noon-time peak captures in diurnal time series. BASE1 captures NOx peaks better (Figure S13 in Supporting Information S1). Applying the same explanation to days with lower O3, the troughs are better captured. The daily max 8h O3 time series is best captured by SENS1, which combines NEMO emissions with the base CMAQ upgrade (Figure S13 in Supporting Information S1). Days with higher O3 levels were associated with better performance than those with lower O3 levels. Both daily max 8h O3 and diurnal O3 show overpredictions for BASE1 and are best captured by SENS1. The increased isoprene in SENS5, similar to SENS1.1, enhances O3. With the additional NOx in SENS6, O3 showed higher overpredictions in diurnal and time-series plots in Figure 3 and Figure S13 in Supporting Information S1.

Spatial Variability

Figure 4 illustrates the performance of the PM2.5 model as a function of region and simulation, and Figure S17 in Supporting Information S1 includes PM2.5 overlay plots. BASE1 shows higher correlations between predictions and observations for PM2.5 in the West, Southeast, Upper Midwest, and Northeast than in other regions. Northwest and West both show positive MBs and NMBs, and the rest of the regions show underpredictions. NMEs across regions range from 30% to 55%, with the lowest rates in the West and the highest in the Northwest. BASE2, with the high-resolution anthropogenic emission from NEMO, increases r in the Upper Midwest, Ohio Valley, Southwest, and Northwest. The largest improvements are in the Northwest and West regions, with the greatest reductions in their NMEs compared with other regions. Some regions, such as the Southeast and Ohio Valley, experience further decreases in PM2.5 due to preexisting underpredictions. However, in the Ohio Valley, the NME improves, whereas in the Southeast, it increases. Compared to BASE2, BASE2.1 generally increases PM2.5 across all regions, with varying magnitudes and a reduced overall NME; the Northwest and West regions are affected to a lesser extent than other regions. Compared to BASE2.1, upgrading the CMAQ version from 5.3.1 to 5.4 in SENS1 decreases PM2.5 domain-wide and r in the Northeast, Southeast, and Northern Rockies, but increases r over the South and West. One difference is that ASOA (secondary organic aerosol from anthropogenic VOCs) increases significantly, whereas BSOA (secondary organic aerosol from biogenic VOCs) decreases moderately in this sensitivity run (Figure S4 in Supporting Information S1). These differences can be explained mainly by changes in the formulation used to estimate ASOA and BSOA in CMAQv5.4 compared to CMAQv5.3.1. In CMAQv5.4, ASOA includes additional species, leading to substantial increases over CONUS. The removal of glyoxal SOA from the BSOA calculations results in reduced BSOA. Anthropogenic point-source and wildfire emissions are treated consistently as semivolatile POA and partitioned into two major categories: primary organic compounds (POC) and primary non-carbon organic matter (PNCOM). Each category is further divided into six volatility groups comprising four gaseous and two fine-aerosol bins, corresponding to 9% Low-VOC (particle phase), 41% Semi-VOC (gas and particle phases), and 50% Intermediate-VOC (gas phase) (Robinson et al., 2007). Subsequent studies indicate that this representation is likely too volatile; the Community Regional Atmospheric Chemistry Multiphase Mechanism (CRACMM) instead assigns a larger fraction of material to the LVOC and SVOC ranges, consistent with Woody et al. (2016) (Woody et al., 2016). By treating emissions as semivolatile, the model accounts for dynamic gas-particle partitioning of primary organics, allowing POA to evaporate upon dilution and subsequently undergo gas-phase oxidation, thereby contributing to SOA formation. This treatment is particularly relevant for biomass-burning plumes, where the evolution of POC and PNCOM species represents the chemical aging and mass enhancement typically observed downwind of wildfire events. Consequently, the present configuration may overestimate the evaporation of wildfire POA. However, the very high concentrations within wildfire plumes imply that a substantial portion of IVOC remains condensed near the emission source. Thus, evaporation likely has a limited influence in the near-source region but may become more important farther downwind. The addition of lightning emissions in SENS2 has minimal impact on simulated PM2.5; increased NO emissions over CONUS are more concentrated over the Atlantic Ocean. A significant increase in MB of PM2.5_NO3− occurs in most of the regions with preexisting underpredictions, which also reduces their NMEs. Changing the gas-phase mechanism from CB6r3 to CB6r5 in SENS3 increases PM2.5 all over CONUS, with more impacts over the Ohio Valley, Northwest, and West regions, which already show overpredictions (in SENS2). The effect of this change on the NME is minimal. SENS4 reduces PM2.5 across the CONUS and more strongly over the Northwest region. The r-value is decreased significantly in the Northern Rockies. This change increases NME across the entire domain, except for the Northwest region. Compared to SENS4, SENS4.1 has no significant impact on any of the statistical parameters. Increased r is observed in SENS5 across 5 regions, and reduced NME across 4 regions. In contrast, SENS6, which exhibits an increase in emissions, shows a more robust increase in MB, and in some regions, reduced r and increased NME, suggesting that a regional emission adjustment may be more beneficial for the emissions used in this work.

Figure 5 presents four O3 performance metrics for BASE1 and the differences between pairs of designed simulations, assessing the impacts of one or more changes in model options, as listed in Table 2, across various climate regions. Figure S18 in Supporting Information S1 includes O3 overlay plots. Heatmap plots of daily max 8h O3 indicate good performance in r for most regions, except for the Southwest, where r is as low as 0.55. The poor performance in the Southwest may be related to O3 underpredictions, likely due to underestimation of NOx and VOC emissions (see Figure S14 in Supporting Information S1) along the southern U.S. border, where the prevailing wind direction is southerly (see Figure S11 in Supporting Information S1), or to underestimation of NOx emissions from lightning. Comparing BASE2 and BASE1, the r and NME improve in most regions except for the Northwest regions, where the underprediction becomes greater as a result of NOx reduction. Compared to BASE2, BASE2.1 shows a decrease in O3 over the Northeast, Southeast, and West regions. The reduction is due to increased O3 deposition on soil and forest surfaces, a new soil resistance parameterization, and in-canopy resistance (US EPA, 2019). Increased isoprene in SENS1.2 increases the O3 concentrations over the VOC-limited regions, such as the West region (e.g., Los Angeles and San Francisco). The domain-wide OH is reduced (see Figure S3 in Supporting Information S1), and so is NOx. NOx reduction to a specific threshold could increase O3 in VOC-limited regions. The impact of NOx lightning emissions in SENS2 is most pronounced over the South and Southeast regions, where more lightning occurred and was dispersed, increasing NMB and NME and reducing r. Changing the gas-phase mechanism to CB6r5 in SENS3 affects O3 most over the Southeast region and is minimal in the Upper Midwest, Southwest, and West regions. SENS4, in which the dry deposition module is modified, has minimal impact in the Northern Rockies and negligible impact elsewhere. SENS4.1 without the ABFLUX shows a slight increase over the South and Southwest regions. SENS5 and 6 both increase O3, but it is more prominent in SENS6 across all regions, with increased NME in most regions due to the availability of additional NOx for O3 formation and because most of CONUS is in a NOx-limited regime for O3 production.

Evaluation With Episodic Field Measurements

The two most significant fire events during FIREX-AQ were the Williams Flats fire on 08/06/2019, with an initial location of (42.83313°N, 115.4817°W) and affected burnt area of 67069 acres, and the second fire, the Snow Creek fire on 08/02/2019, with an initial location of (47.9799°N, 118.624°W) and an affected burnt area of 66% of that of the Williams Flats fire. Figure 6 presents statistical evaluations of non-fire and fire events from 22 July 2019 to 31 August 2019 based on data from all DC-8 flights in the western region (124°–110°W) at altitudes below 3,000 m. In both fire events and non-fire events, Aerosol Optical Extinction (AOE) @550 nm is underpredicted by all simulations. Among them, SENS1.2 with the added wildfire POA improved both NMB and NME. In general, benzene is underpredicted in all simulations, including fire conditions, consistent with previous work (Pye et al., 2024). BASE 1 showed the smallest magnitude NMB for benzene of all simulations, indicating less bias in the NEI2016v1 emissions than the NEMO 2019 emissions for non-fire sources of benzene.

Another species that exhibits lower bias and error in concentration during fire events is ethane (C2H4), suggesting that it is more accurately represented in fire emissions than other species. During the non-fire events, only BASE1 showed higher NMB and NME compared to other simulations. As the major formation pathway for HNO3 is the oxidation of NOx by OH, the increased isoprene in SENS1.1 indicates a decrease in OH consumption, as isoprene competes for this radical; thus, less OH is available for NOx conversion to HNO3, resulting in lower HNO3 concentrations. The GBBEPx fire emissions include isoprene and terpene emissions, which may explain part of the isoprene difference between fire and non-fire events. Other sensitivity tests that impact isoprene performance show changes in OH. Isoprene exhibits high errors and small NMBs across all simulations, except SENS1.1, which shows a 200% increase in bias when isoprene emissions are doubled. BASE2 and BASE2.1 yield the lowest NMBs for isoprene, indicating that higher VOC in NEMO consumes more OH, leaving less available for isoprene oxidation and thus higher isoprene. Although Table S5 in Supporting Information S1 shows lower isoprene concentrations, it is representative only of AQS sites, whereas Figure S3 in Supporting Information S1 shows an increased isoprene domain-wide. BASE1 shows two major differences for NOx in no fire events: overpredictions with an NMB of 47.5%, larger than all other NMBs, and higher NME changes to underpredictions and lower NMEs in all other simulations. SENS1.1 shows lower NOx in both fire and non-fire events, where increased isoprene may lead to greater NOx titration at night, reducing NOx concentrations. O3 mostly maintains similar performance, except for SENS1.1, which exhibits increased isoprene, resulting in slightly less underprediction in these regions. OA performance improves in SENS1.2 with increased wildfire POA, underscoring the importance of a better understanding of wildfire emissions and chemistry. Isoprene and NOx are overpredicted in SENS5 and SENS6, respectively, but they improve O3 prediction in both fire and non-fire events in SENS6 for NMB and NME.

The most significant event on 6 August 2019, during FIREX-AQ is further examined using time-series analysis of species with the most notable differences. Figure 7 shows the DC-8 flight time series plots of selected species for non-fire and fire events, and Figure 8 shows the time series plots of total PM2.5, Cl−, and OC for one out of two AQS sites.

AOE, an indicator of aerosol mass in both fire and non-fire events, shows underpredictions for aerosol emissions from the wildfire inputs (i.e., GBBEPx). The AOE predictions from SENS1.2 indicate that increased wildfire POA emissions more accurately reproduce the observed high AOE values compared with other sensitivity tests. Some observed peak values seem to be missed, suggesting a lack of data in that period (e.g., 2019-08-06 21:00-22:00). EC, on the other hand, is overpredicted in non-fire events and during some hours in the fire event (i.e., 2019-08-06 19:36-20:22), but underpredicted during more hours the fire event (e.g., 2019-08-06 23:26-2019-08-07 00:56). EC could also be emitted from wildfire in addition to the anthropogenic sources. Model biases during non-fire events may indicate uncertainties associated with anthropogenic emissions. Several additional factors may contribute to the biases in CMAQ predictions. For example, the meteorological model (WRF) does not account for the elevated heat produced by fire flames and therefore does not increase the boundary plume height. The regridding of anthropogenic emissions may dilute emissions across larger cells. At 12 km resolution, sub-grid-scale fire plume structures cannot be fully captured, leading to greater numerical dilution and faster mixing of fire emissions with background air. As a result, near-source peak concentrations may be underestimated, while regional-scale impacts are more reliably represented. The first three BASE simulations, based on the older CMAQv5.3.1, show slightly lower AOE predictions than all SENS simulations. SENS1 performs better in AOE prediction with CMAQv5.4 and BEIS4 than with data from DC-8 flights (Figure 7). Comparing EC with the only AQS site with data (i.e., station number 160010010) suggests that BASE1 captures daily EC better (Figure 8).

On the other hand, PM2.5 from BASE1 showed greater overprediction than in other simulations.

Conclusion

In this work, CMAQv5.3.1 is updated to CMAQv5.4 in the offline National Air Quality Forecast Capability (NAQFC), and the updated NAQFC is tested using the high-resolution NEMO data set in nine simulations with different model configurations during the FIREX-AQ 2019 campaign. The simulations using CMAQv5.3.1 and CMAQv5.4 (a total of 11 simulations) are designed to examine the effects of one or more changes to model representations of key processes in CMAQv5.4 or to model emission inputs (anthropogenic emissions, wildfire POA emissions, isoprene emissions, lightning emissions) on NAQFC forecasting skill. The simulations designed to examine the impacts of process representations in CMAQv5.4 include the newer Biogenic Emission Inventory System 4, gas-phase mechanism Carbon Bond 6 revisions 5 (CB6r5), an updated dry deposition module (STAGE), and ammonia bi-directional exchange. The simulation results are evaluated over CONUS and nine climate regions using ground-based AQS and NASA DC-8 flight data.

Incorporating NEMO emissions exacerbates the underprediction of PM2.5 relative to the initial configuration, while improving O3 prediction accuracy (BASE2). Switching M3Dry to STAGE (BASE2.1) in CMAQv5.3.1 alleviates PM2.5 underpredictions with improved accuracy. Updating CMAQv5.3.1 to CMAQv5.4 (SENS1) had minimal impact across the CONUS on PM2.5. Doubling isoprene emission (SENS1.1) improves PM2.5 predictions, yet at the cost of drastically deteriorating isoprene statistical performance. Doubling wildfire POA emission (SENS1.2) indicates the need for improvements in PM2.5 predictions in fire events. Adding lightning NOx (SENS2) increases O3 and its transport mostly over Southeast regions. Updating the gas-phase mechanism from CB6r3 to CB6r5 (SENS3) yields significant improvements across all PM2.5 metrics. Swapping STAGE parameterization from Shu et al. (2022) to Emerson et al. (2020) (SENS4) reduces PM2.5 prediction over all regions and increases the NME. Turning off the bidirectional ammonia flux (SENS4.1) results in a slight decline in performance, suggesting that it may be beneficial to leave it on in future NAQFC systems. A combined adjustment that doubles the wildfire POA and increases isoprene by 50% (SENS5) addresses the underprediction of PM2.5 at a slight cost of O3 accuracy. Finally, adjustments to NEMO emissions (SENS6) further improve PM2.5 underprediction at the cost of deteriorating O3 performance.

Limitations in this work include the uncertainties of emission inputs, wildfire emissions, the propagation of uncertainties of the meteorological model to the chemical model, and the grid interpolation using the meteorological input interpolator (NOAA-EPA Atmosphere-Chemistry Coupler (NACC). Additionally, the offline-coupled model employed in this work does not account for the impact of chemistry on meteorology. Up-to-date emissions, in particular, more accurate wildfire emissions, will improve model performance. For this work, CMAQ lightning parameterization based on the World Wide Lightning Location Network (WWLLN) from EPA’s Air QUAlity TimE Series Project (US EPA, 2021) has been utilized; however, it is suggested to use a scaled WWLLN if the National Lightning Detection Network data set is not available (Kang et al., 2022). The AQS site has one set of EC and OC observational data every 3 days, which makes interpreting the model evaluation challenging. For PM2.5_OC, the significant differences across all simulations are limited to changes in anthropogenic emissions (BASE1 to BASE2) and dry deposition from M3Dry to STAGE (BASE2 to BASE2.1), with mixed performance at peaks and lows. Future work should utilize an online-coupled NAQFC model at a higher resolution to fully leverage the high-resolution 1-km emissions from NEMO.
