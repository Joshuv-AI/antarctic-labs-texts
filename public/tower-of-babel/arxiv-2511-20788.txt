Hund-projected Kanamori model: an effective description of Hund’s metals near the Mott insulating regime
Johan Carlström

Abstract
Hund’s coupling plays a decisive role in shaping electron correlations of multi-orbital systems, giving rise to a class of materials–Hund’s metals–that combine local-moment physics with metallic transport. Here we derive an effective low-energy description of such a system near the Mott insulating regime, starting from the multi-orbital Hubbard-Kanamori Hamiltonian and projecting onto the high-spin manifold favored by Hund’s first rule. The resulting Hund-projected Kanamori model captures the interplay between carrier motion and magnetic correlations in the presence of strong Hund’s coupling. In the undoped limit, the model reduces to a spin- N / 2 N/2 Heisenberg system with suppressed quantum fluctuations, approaching the classical limit for realistic five-band configurations. Upon doping, carrier motion couples strongly to the spin background and drives ferromagnetic correlations through a Hund-enhanced kinetic mechanism analogous to, but much stronger than, Nagaoka ferromagnetism. Owing to its reduced sign problem, the model can be addressed with advanced path-integral methods to determine quasiparticle structure and effective interactions between carriers-quantities that are challenging to obtain with other methods. This framework establishes a microscopic bridge between the Kanamori model and the emergent magnetic and transport phenomena characteristic of Hund’s metals.



Strongly correlated electronic systems occupy a central position in condensed matter physics owing to their propensity to host emergent quantum phases such as unconventional superconductivity, spin and charge order, orbital-selective states, and non-Fermi-liquid behavior. For decades, the study of strong correlations was almost synonymous with high-temperature superconductivity and microscopic descriptions based on doped Mott insulators

;
Lee et al. 2006
;
Scalapino 2012
. More recently, correlation-driven phenomena have also been realized in moiré heterostructures, where a small twist angle or layers with an incommensurate interatomic spacing produces superlattices with anomalously narrow bands and enhanced electronic interactions

;
Cao et al. 2018b
;
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Pan et al. 2020
;
Wu et al. 2018
;

A central theme uniting these systems is the competition between magnetic order and carrier delocalization

;
Kane et al. 1989
;
Koepsell et al. 2019
;
Koepsell et al. 2021
. This interplay is believed to underlie a variety of emergent states, including high-temperature superconductivity

;
Schrieffer et al. 1989
;

, quantum spin-liquids
Glittum et al. 2025
, and kinetic magnetism

;

;
Carlström 2022
;
Tao et al. 2024
, where magnetic fluctuations can mediate effective pairing interactions leading to phase-separation and condensation

;

.

In conventional strongly correlated lattice descriptions such as the Hubbard or
the
t
-
J models, as well as multi-orbital generalizations thereof, strong quantum fluctuations suppress magnetic order even in the absence of charge carriers, particularly in low-spin systems

.
In contrast, multi-orbital compounds with strong Hund’s coupling–such as the iron pnictides, chalcogenides, and ruthenates–exhibit a qualitatively different form of correlated behavior
Georges et al. 2013
.
These so-called Hund’s metals display large local magnetic momenta owing to Hund’s first rule, that persist from Mott insulating states well into the metallic regime.
The impact of Hund’s coupling on charge-fluctuations is somewhat contradictory: At half filling it increases the effective contact repulsion, driving Mott insulating behavior. Away from half filling, it enhances charge fluctuations, promoting metallicity instead–a dual behavior referred to as the Janus effect
de’ Medici et al. 2011
.
The tendency to form large local spin moments should be expected to dramatically reduce quantum spin-fluctuations. At the same time, compliance with Hund’s rule should clearly make delocalization especially sensitive to spin-correlations. The emerging picture is thus of competition between almost classical magnetism, and a very potent form of kinetic ferromagnetism. This clearly sets Hund’s metals apart from other strongly correlated systems.

Materials with strong Hund’s coupling
host an array of unconventional phenomena, including multiband superconductivity, orbital-selective Mott phases, and broken time-reversal symmetry

Because many of these systems lie close to the Mott insulating regime in terms of filling

;
Lafuerza et al. 2017
;

In this work, we derive such a framework, starting from the multi-orbital Hubbard-Kanamori Hamiltonian and projecting onto the high-spin manifold favored by Hund’s rule. The resulting Hund-projected Kanamori Model (HPKM) constitutes an effective low-energy theory for doped Hund’s systems near the Mott regime derived with two assumptions: That charge-fluctuations are short-lived and can be integrated out, and that fluctuations to lower-spin states–violating Hund’s first rule–are small. Despite the complexity of the original Hamiltonian, the projected model takes the form of a doped spin system with strongly suppressed quantum fluctuations and a tractable structure well-suited for path-integral and diagrammatic methods. It thereby provides a microscopic foundation for studying the magnetic dynamics and carrier-induced phenomena characteristic of Hund’s metals beyond mean-field theory.

.1
The Kanamori Model

The paradigmatic model of Hund’s metals is the Hubbard-Kanamori Hamiltonian
Georges et al. 2013
, with an on-site interaction on the form:

\displaystyle H_{\text{K}}\!\!=\!U\!\sum_{\alpha}n_{\alpha\uparrow}n_{\alpha\downarrow}\!\!+\!U^{\prime}\!\sum_{\alpha\not=\beta}n_{\alpha\uparrow}n_{\beta\downarrow}\!+\!(U^{\prime}\!\!-\!J\!)\!\sum_{\alpha>\beta}\!n_{\alpha\sigma}n_{\beta\sigma}

\displaystyle-J\sum_{\alpha\not=\beta}a_{\alpha\uparrow}^{\dagger}a_{\alpha\downarrow}a_{\beta\downarrow}^{\dagger}a_{\beta\uparrow}-P\sum_{\alpha\not=\beta}a_{\alpha\uparrow}^{\dagger}a_{\alpha\downarrow}^{\dagger}a_{\beta\downarrow}a_{\beta\uparrow}.

(1)

Here,
\alpha
denotes the band index.
The principal energy scales of this model are the Coulomb repulsion
U
, and the Hund’s coupling
J
. The inter-orbital repulsion between electrons with opposite spin is given by
U^{\prime}=U-2J
, while the interaction between electrons in different orbitals with the same spin is reduced further to
U^{\prime}-J
. For the pair-conversion amplitude we generally have
P=J
, though we will treat this term on a separate footing.
The most general form of the hopping term is given by:

\displaystyle H_{0}=\sum_{ij\alpha\beta\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}t_{ij\alpha\beta}a_{i\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}a_{j\beta\sigma^{\prime}}.

(2)

For simplicity, we restrict ourselves to nearest-neighbor processes that preserve both spin and orbital quantum numbers, though the generalization to other cases is straightforward.

The principal effect of Hund’s coupling is to maximize total spin, consistent with Hund’s first rule.
With
N
bands and the constraint that the orbital occupancy is given by
\bar{o}=[o_{1},o_{2}...o_{N}]
, the Kanamori Hamiltonian (
1
) predicts a ground state spanned by

\displaystyle|S_{M}^{\text{n}},S_{z}\rangle\;,\text{with }|S_{z}|\leq S_{M}^{\text{n}},\;\text{where}

(3)

\displaystyle S_{M}^{\text{n}}=\frac{1}{2}n,\;\;n=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}[1-(-1)^{o_{i}}].

(4)

Here,
n
is the number of orbitals that contribute to total spin (by being singly occupied) and
S_{M}^{n}
is the corresponding maximal spin.
For
H_{K}[P=0]
, and occupation number
n
, the first excited state is given by:

\displaystyle|S_{M}^{\text{n}}-1,S_{z}\rangle\;\text{with }E\Big(S_{M}^{\text{n}}-1\Big)=E\Big(S_{M}^{\text{n}}\Big)+nJ,

(5)

which defines a “Hund’s gap”
\Delta_{H}=nJ
, associated with violating Hund’s first rule.
For real materials, this energy scale can be quit large: Recent estimates for iron-based superconductors–see Table
1
–indicate that
J\sim 0.3-0.6
eV, implying a gap of
\Delta_{H}\sim 1.5-3
eV at half filling, which is much greater than the energy scale of any itinerant terms in the Hamiltonian. Processes that excite the system by
\Delta_{H}
will thus be strongly suppressed, prompting us to introduce a Hund’s projector:

\displaystyle P^{H}=\Pi_{j}\sum_{\bar{o}^{j}}\sum_{S_{z}^{j}=-S_{M}^{n^{j}}}^{S_{M}^{n^{j}}}|S^{n^{j}}_{M},S^{j}_{z}\rangle\langle S^{n^{j}}_{M},S^{j}_{z}|,

(6)

where
\bar{o}^{j}
is the orbital occupation of the site
j
and
n_{j}=n_{j}[\bar{o}^{j}]
is given by Eq. (
4
). The operator (
6
) thus projects onto states satisfying the condition that the total spin on any given site is maximal for its given orbital occupation, consistent with the Kanamori Hamiltonian (
1
) and Hund’s first rule. Eventually, we will constrain our Hilbert space to the subspace on which
P^{H}
projects, while considering short-lived excitations across the Hund’s gap as emergent processes.

An additional effect of Hund’s coupling is its impact on charge fluctuations. Given two sites where all orbitals are singly occupied, in a ground state of the type (
3
), the energy cost of creating a particle-hole pair is given by

\displaystyle U_{\text{eff}}\!=\!E(N\!+\!1)\!+\!E(N\!-\!1)\!-\!2E(N)\!=\!U\!+\!J(N\!-\!1).

(7)

At half filling, a key consequence of Hund’s coupling is thus a substantial increase of the effective on-site repulsion, which drives Mott insulating behavior. It should be noted however, that away from half filling, Hund’s coupling instead promotes metallicity
Georges et al. 2013
.

Finally, we consider the impact of the pair-conversion term in (

\displaystyle E_{o,\alpha\beta}=U_{\text{eff}}-U^{\prime}+\delta\mu_{\alpha\beta}=J(N+1)+\delta\mu_{\alpha\beta},

(9)

\displaystyle E_{o,\beta\alpha}\sim E_{o,\alpha\beta}+2J(N+1).

(10)

P=J

\displaystyle|o_{GS}\rangle\sim\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{(2N+1)^{2}}}|o_{\alpha\beta}\rangle+\frac{1}{2(N+1)}|o_{\beta\alpha}\rangle.

(11)

.3
Gutzwiller-projection

If
U_{\text{eff}}\gg t
, the lifetime of particle-hole pairs will be short, and it is a good approximation to treat such fluctuations as emergent processes, while applying Gutzwiller projection to the system
Chao et al. 1978
.
In multi-orbital systems, we have the prospect of bands being both hole-doped or particle doped, meaning that we either project out doublons or vacant sites in a given orbital. To simplify the representation we therefore conduct a spin-preserving particle-hole transformation of the bands that are particle doped, given by

\displaystyle a^{\dagger}_{\sigma}\Rightarrow a_{-\sigma},\;a_{\sigma}\Rightarrow a_{-\sigma}^{\dagger}.

(12)

In the new representation, all carriers are hole-like.
The delocalization part of the resulting model is described by projected hopping:

\displaystyle H_{t}=\sum_{\langle ij\rangle\alpha\sigma}t_{\alpha}\tilde{a}^{\dagger}_{i\alpha\sigma}\tilde{a}_{j\alpha\sigma}

(13)

where
\tilde{a}_{i\alpha\sigma}=a_{i\alpha\sigma}(1-n_{i\alpha,-\sigma})
. It should be noted that
t_{\alpha}\to-t_{\alpha}
under the mapping (
12
), though this only matters on non-bipartite lattices.

From the charge fluctuations, we obtain super-exchange processes of the form:

\displaystyle H_{S}\!=\!\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,\alpha}I_{\alpha}\Bigg\{\frac{\hat{S}_{\alpha-}^{i}\hat{S}_{\alpha+}^{j}\!+\!\hat{S}_{\alpha+}^{i}\hat{S}_{\alpha-}^{j}}{2}\!+\!\hat{S}_{\alpha z}^{i}\hat{S}_{\alpha z}^{j}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4}n_{i\alpha}n_{j\alpha}\Bigg\},\;I_{\alpha}\!=\!\frac{4t_{\alpha}^{2}}{U_{\text{eff}}},

(14)

where
\hat{S}^{i}_{\alpha\pm}
raises/lowers the spin of the
\alpha-
orbital on site
i

Chao et al. 1978
. Using Eq. (
7
) and the estimates from the Table. (
1
), we find that
I_{\alpha}=0.025-0.1
eV.

Interactions between carriers take the form

\displaystyle H_{V}=\sum_{i}V_{\alpha\beta}n_{i\alpha}n_{i\beta},

(15)

where
V_{\alpha\beta}
is given by Eq. (
8
). Note that this interaction will depend on whether the carriers were particle or hole-like in the original description.

To take into account the Hund’s coupling, we follow a strategy similar to Gutzwiller projection, and work in the subspace dictated by the projector
P^{H}
, (defined in Eq.
6
), while treating excitations across the Hund’s gap as emergent processes.

.4
Hund-projected magnetism

Operating with
H_{S}
on a ground state of the type (
3
), we obtain two principal contributions:

\displaystyle H_{S}|S_{M}^{n},S_{Z}\rangle\!=\!P^{H}H_{S}|S_{M}^{n},S_{Z}\rangle\!+\!(1\!-\!P^{H})H_{S}|S_{M}^{n},S_{Z}\rangle.

The latter term violates Hund’s first rule to an energy cost of at least
\Delta_{H}
. In Matsubara theory, this exited state will decay with an imaginary lifetime of
\sim 1/\Delta_{H}
, leading to emergent processes with an effective energy scale

\displaystyle\epsilon\sim\frac{I^{2}}{4\Delta_{H}}\sim 0.002-0.017\;I

(16)

near half filing. Virtual magnetic interactions of this type are thus sub-leading by
2-3
orders of magnitude, and it is therefore an excellent approximation to discard them at temperatures
T\ll\Delta_{H}
. In what follows, we will thus project out high-energy contributions from the magnetic interactions by taking

\displaystyle H_{S}\to P^{H}H_{S}.

(17)

For the spin raising/lowering operator of the electron
\alpha
, we note that
\hat{S}_{\alpha\pm}
must necessarily alter
S_{z}
by unity. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle P^{H}\hat{S}_{\alpha\pm}|S_{M}^{n},S_{z}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|S_{M}^{n},S_{z}\pm 1\rangle\langle S_{M}^{n},S_{z}\pm 1|\hat{S}_{\alpha\pm}|S_{M}^{n},S_{z}\rangle

(18)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle|S_{M}^{n},S_{z}\pm 1\rangle\gamma_{S_{M}^{n}S_{z}}^{\pm}.

Evaluation of the inner product–see appendix I–gives

\displaystyle\gamma_{S_{M}^{n}S_{z}}^{-}=\frac{\sqrt{(S_{M}^{n}+S_{z})(S_{M}^{n}-S_{z}+1)}}{2S_{M}^{n}},

\displaystyle\gamma_{S_{M}^{n}S_{z}}^{+}=\frac{\sqrt{(S_{M}^{n}+S_{z}+1)(S_{M}^{n}-S_{z})}}{2S_{M}^{n}}.

(19)

For the
\hat{S}_{z}
terms in the model (
14
), we first note that these preserve
S_{z}
. Therefore, we must have

\displaystyle P^{H}\hat{S}_{\alpha z}|S_{M}^{n},S_{z}\rangle\!=\!P^{H}\!\Big\{u|S_{M}^{n},S_{z}\rangle\!+\!v|S\!\not=\!S_{M}^{n},S_{z}\rangle\Big\}\;\;

\displaystyle=|S_{M}^{n},S_{z}\rangle\langle S_{M}^{n},S_{z}|\hat{S}_{\alpha z}|S_{M}^{n},S_{z}\rangle=\frac{S_{z}}{n}|S_{M}^{n},S_{z}\rangle.\;\;

(20)

Combining the results (
18
) and (
20
), we can write the projected Hamiltonian (
17
) as

\displaystyle P^{H}H_{S}=I^{T}_{ij}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\Bigg\{\frac{\hat{S}_{-}^{i}\hat{S}_{+}^{j}+\hat{S}_{-}^{j}\hat{S}_{+}^{i}}{2n_{i}n_{j}}+\frac{\hat{S}_{z}^{i}}{n_{i}}\frac{\hat{S}_{z}^{j}}{n_{j}}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\alpha}I_{\alpha}n_{i\alpha}n_{j\alpha},\;I^{T}_{ij}=\sum_{\alpha}I_{\alpha}n_{i\alpha}n_{j\alpha}.\;

(21)

At half filing, the spin sector is thus governed by an emergent spin-
N/2
Heisenberg model. For
N=5
, mean-field theory–see Appendix II–suggests that quantum spin fluctuations are modest at approximately
4\%
on the square lattice, and even smaller in three dimensions. This places us close to the classical limit, with a coupling strength

\displaystyle J_{\text{classical}}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\alpha}I_{\alpha}\sim 0.03-0.125\text{ eV}.

(22)

On a cubic lattice, this would give an ordering temperature of
T_{c}\sim 500-2100\text{K}
, though it should be stressed that many Hunds metals feature a weaker inter-layer interaction and that the presence of carriers tends to reduce the ordering temperature.

.5
Charge carriers

To establish the role of mobile dopants, we construct the analogue of the delocalization part of the Hamiltonian [Eq. (
13
)] within the Hund-projected subspace.
We begin by defining the normalization coefficients of the spin ladder operators as follows:

\displaystyle\xi_{S,S_{z}}^{\pm}\hat{S}_{\pm}|S,S_{z}\rangle=|S,S_{z}\pm 1\rangle,\;|S_{z}\pm 1|\leq S

(23)

\displaystyle\chi_{S,l}^{\pm}\hat{S}_{\pm}^{l}|S,\mp S\rangle=|S,\pm(l-S)\rangle,\;|S-l|\leq S.

(24)

Consider a site with
n
particles in a state that maximizes total spin. If the orbital
\alpha

Assuming

\displaystyle a_{\uparrow\alpha}^{\dagger}|S_{M}^{n},-S_{M}^{n}+l\rangle=a_{\uparrow\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{S}_{+}^{l}|S_{M}^{n},-S_{M}^{n}\rangle\chi^{+}_{S^{n}_{M},l}

\displaystyle|R_{\alpha}\rangle=|S_{M}^{n+1}-1,1-S_{M}^{n+1}\rangle_{\alpha}

.6
Hund-projected model

Combining the hopping integral (

) we obtain the Hund-projected Kanamori model:

\displaystyle H=\sum_{\langle ij\rangle\alpha}\tilde{t}_{\alpha}c^{\dagger}_{i\alpha}c_{j\alpha}\hat{b}_{i}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{\gamma}^{\uparrow}_{ij}+c^{\dagger}_{i\alpha}c_{j\alpha}\hat{\gamma}_{ij}^{\downarrow}+\sum_{i}V_{\alpha\beta}n_{i\alpha}n_{i\beta},

\displaystyle+I^{T}_{ij}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\Bigg\{\frac{\hat{S}_{-}^{i}\hat{S}_{+}^{j}+\hat{S}_{-}^{j}\hat{S}_{+}^{i}}{2n_{i}n_{j}}+\frac{\hat{S}_{z}^{i}}{n_{i}}\frac{\hat{S}_{z}^{j}}{n_{j}}\Bigg\}-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\alpha}I_{\alpha}n_{i\alpha}n_{j\alpha},

(40)

where
\tilde{t}_{\alpha}=\pm t_{\alpha}
and the sign depends on whether the orbital
\alpha
was particle like (
+
) or hole-like (
-
) in the original representation.
The spin-less fermions in this model represent charge carriers, while the bosonic occupation number counts the number of spin-up electrons on a site. The latter is constrained to

\displaystyle\hat{l}=\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}\leq\hat{n}=N-\sum_{\alpha}c^{\dagger}_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}.

(41)

The interactions in the model (
40
) are given by

\displaystyle V_{\alpha\beta}=U^{\prime}-J\;\;\text{or}=-U^{\prime},

(42)

for same and different charges respectively.
The effective hopping integral is

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{ij}^{\uparrow}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}_{i}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}_{j}+1}},\;\;\hat{\gamma}_{ij}^{\downarrow}=\sqrt{1-\frac{\hat{b}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{i}}{\hat{n}_{i}}}\sqrt{1-\frac{\hat{b}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{j}}{\hat{n}_{j}+1}}.

and the super-exchange term takes the form

\displaystyle I^{T}_{ij}=\sum_{\alpha}I_{\alpha}c_{i\alpha}c_{i\alpha}^{\dagger}c_{j\alpha}c_{j\alpha}^{\dagger}.

(43)

For a low carrier density, we can make the approximation

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}^{\uparrow}\approx\frac{1}{N},\;\;\;\hat{\gamma}^{\downarrow}_{ij}\approx\sqrt{1-\frac{\hat{b}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{i}}{N}}\sqrt{1-\frac{\hat{b}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{j}}{N}}

(44)

which becomes exact for isolated dopants. In this scenario we can also use

\displaystyle I^{T}_{ij}\approx\sum_{\alpha}I_{\alpha},

(45)

which becomes exact in the absence of carriers.

.7
Excitations across the Hund’s gap

To complete our discussion of dopants, we now turn to kinetic processes that transiently excite the system across the Hund’s gap (see Eq.
30
). Such processes involve intermediate states in which the local spin configuration temporarily violates Hund’s rule, incurring an energy cost on the order of
\Delta_{H}
. Starting from Eqs. (
25
-
26
), the corresponding excited state can be expressed as

\displaystyle\chi^{+}_{S_{M}^{n},l}\hat{S}_{+}^{l}|R_{\alpha}\rangle\equiv\lambda_{n,l}\chi^{+}_{S_{M}^{n},l}\hat{S}_{+}^{l}\Bigg|\frac{n}{2}-1,1-\frac{n}{2}\Bigg\rangle_{\alpha},

where
\lambda
is the amplitude of the state.
The subscript
\alpha
here indicates that the excited state was generated by creating a particle in the
\alpha-
orbital. This is important since states which do not maximize total spin are typically degenerate so that
|R_{\alpha}\rangle\not=|R_{\beta}\rangle
in general.

Applying an annihilation operator and projecting on the Hund’s ground state we find

\displaystyle P^{H}a_{\beta\sigma}\lambda_{n,l}\chi^{+}_{S_{M}^{n},l}\hat{S}_{+}^{l}\Bigg|\frac{n}{2}-1,1-\frac{n}{2}\Bigg\rangle_{\alpha}

(46)

\displaystyle\equiv\kappa_{\alpha\beta\sigma l}\Bigg|\frac{n-1}{2},l+1-\frac{n+\sigma}{2}\Bigg\rangle_{o^{\prime}},

(47)

where
o^{\prime}
denotes the orbital occupation after annihilating a particle in the band
\beta
, and
\kappa
is the amplitude of the resulting state.

To derive the emergent process associated with kinetic excitation and decay, consider two subsequent tunneling events:

\displaystyle t^{2}a^{\dagger}_{i\alpha\sigma}(\tau)a_{j\alpha\sigma}(\tau)a^{\dagger}_{j\beta\sigma^{\prime}}(\tau^{\prime})a_{k\beta\sigma^{\prime}}(\tau^{\prime}),\;\tau>\tau^{\prime}.

(48)

Here, we are interested in the contribution when
\alpha^{\dagger}_{j\beta\sigma^{\prime}}
excites the site
j
across the Hund’s gap. The lifetime of this state is
\delta\tau=1/\Delta_{H}
and with
\sigma^{\prime}=\;\uparrow
, we obtain an emergent pair propagation term

\displaystyle H_{h}\!\sim\!\frac{-t^{2}}{\Delta_{H}}c_{j\alpha}^{\dagger}c_{j\beta}\Bigg(\delta_{\sigma,\uparrow}\!+\!\delta_{\sigma,\downarrow}\frac{\hat{b}_{j}^{\dagger}}{\sqrt{\hat{l}_{j}+1}}\Bigg)\kappa_{\alpha\beta\sigma l_{j}}P^{H}a^{\dagger}_{i\alpha\sigma}a_{k\beta\uparrow},

(49)

where
a^{\dagger},a
take the form (
37
). Some values of
\kappa
are given in table
3
. These indicate that pair propagation (
49
) is strongly dependent on the spin background, and notably vanishes for a polarized system.

The ideal circumstances for pair propagation is on an antiferromagnetic background.
Assuming that
S_{z}=S_{M}^{n}
on
i,k
,
S_{z}=-S_{M}^{n}
on
j
and
\sigma=\sigma^{\prime}=\uparrow
we find

\displaystyle H_{h}\sim-t_{p}c_{j\alpha}^{\dagger}c_{j\alpha}\hat{c}_{i\alpha}\frac{\hat{b}_{i}^{\dagger}}{\sqrt{\hat{n}_{i}+1}}c^{\dagger}_{k\alpha}\frac{\hat{b}_{k}}{\sqrt{\hat{n}_{k}}}

(50)

\displaystyle\text{with}\;t_{p}=\frac{t^{2}}{\Delta_{H}}\kappa_{AF},\;\;\;\kappa_{AF}=1-\frac{1}{n}.

(51)

Here, we obtain two principal contributions corresponding to
i=k
and
i\not=k
. Assuming that the sites are not occupied by other carriers, we can use (
44
). For
i=k
we find

\displaystyle H_{NN}=-t_{p}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle\alpha}c_{j\alpha}^{\dagger}c_{j\alpha}\hat{c}_{i\alpha}c^{\dagger}_{i\alpha}

(52)

which describes a nearest neighbor repulsion with magnitude
t_{p}
. For
i\not=k
we find

\displaystyle H_{p}=-t_{p}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle\langle jk\rangle\alpha}c^{\dagger}_{i\alpha}c_{j\alpha}c_{j\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{k\alpha}\frac{\hat{b}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{i}}{N}

(53)

which describes pair propagation. For opposite spins (i.e.
S_{z}=-S_{M}^{n}
on
i,k
etc) we analogously find

\displaystyle H_{p}=-t_{p}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle\langle jk\rangle\alpha}c^{\dagger}_{i\alpha}c_{j\alpha}c_{j\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{k\alpha}

(54)

by propagating spin down electrons.
We thus obtain two principal contributions from transient excitations across the Hund’s gap in an antiferromagnet: Nearest neighbor repulsion and a form of pair propagation that preserves the magnetic order. The potency of this mechanism diminishes as antiferromagnetic correlations weaken, and vanishes completely for a ferromagnet.

.8
Discussion

In conclusion, we have derived an effective low-energy model of a Hund’s metal near the Mott insulating regime. The undoped system exhibits strong antiferromagnetism. For five bands, quantum spin fluctuations are found to be small, placing the system close to the classical limit (see Appendix II).
The motion of doped carriers depends strongly on the local spin correlations (see Eq.
39
), driving a form of kinetic ferromagnetism that is notably different from other described cases: It does not depend on the interference of paths, but instead emerges at the level of effective hopping. This points to a mechanism far more potent than that of conventional single-band lattice models, which are frequently relatively weak
Brinkman and Rice 1970b
. The emergence of pronounced Nagaoka-type
Nagaoka 1966
ferromagnetism is in good agreement with first-principles studies.

The original Kanamori model is notoriously challenging from a computational perspective, and most existing theoretical results rely on dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT). By contrast, the Hund-projected Kanamori Model derived here offers a far simpler and more tractable framework. Notably, the sign problem is extensive only in the number of mobile carriers, as sign changes arise solely from combined spin and charge motion.
This property enables the use of advanced path-integral techniques-such as the Worm algorithm
Prokof’ev et al. 1998
and suitable diagrammatic methods
Carlström 2021
;
Carlström 2018
;
Carlström 2017
-to study few-carrier problems in detail, allowing direct access to the structure of spin polarons
Blomquist and Carlström 2020
and the effective interactions between them
Blomquist and Carlström 2021
. These observables are generally inaccessible within DMFT.
Our work thus establishes a controlled route from the microscopic Kanamori Hamiltonian to a low-energy theory suitable for high-precision numerical studies.
The resulting model captures the essential physics of Hund’s metals: the intricate interplay between spin correlations, carrier motion, and Hund’s coupling that underlies their anomalous electronic structure.

I. Matrix elements of the spin lowering/raising operator

For the spin lowering operator of the electron
\alpha
, we note that
S_{\alpha}^{-}
must necessarily reduce
S_{z}
by unity. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle P^{H}\hat{S}_{\alpha}^{-}|S_{M},S_{z}\rangle=|S_{M},S_{z}-1\rangle\langle S_{M},S_{z}-1|\hat{S}_{\alpha}^{-}|S_{M},S_{z}\rangle.

(55)

The inner product can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\langle S_{M},S_{M}|(\hat{S}^{+})^{l+1}\hat{S}_{\alpha}^{-}(\hat{S}^{-})^{l}|S_{M},S_{M}\rangle\Pi_{a=0}^{l}\xi_{a}\Pi_{b=0}^{l-1}\xi_{b}

where

\displaystyle\xi_{a}^{-1}=\sqrt{(S_{M}+[S_{M}-a])(S_{M}-[S_{M}-a]+1)}

(57)

are the coefficients for the spin lowering operators with
S_{Z}=S_{M}-a
.
Since
[\hat{S}_{\alpha}^{-},\hat{S}^{-}]=0
we can write

\displaystyle\langle S_{M},S_{M}|(\hat{S}^{+})^{l+1}(\hat{S}^{-})^{l}\hat{S}_{\alpha}^{-}|S_{M},S_{M}\rangle\Pi_{a=0}^{l}\xi_{a}\Pi_{b=0}^{l-1}\xi_{b}

\displaystyle=\langle S_{M},S_{M}|(\hat{S}^{+})^{l+1}(\hat{S}^{-})^{l}|S_{M},S_{M}-1\rangle

\displaystyle\times\langle S_{M},S_{M}-1|\hat{S}_{\alpha}^{-}|S_{M},S_{M}\rangle\Pi_{a=0}^{l}\xi_{a}\Pi_{b=0}^{l-1}\xi_{b}

\displaystyle=\langle S_{M},S_{M}|(\hat{S}^{+})^{l+1}(\hat{S}^{-})^{l+1}|S_{M},S_{M}\rangle

\displaystyle\times\frac{\xi_{0}}{\sqrt{N}}\Pi_{a=0}^{l}\xi_{a}\Pi_{b=0}^{l-1}\xi_{b}=\frac{\xi_{0}\xi_{l}^{-1}}{\sqrt{N}}\equiv\gamma_{S_{M}S_{z}}^{-}

Using
57
and
\xi_{0}=N^{-1/2}=(2S_{M})^{-1/2}
we find the inner product

\displaystyle\gamma_{S_{M}S_{z}}^{-}=\frac{\sqrt{(S_{M}+S_{z})(S_{M}-S_{z}+1)}}{2S_{M}},

\displaystyle\gamma_{S_{M}S_{z}}^{+}=\frac{\sqrt{(S_{M}+S_{z}+1)(S_{M}-S_{z})}}{2S_{M}}

(58)

where the latter follows by symmetry.
Combining (
55
) and (
58
) we conclude

\displaystyle P^{H}\hat{S}_{\alpha}^{\pm}|S_{M},S_{Z}\rangle=\gamma_{S_{M}S_{z}}^{\pm}\hat{S}_{N}^{\pm}|S_{M},S_{Z}\rangle,

(59)

where
\hat{S}_{N}^{\pm}|S,S_{z}\rangle=|S,S_{z}\pm 1\rangle

I.2
II. Holstein-Primakoff mean field theory

As a starting point, we consider the Hund’s projected spin Hamiltonian at half filling

\displaystyle P^{H}H_{S}=I_{T}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\Bigg\{\frac{S_{i}^{-}S_{j}^{+}+S_{j}^{-}S_{i}^{+}}{2N^{2}}+\frac{S_{i}^{z}}{N}\frac{S_{j}^{z}}{N}\Bigg\}

In the next stage, we apply a Holstein-Primakoff type map of the form

\displaystyle S_{i}^{z}=S-a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i},\;\;S_{i}^{+}=a_{i}\sqrt{N},\;\;S_{i}^{-}=a^{\dagger}_{i}\sqrt{N}\;\;\;\;\text{A-lattice}

\displaystyle S_{j}^{z}=b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{j}-S,\;\;S_{j}^{+}=b_{j}^{+}\sqrt{N},\;\;S_{j}^{-}=b_{j}\sqrt{N}\;\;\;\;\text{B-lattice}

Where
S^{\dagger}=a_{i}\sqrt{N}
etc result from the approximation
\langle a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}\rangle
and
\langle b_{i}^{\dagger}b_{i}\rangle\ll N
.
We find

\displaystyle P^{H}H_{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{T}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\Bigg\{\frac{a_{i}^{\dagger}b_{j}^{\dagger}+a_{i}b_{j}}{2N}

(61)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{-S^{2}-a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{j}+S(a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}+b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{j})}{N^{2}}\Bigg\}.

The quartic term is the smallest, and scales as
\sim A_{s}^{2}/N
, where
A_{s}
denotes the spin-wave amplitude, so we proceed to drop it.

Noting that
S=N/2
we find

\displaystyle P^{H}H_{S}\approx I_{T}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\Bigg\{\frac{a_{i}^{\dagger}b_{j}^{\dagger}+a_{i}b_{j}+a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}+b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{j}}{2N}-\frac{1}{4}\Bigg\},

where the last term is the classical energy.
Fourier transforming, the quantum part becomes

\displaystyle H_{\text{Q}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}A_{\mathbf{k}}(a^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}a_{k}+b^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}b_{\mathbf{k}})+B_{\mathbf{k}}(a_{\mathbf{k}}b_{-\mathbf{k}}+a_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}b_{-\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle A_{k}=\frac{zI_{T}}{2N},\;B_{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{zI_{T}}{2N}\gamma_{\mathbf{k}},\;\gamma_{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{1}{z}\sum_{\delta}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\delta}

Adopt a spinor notation
\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}=[a^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}},b_{-\mathbf{k}}],\;\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}=[a_{\mathbf{k}},b_{-\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}]
to obtain

\displaystyle H_{Q}=\sum_{k}\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}h_{\mathbf{k}}\Psi_{\mathbf{k}},\;h_{\mathbf{k}}=(A_{\mathbf{k}}\sigma_{0}+B_{\mathbf{k}}\sigma_{x}).

(63)

The time evolution of an operator
\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}
is given by

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}=[\Psi_{\mathbf{k}},H_{\text{Q}}]=\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{k}}\Psi_{\mathbf{k}},\;\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{k}}=\sigma_{3}h_{\mathbf{k}}.

(64)

The appropriate Bogolyubov transform is

T=\begin{bmatrix}\cosh r&-\sinh r\\
-\sinh r&\cosh r\end{bmatrix},\;r=\frac{1}{2}\arctan\frac{B}{A}.

This gives

\displaystyle T^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{k}}T=\omega_{\mathbf{k}}\sigma_{3}=D_{\mathbf{k}},\;\omega_{\mathbf{k}}=\sqrt{A^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}-B_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}.

(65)

Introducing new operators

\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}=T\Phi_{\mathbf{k}},\;\Phi_{\mathbf{k}}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{\mathbf{k}}\\
\beta_{-\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix},

the time evolution is given by

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\Phi_{\mathbf{k}}=D_{\mathbf{k}}\Phi_{\mathbf{k}}

(66)

implying a Hamiltonian matrix

\displaystyle\tilde{k}_{\mathbf{k}}=\sigma_{3}D_{\mathbf{k}}=\omega_{\mathbf{k}}\sigma_{0}.

(67)

In the ground state, we find

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathbf{k}}|0\rangle=\beta_{\mathbf{k}}|0\rangle=0,

(68)

implying

\displaystyle\langle a_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle=v_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg(\frac{A_{\mathbf{k}}}{\sqrt{A_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}-B_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}}-1\Bigg).

(69)

The total fluctuation is then given by

\displaystyle\langle a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}\rangle=\sum_{k}\langle a_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{1}{2}\Bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\gamma_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}}-1\Bigg).

(70)

On a square/cubic lattice we find

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{1}{2D}\sum_{l=1}^{D}2\cos k_{l}

(71)

where
D
is the dimension. On the square lattice, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle a_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle\approx 0.197.

(72)

This result is independent of the total spin. For a five band model, we thus find that fluctuations are approximately
4\%
in a given band.
