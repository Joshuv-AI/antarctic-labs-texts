Burden and determinants of asymptomatic malaria among adults living in urban and rural areas in Gabon in 2023: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Bridy Chesly Moutombi Ditombi, Noé Patrick M’Bondoukwé, Jacques Mari Ndong Ngomo, Helena Noeline Kono, Bedrich Pongui Ngondza, Meredith Ada Mengome, Luccheri Ndong Akomezoghe, Dimitri Ardin Moussavou Mabika, Coella Joyce Mihindou, Reine Moutongo, Denise Patricia Mawili-Mboumba, Marielle Karine Bouyou-Akotet
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAsymptomatic malaria represents a major challenge for malaria control and elimination efforts, particularly in endemic regions such as Gabon, where adult reservoirs are under-investigated. This study aimed to assess the burden and determinants of asymptomatic Plasmodium falciparum infection among adults in urban and rural communities in Gabon.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional survey was conducted between January and December 2023 in Bitam (rural), Libreville, and Owendo (urban). Adults aged ≥ 18 years with no malaria symptoms or recent antimalarial treatment were included. Demographic, socio-economic, and ITN-use data were collected via structured questionnaire. Malaria was diagnosed by microscopy. Logistic regression models were used to identify factors associated with asymptomatic infection.ResultsAmong 1,496 participants, the overall prevalence of asymptomatic P. falciparum infection was 15.3%, significantly higher in rural areas (22.4%) than in urban settings (4.1%; p < 0.01). Parasite densities were also higher in rural areas. Independent risk factors for asymptomatic malaria included rural residence (aOR: 7.1; 95% CI: [4.4–9.8]), being a worker (aOR: 5.2; 95% CI: [3.4–7.8]), attending school (p < 0.01). ITN use was low (32.7%) and not significantly protective in multivariate analysis.ConclusionThe substantial burden of asymptomatic malaria in adults, particularly in rural Gabon, underscores the need to broaden malaria control strategies. These interventions must be broadened to include adult populations, considering occupational exposure and local transmission dynamics. Expanding screening and treatment and improving ITN access and use are critical to reduce the hidden reservoir and achieve malaria elimination.Keywords: Asymptomatic malaria, Plasmodium falciparum, Adults, Rural–urban disparity, Gabon

Background

Malaria remains a major public health problem in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), with the WHO African Region accounting for an estimated 246 million cases in 2023, representing about 95% of the global burden [1]. Between 2020 and 2024, both malaria cases and deaths increased worldwide, and the epidemiology of the disease has become increasingly complex, with substantial heterogeneity in prevalence, parasite distribution, and persistent challenges in control efforts across regions [2]. While some countries have achieved notable progress, others continue to face obstacles, particularly in areas characterized by dense forest cover, heavy rainfall, and socio-economically disadvantaged populations [3, 4]. Gabon, a malaria-endemic country where Plasmodium falciparum predominates and malaria accounts for up to half of febrile cases among both outpatients and inpatients, illustrates this dynamic. Malaria is widely heterogeneous in the country as revealed by data from the different sentinel sites notably in: Oyem, Libreville, Lambaréné, Makokou and Franceville [5, 6] The introduction of artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) in 2005, together with the scale-up of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) and sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine for intermittent preventive treatment during pregnancy (IPTp-SP), initially resulted in a marked decline in malaria prevalence, from 31.2% in 2005 to 18.3% in 2008 [5, 7]. However, this progress was not sustained, and a rebound was reported in 2011, with prevalence reaching 44% in rural areas and 25% in urban sentinel sites [5]. More recently, in Libreville, malaria prevalence among febrile patients increased from 26.4% in 2018 to 41.4% in 2022, highlighting the persistence of transmission [8]. Moreover, a significant morbidity persists among older children and adults; indeed, age-related differences in infection prevalence are reported, with adolescents being more frequently infected (45.7%) than children under five years (28.6%) and those aged 5–10 years (38.2%) [5, 8].

The epidemiology of malaria is characterized by a significant variability between urban and rural areas [8, 9]. Urbanization, socio-economic conditions, and environmental factors contribute to differing transmission dynamics. For instance, while urban areas may benefit from better access to healthcare and preventive measures, they also face challenges such as increased population density and mobility, which can enhance transmission. Conversely, rural areas often experience higher transmission rates due to limited access to healthcare and preventive resources, and a greater reliance on traditional practices.

Asymptomatic malaria poses a challenge for malaria control and elimination, as P. falciparum asymptomatic carriers represent a non-negligeable parasite reservoir [10]. It is estimated that overall prevalence of asymptomatic malaria in children is 25% in SSA [11]. Such individuals become a reservoir for parasite transmission and may progress to symptomatic malaria. Asymptomatic infections can sustain transmission and have a challenging impact on malaria control efforts [12].

Factors such as the use of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), educational attainment, and occupational status may play a crucial role in influencing the prevalence of asymptomatic malaria. Studies have shown that the use of ITNs can significantly reduce malaria transmission, yet their effectiveness is often inconsistent in urban settings where uptake may be lower [13, 14].

Despite extensive data from East and West Africa, the epidemiology of asymptomatic malaria in Central Africa, including Gabon, remains poorly documented. Moreover, data from adult populations are critically lacking. Such elucidating information may help in the design of more effective and efficient interventions and strategies to reduce the burden of the infection and disease in Gabon.

This study aims to determine the frequency of microscopic-confirmed asymptomatic malaria infection in adults and the associated factors in urban and rural populations in Gabon.

Methods

Study areas and study period

A community based cross-sectional study was conducted in rural and urban areas in Gabon between January and December 2022. The rural site was Bitam city in the north of Gabon in the Woleu-Ntem province about 30 km from the Cameroonian border and near Equatorial Guinea (146 km from Libreville). At Bitam, all villages situated along the forest until the border of Cameroon were investigated, as the population size of each village did not exceed 100 inhabitants. The primary economic activities of the residents consist of agriculture, while hunting and fishing provide the main source of family food.

Two urban sites were selected: Libreville, the capital city of Gabon situated on the borders of the Guinea gulf in West Central Africa and the Komo River; and Owendo city located 20 km from Libreville.

Gabon has an equatorial climate, average temperatures remain relatively constant throughout the year, with an average of 30 °C. Malaria transmission is stable and perennial, with small seasonal fluctuations.

Study population

In the study sites, a non-probability convenience sampling approach was used to recruit participants from both urban and rural settings.

In Libreville, recruitment took place in the same neighbourhood as our previous 2019 survey [15]. Local administrative authorities (chefs de quartiers) were engaged to disseminate information about the study during awareness campaigns conducted by the study team. At the same time, a concomitant stepwise survey on cardiometabolic disease risk factors (CMDRF) included apparently healthy adults (without any clinical symptoms since more than 28 days and with abnormal blood test results) from Libreville and Owendo was ongoing [16]. These apparently healthy adults voluntarily presented themselves at the hospital for malaria screening and questionnaire completion. Participants were therefore self-selected following community sensitization, and no random household selection was performed.

In Bitam, recruitment and enrolment included all villages located near forested areas up to the Cameroonian border. A mobile laboratory was established in the field to perform malaria diagnosis and data collection. The fieldwork team was supported by community health workers, who had been sensitised and trained by the study staff to facilitate community mobilization and participant enrolment.

Sample size calculation

The minimum required sample size was calculated using the following formula: n = (Z2 × p × (1 – p))/d2, where (Z = 1.96) (corresponding to a 95% confidence level), (p) represents the expected prevalence of asymptomatic malaria based on prior data (15% in the urban sites in 2019 [15] and 30% in rural communities in 2024 [9]), and (d) the precision (a 5% margin of error and 95% confidence level). Therefore, the study aimed to recruit at least 200 participants from the urban sites (Libreville and Owendo) and 320 participants from the rural site (Bitam).

These thresholds were considered sufficient to explore the prevalence and potential risk factors of asymptomatic malaria, despite the non-random nature of participant selection.

Inclusion criteria

Apparently healthy, individuals who were permanent resident for at least two years in the study area, aged over 18 years, with no history of antimalarial treatment in the four weeks prior to the surveys were included. Based on the data of the sentinel site that report a history of fever of even 14 days prior to the diagnosis of clinical malaria, those participants with a history of fever during the 15 days prior to the study were not included [6].

Data collection

A short, structured questionnaire was administered to collect demographic and socio-economic data including age, sex, residential area (rural, urban), occupation and educational level. Participants were also asked about their use of ITNs. Peripheral blood samples were collected for malaria detection and haemoglobin measurement.

Biological procedures

Malaria diagnosis

Thick and thin-blood smears were prepared and stained with 10% Giemsa for 15 min. Thick smears were screened for the presence of Plasmodium parasites following the Lambarene’s method as previously described [6]. After air-drying, the stained slides were examined under oil-immersion, to detect and identify Plasmodium parasites. Parasitaemia was expressed as the number of parasites per microlitre of blood (p/µL). A slide was considered negative if no parasite was seen in 100 oil-immersion fields. Quality control was performed by the slide reading by two independents well trained microscopists; the second reader was blinded to the initials results. In case of discrepancies, slides were re-examined by a third reader, and the parasitaemia of the two closest parasitaemia was taken. The case definition of Plasmodium infection was a slide with the confirmed presence of Plasmodium sp.

Haematological parameters measurement

Haemoglobin (Hb) level was measured using a portable analyser ABX HORIBA Micros 60 in rural areas, and in urban areas, by using a Sysmex XN 350 automate analyser.

Definitions

Occupational categories were classified into three main groups for analysis: “worker”, “middle manager” and “unemployed”. The category “worker” referred to individuals engaged in manual or low-skilled labour. In Bitam (rural site), this group consisted almost exclusively of labourers employed in the sole large-scale oil palm plantation of the region (OLAM), the main employer of the surrounding villages. Employment in this setting often involved early morning and late-afternoon outdoor activities in or near forested areas, increasing potential exposure to malaria vectors.

In Libreville (urban site); the “worker” category included security guards, domestic workers, merchants, and similar occupations, many of which require evening or night shifts, thereby increasing exposure to mosquito bites.

The term “middle manager” referred mainly to civil servants or office-based supervisory staff, whose work is predominantly conducted during standard daytime hours, generally involving lower occupation exposure to malaria vectors compared to manual labourers.

The “unemployed” category included students, job seekers, and individuals without a regular income-generating occupation. The potential risk for malaria within this group varied according to daily activities, some which also involved outdoor exposure.

Asymptomatic malaria was defined for a person with no recent history of symptoms and/or signs of malaria, with laboratory confirmation of parasitaemia. Infection was considered asymptomatic when the individuals body temperature was under 37.5 °C at presentation and there was no history of fever within the last 14 days. The WHO criteria used to defined anaemia: haemoglobin (Hb) ≤ 11 g/dL, 10.1–9 g/dL as mild, 8.0–5.0 g/dL as moderate, and < 5.0 g/dL is regarded as severe malaria.

Ethical considerations

This study was part of the CANTAM 3 EPI project (EDCTP- CSA2020NoE-3100) which was reviewed and approved by National Ethic Committee for research (PROTN027/2022/CNE/SG-P). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before their inclusion in the study. Parents or legal guardians signed on behalf of adolescents their gave their assent. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines of Helsinki declaration. Malaria diagnosis was free, and infected patients were treated with artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT).

Data analysis

Data were double entered into Excel Microsoft sheet and analysed using R software. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables were summarised as medians with interquartile ranges [IQR, 25th–75th percentile].Descriptive analyses were conducted to summarize the characteristics of the study population. The prevalence of asymptomatic malaria was determined. Differences in prevalence and parasitaemia levels between urban and rural areas were assessed using Chi-Square Test for categorical variables and Kruskal–Wallis Test or ManWithney Test for continuous variables. Bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to investigate the association with sociodemographic factors and ITN use (independent variables) and asymptomatic microscopic Plasmodium infection (dependant variable). To control for confounders, the independent variables found associated with the dependant variable in the bivariate analysis with a p-value below 0.20 were used for the multivariate logistic regression analysis. Crude (cOR) or adjusted (aOR) odds ratio were presented together with their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of study participants

A total of 1496 participants were enrolled in this study, 965 (64.5%) in rural and 531 (35.5%) in urban sites. Women predominated and made up 60.5% of the participant population. The participant median age was 44 [32–56] years, 62.8% (n = 941/1496) were aged under 50 years. ITNs use the previous night was reported by only 32.7%. Hb levels could be measured in 824 participants, the frequency of anaemia was 22.7% (n = 187/824). The median Hb level was 12.3[11.0–13.3] g/dL without significant difference between rural (12.0[10.6–14.0] g/dL and urban (12.4[11.3–13.4] g/dL) communities (p = 0.24). Among the 187 anaemic individuals 55.1% (n = 103) had moderate anaemia.

Prevalence of asymptomatic malaria

Overall, 238 (15.3%) participants had microscopically confirmed Plasmodium asymptomatic infection. Plasmodium falciparum was the only species identified. Malaria prevalence was significantly higher in rural compared to urban sites (p < 0.01) (Table 1).

Asymptomatic malaria and sociodemographic factors

Last level of school attended was significantly associated with P. falciparum infection, individuals with primary or secondary school level having the highest prevalences (p < 0.01) (Table 1). A trend towards higher prevalence and parasitaemia was also observed in men and individuals under 20 years of age (Table 1). Bivariate analyses showed that rates of asymptomatic malaria varied by education level, employment category, and urban versus rural residence (Table 1).

The multivariate logistic regression analysis confirmed that education, residing area, professional status were risks factors for P. falciparum asymptomatic infection in the study population. Compared to urban community, rural inhabitants had seven times (aOR:7.1[4.4–9.8]; p < 0.01) higher risk of asymptomatic malaria. The risk of malaria was 5 times higher in workers compared to unemployed participants (aOR: 5.2[3.4–7.8]; p < 0.01). It was more than 2 times higher in people with primary school or secondary school (aOR: 2.6[1.6–4.0]; p < 0.01).

Regarding parasite density, it was 20 [14–20] p/µL in participants with a positive blood smear. Parasite densities were universally low, with all values below 1,500 parasites/µL. Median parasitaemia varied significantly with educational level (p = 0.01), being highest among participants with a university education (56 [18–121] p/µl) and lowest among those with no formal education (14 [10–21] p/µl). Rural residents had markedly lower parasite densities (17.5 [14–28] p/µl) compared to urban residents (117 [79–195] p/µl, p < 0.01).

Asymptomatic malaria prevalence according to ITNs use and Hb level

No significant difference was observed between ITN users and non-users (Table 1). The median Hb level was 12.3[11.0–14.0] g/dL in participants with a positive blood smear and 12.3 [11.0–13.6] g/dL in the uninfected ones (p = 0.69). Similarly, the rate of anaemic participants was similar in the presence (21.8%; n = 24/110) or in the absence (22.8%; n = 163/715) of Plasmodium parasitaemia (p = 0.92). There was no difference in parasite density between anaemic and non-anaemic participants (p = 0.78).

Comparison between rural and urban communities

The association between the socio-demographics factors, ITN use, and the rate of positive blood smear was assessed according to the residence area, revealing different risk factors. In urban community, only being unemployed was identified as a significant risk factor for asymptomatic malaria (Table 2). This association remained significant after the multivariate logistic regression (aOR:3.4 IC95%: [1.1–7.1]; p = 0.03).

Discussion

Almost 20 years after implementation of ITN, IPTp-SP and ACT in Gabon, malaria transmission remains high across the country. Data from rural and semi-urban areas report a resurgence of symptomatic malaria cases, which started prior to the COVID-19 pandemic [8, 9] and increased thereafter. This study highlighted a substantial burden of asymptomatic malaria (15.9%) in adults from three communities in Gabon. This prevalence is similar to that reported in a study performed in 2013 in different settlements in Gabon (18.8%), as well as in Uganda (15.4%) in 2020 [20, 21]. Other authors report higher prevalences in Cameroon (38.4%) and Kenya (42%) [22, 23]. This difference could be attributed to the methodology used in the different studies, the study population, and the ecology of sites, as well as the level of malaria endemicity in the different study areas.

The prevalence in the urban community was 4.1% and slightly lower than that reported in 2019 at Owendo (5.9%) [15]. In urban areas, housing infrastructure has improved considerably, with sealed wells and windows fitted with insect screens. These features help reduce exposure to malaria vectors, likely contributing to the lower prevalence of asymptomatic Plasmodium falciparum infection observed in urban populations compared with rural areas. Furthermore, urban dwellers may have better knowledge of malaria risk and prevention. All these factors, including fewer suitable mosquito breeding sites, reduce exposure to the vectors. The lower prevalence of asymptomatic malaria observed in Libreville and Owendo, compared to the rural city of Bitam is consistent with findings across others sub-Saharan Africa. Rural residents were thus more than six times more likely to harbor asymptomatic infection, underscoring a persistent rural transmission intensity. Indeed, urban environments are less favourable to vector species, particularly Anopheles gambiae, which has a strong preference for clean water. The increasing insalubrity and polluted water in the greater Libreville area may be leading to the reduction in the Anopheles population.

A recent meta-analysis reported that the prevalence of asymptomatic malaria among older children in Central Africa is approximately 30% [11]. This pattern reflects the age-related acquisition of partial immunity, whereby adults remain largely asymptomatic but continue to carry parasites at low densities, thus contributing to the hidden reservoir of transmission. Adults therefore represent a significant parasite reservoir in Libreville, most likely due to low ITN usage and more frequent exposure to mosquito bites compared to children. Parasite densities in our study population were universally low (< 1500 parasites/µL), but low-density and submicroscopic infections contribute substantially to ongoing transmission and complicate elimination efforts [24, 25]. These findings emphasize the need to consider adults, often overlooked in malaria prevention programmes, as an important target group for control intervention.

Nevertheless, the risk of infection is highly heterogeneous across the country, with prevalence estimates of ~ 22% in rural Bitam compared to ~ 4% in the urban settings of Libreville and Owendo. Given that around 84% of the Gabonese population lives in urban areas, the overall burden of asymptomatic malaria infections is distributed between both contexts, but rural areas remain a critical target due to their > fivefold higher risk. Moreover, travel between rural and urban areas likely contributes to maintaining the urban reservoir, reinforcing the need for differentiated strategies: intensified ITN coverage and health education for urban adults, and strengthened control measures in rural hotspots.

In rural areas, conditions are favourable for exposure to mosquito bites, so repeated exposure to the bites of infectious mosquitoes carrying malaria parasites makes the host's immune system better able to control parasite density, thus preventing the onset of clinical symptoms [26]. A study carried out in 2008 in several rural villages in Gabon reported an estimated overall prevalence of 6.2%, rising to 37.2% [27]. Although an increase in malaria transmission has been reported across Gabon, a marked heterogeneity in endemicity levels is observed. Recent studies have shown that prevalence ranges from below 20% in urban areas such as Libreville and Franceville to over 40% in rural communities like Makokou and Dienga in the South [8, 28].

Bitam is a hyperendemic zone, with much more vegetation, and the villages surveyed were close to forested areas, a propitious environment for vector multiplication. Indeed, environmental factors such as proximity to forest, household density and altitude tend to be significantly and positively correlated with malaria risk [29]. The high prevalence of asymptomatic malaria in rural areas could be due to exposure-related immunity, which occurs when people frequently exposed to parasites eventually develop premunition [30]. Furthermore, the higher parasitaemia observed in urban residents compared to rural participants despite overall low levels, may also reflect differences in exposure and immunity. In areas of intense transmission, repeated exposure promotes premunition and more efficient parasite control, whereas in low-transmission settings such as Libreville, adults may develop weaker immunity, resulting in higher parasite densities when infected [31–33].These findings underline the importance of considering local epidemiological diversity when designing and implementing malaria control interventions, in the context of ongoing malaria transmission in Gabon.

Sociodemographic factors were independently and differentially associated with asymptomatic malaria carriage in urban and rural communities. Men appeared more frequently infected than women, as previously reported by Kumari et al. in India [12]. This observation likely reflects gender-specific behavioural patterns. While women generally remain at home after work and during the night, men are more likely to engage in late-afternoon or overnight professional and social activities. In urban settings, they often stay outdoors beyond working hours, whereas in rural areas, they may spend the night in the forest for hunting or agricultural activities or gather with other men in the “corps de garde” to socialise late into the night. These behaviours increase their exposure to mosquito bites.

Occupational exposure appears to play a key role in the higher risk of asymptomatic malaria among rural male workers. Bitam, a forested area, is particularly prone to transmission, and stays in the forest for hunting or farming overnight, some well-established risk factors [11, 22, 34]. Additionally, specific occupations such as farming, hunting, guarding, housework, and studying were associated with increased asymptomatic carriage [35, 36]. Interestingly, most unemployed individuals in urban settings were students, whereas in rural Bitam, the unemployed were mainly engaged in informal subsistence activities, including farming or hunting, often in the forest or in farm/bush around their house, thus, further increasing their exposure risk. On this note, individuals with secondary or university education, especially retirees who have returned to settle in villages, were frequently involved in managing their own plantations. These educated individuals often live off the proceeds of their agricultural production and spend long hours working outdoors. This could partly explain the unexpectedly higher prevalence of asymptomatic carriage among those with higher education levels compared to middle managers.

These findings underline the importance of adapting malaria control strategies to behavioural and occupational contexts. Indeed, encouraging workplace intervention and the use of long-sleeved shirts and trousers during outdoor and forest-based activities, especially among men and farmers. Then, promoting ITN distribution and communication campaigns which specifically target adult men and older individuals, not just children and pregnant women. Moreover, tailoring health education to rural retirees and educated populations regarding the continued risk of malaria for retired individuals engaging in agricultural work, even if they consider themselves knowledgeable and community engagement in behavioural by Integrating malaria prevention into rural development schemes should be considered key interventions for malaria control in rural settings. [37, 38].

Despite global efforts to promote ITN use, their protective effect in our study population was limited, likely due to low coverage, improper use, or insecticide resistance. Similar findings have been reported in Nigeria, where ITN use was not associated with asymptomatic P. falciparum carriage, although evidence from Cameroon and elsewhere in sub-Saharan Africa indicates that not using or misusing ITNs increases infection risk by more than threefold [11;39,40]. The condition and longevity of nets were not assessed, but these factors, together with the long interval since the last distribution campaign and disruptions caused by COVID-19, may have reduced their effectiveness. While ITNs remain a cornerstone of malaria prevention, consistent and correct use is crucial. Adults, who often serve as an important asymptomatic reservoir in rural areas, should also be targeted through active screening and education, alongside children and pregnant women, to optimize malaria control and reduce household transmission.

This study has some limitations. The prevalence of asymptomatic plasmodial infection in this study may have been underestimated, as only microscopy was used for the diagnosis. Patients with submicroscopic infections may have been classified as negative, leading to an underestimation of the true prevalence of asymptomatic malaria. The convenience sampling may limit the generalizability and self-reporting of ITN use may introduce reporting bias. This sampling approach was chosen for logistical feasibility, to take advantage of ongoing public health surveys; and to optimise recruitment within the available budget. We acknowledge that convenience sampling may introduce selection bias, potentially affecting representativeness. The study populations may therefore differ from general underlying populations in socio-demographic characteristics, health-seeking behaviours, or malaria exposure risk. Nevertheless, this study is among the few to assess asymptomatic microscopic Plasmodium carriage in non-pregnant adults in endemic Central Africa. Despite this sampling method, we enrolled approximately three times more participants than initially estimated. This was driven by heightened health-seeking behaviour during the COVID-19 period in Libreville, strong community mobilization, free malaria screening, and mobile laboratory support in Bitam, as well as the higher participation of women in both settings. The large sample size reinforces the robustness of our findings and strengthens the validity of our data that highlights important intervention gaps. Targeted strategies for adult populations including manual labourers and the unemployed as well as introduction of community-wide screening using ultra-sensitive RDTs or molecular tools will help clarify the prevalence and risk of asymptomatic infection.

Conclusion

Asymptomatic Plasmodium falciparum carriage is over five times more frequent in Bitam, a rural area, than in the capital city. Gabon’s resurgence in malaria highlights low adult coverage of preventive measures and pronounced rural urban disparities. National strategies must therefore extend beyond clinical case management to include active surveillance and adult-focused prevention. In rural villages, this requires active testing and treatment, strengthened vector control, and targeted prevention, whereas in urban settings, understanding the determinants of asymptomatic infection is essential to guide context-specific interventions.
