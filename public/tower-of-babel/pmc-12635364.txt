Robust speed and levitation control of high-speed trains using TSK type-2 fuzzy sliding mode strategy
Authors: Suleiman Ibrahim Mohammad, Asokan Vasudevan, Ahmad Abu Jrai, N Beemkumar, Ali Khatibi, Abinash Mahapatro, Ashwin Jacob, Laith Hussein Jasim, Rishabh Thakur, Abdolreza Khorasanikia Asl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-speed trains are very sensitive due to their very high speed of movement, so the slightest defect or fault is not acceptable. This sensitivity necessitates the development of advanced and robust control strategies capable of handling dynamic uncertainties, nonlinearities, and external disturbances commonly present in high-speed rail systems. To achieve this, the control system must be very precise and eliminate the smallest errors. In this paper, a very precise nonlinear controller is designed by combining the TSK type-2 fuzzy system with the sliding mode control (SMC) method. The integration leverages the robustness of sliding mode control and the superior uncertainty modeling capability of TSK Type-2 fuzzy logic, aiming to overcome the limitations of conventional SMC and Type-1 fuzzy approaches. The TSK Type-2 fuzzy system can estimate sliding surfaces well and the control system will be very fast and accurate. In the simulation section, an attempt has been made to apply the parameters of a real train in order to evaluate it more accurately with the proposed control system. The results show the high efficiency of the proposed control system so that the RMSE of the control system reaches less than 1%. Compared to other existing control methods, the proposed controller demonstrates significant improvements in tracking accuracy, vibration reduction, and control effort minimization. Theoretical analysis based on Lyapunov stability further confirms the stability and reliability of the closed-loop system.Keywords: Fuzzy system, Lyapunov stability, Sliding mode control, High-Speed trainSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

High-speed Railways (HSR) has brought a transformative change in intercity passenger transport over medium and long distances, providing remarkable benefits such as convenience, punctuality, comfort, low carbon emissions, and sufficient socio-economic benefits for society. By 2020, HSR lines over 52,000 km were in commercial operations worldwide, with 12,000 km additional under construction. It is important to note that at high operating speeds, the airflow around a train is greatly disrupted, leading to a substantial increase in power demand. For example, a train traveling 480 km/h requires about 27 times more power than one operating at 160 km/h. It is partly due to the fact that the air density near the ground is much higher than at cruising altitudes for aircraft (around 35,000 feet), resulting in more aerodynamic resistance and strong vibrations. Despite these challenges, railway operators and manufacturers continue to push for higher speeds, developing innovations that enable ultra-fast travel while preserving passenger comfort. It would be quite easy to achieve such speed if trains could operate on completely straight and level tracks; However, the real-world situation presents obstacles such as gradients and uneven terrain-especially in Europe, where rivers, mountains, and historical railway alignments cut major routes1–3.

Various control methods such as Sliding mode control (SMC)4–6, fuzzy logic control7–10, and artificial neural network-based control methods11–14 have been proposed for non -linear systems performing dynamics similar to HSR systems. One of the major challenges in the transport industry is the design of the active levitation system. In such systems, actuators such as hydraulic, pneumatic, and electro-hydraulic devices such as riding comfort and vibration control accuracy are integrated in parallel with suspension springs. These researches use real -time data from both vehicle chassis (wagon frame) and bogie to implement appropriate control laws. The sliding mode control system is particularly effective in handling uncertainties. Several studies have detected different sliding surface design approaches for the lavitation systems, including optimal methods, model-model-rich strategies, strong designs, integral criteria and other stability-driven techniques15–17.

Under a predetermined desired speed profile, the primary objective of automated train operation (ATO) is to obtain accurate speed tracking. Many automated train control strategies have been proposed in literature. Among them, proportional–integral–derivative (PID) control is widely applied to the ATO system due to its simplicity18. In19, cruise controllers for high-speed trains for two specific traction types are designed using mixed control approaches. Robust adaptive control strategies have been developed to ensure highly accurate speed and position tracking by optimizing power output and braking distribution between vehicles20. Other intelligent control techniques, such as expert systems and reinforcement learning, optimal driving methodologies, model predictive control (MPC), and model-free adaptive control (MFAC), have also been explored21–24. It is worth noting that these methods are primarily time-based, ensuring the desired tracking performance asymptotically as time approaches infinity. However, in practical scenarios - where the train operation is constrained within a finite time interval - the correct tracking cannot be guaranteed. In addition, most train operation control strategies are model-based, which means their performance and reliability depends largely on the accuracy of mathematical models. In fact, the dynamics of the train cannot be captured with complete precision due to the presence of unmodeled dynamics and external disturbances. High-speed trains operate as complex nonlinear systems with high inertia, in open environments and under the influence of external disturbances, which imposes several limitations on controller design. Among the advanced methods, MPC has found wide application in optimal and online control of trains due to its ability to handle multiple constraints and high robustness. However, the strong dependence of these methods on the accuracy of the mathematical model and the sensitivity to communication delays and dynamic uncertainties limit their performance and reliability in real-world conditions. These limitations highlight the necessity of developing more robust control methods25.

Recent studies on active control approaches have also been reported26–28, which further highlight the importance of robust control strategies under uncertain and nonlinear conditions. Also, in many previous studies, linear or simplified nonlinear models have been employed for controller design in order to avoid the complexity of full-scale modeling. For example, in some works, the nonlinear model is linearized around an operating point and mixed H∞​ or decoupling controllers are designed. However, such approaches omit part of the system’s actual dynamics, thus providing only a partial description of the train’s behavior. Even in studies where nonlinear methods are applied, the models are often simplified versions of the full multi-body model; for instance, the position of a single car is described using a second-order differential equation, which captures only a fraction of the overall train dynamics29,30. In high-speed train designs, two main configurations are commonly used: Push-Pull Driving (PPD) and Distributed Driving (DD). Comparative studies have shown that DD trains generally offer superior performance in velocity tracking and disturbance rejection, whereas PPD trains are advantageous in terms of energy savings and lower maintenance costs. However, the velocity tracking problem for PPD trains is more challenging, since they possess larger internal dynamics, and stability of these dynamics must be guaranteed before meaningful tracking can be achieved. In real-world operation, high-speed trains are subject to significant parametric uncertainties and various external disturbances. Passenger load variations lead to considerable changes in the total mass of the train, which is a major source of uncertainty in the control system. Other parametric uncertainties include variations in mechanical resistance coefficients due to environmental conditions and the nonlinear stiffness behavior of couplers. Moreover, external disturbances such as crosswinds, tunnel resistance, ramp resistance, track slope, and curve resistance have a strong impact on stability and ride quality. These challenges make controller design highly demanding and highlight the necessity of robust control methods capable of compensating for such effects31–33.

In this paper, a nonlinear model of a comprehensive train system is first examined, and the sliding mode control method is applied to this system. One of the famous deficiencies of traditional SMC is the Chetoring phenomenon, which is quite low in the proposed approach through the use of a flexible type-2 takgi-sugeno-kant (TSK) fuzzy system. To address the uncertainties of the system in Levitation Control, a comparative control law based on TSK Type − 2 fuzzy system has been developed to estimate the upper range of such uncertainties. It is important to note that Type-1 fuzzy logic assigns a crisp membership value to each input, which limits its ability to capture high levels of uncertainty. In contrast, Type-2 fuzzy logic introduces the concept of the footprint of uncertainty (FOU), enabling it to model uncertainties more effectively. This property makes Type-2 fuzzy systems particularly suitable for high-speed train applications, where operating conditions are influenced by dynamic disturbances and parameter variations. Therefore, the integration of TSK Type-2 fuzzy logic with sliding mode control enhances robustness and ensures better performance than conventional Type-1 approaches.

Previous studies have employed traditional sliding mode control, which suffers from the chattering phenomenon and reduced robustness under high uncertainties. Type-1 fuzzy logic controllers have also been proposed, but their crisp membership values limit the ability to model dynamic uncertainties effectively. In contrast, the proposed adaptive TSK Type-2 fuzzy sliding mode control combines the robustness of sliding mode with the enhanced uncertainty modeling capability of Type-2 fuzzy logic. This integration significantly reduces chattering, improves tracking performance, and ensures better adaptability compared to existing methods. The rest of the paper are organized as follows: First, full nonlinear train model is introduced; Next, the TSK Type − 2 fuzzy system is described in detail; And finally, the proposed control strategy with simulation results is presented to validate the effectiveness of the approach.

Type − 2 fuzzy system

In 1965, Zade introduced the type − 1 fuzzy logic. A decade later, in 1975, he proposed to the Type − 2 fuzzy logic to address some boundaries contained in Type − 1 Fuel System. In Type − 1 fuzzy logic, the degree of membership is represented by an accurate (crisp) number. In contrast, Type − 2 provides a fuzzy number for a degree of fuzzy logic membership, which is better that it is capable of handling high -level uncertainty conditions. When uncertainty in data is such that a single numerical value cannot express the degree of membership sufficiently, a fuzzy number is used instead. Conclusions

In this study, an advanced strategy was proposed for simultaneous control of speed and levitation in high-speed trains, which is based on the integration of TSK Type-2 fuzzy logic with sliding mode control. An accurate nonlinear model of the train system was first developed, and the proposed hybrid controller was designed to resolve major challenges such as strong nonlinearities, parameter uncertainties, unknown airgap velocity, constrained outputs and external disturbances. By incorporating the TSK Type-2 fuzzy logic, the method effectively eliminates one of the major limitations of traditional SMC (accurate determination of sliding surface), increasing adaptability and control accuracy. The primary purpose of this structure was to reduce both control efforts and chattering without compromising stability or tracking accuracy. The simulation results under different levels of uncertainty confirmed the controller’s rapid reactions, high tracking accuracy and the ability to reject strong disturbances. Comparative analyses have shown that the proposed method is much better than Type-1 fuzzy sliding mode controllers and other benchmark techniques, especially in high-uncertainty scenarios. Beyond its direct application in the high-speed train system, the approach can be extended to other advanced transport systems or complex nonlinear control problems, requiring strength, adaptability and low control costs.
