Evaluating multi-source precipitation data for streamflow simulation using the SWAT model in the Alpine Manas River Basin, Northwest China
Authors: Li Tan, Chanjuan Zan, Tie Liu, Jiayu Xiong, Aoxiang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Due to the influence of topography, climate, and other factors, the applicability of precipitation products varies significantly across different regions. Investigating the differences among multi-source precipitation products and their suitability for streamflow simulation in cold and alpine basins not only helps to reveal the advantages and limitations of each product but also plays an important role in improving the accuracy and application of hydrological models. Combining comprehensive indexes for precipitation magnitude and precipitation event detection capability, and the SWAT model, this study compares and analyzes the accuracy of four precipitation products (CMFD, MSWEP, ERA5-LAND, IMERG) in the arid regions of Xinjiang and explores their applicability in streamflow simulation for the alpine Manas River Basin. All four products demonstrate consistent intra-annual precipitation variation patterns. Significant discrepancies emerge specifically during summer. IMERG yields the lowest precipitation estimates, underestimating monthly totals with underestimations reaching approximately 39% to 45.7% in spring and autumn. Conversely, IMERG overestimates precipitation by 6.43% to 35.2% in July and August. Both CMFD and MSWEP underestimate precipitation during the rainy season while overestimating it in the dry season. The annual mean precipitation is overestimated by 4.05% to 6.59%. ERA5-Land exhibits a substantial overall overestimation of approximately 45.6%. CMFD demonstrates optimal overall precipitation performance with higher station correlation and relatively stable behavior across precipitation intensity levels. MSWEP and ERA5-Land exhibit comparable performance, though MSWEP shows smaller daily-scale biases than CMFD. MSWEP-based daily streamflow simulations yielded optimal results, achieving Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE) > 0.65 during both calibration and validation periods with PBIAS < 15%. CMFD and ERA5-Land generated similar streamflow simulations, consistently overestimating streamflow by 12.79 ~ 27.07% in both periods. This study offers valuable references for streamflow simulation and water resource management in data-scarce alpine basins.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27391-1.

Introduction

Precipitation serves as a fundamental driver of the hydrological cycle and a critical meteorological parameter for hydrologic modeling. Accurate spatiotemporal distribution of precipitation is essential for reliably estimating water resources and understanding their mechanisms across hydrological processes1–3. Topography significantly influences precipitation distribution, resulting in high spatiotemporal heterogeneity and discontinuity. This complexity renders precipitation among the most challenging meteorological variables to quantify. Consequently, precise precipitation data are paramount for water resource assessment in topographically complex alpine regions.

Precipitation data acquisition primarily relies on ground-based observations, interpolation-based gridded datasets, weather radar/satellite retrievals, and numerical model simulations. While ground stations provide accurate measurements, streamflow modeling faces significant challenges in high-elevation regions due to sparse station networks and data discontinuity4,5. Gridded precipitation datasets derived from observational data convert station measurements into grid-based formats, enabling point-to-areal precipitation transformation and enhancing spatial representation6. However, considerable errors persist in topographically complex regions. The advent of precipitation meteorological satellites has established remote sensing as a primary tool for global precipitation estimation. This spurred successive missions including Global Precipitation Climatology Project (GPCP) dataset7, Global Satellite Mapping of Precipitation (GSMaP)8, Tropical Rainfall Measuring Mission (TRMM)9, Global Precipitation Measurement (GPM)10. These initiatives have significantly enhanced the spatiotemporal resolution of precipitation products. By the late 20th century, data assimilation techniques emerged to reconstruct high-resolution precipitation fields through integrating spatiotemporally uneven ground observations with physical process simulations and numerical models. These datasets enable optimal forcing for distributed hydrological modeling11. While existing precipitation products exhibit strengths in capturing spatial patterns, their applicability varies substantially in complex-terrain alpine regions. Consequently, accurate understanding of regional hydrological processes and water resource quantification necessitates assessing precipitation product suitability in such alpine environments.

Surface streamflow serves as a critical indicator for regional water resource assessment. Streamflow simulation methodologies primarily bifurcate into process-driven and data-driven models. Data-driven models automatically learn feature relationships between streamflow and input variables, yet lack explicit representation of physical processes within the hydrological cycle12,13. Process-based hydrological models are developed through physically grounded representations of the hydrological cycle. These models integrate interactions among hydrological processes while accounting for basin specific characteristics, including topography, land use, and soil types, to mathematically describe moisture fluxes such as precipitation, evaporation, infiltration, and streamflow generation14–16. Distributed hydrological models, including MIKE SHE, VIC (Variable Infiltration Capacity), SWAT (Soil and Water Assessment Tool), HEC-HMS (Hydrologic Engineering Center’s Hydrologic Modeling System), and JULES (Joint UK Land Environment Simulator), characterize basin spatial heterogeneity through distributed parameters17–21. These represent the most widely implemented process-driven frameworks. The SWAT has achieved global prominence due to its advanced architecture, robust functionality, and computational efficiency. Enhanced accessibility through ArcGIS integration further streamlines its application. Streamflow simulation constitutes one of SWAT’s primary functions. Studies indicate that SWAT exhibits lower accuracy in short-term streamflow simulations compared to long-term modeling22,23. Research in Sweden validated SWAT’s applicability across diverse hydroclimatic and land surface conditions24. Studies in southern Alberta, Canada confirmed its effectiveness in low precipitation basins incorporating snowmelt processes25.streamflowAlthough the SWAT model does not include a glacier module, its ability to incorporate snowmelt and permafrost effects makes it particularly suitable for cold regions in Northwest China26–28. It has been successfully applied in several representative basins, including the upper Heihe River Basin, Manas River Basin, Yarkant River Basin, Urumqi River Basin, and Kaidu River Basin29–33, demonstrating its robustness and applicability in hydrological simulations of alpine basins.

Xinjiang, situated in the core of Central Asia, features a unique three-mountain-flanking-two-basins topography that sustains numerous alpine basins. Snow and glacier melt constitute the primary source of river streamflow. Complex terrain further impedes ground station deployment in Xinjiang’s alpine regions, with existing stations predominantly clustered in plains. Accurate precipitation inputs and efficient streamflow modeling are critical for regional water resource management. Therefore, this study selects the alpine Manas River Basin (MRB) as study area. Four high-spatiotemporal-resolution precipitation products (CMFD, MSWEP, ERA5-Land, and IMERG), undergo comprehensive evaluation across three dimensions including precipitation amount, intensity, and event detection capabilities. Their hydrological responses are subsequently assessed using SWAT modeling in the Manas headwaters. Findings provide theoretical frameworks and data foundations for hydro-meteorological studies in alpine basins.

Materials and methods

Study area

Xinjiang is located in the interior of the Eurasian continent, far from the ocean, with annual precipitation not exceeding 200 mm34–36. The complex topography of three mountains and two basins make it difficult to establish ground meteorological observation stations, resulting in few stations and scarce precipitation data. The alpine Manas River Basin (MRB), located on the northern slope of the central Tianshan Mountains, is bordered by the Taxi River to the east, the Kuitun River to the west, and spans the Tianshan Mountains from north to south. The geographical extent is roughly 43º04’N to 43º58’N and 85º00’E to 86º15’E, with a total area of 5109.3 km2 (Fig. 1). The main source originates from the northern slope of Mount Yilianhabirga in the Tianshan Mountains. The Manas River, with a total length of 324 km, is the longest river on the southern edge of the Gurbantunggut Desert. The average annual streamflow at the basin outlet reaches 12.91 × 108 m3, with elevations ranging from 850 to 5138 m. The KSWT hydrological station, located upstream on the Manas River, is situated at the river outlet (85.95ºE, 43.98ºN, 955 m elevation), serving as a control section where the main and tributary rivers converge. Streamflow in the alpine MRB is primarily composed of snowmelt and precipitation. It is not only a region highly sensitive to global climate change, but also a hotspot for research on water cycle changes.

IMERG

The IMERG precipitation product, which succeeded the Tropical Rainfall Measuring Mission (TRMM) Multi-satellite Precipitation Analysis (TMPA), offers a more extensive coverage, longer time series, and higher accuracy and spatiotemporal resolution compared to its predecessor10. IMERG is the algorithm used to generate the three levels of GPM products and integrates data from all passive microwave instruments aboard the GPM satellites to provide rainfall estimates. The algorithm is designed to calibrate, merge, and interpolate estimates from satellite microwave precipitation measurements, microwave-calibrated infrared estimates, gauge-adjusted precipitation, and other potential precipitation estimates across fine temporal and spatial scales37. For this study, the daily precipitation dataset generated by the IMERG algorithm, obtained from NASA’s Data and Information Services Center (https://disc.gsfc.nasa.gov/datasets/GPM_3IMERGDF_06/), was used.

MSWEP

The MSWEP dataset is a global precipitation product that integrates gauge observations, satellite remote sensing, and reanalysis data. It was developed to deliver more accurate precipitation estimates with high spatiotemporal resolution, addressing limitations in coverage, accuracy, and timeliness associated with single-source datasets38. MSWEP features near-real-time updates with a latency of approximately three hours, making it uniquely suited for operational applications and the rapid detection of recent extreme precipitation events, capabilities not commonly found in other datasets. Since the release of Version 1, the dataset has undergone multiple refinements to improve its accuracy and reliability. MSWEP V2.8, which provides precipitation data at a 3-hour temporal resolution(https://www.gloh2o.org/mswep/), was employed in this study in its daily-aggregated form.

CMFD

The CMFD, developed by the Institute of Tibetan Plateau Research, Chinese Academy of Sciences, is the first high-resolution, long-term reanalysis dataset specifically designed for land surface modeling in China39,40. It integrates international datasets, including Princeton Reanalysis, GLDAS, GEWEX-SRB radiation, and TRMM precipitation, with in situ observations from the China Meteorological Administration (https://data.tpdc.ac.cn/). Its data accuracy lies between ground-based observations and satellite retrievals and is considered superior to existing global reanalysis products over the region. The daily precipitation data was aggregated from 3-hourly records.

ERA5-Land

The ERA5-Land was developed using ECMWF’s land surface model with high-resolution global numerical integration. This dataset assimilates historical meteorological records to optimize the consistency between model outputs and actual observations and was officially released on July 12, 2019 (https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/). Compared to its predecessors, ERA-Interim and ERA5, ERA5-Land offers enhanced spatial resolution, improved from 0.75º and 0.25º to 0.1º, respectively41,42. In this study, daily precipitation was derived by aggregating the original hourly data.

Station data

A total of 37 meteorological stations across Xinjiang were used to collect daily precipitation data (http://data.cma.cn). Among these, 13 stations provided complete data for the period 2000–2018, 21 stations covered the period from January 1, 2000, to August 31, 2014, and 3 stations had data from January 1, 2000, to August 31, 2007 (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table S1). Data from all stations were used for the evaluation of precipitation product performance. Given the spatial distribution and data gaps in the meteorological stations of the MRB, only data from a single station located in the upper basin (ID 51356) were ultimately used as meteorological inputs for hydrological modeling. These data include precipitation, maximum and minimum temperature, wind speed, relative humidity, and sunshine duration. Additionally, streamflow data from the KSWT Hydrological Station (sourced from the Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region Hydrological Bureau) were collected for hydrological modeling, model calibration, and validation in the upstream alpine area. The SWAT model was initialized with a warm-up period from 2000 to 2001, followed by calibration (2002–2012) and validation (2013–2018) periods.

Spatial data

DEM, land use, and soil type data are essential environmental spatial data for forcing the SWAT model (Table 2). DEM data can be used to calculate watershed spatial attributes such as slope, aspect, and slope length, and can also extract parameters related to watershed terrain and hydrological features, such as river networks, basins, and subbasin boundaries, through hydrological analysis algorithms. The DEM data used in this study is sourced from the Geospatial Data Cloud (https://www.gscloud.cn/), with a resolution of 30 m from the ASTER GDEM V2 dataset. Land use and land cover data are crucial surface parameters for delineating HRUs and calculating evapotranspiration in the SWAT model. These data were provided by the Xinjiang Institute of Ecology and Geography, Chinese Academy of Sciences, with a spatial resolution of 30 m. Given the limited human activity and small land use change in the upstream area of the watershed, the land use data used in this study is fixed in 2010. Soil is an important factor affecting watershed model simulations, as surface streamflow processes are closely related to soil type and properties. The soil database used in this study is from the global Harmonized World Soil Database (HWSD) at a scale of 1 km (https://www.fao.org/soils-portal), developed by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO). This database directly provides the physical and chemical soil properties required by the SWAT model, including rock content (ROCK), clay content (CALY), sand content (SAND), silt content (SILT), organic carbon content (SOL_CBN), pH (SOL_PH), calcium carbonate content (SOL_CAL), and electrical conductivity (SOL_EC). Additional properties, such as available water content (SOL_AWC), bulk density (SOL_BD), and saturated hydraulic conductivity (SOL_K), are estimated using the SPAW program developed by the USDA. The erodibility factor (USLE_K) is calculated based on the Williams equation.

Soil and water assessment tool (SWAT)

SWAT is a time-continuous, physically based semi-distributed watershed hydrological model developed by the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) in the early 1990s33. The model divides the watershed into subbasins based on topography, river networks, and hydrological systems to reduce the impact of spatial and temporal variations in land surface and climatic factors on simulation accuracy. The model calculates rainfall, evapotranspiration, surface streamflow, soil infiltration, streamflow, and groundwater based on the water balance, aggregates these processes within subbasins, and routes the flow to tributaries. Finally, the flow is calculated into the main river, providing the output at the watershed outlet43.

Before conducting hydrological modeling, it is necessary to standardize the projection of the input raster data to ensure the accuracy of watershed delineation and calculations. The model generates river networks and nodes based on topography, land use, and soil type within the watershed, delineates subbasins, and defines Hydrological Response Units (HRUs)15,44. The minimum proportion of land use type, soil type, and slope to be ignored within each HRU is set to 3%. The calibration and validation process is based on the daily streamflow sequence from the KSWT hydrological station, located at the outlet of the MRB, for the period 2002 ~ 2018. The SUFI-2 algorithm in SWAT-CUP software is used, with the Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE) coefficient optimized as the calibration objective.

Evaluation metrics

The correlation coefficient (CC), root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and Bias were selected to evaluate the data quality of precipitation products in the arid regions of Xinjiang. The CC represents the degree of linear fit between the gridded data and the precipitation measured at the observation stations. The closer the value is to 1, the stronger the relationship, indicating that the difference between the gridded data and the observed values is smaller, and the model’s performance is better. RMSE is the square root of the error and is used to measure the magnitude of the error between the gridded data and the observed values. A lower RMSE value, closer to 0, indicates higher precision in the gridded data. MAE is defined as the average of the absolute differences between the observed values and the gridded data, reflecting the average absolute error between the two. A smaller MAE value indicates lower error. Bias represents the deviation between the observed values and the gridded data. The closer the Bias value is to 0, the better. To evaluate the performance of the model’s streamflow simulations, four validation metrics were used to compare the simulated results with observed streamflow data. These metrics include the Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE), Coefficient of Determination (R²), Percent Bias (PBIAS), and the Root Mean Square Error to Observation Standard Deviation Ratio (RSR).

The NSE ranges from -∞ to 1, with higher values indicating better model performance. For monthly-scale simulations, results are considered satisfactory when 0.65 < NSE ≤ 0.75, and excellent when NSE > 0.75. The R² ranges from 0 to 1, with values closer to 1 indicating stronger model correlation. An R² value greater than 0.5 is considered acceptable, while values greater than 0.8 are considered excellent. PBIAS represents the systematic bias between simulated and observed values, both in terms of direction and magnitude. Positive values indicate underestimation by the model, while negative values suggest overestimation. The optimal value of PBIAS is 0. For monthly-scale streamflow simulations, results are acceptable when 15% ≤ |PBIAS| < 25%, satisfactory when 10% ≤ |PBIAS| < 15%, and excellent when |PBIAS| ≤ 10%45. RSR normalizes the RMSE using the standard deviation of the observed values, combining error metrics with additional information as suggested by Legates & McCabe46. The optimal value of RSR is 0, corresponding to zero residual variability, while higher values indicate decreasing simulation accuracy. For monthly-scale simulations, an RSR value of 0.6 < RSR ≤ 0.7 is considered acceptable, 0.5 < RSR ≤ 0.6 is considered satisfactory, and RSR ≤ 0.5 reflects excellent model performance. Therefore, for daily-scale streamflow simulations, the evaluation thresholds for NSE, |PBIAS|, and RSR are lower. A value of 0.65 for NSE, 15% for |PBIAS|, and 0.6 for RSR are considered indicative of excellent performance. Results

Evaluation of precipitation product performance in arid regions

Daily precipitation assessment

The evaluation metrics are presented through boxplots and tables, where the boxplots illustrate the distribution of each metric across 37 stations, and the table displays the average values of these metrics. On a daily scale (Fig. 2; Table 4), the CMFD dataset exhibits the highest CC, with an average value of 0.54. The ranking of the datasets in terms of CC is CMFD > MSWEP > ERA5-Land > IMERG. The datasets show similar performances in terms of RMSE and MAE, with CMFD performing the best. The corresponding average RMSE and MAE values for CMFD are 1.64 mm and 0.45 mm, respectively. MSWEP and ERA5-Land perform similarly, with average RMSE values of 1.84 mm and 1.89 mm, and average MAE values of 0.54 mm and 0.66 mm, respectively. IMERG exhibits the largest errors, with average RMSE and MAE values of 2.32 mm and 0.71 mm, respectively. Except for the IMERG dataset, which shows a clear underestimation, the other datasets generally exhibit varying degrees of overestimation. The ranking of the datasets by average bias, from smallest to largest, is as follows: MSWEP < CMFD < IMERG < ERA5-Land. MSWEP and CMFD show similar performance, with bias values of 0.1 and 0.15, respectively. IMERG and ERA5-Land also perform similarly, with bias values of 0.45 and 0.52, respectively. However, the bias distribution for CMFD has the narrowest interquartile range, indicating that this product’s accuracy control is relatively stable. In terms of precipitation event detection ability, CMFD, MSWEP, and ERA5-Land all have average POD values exceeding 0.65, with relatively low false alarm rates. Overall, the CMFD dataset exhibits the best accuracy and precipitation detection capability on a daily scale.

Monthly precipitation assessment

The daily precipitation data was aggregated to the monthly scale for further evaluation of the performance of the four precipitation products. It is important to note that since the POD, FAR, and CSI metrics primarily reflect the precipitation event detection capability on a daily scale, the analysis of the monthly scale improvement rate only considers the performance of CC, RMSE, MAE, and Bias. After aggregating to the monthly scale, the CC of the four precipitation products significantly improved. Both CMFD and ERA5-Land achieved correlation coefficients exceeding 0.9. In terms of RMSE, MAE, and bias, CMFD performed the best, with values of 115.15 mm, 67.69 mm, and 0.039, respectively. MSWEP followed, with a bias of 0.065, while ERA5-Land and IMERG showed comparable performance (Fig. 3). Overall, CMFD performed the best, MSWEP underestimated precipitation in high-value ranges, ERA5-Land generally overestimated precipitation, and IMERG both underestimated and exhibited the most dispersed distribution.

In terms of the total monthly precipitation, the multi-year average monthly precipitation trends for both station observations and the four precipitation products are generally consistent, although discrepancies in precipitation amounts exist (Fig. 4). The CMFD and MSWEP datasets exhibit comparable performance, closely aligning with the observed precipitation. However, an underestimation is evident during the high precipitation months of June to August, with average underestimations of 55.86 mm and 103.76 mm for CMFD and MSWEP, respectively. The most significant underestimations occur in June, with values of 77.1 mm and 135.55 mm, respectively. Conversely, overestimations are typically observed in the low precipitation months of January to April and October to December, with average overestimations of 47.56 mm and 93.24 mm. Notably, MSWEP exhibits substantial overestimation in November and December, with values of 145.52 mm and 215.35 mm, respectively. The ERA5-Land dataset, due to its relatively high FAR on the daily scale, results in overestimation across all 12 months after aggregation to the monthly scale, with an average overestimation of 188.59 mm. The IMERG dataset, characterized by a low POD and high FAR, does not substantially improve the monthly total precipitation estimates. Except for overestimations in July and August (54.46 mm and 216.03 mm, respectively), IMERG consistently underestimates by 20.6 ~ 219.5 mm in the remaining months, which may be attributable to the sensor characteristics of the IMERG data acquisition system.

Evaluation of performance across different precipitation intensities

Xinjiang follows a different precipitation classification standard compared to eastern China. According to the local standard Precipitation Classification Levels49 of the Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, 24-hour precipitation amounts are classified into specific categories (Fig. 5).

From the evaluation metrics of precipitation intensity, all products show the best performance in the 0.1–6 mm/d range. As precipitation intensity increases, the CC decreases to varying degrees (with CMFD showing an improvement at intensities > 48 mm/d, reaching 0.44). RMSE, MAE, and the Bias all increase to varying extents. This trend shows a decline in performance as precipitation becomes more intense, though CMFD remains the most accurate, followed by MSWEP and ERA5-Land, with IMERG performing the least well. Notably, ERA5- Land exhibits a positive bias within the 0.1–6 mm/d range, indicating an overestimation tendency, with a bias of 0.3.

For precipitation event prediction, CMFD, MSWEP, and ERA5-Land perform best for light rain events, with POD values of 0.7, 0.63, and 0.64, respectively. As precipitation intensity rises, POD values decrease. However, CMFD and MSWEP show a recovery in POD for values greater than 48 mm/d, whereas IMERG’s POD drops sharply after daily precipitation exceeds 6.1 mm. The trends in CSI follow those of POD. Regarding the FAR metric, CMFD shows the greatest stability, with an average FAR of 0.64 across all precipitation levels. Even for intensities greater than 48 mm/d, the FAR remains 0.6. In contrast, FAR increases for the other three products as precipitation intensity rises.

Hydrological model parameter sensitivity, calibration and validation

Given the sparse distribution of meteorological stations and data scarcity in the alpine MRB, hydrological modeling, calibration, and validation were performed using meteorological data from the nearby national ground station (ID. 51356) and observed streamflow data from the KSWT hydrological station at the basin outlet. The SWAT model warm-up period was set from 2000 to 2001, with the calibration and validation periods spanning 2002–2012 and 2013–2018, respectively. To ensure consistency between gridded precipitation datasets and the in-situ station data, precipitation values corresponding to the 51,356 station were extracted from the nearest grid cell of each dataset based on the station’s geographic coordinates. This approach has been widely used in regional hydrological studies and provides a consistent comparison between observed and gridded precipitation inputs. It is important to note that after the model was calibrated and validated, the four precipitation products were introduced without further recalibration. This approach avoids potential parameter bias and ensures consistency in evaluating the precipitation inputs, as recalibration could lead to erroneous parameters and unreliable assessment outcomes50.

Due to the large number of parameters involved in the SWAT model, sensitivity analysis is required to reduce the number of parameters for calibration and improve simulation accuracy. This allows for the identification of the key parameters affecting the model’s performance in the alpine MRB. Based on research from the alpine MRB and similar inland river basins, 24 parameters related to streamflow were selected for analysis28,33. Parameter sensitivity analysis and optimal parameter range identification were conducted using the SUFI-2 algorithm within the SWAT-CUP software, with the number of iterations set to 1000. This process determined the top 16 most sensitive parameters and their optimal value ranges (Supplementary Table S2), which were subsequently used for model calibration. The calibrated model was then validated against daily streamflow observations from 2013 to 2018. To better compare the heterogeneous responses of streamflow simulations to multi-source precipitation inputs, the calibration and validation results are comprehensively contrasted in Fig. 6, with detailed findings presented in Sect. 3.3.

Among the 16 most sensitive parameters, apart from the baseflow alpha factor (ALPHA_BF), precipitation and temperature are the most direct meteorological factors influencing the hydrological processes in the MRB. According to the sensitivity ranking, it is evident that the ALPHA_BF, which is closely related to the winter minimum streamflow, exhibits the highest sensitivity due to the basin’s primary reliance on glacier and snowmelt water for replenishment. Following this, the snowmelt base temperature (SMTMP), snowfall temperature (SFTMP), maximum snowmelt factor (SMFMX), temperature laps rate (TLAPS), minimum snowmelt factor (SMFMN), and snowpack temperature lag factor (TIMP) all influence the snowmelt streamflow process, with SMTMP and SFTMP showing particularly high sensitivity. This is consistent with the streamflow characteristics in the basin. It is important to note that precipitation data from meteorological stations in the plain areas do not accurately reflect the precipitation in the mountainous region, leading to a second-highest sensitivity for the precipitation laps rate (PLAPS). Parameters such as the streamflow curve number (CN2), river hydraulic conductivity coefficient (CH_K2), and the Manning’s coefficient for surface flow (OV_N) impact the surface streamflow discharge. The surface streamflow lag time (SURLAG) influences the replenishment time of surface streamflow.

Effectiveness of precipitation products for streamflow simulation

Based on the calibrated and validated hydrological model, new simulations were conducted by driving the model with precipitation data from multiple sources. This approach allowed for a comparative evaluation of the streamflow simulation performance of the different precipitation products. Figure 6 presents the daily streamflow simulation results of the SWAT model driven by ground station precipitation and four different precipitation products in the MRB. Overall, all five precipitation inputs can reproduce the hydrological processes of the basin, although different data sources influence the model outputs to varying degrees. The model driven by station precipitation exhibits an NSE of 0.77, R² of 0.78, RSR of 0.48, and a PBIAS of −2.16% during the calibration period (2002–2012). Although there is slight overestimation, the overall accuracy remains high. During the validation period (2013–2018), the performance indicators remain largely consistent, except for PBIAS, which increases to 8.49%. Despite the underestimation, the model exhibits good stability across different phases and performs excellently.

Under the same model structure and parameter conditions, streamflow simulations driven by precipitation data from different periods exhibit both overestimation and underestimation, further highlighting the significant impact of precipitation variability and uncertainty on streamflow simulation results. In contrast, streamflow simulations driven by MSWEP precipitation show excellent performance during the calibration period, though model accuracy slightly declines during the validation period. The overestimation of precipitation from December 2017 to March 2018 leads to an overestimation of streamflow during the 2018 flood season. Streamflow simulations driven by CMFD and ERA5-LAand show an overestimation of approximately 17% during the calibration period. In the validation period, CMFD’s overestimation increases, while ERA5-Land’s overestimation improves, leading to an overall increase in streamflow simulation accuracy. Meanwhile, the consistent underestimation of precipitation in IMERG data results in a significant underestimation of streamflow, with an average underestimation of 67.16%, an NSE less than 0.3, and an RSR greater than 0.8.

In terms of streamflow simulation across different flow regime (Table 5), the overall performance from 2002 to 2018 showed that the MSWEP precipitation data resulted in a significant improvement over the station-based input, particularly in high flow, which increased by about 50%. In both median and low flow regimes, the improvements were approximately 33.3%, making MSWEP the most optimal precipitation input among the four datasets. Although the ERA5-Land data significantly improved the underestimation of streamflow in high flow (with only a 1% underestimation), it overestimated streamflow by approximately 35% and 30% in the median and low flow regimes, respectively. The CMFD data exhibited similar results to ERA5-Land. In contrast, the IMERG data consistently underestimated streamflow across all three flow regimes, with biases ranging from 65% to 72%.

Discussion

Heterogeneity of multi-source precipitation products

Multi-source precipitation products offer critical support for data-scarce regions, particularly where in situ observations are limited or absent. However, their performance varies considerably across spatial and temporal scales. At the monthly scale, these products typically exhibit stronger correlations and improved detection of precipitation events compared to daily estimates, yet cumulative errors tend to amplify as the temporal scale increases. The discrepancies largely stem from differences in the methodologies used to generate each product. In this study, we systematically evaluated the performance of four widely used precipitation datasets, CMFD, IMERG, MSWEP, and ERA5-Land, over the Xinjiang region of northwestern China. Among them, CMFD consistently outperformed the others across multiple statistical metrics, including CC, RMSE, MAE, Bias, POD, FAR, and CSI. The superior accuracy of CMFD can be attributed to its integration of approximately 2,400 high-density meteorological stations across China, coupled with dynamic fusion techniques and bias correction using multi-source remote sensing data. Although comparative evaluations of multi-source precipitation products remain limited in cold, arid alpine regions, existing studies have reported similar findings. For example, Xu51 demonstrated that CMFD outperforms IMERG in the Yarkant River Basin of Xinjiang, while other recent studies52,53 confirmed CMFD’s superior ability to reproduce precipitation patterns across the Tibetan Plateau compared to alternative products.

Distinguished among global precipitation datasets, MSWEP uniquely incorporates daily rain gauge observations, allowing it to correct for temporal mismatches arising from inconsistent gauge reporting times. This correction markedly reduces timing-related errors and enhances the representation of precipitation frequency distributions54. Its demonstrated capacity to characterize the spatial variability of precipitation across the Tianshan Mountains affirms the promise of multi-source fusion approaches in regions with complex topography55. However, the spatial and temporal distribution of daily gauge observations integrated into the MSWEP dataset remains highly heterogeneous. In data-sparse regions such as parts of Africa, the limited density of ground-based stations shifts the data assimilation weight toward satellite and reanalysis sources. This imbalance can exacerbate inherent systematic biases associated with the underlying ERA5-derived climatological framework, including the underestimation of extreme precipitation events and the overestimation of light rainfall frequency38,56,57. In the arid regions of Xinjiang, despite MSWEP’s integration of 76,747 rain gauge stations globally, the local station density remains sparse, and precipitation is dominated by short-duration convective events. These characteristics pose challenges for MSWEP’s correction strategies, which are primarily based on large-scale climatological patterns and thus struggle to capture the transient nature of localized precipitation. As a result, its performance is somewhat constrained. Nevertheless, MSWEP still achieves accuracy comparable to CMFD and outperforms both IMERG and ERA5-Land products in the region. Similarly, ERA5-Land couples the ERA5 atmospheric reanalysis with the HTESSEL (Hydrology Tiled ECMWF Scheme for Surface Exchanges over Land) land surface model, enabling refined simulations of surface hydrological processes41. Its precipitation estimates are generated through physically based parameterization schemes within the atmospheric model, assimilating both satellite and ground-based observations globally. This confers a degree of physical consistency, particularly in data-scarce regions. ERA5-Land exhibits relatively stable CC distributions and achieves values around 0.4 at the daily scale, indicating moderate detection capability—second only to CMFD. However, the simplified nature of its parameterization schemes can lead to unstable error patterns and a higher propensity for precipitation misclassification.

Although IMERG v06 enhances reliability in high-latitude regions through the integration of microwave and infrared observations and correction with GPCC gauge data58, its applicability in China’s inland arid regions, particularly Xinjiang, remains limited. The absence of ground-based radar observations in these areas, coupled with complex environmental conditions, introduces substantial biases and constrains the dataset’s performance59. Multiple studies have shown that IMERG exhibits systematic biases in the Xinjiang, characterized by a paradoxical overestimation of light precipitation and underestimation of heavy precipitation events60. Most bias evaluations of IMERG indicate a global overestimation of light precipitation events (< 5 mm/day). In Xinjiang, where approximately 90% of precipitation events fall within this category, the associated biases can reach 10% to 25%, with the largest systematic errors occurring in arid desert regions61,62. Further studies reveal a pronounced seasonal dependency in IMERG’s accuracy. In winter, solid-phase precipitation introduces substantial retrieval uncertainties due to the difficulty of distinguishing microwave signals from atmospheric ice particles and snow-covered surfaces, both of which exhibit similar scattering characteristics. These cold-season errors underscore the limited adaptability of satellite retrieval algorithms in arid regions, where precipitation intensity, phase, and complex terrain interact in tightly coupled ways63–65. Some studies have also pointed out that IMERG data suffer from the absence of a robust daily-scale precipitation calibration algorithm66, limited ground-based radar coverage that undermines the reliability of cloud and precipitation profile databases, and poor retrieval performance of passive microwave remote sensing in winter and high-latitude regions42,67. Moreover, the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of ground-based meteorological stations in Xinjiang may compromise the representativeness of validation data. This uneven distribution can also lead to spatial imbalances in the GPCC correction weights, thereby weakening the effectiveness of IMERG’s localized calibration. As a result, the intrinsic systematic biases of the algorithm, such as the pronounced clustering of pixel-level errors in desert regions, may be further amplified. These findings are broadly consistent with the evaluation results of this study, in which most validation stations, located predominantly in plains, showed larger daily-scale biases for IMERG compared to the other three products. Overall, the performance of IMERG in Xinjiang highlights the common challenges faced by global satellite-based precipitation products in arid regions. While algorithmic improvements can mitigate specific errors, such as those related to alpine biases, persistent obstacles remain, including signal interference over complex terrain, uncertainties in physically retrieving precipitation phase transitions, and the limitations of calibration in data-sparse regions. These challenges may be progressively addressed through the deep integration of multi-source data and regionally adaptive algorithm frameworks.

Impact of different precipitation inputs on streamflow simulation

Precipitation is a key input variable in hydrological models and often represents the largest source of uncertainty among input parameters. The accuracy of precipitation data directly affects the reliability and precision of hydrological simulations. While ground-based gauge observations are generally considered the most reliable source of precipitation data, their utility is often constrained by sparse and uneven spatial coverage, limited temporal resolution, and challenges in long-term maintenance, factors that may compromise data quality and consistency.

In inland river basins, most ground-based meteorological stations are in plains, resulting in limited observational coverage of mountainous areas. Consequently, streamflow simulations in such regions often require the use of large PLAPS in models like SWAT to compensate for higher precipitation in mountainous zones. Gridded precipitation products offer broad spatial coverage and long-term temporal records, but their suitability varies across regions, especially in areas with complex terrain. Strong performance in precipitation event detection does not necessarily translate into effectiveness in hydrological modeling68. For instance, although the CHIRPS product generally performs poorly in streamflow simulations across arid regions, it yielded the best results in hydrological modeling for the Kaidu River basin and demonstrated high accuracy in precipitation assessments69. In the Jing River basin, MSWEP ranked third in precipitation evaluation and showed consistent performance by also ranking third in streamflow simulation, indicating its reliability as a precipitation input for hydrological applications in this region. By contrast, ERA5-Land performed comparably to MSWEP in precipitation evaluation but exhibited a marked drop in performance during hydrological simulation. Interestingly, IMERG showed only moderate performance in the precipitation assessment phase, yet ranked second in streamflow modeling performance68. Therefore, precipitation datasets should be systematically evaluated prior to their application in hydrological modeling, as regional differences in performance can significantly affect simulation outcomes. In the Li River basin, CMFD precipitation data demonstrated strong agreement with in-situ observations across daily, monthly, and annual scales, and yielded the best overall performance in SWAT-based streamflow simulations70. The MSWEP dataset has been recognized as a reliable precipitation source for hydro-meteorological studies in China71, whereas ERA5 often exhibits precipitation overestimation72. With improved spatial resolution (0.1º), ERA5-Land outperformed CMFD in streamflow simulations over the Nyang River basin on the Tibetan Plateau51. These findings are consistent with the results of this study, in which CMFD showed the best precipitation accuracy in the mountainous MRB and effectively captured the basin’s hydrological processes. Nevertheless, its residual biases warrant further attention, and appropriate corrections could enhance its suitability for regional hydro-meteorological applications. Notably, MSWEP and ERA5-Land, despite ranking slightly behind CMFD in precipitation accuracy, outperformed CMFD in streamflow simulation, underscoring the need to assess both meteorological and hydrological utility when selecting precipitation products.

Uncertainty analysis of streamflow simulation

Uncertainty in streamflow simulation using hydrological models primarily arises from model inputs, model parameters, and model structure. Distributed physical-based models are built upon GIS (Geographic Information System) and RS (remote sensing) technologies, enabling the incorporation of a wide range of watershed characteristics, such as topography, land use, soil type, and vegetation cover, derived from remotely sensed data. These spatially distributed inputs allow the model to more accurately capture the heterogeneity of the underlying surface conditions within the basin. Meteorological data serve as critical input parameters for hydrological models. Among these, precipitation exerts a far greater influence on hydrological prediction uncertainty compared to temperature, as uncertainties in streamflow simulations are more strongly correlated with the overall uncertainty in precipitation estimates73. Due to the lack of ground-based observation stations within alpine regions of the basin, precipitation products remain subject to significant uncertainty. This leads to discrepancies between simulated and observed streamflow peak values. To compensate for underestimated precipitation in alpine regions, the model often requires the use of a relatively high PLAPS. In addition, uncertainty in model parameters also plays a critical role in influencing the accuracy of streamflow simulation. Sensitivity analysis revealed that streamflow simulations in the alpine region of the MRB are most sensitive to the ALPHA_BF and the PLAPS, both showing statistically significant influence (p < 0.05). These parameters highlight the critical role of groundwater contribution and precipitation distribution in shaping streamflow dynamics. Secondary sensitivity was observed for SMTMP and SFTMP, underscoring the importance of snow-related processes in hydrological modeling within alpine catchments. These parameters, to varying degrees, reflect the inherent characteristics of the watershed and its differential responses to climate variability. However, they are often difficult to estimate accurately in practical applications. Moreover, due to the heterogeneity of underlying surface conditions, the optimal parameter values may vary significantly across different sub-basins, further complicating model calibration and introducing additional uncertainty into hydrological simulations74,75. A common solution to address this issue is the use of SWAT-CUP for parameter optimization. However, in alpine basins, the lack of sufficient observational data remains a significant challenge, limiting the effectiveness of parameter calibration and hindering model performance in these regions4,15,76. Therefore, parameter transfer approaches are often employed across different subbasins within the same basin to ensure consistency in model application and to reduce the complexity and workload associated with individually calibrating each subbasin44. However, these approaches can also introduce additional uncertainty into the simulation results due to potential mismatches in subbasin characteristics. The SWAT model employs the degree-day factor method to simulate snowmelt streamflow, offering a computationally simple and efficient approach. In the high-altitude mountainous regions of northwestern China, where the climate is cold and precipitation are sparse, streamflow is influenced not only by temperature but also by a combination of factors including complex terrain, climate variability, and solar radiation. As a result, streamflow simulations based on the degree-day factor method are subject to considerable inaccuracies in such regions32. Although energy- and mass-balance snowmelt models offer a more physically realistic alternative, they are inherently more complex and require a greater number of input parameters. This complexity not only increases uncertainty in the simulation process, but also poses challenges due to the current limitations in observational and experimental capabilities, which are insufficient to fully support physically based distributed snowmelt streamflow modeling32,77,78. Therefore, future improvements in the accuracy and reliability of precipitation-driven streamflow simulations may be achieved through enhanced precipitation monitoring networks, refined parameter estimation techniques, and continuous advancement in model structures and algorithms79.

Conclusion

Given the scarcity of available observation stations in the alpine Manas River basin, this study first conducts a comprehensive evaluation of four high-spatiotemporal-resolution precipitation products (CMFD, MSWEP, ERA5-Land, IMERG) across Xinjiang’s arid zone, focusing on precipitation event detection capability, total precipitation accuracy, intra-annual distribution patterns. Subsequently, SWAT-based hydrological modeling quantifies the adaptability of multi-source precipitation products for streamflow simulation. This work establishes fundamental benchmarks for hydro-meteorological research in data-scarce alpine basins. The specific conclusions are as follows: Among the four precipitation products, CMFD is the best performing dataset in daily-scale precipitation evaluation. It has the best fit with ground observation data (CC = 0.54), the smallest RMSE (1.64 mm), the smallest MAE (0.45 mm), a slight overestimation (4.05%), and good precipitation detection ability (POD = 0.74). MSWEP and ERA5-Land perform second best, with MSWEP’s bias being 33.3% better than CMFD. ERA5-LAND overall overestimates precipitation by 45.59%. IMERG performs poorly on a daily scale, with overestimation for light precipitation and underestimation for heavy precipitation. Its POD is only 0.28, while its FAR is as high as 0.76, and its CSI is only 0.13. On a monthly scale, the results are like those on the daily scale. CMFD and MSWEP both show underestimation during the rainy season and overestimation during the non-rainy season, but with the smallest biases of 0.039 and 0.065, respectively. ERA5-Land overestimates precipitation in every month but has the highest CC like CMFD, at 0.90 and 0.92, respectively. CMFD also demonstrates the most stable performance across different precipitation intensities, which is related to the integration of CMFD with China’s ground station precipitation data. When driving the SWAT model for the alpine MRB, the simulated streamflow reflects the seasonal distribution characteristics of the observed streamflow. MSWEP and ERA5-LAND also perform better than CMFD, with NSE values greater than 0.65 and RSR values less than 0.6. IMERG, however, has insufficient ability to simulate daily streamflow, underestimating streamflow by 65.44% to 68.87%, and also underestimating baseflow.

These findings demonstrate substantial disparities in precipitation product performance for streamflow modeling across arid and alpine regions. Crucially, meteorological superiority does not guarantee hydrological efficacy. While CMFD excels in precipitation representation, MSWEP and ERA5-Land achieve superior streamflow simulation accuracy in MRB. This divergence underscores the critical importance of product-specific calibration for hydrological applications.
