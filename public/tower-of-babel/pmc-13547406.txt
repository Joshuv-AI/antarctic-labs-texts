Biosynthetic mechanism and ecological function of chirality in Solanum steroidal alkaloids
Authors: Yang Gao, Wei Song, Xuan Zhang, Wenfeng Ye, Yibin Lin, Junyu Ma, Ying Fu, Boyang Wang, Wenxian Lan, Xiaoyan Xu, Weijie Huang, Chen Lin, Ian T Baldwin, José Maurício S Bento, Zheyong Xue, Jiancai Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChirality fundamentally influences the bioactivity and ecological roles of plant natural products. Steroidal glycoalkaloids (SGAs) in tomato and wild relatives exhibit this stereochemical variation. Forward genetics identifies C26 hydroxylase GAME8 as the key enzyme controlling SGA chirality. Cultivated tomato harbors two identical GAME8 copies yielding S-configured intermediates, whereas Solanum pennellii homologs GAME8a and GAME8b dominantly produce R- and S-configured products, respectively. Site-saturation mutagenesis and molecular docking reveal four residues sufficient to switch this chirality. Bioassays demonstrate that R-type SGAs, predominant in wild tomato, offer superior defense against the specialist pest Phthorimaea absoluta, although chirality does not affect resistance to the generalist Spodoptera litura. Collectively, these results elucidate the biosynthetic mechanism and ecological significance of SGA chirality, establishing a basis for tailoring phytochemical stereochemistry to enhance crop protection.Subject terms: Plant ecology, Secondary metabolism, Natural variation in plants

Introduction

Two molecules are stereoisomers if they share the same elemental composition and atomic connectivity but differ in their spatial arrangement of atoms1. Chirality typically arises in stereoisomers possessing an asymmetric center, such as a carbon atom with four distinct substituents2. Chiral molecules are ubiquitous in living organisms; for example, all ribosomal peptides are synthesized using L-configured amino acids, not D-configuration3. The vast majority of biologically active secondary metabolites produced by plants, including alkaloids4, terpenoids5 and flavonoids6, occur as specific stereoisomers or mixtures thereof. Chirality is fundamental to the biological function, ecological interactions, and pharmacological activity of these molecules. Enantiomers often exhibit strikingly different interactions with biological targets, such as enzymes, receptors, and transporters, leading to profound differences in odor, taste, toxicity, therapeutic efficacy, and ecological consequences7. Gossypol, an axially chiral mixture of (−)-gossypol and (+)-gossypol that accumulates in cottonseed, is an example: (−)-gossypol has specific antitumor and antispermatogenic activity in humans, while (+)-gossypol is primarily toxic to insect pests8,9. Although the therapeutic activities of many chiral plant natural products have been thoroughly studied, the ecological consequences for the producing plants remain surprisingly unknown, even though these consequences should shape the evolution of chirality in nature.

Chirality is not an incidental by product of natural products biosynthesis, but is precisely regulated by the plants’ enzymatic machinery. The production of each enantiomer varies among organs of a single organism or across species within the same genus or family, and results from differences in enzyme sequence, expression levels or interactions among associated proteins10. The conformational distinction between R- and S-configured norcoclaurine, a benzylisoquinoline alkaloid, lies in the pyridine ring formed during dopamine and 4-hydroxyphenylacetaldehyde condensation catalyzed by norcoclaurine synthase (NCS)11. Substituting even one of the three key amino acid residues surrounding the catalytic pocket will convert S-enantiospecific NCS in lotus (Nelumbo nucifera) to an R-selective enzyme12. Through sequential modifications, S-norcoclaurine is converted to S-reticuline, the central intermediate in the biosynthesis of benzylisoquinoline alkaloid13. Subsequent cytochrome P450 and aldo-keto reductase catalyze S-to-R epimerization of reticuline, which together form an enzyme system termed reticuline epimerase14. Beyond the stereospecific biosynthetic enzymes and epimerases, dirigent proteins provide an alternative mechanism for generating asymmetric products (e.g. phenylpropanoids and gossypol)9,15. Quantitative trait locus (QTL) analysis is a robust forward genetic approach frequently used for deciphering biosynthetic genes or regulatory elements of plant natural products16. However, it has been rarely used to elucidate the genetic controls of natural products chirality.

Steroidal glycoalkaloids (SGAs) are a class of specialized metabolites found in Solanum plants, including tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), potato (Solanum tuberosum), eggplant (Solanum melongena) and their wild relatives, largely containing a nitrogenous steroidal aglycone and decorated with glycoside residues17. The biosynthesis of SGA starts from cholesterol, involving subsequent steps of oxidation, transamination, cyclization, reduction, and a series of glycosylation reactions18–20. These processes are controlled by a class of genes dubbed GLYCOALKALOID METABOLISM (GAME). SGAs have long been thought to function as chemical defenses against a broad range of plant pathogens and insect herbivores21,22, as well as antinutritional factors with anti-cancer and anti-inflammatory activities23,24. Mutation of an essential scaffold protein in the SGA biosynthesis pathway, namely GAME15, dramatically decreases pest resistance in potato and black nightshade (Solanum nigrum)25,26. Moreover, simply by adding an extra glucose or xylose moiety to the trisaccharide SGA backbone can greatly increase the resistance of potato to pathogens and insect pests27.

Beyond the highly diverse glycoside decorations, the aglycone also contributes to SGA diversity, mainly through isomerism of the F-ring. A predicted F-ring enantiomer of α-tomatine has been identified from tomato and named as filotomatine28, but its precise structure, generation mechanism, and bioactivity remain unclear. While numerous of GAME genes have been characterized and SGAs can be ectopically synthesized in Nicotiana benthamiana, the controlling determinant of SGA chirality remains unknown. The F-ring is proposed to form spontaneously from a 26-amino-furostanol intermediate20, and it has been hypothesized that the F-ring can re-open under the relatively low pH conditions of tomato cells, leading to α-tomatine and filotomatine interconversions29. However, this pH-dependent mechanism lacks experimental validation.

In this study, we found that the SGAs in the leaves of the pest-resistant wild tomato Solanum pennellii are dominated by an isomer of α-tomatine, identified as filotomatine. Using a population of backcross inbred lines (BILs) derived from S. pennellii and S. lycopersicum, we identified the gene responsible for determining whether α-tomatine or filotomatine is produced. Amino acid mutagenesis combined with enzyme catalytic assays and molecular docking, elucidated the catalytic mechanism responsible for F-ring epimerization in SGAs. Finally, using mutants of different biosynthetic genes that produce specific isomers, we tested the defensive function of the two enantiomers against two major pests of tomato, a specialist Phthorimaea absoluta and a generalist Spodoptera litura.

Results

QTL mapping of the causal gene of stereodiversity of SGAs in wild tomato

Wild tomatoes serve as a valuable genetic resource for crop breeding owing to their adaptation to diverse biotic and abiotic stresses30. To identify the metabolic basis of these versatile adaptations, we profiled the leaf metabolites of cultivated tomato (M82) and wild tomato Solanum pennellii (LA0716), with particular attention to the SGAs, the best-studied class of specialized metabolites in Solanum genus. Consistent with previous reports31, α-tomatine was the dominant SGA in M82, with minor amounts of its isomer, filotomatine. In contrast, filotomatine dominated the SGA profile of S. pennellii leaves (Fig. 1A). MS² spectra analysis confirmed identical fragmentation patterns for α-tomatine and filotomatine (Supplementary Fig. 1). Filotomatine was first reported and named by Cataldi et al.28, who used multistage mass spectrometry for its characterization and predicted a shared tetrasaccharide with α-tomatine but with soladulcidine as the aglycone. To determine filotomatine’s precise structure, we purified the compound from S. pennellii leaves and performed nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis. The NMR results confirmed the predictions that filotomatine’s aglycone is soladulcidine, with an F-ring configuration of 22 R,25 R – distinct from the 22S,25S configuration in α-tomatine (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Fig. 2 and Table 1), and that the C-3 tetrasaccharide was identical in both compounds. Notably, while filotomatine content is substantially higher in S. pennellii than in cultivated tomato, total SGA levels in S. pennellii were only about one-fourth of those in cultivated tomato (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Tissue-specific analysis revealed that filotomatine accumulation is highly specific to S. pennellii leaves; other S. pennellii tissues (roots, flowers and fruits) and all tested tissues of cultivated tomato were dominated by α-tomatine (Supplementary Fig. 3).

We sought to identify the causal gene responsible for the two SGA isomers in tomato. To this end, we compared the ratios of α-tomatine and filotomatine in the leaves of introgression lines (ILs), each containing a single chromosomal segment of S. pennellii introgressed into the M82 background32. IL6-2 was the only tested line exhibiting a chemical profile similar to that of S. pennellii (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. 4). To more precisely map the causal gene within the IL6-2 chromosomal segment, we utilized BILs generated by crossing S. pennellii and M8233. This analysis delimited the causal gene to a 3.28 Mb region (Fig. 1C). This region harbors two GAME8 genes, previously reported to control the hydroxylation at C-26 in the SGA biosynthetic pathway18, designated as GAME8a and GAME8b. The coding sequences of SpGAME8a (Sopen06g021190) and SpGAME8b (Sopen06g021210) shares 98.87% identity, whereas the sequences of SlGAME8a (Solyc06g061023) and SlGAME8b (Solyc06g061027) are identical and are hereafter designated SlGAME8 (Fig. 1C).

Hydroxylation of the C26 methyl group into a hydroxymethyl moiety by GAME8 generates four distinct substituents on C-25 (CH2OH, CH3, H and cholesterol backbone), thereby introducing a chiral center at this carbon. Theoretically, the GAME8 reaction product could exist as enantiomers, either the 25S or 25R oxidation product. It is plausible that this chirality is maintained through subsequent steps in F-ring biosynthesis, ultimately determining whether plants accumulate α-tomatine or filotomatine (Supplementary Fig. 5). Furthermore, the expression profiles of SpGAME8a and SpGAME8b correlated well with the ratio of α-tomatine to filotomatine across different tissues in S. pennellii and the BIL6676 that harbors SpGAME8a and SpGAME8b. Specifically, SpGAME8a transcript abundance was higher than SpGAME8b in leaves, which accumulate higher levels of filotomatine. Conversely, SpGAME8a expression was lower than SpGAME8b in other tissues dominated by α-tomatine accumulation, such as roots, flowers, and fruits (Fig. 1D, E and Supplementary Fig. 6). In contrast, cultivated tomato harbors only one type of GAME8 and is dominated by α-tomatine in all the tested tissues (Supplementary Fig. 3). Collectively, these results indicate that different GAME8 homologues control the stereodiversity of the F-ring in SGAs, with SpGAME8a producing filotomatine, and SpGAME8b and SlGAME8 producing α-tomatine.

GAME8 is responsible for the stereodiversity of F-rings in SGAs

To test whether GAME8 is responsible for the differential accumulations of α-tomatine and filotomatine, we performed heterologous expression experiments in Nicotiana benthamiana. First, we co-expressed GAME6, the first committed biosynthetic gene for SGAs, and its scaffold protein GAME15 with our previously developed high-efficiency cholesterol synthesis module (CSG module). This CSG module comprises ten cholesterol biosynthetic genes from the medicinal plant Paris polyphylla34 (Supplementary Fig. 7). Previously identified P. polyphylla cytochrome P450 enzymes, PpCYP90B52 (producing 22S-hydroxycholesterol)35 and PpCYP90B27 (producing 22R-hydroxycholesterol)36, were used as standards to characterize the stereochemistry of hydroxycholesterol aglycone. GC-MS analysis of the alkaline hydrolysis products revealed that both S. pennellii and cultivated tomato GAME6 orthologs exclusively synthesized 22S-hydroxycholesterol, with no detectable 22R epimer (Supplementary Fig. 8). Consistent with the recently reported glucuronosyltransferase activity of GAME1525, LC-MS analysis of the products indicated that the dominant product in N. benthamiana leaves was hydroxycholesterol glucuronide (Supplementary Fig. 9).

To determine the stereochemical configuration of the resulting 22,26-dihydroxycholesterol, GAME8s from S. pennellii were individually co-expressed in N. benthamiana with a class of genes including CSG module, GAME6, and GAME15, which named as Comb1. The dominant product in Comb1 heterologously expressed N. benthamiana leaves was 22,26-dihydroxycholesterol glucuronide (Supplementary Fig. 10). The MS2 spectra of 22,26-dihydroxycholesterol glucuronides produced by SpGAME8a and SpGAME8b were identical in both positive and negative ion modes, yet exhibited distinctly different retention times in liquid chromatography, indicating the stereoisomeric differences. To further verify the product identity, we acid-hydrolyzed the samples and obtained a pair of peaks whose exact mass and MS2 fragmentation matched those of 22,26-dihydroxycholesterol, with retention times differing between SpGAME8a and SpGAME8b products (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. 11A–C). The 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis of these two compounds showed the distinct chemical shift of the proton signal for C-26 (Supplementary Fig. 11D), indicating the different configuration at the C25 position. However, due to the lack of reference NMR spectra for either chiral form, we were unable to unambiguously assign the product to a specific isoform. Together, these data are consistent with the inference that SpGAME8a and SpGAME8b directly govern the production of 25R- or 25S-type of 22S,26-dihydroxycholesterol.

We designed a module (Comb2) comprising downstream GAME genes, including GAME11, GAME4, GAME12, GAME25 and S5αR2 which sequentially catalyze C16 hydroxylation, C26 oxidation18, transamination20, dehydrogenation19 and reduction37, to finally convert 22,26-dihydroxycholesterol into the SGA aglycone. GAME8 variants were individually co-expressed with Comb2 in N. benthamiana. Metabolite profiling revealed that the expression of SlGAME8 or SpGAME8b led to the accumulation of the S-type SGA aglycone (tomatidine), whereas SpGAME8a expression resulted in the R-type aglycone (soladulcidine) (Fig. 2C). Four UDP-glycosyltransferases (GAME1, GAME17, GAME18 and GAME2-NEW38), which were previously reported to control SGA aglycone glycosylation, were co-expressed with upstream genes (collectively referred as Comb3) to assess the chirality of the final products. The results showed that SpGAME8a decides the yield of filotomatine, whereas SpGAME8b or SlGAME8 decides the yield of α-tomatine (Fig. 2D).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the filotomatine-accumulating phenotype in S. pennellii is attributed to SpGAME8a, and that the single type of SlGAME8s is responsible for the α-tomatine-accumulating phenotype in cultivated tomato.

The mechanism of GAME8 charging SGA chirality

Considering the high sequence identity of GAME8 between S. pennellii and cultivated tomato, we propose that GAME8 serves as an excellent model for elucidating the mechanism by which catalytic enzymes generate chirality. The amino acid sequence comparison revealed that only seven divergent residues differ consistently between SpGAME8a (25R stereoselectivity) and SpGAME8b/SlGAME8 (25S stereoselectivity), including one located within the transmembrane domain, which is unlikely to affect enzymatic activity (Fig. 3A). We then conducted site-directed mutagenesis of individual or combinations of the different residues within the SpGAME8a background, followed by in vivo enzyme activity assays in N. benthamiana. Assays of single-point mutants revealed that variants V33I and R144K did not affect the stereospecificity of SpGAME8a, maintaining a ratio of downstream product soladulcidine (aglycone of filotomatine) to tomatidine (aglycone of α-tomatine) approximately 80:20 (Fig. 3B). In contrast, substituting any of the F309L, I374L, S398F, or T401I residues significantly increased the relative abundance of tomatidine. I374L contributed the most, shifting the soladulcidine to tomatidine ratio to 49:51 (Fig. 3B). Combinatorial mutagenesis of these four functional residues (F309L/ I374L/ S398F/ T401I) demonstrated a clear dose effect. The quadruple mutant F309L/ I374L/ S398F/ T401I produced a proportion of the two enantiomers similar to that produced by SpGAME8b and SlGAME8 (Fig. 3B). To test whether the background sequence of SpGAME8a influences enzyme stereospecificity, we performed analogous mutagenesis within the SpGAME8b and SlGAME8 backgrounds. Consistently, the results showed that L374I alone contributed about half of the stereospecificity effect, and mutating all four key residues L309F/ L374I/ F398S/ I401T could convert a tomatidine-determining GAME8 enzyme into a soladulcidine-determining enzyme (Fig. 3C, 3D).

To elucidate how the four key amino acid residues govern the stereoselectivity of SpGAME8a, we performed molecular docking analyses to compare the binding positions of the substrate 22S-hydroxycholesterol glucuronide in SpGAME8a and its individual or combined mutants. As the crystal structure of GAME8 remains unsolved, we predicted its structure using AlphaFold339 based on the corresponding gene sequences (Supplementary Fig. 13), and docked 22S-hydroxycholesterol glucuronide into the active site using AutoDock Vina40 to investigate substrate positioning.

The stereoselectivity of GAME8 is dictated by regioselective hydroxylation of the terminal isopropyl group on the substrate. Docking analysis of SpGAME8a with 22S-hydroxycholesterol glucuronide revealed that this selectivity is governed by the distance between the heme oxygen atom and the two hydrogen atoms (H1 and H2) of the isopropyl group, denoted as d1 and d2, respectively41 (Fig. 4A). The difference between d1 and d2 (d1-d2) dictates the chirality of the product. Wild-type SpGAME8a, with the d1-d2 value of −2.2 Å, favors hydroxylation at H1, leading to the formation of the R-configured intermediate that yields soladulcidine in the end (Fig. 4B). Conversely, in the SpGAME8a-F309L/ I374L/ S398F/ T401I mutant, this value shifts to 2.3 Å, corresponding to a reversal in the R/S product ratio (Fig. 4B). This shift is attributed to the introduction of substituted amino acids, which alter the substrate’s orientation, increasing the d1-d2 distance and favoring the production of the 25S-type product (Fig. 4D–G). In the F309L/ I374L/ S398F/ T401I quadruple mutant, the substrate encounters a more constrained space on the left side of the enzyme pocket and an expanded space on the right. This altered microenvironment results in a shorter d2 than d1, ultimately switching the stereoselectivity to the 25S-type (Fig. 4H). Consequently, hydroxylation regioselectivity is reversed, thereby switching the chirality of the final product.

To investigate whether the function of the four key amino acid residues is conserved across other Solanum species, we performed mutagenesis targeting these residues in the GAME8 genes of potato (StGAME8, also known as PGA142) and black nightshade (SnGAME843) (Supplementary Fig. 12A). Wild-type StGAME8 produced tomatidine as the most dominant aglycone (91%). Among the variants, I374L exerted the largest effect on stereoselectivity, thereby decreasing the relative production of tomatidine to 57%. Substituting all four key residues L309F/ L374I/ F398S/ I401T nearly reversed the stereoselectivity of StGAME8, resulting in soladulcidine as the most abundant product (82%) (Supplementary Fig. 12B). Wild-type SnGAME8 produced almost exclusively soladulcidine, consistent with previous reports that black nightshade primarily accumulates 22R,25R-type SGAs43. The SnGAME8-I386L variant produced 23% tomatidine, and substituting of all four key residues F321L/ I386L/C410F/T413I increased the proportion of tomatidine to 50% (Supplementary Fig. 12C).

Filotomatine enhances pest resistance in tomato

To elucidate the ecological roles of different chiral SGAs in tomato, we generated CRISPR/Cas9 mutants targeting SpGAME8a in the filotomatine-accumulating BIL6676 background, and SlGAME4 in the α-tomatine-accumulating M82 background. We obtained two homozygous SpGAME8a mutant lines without T-DNA insertions; both mutations resulted in frameshifts and premature stop codons (Fig. 5A). Consistent with the stereoselectivity of SpGAME8a, filotomatine levels decreased by 88% and 80% in the two mutant lines, with no apparent morphological defects or alterations in α-tomatine content (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. 14). In the α-tomatine-accumulating M82, we generated a SlGAME4 mutant with a 4-bp deletion, which eliminated both SGA enantiomers (Fig. 5C, D). Notably, uttroside B, a nitrogen-free saponin derived from a shared upstream pathway with SGAs26, accumulated specifically in the SlGAME4 mutant, but not in SpGAME8a mutants (Supplementary Fig. 15).

The tomato pinworm (Phthorimaea absoluta), an invasive specialist pest native to South America, now threatens tomato production globally44. To assess its response to SGAs, we compared its performance on different SGA-accumulating tomato lines by a non-choice bioassay. Counterintuitively, despite the complete abolition of SGA production in slgame4 mutant plants, the larvae gained significantly less weight on these mutants compared to these on α-tomatine-accumulating M82 plants (Fig. 5E). This growth deficit persisted into the pupal stage (Supplementary Fig. 16A). Conversely, larval performance was significantly better on the spgame8a mutant than on its wild-type BIL6676 background (Fig. 5E and Supplementary Fig. 16B). Notably, despite similar total SGA levels in M82 and BIL6676 (Supplementary Fig. 3E), larval growth was more suppressed on BIL6676. We further reared P. absoluta larvae on artificial diets supplemented with physiologically relevant, equimolar amounts of filotomatine, α-tomatine, or uttroside B. Consistent with the plant assays, filotomatine conferred significantly greater resistance against P. absoluta than α-tomatine (Supplementary Fig. 16C). Interestingly, uttroside B also exhibited a strong trend toward enhanced defense compared with α-tomatine, although the difference was not statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. 16C). To determine whether the tomato pinworm detoxifies SGAs via deglycosylation, as reported for tomato pathogens45, we analyzed SGAs in the frass of tomato pinworm larvae. While both α-tomatine and filotomatine were thoroughly deglycosylated in frass that had been excreted for 36 h, the SGAs remained intact (unmodified) in fresh frass (Supplementary Fig. 17), indicating that tomato pinworm does not deglycosylate SGAs in its gut.

To gain deeper insights into the ecological role of SGA chirality, we evaluated the performance on tomato lines accumulating distinct SGAs of a generalist pest, the tobacco cutworm (Spodoptera litura) that is one of the most widespread and destructive agricultural pests and that feeds on over 100 crops46. In contrast to the specialist P. absoluta, S. litura larvae grew significantly better on the SGA-free slgame4 mutant than on its wild type M82 background (Fig. 5F). Similarly, larval performance was enhanced on the spgame8a mutant, which accumulates reduced levels of filotomatine, compared to its wild-type BIL6676. However, larval growth was comparable on the two wild-type plants, M82 and BIL6676, which accumulate similar total levels of SGAs but with different enantiomeric compositions (Fig. 5F). This indicates that filotomatine and α-tomatine exert equivalent defensive effects against this generalist pest. To further test this inference, we reared larvae on artificial diets supplemented with physiologically relevant and equal amounts of filotomatine, α-tomatine or uttroside B. The results confirmed that the two SGA enantiomers inhibit larval growth to a similar extent, whereas the saponin uttroside B does not (Supplementary Fig. 16D).

Discussion

SGAs have long been recognized as powerful components of the defensive arsenal in Solanum plants17, and chirality is known to be fundamental to the function of biomolecules47. However, the ecological roles of the distinct SGA enantiomers remain poorly understood. Building on the knowledge of SGA enantiomer biosynthesis in this work, we generated stereospecifically modified plants, which serve as an excellent tool to investigate enantiomer-specific functions. Comparison of P. absoluta performance on spgame8a and its wild-type BIL6676 background clearly demonstrated the defensive role of filotomatine against this specialist pest (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, by comparing insect performance on M82 and BIL6676 plants that accumulate similar total SGA levels but differing dramatically in their enantiomeric composition, we confirmed that α-tomatine has lower defensive efficacy than filotomatine against this specialist pest. Consistently, the α-tomatine depleted slgame4 mutant exhibited markedly enhanced defense against P. absoluta compared to M82. Notably, two recent studies reported that mutations in GAME15 dramatically decreased Solanum plants’ resistance to pests25,26, providing solid genetic evidence for GAME15’s contribution to herbivore resistance. GAME15 controls the biosynthesis of uttroside B in S. nigrum leaves, and game15 mutant plants clearly demonstrated the defense role of uttroside B against Empoasca decipiens and Leptinotarsa decemlineata, two important Solanum pests. Meanwhile, slgame4 mutant plants accumulated large amounts of uttroside B. A comparison of the defensive functions of SGA enantiomers and uttroside B by supplemented into artificial diet suggested that the enhanced defense observed in SGA-depleted slgame4 mutants may be counterbalanced by increased saponin accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 16C). Thus, pest-resistant breeding that aims to use particular defensive metabolites should consider the influence of these metabolites on the related metabolic network.

The remarkable diversity of plant specialized metabolites is thought to have evolved in response to a wide range of ecological interactions, with individual compounds addressing specific ecological needs48. Here, we extend this concept by exploring the ecological significance of stereospecific metabolites in mediating interactions with pests of different feeding habits. Our results showed that stereochemistry confers distinct ecological activities towards different interacting organisms. Specifically, two SGA enantiomers exhibited indistinguishable defensive effects against the generalist pest S. litura. In contrast, uttroside B showed only minimal, if any, inhibitory effect on S. litura larval growth in artificial diet assays (Supplementary Fig. 16D), and its accumulation in slgame4 mutants did not enhance resistance to this pest (Fig. 5F). Notably, while both P. absoluta and S. litura are important tomato pests, S. litura is rarely reported to feed on wild tomato S. pennellii, whereas P. absoluta shares a long co-evolved history with it. We therefore speculate that the accumulation of the filotomatine enantiomer in S. pennellii was likely driven by its interaction with specialist pests, like P. absoluta. Conversely, the dominance of α-tomatine in cultivated tomatoes likely reflects historical domestication for broad-spectrum resistance against generalist pests, prior to the modern-era spread of P. absoluta44. To determine whether different herbivory indeed act as evolutionary forces driving the stereotyped specialization of plant defensive metabolites, future studies should analyze the fitness of tomato plants that produce stereotypical SGAs under interactions with multiple herbivores in natural habitats over evolutionary time.

Through forward genetic mapping using the high-resolution population of BILs33, we confirmed that GAME8 is the sole locus that is necessary and sufficient to control the F-ring stereochemistry of SGA. While we were preparing this manuscript, a study identified GAME8 as the controller of the C25 configuration in multiple Solanaceae plants49. This work revealed that the phenomenon of two-copy GAME8 is prevalent among Solanum plants and even extends to Capsicum annuum, a closely related solanaceous crop. This distribution indicates that the capacity to produce both enantiomers, rather than only one version, significantly contributes to plant fitness. Geographical analysis of Solanum cheesmaniae further supported the hypothesis that different habitats influence SGA stereodiversity49. Among the eleven wild tomato species and two cultivated tomato accessions examined, S. pennellii was the only species that dominantly accumulates 25R-type SGAs in its leaves. Although both SpGAME8a and SpGAME8b cluster phylogenetically within the 25S stereoselectivity clade, SpGAME8a is highly expressed in leaves, exhibits 25R stereospecificity. This apparent contradiction suggests that distinct ecological interactions, probably mediated by tomato pinworm herbivory, drove the unique evolutionary trajectory of S. pennellii. Collectively, the broad evolutionary analysis in this recent study and the comprehensive functional characterization of our work underscore the inference that sophisticated functional roles drive the genetic adaptions that underpin the stereodiversity of plant specialized metabolites.

GAME8 controls the hydroxylation of C26, and different GAME8 homologues confer distinct chirality at C25 (Fig. 2B). GAME8 also determines the conformation of the F-ring of SGAs (Fig. 1). Besides, our ectopic biosynthesis experiments have shown that downstream enzymes can convert each chiral type of GAME8 product into its corresponding chiral type of SGA aglycone (Fig. 2C). These indicate that the GAME8’s downstream enzymes, including GAME11, GAME4, and GAME12, lack strict stereoselectivity towards their substrates. Theoretically, the F-ring could adopt four different conformations due to its two chiral centers (C22 and C25), yet only two enantiomers were detected from all reported Solanum plants. We postulate that this results from the product-specificity of GAME6, which exclusively produces 22S-hydroxycholesterol, thereby restricting the final products to just two enantiomers.

All tested GAME8 orthologs from S. pennellii, S. lycopersicum, S. tuberosum and S. nigrum exhibited some degree of catalytic promiscuity with respect to product stereochemistry (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 12). This promiscuity explains why the sequence of two GAME8 copies in cultivated tomato are identical, yet the plant accumulates a small amount of filotomatine. Although changing four key amino acids based on natural GAME8 sequences could completely reversed the enzyme’s stereoselectivity, its inherent catalytic promiscuity persisted. This stereoisomeric promiscuity of GAME homologues was also observed across the entire Solanaceae family, including the highly basal species Cestrum imbricatum49. SGA chirality is achieved through amino acid residues surrounding the active pocket. Mechanistically, these residues position the isopropyl moiety via steric hindrance and direct the corresponding methyl group to become proximal to oxygen atoms, facilitating its hydroxylation. The SpGAME8a-F309L mutation substitutes a bulky phenylalanine with a smaller leucine that enlarges the cavity on the substrate’s right side (Fig. 4). This rightward shift of the substrate increases the O-H1 distance (d1) while decreasing the O-H2 distance (d2), and the larger d1-d2 value elevates the yield of the S-configured product. The SpGAME8a-I374L/S398F/T401I mutants exert similar effects by introducing amino acids with increased steric hindrance at the left side of the catalytic pocket. When these four mutations are combined in SpGAME8a-F309L/I374L/S398F/T401I, the substrate experiences a significantly expanded space on its right side and a crowded space on its left side, finally resulting in a larger d1 than d2 and 92% of S-chiral 22,26-dihydroxychlolesterol glucuronide. Similar inversions of regioselectivity in P450-catalyzed hydroxylation have been reported on hydroxylases of lithocholic acid50 and 16β steroid41. Future enzyme structure-guide mutagenesis to further enforce this rightward orientation may lead to a GAME8 enzyme that produces a higher percentage, perhaps even a pure, S-chiral product.

Plants produce a plethora of secondary metabolites to adapt to harsh environments, such as attacks from herbivores and pathogens. Chirality is a critically important characteristic of plant specialized metabolites. By using the chirality of SGAs as an example, our study reveals how stereochemistry contributes to plant defense and the underlying enzyme-catalyzed mechanisms. Engineering of stereoselective biosynthetic enzymes for plant specialized metabolites provides promising strategies for pest-resistant crop breeding. Notably, given the distinct bioactivities of SGA enantiomers against different pests, the potential influence of stereochemistry on fruit taste and anti-nutritional properties warrants careful consideration when engineering SGA pathways to enhance crop resistance.

Methods

Chemicals

Analytical standard of tomatidine (Cat# SJ-MN0296A) was purchased from SparkJade. α-tomatine (Cat# B21339) and solasodine (Cat# B20017) were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. Soladulcidine (Cat# S676530) was purchased from Chengdu PureChem-Standard Co., Ltd. Pyridine-d5 (Cat# 532975) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Ethyl acetate (Cat# G23272C) was purchased from Shanghai Titan Scientific. Ethanol (Cat# 10009218), n-hexane (Cat# 80068618), and acetonitrile (Cat# 400641692, HPLC grade) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent. Methanol (Cat# 34860, HPLC grade) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Plant materials

Solanum lycopersicum cv. M82 and Solanum pennellii (LA0716), all introgression lines (ILs) and BILs used in this study were obtained from the Tomato Genetics Resource Center (TGRC). Tomato and Nicotiana benthamiana plants were cultivated in controlled growth chambers maintained at 25 °C: 20 °C, day: night under a 16 h/8 h (light/dark) photoperiod.

Metabolite extraction

For SGAs LC-MS analysis, fresh tomato leaves were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and pulverized using a mortar and pestle. Approximately 100 mg of the resulting powder was weighed into a 2 mL microcentrifuge tube. Extraction solvent (80% [v/v] aqueous methanol, 1 mL per 100 mg tissue) was added, followed by vortexing for 1 min. Samples were centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 30 min at 10 °C. The supernatant was carefully transferred to a new 2 mL microcentrifuge tube and centrifuged again under identical conditions, then supernatants were transferred to a 2 mL glass autosampler vial. For SGAs aglycone analysis, approximately 20 mg of freeze-dried tobacco leaf tissue was weighed into a 2 mL microcentrifuge tube. A 1 mL saponification reagent (10% KOH [w/v], 10% H2O [v/v], 90% ethanol [v/v]) was added. Samples were incubated at 65 °C for 2 h, then tube caps were opened and samples were maintained at 65 °C to facilitate complete ethanol evaporation. After solvent evaporation, 500 μL of ddH₂O and 500 μL of ethyl acetate were added to each sample. Tubes were vortex mixed for 1 min to extract compounds, then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 3 min. The upper organic phase was transferred to a new 2 mL microcentrifuge tube. This liquid-liquid extraction procedure was repeated twice with fresh 500 μL aliquots of ethyl acetate. Combined ethyl acetate extracts were dried completely under reduced pressure using a centrifugal evaporator. The dried residue was reconstituted in 300 μL of ethyl acetate and transferred to a 2 mL glass autosampler vial. For GC-MS analysis, 10 μL supernatant was taken from the upper layer (ethyl acetate layer) and transferred to a 2 mL glass autosampler vial. The ethyl acetate was dried under a stream of nitrogen gas and 50 μL 1-(trimethylsilyl) imidazole-pyridine mixture was added, then incubated at 70 °C for 30 min. For frass analysis, third-instar larvae were transferred from tomato (M82) leaves to sterile Petri dishes. Fresh fecal pellets (10 mg) were collected into sterile 1.5-mL tubes, homogenized in 100 μL sterile H₂O, and incubated at 28 °C. Aliquots were processed at 0, 6, and 36 h post-collection by adding methanol (1:4 v/v homogenate: methanol), followed by centrifugation (14,000 × g, 30 min, 4 °C). Resulting supernatants were subjected to LC-MS analysis.

LC-MS analysis

Samples were analyzed using a Q Exactive quadrupole orbitrap high resolution mass spectrometry coupled with a Dionex Ultimate 3000 RSLC (HPG) ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC-Q-Orbitrap-HRMS) system (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with a HESI ionization source. The injection volume was 2 µL. Samples were separated with ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm particle size; Waters).

The mobile phase consisted of 0.01% formic acid and 2 mM ammonium formate in water (mobile phase A), and methanol: acetonitrile (50:50, v/v, mobile phase B), the gradient elution was set as follows: method 1, 0.0–1.0 min, 2.0% B; 1.0–25.0 min, 2.0%–98.0% B; 25.0–27.0 min, 98% B; 27.1–30.0 min, 2.0% B. Method 2, 0.0–0.5 min, 20.0% B; 0.5–8.0 min, 20.0–60.0% B; 8.0–10.0 min, 60.0–98.0% B; 10.0–12.0 min, 98% B; 12.1– 15.0 min, 20.0% B. Method 3: 0.0–1.0 min, 30.0% B; 1.0–20.0 min, 30.0–100.0% B; 20.0 − 27.0 min, 100% B; 27.1–30.0 min, 30.0% B.

For uttroside B (mobile phase A: 0.1% formic acid in water; mobile phase B: acetonitrile): 0.0–1.0 min, 5.0% B; 1.0–11.5 min, 5.0–28.0% B; 11.5–18.5 min, 28–100% B; 18.5–20.0 min, 100% B; 20.0–20.5 min, 100–5% B; 20.5–23 min, 5% B.

For 22, 26-dihydroxycholesterol-GlcA/22, 26-dihydroxycholesterol (mobile phase A: 0.1% formic acid in water; mobile phase B: acetonitrile): 0.0−1.0 min, 30.0% B; 1.0–20.0 min, 30.0–100.0% B; 20.0–27.0 min, 100% B; 27.1–30.0 min, 30.0% B.

The flow rate was 0.30 mL/min and column temperature was maintained at 40 °C.

All MS experiments were performed in positive ion modes using a heated ESI source. The source and ion transfer parameters applied were as follows: spray voltage 3.5 kV. For the ionization mode, the sheath gas, aux gas, capillary temperature and heater temperature were maintained at 40, 10 (arbitrary units), 300 °C and 350 °C, respectively. The S-Lens RF level was set at 50. The Orbitrap mass analyzer was operated at a resolving power of 70,000 in full-scan mode (scan range: 80–1200 m/z; automatic gain control (AGC) target: 1e6) and of 17,500 in the Top 6 data-dependent MS2 mode (stepped normalized collision energy: 20, 40 and 60; injection time: 50 ms; isolation window: 1.5 m/z; AGC target: 1e5) with a dynamic exclusion setting of 3.0 s. Xcalibur 4.6 was used for LC-MS data analysis. The number of technical and/or biological replicates was indicated in the Figure legend.

Purification of filotomatine/uttroside B

Filotomatine was purified from Solanum pennellii WT leaves, and uttroside B was purified from Solanum lycopersicum slgame4 leaves. The extraction procedure was as follows: first, 100 g of fresh plant leaves were thoroughly ground in liquid nitrogen. The resulting powder was stirred in 1 L of methanol at 45 °C with a magnetic stir bar for 2 h and then subjected to ultrasonication for 30 min. After filtration, the plant residue was subjected to a second extraction with 500 mL of methanol. The combined extracts were concentrated under reduced pressure to a final volume of 40 mL. Finally, the solution was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min twice, obtaining the crude extract. Crude extract separation was performed using an Agilent 1260 Infinity semi-preparative HPLC system. Chromatography was conducted on a Welch Ultimate XB-C18 column (21.2 × 250 mm, 5 µm particle size) maintained at 40 °C. The sample injection volume was 5 mL. The mobile phase consisted of (A) 0.5% (v/v) formic acid in water and (B) methanol. A linear gradient was applied as follows: 40% B (0–10 min), increasing to 95% B (60 min), then to 100% B (72 min). The column was re-equilibrated at 40% B from 73 to 100 min. The flow rate was 10 mL/min. Eluted fractions were collected automatically at 1-min intervals between 10 min and 72 min. Because α-tomatine and filotomatine lack ultraviolet absorbance, all collected fractions were analyzed by LC-MS to detect the presence of ions corresponding to the protonated molecule [M + H]⁺ of tomatine (m/z 1034.55). Due to the challenging separation of filotomatine from α-tomatine under these initial conditions, all fractions containing the m/z 1034.55 ion were combined. This combined eluate was concentrated to dryness under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator. The dried extract obtained from the first purification was reconstituted in 1 mL of methanol. Secondary separation was performed using the Agilent 1260 Infinity system and an Agilent Eclipse XDB-C18 column (4.65 × 250 mm, 5 µm). The column temperature was set to 45 °C. A 100 µL aliquot of the reconstituted sample was injected. The mobile phase consisted of (A) 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in water and (B) methanol. The applied gradient was: 10% B (0–1 min), increasing linearly to 95% B (20 min), held at 95% B (20–25 min), followed by column re-equilibration at 10% B from 25.1 to 30 min. The flow rate was 1 mL/min. Fractions were collected at 0.3 min intervals between 9 min and 20 min. All fractions collected during the secondary separation were analyzed by LC-MS. Fractions containing only the α-tomatine isomer were pooled together. This pooled fraction containing the purified α-tomatine isomer was concentrated to dryness under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator.

Structural characterization of filotomatine

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) experiments of filotomatine were conducted on a Bruker Advance III 500 MHz spectrometer operating at 298 K. The purified filotomatine was dissolved in Pyridine-d₅ for analysis. Chemical shifts were referenced to the residual solvent signals of pyridine-d5 (δH 8.74, δC 150.35). The structure of filotomatine was determined by one-dimensional and two-dimensional NMR spectroscopy (¹H NMR, 13C NMR heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC), heteronuclear multiple bond correlation (HMBC), correlation spectroscopy (COSY) and total Correlation Spectroscopy spectrum (TOCSY), Supplementary Fig. 2). The 13C NMR chemical shifts of filotomatine coincide very well with those of soladulcidine B (22R, 25R)51 but show clear differences from α-tomatine (22S, 25S)52 (Supplementary Table 1). Thus, the NMR assignments (Supplementary Table 1) confirmed that filotomatine has the 22R, 25R configuration. The most diagnostic carbon atoms are C-23 (filotomatine: δ 34.20; soladulcidine B: δ 34.2; α-tomatine: δ 26.9), C-26 (filotomatine: δ 47.59; soladulcidine B: δ 47.4; α-tomatine: δ 50.4), C-24 (filotomatine: δ 30.53; soladulcidine B: δ 30.4; α-tomatine: δ 29.1), C-20 (filotomatine: δ 41.48; soladulcidine B: δ 41.7; α-tomatine: δ 42.9), C-22 (filotomatine: δ 98.10; soladulcidine B: δ 98.3; α-tomatine: δ 99.2), C-27 (filotomatine: δ 19.40; soladulcidine B: δ 19.40; α-tomatine: δ 19.7). Because these diagnostic carbons lie close to the C‑22 and C‑25 chiral centers in the F‑ring, they are expected to be the most affected by the configurational differences between the two epimeric series, which accounts for their diagnostic value.

Agrobacterium-mediated transient expression in N. benthamiana

Transient expression in N. benthamiana was performed as previously described by Yin et al.34. All candidate genes were amplified by PCR using primers listed in Supplementary Data 1 and cloned into the pEAQ-HT expression vector by using the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). All expressed amino acid sequences are provided in Supplementary Data 2. Verified recombinant vectors were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 harboring the pSoup-P19 plasmid (Weidi Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Agrobacterium strains were inoculated into 3 mL of liquid LB medium (50 μg/mL kanamycin and 25 μg/mL rifampicin) at 28 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 16–24 h. Cultures were diluted 1:100 in fresh medium and grown for an additional 12–24 h, washed with Agromix buffer (10 mM MES [pH 5.6 with KOH], 10 mM MgCl₂, 200 μM acetosyringone), and resuspended in Agromix to an OD600 of 0.2. For transient expression, bacterial suspensions were infiltrated into the abaxial side of leaves from 5 to 6 weeks old N. benthamiana plants using a 1 mL needleless syringe. For co-infiltration experiments, equal volumes of bacterial suspensions were mixed prior to infiltration. Infiltrated plants were maintained under glasshouse conditions. Leaf tissues were harvested 6 days post-infiltration, flash frozen, and lyophilized using a vacuum freeze dryer.

Purification and NMR characterization of 22S, 26-dihydroxycholesterol-GlcA

Fifty grams of transiently transformed N. benthamiana leaves expressing CSG, SpGAME15, SpGAME6, and SpGAME8(a/b) were thoroughly ground in liquid nitrogen and resuspended in 1 L of methanol, sonicated for 15 min, and incubated at 50 °C with constant mixing, then washed twice with 1 L of n-Hexane and dried using a rotary evaporator. The dried extracts were resuspended in a minimal amount of dichloromethane/ methanol and separated using a silica column packed with Silica Gel 60 (200–300 mesh). A gradient of dichloromethane/ methanol (from 1:0 to 1:1) was used as the mobile phase. The eluate was collected with a fraction collector and all collected fractions were analyzed by LC–MS to detect the presence of ions corresponding to the protonated molecule [M-H]- of 22,26‑dihydroxycholesterol at m/z 593.3702. Subsequently, fractions that contained 22,26‑dihydroxycholesterol were further purified using an Agilent 1260 Infinity system equipped with an Agilent Eclipse XDB‑C18 column (4.65 × 250 mm, 5 µm). The column temperature was maintained at 45 °C. A 20 µL aliquot of the reconstituted sample was injected. The mobile phase consisted of (A) 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in water and (B) methanol. The gradient program was as follows: 30% B from 0 to 1 min, linearly increased to 100% B over 20 min, held at 100% B from 20 to 27 min, then re‑equilibrated at 30% B from 27.1 to 30 min. The flow rate was 1 mL/min. Fractions were collected at 20 s intervals between 13 min and 20 min. All fractions obtained from this secondary separation were again analyzed by LC–MS. Those fractions that contained only 22,26‑dihydroxycholesterol were pooled together and subsequently subjected to NMR analysis. The purified 22S, 26-dihydroxycholesterol-GlcA was dissolved in CD₃OD. NMR experiments were conducted on a Bruker AVANCE III HD spectrometer equipped with a cryogenic probe operating at 298 K.

LC-MS analysis of 22, 26-dihydroxycholesterol-GlcA and 22, 26-dihydroxycholesterol

Leaf tissues from N. benthamiana transient co-expressing CSG, SpGAME6, SpGAME8a/SpGAME8b, SpGAME15 were ground, and metabolites were extracted with methanol at a ratio of 0.1 g/mL by vortexing and sonication for 2 min. The extract was directly subjected to LC-MS for the analysis of 22S, (25R/S)26-dihydroxycholesterol-GlcA.

For the analysis of 22S, (25R/S)26-dihydroxycholesterol, the extract was mixed with an equal volume of 2 M HCl and incubated at 75 °C for 2 h. After cooling to room temperature, the mixture was neutralized with 1/4 volume of 4 M NaOH. Methanol was removed by rotary evaporation under reduced pressure for 2 h. The resulting aqueous phase was extracted twice with 1 mL ethyl acetate. The combined ethyl acetate fractions were dried under rotary evaporation, then dissolved in 200 μL methanol and analyzed by LC-MS.

Due to the unavailability of commercial standards for 22S, (25R/S)26-dihydroxycholesterol-GlcA and 22S, (25 R/S)26-dihydroxycholesterol, these metabolites were characterized by comparison of their mass spectrometric data to those reported in previous studies18,25. Detection was performed using the following ions: for 22S, (25R/S)26-dihydroxycholesterol-GlcA, m/z 577.3721 in positive mode and m/z 593.3691 in negative mode; for 22S, (25R/S)26-dihydroxycholesterol, m/z 401.3431 in positive mode. Corresponding MS2 spectra are provided in Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11.

GC-MS analysis

GC-MS analysis was performed as previously described by Yin et al.34. Metabolite analysis was performed on a Thermo ISQ-LT GC-MS system equipped with a TG-5HT capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.10 μm). Derivatized samples (1 μL) were injected in split mode (10:1 ratio) at 250 °C. Helium carrier gas flow was maintained at 1.2 mL/min. The oven temperature program was: 170 °C (hold 2 min). Ramp to 290 °C at 6 °C/min (hold 4 min). Ramp to 340 °C at 25 °C/min. Mass detection employed electron ionization in SCAN mode (m/z range 60–800) with a 10 min solvent delay. Chromeleon 7.2.7 was used for GC-MS data analysis.

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated Gene Knockout

Knockout mutants of spgame8a in BIL6676 and slgame4 in M82 were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 technology. Target-specific sgRNAs were designed via the CRISPR-P v2.0 platform (http://crispr.hzau.edu.cn/CRISPR2/). The sgRNAs (SpGAME8a sgRNA: TAGTTACAATGGTCAAAGAT, SlGAME4 sgRNA: ATTGGATCCTGGGCATGATT) were introduced into the pHCE vector. Constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing and transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 by Weimi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Changzhou, China) for plant genetic transformation.

Multiple sequence alignment

Amino acid sequences of GAME8s were aligned using Clustal Omega (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/msa/clustalo).

RNA-seq

Fresh tissues (leaves, roots, flowers, fruits) from S. lycopersicum cv. M82 and S. pennellii (LA0716) were collected, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and homogenized. Three biological replicates per tissue type were processed for transcriptomic sequencing. Total RNA was extracted using TransZol Up Plus RNA Kit (TransGen Biotech), followed by RNA quality control, library preparation (Illumina platform), paired-end 150 bp sequencing and data analysis performed by a commercial provider.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from leaves, roots and stems of 1-month-old LA0716 and BIL6676 plants grown under glasshouse conditions (25 °C, 16-h/8-h light/dark photoperiod) using the TransZol Up Plus RNA kit (TransGen Biotech). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from approximately 1 µg of total RNA using the Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit with gDNA Clean for qPCR (Accurate Biology), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using SYBR®Green Realtime PCR Master Mix (TOYOBO) on a CFX96™ real-time system. The Ubiquitin genes (Sopen02g027600 for LA0716 and Solyc02g082800 for BIL6676) were used as the reference. Four biological replicates were analyzed for each sample. Primers used for expression analysis of SpGAME8a and SpGAME8b are listed in Supplementary Data 1.

Molecular docking

Protein structures of GAME8a, GAME8b and the different mutants were predicted using AlphaFold3. Based on the predicted structures, the heme group was modified to form the active species Compound I (Cpd I) by adding an oxygen atom above the iron atom, with a Fe=O bond length of 1.62 Å. Quantum Chemistry software was used to perform geometry optimization calculations of the substrate to determine its appropriate conformation. AutoDock Tools was employed to generate PDBQT files for both the protein (as the receptor) and the substrate (as the ligand). AutoDock Vina was used to obtain the top-scoring conformation from the docking results. The coordinates of the Fe atom served as the center, with a 15 Å × 15 Å × 15 Å box defining the substrate docking space. All protein sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Data 2.

AlphaFold3 structure prediction

The structural models of SpGAME8a and SpGAME8a-F309L/ I374L/ S398F/ T401I were generated using AlphaFold3 through the AlphaFold Server on June 24, 2025, following the method described by Abramson et al. 39. The amino acid sequence used for the prediction of SpGAME8a comprised 498 residues and corresponded to the full-length protein encoded by UniProt entry identifier A0ABM1H2Q1. SpGAME8a- F309L/ I374L/ S398F/ T401I was derived from SpGAME8a and contained four amino acid substitutions. The complete amino acid sequences submitted for AlphaFold3 prediction are provided in Supplementary Data 2. Structural models of the single-point variants SpGAME8a-F309L, SpGAME8a-I374L, SpGAME8a-S398F, SpGAME8a-T401I were generated by introducing the corresponding amino acid substitutions in silico into the SpGAME8a using PyMOL.

Each AlphaFold3 prediction included one protein chain and one heme cofactor. Five ranked candidate models were generated for each protein, and the top-ranked model, as determined by the AlphaFold3 ranking score, was selected for subsequent structural analysis and molecular docking. Model confidence was evaluated using the predicted local distance difference test score (pLDDT) and predicted aligned error (PAE). The top-ranked models are shown from two viewing angles and colored according to their pLDDT values in Supplementary Fig. 13, together with the corresponding PAE matrices. The overall pLDDT distributions and PAE profiles indicated that the selected models exhibited high confidence in the structurally ordered regions relevant to the subsequent docking analyses.

Site-directed mutagenesis

Mutagenesis of the GAME8 genes (SpGAME8a, SpGAME8b, SlGAME8, StGAME8, and SnGAME8) was performed using the QuickMutation™ Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Mutagenic primers were designed using the online primer design tool (https://crm.vazyme.com/cetool/singlepoint.html). All mutagenized protein sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 2.

Bioassay

Phthorimaea absoluta (Lepidoptera: Gelechiidae) was originally collected from tomato fields in Xinjiang province, China. Larvae were kept in controlled chamber maintained at 25 °C: 20 °C, day: night under a 16/8 h, light: dark photoperiod in CEMPS, Shanghai, and fed on tomato var. M82 plants. Newly emerged larvae were placed on 5-week-old plant leaves. Larvae were collected and weighed 7 days after release, and pupae were harvested and weighed at 14 dpi. Mixed-sex larvae were used in all experiments.

Spodoptera litura (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae) was originally collected from the wild in Shanghai, China. Larvae were kept in a controlled chamber maintained at 25 °C/20 °C (day/night) under a 16 h/8 h (light/dark) photoperiod in CEMPS, Shanghai, and fed on artificial diet. Uniformly sized third-instar larvae were released onto tomato seedlings and then collected and weighed at 2 dpi. For compound feeding assays, α-tomatine, filotomatine, and uttroside B were dissolved in DMSO and incorporated into an artificial diet. Uniformly sized second-instar larvae were weighed and introduced onto the prepared diet; larvae weights were recorded again after 2 days. The artificial diet was prepared with the following components per kilogram: soybean flour (100 g), wheat germ (80 g), yeast powder (26 g), casein (8 g), vitamin C (8 g), choline chloride (1 g), sorbic acid (2 g), cholesterol (0.2 g), inositol (0.2 g), and agar (26 g).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics 25). Two-tailed Student’s t-test (for two groups) or ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test (for three or more groups); when Levene’s test p < 0.05, Welch’s ANOVA followed by Games-Howell post hoc test was used instead.
