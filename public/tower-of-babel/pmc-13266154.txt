Minimal Clinically Important Difference and Patient Acceptable Symptomatic State after arthroscopic posterior labral repair in a non-military cohort
Authors: Ignacio Pasqualini, Benjamin E Jevnikar, Nicolás Lucero Viviani, Ignacio Tanoira, Luciano A Rossi, Maximiliano Ranalleta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypothesis and BackgroundPosterior glenohumeral instability is an increasingly recognized cause of shoulder dysfunction in young, active individuals. Although arthroscopic posterior labral repair (PLR) produces favorable outcomes, procedure-specific thresholds defining clinically meaningful improvement remain incompletely established, particularly for sport-specific patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs). The purpose of this study is to define Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) and Patient Acceptable Symptomatic State (PASS) thresholds for the Rowe, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES), and Athletic Shoulder Outcome Scoring System (ASOSS) scores after arthroscopic PLR and determine the proportion of patients achieving these benchmarks.MethodsConsecutive patients undergoing isolated arthroscopic PLR between 2018 and 2023 with ≥12-month follow-up were retrospectively analyzed. Pre-operative and 1-year PROMs (Rowe, ASES, and ASOSS) were collected. MCID was calculated as one-half standard deviation of score change. PASS was determined using an anchor-based satisfaction question with receiver operating characteristic analysis to identify optimal cutoffs. Area under the curve assessed discriminative ability.ResultsSeventy patients (mean age, 20.5 ± 4.0 years; 85.7% male) were analyzed. All PROMs improved significantly at 1 year (Rowe: 41.0 ± 11.5 to 81.0 ± 13.2; ASES: 53.2 ± 3.0 to 89.1 ± 7.3; ASOSS: 54.1 ± 12.6 to 84.9 ± 8.6; all P < .001). Satisfaction was reported by 91% and 90% returned to sport at 4.8 ± 1.9 months, with 81% returning at their preinjury level. Recurrent instability occurred in 5.7% and revision in 2.9%. MCID thresholds were 7.1 (Rowe), 4.2 (ASES), and 8.3 (ASOSS); PASS thresholds were 50, 70, and 65, respectively. Achievement rates were high (Rowe 98.6% MCID/100% PASS; ASES 100%/100%; ASOSS 94.3%/100%). Receiver operating characteristic analysis demonstrated acceptable-to-excellent discrimination (Rowe area under the curve 0.79 [95% confidence interval, 0.68-0.90]; ASES 0.85 [0.72-0.99]; ASOSS 0.86 [0.76-0.95]).ConclusionArthroscopic PLR reliably produces clinically meaningful improvement. Procedure-specific MCID and PASS thresholds for Rowe, ASES, and ASOSS scores were established in a non-military cohort, providing practical benchmarks for outcome interpretation and counseling.Keywords: Posterior shoulder instability, Glenohumeral joint, Labral tear, Arthroscopic surgical procedures, Treatment outcome, Patient-reported outcome measures, Minimal Clinically Important Difference, Patient acceptable symptomatic state

Posterior glenohumeral instability is an increasingly recognized cause of shoulder pain and dysfunction, particularly among young, active individuals and athletes.6,11,12,20,24, 25, 26 Although arthroscopic posterior labral repair (PLR) has become the preferred surgical treatment for symptomatic posterior instability, defining what constitutes a clinically meaningful improvement remains challenging.8,15,17,20,22,23 In the current era of value-based care, patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) have become central to evaluating procedural success, providing a patient-centered lens that complements objective measures of stability and function.15,23 Among these, the Rowe, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES), and Athletic Shoulder Outcome Scoring System (ASOSS) scores are widely used to quantify recovery following shoulder stabilization procedures.15, 16, 17,21,23

Interpretation of PROMs, however, requires translation from statistical to clinical significance. Metrics such as the Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) and the Patient Acceptable Symptomatic State (PASS) have therefore emerged as essential benchmarks for quantifying meaningful post-operative improvement.5,16,23 The MCID represents the smallest change in a PROM that patients perceive as beneficial, while the PASS defines the post-operative state beyond which patients consider themselves well and satisfied with treatment. Establishing procedure-specific MCID and PASS thresholds enables surgeons to contextualize PROM changes, improve patient counseling, and enhance comparability across studies.

To date, only a limited number of studies have defined these thresholds in shoulder instability. Pasqualini et al established MCID and PASS values for the Rowe and ASOSS scores after arthroscopic Bankart repair and further demonstrated that these thresholds vary substantially according to patient-specific factors such as sex, age, and athletic participation.16 In posterior instability specifically, Scanaliato et al reported MCID and PASS thresholds for the ASES, Rowe, and Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation (SANE) scores after arthroscopic PLR in a homogenous military cohort,23 while Mullen et al more recently defined MCID, PASS, and substantial clinical benefit thresholds for the ASES, Brophy, Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System Global-10 (PROMIS-10), Subjective Shoulder Value (SSV), and visual analog scale (VAS) after primary arthroscopic posterior stabilization.14 These studies substantially advanced the field, but important gaps remain. Prior posterior instability literature has been limited either by highly specific military populations or by PROM selections that did not include a sport-specific measure such as the ASOSS. Accordingly, clinically meaningful thresholds for ASOSS after isolated arthroscopic PLR in a broader non-military cohort remain undefined.15

The present study expands upon this prior work by establishing MCID and PASS thresholds for the Rowe, ASES, and ASOSS scores after isolated arthroscopic PLR in a broader, non-military cohort. To our knowledge, this is the first study to define ASOSS-based clinical significance thresholds in posterior instability and provides procedure-specific benchmarks for a civilian population undergoing PLR. By integrating multiple validated PROMs, including the sport-specific ASOSS, this study provides procedure-specific benchmarks for interpreting clinically meaningful improvement after arthroscopic PLR.

Methods

Study design and population

A retrospective review was performed of consecutive patients who underwent arthroscopic PLR at a single academic institution between January 2018 and December 2023. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board, and the requirement for individual informed consent was waived in accordance with institutional guidelines for retrospective studies. Patients were eligible if they had symptomatic posterior glenohumeral instability confirmed by clinical examination and advanced imaging. Symptomatic instability was defined as posterior shoulder pain and/or mechanical symptoms with instability or apprehension during provocative examination maneuvers (eg, jerk test or posterior load-and-shift testing) with imaging confirmation of posterior labral pathology. In addition, eligible patients had undergone arthroscopic PLR with a minimum of 12 months of clinical follow-up and complete pre-operative and 1-year post-operative PROMs (n = 98). Exclusion criteria included previous ipsilateral shoulder surgery (n = 4), multidirectional or anterior instability (n = 1), concomitant full-thickness rotator cuff or Superior Labrum Anterior to Posterior (SLAP) repair (n = 2), glenoid bone loss more than 20% on pre-operative imaging (n = 2), and humeral avulsion of the glenohumeral ligament lesions (n = 1). After excluding the remaining patients due to incomplete 1-year PROMs (n = 18), a total of 70 patients met the inclusion criteria and were analyzed, representing a 79.5% follow-up rate (Fig. 1). All patients included in the present study were athletes. 1-year PROMs were collected via electronic capture and phone calls. Demographic and clinical variables including age, sex, arm dominance, number of instability episodes, glenoid bone loss, and number of anchors used were recorded via chart review for all patients.

Surgical technique and rehabilitation protocol

All procedures were performed by fellowship-trained orthopedic surgeons specializing in shoulder and sports medicine. Patients were positioned in the lateral decubitus position under general anesthesia with an interscalene block. Diagnostic arthroscopy was performed to confirm isolated posterior pathology. Labral repair was performed with suture anchor fixation. In addition, capsulolabral plication was routinely performed using additional sutures to reduce capsular volume and restore tension to the posterior capsule, consistent with previously described techniques.3,17,18 No patients underwent concomitant anterior, superior, or rotator cuff procedures. Post-operatively, patients were immobilized in a sling for 4 weeks followed by passive and active-assisted range-of-motion exercises. Progressive strengthening began at approximately 8 weeks, and return to unrestricted sport was permitted once patients achieved pain-free range of motion and restoration of preinjury strength, typically at 4 to 6 months post-operatively.

Outcome measures

Patients completed 3 validated PROMs both pre-operatively and at 1-year follow-up: the Rowe score, which assesses shoulder stability, motion, and function; the ASES score, which evaluates pain and daily function; and the ASOSS, a sport-specific PROM designed to assess shoulder function, pain, and performance during athletic activity. At 1 year, patients also responded to a binary satisfaction anchor question: “Taking into account your pain, function, and ability to perform daily and athletic activities, do you consider the current state of your shoulder satisfactory?” Return to sport, level of play, recurrence, revision, and time to return were recorded. Recurrent instability was defined as post-operative symptomatic posterior instability including episodes of posterior subluxation or dislocation confirmed clinically or cases requiring revision stabilization.17,19 Change in PROM score was defined as the difference between pre-operative and 1-year post-operative scores.

Determination of Minimal Clinically Important Difference and patient acceptable symptomatic state

The MCID was determined using a distribution-based approach and defined as one-half of the standard deviation of the change in each PROM score from baseline to 1-year follow-up. This method estimates the smallest score improvement corresponding to a meaningful change from the patient's perspective. A distribution-based approach was selected as it is widely used and appropriate in the absence of a sufficiently granular anchor; the binary satisfaction question used in this study was suitable for defining PASS but did not provide adequate resolution to support anchor-based MCID calculation. The PASS was determined using an anchor-based approach referencing the binary satisfaction question. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed independently for each PROM to identify the optimal cutoff point that maximized sensitivity and specificity using the Youden index. The area under the curve (AUC) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to evaluate the discriminatory power of each PROM in distinguishing between satisfied and unsatisfied patients.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were presented as absolute numbers and percentages. Comparisons between pre-operative and 1-year PROM scores were made using paired t-tests. AUC values were interpreted as follows: 0.7-0.8 indicated acceptable discrimination, 0.8-0.9 excellent discrimination, and values more than 0.9 outstanding discrimination. Because this study was designed as a retrospective review of consecutive patients treated during the study period, an a priori sample size calculation was not performed. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX), and a P value less than .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient demographics and surgical characteristics

A total of 70 patients who underwent arthroscopic PLR were included in the final analysis. The mean age at the time of surgery was 20.5 ± 4.0 years. The majority of patients were male (85.7%) and involved the right shoulder in 57.1% of cases. The dominant arm was affected in 58.6% of patients. On average, patients reported 1.3 ± 0.7 episodes of instability before surgical intervention (Table I).

Patient-reported outcome measures and satisfaction

At baseline, mean Rowe, ASES, and ASOSS scores were 41.0 ± 11.5, 53.2 ± 3.0, and 54.1 ± 12.6, respectively. At 1-year follow-up, all scores demonstrated significant improvement, with mean Rowe, ASES, and ASOSS scores increasing to 81.0 ± 13.2, 89.1 ± 7.3, and 84.9 ± 8.6, respectively. These results represent mean improvements of approximately 40 points in the Rowe score, 36 points in the ASES, and 31 points in the ASOSS over the first post-operative year, indicating marked functional recovery and return of shoulder stability and performance (Fig. 2).

At 12 months post-operatively, 91.4% (n = 64) of patients reported being satisfied with their surgical outcome (Table I).

Return to sport and activity

Overall, 90.0% (n = 63) of patients successfully returned to sport at a mean of 4.8 ± 1.9 months post-operatively. Among those who returned, 81.4% (n = 57) resumed participation at their preinjury level of play, 8.6% (n = 6) returned at a decreased level, and 10.0% (n = 7) were unable to return to sport (Table I).

Recurrence and revision

Recurrent instability was observed in 5.7% (n = 4) of patients during the follow-up period. Revision surgery was required in 2.9% (n = 2) of cases (Table I).

Minimal Clinically Important Difference and patient acceptable symptomatic state thresholds

The MCID thresholds were 7.1 points for the Rowe score, 4.2 points for the ASES score, and 8.3 points for the ASOSS. Nearly all patients exceeded these thresholds, with 98.6% achieving the MCID for Rowe, 100% for ASES, and 94.3% for ASOSS (Table II).

Discussion

The present study establishes MCID and PASS thresholds for the Rowe, ASES, and ASOSS scores following isolated arthroscopic PLR in a civilian, non-military cohort. These findings provide procedure-specific benchmarks for interpreting post-operative recovery after PLR and complement recent work defining clinically meaningful outcome thresholds following posterior shoulder stabilization.14,16,23 The MCID thresholds were 7.1 for the Rowe, 4.2 for the ASES, and 8.3 for the ASOSS, while the PASS thresholds were 50, 70, and 65, respectively. Nearly all patients surpassed these thresholds, with excellent functional outcomes, high satisfaction, and a 90% return-to-sport rate at a mean of 5 months post-operatively. Although an independent visual analog pain scale was not collected in this cohort, the ASES score incorporates a validated pain component, and the substantial post-operative improvement in ASES scores likely reflects meaningful reductions in pain as well as improvements in function. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that arthroscopic PLR produces rapid, durable, and clinically meaningful improvements in shoulder function and sport participation.

Our MCID and PASS thresholds should be interpreted alongside prior work in both anterior and posterior instability, with the understanding that these values are highly context dependent. Pasqualini et al reported MCID values of 8.2 and 8.7 and PASS thresholds of ≥80 and ≥90 for the Rowe and ASOSS scores following arthroscopic Bankart repair, suggesting that clinically meaningful thresholds differ across instability direction and surgical indication.16 In posterior instability specifically, Scanaliato et al reported higher PASS thresholds in a military cohort (Rowe 90, ASES 80, SANE 85),23 while Mullen et al recently identified an ASES PASS threshold of 91.75 after primary arthroscopic posterior stabilization using a broader set of outcome instruments including Brophy, PROMIS-10, SSV, and VAS.14 The lower PASS thresholds observed in the present study (Rowe 50; ASES 70; ASOSS 65) likely reflect several important differences in study design and patient population. First, our cohort was restricted to patients undergoing isolated arthroscopic PLR, providing a procedure-specific benchmark, whereas Mullen et al evaluated a broader posterior stabilization population.14 Second, our study incorporated the sport-specific ASOSS and the stability-focused Rowe score, which may capture recovery priorities differently than more general symptom-based instruments. Finally, differences in cohort composition and post-operative expectations likely contributed, as our civilian population included both recreational and competitive athletes rather than a uniform high-demand cohort. Taken together, these findings suggest that clinically meaningful PROM thresholds following posterior instability surgery are not universal, but rather depend on the patient population, procedure performed, and outcome instrument used. Importantly, to our knowledge, the present study is the first to establish ASOSS-based MCID and PASS thresholds in posterior shoulder instability, providing a sport-specific framework for interpreting outcomes after PLR.

Return-to-sport outcomes in the present study compare favorably with prior literature.2,7,13,17 Nearly 90% of patients resumed sports participation, with more than 80% returning at their preinjury level. These rates mirror or exceed those reported in contemporary series of posterior stabilization procedures, which typically range from 70% to 90%.2,7,13 The mean time to return to sport of approximately 5 months aligns with established rehabilitation timelines and underscores the procedure's capacity to facilitate early and reliable functional recovery.10,17 The low recurrence (5.7%) and revision (2.9%) rates observed in our cohort are also consistent with prior work,2,13,17 further affirming the durability of arthroscopic repair in appropriately selected patients. Altogether, these outcomes are comparable to, or better than, those seen after anterior stabilization procedures in similar athletic populations.1,4,9

By defining MCID and PASS thresholds for multiple validated PROMs, including the sport-specific ASOSS, this study provides surgeons with practical, procedure-specific benchmarks for evaluating clinical success. These thresholds can inform shared decision-making, facilitate more nuanced post-operative counseling, and enable standardized outcome reporting across studies. Importantly, our data suggest that achieving patient satisfaction after PLR does not require absolute PROM scores equivalent to those seen after anterior stabilization, reflecting differing expectations and functional demands. Incorporating these metrics into both clinical practice and research can improve alignment between surgeon-defined success and patient-perceived recovery.

This study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. Its retrospective design introduces potential selection bias, and although follow-up was complete at 1 year, longer-term outcomes are necessary to confirm durability of improvement and recurrence rates. The cohort consisted primarily of young, active individuals, which may limit generalizability to older or less athletic populations. Furthermore, the high proportion of satisfied patients in this cohort may influence PASS calculations and could contribute to lower derived thresholds, a limitation that has been noted in prior anchor-based PROM threshold studies. Additionally, while ROC analysis demonstrated strong discriminative validity, the modest sample size may have influenced AUC precision. Moreover, an independent pain metric (eg, VAS) was not collected, limiting isolated assessment of pain despite inclusion of a validated pain component within the ASES score. In addition, detailed characterization of activity level (eg, competitive vs. recreational athletes, overhead activity, occupational demands) and trauma history was not consistently available, precluding subgroup analyses and limiting evaluation of how these factors may influence outcomes and PASS thresholds. Accordingly, these thresholds should be interpreted as cohort-specific estimates rather than universally generalizable benchmarks, as variability in patient activity level, functional demands, and expectations may meaningfully influence perceived satisfaction following PLR. Finally, MCID was derived using a distribution-based approach rather than an anchor-based method, as a sufficiently granular external anchor was not available; although widely accepted,17,19 this approach may be less directly reflective of patient-perceived clinical change.

Conclusion

This study defines MCID and PASS thresholds for the Rowe, ASES, and ASOSS scores following isolated arthroscopic PLR in a non-military cohort. These procedure-specific benchmarks may help surgeons interpret PROM improvement, counsel patients regarding expected recovery, and contextualize outcomes following PLR. The identified thresholds—7.1, 4.2, and 8.3 for MCID and 50, 70, and 65 for PASS, respectively—provide clinically meaningful benchmarks for outcome interpretation. Nearly all patients achieved these thresholds, demonstrating that PLR yields excellent functional recovery, high satisfaction, and reliable return to sport. These procedure-specific values expand the understanding of meaningful improvement after posterior stabilization and support the use of PROM-based metrics in evaluating patient-centered success.
