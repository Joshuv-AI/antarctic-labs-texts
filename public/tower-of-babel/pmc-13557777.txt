Diversity and climatic distribution of Sphingidae (Lepidoptera) in Mexico
Authors: Jorge L León-Cortés, Andrea Murillo-Vázquez, Oliver Rodríguez-Aguilar, Ian J Kitching, Konrad Fiedler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHere we revisit and update the state of knowledge on the Sphingidae fauna of Mexico. Our aims were to: (1) document the species diversity of Mexican Sphingidae; (2) generate spatial distribution models for sphingid species; and (3) discuss distributional patterns and the conservation implications for key taxa that, due to their life-history characteristics and restricted distributions, may require conservation actions. We compiled a total of 9,985 records representing 202 taxa (185 species and 17 subspecies) in 49 genera and three subfamilies—Macroglossinae, Sphinginae, and Smerinthinae. We report Perigonia odileae Haxaire & Melichar, 2025, and Perigonia pallida Rothschild & Jordan 1903, as new Sphingidae records for the Mexican realm. Species distribution models indicated that most sphingid genera in Mexico have a predominantly Neotropical biogeographic affinity: 12 genera (24%) exhibit a Neotropical distribution, seven (14%) are exclusively Nearctic, eight (16%) occur in both regions, and 22 (45%) are largely Neotropical but extend marginally into the Nearctic. We discussed taxonomic and distributional considerations for taxa that represent confirmed species (based on barcoded specimens in BOLD), along with species records representing significant expansions into the Mexican realm. Maintaining a network of threatened ecotopes—such as cloud forests and tropical rainforests—will be crucial for preserving sphingid populations and for promoting population (re)colonization processes.Keywords: Biogeography, Mesoamerican hotspot, richness, taxonomic note, moth conservation

Introduction

The family Sphingidae Latreille has been the subject of numerous types of research, including surveys of local and regional faunas (eg Brown and Donahue 1989, Common IFB 1990, Lödl 1993, Gómez-Nucamendi et al. 1999, Meerman 1999, Guevara-Capelo et al. 2002, Moré et al. 2005, Lara-Pérez et al. 2020, Muñiz-Ramírez et al. 2026), taxonomy (eg Rothschild and Jordan 1903, Hodges 1971), immature stages (eg Moss 1912, 1920), patterns of abundance and species richness (eg Stradling et al. 1983), zoogeography (eg Schreiber 1978), autecology and evolutionary biology (eg Janzen 1984, 1986, 1993, Fink 1995, Ponce et al. 2015), pollination ecology (eg Janzen 1983, Willmott and Búrquez 1996, Agosta and Janzen 2005), and economic significance (eg Common IFB 1990, Clavijo and Chacín 1992).

For New World Sphingidae, major taxonomic and biogeographic works include Rothschild and Jordan (1903, 1910), Moss (1912, 1920), Hodges (1971), Schreiber (1978), and Kitching and Cadiou (2000). In Mexico, studies on sphingids include the works of Mooser (1939) and Hoffmann (1942). These authors recognized around 150 species in the country, but progressive surveys in poorly collected areas have increased the number of species and subspecies to over 200 (Cary 1963, Brown and Donahue 1989, White et al. 1991, Smith 1995).

Rothschild and Jordan (1903) were the last authors to treat the family comprehensively, although most species—including those described after 1903—were illustrated in the diagnostic summary of the world fauna published by d’Abrera (1987). Rothschild and Jordan treated 772 species, and their classification was based mainly on adult morphological characters. However, because they studied only dried male and female genitalia, they were unable to fully analyze the complete range of genital characters (Hodges 1971).

Previously placed in their own superfamily (Kusnetzov and Stekolnikov 1985), Sphingidae are now treated as one of nine families within Bombycoidea sensu stricto (Brock 1971, Holloway 1987, Minet 1994). Morphological and molecular analyses strongly support the monophyly of Sphingidae within the superfamily Bombycoidea (Regier et al. 2001). The family Sphingidae comprises approximately 1,700 described species classified into about 200 genera (Moré et al. 2005, Kawahara et al. 2009), and four subfamilies: Langiinae, Macroglossinae, Smerinthinae, and Sphinginae, the latter three of which are further divided into several tribes and subtribes (Kitching et al. 2018). Despite being the subject of numerous phylogenetic studies (eg Kawahara et al. 2009, Kawahara and Barber 2015), the phylogeny of the Sphingidae remains only partially resolved (Timmermans et al. 2019).

Here we revisit and update the state of knowledge on the Sphingidae fauna of Mexico. Our aims were to: (1) document and update species diversity of Mexican Sphingidae; (2) produce distribution maps for the species present in the country; and (3) discuss distributional patterns and the conservation implications for key taxa that, due to their life-history characteristics and restricted distributions, may require conservation actions. Our work helps identify information gaps and highlights priority areas for future research on Sphingidae in Mexico.

Sphingidae Biology

Sphingids (hawkmoths or sphinx moths) are medium to large-sized moths, with strong, narrow wings and streamlined, conical bodies (Haber 1983). Basic morphological characters of sphingids are as follows: hindwing venation with veins Rs and Sc connected by a cross-vein (R1) at about the middle of the discal cell (Hodges 1971); generally, a well-developed proboscis, although its length is variable and, in many genera (mostly in subfamily Smerinthinae), is reduced and nonfunctional. The labial palps are usually large, but, together with the pilifers, are modified in the Afrotropical genus Xanthopan Rothschild & Jordan and members of the Acherontiini and Choerocampina into ultrasonic hearing organs. The antenna is generally narrow, acute, and slightly swollen apically. Antennae in the males appear thicker due to fasciculate setae on their undersides, whereas those of females are filiform with only a short pile of setae ventrally (Rothschild and Jordan 1903, Hodges 1971, Haber 1983, Holloway 1987). The frenulum and retinaculum are usually present and strong. The eggs are globular or slightly flattened. The larva is cylindrical with a single median dorsal horn on segment A8, although this can be reduced to a button-like structure in some genera. After the first instar, the larva has sparse, very fine, secondary setae and often also prominences arranged in transverse rows. Pupae are fusiform, with the head narrower than the thorax. Apart from genitalia, the sexes differ in that females are usually larger than males, with larger abdomens and broader, more rounded wings.

Most species are nocturnal and are readily attracted to light sources. However, some species are crepuscular, and these rarely come to light, being generally caught while nectaring at flowers. A small number of genera (eg Aellopos, Eupyrrhoglossum) are strictly diurnal and are only observed at flowers or while drinking water from puddles or streams, and there are a few species, such as Hyles lineata, that fly during the day, at dusk and at night. A few species have very broad distributions, such as Agrius cingulata (Fabricius), which occurs throughout the New World from Canada to Argentina, but most species have much more restricted ranges. Sphingids occur in most biomes and on all non-polar continents, but species richness is highest in tropical areas. Most adult sphingids have a functional proboscis, which they use to imbibe nectar from flowers and water from streams and puddles, and can perform multiple visits to flowers during the day and nighttime (Pittaway 1993, Pescador 1994). Thus, they can feed themselves for relatively long periods of time (weeks or months), which allows them to allocate and gain access to resources and live longer than those moths that rely on stored fat reserves. By living longer, female sphingids are under less time pressure to mate and oviposit. Hence, although females do initially attract males using pheromones in the same way as many other groups of moths, it would appear that upon arriving, male sphingids often must undertake a courtship ritual involving use of pheromones, distributed by their hair pencils, and genital stridulatory organs—though several groups of sphingids, most notably the Smerinthini and New World Sphingini, lack these structures. As a result, it has been suggested that female sphingids might be quite selective in their choice of mates. Once mated (probably multiple times for both sexes), females usually lay only a single egg per host plant. Eggs generally hatch within a week, and because of the high nutritional content of many of their host plants, larval development can likewise be fast, lasting only two to five weeks. However, like their sister group, the family Saturniidae, many hawkmoths of the tribe Smerinthini feed on trees with low nutritional and high tannin content and thus require much longer to pass through this stage (Janzen 1984, Holloway 1987).

The distributions of sphingids are closely related to those of their host plants. Larvae may feed on only a single genus or several closely related genera within a single family (Janzen 1984). However, across its range, a sphingid species may be recorded on numerous plants, and although a degree of local specialization has been observed by some authors (eg Janzen 1984, 1986, 1993), it remains to be critically studied. Most larvae do not sequester toxins from their host plants and rely on crypsis as their primary means of defence, backed up in some genera by eyespot displays when disturbed. Even the larvae of the Hyles euphorbiae (Linnaeus) complex in the Old World, which feed on toxic Euphorbia L. species and are black with white, yellow and red markings—a classic warning coloration, do not seem to sequester the phorbol toxins of their food plants to any great extent and the color appears to be a warning of the toxic qualities of the gut contents rather than of the larva itself (Hundsdoerfer et al. 2005). Pupation is usually either underground or in the surface litter. In the latter case, a weak silk cocoon may be present, whereas in the former, a cocoon is almost entirely absent. The duration of the pupal stage can be very short, less than two weeks in some continuously brooded species, or may last for a few months, if the species passes the dry season in this stage (Kitching and Cadiou 2000).

Materials and Methods

Sphingidae Occurrence Data

We compiled occurrence data for sphingids in Mexico from multiple public databases, including the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF), the Barcode of Life Data System (BOLDSYSTEMS), the National Commission for the Use and Knowledge of Biodiversity in Mexico (CONABIO), the National Insect Collection at the Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México (IBUNAM), the Natural History Museum, London, UK, and the Canadian National Collection of Insects, Arachnids, and Nematodes (CNC). Additional records were obtained from the McGuire Center for Lepidoptera & Biodiversity and the Entomological Collection at El Colegio de la Frontera Sur, Unidad San Cristóbal de las Casas (ECOSC-E), as well as from previous research on the study species and from our extensive fieldwork in Mexico. Species’ identifications, taxonomy, and nomenclature were supported by the Sphingidae Taxonomic Inventory website (Kitching 2025).

Sphingidae Distributions

We compiled 9,985 faunistic records of the family Sphingidae from Mexico. The database was subsequently filtered to retain only species-level records suitable for distribution modeling (ie 4,128 faunistic records; see Supplementary Material S1). A total of 108 Mexican Sphingidae taxa met the modeling requirements (Supplementary Materials S1 and S4). Following recommendations for presence-only modeling algorithms (eg GARP and MaxEnt, Stockwell and Peters 1999, Peterson et al. 2007, Wisz et al. 2008, Stoa et al. 2019), we retained only species with at least ten unique sample points. Duplicate records were removed, and we excluded species with fewer than ten records or whose spatial records did not meet a minimum separation distance of 1 km. The distance between points must be equal to or greater than the spatial resolution of the model predictor layers to avoid biases in spatial sampling and information duplication (Aiello-Lammens et al. 2015, Rodríguez-Aguilar et al. 2023). These procedures were implemented in ArcMap 10.4.1 using SDMTools (Hernandez et al. 2006, Wisz et al. 2008, Boria et al. 2014, Brown 2014).

We selected 19 bioclimatic variables from the WorldClim database at a spatial resolution of 30 arc-seconds (Hijmans et al. 2005), based on their biological relevance in previous distribution modeling studies of moths (Diniz-Filho et al. 2010, Ferro and Melo 2011). These included annual mean temperature (bio1), mean diurnal range (bio2), isothermality (bio3), temperature seasonality (bio4), maximum temperature of the warmest month (bio5), minimum temperature of the coldest month (bio6), temperature annual range (bio7), mean temperatures of the wettest (bio8), driest (bio9), warmest (bio10), and coldest (bio11) quarters; and annual (bio12), wettest-month (bio13), driest-month (bio14), and seasonal (bio15) precipitation, alongside precipitation of the wettest (bio16), driest (bio17), warmest (bio18), and coldest (bio19) quarters. For each species, we retained only those variables with pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients lower than 0.85 (Osorio-Olvera et al. 2020).

Potential Distribution Modeling

We used Maximum Entropy species distribution modeling (MaxEnt version 3.4.4, Phillips et al. 2004) to estimate potential species distributions. MaxEnt generates predictions from presence-only data and environmental variables (Phillips et al. 2004, 2006) and has proven robust even with relatively small sample sizes (≥10 occurrences), frequently outperforming other presence-only methods. Its use has expanded across many taxonomic groups—including lepidopterans—and across diverse geographic regions (Wisz et al. 2008, Montañez-Reyna et al. 2022, Moguel-Cárdenas et al. 2024).

To generate the distribution models, we first delimited the calibration area (M) based on the physiographic regions of the world and the distribution of presence records (Manzanilla-Quiñones et al. 2019, Montoya-Jiménez et al. 2022). We partitioned occurrence records, using 75% for model calibration (training) and 25% for model evaluation (testing). Model settings included 25 replicates, 1,000 iterations, 50,000 background points, and a convergence threshold of 0.00001 (Martínez-López et al. 2021, Montañez-Reyna et al. 2022). Variable importance was assessed using Jackknife tests generated by MaxEnt, which (1) exclude each variable in turn, (2) include each variable independently, and (3) include all variables simultaneously (Phillips et al. 2006).

Model performance and optimal parameterization were evaluated using the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) omission–commission framework, focusing on the area under the curve (AUC) values. Models with higher AUC values—closer to 1.0—were considered more accurate (Pearce and Ferrier 2000, Phillips et al. 2006). We selected models with AUC values greater than 0.8 (Supplementary Material S4), as models with AUC values between 0.8 and 1.0 are generally considered to have high predictive performance (Baldwin 2009). Potential distribution models were projected from the calibration area (M) to the polygon of the Mexican Republic (1,949,850 km²), obtained from Instituto Nacional de Estadística y Geografía [INEGI] (2021). Raster maps in which each pixel represents the estimated probability of species occurrence and with values closer to 1 indicating higher suitability (Phillips et al. 2004).

Sampling Completeness

Sampling completeness for Mexican Sphingidae was estimated using the sample coverage (Ĉ) as implemented in the iNEXT package (Chao et al. 2016). Coverage estimates were based on the total number of occurrence records (individuals). We calculated the effective number of species following Hsieh et al. (2016):

0D: species richness,

1D: the exponential of the Shannon index,

2D: the inverse of the Simpson index.

The last two measures emphasize the contribution of relatively abundant species and complement traditional richness estimates.

Results and Discussion

Sphingidae Diversity

We gathered 9,985 faunistic records of the family Sphingidae in Mexico representing 202 taxa (185 species and 17 subspecies) in 49 genera and three subfamilies—Macroglossinae, Sphinginae, and Smerinthinae. The genera with the highest number of species are Xylophanes Hübner (34 spp.), Manduca Hübner (22 spp.), Eumorpha Hübner (14 spp.), Sphinx Linnaeus (12 spp.), and Lintneria Butler (11 spp.; Fig. 1). We reported Perigonia odileae Haxaire & Melichar and Perigonia pallida Rothschild & Jordan as additional new Mexican records (Fig. 2; Supplementary Material S2). Two genera (4%)—Monarda Druce and Trogolegnum Rothschild & Jordan—are Mexican endemics (Supplementary Material S1). Sphingidae Distributions

Eight genera (16%) occur in both regions. Another 22 genera (45%) are essentially Neotropical but are marginally represented in the Nearctic.

Most Mexican sphingid species are tropical and have broad distributional ranges across the New World. Schreiber (1978) discussed the faunal composition and general distribution of Neotropical Sphingidae, using species counts within grid squares obtained by overlaying dot maps. He recognized 18 generalized distributional ranges for Neotropical Sphingidae, four of which occur in Mexico and broadly correspond to major biogeographic regions recognized elsewhere (Morrone et al. 2002, Luna et al. 2004): the Mexican High Plateau, tropical evergreen forests of eastern Mexico, Yucatán woodland, and dry forests of western Mexico (Schreiber 1978). However, more recent data suggest that some sphingids have wider distributions than originally reported and subsequent taxonomic revisions have updated many of his species’ boundaries (Supplementary Material S3).

Some sphingids can be regarded as widespread and common in Mexico (eg Hyles lineata, Manduca quinquemaculatus, Erinnyis ello, Aellopos tantalus), whereas others have relatively restricted distributions (eg Adhemarius blanchardorum, Ceratomia hoffmanni, and Aellopos titan), including endemic species such as Lintneria pitzahuac from Aguascalientes (Supplementary Material S3). Some species reach their northern distribution limits in tropical Mexico (eg Adhemarius donysa and A. ypsilon; Supplementary Material S3) and may be regarded as highly localized or to have relatively small population sizes, as in previous intensive inventories we have only recorded a few individuals at tropical rainforest locations (eg Perigonia odileae, P. pallida; Supplementary Material S2). Although some sphingids can be relatively common in their biotopes, they have specific ecological requirements that result in localized distributions (eg Xylophanes katharinae and X. josephinae).

However, several Mexican biogeographic provinces encompass a wide diversity of habitats, many of which remain poorly sampled, such as the tropical and cloud forests of Oaxaca and Guerrero, the dry regions of Puebla and San Luis Potosí, the Mexican High Plateau, and the Yucatán peninsula. The presence of numerous host plants of widespread and generalist species such as Agrius cingulata, Cocytius duponchel (Poey), and Xylophanes tersa (Linnaeus) suggests that these species should also occur in the Yucatán Peninsula. Their apparent absence may reflect extensive habitat modification during the Maya period (Schreiber 1978), but could also be due to collecting bias, as common and widespread species are often under-recorded.

Our spatial modeling approach confirmed that several species exhibit distributions corresponding to recognized Mexican biogeographic provinces (Morrone et al. 2002), including -mainly- the Sierra Madre Oriental province (eg Sphinx leucophaeata Clemens, Xylophanes anubus (Cramer); see Halffter 1987, Luna et al. 2004), the Mexican High Plateau [eg Eumorpha typhon (Klug), Monarda oryx Druce and Trogolegnum pseudambulyx (Boisduval)], and the Balsas province [eg Neogurelca mulleri (Clark), Lintneria balsae (Schaus) and Sphinx chisoya (Schaus) (Supplementary Material S3)]. The Californian province in the northwestern portion of the Baja California Peninsula harbors a fauna composed primarily of temperate species, including several elements associated with montane and oak woodlands, eg Sphinx sequoiae (Boisduval), Hemaris thetis (Boisduval) and Proserpinus clarkiae (Boisduval). In contrast, the Baja California province in the southern part of the peninsula contains distinctive elements at the species level, eg Euproserpinus phaeton Grote & Robinson, Smerinthus astarte Strecker and Lintneria xantus (Cary).

Two species in monotypic genera, Monarda oryx and Trogolegnum pseudambulyx (Boisduval), are endemic to the Mexican High Plateau. Endemic subspecies are most frequently found on peninsulas (eg Manduca rustica cortesi Cary, Pachysphinx occidentalis peninsularis Cary, and Callionima falcifera guaycura (Cary) in Baja California; Brown and Donahue 1989) or on islands (eg Erinnyis obscura socorroensis Clark and Perigonia lusca continua Vázquez; Palacios-Vargas et al. 1982). Although endemic subspecies have been reported from other continental regions, additional data are required to confirm their status as geographically differentiated taxa (eg Sphinx chersis pallescens (Rothschild & Jordan) from Durango, Sonora and Zacatecas and Sphinx libocedrus achotla Mooser (Mooser 1939, 1962), from Aguascalientes, Baja California Sur, Colima, Querétaro, San Luis Potosi, Sonora and Zacatecas.

Concluding Remarks

Over recent decades, a substantial number of surveys across the Mexican countryside have improved sphingid records, particularly in regions such as Los Tuxtlas (Veracruz), suburban areas around Mexico City, Chamela (Jalisco), Morelos, the Balsas Basin, and parts of Chiapas. Nevertheless, significant gaps remain in southern Mexico (eg the Lacandon and Calakmul rainforest reserves, and the cloud forests of the Sierra Madre de Chiapas) and in northern regions (eg desert and oak forest habitats in Chihuahua and the Zacatecas region, as well as portions of the Mexican High Plateau). Given the ecological complexity of these habitats, achieving comparable sampling coverage—even at coarse spatial scales—will be difficult in the short term. A more complete understanding of sphingid distributions will therefore depend on increased sampling in these under-surveyed areas. An important proportion of Mesoamerican sphingid species has been insufficiently studied to determine whether they should be considered endangered or vulnerable (IUCN 2018). In this regard, our modeling approach represents a preliminary step toward accurately defining the spatial distributions of taxa that may have localized distributions. The concern is that, as with other ­lepidopterans, insect species considered to be at risk of global decline and extinction are often narrow endemics (Thomas 2011).

Historically, sphingid distribution maps have been generated using specimen locality data alone, with little reference to habitat, vegetation types, or broader landscape features. Although this traditional approach provides a useful starting point, incorporating, for instance, species–habitat relationships could offer advantages: it may help identify apparently suitable yet unrecorded areas—ie priority sites for future sampling (Rushton et al. 2000)—and complement coarse survey data by predicting species abundance within “occupied” areas or their potential presence in poorly documented regions (Edwards et al. 1996). However, for many parts of Mexico, and for a substantial number of sphingid species, the need for distributional information far exceeds the resources available to Mexican entomologists.

Overall, sphingid recording efforts—like those for other charismatic insect groups in Mexico—have focused on the most accessible areas of the country. However, knowing only that a species occurs in a given area provides no information about the size or status of its populations. Thus, we advocate implementing conservation actions in and around areas of high climatic suitability and at locations where localized sphingid species have been recorded, in order to reduce the risk of local population extinctions. Detailed surveys and long-term monitoring programs at a wide range of spatial scales (including the current protected-area network of the Mesoamerican biodiversity hotspot, Falcón-Brindis et al. 2021) are also needed to assess changes in the status of all species, and not just of the rarest.
