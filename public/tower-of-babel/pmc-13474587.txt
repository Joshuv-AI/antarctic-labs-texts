Effects of blood-flow restriction training on dynamic postural control and ankle function in individuals with chronic ankle instability: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Jiapeng Liu, Xikai Lin, Peng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDecreased ankle-muscle strength and activation can lead to postural control disorders in individuals with chronic ankle instability (CAI). Blood-flow restriction training (BFRT) promotes muscle growth and function at low loads, representing a safe and efficient rehabilitation strategy. However, previous small-scale studies have shown inconsistent results. This review conducted a meta-analysis of existing randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to investigate the influence of BFRT on the dynamic postural control and ankle function of CAI individuals.MethodsPubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and EBSCO were searched until November 16, 2025. The primary outcomes included Y-balance test (YBT) distance, ankle-muscle strength, and ankle-muscle activation, whereas the secondary outcome was Cumberland ankle instability tool (CAIT) score. Methodological quality was assessed using Cochrane Risk-of-Bias tool (version 2) and PEDro scale. We conducted a meta-analysis using a random-effects model via the metafor package in R software.ResultsA total of nine RCTs involving 252 participants were included in this work. Results showed that compared with the control group, BFRT may improve the YBT anterior distance (standardized mean difference [SMD] = 0.57, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.18 to 0.96; low-certainty evidence) and comprehensive score (SMD = 0.31, 95% CI 0.16 to 0.45; very low-certainty evidence), dorsiflexion muscle strength (SMD = 1.12, 95% CI 0.09 to 2.15; very low-certainty evidence), tibialis anterior muscle activation (SMD = 0.92, 95% CI 0.41 to 1.43; very low-certainty evidence), and CAIT score (MD = 2.30, 95% CI 0.89 to 3.71; very low-certainty evidence).ConclusionBFRT may improve the dynamic postural control, ankle-muscle strength, muscle activation, and function score of CAI individual. However, further high-quality RCTs are warranted to confirm these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-07018-5.

Introduction

Acute lateral ankle sprains represent one of the most common injuries in sports activities [36]. It typically results from excessive hindfoot inversion or plantarflexion-adduction, leading to damage of lateral ligament complex [14]. In the United States, ankle sprains result in approximately two million emergency department visits annually [7, 12]. The ankle sprain has a high recurrence rate, which approximately 40% of individuals who experience an initial ankle sprain may develop chronic ankle instability (CAI) with persistent residual symptoms such as pain, recurrent sprains, and frequent episodes of the ankle giving way [8, 38]. Prolonged joint instability may also lead to posttraumatic osteoarthritis [15]. CAI causes great pain to individuals and affects their quality of life and physical activity levels [19]. The high cost of treatment also brings a huge economic burden to individuals and society [15]. In the United States emergency department, $1029 per event of ankle sprain was charged [45]. The effective treatment of CAI must be explored to reduce the burden of social medical care and improve a patient’s quality of life.

Extensive evidence suggests that deficits in postural control are one of the most important risk factors contributing to recurrent ankle sprains in individuals with CAI [22, 49]. Evidence indicates that dynamic balance performance, such as Y-balance test (YBT) distance, is associated with the risk of ankle sprain [16]. Postural control depends on integrated sensory input (visual, proprioception, and vestibular system), central information processing in the central nervous system, and motor output [43]. Among them, ankle-muscle strength and neuromuscular control are crucial for motor output [18]. Ankle muscle strength has been shown to be associated with dynamic postural control [50]. Delayed or insufficient activation of ankle-stabilizing muscles, such as the peroneus longus and tibialis anterior, can contribute to recurrent sprains in individuals with CAI [49]. Therefore, how to effectively enhance the muscle strength of CAI individuals has become an important issue in the field of sports medicine.

Blood-flow restriction training (BFRT) involves placing restrictive bands or blood-pressure cuffs on the proximal region of the limbs and inflating them to a pressure sufficient to restrict venous return, allowing only partial arterial blood flow during exercise [6, 47]. The intramuscular hypoxic environment created by BFRT can induce vascular endothelial growth and high levels of metabolic stress, which may lead to hypertrophy [32, 42]. Additionally, due to the reduction in joint stress, it increases training tolerance and adherence [6]. Studies [30, 51] indicate that BFRT can significantly improve dynamic postural control capability and functional performance scores in individuals with CAI. However, despite encouraging findings, research on the therapeutic efficacy of BFRT in individuals with CAI continues to yield conflicting results. This inconsistency may be attributed to variables such as intervention duration and BFRT parameters (e.g., pressure value and cuff width). In addition, confounding factors such as participant age, sex ratio, and severity of instability may influence the results. Therefore, conducting a comprehensive systematic review is necessary to explore potential moderating factors affecting intervention efficacy.

The present study systematically evaluated the effects of BFRT on postural-control ability, muscle strength, muscle activation, and function scores in individuals with CAI and further analyzed potential moderating factors, aiming to provide evidence for the clinical application of BFRT. We hypothesized that BFRT substantially improved dynamic postural control and ankle function in individuals with CAI.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This review adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Statement guidelines [46]. The study has been registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (Registration number CRD42025638485). The PRISMA checklist is listed in Supplementary Appendix A.

Search strategy

We systematically searched the PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, Web of Science, and EBSCO to identify research articles that evaluated the effects of BFRT on individuals with CAI. All relevant articles published globally in any language from database inception through November 16, 2025 were included, without restrictions on publication dates. The search was conducted using a combination of subject headings and keywords. Search terms included “Chronic ankle instability,” “CAI,” “ankle instability,” “ankle sprain,” “Functional ankle instability,” “FAI,” and “blood flow restriction training,” “occlusion training.” The search strategies are detailed in Supplementary Appendix B. Two independent researchers performed the searches according to a defined formula. Disagreements were resolved through consultation with a third researcher. Endnote X9 software was utilized to screen and manage the aforementioned reports. All duplicate articles were removed, and articles were screened according to the inclusion/exclusion criteria.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) individuals with CAI were included; (2) intervention involving BFRT plus control group; to ensure stable therapeutic effects and lasting physiological adaptations, the intervention duration was set at a minimum of 4 weeks; (3) comparison of ankle rehabilitation training (including strength training, balance training, and plyometric training); (4) randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were considered for the study; and (5) outcomes including YBT distance, ankle-muscle strength, ankle-muscle activation, and function score.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) lack of outcome data; (2) case reports or descriptive studies, or research not published as peer-reviewed journal articles, including dissertations, book chapters, and conference abstracts; (3) duplicate publications where identical data for the same variables and groups were reported across multiple publications by the same author(s); for this case, duplicates were removed to retain a single record for analysis; and (4) serious flaws in the research design (such as lack of true randomization despite being labeled as RCTs, or substantial baseline incomparability between groups).

Data extraction

Data were extracted independently by two researchers. A third researcher resolved any disagreements through discussion and consensus. The following data were extracted from the included studies: (1) basic study information, i.e., authors’ names, publication year, sample size (intervention/control group), participants’ age and sex; (2) interventions: intervention method in the experimental and control groups, intervention frequency and duration; and (3) outcomes: primary (YBT distance, ankle-muscle strength, and ankle-muscle activation) and secondary outcomes (Cumberland ankle instability tool [CAIT] score).

Quality appraisal

Two independent reviewers assessed the risk of bias for the included studies through the Cochrane Risk-of-Bias tool (version 2). The Physiotherapy Evidence Database (PEDro) was searched to identify the PEDro scale score and each trial was then reviewed by two co-authors to confirm the PEDro scale score. Where a trial was not listed on the PEDro, two authors independently rated the trial using the PEDro scale [34]. The overall quality of the evidence was summarized by two researchers with the use of a summary of results table generated by Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) [46]. The outcome can be scored as “high,” “moderate,” “low,” and “very low” depending on risk of bias, inconsistency, indirect evidence, impression, and publication bias. In cases of disagreement, a third researcher was consulted to assist in judgment.

Statistical analysis

All data were statistically analyzed and presented using R software (version 4.3.2). Since the outcomes were continuous variables, pooled effect estimates were calculated using the change values from baseline to post-intervention, their standard deviations, and total number of participants. Additionally, 0.5 served as the correlation coefficient to calculate the difference in the standard deviations before and after the intervention [5]. This assumption was grounded in the widely accepted moderate level of test–retest reliability in the literature. We performed a meta-analysis using a random-effects model in R using the metafor package, which accounts for between-study heterogeneity by appropriately weighting studies. When measurement methods and units were consistent, mean difference (MD) with 95% CI was used as the effect size measure; otherwise, standardized MD (SMD) with 95% CI served as the effect size indicator. Statistical significance was considered if the 95% CI of MD/SMD did not cross zero. Effect sizes were considered small when 0.2 ≤ SMD < 0.5, medium when 0.5 ≤ SMD < 0.8, and large when SMD ≥ 0.8. To ensure the high quality and validity of the analysis, we performed summary meta-analysis only when more than three findings (n ≥ 3) were included.

Given the different reach directions (anterior, posteromedial, posterolateral, and comprehensive score) of the YBT, different muscles of the ankle-muscle strength test (dorsiflexion, plantarflexion, inversion, and eversion) and muscle-activation test (tibialis anterior and peroneus longus), subgroup analysis was performed on the result analysis. Sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out approach (i.e., deleting one study each time and repeating the analysis) was applied to analyze the effect of each study on the overall effect size. Statistical heterogeneity among trials was examined using the Cochran Q statistic, which tests the null hypothesis that no difference in effect sizes exists across studies [5]. Studies with I2 values exceeding 50% were considered to have substantial heterogeneity [20]. Funnel plots were used [10] along with Egger’s regression test [9, 29] to evaluate the possibility of publication bias. Potential effect moderators (total number of interventions, age, proportion of males, pressure value, and cuff width) were explored using univariable meta-regression models.

Results

Study selection

Electronic databases and manual searches identified 328 articles. A total of 83 duplicate studies were removed, and 196 articles were excluded after title and abstract screening. After screening and reading the remaining articles, nine RCTs [21, 28, 30, 31, 35, 40, 51, 53, 54] were finally included. Seven of these studies were published in English, while one was in Chinese and another in Korean (Fig. 1).

Quality assessment

ROB analysis showed one study as “low risk,” six as “some concerns,” and two as “high risk.” All studies showed “low risk” in “missing outcome data” and “measurement of the outcome.” Bias was observed in two studies for “randomization process” and in seven studies for “selection of the reported results.” Bias was found in six studies for “deviations from intended interventions” (Supplementary Appendix D and E). PEDro scores of 9 included studies ranged from 4 to 9 with a mean of 6.56 indicating a moderate methodological quality (Supplementary Appendix F). In accordance with the GRADE system, the results on all outcomes had very low and low levels of certainty. Table 2 presents a summary of the certainties of evidence. The downgrade was primarily due to the imprecision and the high risk of bias.

Meta-analysis results

Y-balance test distance

Six studies [21, 28, 31, 51, 53, 54] reported the YBT distance. The findings revealed that BFRT significantly improved the YBT distance in CAI individuals compared with control group with small effect size (SMD = 0.35, 95% CI 0.25 to 0.44). The results indicated less heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 0.0%, P = 1.00) (Fig. 2). In subgroup analysis, BFRT showed a significant improvement compared with the control group in the YBT anterior distance with medium effect size (SMD = 0.57, 95% CI 0.18 to 0.96) and comprehensive score with small effect size (SMD = 0.31, 95% CI 0.16 to 0.45). In terms of posterolateral distance (SMD = 0.29, 95% CI − 0.05 to 0.63) and posteromedial distance (SMD = 0.29, 95% CI − 0.15 to 0.73), no statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups (Fig. 2).

Forest plot for the Y-balance test distance

Ankle-muscle strength

Six studies [21, 30, 35, 40, 51, 53] reported ankle-muscle strength. The findings of meta-analysis revealed that BFRT significantly improved ankle-muscle strength in individuals with CAI compared with control group with large effect size (SMD = 0.96, 95% CI 0.41 to 1.52; 95% PI − 1.31 to 3.24). The findings unveiled high heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 82.9%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3). Under subgroup analysis, BFRT showed a significant improvement compared with the control group in the dorsiflexion muscle strength with large effect size (SMD = 1.12, 95% CI 0.09 to 2.15). No group differences were found in terms of plantar flexion muscle strength (SMD = 1.09, 95% CI − 0.40 to 2.59), inversion muscle strength (SMD = 0.50, 95% CI − 0.55 to 1.55), and eversion muscle strength (SMD = 1.11, 95% CI − 1.94 to 4.15) (Fig. 3).

Ankle-muscle activation

Three studies [28, 31, 54] reported ankle-muscle activation. The findings revealed that BFRT significantly improved the ankle-muscle activation in CAI individuals compared with control group with medium effect size (SMD = 0.61, 95% CI 0.16 to 1.06). The results indicated low heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 45.3%, P = 0.10) (Fig. 4A). In subgroup analysis, BFRT showed a significant improvement compared with the routine rehabilitation training in the tibialis anterior activation with large effect size (SMD = 0.92, 95% CI 0.41 to 1.43). In terms of peroneus longus activation (SMD = 0.31, 95% CI − 0.31 to 0.92), no statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups (Fig. 4A).

Function score

Four studies [21, 28, 30, 31] reported CAIT score. The findings of meta-analysis revealed that BFRT significantly improved CAIT score in individuals with CAI compared with control group (MD = 2.30, 95% CI 0.89 to 3.71). The results indicated low heterogeneity between studies (I2=0.0%, P = 0.71) (Fig. 4B).

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

For outcomes exhibiting high heterogeneity (ankle-muscle strength), sensitivity analysis was conducted by systematically excluding individual studies to evaluate the impact of heterogeneity on overall outcomes. If excluding a particular study resulted in reduced heterogeneity (I2), then that study could be identified as a potential source of the observed high heterogeneity. After excluding studies that contributed to heterogeneity, we compared the weighted mean differences before and after sensitivity analysis. Sensitivity analysis revealed no directional changes in weighted mean differences across all outcomes (e.g., from statistically significant to non-significant). Thus, despite substantial heterogeneity among study findings, excluding heterogeneous studies did not significantly alter the results, suggesting the robustness of the meta-analysis findings (sensitivity analysis forest plots in Appendix G). The Egger test and funnel plots for ankle-muscle strength (P < 0.001) suggested the presence of publication bias. The funnel plots showed asymmetry among outcomes of ankle-muscle activation and CAIT score. However, the relatively small number of included studies may increase the risk of Type I error. Trim-and-fill analysis confirmed that the significant findings for ankle muscle activation (P = 0.015) and CAIT score (P = 0.018) remained robust against publication bias. Further details are provided in Appendix H.

Meta-regression

Meta-regression analysis was used to explore confounding factors affecting outcome effect sizes, which were the total number of interventions, age, proportion of males, pressure value, and cuff width. No significant effect of all covariates on Y-balance test distance, ankle-muscle activation, and Cumberland ankle instability tool score was found. The improvement in ankle-muscle strength was related to the proportion of males (β = 0.02, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.04) but not to other covariates (Supplementary Appendix I).

Discussion

This study explored the effect of BFRT on dynamic postural control and ankle function in individuals with CAI. Results showed that BFRT, compared with control group alone, can substantially improve the YBT anterior distance and comprehensive score, dorsiflexion muscle strength, tibialis anterior muscle activation, and functional score.

Principal findings

Our study revealed that BFRT significantly improved YBT distance in CAI individuals compared with control group. This finding indicated that BFRT had greater benefits in improving dynamic postural control in CAI individuals. Postural control require the coordination of multiple components of the sensorimotor system (integrated sensory input, central nervous system information processing, and motor output) [43]. To prevent potential injuries, the nervous system must acquire proprioceptive information and appropriately provide feedforward or reflexive motor commands to muscle tissues for joint stabilization [23, 27]. A failure to provide adequate neuromuscular responses from the provided sensory information can result in postural control defects and ankle sprain [18]. Given that dynamic postural control deficits have been identified as a risk factor for ankle sprains in CAI individuals [39], this result may suggest that BFRT can help reduce the risk of ankle sprains. Notably, due to the lack of long-term follow-up data, our inferences require further research for confirmation. Although the exact mechanism by which BFRT induces an increase in dynamic postural control remains unclear, most researchers currently believe that reduced blood flow creates an intramuscular hypoxic environment, thereby increasing muscle activation via type III and IV afferent nerve fibers [2]. Current evidence supports that BFRT is superior to control group in improving tibialis anterior activation. Additionally, the cortical excitability at the lower limb occlusion can spill over to the proximal control site, enabling the recruitment of greater muscle mass and further improvements in pelvic stability and dynamic postural control [33]. Burkhardt et al. [3] found that CAI individuals show increased activation of the vastus lateralis and soleus when using BFRT for dynamic balance training. Specifically, enhanced muscle activation facilitates anticipatory joint stabilization through feedforward control and promotes rapid recovery of the center of mass via feedback mechanisms [25].

The results of our study supported that BFRT was superior to control group in improving dorsiflexion muscle strength. The use of tourniquet cuffs during training can partially restrict arterial blood flow and block venous return to the active muscle [6, 47]. The outcome is an ischemic/hypoxic environment that increases the level of metabolic stress and recruitment of rapidly contracting muscle fibers [42]. Additionally, hypoxia and metabolic stress owing to blood-flow restriction trigger the mammalian target of rapamycin and the mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathways [1, 42]. The mammalian rapamycin target protein pathway is a key regulator of anabolic and catabolic signaling in skeletal muscle [58]. Thus, BFRT may contribute to improvements in muscle strength and further improvements in postural control and functional score. Previous studies have confirmed that BFRT combined with low-load resistance training exerts similar effects on muscle functional adaptation as high-load resistance training [55, 57]. Additionally, lower levels of pain and perceived fatigue have been observed during and after BFRT [6]. BFRT can also reportedly increase muscle strength and volume in musculoskeletal populations [52]. Overall, the findings of this study were similar to those found in other populations.

Recent research [11, 26] demonstrates that balance training is similar to strength training in improving dynamic postural control. This finding is interesting because these two interventions have different therapy goals (balance training focuses on improving proprioception, whereas strength training focuses on strengthening the ankle muscles typically affected by ankle sprains). This finding may reflect the beneficial effects of muscle strength on postural control. Improving the strength of ankle muscles may allow individuals with CAI to compensate for changes in ankle ligament mechanoreceptor activity [17]. However, traditional ankle rehabilitation training may not be optimal. Supplementing ankle rehabilitation training with blood-flow restriction may be more clinically effective because findings show that BFRT is superior to ankle rehabilitation training in improving postural control and ankle-muscle strength.

As the main dorsiflexor of the ankle, the tibialis anterior plays an important role in maintaining ankle stability. Increased activation of the tibialis anterior muscle helps increase the dorsiflexion angle of the ankle joint, enabling CAI individuals to better buffer the ground impact force and reduce their risk of lower limb injuries [4]. Additionally, tibialis anterior weakness and/or decreased activation can reduce constraint on the talonavicular joint, leading to increased internal rotation of the foot and exposing the lateral ankle structures to greater stresses [44, 56]. Current evidence suggests that BFRT significantly increases tibialis anterior activation in CAI individuals. In other words, the BFRT shows “safer” biomechanics than the control group. Considering the function of the tibialis anterior, higher tibialis anterior activation may help reduce the risk of ankle sprain in individuals with CAI. Evidence suggests that BFRT positively affects muscle activation compared with functional training [48]. Killinger et al. [24] indicates that combining resistance eversion and dorsiflexion exercises with BFRT can significantly increase the activation of the peroneus longus and tibialis anterior muscles in CAI individuals. Overall, these results suggest that BFRT has a positive impact on promoting ankle-muscle activation.

However, not all outcomes improved significantly following BFRT. Specifically, no significant effects were found for YBT posteromedial and posterolateral reach distance, ankle plantarflexion, inversion/eversion strength, or peroneus longus activation. Most included studies applied BFRT at the proximal thigh. As an anterior muscle, the tibialis anterior is more involved in anterior postural control and dorsiflexion tasks, which may make it more responsive to the metabolic and neuromuscular adaptations induced by this BFRT protocol. In contrast, posteromedial/posterolateral stability and inversion/eversion control rely more heavily on frontal-plane stability and regulation by the peroneal musculature. Therefore, the current BFRT protocols may not have provided sufficient task-specific stimulation for these functions. Additionally, the limited number of studies and small sample sizes may have reduced the statistical power to detect significant differences.

Comparison with other studies

Some recent evidence points to the superiority of BFRT over control group in improving muscle strength and functional score in individuals after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction [13, 52]. Overall, the results of the current study were similar to those found in individuals with anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction. Our findings supported and strengthened previous ones indicating BFRT’s positive effect on muscle-strength improvement in individuals with CAI. To exclude factors that might influence the outcomes, we conducted the most comprehensive regression analyses possible for all outcomes. The meta-regression results indicated that the proportion of male participants influenced the ankle muscle strength outcomes, with effect sizes tending to increase as the proportion of males increased. This finding may be attributed to sex-related differences in muscle characteristics. Compared with females, males generally exhibit greater muscle mass, higher absolute strength, and a larger proportion of Type II muscle fibers, which contribute to superior explosive power performance [41]. Since our strength metrics primarily reflect short-term maximal output, studies with a higher proportion of male participants may yield larger effect sizes. Furthermore, the heterogeneity of control groups may influence the observed efficacy of BFRT. For instance, the relative benefits of BFRT may vary across different control interventions, such as BT and SBT (ST plus BT), thereby limiting the universal applicability of the conclusions. Therefore, these findings should be applied with caution in CAI clinical practice, and future studies using standardized protocols are required to resolve these discrepancies.

Clinical implications

In addition to proprioception, the neuromuscular control and restoration of muscle strength are the physiologic bases for effective CAI rehabilitation. Therefore, measures aimed at enhancing strength may be beneficial in CAI. For the management of CAI, the results of the current meta-analysis supported the effectiveness of BFRT. In other words, if a physician is choosing a safe and low-cost treatment to improve postural control and ankle function in an individual with CAI, applying BFRT in addition to ankle rehabilitation training may be a better choice. Notably, given the low or very low quality of the available evidence, although the currently available literature is promising, more high-quality studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm the findings. The ideal duration has not been specified. However, a duration of 4–6 weeks is reportedly sufficient to improve postural control and functional score and has been recommended for clinical use [37]. Among the included literature, intervention duration ranges from 4 weeks to 6 weeks. Regression analysis showed that the Total number of interventions did not have a significant impact on the outcome.

Limitations

This study has some shortcomings. First, some RCTs in this study lacked appropriate blinding, which may have introduced performance and detection bias. Awareness of intervention allocation by participants or assessors may have influenced training performance and outcome assessment, potentially leading to an overestimation of the treatment effects. Second, the small sample size and the limited number of studies may have reduced the reliability of our findings. Third, we acknowledge the substantial heterogeneity in the ankle muscle strength outcomes. To address this, we conducted comprehensive meta-regression analyses and found that the proportion of male participants may have contributed to the observed variability. Fourth, although meta-regression showed no significant effect of age, Jeong et al. included older participants compared with other studies, which may contribute to heterogeneity. Fifth, the source studies contained a limited number of long-term observational reports, and these could not be statistically pooled. The duration of BFRT’s effects on postural control and ankle function in individuals with CAI remains unknown. Finally, dynamic postural control is influenced by multiple factors, including proprioception, sensory reweighting, pain, fear, and joint laxity, not solely by the strength improvements facilitated by BFRT. Future research should consider evaluating these additional factors.

Conclusion

Compared with control group, BFRT may improve the dynamic postural control, ankle-muscle strength, muscle activation, and function score of CAI individuals. However, the interpretation of these findings should be approached with caution due to the low to very low quality of evidence, small sample sizes, and substantial heterogeneity among the included studies. In addition, the underlying mechanisms and long-term effectiveness of BFRT in the management of CAI remain unclear. Therefore, while BFRT may represent a potentially beneficial adjunctive rehabilitation strategy, further rigorously designed RCTs with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods are required before definitive clinical recommendations can be established.
