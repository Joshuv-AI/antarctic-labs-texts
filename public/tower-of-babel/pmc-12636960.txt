Development of the Social Relationship Expectations Scale: protocol for a multi-country, multi-method observational study
Authors: Samia C Akhter-Khan, Theresia Ell, Lilly Buck, Matthias Haucke, Martina Luchetti, Leon Li, Anthony Ong, Kia-Chong Chua, Rosie Mayston, Matthew Prina, Shuyan Liu, Gloria Wong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionWhile loneliness has been recognised as a global public health concern, there are still knowledge gaps about how to prevent or reduce loneliness. The Social Relationship Expectations (SRE) Framework (Akhter-Khan et al, 2023) has been developed to enhance mechanism-based interventions targeting individuals’ expectations for social relationships. However, no scale has yet been developed to measure these expectations. We aim to measure SRE across the six interdependent dimensions identified in the theoretical framework, across diverse settings. This protocol outlines the methodology for developing the SRE scale.Methods and analysisThe scale will be developed using both inductive and deductive techniques in a multicountry observational study. First, items will be extracted from published qualitative studies on loneliness and SRE across 15 lower-middle-income countries and from a qualitative focus group study with older Myanmar and Thai adults. Second, using a Delphi process for item development, experts across five world regions (Africa, the Americas, Asia, Europe and Oceania) will be involved in the item selection and scale creation process. A preliminary item pool will be administered in English, German and Chinese. Classical test theory as well as network analysis will be used to assess the dimensionality of the scale, understand item relationships and clusters, and select the final items for the SRE scale.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval for the scale development has been obtained from King’s College London (reference number: MRSP-24/25-46512). Informed consent will be obtained from all participants prior to completing the cognitive interviews and online surveys. Results will be disseminated in peer-reviewed journals in collaboration with coauthors across different countries and disciplines.Keywords: Percieved Social Support, Social Interaction, Psychometrics, Depression & mood disorders, MENTAL HEALTH, PUBLIC HEALTH

Introduction

The WHO recognises loneliness as a global public health issue.1 Loneliness is not only a negative psychological experience but also a risk factor for dementia, Alzheimer’s disease, depression, stroke and mortality.2,5 However, preventing and reducing loneliness is challenging, as interventions have been shown to not be successful when using a one-size-fits-all approach.6 7 Instead, reviews recommend targeting interventions to specific subgroups or individuals.7,9 Tailoring interventions has remained challenging, as loneliness is a complex and multifaceted construct.8 The psychological literature emphasises that loneliness is not equivalent to objective social isolation (e.g., limited social network with few friends, family members or acquaintances and physical isolation) but is influenced by subjective perceptions and expectations of social interactions.10 11 Despite the growing body of research on loneliness, there remains a gap in understanding the specific role that social relationship expectations (SRE) play in the development of loneliness.

The cognitive discrepancy theory of loneliness posits that loneliness arises when there is a mismatch between an individual’s expectations for their social relationships and their actual experiences.10 Expanding on this theory, the SRE Framework was recently developed, underscoring the importance of expectations as a core mechanism driving feelings of loneliness.12 That is, individuals may feel lonely not solely due to their number and quality of social contacts but also due to their contacts not meeting their relationship expectations. Existing scales for assessing loneliness, such as the UCLA Loneliness Scale (an indirect measure of loneliness)13 or the De Jong Gierveld (DJG) Loneliness Scale (an indirect measure of social and emotional loneliness),14 15 focus predominantly on the subjective experience of loneliness or the frequency and quality of social interactions. Whereas scales have been developed to assess basic psychological needs, including autonomy, competence and relatedness, only the dimension for relatedness (i.e., feeling close to and cared for by others) seems to be relevant to loneliness.16 Importantly, existing scales do not comprehensively address unmet expectations leading to loneliness—the core mechanism—limiting their ability to guide effective interventions. Since these expectations vary across cultures, there is a need for a scale that considers cross-cultural variation regarding the core mechanism of loneliness beyond Western, high-resource populations.

The development of the SRE Scale aims to address this gap by assessing the specific expectations individuals have about their social relationships across various dimensions.12 Our proposed SRE scale aims to be a novel and comprehensive tool for understanding and addressing the core mechanism of loneliness, with its development informed by research in both lower-middle-income and high-income countries. The SRE Framework hypothesises six expectations that change across the lifespan and individual contexts: proximity (having social contacts available), support (feeling cared for and able to rely on others), intimacy (feeling close, understood and listened to), fun (sharing interests and enjoyable experiences), generativity (having opportunities to contribute meaningfully) and respect (feeling valued and actively included).12 Whereas current scales only indicate whether a person feels lonely or not, the SRE scale could indicate which specific expectations are not met for a given person, thereby offering a more targeted approach for interventions. Moreover, expectations about social relationships, as well as their fulfilment, can vary depending on individual variables such as personality, past experiences, socioeconomic status and health status.12 Cultural contexts also play a crucial role, as societal norms and values shape what individuals consider to be adequate or desirable social relationships.12 Additionally, these expectations are not static and may evolve across the lifespan, influenced by developmental stages, life transitions and ageing processes.12 Therefore, the SRE scale, in line with its theory, will be designed to account for these contextual variations, ensuring its applicability across diverse populations and life stages.

Developing the SRE scale will have significant implications for research and clinical practice. First, by focusing on SRE and their contextual variability, the SRE scale will provide insights, in addition to current loneliness scales, into the cognitive processes underlying loneliness, which can differentiate between individuals who feel lonely—despite frequent social interactions and a large social network—and those who do not. Second, the focus on SRE will allow for the development of tailored interventions aimed at recalibrating unrealistic expectations or enhancing the quality of social relationships to meet these expectations. Hence, the new scale can clarify “what people expect from their social ties, how those expectations evolve over time, and why certain discrepancies may be more distressing in specific contexts” (p.5).17 Third, the SRE scale can be employed in both clinical and non-clinical settings, making it a versatile tool for researchers and practitioners in psychology, psychiatry and social work. It can also be used in public health initiatives to address loneliness at a community level.

This protocol leverages expert consensus and cutting-edge statistical methods to develop a robust and theoretically grounded SRE scale, with the aim of advancing research in psychology and affiliated social sciences. While the aim of the SRE scale is not to replace existing loneliness scales, its development will address a critical gap in loneliness research and contribute to a more nuanced understanding of the psychological mechanisms at play. Ultimately, our aim is that this scale will be an essential tool for researchers and clinicians aiming to explore and mitigate the cognitive bases of loneliness and to enhance well-being and social connectedness in diverse populations.

The objectives of the present study are threefold: (1) to develop a comprehensive item pool reflecting the six theoretical dimensions of the SRE Framework; (2) to refine the SRE Scale using expert consensus through the Delphi method18 and (3) to construct the SRE scale using classical test theory and network analysis techniques.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This study will use a multi-method design involving qualitative item generation through expert consultation and iterative Delphi rounds and quantitative analysis using classical test theory and network analysis. The procedure for the scale development will follow the best practices for multi-country scale development outlined by Zhao et al,19 specifically, steps 1–7 (item and scale development). Overall, the process for developing the SRE scale will involve three phases: Comprehensive item generation, prioritisation of items through a Delphi process, and the assessment of the initial SRE Scale in three different languages: English, German and Chinese (figure 1).

Phase 1: comprehensive item generation

Items will be generated using deductive and inductive approaches. The domains of the scale are based on the conceptual model of the SRE Framework, that is, the core mechanism of loneliness, developed by Akhter-Khan et al.12 Initial items will be developed from the SRE Framework based on (1) a published systematic review of qualitative studies in lower-income and lower-middle-income countries,20 (2) existing loneliness scales and (3) a published qualitative study that included focus group discussions with Thai older adults and Myanmar migrants in northern Thailand.21 These studies will be used to develop the initial pool of items reflecting the six SRE: proximity, support, intimacy, fun, generativity and respect.

Phase 2: prioritisation of items through a Delphi process

Candidate items from phase 1 will be evaluated by an expert panel. Specialists in psychology, social sciences and psychometrics will participate in a three-step Delphi process to generate and refine the item pool in English. Experts with a minimum of 5 years of research experience and at least two peer-reviewed publications on social relationships, social connection or loneliness from various disciplines will be invited to participate. Project collaborators will identify international professional networks across different regions and contact them to distribute the invitation to their members, for example, the Global Initiative on Loneliness and Connection. Experts will be selected based on their level of expertise on social relationships research (eg, PhD candidate, 5 years of research experience), geographical region (as diverse as possible), age (including experts across the lifespan) and gender (approximating an equal distribution). This broad, multidisciplinary approach aims to maximise global engagement and participation. Additionally, the project will be promoted through relevant social media channels to reach a wider professional audience.

The first and second round of the Delphi process will be an international online survey, allowing diverse researchers from various regions to participate anonymously, thereby minimising the influence of dominant groups or individuals. The third round will be an online consensus meeting to finalise the structure and content of the SRE Scale. This process will prioritise and refine the list of items for inclusion in the SRE scale. The Delphi process will be conducted over a period of 6 months. All participants who completed round 1 will be invited to participate in round 2. Attrition rates of experts between rounds will be monitored and analysed.

For round 1 of the Delphi process, the comprehensive list of SRE will be transformed into survey questions. Experts will be introduced to the SRE Framework and will be asked to evaluate items based on their importance to each dimension of the SRE using a Likert scale ranging from 1 (not important) to 9 (highly important), following the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation methodology for determining item relevance.22 Experts will also have the opportunity to suggest additional items. A minimum of three coauthors with expertise in scale development or qualitative research will review the survey questions to ensure clarity and suitability prior to dissemination to experts. The language will be adapted to be clear and accessible for experts from various cultural and geographic backgrounds. A pilot test with a diverse group of professionals from regions such as Europe, Asia, Africa and the Americas will be conducted to ensure the survey’s face validity before its official distribution.

Following the two survey rounds, the expert panel, composed of our international research team, will meet to establish consensus on the final items included in the survey and the answer format. Experts will be asked to confirm the items classified as ‘consensus in’ or ‘consensus out’ based on the Delphi survey results, following the procedure outlined by Potter et al.23 If there are items that do not reach consensus, experts will anonymously rescore them. Facilitated discussions and subsequent rounds of revoting will continue until consensus is reached. As there is no clear guideline on how many experts must be included in a Delphi process to reach consensus, we will aim for an estimated sample size of 30 experts across disciplines participating in both rounds, of which at least 10 will participate in the consensus meeting.

Phase 3: assessment of the initial SRE scale in three different languages

The items from the initial SRE scale developed in the first two phases will be translated and back-translated by bilingual researchers from our team to German and Chinese using the Brislin procedure, which includes translating items into the target language, back-translating into the source language by an independent translator, and resolving inconsistencies through discussion.19 Cognitive interviews will be conducted with a minimum of 10 people for each of the three languages to assess which questions correspond to the dimensions and to facilitate understanding of items.19 The planned sample sizes are based on empirical results showing that most of the new information in a qualitative dataset is obtained in the first five to six interviews and that 92% data saturation is reached after 10 interviews.24 Participants will be recruited using purposive sampling to ensure heterogeneity in the sample regarding factors that may affect relationship expectations and their fulfilment, including age, gender, socioeconomic status (eg, education), past experiences (eg, migration) and personality (eg, extraversion, openness to experience). Following cognitive interviews and final adjustments to the items, the preliminary SRE scale will be administered to 600 participants in an online survey in English (n=200), German (n=200) and Chinese (n=200). Participants will be recruited using an online data collection panel (eg, Rakuten Insight). Participants aged ≥18 years who speak English, German or Chinese will be eligible to participate in the survey. In addition to the SRE scale, we will also include the multidimensional DJG Loneliness Short Scale, which has previously been validated in German,15 English25 and Chinese.26 We acknowledge the limitation that online panels may lead to sampling biases towards younger and more educated individuals. We will conduct exploratory analyses to evaluate whether factor structures remain stable across different subgroups, for example, regarding age and education. While such biases are less critical in the development phase, they will be explicitly addressed in the validation phase, during which representative sampling techniques will be applied (eg, by using population weights) to assess the scale’s psychometric properties and generalisability.

Data analysis

Data will be collected using an online software (eg, Rakuten, Formr), stored in a password-protected cloud database, and analysed via R. For phase 2, following the consensus definitions by Potter et al, the proportion of experts scoring each item as ‘not important’ (score 1–3), ‘equivocal’ (score 4–6) and ‘very important’ (score 7–9) will be calculated in order to classify items as ‘consensus in’ that are scored as very important (7–9) by ≥70% and not important (1–3) by <15% of the expert group—and ‘consensus out’ for those that are scored as not important (1–3) by ≥70% and very important (7–9) by <15% of the experts.23 If none of the criteria are met, no consensus is reached, and items will need to be voted on at the online consensus meeting. For further details on the consensus definitions, please refer to Potter et al.23

The optimal sample size for scale development remains a topic of debate in the literature. We aim for 200 participants per country (N=600) based on the following recommendations. First, several sources suggest a range of 5–15 respondents per item.27,29 Thus, collecting data from 200 participants per country (USA, Germany and Hong Kong) should more than suffice to meet the recommended range of 5–15 respondents per item in phase 3. Second, Knekta et al30 emphasise that sample size requirements for exploratory factor analysis (EFA) depend on study-specific characteristics such as the number of factors, items per factor, communalities and factor loadings rather than on a single effect size. Under moderately good conditions (eg, communalities of 0.40–0.70, ≥3 items per factor), a sample of 200 participants is typically sufficient for stable factor recovery. For the SRE scale, we anticipate meeting these conditions. That is, we expect each of the six expectations to be measured with at least three items, and we expect item communalities to fall within the recommended range. Item communalities were generally >0.50 in previous studies that pertained to similar theoretical constructs, factor numbers and sample sizes as our study, eg, a recent study on the Relational Needs Satisfaction Scale.31 Our planned sample size, therefore, aligns with established guidance for EFA and scale development.

The first step in our analysis will be the reduction of items. We will exclude any items with cumulative missing data exceeding 30%. Following this, EFA will be applied to reduce the item pool and explore the dimensional structure of the scale. We will conduct and compare separate EFAs for the total sample as well as for each country individually, with the goal of identifying a set of items and a factor solution that is well-interpretable and works similarly across the countries. EFA is a widely used statistical method to identify the underlying factors or dimensions that explain the variability in participant responses.32 It is appropriate in this context, as no prior factor structure for the items has yet been established. The goal of EFA is to group items together that measure similar content and identify those that are redundant or irrelevant to the underlying dimensions. This process results in a refined version of the scale that more accurately reflects the theoretical dimensions of the SRE Framework. The number of factors in the EFA will be determined based on a combination of extraction criteria such as parallel analysis33 and Empirical Kaiser Criterion34 as well as theoretical considerations and factor interpretability. To further quantify whether the factor structure is comparable across the different cultural or linguistic groups, we will conduct a Multi-Group Confirmatory Factor Analysis (MGCFA), the main statistical technique for multicountry scale development.19 This method tests cross-language measurement invariance by constraining measurement parameters, such as factor loadings and intercepts, to be equal across groups.35 For the MGCFA, the weighted least squares means and variance adjusted model estimation method will be used for categorical data and robust maximum likelihood for continuous data.36 The model fit will be assessed using several indices, including the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) and Standardised Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). A good model fit will be indicated by RMSEA <0.08, CFI>0.95, TLI >0.95 and SRMR <0.06.37 The goal of the MGCFA analyses, combined with the EFA results, is to identify and select the most invariant set of items and factor solutions (ie, regarding conceptual meaning, sensitivity and relevance) across countries.

Additionally, network analysis will be used to model the relationships between scale items and assess the impact of each item on others within the network. In this approach, items are represented as nodes and their associations as edges, which enables the visualisation of the strength and nature of connections between items.38 This method provides a detailed understanding of the structure and interconnections between items, helping to identify items that play a significant role in the network.39 Centrality measures, such as node strength or betweenness, can be used to highlight influential or crucial items, guiding the refinement of the item pool.40 41 Moreover, the network analysis will allow an exploration of how SRE relates to loneliness, as measured by the DJG loneliness scale.

To assess the accuracy of the estimated network, we will employ bootstrap routines to analyse the stability of centrality indices and determine whether network connections and centrality estimates vary across variables.42 Accordingly, we will report the correlation stability coefficient and present the results of the bootstrapped difference test for edge weights and centrality indices. In addition, to compare the networks across countries, the Network Comparison Test will be used.43 This method employs resampling-based permutation testing to compare network structures, edge strength and global strength across different groups, allowing for the detection of country-specific differences in the network structure while maintaining an overall structure that captures common patterns.

By combining both EFA and network analysis, we aim to derive the most comprehensive insights into the structure of the SRE scale, taking into account the cultural contexts of the three countries—the USA, Germany and Hong Kong. EFA serves as the primary method to explore the factor structure and to identify whether the data align with the theoretical constructs of the SRE Framework in each country. In contrast, network analysis offers a complementary perspective, providing a dynamic and deeper understanding of how items interact within the scale across the different cultural settings.

If network analysis and factor analysis yield divergent conclusions, reconciliation involves evaluating their assumptions, empirical fit and theoretical implications. Factor analysis assumes that latent variables underlie item correlations, whereas network analysis views constructs as systems of interconnected variables. However, in cases where EFA and network analysis suggest divergent structures, item selection will primarily follow the EFA results, given its stability and established role in scale development.44 Network analysis findings will be considered supplementary. Items with high centrality but weak EFA loadings may be retained if there is a strong theoretical rationale or practical relevance. This approach reflects the complementary nature of the two methods, acknowledging that EFA captures latent dimensions while network analysis emphasises inter-item interactions.42 If discrepancies arise, another possible next step could be to explore hybrid approaches such as Exploratory Graph Analysis.40 Assessing practical implications can further clarify which model best represents the data. Differences in the results of the EFA and network analysis could help uncover a more nuanced understanding of the assessed constructs. Overall, this dual approach strengthens the development of the SRE scale by accounting for potential cultural differences and enhances its ability to capture the complexity of SRE in diverse contexts.

Patient and public involvement

The consensus meeting of the Delphi process (round 3) will feature a presentation and discussion of the selected items, with contributions from people who feel lonely (or have felt lonely before) to ensure that items are understandable and relevant. Participants with lived experience of loneliness will have the same voting rights as scientific experts and will be equally weighted in the consensus meeting. Following the Delphi process, all items will be discussed and revised based on the input from the diverse participants who participated in the cognitive interviews conducted in three languages.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval for this project has been obtained from the institutional review board at King’s College London (MRSP-24/25-46512). The study will follow the Declaration of Helsinki and ensure informed consent, confidentiality and voluntary participation. For the cognitive interviews, informed consent will be acquired by written (where possible) or verbal consent. For the anonymous submission of the online survey, a written description of the research will be provided to all potential participants at the start of the survey, and it will be made clear that the submission of the survey indicates the granting of consent. Results will be submitted for publication in peer-reviewed journals and presented at relevant international conferences.

The funder has no role in the design, collection, analysis and interpretation of the data.
