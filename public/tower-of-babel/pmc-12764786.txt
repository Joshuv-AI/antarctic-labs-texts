Chain-ordered Pt atoms with delocalized electronic structure for improved hydrogen production
Authors: Xiaoru Sang, Peikun Zhang, Wenyan Shi, Xiuyun An, Zude Shi, Linyuan Chen, Hang Xia, Yubin Jiang, Jinbo Wang, Tomasz Kosmala, Caitian Gao, Chengshi Gong, Xiankui Wei, Zhuhua Zhang, Yongmin He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As the smallest one-dimensional structure, single-atom chains exhibit many one-dimensional quantized behaviors, such as Tomonaga-Luttinger liquid behavior, high-temperature superconductivity, and fluctuations. In contrast to single-atom catalysts, there is growing interest in exploring whether and how the point-to-point interaction occurs between neighboring metal atoms in 1D chains. Here, we controllably fabricate Pt chains and single atoms in MoS2 films with similar coordination environments and conduct model studies on their catalytic behaviors in hydrogen production. Combining calculations with field effect transistor and micro-electrochemical measurements, we reveal that the chains exhibit significant delocalized electron densities, which underpins their metallic behavior and engenders a fascinating center site with lower Gibbs free energy than that of single atoms. Notably, we demonstrate that the chain density can be well-tuned, achieving a maximum of 2.82 Pt/nm2, which enables a competitive turnover frequency and wafer-scale hydrogen production. This work offers insights into our understanding of the possible synergistic effect between metal atoms in a 1D arrangement for electrocatalysis.

Introduction

With the development of advanced fabrication methods and atomic-imaging techniques over the past few decades, it is now possible to tailor nanomaterials down to an atomic scale. For instance, 0D single atoms have been dispersed on substrates, and both 1D single-atom chains and 2D single-atom layers have been realized. For example, graphene and hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN) are representative single-atom layer materials in which atoms are arranged in two-dimensional (2D) honeycomb lattices, showing promising applications1,2. Another example is single-atom catalysts, where metals are dispersed as isolated atoms, demonstrating high activity and selectivity in heterogeneous catalysis3–7. Compared with the structures mentioned above, single-atom chains made up of metal-atom strings are considered the most straightforward 1D system, attracting intense attention in the physical field. For example, previous studies have reported that they could serve as ideal model systems for investigating condensed matter theories (e.g., high-temperature superconductivity8, topological phases9, and quantum spin Hall effect10) as well as complex phenomena in many-body systems (e.g., Aubry-type leptonics11, the final state12, Majorana fermions13, and chiral solitons14,15).

However, despite extensive research on single-atom catalysts, few studies have focused on single-atom chains in catalysis. This is primarily due to the formidable challenge of precisely arranging single atoms into 1D arrays, which often limits their catalytic potential. Although a series of fabrication methods, e.g., mechanically controllable breaking junction (MCBJ)16–20, twisted 2D bilayer materials21, self-assembly22–27, and ligand-assisted synthesis28–33, have been developed, these approaches require strict growth conditions that greatly restrict their catalytic applicability. For example, single-atom chains prepared via MCBJ typically suffer from short length (<5 atoms in length) and structural fragility, while self-assembled atom wires are extremely unstable in an atmosphere.

In our recent work, we reported a wafer-scale network of Pt single-atom chains in transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs) films grown by a chemical vapour co-deposition method34. The obtained atomic chains have an average length of up to ~17 nm and are highly stable in air and solution. Encouragingly, this strategy is expected to offer a promising opportunity to study the catalytic behavior of single-atom chains. More importantly, compared with single-atom catalysts with isolated single atoms, this system can serve as an ideal 1D model for exploring potential point-to-point interaction between neighboring metal atoms along 1D chains, thereby clarifying how their synergistic mechanism arises. Furthermore, it could help guide the design of 1D-arranged active sites to improve catalytic performance.

In this work, we conducted model studies to investigate how the catalytic behavior of Pt chains differs from that of Pt single atoms, and then explored their potential toward hydrogen production. First, we performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations to examine their electronic structures and screen their possible active sites, demonstrating the strong electronic delocalization of Pt atoms in chains. Next, using a mass flow-controlled chemical vapor deposition (CVD) method, we precisely controlled the formation of Pt chains and isolated single atoms with similar coordination environments. This approach enabled a detailed investigation of their electronic and catalytic properties through field-effect transistors (FET) and micro-electrochemical devices. Finally, we tuned and optimized the chain density to a maximum of 2.82 Pt/nm2, yielding numerous vertex structures that further contributed to hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) performance, that is, an overpotential of less than 10 mV at the current density of 10 mA/cm2.

To comprehend the origin of those active sites, we further calculated their band structures and projected densities of states (DOS), as shown in Fig. 1d and h. In the Pt atom-MoS2 system, introducing isolated single atoms in the MoS2 lattice appears to create new defect energy levels near the Fermi level35, thereby lowering the position of the conduction band edge and consequently reducing the overall band gap to 0.47 eV (Fig. 1d). By contrast, when Pt atoms are arranged into 1D chains in MoS2, we observed significant delocalized electron densities between adjacent Pt atoms, which increases the DOS across the band gap and imparts metallic behavior to the Pt chains (Fig. 1h). Consequently, the electron state near the Fermi level could provides an abundant supply of electrons for proto-hydrogen adsorption36–38. Besides, the partial charge densities indicate the considerable interaction of Pt-Pt and Pt-S for the Pt chain-MoS2 system (Supplementary Fig 2). Therefore, compared with isolated Pt atoms, 1D-arrayed Pt chains display pronounced electronic delocalization, which significantly modifies the electronic structure of Pt MoS2 systems, correlating with enhanced HER activity.

Pt chains and isolated single atoms in MoS2 were synthesized using a mass flow-controlled CVD method (Supplementary Fig 3). The typical procedure comprises two steps: i) pre-deposition of Pt atomic clusters on the SiO2/Si substrate by electron beam evaporation, forming Pt nanoclusters with sizes ranging from 0.6 to 1.5 nm dispersed across the SiO2/Si substrate (Supplementary Fig 4); ii) the fabrication of either 1D Pt chains or isolated Pt atoms can be obtained by growing MoS2 on those substrates at different metal precursor mass fluxes. For 1D Pt chains, the growth process is similar to our previous work34, where MoO3 was used as metal precursors to facilitate a “surf-zip” growth mechanism. However, for isolated Pt atoms, salt (e.g., NaCl in our experiment) was added to generate volatile MoOCl4 precursors, which significantly increased the MoS2 growth rate and thereby inhibited the above “surf-zip” kinetic process39,40. As a result, Pt atoms were randomly isolated within the MoS2 lattices.

To probe the atomic structures of Pt chain-MoS2 and Pt atom-MoS2 samples, we employed scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) in combination with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping. We observed a uniform distribution of Pt, S, and Mo in both films (Fig. 2e and f), confirming the incorporation of Pt in both samples. Furthermore, the EDS data clearly demonstrate a Mo/S atomic ratio of approximately 1:2 (Supplementary Table 2). Figure 2g shows that Pt chains have lengths ranging from 1.2 to 5.3 nm, and their angles are close to 60° (Supplementary Fig 7), consistent with the rotation of the MoS2 crystal structure (Supplementary Fig 8). The distance between adjacent Pt atoms is around 3.4 Å (right in Fig. 2g), a value larger than that of the conventional Pt–Pt bonds (e.g., 2.78 Å in Pt (111) surface46). In contrast, Pt atoms in the Pt atom-MoS2 sample are uniformly dispersed without a chain-like arrangement (Fig. 2h). Notably, the Pt intensity profiles measured are similar to the Pt atom-MoS2 sample, suggesting their identical Pt-S bonds. This conclusion could be rationalized by our simulation analysis of annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (ADF-STEM) imaging, confirming that the Pt atoms in the Pt chain-MoS₂ are embedded within the MoS2 film and bonded to S atoms (Supplementary Fig 9a). In contrast, the Pt atoms in the Pt atom-MoS2 sample occupy Mo vacancies while similarly bonding to adjacent S atoms (Supplementary Fig 9b). Therefore, the coordination environments of Pt atoms in both samples are similar, and their STEM signal intensities are comparable (Fig. 2g and h). Together, these results indicate that our mass flow-controlled CVD method enables the growth of Pt chains and isolated Pt atoms in MoS2 films with similar Pt coordination, providing a platform to study distinct properties solely based on their 1D atomic arrangement.

Next, we fabricated FET devices based on Pt chain-MoS2 and Pt atom-MoS2 samples (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig 10). The resulting Ids–Vds curves are linear and symmetrical, reflecting good electrical contact between the Cr/Au electrode and the films (Fig. 3b). As shown in Fig. 3c, pure MoS2 displays n-type semiconducting behavior, in agreement with previous FET measurements42,47, whereas Pt atom-MoS2 reveals weak p-type modulation, implying that the introduction of Pt atoms significantly modifies the electronic structures of the MoS2 host materials. Furthermore, despite similar Pt content, the Pt chain-MoS2 device demonstrates higher conductance and markedly reduced modulation compared to the Pt atom-MoS2 counterpart. Micro-electrochemical devices were also used to evaluate the HER activity of Pt atom-MoS2 and Pt chain-MoS2 with the same Pt loading (0.1 Pt/nm2), as illustrated in Fig. 3d. To eliminate the interference from the contact barrier between the Au electrode and MoS2, a CVD-grown graphene layer was employed as the electron-injection substrate, ensuring reliable microcell measurements43,44,48–51. The fabrication procedures of the micro-electrochemical device are shown in Supplementary Fig 11. Figure 3e and f present the typical linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) polarization curves and the corresponding Tafel slopes, respectively. It is evident that the Pt chain-MoS2 delivers an overpotential of 155 mV at 10 mA cm-2 and Tafel slope of 84 mV dec-1, demonstrating enhanced HER activity over Pt atom-MoS2 and pure MoS2 (Pt atoms: η10 is 320 mV, Tafel slope is 123 mV dec-1; pure MoS2: η10 is 389 mV, Tafel slope is 135 mV dec-1). These results confirm that the center sites located at the Pt chains exhibit an optimized Gibbs free energy for HER, as predicted by the theoretical calculations above. By combining experimental measurements with theoretical calculations, we demonstrate that point-to-point interactions between neighboring metal atoms along 1D chains contribute to metallic behavior and enhanced catalytic activity compared to isolated atom structures.

Building on the insights gained from the above model studies on single-atom and chain systems, we now aim to increase the density of Pt chains for practical HER applications. Although a higher density typically requires a greater amount of Pt atomic precursors, prolonged deposition times result in precursor aggregation into large nanoparticles that are detrimental to Pt chain formation. To obtain high-density, small-size nanoclusters, we adopted several strategies: i) lowering the substrate temperature (nearly at 15 °C) during the deposition process in our e-beam setup; ii) shortening the deposition time to 1 min while increasing the evaporation cycles to 5-10 times, thereby facilitating secondary nucleation of Pt nanoclusters on the substrate. As a result, the as-deposited Pt precursors with different densities could be distributed uniformly without forming large clusters, as confirmed by our high-angle angular dark field-scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) imaging (Supplementary Fig 13). In contrast, suboptimal deposition conditions produced large clusters during growth (Supplementary Fig 12). Benefiting from the above strategies, the density of Pt chains could be effectively controlled (Fig. 4a), achieving a high density of up to 2.82 Pt/nm2, corresponding to an atom ratio of 3.96%, as confirmed by STEM and EDS analyses (Supplementary Fig 14). Notably, an increase in the density not only lengthens the chains (Fig. 4b) but also, more importantly, increases the number of chain vertices in the samples (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig 15). Specifically, the two S atoms connected to the Pt atom at the vertex form a smaller angle in the first coordination shell, leading to a higher concentration of delocalized electrons at the vertex compared with straight chains, as shown in Fig. 4e. Figure 5a and b shows the polarization curves and the corresponding Tafel plots for samples with varied Pt densities measured in 0.5 M H2SO4. It is clear that as the Pt density increases from 0.11 to 2.82 Pt/nm2, the HER catalytic activity of Pt chain-MoS2 gradually approaches that of Pt metal. Specifically, the overpotential at 10 mA cm−2 decreases from 615 mV to 9.98 mV (Fig. 5a), and the Tafel slope declines from 138 mV dec⁻¹ to 58 mV dec⁻¹ (Fig. 5b). Furthermore, the turnover frequency (TOF) for H2 evolution, normalized by the number of Pt atoms, is shown in Fig. 5c (see calculation details in Supplementary Note 1). . Remarkably, a higher density of Pt chains delivers an improved TOF at a lower potential, which can be attributed to two factors: i) the increased number of vertex sites and ii) the enhanced conductance of the films (Supplementary Fig 17)53. Finally, as a proof-of-concept for hydrogen generation, a centimeter-scale high-chain-density film was transferred onto an Au substrate for mass production of hydrogen, as shown in Fig. 5d. A large amount of H2 bubbles was produced during the reaction, demonstrating the tantalizing potential of Pt chain catalysts for practical hydrogen production. In summary, we have both theoretically predicted and experimentally confirmed that Pt chains show distinct catalytic behavior compared to single atoms, and further applied these Pt chains for wafer-scale hydrogen production. These chain and single-atom structures, grown using a mass flow-controlled CVD method, share similar coordination environments, as supported by our complementary characterizations. Combined device measurements and DFT calculations suggest that Pt chains exhibit metallic behavior and feature fascinating center sites with competitive Gibbs free energy compared to single atoms, potentially linked to the substantial delocalized electron densities in the chains. Notably, the chain density can be tuned to a maximum value up to 2.82 Pt/nm2, forming numerous vertex sites that significantly contribute to improved HER activity. Methods

Growth of Pt chain-MoS2 and Pt atom-MoS2

Synthesis of Pt chain-MoS2 films

Pt nanoclusters were deposited on clean SiO2/Si substrates using e-beam evaporation with a rate of 0.01 Å/s for deposition times of 20 s, 50 s, 100 s, and 300 s, respectively. These Pt-coated SiO2/Si substrates were then employed in the chemical vapor deposition (CVD) of MoS2 films. In this process, substrates were placed in a face-down configuration on an aluminum oxide boat containing MoO3 powders (AR, 99.5%, Aladdin) at the center of a quartz tube reaction chamber, while sulfur powders (CP, 99.5%, Changlian Shanghai) were loaded in another crucible upstream in the tube. After purging the CVD system with 200 sccm of Ar for 5 minutes, the center of the furnace was heated from room temperature (25 °C) to growth temperature at a rate of ~50 °C/min under continuous 60 sccm of Ar flow. The Pt chain-MoS2 films are synthesized by maintaining a temperature of 650 °C for 4 minutes for the Mo sources, while the S sources are maintained at around 230 °C. Finally, the furnace cools naturally under the protective argon atmosphere.

Synthesis of Pt atom-MoS2 films

The Pt nanoclusters are deposited on clean SiO2/Si substrates using e-beam evaporation with a speed of 0.01 Å/s for 100 s. The as-prepared Pt-coated SiO2/Si substrates are used as the substrate for the chemical vapor deposition (CVD) of MoS2 films, facing down on an aluminum oxide boat containing mixtures (MoO3 powders and NaCl powders (AR, 99.5%, Greagent) in the center of a quartz tube reaction chamber, and sulfur powders are loaded in another crucible upstream in the tube. After purging the CVD system with a 200 sccm argon flow for 5 minutes, the center of the furnace was heated from room temperature (25 °C) to growth temperature at a rate of ~50 °C/min under continuous argon gas flow of 60 sccm. The Pt atom-MoS2 films are synthesized by maintaining a temperature of 650 °C for 4 minutes in the Mo source, while the S source is kept at approximately 230 °C. The furnace cools naturally under the protective argon atmosphere.

Device fabrication

SiO2/Si chips (15 mm × 15 mm, with a 285 nm SiO2 layer) with 32 pre-patterned Au contact pads were fabricated by a conventional photolithography or a peelable-resist PDMS-assisted dry-transfer lithography. The latter includes three typical steps: i) mechanically peelable resist was first spin-coated onto a donor substrate and patterned using a mask aligner (JD6S-A47P) with UV exposure to define the electrode geometry; ii) the resulting resist mask was transferred onto the target chip using a PDMS-assisted stamping technique, followed by metal deposition via thermal evaporation. iii) the resist and excess metal were then removed through tape-based dry stripping, yielding clean and well-defined 32-channel Au electrode arrays. Next, the as-prepared samples were transferred onto the chips using a polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA)-assisted method. Electron-beam lithography (TESCAN VEGA4) was then used to define the metal electrodes on the samples, which were followed by the thermal evaporation of 5 nm Cr and 60 nm Au. Finally, the device chips were coated with a 1 μm PMMA resist layer (ALLRESIST 950 K A8 2500 rpm 60 s), and windows were patterned in the PMMA by electron-beam lithography (Raith 150Two) to expose the designated regions on the samples. After development (1 min in 1:3 methyl isobutyl ketone (MIBK)/ international phonetic alphabet (IPA) solution and 1 min in IPA), the micro-electrochemical devices were dried with Ar gas.

Electronic measurements

Following the device fabrication of the Pt chain-MoS2 and Pt atom-MoS2 on SiO2/Si, the samples were loaded into a vacuum probe station (≈1×10⁻⁴ Torr) at 25 °C. Electrical measurements were then carried out in a SEMISHARE CG-O-4 cryogenic probe station configured with two Keithley 2450 source meters53.

Micro-electrochemical measurements

A three-electrode configuration was employed for the in-situ electrochemical measurement (see the micro-cell schematic in Fig. 3d). All tests were carried out in 0.5 M H2SO4 (PH ≈ 0, electrolyte freshly prepared from ultrapure water prior to each experiment). An Ag/AgCl reference electrode (filled with 3 M KCl) and a high-purity graphite counter electrode were used44. The other electrode in the three-electrode configuration connected to the films served as the working electrode (tungsten needle). Throughout all experiments, catalytic activity was restricted to the exposed regions of the films (60 μm2), with the remaining electrolyte-contacted areas passivated by a PMMA coating. Theoretical calculations

All the theoretical calculations were performed with the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package55–57. The structural optimizations were carried out using the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functional, along with projector-augmented wave (PAW) potentials58. The electronic wave functions were expanded in a plane wave basis set with a kinetic energy cutoff of 500 eV. The electronic wavefunctions were expanded using a plane-wave basis set with a kinetic energy cutoff of 500 eV. For Brillouin zone sampling, a 1 × 10 × 1 Monkhorst–Pack k-point mesh was adopted for the Pt single-atom chain model, and a 5 × 5 × 1 mesh was used for the Pt single-atom model. The electronic iteration convergence threshold was set at 10⁻⁵ eV. All structures were fully relaxed until each atom’s force was <0.01 eV Å−1. A vacuum layer of approximately 15 Å was included in the models to avoid spurious interactions between periodic images. We considered the van der Waals (vdW) interaction using the DFT-D3 method59. The implicit solvent model was considered using VASPsol to describe the adsorption strength in solvent systems60.

Material characterization

The micromorphology and atomic structure of samples were characterized by an optical microscope (NM910-TR, NEXCOPE), a field-emission scanning electron microscope (SEM, TESCAN MIRA 3), and transmission electron microscopes equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (TEM, FEI Tecnai F30, operated at 300 kV and Talos F200S operated at 200 kV). Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) was performed using JEOL ARM-200F (equipped with CEOS CESCOR aberration corrector, operated at 80 kV) and a ThermoScientific Themis Z (operated at 300 kV). In EDS analysis, JEOL’s certified database and ZAF correction models were employed, where Pt Lα is used as the primary reference peak to calculate the Pt mass and atomic ratios. The atomic-resolution HAADF images were deconvoluted using the Richard-Lucy method to enhance the contrast. STEM simulations were performed by the Prismatic Software package61,62. Molecular vibrations and chemical compositions were examined using micro-Raman spectroscopy (Raman, Jobin-Yvon LabRam HR800, 532 nm YAG laser) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Kratos AXIS Ultra, Al Kα source, 1486.6 eV), respectively.
