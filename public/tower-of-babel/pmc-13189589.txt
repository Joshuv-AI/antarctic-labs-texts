From Posttraumatic Lipoma to Hemangioma: 2 Foreign Bodies’ 4-decade Unnoticed Presence
Authors: Yuanbo Kang, Nanze Yu, Xiao Long, Jiuzuo Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Traumatic masses can present a diagnostic challenge, often encompassing both neoplastic and nonneoplastic lesions. This article reports a unique case of a 51-year-old woman with a recurrent subcutaneous mass initially diagnosed as fibrolipoma through multiple surgical excisions. Despite previous histopathologic analyses, the definitive diagnosis was established as a posttraumatic hemangioma with clear traumatic etiology. The patient had a history of a penetrating slingshot injury that went unnoticed until retrospective examination following surgical intervention revealed retained foreign bodies contributing to mass recurrence. Imaging studies indicated irregular nodular formations suggestive of vascular anomalies. This case highlights the importance of considering traumatic factors in the differential diagnosis for plastic surgeons managing soft-tissue masses, and the need for comprehensive surgical excision with intraoperative assessment to prevent misdiagnosis, particularly in recurrent benign tumors. The findings underscored the potential for persistent irritative factors and the limitations of preoperative biopsies in accurately diagnosing complex lesions.

Traumatic masses encompass both neoplastic and nonneoplastic lesions. Neoplastic lesions include posttraumatic lipoma, typically a pseudolipoma caused by fat herniation through fascial defects; hemangioma; and rare angioendotheliomatosis and glioma. Nonneoplastic lesions include fat necrosis, often self-resolving; pseudoaneurysms; and chronic hematomas that frequently manifest with atypical presentations.1–4

Although neoplastic masses are conventionally attributed to genetic predisposition, environmental exposure, or idiopathic pathogenesis, the role of traumatic injury remains underrecognized in clinical diagnosis.5 Given the relatively low incidence of traumatic etiology, current literature predominantly focuses on primary neoplasms, with insufficient attention to trauma-induced mechanisms.

Critically, unresolved foreign bodies, such as organic or metallic microparticles, may trigger inflammatory cascades,1 leading to mass formation. We herein report a remarkable case initially diagnosed as fibrolipoma through multiple pathological examinations, which was ultimately confirmed as posttraumatic hemangioma of the trunk, a rare entity with traumatic etiology, following complete excision.

CASE REPORT

Primary History Taking

A 51-year-old woman presented with a recurrent subcutaneous mass in the right lumbodorsal region (Fig. 1), initially noted in her 20s. The patient had undergone 4 prior local excisions, with histopathologic analysis from the second and fourth procedures confirming fibrolipoma. She denied associated erythema, pain, or ulceration. Physical examination revealed a 5 × 3 cm firm subcutaneous mass with indistinct margins and moderate mobility beneath surgical scars. Preoperative ultrasonography identified a 4.8 × 3.2 × 1.3 cm irregularly contoured mass with relatively defined borders suggestive of nodular coalescence. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated clustered subcutaneous nodules at T12–L2 levels exhibiting T1-weighted hypointensity, T2-weighted hyperintensity, and fat-suppressed T2 hyperintensity, with indistinct margins spanning 3.4 × 1.8 cm (Fig. 2). Computed tomography revealed heterogeneous soft-tissue density with calcification in the right lumbar subcutaneous compartment. Due to recurrent growth following multiple excisions under local anesthesia, the patient requested definitive resection. Consequently, general anesthesia was selected for complete tumor excision.

Intraoperative Finding

Operative dissection using a No. 15 blade exposed the mass deep to the latissimus dorsi muscle. The lesion demonstrated adherence to surrounding adipose tissue, rib periosteum, and intercostal musculature. Blunt dissection facilitated exposure, revealing 2 firm white spherules approximately 0.5 cm in diameter in the mass (Fig. 3). Both the mass and spherules were excised for histopathologic assessment. There was oozing from the surgical site after resection. The surgical field underwent copious irrigation followed by meticulous hemostasis via bipolar cautery. A closed suction drain was placed before layered fascial and cutaneous closure.

Intraoperative findings included an unencapsulated, irregular mass exhibiting a reddish hue compared with typical lipomas, with 2 well-circumscribed ovoid foreign bodies identified within the lesion.

Postoperative Investigation

These calcific densities identified intraoperatively prompted retrospective image reevaluation. Preoperative computed tomography and MRI both demonstrated corresponding hyperdense and signal-altered foci, respectively. Postoperative inquiry elicited the patient’s recollection of a penetrating slingshot injury sustained at approximately age 5. The stones were catapulted by playmates into her back, with medical attention omitted due to juvenile apprehension regarding parental reprimand. Final histopathology confirmed hemangioma with focal organized intravascular thrombi and surrounding adipose tissue hyperplasia. Two-month postoperative ultrasonography showed no sonographic evidence of recurrence.

DISCUSSION

The development of posttraumatic pseudolipomas, that is, without capsules, is linked to fat tissue prolapse through Scarpa fascia following soft-tissue trauma.6 After blunt trauma, damaged and necrotic cells release inflammatory cytokines and mediators,1 stimulating preadipocyte proliferation, a process partially overlapping with the formation of lipoma with capsules. Posttraumatic lipomas have been documented in facial, cervical, extremity, and thoracodorsal regions.1 Before pathological confirmation in this surgery, we considered this case a recurrent lipoma with a traumatic history. The pathological diagnosis, however, was unexpected to us. Upon reviewing the MRI scans, we found that the hyperintensity of the mass on the T2-weighted fat-suppressed sequence is indeed distinct from that of conventional lipomas. Regarding recurrence after multiple excisions, intraoperative findings suggest contributing factors: (1) persistent irritation from retained foreign bodies prevented complete resolution; (2) inadequate exposure during prior local anesthesia procedures (likely explaining pathological discrepancies); and (3) surgical trauma itself potentially activated the growth of residual mass. The long-retained foreign bodies and iatrogenic trauma synergistically triggered inflammatory cascades and vascular proliferation, highlighting the role of iatrogenic trauma in perpetuating lesion pathogenesis. In clinical practice, it is easy to fall into the trap of relying on previous pathological results—when a mass is diagnosed as benign, such as fibrolipoma, we may overlook the possibility of hidden causes, especially when the patient has a vague trauma history. This case reminds us that “benign recurrence” is never normal and always deserves a deeper look. No recurrence was observed during the 2-month postoperative follow-up. Given the potential insufficiency of preoperative biopsy, which may lead to difficulty in establishing a definitive diagnosis,7 we advocate complete excision with intraoperative frozen section assessment of surgical margins rather than preoperative biopsy.

The development of hemangioma often involves adipose or other nonvascular components. Delicate branching adipose-fibrous septations and lobulated configurations have been documented within vascular anomalies. Reactive perivascular adipose proliferation may generate heterogeneous hyperintensity on both T1- and T2-weighted imaging. Diagnostically, intramuscular vascular anomalies are easily underdiagnosed and misdiagnosed.8 Posttraumatic hemangiomas typically exhibit pronounced T2 hyperintensity with iso- to hypointense T1 signals.9 This patient’s MRI demonstrated clustered T1-hypointense and T2-hyperintense nodules with fat-suppressed T1-hypointense signals and fat-suppressed T2-hyperintense signals with indistinct margins (Fig. 2). These characteristics align with documented findings but differ from the uniform T1 hyperintensity of conventional lipomas. Consequently, the diagnosis was confirmed as posttraumatic hemangioma with reactive adipose hyperplasia. Therapeutically, documented cases used pharmacological intervention for posttraumatic reactive vascular lesions. Topical timolol maleate 0.5% ophthalmic solution achieved complete regression within 6 weeks in diffuse capillary hyperplasia cases, leveraging timolol’s vascular endothelial growth factor suppression and proapoptotic effects on proliferating endothelium.10 For confirmed reactive vascular endothelial hyperplasia, pharmacotherapy represents a viable alternative.

For plastic surgeons managing recurrent benign soft-tissue masses, persistent irritative factors (eg, retained foreign bodies) and iatrogenic factors must be considered. Even with clear prior pathological diagnoses, “mindset inertia” should be avoided—comprehensive surgical excision with intraoperative frozen section assessment of margins is preferred over preoperative biopsy, as demonstrated in this case.

In conclusion, traumatic factors could cause misdiagnosis and recurrence in benign soft-tissue masses. Clinicians should thoroughly assess trauma history, avoid mindset inertia, and prioritize complete surgical excision to ensure definitive treatment.
