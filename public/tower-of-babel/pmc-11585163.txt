Carbimazole-induced eosinophilic gastroenteritis in a young female with abdominal pain and ascites: a case report

Background

Eosinophilic gastroenteritis (EGE) is a rare disease characterized by eosinophilic infiltration into one or more layers of the gastrointestinal tract [1]. It commonly affects children more than adults [1, 4]. The clinical features depend on the site of gut involvement, but the most common symptoms include abdominal pain and diarrhea [1]. The most common cause reported in the literature is hypersensitivity, as many patients have a history of seasonal allergies, atopy, asthma, food allergies, and so on [2]. However, drugs can be a rare triggering factor. In the literature review, we found multiple case reports of EGE; however, only one other case of carbimazole-induced eosinophilic gastritis has been reported [3].

Case presentation

The patient is a 36-year-old single female resident in the United Arab Emirates (UAE) but originates from the Philippines. She presented to our emergency department with a 2-week history of epigastric pain radiating to both upper quadrants and aggravated by food and strenuous activity. Associated symptoms included reduced appetite, abdominal distention, night sweats, fatigue, and two episodes of non-bloody vomiting. There was no change in bowel habits, fever, weight loss, or jaundice.

Her medical history was significant for a diagnosis of hyperthyroidism established a year prior, for which she was prescribed carbimazole 5 mg daily. She decided to discontinue taking carbimazole two weeks after the start of her symptoms. She was prescribed pantoprazole and antibiotics before presenting to our institution; however, it did not improve her symptoms. There was no history of smoking or alcohol consumption. She has regular menstrual cycles.

On examination, she was afebrile and hemodynamically stable. She looked well in general appearance. The examination was significant for epigastric tenderness and generalized abdominal distention with positive shifting dullness. There was no pedal edema or raised jugular venous pressure (JVP). The chest was clear on auscultation. No rash or joint involvement was noticed. Thyroid and associated examinations were unremarkable.

Investigations

Initial laboratory tests shown in Tables 1 and 2 revealed an elevated white cell count with significant eosinophilia. Inflammatory markers were normal, including erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), liver function tests, thyroid function tests, and stool routine analysis. Contrast computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis shown in Fig. 1 revealed diffuse circumferential wall thickening involving the antrum, pyloric region, duodenum, and proximal ileal loops (likely of infective/inflammatory aetiology) and moderate to severe ascites (Fig. 1). Ultrasound-guided aspiration of the ascitic fluid, detailed in Table 3, revealed an eosinophil-rich exudative fluid with a total cell count of 1749 cells/mm3 with reactive mesothelial cells. Two liters of fluid were drained in total. The cytology report was negative for malignant cells. Tuberculosis (TB) polymerase chain reaction (PCR) of the ascitic fluid was also negative.

She was referred to a gastroenterologist for an upper gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy, depicted in Fig. 2, which revealed a mild erythematous gastroduodenopathy and multiple biopsies were taken. She was discharged on oral pantoprazole and antiemetics with an early appointment to the gastroenterology clinic. However, she was readmitted after 1 week with similar complaints. A colonoscopy was done in the second admission, which was normal, but biopsies were taken from the terminal ileum. The biopsy later reported mild nonspecific inflammatory changes with a predominance of eosinophils in the lamina propria (up to 50 eosinophils/HPF) without intraepithelial eosinophils.

Differential diagnosis

Based on her initial lab workup, eosinophilic gastroenteritis was the most likely diagnosis for this lady, which was later confirmed on biopsy from the duodenum and terminal ileum shown in Fig. 3. Since she had a history of carbimazole intake, we entertained that as the most likely cause, as all other possible causes of hypereosinophilia, such as parasitic infections, vasculitis, autoimmune disorders, tuberculosis, and malignancy, were ruled out.

Treatment

The patient responded well to oral prednisolone 40 mg/day and was discharged with follow-up in the gastroenterology and endocrinology clinics.

Outcome and follow-up

On her follow-up visit after 2 weeks, she was clinically better, with no abdominal pain, and improved appetite. Abdominal distention had subsided. Steroids were continued in a tapering dose and eventually stopped after 8 weeks of therapy. The endocrinologist reviewed her and advised her to regularly monitor her thyroid function tests every 8 weeks. She was not restarted on carbimazole as she had normal thyroid functions.

Discussion

The most striking abnormality in this case was the marked eosinophilia, evident from the full blood count (FBC), blood film, ascitic fluid, and gastrointestinal biopsies (Fig. 3). The challenge was deciphering the trigger and reassuring the patient of a favorable diagnosis. Eosinophilic gastroenteritis (EGE) is a rare inflammatory disorder characterized by eosinophilic infiltration of the intestinal wall [1]. First described eight decades ago, it remains a mystery due to the wide variations in clinical presentations [5]. Not much is known about the prevalence of this condition in our region, but studies from the USA have found a prevalence of 8.4 to 28 per 100,000, with a stable increase in incidence over the past 50 years [6]. In adults, it typically affects those in their third and fourth decades of life [1]. Risk factors for developing the disease are multifactorial. Reports of familial cases suggest genetic factors may also play a role [7].

The presence of allergic disorders, including asthma, rhinitis, eczema, and drug or food intolerances, are present in 45% to 63% of the reported EGE cases [8, 9]; moreover, 64% of reported cases include a family history of atopic diseases [10, 11]. Some studies have found an association with other autoimmune conditions, such as celiac disease [7], ulcerative colitis [9] and systemic lupus erythematosus [10]. These data collectively suggest that EGE may result from immune dysregulation in response to an allergic reaction, yet a triggering allergen is not always identified. Other environmental factors, such as parasitic infestation and drugs, may also act as predisposing agents [8, 9].

Conclusions

Diagnosis of EGE requires three criteria, namely (1) presence of gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms, (2) histologic evidence of eosinophilic infiltration in one or more areas of the GI tract, and (3) exclusion of other causes of tissue eosinophilia. Our patient fulfilled all the criteria; additionally, she had a positive history of atopic tendencies and drug exposure. The diagnosis of hyperthyroidism was established a year ago, and in another facility, so we were not sure of the underlying etiology of hyperthyroidism. Upon subsequent follow-up, her thyroid function remained stable. The case highlights the need for a collaborative multidisciplinary approach toward managing rare conditions.
