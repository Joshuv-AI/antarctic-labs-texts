Prevalence and factors associated with metabolically unhealthy normal weight among adult Ghanaian migrants in Europe and non-migrants in Ghana: cross-sectional analysis of the multicentre RODAM study
Authors: Birhan Ewunu Semagn, Erik Beune, Karlijn A C Meeks, Eva van der Linden, Matthias B Schulze, Charles Agyemang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndividuals with metabolically unhealthy normal weight (MUNW) are at an increased risk of diabetes and cardiovascular diseases. However, how common this phenotype is and what drives it remains poorly understood in the sub-Saharan African (SSA) populations. This study assessed the prevalence and factors associated with MUNW among adult Ghanaian residents in Europe and non-migrant Ghanaians residing in urban and rural Ghana.MethodThis cross-sectional, multicentre study used data among 1246 normal-weight adults (≥25 years) from the Research on Obesity and Diabetes among African Migrants (RODAM) study, which was conducted among Ghanaians in rural and urban Ghana and Ghanaian migrants in three European cities (Amsterdam, Berlin and London). Age-standardised prevalence of MUNW was compared across sites. Prevalence ratio (PR) and 95% CIs across the study sites were estimated using Poisson regression with robust variance. In each study site, a multivariable logistic regression was used to identify associated factors.ResultAge-standardised prevalence of MUNW was 74.1% (95% CI 67.2% to 81.5%) in rural Ghana, 69.4% (62.1% to 77.4%) in urban Ghanaian and 69.4% (61.0% to 78.6%) in Ghanaians living in Europe. No significant difference was found in the adjusted PR between Ghanaians in rural Ghana and those in urban Ghana (PR: 0.95, 95% CI 0.80 to 1.04) or migrant Ghanaians in Europe (PR: 0.98, 0.86 to 1.10). Higher age was significantly associated with higher prevalence of MUNW in all sites. In urban Ghana, high levels of physical activity were associated with lower odds of MUNW. Among migrants, excessive alcohol intake and elevated waist-to-hip ratio increased MUNW odds, while better dietary quality reduced odds of MUNW.ConclusionMUNW is highly prevalent among Ghanaian migrants and non-migrants, showing no significant site differences. Addressing the hidden metabolic unhealthiness within normal weight individuals and targeting context-specific factors could help reduce cardiometabolic risks in SSA populations.

Background

Obesity is one of the established risk factors for most cardiometabolic diseases.1 The mechanism through which obesity is linked to an increased risk of diabetes and cardiovascular diseases (CVD) involves the alteration of metabolic risk factors, such as increased insulin resistance, increased blood pressure and endothelial dysfunction.23 Interestingly, individuals with normal body weight might also have elevated cardiometabolic risk factors that increase the risk of developing diabetes and CVD.4–6 It is also common clinical practice to encounter a patient with normal body weight but suffering from cardiometabolic diseases.

In recent years, this phenotype of metabolic abnormality among lean individuals, called metabolically unhealthy normal weight (MUNW), is gaining attention in cardio-metabolic literature.4–16 MUNW is a metabolic phenotype first described by Ruderman et al,17 characterised by the presence of metabolic abnormalities such as dyslipidaemia, hyperglycaemia, insulin resistance and hypertension in individuals with normal body mass index (BMI).4518 Evidence from a meta-analysis demonstrates that individuals with MUNW face a higher risk of cardiometabolic morbidity and mortality compared with metabolically healthy individuals with obesity. Furthermore, the risk of cardiometabolic diseases associated with MUNW is comparable to that of metabolically unhealthy obese individuals.19 Hence, the long-standing assumption that normal weight equates to good health has been refuted and assessing MUNW in population-based studies has been recommended.20

Clinical and public health messages have long emphasised obesity as the primary risk factor,21 which may lead to persons with normal BMI but metabolic abnormalities delaying seeking medical attention until they develop diabetes, CVD or other related problems. Assessing the prevalence and associated factors of MUNW can enhance public health initiatives focused on screening and early diagnosis of metabolic abnormalities, regardless of BMI status.

Previous studies have shown that the burden and factors linked with MUNW vary in different contexts and population groups.22 A study conducted in Colombia identified older age, rural residency and increased waist circumference as factors associated with MUNW.4 Similarly, research among the Iranian population found that age and smoking were significant factors linked to MUNW.23 A systematic review also highlighted healthy dietary intake as a determinant of MUNW.24 Body fat distribution emerged as another variable significantly associated with metabolic dysfunction in individuals with normal weight.25 Little is known about the burden and factors associated with MUNW among sub-Saharan African (SSA) population, among whom an increasing burden of cardiometabolic disease has been noted as a concern both for SSA migrants in Europe and non-migrants in SSA.2627

The likelihood of engaging in unhealthy lifestyle that affects metabolic health also varies across different contexts. For instance, previous studies have shown that SSA migrants living in high-income countries tend to be less physically active than those still residing in SSA.28 Additionally, there is a higher consumption of alcohol and smoking among SSA residents in high-income countries compared with their counterparts living in SSA.2930

However, despite the increasing burden of cardiometabolic disease for SSA population, the prevalence of MUNW and its associated factors remains poorly studied both among SSA population within the African continent and SSA-origin population in high-income countries. Hence, this study primarily aimed to assess the prevalence of MUNW and identify factors associated with MUNW among Ghanaian migrants in Europe and non-migrants in urban and rural Ghana. Additionally, we estimated the prevalence of metabolic unhealthiness among overweight/obese Ghanaians in these locations to provide contextual comparison.

Methods

Study design, data source and setting

This is a cross-sectional study that draws on data from the Research on Obesity and Diabetes among African Migrants (RODAM) study (https://www.rod-am.eu/). The RODAM study is a multicentre cross-continental study that investigated obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus among a SSA population (Ghanaian) living in Ghana (urban Ghana and rural Ghana), as well as Ghanaians living in three European cities (Amsterdam, Netherlands, Berlin, Germany and London, UK). The RODAM baseline data was collected between 2012 and 2015. The study rationale, sampling technique and variables captured in the RODAM study have been discussed elsewhere.31 The primary analysis for this study are based on the normal-weight (BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m2) RODAM study participants. Out of the overall RODAM baseline population, 1973 participants were classified as having normal weight and 3702 as overweight/obese. The age-standardised prevalence of MUNW across the three study sites was estimated using 1906 adults with normal weight, with no missing data for age and the outcome variable MUNW. All other descriptive statistics and regression analyses were conducted on a subset of 1246 participants with complete data for all variables included in the study. The characteristics of missing values is summarised for each variable across study site and appended in online supplemental appendix 1. The mean age was 43 years (SD=15) among cases with missing data, and the mean BMI was 22.21 kg/m² (SD=1.81). Additionally, the prevalence of MUNW was 71% among cases with missing data.

Variables and measurements

Dependent variable

The primary outcome MUNW was defined as proportion of normal weight adults with a metabolic alteration, that is, normal weight but metabolically unhealthy. Normal weight was defined as a BMI of 18.5–24.9 kg/m2. Currently, there is no clear consensus on the definition of ‘metabolic health’. Literature suggests that metabolic health is related to but distinct from the absence of metabolic syndrome.32 Previous studies used various combinations of criteria of metabolic syndrome for defining metabolic health.3334 This study defines metabolically unhealthy individuals as those with metabolic alterations, characterised by abnormalities in any of the four indicators (fasting glucose, blood pressure, triglycerides or high density lipoprotein) or using any related medications as outlined in the harmonised definition of metabolic syndrome.43235–37 Alterations in metabolic health indicators were defined as follows: Elevated fasting glucose defined as fasting glucose of ≥5.6 mmol/L or when an individual reported use of hypoglycaemic medications.3637 Elevated blood pressure was defined as a systolic blood pressure of ≥130 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure of ≥85 mmHg, or hypertension based on the use of antihypertensive medication.3637 High triglyceride level was defined as triglyceride level ≥1.7 mmol/L or the use of antilipidemic medication.3637 Reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-cholesterol) was defined as HDL<1.0 mmol/L for men and HDL <1.3 mmol/L for women.3637

Despite the ideal state of optimal metabolic health characterised by no alterations in any metabolic health indicators,37 there are publications that still define metabolic unhealthiness in normal-weight individuals as the presence of two or more alterations,2338 thus allowing metabolically healthy individuals to have one metabolic alteration. Hence, we have also taken into account alternative definitions like having two or more alterations among the four indicators (online supplemental appendix 2). Considering an alternative definition could provide insights into how prevalence varies and serves as evidence for future work towards developing an agreed definition of MUNW.

Biochemical measurements

Fasting blood samples were collected for the RODAM study participants at each site. Trained technicians or research assistants manually processed the samples, aliquoted them immediately (within 1 hour to a maximum of 3 hours for veni puncture) and stored them at the local sites at −20 °C. The samples were then transported to the local research centres’ laboratories and stored at −80 °C. A standardised procedure across all sites was followed to ensure that each sample was collected, handled, processed, transported and stored consistently. It was finally transported to Berlin, Germany where biochemical analyses were performed to assess blood glucose as well as lipid metabolism (including HDL, triglycerides and total cholesterol). LDL-C concentration was determined using Friedewald equation for those whose triglyceride levels were less than 4.5 mmol/L.3940

Physical measurements

All physical measurements at each study site were conducted following standardised procedures. Standardised procedures included participant preparation, such as participants fasted overnight for about 8 hours and having the participant sit for at least 5 min before taking the reading. Blood pressure was measured using a validated semiautomated device (Microlife WatchBP home, Microlife AG Swiss Corporation, Widnau, Switzerland). Blood pressure was measured three times on the left upper arm and the mean of the second and third measurement was used for analysis. A portable stadiometer (SECA 217) was used to measure height, while weight was measured using the SECA 877 scale. BMI was calculated by dividing weight by the square of height.31 Waist circumference (in centimetres) was measured at the midpoint between the lower rib and the upper edge of the iliac crest using a measuring tape, while hip circumference was taken around the greater trochanter of the hip. Waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) was calculated using measurements of waist and hip circumferences. The WHO classification was applied to define elevated risk as WHR≥0.90 for men and ≥0.85 for women.41

Independent variables

Residence was initially recorded as Amsterdam, Berlin, London, urban Ghana and rural Ghana, but for this study, it was recoded into three categories—Europe (combining Ghanaian participants from Amsterdam, Berlin and London), urban Ghana and rural Ghana—due to the small number of normal-weight participants in the European cities. Sex was classified into males and females. Age was a continuous variable that spanned from 25 to 70 years. For this study, relationship status was categorised into three groups: married/registered partnership, cohabiting/never married and divorced/separated/widowed. Education was classified into four groups: never attended school or elementary school only, lower vocational schooling or lower secondary schooling, intermediate vocational schooling or intermediate/higher secondary schooling and higher vocational schooling or university.

The Dietary Quality Index International was used to measure dietary quality. It was a nutritional quality index developed especially to compare dietary quality among populations living in various contexts. Variety, adequacy, moderation and balance are the four primary aspects of dietary quality. It encompasses the numerous components of a healthy diet, such as the intake of macronutrients, fruits and vegetables, sodium, meat, fish, grains, fibre, micronutrients and so on.42 For this study, the index was formulated based on the Food Propensity Questionnaire developed specifically for the Ghanaian population residing in different contexts.43

The score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater dietary quality.44 The food groups evaluated in the Ghana-specific Food Propensity Questionnaire for calculating the dietary quality index have been discussed previously.4345

Physical activity was assessed using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire and was classified as low, moderate or high based on the total physical activity in MET-minutes per week (MET = metabolic equivalent of the task).46

Furthermore, alcohol consumption was recorded in the Food Propensity Questionnaire and classified as no alcohol, moderate intake or excessive intake based on a maximum of two glasses for men and one glass for women daily intake of alcohol.44 Smoking was assessed based on the question “do you smoke at all?” and categorised as yes, no I have never smoked or no, but I used to smoke.

Stress at home/work was measured using items from the INTERHEART study.47 The combined stress score at home and work was classified as never experienced stress, experiences some periods of stress at home or at work, experiences several periods of stress at home or at work and experienced permanent stress at home or at work. Perceived discrimination was assessed using the everyday discrimination scale and was classified as yes or no.48 Negative life events were assessed by asking about any bad life event that occurred within the previous 12 months, using items from the life threatening experiences scale, and were categorised as yes or no.49

Data management and analysis

The data management and analysis were done using R V. 4.4.0. Prior to the analysis, the normality assumption of continuous variables (visually using histogram) as well as the values and labels of categorical variables were checked. Normally distributed continuous variables were summarised using mean and SD, whereas median (IQR) was used for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were summarised using frequency and proportion and presented in a table. Socio-demographic and behavioural factors were compared across the three study sites using χ2 test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA).

The age standardised prevalence of MUNW was calculated stratified by study site: Europe, urban Ghana and rural Ghana. The entire RODAM sample was used as the standard population. Direct standardisation was performed using the dsr package in R with the entire RODAM sample as the standard population. A bar graph was used to show alteration in metabolic health among normal weight versus overweight/obese adults across the study sites.

Prevalence ratio (PR) and corresponding 95% CIs across the study sites were estimated using Poisson regression with robust variance. 50Adjustment was made for covariates in three models. Model 1 was adjusted for sex, age, education and relationship status; model 2 was adjusted for model 1 plus dietary quality index, alcohol intake, smoking, physical activity level and WHR. Model 3 was adjusted for model 2 plus stress at home/work, discrimination and negative life events.

Logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with MUNW. Prior to the multivariable logistic regression analysis, a bivariable logistic regression analysis was done between MUNW and the independent variables sex, age, education, relationship status, dietary quality, alcohol consumption, physical activity level, smoking, WHR, perceived discrimination, psychosocial stress and negative life event stratified by study site (rural Ghana, urban Ghana and Europe). Variables with p≤0.25 were included in the multivariable models of each study site.51 The multivariable model’s result is displayed as AOR with corresponding 95% CIs. The independent variables were examined for multicollinearity, and all of them had a low (less than 2) variance inflation factor (VIF). The Hosmer and Lemeshow goodness of fit test was used to determine the model’s goodness of fit, and the findings show sufficient fit with p=0.76 for rural Ghana, p=0.93 for urban Ghana and p=0.21 for Europe. Variables in the multivariable model with p≤0.05 were declared as having a statistically significant association.

Sensitivity analysis

Given the large proportion of missing data, we performed multiple imputation for all independent variables using the mice package in R, generating 36 imputations (corresponding to the percentage of missing data). Continuous variables, binary variables and categorical variables were imputed using predictive mean matching (pmm), logistic regression (logreg) and polytomous regression (polyreg), respectively. We then conducted bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses, stratified by residency, using the imputed datasets.

Among the three groups—rural Ghanaians, urban Ghanaians and Ghanaian migrants residing in Europe—differences were observed across socio-demographic, behavioural and metabolic health indicators. Rural Ghanaians exhibited the lowest educational attainment and reported the highest levels of severe or persistent stress at home or work, along with a greater frequency of negative life events. They also showed the highest prevalence of reduced HDL cholesterol levels. Urban Ghanaians, in contrast, had higher prevalence of low physical activity and elevated waist-to-hip ratio. Elevated blood pressure was the most frequently altered metabolic indicator among both urban Ghanaians and Ghanaian migrants in Europe. The latter group also reported significantly higher levels of excessive alcohol intake and current smoking. Additionally, perceived discrimination was reported more among Ghanaian migrants in Europe than in the other groups (table 1).

A higher proportion of individuals with MUNW were female, residing primarily in urban Ghana, and often had low educational attainment. Most were non-smokers, married or in registered partnerships, exhibited elevated waist-to-hip ratio and frequently reported experiencing negative life events (table 2).

Prevalence of metabolically unhealthy normal weight (MUNW)

The age-standardised prevalence of MUNW was 74.1% (95% CI 67.2% to 81.5%) in rural Ghana, 69.4% (95% CI 62.1% to 77.4%) in urban Ghanaian and 69.4% (95% CI 61.0% to 78.6%) in Ghanaians living in Europe (figure 1).

Age-standardised prevalence of MUNW and metabolically unhealthy overweight/obesity by place of residence among RODAM study participants. Error bars are 95% CIs. MUNW, metabolically unhealthy normal weight; RODAM, Research on Obesity and Diabetes among African Migrants.

For comparison, the age-standardised prevalence of metabolic unhealthiness among overweight or obese individuals (n=3540) was 83.2% (95% CI 69.1% to 99.3%) in rural Ghana, 82.8% (95% CI 76.0% to 90.0%) in urban Ghana and 82.0% (95% CI 78.3% to 85.8%) among Ghanaians in Europe (figure 1).

Based on the alternative definition of metabolic unhealthiness—defined as having two or more alterations among the four indicators—we observed MUNW prevalence of 25%, 29%, and 25% in rural Ghana, urban Ghana, and Europe, respectively. In contrast, the prevalence of metabolic unhealthiness among overweight or obese individuals was 48%, 39%, and 41% in rural Ghana, urban Ghana, and Europe, respectively (online supplemental appendix 2).

There was no significant difference in the prevalence of MUNW between Ghanaians in rural Ghana and those in urban Ghana (PR: 0.95, 95% CI 0.80 to 1.04) or migrant Ghanaians in Europe (PR: 0.98, 95% CI 0.86 to 1.10) (figure 2).

When evaluating each component of the metabolic health indicators used in our definition, elevated blood pressure and elevated fasting glucose were highly prevalent among normal weight Ghanaians living in urban Ghana and Europe compared with those in rural Ghana. Conversely, reduced HDL levels were more prevalent among normal weight residents in rural Ghana compared with those in Europe and urban Ghana (figure 2).

In the sex stratified analysis, the age-standardised prevalence of MUNW in females was 80.4% (95% CI 71.0% to 90.6%) in rural Ghana, 72.2% (95% CI 62.3% to 83.2%) in urban Ghanaians and 67.5% (95% CI 55.4% to 81.5%) in Ghanaians living in Europe (figure 3). Among males, the age-standardised prevalence of MUNW was 63.9% (95% CI 54.5% to 74.4%) in rural Ghana, 64.9% (95% CI 54.6% to 76.4%) in urban Ghanaians, and 72.5% (95% CI 63.7% to 82.0%) in Ghanaians living in Europe (figure 3).

There was no significant difference in the prevalence of MUNW between female Ghanaians in rural Ghana and those in urban Ghana (PR: 0.93: 95% CI 0.83 to 1.03) or migrant Ghanaians in Europe (PR: 0.88, 95% CI 0.73 to 1.06). Elevated blood pressure was more prevalent among female residents in urban Ghana and Europe compared with those in rural Ghana. Conversely, reduced HDL levels were more prevalent among female residents in rural Ghana (online supplemental appendix 3).

There was no significant difference in the prevalence of MUNW between male Ghanaians in rural Ghana and those in urban Ghana (PR: 1.01, 95% CI 0.87 to 1.18) or migrant Ghanaians in Europe (PR: 1.04, 95% CI 0.87 to 1.25). Elevated blood pressure was more prevalent among male residents in urban Ghana and Europe compared with those in rural Ghana. Conversely, reduced HDL levels were more prevalent among male residents in rural Ghana (online supplemental appendix 4).

Factors associated with MUNW in rural Ghana, urban Ghana and Europe

In rural Ghana in the multivariable model, age was found to have significant association with MUNW (AOR=1.04, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.06). In urban Ghana, in the multivariable model age (AOR=1.03, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.05) and high level of physical activity (AOR: 0.55, 95% CI 0.33 to 0.90) were found to have statistically significant association with MUNW. Among Ghanaian migrants in Europe, in the multivariable model age (AOR=1.07, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.10), dietary quality (AOR: 0.95, 95% CI 0.91 to 0.99), excessive alcohol intake (AOR: 3.04, 95% CI 1.18 to 8.95) and WHR (AOR: 1.90, 95% CI 1.11 to 3.28) were found to have significant association with MUNW (online supplemental appendix 5).

Sensitivity analysis

In rural Ghana, the multivariable model showed that age (AOR = 1.04; 95% CI 1.02 to 1.06), being female (AOR = 2.00; 95% CI 1.27 to 3.14) and moderate alcohol intake (AOR = 1.54; 95% CI 1.03 to 2.31) were significantly associated with MUNW. In urban Ghana, age (AOR = 1.03; 95% CI 1.01 to 1.05) and high level physical activity (AOR = 0.52; 95% CI 0.32 to 0.85) showed significant associations with MUNW. Among Ghanaian migrants in Europe, age (AOR = 1.06; 95% CI 1.04 to 1.08), high level physical activity (AOR = 0.61; 95% CI 0.38 to 0.96) and WHR (AOR: 1.86, 95% CI: 1.24, 2.77) were also significantly associated with MUNW (online supplemental appendix 6).

Discussion

Key findings

Our findings show a high prevalence of MUNW among both Ghanaian men and women, with no significant difference in geographic location. While the prevalence of metabolic unhealthiness was higher among those with overweight or obesity than among normal-weight Ghanaians, it remained substantial in both groups. Among the four indicators of metabolic health, elevated blood pressure was particularly common among normal-weight Ghanaians residing in urban Ghana and Europe compared with those living in rural Ghana. In contrast, reduced HDL cholesterol levels were more prevalent among normal-weight individuals in rural Ghana than among those in urban Ghana or Europe.

Furthermore, we have identified factors associated with MUNW through the site-specific, stratified analyses. Age was significantly associated with MUNW across all sites. In urban Ghana, a high level of physical activity was identified as a protective factor against MUNW. Among Ghanaians residing in Europe, excessive alcohol intake and an elevated WHR were associated with increased risk of MUNW, while good dietary quality appeared to be a protective factor. Consistent with the analysis based on the complete dataset, we also found a significant association between age and MUNW across all sites in the sensitivity analysis. In the sensitivity analysis, we additionally identified female sex and moderate alcohol intake as factors associated with an increased risk of MUNW in rural Ghana, and high levels of physical activity as being associated with lower odds of MUNW among Ghanaians in Europe.

Discussion of key findings

Obesity is a well-established risk factor for CVD and diabetes, primarily through its effects on metabolic health indicators, such as blood lipids, blood pressure and blood glucose.23 However, it is particularly concerning to observe a high burden of metabolic unhealthiness that exist despite having a normal body weight, with only less than one-third of normal weight adults found to have optimal metabolic health. Our findings are consistent with studies conducted in the USA, Colombia and Korea, which also reported a high prevalence of metabolic alterations despite normal weight. In the USA, only one-third of normal-weight adults were found to have optimal metabolic health,52 while in Colombia and Korea, one-fourth and half of normal weight adults, respectively, were identified as having MUNW.453 Another important finding from our analysis, which compared the prevalence of metabolic alterations between normal weight and overweight/obese adults, was that metabolic unhealthiness was just as common in normal weight individuals as it was in those who were overweight or obese, though the prevalence was somewhat lower in the normal-weight group. These findings suggest that cardiometabolic diseases preventive efforts should target all individuals, regardless of their BMI.

Importantly, the prevalence of MUNW varies depending on the criteria used to define metabolic health.35 In our study, we alternatively defined metabolic unhealthy as having two or more metabolic alterations as used in other studies conducted, for example, in Iran and China.2338 Using this definition, we found the prevalence of MUNW to be 24.7% in rural Ghana, 25.2% among Ghanaians in Europe and 28.6% among urban Ghanaians, which is lower than the prevalence we found based on the definition of having at least one metabolic alteration. This highlights the need for a standardised definition of metabolic health status and its association with cardiometabolic disease to prevent underestimation or overestimation of cardiometabolic risk.

Among the four indicators of metabolic health, elevated blood pressure was highly prevalent among normal-weight adults in urban Ghana and Europe, while reduced HDL cholesterol was more common in rural Ghana. This finding aligns with previous research that reported a high prevalence of elevated blood pressure among Ghanaian immigrants in high-income countries, whereas reduced HDL was more prevalent in rural Ghana.54 This may be attributed to the differing dietary patterns observed across the three sites, as highlighted in a previous RODAM publication.45

Age was found to be a significant factor associated with MUNW across all study sites, with older age associated with an increased risk of MUNW. Similar findings have been reported in studies conducted in Iran and Colombia.423 This association may be attributed to physiological changes that occur with ageing.55 Additionally, previous research has shown that older adults are more likely to engage in unfavourable health behaviours, such as physical inactivity.56 These findings suggest that the prevalence of MUNW is likely to rise as the global population ages, particularly given the increasing life expectancy driven by scientific and medical advancements.

High levels of physical activity were found to have a significant protective association with MUNW among residents of Urban Ghana. This finding aligns with a study from South Korea,13 which reported an increased risk of MUNW among participants who did not engage in vigorous physical activity. The findings of our study suggest that promoting physical activity may also play a significant role in preventing cardiometabolic diseases among individuals with normal weight.

A higher dietary quality index was found to be a protective factor, while excessive alcohol intake was a risk factor associated with MUNW among Ghanaian residents in Europe. This is consistent with findings from previous studies, which have shown that poor diet and high alcohol consumption are linked to unfavourable metabolic health.2457 These results highlight the importance of promoting healthy eating habits and reducing excessive alcohol intake among Ghanaian migrants in Europe.

An elevated WHR was also associated with MUNW among Ghanaians in Europe. This finding aligns with results from the Jackson Heart Study, which identified increased visceral fat distribution as a factor contributing to metabolic unhealthiness among normal-weight African-Americans.25

The overall public health implication of this study is the need to prioritise metabolic health and incorporate metabolic health screening alongside efforts to address obesity in order to reduce the burden of cardiometabolic diseases. Current prevention and screening approaches often focus on individuals who are overweight/obese,21 which may lead to missed diagnoses of metabolic abnormalities in those with normal weight (ie, hidden metabolic unhealthiness). In addition, while promoting physical activity may help reduce the prevalence of MUNW in urban Ghana, reducing alcohol consumption and improving dietary quality could be effective strategies for lowering the burden among Ghanaians living in Europe.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the prevalence and factors associated with MUNW among SSA populations living in different contexts. A major strength of the study is the use of the RODAM study design, an innovative and well-standardised approach applied across all study sites.39 It enables identification of differences based on the context in which individuals reside by comparing the same population living in different environments. Another strength of this study is the use of BMI to define normal weight, given its widespread application in clinical and public health practice. It underscores the potential for metabolic abnormalities to remain undetected when BMI is used as the sole criterion for assessing cardiometabolic health risk. Additionally, a strength of our study is that we performed multiple imputation to handle the missing data, and we were able to identify additional factors associated with MUNW in urban Ghana and among Ghanaians in Europe, which were not detected in the complete-case analysis.

One limitation of our study is that metabolic health indicators were assessed based on measurements taken on a single occasion, which is common in many cross-sectional epidemiological studies but may not fully capture long-term metabolic health status. The cross-sectional nature of the RODAM study limits the ability to establish temporal causality between factors and the development of MUNW. Another limitation of our study is the lack of an agreed definition for MUNW. Given that ideal metabolic health implies having no abnormalities in metabolic health indicators, we believe the definition we used more accurately reflects the optimal level of metabolic health in individuals with normal weight. Additionally, we considered an alternative definition—allowing the presence of one out of the four metabolic health abnormalities—to help inform future efforts towards establishing a consensus definition of metabolic health. Furthermore, we did not formally test if associations of determinants differ across study sites. Therefore, we cannot rule out that, for example, elevated WHR is a determinant of MUNW also in rural and urban Ghana given that OR point estimates also point towards positive associations. That body fat distribution patterns might be important determinants of MUNW has been increasingly recognised.56

Conclusion

Our study found a high prevalence of MUNW among both migrant and non-migrant Ghanaian populations and observed that the prevalence of metabolic unhealthiness was substantial both among normal-weight and overweight or obese Ghanaians, though the prevalence was somewhat lower in the normal-weight group. This underscores the need to consider cardiometabolic risk beyond BMI alone. We also identified variation in factors associated with MUNW across different settings—rural Ghana, urban Ghana and among Ghanaians living in Europe. While age was a common factor across all geographic sites, being female and moderate alcohol intake were significant factors in rural Ghana, and physical activity was a significant factor in urban Ghana. Among Ghanaians in Europe, excessive alcohol intake, poor dietary quality, high level physical activity and elevated waist-to-hip ratio were significant factors. These findings highlight the need for implementing context-specific interventions to reduce the burden of cardiometabolic diseases.
