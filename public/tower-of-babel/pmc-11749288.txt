The continuing fight for abortion rights: taking stock of the evidence
Authors: Eszter Kismödi, Emma Pitchforth, TK Sundari Ravindran, Laura Ferguson, Mindy Jane Roseman, Jane Cottingham, Sapna Desai
Published: 2024

Introduction

The curation of this special collection of previously published Sexual and Reproductive Health Matters (SRHM) articles on abortion is particularly relevant in this moment.

It has been 30 years since the world recognised that reproductive rights are human rights. And yet, while sexual and reproductive health has seen significant improvements, gains in access to safe abortion remain tenuous at best – continuing to be subject to backlash, depending on the changing political situation of any given country. As SRHM launches this special collection, Donald Trump has recently won the 2024 US presidential election. Based on his stated positions, this is potentially catastrophic for the reproductive health and rights of women and other marginalised groups, both nationally and internationally, and we are reminded of the fragility of hard-fought gains for sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR).

The SRHM journal – formerly Reproductive Health Matters (RHM) and referred to hereafter as “the Journal” – has been consistently and radically outspoken about the central importance of access to safe, legal abortion for women's health and rights. In anticipation of the 1994 International Conference on Population and Development (ICPD) in Cairo, RHM published the second issue of the Journal on Making abortion safe and legal: the ethics and dynamics of change (https://www.tandfonline.com/toc/zrhm20/1/2?nav=tocList). Per the introduction, “The safety and legality of abortion are matters of life and death for women”.1 Since then, through changing iterations of priorities in different national and international arenas, the Journal has ensured that abortion – as a matter of health care, human rights and justice – remains relevant and visible. In the past 30 years, at least one article on abortion has been published in every issue, ranging from policy analyses and commentaries about trends in liberalisation of laws to research studies and documentation of women's voices and provider practices.

The landmark agreements of the ICPD Programme of Action defined “reproductive health” and “reproductive rights” for the first time.2 The Programme of Action laid the ground for the development of international standards, actions and goals for those concepts. However, the 179 governments who adopted the document were unable to agree to wording that might suggest abortion should be legal; they were categorical that it should never be considered as a method of family planning. The furthest they came was to agree that “In circumstances where abortion is not against the law, such abortion should be safe”.2 This text of the so-called Cairo compromise echoed through every subsequent international conference, beginning with the 1995 Fourth World Conference of Women in Beijing.

No other healthcare issue has been imbued with as much political charge as abortion. Over the last three decades, the demand for abortion access has mobilised feminists, women's health and rights activists and professionals, human rights defenders, researchers, academics and politicians worldwide to push for and achieve legal reforms in countries as diverse as Argentina, Benin, Colombia, Ethiopia, Ireland, Mexico, Nepal and Thailand. These actions include movements for self-managed care backed up by peer-reviewed scientific evidence on its safety. At the same time, however, regressive forces have never been so powerful, as evidenced by the 2022 US Supreme Court decision Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization, which overthrew the Roe v. Wade ruling of 1973 through which women's constitutional right to have an abortion had been protected for almost 50 years.

It is in this context of political volatility, of uncertainty but also possibility, that we offer this special collection.

The 2024 SRHM special collection on abortion

SRHM is launching this special collection with 31 articles, selected via consensus by the present authors from over 150 relevant papers to foster and amplify the kinds of evidence that can be used for action. Most were published in the last 15 years, and they represent diverse themes, regions and methodological approaches. Our hope is that the collection can be used to identify existing evidence gaps and contribute to constructing a dynamic research agenda.

The articles span four themes: politics, laws and policies; service delivery in diverse contexts; technological advances; and women's experiences (while acknowledging that people other than women can also have abortions).

Politics, laws and policies3–15

The 2009 special issue on Criminalisation of HIV, sexuality and reproduction (https://www.tandfonline.com/toc/zrhm20/17/34?nav=tocList) was pioneering in terms of being a peer-reviewed journal issue on this subject in relation to SRHR. Since then, many Journal articles have documented various aspects of criminalisation, including how it and restrictive abortion laws perpetuate stigma and discrimination, result in negative health consequences and disproportionately affect the most marginalised in society. Some important articles in the present collection document the public health impact of abortion laws, and such data have been used in international health and human rights standards development and efforts to change national abortion law. Other articles analyse how legal reforms are necessary but, alone, are insufficient to ensure access to abortion services; resources are needed for implementation, including (but not limited to) human, financial and technical resources. The selected articles are illustrative of the Journal as a trusted source of evidence on how social, cultural, religious and political forces can block effective legal change and pose barriers to equitable access.

Service delivery in diverse contexts16–20

The Journal has published many articles on advances in medical technology, including the 2014 supplement on Expanding access to medical abortion (https://www.tandfonline.com/toc/zrhm20/22/sup44?nav=tocList). The five articles selected for the present collection cover the importance of ensuring abortion services for all, but especially marginalised communities, as well as the importance of different groups themselves being included in service design. The content highlights the legal imperative to provide abortion services, including post-abortion care in humanitarian settings. The articles report a range of innovative methods to understand the provision and experience of abortion care and point to the value of networked clinics and routinely collected data to robustly examine service utilisation and quality of care. They also include analyses of abortion for underserved and understudied populations, including young adolescents (10–14 years of age), people in humanitarian settings, and 2SLGTBQIA+ and Indigenous populations.

Technological advances21–24

As well as medical technology, the Journal has focused on digital technologies and their link to medical developments, including themed issues on Self-care interventions and rights-based access (https://www.tandfonline.com/toc/zrhm21/29/3?nav=tocList) in 2022 and Digital technologies and SRHR (https://www.tandfonline.com/toc/zrhm21/31/4?nav=tocList) in 2023. The four articles included in the present collection highlight complexities of measurement – and the limitations of technology – in providing enabling and supportive environments for all before, during and after abortion.

Women's experiences25–31

Finally, and most critically, we include a snapshot of evidence on women's lived experiences of sexual and reproductive health across marginalised populations and settings. These seven articles highlight how the Journal has uniquely contributed to epistemic justice, legitimising unheard voices and innovative methodological approaches to evidence that can be made available to programme planners and policymakers.

Continuing the fight: knowledge generation for abortion rights

With this collection, we intend to not only review past work but also provide a foundation for continued evidence generation on access to SRHR. While these articles demonstrate the wide range of evidence in support of individuals’ rights to abortion, they also highlight how much remains to be done. There is a paucity of empirical research on, for example, young adolescents, people living with disabilities, transpersons, migrants, internally displaced persons and those experiencing armed conflict.

SRHM is convening a meeting with its global and regional partners in early 2025 to track current research initiatives regarding abortion. The meeting will form the basis of a call for papers that will be posted on the SRHM website (https://www.srhm.org/) soon afterwards. We hope that, following engagement with the present collection, our readers will continue to contribute evidence on abortion as a human right, and indeed continue the fight.
