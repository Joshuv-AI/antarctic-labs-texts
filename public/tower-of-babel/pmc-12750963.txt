The association between copeptin and hypertension in children and adolescents: a systematic review
Authors: Sayed Yousef Mojtahedi, Paniz Pourpashang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Copeptin, a stable surrogate marker of arginine vasopressin, has been implicated in blood pressure regulation, but its role in pediatric hypertension remains unclear. This systematic review evaluates the association between copeptin levels and hypertension in children and adolescents. A comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane Library up to February 21, 2025. Additional sources, including Google Scholar and reference lists of relevant articles, were screened to ensure literature saturation. Observational studies (cohort, case-control, and cross-sectional) assessing copeptin levels in relation to blood pressure in pediatric populations (< 18 years) were included. Copeptin levels were assessed using validated biochemical methods, including enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale was used to assess the quality of included studies. Due to heterogeneity in study designs and outcome measurements, a narrative synthesis was performed. Twelve studies met the inclusion criteria, with sample sizes ranging from 25 to 351 participants. Some studies reported a significant association between higher copeptin levels and hypertension, particularly in children with obesity or metabolic disorders. However, other studies found no significant correlation. Differences in study design, sample characteristics, copeptin measurement methods, and confounding factors contributed to the heterogeneity of findings. The evidence on the association between copeptin and hypertension in children is inconclusive. While some studies support its potential role as a biomarker, inconsistencies highlight the need for standardized methodologies and longitudinal studies. Future research should explore copeptin’s clinical utility in pediatric hypertension risk stratification.Systematic review registration number PROSPERO: CRD420251003190.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13052-025-02145-x.

Main text

This systematic review of 12 observational studies (sample sizes 25–351 participants) evaluated the association between copeptin levels and hypertension in children and adolescents under 18 years. Some studies reported significantly higher copeptin levels in hypertensive groups, particularly those with obesity, metabolic disorders, or pulmonary arterial hypertension, with positive correlations noted in specific subpopulations. However, the majority found no significant association, including in cohorts with type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Heterogeneity in study designs, population characteristics, copeptin measurement methods (e.g., ELISA), and confounders like hydration status contributed to inconsistent findings. Overall, evidence on copeptin’s role as a biomarker for pediatric hypertension is inconclusive, highlighting the need for standardized, longitudinal research to clarify its clinical utility in risk stratification and early detection.

Introduction

Hypertension, defined as consistently elevated blood pressure, is a major public health concern globally, and its prevalence in children and adolescents has been rising in recent decades [1]. Once considered an adult disease, hypertension is now being increasingly diagnosed in pediatric populations [2]. Studies have shown that childhood hypertension is a significant risk factor for the development of cardiovascular disease (CVD) later in life, including coronary artery disease, heart failure, stroke, and chronic kidney disease [3]. The long-term consequences of untreated pediatric hypertension necessitate early detection and effective management strategies to prevent the onset of adult-related cardiovascular conditions [4].

The detection of childhood hypertension, however, is often challenging, as it requires accurate blood pressure measurement in a clinical setting, which may not always be feasible in routine pediatric care [5]. Furthermore, the pathophysiology of pediatric hypertension remains complex and multifactorial, involving genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors. As such, there is an urgent need for identifying biomarkers that can aid in the early diagnosis, monitoring, and treatment of pediatric hypertension [6].

Copeptin is a precursor peptide of AVP, which is synthesized and secreted from the posterior pituitary gland in response to various physiological stimuli such as dehydration, stress, and blood volume changes [7]. AVP plays a crucial role in regulating water balance, vasoconstriction, and blood pressure by affecting kidney function and vascular tone [8]. Copeptin is released in parallel with AVP but has a significantly longer half-life, making it a more stable and reliable biomarker for assessing the activity of the vasopressinergic system in the body [9]. Copeptin’s role in vasoconstriction, fluid retention, and endothelial dysfunction has led to increasing interest in its potential association with hypertension [10].

In children and adolescents, the AVP-copeptin axis may directly contribute to hypertension through mechanisms such as enhanced vasoconstriction via V1a receptors, increased sodium and water retention in the kidneys via V2 receptors, and sympathetic nervous system activation, which can exacerbate blood pressure elevation in vulnerable populations [11, 12]. These processes are particularly relevant in pediatric contexts, where factors like obesity, insulin resistance, and rapid growth may amplify AVP-mediated effects, leading to early endothelial dysfunction and impaired pressure natriuresis [13]. For instance, elevated copeptin levels could reflect chronic stress or dehydration responses that disproportionately affect developing vascular and renal systems, potentially accelerating hypertension onset in at-risk children compared to adults [14].

However, the role of copeptin in pediatric hypertension remains understudied. While few studies have investigated copeptin levels in children [15], the findings are inconsistent and limited, making it unclear whether copeptin could be used as a reliable biomarker for hypertension in children or adolescents [16].

Given the increasing prevalence of hypertension in children and the potential implications of copeptin as a biomarker for early diagnosis, there is a clear need to further investigate the association between copeptin levels and pediatric hypertension. The lack of comprehensive systematic reviews examining this relationship highlights an important research gap that this study aims to address. By synthesizing and critically appraising the available evidence, this review seeks to provide a clear understanding of the current state of knowledge regarding copeptin as a biomarker for hypertension in children.

Methods

The study methodology followed systematic review methods proposed by the Cochrane Handbook [17], and reported findings following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [18]. The protocol of this systematic review has been registered in the international prospective register of systematic reviews (PROSPERO; #CRD420251003190; http://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO).

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed across multiple electronic databases to identify relevant studies investigating the association between copeptin levels and hypertension in children. The databases searched included PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane Library. Additionally, Google Scholar was used as a supplementary source to identify any grey literature, conference proceedings, and unpublished studies.

The search strategy was developed using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords related to copeptin and pediatric hypertension. The key search terms included (“copeptin” OR “arginine vasopressin precursor” OR “vasopressin-associated glycopeptide”) AND (“hypertension” OR “high blood pressure” OR “elevated blood pressure”) AND (“children” OR “pediatrics” OR “adolescents” OR “youth”). These terms were adapted to the specific indexing and search functionalities of each database. Boolean operators (AND, OR), truncation (*), and wildcard symbols were applied as appropriate to maximize search sensitivity. Details of the search strategy used in the PubMed database are shown in Supplementary File 1.

No language restrictions were imposed to ensure comprehensive coverage of the literature. The search was limited to human studies and included all available studies published up to 21 February 2025. To identify additional relevant studies, the reference lists of all included articles and relevant systematic reviews were manually screened. The review considered both peer-reviewed journal articles and non-peer-reviewed sources, such as preprints, grey literature, conference proceedings, and unpublished studies, provided they met the eligibility criteria and were identified through the specified sources.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion in this systematic review if they met the following criteria:

Study design

Only observational studies (cohort, case-control, or cross-sectional studies) were considered for inclusion. Experimental studies, case reports, reviews, and meta-analyses were excluded.

Publication type

Peer-reviewed articles, preprints, grey literature, conference proceedings, and unpublished studies were eligible, provided they reported original data and met other criteria.

Population

Studies that included children and adolescents aged < 18 years were eligible. Studies focusing on populations with specific comorbidities (e.g., obesity, renal diseases, or endocrine disorders) were included, provided they also assessed blood pressure in relation to copeptin levels.

Exposure

Studies must have assessed copeptin levels as a primary or secondary variable. The measurement of copeptin should be done using a validated method (e.g., ELISA], radioimmunoassay [RIA], or similar techniques).

Outcome

Studies that reported blood pressure measurements (either systolic blood pressure [SBP], diastolic blood pressure [DBP], or both) in relation to copeptin levels were included. Blood pressure must have been assessed using standard methods, such as an automated sphygmomanometer or auscultatory technique. Studies that reported hypertension or the presence of high blood pressure as an outcome were eligible.

Language

Studies published in English or with English-language abstracts were considered eligible for inclusion. No restrictions were placed on the publication year.

This review considered both prospective and retrospective studies, provided they met the above criteria. Studies with incomplete data or unclear methodology were also excluded after a thorough quality assessment.

Selection process

The study selection process followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines and was conducted in three main stages: identification, screening, and inclusion. Initially, all retrieved records from the electronic database searches were imported into EndNote (version X20) for reference management. Duplicate records were identified and removed before proceeding to the screening phase.

Two independent reviewers (PP and SYM) screened the titles and abstracts of all identified studies to assess their relevance based on the predefined eligibility criteria. Screening was not performed blinded to authors or journals, as this was not deemed necessary given the objective nature of the eligibility criteria; however, the independent dual-reviewer process with consensus resolution minimized potential bias. Studies that were clearly irrelevant, such as reviews, case reports, or those focusing on non-pediatric populations, were excluded at this stage. Any discrepancies between the reviewers were discussed, and if a consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (MS) was consulted to resolve the disagreement.

Following the initial screening, the full texts of potentially eligible studies were obtained and assessed in detail. Each study was carefully reviewed to determine whether it met the inclusion criteria regarding study design, population, exposure (copeptin levels), and outcome (hypertension or blood pressure measurements). Studies that did not fulfill the eligibility requirements were excluded, with reasons for exclusion documented for transparency.

After the full-text review, the final set of eligible studies was included in the systematic review. Any remaining disagreements between the two primary reviewers were resolved through discussion or arbitration by a senior reviewer. The entire selection process was documented using a PRISMA 2020 flow diagram.

Data extraction

A standardized data extraction process was employed to ensure consistency and accuracy in collecting relevant information from the included studies. Two independent reviewers (PP and SYM) systematically extracted data using a pre-designed data extraction form. Data extraction was not performed blinded to authors or journals, as this was not mandated by the review protocol, but the use of two independent reviewers with a structured form and consensus resolution ensured objectivity and reduced bias. Any discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved through discussion, and if needed, a third reviewer (MS) was consulted to reach a consensus.

The extracted data included key study characteristics such as author name, publication year, study design (cohort, case-control, or cross-sectional), country, sample size, and participant demographics (age, sex, and relevant clinical characteristics). Information related to the exposure variable, including the method of copeptin measurement (e.g., ELISA, RIA, or other validated techniques) and copeptin concentration levels, was collected. Additionally, outcome-related data, such as blood pressure measurements (systolic and diastolic blood pressure) and definitions of hypertension used in each study, were recorded.

Further details on potential confounders and covariates considered in the statistical analyses of the included studies were also extracted. These included factors such as body mass index (BMI), renal function markers, glucose metabolism parameters, and other cardiovascular risk factors. Whenever necessary, corresponding authors of studies with incomplete or unclear data were contacted to obtain additional information.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias in the included studies was assessed using the NOS [19], which is a validated tool for evaluating the quality of observational studies. Two independent reviewers (PP and SYM) assessed the methodological quality of each included study, specifically scoring each study on the NOS domains of selection, comparability, and outcome/exposure. Disagreements on NOS scoring were resolved through discussion between the two reviewers to reach a consensus, focusing on the interpretation of criteria such as participant selection, confounder adjustment, and outcome assessment. If consensus could not be reached, a senior reviewer (MS), with expertise in systematic reviews and pediatric nephrology, was consulted to arbitrate and provide a final score, ensuring consistency and objectivity.

The NOS evaluates studies based on three main domains: selection of study participants, comparability of study groups, and assessment of exposure or outcome. For case-control and cohort studies, a total of nine stars can be assigned, with higher scores indicating higher study quality. For cross-sectional studies, a modified version of the NOS was used to assess methodological rigor.

In the selection domain, aspects such as the representativeness of the study population and the adequacy of case and control definitions were evaluated. The comparability domain assessed whether important confounding variables, such as age, sex, BMI, renal function, and cardiovascular risk factors, were adequately controlled for in the analysis. The outcome or exposure domain examined the reliability of outcome assessments, the method of exposure measurement (copeptin levels), and the adequacy of follow-up periods in longitudinal studies. Studies with a NOS score of 7–9 were considered to have a low risk of bias, those with a score of 5–6 were categorized as having a moderate risk of bias, and those scoring below 5 were classified as having a high risk of bias.

Data synthesis and analysis

Given the heterogeneity of the included studies in terms of study design, participant characteristics, copeptin measurement techniques, and blood pressure outcomes, a narrative synthesis of the findings was conducted. We considered performing a quantitative meta-analysis to pool results where possible (e.g., mean differences in copeptin levels between hypertensive and normotensive groups), as this could enhance the review’s impact; however, it was deemed inappropriate due to substantial clinical, methodological, and reporting heterogeneity. Clinically, studies involved diverse pediatric subpopulations (e.g., primary hypertension, obesity-related hypertension, type 1 or 2 diabetes with hypertension, and pulmonary arterial hypertension), which introduced variability in underlying pathophysiology and confounders. Methodologically, designs varied (e.g., case-control vs. cross-sectional), copeptin assays differed (e.g., ELISA vs. radioimmunoassay, with inconsistent units such as pg/mL or ng/mL), and blood pressure assessments were heterogeneous (e.g., clinic-based vs. ambulatory). Reporting was inconsistent: some studies provided mean copeptin levels with standard deviations, others reported medians with interquartile ranges, correlations, or odds ratios, and several lacked sufficient quantitative data for pooling (e.g., only p-values or qualitative statements). Only a limited subset (approximately 4–5 studies) reported comparable data on copeptin levels in hypertensive versus normotensive groups, but even these had incompatible formats or units, precluding reliable synthesis. As no quantitative meta-analysis was performed, statistical heterogeneity (e.g., I²) was not calculated.

The data extracted from each study were synthesized qualitatively, focusing on the direction, strength, and consistency of the reported association between copeptin levels and hypertension in children. The studies were grouped based on their study design (cohort, case-control, or cross-sectional), and the findings were summarized to identify any patterns or trends.

For each study, the key information, such as the sample size, population characteristics (age, sex, and relevant comorbidities), copeptin measurement techniques, and hypertension definitions, was examined. The primary focus was on whether the studies found a positive, negative, or no association between copeptin levels and hypertension in children.

To enhance the synthesis, studies that used similar methodologies and measurement techniques for copeptin were examined together, with the aim of identifying consistent findings. Additionally, studies reporting on the relationship between copeptin and both systolic and diastolic blood pressure were analyzed to discern if differences in blood pressure measurement types influenced the reported associations. Where possible, the consistency of findings across studies was noted, and potential reasons for any discrepancies were explored, such as differences in participant characteristics, sample sizes, and methodological quality. The findings were then discussed in the context of the broader literature, highlighting the strengths and limitations of the included studies, and providing recommendations for future research.

Results

Study selection

The literature search identified 463 records from databases and 0 from registers, for a total of 463 records via databases and registers (Fig. 1). After removing 168 duplicates, 295 unique records were screened based on titles and abstracts, with 138 excluded as irrelevant (e.g., non-pediatric populations, reviews, or unrelated topics). This left 157 reports sought for full-text retrieval, of which 127 could not be retrieved. The high number of unretrieved reports was primarily due to the comprehensive search strategy, which included grey literature and supplementary sources like Google Scholar and Scopus, yielding many conference abstracts, preprints, unpublished theses, and proceedings without accessible full texts. Additional barriers included paywalls not covered by institutional subscriptions, outdated or broken links, and full texts unavailable in open repositories or through interlibrary loans. Of the 30 reports successfully retrieved and assessed for eligibility, 18 were excluded (12 for incomplete reports, 3 for non-English language, and 3 for lacking related outcomes), resulting in 12 eligible studies from this pathway.

Association between copeptin levels with hypertension

The findings of this systematic review present a mixed but notable relationship between Copeptin levels and hypertension in children and adolescents. Some studies reported a significant association, indicating that higher copeptin levels were observed in hypertensive individuals compared to normotensive controls. For instance, Ivanko et al. in a case control study investigated the relationship between copeptin levels and hypertension in adolescents aged 17 years by comparing hypertensive individuals with normotensive controls. The findings indicate that although copeptin levels were slightly higher in hypertensive adolescents compared to normotensive peers, the association was not statistically significant. The study found that copeptin levels tended to increase with rising blood pressure, but the observed differences did not reach the level of statistical significance (P >0.05) [22]. In line with this study, Gor et al. in a case control study among the children with obesity observed a higher concentration of Copeptin among the patients with hypertension than control group. Also, the results of this study revealed a positive correlation between copeptin levels and blood sodium levels (r = 0.335; P = 0.037) [23]. Similar findings were reported in another study. Gaheen et al. examined the correlation between plasma copeptin levels and some clinical condition among the children with pulmonary arterial hypertension. Their study revealed a significant positive correlation between Copeptin levels and pulmonary arterial hypertension [24]. Another observational study among the adolescents with hypertension confirmed that patients with hypertension had significantly higher concentration of Copeptin than control group [31].

In contrast with these findings, most of the included studies did not find a significant correlation between serum copeptin levels and hypertension. El-Raufe El-Masry et al. in a case control study among the Egyptian children with obesity, evaluated the association of Copeptin levels with anthropometrical variables and blood pressure. They found that Copeptin levels was significantly higher among the obese children than control group. However, they didn’t observe any significant correlation between copeptin levels with blood pressure and lipid profile [20]. In line with the results of this study, another cross-sectional study on adolescents with type 2 diabetes revealed a non-significant association between copeptin concentration with odds of hypertension [21].

Children and adolescents with type 1 diabetes are one of the groups in which researchers have investigated the relationship between copeptin and high blood pressure in various studies. Although a positive association between increased copeptin concentration and high blood pressure was observed in the study by Wiromrat et al. [26], these results were not confirmed in two other studies [27, 29, 30].

Discussion

This systematic review aimed to evaluate the association between copeptin levels and hypertension in children and adolescents. The findings were mixed, with some studies reporting a significant positive association between elevated copeptin levels and hypertension, while others found no significant correlation. Notably, studies conducted in specific subpopulations, such as obese children or those with type 1 diabetes, reported varying results, indicating the potential influence of underlying metabolic conditions on this association. Overall, while there is some evidence suggesting a link between copeptin and blood pressure regulation in pediatric populations, the inconsistency among studies highlights the need for further research.

The role of copeptin as a biomarker for hypertension has been extensively studied in adults, where elevated levels have been associated with increased cardiovascular risk, impaired renal function, and metabolic disorders [32, 33]. Due to its stability, copeptin has been studied as a biomarker in several cardiovascular and metabolic disorders, including heart failure, sepsis, acute myocardial infarction, and chronic kidney disease [34]. Elevated levels of copeptin have been suggested as markers of stress-induced hypertension, renal dysfunction, and cardiovascular risk [11].

Numerous studies in adult populations have demonstrated that copeptin is a reliable surrogate marker for AVP activity, which plays a crucial role in blood pressure regulation, water homeostasis, and cardiovascular health. Elevated copeptin levels in adults have been linked to increased blood pressure, endothelial dysfunction, and heightened cardiovascular disease risk, reinforcing its potential as a predictive biomarker for hypertension [16, 35].

However, the evidence in pediatric populations remains inconsistent and less well-documented. Some studies included in this review found a significant association between copeptin and hypertension, suggesting that the vasopressinergic system may contribute to blood pressure dysregulation even at a young age. For example, studies conducted among obese children or adolescents with metabolic disorders reported a stronger correlation between copeptin levels and hypertension, possibly due to the interplay of obesity-related inflammation, insulin resistance, and altered renal sodium handling [23]. Similarly, studies on adolescents with primary hypertension found higher copeptin levels compared to normotensive controls, reinforcing the hypothesis that AVP-mediated pathways may contribute to early-onset hypertension [26, 31].

The divergence between adult and pediatric findings likely stems from a combination of developmental, hormonal, and methodological factors unique to children and adolescents [7, 36]. Developmentally, children undergo rapid growth and vascular remodeling, which may alter AVP-copeptin dynamics compared to adults with more stable cardiovascular systems. Hormonally, puberty introduces fluctuations in sex hormones and stress responses that can influence AVP secretion patterns, potentially attenuating or amplifying copeptin’s association with blood pressure in ways not seen in adults [37]. For instance, pediatric populations may exhibit heightened sensitivity to dehydration or stress-induced AVP release, affecting copeptin levels differently. Methodologically, pediatric studies often face challenges such as smaller sample sizes, variable blood pressure measurement techniques (e.g., clinic-based vs. ambulatory), and inconsistent control for confounders like hydration status or dietary sodium, which are more standardized in adult studies. These factors may dilute or obscure the consistent associations seen in adults, where larger, more homogeneous cohorts and standardized protocols are common [38].

Conversely, several studies did not observe a significant correlation between copeptin levels and blood pressure [27, 28], raising questions about the consistency of this association. The lack of agreement among studies may be attributed to a combination of factors, including differences in study design (e.g., case-control vs. cross-sectional), population characteristics (e.g., age ranges, BMI, and comorbidities such as obesity or diabetes), methodological variations (e.g., ELISA vs. immunoassays with differing sensitivity, clinic-based vs. ambulatory blood pressure measurements), unaccounted confounders (e.g., hydration status, sodium intake, renal function), and limited statistical power due to small sample sizes.

Another key factor to consider is the developmental and physiological differences between children and adults [36]. While copeptin has been firmly established as a marker of cardiovascular and renal dysfunction in adults, its role in children may be modulated by growth, hormonal fluctuations, and metabolic adaptations unique to pediatric populations [34]. The influence of puberty, differences in AVP secretion patterns, and varying responses to stress and hydration may further contribute to the observed variability in findings [11]. Overall, the comparison with adult literature suggests that while copeptin may play a role in hypertension pathophysiology, its significance in pediatric hypertension remains unclear. The discrepancies in findings highlight the need for standardized, large-scale longitudinal studies to better understand the temporal relationship between copeptin levels and blood pressure changes in children and adolescents.

Copeptin, as a stable surrogate marker of AVP, plays a central role in several physiological pathways that may contribute to the development of hypertension. AVP is a key hormone involved in maintaining water-electrolyte balance and vascular tone [39]. It exerts its effects through V1a and V2 receptors, with the former mediating vasoconstriction and the latter regulating water retention in the kidneys. Elevated AVP activity, reflected by increased copeptin levels, can lead to persistent vasoconstriction and sodium retention, both of which contribute to increased blood pressure. Chronic AVP activation may also enhance vascular resistance and impair pressure natriuresis, further exacerbating hypertension [34].

The kidneys play a crucial role in long-term blood pressure regulation, and AVP is involved in modulating renal sodium reabsorption. The activation of V2 receptors in the collecting ducts increases aquaporin-2 expression, promoting water retention. Additionally, AVP stimulates sodium reabsorption through the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC), leading to volume expansion and increased cardiac output [40]. Children with higher copeptin levels may have an impaired ability to excrete sodium, resulting in greater susceptibility to hypertension, particularly in those with high dietary sodium intake. Also, elevated AVP levels are associated with increased sympathetic nervous system (SNS) activity, which is a well-recognized contributor to hypertension. AVP interacts with central nervous system pathways to enhance sympathetic outflow, leading to increased heart rate, vasoconstriction, and elevated blood pressure [41, 42]. This mechanism may be particularly relevant in children with obesity, where SNS overactivation is commonly observed.

Emerging evidence suggests a link between copeptin, insulin resistance, and metabolic syndrome. AVP influences glucose metabolism through interactions with hepatic glucose production and pancreatic insulin secretion [43]. Higher copeptin levels have been associated with insulin resistance, hyperglycemia, and dyslipidemia, all of which are risk factors for hypertension. In pediatric populations, particularly those with obesity or type 2 diabetes, these metabolic disturbances may amplify the hypertensive effects of copeptin [44].

Several factors may have contributed to the variability in results among the included studies. Differences in study populations, including age ranges, BMI, and comorbid conditions such as obesity and diabetes, may influence copeptin levels and their relationship with hypertension. Furthermore, variations in the methods used to assess blood pressure (e.g., ambulatory vs. clinic measurements) and copeptin (e.g., ELISA vs. immunoassay techniques) could introduce inconsistencies in findings. It is also important to consider the impact of hydration status, dietary sodium intake, and physical activity, which were not consistently controlled for across studies.

Clinical implications

The potential role of copeptin as a biomarker for pediatric hypertension remains uncertain. If future studies establish a stronger association, copeptin could serve as a non-invasive marker for early hypertension detection, particularly in high-risk groups such as obese children. However, given the current conflicting evidence, routine clinical use of copeptin for this purpose cannot yet be recommended. Additional research is required to determine whether copeptin measurement provides incremental value over traditional blood pressure assessments and other cardiovascular risk markers.

Strengths and limitations

One of the key strengths of this systematic review is its comprehensive approach in synthesizing evidence on the association between copeptin and hypertension in pediatric populations. By including multiple studies from different geographical regions, this review provides a broad perspective on the topic. Furthermore, adherence to rigorous systematic review methodologies, including the use of PRISMA guidelines and risk of bias assessment with NOS, enhances the reliability of the findings.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the heterogeneity among studies in terms of population characteristics, sample sizes, and measurement techniques may limit the comparability of findings. Second, most of the included studies had a cross-sectional design, preventing the establishment of causal relationships between copeptin and hypertension. Third, potential confounders such as hydration status, dietary sodium intake, renal function, and metabolic conditions were not consistently controlled for across studies. Lastly, publication bias may have influenced the results, as studies with null findings are less likely to be published.

Conclusion

In summary, while some evidence suggests a potential association between copeptin levels and hypertension in children and adolescents, findings remain inconsistent. Future research should prioritize well-designed prospective cohort studies with standardized methodologies to establish copeptin’s clinical utility in pediatric hypertension risk stratification and management. Specifically, studies should employ longitudinal designs with large, diverse pediatric cohorts (e.g., including healthy children and those with comorbidities like obesity or diabetes) followed over several years to assess temporal relationships between copeptin levels and blood pressure changes. Standardized copeptin measurement techniques (e.g., high-sensitivity ELISA) and consistent blood pressure assessment methods (e.g., 24-hour ambulatory monitoring) are critical to reduce methodological heterogeneity. Additionally, studies should control for key confounders such as hydration status, dietary sodium intake, and pubertal stage, and include outcomes like hypertension incidence, progression, or response to interventions. Such designs could clarify whether copeptin serves as a reliable, non-invasive biomarker for early detection or monitoring of hypertension in high-risk pediatric populations, potentially guiding targeted interventions.
