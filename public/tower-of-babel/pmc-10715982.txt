Atrial Tachycardia-Induced Cardiomyopathy in Late Pregnancy
Authors: Alexander Slaten, Justin Arunthamakun, Omar Jawaid, Sangita Sudharshan, Jasen Gilge, Giorgio Zanotti, Tovah Buikema, Mary Norine Walsh
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractTachycardia-mediated cardiomyopathy is an established cause of left ventricular dysfunction. The development of cardiomyopathy depends on type, rate, and duration of tachyarrhythmia. Early recognition and treatment are critical in preventing left ventricular dysfunction and heart failure. Normal physiologic changes in pregnancy can complicate the early recognition and treatment of pathologic tachyarrhythmia.Key Words: atrial tachycardia, cardiomyopathy, heart failure, pregnancy

Case Presentation

A 26-year-old gravida 1, parity 0 woman with gestational anemia, but no personal or familial cardiac history presented to obstetric (OB) triage at 36.6 weeks complaining of emesis, dyspnea, and severe back pain. Heart rate was 193 beats/min, initial blood pressure was 122/78 mm Hg, and arterial oxygen saturation of 93% on 8-L nasal cannula. Initial respiratory rate was 42 breaths/min, but this improved to 18 breaths/min with supplemental oxygenation. On physical exam, she was gravid, in mild distress with normal mentation, and dyspneic with diminished breath sounds in bilateral bases and scattered rhonchi. Her extremities were warm with 1+ bilateral lower extremity edema. Initial laboratory evaluation was unremarkable aside from the anemia. A computed tomography angiography chest demonstrated pulmonary edema but was unremarkable for aortic dissection or pulmonary embolism. Her presenting electrocardiogram demonstrated a narrow complex supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) (Figure 1). Given stable blood pressure at presentation and electrocardiogram consistent with SVT, adenosine infusion was administered with no change in heart rate or rhythm. Cardiology was urgently consulted. A bedside point-of-care ultrasound revealed a severely reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of <10%.

Question 1: What Are Common Etiologies for Reduced LV Function in Pregnancy?

The differential diagnosis for reduced LV function in pregnancy can include pregnancy-specific and nonpregnancy–specific causes. Pregnancy-specific causes primarily include peripartum cardiomyopathy (CM). Non-pregnancy–specific causes of LV dysfunction include ischemic, dilated, tachy-mediated, viral-induced, pre-existing genetic or familial, and stress-induced CMs. Spontaneous coronary artery dissection can occur in pregnant and nonpregnant patients, and the increased occurrence of multivessel spontaneous coronary artery dissection in pregnancy can result in LV dysfunction.

On presentation, the cause of the patient’s LV dysfunction was uncertain. The presenting electrocardiogram was consistent with SVT that was unresponsive to adenosine. Given that she presented with stable blood pressures, sedation and cardioversion was not immediately pursued. It was initially unclear whether tachycardia was the cause of her CM because the duration of the tachycardia was unknown.

Question 2: In the Setting of Cardiogenic Shock, Which Factors Determined a Need for Use of Mechanical Circulatory Support in Our Patient?

It is recommended that pregnant patients with cardiogenic shock should be monitored and treated in a facility where mechanical circulatory support is readily available.

Given stable blood pressure on presentation, cardiogenic shock was not initially recognized. Duration of her tachycardia was not initially known. Because of persistent tachycardia and hypoxia, the original plan was for urgent delivery via cesarean section in the operating room with cardiac anesthesia assistance. While awaiting transfer to the operating room, she was given 80 mg of intravenous furosemide for diuresis, but despite this she developed progressive pulmonary edema and respiratory failure requiring intubation. Intubation was accompanied by fetal distress, which led the OB team to make the decision to pursue urgent cesarean section. Immediately following delivery of her healthy infant, the patient suffered hemodynamic collapse. Due to refractory hypotension despite increasing pressor and inotropic support, the patient was cannulated for venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation with placement of an axillary percutaneous LV assist device for LV venting. A transthoracic echocardiogram was subsequently performed revealing a dilated LV with LV end-diastolic volume of 5.8 cm and severely reduced LVEF of 5%-10%. Severe mitral regurgitation due to annular dilatation was appreciated along with severely dilated left atrium. A moderate-sized pericardial effusion (1.5 cm) was noted with no echocardiographic evidence of tamponade (Figure 2). These echocardiographic findings were significantly different from those typically observed in pregnancy (Table 1).

Question 3: What Information Suggested Tachycardia-mediated CM as the Primary Cause of This Patient’s Reduced LV Function?

The development of tachycardia-mediated CM can occur in the setting of paroxysmal or persistent tachyarrhythmia and depends on type, rate, and duration of arrhythmia. Atrial fibrillation and atrial flutter are the most common arrhythmias to induce CMs. However, other arrhythmias such as incessant SVT, including atrial tachycardia (AT), can also be detrimental.1 It has previously been reported that 30% of patients with an incessant rapid AT can be associated with CM. Given that ATs are not as common, this is an infrequent occurrence. Myopathies due to tachycardia also heavily depend on duration and rate. The faster the heart rate, the earlier the onset of myopathy and vice versa. The myopathy may occur in a matter of days if the heart rate is >150 beats/min. However, it may develop over several weeks if the heart rate is over 100 beats/min but under 150 beats/min. In general, the diagnosis is one of exclusion.2 Once stable on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, prior documentation from outpatient OB evaluations was sought and reviewed. Documentation revealed elevated resting heart rates for several months prior to the patient’s presentation with LV dysfunction. Heart rates, without electrocardiographic documentation, were recorded as 112 to 140 beats/min during the first 7 months, then 160 beats/min the last 2 months. These heart rates are significantly increased compared to expected rates for normal heart rates of the same gestational age (Table 2). Our patient had reported no cardiac symptoms during these evaluations and no electrocardiograms were obtained.

In consultation with electrophysiology, advanced heart failure cardiology, cardiovascular surgery, and critical care medicine, the etiology of CM was evaluated. Coronary artery disease had a low pretest probability given the patient’s young age and lack of comorbidities. Her echocardiogram excluded any valvular disease as well. Unfortunately, she was too unstable to undergo further testing with a cardiac magnetic resonance or positron emission tomography scan. Given her incessant tachycardia with rates of 160 beats/min for 2 months, tachycardia-mediated CM was the leading diagnosis. We could not exclude another etiology; however, it was certain the persistent tachycardia was a significant contributor, and thus, it was treated aggressively.

Question 4: What Therapies Exist for Treatment of AT?

Various means of treatment exist for AT. Atrioventricular nodal agents such as beta blockers and calcium-channel blockers are frequently used to control the ventricular rate in attempts to minimize symptoms and prevent a CM. A wide range of antiarrhythmics can be used for rhythm control as well. In those with a structurally normal heart and no evidence of obstructive coronary disease, Class Ic agents can work well. Potassium-channel blockers, such as sotalol or dofetilide, can also exhibit therapeutic effects, but they require close monitoring for QTc prolongation. In those with significant contraindications to the aforementioned medications, amiodarone is also an option, but it is often reserved as a last resort due to the potential long-term side effects. For those with frequent or incessant AT, a catheter-based ablation can be very successful with low risk of complications.1 This may be the preferred method of treatment for those intolerant of or unwilling to take medications.

Because of the patient’s presentation with cardiogenic shock, beta-blockers were avoided. After delivery, the patient received several grams of amiodarone followed by attempted cardioversion, but the arrhythmia remained incessant. The decision was made to pursue radiofrequency catheter ablation as a means of treatment for the patient’s tachyarrhythmia given that she was refractory to amiodarone. She underwent an electrophysiological study during which she was in a tachycardia with a heart rate of 130 beats/min (Figure 3). The P-wave morphology suggested right-sided AT. Electroanatomic mapping localized the tachycardia to the lateral tricuspid isthmus. Initial ablations at this site terminated the tachycardia; however, 2 other ATs ensued originating from the high lateral and inferior tricuspid annulus and were successfully ablated (Figure 4).

Question 5: How Does Treatment of Tachyarrhythmia and LV Dysfunction Result in Improvement in Cardiac Function?

In the setting of tachycardia-mediated CM, early recognition and rate control or termination of the arrhythmia is critical in preventing LV dysfunction.

Following ablation, there was rapid improvement in the patient’s hemodynamics and shock resolved. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation was weaned; she was successfully decannulated; and the percutaneous LV assist device was removed. An echocardiogram the day after ablation showed LVEF of 40%. She was discharged home in stable condition on postoperative day 10. She elected not to breastfeed and was initiated on maximum tolerated guideline-directed medical therapies including sacubitril/valsartan, metoprolol succinate, spironolactone, and empagliflozin. An echocardiogram prior to discharge showed recovery of her LVEF. Because of intermittent recurrence of AT, she continued on oral amiodarone. Several months later, she had had no recurrences on monitoring and amiodarone was discontinued. Repeat echocardiogram showed LVEF of 50% to 55%.

Perspectives

Because of normal physiologic change in pregnancy, an increase in heart rate is expected. Distinguishing physiologic from pathologic tachycardia can be challenging. Further investigation with electrocardiogram or ambulatory monitoring is recommended in any pregnant patient with sustained heart rates greater than expected physiologically. In the case of cardiogenic shock in pregnancy, multidisciplinary care involving the disciplines of OB, maternal fetal medicine, cardiology, cardiac surgery, and electrophysiology are often needed to make diagnostic and treatment decisions that can save two lives.
