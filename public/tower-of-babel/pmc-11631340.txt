Hierarchical simplicial manifold learning
Authors: Wei Zhang, Yi-Hsuan Shih, Jr-Shin Li, Alberto Guadagnini
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLearning global structures, i.e. topological properties, inherent in complex data is an essential yet challenging task that spans across various scientific and engineering disciplines. A fundamental approach is to extract local data representations and use them to assemble the global structure. This conjunction of local and global operations catalyzes the integration of tools from algebraic and computational topology with machine learning. In this article, we propose a hierarchical simplicial manifold learning algorithm, constituted by nested clustering and topological reduction, for constructing simplicial complexes and decoding their topological properties. We show that the learned complex possesses the same topology as the original embedding manifold from which the data were sampled. We demonstrate applicability, convergence, and computational efficiency of the algorithm on both synthetic and real-world data.Keywords: manifold learning, topological data analysis, computational homology, clustering

Significance Statement

Extracting topological features from high-dimensional big data is a crucial yet difficult task across scientific domains, enabling numerous practical applications. An effective approach to address this challenge is to reconstruct the underlying embedding manifold of the data. However, the exploration of reliable and efficient manifold learning methods remains limited. The novel contribution of this work is to develop the method of hierarchical simplicial manifold learning. This method systematically constructs a topological space, represented as a simplicial complex, which possesses the same topological properties as the embedding manifold of the data. This approach integrates machine learning with algebraic topology manifesting the computational efficiency and mathematical rigor for effectively learning critical topological features using synthetic and real-world data.

Introduction

The rapid advancement in big data technologies has significantly enhanced our ability to collect, explore, and analyze complex data. This progress empowers the extraction of valuable information that is essential to accelerate the development of real-world applications and facilitate decision-making processes. While data are abundant in the era of big data, how to effectively exploit local data and their representations, such as nearest neighborhoods or level sets, to exact meaningful global features embedded in the entire dataset remains a pressing challenge across various scientific domains. Prominent examples include finding succinct low-dimensional representations of high-dimensional data in computer vision (1–5), reconstructing 3D images based on their 1D and 2D projections in imaging science (6–10), and analyzing textual corpora by collecting lexical information in natural language processing (11, 12). Central to the attempt to address these applied problems is topology (13), which is the mathematics concerning with the invariant global structures (e.g. connectedness, compactness, and holes) of a space under continuous deformations (e.g. twisting, bending, and stretching). The rapid growth in sensing and computing technologies has spurred the utility of algebraic topology theories for good use of computation. This in turn catalyzes the emergence of topological data analysis for learning invariant properties and geometry in data using notions and tools from topology (14). This fast-growing field has stimulated the search for broader applications including shape recognition (15, 16), disease diagnosis (17–19), network inference (20), material analysis (21, 22), graph reconstruction (23–25), image processing (26, 27), computer vision (28, 29), and data skeletonization (30).

Extensive approaches have been proposed to reconstruct manifolds from sampled data, among which the predominant focus is on learning low-dimensional representations of the data, namely, dimensionality reduction. Widely used techniques include principal component analysis (PCA) and kernel PCA, multidimensional scaling, Isomap, locally linear embedding, and diffusion maps (2, 3, 5). Such dimensionality reduction algorithms are built upon constructing local coordinate representations for individual datapoints using various distance metrics, and typically unable to capture the precise topological properties inherent in the dataset. In recent years, algebraic topology has gained considerable recognition for its role in topological data analysis and manifold learning. In particular, persistent homology has emerged as a mainstream method for computing topological features from data (31–33). However, algorithms of persistent homology are confronted with the notorious “curse of dimensionality” because the computational cost grows rapidly with the increase in the size and dimension of datasets (34). Additionally, their performance may be largely degraded on spatially imbalanced data, when the sampling density varies across the entire dataset. In this article, we develop a hierarchical simplicial manifold learning (HSML) method to fill these gaps.

Methods

Learning global structures, i.e. topology, inherent in big data exhibits a 2-fold challenge. It involves precise grouping of datapoints to extract faithful local information, and then proper assembly of the decoded local information to retrieve the global structure. Intuitively, local information is essentially documented in the groups of adjacent datapoints (neighborhoods). Hence, it can be revealed by means of clustering, which groups nearby datapoints into the same cluster and remote datapoints into others. In general, clustering is a self-organized procedure, and a datapoint may be grouped into a different set of its nearby points given different criteria, e.g. the choice of initial conditions and the number of clusters, set in a clustering algorithm (35). This implies that distinct clustering schemes may potentially result in varying interpretations of local structures associated with each individual datapoint. Figure 1A illustrates such a circumstance, where three different clustering schemes result in three different local structures, B={B1,B2,B3}, G={G1,G2,G3}, and R={R1,R2,R3}, for the dataset D owing to various clustering criteria.

Schematic illustration of HSML. A, B) Nested clustering for construction of neighborhood system U: A) illustrates neighborhoods, i.e. clusters {Bi,Gj,Rk}, i,j,k=1,2,3, generated by three rounds of parallel clustering for dataset D and B) depicts the synthesis of U using intersection operations on the neighborhoods. C) Topological reduction for construction of simplicial complex N(U) generated by U, in which the 0-simplices (vertices) are given by the centroids of the clusters shown in A), and the 1-simplices (edges) and 2-simplices (triangle solids) are formed by connecting the centroids of the pairwise and three intersecting neighborhoods illustrated in B), respectively.

To effectively overcome this issue and properly leverage the presented clustering idea, we propose the method of HSML. This approach systematically constructs a topological space, in the form of a simplicial complex, which represents the comprehensive topological properties within the given dataset. Our method is based on a hierarchical and sequential procedure enabled by a hybrid algorithm involving nested clustering and topological reduction.

Nested clustering

The HSML algorithm is initialized by performing parallel clustering, which exhausts all potential clusters for the given dataset. In this step, each datapoint is evaluated to identify its neighboring points using various clustering criteria, and the resulting clusters are used to create a neighborhood system (or called a neighborhood basis). The neighborhood system is essentially constructed by utilizing set operations, specifically by taking all possible finite intersections among the acquired clusters (neighborhoods). A simple illustration of our approach is displayed in Fig. 1A and B, where a neighborhood system of dataset D is generated by the nine clusters obtained from three rounds of parallel clustering, i.e. {Bi,Gj,Rk}, i,j,k=1,2,3. In this example, the neighborhood system is synthesized by these clusters and their 1- and 2-intersections (referred to as nested clustering), given by U={Bi,Gj,Rk,Bi∩Gj,Bi∩Rk,Gi∩Rk,Bi∩Gj∩Rk:i,j,k=1,2,3}. Note that the number of clusters in each round of parallel clustering, Bi, Gj, or Rk, as in Fig. 1A, is determined by the chosen clustering criteria and can be different.

Mathematically speaking, the neighborhood basis determines the topology (global structure) of the underlying space that embeds the dataset, i.e. from which the data are sampled. This embedding manifold, denoted M, can be exhibited in the form of a simplicial complex constructed by using the neighborhood basis (see Section S1.1). Following nested clustering, we develop a topological reduction approach to construct the simplicial complex N(U), generated by the neighborhood basis U for dataset D, based on the notion of collapse in algebraic topology.

Topological reduction

In this approach, we first collapse the resulting neighborhoods from nested clustering to their cluster centroids, which yields 0-simplices. Next, the centroids of neighborhoods with nonempty 1-intersections are connected to form 1-simplices. Inductively, we use the centroids of neighborhoods with nonempty n-intersections to build n-simplices. For example, in Fig. 1C, Bi, Gj, and Rk are collapsed to 0-simplices, the nonempty 1-intersections are used to form 1-simplices (edges connecting two centroids), and the nonempty 2-intersections are used to constitute 2-simplices (solid triangles with three centroids as vertices) in N(U).

Decoding manifold topology

The topological properties of the learned simplicial complex N(U) can then be revealed by computing its cohomology or homology groups. Here, we compute its cohomology groups over the field of real numbers R. This is equivalent to computing Betti numbers of N(U) (see Section S3). Methods for computing homology groups, such as those based on persistent homology, can also be used.

Topological convergence

Ultimately, as the amount of data increases, the presented nested clustering (Fig. 1) will reach a comprehensive set of local structures, in other words, convergent to a neighborhood basis (e.g. U) of topology of the embedding manifold (e.g. M). We prove this convergence property by showing that a sequence of simplicial complexes generated with an increasing number of clusters by HSML will arrive at a stabilized topological characteristic (see Fig. 2A and Section S2).

B) Robustness of HSML to the noisy toric data (original toric data perturbed by Gaussian white noise of amplitude 0.5). Results

Below, we demonstrate the proposed HSML algorithm on diverse synthetic and real-world data to showcase its applicability and effectiveness.

HSML on synthetic data

Toric data. Figure 2 illustrates the application of the HSML algorithm to the dataset containing 70,000 uniformly sampled points from a 2D torus T2. In this example, we constructed simplicial complexes by performing nested clustering and topological reduction repeatedly with different choices of parameters. This includes varying the number of rounds (denoted m) of parallel clustering and the number of clusters (denoted n). Specifically, m and n were varied from 1 to 20 in increments of 1 and from 50 to 100 in increments of 5, respectively, for a total of 220 experiments. In these cases, nested clustering resulted in the neighborhood basis Umn, followed by the topological reduction which generated simplicial complexes N(Umn) for each pair of (m,n) for m=1,2,…,20 and n=50,55,…,100. Note that, in each experiment, we chose the same number of clusters, n, in every round of clustering, which in general can be different. We then computed the Betti numbers, bi(Umn), i=0,1,2, of the generated simplicial complexes N(Umn) for each pair of (m,n). Figure 2A displays the plot of bi(Umn) versus (m,n), from which we observe the fast convergence of b0(Umn), b1(Umn), and b2(Umn) to b0(T2)=1, b1(T2)=2, and b2(T2)=1, respectively (see Section S2). It is worth noting that the reduced topological dataset for (m,n)=(20,100) contains 2,000 cluster centroids, which is a significant reduction, i.e. 2.9%, of the original dataset consisting of 70,000 datapoints. Moreover, to demonstrate the robustness of the HSML algorithm to noise, we applied the algorithm to perturbed torus data by adding zero-mean Gaussian white noise of amplitude 0.5 to the dataset. The learned simplicial complex from the noisy data shown in Fig. 2B revealed T2 when (m,n)=(20,100).

Spherical data. We further demonstrate the utility and performance of the HSML algorithm in learning topological properties from high-dimensional data (dimensions >3). Specifically, we use three synthetic datasets, each containing 10,000 points of dimension k, uniformly sampled from the (k−1)-dimensional unit sphere Sk−1 for k=4,5,6. We applied the HSML algorithm using 20 rounds of clustering with 30 clusters in each round. The Betti numbers of the three learned simplicial complexes are presented in Table 1, and they coincide with the ground truth Betti numbers of Sk−1 for k=4,5,6.

Structure analysis of porous media

Extracting topological properties from a dataset is essential for a wide range of real-world applications that play a significant role in our daily lives. To demonstrate the practical effectiveness and utility of the HSML algorithm, we apply it to comprehend pore structures in porous media. This understanding is crucial for detecting changes in water distributions during evaporation, enabling the monitoring of variations in groundwater levels, and facilitating control over the growth of plant roots (36). The pore structures within a porous medium can be deciphered by quantifying holes, in this context, defined by the regions surrounded by water. Figure 3A displays an X-ray CT image of fluid filled in a column of packed glass beads (37). The image is composed of voxels, each of which is a simplicial complex consisting of twelve 1-simplices (edges), six 2-simplices (faces), and one 3-simplices (cubical solid). Then, the number of holes in this porous medium can be represented by the first Betti number of the simplicial subcomplex within the “image complex” composed of voxels with a value of 1.

Pore structure analysis by HSML. A) X-ray CT image of the water in the studied porous material. B) A connected component of the image. C) The learned simplicial complex by HSML applied to the image component in (B).

To elaborate the utilization of the HSML algorithm to this application, we first examined the partial image displayed in Fig. 3B and manually counted a total of 17 holes contained in this section of the porous medium. We then collapsed each voxel to its center and represented it by a 3D vector. The collection of these vectors corresponding to the voxels with value 1 gives rise to a reduced topological dataset C. In the HSML algorithm, we chose 10 rounds of clustering (m=10) and 500 clusters in each round (n=500). The learned simplicial complex N(C) is shown in Fig. 3C, and its Betti numbers were computed using its cohomology groups, yielding b0(C)=1, b1(C)=17, and b2(C)=b3(C)=0. This indicates the presence of 17 holes in this particular section, which is consistent with the counted value confirming the accuracy of the analysis. We then applied the HSML algorithm to the entire image shown in Fig. 3A. The calculated zeroth to third Betti numbers are 83, 496, 0, and 0, respectively, which also coincide with the results obtained by alternative approaches (37). This consistency provides additional validation and reinforces the accuracy and reliability of the HSML analysis.

Synchronization analysis of electronic oscillator networks

In addition to static data, HSML is also applicable to data representing dynamic characteristics, such as time series. To showcase its capability in the domain of time series analysis, we apply HSML to data collected from a network of electronic oscillators and represent the rhythmic patterns of the network using topological features of the learned simplicial complexes. The dataset consists of experimental recordings from a network of 28 Rössler oscillator circuits with the coupling topology shown in Fig. 4A, where the coupling strength between each pair of coupled oscillators is identical. The experiment was conducted 100 times, each with a different coupling strength uniformly scaled from 0 to 1 in increments of 0.01. For each run of the experiment, the data recorded from each oscillator is a time series representing its output voltage measured at 30,000 time slots (38).

Synchronization analysis of electronic oscillator networks using HSML. A) The coupling topology of a network of 28 Rössler electronic oscillators. B) The Kuramoto order parameter time series of the network with the coupling strengths κ=0, 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3. C) The Betti numbers of the learned simplicial complexes versus the coupling strength κ.

A widely used synchrony measure for an oscillator network is the Kuramoto order parameter, which ranges from 0 to 1, with 1 indicating full network synchrony (39). Figure 4B plots the order parameter time series for data collected under different coupling strengths κ=0,0.1,0.2,0.3. To use the HSML algorithm for synchronization analysis, we treat the data for each run of the experiment as 30,000 points sampled from a 28-dimensional manifold, and then performed 28 rounds of clustering with 50 clusters in each round. Figure 4C shows the Betti numbers of the learned simplicial complexes for the same four cases considered in Fig. 4B. We observe a significant difference in the Betti numbers of the simplicial complexes, representing varying levels of synchronization within the network. More precisely, as the coupling strength increases, resulting in a higher Kuramoto order parameter and greater synchrony, the higher-order Betti numbers of the learned simplicial complex diminish. This result has significant physical meaning. We observe that the voltages of synchronous oscillators change in unison, making their measurement time series identical. This naturally reduces the dimensionality of the manifold embedding the data of the entire network, leading to zero in the learned Betti numbers of orders higher than the dimension of the embedding manifold. This interpretation further illustrates HSML’s capability for dimensionality reduction as a classical manifold learning tool.

HSML-enabled topological representation learning

Accompanied by the construction of simplicial complexes, the HSML algorithm, as we have illustrated, also directly reveals the topological properties of the learned complexes in terms of cohomology groups and Betti numbers. This capability enables the utilization of HSML as a feature engineering machinery to discover topological representations of datasets. Such representations can subsequently be used to train machine learning models for various data analytics and learning tasks. Here, we showcase the power of HSML-extracted features in both supervised and unsupervised learning, two important machine learning paradigms.

Topological classification of genetic predisposition to alcoholism. In this application, we demonstrate the exceptional effectiveness of using topological quantities learned by HSML in supervised learning. We consider a classification problem concerning the correlates of genetic predisposition to alcoholism using electroencephalogram (EEG) data. The dataset contains EEG recordings of two subjects, one alcoholic and one control, who completed 87 and 97 runs of a clinical trial, respectively (40). Each run consists of 1-s EEG recording, sampled at the rate of 256 Hz, with voltage measured by 64 electrodes placed on a subject’s scalp. Fig. 5A and B plots the data for the alcoholic and control subjects, respectively, which exhibit a high degree of similarity. Therefore, distinguishing the two groups by direct observation of the EEG data is challenging. Here, we leverage HSML-extracted features to classify the two groups.

Topological classification of alcoholic and control subjects by applying HSML to their EEG recordings. The EEG recordings of an alcoholic subject (A) and a control subject (B). C) The 3-tuples composed of the first, second, and third Betti numbers learned from the EEG data of the alcoholic (dots) and control (squares) subjects. D) The confusion matrix resulting from the 5-fold cross-validation for the support vector machine, which is trained by using the Betti numbers learned by applying the HSML algorithm to the entire EEG dataset.

For the EEG data collected from each run of clinical trials, we chose 64 rounds (m=64) of clustering with 30 clusters (n=30) per round to construct a 64-dimensional simplical complex and then computed its Betti numbers. As an illustration, Fig. 5C displays the 3-tuples consisting of the first, second, and third Betti numbers of the constructed simplical complexes. We observe that the two subjects can already be clearly distinguished, with just three Betti numbers. To justify this claim, we used all 184 (87 + 97) sets of 64-tuples of Betti numbers to train a support vector machine and then performed a 5-fold cross-validation, which achieved a 95.7% classification accuracy. The confusion matrix is shown in Fig. 5D.

Semantic clustering for analysis of social media posts. In this application, we demonstrate the superior effectiveness of using topological features learned by HSML in unsupervised learning. We consider a semantic clustering problem for social media post analysis. The dataset used in this application documents comprehensive information on climate change-related topics from Reddit, where each datapoint consists of 16 attributes (41). In particular, we considered two topics, flooding and wildfires, with sample sizes of 4,472 and 7,415, respectively. The text attributes for these datapoints are shown in Fig. 6A. To ensure unbiased learning results, we randomly selected 1,000 samples from each topic and repeated the selection process 50 times. This resulted in 100 subdatasets, each with a sample size of 1,000. For each subdataset, we retained seven numerical attributes in the datapoints and applied HSML with 20 rounds (m=20) of clustering and 30 clusters (n=30) per round to construct a 7D simplical complex. The distribution of the Betti numbers across the learned 50 simplical complexes for each topic is shown in Fig. 6B. We then applied k-means algorithm to cluster the obtained 100 sets of Betti numbers into two groups. The matching matrix for the external evaluation of the clustering performance is shown in Fig. 6C, which gives the Rand Index 0.79.

A) The text attributes of the data concerning the topics “flooding” and “wildfire.” B) The distributions of the Betti numbers learned by using the numerical attributes of the data for both topics. Discussion

Homotopy equivalent manifold learning

In this work, we illuminate clustering as an essential tool for simplicial manifold learning and demonstrate the use of cluster centroids as a reduced dataset to construct simplicial complexes. Notably, the learned simplicial complex exhibits a Čech nerve structure (see Section S1), which is of great significance in topological data analysis. This guarantees that the learned complex S=N(U) is homotopy equivalent to the embedding manifold M, i.e. equivalent to M up to continuous deformations (42), provided the data are sufficient. Because the Čech nerve is essentially determined by the clusters, the cluster centroid is a primary choice as a representation of a cluster. In fact, any point within a cluster other than its centroid can serve as the representative point for the same purpose. This implies that different choices of the cluster representatives yield the same Čech nerve and thus reveal the same topological properties up to homotopy equivalence. Nonetheless, our choice of cluster centroids in the HSML algorithm offers significant practical advantages, such as computational efficiency and robustness to noise.

Robustness to noise and perturbations

The proposed HSML algorithm demonstrates resistance to data noise. At a local level, the acquisition of a cluster centroid involves averaging over all the points within the cluster, which yields a statistically noise-robust geometric representation of neighborhood systems. On a global scale, small perturbations applied to the learned simplicial complex correspond to homotopy equivalent operations, resulting in deformed simplicial manifolds that retain topological equivalence with the original learned complex. Moreover, owing to homotopy invariance of cohomology, such perturbations also preserve the cohomology groups, thereby maintaining the learned topological features. This is illustrated in Fig. 2B.

To quantitatively analyze the impact of data noise on the topology of the learned simplicial complex, we adopt the concept of signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) from signal processing, which is defined as the ratio of the power of a signal to the power of noise (43), and extend it to topological datasets. We recall the topological meaning of Betti numbers, as illuminated in the pore analysis example, which represent the numbers of holes in a manifold. In this context, the notion of topological signal can be introduced as a real-valued function defined on the set of holes in the groundtruth manifold. Following this idea, we define the value of the topological signal evaluated at a “k-dimensional hole” as the radius of the largest k-dimensional sphere that fits inside the hole. The power of this signal is then given by the square of this radius. This extends the classical notion of SNR to a topological context, which we refer to as topological SNR.

To evaluate the robustness of the HSML algorithm against noise, we conduct a comprehensive noise analysis for HSML using the introduced topological SNR as a measure. Specifically, we revisited the previously used synthetic toric data (Fig. 2A) for which the topological signal is given by the constant function 1. In this case, we perturbed the data using various types of noise with different topological SNR values. Figure 7A shows the plot of the learned simplicial complexes, along with their corresponding Betti numbers, versus the topological SNR expressed in decibels (dB). We observe that the tolerance of topological SNR for the Betti numbers to persist at the groundtruth values, i.e. b0(T2)=1, b1(T2)=2, and b2(T2)=1, is around 14 dB across different types of noise. In most application domains, such as telecommunication, imaging processing, and instrumentation engineering, the SNR of a signal is typically required to be at least 20 dB (44–46). This demonstrates the excellent noise robustness property of HSML. It is also worth mentioning that noise intensity is the major factor affecting the learned Betti numbers, while the correlations between different components (along different dimensions) of the noise have minimal influence on the learning performance. This is illustrated in Fig. 7B, where the noise intensity is measured by the root mean square amplitude (RMSA).

Analysis of robustness of HSML to data noise by using synthetic toric data. A) The learned simplicial complexes and their Betti numbers versus topological SNR for white, pink, blue, and purple noise. B) The learning performance versus the correlations between different components of noise with varying intensity, where σij denotes the correlation between the ith and jth components of the noise for 1≤i<j≤3.

Variants of HSML

In addition to Čech nerves, the HSML framework can also be used to generate other types of simplicial complexes (see Section S1). However, it can be shown that, given sufficient data, all of these simplicial complexes possess the same topological properties identical to those of the embedding manifold M in the sense of homotopy equivalence (see Section S3). Because homotopy equivalent spaces share the same cohomology groups (42), this is the inspiration for us to utilize topological features derived from cohomology groups, e.g. Betti numbers, to learn manifold topology and perform convergence analysis of the HSML algorithm.

Computational complexity and efficiency

The HSML algorithm achieves the learning of simplicial complexes through an exclusive use of clustering. This process can be efficiently accomplished by various unsupervised learning algorithms, such as k-means clustering, Gaussian mixture model clustering, and DBSCAN. Notably, these algorithms offer a worst-case runtime complexity as low as O(n2), where n denotes the number of datapoints (47). Additionally, our approach to computing cohomology groups is based on topologically reduced datasets, and the choice of R as the coefficients of cohomology allows for learning topological features by simply evaluating matrix ranks (see Section S3). This significantly enhances computational efficiency by leveraging established computational linear algebra techniques, such as singular-value decomposition and Gaussian elimination. This advancement elevates the application potential and accessibility of topological data science.

Comparison to state-of-the-art algorithms

The HSML algorithm is able to learn simplicial manifolds and their topological features that are undecipherable by existing topological analysis tools. In Fig. 8, we present a comparison between the HSML algorithm and a widely used persistent homology algorithm (48, 49). We applied both algorithms to learn the topological structures of a figure-eight, composed of two tangent circles of significantly different radii measuring 5 and 0.5, respectively (Fig. 8A), using its sampled data in the presence of uniform noise of intensity 0.5. Because this graph, denoted by the wedge sum S1∨S1=˙U, exhibits two holes bounded by the two circles, its Betti numbers are b0(U)=1 and b1(U)=2. Applying the HSML algorithm with 30 rounds of clustering and 30 clusters in each round yielded a simplicial complex that precisely delineates the shape of the figure-eight as shown in Fig. 8B. Notably, the Betti numbers of this learned complex agreed with those of U. However, the persistent homology algorithm was unable to unambiguously detect the two holes, shown in Fig. 8C.

Comparison between HSML and persistent homology. A) Data (left) sampled from the figure-eight S1∨S1, composed of two circles with radii 5 and 0.5 (right), and perturbed by uniform noise of intensity 0.5. B) The simplicial complex and its Betti numbers learned by using HSML. C) The bar code representation (top) of the persistent homology groups learned from the data; and the simplicial complexes, together with their Betti numbers, constructed using three different filtration radii (bottom), where the two holes of the figure-eight were not identified.
