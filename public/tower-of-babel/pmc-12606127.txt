Impact of CNS medication burden and drug interactions on delirium in patients with advanced cancer: a multicenter prospective observational study
Authors: Min Jung Geum, Shin Hye Yoo, Si Won Lee, Moonki Hong, Eun Hee Jung, Yu Jung Kim, Beodeul Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Delirium is a common neuropsychiatric complication in patients with advanced cancer. Central nervous system (CNS)-active medications are established risk factors for delirium; however, these patients often require polypharmacy for symptom management, resulting in a high medication burden and increased risk of drug-drug interactions (DDIs). The impact of CNS medication burden and DDIs on delirium remains unclear. This multicenter, prospective observational study examined the association between CNS medication burden, measured using the CNS standardized daily dose (SDD), DDIs, and delirium incidence, duration, and mortality in hospitalized patients with advanced cancer. Among 190 patients, 20% developed delirium. CNS SDD was associated with delirium risk (adjusted OR [aOR]: 1.04, 95% CI: 1.0004–1.08). In categorical analyses, patients with a CNS SDD ≥ 10 had a significantly higher risk of delirium (aOR: 4.29, 95% CI: 1.33–15.47). Potential DDIs increasing exposure to delirium-risk medications had an aOR of 4.14 (95% CI: 0.96–17.41). Although neither CNS SDD nor DDIs affected delirium duration, opioid burden was associated with increased mortality. CNS medication burden and DDIs may be important factors for delirium and clinical outcomes. These findings underscore the need for medication optimization and proactive DDI monitoring to reduce the risk of delirium and improve patient outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23079-8.

Introduction

Delirium is a common and acute neuropsychiatric complication in patients with advanced cancer and is frequently underrecognized1. These patients are particularly susceptible to delirium due to the decline in overall health resulting from ongoing chemotherapy and concurrent pharmacological treatments. Delirium is associated with the discontinuation of intensive treatment, prolonged hospitalization, diminished quality of life, and increased mortality2,3.

Given its multifactorial etiology, various clinical and pharmacological factors contribute to its onset and progression3,4. Among these, medication-related factors are particularly significant due to the substantial medication burden in patients with cancer. Opioids are commonly used for pain management in advanced cancer. However, high opioid doses have been associated with a significantly increased risk of delirium5,6.

Beyond opioids, central nervous system (CNS)-active medications play a critical role in delirium-related outcomes, including incidence, severity, hospitalization due to delirium, and mortality2,7–12. However, most existing studies have evaluated the use of these medications as a risk factor rather than comprehensively assessing medication burden based on dosage or polypharmacy. This limitation represents a significant gap, considering that polypharmacy is common among patients with cancer13, with more than half receiving at least one CNS-active medication14.

Additionally, drug-drug interactions (DDIs) are a major concern due to the complexity of medication regimens in this population13–17. DDIs involving CNS-active drugs may amplify neurotoxic effects, potentiate sedation, and impair cognitive function, thereby increasing the risk of delirium. The extent to which DDIs contribute to delirium incidence and outcomes in patients with advanced cancer remains unclear.

To address these knowledge gaps, we conducted a multicenter prospective observational study to examine the impact of inpatient medication burden—including CNS medication burden (measured as CNS standardized daily dose; SDD) and DDIs—on delirium incidence, duration, and mortality in patients with advanced cancer. This study aimed to elucidate these associations to inform strategies for optimizing medication management to mitigate delirium risk and improve clinical outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 190 patients were enrolled in the study. The baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1, and detailed demographic and clinical information is provided in Table S2. The mean age was 67.4 years; males comprised 50.0% of the cohort. Among the patients, 20.5% were classified as overweight or obese, and 5.3% had hearing impairment. The proportions of current drinkers and smokers were 9.5% and 1.1%, respectively. During hospitalization, 21.6% of the patients received chemotherapy, and 7.9% had a prior history of delirium (Table 1).

A mean of 3.1 potential DDIs per patient was identified. Among these, those potentially associated with delirium were classified into four categories: interactions that could induce CNS depression (58 patients), serotonin syndrome (56 patients), sedation (13 patients), and increased exposure to delirium-risk medications (13 patients; Table 2).

Delirium incidence

Delirium occurred in 38 of the 190 patients (20%), with the hypoactive subtype being the most common (11.1%; Table 3). The median time to delirium onset was 2.5 days (IQR: 2–5.75) post-admission.

In multivariable regression analysis, CNS medication burden, assessed as a continuous CNS SDD, was marginally associated with an increased risk of delirium (adjusted OR [aOR]: 1.04, 95% CI: 1.0004–1.08; Table 4), although the effect size was minimal. When analyzed categorically, a CNS SDD ≥ 10 was significantly associated with an increased risk of delirium (aOR: 4.29, 95% CI: 1.33–15.47; Table 4). Subgroup analyses showed that a non-opioid SDD ≥ 10 significantly increased the risk of delirium (aOR: 8.98, 95% CI: 1.65–47.44), whereas an opioid SDD ≥ 10 was not statistically significant (aOR: 2.97, 95% CI: 0.94–9.67; Table S4). Among drug classes, antiepileptic SDD was significantly associated with delirium (aOR: 1.12, 95% CI: 1.004–1.25; Table S4). The restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis suggested an upward trend in the odds ratio for delirium with increasing CNS SDD, focusing on the range where the curve could be reliably estimated (Fig. 1).

Among the DDI categories, interactions that increased exposure to delirium-risk medications showed elevated aORs but were not statistically significant (aOR: 4.14, 95% CI: 0.96–17.41; Table 4). These DDIs were identified in 17 cases among 13 patients. The most frequent interaction involved concomitant use of opioids with celecoxib or duloxetine (9 cases), leading to increased opioid exposure. Other common interactions involved opioids and antibiotics (3 cases, including fluconazole and ciprofloxacin), also resulting in elevated opioid levels. In addition, single cases were observed of various other interactions that could increase exposure to specific drugs (one case each for diazepam, escitalopram, lidocaine, morphine, and paroxetine).

Delirium duration

The mean duration of delirium was 5.2 days (Table 3). Neither CNS medication burden nor DDIs were significantly associated with delirium duration in multivariable or subgroup regression analyses (Table S5).

Overall survival

The median follow-up duration was 49 days (IQR: 25–144.5) and 92.6% of the cohort had died by the end of the follow-up (Table 3). Survival analysis stratified by CNS medication usage revealed that patients receiving CNS medications had a significantly shorter median overall survival of 46 days, compared with 127 days in those not receiving CNS medications (p < 0.01 by log-rank test; Fig. 2). In the baseline Cox model, a violation of the proportional hazards assumption was observed for chemotherapy during hospitalization. To address this, we applied a stratified Cox model using chemotherapy during hospitalization as the stratification factor, which satisfied the proportional hazards assumption for the overall model. CNS SDD, analyzed as a continuous variable, was not associated with mortality (adjusted HR [aHR]: 1.00, 95% CI: 0.99–1.02; Table S6). However, when analyzed as a categorical variable, a CNS SDD > 0 was associated with an increased risk of mortality, primarily driven by opioid SDD: >0 and < 3 (aHR: 2.39, 95% CI: 1.49–3.83), ≥ 3 and < 10 (aHR: 1.76, 95% CI: 1.11–2.80), and ≥ 10 (aHR: 1.68, 95% CI: 1.08–2.59; Table S6).

Among DDIs, none were significantly associated with mortality. However, interactions with the potential to induce CNS depression (aOR: 1.42, 95% CI: 0.998–2.01) and those associated with serotonin syndrome (aOR: 1.29, 95% CI: 0.92–1.82) showed elevated aHR (Table S6). Interactions with the potential to induce CNS depression were identified in 152 cases among 58 patients, while those associated with serotonin syndrome were observed in 131 cases among 56 patients. The most frequent interactions within these two categories involved multiple opioid combinations (81 cases). Other notable interactions contributing to CNS depression included opioid–metoclopramide (31 cases) and opioid–zolpidem interactions (6 cases), with all other interactions occurring in five or fewer cases. Regarding serotonin syndrome-related interactions, duloxetine–opioid (14 cases), chlorpheniramine–opioid (7 cases), and trazodone–opioid (6 cases) were identified, with all remaining interactions occurring in five or fewer cases.

Discussion

This study provides valuable insights into the impact of inpatient CNS medication burden and DDIs on delirium incidence and survival outcomes in patients with advanced cancer. The findings highlight the significant role of CNS medication burden in increasing the risk of delirium. There was a significant association between higher CNS medication burden (CNS SDD) and delirium incidence in our cohort. Patients with a CNS SDD ≥ 10 exhibited an increased risk of delirium, suggesting that the summative burden of CNS medications plays a critical role in delirium development. The increased delirium risk associated with opioid dosage has been documented in prior studies, aligning with our findings6,18. Although previous research has examined the correlation between medication burden and delirium, many of those studies focused solely on specific drug lists using tools such as the anticholinergic risk scale or drug delirium scale19–21. This study extends the existing literature by demonstrating the broader impact of CNS medication burden on delirium risk. Given the extensive use of CNS medications for symptom management in patients with cancer, these results are particularly relevant to real-world clinical practice, emphasizing the need for careful dose optimization. These findings are also consistent with previously suggested approaches for managing drug-induced delirium, which emphasize systematic medication review, discontinuation or tapering of delirogenic drugs, and minimization of polypharmacy4.

While the CNS SDD categories of 0, > 0 to < 3, and ≥ 3 were based on prior literature, that study was conducted in nursing home residents22. In contrast, our study included patients with advanced cancer receiving palliative care, among whom CNS medication use for pain control and symptom management was more extensive, resulting in a higher overall distribution of SDD values.

To account for this distribution, we introduced an additional cut-off at CNS SDD ≥ 10, which approximated the upper quartile of our cohort, and found it to be associated with delirium risk. However, analyses at higher SDD values were limited by the small number of patients, as reflected in the wide confidence intervals in both regression and spline analyses.

These findings should therefore be interpreted with caution, and future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate this cut-off and further clarify the clinical implications of a high CNS medication burden.

Subgroup analysis revealed that, among non-opioids, only the SDD of antiepileptics was potentially associated with delirium risk (aOR: 1.12). However, when considering the cumulative SDD of all non-opioid CNS medications, a threshold ≥ 10 was associated with a markedly increased delirium risk (aOR: 8.98). This finding suggests that polypharmacy involving multiple CNS medications has a greater impact on delirium risk than any single medication alone. Most antiepileptics administered in this study were gabapentinoids, a class of drugs for which previous research has yielded conflicting findings regarding their association with delirium23,24. However, our study establishes a link between antiepileptic SDD and delirium risk, emphasizing the importance of appropriate dosing rather than mere drug presence. This finding aligns with existing guidelines, which suggest that high initial doses of gabapentinoids may contribute to delirium25. Since patients with cancer frequently use gabapentinoids for neuropathic pain management, careful dose optimization may help mitigate delirium risk in this population.

Beyond medication burden, we examined potential DDIs that could influence delirium risk. While no associations were statistically significant, those involving increased exposure to delirium-risk medications, yielded relatively higher aOR. The most frequent potential DDIs involved cytochrome P450 2D6 (CYP2D6) inhibitors (e.g., duloxetine) or CYP3A4 inhibitors (e.g., fluconazole) combined with opioids that are substrates of these enzymes (e.g., oxycodone), potentially altering opioid levels. Opioids undergoing phase I metabolism via cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzymes, such as oxycodone, fentanyl, and tramadol, are particularly susceptible to DDIs, whereas those primarily metabolized through phase II pathways, like morphine and hydromorphone, tend to have fewer CYP450-related interactions26,27. The recent cohort by Wei et al. observed that among older adults on antidepressants including duloxetine, initiation of CYP2D6-metabolized opioids was associated with increased pain-related hospitalization and emergency department visits28. Given the necessity of opioid therapy for pain management in patients with advanced cancer, avoiding such drug combinations may not always be feasible. Nonetheless, considering potential DDIs during drug selection, dose adjustments, response evaluations, and adverse effect monitoring may provide an effective strategy to reduce delirium risk. Further research is warranted to establish whether such DDIs translate into clinically meaningful outcomes.

In our study, delirium duration was not significantly associated with CNS medication burden or DDIs. This finding suggests that other clinical variables, such as baseline cognitive function, underlying disease progression, and individualized treatment approaches may play a more dominant role in determining the duration of delirium. Previous studies have similarly indicated that multifactorial elements, including the effectiveness of intervention strategies and patient-specific vulnerabilities, influence delirium duration29,30. Notably, this analysis included only medications administered before the onset of delirium, excluding drugs used for delirium management, which could have influenced its duration. Additionally, as this was an observational study, delirium treatment varied among patients, incorporating psychiatric consultations, pharmacological interventions, and other supportive strategies. These diverse treatment approaches may have contributed to variability in delirium duration, potentially limiting the ability to detect significant associations with CNS medication burden or DDIs.

Overall survival was significantly shorter in patients receiving CNS medications compared to those not receiving these drugs. Subgroup analysis indicated that this association was primarily driven by opioid burden rather than non-opioid CNS medications. Although our models were adjusted for relevant covariates, overall survival is influenced by multiple factors, including disease progression, and the possibility of residual confounding cannot be excluded3. High-dose opioid use has been associated with an increased risk of adverse effects, and prior studies have suggested that DDIs may also influence clinical outcome4. In our cohort, DDIs capable of inducing CNS depression and those related to serotonin syndrome showed relatively higher HRs, although not statistically significant. Overall, these findings highlight the importance of cautious opioid dose titration and close monitoring of medication use to optimize both symptom control and survival outcomes.

This study has some limitations. First, as an observational study, causality cannot be definitively established. Although adjustments were made for key baseline characteristics to control for confounders, residual confounding factors may still exist. Second, medications administered after the onset of delirium were excluded from the analysis, which may have led to an underestimation of their potential impact on delirium duration and overall survival. Third, the analysis of DDIs was limited to potential interactions regardless of whether a clinically significant interaction actually occurred. This approach may overestimate the clinical impact of DDIs. Fourth, the study was conducted across tertiary university hospitals with palliative care settings, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other healthcare settings. Finally, the relatively small number of patients who developed delirium (n = 38), together with the use of multiple statistical tests, may have increased the risk of false-positive findings and limited the statistical power to detect certain associations, particularly for delirium duration and DDI subgroups. As a result, some true effects may not have reached statistical significance. Further studies with larger sample sizes and diverse clinical settings are needed to validate our findings.

Despite limitations, this study is, to our knowledge, the first multicenter prospective cohort study to analyze the association between inpatient CNS medication burden and DDIs with delirium and survival in patient with advanced cancer. The findings highlight the importance of careful dose titration of opioids and non-opioid CNS medications to achieve adequate symptom control and reduce the risk of delirium. Even when individual CNS medication doses are appropriate, total CNS medication burden should be routinely assessed, and unnecessary polypharmacy should be minimized, particularly in patients at high risk for delirium. Additionally, proactive identification of DDIs, especially those that increase opioid exposure, CNS depression, and serotonin syndrome, should be integrated into clinical workflows to enhance patient safety. Personalized medication regimens tailored to patient-specific risk factors may improve both delirium prevention and survival outcomes, further emphasizing the need for individualized therapeutic strategies in managing CNS medications in patients with advanced cancer.

Conclusion

This study underscores the role of inpatient CNS medication burden and DDIs in delirium risk and survival outcomes among patients with advanced cancer. The findings suggest that a higher CNS medication burden is associated with increased delirium incidence and a opioid burden is associated with increased mortality. Potential DDIs, particularly those that may increase delirium-risk medication exposure, CNS depression, or serotonin toxicity, require careful consideration in clinical practice. These results highlight the need for risk stratification and proactive medication review (assessment and reconciliation) strategies to optimize patient outcomes.

Methods

Study design and population

This study was designed as a multicenter, prospective, observational cohort study incorporating a patient-based registry. Data were collected from four tertiary medical institutions in South Korea: Seoul National University Bundang Hospital, Yonsei University Severance Hospital, CHA University Bundang Medical Center, and Seoul National University Hospital. The study period spanned from May 1, 2021, to May 31, 2023.

Institutional review board (IRB) approvals were obtained from all participating centers before study initiation (CHA University, CHAMC 2021-03-054-002; Seoul National University, H-2103-028-1201; Seoul National University Bundang Hospital, B-2104/681 − 405; and Yonsei University, 4–2021-0323). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to study enrollment. All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

The study population comprised patients admitted to the Acute Palliative Care Units (APCUs) of the participating hospitals within the defined study period. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients aged ≥ 20 years, (2) those diagnosed with advanced solid malignancies, and (3) individuals admitted to the APCU. Exclusion criteria were applied to obtain a more homogeneous study sample. Patients were excluded if they: (1) had a hospital stay exceeding 3 months (n = 4), (2) were transferred to other departments before completion of APCU care (n = 7), or (3) experienced terminal delirium, defined as a single episode of delirium occurring within 2 weeks before death (n = 20). Patients with hospital stays > 3 months were excluded to avoid including outliers in terms of hospitalization length. Those transferred from the APCU were excluded to ensure complete data collection within the palliative care setting.

After applying these criteria, the final study population comprised 190 patients admitted to the APCUs of the four participating centers. At study entry, participants underwent a comprehensive assessment, including the collection of demographic and disease characteristics, family and social histories, medical history, and functional measures. All patients were followed from their index date, defined as the date of hospital admission, until death from any cause or the end of the study period (November 30, 2024), whichever occurred first.

Exposure measurements

We collected comprehensive data on all medications administered during each patient’s hospitalization, including the drug name, dosage, route, and duration of treatment. All systemically administered medications were included, whereas topically administered medications were excluded from the analysis. Calculation of CNS SDD and assessment of potential DDIs were based on medications administered throughout hospitalization, with drugs initiated after delirium onset excluded, since they were likely used for treatment or symptom management.

CNS medication burden

CNS medications were defined as opioid analgesics (ATC code: N02A), sedatives (N01AX, N03AE, N05BA, N05CC, N05CD, N05CF, N05CM), antipsychotics (N05A), antidepressants (N06A), antiepileptics (N03A, N02BF), cholinergics (N06DA, N07A), and anticholinergics (A03AA, A03AB, A03B, A03CA, A03DA, A04AD01; scopolamine, N04A, N05BB01; hydroxyzine, R03BB, R06AA02; diphenhydramine, S01FA).

To quantitatively assess the burden of CNS medications, we calculated a CNS SDD for each patient using a previously validated method22,31. Specifically, the actual daily dose of each CNS medication was divided by its minimum effective geriatric daily dose (MEGDD), and the resulting ratios were summed to derive the overall CNS medication burden22,31. For determining the MEGDD, if a geriatric dosing recommendation was available in any of the three databases (Micromedex®, UpToDate®, Ministry of Food and Drug Safety [MFDS]), the smallest recommended dose was used; otherwise, the smallest adult dose was applied (Table S1). In the case of opioids, the MEGDD was defined as 10 mg of oral morphine sulfate22,31. The primary analyses were conducted with CNS SDD as a continuous variable. Additionally, based on a published approach22 and the distribution of the data, the CNS SDD was categorized into the following groups: 0, > 0 – <3, ≥ 3 – <10, and ≥ 10.

Drug−drug interaction

Potential DDIs were assessed using the electronic version of Micromedex® software. For each patient, all concomitantly administered medications were analyzed for potential interactions. Interactions relevant to delirium were classified into the following categories: CNS depression, serotonin syndrome, sedation, and increased exposure to medications associated with delirium risk30,32,33. Delirium-risk medications were identified based on previous studies, which include the CNS agents described above30,33,34.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the incidence of delirium. Delirium episodes were identified from daily clinical assessments recorded in medical records and confirmed using the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) diagnostic criteria for delirium35. Secondary outcomes included delirium duration and overall survival. Delirium duration (in days) was calculated for those who developed delirium, defined as the number of days from delirium onset until the resolution of symptoms. Outcomes were categorized based on CNS SDD and DDI profiles to evaluate their association with the measured endpoints. Subgroup analyses assessed the impact of the SDD categories for both opioids and non-opioids and the SDD of individual non-opioids classes, including patients regardless of concomitant CNS medication use. In addition, we estimated the predicted delirium incidence according to CNS SDD levels and compared overall survival based on CNS medication usage.

Covariates

Based on a comprehensive literature review, we identified covariates that could potentially confound the relationship between CNS medication use and delirium3,6,36,37. The study accounted for various patient-related factors, including age, sex, cohabitation status, and use of medical aids. Clinical characteristics such as body mass index (BMI) categories (< 18.5 kg/m² [underweight], 18.5–25 kg/m² [normal], 25–30 kg/m² [overweight], and ≥ 30 kg/m² [obese]) were also considered. Additional variables were prior history of delirium, alcohol consumption, and smoking status. Cancer-related factors included chemotherapy during hospitalization. All variables were extracted from medical records.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages (%), and continuous variables are expressed as mean with standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range. Logistic regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the effects of CNS medication burden and potential DDIs with delirium incidence. Linear regression analyses were used to assess the effects of these variables on delirium duration. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) and standardized coefficients (β), respectively, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). In the analysis of delirium duration, two extreme outliers, defined as values greater than three times the interquartile range above the third quartile, were excluded. After outlier exclusion, the approximate normality of residuals was confirmed. The Cox proportional hazards regression model was used for survival analysis, and results are presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. When a violation of this assumption was detected, a stratified Cox model was applied for the relevant covariate38. The same regression methods were also applied for subgroup analyses of CNS medication classes. Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) in regression models, and no concerning multicollinearity was detected. An RCS curve was constructed to explore potential nonlinear associations between CNS SDD and the OR for delirium incidence. These models were adjusted for the same covariates as the main regression analyses. Additionally, survival outcomes stratified by CNS medication use were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between groups were evaluated with the log-rank test.

A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using R, version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).
