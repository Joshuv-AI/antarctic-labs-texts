How to manage thyroid eye disease: recent advances – a narrative review
Authors: Georgios K Markantes, Grigoris Effraimidis, Olga Karapanou, Katerina Saltiki, Marina Michalaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thyroid eye disease (TED) is a complex autoimmune disorder and the most common extrathyroidal manifestation of Graves’ disease, characterized by orbital inflammation, tissue remodelling, and varying degrees of functional and cosmetic impairment. Although often mild, moderate-to-severe and sight-threatening forms can significantly compromise vision and quality of life. Advances in the understanding of TED pathophysiology – particularly the roles of orbital fibroblasts, thyrotropin receptor and insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor signalling– have transformed its diagnostic and therapeutic landscape. This manuscript provides a comprehensive and updated overview of epidemiology, risk factors, clinical presentation, diagnostic evaluation and management of TED. Current treatment strategies emphasize early risk-factor modification, restoration of euthyroidism, and a multidisciplinary approach tailored to disease activity and severity. Intravenous glucocorticoids remain the cornerstone of therapy for active moderate-to-severe disease, whereas adjunctive immunosuppressive agents and orbital radiotherapy are used in selected cases. The advent of targeted biologic therapies, particularly teprotumumab, as well as rituximab and tocilizumab, has significantly expanded therapeutic options and enabled a more personalized, phenotype-driven approach. Surgical rehabilitation continues to play a pivotal role in the inactive phase. Emerging therapies targeting novel molecular pathways hold promise for further improving patient outcomes. Despite these advances, challenges related to accessibility, cost, long-term safety and optimal patient selection persist. Ongoing research and international collaboration are expected to refine management strategies and enhance the quality of life of patients with TED.

Introduction

Thyroid eye disease (TED) has been recognized for at least two centuries. It was described by Robert James Graves and others in the late 18th to early 19th century, though an earlier description is attributed to the Persian physician al-Jurjani in the 12th century.1 Over time, several terms have been used, including Graves’ ophthalmopathy and thyroid-associated orbitopathy. However, ‘thyroid eye disease’ is the preferred contemporary designation.1,2

TED is an autoimmune disorder closely associated with Graves’ disease (GD). It is characterized by immune-mediated inflammation of the extraocular muscles and orbital adipose tissue, leading to the hallmark clinical features of proptosis, conjunctival hyperaemia, periorbital oedema and diplopia. In severe cases, sight-threatening complications may occur, including compressive optic neuropathy and corneal breakdown.2

Scope and methodology of the literature search

This narrative review aims to provide a comprehensive and up-to-date overview of TED, focusing on its epidemiology, pathophysiology, clinical presentation, diagnosis and contemporary management strategies. Particular emphasis is placed on recent advances in targeted therapies and evolving treatment algorithms. A structured literature search was conducted using the electronic databases PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science. Articles published in English from January 2000 to April 2026 were considered, with priority given to publications from the last decade. The search employed combinations of the following keywords: “thyroid eye disease”, “Graves’ orbitopathy”, “Graves’ ophthalmopathy”, “pathophysiology”, “diagnosis”, “management”, “glucocorticoids”, “teprotumumab”, “rituximab”, “tocilizumab”, “biologic therapy” and “targeted therapy”. Additional sources were identified through manual screening of reference lists of relevant articles and international guidelines, including those from the European Thyroid Association (ETA), the American Thyroid Association (ATA) and the European Group on Graves’ Orbitopathy (EUGOGO). Priority was given to randomized controlled trials, meta-analyses, systematic reviews, consensus statements and high-quality observational studies. Publications deemed to be of limited relevance or methodological rigour were excluded. The final selection of studies was based on their scientific quality, clinical relevance and contribution to the current understanding of TED.

Review

Although TED is the most common extrathyroidal manifestation of GD, it remains relatively uncommon in the general population, whilst its euthyroid variant has been included in the European Reference Network (ERN) of rare diseases. In Europe, the annual incidence is 5 per 100,000 individuals, with a prevalence of 155 per 100,000.3 In the USA, TED affects 8.9 per 100,000 person-years in women and 1 per 100,000 person-years in men.4 Approximately 40% of patients with GD develop TED; most cases are mild, 6% are moderate-to-severe and only 0.5% are sight-threatening.5,6

TED rarely occurs in the absence of hyperthyroidism (approximately 5% of cases). Amongst these patients, most have autoimmune hypothyroidism, whilst the remainder are euthyroid. Regardless of thyroid functional status, the vast majority exhibit circulating TSH receptor (TSHR) antibodies (TRAb).2,7 Although the disease is more frequent in women, it is more severe in men. A biphasic age distribution has been observed with peak incidence in women at 40–44 and 60–64 years and in men at 45–49 and 65–69 years. TED is more prevalent in African Americans, followed by white people, and has lower rates amongst Asians, in whom sex distribution appears more balanced.8

Pathophysiology

The principal effector cell in TED is the orbital fibroblast. In TED, these cells undergo aberrant activation and proliferation, contributing to both the recruitment of inflammatory cells within the orbit and to tissue remodelling. Activated fibroblasts differentiate into adipocytes and myofibroblasts, thereby mediating orbital fat and extracellular matrix expansion, fibrosis and progressive orbital crowding (Figure1A).9,10

A shared autoantigen between the thyroid gland and the orbit is TSHR, a G protein-coupled receptor expressed on thyroid follicular cells and orbital fibroblasts. In GD, circulating TRAb bind to TSHR on orbital fibroblasts, activating intracellular signalling pathways, primarily the adenylate cyclase–protein kinase A and diacylglycerol–phosphatidylinositol pathways. This results in enhanced cellular proliferation, adipogenic and myofibroblastic differentiation, and increased synthesis of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) and pro-inflammatory cytokines.9,10 TSHR expression is upregulated in orbital fibroblasts and adipocytes from patients with TED. Increased expression of the insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF1R) has also been demonstrated in orbital fibroblasts and lymphocytes. Although IGF1R does not appear to function as a primary autoantigen (IGF1R antibodies are rarely found in patients, whilst immunoglobulins isolated from patients do not directly activate it),11,12 it exhibits close spatial and functional interaction with TSHR. Coactivation of these receptors amplifies downstream signalling, and IGF1R inhibition attenuates TRAb-mediated fibroblast stimulation (Figure 1A).13

Activated orbital fibroblasts secrete cytokines (e.g. IL-6, IL-8, TNF and IFNγ), chemokines (e.g. RANTES), adhesion molecules (e.g. ICAM1) and other inflammatory mediators (PDGF, prostaglandins), promoting infiltration by T and B lymphocytes, macrophages and mast cells. Direct T cell–fibroblast interaction via the CD40–CD40 ligand pathway further enhances immune activation.10,14 Orbital fibroblasts represent a heterogeneous population. In TED, approximately half of fibroblasts express the surface antigen CD34 and derive from bone marrow-origin fibrocytes, whereas fibroblasts in normal orbit are predominantly CD34−. CD34+ cells display higher TSHR and IGF1R expressions and greater cytokine responsiveness, whilst CD34− fibroblasts negatively regulate CD34+, possibly via the SLIT2 molecule.15 Activated fibroblast and myofibroblast-derived excessive production of GAGs, particularly hyaluronan, increases tissue hydration and vascular permeability, leading to enlargement of extraocular muscles and soft tissues. A subset of fibroblasts exhibits adipogenic differentiation; adipocytes display abundant TSHR expression and rapidly multiply, contributing to orbital expansion and proptosis.9,10,15

Oxidative stress is increasingly recognized as a pathogenic contributor. Hyperthyroidism and TED are associated with increased reactive oxygen species production, which promote fibroblast proliferation and cytokine release (Figure 1A). Studies have shown that circulating oxidative stress markers correlate with TED clinical activity.16 Smoking, a well-established risk factor, enhances oxidative pathways and stimulates fibroblast differentiation and inflammatory signalling, potentially via RAGE (receptor for advanced glycation end products)–NF-κB and hypoxia-inducible factor-1α pathways.17 Emerging evidence also implicates epigenetic mechanisms, including altered DNA methylation and non-coding RNA expression, whilst the potential modulatory role of the gut microbiome remains under investigation.9

Clinical presentation

TED may have variable clinical presentations (Figures 2 and 3). In most patients, involvement is bilateral, either symmetrical or asymmetrical, although unilateral presentation may occur. Periorbital tissues commonly exhibit erythema and oedema due to inflammatory infiltration. Eyelid retraction is amongst the most frequent clinical signs, occurring in up to 90% of patients.18 Its pathogenesis is multifactorial and includes increased sympathetic tone, inflammatory involvement of the levator palpebrae superioris and Müller’s muscle, and compensatory overaction of the levator muscle secondary to inferior rectus restriction. Conjunctival inflammation is also common, manifesting as hyperaemia and chemosis, often accompanied by ocular discomfort and epiphora.2,8

Because the orbit is a rigid bony cavity with an anterior opening, any increase in intraorbital volume, resulting from inflammation, GAG accumulation and adipose tissue expansion, leads to anterior displacement of the globe. Proptosis of varying severity occurs in approximately 60% of patients.10

Dry eye symptoms and foreign body sensation are characteristic features of TED. These arise from eyelid retraction, increased eyelid tension and friction, proptosis, lagophthalmos, and dysfunction of the lacrimal and meibomian glands. Ocular surface exposure may result in exposure keratopathy, presenting as keratoconjunctivitis, punctate keratitis or, in severe cases, corneal ulceration, with associated pain, photophobia, and visual disturbance.19

Extraocular muscle involvement occurs in up to 40% of patients clinically and even more frequently on imaging. The inferior rectus muscle is most commonly affected, followed by the other recti; oblique muscles are rarely involved. Extraocular muscle dysfunction manifests as restrictive strabismus and diplopia.10 Dysthyroid optic neuropathy (DON) develops in 5–10% of cases due to optic nerve compression and/or stretching, presenting with decreased visual acuity, impaired colour vision, visual field defects and risk of permanent vision loss.20

Natural course and risk factors

The onset of TED is closely linked to the development of hyperthyroidism. In approximately 85% of patients, TED manifests within 18 months of hyperthyroidism onset. Hyperthyroidism typically precedes ocular involvement although, in a minority of cases, TED may present first. According to the classical Rundle model, TED follows a biphasic course: an initial active phase dominated by inflammation, lasting 6–24 months, followed by a chronic inactive phase characterized primarily by fibrosis.2,6,10 Importantly, disease activity at a given time point does not necessarily correlate with severity. Reactivation after entry into the inactive phase occurs in 5–15.7% of patients, most commonly within 5 years of initial presentation.21 However, late reactivation – even decades later – and cases with multiple relapses have been reported.21

Several risk factors have been identified for the development or reactivation of TED. Cigarette smoking is the strongest modifiable risk factor, conferring up to a tenfold increased risk.10 Smoking triggers and exacerbates pre-existing TED through multiple mechanisms (Figure 1A), including the induction of oxidative stress, activation of hypoxia-related pathways and enhanced autoimmune activity. Recent studies have demonstrated that cigarette smoke extract activates receptors for advanced glycation end products in orbital fibroblasts, leading to increased inflammation via the reactive oxygen species–NF-κB signalling pathway.17 Prolonged hyperthyroidism (odds ratio (OR) 3.35 for >4 months versus <1 month),22 high circulating TRAb levels (OR 1.27 for each 1 IU/L over 4 IU/L),23 radioactive iodine (RAI) therapy (OR 4.05),24 hypercholesterolaemia,25 infections and SARS-CoV-2 vaccination are also associated with increased risk. Although certain genetic polymorphisms have been linked to susceptibility in small studies, robust validation is lacking.26 The PREDIGO score has been developed to estimate the likelihood of TED in patients with GD, incorporating Clinical Activity Score (CAS), TRAb levels, smoking status and duration of hyperthyroidism. It demonstrates a high negative predictive value (91%), helping to identify patients at low risk, but a limited positive predictive value (28%).22 Risk factors for severe or complicated TED include male sex, age over 50 years, smoking, unstable thyroid function, diabetes mellitus (DM) and RAI therapy.8

Diagnosis and differential diagnosis

TED diagnosis is usually straightforward in the typical setting of concomitant GD hyperthyroidism and characteristic ocular manifestations. To avoid missing mild or atypical presentations, accurate diagnosis of GD is essential, meaning that TRAb measurement in all cases of hyperthyroidism is imperative. In addition, all patients with GD should be systematically screened for ocular involvement at each visit.2

Comprehensive clinical evaluation is guided by recommendations from international expert groups. Initial assessment, including soft tissue inflammation, eyelid position, pupillary reflexes, proptosis measurement and diplopia grading in the Gorman scale, may be performed by non-specialists, but ophthalmological evaluation is essential for visual acuity, colour vision, slit-lamp examination, fundoscopy, visual fields and intraocular pressure measurement.2

Therapeutic decision-making depends on accurate assessment of disease activity and severity. Tools like NOSPECS and VISA (vision–inflammation–strabismus–appearance) have been extensively used, despite not providing specific categorization of disease activity or severity.27,28 The most recent consensus by the ETA and ATA recommends the CAS for activity assessment. The CAS is a simple additive score based on the presence of inflammatory signs and symptoms: the original 7-item CAS (pain, redness, swelling, chemosis, caruncle inflammation, eyelid oedema) is used at baseline, whilst a 10-item version incorporates three additional progression items (increase in proptosis, decrease in eye movements or loss of visual acuity) at follow-up. A CAS of ≥3/7 at the first visit or ≥4/10 at follow-up visits defines active TED in current EUGOGO and ATA/ETA recommendations.2 A limitation is that CAS is built entirely from binary items (each sign scored 0/1), so partial improvement in a given feature does not change the score, and patients are ultimately categorized only as ‘active’ or ‘inactive’, making CAS relatively insensitive for capturing graded changes in activity over time.

Quality of life (QoL) should be assessed at each visit using validated disease-specific instruments such as the Graves’ orbitopathy-QoL (GO-QoL) or the TED-QoL questionnaires.2,29

Orbital imaging is not routinely required for diagnosis but is indicated in atypical cases, such as euthyroid patients, unilateral disease or disproportionate orbital pain, or when DON is suspected.2 The differential diagnosis includes orbital cellulitis, myasthenia gravis, neoplasms, infiltrative disorders, carotid-cavernous fistula and idiopathic orbital inflammation. Both computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrate the hallmark features of TED: enlargement of extraocular muscles with tendon sparing and expansion of orbital fat (Figures 4 and 5). CT is more cost-effective and better delineates bony anatomy, whereas MRI, particularly with short Tau inversion recovery and diffusion-weighted sequences, can help distinguish inflammatory from fibrotic changes.8 Imaging findings suggestive of DON include apical crowding, fat prolapse through the superior orbital fissure, superior ophthalmic vein dilation and marked extraocular muscle enlargement; for the latter, several quantification indices exist.20

Treatment

General measures

Management of TED begins with aggressive modification of risk factors. Smoking cessation is paramount, and patients should be strongly encouraged to enrol in structured cessation programmes and to avoid passive smoke exposure.2 Restoration and maintenance of stable euthyroidism are equally critical because both hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism adversely affect the course of TED.30 Dyslipidaemia should also be appropriately treated. Mild cases may be managed in non-specialist settings. However, moderate-to-severe or sight-threatening disease should ideally be treated in specialized centres by multidisciplinary teams.2

Local supportive therapy

Local measures are required in nearly all patients, tailored to the type and severity of ocular involvement. Dry eye symptoms are managed with liberal use of artificial tears and lubricating drops, gels or ointments at night. To reduce corneal exposure, eyelid taping or protective eye shields may be used. In cases of epithelial breakdown, intensive lubrication combined with topical antibiotics and ocular protection is indicated. If conservative measures fail, temporary tarsorrhaphy may be performed. Elevating the head during sleep can reduce periorbital oedema. In patients with lid retraction who have contraindications, intolerance and/or complications related to systemic glucocorticoids or other immunotherapies, periocular triamcinolone injection is an effective alternative treatment, especially in active disease of recent (<6 months) onset.31,32 Diplopia can be alleviated with prisms or occlusion of the most affected eye, whilst botulinum toxin injections into affected extraocular muscles may provide temporary relief. Botox into the levator or Müller’s muscle may also improve upper eyelid retraction.2

Management of hyperthyroidism

Optimal control of GD hyperthyroidism is essential. First-line treatment options include antithyroid drugs (ATDs), total thyroidectomy and RAI. ATDs are the most widely used initial therapy worldwide33 and generally do not significantly alter the natural course of TED; most patients remain stable or improve, with worsening occurring in fewer than 5%.34 Improvement may relate to declining TRAb levels and restoration of euthyroidism.

RAI therapy, in contrast, is associated with a 10–30% risk of new-onset or worsening TED, with meta-analyses showing higher relative risk compared to ATDs (OR 1.75).35,36 The risk is greatest in smokers (OR 5.2) and in patients with high TRAb levels, uncontrolled hyperthyroidism or pre-existing TED.37 The proposed mechanism involves thyroid antigen release and sustained TRAb elevation following thyroid tissue destruction.38 RAI is contraindicated in active moderate-to-severe or sight-threatening TED. In patients without TED or with mild/inactive disease, RAI may be administered with glucocorticoid prophylaxis in high-risk individuals. Recommended prophylaxis consists of oral prednisone 0.3–0.5 mg/kg daily for 1 month, followed by gradual tapering over two additional months, whilst regimens of lower dose/duration have been proposed.39

Thyroidectomy does not appear to worsen TED and may even be beneficial. In patients with GD without TED, treatment with thyroidectomy or ATDs is associated with similar rates of TED development, which are significantly lower compared to RAI treatment.34 In pre-existing TED, thyroidectomy performs equally or better than ATDs with respect to TED evolution.34 In a recent meta-analysis of 55,000 patients, thyroidectomy was associated with lower risk of TED progression compared to RAI (HR 0.25, 95% CI 0.16–0.41) and to ATDs, although the latter did not reach statistical significance (HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.22–1.54).36 Total thyroid ablation (thyroidectomy followed by RAI) has shown promising results in small randomized studies of active TED treated with IV glucocorticoids, with faster improvement and no TED progression during follow-up compared with thyroidectomy alone. However, long-term outcomes appear similar, and current evidence is considered insufficient to support routine use. This strategy may be considered only in highly selected patients already scheduled for surgery and glucocorticoid therapy in experienced centres.8

Pharmacological management

Therapeutic decision-making in TED is primarily determined by disease activity and severity, and secondarily by QoL impairment, disease duration, risk of progression, patient age and comorbidities.2 In general, active disease warrants immunosuppressive therapy, whereas inactive disease is more often managed with rehabilitative surgical interventions (Figure 6).

Mild TED

In mild TED, the only approved pharmacological intervention is selenium supplementation. Selenium is incorporated into antioxidant and immunomodulatory selenoproteins such as glutathione peroxidase.40 Clinical studies in selenium-deficient European populations have demonstrated that 6 months of selenium administration improves CAS and QoL, whilst reducing the risk of disease progression compared with placebo, supporting its use primarily in patients with mild, early TED in such regions.40 In contrast, a randomized study from selenium-sufficient South Korea did not show clinically meaningful improvement in GO-QoL or disease activity with selenium supplementation, suggesting that baseline selenium status is a key determinant of therapeutic efficacy and that routine use in selenium-replete populations remains controversial.41 However, its efficacy has not been established in moderate-to-severe disease, and selenium is not considered sufficient as monotherapy in these settings. The recommended dose is 200 mcg daily for 6 months.2 Potential adverse effects of selenium – reported inconsistently across studies – include a possible increased risk of type 2 DM, prostate cancer and cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, whilst clinically significant toxicity (with gastrointestinal symptoms, alopecia and dermatitis) is unlikely with the 6-month regimen used in TED and is mainly associated with higher doses and prolonged exposure.40

Importantly, mild TED may be managed as moderate-to-severe disease if QoL impairment is substantial. In such cases, systemic glucocorticoids may be considered. Earlier guidelines by EUGOGO favoured intravenous glucocorticoid (IVGC) administration,39 whereas more recent recommendations accept oral glucocorticoids (OGC) for mild disease with symptomatic soft tissue inflammation, although their efficacy and tolerability are inferior to those of IVGC.2,39 A commonly used regimen in moderate-to-severe TED consists of initiating prednisone at 100 mg daily or 1 mg/kg, followed by gradual tapering (5–10 mg per week) over 4–6 months, with a cumulative dose of approximately 4 g; lower doses (e.g. starting with prednisone 0.3–0.5 mg/kg with tapering over 2–3 months) are typically used if OGC are chosen in the context of mild TED.39 In patients with very mild symptoms, watchful waiting remains an acceptable strategy.2

Moderate-to-severe TED

Glucocorticoids

All major guidelines recommend glucocorticoids as first-line therapy for the management of active, moderate-to-severe TED.2,39,42 Glucocorticoids act primarily by suppressing cytokine production and inhibiting the activity of neutrophils, macrophages and lymphocytes (Figure 1B). Their use in TED spans more than six decades, providing extensive clinical experience, and they remain a relatively low-cost therapeutic option. Intravenous administration is preferred due to superior efficacy compared with oral regimens. The most widely adopted protocol consists of intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 500 mg weekly for 6 weeks, followed by 250 mg weekly for an additional 6 weeks, yielding a cumulative dose of 4.5 g.2 Lower cumulative doses have been evaluated but are associated with reduced efficacy, whereas higher doses confer no substantial additional benefit and are linked to a significantly increased risk of adverse events. Importantly, the cumulative dose of methylprednisolone should not exceed 8 g, given the substantial risk of severe hepatotoxicity.2

Glucocorticoids are effective in reducing disease activity. With IVGC, about three out of four patients experience a reduction in CAS of at least 3 points, and disease inactivation (CAS <3) is achieved in approximately 70% of patients (with overall response rates across series typically 60–80%), whereas OGC achieve lower response rates of around 50%. Failure to inactivate the disease occurs in 20–40% of patients treated intravenously and in approximately half of those receiving OGC.2 Relapse following an initial response has been reported in up to 30% of patients treated with IVGC.43 With respect to proptosis, glucocorticoids provide minimal benefit. Some studies report a modest reduction of approximately 1–2 mm; however, most demonstrate no clinically meaningful effect.2

Data regarding diplopia are conflicting. Individual studies have reported outcomes ranging from marginal improvement to more than 50% reduction in primary gaze diplopia.2,8 These discrepancies are reflected in meta-analyses: Zang et al. reported improvement or resolution of diplopia in 63.8% of patients in randomized studies and 59.3% in non-randomized studies following IVGC,44 while Douglas et al. found no statistically significant advantage of IVGC over placebo in diplopia response (OR 2.69, 95% CI 0.94–7.70).45 Higher cumulative doses (e.g. methylprednisolone 750 mg weekly for 6 weeks followed by 500 mg weekly for an additional 6 weeks) may be more likely to exert a favourable effect on diplopia.43 Improvements in QoL have been reported in 50–70% of treated patients.2

Very frequent (>10%) adverse effects of glucocorticoids include hyperglycaemia, gastrointestinal disturbances, weight gain and cushingoid features (the latter two primarily with OGC). Frequent (1–10%) complications include hypertension, elevated intraocular pressure, insomnia, depression, psychosis and infections. Rare (<1%) complications include severe hepatic injury, myocardial infarction, stroke and death. Prior to treatment initiation, patients should be screened for viral hepatitis, latent tuberculosis (TB), DM and arterial hypertension. During therapy, glucose levels, blood pressure and liver function tests should be monitored and prophylactic measures, such as proton pump inhibitors or osteoporosis prevention, should be considered when appropriate.2

Although concern has been raised that this supraphysiologic, weekly-pulse regimen could suppress the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, the largest cohort to date evaluating adrenal responsiveness by high-dose ACTH stimulation testing 7–10 days after completion of the standard 12-dose protocol found preserved adrenal function in all patients, suggesting that clinically significant early post-treatment adrenal insufficiency is uncommon and that routine biochemical screening is not required in asymptomatic patients.46

Lack of clinical improvement or disease worsening 6–8 weeks after initiation of IVGC strongly predicts treatment failure and should prompt consideration of alternative interventions such as monoclonal antibody therapy or orbital radiotherapy. In cases of partial response following completion of the intravenous regimen, management options include observation (in stable TED), transition to alternative therapy or administration of additional glucocorticoid infusions, provided that the cumulative dose does not exceed 8 g of methylprednisolone.2 In contemporary practice, IVGC therefore remains the backbone of first-line therapy for active moderate-to-severe TED, with biologic or other adjunctive treatments considered for patients with inadequate response, intolerance or specific disease phenotypes.

Mycophenolate

Mycophenolate inhibits inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase, a key enzyme in de novo purine synthesis, thereby suppressing B and T lymphocyte proliferation (Figure 1B). It has been used in TED for over a decade as a first-line therapy instead of or, more frequently in combination with, glucocorticoids and its cost is relatively low. It is administered orally for 24 weeks, either as mycophenolate mofetil 500 mg twice daily (BID) or mycophenolate sodium 360 mg BID.2

The only head-to-head trial comparing mycophenolate monotherapy with glucocorticoids, which suggested superiority of mycophenolate in improving inflammation, proptosis and diplopia, was subsequently retracted.47 Combination therapy with IVGC versus IVGC alone has been evaluated in two randomized controlled trials (RCTs). In the first RCT, no significant difference was observed in the primary outcome; however, post hoc analysis showed superiority of the combination in terms of the EUGOGO composite index.48 In the second RCT, combination therapy was superior to IVGC monotherapy in reducing CAS, decreasing proptosis and improving QoL.49 Earlier EUGOGO guidelines endorsed IVGC–mycophenolate combination therapy as first-line treatment for moderate-to-severe TED,42 whereas more recent ATA and ETA guidelines adopt a more cautious position and list mycophenolate as an optional component rather than a universal first-line requirement.2 Mycophenolate has also been combined with OGC, demonstrating favourable outcomes in one study, with disease inactivation in 92.5% of patients and improvement in proptosis (83%) and diplopia (94%) at 12 months.50

At the doses used in TED, it is generally well tolerated. Adverse events are uncommon and usually mild, including hypokalaemia, transient transaminase elevation, and infections. Importantly, RCTs did not show increased adverse events when mycophenolate was added to IVGC. Its use should be avoided in chronic infections and applied cautiously to patients with gastrointestinal, hepatic or chronic renal disease.2

Rituximab

Rituximab is a monoclonal antibody targeting the CD20 receptor on B lymphocytes, resulting in B cell depletion (Figure 1B). It has been used in TED as a second-line treatment for approximately 15 years; however, its use remains off-label and relatively costly. It is administered intravenously, typically as a single 500 mg dose or two 1000 mg infusions given 2 weeks apart,2 although more recent open-label series suggest that lower doses (e.g. 100 mg) may still reduce disease activity in selected patients.51,52 Data on optimal dosing are heterogeneous, and very low-dose regimens are as effective as conventional doses remains uncertain. A recent study compared the efficacy and safety of three dosing regimens (single dose of 100 mg, single dose of 500 mg and two 1000 mg doses) and suggested that a single 500 mg dose should be the mainstay of treatment.53

Two RCTs have reported conflicting results regarding its efficacy; the two trials differed regarding the baseline patient characteristics and the control group used. In one trial enrolling patients with recent-onset, highly active TED, rituximab was compared to IVGC and outperformed it, achieving a mean CAS reduction of 3.8 points and disease inactivation in 100% of patients, without relapses;54 these findings were supported by observational studies.55,56 In contrast, a second RCT including patients with longer disease duration and lower baseline activity showed no significant benefit over placebo.57 These findings suggest greater efficacy in early, active TED. Rituximab has minimal impact on proptosis and diplopia but improves QoL in 62–77% of patients. Its principal indication appears to be induction of inactivation in recent-onset (<9 months), moderate-to-severe TED with prominent soft tissue involvement, particularly when IVGC are ineffective or contraindicated.2

Adverse events occur in up to 75% of patients. Mild reactions include infusion-related symptoms (fever, pruritus and nasal congestion), gastrointestinal complaints and hypotension, whilst severe events include cytokine release during infusion (manifesting as orbital oedema and/or transient blindness), vasculitis, anaphylaxis and arrhythmias. Premedication with paracetamol, antihistamines and glucocorticoids is recommended before each dose. Treatment should be avoided in active or chronic infections (TB and hepatitis) and unstable cardiovascular disease, and appropriate screening is required prior to initiation.2

Tocilizumab

Tocilizumab is a monoclonal antibody targeting the pro-inflammatory IL-6 receptor (Figure 1B). It has been used in TED as a second-line treatment for approximately a decade; treatment is off-label, and its cost is relatively high. The standard regimen is intravenous tocilizumab 8 mg/kg (maximum 800 mg) every 4 weeks for four infusions, although longer courses have been administered in some studies.2 Subcutaneous administration has also been explored in small series at 162 mg weekly for 1–6 months, with encouraging results.58,59 No head-to-head trials have compared intravenous to subcutaneous regimens, and their equivalence is therefore unknown.

The effect of tocilizumab on TED activity is consistently favourable. Across most studies, a decrease in CAS of >3 points has been observed, and disease inactivation has been achieved in 75–100% of treated patients (approximately 90% in a meta-analysis).60 Reported relapse rates after treatment are low, around 7–8%.61 In contrast, outcomes for proptosis are less consistent. Non-randomized studies generally suggest improvement in proptosis, and two meta-analyses reported statistically significant mean reductions of 1.87 mm and 2.05 mm versus baseline.60,62 However, the only RCT demonstrated an initial 1.5 mm advantage over placebo that was not sustained over time.63 Evidence for diplopia is similarly mixed: non-randomized studies have reported improvement in more than half of patients, and one meta-analysis estimated a 56% improvement rate.60 Conversely, the RCT found diplopia improvement in only 7% of patients,63 whilst another meta-analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant change from baseline.62 QoL, however, appears to improve consistently with treatment. A recent study assessed prolonged therapy over 1 year, delivered either intravenously every 4 weeks or subcutaneously weekly for 6 months followed by dosing every 2 weeks.64 Disease inactivation was achieved in 58% of patients at 6 months and 92% at 12 months with relapse in 8%. Treatment improved proptosis (89% of participants had proptosis at baseline and only 50% at 18 months) and diplopia (32% at 18 months versus 88% at baseline). Improvements in proptosis and diplopia became statistically significant after 6 months, suggesting that these domains may require longer exposure than the conventional 4-month regimen. Taken together, these data support the use of tocilizumab primarily as a second-line option in active moderate-to-severe TED with prominent inflammatory activity and inadequate response or contraindications to IVGC.

Adverse events occur in approximately 30–50% of patients and most commonly include fatigue, hyperlipidaemia, neutropenia, mild infections and elevated liver enzymes. Less frequent but serious events include thrombocytopenia, severe infections, marked hepatotoxicity and anaphylaxis. Tocilizumab should be avoided in active infection, chronic infections (TB and hepatitis) and significant liver disease. Baseline screening, apart from screening for hepatitis and latent TB, should include complete blood count, lipid profile and liver function tests, with continued monitoring during therapy.2

Teprotumumab

Teprotumumab is a monoclonal antibody directed against IGF1R (Figure 1B), and it is the most recently introduced agent for TED. It was initially approved by the FDA in 2020 for the treatment of active moderate-to-severe TED, representing the first disease-specific therapy for this condition. In Europe, its approval was considerably delayed; however, following a positive opinion by the EMA in 2025, it subsequently received marketing authorization by the European Commission, allowing its use across European Union member states (https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/medicines/human/EPAR/tepezza). Despite this regulatory approval, access to teprotumumab in Europe remains variable and is largely dependent on national reimbursement policies and cost considerations, which may limit its widespread clinical implementation compared with the USA. The cost of teprotumumab is exceptionally high, approximately 20-fold greater than that of other monoclonal antibodies used in TED, limiting accessibility in many settings. Despite its high cost and potential adverse effects, current ATA and ETA consensus considers teprotumumab a preferred first-line option for many patients with active, moderate-to-severe TED particularly when proptosis and/or diplopia predominate,2 whereas EUGOGO guidelines continue to recommend IVGC (with mycophenolate) as the standard first-line therapy, reserving biologics for second-line use or selected cases.42 Teprotumumab is administered intravenously every 3 weeks for a total of eight infusions (initial dose 10 mg/kg, followed by 20 mg/kg for the remaining seven doses).2

Teprotumumab achieves disease inactivation (CAS 0–1) in 60–70% of patients.65 It is the most effective pharmacological therapy currently available for proptosis and diplopia. Mean proptosis reduction ranges from 2.68 to 3.50 mm, with 77–90% of patients achieving a ≥2 mm reduction. Improvement in diplopia of at least one grade on the Gorman scale occurs in 65–68% of patients.65–68 Notably, patients with TED refractory to other therapies also demonstrate substantial benefit, with CAS reduction ≥2 in 93.8%, proptosis reduction ≥2 mm in 85.9% and diplopia improvement ≥1 grade in 69.1%.69 QoL improves significantly with treatment. Therapeutic effects appear durable in most patients, persisting for 1–1.5 years after completion of therapy. In follow-up studies, inactive disease was maintained in approximately 70% of patients, proptosis reduction ≥2 mm in 67–68% and diplopia improvement ≥1 grade in 69–73%.65 However, some reports indicate relapse rates of 59–66%, primarily driven by recurrence of proptosis.67,70

Emerging evidence suggests that teprotumumab may also benefit patients with long-standing, inactive TED. In a study of 62 patients with inactive disease of 2–10 years’ duration and baseline proptosis ≥3 mm, mean proptosis reduction was 2.41 mm with teprotumumab versus 0.92 mm with placebo.71 A ≥2 mm reduction was achieved in 62% versus 25%, respectively. No significant difference in diplopia improvement was observed, although few patients had diplopia at baseline.

Adverse events are common, occurring in approximately 80% of patients, with 4–12% discontinuing therapy.2 Most events are mild to moderate. Frequent adverse effects include muscle spasms (25–60%), gastrointestinal symptoms (nausea, dysgeusia; 15–40%), skin changes (dryness, alopecia; ~40%) and hyperglycaemia (10–30%), which is usually mild but may require insulin in patients with diabetes.72,73 Of particular concern are otological adverse events, affecting up to 80% of patients and reflecting the role of IGF1 signalling in the inner ear. Early trials reported sensorineural hearing loss in 10–15% of patients. A dedicated safety study identified otological symptoms in 31% (hearing loss, tinnitus, autophony, ear fullness, pain), with 40–50% persisting 1–1.5 years post-treatment.74 Another small study reported subjective auditory symptoms in 81.5% of recipients; whilst tinnitus, fullness and autophony largely resolved, hearing loss persisted in approximately 55%.75 Baseline and interval audiometric evaluation is therefore recommended by some authors.73

Other pharmaceutical options

Several additional agents have been investigated in TED, although the supporting evidence remains limited and none are recommended as first-line therapies in current guidelines.

Azathioprine has not demonstrated superiority over placebo as monotherapy in TED. However, its addition to glucocorticoids or orbital radiotherapy may enhance therapeutic efficacy. Its use is limited by potentially serious adverse effects, including bone marrow suppression and increased susceptibility to infections.76–78 Cyclosporine similarly does not appear superior to OGC when used alone, but combination therapy with glucocorticoids has shown greater efficacy than either agent as monotherapy.79,80 Nephrotoxicity and hypertension are its principal adverse effects. Methotrexate has been evaluated in small prospective studies and appears to reduce inflammation and CAS in patients who are refractory to or intolerant of glucocorticoids, or who relapse during tapering of OGC.81 Two small studies from a single centre reported that oral sirolimus, an mTOR pathway inhibitor, administered for 12 weeks was superior to IVGC in reducing CAS and proptosis at 24 weeks, although this difference was not maintained at 48 weeks.82,83 Given its antifibrotic mechanism of action, sirolimus may prove particularly useful in patients with TED-associated restrictive strabismus.84

Statins lower cholesterol levels and exert pleiotropic effects. Evidence suggests that adding statins to IVGC in patients with hypercholesterolaemia and TED enhances therapeutic outcomes by reducing inflammation and fibrosis. This is supported by the phase II randomized controlled STAGO trial, which demonstrated improved response rates with the combination of IVGC and atorvastatin compared to IVGC alone.85 Retrospective studies further associate statin use with a lower incidence of TED in newly diagnosed GD and a reduced need for rehabilitative orbital surgery in severe cases.86 Collectively, these findings support considering statins, particularly atorvastatin, as an adjunct to IVGC in patients with hypercholesterolaemia and moderate-to-severe active TED. However, current evidence is insufficient to recommend routine use irrespective of lipid status or in mild disease. Whether these benefits represent a class effect or are specific to certain agents remains uncertain, and no randomized data support statin therapy as a primary treatment for TED.

Other agents, including somatostatin analogues, TNF inhibitors and intravenous immunoglobulin, have shown limited efficacy and/or an unfavourable safety profile.

Agents in development

Beyond currently available treatments, multiple novel therapies targeting distinct pathophysiological pathways in TED are under clinical investigation (Figure 1B).

Several next-generation IGF1R antagonists are in phase I–III trials. Veligrotug has published phase III data demonstrating promising results in disease inactivation, proptosis and diplopia, even in patients with long-standing disease.87 Other IGF1R-targeting agents are being developed for subcutaneous (e.g. lonigutamab) or oral (e.g. linsitinib) administration, potentially improving convenience and accessibility.88 Monoclonal antibodies targeting the TSHR, such as K1-70 and GenSci098, are currently in phase I studies. Early data for K1-70 suggest reductions in CAS and proptosis in patients with TED.89,90 Small-molecule TSHR antagonists, including SYD5115, ANTAG and S37a, are also under development. Belimumab, a monoclonal antibody against B cell activating factor (BAFF), has shown comparable efficacy to IVGC in achieving disease inactivation but appears less effective in reducing CAS and proptosis.91 Another emerging strategy involves inhibition of the neonatal fragment crystallizable receptor (FcRn), which protects IgG from degradation. Subcutaneously administered monoclonal antibodies targeting FcRn, such as batoclimab and efgartigimod, are currently in phase II–III trials. Batoclimab has demonstrated significant reductions in TRAb in patients with TED.92 Additional investigational agents include inflammasome inhibitors (e.g. kamuvudine-9), IL-6 receptor inhibitors, such as satralizumab and TOUR006, and IL-17A receptor inhibitors (e.g. secukinumab), reflecting the expanding therapeutic landscape in TED.

Radiotherapy

Orbital radiotherapy was more widely used in the past, prior to the advent of monoclonal antibody therapies. It acts by reducing the number of and/or inhibiting lymphocytes and orbital fibrocytes. The standard regimen consists of 20 Gy administered in 10 fractions, although alternative fractionation schemes have shown comparable efficacy. Potential risks include retinal damage (requiring particular caution in patients with DM) and a theoretical increased risk of secondary malignancies within the radiation field; consequently, age under 35 years is considered a relative contraindication.2

Radiotherapy has been reported to be effective in up to 60% of patients, although outcomes vary depending on study design and response criteria.93 Two RCTs compared radiotherapy to sham radiation: one demonstrated improvement in diplopia and ocular motility but no significant effect on inflammation or proptosis, whereas the other – conducted in patients with relatively long-standing TED – showed no significant benefit.94,95 Radiotherapy has also been evaluated in combination with glucocorticoids. In conjunction with OGC, two RCTs demonstrated superiority of the combination over glucocorticoids monotherapy in reducing inflammation, improving ocular motility, and decreasing extraocular muscle thickness, whilst a third found no significant difference.96,97 Three retrospective studies have reported on the combination of radiotherapy with IVGC: one showed improved ocular motility and absence of DON during follow-up in the combination group,98 whilst the other two demonstrated marginal superiority in ocular motility.99,100 Overall, the principal indication for RT appears to be active TED with significant diplopia and restrictive extraocular muscle involvement, particularly in patients with steroid-refractory disease or when access to or suitability for biologic therapies is limited.

Treatment selection in active moderate-to-severe TED

Given the heterogeneity of clinical manifestations and the differential efficacy of available therapies across disease domains, management of active moderate-to-severe TED must be individualized (Figure 6).2,8 If the primary objective is reduction of inflammation and achievement of disease inactivation, IVGC constitute first-line therapy, with rituximab or tocilizumab considered second-line options in non-responders; teprotumumab and the combination of glucocorticoids with radiotherapy may follow in selected cases. If proptosis reduction is the main goal, teprotumumab is likely the only effective pharmacological option, although prolonged tocilizumab therapy may have a role. When improvement of ocular motility and limitation of diplopia is prioritized, teprotumumab is likely the preferred first-line agent, with IVGC – possibly at higher cumulative doses – alone or in combination with radiotherapy, and tocilizumab representing alternative strategies. Treatment decisions should incorporate drug availability, cost and the potential adverse effects associated with each modality, and should ideally be made through shared decision-making within an experienced multidisciplinary TED team.

Dysthyroid optic neuropathy

Radiological findings are critical in guiding treatment decisions for DON. In cases of apical compression of the optic nerve, response to glucocorticoids is more likely, whereas optic nerve stretching secondary to marked proptosis is typically amenable only to surgical correction. First-line therapy for DON consists of high-dose IVGC, administered as 0.5–1 g methylprednisolone daily for 3 consecutive days or on alternate days, with repetition after 1 week. If no improvement is observed in visual acuity, visual fields and colour vision, urgent orbital decompression is indicated.2 High-dose IVGC are effective in approximately 50% of patients.101,102

Orbital radiotherapy is mainly used as adjunctive therapy to IVGC in active TED, particularly in patients with predominant extraocular muscle involvement; its role as prophylaxis in high-risk patients remains controversial and is based mainly on retrospective data: Two studies have shown superiority over OGC in preventing the development of DON and the need for surgery.98,103 A RCT comparing IVGC alone versus IVGC combined with RT in early, progressive TED (CRISEPTED study) is currently ongoing. Radiotherapy has also been used in patients with established DON: one retrospective study showed that radiotherapy outperformed OGC in relieving compressive optic neuropathy, whilst another reported that combination therapy with OGC avoided surgical decompression in over 90% of patients.103,104 Radiotherapy (with or without concurrent IVGC) is considered an acceptable therapy in DON by current guidelines.2

More recently, teprotumumab and tocilizumab have been evaluated in patients with DON, with encouraging preliminary results. In a case series of 24 patients, teprotumumab led to rapid visual improvement in 87.5%.105 Similarly, a prospective study of 13 patients treated with tocilizumab demonstrated significant improvement in visual acuity, and none required surgical decompression.106 These preliminary data suggest that teprotumumab and tocilizumab may represent potential alternatives or adjuncts in DON,8 but current evidence is limited to small series and they are not yet recommended as standard therapy in guidelines.

Surgical management of DON involves orbital decompression, with removal of portions of the medial and/or inferior orbital walls in case of apical compression, and with excision of orbital fat in case of optic nerve stretching. If TED is active, concurrent medical therapy aimed at inactivation should be administered. Surgical decompression generally results in substantial visual improvement, with complete resolution of DON in 75–90% of cases.107 Prognosis is less favourable when intervention is delayed or in older patients and those with DM.

Surgical rehabilitation in inactive TED

Surgical correction of TED-related sequelae requires stable inactive disease for at least 4–6 months and sustained euthyroidism.2 Procedures are performed in stages: orbital decompression, followed by strabismus or diplopia, and finally eyelid abnormalities (Figure 6). Not all patients require all three interventions. Goals of care and patient expectations must be carefully discussed in advance.

Orbital decompression in inactive TED is indicated for disfiguring proptosis, persistent corneal exposure or symptomatic orbital congestion. In vision-threatening DON it is performed urgently during the active phase as part of sight-saving therapy. Techniques include removal of orbital fat, resection of portions of the medial and/or inferior orbital walls and, in selected cases, displacement of fat or muscle into the paranasal sinuses. Potential complications include cerebrospinal fluid leak, oscillopsia, cheek hypoesthesia and new-onset strabismus.107–110 Strabismus surgery aims to expand or restore the field of binocular single vision. Affected extraocular muscles are recessed by detaching and reattaching them posteriorly on the globe. Tendon lengthening using grafts or sutures may also be performed. Adjustable sutures are commonly employed to allow postoperative fine-tuning once the patient is awake.111–113 Eyelid retraction due to levator and/or Müller muscle dysfunction is managed by recession or myotomy with lengthening, whereas lower eyelid malposition may require elevation with a spacer graft. Excess periocular fat is treated with blepharoplasty.2

Emerging data suggest that teprotumumab may benefit selected patients with long-standing, inactive TED, particularly when proptosis predominates. Current evidence remains limited to a single randomized trial and small series, with modest mean effects and uncertain long-term durability.71,114 At present, anti-IGF1R therapies cannot replace staged surgical rehabilitation in most patients with inactive TED but they may assume a future role in selected cases with chronic TED.

Conclusions

In conclusion, TED represents a complex autoimmune disorder in which disease activity and severity do not always parallel with each other, necessitating a careful and individualized therapeutic approach (Figure 6). Advances in the understanding of its pathophysiology, particularly the role of orbital fibroblasts, TSHR and IGF1R signalling, have led to the development of targeted therapies that have significantly expanded the treatment armamentarium. Although IVGC remain the cornerstone for controlling inflammation, newer biologic agents, such as rituximab, tocilizumab and the latest teprotumumab, offer additional options for phenotype-driven management in carefully selected patients, particularly those with prominent proptosis or refractory disease. Despite these advances, important challenges remain, including treatment accessibility, cost, long-term safety and optimal patient selection. A multidisciplinary, personalized approach that integrates disease activity, severity and predominant clinical features is essential to optimize outcomes, whilst ongoing research is expected to further refine therapeutic strategies and improve the QoL of patients with TED.
