The strength of weak coupling
Alastair Kay, Christino Tamon

Abstract
. A paradoxical idea in quantum transport is that attaching weakly-coupled edges to a large base graph creates high-fidelity quantum state transfer. We provide a mathematical treatment that rigorously prove this folklore idea. Our proofs are elementary and build upon the Feshbach-Schur method from perturbation theory. We also show the idea is effective in circumventing Anderson localization in spin chains and finding speedups in hitting times useful for quantum search.



1.
Introduction

A continuous-time quantum walk on a graph
G
is a time-varying unitary matrix
U(t)=\exp(-itA)

where
A
is the adjacency matrix of
G
.
This framework is useful for developing quantum algorithms (see
[
16
,
17
]
),
which had culminated in a quantum walk algorithm with provable exponential speedup

[
9
]
(a guarantee missing in both Shor factoring and Grover search).

For two vertices
a,b
of
G
with characteristic vectors
e_{a},e_{b}
, let their fidelity function be
f_{a,b}(t)=|\langle e_{b},U(t)e_{a}\rangle|
.
By Born’s rule, the probability of measuring
b
at time
t
given that
the quantum walk starts at
a
is
f_{a,b}(t)^{2}
.
We say
G
has pretty good state transfer (PGST) from
a
to
b
if for any
\epsilon>0

there exists a time
t_{0}
so that
f_{a,b}(t_{0})\geq 1-\epsilon
(see
[
18
,
36
]
);
it is called perfect state transfer (PST) if
\epsilon=0
is realizable.
Quantum state transfer is fundamental to quantum communication as it can be used as a building block
for protocols such as entanglement generation and state preparation (see
[
11
,
12
,
1
,
21
,
23
]
).

Although PST/PGST are useful abstractions, there are several important obstacles in developing
state transfer in practice. Arguably, PST is impossible to realize in real-world applications.
Recently, due to experimental challenges in realistic scenarios,
a stronger emphasis has been placed on
high-fidelity
state transfer (HFST) (see
[
8
,
29
,
30
,
37
,
14
]
).
Here, for a small but fixed
\epsilon_{0}>0
, we ask for an efficient time
t_{0}
for which
f_{a,b}(t_{0})\geq 1-\epsilon_{0}
.
This differs markedly from PGST as
\epsilon_{0}
is not required to be arbitrarily close to
0

and time
t_{0}
must be a polynomial function in the relevant parameters (such as graph size or diameter or transfer distance).
Here, the emphasis is on transfer time which is neither nondeterministic nor astronomical.

We explore a paradoxical idea (called T.
rex
) which attaches weakly-coupled pendant edges (or tiny arms) to a base graph (large body).
This idea was studied by Wójcik
et al.
(see
[
38
,
39
]
).
Here, we describe a mathematical treatment of this idea using the Feshbach-Schur method in perturbation theory (see
[
22
,
19
]
).
Within this framework, we rigorously prove that high-fidelity quantum state transfer occurs.
The advantage of using the Feshbach-Schur method is that it is elementary
and it insulates asymptotic arguments (the bane of perturbation theory).
We also prove that the transfer time of the T.
rex
scheme does not depend on the diameter.
In contrast, methods based on strong potentials exhibit exponential scaling with the diameter (see
[
29
,
30
]
).

Another formidable obstacle for state transfer is Anderson localization (see
[
4
,
26
,
33
]
).
By definition, localization is the absence of transport across a spin chain due to random diagonal perturbations.
We show how the T.
rex
idea overcomes this obstacle by constructing a robust state transfer protocol which has high fidelity.
Our protocol exploits nonsingularities of the underlying spin chains in a direct way along with the use of a few clean qubits.

We also apply the T.
rex
idea to hitting times on graphs with consequences to quantum search.
For the latter, we reimagine the vertex-located oracle (see
[
16
]
) as a pendant edge oracle.
The search algorithm simply “pitches” a random pendant edge onto the graph and apply the T.
rex
walk.
This reduces quantum search, viewed as reverse mixing, to a quantum hitting process (which is a standard
technique in Markov chain theory
[
28
]
).

As concluding remarks, the T.
rex
idea had already found two unexpected applications elsewhere:
a simple matrix inversion quantum algorithm
[
25
]

and an optimal
perfect
state transfer protocol robust against timing sensitivities
[
24
]
.
We anticipate further uses of the T.
rex
idea which we leave to future treks.

2.
Preliminaries

We review notation and terminology used in this work.
Let
\Mat_{n}(\mathbb{R})
be the set of all
n\times n
real matrices.
We use
\mathbb{1}_{n}
and
J_{n}
to denote the identity and all-one matrices of order
n
, respectively;
we omit the subscript, whenever the dimensions are clear from context.
The spectrum of
A\in\Mat_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is denoted
\sigma(A)
. In most cases, we will restrict ourselves to symmetric matrices, whose spectrum exists and whose eigenvalues are real.

For an invertible matrix
A\in\Mat_{n}(\mathbb{R})

with
\lambda_{\max}(A)=\max\{|\lambda|:\lambda\in\sigma(A)\}

and
\lambda_{\min}(A)=\min\{|\lambda|:\lambda\in\sigma(A)\}
,
the
condition number
of
A
is defined as

\kappa(A)=\norm{A}\norm{A^{-1}}=\frac{\lambda_{\max}(A)}{\lambda_{\min}(A)}

where
\norm{A}
is the spectral norm of
A
(see
[
20
]
, page 385).
We shall assume
\norm{A}
is bounded.

For a matrix
A\in\Mat_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, the
resolvent
of
A
is given by

(1)

R(\zeta,A)=(\zeta\mathbb{1}-A)^{-1}.

Its resolvent set
\rho(A)
is the set of all values
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
for which
\zeta\mathbb{1}-A
is invertible (and bounded);
note that
\sigma(A)=\mathbb{C}\setminus\rho(A)
.
We state two useful identities involving the resolvent.

Lemma 2.1
.

(Teschl
[
35
]
)
For matrices
A,B\in\Mat_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and for
\zeta,\mu\in\mathbb{C}
, we have

(2)

\displaystyle R(\zeta,A)-R(\mu,A)

\displaystyle=(\mu-\zeta)R(\zeta,A)R(\mu,A),\ \ \mbox{ $\zeta,\mu\in\rho(A)$}

(3)

\displaystyle R(\zeta,A)-R(\zeta,B)

\displaystyle=R(\zeta,A)(A-B)R(\zeta,B),\ \ \mbox{ $\zeta\in\rho(A)\cap\rho(B)$.}

For
z\in\mathbb{C}
and a subset
S\subset\mathbb{C}
, the distance of
z
to
S
is defined as

d(z,S)=\inf\{|z-a|:a\in S\}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For a matrix
A\in\Mat_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and a scalar
\zeta\not\in\sigma(A)
, we have

\norm{R(\zeta,A)}=1/d(\zeta,\sigma(A))
.

Proof.

Note
\norm{(\zeta\identity- A)^{-1}}=\sup\{|\zeta-\lambda|^{-1}:\lambda\in\sigma(A)\}
.
The latter equals to
1/\inf\{|\zeta-\lambda|:\lambda\in\sigma(A)\}
, proving the claim.
∎

For simplicity, we sometimes identify a projection operator
P
with its range
\ran(P)
.

3.
The Feshbach-Schur method

Let
H\in\Mat_{n}(\mathbb{R})
be a symmetric matrix and let
P_{0},P_{1}
be a pair of orthogonal projections
satisfying
P_{0}+P_{1}=I
.
Clearly,
H
admits a block decomposition given by

H=(P_{0}+P_{1})H(P_{0}+P_{1})=P_{0}HP_{0}+P_{0}HP_{1}+P_{1}HP_{0}+P_{1}HP_{1}.

Notation
: Let
H_{j,k}=P_{j}HP_{k}
, for
j,k=0,1
, denote the projected operators (acting on the full space). On the other hand, we let
H_{P_{j}}
denote

P_{j}HP_{j}
when restricted to
\ran(P_{j})
.

To motivate the ensuing discussion, suppose we wish to solve the eigenvalue-eigenvector equation given by

(4)

\begin{pmatrix}A_{0}&A_{1}\\
A_{2}&A_{3}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{0}\\
\psi_{1}\end{pmatrix}=\lambda\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{0}\\
\psi_{1}\end{pmatrix}.

This implies

\displaystyle A_{0}\psi_{0}+A_{1}\psi_{1}

\displaystyle=\lambda\psi_{0}

\displaystyle A_{2}\psi_{0}+A_{3}\psi_{1}

\displaystyle=\lambda\psi_{1}.

The second equation yields

(5)

\displaystyle\psi_{1}

\displaystyle=(\lambda\mathbb{1}-A_{3})^{-1}A_{2}\psi_{0}

which we can substitute into the first to obtain

\left(A_{0}+A_{1}(\lambda\mathbb{1}-A_{3})^{-1}A_{2}\right)\psi_{0}=\lambda\psi_{0}.

Notice this is related to Schur complementation.

Based on these ideas, if we let

(6)

F(\lambda)=[H+HP_{1}(\lambda\mathbb{1}-H_{P_{1}})^{-1}P_{1}H]_{P_{0}},

we may reduce the eigenvalue-eigenvector problem

H\psi=\lambda\psi
to a fixed-point equation

(7)

F(\lambda)\psi=\lambda\psi

provided
\lambda\mathbb{1}-H_{P_{1}}
is invertible in
\ran(P_{1})
.
Solving for
\lambda
and
\psi
in
Eq.

7
,
the resulting eigenvector of
H
is

\Psi=Q(\lambda)\psi

where

Q(\lambda)=P_{0}+P_{1}(\lambda\mathbb{1}-H_{P_{1}})^{-1}P_{1}HP_{0}.

From the notation, it will be clear from context whether the operator acts
on the full or projected spaces.

This reduction of the eigenvalue problem of
H
in
Eq.

4

to a nonlinear problem involving
F(\lambda)
in
Eq.

7
is known as the
Feshbach-Schur
method
(see
[
15
,
19
,
34
]
).

3.1.
The T.
rex
Cometh

We focus on
linear
perturbation theory (see
[
22
]
, page 72) where

H=H_{0}+\delta W,\ \hskip 21.68121pt\ \mbox{ where $\delta\kappa\ll 1$.}

Here
\kappa
will be the condition number of the relevant base graph (see below).
Let
\lambda_{0}
be an eigenvalue of
H_{0}
of multiplicity
d
, for
d\geq 2
.
Let
P_{0}
be the projection onto
N_{0}=\nul(\lambda_{0}\mathbb{1}-H_{0})

and
P_{1}=\mathbb{1}-P_{0}
be the projection onto the orthogonal complement
N_{0}^{\perp}
.

The map
F(\lambda)
of
H
relative to
P_{0}
is defined as

(8)

F(\lambda):=\lambda_{0}\mathbb{1}+\delta W_{P_{0}}+\delta^{2}W_{01}(\lambda\mathbb{1}-H_{P_{1}})^{-1}W_{10},

under the assumption that
\lambda\mathbb{1}-H_{P_{1}}
is invertible in
\ran(P_{1})
.

Theorem 3.1
.

(Gustafson-Sigal
[
19
]
and Dusson et al.
[
15
]
)

The map
F(\lambda)
satisfies

(9)

H\Psi=\lambda\Psi\ \mbox{ if and only if }\ F(\lambda)\psi=\lambda\psi,\ \hskip 18.06749pt\ \mbox{ for $\psi\in\ran(P_{0})$}

where vectors
\psi
and
\Psi
are related through

(10)

\Psi=Q(\lambda)\psi\ \ \mbox{ and }\ \ \psi=P_{0}\Psi

with

(11)

Q(\lambda)=P_{0}+\delta P_{1}(\lambda\mathbb{1}-H_{P_{1}})^{-1}W_{10}.

Thus,
\Psi=\psi+\psi^{\perp}
, for orthogonal
\psi
and
\psi^{\perp}
,
where

(12)

\psi^{\perp}=\delta P_{1}(\lambda\mathbb{1}-H_{P_{1}})^{-1}P_{1}W\psi

Suppose
\zeta_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\psi_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
are fixed-point solutions of
Eq.

9
:

F(\zeta_{k})\psi_{k}=\zeta_{k}\psi_{k},\ \hskip 14.45377pt\ \mbox{ for $k=1,\ldots,d$.}

Assumption A
: The scalars
\zeta_{k}
are
distinct
.
In the parlance of perturbation theory, the degeneracy of
\lambda_{0}
is
fully resolved
.

By
Theorem

3.1
, each
\zeta_{k}
is an eigenvalue of
H
with eigenvector

\Psi_{k}=\psi_{k}+\psi_{k}^{\perp},\ \ \ \mbox{ where $\psi_{k}^{\perp}=\delta P_{1}R(\zeta_{k},(H_{11})_{P_{1}})P_{1}W\psi_{k}$.}

Lemma 3.2
.

(Gustafson and Sigal
[
19
]
, page 138)

Let
\lambda
be a fixed-point solution of
F(\lambda)
. Then,

\lambda=\lambda_{0}+\delta\mu(\delta)+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{3}),

where
\mu(\delta)\in\sigma(M)
with

M=W_{P_{0}}+\delta[W_{01}R(\lambda_{0},(H_{11})_{P_{1}})W_{10}]_{P_{0}}.

Lemma

3.2
can be applied to a more general setting than what is considered here
(see
[
19
]
for further details).

Assumption B
:
We make the following additional assumptions:

(i)

W
acts trivially in the
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
subspaces
.

That is,
W_{00}=W_{11}=0
. In effect,
W
connects the subspaces
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
.

(ii)

\lambda_{0}=0
.

If not, we may always take the diagonal shift
H_{0}-\lambda_{0}I
.

(iii)

(H_{0})_{P_{1}}
is (sufficiently) nonsingular or has good condition number
.

This implies that for a (small enough)
\kappa
, we have

(13)

d(0,\sigma((H_{0})_{P_{1}}))\geq 1/\kappa.

But, in some cases, we can remove this assumption (see
Section

5.1
).

Using the third assumption, by
Lemma

2.2
,
we have

\norm{R(0, (H_0)_{P_1})}\leq\kappa.

From
Lemma

3.2
, we have
\mu(\delta)\leq\delta\kappa
which implies

(14)

|\lambda|=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}\kappa).

Furthermore,

(15)

\displaystyle d(\lambda,\sigma((H_{0})_{P_{1}}))

\displaystyle\geq d(0,\sigma((H_{0})_{P_{1}}))-|\lambda|

(16)

\displaystyle\geq 1/\kappa-\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}\kappa).

As
\delta\kappa\ll 1
, we see that

(17)

d(\lambda,\sigma((H_{0})_{P_{1}}))=\Omega(1/\kappa).

3.2.
Effective Hamiltonian

In what follows, let
\varepsilon:=\delta\kappa
.
To track our quantum walk within the subspace
P_{0}
, we use the effective Hamiltonian (defined below)
which we will derive from the (full) perturbed Hamiltonian
H
.

Assume that the spectral decomposition of
H
is given by

H=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\zeta_{k}\Psi_{k}^{\dagger}\Psi_{k}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sum_{\theta}\theta Q_{\theta}}

where
\zeta_{k}
are the
d
distinct eigenvalues (split off from the eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
by perturbation)
and
\theta
represent the other eigenvalues (with eigenprojectors
Q_{\theta}
).
Furthermore, let

\Psi_{k}=\psi_{k}+\psi_{k}^{\perp}\ \hskip 36.135pt\ (k=1,\ldots,d)

where
\psi_{k}\in P_{0}
are the fixed-point solutions of the Feshbach map (see
Theorem

3.1
).

Claim 3.1
.

For
k=1,\ldots,d
,
\norm{\psi_k^\perp}/\norm{\psi_k}\leq\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
,
whence
\norm{\psi_k}^{2}\geq 1-\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
and
\norm{\psi_k^\perp}^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
.

Proof.

First, we show
\norm{\psi_k^\perp}/\norm{\psi_k}\leq\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
; that is, the length of

\psi_{k}^{\perp}
is negligible compared to the length of
\psi_{k}
. To see this, note

\displaystyle\lVert{\psi_{k}^{\perp}}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\delta\norm{P_1 R(\zeta_k,H_{11}) P_1 W}\norm{\psi_k}

\displaystyle\leq\delta\norm{R(\zeta_k,P_1 H_0 P_1)}\norm{\psi_k},\ \ \ \mbox{ as $\norm{P_1},\norm{P_1 W}\leq 1$ }

\displaystyle\leq\delta\frac{1}{d(\zeta_{k},\sigma(P_{1}H_{0}P_{1}))}\norm{\psi_k},\ \ \ \mbox{ by \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{lemma:resolvent-bound}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)\norm{\psi_k},\ \ \ \mbox{ using \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eqn:aux-bound}.}

Now, we have

1=\norm{\Psi_k}^{2}=\norm{\psi_k}^{2}+\lVert\psi_{k}^{\perp}\rVert^{2}\leq(1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}))\norm{\psi_k}^{2}
.
∎

Let
\tilde{\psi}_{k}=\psi_{k}/\norm{\psi_k}
be the state
\psi_{k}
normalized.
We shall show
\{\tilde{\psi}_{k}:k=1\ldots,d\}
is a nearly-orthonormal basis for
P_{0}
.

Claim 3.2
.

If
k\neq\ell
,
\langle\tilde{\psi}_{k},\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}\rangle\leq\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
.

Proof.

We have

\langle\Psi_{k},\psi_{k}^{\perp}\rangle=\norm{\psi_k^\perp}^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
by
3.1
.
Thus,

\langle\psi_{k},\psi_{\ell}\rangle=\langle\Psi_{k}-\psi_{k}^{\perp},\Psi_{\ell}-\psi_{\ell}^{\perp}\rangle=-\langle\Psi_{k},\psi_{\ell}^{\perp}\rangle-\langle\psi_{k}^{\perp},\Psi_{\ell}\rangle+\langle\psi_{k}^{\perp},\psi_{\ell}^{\perp}\rangle\leq\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})

because

|\langle\psi_{k}^{\perp},\psi_{\ell}^{\perp}\rangle|\leq\norm{\psi_k^\perp}\norm{\psi_\ell^\perp}\leq\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
,
by
3.1
.
Now, observe
\norm{\psi_k}^{2}\geq 1-\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
, again by
3.1
.
∎

Let
V
be a matrix whose
k
th column is the vector
\tilde{\psi}_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,d
.
Consider the Gram matrix
M=V^{\dagger}V
whose entries are
M_{jk}=\langle\tilde{\psi}_{j},\tilde{\psi}_{k}\rangle
.

Claim 3.3
.

If
d\varepsilon^{2}=o(1)
,
\myspan\{\tilde{\psi}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\psi}_{d}\}=P_{0}
.

Proof.

By
3.2
,
M_{k,k}=1
and

|M_{j,k}|\leq\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
. If
(d-1)\varepsilon^{2}=o(1)
,
M
is strictly diagonally dominant and
hence invertible (see
[
20
]
, Theorem 6.1.10). Thus,
M
has rank
n
, and so does
V
.
As each
\tilde{\psi}_{k}
lies in
P_{0}
(which has dimension
d
), we are done.
∎

Claim 3.4
.

For any normalized
\psi\in P_{0}
,

\norm{\sum_\theta Q_\theta\psi}^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}(d\varepsilon^{2})
for each
\theta
.

Proof.

By
3.3
,
\psi=\sum_{k}c_{k}\tilde{\psi}_{k}
, for some
c_{k}
, or

\psi=Vc
, where
c
is the vector whose
k
th entry is
c_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,d
.
Note
c\neq 0
as
\psi\in P_{0}
.
As
\psi
is normalized,

1=\norm{\psi}_{2}^{2}=c^{\dagger}V^{\dagger}Vc=c^{\dagger}Mc.

But,
M=I+A
, where
|A_{jk}|=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
for
j\neq k
, and
A_{jj}=0
, by
3.2
.
Therefore,

1=c^{\dagger}Mc=\norm{c}_{2}^{2}+c^{\dagger}Ac=\norm{c}_{2}^{2}\left(1+\frac{c^{\dagger}Ac}{\norm{c}_{2}^{2}}\right).

By Rayleigh’s principle, the ratio
c^{\dagger}Ac/\norm{c}_{2}^{2}
is bounded in absolute value by
\norm{A}_{2}
,
which is
\mathcal{O}(d\varepsilon^{2})
.
Hence,
\norm{c}_{2}^{2}=1-\mathcal{O}(d\varepsilon^{2})
.
By Cauchy-Schwarz,
\sum_{k}|c_{k}|\leq\sqrt{d}\norm{c}_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}
.

Since
\Psi_{k}=\psi_{k}+\psi_{k}^{\perp}
and
\sum_{\theta}Q_{\theta}\Psi_{k}=0
, we have

\sum_{\theta}Q_{\theta}\psi_{k}=-\sum_{\theta}Q_{\theta}\psi_{k}^{\perp}
.
But,
\norm{\sum_\theta Q_\theta\psi_k^\perp}\leq\norm{\psi_k^\perp}\leq\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
by
3.1
,
as
\sum_{\theta}Q_{\theta}
is a projection operator.
This shows
\norm{\sum_\theta Q_\theta\psi_k}\leq\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
.
Therefore,

\norm{\sum_\theta Q_\theta\psi}\leq\sum_{k}|c_{k}|\norm{\sum_\theta Q_\theta\tilde{\psi}_k}\leq\sqrt{d}\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon).

This shows
\norm{\sum_\theta Q_\theta\psi}^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}(d\varepsilon^{2})
.
∎

Claim 3.5
.

For any normalized states
\psi_{a},\psi_{b}\in P_{0}
, we have

|\langle\psi_{b},\sum_{\theta}e^{i\theta}Q_{\theta}\psi_{a}\rangle|\leq\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d}\varepsilon)
.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{Q}=\sum_{\theta}e^{i\theta}Q_{\theta}
and note
\tilde{Q}^{\dagger}\tilde{Q}=\sum_{\theta}Q_{\theta}
.
We have

|\langle\psi_{b},\tilde{Q}\psi_{a}\rangle|^{2}\leq\norm{\psi_b}^{2}\norm{\tilde{Q}\psi_a}^{2}\\
\leq\langle\psi_{a},\tilde{Q}^{\dagger}\tilde{Q}\psi_{a}\rangle\\
\leq\langle\psi_{a},\sum_{\theta}Q_{\theta}\psi_{a}\rangle.

As
\sum_{\theta}Q_{\theta}
is a projector, the last expression equals
\norm{\sum_\theta Q_\theta\psi_a}^{2}

which is at most
\mathcal{O}(d\varepsilon^{2})
by
3.4
.
∎

To obtain an orthonormal basis, we apply Gram-Schmidt to the vectors
\tilde{\psi}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\psi}_{d}
.
Suppose
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{d}
are the resulting vectors (which span
P_{0}
).
Note that

\rho_{k}=\tilde{\psi}_{k}+\mathcal{O}_{d}(\varepsilon^{2}).

The notation
\mathcal{O}_{d}(\varepsilon^{2})
hides constant factors that depend on
d
;
if
d=\mathcal{O}(1)
, this is simply
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
.

The
effective Hamiltonian

\mathbb{h}
(relative to
P_{0}
) is defined as

(18)

\mathbb{h}:=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\zeta_{k}\rho_{k}^{\dagger}\rho_{k}.

For two normalized states
\phi_{a},\phi_{b}\in P_{0}
, we have

(19)

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{b},e^{-itH}\phi_{a}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}e^{-it\zeta_{k}}\langle\phi_{b},\Psi_{k}\rangle\langle\Psi_{k},\phi_{a}\rangle+\langle\phi_{b},\sum_{\theta}e^{-it\theta}Q_{\theta}\phi_{a}\rangle

(20)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}e^{-it\zeta_{k}}\langle\phi_{b},\Psi_{k}\rangle\langle\Psi_{k},\phi_{a}\rangle+\mathcal{O}_{d}(\varepsilon),\ \ \mbox{ by \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{claim:tail-bound}}.

Since
\phi_{a}\in P_{0}
and
\Psi_{k}=\psi_{k}+\psi_{k}^{\perp}
, we get

\langle\Psi_{k},\phi_{a}\rangle=\langle\psi_{k},\phi_{a}\rangle=\norm{\psi_k}\langle\tilde{\psi}_{k},\phi_{a}\rangle

and similarly for
\phi_{b}
.
Therefore,

(21)

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{b},e^{-itH}\phi_{a}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}e^{-it\zeta_{k}}\norm{\psi_k}^{2}\langle\phi_{b},\tilde{\psi}_{k}\rangle\langle\tilde{\psi}_{k},\phi_{a}\rangle+\mathcal{O}_{d}(\varepsilon)

(22)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}e^{-it\zeta_{k}}\norm{\psi_k}^{2}\langle\phi_{b},\rho_{k}\rangle\langle\rho_{k},\phi_{a}\rangle+\mathcal{O}_{d}(\varepsilon)

(23)

\displaystyle=(1-\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}))\langle\phi_{b},e^{-it\mathbb{h}}\phi_{a}\rangle+\mathcal{O}_{d}(\varepsilon)

as
\norm{\psi_k}^{2}=1-\Omega(\varepsilon^{2})
by
3.1
.

So, if there is state transfer from
\phi_{a}
to
\phi_{b}
via
\mathbb{h}
, then
there is state transfer between the two states via
H
, or

(24)

|\langle\phi_{b},e^{-itH}\phi_{a}\rangle|\geq 1-\mathcal{O}_{d}(\varepsilon).

The fidelity is arbitrarily close to
1
because
\varepsilon=\delta\kappa=o(1)
.

This shows state transfer on
H
can be reduced to a (smaller) state transfer on
\mathbb{h}
.
Unfortunately, the location of each perturbed eigenvalue
\zeta_{k}
(relative to
\lambda_{0}
)
is only known
asymptotically
. Perfect (and pretty good) state transfer requires exact conditions
on the eigenvalues and eigenprojectors (see
[
13
]
).
We will show how to handle these problems for two-fold degeneracy.

4.
Constructing High-fidelity State Transfer

The following notion of high-fidelity state transfer is relevant to our results.

Definition 1
.

(High-fidelity state transfer)

Let
G
be a graph with adjacency matrix
A(G)
. Let
a,b
be two vertices of
G
.
We say
G
has high-fidelity state transfer from
a
to
b
if for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)

there is an effectively computable time
t=t(\epsilon)
so that

(25)

|\bra{b}e^{-iA(G)t}\ket{a}|\geq 1-\epsilon.

In most cases,
t(\epsilon)
is a polynomial function in

1/\epsilon
which may also depend on some properties of the underlying graph
G
(through
A(G)
, say).
We will adopt the convenient Dirac notation which is standard in quantum information.
Thus,
\ket{\alpha}
denotes a normalized column vector,
while the row vector
\bra{\alpha}=\ket{\alpha}^{\dagger}
denote its conjugate transpose.
The dimensions will be clear from context.
For
\delta>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, let
B_{\delta}(x)
denote the interval
\{y:|y-x|<\delta\}
.

Definition 2
.

(
\gamma
-cospectrality)

Let
G
be a graph with a nonsingular adjacency matrix
A
.
Two normalized vectors
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}^{V(G)}
are
\gamma
-cospectral in
G
if there exists
\eta>0
, with
B_{\eta}(0)\subset\rho(G)
, so that

|\bra{\alpha}R(\lambda,A)\ket{\alpha}-\bra{\beta}R(\lambda,A)\ket{\beta}|=\gamma_{0}|\bra{\alpha}R(\lambda,A)\ket{\beta}|,\ \ \ \mbox{for all $\lambda\in B_{\eta}(0)$}

for some
\gamma_{0}\leq\gamma
.

For a graph
G
, we use
R(\lambda,G)
in place of
R(\lambda,A)
.
Recall that we say two vertices
a,b
in
G
are
cospectral
if their walk counts match, that is,

\bra{a}A^{k}\ket{a}=\bra{b}A^{k}\ket{b}
for all integers
k\geq 1
. It is known that cospectrality is
a necessary condition for perfect (and pretty good) state transfer between
a
and
b
(see
[
13
]
).
Our notion of
\gamma
-cospectrality is weaker than cospectrality,
as
\bra{\alpha}A^{k}\ket{\alpha}=\bra{\beta}A^{k}\ket{\beta}
, for
k\geq 0
,
implies
\bra{\alpha}R(\lambda,G)\ket{\alpha}=\bra{\beta}R(\lambda,G)\ket{\beta}
,
for
\lambda\in\rho(G)
.

We state our main result in this section.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
G_{0}
be a connected graph with adjacency matrix
A
, with
\norm{A}=1
, and condition number
\kappa<\infty
.
Let
G=G_{0}\cup\overline{K}_{2}
be a disjoint union with adjacency matrix
H_{0}
.
Suppose
\ket{\alpha},\ket{\beta}
are
(2\gamma)
-cospectral vectors in
G_{0}

and
\delta\in(0,1)
satisfy

(26)

\delta\kappa\ll 1

and

(27)

|\bra{\alpha}A^{-1}\ket{\beta}|\gg\delta^{2}\kappa^{3}.

If
a,b
are the vertices of
\overline{K}_{2}
,
let
W=\delta(\outerproduct{a}{\alpha}+\outerproduct{\alpha}{a}+\outerproduct{b}{\beta}+\outerproduct{\beta}{b})
.
Then,

(28)

\displaystyle|\bra{b}e^{-i\tau(H_{0}+\delta W)}\ket{a}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1-o(1)}{\sqrt{1+\gamma^{2}}}-o(1)

where

(29)

\tau=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\delta^{2}|\bra{\alpha}A^{-1}\ket{\beta}|}\right).

Proof.

The adjacency matrix of the disjoint union
G=G_{0}\cup\overline{K}_{2}
is
H_{0}=O_{2}\oplus A
.
The Hamiltonian
H=H_{0}+\delta W
encodes a weak coupling
between the isolated vertices
a,b
and the base graph
G_{0}
.

By Equation (
26
),
d(0,\sigma(G_{0}))\geq 1/\kappa
.
Using
Lemma

2.2
and
Eq.

17
,
for all
\lambda\not\in\sigma(G_{0})
we obtain

(30)

\norm{R(\lambda,G_0)}\leq\kappa.

The two-dimensional
0
-eigenspace of
H_{0}
is spanned by the characteristic vectors
\ket{a}
and
\ket{b}
.
Let
P_{0}=\outerproduct{a}{a}+\outerproduct{b}{b}
be the projection onto
\nul(H_{0})
and
P_{1}=I-P_{0}

The effective Hamiltonian
\mathbb{h}=F(\lambda)
(or the Feshbach-Schur map) is given by

\displaystyle F(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\delta^{2}W_{01}(\lambda I-A)^{-1}W_{10}

\displaystyle=\delta^{2}(\outerproduct{a}{\alpha}+\outerproduct{b}{\beta})(\lambda I-A)^{-1}(\outerproduct{\alpha}{a}+\outerproduct{\beta}{b})

\displaystyle=\delta^{2}\begin{pmatrix}\bra{\alpha}R(\lambda,G_{0})\ket{\alpha}&\bra{\alpha}R(\lambda,G_{0})\ket{\beta}\\

(2\epsilon)

Therefore,

Thus, if we take

(32)

\tau=\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{1}{\delta^{2}\omega(\lambda)},

we get

(33)

\bra{b}e^{-i\tau F(\lambda)}\ket{a}=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{1+\gamma^{2}}}\sin(\tau\sqrt{1+\gamma^2}).

As the exact location of
\lambda

\lambda_{0}=0
to approximate it.
Notice that

\displaystyle|\omega(\lambda)-\omega(0)|

\displaystyle=|\bra{\beta}(R(\lambda,G_{0})-R(0,G_{0}))\ket{\alpha}|

\displaystyle\leq\norm{R(\lambda,G_0) - R(0,G_0)},\ \ \ \mbox{ property of the norm}

\displaystyle=|\lambda|\norm{R(\lambda,G_0)}\norm{R(0,G_0)},\ \ \ \mbox{ by the first resolvent formula}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(|\lambda|\kappa^{2}),\ \ \ \mbox{ as each resolvent is at most $\kappa$ by \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eqn:resolvent-bound}.}

\tau_{0}:=\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{1}{\delta^{2}\omega(0)}.

Notice that

\tau_{0}=\tau(1+o(1))

which follows from

because

\left|\frac{\omega(\lambda)}{\omega(0)}-1\right|=\frac{|\omega(\lambda)-\omega(0)|}{|\omega(0)|}=o(1),

by

34

Therefore, we have

(36)

Combining this with

.

4.1.
Weak coupling versus strong potential

For high-fidelity state transfer, we compare the T.
rex
method (weak edge couplings)
with methods based on strong potential (large self-loop weights) (see
[
30
,
29
]
).
We restate the main results in Lippner and Shi
[
30
]
.
Let
G
be a graph with maximum degree
m
and let
a,b
be a pair of vertices in
G
of distance
d
from
each other and with cospectrality
c
(see Definition 2.6 in
[
30
]
).
If we attach self-loops of weight
Q
to both
a
and
b
where

Q>\frac{16}{\epsilon^{1/\alpha}}m^{1+\beta}

with
\alpha=\min\{2,c-d+1\}
and
\beta=\max\{1/2,d/(c-d+1)\}
,
then there is state transfer from
a
to
b
with fidelity at least
1-\epsilon
(see Theorem 1.4 in
[
30
]
)
in time
t_{0}
(see Theorem 1.6 in
[
30
]
) where

t_{0}<2\pi(Q+m)^{d-1}.

Here, state transfer time scales exponentially with the distance
d
(or diameter, in the worst case).
Intuitively, this method increases the potential on
a
and
b
in such a way that the graph
G
becomes
negligible (which facilitates state transfer) but at the expense of the distance (of the order
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon^{d})
).
In contrast, the state transfer time for the T.
rex
method depends mainly on the condition number of
A(G)
.
For example, for the Krawtchouk chain of length
n
, the condition number is
\mathcal{O}(n)
and for the unweighted path

P_{n}
the condition number is
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
. Thus, the state transfer time of our scheme is
polynomial
in the
diameter.

Almeida

Then,

5.1.
Resonant tunneling

We describe
resonant tunneling
, a variation of the T.
rex
method, which works (under mild assumptions)
for singular base graphs.
Suppose the base graph
G_{0}
has an adjacency matrix
H_{0}
with zero as a
simple
eigenvalue and
\ket{\rho}
as its corresponding eigenvector.
Consider the disjoint union
G=G_{0}\cup\overline{K}_{2}
and suppose
a
and
b
are the vertices of the coclique
\overline{K}_{2}
;
here, the adjacency matrix of
G
is
H=H_{0}\oplus O_{2}
.

Choose two vertices
\alpha
and
\beta
in
G_{0}
and attach the T.
rex
arms through the coupling

W=\outerproduct{a}{\alpha}+\outerproduct{\alpha}{a}+\outerproduct{b}{\beta}+\outerproduct{\beta}{b}
.
Now, we consider the perturbed Hamiltonian

H(\delta)=H+\delta W.

Let
P_{0}=\outerproduct{a}{a}+\outerproduct{b}{b}+\outerproduct{\rho}{\rho}
be the projection onto the zero eigenspace of
H
.
Suppose
P_{0}(\delta)
is the eigenprojector of
H(\delta)
corresponding to
P_{0}
after perturbation.
Let
U
be the direct rotation operator from
P_{0}(\delta)
to
P_{0}
. This unitary operator is called
the
Schrieffer-Wolff
transformation (see Bravyi
et al.

[
6
]
, Definition 3.1).
In this framework, the effective Hamiltonian relative to
P_{0}
is given by

(39)

\mathbb{h}=P_{0}U(H+\delta W)U^{\dagger}P_{0}.

If
\delta
is sufficiently small and the spectral gap between
0
and the other eigenvalues of
H_{0}
are large enough, then

e^{-it\mathbb{h}}=U^{\dagger}e^{-it(H+\delta W)}U.

provided the initial and target states of interest are both in
P_{0}
. This is because
P_{0}(\delta)
is an invariant
subspace of
H+\delta W
. Moreover, up to a first-order approximation of
U
, we get (see
[
6
]
, Equation 3.13)

(40)

\mathbb{h}=P_{0}HP_{0}+\delta P_{0}WP_{0}.

In our T.
rex
setting, under the
\{\ket{a},\ket{\rho},\ket{b}\}
basis, we have

\mathbb{h}=\epsilon\Delta\begin{pmatrix}0&\innerproduct{\alpha}{\rho}&0\\
\innerproduct{\alpha}{\rho}&0&\innerproduct{\beta}{\rho}\\
0&\innerproduct{\beta}{\rho}&0\end{pmatrix}

where
\Delta=\min\{|\lambda|:\lambda\in\Sp(G_{0}),\lambda\neq 0\}
(which is included so that
the effective Hamiltonian has a bounded norm) and

\epsilon
is a sufficiently small constant.
Assuming
\innerproduct{\alpha}{\rho}\neq 0
and
\innerproduct{\alpha}{\rho}=\innerproduct{\beta}{\rho}
,
we see that the quantum walk on
\mathbb{h}
maps
\ket{a}
to
\ket{b}
in time

\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{1}{\epsilon\Delta|\innerproduct{\alpha}{\rho}|}.

Remark
:
The Laplacian
L=D-A
of a connected undirected graph
G
of order
N
has zero as a simple eigenvalue
with the normalized all-one vector as eigenvector. Thus, resonant tunnelling occurs in time

\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N}\epsilon^{-1}\Delta^{-1})
where
\Delta=\lambda_{2}(G)
is the algebraic connectivity of
G
.

5.2.1.
Path

The path

\{1,\ldots,N\}
with edges
(j,k)

The eigenvalues of

\lambda_{j}=2\cos(j\pi/(N+1))

\innerproduct{k}{\lambda_j}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{N+1}}\sin(jk\pi/(N+1))

where
j,k=1,\ldots,N

If
N
is odd,
\lambda_{k}=0
for
k=(N+1)/2

\ket{\rho}
satisfies
and
|\innerproduct{1}{\rho}|=\sqrt{2/(N+1)}\sim 1/\sqrt{N}
.
Moreover,
\Delta=2\sin(\pi/(N+1))\sim 1/N

\lambda_{k\pm 1}=2\cos((k \pm 1)\pi/(N+1))=\mp 2\sin(\pi/(N+1))
.
By resonant tunneling, the quantum hitting time is
\order{N^{3/2}\delta^{-1}}
.
In
[

]
, a quantum hitting time that scales linearly with

was claimed
but we were unable to corroborate this.

5.2.2.
Cycle

The cycle
C_{N}
has
\mathbb{Z}_{N}=\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\}
as its vertices and
(k,k\pm 1\pmod{N})
as its edges.
If
N
is even, where
N=2m
for
m
even, then the quotient of
C_{N}
(modulo an equitable partition)
is a weighted path
\tilde{P}_{m+1}
of odd length (which differs from the unweighted path
P_{m+1}
by
the
\sqrt{2}
weights on the pendant edges). As the quantum walks on
C_{N}
and on
\tilde{P}_{m+1}

are equivalent relative to end-to-end transfer, we may appeal to resonant tunneling for
P_{m+1}
.
So, the quantum hitting time is
\order{N^{3/2}\delta^{-1}}
.

5.2.3.
Clique

The complete graph (or clique)
K_{N}
contains all edges
(k,\ell)
, whenever
1\leq k\neq\ell\leq N
.
The quotient of
K_{N}
with cells
\{1\}
,
\{2,\ldots,N-1\}
and
\{N\}
is a weighted path given by

(41)

J=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt{N-2}&0\\
\sqrt{N-2}&N-3&\sqrt{N-2}\\
0&\sqrt{N-2}&0\end{pmatrix}.

The eigenvalues of
J
are approximately
\sqrt{N-2}(c\pm d)
, where
c=\tfrac{1}{2}(N-3)/\sqrt{N-2}

and

\ket{\rho}
satisfies
|\innerproduct{1}{\rho}|=1/\sqrt{2}
.
We apply resonant tunneling with
\Delta\sim|c-d|\sim 1/\sqrt{N}
(after dividing by the spectral norm).
Therefore, the quantum hitting time is
\order{\sqrt{N}\delta^{-1}}
.

Hypercube

The

N=2^{d}
) and contains edges of the form
(\alpha,\alpha\oplus e_{k})
,
for
k=1,\ldots,d
, where
\oplus
denotes the bitwise exclusive OR operation.
From Christandl
et al.

[
11
,
12
]
, it is known that perfect state transfer occurs between
antipodal vertices of
Q_{d}
in time
\tfrac{\pi}{2}\log_{2}N
(without the T.
rex
arms).
This result was already known in the earlier work of Moore and Russell; see the appendix in
[
31
]
.
We obtain the same
\mathcal{O}(\log_{2}N)
bound by using T.
rex
arms.

5.2.5.
Rook’s graph

The rook’s graph is defined as the Cartesian product
K_{m}\Box K_{m}
(hence
N=m^{2}
).
It is a strongly regular graph whose eigenvalues are
2m-2
(of multiplicity one),
m-2
(of multiplicity
2m-2
),
and
-2
(of multiplicity
(m-1)^{2}
). For vertices
\alpha=(1,1)
and
\beta=(m,m)
, we get

|\bra{\beta}A(K_{m}\Box K_{m})^{-1}\ket{\alpha}|\sim 1/m^{2}
. Hence, the quantum hitting time is

\order{N\delta^{-2}}
which shows no asymptotic speedup.

5.2.6.
Barbell

The barbell graph
B_{N}
is obtained from the disjoint union
2K_{N}\cup P_{N}
by identifying the leaf vertices of
P_{N}

with two vertices chosen from distinct cliques.
Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be two vertices from the two
K_{N}
s that are not attached to the path
P_{N}
.
The barbell has a quotient structure (see
Fig.

4
) described by a weighted path of
N+4
vertices,
whose adjacency matrix is a Jacobi matrix
H
given by

(42)

H=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt{N-2}&0&&&&&\\
\sqrt{N-2}&N-3&\sqrt{N-2}&&&&&\\
0&\sqrt{N-2}&0&1&&&&\\
&&1&0&1&&&\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
&&&1&0&1&&\\
&&&&1&0&\sqrt{N-2}&0\\
&&&&&\sqrt{N-2}&N-3&\sqrt{N-2}\\
&&&&&0&\sqrt{N-2}&0\end{pmatrix}

To ensure bounded norm, we need to scale
H
by
1/N
.
Depending on the parity of
N
, we show a comparison of the two T.
rex
methods:
resonant tunneling versus Feshbach-Schur.

Case 1:
N
is odd
.
Observe that
\tfrac{1}{N}H
has zero as a simple eigenvalue with eigenvector
\ket{\rho}
whose entries are given by

(43)

\innerproduct{k}{\rho}=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{N+5}}\times[\![\mbox{$k$ is odd}]\!]\times(-1)^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}

for
k=1,\ldots,N+4
. This allows us to apply resonant tunneling.
To estimate the spectral gap to the zero eigenvalue, note the cliques at the end of the barbell
have similar structure to
Eq.

41
which has distance
\kappa\sim 1/N
to eigenvalue zero.
But, the eigenvalues of the path
P_{N}
, after scaling by
1/N
, has gap
1/N^{2}
. This yields an effective Hamiltonian

(44)

\mathbb{h}=\frac{\delta}{N^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}0&\innerproduct{\alpha}{\rho}&0\\
\innerproduct{\alpha}{\rho}&0&\innerproduct{\beta}{\rho}\\
0&\innerproduct{\beta}{\rho}&0\end{pmatrix}.

As
\innerproduct{\alpha}{\rho}=\innerproduct{\beta}{\rho}=\sqrt{2/(N+5)}\sim 1/\sqrt{N}
for
\alpha=1
and
\beta=N+4

(by
Eq.

43
), this gives a quantum hitting time of
\mathcal{O}(N^{5/2}\delta^{-1})
.

Case 2:
N

.
We apply the T.
rex
method using the Feshbach-Schur map.
Consider the Jacobi matrix
J
, parametrized by
a
,
b
, and
c
, given by (see
Fig.

4
)

(45)

J=J(a,b,c)=\begin{pmatrix}0&a&&&&&&&\\
a&b&a&&&&&&\\
&a&0&c&&&&&\\
&&c&0&c&&&&\\
&&\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\\
&&&&&0&c&&\\
&&&&&c&0&a&\\
&&&&&&a&b&a\\
&&&&&&&a&0\\
\end{pmatrix}

Recall
J^{-1}=\Adj(J)/\det(J)
, where
\Adj(J)
is the adjugate of
J
(see
[
20
]
, Section 0.8.2).
Therefore, we have
\bra{\alpha}J^{-1}\ket{\beta}=\det(J_{\beta,\alpha})/\det(J)
,
where
J_{\beta,\alpha}
is
J
with row
\beta
and column
\alpha
removed.
By direct calculation, we see that
\det(J_{\beta,\alpha})=a^{4}c^{N-1}
and
\det(J)=\pm a^{4}c^{N}
.
Therefore,

|\bra{\alpha}J^{-1}\ket{\beta}|=c^{-1}.

A similar calculation shows
\det(J_{\alpha,\alpha})=\det(J_{\beta,\beta})=a^{2}c^{N}
, which implies

|\bra{\alpha}J^{-1}\ket{\alpha}|=|\bra{\beta}J^{-1}\ket{\beta}|=\frac{1}{a^{2}}.

The effective Hamiltonian is thus given by

(46)

\mathbb{h}=\frac{\delta^{2}}{\kappa}\begin{pmatrix}\bra{\alpha}J^{-1}\ket{\alpha}&\bra{\alpha}J^{-1}\ket{\beta}\\
\bra{\beta}J^{-1}\ket{\alpha}&\bra{\beta}J^{-1}\ket{\beta}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{\delta^{2}}{\kappa}\begin{pmatrix}a^{-2}&c^{-1}\\
c^{-1}&a^{-2}\end{pmatrix}.

After a diagonal shift (which can be done without loss of generality), we have
\mathbb{h}=(\delta^{2}/c\kappa)X
.
For the normalized barbell, we have
a=1/\sqrt{N}
,
b=1
,
c=1/N
.
Furthermore, the normalized barbell has condition number
\kappa\sim\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
.
Thus, we obtain a quantum hitting time of
\mathcal{O}(N\delta^{-2})
(which is nearly optimal due to
Lieb-Robinson bounds
[
7
,
32
]
).

5.3.
Quantum Search on Edge

In quantum spatial search (see
[
16
,
10
]
), given a graph
G=(V,E)
with adjacency matrix
A
,
we ask if there is
\gamma
(global scaling) so that for any vertex
\alpha\in V
(oracle location),
we have
\tau
(search time) such that

|\bra{\alpha}e^{-i\tau(\gamma A+\outerproduct{\alpha}{\alpha})}\ket{\rho}|\geq 1-\epsilon

where
\ket{\rho}
is the normalized Perron eigenvector of
G
.
This captures an analog variant of Grover search on arbitrary graphs.
The quadratic speedup requirement is expressed as
\tau=\mathcal{O}(1/|\innerproduct{\alpha}{\rho}|)
.
Typically, we require
\epsilon=o(1)
but it can be relaxed to a bounded constant.
In this framework, spatial search can be viewed as reverse mixing (as we transform
\ket{\rho}
to
\ket{\alpha}
)
under the rank-one perturbation of
A
(induced by the oracle position).

Imagine a new framework where the oracle, instead of being a strong potential located at a vertex,
is now a pendant edge weakly coupled to
G
.
To find this pendant edge (or oracle), we randomly place another pendant edge on the graph
(which, in the vertex model, is the Random Target Lemma
[
28
]
).
Next, we appeal to the T.
rex
argument to claim a high-fidelity state transfer between the two pendant edges.
For families of vertex-transitive graphs, we may use the hitting times given in Table
1
.
In contrast, the same idea but with strong vertex potentials will run into the same issues as outlined
in
Section

4.1
.

6.
Conclusions

The T.
rex
idea is a paradoxical, yet powerful, method for solving quantum transport problems.
We gave a mathematical treatment of the T.
rex
idea using the Feshbach-Schur method from perturbation theory
[
19
]
.
A summary of our results and related open questions is given in the following:

(1)

An elementary and rigorous proof that the T.
rex
method yields high-fidelity state transfer.

The requirements on the base graph is modest: it should have good condition number and its two vertices involved
in the state transfer must be
\gamma
-cospectral.
In contrast to perfect and pretty-good state transfer (see
[
13
]
), the latter condition is less stringent
than
strong
cospectrality.
Also, see
[
14
]
for a related work on peak state transfer.

(2)

The transfer time of the T.
rex
protocol does not depend directly on the diameter.

In contrast, the transfer time of protocols based on strong potentials increase exponentially
with the diameter (see
[
29
,
30
]
).
Is there a speed limit for high-fidelity state transfer?

(3)

A high-fidelity state transfer protocol robust against localization in spin chains.

The key idea was to exploit nonsingularity of the underlying spin chains along with a few protected vertices.
Our protocol was able to overcome
arbitrary
noise (while possibly trading off state transfer time), in sharp contrast to the negative results in
[
26
]
.

(4)

A simple method to find speedups in hitting times.

The versatility of the T.
rex
method was evident from the variety of graphs that were analyzed
(thereby avoiding
ad hoc
methods).
We then derived a quantum search algorithm to locate an oracle hiding as a pendant edge.

To complement the above list, we mention other related results inspired by the T.
rex
paradigm:

(5)

A simpler matrix inversion quantum algorithm
[
25
]
.

This is based on attaching multiple T.
rex
arms to a nonsingular base graph.

(6)

An optimal PST protocol robust against timing sensitivities on spin chains
[
24
]
.

This exploits a link between weak couplings and the second derivative of the PST curve.

We strongly suspect there are further uses of the T.
rex
method waiting to be unearthed.
