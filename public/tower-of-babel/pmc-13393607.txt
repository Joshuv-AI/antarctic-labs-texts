Evaluation of high-fidelity simulation on clinical readiness among Thai undergraduate nursing-midwifery students: a mixed-method study
Authors: Pairin Sukontrakoon, Sailom Gerdprasert, Pranee Pongrua, Arissara Sawatpanich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo address the scarcity of clinical childbirth placements resulting from declining birth rates and pandemic disruptions, High-Fidelity Simulation-Based Education (HF-SBE) has emerged as a key pedagogical strategy. However, its effectiveness in enhancing knowledge, self-confidence, and learning experiences among undergraduate nursing-midwifery students remains limited. Hence, this study aimed to evaluate students’ knowledge, self-confidence, and clinical performance quantitatively, while qualitatively exploring their learning experiences using HF-SBE within the Thai clinical education framework.MethodsA sequential explanatory mixed-methods design was employed. The quantitative phase used a single-group, quasi-experimental repeated-measures design with 168 third-year nursing-midwifery students recruited. Data on knowledge and self-confidence were collected at three time points: baseline, post-simulation, and post-clinical placement, while clinical performance was observed during simulation. In the subsequent qualitative phase, 24 students participated in focus group discussions to provide a deeper exploration of their learning experiences. The Friedman test and Wilcoxon Signed Rank test were employed for statistical comparisons, while qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis.ResultsKnowledge scores significantly improved from baseline to post-simulation (p < .001); although scores slightly declined following clinical practice, they remained significantly higher than baseline (p < .001). Self-confidence scores increased consistently across all time points (p < .001). Performance observations indicated that emergency-related tasks (including oxygen administration and SBAR-based notification for abnormal fetal heart rate patterns) as well as comfort promotion through facial wiping, were performed correctly. However, omissions were occurred in routine standards, specifically clinical documentation, self-introduction, and hand hygiene. Qualitative results generated two themes: (1) knowledge integration and application, and (2) confidence building and clinical preparedness.ConclusionsHF-SBE is an effective and feasible approach for enhancing students’ knowledge, self-confidence, and clinical readiness in childbirth care. However, future curriculum design should emphasize routine professional standards and procedural compliance alongside emergency management to ensure holistic midwifery practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04799-2.

Background

Despite advances in medical care, maternal mortality remains a major global health challenge. In 2023, the global maternal mortality ratio (MMR) was 197 deaths per 100,000 live births, far from the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) target of reducing MMR to 70 by 2030 [72]. In Thailand, the MMR reached 19.89 deaths per 100,000 live births in 2025, exceeding the national target of 15 deaths [40]. These deaths are primarily attributed to the “Three Delays Model,” specifically: (1) delay in seeking care, (2) delay in reaching care, and (3) delay in receiving quality care. Notably, about 81% of deaths within the third delay stem from provider-related factors, including delayed risk assessment, inaccurate estimation of blood loss, and a lack of emergency expertise [41]. Because these factors are largely preventable, the World Health Organization emphasizes skilled birth attendance, equipped with life-saving resources, as a key intervention for reducing maternal deaths [72]. Well-trained professionals, especially nurse-midwives with strong clinical skills and the ability to respond rapidly to unexpected complications, play a vital role in averting preventable maternal deaths [47, 72].

Strengthening midwifery education to international standards by developing strong clinical competencies is essential for improving the quality of care and reducing maternal mortality [65, 71]. However, the quality of pre-service midwifery education is often constrained by inadequate teaching resources, limited infrastructure, and insufficient opportunities for clinical practice [45, 47]. In particular, declining birth rates and the limited availability of clinical placements have reduced nursing students’ opportunities to gain hands-on experience in labor, childbirth, and other essential clinical skills [38, 52, 75]. Thailand has experienced a sustained decline in births, falling from roughly 1.2 million in 1971 to fewer than 500,000 in 2022 [23]. Similarly, the total fertility rate, the average number of children a woman has during her reproductive years, fell from 5.0 in the mid-1970s to 2.2 in the early 1990s, and further declined to 1.1 in 2023 [39]. These demographic and educational constraints limit students’ opportunities to gain the clinical experience needed to develop core competencies, leaving them less prepared for real-world practice [5, 34]. Consequently, the need for innovative approaches to nursing education and training has become increasingly apparent. Simulation-based education (SBE) has emerged as a critical strategy to address these challenges, including limited clinical placement opportunities and disruptions such as the COVID-19 pandemic [28, 30, 42].

In recent years, simulation-based education (SBE) has been widely adopted in nursing and midwifery education [12, 50, 70]. Previous studies have demonstrated that SBE plays a vital role in improving midwifery students’ ability to handle emergency obstetric situations effectively [14, 43], particularly during childbirth which involves the active delivery of the baby [9, 12]. The benefits of SBE have been reported across key outcomes including knowledge acquisition [9, 14, 43, 50, 66], self-confidence [30, 43], satisfaction [9, 30, 43, 50], and clinical practice competence [9, 43, 50, 66]. In addition, simulators are classified by fidelity, which reflects the degree of realism and learner interaction [22, 31]. High-fidelity simulations, in particular, replicate real clinical scenarios, allowing students to engage in hands-on activities that mirror the complexities of healthcare practice [25, 53, 60]. Evidence suggests that the use of higher-fidelity mannequins enhances immediate knowledge and psychomotor skill outcomes compared to lower-fidelity models [10, 33, 57]. Moreover, many studies have shown that high-fidelity simulation-based education (HF-SBE) enhances knowledge and confidence among midwifery students [3, 25, 55].

Despite these global findings, most of these studies have been conducted outside of Thailand, leaving a gap in understanding how HF-SBE contributes to clinical readiness within the specific Thai educational framework. While Kuesakul et al. [30] recently identified increased satisfaction and self-confidence following antenatal simulation-based learning, their study was limited to the antenatal period. Consequently, the effectiveness of HF-SBE during childbirth on the clinical readiness among Thai undergraduate nursing students remains insufficiently unexplored in the Thai undergraduate context. This study addresses this gap by examining changes in clinical readiness, specifically knowledge and self-confidence, following the implementation of HF-SBE in childbirth care among Thai nursing-midwifery students. The significance of this study lies in its comprehensive approach, which pairs pre- and post-simulation outcomes with a follow-up assessment after clinical placement. By synthesizing these quantitative data with a qualitative exploration of student perspectives, the findings provide new insights into how HF-SBE prepares students for real-world practice and supports the transition to professional clinical midwifery.

Theoretical framework

This study was guided by the integration of Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory and Jeffries’s framework for designing HF-SBE. Jeffries’s nursing simulation-based teaching model emphasizes realistic scenarios and comprises three phases: (1) pre-briefing, (2) simulated clinical experience, and (3) debriefing [26]. This model guided the structure of the HF-SBE in the present study, using pre-briefing to prepare students, simulation to provide hands-on clinical experience, and debriefing to facilitate reflective learning. This framework provides a structured approach in designing, implementing, and evaluating nursing simulation activities to allow the interaction between the educator, student, and learning environment.

This learning process aligns with Kolb’s learning cycle, which consists of four stages: (1) concrete experience, (2) reflective observation, (3) abstract conceptualization, and (4) active experimentation [29]. In this study, students participated in a realistic clinical scenario through HF-SBE, gaining concrete experience by performing childbirth care activities individually or collaboratively. They then engaged in debriefing, during which they reviewed events, exchanged perspectives with peers and instructors, and reflected on their observations to construct new knowledge. Students subsequently progressed to abstract conceptualization by synthesizing their insights into appropriate care strategies. Finally, through active experimentation, students applied their newly constructed knowledge to real clinical practice.

Accordingly, this study employed Jeffries’s framework, guided by Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory, to design and evaluate a HF-SBE program. The primary aim was to assess students’ knowledge, self-confidence, and clinical performance in childbirth care, while qualitatively exploring their learning experiences to gain an understanding of their professional development. Methods

Research design

Guided by a pragmatic paradigm, this study employed a sequential explanatory mixed-methods design to generate both generalizable outcomes and in-depth experiential insights [15]. The quantitative phase used a single-group, quasi-experimental repeated-measures design to evaluate changes in students’ knowledge and self-confidence across three time points: baseline (pre-simulation), post-simulation, and following clinical placement. Additionally, students’ clinical performance was assessed through direct observation during the HF-SBE to evaluate their clinical competence. Subsequently, a descriptive qualitative approach using focus group discussions was conducted to provide deeper insights into HF-SBE as an educational approach, helping to contextualize the quantitative findings and the students’ readiness for clinical practice.

Study population and eligibility criteria

The study population consisted of third-year undergraduate students enrolled in the Maternal-Newborn Nursing and Midwifery Practicum I course at a university in Bangkok, Thailand. Inclusion criteria were full-time students who: (1) were enrolled in the undergraduate nursing and midwifery program; (2) had completed the prerequisite theoretical course in Maternal-Newborn Nursing and Midwifery, which covered nursing and midwifery care for women during pregnancy, childbirth, and the puerperium; (3) had not previously enrolled in this practicum course; (4) were conversant in Thai language; and (5) had a smartphone or electronic device to complete the online questionnaire. However, students were excluded if they were unable to attend on-campus simulation sessions due to mandatory health-related quarantine or institutional COVID-19 safety protocols.

Sampling and sample size

For the quantitative phase, a total population sampling approach was employed to maximize coverage of the target cohort [54]. All third-year students currently enrolled in the undergraduate nursing and midwifery program were approached to participate in this study. Because the HF-SBE was a core curricular component, a formal sample size calculation was not performed. Thus, the entire cohort was recruited to ensure educational equity, providing all students with equal access to the simulation intervention.

Of the 178 students who were initially approached, 10 were excluded: 2 declined to participate, and 8 were ineligible due to COVID-19 quarantine. The final sample consisted of 168 students who provided informed consent and completed the quantitative phase of the study, yielding a response rate of 94.4%. The 168 participants were organized into groups of 7–8 students, based on their assigned clinical practice periods in the labor and delivery unit. Each group rotated through the unit and completed the quantitative questionnaires at intervals corresponding to their rotation. Following the completion of the quantitative phase, purposive sampling was initiated to recruit participants for the qualitative phase. Students from the final four rotation groups were selected as information-rich cases [51] because their recent transition to clinical practice allowed for a fresh, comparative reflection on their readiness while minimizing recall bias. Inclusion criteria remained consistent with the quantitative phase. Specific exclusion criteria for the qualitative phase included students who did not complete all simulation scenarios or could not commit to the full interview duration. Those who agreed to participate formed four focus groups, each comprising six participants. This resulted in a total qualitative sample of 24. This sample size was deemed sufficient to reach data saturation, where no new themes or insights emerged from the discussions. A flowchart of participant recruitment is presented in Supplement 1.

Setting

The study was conducted in a high-fidelity simulated delivery room in the university’s clinical laboratory and in the clinical labor and delivery unit at a university hospital in Bangkok, Thailand. In Thailand, most nurses hold dual licensure in nursing and midwifery and are authorized to manage normal vaginal deliveries. While their clinical roles vary by hospital tier, this training focused on labor support and early recognition of complications requiring obstetric intervention to ensure practical readiness across various healthcare tiers, from community settings to university hospitals, particularly in facilitating timely referrals and interventions.

The simulated room featured the SimMom full-body interactive birthing simulator, equipped with standard obstetric supplies and emergency medical equipment for childbirth care scenarios.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted from January to May 2022. Students who agreed to participate signed consent forms and completed an online questionnaire regarding personal information before their practical training commenced. For quantitative phase, knowledge and self-confidence in childbirth care were assessed at three time points: (1) before HF-SBE (baseline); (2) immediately after the HF-SBE (post-simulation); and (3) after completing clinical practice in the labor and delivery unit (post-clinical placement), via online questionnaires accessed through a QR code. Students’ performance in childbirth care during their first simulation practice was independently assessed by direct observation through a one-way mirror.

The childbirth HF-SBE was integrated into the Maternal-Newborn Nursing and Midwifery Practicum I course. Students were assigned to small groups of 3–4 to practice in simulated scenarios. The learning modules were designed by experienced instructors based on the course learning outcomes and validated by a simulation specialist and two maternal-newborn nursing experts. The scenarios were programmed into the simulator and pilot-tested with three third-year nursing students who were not part of the study sample. The HF-SBE focused on clinical assessment; intrapartum care and support; management of emergency obstetric situations (e.g., non-reassuring fetal heart rate); professional communication (e.g., explaining the treatment plan, responding to women’s needs, SBAR-based notification to the obstetrician); infection control (e.g., using hand sanitizer and maintaining sterile technique); and documentation. A detailed description of the simulation module and scenario planning is provided in Supplement 2.

The intervention followed a standardized one-hour session format, consisting of a pre-briefing (10 min), the simulation scenario (30 min), and a structured debriefing (15–20 min). Each simulation began with a pre-briefing to establish a safe learning environment, clarify learning objectives, and explain the simulator’s technical features and roles. During the simulation, students performed childbirth care using a high-fidelity birthing simulator while being observed through a one-way mirror. Following the scenario, a structured debriefing session was conducted. During this session, an instructor provided feedback and utilized reflective questions to help students analyze the scenario, reflect on their performance, and discuss areas for improvement. This process aimed to enhance clinical readiness prior to the students’ transition to the labor and delivery unit.

Four semi-structured focus group interviews were conducted in Thai by one researcher (S.G.), an experienced female maternal-newborn nursing and midwifery instructor with over 30 years of expertise in labor and delivery. All interviews were held in a private conference room on the final day of clinical practice, following the completion of the quantitative phase. Each session lasted approximately 60 min and followed a semi-structured interview protocol (Supplement 3). Due to participant comfort, the focus group discussions were not audio-recorded. To ensure data accuracy and rigor, a second researcher (P.P.) served as a dedicated note-taker, capturing verbatim quotes and non-verbal cues. Immediately following each session, the researchers (S.G. and P.P.) met to synthesize these records into expanded field notes. To further enhance trustworthiness, member checking was performed at the end of each session to confirm for accuracy reflecting the participants’ perspectives.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Ethical Review Committee for Research in Humans of the Faculty of Medicine Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University (ID MURA number 2021/1016).

Data analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using STATA version 16.1 [61]. Personal data and clinical performance were analyzed using descriptive statistics, including frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test indicated that knowledge and self-confidence scores were not normally distributed; therefore, the Friedman test was used to compare scores across the three measurements. Additionally, the Wilcoxon Signed Rank test was employed to conduct pairwise comparisons of the scores. A significance level set at 0.05.

Qualitative data were analyzed using manual thematic analysis [7]. The analysis was performed on the expanded field notes and verbatim quotes captured during the focus groups. Microsoft Word and Excel were utilized to systematically organize, de-identify, and manage the data. The researchers reviewed the records repeatedly to ensure accuracy and achieve data immersion. Each researcher independently coded the data to identify meaningful units, and initial codes were then compared and refined through collaborative discussion to resolve discrepancies and ensure inter-coder agreement. Related codes were organized into categories, and these categories were then synthesized into major themes. Finally, representative quotations were selected to illustrate each theme, and all stages of the analysis process were reviewed and verified to ensure trustworthiness and consistency in the findings.

Results

Demographic data

A total of 168 participants in quantitative data, the majority of them were female (95.80%). Their age ranged from 20 to 23, and a mean age was 20.90 years (SD = 0.62). Their mean GPA of 3.03 (SD = 0.39). About 81.5% had previous experience with virtual simulation-based learning. For qualitative data, a total of 24 students participated in four focus group interviews. The participants’ age range was 20–22 years, with a mean age of 20.75 years (SD = 0.61). Most of them were female (91.7%) and their mean GPA was 3.10 (SD = 0.31). About 83.3% had previous experience with virtual simulation-based learning.

Quantitative results

Nursing students showed an improvement in knowledge following the simulation. As the data were not normally distributed, results are reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). The median knowledge score increased from 5 (IQR: 4–6) at baseline to 8 (IQR: 7–9) immediately after the simulation. This improvement was sustained following clinical practice, with the median score of 8 (IQR: 7–9). Similarly, students’ self-confidence in caring for women during childbirth showed a steady increase across the three time points. The median self-confidence score rose from 32 (IQR: 30–35) at baseline to 40 (IQR: 38–41) after the simulation, further reaching 41 (IQR: 40–44) following clinical practice. Friedman test results indicated that these increases in both knowledge (χ2 = 246.50, p < .001) and self-confidence (χ2 = 295.18, p < .001) were statistically significant. There was a moderate-to-large effect size for knowledge (Kendall’s W = 0.49) and a large effect size for self-confidence (Kendall’s W = 0.59), as shown in Table 1.

In Table 2, pairwise comparisons indicated that knowledge scores significantly increased after the simulation, compared to both pre-simulation and post-clinical practice scores (p < .001). The effect size was large for the comparison between pre-simulation and post-simulation (r = .89, 95% CI [0.73, 1.03]) and between pre-simulation and post-clinical practice (r = .84, 95% CI [0.69, 1.00]). Notably, knowledge scores after simulation were significantly higher than after the clinical practice (p < .001), with a moderate effect size (r = -.30, 95% CI [-0.45, -0.14]). For self-confidence in caring for women during childbirth, students showed a significant increase after the simulation, with further increases observed following clinical practice (p < .001). Notably, self-confidence levels after clinical practice were significantly higher than both pre-simulation and post-simulation scores (p < .001). Large effect sizes were observed for the comparisons between pre-simulation and post-simulation (r = .87, 95% CI [0.72, 1.03]), pre-simulation and post-clinical practice (r = .88, 95% CI [0.73, 1.04]), and post-simulation and post-clinical practice (r = .57, 95% CI [0.42, 0.73]).

As shown in Table 3, the majority of student groups demonstrated correct performance in emergency-related tasks. Specifically, high rates of appropriate performance were observed for oxygen administration via mask (83.64%) and SBAR-based notification to the obstetrician (78.18%) when abnormal fetal heart rate patterns (late deceleration) occurred. In addition, the assessment of maternal pain and comfort promotion through facial wiping was both correctly performed by 67.27% of the groups.

However, significant errors in clinical reasoning were observed regarding oral hydration and pain management. Despite the simulator (SimMom) explicitly requesting hydration, 74.54% of groups managed oral intake incorrectly. Students frequently withheld ice chips based on unfounded clinical justification, such as potential aspiration risks or concerns that full bladder would inhibit uterine contractions. Similarly, 69.09% of groups provided unjustified explanations for withholding analgesics, either citing incorrect rationales or notifying the obstetrician without providing an evidence-based explanation to the SimMom. Furthermore, gaps in routine professional standards persisted, with the highest rates of omission (tasks not performed) for clinical documentation (90.91%), self-introduction to the woman (81.82%), and hand hygiene before and after providing care (67.27%).

Qualitative results

Qualitative findings further enriched the understanding of students’ experiences with HF-SBE. Thematic analysis identified two key themes emerged including (1) knowledge integration and application, and (2) confidence building and clinical preparedness. Together, these themes illustrate how HF-SBE fosters a learning environment that reinforces theoretical knowledge while cultivating psychological readiness essential for clinical practice.

Knowledge integration and application

Participants reported that simulation enhanced clinical clarity and responsive care by allowing them to visualize the sequence of clinical events, understand the rationale behind interventions, what they have to do, and anticipate potential complications. One participant described, “It helps me learned how to perform the care, when and how to encourage the woman [to push], and for how long. In theory, we are supposed to have her push 2–3 times per contraction, so it made much clearer. The patient wanted water, and I didn’t know what to do because she was supposed to NPO. Through the simulation, I learned that giving ice chips is acceptable. I think I can perform the care better now.” (FG1, P3). Another participant added that “It helped me learn to care not only for the woman but also for the fetus. During the simulation, I hadn’t paid attention to the fetal heart rate slowing down until the SimMom asked if her baby was still healthy. That made me check the monitor, and I noticed a late deceleration, followed by a significant drop in the fetal heart rate. That’s how I learned from the experience.” (FG2, P6) These experiences highlighted those cues provided during the simulation; such as verbal prompts or physiological monitor changes, helped students recognize critical signs, make timely decisions, and translate theoretical knowledge into practice.

Moreover, participants also described gaining greater clinical clarity regarding professional responsibilities, particularly documentation. Reflecting on the scenario during debriefing helped students recognize what information was clinically significant, what should have been documented, and why accurate and timely documentation is essential. One participant shared, “We didn’t perform the documentation during the simulation, but we learned what we should have documented during the debrief. I felt more confident in documentation and learned how to write it correctly, when to document, and how to record the outcomes. If I hadn’t learned beforehand, I wouldn’t have known how to document when the nurse asked me to do it.” (FG3, P3) Participants noted that identifying mistakes, such as initially overlooking a slowing fetal heart rate or omitting documentation, deepened their learning by prompting corrective action and reflection during debriefing. The debriefing session further allowed students to review their actions, consider alternatives, and clarify misunderstanding, thereby reinforcing critical thinking, and overall clinical preparation. As one participant shared that the debriefing helped her learn and feel more prepared for clinical placement, “I was nervous and didn’t know what to do, feeling very unsure about providing nursing care. What I read in the books was very different from the real situation. But, after discussing with the instructor at the end [during the debrief], I understood much more. It made me feel more confident and ready to help women.” (FG2, P4). Overall, students described the HF-SBE as an influential component of their learning that strengthened how they understood, applied, and embodied clinical practice.

Confidence building and clinical preparedness

This theme captures students’ emotional and psychological development, illustrating their progression from uncertainty and self-doubt to increased confidence and clinical readiness through HF-SBE. Participants frequently reported that the early stages of the simulation triggered intense feelings of anxiety, incompetence, and freezing. As one participant shared, “I really learned what it feels like when a woman is in pain. At first, I was shocked because her scream was so loud. I had no idea she would react like that; it was frightening. Without the simulation, I probably would have panicked, not knowing what to do, and just stood there like a wall.” (FG2, P5) This startled response emerged as a common thread. Another participant noted that the pressures and unpredictability of the scenario initially caused them to freeze as they struggled to reconcile theoretical knowledge with the chaotic reality of the labor room, “I thought I understood the nursing care from theory and had done some reading, but when I encountered the situation in the simulation, it was confusing and chaotic for me. When the patient cried out, I didn’t know what I was supposed to do. However, I later realized that when the patient cried out, it corresponded with uterine contractions. This helped me learn that the patient should be encouraged to push during contractions, not simply whenever she feels the urge. I feel confident in caring for a woman during labor. I know how to provide encouragement.” (FG4, P4). As the simulation progressed, students transitioned from a state of anxiety toward increased self-confidence. This transition was facilitated by the high-fidelity environment, which allowed students to actively engage, recover from initial uncertainty, and prepare for clinical placement. Many participants reported that HF-SBE enhanced their confidence in performing clinical tasks. One participant explained, “It gave me confidence in nursing step by step. I knew that when a woman showed signs of pain and her uterus contracted, I could encourage her to push.” (FG3, P1) This development culminated in a tangible sense of readiness that translated into real-world practice. Students felt equipped not only with technical skills but also with communication strategies needed to manage complex patient interactions. One participant who encountered a similar situation during their clinical placement shared her experience, “A woman in a real situation said the same thing as in the simulation. She said she didn’t want to push anymore and asked for a C-section. I responded, ‘You can do it. When you feel pain from the contraction, focus on pushing. Try to control your breathing and follow the guidance provided.” (FG1, P1) Participants emphasized that HF-SBE familiarized them with the complexity of real clinical environments, thereby increasing their confidence in managing similar situations in practice. Ultimately, the experiences contributed to a smoother transition from the academic uncertainty to the professional confidence required in the labor and delivery setting.

Discussion

This study contributes to the literature on global nursing education by providing insight not only into outcome improvements but also into the underlying process of knowledge integration and confidence building. The study responds to ongoing challenges in nursing education, specifically the need to enhance knowledge acquisition and confidence in childbirth care while ensuring patient safety amidst declining birth rates. The findings highlight the educational value of integrating HF-SBE into nursing and midwifery curricula in resource-constrained or low-birth-rate contexts.

Theoretical knowledge scores showed a significant increase immediately following HF-SBE, indicating that HF-SBE positively influenced students’ cognitive grasp of childbirth care. These improvements may be attributed to the active and experiential nature of HF-SBE, which immerses students in realistic clinical environments that require real-time decision-making, problem-solving, and the management of complex and dynamic childbirth scenarios [4, 13, 70, 73]. These results were supported by qualitative findings, as students reported that the realistic scenarios bridged the gap between theoretical textbook descriptions and clinical application. By structuring HF-SBE activities according to Kolb’s experiential learning cycle [29], the intervention facilitated deeper cognitive processing and the integration of knowledge across cognitive and psychomotor domains [21, 25, 59, 62]. Furthermore, the structured debriefing session allowed students to reflect on their performance, analyze their clinical reasoning, clarify misconceptions, and integrate feedback from educators and peers. As a result, they were able to consolidate experiential insights into coherent and transferable clinical knowledge [1, 19, 25]. Overall, the findings suggest that HF-SBE not only strengthens theoretical knowledge but also supports its transfer to real-world clinical contexts, consistent with previous studies [3, 25, 55]. These findings highlight the potential of HF-SBE in clinical preparation to enhance knowledge readiness essential for effective clinical practice [60].

Although knowledge scores slightly declined after clinical practice, they remained significantly higher than pre-HF-SBE levels, indicating a sustained benefit of HF-SBE. This decline likely reflects the theory-practice gap, where clinical protocols and available equipment in the clinical setting often diverge from the evidence-based standards taught in the classroom or simulation laboratory [63]. When students encounter a discrepancy between theory and clinical practice, specifically regarding clinical procedures and materials, it can impede the achievement of learning goals [58, 63]. In this study, practicing in a clinical setting potentially exposed students to pragmatic variation such as patient-specific contexts, interprofessional healthcare team dynamics, and inconsistent equipment availability. Consequently, these variations may have hindered their ability to reinforce theoretical knowledge [36, 37, 74]. Furthermore, environmental variables like declining birth rates and the COVID-19 pandemic may have further influenced knowledge acquisition by constraining learning opportunities. When clinical opportunities for practicing childbirth skills are limited, natural knowledge decay may occur [2, 44, 46, 56]. This suggests that while clinical exposure is invaluable, it may not consistently provide the high-acuity scenarios required to maintain specialized skills. Consequently, there may be a need for just-in-time simulation refreshers — brief, targeted sessions integrated within the clinical setting to mitigate knowledge decay and ensure students remain prepared for childbirth care [8, 24]. Such refreshers would offer curriculum designers a practical strategy to bridge the gap between simulation and the unpredictable nature of clinical practice. However, the fact that scores remained significantly above baseline suggests that HF-SBE provides a durable cognitive foundation. Its immersive nature likely promotes deeper engagement and memory consolidation [11, 69], supporting the durability of learning beyond the immediate intervention period [27, 64]. Nevertheless, these sustained scores likely reflect the synergistic effect of both simulation and subsequent clinical practice, and may be influenced by the testing effect, where repeated assessment contributes to knowledge retention. Further research is needed to determine if this foundation persists into professional practice.

Students’ confidence in childbirth care increased significantly after HF-SBE and clinical practice. Notably, students’ self-confidence demonstrated a sustained upward trajectory, reaching its peak after clinical practice, in contrast to theoretical knowledge which declined slightly post-clinical placement. This continuous growth suggests that HF-SBE provides a critical foundation, reducing initial performance anxiety and enhancing students’ readiness for the clinical setting [16, 60]. Qualitative findings corroborate the quantitative results, revealing that HF-SBE helped students overcome their initial anxiety, motivating them to better support women during childbirth and fostering the development of their competence during clinical practice. These findings align with previous research demonstrating a positive influence of simulation on students’ confidence [3, 25, 43, 55, 67]. Specifically, HF-SBE effectively boosts confidence by providing opportunities for hands-on practice with equipment and materials [62]. Moreover, the social, reflective, and emotionally supportive nature of debriefing sessions strengthens students’ confidence in managing clinical situations [19, 69].

However, further analysis reveals a calibration gap between subjective self-confidence and objective performance during the initial simulation [32, 49]. While successful execution of high-priority emergency tasks likely bolstered students’ confidence, significant omissions were observed in routine standards. This suggests that early increases in self-confidence may reflect perceived competence in high-acuity actions rather than a comprehensive mastery of holistic care. Furthermore, the rise in self-confidence following HF-SBE may primarily reflect a reduction in performance anxiety [48]. Qualitative findings in this study corroborate this, demonstrating that students perceived HF-SBE as a supportive environment that transformed their initial anxiety into an enhanced sense of confidence and preparedness. This initial boost in confidence is critical, as it provides the psychological readiness necessary for students to engage in complex tasks while their technical skills are still developing. Ultimately, subjective self-reports and objective observational data offer distinct yet complementary perspectives. While objective data provide precise indicators of clinical performance, subjective data capture students’ emotional and cognitive readiness. Integrating both forms of data, this study provides a nuanced understanding of learning processes and helps identify specific targets for tailored interventions in future HF-SBE design.

In addition, the quantitative results for childbirth care performance revealed a notable disparity between students’ responsiveness to high-acuity cues and their adherence to routine protocols. This discrepancy may reflect a cognitive tunneling effect, a phenomenon in which the intense cognitive load of a high-pressure scenario narrows students’ focus to the most immediate perceived threats [68]. It is plausible that, during the simulation, students experienced heightened anxiety related to the complexity of the scenario and required procedures [18]. These urgent tasks may have induced a form of tunnel vision, whereby students prioritize immediate life-saving actions over fundamental nursing standards [20], suggesting that such routines have not yet been fully internalized as integral components of professional practice. In our study, the structured performance checklist and targeted debriefing were implemented to enable educators to identify these omissions and emphasize the importance of consistently practicing basic safety protocols. Future strategies should integrate routine safety practices directly into scenario design and assessment criteria, as well as incorporating reflective debriefing that explicitly links technical performance with core nursing standards. Furthermore, the omission of interpersonal behaviors (i.e. self-introduction) highlighted a critical challenge in nursing education. While the high-fidelity mannequin provided vocal responses, it might be insufficient for eliciting therapeutic communication. Integrating additional pedagogical approaches, such as standardized patient-based education or AI-assisted assessment could provide a more robust framework for narrowing the gap between technical proficiency and professional communication skills [17].

Ultimately, these findings highlight the diagnostic value of HF-SBE in identifying gaps in both routine compliance and clinical reasoning that theoretical assessments missed. By providing a safe, controlled environment, HF-SBE allows mistakes to be identified and addressed without compromising patient safety [13, 25, 35]. The qualitative results explain and support this mechanism, illustrating structured debriefing process further facilitates critical reflection and constructive feedback. These identified performance gaps are transformed into deeper understanding and a heightened sense of clinical readiness. Overall, these findings contribute to the evidence that HF-SBE is an effective pedagogical approach for developing psychomotor, behavioral, and cognitive skills in nursing and midwifery education [9, 13, 21, 25, 43, 50, 59, 62, 70].

Limitation

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, while this study employed a mixed-methods approach, integrating a quasi-experimental, repeated-measures design with descriptive qualitative research. The use of a single-group, quasi-experimental repeated-measures design limits the ability to infer direct causality. A control group was not utilized due to ethical concerns regarding educational equity, as withholding high-fidelity simulation from a subset of students was deemed potentially detrimental to their clinical preparation. Consequently, while we observed significant within-subject changes, we cannot definitively exclude external confounding variables.

Second, the study assessed clinical performance only during the initial simulation practice. The absence of a baseline or follow-up performance assessment makes it difficult to quantify the exact magnitude of psychomotor skill acquisition. However, through the triangulation of objective observations with subjective knowledge and self-confidence scores, a calibration gap was identified. This highlights the diagnostic value of the simulation in revealing performance deficits that students may not have previously recognized.

Third, the relatively homogenous sample drawn from a single institution and the purposive sampling for qualitative phase may increase the risk of selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings beyond the single institution studied. In addition, the reliance on self-reported measures introduces potential response bias and social desirability, where students may over- or under-estimate their clinical confidence. Furthermore, repeated exposure to the same assessment measures may have introduced testing effects, which could influence participants’ responses over time.

Finally, although the follow-up assessment after clinical practice provides insight into short-term retention, the study did not evaluate long-term knowledge trajectories. Future research could consider longitudinal, multi-center designs with randomized control groups to further evaluate the sustained impact of HF-SBE on clinical practice. Despite these limitations, the study offers valuable insights into the feasibility of HF-SBE in nursing and midwifery education.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that HF-SBE is an effective and feasible approach for enhancing students’ knowledge, confidence, and clinical readiness in childbirth care. By integrating quantitative outcomes with qualitative experiential data, the findings provide insight not only into what students learned but how the HF-SBE support knowledge acquisition and confidence building. Importantly, this study highlights HF-SBE as a vital educational strategy for bridging the theory-practice gap, particularly in contexts where clinical opportunities are constrained by declining birth rates or public health crises. These findings support the integration of high-fidelity simulation into nursing and midwifery curricula to ensure students possess the necessary competence before transitioning to real-life clinical practice.
