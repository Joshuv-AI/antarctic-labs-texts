The future pharmacy is key to better health for me and the NHS: a patient's personal view
Authors: Marlene Winfield
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article is a patient view of how the future pharmacy could play a key role in devolving routine care for minor ailments and management of long-term conditions. As someone with three long-term conditions, I see an expanded role for pharmacies in supporting me to manage them effectively, filling a major gap: someone to see me and my medications regimen as a whole. A comprehensive national future pharmacies transformation programme is key to NHS sustainability. It must be fully funded, well planned and well executed.

As I have written before in these pages,1 I am retired and managing three long-term conditions. I await an artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled ‘digital health buddy’ to organise my self-care so that I stay as well as I now am for as long as possible.

Alongside my digital buddy, my self-care dream team would include my community pharmacy. It is perfectly placed to be my go-to not only for routine prescriptions, but also to support the care I give myself in my home 99.9% of the time.

As a patient/customer, I have an idea of what a transformed community pharmacy could add to my dream team. I'll explain this under four headings – access, adherence, assisting self-care, and assurance.

Access

I take eye drops that are keeping my vision at 20/20 despite a potentially serious eye condition, so I do have moments of fear when I hear people ahead of me in the queue being told that their medicines are not available due to shortages. I have been lucky so far, but sometimes I do not get my full supply all at once. I do worry that, as a country, the UK might be as bad at storing up medicines as we are at storing up energy or protective clothing against future shortages, or when a medicine is discovered to have new uses.

I know that pharmacies on the high street are regularly closing, which is particularly difficult in rural areas. Fortunately, online ordering and delivery services now abound and future-ologists say that, one day soon, medicines will be delivered to my door by drones. All very well, but it leaves even fewer opportunities for asking questions or getting advice, for example if I have special challenges when taking medicines.

I have heard that older people are the most likely to collect their prescriptions. I wonder to what extent that is influenced by the fact that medicines are free for us and nearly £10 for others.

In my dream team, community pharmacies would be regarded as key resources. My pharmacist would know me and my medications and pre-order them to fill my regular prescriptions. And a protective eye would be kept on those who may not be able to afford their prescriptions.

Adherence

It is well known that up to half of people do not take their medicines as intended and that it can lead to harm, including admission to hospital.2 Eye drops are quite tricky to take. When prescribed them, I found a video, and instructions were included in the pack. Confusingly, I was given three different times for keeping my eyes shut, so to be safe I chose the longest. There was no one to watch me to ensure that I was doing it correctly: it is difficult to watch yourself. I thought about asking the pharmacist, but didn't know if it was within her brief.

In my dream team, I would look to the pharmacy to be the one stop shop for all such wisdom, and my hospital or surgery would point me there.

I have to be mindful that any new drug I am prescribed or self-prescribe might not work well with what I am already taking. Even when I buy over-the-counter hay fever spray, I ask if it will affect my eye drops. In my time at the Department of Health, I spoke to a lot of patients whose treatment in silos led to medication for one condition exacerbating the symptoms of another.

In my dream team, my pharmacy would be the guardian of my complete medicines regimen, always there to advise how to manage multiple medicines, what medicines do and don't go well together, and if there are things that I no longer need to take.

When I am prescribed a new medicine, the pharmacist would explain why I needed it, the potential side effects and what to do about them, what results I could expect and how long it could take. I could discuss my fears, such as becoming dependent, and any religious or cultural issues regarding my treatment. The pharmacy would have a whole digital library of information about medicines and medical devices – in different languages and formats – that they could offer me. Being drawn from such a diverse range of people, pharmacists can bring a real understanding of the impact of culture and beliefs on how people manage their medications, helping to reduce health inequalities.

Assisting self-care

There is much talk these days about devolving certain aspects of care to allied health professionals. Where I sit, there is huge potential for devolution. In fact, I wonder if the NHS is sustainable in the future without it. I would hope that, over time, there would evolve a clear and more sensible division of responsibilities among me, my surgery, my hospitals and my pharmacy, so that each enhanced the other rather than increasing the potential for confusion or conflict.

In my dream team, the pharmacist would diagnose minor ailments and prescribe medicines for certain conditions like cystitis without my having to go via the GP surgery route. As many a woman knows, cystitis has an uncanny way of appearing on a Friday night just after surgeries close. To some extent, that sort of diagnosis and prescribing is starting to happen, but it is too piecemeal.

As well as acute problems, in my dream team the pharmacy would better support me in managing my multiple long-term conditions. They could, for example, send me reminders of the regular blood tests I need or when I am due to have a bone density scan. In the past, there has been confusion about whether the surgery or the hospital should be reminding me and so it didn't happen. I have now taken on the reminder role myself and it works for me, but not everyone can do that. Alternatively, as well as information resources, my pharmacy's digital library could include an array of approved digital tools that they could prescribe to help me manage my self-care effectively.

Assurance

I already trust the pharmacy to give me high-quality, approved medicines. I am not as confident that they will always spot the wrong medicine or the wrong dosage, but I hope they would. I have no idea how reliable the technology is that flags up errors or incompatibilities. Moreover, this only works if my pharmacy's records contain everything I have been prescribed. It won't, of course, have any over-the-counter drugs or dietary supplements or homeopathic medicines that I might prescribe for myself.

In my dream team, the pharmacy would periodically check with me to ensure that they have the full picture of what I am taking and I would know that I should inform them about anything I added myself. When I was prescribed a new medicine with serious potential side effects, they would ensure that I understood exactly how to take it and would ring me in the first few weeks to find out how I was getting on. That actually happened once and I was pleasantly surprised. And they could explain to me about any precautions I should be taking, for example when having dental treatment while taking a bone density medicine. In an ideal world, that watchfulness would be routine for me and for others like me everywhere in the country.

And finally, I know from a lot of research, most recently by NHS England,3 that while a majority of people do not worry a great deal about the privacy of their health data, up to a third do. And so I want to see that precautions are taken to keep people's information private. I am aware that I regularly give my name, address and date of birth in a public waiting area and sometimes my medications too. I don't worry too much that there might be hackers lurking there to steal my identity or expose my vulnerabilities, but others with more sensitive conditions might. I would rather have any conversations that are more private in a private space: I note that more and more pharmacies now have consulting rooms.

In my dream team, the pharmacy would be a place where a visible culture of clinical safety and patient confidentiality sat happily alongside the necessary commercial culture.

Conclusion

In a world where self-care is the primary mode for me and for many other people with long-term conditions, and where we may get care in several unconnected places, there is an unfilled role for someone to look at each of us as a whole. It is a big gap that can cause unintended harm when unfilled. Who is better placed to close that gap than my community pharmacy?

If my care is to be devolved properly, then the future supporting role of the pharmacy needs to be rethought. This will require a review of staffing, training, prescribing rights, regulation, organisational culture, layouts of rooms, use of time, digital skills, and the optimum information technology and information resources. It would be a major transformation. I know that bits of this are already happening all over the country, but in a fairly ad hoc way.

I fear that, instead, more and more demands will be offloaded onto pharmacies that are ill-equipped to meet them. An enhanced role for pharmacies should be recognised as crucial to the future sustainability of the NHS. A properly funded and comprehensive national transformation programme should start now.
