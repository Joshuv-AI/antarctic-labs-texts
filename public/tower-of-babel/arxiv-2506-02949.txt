Dynamic Programming Techniques for Enhancing Cognitive Representation in Knowledge Tracing
Lixiang Xu, Xianwei Ding, Xin Yuan, Richang Hong, Feiping Nie, Enhong Chen, Philip S. Yu

Abstract
Knowledge Tracing (KT) involves monitoring the changes in a student’s knowledge over time by analyzing their past responses, with the goal of predicting future performance. However, most existing methods primarily focus on feature enhancement, while overlooking the deficiencies in cognitive representation and the ability to express cognition—issues often caused by interference from non-cognitive factors such as slipping and guessing. This limitation hampers the ability to capture the continuity and coherence of the student’s cognitive process. As a result, many methods may introduce more prediction bias and modeling costs due to their inability to maintain cognitive continuity and coherence. Based on the above discussion, we propose the Cognitive Representation Dynamic Programming-based Knowledge Tracing (CRDP-KT) model. This model employs a dynamic programming algorithm to optimize cognitive representations based on the difficulty of the questions and the performance intervals between them. This approach ensures that the cognitive representation aligns with the student’s cognitive patterns, maintaining overall continuity and coherence. As a result, it provides more accurate and systematic input features for subsequent model training, thereby minimizing distortion in the simulation of cognitive states. Additionally, the CRDP-KT model performs partitioned optimization of cognitive representations to enhance the reliability of the optimization process. Furthermore, it improves its ability to express the student’s cognition through a weighted fusion of optimized record representations and relationships learned from a bipartite graph. Finally, experiments conducted on three public datasets validate the effectiveness of the proposed CRDP-KT model.

A good cognitive representation should ensure continuity (performance persistence) and coherence (alignment with cognitive rules), but raw records often suffer from discontinuity and incoherence, particularly for problems with varying difficulty within the same knowledge point. For problems with the same knowledge point but varying levels of difficulty, there are two types of incoherence and two types of discontinuity.

For incoherence:
i) If a student later correctly answers a more difficult question and performs well within the interval, he is more likely to have correctly answered an earlier, simpler question. However, the raw record does not effectively represent this (potentially due to a slipping).
ii) If a student later incorrectly answers a simpler question and performs poorly within the interval, he is more likely to have incorrectly answered an earlier, more difficult questions. However, the raw record also does not effectively represent this (potentially due to guessing).

For discontinuity:
i) If a student answers the previous question incorrectly and the next question correctly, but performs exceptionally well on both related and unrelated questions within an appropriate distance, the response state for the previous question should maintain continuity with the correct answer, making it more likely that the previous question would have been answered correctly. However, the raw record does not effectively reflect this, possibly due to a slipping factor.
ii) If a student answers the previous question correctly and the next question incorrectly, but performs very poorly on both related and unrelated questions within an appropriate distance, the response state for the previous question should maintain continuity with the incorrect answer, making it more likely that the previous question would have been answered incorrectly. However, the raw record does not effectively capture this, possibly due to guessing factors.

To address the above issues, we propose the CRDP-KT model, which uses dynamic programming algorithms to optimize the representation of records. At the same time, the CRDP-KT model introduces a bipartite graph to establish relationships between different questions. Through training, it generates relationship embeddings and integrates them with the optimized records to form an optimal combination of representations for different question records, collectively capturing the student’s cognitive state within a particular knowledge domain.

First, we designed a cognitive representation optimization module based on dynamic programming algorithms, which enhances the representation of cognitive states by considering exercise difficulty and learning performance across questions. This enables the cognitive representation to maintain coherence and continuity throughout the overall learning process.

Second, considering the staged and limited nature of students’ cognition, we designed a partition-based optimization module that divides the student’s exercise sequence into partitions and optimizes each partition, ensuring the reliability of the overall optimization process

The CRDP-KT model constructs relationships between questions using a bipartite graph, generating relationship embeddings that are combined with optimized record representations. This allows for the effective aggregation of optimal solution representations across different knowledge points, better reflecting the student’s cognitive state. This approach enhances the model’s ability to track learning state while leveraging the bipartite graph’s advantages to provide more accurate and in-depth analysis of the complex associations between knowledge points and the student’s individual cognitive traits.

Finally, experiments conducted on three public datasets demonstrate that the CRDP-KT model effectively improves the expression capability of cognitive representations. Additionally, we performed ablation experiments on the various modules and key factors of CRDP-KT, which also highlighted the effectiveness and superior performance of each module and factor in enhancing the representation of cognitive states.

II-B

Multiple Learning Behaviors and Multi-feature Embedding

Xu et al.
[

Although LBKT provides valuable insights, it also has some limitations. Specifically, LBKT treats learning behaviors as static and does not take into account dynamic adjustments based on students’ subsequent actions. Additionally, LBKT overlooks important interfering factors, such as guessing and slipping, which can lead to inaccurate assessments of a student’s true knowledge state. In contrast to the representation of multiple learning behaviors, multi-feature representations have also gained attention. Huang et al.

XKT incorporates several innovative features, including multi-feature embedding, a cognition processing network, and an MIRT-based neural predictor, which work together to provide more accurate and interpretable predictions of student performance, thereby enhancing the overall effectiveness of knowledge tracing systems.

II-D

Summary

Previous knowledge tracing methods have primarily focused on enhancing features, often neglecting the crucial role of record representations in accurately reflecting students’ cognitive states and the influence of non-cognitive factors. This oversight has led to challenges in the coherence and consistency of the models. Additionally, these methods tend to overestimate the expressive power of raw records and fail to capture the subtle progress in student learning. To address these issues, this paper introduces the CRDP-KT (Cognitive Representation Dynamic Programming Knowledge Tracing) model. The CRDP-KT model utilizes dynamic programming to optimize cognitive representations, ensuring a more coherent and consistent representation of the learning process by simultaneously considering both student performance and question difficulty. Furthermore, the model takes into account the stages and limitations of student cognition, optimizing interaction sequences to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the overall optimization phase. At the same time, CRDP-KT model is the use of bipartite graph learning, which captures the complex relationships between problems and interactions. This significantly enhances the model’s ability to express the evolution of students’ cognitive states over time. Empirical results show that the CRDP-KT model outperforms previous knowledge tracing models, offering improved accuracy in predicting student performance and better interpretability of learning behaviors.

u_{1}^{k}=1

\xi_{1}^{k}<\xi_{k+2}^{0}
, and
\xi\in\{0,1\}

\xi_{1}^{k}=0
,
\xi_{k+2}^{0}=1
, so its next state is
\xi_{1}^{k+1}=0+1=1

u_{1}^{k}=-1

\xi_{1}^{k}>\xi_{k+2}^{0}

\xi\in\{0,1\}

\xi_{k+2}^{0}=0

\xi_{1}^{k+1}=1-1=0

IV-B

Cost Calculation Based on Interaction Sequences

We minimize the cost of recording deficiencies by adjusting the response state of the questions, aiming to determine the optimal historical representation based on the student’s subsequent performance. Although students may correctly answer harder questions in the past—possibly due to guessing—if they then repeatedly answer the easier questions incorrectly, they are not considered capable of genuinely mastering the harder ones. In such cases, the question representation is deemed suboptimal. On the contrary, although student who answer even easier questions incorrectly (possibly by a slip factor), but they are able to repeatedly solve difficult problems later on. Therefore, we do not consider them truly incapable of answering easier questions, indicating that the question representation is not optimal.
The corresponding cost function is as follows:

J(\xi_{1}^{k})=\sum_{n=k}^{N}\gamma^{p-k}U(\xi_{1}^{p},u_{1}^{p}),

(16)

where
U
is the effective function and
\gamma
is the discount factor, set
0<\gamma\leq 1
. The effective function is as follows:

U(\xi_{1}^{p},u_{1}^{p})=|(\xi_{1}^{p}-u_{1}^{p})-\xi_{1}^{p}|=|-u_{1}^{p}|.

(17)

Starting from the
k
-th state in the first exercise, the objective is to count the number of discontinuities or incoherencies in the record representation that occur in subsequent states. A higher number of discontinuities or incoherencies corresponds to a greater cost, whereas fewer occurrences result in a lower cost. According to the principles of optimal control theory and dynamic programming, the optimal cost function seeks to minimize these disruptions over time. This cost function reflects the need for continuity and coherence in the representation, ensuring the most efficient trajectory through the system. According to the optimal control theory of dynamic programming, the optimal cost function is

J^{*}\big(\xi_{1}^{k}\big)=\min_{u_{1}^{k},u_{1}^{k+1},\ldots,u_{1}^{N}}\sum_{p=k}^{N}\gamma^{p-k}U\big(\xi_{1}^{p},u_{1}^{p}\big).

(18)

It can be written as:

\displaystyle J^{*}\big(\xi_{1}^{k}\big)=\min_{u_{1}^{k}}\{U\big(\xi_{1}^{k},u_{1}^{k}\big)+\gamma\min_{u_{1}^{k+1},u_{1}^{k+2},\ldots,u_{1}^{N}}\sum_{p=k+1}^{N}

(19)

\displaystyle\gamma^{p-k-1}U\big(\xi_{1}^{p},u_{1}^{p}\big)\}.

Thus,
J^{*}(\xi_{1}^{k})
satisfies the discrete sequence Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation:

J^{*}\big(\xi_{1}^{k}\big)=\min_{u_{1}^{k}}\{U\big(\xi_{1}^{k},u_{1}^{k}\big)+\gamma J^{*}\big(\xi_{1}^{k+1}\big)\}.

(20)

The corresponding optimal control
[
54
]
is as follows:

u^{*}\big(\xi_{1}^{k}\big)=\arg\min_{u_{1}^{k}}\{U\big(\xi_{1}^{k},u_{1}^{k}\big)+\gamma J^{*}\big(\xi_{1}^{k+1}\big)\}.

(21)

The corresponding optimal state is as follows:

\xi^{*}\big(\xi_{1}^{k}\big)=\arg\min_{\xi_{1}^{k}}\{U\big(\xi_{1}^{k},u_{1}^{k}\big)+\gamma J^{*}\big(\xi_{1}^{k+1}\big)\}.

(22)

IV-C

Subregion Optimizations

According to educational psychology theory, learning state and learning ability of students are characterized by distinct stages and limitations, with students exhibiting different states at various stages. This is also reflected in the sequence of student interactions. Questions within the same stage more accurately capture the continuity and consistency of responses, while those across stages tend to be intermittent. If two exercises are are spaced too far apart, learning performance between them becomes sparse and unfocused. Additionally, for the previous stage, we need to ensure the authenticity of the questions in the later stage to maintain the reliability of the algorithm. Therefore, we need to perform partitioned optimization to obtain the optimized representations for each stage and then optimize the entire process. The foundation of all our optimizations is based on dynamic programming algorithms (see sections
IV-A
and
IV-B
).
Let

\Omega=\left\{\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3},\omega_{4},\dots,\omega_{t}\right\},

(23)

where
\Omega
is the overall interval of the sequence of student interactions, which is divided into
t
intervals. Each interval is assigned
p
number of interactions, and if the last one is less than
p
, the residual amount is assigned to the last interval. We take out the sequences with the same knowledge points from the sequences and keep only their answer states and corresponding exercise difficulties, and finally get the state-difficulty sequence
I=\{I_{1},I_{2},I_{3},I_{4},\dots,I_{t}\}
with different state-difficulty sequences assigned to each interval. Next, each interval is optimized separately. The same optimization process is performed for each interval, and the optimization process is described in sections
IV-A
and
IV-B
. After each interval is optimized, an overall optimization is performed, and finally, we get the optimal solution for the cognitive representation.

IV-D

Evaluation and Prediction

Bipartite graph training establishes relationships between related problems, merging optimized cognitive records to reflect the student’s cognitive state, forming an optimal matching representation that reflects the relationships between problems. Our assessment task then fuses the optimized cognitive representations with embeddings. The hidden layer
h_{t-1}
, generated from input
x_{t-1}
, predicts
\hat{y}_{t}
, which is compared to the true result
y_{t}
. The closer the two are, the better the performance.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we present the CRDP-KT model to improve students’ cognitive representations. Unlike previous models, it focuses on accurately representing raw cognitive states and refines them using a dynamic programming algorithm, ensuring coherence and continuity . The model also uses a bipartite graph to create relational embeddings, enhancing cognitive expression. We tested the model on three public datasets, comparing it with seven state-of-the-art methods, and achieved top performance. Despite strong experimental results, real-world educational applications face challenges. Future research could expand the model to tasks like personalized learning and student behavior prediction, and include non-cognitive factors like emotions and motivations for better results. We also aim to improve computational efficiency for large datasets.
