Gossypiboma mimicking a hydatid cyst: A case report
Authors: S Bourabaa, H El Hamdani, M Hamid, M Zhim, A Settaf
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRetained foreign bodies have become very rare in countries where the safety rules in the operating theater are very rigorous and follow precise guidelines. Maintaining awareness of this issue among surgeons and radiologists is of paramount importance to minimize avoidable morbidity and facilitate the selection of the most suitable therapeutic strategy. This consideration is particularly relevant in the differential diagnosis of hydatid cysts. This study describes a case of intra-abdominal gossypiboma, which mimicked hydatid cyst on preoperative assessment.Case presentationWe report the case of a 63-year-old female who was referred to our department for the management of a hepatic hydatid cyst. She has history of open cholecystectomy and oophorocystectomy. During the intervention, we discovered a 10 cm mass located in the interhepatogastric region. Complete resection of the mass was performed, and pathology results were compatible with a piece of gauze surrounded by reactive changes (gossypiboma).DiscussionGossypiboma is undeniably a source of concern for surgeons. It's a genuine and serious surgical complication which can potentially arise from any type of surgery and may manifest with diverse complaints. However, it is crucial to emphasize that this complication is preventable with the primary preventive measure being meticulous counting of surgical materials during the procedure.ConclusionThe potential embarrassment experienced by the surgeon and the significant legal consequences associated with this iatrogenic complication are substantial. Hence, it becomes imperative to adopt all requisite preventive measures to avert such incidents, as there is no excuse that can justify their occurrence.Keywords: Abdominal surgery, Case report, Hydatic cyst, Gossypiboma

Introduction

Textiloma is derived from textile (a woven fabric), gossypiboma is derived from Latin gossypium (the genus of cotton plants) and gauzoma is taken from surgical gauze [1]. All these three items are retained surgical items such as sponge, instrument, tool or device that are accidentally left in the patient at the completion of a surgery or other procedure. This iatrogenic, unintentional yet preventable misfortune, which is unfortunately often under-reported, can result in substantial morbidity, affecting nearly 50 % of patients, and carries a significant mortality rate ranging from 11 % to 35 % [2], and entails embarrassment as well as medico-legal consequences [3].

The incidence of gossypiboma is between 1 in 100 and 1 in 3000 for all surgical procedures [4], and 1 case in every 1000–1500 abdominal operations per year (most commonly complicated by its occurrence) [5]. The real incidence, however, may be higher because some patients remain asymptomatic and are never discovered, and because of the lack of documentation of some diagnosed cases, as well as the fear of legal repercussions [6].

Because of its rare occurrence and non-specific clinical and radiographic presentations, a gossypiboma is rarely considered and can be easily overlooked. We hereby report a case of a gossypiboma initially misdiagnosed as echinococcal cyst, which symptoms presented after 28 years.

This work has been reported in line with the SCARE 2020 criteria [7].

Case report

We present the case of a 63-year-old female patient, born and residing in the mountainous region of northern Morocco. She had undergone open cholecystectomy 30 years ago for acute lithiasic cholecystitis, and oophorocystectomy at age of 25 for ovarian cyst in another medical facility. She was referred by family physician to General Surgery Department for the management of hydatid cyst. She presented with epigastric pain from the last two years without any other specific symptoms. The abdominal examination revealed epigastric tenderness.

Ultrasound showed a hepatic mass with a dual cystic and fleshy component, located between segments II and III, with internal calcifications. Abdominal CT scan revealed a well-defined oval formation containing calcifications measuring 8 cm, in contact with the gastric antrum and the body of the pancreas medially and located posteriorly to the common hepatic artery without signs of invasion, suggestive of pseudo-tumoral hydatid cyst (Fig. 1A-B). Hepatic Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) showed a mass in liver segment IVb, roughly oval in shape, well-defined, with lobulated borders, heterogeneous T2 signal, no diffusion restriction, and no enhancement after Gadolinium injection, suggestive of a stage IV-V Gharbi hydatid cyst (Fig. 1-C). All laboratory values including hydatid serology were normal.

Radiological images showing an oval, well-defined, heterogeneous, partially calcified, avascular formation measuring 8 cm in the major axis, in contact with the gastric antrum and the body of the pancreas medially, and located posteriorly to the common hepatic artery, without signs of invasion, suggestive of pseudo-tumoral hydatid cyst. A: coronal CT scan slice. B: non-contrast (1) and contrast-enhanced (2) abdominal CT scan slice. C: MRI findings.

Open hepatic cyst excision was programmed. The intervention was made through a right subcostal incision extended to the left, with a bolster under the shoulders. During the surgical exploration, we discovered a cystic thick-walled mass in the interhepatogastric region, on the anterior surface of the left liver, adhering to the hepatic capsule and gastric serosa, measuring 10 × 5 cm. The mass was well dissected and completely resected using the bipolar forceps and the thermofusion forceps. The abdomen was closed with all precautions and counts of surgical items.

In accordance with medical ethics, the specimen was shown to the patient and her family. We explained to them that this is a rare but possible surgical complication, particularly in our countries with limited resources. We subsequently informed the hospital where she was previously operated on to strengthen preventive measures in order to avoid this accident in the future. The patient made an uneventful recovery and was discharged on post-operative day 4. She was seen one month later and was in good health with resumption of daily activities.

Results

Gross examination of the mass showed a cystic formation (Fig. 2). Upon sectioning, the presence of textiloma was observed. Microscopically, necrotic-hemorrhagic changes were observed along with a polymorphic inflammatory reaction, characterized by the development of giant cells around textile fibers.

Macroscopic aspect of an open surgical specimen, showing gaps and vacuoles, fulfilled by purulent sterile secretion and the presence of a partially degraded textile foreign body.

Discussion

The first description of gossypiboma was reported by Wilson in 1884 [8]. The literature review (117 published cases from 1952 to 1993) emphasizes the predominance of intraperitoneal gossypibomas (52 %), but other sites are also involved: gynecological (22 %); urological and vascular (10 %); orthopedic and spinal (6 %) [9].

There are two physiological responses leading to detection of gossypiboma. The acute form, in which an exudative reaction dominates the clinical picture, along with abscess formation and skin fistulas, is symptomatic in the early postoperative period. The differential diagnosis in such cases includes postoperative collection, hematoma and non-foreign-body abscess. The second type is the chronic form, as seen in our patient, adhesions and encapsulation eventually lead to the development of a foreign-body granuloma, and the lesion may present as a mass. In such cases, the differential diagnosis typically includes tumors [10]. Generally, gossypibomas are inert and do not undergo any decomposition. They provoke an aseptic granuloma with a surrounding inflammatory reaction leading to a complete encapsulation with no clinical manifestation. These forms are not uncommon and most often discovered fortuitously many years after the surgery. Some published cases were diagnosed after forty years [11]. Depending on location within the peritoneal space, clinical symptomatology may vary from mild abdominal pain to serious complications such as bowel or visceral perforation, obstruction, fistula formation, or sepsis [12].

CT and MRI data were compatible but not specific to gossypibomas; this is mainly due to radiological variations depending on their location and chronicity, and the type of reactions that they cause in the body; sometimes, it may be misdiagnosed as a haematoma, neoplastic lesion or hydatid disease.

Therapy consists of the removal of the gossypiboma on laparoscopy or laparotomy with treatment of complications. Only reoperation allows a definitive diagnosis [13]. Laparotomy was used to remove the gossypiboma in our case and traditionally, the open approach has been more commonly used compared to the laparoscopic approach. This was due to the belief that intense foreign body reaction and dense adhesions around the sponge might make the laparoscopic approach challenging [14].

In the literature review by Le Neel et al. [15], the excision of the gossypiboma indeed resulted in a complication-free recovery in 70 patients (59.8 %). However, complications worsened the condition of 25 patients (21.3 %), and 22 patients died (18.9 %). Twenty-one out of the 22 deaths can be attributed to abdominal gossypibomas and involve symptomatic gossypibomas that were recognized late and required more aggressive measures such as intestinal and/or colonic resection, with a significant percentage of severe complications, particularly sepsis.

From a medico-legal point of view, forgetting a foreign body during surgery most often constitutes a fault for the judge, especially since its consequences can be serious on the patient. The finding of a textiloma always calls the responsibility of the surgeon into question because it is an involuntary personal fault. The surgeon could be condemned for compensation of the victim or his heirs. He may also face criminal penalties according to the conclusions of the expert who carried out the audit [16].

In our case, the patient had accepted that this was an unintended surgical complication that could happen to any surgeon. There had been no claims. Thus honesty and respect for medical ethics also make it possible to protect colleagues against claims.

Gossypiboma is associated with various factors such as complicated surgeries involving multiple teams and procedures, which increase the risk of information loss with each change of staff. Emergency surgical procedures also pose a risk as sponge and instrument counting may not be a high priority. Other factors include obesity, poor communication among the team, incorrect or failed surgical count, and high blood loss intraoperatively [17]. In our case, the previous surgery was done in another hospital and therefore we could not find the associated factors which lead to retained gauze other than obesity (BMI equal to 33).

In an attempt to reduce the number of cases of retained sponges, the World Health Organization (WHO) launched the World Alliance for Patient Safety program in 2004, followed by the Second Global Patient Safety Challenge, titled ‘Safe Surgery Saves Lives’ in 2008. Participants discussed, among other objectives, how to prevent sponges or other instruments in the surgical wound from being inadvertently retained. This was later included in the WHO Surgery Safety Checklist (2009) and the WHO Surgery Checklist Implementation Manual (2009). New tools as bar codes automated counting systems and counters, radiofrequency labelled sponges and radio frequency readers can avoid manual errors in counting [18,19].

Conclusion

Prevention of gossypiboma is far more important than cure. Although gossypiboma is relatively rare, wide awareness of this problem in mandatory to avoid unnecessary morbidity.

It is crucial to take all necessary measures to prevent its occurrence. The embarrassment faced by the surgeon and the medicolegal implications of this iatrogenic complication are tremendous and all preventive measures should be taken to avoid this as no excuse is justifiable.

Strict adherence to swab counts as recommended in the guidelines from the Association of Perioperative Registered Nurses (AORN) [20], and avoiding staff changes during procedures is important in decreasing the incidence of gossypiboma. Additionally, utilizing minimally invasive accesses, simple precautions such as theater staff regular education, following the WHO checklist should be the way to ensure patient safety and minimize the risk of such complications. Gossypiboma could change any pre-operative diagnosis, so it should be in the list of any differential diagnosis when the patient has a previous procedure or surgery.
