Enhancing Drug Response Prediction in Epilepsy with Emerging Multimodal Models: Focus on Clinical, Pharmacologic, and Genomic Factors
Authors: Alison Anderson, Richard Shek-kwan Chang, Mark Howard, Zhibin Chen, Duong Nhu, Zongyuan Ge, Patrick Kwan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Epilepsy is a common neurological disorder, with approximately one-third of the affected population developing drug-resistant epilepsy despite the exponentially increasing number of antiseizure medications (ASMs) that are available. Current ASM selection remains largely empirical, lacking reliable biomarkers to predict treatment response. Pharmacogenomics is an important factor underlying drug response. The development of models designed to predict drug response in epilepsy that include genomic information is at an early stage. Advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and deep learning (DL) offer new opportunities for multimodality modelling and, in particular, models that can reason over both a patient’s genomic risk factors and their clinical features (e.g. epilepsy type, magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]/electroencephalography [EEG] findings). More broadly, new technologies are driving a paradigm shift towards agentic AI, whereby decision-support agents will coordinate medical information and propose actions. These advances have implications for individualised ASM prescribing in epilepsy. For example, rather than modelling thousands of genetic variants, a DL model can operate with compressed representations of data obtained from AI foundation models that have been trained on huge atlases to learn representations of general biology, enhancing capacity for small epilepsy cohorts. In this review, we summarise how emerging technologies are being harnessed and highlight strategies for building capacity. We specifically focus on clinical, pharmacological, and genomic data, discussing the ways in which drug information can be formatted to take advantage of DL model capabilities. We describe AI foundation models and provide examples of how they can be utilised in epilepsy research. We conclude with a summary of ongoing challenges, including model evaluation, data availability, ethical considerations, and barriers to clinical translation.

Key Points

Artificial intelligence models for predicting individual treatment are rapidly evolving.We have shown that models that can reason across both clinical and genomic data are transformative for clinical care.In the future, artificial intelligence models could make it easier to match patients with the seizure medication that will work best for them.Transformative progress will require collaborative, standardised data collection to improve sample sizes and methodological innovations that harness the full potential of the technology.

Introduction

Epilepsy is a common chronic neurological disorder worldwide, with a prevalence in the general population of about 10 per 1000 people [1]. More than 50 million people live with epilepsy, accounting for 13 million disability-adjusted life years globally [1, 2, 3]. Recurrent seizures can lead to physical injuries and psychosocial and cognitive dysfunction, while comorbidities such as learning disabilities and psychiatric disorders complicate epilepsy management. People with epilepsy have increased premature mortality, including sudden unexpected death in epilepsy.

Antiseizure medications (ASMs) remain the primary treatment modality for epilepsy. The number of ASMs available has significantly increased in the past 2 decades, with more than 25 on the market currently [4]. However, these new ASMs have not demonstrated superior efficacy compared with their older counterparts. The proportion of people with drug-resistant epilepsy has remained stable at around 30% [5, 6]. Currently, the selection of ASM is largely empirical. The choice of drug depends on the clinician’s judgement, considering demographic and clinical factors, comorbidities, and potential drug interactions. If the initial ASM fails to control seizures, a second ASM may be added to or substituted for the first.

According to the definition of the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE), a diagnosis of drug-resistant epilepsy can be made if a patient does not achieve at least 12 months of sustained seizure freedom after adequate trials of two appropriate ASMs [7]. Currently, there are no reliable biomarkers available to predict ASM outcomes. Patients often undergo a trial-and-error process until the effective ASM(s) can be selected; it can take years to achieve the goal of seizure freedom or confirm the diagnosis of drug-resistant epilepsy.

The concept of personalised or precision medicine has gained significant attention in recent years [8]. A typical example is in the realm of oncology. Genetic and molecular profiling of tumours makes targeted therapies possible on an individual level. In the field of epilepsy, the application of personalised medicine is far from mature. It is envisaged that selection of ASM can be individualised according to multimodal information including demographic, clinical, radiological, electrophysiological, and even genetic characteristics [8].

Artificial intelligence (AI) refers to the capability of a machine to imitate intelligent human thinking and learning. AI has a wide potential application in medicine, including reducing errors, streamlining workflow, and reducing human resources. The application of AI in assisting clinical decision-making has been explored as early as in the 1970s when MYCIN was designed as a clinical decision support system software to facilitate the management of bacterial infections [9].

Machine learning (ML) is a branch of AI that helps to solve many complicated problems. ML can be broadly divided into supervised learning and unsupervised learning. In supervised learning, a human-labelled training dataset is used to train the ML algorithm. k-Nearest neighbours (kNN) and random forest are common examples of supervised learning. Unsupervised learning does not require a priori selection of potential predictor variables in model training. Rather, the model can extract features from raw data, recognising hidden patterns and associations. Deep learning is a more advanced type of ML. The algorithm structure is in the form of a multilayered artificial neural network. The inputs go through artificial neuron layers in a nonlinear way and map into outputs [10]. Depending on data type, specific deep learning architectures are applied to best learn hidden features, such as 2-dimensional convolutional neural network (2D-CNN) for images and 1D-CNN and transformer models for electroencephalography (EEG) and bio-signals from wearable devices [11, 12]. As the size of the training data grows, the number of layers can be increased to capture the complexity of the data and improve the performance.

In recent years, several ML tools specific to genomic information and prediction of treatment response have emerged. Transformer models, introduced in 2017, have self-attention mechanisms that allow them to decide which pieces of input information are most important to focus on. This capability enables them to more effectively capture context, meaning, and long-range relationships within data [13]. Large language models (LLMs) are language-based technologies with sophisticated deep learning architectures that are typically transformer based [14]. In genomics, sequences of DNA, known as k-mers, are tokenised as “words” for input into models that can then be trained to identify regions important for gene regulation and to predict the impact of genetic variation on the regulatory activity at these loci [15]. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) frameworks extend the reasoning capabilities of LLMs with dynamic access to external knowledge, enabling concise synthesis from trusted sources. This approach is particularly well-suited to the clinical interpretation of genetic variants, whereby evidence of functional effects must be integrated across diverse investigative methods [16].

Together these advances have led to the emergence of foundation models. Trained on very large data corpora, foundation models typically adopt a self-supervised learning approach, and can be adapted for many different downstream tasks [17]. Of particular interest for predicting individual response to medication are foundation models derived from DNA and RNA sequence data.

The capacity for AI to reason and predict across multiple complex biomedical inputs ushers in a new era of state-of-the-art medical modelling. Harnessing this technology to predict individual patient response to ASMs is a major focus of current research. A deep learning model that predicted response to ASMs for people with newly diagnosed epilepsy patients based on retrospective clinical information demonstrates the feasibility of this approach [18] (Table 1). A multisite, prospective, double-blind, randomised, controlled trial to prospectively evaluate this model is currently underway [19]. Results from three further studies indicate that training models on both genomic and clinical information improves predictive capacity [20–22]. Currently, multimodal modelling with clinical, pharmacological, and genomic data in epilepsy is in its infancy. To build capacity, this review takes a deep dive into data representation techniques and relevant emerging technologies. We further discuss challenges, including data availability, model evaluation, ethical considerations, and barriers to clinical translation. Clinical Features

Typical clinical features used to characterise a patient’s epilepsy include demographic information and medical history, such as the type of epilepsy, age at treatment initiation, and the number of seizures prior to treatment. Additional relevant clinical information may include intellectual disability, psychiatric disorders, a history of febrile seizures, childhood central nervous system infections, significant head trauma, hypoxic injury, or cerebrovascular disease. Lifestyle factors, such as alcohol or substance abuse, and a family history of epilepsy in a first-degree relative have also been used as input variables. Diagnostic test results, particularly EEG or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings, can further contribute to clinical features [18, 21, 22].

The ML method applied by Silva-Alves et al. [22] found the presence of hippocampal sclerosis as detected by MRI to be the best parameter for discriminating mesial temporal lobe epilepsy patients with poor response to ASMs. MRI findings were the top clinical contributor in a model that combined genomic and clinical features [21] and a main contributing factor in an earlier study by Hakeem et al. [18] that used the same clinical features to predicted success with the first ASM for individual epilepsy patients. Wang et al. [23] described classifiers to predict oxcarbazepine outcomes in newly diagnosed epilepsy by combining EEG complexities and 15 clinical features.

In many studies, clinical features were selected to train the ML models based on the current knowledge on ASM pharmacology. For example, in clinical practice, the selection of ASM is informed by the classification of an individual's epilepsy. Certain ASMs that modulate neuronal voltage-gated sodium channels such as carbamazepine or phenytoin can exacerbate myoclonic seizures. Age and sex are also important determinants of drug selection as, for instance, valproate should be avoided in women with child-bearing potential owing to its teratogenic risk. When selecting clinical features for inclusion in drug outcome prediction models, expert intuition based on clinical experience is often employed. This hypothesis-driven approach contrasts with the data-driven approach in which feature selection is determined by the model itself. The latter approach is often applied for high-dimensional genomic features or to extract features from molecular characteristics or descriptions of drugs, which are further discussed below.

Hypothesis-driven strategies can inadvertently exclude subtle, non-obvious interactions or higher-order relationships among variables. ML techniques have been developed to evaluate even large numbers of variables simultaneously specifically to uncover complex, nonlinear interactions that may be difficult to anticipate using traditional clinical reasoning alone. These strategies need not be mutually exclusive. Feng et al. [21], for example, employed a hybrid method where a hypothesis-driven approach was used to select genes and variants of interest, and further feature selection was applied by the attention heads within the transformer model architecture. In this way, prior biological knowledge constrained the input space, while the model retained the flexibility to identify higher-order interactions and context-specific relationships that may not have been pre-specified.

Quantifying the relative contributions of clinical variables by using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis, a unified approach to interpreting model predictions [24], not only provides an insight into these models but also contributes to our understanding of responsiveness to ASM. For example, Hakeem et al. [18] and de Jong et al. [20] both consistently confirmed psychiatric illness and seizure frequency play important roles in drug responsiveness.

Drug-Based Features

Molecular Features

The names of drugs are commonly used as inputs in predictive models. Deep learning models can, however, reason over complex molecular representations of drugs, including graphic networks that capture relationships between atoms and bonds, an often-overlooked opportunity to improve model performance. Information about drugs can be encapsulated and formatted in a range of different ways.

The choice of format can depend on the model architecture and the research question. A basic approach is to use the drug name or a simple numeric encoding such as a vector of 0s and 1s (one-hot encoding) as adopted by Feng et al. [21]. More information can be included using chemical descriptors known as “molecular fingerprints”. These fingerprints capture the properties of chemicals (e.g. atoms and bonds, functional group, or presence of chemical motifs) in a consistent form of numerical or bit-string representations with low computational cost and are thus ideal for ML [25]. Common types include PubChem, Molecular ACCess System, Mini FingerPrint, and SMIles FingerPrint [26–29].

Descriptors can be used directly as inputs to a model, using for instance the Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES) [30]. They can also be used to generate alternative model inputs that range from simple scores to represent how easy a molecule is to synthesise [31] to complex representations that are optimised for a specific research question.[31] For instance, to predict drug interaction with ion channels, Zheng et al. [32] generated novel descriptors derived from SMILES using the word2vec algorithm for generating word embeddings. Huang et al. [33] used three different molecular fingerprints to comprehensively characterise molecules at different levels of granularity for input into a CNN-based architecture. The developers of a deep learning method, SubCDR [34], designed to predict cancer drug resistance, used the breaking of retrosynthetically interesting chemical substructures (BRICS) method to extracts important substructures (subcomponents) from SMILES, and used them to construct a feature vector using the extended connectivity fingerprints (ECFPs) format. ECFPs are one of a class of topological fingerprints, originally developed for substructure and similarity searching, that can represent an infinite number of molecular features with high versatility [35, 36].

3D fingerprints encode 3D-shape features such as atomic coordinates, bond angles, and the spatial arrangement of pharmacophores: parts of a chemical that are responsible for biological or pharmacological activity, including interaction with target molecules. 3D fingerprints have demonstrated utility for identifying compounds with similar activity to existing drugs, or that mimic the activity of naturally occurring molecules such as hormones (drug-like mimetics) [37, 38]. Khan et al. [39] investigated how the structure of a drug impacts on gene expression in cancer cells using advanced 3D structural descriptors. A 4D fingerprint formalism is also available and includes a methodology parameter whereby the quantitative 3D model (e.g. conformation, alignment, and atom type) is optimised as a function of the methodology parameter [40]. This approach has been adapted for tasks such as drug repurposing analysis and predicting skin sensitisation by chemical compounds [41, 42].

Graph-based representations can capture structural information that may be lost when drugs are represented as strings. The RDKit is a piece of open-source chemical informatics software that can convert SMILES into drug-specific molecular graphs that reflect interaction between atoms within a drug [43]. This representation can be used with convolutional network architectures and has been applied to predict anticancer drug responsiveness [44].

The utility of molecular fingerprints within the context of ASM response prediction in epilepsy remains to be comprehensively explored. SMILES has been used in a study to investigate ASM outcome prediction using MRI scans and reports [45]. Molecular fingerprints may have potential utility in related research tasks such as in silico modelling for detecting drug–drug interactions, or virtual screening to select compounds for therapeutic evaluation in induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived cellular models of epilepsy.

Drug Synergy

Polytherapy involving the prescription of two or more ASMs is often used in the treatment of epilepsy. AI has been used to predict synergistic drug interactions, predominantly in the cancer field [46, 47]. DeepSynergy uses chemical and genomic features as inputs to a multi-layer perceptron model to predict synergistic drug combinations [48]. SYNDEEP harnesses a deep neural network architecture and a broader input feature set including protein–protein and protein–metabolite interactions, and Morgan fingerprint counts, which represent the number of times a particular substructure (e.g. aliphatic and aromatic rings, H-bond donors and receptors) is detected in a molecule [49]. The DGSSynADR model uses a graph structure and a graph convolutional network (GCN) for feature extraction to identify anticancer drug combinations [50]. The developers of SYNDEEP note that combining drug physiochemical features with genomic information significantly enhanced model performance. These or similar methods could be harnessed to explore synergy between ASMs.

Drug Toxicity

ASMs, like most drugs, can elicit severe adverse effects in some individuals. AI models used to predict the toxicity of chemicals could potentially be harnessed to evaluate risk of adverse reactions or to include this contextual information in drug response predictive models. Chemical space networks (CSNs) are random graphs that allow evaluation of network organisation, such as hubs and communities and metrics, such as degree and eigenvector centrality, with promising utility in predictive toxicity and the design of safer drugs [51].

These models predict toxicophores, also known as structural alerts, that associate chemical fragments with toxicological endpoints. It is considered that these graph representations outperform traditional methods that fail to exploit data pairwise relations [52]. For instance, Amoroso et al. [51] used CSNs to analyse network topology in developmental toxicity (abnormal development of offspring due to exposure to harmful agents) and to evaluate whether toxicophores within a community share specific drug targets. A graph generated from 684 toxic and 135 non-toxic chemicals contained three distinct communities, one of which comprised aryl derivatives mainly comprising barbiturates, hydantoins, and amino alcohols, commonly used as ASMs. The authors suggest that features derived from this model could be combined with physiochemical descriptors and fingerprints to enhance toxicity-related modelling. Mastrolorito et al. [52] used CSNs to provide interpretable results for putative toxicities, including developmental toxicity, skin irritation, and hepatoxicity, all of which have been associated with ASMs, with a model architecture that elucidated structural alteration related to a given toxicity.

Alternative approaches include an augmented GCN model that eliminates reliance on predefined molecular descriptors and fingerprints and a fragment-based graph transformer network for predicting reproductive toxicity [53, 54]. Overall, this technology is in its nascent stage, and further improvements can be expected. In epilepsy, combined modelling of ASMs and drugs of differing toxicities could elucidate converging pathways to inform investigations into genetic risk for nuanced and complex adverse responses to drugs such as reduced IQ, and other teratogenic effects.

Genomic-Based Features

The inclusion of DNA sequencing data in AI modelling is challenging given the high number of nucleotide bases, and the many different feature types present in the human genome. An important consideration is that the function of DNA sequences is dynamic and highly context dependent, meaning that the functional consequence of genetic variation is dependent on factors such as tissue and specific cell-type activity, with, for instance, some encoded proteins being essential for one tissue but functionally benign in others.

In terms of gene regulation, the consequence of variation is also dependent on conditions within the cellular environment, meaning that some biological mechanisms only occur, and become sensitive to genetic variation, in the presence of certain stimuli such as exposure to a drug. In this scenario, variants that modify a phenotypic trait, commonly referred to as expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL), are termed response eQTLs (reQTLs). This contextual nuance and complexity challenge the task of selecting DNA sequences for input to predictive models. DNA is essentially acontextual: the order of A, C, G, and T letters is the same in every cell of the body, and on their own, DNA sequences do not convey any information on how they might function in a particular tissue or cell type.

A common current strategy involves late integration, whereby DNA information, particularly findings from genome-wide association studies (GWASs), is used to look for variant enrichment within specific genes or pathways that have been elucidated through alternative modalities and, in particular, other omics data types. For instance, Wamsley et al. [55] linked changes occurring in the brain in people with autism spectrum disorder to genetic susceptibility by first identifying cell type-specific regulons (gene regulatory networks comprising transcription factors and their target genes) using single-nucleus RNA sequencing and single-nucleus assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing from postmortem brain tissue. They then determined the overlap of regulon genes with genes found to be association with autism spectrum disorder as reported in the SFARI Gene Module database, which catalogues evidence curated from published literature.

To more fully exploit the power of AI, early integration methods that allow algorithms to learn and reason across DNA and information from other data modalities simultaneously are desirable. This requires optimal ways in which to select variant spectra for modelling, or integration with relevant contextual information that will allow models to find biologically meaningful patterns in the data.

Dimensionality reduction is an important pre-modelling step. To date, studies that include genomic features in models to predict response to ASMs have adopted knowledge-based strategies to restrict the number of variants for analyses. This includes selection of variants that impacts on gene sets of interest, such as those associated with epilepsy and the pharmacogenomics of ASMs, genes involved in specific biological pathways or Gene Ontology terms, or genes that are associated with the mechanisms of action [20].

An early example of multi-gene pharmacogenomic modelling in epilepsy was provided by Petrovski et al. [56]. They developed a multi-single nucleotide polymorphism (multi-SNP) classifier to predict ASM outcomes in newly treated epilepsy. Using genotypes for 4041 SNPs across 279 candidate genes, they ranked the association between the SNPs and treatment outcomes by Golub score. Finally, five SNPs—two in SCN4B, one each in GABBR2, SLC1A3, and KCNQ1—were selected as inputs of the model. The kNN algorithm was used in the multi-SNP model, achieving a cross-validated predictive accuracy of 83.5% and outperforming any single-SNP model in two validation cohorts.

Shazadi et al. [57] then evaluated the generalisability of the same multi-SNP model in two large independent cohorts from another country. Direct validation was suboptimal, with the model failing to predict treatment response across the validation cohorts. However, a leave-one-out cross-validation within the validation cohorts revealed that the same five-SNP combination retained predictive power for patients treated with carbamazepine or valproate, but not for those receiving other ASMs. This suggested that while the original model did not generalise directly across populations, the underlying SNP panel may have drug-specific predictive power that warrants further exploration. Silva-Alves et al. [22] selected variants within 11 pharmacokinetic-related genes. Feng et al. [21] generated genomic features based on the known or predicted functional consequence of variants. de Jong et al. [20] evaluated the predictive performance of 38 gene-set features, a feature based on the burden of variants on a specific receptor targeted by the drug of interest, and a polygenic risk scores feature.

Polygenic Risk Scores

Polygenic risk scores represent the cumulative effect of variants associated with a phenotype, including pharmacogenomic phenotypes, based on results from GWASs. Polygenic risk score is considered an appropriate data reduction method for predicting drug response, because like many complex diseases, most drug responses are likely to be highly polygenic. However, it must also be considered that drug response is a complex phenotype with heterogeneous determinants (e.g. absorption, metabolism, or variants in drug target genes) that could dilute the genetic signal.

Methods for generating a polygenic risk score have evolved from the simple approach of taking a tally of the number of risk alleles for each individual to complex methods that take into consideration the variation in how much each variant contributes to a phenotype, the correlation among SNPs due to linage disequilibrium, and Bayesian regression methods that explicitly model causal effects to enhance performance [58, 59]. A key challenge for polygenic risk score analysis in pharmacogenomics is that the base cohorts required for summary statistics (effect sizes, standard errors, and p values) are predominantly disease based.

A polygenic risk score derived from disease-related base cohorts may fail to represent the full heritability of a drug response, with one specific example demonstrating that any polygenic risk score developed from disease GWASs explained at most 46% variability in low-density lipoprotein cholesterol response [59]. The development of polygenic risk scores from pharmacogenomics studies is challenged by reduced power due to the low availability of this type of study [59]. Simulation studies suggest that a method that leverages the power of both risk scores derived from disease-related base cohorts and the predictive capacity of scores derived from pharmacogenomics-specific studies improves prediction accuracy. Whether this combined approach or either base cohort used alone is best remains unclear and will ultimately depend on data availability [59]. Polygenic risk scores are inherently population dependent, and this limitation can be exacerbated when sample sizes are modest.

A lack of non-European GWAS data means that cross-population prediction is also challenging. Methods for trans-ethnic analysis developed for disease-related polygenic risk scores can be applied to enhance performance [59]. Polygenic risk scores that are constructed from multiple related disease types, and the inclusion of SNPs with low statistical significance as compared to selection based on high significance thresholds (p value < 5 × 10−8), may also improve performance [58, 59].

To generate polygenic risk scores as an input feature for their prediction models, de Jong et al. [20] used summary statistics from epilepsy phenotype-related SNPs obtained from the PheWAS Catalog. How the polygenic risk score feature contributed to the model was not reported, but based on SHAP analysis of their best model, the strongest genomic feature predictors were the presence of variants overlapping SV2A, the receptor targeted by their drug of interest (brivaracetam), which negatively impacted the probability of response, and a Gene Ontology gene set, comprising genes associated with microtubule binding, which positively impacted response.

Functional Consequence

Silva-Alves et al. combined 56 SNPs with clinical variables to increase accuracy from 0.4568 (clinical variables only) to 0.8177 in a random forest algorithm for predicting drug response in patients with drug-resistant mesial temporal lobe epilepsy [22]. This study focussed on SNPs that impact on drug metabolism and transport, and an SNP within the CYP1A2 gene was found to be the most important variable in the prediction model. Feng et al. [21] applied an alternative approach whereby variants were selected based on evidence that they impact on the expression of genes (based on eQTL data) or modify the binding affinity of transcription factors that play a critical role in gene regulation, the rationale being that these mechanisms mediate cellular response to drug exposure and are likely sensitive to reQTLs. A published AI tool was used to generate scores representing the likelihood that a variant will impact the binding affinity of one or more of 515 transcription factors genome wide, and the feature constructed using these scores gave the best predictive performance when modelled in combination with clinical information. Overall, these findings confirm that the summation of variant burden is a useful approach and that experimentation with different approaches for selecting sub-sets for aggregation can elucidate strong predictors.

Input Format and Model Architecture

As with drug information, genomic information can be formatted in different ways to optimise model performance. Many models designed to predict response to cancer drugs encode somatic variants as 1 for mutation and 0 for wildtype, and germline genotypes are typically encoded as 0 for non-carriers, 1 for heterozygous carriers, and 2 for homozygotes. The summed values can be used to construct matrices comprising, for instance, individual gene or gene-set burdens [20, 22]. Depending on the model architecture, noise reduction and further feature selection can be achieved. Transformer models with attention mechanisms, for example, allow the model to dynamically focus on, and weigh the importance of, different data points within an input when producing an output, allowing irrelevant data to be suppressed [60, 61]. Li and Kalofonou [62] improved performance for cancer drug response prediction by including a t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding method, to dimensionality reduce DNA mutation and copy number information in a deep neural network architecture.

Biological systems are inherently interconnected, with gene/protein complexes or interactions between enhancer and promoter regions conferring combinatorial effects. These interactions can be encapsulated within graphs/networks for graph-based modelling. Graph-based models have been adopted in the cancer domain to predict drug response [44, 63, 64].

Multimodal Data Resources

In epilepsy, AI models are predominantly trained and tested using data from patient cohorts. Distinct cohorts are commonly pooled to enhance statistical power or used to test the generalisability of a model. In the cancer domain, the construction of large-scale public multimodal data corpora has driven innovation in modelling techniques. [65–67]. Specific to neuroscience, the PsychENCODE Consortium established a data compendium to enhance capacity to predict the modifying effects of genomic variation within noncoding regions of DNA, and improve understanding of how it might contribute to psychiatric disorders [68]. This infrastructure provides strategic and methodological guidance for capacity building in epilepsy research. The epigenetic and transcriptomic landscape was profiled from more than 2000 human postmortem brain samples covering a variety of brain regions and pathological states. Tens of thousands of DNA regions that contain gene regulatory elements that are active in the brain were identified. This data compendium includes single-cell-level data, profiles for different stages throughout foetal development, and data from an exploration of the molecular effects of antipsychotics in the human brain [69, 70].

Foundation Models

Massive archives of data generated from single-cell RNA sequencing, known as single-cell transcriptomics, allow models to learn what has been termed the “language of cells” [71]. These resources have been exploited for the development of foundation models, a type of model that learns representations of general biology that can, in turn, be applied to more topic-specific models [72].

In a similar fashion, models trained on genome-wide functional genomics data can generalise beyond the training dataset with wide-ranging application. This is because the general knowledge is captured as embeddings, vector representations in the foundation model, that can be used as input for topic-specific models for varied downstream tasks and research questions. This method of taking knowledge (learned features, weights, representations) from a foundation model and applying it to related tasks is a form of transfer learning. In epilepsy, foundation models can be used to prioritise genetic variants for diagnostics or predictive modelling by, for example, identifying the cell types (neuron or glial) where variants will have most impact or generating scores that represent variant impact on regulatory mechanism, or extracting pretrained embeddings to use as genomic features.

The scFoundation model was pretrained on over 50 million single-cell RNA sequencing data to model 19,264 genes with ~100 million parameters [71]. The outputs of the encoder were pooled into cell-level embeddings that were used as substitutes for gene expression profiles for tissue cancer drug response prediction and single-cell cancer drug response classification [71]. The Geneformer foundation model was trained to identify context-specific gene networks to enable context-specific predictions, whereby each input gene was embedded into a 256-dimensional space that encodes the characteristics of the gene specific to the context of each input cell [73]. scGPT is a generative foundation model that draws parallels between natural language processing and cellular biology modelling [74].

Generative models are designed to predict the next token in a sequence, such as predicting the correct word to fill in the blank in a given sentence or, in a biological context, predict amino acids in a protein sequence [72]. Similarly, scBERT treats genes like tokens and cells like sentences, and was pretrained on 1 million unannotated single-cell RNA sequencing data for downstream cell type annotation tasks [75]. GenePT leverages ChatGPT embeddings of genes, based on text descriptions of individual genes in the literature, and claims comparable and often better performance than data-based models such as Geneformer and scGPT [76].

Specific to DNA data, the Enformer foundation model, was trained to predict chromatin states and gene expression from genomic DNA sequences. This involved the prediction of enhancer–promoter interactions [77]. Dalla-Torre et al. [78] developed Nucleotide Transformer, which comprises multiple foundation models pretrained on 3202 diverse human genomes and 850 genomes from various species, with between 500 million and 2.5 billion parameters. The models can be fine-tuned at low cost for specific downstream tasks and used for transfer learning.

In ML, the term “zero-shot” refers to the ability of a model to apply prior knowledge learned during pretraining to unseen tasks. An example would be to make predictions without having seen labelled examples for a specific task. Zero-shot scores are used to measure how good a model is at predicting a task it has not been trained on and are based on embedding comparisons (e.g. cosine similarity between embeddings).

An example is the classification of genetic variants (benign vs. likely deleterious), where the model is not trained to predict the classes, but is trained to produce embeddings that represent the context of nucleotide sequences, in terms of conservation, protein coding, or regulatory potential [80]. The difference in what is learned from wildtype and mutated DNA sequences can, however, be used as a proxy for the functional consequence of the latter. This is achieved by embedding DNA sequences that contain variations, and generating scores based on the shift in embeddings derived from these data and the original wildtype data for each genetic variation.

The authors of the Nucleotide Transformer used this approach to show that the Multispecies 2.5B model performed well, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.80, for predicting genetic variants annotated as pathogenic in the ClinVar database [78]. This methodology could be adopted to refine polygenic risk scores or other variant groupings prior to input into models for AI-guided ASM prescribing in patients with epilepsy.

Comparison of zero-shot scores derived from eQTL variants and those that are linked to DNA methylation, known as (meQTLs), found the best-performing models were those trained on human sequences only. In comparison, identification of pathogenic variants gave better performance in multispecies models. Across models, the lowest cosine similarity scores were assigned to synonymous as well as protein-disruptive (e.g. missense and stop-gained) variants. This is an interesting observation, as synonymous variants have long been considered to be silent and are regularly filtered out in variant curation pipelines despite being increasingly implicated in disease. They have also been reported to have other functional effects, including modifying drug response [79]. A synonymous variant within the ABCB1/MDR1 gene implicated in multidrug resistance, for instance, confers subtle but functionally important effects that are difficult to detect with standard assays [80]. The ability of the Nucleotide Transformer to identify synonymous variants with functional importance could improve on current variant selection methods that depend upon knowledge-based annotations or statistical frequencies.

In summary, foundation models are trained on huge atlases to provide a learned manifold of biologically plausible states. In transcriptomics, they learn patterns including gene–gene co-regulation structure, cell-state (e.g. glial cell activation states form gradients, and normal neuronal states occupy structured regions), and regulatory hierarchies (developmental trajectories form arcs). Cells from epilepsy patients can be passed through these pretrained embeddings to detect divergence from canonical landscapes. This allows us to ask research questions such as (1) do focal and generalised epilepsy show fundamentally different shifts when compared to the same foundation model embedding, or do convergent pathways emerge; or (2) do embedding shifts correlate with seizure frequency or associate with drug resistance.

From a genomic perspective, we can compare patterns in variant consequence prediction scores between drug-responsive and drug-resistant patients or determine whether patients with focal epilepsy show stronger disruption to specific enhancers while generalised epilepsy patients show widespread neuronal regulatory perturbation. These questions have implications not only for constructing genomic features for modelling, but also therapeutic strategies or patient stratification for trials. Furthermore, the capacity of deep learning models to operate with compressed representations of high-dimensional data enhances capacity for analysis with small epilepsy cohorts. Thus, foundation models not specifically trained on epilepsy can indirectly support epilepsy research.

Model Performance

As with all technological advances, the development of model architecture and methods are outpacing capacity for effective evaluation. The Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD)+AI checklist is a tool to assist authors to report studies in a way that allows policymakers, end users, and patients to understand the data, methods, findings, and conclusions of AI-driven research [81].

Crucially, the TRIPOD+AI guideline prioritises generalisability, explicitly encouraging external and temporal validation over simple random splitting. It also requires reporting discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. Well-calibrated probability estimates are essential where AI tools are used in the clinical setting for shared decision-making, for example, to guide ASM selection at treatment initiation. Furthermore, to move toward prospective validation, decision curve analysis should be included in addition to traditional performance metrics (AUC, specificity, precision, and F1 scores) to evaluate the performance of predictive models. Most importantly, performance metrics must be contextualised by clinically relevant epilepsy outcomes. In predicting ASM response, AUC alone is insufficient without high precision to minimise the “trial-and-error” burden of ineffective prescriptions. Conversely, high sensitivity is requisite to ensure potential responders are not prematurely classified as drug resistant and denied effective therapy.

Calibration analysis (e.g. calibration plots, Brier score, expected calibration error) assesses whether predicted probabilities accurately reflect observed outcomes, which is critical for risk-based decision-making. Decision curve analysis further evaluates the net clinical benefit of the model across a range of threshold probabilities, demonstrating whether its use would improve treatment decisions compared with standard strategies (e.g. treat all or treat none).

There is no universal standing in selecting performance metrics. Although the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) is common employed to evaluate machine models, it can be misleading when the dataset is imbalanced. Ideally, a model is both sensitive and specific, but in reality, it is often a trade-off by threshold selection.

In epilepsy, sometimes it is desirable to have a model with high sensitivity, for example, in prediction of sudden death in epilepsy (SUDEP). Patients with SUDEP potential can be identified and lifesaving preventive measures can be applied. However, in the case of predicting drug-resistant epilepsy, specificity may be preferable as costly or invasive investigations may be triggered in positively predicted cases.

Hence, while it is advisable for model developers to provide the performance metrics as comprehensively as possible, readers should interpret these metrics critically in light of the clinical context, instead of just scanning through the apparent figures.

Clinical interpretation: Even seemingly modest improvements in prediction, such as a 5% gain in identifying patients likely to achieve seizure freedom, may be clinically and economically meaningful. Epilepsy is associated with substantial morbidity, excess mortality, and societal cost, and modelling of the Australian working-age epilepsy population has demonstrated that increasing seizure freedom from approximately 70 to 75%, an absolute gain of 5%, is associated with billions of dollars in retained productivity over a lifetime horizon. A 10% increase in seizure freedom, to 80%, was estimated to prevent approximately 1600 premature deaths and generate over 1 billion Australian dollars in healthcare savings, alongside several billion dollars in productivity gains [84].

In this context, even a 5% improvement in predictive accuracy, if it translates into earlier and more appropriate therapy allocation, could meaningfully reduce time to seizure control, lower cumulative healthcare costs, and improve survival and quality of life at the population level.

The Hakeem et al. [18] study relied solely on clinical variables to predict treatment outcomes. The observed drop in performance between training and validation suggests that clinical factors alone may be heterogeneous, overlapping, and insufficient to capture the full biological complexity of epilepsy. While such variables reflect established prognostic determinants, they likely represent only a partial view of the underlying disease mechanisms driving treatment response. Incorporating additional modalities, such as genomic data, quantitative EEG features, or raw neuroimaging inputs, may provide complementary biological signals and improve generalisability by capturing dimensions of variability that are not observable through routine clinical descriptors alone.

The persistent observation that approximately two-thirds of patients with newly diagnosed epilepsy achieve sustained seizure freedom with currently available ASMs raises the possibility of a therapeutic plateau, as this proportion has remained relatively stable despite decades of pharmacological innovation and increasingly sophisticated analytical methods. If this reflects an underlying biological constraint rather than limitations in modelling, then more complex algorithms alone may not increase the absolute proportion of responders. However, even within a potential ceiling, meaningful improvements remain achievable through precision allocation and a reframing of success metrics.

ML may not raise the overall remission rate beyond ~66%, but it can substantially improve who receives the right therapy earlier, thereby shortening time to seizure control, reducing exposure to ineffective medications, and limiting cumulative adverse effects. In this context, clinical progress is measured not by surpassing the population-level ceiling, but by optimising treatment trajectories and enhancing patient-centred outcomes such as faster remission, lower relapse risk, reduced seizure burden, and improved quality of life.

Feng et al. [21] applied their best model to 100 randomly selected participants in their development cohort of newly diagnosed epilepsy. This showed that if all the patients were administered the highest ranked ASMs by the model as the first ASM, their predicted mean probability of achieving seizure freedom (68%) would be substantially higher than the observed seizure-free rate (47.2%) in the cohort. Although indirect, this comparison implies that the model may have the potential to assist clinicians in selecting the most-effective treatment for individual patients.

Clinical Application

A pioneering, double-blind, randomised trial that will evaluate and provide class 1 evidence for the effectiveness for a decision-support tool for neurologists to select the first ASM for adults with newly diagnosed epilepsy is underway [19]. This study will provide robust estimates of risk rates and expected effect sizes to inform future developments, and strategic insights into adoption and ease of use. Trials of this nature are essential in the current climate whereby the future of medicine is trending towards agentic AI, whereby decision-support agents will coordinate medical information and propose actions.

The genomic features applied in the study by Feng et al. [21] can be easily adapted for flexible inclusion of genomic data in clinical decision-support tools. The variant positions (or aggregated positions) can be replaced by a numbered key, and the keys and associated scores (representing personalised risk) in text format can be uploaded to web-based AI-driven tools, avoiding direct use of sensitive genome-wide data. We envisage a future scenario whereby patient DNA can be sequenced at the point of care, and the burden of variation within DNA regions of interest rapidly assessed. Alternatively, the required genotypes can be obtained by interrogating pre-sequenced databases as countries implement genetic sequencing of newborns [85]. The genomic features can then be applied in a “plug and play” scenario whereby personalised, specific, predictive genomic features can be inputted into matched AI models that are trained to provide clinical question-specific advice.

Ongoing Challenges

Data availability: The large-scale data required for model training introduces technical and ethical challenges. Collaborative consortia can be logistically and financially challenging to establish but provide powerful enabling infrastructures and are the ultimate solution to the statistical limitations of small cohorts. In epilepsy, Epilepsy 25 (Epi25) is the largest whole-exome sequencing study of epilepsy to date, with more than 54,000 human exomes, comprising 20,979 deeply phenotyped patients from multiple genetic ancestry groups with diverse epilepsy subtypes and 33,444 controls [86].

Moving forward, the collection of whole-genome sequence data coupled with additional omic data could greatly expand capacity and provide insight into the role of cell type and regulatory DNA genetic perturbation in the epilepsies. For example, phase II of the PsychENCODE project [87] derived multiomics datasets from postmortem prefrontal cortex samples across 206 patients with neuropsychiatric disorders and 182 healthy controls, leading to integrative modelling that prioritised ∼250 disease-risk genes and drug targets with associated cell types.

Missing data can be a major limitation, particularly for genomics, which are not routinely collected. The inclusion of genomics has been shown to improve predictive performance in both epilepsy and cancer, despite low availability restricting sample sizes [66]. This issue may resolve over time, as reduced costs increase adoption of sequencing in the clinical setting. Institutional infrastructure that supports quality control and standardisation of data collections can improve capacity for data sharing.

Ethical considerations: Patient consent and privacy is of critical importance, particularly for genomic data, which carries risk of reidentification, and data protection laws can restrict data sharing [88]. Federated learning, whereby raw training data remains distributed across individual institutions, is one solution [61]. In this scenario, researchers at each data site train individual model parameters using their data and predefined hyperparameters (e.g. batch size, learning rate, number of layers or attention heads). The parameters are then encrypted and transmitted to a common server, where they are aggregated, and outcomes from the aggregated analysis are returned to each participating site [89].

Data bias and data discrimination are also important consideration in the use of genomic data in ML models. For instance, clinical prediction models have been found to underperform in women and ethnic and racial minorities [90]. As model complexity increases through the inclusion of additional modalities (especially when those modalities include complex technologies such as EEG and MRI), it is likely that persons in low-resource environments (locally and globally) will be disadvantaged and experience further marginalisation and exclusion from scientific advancements, which is a breach of their human rights [90].

The FUTURE-AI international consensus framework provides guidelines for trustworthy and deployable AI in healthcare. Recommendations cover strategies from design and data collection stages through to clinical deployment.

Interpretability: The ability to interpret model outputs is critical for clinical translation, as treatment decisions in epilepsy directly affect long-term patient outcomes. While the SHAP method is widely used to provide feature-level attribution in tabular models, the applicability is largely limited to structured clinical or genomic variables. For models that learn arbitrary or high-dimensional representations, such as latent embeddings derived from EEG, imaging, or multimodal fusion, direct attribution becomes substantially more challenging, and verbose mechanistic explanations may not be feasible. In such settings, trust can be supported through complementary evaluation strategies that emphasise reliability and clinical utility rather than feature-level interpretation alone.

Calibration analysis and decision curve analysis can help demonstrate clinical utility and provide objective, clinically meaningful evidence that supports confidence in AI systems even when internal representations are not directly interpretable.

Medication modelling: Published models tend to focus on commonly used medications. This is probably due to the limited availability of clinical data. More uncommon but effective options may be overlooked in personalised medicine. Most existing studies evaluate treatment response at a single clinical time point, such as early prediction of drug resistance at diagnosis or seizure freedom at a predefined follow-up. Although some models predict outcomes after treatment initiation, they typically rely on static measurements captured at one time point and do not incorporate longitudinal dynamics. Meanwhile, growing evidence indicates that brain states and autonomic physiology evolve over time following drug administration, suggesting that treatment response is inherently temporal [2, 3]. Where data are available, explicit modelling of these trajectories could improve predictive performance and enable earlier, more informed clinical decision-making.

Phenotype variability: It will be important to determine how differences in epilepsy types and aetiologies impact on predictive modelling. In the study by Feng et al. [21], generalisability was tested by training a model on both newly diagnosed focal and generalised epilepsy patients or on focal only and validating in an independent cohort of newly diagnosed focal epilepsy patients. In both instances, multimodals outperformed unimodals, AUC values were similar, and the same genetic feature gave the best performance.

Comprehensive clinical feature representation is essential for building reliable prognostic models in epilepsy. Hakeem et al. [18] incorporated 16 clinical variables that comprehensively captured established epilepsy-associated prognostic factors, including seizure type, EEG abnormalities, neuroimaging findings, demographic characteristics, and treatment-related features. These variables reflect the core determinants of treatment response identified in prior epidemiological and clinical research.

Importantly, the deep learning model was not limited to evaluating these factors independently; rather, it learned complex, non-linear interactions and higher-order patterns across them. This allows the AI model to capture implicit relationships between drug type, age, developmental context, and other clinical characteristics without requiring manual specification of interaction terms. As such, heterogeneity related to different ASMs, age-dependent pharmacological effects, and epilepsy subtypes is learned directly from the data through the model’s representation learning process.

Most of the studies to date focused on adult rather than paediatric populations. There are a few studies involving children, particularly on tuberous sclerosis complex [23, 91, 92].

Conclusions

The development of AI-driven prediction models is a rapidly evolving research space. Large-scale experimental screening efforts that apply diverse investigative modalities across tissue and cell types, including induced pluripotent cell and organoid models, are providing, for the first time, rich contextual maps of cell function, under both normal and pathological conditions. There is also rapid advancement in AI-driven prediction of cell type-specific function of DNA sequences and how genetic variation can impact function. The ability to capture baseline or normal function provides critical “ground truth” for AI models. These emerging molecular foundation models replace reliance on existing gene annotation resources, canonical pathway information, and public literature that are often incomplete, biased towards well-researched genes such as the breast cancer genes, or lack information regarding cell type-specific function.

Multimodality models are transformative, allowing clinical data to be integrated not only with genomics but also information on the chemical characteristics and functional mechanisms of drugs. Multimodal modelling with clinical, pharmacological, and genomic data in epilepsy is in its infancy. Building capacity will require collaborative efforts to generate multimodal data repositories to address restrictions due to small cohort sizes and missing data. Technically, experimentation with different data representations, modelling techniques such as transfer learning, and base model types (e.g. transformers vs. LLMs) will be required to optimise predictive performance. Foundation models can provide indirect support in ways not previously envisaged. Robust evaluation of model performance and randomised controlled trials will be needed to establish benchmarks, while compliance with TRIPOD+AI and FUTURE-AI consensus guidelines will help to ensure ethical and effective clinical translation within an emerging agentic-AI-driven future.
