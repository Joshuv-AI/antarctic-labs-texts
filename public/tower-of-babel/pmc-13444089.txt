Prognostic significance of IDH1 promoter methylation and associated genome-wide alterations in breast cancer
Authors: Summayya Anwar, Azam Khan, Sadia Yasmin, Qurat Ulain, Alfar Ahmad, Erum Najeeb, Mirza Imran Shahzad, Muhammad Saeed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDysregulation of IDH1, a key metabolic enzyme, is widely reported across cancers and affects both tumor biology and patient prognosis. However, its regulation and associated epigenetic alterations in breast cancer (BC) remain unclear. This study examined methylation of two CpG islands (I1, I2) within the IDH1 promoter and a putative distal enhancer in clinical BC samples, correlating these patterns with IDH1 expression and clinicopathological features. Expression of PFKP, HIF-1α, and SIX1 was profiled to assess metabolic and developmental pathways. Additionally, genome-wide methylation analysis of IDH1-stratified TCGA-BRCA cohorts was performed to identify topological probe clusters with potential regulatory relevance. Twelve CpGs within I1 (− 109 to − 205 bp) were significantly hypermethylated in BC and showed an association with reduced IDH1 expression and a trend toward improved survival in this cohort, outperforming IDH1 expression in ROC analysis. I1 methylation was markedly higher in postmenopausal, metastatic, and TNBC cases and mapping to key transcription factor binding sites (CTCF, MYC, STAT3), suggesting disruption of regulatory complexes. PFKP, HIF-1α, and SIX1 were upregulated in these cases, indicating metabolic and developmental reprogramming. Genome-wide methylation analysis of IDH1-stratified TCGA cohorts revealed widespread alterations, with 33% of differentially methylated probes mapping to intergenic regions. High-density clusters on chromosomes 13 (85.7/kb) and 6 (32.5/kb), along with hypermethylation of CLDN18 and HRNBP3 promoters, implicated chromatin remodeling and splicing as frequent IDH1-associated events. Network analysis identified 332 hub genes, including HNRNPA1 and CBX5, reinforcing the role of epigenetic deregulation in BC pathogenesis. These findings suggest that IDH1 promoter methylation may hold prognostic relevance and suggest that altered IDH1 regulation contributes to broader epigenetic and chromatin remodeling events in BC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52790-3.Keywords: IDH1 methylation, Breast cancer, Promoter methylation, PFKP, HIF-1α, SIX1, PrognosisSubject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Genetics, Oncology

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women worldwide. According to the WHO, approximately 2.3 million cases were reported globally in 2020, resulting in 685,000 deaths 1.

Genetic and epigenetic alterations underpin cancer progression and significantly influence clinical outcomes 2,3. Aberrant methylation profiles in cis-regulatory regions of proto-oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes can disrupt critical gene networks, compromise the tumor microenvironment, and promote aggressive cancer phenotypes 4.

Isocitrate dehydrogenases (IDHs) are NADP-dependent enzymes involved in oxidative decarboxylation of isocitrate to alpha-ketoglutarate (α-KG) in the Krebs cycle 5. Mutant IDH1 converts α-KG to 2-hydroxyglutarate (2-HG) using NADPH, leading to 2-HG accumulation and inhibition of TET-mediated active demethylation 6,7. Metabolic reprogramming in cancer alters the availability of one‑carbon metabolites such as SAM, thereby influencing DNA methylation patterns at promoter regions of tumor‑associated genes 8.

Although IDH1 mutations occur in multiple tumor types, their prognostic relevance appears context‑dependent, showing favorable outcomes primarily in gliomas 8, whereas in acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) they do not independently predict prognosis 9. In BC, IDH1 mutations are rare 10, yet aberrant IDH1 expression disrupts cellular homeostasis. IDH1-deficient tissues exhibit heightened oxidative stress, emphasizing its role in cellular redox homeostasis 11, underscoring its central role in redox regulation. Therefore, regulation of IDH1 levels in proliferating cells is critical in carcinogenesis, suggesting that non-genetic factors such as promoter methylation may influence IDH1 activity and modulate the tumor microenvironment.

While IDH1 mutations have been linked to genome-wide methylation alterations in human cancers 12, the methylation status of its promoter and cis-regulatory elements, along with their prognostic implications remain poorly understood. A previous study reported that IDH1 hypermethylation is uncommon in gliomas 13, but its clinical relevance was not evaluated. Moreover, in BC, IDH1 promoter methylation profiles are largely unexplored, limiting insights into its regulatory roles and therapeutic potential.

The present study investigates IDH1 promoter methylation across proximal and distal regulatory elements to assess its clinical significance in BC. Furthermore, genome-wide methylation patterns from TCGA-BRCA were analyzed to construct CpG–gene networks and identify IDH1-associated topological probe clusters with potential regulatory influence.

Methodology

Patient identification and tissue samples collection

Fresh tumor tissues (n = 300) and adjacent normal control tissues (ANCTs) were collected from breast cancer patients undergoing surgery at Holy Family Hospital, Rawalpindi, and the Pakistan Institute of Medical Sciences, Islamabad. Of these, 242 specimens with a clear demarcation between tumor and healthy tissue were selected for downstream analyses. ANCTs were identified through microscopic clinical evaluation by an oncologist, ensuring that the sampled regions were located at least 2 cm away from the tumor margin. All tissues were preserved in RNAlater solution to prevent RNA degradation.

Patients with a history of cancer or precancerous conditions were excluded. Additionally, patients with comorbid conditions, including diabetes, hypertension, or cardiovascular disorders, were also excluded. A subset of 102 samples with high-quality DNA was selected for methylation analysis.

Sample collection followed institutional ethical guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki, with prior approval from the COMSATS University Islamabad Ethical Review Board (Approval No. CUI/Bio/ERB/2017/23) and informed consent from all participants. RNA extraction and real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissue samples using TRIzol® reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA; Cat# 15596026) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and stored at − 80 °C. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the FIREScript RT cDNA Synthesis Kit (Solis BioDyne, Tartu, Estonia; Cat# 06-15-00050). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using EvaGreen PCR Master Mix (Solis BioDyne; Cat# 08-24-00001). Gene-specific primers for IDH1, PFKP, HIF-1α, and SIX1 were synthesized by Macrogen Korea. β-actin was used as the internal control.

qPCR was conducted on the StepOne Plus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using SYBR Green-based detection. Cycling conditions included: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, gene-specific annealing for 1 min, extension at 72 °C for 20 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 1 min. Relative gene expression was calculated using Livak’s ΔΔCt method 23. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

DNA extraction and bisulfite conversion

Genomic DNA was extracted from tumor and ANCT tissues using the standard phenol–chloroform method and stored at − 20 °C. For bisulfite conversion, 200–500 ng of DNA per sample was treated using the EpiJET Bisulfite Conversion Kit (Thermo Scientific, USA; Cat# K1461) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Methylation-specific PCR (MSP)

Methylation-specific primers targeting the canonical reference transcript of the IDH1 (ENST00000415913.1) proximal promoter (GRCh37/hg19; 2:209,119,046-209,120,046; reverse strand) and a distal regulatory region (2:209,129,567-209,129,744) were designed using the MethPrimer online tool14. The putative distal enhancer was annotated using ENCODE candidate cis‑regulatory element (cCRE) datasets15. MSP was performed using both methylation-specific and unmethylation-specific primers on ~ 100 ng of bisulfite-converted DNA, amplified with HOT FIREPol Blend Master Mix (Solis BioDyne; Cat# 04–25-00,115).

Cycling conditions included: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 12 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 51 °C for 50 s, and extension at 72 °C for 3 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Commercially available fully methylated and fully unmethylated human genomic DNA (Thermo Scientific; Cat# C756N49) were used as the positive controls for the methylated (M) and unmethylated (U) MSP reactions, respectively. PCR‑grade water served as the no‑template negative control, and unconverted genomic DNA was included as an additional negative control to confirm bisulfite‑conversion specificity.

MSP products were visualized on 2% agarose gels under UV light using the BioDoc Analyze system (Biometra). For semi-quantitative, relative comparisons, band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software. Because the methylated (M) and unmethylated (U) reactions are performed independently and may differ in amplification efficiency, MSP was used only for relative methylation assessments. Because MSP amplification is driven by primer‑defined CpG context, the assay infers relative enrichment of methylated versus unmethylated epitypes rather than absolute per‑CpG methylation levels. Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R (version 4.2.2). Expression data for target genes (IDH1, PFKP, HIF-1α, and SIX1) were normalized against β-actin. Correlations with clinicopathological parameters were assessed using the Pearson correlation coefficient. Statistical significance was evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test, ANOVA, and Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test, with p < 0.05 considered significant and p < 0.001 very significant.

Risk stratification was performed by calculating hazard ratios (HRs) through univariate and multivariate analyses. The predictive potential of molecular markers was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves.

Data acquisition and processing

Matched RNA-Seq and DNA methylation data (Illumina Infinium HumanMethylation450K) for TCGA-BRCA were obtained from the Genomic Data Commons using TCGAbiolinks 16. Integrated analysis on 220 primary tumor samples with both data types was performed. RNA-Seq counts were filtered to remove genes with counts per million (CPM) < 1 in ≥ 10% of samples and samples in the lowest 1% of total counts, yielding 18,368 genes. For methylation, beta values were extracted, and probes were excluded if detection p-value > 0.01 in > 1% of samples, located on sex chromosomes, cross-reactive, or containing SNPs at CpG sites17.

Sample stratification based on IDH1 expression

Normalized IDH1 counts were extracted from the filtered RNA-Seq dataset. Samples in the lowest 15th and highest 85th percentiles of IDH1 expression were classified as IDH1-Low and IDH1-High, respectively, to maximize contrast between groups for methylation analysis.

Differential methylation and functional enrichment analysis

Differential methylation between IDH1-Low and IDH1-High groups was assessed using limma 18, with CpG sites considered significant at FDR < 0.05. Significantly differentially methylated probes (DMPs) were annotated to the nearest transcription start site (TSS) using IlluminaHumanMethylation450kanno.ilmn12.hg19 and classified as promoter-associated using the mCSEA package 19. To further assess the regulatory potential of non-genic probes, coordinate-based clustering of significant probes within IDH1-stratified cohorts was performed using a 100 kb sliding window. Clusters containing ≥ 3 probes were designated as topological probe clusters, reflecting local aggregation rather than assumptions of uniform probe spacing.

Gene-CpG network construction and hub gene identification

A bipartite network representing promoter–CpG associations was constructed using igraph 20 R package version 4.4.3, where nodes corresponded to genes or CpG probes and edges linked CpGs to their associated genes. Hub genes were defined as those with the highest degree of connectivity within the network. Hub status reflects the convergence of multiple significant, promoter‑associated CpGs mapped to the same gene after clustering, not the number of CpG probes originally represented for that gene. This approach enabled visualization of promoter–CpG associations and identification of highly connected hub genes for downstream biological interpretation. The complete workflow of the study is given in Fig. S1.

Results

Promoter methylation analysis of IDH1 in BC

Promoter methylation analysis of the IDH1 gene was conducted using methylation-specific PCR on BC and adjacent normal control tissues (ANCT). The IDH1 promoter region (1 kb upstream of the transcription start site, TSS) contains two CpG islands. Island 1 (I1), Proximal to the TSS (~ 12 bp upstream), spans 179 bp and contains 70% GC content (GRCh37/hg19; chr2:209,119,058–209,119,256). In contrast, island 2 (I2), although larger, has a 67% GC content and is situated ~ 746 bp upstream of the TSS, covering positions 209,119,792 to 209,120,144. Additionally, the methylation status of a distal regulatory enhancer region (209,129,567 to 209,129,744) was also analyzed.

Twelve CpGs within I1 (− 109 to − 205) were significantly hypermethylated in BC compared to ANCT (p < 0.001; Fig. 1A). In contrast, CpGs within I2 showed no significant difference (Fig. 1B), indicating that I1 likely holds stronger regulatory relevance. Six CpGs in the putative distal enhancer p were markedly hypomethylated in BC (p < 0.001; Fig. 1C), suggesting an alternative regulatory effect. Methylation in the I1 region was significantly higher among post-menopausal patients (p < 0.017), metastatic cases (p < 0.002), and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC; p < 0.017). No significant differences were observed across age groups or histological subtypes (Fig. 1D). Tumors of grades I and II also exhibited hypermethylation than grades III and IV, indicating that I1 hypermethylation may reflect tumor‑subgroup‑specific epigenetic states in BC.

M-values density plots further confirmed elevated I1 methylation in tumor samples, consistent with the ΔM (Tumor—ANCT) distribution (Fig. 1E). Conversely, I2 showed low and similar methylation levels in both groups, and the putative distal enhancer displayed hypomethylation in tumors relative to ANCT. These patterns underscore region-specific, complex, context-dependent regulatory mechanisms affecting IDH1 expression.

ENCODE data revealed ~ 80 cis-regulatory protein binding sites within these regions, including POLR2A, RBBP5, PHF8, IRF4, CHD1, EP300, and GABPA (Table S2). Strand-specific motif scanning using JASPAR identified frequent motifs for 11 proteins, predominantly in region I1 (~ 172 motifs; Fig. 1E). Aberrant methylation may disrupt the binding of key regulators such as CTCF, EGR1, and MYC, potentially altering IDH1 transcriptional control in BC.

Furthermore, ENCODE whole-genome transcription factor (TF) data were used to analyze cis-regulatory protein binding within target regions to assess the functional consequences of aberrant methylation. Approximately 80 cis-regulatory binding sites were identified in these regions (Table S2), including key factors such as POLR2A, RBBP5, PHF8, IRF4, CHD1, EP300, and GABPA. In addition to TFs, several cofactors and chromatin modifiers were also detected, suggesting that deregulated methylation profiles may alter the chromatin landscape and contribute to widespread changes in the tumor microenvironment. Using JASPAR data, strand-specific motif scanning revealed frequent motifs for 11 proteins, predominantly in region I1 (~ 172 motifs; Fig. 1E). Notably, aberrant methylation may disrupt the binding of critical regulators, including CTCF, EGR1, and MYC, particularly at motifs located on the antisense strand, potentially impairing IDH1 transcriptional control in breast cancer.

Deregulation of IDH1 expression in BC

Further, the expression profiles of IDH1 transcripts in BC and ANCT controls were analyzed using qPCR analysis. IDH1 expression was significantly downregulated in BC compared to controls (Fig. 2A), highlighting its prognostic relevance. Notably, markedly lower IDH1 expression was observed in TNBC, post-menopausal, and metastatic BC cases (Fig. 2B). However, cohorts based on age groups and histological types did not show significant differences. Early-grade tumors (GI and GII) exhibited lower expression compared to GIII and GIV, indicating grade‑associated expression trends.

Expression and methylation profiles of IDH1 and associated markers in breast cancer. (A) IDH1 transcript expression in BC and ANCT cases; (B) Distribution of IDH1 expression among various clinicopathological cohorts of BC; (C) Mean comparison of clinicopathological cohorts, Tukey’s HSD test; (D–F) PFKP, HIF-1α, and SIX1 transcript expression, respectively.

Importantly, BC cohorts with low IDH1 expression exhibited hypermethylation in the I1 region, suggesting a potential regulatory effect. To further explore the regulatory implications of IDH1 promoter methylation in the target I1 region across key clinical subtypes, Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test was performed. Age groups and tumor size again showed no significant differences for IDH1 promoter methylation and expression (Fig. 2C). However, IDH1 methylation at I1 was consistently significant across hormonal, menopausal, staging, and metastatic cohorts. IDH1 expression remained significant in disease stages and hormonal types, but lost significance in menopausal and metastatic cohorts, indicating that methylation at the I1 region may function as a more stable prognostic indicator than expression alone. Although I1 hypermethylation was frequent in these cohorts, this enrichment likely reflects a molecularly distinct subset within these broad classifications rather than a direct indicator of aggressiveness.

Expression of PFKP was upregulated in these BC cases (Fig. 2D), indicating a potential metabolic shift to meet the altered energy demands of tumor tissues. Similarly, hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF-1α) and sine-oculis homeobox (SIX1) were also elevated (Fig. 2E & F), indicating metabolic and developmental reprogramming under hypoxic conditions. Collectively, these findings underscore a coordinated molecular axis influencing the tumor microenvironment.

Molecular interactions underscoring BC prognosis

Pearson’s correlation analysis revealed a moderate inverse relationship between IDH1 expression and promoter methylation in the I1 region (R = − 0.37, p < 0.008; Fig. 3A), supporting the regulatory association. No significant association was observed between IDH1 expression and methylation in the I2 region (R = 0.09, p = 0.53). HIF-1α exhibited a significant moderate negative correlation with IDH1 expression (R = − 0.47, p < 0.001), indicating hypoxia‑linked suppression of IDH1. SIX1 showed a non-significant weak association with both IDH1 (R = 0.05, p = 0.66) and HIF-1α expression (R = 0.04, p = 0.76) in these breast cancer cases. Among clinicopathological factors, metastasis and hormonal subtypes demonstrated strong positive Spearman correlations with I1 methylation, whereas tumor grade showed a negative association (Fig. 3B), highlighting the prognostic relevance of clinical parameters. I2 methylation did not correlate with clinical variables; however, enhancer methylation was negatively associated with disease status and hormonal subtypes, indicating site‑specific methylation dynamics.

Prognostic significance of IDH1 expression and methylation in BC

Kaplan–Meier (log-rank) analysis indicated significantly poor overall survival (OS) among patients with downregulated IDH1 expression (median OS: 58 months; p < 0.001) (Fig. 3C). I1 hypermethylation showed an association with improved survival, whereas I2 methylation showed no prognostic significance.

Univariate Cox analysis (Table 2) showed reduced hazard association with IDH1 expression (HR = 0.29, p < 0.01). I1 methylation showed a trend toward reduced risk (HR = 0.66, p < 0.06). Among clinical factors, hormonal subtypes and metastasis were linked to increased risk, though these associations did not reach statistical significance.

In multivariate analysis, I1 methylation remained significantly associated with reduced risk (HR = 0.42, p < 0.035), whereas IDH1 expression retained a protective effect but lost statistical significance (HR = 0.94, p = 0.94), suggesting potential collinearity or confounding with other variables. Variance inflation factor (VIF) values were within acceptable limits (< 5), although IDH1 expression exhibited relatively higher VIFs (4.15), indicating moderate multicollinearity. TNBC exhibited a markedly elevated risk (HR = 13, p < 0.01).

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated that IDH1 profiles were good predictors of BC pathogenesis (Fig. 3D). The partial area under the curve (pAUC within 100%–75%) was approximately 63%, with a sensitivity of 57% in this range. In contrast, I1 methylation produced a pAUC value of 100% within this dataset; however, this result should be interpreted cautiously. Because ROC analyses were performed on the same cohort used to derive the methylation profiles, the estimate may be overly optimistic—particularly given the limited sample size and absence of external validation. These ROC findings are therefore exploratory and require confirmation in independent, larger cohorts with standardized inclusion criteria. Conversely, I2 methylation did not demonstrate discriminatory capacity (pAUC = 53%).

Altered IDH1 levels impair global DNA methylation

In TCGA-BRCA, IDH1 expression was significantly lower in tumor tissues compared to controls (Fig. 4A; p < 0.001), corroborating clinical findings. Cases with low IDH1 expression exhibited significantly higher global methylation (Fig. 4B), suggesting a relationship between IDH1 levels and methylation status that warrants further mechanistic investigation.

Differential methylation analysis between high and low IDH1 groups identified 5577 significant differentially methylated probes (FDR < 0.05), mapping predominantly UCSC RefSeq regions (3724 probe; 66.4%) and intergenic regions (33.2%; Fig. 4C). Most significant probes showed positive fold-change trend (Fig. 4D). Of these, 2945 (52.8%) mapped to open seas (low GC content), 919 (16.5%) to CpG islands, and the remainder to shores and shelves (Fig. 4E), indicating widespread epigenetic alterations.

Coordinate-based clustering revealed 143 topological clusters with potential regulatory relevance (Table S3). The largest clusters by size were S239 and S405, each comprising 51 probes per 100 kb (Fig. 4F) and located on chromosomes 2 and 5. Although these were the largest by size, they exhibited moderate regulatory potential with probe densities of 0.07/kb and 0.08/kb, respectively. The densest clusters occurred on chromosome 13 (R874; 85.7/kb), and 6 (R465; 32.3/kb), indicating potential regulatory hotspots.

Among the top 10 associated genes were EBF3 (transcription factor), ATP11A (lipid transporter), OBSCN (giant muscle protein), and PGBD5 (transposable element factor) (Fig. 4G), highlighting diverse functional pathways. Predominantly, the significant probes were hypermethylated and located in 3′ UTRs, gene bodies, and intergenic regions (Fig. 4H), suggesting broad epigenetic remodeling.

Gene–CpG networks modulating tumor microenvironment

To examine the genome-wide distribution of significant probes across all chromosomes, a Manhattan plot was generated (Fig. 5A). Notably, most of the significantly methylated probes mapped to chromosome 2, where the IDH1 gene resides, suggesting that IDH1 profiles contribute to epigenetic alterations in neighboring loci. Promoter enrichment analysis of the ranked list of significant probes was performed using the mCSEA package with stringent parameters (≥ 5 CpGs; Fig. 5B). Among the top 20 enriched promoters, four-CLDN18 (epithelial junction protein), SGCE (sarcoglycan epsilon), HRNBP3 (RNA splicing protein), and PGBD5 (transposable element factor) were hypermethylated. Given the critical functions of these genes, altered promoter methylation indicates extensive regulatory mechanisms influencing gene activity in BC pathogenesis.

The gene–CpG network revealed 332 hub genes (≥ 3 edges; Fig. 5C & D),) including CIDEB (cell death-inducing factor), LTB4R2 and LTB4R (immune receptors), HNRNPA1 and HNRPA1L-2 (splice factors), and CBX5(heterochromatin protein) each had 14 edges, while ZBTB37 (transcription factor), NDUFAF3 (NADH dehydrogenase assembly factor), DALRD3 (tRNA protein), and SLC3A2 (amino acid transporter). Their high connectivity suggests key regulatory roles in apoptosis, splicing, and chromatin remodeling.

Discussion

The IDH1 gene is located on human chromosome 2q34 and encodes a ~ 47 kDa protein localized in the cytoplasm and peroxisomes. Genetic alterations in IDH1 have been associated with several malignancies, including glioma, acute myeloid leukaemia 9, and chondrosarcoma21. Although IDH1 mutations are rare in BC, dysregulated IDH1 expression disrupts key cellular processes, reinforcing the importance of its non‑genetic regulation. While IDH1 mutations exhibit limited prognostic value in gliomas 8 and AML patients, IDH1-associated alterations in the tumor microenvironment appear widespread. Despite this, the regulatory mechanisms governing the IDH1 locus remain largely unexplored. Our recent findings indicate that IDH1 expression alone does not predict overall prognosis in BC; rather, clinical factors and promoter methylation also influence survival, underscoring the importance of IDH1 regulation in carcinogenesis. Previous studies suggest that IDH1 mutations may alter global methylation profiles 12; however, the impact of these deregulated profiles on the tumor microenvironment remains poorly understood. Furthermore, the regulatory influence of methylation on the IDH1 locus itself is still inadequately characterized.

This study examined the methylation status of the IDH1 promoter within two proximal CpG islands and a putative distal enhancer element in BC and ANCT. Twelve CpGs located near the transcription start site (TSS; positions − 109 to − 205) were significantly hypermethylated in BC compared to ANCT, whereas CpGs within the second island (I2; positions − 947 to − 1070) showed no significant difference. These findings suggest that CpGs in the I1 region may exert greater regulatory influence over IDH1 expression. The I1 region was also identified as a critical regulatory hotspot enriched for multiple cis-regulatory protein-binding sites. Since IDH1 is transcribed from the antisense strand, methylation in these regions may disrupt the binding of key regulators. Notably, binding sites for CTCF (chromatin-associated), EGR1 (growth response protein), MYC (proto-oncogene), STAT3 (signal transducer), YY1, and USF1 (transcription factors) were detected on the antisense strand, indicating extensive regulatory shifts in both epigenomic and transcriptomic landscapes driven by aberrant methylation. Importantly, I1 methylation varied across clinicopathological cohorts, suggesting context-specific roles in patient survival and prognosis. Similar trends in the prognostic relevance of epigenetic alterations have been reported for other genes in BC 22–24.

For methylation trends, CpGs in the I1 region exhibited positive ΔMeth (Tumor—Control) profiles, whereas the enhancer region showed negative ΔMeth values. It is worth mentioning that the putative enhancer proximal to IDH1 also lies ~ 1 kb upstream of the PIKFYVE gene, suggesting that its regulatory influence might extend beyond IDH1. In contrast, CpGs within I2 displayed no significant difference, despite spanning a larger genomic region than I1. These patterns indicate that IDH1 is regulated through context-dependent epigenetic mechanisms. Unlike mutations that occur in dividing cells in a stochastic manner, DNA methylation patterns are maintained tightly, which helps establish clonal expansion and lineage specificity in these cells. Due to the heterogeneous nature of tumor tissues, these mechanisms are critical in maintaining lineage-specific subpopulations resistant to therapeutic regimes.

The observation that I1 hypermethylation is more common in clinically aggressive subgroups but appears to be linked with better survival should be approached with caution. Rather than suggesting a protective role, this pattern might reflect molecularly distinct subpopulations within these broad clinical groups, influenced by diverse epigenetic states or subclonal dynamics. These context‑dependent epigenomic configurations can lead to complex, non‑linear relationships with clinical outcomes, so the survival trend seen here should be regarded as preliminary until confirmed by studies in independent cohorts.

Motif‑scan analysis identified predicted binding sites for CTCF, EGR1, and MYC within the aberrantly methylated I1 region, suggesting that this locus may intersect with regulatory pathways involved in chromatin insulation, stress‑responsive transcription, and growth‑associated signalling. While these motifs overlap differentially methylated CpGs, the present study does not provide direct evidence of transcription‑factor occupancy; therefore, any regulatory impact remains hypothetical. A logical next step will be to integrate public breast cancer ChIP‑seq datasets to assess whether these factors bind this region in vivo and whether binding patterns correlate with methylation status.

The aberrant methylation in I1 was found to be significantly associated with IDH1 levels, possibly due to perturbed TF binding sites. However, its association with I2 methylation was not significantly different, highlighting the regulatory potential of I1 methylation in BC. Furthermore, the expression of the platelet isoform of PFKP, HIF-1α, and SIX1 was found to be upregulated in these BC cases, highlighting a potential metabolic reprogramming in these proliferating cells. Elevated HIF-1α levels were previously reported in IDH1 mutant glioma cases 25 but not in AML 9. This apparent discrepancy is probably because hypoxic conditions are primarily triggered in solid tumors like glioma and BC, as indicated by elevated HIF-1α expression. However, the exact mechanism remains to be elucidated. We also observed elevated SIX1 levels in BC, suggesting a potential developmental reprogramming in these tumor tissues. Earlier studies have also corroborated higher SIX expression in BC 26. Upregulated SIX1 expression has been shown to maintain cancer stem cells (CSCs) by conferring self-renewal ability and regulation of growth-promoting mechanisms in carcinogenesis 27.

We observed that reduced IDH1 expression was associated with poorer overall survival, whereas I1 hypermethylation was associated with improved outcome in this cohort. Multivariate analysis further identified tumor grade, histological type, and hormonal status as influential factors in overall prognosis. Additionally, ROC analysis demonstrated that IDH1 methylation profiles outperformed IDH1 expression profiles in classifying BC pathogenesis; however, we interpret this cautiously, as same‑cohort ROC analyses can yield optimistic estimates, particularly in smaller sample sizes. These findings are therefore exploratory and require validation in larger, independent cohorts before any biological or clinical inferences can be made.

Although IDH1 mutants are expected to elevate differential global methylation in tumor cells by perturbing TET-mediated demethylation due to 2HG production, hypermethylation is only observed consistently in gliomas, whereas other mutant cancers, including acute myeloid leukemia (AML), cholangiocarcinoma, and melanoma, consistently show hypomethylation. This suggests that, besides IDH1 mutations, its protein turnover is also critical in maintaining global methylome levels, highlighting the significance of regulatory mechanisms that maintain IDH1 turnover in carcinogenesis.

Analysis of IDH1-stratified TCGA-BRCA data revealed a global shift in methylation, indicating a role in genome-wide epigenetic remodeling. Differential methylation significantly affected genes such as EBF3, ATP11A, OBSCN, and PGBD5, which are involved in transcription, lipid transport, and cell growth. Notably, 33% of probes mapped to intergenic regions (open seas, CpG islands, shores, and shelves). Clustering of these intergenic probes identified 143 regulatory hotspots, with the highest probe densities on chromosomes 13 and 6, suggesting localized coordination hubs for chromatin modification.

Cluster R874 (chr13:40,762,435–40,762,469; 13q14.1) lies adjacent to a genomic segment previously reported as highly susceptible to copy‑number alterations (CNAs) in breast cancer 28 and harboring the RB1 locus. Given the instability of this region and the extremely narrow physical width of the cluster (35 bp), CNV‑related confounding cannot be fully excluded. In contrast, cluster R465 (chr6:26,569,445–26,569,537; 6p22.2) shows no evidence of CNV events in TCGA‑BRCA datasets29, although CNV gains at this locus have been documented in PTEN‑hamartoma tumor syndrome (PHTS). Therefore, both clusters should be interpreted with caution, and orthogonal validation—such as targeted bisulfite sequencing or locus‑specific copy‑number profiling—will be required to confirm whether these represent genuine regulatory hotspots rather than CNV‑associated artifacts.

Enrichment analysis identified hypermethylation of the promoters of CLDN18, SGCE, HRNBP3, and PGBD5, implicating junction integrity, splicing, and transposable element regulation in BC pathogenesis. HRNBP3 (also known as RBFOX3), an RNA-binding protein and neuronal nuclei antigen, has been associated with poor prognosis in lung30 and hepatocellular carcinoma31. Its involvement in neurological disorders 32 and co-expression with synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2B (SV2B) 33, combined with frequent IDH1 mutations in glioma, suggests a possible link between IDH1-mediated splicing and BC prognosis. These findings don’t establish causality, but they align with strong evidence from other tumor types12 showing that mutant IDH1/2 can induce widespread epigenetic reprogramming. Alternatively, chromatin‑domain or TAD architecture, regional chromosomal instability, or the influence of other genes located on chromosome 2 may also contribute to such widespread changes.

Gene–CpG network analysis highlighted 332 hub genes, with the highest connectivity observed for CIDEB, LTB4R2, HNRNPA1, and CBX5, indicating key roles in apoptosis, immune signaling, splicing, and chromatin regulation. The identification of HNRNPA1 as a major hub reinforces the hypothesis that IDH1 deregulation influences the tumor microenvironment through extensive epigenomic and epitranscriptomic activity. HNRNPA1 has been implicated in carcinogenesis 34 via MYC and HIF-1α-driven upregulation 35. Although our in vitro data confirm elevated HIF-1α in cases with deregulated IDH1, the role of HNRNPA1 in BC remains to be validated. Interestingly, MYC-binding motifs are enriched at the IDH1 locus; whether this directly involves HNRNPA1 activity is unclear. The network analysis reflects coordinated methylation patterns, but it cannot establish causality or directionality.

Collectively, these findings suggest that IDH1 deregulation is associated with genome-wide methylation and chromatin remodeling features, modulating transcriptional programs and critical biosynthetic pathways in BC progression and prognosis. This study highlights the clinical significance of IDH1 methylation in BC patients and emphasizes the role of key molecular and demographic factors in shaping overall prognosis. Integrating IDH1 expression with other biomarkers—such as PFKP, SIX1, and HIF-1α—may provide novel insights into the clinical landscape that influence treatment outcomes. Importantly, our data suggest that IDH1 expression alone is insufficient to predict BC prognosis, underscoring the need for further research into its regulatory mechanisms and interplay with other molecular factors. Additionally, IDH1-associated epigenetic alterations warrant investigation into the activities of RNA-binding proteins that may influence survival and disease progression.

Limitations and future‑work plan

The present study is retrospective and cross‑sectional; therefore, the findings reflect associations rather than causal relationships. Future investigations using longitudinal or interventional study designs and larger, prospectively collected cohorts will be required to establish causal mechanisms. In addition, although we optimized MSP conditions and validated primer specificity, this method remains semi‑quantitative and infers enrichment of methylated epitypes rather than base‑resolved CpG density. Because we did not generate 0–100% calibrated methylation standards or contrived DNA mixtures, amplification‑efficiency differences between methylated and unmethylated reactions cannot be fully excluded. Future work will incorporate serial dilution curves and high‑resolution melting‑based validation (MS‑HRM) to refine methylation estimates and confirm MSP‑derived trends. Because a dedicated CNV-specific analysis was not performed, we cannot fully exclude the possibility that copy‑number variation may influence methylation measurements at some loci; however, the probe‑aggregation approach used for hotspot identification reduces sensitivity to CNV‑related artifacts.
