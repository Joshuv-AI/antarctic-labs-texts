Letter to the Editor Regarding ‘Tafamidis 61 mg Patient Characteristics and Persistency? A Retrospective Analysis of German Statutory Health Insurance Data (IQVIA™ LRx)’
Authors: Arnt V Kristen, Carsten Tschöpe, Stephanie Schwarting, Fabian aus dem Siepen
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

This non-interventional, retrospective, observational cohort study by Attal et al. [1] – a collaborative work of statisticians and industry partners – thought to evaluate the prescription pattern of tafamidis 61 mg in a real-world setting of German patients, diagnosed with transthyretin cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM).

The primary objective of this study was to describe tafamidis 61 mg treatment patterns (i.e., treatment initiation, days of supply, adherence rates, and persistence), extracted from German statutory health insurance data. In a second line, demographics and concomitant medication of patients dispensed prescriptions of tafamidis 61 mg QD were analyzed.

Due to its industrial dependence, this study lacks a reflection of the currently far-reaching, multifaceted therapeutic strategies in ATTR-CM that are taken into consideration by physicians and daily caregivers and that drive medical decision-making on specific therapy.

Additionally, they concluded having identified opportunities for improvement of concomitant medication in patients with ATTR-CM beyond treatment with tafamidis 61 mg QD. Their conclusion is based on the fact that they observed a minimal decrease in beta-blocker (BB) prescription upon tafamidis initiation. Again, this observation was not comprehensively illuminated in the context of the current heart failure guidelines and the gap that is present in its crosslink to ATTR-CM. There remains an unmet need for clarification regarding the extent to which beta-blockers are beneficial in the management of ATTR-CM. Currently, a cautious use is recommended due to heart rate-dependent cardiac output upon fixed-stroke volumes. Thus, medically forced reduction of heart rate by using BB can lead to clinical deterioration in selected patients. Therefore, the decrease from 67.8% before treatment to 61.3% during tafamidis 61 mg treatment does not appear to be clinically meaningful and is likely attributed to a more cautious approach by physicians. Although their observation appears to be statistically significant, this should not allow for any conclusions that patients in Germany are being treated inadequately.

A recently published Chinese meta-analysis showed that many patients with amyloidosis had been treated with BB across the world without an impact on overall survival. Italian colleagues highlighted in a comment the value of BB for the treatment of cardiomyopathy patterns of amyloidosis [2, 3]. The Heidelberg group recently analyzed their cohort and found a negative effect of beta-blocker therapy, which was only associated with hereditary ATTRv patients, mostly suffering from combined neurological and cardiac symptoms. This contrasts with earlier results and might be influenced by a higher percentage of ATTRv patients in the historical cohort and their autonomic burden as an underlying cause [4, 5]. A large retrospective study from the NAC London demonstrated that BB treatment was discontinued in 22% of all patients and no benefit in survival could be observed except in a subgroup of patients with an LVEF < 40%. The authors should also be aware that even in their pivotal trial of tafamidis there was a concomitant BB treatment in nearly one-third of all patients [6]. In the end, BB (and or amiodaron) should only be initiated in patients with ATTR-CM if atrial arrhythmias with a persistent heart rate greater than 100 bpm are present and chronotropic incompetency has been excluded.

However, as heart failure treatment does not exclusively rely on BB prescription, the authors could have included other drug classes as well as their dosing in the analysis.

Their analysis on the four most-prescribed drugs in this patient cohorts included loop diuretics (73.0%), beta-blocking agents (61.3%), direct factor Xa inhibitors (59.1%), and statins (52.7%). It is unclear why they did not analyze prescriptions of more relevant drugs for heart failure treatment in this patient cohort, including thiazide diuretics, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, ARNI, and SGLT2 inhibitors, as well as the combined use of these drugs. Such an analysis could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the treatment's effects on heart failure in this population.

As they did not observe an increase in the prescription of loop diuretics, they assumed disease stability in patients treated with tafamidis.

However, it was overlooked that it is not the initiation of diuretics but rather the escalation of dosage that might reflect a the progression of ATTR-CM. Also, dose-escalation schemes and adjustments of diuretics are necessary over several months after diagnosis. Finally, most patients in Germany were referred to a tertiary center with specific and dedicated outpatient clinics with the ability to educate their patients regarding lifestyle changes that are necessary after diagnosis, such as daily weighing and restriction of fluid intake.

Unfortunately, there were no reports on prescriptions of sequential nephron blockade, additional prescription of mineralocorticoid antagonists, or SGLT2 inhibitor treatment that may be used in combination with loop diuretics to treat progression of ATTR-CM.

Statins have been one of the four most-prescribed drug classes in this patient cohort. As statins are not associated with ATTR-CM nor with heart failure due to ATTR-CM, this finding may simply reflect their prescription in a cohort of elderly people with confounding presence of coronary or peripheral artery diseases. From the clinician’s perspective, a change from rosuvastatin to other statins during tafamidis treatment would have been of real interest as drug–drug interactions of tafamidis and rosuvastatin have been reported. Therefore, it would have been interesting to know whether a change of rosuvastatin prescription occurs in this cohort.

Lastly, they reported that prescriptions for direct factor Xa inhibitors were more frequent during tafamidis 61 mg treatment than before (P < 0.0001). As the authors have stated, the significant increase in direct factor Xa inhibitors (from 55.0% to 59.1%) is expected and recommended, since it is prescribed to prevent thromboembolic events in patients with ATTR-CM and atrial fibrillation, but this has nothing to do with “common symptoms” which the authors declared as the reason for the prescription. There is no evidence of an interaction between tafamidis treatment and the use of direct factor Xa inhibitor prescriptions. The prevalence of atrial fibrillation increases during the clinical course of ATTR-CM, affecting almost two-thirds of the patients with ATTR-CM.

The authors also missed the chance take a deep dive into other medications that had been flagged as harmful in amyloidosis, such as calcium antagonists and digoxin derivates. Although there is an underlying mechanism that makes it reasonable to ban these two substances, the overall recommendation relies on a few case reports from the mid-1980s [7].

While the statistical analysis of the dataset appears thorough, it is evident that the authors lack the necessary connection and experience with the daily clinical practice and management of patients with ATTR-CM. This certainly raises questions about the scientific acceptance and transferability of these data.

Upon our critical appraisal, we strongly encourage the authors to correct their conclusions in line with scientific and medical understanding and finally to prevent harm in patient care. Our understanding of the scientific collaborations between industry and physicians differs, and pointing fingers at German cardiologists for a perceived lack of knowledge in managing amyloidosis patients is inappropriate, especially by those not involved in the routine management of patients with ATTR-CM.
