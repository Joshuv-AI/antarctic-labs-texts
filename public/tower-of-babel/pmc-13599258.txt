Ocular manifestations of lichen planus: a comparative analysis of tear function, choroidal thickness, and retinal parameters
Authors: Furkan Çiftci, İsa An, Gökhan Çelik, Tuncay Karaçocuk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo assess tear function, retinal and choroidal thickness, retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness, and ganglion cell complex (GCC) thickness in patients with lichen planus (LP) compared with healthy controls with comparable age and sex distribution.MethodsThis prospective case-control study included 35 participants with LP and 35 healthy control participants. Only the right eye of each participant was included in the analysis. Tear function was evaluated using the Schirmer I test and tear break-up time (TBUT). Axial length (AL) was measured using optical biometry, and intraocular pressure (IOP) was measured by pneumotonometry, whereas central retinal thickness (CRT), RNFL thickness, and GCC thickness were assessed using spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT). Choroidal thickness was measured at the subfoveal, nasal 1500 μm, and temporal 1500 μm regions using enhanced depth imaging OCT (EDI-OCT). Intraobserver reliability was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).ResultsPatients with LP demonstrated significantly lower Schirmer I test and TBUT values than controls (Schirmer I: 10.34 ± 2.17 mm vs. 18.37 ± 2.35 mm; TBUT: 10.74 ± 1.99 s vs. 13.06 ± 0.64 s; both p < 0.001). The pre-specified primary choroidal outcome, SFCT, was significantly lower in the LP group than in the control group (299.43 ± 30.03 μm vs. 345.40 ± 41.89 μm; p < 0.001). The secondary choroidal outcomes were also lower at the nasal 1500 μm region (272.23 ± 62.36 μm vs. 303.60 ± 42.09 μm; p = 0.041) and temporal 1500 μm region (283.43 ± 66.56 μm vs. 328.20 ± 42.51 μm; p = 0.011). No significant differences were observed in AL, IOP, CRT, RNFL thickness, or GCC thickness between the overall LP and control groups. In the exploratory subgroup analysis, GCC thickness was lower in patients with oral involvement than in those with cutaneous-only LP in the unadjusted comparison; however, this difference did not remain significant after Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction. No significant correlations were identified between disease duration and ocular parameters.ConclusionsLP may be associated with reduced tear function and lower choroidal thickness, while overall retinal structural parameters were comparable with those of healthy controls. The GCC finding in the oral involvement subgroup should be regarded as exploratory and interpreted cautiously. Larger longitudinal studies incorporating standardized disease activity assessment, dry eye symptom scoring, and advanced vascular imaging are needed to clarify the ocular implications of LP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12886-026-05348-w.Keywords: Lichen planus, Choroidal thickness, Optical coherence tomography, Tear function, Ganglion cell complex

Introduction

LP is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by T lymphocyte–mediated autoimmune features, with an unknown etiology, affecting the skin, mucous membranes, and skin appendages [1]. The prevalence of LP is estimated to be approximately 0.5–1% in the general population [2]. Mucosal involvement has been reported in approximately 30–70% of patients with LP [3]. Ocular involvement, a rare manifestation of LP, typically accompanies cutaneous or oral lesions [4, 5]. It may present with complex ocular findings, including cicatricial conjunctivitis, corneal involvement, or eyelid abnormalities. Conjunctival involvement can lead to complications such as blepharitis, symblepharon, and lacrimal duct pathologies, whereas corneal involvement often results in dry eye, keratitis, or corneal ulcerations [4–9].

The choroid, the primary vascular network supplying the outer retinal layers and retinal pigment epithelium, has the highest blood flow within the eye [10, 11]. Its intense vascularization makes it susceptible to systemic inflammatory processes, and studies have shown that choroidal structure may be affected in numerous multisystemic diseases [12]. Similar to LP, chronic inflammatory skin diseases such as psoriasis and rosacea have also been investigated for choroidal alterations [13, 14]. Consequently, research investigating choroidal alterations and retinal vascular involvement in chronic inflammatory diseases is expanding. Given the chronic inflammatory nature of LP, it may impact choroidal vascularization. However, studies evaluating both anterior and posterior segment structures in LP remain limited [15, 16].

Although choroidal thickness has been proposed as an indirect marker of inflammatory disease activity [12], it is influenced by demographic and ocular factors, particularly age, sex, and AL [17]. EDI-OCT provides a noninvasive, reliable, and objective method for in vivo visualization and quantitative assessment of choroidal structures [18].

This prospective case-control study aims to compare tear function, IOP, AL, CRT, RNFL thickness, GCC thickness, and choroidal thickness measured in multiple regions using EDI-OCT between patients with LP and healthy controls. To our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate GCC and RNFL thickness in LP patients using OCT.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This prospective case–control study was conducted at the Ophthalmology Clinic of Şanlıurfa Training and Research Hospital. The study protocol was approved by the Harran University Ethics Committee (Approval No: HRU/20.20.21) and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

The study included 35 participants with LP and 35 healthy control participants. Participants aged 18–55 years were eligible for inclusion. Healthy controls were selected to provide group-level comparability with the LP group in terms of age and sex; individual pairwise matching was not performed. To ensure statistical independence, only the right eye of each participant was included in the analysis.

To minimize confounding from systemic or ocular conditions that may influence retinal or choroidal parameters, individuals with systemic vascular disease (e.g., diabetes mellitus, hypertension) or ocular disorders affecting the posterior segment (e.g., glaucoma, uveitis) were excluded. Additional exclusion criteria included pregnancy or lactation; endocrine, cardiac, hepatic, renal, hematological, autoimmune, or other systemic inflammatory disorders; use of systemic medications potentially affecting ocular tissues (e.g., corticosteroids, oral retinoids, antimalarial agents); history of ultraviolet phototherapy; prior intraocular surgery or laser procedures; contact lens use; smoking; and alcohol consumption. Participants with a spherical equivalent outside the range of − 3.00 to + 3.00 D were also excluded.

Dermatological diagnosis and classification of lichen planus

The diagnosis of cutaneous LP was established by an experienced dermatologist based on compatible clinical findings and histopathological confirmation. Clinically, cutaneous LP was defined by the presence of characteristic pruritic, violaceous, flat-topped polygonal papules or plaques, with or without Wickham striae, in typical anatomical distributions. Biopsy was performed in all patients with suspected cutaneous LP. Histopathological confirmation was based on lichenoid/interface dermatitis, including band-like lymphocytic infiltration at the dermoepidermal junction, basal cell vacuolar degeneration, hypergranulosis and/or irregular acanthosis, saw-tooth rete ridge configuration, and Civatte bodies.

Oral involvement was assessed by dermatological examination of the oral mucosa. Oral LP was clinically suspected in patients with compatible oral mucosal lesions, including symmetrical reticular white striae/Wickham striae, plaque-like lesions, erosive lesions, or ulcerative lesions involving the buccal mucosa, tongue, gingiva, or other oral mucosal sites. In patients with clinically suspected oral involvement, histopathological confirmation was based on basal cell degeneration, band-like lymphocytic infiltration, and Civatte bodies.

According to these clinical and histopathological criteria, patients were classified as having cutaneous-only LP or cutaneous LP with oral involvement. All included patients had classic cutaneous LP. Among patients with oral involvement, all oral mucosal lesions were of the reticular type. Only patients who had not received treatment for LP before study enrollment were included. Representative clinical findings of cutaneous and oral LP are shown in Fig. 1.

Representative clinical findings of lichen planus. (A) Cutaneous lichen planus presenting with violaceous, flat-topped papules and plaques. (B) Oral mucosal involvement showing reticular white striae compatible with oral lichen planus

For all participants, demographic characteristics, relevant medical history, disease duration, treatment status, and anatomical sites of LP involvement were recorded. Disease duration was defined as the interval between the documented date of dermatological diagnosis of LP and the ophthalmological examination at which ocular measurements were obtained. The diagnosis date was obtained from dermatology clinic records. The cohort was not restricted to patients who were newly diagnosed at the time of ophthalmological assessment; however, all included patients underwent ophthalmological evaluation before initiation of LP-specific treatment.

Evaluation of tear functions

Tear secretion was assessed using the Schirmer I test without topical anesthesia. Standardized filter paper strips (Whatman No. 41) were placed in the lateral third of the lower eyelid, and the wetted length was measured in millimeters after 5 min.

Tear film stability was evaluated using the TBUT test. Following fluorescein instillation into the conjunctival sac, the interval between the last complete blink and the appearance of the first dry spot was measured under slit-lamp biomicroscopy with cobalt blue illumination. The mean of three consecutive measurements was used for analysis.

Ophthalmological examination and ocular imaging

All participants underwent a comprehensive ophthalmological examination, including assessment of best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) and measurement of IOP by pneumotonometry. AL was measured using the LensStar 900 device (Haag-Streit AG, Köniz, Switzerland). After cycloplegia induced with topical 1% cyclopentolate (Alcon), SD-OCT (Nidek RS-3000, Nidek Co., Ltd., Gamagori, Japan) was used to assess CRT, RNFL thickness, and GCC thickness.

OCT image quality was assessed using the device-provided signal strength index (SSI). Only scans with SSI ≥ 7/10, correct centration, clear visualization of the choroidoscleral interface, and no significant motion artifacts or segmentation errors were included. Scans not meeting these criteria were repeated when possible or excluded from analysis. Retinal segmentation outputs were reviewed before analysis to identify gross segmentation errors. Manual choroidal thickness measurements were performed on anonymized OCT images by an examiner masked to participant group allocation and oral involvement status.

Choroidal thickness was measured manually using enhanced depth imaging (EDI) mode on the same device and defined as the distance from the outer border of the retinal pigment epithelium to the inner border of the sclera. Choroidal thickness was measured at three locations: the foveal center, defined as subfoveal choroidal thickness (SFCT), and at 1500 μm nasal and 1500 μm temporal to the fovea (Fig. 2). To minimize potential diurnal variability, all OCT examinations were performed between 09:00 and 11:00.

Choroidal thickness measurement using EDI-OCT. Choroidal thickness was measured at the subfoveal, temporal 1500 μm, and nasal 1500 μm regions in patients with LP

All ophthalmic examinations, including tear function tests, were conducted by an ophthalmologist. To assess intraobserver repeatability, EDI-OCT measurements were repeated twice by the same investigator in a randomly selected subset of 20 participants, and the ICC was calculated. ICC was estimated using a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement; high reliability was defined as ICC ≥ 0.90.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and median (minimum–maximum), and categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

Between-group comparisons of continuous variables were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Correlation analyses were performed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The pre-specified primary structural outcome was SFCT. Nasal and temporal choroidal thickness measurements at 1500 μm were evaluated as secondary choroidal outcomes. No multiplicity adjustment was applied to these secondary choroidal comparisons. Therefore, the primary inference was based on SFCT, whereas nasal and temporal choroidal thickness findings were interpreted as supportive secondary outcomes. Multiplicity adjustment was applied only to the subgroup comparisons according to oral involvement.

To determine whether LP was independently associated with ocular structural parameters, multivariable linear regression analyses were performed. Separate regression models were constructed for the primary choroidal outcome, SFCT, the secondary choroidal outcomes, nasal and temporal choroidal thickness at 1500 μm, and the secondary retinal structural outcomes, CRT, RNFL thickness, and GCC thickness. In each model, the corresponding ocular structural parameter was entered as the dependent variable, while group status (LP vs. control) was entered as the main independent variable, with adjustment for age, sex, and AL. The assumptions of all six multivariable linear regression models were evaluated using residual diagnostics. Normality of residuals was assessed by visual inspection of histograms and normal P–P plots. Homoscedasticity was assessed by inspecting plots of standardized residuals against standardized predicted values. Linearity of the continuous predictors was evaluated using partial regression plots. Potentially influential observations and outliers were assessed using standardized residuals and Cook’s distance. Multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF) values.

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power software version 3.1.9.7. The primary outcome for sample size calculation was the between-group difference in SFCT between patients with LP and healthy controls. Given the large between-group difference in SFCT previously reported by Özlü et al. [15], a conservative medium-to-large standardized effect size of Cohen’s d = 0.70 was assumed for sample-size planning. Using a two-tailed independent samples t-test, an alpha error probability of 0.05, a statistical power of 80%, and an allocation ratio of 1:1, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 68 participants, with 34 participants per group. To account for possible unusable OCT images or missing data, 35 participants were included in each group. This sample size calculation was intended for the primary LP versus control comparison; subgroup analyses according to oral involvement were exploratory and not separately powered.

For the exploratory subgroup analysis according to oral involvement, p-values were considered unadjusted and interpreted cautiously because only six patients had oral involvement. Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction was applied to the subgroup comparisons to account for multiple testing.

For descriptive effect estimation, mean differences with 95% confidence intervals for continuous variables were calculated as LP group minus control group and are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Results

The study included 35 participants with LP and 35 healthy control participants. Only the right eye of each participant was included in the analysis. The LP group consisted of 15 females and 20 males, with a mean age of 35.37 ± 12.12 years (median: 32 years; range: 18–55 years). The control group included 16 females and 19 males, with a mean age of 31.29 ± 9.41 years (median: 28 years; range: 19–52 years). The two groups were comparable in terms of age (p = 0.176) and sex distribution (p = 0.810) (Table 1).

BCVA did not differ significantly between the LP and control groups (0.04 ± 0.03 logMAR vs. 0.03 ± 0.03 logMAR; p = 0.459). Tear function parameters were significantly lower in the LP group. The mean Schirmer I test score was 10.34 ± 2.17 mm in the LP group and 18.37 ± 2.35 mm in the control group (p < 0.001). Similarly, TBUT was shorter in the LP group than in the control group (10.74 ± 1.99 s vs. 13.06 ± 0.64 s; p < 0.001). No statistically significant differences were observed between the groups in AL (23.49 ± 0.23 mm vs. 23.48 ± 0.16 mm; p = 0.939) or IOP (14.37 ± 1.66 mmHg vs. 14.25 ± 1.84 mmHg; p = 0.467) (Table 1).

Intraobserver reliability of EDI-OCT measurements was evaluated using the ICC. The ICC values were 0.94 (95% CI: 0.90–0.97) for SFCT, 0.92 (95% CI: 0.87–0.96) for nasal choroidal thickness, and 0.93 (95% CI: 0.89–0.97) for temporal choroidal thickness, indicating high measurement reliability.

The pre-specified primary structural outcome, SFCT, was lower in the LP group than in the control group (299.43 ± 30.03 μm vs. 345.40 ± 41.89 μm; p < 0.001). The secondary choroidal outcomes, nasal and temporal choroidal thickness at 1500 μm, were also lower in the LP group. Nasal choroidal thickness was 272.23 ± 62.36 μm in the LP group and 303.60 ± 42.09 μm in the control group (p = 0.041), while temporal choroidal thickness was 283.43 ± 66.56 μm and 328.20 ± 42.51 μm, respectively (p = 0.011) (Table 1).

CRT was comparable between the LP and control groups (238.20 ± 21.29 μm vs. 231.54 ± 19.81 μm; p = 0.088). Likewise, no significant differences were found in RNFL thickness (101.46 ± 5.94 μm vs. 101.86 ± 5.90 μm; p = 0.782) or GCC thickness (92.26 ± 4.25 μm vs. 91.20 ± 3.30 μm; p = 0.373) (Table 1).

In multivariable linear regression analyses adjusted for age, sex, and AL, LP remained independently associated with lower choroidal thickness. Compared with the control group, participants with LP had significantly lower SFCT (adjusted B = − 46.61 μm, 95% CI: −64.71 to − 28.52, p < 0.001), nasal choroidal thickness at 1500 μm (adjusted B = − 29.46 μm, 95% CI: −55.81 to − 3.11, p = 0.029), and temporal choroidal thickness at 1500 μm (adjusted B = − 40.83 μm, 95% CI: −68.15 to − 13.50, p = 0.004). For the secondary retinal structural outcomes, LP was not independently associated with CRT (adjusted B = 5.81 μm, 95% CI: −3.76 to 15.39, p = 0.230), RNFL thickness (adjusted B = − 0.13 μm, 95% CI: −2.99 to 2.73, p = 0.928), or GCC thickness (adjusted B = 1.01 μm, 95% CI: −0.88 to 2.90, p = 0.289). Across all six regression models, no relevant multicollinearity was detected (VIF range, 1.03–1.04; tolerance range, 0.96–0.97), and residual diagnostics showed no major violations of normality, homoscedasticity, or linearity. Standardized residuals remained within ± 3 in five of the six models; one observation in the CRT model had a standardized residual of 3.35. However, Cook’s distance remained below 1 in all models (maximum, 0.261), and no highly influential observations were identified.

Patients with LP were further stratified according to the presence of oral involvement. Of the 35 patients with LP, 29 had cutaneous-only disease and 6 had concurrent oral involvement. BCVA was similar between the cutaneous-only and oral involvement subgroups (0.04 ± 0.03 logMAR vs. 0.04 ± 0.02 logMAR; p = 0.893). No statistically significant differences were observed between the cutaneous-only and oral involvement subgroups in Schirmer I test score, TBUT, AL, IOP, choroidal thickness measurements, CRT, or RNFL thickness (Table 2). In the unadjusted exploratory subgroup analysis, GCC thickness was lower in patients with oral involvement than in those with cutaneous-only LP (89.16 ± 3.31 μm vs. 92.89 ± 4.18 μm; unadjusted p = 0.039). However, this difference did not remain significant after Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction for multiple comparisons (adjusted q = 0.546). Therefore, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because only six patients had oral involvement and subgroup analyses were exploratory.

Discussion

This prospective case-control study evaluated tear function, CRT, choroidal thickness, RNFL thickness, and GCC thickness in patients with LP, with results compared to those of healthy controls with comparable age and sex distribution.

Schirmer I and TBUT values were significantly lower in the LP group compared with healthy controls. However, no correlations were found between these parameters and disease duration or the presence of oral involvement. Similarly, no differences in tear function were observed between patients with cutaneous-only LP and those with concurrent oral LP.

Several studies in the literature have reported tear dysfunction associated with LP. Mohebbi et al. [4] reported that conjunctival inflammation due to LP may lead to blepharitis, canalicular obstruction, and pronounced dry eye symptoms. Durrani et al. [8] identified canalicular obstruction in 8 of 184 LP cases, highlighting the disease’s impact on the lacrimal drainage system. Similarly, Brewer et al. [9] described a case series of 11 patients in which conjunctival involvement with scarring secondary to LP resulted in severe ocular surface disorders.

The impact of LP on the ocular surface is further supported by a conjunctival impression cytology study by Şanlı et al. [19]. That study reported significantly lower Schirmer I test scores in patients with LP, along with a marked reduction in goblet cell density. Although TBUT values were reduced in the LP group, statistical significance was not achieved, likely due to the limited sample size. These findings indicate that LP affects not only the skin and mucosal surfaces but also ocular parameters such as tear secretion, mucus production, and tear film stability.

Similarly, Özlü et al. [15] reported significantly lower Schirmer I and TBUT values in patients with LP and found negative associations of both parameters with disease duration and oral mucosal involvement. In contrast, although our study observed significant reductions in these parameters, no significant correlation was found with disease duration. This suggests that tear dysfunction in LP may follow a heterogeneous course, possibly due to individual variations in inflammatory responses. These findings suggest that LP may be associated with tear film instability and reduced tear secretion; however, the mechanisms underlying this association could not be directly evaluated in the present study. Therefore, patients with LP should be evaluated not only for dermatological findings but also for ocular symptoms, particularly dry eye manifestations, to facilitate early diagnosis and treatment.

Posterior segment analyses in our study revealed significantly reduced subfoveal, nasal, and temporal choroidal thickness in patients with LP compared with healthy controls, while CRT was comparable between groups. This association remained significant after adjustment for age, sex, and AL. No significant correlations were found between choroidal thickness measurements and disease duration or oral involvement. These findings are partly consistent with Özlü et al. [15], who reported reduced choroidal thickness and no significant difference in retinal thickness between patients with LP and healthy controls. Unlike our study, however, they found significant negative correlations of SFCT with both disease duration and oral mucosal involvement. In contrast, El-Sadek et al. [16] reported increased choroidal thickness in LP, suggesting that inflammatory activity and disease chronicity may contribute to the variability in choroidal findings.

Inflammatory skin diseases such as psoriasis, which share some immunopathogenetic features with LP, have also shown inconsistent choroidal findings. Türkcü et al. [20] reported increased choroidal thickness, whereas Kılıç et al. [21] found no significant difference but observed a positive correlation with disease duration. Aksoy et al. [22] reported increased choroidal thickness and RNFL thinning in severe psoriasis, while Ersan et al. [13] found no overall difference but noted increased SFCT in severe cases. These findings suggest that choroidal thickness changes in inflammatory skin diseases may be influenced not only by disease presence or duration, but also by disease severity, inflammatory activity, vascular involvement, disease control, and individual immune responses.

A review by Steiner et al. [12] similarly emphasized that choroidal thickness may vary according to disease activity and chronicity in systemic autoimmune and inflammatory diseases. However, this early-phase thickening/chronic-phase thinning framework should be interpreted cautiously when applied to our LP cohort, as the present study did not include disease activity scores, inflammatory biomarkers, OCTA, or choroidal vascularity indices. In our study, disease duration was based on the documented interval between dermatological diagnosis and ophthalmological examination, rather than initial symptom onset. Thus, the relatively short documented duration may not fully reflect the actual disease duration or cumulative inflammatory burden of LP. The observed choroidal thinning should therefore be interpreted as an associative finding rather than evidence of a specific chronic inflammatory, vascular, or atrophic mechanism.

The clinical significance of this choroidal thinning also remains uncertain. In the present cohort, BCVA and CRT were comparable between the LP and control groups, suggesting that the observed choroidal difference was not associated with detectable visual functional impairment or macular structural alteration in this study. Moreover, no validated threshold currently exists to define clinically meaningful choroidal thinning in LP. Therefore, although choroidal thickness may provide useful structural information in research settings, it should not yet be considered a validated biomarker of ocular involvement in LP.

In addition to choroidal thickness and CRT, this study evaluated RNFL and GCC thickness in patients with LP. No significant differences in RNFL or GCC thickness were observed between the LP and control groups. Previous studies in psoriasis, a chronic inflammatory skin disease with some immunopathogenetic similarities to LP, have reported inconsistent findings regarding RNFL and ganglion cell layer parameters. Korkmaz et al. [23] reported preserved RNFL thickness but reduced GCC thickness, whereas Ersan et al. [13] and Şahin et al. [24] found no significant differences in RNFL or macular ganglion cell–inner plexiform layer thickness. In contrast, Aksoy et al. [22] reported significant RNFL thinning in patients with severe psoriasis. In our exploratory subgroup analysis, GCC thickness was lower in patients with oral involvement in the unadjusted comparison. However, this finding was based on only six patients with oral involvement and did not remain significant after Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction for multiple comparisons. Therefore, it should not be interpreted as evidence of neurosensory damage or subgroup-specific retinal involvement. Larger studies with adequate subgroup sample sizes, standardized LP activity assessment, inflammatory biomarkers, and multimodal retinal imaging are needed to determine whether oral involvement has any independent association with inner retinal structural parameters in LP.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was relatively limited, especially in the oral involvement subgroup, which included only six patients. Therefore, subgroup analyses were exploratory and underpowered for definitive clinical or mechanistic conclusions. Although Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction was applied, the GCC difference did not remain significant after correction and should be interpreted cautiously. Second, disease duration was based on the documented interval between dermatological diagnosis and ophthalmological examination. Because some patients may have had LP manifestations before seeking dermatological care, the reported duration may underestimate the actual duration and cumulative inflammatory burden of the disease, limiting interpretation of chronicity-related ocular mechanisms. Third, manual choroidal thickness measurements were performed on anonymized OCT images by an examiner masked to group allocation and oral involvement status. However, interobserver reliability was not assessed. Therefore, observer-dependent variability inherent to manual measurements cannot be fully excluded. Fourth, a standardized LP severity or activity score was not available. Inflammatory biomarkers were also not assessed. Consequently, we could not determine whether the ocular findings were related to current dermatological activity, disease severity, or systemic inflammatory status. Fifth, although only treatment-naïve patients were included, the single-time-point study design did not allow assessment of longitudinal changes in ocular parameters. Sixth, OCTA and choroidal vascularity index measurements were not performed. Therefore, vascular or stromal mechanisms underlying choroidal thickness changes could not be directly evaluated. Seventh, objective tear function tests were assessed, but a dry eye symptom score, corneal fluorescein staining, tear osmolarity, meibomian gland evaluation, and detailed ocular surface staining scores were not included. Therefore, the tear findings should be interpreted as objective tear function abnormalities rather than as a comprehensive dry eye disease assessment. Finally, the strict exclusion criteria reduced potential confounding but may limit generalizability. In particular, smokers, individuals with systemic diseases, contact lens users, and participants with high refractive error were excluded. Overall, these limitations restrict the causal, mechanistic, and clinical interpretation of the findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, patients with LP showed reduced tear function and lower choroidal thickness compared with healthy controls, and the association between LP and reduced choroidal thickness remained significant after adjustment for age, sex, and AL. No significant differences were observed in RNFL or GCC thickness between the overall LP and control groups. The lower GCC thickness observed in the oral involvement subgroup was exploratory and did not remain significant after correction for multiple comparisons; therefore, it should not be interpreted as evidence of neurosensory impairment. Overall, these findings should be interpreted as associative rather than mechanistic. Further longitudinal studies incorporating standardized LP activity scores, inflammatory biomarkers, OCTA, choroidal vascularity indices, dry eye symptom assessment, and larger oral involvement subgroups are needed to clarify the ocular implications of LP.
