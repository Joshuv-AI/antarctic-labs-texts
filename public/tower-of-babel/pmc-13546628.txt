Fatal Pharyngeal Cellulitis Caused by Rhizopus microsporus in a Patient with Acute Myeloid Leukemia
Authors: Junqing Wu, Xueping Luo, Shan Fu, Shuihong Zhou, Jianqin He, Dan Zhang, Weiyan Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPatients with immunodeficiency are highly susceptible to life-threatening fungal infections. Pharyngeal cellulitis caused by Rhizopus microsporus are exceptionally rare.Case PresentationWe reported a case of relapsed acute myeloid leukemia (AML) complicated by post-chemotherapy Rhizopus microsporus pharyngeal cellulitis. This cellulitis resulted in severe tissue necrosis, pharyngeal obstruction, and sub-sequent suffocation. Emergency bedside tracheotomy was administered after acute respiratory distress. The metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) identified Rhizopus microsporus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Candida albicans, and SARS-CoV-2. Despite surgical intervention and combination antimicrobial therapy (amphotericin B, posaconazole, daptomycin, ceftriaxone, and molnupiravir), the patient stabilized for 2 months before culminating in fatal carotid artery rupture.ConclusionRhizopus microsporus-related pharyngeal cellulitis is rare yet highly aggressive, demanding timely diagnosis and close monitoring. This case highlights the critical role of rapid mNGS in diagnosing polymicrobial infections, underscores the necessity of combining aggressive surgical debridement with antifungal/antimicrobial regimens, and stresses rigorous surveillance to prevent life-threatening vascular complications.Keywords: Rhizopus microsporus, throat obstruction, metagenomic next-generation sequencing, immune dysfunction, acute myeloid leukemia

Introduction

Pharyngeal masses most commonly arise from cysts, neoplasms, or foreign bodies.1 In contrast, oropharyngeal obstruction resulting from fungal infection with associated tissue necrosis is exceedingly uncommon.

Rhizopus species are invasive opportunistic fungi classified within the Mucoraceae family.2 The global incidence of mucormycosis ranges from 0.05 to 9.5 cases per 100,000 population annually.3 India bears the highest disease burden worldwide, with an incidence of 14 cases per 100,000 people per year, compared to 0.3 in the United States and 0.12 in France.3

Rhizopus microsporus is ubiquitously distributed in the environment and commonly isolated from soil and decaying plant debris.4 It propagates via sporangiospores, and human infection occurs through three main routes: inhalation of airborne spores, ingestion of contaminated food, or direct percutaneous inoculation into open wounds.4 Microscopic examination of Rhizopus microsporus isolated from tracheal aspirate cultures reveals broad, aseptate hyphae, alongside spherical sporangiophores and rhizoids.5,6 It predominantly affects immunocompromised hosts, such as patients with hematologic malignancies, uncontrolled diabetes, or those receiving immunosuppressive therapy.7–10

Mucormycosis features vascular invasion, which causes vascular occlusion, impaired tissue perfusion and subsequent tissue necrosis.11 Tissue necrosis is an indispensable hallmark of this infection.12 Multiple factors govern the virulence of Mucorales. First, CotH proteins interact with host endothelial GRP78 (glucose-regulated protein) to facilitate adhesion and endothelial penetration. Second, the high-affinity iron acquisition system triggers a cascade of events that drives angioinvasion and tissue necrosis. Third, abundant secreted proteases, lipases and glycosidases further promote tissue and vascular infiltration.13

The COVID-19 pandemic has been linked to a notable increase in mucormycosis cases.14 COVID-19-associated mucormycosis arises partly from the pathogens’ thermotolerance, which enables survival at elevated temperatures. Additional COVID-related risk drivers include endothelial injury, hyperferritinemia, excess iron/zinc, and severe hyperglycemia.15

Rhino-orbital-cerebral mucormycosis is the most prevalent clinical subtype, followed by pulmonary mucormycosis.3,16 By contrast, clinical presentation with pharyngeal cellulitis constitutes a rare phenotypic variant of this disease.

Mucormycosis is characterized by rapid progression and carries a high mortality rate of approximately 46%.2,16 Poor prognostic indicators include neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, positive cultures from non-sterile sites, steroid use, and the absence of surgical intervention.17

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) has emerged as a vital tool for etiological diagnosis of fungal infections. It exhibits prominent strengths including high analytical sensitivity, capability to identify mixed infections, and capacity to detect rare fungal pathogens. Nevertheless, its clinical application is limited by several drawbacks such as false-positive signals, incomplete microbial reference databases, lack of uniform operational standards, and relatively high testing costs.18

We presented a case of a patient with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) who developed pharyngeal cellulitis after chemotherapy, resulting in throat obstruction and tissue necrosis. Sputum culture and mNGS concurrently identified Rhizopus microsporus, Candida albicans, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and SARS‑CoV‑2, while blood cultures yielded negative results. Although surgical debridement and systemic antifungal therapy were administered, the clinical course was complicated by carotid artery rupture, which resulted in a fatal outcome.

Case Presentation

The patient was a 54-year-old female diagnosed with AML for about six years. She achieved complete remission (CR) after induction chemotherapy and then underwent additional courses of consolidation chemotherapy. She remained in continuous CR for three years before relapsing and received further courses of chemotherapy, achieving CR again. Half a year later, she relapsed once more, prompting her current admission.

Upon admission, she received anti-leukemia chemotherapy. On the third day post-chemotherapy, she suffered from neutropenia and developed a sore throat and left neck swelling with a neutrophil count of 0.09×109/L. Two days later, her symptoms worsened, with throat obstruction, increased neck swelling, and a fever of 38.5°C. Laboratory findings showed a C-reactive protein of 148.5 mg/L. A throat swab con-firmed SARS-CoV-2 infection. She was initially treated with imipenem and vancomycin, followed by cefoperazone sulbactam. No specific antiviral therapy for SARS-CoV-2 was available at the time. Computed tomography (CT) revealed low-density lesions in the nasopharynx and oropharynx with gas accumulation, suggestive of cellulitis (Figure 1A). The sputum culture yielded Candida albicans, while the blood culture result was negative. Subsequently, the blood mNGS analysis was performed, which detected Rhizopus microsporus with a DNA sequence read count of 1224. Daily sputum and blood cultures were conducted. Nine days later, Rhizopus microsporus was identified in the sputum culture, whereas the blood culture remained negative. After an additional 5 days, the sputum cultures detected the Klebsiella pneumoniae. On the following day, a biopsy of the pharyngeal mass confirmed the presence of fungal elements, including Candida and Aspergillus or Mucor species (Figure 2A and B). Concurrently, mNGS analysis was also conducted on the mass tissue. The results showed DNA sequence corresponded to Klebsiella pneumoniae (sequence read count of 1,197,108), Rhizopus microsporus (1,142,186), and Candida albicans (26,499). RNA sequences corresponded to Klebsiella pneumoniae (30,310), Rhizopus microsporus (10,349), Candida albicans (185), and SARS-CoV-2 (21). Combining the patient’s clinical manifestations and multiple laboratory findings, clinicians confirmed a polymicrobial pharyngeal coinfection, among which Rhizopus microsporus was the predominant pathogen. The antimicrobial therapy was given, including amphotericin B, posaconazole (focus on Rhizopus microsporus), ceftriaxone (focus on Klebsiella pneumoniae).

During the course of treatment, the lesion underwent marked enlargement (Figure 1B and C). The patient suffered a sudden airway obstruction followed by cardiac arrest, necessitating an emergency bedside tracheotomy and ventilator-assisted ventilation support. Follow-up CT showed progression of cellulitis, with infection extending to the left external carotid artery (Figure 1D). At that time, molnupiravir for SARS-CoV-2 was administered. Surgical intervention was performed to resect the pharyngeal mass and debride necrotic tissue (Figure 2C and D). Intraoperative findings revealed extensive involvement of the soft palate, uvula, tonsils, and posterior pharyngeal wall. Histopathological examination confirmed necrosis of fibrous, fatty, and muscle tissues, with fungal hyphae consistent with Aspergillus or Mucor species (Figure 2E). The exposed internal carotid artery was covered with a tissue flap. Postoperatively, the patient continued on anti-infective therapy, and carotid artery CT angiography (CTA) showed no abnormalities. However, two months after discharge to a local hospital, she died from hemorrhagic shock secondary to internal carotid artery rupture.

Discussion

Mucormycosis is categorized into six distinct clinical variants according to the primary site of involvement: rhino-orbital-cerebral, pulmonary, cutaneous, gastrointestinal, disseminated, and rare-site infections.19,20 Rhino-orbital-cerebral involvement is the most common, followed by pulmonary infections.14 Mucormycic rhinosinusitis may extend into the oral cavity and present with odontalgia.21 One previous study summarized a total of only 16 patients diagnosed with upper respiratory tract mucormycosis, with lesions distributed in the larynx, subglottis and trachea.22 Another case involving the parapharyngeal space has also been reported.23 Our case illustrated a localization to the oropharyngeal region, a rare site of primary infection.

Mucorales produce sporangiospores, which are inhaled into the respiratory tract. These spores adhere to and invade respiratory epithelial cells via surface proteins. Germination of the spores generates hyphae that cross tissue planes, ultimately causing tissue ischemia, necrosis, and hematogenous dissemination.11 A pathognomonic feature of the infection is its marked angioinvasion, resulting in widespread thrombosis and subsequent tissue necrosis.24 In our case, the infection resulted in tissue necrosis and the rapid development of the mass, culminating in air-way obstruction and cardiac arrest, which necessitated an emergency bedside tracheotomy.

The patient died from carotid artery rupture several months after surgery. This fatal complication likely stemmed from the angioinvasion of the internal carotid artery by Rhizopus microsporus, which induced fibrosis, thinning and destruction of the vascular wall.11 Surgical debridement was not able to restore the structural integrity of the damaged arterial wall. The diseased vessel, previously partially encased by necrotic tissue, became further exposed intraoperatively. Persistent residual infection continued to erode the vascular wall and predispose the formation of a pseudoaneurysm.11 Exposed vessel tissue was subjected to repetitive mechanical irritation and hemodynamic fluctuations. Ultimately, rupture of the internal carotid pseudoaneurysm triggered catastrophic hemorrhaging resulting in death.

Radiologically, mucormycosis may be suggested by findings such as the “halo sign” on computed tomography, characterized by a perimeter of ground-glass opacity surrounding a nodular infiltrate.2 Nevertheless, imaging features often lack specificity, and clinical symptoms can be non-discriminatory, frequently leading to diagnostic delays that contribute to the persistently high mortality associated with the disease. In this case, the pharyngeal CT scan only revealed a hypodense lesion, which was highly suggestive of infection. Subsequent CT scans indicated continuous enlargement of the lesion, yet no specific imaging features pointed toward particular pathogens. Relying solely on radiological findings, it was impossible to quickly identify the causative agent or establish a definitive diagnosis.

While CT imaging showed gas within the lesion, Rhizopus microsporus itself is not a known gas-producer. We therefore suspect that the gas arose from other causes. The most likely explanation is a secondary bacterial infection following fungal mucosal damage, with gas resulting from bacterial metabolism. Additionally, gas could be released during tissue necrosis or introduced from the oropharynx during swallowing, given its direct exposure to the external environment.

The diagnostic gold standard of mucormycosis remains histopathological demonstration of invasive hyphae accompanied by a positive fungal culture.25 Although fungal cultures offer high specificity, their clinical utility is limited by prolonged turnaround times, considerable false-negative rates, and an overall sensitivity of only approximately 25%.26 In our case, daily blood and sputum cultures were performed. The blood cultures consistently yielded negative results. Nine days after the blood mNGS results were obtained, Rhizopus microsporus was first identified in the sputum culture. Additionally, a biopsy of the pharyngeal mass was conducted. However, histopathological analysis was unable to precisely identify the specific type of fungus. In contrast, mNGS proved instrumental in achieving an early diagnosis and guiding subsequent antimicrobial strategy.27

The mNGS enables the detection of Mucorales nucleic acids in peripheral blood and tissue specimens as an auxiliary diagnostic assay.11 It is particularly valuable for critically ill, complicated cases where specimens from the infection site are inaccessible, conventional tests yield negative results, or rapid pathogen identification is urgently required.28 For high-risk patients, mNGS testing can be submitted concurrently alongside traditional examinations including microbial culture and histopathology as an adjunctive diagnostic measure.28 Repeated testing is recommended to improve diagnostic accuracy.29 Nevertheless, false-positive results are observed with mNGS. A single positive mNGS cannot independently establish a definitive diagnosis.30 Clinicians should also remain cautious when low read counts of Mucorales are detected.31 The comprehensive interpretation integrating host risk factors, clinical manifestations, imaging findings, routine microbiology and histopathological evidence is mandatory.28 Retrospective study reported that the positive predictive value of peripheral blood mNGS for identifying Mucorales sequences stands at approximately 72.6%.32 This indicates a substantial false-positive rate, predominantly attributed to environmental contamination, reagent contamination, and nucleic acid cross-contamination arising during specimen processing.28 In our case, the patient was immunocompromised with concurrent COVID-19 infection, placing him at high risk for mucormycosis. The patient presented with recurrent high-grade fever, and imaging examinations revealed oropharyngeal cellulitis. Rhizopus microsporus was detected via blood mNGS, while the identical pathogen was identified from sputum culture after 9 days. As the patient’s oropharyngeal cellulitis progressively enlarged, a biopsy of the pharyngeal mass was performed. The tissue mNGS confirmed the presence of Rhizopus microsporus. Subsequent histopathological examination of the resected pharyngeal lesion after surgical excision verified Mucorales infection. The patient achieved transient clinical improvement following targeted anti-fungal therapy. Combined with clinical manifestations, laboratory tests, imaging findings and histopathological evidence, mNGS facilitates rapid pathogen identification. It provides reliable laboratory evidence for timely initiation of targeted anti-infective treatment and helps optimize patient clinical outcomes.

Tissue mNGS indicated a polymicrobial infection. We believe that Rhizopus microsporus played the most significant role. The infection with Rhizopus microsporus was of longer duration compared to the other pathogens, and had a high sequence count on mNGS. Besides, Rhizopus microsporus is known for its highly aggressive vascular invasion, and is able to lead to tissue necrosis.11 SARS-CoV-2 might act as the initial trigger by damaging the respiratory mucosal and epithelial barrier, impairing host immune clearance capacity and thereby creating a permissive microenvironment for secondary infections.33 As opportunistic pathogens, Klebsiella pneumoniae and Candida albicans could cause invasive infection, which synergizes with Rhizopus microsporus to induce localized cellulitis.

The management of mucormycosis rests on a triad of foundational measures: prompt administration of systemic antifungal therapy, aggressive surgical debridement of necrotic tissues, and diligent correction of underlying predisposing risk factors. First-line antifungal agents include liposomal amphotericin B, posaconazole, and isavuconazole.34 The superiority of combination antifungal therapy over monotherapy remains controversial.35 In this case, a combination of amphotericin B and posaconazole was instituted, resulting in initial clinical stabilization. However, this favorable response was ultimately overshadowed by a catastrophic carotid artery rupture, likely precipitated by surgical trauma and arterial exposure, and vascular invasion of Mucor infection, culminating in fatal hemorrhagic shock. This tragic outcome underscores the critical importance of meticulous surgical technique and comprehensive postoperative surveillance in such high-risk scenarios.

Our case has several limitations. First, the patient suffered from polymicrobial mixed infection, making it impossible to accurately separate the respective synergistic tissue-damaging effects of different pathogens. Second, conventional tests featured long turnaround time and low positivity rates. It took several days to obtain positive sputum culture results, and histopathology failed to achieve accurate fungal species identification. Although mNGS enables rapid pathogen identification, it serves merely as an auxiliary diagnostic tool rather than the gold standard. Third, anti-infective medications were likely administered with a delay. Even after surgical resection of lesions, the infection had progressed rapidly and invaded vital blood vessels. Lastly, findings from this single case possess limited generalizability. The patient belonged to a distinctive population with chemotherapy-induced neutropenia complicated by SARS-CoV-2 infection. Without a control cohort, the diagnostic and therapeutic experience summarized here cannot be readily extrapolated to other patients with mucormycosis.

Conclusion

We herein reported a patient with AML who developed chemotherapy-induced neutropenia. Severe pharyngeal cellulitis occurred as a result of coinfection of SARS-CoV-2, Rhizopus microsporus, Klebsiella pneumoniae and Candida albicans. Despite combination anti-infective pharmacotherapy and surgical intervention, the patient ultimately succumbed to internal carotid artery rupture, which was predominantly driven by angioinvasion of Rhizopus microsporus. This case underscores the critical clinical value of early diagnosis and sustained long-term surveillance. It high-lights the utility of mNGS for rapid diagnosis and affirms the necessity of a combined modality approach incorporating both surgical intervention and antifungal therapy. Despite initial improvement, the patient succumbed to carotid artery rupture, emphasizing the imperative for vigilant postoperative management and the persistent challenges in treating mucormycosis in profoundly immunocompromised hosts.
