Noble gravitational atoms: Self-gravitating black hole scalar wigs with angular momentum number
Miguel Alcubierre 1, Juan Barranco 2, Argelia Bernal 2, Juan Carlos Degollado 3, Alberto Diez-Tejedor 2, Miguel Megevand 4, Darío Núñez 1, Olivier Sarbach 5

Abstract
We present new spherically symmetric solutions of the Einstein-Klein-Gordon equations in a quasi-stationary approximation that describe self-gravitating scalar field configurations around a black hole, including angular momentum number ℓ \ell . An approach analogous to the one which gives rise to ℓ \ell -boson stars is used here to construct self-gravitating “gravitational atoms” with ℓ ≥ 0 \ell\geq 0 . We refer to these new solutions as noble gravitational atoms , by analogy with noble atoms, which are characterized by closed electron shells. We show that, in the proper limit, noble gravitational atoms approach ℓ \ell -boson stars globally, displaying noticeable differences only in a region very close to the event horizon. Noble gravitational atoms with ℓ > 0 \ell>0 sometimes present density maxima located at relatively large radii, with small density close to the horizon for ℓ > 1 \ell>1 . Furthermore, they do not always present the typical density spike at the event horizon if ℓ > 0 \ell>0 ; on the contrary, they sometimes exhibit a small dip there. When ℓ = 0 \ell=0 , a spike can appear, but its contribution to the total mass density is always negligible. The size, density, and lifetime of these objects vary significantly depending on the parameters, being in some cases as large as galaxies, as dilute as dark matter, and as long-lived as the Universe itself.

Next, let us analyze the self-gravitating case. Here we introduce the parameter

\displaystyle p_{0}

2.5
Accretion time and validity of the quasi-stationary approximation

Here, we briefly discuss the range of validity of the quasi-stationary approximation by analyzing the time evolution of the metric coefficients.
So far, we have not considered the equation for
\dot{m}
.
Using equations (
3a
,
3b
) one finds

\dot{m}=\frac{\kappa r^{2}}{2}\frac{a}{\gamma^{2}}\left\{\frac{2m}{r}\left[T(n,n)+T(w,w)\right]+\left[1+\left(\frac{2m}{r}\right)^{2}\right]T(n,w)\right\},

(33)

in the generalized Eddington-Finkelstein gauge. Next, using equations (
11a
,
12a
,
12b
,
12c
) one obtains

\dot{m}=\kappa_{\ell}r^{2}ae^{2\sigma t}\left[\frac{2m}{r}\left|\frac{s}{a}\psi_{\ell}\right|^{2}+\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)\mbox{Re}\left(\frac{s^{*}}{a}\psi_{\ell}^{*}\psi_{\ell}^{\prime}\right)\right].

(34)

At the horizon
r=2m=2M
and at
t=0
this simplifies to

\dot{m_{0}}:=\left.\dot{m}\right|_{t=0,r=2M}=\kappa_{\ell}\frac{(2M)^{2}|s|^{2}|A|^{2}}{\left.a\right|_{t=0,r=2M}},

(35)

which gives the mass accretion rate. Accordingly, we can introduce the time scale

t_{a}:=\frac{M}{\dot{m_{0}}}=\frac{\left.a\right|_{t=0,r=2M}}{4\kappa_{\ell}M|s|^{2}|A|^{2}},

(36)

and the quasi-stationary approximation is expected to be valid as long as

0\leq t\ll t_{0}:=\frac{1}{|\sigma|},\qquad 0\leq t\ll t_{a}.

(37)

Additionally, we will find it useful to evaluate the quotients
t_{0}/t_{L}

and
t_{a}/t_{L}
, which provide an estimate of the lifetime
t_{0}
and the accretion time
t_{a}
relative to the light-crossing time of the object,
t_{L}=2R_{99}
.
Here, we have defined, as usual,
R_{99}
as the radius of a sphere containing 99% of the object’s mass.
As a reference, the universe’s age relative to the Milky Way galaxy’s light-crossing time is approximately 68,000. We will see that many of our solutions have
t_{0}/t_{L}
and
t_{a}/t_{L}
much larger than this value.

2.6
\ell
-Boson star limit

Next, we show that the eigenvalue problem (
18
) for
(\psi_{\ell},m,a)
reduces to the corresponding problem determining
\ell
-boson stars when
s=i\omega
is purely imaginary and the horizon boundary conditions (
30
) are replaced with regularity conditions at the center.

To this purpose, one first needs to understand the relation between the generalized Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates
(t,r)
and the Schwarzschild-type coordinates
(T,r)
, say, which are usually used to construct the boson star solutions. If the metric functions
a
and
m
depend on
r
only, it is simple to verify that the coordinate transformation

t=T+\Theta(r),\qquad\Theta(r):=\int^{r}\frac{2m(\tilde{r})}{\tilde{r}-2m(\tilde{r})}\frac{d\tilde{r}}{a(\tilde{r})},

(38)

brings the metric (
1
) into the form

ds^{2}=-a(r)^{2}\left(1-\frac{2m(r)}{r}\right)dT^{2}+\frac{dr^{2}}{1-\frac{2m(r)}{r}}+r^{2}d\Omega^{2}.

(39)

Of course, the metric functions
a(r)
and
m(r)
are unaffected by this transformation, since they only depend on
r
by assumption. In contrast to this, the scalar field profile
\psi_{\ell}(r)
which is related to the time-dependent scalar field
\phi_{\ell}(t,r)
via the mode ansatz (
10
) is affected by this transformation since

\phi_{\ell}(t,r)=e^{st}\psi_{\ell}(r)=e^{sT}e^{s\Theta(r)}\psi_{\ell}(r),

(40)

such that with respect to Schwarzschild-type coordinates, the scalar field profile is

\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}(r)=e^{s\Theta(r)}\psi_{\ell}(r).

(41)

If
s=i\omega
is purely imaginary, this is only a phase difference; however, the derivative of the profile transforms non-trivially:

\tilde{\psi}^{\prime}_{\ell}(r)=e^{s\Theta(r)}\left[\psi^{\prime}_{\ell}(r)+\frac{s}{a}\frac{2m}{r-2m}\psi_{\ell}(r)\right],

(42)

and we see that the second term diverges at
r=2m
.

Re-expressing the system (
18
)
in terms of
\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}
one finds:

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\tilde{B}\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}^{\prime}+\tilde{C}\tilde{\psi}_{\ell},

(43a)

\displaystyle 2m^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{\ell}r^{2}\left[\frac{1}{1-\frac{2m}{r}}\frac{\omega^{2}}{a^{2}}\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}^{2}+\mu_{\ell}^{2}\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}^{2}+\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}^{\prime 2}\right],

(43b)

\displaystyle\frac{a^{\prime}}{a}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{\ell}r\left[\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}^{\prime 2}+\frac{1}{1-\frac{2m}{r}}\frac{\omega^{2}}{a^{2}}\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}^{2}\right],

(43c)

where we have assumed without loss of generality that
\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}
is real-valued, and where

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{B}&:=B+\frac{4m}{r}\frac{s}{a},\\
\tilde{C}&:=C-\frac{s}{a}\frac{B}{1-\frac{2m}{r}}+s\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)\left[\frac{1}{a}\frac{1}{1-\frac{2m}{r}}\right]^{\prime}.\end{split}

(44)

Taking into account that
\alpha:=a\sqrt{1-2m/r}
is the lapse, equations (
43b
,
43c
) agree with equations (7a) and (7b) in Ref.
[
42
]
discussing the
\ell
-boson star configurations (note that in that reference
\gamma
is defined as
\gamma=(1-2m/r)^{-1/2}
which is different than in this article).
Note also that equation (
34
) for
\dot{m}
is transformed into

\dot{m}=\kappa_{\ell}r^{2}\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)\mbox{Re}\left(s^{*}\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}^{*}\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}^{\prime}\right),

(45)

under the transformation (
41
), whose right-hand side is zero when
s
is purely imaginary and
\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}
is real.

Although the transformation (
41
) maps the problem (
18
) for
(\psi_{\ell},m,a)
to the problem (
43
) for
(\tilde{\psi}_{\ell},m,a)
which, together with appropriate regularity conditions at
r=0
and asymptotic conditions at
r\to\infty
yields the
\ell
-boson star solutions, it is by no means evident that the solutions of (
18
) with the horizon boundary conditions (
30
) should converge to regular solutions of (
43
) in the limit
r_{0}=2M\to 0
. Therefore, this problem will be analyzed numerically in the next section.

2.7
Test field limit

In the test-field limit, where the mass is constant

m(r)=M
and
a(r)=1
, as discussed at the beginning of Section
2.4
, we introduce for convenience the scaled function

\frac{1}{r}\tilde{u}_{\ell}=\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}
, so that in this case the equation (
43a
) can be written in terms of the variable
z
as
[
24
]
:

-\left(\frac{z}{z+1}\frac{d}{dz}\right)^{2}\tilde{u}_{\ell}+\left[\frac{\ell(\ell+1)z}{(z+2)^{3}}+\frac{z}{(z+1)^{4}}-\frac{4(\mu M)^{2}}{z+1}\right]\tilde{u}_{\ell}=-\Omega^{2}\tilde{u}_{\ell}\;,

(46)

where

\Omega=2M\sqrt{\mu^{2}+s^{2}}
.
Equation (
46
) has two regular points at
z=0
and
z=-1
, and an irregular point at
z=\infty
, and thus its solution can be written taking this into account, close to
z=0
.
Regular solutions that also describe the ingoing modes toward the horizon close to
z=0
behave as
z^{-2Ms}
. Furthermore, close to
z=1
the solution behaves as
(z+1)
.
In the asymptotic region
z\rightarrow\infty
, we are interested in solutions with exponential decay of the form
e^{-\Omega z}
.
A solution to equation (
46
) that satisfies the required behavior near
z=0
can be written in terms of the confluent Heun function
H_{C}

[
51
]
as

\tilde{u}_{\ell}(z)=(z+1)z^{2Ms}e^{-\Omega z}H_{C}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,1,2\Omega,-z)\;,

(47)

where

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\ell(\ell+1)+4M^{2}(\mu^{2}+2s^{2})+\Omega(1+4Ms)-2Ms\;,

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=4M^{2}(\mu^{2}+2s^{2})+2\Omega(1+2Ms)\;,\qquad\gamma=1+4Ms\;.

The function
H_{C}
is defined with the convention used in
Mathematica

[
52
]
.
Furthermore, equation (
46
) can be written as a Schrödinger-like equation,

\left[-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{r^{*}}^{2}}+V_{\rm eff}(r^{*})\right]\tilde{u}_{\ell}(r^{*})=-s^{2}\tilde{u}_{\ell}(r^{*}),

(48)

with the effective potential given by

V_{\rm eff}(r^{*}):=\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)\left[\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^{2}}+\frac{2M}{r^{3}}+\mu^{2}\right],\qquad r=r(r^{*}),

(49)

where the Regge-Wheeler tortoise coordinate
r^{*}:=r+2M\ln(r/2M-1)\,\in(-\infty,\infty)
has been used.

The effective potential has a local minimum if the following condition is satisfied
[
22
]
:

(\mu M)^{2}<-\frac{1}{32}(\ell^{2}+\ell-1)(\ell^{2}+\ell+1)^{2}+\frac{1}{288}\sqrt{3(3\ell^{4}+6\ell^{3}+5\ell^{2}+2\ell+3)^{3}}\,,

(50)

which is an indication that quasi-bound solutions for the scalar field can exist (see also table
1
).

The test field values of
s=\omega+i\sigma
in an approximation valid for small
\mu M
are given in
[
36
]
. The expressions are

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{n\ell}}{\mu}

\displaystyle\approx\sqrt{1-\frac{(\mu M)^{2}}{(\ell+n)^{2}}}\,,

(51a)

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{n\ell}}{\mu}

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{2^{4\ell+4}\,(\mu M)^{4\ell+5}\,(2\ell+n)!\,\ell!^{2}\,\prod\limits_{j=1}^{\ell}\left(16\left(\mu M\right)^{2}+j^{2}\right)}{(\ell+n)^{2\ell+4}\,(n-1)!\,(2\ell)!^{2}\,(2\ell+1)!^{2}}\,.

(51b)

Note that here we define
n
such that
n=1
gives the ground-state solution. Hence, the results shown here correspond to evaluating these expressions at
n=1
.
We note that the procedure in
[
36
]
is in principle only valid for
\ell>0
. However, as we have verified in
[
23
]
, these expressions are also true for
\ell=0
.

In figure
1
, we show some effective potentials and some “energy levels”
\omega_{n\ell}
and density profiles for an illustrative example with

and

We see that the energy levels accumulate at the asymptotic value of

The density profiles show an oscillatory behavior inside the potential well, where

4
Conclusions

We have presented new astrophysically relevant solutions to the Einstein-Klein-Gordon equations, describing quasi-stationary systems that contain a black hole surrounded by a self-gravitating scalar field distribution. Although spherically symmetric, these objects are parametrized by an angular momentum number
\ell
, and therefore they have been named “noble gravitational atoms”.
Noble gravitational atoms can vary greatly in shape, size, mass, and lifetime, depending on the following parameters: the angular momentum number
\ell
, the scalar field mass
\mu
, the black hole mass
M
, and the scalar field amplitude
A
.

One feature that differentiates
\ell>0
from
\ell=0
noble gravitational atoms is that the former admit solutions with a density maximum located far away from the black hole horizon.
Additionally, they do not always exhibit a density spike at the horizon; sometimes, they show a small dip instead. Nonetheless, we have seen that in all cases, these spikes and dips have a negligible contribution to the mass distribution.
This behavior contrasts with the expectations for conventional cold dark matter and could, at least in principle, offer a way to discriminate between the two hypotheses.

We also find some similarities between the
\ell>0
and the
\ell=0
cases.
Some solutions are extremely dilute, extremely large relative to the central black hole, and extremely long-lived, with lifetimes even larger than that of the Universe.
Interestingly, these properties, together with the particular values of black hole and scalar field masses for which they exist, are compatible with ultralight dark matter in galactic cores.
Large, dilute, and long-lasting noble gravitational atoms with
\ell>0
are also similar to those with
\ell=0
in that they coincide almost perfectly with
\ell
-boson stars (of the same
\ell
), except only for an extremely small region close to the event horizon.

In general, noble gravitational atoms, as their
\ell
-boson star counterparts,
can become larger as the angular momentum number
\ell
increases.
This characteristic may help extend the galactic halo core model mentioned earlier to cover a larger fraction of the galaxy, more so if one also considers solutions that combine multiple angular momentum numbers
\ell
(and possibly even multiple mode numbers
n
beyond the ground state), potentially allowing the model to reproduce the full galactic size.
We leave such studies for future work.

Appendix B
Collisionless cold dark matter halo model

In this appendix, we provide the details needed to reproduce the green curve of figure
5
. In the collisionless cold dark matter model, the combined black hole dark matter halo system is commonly described using the following piecewise density profile
[
7
,
56
,
57
]

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm CDM}(r)=\begin{cases}0&r<r_{0},\\
\rho_{\mathrm{NFW}}(R_{\mathrm{sp}})\left(\dfrac{R_{\mathrm{sp}}}{r}\right)^{\gamma}&r_{0}\leqslant r<R_{\mathrm{sp}}\\
\rho_{\mathrm{NFW}}(r)&r\geqslant R_{\mathrm{sp}},\end{cases},

(57)

where each radial interval is determined by the astrophysical processes that dominate at that particular scale.

In the region
r\geq R_{\textrm{sp}}
, where the influence of the black hole is negligible, we assume the Navarro-Frenk-White profile,

\rho_{\mathrm{NFW}}(r)=\frac{\rho_{s}}{\left(\dfrac{r}{r_{s}}\right)\left(1+\dfrac{r}{r_{s}}\right)^{2}},

(58)

as motivated by cosmological
N
-body simulations
[
58
,
59
]
. In this expression,
\rho_{s}
and
r_{s}
represent a characteristic density and a scale radius, whose values depend on the specific galaxy under consideration. For the Milky Way
[
60
,
61
]
, we can take
r_{s}=18.6~\rm{Kpc}
and
\rho_{s}=\left(\frac{R_{0}}{r_{s}}\right)\left(1+\frac{R_{0}}{r_{s}}\right)^{2}\rho_{\odot}
, where
\rho_{\odot}=0.4~\rm{GeV~cm}^{-3}
is the local dark matter density in the Solar System, located at a galactocentric radius of
R_{0}=8.2~\rm{Kpc}
.

As the radius decreases and one approaches the central black hole, additional dynamical processes can modify the Navarro-Frenk-White profile in the region
r_{0}\leq r<R_{\rm{sp}}
, forming a black hole spike. This spike is commonly described by the phenomenological profile
\rho_{\textrm{CDM}}\propto(R_{\textrm{sp}}/r)^{\gamma}
, where
\gamma
denotes the slope of the spike and
R_{\textrm{sp}}
represents its characteristic radius. Under the assumption that the black hole grows adiabatically at the center of an initially cuspy halo, Gondolo and Silk estimated a spike slope of
\gamma=7/3

[
4
]
.
The spatial extension of the spike is much more uncertain, as it depends on the detailed evolutionary history of the black hole and its host. For practical purposes, we will use the conservative estimate
R_{\textrm{sp}}=10~\rm{pc}
, consistent with the choice made in
[
56
]
.
Finally, inside the event horizon,
r<r_{0}
, we have set the dark matter density to zero.
