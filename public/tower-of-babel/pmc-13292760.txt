Fully self-gated dual-venc five-dimensional flow magnetic resonance imaging for simultaneous imaging of the cardiovascular and portal venous systems: A feasibility study
Authors: Yuyang Ren, Wen Ye, Wenchao Yang, Wenjian Liu, Haotian Hong, Junpu Hu, Jiayu Zhu, Haikun Qi, Fei Shan, Peng Hu, Zijian Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo develop and evaluate a fully self-gated dual-venc five-dimensional (5D) flow cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) technique for joint assessment of cardiovascular and portal venous hemodynamics under free breathing.MethodsA dual-venc 5D flow sequence using a spiral-in Cartesian sampling trajectory was implemented. Cardiac and respiratory motion were retrospectively resolved using principal component analysis of self-gating signals, followed by total variation regularized reconstruction. The technique was first tested in a custom-built phantom with steady flow and stationary parts, and compared with conventional four-dimensional (4D) flow. It was then evaluated in healthy volunteers and in patients with cirrhosis or portal hypertension, including post-transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) cases. No contrast agent was administered for either the phantom or in vivo scans, with an in vivo 5D flow scan time of approximately 15 min per subject. Hemodynamic parameters in both the cardiovascular and portal venous systems were quantified and compared with conventional breath-held, ECG-triggered two-dimensional (2D) phase-contrast (PC) MRI. For each subject, flow internal consistency (IC) across vessel planes and respiration-resolved flow variations were also computed.ResultsPhantom experiments demonstrated an average 67% improvement in velocity-to-noise ratio (VNR) and similar flow quantification compared with fully sampled 4D flow MRI. In vivo evaluation included 12 healthy volunteers, 8 cirrhosis patients, and 9 post-TIPS patients. Across all cohorts, dual-venc 5D flow achieved substantial VNR enhancement (approximately 200% on average), high flow IC (>82%), and strong agreement with 2D PC MRI for both peak and net flow (bias <10%). Respiration-resolved analysis revealed a consistent increase in net flow in the main pulmonary artery (MPA), right pulmonary artery (RPA), and superior vena cava (SVC) during inspiration, along with elevated SVC peak flow.ConclusionsFully self-gated dual-venc 5D flow MRI enables simultaneous, respiration-resolved quantification of cardiovascular and portal venous hemodynamics within a clinically feasible scan time and with high VNR. This technique provides a promising tool for characterizing systemic hemodynamics in cirrhosis and for evaluating portal-systemic circulatory interactions following TIPS.Keywords: 5D flow, Respiratory-resolved flow, Dual-venc, Cardiovascular hemodynamics, Portal venous system, Cirrhosis

Introduction

The progression of chronic liver disease and cirrhosis induces widespread pathophysiological changes that affect both hepatic and extrahepatic organs, and can cause hemodynamic abnormalities such as portal hypertension, pulmonary hypertension, and cardiovascular dysfunctions [1], [2]. Clinically significant portal hypertension, defined as a hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) ≥10 mmHg [3], elevates cardiac output and predisposes patients to chronic cardiac impairment. Even in the absence of preexisting heart disease, cirrhosis often causes functional and structural cardiac changes collectively termed cirrhotic cardiomyopathy [4].

Transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) creation, commonly used to treat portal hypertension, reduces portal pressure by establishing a portocaval shunt [5]. However, this increased venous return can further elevate cardiac output and reduce systemic vascular resistance. Consequently, up to 20% of patients develop heart failure within one year after TIPS [6]. These interactions highlight the need for comprehensive and joint assessment of cardiovascular and hepatic hemodynamics to better evaluate systemic circulatory alterations in cirrhosis and related interventions.

Four-dimensional (4D) flow cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) [7] is well-known for providing time-resolved three-dimensional hemodynamic information and has significant clinical applications in cerebrovascular [8], [9], cardiovascular [10], [11], and hepatic vascular systems [12], [13], [14], [15]. However, simultaneous imaging of cardiac and hepatic hemodynamics can be hindered by respiratory motion and the significantly different flow velocities in the two regions. To compensate for respiratory motion, diaphragm navigator or bellows belt-based gating is commonly used in cardiovascular flow imaging [16]. However, in hepatic imaging, navigator excitation can affect the imaging volume, and respiratory gating can substantially reduce the scan efficiency. In recent years, many works on 4D flow CMR demonstrate that respiratory motion can be extracted by self-gated methods, eliminating the need for navigators or external devices [17], [18], [19], [20]. The motion information enables respiratory-resolved reconstruction, termed as five-dimensional (5D) flow CMR [20], [21], [22], [23], [24], which improves image quality and allows hemodynamic assessment across different respiratory phases.

Meanwhile, the large difference between the cardiovascular and portal venous flow velocities makes it challenging to select a proper velocity encoding (venc) for simultaneous imaging. High vencs are typically used to encode fast cardiovascular flows, however, the velocity-to-noise ratio (VNR) may then be insufficient for the hepatic region. Conversely, low vencs are needed to encode slow hepatic flows but introduce phase wrapping in cardiac regions. To address such challenges, multi-venc, typically dual-venc acquisitions, was proposed to extend the velocity encoding range [25], [26]. Several studies demonstrate that multi-venc can enhance VNR and improve flow quantification accuracy in cerebrovascular or cardiovascular systems [27], [28], [29], [30], [31]. However, simultaneous cardiovascular and hepatic flow imaging has not yet been reported.

In this study, we present a fully self-gated dual-venc 5D flow MRI technique for free breathing, joint assessment of cardiovascular and portal venous hemodynamics. Leveraging k-t sparsity, the scan can be completed within 15 min while resolving respiratory phases. The proposed method was tested in a custom-built flow phantom, and evaluated in healthy volunteers, cirrhosis patients, and post-TIPS patients, demonstrating its potential to facilitate comprehensive and noninvasive assessment of systemic hemodynamics.

Methods

Data acquisition

Following the pseudo-spiral sampling strategy described in prior work [32], [33], we adopted a similar spiral-in Cartesian trajectory with additional random angular shifts for our method. Illustration of the sampling trajectory, showing three spiral arms with 10 sampling points on each path. Green, red, and blue dots denote the low-venc, high-venc, and flow-compensation encodings, respectively

For the dual-venc acquisition, a seven-point velocity encoding scheme was adopted, including one flow-compensation reference and six flow-sensitive encodings along the three orthogonal directions at both low and high vencs. The venc assigned to each sampling point was randomly selected during acquisition to maintain incoherent subsampling for different vencs, while the total number of sampled points for each venc remained balanced.

Physiological motion extraction

The repeatedly acquired k-space center lines of each spiral path were used for self-gating (SG). The SG lines were first Fourier transformed to obtain the superior-inferior (SI) direction projections. As shown in Fig. 2, cardiac and respiratory motion signals were then derived using principal component analysis (PCA) [34], [35], [36].

Extraction of cardiac and respiratory motion signals from the self-gating projection data using PCA. The displayed projection signal represents the sum-of-squares combination of the projections from all coils. Red triangles represent the detected cardiac triggers and 20 cardiac phases can be resolved. For respiratory motion, four phases (Resp 1-4) are resolved. PCA principal component analysis

Using the extracted motion signals, the acquired data were sorted into cardiac and respiratory phases. For cardiac binning, peaks in the extracted cardiac signal were identified as triggers for each cardiac cycle. Each cycle was divided into the desired number of cardiac phases and each readout was assigned to its corresponding phase. For respiratory binning, the respiratory signal was divided into four phases from end-expiration to end-inspiration using the quartiles of the signal amplitude, ensuring an equal number of samples in each respiratory phase.

In vitro experiments

The 5D flow sequence was first validated using a custom-built flow phantom consisting of a tube with an inner diameter of 12 mm wrapped around a stationary water phantom. Pure water, without contrast agent, was circulated at a constant speed using a water pump. Data were acquired on a 3T scanner (uMR890; United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China) with the following parameters: field of view (FOV) = 312 × 240 × 114 mm3, isotropic spatial resolution = 1.5 mm, venclow = 50 cm∕s, venchigh = 150 cm∕s, repetition time (TR) = 6.5 ms, flip angle (FA) = 7∘, and a total scan time of 12 min. The data were sorted into 10 cardiac phases assuming a virtual cardiac cycle of 600 ms, resulting in 20-fold undersampling. For comparison, fully sampled traditional 4D flow was also acquired using identical imaging parameters.

In vivo experiments

This study was approved by the local institutional ethics committee, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. No contrast agent was administered for the in vivo scans, and all subjects fasted for at least two hours before scanning. Data were collected from 29 participants, including 12 healthy volunteers (age 29.5 ± 11.1 years; 9 male) and 17 patients. Among the patients, 8 had decompensated cirrhosis secondary to hepatitis B (age 53.0 ± 8.6 years; all male), and 9 had undergone TIPS procedures (age 59.5 ± 6.0 years; 5 male). Imaging was performed on 3T scanners (uMR890 and uMR870; United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China) with the following parameter ranges: FOV = 320 × (320–380) × (80–120) mm3, isotropic spatial resolution = 2–2.5 mm, venclow = 30–50 cm∕s, venchigh = 150–180 cm∕s, TR = 5.8–6 ms, and FA = 7∘. The relatively large FOV was used to ensure coverage of both the cardiac and hepatic regions. With 20 cardiac phases and 4 respiratory phases resolved, total scan time was approximately 15 min, corresponding to an acceleration factor of 20–23.

Reference measurements were obtained using 2D PC-MRI at the ascending aorta (AAo), main pulmonary artery (MPA), superior vena cava (SVC), inferior vena cava (IVC), and portal vein (PV) following a standard 2D PC protocol. The reference data were acquired with breath-holding and prospective ECG triggering. The spatial resolution was 2 × 2 × 5 mm3 and 20 cardiac phases were resolved. The reference data were reconstructed using generalized autocalibrating partially parellel acquisition (GRAPPA) with an acceleration rate of 2. The venc was set to 50 cm/s for the PV and 150 cm/s for all other vessels. However, for some patients, certain 2D acquisitions may fail due to difficulty performing adequate breath-holds.

Finally, for both healthy volunteers and patients, the cardiac motion extracted from the SG signals was compared with conventional ECG R–R trigger timings, and the extracted respiratory motion was compared with recordings from the millimeter-wave radar [41], which is used for respiration monitoring and is available on both scanner systems.

Self-gating accuracy

The mean R–R interval error between the SG-derived cardiac signal and ECG was 14.93 ± 8.08 ms for healthy volunteers, 16.21 ± 6.74 ms for cirrhotic patients, and 17.04 ± 7.86 ms for post-TIPS patients. For respiratory motion, the SG-extracted signal also showed a strong agreement with the millimeter-wave radar recordings. Comparison between 5D flow and 2D-PC

Using both 5D flow and 2D PC MRI, peak and net flows at AAo, MPA, SVC, IVC, and PV at end-expiration (Resp1 state) were compared across healthy volunteers, cirrhotic patients, and post-TIPS patients. Bland–Altman analyses are presented in Fig. 6 and Figure S1. In most vessels, no significant differences were found between the two methods for either peak flow or net flow. Exceptions included peak and net flow in the MPA of healthy volunteers, peak and net flow in the AAo of post-TIPS patients, and peak flow in the SVC and PV. However, all these differences exhibited biases within 10%.

Analysis of internal consistency

ICs of the net flows across all respiratory states for each vessel plane are shown in Figure S2. For healthy volunteers, net flows demonstrated high IC across all respiratory states ( > 90%), with no significant differences between states. Cirrhotic patients also showed high consistency (> 87%), with no significant respiratory-state dependence except in the bifurcation case IVCUp + SVC = MPA, where the IC decreased to approximately 87% at end-inspiration. For post-TIPS patients, IC remained above 82% across all vessels, and no significant differences were observed across respiratory states.

VNR analysis

Quantitative and qualitative comparisons of VNR between the dual-venc and high-venc-only data of 5D flow are presented in Fig. 7 and Video S1. Quantitatively, the dual-venc acquisition significantly reduced background velocity noise and improved the average VNR by approximately 200%. Qualitatively, the streamline visualizations for a healthy volunteer, a cirrhosis patient, and a post-TIPS patient demonstrate that the dual-venc data produce smoother and less noisy streamlines, enabling clearer depiction of flow in both the cardiovascular and portal venous systems.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and evaluated a fully self-gated dual-venc 5D flow sequence capable of jointly assessing cardiovascular and portal venous hemodynamics across different respiratory states within 15 min. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated substantial improvements in VNR using the proposed method, and flow quantification showed strong agreement with measurements obtained from conventional 4D flow and 2D PC CMR.

Because peak velocity measurements can be noisy, we focused on more stable flow metrics, namely peak flow and net flow, when comparing the proposed method with conventional techniques. Bland–Altman analyses demonstrated strong overall agreement between 5D flow and 2D PC CMR for both peak and net flow across multiple vascular locations. Additional validation using IC analysis further supported the performance of 5D flow. For single, nonbifurcating vessels, IC exceeded 95% across all respiratory states, and remained above 82% for vessels with bifurcations. These consistently high IC values highlight the robustness and reliability of the proposed 5D flow method for comprehensive hemodynamic assessment.

Respiration-resolved imaging also enabled comparison of peak and net flow across different respiratory states. During inspiration, significant increases in net flow were observed in the MPA, RPA, and SVC, along with a significant increase in SVC peak flow. These observations are also consistent with the finding of increased venous return during inspiration [42], [43], [44]. Despite the overall strong agreement between methods, peak or net flow measured by 5D flow was up to approximately 10% lower than those from 2D PC in several cases. This discrepancy may stem from differences in acquisition conditions and reconstruction methods. Whereas 2D PC was performed during breath-holding over a few cardiac cycles, 5D flow was acquired under free breathing over a 15-min period. Variability in breathing patterns may therefore contribute to differences in flow measurements. Additionally, 2D PC was scanned with a low acceleration factor, while 5D flow employed substantially high acceleration. Multiple reconstruction settings and regularization weights were tested, and the final reconstruction parameters were chosen to optimize image quality while minimizing flow discrepancies relative to 2D PC.

In our method, repeated k-space center sampling enabled the extraction of both cardiac and respiratory motion signals directly from the acquired imaging data, allowing motion-state binning without ECG triggering, diaphragm navigators, or external respiratory devices. Although the SG-derived respiratory signal showed strong agreement with millimeter-wave radar recordings in this study, external respiratory monitors such as millimeter-wave radar and respiratory bellows rely on body-surface motion and may be more sensitive to breathing patterns and patient positioning. Therefore, self-gating may provide a more robust respiratory surrogate for free breathing 5D flow CMR, but at the cost of an approximately 11.1% (1/9) increase in scan time due to the repeated k-space center sampling. To leverage self-gating acquisitions without introducing substantial eddy currents, particularly at the k-space center, different vencs are acquired along the spiral-in arms, rather than at identical k-space locations as in conventional 4D flow [31]. This acquisition scheme may introduce additional background phase variations between different venc images. Nonetheless, these effects can be mitigated by applying a third-order polynomial fit to stationary background tissue [15], allowing accurate reconstruction of velocity phase maps.

Concomitant gradient field correction was not implemented in this study. However, phantom experiments suggested that its impact was likely limited under the present conditions, as the mean and standard deviation of the stationary background phase were close to zero. Finally, although a wide range of venc values were used for the dual-venc acquisition, the threshold-optimized unwrapping algorithm remained robust, and no unwrapping failures were observed. Nonetheless, validation in a larger cohort may be necessary to further confirm its reliability.

Limitations

Compared with separate acquisitions of cardiovascular and portal venous flow, the proposed method requires a scan time of approximately 15 min, and the reconstruction process requires approximately 15 min. The relatively long acquisition and reconstruction time may hinder its clinical translation. However, substantially enhanced VNR can be achieved by using dual-venc, which may facilitate early detection of abnormal flow patterns. Additionally, by sharing the same flow-compensation and flow-sensitive encodings for different regions, time efficiency can be improved, especially when high VNR of the heart is preferred. Another limitation is the potential for respiratory motion artifacts, particularly in patients with irregular or unstable breathing. This may partially explain the lower ICs observed in motion-sensitive regions, such as between the PV and its anterior branches (SMV and SV), or between the MPA and systemic venous returns (IVC and SVC), where inter-subject flow variability was also higher. Future work will focus on incorporating more advanced acquisition schemes, retrospective motion-correction strategies, and improved reconstruction methods to enhance image quality, increase resolution, and further reduce artifacts.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed a fully self-gated dual-venc 5D flow CMR method capable of jointly and reliably quantifying blood flow in both the cardiovascular and portal venous systems with high VNR. This approach provides a noninvasive, radiation-free alternative for comprehensive assessment of heart–liver hemodynamics using phase-contrast CMR. This method holds potential for evaluating and monitoring systemic hemodynamics and cardiac risk in patients with cirrhosis, particularly those with portal hypertension or undergoing related interventions such as TIPS.
