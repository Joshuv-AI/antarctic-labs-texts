Motivations and internship experiences of registered nurses pursuing Master of Nursing Specialist education in China: a phenomenological analysis
Authors: Dong Zhu, Haili Zhu, Wanting Xie, Han Fu, Jianhong Lv, Qing Yuan, Xueqin Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimThis study aimed to explore the motivations and internship experiences of 11 registered nurses in China pursuing Master of Nursing Specialist education(MNS).MethodsUsing purposive and snowball sampling, master of nursing students from eleven universities were recruited. Semi‑structured interviews were conducted and analysed using interpretative phenomenological analysis.ResultsThree major themes and eight sub-themes were identified, including drivers for pursuing MNS education (workplace stress, career development dilemmas, personal growth and degree drive), transformation of self-perception (self-value recognition, self-awareness awakening), and transformation of professional perception (building of professional identity in specialty practice, challenges to professional identity in general clinical practice, influence of mentorship during MNS clinical internships).ConclusionParticipants’ narratives indicated complex motivations driving their decision to pursue MNS education. During the internship phase, participants described their evolving self-perception and professional identity, often underscoring the critical dual role of mentors. These insights, drawn from participants’ experiences, suggest that clinical departments and policymakers should optimise the professional environment and expand development opportunities for MNS nurses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04074-w.

Introduction

The Master of Nursing Specialist (MNS) degree was officially sanctioned by the Academic Degrees Committee of the State Council of China in 2010, aiming to foster high-calibre, application-focused, and specialized nursing professionals [1, 2]. MNS (note1) programs in China are offered full-time and part-time [3]. The full-time MNS curriculum extends over three years, requiring students’ dedicated engagement in both didactic learning and extensive clinical immersion. The program is comprehensively structured around two principal components: academic coursework and clinical practice. Within this framework, the clinical internship (hereafter referred to as “internship”) serves as an indispensable and central element of MNS training, typically lasting between 12 and 18 months [4, 5]. Notably, a segment of the full-time MNS student population consists of registered nurses with clinical experience. These individuals, having accumulated several years of clinical nursing practice after their bachelor’s degree [6], contribute invaluable practical insights.Nevertheless, their re-entry into an academic setting necessitates a rigorous process of integrating and re-contextualizing their empirical knowledge within systematic theoretical constructs [7]. This distinctive period of professional metamorphosis presents a confluence of challenges and opportunities, underscoring the imperative to comprehend these seasoned registered nurses’ motivations and lived experiences as they embark on advanced studies. Current literature lacks clear distinctions between registered nurses and fresh graduates in the Master of Nursing (MNS) student population, and no specialized research has been conducted [8–10]. Consequently, this study investigates registered nurses’ motivations and clinical internship experiences pursuing MNS education. By elucidating their subjective interpretations and perceptions of this critical developmental phase, this research aims to furnish educators and policymakers with actionable insights to refine MNS programs, thereby addressing the specific needs of these experienced learners. Such efforts are poised to elevate the quality of advanced nursing talent cultivation, directly enhance patient care outcomes, and propel the sustained evolution of the broader nursing profession.

Method

This study employs Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) for its exploratory approach. Proposed by British psychologist Jonathan Smith, IPA is grounded in a robust theoretical foundation that integrates phenomenology, hermeneutics, and case study research [11]. IPA is fundamentally phenomenological, exploring human experience as individuals live and understand it. Building upon this phenomenological heritage, this study draws explicitly upon the philosophical insights of Martin Heidegger. Heidegger’s phenomenology centres on the profound exploration of “Dasein” (Being-there), or “Being-in-the-world.” He emphasised that human existence is not an isolated consciousness but is always inextricably situated within a concrete “lifeworld,” constituted by interpersonal relationships, culture, and history. For Heidegger, understanding is inherently interpretive; meaning is not external to Dasein but is foundational, making the world itself possible [12]. Therefore, through Heidegger’s lens, our analysis extends beyond merely identifying “what” the master’s nursing internship experiences entail. Instead, we delve into “what these experiences mean” within their “lifeworlds” and how these meanings actively shape their “Dasein.” This theoretical framework enables us to transcend a simple description of phenomena, revealing the “Worldliness” of participants’ experiences—that is, how these experiences are profoundly shaped and understood within specific social, cultural, and personal contexts—thereby facilitating a deeper, more nuanced interpretation of registered nurses’ lived experiences during their master’s internships. Consistent with IPA’s core aims, the method’s uniqueness lies in its in-depth investigation of how individuals perceive, experience, and understand life events, seeking to illuminate the intricate relationship and interaction between individuals and specific events or processes (i.e., “phenomena”) [13]. At the operational level, Smith advocates for selecting small-scale, focused samples and using detailed case analyses to obtain high-quality textual data [12]. Data were collected using a semi-structured interview guide developed specifically for this study. The full interview protocol is provided in Supplementary File 1. In this study, “experience” refers to the internship experience of registered nurses pursuing a MNS education. IPA also inherently possesses hermeneutic characteristics, recognising that individual narratives reflect their self-constructive experience process. Researchers, therefore, explore participants’ inner experiences through rigorous interpretation. Consequently, this study explores the motivations and internship experiences of registered nurses pursuing MNS education, striving to uncover the profound meanings of these experiences through IPA’s interpretive depth.

Participants

The study comprised 11 female registered nurses enrolled as full-time MNS postgraduate students across different universities. The research group was limited to women based on two reasons: First, considering the social and cultural background of China’s nursing profession, where women form the majority, female nurses often face multiple life pressures, such as marriage and childbirth, during this critical stage of pursuing a postgraduate degree. Focusing on this group helps to deeply understand their unique dilemmas, challenges, and meaning construction. Second, to comply with the IPA requirements for sample homogeneity, we focused on a single gender group to avoid introducing excessive interference factors due to differences in career development, social roles, and life pressures between genders, thereby ensuring that the research findings can more accurately and profoundly reflect the shared experiences and meanings of this group.

Participants were recruited through purposive sampling and snowball sampling methods. Inclusion criteria included: (a) being a full-time MNS student; (b) holding a registered nurse qualification and possessing over two years of clinical practice experience; (c) completing at least 12 months of an intership during MSN education. The participants were contacted in advance and provided a brief introduction about the researchers, the research topic, and its purpose. Each participant agreed upon a specific interview date, time, and location. All respondents had completed a clinical internship of at least 12 months during the interview. According to their status, they can be divided into two categories: (1) They are still in the internship period (at least 12 months of internship); (2) They have completed an internship but have not been assigned to a formal position and are in the transition stage of their career.

Before the formal interview, a pre-interview with 4 participants was conducted to refine the outline. Formal interviews were then conducted with 11 participants, meeting IPA’s minor sample requirements [12]. Participant details are provided in Table 1.

Data collection

Data collection took place from March 2025 to July 2025. This study recruited participants through online purposive sampling and snowball sampling. During the recruitment process, we contacted 34 students: 12 did not respond to invitations, eight were excluded due to non-compliance with inclusion criteria, and 3 declined participation due to scheduling conflicts, which we respectfully acknowledged. Two researchers with extensive experience in qualitative research and training in phenomenological interview techniques conducted all interviews. Notably, one interviewer is a registered nurse. This background fostered empathy with participants and provided a deeper understanding of their professional context and the challenges associated with pursuing MNS education. To ensure consistency and depth in the interviews, both interviewers collaboratively designed and revised the outline, conducted practice interviews (where one interviewer interviewed the other) [14]. The interviews were primarily conducted via telephone, each lasting approximately 30 to 60 min. After the interviews, two researchers transcribed the recordings and carefully reviewed them to ensure the accuracy of the conversation content.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed according to the six stages of IPA analysis. A case-by-case approach was adopted. Each transcript was independently analyzed before moving on to the next stage to maintain the case study focus emphasized by IPA [15]. The specific steps for analyzing each case were as follows [12]: ①Start with the first case; ②reading and rereading it; ③Make exploratory notes; ④Develop experiential statements; ⑤Look for connections in the experiential statements; ⑥Name personal experience themes (PETS) and integrate them; Continue to analyze the other cases independently.

Ethics and morality

This study adheres to the ethical principles of the “Declaration of Helsinki” and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Hunan University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, with approval number [2025] 287. Due to logistical challenges in obtaining written signatures from participants in different areas, oral informed consent was secured following IRB-approved protocols and documented by the researcher. The participants’ names were not used within the research ethics framework, and the encodings in the interview texts were made as “P1, P2, P3…”.

Result

According to the analysis, three major themes and eight sub-themes were identified, including drivers for pursuing MNS education (workplace stress, career development dilemmas, personal growth and degree drive), transformation of self-perception (self-value recognition, self-awareness awakening), and transformation of professional perception (building of professional identity in specialty practice, challenges to professional identity in general clinical practice, influence of mentorship during MNS clinical internships).

Drivers for pursuing MNS education

Workplace stress

Participants consistently described that the pressures encountered in their workplaces significantly motivated them to pursue an MNS degree. They attributed these difficulties to their initial lack of experience upon entering the profession and a limited understanding of workplace power dynamics. Participant 2 serves as a prime example, whose feelings of “not belonging” due to exclusion and a sense of powerlessness after a failed transfer request directly catalyzed her decision to pursue advanced education.

When I first got my bachelor’s degree, I was very young and completely clueless about workplace relationships. Cliques started forming in the department, which bothered me a lot, and I felt like I didn’t fit in. Because the head nurse valued me, I became a target for some senior nurses. I tried to apply for a transfer, but it didn’t work out. (P2)

When I first got my bachelor’s degree, I was very young and completely clueless about workplace relationships. Cliques started forming in the department, which bothered me a lot, and I felt like I didn’t fit in. Because the head nurse valued me, I became a target for some senior nurses. I tried to apply for a transfer, but it didn’t work out. (P2)

According to participants, the demanding nature of night shifts also emerged as a strong motivation for pursuing an MNS. The experience of participant 4 is a classic example; she recounted the arduous night shifts at her private hospital, where registered nurses faced excessive workload pressure due to the hospital’s profit-driven operational model.

I used to work in a general surgery department at a private hospital, where profit was prioritized. Night shifts were particularly grueling. Normal night shifts usually ended by midnight, but I often stayed until two or three in the morning. (P4)I decided to pursue graduate studies to change my current work situation, especially to get rid of frequent night shifts. I was tired of the night shift work model. (P7)

Career development dilemmas

Participants expressed significant concerns regarding their career prospects when discussing their motivations for pursuing advanced study. Participant 3 deeply resonated with this sentiment. As a contracted employee through a labour dispatch agency (an employment model standard in China where an external agency manages recruitment, payroll, and benefits, while the employee works at a host company), she articulated her limitations in promotion pathways as a non-official staff member.

My job responsibilities were almost identical to those of official staff, but there were huge disparities in treatment. This unfairness made me feel dissatisfied and demoralized, seeing no prospects for career development. (P3)

My job responsibilities were almost identical to those of official staff, but there were huge disparities in treatment. This unfairness made me feel dissatisfied and demoralized, seeing no prospects for career development. (P3)

Registered nurses in grassroots hospitals similarly encountered comparable workplace dilemmas, including promotion barriers and unequal treatment. Participant P10 offered a typical example, highlighting the scarcity of promotion opportunities in such hospitals and emphasising the crucial role of academic qualifications in overcoming these career impasses and achieving upward professional mobility.

The hospital I worked at in the county was not large; work was hectic, but the salary was low. I often felt my opportunities for advancement were limited. A higher degree would undoubtedly serve as a stepping stone if I wanted to be promoted to a higher position. (P10)

The hospital I worked at in the county was not large; work was hectic, but the salary was low. I often felt my opportunities for advancement were limited. A higher degree would undoubtedly serve as a stepping stone if I wanted to be promoted to a higher position. (P10)

Many hospitals place significant emphasis on research output when evaluating staff for promotion. Participants reported that they needed to dedicate substantial energy to daily clinical work and actively publish research to secure promotion opportunities. As research capability became a formidable hurdle within the promotion mechanism, it created significant career development challenges for registered nurses.

When our hospital evaluated for ‘Top Ten Nurses’ or assessed for promotion, research publications and achievements were almost always the primary criteria. Faced with such fierce competition, I had no choice but to adapt and change. (P11)

When our hospital evaluated for ‘Top Ten Nurses’ or assessed for promotion, research publications and achievements were almost always the primary criteria. Faced with such fierce competition, I had no choice but to adapt and change. (P11)

Personal growth and degree drive

Participants’ aspirations for advanced study were ignited by their perception of academic credentials’ importance and the comparative influence of their peers’ educational attainment. Participant 1 admitted to holding only an adult self-taught bachelor’s degree (obtained through self-study), while her peers with higher qualifications served as benchmarks for self-improvement. This comparative effect fueled her desire for further education, viewing it as crucial for enhancing self-worth.

Pursuing higher education was my core motivation. Some of my classmates graduated from 211 and 985 universities (which are China’s top-tier institutions; ‘211’ refers to a national project to strengthen around 100 key universities, and ‘985’ to an even more selective group of elite universities), which made me yearn for a higher degree. (P1)

Some of my classmates graduated from 211 and 985 universities (which are China’s top-tier institutions; ‘211’ refers to a national project to strengthen around 100 key universities, and ‘985’ to an even more selective group of elite universities), which made me yearn for a higher degree. (P1)

Participant 6 also articulated her consideration for continuous progress. She described an intrinsic rejection of professional stagnation and pursuing continuous growth as the inner drive to seek advanced education, broadening her future options.

I always felt I could not stand still. Pursuing a master’s degree is not just academic advancement but also paving the way for my career. With a master’s degree, I will have more choices and greater opportunities. (P6)

Furthermore, participants measured their progress by comparing their career trajectories with those of others. For instance, Participant 5 described a “sense of relative disadvantage” stemming from social comparison. Her concerns about her professional standing and the benchmark of her peers’ achievements ignited an intrinsic desire to bridge the gap and seek more stable career development through an MNS.

I did not get an official position when I graduated with my bachelor’s degree. Some of my classmates went to large hospitals in big cities and secured staff positions within the system (often called an ‘iron rice bowl,’ signifying extremely stable employment with minimal risk of job loss and comprehensive welfare benefits). Transformation of self‑perception

Self-value recognition

In the initial phase of their MNS internships, participants consistently described feeling treated as unpaid labour, lacking appropriate compensation and due respect. This perception was often linked to the inherently unpaid nature of nursing internships and their preconceived negative notions derived from prior internship experiences.

I felt like I was merely providing unpaid labor for others. My past internship experiences ingrained in me the idea that internships were simply about doing free work. (P10)

I felt like I was merely providing unpaid labor for others. My past internship experiences ingrained in me the idea that internships were simply about doing free work. (P10)

Furthermore, participants expressed a pessimistic outlook towards the current internship, attributing this attitude to their existing clinical experience. Participant 6 articulated that when internship content largely overlapped with their prior experience and lacked sufficient challenge, they found it difficult to perceive the value of professional growth.

Having previously supervised interns during my hospital work, undergoing another internship felt somewhat redundant, even like a waste of time. (P6)

Having previously supervised interns during my hospital work, undergoing another internship felt somewhat redundant, even like a waste of time. (P6)

However, as the internship progressed and deepened, participants began to experience an intrinsic sense of satisfaction and accomplishment. This positive shift occurred as they encountered new professional knowledge and skills and received abundant positive feedback in clinical practice, ultimately affirming their professional worth.

During my cardiology internship, I was exposed to a wealth of knowledge and skills I had never encountered before. I gradually realized the practical benefits of the internship, which completely altered my previous perspective. (P6)When I utilized specialized knowledge to address patients’ problems and received numerous positive responses, I gradually began to grasp the true meaning and value of nursing work. (P4)

During my cardiology internship, I was exposed to a wealth of knowledge and skills I had never encountered before. I gradually realized the practical benefits of the internship, which completely altered my previous perspective. (P6)

When I utilized specialized knowledge to address patients’ problems and received numerous positive responses, I gradually began to grasp the true meaning and value of nursing work. (P4)

Self-awareness awakening

According to participants’ narratives, a gradual awakening of their self-awareness also occurred during the MNS internship phase. This was primarily manifested as a transformation in their thinking patterns.

Previously, when performing basic procedures like injections, I would simply follow my instructors’ methods without much critical thought. However, during this internship, I began to deliberate actively. When encountering issues, I would consult professional literature to find answers from a theoretical standpoint. (P8)

Previously, when performing basic procedures like injections, I would simply follow my instructors’ methods without much critical thought. However, during this internship, I began to deliberate actively. When encountering issues, I would consult professional literature to find answers from a theoretical standpoint. (P8)

Furthermore, this awakening transformed their interactions with authority figures and superiors, shifting from traditional blind obedience to self-assertion. Participant 2 described the profound impact of this shift and the growth of her inner strength.

What struck me most profoundly was learning to assert myself, even when confronting my mentors or superiors. Previously, I lacked the courage; whatever the head nurse said, I would comply. I could only obediently follow even if she was mistaken or unfairly treated me. Now, I feel I have matured, and my inner strength has grown increasingly powerful. (P2)

What struck me most profoundly was learning to assert myself, even when confronting my mentors or superiors. Previously, I lacked the courage; whatever the head nurse said, I would comply. I could only obediently follow even if she was mistaken or unfairly treated me. Now, I feel I have matured, and my inner strength has grown increasingly powerful. (P2)

Participant 11 recounted a similar experience:“During the internship, I refused to accept tasks that fell outside my scope of work, even when assigned by the department’s head nurse.” They described a transformation from their previous submissiveness to now daring to challenge authority. This experience of “learning to refuse” and the “growth of inner strength” were hallmarks of their awakened self-awareness, signifying an enhanced sense of personal agency.

Transformation of career perception

Building of professional identity in specialty practice

Participants described how their experiences during specialized ward internships led to a profound reshaping and enhancement of their sense of value and accomplishment within the nursing profession. They perceived that, as specialist nurses, they could command respect comparable to that of physicians, leading to a new understanding of their profession’s standing. Participant 2 exemplified this, experiencing a heightened sense of professional value and accomplishment due to patients’ specialized recognition and respect, which fundamentally reshaped her perception of nursing’s professional status.

During my internship in the wound clinic, I felt that being a nurse could bring the same level of respect and sense of accomplishment as being a doctor. Patients referred to me as ‘teacher,’ a title typically reserved for physicians (rather than nurses). It felt profoundly different. (P2)

During my internship in the wound clinic, I felt that being a nurse could bring the same level of respect and sense of accomplishment as being a doctor. Patients referred to me as ‘teacher,’ a title typically reserved for physicians (rather than nurses). It felt profoundly different. (P2)

Participant 4 likewise strongly articulated this sense of value derived from recognition based on specialized competence:

I strongly felt the professional worth of being a specialist nurse. Each time I assisted patients, they provided abundant positive feedback, which made me feel deeply appreciated. This kind of recognition, rooted in my professional abilities, was something I had never experienced before while working in other clinical departments. (P4)

I strongly felt the professional worth of being a specialist nurse. Each time I assisted patients, they provided abundant positive feedback, which made me feel deeply appreciated. This kind of recognition, rooted in my professional abilities, was something I had never experienced before while working in other clinical departments. (P4)

Participant 11, moreover, grasped the value inherent in specialized competence by observing her mentor’s application of expert knowledge to solve patient problems.

In the Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) nursing clinic, my mentor utilized TCM knowledge and thinking to address patients’ nursing issues, which incredibly impacted me. Patients, too, marveled at the profound effectiveness of TCM. (P11)

In the Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) nursing clinic, my mentor utilized TCM knowledge and thinking to address patients’ nursing issues, which incredibly impacted me. Patients, too, marveled at the profound effectiveness of TCM. (P11)

Collectively, these experiences reinforced participants’ professional identity as specialist nurses and fostered a more profound affirmation of their specialized capabilities and contributions.

Challenges to professional identity in general clinical practice

In stark contrast to the professional identity gained during specialized rotations, registered nurses assigned to general clinical wards described a significant lack of professional identity. Participant 3 illustrated her dilemma, observing a disparity in patients’ attitudes towards doctors and nurses, and consequently feeling her professional value was not fully acknowledged.

Nursing work did not bring me much professional identity. I sometimes encountered patients who treated doctors and nurses differently during my internship. When facing doctors, patients would be very grateful and respectful, but when facing nurses, they might be indifferent, or even display negative attitudes. (P3)

Nursing work did not bring me much professional identity. I sometimes encountered patients who treated doctors and nurses differently during my internship. When facing doctors, patients would be very grateful and respectful, but when facing nurses, they might be indifferent, or even display negative attitudes. (P3)

Participant 2’s experience in a general ward also left her frustrated and perplexed. Her account highlighted external scepticism regarding the value of MNS education and a narrow perception of nurses’ career development prospects. This notion, equating highly educated nurses with basic task performers, significantly undermined her professional identity.

I had a very unpleasant experience in one department, which was a general ward. Someone once asked me: ‘What is the point of getting a master’s degree? After graduation, you’ll still be working on the front lines, giving injections, inserting catheters, and it will take years before you earn good money. (P2)

At a deeper level, Participant 9’s description directly reflected concerns about the scope for development within the Chinese nursing profession. She believed domestic nurses had limited autonomy and growth opportunities compared to their international counterparts, further impacting their sense of accomplishment. Participant 9’s feelings of a “low ceiling” and “insufficient sense of accomplishment” in nursing reflected her disappointment with her professional role and development potential, leading to a significant lack of professional identity.

During my internship, I deeply realized that the ceiling for the nursing profession is incredibly low, with severely limited autonomy and growth opportunities, especially compared to abroad. For instance, unlike nurses abroad, we do not have prescribing authority, which always makes me feel a lack of accomplishment in nursing. (P9)

This created a dual-directional impact on their professional trajectories. Participants universally expressed mentors’ profound impact on their internship period and personal lives, regarding them as crucial guiding forces in their professional journeys.

It is no exaggeration to say that during my MNS internship, my mentor profoundly influenced me. She is a pivotal figure in my life’s journey, and I hold the deepest respect for her, continuously learning from her. (P4)

It is no exaggeration to say that during my MNS internship, my mentor profoundly influenced me. She is a pivotal figure in my life’s journey, and I hold the deepest respect for her, continuously learning from her. (P4)

Conversely, some participants viewed encountering dedicated and responsible mentors as a stroke of luck, underscoring the “randomness” of this aspect in postgraduate training. Participant 1, for instance, described the decisively positive impact a committed and responsible mentor had on her postgraduate career development, with this “fortunate” encounter becoming a powerful catalyst for her learning and growth.

I believe postgraduate training can sometimes be quite random. You might encounter an excellent or less effective mentor, as there is not a standardized approach to postgraduate education. I consider myself very fortunate to have met such a dedicated and responsible mentor. (P1)

Discussion

Drivers for pursuing MNS education

Research findings indicate that multiple factors, including workplace pressure, career development challenges, and personal growth considerations, drive registered nurses’ pursuit of MNS education. Participants reported that excessive workload and night shift burden are core contributors to workplace pressure. Nurses in private hospitals, in particular, experience immense night shift pressure, which is closely linked to their profit-driven orientation and stringent cost-control strategies, often resulting in night-time workloads far exceeding reasonable limits. However, a nationwide shortage of nursing staff is a prevalent challenge in both public and private hospitals [16]. This scarcity is further exacerbated during night shifts due to even fewer nurses being on duty, intensifying the pressure on registered nurses. Although the National Health Commission once mandated a nurse-to-actual-open-bed ratio of no less than 0.5:1 for wards in secondary and above hospitals (ideally, one nurse caring for two patients) [17], the reality of limited night shift staffing often compels each nurse to manage a patient load far beyond their capacity. Given that night shifts are an inherent aspect of the nursing profession, prolonged and overloaded night work not only severely impairs nurses’ physical and mental health, leading to burnout and turnover [18], but also significantly compromises nursing quality, increases medical errors, and threatens patient safety due to fatigue [19]. These phenomena are highly consistent with occupational psychology research, which posits that improving the work environment and alleviating work pressure are effective strategies for enhancing employee well-being [20]. Therefore, we recommend shifting the focus from ‘night shifts themselves’ to ‘optimizing the night shift work environment and support systems’. This includes requiring hospital management to re-evaluate and strictly implement nurse-to-patient ratio standards, especially during night hours; providing more equitable scheduling, adequate night shift allowances, and guaranteed rest periods; and establishing effective psychological support mechanisms to alleviate nurses’ night shift burden. Only by adopting such comprehensive measures can we ensure nurses’ well-being is safeguarded while they provide high-quality care, thereby fostering the stability and development of the nursing workforce.

Furthermore, registered nurses in grassroots hospitals and those employed by labour dispatch agencies face significant career development challenges, limited promotion opportunities, notable salary disparities, and restricted professional growth trajectories. This sense of professional stagnation is not coincidental; it reflects structural limitations and systemic challenges within the career development pathways for grassroots nurses. Lower educational attainment and narrow career advancement channels limit nurses’ professional development and promotion prospects [21]. In response, pursuing advanced degrees like MNS is precisely the effective strategy registered nurses employ to overcome these challenges, and it also reflects their drive for personal growth. This pursuit of higher education is not merely a reaction to external career opportunities but, at a deeper level, embodies nurses’ profound desire for personal and professional advancement. With society’s increasing emphasis on academic qualifications, particularly in today’s highly competitive job market, a degree symbolizes knowledge and competence and is a crucial stepping stone to higher positions [22, 23].

In the healthcare industry, higher education directly enhances professional capabilities, expands career development opportunities, and shapes broader career trajectories [24]. Research capability is increasingly vital for evaluating advanced nursing professionals within the current medical system’s promotion framework. Given that many registered nurses with bachelor’s degrees often lack sufficient proficiency in research methodologies, pursuing MNS education is a crucial pathway to acquire research knowledge and thus systematically enhance their workplace competitiveness. However, beyond academic qualification enhancement, we propose exploring more diversified approaches to help nurses improve their professional competitiveness and secure promotion opportunities.

Transformation of self-perception

During their MNS internship, participants’ self-identity development progressed from an initial phase of confusion and struggle to a later stage of awakening and growth. In the early stages of their internship, they exhibited profound respect and deference to their superiors, often complying with and striving to fulfil even unreasonable requests. This behavioural pattern profoundly reflected a submissive inclination in their self-perception and a firm reliance on “authority” [25]. This phenomenon aligns strongly with Chinese society’s deeply rooted Confucian traditional values, prioritising propriety, filial piety, and reverence for and obedience to elder authority [26, 27]. These values shape social hierarchical relationships and profoundly influence individual behavioural patterns and identity formation. Indeed, this over-reliance on authority is also consistent with general findings from research on Chinese university students during their developmental stages [28]. Consequently, during the initial internship phase, the traditional concept of “revering teachers and valuing the Way” dominated their attitudes and behaviours. However, as clinical internships progressed and professional experience gradually accumulated, they began awakening and growing their self-identity. They gradually recognised that they were not merely serving others or unthinkingly obeying superiors, but started clearly defining their capabilities and actively planning their career paths. This transformation was not instantaneous but emerged from sustained self-reflection, driven by modern society’s increasing emphasis on individual value and self-development. Thus, the MNS self-identity development, moving from initial confusion and struggle to awakening and growth, vividly illustrates the tension and eventual integration between traditional cultural values and contemporary societal frameworks within their personal experiences.

Furthermore, while this study profoundly reveals the unique trajectory of MNS self-identity development within the Chinese cultural context, some of its core mechanisms and developmental principles may possess broader cross-cultural and cross-situational universality. As an advanced learning environment, graduate education universally fosters critical thinking, self-reflection, and professional identity reshaping. Students typically transition from passive knowledge reception to active knowledge construction irrespective of cultural background. The global nursing workforce’s universal demand for advanced practice and lifelong learning further corroborates the value of education in promoting professional growth and self-identity development [29]. Future research could undertake cross-cultural comparisons to delve deeper into these universalities and specificities, offering more comprehensive insights for global nursing education and career development.

Transformation of professional perception

In China, specialist nurses achieve professional recognition and respect that is more akin to physicians within their specific domains [30], significantly enhancing their professional identity [31]. In contrast, general nursing staff often experience a weaker professional identity due to lower societal recognition and institutional constraints [32]. Participants described gaining a strong sense of professional identity in specialist practice, where their expertise earned respect from patients. Conversely, in general clinical settings, they encountered differing patient attitudes and experiences where their advanced degrees were questioned for performing only basic procedures. These experiences led them, in constructing their professional identity, to potentially view basic nursing as an auxiliary or lower-level task. We argue that this cognitive bias is not an objective judgment of the intrinsic value of basic nursing but rather a temporary deviation in their professional identity construction, significantly influenced by the clinical environment and external perceptions. This reflects how the nursing profession suffers from societal stereotypes, often not being perceived as autonomous [33]. In fact, basic nursing is central to nursing, profoundly impacting patients’ quality of life, recovery, and dignity. However, society holds a contradictory view of its value: simultaneously demanding it yet devaluing it as simple labour. This devaluation stems from stereotypes, the medical system’s emphasis on diagnosis and treatment, and the often invisible outcomes of nursing care [33]. Consequently, the state and government departments must promote the fundamental value of nursing to the public. Such efforts would strengthen nurses’ professional identity in basic nursing and underscore its clinical significance, ultimately enhancing their societal status.

Furthermore, the clinical education model experienced by participants may also be a significant factor influencing their professional identity. In the MNS education phase in China, clinical practice typically employs a mentorship system. In most cases, students are guided by clinical mentors and undertake internships at the mentor’s affiliated hospital [34]. During their clinical internships, they establish the closest professional bond with their mentors, marking a critical stage in their professional development. The traditional cultural concept of “imparting knowledge, resolving doubts, and cultivating virtue” is deeply rooted in the education system, making mentors not only transmitters of knowledge but also guides in shaping students’ thinking and character. This “direct and profound influence” makes the “significant individual differences” among mentors a substantial risk. Interview results indicated that the “randomness” and “non-standardization” of the mentorship system contribute to uneven MNS training quality and impaired professional identity. We recommend establishing clear, transparent, equitable mentor assessment and evaluation mechanisms. Beyond the mentorship system, hospitals and universities should jointly foster a supportive learning environment to help MNS students better navigate challenges and successfully construct their professional identity.

Limitations

While we intentionally selected female participants to ensure sample homogeneity, this methodological choice inherently limited our ability to thoroughly explore the potential profound impacts of gender identity or broader socio-cultural contexts on their MNS internship experiences and the development of their self-identity. While narrowing the scope of inquiry into these particular socio-cultural elements, this group-specific approach provides valuable direction for future research to delve deeper into these dimensions. Furthermore, this study employed IPA, a method whose core tenet lies in the researcher’s in-depth interpretation of participants’ lived experiences. Consequently, the research findings are, to some extent, shaped by the researcher’s perspective, experiences, and theoretical framework, which inevitably introduces a degree of interpretive subjectivity. Finally, due to geographical constraints, telephone interviews were necessitated as the primary data collection method. While this format ensured the study’s feasibility and accessibility, it also resulted in the absence of non-verbal cues such as body language and facial expressions. This limitation potentially restricted a comprehensive understanding of participants’ underlying emotions and the full depth of their expressed meanings.

Conclusion

This study explored three main themes: drivers for pursuing MNS education, transformation of self-perception, and transformation of professional perception. Based on participants’ narratives, this study revealed the complex motivations driving their decision to pursue an MNS education. These findings offer valuable insights for educators to understand the underlying needs and contextual factors influencing registered nurses’ advanced study. During the internship phase, participants described their evolving self-perception and professional identity, highlighting the crucial dual role played by mentors in this transformative process. These insights provide a strong foundation for clinical practice departments and policymakers to optimise the professional identity environment.

Note 1

This study focuses on full-time Master of Nursing Speciality (MNS) programs in China. Admission to these programs is highly competitive, requiring candidates to pass a rigorous national entrance examination and a university-specific re-examination. Individual universities determine Enrollment quotas independently, influenced by factors such as faculty strength, available resources, and national educational planning set by the Ministry of Education. While tuition fees are typically charged, various scholarships and grants are available to support students. The MNS curriculum encompasses two main components: structured coursework and extensive core clinical practice. Coursework includes foundational general subjects, degree-specific core courses, specialisation-specific modules, and elective courses. During the clinical practice phase, students undertake advanced rotations in hospitals under the direct guidance of clinical mentors. Assessment methods are diverse, comprising theoretical examinations, clinical performance evaluations, case analyses, and a final thesis defence. Graduation requirements include completing all required academic credits, the prescribed clinical practice hours, and the final thesis defence. The MNS program is a high-intensity, intensive training process designed to cultivate versatile nursing professionals with a solid theoretical foundation and advanced practical competencies.
