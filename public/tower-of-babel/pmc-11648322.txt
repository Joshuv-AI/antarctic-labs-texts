Zero-Dollar Copayment Impact on Adherence Scores for Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services Star Ratings Generic Medications
Authors: Abigail Wedoff, Jocelyn McDougal, Karan Patel, Naomi Y Yates, Rachana J Patel, Thomas Delate
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) Star Ratings system pushes Medicare Advantage health plans to achieve ever greater attainments in key metrics, including adherence to hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA reductase inhibitor (statins), renin-angiotensin system (RAS) antagonist, and noninsulin antihyperglycemic (DM) medications. The purpose of this observational study was to evaluate the impact of expanding a $0 copayment (copay) benefit from mail order–only to mail order plus retail pharmacies on adherence to statin, RAS, and DM medications.MethodsMedicare beneficiaries with and without a $0 copay expansion who received ≥ 1 dispensing of a generic, CMS Star Ratings RAS, statin, and/or DM medication during both 2021 and 2022 were included. Outcomes included changes in proportion of days covered (PDC) from 2021 to 2022 and proportions of patients with a PDC ≥ 0.8 in 2022.ResultsOverall (N = 65,716), patients had a high (> 0.930) mean baseline PDC. Patients with $0 copay expansion had a statistically significant greater mean PDC increase for statin (adjusted P = 0.038), reduction for RAS (adjusted P = 0.036), and no difference for DM (adjusted P = 0.696). Patients with a $0 copay expansion had statistically significant higher proportions of beneficiaries with a PDC ≥ 0.8 for statin (adjusted P = 0.003) and RAS (adjusted P = 0.003) but not DM (adjusted P = 0.256).DiscussionAn expanded $0 copay was associated with minor increased generic statin medication adherence.ConclusionIn populations with a high baseline PDC, expanding a $0 copay benefit on generic statin, RAS, and DM medications to dispensing outside of mail order may only contribute slightly to an increase or sustainment of a health plan’s CMS Star Ratings.

Introduction

Medication adherence has garnered increasing attention from Medicare Advantage (MA) health plans since the addition of the medication adherence measure to the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) Medicare Part D Star Ratings program in 2012.1,2 The Star Ratings program’s medication adherence score (using the proportion of days covered [PDC] per beneficiary-year metric) evaluates beneficiary claims-based adherence to renin-angiotensin system (RAS) antagonist, Hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA reductase inhibitor (statins), and noninsulin antihyperglycemic (DM) medications with each medication category’s adherence score triple-weighted in the Star Ratings calculation.3

In the quest for continuous improvement to better address health care priorities and gaps, CMS continues to raise PDC cutpoints (ie, percentage of beneficiaries in the denominator who have a PDC ≥ 0.8) for RAS, statin, and DM medication adherence scores and have never reduced a cutpoint in their Star Ratings.4 For example, the PDC cutpoints for RAS, statin, and DM medications have risen from 88%, 87%, and 85% in 2019 to 91%, 91%, and 90% in 2022, respectively. In addition, CMS indirectly penalizes health plans due to the structure of the adherence measures. For example, if a beneficiary had 2 fills for a RAS at the beginning of the year but then her/his practitioner discontinued the RAS because of a blood pressure of < 130 / < 80 mmHg, the beneficiary would continue in the PDC denominator for the remainder of the year and achieve a low PDC because there were no additional fills for the RAS. Thus, health plans are incentivized to increase their beneficiaries’ RAS, statin, and DM medication adherence, overall, to address these challenges.

Numerous factors can impact claims-based medication adherence, including prescription drug copayments (copays).5 6 Previous studies have reported associations between medication copays and adherence based on the concept that medication affordability (ie, ability to pay for one’s medication) is correlated with increased medication use.6,7 Although it has been hypothesized that decreased copays will increase adherence,8 there is a paucity of real-world evidence regarding the association of reduced copays with improvements in CMS Star Ratings medication adherence scores.

In response to CMS’s continued raising of medication adherence score cutpoints for Star Ratings and a belief that a very slight change in financial conditions could lead to a large difference in medication affordability in a population with fixed incomes (eg, Medicare beneficiaries),9 the Colorado and Georgia Kaiser Permanente regions began offering a no copay ($0 copay) benefit to their MA plan members for generic medications from Tier 1 and Tier 2 CMS Star Ratings dispensed through their mail order pharmacies.10 To further increase CMS enrollment, the $0 copay benefit was expanded in 2022 to include both mail order and retail pharmacy dispensing of Tier 1 and Tier 2 generics. As not all MA plan sponsors participated in the $0 copay benefit during all years, 2 groups of beneficiaries naturally arose: those that did have an expanded $0 copay benefit and those that did not have a $0 copay benefit. The purpose of this real-world analysis was to evaluate if an association exists between expanding a $0 copay to include both mail order and retail pharmacy dispensing with changes in adherence to CMS Star Ratings Tier 1 and Tier 2 statin, RAS, and DM generic medications. Information from this evaluation will inform Medicare beneficiaries and their caregivers and policy makers on the value of a $0 copay benefit when expanding it to include both mail order and retail pharmacy dispensing.

Methods

Study setting and design

This was an observational, multicohort study of Medicare beneficiaries from 2 integrated health care delivery systems: Kaiser Permanente Colorado and Kaiser Permanente Georgia. Kaiser Permanente Colorado serves approximately 520,000 members, including 66,000 Medicare beneficiaries, and Kaiser Permanente Georgia serves approximately 320,000 members, including 36,000 beneficiaries. Kaiser Permanente utilizes multiple programs and processes to improve Medicare beneficiaries’ adherence including: 1) modeling algorithms to identify beneficiaries likeliest to be nonadherent; 2) text messaging to alert beneficiaries to prescriptions needing refilling; 3) interactive voice response telephone calling to allow beneficiaries to refill prescriptions; 4) live telephone calling from technicians and pharmacists in beneficiaries’ preferred languages to provide prescription refilling and adherence consulting, respectively; and 5) longer-term medication supplies. These programs and processes have contributed to Kaiser Permanente’s lofty statin, RAS, and DM medication adherence scores and its 4- to 5-star CMS Star Ratings.11

In addition to the above Kaiser Permanente adherence programs and processes, a subset of Kaiser Permanente Colorado and Kaiser Permanente Georgia Medicare Part D beneficiaries were initiated on a $0 copay benefit for generic Tier 1 and Tier 2 statin, RAS, and DM medications dispensed from a Kaiser Permanente mail order pharmacy. This benefit was expanded in 2022 to include not only mail order but also Kaiser Permanente retail pharmacies ($0 copay group). Other Kaiser Permanente Colorado and Kaiser Permanente Georgia beneficiaries continued with their usual copays, ranging from $5 to $15, in 2021 and 2022 (usual copay group). Beneficiaries in both groups had continuous eligibility in their respective Kaiser Permanente regions and in their copay groups in both 2021 and 2022. The index date for both groups was January 1, 2022. Beneficiaries could contribute to more than 1 medication analysis if they were receiving > 1 eligible medication class, but they could not be in more than 1 copay group.

Kaiser Permanente is a not-for-profit integrated health care delivery system that provides comprehensive medical services. Kaiser Permanente Colorado and Kaiser Permanente Georgia offer patients a variety of health plans where their practitioners utilize an electronic health record (EPIC Systems Corp). Pharmacy, membership, encounter data, and coded and free-text information from within the delivery system, as well as from other contracted and affiliated facilities, are captured in Kaiser Permanente’s administrative and claims databases. PDC was calculated by the CMS contractor, Acumen, LLC, using Kaiser Permanente–provided medication dispensing data. This study did not require institutional review board oversight as it did not meet the regulatory definition of research involving human participants at 45 CFR 46.102(l).

Beneficiary population

Eligible beneficiaries ≥ 65 years of age were in both the 2021 and 2022 denominator of the Acumen, LLC data (so that 2021 to 2022 change in PDC could be calculated) and received ≥ 1 dispensing of a generic, CMS Star Rating RAS, statin, and/or DM medication during both study years. Beneficiaries were excluded if they had ≥ 1 dispensing of brand RAS, statin, and/or DM medication in either study year. In addition, beneficiaries who had received medical financial assistance (MFA), qualified for a low-income subsidy (LIS) from the US government, were enrolled in hospice, had a diagnosis of end-stage renal disease, disenrolled from Kaiser Permanente, or died during the study years were excluded. Criteria were applied by Acumen, LLC except brand dispensing, receipt of MFA, and qualifying for LIS.

Study outcomes

The primary outcomes were changes in PDC from 2021 to 2022 for RAS, statin, and DM medications. Secondary outcomes included the proportions of beneficiaries with a PDC ≥ 0.8 in 2022 for RAS, statin, and DM medications. PDC is a ratio of the days covered by a prescription during a given measurement period. CMS considers a beneficiary adherent to a medication if the beneficiary’s PDC is ≥ 0.8 for the prescribed medication class.12

Subanalyses were performed by restricting the cohorts to beneficiaries with a respective 2021 PDC < 0.8 to assess for baseline PDC ceiling effects on changes in PDC. In addition, beneficiaries with an available geocoded household income were assessed for an interaction of income and copay group on changes in PDC.

Data collection

Beneficiaries were identified from the Acumen, LLC data files provided to Kaiser Permanente Colorado and Kaiser Permanente Georgia from the CMS. Acumen, LLC records were used to identify RAS, statin, and DM cohorts and PDC for 2021 and 2022. Electronic Kaiser Permanente administrative pharmacy records were used to identify brand medication dispensing and membership records were used to identify beneficiaries who received MFA and/or qualified for LIS. Kaiser Permanente administrative electronic medical and membership records were used to identify beneficiaries’ copay benefits and characteristics (eg, date of birth, sex, race/ethnicity, comorbidities). Beneficiary characteristics were collected during the 365 days prior to the index date (baseline).

Data analysis

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied. All beneficiaries meeting inclusion and exclusion criteria were included in the study. Age was calculated as of the index date. Beneficiaries were grouped by copay benefit. The presence of specific comorbidities was determined using the Quan adaptation of the Charlson Comorbidity Index.13

Beneficiary characteristics and PDC outcomes are reported as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate, for continuous data (eg, age) and proportions for nominal data (eg, sex). Characteristics were compared between groups using t tests (or nonparametric equivalent, as appropriate) or χ2 tests of association. Changes in PDC from 2021 to 2022 and proportions of beneficiaries with a PDC ≥ 0.8 in 2022 were calculated.

Unadjusted comparisons in PDC changes and proportions with a PDC ≥ 0.8 were made within the cohorts and between the groups with t tests and χ2 tests of association, respectively. Adjusted, multivariable, linear, and logistic regression modeling of PDC changes and proportions with a PDC ≥ 0.8, respectively, were performed. Potential confounders included in the models were based on clinical judgment and statistical analyses of baseline characteristics that could impact follow-up adherence. Included were the standard demographics, age, sex, and race/ethnicity; baseline PDC as it was judged to be correlated with follow-up PDC; Charlson Comorbidity Index as disease burden would likely require additional chronic medication use; dementia and mild cognitive impairment because beneficiaries with these behavioral indications may not understand their disease or why they need their medication; and depression as it may impact motivation to be adherent to a medication regimen.

Subanalyses were performed by restricting the cohorts to beneficiaries who had a 2021 PDC < 0.8 and then beneficiaries who had a geocoded household income. Quartiles of incomes were calculated (< $52,517; $52,517 to < $67,083; $67,083 to < $83,817; and ≥ $83,817). Unadjusted and adjusted analyses were performed.

Overall, beneficiaries were older (mean age ∼74 years) with a mean baseline PDC ~0.94, had a modest burden of chronic disease, were an even mix of sexes, and had diverse racial/ethnic identities. There were no statistically significant differences in baseline mean PDC between the $0 copay and usual copay groups in all cohorts (all P > 0.05; Tables 1–3).

The $0 copay group had a statistically significant higher follow-up mean PDC in the statin (unadjusted P = 0.006) and RAS (unadjusted P < 0.001) groups, but not the DM (unadjusted P = 0.410) group (Table 4). The $0 copay group had a statistically significant greater increase in mean change in PDC in the statin (adjusted P = 0.038), greater reduction in mean change in PDC in the RAS (adjusted P = 0.036), and no difference in mean change in PDC in the DM (adjusted P = 0.696) groups. The $0 copay group had statistically significant higher proportions of beneficiaries with a follow-up PDC ≥ 0.8 in the statin (adjusted P = 0.003) and RAS (adjusted P = 0.003) groups, but not the DM (adjusted P = 0.256) group.

In the first subanalysis, there were limited beneficiaries with baseline PDC < 0.8: statins N = 3080, RAS N = 2168, and DM N = 782. The $0 copay group had a statistically significant greater mean change in PDC in the statin (0.187 vs 0.171, adjusted P = 0.024), RAS (0.197 vs 0.186, adjusted P = 0.014), and DM (0.203 vs 0.174, adjusted P = 0.047) groups. The $0 copay group had statistically significant higher proportions of beneficiaries with a follow-up PDC ≥ 0.8 in the statin (75.5% vs 70.2%, adjusted P = 0.013) and RAS (76.5% vs 70.7%, adjusted P = 0.006) groups, but not the DM (74.6% vs 67.3%, adjusted P = 0.085) group.

In the second subanalysis, there were 30,518 beneficiaries who were geocoded to a household income. All quartiles in both the $0 copay and usual copay groups had minimal mean decreases (eg, < −0.010) in follow-up PDC for the statin and RAS cohorts and a minimal mean increase (eg, < 0.010) in follow-up PDC in the DM cohort. In adjusted models of the change in follow-up PDC between the $0 copay and usual copay groups, increasing income was not statistically significantly associated with increasing follow-up PDC in any cohort (all P > 0.05).

Discussion

This observational analysis of approximately 66,000 Medicare beneficiaries who did and did not have a $0 copay benefit expanded from mail order–only to mail order and retail pharmacy dispensing identified that year-over-year change in adherence scores for generic statin medications with CMS Star Ratings was improved for beneficiaries with the expanded $0 copay benefit. Change in generic RAS medication PDC was reduced more than for beneficiaries without a $0 copay benefit, although there was no between-group difference in the PDC changes for generic DM medications. Although there was an improvement in PDC for statins, this improvement was likely not clinically meaningful and owing to the minimal difference in PDC change between the groups. These findings are important as they are from one of the first real-world studies to have examined the use and expansion of a $0 copay benefit to improve adherence to generic medications with CMS Star Ratings. These findings suggest that, given the ever-increasing CMS adherence score cutpoints and, thus, increasing difficulty in attaining high CMS Star Ratings, an expansion of a $0 copay benefit to include retail pharmacy dispensing may improve adherence for some generic medications in beneficiaries with a high baseline PDC. Unfortunately, the impact of these improvements is likely clinically negligible and may only slightly contribute to an increase/sustainment in a health plan’s CMS Star Ratings.

The authors did identify clinically meaningful improvements in year-over-year changes in adherence scores for generic statin, RAS, and DM medications with CMS Star Ratings in beneficiaries with the expanded $0 copay benefit and baseline PDC < 0.8. But the differences in change values were minor and possibly due to regression to the mean PDC. No interaction between copay benefit type with household incomes and change in adherence scores was identified. Nevertheless, there does appear to be a ceiling effect whereby beneficiaries with low baseline PDC may experience meaningful improvement in PDC with the expanded $0 copay benefit. Unfortunately, the implementation of a $0 copay benefit in this population alone would be challenging.

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing $0 copay to usual copay have been conducted. Kulik and colleagues randomized patients who had a coronary artery bypass graft surgery (CABG; n = 1052) and those who did not undergo CABG (n = 4803) to $0 copay or usual copay for statins and antihypertensive medications.14 Among participants with a CABG, the $0 copay benefit increased the proportion of patients with ≥ 80% medication possession ratio (MPR) during follow-up by approximately 50% to 60% for statins and antihypertensives but only approximately 25% to 30% for participants without CABG.14 Choudhry and colleagues conducted a similar RCT in patients who had a myocardial infarction.15 Patients were randomly assigned to $0 copay or usual copay and assessed for follow-up MPR for statins and antihypertensives (after myocardial infarction hospitalization discharge). Participants in the $0 copay arm (n = 2223) had approximately 4% to 6% higher MPR than patients in the usual copay arm (n = 2267) during follow-up.15 These studies had considerably numerically lower follow-up adherence scores in their participants than the authors’ populations; however, neither RCT provided participants’ baseline MPR or mean changes in MPR. Nevertheless, the numerically lower follow-up adherence scores suggest that their participants had considerably numerically lower baseline adherence than the authors’ populations. In addition, both RCTs included brand and generic medications in their MPR calculations and they evaluated adherence in very specific patient populations as opposed to the authors’ general beneficiary population.14,15

Among real-world studies, Maciejewski and colleagues conducted a quasiexperimental study on the effect of copay being reduced to $0 for generic metformin.16 Compared with patients with usual copay (n = 2201), patients with $0 copay (n = 2201) had a statistically significant, adjusted mean 3.76% (~0.038 PDC) increase in year-over-year change in their MPR.16 The authors did not identify any difference in mean generic DM medication year-over-year PDC change between their beneficiaries who had usual and $0 copays except in patients with a baseline PDC < 0.8. In both groups of Maciejewski and colleagues’ patients, their baseline MPR was approximately 75%; thus, much lower, numerically, than the authors’ overall cohorts’ mean PDC. Chernew and colleagues in their quasiexperimental study examined year-over-year changes in MPR between 2 employers: one that reduced copays to $0 for generic and 50% for brand statins, antihypertensives, and DM medications and another that maintained copays.17 They reported that year-over-year differences in mean MPR change were 3.39%, ~2.75%, and 4.02% for statins, antihypertensives, and DM medications, respectively.17 Their baseline MPRs were 53%, 68%, and 69% for statins, antihypertensives, and DM medications (ie, considerably lower, numerically, than the authors’ populations’ adherence scores), respectively, and they did not assess generic medications separately.15 Pawaskar and colleagues assessed 10-month generic DM medication adherence among fee-for-service Medicare beneficiaries (N = 131,594) who had copays of $0 to > $10.6 They identified no consistent pattern of decreasing PDC with increasing copay categories.6 They did not provide baseline PDC.

The authors identified no interaction between copay benefit type with household income and adherence. Cong and colleagues conducted a retrospective analysis of $0 copay vs usual copay for a large subset of brand and generic medications for chronic diseases.18 They followed patients in 3 quartiles of household income (< $40,000; ≥ $40,000 to < $100,000; ≥ $100,000) for 10 months to assess PDC. Although they reported that the mean change in PDC was −0.013 for patients in the usual copay group, patients with $0 copay had an overall 0.007 mean increase PDC, with patients in the lowest income in the $0 copay group having the largest mean increase in PDC (0.012).18 Although the authors identified similar modest improvements in overall PDC, their findings are difficult to fully compare to Cong and colleagues’ as the latter did not provide baseline PDC and included a number of medications bundled into 1 PDC per patient. In addition, the authors excluded beneficiaries who qualified for LIS, so low-income beneficiaries who may have been less likely to afford medications were not in their analysis.

The authors’ findings are relevant to health plans trying to understand the relationship between expanding copay benefits and adherence to generic medications with CMS Star Ratings. These findings suggest that increasing pressure to improve/sustain high CMS Star Ratings may encourage medication benefit changes that result in only short-term, limited effects on adherence. As medication adherence has multifaceted factors that affect it, health plans, especially those with already high PDC, will need to consider holistic approaches (eg, beneficiary and caregiver education on adherence’s value, optimization of treatment, use of adherence aids, practitioner education on adherence monitoring)19 to improve/sustain already high CMS Star Ratings. Nevertheless, the authors did identify some incremental gain for use of an expanded $0 copay benefit so cost-effective analysis of this benefit should be conducted.

Limitations

This study was strengthened by a large, diverse racial/ethnic population of beneficiaries with data collected from CMS and Kaiser Permanente databases. The authors examined 3 medication classes, conducted robust adjusted analyses, and performed detailed subanalyses to further elucidate the relationship between an expanded $0 copay benefit and adherence. This study did have limitations. The authors did not compare the implementation of a $0 copay benefit to a usual copay benefit but rather an expansion of a $0 copay benefit. The authors included only Medicare beneficiaries, possibly limiting extrapolation of their results to a non-Medicare population. Although the authors did adjust for factors that may have affected adherence, there could be unmeasured factors that were not accounted for. Additional study is recommended to analyze the effect of other factors (eg, health literacy, health/medication beliefs) on adherence. The authors’ analysis included only generic medications. Beneficiaries receiving a (high-cost) brand medication with a $0 generic copay benefit may have distinct outcomes that the authors did not assess (eg, being incentivized to switch to a low-cost generic medication). The authors’ analysis of the relationship between household income and adherence only included beneficiaries who could be geocoded. Nevertheless, that sample included 46% of the authors’ beneficiary population. Finally, beneficiaries in the authors’ overall population had a very high baseline mean PDC. The authors did perform subanalyses on lower PDC beneficiaries who responded more effectively to a $0 copay benefit; however, implementing an exclusive benefit for them would not be feasible.

Conclusions

This real-world study of approximately 66,000 Medicare beneficiaries identified that expansion of a $0 copay benefit from mail order pharmacy–only dispensing to mail order and retail pharmacy dispensing was associated with a minor improvement in PDC for generic statin medications, but not generic RAS or DM medications. In populations with a high baseline PDC and established $0 copay benefit via mail order, the impact of expanding a $0 copay benefit to mail order and retail pharmacies for generic statin, RAS, and DM medications is likely clinically negligible and may only slightly contribute to an increase/sustainment in a health plan’s CMS Star Ratings. Medication adherence is multifaceted and can be impacted by a myriad of factors. Copays are an important aspect of medication adherence, but for lower-cost generic medications, other factors may impact adherence more. Future real-world studies in a large Medicare beneficiary population with a high baseline PDC should be performed to confirm these findings and/or identify the cost-effectiveness of an expanded $0 copay benefit.
