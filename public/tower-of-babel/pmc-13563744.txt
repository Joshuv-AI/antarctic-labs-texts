Associations of the Chinese healthy eating index and dietary diversity score with depressive and anxious symptoms among older adults
Authors: Xinyan Ma, Hanqing Zhao, Yuhong Zhao, Chao Huang, Hao Ai, Minghui Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDepression and anxiety significantly contribute to the mental health disease burden. Although healthy dietary patterns may mitigate mental distress, evidence regarding the associations of dietary quality and dietary diversity with mental health remains limited. This study aimed to investigate the independent and joint associations of the Chinese Healthy Eating Index (CHEI) and Dietary Diversity Score (DDS) with depression, anxiety, and their co-occurrence among Chinese older adults.MethodsWe utilized cross-sectional data from the 2018 Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey, which included a total of 9,455 participants aged 65 years and older. The CHEI and DDS were applied to evaluate dietary quality and dietary diversity, respectively. We employed logistic regression models to examine these associations.ResultsIn the fully adjusted model, higher CHEI was associated with lower odds of depression (OR [odds ratio] tertile [T] 3 vs. T1= 0.63, 95% CI [confidence interval]: 0.53, 0.75), anxiety (OR T3 vs. T1= 0.61, 95% CI: 0.51, 0.74), and their co-occurrence (OR T3 vs. T1= 0.66, 95% CI: 0.51, 0.84). A 10-unit increment in CHEI was associated with 33% lower odds of depression, 32% lower odds of anxiety, and 32% lower odds of their co-occurrence. Similarly, a higher DDS was inversely associated with the odds of depression (OR T3 vs. T1= 0.59, 95% CI: 0.50, 0.70), anxiety (OR T3 vs. T1= 0.56, 95% CI: 0.47, 0.67), and their co-occurrence (OR T3 vs. T1= 0.60, 95% CI: 0.47, 0.76). Each one-unit increase in DDS was correlated with a 12% lower odds of depression, anxiety, and their co-occurrence. Moreover, compared to the group with low CHEI and low DDS, the group with high CHEI and high DDS showed significantly lower odds of depression, anxiety, and their co-occurrence.ConclusionsThe study revealed that higher CHEI and DDS scores were inversely associated with depressive symptoms, anxious symptoms, and their co-occurrence among Chinese older adults. Additional longitudinal research is required to confirm our findings.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08517-9.

Introduction

The 2021 Global Burden of Disease Report indicates that depression and anxiety rank among the most prevalent mental health disorders worldwide, collectively affecting over 300 million people. The incidence of these disorders continues to rise steadily [1]. According to a 2013 estimation, mental disorders accounted for over 10 million disability-adjusted life years in China. This burden was projected to increase by approximately 10% by 2025 [2]. Furthermore, epidemiological evidence suggests that depressive and anxiety disorders substantially increase the risk of cardiovascular disease, hypertension, and cancer, as well as mortality [3–6]. Older adults are more susceptible to chronic illnesses and adverse life events throughout aging. Due to this heightened biological and psychosocial vulnerability, they are prone to experiencing more severe psychological distress [7]. Accumulating evidence has showed that the elderly population bears a higher burden of mental health impairments and faces more prominent psychological challenges compared with younger age groups [8, 9]. Thus, identifying modifiable lifestyle factors for mental impairment among the elderly is of vital importance. A growing body of evidence indicates that diet, as one such modifiable lifestyle factor, may influence mental health.

The recently updated Chinese Dietary Guidelines (2022) have, for the first time, introduced the Eastern Healthy Dietary Pattern [10]. This new framework aims to promote dietary diversity and quality by encouraging a variety of foods and providing specific intake recommendations for each food category. This dietary pattern recommends increased consumption of fresh vegetables and fruits, fish, whole grains, and soy products, while reducing salt intake [11]. Given China’s large population and rich culinary history, understanding the impacts of dietary quality and dietary diversity on the health of older adults represents a critical area of public health research. Large-scale epidemiological studies have indicated that the Chinese Healthy Eating Index (CHEI) and Dietary Diversity Score (DDS) are valid indicators for assessing dietary quality and dietary diversity, respectively [11]. To date, only one cohort study including 10,436 Chinese adults has explored the joint association of high CHEI and high DDS with all-cause mortality, reporting that adherence to both high CHEI and high DDS was associated with a reduced risk of all-cause mortality [11]. Therefore, we hypothesized that adherence to both high CHEI and high DDS was associated with favorable mental health outcomes and confers protective impacts against psychological distress in the elderly population.

Using data from a nationwide survey in mainland China, this study sought to examine the independent and joint associations of CHEI and DDS with depressive symptoms, anxious symptoms, and their co-occurrence among older adults. These findings are expected to provide novel insights into the relationship between diet and mental health in Eastern populations.

Methods

Study population

The present study utilized data from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), a community-based, nationally representative prospective cohort that collects data on health-related factors among adults aged ≥ 65 years in China [12, 13]. Investigators collected information on participants’ demographic characteristics, lifestyles, dietary intake, and disease status through face-to-face interviews. In addition, physical examinations (e.g., heart rate, body weight, calf circumference, and hip circumference measurements) were conducted for all participants. Cross-sectional data for this study were derived from the eighth wave (2018) of the CLHLS. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Biomedical Ethics, Peking University (IRB0001052-13074).

A total of 15,874 elderly participants were initially recruited. Exclusions were made for individuals with: (1) incomplete dietary intake information; (2) incomplete mental health data; (3) a baseline age of less than 65 years; and (4) missing covariate data. Finally, the analytic sample for this study comprised 9,455 participants (Supplementary Figure S1).

Dietary assessment

A simplified food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) was employed to assess dietary intake, a validated instrument widely employed in prior studies [14, 15]. The question, “How often do you eat this food ?” was posed for each food item [16]. Dietary intake of participants at the time of survey was assessed by well-trained investigators. A wide range of food items were assessed, including staple foods, fresh fruits, edible oils, pickled vegetables, fresh vegetables, legumes and legume products, garlic, nuts, fungi and algae, meat, aquatic products such as fish, eggs, dairy products, white sugar or candies, medicinal herbs, and tea. Both CHEI and DDS are widely used dietary indicators in nutritional epidemiological research, and their reliability and validity have been supported by previous studies [17–19]. In our study, higher CHEI and DDS scores indicated greater adherence to dietary quality and dietary diversity, respectively. Dietary quality was assessed using the modified CHEI derived from the CLHLS, which comprises 13 food groups [18, 20], including fruits, vegetables, kimchi, sugar, tea, meat, fish, eggs, legumes, garlic, nuts, mushrooms, and milk. The detailed scoring criteria were provided in Supplementary Table S1. The total CHEI score ranges from 0 to 50, reflecting a healthier dietary pattern with higher scores.

Dietary diversity was assessed using the DDS, which was calculated based on the consumption of nine food groups: vegetables, fruits, nuts, meat, legumes and legume products, fish, eggs, dairy products, and tea [21]. The frequency of fruit and vegetable intake was recorded as daily, often, occasionally, or seldom/almost never, with a score of 1 assigned for daily/often consumption and 0 for occasional or seldom/almost never consumption. For the remaining food items, recorded as daily, weekly, monthly, occasionally, or seldom/almost never, a score of 1 was assigned for daily/weekly intake and 0 for monthly, occasional, or seldom/almost never intake. The total DDS score ranges from 0 to 9, reflecting better adherence to dietary diversity as scores increase.

Assessment of depressive and anxious symptoms

We used the 10-item Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10) to assess depressive symptoms in the elderly population. Each item is scored from 0 to 3 points. In our study, a total score of 10 or higher indicates depressive symptoms among elderly participants, and this cutoff value has been validated as effective in most previous studies [22, 23].

We used the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7) to assess anxiety symptoms. Each item is scored from 0 to 3 points. In our study, a total score of 5 or above indicates the presence of anxiety symptoms in the participant [24, 25].

The co-occurrence of depressive and anxiety was defined as their concurrent presence [26].

Assessment of covariates

In the current study, based on both self-reported and objectively measured indicators, we adjusted for potential confounding factors related to demographic characteristics, lifestyle, and chronic diseases. These include age, sex (male/female), body mass index (BMI), ethnicity (Han/other), residential area (urban, town, or rural), annual income (≥ 30,000 CNY/< 30,000 CNY), exercise status (yes/no), marital status (cohabiting with a partner/not cohabiting with a partner), labor status (yes/no), alcohol consumption (yes/no), smoking status (yes/no), and hypertension (yes/no).

BMI was derived from weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m²). Hypertension was defined based on the average of two consecutive blood pressure measurements, with an average systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg.

Statistical analyses

In our study, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. For normally distributed data, we expressed results as mean ± standard deviation and performed comparisons with t-tests or ANOVA. For non-normally distributed data, we reported median (interquartile range [IQR]) and made comparisons using the Mann-Whitney U test or the Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency (%) and compared using the chi-square test. Multivariable logistic regression was employed to assess the associations of CHEI and DDS with mental health outcomes. We employed three models to adjust for covariates: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, and BMI; and Model 3 was further adjusted for ethnicity, annual income, residence, marital status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, labor status, physical activity status, and hypertension. In addition, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis with three knots located at the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles was performed to assess potential non-linear relationships underlying these associations, while E-value analysis was applied to quantify the potential influence of unmeasured confounding factors.

Evaluate the joint associations of CHEI and DDS with mental health outcomes, we further categorized participants into four groups based on the median values of CHEI and DDS and compared their associations with mental health outcomes. The group with both high CHEI and high DDS scores served as the reference group.

Subsequently, we conducted subgroup analyses stratified by age (65–79 years, 80–99 years, or ≥ 100 years), sex (male or female), smoking status (yes or no), alcohol consumption (yes or no), residential area (urban, town, or rural), and BMI (< 18.5, 18.5–23.9, or ≥ 24 kg/m²) to examine the associations of CHEI, DDS, and their combination with mental health outcomes. The multiplicative interactions between CHEI, DDS, and their combination and these stratification factors were also examined. To avoid bias arising from the low cutoff of GAD-7 ≥ 5, which would capture numerous subclinical cases, we conducted sensitivity analyses using the higher threshold of GAD-7 ≥ 10.

All analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute), with two-tailed tests and a P-value < 0.05 defining statistical significance.

Results

Characteristics of the study participants

This study ultimately included 9,455 elderly individuals (median [IQR] age, 82 [74, 92]), with 4,361 males and 5,094 females. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the elderly population. In the present study, the prevalence rates of depression, anxiety, and their comorbidity among elderly participants were 13.74%, 11.74%, and 6.08%, respectively. Significant differences were detected across CHEI (T1 - T3) in age, BMI, sex, marital status, ethnicity, place of residence, income, alcohol consumption, physical activity, employment status, and hypertension (All P < 0.05). In addition, significant differences were detected across DDS (T1 - T3) in age, BMI, sex, marital status, ethnicity, place of residence, income, alcohol consumption, physical activity, employment status, and hypertension (All P < 0.05).

Associations between CHEI and DDS and depression and anxiety

Table 2 presents the associations of CHEI and DDS with mental health outcomes. In the fully adjusted model, participants in the highest tertile of CHEI, compared with those in the lowest tertile, were correlated with reduced odds of depression (OR = 0.63, 95% CI = 0.53, 0.75), anxiety (OR = 0.61, 95% CI = 0.51, 0.74), and their co-occurrence (OR = 0.66, 95% CI = 0.51, 0.84). Similarly, each 10-unit increase in CHEI was associated with a 33%, 32%, and 32% reduction in the odds of depression, anxiety, and their co-occurrence, respectively. Furthermore, in the RCS analysis, we observed no evidence of a nonlinear association between CHEI and depression, anxiety, and their co-occurrence (Supplementary Figure S2).

Combined impacts of CHEI and DDS on depression and anxiety

Compared to individuals with high CHEI and high DDS, those with low CHEI and low DDS were associated with higher odds of depression (OR = 1.66, 95% CI = 1.42, 1.94), anxiety (OR = 1.59, 95% CI = 1.34, 1.88), and their co-occurrence (OR = 1.60, 95% CI = 1.28, 2.00). The results were presented in Fig. 1. Notably, the group with both CHEI and DDS above the median exhibited significantly lower odds of depression, anxiety, and their co-occurrence compared to the other three groups, indicating that CHEI and DDS were both important.

Discussion

This study found that higher CHEI and DDS were correlated with reduced likelihood of depression, anxiety, and their co-occurrence. Furthermore, joint effect analysis revealed that adherence to both high CHEI and high DDS was significantly associated with reduced odds of depressive symptoms, anxious symptoms, and their co-occurrence in older adults.

Our study revealed that adherence to a higher CHEI was associated with lower odds of depression, anxiety, and their co-occurrence. Although evidence on the correlation between mental health and CHEI remains limited, research on CHEI in relation to health-related outcomes has been reported. For example, a prospective cohort study involving 1,342 Chinese adults indicated that a higher CHEI was correlated with a lower incidence of hypertension [27]. Another prospective study with a median follow-up of 12 years suggested that for every 5-point increase in CHEI score, the risk of diabetes may be reduced by 17% [28]. Additionally, a study based on the China Health and Nutrition Survey reported that a higher CHEI was associated with lower mortality [29]. These findings may provide preliminary support for the evidence linking CHEI to mental health. Our findings await confirmation in further prospective studies.

Our study was consistent with previous research in finding an association between a higher DDS and lower risks of depression and anxiety. For example, a study among pregnant and postpartum women reported a negative correlation between DDS and both depression and anxiety [30]. Research conducted in a population of Nepalese women of childbearing age indicated that adherence to dietary diversity was negatively correlated with depression and generalized anxiety disorder [31]. Additionally, a cross-sectional study in China demonstrated that adequate dietary diversity was associated with reduced anxiety, depression, and their co-occurrence [32]. These findings may be explained by the fact that dietary diversity ensures sufficient intake of micronutrients, such as antioxidants and vitamins. This, in turn, can improve metabolic health and reduce chronic inflammation, both of which are factors known to contribute to mental health impairment [33, 34].

Joint effect analysis revealed that older adults with both high CHEI and high DDS were significantly associated with lower odds of anxiety, depression, and their co-occurrence. Recent advances in lifestyle and economic levels in China have spurred growing consumption of meat, eggs, and dairy, thereby enhancing dietary diversity [35, 36]. However, dietary diversity does not guarantee higher dietary quality, especially if it is achieved through increased consumption of energy-dense but nutrient-poor foods [37, 38]. This emerging pattern may potentially weaken the protective effects typically associated with dietary diversity. Consequently, it is crucial to emphasize both adherence to dietary diversity and the quality of the diet. Dietary diversity guarantees adequate energy and diverse nutrient intake, whereas dietary balance ensures optimal quantity and proportion across food groups. The potential biological mechanisms underlying these findings may be attributed to the abundant anti-inflammatory nutrients and micronutrients derived from a high CHEI and high DDS dietary pattern, which could mitigate neuroinflammation and promote the synthesis of mood-regulating neurotransmitters [39, 40]. Adherence to the high CHEI and high DDS dietary pattern may also improve cardiovascular function and sleep quality in older adults, thereby further reducing the risk of late-life psychological distress [41, 42].

Interestingly, the observed inverse association between the combination of CHEI and DDS and depressive symptoms among township and rural populations, but not in urban residents, along with the significant interaction effect, may be attributed to several factors: Urban populations were often exposed to a variety of environmental and socioeconomic stressors, such as environmental pollution, work pressure, and social isolation [43, 44]. These potential risk factors for depression may mediate the etiological pathways, thereby attenuating the beneficial effects of diet on mental health outcomes. In contrast, such stressors tend to be less intense in rural and township settings, where the beneficial roles of diet in regulating neuroinflammation and oxidative stress were more likely to manifest [45, 46]. Additionally, the composition and implications of the combination of CHEI and DDS may vary across regions. In urban areas, consumption patterns might include higher proportions of takeout foods and sugar-sweetened beverages, potentially diluting nutritional quality [47]. Conversely, residents in rural and township areas tend to consume more locally sourced whole foods, which may yield greater biological benefits owing to favorable food environments and lower intake of ultra-processed foods. These findings underscore the importance of contextual factors in the association between diet and mental health. Consequently, public health strategies should develop regionally tailored dietary guidelines rather than promoting uniform recommendations. Unraveling the specific socio-environmental and biological pathways underlying this interaction should be a research priority.

To the best of our knowledge, this study offers novel evidence that the combination of high CHEI and high DDS was associated with improved mental health among Chinese older adults. The findings were enhanced by the use of a large, nationally representative sample. Moreover, a series of subgroup, interaction, and sensitivity analyses were conducted, ensuring the robustness of our conclusions. Several limitations warrant attention. First, the cross-sectional design precludes us from determining the temporal order of exposures and outcomes, so causal relationships cannot be inferred. Second, reverse causation is a major concern: older adults suffering from depressive or anxious symptoms may change their eating habits and reduce dietary diversity and healthy diet adherence, which may partly explain the observed correlations. Future prospective longitudinal studies are required to disentangle these bidirectional relationships. Third, we measured dietary exposure via a brief non-quantitative FFQ, which only collects information on eating frequency instead of participants’ actual food intake amounts. This limitation could lead to measurement bias. The use of self-reported data for both exposures and outcomes also raises the possibility of misclassification. Future research employing precise dietary quantification is warranted to confirm these associations and inform specific dietary guidelines. Fourth, the generalizability of our findings is limited by the exclusive focus on Chinese older adults. To extend the findings, future studies should include younger adults and multi-ethnic populations. Lastly, even after adjusting for a wide array of confounding factors, residual and unmeasured confounders such as total energy intake and environmental factors cannot be ruled out. Nevertheless, E-value analysis suggested that considerable confounding would be necessary to fully account for the observed association.

Conclusions

Our study found that higher CHEI and DDS scores were associated with lower odds of depression, anxiety, and their co-occurrence. These findings support the joint beneficial impacts of CHEI and DDS on mental health among Chinese older adults and highlight the need to consider both dietary quality and diversity when developing dietary guidelines for this population. Further prospective studies are warranted to verify these associations.
