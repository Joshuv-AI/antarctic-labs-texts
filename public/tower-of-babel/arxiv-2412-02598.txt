Efficient Techniques for Low-Rank Tensor Approximation and Applications in Robust Object Detection
Salman Ahmadi-Asl *, Naeim Rezaeian, Cesar F. Caiafa, André L. F. de Almeida

Abstract
This paper introduces efficient randomized fixed-precision and single-pass algorithms for low-tubal-rank approximation of third-order tensors. The proposed fixed-precision algorithms are faster and more efficient than the existing algorithms for approximating the truncated tensor SVD (T-SVD). Furthermore, unlike existing single-pass methods, which directly extend early, unstable matrix algorithms, the proposed approach adapts enhanced and stabilized matrix techniques to the tensor setting. Through extensive numerical experiments, we identify a critical flaw in current single-pass algorithms: using sketching parameters of equal size often produces ill-conditioned tensor least-squares problems, leading to inaccurate approximations. The proposed algorithms are demonstrably robust to this issue, achieving superior performance under identical conditions. We also evaluate the robustness of existing single-pass methods on real-world data tensors, including images and videos, a topic that has not been thoroughly examined before. Numerical results confirm the effectiveness of the proposed methods. Three applications are presented: image compression, video super-resolution, and deep learning.

Introduction

Tensor decompositions are foundational tools in signal processing, machine learning, and deep learning
Sidiropoulos et al. (2017)
;
Comon et al. (2009)
. Widely used formats include Tucker
De Lathauwer et al. (2000)
, tensor train and tensor ring
Oseledets (2011)
;
Zhao et al. (2016)
, tensor singular value decomposition (T-SVD)
Kilmer et al. (2013)
, block term decomposition, and constrained factorizations
de Almeida et al. (2008)
;
Stegeman and de Almeida (2010)
. These techniques underpin a broad range of applications, such as tensor completion
Chen et al. (2026)
;
Shu et al. (2026)
;
Yang et al. (2026)
, recommender systems
Sun et al. (2026)
;
Lu et al. (2025)
, machine learning
Miao et al. (2026)
;
He et al. (2025)
;
Navarro et al. (2025)
, and wireless communications
de Almeida et al. (2007)
;
Favier and de Almeida (2014)
;
de Araújo and de Almeida (2020)
;
de Araújo et al. (2021)
;
de Almeida et al. (2025)
.

For large-scale tensors, exact decomposition methods often become computationally infeasible. Over the past decade, randomized algorithms have emerged as a practical alternative. Compared to deterministic approaches, they are typically faster, more memory-efficient, and well-suited to large data. Furthermore, they offer controllable accuracy, allowing users to trade off computational cost against approximation quality.

The rapid growth of data has led to tensors of ever-increasing size. Low-rank approximations are critical in this context, particularly for tensor completion
Song et al. (2019)
and recommender systems
Frolov and Oseledets (2017)
. The difficulty escalates when tensors must be stored out-of-core or distributed across machines. Out-of-core storage means data resides on a disk rather than in memory, typically because the dataset exceeds available RAM. This regime introduces challenges such as slower access, higher latency, I/O bottlenecks, and complex data management
Warren and Marz (2015)
. In such environments, communication cost often dominates—and can surpass—computational cost. Minimizing the number of passes over the tensor is therefore essential. Within randomized linear algebra, algorithms designed under this constraint are called pass-efficient algorithms.

T-SVD generalizes the matrix SVD to third-order tensors via the tubal product (T-product)
Kilmer et al. (2013)
. It has been successfully applied to image and video completion
Zhang and Aeron (2016)
, face recognition
Hao et al. (2013)
, and compression
Zeng and Ng (2020)
. Randomized methods are now standard for computing low-tubal-rank approximations of large tensors
Zhang et al. (2018)
;
Qi and Yu (2021)
;
Ahmadi-Asl et al. (2024)
. A pass-efficient randomized T-SVD algorithm was proposed in
Ahmadi-Asl et al. (2024)
requiring
v\geq 2
passes. However, many real-world applications permit only a single pass (
v=1
), motivating the need for dedicated
single-pass algorithms.

To date, the only single-pass randomized T-SVD algorithms we are aware of are those in
Qi and Yu (2021)
. While seminal, they have notable limitations: they do not exploit the fast T-product and T-QR decomposition from
Lu et al. (2019)
, and they are based on earlier matrix algorithms
Tropp et al. (2017)
that have since been improved and stabilized
Bjarkason (2019)
. Another related line of work
Tarzanagh and Michailidis (2018)
constructs a CUR-like approximation by sampling lateral and frontal slices and using the pseudoinverse of their intersection as the core tensor (see Figure
1
). This approach suffers from poor conditioning and performs especially poorly when the number of selected frontal and lateral slices is equal—a weakness that we will demonstrate numerically.

Figure 1:
Illustration of randomized low-tubal-rank approximation via slice sampling
Tarzanagh and Michailidis (2018)
. Lateral slices (brown) and frontal slices (blue) are sampled from the original tensor to construct a low-rank approximation using their intersection as the core tensor. This approach requires only a single pass over the data but suffers from numerical instability when the number of selected lateral and frontal slices is equal.

To address these limitations, this paper proposes several novel robust single-pass algorithms for the T-SVD framework. Through extensive empirical evaluation against established baselines, we demonstrate that the proposed methods yield superior robustness and predictive performance. Furthermore, we showcase the practical utility of these algorithms in image super-resolution and object detection. To the best of our knowledge, this work represents the first application of single-pass randomized tensor decompositions to these computer vision tasks.

In addition, we study randomized fixed-precision algorithms for low-tubal-rank approximation. These are valuable when the tubal rank is unknown a priori: given an error tolerance, the algorithm must automatically select an appropriate rank and produce the corresponding approximation. Building on
Feng and Yu (2023)
;
Ding et al. (2020)
, we refine the fixed-precision method proposed in
Ahmadi-Asl (2023)
. Extensive simulations show that the refined algorithm is significantly faster and more computationally efficient.

Related Work

In this section, we review the existing literature on randomized algorithms for tensor decompositions, with a particular focus on single-pass and fixed-precision methods. We identify the key limitations of current approaches and position our contributions within this landscape.

2.1
Tensor Decompositions and the T-Product

Tensor decompositions have become essential tools for multidimensional data analysis. Tucker decomposition
Tucker (1966)
and CANDECOMP/PARAFAC (CP) decomposition
Carroll and Chang (1970)
are among the most widely used models. However, these decompositions do not fully exploit the spectral structure of third-order tensors. Tensor-train (TT) decomposition
Oseledets (2011)
offers a hierarchical representation but requires careful rank selection. More recently, the T-product framework introduced by Kilmer et al.
Kilmer and Martin (2011)
;
Kilmer et al. (2013)
provides an elegant algebraic foundation for third-order tensors, treating them as linear operators on matrices. This framework has enabled the development of tensor singular value decomposition (T-SVD)
Kilmer et al. (2013)
, which generalizes the matrix SVD while preserving many of its properties, including optimal low-rank approximation.

Despite its theoretical elegance, the computational cost of T-SVD is prohibitive for large-scale tensors, requiring
O(I_{1}I_{2}\min(I_{1},I_{2})I_{3})
operations. This limitation has motivated a rich line of research on randomized algorithms for T-SVD, which we review below.

2.2
Randomized Algorithms for T-SVD

Randomized algorithms have emerged as powerful tools for computing low-rank approximations of large matrices and tensors
Halko et al. (2011)
;
Martinsson and Tropp (2020)
. The core idea is to project the data onto a lower-dimensional subspace using random sketching matrices, dramatically reducing computational complexity while maintaining high accuracy with high probability.

2.2.1
Multi-Pass Randomized T-SVD

Zhang et al.
Zhang et al. (2018)
proposed the first randomized T-SVD algorithm (rT-SVD) based on the T-product framework. Their method computes a sketch
\underline{\mathbf{Y}}=\underline{\mathbf{X}}*\underline{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times K\times I_{3}}
, where
\underline{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{2}\times K\times I_{3}}
is a random tensor, and
\underline{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}
then constructs an orthonormal basis
\underline{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times K\times I_{3}}
via tensor QR (T-QR) (to be discussed), and finally computes
\underline{\mathbf{X}}\approx\underline{\mathbf{Q}}*(\underline{\mathbf{Q}}^{T}*\underline{\mathbf{X}})
. This algorithm requires two passes over the data tensor. For a power iteration
q
, we need to compute
(\underline{\mathbf{X}}*\underline{\mathbf{X}}^{T})*\underline{\mathbf{X}}*\underline{\Omega}
, which needs
2q+2
passes. this framework was extended in
Ahmadi-Asl et al. (2024)
to support an arbitrary number of passes
v\geq 2
, offering a flexible trade-off between accuracy and computational cost.

2.2.2
Single-Pass Randomized T-SVD

Single-pass algorithms are crucial when data cannot be stored in memory or revisited (e.g., streaming data). The first single-pass randomized T-SVD algorithms were introduced by Qi and Yu
Qi and Yu (2021)
. Their method computes two sketches:
\underline{\mathbf{Y}}=\underline{\mathbf{X}}*\underline{\Omega}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times K\times I_{3}}
and
\underline{\mathbf{W}}=\underline{\Omega}_{2}*\underline{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}
, where
\underline{\Omega}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{2}\times K\times I_{3}}
and
\underline{\Omega}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times I_{1}\times I_{3}}
, then approximates
\underline{\mathbf{X}}\approx\underline{\mathbf{Q}}*(\underline{\Omega}_{2}*\underline{\mathbf{Q}})^{\dagger}*\underline{\mathbf{W}}
, where
\underline{\mathbf{Q}}
is an orthonormal basis for
\underline{\mathbf{Y}}
. This approach generalizes the matrix single-pass algorithm of Tropp et al.
Tropp et al. (2017)
.

Independently, Tarzanagh and Michailidis
Tarzanagh and Michailidis (2018)
proposed a cross-tensor approximation (TCUR) method that samples lateral and horizontal slices and uses their intersection as the core tensor. While this method requires only one pass, it suffers from the limitation that the selected slices may not effectively capture the dominant singular subspaces. Similar TCUR methods were proposed in
Ahmadi-Asl et al. (2024)
.

2.3
Limitations of Existing Single-Pass Methods

Despite their pioneering nature, existing single-pass T-SVD algorithms have several critical limitations that have not been adequately addressed in the literature:

1.

Conditioning issues when
L=K
: As shown in Bjarkason’s work on matrix algorithms
Bjarkason (2019)
, single-pass methods become numerically unstable when the sketch sizes satisfy
L=K
due to ill-conditioned least-squares problems. This issue has been largely ignored in the tensor literature. Both Qi and Yu
Qi and Yu (2021)
and Tarzanagh and Michailidis
Tarzanagh and Michailidis (2018)
do not report empirical instability and do not provide a principled solution for this issue. The experiments confirm that when
L=K
, baseline methods produce PSNR values as low as
9.02
dB (see the experimental results).

2.

Lack of regularization mechanisms:
Existing methods rely on T-QR decomposition for orthonormalization, which does not provide regularization. When sketches are ill-conditioned, the computed pseudoinverse
(\underline{\Omega}_{2}*\underline{\mathbf{Q}})^{\dagger}
becomes numerically unreliable.

3.

No theoretical guarantees for the tensor case:
While matrix single-pass algorithms have rigorous error bounds
Tropp et al. (2017)
;
Bjarkason (2019)
, these guarantees have not been fully extended to the T-product tensor setting. The proofs in
Qi and Yu (2021)
rely on slice-wise arguments that do not account for the coupling between frontal slices in the Fourier domain.

4.

No adaptation for fixed-precision setting:
Existing single-pass methods require the target rank
R
as an input. In many real-world applications, the optimal rank is unknown a priori, necessitating fixed-precision algorithms that automatically determine the rank from an error tolerance.

2.4
Recent Developments in Randomized Tensor Decompositions

Several recent works have advanced randomized tensor computations, though none address the specific limitations identified above.

Che and Wei
Che and Wei (2019)
proposed randomized algorithms for TT decomposition. However, these methods focus on higher-order tensors and do not leverage the T-product structure.

For fixed-precision low-rank approximation, Yu et al.
Yu et al. (2018)
proposed adaptive randomized algorithms for matrices that automatically determine the rank from an error tolerance. This idea was extended to tensors via the T-product in
Ahmadi-Asl (2023)
, introducing Algorithm
11
. Feng and Yu
Feng and Yu (2023)
recently improved the matrix fixed-precision algorithm by replacing the QR decomposition with the LU decomposition and allowing an odd number of passes. However, their tensor extensions remain unexplored.

Zeng et al.
Zeng et al. (2025)
proposed randomized tensor sketch methods for online network security monitoring, demonstrating the practical value of pass-efficient algorithms. An incremental algorithm is proposed in
Abdelgawad et al. (2025)
for computing the TSVD of streaming tensors. Chen et al.
Chen et al. (2026)
developed rank-revealing Bayesian tensor completion methods, highlighting the ongoing need for adaptive rank estimation.

2.6
Positioning of Our Work

This work builds upon but substantially extends the existing literature in the following ways:

1.

Unlike
Qi and Yu (2021)
and
Tarzanagh and Michailidis (2018)
:
We provide a principled regularization mechanism (truncated T-SVD with parameter
H
) that eliminates the instability when
L=K
. We address these contributions theoretically and numerically, with an ablation study quantifying the resulting improvements.

2.

Unlike
Ahmadi-Asl (2023)
:
In one of our proposed algorithms, we reduce the number of passes from
2q+2
to any arbitrary
q
, making the method more practical for streaming applications. We also replace T-QR with T-LU for better parallelization.

3.

Unlike all prior work
We are the first to apply single-pass randomized T-SVD to tensor completion and image super-resolution, demonstrating practical utility beyond compression.

4.

Theoretical contribution:
We provide the first error bounds for regularized single-pass T-SVD that explicitly account for the conditioning improvement via truncation.

In summary, while our algorithms are inspired by existing matrix methods
Bjarkason (2019)
;
Ding et al. (2020)
;
Feng and Yu (2023)
, their extension to the T-product tensor setting is non-trivial due to the coupling between frontal slices in the Fourier domain. Our theoretical analysis, regularization strategy, and empirical validation collectively represent a significant advancement over the state of the art.

{Definition}

[f-diagonal tensor]
If all frontal slices of a tensor are diagonal, then the tensor is called an f-diagonal tensor.

[Random tensor]
A tensor

{Lemma}

Let
\underline{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times I_{1}\times n_{3}}
be a third-order tensor, and let
\underline{\mathbf{B}}^{T}
denote its transpose under the t-product. Define
\underline{\mathbf{G}}=\underline{\mathbf{B}}^{T}*\underline{\mathbf{B}}
, where
*
denotes the t-product (circular convolution in the third dimension). Then the following identity holds:

\|\underline{\mathbf{B}}\|_{F}^{2}=\operatorname{trace}\bigl(\underline{\mathbf{G}}(:,:,1)\bigr),

where
\underline{\mathbf{G}}(:,:,1)
is the first frontal slice of
\underline{\mathbf{G}}
.

{proof}

Let
\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{B}}}
denote the tensor obtained by taking the discrete Fourier transform along the third dimension of
\underline{\mathbf{B}}
. Under the t-product, circular convolution in the spatial domain becomes pointwise matrix multiplication in the Fourier domain. Specifically, for each frequency
k=1,2,\dots,I_{3}
,

\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{G}}}(:,:,k)=\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{B}}^{T}}(:,:,k)\,\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{B}}}(:,:,k).

By the definition of tensor transpose,

\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{B}}^{T}}(:,:,k)=\bigl(\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{B}}}(:,:,k)\bigr)^{H},

where
H
denotes the conjugate transpose. Therefore,

\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{G}}}(:,:,k)=\bigl(\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{B}}}(:,:,k)\bigr)^{H}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{B}}}(:,:,k).

Now, consider the first frontal slice
\underline{\mathbf{G}}(:,:,1)
. By the inverse DFT formula,

\underline{\mathbf{G}}(:,:,1)=\frac{1}{I_{3}}\sum_{k=1}^{I_{3}}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{G}}}(:,:,k).

Taking the trace,

\operatorname{trace}\bigl(\underline{\mathbf{G}}(:,:,1)\bigr)=\frac{1}{I_{3}}\sum_{k=1}^{I_{3}}\operatorname{trace}\left(\bigl(\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{B}}}(:,:,k)\bigr)^{H}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{B}}}(:,:,k)\right).

For any matrix
\mathbf{M}
,
\operatorname{trace}(\mathbf{M}^{H}\mathbf{M})=\|\mathbf{M}\|_{F}^{2}
. Hence,

\operatorname{trace}\bigl(\underline{\mathbf{G}}(:,:,1)\bigr)=\frac{1}{I_{3}}\sum_{k=1}^{I_{3}}\|\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{B}}}(:,:,k)\|_{F}^{2}.

By Parseval’s theorem (unitary DFT),

\|\underline{\mathbf{B}}\|_{F}^{2}=\frac{1}{I_{3}}\sum_{k=1}^{I_{3}}\|\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{B}}}(:,:,k)\|_{F}^{2}.

Therefore,

\operatorname{trace}\bigl(\underline{\mathbf{G}}(:,:,1)\bigr)=\|\underline{\mathbf{B}}\|_{F}^{2}.

Thus the lemma is proved.

{Lemma}

[Cyclic invariance of the trace of the first frontal slice]

Let
\underline{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}
and

\underline{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{2}\times I_{1}\times I_{3}}
be two conformable tensors.
Then

{\rm trace}\!\left((\underline{\mathbf{A}}*\underline{\mathbf{B}})^{(1)}\right)={\rm trace}\!\left((\underline{\mathbf{B}}*\underline{\mathbf{A}})^{(1)}\right),

(2)

where
*
denotes the t-product and
(\cdot)^{(1)}
denotes the first frontal slice.

{proof}

Let

\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{A}}}={\rm fft}(\underline{\mathbf{A}},[],3),\qquad\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{B}}}={\rm fft}(\underline{\mathbf{B}},[],3),

and denote their frontal slices by

\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{A}}}^{(k)}
and
\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{B}}}^{(k)}
,

k=1,2,\ldots,I_{3}
. By the definition of the t-product,

\widehat{(\underline{\mathbf{A}}*\underline{\mathbf{B}})}^{(k)}=\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{A}}}^{(k)}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{B}}}^{(k)},\qquad k=1,2,\ldots,I_{3}.

Since the first frontal slice is obtained from the inverse discrete Fourier transform,

(\underline{\mathbf{A}}*\underline{\mathbf{B}})^{(1)}=\frac{1}{I_{3}}\sum_{k=1}^{I_{3}}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{A}}}^{(k)}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{B}}}^{(k)}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle{\rm trace}\!\left((\underline{\mathbf{A}}*\underline{\mathbf{B}})^{(1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{I_{3}}\sum_{k=1}^{I_{3}}{\rm trace}\!\left(\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{A}}}^{(k)}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{B}}}^{(k)}\right).

Using the cyclic invariance of the matrix trace,

{\rm trace}(XY)={\rm trace}(YX),

for every conformable pair of matrices
X
and
Y
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\rm trace}\!\left((\underline{\mathbf{A}}*\underline{\mathbf{B}})^{(1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{I_{3}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{3}}{\rm trace}\!\left(\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{B}}}^{(k)}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{A}}}^{(k)}\right)

\displaystyle={\rm trace}\!\left((\underline{\mathbf{B}}*\underline{\mathbf{A}})^{(1)}\right),

which proves (
2
).

{Remark}

The above result extends immediately to any finite number of conformable tensors.
Indeed, if
\underline{\mathbf{A}}_{1},\underline{\mathbf{A}}_{2},\ldots,\underline{\mathbf{A}}_{m},
are conformable for the t-product, then

{\rm trace}\!\left((\underline{\mathbf{A}}_{1}*\underline{\mathbf{A}}_{2}*\cdots*\underline{\mathbf{A}}_{m})^{(1)}\right)={\rm trace}\!\left((\underline{\mathbf{A}}_{2}*\cdots*\underline{\mathbf{A}}_{m}*\underline{\mathbf{A}}_{1})^{(1)}\right),

that is, the trace of the first frontal slice is invariant under any cyclic permutation of the tensor factors. The proof follows by applying the matrix trace identity

{\rm trace}(X_{1}X_{2}\cdots X_{m})={\rm trace}(X_{2}\cdots X_{m}X_{1}),

to each frontal slice in the Fourier domain and then using the inverse Fourier transform.

The T-product provides a straightforward way to generalize standard matrix decompositions—such as QR, LU, eigenvalue decomposition, and SVD—to tensors. For a tensor

Notably, the T-SVD provides a useful decomposition by representing a tensor as a T-product of three tensors. The first and last tensors are orthogonal, while the middle tensor is f-diagonal. Let
\underline{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}
be a tensor; then the T-SVD of this tensor is given by

\displaystyle\underline{\mathbf{X}}=\underline{\mathbf{U}}*\underline{\mathbf{S}}*\underline{\mathbf{V}}^{T},

(5)

where
\underline{\mathbf{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times I_{1}\times I_{3}}

\underline{\mathbf{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}

\underline{\mathbf{S}}
in the tensor SVD is not necessarily symmetric. Here,
\underline{\mathbf{U}}
and
\underline{\mathbf{V}}
are called the left and right singular tensors. The number of non-zero diagonal tubes of
\underline{\mathbf{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}
is called the tubal rank. The formal definition of the tubal rank is as follows.

{Definition}

The tubal rank of a tensor
\underline{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}
is defined as the maximum rank among all frontal slices in the Fourier domain:

\mathrm{rank}_{t}(\underline{\mathbf{X}})=\max_{i=1,2,\ldots,I_{3}}\mathrm{rank}(\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}(:,:,i)).

(6)

I_{3}
independent matrix SVD problems. The truncated T-SVD is defined by truncating the tensors
\underline{\mathbf{U}}
,
\underline{\mathbf{S}}
, and
\underline{\mathbf{V}}

\underline{\mathbf{X}}

\displaystyle\underline{\mathbf{X}}\approx\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{R}*\underline{\mathbf{S}}_{R}*\underline{\mathbf{V}}_{R}^{T},

(7)

where
\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{R}=\underline{\mathbf{U}}(:,1:R,:)\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times R\times I_{3}}
,
\underline{\mathbf{V}}_{R}=\underline{\mathbf{V}}(:,1:R,:)\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}
, and
\underline{\mathbf{S}}_{R}=\underline{\mathbf{S}}(1:R,1:R,:)\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times R\times I_{3}}

\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{R}
and
\underline{\mathbf{V}}_{R}
are the left and right leading singular tensors. Figure

5
Proposed Randomized Single-Pass Algorithms

Randomized single-pass algorithms process a stream of data in a single pass and do not need to store the entire dataset in memory, which is crucial for large datasets that cannot fit in memory. They play a vital role in processing massive datasets and providing efficient solutions in various applications. Their ability to process data in a single pass, reduce memory usage, and handle worst-case scenarios makes them essential for real-time data analysis, data streaming, and machine learning tasks. For these reasons, developing single-pass and, more generally, pass-efficient algorithms for fast low-rank tensor approximation has been a hot topic over the last decade.

Let us first briefly introduce the standard randomized algorithm with oversampling and power iteration for computing a low-tubal-rank approximation of a tensor, as it will be needed later in this paper. Algorithm

outlines the basic randomized T-SVD with power iteration for computing a low-rank approximation of a third-order tensor
\underline{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}
. The algorithm first generates a random tensor
\underline{\bm{\Omega}}
and forms the sample tensor
\underline{\mathbf{X}}*\underline{\bm{\Omega}}
, whose range approximates that of
\underline{\mathbf{X}}
. An orthonormal basis
\underline{\mathbf{Q}}
for this sample tensor is then computed via the T-QR decomposition. To improve the accuracy of the approximation, the power iteration scheme is applied
p
times, alternating between the tensors
\underline{\mathbf{X}}^{T}
and
\underline{\mathbf{X}}
, which enhances the decay of the singular values. Subsequently, the reduced tensor
\underline{\mathbf{B}}=\underline{\mathbf{Q}}^{T}*\underline{\mathbf{X}}
is formed, and its economic T-SVD is computed. Finally, the rank-
k
factors
\underline{\mathbf{U}}
,
\underline{\mathbf{S}}
, and
\underline{\mathbf{V}}
are obtained by truncating the resulting T-SVD components to the first
k
frontal slices.

5.1
Existing Single-Pass Methods and Their Limitations

In this paper, we focus on the T-SVD and propose new single-pass algorithms. To the best of our knowledge, the only single-pass algorithms proposed for low-tubal-rank approximation of tensors were proposed in
Tarzanagh and Michailidis (2018)
;
Qi and Yu (2021)
. Let us discuss these single-pass
algorithms below.

5.2
Cross-Tensor Approximation

The proposed approach in
Tarzanagh and Michailidis (2018)
is based on cross-tensor approximation. Specifically, for a given tensor
\underline{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}
, a low-tubal-rank approximation is computed by sampling some lateral and horizontal slices of the original data tensor
\underline{\mathbf{X}}
, as illustrated in Figure
1
. Let
\underline{\mathbf{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times L\times I_{3}}
and
\underline{\mathbf{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}
be selected lateral and horizontal slices of the data tensor
\underline{\mathbf{X}}
. Note that the lateral and frontal slices do not necessarily need to have the same size. . Then, a low-tubal-rank approximation is computed as follows:

\displaystyle\underline{\mathbf{X}}\approx\underline{\mathbf{C}}*\underline{\mathbf{U}}*\underline{\mathbf{R}},

(9)

where
\underline{\mathbf{U}}=\underline{\mathbf{C}}^{\dagger}*\underline{\mathbf{X}}*\underline{\mathbf{R}}^{\dagger}
. The middle tensor
\underline{\mathbf{U}}
is the optimal candidate as it minimizes the residual
\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}-\underline{\mathbf{C}}*\underline{\mathbf{U}}*\underline{\mathbf{R}}\|_{F}
. This is known as cross-tensor approximation or tensor CUR (TCUR) approximation. The tensor CY (TCY) and tensor YR (TYR) approximations are defined as
\underline{\mathbf{X}}\approx\underline{\mathbf{C}}*\underline{\mathbf{Y}}
and
\underline{\mathbf{X}}\approx\underline{\mathbf{Y}}*\underline{\mathbf{R}}
for given lateral and horizontal slices
\underline{\mathbf{C}}

and
\underline{\mathbf{R}}
, respectively.

Notably, this approach requires only one pass over the data to compute the middle tensor
\underline{\mathbf{U}}
. Our experimental results confirm that this method is stable with respect to the number of selected lateral and horizontal slices. However, it has three main drawbacks.

First, its accuracy is generally lower than that of other techniques because the factor tensors
\underline{\mathbf{C}}
and
\underline{\mathbf{R}}
are not very accurate estimates of the left and right singular tensors. Second, it typically requires a higher tubal rank to achieve the desired accuracy. Third, the computation of
\underline{\mathbf{C}}^{\dagger}*\underline{\mathbf{X}}*\underline{\mathbf{R}}^{\dagger}
is more expensive than multiplication with random tensors, as one can use structured random tensors to accelerate the computations.

In
Tarzanagh and Michailidis (2018)
, it is suggested to use
\underline{\mathbf{U}}
as the intersection tensor of some selected lateral and horizontal slices, as shown in Figure

{Theorem}

Let
\underline{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}
be a tensor, and let
\underline{\mathbf{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times L\times I_{3}}
and
\underline{\mathbf{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}
be selected lateral and horizontal slices (not necessarily optimal). Let
\underline{\mathbf{U}}=\underline{\mathbf{C}}^{\dagger}*\underline{\mathbf{X}}*\underline{\mathbf{R}}^{\dagger}
. Then, the approximation error satisfies

\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}-\underline{\mathbf{C}}*\underline{\mathbf{U}}*\underline{\mathbf{R}}\|_{F}\leq\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}-P_{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}*\underline{\mathbf{X}}\|_{F}+\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}-\underline{\mathbf{X}}*P_{\underline{\mathbf{R}}}\|_{F},

where
P_{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}=\underline{\mathbf{C}}*\underline{\mathbf{C}}^{{\dagger}}*\underline{\mathbf{X}}
and
P_{\underline{\mathbf{R}}}=\underline{\mathbf{X}}*\underline{\mathbf{R}}^{{\dagger}}*\underline{\mathbf{R}}
are orthogonal projections onto the range of
\underline{\mathbf{C}}
and
\underline{\mathbf{R}}^{T}
, respectively.

{proof}

Let
\underline{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}
be the original data tensor,
\underline{\mathbf{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times L\times I_{3}}
be the selected lateral slices, and
\underline{\mathbf{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}
be the selected horizontal slices. Denote by
\underline{\mathbf{U}}=\underline{\mathbf{C}}^{\dagger}*\underline{\mathbf{X}}*\underline{\mathbf{R}}^{\dagger}
the middle tensor, which minimizes the residual
\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}-\underline{\mathbf{C}}*\underline{\mathbf{U}}*\underline{\mathbf{R}}\|_{F}
for fixed
\underline{\mathbf{C}}
and
\underline{\mathbf{R}}
.

Apply the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) along the third mode of all tensors. For each frontal slice
i=1,2,\dots,I_{3}
in the Fourier domain, we have matrices:

\displaystyle\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}=\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}(:,:,i),\,\,\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}^{(i)}=\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}(:,:,i),\,\,\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{R}}}^{(i)}=\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{R}}}(:,:,i).

The T-product in the spatial domain corresponds to the matrix multiplication in the Fourier domain, so for each slice
i
:

\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{U}}}^{(i)}=(\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}^{(i)})^{\dagger}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}(\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{R}}}^{(i)})^{\dagger},

(10)

and the approximation becomes
\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}^{(i)}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{U}}}^{(i)}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{R}}}^{(i)}
.

For each frontal slice
i
, consider the matrix CUR decomposition. Let
P_{\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}^{(i)}}=\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}^{(i)}(\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}^{(i)})^{\dagger}
be the orthogonal projection onto the column space of
\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}^{(i)}
, and let
P_{\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{R}}}^{(i)}}=(\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{R}}}^{(i)})^{\dagger}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{R}}}^{(i)}
be the orthogonal projection onto the row space of
\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{R}}}^{(i)}
. Then,

\displaystyle\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}-\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}^{(i)}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{U}}}^{(i)}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{R}}}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}-P_{\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}^{(i)}}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}P_{\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{R}}}^{(i)}}

\displaystyle=(\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}-P_{\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}^{(i)}}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)})

\displaystyle+P_{\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}^{(i)}}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}(I-P_{\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{R}}}^{(i)}}).

(11)

Taking the Frobenius norm and applying the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}-\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}^{(i)}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{U}}}^{(i)}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{R}}}^{(i)}\|_{F}\leq\|\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}-P_{\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}^{(i)}}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}\|_{F}

(12)

\displaystyle+\|P_{\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}^{(i)}}(\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}-\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}P_{\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{R}}}^{(i)}})\|_{F}.

(13)

Since
P_{\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}^{(i)}}
is an orthogonal projection,
\|P_{\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}^{(i)}}\|_{2}\leq 1
, so,

\|P_{\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}^{(i)}}(\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}-\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}P_{\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{R}}}^{(i)}})\|_{F}\leq\|\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}-\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}P_{\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{R}}}^{(i)}}\|_{F}.

(14)

Now, let

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}-P_{\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}^{(i)}}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}\|_{F}=a_{i},\quad\quad\|\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}-\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}P_{\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{R}}}^{(i)}}\|_{F}=b_{i}.

(15)

Thus, for each slice
i
,

\|\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}-\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}^{(i)}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{U}}}^{(i)}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{R}}}^{(i)}\|_{F}\leq a_{i}+b_{i}.

(16)

The tensor Frobenius norm in the spatial domain relates to the Frobenius norms in the Fourier domain by

\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}-\underline{\mathbf{C}}*\underline{\mathbf{U}}*\underline{\mathbf{R}}\|_{F}^{2}=\frac{1}{I_{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{I_{3}}\|\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}-\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}^{(i)}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{U}}}^{(i)}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{R}}}^{(i)}\|_{F}^{2}.

(17)

Applying the bound from Step 2,

\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}-\underline{\mathbf{C}}*\underline{\mathbf{U}}*\underline{\mathbf{R}}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{I_{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{I_{3}}(a_{i}+b_{i})^{2}.

(18)

Now define vectors
{\mathbf{a}}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{I_{3}})
and
{\mathbf{b}}=(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{I_{3}})
. The right-hand side can be written as

\frac{1}{I_{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{I_{3}}(a_{i}+b_{i})^{2}=\|{\mathbf{a}}+{\mathbf{b}}\|_{*}^{2},

(19)

where the norm is
\|\cdot\|_{*}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{I_{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{I_{3}}(\cdot)^{2}}
.

By the triangle inequality,

\|{\mathbf{a}}+{\mathbf{b}}\|_{*}\leq\|{\mathbf{a}}\|_{*}+\|{\mathbf{b}}\|_{*}.

(20)

But note that

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{a}}\|_{*}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{I_{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{I_{3}}a_{i}^{2}}=\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}-\underline{\mathbf{X}}_{L}\|_{F},

(21)

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{b}}\|_{*}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{I_{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{I_{3}}b_{i}^{2}}=\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}-\underline{\mathbf{X}}_{K}\|_{F}.

(22)

Therefore,

\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}-\underline{\mathbf{C}}*\underline{\mathbf{U}}*\underline{\mathbf{R}}\|_{F}\leq\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}-\underline{\mathbf{X}}_{L}\|_{F}+\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}-\underline{\mathbf{X}}_{K}\|_{F}.

(23)

This completes the proof.

{Remark}

Depending on the process of sampling slices either deterministically or randomly, different upper bounds can be derived for
\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}-P_{\underline{\mathbf{C}}}*\underline{\mathbf{X}}\|_{F}
and
\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}-\underline{\mathbf{X}}*P_{\underline{\mathbf{R}}}\|_{F}
.

5.3
Tensor Sketch Method

The single-pass method proposed in
Qi and Yu (2021)
generalizes the single-pass matrix algorithm
Tropp et al. (2017)
to the tensor setting. To be more precise, let
\underline{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}
be given. Then, in the first step, two sketches are computed from
\underline{\mathbf{X}}
by multiplying it by two random tensors
\underline{\Omega}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{2}\times(K+R)\times I_{3}}
and
\underline{\Omega}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{(L+R)\times I_{1}\times I_{3}},\,(K\leq L)
as follows:

\displaystyle\underline{\mathbf{Y}}=\underline{\mathbf{X}}*\underline{\Omega}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times(K+R)\times I_{3}},\quad\underline{\mathbf{W}}=\underline{\Omega}_{2}*\underline{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(L+R)\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}.

(24)

Suppose that
\underline{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times(K+R)\times I_{3}}
is an orthonormal basis for the range of the tensor
\underline{\mathbf{Y}}
, computed by the T-QR algorithm, and a low-tubal-rank approximation is computed as

\displaystyle\underline{\mathbf{X}}\approx\underline{\mathbf{Q}}*(\underline{\mathbf{Q}}^{T}*\underline{\mathbf{X}}).

(25)

Here,
L
and
K
are called sketch sizes, and
\underline{\mathbf{Y}}
or
\underline{\mathbf{W}}
are called sketches of the tensor
\underline{\mathbf{X}}
. However, since in (
24
), we passed the data tensor once, we should avoid computing
\underline{\mathbf{Q}}^{T}*\underline{\mathbf{X}}
in (
25
) again because it requires us to view the original data
\underline{\mathbf{X}}
one more time.
This can be estimated as follows

\displaystyle\underline{\mathbf{Q}}^{T}*\underline{\mathbf{X}}\approx(\underline{\Omega}_{2}*\underline{\mathbf{Q}})^{{\dagger}}*\underline{\mathbf{W}}.

(26)

So this single-pass or one-view method combines range and co-range sketches in a single pass over the data tensor
\underline{\mathbf{X}}
and then generates a low-tubal-rank estimate based on information included in the sketches.
This algorithm is summarized in Algorithm
7
. Consider a streaming setting where the data tensor
\underline{\mathbf{X}}
is never entirely kept in random-access memory but is instead given as a finite stream of linear updates.

\underline{\mathbf{X}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\underline{\mathbf{H}}_{n}.

Here, each
\underline{\mathbf{H}}_{n}
is deleted once it is used, and we can gradually sample from
\underline{\mathbf{X}}
as each innovation tensor
\underline{\mathbf{H}}_{n}
is provided as follows.

\underline{\mathbf{X}}*\underline{\Omega}_{1}=\sum_{i=n}^{N}\underline{\mathbf{H}}_{n}*\underline{\Omega}_{1},\quad\underline{\Omega}_{2}*\underline{\mathbf{X}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\underline{\Omega}_{2}*\underline{\mathbf{H}}_{n}.

This is an example where single-pass algorithms are applicable because each
\underline{\mathbf{H}}_{n}
could represent a sparse tensor containing a few elements of
\underline{\mathbf{X}}
.

5.4
The Conditioning Problem and Our Solution

As discussed in
Bjarkason (2019)
, the single-pass algorithms for matrices usually give unreliable approximations when
L=K
due to some instability issues in solving badly conditioned least-squares problems. We observed the same behavior with tensors and will report it in our simulations. According to (
26
), we need to solve the linear tensor equation
(\underline{\Omega}_{2}*\underline{\mathbf{Q}})*\underline{\mathbf{Y}}=\underline{\mathbf{W}}.

The coefficient tensor
\underline{\Omega}_{2}*\underline{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(L+R)\times(K+R)\times I_{3}}
has
L+R
and
K+R
horizontal and lateral slices, respectively. One way to avoid the badly conditioned coefficient tensor is to consider
L>K
and solve an overdetermined and better-conditioned problem. However, this requires fine-tuning the parameters
L
and
K
to obtain an acceptable approximation, which imposes additional costs of carefully identifying the best values; otherwise, there is a risk of losing accuracy. On the other hand, as highlighted in
Bjarkason (2019)
, another reason to address the disadvantages of selecting
L=K
is that
K
may pose a processing bottleneck in some applications. Given computational limitations, it would be tempting to set
L=K
to obtain as much information as possible. Nevertheless, the requirement to retain both the sketching and random-sample matrices imposes a memory constraint on the one-view approach.
Finally, as noted in
Tropp et al. (2017)
, prior knowledge of matrix singular values can guide the selection of
L
and
K
, but such information is often unavailable in practice, reducing the utility of these suggestions.

It is worth emphasizing that in all our proposed single-pass algorithms, the sketches
\underline{\mathbf{Y}}
and
\underline{\mathbf{W}}
are significantly smaller than the original tensor. Computing their left singular tensor is therefore inexpensive compared to obtaining an orthonormal basis for the range sketch. This is particularly relevant because, in single-pass methods, the dominant computational cost typically arises from multiplying
\underline{\mathbf{X}}
with random tensors to generate range and
co-range sketches.

5.5
Proposed Stabilized Single-Pass Algorithms

To overcome the drawback of unstable tensor approximation mentioned previously, more stabilized and improved algorithms (see Algorithms 7 and 8 in
Bjarkason (2019)
) were developed in
Bjarkason (2019)
for matrices. Motivated by their efficiency, we generalized them to the tensor case, which is summarized in Algorithms
8
and
9
. The principal idea is to use the truncated T-SVD for getting an orthonormal basis for the full range of the sketches
\underline{\mathbf{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times(K+R)\times I_{3}}
or
\underline{\mathbf{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L+R\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}
instead of applying the T-QR decomposition. Let us explain this in detail. Instead of considering
\underline{\mathbf{Q}}
as an orthonormal basis for
\underline{\mathbf{Y}}
, we compute
[\underline{\mathbf{Q}},\sim,\sim]=\mathrm{Truncated\,T\operatorname{-}SVD}(\underline{\mathbf{Y}},H+R)
, where
0\leq H\leq K
. This means that
\underline{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times(H+R)\times I_{3}}
contains the leading left singular tensors of
\underline{\mathbf{Y}}
, and if
H<K
, then the coefficient tensor
\underline{\Omega}_{2}*\underline{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(L+R)\times(H+R)\times I_{3}}
should provide a more conditioned problem, even for
L=K.

{Theorem}

Let
\underline{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}
be a tensor with tubal rank
R
. If the parameters
L
,
K
, and
H
satisfy
L\geq K>H\geq R+P
for some oversampling parameter
P\geq 2
, then Algorithms
8
and
9
produce an
\epsilon
-stable approximation whenever their corresponding matrix variants do. Specifically,

\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}-\underline{\mathbf{U}}*\underline{\mathbf{S}}*\underline{\mathbf{V}}^{T}\|_{F}\leq(1+\epsilon)\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}-\underline{\mathbf{X}}_{R}\|_{F},

(27)

where
\epsilon
is a constant that depends on the tensor dimensions.

{proof}

Let
\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}
denote the Fourier transform of
\underline{\mathbf{X}}
along the third mode. In the Fourier domain, the T-SVD decouples into
I_{3}
independent matrix SVD problems. For each frontal slice
\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}=\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}(:,:,i)
,
i=1,2,\ldots,I_{3}
, we define the corresponding sketches:

\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{Y}}}^{(i)}=\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}\widehat{\underline{\Omega}}_{1}^{(i)},\quad\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{W}}}^{(i)}=\widehat{\underline{\Omega}}_{2}^{(i)}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)},

where
\widehat{\underline{\Omega}}_{1}^{(i)}
and
\widehat{\underline{\Omega}}_{2}^{(i)}
are the corresponding frontal slices of the random tensors in the Fourier domain. Since the entries of the random tensors in the spatial domain are standard Gaussian, their Fourier transforms remain Gaussian (up to a scaling factor of
1/\sqrt{I_{3}}
) due to the unitary nature of the DFT. Therefore, the conditions of the matrix single-pass algorithms from
Bjarkason (2019)
apply independently to each frontal slice.

For each slice
i
, we apply the matrix single-pass analysis from
Bjarkason (2019)
. For each frontal slice
i
, let
\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{U}}}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}\times R}
,
\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{S}}}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{C}^{R\times R}
, and
\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{V}}}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{2}\times R}
be the factors obtained by applying the matrix version of Algorithms
8
and
9
to the slice
\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}
with the same parameters
L
,
K
,
H
, and
R
. According to our assumption, the corresponding matrix algorithms provide
\epsilon_{i}
-stable approximations; for each frontal slice, we have

\|\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}-\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{U}}}^{(i)}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{S}}}^{(i)}(\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{V}}}^{(i)})^{*}\|_{F}\leq(1+\epsilon_{i})\|\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}-\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}_{R}^{(i)}\|_{F},

where
\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}_{R}^{(i)}
is the best rank-
R
approximation of
\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}
. Considering

\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}-\underline{\mathbf{U}}*\underline{\mathbf{S}}*\underline{\mathbf{V}}^{*}\|_{F}^{2}=\frac{1}{I_{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{I_{3}}\|\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}-\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{U}}}^{(i)}\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{S}}}^{(i)}(\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{V}}}^{(i)})^{*}\|_{F}^{2},

and applying the slice-wise bound, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}-\underline{\mathbf{U}}*\underline{\mathbf{S}}*\underline{\mathbf{V}}^{T}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{I_{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{I_{3}}(1+\epsilon_{i})^{2}\|\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}-\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}_{R}^{(i)}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon)^{2}\frac{1}{I_{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{I_{3}}\|\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}^{(i)}-\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{X}}}_{R}^{(i)}\|_{F}^{2},

\displaystyle=(1+\epsilon)^{2}\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}-\underline{\mathbf{X}}_{R}\|_{F}^{2},

where
\epsilon=\max_{i}\epsilon_{i}
. Taking square roots completes the proof.

Conditioning Improvement.

The truncation parameter
H
acts as a regularization parameter. Since
\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{Q}}}^{(i)}
contains only the leading
H
singular vectors of
\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{Y}}}^{(i)}
, the effective condition number of

is controlled by
\sigma_{H}(\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{Y}}}^{(i)})/\sigma_{1}(\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{Y}}}^{(i)})

when

. This improved conditioning ensures numerical stability even when

.

Proposed Randomized Fixed-Precision Algorithms

Randomized fixed-precision algorithms for tensors automatically estimate an appropriate/optimal tensor rank and the corresponding low-rank tensor approximation. These algorithms are crucial when we have no knowledge of the data’s rank or when estimating it is difficult. In such scenarios, one needs to estimate the optimal rank and the corresponding low-rank approximation for a prescribed error bound. To proceed, we formally define a fixed-precision approximation and the adaptive rank estimation process.

{Definition}

A randomized fixed-precision algorithm for tensor approximation takes as input a tensor
\underline{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}
and an error tolerance
\epsilon>0
and outputs a tubal rank
R
and factors
\underline{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times R\times I_{3}}
,
\underline{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}
such that

\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}-\underline{\mathbf{Q}}*\underline{\mathbf{B}}\|_{F}\leq\epsilon\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}\|_{F},

(31)

with high probability.

{Definition}

The adaptive tubal rank
R(\epsilon)
for a given precision
\epsilon
is defined as

R(\epsilon)=\min\left\{R:\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}-\underline{\mathbf{X}}_{R}\|_{F}\leq\epsilon\|\underline{\mathbf{X}}\|_{F}\right\},

(32)

where
\underline{\mathbf{X}}_{R}
is the best tubal rank-
R
approximation of
\underline{\mathbf{X}}
.

A randomized fixed-precision algorithm was developed
Ahmadi-Asl (2023)
, and simulations on synthetic and real-world data showed significant acceleration in computing the approximate truncated T-SVD. It generalized the matrix case
Yu et al. (2018)
to the tensor case. This algorithm is presented in Algorithm
11
. The algorithm requires oversampling, a power iteration, a tolerance, and a block size, and it gradually estimates the tubal rank and computes the corresponding low-rank tubal approximation. Lines 6–9 constitute the power-iteration stage for improving accuracy, especially when the singular values of the data tensor’s frontal slices do not decay quickly.

6.1
Algorithmic Improvements and Theoretical Analysis

However, recently, the matrix version of this algorithm was further improved in
Feng and Yu (2023)
and
Ding et al. (2020)
. Motivated by the interesting results reported in that paper, we enhance our previously randomized fixed-precision algorithm. We improve the algorithm proposed in
Ahmadi-Asl (2023)
in several ways. First, for a given power iteration, Algorithm
11
requires
2q+2
passes over the original data tensor. This means that for a given power iteration, we are forced to view the original data in an even number of passes. Clearly, this is a limitation, as we have shown in
Ahmadi-Asl et al. (2024)
that, for images or videos, three passes are usually sufficient to obtain satisfactory results. However, Algorithm
11
does not support three passes; for
q=2
, we will have four passes, and this additional pass can be expensive for very large-scale data tensors or when the underlying algorithm requires many iterations to converge. Indeed, we aim to improve Algorithm
11
, and our newly proposed method summarized in
Algorithm
12
addresses this problem. A given error bound can provide an estimation of the tubal rank and a low-tubal-rank approximation for any number of passes. Please note that in Algorithm
11
,
q
is the power iteration parameter while
q
is the number of passes in Algorithm
12
. Similar to
Ding et al. (2020)
, we do the following modifications to Algorithm
11
to be more pass-efficient:

•

Employing the T-EIG decomposition for computing the truncated T-SVD of
\underline{\mathbf{B}}
.

•

Ignoring one orthonormalization process in each round of the power iteration loop.

•

Replacing the T-LU decomposition instead of T-QR for the orthonormalization process in the power iteration (except the last round of the iteration).

•

Modifying the algorithm to allow an odd number of passes.

and
\underline{\mathbf{B}}
with an approximation accuracy equivalent to that of Algorithm

{Theorem}

Let
\underline{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3}}
and

\underline{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{2}\times k\times I_{3}}
be a random tensor,
\underline{\mathbf{Y}}=\underline{\mathbf{X}}*\underline{\Omega},
\underline{\mathbf{W}}=\underline{\mathbf{X}}^{T}*\underline{\mathbf{Y}}
, and the economic T-SVD of
\underline{\mathbf{Y}}
is
\underline{\mathbf{Y}}=\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{U}}}*\widehat{\underline{\bf S}}*\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{V}}}^{T}
. Setting

\displaystyle\underline{\mathbf{Q}}=\underline{\mathbf{Y}}*\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{V}}}*\widehat{\underline{\bf S}}^{-1},\,\,\underline{\mathbf{B}}=(\underline{\mathbf{W}}*\widehat{\underline{\bf V}}*\widehat{\underline{\bf S}}^{-1})^{T},

(36)

gives an approximation
\underline{\mathbf{Q}}*\underline{\mathbf{B}}
to
\underline{\mathbf{X}}
that has the same accuracy of the basic randomized Algorithm
5
(without power iteration or oversampling) and

||\underline{\mathbf{B}}||_{F}^{2}={\rm trace}(\underline{\mathbf{G}}^{(1)}),

where
\underline{\mathbf{G}}^{(1)}=\underline{\mathbf{G}}(:,:,1)
is the first frontal slice of
\underline{\mathbf{G}}=\underline{\mathbf{W}}*\underline{\mathbf{W}}^{T}*(\underline{\mathbf{Y}}^{T}*\underline{\mathbf{Y}})^{-1}.

{proof}

The proof of this theorem is very similar to that of Proposition 1 in
Feng and Yu (2023)
. Since
\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{U}}}
consists of the left singular tensors of
\underline{\mathbf{Y}}
, it likewise satisfies the orthonormalization condition
{\rm orth}(\underline{\mathbf{Y}})
. So we can considering the following identities:

\displaystyle\underline{\mathbf{Q}}={\rm orth}(\underline{\mathbf{X}}*\underline{\Omega})={\rm orth}(\underline{\mathbf{Y}})=\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{U}}}=\underline{\mathbf{Y}}*\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{V}}}*\widehat{\underline{\bf S}}^{-1}.

(37)

Then, substituting (
37
) in
\underline{\mathbf{B}}=\underline{\mathbf{Q}}^{T}*\underline{\mathbf{X}}
, we have

\displaystyle\underline{\mathbf{B}}\equiv\underline{\mathbf{Q}}^{T}*\underline{\mathbf{X}}=(\underline{\mathbf{W}}*\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{V}}}*\widehat{\underline{\bf S}}^{-1})^{T},

(38)

where
\underline{\mathbf{W}}=\underline{\mathbf{X}}*\underline{\mathbf{Y}}
.
Given that
\underline{\mathbf{Q}}
results from orthonormalizing
\underline{\mathbf{X}}*\underline{\Omega}

\underline{\mathbf{Q}}*\underline{\mathbf{B}}
delivers accuracy comparable to that of
\underline{\mathbf{X}}\approx\underline{\mathbf{Q}}*\underline{\mathbf{B}}
in Algorithm
5
, but without power iteration or oversampling.

Now, using the fact that
\|\underline{\mathbf{B}}\|_{F}^{2}={\rm trace}(\underline{\mathbf{G}}^{(1)})={\rm trace}(\underline{\mathbf{G}}(:,:,1))
, where
\underline{\mathbf{G}}^{(1)}
is the first frontal slice of
\underline{\mathbf{G}}=\underline{\mathbf{B}}^{T}*\underline{\mathbf{B}}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\underline{\mathbf{B}}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm trace}(\underline{\mathbf{G}}^{(1)})={\rm trace}((\widehat{\bf S}^{-1}*\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{V}}}^{T}*\underline{\mathbf{W}}^{T}*\underline{\mathbf{W}}*\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{V}}}*\widehat{\underline{\bf S}}^{-1})^{(1)})

\displaystyle{\rm trace}((\underline{\mathbf{W}}^{T}*\underline{\mathbf{W}}*\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{V}}}*\widehat{\underline{\bf S}}^{-1}*\widehat{\underline{\bf S}}^{-T}*\widehat{\underline{\mathbf{V}}}^{T})^{(1)})

\displaystyle{\rm trace}((\underline{\mathbf{W}}^{T}*\underline{\mathbf{W}}*(\underline{\mathbf{Y}}^{T}*\underline{\mathbf{Y}})^{-1})^{(1)}).

6.3
Practical Decision Framework for Algorithm Selection

The proposed algorithms address different operational scenarios in large-scale tensor computations. The choice of algorithm depends on three key factors: (1) data accessibility (can data be revisited?), (2) prior knowledge of the target rank, and (3) computational constraints (time, memory, and parallel resources). So the decision workflow can be presented as follows:

1.

Can the data be stored and revisited?

•

No (streaming data, limited memory): Use single-pass algorithms (Algorithms
8
,
9
, or
10
).

•

Yes: Proceed to step 2.

2.

Is the target tubal rank known a priori?

•

No: Use fixed-precision algorithms (Algorithms
12
or
13
).

•

Yes: Use multi-pass randomized T-SVD (rT-SVD) for higher accuracy or single-pass algorithms if data cannot be revisited.

3.

What are the computational constraints?

•

Limited time: Use T-LU based algorithms (Algorithm
12
) for 25–30% speedup.

•

Limited memory: Use single-pass algorithms (Algorithms
8
–
10
).

•

Parallel resources available: Use T-LU for better parallelization.

The typical use cases of the algorithms are described below:

•

Video surveillance (streaming): Algorithm
9
(single-pass, stabilized) is ideal for real-time video processing where data cannot be stored.

•

Image compression (batch processing): Algorithm
12
or
13
(fixed-precision) for optimal rank selection.

•

Scientific computing (known rank): Multi-pass rT-SVD for maximum accuracy, or Algorithm
8
for single-pass approximation.

In this section, we evaluate the performance of the proposed algorithms on both synthetic and real-world data tensors. All experiments were conducted using MATLAB version R2024a on a computer equipped with a 2.60 GHz Intel(R) Core(TM) i7-5600U processor and 8 GB of memory.

Conclusions

This paper introduced several new randomized algorithms for low-tubal-rank tensor approximation within the T-product framework, addressing key limitations of existing single-pass and fixed-precision methods.

We proposed three stabilized single-pass algorithms (Algorithms
8
–
10
) that overcome the numerical instability of prior methods when sketch sizes are equal (
L=K
). By introducing a truncation parameter
H
as a regularizer, our methods achieve
\epsilon
-stability (
Theorem 2
) and consistently produce accurate approximations where baselines fail catastrophically.

For fixed-precision approximation, two improved algorithms (Algorithms
12
and
13
) were provided that automatically estimate the tubal rank from an error tolerance. Unlike prior work, our methods support an arbitrary number of passes (including odd counts) and replace T-QR with T-LU decomposition, achieving
25
–
30\%
speedups with minimal accuracy loss.

Extensive experiments on synthetic data, image/video compression, image super-resolution, and deep learning tasks demonstrated that our proposed algorithms significantly outperform existing baselines in both accuracy and runtime. Notably, we are the first to apply single-pass randomized tensor decompositions to tensor completion and image super-resolution, achieving
2
–
3\times
speedups over deterministic methods while enabling correct object detection on structurally degraded images.

Future work includes extension to higher-order tensors, adaptive selection of the truncation parameter
H
, streaming implementations, and applications to video inpainting and 3D medical imaging.
