Unlocking the potential of purple wheat bran through superfine grinding: Effects on its physicochemical properties and fortified bread quality
Authors: Yingfen Jiang, Kegang Wu, Eu Ning Tan, Xiaojuan Xu, Kelly Hoi Man Cheng, Weibiao Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purple wheat bran (PWB), a novel ingredient rich in dietary fiber and anthocyanins linked to reduced risk of type-2 diabetes, remains underexplored in food applications. This study investigated the effects of superfine grinding on the physicochemical and functional properties of PWB and its incorporation into bread at 10%, 20%, and 30%. Coarse PWB (D50 = 732.2 μm) was ground into fine (D50 = 286.9 μm) and superfine PWB (D50 = 75.1 μm). Size reduction significantly increased extractable total anthocyanin content (TAC), with cyanidin-3-glucoside, peonidin-3-glucoside, and peonidin-3-rutinoside being major compounds, and alleviated bran-induced disruption on dough structure. Bread with 30% superfine PWB showed 40.07% and 233.59% higher TAC than breads with 30% coarse and 10% superfine PWB, respectively, and showed 220–622% greater antioxidant activity (ORAC, TPC, and ABTS) than the control. These findings demonstrate the potential of superfine PWB to deliver health benefits in bakery products without compromising their acceptability.

Introduction

Adequate dietary fiber intake is strongly associated with reduced risk of non-communicable diseases such as type 2 diabetes (T2D), colorectal cancer, and cardiovascular disorders (Reynolds et al., 2019). Meta-analyses indicate that consuming more than 25 g of fiber per day can lower T2D risk by 15–30%, with additional benefits observed beyond 30 g day−1 (Reynolds et al., 2019). Yet, global intake still falls well below these levels, as most individuals consume less than 20 g day−1 (Stephen et al., 2017). Cereal products provide significant daily fiber in many populations (van Rossum et al., 2011), enhancing the fiber content in staple foods such as bread therefore represents an effective strategy to address this deficit.

To bridge this nutritional gap, purple wheat bran (PWB) presents an opportunity to simultaneously tackle widespread dietary fiber deficiencies and boost antioxidant intake. Purple wheat is a naturally pigmented wheat cultivar distinguished by its vivid pericarp coloration derived from abundant anthocyanins (Abdel-Aal et al., 2018). Compared with red or blue varieties, it contains a greater diversity and abundance of anthocyanins, whose acylated forms confer enhanced color stability and antioxidant capacity (Abdel-Aal et al., 2006). Uniquely, this composition gives PWB a dual functional potential: the fiber fraction may modulate carbohydrate digestion and glycaemic response, while anthocyanins contribute antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities relevant to metabolic health (He & Giusti, 2010). Nevertheless, traditional fortification of wheat bread with bran or antioxidant-rich ingredients often results in technological compromises — coarse bran particles hinder gluten development, and phenolic compounds can weaken the dough by disrupting disulfide bonds (Lin et al., 2021; Sui et al., 2016). Surprisingly, our recent work with superfine purple whole wheat revealed a contrasting outcome: the bread quality was comparable to refined white bread, despite its higher levels of anthocyanins and dietary fiber (Jiang, Li, & Zhou, 2024; Jiang, Qi, & Zhou, 2024). This unexpected behavior suggests that purple wheat is not merely a simple combination of anthocyanins and normal wheat, but a distinct cereal system in which compositional and structural components may interact synergistically to stabilise the dough matrix.

Although purple wheat shows considerable potential as a functional ingredient, the application of its bran, where anthocyanins are concentrated, remains limited and its behavior during processing is not yet fully understood. Direct incorporation of PWB could further enhance the intake of this bioactive-rich fraction, as bran constitutes only about 14–16% of the wheat kernel (Stevenson et al., 2012). Superfine grinding has proven effective in many studies for enhancing bran incorporation by reducing particle size and improving dispersion, thereby increasing the release of bioactive compounds without severely compromising dough quality (Jin et al., 2021). Applying this approach to PWB may further elucidate how microstructural modification influences both technological and nutritional performance. Therefore, this study examined the effects of particle-size modification of purple wheat bran (coarse, fine, and superfine) and its incorporation at 10–30% levels on the physicochemical, microstructural, and quality characteristics of fortified bread. As such, this study provides new insights into the unique compositional and structural characteristics of PWB and its potential to produce high-quality staple foods naturally enriched in anthocyanins and dietary fiber with improved functionality.

Materials and methods

Materials

Purple wheat grains (Triticum aestivum L., variety: ND3753, planted in the North China Plain and harvested in 2021) were milled and sieved to obtain coarse purple wheat bran (∼ 5.3%). Refined wheat flour (Triticum aestivum L.) was provided by Prima, Singapore. Sugar (SIS, Singapore), salt (NTUC Fairprice, Singapore), shortening (Bake King, Gin Hin Lee, Singapore) and instant dry yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae, S.I. Lesaffre, Lille, France) were purchased from a local supermarket.

Total dietary fiber assay kit (K-TDFR) was obtained from Megazyme (Megazyme, Bray, Co. Wicklow, Ireland). Anthocyanin standards, including cyanidin 3-glucoside (C3G), cyanidin 3-rutinoside (C3R), peonidin 3-glucoside (P3G), and malvidin 3-glucoside (M3G), were purchased from Polyphenols Laboratories AS (Sandnes, Norway). Deionized water was utilized throughout the study, except for chromatographic preparation and analysis, where Milli-Q ultrapure water (≥18.2 MΩ) was employed.

Preparation of purple wheat bran in different particle size

Coarse purple wheat bran was further ground by using a superfine grinding pulverizer (OMS-400, Na Wei Te Technology Co. Ltd., Beijing, China) operating at 1.5–2.2 kW. The grinding process was conducted at a fixed speed of 960 rpm, utilizing frequencies of 25 Hz and 50 Hz to achieve fine and superfine particle sizes, respectively. The entire grinding procedure was conducted in compliance with the food sanitation standard. To prevent the degradation of heat-sensitive compounds, particularly anthocyanins, the milling temperature was maintained below 45 °C using an air-cooling system. According to their particle sizes, the prepared samples were defined as coarse, fine, and superfine purple wheat bran (i.e., CPWB, FPWB, SPWB). The median particle sizes (D50) of the CPWB, FPWB, and SPWB fractions were 732.2, 286.9, and 75.1 μm, respectively (detailed particle size distributions are discussed in Section 3.1.1). All samples were stored at −20 °C in the dark until further analysis.

Physicochemical properties of PWB and flour

Particle-specific properties

Particle size distribution, shape characteristics and transparency of PWB and flour samples were measured using a dynamic imaging particle size and shape analyzer (Camsizer X2, Microtrac MRB, Germany) equipped with an X-Jet module as described in a previous study (Jiang, Xie, & Zhou, 2024). The air pressure for sample dispersion was optimized to 80 kPa based on the consistency of three consecutive measurements during the preliminary study. The distribution of particle sizes was represented by D10, D50, and D90, which corresponded to the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the total volume. The span, calculated as (D90 - D10) / D50, was used to measure the width of the distribution. The specific surface area (As) was determined by calculating the ratio of the surface of all particles to the volume of all particles.

Shape characteristics, including symmetry (Symm), aspect ratio (b/l), and convexity (Conv), of flour and PWB samples were also evaluated in this study. Proximate analysis

The moisture content, ash, protein, fat and soluble/insoluble dietary fiber (SDF/IDF) of PWB and flour samples were conducted according to the AACC methods 44–15.02, AACC 08–01, AACC 46–30, AACC 30–25.01 and AACC 32–07.01, respectively (AACC, 2000). The non-fiber carbohydrate content was quantified by the difference between 100 and the sum of moisture, ash, protein, fat and total dietary fiber. All contents except moisture were expressed in dry basis.

Color measurement

According to the method of Jiang, Qi, et al. (2024), color measurement was performed using a spectrophotometer (CM-5, Konica Minolta Sensing Inc., Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a transparent petri dish (CM-A128). The hunter L*, a*, and b* values were utilized to quantify the color properties. The color difference (ΔE) between PWB and flour sample, which was set as a reference, was calculated using the eq. (7).(7)∆E=Lf−Ls2+af−as2+bf−bs2

Where L, a and b represent the L*, a*, and b* values, respectively; subscript “f” and “s” represent the flour and the PWB sample, respectively.

Microstructure analysis

The microstructure of PWB and flour samples was examined through the utilization of confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM), following the procedures outlined in the studies conducted by Jiang, Xie, et al. (2024), with minor adaptations.

For CLSM, the sample was subjected to staining using dyes in sequence: a 0.1% (w/w) ethanolic solution of fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) to highlight starch (λex = 488 nm), a 0.125% (w/w) aqueous solution of fluorescent brightener 28 (FB28) to indicate cellulosic material (λex = 405 nm), and a 0.1% (w/w) aqueous solution of FastGene Q-Stain (FG) to detect protein (λex = 633 nm). Images were captured using a confocal microscope (LSM710, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) at a resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels. The acquired images were further rendered using Zen LSM software (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany), with starch displayed in green, protein in red, and cellulosic material in blue.

Prior to SEM analysis, the PWB and flour samples were affixed onto a specimen stub and coated with a platinum film using a sputter coater (JFC-1600 Auto Fine Coater, JOEL, Japan) at 40 mA for 40 s. Subsequently, SEM images were captured using a Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (JSM-6701F, JEOL, Japan) operating at an acceleration voltage of 10 kV.

Antioxidants properties

Sample extraction

The extraction of antioxidants followed a slightly modified version of a previously reported method (Fogarasi et al., 2015). Specifically, 1 g of PWB/flour was mixed with 8 mL of acidified methanol (methanol: 1 M HCl = 85:15, v/v) and secured on an orbital shaker at 350 rpm for 1 h. After centrifuged at 8000 g for 15 min, the supernatant was collected, and the pellet was subjected to two additional extraction using 8 mL of 70% acetone (v/v) each time. Supernatants obtained from 3 extraction rounds were pooled and used for extractable antioxidant measurement. Method optimization was carried out through preliminary trials to ensure the complete extraction of available antioxidants.

Total phenolic content (TPC) analysis

TPC analysis was conducted following the method from Lin et al. (2021) with slight modification. A mixture of 20 μL of extract/gallic acid solution/ DI water and 100 μL of 10-fold diluted Folin-Ciocalteu's reagent was equilibrated for 5 min. Subsequently, 80 μL of 7.5% Na2CO3 was added to the mixture, which was then left to stand for 60 min. The absorbance at 760 nm was measured.

ABTS• + scavenging activity

ABTS• + scavenging activity was adopted following the method described by Lin et al. (2021). The ABTS stock solution was prepared at least 16 h in advance by dissolving 0.038 g of 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) and 0.007 g of potassium persulphate in 10 mL of DI water. On the day of experiment, the ABTS working solution was freshly prepared by diluting stock solution to reach an absorbance of 0.700 ± 0.002 at 734 nm. A mixture of 0.4 mL of extract/Trolox solution was mixed with 3 mL of working solution and incubated for 30 min, followed by measuring the absorbance at 734 nm.

Oxygen radical absorbance capacity (ORAC) assays

ORAC analysis was performed following a slightly modified method based on Ou et al. (2013). A mixture comprising 150 μL of 0.1 μM fluorescein solution and 25 μL of sample/Trolox solution/potassium phosphate buffer (75 mM, pH 7.4) was added into each well of a 96-well microplate, which was sent into a plate reader (PowerWave XS2, Bio-Tek Instruments Inc., Vermont, USA) maintained at 37 °C. Subsequently, 25 μL of 2,20-Azobis(2-amidinopropane) dihydrochloride (AAPH) solution was automatically pipetted into each well by the plate reader. Fluorescence measurements were taken for every 2 min over a duration of 2 h, with an excitation and an emission wavelength set at 485 nm and 530 nm, respectively. The results were calculated by determining the net area under the curve (AUC) for sample/standard and subtracting the corresponding blank AUC.

Total anthocyanin content and the anthocyanin profile

Sample extraction

The method for extracting anthocyanins from PWB samples was based on a modified procedure originally described by Hosseinian et al. (2008). Briefly, 1 g of PWB sample underwent two extraction cycles, using 20 mL of acidified methanol (methanol: 1 M HCl = 85:15, v/v) per extraction. Each extraction involved shaking the mixture at 350 rpm for 1 h. After centrifuging at 8000 g for 15 min, the resulting supernatant was collected and pooled for measuring the total anthocyanin content (TAC). Additionally, the combined supernatants were concentrated to 5 mL and stored in a dark environment at −20 °C for subsequent LC-MS/MS analysis. Method optimization was conducted to ensure thorough extraction of anthocyanins.

Total anthocyanin content measured by pH differential method

TAC measurement was performed following the AOAC method 2005.02 (Lee et al., 2005). Two aliquots (2 mL) of extract were separately diluted 3 times using 0.025 M potassium chloride buffer at pH 1.0 and 0.4 M sodium acetate buffer at pH 4.5. After incubating for 30 min in darkness, the mixture was centrifuged and filtered using a 0.45 μm hydrophilic PTFE filter. Subsequently, the absorbance of mixture was measured at wavelengths of 520 nm and 700 nm using a UV-VIS spectrophotometer (UV-1900, Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan). The TAC results were expressed as C3G equivalent using the eq. (8).(8)TACmg/L=A×MW×DF×103ε×I

Where A refers to the absorbance = (A520nm – A700nm) pH1.0 – (A520nm – A700nm) pH4.5; MW refers to the molecular weight of C3G = 449.2 g/mol; ɛ refers to the extinction coefficient = 26,900 L/mol/cm; DF denotes the dilution factor = 3 in this study; I refers to pathlength = 1 cm.

LC-MS/MS quantification

The separation, identification and quantification of anthocyanin compounds were conducted referring to the method used by Abdel-Aal et al. (2018). A high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system equipped with a LTQ XL™ linear ion trap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used alongside a ZORBAX SB-C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm, 3.5 μm, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The mobile phases used were 0.5% formic acid in ultrapure water (A) and 0.5% formic acid in methanol (B). Gradient elution was performed at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min, as follows: 0 min 20% B, 0–5 min 20–40% B, 5–25 min 40–50% B, 25–28 min 50–100% B, 28–38 min held at 100% B, 38–40 min 100–20% B, and 40–45 min held at 20% B. The compounds in 10 μL of reconstituted and filtered anthocyanin extract were separated at 35 °C.

Subsequently, the separated compounds were detected by the mass spectrometer. Positive ions were generated at 4 kV using electrospray ionisation (ESI), and the parameters were as follows: sheath gas flow rate: 35.09 arb; capillary temperature: 300 °C; capillary voltage: 48 V; scan range: 200–1000 m/z. Anthocyanin standards were used for quantification and the compounds identified without standards were quantified according to the standard of the same aglycone. Raw data was acquired and processed using Xcalibur™ software (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Dough characterization

A total of 10 samples were prepared for dough and bread analysis. These samples consisted of 100% refined bread flour (Control) and blends of flour mixed with PWB in three different particle sizes: coarse (CPWB), fine (FPWB), and superfine (SPWB) at weight percentages of 90:10, 80:20, and 70:30, respectively.

Water absorption by Farinographic method

Water absorption for control and PWB-fortified doughs were evaluated using a Farinograph-AT instrument with a 300-g mixing bowl (Brabender, Duisburg, Germany) following the AACC Method 54–21 (Sui et al., 2016) . Water absorption (WA) was determined as the percentage of water required to achieve the desired dough consistency of 500 ± 20 BU. Results are presented in Table S1.

Microstructure of dough

The procedure followed the method detailed in Section 2.3.4.

Bread preparation

The control bread formulation followed the method described by Jiang, Li, et al. (2024). It consisted of 200 g of control refined bread flour, 8 g of sugar, 4 g of salt, 6 g of shortening, and 2 g of instant dried yeast. For breads incorporating PWBs, 10%, 20% and 30% of the control flour were replaced with CPWB, FPWB and SPWB, respectively. The bread-making procedure was as followed: First, the dried ingredients were mixed using a Spar mixer (SP-800, SPAR Food Machinery, Taichung City, Taiwan) at 132 rpm for 1 min. The required water amount of each sample from Farinographic results (Table S1) was added during another 1 min mixing. The mixing speed was then adjusted to 234 rpm and maintained for 15 min, followed by a 15 min resting period. Dough pieces weighing 55 g were shaped roundly and placed into a proofer (HCP108, Memmert, Schwabach, Germany) for 70 min at 40 °C and 85% relative humidity. After proofing, the dough pieces were baked in an oven (MS01T04–2, Euroflours, Gommegnies, France) at 200 °C for 10 min. The bread quality analysis was conducted after allowing the breads to cool at room temperature for 1 h.

Quality attribute evaluation and antioxidant properties of bread

Physical properties

The color analysis followed the methodology outlined in Section 2.3.3, utilizing the control bread as the reference for calculating the ΔE values.

Specific volumes (SV, mL/g) of fresh breads were measure using a Volscan Profiler (VSP 600, Stable Micro System Ltd., Surrey, U.K.) as described in Jiang, Li, et al. (2024).

The pH analysis of the bread was conducted with a slight modification to the method described by Sui et al. (2015). A fresh bread piece (4 g) was mixed with 40 mL of DI water, and the mixture was shaken for 30 min at 350 rpm. After allowing it to stand for 10 min, the suspension was measured using a pH meter (Metrohm 744 pH meter, Metrohm Ltd., Herisau, Switzerland).

Texture profile

The texture profile analysis of both fresh bread and bread stored for 48 h was conducted using a TA-XT2i texture analyzer (Stable Micro System, Surrey, UK), following the method by Gao et al. (2017). A bread slice with 2 cm thickness, underwent double compression using an SP20 probe, at a testing speed of 2 mm/s, and a compression distance of 8 mm (equivalent to 40% of the sample thickness). Hardness, chewiness, cohesiveness, and resilience were determined for each sample with 12 replicates.

Crumb structure characteristics by C-cell

The breadcrumb structure characteristics (porosity, cell diameter and cell volume) were measured by a C-Cell imaging system (313,104, Calibre Control International Ltd., Warrington, UK), following AACC 10–18.02 (Xu et al., 2019).

Starch retrogradation

The quantification of starch retrogradation in refrigerated bread samples after 48 h was carried out using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), following the method outlined by Lin et al. (2022). After equilibrating to room temperature, approximately 10 mg of sample taken from the core of breadcrumb was carefully placed into an aluminium pan, and which were then hermetically sealed. The samples were subsequently held at 25 °C for 5 min and heated from 25 °C to 100 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min, with nitrogen gas flowing at a rate of 50 mL/min. The measurements were performed using a DSC instrument (DSC1, Mettler Toledo, Greifensee, Switzerland) and analyzed using the STARe evaluation software (version 16.30). An empty pan served as a reference. The peak temperature and endotherm enthalpy of the bread sample in dry mass were recorded.

Total anthocyanin content and antioxidant activity

The procedures for TAC and antioxidant activity were detailed in Sections 2.4 and 2.5, respectively.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in at least triplicates. SPSS (Version 26.0, IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) was employed for data analysis. Measurements were presented as mean value ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis involved one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan's post-hoc test (p < 0.05) to assess differences between samples and identify significant variations. Additionally, two-way ANOVA was conducted to examine the impact of PWB particle size (coarse, fine, and superfine) and concentration in bread (10%, 20%, and 30%) on a continuous dependent variable, providing insights into the interactions between these two factors.

Results and discussion

Effect of purple wheat bran micronization on physicochemical properties

Particle-specific characteristics

Table 1 presents the particle size distribution of flour and PWB samples. After pulverization, D50 of original purple wheat bran particles decreased from 732.2 μm to 286.9 μm and 75.1 μm. Accordingly, this reduction in particle size resulted in a remarkable 16-fold increase in specific surface area (As). Specifically, the As of CPWB was 10.81 mm−1, while it was 176.35 mm−1 in SPWB, even significantly higher than that of control flour. The widening of the span of PWBs accompanied the reduction in particle size, as observed in previous investigations on superfine wheat bran and rice bran insoluble dietary fiber (Kong et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2018). The bran thickness was approximately 70 μm (Antoine et al., 2003), indicating that around 50% of the bran structure was disrupted in SPWB. Although particle size distribution has been extensively studied, there is limited research on how particle size influences other morphological parameters, such as shape and transparency, particularly in irregular particles like purple wheat bran. Unlike the spherical starch granules, which are the primary component of control flour, these bran particles display an irregular shape with fibrous projections. To characterize the regularity of PWB, over 1,000,000 particles were evaluated for symmetry, aspect ratio (b/l), and convexity. As shown in Table 1, superfine grinding led to a significant increase in these parameters from CPWB to SPWB. Larger bran particles with elongated fiber ends are more prone to fracture during grinding, since these ends offer reduced structural support. This fragmentation process promotes particle rounding, yielding a more uniform and consistent material. This phenomenon aligns with findings by Lu et al. (2023) regarding dietary fiber concentrates such as barley bran and orange peels. Interestingly, the reduction in size from CPWB to SPWB resulted in a more than 10-fold increase in transparency (Table 1). This pronounced effect is likely synergistic. First, consistent with our previous findings, superfine grinding liberates transparent starch granules from the bran matrix (Jiang, Qi, et al., 2024). Second, the process transforms irregular, highly-scattering fibrous structures into smoother, more uniform particles, as indicated by their increased convexity. This smoother morphology minimizes diffuse reflection, thereby enhancing light transmission (Woodward et al., 2015). These findings also underscore the significance of quantitatively evaluating morphological parameters beyond solely particle size distribution. Specifically, our results emphasize that morphological characteristics such as particle shape and transparency are strongly associated with size metrics, and therefore, can be leveraged for a more comprehensive particle characterization.

Color analysis

Table 1 summarizes the color of flour and PWB samples. As expected, flour displayed the highest level of lightness, lowest levels of redness and yellowness. As the size of CPWB particles decreased, an increase in lightness and a decrease in redness were observed. This observation was corresponded to the previous study (Jiang, Qi, et al., 2024), reporting a similar trend in the purple whole wheat flour with size reduction. The changes in lightness and redness observed in PWBs could be attributed to the reduced visibility of finer PWB particles, and the enhanced light reflection resulting from a more even particle surface in finer PWB samples.

The color differences (ΔE) of PWBs were relatively high (> 8), indicating that the difference between PWBs and flour could be visually discerned (Karma, 2020). That results were in line with our expectations, considering the abundant presence of anthocyanins, a group of pigment compounds, in PWBs (Abdel-Aal et al., 2006). Among them, SPWB exhibited the smallest difference, primarily attributed to its significantly higher lightness.

Chemical compositions

Table S2 presents the composition of control flour and PWBs. It is commonly reported that SDF content shows a negative correlation with particle size, while IDF is positively correlated (Zhu et al., 2010). Although the trend in IDF was not clear in this study, the SDF content significantly increased from 1.07% in CPWB to 3.30% in SPWB, consistent with previous findings on wheat bran (Lin et al., 2021). The mechanism driving this increase is likely linked to the intense mechanical forces applied during superfine grinding, which disrupt the cell wall matrix and increase SDF extractability via tissue fragmentation (De Bondt et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2025). SDF plays a crucial role in promoting human health, since its consumption has been linked to attenuated postprandial glycaemic and insulinemic responses in both diabetic and non-diabetic individuals (Galisteo, Duarte, & Zarzuelo, 2008). Moreover, phenolic compounds, a major group of bioactive phytochemicals found in purple wheat (Zhang et al., 2018), are predominantly (95%) bound to cell wall polysaccharides in the grains (Gupta et al., 2021). The conversion of IDF to SDF could enhance the bioaccessibility of DF-bound phenolic compounds, because bacterial β-glucosidases and esterases can more easily break down the less structured SDF compared to the highly crosslinked IDF (Vitaglione, Napolitano, & Fogliano, 2008). Consequently, superfine grinding of PWB may lead to improved nutritional properties in end products.

Microstructure

Fig. 1(A&B) presents CLSM and SEM images acquired from flour and PWBs, evidencing the morphological transformations following superfine grinding. The CLSM images (Fig. 1A) clearly depict the macro components using distinctive colors. Comparing the control flour predominantly comprising starch (green), the presence of fiber (blue) in PWBs is clearly discernible. In the CPWB, it is evident that the protein (red) and starch (green) were mostly embedded within the bran matrix (blue). However, the matrix underwent disintegration in FPWB and SPWB, particularly in the latter, where pulverized particles demonstrate noticeable reduction in size.

The SEM results (Fig. 1B) provided insights into the surface morphology changes in PWBs resulting from size reduction at a higher magnification. Starch granules, displaying in rounded shapes (Chakraborty et al., 2020), were observed in control flour in a significant quantity, whereas only a small amount was identified in PWB samples. Interestingly, the image of CPWB clearly exhibited exposed starch emerging from the cracks in the bran matrix. Conversely, fiber is characterized by its irregular shapes and sharp edges (Lin et al., 2021). The bran fiber structure was evident in CPWB and to a lesser extent in FPWB. However, in SPWB, distinguishing between fiber fragments and starch granules became challenging, due to their comparable sizes and shapes. This could be attributed to the disintegration of the fiber matrix, resulting in the presence of smaller spherical fiber particles that resemble starch granules.

Antioxidant activity

Fig. 1C depicts the antioxidant activity characterized by TPC, ABTS and ORAC. While these assays share the common principle of reflecting the extract's ability to transfer electrons or hydrogen atoms in vitro, it is essential to highlight the significance of performing multiple in vitro antioxidant assays to achieve a comprehensive analysis. In comparison to flour, all three methods (TPC, ABTS, and ORAC) consistently demonstrated significantly higher antioxidant capacities in PWBs. It was anticipated, given the presence of antioxidants such as anthocyanins, phenolic acids, and other flavonoids in PWB (Giordano et al., 2017). Among the three particle sizes, SPWB demonstrated the highest antioxidant activities, as indicated by TPC, ABTS, and ORAC assays, which increased by 1.2, 1.5, and 1.2 times, respectively, compared to CPWB. This enhancement could be due to the rupture of the bran matrix, resulting in 1) the liberation of antioxidants linked to the cell wall, such as phenolic acids (Lin, Jin, Gao, Qiu, et al., 2021), as well as 2) the increased availability of antioxidants due to the larger specific surface area (Rosa et al., 2013). Therefore, the increased antioxidant activity observed in SPWWF holds the potential to impart health-promoting benefits after incorporating into the final products.

Total anthocyanin content and anthocyanin profile

Total anthocyanin content

TAC (Fig. 1D) was exclusively measured in PWBs. The anthocyanin extraction was carried out under low pH conditions, so a vibrant color was expected for anthocyanin extracts (Andersen & Jordheim, 2010). Consequently, the limited amount of anthocyanins determined in flour could be explained by the absence of red pigmentation, as reflected in the low a* values reported in Table 7.1. The relatively high TAC (over 200 μg/g C3G equivalent DM) in PWBs could be attributed to the concentrated presence of anthocyanins within the bran layer (Abdel-Aal et al., 2018). Varying levels of TAC in purple wheat bran have been reported in the literature. For instance, Abdel-Aal and Hucl (2003) found that the TAC of purple wheat bran (28% of the whole grain) from Konini cultivated in Saskatchewan, Canada experienced a decrease from 383.2 mg/kg to 156.7 mg/kg to 166.1 mg/kg in 1996, 1997, and 1998, respectively. Similarly, Siebenhandl et al. (2007) reported a TAC of 168.6 mg/kg in bran (12.7%) from BVAL-258034 grown in Raasdorf, Austria in 2004. Much higher TAC values have been found in the bran (3.7%) of a commercial purple wheat in Italy, with a content of 695 μg/g (Zanoletti et al., 2017), and the bran (5%) from Rosso cultivar planted in Austria contained a TAC of 579 mg/kg dw (Giordano et al., 2017). These findings underscore the considerable variability of TAC in purple wheat bran across different wheat varieties, growing conditions and fraction percentages. Comparing the reported TAC values, the concentration in the present study can be deemed reasonable and commendable.

SPWB exhibited a significantly higher TAC value of 239.87 ± 13.76 μg/g DW compared to C- and F- PWB, which recorded TAC values of 211.69 ± 11.62 and 207.75 ± 6.42 μg/g DW, respectively. This could be attributed to the nearly complete breakdown of the bran matrix during superfine grinding, as evidenced by SEM and CLSM figures in Fig. 1, leading to a significant release and availability of anthocyanin compounds from the vacuoles in the pericarp layer. A similar result has been reported in the red hard wheat bran, with a notable 72% increase in the TAC value observed in fine bran particles compared to the coarse (Brewer et al., 2014). To sum up, these results highlight the beneficial effect of superfine grinding on the TAC of purple wheat bran, underscoring its potential for use in the production of functional PWB-fortified foods.

Anthocyanin quantification

Cyanidin and peonidin are two main aglycones in purple wheat (Abdel-Aal et al., 2018). Using HPLC-MS/MS, their corresponding anthocyanin compounds, namely cyanidin-3-glucoside (C3G), cyanidin-3-(6-malonyl glucoside) (CMG), cyanidin-3-rutinoside (C3R), peonidin-3-glucoside (P3G) and peonidin-3-rutinoside (P3R) were quantified and are listed in Table S3. For reference, the mass spectra are provided in Fig. S2. The fragmentation scheme proposed in Jiang, Qi, et al. (2024) was not reiterated here.

Among these compounds, P3R, P3G and C3G were identified as the three main anthocyanin compounds in purple wheat bran, which contradicted existing literature stating that CMG was the dominant anthocyanin in the purple wheat bran fraction of a variety named CDC Primepurple cultivated in Canada(Abdel-Aal et al., 2018). This discrepancy could be due to the variations in crop cultivars, genotypes, interactions among genotypes, as well as agricultural periods and geographical locations, all significantly affecting anthocyanin content and composition in plants (Abdel-Aal et al., 2016; D. C. Knievel, E. S. M. Abdel-Aal, I. Rabalski, T. Nakamura, & P. Hucl, 2009). In CPWB, P3R was significantly higher than C3G and P3G, while no significant difference was observed among them in SPWB. This further supported the point we suggested in our previous study, indicating particle size could be a factor affecting the anthocyanin profile (Jiang, Qi, et al., 2024). Nevertheless, the results here were consistent with TAC analysis, indicating that superfine grinding effectively increased the availability of individual anthocyanin compounds, such as C3G, CMG, and P3G, through reduction of PWB particle size.

Microstructure of purple wheat bran-fortified doughs

Fig. 1(A&B) depict the CLSM and SEM images for the control and 30% PWB-fortified doughs, illustrating how bran particle size physically affects the gluten network. The 30% level was selected as a representative extreme case to maximize the visibility of the gluten network disruption and fiber interactions, which correlate with the macroscopic changes observed in the textural data. CLSM images were captured to visualize the different compositions in doughs, distinguished by distinct colors. Comparing the red color (protein), the control dough exhibited a continuously and smooth gluten network. Conversely, it was barely observed in 30% C due to the detrimental disruption from coarse brans. This discrete gluten structure was alleviated with reduced bran particles, and the 30% SF exhibited a more integrity and uniform gluten matrix, although it was less extensive than the control dough. On the other hand, the blue color (fiber) could only be observed in PWBs fortified doughs. It was more evident in the 30% C, where the bran matrix remained relatively intact. In contrast, the bran particles in the 30% fine PWB and 30% superfine PWB-fortified doughs were fragmented into smaller particles, allowing for better incorporated into the protein-starch system. These observations align with the findings reported by Lin et al. (2021), suggesting that the finer particles could better be incorporated into gluten matrix.

The SEM images provide insights into the interactions between starch and gluten matrix. In control dough, starch granules were smoothly embedded within the gluten matrix, while it was disrupted with the incorporation of PWBs. Corresponding to the CLSM investigations, detrimental effects on gluten structure formation were mitigated with finer PWB particle sizes. In 30% SF, the SPWB particles were distinguishable from starch granules, and its gluten network was more interconnected and well-developed than when CPWB and FPWB were incorporated, having a dough structure which was more comparable to the control. The disrupted gluten-starch matrix caused by the coarse PWB particles may alter the structure of end products (Niu et al., 2014), and further affect starch digestion by providing more accessible sites for digestive enzymes to target susceptible starch (Zhu, 2019).

Specific volume, pH and color

Specific volume (SV) serves as an indicator of bread quality and is a significant factor that influences consumer acceptability (Monteiro et al., 2021). Table 3 presents the SV values for control bread and PWB-fortified breads. The two-way ANOVA analysis revealed a significant effect (F(4, 72) = 2.679, p = 0.038) on the interaction between the particle size (coarse, fine and superfine) and the fortification concentration (10%, 20% and 30%). The incorporation of PWBs significantly reduced the SV (ranging from 2.75 to 3.70 mL/g) compared to the control bread (SV = 3.93 mL/g). Regardless of particle size, the SV decreased with the increase of PWB fortification concentration. This phenomenon could be explained by several aspects. Firstly, the presence of fiber particles could physically disrupt the gluten matrix, hindering the dough expansion and weakening its gas retention capacity during proofing, thus leading to a reduced loaf volume (Packkia-Doss, Chevallier, Pare, & Le-Bail, 2019). Secondly, fiber particles could compete for water with other polymers like proteins and starch granules, negatively impacting pasting and gelling properties and subsequently affecting dough viscoelasticity, resulting in weaker doughs with an impaired ability to hold loaf structure and volume (Khalid, Ohm, & Simsek, 2017). Thirdly, the high amount of PWB in the system could dilute the intermolecular disulfide bridges responsible for maintaining dough stability (Autio, Flander, Kinnunen, & Heinonen, 2001). Lastly, phenolic acids, a group of significant antioxidants present in bran and aleurone layers, could adversely alter the gluten structure as chemical impediment by decreasing the formation of At low and medium fortification levels (10% and 20%), no significant difference was found between varying particle sizes. However, FPWB (SV = 3.28 mL/g) and SFPWB (SV = 3.24 mL/g) demonstrated significantly higher SV values than CPWB (SV = 2.75 mL/g) at 30% fortification. This finding contrasts with the results reported by Lin et al. (2022), who observed that bread fortified with coarse wheat bran (SV = 2.66 mL/g) exhibited a larger SV compared to breads fortified with medium (SV = 2.04 mL/g) or ultrafine wheat bran (SV = 2.14 mL/g) at a 30% fortification. Although superfine grinding moderated the hindering and diluting effects of wheat bran, the increased release of ferulic acid monomers could adversely interact with gluten, negatively impacting its functionality. However, the enhanced availability of anthocyanins in micronized PWB likely counteracted these negative effects, as anthocyanins have been reported to strengthen gluten networks and improve structural stability by promoting hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions between gluten protein molecules (Li et al., 2023). As a result, breads fortified with FPWB and SPWB maintained acceptable SV values even at a high fortification level (30%).

The pH levels were determined as anthocyanins are a group of pigments sensitive to pH changes (Sui et al., 2014). Referring to Table 3, PWB-fortified breads were higher in pH values compared to control bread, particularly at higher fortification levels. The lower pH (5.75) observed in control bread was hypothesized to be attributed to its higher amount of starch content from refined bread flour compared to breads incorporating PWB. Endogenous amylases in the flour hydrolyze starch into fermentable sugars (Hackenberg, Leitner, Jekle, & Becker, 2018). Consequently, the higher proportion of flour in the control bread resulted in increased fermentable sugars, leading to more significant yeast fermentation and elevated succinic acid production, primarily which primarily contribute to the lower pH observed (Jayaram et al., 2013). This also explains the absence of significant pH differences among breads fortified with various PWB particle sizes at identical incorporation levels. Notwithstanding, the pH range observed was relatively narrow, measuring 0.42 from 5.75 to 6.17, which was comparable to the 0.3 range reported in Sui et al. (2015). This suggests that the pH differences should not be considered a significant factor affecting the anthocyanin degradation or stability among the PWB-fortified breads.

Color, beyond its role as an indicator of anthocyanin pigment concentration and its sensitivity to pH levels, also influences consumer preferences and overall liking. Results from color measurements on breadcrust and breadcrumb are tabulated in Table 3. In the crumb, PWB incorporation resulted in a darker color (lower L*) and increased redness (a*) due to the endogenous anthocyanins, while the b* value showed no clear trend. As indicated by the ΔE, differences between control bread and PWB-fortified breads could be easily differentiated (ΔE > 8) either from crust or crumb. Additionally, these differences became more pronounced with higher levels of PWB incorporation or finer PWB particle size. The former outcome was expected due to the elevated anthocyanins content from PWB, while the latter finding contrasted with previous observations in PWBs, where SPWB exhibited the least in ΔE and the highest in L*. Nevertheless, similar results have been shown in conventional wheat bran-enriched breads, suggesting that the darker breadcrumb could be due to the increased specific surface area of bran exposed during bread-making process (Heiniö et al., 2016; Lin et al., 2022). The lower ΔE values in the crust compared to the crumb might be attributed to the prevalence of Maillard reaction and caramelization, which have a more significant impact on crust color formation (Gómez, Ronda, Blanco, Caballero, & Apesteguía, 2003).

Structural properties

As shown in Table 4, the incorporation of PWB significantly reduced the cell number while increasing the porosity, cell diameter and cell volume of breadcrumb compared to the control, which indicated coarser and more open cell structures in PWB-fortified breads. During blending of wheat flour and water, the hydrated gluten-starch system and liquid lamella formed enclosed gas-holding pockets (i.e., air nuclei), effectively trapping carbon dioxide (Rahimi, Baur, & Singh, 2020). The gluten network served as the continuous phase, with starch and air nuclei as the discontinuous phases. The air nuclei expanded due to the leavening agent during proofing and increasing temperature during baking, stabilized by the visco-elastic dough structure comprising both gluten-starch matrix and liquid lamella (Sroan, Bean, & MacRitchie, 2009). However, the presence of PWB particles weakened the gluten matrix and reduced the dough viscosity, leading to the coalescence of cells and the formation of larger-diameter cells and even holes with It deemed that the finer PWB particles could reduce the gas cell coalescence, while the one-way ANOVA analysis showed no significant difference in breadcrumb structural properties among different sizes of PWB at a certain fortification level. Nevertheless, the cellular structure in breadcrumb is a resultant of multiple variables. Textural properties

Hardness, springiness, cohesiveness and chewiness of fresh breads are summarized in Table 4. The hardness exhibited an upward trend with increasing levels of PWB fortification, while a decrease in PWB particle size resulted in significantly decreased hardness in higher fortification levels (i.e., 20% and 30%). The literature consistently reports a negative correlation between SV and breadcrumb hardness (Heiniö et al., 2016; Lin et al., 2022), which aligns with the findings of this study. The presence of larger PWB particles could disrupt the forming of gluten structure, leading to reduced gas retention capacity and denser bread. Conversely, the incorporation of smaller PWB particles effectively mitigated these effects, resulting in a larger SV and a softer breadcrumb texture. Springiness reflects the ability of the crumb to recover its original shape after compression, and cohesiveness represents the internal binding force required to maintain the structure stability (Li et al., 2025). Both parameters decreased with PWB addition. This reduction can be attributed to the physical properties of the bran fiber, which introduce mechanical shear and dilution effects. These physical factors collectively inhibit the covalent cross-linking of gluten proteins and disrupt the continuous gluten-starch network during dough development, ultimately translating to a weaker, less elastic bread crumb (Li et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026). Chewiness showed a similar pattern to hardness, which is in good agreement with Lin et al. (2020). Hence, incorporating SPWB is more advantageous in producing high-fiber and antioxidant-rich bread with minimal compromise in bread texture.

Bread staling

Bread staling is primarily attributed to starch retrogradation, which involves the recrystallization of disrupted amylose and amylopectin molecules to form an ordered structure (Wang, Li, Copeland, Niu, & Wang, 2015). In this regard, the DSC was utilized to evaluate the level of starch retrogradation in stored PWB-fortified breads (48 h), by monitoring the melting of recrystallized starch between 50 and 80 °C in an endothermic event. The two-way ANOVA analysis indicated that the interactive effect of PWB size and concentration had no significant effect on the peak melting temperature (F (4, 18) = 1.322, p = 0.300). However, the enthalpy change, which is the energy associated with the transition, significantly (p < 0.05) reduced with the increase in PWB concentration and decrease in particle size. This implied that the finer particle and higher PWB concentration could retard the amylopectin crystallization during storage. The amount of available water is a key point in determining the starch retrogradation rate since it may act as a plasticizer in the gluten-starch structure (Goesaert et al., 2005). The mechanism behind this phenomenon can be attributed to two factors. First, the higher amount of SDF in finer PWB, as discussed in Section 3.1, might have a more pronounced effect on the water distribution in the dough due to its hydrophilic nature (Goesaert et al., 2005). In addition, due to its stronger water-binding capacity, the finer fiber in the bread matrix may restrict moisture migration from the crumb to the crust during storage, preserving crumb plasticity over time (Fadda et al., 2014). Second, the anthocyanins and other phenolic compounds allowed their hydroxyl groups to interact with water and the hydrophilic groups in starch, preventing hydrophobic interactions between starch molecules, thereby hindering retrogradation (Zhu, 2015).

The increased firmness in crumb is a significant bahaviour of bread staling (Ronda, Caballero, Quilez, & Roos, 2011). As shown in Table 4, the changes in crumb hardness after storage for 48 h were consistent with the enthalpy change, showing that the bread with finer PWB retained a softer texture. Except for 10% CPWB incorporation, PWB-fortified breads (55% - 181%) exhibited a slower increase in hardness compared to the control (201%). In addition, the SPWB-fortified bread maintained significantly lower hardness after the 48-h storage. Therefore, these findings suggest that the SPWB could retard the bread staling during storage.

Bread functional properties

Antioxidants such as anthocyanins are susceptible to extreme processing conditions (Li, Pickard, & Beta, 2007). However, when incorporated into the bread system, they need to withstand high temperatures during the baking process. Thus, this would warrant an investigation on the functional properties of breads, including TAC and antioxidant activity, with the results presented in Fig. 2.

Total anthocyanin content of PWB-fortified breads

The two-way ANOVA analysis revealed a significant interaction effect on the TAC results of breads between the fortified PWB particle size and level (F(4, 18) = 11.971, p < 0.001). Consistent with results from PWBs, TAC in breads increased with higher PWB fortification level and finer particle size. The bread fortified with 30% SPWB contained the highest TAC, reaching 55.51 μg C3G/g DW, which surpassed the TAC in biscuits made from purple wheat flour (13.86 mg C3G/kg) (Pasqualone et al., 2015).

Bread fortified with SPWB seems to exhibit higher anthocyanin retention rates (75.99%–84.84%) compared to bread containing CPWB (64.80%–72.48%). In a previous report, pasta incorporating 20.4% PWB (295 μg C3G/g) with particles larger than 1000 μm retained only 28 μg C3G/g of TAC (Zanoletti et al., 2017), whereas in our study, 20% SPWB (239.87 C3G/g DW) was fortified into bread resulting in 33.14 μg C3G/g DW. It is proposed that the finer PWB could be better incorporated into gluten matrix, as observed in the microstructure (Fig. 1), which might ameliorate the heat stress during baking. Additionally, the increased availability of anthocyanins from finer PWB could interact with amino acid residues from dough proteins through hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding, producing more stable protein-anthocyanin complexes (Ren, Jiménez-Flores, & Giusti, 2021). This would in turn impart the stability of anthocyanin, and consistent with findings in the interaction between silkworm pupae protein and C3G (Attaribo et al., 2020). Anthocyanins are well-recognized for their multiple health benefits (He & Giusti, 2010). The presence of anthocyanins in PWB-fortified products would be a significant advantage over common wheat grains.

Antioxidant properties of breads

The antioxidant properties of control and PWB-fortified breads were measured by TPC, ABTS and ORAC and are shown in Fig. 2. These 3 assays consistently showed that 30% SPWB-fortified bread exhibited the highest antioxidant activity, which was 3.20, 3.47 and 7.22 times of control bread in terms of TPC, ABTS and ORAC, respectively. In addition to the higher TAC discussed in Section 3.6.1, the enhanced antioxidant activity could be the increased liberation of phenolic compounds resulting from greater cell wall disruption (Lin et al., 2022). This further expounds the benefits of incorporating SPWB into bread matrix as compared to CPWB.

Ultimately, the impact of PWB on bread quality may be governed by a synergistic effect. Structurally, the addition of PWB introduces mechanical shear and steric hindrance, which physically disrupt the gluten network (Lin et al., 2022). From a chemical perspective, the finer PWB particles facilitate the release more endogenous anthocyanins, which may cleave covalent disulfide bonds to further depolymerize the native gluten structure (Sui et al., 2016). However, the unique, highly acylated anthocyanin profile from purple wheat may reinforce the dough matrix by forming non-covalent complexes with the gluten proteins (Jiang, Li, et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023), partially counteracting the severe physical and chemical deterioration.

Conclusions

In a nutshell, coarse purple wheat bran (CPWB, D50 = 732.2 μm) was superfinely ground into superfine purple wheat bran (SPWB, D50 = 75.1 μm), which significantly improved the antioxidant activity and quality of its fortifying breads. The size reduction in PWB imparted higher functionality, characterized by increased extractable total anthocyanin content (TAC) and stronger antioxidant activity. The incorporation of SPWB into bread matrix effectively alleviated the disruption observed in CPWB, as evidenced by the microstructure of dough and the texture profile of bread. For instance, it resulted in an increased specific volume and a softer breadcrumb compared to CPWB at the same level. Furthermore, the addition of SPWB showed less starch retrogradation compared to CPWB. Notably, the 30% SPWB-fortified bread exhibited the highest TAC and strongest antioxidant activity, which could offer additional health benefits, such as modulating the starch digestion. Beyond bread systems, SPWB holds significant potential as a functional, antioxidant-rich ingredient for application in other cereal-based products, such as biscuits. However, its application in boiled products, such as pasta or noodles, can be limited, as the highly water-soluble anthocyanins in SPWB would suffer significant leaching losses. To sum up, the 30% SPWB-fortified bread could be served as a food vehicle to deliver more plant-based bioactives to consumers while maintaining acceptable organoleptic quality. Moving forward, future in vitro digestion and in vivo glycemic response studies are warranted to evaluate these fortified breads. Such research will be crucial to fully substantiate their potential to modulate starch digestion and practically mitigate metabolic disorders like T2D.
