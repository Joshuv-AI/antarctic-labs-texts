Abstract
. For each prime p p , this paper constructs compact complex hyperbolic 2 2 -manifolds with an isometric action of ℤ / p ​ ℤ \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} that is not free and has only isolated fixed points. The case p = 2 p=2 is special, and finding general examples for p = 2 p=2 is related to whether or not complex hyperbolic lattices are conjugacy separable on torsion.



1.
Introduction

The purpose of this note is to prove the following result.

Theorem 1.1
.

For all primes
p
, there is a complex hyperbolic surface
X
with an action of
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
that is not free and has only isolated fixed points.

The existence of such actions was asked of us by J. Lott in the case of
p=2
in connection with his work on Ricci flow on Kähler surfaces. The proof of Theorem
1.1
is relatively straightforward for odd primes, but one does find an intriguing difference between the two constructions of arithmetic lattices, namely those of
simplest
and
division algebra
types. We refer the reader to §
2.4
for restrictions on torsion in arithmetic lattices in
\mbox{\rm{SU}}(2,1)
of division algebra type implying that those lattices can only be used to prove Theorem
1.1
when
p\equiv 1\pmod{3}
. The full proof of Theorem
1.1
is given in §
3
.

The case
p=2
is handled through an explicit example whose existence is somewhat fortuitous. This case is more delicate due to the fact that
2
-torsion in lattices with isolated fixed points are not obviously differentiated from complex reflections of order
2
in finite quotients. See §
5
for more on this and connections to open questions like conjugacy separability of complex hyperbolic lattices.

We close the introduction with a remark and some terminology. If
M
is a compact complex hyperbolic surface,
F
is a finite group of isometries with isolated fixed points, and
X=M/F
, then
X
is a normal complex surface with cyclic quotient singularities such that the projection
M\to X
is unbranched (i.e., unramified in codimension one). This equips
X
with a metric of constant holomorphic sectional curvature
-1
whose singularities are precisely at the singularities of
X
. It seems reasonable to call such an
X
a
singular complex hyperbolic surface
, and quotients of fake projective planes give an interesting class of examples (e.g., see
[
7
]
). It would be interesting to understand these surfaces better, especially those of small volume. In §
4
we provide several concrete examples.

2.
Complex hyperbolic preliminaries

To make this note relatively self-contained we recall some basic facts about complex hyperbolic lattices and their quotient orbifolds, particularly those which are arithmetic. For further details on complex hyperbolic geometry, see
[
6
]
. Throughout,
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}
will denote complex hyperbolic
n
-space, and by a complex hyperbolic lattice we mean a discrete subgroup
\Gamma<\mbox{\rm{SU}}(n,1)
such that
\mathbb{H}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}/\Gamma
has finite volume. When
\Gamma
is torsion-free the quotient is a manifold, and otherwise it is an orbifold. When
n=2
and
\Gamma
is torsion-free we call
X=\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}/\Gamma
a
complex hyperbolic surface
.

2.1.
Complex hyperbolic 2-orbifolds

This paper will require a some basic facts about torsion in complex hyperbolic lattices. An element of
\mbox{\rm{SU}}(2,1)
is
elliptic
if it has a fixed point in
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
. The geometry of an elliptic element’s action on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
is reflected in its eigenvalues and how the eigenspaces behave under the hermitian form used to define
\mbox{\rm{SU}}(2,1)
:

(1)

Suppose
g
is elliptic with only two distinct eigenvalues.

(a)

If the
2
-dimensional eigenspace is signature
(1,1)
, then
g
is a
complex reflection
acting by the identity on a totally geodesic
\mathbb{H}^{1}_{\mathbb{C}}
in
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
and by an element of
\mbox{\rm{U}}(1)
on its normal bundle.

(b)

If the
2
-dimensional eigenspace is positive definite, then
g
is a
reflection through a point
. In particular, it has a unique fixed point in
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
and the action of
g
on its tangent space, which is canonically identified with the rank
2
eigenspace, is by a nontrivial scalar multiple of the identity.

(2)

If
g
is elliptic with three distinct eigenvalues, then it is
regular elliptic
. Here,
g
has a unique fixed point in
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
where the induced action on the tangent space to that point is by a nonscalar cyclic group.

Note that an element with only one eigenvalue is scalar, hence it acts trivially on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
. Of particular importance in this paper is the observation that an elliptic element of
\mbox{\rm{SU}}(2,1)
fails to have an isolated fixed point on
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
if and only if it is a complex reflection.

Remark 2.1
.

There are no regular elliptic elements in
\mbox{\rm{SU}}(2,1)
with order two action on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
, simply since one cannot select three distinct numbers from
\{\pm 1\}
. This is why the prime
2
is exceptional for the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Suppose
\Gamma<\mbox{\rm{SU}}(2,1)
is a lattice and
\Lambda<\Gamma
is a torsion-free, normal subgroup of finite index. Then the finite group
F=\Gamma/\Lambda
admits an isometric action on the manifold
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}/\Lambda
. Whether or not
F
has isolated fixed points is completely characterized by the torsion elements of
\Gamma
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose
X=\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}/\Lambda
is a finite-volume complex hyperbolic
2
-manifold and
F
is a finite group of isometries of
X
. Writing
X/F
as
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}/\Gamma
, then
F
has isolated fixed points if and only if
\Gamma
contains no complex reflections.

Proof.

If
F
acts freely, then any torsion in
\Gamma
is central, so there is nothing to prove. If
f\in F
has a fixed point
y\in X
, then the lift
\widetilde{f}
of
f
to
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
defines a torsion element of
\Gamma
whose action on a lift of
y
models the action of
f
. In particular,
y
is an isolated fixed point if and only if
\widetilde{f}
is not a complex reflection. The lemma follows.

Essential to this paper for the odd prime case of Theorem
1.1
is that complex reflections and regular elliptic elements are distinguished by congruence quotients.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose that
E
is a number field with ring of integers
R_{E}
and
\Gamma<\mbox{\rm{SU}}(2,1)
is a lattice contained in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(R_{E})
. Assume that
\Gamma
contains elements
\sigma,\tau
such that
\sigma
is a complex reflection and
\tau
is regular elliptic. Then for all but finitely many prime ideals
\mathcal{Q}
of
R_{E}
, the images of
\sigma
and
\tau
in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(R_{E}/\mathcal{Q})
under reduction modulo
\mathcal{Q}
are not conjugate in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(R_{E}/\mathcal{Q})
and, moreover, the image of
\tau
is nontrivial.

Proof.

Since
\sigma
has a repeated eigenvalue over
\mathbb{C}
and
\tau
has distinct eigenvalues, their characteristic polynomials
\chi_{\sigma}(t)
and
\chi_{\tau}(t)
are different elements of
R_{E}[t]
. Thus there are only finitely many prime ideals
\mathcal{Q}
of
R_{E}
so that
\chi_{\sigma}(t)
and
\chi_{\tau}(t)
are congruent modulo
\mathcal{Q}
, i.e., define the same element of
(R_{E}/\mathcal{Q})[t]
. If the reductions
\widehat{\sigma}
and
\widehat{\tau}
of
\sigma
and
\tau
are conjugate in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(R_{E}/\mathcal{Q})
, then they have the same characteristic polynomial over the finite field
R_{E}/\mathcal{Q}
, hence there are only finitely many primes
\mathcal{Q}
for which
\widehat{\sigma}
and
\widehat{\tau}
are conjugate in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(R_{E}/\mathcal{Q})
. Applying the same argument to
(t-1)^{3}
ensures that the image of
\tau
is nontrivial.

2.2.
Arithmetic complex hyperbolic surfaces

Throughout this discussion,
k
will be a totally real number field and
E/k
a CM-extension (i.e., a totally imaginary quadratic extension of
k
). The rings of integers of these field will be denoted
R_{k}
and

R_{E}
respectively. We will also fix an embedding of
E\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
so that complex conjugation restricts to the identity on
k
, and refer to this as the identity embedding of
E
.

We now recall the construction of arithmetic subgroups of
\mbox{\rm{SU}}(2,1)
, beginning with those of
simplest type
. Let
V
be a
3
-dimensional vector space over
E
equipped with an
E
-defined Hermitian form. That is,
H\in\mathrm{GL}(3,E)
satisfies
H=\overline{H}^{t}
. Furthermore, we also assume that
H
has signature
(2,1)
, and that for any nonidentity embedding
\nu:k\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
and one (hence either) extension of
\nu
to an embedding of
E
, the matrix
H^{\nu}
obtained by applying
\nu
entry-wise has signature
(3,0)
.
We can and will assume that the entries of
H
lie in
R_{E}
. Define the algebraic group
\mbox{\rm{SU}}(H)
as

\mbox{\rm{SU}}(H)=\{g\in\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{C}):gH\overline{g}^{t}=H\}.

The subgroup
\mbox{\rm{SU}}(H,R_{E})=\mbox{\rm{SU}}(H)\cap SL(3,R_{E})
is an arithmetic lattice of
\mbox{\rm{SU}}(H,\mathbb{R})
that, after appropriate conjugation in
\mathrm{GL}(3,\mathbb{C})
to take
H
to the standard form, determines an arithmetic lattice in
\mbox{\rm{SU}}(2,1)
. The arithmetic lattices of simplest type are those determined by the totality of all commensurability classes of the groups described above. Finally,
\mbox{\rm{SU}}(H,R_{E})
is cocompact if and only if
k\neq\mathbb{Q}
.

Remark 2.4
.

In
[
9
]
, it is shown that if
G
is a finite subgroup of
\mbox{\rm{U}}(2)\times\mbox{\rm{U}}(1)
, then there exists an arithmetic lattice of simplest type that contains a subgroup isomorphic to
G
.

The other arithmetic lattices are those of
division algebra type
. Continuing with the above notation, let
A
be a division algebra over
E
with center
E
and degree three over
E
, so then
A
is a
9
-dimensional
E
-vector space. Suppose that
A
also admits an involution
\tau
such that the restriction
\tau|_{E}
from the natural inclusion
E\hookrightarrow A
is complex conjugation; such an involution is called an
involution of the second kind
. A Hermitian element of
A
is an element
h\in A^{*}
such that
\tau(h)=h
. Now
A\otimes_{E}\mathbb{C}\cong M(3,\mathbb{C})
and we insist that
h
has signature
(2,1)
and has signature
(3,0)
after applying any embedding
\sigma:E\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
extending a nonidentity embedding of
k
.

Define the algebraic group
\mbox{\rm{SU}}(h,A)
as:

\mbox{\rm{SU}}(h,A)=\!\left\{x\in A^{1}:\tau(x)hx=h\right\}\!,

where
A^{1}
denotes the elements of
A
with reduced norm
1
; note that the reduced norm becomes the determinant under any matrix embedding of
A
. Let
\mathcal{O}\subset A
be a
R_{E}
-order of
A
and define the arithmetic lattice
\mbox{\rm{SU}}(h,\mathcal{O})=\mbox{\rm{SU}}(h,A)\cap\mathcal{O}
. This then defines an arithmetic lattice in
\mbox{\rm{SU}}(2,1)
. The arithmetic lattices of division algebra type are those determined by the totality of all commensurability classes of the groups described above.

Remark 2.5
.

All arithmetic lattices of division algebra type are cocompact. In fact, it is well-known to experts that taking
k=\mathbb{Q}
and
E=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})
for some square-free
d>0
in the construction of lattices of simplest type provides the only commensurability classes that are not cocompact.

The next two subsections study torsion in arithmetic lattices of simplest type and division algebra type, respectively.

2.3.
The groups
G(m,p,2)

This section describes a special case of the results in
[
9
]
in some detail. Fix integers
m,p\geq 2
such that
p
divides
m
. In what follows,
\mu_{m}
will denote the group of all
m^{th}
roots of unity. First, consider the abelian subgroup

A(m,p,2)=\!\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{1}&0\\
0&\xi_{2}\end{pmatrix}\ :\ \xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\mu_{m},\,(\xi_{1}\xi_{2})^{\frac{m}{p}}=1\right\}

of
\mbox{\rm{U}}(2)
, which is normalized by the element
w\in\mbox{\rm{U}}(2)
of order two acting by coordinate permutations. Then
G(m,p,2)=A(m,p,2)\rtimes\langle w\rangle
. Note that
G(m,m,2)
is simply the dihedral group of order
2m
. The following records some standard facts about
G(m,p,2)
.

Proposition 2.6
.

The group
G(m,p,2)
has the following properties:

(1)

G(m,p,2)
preserves the standard positive definite hermitian form on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
;

(2)

|G(m,p,2)|=\frac{2m^{2}}{p}
;

(3)

complex reflections in
G(m,p,2)
have order
2
or dividing
\frac{m}{p}
;

(4)

complex reflections in
G(m,p,2)
not of order
2
are contained in
A(m,p,2)
;

(5)

if
p\geq 3
is odd and
\gcd(\frac{m}{p},p)=1
, then no power of an element of
G(m,p,2)
of order
p
is a complex reflection;

(6)

if
p\geq 3
, then
G(m,p,2)
contains an element of order
p
with distinct nontrivial eigenvalues.

Proof.

The first statement is clear from the fact that diagonal matrices and
w
all preserve the standard inner product on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. See
[
8
, §2.2]
for the second statement and
[
8
, Lem. 2.8]
for the third and fourth. The fifth statement is simply that no nontrivial divisor of
p
divides
2
or
\frac{m}{p}
. For the last statement, the element

(1)

g_{p}=\begin{pmatrix}\zeta_{p}&0\\
0&\zeta_{p}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}

is in
G(m,p,2)
for any primitive
p^{th}
root of unity
\zeta_{p}
and has distinct eigenvalues if
p\geq 3
.

2.6
,
complex reflection
means the classical definition: an element of
\mbox{\rm{U}}(2)
with
1
as an eigenvalue. However, this ends up having the same meaning once we let
G(m,p,2)
act on
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. Specifically, take
\mbox{\rm{U}}(2,1)
with respect to the standard hermitian form
|z_{1}|^{2}+|z_{2}|^{2}-|z_{3}|^{2}
on
\mathbb{C}^{3}
, which by Proposition

(2)

\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{1}&0\\

of

in
\mbox{\rm{U}}(2,1)
. Note that
G(m,p,2)
then embeds in
\mbox{\rm{PU}}(2,1)

Convention:
For the remainder of this paper,
G(m,p,2)
is considered as embedded in
\mbox{\rm{U}}(2,1)
by Equation (

Fortunately, the two notions of complex reflection coincide.

Lemma 2.7
.

An element
g\in A(m,p,2)
is a complex reflection in
G(m,p,2)
if and only if it acts on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
as a complex reflection, and
g
acts as a regular elliptic if and only if
\xi_{1}\neq\xi_{2}
and neither equals one.

Proof.

Inspection of Equation (
2
) shows that
g
is a complex reflection as an element of
\mbox{\rm{U}}(2)
if and only if it has
1
as a repeated eigenvalue as an element of
\mbox{\rm{U}}(2,1)
, and
g
then has a repeated eigenvalue for which the
2
-dimensional eigenspace has signature
(1,1)
. Equivalently,
\det(g)^{-\frac{1}{3}}g\in\mbox{\rm{SU}}(2,1)
, which has the same action on
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
as
g
, has a repeated eigenvalue with eigenspace of signature
(1,1)
, which means precisely that
g
acts on
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
as a complex reflection. A similar argument shows that
\xi_{1}\neq\xi_{2}
with neither equal to one if and only if
g
acts as a regular elliptic.

2.4.
Torsion in lattices of division algebra type

Torsion in an arithmetic lattice of division algebra type is quite special. The first restriction on torsion in lattices of division algebra type is as follows.

Lemma 2.9
.

Suppose
\Gamma<\mbox{\rm{SU}}(2,1)
is an arithmetic lattice of division algebra type and
\tau\in\Gamma
has finite order and is not central. Then
\tau
is regular elliptic.

Proof.

Let
A
be the division algebra associated with
\Gamma
and
E
its center. Since
A
can be realized in
\mathrm{M}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
as the
E
-span of any finite-index subgroup of
\Gamma
, and
\tau
normalizes some finite-index subgroup of
A^{1}\cap\Gamma
,
\tau
normalizes
A
. By the Skolem–Noether theorem, there is some scalar matrix
\xi
(i.e., an element of the kernel of the conjugation action) so that
\sigma=\xi\tau
is in
A^{*}
. Note that
\sigma
has distinct eigenvalues if and only if
\tau
does. Then
\sigma
has a cubic characteristic polynomial
f(t)\in E[t]
. If
\tau
does not have distinct eigenvalues, then
f(t)
has a linear factor
(t-\lambda)
for some
\lambda\in E
. It now follows from the Cayley–Hamilton Theorem that
\tau-\lambda\mathrm{Id}
is a zero-divisor in
A
, contradicting the fact that
A
is a division algebra. This completes the proof.

The following generalizes an observation due to Prasad and Yeung
[
11

Lemma 2.10
.

Suppose that
\Gamma<\mbox{\rm{SU}}(2,1)
is of division algebra type and
\tau\in\Gamma
acts on
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
as an elliptic element of order
n
. Then
\varphi(n)
or
\varphi(3n)
is divisible by
3
, where
\varphi
is Euler’s totient function. In particular, if
p>3
is prime then
p\equiv 1\pmod{3}
.

Proof.

It suffices to consider
\Gamma
a maximal lattice in
\mbox{\rm{SU}}(2,1)
. It is known that
\Gamma\cap A^{1}
is a normal subgroup of
\Gamma
with index a power of
3
(see the Remark after Eq. (0) in
[
11
]
). Since
3\mid\varphi(9)
, it suffices to prove the lemma for
\tau\in A^{1}
, which implies that
\tau^{n}
is central in
A
. Moreover, the subalgebra
E(\tau)
is a commutative subalgebra, hence it is a finite extension of
E
. Note that
\tau\notin E
, since otherwise it is central in
A
. Therefore
E(\tau)/E
is an extension of degree three.

Since
\tau^{n}
has reduced norm one, and the reduced norm on the center is simply
z\mapsto z^{3}
, it follows that
\tau^{n}
is a
3^{rd}
root of unity. In particular,
\tau
is either an
n^{th}
or
(3n)^{th}
root of unity, hence its minimal polynomial over
\mathbb{Q}
has degree
\varphi(kn)
for
k\in\{1,3\}
. Since
\mathbb{Q}(\tau)
is then Galois over
\mathbb{Q}
, it follows that
\overline{E}(\tau)
remains a degree
3
extension of the Galois closure
\overline{E}
of
E
over
\mathbb{Q}
. The diagram

Proposition 2.11

Suppose that

\Gamma<\mbox{\rm{SU}}(2,1)

\zeta

\varphi(p)=p-1

, then
L=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)
contains a subfield
E
so that
L/E
is Galois of degree

. Then

is the maximal totally real subfield of

, and
E/k
is quadratic. Thus
E/k
is a CM-extension, since
E
is Galois over

and hence totally complex.

Let
A
be a cyclic division algebra with center

and involution of second kind containing

\mathcal{O}
containing the ring of integers

of

\Gamma<\mbox{\rm{SU}}(2,1)

naturally lives in
\Gamma
. Thus
\Gamma
contains an element of order

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Theorem
1.1
will be a consequence of our next stronger result.

Theorem 3.1
.

For any prime
p\in\mathbb{Z}
, there exist infinitely nonisomorphic cocompact torsion-free lattices
\Lambda_{p}^{i}<\mbox{\rm{SU}}(2,1)
, such that the quotient manifold
X_{i}=\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}/\Lambda_{p}^{i}
admits an isometric action by
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
that is not free and has only isolated fixed points.

The proof will separate the cases of
p
an odd prime, and
p=2
. However, in both cases we will make use of the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Using the notation of §
2.2
, suppose that
\Gamma
is a subgroup of
\mbox{\rm{SU}}(H,R_{E})
that contains a regular elliptic element of order
p
. Then there are infinitely many nonisomorphic subgroups
\Lambda_{i}<\Gamma
of finite index so that the quotient orbifold
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}/\Lambda_{i}
admits an isometric action by
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
that is not free and has only isolated fixed points.

Proof.

Fix an elliptic element
\tau\in\Gamma
of order
p
as in the hypothesis. By Lemma
2.3
, there are infinitely many prime ideals
\mathcal{Q}_{i}\subset R_{E}
so that the image of
\tau
is nontrivial modulo
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
and no complex reflection in
\Gamma
has reduction that is
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(R_{E}/\mathcal{Q}_{i})
-conjugate to the reduction of
\tau
. Let

\rho_{i}:\Gamma\to\mathrm{SL}_{3}(R_{E}/\mathcal{Q}_{i})

be the reduction homomorphism,
\Lambda_{i}
be its kernel, and
\widehat{\tau}_{i}=\rho_{i}(\tau)
, which is an element of order
p
since
\tau
has prime order
p
in
\Gamma
.

Note that
\Lambda_{i}
is torsion-free for all but finitely many
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
by a classical result of Minkowski, so we can assume that
X_{i}=\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}/\Lambda_{i}
is a compact complex hyperbolic manifold for all
i
. Moreover, since Strong Approximation holds for
the ambient algebraic group
\mbox{\rm{SU}}(H)
, the reduction homomorphism is onto for all but finitely primes
\mathcal{Q}_{i}

[
14
]
. Since the
R_{E}
only contains finitely many prime ideals of bounded norm, the reductions modulo
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
then have order going to infinity, i.e., the
X_{i}
have volume going to infinity and hence are distinct.

Then
\widehat{\tau}_{i}
defines an isometry of
X_{i}
with order
p
. Since
\Lambda_{i}
is torsion-free, no complex reflection in
\Gamma
is in
\Lambda_{i}
. It was also already noted that no complex reflection can have reduction conjugate into

F_{i}=\langle\widehat{\tau}_{i}\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}

inside
\Gamma/\Lambda_{i}
. Thus
\Gamma_{i}=\rho_{i}^{-1}(F_{i})<\Gamma
contains no complex reflections. Then
F_{i}
acts on
X_{i}
with isolated fixed points by Lemma
2.2
, so the proof of the lemma is complete.

3.1.
Odd primes

For the case of
p
an odd prime, we will construct a lattice
\Gamma_{p}<\mbox{\rm{SU}}(2,1)
of simplest type containing a regular elliptic element of order
p
. Consider a
p^{th}
root of unity
\zeta_{p}
, set
E=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{p})
, let
k
be its maximal totally real subfield, and consider the identity embedding of
k
sending a generator
\zeta_{p}+\zeta_{p}^{-1}
to
2\cos(2\pi/p)
.

Then
k
has degree
d=\frac{p-1}{2}
over
\mathbb{Q}
, and it is a classical fact that the various real embeddings of
k
embed
R_{k}
as a lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In particular, there is an element
\beta\in R_{k}
so that
\beta<0
under the identity embedding of
k
and
\nu(\beta)>0
for all other embeddings
\nu
of
k
. Thus the hermitian form
H_{\beta}
given by
|z_{1}|^{2}+|z_{2}|^{2}+\beta|z_{3}|^{2}
defines an arithmetic lattice of simplest type.

Lemma 3.3
.

The lattice
\mbox{\rm{SU}}(H_{p},R_{E})
defined in this section contains a regular elliptic of order
p
.

Proof.

Under the embedding from Equation (
2
), the group
G(p,p,2)
also preserves the hermitian form
H_{\beta}
, and thus
S(p,p,2)
defines a subgroup of
\mbox{\rm{SU}}(H_{\beta},R_{E})
containing a regular elliptic of order
p
by Corollary
2.8
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
for
p
odd.

If
p>3
, then set
\Gamma=\mbox{\rm{SU}}(H_{\beta},R_{E})
. This contains a regular elliptic element of order
p
by Lemma
3.3
, and is cocompact since
k\neq\mathbb{Q}
. By Lemma
3.2
, this proves Theorem
3.1
. When
p=3
, the lattice is not cocompact, so an adjustment is necessary. Replacing
\zeta_{3}
with
\zeta_{9}
, one still finds
S(3,3,2)
inside
\mbox{\rm{SU}}(H_{\beta},R_{E})
, which is now cocompact since

, so the theorem again follows.

3.2.
p=2

To handle this case, we will make use of an example from
[
4
]
(see Theorem 1.2 therein with
p=3
). This example is discussed in
[
5
]
in more detail, where it is the group denoted by
\mathcal{S}(\overline{\sigma}_{4},3)
. Henceforth we will denote this group by
\Gamma
, and note that
\Gamma
is cocompact. A presentation for
\Gamma
is:

\Gamma=\!\left\langle R_{1},R_{2},R_{3},J~|~R_{1}^{3},(R_{1}J)^{7},J^{3},JR_{1}J^{-1}=R_{2},JR_{2}J^{-1}=R_{3},R_{1}R_{2}R_{1}R_{2}=R_{2}R_{1}R_{2}R_{1}\right\rangle\!.

See
[
5
, Tb. 5.1]
and note that, as stated after Table 5.1, the last two relations there can be omitted. Although not visible in the presentation given above, the element
R_{1}R_{2}
has order
12
(again, see
[
5
, Tb. 5.1]
), and so in particular there is an element in
\Gamma
of order
2
.

As shown in
[
4
, Thm. 1.1]
,
\Gamma
is arithmetic of simplest type with:

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{21})

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3},\sqrt{-7})

The fact that
\Gamma
is simplest type can be deduced from
[
4
, §6]
, and also follows from

[
12
, Thm. 1.4]
using the existence of a complex reflection in
\Gamma
. As discussed in
[
4
, §6.1]
,
\Gamma
can be conjugated to preserve a Hermitian form
H
whose entries are algebraic integers in the field
L
such that matrices representing
R_{1}
and
J
also have entries in
L
. From the descriptions of
R_{1}
and

given in
[

we see these matrices have determinant 1, and so from the presentation for

we deduce that,
since

and

are conjugates of

, they also have determinant

\Gamma<\mbox{\rm{SU}}(H,R_{L})

Then

classifies the finite subgroups of

4.
Examples

This section catalogues some examples for small odd primes.

4.1.
Fake projective planes

Cartwright and Steger completion of the classification of fake projective planes showed that they are all of division algebra type. They have Euler characteristic
3
, which implies by Chern–Gauss–Bonnet that they are minimal volume manifolds. Thus any automorphism has fixed points, and they are isolated by Lemma
2.9
and Lemma
2.2
. The automorphism groups of fake projective planes are also classified, and one sees actions of
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
for
p=3,7

[
7
]
.

4.2.
The Cartwright–Steger surface

The Cartwright–Steger surface
[
2
]
is a minimal-volume compact complex hyperbolic
2
-manifold, and it admits an action of
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
(e.g., see
[
13
, §5]
). One can check directly that all torsion elements are reflections through points and regular elliptics, so the action has isolated fixed points. In contrast with fake projective planes, this example is arithmetic of simplest type. Moreover, one can show that some of the
3
-torsion associated with isolated fixed points of the
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
action are reflections through points. Thus this example is of a slightly different kind than those produced using the methods of this paper.

4.3.
An example for
p=5

For the pair
E=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{5})
and
k=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})
, one of the famous lattices
\Gamma_{\Sigma\mu}
constructed by Deligne and Mostow
[
3
]
ends up being
\mbox{\rm{PU}}(h,R_{k})
for the appropriate
h
. If
\mathfrak{p}_{5}
is the prime dividing
5
and
\Gamma(\mathfrak{p}_{5})
is the congruence subgroup of
\Gamma_{\Sigma\mu}
of level
\mathfrak{p}_{5}
, then all torsion subgroups of
\Gamma(\mathfrak{p}_{5})
are isomorphic to
(\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z})^{2}
generated by complex reflections. See
[
1
, Prop. 3.12]
and the surrounding discussion.

Note that each
(\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z})^{2}
, while generated by complex reflections, contains regular elliptic elements of order
5
. The congruence subgroup
\Gamma(\mathfrak{p}_{5}^{2})
of level
\mathfrak{p}_{5}^{2}
is torsion-free, and thus admits an isometric action of
\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}
with nonempty set of isolated fixed points by Lemma
2.3
and the same argument as the proof of Lemma
3.3
. One can show using known formulas for Euler characteristics of Deligne–Mostow orbifolds that
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}/\Gamma(\mathfrak{p}_{5}^{2})
has Euler characteristic
1875
.

5.
Final comments

As mentioned in the introduction, it would be interesting to understand the singular complex hyperbolic surfaces of small volume. With Lemma
2.2
in mind, we say that
X=\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}/\Gamma
is a
singular complex hyperbolic surface
if all torsion in
\Gamma
is reflections through points and regular elliptics. For each prime
p
, Theorem
1.1
produces closed singular complex hyperbolic surfaces for which each singular point is a cyclic quotient singularity of order
p
. We expect that the methods of this paper can also produce similar examples with
p
replaced with any natural number
n>1
.

Question 5.1
.

Fix a prime
p
.

(1)

What is the smallest volume closed singular complex hyperbolic surface for which all singular points have order
p
?

(2)

What is the smallest volume closed complex hyperbolic manifold admitting an action of
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
with isolated fixed points?

Remark 5.2
.

Fake projective planes and the Cartwright–Steger surface answer Question
5.1
(2) for
p=3
and
p=7
. See §
4
.

For large
p
, the methods of this paper fail for noncompact arithmetic lattices, i.e., the
Picard modular groups
. Indeed, these are contained in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(R_{d})
, where
R_{d}
is the ring of integers of
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})
. Independent of
d
, these groups can only have torsion of uniformly bounded order. Indeed, by Cayley–Hamilton their eigenvalues are roots of unity with bounded degree over
\mathbb{Q}
. Moving to arithmetic lattices, there are only famously finitely many commensurability classes known, and there is a universal bound for the torsion orders in any commensurability class since commensurability classes of nonarithmetic lattices have a unique maximal element for inclusion. Thus, the following question seems extremely difficult, since it would require constructing infinitely many new commensurability classes of nonarithmetic lattices.

Question 5.3
.

For each prime
p
, is there a noncompact finite-volume complex hyperbolic
2
-manifold
Y_{p}
admitting an action of
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
with only isolated fixed points?

Lastly, the difficulty in the case
p=2
lies in the fact that we cannot distinguish between a complex reflection of order
2
and a reflection through a point with order
2
in congruence quotients. Recall that a group
\Gamma
is
conjugacy separable
if for all nonconjugate pairs of elements
\sigma,\tau\in\Gamma
, there is a finite quotient
F
of
\Gamma
where the images of
\sigma
and
\tau
are not conjugate. Further,
\Gamma
is
conjugacy separable on torsion
if distinct conjugacy classes of torsion elements remain nonconjugate in some finite quotient. A positive answer to the following question would easily give many more examples satisfying the conclusion of Theorem
1.1
.

Question 5.4
.

Are complex hyperbolic lattices conjugacy separable? Are they conjugacy separable on torsion?

Many fundamental groups of real hyperbolic manifolds, and in fact the broad class of virtually compact special hyperbolic groups, are known to be conjugacy separable
[
10
]
. However, we do not know a single complex hyperbolic lattice (other than the Fuchsian groups in
\mbox{\rm{SU}}(1,1)
, of course) that is conjugacy separable.
