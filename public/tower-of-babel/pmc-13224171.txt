A large peri-aneurysmal cyst formation following coil embolization of an unruptured middle cerebral artery aneurysm: A case report and review of the literature
Authors: Daiki Matsuda, Yushi Nagano, Hiroshi Hosoyama, Masaaki Yamamoto, Shunichi Tanaka, Sei Sugata, Ryosuke Hanaya, Kazunori Arita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Peri-aneurysmal cyst formation is a rare but serious complication that may develop after endovascular treatment.Case Description: A 72-year-old woman underwent endovascular treatment for an incidentally found oblong middle cerebral artery aneurysm measuring 17 mm in its largest diameter, resulting in complete obliteration. Peri-aneurysmal parenchymal edema, however, began to develop 1 year after the procedure. Follow-up magnetic resonance imaging revealed the emergence of a small cyst and a gradual increase in peri-aneurysmal edema in the basal ganglia. A susceptibility-weighted imaging showed hemosiderin deposition in the basal ganglia and cerebral cortices around the aneurysm. The cyst finally grew to 55 mm at 71 months after the initial treatment, causing headaches and cognitive dysfunction. Wide fenestration of the cyst wall, pathologically confirmed as gliosis, resulted in shrinkage of the cyst and rapid improvement of symptoms.Conclusion: A review of the literature identified 14 cases of symptomatic peri-aneurysmal cyst formation following coil embolization, including the present case. Based on this review, peri-aneurysmal cyst formation should be anticipated after repeated endovascular treatments for large aneurysms embedded in the brain, particularly when peri-aneurysmal brain edema develops. Continuing microhemorrhage from the aneurysm or vasa vasorum may play a role in the cyst formation.

INTRODUCTION

Recent advances in endovascular techniques have substantially expanded the indications for coil embolization, enabling favorable outcomes even in wide-necked, morphologically complex, and large intracranial aneurysms.[10,13,20,27] Alongside these technical achievements, however, procedure-related complications specific to endovascular therapy remain clinically relevant. These include intraoperative aneurysm rupture, parent artery occlusion, distal thromboembolic events, and hemorrhagic complications related to prolonged antiplatelet therapy.[14] Among the less common but increasingly recognized delayed complications is the formation of a peri-aneurysmal cyst after coil embolization, which may gradually enlarge and become symptomatic years after treatment.[5,22,25]

In this report, we describe a rare case of a large perianeurysmal cyst that developed and progressively enlarged over approximately 6 years following coil embolization of an unruptured middle cerebral artery (MCA) aneurysm. The cyst eventually reached a diameter of 5.5 cm, causing symptoms of increased intracranial pressure and cognitive decline.

We also review previously reported cases of symptomatic peri-aneurysmal cyst formation after coil embolization and discuss potential risk factors and underlying mechanisms associated with this delayed complication.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 72-year-old woman with a history of hypertension and hyperlipidemia, both well controlled with medication, underwent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for the evaluation of dizziness. Incidentally, an oblong right MCA aneurysm measuring 17 mm in its largest diameter was detected [Figure 1a and b]. It was embedded in the base of the right basal ganglia. The aneurysm was located at the bifurcation of the right MCA and had a wide neck [Figure 1c], which was markedly calcified on computed tomography. Given these features, microsurgical clipping was considered technically challenging, and endovascular treatment was selected. Stent-assisted coil embolization was performed using 14 coils with a total coil length of 316 cm, resulting in complete occlusion of the aneurysm (Raymond-Roy class I) [Figure 1d-f]. The devices used included one Enterprise-2 stent (Cerenovus, USA), four Target XL 360 coils (Stryker, USA), six Galaxy G3 coils (Johnson & Johnson, USA), three Axium Prime 3D coils (Medtronic, USA). The postoperative course was uneventful, with no new neurological deficits. However, follow-up MRI obtained 1 year later revealed peri-aneurysmal brain edema involving the right basal ganglia and basal frontal region [Figure 2]. Angiography demonstrated recanalization at the aneurysm neck due to coil compaction. A second stent-assisted coil embolization was therefore performed using 6 coils with a total coil length of 44 cm, again achieving complete occlusion (Raymond-Roy class I) [Fig. 3a and b]. The devices used included one low-profile visualized intraluminal support (LVIS) Blue stent (MicroVention TERUMO, USA), two Target XL 360 coils, and four Galaxy G3 coils.

Serial follow-up MRI thereafter showed gradual progression of the peri-aneurysmal brain edema. At 58 months after the initial embolization, MRI demonstrated the appearance of a small cyst located posterosuperior to the aneurysm, along with enlargement of the peri-aneurysmal brain edema [Figure 4a]. At 67 months, the cyst had enlarged to 35 mm in diameter but remained asymptomatic [Figure 4b]. At that time, angiography confirmed complete obliteration of the aneurysm and stability of the aneurysm size. At 71 months after the initial treatment, the patient developed signs of increased intracranial pressure, including progressive headache, nausea, and vomiting, accompanied by cognitive dysfunction. MRI revealed marked enlargement of the cyst extending superiorly to the aneurysm, with associated worsening of the surrounding edema [Figure 4c and d]. Contrast-enhanced MRI showed no enhancement of the cyst wall. The cyst also extended anteriorly to the aneurysm, reaching a maximum diameter of 55 mm [Figure 4e]. Over the course of follow-up, hemosiderin deposition appeared in the right basal ganglia and in the cortices of the insula and anterior temporal lobe on susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) [Figure 5a and b]. The extent of hemosiderin deposition increased on later images [Figure 5c and d].

To relieve symptoms of increased intracranial pressure, surgical cyst fenestration was performed through a small right frontotemporal craniotomy. After opening the right Sylvian fissure, the cyst wall was identified immediately beneath the thinned cortex of the right insula [Figure 6a]. Following aspiration of thin xanthochromic cyst contents and biopsy of the cyst wall, wide fenestration of the cyst wall exposed the aneurysmal body, through which the inserted coils were identified [Figure 6b]. No residual blood flow was observed. Several vasa vasorum were noted. The Sylvian fissure was left open without arachnoid plasty.

Histologically, the cyst wall consisted of gliotic tissue composed of hypertrophic astrocytes and inflammatory cells [Figure 6c]. Hemosiderin deposition was scattered in the gliotic tissue [Figure 6d].

Postoperatively, the patient’s symptoms of increased intracranial pressure resolved promptly, and her cognitive function gradually improved. Follow-up MRI 4 months later demonstrated complete resolution of the cyst and a marked reduction of the surrounding brain edema [Figure 6e and f].

Literature review

Our literature review identified 17 reported cases of peri-aneurysmal cyst formation following coil embolization.[2,5,7-9,16-19,21,22,25,26] Among these, three cases were asymptomatic.[18,26] Tables 1 and 2 summarize the backgrounds and treatments of 14 cases of symptomatic perianeurysmal cyst formation, including the present case, which represents the largest peri-aneurysmal cyst reported to date.

The subjects comprised 10 females and 4 males, with a median age of 69 years (range, 51–80 years) at the time of the initial endovascular treatment.

Initial endovascular treatment consisted of simple coil embolization in 7 patients and stent-assisted coiling in 7 patients. The initial angiographic occlusion status was Raymond–Roy class I in 6 patients and class II in 6 patients; occlusion status was not reported in 2 patients.

The median diameter of the peri-aneurysmal cyst at symptomatic presentation was 31.5 mm (range, 20–55 mm). The median time from initial embolization to symptomatic cyst presentation was 34.5 months (range, 13–240 months). During this interval, additional endovascular treatments were performed in 10 patients.

Symptoms varied widely, including headache, hemiparesis, and gait disturbance, depending on the size and location of the cyst.

Peri-aneurysmal brain edema, distinct from peri-cystic edema, developed after endovascular treatment in 11 patients.

Surgical treatment for symptomatic peri-aneurysmal cysts was performed in 10 patients, with 6 requiring additional procedures because of recurrence. Treatment modalities included craniotomy with cyst wall opening or fenestration (n = 5), endoscopic fenestration (n = 2), stereotactic aspiration (n = 2), and aneurysm removal (n = 2). Cyst reduction or disappearance and corresponding symptom improvement or resolution were achieved in 9 of the 10 patients. Histopathological examination of the cyst wall revealed gliosis in 4 patients and eosinophilic fibrous tissue in 1. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case demonstrating peri-aneurysmal hemosiderin deposition.

DISCUSSION

Cyst formation around an aneurysm is a relatively rare occurrence. In 2023, Wang et al. reviewed 28 previously reported cases.[25] Peri-aneurysmal cysts were diagnosed concurrently with the associated aneurysm in 15 cases. However, nearly half of the cysts (n = 13/28) developed after endovascular treatment, and 10 of these were symptomatic.

We attempted to identify risk factors for cyst formation and to clarify the clinical course of this rare complication through a literature review of 14 symptomatic peri-aneurysmal cysts that developed after endovascular treatment, including three additional cases reported after Wang’s review and our own case.[5,8,22]

The mechanism of cyst development remains unclear. Several theories have been proposed, but none has been definitively established. Our hypothesis regarding cyst formation is as follows. First, aneurysm size tends to be relatively large: the median diameter was 18 mm, and 12 aneurysms (85.7%) were 15 mm or larger in these 14 patients. The large size implies that a substantial portion of the aneurysm wall was embedded in the brain parenchyma, as in our case, rather than located in the subarachnoid space. Inflammatory mediators produced by thrombosis of large aneurysms may therefore more readily exude from the aneurysm wall into the adjacent brain through a broad contact surface.[1,22,23,25]

Second, a large number of coils required to occlude these aneurysms (20 coils with a total length of 360 cm in our case) may induce an excessive metal-related inflammatory reaction, which may also be transmitted to the adjacent brain tissue.

Third, 71.4% (n = 10/14) of patients underwent two or more endovascular treatments. Repeated hemodynamic alterations in and around the aneurysm may therefore cause additional injury to the adjacent brain.[25,26]

Fourth, the water-hammer effect generated by a large and rigid mass, a densely coil-packed and thrombosed aneurysm, may mechanically damage the adjacent brain parenchyma, causing encephalomalacia.[3,7,22]

Fifth, aneurysm thrombosis may disturb the flow of small perforating arteries, resulting in peri-aneurysmal hypoperfusion.[7,22]

The aforementioned forms of brain injury associated with endovascular treatment of large aneurysms may result in progressive peri-aneurysmal edema, which was observed in 78.6% (11/14) of cases. The damaged brain surrounding the aneurysm may serve as a reservoir for exudate derived from the aneurysm, ultimately leading to cyst formation.[7]

Sixth, hematoma formation around the aneurysm may contribute to cyst development. Blood breakdown products may promote cyst formation.[4,11] However, among the 14 cases reviewed, 7 were identified without a history of rupture. Micro- or subclinical hemorrhage from the aneurysm, or from enlarged vasa vasorum on the aneurysm wall, may also lead to cyst formation in these cases.[2,12,24] In our case, hemosiderin deposition on SWI in the basal ganglia and cerebral cortices surrounding the aneurysm appeared 13 months after the initial treatment and increased on subsequent MRI. Hemosiderin deposition was also confirmed histologically in the biopsy specimen, a finding not previously described.

Another study proposed by Zammit et al. pathogenesis of peri-aneurysmal cyst formation involves dilated perivascular Virchow-Robin spaces.[26] Outflow of interstitial fluid from these perivascular spaces may be obstructed by large aneurysms, leading to fluid accumulation. However, enlarged perivascular spaces are rarely accompanied by brain edema until they become considerably enlarged. In contrast, perianeurysmal edema usually precedes peri-aneurysmal cyst formation, as observed in our case.

Disturbance of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics caused by large aneurysms has also been suggested as a possible mechanism of peri-aneurysmal cyst formation.[4] However, the cyst contents were usually thin xanthochromic, as in our case, rather than clear and CSF-like.

Although only two cases in this patient population involved treatment with coated coils (Cerecyte coil and Hydrogel coil),[2,16] the use of coated coils may exacerbate inflammatory reactions and peri-aneurysmal edema, potentially contributing to cyst formation.[6,15]

It is noteworthy that the lag time from initial endovascular treatment to symptomatic cyst presentation varied widely, with a median of 34.5 months (range, 13–240 months). Therefore, prolonged follow-up after endovascular treatment of large aneurysms is warranted to detect the development of peri-aneurysmal cysts, particularly in cases with post-treatment peri-aneurysmal edema.

Surgical management of these cysts may be challenging, as more than half of the reviewed cases required additional interventions, likely because of the viscous nature of the cyst contents and continuous exudation from the aneurysm. Wide cyst wall opening through craniotomy, as performed in our case, may therefore represent an effective approach. Extirpation of the coil-filled aneurysm may be considered as a final option in recurrent cases.[8,17]

CONCLUSION

Physicians should be aware of the possibility of slowly growing peri-aneurysmal cysts following endovascular coiling of large aneurysms, particularly in cases in which perianeurysmal edema is observed on early postoperative MRI. A combination of intra-aneurysmal inflammatory processes, water-hammer effects exerted by embolized aneurysms, prior hemorrhage or recurrent microhemorrhage, and ischemic encephalomalacia of the surrounding brain may contribute to cyst formation. A wide cyst wall opening into the subarachnoid space may facilitate cyst reduction and symptomatic improvement.
