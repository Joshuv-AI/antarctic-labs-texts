Monitoring the fate of paternal mitochondria and their elimination in rice zygotes
Authors: Hanifah Aini, Riho Tomishima, Kasidit Rattanawong, Mari Tanaka, Hiroyuki Tsuji, Yumi Goto, Mayuko Sato, Kiminori Toyooka, Takashi Okamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondria are preferentially transmitted from the maternal plant in most angiosperms, including rice, and paternal mitochondria are generally eliminated during microgametogenesis and/or in zygotes. The mechanism by which paternal mitochondria are eliminated progresses during plant reproductive processes. In the present study, we examined the distribution of paternal mitochondria in rice sperm cells and zygotes produced through the in vitro fertilization (IVF) of isolated rice gametes. Male gametes of rice possess mitochondria with nucleoids, suggesting the potential transfer of paternal mitochondria and their DNA into zygotes on fertilization and subsequent selective elimination of paternal mitochondria in the zygote. To intensively monitor the fate of rice paternal mitochondria in zygotes immediately after gamete fusion, time-lapse observations were conducted in paternal mitochondria labeled with GFP from rice zygotes produced using an IVF system. The results showed that the paternal mitochondria are progressively degraded during the early developmental stage at 1 to 3 h after fusion (HAF), leaving a small number of paternal mitochondria at 6 HAF. The remaining paternal mitochondria were considered to be degraded in later developmental-stage zygotes because paternal mitochondrial DNA-derived single-nucleotide polymorphisms were not detected in the sequencing reads of genomic DNA prepared from inter-subspecific hybrid rice. In addition, treatment with autophagy inhibitors stabilized the paternal mitochondria in zygotes. This suggests that the autophagy-dependent massive and selective elimination machinery for male mitochondria functions in rice zygotes immediately after gamete fusion and supports the strict maternal inheritance of mitochondria in rice.

Introduction

Mitochondria and plastids are endosymbionts of Eubacteria that are transformed into organelles inside their host cells. As endosymbionts, the original mitochondria and plastids contain their own genomic DNA or nucleoids. During the evolutionary process, they lost the bulk of their genomes, and most of the retained genomes were transferred to the nucleus of the host cell. This symbiotic process was achieved through an elaborate mechanism for organelle biogenesis and metabolite exchange, the acquisition of host-derived properties, and the loss of eubacterial identity (Dyall et al. 2004). Although endosymbionts still contain their own DNA and protein-synthesizing machinery, they are not fully autonomous because most of the polypeptides required for their functions are encoded in the nuclear genome and are posttranslationally transported from the cytoplasm (or nucleus) into the mitochondria/plastids (reviewed in Logan 2006).

Upon fertilization in angiosperms, male and female gametes unite to form a zygote. The plasma membranes of both gametes are fused through plasmogamy, followed by karyogamy, a fusion of male and female nuclei (Faure et al. 1993; Mogensen and Holm 1995; Ingouff et al. 2007; Ohnishi et al. 2014), and then the parental genomes in zygote nucleus synergistically function toward the progression of zygotic embryogenesis (Meyer and Scholten 2007; Ingouff et al. 2010; Nodine and Bartel 2012; Abiko et al. 2013; Anderson et al. 2017; Chen et al. 2017; Rahman et al. 2019; Zhao et al. 2019). In contrast to the biparental transmission of the nuclear genome to progeny, mitochondria are generally known to be transmitted uniparentally, mainly from the maternal side; in short, only mitochondria existing in the egg cell are transmitted to the progeny (Birky 1995; Sato and Sato 2013; Greiner et al. 2015).

The modes of mitochondrial inheritance in angiosperms are categorized as maternal, biparental, and paternal inheritance (Mogensen 1996). This categorization is mainly based on cytological observations of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) in male gametes (mature generative cells/sperm cells) in pollen grains. The absence or presence of mtDNA in male gametes has been mainly utilized as an indicator of maternal inheritance or biparental/paternal inheritance, respectively (Nagata 2010). For example, species showing maternal inheritance of mitochondria and the disappearance of mitochondrial nucleoids in the generative cells of mature pollen grains have been reported from fluorescence microscopy observations of DAPI-stained pollen grains in Arabidopsis, wheat, tobacco, Lilium longiflorum, Nerium indicum, Commelina communis, Tradescantia reflexa, and Cosmos bipinnatus (Miyamura et al. 1987). A massive decrease in mtDNA content during microgametogenesis in the generative cell of mature pollen was also observed by quantifying fluorescence images of mtDNA from Technovit 7100 resin sections using a photonic microscopic system after double-staining with DAPI and 3,3′-dihexyloxacarbocyanine iodide in Arabidopsis, Actinidia deliciosa, Rhododendron mucronatum, Petunia hybrida, Antirrhinum majus, and Medicago sativa (Nagata et al. 1999). Moreover, the degradation of mtDNA during microgametogenesis was quantitatively estimated via immunocytochemical analysis of developing and mature pollen grains of Hordeum vulgare, which showed that 97% of the mtDNA was lost during sperm cell formation (Sodmergen et al. 2002). In addition, semiquantitative PCR analysis of isolated sperm cells provided evidence that the massive degradation of mtDNA during microgametogenesis in Arabidopsis, A. majus, and tobacco results in the formation of sperm cells that possess 0.083, 0.47, and 1.0 copies of mtDNA per cell, respectively (Wang et al. 2010).

In contrast to the intensive investigations of mtDNA levels in male gametes, our knowledge of the fate of paternal mitochondria in zygotes is limited in Arabidopsis. In Arabidopsis, fluorescence microscopic observations of green fluorescent protein (GFP)-labeled mitochondria showed the presence of a mitochondrial structure in the generative cell, where paternal mitochondria lacking mtDNA were delivered into zygotes through fertilization and selectively degraded within 5 h after fertilization (Matsushima et al. 2008).

The presence of mtDNA in generative and sperm cells has also been demonstrated in Cucumis sativus, Oenothera sp., Pharbitis nil, Rhododendron indicum, Trifolium repens, and Vicia sativa (Miyamura et al. 1987). Additionally, an increase in the mtDNA content during microgametogenesis has been reported in Musa acuminata (Nagata et al. 1999), Pelargonium zonale (Nagata et al. 1999; Wang et al. 2010), and Cucumis melo (Wang et al. 2010). Among these plant species, the paternal/biparental transmission of mitochondria has been verified in Musa acuminata (Fauré et al. 1994), Cucumis melo (Havey et al. 1998), Cucumis sativus (Matsuura 1995; Havey 1997), and in Pelargonium zonale (Kuroiwa et al. 1993; Weihe et al. 2009; Apitz et al. 2013); however, the mode of mitochondrial inheritance has not been examined in the other plant species mentioned. Moreover, the fate of paternal mitochondria and mtDNA in zygotes into which paternal mitochondria or mtDNA are delivered upon gamete fusion has not been addressed in all the plant species previously mentioned, and our knowledge of the stability, fate, and possible degradation machinery of functional paternal mitochondria possessing mtDNA during zygote development remains lacking.

Rice, an excellent model plant for monocotyledonous plants and a globally important staple food for the world population (Laik et al. 2014), is a typical example of a plant undergoing maternal inheritance (Yang et al. 2020). Genetic analysis mainly revealed that mtDNA-derived traits, such as cytoplasmic male sterility, are transmitted through female parents (Toriyama 2021). However, to the best of our knowledge, the cytological verification of the existence of mitochondria and mtDNA in sperm cells and precise monitoring of the fate of paternal mitochondria in zygotes after fertilization have not been conducted in rice.

In this study, the presence of mtDNA in the male gametes of rice was first investigated by staining isolated sperm cells with N-aryl pyridocyanine 3 (PC3), a fluorescent probe for DNA staining, and SYBR Green I (SBG) (Uno et al. 2021; Aini et al. 2022), indicating that rice sperm cells possess mitochondria in which nucleoids derived from mtDNA were detected. This suggests that functional mitochondria in rice sperm cells can be delivered into the zygote via gamete fusion and that paternal mitochondria may be involved in the cellular function of rice zygotes and their derivative cells. Therefore, to monitor the fate of paternal mitochondria during the development of rice zygotes, transgenic rice plants expressing mitochondria-targeted green fluorescent protein (mtGFP) or red fluorescent protein (mtRFP) under a ubiquitin promoter were prepared, sperm and egg cells isolated from these rice plants were electrically fused to produce zygotes, and selective and time-lapse observations of paternal and maternal mitochondria in rice zygotes were conducted. In addition, the possibility of transmission of paternal mitochondria from zygotes into mature plants and the degradation pathways of paternal mitochondria after fertilization were examined to understand the fate of paternal mitochondria in rice.

Materials and methods

Plant materials, isolation of gametes, production of zygotes via gamete fusion, and zygote culture

Oryza sativa L. cv Nipponbare (NB), Oryza sativa L. cv. Kasalath (KS), inter-subspecific hybrid rice plants derived from zygotes between an egg cell from NB with a sperm cell from KS (NBKS hybrids), and transformed rice plants expressing mitochondria-targeted GFP/RFP were grown in an environmental chamber (K30-7248; Koito Industries, Yokohama, Japan) at 26 °C under a 13-h light/11-h dark photoperiod. Egg and sperm cells were isolated from rice flowers, as described by Uchiumi et al. (2006), and zygotes were produced in vitro through the electrofusion of isolated gametes, as described by Uchiumi et al. (2007). The zygotes were cultured using the “droplet culture” method, according to Nakajima et al. (2010). Briefly, the zygotes produced were transferred into a droplet of N6Z liquid medium overlaid with mineral oil (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) on a glass-bottom dish (AGC Techno Glass Co., Ltd., Shizuoka, Japan), after which cultured rice cells (Oc line, provided by the RIKEN Bio Resource Centre, Tsukuba, Japan) were transferred into the droplets as feeder cells. Thereafter, zygotes in the droplets were cultured for the indicated time at room temperature (22–26 °C). NBKS hybrid plants were created through in vitro electric fusion of an egg cell from NB with a sperm cell from KS, as previously described (Rahman et al. 2019).

Wheat plants (Triticum aestivum L. cv Fielder) and Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor L. inbred line TX430) plants were grown in an experimental field at Tokyo Metropolitan University in Tokyo, Japan (35°37′11.6″N × 139°23′05.7″E), and sperm cells were isolated from wheat and sorghum flowers according to Onda et al. (2025).

Vector construction and preparation of transformants

Transformed rice plants expressing GFP or RFP fused with mitochondria-targeting sequences derived from Arabidopsis F1-ATPaseγ at the amino terminus were prepared. The plasmid vectors pGreen0029 harboring LAT52 promoter::mtGFP and LAT52 promoter::mtRFP (Matsushima et al. 2008) were gifted by Wataru Sakamoto (Okayama University, Japan). These plasmids were used as templates for PCR amplification of the DNA region encoding mtGFP or mtRFP using the specific primers 5′-TCTAGAATGGCTAATCGTTTCAGATCA-3′ and 5′-GAGCTCTTACTTGTACAGCTCGTCCAT-3′ for mtGFP, or 5′-TCTAGAATGGCTAATCGTTTCAGATCA-3′, and 5′-GAGCTCTTAGGCGCCGGTGGAGTG-3′ for mtRFP. The amplified PCR product was subcloned into the pGEM-T Easy vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), and the plasmid harboring the mtGFP or mtRFP sequence was digested with XbaI and SacI. The excised DNA fragment was subcloned into the XbaI-SacI site of the pIG121-Hm-based Ubi promoter::GUS binary vector (Abiko et al. 2013). The Ubi promoter::mtGFP or Ubi promoter::mtRFP binary vector was used to transform rice plants. Agrobacterium tumefaciens LBA4404 was transformed with the Ubi promoter:mtGFP or Ubi promoter:mtRFP binary vector, and transgenic rice plants were prepared as described by Toki et al. (2006). The resultants transformed rice plants were named mtGFP or mtRFP rice plants.

Fluorescent staining and microscopic observations of isolated rice gametes and in vitro–produced zygotes

The sperm cells isolated from wild-type rice plants, wheat, and sorghum were transferred into 2 μL droplets of 370 mOsmol kg−1 H2O mannitol solution containing 0.1 μM, 1 μM, or 2 μM PC3, or 2 μg ml−1 or 10 μg ml−1 DAPI (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). Within 15 min of incubation, cells were observed using a Nikon C2 confocal microscope (Nikon NIS-Elements AR Ver.450, Tokyo, Japan) or a Nikon superresolution confocal laser microscope system AX with NSPARC (Nikon, Japan) with 561-nm excitation wavelengths and 570- to 620-nm emission wavelengths for PC3, and 405-nm excitation wavelengths and 425- to 475-nm emission wavelengths for DAPI.

The rice gametes isolated from transformed rice plants expressing mtGFP/RFP were transferred into 2 μL droplets of 370 mOsmol kg−1 H2O mannitol solution and observed using a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM 710, Zeiss, Jena, Germany) at 543-nm excitation wavelengths and >560-nm emission wavelengths for mtRFP and at 488-nm excitation wavelengths and 505- to 530-nm emission wavelengths for mtGFP. The rice gametes isolated from transformed rice plants expressing mtGFP were also transferred into 2 μL droplets of 370 mOsmol kg−1 H2O mannitol solution containing 1 μM PC3 (Uno et al. 2021), incubated for 10 min at 22 to 26 °C in the dark, and transferred into fresh mannitol droplets three times for washing as described by Aini et al (2022). The stained gametes were then observed using a Nikon C2 confocal microscope with 561-nm excitation wavelengths and 570- to 620-nm emission wavelengths for PC3, and 488-nm excitation wavelengths and 500- to 550-nm emission wavelengths for mtGFP.

Costaining of sperm cells isolated from wild-type rice plants was conducted by transferring the isolated sperm cells into a droplet of 370 mOsmol kg−1 H2O mannitol solution containing 0.1 μM PC3 and 1/10,000 SBG (Takara, Tokyo, Japan) or 0.1 μM PC3 and 1 μg mL−1 MitoTracker® Red CM-H2 XRos (MTR; Molecular Probes, Eugene, Oregon, USA) and 1/10,000 SBG. Within 15 min of incubation, cells were observed using a Nikon superresolution confocal laser microscope system AX with NSPARC with 488-nm excitation wavelengths and 500- to 550-nm emission wavelengths for SBG, and 561-nm excitation wavelengths and 570- to 620-nm emission wavelengths for PC3 and MTR.

To estimate the number of mitochondria in a sperm cell, sperm cells isolated from a transformed rice plant expressing mtGFP were observed using a Nikon C2 confocal microscope with 488-nm excitation wavelengths and 500- to 550-nm emission wavelengths, and Z-stack images were obtained. Thereafter, the mitochondrial number at each layer was counted manually to avoid double counting the same mitochondria in the other layers and to fully count the estimated number of mitochondria in the whole sperm cell.

To monitor the dynamics of sperm-derived mitochondrial number in the developing rice zygotes, sperm cells expressing mtGFP were fused with egg cells expressing mtRFP or wild-type egg cells, and the resultant zygotes were observed at the indicated times (1–6 h after gamete fusion) using a Nikon C2 confocal microscope with 561-nm excitation wavelengths and 570 to 620-nm emission wavelengths for mtRFP, and 488-nm excitation wavelengths and 500 to 550-nm emission wavelengths for mtGFP. Z-stack images were obtained, and the paternal mitochondrial number at each layer was counted manually to avoid double counting the same mitochondria in the other layers and to fully count the estimated number of paternal mitochondria in the whole zygote.

Processing of fluorescence microscopy images of egg cells, sperm cells, and zygotes

Fluorescent images obtained from confocal microscopy observations were processed using Fiji (https://imagej.net/software/fiji/; GNU General Public License) software (Schindelin et al. 2012) and adjusted to the same position in the frame using GIMP 2.10 (https://www.gimp.org/downloads/; GNU General Public License).

Statistical analysis and data visualization of paternal mitochondrial number

The number of paternal mitochondria in the sperm cells and the zygotes was counted manually from the z-stack confocal images as previously mentioned. Data of paternal mitochondria in the zygotes were statistically analyzed using ANOVA and Tukey's honest significant difference in RStudio 4.2.2 (https://cran.r-project.org/) or a paired t test in Microsoft Excel. The detailed analysis procedures were presented in the legends for Figs 3–7. All the obtained data were further processed using the tidyiverse package in RStudio 4.2.2 for visualization.

Preparation of genomic DNA for whole-genome resequencing

Leaf samples were collected from the NB, KS, and NBKS hybrid plants (lines A3, B4, and C2, respectively). Approximately 200 mg of leaf material was ground in liquid nitrogen, and the resulting powder was transferred and redissolved in the lysis buffer provided in the NucleoSpin Plant II kit (Macherey-Nagel, Clonetech, CA, USA). Genomic DNA was isolated following the manufacturer's instructions, and the quality and quantity of the isolated genomic DNA were verified using a Nanodrop 2000 (Fisher Scientific, Hampton, USA). Genomic library construction was performed using a Nextera DNA Flex Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. Thereafter, the obtained genomic libraries were purified using Agencourt AMPure XP (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) and subjected to quantity and quality determination using a Qubit 3 Fluorometer with the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) and the Agilent 2100 BioAnalyzer with a high-sensitivity DNA chip (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). After checking the quality and quantity of the purified libraries, 150-bp paired-end reads were generated on an Illumina HiSeq X platform (Illumina) with an average depth of approximately 20-fold coverage for each sample at Macrogen-Japan (Tokyo, Japan).

Processing and analyses of data obtained from genome sequencing

The Illumina read sequences obtained were evaluated using FastQC (v0.11.7; Cock et al. 2010), and adapter and low-quality sequences were trimmed using Trimmomatic (0.38; Bolger et al. 2014) with the following parameters: leading = 20, tailing = 20, sliding window = 4:15, and minlen = 36. The processed reads (trimmed sequence reads) were mapped to the rice mitochondrial reference genome sequences (https://rapdb.dna.affrc.go.jp/download/irgsp1.html) using BWA (o.7.17-r1188; Li and Durbin 2009), and duplicate reads were removed using the Picard toolkit (version 2.18.11; https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). Picard was used to generate an interval list, excluding N bases and repeats in the reference sequence, and the resulting file was used for mutation detection using SAMtools. Single-nucleotide variant calling (single-nucleotide polymorphisms [SNPs] and insertion-deletion extraction) was performed using SAMtools (1.9; http://www.htslib.org/doc/1.9/samtools.html). Finally, genome annotation was performed using snpEff (Cingolani et al. 2012), and all annotated samples were integrated for further analysis.

Treatment of zygotes with inhibitors of cysteine proteinase, proteasomes, or autophagy

E64-c (Peptide Institute, Inc., Osaka, Japan) was dissolved in DMSO in a 10 mM stock solution; 3-methyladenine (3-MA; Funakoshi Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was dissolved with H2O into a 100 mM stock solution; MG132 and Lactacystin (Peptide Institute, Inc., Osaka, Japan) were dissolved in DMSO and H2O, respectively, to create 10 mM stock solutions. The rice zygotes produced via fusion of wild-type rice egg cells and rice sperm cells expressing mtGFP were transferred into a droplet of N6Z liquid medium containing MG132 (10, 50, and 75 μM), Lactacystin (1, 5, and 10 μM), E64-c (1, 10, and 100 μM), or 3-MA (1, 5, 10, and 15 mM), and the paternal mitochondria in zygotes at 1 or 1 to 2 HAF and 6 or 5 to 6 HAF were observed as described in the previous discussion. Z-stack images were obtained, and the paternal mitochondrial number at each layer was counted manually to avoid double counting the same mitochondria in the other layers and to fully count the estimated number of paternal mitochondria in the whole zygote.

Ultrastructural observations of egg cells and zygotes

Isolated rice egg cells were embedded in a droplet of mannitol solution (370 mOsmol kg−1 H2O) solidified with 2% low-melting agarose (Sigma Type IX-A), and the embedded egg cells were fixed with 2% glutaraldehyde in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) and mannitol solution (370 mOsmol kg−1 H2O) for 13 h at 4 °C. After washing with mannitol solution containing 50 mM potassium phosphate (pH 7.4), the embedded egg cells were postfixed with 2% osmium tetroxide (OsO4) in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) and mannitol solution (370 mOsmol kg−1 H2O) for 2 h at room temperature. Rice zygotes were produced as previously noted and embedded in a droplet of N6Z medium solidified with 2% low-melting agarose. The embedded zygotes were fixed with 2% glutaraldehyde in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) and mannitol solution (450 mOsmol kg−1 H2O) for 17 h at 4 °C. After washing with mannitol solution containing 50 mM potassium phosphate (pH 7.4), the embedded zygotes were postfixed with 2% OsO4 in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) and mannitol solution (450 mOsmol kg−1H2O) for 2 h at room temperature. After postfixation, embedded egg cells and zygotes were washed with 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) and further fixed with 2% OsO4 in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 3 h at room temperature. The samples were washed with 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer, dehydrated through a graded methanol series (25%, 50%, 75%, 90%, 100%), infiltrated with increasing concentrations of Epon 812 resin (TAAB) mixed with propylene oxide (ratios of 3:1, 1:1, 1:3), embedded in 100% Epon812, and finally polymerized at 60 °C for 72 h. Semithin sections (500 nm thick) were cut using an ultramicrotome (UC Enuity, Leica Microsystems) equipped with a diamond knife (Histo, Diatome). Sections were mounted onto glass slides and stained with 0.4% uranyl acetate solution for 20 min, followed by lead stain solution (18-0875, Sigma-Aldrich) for 2 min. Prior to observation, sections were coated with osmium for 3 s using an osmium coater (HPC-1SW; Vacuum Device). Observation was performed using a field emission scanning electron microscope (SU8600, Hitachi High-Tech, Japan) equipped with an out-column crystal-type backscattered electron detector operated at 5 kV.

Results

Mitochondrial nucleoids were detected in rice sperm cells

When rice sperm cells isolated from a wild-type plant were stained with PC3 at 1 μM or 0.1 μM, small foci possibly derived from mitochondrial nucleoids were detected in the cytoplasm of the sperm cells around the nucleus (Fig. 1a). PC3 is a long-wavelength absorptive DNA-binding dye containing a pyridocyanin backbone and has great cell permeability and exceptionally high DNA specificity over RNA in living cells. Importantly, this dye can be used to stain the nuclear and organellar DNA separately by adjusting the concentration (Uno et al. 2021). In case of rice sperm cell, nuclear DNA was clearly visualized by PC3 staining at the concentration of 2 μM (Fig. 1a). When isolated rice sperm cells were stained with DAPI, a conventional DNA staining dye, at 2 μg ml−1 or 10 μg ml−1, strong DAPI signal was detectable in the nucleus although small foci possibly derived from mitochondrial nucleoids was not detected (Fig. 1b). This staining profile will be explained by the differences of DNA amount presented between the nucleus and mitochondrial nucleoid and consistent with the staining profile of living Japanese medaka sperms, whose mitochondrial nucleoids were not visualized with DAPI staining but with SBG staining (Nishimura et al. 2006). The presence of mtDNA in the rice sperm cell was further confirmed by costaining the sperm cells with PC3 and SBG (Uno et al. 2021; Aini et al. 2022) at a concentration of 0.1 μM and 1/10,000, respectively. The result showed overlapping signals from both DNA staining dyes in the cytoplasm (Fig. 1c). This suggests that mtDNA is not degraded but is relatively stable during microgametogenesis in rice and that sperm mitochondria can be transferred into zygotes via gamete fusion on fertilization. Therefore, to trace the fate of the sperm-derived mitochondria inside the zygote, transgenic rice plants expressing mtGFP or mtRFP were generated as described in the Materials and Methods.

White arrowheads in c) indicate mitochondrial nucleoids.

Mitochondria with or without nucleoids coexisted in rice sperm cells

Transgenic rice plants expressing GFP and RFP tagged with a mitochondrial targeting signal under the ubiquitin promoter were produced, and mitochondria in sperm and egg cells isolated from the flowers of the transformed plants were successfully visualized (Fig. 2a, ba, bb, bf, and bg). When sperm cells expressing mtGFP were stained with PC3, mitochondrial nucleoids were detected (Fig. 2bc). In addition to nucleoid-containing mitochondria, mitochondria lacking DNA (nucleoids) were also observed (Fig. 2bd, Be), suggesting that mitochondria with or without nucleoids coexist in rice sperm cells. In contrast, when egg cells expressing mtGFP were stained with PC3, almost all mitochondria in the egg cells contained 1 or more nucleoids (Fig. 2bf–bj). The coexistence of mitochondria with and without nucleoids in rice sperm cells was validated by the results of costaining the sperm cells with 1 μg ml−1 MTR and 1/10,000 SBG. Green fluorescence-derived SBG was detectable within several of the red fluorescence-derived MTR (Fig. 2c). This suggests that the degradation of mtDNA progresses in a certain proportion of mitochondria in sperm cells or generative cells during microgametogenesis and that the remaining mitochondria harboring nucleoids exist in rice sperm cells as putatively functional mitochondria.

Paternal mitochondria were massively and selectively eliminated in rice zygotes during early developmental stages

To precisely track paternal mitochondrial fate and behavior in rice zygotes, sperm cells expressing mtGFP were fused with egg cells expressing mtRFP, and time-lapse observations of the zygotes were conducted until 6 HAF. In this study, time-lapse observation of the produced zygotes was limited to 6 HAF to clearly distinguish GFP-labeled paternal mitochondria from RFP-labeled maternal mitochondria. In rice zygotes, karyogamy was completed at approximately 4 HAF, and de novo gene expression was actively initiated on or after the completion of karyogamy (Ohnishi et al. 2014). Therefore, transgenes encoding mtGFP and mtRFP were transcribed from the paternal and maternal nuclear genomes, respectively, and newly synthesized mtGFP/RFP proteins can be transported into all mitochondria in zygotes, resulting in indistinguishable paternal and maternal mitochondria. Most mitochondria in the zygotes at 7 and 9 HAF were labeled with both GFP and RFP (Figure S1).

In zygotes at a very early developmental stage (1 and 2 HAF), GFP-labeled small foci representing sperm-derived mitochondria were abundantly detected (Fig. 3a; Video S1a). These paternal mitochondria were dispersed throughout the cytoplasm of the zygotes. At these stages, fusion between the paternal and maternal mitochondria did not appear to progress in the zygotes, as mitochondria labeled with both GFP and RFP were not detected. In zygotes at 3 and 4 HAF, paternal mitochondria were less observable than those at 1 and 2 HAF (Fig. 3a). This suggests that the degradation of paternal mitochondria occurred gradually. Only a small number of paternal mitochondria were detected in 5- and 6-HAF zygotes (Fig. 3a; Video S1b).

To estimate the number of paternal mitochondria in a sperm cell prior to gamete fusion, z-stack images of rice sperm cells expressing mtGFP were acquired, and the green-dotted signals representing mitochondria were quantified. A large amount of GFP-labeled mitochondria was observed in the rice sperm cells (Fig. 3b; Video S2), with estimated number ranging from 44 to 79, and an average of approximately 60 mitochondria per cell (Fig. 3c). Using the same quantification approach, paternal mitochondrial number in rice zygotes were subsequently counted at the indicated time points to monitor the elimination of paternal mitochondria in the developing rice zygotes, and the temporal changes in paternal mitochondrial number are presented in Fig. 3d. In the zygote at 1 HAF, the number of sperm-derived mitochondria was comparable to that observed in the sperm cells (Fig. 3c, d). These findings suggest that paternal mitochondria are not massively eliminated in zygotes at 1 HAF and that paternal mitochondrial numbers in 1 HAF zygotes varied widely, ranging from 20 to 80, reflecting the differences in mitochondrial number of an individual sperm cell that was fused with an egg cell to form a zygote.

The present time-lapse observation of paternal mitochondria in developing zygotes showed that the paternal mitochondria were massively degraded between 1 and 3 HAF (ANOVA with post hoc Tukey's honestly significant difference test, P < 0.05). Thereafter, paternal mitochondria continued to degrade until 6 HAF (Fig. 3d). In contrast to paternal mitochondria, maternal mitochondria labeled with mtRFP were stably detected during the course of observation (Fig. 3a and Figure S2, left panels). In addition, mtGFP-labeled maternal mitochondria were also stably detected in the fused egg between an egg cell expressing mtGFP and 1 expressing mtRFP at 6 HAF (Figure S2, right panels). These differences in stability between paternal and maternal mitochondria in the zygote suggest that the degradation or elimination of mitochondria in rice zygotes selectively progresses to paternal mitochondria. Although most paternal mitochondria disappeared during the early zygotic stage, a few paternal mitochondria were still detectable within the zygote at 6 HAF. However, the fate of the remaining paternal mitochondria after 6 HAF was untraceable (Figure S1). Therefore, it is still questionable whether the remaining paternal mitochondria are degraded in later developmental stages in zygotes or stably exist in zygotes and pass onto the offspring.

Paternal mitochondria-derived SNPs were not detected in the mtDNA of NBKS inter-subspecific hybrid plants

Owing to the limitations of the cytological observation of paternal mitochondria in zygotes, another approach was attempted to assess the possible retention of paternal mtDNA in the offspring. Japonica rice cultivar NB egg cells were fused with aus rice cultivar KS sperm cells, and the resultant NBKS hybrid zygotes were cultured and regenerated into mature NBKS hybrid plants. Genomic DNA from the leaves of the 3 NBKS hybrid lines (NBKS-A3, -B4, and -C2) and both parental lines (NB and KS) was sequenced, and the SNPs derived from KS (paternal) mtDNA in the NBKS hybrid mitochondrial genome were examined. After setting the SNP positions from the KS mitochondrial genome, sequence reads from the NBKS hybrids were separated into KS or NB origins. When the sequence reads from the 3 NBKS hybrid lines were mapped onto the SNP positions, all sequence reads were mapped to NB-derived SNPs (Table 1). This result suggests that the mtDNA SNPs from the paternal side are not retained in the leaf tissue that developed from hybrid NBKS zygotes and that the paternal mitochondria detected in 6 HAF zygotes were strictly degraded in zygotes or after the zygotic stages in rice. In addition, this result indicates that the estimation of mitochondrial inheritance type based on the existence of nucleoids in paternal mitochondria (Nagata 2010) is not applicable in rice.

Paternal mitochondria in rice zygotes are eliminated through the autophagic pathway

The lack of paternal mtDNA-derived SNPs in mature rice plants indicates that all paternal mitochondria were potentially selectively eliminated through a degradation mechanism. In animals such as Drosophila (Politi et al. 2014), Caenorhabditis elegans (Al Rawi et al. 2011; Sato and Sato 2011; Sasaki and Sato 2021), mice (Al Rawi et al. 2011; Rojansky et al. 2016), and pigs and Rhesus monkeys (Song et al. 2016), it has been reported that paternal mitochondria were likely to be eliminated through a lytic compartment such as lysosomes via mitophagy/autophagy possibly due to the large size of the organelle. However, other mechanisms, such as the ubiquitin-proteasome system, may also be involved in degrading the paternal mitochondria in zygotes (Sato and Sato 2013; Song et al. 2016). However, the elimination of paternal mitochondria in zygotes after fertilization has not been well investigated in plant species, including rice. This lack of knowledge regarding the mechanism of paternal mitochondrial degradation is mainly due to the difficulty of accessing the zygote, which arises and develops in the embryo sac that is deeply embedded in the ovary. Therefore, in the present study, in vitro fertilization (IVF)-produced zygotes were used to investigate the possible degradation mechanism of rice paternal mitochondria during the early developmental stages of zygotes.

To suppress the proteinase activity in lytic compartments (vacuoles) in zygotes, rice zygotes produced through the fusion of sperm cells expressing mtGFP and egg cells from wild-type rice plants were cultured with E64-c, a proteinase inhibitor that irreversibly inhibits the activity of cysteine proteinases by covalently binding to the sulfhydryl groups in the active site of the proteinase (Tamai et al. 1986) at concentrations of 1, 10, and 100 μM, and the number of paternal mitochondria in the zygotes was counted at 1 to 2 and 5 to 6 HAF. Most paternal mitochondria were barely detected in E64-c-treated zygotes at all concentrations at 5 to 6 HAF (Fig. 4a; Video S3). The number of paternal mitochondria in all E64-c-treated zygotes at 5 to 6 HAF was also significantly lower than that in the treated zygotes at 1 to 2 HAF at all concentrations (paired t-test, P < 0.05), which was equivalent to the number of paternal mitochondria in the zygotes without E64-c treatment at 5 to 6 HAF (ANOVA with post hoc Tukey's honestly significant difference test, P < 0.05; Fig. 4b), suggesting that E64-c has little effect on the stabilization of paternal mitochondria within the zygotes.

This observation can be attributed to 2 factors. First, paternal mitochondria are degraded by alternative degradation processes, such as the proteasome pathway. Another possibility is that although GFP-labeled paternal mitochondria are transported into the lytic compartment by the autophagic pathway and paternal mitochondria exist in the lytic compartment, the GFP-derived fluorescent signal disappeared due to the effect of acidic pH in the vacuoles. Autophagosomes containing paternal mitochondria fuse with vacuoles, and the environmental conditions around the paternal mitochondria in autophagic bodies become acidic (Moriyasu and Ohsumi 1996). This acidic condition may cause the quenching of GFP fluorescence signals derived from paternal mitochondria within these cellular vesicles. The intensity of GFP fluorescence is pH-sensitive; the fluorescence of wild-type GFP is stable at pH 6 to 10 and decreases at a pH lower than 6 (reviewed in Campbell and Choy 2001). Therefore, we examined the effects of 2 types of proteasome inhibitors: MG132, a potent, reversible, and widely used inhibitor of the 26S proteasome (Lee and Goldberg 1998; Costanzi et al. 2021), and Lactacystin, a more selective and specific proteasome inhibitor (Kisselev and Goldberg 2001), on the stability of paternal mitochondria in rice zygotes. Interestingly, the stability of paternal mitochondria remained unchanged after treatment with these inhibitors (Figs. 5 and 6; Videos S4 and S5), suggesting that the proteasome-dependent pathway is not involved in the elimination of paternal mitochondria.

Thereafter, to investigate the second possibility, autophagy was inhibited at an earlier stage by treating rice zygotes with 3-MA, a phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) inhibitor that blocks autophagosome formation (Wu et al. 2010). In 3-MA-treated zygotes at 5 to 6 HAF, GFP-derived foci from paternal mitochondria were visible, and paternal mitochondria were abundantly detected in zygotes treated with 3-MA at concentrations of 10 or 15 mM (Fig. 7a; Video S6). Even though the number of paternal mitochondria was statistically different between 1 to 2 and 5 to 6 HAF at all tested 3-MA concentrations (paired t-test, P < 0.05), paternal mitochondrial number at 5 to 6 HAF confirmed that 3-MA stabilizes the paternal mitochondria in the developing rice zygotes in a concentration-dependent manner and that the numbers of paternal mitochondria in zygotes treated with 10 and 15 mM 3-MA at 5 to 6 HAF are significantly higher compared with the number of paternal mitochondria at 5 to 6 HAF zygotes without 3-MA treatment (ANOVA with post hoc Tukey's honestly significant difference test, P < 0.05; Fig. 7b). These results suggest that paternal mitochondria in rice zygotes are eliminated through the autophagy pathway.

Furthermore, to examine the effect of 3-MA on the development of rice zygotes, rice zygotes produced by fusion of sperm cell expressing mtGFP with egg cell wild-type were treated with 3-MA at 10 and 15 mM and cultured for 42 h. Although mtGFP signals, which were derived from karyogamy and subsequent de novo expression of mtGFP transgene, were detectable in the cultured zygote, the zygotes showed developmental arrest without division profile (Figure S3). These suggest that the autophagy-dependent cellular events including the post-fertilization paternal mitochondria clearance will be required for zygotic development.

In addition to the pharmacological test using 3-MA on the stability of paternal mitochondria in the zygotes, ultrastructural observation of rice egg cells and zygotes was conducted for investigating the possibility whether autophagic pathway functions in the possible elimination of paternal mitochondria. Observation of resin sections of fixed rice zygotes and egg cells under a field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM) showed that autophagic bodies were abundantly detected in the zygotes and mitochondria appear to be engulfed within possible autophagosome, although autophagic body was hardly detectable in the egg cell (Fig. 8). These suggest that autophagy pathway is activated in zygotes after fertilization and function in the degradation of paternal mitochondria.

Discussion

Mitochondrial nucleoids with a coccoid shape were abundant in rice sperm cells stained with PC3 and/or SBG (Fig. 1a, c), and the mitochondria visualized using mitochondria-tag GFP or MTR mainly contained the nucleoids (Fig. 2bd–be, cd–ce). Even though rice is known to inherit their mitochondria maternally (Yang et al. 2020), the amount of mitochondrial nucleoids in a rice sperm cell appeared to be larger compared with the amount of mtDNA in sperm cells of typical plants showing maternal inheritance, in which the mitochondria and/or mtDNA mostly disappears, such as in Hordeum vulgare (Sodmergen et al. 2002), Arabidopsis, tobacco (Wang et al. 2010), wheat, Lilium longiflorum, Nerium indicum, Commelina communis, Tradescantia reflexa, Cosmos bipinnatus (Miyamura et al. 1987), Actinidia deliciosa, Rhododendron mucronatum, Petunia hybrida, Medicago sativa (Nagata et al. 1999), and Antirrhinum majus (Nagata et al. 1999; Wang et al. 2010). Judging from this apparent and typical feature of paternal mitochondria and mtDNA of maternal inheritance-type plants, our fundamental observation of mitochondria in rice sperm cells revealed a unique mitochondrial feature of abundant mtDNA among plant species of the possible/putative maternal inheritance type. Therefore, to examine whether detection of mtDNA in sperm cells by PC3 staining is limited to rice or detectable in sperm cells from other plant species, sperm cells isolated from wheat and sorghum flowers were subjected to PC3 and DAPI staining. Interestingly, staining of wheat and sorghum sperm cells showed a similar pattern to those observed in rice sperm cells (Figure S4), suggesting that DAPI staining alone may not always provide sufficient sensitivity to detect these small amounts of mtDNA, and that retention of mtDNA may not be unique but a common feature in plants, particularly in cereal crops.

After entering zygotes (fused egg cells) through gamete fusion, the paternal mitochondria become massively degraded at the very early stage of zygotic development in rice (Fig. 3; Video S1). This phenomenon of rapid and effective elimination of paternal mitochondria in zygotes at very early developmental stages has not been reported in angiosperms. However, the fate and dynamics of paternal mitochondria in zygotes are fundamental in the fields of sexual reproduction and developmental biology in plants because the inheritance mode of cytoplasmic organelles is highly related to the genome composition of the progeny. In this study, a rice IVF system was successfully utilized, providing the precise number of paternal mitochondria in whole sperm cells and zygotes just after gamete fusion by acquiring z-stack images of isolated rice sperm cells and in vitro–produced zygotes with a confocal microscope. In addition to the selective and massive degradation of paternal mitochondria in rice zygotes at the early developmental stage (1–6 HAF), paternal mitochondria continued to be detected until 6 HAF, although the number of mitochondria detected was small. Further investigations were then conducted to examine the possible partial retention and transmission of paternal mtDNA into progenies using SNP-based genomic sequencing of NBKS rice hybrid plants. Undetected sequence reads containing SNPs from paternal mtDNA (Table 1) indicated that paternal mitochondria and mtDNA are completely eliminated in zygotes at mid- or late developmental stages or after the zygotic stage and that the mitochondria in rice are strictly inherited from the mother, consistent with a previous report (Yang et al. 2020). Our findings revealed that the estimation of mitochondrial inheritance type based on the existence of nucleoids in the mitochondria of male gametes (Nagata 2010) was not the case in rice, in which paternal mitochondria possessing relatively abundant mtDNA were rapidly eliminated in zygotes. Similar elimination events have been reported in plastids of Pisum sativum, where a cytological approach involving the detection of plastid DNA (ptDNA) stained with DAPI and detected using a fluorescence microscope suggested the possible biparental inheritance of plastids; however, restriction fragment length polymorphism analysis showed that only ptDNA from the maternal side existed in the F1 Progeny (Polans et al. 1990).

In rice, paternal mitochondria and their nucleoids are partially degraded during microgametogenesis, resulting in paternal mitochondria with or without nucleoids in mature sperm cells. In Arabidopsis, the elimination of the paternal mitochondrial nucleoid is completed before pollen maturation (Miyamura et al. 1987), leaving mitochondrial structures lacking nucleoids present in mature sperm cells (Matsushima et al. 2008). The delivery of mitochondria with or without nucleoids from the paternal side (rice and Arabidopsis types) to female gametes through fertilization appears to be recognized by the degradation machinery that targets these cytoplasmic organelles for elimination upon entering the female gamete. In tobacco, organellar DNA is generally excluded from the generative cells during pollen mitosis I (Hagemann and Schröder 1989; Chung et al. 2023). Moreover, the nucleoid (organellar DNA) is not detectable at the bicellular pollen grain stage (Miyamura et al. 1987). However, the paternal leakage of organellar DNA, such as ptDNA, has been reported in tobacco (Yu et al. 1994; Ruf et al. 2007; Svab and Maliga 2007), and chilling stress has been reported as 1 of the causes of plastid inclusion into generative cells in tobacco, resulting in the transmission of ptDNA from the paternal side to the progeny (Chung et al. 2023). This suggests that the uncommon delivery of paternal plastids to zygotes through fertilization in tobacco results in the failure of their complete elimination because of the lack of a developed degradation system in tobacco zygotes. These differences in the elimination system for paternal cytoplasmic organelles may be explained by the heterochrony in the timing of the elimination or degradation of cytoplasmic organelles (DNA) during reproductive processes.

The massive elimination of paternal mitochondria in rice zygotes and the nondetection of paternal mitochondria-derived SNPs in inter-subspecific rice hybrids generate questions regarding the degradation mechanism of rice paternal mitochondria after fertilization. Therefore, we performed further investigations since the IVF system allows us to test the effects of various proteinase and proteasome inhibitors on the possible degradation processes. Interestingly, the massive elimination of paternal mitochondria was not halted in rice zygotes treated with cysteine proteinase inhibitors, such as E64-c (Fig. 4; Video S3). However, blocking autophagy by interrupting the formation of autophagosomes on 3-MA treatment effectively preserved the paternal mitochondria in zygotes in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 7; Video S6). Moreover, electron microscopy observation by the section SEM method revealed the presence of autophagic bodies containing engulfed mitochondria in the zygotes, whereas no such structure was visible in the egg cells (Fig. 8). The possible involvement of an alternative degradation pathway, the ubiquitin-proteasome system, was also tested through MG132 and Lactacystin treatments of zygotes; however, these 2 inhibitors did not effectively prevent the elimination of rice paternal mitochondria (Figs. 5 and 6; Videos S4 and S5). These results confirmed that autophagy is likely the main degradation machinery for eliminating paternal mitochondria in rice zygotes. However, the possibility that autophagy-independent degradation pathway involves in degradation of paternal mitochondria cannot be excluded, as degradation of paternal mitochondria in zygotes was not completely inhibited by 3-MA treatment but partially proceeded (Fig. 7b).

Taken together, the present investigations on the fate and dynamics of paternal mitochondria in rice zygotes using an IVF system indicate that paternal mitochondria with or without nucleoids are selectively eliminated in rice zygotes at a very early developmental stage through autophagy. Moreover, the results obtained in this study will provide fundamental knowledge for elucidating the molecular machinery for the preferential elimination of paternal mitochondria in zygotes and the subsequent maternal inheritance of mitochondria in plants. Nonetheless, further investigation of the genetic factors that responsible for the recognition of paternal mitochondria in the rice zygotes and their subsequent targeting for elimination by RNA sequence and transcriptomic analysis would be critical to complement the current information and thus elucidate the detailed elimination process of paternal mitochondria after fertilization. In addition, precise monitoring of the developmental profile of rice progeny that maintain mitochondria from the paternal side and investigation of the consequences of the presence of paternal mitochondria would be imperative issues to be addressed.
