Chromosome-level genome assembly of the small-sized Taihang donkey (Equus asinus)
Authors: Yuanyuan Li, Mo Feng, Yu Liu, Kaibo Guo, Xinyu Wang, Tao Yang, Hetong Zhang, Wenhui Xing, Shiyu Qian, Jiahui Wu, Chunjiang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
China harbors a rich diversity of donkey breeds, with small-sized donkeys (<110 cm) representing a largely underexplored group. Here, we present the first high-quality, chromosome-level genome assembly of a small-sized donkey, generated using PacBio HiFi sequencing (286.7 Gb), Hi-C scaffolding (240.47 Gb), and annotated with RNA-seq data. The final assembly has a total length of 2.7 Gb and comprises 32 chromosomes (including both X and Y chromosomes), in which five chromosomes were fully assembled without gaps. It possesses a scaffold N50 of 106.70 Mb and 84 contigs (contig N50 = 63.60 Mb), and captures 99.2% of BUSCO genes. The assembly achieved a consensus quality value (QV) of 77.44, corresponding to an extremely low base-level error rate, indicating exceptional nucleotide accuracy. This high-quality genome provides a valuable resource for investigating genetic variation, adaptive evolution, and domestication processes in small-sized donkeys, and will facilitate the conservation and sustainable utilization of rich donkey genetic resources in China.

Background & Summary

China has a donkey breeding history spanning over 4,000 years and possesses one of the world’s richest genetic diversities in donkeys1. Through long-term adaptation to diverse ecological environments and selective breeding for transportation, farming, and meat production, numerous indigenous donkey breeds have developed. Currently, there are 24 recognized local donkey breeds in China2. Based on body size, these breeds are classified into three categories: large-sized (>130 cm, e.g., Guanzhong, Dezhou, Hetian Gray, Guangling, and Jinnan), medium-sized (110–130 cm, e.g., Yangyuan, Linxian, Qingyang, and Jiami), and small-sized (<110 cm, e.g., Taihang, Sichuan, Qinghai, and Xinjiang). These local breeds are widely distributed across different ecological regions of China, exhibiting remarkable phenotypic and genetic diversity shaped by environmental adaptation and geographic isolation2.

In recent years, the availability of high-quality genome assemblies of large-sized donkeys3,4 has greatly advanced our understanding of the genetic basis underlying important traits in these breeds5,6. However, genomic resources for small-sized donkeys remain extremely limited, which hinders comprehensive comparative genomic studies and the exploration of adaptive evolution among breeds of different body sizes. Generating and analyzing genomic data for small-sized donkeys will therefore provide valuable insights into their unique adaptive features, evolutionary history, and genetic diversity, and will contribute to the conservation and sustainable utilization of China’s rich donkey genetic resources.

In this study, we report the first high-quality, chromosome-level genome assembly of a small-sized indigenous donkey generated using PacBio HiFi circular consensus sequencing (286.7 Gb) and high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) scaffolding (240.47 Gb of raw data), annotated with RNA-seq short reads. The final assembly comprises 30 autosomes and two sex chromosomes (both X and Y) with a scaffold N50 of 106.70 Mb and consists of 84 contigs with a contig N50 of 63.60 Mb. Remarkably, five chromosomes were completely assembled without gaps, indicating outstanding assembly continuity. The assembly also demonstrates exceptional completeness, with 99.2% of BUSCO genes identified, and only 0.6% and 0.2% of genes missing or fragmented, respectively. Collectively, this first small-sized donkey (SSD) genome assembly provides a valuable genomic resource for exploring the specific genetic variations related to body-size traits and offers new insights into their origin, domestication, and evolutionary development.

Methods

Sample collection

A blood sample was collected from a small-sized male adult of the Taihang donkey breed at the Science and Technology Backyard of China Agricultural University, Yanqing District, Beijing. The sample was subsequently subjected to PacBio sequencing, Hi-C sequencing, and transcriptome sequencing. Genomic DNA extraction, genome sequencing, and RNA sequencing were all performed by Frasergen (Frasergen Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations of the ethics committee of China Agricultural University.

DNA and RNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted using a modified cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method. Briefly, whole blood samples were lysed in 4 × CTAB buffer containing β-mercaptoethanol and incubated at 65 °C for 1.5 h. After incubation, the samples were cooled to room temperature, and DNA was extracted with chloroform–isoamyl alcohol (24:1) followed by ethanol precipitation. DNA purity was assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer by measuring A260/280 and A260/230 ratios. DNA concentration was quantified using a Qubit fluorometer, and DNA integrity was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis to ensure high molecular weight DNA for subsequent sequencing. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA purity and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). RNA degradation was further evaluated by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis.

Genome sequencing

The HiFi sequencing was conducted on the PacBio Revio platform, leveraging the SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 2.0 (Pacific Bioscience) for library construction. Original sequencing data were processed via SMRTlink and CCS tools to split subreads and generate HiFi reads, with the standards of ZMW (Zero-Mode Waveguide) >3 and an accuracy consensus sequences >99%. Genome assembly

Filtered HiFi reads were assembled into contigs using hifiasm (v0.21.0-r686), yielding a contig-level genome10. To construct chromosome-level scaffolds, Hi-C reads were aligned to the contigs generated in this study, and the latest telomere-to-telomere gap-free donkey genome (CAU_T2T_donkey) was used as guidance to construct chromosome-level scaffolds using HapHiC(Table 2)11–13. The scaffolds were finally anchored onto 32 chromosomes (30 autosomes plus X and Y), resulting in the SSD assembly with a total length of 2,707,699,689 bp (Fig. 1, Table 3)14. The contig N50 and scaffold N50 were 63.60 Mb and 106.70 Mb, respectively. A few chromosomes were assembled as single contigs, while most consisted of 2–3 contigs and some of 5–6 contigs, indicating good overall continuity with relatively few gaps (Table 3)14. Sequenced Hi-C reads were mapped to the contigs, and the contact matrix was constructed. Hi-C data were used to generate a chromosome-scale contact map with Juicer for assembly validation. The resulting Hi-C contact map (Fig. 1) allowed visualization of chromosomal interactions and detection of potential misassemblies. Based on the contact map, minor manual adjustments were made to resolve a few misjoins or inconsistencies. However, the majority of scaffolds were consistent with Hi-C contacts, and no extensive manual curation was necessary. Traditional k-mer–based genome survey analyses were not performed, as the combination of PacBio HiFi long reads and Hi-C data provided direct and accurate characterization of genome size and chromosomal structure. Assembly quality assessment

The completeness of the assembled genome was assessed using BUSCO(v6.0.0)15 with the mammalia_odb12 dataset, which comprises conserved single-copy orthologs extracted from OrthoDB v1016 as the reference. Overall, 99.2% complete BUSCOs were identified in the SSD assembly (Table 4). In addition, the base-level accuracy of the assembly was evaluated using a k-mer–based approach implemented in Merqury (v1.3)17. K-mers (k = 19) were generated from PacBio HiFi reads using Meryl (v1.4.1)18 and compared with the assembled genome, resulting in a consensus quality value (QV) of 77.44, corresponding to an estimated base error rate of 1.80 × 10−8. Furthermore, the genome sequences mounted by Hi-C were sorted according to the reference genome using NgenomeSyn (v1.41)19 to perform a collinearity analysis. The result indicated good collinearity with the CAU_T2T_donkey genome (Fig. 2). Moreover, we mapped the whole genome resequencing data of 6 small-sized Yunnan donkeys to the SSD genome. The analysis illustrated high mapping quality with an average mapping ratio of 99.94% and an average properly mapping ratio of 99.58% (Table 5). Annotation of repetitive sequences

For homology-based identification, known transposable elements (TEs) were annotated using RepeatMasker (v4.2.1)20 with the Repbase TE database as a reference library20,21. For de novo prediction RepeatModeler (v 2.0.7) was used to construct a genome-specific repeat library, which captured both known and novel repetitive elements including TEs, low-complexity regions, and unclassified repeats by integrating two complementary algorithms, RECON22 and RepeatScout23. Low-complexity repeats, satellites, and simple repeats were also annotated via RepeatMasker20. Finally, using both homology-based (RepeatMasker) and de novo approaches for repeat annotation, we identified 1,084,698,958 bp of repetitive sequences (40.06% of the genome) with RepeatMasker and 1,126,966,458 bp (41.62% of the genome) with de novo prediction, respectively. A total of 2.21 Gb sequences were identified as combined repetitive sequences, accounting for 81.68% of the whole genome length (Table 6). Protein-coding gene annotation

To comprehensively identify protein-coding genes, we annotated the SSD genome using the BRAKER pipeline (v3.0.6)24. BRAKER integrates RNA-Seq–based evidence with protein homology information to generate species-specific gene models by automatically training GeneMark-ETP and AUGUSTUS25. Transcriptome data in FASTQ format were provided to BRAKER to assist model training, while homologous protein sequences from closely related and representative mammalian species were used with ProtHint to support exon boundary identification. The dataset included protein sequences from seven species: horse (Equus caballus, GCA_041296265.1)26, cattle (Bos taurus, GCA_002263795.4)27, sheep (Ovis aries, GCA_016772045.2)28, crab-eating macaque (Macaca fascicularis)29, human (Homo sapiens, GCA_000001405.29)30, mouse (Mus musculus, GCA_000001635.9)31, and cat (Felis catus, GCA_018350175.1)32.

As a result, a total of 17,120 protein-coding genes and 21,281 mRNAs were predicted in the SSD genome. The total CDS length reached 24,583,486 bp, with an average CDS length of 1,436 bp. Functional annotation of protein-coding genes

Functional predictions for the protein-coding genes of the SSD genome were obtained by sequence similarity analyses using BLASTP (v2.6.0+)33,34 against multiple protein and domain databases, including InterPro35, Reactome, eggNOG-mapper36, KEGG37, Swiss-Prot38, and TrEMB38. All alignments were performed using a conservative e-value threshold of 1e−5. Gene Ontology (GO) terms were assigned based on mappings from the InterPro results.

In total, 16,477 genes (96.24%) were functionally annotated by at least one database, while 644 genes (3.76%) remained unannotated (Table 8). The annotation coverage for individual databases was as follows: InterPro, 16,694 (97.51%); GO, 14,069 (82.18%); Reactome, 13,894 (81.16%); eggNOG-mapper, 15,996 (93.43%); KEGG, 14,844 (86.71%); Swiss-Prot, 15,934 (93.07%); TrEMBL, 16,471 (96.21%) (Table 8). Technical Validation

To ensure the high quality of the SSD genome assembly, a comprehensive series of quality control and validation steps were implemented throughout the experimental and computational workflow. Raw PacBio HiFi reads were filtered using fastp (version 0.23.4; https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp)44 to remove low-quality sequences and adapter contamination, ensuring that only high-quality data were retained for assembly. Filtered reads were assembled into contigs using hifiasm (version 0.21.0-r686; https://github.com/chhylp123/hifiasm)10, and chromosome-level scaffolds were generated with HapHiC (version 1.0.7; https://github.com/zengxiaofei/HapHiC)45, guided by Hi-C contact maps. Hi-C data were processed using Juicer (version 1.6; https://github.com/aidenlab/juicer) to construct contact matrices for the detection of potential misjoins. Minor manual adjustments were performed based on the Hi-C contact maps, but the majority of scaffolds showed strong consistency with chromatin interaction signals and therefore required no extensive manual curation. Genome completeness was assessed using BUSCO (version 6.0.0; https://busco.ezlab.org) with the mammalia_odb12 database15. The SSD genome assembly achieved a BUSCO completeness score of 99.2%, including 98.5% single-copy genes and 0.7% duplicated genes, with 29 fragmented and 70 missing BUSCOs (Table 4), indicating a highly complete gene space. Base-level assembly accuracy was evaluated using a k-mer–based approach implemented in Merqury (version 1.3; https://github.com/marbl/merqury)17. In this analysis, k-mers (k = 19) were generated from PacBio HiFi reads using Meryl (version 1.4.1; https://github.com/marbl/meryl)18 and compared with the assembled genome to estimate the consensus quality value (QV). Whole-genome sequences were mapped back to the assembly using BWA (version 0.7.17; https://github.com/lh3/bwa)46,47 with default parameters, achieving an average mapping rate of 99.94% (Table 5), which further validated the accuracy and continuity of the assembly. Protein sequences were functionally annotated using DIAMOND (version 2.1.8; https://github.com/bbuchfink/diamond) against the UniProt Swiss-Prot and TrEMBL protein databases (downloaded from https://www.uniprot.org), as well as InterProScan (version 5.75–106.0; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/interproscan.html)35, Gene Ontology (GO; https://geneontology.org)35, eggNOG-mapper (http://eggnog-mapper.embl.de)36, Reactome (https://reactome.org), and KEGG (https://www.genome.jp/kegg)37. In total, 96.24% of the predicted genes were annotated (Table 8), demonstrating the reliability and completeness of gene annotation.
