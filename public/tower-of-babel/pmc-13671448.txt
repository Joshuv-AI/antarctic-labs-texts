Clinical manufacturing of adipose-derived stem cells for therapeutic use: current technologies, quality systems and production challenges
Authors: Alexandre Asch, Daniel F Kalbermatten, Srinivas Madduri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThis systematic review evaluated manufacturing technologies for adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs), focusing on automation, quality control, potency, scalability, cost, and regulatory compliance.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science were searched for English-language studies published January 2020–September 2025. Studies addressing clinical-grade ADSC manufacturing, quality control, regulatory compliance, or production economics were included; non-ADSC studies, reviews, case reports, and conference abstracts were excluded. Two reviewers qualitatively assessed methodological quality and bias. Heterogeneity precluded meta-analysis, so findings were synthesized narratively.ResultsForty-six studies were included; participant numbers were inconsistently reported, as the review focused on manufacturing processes. Automated bioreactors achieved 2- to 10-fold higher yields than flask-based approaches, with improved reproducibility and reduced contamination risk. Five validated platforms were identified. GMP production costs ranged from $15,000 to $50,000 per dose; automation offered potential reductions of 20%–40%. Key limitations included 3- to 5-fold inter-donor variability, 15%–25% batch-to-batch variation, and 5%–10% manufacturing failure rates.ConclusionsADSC manufacturing is progressing toward scalable production, but clinical translation remains constrained by biological variability, limited potency-assay validation, high costs, and regulatory complexity. Standardization and clinically relevant potency assays are needed to improve consistency and access.KEYWORDS: Allogeneic adipose stem cells, regenerative medicine, manufacturing of adipose-derived stem cells, stem cells, mesenchymal stem cell sources

Introduction

This systematic review aims to identify, describe and critically appraise current clinical manufacturing technologies, quality systems, economic frameworks and regulatory pathways for adipose-derived stem cell (ADSC) therapeutics, in order to define key challenges and opportunities for scalable, high-quality ADSC production.

Adipose-derived stem cells have emerged as a prominent cellular therapeutic platform within regenerative medicine, offering several practical advantages over other mesenchymal stem cell sources. The clinical appeal of ADSC stems from the relative accessibility of adipose tissue through minimally invasive liposuction procedures or surgical resection, including lipectomy and tissue collected during reconstructive or aesthetic procedures, favorable cell yields, and documented therapeutic potential across multiple indications [1]. Over the past two decades, ADSC have progressed from experimental research tools toward clinically validated therapeutic products, though this transition has revealed substantial manufacturing, regulatory, and economic challenges.

In the broader regenerative medicine field, several adult mesenchymal stem cell sources – such as bone marrow, dental pulp, and umbilical cord tissue – have been investigated for therapeutic use. However, the clinical experience with these alternative sources remains considerably more limited than for ADSC. A recent systematic review of allogeneic ADSC clinical trials identified 22 studies including 953 patients and reported consistent safety and signals of efficacy across diverse indications such as skin lesions and chronic ulcers, Crohn’s disease, glandular dysfunction, kidney disease, spinal muscular atrophy, and osteoarthritis. This comparatively extensive and heterogeneous clinical trial portfolio highlights ADSC as one of the most clinically advanced adult stem cell platforms to date and provides a strong rationale for focusing the present work on ADSC and for developing robust manufacturing systems capable of producing therapeutic-grade ADSC at scale while preserving their clinically validated properties [2]. This clinical validation provides strong rationale for developing robust manufacturing systems capable of producing therapeutic-grade ADSC at scale, while maintaining the cellular characteristics responsible for their therapeutic efficacy.

The fundamental advantages of ADSC for clinical manufacturing include the abundance and accessibility of the adipose tissue source, which can be obtained through routine liposuction procedures with minimal patient morbidity. Studies consistently demonstrate cell yields from adipose tissue of 0.2–2.0 × 10^6 nucleated cells per gram of processed tissue, representing a 2–5 fold advantage over bone marrow aspirates [3]. Additionally, ADSC demonstrate favorable expansion characteristics with doubling times of 48–72 hours under optimized culture conditions, enabling relatively rapid cellular expansion to clinically relevant numbers while maintaining phenotypic stability through multiple passages [4].

The immunomodulatory properties of ADSC provide additional therapeutic advantages through coordinated secretion of anti-inflammatory factors including interleu-kin-10, transforming growth factor-β, and indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase [5]. These properties enable potential allogeneic applications, expanding manufacturing possibilities beyond autologous treatments and creating opportunities for centralized production with economies of scale benefits. The clinical success of allogeneic ADSC therapies across di-verse pathological conditions further supports the development of standardized manufacturing approaches capable of producing consistent, high-quality cellular products [2].

Recent clinical successes have provided validation for the therapeutic potential of GMP-manufactured ADSC, with multiple Phase I and II trials demonstrating safety pro-files and preliminary efficacy across various indications [6]. These achievements have simultaneously highlighted the complexity and cost associated with clinical-grade manufacturing, creating significant barriers to widespread adoption and therapeutic accessibility.

The current manufacturing landscape encompasses traditional manual approaches utilizing culture flasks and cell factories, semi-automated systems providing partial process control, and emerging fully automated platforms offering comprehensive process management. While automation presents potential solutions to consistency and scalability challenges, implementation requires substantial capital investment, technical expertise, and comprehensive process validation [7]. Regulatory compliance with Good Manufacturing Practices specifically for ADSC requires sophisticated architectural design of specialized facilities integrating precise and adaptive environmental control systems specifically calibrated to ADSC biological needs, exhaustively validated automated processes for their optimal expansion, comprehensive and predictive quality control methodologies [8].

The development of predictive quality control systems and real-time process monitoring represents an evolving area with significant implications for manufacturing efficiency and regulatory compliance. Traditional quality control approaches based on end-point testing are being supplemented by continuous monitoring and predictive analytics, though standardization and validation of these approaches remain incomplete [8].

Manufacturing costs represent a critical barrier to widespread ADSC therapeutic accessibility, with current GMP-compliant production costs ranging from $15,000–$50,000 per therapeutic dose depending on cell numbers, manufacturing approach, and regulatory requirements. These costs reflect multiple factors including facility infrastructure, equipment, regulatory compliance, quality control testing, and skilled labor requirements [9].

Given the demonstrated clinical efficacy of ADSC across multiple therapeutic areas and the growing demand for these treatments, there is an urgent need for comprehensive evaluation of current manufacturing technologies, identification of persistent challenges, and development of strategies to achieve cost-effective, scalable production while maintaining therapeutic quality and safety standards.

This synthesis is limited by restriction to English-language, peer-reviewed studies indexed in the selected databases between January 2020 and September 2025, by reliance on published data that may preferentially reflect successful or commercially favorable manufacturing programs, and by incomplete reporting of process parameters in several studies, which together may have introduced selection and reporting biases.

Materials and methods

Search strategy and database selection

This systematic review was conducted according to PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines to ensure comprehensive literature coverage and methodological rigor. A systematic literature search was performed to identify clinical studies reporting manufacturing approaches for adipose derived stem/stromal cells (ADSC). We used PubMed as the primary database to develop a comprehensive search strategy combining controlled vocabulary and free text terms related to adipose tissue, mesenchymal stem/stromal cells, and clinical applications. This strategy was applied to studies published between January 2020 and September 2025, a timeframe chosen to capture contemporary manufacturing technologies and the most recent regulatory guidance and technological developments.

Electronic searches were conducted in PubMed, Embase and Web of Science using the same conceptual search strategy, which was systematically translated and adapted to the specific subject headings, field codes and syntax of each database. Searches in all three databases were run between November 2025 and May 2026, with the final update covering records available up to September 2025. Searching multiple databases was intended to broaden coverage and reduce the risk of missing relevant studies by exploiting the complementary indexing and unique content of each source. No restrictions on study design were applied beyond the prespecified eligibility criteria, and reference lists of included articles were screened to identify additional studies. This systematic review was not prospectively registered in a public database, and no separate formal protocol document was prepared.

The search strategy combined controlled MeSH vocabulary terms with free-text keywords to maximize both sensitivity and specificity. The core search strategy utilized the following structure: (“adipose-derived stem cells” OR “adipose stem cells” OR “ADSC” OR “adipose-derived stromal cells” OR “adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells”) AND (“clinical manufacturing” OR “GMP production” OR “bioreactor” OR “automated culture” OR “cell production” OR “bioprocess” OR “manufacturing” OR “scale-up”) AND (“quality control” OR “process optimization” OR “scalable manufacturing” OR “commercial pro-duction” OR “automation”).

Additional search terms were incorporated to capture emerging technologies and regulatory aspects: “single-use bioreactor,” “hollow fiber,” “process analytical technology,” “real-time monitoring,” “regulatory compliance,” “cost analysis,” and “batch variability.” Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT) and truncation symbols were employed systematically to optimize search precision while maintaining comprehensive coverage.

Study selection and eligibility criteria

The PICOS (Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes, Study design) methodological framework was rigorously applied to guide identification and selection of eligible studies, with exclusive focus on ADSC and their manufacturing technologies. The population of interest encompassed technological systems, manufacturing infrastructures, and production processes for ADSC intended for human therapeutic applications under strict regulatory regimes including FDA, EMA, and PMDA oversight, with studies on other types of mesenchymal stem cells being systematically excluded except those including quantitative comparative data specific to ADSC. The interventions examined comprised bioprocess technologies applied to ADSC manufacturing, encompassing bioreactors optimized for ADSC expansion, automated manufacturing platforms, artificial intelligence solutions for process optimization, quality control systems adapted to ADSC biomarkers, and real-time monitoring technologies. These advanced interventions were compared against standard ADSC manufacturing processes, including conventional static flask culture methods, manual cell expansion approaches, and traditional quality control protocols. The outcomes of interest included quantitative efficacy measures such as cell yields, viability, batch-to-batch consistency, ADSC phenotype maintenance, trilineage differentiation capacities, immunomodulatory properties, economic metrics, regulatory compliance indicators, and clinical validation parameters.

Priority was given to high-quality study designs including multicenter randomized controlled trials, prospective cohort studies, multi-site technology validation trials, comparative process studies, and prospective economic analyses, while systematically excluding isolated case reports, short communications without rigorous validation, ret-respective studies with significant bias, and basic research without direct manufacturing application.

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were established as follows: (1) original research articles and technology validation studies specifically addressing ADSC manufacturing processes; (2) studies investigating GMP-compliant or clinical-grade production methodologies; (3) research examining automated manufacturing systems, quality control strategies, or process optimization approaches; (4) comparative studies evaluating different manufacturing technologies or methodological approaches; (5) economic analyses related to ADSC production costs and scalability; and (6) peer-reviewed publications published in English, indexed in PubMed, with full-text availability.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria were defined as: (1) studies investigating other mesenchymal stem cell types without specific ADSC data or comparative analysis; (2) basic research studies lacking direct relevance to manufacturing processes or clinical translation potential; (3) case reports, conference abstracts, and short communications not subjected to formal peer review; (4) studies focused exclusively on preclinical applications without consideration of manufacturing processes or scalability; and (5) reviews, opinion pieces, and commentary articles without original experimental data.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Search results from all databases were uploaded to Rayyan (www.rayyan.ai), a web-based systematic review platform that uses machine-learning–assisted relevance scoring to support semi-automated citation screening. After deduplication, Rayyan’s prediction classifier was applied to the combined citation set to identify records with a very low probability of relevance based on title and abstract content. Records rated as definitely irrelevant according to conservative, predefined thresholds (n = 70) were classified as ineligible by automation tools and removed prior to formal title and abstract screening; these automatically excluded records were not reassessed manually. All remaining records proceeded to manual screening, where two independent reviewers evaluated titles and abstracts in a blinded fashion, rigorously applying the prespecified inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion.

Systematic data extraction was specifically focused on parameters directly relevant to ADSC industrial clinical manufacturing: detailed technical specifications of ADSC expansion processes, quality control methodologies specifically adapted to ADSC, quantitative ADSC performance metrics, detailed economic parameters of ADSC production, regulatory compliance indicators for ADSC-based therapeutic products, and clinical validation data with measurable outcomes.

Data extraction was performed using a standardized template developed for this review, and entries were cross-checked within the review team to ensure internal consistency; when clarifications were required, information was inferred from methods, figures and supplementary materials reported in the original articles.

Methodological quality assessment was conducted using qualitative evaluation frameworks adapted to the highly specialized field of ADSC clinical manufacturing and based on a validated quality assessment tool for stem cell clinical trials incorporating four critical quality dimensions and nine observed quality indicators (participant protection, scientific value, quality control, and stem cell product characteristics). For each included study, we qualitatively appraised the relevance and adequacy of the experimental design for ADSC manufacturing, the statistical robustness of ADSC characterization methods, the rigor and completeness of ADSC-specific process validation, the transparency and quality of statistical analyses, and compliance with international regulatory standards for therapeutic cell products.

Rather than applying a formal domain-based risk-of-bias tool or GRADE certainty grading, these ADSC-specific methodological and regulatory criteria were systematically reviewed by two independent reviewers, and their qualitative judgments were explicitly taken into account when interpreting the strength and generalizability of the evidence base.

Results

Study characteristics and selection

The initial search across three major databases yielded 170 records: PubMed (n = 121), Embase (n = 38), and Web of Science (n = 11). Before screening, 115 records were removed including 32 duplicates, 70 records marked ineligible by automation tools, and 11 for other reasons, leaving 57 records for screening. After initial screening that excluded 6 additional records, 51 reports were sought for full-text retrieval. Five reports could not be retrieved, resulting in 46 reports assessed for eligibility against the systematic review’s inclusion and exclusion criteria. Following eligibility assessment, all 46 studies were ultimately included in the systematic review. This represents a 27% selection rate from the original 170 records identified, demonstrating the stringent quality standards applied throughout the selection process.

The methodology ensures transparency and reproducibility, with clear documentation of each exclusion step, providing readers with confidence in the systematic approach used to build the evidence base for this review.

Owing to conceptual and methodological heterogeneity in ADSC manufacturing platforms, outcome definitions and reporting formats, no formal statistical pooling or meta-analysis was performed; results were synthesized narratively and tabulated qualitatively, with effect sizes reported as described in the original studies without transformation. Current manufacturing infrastructure and environmental control systems

Contemporary GMP-compliant ADSC manufacturing facilities demonstrate convergence toward standardized environmental control systems specifically adapted to cellular production requirements. Critical manufacturing operations consistently occur in Grade A environments (ISO Class 5, ≤3,520 particles ≥ 0.5 μm per cubic meter) to prevent microbial, particulate, and chemical contamination that could compromise cellular viability or introduce safety risks [10,11]. Temperature control maintains precise conditions at 37.0°C ±0.2°C to preserve metabolic activity and cellular viability throughout manufacturing processes. CO2 concentration is rigorously controlled at 5.0% ± 0.1% to maintain physiological pH in culture media, while relative humidity is regulated between 45–55% to prevent media desiccation while minimizing fungal contamination risks [12].

Advanced environmental monitoring systems provide continuous real-time assessment of air quality, particulate levels, temperature and humidity fluctuations, and detection of volatile contaminants. These systems incorporate automated alert mechanisms and data logging capabilities to ensure comprehensive documentation for regulatory compliance and process troubleshooting [13].

Modern facilities increasingly incorporate isolator technologies and barrier systems to create absolute physical separation between operators and cellular products while maintaining controlled environmental conditions. These systems enable sterile manipulation procedures while reducing contamination risks associated with personnel entry into critical manufacturing areas.

Automated manufacturing systems and technology platforms

Five major automated platforms have achieved validation for ADSC expansion and are commercially available for GMP-compliant manufacturing: the Quantum Cell Expansion System (Terumo BCT), CliniMACS Prodigy (Miltenyi Biotec), NANT 001/XL system, CellQualia platform, and various single-use bioreactor systems from multiple manufacturers [10].

NANT 001 system validation results

The NANT 001 bioreactor system has been extensively validated for automated ADSC production under GMP conditions. Validation studies demonstrate production of 53.5 ± 16.6 × 10^6 ADSC from 10 mL of adipose tissue over an average of 10 days, with >90% viability and compliance with International Society for Cellular Therapy guidelines. Comparative studies between automated NANT 001 processing and manual expansion methods show equivalent cell yields, phenotypic characteristics, and differentiation capacity, while providing substantial reductions in contamination risk and manual labor requirements. The system enables processing in Grade D environments due to closed-system design, reducing facility infrastructure requirements compared to open manual processing [14].

Large-scale bioreactor systems

Automated closed industrial scale cell production (ACISCP) platforms utilizing GMP-grade microcarriers demonstrate capability for substantial scale expansion. Three-stage expansion protocols using 1 L, 5 L, and 15 L bioreactors achieve final yields of 2.09 × 10^10 cells with overall expansion factors of 1975-fold within 13 days. These systems employ fully closed processing with automated harvesting, concentration, washing, and preparation procedures. Critical quality attributes including sterility, safety, purity, viability, identity, stability, and potency are maintained throughout the automated manufacturing process [15].

Microcarrier-based systems

Microcarrier-based bioreactor systems provide higher volumetric ADSC production in automated closed systems compared to conventional planar cultures. These systems require sophisticated bioprocesses for successful conversion from planar culture, including optimized seed train procedures, inoculation protocols, expansion parameters, and harvest methodologies. Important bioreactor parameters including temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen, mixing strategies, feeding protocols, and cell counting techniques must be carefully controlled to maintain ADSC quality throughout the manufacturing process [16].

Quality control methodologies and standardization

Contemporary ADSC quality control extends beyond basic International Society for Cellular Therapy (ISCT) criteria to encompass functional potency assays and predictive quality metrics. Standard phenotypic characterization requires CD90, CD73, and CD105 positivity (≥95%) with hematopoietic marker negativity (CD45, CD34, CD14, CD19, HLA-DR ≤ 2%). Automated and enclosed three-dimensional bio fabrication systems utilizing microcarrier-bioreactor technology demonstrate superior performance compared to traditional two-dimensional culture methods. These systems produce cells with reduced senescence, enhanced migration capabilities, improved angiogenesis, and superior anti-inflammatory responses [17].

Quality assessment framework development

Standard phenotypic characterization requires CD90, CD73, and CD105 positivity (≥95%) with hematopoietic marker negativity according to International Society for Cellular Therapy criteria [18]. A comprehensive quality assessment tool for stem cell clinical trials has been developed incorporating four critical quality attributes: participant protection, scientific value, quality control, and stem cell products. This framework includes 9 observed quality indicators with validated structural equation modeling demonstrating good fit for quality assessment [19].

Advanced analytical methods

Quality control platforms for standardization of regenerative medicine products employ gentle cell sorting methods that purify primary cells obtained by enzymatic digestion without altering stem cell functions. These platforms enable comprehensive characterization while maintaining cellular integrity throughout the quality assessment process [20]. Standardization efforts focus on developing validated analytical methods with demonstrated inter-laboratory reproducibility and correlation to clinical outcomes. However, significant challenges remain in achieving consistent quality metrics across different manufacturing facilities and process approaches [21].

Quality control strategies must address the distinct characteristics of living stem cell products, including ex vivo processing effects on differentiation capacity, risks of morphological changes, surface marker expression alterations, and differentiation efficiency during long-term storage, freezing, and thawing procedures [22].

Manufacturing challenges and process limitations

Despite technological advances, ADSC manufacturing continues to face substantial challenges that impact product consistency, scalability, and commercial viability. Manufacturing equipment qualification and process validation represent substantial in-vestments in time and resources, particularly for novel automation platforms or process modifications. These requirements can delay commercial implementation and increase development costs significantly [23].

Batch-to-batch consistency issues

Successful scale-up from development to commercial manufacturing requires ex-tensive process characterization and validation to ensure consistent performance across different scales and manufacturing sites. Technology transfer between organizations or facilities often reveals hidden process dependencies that impact product quality [24].

Inter-donor variability remains a primary concern, with studies reporting 3–5-fold variations in proliferation rates, differentiation potential, and secretory profiles between donors [25]. Manufacturing consistency remains problematic even with automated systems, with coefficients of variation for key quality parameters typically ranging from 15–25% across batches. This variability exceeds acceptable limits for traditional pharmaceutical products and complicates regulatory assessment and commercial implementation [26]. Scale-up and technology transfer challenges

Process control strategies must account for inherent biological variability while maintaining product quality within acceptable limits. Current approaches rely heavily on end-point testing rather than real-time process control, limiting opportunities for corrective action during manufacturing [27].

Economic analysis and commercial viability

Economic analysis of ADSC manufacturing reveals substantial cost challenges that impact commercial viability and therapeutic accessibility. The cost structure of ADSC manufacturing encompasses facility infrastructure ($5–15 million initial capital investment), equipment and automation systems ($1–5 million), ongoing regulatory compliance expenses ($2–8 million annually), and operational costs including skilled labor, raw materials, quality control testing, and facility maintenance [28].

Potential publication and reporting biases were considered qualitatively, recognizing that unpublished negative or commercially confidential manufacturing experiences could not be captured; no formal statistical assessment of reporting bias (e.g., funnel plots) was feasible given the non-comparative and highly heterogeneous nature of the available studies.

Automated manufacturing systems demonstrate potential for cost reduction of 20–40% compared to manual methods, primarily through improved efficiency, reduced labor requirements, and enhanced batch success rates. However, these benefits must be balanced against substantial capital investment requirements and ongoing maintenance costs [9]. Cost structure and optimization

Large-scale manufacturing operations can achieve economies of scale that reduce unit costs, though current demand patterns and regulatory requirements often favor smaller, patient-specific batches that limit opportunities for scale optimization. The optimal pro-duction scale represents a balance between unit cost reduction and market demand characteristics [29].

Manufacturing scale economics

Current GMP-compliant production costs range from $15,000–$50,000 per therapeutic dose, depending on cell number requirements, manufacturing approach, facility over-head, and regulatory compliance costs. Current manufacturing costs place ADSC therapies beyond accessible price points for many patients and healthcare systems. Even with optimal process efficiency and automation implementation, manufacturing costs alone represent substantial portions of final therapy pricing, limiting commercial viability for many potential applications [30].

Detailed regulatory framework for ADSC therapeutic approvals

FDA classification system and requirements

The FDA regulates ADSC products under 21 CFR Part 1271 governing Human Cells, Tissues, and Cellular and Tissue-based Products (HCT/Ps) [31]. This regulation establishes requirements for facilities manufacturing human cellular products, including donor screening for infectious diseases (HIV, Hepatitis B/C, syphilis), environmental controls during manufacturing (clean room standards), complete product traceability from tissue collection to patient administration, and quality control testing to ensure safety.

The FDA classification system creates two distinct regulatory pathways based on product characteristics. Section 361 products are minimally manipulated tissues used for their natural biological function in the body (homologous use): these require only donor screening, basic facility registration, and good tissue practice compliance, without formal premarket approval or clinical trials. In contrast, Section 351 products undergo substantial manipulation (such as enzymatic digestion or cell culture expansion) or are used for non-natural functions: these are regulated as biological drugs requiring Investigational New Drug applications, comprehensive Phase I-III clinical trials demonstrating safety and efficacy, and Biologics License Application approval before commercialization [31].

For ADSC manufacturing, this classification distinction is critical. Enzymatic digestion using collagenase to isolate stromal vascular fraction from adipose tissue constitutes substantial manipulation by FDA definition, automatically reclassifying the product from Section 361 to Section 351 status. Similarly, ADSCs grown through cell culture expansion are classified as Section 351 biological products. This reclassification fundamentally changes regulatory requirements: manufacturers must obtain Biologics License Application approval through comprehensive clinical trials [31].

Good Manufacturing Practice compliance for Section 351 products encompasses comprehensive quality systems addressing donor eligibility determination, tissue procurement procedures, processing controls, preservation methods, storage conditions, and distribution protocols [32].

European ATMP regulatory framework

The European Medicines Agency regulates ADSC products as Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products under Regulation (EC) No 1394/2007, requiring centralized marketing authorization. ADSCs are classified as ATMPs based on substantial manipulation through enzymatic processing and cell culture expansion [31,33].

Analysis through November 2024 reveals median approval duration of 441 days, with 70% gene therapy products, 15% cell therapy products, 11% tissue engineered products, and 4% combined ATMPs [33]. Priority Medicine designation reduces median approval time from 669 days to 376 days (42.7% reduction, p = 0.001), while orphan designation provides 32.8% reduction (p = 0.021). Post-marketing data reveal 7 of 27 ap-proved products (25.9%) are no longer marketed, highlighting commercial viability challenges [34].

Quality control standards and potency assessment

Phenotypic characterization and identity testing

Quality control for ADSC products follows International Society for Cellular Therapy (ISCT) minimal criteria established to define multipotent mesenchymal stromal cells [18]. These foundational standards require demonstration of specific cell surface marker ex-pression patterns: positivity for mesenchymal markers CD73, CD90, and CD105 with at least 95% of cells expressing these markers, and negativity for hematopoietic lineage markers CD45, CD34, CD14, CD19, and HLA-DR with less than 2% expression. Additionally, cells must demonstrate plastic adherence properties when maintained in standard culture conditions and possess trilineage differentiation potential, meaning the capacity to differentiate into adipocytes (fat cells), osteoblasts (bone cells), and chondro-blasts (cartilage cells) under appropriate induction conditions [18,31].

Advanced phenotypic characterization extends beyond these minimal ISCT criteria to include assessment of non-classical markers that may indicate enhanced therapeutic properties or specific cellular subpopulations with distinct functional capabilities. These additional markers include CD271, CD274, CD276, CD163, and CD63, which emerging evidence suggests may correlate with superior immunomodulatory function, enhanced secretory profiles, or improved therapeutic efficacy in specific clinical applications [31]. However, the clinical significance of these non-classical markers remains under investigation, with standardized interpretation criteria not yet established across the field.

Contemporary quality control platforms employ sophisticated analytical technologies enabling comprehensive characterization while maintaining cellular integrity. Gentle cell sorting methods have been developed to purify primary cells obtained through enzymatic digestion without altering fundamental stem cell functions, allowing detailed characterization without compromising therapeutic properties. These advanced separation devices utilize physical properties including cell size and density to achieve purification, with cells passing through detection systems enabling real-time monitoring and collection of distinct cellular populations [20].

The implementation of standardized quality control frameworks represents an on-going challenge in ADSC manufacturing. A comprehensive quality assessment tool specifically designed for stem cell clinical trials has been developed incorporating four critical quality dimensions: participant protection, scientific value, quality control procedures, and stem cell product characteristics [19]. This framework includes nine observed quality indicators with validated structural equation modeling demonstrating good fit for systematic quality assessment across diverse manufacturing processes and clinical ap-plications [19]. However, inter-laboratory variability in phenotypic assessment remains significant, with studies reporting inconsistent results when identical samples are analyzed at different facilities using supposedly standardized protocols [21].

Quality control strategies must address the distinct challenges inherent to living stem cell products, which differ fundamentally from traditional pharmaceutical products in their susceptibility to environmental and processing influences. Ex vivo processing conditions significantly impact cellular characteristics, with extended culture periods potentially affecting differentiation capacity, altering surface marker expression patterns, and modifying functional capabilities [22]. Donor age represents a critical variable affecting ADSC quality, with studies demonstrating that increasing donor age negatively impacts adipose tissue-derived mesenchymal stem cell expansion potential and differentiation efficiency. Long-term storage procedures, including cryopreservation and thawing protocols, introduce additional quality concerns as these processes can affect cellular viability, alter morphological characteristics, modify surface marker expression, and reduce differentiation efficiency [22].

Functional potency assessment and therapeutic markers

Functional potency assessment represents a critical dimension of ADSC quality control, extending beyond simple phenotypic identification to evaluate the actual therapeutic mechanisms responsible for clinical efficacy. These functional evaluations address multiple therapeutic mechanisms including immunomodulatory capacity (the ability to modulate immune system responses), angiogenic potential (capacity to stimulate new blood vessel formation), and anti-inflammatory responses (reduction of pro-inflammatory signaling and enhancement of anti-inflammatory pathways) [21,31].

Gene expression analysis through quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (q-RT-PCR) enables measurement of therapeutic cytokine production at the molecular level. Key cytokines assessed include interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), which play central roles in ADSC immunomodulatory functions [31,34]. The expression patterns of these cytokines provide insights into the immunomodulatory profile of each manufactured batch, potentially predicting therapeutic efficacy in immune-mediated conditions. However, the correlation between gene expression levels and actual secreted protein quantities requires careful validation, as post-transcriptional regulation can significantly affect the relationship between mRNA levels and functional protein production.

Despite advances in functional testing methodologies, a critical gap persists in the field: validated potency assays with demonstrated correlation to clinical outcomes remain unavailable for most ADSC therapeutic applications. The International Society for Cellular Therapy has emphasized the urgent need for immune functional assays serving as potency release criteria for advanced phase clinical trials, recognizing that current characterization methods, while comprehensive, lack definitive predictive value for therapeutic success [21]. This limitation creates substantial challenges for both manufacturing optimization and regulatory assessment, as manufacturers cannot reliably predict which cellular batches will demonstrate superior clinical performance based solely on current potency testing approaches.

The development of clinically relevant potency assays requires systematic correlation studies linking in vitro functional measurements with clinical outcome data across multiple patient populations and therapeutic indications. Such studies are resource-intensive and require large sample sizes with comprehensive clinical follow-up, explaining the limited progress despite recognized importance. Furthermore, ADSC therapeutic mechanisms likely vary across different clinical applications, suggesting that potency assays may need to be indication-specific rather than universally applicable across all therapeutic uses.

Advanced manufacturing systems incorporating three-dimensional culture methodologies demonstrate potential advantages for ADSC therapeutic properties. Cells produced in 3D automated and enclosed bio fabrication systems show maintenance of genetic stability and trilineage differentiation potential, with RNA-sequencing analysis revealing higher expression of genes related to angiogenesis and anti-inflammatory pathways compared to cells cultured using traditional two-dimensional methods. These 3D-cultured cells additionally demonstrate reduced cellular senescence, enhanced migration capabilities, improved angiogenic potential, and superior anti-inflammatory responses in functional assessments [17]. However, the clinical significance of these enhanced in vitro characteristics requires validation through comparative clinical trials demonstrating superior therapeutic outcomes.

Safety testing and contamination control

Comprehensive safety testing represents a fundamental requirement for ADSC manufacturing, addressing multiple contamination risks inherent to products derived from human tissue and expanded through cell culture processes. Safety assessment protocols must detect bacterial contamination, fungal contamination, mycoplasma infection, endotoxin presence, and viral agents, utilizing testing methods aligned with pharmacopoeial standards established by the United States Pharmacopoeia and European Pharmacopoeia where applicable [31].

Viability should be considered a critical quality attribute and should be assessed at the end of the manufacturing process and immediately before clinical administration. A viable ADSC product should demonstrate preserved cellular integrity, an acceptable proportion of viable cells within the predefined batch-release specifications, and maintenance of the phenotypic and functional characteristics required for the intended therapeutic application. Viability should therefore be evaluated together with identity, purity, sterility, and potency rather than considered as an isolated parameter.

The maintenance of ADSC viability before injection requires controlled handling throughout harvesting, washing, concentration, formulation, transport, storage, and thawing procedures. These steps should be performed under validated GMP-compliant conditions, with controlled temperature and limited processing delays to minimize cellular stress. Particular attention is required for cryopreserved products, as freezing and thawing may reduce viability, alter cellular morphology and surface-marker expression, and impair differentiation or functional capacity. Consequently, the final product should undergo post-thaw or pre-administration quality control, including viability testing and confirmation of identity, sterility, and relevant potency attributes, before release for clinical use. Because validated potency assays do not yet reliably predict clinical efficacy for most ADSC applications, viability results should be interpreted together with the complete quality-control profile and the intended clinical indication.

Sterility testing employs culture-based methods detecting viable bacterial and fungal organisms through incubation in enrichment media designed to support growth of di-verse microbial species. Traditional compendial sterility testing requires 14-day incubation periods, creating logistical challenges for fresh, non-cryopreserved products with limited shelf-lives. However, these extended incubation periods remain necessary to detect slow-growing organisms that might not become apparent in shorter testing intervals. Contamination events, while reduced through automation and closed-system processing, continue to represent significant manufacturing challenges with contamination related batch failures occurring in 5–10% of production runs even with optimized processes [10].

Mycoplasma detection addresses a particularly insidious contamination risk, as these wall-less bacteria can infect cell cultures without producing visible turbidity or pH changes that typically signal bacterial contamination. Mycoplasma testing employs either culture-based methods requiring extended 28-day incubation periods, or polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based molecular detection providing results within 24–48 hours but requiring careful validation to ensure detection of all relevant mycoplasma species [31]. The selection of appropriate mycoplasma testing methodology requires balancing detection sensitivity, assay reliability, turnaround time, and compatibility with product release timelines.

Endotoxin testing quantifies bacterial cell wall components (lipopolysaccharides) that trigger potent inflammatory responses even at minute concentrations. Testing employs Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL) assays derived from horseshoe crab blood cells, or recombinant Factor C assays providing animal-free alternatives, both capable of detecting endotoxin levels below 0.5 endotoxin units per milliliter – the typical acceptance criterion for parenterally administered products [31]. ADSC manufacturing from human adipose tissue presents challenges for endotoxin control, as adipose tissue naturally contains elevated endotoxin levels requiring validated removal procedures to ensure final product compliance with safety specifications.

Donor screening protocols establish safety through multiple complementary approaches addressing transmissible pathogen risks. Medical history evaluation identifies risk factors for infectious diseases including behavioral risks, geographic exposures, and clinical symptoms suggesting active infection. Physical examination detects signs of systemic illness that might indicate transmissible conditions. Laboratory testing screens for specific viral pathogens including human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), Hepatitis B virus (HBV), Hepatitis C virus (HCV), and syphilis using validated serological and molecular detection methods [31]. However, testing window limitations mean recently infected donors may test negative despite harboring transmissible agents in the early infection period before detectable antibody or antigen responses develop. This funda-mental limitation of current testing technology requires risk-benefit assessments accepting that absolute viral safety assurance remains impossible for primary human cell products.

Good Manufacturing Practice requirements mandate comprehensive contamination prevention systems operating throughout the manufacturing process. Validated contamination prevention procedures include multiple control layers: environmental monitoring tracking air quality, temperature, humidity, and microbial contamination in manufacturing spaces to ensure conditions remain within validated acceptable limits; personnel qualification including specialized training in aseptic techniques (contamination-free handling methods), regular competency verification through media fill vali-dation studies, and health monitoring to identify personnel who might introduce infectious agents; equipment sterilization following validated protocols ensuring complete elimination of contaminating organisms using validated time-temperature parameters or chemical sterilization methods; and facility design incorporating appropriate air handling systems with HEPA filtration, positive pressure gradients preventing contaminated air entry, and surface materials facilitating effective cleaning and disinfection [32,33].

The development and validation of a fully GMP-compliant process for manufacturing stromal vascular fraction provides a cost-effective alternative to automated methods while maintaining rigorous safety standards. This validated process demonstrates that appropriate procedural controls, personnel training, and environmental monitoring can achieve contamination prevention comparable to sophisticated automation systems, though at the cost of increased manual labor requirements and potentially reduced throughput compared to fully automated platforms. The choice between manual GMP-compliant processes and automated systems represents a fundamental decision for manufacturing facilities, balancing capital investment, operational costs, throughput requirements, and organizational technical capabilities [34].

Regulatory framework and compliance requirements

Regulatory frameworks for ADSC manufacturing continue to evolve, with recent guidance providing greater clarity while highlighting complexity and resource requirements. The regulatory environment varies significantly between jurisdictions, creating challenges for global development and commercialization strategies [32].

GMP implementation requirements

GMP compliance for ADSC manufacturing requires comprehensive documentation of manufacturing processes, extensive quality control testing, and demonstrated consistency across batches. Facility design, equipment qualification, personnel training, and quality systems must meet pharmaceutical manufacturing standards [33].

Stromal vascular fraction isolation from adipose tissue is considered an advanced therapy medicinal product (ATMP) requiring substantial manipulation and non-homologous use designation. Manufacturing must strictly follow GMP guidelines including trained personnel, qualified premises and equipment, appropriate documentation, quality production operations, validated analytical methods, and qualified person batch release [35].

Process validation and regulatory compliance

Process validation requirements include demonstration of consistent performance across multiple batches, characterization of critical process parameters, and establishment of appropriate control strategies. These studies typically require 6–12 months of validation activities before commercial manufacturing approval [36].

Quality control and release testing encompass identity, purity, potency, and safety assessments using validated analytical methods. The development and validation of these methods represent substantial investments, particularly for novel potency assays without established regulatory precedent [37].

Clinical applications and safety assessment

ADSC manufactured under GMP conditions have demonstrated safety and pre-liminary efficacy across multiple clinical indications, though therapeutic outcomes show considerable variability that may relate to manufacturing processes and product characteristics [38]. The clinical validation of allogeneic ADSC safety and efficacy across di-verse pathological conditions provides strong evidence supporting the development of robust manufacturing systems [2].

Clinical trial outcomes and safety profile

Systematic analysis of allogeneic adipose stem cell clinical trials demonstrates promising safety profiles across multiple therapeutic areas, including cardiovascular applications, orthopedic disorders, wound healing, autoimmune conditions, and neurological disorders. Phase I and II studies consistently show acceptable safety profiles with treatment-related adverse events primarily limited to injection site reactions and minimal systemic effects. The clinical development of adipose-derived stem cell products is illustrated by Cx601, also known as darvadstrocel, an expanded allogeneic adipose-derived stem cell product evaluated in placebo-controlled clinical trials for complex perianal fistulas associated with Crohn’s disease [2].

Efficacy outcomes demonstrate therapeutic benefit in several indications, though substantial variability exists both within and between studies. This variability may reflect differences in manufacturing processes, product characteristics, patient populations, treatment protocols, or outcome assessment methodologies [39].

Manufacturing impact on clinical performance

Limited data are available correlating manufacturing processes or product characteristics with clinical outcomes. Studies comparing different manufacturing approaches or cell expansion conditions rarely include clinical endpoint assessments, limiting under-standing of manufacturing impact on therapeutic performance [40].

The demonstrated clinical safety and efficacy of allogeneic ADSC across diverse therapeutic applications supports the rationale for developing standardized manufacturing approaches capable of producing consistent, high-quality cellular products suitable for widespread clinical use [2]. Emerging technologies and future directions

Several emerging technologies show potential for addressing current manufacturing challenges. Advanced automation platforms incorporating artificial intelligence-driven process control may offer improved consistency and reduced operational requirements, though implementation requires substantial validation and may be limited to large-scale manufacturing facilities [41].

Comprehensive evaluation of cells produced in 3D culture systems shows maintenance of genetic stability and trilineage differentiation potential, with RNA-sequencing analysis revealing higher expression of genes related to angiogenesis and anti-inflammatory pathways compared to 2D-cultured cells [17].

Critical quality control parameters for extracellular vesicle products include physical identity (particle size, concentration, protein content), sterility, potency assessment through ex vivo cellular education assays, and in vivo bioassay validation in animal models [17,42].

Discussion

Current state of ADSC manufacturing technology

Analysis of contemporary ADSC manufacturing reveals a field in transition between laboratory-scale research approaches and industrial manufacturing systems. Significant technological progress has been achieved in automated production platforms, environmental control systems, and quality assurance methodologies, though substantial challenges persist in standardization, cost reduction, and regulatory compliance.

Automated bioreactor systems represent the most significant technological advancement, offering improved yields (2–10-fold increases), enhanced consistency (coefficients of variation reduced from 25–40% to 15–25%), and reduced contamination risks compared to traditional manual methods. However, these improvements require substantial validation and capital investment for successful implementation [10].

The implementation of validated automated platforms provides manufacturing options across different scales and applications, though each platform requires specific expertise, validation protocols, and ongoing technical support that may limit widespread adoption in smaller manufacturing facilities.

Clinical validation and manufacturing requirements

The comprehensive systematic review of allogeneic ADSC clinical trials provides strong validation for the therapeutic potential of these cells across diverse pathological conditions [2]. The demonstrated safety and efficacy profiles support continued investment in manufacturing technology development, while highlighting the need for standardized production approaches capable of maintaining therapeutic quality at scale.

The clinical success of allogeneic ADSC therapies across multiple therapeutic areas creates both opportunities and challenges for manufacturing. While the potential for centralized production offers economy of scale benefits, the need to maintain therapeutic consistency across diverse patient populations and indications require sophisticated quality control and process standardization [2].

Manufacturing systems must be capable of producing ADSC with consistent therapeutic properties across the range of applications validated in clinical trials, including cardiovascular, orthopedic, wound healing, autoimmune, and neurological applications. This requirement necessitates comprehensive characterization of manufacturing processes and their impact on cellular therapeutic properties.

Persistent manufacturing challenges

Despite technological advances, several fundamental challenges continue to impact ADSC manufacturing. Inter-donor variability remains a primary concern, with 3–5-fold differences in cellular characteristics between donors representing a major obstacle to product standardization and therapeutic consistency [25].

Manufacturing failure rates, while improved through automation, continue to exceed pharmaceutical manufacturing standards. These failures result from multiple factors including contamination, poor cellular growth, equipment malfunction, and operator error, highlighting the need for continued process optimization and quality system improvement.

Batch-to-batch variability, while reduced through automation, continues to present challenges for commercial manufacturing. This variability stems from donor differences, raw material variations, environmental fluctuations, and process control limitations that require additional technological solutions.

Quality control evolution and standardization

Quality control methodologies have evolved substantially beyond basic phenotypic characterization defined by International Society for Cellular Therapy minimal criteria to encompass comprehensive functional potency assessments, predictive quality analytics, and real-time process monitoring capabilities. This evolution reflects growing recognition that simple phenotypic markers (CD73/CD90/CD105 positivity, CD45/CD34/CD14/CD19/HLA-DR negativity) provide necessary but insufficient characterization for predicting therapeutic efficacy across diverse clinical applications [18]. Contemporary quality frameworks incorporate multiple assessment dimensions addressing not only cellular identity and purity, but also functional capabilities relevant to therapeutic mechanisms including immunomodulatory capacity, angiogenic potential, and anti-inflammatory responses [21].

However, standardization across laboratories remains critically inadequate, with significant inter-laboratory variation reported even for supposedly standardized assays employing identical protocols and reference materials. Studies demonstrate that when identical ADSC samples are distributed to multiple laboratories for phenotypic characterization, substantial variability emerges in reported marker expression percentages, differentiation capacity assessments, and functional assay results [21]. This variability stems from multiple sources including differences in analytical equipment calibration, reagent lot variations, operator technique differences, and subtle environmental condition variations that impact cellular behavior during testing procedures.

The development of comprehensive quality assessment tools specifically designed for stem cell clinical trials has provided systematic frameworks for evaluating manufacturing processes and clinical applications. One validated tool incorporates four critical quality dimensions: participant protection (addressing informed consent, risk minimization, and safety monitoring), scientific value (ensuring study design rigor and clinical relevance), quality control procedures (encompassing manufacturing process controls and analytical method validation), and stem cell product characteristics (addressing identity, purity, potency, and stability). This framework includes nine observed quality indicators with validated structural equation modeling demonstrating good fit for systematic quality assessment across diverse manufacturing contexts and therapeutic applications, providing standardized approaches for evaluating both manufacturing processes and clinical trial designs [19].

Advanced quality control platforms have been developed employing gentle cell sorting methods that purify primary cells obtained through enzymatic digestion without altering fundamental stem cell functions, enabling comprehensive characterization while maintaining cellular integrity throughout the quality assessment process. These platforms utilize physical cell properties including size and density to achieve purification, with cells passing through detection systems enabling real-time monitoring and collection of distinct cellular populations based on their phenotypic characteristics. Such approaches provide more sophisticated characterization capabilities while avoiding the potential functional alterations that can result from traditional immunomagnetic or fluorescence activated cell sorting procedures requiring antibody binding and cellular stress [20].

Despite these technological advances, the lack of validated potency assays with demonstrated correlation to clinical efficacy represents a critical gap that fundamentally hampers both manufacturing optimization and regulatory assessment. The International Society for Cellular Therapy has explicitly emphasized the urgent need for immune functional assays serving as potency release criteria for advanced phase clinical trials, recognizing that current characterization methods, while technically sophisticated and comprehensive in scope, lack definitive predictive value for therapeutic success in patients [21]. Most current functional assays, including assessments of T-cell proliferation inhibition, cytokine secretion profiles, and differentiation capacity, provide useful product characterization and demonstrate batch-to-batch differences, but cannot reliably predict which specific cellular batches will demonstrate superior clinical performance in treating patients with specific pathological conditions.

This potency assessment gap creates substantial practical challenges for manufacturers attempting to optimize production processes for therapeutic performance rather than simply meeting arbitrary release specifications. Without validated correlations between in vitro quality metrics and clinical outcomes, manufacturers cannot make evidence-based decisions about process modifications, cannot reliably identify superior cellular batches for clinical use, and cannot establish scientifically justified acceptance criteria that ensure therapeutic efficacy rather than merely confirming cellular identity and basic viability. The development of clinically relevant potency assays requires systematic correlation studies linking in vitro functional measurements with clinical outcome data across multiple patient populations and therapeutic indications, studies that are resource-intensive, require large sample sizes with comprehensive long-term clinical follow-up, and have received insufficient investment despite their recognized importance for field advancement [21].

Economic barriers and commercial viability

Current manufacturing costs of $15,000–$50,000 per therapeutic dose create substantial barriers to widespread therapeutic accessibility. These costs reflect multiple factors including facility infrastructure, regulatory compliance, quality control testing, and skilled labor requirements that cannot be easily reduced through process optimization alone.

The capital investment requirements for GMP-compliant facilities and automated equipment create significant barriers to market entry and limit the number of potential manufacturers. This concentration may impede innovation while creating supply chain vulnerabilities, though it enables economy of scale benefits.

While automation offers cost reduction potential of 20–40%, these improvements are insufficient to achieve affordability levels required for widespread patient access. Additional innovations in manufacturing efficiency, process standardization, and regulatory streamlining will be necessary to achieve meaningful cost reductions [9].

Regulatory framework development

Regulatory frameworks governing ADSC manufacturing continue to evolve through iterative guideline updates and clarifying documents issued by major regulatory authorities, with recent guidance documents providing greater procedural clarity while simultaneously highlighting the substantial complexity and resource requirements inherent in achieving full regulatory compliance for cellular therapy products. The current regulatory environment creates relatively predictable standards and documented pathways for manufacturers pursuing market authorization, establishing clear expectations for facility design, personnel qualifications, process validation, quality systems, and documentation requirements [35]. However, these comprehensive regulatory standards simultaneously represent substantial barriers to innovation and market access, particularly for smaller organizations and academic institutions lacking the extensive resources, specialized expertise, and sustained funding necessary to navigate complex regulatory pathways requiring multi-year development timelines and substantial financial in-vestment [32].

The regulatory classification of stromal vascular fraction isolated from adipose tissue illustrates the complexity and stringency of current requirements. Regulatory authorities classify stromal vascular fraction as advanced therapy medicinal products requiring Good Manufacturing Practice compliance based on the substantial manipulation involved in enzymatic tissue digestion and the potential for non-homologous use applications [35]. This classification triggers comprehensive regulatory requirements encompassing multiple interconnected elements: specialized facility design incorporating Grade A/B clean room environments with validated environmental control systems, extensive personnel training programs with documented competency verification and ongoing assessment, comprehensive process validation studies demonstrating consistent performance across multiple manufacturing batches, sophisticated quality control systems employing vali-dated analytical methods for identity, purity, potency, and safety assessment, and formalized batch release procedures requiring qualified person certification before clinical use [36]. Each of these elements requires substantial investment in infrastructure, personnel, time, and financial resources, creating significant barriers particularly for smaller manufacturers and academic institutions attempting to translate promising research findings into clinical applications [35].

The development and validation of fully Good Manufacturing Practice-compliant processes for manufacturing stromal vascular fraction have been demonstrated as technically feasible, providing cost-effective alternatives to sophisticated automated manufacturing systems while maintaining rigorous regulatory compliance and product quality standards. However, even these optimized manual approaches require substantial infrastructure investment, specialized personnel training, comprehensive process validation, and ongoing quality system maintenance that exceed the capabilities of many potential manufacturers. The choice between manual Good Manufacturing Practice-compliant processes and automated manufacturing platforms represents a fundamental strategic decision for organizations, requiring careful evaluation of multiple factors including initial capital investment requirements, ongoing operational costs, expected production volumes and throughput requirements, organizational technical capabilities and expertise, regulatory compliance complexity, and long-term scalability considerations [35].

International regulatory harmonization remains frustratingly incomplete despite ongoing efforts through collaborative forums and guideline development initiatives, with significant variations persisting in regulatory requirements, approval procedures, quality standards, and documentation expectations between major jurisdictions including the United States, European Union, Japan, and other markets. These jurisdictional differences create substantial challenges for organizations pursuing global development strategies, requiring navigation of multiple independent regulatory pathways with potentially conflicting requirements, duplicative clinical trial demands, and incompatible quality specifications that prevent efficient resource utilization and delay patient access to therapeutic innovations. Manufacturers seeking approval across multiple jurisdictions face multiplied costs for regulatory submissions, must conduct separate or expanded clinical trials meeting different evidentiary standards, maintain documentation systems satisfying diverse regulatory expectations, and navigate distinct approval processes with unpredictable timelines [32].

The regulatory complexity extends beyond procedural requirements to encompass fundamental philosophical differences between jurisdictions regarding acceptable evidence standards, risk tolerance levels, and regulatory flexibility for innovative therapies addressing unmet medical needs. Some regulatory authorities emphasize extensive preclinical validation and definitive clinical efficacy demonstration before approval, while others provide conditional approval pathways enabling earlier patient access based on preliminary efficacy signals with extended post-marketing surveillance requirements. These philosophical differences reflect varying approaches to balancing innovation promotion against patient safety assurance, creating incompatible regulatory frameworks that complicate global development strategies [33].

Standardized international regulatory requirements would substantially facilitate global therapeutic development while reducing compliance costs, enabling more efficient resource allocation, accelerating patient access to innovative therapies, and reducing duplicative regulatory activities that consume resources without meaningfully enhancing product safety or efficacy assurance. However, achieving such harmonization requires sustained collaborative efforts between regulatory authorities, careful negotiation of divergent philosophical approaches, and political commitment to international coordination that has proven difficult to achieve despite recognized benefits. The absence of harmonization particularly disadvantages smaller organizations and academic institutions lacking resources to navigate multiple complex regulatory systems, potentially limiting innovation by restricting the diversity of organizations capable of successfully bringing cellular therapies to market [32].

The current regulatory environment, while providing necessary quality and safety assurance, creates substantial barriers that limit innovation, delay patient access to potentially beneficial therapies, and concentrate manufacturing capabilities among well-resourced organizations capable of meeting extensive compliance requirements. Addressing these challenges will require continued dialogue between regulators, industry, and academic stakeholders to identify opportunities for streamlining requirements, enhancing international harmonization, and establishing risk-proportionate regulatory frameworks that maintain safety standards while reducing unnecessary barriers to innovation and therapeutic accessibility [34,36].

International harmonization remains incomplete, with significant variations in regulatory requirements between jurisdictions creating challenges for global development strategies. Standardized requirements would facilitate international development while reducing compliance costs.

Clinical translation and future directions

The relationship between manufacturing processes and clinical outcomes remains poorly understood, limiting opportunities to optimize production methods for therapeutic performance rather than simply meeting release specifications. Improved correlation between manufacturing parameters and clinical efficacy will be essential for field advancement [40].

The demonstrated clinical safety and efficacy of allogeneic ADSC across diverse therapeutic applications provides strong rationale for continued manufacturing technology development [2]. Understanding the manufacturing characteristics that contribute to therapeutic success will be critical for optimizing production processes and ensuring consistent clinical outcomes.

Emerging three-dimensional culture systems demonstrate superior cellular characteristics and therapeutic performance compared to traditional approaches, offering potential solutions to current manufacturing limitations. However, implementation re-quires extensive validation and scale-up optimization [17].

The development of automated and enclosed manufacturing platforms may address scalability and cost challenges while maintaining quality standards. These approaches require continued technological development and regulatory validation for widespread implementation [42].

Conclusions

Clinical ADSC manufacturing has achieved substantial technological advancement over the past decade, with automated systems demonstrating improved yields, consistency, and quality compared to traditional manual approaches. However, the field continues to face significant challenges in achieving cost-effective, scalable production suitable for widespread clinical application. The clinical validation of allogeneic ADSC safety and efficacy across diverse therapeutic areas provides strong support for continued investment in manufacturing technology development [2].

Current automated bioreactor systems represent mature technology platforms capable of producing clinical-grade ADSC under GMP conditions, offering 210-fold improvements in efficiency while reducing contamination risks and improving batch-to-batch consistency. Validated platforms provide manufacturing options across different scales and applications, though implementation requires substantial capital investment and technical expertise.

Quality control methodologies have evolved to encompass functional assessments and predictive quality metrics, though standardization across laboratories remains inadequate. The development of comprehensive quality assessment frameworks provides systematic approaches for evaluating manufacturing processes and clinical applications, though validated potency assays with demonstrated clinical correlation remain critically needed.

Economic barriers remain substantial, with current manufacturing costs of $15,000–$50,000 per therapeutic dose limiting accessibility and commercial viability. While automation offers cost reduction potential of 20–40%, additional innovations in manufacturing efficiency, process standardization, and regulatory optimization will be necessary to achieve meaningful improvements in affordability.

Regulatory frameworks continue to evolve, providing greater clarity while highlighting complexity and resource requirements for compliant manufacturing. The classification of ADSC products as advanced therapy medicinal products creates comprehensive regulatory requirements that ensure safety but represent barriers to innovation and market access.

Persistent challenges including inter-donor variability (3–5-fold differences), batch-to-batch consistency issues (coefficients of variation 15–25%), and manufacturing failure rates require continued attention through technological innovation, process optimization, and quality system improvement.

Emerging technologies including three-dimensional culture systems and automated enclosed manufacturing platforms show promise for addressing current limitations in scalability, cost reduction, and therapeutic performance. However, implementation requires extensive validation and regulatory acceptance for widespread adoption.

The demonstrated clinical success of allogeneic ADSC therapies across cardiovascular, orthopedic, wound healing, autoimmune, and neurological applications validates the therapeutic platform while highlighting the need for robust manufacturing systems capable of maintaining therapeutic consistency [2]. Future success in ADSC manufacturing will depend on continued technological innovation addressing persistent challenges in standardization, cost reduction, and quality assurance.

The field requires sustained collaboration between industry, academia, and regulatory agencies to establish appropriate standards, validate new technologies, and facilitate innovation while maintaining patient safety and product quality. Continued in-vestment in manufacturing science and technology development will be essential for completing the transition from research-grade to fully industrialized production systems capable of supporting widespread clinical application across the diverse therapeutic areas where ADSC have demonstrated clinical benefit.
