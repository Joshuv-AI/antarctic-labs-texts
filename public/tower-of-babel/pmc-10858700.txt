Impact of general or regional anaesthesia on recurrence of colorectal cancer after surgery: Systematic review
Authors: Alisha Chachra, Satheesh Gunashekar, Ajit Kumar, Nitish Thakur, Arun Jagath
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background and Aims:Studies have suggested that anaesthetic agents have modulatory effects on the immune system, leading to cancer recurrence. The association between colorectal cancer (CRC) recurrence and anaesthesia is still unclear. Therefore, this systematic review aimed to determine the association between the recurrence of CRC after surgery and anaesthesia.Methods:A database search of PubMed, Cochrane, Embase, and Scopus was performed for articles on the recurrence of CRC after surgeries under general anaesthesia (GA) and regional anaesthesia (RA), published between January 2002 and January 2023. Qualitative and risk-of-bias assessment of retrospective studies was performed using the Newcastle–Ottawa scale (NOS). Synthesis Without Meta-analysis guidelines were used to report data synthesis. The primary outcome was cancer recurrence, and the secondary outcomes were disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival. The standardised metric to represent data synthesis was the median hazard ratio (HR). Evidence quality was rated as per GRADE pro-GDT.Results:A total of six retrospective cohorts were identified through the literature search for inclusion. The risk of bias was low in all studies and was rated good quality as per the NOS. The pooled risk ratio for cancer recurrence in the two studies was 1.04, 95% confidence interval 0.98–1.10 (P = 0.20). The median HR for cancer recurrence was 0.895. DFS was not statistically significant with GA or RA, with a median HR of 1.06.Conclusion:No conclusive association was found between regional anaesthesia and colorectal cancer recurrence. However, due to a lack of studies reporting cancer recurrence and less data for comparison and different intervention groups, a conclusive association cannot be made.

INTRODUCTION

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third-most common cancer diagnosed worldwide, according to Global Cancer Observatory 2020, and contributes to a substantial proportion of cancer-related mortality.[1] With advancements in surgical techniques and improved awareness, there is continuous improvement in all stages of survival. Approximately 20%–30% of patients with stage I–III CRC develop recurrence.[2] The metastatic pattern and cancer recurrence have been found to be associated mainly with the anatomical location of the primary tumour, but the tumour stage also affects the recurrence rate.[2] Primary tumour location and tumour stage have prognostic implications in patients with early-stage CRC.[3] Though surgery is the curative treatment for CRC, it can itself paradoxically lead to the spread of tumour cells during manipulation, leading to recurrence or tumour metastasis.[4]

All types of anaesthetic agents have been found to modulate the immune system and exert effects on innate and adaptive immunity and neuroendocrine response.[5] Opioids may have pro-angiogenic effects, whereas regional anaesthesia (RA) techniques are associated with lower rates of cancer recurrence. Evidence is thus arising about the possible relation between types of anaesthesia and cancer recurrence.[6] Local anaesthetics have been found to inhibit the metastatic process.[7] Clinical research investigating long-term outcomes in post-surgery oncological patients is inconsistent. Thus, we performed a systematic review to evaluate the recurrence of CRC under general anaesthesia (GA) or RA in patients undergoing CRC surgery.

METHODS

Study design

The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (vide registration number CRD42022366638). The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines and Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) guidelines were followed while reporting the systematic review.[8]

Eligibility criteria

The studies that evaluated the effects of GA or RA on recurrence in CRC patients were included in this review. GA included the use of total intravenous anaesthesia (TIVA) or inhalational anaesthetic agents, and RA included epidural anaesthesia or spinal anaesthesia. Thus, the comparison in selected studies included two possible scenarios in the selected studies: (1) RA and GA versus sole GA, and (2) TIVA with inhalational anaesthesia (as a sub-classification of GA).

A well-formulated Population, Intervention, Control, Outcome (PICOS) design was used for the inclusion of studies [Table 1]. Animal studies, articles not published in English, articles whose full texts were not available, and ongoing studies were not included. Systematic reviews, literature reviews, scoping reviews, case reports, editorials, and conference abstracts were excluded. Patients of CRC who were not operated for CRC or had recurrence or metastasis were excluded. The studies were also excluded if the study methods and designs were not properly stated and reported inadequate data. Studies with a follow-up period of less than 6 months were excluded.

Electronic literature search and study selection

A database search of PubMed, Cochrane, Embase, and Scopus was done for articles published between January 2002 and January 2023. The Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms used to carry out the e-search were ‘colorectal cancer’, ‘recurrence’, ‘general anaesthesia’, and ‘regional anaesthesia’ [Supplementary Material 1]. Two reviewers independently performed an online literature search by using different combinations of the MeSH terms. Manual screening of abstracts, references, and bibliographies of selected articles was performed. Once the abstract was analysed and found appropriate, the full texts of all selected articles were studied. Another reviewer independently assessed and decided on the articles to be included in the final analysis.

Data extraction

Two independent reviewers went thoroughly through all the selected articles. Reviewers did independent and thorough data extraction from all articles and summarised it in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet by using a standardised data extraction form. The data extracted included basic study characteristics (author, publication year, study design, and sample size), patient demographics, and outcome (recurrence of CRC, disease-free survival (DFS), and overall survival) [Table 2]. Included studies evaluated the recurrence of CRC after administration of GA or RA in patients undergoing CRC surgeries. Four studies compared GA with RA versus GA alone, whereas the other two studies compared inhalational anaesthesia with TIVA.

Quality assessment of individual studies

The risk of bias and quality assessment for included retrospective cohorts was done using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS). Two independent reviewers assessed the included studies. For differences in bias assessment by the reviewers, discussion and adjudication were done. The quality assessment was described as good, fair, or poor quality, where scoring was done for each parameter. The NOS includes eight domains for assessing the risk of bias [Table 3].

Outcome analysis

The primary outcome was cancer recurrence after surgery under anaesthesia in patients with CRC. The selected studies variably reported DFS and overall survival, which were reported as secondary outcomes. Cancer recurrence was defined as the number of patients in whom the cancer recurred after a median time of 1–5 years post the date of surgery. DFS was defined as time till the recurrence of cancer. Overall survival was time from the date of surgery till death or the end of the follow-up period.

Other variables such as age, gender, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status, and TNM (tumour, node, metastasis) staging were also mentioned. Blood transfusion and its perioperative role in CRC surgeries was also reported as a secondary outcome.

Data synthesis

A total of six studies were included for systematic review. The intervention groups in four studies included a comparison between RA and GA versus RA, and the two studies compared inhalational versus TIVA.[91011121314] Due to overall heterogeneity and lack of data in the studies, two studies were used for data synthesis for the primary outcome of cancer recurrence. Gottschalk et al.[9] and Cummings et al.[12] compared cancer recurrence in CRC patients undergoing surgery under RA [epidural anaesthesia (EA)] versus no RA. Two other studies were used for data synthesis for DFS.[1314] Hasselager et al. and Lee et al. evaluated the association of TIVA versus inhalational in cancer recurrence. Thus, the criteria of using a subset of two studies and grouping are attributed to similar study designs and outcome reporting.

The standardised metric used for data synthesis and analysis for cancer recurrence and DFS is effect size. A meta-analysis could not be performed due to the variability of intervention groups, and combining two studies for meta-analysis would not bear significant outcomes. Thus, a systematic review with SWiM was reported. The effect estimates are described as the median hazard ratio (HR) for both outcomes. This method of data synthesis was chosen to describe the range and distribution of effects.[15]

Evidence quality

The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) method was used. GRADEpro GDT (Guideline Development Tool) (Software, McMaster University and Evidence prime, 2022, available from ‘www.gradepro.org’) was used to evaluate the quality of reviewed evidence by considering factors such as study design, risk of bias, directness of outcomes, precision in results, bias due to publishing, and dose relationship with response and confounders. The GRADE thus obtained may represent high, moderate, low, or very low-quality evidence.[16]

RESULTS

Search results and study selection

A database search was performed for relevant articles from January 2002 till January 2023, which resulted in 462 studies from Cochrane, Embase, Scopus, and PubMed with the MESH terms described in Supplementary Material 1. Out of these, 70 were duplicates, and the remaining 219 records were screened after selecting only articles. A total of 200 studies were excluded after reading the titles and abstracts, and the remaining 19 were sought for retrieval. Eligibility of 16 articles was assessed. Existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses, ongoing trials, and studies that included metastasis and lack of data were excluded. A total of six studies were finally included, as outlined in the PRISMA flowchart [Figure 1]. A systematic review and meta-analysis were planned in the protocol; however, a meta-analysis could not be performed due to methodological diversity and comparison between different intervention groups in the selected studies. Instead, a systematic review of the applicable clinical evidence was completed by SWiM.

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flow-chart depicting the selection and exclusion of studies

Study characteristics

The studies selected for recurrence in CRC were retrospective cohort studies. A total of six studies were included in the systematic review with 58,075 patients. The sample size varied between 424 and 40,377 patient case records. The median follow-up ranged from 1 to 5 years. The patients selected belonged to ASA physical status I–III. Four studies included a comparison between GA with RA (EA) versus GA with no epidural or spinal or patient-controlled analgesia (PCA), whereas two studies compared inhalational and TIVA in CRC surgeries [Table 2].[91011121314]

Risk-of-bias assessment

All the non-randomised retrospective studies were scored as per the NOS for risk-of-bias assessment.[17] The highest NOS score was 9. The risk-of-bias assessment was done by two reviewers independently. Any queries were settled by discussion with a third reviewer. All retrospective cohorts were rated as good quality as per NOS [Table 3]. Primary sources of potential bias could be confounding factors due to age, gender, ASA physical status, and cancer stage. As all the studies are retrospective cohorts, there may be bias due to recall or misclassification of data. However, it is worthwhile to note that five out of six studies used propensity matching to reduce the bias. The retrospective cohort by Hasselager et al.[13] is a registry-based study using the Danish CRC group database and included 11,560 patients undergoing CRC surgeries. Therefore, this study has higher evidence compared to the remaining studies because the patient selection represents the community due to data collection from multiple registries.

Results of individual studies

Cancer recurrence

Three studies estimated cancer recurrence in the form of HR, absolute values, and percentages.[91213] Out of these, two studies compared GA with RA versus GA alone and included ASA physical status I–III patients with non-metastatic CRC from population-based data undergoing similar surgical procedures, with no significant difference between the groups.[912] The duration of follow-up was different in the studies, ranging from 1.8 to 5 years [Table 2]. Blood transfusion, TNM staging, and ASA physical status were defined in all the studies, and covariate balancing was done.

Gottschalk et al.[9] compared the effect of RA (EA) versus no RA in 669 patients undergoing CRC surgery with stage 0–IV. They reported no association between epidural use and recurrence with an adjusted estimated HR = 0.74 and 95% confidence interval (CI) =0.45–1.22 (P = 0.24). However, epidural use was found beneficial in patients aged >64 years. Cummings et al.[12] evaluated a large cohort of 40,377 patients aged >66 years and concluded overall 4-year cancer recurrence as 14.3% in the RA group and 13.8% in the no RA group. After adjusting for covariates, no association between cancer recurrence and epidural anaesthesia was determined, with odds ratio = 1.05 and 95% CI = 0.95–1.15 (P = 0.28). Thus, from these two studies, no significant effect of RA was found on recurrence, with a pooled risk ratio = 1.04 and 95% CI = 0.98–1.10 (P = 0.20). The summary of studies is described in Table 4. The median HR for these studies was estimated as 0.895, which implies that the median hazard of cancer recurrence in RA is 89.5% compared to no RA.

The third study by Hasselager et al.[13] differed in the intervention as they compared inhalational anaesthesia with TIVA in a registry-based study, which included 11,560 patients. The 5-year probability of recurrence was reported as 20.8% and 18.8% for the inhalational anaesthesia and TIVA groups, respectively. They concluded an increased risk of recurrence in patients undergoing CRC surgery with inhalational anaesthesia compared with TIVA (HR = 1.12; 95% CI = 1.02–1.23), which could be attributed to favourable effects of TIVA.

Disease-free survival, overall survival, and mortality

The other three studies reported 5-year DFS, 5-year overall survival, and mortality as percentages and HR.[101114] Gupta et al.[10] studied 655 patients undergoing colorectal surgery under EA or PCA. Five-year mortality was 23%, in EA and 22% in PCA.

Three studies reported only DFS.[111314] Day et al.[11] evaluated 5-year DFS in 424 CRC patients undergoing laparoscopic colorectal resection. The interventions were epidural, spinal, and PCA groups, and 5-year DFS was found to be 72%, 76%, and 72%, respectively. The epidural group had a more significant number of ASA physical status III patients, which could be the probable reason for reduced overall survival and laparoscopic surgeries, which cause less immunosuppression.

Hasselager et al.[13] concluded that the association between anaesthesia and DFS was not statistically significant, with an HR of 1.04 and 95% CI = 0.98–1.11. In the study by Lee et al.,[14] 2127 patients undergoing CRC surgery under sevoflurane (inhalational) versus propofol (TIVA) were evaluated for 53-month DFS and neutrophil–lymphocyte ratio (NLR). The recurrence-free survival (HR = 1.08; 95% CI = 0.84–1.40; P = 0.54) and overall survival (HR = 1.09; 95% CI = 0.68–1.75; P = 0.72) did not significantly differ between the two groups regardless of whether sevoflurane or propofol was used. Thus, DFS was not significantly reduced, with a pooled risk ratio of 0.98 and 95% CI = 0.95–1.02 (P = 0.39). The median HR was estimated to be 1.06 on data synthesis.

Overall survival was reported in three studies.[111214] Day et al.[11] found that the 5-year overall survival after laparoscopic colorectal resection for epidural, spinal, and PCA groups was 69.4%, 78.2%, and 77%, respectively (P = 0.622). Cummings et al.[12] reported a 5-year overall survival of 61% in the epidural group and 56% in the PCA group. A significant association between epidural use and improved overall survival was found with an adjusted HR of 0.91 and 95% CI = 0.87–0.94 (P < 0.001). However, in the study by Lee et al.,[14] overall survival did not significantly differ between the two groups regardless of sevoflurane or propofol use, with an HR of 1.09 and 95% CI = 0.68–1.75 (P = 0.72).

Blood transfusion

Cummings et al.[12] reported that blood transfusion was a significant predictor of recurrence and mortality. In contrast, Day et al.[11] reported blood transfusion to have an immunosuppressive effect, but there was no conclusive evidence of an association between blood transfusion and recurrence or mortality.

Limitations of data synthesis

Diversity in intervention groups in studies and variability in reporting the outcomes resulted in the summarising of effect estimates by using the median HR. Because two studies were compared, this method did not account for the difference in the sample size in the studies.

Certainty of evidence

The GRADEpro GDT for primary outcome cancer recurrence was ‘very low’ evidence quality. There were serious issues with the precision of the included studies. The evidence quality was ‘low’ for DFS as shown in Supplementary Material 2.[16]

Clinical heterogeneity

Clinical heterogeneity was assessed by tabulating the commonalities and differences between the studies.[18] The studies are common on the grounds of study design - all are retrospective cohorts that include patients undergoing surgery for CRC. All studies evaluated adult patients belonging to ASA physical status I–III, and balanced anaesthesia was given to all patients. Major differences include different intervention groups - four studies compared GA and RA versus GA, whereas two studies compared inhalational versus TIVA. The studies had different outcomes reported and variable follow-up periods.

DISCUSSION

Our systematic review demonstrated no significant association between anaesthesia and cancer recurrence in patients undergoing colorectal surgeries under anaesthesia.

Recurrence or metastasis after cancer surgery can be due to minimal residual disease or surgical stress and/or anaesthetic agents/techniques, which may facilitate tumour spread due to the suppression of immune and inflammatory responses.[19] Neuraxial blockade in the form of epidural anaesthesia is thought to suppress neuroendocrine stress and thus preserve cellular immunity.[20] However, in animal studies, inhalational anaesthetics were found to inhibit the function of NK cells, which correlates with an increased metastatic burden.[21]Although no clear association has been found between recurrence and anaesthesia in various types of cancers, some studies have reported decreased recurrence. Biki et al.[22] conducted a retrospective study to establish an association between prostate cancer recurrence after anaesthesia. They concluded that substituting epidural analgesia for postoperative opioids was associated with substantially less risk of biochemical cancer recurrence. Larger studies of breast and lung cancer surgery have found no association between cancer recurrence and anaesthesia.[2324]

Hasselager et al.[13] found increased recurrence of cancer under inhalational compared to GA. Gottschalk et al.[9] reported no association of EA with cancer recurrence but some potential benefits in the elderly. This can be attributed to the probable reduction of opioid requirement due to EA, which, in turn, causes lesser opioid-induced depression of NK cells. Thus, older people may have potentially diminished NK cell response and may benefit more from epidural analgesia.[25] Cummings et al.[12] reported no significant reduction in cancer recurrence in the epidural group. Cancer recurrence was compared in two studies by using the median HR. We found that the median HR was 0.895, which signifies that RA has an 89.5% hazard of cancer recurrence. However, as there was a lack of studies and data, conclusive associations could not be found.

Jun et al.[26] conducted a retrospective observational study and found that TIVA during oesophageal cancer surgery was associated with better postoperative survival rates compared with volatile anaesthesia. In radical prostatectomy, Wuethrich et al.[27] performed a retrospective analysis and found that GA with EA was associated with a reduced risk of clinical cancer progression versus GA with IV analgesia; no difference was observed in biochemical recurrence-free survival or overall survival. It is also plausible that the effect of the type of anaesthesia on metastasis also depends on the type of cancer.[13]

Inhalational anaesthetic agents have been shown to systemically impair immune function by inducing T-lymphocyte apoptosis, attenuating nature killer (NK) cell activity, decreasing the Th1/Th2 ratio, and increasing the levels of pro-tumorigenic cytokines and matrix metalloproteinase (MMPs). Thus, they cause immunosuppression and promote tumour metastasis.[2829] Propofol increased cytotoxic T-lymphocyte activity, preserved NK cell function, decreased pro-tumorigenic cytokines, and exhibited anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidation properties through inhibiting cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) function.[30] The causal link between anaesthetics, perioperative immunosuppression, and survival remains to be elucidated.

Three studies did not report cancer recurrence under anaesthesia; however, other important postoperative outcomes, including DFS, overall survival, and mortality, were addressed.[101114] The type of anaesthetic agent can have long-term effects such as DFS, overall survival, and mortality.[31] Gupta et al.[10] found significantly lower mortality in patients receiving an epidural for the management of postoperative pain for rectal but not colonic cancer compared with those having PCA. Yap et al.[32] compared volatile anaesthesia and propofol-based TIVA and concluded that it improved RFS, but the choice of agent did not affect the recurrence. DFS was also not significantly reduced, with a pooled risk ratio of 0.98, 95% CI = 0.95–1.02 (P = 0.39) and a median HR of 1.06.

In the study by Day et al.[11], which evaluated patients undergoing laparoscopic colorectal resection for adenocarcinoma, postoperative EA had no significant advantage in 5-year overall or DFS over opioid analgesia. Christopherson et al.[33] reported that epidural supplementation was associated with enhanced survival among patients without metastases before 1.46 years. Still, they did not affect the survival of patients with metastases. However, the authors stated the need for additional studies to confirm or refute these findings.

In patients with rectal cancer resection (stage I–III), Wu et al.[34] concluded that postoperative EA also did not improve recurrence or mortality, with a follow-up duration of 46.1 months, when compared to opioid analgesia. A retrospective study by Exadaktylos et al.[35] demonstrated improved 3-year DFS in breast cancer surgery when using paravertebral block to supplement GA versus GA alone. In ovarian carcinoma, Lin et al.[36] found EA to be associated with reduced mortality. However, a similar study by Capmas et al.[37] and Lacassie et al.[38] found no benefit in terms of recurrence and survival.

In colorectal carcinoma with hepatic metastases for hepatic resection, Zimmitti et al.[39] found RA to be associated with improved recurrence-free survival but not overall survival. A similar study by Gao et al.[40] found RA to be associated with worse survival outcomes versus GA alone.

Other retrospective studies have been conducted on the use of perioperative EA in CRC patients. However, they reported survival as their primary outcome and thus could not be included. The drawbacks of the majority of existing studies are various confounding factors, including tumour biology and stage, allogeneic blood transfusions, other concomitant medications, and the role of neoadjuvant and adjuvant anti-cancer therapies.[41] These preoperative factors also play a major role in affecting the recurrence of cancer. There is a further need for prospective clinical trials to explain the role of anaesthetic agents on cancer recurrence and prognosis. There are several ongoing clinical trials in this area which may shed some light on the topic and could not be included.[42]

Because all included studies are retrospective, there are limitations due to missing data or misclassification. It also increases the risk of errors in terms of allocation and selection into groups and a higher number of confounding factors. However, it is worthwhile to notice that five of the six retrospective studies included a propensity-matched cohort, which does limit the risk of confounders to an extent. Various studies, retrospective and prospective, have been conducted to study recurrence and other outcomes, such as survival and mortality in different types of cancers. However, multiple retrospective studies that have been published on CRC evaluated DFS and overall survival as outcomes majorly, thus limiting our selected studies to six.

CONCLUSION

This review concludes that there is no significant association between RA and CRC recurrence. No conclusive improvement was found in DFS. The level of evidence recommended by GRADE analysis is ‘very low’ for cancer recurrence and ‘low’ for DFS. There is scope for further studies, such as well-designed RCTs with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods, to determine the association between CRC recurrence and anaesthesia.
