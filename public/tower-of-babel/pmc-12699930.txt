Paraneoplastic cochleovestibulopathy associated with breast cancer: a case report of two patients and literature review
Authors: Liqin Yang, Xueqing Liu, Wenxia Li, Songming Tang, Yanan Hu, Ting Zhang, Xun Li, Wei Yue, Shujuan Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParaneoplastic cochleovestibulopathy (PCVP) is a rare, atypical neurological paraneoplastic syndrome of the nervous system that is often misdiagnosed and mistreated.Case presentationWe report two cases of middle-aged women who presented with bilateral sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) as the initial symptom. Pure-tone audiometry (PTA) demonstrated bilateral hearing impairment, while brainstem auditory evoked potentials (BAEP) and cochlear electrograms were within normal limits. Otoacoustic emissions indicated bilateral cochlear dysfunction. Cranial imaging excluded intracranial organic and vascular lesions. After corticosteroid therapy proved ineffective, positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) combined with histopathological examination confirmed the presence of breast cancer, leading to a diagnosis of PCVP. Despite receiving endocrine therapy (Case 1) or surgical excision (Case 2), neither patient exhibited significant improvement in PTA during one year of follow-up.ConclusionPCVP should be considered in middle-aged patients who present with progressive bilateral hearing loss, often accompanied by vestibular dysfunction and clinical signs of cerebellar or brainstem involvement. Regular monitoring with PET/CT is recommended, and female patients in particular should undergo screening for breast cancer.Keywords: Hearing loss, Breast cancer, Neurological paraneoplastic syndrome, Case report

Background

Paraneoplastic cochleovestibulopathy (PCVP) was first described by Greene in 2014 as an atypical form of paraneoplastic neurological syndrome (PNS) [1]. It is characterized by sudden or rapidly progressive hearing loss or vestibular dysfunction that is unresponsive to high-dose oral or intratympanic steroid therapy and is typically accompanied by the presence of malignant tumors. In most cases, antibodies associated with high-risk paraneoplastic tumors can be detected [1]. Previous case reports have described associations between PCVP and several malignancies, including breast adenocarcinoma [2], small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) [3], thymoma [4], and lymphoma [5]. Although PCVP is not classified as a classical subtype of PNS, paraneoplastic mechanisms remain a potential etiology of cochleovestibular dysfunction [6, 7]. Due to its atypical clinical manifestations, PCVP is often misdiagnosed and inappropriately treated. Its rarity further limits the number of reported cases, hindering a comprehensive understanding of its clinical features. Here, we present two cases of Chinese patients with PCVP associated with breast cancer, both of whom exhibited sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) and vertigo as initial symptoms, along with a literature review to raise clinical awareness of this rare syndrome.

Case presentation

Case 1

A 40-year-old woman was admitted to Tianjin Huanhu Hospital one month after the sudden onset of vertigo with unsteady gait. Ten months earlier, she had experienced left-sided hearing loss of unknown etiology, which later progressed to involve the right ear. Over the following month, she developed bilateral progressive hearing loss, more pronounced on the right side, despite treatment with oral prednisone during that period (Fig. 1A, B). One month before admission, she underwent cochlear implantation. Neurological examination revealed impulsive and monotonous speech with suspected explosive verbal output, bilateral nystagmus, and cerebellar ataxia. Pure-tone audiometry (PTA) demonstrated a 60 dB threshold, consistent with bilateral SNHL, while brainstem auditory evoked potentials (BAEP) and cochlear electrograms were normal. Computed tomography (CT) of the temporal bone, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain, and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) showed no structural abnormalities. Cerebrospinal fluid and serum tissue-based assays revealed positive immunofluorescence staining on cerebellar, hippocampal, and cortical neuronal membranes, suggesting the presence of autoantibodies against neuronal antigens, although specific antibodies were not identified. The patient received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and intravenous methylprednisolone. Although she experienced slight relief from dizziness, her hearing loss did not improve.

At the three-month follow-up, the patient underwent a chest CT scan due to respiratory problems, and the results showed multiple enlarged lymph nodes in the right axilla. Breast ultrasonography identified a deep hypoechoic nodule with calcification in the right nipple region, categorized as BI-RADS 4a. Histopathological examination confirmed poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma. Biopsy of the right axillary lymph nodes (hematoxylin and eosin staining, 40×) demonstrated fibrous and lymphatic tissue with background infiltration of poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma (Fig. 2). Immunohistochemical analysis showed approximately 100% estrogen receptor positivity, HER2 (2+), CK (+), CK7 (+), and GATA-3 (+), with negative expression for CgA, Syn, CD56, P40, SOX-2, GCDFP-15, and PAX-8, consistent with a mammary origin. Positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) and pathological findings confirmed metastatic adenocarcinoma in the right axillary lymph node. The patient declined surgical intervention and was treated with endocrine therapy for breast cancer. At the one-year follow-up, she remained on endocrine therapy, and serial chest CT scans demonstrated a reduction in the size of the right axillary lymph nodes (Fig. 3). Unfortunately, hearing outcomes after cochlear implantation could not be assessed.

Case 2

A 54-year-old woman was admitted to Tianjin Huanhu Hospital with a complaint of vertigo persisting for more than two weeks. Physical examination revealed bilateral nystagmus and cerebellar ataxia. She had a 10-year history of autoimmune thyroid disease (ATD) and had been receiving continuous levothyroxine therapy, with doses adjusted between 50 and 75 µg according to regular laboratory monitoring. During the current hospitalization, thyroid function was well controlled (TSH: 1.5 mIU/L). Repeat antibody testing showed a reduction in the anti-TPO antibody titer to 180 IU/mL, and thyroid ultrasonography demonstrated mild thyroid atrophy compared with previous examinations.

The patient had developed unexplained bilateral hearing loss eight months before admission. PTA revealed air-conduction thresholds averaging 50 dB in both ears and an A-type audiometric pattern, consistent with bilateral SNHL. BAEP and cochleography were normal, whereas otoacoustic emissions indicated cochlear dysfunction in both ears. Temporal bone CT and cranial MRI revealed no significant abnormalities. Serum and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) testing for a comprehensive panel of paraneoplastic antibodies (anti-Hu, -Yo, -Ri, -SOX1, -Titin, -Recoverin, -Ma2/Ta, -Ma1, -CV2, -Amphiphysin, -GAD65, -Tr, -Zic4, and -PKCγ) was negative. The patient underwent a one-month course of oral prednisolone and intravenous immunoglobulin therapy, however, her hearing did not improve (Fig. 1C and D). Subsequent systemic PET/CT revealed a breast mass, and pathological examination confirmed invasive ductal carcinoma. The tumor was surgically excised shortly after diagnosis. At one-year follow-up, the patient reported resolution of vertigo, however, her hearing did not significantly recover, and she required a hearing aid.

Based on the clinical characteristics and the detection of breast cancer, we diagnosed these two patients with PCVP associated with breast cancer. Literature review

To summarize the clinical features of PCVP, we conducted an electronic search of the PubMed database for articles published between 1980 and 2024. The search strategy included the following terms: ((“cochleovestibulopathy” OR “cochlear vestibular disease” OR “hearing-vestibular disorders” OR “inner ear disease”) AND (“paraneoplastic neuropathy” OR “paraneoplastic syndrome” OR “paraneoplastic neurological syndrome”)) AND ((“1980/01/01” [Date—Publication]: “2024/12/30” [Date—Publication])). Eligible studies were screened and assessed for inclusion.

We identified 46 patients from five published studies and combined them with the two cases presented herein, yielding a total of 48 patients [2, 3, 5, 7, 8]. The clinical characteristics of these patients are summarized in Table 1. Among the 48 patients, 33 were male. In a retrospective analysis by Greene et al. [5], the median age of onset for SNHL was 42 years, with a predominance in females (n = 9, 69%). Malignancies were diagnosed between two weeks and two years following SNHL onset, with tumors rarely preceding neurological symptoms. In contrast, Hammami et al. [7] reported that the majority of patients were male (n = 23, 88%), with KLHL11-IgG positivity and seminoma as the most common associations, the average age of onset was 45 years.

The initial clinical presentation of PCVP typically involves rapidly progressive bilateral SNHL and vestibular dysfunction. These symptoms often co-occur with, or are followed by, brainstem or cerebellar involvement. The most frequent symptoms (Fig. 5A) included SNHL (46/48, 96%), ataxia (36/48, 75%), limb dysmetria (22/48, 46%), diplopia (21/48, 43%), tinnitus (19/48, 40%), nystagmus (18/48, 37%), dysarthria (14/48, 29%), and vertigo (12/48, 25%). Less common symptoms included altered mental status. Notably, two patients presented with isolated tinnitus [7]; thus, the possibility of PCVP should be considered even in the absence of marked hearing loss. Hearing loss patterns were available for 24 patients (Fig. 5B). The most common audiometric pattern was flat hearing loss (n = 12, 50%), followed by high-frequency loss (n = 9, 37%) and low- to mid-frequency loss (n = 3, 13%). Excluding six cases where paraneoplastic antibody testing was not performed, the most frequently detected antibody was KLHL11-IgG (n = 21, 44%) (Fig. 5C), followed by Hu-IgG (n = 5, 10%), ANNA1-IgG (n = 3, 6%), amphiphysin-IgG (n = 2, 4%), LUZP4-IgG (n = 1, 2%), AChR-IgG combined with VGKC-IgG (n = 1, 2%), and GAD65-IgG (n = 1, 2%). Seven patients tested negative for known paraneoplastic antibodies [2, 3, 5, 7, 8]. The most commonly associated malignancies were testicular/extratesticular germ cell tumors (n = 14, 29%), lung cancer (n = 7, 15%), breast cancer (n = 5, 11%), and thymoma (n = 5, 10%) (Fig. 5D).

Due to the rarity of PCVP, no large-scale randomized controlled trials have been conducted to evaluate treatment efficacy. The primary therapeutic goal is the management of the underlying malignancy. A retrospective study reported that patients who received oncologic treatment (radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and/or surgery) were 1.5 times more likely to experience neurological improvement than those who did not receive treatment [10]. However, Greene [5] concluded that no treatment modality, including IVIG, corticosteroids, or immunosuppressants, significantly improved hearing outcomes. Similarly, Hammami et al.[6] found that corticosteroid therapy worsened hearing in 16 cases (73%) and improved it in only 2 cases (9%).

In our cases, Case 1 received oncologic treatment after the diagnosis of breast cancer, with follow-up showing a reduction in the size of the right axillary lymph node, though the hearing prognosis could not be evaluated post-cochlear implantation. Case 2 underwent surgical resection of the breast tumor; however, her hearing did not significantly improve, and she eventually required a hearing aid.

Discussion

PCVP is a rare clinical entity that remains diagnostically challenging, particularly because neurological symptoms often precede identification of the underlying malignancy. Differential diagnoses must carefully exclude direct neoplastic involvement of the nervous system, treatment-induced neurotoxicity, metabolic disturbances, and infections. In this report, we describe two additional cases that contribute to the growing body of literature on PCVP and provide further clinical insight into this rare syndrome.

The pathogenesis of PCVP is not yet fully understood. Early research by Chen et al. [11] suggested that antigen-induced cytokine production, especially elevated interleukin-2 levels, may cause damage to auditory and vestibular structures, leading to symptoms such as tinnitus, vertigo, and hearing loss. In severe cases, this may culminate in cochlear fibrosis and ossification. More recent studies support a mechanism involving immune cross-reactivity [12]. Specifically, it has been proposed that antibodies generated against tumor antigens may cross-react with inner ear melanocytes, disrupting endolymphatic homeostasis and inducing auditory-vestibular dysfunction [1, 13]. In a pathological study of paraneoplastic anti-KLHL11-IgG ence observed a T-cell–mediated inflammatory response characterized by non-necrotizing granulomas was observed, suggesting that T-cell autoimmunity plays a critical role in disease pathogenesis.

Previous studies have shown that PCVP is often associated with central nervous system disorders, such as encephalitis or encephalomyelitis, which may include symptoms such as ataxia, dizziness, dysarthria, and nystagmus [7]. In our cases, the presence of bilateral nystagmus of the eyes, cerebellar ataxia, and explosive speech in Case 1 further supports the involvement of cerebellar circuits. Audiometric evaluations in PCVP typically demonstrate flat or high-frequency SNHL in both ears. This pattern differs significantly from that of Ménière’s disease, which often presents with fluctuating low-frequency hearing loss in a single ear, frequently accompanied by tinnitus. Therefore, distinguishing PCVP from peripheral vestibulopathies is critical, particularly in cases of unilateral low-frequency hearing loss [14, 15]. In Case 1, no definitive paraneoplastic antibodies were detected, yet the diagnosis of PCVP remained clinically supported. It is important to recognize that a negative paraneoplastic antibody panel does not rule out PCVP, as the antibody spectrum continues to evolve. Several factors may account for antibody non-detection, including low antibody titers, degradation, tissue sequestration, the presence of non–humorally mediated mechanisms, or altered antigenic epitopes that evade detection by available assays.

PCVP syndromes share certain clinical manifestations with autoimmune vestibular disorders, which can easily lead to diagnostic confusion. Therefore, differential diagnosis is essential. We summarize four key distinctions [16–18]. First, the etiology and pathogenesis differ. Paraneoplastic syndromes are remote effects of malignant tumors, typically mediated by anti-neuronal antibodies. In contrast, primary autoimmune vestibulopathy is usually associated with systemic autoimmune diseases (e.g., sarcoidosis, lupus, rheumatoid arthritis) or may be confined to the inner ear/vestibular system, and its specific antibodies often remain unidentified. Second, the clinical manifestations differ. Although both conditions can cause vertigo, unsteadiness, and hearing impairment, paraneoplastic disorders are more frequently accompanied by neurological “red flags,” such as cerebellar ataxia, ocular motor disturbances (e.g., gaze palsy, opsoclonus-myoclonus), and severe dysarthria, as observed in our reported case [17]. By contrast, isolated vestibular symptoms are more indicative of primary autoimmune vestibulopathy. Third, the diagnostic markers differ. A positive antibody test (e.g., anti-Yo, anti-Ri, anti-Hu) strongly supports a paraneoplastic etiology. We also discussed that, even in seronegative cases, the diagnosis may lean toward a paraneoplastic cause based on clinical features, response to treatment, and the eventual discovery of an underlying tumor. Finally, the response to treatment and prognosis differ. Primary autoimmune vestibulopathy may respond favorably to corticosteroids [16], whereas management of paraneoplastic syndromes focuses on treating the underlying tumor, and recovery of neurological symptoms is often more challenging and may be incomplete. Vertigo, SNHL, and tinnitus are common and specific clinical symptoms in patients with paraneoplastic anti-KLHL11-IgG encephalitis [19], whose hearing loss first involves high-frequency hearing in both ears and is specific to men [19, 20]. We found that male patients presenting with progressive cochlear vestibular symptoms and progressive ataxia should be evaluated for paraneoplastic KLHL11-IgG and potential germ cell tumors, and that early recognition would have an impact on neurological and tumor prognosis, as aggressive immunosuppression could benefit patients [8, 19, 20]. In contrast, female patients should be closely monitored for breast cancer.

Conclusion

We report two cases of PCVP in middle-aged women presenting with progressive bilateral SNHL as the initial symptom, later found to have breast cancer. In both cases, hearing loss originated in the cochlea and progressed more rapidly than age-related hearing loss but more gradually than sudden deafness, typically evolving over several days to months. PCVP should be considered in middle-aged patients with progressive bilateral SNHL, especially when corticosteroid therapy is ineffective and other causes have been excluded. Whole-body PET/CT should be incorporated into the diagnostic workup, with particular attention to breast cancer screening in female patients and testicular/extratesticular germ cell tumor screening with KLHL11-IgG testing in male patients. Our literature review highlights the importance of early recognition and comprehensive oncological evaluation in patients presenting with unexplained progressive cochleovestibular symptoms.
