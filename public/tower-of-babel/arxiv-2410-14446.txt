1.
Introduction

Let
\displaystyle G
be a finite group and let
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}[G]
be the integral group ring of
\displaystyle G
. The group

K_{-1}\mathbb{Z}[G]

naturally appears as a building block for the computation of higher
\displaystyle K
-theory and
\displaystyle A
-theory groups, for example via the Farrell-Jones conjecture
[
Lüc05
]
, and plays an important role in questions in geometric topology. An understanding of the groups
\displaystyle G
for which
\displaystyle K_{-1}\mathbb{Z}[G]
is non-trivial is thus useful both for calculations as well as for the construction of counterexamples. Fortunately, the negative
\displaystyle K
-theory of finite groups remains fairly accessible by means of representation theoretic tools, and a general description has been given by Carter.

Theorem 1.1

(Carter,
[
Car80
]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle G
be finite. The group
\displaystyle K_{-1}\mathbb{Z}G
has the form

K_{-1}\mathbb{Z}[G]=\mathbb{Z}^{r}\oplus(\mathbb{Z}/2)^{s}

where

r=1-r_{\mathbb{Q}}+\sum_{p|~|G|}(r_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}-r_{\mathbb{F}_{p}})

and
\displaystyle s
is equal to the number of irreducible
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
-representations
\displaystyle I
with even Schur index
\displaystyle m(I)
but odd local Schur index
\displaystyle m_{p}(I)
at every prime
\displaystyle p
dividing the order of
\displaystyle G
.

Further computations of negative
\displaystyle K
-theory can be found in Lafont, Magurn, Ortiz
[
LMO09
]
for the groups
\displaystyle D_{n}
,
\displaystyle D_{n}\times C_{2}
and
\displaystyle A_{5}\times C_{2}
, as well as in Magurn
[
Mag13
]
for the groups
\displaystyle C_{n}
,
\displaystyle\text{Dic}_{n}
,
\displaystyle\tilde{T}
,
\displaystyle\tilde{O}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{I}
. As is clear from Carter’s theorem, a computation of the negative
\displaystyle K
-theory boils down to the computation of the values
\displaystyle r
and
\displaystyle s
for a concrete group
\displaystyle G
. We will show how to do this algorithmically and use the computer language
\displaystyle GAP
,
[
20
]
, to create tables of all groups of order
\displaystyle\leq 28
together with the value of
\displaystyle K_{-1}\mathbb{Z}[G]
, as well as tables of all groups of order
\displaystyle\leq 100
for which
\displaystyle s>0
.

Remark 1.2
.

A more conceptual understanding of the groups
\displaystyle K_{-1}\mathbb{Z}[G]
as equivalence classes of
singular characters
of
\displaystyle G
is possible, and is discussed in previous work of the author
[

]
.

In the following, fix a finite group
\displaystyle G

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle G

K_{-1}\mathbb{Z}[G]\cong\mathbb{Z}^{r(G)}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/2^{s(G)}

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle G

r(G)=1-r_{\mathbb{Q}}+\sum_{p|n}(r_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}-r_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}),

where
\displaystyle r_{k}

\displaystyle G
over the field
\displaystyle k
and
\displaystyle s(G)
is equal to the number of isomorphism classes of irreducible
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle G

2.1.
The free rank
\displaystyle r(G)

To compute
\displaystyle r(G)
we need to compute the number of irreducible representations of
\displaystyle G
over the fields
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{F}_{p}
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}_{p}
. An essential tool for this is Berman’s theorem. For the following, fix a field
\displaystyle k
of characteristic
\displaystyle p
. We call an element
\displaystyle g
of
\displaystyle G

\displaystyle p
-
singular
if
\displaystyle p
divides the order of
\displaystyle g
and
\displaystyle p
-
regular
otherwise. Note that if
\displaystyle p
does not divide the order of
\displaystyle G
, all elements of
\displaystyle G
are
\displaystyle p
-regular. Let
\displaystyle m
be the exponent of
\displaystyle G
, i.e. the least common multiple over all orders of elements of
\displaystyle G
. Denote by
\displaystyle\zeta_{m}
a primitive
\displaystyle m
-th root of unity. The Galois group
\displaystyle\text{Gal}(k(\zeta_{m}):k)
can by identified with a subgroup
\displaystyle T_{m}
of
\displaystyle(\mathbb{Z}/m)^{\times}
, since any
\displaystyle\phi\in\text{Gal}(k(\zeta_{m}):k)
is uniquely defined by its image of
\displaystyle\zeta_{m}
, and so
\displaystyle\phi(\zeta_{m})=\zeta_{m}^{t}
for a unique
\displaystyle t\in T_{m}
. We say two elements
\displaystyle g_{1}
and
\displaystyle g_{2}
of
\displaystyle G
are
\displaystyle k

if there exists
\displaystyle h\in G
and a
\displaystyle t\in T_{m}
such that
\displaystyle g_{1}^{t}=hg_{2}h^{-1}

Theorem 2.1

(Berman,
[
Ber56
]
, see also
[
Rei64
]
)
.

The number of irreducible
\displaystyle k
-representations of
\displaystyle G
is equal to the number of
\displaystyle k
-conjugacy classes of
\displaystyle p
-regular elements of
\displaystyle G
.

For the field
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
, the Galois group
\displaystyle\text{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m}):\mathbb{Q})
is always equal to the entire group
\displaystyle(\mathbb{Z}/m)^{\times}
. The number
\displaystyle r_{\mathbb{Q}}
is thus equal to the number of conjugacy classes of cyclic subgroups of
\displaystyle G
.

In the case of the field
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}_{p}
a number
\displaystyle t
is in
\displaystyle t\in T_{m}
iff
\displaystyle t
is not divisible by
\displaystyle p
and
\displaystyle t
is congruent to a power of
\displaystyle p
mod
\displaystyle\mu

[
GS13
]
Chapter IV, Section 4.

The following lemma will also be useful.

Lemma 2.2

(Magurn
[
Mag13
]
, see Lemma 1.)

For each prime
\displaystyle p

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle r_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}-r_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}
is equal to the number of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-conjugacy classes of
\displaystyle p
-singular elements in
\displaystyle G

Remark 2.3
.

In particular, if the group
\displaystyle G
is a
\displaystyle p
-group, all non-trivial elements of
\displaystyle G
are
\displaystyle p
-singular, hence the number
\displaystyle r_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}-r_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-conjugacy classes of non-trivial elements. In this case the exponent

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}_{p}

More generally,

Remark 2.4

We want to summarize two conditions for the vanishing of negative

•

The free rank
\displaystyle r

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle K_{-1}Z[G]
when the order
\displaystyle n
of the group
\displaystyle G
is divisible by
\displaystyle 4
. Moreover, we want to highlight that the binary polyhedral groups

\text{Dic}_{n},\tilde{O},\tilde{I}

play a special role as examples of groups with
\displaystyle s>0
. These are finite subgroups of
\displaystyle SU(2)
, called dicyclic group of order
\displaystyle n
for
\displaystyle n\geq 4
, binary octahedral and binary icosahedral group, respectively. They are defined as the inverse images under the double covering
\displaystyle SU(2)\rightarrow SO(3)
of the groups

C_{2n},O,I

with
\displaystyle C_{2n}
the cyclic group of order
\displaystyle 2n
,
\displaystyle O
being the octahedral group and
\displaystyle I
the icosahedral group, thought of as the symmetries of the regular
\displaystyle 2n
-gon, cube and icosahedron, respectively. Magurn
[
Mag13
]
computes the negative
\displaystyle K
-theory groups of all of these. The smallest group with
\displaystyle s>0
is the group
\displaystyle Q_{16}=\text{Dic}_{4}
. Note that if
\displaystyle n=2^{k}
is a power of
\displaystyle 2
, the group
\displaystyle\text{Dic}_{n}
is also called generalized quaternion group
\displaystyle Q_{2^{k+2}}
of order
\displaystyle 4\cdot 2^{k}=2^{k+2}
.

Similarly to the previous section, in order to compute a table of all groups with non-trivial
\displaystyle s
, we use the function sOfGroup constructed in the previous section as well as the functionality provided by the SmallGroup library in
\displaystyle GAP
. We will only print groups with non-trivial
\displaystyle s
. If the group
\displaystyle G
is of the form
\displaystyle\text{Dic}_{n},\tilde{O}
,
\displaystyle\tilde{I}
, or a product of these and another group, we address the group as such in the column “Structure”. If it is not of this form, we include a printout of the functionality StructureDescription of
\displaystyle GAP
. Just as in the previous section, we want to warn the reader that StructureDescription(G) does not specify the group
\displaystyle G
up to isomorphism, but only serves to give a quick idea of the type of group which
\displaystyle G
is.

If
\displaystyle G
has a quotient
\displaystyle G/N
with
\displaystyle s(G/N)>0
, then each irreducible
\displaystyle G/N
-representation
\displaystyle(I,\rho)
that contributes to
\displaystyle s(G/N)
gives a
\displaystyle G
-representation
\displaystyle(I,\rho\circ\pi)
with
\displaystyle\pi\colon G\rightarrow G/N
, which contributes to
\displaystyle s
. In the column “Quotients” we have included all smaller groups with
\displaystyle s>0
which appear as quotients. Most groups which are not binary polyhedral groups which appear in our list are, in fact, explained by having binary polyhedral groups as quotients, i.e. the group ring
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}[G]
fails the Eichler condition.

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}[G]
is the condition that
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}[G]\otimes\mathbb{R}\cong\mathbb{R}G
contains no copies of the quaternions
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}
in its Wedderburn decomposition, see
[
SE70
]
, Chapter 9., also
[
Nic20
]
.
