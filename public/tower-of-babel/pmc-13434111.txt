Morpho-physical and biochemical traits associated with potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) genotypes conferring resistance to potato tuber moth, Phthorimaea operculella (Zeller)
Authors: Shashikumar E. Shivannanavara, Kennedy Ningthoujam, Mareena Sorokhaibam, Adarsh Kumar, Jyotim Gogoi, A. P. Abhishek Gowda, Mahesh Pathak, Rajkumar Josmee Singh, Som Dutt, Dwipendra Thakuria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Potato is a nutrient dense crop providing livelihoods to millions in developing countries and has become an important non-cereal crop worldwide. Potato is a versatile crop grown for food and industrial purposes. The Potato Tuber Moth, Phthorimaea operculella is a destructive pest of potato both on field and storage. Its damage threatens potato production and livelihoods of many potato growers particularly small landholder farmers. In this study, nine local and six commercial potato varieties were screened for resistance to potato tuber moth based on their ovipositional and feeding preferences in the foliage parts of potato plant. The role of the associated morpho-physical and biochemical traits of the potato varieties in conferring the resistance was assessed. The local varieties were comparatively resistant to potato tuber moth than the commercial varieties. Local varieties Phan San Minit and Lah Ksain were less preferred for both oviposition and feeding by potato tuber moth. Expression of resistance was found positively correlated with higher glandular and non-glandular trichome density on leaves, higher trichome length, high concentration of glycoalkaloids α-solanine and α-chaconine; and phenolic compounds chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid and flavonoids compared to the susceptible Kufri Jyoti and Kufri Megha varieties. Whereas, total soluble sugars had little role in conferring resistance to potato tuber moth. The resistant potato varieties with these traits may be used as sources of resistance in future breeding programs to enhance resistance to potato tuber moth in cultivated potato varieties.

Introduction

Potatoes are considered the fourth most significant food crop worldwide, following rice, wheat and maize, and they contribute substantially to global food security1. Global potato production reached 383 million tons with China and India being the leading producers, contributing 93.5 and 60.1 million tons, respectively2. Potato is typically a temperate crop and is mainly grown as winter crop in India. Under high temperature, the tuber formation is affected which leads to poor yield. Meghalaya’s climatic condition characterized by cool and frost-free seasons with high relative humidity and rainfall are best suitable for potato production. The average potato production in Meghalaya is 196.125 metric tons3. Meghalaya has a relatively higher proportion of total cropped area to potato cultivation than several major potato-producing states in India. However, the state’s average potato productivity remains relatively low, ranging from approximately 9.1 to 9.2 tons per hectare, which is nearly half the national average of 18–23 tons per hectare4. The decline in potato production can be attributed to a range of constraints among which insect pests represent a major limiting factor. In Meghalaya, the major insect pests affecting potato crops include cutworms, white grubs, leaf-eating caterpillars and aphids5; among which the potato tuber moth (PTM), Phthorimaea operculella (Lepidoptera: Gelechiidae) is considered the most destructive pest affecting potato cultivation causing significant losses in both field and storage conditions. In field condition it causes mining on foliage and exposed tubers and in storage condition it causes mining of tubers thereby facilitating secondary infections by fungi and bacteria, leading to partial or complete rotting of tubers. PTM exhibits a preference for oviposition on the foliage of host plants. This pest has been reported to feed on over 60 plant species worldwide with majority belonging to the Solanaceae family6.

PTM caused extensive damage in East Khasi Hills district of Meghalaya which can exceed 50% in plant infestations and reached 100% tuber damage under indigenous storage conditions intended for seed preservation7.

Although chemical pesticides are effective in controlling PTM, their continuous and repetitive use caused environmental contamination, development of insecticidal resistance and poses significant health risks to both consumers and growers. An alternative control method viz., host plant resistance serves as the first line of defense against pest attacks and minimizes the use of chemical pesticide and hence is environmentally safe and sustainable. Resistance mechanisms such as antixenosis (non-preference) and antibiosis (adverse effects on pest biology) play a critical role in enhancing the resistance of potato plants and tubers to PTM.

Numerous studies have investigated varietal resistance in potatoes8–17. Morphological traits of host plants particularly leaf trichomes, play a significant role in influencing insect behavior and population dynamics18. Trichomes are often the first point of contact for arthropods with the plant surfaces, making them critical determinants of antixenosis resistance mechanisms. Such resistance which deters feeding or oviposition is frequently associated with the presence, type and density of trichomes and is especially prevalent in major crop groups, including solanaceous plants13. Trichomes are broadly categorized into two types: glandular, which possess a glandular head and non-glandular. Trichomes perform several ecological functions by conferring tolerance to biotic and abiotic stresses, such as protection against the attack of insects and microorganisms, excess ultraviolet light, heat, drought and heavy metals19. Both types have been well documented for their protective roles in which glandular trichomes through the secretion of chemical compounds and non-glandular trichomes through their structural attributes help plants adapt to or defend against environmental stress such as UV radiation and low temperatures20. Glandular trichomes also contribute to herbivore deterrence by physically entrapping insects in sticky secretions and releasing defensive chemicals21.

In potatoes, mainly there are two major biochemicals that resist the attack of PTM pests: glycoalkaloids (α-solanine & α-chaconine) and phenolics (chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid, flavonoids)22. The glycoalkaloids such as α-solanine and α-chaconine constitute approximately 95% of the total glycoalkaloid (GA) content and are thus commonly referred to as total glycoalkaloids23. These compounds function as natural defense agents, exhibiting toxicity against a range of insect pests including PTM larvae by inhibiting feeding and disrupting development. Glycoalkaloids content of the foliage is also an important factor in protecting plants from fungi, insects and predators. GA is naturally present in all potato tissues throughout the plant’s growth cycle and tend to accumulate in higher concentrations in metabolically active organs such as young leaves, flowers and unripe berries24,25.

Phenolic compounds in potatoes particularly chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid22 and various flavonoids26 are known for their antioxidant properties and have also been shown to exert insecticidal effects by interfering with insect metabolism and development.

Potato varieties which were earlier used by Lyngkhoi, P. R27. for screening of tuber parts were used in the present study for screening and evaluation for resistance to P. operculella in foliage parts and to explore the possible role of various morphological and biochemical factors in the expression of plant resistance. Identifying the source of resistance and exploiting it in the form of resistance varieties is an eco-friendly and economically viable strategy for insect pest management.

Materials and methods

The present study was carried out in the laboratory and polyhouse of Entomology, School of Crop Protection, College of Post Graduate Studies in Agricultural Sciences (CPGS-AS), CAU (I), Umiam, Meghalaya, India, which is located at N 25.6532º, E 91.8843º.

Potato varieties

The commercial potato varieties viz., Kufri Jyoti, Kufri Megha, Kufri Kanchan, Kufri Girdhari, Kufri Giriraj and Kufri Himalini were procured from Indian Council of Agricultural Research, Central Potato Research Station (ICAR-CPRS), Upper Shillong, Meghalaya. The local varieties were collected from farmers and local markets across East Khasi Hills and West Khasi Hills districts of Meghalaya. The local varieties viz., Lah Saw Khasi, Phan Saw, Lah Khasi, Lah Ksain were obtained from Lewduh market, East Khasi Hills; Lah Shidieng, Lah Saw Smit, Lah Taret were obtained from Smit, East Khasi Hills; Lah Cabirad from Pepbah market, East Khasi Hills and Phan San Minit from Nongstoin, West Khasi Hills. These local varieties are majorly grown in East Khasi Hills and West Khasi Hills districts of Meghalaya and are potential source of micronutrients particularly Zinc28. However, they generally produce lower yields than commercial varieties. The cultivar Kufri Giriraj (240.7 q/ha) and Kufri Jyoti (214.2 q/ha) recorded significantly higher mean yields than local varieties Lah Taret (194.7 q/ha) and Lah Syntiew (192.2 q/ha)29. Consequently, commercial varieties are increasingly replacing traditional local varieties due to their superior yield performance.

Insect rearing

The potato tubers infested with Potato tuber moth (PTM) were collected from Indian Council of Agricultural Research, Central Potato Research Station (ICAR-CPRS), Upper Shillong, Meghalaya. The collected tubers were kept in rearing cages (45 × 45 × 45 cm) and were incubated at 25 ± 2º C with 70 ± 5% RH. Fresh tubers were introduced every twenty days in order to maintain the insect colony. The pupal stages were collected and kept in plastic jar of dimension 10 × 3.5 cm and covered with muslin cloth for adult emergence. A filter paper was provided above the muslin cloth for oviposition. Cotton swab with 5% honey solution with vitamin E was provided as a source of food for the adults. The eggs collected were kept in a plastic jar until hatching; after which, fresh tubers were provided for neonate development for second generation and so on28. In this way, the rearing cycle and multiplication of PTM was continued to provide sufficient eggs for the experiment.

Experimental design

Screening of potato varieties were done based on ovipositional and feeding preferences through choice and non-choice test of the foliage parts. Choice test was conducted in Entomology laboratory and non-choice test was conducted in polyhouse condition, each with three replications in a Complete Randomized Design (CRD). Evaluation of resistance was done based on foliage characteristics and biochemical factors, which were studied in the entomology laboratory.

Evaluation based on oviposition in choice test

The oviposition chamber consisted of a wire screen cage measuring 45 cm in length, 45 cm in breadth and 54 cm in height in which the above cut plant parts (45 Days after planting) of 25 cm (includes leaves, stem and flower) of each variety was kept inserted in a conical flask (containing water) and placed inside the chamber on a tray in a randomized manner. Ten pairs of virgin male and female potato tuber moths were introduced into the cage and allowed free access inside the chamber. No additional food or water was provided. Each test was replicated three times. The test was conducted under controlled conditions at 25± 2º C with 60–70% R.H. and 12 h photoperiod. After 72 h the adults were removed from the chamber and the location and numbers of eggs laid were recorded as described by Gurr31 with slight modification in size of cage i.e., measuring 45 cm × 45 cm × 54 cm (length × breadth × height) and numbers of male and female released.

Evaluation of potato varieties based on ovipositional and feeding preference in non-choice test

To conduct the non-choice ovipositional preference test, one plant of each variety was cultivated in individual pots, with three replications inside the polyhouse. As the plants reached maturity (45–55 Days after planting), a protective nylon net was placed over each pot to shield the plant from any external interference. Specifically, ten adult insects were introduced into each pot, maintaining a sex ratio 1:1 (five males to five females), allowing them to mate and lay eggs. After 72 h, the adults were removed from the cages and the number of eggs deposited on upper and lower surface of the leaves and on the petiole was counted to determine the susceptibility of different potato cultivars to potato tuber moth, as described by Wang et al32. with slight modification. The eggs were allowed to hatch directly on the foliage for feeding preference analysis. Fifteen days later, the infested leaves were harvested and the feeding preference of the neonate larvae was evaluated using the scale described by Fenemore33. The varieties were rated (score 0–7) at different levels of infestation based on mining on the leaves viz., clean (no symptoms on leaves) as 0, slight infestation (small narrow mining covering 1% or less leaf area) as 1, slightly to moderate infestation (small narrow mining covering 1–10% of leaf area) as 3, moderate infestation (wider mining covering 11–25% of leaf area) as 5 and severe infestation (infestation causing rolling of the leaves) as 7 (Table 1).

Morphological factors of resistance

The evaluation of resistance was conducted by analyzing the foliage characteristics namely, leaf thickness, trichome type (glandular and non-glandular), trichome density, trichome length, distance between the trichomes and trichome shape (straight/curved). The cut leaf sections (about 1 × 1 cm) from the collected leaves were mounted on microscope slides using a mounting medium (water). The leaf sections were ensured to be flat and evenly mounted. The leaf sections were examined under a zoom stereomicroscope (375X) to observe the trichomes.

Biochemical factors of resistance

The biochemical basis of resistance against PTM was studied using leaf samples. The phenolic compounds (chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid and flavonoids) and total soluble sugar (TSS) were estimated for all the fifteen varieties. Leaf samples from growing shoots of 45–55 days old plant were collected, dried and powdered which were used for biochemical analysis. The amount of chlorogenic acid was estimated using the procedures suggested by Griffiths et al34. by using commercially available 5-caffeoylquinic acid. Caffeic acid was determined by UV-vis spectrophotometer as described by Luthria35. The concentration of caffeic acid was determined from a standard curve prepared using caffeic acid in the range of 10–100 µg and results were expressed as mg caffeic acid equivalents (CAE) per 100 g DW. Flavonoids were determined by methanolic extract method as given by Balaba et al.36. The concentration of flavonoids was determined from a standard curve prepared using rutin in the range of 40–200 µg and results were expressed as mg rutin equivalents (RE) per 100 g DW. Total soluble sugars were estimated by using the methodology given by Dubois et al37.. The concentration of total soluble sugar was calculated from glucose standards (10–50 µg).

Glycoalkaloid (α-solanine and α-chaconine) estimation was conducted on leaf samples. For these, four cultivars were selected for analysis based on prior screening results i.e., two varieties exhibiting slight infestation, one with moderate infestation and one with severe infestation were chosen. For quantification of glycoalkaloids, leaf samples were sent to Central Potato Research Institute (CPRI), Shimla, Himachal Pradesh.

Statistical analysis

All data were subjected to a normality test using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Trichome density were log-transformed, while data from the biochemical analyses were square root-transformed to meet the assumptions of normality. Statistical analysis was performed using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) under a Completely Randomized Design (CRD). Treatment means were compared at a significance level of 0.05. Significant differences between mean values were determined using Duncan’s Multiple Range Test (DMRT). Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to assess the relationships between variables. All analyses were conducted using SPSS software.

Feeding preference study

The extent of foliage damage varied considerably among the fifteen potato varieties, with damage scores ranging from 1 to 7, representing infestation levels from slight to severe. The varieties Phan San Minit, Lah Ksain, Lah Saw Smit and Lah Saw Khasi were assigned Score 1, exhibiting only small narrow mining covering 1% or less of the leaf area. Kufri Jyoti, Kufri Megha and Kufri Giriraj were assigned Score 7, characterized by rolled leaves, indicating high susceptibility to PTM (Table 1).

Non-glandular trichome characterization

The highest density of non-glandular trichomes was recorded in Phan San Minit with greater density on the lower leaf surface (1301 trichomes/20 mm²) compared to the upper surface (166 trichomes/20 mm²) with less spacing between the trichomes (0.32 mm), followed by Lah Ksain with 1206.88 trichomes/20mm2on lower leaf surface and 125.66 trichomes/20mm2on upper leaf surface with lesser spacing between the trichomes (0.35 mm). In contrast, the lowest trichome density was observed in Kufri Jyoti with 209.44 trichomes/20 mm² on the lower surface and 13 trichomes/20 mm² on the upper surface with more spacing between the trichomes of 1.13 mm (Image 1, 2). Correlation results

In the oviposition of choice test, significant negative correlations were observed between oviposition (on upper and lower leaf surface) and non-glandular trichome densities (Table 8) on both the upper (r = − 0.866) and lower (r = − 0.689) leaf surfaces. Trichome length also showed a slightly negative correlation (r = − 0.616), indicating that higher trichome density and longer trichomes are associated with reduced oviposition. Similarly, glandular trichome density was negatively correlated (r = − 0.794) with egg-laying (Table 9). On the other hand, significant positive correlations were found with distance between the trichomes in both non-glandular (r = 0.878) and glandular trichome (r = 0.920), suggesting that greater the spacing between trichomes, density of the trichomes decreased and may encourage oviposition. No significant correlations were observed with glandular trichome length and length and width of the gland in glandular trichomes.

In the oviposition of non-choice test, a similar trend was observed. Egg laying was significantly negatively correlated with upper (r = − 0.922) and lower (r = − 0.853) non-glandular trichome densities (Table 8). While trichome length showed a negative correlation (r = − 0.532). Glandular trichome density again displayed a strong negative correlation (r = − 0.872). Positive correlations were noted between oviposition and distance between the trichomes in both non-glandular (r = 0.872) and glandular trichome (r = 0.872), reinforcing the pattern observed in the choice test. As with the choice test, no significant relationships were found with glandular trichome length and length and width of the gland in glandular trichomes.

In contrast, foliage feeding also exhibited the same pattern. Feeding activity was negatively correlated with upper (r = − 0.735) and lower (r = − 0.608) leaf surface non-glandular trichome densities, as well as with trichome length (r = − 0.651) and glandular trichome density (r = − 0.642). These results indicate that higher trichome density and length may be associated with decreased foliage damage due to feeding. Conversely, distance between the trichomes in both non-glandular (r = 0.864) and glandular trichomes (r = 0.895) were positively correlated with feeding, suggesting that more compact leaf structures may reduce to herbivory by P. operculella. No significant relationships were found with glandular trichome length and length and width of the gland in glandular trichomes (Tables 8 and 9).

The correlation analysis revealed significant relationships between various secondary metabolites and insect behavioral responses in both leaves and tuber peels (Table 10). In leaf tissues, oviposition preference under choice conditions was strongly and negatively correlated with chlorogenic acid (r = − 0.872), caffeic acid (r = − 0.825) and flavonoids (r = − 0.823), indicating that higher levels of these compounds deterred egg laying. Under non-choice conditions, where chlorogenic acid (r = − 0.889), caffeic acid (r = − 0.900) and flavonoids (r = − 0811) also showed significant negative correlations with oviposition. A similar trend was observed in feeding behavior on leaves also showed a negative correlation with chlorogenic acid (r = − 0.793), caffeic acid (r = − 0.731) and flavonoids (r = − 0.784), suggesting a complex role of these compounds, possibly acting as feeding deterrent. Total soluble sugars showed no significant correlation with feeding behavior (r = − 0.430) and oviposition (r = 0.409, r = 0.371).

Discussion

Fifteen potato varieties including nine local and six commercial varieties were screened for resistance to potato tuber moth based on ovipositional and feeding preferences. The local varieties were comparatively less preferred by the PTM for both oviposition and feeding than the commercial varieties screened.

Phan San Minit recorded the lowest mean egg count (on leaves and petioles) followed by Lah Ksain while Kufri Jyoti recorded the highest mean egg count followed by Kufri Megha. Lower surface of leaves was generally preferred by the moth for oviposition as compared to upper surface of the leaves; while petioles recorded least oviposition across all the varieties. Gurr31 recorded highest number of eggs laid by PTM on leaves of S. tuberosum with majority on the lower surface and the lowest number of eggs on the petiole of S. chacoense. A strong cultivar-specific ovipositional preference by PTM was observed during the study in both choice and non-choice experiment as was earlier reported by Malakar and Tingey38.

PTM female showed discrimination on ovipositional preference when provided with multiple choices of hosts as was recorded in the oviposition on Lah Ksain where non-choice test was 11 times more than choice test; on Kufri Jyoti where non-choice test was 2.7 times more than choice test. This suggests that the PTM females did not care for their suitability for oviposition in case of single cultivar27,33 or host.

The level of foliage damage among the fifteen potato varieties varied considerably, with damage scores ranging from 1 to 7, corresponding to degrees of infestation from slight to severe. Slight infestation was observed in local varieties viz., Phan San Minit, Lah Ksain, Lah Saw Smit and Lah Saw Khasi whereas severe infestation was observed on commercial varieties Kufri Jyoti, Kufri Megha and Kufri Giriraj.

Oviposition and foliage feeding by PTM was found to be inhibited by high trichome density of the potato varieties. Higher trichome density (glandular and non-glandular trichomes) was associated with less oviposition and feeding preference, thus indicating that the trichomes act as a deterrent against insect activity. Trichome characteristics of potato plants play a significant role in resistance against various other insect pests viz., green peach aphid and potato leafhopper39; Colorado potato beetle40. Highest trichome density was recorded in Phan San Minit and lowest in Kufri Jyoti. Similar findings of less oviposition associated with trichome dense potato varieties were reported in earlier studies41–43. Deterrence of oviposition by PTM related to high trichome density has also been observed in tomato44 and tobacco plants45. Besides adverse effects on oviposition and foliage feeding, trichome density of potato is significantly positively correlated to larval parasitism of P. operculella by Bracon hebetor46. Integration of potato varieties with dense foliar trichomes, along with other control measures is recommended as a component of integrated pest management (IPM) strategy for controlling P. operculella in potato cultivation. However, physical factors cannot be solely responsible for determining oviposition sites, since eggs are normally laid on or adjacent to plants suitable for the larvae47.

In addition to morphological traits, the biochemical components viz., glycoalkaloid and phenolic compounds present in the leaves of potato varieties have a profound role in imparting resistance against PTM. In the present study, higher concentration of glycoalkaloids (α-solanine and α-chaconine) and phenolic compounds (chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid and flavonoids) in leaves of potato were found in the varieties less preferred by PTM for oviposition and feeding. However, it was observed that total soluble sugars have no significant association with PTM activity. Similarly, total soluble sugar didn’t correlate to oviposition on foliage32 and larval mortality of PTM22.

Our findings further support earlier reports by Pacifico et al.22 on adverse effects of glycoalkaloid and phenolic compounds on PTM. α-chaconine content in leaves consistently exceeded those of α-solanine in all the varieties examined. A similar trend was also reported by Li et al.48 in potato. Numerous reports demonstrated the defensive role of glycoalkaloids in various plant-insect interactions. Solanum alkaloids, including α-solanine, α-chaconine and solanidine, exert significant feeding-deterrent effects on the spruce budworm (Choristoneura fumiferana)49. Similarly, inhibition of larval development in red flour beetle (Tribolium castaneum)50 and suppression of feeding behavior in adults of Trogoderma granarium51 by glycoalkaloids has been well documented.

Sanford et al.52 observed that populations of potato plants exhibiting resistance to the potato leafhopper possessed significantly higher mean concentrations of α-chaconine and α-solanine compared with unselected populations. Koch53 further noted that glycoalkaloids not only reduced feeding by larvae of the Colorado potato beetle (Leptinotarsa decemlineata), but also induced post-ingestive effects such as tremors and regurgitation. Tingey54 reported that both feeding activity and survival of potato leafhopper nymphs were negatively correlated with total glycoalkaloid (TGA) levels. Jonasson and Olsson55 found that wireworms preferentially fed in areas with low TGA concentrations, avoiding sites with higher TGA content.

The adverse effects and deterrent properties of phenols and polyphenols have been demonstrated on other insects viz., H. armigera in tomato32,56,57, pigeonpea58,59, Colorado potato beetle (Leptinotarsa decimlineata) on potato plants26, H. zea60, Spodoptera litura61. Plant phenolics play an important role in resistance mechanisms by disrupting insect gut metabolism and by inhibiting nutrient absorption by covalent binding to dietary proteins, lipids and digestive enzymes62. The local potato varieties Phan San Minit and Lah Ksain accumulated higher concentrations of glycoalkaloids and experienced lower PTM infestation than the commercial varieties Kufri Himalini and Kufri Jyoti, which had lower glycoalkaloid concentrations. This observation suggests that higher glycoalkaloid levels may contribute to enhanced resistance against PTM infestation. The cool temperatures and high rainfall prevalent in these regions may favour the accumulation of glycoalkaloids in local varieties. Hellenas63 reported that environmental conditions, particularly the combination of cold temperatures, excessive rainfall and limited sunlight, markedly enhance glycoalkaloid synthesis. Similarly, Jadhav and Salunkhe64 and Liljemark and Widoff65 observed that lower temperatures promote greater glycoalkaloid accumulation compared to higher temperatures. Furthermore, Bushway and Ponnampalam66 reported that the tubers stored at 3.3 °C exhibited higher amount of glycoalkaloid compared to those with stored at 7.7 °C.

Conclusion

Evaluation of factors or traits associated with expression of resistance in potato varieties is essential for the management of PTM. Continuous and repetitive use of pesticides for pest control has caused serious health hazards, environmental contaminations and many undesirable effects. Alternatively, adoption of resistant cultivars or varieties is environmentally friendly, economically viable and sustainable approach which is compatible in any Integrated Pest Management programmes. In this study the morpho-physical and biochemical traits of local and commercial potato varieties in imparting ovipositional and feeding deterrence were assessed. Overall local varieties were comparatively resistant to PTM than commercial potato varieties. Also, the ovipositional and feeding preferences of the PTM is strongly affected by morpho-physical and biochemical characteristics. Local varieties such as Phan San Minit and Lah Ksain consistently showed low susceptibility to PTM, evidenced by reduced egg laying and minimal leaf mining, which was strongly associated with higher densities of trichomes. The local varieties exhibited higher concentrations of glycoalkaloids, chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid and flavonoids, thus imparting the resistance to these varieties. The findings of the study underscore the potential of integrating morphological and biochemical markers for selecting PTM-resistant potato varieties.
