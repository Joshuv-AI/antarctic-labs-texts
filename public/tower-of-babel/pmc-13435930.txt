Maternal thyroid disease and the risk of excessive bleeding in pregnancy: a Danish nationwide cohort study
Authors: Ida Marie Nørum Wigh, Jesper Karmisholt, Peter Vestergaard, Stig Andersen, Søren Risom Kristensen, Stine Linding Andersen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThyroid hormones are involved in the regulation of haemostasis, and it has been hypothesised that abnormal levels of thyroid hormones are associated with an increased risk of excessive bleeding. However, this association is yet to be investigated in pregnant women.MethodsWe performed a retrospective nationwide cohort study of Danish pregnant women from 1999 to 2015. Information on maternal hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism diagnosed before/during or after the pregnancy, as well as outcomes of bleeding in early and late pregnancy, was collected via the Danish nationwide health registers on hospital diagnoses and drug prescriptions. Multivariate logistic regression was used to calculate adjusted odds ratio (aOR) with a 95% confidence interval (95% CI) for the association between maternal thyroid disease and outcomes of bleeding.ResultsThe study cohort included 1,149,871 pregnancies. Maternal hyperthyroidism was diagnosed in 17,702 pregnancies and hypothyroidism in 27,674 pregnancies. Maternal hyperthyroidism diagnosed before/during (aOR 1.11 (95% CI 1.02;1.21)) and after the pregnancy (aOR 1.11 (95% CI 1.01;1.22)) as well as hypothyroidism diagnosed before/during (aOR 1.11 (95% CI 1.03;1.19)) and after the pregnancy (aOR 1.10 (95% CI 1.02;1.18)) were associated with an increased risk of bleeding in the early pregnancy. For bleeding diagnosed in late pregnancy, we found an association only when maternal hypothyroidism was first diagnosed after the pregnancy (aOR 1.22 (95% CI: 1.07;1.40)).ConclusionIn a large, nationwide cohort, maternal hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism showed associations with diagnoses of bleeding in pregnancy. Results provide clues on the role of undiagnosed and untreated maternal thyroid disease in pregnancy.Trial registrationNot applicable.Keywords: Hyperthyroidism, Hhypothyroidism, Haemostasis, Coagulation, Autoimmunity

Background

Thyroid diseases are common endocrine disorders that affect women more often than men, and in younger individuals, the disorders are often of autoimmune origin [1, 2]. Thyroid hormones affect all cells in the body and are essential for the maintenance of homeostasis [3]. Subsequently, symptoms of thyroid disease are often nonspecific [4], and biochemical assessment of thyroid function is essential in diagnosis and monitoring of thyroid diseases [5]. Thyroid hormones are key regulators in many organ systems and play a role in the regulation of haemostasis [6]. Consequently, it has been hypothesised that disorders with abnormal levels of thyroid hormones may have adverse effects on the haemostasis [6]. A well-regulated haemostasis is essential in response to tissue damage, and it requires a balanced activation of blood cells, coagulation factors, and natural anticoagulants to hinder excessive bleeding and prevent thrombosis [7]. It has been reported that components such as von Willebrand factor (vWF), coagulation factors, and fibrinogen are affected when thyroid hormone levels are dysregulated [8]. These initial findings have led to the hypothesis that hypothyroidism induces decreased activity of the haemostatic system and an increased risk of bleeding [9], and, in contrast that hyperthyroidism causes increased activity and a higher risk of thrombosis [10].

More evidence is needed to extend the hypothesis and to investigate the associations between thyroid function and haemostasis in different patient populations. Pregnancy is a state characterised by physiological alterations in maternal thyroid function and in haemostasis [11]. Regulation and accurate activation of the haemostasis is essential during pregnancy, and bleeding is a potentially painful and life-threatening complication for mother and child [12]. We speculated on the association between maternal thyroid disease, hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism, and the risk of bleeding in pregnancy. The systematic collection of health data in the Nordic countries enabled us to investigate this association in a large Danish nationwide cohort. Furthermore, biochemical assessment of thyroid function parameters and thyroid autoantibodies in early pregnant women was conducted in a regional sub-cohort, allowing for evaluation with varying definitions of exposure.

Methods

Study cohorts

This was a retrospective cohort study, utilising information from Danish nationwide registers and a regional biobank. The study population included a nationwide cohort established from 1999 to 2015 and a regional cohort established from 2011 to 2015 as a subgroup of the nationwide cohort (Fig. 1). We established the nationwide cohort using the Danish Medical Birth Register (MBR), which holds registrations on all births in Denmark and characteristics of the mothers [13]. The cohort included all singleton live- and stillbirths, defined as birth of a child with no sign of life in or after gestational week 22 [14], registered in the MBR from January 1, 1999, to December 31, 2015 (n = 1,011,278). Moreover, we identified spontaneous abortions, defined as involuntary loss of pregnancy before pregnancy week 22 [15], via the Danish National Hospital Register (DNHR). The DNHR holds information on registered diagnoses for all in- and outpatients in Danish hospitals according to the 10th International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) from 1994 [16]. Spontaneous abortions are registered as ICD-10 code O02 or O03 for all patients managed in hospitals along with a registration of gestational age at the time of diagnosis. Thus, we included all spontaneous abortions registered in the DNHR from January 1, 1999, to December 31, 2015 (n = 138,593), and the combined nationwide cohort included 1,149,871 Danish pregnancies from 1999 to 2015 with an outcome of livebirth, stillbirth, or spontaneous abortion. We did not include elective abortions, molar, or ectopic pregnancies.

Illustration of the study period of the nationwide and the regional cohort (top panel) and the diagnoses considered in early and late pregnancy (lower panel). LMP; last menstrual period, ICD-10; 10th International Classification of Diseases

The regional cohort included pregnancies in the North Denmark Region from 2011 to 2015. This region holds approximately 10% of the Danish population, and a biobank with blood samples from pregnant women was established in the North Denmark Region Pregnancy Cohort (NDRPC) [17]. The NDRPC included all pregnant women in the North Denmark Region who had a blood sample drawn from June 28, 2011, to January 5, 2015, as part of the Danish prenatal screening program for chromosomal anomalies [18]. Participation in the screening program is generally high [19], and typically, the blood sample is drawn during the first pregnancy visit to a general practice (median pregnancy week 10 in the NDRPC). Thus, the NDRPC samples were drawn for non-thyroidal reasons as part of routine prenatal care and stored as serum residue samples at -80° Celsius in a biobank [17]. Thereby, we included a total of 15,454 pregnancies with biological specimens from the mothers in early pregnancy. Outcomes of livebirths and stillbirths (n = 14,562) and spontaneous abortions (n = 892) were assessed using the same data sources.

Exposure

In the nationwide cohort, we defined exposure to maternal hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism in pregnancy from hospital diagnoses in the DNHR and from redeemed prescriptions of thyroid medication in the Danish National Prescription Register (DNPR). The DNPR holds information on all drugs redeemed from Danish pharmacies since 1995, registered according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification System (ATC) [20]. Hyperthyroidism was firstly defined as a hospital diagnosis of hyperthyroidism (ICD-10: E05) (25% of cases), minimum of two redeemed prescriptions of antithyroid drugs (ATC: H03BA02, H03BB01, H03BB02) (16%), or both (59%). Secondly, hypothyroidism was defined among women with no registrations of hyperthyroidism as a hospital diagnosis of hypothyroidism (ICD-10: E03) (4% of cases), minimum two redeemed prescriptions of thyroid hormone (ATC: H03AA01, H03AA02) (43%), or both (53%). For each woman, the onset of hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism was assessed before, during, and up to five years after the pregnancy. The timing of onset was defined by the registered date of the first hospital diagnosis or the first redeemed prescription, whichever came first. We made an assessment in the five years following the pregnancy because women diagnosed with thyroid disease in those years often have been found to have biochemical signs of undiagnosed thyroid dysfunction in the NDRPC at the time of the pregnancy [21]. Thus, we evaluated two groups of women with thyroid disease in pregnancy: one group with known disease (diagnosed before/during the pregnancy) and one group with potentially unknown or undiagnosed thyroid disease (first time diagnosed after the pregnancy).

In the regional cohort, we defined exposure to maternal hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism in pregnancy by the biochemical measurement of thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), free thyroxine (fT4), thyroid peroxidase antibodies (TPO-Ab), and thyroglobulin antibodies (Tg-Ab) in stored blood samples from early pregnancy. The biochemical measurements were performed in 2015 to 2016 on an ADVIA Centaur XPT (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) as previously described in detail [17]. We used established method- and pregnancy week-specific reference intervals for TSH classification of maternal hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism in pregnancy, which ranged from 0.02 mIU/L (lower reference limit) to 3.65 mIU/L (upper reference limit) [17] and evaluated the thyroid autoantibodies according to the previously established method- and pregnancy-specific cut-offs (TPO-Ab above 60 U/mL and Tg-Ab above 33 U/mL) [22].

Outcome and covariates

In the nationwide and regional cohorts, we identified outcomes of bleeding via hospital diagnoses registered in the DNHR. We identified in- and outpatient contacts with diagnoses of bleeding (ICD-10: N93, R58, O20, O46, and O67) from 1998 to 2016, defining the onset as the date of hospital entry with the diagnosis of interest. We categorised the outcome as early and late pregnancy bleeding stratified by pregnancy week 20 (Fig. 1). Early pregnancy bleeding was evaluated for all pregnancies in the cohort, whereas late pregnancy bleeding was only assessed for pregnancies resulting in birth, and not spontaneous abortion, to ensure follow-up up until the outcome studied. Early pregnancy bleeding was defined as a diagnosis of pregnancy bleeding (O20 or O46) or unspecific bleeding (N93 or R58) when the diagnosis was registered in the period from the date of last menstrual period (LMP) up to and including the 20th pregnancy week (Fig. 1). Late pregnancy bleeding was defined as a diagnosis of pregnancy bleeding (O46), bleeding at birth (067), or unspecific bleeding (N93 or R58), when the diagnosis was registered in the period from the beginning of the 21st week of pregnancy and up to two weeks after birth (Fig. 1). This window postpartum was included to account for any delay in the registration of bleeding at birth.

In the regional cohort, information was available on the postpartum bleeding volume quantified in millilitres (ml) for 13,584 of the 14,562 pregnancies ending with birth. This information was available through the registration of the additional diagnosis of bleeding volume (VPHxxxx) along with the diagnostic code for postpartum bleeding (ICD-10: O72). The registration practice in the North Denmark Region during the study period included this diagnosis for all births, not just when a diagnosis of postpartum bleeding was given. Thus, we had a continuous measure of blood loss at birth, and from this we defined postpartum haemorrhage (PPH) as a bleeding volume exceeding 500 ml after vaginal birth and 1000 ml after a caesarean section [23].

Information on maternal covariates was available from the registers and Statistics Denmark. For the nationwide and regional study cohort, we found information on maternal age at the time of the pregnancy outcome, parity, and origin (born in Denmark/not born in Denmark). Information on maternal smoking in pregnancy and pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) was only available for pregnancies resulting in birth because this information was accessed through the MBR. BMI was first registered from 2005 onwards.

Statistical analyses

We reported descriptive results as numbers (n) and frequencies (%). We evaluated outcomes of early and late pregnancy bleeding in the nationwide and the regional cohort using logistic regression to estimate a crude and adjusted odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (95% CI). Robust standard errors were used to adjust for dependency between multiple pregnancies in the study period. Each pregnancy was followed until a diagnosis of bleeding or the end of pregnancy, whichever came first, and pregnancies resulting in a spontaneous abortion were only included in the analyses on bleeding in early pregnancy. The adjusted model included maternal age, parity, ethnicity, and pregnancy outcome. For the evaluation of bleeding in late pregnancy, information on maternal smoking was included in the adjusted model (nationwide and regional cohort), and BMI (regional cohort). In addition, we made a sub-analysis in the national cohort, including pregnancies after 2005 and adjusted for BMI.

In the regional cohort, we used logistic regression to evaluate the association between maternal thyroid function and thyroid autoantibody status and the categorical outcome of PPH. Furthermore, maternal TSH and fT4 were evaluated as continuous exposure variables in the logistic regression with the categorical outcome of PPH. Linear regression was used to evaluate the association between maternal TSH and fT4 as continuous exposure variables and the continuous outcome of postpartum bleeding volume. The continuous variables were considered prior to analyses using graphical plots, and the visual inspection revealed few extreme outliers in the dataset (TSH > 50 mIU/l (n = 6), fT4 > 40 pmol/l (n = 3), bleeding volume > 4000 ml (n = 7)). Furthermore, scatterplots of (a) the dependent and independent variables and of (b) the residuals and fitted values of the regression model revealed no indication of non-linearity.

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 18.0 (StataCorp LCC, Texas).

Discussion

Principal findings

In this large nationwide study of more than one million pregnancies in Denmark, we investigated the association between maternal thyroid disease and outcomes of bleeding in pregnancy in a register-based design. Results provide clues of a slightly increased risk of being diagnosed with bleeding in pregnancy among women with hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism, even if the findings in a smaller regional sub-cohort with biochemical assessment of thyroid function and thyroid autoantibodies in early pregnancy did not corroborate the nationwide findings. The findings add to the hypothesis that thyroid hormones play a role in the regulation of haemostasis and encourage further, large studies with a more direct assessment of exposure and outcome.

Interpretation

The interplay between thyroid disease and bleeding complications in pregnancy is of clinical relevance, and evidence to substantiate a possible association may inform the clinical management of pregnant women [11]. Thyroid disease in pregnant women is a concern, considering the role of maternal thyroid hormones in foetal development [24] and the risk of adverse pregnancy complications, when maternal thyroid disease is left untreated [25]. Likewise, complications of bleeding in pregnant women may be severe and constitute a risk for the pregnancy and the pregnant woman’s health [12]. The physiological alterations in a normal pregnancy influence maternal thyroid function as well as the haemostatic balance, underlining the challenge of assessment during pregnancy and the importance of evaluating the hypothesis in this population group specifically [11].

Evidence on the association between thyroid disease and haemostasis emerges from non-pregnant adults, and the hypothesis is mainly that hypothyroidism, and not hyperthyroidism, is associated with an increased risk of bleeding as the clinical sign of a lack of function in the haemostatic system [9]. We hypothesised that this association would similarly apply to pregnant women and designed this study to evaluate the outcome of bleeding in pregnancy. Thus, we expected that maternal hypothyroidism, but not hyperthyroidism, would be associated with diagnoses of bleeding in pregnancy. Our findings differed depending on whether diagnoses of bleeding were evaluated in early or late pregnancy. For outcomes of early pregnancy bleeding, we found that hypothyroidism as well as hyperthyroidism were associated with an increased risk, and that the risk estimate for each subtype of maternal thyroid disease indicated a slight and similar risk. Previous studies have found an increased risk of early pregnancy loss in women with both hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism [26, 27], and we speculate, whether the associations with early pregnancy bleeding reflect this known association with early pregnancy loss. However, our cohort included pregnancies resulting in spontaneous abortions and births, and associations with early pregnancy bleeding were found even after the adjustment for the outcome of pregnancy. This indicates an increased risk of being diagnosed with bleeding in early pregnancy among women with hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism who ultimately end the pregnancy with the birth of a live-born child. Even if the pregnancy outcome is positive, the outcome of early pregnancy bleeding may be a sign of threatening miscarriage, causing much concern in the patient along with additional hospital visits and clinical follow-up. It should be emphasised that the association between maternal hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism and early pregnancy bleeding observed in the nationwide cohort was not corroborated in the regional sub-cohort with biochemical assessment of maternal thyroid function. Furthermore, the role of confounding should be acknowledged and unmeasured and residual confounding may exist. Our study is the first to investigate the outcome of bleeding diagnosed in early pregnancy, specifically, but further large studies are warranted to extend the findings and to elaborate on the role of other determinants and underlying mechanisms.

For the outcome of bleeding in late pregnancy, an association was found with maternal hypothyroidism only, which supports our hypothesis. Notably, we found an association only when maternal hypothyroidism was first diagnosed after the pregnancy, and this definition of exposure was included to assess the role of undiagnosed and untreated maternal thyroid disease. Women diagnosed before or during pregnancy were likely monitored more closely and, especially for women with hypothyroidism, were more likely to receive treatment during pregnancy. Symptoms of thyroid disease may be nonspecific, and it has been found that women diagnosed with hypothyroidism in the years following a pregnancy often had abnormal TSH already during pregnancy [21]; however, these women did not receive treatment during pregnancy. It was surprising that we were unable to replicate the findings from the nationwide cohort in the regional sub-cohort, which included an actual assessment of maternal TSH in early pregnancy. We speculate on the size of the study population. Even if the NDRPC included around 15,000 pregnant women with biochemical assessment, the number of exposed cases was limited, particularly if the most severe cases of maternal hypothyroidism were to be identified. Additionally, the method for diagnosis of thyroid disease in the national and the regional cohorts differed which may have influenced the severity of maternal thyroid disease encountered. In the national cohort, the use of diagnoses and prescriptions of thyroid medication provided an indirect assessment of exposure, but this approach was more likely to identify actual thyroid disease diagnosed and treated by a doctor. In the regional cohort, exposure assessment was direct from actual biochemical measurement of maternal thyroid function; however, this may have captured less severe abnormalities in maternal thyroid function that did not necessarily reflect consistent abnormalities with repeated testing. Thus, the included cases in the regional cohort might have been less severe which could affect the associations observed. We could evaluate the bleeding volume for almost all pregnant women in this cohort; however, this analysis was also dominated by women with normal or slightly elevated TSH.

The outcome of PPH has been considered in women with hypothyroidism, and some of the earliest clinical reports pointed towards a role of maternal overt hypothyroidism, and not subclinical disease [28]. Later findings from observational cohort studies have shown more diverse results on the association between maternal hypothyroidism and PPH. Some studies found that maternal TSH levels above 2.5 or 4.0 mIU/L during pregnancy were associated with an increased risk of PPH [29, 30]. In contrast, others did not find an association with subclinical hypothyroidism or the presence of thyroid autoantibodies in euthyroid subjects [31, 32]. Overall, the existing evidence points towards a role of overt hypothyroidism or more than minor abnormalities in maternal thyroid function, and this emphasises the need for large-scale studies with a substantial number of exposed cases to robustly determine the association in the more extreme ranges of maternal TSH in pregnancy.

In the regional sub-cohort, we could evaluate the association between thyroid autoantibodies and outcomes of bleeding in pregnancy. We did not find an increased risk of early and late pregnancy bleeding in women who were positive for TPO-Ab or Tg-Ab. We adjusted these analyses for maternal TSH to evaluate the role of thyroid autoimmunity per se, and the lack of association aligns with the previous study that found no increased risk of PPH in euthyroid TPO-Ab-positive pregnant women [32]. Still, we speculate on the role of thyroid autoimmunity when considering outcomes of bleeding. In general, a link between autoimmune disorders and haemostasis is considered from a clinical perspective, and scientifically, it is proposed that the type of haemostatic imbalance may relate to the subtype of autoantibody generated in a specific condition [33]. Following this line, more studies are encouraged to evaluate the association between haemostasis outcomes and thyroid autoantibody subtypes, and to determine whether the hypothesised opposite effects of hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism on the haemostatic system relates to the specific subtype of thyroid autoantibody.

Methodological considerations

Our register-based study population was large and nationwide, relying on routine registrations of health care data, which reduces the risk of selection bias. The use of secondary research data limits the available variables to information registered as part of routine care and relies on the registration practices of the health care providers. The validity of the Danish nationwide registers is generally high [16], and diagnoses of thyroid disease have been validated [34]. The combination of hospital diagnoses and redeemed drug prescriptions in the exposure assessment is a strength, but the register-based design limited the possibility to study outcomes according to any shift in maternal thyroid function during follow-up. We identified women diagnosed with thyroid disease after the pregnancy as a proxy for exposure to undiagnosed and untreated disease in the pregnancy. This methodology implies follow-up for definition of exposure after the time of the outcome in pregnancy; however, the risk of immortal time bias is considered marginal in this age group. We combined women diagnosed with thyroid disease before and during pregnancy because the number of women newly diagnosed in pregnancy did not allow for stratified analyses according to the number of exposed cases. Considering diagnoses of bleeding in the DNHR, these have not been validated in pregnancy specifically, but a high positive predictive value for non-traumatic bleedings in non-pregnant adults has been found [35]. Our nationwide cohort included pregnancies that ended with spontaneous abortions that were identified via hospital diagnoses, thus, with a risk that early pregnancy bleeding or pregnancy loss with no hospital contact were not captured. However, the finding that 12% of pregnancies ended with spontaneous abortions align with other reports [36], and we do not expect any misclassification in the registration to be differential according to the study exposure. A risk of information bias from diagnostic misclassification of the outcome will exist in our cohort; however, we find it most likely that this potential bias is nondifferential according to our study exposure. Our study was conditioned on hospital diagnoses in the DNHR; thus, women managed with early pregnancy loss or symptoms of bleeding in general practice alone were not included in the cohort.

Conclusion

This was the first large study to evaluate the outcome of bleeding diagnoses in early and late pregnancy among women with hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism. Results provide clues on an association between maternal thyroid disease in pregnancy and outcomes of bleeding and support further investigations on the hypothesis that specifically hypothyroidism associates with an increased risk of bleeding. Future studies with large cohorts and direct assessment of exposure and outcome are warranted to extend findings and inform clinical practice.
