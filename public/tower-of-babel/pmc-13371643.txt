Targeting metabolic dysregulation in postmenopausal breast cancer: Synergistic effects of nutrition-based interventions and exercise on molecular and cellular pathways
Authors: Zhou Su, YunPeng Zhao, Xiaoqian Cui, Yu Niu, Sima-sadat Sabihi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Postmenopausal females diagnosed with breast cancer who are undergoing aromatase inhibitor (AI) therapy often exhibit metabolic disturbances, which may manifest as insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, increased adiposity, and an elevated risk of cardiovascular events. Such metabolic disruptions have the potential to undermine the efficacy of treatment and intensify therapy-related adverse effects, thereby diminishing overall quality of life. The implementation of functional nutrition and exercise-based interventions has emerged as a viable adjunctive therapeutic strategy aimed at addressing these metabolic complications by influencing fundamental mechanistic pathways, including chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, hormonal imbalances, and mitochondrial dysfunction. This review systematically investigates the phenomenon of metabolic dysregulation within this specific patient demographic and emphasizes the impact of particular dietary constituents in conjunction with organized aerobic and resistance training on critical metabolic regulators, including AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors, and insulin signaling cascades. The therapeutic efficacy associated with the incorporation of these lifestyle modifications is examined within the framework of optimizing glucose homeostasis, lipid profiles, and attenuating systemic inflammation. Comprehending these mechanistic pathways establishes a basis for the formulation of personalized functional nutrition and exercise protocols designed to alleviate metabolic adverse effects induced by artificial intelligence, augment therapeutic outcomes, and enhance overall health in individuals diagnosed with postmenopausal breast cancer.

Introduction

In premenopausal women, findings from case–control studies examining estradiol levels have been inconsistent, likely due to the wide fluctuations of hormone concentrations across the menstrual cycle [1]. This variability makes it difficult to reliably estimate a woman’s long-term exposure to endogenous estradiol based on a single measurement. By contrast, case–control studies in postmenopausal women have shown more consistent evidence supporting a link between higher estradiol levels and breast cancer risk. Even so, interpretation remains challenging, as the presence of breast cancer itself may influence hormone concentrations observed in such studies [1, 2]. Recently, prospective studies have provided clearer insights, showing that postmenopausal women who later develop breast cancer generally have higher baseline estradiol levels than those who remain cancer-free [3]. Overall, the indirect evidence that estrogens influence breast cancer risk is strong and widely accepted. For example, late onset of menstruation and earlier menopause are protective, likely because they lower total lifetime exposure to ovarian hormones. Despite decades of research, however, debate continues over whether higher circulating estradiol levels in postmenopausal women are directly linked to breast cancer risk. Much of this doubt comes from inconsistencies in earlier studies, many of which were limited by small sample sizes, retrospective designs, and less sensitive hormone measurement methods [2–4].

With global rates of overweight and obesity projected to continue climbing [5, 6], and breast cancer incidence is also on the rise worldwide, understanding the biological mechanisms underlying the obesity–breast cancer connection has become increasingly urgent.

Obesity and metabolic dysfunction are not independent of estrogen signaling but are mechanistically intertwined, particularly in postmenopausal women [7]. Following menopause, adipose tissue becomes the primary site of estrogen biosynthesis through increased aromatase activity, leading to elevated local and systemic estrogen levels. In obese individuals, expanded adipose tissue mass further amplifies this process, resulting in higher estrogen exposure despite low ovarian production. Concurrently, metabolic disturbances such as insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia reduce circulating levels of sex hormone–binding globulin (SHBG), thereby increasing the bioavailability of free estrogens [7, 8]. Chronic low-grade inflammation associated with obesity, mediated by adipokines such as leptin and pro-inflammatory cytokines, additionally upregulates aromatase expression and enhances estrogen signaling within breast tissue. Thus, obesity-driven metabolic dysregulation promotes breast cancer development through both estrogen-dependent mechanisms, via increased estrogen production and activity, and estrogen-independent pathways involving insulin signaling, inflammation, and altered cellular energetics. These interconnected processes highlight the need to consider metabolic health and estrogen biology as part of a unified framework when examining breast cancer risk and progression in postmenopausal women [7, 8].

While this review primarily focuses on estrogen receptor–positive (ER+) breast cancer in postmenopausal women, it is important to note that obesity-related metabolic dysregulation also contributes to estrogen receptor–negative subtypes, including triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). In these cases, the effects of exercise and nutritional interventions are mediated predominantly through estrogen-independent mechanisms such as insulin signaling, inflammation, and metabolic reprogramming.

Metabolic dysregulation in postmenopausal breast cancer survivors

Most breast cancers are ER + and develop after menopause [6, 9]. At first glance, this may seem paradoxical, as circulating estrogen levels decline sharply with the loss of ovarian follicular activity during the menopausal transition, thereby eliminating the primary source of endogenous estrogens in women [9]. In fact, estrogen concentrations in normal-weight postmenopausal women are generally considered too low to consistently activate ERs, as evidenced by the absence of endometrial proliferation in most cases. Until roughly a decade ago, accurate quantification of postmenopausal estrogen was limited by the poor sensitivity of conventional assays, which often failed to detect these low hormone concentrations [10]. Advances in methodology, including ultra-sensitive radio-immunoassays and liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), have now enabled the reliable measurement of systemic estrogens after menopause. These techniques have revealed that postmenopausal women with obesity exhibit higher circulating estrogen levels compared to their lean counterparts [11]. This elevation is believed to promote estrogen-driven cancers of the breast and uterus and reflects enhanced steroid hormone production by adipose tissue, which becomes the primary site of estrogen biosynthesis after menopause. Aromatase, the enzyme controlling the rate-limiting step of estrogen production, is consistently upregulated in adipocytes, including those within breast tissue, as a function of both menopausal status and body mass index (BMI) [12–14]. Interestingly, despite circulating estrogen concentrations being 10–15 times lower in postmenopausal women than in premenopausal women, the levels of estrogens within breast tissue appear to be comparable between the two groups [15].

Obesity reflects a state of chronic energy surplus, whereas cancer is characterized by heightened energy demand driven by uncontrolled cell proliferation, one of its defining hallmarks. As a result, metabolic pathways responsible for sustaining energy balance are frequently altered in both conditions. A major focus of cancer metabolism research has been the observation that many tumor cells reprogram their energy production away from mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and toward glycolysis, even in the presence of oxygen, a process known as the Warburg effect. Although glycolysis generates far less ATP than respiration, the markedly elevated glucose uptake in cancer cells ensures adequate ATP production while simultaneously providing essential intermediates for the synthesis of nucleotides, amino acids, and lipids needed to support rapid cell division. Excess glycolytic end products are released as lactate, which not only reshapes the tumor microenvironment but can also serve as a metabolic fuel for neighboring proliferating cells, whether malignant or non-malignant. This reliance on aerobic glycolysis further allows cells to sustain growth under hypoxic conditions [16–18]. Energy and biomass production depend on the coordinated activity of multiple regulatory proteins. Growth factors, through activation of the PI3K–AKT pathway, enhance glucose uptake and its conversion into glucose-6-phosphate, fueling both energy generation and the synthesis of macromolecules, including amino acids, nucleotides, and fatty acids. In parallel, elevated ATP levels inhibit the metabolic sensor AMPK, which suppresses fatty acid oxidation while favoring anabolic processes such as cholesterol, protein, and lipid biosynthesis through intersecting downstream signaling cascades [18].

mTOR and AMPK pathways

The tumor suppressor LKB1, encoded by the STK11 gene, is a multifunctional kinase and transcriptional regulator that plays a pivotal role in controlling cellular metabolism and maintaining cell cycle integrity. Within the nucleus, LKB1 directly interacts with p53, influencing the transcriptional regulation of p21, a critical effector of p53-mediated cell cycle arrest [19, 20]. Beyond its nuclear functions, LKB1 serves as a primary upstream kinase for AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and other members of the AMPK-related kinase family, positioning it as a central node in energy-sensing and metabolic regulation. AMPK itself is widely recognized as a master regulator of cellular energy homeostasis. This heterotrimeric serine/threonine kinase consists of catalytic α and regulatory β and γ subunits, the latter of which binds nucleotides such as AMP, ADP, and ATP. Changes in the AMP/ATP or ADP/ATP ratio reflect fluctuations in cellular energy status, allowing AMPK to act as a sensitive metabolic sensor [21]. When AMP or ADP binds to the γ subunit, AMPK undergoes a conformational shift that facilitates phosphorylation of the α subunit at Thr172 by upstream kinases such as LKB1. This activation mechanism ensures that AMPK responds rapidly to energy stress, hypoxia, or nutrient deprivation [21, 22].

Once activated, AMPK phosphorylates a wide array of downstream targets, coordinating both catabolic and anabolic pathways. For instance, phosphorylation of acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) inhibits fatty acid synthesis, while phosphorylation of TSC1/TSC2 suppresses protein translation and anabolic metabolism through inhibition of the mTOR pathway [23]. Additionally, AMPK activation enhances fatty acid β-oxidation and promotes mitochondrial oxidative metabolism, supporting efficient ATP generation. Importantly, AMPK can also phosphorylate p53, thereby inducing cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, and inhibit HIF1α, directly counteracting the Warburg effect [23]. In this way, AMPK exerts strong tumor-suppressive effects by limiting uncontrolled growth and reprogramming cancer cell metabolism [23].

Evidence from human breast tissue supports this tumor-suppressive function. Normal breast epithelial cells display higher AMPK phosphorylation compared with breast cancer cells, and tumors frequently show reduced phosphorylation of ACC, consistent with a loss of AMPK activity. These findings originally led to the view of AMPK as a key metabolic tumor suppressor [24]. However, accumulating evidence indicates that the role of AMPK in cancer is far more complex [25]. While AMPK activation can limit growth under nutrient-rich conditions, it may paradoxically support tumor survival under stress. In nutrient-deprived or hypoxic environments, AMPK promotes autophagy, a process that recycles intracellular components to generate metabolic substrates. Through enhanced fatty acid oxidation, AMPK also increases ATP production, providing cancer cells with the energy required to survive otherwise lethal conditions. Thus, while AMPK can suppress tumor initiation by restricting anabolic metabolism, it may also facilitate tumor progression by enabling metabolic flexibility and survival during therapy-induced or microenvironmental stress [25, 26].

In breast cancer, this dual role has important therapeutic implications. Pharmacological activators of AMPK, such as metformin, have been widely studied for their potential anti-tumor effects, with epidemiological data suggesting reduced cancer incidence and mortality among diabetic patients taking the drug. Yet, AMPK activation may also create an adaptive advantage for tumors in energy-limited niches. Consequently, whether AMPK should be universally stimulated or selectively inhibited may depend on tumor context, stage, and metabolic profile [26]. Overall, the LKB1–AMPK axis represents a critical link between energy sensing, cell cycle regulation, and cancer metabolism. While its tumor-suppressive functions are well established, its ability to promote cancer cell survival under stress highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of how targeting this pathway could be leveraged in breast cancer therapy.

Inflammation and adipokines

Adipokines are signaling molecules secreted by adipose tissue that coordinate metabolic and immune processes throughout the body. In the context of breast cancer, these molecules may arise from general adipose stores, the tumor microenvironment, or the cancer cells themselves, facilitating bidirectional communication between fat tissue and tumors. Adipokines display both pro- and anti-inflammatory properties, and the balance between these opposing effects shapes the overall inflammatory state that influences cancer risk and progression [27, 28]. Among the many adipokines identified, adiponectin and leptin have received the greatest attention for their contrasting roles in obesity-related breast carcinogenesis [28]. Adiponectin, typically reduced in obesity and hyperinsulinemia, is a protective, anti-inflammatory molecule that enhances insulin sensitivity and regulates glucose and lipid metabolism [28]. Higher circulating adiponectin levels are consistently associated with a lower risk of postmenopausal, and possibly premenopausal, breast cancer [29–31]. Its antitumor actions occur mainly through two interconnected signaling pathways. First, activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) inhibits anabolic and proliferative mechanisms, induces tumor-suppressor proteins such as p53 and p21, and promotes apoptosis [32]. Second, inhibition of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR cascade suppresses cell growth and proliferation. Adiponectin also influences the local microenvironment by reducing aromatase activity in adjacent adipocytes, thereby decreasing estrogen synthesis and limiting estrogen receptor stimulation in breast tissue. Together, these effects contribute to its anti-neoplastic and anti-inflammatory potential, counteracting obesity-induced metabolic stress [32–34].

In contrast, leptin is a pro-inflammatory adipokine whose circulating levels rise in obesity and insulin-resistant states. Produced by both adipose and breast cells, leptin promotes chronic inflammation by stimulating cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 [35, 36]. Elevated leptin concentrations are linked with increased breast cancer risk and aggressiveness, independent of body mass index [37, 38]. Acting through its receptors expressed on breast cancer cells, leptin activates proliferative and anti-apoptotic signaling via JAK2/STAT3, MAPK, and PI3K/Akt pathways. It also enhances local aromatase activity and promotes secretion of IL-8 and IL-18, supporting tumor survival and angiogenesis [39, 40]. Overall, the interplay between low adiponectin and high leptin levels forms a critical link between obesity, inflammation, and breast cancer, suggesting that restoring adipokine balance may represent an important therapeutic and preventive target in obese postmenopausal women.

Estrogen metabolism: aromatase activity in adipose tissue

After menopause, estrogens generated by adipose tissue, including those produced locally within the breast, are thought to fuel the progression of ER+ breast cancers. Aromatase, the key enzyme that catalyzes estrogen biosynthesis, is encoded by the CYP19A1 gene. Its expression in breast adipose stromal cells is tightly regulated by factors originating both from tumors and adipose tissue (Fig. 1). In humans, CYP19A1 transcription is controlled by tissue-specific promoters positioned upstream of 11 untranslated first exons. Since not all of these exons have murine equivalents, aromatase regulation differs between mice and humans. Consequently, much of the research on obesity-associated and breast cancer–related estrogen production has relied on studies using isolated human cells or transgenic mice engineered to express human aromatase in mammary tissue [41].

In both obesity and breast cancer, breast adipose stromal cells undergo a promoter switch: aromatase transcription shifts from promoter I.4 to the coordinated activation of promoters I.3 and PII. This transition, driven by external cues such as inflammatory mediators and adipokines, has provided major insight into molecular mechanisms of aromatase regulation and identified potential therapeutic opportunities. Metabolic signaling pathways are particularly implicated in this process (Fig. 1) [42, 43]. For instance, transcription from promoters I.3 and PII depends on CREB. Early evidence linking obesity to breast cancer showed that LKB1-mediated activation of AMPK suppresses aromatase expression by sequestering the CREB co-activator CRTC2 (also known as TORC2) in the cytoplasm. Later studies revealed that CRTC1 and CRTC3 also enhance aromatase transcription by binding to promoter PII in adipose stromal cells [42].

Obesity-related factors such as leptin and PGE2 antagonize the LKB1–AMPK pathway by downregulating LKB1 expression and preventing AMPK activation at Thr172. Instead, they stimulate phosphorylation at Ser485, which inhibits AMPK. This disruption promotes nuclear translocation of CRTC1–CRTC3 complexes, leading to higher aromatase expression. These findings not only clarify the molecular connection between obesity and breast cancer risk but also explain conditions such as the elevated aromatase expression seen in boys with Peutz–Jeghers syndrome, caused by germline mutations in STK11 (encoding LKB1) [42]. In contrast, adiponectin, a hormone linked to leanness, activates AMPK and represses aromatase in breast adipose cells, suggesting a protective role. Supporting this, the antidiabetic drug metformin, which activates AMPK via LKB1 to inhibit hepatic gluconeogenesis, also suppresses aromatase expression at clinically relevant doses. Notably, metformin selectively inhibits promoters I.3 and PII but not I.4, indicating that it can specifically reduce adipose tissue–derived estrogen production without affecting aromatase in bone and joints [44].

Aromatase promoters also contain p53 response elements. A high-affinity binding site located just upstream of the transcription start site enables p53 to act as a repressor of CYP19A1. Mutations at this site elevate aromatase transcription. Supporting this, pharmacological stabilization of p53 with RITA (a molecule that blocks HDM2-mediated degradation of p53) lowers aromatase expression in breast adipose stromal cells. RITA simultaneously increases SCO2 expression, consistent with enhanced p53-driven metabolic activity [45]. Clinically, women with Li–Fraumeni syndrome (caused by germline TP53 mutations) show increased aromatase immunoreactivity in breast adipose stromal cells, which may explain their heightened susceptibility to ER+ breast cancers. Mechanistic studies further demonstrated that PGE2 suppresses phosphorylation of p53 at Ser15, a modification normally mediated by ATM or AMPK that stabilizes p53 by disrupting its association with HDM2. Reduced phosphorylation at this site likely explains the lower nuclear levels of p53 observed in adipose stromal cells from obese individuals or after in vitro exposure to leptin and PGE2 [46].

Another regulator of aromatase is HIF1α. This transcription factor binds to a hypoxia response element overlapping the proximal CREB site of promoter PII, enhancing promoter activity. While HIF1α is classically stabilized under hypoxic conditions, studies show that PGE2 can also stabilize HIF1α independent of oxygen tension, enabling it to mediate PGE2-induced aromatase expression. Similarly, leptin stabilizes HIF1α, which cooperates with PKM2 to further promote aromatase transcription. Consistent with these mechanistic data, nuclear HIF1α levels correlate positively with aromatase expression in breast adipose stromal cells of women and in mouse mammary fat pads, with both associations strengthened by obesity [47, 48].

Exercise and metabolic health

Physical activity represents a cornerstone of non-pharmacological strategies for improving health outcomes in postmenopausal breast cancer survivors. Beyond its well-established benefits for cardiovascular fitness and body composition, exercise directly influences key metabolic pathways that are frequently dysregulated in obesity and breast cancer, including insulin sensitivity, lipid metabolism, inflammation, and mitochondrial function.

Epidemiological data reinforce the mechanistic evidence that exercise favorably modulates breast cancer risk through its effects on metabolic health. In a large population-based case–control study, Carpenter et al. [49] demonstrated that sustained lifetime physical activity since menarche was associated with a markedly reduced risk of postmenopausal breast cancer, but only among women who maintained relative weight stability in adulthood. Women who exercised at least 17.6 MET-hours per week throughout life had a 45–60% lower risk compared to inactive peers; however, this benefit was absent in those with excessive adult weight gain [49]. These findings suggest that the protective effect of exercise is closely tied to metabolic balance, particularly the avoidance of obesity-driven hormonal and inflammatory disturbances. The results parallel experimental evidence that weight gain and adiposity promote insulin resistance, elevated aromatase activity, and increased estrogen bioavailability, counteracting the favorable endocrine shifts induced by physical activity. Thus, long-term exercise, when combined with weight management, emerges as a powerful strategy for mitigating metabolic dysfunction and reducing breast cancer risk in postmenopausal women [49].

Zimmer and colleagues [50] conducted a large prospective cohort study to investigate the prognostic role of physical activity in postmenopausal women with advanced, hormone receptor–positive breast cancer undergoing standardized endocrine therapy with everolimus and exemestane [50]. The results demonstrated that baseline physical activity was a significant predictor of clinical outcome. Women classified as active had a 16% lower hazard of disease progression or death compared to those insufficiently active, as indicated by an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.84. Importantly, this association was evident for activity status at treatment initiation but not for changes in activity over time, suggesting that baseline fitness or physiological reserve might exert the strongest influence on survival outcomes. In addition to improved PFS, active patients experienced fewer adverse events, including both severe and non-severe toxicities, during endocrine therapy. These women also reported higher quality of life and lower fatigue scores at follow-up compared with inactive participants, highlighting that the benefits of physical activity extended beyond clinical endpoints to overall well-being [50]. They suggest that physical activity may enhance systemic resilience through mechanisms such as improved metabolic flexibility, reduced chronic inflammation, and strengthened immune surveillance, thereby influencing both tumor biology and treatment tolerability. While causality cannot be firmly established due to the observational design, the large homogeneous cohort and consistent associations across outcomes support the role of physical activity as a clinically meaningful factor in survivorship and treatment response.

De Paulo and colleagues [51] conducted a cross-sectional study to explore whether physical activity levels are linked to metabolic risk markers in postmenopausal women receiving aromatase inhibitor (AI) therapy for breast cancer. They enrolled 101 postmenopausal women aged 50–80 years and divided them into two groups: a control group without cancer (GW; n = 65) and a breast cancer group under AI therapy (GC; n = 36). In women without cancer (GW), the study found significant inverse correlations between physical activity and several metabolic risk markers: VLDL cholesterol (β = − 0.036; 95% CI − 0.068 to − 0.004), triglycerides (β = − 0.036; 95% CI − 0.015 to − 0.002), fasting glucose (β = − 0.029; 95% CI − 0.047 to − 0.012), and CRP (β = − 0.044; 95% CI − 0.085 to − 0.003). These relationships suggest that greater physical activity is associated with more favorable lipid profiles, lower glycemia, and reduced systemic inflammation in healthy postmenopausal women. However, within the AI-treated breast cancer group (GC), no significant associations emerged between physical activity and any of the metabolic variables. In other words, among women using aromatase inhibitors, higher self-reported physical activity did not correlate with better lipid, glucose, or inflammatory profiles [51]. The authors interpret the absence of correlations in the AI group as indicative that AI therapy may blunt or disrupt the beneficial metabolic effects of habitual exercise, or that, in this population, the intensity or quantity of activity was insufficient to overcome treatment-associated metabolic constraints. An alternative explanation considered is that women on AIs may engage in lower-intensity activity overall, limiting the detectable influence on metabolic biomarkers. The cross-sectional design, however, prevents causal inference: the authors cannot conclude whether AI therapy suppresses exercise benefits, or whether unmeasured confounders (e.g. disease severity, medication adherence, underlying metabolic health) underlie the lack of association [51].

In one compelling trial, Gonzalo-Encabo et al. [52] evaluated how biomarker changes in postmenopausal women responded not only to an exercise intervention, but also to subsequent weight regain. Although participants achieved favorable changes in estrogen and insulin–related biomarkers during the active intervention, modest regain of weight was linked to reversal of many of these improvements, specifically, rises in estradiol, estrone, free estradiol, insulin, and HOMA-IR, along with decreases in SHBG. This study emphasizes the challenge of maintaining metabolic benefit over time and suggests that long-term adherence or additional strategies to offset weight rebound are central to leveraging exercise as an intervention in breast cancer risk modulation [52].

Participants underwent a structured exercise program that led to modest weight loss, which was accompanied by favorable shifts in several biomarkers closely associated with postmenopausal breast cancer risk. Specifically, reductions were observed in circulating estradiol, estrone, and free estradiol, alongside improvements in insulin sensitivity and increases in sex hormone–binding globulin (SHBG). These findings were consistent with the idea that exercise-mediated weight loss can reduce estrogen exposure and improve metabolic control, thereby potentially lowering breast cancer risk [52].

However, the study’s most striking observation was that these benefits were not durable in the face of weight regain. Even a relatively small regain of ~ 1.8 kg was sufficient to reverse many of the favorable changes. Women who regained weight showed significant increases in estradiol, estrone, free estradiol, fasting insulin levels, and HOMA-IR, indicating a rapid deterioration in both hormonal balance and insulin regulation. In parallel, SHBG concentrations decreased, which is clinically important since lower SHBG levels increase the fraction of bioavailable estrogen capable of binding to estrogen receptors. The combination of heightened estrogen exposure and worsened insulin resistance reestablished a metabolic milieu that favors tumor progression, essentially negating the protective impact of the initial exercise intervention [52].

Thus, this study illustrates a critical clinical challenge: although structured exercise is an effective strategy for reducing breast cancer–related metabolic risk factors, the sustainability of these benefits hinges on preventing weight relapse. From a survivorship perspective, the results highlight the need to integrate exercise programs with behavioral support, dietary interventions, and long-term lifestyle strategies to ensure that metabolic and hormonal improvements are preserved over time [52].

In a recent clinical trial, Cespedes et al. [53] evaluated the impact of a three-month multimodal program combining structured physical exercise with health education in postmenopausal women receiving aromatase inhibitors. At baseline, more than one-third of participants (37.7%) met the diagnostic criteria for metabolic syndrome, a prevalence that fell to 15.1% following the intervention. Improvements were most pronounced in specific components of metabolic risk: significant reductions were observed in waist circumference, blood pressure, and serum triglycerides concentrations. By contrast, fasting glucose and HDL cholesterol did not change significantly, suggesting that some features of metabolic syndrome may be more resistant to short-term lifestyle modification. Beyond metabolic outcomes, participants reported marked gains in quality of life, with improvements detected across the EORTC QLQ-C30, BR23, and EQ-5D scales, underscoring the broader psychosocial benefits of combined lifestyle interventions. Predictive modeling further revealed that adherence to health education sessions and marital status were independent predictors of metabolic improvement, highlighting the importance of both behavioral engagement and social support in sustaining lifestyle change [53].

Thomas et al. [54] addressed the high prevalence of metabolic syndrome among postmenopausal breast cancer survivors and tested whether a moderate aerobic exercise intervention could improve metabolic risk factors over six months. Their rationale was that metabolic syndrome, a cluster of abdominal adiposity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and glucose dysregulation, elevates the risk for cardiovascular disease and may also contribute to worse cancer outcomes in survivors. The study was conducted within the Yale Exercise and Survivorship (YES) trial. Sixty-five sedentary breast cancer survivors (within 1–10 years’ post-diagnosis, stages 0–IIIa) were randomized into two arms: an aerobic exercise intervention (EX; n = 35, mean BMI ~ 30.8 kg/m²) or usual care (UC; n = 30, mean BMI ~ 29.4 kg/m²). The exercise prescription aimed for supervised aerobic sessions, to achieve 120 min/week; “adherence” was defined as meeting at least that threshold (≈ 80% of target).

At baseline, 55.4% of participants met criteria for metabolic syndrome. Over the six-month period, there was no statistically significant difference in the change in metabolic syndrome prevalence or in the mean metabolic syndrome z-score between the EX and UC arms [54]. However, when stratifying by exercise adherence, those participants who met at least 120 min/week showed a significant reduction in metabolic syndrome z-score (mean change ≈ − 0.76 ± 0.36) compared to nonadherers, who had an increase (mean + 0.80 ± 0.42), P = 0.009. Among individual components, the authors report a significant decrease in fasting glucose levels associated with the exercise intervention; changes in other components were more modest and did not consistently reach statistical significance [54]. Interestingly, the authors state that the observed metabolic benefits of exercise were not fully explained by reductions in fat mass in their sample, suggesting that exercise may confer benefits beyond pure weight loss. They also highlight that the dose of exercise achieved was relatively modest (less than the full ACSM guidelines), yet still tolerated well in this population and sufficient to produce measurable glucose and metabolic improvements. Because of the limited sample size and modest duration, the authors caution that the study may not have had sufficient power to detect changes across all metabolic syndrome criteria or prevalence. They also note that supervised exercise settings may not fully generalize to real-world, unsupervised conditions [54].

Gonzalo-Encabo and colleagues [55] carried out a randomized controlled trial to test the effects of different exercise modalities on circulating sex hormones and body composition in obese or overweight postmenopausal women [55]. The study recruited 35 sedentary, obese postmenopausal women, who were randomly assigned to one of three groups: an endurance (EN) training group (n = 10), a concurrent (CON) training group combining endurance + resistance (n = 13), or a control (C) group (n = 12), over a 12-week supervised intervention. Training was matched in time (3 sessions per week, each 60 min). In terms of body composition, the intervention produced a statistically significant reduction in total fat mass across exercisers (~ 5.3%) relative to baseline. Within the concurrent group, lean body mass also increased (~ 1.5%), reflecting a preservation or gain of muscle despite fat loss [55]. Hormonal changes differed by exercise group. In the endurance (EN) group, there were significant declines in DHEA-S (~ − 13%) and in total testosterone (~ − 40%) as well as free testosterone (~ − 41%) after training. Conversely, in the concurrent (CON) group, total testosterone increased by ~ 25% and free testosterone by ~ 21%. SHBG and estradiol changes were less consistent when assessed by group alone [55]. The authors also conducted a stratified analysis by fat mass loss threshold (> 2 kg vs. <2 kg). In women who lost more than 2 kg of fat mass, there was a notable increase in SHBG (~ 21%) and further reduction in DHEA-S (–13%). This suggests that the magnitude of fat loss itself may moderate hormonal responses to exercise, beyond the modality of training [55]. Overall, the trial provides empirical evidence that not only whether one exercises, but how one exercises (endurance vs. mixed training) and the extent of fat mass loss, are key determinants of how sex hormone profiles shift in obese postmenopausal women [55].

Viskochil and colleagues [56] set out to evaluate the effects of a structured aerobic exercise intervention on markers of insulin resistance and glucose metabolism in postmenopausal breast cancer survivors, recognizing insulin resistance as a possible contributor to recurrence risk. The trial enrolled 15 postmenopausal breast cancer survivors who were previously inactive (not meeting ≥ 150 min/week exercise guideline) and implemented a supervised aerobic training protocol of 60 min per session, 3–4 times per week, achieving on average 156.8 ± 16.6 min/week. Following the 12-week intervention, participants exhibited statistically significant improvements in estimated VO₂peak (+ 2.8 ± 1.4 mL/kg/min) and a modest but significant weight loss (–1.1 ± 0.8 kg) (both P < 0.05). However, the changes in insulin metrics were much more nuanced. There was no statistically significant change across fasting insulin, peak insulin, or iAUC, nor in overall insulin sensitivity index (C-ISI) [56].

The only timepoint showing a meaningful reduction in insulin was at 120 minutes’ post-glucose ingestion (from 68.8 ± 34.5 to 56.2 ± 31.9 µU/mL; P < 0.05). Interestingly, the authors observed significant interactions with aromatase inhibitor (AI) use: non-AI users tended to show reductions in peak insulin (–11.99 µU/mL) and iAUC (–24.03 µU/mL), whereas AI users showed increases in these same metrics (+ 13.91 µU/mL for peak, + 32.73 µU/mL for iAUC). This suggests that AI therapy may blunt or reverse the favorable insulin responses to exercise in this population [56]. They also note the small sample size, absence of a control group, and potential heterogeneity in participants’ baseline metabolic states as limitations. In summary, the study offers a cautionary note: while exercise modestly improved fitness and body weight, its impact on insulin-glucose dynamics was limited in this cohort. The modulatory effect of AI use highlights that patient-specific treatment context may condition metabolic responsiveness. This insight underscores the need for longer, larger trials and perhaps combination strategies (e.g. exercise + dietary or pharmacological adjuncts) to more robustly modulate insulin resistance among breast cancer survivors [56].

Nuri et al. [57] conducted a randomized controlled trial to examine whether a combination of aerobic and resistance training could improve components of the metabolic syndrome in postmenopausal breast cancer survivors. They enrolled 29 postmenopausal women with breast cancer, with a mean age of approximately 58.3 years. These women were randomly allocated into two groups: an experimental group (n = 14) which underwent a 15-week exercise program, and a control group (n = 15) which maintained usual lifestyle. The exercise intervention comprised walking sessions twice weekly and resistance training twice weekly on non-walking days, amounting to four sessions per week [57]. Their results indicated that the exercise group demonstrated statistically significant, favorable changes across a broad spectrum of metabolic syndrome–related parameters relative to the control group. Specifically, the experimental arm improved VO₂peak (reflecting enhanced cardiorespiratory fitness) and lowered resting heart rate. Among anthropometric measures, participants in the exercise group exhibited reductions in body weight, BMI, and WHR. Systolic blood pressure also declined in the intervention group. On the metabolic front, the exercise cohort showed improvements in fasting insulin and glucose levels, reductions in insulin resistance metrics, and favorable shifts in lipid parameters (increases in HDL cholesterol and decreases in triglycerides). All these changes reached statistical significance when comparing post-training values between the exercise and control groups [57]. This trial provides evidence that a relatively modest but combined exercise regimen, mixing walking and resistance training, can beneficially modify multiple features of the metabolic syndrome in postmenopausal breast cancer survivors over a 15-week period. The improvements span aerobic fitness, anthropometrics, blood pressure, glycemic control, and lipid profile, supporting the concept that structured physical activity is a potent nonpharmacologic tool to counter metabolic dysregulation in this patient group [57].

Leite and colleagues [58] investigated how resistance training, and subsequent detraining, affects body composition, muscle strength, lipid profiles, inflammatory cytokines, and oxidative stress markers in postmenopausal breast cancer survivors receiving tamoxifen. They hypothesized that supervised resistance exercise would improve anthropometric and biochemical health markers, and that detraining might reverse some benefits. The study recruited 14 postmenopausal breast cancer survivors on tamoxifen therapy and subjected them to 12 weeks of supervised resistance training followed by 12 weeks of detraining (i.e. cessation of supervised exercise) [58]. After the 12-week resistance training phase, participants exhibited a statistically significant reduction in fat mass (from 39.4 ± 6.9% to 37.7 ± 6.8%) and an increase in fat-free (lean) mass (from 39.3 ± 4.9% to 40.3 ± 5.6%). Muscle strength improved accordingly. Lipid improvements included reductions in triglycerides (from 156 ± 45 to 123 ± 43 mg/dL) and total cholesterol (from 202 ± 13 to 186 ± 16 mg/dL). HDL-cholesterol increased (from 47 ± 9 to 56 ± 9 mg/dL) and remained elevated above baseline even after detraining (53 ± 10 mg/dL). In terms of inflammatory and oxidative/antioxidant markers, the authors observed increases in IL-6 (24.65 ± 10.85 to 41.42 ± 22.88 pg/mL) and decreases in IL-17 (2.42 ± 0.32 to 1.69 ± 0.19 pg/mL) following training. Oxidative stress, as assessed by TBARS, declined (1.91 ± 0.19 to 1.03 ± 0.10 µmol/L). Activities of key antioxidant enzymes—superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase—also reduced after training (SOD, from 24.65 ± 10.85 to 41.42 ± 22.88 U/gHb; catalase, from 445.9 ± 113.0 to 345.8 ± 81.7 k/gHb·s). Interestingly, after detraining, many of these gains reversed: muscle strength declined, lipid markers partially regressed (though HDL stayed above baseline), and oxidative/antioxidant marker improvements tended toward baseline levels [58].

Jones et al. [59] conducted a randomized controlled trial to assess whether a six-month aerobic exercise intervention could reduce systemic inflammatory markers, specifically interleukin-6 (IL-6), C-reactive protein (CRP), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), in postmenopausal breast cancer survivors. The authors hypothesized that exercise might attenuate chronic low-grade inflammation, which is implicated in cancer recurrence and comorbid conditions, and that reductions in adiposity could mediate these effects [59]. The trial recruited 75 women from the Yale-New Haven Hospital Tumor Registry and randomized them to either an aerobic exercise group or a usual care (control) group. At baseline, IL-6 and CRP were positively correlated with both total body fat and BMI, and inversely correlated with daily pedometer step counts (i.e., higher physical activity associated with lower inflammation). However, in intention-to-treat analyses, the authors found no significant differences in changes of IL-6, CRP, or TNF-α between the exercise and control groups over six months. Nonetheless, in secondary analyses restricted to exercisers who achieved at least 80% of the intervention goal, there was a statistically significant reduction in IL-6 compared with less adherent participants. TNF-α and CRP did not show significant changes even in this adherent subgroup [59]. The authors interpret these results as indicating that under the conditions of this trial (moderate-intensity aerobic training over six months without a weight-loss focus), exercise alone may not robustly modulate systemic inflammatory markers in breast cancer survivors at large. The observed reduction in IL-6 among more adherent participants suggests a threshold or dose–response effect, signaling that adequate intensity, consistency, or concomitant weight loss might be necessary to achieve anti-inflammatory benefits [59]. Limitations include reliance on peripheral inflammatory biomarkers (which may not reflect localized microenvironment changes), the relatively short intervention duration, and limited power to detect small biomarker shifts. There is also potential selection bias in the subgroup analysis of adherent participants.

Matthews and colleagues probed whether a 12-month moderate-to-vigorous exercise intervention could alter circulating estrogens and their metabolic pathways in postmenopausal women [60]. In this randomized controlled trial, 153 postmenopausal women were assigned to a year-long exercise program, and 153 to control. The exercise group averaged about 178 min per week of activity over the 12-month period. Cardiorespiratory fitness was assessed via treadmill testing. Blood samples were collected at baseline and 12 months, and using stable isotope dilution liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC/MS-MS), the investigators measured total parent estrogens (estrone, estradiol) plus 13 estrogen metabolites (including conjugated and unconjugated forms) spanning the 2-, 4-, and 16-hydroxylation pathways [60]. At 12 months, the exercise group had significantly greater fitness, about 13% higher cardiorespiratory capacity compared to controls (P = 0.0001). In terms of hormonal changes, total estradiol declined by approximately 10% in the exercise arm relative to control (P = 0.04). However, when examining the spectrum of estrogen metabolites, the authors found no significant exercise-induced shifts in concentrations of metabolites in the 2-, 4-, or 16-hydroxylation pathways, nor in the ratio of 2-pathway metabolites to parent estrogens [60].

Interestingly, in exploratory analyses, greater improvements in fitness (rather than simply the exercise assignment) were statistically associated with lower levels of 2-pathway metabolites, suggesting that individual physiological adaptability to training might influence metabolic patterns. Even so, the main randomized comparison did not yield evidence that the intervention reprogrammed estrogen metabolism in a direction favorable for lowering breast cancer risk via those pathways. From these findings, the authors conclude that while moderate-to-vigorous exercise can reduce total estradiol in postmenopausal women and improve fitness, it is unlikely that changes in estrogen metabolic profiles (i.e. diversion toward 2-hydroxylated products) are the mediating mechanism of exercise’s protective effect. Thus, they suggest that alternative pathways, such as insulin sensitivity, body composition changes, inflammation, or immune modulation, may be more plausible mechanisms linking physical activity to reduced breast cancer risk [60].

Overall, evidence from the studies reviewed in this section indicates that structured exercise, whether aerobic, resistance, or combined, can improve metabolic and hormonal health in postmenopausal breast cancer survivors (also summarized in Table 1). Interventions consistently reduce body fat, particularly visceral adiposity, enhance lean mass and cardiorespiratory fitness, and improve lipid and glucose regulation. Trials have shown decreases in triglycerides, insulin, and estradiol levels, alongside increases in HDL cholesterol and SHBG, suggesting beneficial shifts in pathways linked to breast cancer risk. Improvements in quality of life and treatment tolerance further support exercise as a key component of survivorship care. However, the collective evidence is tempered by several limitations. Most trials included small samples and short durations, limiting assessment of long-term outcomes. Variations in exercise mode, intensity, and adherence make comparisons difficult, and many studies lacked comprehensive dietary or behavioral control. Hormonal therapy use, particularly aromatase inhibitors, may blunt metabolic responses, contributing to inconsistent findings for insulin sensitivity and lipid markers. Additionally, while inflammatory and hormonal changes are directionally favorable, they are often modest and inconsistent across biomarkers.

Clinical trials discussing the effects of exercise training on the metabolic status of breast cancer survivors

Although the primary emphasis of this review has been on estrogen receptor–positive breast cancer, emerging evidence suggests that the metabolic effects of exercise and nutritional interventions are also highly relevant to estrogen receptor–negative subtypes, particularly TNBC. Unlike ER+ tumors, TNBC is largely driven by estrogen-independent mechanisms and exhibits a pronounced reliance on metabolic reprogramming, including increased glycolysis, lipid metabolism, and sensitivity to systemic insulin and inflammatory signaling.

Emerging evidence indicates that lifestyle factors, particularly physical activity and metabolic health, play a significant role in TNBC progression and outcomes. Regular exercise has been associated with reduced tumor growth and improved prognosis, potentially through systemic metabolic regulation rather than hormone-dependent mechanisms. Specifically, exercise-induced adaptations influence whole-body energy homeostasis, leading to improvements in insulin sensitivity, reductions in obesity-related risk factors, and modulation of the tumor microenvironment. Mechanistically, these effects are linked to the inhibition of key oncogenic signaling pathways, particularly the PI3K/Akt/mTOR axis, which is central to cell proliferation and survival in TNBC [61].

In a retrospective cohort study by Mozaffar Aznab et al. [62], the survival outcomes of 110 patients with TNBC treated between October 2009 and July 2018 were evaluated following combination chemotherapy alongside structured lifestyle modification recommendations, including dietary changes, weight management, and regular physical activity. Patients received five cycles of docetaxel, epirubicin, and cyclophosphamide every 21 days, followed by two additional cycles of epirubicin, cyclophosphamide, and 5-fluorouracil. The median and mean follow-up durations were 52.3 months and 45.6 months, respectively. Among the cohort, 8 patients presented with metastatic disease, while 103 patients received adjuvant therapy, and 13 patients experienced relapse during follow-up. The study reported a median relapse-free survival (RFS) of 44.4 months and a mean overall survival (OS) of 49.2 months, with 20 deaths recorded and 90 patients remaining alive at the end of the observation period [62].

Importantly, survival outcomes differed significantly according to p53 expression status. Patients with p53-negative tumors demonstrated a mean OS of 64.2 months, compared with poorer outcomes among p53-positive cases, in whom eight relapse events were observed versus two relapses in the p53-negative group (P = 0.021). Additionally, disease-free survival (DFS) was substantially higher in the p53-negative subgroup (96.1%) compared with 76.3% in the p53-positive subgroup, suggesting a prognostic association between p53 status and treatment response in this population. Overall, the authors concluded that the integration of lifestyle interventions with chemotherapy was associated with reduced relapse rates during the first three years after treatment and improved survival outcomes, particularly among patients with favorable molecular characteristics such as negative p53 expression [62].

In a randomized controlled trial, 173 women with stage I–III breast cancer initiating chemotherapy were assigned either to a structured exercise and nutrition intervention (n = 87) or usual care (n = 86) to evaluate whether lifestyle modification improves chemotherapy completion and treatment response [63]. The intervention consisted of a home-based physical activity program combined with individualized dietary counseling delivered by oncology-certified registered dietitians during chemotherapy. The primary endpoints included relative dose intensity (RDI), a measure of chemotherapy completion, and pathologic complete response (pCR) [63].

Participants in the intervention arm demonstrated significant improvements in physical activity levels and diet quality compared with controls, confirming adherence and feasibility of the lifestyle program during active treatment [63]. Importantly, the intervention was associated with improved treatment response, with pCR observed in approximately 53% of patients in the intervention group compared with 28% in the usual-care group, suggesting a clinically meaningful enhancement in chemotherapy effectiveness. However, relative dose intensity remained high in both groups, indicating that chemotherapy completion rates were already favorable and not significantly different between arms [63]. These findings support the hypothesis that structured lifestyle interventions implemented during chemotherapy may improve tumor response outcomes without compromising treatment delivery, highlighting the potential role of exercise and nutrition as adjunct strategies to optimize systemic therapy effectiveness in breast cancer. The authors concluded that early integration of behavioral interventions following diagnosis represents a feasible and potentially impactful approach to improving short-term oncologic endpoints such as pCR, warranting further investigation in larger trials and across molecular subtypes [63].

In a randomized controlled trial evaluating the impact of a structured lifestyle intervention during chemotherapy, 173 women with stage I–III breast cancer were assigned to either a 12-month nutrition and exercise intervention (n = 87) or usual care (n = 86) to assess whether behavioral modification could attenuate declines in patient-reported quality-of-life (QoL) outcomes during treatment [64]. Participants had a mean age of 52.8 ± 11.1 years, and 51% were diagnosed with stage I disease at baseline. QoL measures were assessed using validated PROMIS-29, PROMIS Cognitive Function, and PROMIS Global Health scales at diagnosis, post-chemotherapy, and at 1- and 2-year follow-up time points [64].

Across both intervention and control groups, chemotherapy was associated with significant short-term deterioration in multiple patient-reported outcomes between diagnosis and post-chemotherapy assessment. Specifically, physical function declined by − 5.5 points in the intervention arm versus − 5.4 in controls, fatigue increased by + 5.4 versus + 6.2 points, social role participation declined by − 5.4 versus − 7.1 points, and cognitive function decreased by − 4.8 versus − 4.3 points, respectively. Similarly, global physical health decreased by − 10.9 points in the intervention group and − 10.1 points in the control group, while global mental health declined by − 11.2 and − 9.7 points, respectively. Notably, anxiety scores improved in both groups, with reductions of − 5.1 points in the intervention arm and − 3.7 points in the usual-care arm [64].

Despite measurable improvements in lifestyle behaviors, including increased physical activity and dietary quality reported elsewhere in the trial analyses, the intervention did not significantly attenuate chemotherapy-related declines in QoL compared with usual care during active treatment. However, most PROMIS outcomes returned to baseline levels by 1 year after randomization and remained stable through 2 years, suggesting recovery of treatment-related functional impairment over time irrespective of intervention assignment. Anxiety remained improved relative to baseline at long-term follow-up [64].

Overall, these findings indicate that although structured nutrition and exercise interventions during chemotherapy are feasible and associated with behavioral improvements, their short-term effect on chemotherapy-related QoL decline appears limited, highlighting the need for more targeted strategies to identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit from lifestyle modification during active systemic treatment.

Integration: functional nutrition combined with exercise as therapeutic strategy

A large pooled analysis incorporated 20 prospective cohort studies comprising over one million women and 36,297 breast cancer cases to assess how body size and weight change throughout adulthood influence breast cancer risk, stratified by menopausal status and tumor receptor subtype [65]. Height was consistently associated with higher breast cancer risk. Each 5 cm increment corresponded to a 6–7% increase in risk, with the effect most pronounced for estrogen and progesterone receptor–positive tumors. Adult body mass index (BMI) demonstrated divergent associations by menopausal status. In premenopausal women, higher adult BMI was inversely related to breast cancer risk. Conversely, in postmenopausal women, higher BMI was positively associated with breast cancer, particularly among those who had never used hormone replacement therapy, and the relationship was strongest for receptor-positive disease [65]. Early adulthood BMI, measured at approximately 18–20 years, displayed a protective role across both menopausal groups. Women with higher BMI in early adulthood had a substantially reduced risk of later breast cancer: approximately 21% lower risk per 5 kg/m² among premenopausal cases and around 11% lower among postmenopausal cases. This protective association was more marked for receptor-negative cancers [65]. Weight gain was strongly associated with elevated risk of breast cancer, particularly in women who began adulthood leaner and for receptor-positive tumors. By contrast, weight gain showed null or inverse associations with premenopausal breast cancer [65]. Overall, the analysis highlights the dual influence of weight at different life stages: leanness in early adulthood combined with excess weight gain later in life markedly increases the risk of postmenopausal, receptor-positive breast cancer. These findings underscore the critical importance of weight management as a modifiable factor in breast cancer prevention strategies (Table 2; Fig. 2).

Naaman and colleagues examined the oncogenic implications of obesity, focusing on how metabolic dysregulation links excess adiposity to breast cancer risk [66]. Their synthesis highlights that postmenopausal, hormone receptor–positive breast cancer risk is strongly influenced not only by BMI and adult weight gain but also by adverse body composition. Increased visceral adiposity and ectopic fat deposition confer elevated risk even in women with a normal BMI, underscoring the limitations of BMI as a sole measure. Mechanistic evidence indicates that obese adipose tissue undergoes hypertrophy, hypoxia, and immune cell infiltration, forming crown-like structures that drive local chronic inflammation. These changes elevate pro-inflammatory cytokines and shift adipokine balance toward higher leptin and lower adiponectin levels, favoring tumor growth. Parallel endocrine disruptions include insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia, and elevated IGF-1 signaling, accompanied by suppression of sex hormone–binding globulin, which increases the availability of bioactive estrogens. Collectively, these metabolic alterations create a proliferative and anti-apoptotic environment in breast tissue [66].

The review also emphasizes intervention data showing that weight loss, particularly reductions in fat mass achieved through diet, exercise, or pharmacologic strategies, improves systemic metabolism and inflammation. Such changes are accompanied by favorable reductions in circulating estrogens and improvements in breast density, supporting the premise that lifestyle modification can mitigate obesity-driven oncogenic pathways. These findings are directly relevant to integrative survivorship strategies, as they demonstrate how functional nutrition and structured exercise may interrupt the biological crosstalk between adiposity, inflammation, and hormone metabolism. By reducing fat mass and improving metabolic health, lifestyle interventions target the very mechanisms that obesity exploits to promote breast carcinogenesis [66].

In this regard, Duggan and colleagues [67] investigated how intentional weight loss affects circulating microRNAs (miRNAs) implicated in obesity in postmenopausal women.

192 Subjects had been randomized to either a reduced-calorie diet, an exercise intervention, a combined diet + exercise arm, or a control group (no intervention). The principal finding was that women randomized to weight-loss interventions showed a significantly greater reduction in miR-122 levels compared to controls (− 7.25% vs. +33.5% in controls). Moreover, miR-122 levels correlated modestly but significantly with the degree of weight loss. In analyses stratified by weight loss magnitude, participants who lost ≥ 10% of baseline weight exhibited an 11.7% decrease in miR-122, whereas those with 0–10% loss had only a 2.0% decrease; controls had a 33.5% increase [67]. For the other 22 miRNAs, no statistically significant differences between intervention and control groups survived after multiple comparisons correction. The authors thus emphasize that among the measured miRNAs, miR-122 was the strongest candidate for modulation by weight loss. They interpret this as evidence that miRNA regulation may represent one mechanism by which weight loss exerts favorable effects on cancer-related biology [67].

Another study investigated the combined effects of a twice-weekly training program (impact-aerobic plus strength exercise) and assessment of dietary habits on body composition among postmenopausal breast cancer survivors undergoing aromatase inhibitor therapy. Recognizing that aromatase inhibitors often contribute to adverse changes in fat distribution and metabolic risk, the authors hypothesized that a structured exercise regimen might counteract fat accumulation, particularly visceral and subcutaneous adiposity, while dietary patterns could moderate these effects [68]. In their methods, forty-three postmenopausal women with BMI ≥ 35 kg/m² under aromatase inhibitor treatment were randomized to an intervention group (n = 21) implementing the combined training or to a control group (n = 22) continuing usual lifestyle. The results revealed that participants in the exercise group experienced significant improvements in body composition compared to controls. Specifically, the intervention group showed reductions in subcutaneous adipose tissue and visceral adipose tissue, as well as a decline in total fat tissue mass. These changes indicate that the combined exercise regimen effectively mitigated adiposity in key compartments even in the context of aromatase inhibitor therapy. From the dietary assessments, participants demonstrated moderate adherence to the Mediterranean diet, although nutritional intake of calcium, zinc, folic acid, and vitamins D, A, and E was reported as low [68].

The study underscores that a twice-weekly combination of impact-aerobic and resistance training can meaningfully improve fat composition among breast cancer survivors on aromatase inhibitors, even in a high-risk metabolic context. The finding that visceral and subcutaneous fat compartments both responded suggests a systemic benefit beyond simple weight loss. Moreover, the dietary data point to the importance of integrating nutrition guidance with exercise interventions: without sufficient micronutrient and dietary quality, the full potential of exercise on metabolic health might not be realized [68].

Friedenreich and colleagues performed a follow-up study to a prior randomized trial comparing higher-dose versus moderate-dose aerobic exercise among inactive, postmenopausal women, assessing whether the differential exercise dose produces sustained changes in breast cancer–relevant biomarkers over a 24-month span [69]. The parent trial had randomized participants to either 300 min/week (high) or 150 min/week (moderate) of prescribed aerobic activity, with biomarker measurement at baseline, 12 months, and 24 months. This follow-up analysis examined biomarker changes during the post-intervention period (months 12 to 24) and overall, from baseline to 24 months [69]. Out of the original cohort, 84.0% of the moderate group and 82.5% of the high group had available fasting blood samples at 24 months (n = 333/400). The authors compared biomarker trajectories across randomized arms, focusing on endogenous sex hormones and other cancer-related biomarkers. They found no significant differences between the HIGH and MODERATE groups in biomarker changes from 0 to 24 months or 12 to 24 months; the ratio of mean changes ranged from 0.97 to 1.06, and all P values exceeded 0.05 [69]. However, in secondary stratified analyses, women who achieved fat mass loss above the median during the trial displayed more favorable biomarker profiles at follow-up, regardless of exercise dose assignment. This suggests that actual adiposity reduction may mediate or amplify the biomarker response to exercise. Thus, the authors interpret that prescribing a higher exercise volume per se (300 vs. 150 min/week) did not lead to greater long-term changes in biomarkers, but that exercise plus fat loss may yield more durable biomolecular benefits [69].

Brown and colleagues conducted a 52-week randomized trial among postmenopausal breast cancer survivors who were insufficiently physically active and classified as overweight or obese, to test whether interventions of exercise, diet, or their combination would favorably alter circulating sex steroid hormone levels [70]. A total of 269 participants were randomized into one of four arms: control, exercise-only, diet-only, or combined exercise + diet. In the exercise-only arm compared to control, estradiol declined by approximately − 1.9% (95% CI: − 12.6, 8.8), SHBG increased by 2.4% (95% CI: − 9.9, 14.6), and testosterone increased by 1.2% (95% CI: − 12.2, 14.5), none of which reached statistical significance. In the diet-only arm versus control, estradiol declined by − 7.8% (95% CI: − 17.6, 1.9), SHBG rose by 8.2% (95% CI: − 4.2, 20.6), and testosterone changed by − 0.8% (95% CI: − 13.6, 12.0), but again these changes were not statistically significant. In the combined diet + exercise arm, estradiol fell by − 6.3% (95% CI: − 16.3, 3.6), SHBG increased by 8.8% (95% CI: − 4.0, 21.7), and testosterone decreased by − 5.3% (95% CI: − 18.0, 7.4), still without reaching significance [70]. Overall, none of the interventions, whether exercise alone, diet al.one, or their combination, produced statistically significant changes in estradiol, SHBG, or testosterone relative to control over 52 weeks in this cohort. However, the authors note that some trends were consistent with hypothesized directions: modest reductions in estradiol and testosterone in the diet and combined arms, and modest SHBG increases, suggesting potential, but weak, hormonal modulation. They also point out that variability in individual responses and the magnitude of weight or fat loss could have influenced detection of effects [70].

A Diet, physical Activity and Mammography (DAMA) study investigated whether long-term lifestyle interventions could alter circulating sex hormone concentrations in healthy postmenopausal women, recognizing these hormones as key mediators of breast cancer risk [71]. A total of 234 women were randomized into four arms: dietary intervention, physical activity intervention, combined diet plus physical activity, or control, and were followed for 24 months [71]. At baseline, sex hormone levels were within the expected postmenopausal range. Over the 24-month period, distinct patterns emerged depending on intervention type. Women in the combined diet and physical activity group experienced the most consistent changes, with reductions observed in total estradiol and free estradiol concentrations relative to controls. This effect was particularly pronounced in participants who achieved greater adherence to both dietary modification and structured physical activity [71]. The dietary intervention alone also produced significant declines in circulating estradiol and estrone levels, accompanied by increases in sex hormone–binding globulin (SHBG). These changes suggest a reduction in bioavailable estrogens. The physical activity intervention independently had a more modest hormonal effect but contributed to favorable shifts when combined with diet. Testosterone and free testosterone levels showed smaller, less consistent reductions across intervention groups, though trends pointed toward lower androgen availability with combined lifestyle modification [71]. Importantly, the observed hormonal shifts were stronger among participants who lost body weight or reduced fat mass during the intervention. Weight loss correlated with greater declines in estradiol and free estradiol and with more substantial rises in SHBG. These findings highlight adiposity reduction as a mediating factor in the hormonal response to lifestyle interventions [71].

In an interim report from a 5-year prospective cohort, 38 postmenopausal women with early breast cancer were followed during chemotherapy to evaluate weight change, metabolic markers, bone mineral density, and patient-reported outcomes [72]. Participants gained an average of 1.2 kg over the treatment period, though this increase was not statistically significant. Despite the limited change in body weight, marked alterations in metabolic parameters were observed. HDL cholesterol levels decreased significantly, while triglycerides rose, indicating a shift toward a less favorable lipid profile. Insulin sensitivity declined modestly, though this change did not reach significance, suggesting early signs of metabolic impairment [72].

Patient-reported outcomes revealed worsening of fatigue and pain across the treatment course. Interestingly, women reported higher physical activity levels compared to baseline, although these self-reports contrasted with the increase in fatigue, suggesting complex behavioral adaptations during treatment. Bone mineral density remained stable, showing no accelerated decline beyond expected age-related changes at this interim stage. The findings highlight that chemotherapy in postmenopausal breast cancer patients can negatively impact lipid metabolism and insulin dynamics even in the absence of significant weight gain. These shifts may contribute to increased long-term cardiometabolic and cancer recurrence risk. By identifying these metabolic vulnerabilities during treatment, the study underscores the need for supportive strategies, particularly lifestyle interventions such as tailored exercise and nutritional programs, to counteract lipid derangements and preserve metabolic health in survivorship [72].

In a SHAPE-2 randomized trial, overweight postmenopausal women were assigned to achieve equivalent ~ 5–6 kg weight loss via diet al.one or primarily through exercise (plus minimal calorie restriction), versus controls [73]. Both intervention arms met the weight loss target: −4.9 kg (diet) and − 5.5 kg (exercise). The exercise group lost significantly more body fat (≈ 1.4 kg more) compared to diet al.one. The exercise arm also showed a greater reduction in free testosterone compared to the diet arm (treatment effect ratio 0.92, p = 0.043). Compared with controls, both interventions yielded favorable shifts in sex hormone profiles: reductions in total and free estradiol (TER ≈ 0.86 and 0.80 for diet; TER ≈ 0.83 and 0.77 for exercise) and increases in SHBG (TER ≈ 1.14 and 1.21). After adjustment for fat mass change, many of these hormonal effects were attenuated, suggesting that fat loss largely mediated the changes [73].

In this SHAPE-2 randomized trial, both intervention groups lost around 6–7% of their body weight [74]. Compared with controls, they showed marked reductions in abdominal fat. The diet group reduced subcutaneous abdominal fat by about 12.5% and intra-abdominal fat by 12%, while the diet plus exercise group achieved reductions of 16% and 14.6%, respectively. Adding exercise to dietary restriction produced a significantly greater loss in subcutaneous fat, with an additional mean decrease of 10.6 cm² (95% CI: − 18.7 to − 2.4) compared with diet al.one. For intra-abdominal fat, the further reduction of 3.8 cm² (95% CI: − 9.0 to 1.3) was not statistically significant. Overall, diet-induced weight loss substantially decreased abdominal adiposity, and exercise enhanced the reduction in subcutaneous fat beyond that achieved through diet al.one [74].

In a 16-week randomized trial, both intervention arms also achieved comparable weight loss, while the control group remained stable. Relative to control, the exercise group showed a 36% reduction in high sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) (treatment effect ratio [TER] = 0.64), and the diet group exhibited a 23% reduction (TER = 0.77). The between-group comparison suggested a somewhat greater benefit from exercise (TER = 0.83). Leptin levels declined significantly in both interventions: TER = 0.55 for the exercise arm and TER = 0.59 in the diet arm versus controls. After adjusting for changes in body fat percentage, these effects lost statistical significance, with a lesser attenuation when adjusting for fitness gains. No significant changes were observed in interleukin-6 (IL-6) or adiponectin in either intervention compared to control [75].

In another 12-month randomized controlled trial, postmenopausal overweight or obese women were assigned to one of four groups: reduced-calorie diet, exercise, diet + exercise, or control [76]. Participants had mean age ~ 58 years and BMI ~ 30.9 kg/m². Compared to controls, the diet group reduced estrone by 9.6%, the exercise group by 5.5%, and the diet + exercise group by 11.1%. Estradiol declined 16.2% in the diet arm, 4.9% in the exercise arm, and 20.3% in the combined arm. Sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) increased by 22.4% with diet and 25.8% with diet + exercise. Free estradiol dropped 21.4% in the diet group and 26.0% in the combined group. Free testosterone fell 10.0% in the diet arm and 15.6% in the combined arm. Greater magnitudes of weight loss correlated with larger changes in estrogen levels and SHBG [76].

An extensive prospective investigation involving over 4,000 individuals diagnosed with stage 0–III breast cancer evaluated the decadal incidence of osteoporotic fractures and their correlations with soy isoflavone consumption, physical activity, and BMI, categorized by menopausal status. The findings indicated that the 10-year incidence of osteoporotic fractures was 2.9% among pre-/perimenopausal women and 4.4% among postmenopausal women. Elevated soy isoflavone consumption was notably linked to a diminished risk of fractures in pre-/perimenopausal subjects (HR = 0.22), whereas no such association was identified in postmenopausal women. In contrast, physical activity exhibited a protective influence in postmenopausal women, adhering to a dose-response relationship (HR = 0.56 for the highest versus lowest levels of physical activity), with no significant effect modification attributable to menopausal status identified. Notably, being overweight was correlated with an increased risk of fractures in pre-/perimenopausal women (HR = 1.81), while a protective trend was observed in postmenopausal women. The results of this investigation underscored the varying influence of lifestyle determinants on skeletal integrity in individuals who have survived breast cancer, contingent upon their menopausal status, and propose that customized interventions incorporating nutritional and exercise modifications may mitigate the likelihood of fractures in this demographic [77].

A closer examination of the studies included in this section reveals that, while dietary approaches vary in structure, several consistent patterns emerge regarding the composition of effective nutrition interventions. Most studies emphasize diets rich in whole, minimally processed foods, with a strong focus on plant-based components such as fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and legumes, which provide fiber, polyphenols, and essential micronutrients. These dietary patterns are often complemented by healthy fat sources, particularly unsaturated fatty acids (e.g., omega-3s), while limiting saturated fats and refined carbohydrates. In addition, interventions frequently incorporate caloric control or energy balance strategies aimed at reducing adiposity, which appears to be a key mediator of metabolic and hormonal improvements. Some studies also highlight the importance of protein adequacy, particularly when combined with resistance training, to preserve lean mass during weight loss. Importantly, the benefits of these dietary interventions are most pronounced when they are sustained over time and paired with structured exercise, reinforcing that both composition (nutrient quality and balance) and adherence are critical determinants of efficacy. Overall, the evidence suggests that a balanced, anti-inflammatory dietary pattern—rather than a single nutrient-focused approach—forms the foundation of effective functional nutrition strategies in this population.

The body of literature examining integrated interventions that combine functional nutrition with structured exercise provides promising indications of therapeutic benefit, yet it also exhibits important methodological constraints that affect the strength and generalizability of the evidence.

First, study designs are heterogeneous. Many investigations adopt randomized controlled trial designs, but others rely on non‑randomized, quasi‑experimental, or pre–post single‑arm approaches. Non‑random allocation and the absence of appropriate control groups increase the risk of selection and performance bias, making it difficult to attribute observed effects specifically to the combined intervention rather than to confounding factors or secular trends. Even in randomized trials, details of allocation concealment and blinding (of outcome assessors, where feasible) are often incompletely reported, limiting the ability to fully assess internal validity.

Second, there is pronounced variability in the composition and standardization of the functional nutrition component. Interventions differ in the types of nutrients or bioactive compounds employed (e.g., specific functional foods, supplements, or dietary patterns), their dosing, timing relative to exercise, and the degree to which adherence is objectively monitored (e.g., pill counts, dietary records, biomarkers). This heterogeneity hampers direct comparison across studies and complicates dose–response interpretation. In many trials, nutritional adherence is assessed primarily via self‑reported intake, which is prone to recall and social desirability bias.

Third, the exercise protocols themselves are not uniform. Studies vary in mode (aerobic, resistance, or combined training), intensity prescription (fixed vs. individualized), session frequency, and program duration. Some interventions include supervised sessions, while others rely largely on home‑based exercise with minimal monitoring. The level of supervision and the methods used to verify compliance (e.g., attendance logs, heart rate monitors, wearable devices) critically affect the fidelity of the intervention and may partly explain discrepancies in outcomes across trials. Under‑reporting of fidelity and progression schemes (e.g., how intensity was adjusted over time) further limits reproducibility.

Fourth, many studies are characterized by modest sample sizes and limited statistical power. Small cohorts restrict the ability to detect clinically meaningful differences, increase the likelihood of type II errors, and narrow the opportunity for prespecified subgroup analyses (e.g., by sex, age, baseline disease severity, or comorbidities). As a result, conclusions regarding which patient subgroups derive the greatest benefit from integrated functional nutrition and exercise remain tentative.

Fifth, outcome measures and follow‑up periods show considerable diversity. Some studies focus on short‑term physiological markers (e.g., biomarkers, body composition, functional tests), whereas others include patient‑centered outcomes such as quality of life or symptom burden. Follow-up durations are often relatively short, capturing only immediate or near‑term responses to the intervention but not its long‑term sustainability or capacity to modify disease trajectory. Infrequent assessment time points and incomplete reporting of adverse events further constrain the evaluation of safety and durability.

Sixth, participant selection and external validity warrant attention. Many trials recruit relatively homogeneous samples (e.g., specific age ranges, limited comorbidity burden, or high baseline motivation), and individuals with more severe disease, multimorbidity, or low socioeconomic resources are underrepresented. This restricts generalizability to the broader clinical population, in whom adherence to complex combined interventions may be more challenging and who may respond differently to nutritional and exercise stimuli.

Finally, integration and attribution of effects pose a methodological challenge inherent to combined interventions. Because functional nutrition and exercise are delivered concurrently, most studies do not include factorial designs capable of disentangling the independent and interactive effects of each component. Consequently, it is often unclear whether observed benefits reflect a true synergistic interaction or the predominant effect of a single component. Few trials systematically compare the combined approach with each modality alone using adequately powered multi‑arm designs.

Collectively, these methodological considerations suggest that, while the existing literature supports the potential of integrating functional nutrition with exercise as a therapeutic strategy, the certainty of evidence is tempered by design limitations, heterogeneity, and incomplete reporting. Future research would benefit from rigorously conducted randomized controlled trials with clearly described and standardized intervention protocols, robust adherence monitoring, adequately powered sample sizes, longer follow‑up, and designs that allow the respective and synergistic contributions of nutrition and exercise to be disentangled.

Challenges and gaps

Despite growing evidence supporting the role of functional nutrition and exercise in improving metabolic health among postmenopausal breast cancer survivors, several key challenges remain. A major limitation is the heterogeneity of this population. Survivors vary widely in age, treatment history, hormonal status, comorbidities, and medication use, all of which influence metabolic responsiveness to lifestyle interventions. This diversity complicates the interpretation of outcomes and the establishment of standardized recommendations. Another critical gap lies in the scarcity of long-term randomized clinical trials integrating mechanistic biomarkers. Most available studies are short in duration, rely on surrogate endpoints, and rarely include molecular measures such as metabolomic or inflammatory signatures that could clarify biological mechanisms and durability of effects. Adherence also presents a major barrier. Psychosocial stressors, cancer-related fatigue, limited access to supervised programs, and socioeconomic constraints often reduce sustained engagement in diet and exercise interventions. Without addressing behavioral and structural barriers, the translational impact of lifestyle strategies remains limited. Finally, precision approaches are urgently needed. Emerging omics technologies, including metabolomics, microbiome profiling, and nutrigenomics—offer the potential to personalize nutrition and exercise prescriptions based on individual metabolic phenotypes. Integrating these precision tools into survivorship research could enable tailored interventions that optimize metabolic resilience and long-term breast cancer outcomes.

Although the collective evidence supporting the integration of functional nutrition with exercise is promising, the methodological landscape of the current literature presents a mixture of notable strengths and important limitations. On the positive side, many studies employ structured intervention protocols, incorporate objective physiological or functional outcome measures, and increasingly adopt randomized designs that strengthen causal inference. However, these strengths are counterbalanced by considerable heterogeneity in nutritional and exercise components, modest sample sizes, variable adherence monitoring, and generally short follow‑up periods. In addition, incomplete reporting of intervention fidelity and limited use of multi‑arm or factorial designs restrict the ability to disentangle the independent and synergistic effects of each component. Taken together, the reviewed studies provide meaningful early evidence for the therapeutic potential of combined strategies, yet methodological inconsistencies highlight the need for more rigorous, standardized, and adequately powered trials to establish stronger and more generalizable conclusions.

Conclusion

The collective evidence synthesized in this review underscores the profound interconnection between metabolic health, obesity, and breast cancer outcomes in postmenopausal women. Functional nutrition and structured exercise emerge not merely as supportive lifestyle choices but as mechanistically grounded therapeutic strategies capable of modulating key biological pathways implicated in tumor progression, metabolic dysregulation, and endocrine imbalance. Across diverse clinical and experimental studies, weight reduction, whether achieved through dietary restriction, physical training, or their combination, has been consistently linked with favorable alterations in adiposity, insulin sensitivity, inflammatory status, and sex steroid hormone profiles. These changes converge on pathways central to postmenopausal breast cancer risk, including reductions in circulating estradiol and leptin, elevations in sex hormone-binding globulin, and suppression of aromatase activity within adipose tissue. Exercise, particularly when integrated with dietary intervention, exerts additional benefits beyond weight loss, enhancing mitochondrial function, cardiorespiratory fitness, and metabolic flexibility. Such improvements support systemic resilience during and after cancer treatment and may contribute to enhanced therapeutic response and reduced recurrence risk. Nevertheless, studies also demonstrate that these metabolic and hormonal improvements are not invariably durable. Even modest weight regain can reverse favorable biomarker shifts, reinstating a pro-inflammatory, hyperinsulinemic, and estrogen-rich milieu conducive to tumor promotion. These findings emphasize that the sustainability of lifestyle-induced benefits hinges on long-term adherence, behavioral reinforcement, and structured follow-up. Future research must move beyond short-term behavioral interventions toward longitudinal, mechanistically informed clinical trials that integrate multi-omics profiling, precise body composition measures, and molecular biomarker endpoints. Such precision frameworks will allow the identification of responders versus non-responders and the tailoring of interventions to individual metabolic phenotypes. Ultimately, the integration of functional nutrition and exercise into survivorship care holds transformative potential, not only to mitigate metabolic disease burden but also to alter the biological trajectory of postmenopausal breast cancer itself, shifting the paradigm from treatment toward sustained metabolic and oncologic remission.

From a clinical perspective, the evidence reviewed highlights the practical relevance of lifestyle-based interventions in the management and long-term care of postmenopausal breast cancer survivors. Across the studies discussed in Sects. 6 and 7, structured exercise interventions—whether aerobic, resistance, or combined modalities—consistently demonstrate beneficial effects on metabolic and hormonal parameters associated with breast cancer prognosis and overall health. These interventions are associated with reductions in total and visceral adiposity, improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness and lean body mass, and favorable changes in metabolic biomarkers, including lower triglyceride, insulin, and estradiol levels, alongside increases in HDL cholesterol and sex hormone–binding globulin. Importantly, these physiological benefits are frequently accompanied by improvements in quality of life and treatment tolerance, reinforcing the role of structured physical activity as a key component of survivorship care. Although some studies suggest that improvements in physical fitness may be linked to favorable shifts in estrogen metabolite patterns, the available randomized evidence indicates that the protective effects of exercise are more likely mediated through broader mechanisms such as improved insulin sensitivity, beneficial changes in body composition, and modulation of inflammatory and immune pathways. Taken together, these findings support the integration of supervised exercise and complementary lifestyle strategies within multidisciplinary survivorship programs. Nevertheless, given the relatively small sample sizes, short intervention durations, and heterogeneity in intervention protocols observed across studies, further large-scale and longer-term trials are needed to refine optimal intervention strategies and strengthen the translation of these approaches into evidence-based clinical recommendations.

Evidence from studies in TNBC suggests that integrating structured lifestyle interventions alongside standard chemotherapy may contribute to improved treatment outcomes and survivorship indicators. For example, a retrospective cohort study of 110 patients reported reduced relapse rates and improved overall survival over a mean follow-up of 45.6 months, particularly among patients with p53-negative tumors who demonstrated higher disease-free survival compared with p53-positive cases. Similarly, findings from the randomized LEANer trial involving 173 patients with stage I–III breast cancer showed that exercise and nutrition interventions during chemotherapy were associated with improved pathologic complete response rates (53% vs. 28%) and measurable improvements in lifestyle behaviors, although short-term QoL declines during treatment were not significantly mitigated compared with usual care. Collectively, these studies support the potential role of lifestyle modification as a complementary strategy to enhance treatment response; however, because TNBC lies outside the primary scope of the present review, these findings are referenced only to illustrate broader evidence supporting adjunctive lifestyle-based strategies in oncology. Further large-scale, subtype-specific, and mechanistic studies are required to clarify causal relationships, identify responsive patient subgroups, and determine the long-term clinical impact of such interventions.
