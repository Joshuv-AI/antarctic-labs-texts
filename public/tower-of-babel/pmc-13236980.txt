Colorectal cancer incidence associated with dietary patterns in the Brazilian population
Authors: Jonas Eduardo Monteiro dos Santos, Marina Campos Araújo, Cosme Marcelo Furtado Passos da Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInadequate diet is one of the leading risk factors for colorectal cancer. However, the relationship between dietary patterns and colorectal cancer incidence is not conclusive. We aimed to assess the association between dietary patterns and colorectal cancer incidence in Brazilian states, using incidence estimates for the year 2020. This ecological population-based study uses the states of Brazil as analytical units. Food consumption data were obtained from the National Dietary Survey (NDS), a subsample of the Brazilian household budget survey (NDS-HBS) 2008–2009. Colorectal cancer incidence was obtained from the Brazilian National Cancer Institute according to sex. Dietary patterns were derived through factor analysis. Models with quasipoisson probability distributions were applied to associate dietary patterns with colorectal cancer incidence. Five dietary patterns were identified: Traditional Brazilian pattern- rice, beans, and red meat; Western pattern - soft drinks, sweetened beverages, snacks, fast foods, and candies; refined carbohydrate and fat pattern - bread, oils, and fats; healthy pattern – leafy and non-starchy vegetables, fruits, and eggs; and Dairy and beverage pattern - coffee and teas, dairy products, and juices. After considering variables related to nutritional status and lifestyle, colorectal cancer in men was associated with Western Pattern (IRR 1.02; 95% CI 1.01–1.04); among women, associations were significant for Western Pattern (IRR 1.03; 95% CI 1.02–1.04), 3 (IRR 1.05; 95% CI 1.01–1.09), and Healthy Pattern (IRR 1.03; 95% CI 1.01–1.05). Colorectal cancer was associated with certain Brazilian dietary patterns in both men and women. Thus, measures to promote adequate diet/nutrition in the Brazilian population should be strengthened and understood as strategies to prevent and control colorectal cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48929-x.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Dietary pattern, Brazilian household budget survey, Factor analysis, Generalized linear modelsSubject terms: Cancer epidemiology, Cancer prevention, Gastrointestinal cancer

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is among the five most incident cancer worldwide, and in Brazil. In 2022, there were an estimated 1.9 million new cases and an age-standardised incidence rate (ASR) of 19/100,000 inhabitants in the world, and 60,118 new cases (ASR: 19.8/100,000 inhabitants) in Brazil1. The disease will remain a public health concern in the coming decades; by 2030, there will be an 80% increase in incidence (2.2 million new cases) compared to 2008. Eight out of ten CRC cases are related to unhealthy lifestyles, including inadequate diet, high prevalence of physical inactivity, alcohol consumption, smoking, and overweight/obesity2–4.

The Brazilian population has experienced a nutritional transition in recent decades, with reduced sources of dietary fibre such as rice and beans, alongside increased consumption of processed and ultra-processed foods5. Furthermore, increasing prevalence of overweight and decreased physical activity has been observed among Brazilian adults. More than half of Brazilian adults are overweight. At the same time, less than one-third engage in weekly sufficient levels of physical activity6. This epidemiological scenario corroborates projections of an increase in CRC cases in Brazil in the coming years7,8.

Few studies to date have evaluated the Brazilian diet and CRC burden. The existing studies have adopted mortality as the outcome9–11, and did not considered non-dietary risk factors for CRC such as overweight, smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical inactivity. Despites the few Brazilian studies that have examined dietary patterns and colorectal cancer10,11, international evidence supports our rationale. The World Cancer Research Fund/AICR Continuous Update Project concluded that diet, nutrition, and physical activity are strongly related to CRC risk4,12. The European Code Against Cancer also highlights dietary risk factors for cancer prevention13. Systematic reviews of cohort studies confirm associations between Western-type dietary patterns, high in red/processed meats and refined foods, and increased CRC risk14. Furthermore, the dietary pattern approach has been recognized as a robust framework in nutritional epidemiology15.

However, evidence on the relationship between CRC and dietary patterns remains limited in the Brazilian context4. We, thus, aimed to assess whether colorectal cancer incidence is associated with Brazilian dietary patterns, considering the above-mentioned non-dietary factors related to the natural history of CRC.

Results

For both women and men, the highest CRC burden was concentrated in the South and Southeast regions, particularly São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro, and Rio Grande do Sul. Risk factor prevalence varied geographically: overweight and physical inactivity were consistently high, while smoking and alcohol consumption were more prevalent among men. Access to healthcare resources, such as oncologists and endoscopy units, was also unequally distributed, with better availability in more developed regions. These findings highlight marked regional and sex-specific differences in both lifestyle risk factors and healthcare infrastructure (Supplementary Tables 1B, 2 A, and 2B).

Selected foods from NDS 2008–2009 were allocated into 15 groups (Chart 1). Five dietary patterns were retained, explaining 43% of dietary variability (Table 1). Food groups with factor loadings equal to or greater than ≥ 0.30 in absolute value for each dietary pattern were: Traditional Brazilian Pattern– rice, beans, and red meat; Western Pattern – soft drinks, sweetened beverages, snacks, fast foods, and candies; Refined Carbohydrate and Fat Pattern – bread, oils, and fats; Healthy Pattern – leafy and non-starchy vegetables, fruits, and eggs; Dairy and Beverage Pattern – coffees and teas, dairy products, and juices. Healthy Pattern showed negative factor loading for the eggs group (-0.37) and Dairy and Beverage Pattern for the teas and coffees group (-0.71).

Discussion

The current study identified five dietary patterns based on foods consumed by a probabilistic sample of the Brazilian population. These dietary patterns showed significant ecological associations with CRC incidence in Brazilians. Three dietary patterns remained associated with CRC in women, while only one dietary pattern was associated with CRC in men after adjustment for nutritional status and lifestyle characteristics. In these models, the associations indicated that increased median scores for consumption of dietary patterns consisting of soft drinks, sweetened beverages, snacks, fast food, and candies (Western Pattern) were associated with increased CRC incidence rates in both sexes.

In contrast, increased median scores for two dietary patterns consisting of bread, oils, and fats (Refined Carbohydrate and Fat Pattern) and leafy and non-starchy vegetables, fruits, and eggs (Healthy Pattern) were associated with higher CRC incidence in Brazilian women.

No association was found in either men or women between any dietary pattern and CRC after adjustment for socio-demographic characteristics and adjustment for variables related to access to cancer diagnosis and treatment. A possible explanation for the absence of statistically significant associations may be related to the multicollinearity verified between dietary patterns and socio-demographic characteristics; the inclusion of these variables may have caused overfitting of the adjusted models and led to the loss of statistical significance; considering dietary patterns and socio-demographic characteristics epidemiologically relevant to colorectal carcinogenesis, we maintained both variables in the adjusted model. Dietary patterns, socio-demographic characteristics, or access to diagnosis and treatment, although representing distinct aspects of the natural history of CRC, may present multicollinearity in an ecological study because they represent the same inequality construct underlying the process of CRC carcinogenesis.

The associations observed in this study are consistent with findings from Latin America and other regions worldwide. Research conducted in Latin America countries has similarly reported positive associations between dietary patterns rich in refined carbohydrates, sugars, and fats, often described as Western-type patterns, and colorectal cancer risk4,13,16. These results mirror evidence from North American and European cohorts, where such dietary profiles are linked to excess body weight, abdominal adiposity, and inflammatory pathways involved in colorectal carcinogenesis12,14. Conversely, studies from Mediterranean and some Asian populations, where dietary patterns include higher intakes of fruits, vegetables, legumes, and whole grains, have shown protective effects against CRC13,17–19. By situating our ecological findings within this broader international landscape, our results reinforce the global relevance of dietary patterns as determinants of colorectal cancer risk.

Meanwhile, some of our results differed from those of previous studies. While previous studies have demonstrated the protective effect of eating fruits, vegetables, legumes, and poultry, our study found that these patterns were positively associated with CRC incidence. However, our findings should be interpreted with the utmost caution since this was an ecologically designed study, where the effect observed at the aggregate level may differ from that potentially seen at the individual level.

The scope of this study involved comparing the median score of dietary patterns to CRC incidence in Brazil´s states according to sex. An unexpected finding was the positive association between the vegetable–fruit-rich pattern and CRC incidence in women. At the ecological level, this association may reflect broader dynamics beyond individual diet quality. Brazilian states with higher consumption of vegetables and fruits are also major producers, where intensive agricultural systems involve greater exposure to pesticides and other chemical agents that have been linked to cancer risk. In addition, these states generally have better diagnostic infrastructure and higher screening coverage, which may lead to greater detection of CRC cases. These ecological mechanisms could partly explain the observed positive association.

Socioeconomic and demographic differences across Brazilian states likely contributed to the variation observed in the associations between dietary patterns and CRC incidence. Per capita consumption of rice, beans, beef, and whole milk was higher in the Central-West region. In comparison, the consumption of potatoes and yoghurt was higher in the wealthier Southeast and South regions. Milk-based preparations (e.g., porridge) and fresh fish were consumed more in the North of Brazil. The survey also showed that food choices were influenced by income. Per capita consumption of rice and beans was higher among the lower two-fourths of the population by income; consumption of corn was three times higher among the poorest Brazilians compared to the wealthiest. Meanwhile, consumption of fruits (bananas and oranges), vegetables, raw salads, and low-fat cheeses was higher among higher-income strata. Contradictorily, the per capita consumption of unhealthy foods such as sweets, soft drinks, pizzas, and snacks was higher among higher-income Brazilians20,21.

Reflecting the impacts of socioeconomic and demographic disparities on the Brazilian population´s eating habits, the highest CRC incidence rates were also found in the wealthiest and most economically developed regions of Brazil (South, Southeast, and Central-West)22,23. Such areas have better access to early diagnosis and treatment and better health services, possibly reflected in the higher number of cases identified10. In this sense, we believe that the adjustment for variables related to access to diagnosis and treatment maintained the absence of the associations observed in the present study as a result of the multicollinearity observed between higher dietary scores and better access to adequate diagnostic and treatment services, represented by the availability high-complexity cancer care units and centres (UNACONS and CACONS). The results also show that improved conditions for screening, early diagnosis and treatment initiated in the initial stages of the disease, and investment in new technologies should implemented in the poorest regions of the country.

Finally, projections indicate that colorectal cancer incidence will increase in the coming decades, and that CRC will continue among the neoplasms with the highest burden in the Brazilian population and the world24. Even as CRC incidence increases, unhealthy diet behaviours continue to grow in Brazil. Compared to the HBS 2008–2009, the HBS 2017–2018 showed a decline in the consumption of rice and beans, staple foods in Brazilians´ diet, especially in the higher income strata5. The frequency of fruit consumption has also decreased, affecting lower-income Brazilians5. There was also a significant increase in the consumption of sandwiches in the two highest income quartiles (83% and 51%, respectively)5. In this context, lifestyle Westernisation should be seen as a significant public health challenge. Therefore, it is necessary to strengthen and disseminate strategies and public policies to promote healthy lifestyles and healthy eating habits, ensuring the right of all individuals to adequate food, food re-education, and income. It is also necessary to inform the population about the risk factors for CRC, since the issue has received insufficient attention in Brazilian society. Most of the population are unaware of the risk factors for CRC. Awareness-raising on this health problem could result in lower exposure to risk factors for CRC over the coming decades, culminating in decreasing prevalence rates of overweight and physical inactivity. A reduction in social inequalities between the regions of Brazil and investment in innovative technologies for cancer control should also provide greater access to early diagnosis and treatment, reducing the high incidence and mortality rates in countries undergoing economic development.

The main limitation of the current study is its ecological design, which does not allow estimating the magnitude of the association between food intake and CRC at the individual level. Brazilian national surveys have made it possible to accurately assess personal food consumption; however, the same surveys do not provide data on chronic diseases such as cancer. Furthermore, Brazil does not have a national database in which data on dietary intake and cancer incidence can be obtained at the individual level. Another limitation refers to national cancer incidence data in Brazil obtained through estimates, since the national coverage of Population-Based Cancer Registries is still low and depends on the quality of secondary data sources such as the Ministry of Health’s Mortality Information System23. We thus believe that these estimates of CRC incidence may be underestimated. Another limitation refers to inference of the results for the Brazilian population: the HBS 2008–2008 cluster sample design only allows inferences at the major geographic regional level and urban versus rural areas20. Thus, it is not advisable to extrapolate the study´s finding to the Brazilian population as a whole, since the analysis used states as the units of observation.

However, our study has several strengths: (i) the inclusion of covariates reported in the literature as important in CRC carcinogenesis; (ii) consideration of data from national surveys with accurate methods; (iii) use of food consumption data obtained from an available qualitative and quantitative instrument (24-hour recall), which allowed us to estimate the average consumption of all food groups consumed by the Brazilian population; and (iv) use of the methodology described in the literature to measure dietary patterns and their association with CRC.

This study contributes epidemiological evidence by showing how population-level dietary patterns are associated with colorectal cancer incidence in Brazil. By stratifying results by sex, we captured relevant differences in both cancer burden and dietary habits. These findings reinforce the role of diet in CRC prevention and provide useful insights for guiding public health strategies and future analytical research. Thus, measures to promote adequate diet in the population should be strengthened and seen as primary strategies to prevent colorectal carcinogenesis.

Method

Study design

We used secondary data to ecologically associate dietary patterns and CRC incidence in Brazilian adults. The 26 states of Brazil and the Federal District were used as units of analysis. The study was conducted in two steps. First, individual-level data from the National Dietary Survey and the National Health Survey were used to derive dietary patterns and to estimate the prevalence of other risk factors (alcohol consumption, smoking, physical inactivity, and overweight). In the second step, colorectal cancer incidence data were obtained at the level of the Brazilian federation units. The individual-level results were then aggregated to match the ecological level of cancer incidence, allowing for state-level analyses.

The number of incident cases of CRC was the outcome, and median dietary pattern scores were used as the primary exposure. Non-dietary variables related to colorectal carcinogenesis were taken into consideration to describe the complexity of the CRC process. The target non-dietary variables were: prevalence of physical inactivity, alcohol consumption, smoking, and overweight; median per capita household income and a variable expressing access to diagnosis and treatment, namely the rate per 500 thousand inhabitants of the number of High-Complexity Cancer Care Units (UNACONS) and High-Complexity Cancer Care Centres (CACONS) in the year 2020 (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Prevalence of physical inactivity (defined as not reaching the minimum recommended 150 min of moderate or 75 min of vigorous physical activity per week); smoking (defined as current tobacco use (daily or occasional); and alcohol consumption (defined as the intake of alcoholic beverages at least once in the 30 days prior to the interview) were obtained from the National Health Survey (NHS) 2013, according to state of Brazil and sex25. Prevalence of overweight, or body mass index (BMI) over 25Kg/m2, by sex and state of Brazil was obtained from the Household Budget Survey (HBS) 2008–200920. The proportion of individuals with income equal to or less than half the per capita minimum wage by sex and state of Brazil was estimated from the HBS 2008–2009. In 2008, the average value of a basic food basket corresponded to half the minimum wage26. Thus, the income variable was assumed to be a proxy for food security, that is, the proportion of the Brazilian population that could afford a basic food basket in 2008 (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

To reflect access to diagnosis and treatment of CRC in Brazil, we obtained the number of High-Complexity Cancer Care Units (UNACONS) and High-Complexity Cancer Care Centres (CACONS). We calculated the rate per 500,000 inhabitants, following the criteria established by the Brazilian Ministry of Health27. The data were extracted from the National Registry of Healthcare Establishments (CNES). The resident population for the rate´s denominator was obtained from the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE)28(Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

New cases of CRC for the year 2020 were obtained from Brazilian National Cancer Institute (INCA) estimates, according to sex and state. Colorectal cancer incidence was defined according to ICD-10 codes C18–C21 (colon, rectosigmoid junction, and rectum). Details on the methodology used by INCA to estimate the number of cancer cases have been published previously23. The other data were obtained from individual levels and converted to aggregate levels from national surveys: the 2008–2009 HBS and the 2013 NHS20,25.

Food consumption assessment

Food consumption data were obtained from the National Dietary Survey (NDS), a subsample (24%) of the HBS conducted by the IBGE in 2008–200920. Although causality was beyond the study´s scope, the choice of NDS 2008–2009 aimed to preserve the criterion of temporal association between exposure (diet) and the target outcome (CRC incidence in 2020), since onset of the disease depends on prolonged exposure to the risk factor.

Two non-consecutive 24-hour recalls obtained 34,003 individuals food consumption data within a week. Participants self-reported foods and beverages consumed over the previous 24 h, informing ingredients, place, time, and portion size20. The amounts of food reported by respondents in household measures (e.g., cups, spoons) were transformed into grams or millilitres, using a standardized table of reference measures for food consumed in Brazil20. The participants reported 1,971 foods. Initially, 1,417 foods (72%) were allocated into 24 groups, following two criteria: (i) foods with similar nutritional characteristics (e.g., fruit, cereals, pasta); (ii) foods bearing either a positive or negative relationship to the natural history of CRC according to the literature (alcoholic beverages, red meat, processed red meat-based foods, vegetables rich in dietary fibre, fish, dairy products, and cheese)3,12.

Before allocating foods to the groups, we performed a descriptive analysis to verify the prevalence of each food´s consumption. Therefore, foods unrelated to any group and consumed by fewer than one hundred participants were not included in these analyses to avoid zero inflation and potential outliers. We then applied the Multiple Source Method (MSM) to correct the intake distribution for intrapersonal variability, to estimate a distribution close to each usual individual intake. This method first estimates the probability of consuming a given food group on a random day and then combines it with the amount consumed to reconstruct the distribution of usual intake. By doing so, the MSM generates both population-level and individual-level estimates of habitual consumption, minimizing random error inherent in short-term recalls. This approach has been widely recommended for large-scale dietary surveys using 24-hour recalls, as it provides more stable and accurate estimates of long-term dietary exposure.

Dietary pattern assessment

Factor Analysis (FA) was used to reduce the number of food groups. To consider the NDS complex design, we performed FA in two steps. First, we constructed a correlation matrix applying the PROC GLM command in the Statistical Analysis System (SAS); in this step, we included the sample design variables; Second, the FACTOR PROCEDURE command estimated the dietary patterns from the correlation matrix29,30. Finally, Varimax orthogonal rotation obtained dietary patterns with independent structures and was easier to interpret30,31.

The number of dietary patterns retained in this process met the criteria of eigenvalue > 1.032,33. Lastly, food groups with factor loadings equal to or greater than 0.30 in absolute value (|λ| ≥ 0.30) were retained in the patterns, following previous literature, so that both positive and negative loadings were considered relevant. We adopted this more conservative cut-off to allow the inclusion of a greater number of food groups, thereby better capturing the complexity of the Brazilian diet and increasing the proportion of dietary variability explained by the retained factors.

We calculated the individual factor scores for each dietary pattern. First, we multiplied the food consumed in each group by the standardised score corresponding to each dietary pattern. Then, we added these scores, resulting in the final individual score. Thus, all food groups were represented in all dietary patterns, but with different weights, since the standardised score for the same food group differed according to dietary pattern. Subsequently, median scores for each dietary pattern were calculated according to state of Brazil and sex. This strategy was adopted to obtain the consumption data at the same level of aggregation as the CRC incidence data (ecological step of the study). Median scores were only calculated for individuals over 20 years of age, considering that CRC incidence is low in younger age groups and is related to genetic factors and family history of the disease34. Finally, dietary patterns were named according to the predominant characteristics of the food groups that defined each pattern.

Statistical analysis

Generalised Linear Regression Models with Quasipoisson probability distribution were used to assess the association between the number of new cases of CRC and the scores of dietary patterns identified by factor analysis. The choice of the model’s probability distribution (Quasipoisson) was based on the need to accommodate the data´s overdispersion, resulting from the event´s characteristics rarity (low incidence of CRC). Variables were included in the models following a hierarchical framework based on the natural history of colorectal cancer. We first tested models with dietary patterns only, then added nutritional status and individual risk factors (smoking, alcohol consumption, physical inactivity), and finally incorporated contextual variables (household income and access to diagnostic and treatment services) to capture geographic differences across states.

Since these were modelling rates, we considered the logarithm of the population as an offset term of the models. We used the IBGE intercensal population estimates for 202028.

The covariables included in the adjusted models were selected from bivariate models based on p-values less than 20% (p < 0.20). However, when a variable had p-value greater than 20% but was aetiologically related to colorectal carcinogenesis, it was maintained in the adjusted models. The models´ regression coefficients (β) were exponentiated. The values represented the Incidence Rate Ratio (IRR), meaning the expected change in CRC incidence rate resulting from an increase of one in the dietary pattern score.

Analyses were performed in the free R software, version 3.6.3. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses in the adjusted models. Exponentiations of the regression coefficients for IRR and their respective confidence intervals were obtained with the jtools statistical package, version 2.1.435.
