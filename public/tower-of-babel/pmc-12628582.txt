A CT-based decision tree model for differentiating sub-3 cm gastric ectopic pancreas from gastrointestinal stromal tumors
Authors: Jiaqi Duan, Yunzhi Zhao, Qihao Shi, Yining Wang, Xiaoshan Huang, Qinpan Rao, Shufeng Fan, Jianxia Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo develop a CT-based decision tree model integrating clinical and imaging features for the preoperative differentiation of gastric ectopic pancreas (GEPs) and gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) with a maximum diameter of less than 3 cm. MethodsThis retrospective study included 86 patients with pathologically confirmed GEPs (n = 26) and GISTs (n = 60), all with lesions smaller than 3 cm. Clinical information and CT features were collected. The dataset was divided into training and testing sets. A decision tree classification model was constructed using key variables selected from the training set via univariate analyses and logistic regression. The decision tree's hyperparameters were optimised using five-fold cross-validation. Diagnostic performance was evaluated on an independent test set, including plotting ROC curves to calculate AUC values, sensitivity, and specificity, alongside using calibration curves to assess goodness-of-fit. Furthermore, the SHAP method was employed to provide visual explanations for the final model's predictions.ResultsThe decision tree model identified four key variables: age (clinical factor) and three CT features: ratio of lesion-to-pancreas attenuation in the arterial phase(A2), lesion long-to-short diameter ratio (LD/SD ratio), and intralesional low attenuation (ILA). The model, based on these four features, achieved an AUC of 0.744(95% CI:0.589–0.950), with sensitivity of 76.9% and specificity of 84.6%. Concurrently, calibration analysis substantiated the model's exceptional predictive precision. The Brier score (0.0648) and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (χ2 = 5.365, df = 8, P = 0.718) both demonstrated a high degree of agreement between the model's predicted probabilities and the actual observed values.ConclusionsThe CT-based decision tree model, integrating four clinical and CT features, provides a reliable and visualized tool for differentiating GEPs from GISTs with a maximum diameter of less than 3 cm, demonstrating strong diagnostic performance.

Introduction

Gastric ectopic pancreas (GEPs) is a congenital developmental anomaly arising from ectopic migration of pancreatic precursor cells during embryogenesis [1, 2]. It is defined as pancreatic tissue located outside the normal anatomical pancreas, possessing acinar and ductal structures, but lacking anatomical or vascular continuity with the pancreas itself [3]. Clinically, GEPs typically present as incidental submucosal lesions of the gastric wall, most commonly located in the antrum and body [4]. The detection rate ranges from 0.5% to 13.7% in autopsy studies, however, the rate of clinical diagnosis is significantly lower [5–7]. Most GEPs are asymptomatic, slow-growing, and benign with extremely low malignant potential, and many patients may opt for a strategy of conservative surveillance [8–10].

In contrast, gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) are the most common submucosal tumors of the stomach, originating from the interstitial cells of Cajal and accounting for over 80% of gastric mesenchymal tumors [11]. GISTs display heterogeneous biological behavior, with approximately 10%–30% demonstrating invasive or metastatic potential, thus warranting early surgical intervention [12]. However, both GEPs and GISTs lack specific clinical manifestations, often presenting with nonspecific gastrointestinal discomfort, occasional bleeding, or being incidentally detected during routine examinations [13–15]. Particularly in lesions smaller than 3 cm, their computed tomography (CT) features often overlap—both can appear as well-demarcated, submucosal, round or ovoid soft-tissue masses with mild to moderate enhancement, making preoperative diagnosis challenging [16].

Currently, definitive diagnosis relies on postoperative histopathology and immunohistochemistry. GEPs typically express pancreatic enzymes, cytokeratin (CK), and periodic acid–Schiff (PAS), while GISTs are commonly positive for CD117 (KIT) and DOG-1 [17]. However, in the absence of effective preoperative noninvasive diagnostic tools, some benign GEPs may be misdiagnosed as GISTs and undergo unnecessary surgery, while low-risk GISTs may experience delayed treatment [18]. Therefore, developing a simple, visual, and operable preoperative diagnostic tool based on imaging features is of significant clinical value.

CT remains the preferred imaging modality for evaluating gastric lesions, offering high spatial resolution, fast acquisition, and excellent image reconstruction capabilities [19]. It is widely used in the anatomic evaluation and enhancement pattern analysis of submucosal gastric lesions [20, 21]. Several studies have attempted to describe the distinguishing CT findings of GEPs and GISTs, including lesion location, attenuation, enhancement pattern, and morphological features [1, 22, 23]. However, most findings are descriptive, and systematic integration of quantitative indicators and standardized diagnostic models is still lacking. In recent years, explainable machine learning models such as decision trees have gained increasing application in medical imaging due to their transparent structure and logical reasoning process. These models are especially suitable for studies with small to medium sample sizes and offer new strategies for clinical decision-making support.

Therefore, this study aimed to develop a CT-based decision tree model incorporating clinical and contrast-enhanced imaging features to distinguish GEPs from GISTs with a maximum diameter of less than 3 cm. The goal was to identify key discriminative imaging variables and validate the diagnostic performance of this visual and user-friendly model, thereby providing a practical preoperative decision-support tool to reduce unnecessary surgical intervention and improve diagnostic accuracy and efficiency.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective study included and analyzed patients diagnosed and treated between January 2014 and December 2024 at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Hospital 1) and the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University (Hospital 2). All lesions were pathologically confirmed by surgical resection or biopsy. The inclusion criteria (Fig. 1) was as follows: (a) patient with histologically confirmed GEPs or GISTs; (b) patient who underwent contrast-enhanced CT scanning including the pancreas before surgery; (c) lesion or lesion center located within the gastric wall; (d) no pancreatic-related treatment prior to imaging; and (e) complete and reliable clinical and imaging data. Exclusion criteria was:(a) lesion located anatomically beyond the gastric wall (e.g., duodenal or peripancreatic region), while completely exophytic lesions arising from the gastric wall were included in our study; (b) poor-quality images unsuitable for analysis; (c) incomplete clinical data; (d) tumor larger than 3 cm in maximum diameter.Fig. 1Flow chart of the study based on pathological criteria

CT protocol

In Hospital 1, abdominal contrast-enhanced CT scans were performed using a multidetector-row CT scanner (SOMATOM Definition Flash, Siemens Healthcare, Germany). In Hospital 2, imaging was conducted using either a LightSpeed VCT or Optima 540 scanner (GE Healthcare, USA). All patients fasted for at least 6 h before the scan. Ten minutes prior to imaging, 10 mg of anisodamine was administered intramuscularly to reduce gastrointestinal peristalsis, followed by oral intake of 800–1000 mL of water to achieve gastric distension. Scans were performed in the supine position, covering the area from the diaphragmatic dome to the pubic symphysis.

CT acquisition parameters were standardized as follows: 120 kVp tube voltage, 200 mAs tube current, 5 mm slice thickness and interval, and 0.6 pitch. Iodinated contrast agents used included Ultravist (Bayer, Germany), Omnipaque (GE Pharmaceuticals (Shanghai), China), Optiray (Liebel-Flarsheim, Canada), and Iohexol (Beijing Beilu Pharmaceuticals, China). A total of 100 mL contrast agent was injected via an antecubital vein using a power injector at 5 mL/s. All patients underwent triphasic contrast-enhanced CT, including unenhanced, arterial, and portal venous phases. Arterial- and portal venous-phase images were obtained approximately 23–25 s and 50–60 s after contrast injection, respectively.

Clinical and imaging analysis

Two abdominal radiologists (with 13 and 31 years of experience in abdominal radiology, respectively) independently and retrospectively reviewed all CT images in a blinded fashion, without knowledge of the patients’ clinical or histopathological results. In cases of disagreement, a consensus was reached through discussion. General clinical data (age, sex, clinical symptoms) and imaging features were recorded. The CT imaging features included:①Location (cardia, fundus, body, and antrum); ②Growth pattern (intraluminal, extraluminal, mixed);③Contour (round, ovoid, hill-like, flat, irregular);④Micro lobulated (Yes or No); ⑤Calcification (Yes or No); ⑥Peritumoral hypodensity line (Yes or No); ⑦ maximum diameter (maximum length diameter); ⑧Short diameter (maximum short diameter); ⑨Long-to-short diameter ratio(LD/SD ratio); ⑩Hyperenhancement of the overlying mucosa(compared with the adjacent normalmucosa)(Yes or No); ⑪CT attenuation values of the pancreas and tumors in the plain, arterial, and portal venous phases (HU,P1/A1/V1); ⑫The arterial phase to pre-contrast attenuation difference was measured as arterial phase minus pre-contrast attenuation (HU, DEAP);⑬The CT attenuation value of portal venous phase minus unenhancement phase (DEVP);⑭Enhancement ratio: (HU portal venous − HU plain/HU plain);⑮Ratio of CT values of lesions to pancreas in the plain, arterial, and portal venous phases (HU lesion/pancreas, P2/A2/V2); ⑯Degree of enhancement (compared to plain: mild < 20 HU, moderate 20–40 HU, marked > 40 HU); ⑰Enhancement pattern (uniformity of the enhancement) (homogeneous or heterogeneous); ⑱Intralesional low attenuation (ILA) (Yes or No).

Hyperenhancement of the overlying mucosa was defined as an intensity-based finding: on arterial or portal-venous phase images, the mucosal layer directly covering the submucosal lesion exhibited visually greater enhancement than the adjacent normal gastric mucosa on the same slice and phase. Degree of enhancement and ROI measurement: Manually drawn circular or oval regions of interest (ROIs) were placed on axial CT images to measure the mean attenuation (in Hounsfield units, HU) of both the pancreas and the lesion on unenhanced, arterial, and portal-venous phase images. ROIs were drawn over the solid, visibly enhancing portion of the lesion, carefully avoiding calcification, hemorrhage, necrosis, cystic change, intratumoral or adjacent vessels, and imaging artifacts. For pancreatic measurements, the ROI was consistently placed at the junction of the body and tail. Each measurement was performed three times on consecutive slices, and the mean value was recorded to ensure reproducibility and minimize random error. The degree of enhancement was quantitatively judged by the difference between the post-enhancement CT attenuation value and the non-enhanced CT attenuation value. If the difference was < 20 HU, the tumor was considered to exhibit a mild enhancement pattern; 20–40 HU was considered to represent a moderate enhancement pattern, and > 40 HU was judged to be a marked enhancement pattern. Peritumoral hypodensity line was defined as a smooth, linear, hypoattenuating interface between the lesion and surrounding gastric tissue. ILA was defined as a region within the lesion demonstrating relatively lower attenuation than adjacent solid tissue on any of the unenhanced, arterial, or portal-venous phase images. Each quantitative feature was measured three times, and the average value was recorded for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Python version 3.13.3 and SPSS version 27. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed and compared using the independent samples t-test; otherwise, they were reported as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test.

The entire dataset was randomly divided into a training set and a testing set at a ratio of 7:3, with the objective of ensuring that the distribution of GEPs and GISTs in both sets closely matched that of the original cohort. The training set comprised 42 GISTs and 18 GEPs, while the testing set contained 18 GISTs and 8 GEPs. On the training set, univariate analysis was first performed on all candidate variables in order to compare differences between GEPs and GISTs groups. Variables with P-values < 0.05 in univariate analysis were included in multivariate logistic regression in order to identify independent predictors. Given the limited overall sample size and small number of target lesions, confidence intervals may appear overly broad and unstable due to sample fluctuations. In order to obtain more robust interval estimates for key predictive indicators, the internal validation method was employed using the Bootstrap technique. Consequently, decision tree classification models were constructed using the key variables selected from the training set. In order to optimise the performance of the model and prevent overfitting, the optimal hyperparameters for the decision trees were determined through a combination of grid search and five-fold cross-validation. Key parameters were optimised on the training set via five-fold cross-validation.

Model performance was assessed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, including the area under the curve (AUC), 95% confidence interval (CI), sensitivity, and specificity. Calibration curves were plotted to evaluate the agreement between predicted and observed probabilities. Additionally, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were computed to interpret the relative contribution of each variable to the model's prediction of GEPs.

Decision tree model development

The optimal hyperparameters for the decision tree were determined by identifying four independent predictors through logistic regression, and then applying grid search optimisation to determine the optimal values for these parameters. The optimal values for these parameters are as follows: maximum tree depth = 4, minimum number of samples per internal node = 2, and minimum number of samples per leaf node = 1 (Fig. 6A). The model incorporated the following variables in a hierarchical structure: A2, ILA, LD/SD ratio, and Age. Within the training set, the robustness of the model was evaluated using the aforementioned fivefold cross-validation. The area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity for each fold were recorded, and their mean and standard deviation were subsequently calculated. (Table 4). Model validation and performance

The decision tree model was validated on the independent testing set. ROC analysis revealed an AUC of 0.744(95% CI:0.589–0.950), with a sensitivity of 76.9% and specificity of 84.6%, indicating strong discriminatory performance (Fig. 7). Discussion

This multicenter study compared the clinical and CT imaging features of GEPs and GISTs with a diameter of less than 3 cm. By integrating four key variables—ILA, A2, LD/SD ratio, and patient age—we constructed a decision tree model with excellent interpretability and visualization capabilities. The model demonstrated outstanding discriminatory power and calibration performance, offering a novel, user-friendly, and explainable tool for preoperative differential diagnosis.

GEPs and small GISTs exhibit highly overlapping imaging features, presenting a significant challenge for clinical diagnosis [16]. However, the two entities differ fundamentally in management strategies: GEPs are generally benign and may be managed conservatively with observation, while GISTs have malignant potential, and even early-stage tumors are often recommended for surgical resection [18]. Accurate differentiation between GEPs and GISTs is thus essential. Traditional imaging indicators, such as lesion location, size, and enhancement patterns, are often insufficient in sub-3 cm lesions, warranting more targeted discriminative tools. Our study addresses this diagnostic gap by constructing a structured decision model incorporating clinical and CT features.

The constructed decision tree model not only displayed excellent classification ability but also provided several methodological advantages. As an interpretable machine learning algorithm widely applied in medical imaging, decision trees are characterized by clear structure, logical transparency, and clinical ease of use [24]. Unlike traditional logistic regression models, decision trees utilize the Gini index to identify features with the highest information gain, forming a hierarchical decision path that enhances clinical usability [25]. In this study, A2 was used as the top-level node, reflecting its core diagnostic role, followed by ILA, LD/SD ratio, and age as subsequent nodes, forming a logically coherent imaging framework. To enhance the interpretability and clinical reliability of the model, we employed SHAP values to explain features contributions, as they can quantify the impact of each variable on model outputs and visualizes the decision-making mechanism, thereby enhancing clinician understanding and confidence in the model. This also lays a technical foundation for achieving precision imaging diagnosis.

Among all variables, A2 was the most discriminative feature. Previous studies have shown that GEPs usually demonstrate moderate arterial phase enhancement, lower than that of normal pancreas but higher than GISTs, likely due to their unique microcirculatory structures [26]. Although GEPs retain acinar and ductal components, their arterial supply derives from local small arteries with venous drainage directly into the portal system, resulting in lower arterial perfusion [27, 28]. These findings are consistent with the studies by Lu et al. [29] and Li et al. [16],emphasizing the importance of quantitatively evaluating this ratio in standard imaging interpretations, particularly for small lesions.

ILA was the second most important variable in the model. Given that conventional signs such as the "central duct sign" are difficult to detect in small lesions due to resolution limitations, we defined all internal low-density features (e.g., ductal dilatation, cystic changes, mucinous areas) as "low attenuation,” improving detection sensitivity and interpretation consistency.

In terms of morphology, the LD/SD ratio also showed significant discriminatory value. Although previous studies suggested a threshold of 1.4 [2, 30, 31], our study identified 1.2 as more clinically meaningful in lesions < 3 cm. This may be due to the near-round or mildly oval shape of small lesions and the partial volume effect caused by CT slice thickness, reducing apparent diameter differences. Hence, 1.2 is a more appropriate threshold for small tumors.

Age, as a clinical ancillary factor, was also included in the model. Consistent with previous findings, our study found that GEPs predominantly occur in younger patients, while GISTs are more common in older individuals [10]. Thus, in younger patients with relatively hypoattenuating, to ovoid lesions and intralesional low attenuation, GEPs should be highly suspected.

Interestingly, although previous literature identified the “peritumoral low-density line” as a characteristic finding of GEPs with high specificity [32, 33], our study observed a low detection rate for this feature. We speculate that this may be attributed to the small lesion size in our cohort, insufficient fibrous capsule formation, and limited mucin secretion, restricting the visibility of this sign and excluding it from the final model.

However, this study has several limitations.First, as a retrospective study, potential selection bias could not be completely avoided.Second, differences in CT scanners and acquisition parameters across centers may have affected image consistency. Minor variations in CT acquisition techniques and contrast bolus-timing might also have influenced enhancement uniformity and quantitative feature evaluation; these factors should be standardized in future multicenter studies.Third, this study focused on noninvasive, imaging-based differentiation between gastric ectopic pancreas and gastrointestinal stromal tumors. In future research, serological tumor markers (such as serum amylase and CA19-9) could be incorporated as complementary indicators to further enhance diagnostic performance.Finally, some decision nodes contained a limited number of cases, which may reduce the statistical robustness of our model. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate the model’s stability and generalizability. In addition, the current model was developed specifically for gastric ectopic pancreas, and its applicability to ectopic pancreatic tissue in other gastrointestinal sites (such as the duodenum or jejunum) warrants further investigation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, by integrating four key features—age (clinical factor), ILA, A2, and LD/SD ratio (imaging features)—we developed a visualized decision tree model specifically designed to differentiate gastric ectopic pancreas from gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs). This model, based on combined clinical and imaging findings, demonstrated robust discriminatory power, interpretability, and clinical applicability, providing a practical noninvasive tool for preoperative diagnosis and optimized patient management.
