Longitudinal strain in left ventricular non-compaction: insights derived from two-dimensional speckle-tracking echocardiography and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging strain
Authors: Mohammadbagher Sharifkazemi, Zahra Hooshanginezhad, Leila Ahmadi, Marjan Jaladat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeft ventricular non-compaction (LVNC) is a heterogeneous cardiomyopathic phenotype, often associated with impaired systolic function. Advanced imaging modalities such as cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) and speckle-tracking echocardiography (STE) allow detailed assessment of myocardial structure and deformation, yet the patterns of global and segmental longitudinal strain (LS) in LVNC remain insufficiently defined. This study aimed to evaluate global and regional LS in patients with CMR-confirmed LVNC using two-dimensional STE and CMR feature-tracking, and to assess the consistency between these two modalities.MethodsThirty-five adults with CMR-confirmed LVNC referred to the Cardiovascular Centers of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (March 2022–December 2023) were retrospectively analyzed. All patients underwent transthoracic echocardiography with LS assessment by 2D-STE, and CMR-derived LS was obtained when image quality permitted. Demographic, clinical, and imaging parameters were recorded. Non-parametric statistical tests were used with correction for multiple comparisons.ResultsThe mean age was 43.3 ± 14.3 years; 71.4% were men. CMR most frequently identified non-compaction in the lateral (91.4%) and apical (51.4%) segments. Global LS was reduced in all patients. STE demonstrated significantly greater LS impairment in apical segments compared with basal segments (P < 0.001; mid vs. basal P = 0.004; apical vs. mid P = 0.017). Patients with a higher number of non-compacted segments exhibited greater LS reduction. In the subset with analyzable CMR strain data, LS values in the apical and mid-ventricular regions were comparable between CMR and STE (P > 0.05), whereas basal strain values differed significantly between modalities (P = 0.003).ConclusionPatients with CMR-confirmed LVNC demonstrated reduced global LS and a consistent pattern of more pronounced apical strain impairment. STE and CMR showed reasonable concordance in mid-apical regions, supporting the complementary role of deformation imaging in the evaluation of LVNC. Incorporating LS into routine assessment may improve detection of functional impairment. Further studies are needed to determine the optimal strain-based approach and clarify its relationship with underlying anatomical abnormalities.

Background

Left ventricular non-compaction (LVNC) is a rare autosomal dominant disorder, characterized by prominent left ventricular (LV) trabeculae, deep inter-trabecular recesses, and a thin compacted layer [1], predominantly involving the apical and mid-ventricular segments of the left ventricle, and rarely affecting both ventricles [2].

LVNC is estimated to affect approximately 0.076% of the general neonatal population [3]. It has a heterogeneous phenotypic spectrum ranging from incidentally detected hypertrabeculation with preserved function to advanced heart failure, arrhythmias (both atrial and ventricular in 18-23% of cases), or thromboembolic events, including ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attack, or peripheral embolism [4]. Distinguishing pathological LVNC from benign or adaptive hypertrabeculation remains challenging because current diagnostic criteria are purely imaging-based and may overdiagnose LVNC in low-risk populations [5].

A multimodality imaging approach is currently recommended for the diagnosis of LVNC. Echocardiography remains the first-line diagnostic tool due to its accessibility and real-time assessment of anatomical abnormalities [6]. Moreover, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) provides robust assessment of global and regional trabeculation, which has been linked to prognosis and may help differentiate LVNC from other cardiomyopathies [7]. STE, on the other hand, can detect subtle systolic dysfunction through deformation imaging even when left ventricular ejection fraction is preserved [8]. Measurement of longitudinal strain (LS) can reveal early functional impairment that may not be evident through traditional measures like LV ejection fraction (LVEF) [9].

In this study we aimed to evaluate wall-specific LV longitudinal strain using 2D-STE and CMR strain in patients with CMR-confirmed LVNC. Because the relationship between anatomical features on CMR and functional abnormalities detected by 2D-STE and CMR strain remains uncertain, we compared strain values across the anterior, lateral, inferior, and septal walls to identify strain patterns in LVNC and assess their consistency with global systolic function and anatomical abnormalities in such patients.

Methods

Study population

This descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted on adult patients (aged 19 to 75 years) with confirmed LVNC, who were referred to the Cardiovascular Disease Centers of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences from March 2022 to 2023. The minimum required sample size was calculated as 20, using MedCalc software, based on the disease prevalence [3], a type I error of 0.05, and a study power of 80%. Accordingly, 35 patients were selected through a convenience sampling method among eligible individuals who provided informed consent. Patients with impaired renal function, MRI-incompatible implanted devices, inadequate echocardiographic image quality due to poor acoustic windows or tachypnea (which prevented accurate delineation of the endocardial border), and those who declined to provide informed consent were excluded from the study.

Study design and imaging modalities

Diagnosis of LVNC was confirmed by CMR examination performed using a 3 Tesla Skyra scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). The primary diagnostic criterion for LVNC was an NC/C > 2.3 at end-diastole. In borderline cases, additional diagnostic criteria were applied, including the Grothoff criterion [7] (non-compacted layer mass of > 15 g/m2) and the Jacquier criterion (NC mass > 20% of total LV mass) [10].

Demographic data, including age, sex, weight, height, and body surface area (BSA), were recorded. Medical history of chronic conditions such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, ischemic heart disease, and diabetes mellitus was also collected.

All participants underwent 2D transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and STE using a GE Vivid E9 ultrasound system, and CMR using the Siemens 3 Tesla Skyra scanner at Shahid Faghihi and Abu Ali Sina Hospitals. Figure 1 illustrates the flowchart of the study stages.

In 2D-TTE, both spectral and tissue Doppler parameters were evaluated. Standard echocardiographic measurements included interventricular septum (IVS) thickness, left and right ventricular dimensions, left atrial (LA) and right atrial (RA) sizes (monoplane method at end-systole), aortic sinus diameter, LVEF (by the Simpson biplane method), right ventricular systolic motion (RVsm), tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), inferior vena cava (IVC) diameter (measured 1–2 cm from the right atrial junction in a subcostal view), systolic pulmonary artery pressure (sPAP, estimated using the modified Bernoulli equation incorporating tricuspid regurgitation jet velocity and right atrial pressure derived from IVC size and respiratory collapsibility), and LV diastolic function (using pulsed Doppler of mitral inflow velocities and tissue Doppler imaging [TDI] of septal and lateral mitral annulus velocities).

STE was performed in all patients to measure LS values across various myocardial segments, including the basal, mid, and apical segments of the LV. Mean LS values were calculated for each level and compared to established normative values (GLS − 16% to − 24% in healthy adults) [11, 12]. A single experienced cardiologist-echocardiographer acquired all images using standard apical views with electrocardiogram (ECG) gating, manually traced endocardial borders using a point-and-click method, and performed strain analysis offline using the TomTec Imaging System. The software generated and allowed adjustment of regions of interest, dividing the LV into basal, mid, and apical regions, including 17 standard segments. Regional LS in each segment and global longitudinal strain (GLS) were calculated. Figure 2 provides an illustrative bull’s-eye plot from a representative patient.

Ethical statement

We conducted this study in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, ensuring the highest ethical standards. Our study was approved by the Ethics Committee for Biomedical Research of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (approval code: IR.SUMS.MED.REC.1401.259). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Released 2013. IBM SPSS Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables. Descriptive statistics, including central tendency and dispersion indices, were used to describe the data. If data distribution was non-normal, as determined by the Shapiro-Wilk test and combined skewness-kurtosis tests, the median with interquartile range (Q1–Q3) was used. The repeated measures analysis of variance test and Bonferroni’s post hoc test were applied for group comparisons. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study, LV mechanics and function were evaluated using STE in patients with CMR-confirmed LVNC. The value of CMR for diagnosing LVNC has been previously established [7, 14]. Our findings demonstrated that both STE and CMR showed LS values below the established reference thresholds, including those with normal LVEF (> 50%). In contrast, mean values obtained from 2D-TTE were all within the normal range. These results confirmed the efficacy of strain imaging in detecting cardiac dysfunction in patients with LVNC, despite normal anatomical findings. This observation aligns with previous studies by Anwer and colleagues [15] and Dorobantu and colleagues [16], which also reported decreased GLS in patients with LVNC, regardless of preserved LVEF value. The reduced GLS, regardless of LVEF, has been attributed to thickened ventricular walls or a small ventricular cavity in such patients. This challenges the routine use of LVEF for clinical assessment and suggests the superiority of GLS over LVEF in diagnosing subclinical cardiomyopathy [17, 18]. Moreover, researchers suggested the association between impaired STE parameters and cardiovascular events in patients with LVNC. This finding, consistent with the results of the present study, highlights the significant role of STE in evaluating myocardial function beyond purely anatomical/structural evaluation, particularly in these patients [15, 16].

Also, we examined the patterns and observed hypertrabeculations and myocardial recesses predominantly in the apical and lateral segments on CMR and TTE, aligning with previous reports [19–21]. Furthermore, we found that a higher number of involved myocardial walls correlated with more pronounced segmental involvement. Interestingly, the bull’s-eye plots derived from STE revealed varied segmental involvement from apex to base, while no specific or consistent pattern was observed in the present study. Our results are in agreement with the findings of Zhu and colleagues, who utilized CMR feature-tracking in 25 patients with LVNC and reported reduced LV strain without a distinct pattern of segmental involvement compared with healthy controls [22]. Comparing STE-derived LS across different segments in our cohort showed that apical LS values were furthest below the normative reference range. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies, which reported greater reductions in strain in apical segments [23–25]. Structural evaluations in previous studies have also shown that apical and mid-papillary segments of the LV are most affected in LVNC, with relative sparing of the basal segments and interventricular septum. Moreover, the number of affected segments has been associated with ventricular dysfunction, mortality, and the need for heart transplantation in pediatric populations [26]. In contrast, a study by Haland and others compared the STE-derived strain between patients with LVNC and healthy individuals and found no significant LS gradient from base to apex [27]. However, their report of globally impaired strain values in the LVNC group across all myocardial regions is consistent with the results of the present study.

When considering the results of CMR, no difference was observed in the mean LS of CMR across different segments. This LS pattern suggests that the severity of mechanical dysfunction may not directly correspond to the anatomical presence of non-compacted myocardium. We propose that the functional impairment seen in LVNC may primarily reflect the underlying cardiomyopathic process rather than the extent or location of trabeculations alone. Yu and colleagues also reported a weak association between fractal dimensions of trabeculations and LVEF and GLS in patients with normal-LVEF LVNC, compared with healthy controls [28].

An additional strength of our study was the simultaneous assessment of LS using both CMR and STE, which enabled direct comparison between the two modalities. The results showed compatibility in apical and mid segments, while CMR indicated a more reduced mean LS in the basal segment compared with STE. Previous studies have also demonstrated alignment between CMR and STE measurement of GLS, with significant variability at the segmental level. Ananthapadmanabhan and colleagues found a strong correlation in GLS measurements between CMR and STE among patients with acute myocardial infarction, suggesting that CMR is an appropriate method for multilayer strain assessment [29], whereas agreement for segmental strain was less consistent due to intervendor variability [30]. Similarly, research by Pryds and coworkers on various cardiovascular diseases highlighted that while GLS values were comparable between the two modalities, segmental strain measurements exhibited notable discrepancies [31]. In our cohort of patients with LVNC, we observed a similar pattern: a close correlation in LS values between CMR and STE in the apical and mid-ventricular segments, but a significant discrepancy in the basal segments. This divergence underscores the potential limitations of one or both imaging techniques in specific myocardial regions and highlights the need for further studies to elucidate the clinical implications of such differences, particularly in patients with LVNC. An additional aspect that warrants consideration is the potential relationship between the burden of trabeculation (derived from STE and CMR) and longitudinal strain values in each segment. Future prospective studies are needed to provide a more precise understanding of how localized structural abnormalities contribute to regional functional impairment. This may clarify whether reduced strain in certain segments reflects true myopathic involvement or simply geometric consequences of excessive trabeculation.

Another factor influencing the results obtained from both STE and CMR feature tracking is the anthropometric factor that impacts the magnitude of basal LS [32]. Recent meta-analyses by Sonaglioni and coworkers revealed that chest deformities, such as reduced anteroposterior chest diameter and anterior chest-wall depression, can lower basal strain values, with a greater impact on STE than on CMR [33, 34]. Although thoracic conformation was not assessed in our cohort, it may have contributed to the subtle discrepancies observed in basal segments between modalities. This represents a limitation of our study.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, reflecting the low prevalence of LVNC and the exclusion of patients with suboptimal image quality on STE or CMR. Additionally, only 16 of 35 patients had CMR data of sufficient quality for strain analysis, which may limit the strength of CMR-derived comparisons. Second, the absence of a healthy control group restricts the generalizability of our findings and may introduce unmeasured confounding. Third, evaluating LS in regions with prominent trabeculations and deep recesses was technically challenging, particularly in apical segments, which may have affected segmental strain accuracy. Fourth, we did not assess patients’ clinical presentations or symptoms, which could have contributed to correlating strain findings with functional status. Fifth, genetic testing was not included, as our study focused primarily on imaging-based relationships between trabeculation and longitudinal strain; however, incorporating genetic data in future studies may further refine patient characterization. Finally, segment-level correlations between NC/C ratio and LS were not evaluated because such detailed trabeculation measurements were not feasible retrospectively. The original CMR scans used in our study were acquired for routine clinical use rather than with a protocol optimized for segmental trabeculation analysis, and consistent slice positions and coverage needed for reliable NC/C measurement were not available. Prospective studies specifically designed to integrate segmental trabeculation mapping with layer-specific strain analysis are needed to address this important issue.

Conclusion

The comprehensive assessment of LV mechanics in patients with CMR-confirmed LVNC using STE and CMR-derived strain analysis in the present cohort, along with the observed patterns, can enhance understanding of myocardial deformation patterns in this unique cardiomyopathy. Also, our results confirmed the value of STE-derived GLS, even in patients with preserved LVEF, reinforcing its role as a sensitive tool for identifying early myocardial dysfunction in patients with LVNC. Interestingly, our results showed LS values were lower than established normative ranges across multiple myocardial regions, regardless of the extent of trabeculations, and no specific or consistent pattern of segmental strain reduction was identified among patients, emphasizing the heterogeneity of mechanical involvement in LVNC. The characteristic distribution of hypertrabeculation, predominantly in apical and lateral segments, was consistent across imaging modalities and aligned with prior reports. Comparing LS values between STE and CMR revealed good agreement in the apical and mid-ventricular segments, while notable discrepancies were observed in the basal region. This highlights the importance of multimodal imaging and suggests caution when interpreting segmental strain differences between techniques. Considering the valuable insights provided by these findings, further studies are needed to address the limitations of this pilot study.
