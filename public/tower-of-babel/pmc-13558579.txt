The High-resolution Urban Meteorology for Impacts Dataset for Atlanta Metropolitan Region (HUMID-Atlanta)
Authors: Tzu-Shun Lin, Andrew J Newman, Howard H Chang, Morgan Lane, Cassandra R O’Lenick, Noah Scovronick, Olga Wilhelmi, Stefanie Ebelt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs urban areas host a large portion of the world’s population, high-resolution gridded meteorological data within cities is required to answer impactful questions across fields including epidemiology and environmental sciences. Urban areas have complex land surface characteristics which significantly impact interactions between the urban surface and atmosphere. We develop a 1 km2 grid spacing meteorological dataset for 2010–2023 using a coupled atmosphere-land-urban modeling system (WRF-Urban) over the Atlanta metropolitan area with the Local Climate Zone (LCZ) classification urban map. We then apply a multivariate machine learning bias correction technique to the WRF-urban temperature and moisture variables that learn shared dependencies, which is critical for heat exposure applications. The bias correction is well validated for air temperature but shows reduced spatial transferability for dew point. We also compute several common heat exposure indices such as National Weather Service heat index, Humidex index, wet bulb globe temperature index, universal thermal climate index, and apparent temperature. The dataset can be used to estimate population exposure to heat and quantify relationships between heat exposure and health endpoints in epidemiologic research, as input to quantitative risk assessments and economic evaluation of heat-health implications, and other microclimate applications.

Background & Summary

Presently, more than fifty percent of the global population resides in urban areas, which is projected to rise to approximately sixty-six percent by 20501. As urban areas expand and meteorological patterns change, the assessment of urban environmental changes becomes increasingly complex and critical. With increased urban populations and growing built-up infrastructure, urban areas are typically warmer than their surroundings, inducing urban heat islands that lead to amplified exposure of urban residents to extreme heat, negatively impacting human health2–4. Given large heterogeneous urban land cover and uses, it is essential to understand the spatiotemporal variability of urban meteorology at a high spatiotemporal resolution over long-time periods to assess their impacts on humans. High-resolution urban meteorology datasets are thus essential for evaluating the exposure and vulnerabilities of urban populations and enhancing local and regional interventions and planning capability in response to changing climate conditions.

Significant efforts have been made to assess human health exposures to urban heat utilizing various sources of meteorological datasets5,6. In-situ observed weather station datasets have often been used to assess the urban temperature variations and their relationships with human exposures6. Despite their often high data quality, these stations are unevenly distributed and may include discontinuous temporal records (e.g., missing data, changes in location or instrumentation), resulting in gaps and/or inconsistencies that may not accurately represent the population’s exposure in urban areas7. In addition, satellite-derived products can assess land-surface temperature changes; however, the land-surface temperature is usually higher than near surface air temperature in the daytime, which leads to overestimation in ambient heat exposure and heat stress2,8,9. One common strategy for using meteorology data is to produce gridded variables by interpolating and extrapolating ground-based observations using statistical approaches10. Nevertheless, these datasets typically lack hourly temporal resolution and often do not accurately account for urban heat islands, as most measurements are situated in non-urban areas. For example, the widely-used Daymet product only provides daily maximum and minimum temperatures, with no diurnal cycle information11.

Products produced using physical-based urban-land surface models provide one solution to the shortcomings of existing observation-based meteorological datasets by explicitly representing spatial and temporal variability in urban environments. A recent dataset, HUMID10 (The High-resolution Urban Meteorology for Impacts Dataset) has been developed for the Conterminous United States, providing long-term daily meteorology data using an urban-land surface physical-based modeling system with post-processing bias correction of station observations. However, HUMID was created using an offline single-layer urban canopy model that was not coupled to an atmospheric model. As a result, it did not account for two-way feedback processes between the atmosphere and land surface, including interactions with atmospheric radiation transfer, microphysics processes (clouds, precipitation), and advection processes. Additionally, this model did not simulate vertical building structures and their effects on atmospheric heat, moisture, and momentum. HUMID also used a daily bias correction method that neglected the diurnal, nonlinear, and multivariate dependence of temperature and moisture fields. Significant advances have been made in high-resolution regional coupled atmosphere-land-urban modeling systems modeling of multiple aspects of urban canopy processes, accounting for land-atmosphere interactions with various levels of complexity12,13. For example, high-resolution (≤3 km grid spacing) atmospheric model simulations with an urban canopy scheme demonstrated that urbanization could increase rainfall in the area downwind of Atlanta during a flood event14. A study in the same region15 demonstrated that model simulations were able to capture both daytime and nocturnal urban heat islands under heat wave conditions. However, coupled model performance is influenced by the choice of boundary conditions, representation of urban areas, urban parameterization, urban morphology, and urban canopy parameters16–18.

One of the most common mesoscale atmospheric models used for urban meteorology research—the Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) model14,19,20 —adopts the MODIS (Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer) single urban land cover type as the default surface dataset, which does not distinguish different urban areas in spatial scales that lead to simulated temperature bias21,22. Additional land-cover datasets utilized in WRF studies encompass the National Land Cover Database (NLCD)21,23–25, which categorizes urban areas into three categories: industrial or commercial (urban-high intensity), high-intensity residential (urban-medium intensity), and low-intensity residential (urban-open space and urban-low intensity). Recently, the local climate zone (LCZ) classification system, comprising eleven urban types at a global resolution of 100 m26,27 was included in WRF-Urban model. The LCZ is a standardized urban classification system that distinguishes urban areas by recognizable neighborhood types based on their morphology and function, facilitating the study of urban heat islands and microclimates across various cities28.

In this study, we advance the HUMID and develop the new product, HUMID-Atlanta, by producing 1-km gridded urban meteorology data through dynamical downscaling using WRF coupled with a multi-layer urban canopy scheme over the Atlanta metropolitan region. The data product consists of 14 years (2010–2023) of hourly meteorological variables such as 2 m air temperature and dew point temperature. We also perform a machine-learning-based multivariate bias correction technique to temperature and dew point using hourly station observations in a post-processing step. We use the term “bias correction” to represent the adjustment of systematic distortions between model outputs and observation data29. However, it is essential to recognize the disparity in scale representativeness between point-wise ground-truth observations and gridded model outputs, which does not imply that gridded model data are inherently erroneous and require “correction”, particularly in ungauged areas. Finally, in this product, we generate supplementary heat indices, including the National Weather Service heat index, Humidex index, apparent temperature, universal thermal climate index, wet-bulb globe temperature index and others, which can be used to better understand and quantify impacts of urban heat islands on urban populations.

Methods

Regional meteorological model simulations

The WRF model [Advanced Research WRF versions 4.5.1]30 is used for simulating urban meteorology in this study. As a limited area, nonhydrostatic model, with a terrain-following Eta-coordinate mesoscale modeling system, WRF has been designed to serve both operational forecasting and atmospheric research needs31. The model herein is configured in two nested grid domains with an outer domain of 5 km horizontal grid spacing (211 by 186 grid cells) and an inner domain of 1 km horizontal grid spacing (171 by 171 grid cells) over regions surrounding the metropolitan region of Atlanta using the Lambert conformal conic map projection (Table 1). The 5 km domain spans most of the Southeast United States, while the 1 km domain focuses on northern parts of Georgia centered on Atlanta (Figure S1). The model is configured with 51 stretched vertical levels, with the top at 50 hPa. The WRF model provides several choices for parameterizations of physical processes, and the following WRF model physics options are used: Thompson microphysics scheme32, Mellor-Yamada-Janjic (MYJ) planetary boundary layer scheme33, Rapid Radiative Transfer Model (RRTMG) shortwave and longwave radiation scheme34, and the Noah-MP land surface model35,36. The dynamical lake scheme in WRF is activated to simulate physical lake processes over Lake Lanier and Lake Allatoona (Fig. 1a). The lake model in WRF includes a one-dimensional thermal diffusion lake scheme to simulate lake surface temperatures37,38 and the lake depth is prescribed from the Global Lake Data Set39. The lake depth data is available at the website (https://www2.mmm.ucar.edu/wrf/users/download/get_sources_wps_geog.html).

We perform WRF simulations for 14 years (2010–2023). We use a 5-day re-initialization forcing strategy instead of a continuous simulation. Each run starts at 0000 coordinated universal time (UTC) and lasts for 120 hours, with the first 48 hours used as spin-up time and is not used for the analysis. This strategy reduces model drift from the driving reanalysis data and provides computational flexibility since simulations are independent of each other and can be computed in parallel and in any sequence. The initial and hourly boundary conditions use the global ERA5 (European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts Reanalysis v5) reanalysis data40. The ERA5 reanalysis is publicly available at websites (https://gdex.ucar.edu/datasets/d633000/ and https://gdex.ucar.edu/datasets/d633006/). The model time step is 30 seconds for the 5 km and 6 seconds for 1 km domains respectively throughout the simulation period.

We use the MetPy v1.7.1 Python library41 (https://github.com/Unidata/MetPy) to calculate the heat index, relative humidity, and dew point temperature based on WRF modeled output of surface pressure, 2-m air temperature, and mixing ratio. The cosine of solar zenith angle is calculated using earthkit-meteo v0.6.2 Python package (https://github.com/ecmwf/earthkit-meteo). For wet bulb temperature, mean radiant temperature, globe temperature, universal thermal climate index, wet bulb globe temperature, wet bulb globe temperature simple, apparent temperature, normal effective temperature and humidex, we calculate using the thermofeel v2.1.742 Python library (https://github.com/ecmwf/thermofeel). The required input variables for calculating these heat indexes are available at the website (https://thermofeel.readthedocs.io/en/latest/index.html). Table S1 lists the description, meteorological inputs, formula sources, method assumptions, and scope of applications for these heat index-related variables.

To calculate heat indexes that incorporate radiation effects, including mean radiation temperature, wet bulb globe temperature, and universal thermal climate index, direct solar radiation at surface is necessary, although it is not a standard WRF output variable43. Thus, we partition total downward solar radiation using a 70% direct radiation proportion based on the assumption used in the Noah-MP land surface model44,45. A sensitivity analysis is performed to quantify the uncertainty in this assumption by assessing the effects of varying the direct-to-diffuse radiation fraction of 0.1 (10%), 0.3 (30%), 0.5 (50%), 0.7 (70%), and 0.9 (90%) on 14-year average of daily maximum mean radiant temperature (MRT), globe temperature (BGT), wet-bulb globe temperature (WGBT), and universal thermal climate index (UTCI). Results show that while MRT exhibits significant sensitivity to this assumption, with difference about 12 K between 0.1 and 0.9 of direct-to-diffuse radiation fraction, the resulting variations in BGT, WGBT and UTCI are more moderate, with differences generally within 1–3 K. The results suggest that although the fixed partitioning introduces uncertainty, its effect on the derived radiation-related heat indices is bound within a smaller range than that of the MRT (Figure S2).

Urban physics scheme

Within WRF, urban models fall into three categories46: a bulk urban parameterization, single-layer urban canopy models (SLUCM47), and multi-layer models including the Building Effect Parameterization (BEP)48 and its coupled version with the Building Energy Model (BEP-BEM)49. BEP considers the three-dimensional characteristics of urban surfaces and the effects of vertical building structures on sources and sinks of heat, moisture, and momentum through the urban canopy layer. It influences the thermodynamic structure of the urban roughness sublayer and hence the lower part of the urban boundary layer. BEP considers effects of vertical (walls) and horizontal (roads and roofs) surfaces on momentum (drag force approach), turbulent kinetic energy, and potential temperature. The radiation at walls and roads considers shadowing, reflections, and trapping of shortwave and longwave radiation in street canyons. Compared to other urban parameterization schemes in WRF, BEP better reproduces vertical temperature profiles in urban areas but does not consider the indoor–outdoor exchange and energy consumption due to other sources such as air conditioning12.

Previous studies over major U.S. metropolitan areas such as Atlanta have demonstrated that multi-layer urban canopy models (BEP and BEP-BEM) are necessary to resolve street-level thermal conditions and urban heat exposure50–52. While BEP-BEM improves explicit representation of anthropogenic heat processes, it also introduces additional uncertainty due to requirement for detailed building energy use and anthropogenic heat emission data, which are often unavailable or poorly constrained53,54. This increased physical complexity also leads to higher computational cost and greater sensitivity to input assumptions. Therefore, we use the BEP scheme for representing urban canopy parameterization in WRF model for the 14-year production simulations.

Static geophysical data

The land surface geophysical data used in the WRF model include terrain height, land/water mask, soil texture, and land cover types, which are obtained using the WRF Preprocessing System (WPS) version 4.5 (https://github.com/wrf-model/WPS). The WPS preprocessor takes geophysical data and aggregates and/or interpolates it to a user specified grid spacing using a variety of interpolation and aggregation routines. We use the default urban fraction map in WPS, which is derived from 30 m National Land Cover Data (NLCD) 2011 data55, which is not based on the LCZ map.

The land cover types are obtained from the CGLC-MODIS-LCZ classification system in WPS27. This data combines the land cover product from the Copernicus Global Land Service Land Cover (CGLC) and the global map of LCZ that categorizes the heterogeneous urban land surface and resample to MODIS IGBP land cover classification system at the 100-m resolution. We use WPS to aggregate the original data resolution to our selected grid, at 5-1 km nested domain. These static geophysical data are publicly available at the website (https://www2.mmm.ucar.edu/wrf/users/download/get_sources_wps_geog.html). We note that the use of default static land cover/use map and urban fraction data throughout the 2010–2023 simulation period may not capture the historical land use changes, which is the limitation of this study and requires future work.

In the Atlanta metropolitan area (Fig. 1b), 19% of the region (2069 km2) is classified as urban, comprised of open low-rise (1665 km2), large low-rise (291 km2), open mid-rise (89 km2), sparsely built (21 km2), compact mid-rise (2 km2), and compact high-rise (1 km2).

Meteorological assimilation data ingest system (MADIS)

To evaluate the WRF simulation results, we compare 2-m air temperature and dew point temperature with the surface mesonet station data from the Meteorological Assimilation Data Ingest System (MADIS), which are automated surface weather stations that report at hourly or more frequent intervals. The MADIS data is obtained through an account email to NCEP.List.MADIS_Support@noaa.gov and more information can be found at the website (https://madis.ncep.noaa.gov/index.shtml). We select hourly data from the MADIS stations and compare to the nearest neighbor grids of hourly WRF outputs at 1-km grid spacing. We only include data for both air temperature and dew point with good quality based on MADIS data quality control flags. Stations with fewer than 500 hourly observations are excluded to ensure sufficient temporal representativeness for bias correction, while maintaining adequate spatial coverage across the Atlanta region.

Table S2 lists the station numbers and numbers of hourly temperature data for each land cover category using the CGLC-MODIS-LCZ classification system. Over the entire study period, 38 stations are classified as urban, and 73 stations are non-urban (Fig. 2a). The locations of the available stations fluctuate throughout time with far fewer stations prior to 2015 (Fig. 2b). In total, there are 1,026,130 and 716,013 hourly temperature data available for non-urban and urban stations, respectively (Table S2).

Multivariate bias correction

To reduce bias of the WRF modeled outputs, we applied a bias correction technique to 2-m air temperature and dew point temperature concurrently using observed data from MADIS stations. Bias correction of air temperature and dew point was conducted using a machine learning approach eXtreme Gradient Boosting56 (XGBoost) due to its flexibility in capturing nonlinear spatiotemporal relationships and incorporating multiple covariates. This method has been widely applied in urban temperature studies and has demonstrated robust predictive performance in similar applications57–59. XGBoost is an ensemble learning model based on decision-trees that specializes at handling complex variable interactions and collinearity (e.g., positive correlation between air and dew point temperature), making it valuable for addressing Earth scientific research60,61.

To ensure spatially independent evaluation, a Leave-One-Block-Out (LOBO) cross-validation scheme is employed. Spatial blocks are constructed based on the spatial decorrelation scale derived from variogram analysis. Specifically, semivariograms are estimated using the SciKit-GStat package62 to quantify the spatial autocorrelation of hourly air temperature and dew point temperature from the MADIS stations. This analysis shows that the mean decorrelation lengths are 35 ± 17 km for air temperature and 29 ± 19 km for dew point temperature across 62,875 hourly timesteps. Based on these results, a block size of 40 km is selected to exceed the characteristic spatial correlation scale for folds independence. After that, an adaptive merging procedure is applied to ensure robust sampling of stations for each fold. Blocks containing fewer than five stations are iteratively merged with their nearest neighboring blocks based on centroid distance. This approach reduces sampling imbalance across folds and mitigates instability associated with sparsely sampled regions. Following this procedure, a total of 9 spatial blocks are retained for cross-validation.

For each fold, one spatial block is held out as the test set, while the remaining blocks are used for training. Within each training fold, hyperparameters are optimized using an inner cross-validation loop based on a grouped 5-fold approach in Python scikit-learn63. XGBoost hyperparameters are optimized using Optuna64 with an objective function to minimize root mean square error (RMSE). The LOBO cross-validation performance is reported as the mean and standard deviation across all folds. After cross-validation, a final model is trained on the entire dataset using the optimal hyperparameters identified during the cross-validation process. The model input features include time-varying variables of WRF modeled 2-m air temperature, dew point temperature, precipitation, downward shortwave radiation at ground surface, 10-m wind speed from u and v-components, year, month, Julian day, and hour, alongside static variables including land cover types, terrain height, and urban fraction. The code is executed and trained using a Python package xgboost v3.2.0 (https://pypi.org/project/xgboost/3.2.0/). The output targets are the differences between observed and modeled air and dew point temperatures, which is then predicted and applied to all WRF simulations by grid cells and hours. In the XGBoost model, the “multi_output_tree” strategy is used to build a singular model for multiple outputs instead of training with one-model-per-target. This method implicitly keeps the covariance between air temperature and dew point temperature; nevertheless, around 1.5% (27,117) of hourly dew point temperatures surpass the air temperature after correction, violating thermodynamic consistency. Such cases are characterized by high-humidity and low-wind conditions, often during the nighttime and winter season (Figure S3). To ensure physical consistency, we implement a physical check under this condition to adjust air temperature upwards to anchor the dew point temperature. This post-processing step has a negligible impact on overall statistics but prevents unphysical states and improves robustness in high-moisture regimes.

Spatial correlation comparison

The global Moran’s I65 is used to compute the degree of spatial autocorrelation which can be used to determine if the spatial pattern is dispersed, clustered, or random. The Moran’s I index ranges from −1 to 1. While the Moran’s I > 0, it shows a positive spatial correlation, which means that the observed pattern is spatially clustered; Moran’s I < 0 indicates a spatially negative correlation, resulting in a dispersed pattern. While the Moran’s I is near to zero, it indicates that the data is spread randomly in space. We compare Moran’s I of the HUMID-Atlanta data with other gridded datasets, Daymet V4 R111 (available at https://daymet.ornl.gov/getdata) and PRISM66 (available at https://prism.oregonstate.edu/downloads/).

Data Records

The data are provided in Network Common Data Form (NetCDF) formats (https://www.unidata.ucar.edu/software/netcdf/). Each file contains all archived variables for all grid points as a three-dimensional array, coordinate variables (XTIME, south_north, west_east), and geophysical attributes. The time is the local standard time (UTC-5 hours) and leap years include 29 February. File names are specified using HUMID_Atlanta_<FREQUENCY><mean/min/max>_<YYYY><MM>.nc naming convention where <FREQUENCY> is the record frequency (i.e., hourly, daily, monthly, and yearly), <YYYY> is the 4-digit year, <MM> is the 2-digit month (for hourly and daily frequency). The daily file contains the daily mean, minimum, and maximum of 24-hourly data; the monthly files are average calculated from daily values, and the yearly files are average calculated from monthly values (<mean/min/max>). The data are archived on the NSF NCAR Geoscience Data Exchange (GDEX) at https://gdex.ucar.edu/datasets/d010031/, which includes all meteorology data files, variable names and metadata, and the static geographical data in WRF67. Technical Validation

Comparisons with Daymet and PRISM

HUMID-Atlanta is compared to two gridded observation-based temperature datasets, Daymet Version 4 R168 and PRISM66 (Parameter-elevation Regressions on Independent Slopes Model) (Table 3). DaymetV4R1 estimated daily minimum and maximum air temperatures at 1-km grid spacing using in situ station observations combined with geophysical attribute (e.g. elevation) interpolation and extrapolation11. PRISM66 used a knowledge-based linear regression framework to estimate daily minimum and maximum air temperatures, as well as the daily mean dew point temperature at 800-m grid spacing. We regridded Daymet and PRISM using nearest neighbor remapping to align with the HUMID-Atlanta grid for the same grid spatial comparisons.

HUMID-Atlanta shows greater spatial heterogeneity of maximum (Fig. 3) and minimum (Fig. 4) temperatures than DaymetV4R1, with higher temperatures in the urban areas than the rural areas (Table 4), while DaymetV4R1’s temperatures are more dependent on the terrain height with a significant negative correlation of −0.89 for maximum and −0.71 for minimum temperatures (Figure S1b and Table S3). HUMID-Atlanta represents the typical patterns of urban heat islands, exhibiting stronger effects at night than during the daytime, and in winter and spring compared to summer and fall (Figure S4-S5). The seasonal urban heat island effect can be attributed to fluctuations in cloud cover that change the surface energy budget and, consequently, temperatures15. Compared to DaymetV4R1, PRISM shows higher daily minimum temperatures in urban areas, particularly in the southwest of Atlanta (Fig. 4a,b). HUMID-Atlanta is relatively closer to PRISM data, as both products show a lower negative correlation between minimum temperature and terrain heights compared to DaymetV4R1 (−0.58 for PRISM and −0.65 for HUMID-Atlanta, against −0.71 for DaymetV4R1 in Table S3). The minimum temperature difference between the three datasets is smaller in summer than in other seasons (Figure S5). Overall, for HUMID-Atlanta, the annual average daily maximum and minimum air temperatures are 0.31 K/0.09 K lower and 0.97 K/0.95 K higher than the DaymetV4R1/PRISM data, respectively.

HUMID-Atlanta also simulates air temperature variations across land cover types, such as relatively low/high temperature in the daytime/nighttime over Lake Lanier due to the use of the explicit 1D lake model while PRISM and DaymetV4R1 just interpolate over the lake. The Moran’s I values for the three air temperature datasets are all significantly positive (p < 0.001), indicating the air temperatures are spatially correlated (Table S4). Nonetheless, HUMID-Atlanta exhibits the lowest Moran’s I values for both daily maximum (0.92) and minimum temperatures (0.83) compared to PRISM (>0.97) and DaymetV4R1 (>0.99), with further reductions after bias correction (from 0.95 to 0.92 for daily maximum temperature and from 0.95 to 0.83 for daily minimum temperature). The reduction in Moran’s I is associated with improvements in bias correction performance metrics (i.e., reductions in bias, RMSE and increases in correlation, Table 5), imply that the increased variability is at least partly physically meaningful, reflecting improved representation of local-scale conditions. Therefore, the lower Moran’s I in HUMID-Atlanta reflects a combination of enhanced representation of fine-scale urban heterogeneity and increased amplification of small-scale variability from dynamical downscaling and statistical bias correction.

In the comparison of the mean temperature differences between urban and non-urban pixels (Table 4), HUMID-Atlanta estimates the magnitude of the urban heat island effect on nighttime minimum temperature to be 0.42 K, which falls within the range of DaymetV4R1 (0.39 K) and PRISM (0.56 K). In contrast, the urban heat island effect on the daytime maximum temperature is 0.08 K for HUMID-Atlanta, while DaymetV4R1 has a modest cooling effect of −0.03 K and PRISM shows −0.08 K. These differences may be attributed to the fact that HUMID-Atlanta calculates daily maximum and minimum temperatures using hourly WRF modeled outputs, whereas DaymetV4R1 and PRISM collect data from in-situ stations, which may contain sub-hourly measurements.

In terms of temporal 2-m air temperature variability across three datasets, the Pearson correlation coefficients of monthly anomaly temperatures (i.e., subtracting multi-year mean for that specific month) between each dataset pairs (e.g., HUMID-Atlanta and PRISM) are over 0.97 (Figure S7). DaymetV4R1 and PRISM are highly positively correlated across the majority of grid cells, with correlation values close to 1.0 but lower in some non-urban grids, such as western Paulding County. HUMID-Atlanta shows more varied correlation patterns, with relatively lower temporal correlation in areas with high urban fractions for maximum temperatures (Figure S7a-b), and correlation of 0.97 with DaymetV4R1 and PRISM.

Compared to the daily mean dew point temperature for 2010–2023 from PRISM (Fig. 5), HUMID-Atlanta is positive correlated with a coefficient of 0.77 (p < 0.001) but has higher values across most of the domain, with the average increase of 0.48 K. Significant spatial heterogeneity exists in HUMID-Atlanta with bias-correction relative to PRISM, especially inside the counties of the Atlanta metropolitan region, as indicated by the Moran’s I index of 0.73, which is lower than PRISM’s values of 0.99 (Table S4). The spatial variability is mostly influenced by distributions of land cover types and the urban fraction, with lower dew point temperatures in areas with a higher urban fraction. The seasonal differences (Figures S6) closely resemble the annual differences. In terms of the contrast between mean dew point over urban and non-urban pixels, HUMID-Atlanta aligns with PRISM, indicating no difference (Table 4).

Validation of bias correction

Figure 6 compares average air and dew point temperatures for each land cover type across MADIS stations, averaged for 2010–2023 using cross-validation method. The raw WRF-Urban model simulated air temperature can reproduce the variability (e.g. high and low temperatures) near the surface for urban and non-urban types. Further, the WRF-Urban model results reflects variations of dew point temperature across land cover types with the highest dew point in evergreen needleleaf forest type and the lowest in open midrise built type. However, the raw model results consistently underestimate the observed dew point values at MADIS stations.

Leave-one-block-out cross-validation of air and dew point temperatures for each land cover types at MADIS stations averaged from 2010 to 2023.

Bias correction using XGBoost corrects the mean leave-one-block-out positive bias of air temperatures to near zero and reduces bias of dew point from −0.37 to −0.20 K (Table 5). It also reduces RMSE by about 30% for air temperature and 20% for dew point and enhances the correlation to about 0.98 with MADIS observations. Across spatial blocks, the bias correction performs consistently for air temperature, with similar variability across spatial blocks in terms of standard deviation of bias, RMSE, and correlation coefficient between raw and bias-corrected results (Table 5). Across land cover types, bias correction reduces the mean air temperature bias except for the slight underestimation for the open midrise built type (Fig. 6a). In contrast, bias-corrected dew point exhibits larger variability across spatial blocks compared to raw data, indicating that bias correction of dew point is spatially nonstationary and generalizes less effectively than for air temperature. The comparison across land cover types indicates no apparent reduction in dew point bias for evergreen needleleaf and deciduous broadleaf forest types, as well as a slight overestimation of dew point for urban types after correction (Fig. 6b). For the diurnal cycle, bias correction mitigates the overestimation of air temperature at non-urban stations and the underestimation during daytime at urban stations; nonetheless, it results in a little underestimation between 21 and 03 hours at urban stations (Fig. 7a,b). The bias correction reduces the daytime dry bias of dew point but slightly underestimates it during 0–7 hours at non-urban stations and slightly overestimates it at urban stations (Fig. 7c,d).

A spatially uncertainty comparison is conducted to assess the robustness and spatial generalization of the bias correction method. Figure 8 presents the raw and bias-corrected uncertainty in air temperature and dew point, quantified using RMSE derived from leave-one-block-out cross-validation and mapped across the 1-km WRF model domain. The results indicate that the bias correction remains stable and spatially consistent for air temperature across the study domain, whereas larger uncertainties are found for dew point, showing reduced spatial transferability, particularly in the northeast part of the Atlanta region. This discrepancy implies the higher sensitivity of near-surface moisture fields to local and heterogeneous processes such as vegetation conditions, surface moisture availability, and atmospheric boundary processes, which are not fully captured by the predictors used within the XGBoost model. Despite this, RMSE values are substantially lower than those of the raw WRF model outputs, demonstrating that the bias correction remains robust in most areas and does not generate spurious results in data-sparse areas.

To understand if bias correction maintains its effectiveness under extreme heat for exposure applications, we compare results for all observed hourly air temperatures larger than 35 °C. Overall, the bias correction remains effective under extreme heat conditions across urban and non-urban stations, although the magnitude of improvement varies by variable and metric (Table S5). For air temperature, the bias is substantially reduced for the full dataset but remains negative during extreme heat events, indicating a persistent cool bias under high-temperature conditions, especially for non-urban stations. Nevertheless, RMSE is reduced by approximately 2 K, and the correlation coefficient improves from 0.22 to 0.57. For dew point temperature, the bias correction performs more consistently between full period and high-temperature conditions. While the raw model outputs exhibit a negative bias under extreme heat conditions (−2.0 K), the bias correction reduces this bias to slightly positive values (0.2 K). Furthermore, RMSE is substantially reduced (by more than 3 K), and correlation improves markedly (from ~0.5 to ~0.96). The overall improvements in RMSE and correlation confirm that the bias correction method remains effective for extreme heat exposure applications.

Summary

In conclusion, the comparison between HUMID-Atlanta and the DaymetV4R1 and PRISM gridded meteorology dataset shows that HUMID-Atlanta has more spatially and slightly larger temporally heterogeneous patterns of air temperature and dew point. All datasets indicate the nocturnal urban heat effect. HUMID-Atlanta advances the previous version of the HUMID10 dataset that provides multiple heat indices on the hourly basis for the Atlanta metropolitan region. HUMID-Atlanta enhances the simulation of temperature and moistures fields by incorporating interactions with atmospheric physical and dynamical processes through a dynamical downscaling using a coupled modeling framework. HUMID-Atlanta also uses the updated urban land cover dataset, which distinguishes urban morphological variations. Furthermore, HUMID-Atlanta implements a machine learning bias correction algorithm based on XGBoost. Results indicate that this bias correction method remains stable and spatially validated for air temperature, but larger spatial variations exist for dew point. Although the bias correction improves overall temperature accuracy, a cool bias remains for extreme air temperatures exceeding 35° C. HUMID-Atlanta is already being used to understand and assess heat-health impacts under urban environments, and this method could be further developed to forecast future meteorological changes and serve as a potential tool for early warning systems.
