Structure-guided surface engineering to improve the catalytic activity of ethylene-forming enzyme
Authors: Mengyuan Wang, Zhuanglin Shen, Lian Wu, Weixue Huang, Jiahai Zhou, Yang Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ethylene-forming enzyme (EFE) is a member of the mononuclear non-heme Fe(II)- and 2-oxoglutarate-dependent oxygenase superfamily, which can oxidize 2-oxoglutarate to form ethylene in an arginine-dependent reaction. While significant enzyme engineering efforts have targeted the active site and surface of EFE, to date, no variant with substantially improved activity has been reported. To enhance catalytic activity and broaden the application potential of EFE, this study developed a surface engineering strategy based on structural analysis and potentially new l-Arg binding information. The resulting variant, E213T, exhibited a 1.5-fold increase in catalytic activity and a 2.4-fold elevation in kcat, l-Arg. Molecular dynamics simulations further revealed that this amino acid substitution reduced the affinity of the surface l-Arg binding site and altered the accessibility of ligands to the catalytic center. Our study provides a new perspective on the distal sites and functional relationships in protein engineering of EFE.

Introduction

Ethylene is an essential gaseous plant hormone in nature, not only regulating diverse growth and developmental processes such as seed germination and dispersal, cell extension, fertilization, and fruit ripening, but also defense against pathogens and stress responses [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. This hormone is biologically produced by plants and certain microbes. In plants, ethylene biosynthesis proceeds from l-methionine through the sequential action of S-adenosyl-l-methionine synthetase, 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC) synthase, and ACC oxidase [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Microbial ethylene production occurs through two principal routes. Escherichia coli and Cryptococcus albidus utilize the NADH:Fe(III)-EDTA oxidoreductase to convert 2-oxo-4-methylthiobutyric acid into ethylene, although this pathway yields only low levels of this hormone [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. The most notable route employed by plant-associated bacteria (e.g., Pseudomonas syringae and Ralstonia solanacearum) and fungi (e.g., Penicillium digitatum) relies on a single Fe(II)/2-oxoglutarate (2OG)-dependent enzyme known as the ethylene-forming enzyme (EFE) [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. Remarkably, the heterologous expression of the efe gene alone enables the production of ethylene in diverse hosts (e.g., bacteria, yeasts) [[20], [21], [22]], positioning EFE as a promising “single-gene” biocatalyst for industrial bioethylene synthesis.

Biochemically, the EFE from P. syringae pv. phaseolicola PK2, catalyzes a branched reaction [23,24]. In one branch (“ethylene-forming”), two 2OG molecules are fragmented to ethylene plus three CO₂ (with l-Arg acting as a bound cofactor). In the other branch (“arginine-hydroxylating”), 2OG is converted to succinate and CO₂ while hydroxylating l-arginine at C5 to give 5‑hydroxy‑l-arginine (which collapses to guanidine and Δ¹-pyrroline-5-carboxylate) (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Reactions catalyzed by EFE.Fig 1 dummy alt text

Reported structures of PsEFE (Table S1) consistently exhibit a double-stranded β-helix (also known as the jellyroll or cupin fold), a hallmark of Fe(II)/2OG-dependent oxygenase [[25], [26], [27], [28]]. In the EFE:2OG:l-Arg complex, both 2OG and l-Arg are bound within the active center, and the C1 carboxyl and C2 keto groups of 2OG form a bidentate coordination with the metal ion, whereas the guanidine groups of l-Arg interact with R171 [25,26]. In the absence of l-Arg, 2OG adopts two different coordination modes: monodentate (EFE:Mn:2OG, PDB code: 5V2X) [25] and bidentate (EFE:Mn:2OG, PDB code: 5MOF) [26]. Given the precise geometry of its active site, numerous studies have introduced amino acid substitutions at the binding residues of 2OG, l-Arg, and the adjacent hydrophobic pockets. However, these alterations typically result in a substantial loss or complete abolition of ethylene-forming activity [[25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30], [31]]. In mutagenesis experiments targeting EFE surface regions distant from the active site, some variants exhibited a marked decrease in activity, whereas others showed a slight increase compared to the wild-type (WT) [25,29,30]. However, no variants that significantly enhance ethylene production have been reported to date.

To enhance catalytic activity and broaden application potential, this study employed a protein surface engineering strategy for the semi-rational design of EFE to modulate substrate access and its distal interaction networks. Based on the structural analysis and reported data, the key regions responsible for the increased catalytic activity were identified. Following site-directed saturation mutagenesis within this region, a distal single-point variant with high catalytic activity was screened. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations revealed that this distal amino acid substitution reduced the affinity of the surface l-Arg-binding site and altered the accessibility of ligands to the catalytic center, thereby enhancing catalytic activity. Our study provides new insights into the distal sites of EFE and their functional relationships in the protein engineering of EFE.

Materials and methods

Protein production and purification

The PsEFE DNA sequence was obtained from Pseudomonas syringae pv. phaseolicola (UNIPROT ID: P32021) and subsequently codon-optimized for heterologous expression. A 6 × His tag was attached to the N-terminus of PsEFE and cloned into the pSJ2 vector. After verification via DNA sequencing, the construct was introduced into competent BL21 (DE3) cells to produce proteins.

For protein production, a single colony was inoculated in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium containing 50 μg/mL ampicillin and grown overnight at 37 °C. The next day, the overnight culture was transferred to 1 L TB medium containing 50 μg/mL ampicillin using a shaker incubating at (37 °C, 220 rpm). Upon reaching an OD600 value of 0.8–1.0, protein expression was induced by a temperature downshift to 16 °C and the addition of 0.2 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside. After another 16–18 h of incubation, the cells were pelleted via centrifugation (8000 × g, 10 min) and stored at –80 °C until further analyses.

For protein purification, the cell pellets were thawed, resuspended in chilled lysis buffer (25 mM HEPES [pH 7.5], 300 mM NaCl, and 5 % glycerol), and disrupted using a French press with a high-pressure homogenizer. The lysates were centrifuged (14,000 rpm, 30 min, 4 °C) to eliminate cellular debris, and the clarified lysate was subjected to Ni-NTA agarose column chromatography (Qiagen). Elution of PsEFE employed buffer B, which was composed of 25 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 300 mM NaCl, 300 mM imidazole, and 5 % glycerol. Protein expression and purification were assessed using SDS-PAGE (Fig. S1 and Fig. S2). The 6 × His tag was then cleaved with TEV protease and removed during Ni-NTA agarose column chromatography. The flow‑through containing untagged proteins was concentrated and stored at –80 °C until subsequent analyses.

Site-directed mutagenesis

EFE variants were created by mutagenesis of the efe gene located in plasmid pSJ2, using the primers listed in Table S2. After the correct sequence of each mutated plasmid was confirmed by DNA sequencing (Sangon Company), the plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells, and protein expression and purification were then carried out as described in the protein production and purification section.

Enzyme activity measurements

Gas chromatography (GC) assays for ethylene

Glass bottles (22 mm × 52 mm) were used for standard assays at a temperature of 25±1 °C. WT and mutant variants of EFE (1 μM) were incubated in 1 mL of 25 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.5) containing 0.5 mM 2-oxoglutarate (2OG), 0.5 mM l-Arg, 2.0 mM l-ascorbic acid, and 0.2 mM (NH4)2Fe(SO4)2. The reactions were quenched after 40 min by adding 0.5 mL of 6 M HCl [32]. For ethylene measurements, 1 mL of gas from the headspace was extracted using a Hamilton gastight syringe and injected into an Agilent GC-8890 gas chromatograph fitted with a thermal conductivity detector, a flame ionization detector, and a HayeSep Q column (2 mm × 1.83 m). Quantification was achieved by integrating the chromatographic peak areas using Agilent OpenLab analytical software and interpolating the values against a pre-established standard calibration curve to derive their absolute concentrations.

Detection of P5C

l-∆−1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate (P5C) was quantified via derivatization employing 2-aminobenzaldehyde [32,18]. A 150 μL aliquot of the assay mixture was combined with 50 μL of 50 mM 2-aminobenzaldehyde (dissolved in 40 % [v/v] ethanol) in a 96-well plate (Corning 3599, Corning Inc., USA). The reaction mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 20 min to generate a yellow derivative. Afterwards, the absorbance at 440 nm was recorded on a SpectraMAX iD5® reader (Sunnyvale, CA, USA) with an extinction coefficient (ε) of 2.58 mM⁻¹·cm⁻¹. The P5C concentration (mM) was calculated as cP5C = [A × dilution factor]/[ε × l], where A = measured absorbance value, dilution factor = 4/3, ε = 2.58 mM⁻¹·cm⁻¹ and l = 0.625 cm.

Steady-state kinetics

We performed steady-state kinetic assays using the GC method described above with the following conditions: 100 nM PsEFE, 25 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 0.2 mM (NH4)2Fe(SO4)2, and 2.0 mM sodium l-ascorbate. The concentrations of the two substrates were varied separately: l-Arg from 0 to 500 μM with the 2OG concentration held at 0.5 mM, and 2OG from 0 to 500 μM with the l-Arg concentration held at 0.5 mM. The reactions were quenched for 10 min, and 1 mL of the headspace gas was measured using an Agilent GC-8890 gas chromatograph. The velocity (nM/min) was calculated according to the following formula: v = [Vheadspace × ppm × 10⁻⁶]/[Vm × t × Vsolution], where Vsolution = 0.001 L and Vm = 22.4 × 10–6 mL/nmol (under standard conditions). Using GraphPad Prism software, the initial rates were fitted to the Michaelis–Menten formula to obtain the Vmax and Km. The turnover number kcat was computed as the Vmax divided by [E]t, where [E]t denotes the total enzyme concentration.

Protein crystallization and structure determination

The purification and crystallization of EFE were performed under anaerobic conditions at 4 °C. Crystals were generated at 16 °C via the sitting-drop vapor-diffusion method, using a 2-μL droplet composed of equal volumes of protein and crystallization solutions. For the co-crystallization experiments, the concentrated protein was incubated with substrates and cofactors on ice for half an hour. The crystals were obtained under the following crystallization conditions: 0.1 M sodium cacodylate (pH 6.5), 25 % (w/v) polyethylene glycol (PEG)-4000. The crystals were cryo-cooled by plunging into liquid nitrogen using a reservoir buffer containing 20 % (v/v) glycerol as a cryoprotective agent.

We collected PsEFE diffraction data at 100 K on Beamline 19U1 (λ = 0.9779 Å) at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility. Data processing, including indexing, integration, and scaling, was performed using the HKL3000 program [33] and the XDS software package [34]. Using the PHASER program, we determined the structure via molecular replacement, using an AlphaFold-predicted model as the search template. Iterative manual rebuilding using COOT software [35] and refinement using PHENIX suite [36] were then performed. The model quality was validated using the Ramachandran plot (Fig. S3), and crystallographic statistics were computed using Phenix.Table One (a module of the PHENIX suite) [37], as summarized in Table S3. Final structural visualization and graphics were created using PyMOL software (http://www.pymol.org/) [38].

Molecular dynamics simulations

The structures for the MD analysis were retrieved from the X-ray diffraction data. The missing residues and side chains were reconstructed using the AMBERTools LEaP module [39]. The AMBER ff14SB force field was used to assign parameters for both the protein and l-Arg substrates [40]. The complexes were solvated within a cubic TIP3P water box [39] and neutrality was maintained by introducing counter ions (Na+/Cl–). The GROMACS 2025.3 [41] suite was used to run all MD trajectories, with the system subjected to full periodic boundary conditions in three dimensions.

The MD simulation workflow was initiated with a rigorous energy-minimization phase, combining the steepest descent and conjugate gradient methods to resolve any initial atomic overlap. Subsequently, the system underwent a 1-ns thermalization period in the NVT ensemble to gradually reach the target temperature (300 K). This was followed by a 1-ns equilibration stage under NPT conditions to allow the system density and periodic box dimensions to converge. Production MD simulations were performed after achieving a stable equilibrium. To ensure reproducibility and the statistical significance of our findings, three independent production runs were performed for each system, with each trajectory extending to a total of 200 ns. To account for long-range electrostatics, the Particle Mesh Ewald scheme [42] was implemented, whereas a 1.0-nm spherical cutoff was used to manage the van der Waals forces. We facilitated a 2-fs integration interval by employing the LINCS constraint algorithm to fix the bond lengths of all hydrogen-containing covalent linkages. The temperature of the system was maintained at 300 K using a velocity-rescaling thermostat (V-rescale) [43], while the pressure was controlled at 1.0 bar using a Parrinello–Rahman barostat [44]. The MD production systems were stable according to the RMSD of the protein (Fig. S4).

For the EFE-2OG/l-Arg complex, an extended adaptive biasing force (eABF) approach [45] was adopted to map the free energy profile. By introducing a collective variable tethered to the distance between Fe and the C5 atom of 2OG as well as between Fe and the C4 atom of l-Arg via a harmonic spring, the eABF enhances sampling through an adaptive biasing force derived from the negative spring force. This dragging mechanism allows for the efficient exploration of the 4°–20° distance range. Simulations were performed using NAMD2.14 [46] and Colvar [47]. Analysis of the resulting ensembles included VMD-based visualization and MM-GB/SA calculations [48] to quantify the thermodynamics of l-Arg binding.

Results and discussion

Overall EFE structure and l-Arg binding pattern

We identified two crystal structures of PsEFE: one for EFE:Fe:2OG at a 1.87-Å resolution, and another for EFE:Fe:2OG:l-Arg⁎ with a higher resolution of 1.66 Å. The overall structure of PsEFE comprises nine α-helices and eight β-strands, together with six major and two minor β-sheets that assemble into a double-stranded β-helix (a structural hallmark of the Fe(II)/2OG-dependent oxygenase superfamily). In the obtained structures, the active center contained only 2OG, with Fe(II) occupying the hexacoordination site involving His189, Asp191, His268, 2OG, and two water molecules (Fig. S5). Structural comparison of the two obtained structures revealed a high overall similarity (RMSD = 0.165 Å). However, a significant difference was observed in the position of the 2OG molecule. In EFE:Fe:2OG, the C2 keto and C1 carboxyl groups of 2OG interact with the guanidine of R277 via hydrogen bonding. By contrast, in EFE:Fe:2OG:l-Arg*, only the C1 carboxyl group of 2OG formed hydrogen bonds with R277 (Fig. S6).

Among the previously reported structures, no EFE structure contains only iron and 2OG at the active site; therefore, we compared EFE:Fe:2OG with EFE:Mn:2OG (PDB code: 5V2X) [25] and EFE:Mn:2OG (PDB code: 5MOF) [26]. We found that the overall structure of EFE:Fe:2OG exhibits higher similarity to that of EFE:Mn:2OG (PDB code: 5MOF; RMSD = 0.190 Å) than that of EFE:Mn:2OG (PDB code: 5V2X; RMSD = 0.263 Å), in which the orientation of the C-terminal α-helix is different (Fig. S7a). Regarding the active site in EFE:Mn:2OG (PDB code: 5MOF), the metal is in bidentate coordination with 2OG. In contrast, in EFE:Fe:2OG and EFE:Mn:2OG (PDB code: 5V2X), 2OG adopted a monodentate binding mode. Notably, the conformation of 2OG in EFE:Fe:2OG differed from that in the other two structures. In EFE:Fe:2OG, the C2 keto and C1 carboxyl groups interacted with R277. By contrast, in the other two structures, only the C1 carboxyl group formed hydrogen bonds with R277 (Fig. S7b-S7e).

Comparing EFE:Fe:2OG:l-Arg* and EFE:Fe:2OG:l-Arg (PDB code: 6VP4) [28], their overall structures differ significantly (RMSD = 0.505 Å), especially in several α-helices at the C-terminal (Fig. S8a). At the active site, 2OG adopts a monodentate binding mode in EFE:Fe:2OG:l-Arg* and a bidentate binding mode in EFE:Fe:2OG:l-Arg (PDB code: 6VP4). Iron, H189, D191, H268, and R171 all exhibited slight displacements. In addition, notable differences were observed between E84 and R316 (Fig. S8b-S8d). A further comparison of the two structures revealed an additional electron density near residue E213 on the protein surface in the presence of l-Arg (EFE:Fe:2OG:l-Arg*), which was absent in the structure without l-Arg (EFE:Fe:2OG) (Fig. 2a and Fig. S9). To further verify whether this site accommodates l-Arg, we performed computational docking of l-Arg to the site around E213 and confined the MD simulation to this region. Because the electron density for l‑Arg was not well-defined, molecular docking was used to generate a plausible initial placement near the observed density. This pose was adjusted and refined to achieve consistency with the electron density map. The calculated binding affinity of –10.6 kcal/mol suggests that l‑Arg binds favorably, and the predicted binding pose is highly consistent with the electron density distribution (Fig. 2b). Structure-guided surface engineering of EFE

Given that the binding pattern of l-Arg on the enzyme surface might affect the catalytic activity of EFE, we targeted this region for protein engineering. Subsequently, we systematically analyzed this region to decipher structure-activity relationships. We compiled the activities of previously reported EFE variants (Table S4) [[25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30], [31]], focusing on those located within the observed region of additional electron density (Fig. S10). Among these, the E213A variant exhibited moderately enhanced activity compared to the WT. This suggests that engineering modifications to the identified region can modulate catalytic activity. Additionally, mapping of the electrostatic potential of EFE revealed a predominantly negative electrostatic patch around the identified region (Fig. S11). This negatively electrostatic environment attracts the positively charged guanidinium group of l-Arg, potentially affecting the catalytic activity of EFE. Characterization of EFE variants regarding enhanced catalytic activity

Based on our structural and computational analyses, saturation mutagenesis was performed at position E213 and its neighboring residues Q31, K32, and Q262 (Fig. 4 and Table S5). At position Q31, substitution with arginine (R) increased the enzyme activity to 116.6 % of the WT level, whereas substitution with methionine (M) enhanced the activity to 129.8 %. Conversely, substitutions with other amino acids, including A, T, Y, W, L, and F, resulted in a substantial loss of activity. For K32, the substitution with tryptophan (W) resulted in an enhancement of the activity to 142.6 %, while the substitution with valine (V) led to an increase to 127.1 %. Most substitutions at K32 did not result in a significant reduction in ethylene formation compared to the WT. Regarding Q262, none of the substitutions resulted in a notable enhancement in ethylene-forming activity. Molecular mechanism for l-Arg with EFE

To study the influence of surface-position substitutions on l-Arg binding, we first performed a computational analysis that included the calculation of the binding affinities for specific variants. Compared to the WT with a predicted binding affinity of –10.6 kcal/mol, the E213C and E213T variants had binding affinities of –1.4 kcal/mol and –3.4 kcal/mol, respectively (Fig. S13). These results showed a substantial decrease in l-Arg-binding affinity at the identified position compared to that in the WT. This suggests that the E213 variants reduce the accumulation of l-Arg at the surface site, potentially allowing more arginine to enter the active site.

Second, to evaluate the accessibility of the ligands (l-Arg and 2OG) toward the catalytic center, classical MD simulations were performed by restraining the ligand-active site distance as the reaction coordinate, without explicitly modeling chemical bond formation. The resulting mean force (PMF) profiles (Fig. 6 and Fig. S14) reflect the free energy cost associated with bringing the ligand closer to the active site. Specifically, the WT system displayed a pronounced free energy maximum of 11.1 kcal mol⁻¹ at an intermediate distance of 7.7 Å, suggesting the presence of a substantial steric and/or electrostatic bottleneck that impedes ligand progression toward the catalytic center. In contrast, the E213T variant has a lower barrier of 7.2 kcal mol⁻¹ at a slightly more distal position of 8.5 Å, consistent with a partial relaxation of this barrier region. An even more substantial reduction is observed for E213C, which exhibits a minimal barrier of 6.5 kcal mol⁻¹ located at a shorter distance of 6.5 Å, together with consistently lower PMF values across the entire distance range. This behavior indicates that the E213C substitution not only lowers the energetic penalty for ligand entry but also stabilizes the intermediate states along the migration pathway. Among the variants, E213C consistently exhibited the lowest PMF values over the entire distance range, suggesting that this amino acid substitution most effectively alleviated steric and/or electrostatic hindrances during ligand entry.

These results indicate that amino acid substitutions at position E213 reduce the affinity of the surface l-Arg-binding site, allowing more l-Arg to be released into the active center. However, these variants alter the accessibility of ligands to the catalytic center, reducing the free-energy barriers associated with bringing the ligands (l-Arg and 2OG) into the active site, thereby potentially enhancing the catalytic activity.

Conclusions

In this study, we solved the two complex structures of the EFE from P. syringae pv. phaseolicola PK2. In the structure of the EFE:Fe:2OG:l-Arg* complex, we observed additional electron density near the residue E213 on the protein surface, which was absent in the structure without l-Arg (EFE:Fe:2OG). Subsequently, multiple computational analyses were performed to confirm that the extra density was consistent with an l-Arg molecule bound to the surface site. To analyze the binding pattern of l-Arg and the relationship between the identified region and catalytic activity, protein surface engineering was performed, and crucial positions were selected. Site-directed and saturation mutagenesis were used to build the library, and a variant (E213T) exhibited higher catalytic activity than the WT. Further MD simulations provided mechanistic insights into these catalytic improvements and revealed that the distal amino acid substitution reduced the affinity of the surface l-Arg binding site and altered the accessibility of the ligands to the catalytic center. These findings enhance our understanding of the relationship between distal sites and catalytic activity in EFE protein engineering.
