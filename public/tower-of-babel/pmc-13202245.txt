Meat flavor of Macrobrachium nipponense processing by gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry, electronic nose and metabolomics is changed by dietary ferulic acid
Authors: Xin Liu, Ling Zhu, Cunxin Sun, Yelin Hou, Yebing Yu, Xiaochuan Zheng, Bo Liu, Sufei Jiang, Yongquan Han, Bo Liu, Gangchun Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the effects of dietary ferulic acid (FA) on muscle flavor in Macrobrachium nipponense using electronic nose (E-nose), gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (GC-IMS), non-targeted metabolomics, and quantitative analysis of free amino acids and nucleotides. E-nose revealed significant alterations in key odor components (inorganic sulfur compounds, nitrogenous substances, short-chain alkanes and oxygenated volatiles). GC-IMS identified 32 volatiles, with FA supplementation driving a dynamic compositional shift: 23 compounds decreased while 8 increased. FA enhanced umami-associated metabolites—aspartic acid, glutamic acid, inosine monophosphate, and guanosine monophosphate, but suppressed bitter amino acids (isoleucine, leucine). Metabolomics further indicated accumulation of flavor intermediates (phenylglyoxal, guanosine). Pathway analysis indicates that the biosynthesis of alkaloids derived from ornithine, lysine, and nicotinic acid indirectly contributes to the regulation of muscle flavor by influencing amino acid metabolism. These results demonstrated that dietary FA mediates muscle flavor by reconstituting aroma profiles, volatile composition, and amino acid/nucleotide metabolism in M. nipponense.

Introduction

Macrobrachium nipponense (M. nipponense), a commercially significant freshwater prawn, was widely distributed throughout Asia (Gao et al., 2024). M. nipponense exhibited the health and a palatability highly prized by consumers. Flavor is the primary determinant of consumer approval (Haritha et al., 2023). However, with the expansion of intensive aquaculture, aquatic animals were exposed to a range of environmental stressors (e.g., high temperature, low oxygen, crowding, etc.) throughout the industrial chain. They may trigger stress responses, especially oxidative stress, affect muscle quality and lead to muscle quality loss and flavor deterioration (Zhang et al., 2023). Oxidative stress induced by various stresses has also been found to cause muscle quality loss and flavor deterioration in some studies (Kamal et al., 2025; Su et al., 2025). It has been shown that oxidative stress not only disrupts lipid metabolic homeostasis but also interferes with the metabolic pathways of amino acids and nucleotides (Xie et al., 2023), which in turn affected the accumulation of key flavor precursors (e.g., glutamate, inosinic acid) in muscle. Securing a sustainable future for the aquaculture industry, it was necessary to take measures to relieve oxidative stress-induced down-regulation of fish quality, notably through the implementation of dietary strategies (Abasubong et al., 2025). One study has shown that supplementing diets with certain phytoconstituents, such as polysaccharides and polyphenols, effectively addresses this problem (Ma et al., 2021). Therefore, developing green antioxidants to improve the muscle flavor of farmed prawns has become a key focus of aquafeed research.

Ferulic acid (FA), as a natural green phenolic acid antioxidant, has attracted much attention in recent years due to its scavenging of free radicals and inhibition of lipid peroxidation (Yu et al., 2020). The ability of FA to alleviate oxidized fish oil-induced gut flora disorders and lipid metabolism has been demonstrated (Liu, Sun, et al., 2024). A dietary mixture of FA and probiotics (Lactobacillus plantarum and Aspergillus oryzae) increased the content of flavor-enhancing constituents in Megalobrama amblycephala, especially flavor nucleotides, ketones, and esters (Chi et al., 2022). However, there was still a lack of systematic studies on the specific regulatory mechanisms of FA on prawn flavor substances, especially the synergistic effects on volatile flavor components (e.g., aldehydes, ketones) and non-volatile flavor-presenting substances (e.g., free amino acids, nucleotides) have not been clarified.

Food flavor-enhancing constituents mainly include non-volatile and volatile substances (Chen et al., 2022). The volatile flavor profile is predominantly comprised of aldehydes, ketones, esters, and alkanes, whereas the non-volatile fraction encompasses free amino acids, nucleotides, organic acids, and betaines. Among these, free amino acids and nucleotides play a dominant role in taste perception (Zhang et al., 2021). Traditional flavor analysis methods were limited in comprehensively elucidating the flavor formation mechanism in complex metabolic networks. To address this, the combination of multi-technologies provided an effective approach: E-nose technology enables the acquisition of a sample's overall flavor profile (Wei et al., 2023). Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry and Electronic nose complement each other, offering holistic insights into flavor profiles across both macro-volatile and micro-component dimensions (Jiang et al., 2024). Additionally, untargeted metabolomics facilitates the study of correlations between metabolites (e.g., free amino acids, nucleotides) and flavor, as well as the monitoring of key flavor precursors (Ma et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2022). The integration of these techniques can elucidate the mechanism by which FA improves muscle flavor at the molecular level, thereby filling a gap in the current study. This study aimed to address the insufficient muscle flavor accumulation in practical culture of M. nipponense, clarified the mechanism by which FA improved muscle flavor, and developed functional feed additives to enhance the muscle quality of M. nipponense, thereby increasing economic benefits in its commercial aquaculture.

Prawn management and sample collection

Prawns were obtained from Dapu Farm, which is affiliated with the Freshwater Fisheries Research Center, Chinese Academy of Fishery Sciences (Wuxi, China). After the prawns were fed a control diet for a 2-week acclimation, 300 prawns of similar size (0.140 ± 0.015 g) and weight (50 prawns per tank, and 3 tanks per group) were selected and fed the control and FA diets, respectively. After an 8-week feeding trial, the uniform size and healthy M. nipponense were selected. These individuals were subsequently rinsed with sterile water, swabbed with 75% ethanol for surface disinfection, and subjected to ice-water anesthesia for 5–10 min. All dissection procedures were performed on ice. The ventral abdominal carapace was surgically opened using sterile scissors. Subsequently, white muscle tissue was carefully excised from both lateral sides of the abdomen using forceps, ensuring complete avoidance of visceral contamination. Surface hemolymph and moisture were meticulously blotted dry with absorbent paper before sectioning. Finally, the muscle samples were aliquoted into appropriately labeled sterile cryogenic vials and − 20 °C fresh storage was used to determine the E-nose, and − 80 °C cryopreservation was used to determine other muscle-related metrics such as the untargeted metabolome.

Electronic nose measurement

Muscle samples (three repetitions per group) accurately weighed (3.00 g) and were placed into 60 mL headspace vials and incubated at 65 °C for 50 min before starting the assay. The aroma profiles of three muscle samples were analyzed using an electronic nose (Airsen PEN3, Analytic Company GmbH, Schwerin, Germany) equipped with ten metal-oxide-semiconductor sensors to differentiate their volatile compositions. The E-nose operating parameters were set as follows: 120 s analysis time, 200 mL/min injection flow rate, 200 s sensor cleaning, 10 s zero adjustment, and 5 s preparation time. Accordingly, the stable signal segment from 110 to 118 s was selected for data acquisition and pattern recognition via the software (WinMuster PEN) (Chen et al., 2024).

GC-IMS measurement

Volatile components in the muscle were analyzed using a FlavourSpec® Flavour Analyser (Gesellschaft für analytische Sensorsysteme mbH, Dortmund, Germany) with analytical support from Shandong Hai Neng Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China). For sample preparation, 2 g of muscle (two prawns per tank, six repetitions per group) was placed in 20 mL headspace vials, incubated at 60 °C for 15 min with agitation (500 rpm), and 500 μL of the headspace gas was automatically injected for detection. The analysis was performed with an MXT-5 (Restek, USA) capillary column (15 m × 0.53 mm ID, 1 μm FT) held at 60 °C, high-purity N₂ as the carrier gas, and an ion mobility spectrometry (IMS) module maintained at 45 °C; the injection needle temperature was set at 85 °C, with a total analysis duration of 20 min. Data acquisition and processing for volatile compounds were conducted using the VOCal, Reporter Plugin and Gallery Plot Plugin of the instrument's software system.

Analysis of free amino acids (FAAs)

Free amino acids in the muscle tissues of M. nipponense (three repetitions per group) were extracted following the method established by Chen et al. (2022), and subsequent amino acid analysis was conducted via Agilent 1100 liquid chromatography (Agilent Technologies Inc., California, USA) combined with OPA-FMOC pre-column derivatization. Chromatographic separation was performed on a 5 μm ODS HYPERSIL column (250 × 4.6 mm) at a constant temperature of 40 °C, with a mobile phase flow rate set at 1.0 mL/min. Mobile phase A consisted of ultrapure water-based sodium acetate solution (6.5 g/L) supplemented with triethylamine (225 μL/L) and tetrahydrofuran (5 mL/L), adjusted to pH 7.20 ± 0.05 with 5% acetic acid; mobile phase B was prepared by mixing sodium acetate aqueous solution (6.5 g/L, pH 7.20 ± 0.05 adjusted with 2% acetic acid) and a mixed organic solvent of acetonitrile and methanol (1:1, v/v) at a volume ratio of 1:4. A UV detector was used for detection at a wavelength of 338 nm (for Pro and Hypro), and the quantitative determination of all amino acids was achieved by the external standard method.

Taste activity value (TAV) was calculated based on the threshold value of each free amino acid, is positively correlated with its perceived flavor intensity. When TAV > 1, it means that the flavor-presenting amino acid contributes to the flavor presentation, while when TAV < 1, the flavor-presenting amino acid does not contribute much to the flavor presentation and the flavor-presenting effect is not significant (Kong et al., 2017).

Analysis of 5′-nucleotides

5′-nucleotides from the muscle samples of M. nipponense (three repetitions per group) were extracted following the method of Chen et al. (2022), with subsequent qualitative and quantitative analysis conducted using an Agilent 1260 Liquid Chromatograph (Agilent Technologies Inc., California, USA). Chromatographic separation was carried out on a Diamonsil C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm) at a constant temperature of 30 °C, with a UV detector set at 254 nm for detection; the chromatographic system was operated at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min with a 5 μL sample injection volume. The mobile phase comprised two methanol-water-phosphoric acid mixtures with different proportions: Phase A was formulated as 50:950:0.5 (v/v/v) and Phase B as 800:200:0.5 (v/v/v).

Metabolomic analysis

Muscle samples (three repetitions per group) were prepared from control and FA groups of M.nipponense, 150 mg of each group was weighed and three replicates of each group were processed for metabolite extraction to obtain the metabolite supernatant according to the method of Zheng, Xu, et al. (2024). Aliquots (50 μL) of the supernatant from each sample were pooled to constitute the quality control sample. Following this, all samples were stored at −80 °C and later subjected to Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry analysis.

Metabolite separation was performed on a Thermo UHPLC-Q Exactive Mass Spectrometer (Q Exactive Orbitrap, Thermo Fisher Scientific, California, USA) equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (100 mm * 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm, column temperature 50 °C, Waters, Cheshire, UK). The Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography (UPLC) system was interfaced with a high-resolution tandem mass spectrometry Xevo G2-XS QTOF (Waters, UK) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source operated in both positive mode (pos mode) and negative mode (neg mode). Key instrumental parameters were optimized as follows: a capillary voltage of 3.5 kV, a desolvent gas temperature of 425 °C, a cone gas flow rate of 50 L/h and a desolvation gas flow rate of 800 L/h. Metabolite detection was conducted over a mass range starting at m/z 50 Da, with high-resolution mode (>50,000 FWHM) and a mass accuracy of better than 1 ppm employed to ensure precise metabolite identification; collision energy in MS/MS mode was set to a dynamically adjustable range of 5–40 eV for fragmentation analysis.

Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal volumes of all experimental samples and analyzed periodically (every 10 samples) throughout the analytical run to monitor instrument stability and data repeatability. The reliability of the LC-MS/MS method was validated by evaluating the relative standard deviation (RSD) of metabolite peak intensities in QC samples. Metabolites with RSD < 30% in QC samples were considered stable and reliable for subsequent data analysis. No significant batch effects were observed, and the analytical system remained stable throughout the entire run, as evidenced by consistent peak intensities and low RSD values in QC samples.

The raw data were filtered using a self-developed perl program, with the following criteria: removal of data with no identifiable substance name and no spectral similarity, direct filtering of substances with greater than 50% missing in the comparison pairs, and use of the K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) algorithm for simulation of missing values in the raw data imported into the Data Mining with R (DMwR) package (v 3.6.2, R ≥ 2.10). Peak alignment, extraction, identification and data correction were performed to obtain retention times, mass-to-charge ratios and ion peak areas of metabolites. Data were normalized using the sample's internal standard (IS) or the sample's total ion current (TIC) using a self-developed perl program.

The pre-processed data were annotated against the Human Metabolome Database (v5.0) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) COMPOUND databases (Release 108.0) using a custom perl programme.

Statistical analysis

The raw data processing was performed using R software (v 3.6.2) (R ≥ 2.10). Differential metabolites (DMs) were identified based on thresholds of p-value <0.05 and a variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1.0 from the Orthogonal Partial Least Squares-discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) model (Zheng, Xu, et al., 2024), Model quality was evaluated using the cumulative R2X (explained variation in X matrix), R2Y (explained variation in Y matrix), and Q2(cum) (predictive ability) derived from seven-fold cross-validation. The model was further validated by 200 permutation tests to avoid overfitting. For comparison, unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) was performed prior to OPLS-DA to confirm inherent clustering without supervised modeling bias. The E-nose response values, values of FAAs, and nucleotides which were presented as mean ± standard error (M ± SE). All data were subjected to an independent sample t-test. In this study, for multiple comparisons, the false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, with significance set at adjusted P < 0.05. GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA), and the Tutools platform (www.cloudtutu.com), a free online data analysis website, were used for data visualization.

Results and discussion

Electronic nose analysis

Fig. 1A and Table S1 showed a radar plot of the response values of the 10 sensors of the electronic nose when fed different diets. There were some differences in the odor profiles of the different samples in the graph. Among the 10 sensors, 5 sensors (W1S, W1W, W2S, W2W and W5S) were found to have prominently different response values (p < 0.05, VIP > 1). The response values of FA group on W1S, W1W, W2S, W2W and W5S probes were prominently higher than those of CT group (P < 0.05). These response values reflect the sensors' ability to distinguish muscle aromas, meaning the five sensors can better differentiate aroma substances between the two groups based on their differential response values. Similar studies found that W1W, W1S, W2S and W5S sensors were associated with changes in prawn quality and were used as the primary sensors for detecting freshness in Penaeus vannamei (Liu, Zhang, et al., 2024). The sensor array exhibited distinct selectivity: W2W is sensitive to organic sulfur compounds, W1W primarily responds to inorganic sulfur compounds, W5S detects small molecular nitrogen oxides, W1S targets short-chain alkanes (e.g., methane), and W2S identifies alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, and ethers (Song et al., 2024). In this study, it was observed that the differences in muscle aroma between the CT and FA groups were mainly due to inorganic sulfur, nitrogen compounds, short-chain alkanes and volatiles such as ethers/aldehydes/alcohols/ketones. The OPLS-DA was a method for visualizing and quantifying sample variation using correlations within the data (Kang et al., 2022). The appealing sensor may have a greater contribution to the OPLS-DA model of this experiment. The OPLS-DA model showed satisfactory explanatory and predictive power: R2X = 0.84, R2Y = 0.964, Q2(cum) = 0.886. The results showed that the CT and FA groups were more clustered within the group, with the intergroups located in the positive and negative semiaxes of the x-axis, as shown in Fig. 1C. There was a good differentiation of volatile compositions between the two diets in the OPLS-DA scoring plots, indicating that the CT group and FA group had significant differences in odor substances. Since the electronic nose technique could not confirm the specific aroma components in each sample, to further explore the aroma components of the meat and the different substances among different diets samples, the volatile substances of different diets of meat samples were further analyzed by the GC-IMS technique.

To thoroughly examine the flavor and aroma attributes of prawn muscle, a comparison of the IMS and National Institute of Standards and Technology databases within the VOCal program was conducted, with the qualitative analysis of each volatile chemical in the CT and FA groups presented in Table S2. 32 compounds were identified in total, and the carbon chains of these compounds were generally concentrated between C4-C9, including 14 aldehydes, 6 ketones, 6 alcohols, 4 esters, and 2 acids, with the most aldehydes detected. Aldehydes play a pivotal role in shaping the overall flavor profile of animal meat (Dong et al., 2024). Most aldehydes possess a low scent threshold for fat, including the saturated linear aldehydes hexanal, heptanal, and nonanal. When the concentration beyond a specific threshold, it generates a sour flavor and other disagreeable odors (Wang et al., 2021). Of course, other aldehydes (such as trans-2-hexenal) can have a positive effect on the desired fruity or fresh aromatic notes when used at appropriate concentrations (Shan et al., 2024). As can be seen in Fig. 2E, the hexanal (monomer) and 2-the hexanal content (the leftmost column in the figure) did not differ prominently (P > 0.05) between the two groups of muscle samples. And as shown in the red dashed box, hexanal (dimer), 2-methylbutyraldehyde, isovaleraldehyde, octanal, benzaldehyde (monomer and dimer), cyclohexanone, heptanal, nonanal, hexanal, propionic acid, furfural, methyl heptenone, 3-methylthiopropanal, isovaleral, and methyl valerate (dimer), 2-butanone (monomer and dimer), sec-octanol, sec-octanone, butyl propionate, 2,3-butanedione, trans-2-pentenal, and methyl isobutanemethanol were obviously higher in CT than the FA group (P < 0.05). The levels of n-pentanoic acid, trans-2-hexenal, methyl isovalerate (monomer), ethyl butyrate, benzyl alcohol, 2-hexanol, glutaraldehyde, and isopentanol in FA were obviously higher than those in the CT group (P < 0.05). Among these, trans-2-hexenal and several esters contributed to pleasant, fresh, and fruity aromas (Kamatou et al., 2008). In contrast, other GC-IMS analyses showed a significant increase in volatile compounds such as esters (e.g., ethyl acetate) aldehydes (e.g., hexanal) and alcohols (e.g., isoamyl alcohol) (Battur et al., 2025). In the present study, dietary FA did not simply cause a universal decrease in aldehydes and ketones, but rather modulated the profile of volatile compounds. It reduced the relative abundance of many off-flavor-related aldehydes and ketones, while maintaining or elevating several compounds associated with desirable aroma. Therefore, dietary FA may optimize the volatile composition toward a more favorable flavor profile in the muscle of M. nipponense.

FAAs and nucleotides

FAAs are the main flavor substances of prawns and have a unique taste. Amino acids exhibit a spectrum of taste characteristics, broadly classified into fresh, sweet, bitter, aromatic and other amino acid taste characteristics (Zhang et al., 2021). Overall, the sensory profile of prawn meat is influenced by the interplay between the identities, levels, and detection thresholds of its taste-active components. The free amino acid content, threshold value, flavor presentation characteristics, TAV and P-value of different diets consumed are shown in Table 2. For TAV > 1, the main taste-active FAAS observed to contribute to the flavor of prawn muscles consuming the different diets were Glycine (Gly), Alanine (Ala), Histidine (His) and Arginine (Arg). Gly was markedly higher in the FA group than in the CT group (p < 0.05). At the same time, Arg was significantly lower than in the CT group (p < 0.01). Previous studies have found that Gly and Ala have a positive effect on freshness and sweetness (Dong et al., 2013). Arg, Met, Ile, Val, and Leu, known for their bitter notes, are associated with undesirable flavors that degrade the sensory quality of aquatic products (Nie et al., 2025). The results showed that the addition of FA to diets significantly altered the flavor substances in muscle, particularly by increasing muscle freshness and reducing bitterness. Similar results were found in the study of the M.amblycephala. Table 2 also showed that the total TAV values for umami, sweetness, and bitterness exceeded 1, indicating that FA addition enhances the perception of amino acids responsible for umami, sweetness, and bitterness in muscle. The total TAV value for umami in the FA group was significantly higher than that in the control group, while the total TAV value for bitter amino acids was significantly lower than that in the control group (p < 0.01). This also indicated that adding FA significantly improved the flavor profile of the muscle, such as enhancing freshness and reducing the accumulation of bitterness in the muscle.

Also, for amino acids with TAV values less than 1, the analysis revealed that the addition of FA significantly increased aspartic acid (Asp) and glutamic acid (Glu) (p < 0.05) and significantly decreased isoleucine (Ile) and leucine (Leu) (p < 0.01). Some relevant studies have found that in aquatic products, Asp and Glu were characterized as typical umami amino acids (UFAAs), demonstrating a potent synergistic effect with umami (Moerdijk-Poortvliet et al., 2022). These results also provided some support for the idea that FA improves muscle amino acid composition.

Flavor nucleotides, as key non-volatile components in fish muscle, engage in synergistic interactions with flavor amino acids to enhance the overall flavor profile (Liu et al., 2018). Fig. 3 has shown the nucleotide content in the muscle of M. nipponense consuming different diets, with a decrease in AMP and UMP, a significant decrease in CMP, and a significant increase in IMP and GMP content after the addition of FA. Following death, nucleotides in fish degrade rapidly through the ATP → IMP → Hx pathway. Among these derivatives, 5′-IMP, 5′-AMP, and 5′-GMP are identified as the principal compounds responsible for the characteristic fresh taste (Mendes et al., 2001). While AMP exhibits a weakly directed impact on muscle flavor, its depletion is attributable to metabolic interconversions—notably into IMP. This biochemical transformation synergizes with other umami constituents, culminating in a significant enhancement of the overall muscular flavor profile. IMP is one of the major nucleotides that determines muscle flavor in aquatic animals, it contributes significantly to freshness and acts synergistically with freshness amino acids (e.g., glutamate) (Manninen et al., 2018). By analyzing the free amino acids, combined with the nucleotide content, it was concluded that the addition of FA improves IMP synergistically with the fresh amino acids and contributes significantly to muscle flavor. Although GMP contributes a distinctive mushroom-like flavor, its overall impact on fish flavor is significantly less than that of IMP, which can be attributed to its relatively lower concentration (Du et al., 2022). In this study, both IMP and GMP were significantly increased by the addition of FA, indicating that FA contributes significantly to muscle flavor. Similar results were found in the study of Triploid Crucian Carp (Carassius auratus), where the addition of Schizandra chinensis with antioxidant function significantly increased the IMP and GMP content of fish and improved muscle flavor (Zuo et al., 2023). Metabolomic analysis

The RSD values of metabolites in QC samples ranged from 20.5% to 30.5%, with an average RSD of 25.0%, demonstrating good stability and reliability of the LC-MS/MS analysis. After metabolite analysis, a total of 746 metabolites were identified in the muscle of M. nipponense from different dietary groups, with 508 detected in positive mode, 188 in negative mode, and 50 in both modes (Fig. 4A). These metabolites were classified into 16 categories, among which lipids and lipid-like molecules, organic acids and their derivatives, organoheterocyclic compounds, and organic oxygen compounds were the most abundant (Fig. 4B). Similarly, relevant studies have found that ingestion of FA modulates several host metabolic pathways, including the Tricarboxylic acid cycle, bile acid, energy metabolism, amino acid and nucleic acid metabolism (Zhang et al., 2018). We used OPLS-DA to characterize the changes in muscle metabolites of M. nipponense fed with different dietary regimens. The OPLS-DA model showed satisfactory explanatory and predictive power: R2X = 0.928, R2Y = 0.996, Q2(cum) = 0.933(positive ion); R2X = 0.854, R2Y = 0.965, Q2(cum) = 0.903 (negative ion). According to KEGG pathway analysis, microbial metabolism in different environments was significantly affected with the biosynthetic pathways of ornithine, lysine and nicotinic acid alkaloids. Ornithine, lysine and nicotinic acid-derived alkaloids all used amino acids or their metabolic intermediates as precursors, and their synthesis process was closely related to protein metabolism (Chu et al., 2023). This metabolic pathway was closely related to the catabolism of amino acids, which is one of the main factors affecting the flavor of muscle. Changes in the abundance of metabolites associated with the synthesis of ornithine, lysine and nicotinic acid-derived alkaloids (Fig. S2) (Chu et al., 2023) resulted in a significant increase in l-glutamic acid in the FA group, which has been shown to play a key role in muscle growth and the development of meat aroma and sweetness in broiler chickens (Emadi et al., 2011), suggesting that FA can indirectly regulate amino acid metabolism through this pathway to influence muscle flavor.

Conclusions

In summary, electronic nose and GC-IMS analyses collectively demonstrated that FA altered the composition of muscle volatile compounds, reshaping the aroma profile by regulating key classes of volatiles (aldehydes, alcohols, ketones, sulfur-containing compounds, and nitrogenous substances) rather than changing the overall types of aroma components. The free amino acid and nucleotide results showed that FA modulated free amino acid and nucleotide metabolism, enhancing umami and sweet taste by increasing the levels of key taste-active components while reducing bitter amino acids. Metabolomic analysis further revealed that FA-mediated flavor improvement was indirectly driven by the regulation of core metabolic pathways (ornithine, lysine, and nicotinic acid alkaloid biosynthesis) and key metabolites, which modulated amino acid metabolism to shape muscle flavor. These results elucidated the mechanisms by which FA improved muscle flavor in M. nipponense. The findings were expected to facilitate the development of functional feed additives to enhance muscle quality, address insufficient muscle flavor accumulation in practical culture of this species, and thereby promote economic benefits in commercial aquaculture.
