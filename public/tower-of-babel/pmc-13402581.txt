Explainable zero-day attack detection in IoMT using transformer-based time-series modeling
Authors: Mahdee Jodayree, Arman Kavoosi Ghafi, Sara Amiri, Puya Shaykholeslami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSecuring Internet of Things (IoT) and Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) networks necessitates intrusion detection systems (IDS) robust against severe class imbalance, zero-day attacks, and deployment constraints. Prior methodologies often rely on heavy feature engineering, stacking ensembles, or data augmentation for rebalancing; yet they remain inherently closed-set and brittle across heterogeneous datasets, thereby limiting calibrated performance in real-world environments. To overcome these limitations, we propose a paradigm shift toward protocol-aware sequence modeling and principled open-set calibration. We introduce a Protocol State-Masked, Multi-scale Transformer that directly incorporates finite-state protocol constraints into the self-attention mechanism. This core architecture is augmented by two auxiliary heads (next-state prediction and violation scoring) and an energy-based open-set module calibrated using Extreme Value Theory. To rigorously verify generalization rather than simple memorization, we train the model on a heterogeneous IoMT benchmark (CICIoMT2024) and evaluate it on a completely held-out, independent testbed (WUSTL-EHMS-2020). On CICIoMT2024, the model achieves 99.62% accuracy and 99.30% macro-F1 (binary classification), and 98.90% accuracy with 98.40% macro-F1 (19-class classification). In zero-day settings, it attains an AUROC-OOD of 0.975 with an FPR@95%TPR of 7.8%. Crucially, on the unseen WUSTL-EHMS-2020 dataset, the model demonstrates strong cross-dataset transfer, reaching ≈ 99.6% overall accuracy with a balanced TPR/TNR of ≈ 99.6%. Ablation studies confirm that both the protocol masking and the auxiliary heads significantly sharpen decision boundaries, substantially reduce false alarms, and improve minority-class balance when compared to data-only Transformer variants and conventional ensembles. Our design effectively mitigates imbalance (via multi-resolution windows, class-aware objectives, and protocol-conditioned learning) and curbs overfitting (through structural supervision and calibrated open-set thresholds), delivering a calibrated, high-fidelity detection system that generalizes across diverse datasets while remaining practical for deployment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50813-7.Keywords: Internet of Things, Internet of Medical Things, Network Intrusion Detection, Open-set Detection, Protocol-aware TransformersSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Internet-connected devices in Internet of Things (IoT) and Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) have surged in scale and heterogeneity, pushing network defenses into safety-critical contexts and tight edge budgets1,2. Recent journal surveys highlight systemic exposure across device–network–cloud layers and underscore the rising cost of missed detections in medical and industrial settings2, while state-of-the-art intrusion detection system (IDS) reviews emphasize the widening gap between risk and deployable performance at the edge3. In parallel, edge or Multi-access Edge Computing (MAEC) surveys chart a shift from cloud-centric analytics to near-sensor processing to curb latency and bandwidth pressures—constraints that fundamentally shape network intrusion detection system (NIDS) design4–6.

We consider an online, network-based IDS for multi-protocol IoMT that operates at packet/flow granularity, must generalize across datasets/protocols, and crucially handle open-set or out-of-distribution (OOD) traffic. The system targets millisecond-level per-flow latency, modest memory/parameter budgets, and deployment on gateways or edge nodes. Edge-AI and MAEC literature consistently reports that meeting such latency or Quality of Service (QoS) targets requires pushing learning and inference to the edge, with careful resource orchestration—assumptions we build into our evaluation protocol and design4–6.

Data-level rebalancing (e.g., SMOTE families and modern stream-imbalance methods) can lift minority-class recall, yet suffers from split sensitivity, potential boundary leakage, and weak protocol awareness7–9. Cross-dataset transfer remains underreported, and pipelines grounded in heavy feature selection often struggle to port across traffic regimes1. More fundamentally, many deep IDS are trained/assessed in closed-set regimes, with calibrated OOD metrics (AUROC-OOD, FPR@95%TPR) seldom reported—despite their centrality in robust deployment10–12.

We encode protocol semantics via finite state constraints into a multi-scale attention backbone (masking and biasing attention by legal dialog transitions), aligning representation learning with message-sequence rules observed in industrial control systems and IoT networks13,14. For open-world operation, we pair the classifier with energy-based and outlier exposure-based scoring, together with tail-calibrated decision thresholds inspired by contemporary out of distribution and open set recognition literature, which shows strong empirical traction for calibrated rejection under distributional shift10,11,15. This combination aims to preserve macro-averaged F1 score on known classes while reliably filtering off-manifold traffic.

We investigate three research questions: first, whether protocol-aware masking in attention improves multi-class macro-F1 under the same latency and parameter budget compared with data-only baselines; second, whether energy-based scoring with extreme-value-theory-style tail calibration can sustain high AUROC-OOD with low FPR@95%TPR in leave-one-attack-out settings; and third, whether the system can uphold near-real-time throughput under edge-realistic constraints without sacrificing detection quality.

Our contributions are threefold: (1) we introduce protocol-aware multi-scale attention that incorporates finite-state protocol priors to enforce legal message progressions, closing a gap highlighted in recent IDS surveys1,2; (2) we provide an open-set calibration module that couples high-accuracy classification with calibrated OOD scoring (energy and outlier-exposure family) plus tail modeling, aligning with recommendations in recent OOD/OSR journal surveys10–12,15; and (3) we conduct a hard evaluation that reports accuracy and macro-F1 on two heterogeneous IoMT datasets alongside OOD metrics (AUROC-OOD, FPR@95%TPR) and explicit edge-time/size profiles, consistent with best-practice evaluation checklists.

Empirically, our protocol-aware model maintains competitive accuracy and macro-F1 across heterogeneous datasets while delivering calibrated OOD performance (high AUROC-OOD, low FPR@95%TPR) under tight edge budgets—demonstrating that semantics-aware attention, paired with principled OOD calibration, can close the risk–performance gap without resorting to brittle feature-engineering pipelines. Our novelty and contributions are as follows:

(1) We introduce a zero-day open-set IDS formulation for IoMT flows, where the model jointly performs closed-set classification and unknown/novel attack rejection, rather than assuming a fixed set of attack types.

(2) We propose PSM-HT, a unified pipeline that couples hierarchical/multi-resolution temporal encoding with protocol/state-aware masking (FSM masking) to better capture IoMT communication structure and suppress spurious correlations in long sequences.

(3) We incorporate an EVT-based calibration layer tailored to the open-set setting, enabling principled thresholding with improved control of FPR@95%TPR under distribution shift.

(4) We provide a comprehensive evaluation protocol (binary, 19-class, and zero-day open-set) and report robust performance and efficiency in an IoMT gateway setting, including latency and parameter budget.

Related work

Recent IDS research clusters into five strands: (1) augmentation-centric methods (e.g., GAN/SMOTE) that rebalance skewed traffic; (2) deep temporal/convolutional models (CNN/LSTM) targeting sequence patterns; (3) feature-selection with classical or boosted learners; (4) stacked/heterogeneous ensembles that trade computation for accuracy; and (5) data-centric frameworks that benchmark defenses and pipelines. Most studies validate on two datasets—often UNSW-NB15, CICIDS/CSE-CIC-IDS2018, NSL-KDD, BoT-IoT, ToN-IoT, or CICIoT2023—reporting Accuracy/F1 (sometimes TPR/FPR). Below, we synthesize representative works16–40 to situate our contribution.

Chu and Lin16 introduced an MLP-based IDS enhanced by GAN-driven augmentation to upsample minority attack classes. Evaluated on BoT-IoT and ToN-IoT, it attained 98.83% and 98.53% accuracy with F1 scores of 96.42 and 97.36, respectively, evidencing robust cross-dataset generalization. The approach markedly improves recall for rare classes; however, GAN training brings non-trivial computational overhead and tuning effort.

Rao and Suresh Babu17 designed an imbalanced-GAN (IGAN) pipeline coupled with LeNet-5 and LSTM for multi-class NIDS under severe skew. On UNSW-NB15 and CICIDS2017, they reported 98.96% and 98.02% accuracy, with TPRs of 95.77% and 96.13% at low FPR (1.15%/0.76%), indicating strong sensitivity to attacks. That said, gains depend on data splits and IGAN configuration.

Yaras and Dener18 developed a hybrid CNN–LSTM with feature selection and cross-domain validation to strengthen transferability. Using CICIoT2023 and ToN-IoT, the model achieved 99.995% and 98.75% (binary) accuracy, respectively, highlighting notable generalization. The study concentrates on DDoS scenarios, and deployment details for edge hardware remain limited.

Ajagbe et al.19 conducted a comprehensive comparison of ML models under class imbalance across CICIDS2017 and UNSW-NB15. They reported up to 100% accuracy on CICIDS2017 and 98.9% on UNSW-NB15, with F1 scores of 98.9 and 97.8; however, FAR on CICIDS was higher (6.38%). The breadth of metrics is valuable, though results are sensitive to configuration choices.

Hossain and Islam20 presented a robustness-oriented IDS that couples targeted feature selection with pragmatic modeling. On NSL-KDD and CICIDS2017, the system delivered 83.58% accuracy (recall 84.49%) and ≈ 97% accuracy, respectively, suggesting practical effectiveness. Performance on NSL-KDD is moderate, which tempers the overall gains.

Ghani, Virdee, and Salekzamankhani21 built a lightweight deep model using a compact feature vector and a streamlined cleaning pipeline for binary detection. Tested on UNSW-NB15 and NSL-KDD, it yielded 91.29% and 89.03% accuracy, favoring efficiency and explainability. Nonetheless, accuracy lags behind state-of-the-art ensembles and the evaluation focuses on binary settings.

Farhan et al.22 established a DNN baseline for network-based IDS and benchmarked it on UNSW-NB15 and NSL-KDD. The approach reported 89.85% and 88.95% accuracy, offering a reproducible reference point for later work. While methodologically clean, it trails more recent deep/ensemble architectures.

Bella et al.23 introduced DNDF-IDS, an ensemble that blends CNN-style feature learning with decision-forest mechanisms. Across CICIDS2017 and UNSW-NB15, reported accuracies span 94.09–99.83%, balancing predictive strength and computational cost. Some outcomes are presented as ranges rather than fine-grained per-class metrics.

Sinha et al.24 advanced a data-centric framework that integrates adversarial defenses with optimization across classical and deep models. On CICIDS2017 and UNSW-NB15, accuracies vary (~ 65–92%) by model and setting, providing a broad comparative lens. The scope is instructive, yet results are scattered and not uniformly optimal.

Chinnasamy et al.25 examined deep learning–driven NIDS methods and reported high detection rates on canonical benchmarks. Using NSL-KDD and UNSW-NB15, the study cites DRs of 98.22% and 99.68%, underscoring strong sensitivity to attacks. Compute-cost and deployment specifics, however, are sparsely described.

Ullah et al.26 devised a multi-model framework for IoT botnet detection and validated it on BoT-IoT and CICIoT2023. The system reached 100% and 99.2% accuracy, offering compelling evidence on contemporary IoT traffic. Even so, potential sensitivity to label inconsistencies warrants caution in broader deployments.

Imran et al.27 formulated an ensemble that fuses “prediction and learning” via Kalman-filter-assisted combination. On UNSW-NB15 and CICIDS2017, they reported 98.801% and 97.02% accuracy, reflecting cross-dataset robustness. The novel fusion is promising, though the pipeline is complex and depends on AutoML tuning.

Al-Turaiki and Altwaijry28 presented a CNN with Deep Feature Synthesis, offering both binary and multi-class variants. On NSL-KDD and UNSW-NB15, binary accuracy reached ≈ 83.28%/95.71%, while multi-class dropped to ≈ 81.29%/≈80.51%, respectively. Thus, the framework is effective for binary UNSW but degrades in multi-class settings.

Kasongo29 implemented an LSTM-based IDS optimized for sequence modeling in network traffic. Evaluated on NSL-KDD and UNSW-NB15, the method achieved 80.52% and 88.11% accuracy, indicating solid baseline behavior. However, it falls short of contemporary state-of-the-art models in raw accuracy.

Song et al.30 engineered CSK-CNN, combining Cluster-SMOTE, K-means, and a two-layer CNN to alleviate imbalance. On UNSW-NB15 and CICIDS2017, multi-class accuracy attained 95.48% and 99.80% (overall 98.77%/99.91%), showing a strong balance between recall and precision. The gains come with the cost of clustering and threshold-tuning complexity.

Urmi et al.31 assembled a stacked ensemble that meta-learns over multiple base classifiers after feature selection. Using UNSW-NB15, CICIDS2017, and NSL-KDD, they reported accuracy up to ~ 99.3% with FAR ≈ 0.5%, indicating stability. Nonetheless, the paper provides limited reporting of training time and compute budget.

Nassreddine et al.32 employed a stacking ensemble with a logistic meta-learner, hybrid feature selection (correlation + embedded XGBoost), and SMOTE–Tomek balancing. On NSL-KDD and CIC-IDS2017, they achieved 99.92% and 99.99% accuracy with AUC near 1.0 using ~ 10 features. Such performance is compelling, yet it relies on a substantial preprocessing and tuning chain.

Alsaffar et al.33 crafted a multi-stage design: LogitBoost+XGBRegressor for feature selection, ResNet for feature extraction, followed by multi-stacking. On UNSW-NB15 and CICIDS2017, multi-class accuracies were 90.29% and 99.69%, respectively. The integrated FS/FE/ensemble stack excels on CICIDS, though UNSW performance is comparatively lower.

Rajagopal et al.34 explored a stacking ensemble with heterogeneous base learners and assessed generalization across heterogeneous datasets. On UGR’16 and UNSW-NB15, they reported 97% and 94% accuracy, respectively, indicating transfer from real traffic to simulated settings. Even so, the study emphasizes accuracy over broader metrics and underperforms on UNSW.

Abbas et al.35 advanced a hybrid ensemble with model selection and optimization, benchmarking on multiple datasets. On NSL-KDD, UNSW-NB15, and CSE-CIC-IDS2018, the method achieved ≈ 99%, 98.53%, and 99.9% accuracy, respectively, with low FAR. Generalization remains linked to optimization choices and dataset characteristics.

In parallel, recent 2025 works in ICS/SCADA-oriented smart-grid settings emphasize privacy-preserving defenses and protocol-specific threat modeling. Dokku et al.36 report a resilient pipeline over IEC 60870-5-104 traffic that couples IDS modeling with BLAKE3-driven dynamic key rotation for encryption hardening. Mrudula et al.37 propose an IEC 60870-5-104 IDS that combines feature optimization with privacy protection and honeypot–firewall integration for proactive mitigation. Pallakonda et al.38 integrate ML-based anomaly detection and temporal modeling with cryptographic privacy protection for ICS communication, while Gopinath et al.39 present a comprehensive BESS-enabled smart-grid framework that merges cyber threat classification with cryptographic and operational resilience measures. Finally, Vaasudevan et al.40 develop a holistic DNP3 security framework spanning detection, classification, and privacy-preserving mechanisms.

Prior IDS studies can be broadly grouped into (1) classical feature-based ML, (2) CNN/RNN sequence models, and (3) transformer-based architectures for flow/packet sequences. Across these strands, three limitations persist: (a) most systems are closed-set, lacking principled open-set/OOD detection and rarely reporting calibrated metrics such as FPR@95%TPR; (b) protocol semantics are often ignored, making cross-protocol generalization—especially in IoMT—fragile and sometimes propped up by resampling (e.g., SMOTE/IGAN) with potential leakage; and (c) latency/compute footprints are under-reported, complicating deployment at gateways or edge devices. Even in protocol- and privacy-aware ICS/SCADA frameworks36–40, evaluation is commonly framed as closed-set detection/classification, leaving calibrated open-set recognition for unseen or evolving attacks underexplored. In contrast, our work directly targets these gaps by formulating zero-day detection as an open-set problem for IoMT flows, integrating protocol/state-aware (FSM) masking, applying EVT-based calibration to control false alarms under distribution shift, and explicitly reporting efficiency indicators (latency and parameter budget) under a unified evaluation across binary, multi-class, and open-set settings.

Our method addresses these gaps by injecting protocol knowledge via Finite State Machine (FSM)-masked, multi-scale Transformer attention, adding auxiliary heads (next-state prediction and protocol-violation scoring) to regularize learning, and performing open-set recognition with energy-based scoring calibrated by EVT, preferably per-protocol. Under leave-one-attack-out on CICIoMT-style data, it reaches ≈ 99.6% binary accuracy and ≈ 98.4% macro-F1 (19-class), while attaining AUROC-OOD ≈ 0.975 with FPR@95%TPR ≈ 7.8% and sustaining 0.9–1.5 ms/flow at ~ 20 M parameters—demonstrating substantially better robustness and deployability than augmentation- or stacking-centric baselines.

Proposed method

Problem setup and datasets

We cast zero-day detection and multi-class intrusion classification in IoMT as supervised learning on multivariate time-series windows. Each sample is a sequence created from packet/flow telemetry (and vitals when available). The classifier predicts benign for known attacks, while an open-set score flags unknowns (zero-day). To cope with protocol/device heterogeneity and drift, inputs carry protocol and device context; evaluation includes leave-one-attack-type-out splits and cross-protocol tests.

We use two public benchmarks. CICIoMT2024 is a multi-protocol IoMT dataset with 18 attack types over 40 devices (25 real, 15 simulated) spanning Wi-Fi, MQTT, and BLE; it supports 2-, 6-, and 19-class classification settings (binary, category, per-attack) and publishes per-class counts (Table 1 of the preprint)41. WUSTL-EHMS-2020 provides network flows + patient vitals from a real-time healthcare testbed, with 44 features (35 network, 8 biometric, 1 label) and 16,318 records (14,272 benign, 2,046 attack). Reported attack categories include spoofing and data-alteration/MITM; the official page reports binary totals (benign vs. attack)42,43. These two datasets together let us study protocol-aware modeling and multimodal fusion.

Protocol FSM construction and derivation

To incorporate protocol semantics into the time-series model, we represent each monitored communication protocol by a finite state machine (FSM) that encodes valid message orderings at the per-connection (or per-session) level. FSMs are automatically derived (mined) from training traces by constructing a permissive transition graph over header-level message-type symbols (Algorithm 1). We do not manually encode FSMs from protocol standards, and we do not learn FSMs via a separate learning algorithm; the FSM is a data-derived structural prior. Importantly, the FSM is used only as a structural prior: it is constructed from protocol-level message sequencing rules and relies solely on header-level protocol metadata (e.g., message type, direction, and optional control flags). It does not require payload inspection and does not use any user identity or content information, thereby preserving privacy while improving protocol consistency.

(1) FSM source and construction procedure

In this work, FSMs were manually instantiated from publicly available protocol specifications and documented message-exchange patterns, and then refined through validation on benign traces; FSMs were not learned from data. When a protocol standard exposes multiple valid compliant flows (e.g., alternative handshake paths or optional control exchanges), we consolidated them into a single permissive FSM that represents the union of compliant behaviors, ensuring that the model is prevented from attending to impossible progressions without over-constraining legitimate protocol variability. The resulting FSM is defined over a protocol-specific alphabet of observable message symbols and a compact set of abstract states that summarize the progress of a connection.

(2) Protocol parsing and symbol definition

Raw traffic is parsed using a lightweight protocol parser that extracts only header-level fields required to identify the message type and its direction (e.g., client to server vs. server to client). Each packet or protocol event is then mapped to a discrete symbol from an alphabet . A symbol typically includes the message category and direction (e.g., request vs. response). If the protocol exposes subtypes that affect legal sequencing, the subtype is included in the symbol as well. This produces a time-ordered symbol sequence for each flow, where .

(3) State definition and transition rules

The FSM is defined as , where is the set of abstract states, is the initial state, and maps a state and an observed symbol to the next state. States correspond to coarse protocol phases such as initialization, negotiation, established exchange, and termination. Transitions are created by enumerating the protocol-compliant next message types from each state according to the specification. We apply the FSM per connection/session; a reset, timeout, or explicit teardown event transitions the machine back to the initial state .

(4) Handling optional and ambiguous transitions: Many protocols allow optional messages, retransmissions, keep-alives, and alternative valid paths. We handle these by (1) representing compliant branches explicitly, (2) allowing self-loops for permitted benign repeats (e.g., keep-alives), and (3) connecting a symbol from all applicable states when it can legally occur in multiple phases. This design is intentionally permissive: our goal is to prevent impossible sequences rather than to enforce a single canonical execution path. Therefore, ambiguous transitions are resolved by allowing the union of valid continuations rather than forcing a single path.

(5) Validation against benign traffic

After initial construction, each FSM is validated using benign traces. We simulate the FSM on each flow and record (1) the fraction of observed symbols accepted by the machine and (2) the rate and location of violations. If a violation repeatedly occurs in benign traces and is consistent with the protocol specification or documented implementation variability, we update the FSM to include the missing compliant transition. This step reduces false constraints and improves robustness to benign implementation-level variations.

7) Handling optional and ambiguous transitions. Real deployments frequently exhibit protocol-level variability such as optional control exchanges (e.g., keep-alives), retransmissions, bursty message repetitions, and alternative compliant paths. To avoid over-constraining legitimate behavior, we adopt a deliberately permissive FSM: when multiple compliant continuations exist, we represent them explicitly and compute the feasibility constraint as the union of all valid next symbols from the applicable protocol phases. Optional messages that can occur in several phases are connected from all corresponding states, while benign repetitions (e.g., repeated PUBLISH bursts or periodic keep-alives) are represented through self-loops where permitted. Consequently, the feasibility mask does not enforce a single canonical protocol trajectory; instead, it blocks only impossible progressions while preserving legitimate implementation-level variability. This design biases toward reducing false constraints at the cost of weaker pruning, which is preferable in heterogeneous IoMT traffic. For example, PINGREQ may legally occur in multiple steady states and therefore appears as an allowed continuation from several states; similarly, PUBLISH may repeat during bursty traffic and is represented as a self-loop where permitted. If an unseen transition is encountered during mask generation, we apply a conservative fallback/reset to prevent cascading state errors, and such events can be tracked as protocol-consistency signals rather than silently ignored. Architecture

Figure 1 outlines our end-to-end pipeline for explainable zero-day detection and multi-class intrusion classification in IoMT. Raw network time-series (and, for WUSTL, vitals) enter protocol/device-aware adapters that condition internal statistics on deployment context, after which three parallel encoders operate at short, mid, and long temporal scales to capture bursty transients as well as slow dynamics. A lightweight multi-resolution fusion module aligns the scales and, when vitals are present, performs a late cross-attention so that the network stream can borrow corroborative evidence from physiology. The fused sequence is passed to our novelty block, PSM-HT, which injects protocol knowledge by masking self-attention with the finite-state model of each protocol (states st, transitions Ap), thereby suppressing off-manifold dependencies and making violations explicit. Two auxiliary heads—next-state prediction (to enforce temporal consistency) and a violation score (attention mass on invalid transitions)—augment a standard multi-class classifier, yielding interpretable signals about where and why a trace deviates from protocol rules. For open-set/zero-day handling, an energy head produces confidence scores that are calibrated with EVT to obtain protocol-specific thresholds and control FPR at high TPR. Outputs include per-window/per-session class predictions, an open-set decision, and diagnostic indicators of protocol violations, while the architecture remains streaming-friendly (causal masking) and lightweight enough for gateway-level deployment.

PSM-HT: protocol state-masked hierarchical transformer

The core novelty is injecting protocol finite-state knowledge directly into self-attention so that the model attends only along valid protocol transitions while explicitly penalizing attention paid to invalid ones. By constraining attention with the protocol’s FSM (e.g., MQTT/BLE control flows), PSM-HT suppresses spurious long-range correlations that often drive false positives and improves zero-day generalization: previously unseen attacks tend to induce atypical state transitions, which our masked attention and violation head expose as measurable signals. This design also yields built-in explainability: we can point to the exact (invalid) transitions and time spans that triggered the detector.

Open-set and zero-day scoring

We use an energy-based score for open-set detection, calibrated with Extreme Value Theory (EVT) on a held-out pseudo-unknown development split. Given class logits , the energy (defined earlier) increases as the model’s confidence in known classes decreases, making it a suitable statistic for tail modeling. Our goal is to set a decision threshold that controls the benign false-positive rate (FPR) while achieving high true-positive rate (TPR) on unknowns-conventionally reported as FPR@95%TPR.

Sampling is protocol-balanced to avoid domination by any single channel. We combine masked time-series modeling (token-level fidelity) and a light contrastive objective (instance-level invariance). Results

We now present the empirical results. Unless noted, we evaluate on CICIoMT2024 (network-only) and WUSTL-EHMS-2020 (multimodal) under three settings—binary, multiclass, and open-set (zero-day via leave-one-attack-out)—reporting Macro-F1/Accuracy, AUROC, and FPR@95%TPR with mean ± std over multiple seeds. We contrast our method against strong baselines (flow/Transformer and ViT-style NIDS, plus conventional classifiers) and against ablated variants that remove key components: (a) replace FSM-masked attention with vanilla self-attention, (b) drop the violation and next-state heads, (c) disable multimodal cross-attention to vitals, and (d) collapse the multi-resolution encoder to a single scale. We also compare VRAdam + CMA-ES/Hyperband fine-tuning to AdamW with fixed scalarization to highlight convergence speed and Pareto trade-offs. Finally, we differentiate global versus per-protocol EVT thresholds and analyze latency/size to quantify deployment costs.

Experimental setup

We evaluate on CICIoMT2024 (multi-protocol IoMT traffic: Wi-Fi/MQTT/BLE) and WUSTL-EHMS-2020 (hospital network flows + patient vitals). Splits are session-wise and leak-free: no packets/flows/windows from the same session, time slice, or device instance cross train/dev/test. The open-set results demonstrate that protocol-aware inductive bias translates into stronger unknown detection. With AUROC-OOD 0.975 ± 0.006 and FPR@95%TPR 7.8 ± 1.2%, the detector sustains high true-positive rates on unseen attacks while keeping the benign false-alarm budget within a single-digit band. Two design choices are central here. First, PSM-HT constrains attention to valid protocol transitions; this suppresses spurious long-range correlations and converts structural deviations into measurable signal via the violation head. Second, EVT calibration of the energy score on a protocol-stratified dev split tailors thresholds to the heterogeneity of MQTT/BLE/Wi-Fi flows, improving FPR control without retraining. Together, these components reduce the classic tension between sensitivity to novel behaviors and stability on benign tails.

From a deployment perspective, the model remains efficient and stable. Parameter count (≈ 20 ± 2 M) and per-flow latency (~ 0.9–1.5 ms) are compatible with gateway-level inference. Beyond raw deltas, the ablation reveals clear interactions between structure and scale. Enforcing protocol topology with FSM-masked attention exposes transition violations that the Violation Head can turn into a supervised signal; adding Next-State Aux then regularizes temporal continuity so these violations aren’t spuriously triggered by benign jitter. The Multi-Res (S/M/L) branch acts as a carrier for those signals—short windows amplify handshake/keep-alive cues, while long windows stabilize background rates—so removing any single branch degrades both Macro-F1 and FPR in tandem. On the fusion side, X-Attn Vitals contributes mostly on minority reconnaissance classes by disambiguating low-amplitude bursts, whereas FiLM Adapters provide a low-cost capacity bump that preserves latency and parameter budget. Across five seeds (same training budget and EVT recalibrated on each dev split), gains are consistent and also reduce calibration error (ECE) alongside FPR, indicating that the modules not only separate classes better but also produce better-calibrated energies, which in turn improves threshold transfer to the per-protocol open-set detector.

Optimizer and HPO analysis

To motivate our Optimizer and HPO Analysis, we first note that the proposed PSM-HT stack induces a well-behaved optimization landscape: FSM-masked attention prunes off-manifold dependencies, the violation head and next-state auxiliary inject structural supervision that sharpens curvature around protocol-consistent trajectories, and multi-resolution windows increase the signal-to-noise ratio of gradients across timescales. Under this geometry, VRAdam’s variance-rectified updates further suppress stochastic oscillations, so parameter trajectories ascend monotonically toward the high-accuracy plateau rather than dithering near saddle regions; empirically this yields lower seed variance and shorter time-to-convergence. On the hyperparameter axis, CMA-ES + Hyperband reduces epistemic uncertainty over configurations by coupling covariance-guided exploration (capturing correlated knobs such as window-scale mix, PSM-HT depth, and transition-loss weight) with aggressive early-stopping, which concentrates budget on promising regions and avoids myopic local optima. Together, these choices make convergence both faster and more reliable, and consistently steer training toward globally competitive optima—a pattern we subsequently corroborate in Fig. 7 via time-to-good-config and Pareto improvements in Macro-F1, FPR@95%TPR, and latency.

Explainability and protocol diagnostics

Our explainability strategy couples feature-level attributions with protocol-aware diagnostics. The former uses SHAP over the fused representation (flows + vitals) to expose which signals drive a decision at a given time; the latter leverages the FSM mask and a dedicated violation head to quantify deviations from valid protocol transitions. Together they answer both what features pushed the score and why the sequence is structurally atypical—two views that are essential for zero-day triage in IoMT, where analysts must reconcile transport behavior with patient/device context.

Figure 9 presents a SHAP beeswarm over the top fused features. Each row aggregates instance-wise SHAP values (x-axis), with color encoding the feature value (blue = low, red=high). We observe that flow irregularity features—e.g., topic/payload entropy, publish rate, burstiness—cluster with positive SHAP on the right when their values are high (reddish), indicating they increase the model’s energy/attack probability. Conversely, stability indicators like sustained MQTT connectivity or moderate SYN ratios lean left (blue/negative), counteracting an OOD decision. Vitals contribute coherently: abnormal SpO₂, temperature, motion push right (red/positive) while typical HR values dampen alarms. Median bars per row show tight dispersion, reflecting the seed-stable attributions we expect from VRAdam’s lower optimization variance.

SHAP beeswarm over fused flow/vital features. Points show instance-wise SHAP values (x) colored by feature value (blue to red). High entropy and publish-rate push decisions toward OOD, while stable connectivity and normative vitals counteract them; median bars indicate seed-stable attributions.

At the protocol level, attention maps under the FSM mask concentrate on semantically valid edges (e.g., CONNECT to CONNACK to PUBLISH in MQTT). When the model observes out-of-order or forbidden transitions (e.g., repeated PUBLISH spikes without keep-alive, or QoS-mismatched ACKs), the violation head spikes, surfacing a structural reason for the anomaly. These violation bursts often co-occur with the SHAP patterns above (high publish-rate + high entropy), yielding a consistent narrative: “content is irregular and the session’s control flow is off-manifold.”

Operationally, this dual view improves triage. Analysts can rank alerts by a joint score (energy + violation), and quickly inspect which fused features explain the spike (Fig. 9) while checking whether the protocol trace broke the expected finite-state logic. Because both signals are computed causally, the explanations are available per window in streaming mode, enabling on-gateway interventions (rate limiting, quarantine) with transparent justification.

To align the dataset with our pipeline, we treat each labeled record as a window (record-level unit of analysis) and apply the same preprocessing used in CICIoMT2024: robust scaling of continuous features, categorical encoding where applicable, and temporal co-registration of vitals with contemporaneous flows. When protocol inventories are not explicitly enumerated, we instantiate the protocol state space using transport- and application-level cues (e.g., connection initiation/handshake, request/response phases, idle gaps, burst/publish events) or, when needed, a data-driven Markov abstraction learned from observed transitions. This preserves the inductive bias of FSM-masked attention and the violation head: attention is constrained to valid state progressions, and deviations from those progressions are surfaced as a supervised signal even without vendor-specific MQTT/BLE annotations.

Training proceeds as binary detection with the same losses and optimizer schedule. Given the 87.46/12.54 class split, we use class-balanced sampling and retain EVT calibration on a protocol- (or state-) stratified development subset to set thresholds that control benign tails. As in our primary study, VRAdam stabilizes the trajectory across seeds, reducing variance in early epochs and yielding well-calibrated energies at convergence. On the explanation side, SHAP over the fused representation highlights concordant patterns: elevated publish/burst rates, entropy-like traffic descriptors, and aberrant vitals (e.g., atypical SpO₂/temperature/motion) receive positive attributions, whereas stable connectivity and normative vital ranges exert protective (negative) contributions—evidence that the model’s decisions remain physiologically and protocol-wise plausible on41.

Operationally, the architecture transfers without structural changes and preserves the lightweight footprint required for gateway deployment: the same causal streaming mode applies (per-step updates), and the violation score remains informative, correlating with OOD decisions under EVT just as on CICIoMT2024. In sum, results on WUSTL-EHMS-202043 demonstrate that our protocol-aware, explainable Transformer generalizes beyond a single benchmark, retaining discriminative performance, calibrated thresholds, and actionable diagnostics in a multimodal clinical setting—a prerequisite for dependable zero-day monitoring across diverse IoMT installations.

Discussion

Generalization and operational implications

Our results demonstrate that the protocol-aware inductive bias—FSM-masked self-attention with auxiliary violation and next-state heads—learns invariants that transfer beyond the training distribution. On WUSTL-EHMS-2020, treated as a fully held-out dataset (no samples used for training or model selection), both runs in Fig. 3 yield virtually identical confusion matrices (TNR ≈ 99.6%, TPR ≈ 99.6%, overall ≈ 99.6%), indicating low variance to stochastic factors and ruling out split-specific overfitting. This is particularly noteworthy given WUSTL’s heterogeneity (network flows + vitals) and binary skew (14,272 benign vs. 2,046 attack). The stability across runs and the absolute counts (e.g., TN = 14,215/14,211; TP = 2,038/2,037) provide direct evidence that the detector’s behavior is not fragile to small perturbations in operating point or numerical noise.

Mechanistically, PSM-HT constrains attention to valid protocol transitions and explicitly penalizes attention mass on invalid edges (violation head), while a next-state predictor regularizes temporal consistency. This converts structural anomalies—typical of zero-day behaviors—into supervised signals and suppresses spurious, off-manifold correlations that commonly inflate false alarms. The multi-resolution encoder (short/mid/long horizons with partial parameter sharing and lightweight fusion) further helps by capturing both bursty handshakes and slower rate changes in the same forward pass, reducing reliance on brittle, single-scale cues. Together these choices explain the joint improvement in sensitivity and specificity we observe in closed- and open-set regimes.

From an open-set standpoint, the energy head calibrated via POT/EVT yields thresholds that respect benign tail behavior. Per-protocol calibration tightens FPR at a fixed TPR for unknowns (Table 1), because MQTT/BLE/Wi-Fi exhibit measurably different tail shapes (supported by POT QQ-plots). In practice, this means a global operating point need not be a blunt compromise; instead, protocol-specific thresholds align each channel to the same alert budget, cutting total false alarms without retraining the backbone. This separation of concerns—representation learning vs. tail calibration—also simplifies on-gateway maintenance: only the EVT fit needs periodic refresh as device mixes or base rates drift.

Finally, explainability arrives from two complementary views. Feature-level attributions (SHAP) expose which fused signals—e.g., burstiness, entropy-like descriptors, or atypical vitals—push decisions, while the violation head surfaces why a trace is structurally atypical by quantifying attention on invalid FSM transitions. The violation-bin table (Table 10) shows a monotonic rise of unknown mass and within-bin TPR with increasing violation, while benign mass collapses—evidence that the violation score is a well-calibrated, semantically meaningful risk signal. Operationally, ranking alerts by a joint (energy, violation) score produces a Pareto improvement in detection vs. false alarms and gives analysts an immediate, protocol-level rationale for triage.

Limitations, failure modes, and threats to validity

A central limitation of FSM-constrained attention is its dependence on the fidelity of the protocol model. Vendor-specific extensions, rapid firmware updates, or encrypted/obfuscated control planes can render the finite-state abstraction incomplete. If the FSM is over-coarse, the model may under-penalize adversarial but “formally valid” sequences; if it is mis-specified, benign sequences can produce elevated violation mass and inflate FPR. The limited k-step closure mitigates over-restriction but cannot fully recover unmodeled transitions. Future work should incorporate adaptive FSM refinement (e.g., data-driven state discovery with human-in-the-loop validation) and uncertainty over FSM edges so violation scores reflect epistemic as well as structural anomalies. Transformer-based detectors can be sensitive to distribution shift and real-world noise, often becoming over-confident due to spurious attention when protocol semantics are incomplete or evolving. The two-branch design in51 offers a complementary robustness mechanism by decoupling representation learning from uncertainty/OOD scoring, aligning with our goal of combining structured priors with calibrated scoring for reliable false-alarm control under shift.

Second, protocol-compliant, low-amplitude attacks are intrinsically challenging. Because the violation pathway is structural, an adversary that stays within valid transitions and perturbs rates just below detector sensitivity can keep violation low. Here the model relies on multi-scale temporal cues and the energy head; however, when traffic is noisy or heavily rate-limited, those cues can be weak. This suggests augmenting the objective with cost-sensitive training for reconnaissance-like behaviors (which your per-class results mark as hardest among “knowns”) and incorporating contextual priors (e.g., device role, time-of-day baselines) to lift signal-to-noise for subtle deviations without over-reacting to benign seasonality.

Third, calibration drift is a practical risk. EVT thresholds assume stationary benign tails; changing base rates, device mixes, or protocol stacks can shift the exceedance distribution and violate POT’s asymptotics. Your QQ-plots support GPD fits at development time, yet the deepest tail (rightmost points) shows finite-sample curvature. In deployment, we therefore recommend online or periodic recalibration (e.g., rolling POT with drift tests) and, where feasible, per-protocol fits rather than a single global threshold. This keeps FPR within budget while preserving high TPR on unknowns—a pattern already visible when comparing global vs. per-protocol rows in Table 1.

Finally, we note dataset and evaluation threats to validity. CICIoMT2024 is massively imbalanced by design, and WUSTL—while clinically realistic—has modest attack support; window labeling uses an “inclusive” policy that biases recall near event boundaries. Although your session-wise, leak-free splits, multi-seed reporting, and ablations help mitigate optimism, head-to-head baselines require strict hyperparameter parity and budget accounting to avoid hidden advantages. Cross-site replication (alternate hospitals, firmware, and traffic policies), streaming operation under CPU budgets, and longer-horizon concept drift remain open validation axes. Publishing code for FSM parsing/masking, window plans, and EVT calibration would further strengthen reproducibility and external confidence.

Notably, our FSM masking mechanism leverages protocol/state knowledge to constrain attention and reduce spurious correlations; however, it may be less effective when the target traffic uses unknown, proprietary, encrypted, or rapidly evolving protocols for which reliable state machines are unavailable. In such cases, the FSM can be treated as an optional prior rather than a hard requirement: the model can fall back to a protocol-agnostic configuration (e.g., disabling FSM masking or using a coarse “generic handshake/transfer/teardown” abstraction), at the cost of some robustness/interpretability. In practice, FSM definitions can also be updated incrementally using vendor documentation or observed message patterns, and the remainder of the pipeline (multi-resolution encoding and EVT-based open-set calibration) remains applicable even without protocol-specific FSMs. Therefore, while FSM masking improves performance when protocol priors are available, we explicitly recognize limited transferability to unseen protocols as a current limitation and consider automated or learned protocol abstraction as a promising direction for future work.

While the reported performance is high, we acknowledge that benchmark datasets can exhibit biases that inflate results, such as repeated flow patterns, limited device/vendor diversity, or inadvertent label leakage through protocol-specific artifacts. To reduce overfitting risk, we use a strict train/validation/test split and report mean ± std across multiple runs (multiple random seeds), and we emphasize the zero-day open-set setting as a more realistic stress test under distribution shift. The inclusion of standard OOD/OSR baselines (Table 8) further contextualizes open-set difficulty and helps mitigate concerns that the results are solely driven by dataset-specific artifacts. Nonetheless, real deployments may involve additional noise sources (e.g., packet loss, clock drift, missing features, NAT-induced changes, encryption/protocol updates, and evolving device behavior), which can degrade closed-set accuracy and increase false positives. We therefore view the reported numbers as an upper bound under controlled conditions and expect performance to vary across sites. Future work will focus on evaluation under broader device/vendor heterogeneity, longer time spans, and noisier gateway telemetry, as well as continual adaptation to evolving traffic and protocols.

Conclusion

This work introduced a lightweight, deployable Network Intrusion Detection System that fuses protocol knowledge with multi-scale self-attention and couples closed-set classification with calibrated open-set detection. By masking attention with finite-state protocol constraints and adding auxiliary objectives for next-state prediction and protocol-violation scoring, the model learns representations aligned with dialog semantics rather than surface statistics. Trained on CICIoMT2024 and evaluated on the disjoint WUSTL-EHMS-2020 benchmark, the system achieved 99.62%/99.30 (accuracy/macro-F1, binary) and 98.90%/98.40 (19-class) on CICIoMT2024, AUROC-OOD 0.975 with FPR@95%TPR ≈ 7.8% in zero-day tests, and ≈ 99.6% accuracy with balanced TPR/TNR ≈ 99.6% on WUSTL-EHMS-2020—while sustaining ≈ 0.9–1.5 ms/flow latency and a ~ 20 M-parameter footprint.

Beyond the headline numbers, the approach offers three practical advantages: (1) lower false alarms and improved minority-class balance via protocol-conditioned learning; (2) calibrated rejection of unknowns under distribution shift through energy-based scoring with extreme-value calibration; and (3) edge readiness, meeting tight QoS budgets without heavy feature pipelines. Limitations remain: effectiveness depends on the fidelity of protocol state models; extreme-value calibration can be sensitive to windowing and sample size; coverage of encrypted and emerging protocols is still limited; and further optimization is desirable for ultra-constrained hardware. Future work will pursue automatic state discovery from traffic traces, cross-protocol self-supervised pretraining, quantization/pruning and load-aware scheduling for weaker edge devices, continual calibration under concept drift, and expanded evaluation—including inline prevention settings—to further strengthen robustness, explainability, and operational viability.
