Contemporary outcomes of the Fontan operation: An Australian and New Zealand Fontan Registry study
Authors: Michael Daley, Igor E. Konstantinov, Supreet Marathe, Tim Hornung, Patrick Disney, Nelson Alphonso, Jelena Saundankar, Gavin Wheaton, Leeanne Grigg, Vishva Wijesekera, Ajay Iyengar, Julian Ayer, Rachael Cordina, Yves d’Udekem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveWe sought to review the impact of extracardiac conduit size and risk factors on the long-term outcomes of the contemporary Fontan procedure: lateral tunnel and extracardiac conduits.MethodsData were collected from a binational registry. Composite outcome of late adverse events included death, transplant, Fontan failure, supraventricular tachycardia (SVT), thromboembolism, or pacemaker implantation.ResultsTransplant-free survival and freedom from Fontan failure was 85% (95% CI, 80%-89%) and 75% (95% CI, 70%-80%) at 30 years, respectively. Extracardiac conduit sizes were available in 1075 patients, with 18 mm (n = 596) and 20 mm (n = 355) being the most common. There was no difference in transplant-free survival, Fontan failure, or late adverse events between patients who received extracardiac conduits with smaller or larger conduits (≤18 mm vs ≥20 mm, P = .41, P = .50, and P = .27, respectively). Only 8 patients (<1%) underwent conduit upsizing during follow-up, all concurrently with another intervention. Lateral tunnel, isomerism, and fenestration were associated with sustained SVT on multivariable analysis (hazard ratio [HR], 2.0, P = .002; HR, 2.4, P < .001; HR, 1.7, P = .013, respectively). Only Fontan fenestration and isomerism were associated with the incidence of thromboembolic events (n = 161) on multivariable analysis (HR, 1.5, P = .021 and HR, 1.4, P = .025, respectively). Landmark analysis was performed from 16 years of age in patients without Fontan failure (n = 813). Transplant-free survival and freedom from Fontan failure were 87% (95% CI, 81%-91%) and 80% (95% CI, 72%-85%) at 20 years from this time point, respectively.ConclusionsDespite recent suggestions, extracardiac conduit sizes do not appear to affect long-term clinical outcomes. Patients with lateral tunnel have a greater incidence of sustained SVT, and Fontan fenestration may increase incidence of late adverse events.

Central Message

The long-term outcomes of the Fontan operation remain excellent in the current era. The size of extracardiac conduit does not currently appear to be a risk factor for long-term clinical events.

Perspective

As Fontan patients live longer, it behooves us to investigate if we are performing the best operation for our patients in the longer term. Recent concerns of deleterious effects from relatively undersized extracardiac conduits were unsupported by the clinical outcomes from our binational registry review. However, further insight may be gained by detailed assessment of liver function and fibrosis.

The Fontan operation, described by Francis Fontan in 1971, was the culmination of decades of work on right heart bypass.1 The procedure provided the first viable option for sustainable univentricular circulation in patients with severe congenital cardiac abnormalities. From its conception to the end of the 20th century, there have been multiple advancements to the procedure in a bid to improve both the short- and long-term outcomes.2, 3, 4, 5, 6

The lateral tunnel (LT) was described by de Leval in 1988 and rapidly became the most frequently used technique until the turn of the millennium, when the extracardiac conduit (ECC) became the most favored technique, likely because of the potential for more favorable hemodynamics and reduced rate of late arrhythmias.3,4 In the Society of Thoracic Surgeons database, 82% of the Fontan operations performed between 2012 and 2022 were with ECCs.7 Recently, the benefit of the ECC has been questioned, with reports of superior early outcomes and decreased risk of late liver cirrhosis with the LT, with particular concerns over the relatively undersized ECC conduit in adulthood.8, 9, 10, 11 With these conflicting reports, there is no consensus of improved survival with either of these 2 techniques, and we wanted to investigate this comparative survival in large dataset.

The Australia and New Zealand Fontan Registry (ANZFR) was established more than a decade ago and is currently the largest Fontan operation registry in the world.12, 13, 14 It collected clinical data for patients who received a Fontan operation in Australia and New Zealand and provided benchmark outcomes for patients with Fontan circulation worldwide. With more than a decade of registry development, we also sought to review the outcomes of the patients with Fontan circulation in Australia and New Zealand and assess the impact of our updated management of these patients.

Methods

Registry Design

The ANZFR was created in 2008, and over the following 5 years data were collected on patients with Fontan circulation from all hospital databases and private cardiology practices across Australia and New Zealand.14 The exact design of the ANZFR has previously been described. Of note, patients with a Björk procedure were excluded from the registry. Ethics approval is granted by participating institutions and bi-nationally (RCH HREC 36260/ACSQHC-ARCR-189; February 15, 2012). Written consent was initially retrospectively sought, then prospectively, with an opt-out enrollment at the time of the Fontan operation. Because of the historical nature of the procedure, patients with atriopulmonary Fontan (n = 263) have their demographics described and then excluded from further analysis, with the exception of Fontan conversions.

Definitions

Preoperative hemodynamic characteristics, including moderate or greater atrioventricular (AV) valve regurgitation and moderate or greater (systemic) ventricular dysfunction, were derived from preoperative investigation reports, multidisciplinary conference and clinic letters, and operation reports. Early mortality was defined as death <30 days after the Fontan operation or before hospital discharge. Fontan failure was defined as the incidence of at least 1 of the following during follow-up: death, heart transplantation, Fontan conversion or takedown, New York Heart Association (NYHA) class III or IV symptoms, plastic bronchitis, or protein-losing enteropathy.

Latest follow-up was defined as the last clinical encounter with the patient. Single-stage Fontan was defined as both caval connections performed in the same procedure.

Prolonged pleural effusion was defined as pleural effusions requiring chest drain for ≥30 days, reintervention for effusions, or the presence of chylothorax for ≥30 days. Thromboembolic events included strokes, reversible ischemic neurologic deficits, transient ischemic attacks, pulmonary emboli, intracardiac thrombi, Fontan circuit or caval thrombi, shunt thrombi, and peripheral deep vein thrombi. Only post-Fontan thromboembolic events were included.

Supraventricular tachycardias (SVTs) included atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, atrioventricular nodal re-entry tachycardias, and other intra-atrial re-entry tachycardias. Only post-Fontan supraventricular tachyarrhythmias were included.

Fontan fenestration was defined as fenestration creation at the time of Fontan operation. The size of fenestration was at surgeon discretion and not collected as part of this study. Similarly, fenestration patency was not routinely assessed during follow-up and was not included in this study.

Era Analysis

Patients were assigned to an era on the basis of the time of their Fontan operation: 1988-1999, 2000-2009, 2010-2019, 2020-present.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Stata, version 14 (StataCorp). Values are defined as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed variables, or median (interquartile range, IQR) for non-normally distributed variables. Because of the non-normal distribution of length of stay, the log of this variable was used for comparison in the multivariable cox regression. Length of stay was also categorized to <12 days, 12 to 18 days, and >18 days.

Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test, and the Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare continuous variables. The log-rank test was used to compare Kaplan-Meier curves. Multivariable Cox regression analysis used for multivariable analyses and included all variables with P < .10 on univariable analysis.

Early mortality occurred in 51 patients, and 8 patients underwent early Fontan takedown and were subsequently excluded from analysis. Median length of stay was 13 days (IQR, 10-20 days). Prolonged pleural effusions (including chylothoraces) occurred in 129 patients (8.0%).

Conversion of Atriopulmonary Fontan

Of the 263 patients with atriopulmonary Fontan operation, 50 underwent Fontan conversion at a median of 19.8 years (IQR, 15.5-27.1 years) post-Fontan operation. Conversion was almost exclusively to an ECC Fontan (n = 47) with only 3 patients converted to LT Fontan. Patients who underwent a Fontan conversion were subsequently excluded from the contemporary cohort, leaving 1606 patients in contemporary cohort.

Fontan failure

A total of 174 patients experienced Fontan failure during the follow-up period. Overall freedom from Fontan failure was 92% (95% CI, 90%-93%), 84% (95% CI, 81%-86%), and 75% (95% CI, 70%-80%) at 10, 20, and 30 years, respectively (Figure 2, B).

Of the 99 patients who developed a Fontan failure-defining condition (excluding death and transplantation as first condition), survival was 75% (95% CI, 63%-84%) and 60% (95% CI, 40%-75%) at 10 and 15 years, respectively. Overall, 53 patients experienced NYHA class III/IV, 48 patients experienced protein-losing enteropathy, 12 experienced plastic bronchitis, 10 underwent Fontan takedown (after discharge), and 9 underwent conversion from LT to ECC. Variables impacting freedom from Fontan failure are analyzed in Table 2. Significant associations included hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS; HR, 1.6; P = .05), moderate or greater ventricular dysfunction pre-Fontan (HR, 2.6, P = .002), fenestration (HR, 2.3, P < .001), and prolonged pleural effusions (HR, 1.8, P = .03).

Of note, 9 patients with LT Fontan underwent conversion to ECC Fontan at a median of 14.7 years (IQR, 8.7-18.7 years) post-Fontan, 2 of whom subsequently died at 10 months and 7.4 years post-Fontan conversion.

Dysrhythmias and Pacemakers

A total of 117 patients who received a Fontan (7.3%) were noted to have pacemakers in situ, 26 of which were implanted at the time of Fontan or during initial hospital stay (20 ECC, 6 LT, P = .54). For late implantation, median time to pacemaker implantation post Fontan was 7.8 (IQR, 3.1-18.2 years).

Sustained SVT post-Fontan was reported in 111 patients (6.9%, 58 ECC and 53 LT) at a median time of 7.4 years (IQR, 1.4-15.3 years) post-Fontan (Figure 3, A). Thromboembolic Events

A total of 161 patients (10.0%) had at least 1 thromboembolic event post-Fontan. These included 84 patients with a cerebroembolic event (stroke, reversible ischemic neurologic deficits, transient ischemic attack), 45 patients with Fontan circuit or caval thrombi, 13 patients with pulmonary emboli, 7 patients with intracardiac thrombi, and 12 with other forms of thromboemboli.

Seven patients had cerebroembolic events with insufficient data to determine timing post-Fontan. Median time to thromboembolic event was 4.0 years (IQR, 10 months to 13.3 years) post-Fontan. On multivariable analysis, isomerism and the presence of Fontan fenestration was associated with the incidence of thromboembolic events (HR, 1.4, P = .025 and HR, 1.5, P = .021, respectively; Table 3). Of note, Fontan type was not associated with the occurrence of thromboembolic events (P = .60) (Figure 3, B).

Late Adverse Events (Fontan Failure, SVT, Thromboembolism, Pacemaker)

Late adverse events occurred in 430 patients. Freedom from late adverse events was 73% (95% CI, 71%-76%), 65% (95% CI, 61%-68%), and 54% (95% CI, 49%-58%) at 15, 20, and 25 years post-Fontan, respectively. Predictors of late adverse events on multivariable analysis included heterotaxy syndrome (HR, 1.6, P = .002), pre-Fontan moderate + atrioventricular valve regurgitation (HR, 1.6, P = .028), and Fontan fenestration (HR, 2.2, P < .001; Table 3). There was no difference in freedom from late adverse events between Fontan types (P = .76) on log-rank test (Figure 3, C).

Era Analysis

Transplant-free survival by Fontan era is presented in Figure E1, A. There was no difference in transplant-free survival between Fontan eras (P = .67) on log-rank test. Freedom from Fontan failure by Fontan era is presented in Figure E1, B. Similarly, there was no difference in freedom from Fontan failure between Fontan eras (P = .43) on log-rank test.

Landmark Analysis From 16 Years of Age

Thirty-two patients underwent the Fontan operation at an age older than 16 years and were excluded because of their advanced age at Fontan. Of the 1574 patients, 723 patients did not have survival follow-up beyond 16 years of age, and a further 38 patients experienced Fontan failure before reaching 16 years of age.

Of the patients surviving to 16 years of age without Fontan failure, transplant-free survival from this time point was 93% (95% CI, 90%-95%) and 87% (95% CI, 81%-91%) at 10 and 20 years, respectively. Similarly, freedom from Fontan failure for these patients was 91% (95% CI, 89%-93%) and 80% (95% CI, 72%-85%) at 10 and 20 years from this time point, respectively. There was no difference in outcomes between LT and ECC in this analysis (Figure E2, A, P = .23 and Figure E2, B, P = .41, respectively).

Extracardiac Conduit Sizes

ECC sizes were available in 1075 patients with 18 mm (n = 596), 20 mm (n = 355), and 22 mm (n = 69), most common. There was no difference in transplant-free survival, Fontan failure, or late adverse events between patients who received ECC with smaller or larger conduits (≤18 mm vs ≥20 mm, P = .41, P = .50, and P = .27, respectively). Similarly, there were no difference in outcomes when we compared conduit sizes in right and left ventricular dominance (P = .34 and P = .41, respectively; Figure 4, A and B). Discussion

More than a decade has passed since the initial reports from the Australia and New Zealand Fontan Registry helped to set a standard for long-term outcomes after the Fontan operation.12, 13, 14 We have observed an expected increase in the proportion of patients who received ECCs within the ANZ Fontan cohort. As the most commonly performed Fontan modification in the world, it behooves us to investigate whether patients who undergo ECC are attaining the best long-term outcomes. With larger cohort numbers and longer follow-up, we were unable to demonstrate a substantial difference in survival or freedom from Fontan failure between patients who received ECC and LT. However, patients who received LT carried a greater incidence of SVT post-Fontan on multivariable analysis. This has previously been theorized to be attributable to atrial suture lines and inclusion of the atrial wall in the Fontan conduit, predisposing it to distension and arrhythmogenicity, and is consistent with previously reported outcomes.14, 15, 16, 17 Interestingly, despite the consistently demonstrated greater burden of atrial tachyarrhythmias, patients who received LT do not have worse long-term survival or incidence of Fontan failure.

Recent reports have suggested ECCs of 16 to 20 mm (most common conduit size in ANZ) become undersized in most adolescent patients who undergo a Fontan, suggesting the LT Fontan may have improved hemodynamics with less power loss at late adolescent/adult sizes and improved organ function.8,18,19 Similarly, computational fluid dynamics have suggested expansion of the ECC conduit to 24 to 32 mm in adults may improve total cavopulmonary connection efficiency.20 For this reason, we performed a landmark analysis of patients at the age of 16 years (assumed adult size) and included those who did not experience any Fontan failure–defining conditions before this. Again, we did not demonstrate a difference in transplant-free survival or freedom from Fontan failure in this cohort between LT and ECC. Although a limited comparison analysis, it is reassuring that there is not demonstrably worse outcomes once patients who received ECC reach adulthood compared with their LT counterparts.

Our analysis of the impact ECC sizes on long-term clinical outcomes did not demonstrate a significant difference between smaller and larger ECCs. However, a small divergence is noted late on the Kaplan-Meier curve, and with more patients and longer follow-up, a difference may become apparent As recent studies have pointed toward an alarming incidence of subclinical liver fibrosis in patients who received an ECC Fontan, it is possible the clinical outcomes defining Fontan failure are too superficial to detect a difference.8 Fontan-associated liver fibrosis remains a diagnostic dilemma with inaccuracies of noninvasive imaging techniques, and as such, no standardized surveillance recommendations currently exist.21, 22, 23

HLHS is often considered to carry worse long-term outcomes after the Fontan operation, although reported outcomes differ in its significance.24, 25, 26, 27 Similar to the initial ANZ Fontan registry reports, we found the presence of HLHS was associated with worse freedom from Fontan failure but was not associated with overall mortality or transplantation.13 Again, these outcomes are reassuring, as we see a gradual increase in the proportion of patients with HLHS across the eras.

Many of the demonstrated variables associated with long-term outcomes are expected as they portray worse pre-Fontan hemodynamics (eg, pre-Fontan AV regurgitation and ventricular dysfunction). Interestingly, Fontan fenestration was associated with worse outcomes in each analysis (transplant-free survival, freedom from Fontan failure, incidence of SVT, thromboembolic events, and composite late adverse events). However, our analysis did not include fenestration size, patency during follow-up, long-term anticoagulation regime, or pre-Fontan ventricular end-diastolic pressure data, which may have impacted these outcomes.28,29 We previously performed a propensity score–matched analysis examining the impact of Fontan fenestration on long-term outcomes and, although no difference in survival or incidence of Fontan failure was found, fenestration was associated with a greater incidence of thromboembolic events, which has been again demonstrated in the current study.30 In addition, the Korean Fontan registry also reported that fenestration was associated with greater NYHA scores and lower systemic oxygen saturations in the long-term, while the TACTIC (The AntiCoagulation Therapy in Congenital heart disease) group, factoring in variables such as fenestration closure and atrial arrythmias, reported no association of Fontan fenestration on long-term outcomes.31,32

Long-term outcomes did not vary depending on the era of Fontan procedure in our current study. Although this may imply a lack of progress in our management of patients with Fontan circulation, patients who experienced a Fontan-failure defining condition (excluding death or transplantation) experienced 75% survival at 10 years. The outcomes of these conditions, which were associated with significantly greater mortality, appear to be improving from previous reports.33, 34, 35 Fontan-associated liver disease, which is not often considered a “Fontan failure-defining condition,” is a form a hepatic dysfunction and is characterized by early silent hepatic fibrosis.23 Although not assessed in the current study, recent reports of substantial hepatic fibrosis by adolescence in the Fontan cohort warrants urgent investigations for prevention and potential therapies.8,36,37 If smaller conduit size genuinely predisposes patients to accelerated hepatic fibrosis, then our data and negative result may emphasize the importance of more detailed long-term liver-related outcomes.

Limitations

The current study and analyses are limited to the data collated by the ANZ Fontan Registry. As such, it carries the usual limitations of a retrospective analysis and is confined by the predefined variables of the registry (eg, pleural drainage duration) and availability of data. Variables such as fenestration size, long-term patency, pre-Fontan ventricular diastolic pressure, and ongoing medical therapies are not routinely collected, whereas other variables were limited by missing data (eg, pre-Fontan AV valve regurgitation and ventricular dysfunction). Although multivariable analyses help to address confounders, the potential association of certain variables (eg, Fontan fenestration) with greater-risk patients may skew the results, which may account for differences in comparison with other reported outcomes. Liver fibrosis data were not included in this study, a condition that may allow earlier detection of Fontan failure, prompt a change in current management, and be a potential target of future therapies. Similarly, given the often insidious development of liver fibrosis, even longer-term data may be needed to detect a difference between cohorts.

Conclusions

The long-term outcomes of patients with a contemporary Fontan in Australia and New Zealand remain excellent through recent eras. Other than tachyarrhythmic burden, there is no discernible difference between LT and ECC outcomes into adulthood. Further investigations into hepatic fibrosis, surveillance strategies, and the impact of Fontan circuit size are an avenue for further improvement in outcomes for these patients.
