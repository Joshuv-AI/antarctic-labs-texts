Abstract

We determine the quadratic points on the modular curves X 0 ​ ( N ) X_{0}(N) for N ≤ 100 N\leq 100 for which this has not been previously done, namely the cases N ∈ { 66 , 70 , 78 , 82 , 84 , 86 , 87 , 88 , 90 , 96 , 99 } . 1.
Introduction

A fundamental problem in arithmetic geometry, with many consequences in the theory of elliptic curves and with many applications to Diophantine equations, is to understand the low degree points on modular curves. Perhaps the most important and well-known families of modular curves are
X_{0}(N)
and
X_{1}(N)
. There has been notably more success in studying
X_{1}(N)
. In particular, the set of all
N
for which
X_{1}(N)
has degree
d
non-cuspidal points is known for
d\leq 4
; see
[
17
]
for the proof for
d=1
,
[
16
,
14
]
for
d=2
,
[
10
]
for
d=3
and
[
12
]
for
d=4
.

On the other hand, the modular curves
X_{0}(N)
turn out to be more difficult. The list of all the
N
with non-cuspidal degree
d
points are known only in degree
d=1
, due to Mazur
[
18
]
who solved the problem for prime
N
, and Kenku (see
[
15
]
and the references therein) who completed the classification for all
N
. We note that while the the list of all
N
with a non-cuspidal point on
X_{0}(N)
is not known for the set of all quadratic fields, it is known for many
fixed
quadratic fields (see
[
4
]
), assuming the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis.

In this paper we study quadratic points on
X_{0}(N)
. One major difficulty in studying quadratic points on
X_{0}(N)
is that, as opposed to
X_{1}(N)
, the modular curve
X_{0}(N)
has non-cuspidal quadratic points for infinitely many
N
, coming from elliptic curves with complex multiplication (CM). However, if one ignores the CM points, then it is expected that there should be only finitely many
N
such that
X_{0}(N)
has non-cuspidal non-CM points. The following conjecture is widely believed.

Conjecture 1.1

(Quadratic isogenies conjecture
[
1
]
)
.

There exists an integer
C
such that if
K
is a quadratic field and
N>C
is an integer, then any
P\in X_{0}(N)(K)
is either a cusp or a CM point.

We emphasize that the constant
C
in this conjecture does not depend on the quadratic field
K
.
1.1
implies the conjecture of Elkies which states that the set of
N
such that
X^{+}_{0}(N)(\mathbb{Q})
contains points that are neither CM nor cusps is finite (see
[
13
]
).

This is a topic that has received considerable attention recently. Bruin and Najman described all the quadratic points on hyperelliptic
X_{0}(N)
such that the Jacobian
J_{0}(N)
has rank
0
over
\mathbb{Q}

[
6
]
. Özman and Siksek then determined all the quadratic points on the non-hyperelliptic
X_{0}(N)
with
J_{0}(N)
having rank
0
over
\mathbb{Q}
and with genus
\leq 5

[
22
]
. Box determined the quadratic points on all the
X_{0}(N)
with genus
\leq 5
and with
J_{0}(N)
having positive rank over
\mathbb{Q}

[
5
]
. Najman and Vukorepa described all the quadratic points on the bielliptic
X_{0}(N)
(for which this had not already been done in previous work)
[
21
]
. Adžaga, Keller, Michaud-Jacobs, Najman, Özman and Vukorepa developed methods to compute quadratic points on a general fixed
X_{0}(N)
, and found all the quadratic points on all such curves of genus
\leq 8
, and of genus
\leq 10
for
N
prime (see
[
1
]
).

We make progress on quadratic points on this topic by
determining the quadratic points on all
X_{0}(N)
for
N\leq 100
, for which this has not been already done. There are
11
such values of
N
; the genus of these
X_{0}(N)
is
9\leq g(X_{0}(N))\leq 11.
In all but one (
N=86
) of these cases the algorithms of
[
1
]
fail, in most cases because we are unable to determine
J_{0}(N)(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}}
. In all of the aforementioned
11
cases,
N
is of the form
N=nd
, where
n
is such that points have
X_{0}(n)
already been determined. This allows us to approach the problem using the “going-down” approach (as referred to in
[
1
]
), which was developed in
[
21
, Section 2]
, and reduces the problem to finding rational points on some quotients of modular curves. This approach has the advantage of being less computational compared to the Atkin-Lehner sieve from
[
1
]
, in the sense that it usually requires very little computation. We note that some of our results are used in the upcoming work of Derickx and Najman
[
11
]
which determines all the
N
such that
X_{0}(N)
has a sporadic point.

We introduce conventions and notation in
Section

2
. The going-down method, as stated in
[
21
, Section 2]
cannot be applied to
N=96
, so we generalize the method; see
Section

3
. We describe the calculations for all the
X_{0}(N)
in
Section

4
, and finally list the results in the tables in
Section

5
.

All the code used in the paper can be found at

https://github.com/F-Najman/q100

2.
Conventions and notation

We will be using the conventions and notation from
[
21
, Section 2]
, which we will essentially repeat here for the convenience of the reader.

The modular curve
X_{0}(N)
is the moduli space of pairs
(E,C)
, where
E
is a generalized elliptic curve with a cyclic subgroup
C
. It is a coarse moduli space.

The notion of
\mathbb{Q}
-curves can be defined over general number fields, but in this paper we will restrict to
\mathbb{Q}
-curves over quadratic fields, as this is exclusively what we will be working with. For the general theory, see
[
9
,
13
]
. An elliptic curve
E
over a quadratic field
K
is called a
\mathbb{Q}
-curve if it is isogenous (over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
) to its Galois conjugate. Throughout the paper, when saying that curves are isogenous, without mentioning over which field, we will always mean over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
. If the cyclic isogeny
E\rightarrow E^{\sigma}
is of degree
d
, we say that
E
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-curve of degree
d
. By
C_{m}
we will denote a cyclic subgroup of order
m
. We allow
\mathbb{Q}
-curves to have complex multiplication. In particular, all elliptic curves with CM are
\mathbb{Q}
-curves.

Let
n
be a positive integer and factor
n=dm
with
(d,m)=1
. Let
w_{d}
be the Atkin-Lehner involution sending a point
x\in Y_{0}(n)(L)
, where
x
corresponds to
(E,C_{d},C_{m})
, to the point
w_{d}(x)
, corresponding to
(E/C_{d},E[d]/C_{d},(C_{m}+C_{d})/C_{d})
. Here, quotienting out by
C_{d}
means mapping by the
d
-isogeny
\mu
such that
\ker\mu=C_{d}
, i.e.
w_{d}(x)=(\mu(E),\mu(E[d]),\mu(C_{m}))
. Thus, non-cuspidal
\mathbb{Q}
-rational points on
X_{0}(n)/w_{d}
correspond to pairs

\left\{(E,C_{d},C_{m}),(E/C_{d},E[d]/C_{d},(C_{m}+C_{d})/C_{d})\right\}

which are
\Gal_{\mathbb{Q}}
-invariant, meaning that either the point
(E,C_{d},C_{m})
is defined over
\mathbb{Q}
or there exists a quadratic extension
K/\mathbb{Q}
with
\sigma
generating
\Gal(K/\mathbb{Q})
such that

(2.1)

(E,C_{d},C_{m})^{\sigma}=(E/C_{d},E[d]/C_{d},(C_{m}+C_{d})/C_{d}),

implying that
E
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-curve of degree
d
with the additional property that
\mu(C_{m})=C_{m}^{\sigma}
. We will say that an elliptic curve
F

corresponds
to a point on
X_{0}(n)/w_{d}
if
j(F)=j(E)
or
j(F)=j(\mu(E))
. In the case of
d=n
, the curve
X_{0}(n)/w_{n}
is denoted by
X_{0}^{+}(n)
and it simply parametrizes a pair consisting of a
\mathbb{Q}
-curve of degree
n
together with its Galois conjugate (without any further conditions). All the fixed points of
w_{d}
correspond to CM elliptic curves.

Let
X/\mathbb{Q}
be a curve. We say a point
x\in X(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
is
\mathbb{P}^{1}
-parametrized if
[\mathbb{Q}(x):\mathbb{Q}]=d
and there exists a degree
d
morphism
\varphi:X\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}
over
\mathbb{Q}
of degree
d
such that
\varphi(x)\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q})
. A point
x\in X(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
that is not
\mathbb{P}^{1}
-parametrized is called
\mathbb{P}^{1}
-isolated. Note that
\mathbb{P}^{1}
-isolated points were previously sometimes referred to as
exceptional
points in the literature; see for example
[
6
]
. For hyperelliptic curves in the form
y^{2}=f(x)
, the quadratic points that are
\mathbb{P}^{1}
-parametrized are those for which
x\in\mathbb{Q}
, while all the others are
\mathbb{P}^{1}
-isolated.

3.
The “going-down” method

In this section we generalize the “going-down” methods of
[
21
, Section 2]
. The following proposition is a common generalization of
[
21
, Propositions 2.2 and 2.3]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
m
and
d
be coprime positive integers. Let
E/\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
be a non-CM
\mathbb{Q}
-curve of degree
d
defined over a quadratic field
K
having in addition an
m
-isogeny defined over
K
with codomain
E_{1}
. Then for some divisor
k
of
m
,
E
corresponds to a rational point on the curve
X_{0}\left(\frac{m}{k}d\right)/w_{d}
, and
E_{1}
corresponds to a rational point on the curve
X_{0}^{+}(k^{2}d)
.

Proof.

In this proof for any non-CM elliptic curves
E,E^{\prime}
, we denote by
E\rightarrow E^{\prime}
a cyclic isogeny; such an isogeny always exists by
[
9
, Lemma A.1]
, and is unique up to sign.

Denote the isogeny
E\to E^{\sigma}
by
\mu
, its kernel by
C_{d}
, and the kernel of the isogeny
E\to E_{1}
by
C_{m}
. Let
\mu(C_{m})\cap C_{m}^{\sigma}=k\mu(C_{m})
, where
\sigma
is the generator of
\Gal(K/\mathbb{Q})
.

Let
E_{2}=E/kC_{m}
. We claim that
E_{2}
and
E_{2}^{\sigma}
are
d
-isogenous. Take a
d
-isogeny from
\phi:E_{2}\to E_{3}
with
\ker(\phi)=\lambda(C_{d})
, where
\lambda:E\to E_{2}
is the isogeny with kernel
kC_{m}
. Then
\phi\circ\lambda:E\to E_{3}
and
\lambda^{\sigma}\circ\mu
have the same kernel
\langle C_{d},kC_{m}\rangle
, so they are equal up to sign. Thus,
E_{3}
and
E_{2}^{\sigma}
are isomorphic, so
E_{2}^{\sigma}
and
E_{2}
are
d
-isogenous. We get the following diagram, where all the lines represent cyclic isogenies of the denoted degree.

which is

corresponds to a rational point on
X_{0}\left(\frac{m}{k}d\right)/w_{d}
.

Let

\ker(E\rightarrow E_{1}^{\sigma})=C_{d}+C_{m}^{\prime},

where
C_{m}^{\prime}
is a cyclic subgroup of
E
of order
m
, which by what we have proved satisfies

and

It follows that

and hence

Hence the isogenies
E_{2}\rightarrow E_{1}
and
E_{2}\rightarrow E_{1}^{\sigma}
are independent, and hence
E_{1}
is
k^{2}d

E_{1}^{\sigma}
, so it corresponds to a point on
X_{0}^{+}(k^{2}d)
.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that the curves
E,E^{\sigma},E_{2}
and
E_{2}^{\sigma}
(together with the appropriate subgroups) in the proof of
Proposition

3.1
correspond to a rational point on
X_{0}\left(\frac{m}{k}d\right)/\langle w_{d},w_{\frac{m}{k}}\rangle
which lifts to a rational point on
X_{0}\left(\frac{m}{k}d\right)/w_{d}
.

We immediately get the following corollary, which we will later often use:

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
n
and
d
be such that
X_{0}(n)
is hyperelliptic and
w_{d}
is the hyperelliptic involution on
X_{0}(n)
. Let
N=m_{1}m_{2}d
, where
m_{2}d=n
and
m_{1}
is coprime to
d
. Then any quadratic point on
X_{0}(N)
that maps to a
\mathbb{P}^{1}
-parametrized point on
X_{0}(n)
induces a
\mathbb{Q}
-rational non-cuspidal point on both
X_{0}(k^{2}d)^{+}
and
X_{0}(\frac{N}{k})/w_{d}
for some divisor
k
of
m_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
E/K
be the curve corresponding to
x\in X_{0}(N)(K)
, where
K
is a quadratic field. Then
E
also corresponds to a point on
X_{0}(n)
which is, by assumption,
\mathbb{P}^{1}
-parametrized. Hence, by
[
6
, Theorem 12]

E
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-curve, and from the arguments of
[
6
]
, it can easily be seen that it is a
\mathbb{Q}
-curve of degree
d
. The result now follows from
Proposition

3.1
.
∎

Let
J_{0}^{-}(p)

(ii)

p\neq 37
and
\#J_{0}^{-}(p)(\mathbb{Q})<\infty.

4.1.
The cases where
X_{0}(n)
has finitely many quadratic points

The easiest cases are when
X_{0}(n)
has finitely many quadratic points. In these cases
N=84,88,90
, and
n
is
n=42,44
or
45
, respectively; the quadratic points on these

can be found in

22

. For
n=42
and
44
, all the points are CM points.

On the other hand,
X_{0}(45)
has 2 isogeny classes, each consisting of 4 non-CM curves with
45
-isogenies. We can immediately see that none of these non-CM curves with a
45
-isogeny will have a
90
-isogeny, as there would be at least 8 non-CM curves with
45
-isogenies in a class containing a non-CM curve with a
90
-isogeny.

Thus, for all 3 of these values of
N
, it remains to check whether any of the quadratic CM points on

lift to quadratic points on
X_{0}(N)
, and if yes, to how many points.

The results of
[

]
, as listed in the GitHub repository

when this is the case. In particular, this is true for the values

and

of those that we consider.

When there exist CM points on

.

have CM by

and elliptic curves with

have CM by

.

be factorizations in

. Note that

. We compute all rational points on
X_{0}(N)/w_{d}
using the (classical) Chabauty method. We use the implementation from
https://github.com/steffenmueller/QCMod
, for which the theory is described in
[
3
,
2
]
.

Next we need to explicitly check that the exceptional (or
\mathbb{P}^{1}
-isolated) points of
X_{0}(n)
do not lift to quadratic points on
X_{0}(N)
. One way this can sometimes easily be done is by seeing that the number of points over a fixed quadratic field would need to be larger than it is, as we did for
N=90
in
Section

4.1
.

Another approach, which always works, to do this for non-CM points is as follows. Let
p:=N/n
(this is always a prime in our cases) and let
a
be the power of
p
dividing
N
, but not
n
. Let
E/K
, for
K
a quadratic field, be an elliptic curve having the same
j
-invariant as the point on
X_{0}(n)(K)
that we want to eliminate. Note that the choice of a a particular quadratic twist of
E
is irrelevant, as having an
N
-isogeny is a quadratic-twist invariant property. If
E
had an
a
-isogeny over
K
, then
\Gal(\overline{K}/K)
would act on the set of
x
-coordinates of the generators of the kernel of such an isogeny.
We factor the primitive
a
-th division polynomial
\psi^{\prime}_{a}:=\psi_{a}/\psi_{\frac{a}{p}}
(where
\psi_{n}
is the standard
n
-th division polynomial) of
E
. If
\psi^{\prime}_{a}
, whose roots are all the
x
-coordinates of generators of cyclic subgroups of order
a
of
E[a]
, has no factors of degree
\leq\varphi(a)/2
, we conclude that there is no
\Gal(\overline{K}/K)
-invariant cyclic subgroup of order
a
of
E
over
K
, and hence no
a
-isogeny. In the most common case of
a=2
, this amounts to just checking whether
E(K)
has a 2-torsion point.

Finally, we need to check whether there are any CM points (possibly even coming from
\mathbb{P}^{1}
-parametrized points on
X_{0}(n)
) using the results of
[
8
]
, and if there are, what their multiplicities are. This is done, as explained already in
Section

4.1
, either by looking at their isogeny graphs as in
Example

4.1
, or by explicitly computing the number of fibers that map to the fixed CM
j
-invariant
j_{0}
with respect to the
j
-map
j:X_{0}(N)\rightarrow X_{0}(1)
.

4.3.
The case
X_{0}(86)

The curve
X_{0}(86)
is the only case where
X_{0}(n)
(in this case
n=43
) is neither hyperelliptic nor does it have finitely many quadratic points. However, the quadratic points on
X_{0}(43)
are described in
[
5
, §4.1]
and it is proved that all but 8 points on
X_{0}(43)
are
\mathbb{Q}
-curves of degree
43
. This means that we can apply our methods after checking that the 8 exceptional points do not lift to quadratic points on
X_{0}(86)
. The curve
X_{0}(86)/w_{43}
is a genus 4 curve whose Jacobian has rank 2 over
\mathbb{Q}
. We again apply the Chabauty method as described in
[
3
,
2
]
to determine all the rational points.

We note that
X_{0}(86)
is the only case
N\leq 100
that we consider here for which the Atkin-Lehner sieve from
[
1
]
succeeds. The Atkin-Lehner sieve also reduces finding quadratic points on
X_{0}(86)
to finding rational points on
X_{0}(86)/w_{43}
. So in this case, our ”going down” method produces the same results as the Atkin-Lehner sieve, but without any computation. We note that the solution forrrr this case was included in the Github repository of
[
1
]
, although it was not mentioned in the paper. We include the list of quadratic points in
Section

5
for the sake of completeness.

4.4.
The case
X_{0}(96)

The case of
X_{0}(96)
is more complicated. We will need some finer considerations of the going-down method to deal with it.

Proposition 4.2
.

The modular curve
X_{0}(96)
has no quadratic points

Proof.

We view all curves in the proof up to
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
-isomorphism. We start by observing that there are no CM elliptic curves over quadratic fields with
96
-isogenies by the results of
[
8
]
.
By
[
6
, Section 3.4]
, every non-CM elliptic curve
E
with a cyclic subgroup
C
of order 48, both defined over a quadratic field
K
, is
12
-isogenous to its Galois conjugate
E^{\sigma}
by an isogeny whose kernel has intersection with
C
of order
6
.

Assume that
E
has a 96-isogeny and does not have CM. It follows we have the following
2
-isogeny diagram, where all lines represent
2
-isogenies, all defined over
K
.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{3}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{4}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{5}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{3}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{4}}

Each of the elliptic curves
E_{i}
and
F_{j}
in the diagram also has a
3
-isogenous curve
E_{i}^{\prime}
and
F_{j}^{\prime}
, and we have an analogous
2
-isogeny diagram.
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{0}^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{1}^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{2}^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{3}^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{4}^{\prime}}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{1}^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{2}^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{3}^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{4}^{\prime}}

By
[
6
, Section 3.4]
, since
E_{0}
is
48
-isogenous to
E_{4}^{\prime}
, it follows that
E_{0}^{\sigma}
is

-isogenous to
E_{0}
by an isogeny whose kernel has intersection with
\ker(E_{0}\to E_{4}^{\prime})
of order
6
. It follows that
E_{0}^{\sigma}
is isomorphic to
F_{1}^{\prime}
.

However, applying analogous reasoning to
E_{1}
and the isogeny
E_{1}\rightarrow E_{5}^{\prime}
, it follows that
E_{1}^{\sigma}
is isomorphic to
F_{2}^{\prime}
.

Since
E_{0}
and
E_{1}
are
2
-isogenous, it follows that
E_{0}^{\sigma}
and
E_{1}^{\sigma}
are
2
-isogenous. Hence,
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are
2
-isogenous. But now the
2
-isogeny graph is not a tree. This implies that all the curves
in the isogeny class have CM, contradicting our assumption.
∎

We can adapt the proof of this proposition to obtain a more general version.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
K
be a quadratic number field. Let
m,n
and
k
be positive integers such that
2^{k}
divides
n
. Suppose that any elliptic curve
E/K
with a
2^{m-1}n
-isogeny defined over
K
is
n
-isogenous over
K
to its Galois conjugate, with the intersection of kernels of the
n
-isogeny and the
2^{m-1}n
-isogeny of size
n/2^{k}
.
Then any elliptic curve
E/K
with a
2^{m}n
-isogeny over
K
is a CM curve.

Remark 4.4
.

Taking
m=5
,
n=12
and
k=1
, we recover the result of
Proposition

4.2
.

Proof.

Let
n=2^{r}\cdot s
, with
s
odd.
Suppose that there is a non-CM elliptic curve
E_{0}/K
with a
2^{m}\cdot n
-isogeny. Then we have the following
2
-isogeny diagram.
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ldots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{m+r-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{m+r}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{m+r-1}}

Each of the elliptic curves
E_{i}
and
F_{j}
in the diagram also has an

-isogenous curve
E_{i}^{\prime}
and
F_{j}^{\prime}
, and we have another
2
-isogeny diagram.
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{0}^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{1}^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E_{2}^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ldots}

with a

-isogeny is a CM curve.

∎
