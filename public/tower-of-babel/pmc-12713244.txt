1 Strengthening Governance and Institutional Frameworks for Maternal Health in India: Insights from the Pradhan Mantri Matru Vandana Yojana (PMMVY)

Neha Katoch1, Swinder Singh2

1Dept of Public Administration, Panjab University Chandigarh. India; 2Dept of Public Administration, CDOE, Panjab University Chandigarh, India

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(33):1

Keywords: Governance, Institutional Framework, Maternal Health, Pre-natal and Post natal care, financial support, Pradhan Mantri Matru Vandana Yojana (PMMVY) and Policy Implementation

Abstract: Introduction: Maternal health remains a pressing issue in India, necessitating robust governance structures and institutional frameworks. India continues to grapple with a high maternal mortality rate, emphasising the need for effective policies and well-structured implementation mechanisms. Launched in 2017 Pradhan Mantri Matru Vandana Yojana (PMMVY) stands as a pivotal initiative aimed at supporting maternal health through providing pre-natal care, post-natal care and financial support to pregnant and lactating women. This paper explores the governance structures and institutional frameworks underpinning PMMVY, offering insights into its effectiveness in addressing maternal health challenges in India. Methodology: This study employed a qualitative approach, utilising document analysis and stakeholder interviews to investigate the evaluation encompassed programme reach, utilisation, and impact on antenatal care, institutional deliveries, and postpartum health. Secondary document analysis involved examining policy documents, reports, and guidelines related to PMMVY to understand its objectives and operational framework. The primary data collection was conducted in the rural areas of Kangra district, Himachal Pradesh, as the district, being the most populous in the state, provided a representative setting for understanding the study objectives. Stakeholder interviews with government officials, healthcare providers, Anganwadi Workers, ASHAs and beneficiaries (Pregnant and Lactating women) provide perspectives on the implementation, challenges, and outcomes of PMMVY. Findings: The analysis reveals that PMMVY has significantly strengthened governance and institutional frameworks for maternal health in India. It has streamlined beneficiary identification processes, improved healthcare accessibility, and enhanced financial support for pregnant and lactating women. Stakeholders acknowledge the initiative's positive impact on reducing maternal mortality rates and improving maternal healthcare utilisation across various states. Challenges such as bureaucratic hurdles, reaching marginalised sections, timely disbursement of financial support and uneven implementation persist. Strengthening monitoring mechanisms and community engagement is crucial for sustained impact. Conclusion: In conclusion, PMMVY serves as a crucial pillar in India's efforts to bolster maternal health governance and institutional frameworks. By providing financial support and healthcare services, the initiative has contributed to improving maternal health outcomes and empowering women economically. Moving forward, sustained commitment to addressing implementation challenges and enhancing accountability will be essential to maximize the initiative's impact nationwide. As India continues its journey towards achieving maternal health targets, lessons from PMMVY underscore the importance of effective governance and institutional support in fostering positive health outcomes for mothers and children. 2 Governance and Health System Responsiveness: Evidence from Patna and Gaya Districts in Bihar, India

Kripa Nandan, Pratap Chandra Mohanty

Indian Institute of Technology Roorkee, Roorkee, India

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(33):2

Keywords: Health Governance, Health System Responsiveness, Patient Satisfaction, India

Abstract: Introduction: Effective governance is a key factor in the efficiency of health systems, impacting the quality, accessibility, and responsiveness of health care. Health System Responsiveness (HSR), which includes the non-medical and non-financial expectations of patients, directly influences the 3A's- Availability, Accessibility, and Affordability. These 3A's are indicators of the governance of the complex Indian Health System. This study uses the WHO framework that was given in 2000. The sampling involved purposive selection of Patna (Non-Aspirational) and Gaya (Aspirational), surveyed 700 households: 350 from each district, with 150 around Primary Health Centres, 50 around Community Health Centres, and 150 around Private Hospitals. Methodology: This study employed a cross-sectional survey using structured questionnaires to evaluate HSR across dimensions such as service quality, accessibility, and patient satisfaction. Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, multivariate regression, and principal component analysis (PCA), while qualitative data were subjected to thematic analysis to provide contextual depth. PCA reduced the complexity of numerous indicators into composite indices based on eigenvalues, encompassing seven key dimensions: dignity, autonomy, confidentiality, prompt attention, quality of amenities, clarity of communication/information, choice of providers, and access to a social support network. These dimensions formed the basis for constructing the HSR Index, which classifies data into five Likert-scale categories: excellent, good, average, poor, and very poor. Regression analysis further assessed the association of health governance and HSR indices with socio-economic and demographic factors. This comprehensive methodological approach enabled a nuanced evaluation of health system performance and highlighted the disparities in responsiveness across the surveyed regions. Results: In Gaya, over 80% of households reported waiting more than thirty minutes for treatment, whereas in Patna, only 6% were treated within that timeframe. Despite longer waits, Gaya respondents evaluated their waiting environment more favorably than those in Patna. No significant variation in waiting times was observed across caste groups. Confidentiality was reported as maintained by 87.39% of respondents, with no significant association between education level and perceptions of confidentiality (χ2 = 2.8994, p = 0.575). Experiences with doctors varied significantly by income, with higher income households reporting more positive interactions (χ2 = 30.0878, p = 0.003). Multivariate analysis revealed that the HSR index was positively associated with health governance (β = 0.085, p = 0.032), while annual income (β = 0.23, p < 0.001) and higher education improved outcomes. Conversely, residing in Patna, belonging to OBC or SC groups, and older age were associated with poorer governance, underscoring persistent socio-economic disparities. Conclusions: This study underscores the critical role of effective governance in shaping health system responsiveness, influencing quality, accessibility, and patient satisfaction. The findings reveal that higher income and education are strongly associated with improved governance outcomes, while regional, caste-based, and age-related disparities persist as barriers. The Health System Responsiveness Index, grounded in the WHO framework, provides nuanced insights into the non-medical determinants of healthcare, particularly confidentiality, dignity, and service access. Evidence from Patna and Gaya highlights the structural inequities that demand targeted policy interventions. Strengthening governance mechanisms is essential to enhance equity, accountability, and efficiency in India’s complex health system.

3 Understanding Kerala’s Palliative Care Model and Policy: A Realist Analysis of Three Witness Seminars Abstract

Gloria Benny1, Devaki Nambiar1,2,3, Hari Sankar4

1The George Institute for Global Health, New Delhi, India; 2Faculty of Medicine and Health, University of New South Wales, Sydney, Australia; 3Prasanna School of Public Health, Manipal Academy of Higher Education, Manipal, India; 4Institute of Tropical Medicine, Antwerp, Belgium

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(33):3

Keywords: Witness Seminar, Palliative care, Decentralisation

Abstract: Introduction India has been the locus of many social movements throughout history; it has been a champion of community and social participation in health globally. This includes the People’s Planning Campaign (PPC) movement, which began in 1996 in the southern Indian state of Kerala, and led to the devolution of power to Local Self-Governments (LSGs). Parallelly, Kerala has been a pioneer in developing community-based palliative care models which have received national and international renown. Our study aimed to understand the linkage between these two related developments; namely, decentralization efforts at LSG level and how they related to the development and institutionalization of tailored programs to address the needs of palliative care patients. Methods We employed an oral history technique called the Witness Seminar (WS) between June to September 2021, where the witnesses together described their experience of decentralization reforms in history. Three WS were conducted virtually with 21 participants from Kerala’s health department, LSG, and civil society who involved with the PPC. Seminars were video recorded. Member checking was carried out using the transcript documents that were transcribed both in English and Malayalam. Inductive thematic analysis of transcripts was carried out by a four-member research team using ATLAS.ti 9. WS transcripts were analysed using a realist approach where we identified Contexts (C), Mechanisms (M), and Outcomes (O) related to the decentralization movement and palliative care efforts in the state. Results Our analysis revealed one overarching “meta-context”, two key mechanisms, and four outcomes of note. To start with, Community Based Organizations (CBOs) developed home based palliative care programs which, following decentralisation reforms, could be expanded by LSGs with PHC linkages in northern and then southern districts of Kerala (C1). The state health department institutionalized and scaled up these programs using N(R)HM (M1). Over time, the state (LSGD) institutionalized interdepartmental convergence (M2). From the get-go, essential equipment and services were made available at the ward level for palliative patients (O1). Over time, palliative care was recognized as a mandatory program for LSG’s plan fund allocation and eventually a state policy was formulated (O2). This integration into policy, however, involved conflict and disagreement with CBO actors who saw the program as advancing without them (O3). As with other forms of institutionalisation, earmarked palliative care funds have vexed integration with other funding mechanisms and reduce appetite for further innovation, even as this has been essential for the palliative care program to be established in the first instance (O4). Conclusion The provision of palliative care was recognized as an essential health service delivery component by the different stakeholders resulted in the formulation of a state policy for palliative care in 2008. Over time, a number of challenges and contradictions have emerged that are recognised. And yet, as acknowledged by the 2022 Lancet Commission on the Value of Death, Kerala’s palliative care programmes and policy truly represent a “realistic utopia” of holistic care for the dying. In addition, this work was supported by an internal grant awarded to the Health Equity Action Lab by the George Institute for Global Health. 4 Responsive Governance in Complex Urban Health Systems: The Case of Adolescent Health in Surat, India

Anuj Prakash Ghanekar1, Mohit Sood1, Khushbu Chauhan1, Vikas Desai2, Pradip Umrigar3, Ashish Naik4

1Former Consultant, Urban Health and Climate Resilience Centre of Excellence (UHCRCE), Surat, India; 2Hon. Technical Advisor, Urban Health and Climate Resilience Centre of Excellence (UHCRCE), Surat, India; 3Medical Officer of Health, Surat Municipal Corporation (SMC), Surat, India; 4Deputy Commissioner, Health and Hospitals, Surat Municipal Corporation (SMC), Surat, India

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(33):4

Keywords: Adolescents, Responsiveness, RKSK, Surat city, Urban Health

Abstract: 1 Introduction Urban adolescents face exclusive health vulnerabilities that demand responsive and integrated health systems. Sustainable Development Goal 3 is directly linked to the health of adolescents. More than a third of the disease burden and nearly 60% of premature deaths among adults can be traced back to behaviours that originated during adolescence. The Rashtriya Kishor Swasthya Karyakram (RKSK) program in India aims to address the multifaceted adolescent health concerns. However, implementing such programs in complex urban health systems presents significant governance challenges. This study examined the responsiveness of Urban Primary Health Centres (UPHCs) in Surat, India towards RKSK preparedness. 2 Methods Surat, a city in Western India, was one of those few cities where 24 UPHCs were selected as urban pilot models for RKSK. This research adopted an embedded case approach where one type of case was nested within the broader case. The broader first-level case was the “on-ground policy rollout of urban RKSK”. This was investigated by examining the two specific case-study UPHCs. Data collection, conducted in 2022, primarily involved qualitative data, supplemented with some quantitative data. Multiple methods were employed to ensure a comprehensive understanding of the situation. The perspectives of relevant health and non-health service providers, (n=67) as well as representative adolescents who use and do not use UPHC services, (n=162) were taken into account. The observation of health centre processes was completed for 138 hours. The analysis through reflexive iteration utilized the theoretical underpinnings of the “7C” framework of RKSK (Coverage, Content, Communities, Clinics, Counselling, Communication, Convergence). 3 Results The study revealed several enablers in UPHC responsiveness to adolescent health needs, including well-defined catchment areas, annual Family Health Surveys, existing infrastructure, and the crucial role of Community Health Workers in linking adolescents to health services. However, challenges persisted, such as limited visibility and awareness of services, inadequate promotion, gaps in patient management, restricted accessibility, staff demotivation, and insufficient language compatibility for migrant populations. Implementation delays were observed in RKSK platforms, and counselling services primarily focused on physical health issues. There was a lack of adolescent participation in health service planning and fragmented convergence among stakeholders. Key recommendations emerged for enhancing UPHC responsiveness, including improving visibility and accessibility, adapting service timings, expanding counselling scope, strengthening staff capacity, and fostering better convergence processes. Restructuring the urban healthcare service pyramid, enhancing quality data collection and analysis, and leveraging technology for communication were also suggested to prepare for RKSK in urban settings. 4 Conclusion This study underscored the need for robust governance structures and adaptive institutional frameworks to address the complex challenges of adolescent health in urban settings. The findings highlighted the importance of cross-sectoral collaboration, flexible policy implementation, and participatory decision-making processes that involve adolescents as key stakeholders. To create truly responsive adolescent health services, urban health systems must evolve beyond siloed approaches and embrace integrated, multi-level governance models. This requires reimagining institutional arrangements, fostering innovative partnerships, and developing context-specific policies that can navigate the complexities of urban environments. 5 Navigating complex health systems during a pandemic: Lessons from India’s COVID −19 Vaccination Program

Palak Nayan Agarwal1, Drishti Sharma1, Keertana Gogia1, Sudipto Roy2, Rajib Dasgupta3, Saif ul Hadi4, Rajat Goyal4

1Health Systems and Policy Research, International AIDS Vaccine Initiative, New Delhi, India; 2Scientist E, Indian Council for Medical Research (ICMR), New Delhi, India; 3Professor and Chairperson, Centre of Social Medicine and Community Health, Jawaharlal Nehru University (JNU), New Delhi, India; 4, International AIDS Vaccine Initiative, New Delhi, India

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(33):5

Keywords: Pandemic preparedness, Health Systems, vaccine introduction, health policy.

Abstract: Introduction: India's population strains its three-tiered public health system, which is primarily utilized by the most marginalized population, and operates alongside a private sector. Coupled with India's dual federal governance structure, this creates complexities in last mile healthcare service delivery. Despite these hurdles, India's COVID vaccination program, world's largest, achieved remarkable success and achieved a coverage of 83% for the first dose and 63% for the second dose within a year of its rollout. This analysis aims to explores the key policy interventions implemented within the existing health system that led to equitable and timely COVID-19 vaccine coverage in India. Methods: A search strategy was utilized to consolidate documents for Qualitative Document Analysis. Firstly, relevant online data bases like government websites which disseminated information on COVID-19 Vaccines or COVID-19 Vaccination program were identified. Secondly, documents pertaining to COVID-19 vaccination program, and COVID-19 vaccines were manually searched in the databases. Documents like guidelines, strategy documents, press releases, parliamentary questions, High-level meeting notes, standing committee reports etc. were sourced. Thirdly the documents were screened as per inclusion and exclusion criteria. The screened documents were imported in NVIVO-12 for qualitative analysis. The documents were then analyzed using the inductive saturation model to code emergent themes by independent reviewers who met to agree upon themes through consensus. Overall, 433 documents were analyzed in 2 rounds of coding. Results: A total of 12 themes were Identified across 4 domains: Policy interventions for ensuring vaccine availability (3 themes) vaccine affordability (2 themes), vaccine accessibility (4 themes) and vaccine acceptability (3 themes). The policy interventions aimed at strengthening health systems factors like infrastructure, human resource, appropriate technology, governance, financing and community outreach were identified. Multi-sectoral collaboration was another Intervention which contributed to effective and timely covid-19 vaccination rollout. The role of National Health Mission from conceptualizing to implementing the vaccination program was of the prime importance and helped unify the state and national health care service delivery machinery. Conclusion: The analysis highlights how interventions at policy level and their implementation by the government of India during the pandemic, enabled a conducive environment to find solutions for challenges faced by a complex health system. Strengthening the research and development infrastructure, streamlining availability through accelerated vaccine manufacturing, enabling scientific regulatory mechanisms, centralized vaccine procurement, rational allocation, rapid human resource deployment and capacity building, utilizing technology for service delivery, addressing structural barriers, deploying effective communication strategies are some of the many interventions implemented through established institutional mechanisms that ensured equitable and timely vaccine access. Many of these interventions can be replicated for incorporation in the existing programs in addition to pandemic preparedness. Nonetheless the document analysis is comprehensive and has tried to incorporate the program's strengths as well as challenges.

6 Role of Health and Wellness Centers in Improving Access and Care for Non-Communicable Diseases in Chhattisgarh, India

Narayan Tripathi, Priyanka Parhad, Kirtti K Bebarta, Vikash R Keshri

State Health Resource Centre (SHRC), Chhattisgarh, India

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(33):6

Keywords: Health and Wellness Centers, Access, Non-Communicable Diseases, Primary care, Ayushman Bharat

Abstract: Introduction: Burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) continues to grow in Low-middle income countries (LMICs) including India. Hypertension and diabetes mellitus are two mains common NCDs in India. In India around 31% of its 30–79 years population is estimated to have hypertension and around two-third of these are not aware about their disease condition. This showed the poor availability of population-based screening of NCDs. India, has proved a growing commitment to Universal Health Coverage (UHC)and launched the Ayushman Bharat Yojana, which includes establishing Health and Wellness Centers (HWCs) by upgrading the existing subcenters. These HWCs offer an expanded range of primary care services, including the management and follow-up care for non-communicable diseases (NCDs). This study evaluates role of HWCs in management of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) in rural Chhattisgarh, India. Method: This study used two data sets, first it used a cross-sectional health facility assessment with a state-representative sample of 400 HWCs. Second it uses the data of two waves of state representative household surveys were conducted in 2019 and 2022. The survey included sample above age of 30 years in the survey. By household survey data sets the role of HWC on detection of NCD was assessed. In health facility assessment a structured tool was used to collect information on essential inputs and health service outputs of HWCs. By facility assessments the performance of HWCs on NCD care was assessed by comparing the NCD output of HWCs against the expected NCD need of the population. Results: Findings from the health facility assessments showed that of monthly patients' footfall at HWC 38% of the patients are of NCDs. HWC are able to provide almost one-fourth of hypertension and one-fifth of diabetes care needs in the rural population. Findings from the two rounds of the household survey showed that the population covered under HWCs had 25% greater chance for detection of NCDs when compare with the population without HWCs. Whereas the NCD patients from the HWC areas had 70% greater chance of accessing public healthcare facilities. Conclusion: The current study concludes that the HWC initiative is bearing fruit in Chhattisgarh though these are early experiences. HWCs have a provision of screening of 30 years above population for two main NCDs i.e. Hypertension and diabetes. If screened positive HWC ensure the confirm diagnosis and treatment initiation along with monthly follow up and drug distribution. Its proximity to the population makes HWCs more useful to the population in term of access. Majority of the NCD cases are from older age group and they find HWC very accessible for their healthcare needs. HWCs might prove to be a crucial architectural modification that made a big difference to provision of primary care services for India's population.

7 Unveiling Gender Disparities in Cardiovascular Disease Burden: Insights from India

Trupti Patil, Arnab Jana, Sarthak Gaurav

Indian Institute of Technology Bombay, Mumbai, India

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(33):7

Keywords: Non-communicable diseases, cardiovascular disease, women, India

Abstract: Introduction: Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) constitute a significant portion of the global and Indian disease burden. Although CVDs affect men more than women, several factors contribute to disparities in their detection and management. Mechanisms specific to sex and gender in women with CVDs remain understudied, resulting in under-recognition, underdiagnosis, and undertreatment, thereby exacerbating the condition in women. Currently, there is no globally recognized policy or SDG specifically targeting CVDs in women. Hence, our study examines the sex-specific and other CVD risk factors among women and men (in India) and evaluates policy instruments (in Maharashtra) to address gaps in health systems' responsiveness to gender-specific health needs. Methods: Guided by the WHO causal framework, we employed a mixed-methods case study approach. We utilized the Multiple Indicators and Multiple Causes (MIMIC) models with data from the National Family Health Survey 5 and complemented it with analysis of policy instrument documents. Results: The MIMIC model suggests metabolic, behavioural, and gender-and-sex-related factors explain 10% of the variability in the CVD burden in women. Whereas metabolic and behavioural risk factors alone account for 19% of the variability in men. Overweight/obesity affects CVD burden similarly in both genders, but raised blood glucose, tobacco and alcohol consumption have a greater impact on men. Gender negatively affects CVD burden, while sex has a positive contribution specifically in women. The resulting model demonstrated good fit measures for women (CFI = 0.817; TLI = 0.763; RMSEA = 0.046) and men (CFI = 0.962; TLI = 0.939; RMSEA = 0.035). Among four sectors—health, education, women’s empowerment, and nutrition—13 policies (59%) highlighted gender, but health policies on CVDs excluded women. Main themes to strengthen policies encompass: 1) enabling decentralization through delegation, devolution, and privatization; 2) strengthening need-based monitoring and evaluation through enforceability, accountability, and technology; 3) expanding equitable and quality services through systems development and improved access. Conclusion: MIMIC models indicate that the CVD burden is linked to metabolic, behavioural, and sex- and gender-specific risk factors, with notable differences observed between women and men. The interplay of gender-specific and sex-specific risk factors across these domains emphasizes the need for gender-responsive interventions to address the disparities in the CVD burden among women and men in India. These insights emphasize the importance of exercising one-size-fits-all health interventions, urging a nuanced approach for greater equity. 8 The Need for Institutionalizing Evidence-to-Policy Process through Building a Health System Research Governance

Shita Listya Dewi, Kadek Darmawan

Center for Health Policy and Management, Faculty of Medicine, Public Health and Nursing, Universitas Gadjah Mada, Yogyakarta, Indonesia

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(33):8

Abstract: Background: Despite efforts to prioritize research and development, there remains a significant gap between policy research findings and their application in health policy formulation and implementation in Indonesia. Yet, evidence-based policies are crucial for improving health outcomes, allocating resources efficiently, and addressing the country's pressing health challenges. Additionally, fragmented data management, limited research capacity, and underfunded research infrastructure further exacerbate the problem of institutionalizing evidence-to-policy process. There seems to be unclear path of evidence-based health policy and practice in Indonesia. Method: The authors used a review of literature and policy documents on health policy research issues in Indonesia. The authors examined national development plans, health sector strategies, and policy research governance frameworks. The review included identifying key challenges, opportunities, and stakeholders in the research-to-policy process. The approach was primarily qualitative, focusing on synthesizing information from diverse sources to provide a holistic view of the issue. Results: The review revealed significant gaps in Indonesia's health system and policy research ecosystem. A disconnect between policy researchers, policymakers, and practitioners hinders effective knowledge translation. Policy research priorities often misaligned with national health goals, and there was limited involvement of policy research actors in the process. Data management was fragmented, with poor integration between different surveys and databases, impeding comprehensive health policy analysis. Human resource and institutional capacity for conducting and translating policy research was limited, with a shortage of policy researchers and inadequate funding. The government has made efforts to improve the situation, such as establishing the national research agency (BRIN) and repositioned the research unit within the Ministry of Health to become a health policy analysis unit (BKPK). However, challenges persist in institutionalizing evidence-based policy and practice. The health system research landscape was characterized by siloed efforts, limited public participation, and inadequate structured mechanisms for translating research into actionable policies. These factors collectively contribute to the underutilization of research in health policy formulation and implementation. In a convening between health system research institution and policymakers in Jakarta on 2024, the need and opportunity to build a working and preferred governance of health system research was apparent in the discussion. Conclusions and Recommendations: Indonesia's health research ecosystem faced significant challenges in translating evidence into policy and practice. This occurs in the context of limited policy research governance, fragmented data systems, and insufficient capacity and funding. To address these issues, there needs to be a comprehensive strategy for policy research governance at the national level, improving human resource and institutional capacity, supporting the development of robust health research ecosystems, promoting relevant and ethical research practices, and fostering the utilization of research in policy and practice. Specifically, a few things need to happen. First, formulating a strategic health policy research plan and mechanism, followed by establishing platforms for experts/advisory board to advise on prioritization of policy analysis and evaluation. This platform should be use to aligning research with national priorities, supporting interdisciplinary research, ensuring public and non-governmental research institutions’ participation, and integrating health research data.

9 Meaningful Engagement of People Living with Non-Communicable Diseases “Nothing for Us, Without Us”: Findings from Stakeholder Workshop in Nepal

Gambhir Shrestha1,2, Shristi Singh1, Pramila KC3, Anu Gomanju4, Kishore Kumar Pradhananga1, Prakash Raj Regmi1, Shanta Lall Mulmi1, Abhinav Vaidya1,5

1Nepal Non-communicable Diseases Alliance, Kathmandu, Nepal; 2Department of Community Medicine and Public Health, Maharajgunj Medical Campus, Institute of Medicine, Kathmandu, Nepal; 3Nepalese Cancer Survivors’ Society, Lalitpur, Nepal; 4Kathmandu Institute of Child Health, Kathmandu, Nepal; 5Department of Community Medicine, Kathmandu Medical College, Kathmandu, Nepal

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(33):9

Keywords: Non-communicable diseases, advocacy, policy, meaningful engagement

Abstract: Background: Despite the existence of Nepal's Multi-Sectoral Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of NCDs (2021-2025) that recognizes the importance of engaging critical stakeholders, including People Living with NCDs (PLWNCDs) in policy dialogues, there has been a noticeable gap in implementing initiatives and programs for their active involvement. To bridge this gap, Nepal NCD Alliance organized a one-day workshop themed "Nothing for Us, Without Us" on November 29, 2023. Its objectives were to create a platform for PLWNCDs to share their experiences, challenges, and success stories, and develop strategies for their meaningful engagement. Methods: The workshop was divided into three sessions: 1. Setting the scene: It featured engaging stories from PLWNCDs such as chronic respiratory diseases, cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, hemophilia, stroke, and cancer to understand the situation of NCDs and their impact on their lives, challenges, and supportive mechanisms. 2. Panel discussions: Panels of PLWNCDs, healthcare professionals, policymakers, and representatives from civil society organizations engaged in discussions on challenges, best practices, and potential policy interventions. 3. Results: A total of 76 participants representing PLWNCDs, policymakers, healthcare professionals, and national and international agencies took part in the workshop. The PLWNCDs shared experiences ranging from financial hardships to the impact of NCDs on personal relationships and societal expectations such as gender discrimination, and the complex interplay of economic, social, and mental health challenges. Despite these challenges, family support and determination play crucial roles in individuals pursuing education, professional achievements, and advocating for improved healthcare infrastructure and awareness. The collective findings from panel discussions and group activities envisioned a future where PLWNCDs actively engage in a patient-centric multidisciplinary mechanism, promoting influential participation and community programs. Challenges identified encompass superstitions, financial barriers, lack of awareness, and societal discrimination. Proposed solutions ranged from policy reforms, enhanced coordination, and advocacy efforts to increased social protection, accessible NCD treatment, and roadmap development for PLWNCD engagement in policy planning. Conclusions: The workshop provided a comprehensive platform for stakeholders to collaboratively envision and strategize the meaningful engagement of PLWNCDs in Nepal. The findings emphasize the importance of targeted policies, increased awareness campaigns, and the development of a roadmap for meaningful engagement of PLWNCDs.

10 Digitally enabled access to medicines: exploring the e-pharmacy ecosystem in India

Jaison Joseph1, Sammy Masibo2, Irene Khayoni2, Francis Wafula2, Gautam Satheesh1, Krishna Nandakumar1, Abdul Salam1, Benjamin Palafox5, Catherine Goodman5, Devaki Nambiar1,3,4

1The George Institute for Global Health, India; 2Strathmore Business School, Nairobi, Kenya; 3Faculty of Medicine, University of New South Wales, Sydney, Australia; 4Prasanna School of Public Health, Manipal Academy of Higher Education, Manipal, India; 5London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine, London, UK

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(33):10

Keywords: e-pharmacies, online pharmacies, digital pharmacies, access to medicines, e-pharmacy market, eco-systems. digital platforms

Abstract: Background In low- and middle-Income countries (LMICs), the growth of the e-pharmacy sector is revolutionizing how people access medications. However, challenges in ensuring the quality of pharmaceutical care, data privacy and regulatory compliance may hinder this sector's contribution to developing more equitable, high quality health systems. As part of a larger study examining these features of the e-pharmacy landscape in two countries, we explored the business models, distribution, and other partnership strategies of e-pharmacies in India. Methods Data for this study were gathered through in-depth interviews (IDIs) with professionals working in or knowledgeable about the e-pharmacy sector, identified via a review of e-pharmacy websites and recommendations from individuals knowledgeable of the commercial and regulatory landscape in India. We conducted nine IDIs in English with professionals from purposively sampled large- and small-scale e-pharmacies in India. Prior to each interview, detailed study information was shared with each participant. Informed consent was sought, and participants described their e-pharmacy business model, unique selling propositions, quality assurance mechanisms, opinions on existing regulations, major challenges faced by e-pharmacies and their suggestions to address these challenges. AI-generated transcripts were checked manually by the lead author and then thematically analyzed in ATLAS.ti 23.3.4 using an inductive approach. Multiple meetings were held of the research team, and Miro used to index findings and examine them in 4–5 group meetings held over a period of three months. Results We found three distinct e-pharmacy models: online-only, hybrid (brick-and-mortar pharmacies also operating online), and "marketplace-like" e-pharmacy aggregators (multiple vendors retailing medicines via a common e-commerce platform). Participants noted that the models were predicated on increasing convenience, turnaround times and convenience for consumers. E-pharmacies partnered with payment gateways and courier partners for delivery. Uploaded prescriptions were validated by a pharmacist (sometimes facilitated by artificial intelligence); orders without prescriptions were referred to a doctor for tele-consultation. Many e-pharmacies partnered with insurance companies, laboratory providers, patient support groups, and wellness clinics to offer comprehensive services. In the absence of regulations specific to e-pharmacies, respondents stated that they complied with the existing brick-and-mortar pharmacy regulations and those by the Insurance Regulatory Authority of India. Participants mentioned the lack of regulation as a major challenge, expressing the need to establish comprehensive regulations or a specific subsection within the existing brick-and-mortar pharmacy regulation for e-pharmacies. Conclusion Our findings offer valuable insights into how the diverse and innovative business models, partnerships and practices of e-pharmacies in India could be leveraged to improve the availability, appropriateness, quality and affordability of medicines, particularly for those currently underserved by traditional brick-and-mortar retail pharmacies. However, harnessing this potential and translating it into a public good requires effective stewardship of this rapidly growing market, of which a robust regulatory framework specific to e-pharmacies must be a core element. 11 Is digital health an enabler or barrier to person-centred care?: the case of India’s Voice Against AIDS app

Carolyn Kavita Tauro, Sundeep Sahay

Department of Informatics, University of Oslo, Norway

BMC Proceedings 2025, 19(33):11

Keywords: person-centred HIV care, socio-technical issues, tech policy

Abstract: Introduction: The fourth strategic objective of WHO's Global strategy on digital health 2020-2025 is to 'advocate people-centred health systems that are enabled by digital health'. Its policy options and actions propose placing people at the centre of digital health transformation, population level digital health applications, mechanisms for effective public participation and transparency and the promotion of an inclusive digital society. Providing person-centred care (PCC) has been the focus of HIV care; although with heterogeneity in the PCC components addressed, actors involved and expected outcomes in PCC interventions. Information technology, such as mobile apps, used in HIV care have evolved rapidly bringing opportunities to provide continued care at facility level, and educate and support patients through improved engagement. However, modern technology is also known to have unintended consequences. In this paper, we explore the role of technology as an enabler or barrier of person-centred HIV care in India. Methods: We conducted a qualitative research - the case of India's Voice against AIDS - a mobile app launched in 2020 to engage with individuals enrolled in the national HIV program in India. Data were collected from February 2021 to October 2022. We carried out a cultural probe designed to document responses towards the information-sharing mobile app. Additionally, 27 interviews, 5 focus group discussions with 5–9 participants each, and several informal individual and group discussions were held. Participants included information systems experts, HIV care consultants and people with lived experiences, at a northern and north-eastern state of India. Documents related to the HIV/AIDS Act (2017) were also studied. Data were thematically analysed by iterative categorisation, using NVivo. Essential ethical measures were made during this study. Results: The IEC app worked as an enabler to PCC that engaged with, and educated potential HIV programme beneficiaries on their care pathway. Usability of the app in multiple local languages and information on nation-wide social protection schemes ensured that more individuals could access health care, promoting greater inclusivity. Information on locations and work timings of service centres ensured that patients could organise their healthcare schedule around their personal and work life. Facts around HIV/AIDS made misinformation defunct so that the app became a reliable information platform. In contrast, some features of the app made it a barrier to PCC. The use of strong instructions, negative terminology, images and colour, stigmatised PWUDs even before they could enter a health care setting. Furthermore, the HIV/AIDS Act included in the app to spread information continued to leave patients to deal with issues of stigmatisation, discrimination and violence in society. Conclusion: The interpersonal component of care as well as the need to translate legislation into action through and despite digital health cannot be underestimated. Our findings highlight the potential of digital health in enhancing care in complex health programs, such as integrated person-centred HIV care. They also highlight socio-technical issues where technology become barriers to care. Such findings imply the prioritisation and expansion of dialogue spaces where such issues are identified to overcome structural barriers to the delivery of person-centred care and socially safe environments. Future research could focus on developing strategies that aim at user-centred technology in health that bridge the socio-technical gaps between the provider and the patient.
