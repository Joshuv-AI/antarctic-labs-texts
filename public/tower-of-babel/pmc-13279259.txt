High‐Salt Diet–Induced Endothelial Dysfunction Is Mediated by Cellular Senescence
Authors: André F. Nascimento, Renata A. M. Luvizotto, Rafael M. Costa, Gustavo F. Pimenta, Ariane Bruder, Thiago Bruder‐Nascimento
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh salt (HS) intake is a known cardiovascular risk factor, yet the mechanisms linking salt intake to endothelial dysfunction remain unclear. We investigated whether HS induces vascular senescence and dysfunction, and whether targeting senescent cells could prevent these effects.MethodsMale C57BL/6J mice were fed a high‐salt diet (HSD [8% NaCl]) for 14 or 28 days. Vascular function was assessed in thoracic aortae and mesenteric arteries using wire myography. Senescence was evaluated by p21, p16, and interleukins (IL) 6 and 1β expression in endothelial cells and whole vessels. To test reversibility, mice received the senolytic drug navitoclax.ResultsA 28‐day, but not 14‐day, HSD caused endothelial dysfunction and increased vascular senescence markers, indicating senescence. Navitoclax treatment reduced senescence markers, restored vasodilation of mesenteric arteries, and improved vascular contractility. HSD exposure elevated inflammatory gene expression in peritoneal immune cells and increased circulating levels of interleukin‐16 (IL16). Thus, we treated mesenteric arteries and mesenteric endothelial cells with recombinant IL16 to analyze whether HSD can induce vascular dysfunction via senescence. IL16 caused endothelial dysfunction and increased p21 expression.ConclusionsProlonged HSD intake induces vascular senescence and dysfunction via immune activation rather than direct endothelial dysfunction, while senolytic therapy prevents HSD‐induced vascular dysfunction. Targeting senescence or inflammation, particularly IL16 signaling, may offer new therapeutic strategies for salt‐sensitive vascular diseases.

High salt intake is a major dietary risk factor for cardiometabolic and vascular disorders, particularly cardiovascular diseases, 1 which account for an estimated 19.1 million deaths globally. 2 Despite recommendations from the American Heart Association to limit sodium intake to 2300 mg/d, average daily consumption remains around 9 g, 3 and unfortunately trends in sodium intake have not changed over the years. 4

While sodium is essential for physiological balance, excessive intake disrupts ion homeostasis and activates the sympathetic nervous system and the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, contributing to insulin resistance and multisystem dysfunction. 5 , 6 Emerging evidence also implicates high salt in modulating immune cell activation, contributing to the development of vascular and cardiorenal damage. 7 , 8 , 9 In addition, excessive salt has been shown to disrupt the endothelial glycocalyx, increase reactive oxygen species production, enhance endothelial stiffness, and alter epithelial sodium channel activity. 10 , 11 , 12 Although the association between a high‐salt diet (HSD) and increased vascular risk is well recognized, the specific cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying salt‐induced vascular injury remain incompletely understood.

Cellular senescence is a stress‐induced state of irreversible growth arrest accompanied by the secretion of pro‐inflammatory factors, collectively known as the senescence‐associated secretory phenotype. 13 , 14 While senescence plays beneficial roles in tumor suppression and wound repair, 15 , 16 the pathological accumulation of senescent cells contributes to tissue dysfunction, including vascular damage. In endothelial cells, senescence is associated with oxidative stress, reduced nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, inflammation, and dysfunction, 17 , 18 , 19 but whether endothelial senescence predicts high‐salt–induced endothelial dysfunction is unknown.

A high‐salt environment generates a broad array of stressors, including oxidative stress and inflammation, that can promote cellular senescence. We therefore hypothesized that endothelial senescence precedes and contributes to endothelial dysfunction during high salt exposure. To test this, mice were fed an HSD for 4 weeks, with or without the senolytic agent navitoclax, and vascular senescence and endothelial function were subsequently assessed. Additionally, we investigated whether high salt directly induces endothelial senescence or whether this effect is mediated through immune system activation.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Mice

Eight‐to 13‐week‐old male C57BL6/J mice were used. Mice were housed in an American Association of Laboratory Animal Care–approved animal care facility in the vivarium at the Department of Physiology and Cell Biology at the University of South Alabama College of Medicine (Mobile, AL). The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee approved all protocols (IACUC Protocol No. 2219557). All experiments were performed in the Department of Physiology and Cell Biology at the University of South Alabama and were in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

High‐Salt Treatment

As described previously, mice were placed on an HSD (8% NaCl) for 14 days or 28 days 20 , 21 , 22 ; a group of mice receiving standard diet (0.49% NaCl) was used as control.

Senolytic Drug Treatment

We used navitoclax (ABT263), which is 1 of the most potent and well‐characterized senolytics with documented efficacy in eliminating senescent cells in preclinical models. 23 , 24 Mice received intraperitoneal injections of navitoclax at a dose of 50 mg/kg per day for 28 days (2 cycles of 5 days on and 14 days off). 23 Navitoclax, obtained from Selleck, was initially dissolved in DMSO (5%) and then resuspended in corn oil (95%). At euthanization, blood samples were collected, and tissues were harvested and processed for subsequent analyses. The heart, liver, spleen, and kidney masses were adjusted relative to the body mass of the mice.

Vascular Function

Vascular reactivity experiments were conducted on thoracic aortae and mesenteric arteries, following protocols previously established by our group. 25 , 26 Thoracic aortae and resistance mesenteric arteries were carefully dissected free of connective tissue, cut into 2‐mm rings, and mounted on a wire myograph (Danish Myo Technology, East Jutland, Denmark) for isometric tension recording using PowerLab software (AD Instruments, South Island, New Zealand). The arterial rings were placed in tissue baths containing warmed (37 °C), aerated (95% O2, 5% CO2) Krebs Henseleit Solution (in mmol/L: 130 NaCl, 4.7 KCl, 1.17 MgSO4, 0.03 EDTA, 1.6 CaCl2, 14.9 NaHCO3, 1.18 KH2PO4, and 5.5 glucose).

For the thoracic aortae, a pretension of 5 mN was applied, while for resistance arteries, tension was set through normalization, as detailed in preview publication. 26 After 30 minutes of stabilization, KCl (60 mmol/L) was used to test arterial viability. Concentration‐effect curves for phenylephrine (α1‐adrenergic receptor–dependent vasoconstrictor), acetylcholine (endothelium‐dependent vasodilator), and sodium nitroprusside (SNP; endothelium‐independent vasodilator) were performed. Some experiments were performed in the presence of N‐nitro‐L‐arginine methylester (L‐NAME; 100 μmol/L, a nitric oxide [NO] synthase inhibitor; Cayman Chemical Co., Ann Arbor, MI) to analyze the involvement of NO on high‐salt–induced vascular dysfunction.

Ex Vivo Protocol for Isolated Mesenteric Artery Incubation With Interleukin‐16

Mesenteric arteries were isolated from 12‐week‐old male C57BL/6J mice and carefully cleaned of perivascular adipose tissue. The arteries were then incubated with recombinant interleukin‐16 (IL16, SinoBiological, Chesterbrook, PA) at a concentration of 50 ng/mL 27 for 24 hours in DMEM, following a previously described protocol. 26 After incubation, the arteries were mounted in a wire myograph to assess vascular function as described above.

Fresh Isolation of Mesenteric Endothelial Cells for Senescence Characterization

We isolated endothelial cells from the mesenteric bed, as described before. 25 Mice were euthanized, and their mesenteric beds were excised and pooled, washed in PBS, and diced into small pieces, which were incubated in DMEM (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), containing10% FBS, 2 mg/mL of collagenase II and 40 mg/mL dispase‐II at 37 °C for 1 hour while shaking. The cell suspension was vigorously vortexed and meshed through 40‐μm nylon cell strainers (Fisherbrand; Thermo Fisher Scientific). After centrifugation, the cell pellet was resuspended in 1× PBS with 0.5% BSA and 2 mmol/L EDTA. Endothelial cells were labeled with CD31‐conjugated magnetic microbeads and sorted using magnetic separation LS columns (Miltenyi Biotech, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). mRNA was isolated as described below, and the purity of endothelial cells was checked by evaluating endothelial cell marker CD31. mRNA expression of p21, p16, interleukin‐6 (IL6), and interleukin‐1β (IL1β) was evaluated to characterize vascular senescence in high‐salt–treated mice. Flow‐through (FT, CD31− cells mainly constituted by vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), fibroblasts, and immune cells) was saved to analyze if a high‐salt diet can induce senescence in the entire vasculature or only in endothelial cells.

Isolation of Mouse Peritoneal Cavity Cells

As previously described, 28 the peritoneal cavity was flushed with 5 mL of PBS containing EDTA (0.01 g/mL) at the time of euthanasia for immune cells harvest. The lavage fluid was centrifuged at 12000g for 10 minutes to collect the cell pellets, which were then washed 3 times and stored at −80 °C for future immune cell analysis.

Proteinuria

Mice were housed in metabolic cages for 24‐hour urine collection. Once collected, urine samples were stored at −80 °C. Proteinuria was assessed as previously described. 25 A volume of 15 μL of each urine sample was separated on 10% SDS‐PAGE gels and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue (Sigma‐Aldrich, Burlington, MA). Albumin (15 μg) was used as a reference control.

Circulating Inflammatory Profile

Plasma levels of circulating chemokines and cytokines were analyzed using pooled samples from 6 mice per group. The analysis was performed with the Proteome Profiler Mouse Cytokine Array Kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN), following the manufacturer's protocol. Band intensities were quantified using the ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA), and the results were presented as fold changes in a heatmap graph.

Histology

Thoracic aortae, livers, hearts, and kidneys were collected and placed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for histologic analysis. After 12 hours in paraformaldehyde, tissues were placed in 70% ethanol until the day of preparing the samples for histology. Tissues were embedded in paraffin, and then samples were sectioned and stained with hematoxylin and eosin.

Plasmatic β‐Hydroxybutyrate

β‐hydroxybutyrate measurement was performed in plasma samples via a colorimetric assay according to the manufacturer's instructions (Cayman Chemical Co.).

Endothelial Cell Culture and Treatment

Mouse mesenteric endothelial cells (MECs) were obtained from Cell Biologics (Chicago, IL; No. C57‐6055) and cultured in Complete Mouse Endothelial Cell Medium supplemented with the Endothelial Cell Medium Supplement Kit (Cell Biologics; No. M1168). Cells between passages 4 and 8 were used for experiments.

To determine whether high salt directly induces endothelial cell senescence, MECs were exposed to a high‐salt medium (190 mmol/L NaCl) 29 for 0 to 96 hours. According to the manufacturer, the basal MEC culture medium contains ≈150 mmol/L NaCl; therefore, an additional 40 mmol/L NaCl was added to achieve the desired final concentration of 190 mmol/L. To assess whether high salt potentiates the senescence process, cells were exposed to the same NaCl concentration and duration in combination with bleomycin (10 μmol/L), a well‐established senescence inducer. 30 Mannitol (190 mmol/L) served as an osmotic control for NaCl, and cells maintained in regular medium (150 mmol/L NaCl) were used as controls.

SA‐ß‐Galactosidase Staining

MECs were seeded in 6‐well plates and treated with recombinant IL16 for 24 hours. SA‐ß‐galactosidase staining was performed using a SA‐ß‐galactosidase staining kit (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA; No. 9860) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Senescent cells were identified as blue‐stained cells under a Keyence microscope. For each experiment, 3 images representing each well were captured and were blindly assessed for SA‐ß‐galactosidase (blue) cell counting.

Western Blot

Aortic and mesenteric protein was extracted using RIPA buffer (30 mM HEPES, pH 7.4150 mmol/L NaCl, 1% Nonidet P‐40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 5 mmol/L EDTA, 1 mmol/L NaV04, 50 mmol/L NaF, 1 mmol/L PMSF, 10% pepstatin A, 10 μg/mL leupeptin, and 10 μg/mL aprotinin). Total protein extracts were centrifuged at 15 000 rpm/10 min and the pellet was discarded. Proteins from homogenates of aortae (25 μg) were used. Proteins were separated by electrophoresis on a polyacrylamide gradient gel (Bio‐Rad Laboratories) and transferred to Immobilon‐P polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 5% skim milk or 1% BSA in Ttris‐buffered saline solution with Tween for 1 hour at 24 °C. Membranes were then incubated with specific antibodies overnight at 4 °C as described in Tables S1. After incubation with secondary antibodies, the enhanced chemiluminescence luminol reagent (SuperSignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate, Thermo‐Scientific; No. 34095) was used for antibody detection.

Real‐Time Polymerase Chain Reaction

mRNA from mesenteric arteries, mouse peritoneal cavity cells and MECs were extracted using RNeasy Mini Kit (Quiagen, North Rhine‐Westphalia, Germany; No. 74106). Complementary DNA was generated by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction with MultiScribe Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo‐Scientific; No. 4319983). Reverse transcription was performed at 58 °C for 50 minutes; the enzyme was heat inactivated at 85 °C for 5 minutes, and real‐time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction was performed with the PowerTrack SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo‐Scientific; No. A46109). Sequences of genes are listed in Table S2. Experiments were performed in a CFX Opus 384 Real‐Time PCR System (Bio‐Rad Laboratories). Data were quantified by 2ΔΔ Ct and are presented by fold changes versus the control group. Fold change is indicative of either upregulation or downregulation.

Statistical Analysis

For comparisons of 3 groups, 1‐way ANOVA followed by the Tukey posttest was used. Differences between 2 groups were determined using Student's t test. Concentration–response curves were analyzed using a nonlinear regression, creating a curve that depicts the relationship between the concentrations of agonists and the resulting contraction/relaxation. Maximal response and negative logarithm of EC50 were determined. All analyses were performed using Prism 8.0. (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA). The level of significance for all variables was 5%.

RESULTS

HSD Causes Endothelial Dysfunction After 28 Days of Treatment

Initially, we aimed to identify the most appropriate time point for assessing endothelial dysfunction in mice subjected to a HSD. Therefore, mice were treated with a HSD for either 14 or 28 days. In the 14‐day group, mesenteric arteries showed no dysfunction in response to KCl (Figure 1A), acetylcholine, or SNP (Figure 1B); however, an increased contractile response to phenylephrine was observed (Figure 1B). In contrast, the 28‐day group exhibited endothelial dysfunction, marked by impaired vasodilation to acetylcholine (Figure 1B), while responses to KCl (Figure 1A), phenylephrine, and SNP (Figure 1B) remained unchanged. Finally, neither 14‐day nor 18‐day presented any changes for body weight gain (Table S3).

Interestingly, in aortae, only the HSD 28‐day group demonstrated reduced vascular contractility to phenylephrine, with no alterations in response to KCl, acetylcholine, or SNP at either time point of high‐salt exposure (Figure S1). Thus, we focused only on evaluating the vascular function in mesenteric arteries.

HSD Causes Vascular Senescence

Next, we examined whether endothelial dysfunction is associated with vascular senescence. Analysis of p21 expression in total abdominal aorta showed a significant increase only after 28 days of an HSD (Figure 1C). To further assess senescence at the cellular level, MECs were isolated as described above, and mRNA expression of p21, p16, interleukin‐6, and interleukin‐1β was quantified by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction. Endothelial cell purity was confirmed by enrichment of the endothelial marker CD31 (Figure 1D). Consistent with the aortic findings, high‐salt feeding for 28 days, but not 14 days, resulted in marked upregulation of p21, p16, IL6, and IL1β in endothelial cells (Figure 1E) as well as in the mesenteric flow‐through fraction (Figure 1F). Based on these observations, indicating that HSD‐induced endothelial dysfunction and senescence emerge only after prolonged exposure, subsequent analyses of senescence were performed in whole‐vessel preparations rather than freshly isolated endothelial cells.

Senolytic Drug Treatment Protects From HSD‐Induced Endothelial Dysfunction

To reverse vascular senescence, the HSD 28‐day group was treated with navitoclax. As an initial step, we administered navitoclax to control mice to verify that this senolytic agent would not alter mesenteric artery function, the vascular senescence profile, or caspase‐3 activity—1 of navitoclax's molecular targets. In control animals, navitoclax did not affect vascular reactivity or caspase‐3 levels. However, although not statistically significant, a mild increase in p21 expression was observed, which likely reflects a transient stress response rather than de novo senescence induction (Figure S2). Consequently, subsequent experiments were performed primarily in 3 groups: control, high salt, and high salt+navitoclax.

Successful clearance of senescent cells was evidenced by a reduction in HSD‐induced p21 expression in vascular tissue following navitoclax treatment (Figure 2A). Functionally, navitoclax did not alter the vascular response to KCl (Figure 2B), but it enhanced contractile responses to phenylephrine and U46619, a thromboxane A2 mimetic (Figure 2C). In parallel, navitoclax preserved endothelial function, as shown by improved acetylcholine‐induced vasodilation and increased sensitivity to SNP (Figure 2D). Moreover, ex vivo incubation with L‐NAME further potentiated the phenylephrine response in high‐salt mice (Figure 2C) and abolished group differences in acetylcholine‐induced relaxation (Figure 2D), suggesting that navitoclax improves vascular function primarily by enhancing NO bioavailability.

Collectively, these findings indicate that an HSD mainly induces endothelial dysfunction, whereas the senolytic treatment improves vascular function through dual mechanisms, involving both endothelial and VSMCs protection.

In addition, high‐salt treatment was associated with increased cleaved caspase‐3 levels in the aorta, which were not significantly altered by navitoclax treatment (Figure S3). While these findings are consistent with the possibility that navitoclax does not broadly suppress apoptotic signaling, definitive conclusions about its mechanism of action cannot be drawn, as we did not assess the cell‐specific distribution of cleaved caspase‐3 in senescent versus nonsenescent populations. Finally, navitoclax treatment did not affect body weight gain (Table S4).

HSD and Senolytic Treatment Do Not Induce With End‐Organ Damage

Endothelial dysfunction can trigger or exacerbate end‐organ damage. Therefore, we evaluated potential organ injury and remodeling in our model. Over the 28‐day HSD period, no significant changes were observed in cardiac mass or histological injury (Figure 3G and 3H), nor in renal mass (Figure 3A), histological injury (Figure 3B), or proteinuria (Figure 3C). Interestingly, high salt intake significantly reduced liver mass, an effect that was partially reversed by senolytic treatment (Figure 3D and 3E). Previous work by McCarthy et al 31 suggested that high salt may impair endothelial function by reducing β‐hydroxybutyrate, a liver‐derived ketone body. However, we did not observe any significant differences in circulating β‐hydroxybutyrate levels across groups (Figure 3F). Finally, no evidence of aortic remodeling was detected in any experimental group (Figure 3I and 3J).

HSD‐Induced Immune Activation and Interleukin‐16 Formation May Participate in Endothelial Senescence and Dysfunction

To investigate whether NaCl directly induces endothelial senescence, we used an in vitro approach by treating MECs with NaCl alone or in combination with bleomycin, a known inducer of senescence. As expected, bleomycin alone elevated p21 expression at 48 and 72 hours (Figure 4A and 4B). In contrast, NaCl treatment, either alone or combined with bleomycin, did not increase p21 levels (Figure 4C and 4D), suggesting that high salt neither initiates nor potentiates endothelial senescence under these conditions.

Given that the immune system contributes to salt‐sensitive blood pressure regulation by promoting inflammation in response to high salt intake, 7 , 8 , 9 we investigated whether 28 days of an HSD activated immune and inflammatory pathways in our model. Notably, peritoneal cavity cells from high‐salt–fed mice exhibited increased expression of several proinflammatory and immune‐related genes, including IL1β, NLRP3, TLR4, CD9, NOX2, Caspase‐1, CCR5, and MyD88 (Figure 5A). Additionally, plasma analysis revealed elevated levels of the cytokines and chemokines tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNFα), C‐C chemokine monocyte chemotactic protein 1 (JE), and tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases 1 (TIMP) (a metallopeptidase inhibitor 1), with a particularly pronounced elevation of IL16 in the high‐salt group (Figure 5B and 5C).

Driven by our transcriptomic findings, we explored the role of IL16 in promoting endothelial dysfunction and senescence. We first assessed the expression of IL16 and its receptors, CD9 and CD4, in the vasculature and confirmed their presence in mesenteric arteries in all groups (Figure 6A). We next incubated isolated mesenteric arteries from C57BL/6J mice with IL16 for 24 hours. This treatment increased p21 expression (Figure 6B) without altering vascular contractility to KCl or phenylephrine (Figure 6C). However, IL16 (50 ng/mL) exposure significantly reduced acetylcholine‐induced vasodilation, indicating impaired endothelial function, whereas the response to SNP was not significantly altered, despite a trend toward reduced sensitivity (P=0.06; Figure 6D). Consistent with the dual effects of navitoclax on endothelial and VSMCs, IL16 appears to induce dysfunction in both vascular cell types. To further confirm the senescence‐inducing effect of IL16 in endothelial cells, SA‐β‐galactosidase activity was assessed after 24 hours of treatment. We first examined the expression of CD9, a tetraspanin that can act as a coreceptor mediating IL16 signaling in cells with low CD4 expression, and IL16 mRNA itself, both of which remained unchanged following IL16 exposure (Figure 6E). However, IL16 treatment markedly increased the number of senescent MECs compared with control conditions (Figure 6F).

DISCUSSION

High salt intake is strongly associated with increased cardiovascular risk. Among the cellular mechanisms implicated, endothelial cells appear to be primary targets of dysfunction under conditions of elevated salt exposure; however, the underlying pathways remain incompletely understood. Emerging evidence suggests that premature vascular aging or senescence may contribute to the development of cardiovascular diseases, although a bidirectional relationship cannot be excluded. In this study, we describe a novel vascular mechanism by which endothelial cells become dysfunctional in response to high salt intake. An HSD accelerates endothelial senescence, which in turn promotes vascular dysfunction. Furthermore, we demonstrate that NaCl does not directly induce senescence in endothelial cells; rather, it acts through an exaggerated immune response, specifically via the production of interleukin‐16, a cytokine not well characterized in the pathogenesis of vascular injury.

Endothelial dysfunction is a hallmark in both patients 32 , 33 and rodents 21 , 34 exposed to high salt intake. Endothelial senescence shares several key features with salt‐induced vascular injury, including reduced NO bioavailability, oxidative stress, and inflammation. 35 , 36 This suggests that senescence may precede or be associated with endothelial dysfunction in a high‐salt environment. In our study, 14 days of high salt exposure did not induce vascular or endothelial senescence; however, after 28 days, both isolated endothelial cells and vascular tissues (aorta and mesenteric arteries) exhibited clear signs of senescence. Given the association between endothelial senescence and vascular dysfunction, our findings suggest that preventing premature endothelial senescence in the setting of an HSD may help preserve vascular function.

The discovery that selective elimination of senescent cells via senotherapy can extend health span and mitigate the adverse effects of aging and cancer therapy has greatly intensified interest in cellular senescence. Several clinical trials are now evaluating senolytic drugs for diverse conditions, including bone metabolism disorders, 37 Alzheimer disease, 38 and diabetic macular edema, 39 while preclinical studies have shown therapeutic benefits in models of atherosclerosis and kidney disease. 40 , 41 In our study, navitoclax effectively cleared vascular senescent cells and improved endothelial function in mice exposed to an HSD. Interestingly, navitoclax also increased vascular contractility and enhanced endothelium‐independent relaxation. Because senescent VSMCs are characterized by reduced contractility and elasticity, 42 these findings suggest that senolytic treatment may rejuvenate VSMCs, restoring a more dynamic and elastic phenotype, at least in mesenteric arteries. Notably, this effect was restricted to disease conditions, as arteries from control mice remained unaltered.

However, vascular senescence may also play a compensatory or protective role. In apolipoprotein E–deficient mice, 23 a model of atherosclerosis, navitoclax reduced plaque stability and increased death, likely by altering both endothelial and VSMCs phenotypes. Thus, while senolytic therapy can enhance vascular function, the accompanying increase in contractility and elasticity could have unintended hemodynamic consequences, such as elevated blood pressure. Moreover, because navitoclax also improved sensitivity to SNP, we cannot exclude that senescent cell clearance contributes to protection through enhanced VSMCs responsiveness.

We next investigated whether endothelial senescence is directly triggered by salt exposure. In vitro experiments have shown that elevated salt concentrations reduce NO bioavailability and cell migration, while promoting oxidative stress and inflammation, 43 suggesting that endothelial cells can sense salt and respond with dysfunction. However, in our experiments, high salt exposure did not induce endothelial senescence directly, nor did it enhance the effects of the senescence‐inducing agent bleomycin A1. As our primary focus was on senescence, we did not explore additional signaling pathways. Nonetheless, our findings raise the possibility that an indirect interaction between salt exposure and endothelial dysfunction may exist.

It is well established that high salt intake induces immune activation and exacerbates inflammatory responses. 8 , 44 To investigate whether systemic immune cells reflect this proinflammatory profile, we analyzed immune cells harvested from the peritoneal cavity of HSD‐treated mice. These cells exhibited elevated expression of several inflammatory markers, indicating an activated immune phenotype. Consistent with this, plasma from high salt–treated mice showed increased levels of various chemokines and cytokines, with a notable upregulation of IL16, also known as lymphocyte chemoattractant factor. IL16 is involved in immune regulation, particularly in the recruitment and activation of T lymphocytes, monocytes, and eosinophils, and is produced by multiple immune cell types including macrophages, T and B cells, mast cells, and eosinophils. 45 The role of IL16 in cardiovascular disease remains poorly characterized. In atherosclerosis, higher IL16 levels have been linked to reduced cardiovascular risk, 46 whereas other studies associate IL16 with adverse outcomes, including cardiac fibrosis, enhanced production of inflammatory cytokines, acute myocardial infarction, and heart failure. 47 , 48 , 49 Our novel findings demonstrate that mesenteric arteries express IL16 receptors and that IL16 can directly induce vascular senescence and dysfunction in ex vivo models. These results position IL16 as a previously unrecognized immune regulator of vascular biology.

Previous studies have shown that 28 days of high‐salt feeding is typically insufficient to produce a sustained hypertensive phenotype, which aligns with our findings showing no structural evidence of renal or cardiac remodeling within this time frame. CONCLUSIONS

In summary, our study demonstrates that a prolonged HSD intake induces vascular senescence and dysfunction, which can be prevented by senolytic treatment with navitoclax. Furthermore, our findings indicate that the immune system, rather than direct endothelial impairment, is a key contributor to HSD‐induced vascular dysfunction. Notably, IL16 appears to play a central role in promoting vascular senescence and may contribute to vascular dysfunction in individuals consuming an HSD. Collectively, these results offer new insights into the mechanisms underlying salt‐induced vascular dysfunction and suggest that targeting senescence or IL16–mediated inflammation may represent promising therapeutic strategies for salt‐sensitive vascular diseases.
