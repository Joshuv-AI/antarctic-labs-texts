Integrated single-cell transcriptome and TCR profiles of hepatocellular carcinoma highlight the convergence on interferon signaling during immunotherapy
Authors: Tianhao Li, Shengnan Guo, Chang Xu, Mingjie Zhang, Cheng Lyu, Huanhuan Xu, Zepeng Hou, Mingshuo Zhang, Xiaobo Li, Jing Ren, Changqing Liu, Dan Kong, Dapeng Hao, Guangyu Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the success of immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-based combination therapies in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), its effectiveness remains confined to a subset of patients. The development of reliable, predictive markers is important for accurate patient stratification and further mechanistic understanding of therapy response.MethodsWe comprehensively analyzed paired single-cell RNA transcriptome and T-cell repertoire profiles from 14 HCC ascites samples, collected from 7 patients before and after treatment with the combination of sintilimab (anti-PD-1) and bevacizumab (anti-VEGF).ResultsWe identify a widespread convergence on interferon (IFN) signaling across various immune cell lineages in treatment-responsive patients with HCC, indicating a common transcriptional state transition in the immune microenvironment linked to immunotherapy response in HCC. Strong IFN signaling marks CD8+ T cells with larger clonal expansion and enhanced cytotoxicity, macrophages toward M1-like polarization and strong T-cell recruitment ability, dendritic cells with increased antigen presentation capacity, as well as highly cytotoxic natural killer cells and activated B cells. By translating our finding to cohorts of patients with HCC, we demonstrate the specificity of IFN-signaling in the prognosis of patients with HCC and its ability to predict immunotherapy response.ConclusionsThis study provides a unique single-cell resource with clonal and longitudinal resolution during ICI therapy and reveals IFN signaling as a biomarker of immunotherapy response in HCC, suggesting a beneficial effect by combining IFN inducers with ICIs for patients with HCC.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality and the sixth most common cancer globally.1 Recently, the success of clinical trials (GO30140 and IMbrave150) using combination therapy with atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1) and bevacizumab (anti-VEGF) marked the beginning of immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) treatment for patients with HCC.2 3 This led to the rapid approval of combination immunotherapy by health authorities in over 70 countries as a new standard of care for patients with HCC. Despite the significantly improved clinical outcomes reported, the majority of patients with advanced HCC show therapeutic resistance to immunotherapy. Stable predictive biomarkers of response or resistance to the combination immunotherapy have not been extensively identified. Current biomarkers including PD-L1 expression and tumor mutational burden have shown unstable associations with therapeutic effects due to the strong heterogeneity in tumor microenvironment (TME).4 5 Therefore, it is increasingly crucial to gain a better understanding of the intricate relationship between TME and therapeutic response, and screen for patients most likely to benefit from the combination immunotherapy.

Increasing evidence reveals that the immunotherapy response is modulated by the functional state of various components within TME, including CD8+ T cells,6 dendritic cells,7 neutrophils,8 B cells,9 and macrophages.10 11 This led us to speculate the existence of a common transcriptional state underpinning multiple TME components in patients with HCC that predict the response to combination immunotherapy. Herein, we pair deep single-cell RNA (scRNA) and T cell receptor (scTCR) sequencing to longitudinally evaluate the dynamic immune cell states in patients with HCC who received combination therapy with sintilimab (anti-PD-1) and bevacizumab. Our findings reveal the convergence on interferon (IFN) signaling by various immune cells in the responders of combination immunotherapy, which demonstrates robust prognostic significance. We further interrogate longitudinal clonal changes associated with this functional state in T cells. This study provides a much-needed and detailed understanding of the prevailing state of immune cells from immunotherapy responders and a predictive signature of prognostic and immunotherapeutic significance for patients with HCC.

Methods

Participation of human subjects and sample acquisition

This study involved seven patients diagnosed with primary HCC and treated at Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital. Each patient underwent clinical diagnosis with at least two imaging examinations (CT, MRI, PET-CT, and ultrasound). On enrollment, all patients presented with first-time ascites and underwent percutaneous abdominal paracentesis to collect 500 mL of ascites for scRNA and scTCR sequencing. Patients were then instructed by the physician to evacuate ascites within a standard maximum drainage limit of 3000 mL/day to alleviate symptoms. On the day of or after drainage, patients received intraperitoneal perfusion of bevacizumab at a dose of 7.5 mg/kg in 250 mL 0.9% saline solution, along with concurrent intravenous administration of 200 mg of the PD-1 inhibitor sintilimab. Following 3–5 days of drug absorption, an additional 500 mL of ascites was collected for scRNA and scTCR sequencing. Samples were classified into responders (PT3, PT7) and non-responders (PT1, PT2, PT4, PT5, PT6) according to the mRECIST1.1 criteria (detailed information about the participants is available in the Supplemental materials and methods and online supplemental table S1).

Library preparation and sequencing

Comprehensive details on sample preparation, library construction, and single-cell sequencing are available in the Supplemental materials and methods.

scRNA transcriptomics data processing

Raw scRNA-seq data were preprocessed using the Cell Ranger pipeline (V.7.0.1, 10X Genomics), followed by rigorous quality control, unsupervised clustering, and cell annotation. Briefly, CellBender12 (V.0.2.0) was used to eliminate systematic background noise. Cells expressing fewer than 200 genes, more than 6000 genes, with counts exceeding 40 000, or exhibiting over 10% of transcripts from the mitochondrial genome were excluded. Additionally, potential multicellular droplets were carefully removed. The Seurat R package13 (V.4.3.0.1) was used for data normalization, unsupervised cell clustering, and identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) among cell clusters for cell type annotation, with batch effects corrected by Harmony.14 Further details of data processing are provided in the Supplemental materials and methods.

Assembly of scTCR repertoire data, clonotype identification, and analysis

We performed single-cell 5′ V(D)J sequence assembly using the Cell Ranger vdj pipeline with the GRCh38 genome as the reference. Only high-confidence and productive annotations of T cell receptor α and β chains were used for subsequent analysis. Cells sharing the same CDR3αβ nucleotide sequences were defined as belonging to the same T cell receptor (TCR) clone. The analysis of TCR clonal abundance and diversity was conducted using the alakazam R package15 (V.1.2.1). To infer a comprehensive clonal abundance distribution, the estimateAbundance function was employed, with confidence intervals obtained through bootstrapping. Clonal diversity was assessed using the alphaDiversity function, which calculates the inverse of the Simpson index. The clonal diversity index (D) for each group was determined as the mean value of resampling realizations, with confidence intervals derived from the SD of the resampling results. Integration of the paired scTCR-seq data with scRNA-seq data was performed based on their matched unique cell barcodes.

Cell differentiation trajectory reconstruction

Pseudotime analysis of CD8+ CTL cell development was inferred using the monocle R package16 (V.2.26.0). The reconstruction of the differentiation trajectory employed the top 500 highly variable genes in CD8+ CTL that were expressed in more than 5 cells. The remaining parameters were set to their default configurations. The direction of cell evolution was evaluated using CytoTRACE17 (V.0.3.3), a computational method capable of inferring differentiation direction without prior knowledge. CytoTRACE scores range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating an earlier stage of differentiation.

Public cohorts’ acquisition and evaluation of immune components

Multiple public cohorts including The Cancer Genome Atlas Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma (TCGA LIHC), International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC) LIRI-JP,18 GO30140, and IMbrave1505 were acquired for subsequent analysis. These datasets underwent deconvolution processing, with CIBERSORTx19 used for estimating the proportions of distinct immune cell subpopulations, and the BayesPrism algorithm (V.2.0)20 employed for deconvoluting transcriptomic data of immune components from these bulk RNA-seq profiles. Detailed explanations of the data acquisition, processing, and deconvolution procedures are provided in the Supplementary materials and methods.

Additional statistical analysis

In addition to the bioinformatics and statistical methods mentioned, all additional statistical analyses and data visualizations were performed using R programming language (V.4.2.2). Overexpression representation analysis based on biological process (BP) terms was conducted using the clusterProfiler R package21 (V.4.9.0), where the BP gene sets were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database.22 Specific functional activity at the single-cell level was assessed using AUCell, a ranking-based scoring method. The gene sets used to represent interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) score, neoantigen-reactivity,23 cytotoxicity,2426 as well as M1-like and M2-like phenotypes27 are shown in online supplemental table S4). M1 likeness was assessed using the t-statistic from a Student’s t-test comparing the relative gene expression levels of M1 versus M2 canonical markers. Cell–cell communication inference and visualization from single-cell transcriptomics were conducted using CellChat (V.2).28 For acquired public bulk RNA sequencing profiles, ISG score, Responder-Likeness Score (RLS), functional activities of cytotoxicity, B cell activation, and antigen presentation were computed using the single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) algorithm29 via the GSVA R package30 (V.1.46.0). Smoothed Cox proportional hazard analysis was performed using the phenoTest R package (V.1.46.0), and Kaplan-Meier curves were plotted with the survminer R package (V.0.4.9). Patients were stratified into ISGhigh and ISGlow groups based on the optimal cut-off determined by the surv_cutpoint function.

Data availability

The raw sequence data reported in this paper have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive31 in the National Genomics Data Center32 via accession number HRA007658. Processed data are available in the Mendeley Data Repository via https://data.mendeley.com/drafts/wyvkw6f95t. Codes for this study are accessible at https://github.com/LTH-HMU/HCC_ascites. Please direct any inquiries or requests for additional information to the corresponding authors.

Results

Transcriptional states of various immune cell types are associated with clinical outcomes of patients with HCC

To characterize cell states associated with immunotherapy, we performed paired scRNA-seq and scTCR-seq on 14 ascites samples from seven HCC subjects before and soon after they received the combination therapy of anti-PD-1 and anti-VEGF (figure 1A and online supplemental file 1). Samples were divided into responders (PT3, 7) and non-responders (PT1, 2, 4, 5, 6) according to the mRECIST1.1 criteria. After rigorous quality control, a total of 104 993 single cells were retained for subsequent analyses. Clustering analysis and canonical marker gene expression revealed five major cell types, including T cells (CD3D, CD8A, CD4), natural killer (NK) cells (KIR and KLR genes), B-lineage cells (CD79A, CD19), monocytes/macrophages (CD68, CD14, FCN1), neutrophils (CSF3R), dendritic cells (DCs) (CLEC9A, CD1C, IL3RA), and epithelial cells (keratin genes) (figure 1B and C, online supplemental figure S1A, and online supplemental table S2). Further subclustering analysis identified a total of 33 cell subpopulations (figure 1C, figures25, and online supplemental figure S2–S5). Matched scTCR profiling was highly consistent with the cell identifications (online supplemental figure S1B).

To better understand the immune landscape related to treatment response, we first examined the cellular abundances and compositions of cell subpopulations (figure 1D,E, online supplemental figure S1C,D). We observed an overall increased proportion of lymphocytes and a decreased proportion of myeloid cells in samples from responders, including T, NK, and B cells, which aligns with the previous findings about the relationship between immune cell composition and immunotherapy response.5 33 Compared with the changes in the relative proportion of cell subpopulations, more striking differences were found for the transcriptional state measured by the large number of significantly DEGs within each cell between responders and non-responders (figure 1F), especially in T cells and macrophages. The majority of DEGs were upregulated in the responders in each cell type, with many common DEGs involved in inflammatory signaling, such as AP1 genes, IFNG, IRF7, and STAT1 (figure 1G and online supplemental table S3). To further confirm whether these differential transcriptional states within each immune cell type link to the tumor control in HCC, we constructed an RLS using the top 50 DEGs found for each immune cell type (see details in Methods section). The RLSs, constructed from different immune cell types, were all significantly associated with improved outcomes in the TCGA LIHC cohort, whether as a continuous variable (figure 1H) or as a category variable (figure 1I). Thus, these data suggested the presence of an enhanced antitumor immune response profile that was shared by various immune subsets and contributed to the tumor control and presumably response to immunotherapy.

Strong IFN signaling marks cytotoxic, less-differentiated, and clonally expanded CD8+ T cells from responders

Among all the immune cell subtypes, the CD8A+ T cells were the most significantly enriched in the responders, whereas no significant difference was observed for CD4+ T cells between responders and non-responders (figure 2A). Unsupervised clustering analyses of T cells identified 14 T cell subtypes including six CD4+ T cell subtypes, five CD8+ T cell subtypes, a proliferating T cell subtype, and two unconventional T cell subtypes, γδ T cells and mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells (figure 2B, (online supplemental figure S2A–C). In line with the enrichment of CD8+ T cells, we observed a remarkably larger number of DEGs between responders and non-responders in CD8+ T cells than in CD4+ T cells, especially in the cytotoxic and clonally expanded CD8+ T cells, including CTLs and NKTs (figure 2C and figure 2D), suggesting a strong link between the transcriptional state of CD8+ T cells and immunotherapy response.

By comparing the transcriptome of CD8+ T cells between responders and non-responders, we found that CD8+ T cells from responders highly expressed genes involved in multiple pathways of IFN signaling, as well as Nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling and cytokine production (figure 2E). In agreement with this, we observed significantly higher expression of ISGs in CD8+ T cells from responders than those from non-responders (online supplemental figure S2D). For instance, IFIT3, a type I IFN-induced gene known to enhance immune response,34 was found expressed in 33.5% of CD8+ T cells from responders, compared with merely 5.4% from non-responders (figure 2F). Furthermore, T cells within large clones were more likely to show IFIT3 expression than monoclonal cells (figure 2G and online supplemental figure S2E). These observations align well with the recent data that immunotherapy response is determined by successful recruitment, activation, and expansion of CD8+ T cells.35 36

Among the CD8+ T cell subtypes, CD8+ CTL was the one most significantly enriched in responders and most clonally expanded (figure 2D and online supplemental figure S2F). To better understand the dynamic transcriptional changes of CD8+ CTLs, we applied Monocle to infer the differentiation trajectory (figure 2H). This analysis showed the trajectory that started from a branch mostly consistent of ISGhigh cells from responders, culminating in the end predominantly occupied by ISGlow cells from non-responders. The differentiation state inferred by CytoTRACE and the number of expressed genes were highly consistent with trajectory inference (online supplemental figure S2G). We further examined the expression dynamics using a signature score for ISGs and the published signatures of cytotoxicity2426 and neoantigen-reactivity23 of CD8+ T cells (online supplemental table S4). Along with the pseudotime of trajectory, we observed consistent declines in the signatures of ISG, cytotoxicity, and neoantigen-reactivity, as well as the expression of related genes (figure 2H and I). These results suggested that the ISGlow CTLs enriched in non-responders represented a subset of highly differentiated T cells, with declined cytotoxicity and neoantigen reactivity. This is consistent with the finding that the loss of less differentiated T cell subset resulted in impaired antitumor immunity37 and suggests that disruption of this differentiation process has the potential to enhance the response to immunotherapy.

Clonal T cell dynamics is associated with immunotherapy response

Paired scRNA/TCR-seq has allowed for enhanced resolution of clonal T cell dynamics in the response to immunotherapy. Our paired scTCR-seq data detected in total of 34 565 T cells with productive TCR profiles from all 14 samples, allowing us to link T cell clones to their cellular phenotypes. As expected, there were significant intrapatient TCR overlaps and minimal interpatient TCR overlaps (online supplemental figure S3A). By linking TCR clonality with phenotypes, we observed that responders exhibited substantially greater T cell clonal expansion than non-responders (figure 3A), and consequently a lower level of clonal diversity (figure 3B). Overall, CD8+ T cell clusters contained substantially larger clones relative to CD4+ T cell clusters, with the CD8+ CTLs showing the highest clonal expansion (figure 3C). Consistent with transcriptomic result (figure 2D), CD8+ T cells in responders also displayed elevated ISG expression at a clonal level relative to CD8+ T cell clones from non-responders (figure 3D).

To examine the dynamics of CD8+ T cell clones in response to treatment, we conducted a clone-match analysis on CD8+ clones present in both pre-treatment and post-treatment. In non-responders, pro-inflammatory related genes, including NKG7,38 IL16,39 and IL2RB,40 were downregulated post-treatment, whereas these genes remained stable in responders (figure 3E). Correspondingly, CD8+ clones in responders showed comparable levels of neoantigen-reactivity pre-treatment and post-treatment, while non-responders exhibited a significant reduction in neoantigen-reactivity post-treatment (figure 3F). Furthermore, we observed a higher proportion of polyclonal CD8+ CTLs post-treatment in responders, in contrast to non-responders (online supplemental figure S3B), suggesting the clonal expansion of CD8+ CTLs as a response to immunotherapy.

We next divided CD8+ T cell clones into ISGhigh clones (larger than the average ISG score of T cell clones, n=903) and ISGlow (lower than the average ISG score of T cell clones, n=1327) according to the average ISG score of cells within each clone. We observed that CD8+ ISGhigh clones demonstrated stronger neoantigen-reactivity compared with ISGlow clones (figure 3G). This was consistent with the increased fraction of CD8+ CTLs after treatment in ISGhigh T cell clones but not in ISGlow clones (figure 3H). Notably, in addition to CD8+ T cells, the ISG signature was found stronger among all the T cells including CD4+ T cells and MAIT cells from responders of immunotherapy (online supplemental figure S3C and D). To compare the plasticity of cell state transition that may related to ISG signature, we also divided T cells into ISGhigh and ISGlow groups and examined the fraction of clonal overlap between different T cell subpopulations pre-treatment and post- treatment (figure 3I). ISGhigh T cells demonstrated increased T cell clonal plasticity between pre-treatment and post-treatment stages, particularly evident for CD8+ CTLs and CD8+ GZMKs, possibly due to their less-differentiation states. Collectively, these findings suggested that the response to immunotherapy in patients with HCC was associated with the baseline IFN-responsive signature in T cells with high neoantigen-reactivity and cell plasticity.

IFN signaling links the phenotype of macrophages to the immunotherapy response

Myeloid cells represented the second largest immune cell type related to clinical response in our dataset (figure 1F). Unsupervised clustering identified 12 myeloid subtypes, including two subtypes of monocytes, five subtypes of macrophages, and four subtypes of dendritic cells (DCs) and neutrophils (figure 4A and online supplemental figure S4A). Further investigation revealed that macrophages exhibited the strongest transcriptional changes associated with clinical response among myeloid cells (figure 4B). Consistently, macrophages from responders show a large number of highly expressed genes involved in pathways of cytokine-mediated signaling, pattern recognition, and response to IFN (figure 4C), suggesting pro-inflammatory cellular phenotypes and probably strong T cell recruitment capability.

Recent single-cell studies have demonstrated that the pro-inflammatory (M1) and anti-inflammatory (M2) polarization of macrophages are continuous cell phenotype transitions rather than specific cell subpopulations.27 Consistent with recent findings in breast and lung cancer,41 42 macrophage subtypes in HCC coexpressed canonical M1 and M2 signatures (online supplemental figure S4B). We thus quantized the macrophage polarization by comparing the expression of M1 relative to M2 canonical markers (see Methods section) and observed highly correlated transcriptional changes among macrophage polarization, clinical response, and ISG signatures (figure 4D), suggesting coordinated cellular dynamics in macrophages. The distribution of signatures at the single-cell level further demonstrated coordinated variation among myeloid cells, with the Mφ_CXCL10 subtype showing the highest M1 polarization and ISG signature, and the strongest difference between responders and non-responders (figure 4E and online supplemental figure S4C). Notably, consistent with the observations in T cells, all the myeloid subtypes show differential M1/M2 polarization and ISG signature between responders and non-responders, further suggesting that IFN signaling marked a widespread transcriptional state transition within immune cells of patients with HCC.

The Mφ_CXCL10 subtype that was characterized by the high expression of IFN-induced chemokines including CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL1143 was significantly enriched in HCC samples of responders (online supplemental figure S4D and E). Cellular communication analysis revealed the strongest connection between Mφ_CXCL10 and activated T cells (figure 4F and G), suggesting its potential for T cell recruitment and consistent with the enrichment of T cells in responders (online supplemental figure S1D). To validate the association with clinical response independently, we measured the average expression of the ligand–receptor gene pairs between Mφ_CXCL10 and T cells using the clinical trials of GO30140 and IMbrave150, which contain in total 300 patients with HCC treated by atezolizumab or the combination of atezolizumab and bevacizumab (figure 4H). Overall, the ligand–receptor genes showed significantly higher expression in patients with HCC benefiting from the immunotherapy.

Convergence on IFN signaling in the immune atlas of patients with HCC responsive to immunotherapy

We next investigated whether the ISG signature, shown in CD8+ T cells and macrophages as well as in previously reported activation states of distinct immune subtypes,7 8 10 11 represented a widespread transcriptional state transition characterizing the overall immune ecotype of patients with HCC responsive to immunotherapy. We averaged the expression of ISGs for each cell subtype in responders and non-responders, respectively, and observed that the expression of ISGs successfully classified cell subtypes of responders from those of non-responders (figure 5A). We further investigated the top 50 most upregulated genes in responders versus non-responders for each cell type (online supplemental table S3) and found that the overlapped genes by different immune cell subtypes were 3 ISGs (figure 5B).

We then applied the ISG signature on the identified subtypes of B cells, NK cells, and DCs (figure 5C–E and online supplemental figure S5A). Consistent with T cells and myeloid cells, all the subtypes of B cells, NK cells, and DCs displayed significantly higher levels of ISG signatures in responders. Compared with cells with a low ISG signature, cells with a high ISG signature showed pathway enrichment in B cell activation, cytotoxicity, or antigen presentation for B cells, NK cells, or DCs, respectively. Cellular communication analysis revealed stronger interactions between immune cells with a high ISG signature than cells with a low ISG signature due to the strong chemokine signaling (online supplemental figure S5B). These results suggested that the expression of ISG characterized a bona fide common activation state shared by various immune components in HCC. Considering that the ISG signature can be induced by exogenous IFNs to drive antitumor immunity,7 our results directed a potential strategy to restore the immune response for patients with HCC that are resistant to immunotherapy.

Independent validations confirm the convergence on IFN signaling and its prognostic value in HCC

To validate our findings independently, we first divided TCGA and IGCG HCC samples into ISGhigh and ISGlow by the enrichment of highly expressed ISGs using ssGSEA and deconvoluted their immune components using CiberSortX.19 Consistent with our results, we observed a significantly higher fraction of M1 versus M2 macrophages and an elevated fraction of CD8+ T cells in ISGhigh samples (figure 6A and online supplemental figure S6A). We further obtained the deconvoluted transcriptomic profiles of immune components from the two cohorts using the BayesPrism algorithm.20 We observed a markedly higher ISG signatures across all immune subtypes in ISGhigh samples compared with ISGlow samples (figure 6B and online supplemental figure S6B), which reinforced our finding about the convergence on IFN signaling across various immune cell types. Consistent with our results, the deconvoluted transcriptomes of CD8+ T cells and NK cells exhibited increased cytotoxicity, B cells displayed a higher level of B cell activation, and DCs demonstrated enhanced antigen presentation in ISGhigh compared with ISGlow samples (figure 6C and online supplemental figure S6C). To test whether the association between IFN signaling and tumor control was specific to HCC, we performed univariate CoxPH analysis for ISG signature on the major cancer types of TCGA and ICGC-LIRI-JP cohort (figure 6D). In addition to melanoma, where the therapeutic efficacy of immunotherapy has been found involving convergence on IFN signaling,44 ISG signature was only predictive of improved survival in HCC (online supplemental figure S6D and E).

To validate the association between ISG signature and the therapeutic efficacy of immunotherapy, we conducted similar analyses on the clinical trials of GO301403 and IMbrave1502. Responders had a higher fraction of CD8+ T cells and a higher ratio of M1 to M2 macrophages (online supplemental figure S6F). Patients with HCC benefiting from ICI therapy exhibited significantly elevated ISG signature across various immune components compared with those who were resistant to the therapy (figure 6E). Moreover, the ISG signature was significantly correlated with enhanced prognostic outcomes in patients with HCC received ICI or the combination of ICI and anti-VEGF (figure 6F and online supplemental figure S6G). Additionally, ISG signature exhibited superior efficacy in distinguishing between responders and non-responders compared with conventional biomarkers, such as PD-1 and PD-L1 expression and the infiltration of CD8+ T cells (online supplemental figure S6H). Similar observations were also found in the Gide et al dataset,45 involving melanoma patients treated with anti-PD-1 alone or in combination with anti-CTLA-4 immunotherapy (online supplemental figure S6I). Collectively, these results confirmed the convergence on IFN signaling by the immune cells of patients with HCCresponsive to ICI therapy and highlighted the potential of combinational treatment between type-I IFN inducers and ICI in HCC.

Discussion

Here, we provide evidence for a common transcriptional state characterized by strong ISG expression across various immune cell types in patients with HCC, indicating the convergence on IFN signaling in the response to immunotherapy. This study yields several insights at the single-cell level into the immune cell states associated with IFN signaling in patients receiving ICI therapy. We first demonstrate that IFN signaling is associated with activation states in adaptive immune cells including T cells and B cells. IFN signaling marks clonally expanded and cytotoxic CD8+ T cells. These confirm prior reports that type I IFNs act directly on the clonal expansion as well as cytolytic activity of CD8+ T cells46 47 and further strengthen the finding by paired scRNA/TCR-seq data and clone-matched analysis. In addition, ISGhigh macrophages and DCs show enhanced antigen presentation and stronger CD8+ T cell recruitment capability, which are consistent with the effect of IFN signaling in priming the antigen-resenting cells and sensitizing “cold” tumors to ICI in preclinical cancer models.48 49 Notably, IFN signaling portrays not only specific immune subpopulations but an overall immune ecotype for a subset of patients with HCC responsive to immunotherapy, a discovery that has been corroborated independently by multiple bulk RNA-seq datasets.

Interestingly, many recent efforts to boost antitumor immunity that explore diverse immune cell types engage immune signaling pathways that converge onto IFN signaling. For instance, an IFN-stimulated neutrophil response is reported linked to tumor control8 and predicts immunotherapy response.50 Blocking TREM2 induces the upregulation of ISGs in monocytes and redirects the differentiation into pro-inflammatory macrophages.10 It has been reported recently that type I IFN activates CD11b+ conventional DC cells to promote antitumor CD8+ T cell immunity.7 In addition, IFN-stimulated DCs significantly enhance the cytotoxicity of NK cells.51 IFN signaling also promotes the antitumor effect through adaptive immunity by enhancing B cell responses52 and T cell effector function53 54 and repressing the expansion of T regulatory cells.55 These studies are in line with our observations, except that we found a widespread IFN signaling across multiple immune cell types, highlighting its particular interest in prognostication and predicting the response to immunotherapy in patients HCC.

IFN signaling has both immune-stimulatory and suppressive effects. Studies from patients and mice indicate that persistent IFN production leads to “adaptive resistance” by inducing PD-L1/PD-1 expression within TME, in both cancer cells and immune cells.55 56 This would make PD-L1/PD-1 blockade a necessary component of an effective combination for patients with cancer to neutralize the immunosuppressive effects of IFN inducers. This has been proven beneficial for patients with melanoma.57 Concordantly, in mouse models of HCC, the combined use of IFNα has been found to significantly amplify the therapeutic effect of PD-1 blockade compared with anti-PD-1 monotherapy.58

Mechanisms of response or resistance to ICI therapy in HCC have not been extensively identified. Compared with previous studies, our research offers a fresh perspective at the single-cell level, revealing that coordinated IFN signaling elevation in the immune landscape of patients responsive to immunotherapy. Our study embarks on the first longitudinal study of single-cell analysis of patients with HCC undergoing immunotherapy, enabling effective tracking of clonal T cell lineages precombination and postcombination therapy with sintilimab and bevacizumab. The therapeutic effect links to the IFN-induced signature in T cells, characterized by high neoantigen reactivity and transitional plasticity among T cell subtype clones. Although limitations of this study include the inability to simultaneously profile intratumor immune cells from these patients and the relatively small sample size, these concerns were largely alleviated by the validations with bulk RNA-seq data. The effect of IFN inducers and ICI combination has been evaluated by many clinical trials and demonstrates promising antitumor activity in specific tumor scenarios.59 While the beneficial effect has been reported for melanoma,57 the effect on HCC has yet to be determined (NCT03732547). Meanwhile, this work suggests that IFN inducers and ICI combination hold a promising prospect for a subset of patients with HCC and is worthy of further investigation. Finally, this study serves as a unique single-cell resource with clonal and longitudinal resolution and provides insights into IFN signaling during ICI therapy.
