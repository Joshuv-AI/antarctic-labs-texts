New Cuban family code: another step forward improving child health and their families
Authors: Angel A Escobedo, Raisa Rumbaut, Marcel Sosa-Carabeo, Tomás Pérez-García
Published: 2026

In 2021, following nationwide consultation, a new family code was initiated in Cuba. It was approved, by referendum on 25 September 2022, with 66.87% of valid votes cast in favour.1 The new code contains 474 articles, is over 100 pages long and is a fully-binding law under the Cuban constitution.2 The former family code—approved in 1975—had remained the foundational law for almost 50 years. Fortunately, Cuban society has become more inclusive and this new code aims to more fully represent the Cuban’s society and its diverse communities, defending the values of equality and non-discrimination.

Since the beginning of the Cuban revolution (1959) the country has achieved impressive advances in the health of its population. These advances have equalled or surpassed that of many industrialised countries and have, furthermore, been achieved in the context of a struggling national economy alongside obvious challenges faced by the US embargo. Two clear examples are in the areas of infant mortality (of 7.1 per 1000 live births, 2024) and life expectancy (approximately at birth in about 77.70 years, 2024).3 This overall progress has been—and continues to be—supported by a particularly accessible model of universal healthcare delivery network alongside the high literacy-educational levels of the Cuban population.

Despite the aforementioned achievements, it is important to recognise that there is still much to be done in order to tackle social inequalities that still exist and persist. Data from various studies and reports provide some insight into these ongoing inequalities.

Cuba, for instance, is presently witnessing a dramatic increase in its ageing population and evidence of low birth rates.4 There are recognised disparities in marginalised and rural communities where children may face various and particular challenges that could lead to long-term detrimental effects on both their physical and cognitive development. There is a high percentage of both grandparents and single parents who are living alone. Both groups face economic hardships and issues of isolation that impact family well-being. Additionally, the ongoing migration crisis has led to many families experiencing separation, which can have detrimental effects on child well-being. Children left behind or those who migrate face challenges such as emotional distress, instability, and changes in their social environment, which can affect their mental health and social development.

The view of the family unit has also—so far—been marked by limited notions of heterosexual, nuclear family models. Furthermore, there have been concerns regarding gender-based violence, and issues in the LGBTQ+ community from youth who often encounter peer bullying from peers and/or discrimination that impacts their mental health and social inclusion, till marriages.4 5

Addressing these aforesaid inequities, and developing community and family collective action and strategies is crucial for safeguarding the health and well-being of children and of the whole population. The new family code exists to address these issues.

It is interesting to consider two of these areas of concern in more detail—including the potential challenges they present—and to reflect on how the new family code proposes to address these concerns.

New family models and children’s rights

The former family code of 1975 was primarily focused on the nuclear family. It often overlooked the rights and roles of extended family members (such as grandparents), and did not address diverse family structures—including single-parent and same-sex couples. The new family code seeks to address these issues. It introduces provisions that recognise the importance of diverse family models, promotes the rights of grandparents in child-rearing, acknowledges the need for further acceptance and of health equality of the LGBTQ+ community alongside the right of same-sex couples to marry and to adopt. Additionally, there are new general conditions for surrogacy and adoption.

The new family code incorporates the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child which clearly outlines and ratifies the role of the family and children’s rights. It advocates the need to prioritise children’s needs in family decision-making and encourages families to view the physical and mental health of their children as paramount. The transmission of positive behaviours and values—from one generation to another—is also highlighted.

By creating a setting where children’s opinions are heard and respected, the family code aims to reduce opportunities for neglect and abuse—obvious risk factors for poor health outcomes in children. We may not forget that some forms of violence considered as a type of ‘disciplinary practices’ for children that include corporal punishment still persist.6 Moreover, there still exists the belief in some families that violence is a legitimate way to discipline a child and changing those beliefs will rest largely on education, cultural shifts and the establishment of vigorous support systems.

The message here is clear. Families are the mainstay of society and their bonds are built on love, responsibility, mutual respect, consideration, affection and support. They are diverse and include heterosexual, same-sex and single-family models. They further ratify full equality between women and men and of the equitable distribution of domestic and childcare tasks among all family members. It is hoped that this kind of family participation will lead to improved health for children, guaranteeing they receive satisfactory nutritional education, healthcare and emotional support.

The role of older people and grandparents

The new family code also encourages the involvement of older people and grandparents in child-rearing, when their involvement is healthy, loving and supportive in nature, the benefits are obvious. It should be said, however, that certain aspects of their involvement may be less straightforward to legally enforce. Various studies carried of older adults have shown particular vulnerabilities among this age group. A small study carried out in 2015, in Havana, the capital of Cuba, showed that of the 197 older adults interviewed, 88 (44.7%) reported that they were victims of domestic abuse. Fifty of these were women. The most common types of abuse were psychological and disrespect for personal space.7 Additionally, an observational study carried out in the elderly population (also in Havana) showed a higher propensity towards vulnerable nutritional status: propensity for underweight was higher in older age groups (OR=1.63, 70–79 years; OR=2.05, ≥80 years).8 Effective enforcement will therefore rely more on education and awareness campaigns in order to inform families about the importance of grandparental respect and their important role in the emotional well-being of children.

Conclusions and future directions

The former family code of 1975—in place for over 50 years—omitted to address many important features of Cuban society. This resulted in a lack of legal protection for many of its population. The creation of the new family code is thus designed to right these omissions—to ensure that each member of Cuban society is acknowledged, represented, and protected.

It should be understood, however, that the success of this new family code will not solely depend on its legal implementation. It will equally depend on some shifts in both individual, family and cultural beliefs and on the effectiveness and accessibility of community/state education and support, monitoring and evaluation. It will also depend on the collective efforts of families, communities and government entities to work together. Cuban society will need to be supported and educated regarding their rights in order to fully embrace and benefit from the new law. It may take time for some communities to assimilate, understand and accept its value. Time will tell. If it is successful, it will serve as a model of excellence, helping to create a more progressive, representative and flourishing environment for both children and adults alike.
