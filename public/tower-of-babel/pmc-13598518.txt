Squamous cell carcinoma in a duplex ureter: a case report
Authors: Ethan Prabaharan, Matthew Alberto, Justin du Plessis, Damien Bolton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report a case of ureteric squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) arising in a partial duplex left collecting system. An 88-year-old male presented with a flank pain and intermittent haematuria to a metropolitan emergency department. CT showed findings suspicious for obstructing malignancy, which was confirmed on biopsy to be progressive well-differentiated ureteral SCC. Widespread metastases to the cervical, mediastinal and para-aortic lymph nodes, lungs, and the L5 vertebral body were detected on follow-up imaging. The patient passed within the following two months. A high degree of caution should be exercised in patients who have anatomical variants or chronic underlying risk factors.

Introduction

Ureteral SCC is a rare entity that accounts for <1% of upper tract urothelial malignancies.1,2 Known risk factors include chronic stimulation, infection, or inflammation,1 which are increased in occurrence in patients with anatomical variants. We report a case of ureteral SCC arising in a duplex ureter.

Case presentation

An 88-year-old male with a known history of chronic kidney disease and recurrent UTIs presented with six weeks of left flank pain and intermittent macroscopic haematuria. There was no mass, fevers, or loss of weight. The patient denied previous kidney issues, but did report that his father had a duplex kidney. The patient was afebrile and haemodynamically stable, with a white cell count of 13.9 x 109/L and eGFR at the patient's baseline of 26 mL/min/1.73m2. Urine microscopy, culture, and sensitivity (MCS) demonstrated leukocytes growing mixed flora. CT demonstrated marked hydronephrosis and hydroureter of the inferior moiety of a duplex left kidney (Fig. 1), with an underlying renal mass and extensive perinephric soft tissue nodularity suspicious for locally advanced malignancy. Ultrasound demonstrated a heterogeneous left kidney with two discrete solid masses, and a duplex left kidney with dilatation of both the upper and lower moieties. A rigid cystoscopy with ureteroscopy and retrograde pyelogram (RGP) was performed, with JJ stent insertion to the upper moiety. An MDT meeting concluded palliative nephrectomy would achieve the best outcomes for the patient, albeit likely to render them dialysis-dependent given his chronic kidney disease. The alternative option of biopsy with stereotactic ablative radiotherapy (SABR) was deemed unlikely to achieve satisfactory control of the patient's flank pain, which was significantly impacting his quality of life. The patient requested time to make a decision and agreed to outpatient follow-up.

The patient represented two weeks later with fever, constipation and dysuria, which was treated as urosepsis. A repeat CT showed a progressive infiltrative soft tissue mass with several new and enlarged lymph nodes and multiple enlarging pulmonary nodules suspicious for metastases. Notably, both the pulmonary lesions and lymph nodes were reported to have increased in size and number since the initial scan two weeks prior.

The patient received intravenous antibiotics and underwent left ureteroscopy with laser and biopsy, RGP, and stent exchange. A soft non-calcified mass in the lower ureter was unable to be passed physically or with contrast, with nil improvement to view with laser (Fig. 2). Cytopathology was again bland, but histopathology diagnosed the left ureteric lesion as well differentiated SCC with no features of urothelial carcinoma. Microscopically, fragments of atypical epithelium with squamoid morphology were visualised. An FDG-PET scan showed findings indicative of locally advanced left sided ureteral SCC with multifocal left perinephric and ureteric involvement as well as widespread metastatic disease, involving the left neck, lungs, nodal stations above and below the diaphragm as well as L5 vertebral body.

The patient received consults from the haematology/oncology and palliative care teams. Given the size of the tumour, significant pain and functional impairment, and rapidly progressive disease, a supportive care approach was agreed on. However, while the initial prognosis from the palliative care team was several months, the patient experienced worsening hypoxia over the next week. A VQ scan was negative for pulmonary embolism but demonstrated a small left pleural effusion in the context of suspected pulmonary metastases. The patient was admitted to the palliative care unit for symptom management of significant pain and delirium and passed away subsequently. The cause of death was deemed to be urosepsis on a background of metastatic ureteral SCC and the case was reported to the coroner's given the recent procedure.

Discussion

Ureteral SCC accounts for less than 1% of renal tract malignancies.1 However, several cases have been reported in the literature of ureteral SCCs in congenitally abnormal renal tracts,3,4 which predispose to squamous metaplasia and malignancy through chronic stones, irritation or infection.5, 6, 7 We report a patient with ureteral SCC in a partial duplex left ureter on a background of recurrent UTIs and chronic kidney disease of unknown cause with no known history of stone disease.

The mechanism of carcinogenesis is thought to involve initial squamous metaplasia from a chronic trigger such as urolithiasis, infection or hydronephrosis.3,4 Progression of disease is thought to result in dysplasia and eventually neoplasia manifesting as ureteral SCC. Additionally, the presence of duplex ureters also increases risk of infection through improper drainage, vesicoureteral reflux or ectopic insertion.1 Notably, in this case the duplex ureters combined into a single entity that inserted in the bladder. Despite this, the patient reported a history of recurrent urinary tract infections, which may represent a trigger for the metaplasia-neoplasia cascade.

Systematic reviews of patients with ureteral SCC demonstrate a lower incidence of detection at earlier stage and subsequent poor overall survival.8 96% of patients presented with pT3+ tumours, with a median survival of 7-11 months for SCC.8,9 However, notably when stratified by disease stage, median survival was similar in ureteral SCC and urothelial carcinoma.8 Primary management is surgical, given minimal significant benefit shown for chemotherapy and radiotherapy in patients with ureteral SCC.8 However, given the small case size, further research is required to establish evidence-based protocols for management of ureteral SCC. Our case is unusual by the fact that the lesion was well-differentiated but presented with numerous metastatic and a rapidly fatal clinical course. This differs to a similar case in the literature of SCC in a duplex ureter which was successfully managed operatively without recurrence at six-month follow-up.10 Notably, the initial ultrasound reported two discrete lesions. However, when further characterised with CT and PET scans, these both appear to be more solid components of a large, heterogenous primary lesion. The PET showed several perinephric and ureteral soft tissue nodules with increased metabolic activity, consistent with extensive logoregional metastatic disease and potential drop metastases in the ureter. In this case, both the rapidly progressive nature of the patient's disease and the increased morbidity and mortality associated with hospital admission contributed to the acute deterioration in the patient's prognosis.

Ureteric SCC is often diagnosed late because of the nature of symptoms, with macroscopic haematuria and loin pain often only presenting at significant tumour size.11 Initial radiological and pathological focus were on ruling out the presence of an underlying urothelial carcinoma, which cytology was consistently negative for. However, in the presence of a congenitally abnormal renal tract, it is possible that otherwise unremarkable findings of squamous cells and keratin debris may have greater significance in predicting SCC.

One key limitation in this case is that pure SCC and UCC with squamous differentiation cannot be distinguished on a small biopsy alone. Given the possibility of urothelial differentiation in an unsampled part of a tumour, definitive diagnosis can only be achieved after histological examination of the whole tumour post-resection. It is also worth noting that differentiation of SCC is based on subjective assessments of degree of nuclear atypia and keratinisation, without widely accepted criteria to distinguish between well, moderately, and poorly differentiated SCC. However, given that even well-differentiated SCC often presents at an advanced stage,8 it may be implied that the degree of differentiated is not as prognostically important in urothelial tract SCCs. These pathological phenomena represent important areas for future education urologists and oncologists in managing patients with SCC and variant ureteral carcinoma.

There remains a paucity of data in the literature regarding the overall behaviour and risk profiling of ureteral SCC, as well as underlying cause in several cases. Further review is required with greater sample sizes to establish a more robust prognostic and therapeutic outlook for ureteral SCC. Salient takeaways from this case include the association between congenital ureteric anomalies and SCC, and the diagnostic challenge posed by duplex systems in ureteroscopic assessment.

Conclusion

Squamous cell carcinoma of the ureter is a rare entity, primarily emerging in cases with chronic irritation, infection or inflammation. In patients with underlying risk factors for atypical malignancies of the ureter and renal tract, a degree of caution must be exercised, given their propensity to be detected later and lead to adverse patient outcomes.
