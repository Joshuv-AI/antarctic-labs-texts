DANIEL: A Distributed and Scalable Approach for Global Representation Learning with EHR Applications
Zebin Wang zebinwang@g.harvard.edu, Ziming Gan zimingg@uchicago.edu, Weijing Tang weijingt@andrew.cmu.edu, Zongqi Xia zxia1@pitt.edu, Tianrun Cai tcai1@bwh.harvard.edu, Tianxi Cai tcai@hsph.harvard.edu, Junwei Lu junweilu@hsph.harvard.edu

Abstract
Classical probabilistic graphical models face fundamental challenges in modern data environments, which are characterized by high dimensionality, source heterogeneity, and stringent data-sharing constraints. In this work, we revisit the Ising model, a well-established member of the Markov Random Field (MRF) family, and develop a distributed framework that enables scalable and privacy-preserving representation learning from large-scale binary data with inherent low-rank structure. Our approach optimizes a non-convex surrogate loss function via bi-factored gradient descent, offering substantial computational and communication advantages over conventional convex approaches. We evaluate our algorithm on multi-institutional electronic health record (EHR) datasets from 58,248 patients across the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center (UPMC) and Mass General Brigham (MGB), demonstrating superior performance in global representation learning and downstream clinical tasks, including relationship detection, patient phenotyping, and patient clustering. These results highlight a broader potential for statistical inference in federated, high-dimensional settings while addressing the practical challenges of data complexity and multi-institutional integration.

Introduction

Markov Random Fields (MRFs) form a fundamental class of probabilistic graphical models used to study the conditional dependencies between high-dimensional variables
(
Wainwright and Jordan 2008
;
Koller and Friedman 2009
;
Sucar 2015
)
. In particular, the Ising model, as a computationally efficient and theoretically supported variant of MRFs, has been widely adopted in modeling the pairwise interactions between
binary
variables in domains where features naturally exhibit dichotomous patterns
(
Torquato 2011
;
Cheng et al. 2014
;
Marinazzo et al. 2014
;
Zhang and Cai 2018
)
. Compared to alternative graphical models such as Bayesian networks
(
Zhao and Weng 2011
;
Bandyopadhyay et al. 2015
;
Chen et al. 2024
)
, the Ising model provides a more interpretable pairwise interaction framework with more scalable computation when inferring network structures
(
Pourret et al. 2008
)
.

Numerous estimation and inference methods have been developed for the Ising model, including the variational methods
(
Chow and Liu 1968
;
Nguyen and Berg 2012
)
, graph recovery methods based on the mutual information
(
Montanari and Pereira 2009
;
Bresler 2015
;
Kandiros et al. 2023
)
, and
M
-estimators based on the convex regularized conditional likelihood loss
(
Shah et al. 2021
;
Zhu et al. 2023
)
. However, these studies often overlook the practical challenges associated with consolidating cross-institutional data under strict confidentiality regulations. For example, deposition transcripts typically contain sensitive information stored separately across multiple law firms, which may be temporary allies in specific legal contexts but otherwise operate independently or even competitively
(
Perlin 2024
)
. Despite the inter-institutional boundaries, collaborative analysis of deposition data is often necessary to support coordinated legal decision-making and case evaluation
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
. On the other hand, recent federated statistical methods introduce surrogate loss optimization to enable distributed learning while alleviating privacy concerns
(
Jordan et al. 2019
;
Duan et al. 2022
;
Lin et al. 2022
;
Fan et al. 2023
;
Nagy et al. 2023
)
. However, these methods either lack theoretical performance guarantees or rely on conventional optimization frameworks involving large-scale matrix computations, which pose substantial scalability challenges in high-dimensional settings. Consequently, the study of MRFs and the Ising model under modern settings, characterized by large-scale multi-source data and stringent privacy constraints, remains largely underexplored.

One of the most prominent application domains for Ising model-based methodologies designed to capture dependencies among large-scale binary variables is the derivation of low-dimensional embeddings that preserve meaningful clinical semantics from Electronic Health Records (EHRs)
(
Wang et al. 2017b
;
Lu et al. 2019
;
Murali et al. 2023
;
Carvalho et al. 2023
)
. The EHR systems capture rich longitudinal clinical data, including diagnoses, treatments, medical procedures, and laboratory results
(
Forrey et al. 1996
;
Choi et al. 2017
;
Li et al. 2019c
;
Theodorou et al. 2023
)
. Particularly, EHR datasets are commonly represented as binary indicators of clinical features, such as the dichotomous indicator of specific diagnoses and treatments within designated time windows
(
Li et al. 2022
;
Piao et al. 2024
)
. Leveraging their large sample size and comprehensive feature coverage, EHRs have become a cornerstone for clinical knowledge discovery and translational research
(
Asaria et al. 2016
;
Bean et al. 2017
;
Munkhdalai et al. 2018
;
Ahuja et al. 2020
;
Christopoulou et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2021b
)
. In particular, the knowledge graph (KG) embedding learning algorithms offer efficient querying and automated reasoning capabilities
(
Zhou et al. 2022
)
, shedding light on the conditional dependencies among medical entities
(
Liu et al. 2015
;
Salem and Abdo 2016
;
Kaewprag et al. 2017
;
Shen et al. 2018
;
Yu et al. 2022
;
Zhao et al. 2023
;
Gan et al. 2025
)
. These embeddings can further support a broad range of downstream tasks, including phenotype classification, disease sub-typing, and predictive modeling
(
Kirby et al. 2016
;
Glicksberg et al. 2018
;
De Freitas et al. 2021
;
Zhou et al. 2025
)
, which reflect the core objectives of clinical research aimed at enhancing disease understanding and advancing precision medicine
(
Carvalho et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2024
)
.

However, while the Ising model is well-suited for learning embeddings of the EHR data with inherent sparsity and low-rank structure, its conventional implementations, such as those studied by
Nussbaum and Giesen 2019
, face two fundamental challenges:

(1)
Computation scalability for large-scale EHR data.
First, the volume of EHR database grows rapidly in complexity and computational demands in the recent years
(
Dash et al. 2019
;
Gan et al. 2025
)
. This makes conventional optimization approaches for statistical graphical models with sparsity and low-rankness, based on convex regularized likelihood loss and require singular value decomposition (SVD) at each iteration
(
Chandrasekaran et al. 2010
;
Ravikumar et al. 2010
;
Liu et al. 2012
;
Meng et al. 2014
;
Shah et al. 2021
)
, increasingly impractical for large-scale applications. Second, the heterogeneity across multiple EHR sources complicates cross-institutional data unification, as format reconciliation of EHR data is both time-consuming and prone to errors
(
Glynn and Hoffman 2019
;
Fu et al. 2020
;
Zhou et al. 2025
)
.

(2)
Stringent healthcare data privacy regulations.
First, the EHR datasets, which contain sensitive personal health information, are subject to strict federal and state privacy laws, where violations may result in serious legal and reputational consequences
(
Annas 2003
;
Mello et al. 2018
;
Apathy and Holmgren 2020
)
. Second, building centralized, professionally managed EHR databases across healthcare institutions that fully comply with legal and administrative requirements is costly and often infeasible
(
Williams et al. 2008
;
Mia et al. 2022
)
. The alternative embedding learning approaches based on graph learning and natural language processing, such as Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)
(
Scarselli et al. 2009
)
and Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT)
(
Devlin et al. 2019
)
, will typically rely on supervised domain-specific fine-tuning which requires centralized access to large volumes of labeled EHR data that are subject to HIPAA compliance
(
Lee et al. 2020
;
Gu et al. 2021
)
, or show their vulnarable to adversarial model extraction
(
Wu et al. 2022
;
Zhao et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
.

To overcome the challenges mentioned above, we propose a distributed approach for learning embeddings for large-scale, multi-institutional binary data subject to privacy regulations, termed
D
istributed
A
rchitecture for
N
on-Convex
I
sing-based
E
mbedding
L
earning (DANIEL). Our framework optimizes a distributed Ising model with a low-rank parameter matrix that captures the semantic embeddings for the features. Without loss of generality, we focus on global EHR embedding by generating visualized KGs and evaluating numerous downstream clinical tasks. To improve computation scalability, DANIEL performs optimization on a non-convex bi-factored surrogate loss that fully leverages the low-rank structure of embeddings intrinsic to EHRs, substantially reducing the dimensionality of matrices updated at each iteration. To address data-sharing limitations, DANIEL adopts a distributed structure where information exchange across institutions is limited to a
single round of communication
involving locally computed
gradients of loss
, without requiring direct sharing of sensitive data. Extensive simulation studies and empirical evaluations on real-world EHR datasets demonstrated the superiority of DANIEL across core downstream tasks of global EHR embedding, including relationship pair detection, patient phenotyping, and patient clustering, under realistic data-sharing constraints, offering substantial practical advantages over existing alternatives.

1.1
Our Contributions

In summary, the contributions of this paper are four-fold:

\bullet

Modern Research Question Formulation.
We introduce the modern setting for learning dependency structures via MRFs, specifically the Ising model, in scenarios where high-dimensional binary data originates from multiple sources governed by strict privacy constraints. This formulation addresses the pressing needs in real-world applications such as collaborative legal research and healthcare analytics, where traditional assumptions of centralized data access and moderate dimensionality are no longer applicable.

\bullet

Scalable and Privacy-Aware Methodology Design.
We propose DANIEL, an unsupervised framework designed to learn global embeddings from large-scale binary datasets with inherent low-rank structure. DANIEL achieves computational scalability by optimizing a non-convex bi-factored surrogate loss based on the Ising model subject to regularization for embedding balance. It ensures privacy and communication efficiency by requiring only one-shot communication of the gradient statistics, without sharing sensitive raw data across institutions.

\bullet

Simulation-Supported Theoretical Guarantees.
We provide theoretical results for the convergence behavior and statistical validity of DANIEL in distributed settings. Specifically, we show that DANIEL
retains the classical rate
held by convex low-rank Ising estimators under centralized settings, and the rate between the DANIEL estimator and the oracle centralized estimator is
of lower order
than their overall estimation error. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first theoretical performance guarantee for a non-convex distributed Ising estimator, with potential generalizability to other loss functions beyond the current model.

\bullet

Extensive Evaluation in EHR-Based Patient Representation Learning.
We evaluate DANIEL on patient representation learning using real-world EHR datasets from two leading healthcare systems, the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center (UPMC) and Mass General Brigham (MGB). DANIEL shows superiority over established MRF-based and BERT-style alternatives by consistently outperforming them across key downstream tasks in healthcare research, including relationship detection, patient phenotyping, and patient clustering.

Related Literature

Low-Rank Parameter Matrix Estimation Methods.

(
Zheng et al. 2015
)
leads the convex relaxation methods with a nuclear norm penalty, with subsequent developments by
(
Fan et al. 2019
;
Park et al. 2024
)
. Despite their theoretical appeal, these approaches are computationally inefficient as they require iterative implementation of the singular value decomposition (SVD) during optimization. Additionally, they are designed for centralized computing environments and are therefore not suitable for application in distributed settings.
Non-convex optimization methods have been considered for other statistical problems
(
Zhang et al. 2016
;
Sun and Luo 2016
;
Chen and Chi 2018
;
Li et al. 2019a
;
Li et al. 2019b
;
Chi et al. 2019
;
Danilova et al. 2022
)
. However, these approaches also rely on centralized data access, which raises significant concerns regarding data security, for example, the risk of extrajudicial surveillance during centralized data aggregation
(
Shokri and Shmatikov 2015
)
.

Distributed Learning.

(
Gao et al. 2022
)
provides a systematic review of the distributed statistical inference methods, which highlights that a vast amount of research considered the surrogate likelihood approach, which only aggregates gradient or Hessian information from local sites
(
Wang et al. 2017a
;
Jordan et al. 2019
;
Duan et al. 2022
;
Fan et al. 2023
)
. However, these approaches typically depend on the convexity of the surrogate loss. Thus, these approaches cannot be directly extended to the non-convex settings we implement in our work for the purpose of prioritizing efficiency and scalability. Several distributed algorithms have been proposed for non-convex problems under the general optimization framework
(
Tatarenko and Touri 2017
;
Sun et al. 2020
;
Xin et al. 2021
;
Gorbunov et al. 2021
;
Zhao et al. 2022
)
. However, these methods generally require multiple rounds of communication and do not offer theoretical guarantees for the estimation error. In contrast, our proposed DANIEL algorithm overcomes both challenges by conducting estimation under a non-convex surrogate loss with one-shot communication, offering a rigorous statistical error rate for the obtained estimator.

Notations.

. We use

Multi-Institutional Data Setup.
We consider a multi-institutional setup where data is collected across
m
distinct institutions, denoted as
\mathcal{S}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{m}
. For simplicity, we assume that
n

\mathcal{S}_{i}
for
i\in 1,...,m
holds
n/m
independent samples. For sample
\ell
from the
i

p

\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}=\big(x_{1}^{(\ell)},x_{2}^{(\ell)},...,x_{p}^{(\ell)}\big)^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}
, where
x_{j}^{(\ell)}\in\{\pm 1\}
indicates the presence or absence of the
j

\ell
. We assume that the dependency structure among features is shared across all institutions and follows the Ising model with a unified
true
parameter matrix
\bm{\Theta}^{*}=\big\{\theta^{*}_{jk}\big\}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
. The probability density is given by

p_{\bm{\Theta}^{*}}(\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)})=\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\Theta}^{*}}\big(X_{j}=x_{j}^{(\ell)}\text{ for all }j\in[p]\big)\propto\exp\bigg\{\sum_{j\neq k}\theta_{jk}^{*}x_{j}^{(\ell)}x_{k}^{(\ell)}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\theta_{jj}^{*}x_{j}^{(\ell)}\bigg\},

(1)

where
\bm{\Theta}^{*}
is
symmetric
, i.e.,
\theta_{jk}^{*}=\theta_{kj}^{*}
. Heuristically, the off-diagonal entries of
\bm{\Theta}^{*}
, i.e.,
\theta_{jk}^{*}
with

j
th and
k

\bm{\Theta}^{*}

\theta_{jj}^{*}
for

, represent the
individual tendencies
of the
j

\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\Theta}^{*})=d\ll p
. Accordingly, we assume that
\bm{\Theta}^{*}
satisfies a low-rank decomposition in the form of
\bm{\Theta}^{*}=\mathbf{U}^{*}{\mathbf{V}^{*}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}
, where
\mathbf{U}^{*},\mathbf{V}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
and
\mathbf{V}^{*}
equals
\mathbf{U}^{*}
subject to a sign per column, i.e.,
\mathbf{V}^{*}=\mathbf{U}^{*}\mathbf{D}^{*}
for some diagonal matrix
\mathbf{D}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with
\mathbf{D}^{*}_{jj}\in\{\pm 1\}
. Here, each row of
\mathbf{U}^{*}
refers to a
d
-dimensional embedding for the corresponding feature, which can be obtained via rank-
d
SVD of
\bm{\Theta}^{*}
. Inspired by existing work, such as
(
Ravikumar et al. 2010
;
van Borkulo et al. 2014
)
, we estimate the parameters
\theta^{*}_{jk}
by considering the conditional distribution of
x_{j}^{(\ell)}
given all the other feature indicators
\mathbf{x}_{-j}^{(\ell)}=\big(x_{1}^{(\ell)},...,x_{j-1}^{(\ell)},x_{j+1}^{(\ell)},...,x_{p}^{(\ell)}\big)^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}
, with a conditional likelihood given as

\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\Theta}^{*}}(X_{j}=x_{j}^{(\ell)}|\bm{X}_{-j}=\mathbf{x}_{-j}^{(\ell)})=\frac{\exp{\big(2\theta_{jj}^{*}x_{j}^{(\ell)}+2\sum_{k\neq j}\theta_{kj}^{*}x_{j}^{(\ell)}x_{k}^{(\ell)}\big)}}{\exp{\big(2\theta_{jj}^{*}x_{j}^{(\ell)}+2\sum_{k\neq j}\theta_{kj}^{*}x_{j}^{(\ell)}x_{k}^{(\ell)}\big)}+1}.

(2)

Based on (
2
), we have the conditional log-likelihood loss for all the
n
samples from
m
institutions as

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\Theta})=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\tau=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{S}_{\tau}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bigg\{2\theta_{jj}x_{j}^{(\ell)}&+2\sum_{k\neq j}\theta_{kj}x_{j}^{(\ell)}x_{k}^{(\ell)}\\
&-\log\Big(\exp\big(2\theta_{jj}x_{j}^{(\ell)}+2\sum_{k\neq j}\theta_{kj}x_{j}^{(\ell)}x_{k}^{(\ell)}\big)+1\Big)\bigg\}.\end{split}

(3)

The low-rank structure of the parameter matrix can also be motivated by a latent mixed-value graphical model, which will be discussed in Section
6
.

3
Methodology

In this section, we propose DANIEL, a distributed algorithm designed to estimate
\bm{\Theta}^{*}
via a non-convex approach. Compared to the Communication-efficient Surrogate Likelihood (CSL) framework
(
Jordan et al. 2019
)
, which operates under a convex loss, DANIEL leverages a non-convex bi-factored surrogate loss to improve efficiency by exploiting the inherent low-rank structure of the dependency structure of interest. In specific, suppose that the
n
samples are partitioned across
m
institutions, denoted by
\mathcal{S}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{m}
, with each institution storing
n/m
samples. We set
\mathcal{S}_{1}
as the
hub institution
, which is responsible for managing communication and aggregating intermediate results. We denote the log-likelihood on the
i
th institution as

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{\Theta})=-\frac{1}{n/m}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bigg\{2\theta_{jj}x_{j}^{(\ell)}&+2\sum_{k\neq j}\theta_{kj}x_{j}^{(\ell)}x_{k}^{(\ell)}\\
&-\log\Big(\exp\big(2\theta_{jj}x_{j}^{(\ell)}+2\sum_{k\neq j}\theta_{kj}x_{j}^{(\ell)}x_{k}^{(\ell)}\big)+1\Big)\bigg\}.\end{split}

We propose the bi-factored surrogate loss

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V};\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{0})=\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{1}(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T})}_{\mathrm{Loss\ on\ Hub}}+\underbrace{\big\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{0})-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{1}(\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{0}),{\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{V}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\big\rangle}_{\mathrm{Gradient\ Correction}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{4}\big\|\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\mathbf{U}-\mathbf{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\mathbf{V}\big\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}_{\mathrm{Embedding\ Balancer}},

(4)

where
\nabla\mathcal{L}_{i}(\cdot)
represents the gradient of
\mathcal{L}_{i}(\cdot)
, and
\nabla\mathcal{L}(\cdot)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{i}(\cdot)
. In (
4
), the initialization
\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{0}
is obtained on the hub institution
\mathcal{S}_{1}
using only its local data, by solving the following convex optimization problem regularized by the nuclear norm

\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{\rm{cvx}}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bm{\Theta}\in\mathcal{T}}\big\{\mathcal{L}_{1}(\bm{\Theta})+\lambda\|\bm{\Theta}\|_{*}\big\},\text{ with }\mathcal{T}=\big\{\bm{\Theta}=\{\theta_{jk}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}\big|\|\bm{\Theta}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq B\big\},

(5)

where
B
is a fixed positive constant. Theorem
7
shows the theoretical rate of
\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{\rm{cvx}}
and ensures that a full convergence in optimizing (
5
) is
sufficient but not necessary
to obtain a valid
\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{0}
, as the rank-
d
SVD of
\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{\rm{cvx}}
, for subsequent optimization. The bi-factored surrogate loss, as indicated by (
4
), consists of three terms: (i) the loss on the hub institution that approximates the overall loss; (ii) a correction term that incorporates global gradient information via a one-shot communication; and (iii) a regularization term that balances
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
to ensure the identifiability of the bi-factor structure, as inspired by
(
Park et al. 2018
)
. After collecting
\nabla\mathcal{L}_{i}(\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{0})
from all institutions, we minimize (
4
) in the hub institution via gradient descent. The hyperparameter
1/4
assigned to the regularization term is tuning-insensitive and potentially replaceable by any positive constant larger than 1/4. As indicated by the summary of DANIEL in Algorithm
1
, our proposed algorithm requires only one round of communication between each institution and the hub institution. In DANIEL, the number of iteration steps
\Gamma
is discussed in Theorem
5
, and the choice of step size
\eta
is discussed in Appendix
A.1
. Finally, we highlight an important structural property of DANIEL in Remark
1
.

4
Theoretical Analysis

In this section, we summarize the theoretical results on DANIEL’s performance and validity. Specifically, we address three core aspects: Section
4.1
establishes the statistical rate of the DANIEL estimator; Section
4.2
highlights the feasibility of the initialization procedure of DANIEL in order to achieve the desired statistical rate; Section
4.3
validates the distributed design of DANIEL by proving that the distance between the distributed DANIEL estimator and its centralized counterpart is
of lower order
than their statistical error to the truth.

4.1
Statistical Rate of DANIEL

First, we show the statistical rate of the proposed DANIEL estimator
\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}=\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}
and illustrate its efficiency.
We begin by stating the set of assumptions necessary for our theoretical analysis. We denote the
per-sample
gradient as
\mathbf{W}_{\ell}(\bm{\Theta})
, such that

\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\Theta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\mathbf{W}_{\ell}(\bm{\Theta})
. Additionally, we denote the empirical Hessian matrix of
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\Theta})
as
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(\bm{\Theta})
, with expectation
\mathbf{H}(\bm{\Theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\Theta}}[\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(\cdot)]
.

Assumption
2
represents the convexity and smoothness conditions of
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\Theta})
at the population level, which is widely used in the theoretical analysis of high-dimensional logistic regression
(
Ravikumar et al. 2010
)
. In particular, Assumption
2
ensures that the loss follows the
restricted strongly convexity and smoothness
conditions, as proposed in the existing literature
(
Negahban et al. 2012
;
Fan et al. 2019
;

5
Simulation Study

In this section, we present simulation studies to evaluate the performance of the proposed DANIEL algorithm, focusing on three key research questions:
RQ1:
How does DANIEL perform in a centralized setting compared to convex optimization-based alternatives in terms of estimation accuracy?
RQ2:
How does DANIEL perform in the proposed distributed setting, as compared with the centralized setting, in terms of estimation accuracy?
RQ3:
How does the computational efficiency of DANIEL compare to existing centralized and distributed baseline algorithms?

In our simulation study, we compare the performance of DANIEL with the baseline methods under both centralized and distributed settings. To adapt the baseline models to distributed settings, we follow the procedures proposed by

6
Application of DANIEL to EHR for Neurodegenerative Diseases

In this section, we evaluate the practical applicability of the proposed DANIEL algorithm using real-world EHR data, focusing on neurodegenerative diseases. Our primary objective is to address the research question: How does DANIEL perform in terms of estimation accuracy across various downstream tasks when compared with centralized non-convex methods, convex optimization-based methods, and machine learning (ML)-based methods?

6.1
Model Description

Constructing patient representations based on EHR data facilitates a range of clinical tasks, such as identifying similar patients
(
Sharafoddini et al. 2017
)
, predicting mortality
(
Allyn et al. 2017
)
, and patient phenotyping
(
Zhu et al. 2016
;
Blinov and Kokh 2021
)
. For each patient, we assume that a latent variable
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
encapsulates essential information influencing feature occurrence and joint distribution in clinical data. This unobservable latent Gaussian variable
\mathbf{y}
, referred to as the
patient embedding
, informs downstream tasks such as patient clustering.
We model the joint distribution
p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
between the binary feature occurrence vector
\mathbf{x}
and the patient embedding
\mathbf{y}
as a mixed-value graphical model previously studied by
Nussbaum and Giesen 2019
:

p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\propto\exp\Big(2\mathbf{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\mathbf{y}+\bm{b}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\mathbf{x}\Big).

(8)

In model (
8
), the feature embedding matrix
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
aligns the distribution of binary variable
\mathbf{x}
with the patient embedding
\mathbf{y}
, describing how latent patient embeddings determine the probabilities of clinical feature occurrences. The marginal distribution of
\mathbf{x}
takes the form

p(\mathbf{x})\propto\exp\big(\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\mathbf{x}+\bm{b}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\mathbf{x}\big)
, which corresponds precisely to the low-rank Ising model considered in our work. To ensure model consistency, we set the linear term coefficients
\bm{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{p})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}
as
b_{j}=(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T})_{jj}
, which ensures that the structure satisfy the Ising model constraints with a low-dimensional positive semi-definite parameter matrix
\bm{\Theta}^{*}=\mathbf{U}^{*}(\mathbf{V}^{*})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}
, where
\mathbf{U}^{*}=\mathbf{V}^{*}=\mathbf{U}
. We use
\bm{\Theta}^{*}
and
\mathbf{U}^{*}
to generate patient profiles and support downstream tasks such as patient clustering, as detailed in Section
6.2
.

7
Discussion

In this paper, we introduce DANIEL, a scalable and privacy-aware framework for representation learning on high-dimensional, multi-source data under strict data-sharing restrictions. We employ Markov Random Fields, specifically the Ising model, for data represented as binary vectors, in a real-world, multi-institution setting where modern data privacy constraints invalidate the key assumptions of the classical theory for centralized low-rank Ising model optimization. Our objective is to learn high-quality global feature representations and patient embeddings for downstream analysis to the codified EHR data, including feature relationship detection, patient phenotyping, and patient clustering that can inform a long-term, precise treatment plan for the patient cohort of interest. To address the technical challenges in scalability and privacy, we develop DANIEL, which implements a distributed Ising framework that optimizes a non-convex bi-factored surrogate of the conditional likelihood. Our design not only exploits the low-rank structure of the data for efficiency as compared with computationally expensive SVD-based approaches, but also limits cross-institutional communication to a single round of non-sensitive gradient exchange, thereby eliminating the transfer of raw data.

Our theory shows that, under standard regularity conditions, the DANIEL estimator attains finite-sample error rates comparable to a centralized oracle while requiring only limited cross-institution aggregation. We further establish the validity of our initialization procedure and show that the gap between the DANIEL estimator in its inherent distributed setting and its centralized counterpart is of lower order than the overall estimation error. Simulation studies across a wide range of configurations demonstrate that the DANIEL estimator achieves lower Frobenius norm estimation error and substantially shorter runtime than baselines focusing on convex optimization that conduct SVD at every iteration until convergence. Our empirical results indicate the robustness of DANIEL and suggest that the theoretical performance guarantees on the DANIEL estimator, including its initialization procedure and its distributed setting, continue to hold even when the conditions on feature dimensionality and the number of participating institutions proposed in our theory are substantially relaxed.

Extensive experiments on the codified EHR from the UPMC-MGB Alzheimer’s Disease dataset demonstrate the superiority of DANIEL in detecting EHR feature pairs with known clinical relationships and distinguishing patients before versus after dementia diagnosis, compared to baseline methods based on convex optimization and modern variants of BERT focusing on biomedical data processing. Furthermore, DANIEL’s patient embedding induces
k
-means clusters that stratify patients by their pace of Alzheimer’s disease progression, suggesting that the learned representations capture disease heterogeneity relevant to long-term care pathways. The feature embeddings, as visualized via knowledge graphs, recover numerous literature-supported medical insights and demonstrate the potential for discovering additional feature co-occurrence patterns in Alzheimer’s disease and multiple sclerosis (MS), a highly correlated disease, which motivates further research.

Looking forward, two future directions warrant future exploration and refinement. On the methodological and theoretical side, we aim to extend the DANIEL framework beyond the low-rank Ising model to broader probabilistic graphical model families. We would consider scenarios such as mixed discrete-continuous variables, time-varying interactions, and ontology-aware structures, which potentially broaden DANIEL’s applicability while preserving a general federated design suitable for privacy-aware studies. On the practical and ML implementation side, we plan to integrate our federated statistical backbone with state-of-the-art LLM-driven sequence encoders and graph-augmented retrieval frameworks, such as GraphRAG
(
Edge et al. 2025
;
Han et al. 2025
)
, to yield hybrid representations that maintain privacy, align with the stated research questions, and further improve downstream performance and utility for end users.

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \underbrace{-\bigg\{\int_{0}^{1}\bigg[\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{1}\big(\bm{\Theta}^{*}+s\big(\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{0}-\bm{\Theta}^{*}\big)\big)-\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{1}\big(\bm{\Theta}^{*}\big)\bigg]ds\bigg\}\mathrm{vec}\big(\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{0}-\bm{\Theta}^{*}\big)\Bigg\}}_{\mathbf{E}_{3}}.

(18)

(21)

with probability at least

\displaystyle\mathrm{vec}(\bm{\Delta})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(\bm{\Theta})\mathrm{vec}(\bm{\Delta})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{D})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bigg\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\sum_{r=1}^{p}\mathrm{vec}\big(\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{(-r)}^{(\ell)}(\bm{\Theta})\big)\mathrm{vec}\big(\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{(-r)}^{(\ell)}(\bm{\Theta})\big)^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\bigg\}\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{D}),

as
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\mathbf{B})=\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{A})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{B})
.
∎

Appendix F
Proof for the Rationality of Assumption

In this section, we prove Proposition
4
as one example where Assumption
3
has been rational.

Proof of Proposition
4
.

First, we compute each entry of the matrix
\mathbf{\Xi}_{\ell}(\bm{\Theta}^{*})=\mathbf{W}_{\ell}(\bm{\Theta}^{*})\mathbf{W}_{\ell}(\bm{\Theta}^{*})^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}
, which is given by the following expression

\big[\mathbf{\Xi}_{\ell}(\bm{\Theta}^{*})\big]_{ij}=\sum_{k\neq i,j}4x_{i}^{(\ell)}x_{j}^{(\ell)}\big(B_{i,\ell}^{*}+B_{k,\ell}^{*}\big)\big(B_{j,\ell}^{*}+B_{k,\ell}^{*}\big)+4x_{i}(B_{i,\ell}^{*2}+B_{i,\ell}^{*}B_{j,\ell}^{*})+4x_{j}(B_{j,\ell}^{*2}+B_{i,\ell}^{*}B_{j,\ell}^{*})

(50)

if
i\neq j
and
\big[\mathbf{\Xi}_{\ell}(\bm{\Theta}^{*})\big]_{ii}=4\sum_{k\neq i}\big(B_{i,\ell}^{*}+B_{k,\ell}^{*}\big)^{2}+4B_{i,\ell}^{*2}
.
In this context, the correlation between the values on vertices in a graphical model is depicted by
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}X_{j}]
, where
X_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}
for any arbitrary
i\in[p]
representing the occurrence pattern for the
i
th EHR code in the graph
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
with
p
codes of interest in total.

Our proof involves finding a lower bound for the diagonal entries of
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{\Xi}_{\ell}(\bm{\Theta}^{*})\big]
and an
upper
bound for the off-diagonal entries of
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{\Xi}_{\ell}(\bm{\Theta}^{*})\big]
using the closed-form expression presented in (
50
), where
\big(X_{i}^{(\ell)}\big)^{2}=1
for any
i\in[p]
. We then show that the matrix
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{\Xi}_{\ell}(\bm{\Theta}^{*})\big]
is diagonally dominant under mild regularization conditions imposed on the Ising model structure. Finally, we show Proposition
4
by applying the Gershgorin circle theorem to obtain an upper bound on the operator norm of
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{\Xi}_{\ell}(\bm{\Theta}^{*})\big]
. By the binary Ising observation, we have

\big|Q_{i,\ell}(\bm{\Theta}^{*})\big|=\Big|2\theta^{*}_{ii}x_{i}^{(\ell)}+2\sum_{j\neq i}\theta^{*}_{ji}x_{i}^{(\ell)}x_{j}^{(\ell)}\Big|\leq 2\sum_{j=1;j\neq i}^{p}\big|\theta^{*}_{ij}\big|\text{\ \ for any \ }i\in[p],\ \ell\in[n].

Since the maximum degree of
\mathcal{G}
is
\tau
, there exists a lower bound for
\big\{\mathbf{\Xi}_{\ell}(\bm{\Theta}^{*})\big\}_{ii}
such that

\displaystyle\big[\mathbf{\Xi}_{\ell}(\bm{\Theta}^{*})\big]_{ii}

\displaystyle=4\sum_{k\neq i}\big(B_{i,\ell}^{*}+B_{k,\ell}^{*}\big)^{2}+4B_{i,\ell}^{*2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{16p}{\big(1+e^{2\sum_{j=1;j\neq i}^{p}|\theta_{ij}^{*}|}\big)^{2}}=\frac{16p}{\big(1+e^{2\|\bm{\Theta}^{*}\|_{1,\infty}}\big)^{2}}.

(51)

We need to study the correlation for the off-diagonal terms. Combining Theorem 1.1 in
Ding et al. 2023
with Lemma C.2 and C.3 in
Neykov and Liu 2019
, we have

\mathbb{E}{[X_{s}X_{t}]}\leq\frac{\tau\tanh{(\theta_{\max})}^{2}}{1-(\tau-1)\tanh{(\theta_{\max})}}\ \ \text{ when }\ \ (s,t)\notin\mathcal{E},

(52)

where
\tau
denote the maximum degree of the graph
\mathcal{G}
. And by Lemma 16 of
Nikolakakis et al. 2021
, we have

\mathbb{E}{[X_{s}X_{t}]}\leq\frac{\tau\tanh{(\theta_{\max})}^{2}}{1-(\tau-1)\tanh{(\theta_{\max})}}+\tanh{(\theta_{\max})}\text{ for }(s,t)\in\mathcal{E}.

(53)

By (
50
), we have for any
i
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1;j\neq i}^{p}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\{\mathbf{\Xi}_{\ell}(\bm{\Theta}^{*})\big\}_{ij}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1;j\neq i}^{p}16p\mathbb{P}(X_{i}X_{j}=1)=\sum_{j=1;j\neq i}^{p}8p(\mathbb{E}[X_{i}X_{j}]+1)

\displaystyle\leq 8p^{2}\frac{\tau\tanh{(\theta_{\max})}^{2}}{1-(\tau-1)\tanh{(\theta_{\max})}}+8p^{2}\tanh{(\theta_{\max})}

\displaystyle\leq 32C^{2}p\tau\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\{\mathbf{\Xi}_{\ell}(\bm{\Theta}^{*})\big\}_{ii}\Big],

where the third inequality is due to
\|\bm{\Theta}^{*}\|_{1,\infty}\leq C/p
,
\theta_{\max}\leq\|\bm{\Theta}^{*}\|_{1,\infty}
and
\tanh(x)\leq x
for
x\geq 0
, and the last inequality is due to (
51
). By applying Gerschgorin’s circle theorem
(
Gerschgorin 1931
)
, we show that the matrix
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{\Xi}_{\ell}(\bm{\Theta}^{*})\big]
has an operator norm of
O(p)
.
∎

Appendix G
Supplementary Discussion on Experiment Configuration

G.1
Hyperparameters for DANIEL in Simulation Study

DANIEL:
The stopping criterion for DANIEL is based on the Frobenius norm of the difference between estimators across successive iterations. Specifically, we compute

\delta_{\gamma}=\big\|\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{\gamma}-\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{\gamma-1}\big\|_{\rm F},\text{ where }\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{\gamma}=\mathbf{U}_{\gamma}\mathbf{V}_{\gamma}^{\top}\text{ for the }\gamma\text{th iteration},

and terminate the iteration when either
\delta_{\gamma}<10^{-5}
or the maximum number of iterations,
\Gamma=50
, is reached. The step size
\eta
is selected via grid search from the range
[0.1,0.3]
, depending on the
(n,p)
setup. The algorithm is initialized using a rank-
d
SVD of a preliminary solution to convex optimization problem (
5
) with the local data at the hub institution
\mathcal{S}_{1}
, obtained by applying 5 gradient updates starting from a
p\times p
zero matrix. The time required in initialization is included in the reported computation time for DANIEL.

G.2
Detailed Explanation for Baseline Methods in Simulation Study

Baseline Methods:
For all four baseline methods, the stopping criterion and step size
\eta
are aligned with those of DANIEL. Additional method-specific hyper-parameters are set as follows: for SV-Soft and SV-Hard, the singular value threshold
\tau
is fixed at
10^{-3}
; for SV-Topd, the number of retained singular values is set to match the true rank
d
of the underlying matrix
\bm{\Theta}^{*}
.

SV-Soft
: After each gradient updates
\bm{\Theta}_{\gamma}^{(0)}=\bm{\Theta}_{\gamma-1}-\eta\nabla_{\bm{\Theta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\Theta})\mid_{\bm{\Theta}=\bm{\Theta}_{\gamma-1}}
, denote the SVD of
\bm{\Theta}_{\gamma}^{(0)}=\mathbf{U}_{\gamma}^{(0)}\bm{\Sigma}_{\gamma}^{(0)}\big(\mathbf{V}_{\gamma}^{(0)}\big)^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}
, where
\bm{\Sigma}_{\gamma}^{(0)}={\rm diag}(\sigma_{\gamma,1},\ldots,\sigma_{\gamma,p})
. A
soft
thresholding is then applied in each iteration as

\bm{\Sigma}_{\gamma}^{\text{SV-Soft}}={\rm diag}\big((\sigma_{\gamma,1}-\tau)_{+},\ldots,(\sigma_{\gamma,p}-\tau)_{+}\big),\text{ and }\bm{\Theta}_{\gamma}=\mathbf{U}_{\gamma}^{(0)}\bm{\Sigma}_{\gamma}^{\text{SV-Soft}}\big(\mathbf{V}_{\gamma}^{(0)}\big)^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}.

SV-Hard
: For
hard
thresholding, we set
\tau
as a hard threshold to the eigenvalues. In each iteration, we have

\bm{\Sigma}_{\gamma}^{\text{SV-Hard}}={\rm diag}\big(\sigma_{\gamma,1}\mathbf{1}_{\sigma_{\gamma,1}>\tau},\ldots,\sigma_{\gamma,p}\mathbf{1}_{\sigma_{\gamma,p}>\tau}\big),\text{ and }\bm{\Theta}_{\gamma}=\mathbf{U}_{\gamma}^{(0)}\bm{\Sigma}_{\gamma}^{\text{SV-Hard}}\big(\mathbf{V}_{\gamma}^{(0)}\big)^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}.

SV-Topd
: In this scenario, we set

\bm{\Sigma}_{\gamma}^{\text{SV-Topd}}={\rm diag}\big(\sigma_{\gamma,1},\ldots,\sigma_{\gamma,r},0,\ldots,0\big),\text{ and }\bm{\Theta}_{\gamma}=\mathbf{U}_{\gamma}^{(0)}\bm{\Sigma}_{\gamma}^{\text{SV-Topd}}\big(\mathbf{V}_{\gamma}^{(0)}\big)^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}.

PSD-Cvx
: In this scenario, we consider the positive semi-definite (PSD) constraints
(
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
)
, which yields the iterative update

\bm{\Sigma}_{\gamma}^{\text{PSD-Cvx}}={\rm diag}\big((\sigma_{\gamma,1})_{+},\ldots,(\sigma_{\gamma,p})_{+}\big),\text{ and }\bm{\Theta}_{\gamma}=\mathbf{U}_{\gamma}^{(0)}\bm{\Sigma}_{\gamma}^{\text{PSD-Cvx}}\big(\mathbf{V}_{\gamma}^{(0)}\big)^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}.

As for the distributed settings of these baseline methods, we propose the loss

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{(b)}(\bm{\Theta};\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{0})=\mathcal{L}_{1}(\bm{\Theta})+\big\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{0})-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{1}(\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{0}),\bm{\Theta}\big\rangle.

For each gradient, we update
\bm{\Theta}_{\gamma}^{(0)}=\bm{\Theta}_{\gamma-1}-\eta\nabla_{\bm{\Theta}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{(b)}(\bm{\Theta})\mid_{\bm{\Theta}=\bm{\Theta}_{\gamma-1}}
, followed by a matrix update after SVD of
\bm{\Theta}_{\gamma}^{(0)}
.

G.3
Hyperparameters for DANIEL’s Implementation to EHR Study

DANIEL:
For the real-world EHR application, we set the maximum number of iterations
\Gamma=200
, the step size
\eta=0.01
, and the latent rank
