Digital innovation and green productivity gap
Authors: Yarong Sun, Lijun Hu, Fang He, Lei Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite rapid progress in green innovation, the limited conversion of technological achievements into productivity gains has resulted in a persistent green productivity gap. this study investigates how digital innovation contributes to narrowing this gap by promoting the effective transformation of green innovation into sustainable economic performance. Based on panel data for 287 Chinese cities from 2009 to 2022, this study explores the mechanisms, moderating factors, and regional variations that shape this relationship. Empirical evidence demonstrates that digital innovation significantly reduces the green productivity gap by improving energy efficiency, optimizing industrial structures, and strengthening environmental regulation. The impact is stronger in inland regions, smaller cities, and central China. Furthermore, private ownership structures and targeted R&D investments further amplify the effect of digital innovation. Policy implications emphasize region-specific strategies for digital-green integration and sustainable productivity transformation.Keywords: Digital innovation, Green productivity gap, Green total factor productivity, Digital-green integrationSubject terms: Economics, Economics, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

In recent decades, accelerating progress in digital technologies has reshaped the global landscape of innovation and economic organization. China has played a pivotal role in this transformation, exhibiting remarkable growth in both technological capability and market activity. According to the Statistical Analysis Report on Patents in Core Industries of the Digital Economy (2024), the global core digital industries obtained approximately 888,000 granted invention patents in 2023, an 11.7% year-on-year increase. China alone contributed about 45.7% of these patents (406,000), representing an impressive 21.2% annual growth. At the same time, according to the China Digital Economy Development Report (2024), the scale of China’s digital economy reached 53.9 trillion RMB, contributing 42.8% to GDP and driving 66.45% of GDP growth. These indicators collectively demonstrate that digitalization has evolved from a supporting function into a central engine of national economic productivity.

Meanwhile, the fusion of digital technologies with conventional industrial sectors has accelerated, marking a critical stage in China’s industrial transformation. In 2023, the digitalization level of China’s industrial sector (25.03%) surpassed that of the service sector for the first time, reflecting a structural shift toward digitally enabled manufacturing. However, patent structure data from 2008 to 2023 reveal a deeper transformation in innovation patterns. While invention patents have continued to expand steadily, utility model patents have declined sharply since 2021 (Fig. 1), signaling a shift from incremental innovation toward more complex and technology-intensive digital innovation systems.

Parallel to this rapid digital transformation, green technological innovation, focusing on low-carbon and environmentally sustainable development, has also gained strong momentum in China. According to data from the China National Intellectual Property Administration (CNIPA), around 97,000 patents related to green and low-carbon technologies were registered in 2023, representing a 14.5% increase over the previous year. However, despite the sustained rise in green invention patents, the sharp decline in utility model patents since 2021 (Fig. 2) reveals structural asymmetries in the composition of green innovation.

Green patent applications and grants in China (2008–2023).

More importantly, the continued growth of green patent output has not translated into commensurate improvements in green total factor productivity (GTFP), suggesting a persistent mismatch between technological advancement and productivity performance (Fig. 3). This discrepancy, here conceptualized as the green productivity gap, reflects the inefficiency in converting green innovation achievements into measurable economic and environmental gains. The underlying causes include uneven innovation quality, weak commercialization efficiency, and limited integration of green technologies into practical production systems. Together, these challenges highlight the urgent need to understand how digital innovation can help bridge the gap between green technological progress and realized productivity improvements.

Green technology innovation and GTFP in China (2008–2022).

Acknowledging the strategic significance of linking digital progress with green transformation, the Chinese government has made digital-green integration a central policy priority. The 2024 Central Economic Work Conference, for instance, emphasized harnessing digital and green technologies to accelerate industrial upgrading and foster sustainable growth. Within this policy framework, understanding how digital innovation enhances green technological capabilities and translates them into productivity improvements, thereby narrowing the green productivity gap, has become a critical research question.

Amid ongoing scientific and industrial transformation, digital innovation has become a central catalyst for structural evolution, driven by emerging business models and technological paradigms. Initially conceptualized by Yoo et al.1, digital innovation refers to the transformative impact of digital technologies on products, processes, organizational structures, and business models. It plays a crucial role in sustaining long-term competitive advantage in the digital era1,2. Typically, digital innovation comprises three interrelated components: digital technologies, innovation processes, and innovation outputs. With digital technologies providing the foundation for product and service transformation3.

Within China’s institutional and industrial context, Yu et al.4 further distinguished two dimensions of digital innovation: first, the digitalization of physical components, and second, innovation within digital technologies themselves. Following Ciriello et al.5 and considering limitations in data accessibility, this research adopts the latter perspective, conceptualizing digital innovation as innovation in digital technologies. This approach provides a clear and operational framework for quantitatively analyzing how digital innovation can help close the green productivity gap and strengthen the linkage between technological advancement and realized productivity gains.

This study offers three major contributions. Conceptually, it refines the definition of digital innovation and highlights its distinctive role in promoting sustainable productivity growth. Unlike previous research that treats the digital economy as a broad and imprecise construct, this study develops a more targeted conceptual framework that centers on digital innovation itself. It further examines how digital innovation helps convert green technological progress into tangible productivity improvements, thereby deepening understanding of the mechanisms through which innovation supports productivity-oriented sustainability and strengthens digital-green integration. Methodologically, this study introduces an integrated analytical framework that links green technological innovation and GTFP through the construction of the green productivity gap. By aligning innovation outputs with realized productivity outcomes, the framework captures the dynamic interdependence between innovation generation and utilization that earlier approaches often overlooked. This unified perspective provides a coherent basis for testing both the underlying mechanisms and contextual boundary conditions that shape the influence of digital innovation on green productivity performance. Empirically, the study applies a comprehensive strategy that combines mechanism testing, moderation analysis, and a range of robustness and endogeneity checks. These procedures collectively confirm the stability, internal validity, and causal reliability of the results. The findings strengthen the explanatory power and policy relevance of the analysis, providing a reliable foundation for understanding how digital innovation drives sustainable productivity transformation and supports high-quality, low-carbon economic growth.

Literature review

Scholarly attention to the nexus between digitalization and green development has grown rapidly. However, most studies continue to examine these dimensions separately, without integrating them into a unified analytical framework. Green technological innovation serves as a critical engine for industrial upgrading, pollution reduction, and ecological sustainability6,7. Simultaneously, the digital economy, characterized by high technological dynamism and relatively low carbon intensity, substantially enhances regional capacity for green innovation8–11. Documented mechanisms include product and process innovation12, cost reduction and resource allocation enhancement8,13, and the promotion of cross-sectoral collaboration. Indirect channels, such as increased openness, industrial upgrading, and market expansion, further strengthen green innovation performance10,11,14–16. However, empirical results also reveal non-linear and regionally heterogeneous outcomes, shaped by differences in resource endowments, industrial composition, and technological maturity10,17,18.

Concurrently, GTFP, an index combining economic efficiency with environmental performance, has emerged as a central indicator for evaluating the sustainability of digital transformation19. Digital technologies have been shown to enhance GTFP18, particularly in manufacturing and other traditional sectors, thereby accelerating the green transition20–25. Nonetheless, the magnitude and direction of these effects vary depending on resource dependency, geographic location, financial development, and market openness26–28. Previous research also highlights several mediating pathways, including green innovation, industrial restructuring, and resource allocation efficiency, and moderating influences such as foreign direct investment, ownership structure, and R&D intensity, which together shape the heterogeneity of digitalization outcomes21,28–31. Crucially, these studies suggest that misalignment between innovation efforts and regional factor endowments can substantially weaken productivity improvements32.

Building on these insights, recent research has shifted attention toward digital innovation as a key mechanism linking technological generation with economic performance. Empirical evidence suggests that digital innovation facilitates the conversion of green technological progress into productivity gains, thereby supporting industrial green transformation33. It also generates spatial spillovers, promoting the diffusion of green technologies and interregional knowledge transfer34. Mechanism-oriented studies emphasize that digital innovation operates through multiple mediating channels, enhanced energy efficiency, optimized industrial structure, and strengthened environmental regulation, all of which improve the capacity of firms and regions to realize the productivity potential of green technologies14,35,36. Conceptually, these findings converge on the idea of a digital-sustainability ecosystem, wherein digital infrastructure, platforms, and innovation capabilities create enabling conditions for resource reallocation, efficiency enhancement, and collaborative innovation. Within this system, digital innovation acts as a catalyst that transforms technological progress into realized green productivity37.

Despite these advances, existing research has yet to systematically conceptualize or empirically evaluate the green productivity gap, the inefficiency in translating green technological achievements into measurable productivity outcomes. Previous work has primarily focused on either green innovation or GTFP, overlooking the structural disconnect between them. The green productivity gap framework extends the traditional innovation-application perspective by explicitly linking technological progress with realized productivity outcomes. By integrating digital innovation into this framework, the present study enriches the digital-green synergy literature by reframing the discussion from an innovation-application mismatch to a productivity-oriented efficiency gap.

In summary, prior studies have provided valuable evidence on the interrelations among digitalization, green innovation, and productivity performance, yet they often treat the digital economy as an overly broad construct lacking precise definitional boundaries. Moreover, most analyses examine green innovation and GTFP separately, without acknowledging their inherent interdependence. To bridge these research gaps, this study focuses on digital innovation as the central technological driver of the digital economy and explores its role in narrowing the green productivity gap. It emphasizes how digital innovation promotes the conversion of green technological advances into sustained improvements in productivity performance. This integrated perspective contributes to a deeper understanding of how green technologies evolve in digitally driven contexts under market-oriented pressures, bridges the long-standing gap between research and practical implementation, and informs the coordinated advancement of green innovation and productivity, offering important implications for both theoretical research and sustainable development policy design.

Theoretical analysis and research hypotheses

Direct impact of digital innovation

Driven by emerging technologies such as data analytics, artificial intelligence (AI), and cloud-based systems, digital innovation has become a pivotal engine in the digital economy, playing a critical role in advancing green technologies and closing the gap between innovation and practical implementation. From a resource-allocation perspective, firm-level productivity improvement depends fundamentally on the effective acquisition and utilization of innovation-related resources38. Digital technologies enhance resource efficiency by precisely identifying market demands through data analytics39, and by facilitating the global and cross-sectoral flow of innovation resources, thus fostering collaborative innovation networks40–42. Enhanced resource mobility and interdisciplinary cooperation accelerate the transformation of green technological progress into realized productivity gains.

In relation to GTFP, digital innovation supports intelligent resource management and process optimization, particularly in energy utilization. These capabilities reduce resource waste, lower operational costs, and enhance production efficiency38,43. Firms adopting digital management systems can dynamically monitor and adjust resource allocation in real time, achieving substantial improvements in cost-effectiveness and output performance44,45. Furthermore, digital platforms facilitate the market diffusion of green technologies by efficiently matching innovators, investors, and end users, thus accelerating the productivity realization of innovation outcomes. By eliminating technological and information barriers and enabling inter-industry collaboration, digital innovation expands the range of productive applications for green technologies and further enhances GTFP46.

Hypothesis H1

Digital innovation significantly reduces the green productivity gap.

Mechanism analysis

Micro-level mechanism: energy efficiency

Innovation theory highlights the pivotal role of digital innovation, particularly through the IoT, big data analytics, and intelligent monitoring, in improving energy efficiency and promoting sustainable production47–49. These technologies facilitate real-time monitoring and accurate evaluation of energy usage, enabling firms to identify inefficiencies in production processes and equipment and to implement targeted energy-saving interventions50–52. By integrating digital systems into energy governance, firms can optimize their energy use structures, stimulate environmentally oriented R&D, and promote the uptake of low-carbon operational practices48,53.

From a cost-benefit standpoint, improvements in energy efficiency lower production costs and release financial resources that can be reinvested in innovation activities, thereby strengthening firms’ capacity to transform green technologies into productivity gains. Moreover, proactive energy management enables compliance with increasingly stringent environmental regulations, enhances market competitiveness, and strengthens corporate environmental reputation54–56. These dynamics also improve firms’ attractiveness to environmentally responsible investors, creating a positive feedback loop that fosters sustained innovation and productivity enhancement57,58.

Hypothesis H2

Digital innovation reduces the green productivity gap by enhancing energy efficiency.

Meso-level mechanism: industrial structure

Industrial upgrading theory posits that digital innovation acts as an important driving force behind the structural shift of industries toward digitalization, intelligentization, and sustainable development20,59. Leveraging big data capabilities, firms can enhance their responsiveness to market dynamics and implement adaptive adjustments in production and strategic planning. Cloud computing, with its strong data processing and resource integration capacity, effectively lowers innovation costs and entry barriers, thereby attracting new participants into the renewable energy and environmental technology sectors. Simultaneously, AI enables intelligent production management, improves operational efficiency, and accelerates the elimination of outdated capacities, prompting traditional industries to shift toward higher value-added and more sustainable configurations.

An optimized industrial structure expands market preference for environmentally friendly goods and services, thereby encouraging increased capital input in green technologies and fostering their conversion into productivity gains. Improved structural configurations also facilitate inter-firm collaboration and knowledge sharing, generating spillover effects that enhance innovation diffusion and overall sectoral productivity60,61. According to industrial linkage theory, structural upgrading not only promotes green innovation but also yields multi-dimensional improvements in GTFP62,63. These adjustments optimize resource allocation, enhance technological and managerial capabilities across industries, and reduce transaction and coordination costs, collectively narrowing the green productivity gap by improving efficiency in transforming innovation into realized productivity8,15,64.

Hypothesis H3

Digital innovation reduces the green productivity gap by optimizing industrial structure.

Macro-level mechanism: environmental regulation

Externality theory underscores the necessity of environmental regulations to internalize the negative externalities of industrial production, such as pollution and resource depletion. Well-designed regulatory frameworks incentivize firms to adopt cleaner production methods and improve environmental performance. Within this context, digital innovation provides essential technical and analytical support. Big data analytics enhance regulatory precision by enabling real-time environmental monitoring and evidence-based decision-making65,66. The IoT facilitates continuous tracking of emissions, improving firms’ compliance with environmental standards16, while AI systems generate predictive insights that strengthen the timeliness and responsiveness of environmental governance52.

Consistent with the Porter Hypothesis, appropriately stringent yet carefully structured environmental policies may encourage innovative activities by guiding enterprises to invest in cleaner and more efficient technologies as part of their compliance strategies67,68. Through digital technologies, clearer and data-driven regulatory standards promote proactive innovation, accelerate the adoption of sustainable production models, and enhance the conversion of green technologies into productivity gains69.

Moreover, digitally enabled environmental regulations enhance GTFP directly by incentivizing investment in cleaner technologies and indirectly by promoting industrial restructuring away from pollution-intensive industries toward greener sectors19.

Hypothesis H4

Digital innovation reduces the green productivity gap by strengthening environmental regulations.

Moderating effects analysis

Ownership structure

Resource dependence theory posits that firms’ innovation capabilities differ substantially depending on their access to and control over critical resources. State-owned enterprises (SOEs), benefiting from strategic policy support, financing advantages, and resource security, are generally better positioned to integrate digital innovation into large-scale initiatives for green transition38. Conversely, private enterprises, despite facing more limited access to state-backed resources, exhibit higher agility, stronger market orientation, and greater adaptability to consumer demand dynamics70–72.

From a strategic decision-making perspective, SOEs tend to emphasize long-term innovation objectives aligned with environmental mandates and national strategies73,74. whereas private firms prioritize short-term profitability, market responsiveness, and rapid product iteration75.

Hypothesis H5

Ownership structure moderates the relationship between digital innovation and the green productivity gap.

R&D investment

The resource-based view identifies sustained technological investment as a fundamental source of firms’ competitive advantage and innovation capacity76–79. Higher levels of R&D investment enable firms to integrate digital technologies into production processes and cultivate interdisciplinary human capital, which are essential for long-term innovation and sustainable productivity growth80,81.

Endogenous growth theory underscores R&D as a key engine for technological progress and sustainable economic development, aligning with national strategies that prioritize digital and green transformation. Similarly, collaborative innovation theory suggests that coordinated public-private R&D investment fosters closer university-industry partnerships, accelerating the transformation of scientific research into industrial productivity gains and enhancing economy-wide technological capabilities82.

However, the effectiveness of R&D investment is determined not merely by its magnitude but also by how efficiently resources are allocated. When R&D resources are targeted toward digital transformation and green productivity enhancement such as clean digital innovation, smart manufacturing, and energy-efficient systems, they enhance the conversion of technological progress into realized productivity improvements, thereby narrowing the green productivity gap. In contrast, misallocated R&D resources, characterized by inefficient, redundant, or misaligned spending on low-productivity or non-green projects, weaken the synergy between digital and green innovation, reduce absorptive capacity, and may even widen the gap between innovation and productivity realization. Thus, the moderating role of R&D investment lies not only in its intensity but also in its strategic targeting, ensuring that technological efforts are effectively aligned with digital-green integration goals to maximize productivity outcomes.

Hypothesis H6

R&D investment moderates the relationship between digital innovation and the green productivity gap.

To capture the inefficiency in translating technological progress into realized productivity outcomes, this study defines the green productivity gap as the difference between the annual increase in green patent applications and the growth in GTFP. Specifically, green patent applications capture the output dimension of innovation activity, reflecting new knowledge creation and inventive activity, while GTFP reflects the application dimension, measuring the degree to which these innovations are effectively transformed into productivity gains that integrate economic performance with environmental sustainability.

A larger green productivity gap indicates a weaker linkage between technological innovation and productivity realization, that is, technological achievements are not efficiently converted into measurable improvements in green productivity. Conversely, a smaller green productivity gap implies stronger innovation-productivity coupling and a more effective digital-green integration process. This operationalization extends beyond traditional innovation-application frameworks by explicitly emphasizing productivity outcomes as the ultimate measure of green technological efficiency. By adopting this approach, the study introduces a novel perspective for evaluating how digital innovation enhances the transformation of green technologies into tangible productivity gains, thereby providing both theoretical and policy-relevant insights.

Core independent variable

Digital Innovation (): Following the earlier definition, this study conceptualizes digital innovation as innovation within digital technologies. It is measured by the proportion of invention patents associated with the digital economy relative to the total number of patent filings, capturing regional heterogeneity in the intensity of digital technological advancement. Digital invention patents represent the most technology-intensive and mature outcomes of digital innovation, directly reflect a region’s capability to generate and apply frontier digital technologies. This indicator effectively captures the technological foundation that drives industrial digitalization and supports the transformation of green technologies into productivity gains. Moreover, patent data are standardized, transparent, and comparable across time and regions, ensuring high reliability and consistency in assessing the spatial and temporal dynamics of regional digital innovation intensity.

Mediator variables

Drawing on the approach of He et al.14, the mediator variable measurement is as follows:

Energy Efficiency (): Ratio of regional GDP to electricity consumption, reflecting productivity per unit energy consumed.

Industrial Structure (): Proportion of tertiary sector value-added to GDP, indicating industrial upgrading.

Environmental Regulation (): Composite index based on industrial pollutant emissions intensity (wastewater, waste gas, solid waste) weighted via the entropy method. Higher index values indicate stronger regulation (reciprocal transformation applied).

Moderator variables

Following the Zheng84, Feng and Guo85, the moderator variable measurement is as follows:

Ownership Structure (): share of the urban workforce employed in non-private entities is used as a proxy for the dominance of the state sector, given its consistency and comparability across cities, capturing the relative presence of state-affiliated employment within urban labor markets.

R&D Investment (): Ratio of government expenditure on science and technology to overall fiscal spending, indicating public R&D investment intensity.

Control variables

Following prior studies, we include variables influencing green technology transformation:

Economic Development Level (): GDP per capita at constant 2008 prices.

Human Capital (): Average schooling years, calculated from education enrollment data across different schooling levels.

Trade Openness (): Total import-export value relative to GDP.

Foreign Direct Investment (): Actual FDI utilization as a proportion of GDP.

Government Intervention (): Fiscal expenditure relative to GDP.

Additional controls: firm size and financial development

Firm size plays an essential role in shaping regional innovation and productivity dynamics. According to Schumpeterian theory, larger firms generally demonstrate stronger innovation performance due to their higher R&D intensity, superior organizational capacity, and economies of scale in technological adoption. In empirical research, firm size is often approximated by variables such as the count of enterprises, employment scale, or total industrial output. In this study, firm size () is proxied by the natural logarithm of the number of industrial firms above the designated scale in each region.

Financial development is another key determinant that promotes the diffusion and utilization of green technologies. A well-functioning financial system enhances capital accessibility, reduces financing constraints, and expedites the low-carbon upgrading of conventional industries. While both the McKinnon and Goldsmith indices are frequently used in the literature, the Goldsmith index, defined as the ratio of total deposits in regional financial institutions to regional GDP (), offers a clearer depiction of financial depth and resource allocation efficiency. Higher index values indicate stronger financial development, which enhances firms’ capacity to finance innovation and sustain productivity growth.

Both firm size and financial development are incorporated as supplementary control factors in the extended baseline specification. As shown in Table 4, both variables are significantly negatively associated with the green productivity gap at the 1% level. Larger firms possess greater resources and capabilities to transform green technological progress into productivity gains, while deeper financial markets provide the necessary capital support for digitalization, innovation diffusion, and sustainable industrial upgrading. Notably, incorporating these additional controls does not materially affect the estimated negative and statistically robust coefficient of digital innovation, further substantiating the reliability and consistency of the principal results.

Endogeneity tests

Instrumental variables (IV) estimation

To alleviate potential endogeneity concerns, the analysis adopts a two-stage least squares (2SLS) approach with four instrumental variables (IVs): the internet penetration rate (IV1), the share of employees in computer services and software industries (IV2), per capita telecommunication business volume (IV3) and mobile phone penetration rate (IV4). All IVs are lagged by nine years (covering 2000–2013) to mitigate simultaneity bias. These lagged indicators effectively reflect the historical development of regional digital infrastructure and technological capacity, ensuring their strong correlation with digital innovation. At the same time, given the substantial time gap, these historical measures are unlikely to exert a direct influence on contemporary green technology innovation, thereby fulfilling the exogeneity assumption.

As reported in Table 7, the results from the first-stage regressions demonstrate that all IVs are highly correlated with digital innovation, with their coefficients remaining positive and statistically significant at the 1% level. Additionally, the Cragg-Donald Wald F-statistics substantially exceed the conventional threshold, indicating that weak instrument bias is not a concern. In the second stage, the estimated coefficients of digital innovation remain negative and significant across all model settings, indicating a persistent inverse relationship with the green productivity gap. These outcomes offer credible causal support for Hypothesis H1, implying that digital innovation contributes effectively to narrowing the inefficiency in transforming green technological progress into realized productivity improvements.

The prominent mediating role of industrial structure highlights its enduring significance in China’s economic upgrading. Industrial evolution involves two complementary dimensions: industrial advancement, characterized by the transition toward sectors with greater technological content and higher value creation, and industrial rationalization, reflected in the enhanced coordination and efficiency of inter-industry linkages. Together, these dynamics constitute essential channels for promoting sustainable productivity gains. Digital innovation accelerates both processes by reducing transaction and innovation costs, improving cross-sectoral resource allocation, and fostering the diffusion of knowledge and technologies. These structural improvements not only stimulate demand for green technologies but also facilitate their commercialization and large-scale adoption, thereby exerting a broader and more persistent influence on narrowing the green productivity gap compared with energy efficiency and environmental regulation.

In particular, enhanced energy efficiency directly supports the realization of productivity gains from green technologies, industrial structure upgrading promotes systemic innovation diffusion, and stricter environmental regulation incentivizes greater green R&D investment. Once these mediating factors are incorporated into the empirical model, the direct effect of digital innovation on the green productivity gap decreases substantially, confirming Hypotheses H2-H4 and clarifying the internal mechanisms through which digital innovation improves the transformation efficiency of green technological progress into productivity outcomes.

The moderating effect of ownership structure suggests that private enterprises are more agile and market-responsive in leveraging digital innovation to enhance productivity. By prioritizing the commercialization and practical application of green technologies, private firms are better able to convert technological advances into measurable productivity gains. Their flexibility in decision-making, stronger market orientation, and competitive innovation incentives collectively enable them to translate innovation into tangible outcomes more efficiently, resulting in a greater reduction in the green productivity gap. In contrast, SOEs, though more aligned with long-term policy objectives and social responsibilities, tend to exhibit institutional inertia and path dependence in technological upgrading. These characteristics may slow the responsiveness of SOEs to digital transformation. Moreover, while preferential access to policy support and financing enhances their stability, it can also lead to resource misallocation and lower innovation efficiency, thereby weakening the narrowing effects of digital innovation and the green productivity gap.

Similarly, the moderating role of R&D investment reveals that the effectiveness of technological investment depends on its alignment with digital transformation strategies and green productivity goals. When R&D investment is not efficiently allocated, such as when resources are directed toward low-impact or redundant projects, the synergy between digital innovation and green productivity improvement diminishes. This misallocation reduces the marginal return on innovation, causes resource inefficiency, and may even widen the green productivity gap. Conversely, when R&D investment is strategically targeted toward clean digital innovation, smart manufacturing, and energy-efficient systems, it enhances firms’ absorptive capacity and strengthens the complementary relationship between digital and green technologies, thereby amplifying the productivity-enhancing effect of digital innovation.

Conclusion and policy implications

Conclusion

In response to the persistent challenge of low conversion efficiency and limited real-world impact of green technological progress, this study examines how digital innovation contributes to narrowing the green productivity gap, which reflects the inefficiency in transforming green technological achievements into realized productivity gains. Using panel data from 287 Chinese cities spanning 2009–2022, the analysis integrates mechanism testing, moderation analysis, and heterogeneity assessment to identify both the structural logic and boundary conditions through which digital innovation enhances green productivity transformation.

Unlike previous studies that primarily focus on how digitalization stimulates the generation of green innovation, typically measured by patent counts or R&D intensity, this study advances the discussion toward the application dimension by introducing the concept of the green productivity gap. This framework captures the disparity between GTFP growth and green patent growth, reflecting the extent to which technological progress translates into actual productivity improvements. By adopting this analytical perspective, the study provides a more comprehensive understanding of how digital innovation not only fosters inventive activity but also strengthens the efficiency of transforming technological progress into sustainable productivity outcomes. This approach yields new empirical evidence and theoretical insights into the bridging role of digital innovation in promoting effective digital-green integration and advancing high-quality economic development.

Policy recommendations

Building upon the above findings, this study proposes region-specific strategies to strengthen the synergistic development of digital innovation and green productivity transformation across China’s diverse urban contexts.

Inland cities: Strengthen digital infrastructure and institutional support

Inland regions generally face weaker digital foundations, insufficient broadband coverage, fragmented data platforms, and limited access to digital finance, all of which constrain industrial upgrading and hinder the diffusion and application of green technologies. Policymakers should therefore prioritize the construction and modernization of high-speed communication networks, integrated industrial data platforms, and digital public-service systems to address structural bottlenecks and narrow the regional digital divide. Expanding inclusive digital-finance channels and strengthening cross-regional data connectivity can better support small and medium-sized enterprises in accessing innovation resources and engaging more actively in the digital-green transition. These initiatives will enhance technological spillovers, improve collaborative efficiency, and strengthen the absorptive capacity of inland economies for digital innovation-driven green transformation, while simultaneously promoting gains in energy efficiency and accelerating the dissemination of environmentally sustainable technologies.

Coastal cities: Promote market-oriented R&D and deepen digital-green integration

Coastal regions already possess mature digital infrastructures and diversified innovation clusters, where the central challenge lies not in generating innovation but in improving its utilization efficiency and market alignment. Accordingly, policy efforts should move beyond foundational investment to focus on optimizing the linkage between digital R&D and market demand. Governments should guide enterprises to integrate digital technologies into high-value-added manufacturing and service sectors while fostering closer collaboration between industries and research institutions. These measures will accelerate the commercialization of green innovations, support industrial structural upgrading, and consolidate the advantages of coastal innovation ecosystems. In addition, cultivating open innovation networks, expanding green consumer markets, and establishing incentive systems that reward effective technological application rather than invention quantity can enhance the transformation efficiency, regulatory effects, and demonstration role of digital innovation in advanced regions.

Cross-regional coordination and capacity-building

Given the strong interdependence between digital and green transformations and the study’s finding that inland and central regions exhibit greater responsiveness to digital innovation, national policymakers should promote cross-regional collaboration to reduce spatial disparities in innovation diffusion. Establishing Digital-Green Cross-Innovation Pilot Zones can serve as an effective platform for linking inland and coastal regions through shared digital infrastructure, joint fiscal incentives, and cross-sector cooperation. Such zones would facilitate technology transfer, data-resource integration, and coordinated industrial upgrading across administrative boundaries. In parallel, national authorities should develop unified data-governance frameworks and differentiated fiscal or tax incentives to encourage innovation spillovers and strengthen regional complementarity. At the same time, strengthening human capital through digital literacy programs, vocational training, and managerial capability development is essential to ensure that workforce skills and institutional capacity evolve in parallel with digital infrastructure and industrial transformation.

Limitations

This study acknowledges certain limitations, particularly regarding the measurement of digital innovation. Currently, digital innovation is operationalized as the proportion of invention patents within the digital economy, which may not comprehensively reflect the diverse and multifaceted nature of digital innovation practices. Future research could adopt more comprehensive indicators that incorporate the integration, implementation, and impacts of digital technologies, while also drawing upon multiple conceptual frameworks to provide a fuller assessment. In addition, regional spillover effects and the complex interactions with concurrent policy initiatives are not fully considered in this study, which may limit the broader applicability of the results. Future research should therefore pursue cross-regional and policy-sensitive analyses to more accurately capture the diverse pathways through which digital innovation shapes variations in the green productivity gap.
