From Operating System to Operating Society: The Next Mission of Medical Informatics
Authors: Kye Hwa Lee, Seoyeon Baik
Published: 2026

The theme of the 2026 Spring Conference of the Korean Society of Medical Informatics (KOSMI), held in Cheongju on June 24–26, was deliberately provocative: “From Operating System to Operating Society: The Next Mission of Medical Informatics.” Across 17 symposia, 10 oral sessions, and 169 submitted abstracts, the meeting offered a notably coherent answer to a question the field has circled for a decade: What is medical informatics for?

For much of its history, medical informatics has served as the hospital’s operating system. The discipline standardized records, embedded decision support in clinical workflows, and built the data substrate—exemplified by openly shared resources such as MIMIC-IV [1]—on which a generation of predictive models was trained. Topol’s formulation of the convergence between human and machine intelligence [2] captures this era well: informatics operated beneath clinical care, improving tasks one model at a time.

That arrangement is now changing. With the arrival of foundation and generalist medical models [3,4], the basic unit of capability has shifted from the single-task model to the reusable, multimodal system [5]. The KOSMI program reflected this shift directly. One symposium on a 24-hospital K-MIMIC dataset and the Korea Health Data Platform (KHDP) showed the data layer consolidating at a national scale; another, on healthcare foundation models and their convergence with physical artificial intelligence (AI), suggested that informatics is no longer confined to the screen. Early demonstrations of autonomous surgical systems [6] make this crossing of boundaries concrete: models are beginning to act in the physical world, not merely generate recommendations about it.

Yet the conference’s center of gravity was not the models themselves. Rather, it was the infrastructure needed to make those models safe enough for routine use, which is where “operating society” carries its force. Symposia on trustworthy medical AI, sustainable AI ecosystems, law and policy in the AI era, workforce development, structural change in the health-information industry, and Korea-led international standardization together portrayed a field absorbing a hard lesson: AI deployment is a societal operation, not merely a technical one. The global literature has reached a similar conclusion, shifting emphasis from accuracy alone toward credible evidence [7], equity [8,9], and governance [10,11]. The questions that dominated the conference floor—How do we trust a model? Who is accountable? Whose data are used, and who benefits?—are questions for a society, not a server, to answer.

The abstracts provided a complementary view. Natural-language processing, medical imaging, and clinical decision support accounted for roughly seven in ten abstracts submitted for oral presentation, echoing themes that now dominate the international literature, from Nature Medicine to NEJM AI [5,12]. The most competitive category was general medical AI/machine learning, in which the acceptance rate fell to 45%; this was less a marker of scarcity than evidence of how crowded the field’s center has become. Newer themes also emerged at the margins: retrieval-augmented generation, multi-agent clinical reasoning, and simulation-based medical education. These areas are not yet stable categories, but they are likely to define the next benchmarks—and the next evaluation gaps [12,13].

Two quieter developments deserve emphasis because each shows the discipline taking ownership of the rules—first society’s rules, then its own. First, the strong showing of standards work—Korea’s leadership in ISO/TC 215 and the spread of harmonization based on the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) Common Data Model—reframes informatics as a discipline that helps write the rules on which a society runs, not merely software that runs within those rules. Second, KOSMI used this cycle to reform its own scholarly governance through a new guideline on multiple submissions and a standardized on-site review rubric benchmarked against AMIA Annual Symposium, MEDINFO (the world congress of the International Medical Informatics Association), and the IEEE Engineering in Medicine and Biology Conference (EMBC). A learned society that operationalizes fairness in its own processes offers the smallest and most credible example of the transition proclaimed by the conference theme.

The scale of the meeting also told its own story. A total of 1,411 participants attended over three days; among the 169 submitted abstracts, 48 were accepted as oral presentations, 55 as e-posters, and 22 as display posters. This strong turnout was in line with recent years and suggests that medical informatics has become a broad, multidisciplinary community rather than a specialist niche.

Three directions therefore merit sustained investment: (1) federated, standards-aligned data commons that allow Korean cohorts to interoperate at national and international scales; (2) prospective, real-world evaluation that prioritizes credible evidence over leaderboard performance; and (3) workforce and governance pipelines that keep pace with deployment rather than trailing it.

The message to carry forward is clear. The “next mission” is not to build a faster operating system, but to provide the connective tissue through which a fair and trustworthy health system can function. Models will continue to improve; what will not build itself is the social infrastructure of trust: validated evidence over demonstrations, equity over average performance, and shared standards over proprietary advantage [7–9]. Medical informatics is uniquely positioned to build that infrastructure. Cheongju suggested that this work has begun.
