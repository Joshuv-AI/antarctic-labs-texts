Examination of kinetic components (pressure and time) of spark plasma sintering on the mechanical properties of WC/W FGM
Authors: Mahdi Ahsanzadeh Vadeghani, Ali Shafyei, Reza Amini Najafabadi, Masoud Atapour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpark plasma sintering (SPS) is one of the new methods in powder metallurgy which has made significant progress over the past two decades and has attracted plenty of attention from researchers and industries. Recently, given the high capabilities of the SPS method, its use for manufacturing functional materials (FGM) has also sparked great research interest. Considering the studies conducted and the limitations of tungsten alloys as well as tungsten carbide alone in applications such as high-energy penetrants in the oil and gas industries along with friction stir welding tools, W/WC FGM samples have been manufactured using the SPS method for the first time in this study to enhance mechanical properties. The aim of this study is to examine the effect of the factors of pressure and time of SPS sintering on the microstructural properties of W/WC FGM. The phase, elemental, and structural properties of the powder, cross-sectional area, and fracture surface of the SPS samples, after the experiments, were characterized using X-ray diffraction and scanning electron microscopy images. The results revealed that the best sintering conditions for creating W/WC FGM bulk with the best mechanical properties included sintering the sample for 15 min, a pressure of 60 MPa, a temperature of 1400 °C, and a heating rate of 200 °C/min. In the optimal case, the W/WC FGM sample consisted of five layers with different chemical compositions and a microstructure without a diffusion zone, with tensile and flexural strengths of 745 and 890 MPa, respectively, and a density of 92.5%.Keywords: Sintering, W/WC FGM, Mechanical properties, Flexural strength, PorositySubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Considering the type of materials employed in modern engineering systems, since the behavior of common materials in industry, such as metal alloys, has failed to fulfil the needs of the aforementioned industries in many cases, researchers have turned to studying and manufacturing new materials with unique properties. In this regard, functionally graded materials (FGMs) have received plenty of attention in recent years thanks to their unique properties for achieving specific functions in engineering structures1–8.

Tungsten and its alloys have many applications in industry given their high melting point (3420 °C), high density (19.3 g/cm3), very strong bonds, and the greatest coherent energy (9.7–109 atom/eV) of all pure elements9–13. Meanwhile, tungsten carbide has also a high melting point (1785 °C), hardness retention at high temperatures, low coefficient of friction, as well as high electrical and thermal conductivity. Compared to tungsten and its alloys, tungsten carbide has greater flexural strength, higher hardness, and higher wear resistance. Also, thanks to its high hardness at high temperatures, it has better creep properties than tungsten. Further, tungsten carbide has lower oxidation kinetics than tungsten. These features have made tungsten carbide a better and safer alternative for some nuclear applications as well as high-energy penetration aid9–11. Drilling tools in the oil and gas industry, radiation shields in the imaging industry, anti-tank bullets in the defense industry, as well as fusion reactors in the nuclear industry are among the most important applications of tungsten and tungsten carbide combinations13.

The most important industrial challenge in the concurrent use of tungsten and tungsten carbide is being solved by modern powder metallurgy and sintering methods. Functionally-Graded Materials (FGMs) and Spark Plasma Sintering (SPS) have been the most modern powder metallurgy and sintering methods over the last two decades, respectively14,15. Functionally-Graded Materials (FGMs) are made of two materials in such a way that the properties change continuously from one material to the other according to a mathematical relationship from one side of the structure to the other14,16. The unique advantage of FGMs is that the change in properties occurs continuously and thus undesirable phenomena such as stress concentration, cracking, and porosity are minimized. The Spark Plasma Sintering (SPS) process refers to the creation of plasma by applying high-frequency DC current simultaneously with pressure and local melting of the surface of powder particles through passing a high-density electric current. This process of applying pulsed current causes plasma to be established in the mold chamber, as well as the presence of plasma during the sintering operation also elevates the sintering speed and significantly shortens the production time while also boosting the quality of parts manufactured by the SPS method14–17. The most important advantages of the SPS method over conventional methods for generating bulk materials are the possibility of: (i) controlling the thermal gradient for FGMs, (ii) local control of the final density, and (iii) precise control of porosity; along with the production of parts with a density very close to the theoretical density, the very short sintering operation time, and achievement of materials with a nanometer structure14,17.

The best method for manufacturing FGMs is a combination of powder metallurgy and sintering. One of the most important sintering methods is spark plasma sintering (SPS)14. This method has been around for 20 years and is considered the most modern sintering method14. Thanks to its short process time and microstructure control, the SPS method has secured a significant position within the industry.

The usage of tungsten and its alloys is growing progressively as suitable materials for several components in fusion reactors as well as high-energy penetrants in the oil and gas as well as military industries15–17. With the emergence of the FGMs mechanism for producing materials, induestires have begun employing tungsten (W) and tungsten carbide (WC) combinations for manufacturing such items. The main problem and objective of this research, considering the limitations of tungsten and tungsten carbide alone in industrial applications, is to examine the kinetic parameters (pressure and temperature) of the spark plasma sintering (SPS) method for manufacturing tungsten-tungsten carbide (WC/W) FGMs.

Sintering process of WC/W FGM

In spark plasma sintering (SPS), four parameters of temperature, time, pressure, and heating rate affect the structure and mechanical properties of the sintered samples. In this study, the effect of two kinetic components, namely sintering time and pressure, has been investigated on the structure and mechanical properties of the W/WC FGM sample. For this purpose, samples with a diameter of 2 cm and a height of 3 cm were sintered. For a detailed study, the thickness of each layer should be 0.6 cm so that the final sample has 3 cm. Initially, the weight of each layer was poured into the mold according to Table 3 and each layers cooled to 20 MPa, after which the layers were stacked and sintered under the conditions of Table 4. To explore the effect of time and pressure, sintering was carried out at three times of 15, 20, and 25 min and pressure at three pressures, 20 MPa, 40 MPa, and 60 MPa. In this research, Nano Zint 10 model device manufactured by Adena Diaco Technology Advanced Equipment Company of Iran was used.

Characterization

Scanning electron microscopy

Structural and morphological studies were performed using a TESCAN-XMU field emission scanning electron microscope manufactured by Volga, Germany, at a voltage of 15 kV. To obtain longitudinal cross-sectional images of the samples, the samples were polished with 60-grit sandpaper and chemically etched in a hot sulfuric acid environment. To make the samples conductive, a thin layer of gold was deposited on the surface of the samples using a sputtering device. After placing the samples inside the device, copper and silver adhesives were employed to connect the samples to each other as well as to the support base.

Semiquantitative energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) analysis was undertaken using a Sung Woo model 550i instrument mounted on a QUANTA PEG 450 scanning electron microscope with a hot cathode electron source supplied by a tungsten filament. The probe diameter used was 1 μm.

Results and discussion

Structural study of W/WC FGM powder

Figure 3 presents the scanning electron microscopic images of the ball milled powder for the sintering process of W/WC FGM. As depicted in the figure, tungsten powder has a quasi-spherical morphology with a particle size of less than 5 microns. By adding nickel and iron to tungsten powder to make the composition 92.5%W2.2%Fe5.25%Ni, the morphology is no longer in quasi-spherical state and the particles are transformed into polyhedral. This polyhedral morphology has a particle size of less than 3 microns but is highly agglomerated. The composition WC10%Co has also a mixture of spherical, elliptical and irregular morphologies. The particle size for this composition varies between 5 and 50 microns. Many studies indicate that spherical morphology is important for sintering powder materials as it helps in flowability and achieving the maximum final bulk density. In other words, compared to other geometric shapes, the spherical geometry would hinder growth of porosity within the structure19,20.

Phase study of W/WC FGM powder

Figure 4 demonstrates the X-ray diffraction results of ball-milled powders according to Table 2 prior to sintering. The composition of 92.5 W-5.25Ni-2.25Fe and 97 W-2.1Ni-0.9Fe is in line with the cubic structure of the gamma phase with the standard card 96–900-6488 and 96–901-3942. Indeed, during ball milling, the Ni (1 1 1) peak shifted to a higher angle and then to a lower angle again, reflecting the formation of a nickel-rich solid solution in which Fe and W were dissolved21. Density and mechanical properties of the W/WC FGM bulk

In this study, the sintering samples were sintered at a constant temperature and heating rate, while the two parameters of pressure and time were varied. Due to the bonding of the layers, it was not possible to examine the density for each layer, with the density report belonging to the entire W/WC FGM bulk, which was obtained by the Archimedes method. The density results of the sintering samples are outlined in Table 5. According to the results, the density rose with increasing stress (pressure). According to many studies, it has been determined that the reduction in density in the spark plasma sintering process at high pressures can occur due to several factors, including evaporation of liquid phases, grain growth, and formation of secondary harmful phases26. However, in this study, as the XRD results show, no secondary phase was formed. In addition, no sintering aid was used. Thus, elevation of pressure from 20 MPa to 60 MPa resulted in the elimination of porosity, grain size distribution, and agglomerated particles along sintering, causing growth of density from 82% to 92.5%. The effect of time did not behave similarly to pressure. With prolongation of the sintering time, the density initially increased and then decreased. In spark plasma sintering (SPS), the main reason for the decline in density at high sintering times is excessive grain growth and the potential for grain boundary slippage, which can result in development of porosity and hence lower density26,27.

Density and hardness are directly related to each other. With elevation of sintering pressure, the hardness of the sintering samples also increased due to the elimination of porosity and the rise in density. With augmentation of sintering pressure, the hardness of the areas near the junction of the W layer and the 92.5 W-5.25Ni-2.25Fe layer grew from 250 Vickers to 389 Vickers. These results are also true for other layers. With increases in sintering pressure, the hardness of the WC-Co layers rose from 1129 to 1426 Vickers and the hardness of the 92.5 W-5.25Ni-2.25Fe layer was enhanced from 315 to 445 Vickers, respectively. The highest density of the sintered samples was 92.5%. Given that no porosity is observed in the fracture section, it is likely that 6.5% of the porosity has been distributed intragranularly in the structure. This is probably due to the high sintering pressure, which leaves fine pores within the structure under hydrostatic capillary pressure33. Figures 8 and 9 also displys tensile and flexural stress-strain curves of the junction of the W layer and the 92.5 W-5.25Ni-2.25Fe layer of sintered tungsten/tungsten carbide FGM samples under different sintering conditions. The results of the flexural and tensile curves show that no necking occurs in any of the samples and the failure is completely brittle. The cause of brittle fracture is the nature of tungsten carbide (WC), which has a very low plastic deformability due to strong covalent bonds28. In fact, it is very difficult for dislocations to move in it, which dose not leads to plastic deformation28. The lack of a ductile region or “neck” in the curve is precisely due to the material’s inability to undergo creep plastic deformation (dislocation of dislocations) before failure. At the end of the linear elastic region, when the stress exceeds the strength of the interatomic and grain bonds, a critical crack forms and propagates rapidly. This rapid crack propagation occurs without prior warning (plastic deformation) and causes the curve to drop abruptly.

Inspection of the effect of time on the mechanical properties of W/WC FGM bulk

The study of the effect of time on the mechanical properties of FGM W/WC is described in this section. To explore the effect of sintering time on the mechanical properties of W/WC FGM bulk, three analyses were performed: bending, tensile, and hardness. Figures 10 and 11 reveal the results of the bending and tensile fracture cross-section images of the sintered samples at a temperature of 1400 °C, a pressure of 20 MPa, and a constant heating rate of 200 °C/min, respectively, as well as times of 15, 20, and 25 min. Tungsten, with a flexural strength of 1000–1200 MPa, has the lowest flexural strength among the other layers. Thus, it is expected that the fracture will take place from the tungsten layer or the junction between the layers. Microstructural studies indicated that in all samples sintered at different times, failure occurred at the joint of the W layer and the 92.5 W-5.25Ni-2.25Fe layer. Table 6 reports the results of the effect of sintering time and pressure on the mechanical properties of the W/WC FGM bulk. The flexural strength of the sintered samples was 368 MPa, 267 MPa, and 118 MPa at 15, 20, and 25 min, respectively. These results indicate that all layers had a diffusion bond, but the diffusion bond strength of the linkage between the W layer and the 92.5 W-5.25Ni-2.25Fe layer was lower than that of the other layers. With prolongation of the sintering time, the flexural strength of the interface between the W layer and the 92.5 W-5.25Ni-2.25Fe layer has diminished. This is due to grain growth. There are two important thermodynamic and kinetic factors for grain growth: increasing time and temperature of 1400 °C would facilitate the conditions for grain growth. With grain growth, mechanical properties also decline due to the reduction of the grain boundary and the opening of the dislocation movement path. Churn and his colleagues28 have shown that four mechanisms including tungsten grain cleavage, tungsten intergranular cleavage, matrix grain cleavage, and matrix cleavage in a flexible manner, may occur for the fracture sections of tungsten grains. All four of these can be observed in the fracture section of sintered tungsten samples, but the conditions for their occurrence depend on the components of the sintering process. According to the results obtained and those of Gong et al.29,30, the fracture cross-section of all samples took place at the tungsten grain boundaries in the area where the weakest coherent strength was present. Gong et al.29,30 found that if the fracture cross-section was caused by cracks in the matrix, it means that the linkage between the two layers was well established. Kunčická and his colleagues31 also observed that with increasing sintering time, tungsten grains began to agglomerate, and if tungsten has been alloyed with other elements such as cobalt and nickel, tungsten agglomerates are surrounded by the matrix of elements31. There are very large pores in all samples, indicating that the samples are not completely densified. However, with prolongation of the sintering time from 15 to 20 min, it seems that the rate of particle fusion has increased. As depicted by the arrow, the fusion of particles results in the fracture cross-section turning into up and down unevenness when bent.

Tensile fracture cross-section images of sintered samples at a temperature of 1400 °C, a pressure of 20 MPa, and a heating rate of 200 °C/min for (a) 15, (b) 20 and (c) 25 min, respectively.

In all samples sintered at different times, the tensile fracture cross-section occurred from the junction of the W layer and the 92.5 W-5.25Ni-2.25Fe layer. The tensile strength of the sintered samples was obtained at times 15, 20, and 25 min, as 254 MPa, 219 MPa, and 88 MPa respectively. Figure 11 displays the scanning electron microscopic images of the tensile cross-section of the sintered samples at different times. According to the obtained results, no noticeable changes were made in the microstructure of the tensile cross-section with prolongation of sintering time. Also, no necking was formed in any of the samples. In a study conducted by Rabin et al.32, if the tungsten content is more than 95%, tensile necking will not occur. However, if the tungsten content is less than 95%, necking will occur and a smooth fracture cross-section will be formed. The results reveal that with increasing time, the cohesion of particles has diminished and the porosity among particles has increased. In a study, Zahabi and colleagues33 explored the effect of sintering time for joining two heterogeneous compounds (Ti-6Al-4 V/C.P.T) using the Gauss error function. Their results indicated that if the diffusion conditions are the same, time cannot play a major role in binding the two heterogeneous compounds. With increasing time, the atomic diffusion distance grows, but due to grain growth, the mechanical properties decline.

The hardness findings confirm the density results. With prolongation of sintering time, the hardness of the areas close to the junction dropped from 250 Vickers to 189 Vickers. Strunz et al.34 reported that with increasing sintering time, the average size of tungsten agglomerates grows. This leads to diminished hardness. These results are also true for other layers. Accordingly, with increasing sintering time, the hardness of WC-Co layers fell from 1129 Vickers to 1002 and the hardness of 92.5 W-5.25Ni-2.25Fe layer declined from 315 Vickers to 209.

Examination of the effect of pressure on the mechanical properties of W/WC FGM bulk

Figure 12 presents the scanning electron microscopic images of the bending section of the sintered samples at different pressures. The results indicate that the bending strength has grown with increasing pressure. In these samples, failure has also occurred from the tungsten region, with the difference that it shows greater bending strength than the sintered samples at different times. The bending strength of the sintered samples was obtained at pressures of 20, 40, 60; as well as 368 MPa, 698 MPa, and 890 MPa, respectively. Upon elevation of the pressure, the demonstrated porosity (locations indicated by arrows) has been eliminated. In other words, by augmenting the pressure, the rate of particle fusion has increased, and at a pressure of 60 MPa, all porosity has disappeared. These results also indicate that the flexural bond strength of the other layers is greater than 890 MPa. The reason for the acceptable diffusion bonding of the WC-Co layer at different cobalt percentages of 5 and 10 wt% is the presence of a viscous liquid phase. The solubility of cobalt in tungsten carbide is not a simple quantity, but a range dependent on temperature and carbon content. At sintering temperatures (about 1400–1600 °C), cobalt partially dissolves in tungsten carbide and forms a viscous liquid phase which holds the tungsten carbide grains together. The solubility of tungsten in the viscous liquid phase of cobalt also varies with carbon content, such that a higher carbon content generally leads to lower tungsten solubility35. Figure 13 exhibits the scanning electron microscopic images of the tensile cross-section of the sintered samples at different pressures. Since the fracture occurred at the junction of the W layer and the 92.5 W-5.25Ni-2.25Fe layer, no necking was formed in any of the samples. According to the images, macroscopically, the fracture surface started in the tensile direction and extended in a river pattern through the tungsten grains. According to the some researchs35–37, if the structure is continuously sintered and there is no porosity within the structure, during tension, primary cracks occur preferentially at the tungsten-tungsten grain boundaries that are approximately perpendicular to the tensile axis. These cracks are generated by plastic strain, and as the percentage of tungsten drops, higher plastic strain is required to generate the crack. The cross-section examination of the sintered samples in this study revealed that there were no cracks in the grains and grain boundaries, which is also in accordance with the observations of Votava research36.

Microstructural study of the optimal W/WC FGM bulk

Figure 14 displays the FESEM images of the bonding surface of different layers in the optimized sample. In the SPS method, elevation of the pressure from 20 to 60 MPa leads to an increase in the number of sparks and promotes the diffusion throughout the junction of the layers. In this formula, represents the chemical potential, denotes the reference and standard chemical potential, shows the normal stress in the junction, and indicates the atomic volume of the diffusion component38. The negative sign of the normal stress in the formula reveals compressive stress. According to the formula, with increasing pressure (normal stress), the chemical potential diminishes. Since the direction of diffusion is always from areas with higher chemical potential to those with lower chemical potential, elevation of pressure in SPS makes the junction a suitable place for diffusion between two layers. Yielding and plastic deformation are other events that occurred in the optimal sample. With growing pressure, the remaining porosity in the junction decreased with bonds being established in the junction of the layers. The results indicate that in none of the layers was a diffusion zone consisting of two layers formed, and only a continuous boundary has been formed. These results are consistent with the data of Tokita et al.40. Their WC/Co microstructure results reveal that even with increasing cobalt weight%, the diffusion zone is not formed, but the boundary between the two layers is clearly visible. It was also found that in the optimized sample, there is no porosity and delamination between each layer. The initial condition for the formation of a diffusion zone between the two layers during sintering is the presence of a chemical potential gradient. This gradient directs the movement of atoms from regions with higher chemical potential to those with lower chemical potential. Time and temperature are critical variables that affect the rate of diffusion and, hence, the formation of the diffusion zone. Now, if in solid-state sintering, conditions are provided for the formation of a liquid phase, the rate and probability of the formation of a diffusion zone are greater owing to the higher rate of liquid phase diffusion than solid phase diffusion41–43.

Conclusion

In the present study, W/WC FGM bulk was produced via spark plasma sintering method under different conditions. The effect of kinetic components (pressure and time) of SPS method was inspected on its mechanical properties. Temperature had a negative effect on the mechanical properties of W/WC FGM bulk owing to facilitating grain growth. On the other hand, increasing pressure promoted the diffusion throughout the common junction of the layers due to increasing the number of sparks. The most important results obtained were as follows:

1) Based on time and pressure studies at constant temperature, it was determined that the best sintering conditions for fabricating a W/WC FGM bulk with the best mechanical properties were sintering the sample for 15 min, pressure of 60 MPa, temperature of 1400 °C and heating rate of 200 °C/min.

2) Phase studies revealed that no impurities or harmful phases were formed during powder ball milling and sintering.

3) In all samples, failure occurred from the interface of the W layer and the 92.5 W-5.25Ni-2.25Fe layer. The reason for the acceptable penetration bonding of the WC-Co layers at different percentages of 5 and 10 wt% cobalt was the presence of a viscous liquid phase.

4) The highest tensile and flexural strengths were obtained for the optimized sample, 745 and 890 MPa, respectively. The microstructure of the bent section samples indicated that only two samples sintered at pressures of 40 and 60 MPa had grain boundaries and homogeneous bonding.

5) In none of the samples was a throated tensile section formed. Macroscopically, the fracture surface started in the tensile direction and spread in a river pattern in the tungsten grains.

6) The density of the sintered samples was measured by the Archimedes method, where the highest density was 92.5%. Since no porosity was observed in the fracture section, it is likely that 6.5% of the porosity was distributed intra-granularly in the structure.

7) Microstructural examination of the surface of the bonded layers indicated that no diffusion zone was formed between the layers and only a continuous boundary was formed.
