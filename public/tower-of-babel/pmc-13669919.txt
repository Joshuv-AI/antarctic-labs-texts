Background

Coronary stent infection is an uncommon but clinically serious complication of percutaneous interventions, reported in the literature at approximately 0.3% to 6.0%, although these estimates are derived largely from heterogeneous case-based reports and should be cautiously interpreted.1 Contemporary evidence remains limited to pooled reviews rather than prospective data sets, yet published reports consistently describe a destructive course in which local infection progresses to arterial wall injury and pseudoaneurysm formation. In a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of reported cases, aneurysmal degeneration was present in 62% of patients, underscoring the severity of this process once infection is established.2,3 Fistulous extension after percutaneous coronary intervention is reported far less often, and presentation with hemoptysis is particularly notable because it suggests progression beyond a localized coronary complication to communication with adjacent thoracic structures, with immediate implications for both sepsis and hemorrhagic risk.1 We report a case of post percutaneous intervention stent placement infection complicated by pseudoaneurysm formation, micro-fistulization, and hemoptysis that required surgical management, highlighting an unusual but clinically important presentation in which early recognition and definitive intervention were central to care.

History of Presentation

A 72-year-old man with longstanding coronary artery disease (CAD) had undergone coronary artery bypass grafting 31 years earlier, followed by multiple percutaneous interventions in recent years. Four years before this visit (August 2022), a pseudoaneurysm involving a previously stented saphenous vein graft (SVG) to the first diagonal branch was treated with repeat stenting. Hemoptysis was first documented at this time. Three years following said percutaneous intervention (May 2025), a second pseudoaneurysm developed in the SVG to the first obtuse marginal (OM) branch and was also treated with covered stenting. Reevaluation at 6-month follow-up (November 2025) showed a patent OM graft stent with recurrent pseudoaneurysm at its distal segment (Figure 1). The clinical course was notable for ongoing hemoptysis from the initial presentation in 2022 and persistence without resolution despite clopidogrel therapy being suspended.

Past Medical History

The patient had longstanding CAD with a notable cardiac history of ischemic cardiomyopathy secondary to previous myocardial infarction, atrial flutter, and right bundle branch block, with an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator in place.

Differential Diagnosis

Considerations included aorto-bronchial fistula, pulmonary malignancy, or infectious pulmonary processes presenting with hemoptysis.

Investigations

Preoperative assessment showed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 35%, NYHA functional class II symptoms, class III angina, and no significant valvular disease. Coronary angiography demonstrated severe left main and proximal right CAD in the setting of previous bypass surgery. The left internal mammary artery graft to the left anterior descending artery was patent, the SVG to the first diagonal branch was occluded, the SVG to the OM branch contained a patent stent with recurrent pseudoaneurysm at the distal covered segment, and the SVG to the distal right coronary artery was patent (Figures 1 and 2, Video 1). Given the recurrent pseudoaneurysmal process, persistent hemoptysis, and coronary anatomy, the patient was referred for redo coronary revascularization.

Management/Intervention

After multidisciplinary review and optimization, the patient underwent redo median sternotomy. The ascending aorta was free of significant disease, and the right greater saphenous vein was harvested endoscopically. Aortic arterial and triple-stage venous cannulation were established, and cardiopulmonary bypass was initiated. Intraoperative inspection identified a vascular fistula at the distal OM-circumflex territory extending into the left lung parenchyma (Figure 3). This was an intraoperative finding, as no previous imaging or operative records had documented this abnormality. The stented SVGs and associated pseudoaneurysm were excised. Dense adhesions increased operative complexity, and intraoperative findings were consistent with graft infection (Figure 4). The vascular fistula to the lung was directly repaired. A single SVG was constructed to the second OM artery, measuring approximately 2.5 mm, with probe-confirmed patency, and 1 proximal aortic anastomosis was created during a single clamp period. The pericardium was left open, and no additional preventive strategies were undertaken. Mediastinal irrigation with a 0.08% gentamicin solution was performed because of initial concern for infection. The patient was weaned off from bypass on inotropic support, with post-bypass echocardiography displaying improved left ventricular ejection fraction to 44%. Cross clamp time was 101 minutes, and total bypass time was 160 minutes.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Estimated blood loss was minimal, no intraoperative complications occurred. The excised stented SVG was sent for pathological evaluation, which demonstrated degenerative changes with calcification and early thrombus formation. The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit in stable condition, completed an expected postoperative course, and was discharged home on postoperative day 7. Follow-up in clinic 3 months after the operation was normal, with no recurrence of hemoptysis.

Discussion

This case underscores a practical surgical principle in the management of recurrent graft-related coronary pseudoaneurysm after repeated percutaneous intervention. Once the process has progressed to recurrent aneurysmal degeneration, persistent hemoptysis, and fistulous extension, the therapeutic goal extends beyond exclusion of the lesion. In that setting, the central problem is not only coronary revascularization, but also removal of the diseased conduit and repair of associated structural injury. Published experience with coronary stent infection remains limited and largely case based, yet available pooled analyses describe a pattern of arterial destruction and pseudoaneurysm formation that supports this operative framework.1, 2, 3

The present case is notable for serial recurrence despite previous covered stent treatment in 2 separate SVG territories. All previous percutaneous interventions were performed in standard fashion without reported procedural complications or unexpected findings. No single unifying technical cause was identified to explain the development of pseudoaneurysms in 2 separate SVGs. The most plausible contributing factor is extensive underlying CAD predisposing to progressive graft degeneration. The occurrence of pseudoaneurysms in multiple graft territories remains rare and not fully understood, underscoring the importance of continued clinical and imaging surveillance in patients with previously stented SVGs.

That sequence suggests that endovascular treatment may provide temporary control of the angiographic lesion while leaving the underlying pathologic substrate unresolved in selected patients. Persistent hemoptysis added important clinical context. In this setting, hemoptysis should not be viewed as an isolated pulmonary symptom, but as a warning sign of extension beyond the graft itself. The intraoperative finding of micro-fistulization into the bronchi confirmed that the disease process had progressed to involve adjacent thoracic structures, thereby increasing both hemorrhagic and infectious risk.1

Surgery was instrumental in addressing the full extent of this complication. Redo revascularization restored inflow to the OM territory, whereas excision of the stented graft and pseudoaneurysmal segment achieved source control that repeat percutaneous intervention could not provide. Operative exposure also allowed direct treatment of the fistulous communication. These findings support a chronic mechanical interaction between a degenerating graft and the adjacent left lung parenchyma as the likely mechanism for micro-fistulization. The micro-fistulization was an intraoperative finding and not the primary indication for redo revascularization, which was driven by recurrent pseudoaneurysm formation and persistent graft pathology. SVG remains an acceptable conduit in the redo setting, and selection in this case was guided by intraoperative assessment and patient-specific factors. For the operating surgeon, this distinction is important, as the operative objective is not limited to aneurysm exclusion, but a conjunct management of infected or structurally compromised graft tissue, associated pseudoaneurysm, and any extension into surrounding mediastinal or pulmonary structures.

Conclusions

The implications of a single case report should remain restrained. This experience does not define a universal treatment algorithm or establish superiority of surgery over catheter-based strategies; however, it does support timely surgical consideration when recurrent SVG pseudoaneurysm follows repeated percutaneous interventions, particularly when accompanied by persistent hemoptysis or evidence of thoracic extension. In carefully selected patients, surgery may offer the most definitive combination of revascularization, source control, and structural repair after failed repeat endovascular management.
