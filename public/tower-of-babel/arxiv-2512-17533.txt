The stable trees revisited
Christina Goldschmidt, Liam Hill

Abstract
We introduce a new, relatively simple, line-breaking construction of the α \alpha -stable tree which realises its random finite-dimensional distributions. This is a direct analogue of Aldous’ line-breaking construction of the Brownian continuum random tree, which is based on an inhomogeneous Poisson process. Here, we replace the deterministic rate function from the Brownian setting by a random rate process, given by a certain measure-changed ( α − 1 ) (\alpha-1) -stable subordinator. Rather than attaching uniformly, the line-segments now connect to locations chosen with probability proportional to the sizes of the jumps of the rate process. We also give a new proof of an invariance principle originally due to Duquesne, which states that the family tree of a Bienaymé branching process with critical offspring distribution in the domain of attraction of an α \alpha -stable law (for OPEN α ∈ ( 1 , 2 ) ) \alpha\in(1,2)) , conditioned to have n n vertices, converges on rescaling distances appropriately to the α \alpha -stable tree. Our proof makes use of a discrete line-breaking construction of the branching process tree, which we show converges to our continuous line-breaking construction.



1
Introduction

1.1
The
\alpha
-stable trees

The
\alpha
-stable trees are a family of random
\mathbb{R}
-trees which constitute the scaling limits of the size-conditioned family trees of Bienaymé branching processes whose offspring distribution is critical and lies in the domain of attraction of an
\alpha
-stable law, for
\alpha\in(1,2]
. The
2
-stable tree is a (constant multiple of) the Brownian continuum random tree (see Aldous
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
); the rest of the family was introduced by Duquesne and Le Gall
[
22
,
23
]
, building on earlier work of Le Gall and Le Jan
[
34
]
. The focus of this paper is the case
\alpha\in(1,2)
.

Fix
\alpha\in(1,2)
and let
L=(L_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a spectrally positive
\alpha
-stable Lévy process, whose distribution is specified by its Laplace transform

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(-\lambda L_{t})\right]=\exp(-t\lambda^{\alpha}),\quad\lambda\geq 0,t\geq 0.

The
\alpha
-stable tree

(\mathcal{T}_{\alpha},d_{\alpha},\mu_{\alpha})
is a random variable taking values in the space of isometry classes of compact metric measure spaces endowed with the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov topology. It is usually defined in a somewhat involved fashion using a normalised excursion
\mathbbm{e}=(\mathbbm{e}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}
of
L
. Indeed one may derive a continuous process called the
height process
via

h_{t}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbbm{1}\left(\mathbbm{e}_{s}<\inf_{r\in[s,t]}\mathbbm{e}_{r}+\epsilon\right)ds,\quad 0\leq t\leq 1,

where the limit exists in probability. The height process then acts as a contour function for an
\mathbb{R}
-tree. More precisely, we define a pseudo-metric
d^{\circ}_{\alpha}
on
[0,1]
via

d^{\circ}_{\alpha}(x,y)=h_{x}+h_{y}-2\inf_{x\wedge y\leq t\leq x\vee y}h_{t}

and an equivalence relation by declaring
x\sim y
if
d^{\circ}_{\alpha}(x,y)=0
. Finally, take
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
to be the quotient space
[0,1]/{\sim}
, which is endowed with the metric
d_{\alpha}
induced on
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
by
d_{\alpha}^{\circ}
. Take
\mu_{\alpha}
to be the push-forward of the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
onto
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
. (See
[
35
,
22
,
24
]
for more details.)

Write
\xi
for a random variable taking values in
\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Suppose that
\mathbb{E}\left[\xi\right]=1
and that

\mathbb{P}\left(\xi>k\right)\sim k^{-\alpha}l(k),\quad\text{as $k\to\infty$,}

where
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a slowly varying function. Then
\xi
is in the domain of attraction of the
\alpha
-stable law represented by the random variable
L_{1}
. Let
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots
be i.i.d. copies of
\xi
. Then there exists a non-negative increasing sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
which is regularly varying of index
1/\alpha
such that

\left(\frac{1}{a_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nt\rfloor}(\xi_{i}-1),t\geq 0\right)\overset{d}{\to}c(L_{t},t\geq 0)

in the Skorokhod sense for some
c>0
. By replacing
a_{n}
with
ca_{n}
for all
n
, we may assume without loss of generality that
c=1
, and we will work with this specific case throughout. Note that the Lévy measure of the Lévy process
L
is given by
C_{\alpha}x^{-\alpha-1}\,dx,x>0
, where
C_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}=\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}
.

Let
T^{n}
be the rooted ordered tree which represents the genealogy of a branching process with offspring distributed as
\xi
, conditioned to have size
n
. We think of this as a metric space by endowing its vertices
V(T^{n})
with the graph distance,
d^{n}
, and additionally we let
\mu^{n}
be the uniform probability measure on its vertices. Theorem 3.1 of Duquesne
[
24
]
proves the convergence of the height and contour processes of
T^{n}
to their continuous analogues (see also Kortchemski
[
33
]
for a streamlined proof). The following result is a corollary.

Theorem 1.1
.

We have

\left(V(T^{n}),\left(\frac{n}{a_{n}}\right)^{-1}d^{n},\mu^{n}\right)\overset{d}{\to}(\mathcal{T}_{\alpha},d_{\alpha},\mu_{\alpha})

as
n\to\infty
, in the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov sense.

Haas and Miermont
[
28
]
provide an alternative approach to the proof of Theorem
1.1
in the case where
\mathbb{P}\left(\xi=k\right)\sim ck^{-\alpha-1}
for some constant
c>0
(see their Theorem 8).

Our aims in this paper are twofold. Firstly, we introduce a novel representation of the
\alpha
-stable tree using a line-breaking construction. Secondly, we use the representation in order to provide a new proof of
Theorem 1.1
, which does not make use of the height process.

1.2
Line-breaking construction

We begin by introducing what we mean by a line-breaking construction. Suppose that
(y_{k})_{k\geq 1}
and
(z_{k})_{k\geq 1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{+}
are sequences such that

1.

(y_{k})
is strictly increasing with
y_{k}\uparrow\infty
;

2.

z_{k}<y_{k}
for all
k
.

We call
(y_{k})
the
cut points
and
(z_{k})
the
attachment points
. Informally, we construct a tree by cutting the half-line
\mathbb{R}^{+}
into segments
(y_{k},y_{k+1}]
and inductively gluing the open end of
(y_{k},y_{k+1}]
at position
z_{k}
. Formally, we define a consistent sequence of metrics on (subsets of)
\mathbb{R}^{+}
: the metric
d_{k}^{\circ}
is defined inductively on
[0,y_{k}]
(with
d_{0}^{\circ}
the trivial metric on
\{0\}
) by

d_{k}^{\circ}(u,v)=\begin{cases}d^{\circ}_{k-1}(u,v)&u,v\leq y_{k-1}\\
d^{\circ}_{k-1}(u,z_{k-1})+|v-y_{k-1}|&u\leq y_{k-1}<v\\
|v-u|&u,v>y_{k-1}\end{cases}

for
u<v\leq y_{k}
. Write
(\mathcal{T}(k),d_{k})
for the space
([0,y_{k}],d_{k}^{\circ})
. There is a unique metric
d^{\circ}
on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
that agrees with
d^{\circ}_{k}
for all
k\geq 1
; let
(\mathcal{T},d)
be the completion of the space
(\mathbb{R}^{+},d^{\circ})
.

By considering the subspaces
[0,t]\subset\mathcal{T}
as
t
varies over
\mathbb{R}^{+}
, we can think of this metric space as growing in time: we continuously grow one branch at a time, and at certain discrete time points
(y_{k})
we switch over to a new branch started from an existing point
(z_{k})
.

Now consider a random increasing càdlàg process
\tau=(\tau_{t})_{t\geq 0}
such that
\tau_{t}<\infty
almost surely for each
t\geq 0
and
\tau_{t}\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
.
We will construct cut points and attachment points using
\tau
. To begin, conditionally given
\tau
, let
(Y_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be the jump times of an inhomogeneous Poisson process on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
with intensity
\tau_{t}\,dt
, arranged in increasing order; these will be the cut points for our tree. To construct the attachment points, we first sample i.i.d. uniform random variables
U_{1},U_{2},\dots
on
[0,1]
, independent of everything else so far. Now we may set

Z_{k}=\inf\{t>0:\tau_{t}>U_{i}\tau_{C_{k}-}\}.

(This picks out the time of a jump of
\tau
in
(0,Y_{k})
chosen with probability proportional to its size. Note that, almost surely,
Y_{k}
is not a jump-time of the process
\tau
, and so
\tau_{Y_{k}}=\tau_{Y_{k}-}
a.s.) If the cut and attachment points are generated in this way, we say that
(\mathcal{T},d)
is
generated by a line-breaking construction with intensity process
\tau
.
The case
\tau_{t}=t
, where
Z_{k}
is uniformly distributed on
[0,Y_{k}]
, is precisely Aldous’ line-breaking construction of the Brownian CRT
[
9
]
.

We now describe the intensity process
\tau
that we we will use. First, let
(\sigma_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be an increasing Lévy process with
\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(-\lambda\sigma_{t})\right]=\exp(-t\alpha\lambda^{\alpha-1})
. Note that this is (a multiple of) an
(\alpha-1)
-stable subordinator. Let
p:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be the probability density function of the random variable
L_{1}
. Then
2.2
below states that the process
(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
defined by

M_{t}=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}ds\right)\frac{p(-\sigma_{t})}{p(0)},\quad t\geq 0,

is a mean-1 martingale in the natural filtration of the subordinator. We use it as a Radon–Nikodym derivative in order to define a new process
(\widetilde{\sigma}_{t})_{0\leq s\leq t}
via change of measure: for any bounded measurable test-function
F:\mathbb{D}([0,t],\mathbb{R}^{+})\to\mathbb{R}
, let

\mathbb{E}\left[F((\widetilde{\sigma}_{s})_{s\leq t})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[M_{t}F((\sigma_{s})_{s\leq t})\right].

(1)

By a standard application of Kolmogorov’s extension theorem, we deduce the existence of a process
\widetilde{\sigma}=(\widetilde{\sigma}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. Moreover,
\widetilde{\sigma}
is an increasing time-homogeneous Markov process (see
2.3
below).

Now consider the
\mathbb{R}
-tree
(\mathcal{T},d)
arising from the line-breaking construction with intensity process
\widetilde{\sigma}
. In
Section 2
, we give an independent proof of the compactness of
(\mathcal{T},d)
. We will also want to endow
(\mathcal{T},d)
with its “uniform” measure. For each
k\geq 1
, define a measure
\mu_{k}:=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\delta_{Y_{i}}
on
(\mathcal{T},d)
. In
Theorem 2.15
, we show that
\mu_{k}
converges as
k\to\infty
to a limiting measure
\mu
on
(\mathcal{T},d)
.

In
Section 3
, we give a description of a discrete line-breaking-type construction of a conditioned Bienaymé tree
T^{n}
. In
Section 4
, we then prove a version of
Theorem 1.1
with
(\mathcal{T},d,\mu)
as the limiting space. We may thus identify this limit as the
\alpha
-stable tree:

Theorem 1.2
.

We have

(\mathcal{T},d,\mu)\overset{d}{=}(\mathcal{T}_{\alpha},d_{\alpha},\mu_{\alpha}).

Along the way, we show that
(\mathcal{T}(k))_{k\geq 1}
realises the
random finite-dimensional distributions
of the
\alpha
-stable tree. That is, for each
k\geq 1
,
\mathcal{T}(k)
has the same distribution as the subtree of
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
spanned by the root and
k
vertices chosen independently according to the measure
\mu_{\alpha}
. (As shown by Aldous
[
11
]
, the random finite-dimensional distributions characterise the law of any continuum random tree.)

As we show in
Section 2
, an analogue of the change of measure (
1
) works for a much broader class of Lévy process/subordinator pairs. In view of this, we conjecture that our construction should generalise to other Lévy trees, at least under certain regularity conditions.

1.3
Discussion

Line-breaking constructions of random
\mathbb{R}
-trees date right back to Aldous’ first paper on the Brownian continuum random tree
[
9
]
. That construction was generalised by Aldous and Pitman
[
8
]
to give the family of
inhomogeneous continuum random trees
(ICRTs), which were further studied in
[
19
,
7
,
6
,
18
,
17
]
. This family is parametrised by a sequence
\theta=(\theta_{i})_{i\geq 0}
such that
\theta_{1}\geq\theta_{2}\geq\dots\geq 0
,
\sum_{i\geq 0}\theta_{i}^{2}=1
and either
\theta_{0}\neq 0
or
\sum_{i=1}\theta_{i}=\infty
. For
i\geq 1
, let
E_{i}\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\theta_{i})
and then set

\tau_{t}=\theta_{0}^{2}t+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\theta_{i}\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq t\right),\quad t\geq 0.

Then use
(\tau_{t})_{t\geq 0}
as the intensity process in a line-breaking construction. (This succinct formulation, which is due to Blanc-Renaudie
[
18
]
, is equivalent to the original formulation of
[
8
]
.) The ICRTs were shown by Camarri and Pitman
[
19
]
to be the scaling limits of a family of discrete models called
p
-trees. We will find it helpful in the sequel to relax the condition
\sum_{i\geq 0}\theta_{i}^{2}=1
to the condition
\|\theta\|_{2}<\infty
, the effect being simply a scaling of all edge-lengths in the tree by
\|\theta\|_{2}
. Let us write
(\mathcal{T}^{\theta},d^{\theta},\mu^{\theta})
for the ICRT with parameter
\theta
. Blanc-Renaudie
[
18
]
proves that
(\mathcal{T}^{\theta},d^{\theta})
is a.s. compact if and only if

\int_{R}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t\mathbb{E}\left[\tau_{t}\right]}\,dt<\infty\text{ for some }R>0.

Other line-breaking constructions with general lengths and uniform attachment mechanism have been studied in
[
13
,
20
,
30
]
.

A first, more complicated, line-breaking construction for the
\alpha
-stable trees was given by the first author with Bénédicte Haas in
[
27
]
in 2015. This construction is not based on an inhomogeneous Poisson point process, but rather on an increasing Markov chain,
(M_{k})_{k\geq 1}
. In order to describe it, we need first to introduce the two-parameter family of
generalised Mittag-Leffler distributions
,
\mathrm{ML}(\theta,\beta)
, for
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\theta>-\beta
. If
X\sim\mathrm{ML}(\theta,\beta)
then
X
takes values in
\mathbb{R}^{+}
and has distribution determined by its moments,

\mathbb{E}\left[X^{n}\right]=\frac{\Gamma(\theta)\Gamma(\theta/\beta+n)}{\Gamma(\theta/\beta)\Gamma(\theta+n\beta)},\quad n\geq 1.

The Markov chain
(M_{k})_{k\geq 1}
has marginal distributions
M_{k}\sim\mathrm{ML}(1-1/\alpha,k-1/\alpha)
and is most easily described via its
backward
transitions, which are such that, for
k\geq 1
,
M_{k}=M_{k+1}\cdot\beta_{k}
, where
\beta_{k}
is independent of
M_{k+1}
and

\beta_{k}\sim\mathrm{Beta}\left(\frac{(k+1)\alpha-2}{\alpha-1},\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\right).

These two properties turn out to completely characterise its distribution.

Now let
B_{2},B_{3},\ldots
be i.i.d.
\mathrm{Beta}(1,(2-\alpha)/(\alpha-1))
random variables, independent of
(M_{k})_{k\geq 1}
. The line-breaking construction generates an increasing sequence of trees
(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}(k))_{k\geq 1}
as we now describe.

Initialise by setting
L_{1}=M_{1}
, and letting
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}(1)
be the tree consisting of a line-segment of length
L_{1}
(rooted at one end).

Then, for
k\geq 1
, given
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}(k)
, at step
k+1
, we attach a line-segment of length
(M_{k+1}-M_{k})\cdot B_{k+1}
onto
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}(k)
at a point to be determined as follows:

•

We choose to attach at a pre-existing branch-point of
\widetilde{T}(k)
with probability
(M_{k}-L_{k})/M_{k}
. Conditionally on being in this case, we pick such a branch-point of degree
d\geq 3
with probability proportional to
d-1-\alpha
.

•

Otherwise, with probability
L_{k}/M_{k}
, we attach at a point chosen according to the normalised Lebesgue measure on
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}(k)
.

(A second version of the construction was also given in
[
27
]
but since it does not apparently reveal any extra information about the stable tree, we do not describe it here. See also Rembart and Winkel
[
38
]
for an alternative perspective.)

In
[
27
]
, it is proved that
(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}(k))_{k\geq 1}
realises the random finite-dimensional distributions of the stable tree. Therefore, the construction makes manifest the distributions of various distances in the tree (see Proposition 1.6 of
[
27
]
for more details). We are able to recover these facts using our new construction, although it requires some work, for example:

Proposition 1.3
.

The first cut time
Y_{1}
of the line-breaking construction with intensity
\widetilde{\sigma}
is such that
\alpha Y_{1}\sim\mathrm{ML}\left(1-1/\alpha,1-1/\alpha\right)
.

The proof is long but fairly straightforward, so we defer it to the appendix.

The use of line-breaking constructions has recently acquired new impetus, beginning with work of Addario-Berry, Blanc-Renaudie, Donderwinkel, Maazoun and Martin
[
1
]
, who gave a survey of analogous
discrete
constructions for uniform random trees with a given degree sequence. We describe a particular instance of this in detail in
Section 3
below, and with randomised degrees it will play a key role in our proof of
Theorem 1.1
. (It has also recently been used to study various aspects of random trees with given degrees in
[
16
,
4
,
2
,
3
]
.)

It is possible to consider mixtures of ICRTs by randomising the parameter
\theta
: that is, we first sample
\theta
according to some distribution and then, conditionally given
\theta
, we sample an ICRT with parameter
\theta
. (In this context, we do not wish to constrain
\|\theta\|_{2}
to take the value 1.) Aldous, Pitman and Miermont
[
6
]
conjectured that the Lévy trees of Duquesne and Le Gall
[
22
]
(which include the stable trees) are mixtures of ICRTs. This statement has been proved in the case of the stable trees by Wang
[
41
]
. (We understand that a complete proof in the general case may appear in a future version of the preprint
[
16
]
.)

Theorem 1.4

(Wang
[
41
]
)
.

Fix
\alpha\in(1,2)
and let
(\Theta_{i},i\geq 1)
be the ordered sequence of jumps of the normalised
\alpha
-stable excursion
\mathbbm{e}
. Then

(\mathcal{T}_{\alpha},d_{\alpha},\mu_{\alpha})\overset{d}{=}(\mathcal{T}^{\Theta},d^{\Theta},\mu^{\Theta}).

As we show below in
5.4
, if
(\Delta_{i},i\geq 1)
is the ordered sequence of jumps of
\widetilde{\sigma}
then

(\Delta_{i},i\geq 1)\overset{d}{=}(\Theta_{i},i\geq 1)

and, moreover,

(\widetilde{\sigma}_{t})_{t\geq 0}\overset{d}{=}\left(\sum_{i\geq 1}\Theta_{i}\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq t\right)\right)_{t\geq 0},

where, conditionally on
\Theta
,
E_{i}\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\Theta_{i})
independently for all
i\geq 1
. So our approach and Wang’s give two different perspectives on the same construction, although this does not seem obvious a priori.

2
A line-breaking construction of the
\alpha
-stable tree

In this section, we first establish the validity of a more general version of the change of measure (
1
). We then prove various properties of
\widetilde{\sigma}
and use them to show that the line-breaking construction with intensity process
\widetilde{\sigma}
yields a compact
\mathbb{R}
-tree. Finally, we study the mass measure on the tree.

2.1
A measure-changed subordinator

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
a,b\geq 0
, and let
\nu
be a measure on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}(x\wedge x^{2})\,\nu(dx)<\infty
. Let
L=(L_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a spectrally positive Lévy process in
\mathbb{R}
with characteristic exponent

\Psi_{L}(\lambda)=ai\lambda+\frac{1}{2}b^{2}\lambda^{2}-\int_{0}^{\infty}(e^{i\lambda x}-1-i\lambda x)\,\nu(dx),

so that
\mathbb{E}\left[e^{i\lambda L_{t}}\right]=e^{-t\Psi_{L}(\lambda)}
. Let
(\sigma_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a (killed) subordinator, not necessarily defined on the same probability space, with characteristic exponent

\Psi_{\sigma}(\lambda)=a-b^{2}i\lambda-\int_{0}^{\infty}(e^{i\lambda x}-1)\,x\nu(dx)=-i\Psi_{L}^{\prime}(\lambda).

Assume that

(i)

\int_{0}^{\infty}(e^{ux}-1-ux)\,\nu(dx)<\infty
holds for all
u\in\mathbb{R}
;

(ii)

\textnormal{Re}\left(\Psi_{L}(\lambda)\right)\gg|\lambda|
as
|\lambda|\to\infty
.

Then
L_{1}
has a density
p
, and the process
M^{\nu,a,b}_{t}:=\exp(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,ds)p(-\sigma_{t})\mathbbm{1}\left(\sigma_{t}<\infty\right)
is a non-negative martingale (in the natural filtration associated to
\sigma
).

The proof of
Theorem 2.1
is quite technical, and as such we defer it to the Appendix. For now, we give an outline of some of the key ideas.

Proof sketch.

We will only illustrate the case
a=b=0
as it reduces notation (but exactly the same argument still works in the general case — we just have more terms to keep track of). Condition (ii) implies that
e^{-\Psi_{L}(\lambda)}
is in
L^{1}
, so by Fourier inversion
L
has a density, namely

p(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-i\lambda x}e^{-\Psi_{L}(\lambda)}\,d\lambda.

In what follows we write
M_{t}=M_{t}^{\nu,a,b}
for brevity. Let
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
be the filtration associated to
\sigma
. We will reduce the martingale property to an equivalent statement about (unconditional) expectations. Let
t,s\geq 0
and define
\sigma_{r}^{\prime}=\sigma_{s+r}-\sigma_{s}
; then
\sigma^{\prime}
has the same law as
\sigma
and is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{s}
, and we may compute

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[M_{t+s}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]

\displaystyle=M_{s}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t+s}\sigma_{r}\,dr\right)\frac{p(-\sigma_{t+s})}{p(-\sigma_{s})}\,\Bigg|\,\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]

\displaystyle=M_{s}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}(\sigma_{s}+\sigma_{r}^{\prime})\,dr\right)\frac{p(-\sigma_{s}-\sigma_{t}^{\prime})}{p(-\sigma_{s})}\,\Bigg|\,\mathcal{F}_{s}\right].

So it is equivalent to show that

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}(c+\sigma_{r})\,dr\right)p(-c-\sigma_{t})\right]=p(-c),\quad\text{ for all }c\geq 0.

(2)

The Fourier inversion formula shows that

p(-c)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(G(\lambda)\right)\,d\lambda,

where
G(\lambda)=ic\lambda-\Psi_{L}(\lambda)
. By condition (i), the characteristic exponent
\Psi_{L}
has an analytic continuation to all of
\mathbb{C}
(obtained simply by replacing
\lambda
with a complex number in its Lévy–Khintchine decomposition), and so
G
has an analytic continuation too. By applying Fourier inversion to the density in the expectation, and then applying Fubini’s theorem, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}(c+\sigma_{r})\,dr\right)p(-c-\sigma_{t})\right]=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(i\lambda\sigma_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{r}\,dr\right)\right]e^{c(t+i\lambda)-\Psi_{L}(\lambda)}\,d\lambda.

We can now handle the expectation using Campbell’s formula: indeed,
\sigma
is a pure jump subordinator, so we can find a Poisson process on
\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
, of intensity
ds\otimes\nu(dx)
, such that the set of atoms
\Pi\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
satisfies

\sigma_{t}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(s,x)\in\Pi\\
s\leq t\end{subarray}}x,

and so we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(i\lambda\sigma_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{r}\,dr\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(s,x)\in\Pi\\
s\leq t\end{subarray}}(i\lambda x+(t-s)x)\right)\right].

Then Campbell’s formula yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(s,x)\in\Pi\\
s\leq t\end{subarray}}(i\lambda x+(t-s)x)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}(e^{i\lambda x+(t-s)x}-1)\,ds\,x\nu(dx)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(e^{i\lambda x}\left(\frac{e^{tx}-1}{x}\right)-t\right)x\nu(dx)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(e^{(t+i\lambda)x}-e^{i\lambda x}-tx\right)\,\nu(dx)\right),

which we recognise as
\exp\big(\Psi_{L}(\lambda)-\Psi_{L}(\lambda-it)\big)
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}(c+\sigma_{r})\,dr\right)p(-c-\sigma_{t})\right]\!

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(i\lambda\sigma_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{r}\,dr\right)\right]e^{c(t+i\lambda)-\Psi_{L}(\lambda)}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\!\exp\Big(\Psi_{L}(\lambda)-\Psi_{L}(\lambda-it)+c(t+i\lambda)-\Psi_{L}(\lambda)\Big)\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\Big(ci(\lambda-it)-\Psi_{L}(\lambda-it)\Big)\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(G(\lambda-it))\,d\lambda.

Thus it remains only to show that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(G(\lambda-it))\,d\lambda=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(G(\lambda))\,d\lambda,

and we can do this by a contour integral (using conditions (i) and (ii) to ensure sufficient decay).
∎

In our case of interest we have
a=b=0
and
\nu(dx)=Cx^{-\alpha-1}\,dx
. It is easy to compute
\textnormal{Re}\left(\Psi_{L}(\lambda)\right)=\Theta(|\lambda|^{\alpha})
and so the second condition of
Theorem 2.1
holds, but the first does not; thus we cannot apply our theorem directly — we instead deduce it by taking limits.

Corollary 2.2
.

The conclusion of
Theorem 2.1
also holds in the case
a=b=0
,
\nu(dx)=C_{\alpha}x^{-\alpha-1}\,dx
.

Proof.

For each
\epsilon\in(0,1]
define a measure
\nu_{\varepsilon}
on
(0,\infty)
by
\nu_{\varepsilon}(dx)=C_{\alpha}x^{-\alpha-1}e^{-\varepsilon x^{2}}\,dx
. We apply
Theorem 2.1
in the case
a=b=0,\nu=\nu_{\varepsilon}
: let
L^{\varepsilon}
and
\sigma^{\varepsilon}
denote the relevant Lévy process and subordinator.

First we must check that the two conditions hold. Condition (i) holds straightforwardly by splitting the integral into
(0,1)
and
[1,\infty)
and using standard estimates. For Condition (ii), we may compute

\displaystyle\textnormal{Re}\left(\Psi_{L^{\varepsilon}}\right)

\displaystyle=\textnormal{Re}\left(-\int_{0}^{\infty}(e^{i\lambda x}-1-i\lambda x)\,\nu_{\varepsilon}(dx)\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-\cos(\lambda x))\nu_{\varepsilon}(\,dx)

\displaystyle=C_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-\cos(|\lambda|x))x^{-\alpha-1}e^{-\varepsilon x^{2}}\,dx.

Now a substitution
x=|\lambda|u
yields that this is equal to

\displaystyle C_{\alpha}|\lambda|^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-\cos(u))u^{-\alpha-1}e^{-\varepsilon u^{2}/|\lambda|^{2}}\,du.

By monotone convergence, the integral in the above expression converges to a finite quantity
I_{\alpha}=\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-\cos(u))u^{-\alpha-1}
as
|\lambda|\to\infty
, and so
\textnormal{Re}\left(\Psi_{L^{\varepsilon}}(\lambda)\right)\sim CI_{\alpha}|\lambda|^{\alpha}
as
|\lambda|\to\infty
. In particular,
\textnormal{Re}\left(\Psi_{L^{\varepsilon}}\right)\gg|\lambda|
.

Hence, letting
p_{\varepsilon}
be the density of
L_{1}^{\varepsilon}
, we see that
M^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\exp(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}^{\varepsilon})p_{\varepsilon}(-\sigma_{t}^{\varepsilon})
is a martingale with mean
p_{\varepsilon}(0)
. As seen in the sketch proof of
Theorem 2.1
, it is easier to work with the condition

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}(c+\sigma_{s}^{\varepsilon})\,ds\right)p_{\varepsilon}(-c-\sigma_{t}^{\varepsilon})\right]=p_{\varepsilon}(-c)\quad\text{ for all }c\geq 0,

and deduce the analogous statement for
\sigma
by taking limits. In what follows, we consider
c\geq 0
fixed. To handle the expectation on the left-hand side, it is helpful to consider a coupling of
\sigma^{\varepsilon}
and
\sigma
. Since
\nu_{\varepsilon}\ll\nu
with Radon–Nikodym derivative
e^{-\varepsilon x^{2}}
taking values in
[0,1]
, we can achieve this by thinning: consider a Poisson process
\Pi\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\times[0,1]
with atoms denoted by
(x,t,u)
and intensity measure
\nu\otimes dt\otimes du
. Then we set

\sigma_{t}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,s,u)\in\Pi:\\
s\leq t\end{subarray}}x\quad\text{ and }\quad\sigma_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,s,u)\in\Pi:\\
s\leq t\\
u\leq e^{-\varepsilon x^{2}}\end{subarray}}x.

This coupling arranges that
\sigma_{t}^{\varepsilon}\uparrow\sigma_{t}
for all
t
, almost surely. Thus we have

\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}(c+\sigma_{s}^{\varepsilon})\,ds\right)\to\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}(c+\sigma_{s})\,ds\right)\quad\text{ a.s.}

Now notice that
p_{\varepsilon}\to p
uniformly: indeed, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle|p_{\varepsilon}(x)-p(x)|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-i\lambda x}\left(\exp(-\Psi_{L_{\varepsilon}}(\lambda))-\exp(-\Psi_{L}(\lambda))\right)\,d\lambda\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|e^{-\Psi_{L_{\varepsilon}}(\lambda)}-e^{-\Psi_{L}(\lambda)}\right|\,d\lambda,

a bound which does not depend on
x
. The integrand tends to 0 as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
and, by our calculations above, is bounded by
2e^{-\delta|\lambda|^{\alpha}\mathbbm{1}\left(|\lambda|\geq 1\right)}
for some
\delta>0
, so by dominated convergence this integral tends to 0. Hence, we may bound

\displaystyle\left|p_{\varepsilon}(-c-\sigma_{t}^{\varepsilon})-p(-c-\sigma_{t})\right|\leq\|p_{\varepsilon}-p\|_{\infty}+|p(-c-\sigma_{t})-p(-c-\sigma_{t}^{\varepsilon})|,

where we know that the first term tends to 0 as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Note that
-c-\sigma_{t}^{\varepsilon}\to-c-\sigma_{t}
a.s., and
p
is continuous at
-c-\sigma_{t}
, so the second term tends to zero a.s. Hence
p_{\varepsilon}(-c-\sigma_{t}^{\varepsilon})\to p(-c-\sigma_{t})
a.s. Combining with the convergence of the exponential terms, we see

\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}(c+\sigma_{s}^{\varepsilon})\,ds\right)p_{\varepsilon}(-c-\sigma_{t}^{\varepsilon})\to\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}(c+\sigma_{s})\,ds\right)p(-c-\sigma_{t})\quad\text{ a.s.}

The proof will be complete if we can deduce that the corresponding expectations converge.

To do this, we will give a uniform bound on all of the expressions above and apply the bounded convergence theorem. To this end we introduce a coupling between
L^{\varepsilon}
and
L
: let
\mu^{\varepsilon}=\nu-\nu^{\varepsilon}
, and observe that
\mu^{\varepsilon}
is a (positive) measure with
\mu^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{+})<\infty
and
\int_{0}^{\infty}x\mu^{\varepsilon}(dx)<\infty
. We can construct a Lévy process
R^{\varepsilon}
independent of
L^{\varepsilon}
with characteristic exponent

\Psi_{R^{\varepsilon}}(\lambda)=i\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}x\mu^{\varepsilon}(dx)-\int_{0}^{\infty}(e^{i\lambda x}-1)\mu^{\varepsilon}(dx),

and with the property that
L^{\varepsilon}+R^{\varepsilon}\overset{d}{=}L
. Letting
\eta
be the law of
R^{\varepsilon}_{1}
, we have

p(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\,\eta(dy),\quad\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}.

However,
R^{\varepsilon}
is the sum of a deterministic drift and a pure jump process, and the intensity measure of the jump process is
\mu^{\varepsilon}
, which is finite. Hence,
\eta
has an atom at
-a_{\varepsilon}=-\int_{0}^{\infty}x\mu_{\varepsilon}(dx)
of mass at least
e^{-\mu^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{+})}
. It follows that

p(x)\geq p_{\varepsilon}(x+a_{\varepsilon})e^{-\mu^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{+})}.

Using that
\mu^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\leq\mu^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
for
\varepsilon\leq 1
, we see there is a constant
M=e^{\mu^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+})}
such that
p_{\varepsilon}(-x)\leq Mp(-x-a_{\varepsilon})
for all
\varepsilon
and all
x
.

Now by Theorem 14.35 of Sato
[
39
]
, there exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
and
\beta=\frac{2-\alpha}{2\alpha-2}
such that

p(-x)\sim C_{1}x^{\beta}\exp\left(-C_{2}x^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\right)\quad\text{ as }x\to\infty,

(3)

and so in particular we can find constants
\widetilde{C}_{1},\widetilde{C}_{2}>0
such that

p(-x)\leq\widetilde{C}_{1}\exp\left(-\widetilde{C}_{2}x^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\right)\quad\text{ for all }x>0.

Hence, we almost surely have the bound

\displaystyle\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}(a+\sigma_{s}^{\varepsilon})\,ds\right)p_{\varepsilon}(-a-\sigma_{t}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(t(a+\sigma_{t}^{\varepsilon})\right)p_{\varepsilon}(-a-\sigma_{t}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq M\exp\left(t(a+\sigma_{t}^{\varepsilon})\right)p(-a-\sigma_{t}^{\varepsilon}-a_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\geq 0}\left(Me^{tx}p(-x-a_{\varepsilon})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\geq 0}\left(M\widetilde{C}_{1}\exp\left(tx-\widetilde{C}_{2}(x+a_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\geq 0}\left(M\widetilde{C}_{1}\exp\left(tx-\widetilde{C}_{2}x^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\right)\right),

which is a finite constant, so we are done by bounded convergence.
∎

Recall the definition of the measure-changed subordinator
\widetilde{\sigma}

Proposition 2.3
.

(\widetilde{\sigma}_{t})_{t\geq 0}

where
\sigma^{\prime}
is a copy of the subordinator
\sigma
independent of
\widetilde{\sigma}
.

Proof.

The increasing property is inherited from that of the subordinator
\sigma
.

Now fix

and suppose that
F
and
G
are suitable test-functions. Let
\sigma^{\prime}
be an independent copy of the subordinator
\sigma

\widetilde{\sigma}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[F(\widetilde{\sigma}_{s},0\leq s\leq t_{1})G(\widetilde{\sigma}_{t_{1}+r},0\leq r\leq t_{2})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t_{1}+t_{2}}\sigma_{u}du\right)\frac{p(-\sigma_{t_{1}+t_{2}})}{p(0)}F(\sigma_{s},0\leq s\leq t_{1})G(\sigma_{t_{1}+r},0\leq r\leq t_{2})\right]

∎

We observe that the Radon–Nikodym derivative is not uniformly integrable: indeed, one can show that
\lim_{t\to\infty}M_{t}=0
a.s. Thus the absolute continuity only holds on compact time intervals and, in particular, almost sure late-time properties of
\sigma
do not carry over to

. In fact, we will see in the next section that the asymptotics of

and

2.2
Properties of
\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}

We aim to show that the line-breaking construction with intensity
\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}
yields a compact
\mathbb{R}
-tree: to do this it will be important to study the moments. We can compute the Laplace transform:

Proposition 2.4
.

Define
G(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}(e^{zx}-1-zx)C_{\alpha}x^{-\alpha-1}\,dx
for
\textnormal{Re}(z)\leq 0
. Then the Laplace transform of
\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}
is given by

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\lambda\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}}\right]=\frac{1}{2\pi p(0)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(G(iu-\lambda)-G(iu-t-\lambda)+G(iu-t)\right)\,du,\quad\textnormal{Re}(\lambda)\geq 0.

The proof of this identity is obtained by an adaptation of the proof of
Theorem 2.1
given in the Appendix. The following are useful consequences:

Corollary 2.5
.

As
t\to\infty
we have
\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}\right]\sim\alpha t^{\alpha-1}
.

Proof.

By differentiating the Laplace transform identity in
2.4
at
\lambda=0
(noting that everything decays rapidly enough for dominated convergence to be valid), we see

\mathbb{E}\left[-\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}\right]=\frac{1}{2\pi p(0)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(G^{\prime}(iu-t)-G^{\prime}(iu)\big)\exp\big(G(iu)\big)\,du.

Recall that
G(iu)=-\Psi_{L}(u)
. Differentiating the expression for
G
(again valid because of suitable decay), we see

G^{\prime}(iu)-G^{\prime}(iu-t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{iux}(1-e^{-tx})C_{\alpha}x^{-\alpha}\,dx

and so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi p(0)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{iux}(1-e^{-tx})C_{\alpha}x^{-\alpha}\exp(G(iu))\,dx\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-tx})C_{\alpha}x^{-\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi p(0)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{iux}e^{-\Psi_{L}(u)}\,du\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-tx})C_{\alpha}x^{-\alpha}\frac{p(-x)}{p(0)}\,dx.

Now a substitution of
x=ty
yields

\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}\right]=t^{\alpha-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-y})C_{\alpha}y^{-\alpha}\frac{p(-y/t)}{p(0)}\,dy,

and by dominated convergence the integral converges to
C_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-y})C_{\alpha}y^{-\alpha}\,dy
. Integration by parts reveals that this is equal to
C_{\alpha}\frac{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}{\alpha-1}=\alpha
.
∎

Corollary 2.6
.

\mathrm{Var}\left(\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}\right)=O(t^{\alpha-1})
as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

Let us write
H=H(\lambda,t,u)=G(iu-\lambda)-G(iu-t-\lambda)+G(iu-t)
. We observe that
H
satisfies the PDE

\frac{\partial H}{\partial u}+i\frac{\partial H}{\partial\lambda}=iG^{\prime}(iu-t),

so in particular we have

\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial\lambda^{2}}=i\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial\lambda}\right).

Using this, we can calculate

\displaystyle 2\pi p(0)\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}e^{-\lambda\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\lambda^{2}}\left(\exp(H(\lambda,t,u))\right)\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial\lambda^{2}}+\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial\lambda}\right)^{2}\right)\exp(H)\,du

\displaystyle=-i\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\partial H}{\partial\lambda}\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\left(\exp(H)\right)\,du+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial\lambda}\right)^{2}\exp(H)\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\partial H}{\partial\lambda}\left(-i\frac{\partial H}{\partial u}+\frac{\partial H}{\partial\lambda}\right)\exp(H)\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\partial H}{\partial\lambda}G^{\prime}(iu-t)\exp(H)\,du,

using integration by parts to obtain the first term in the third line. Expanding and evaluating at
\lambda=0
, we deduce that

\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{2\pi p(0)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G^{\prime}(iu-t)(G^{\prime}(iu-t)-G^{\prime}(iu))\exp(G(iu))\,du.

Noting that
G(iu)=-\Psi_{L}(u)
, we further compute that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi p(0)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G^{\prime}(iu-t)(G^{\prime}(iu-t)-G^{\prime}(iu))e^{-\Psi_{L}(u)}\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{\alpha}^{2}}{2\pi p(0)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\Psi_{L}(u)}\int_{0}^{\infty}(e^{(iu-t)x}-1)x^{-\alpha}\,dx\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{iuy}(e^{-ty}-1)y^{-\alpha}\,dy\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{\alpha}^{2}}{2\pi p(0)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{-\alpha}y^{-\alpha}(e^{-ty}-1)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\Psi_{L}(u)}\left(e^{iu(x+y)}e^{-tx}-e^{iuy}\right)\,du\right)\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{\alpha}^{2}}{p(0)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{-\alpha}y^{-\alpha}(e^{-ty}-1)\left(e^{-tx}p(-x-y)-p(-y)\right)\,dx\,dy.

Now recall from
2.5
that
\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}\right]=\frac{C_{\alpha}}{p(0)}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-tx})x^{-\alpha}p(-x)\,dx
. Squaring this and writing it as a double integral, we obtain the following formula for the variance:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}\right)=\frac{C_{\alpha}^{2}}{p(0)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{-\alpha}y^{-\alpha}(1-e^{-ty})\Bigg(

\displaystyle p(-y)p(0)-e^{-tx}p(-x-y)p(0)

\displaystyle\quad-(1-e^{-tx})p(-x)p(-y)\Bigg)\,dx\,dy.

The term in large brackets can be decomposed into
J_{1}+J_{2}+J_{3}
, where

\displaystyle J_{1}

\displaystyle=p(-y)(p(0)-p(-x)),

\displaystyle J_{2}

\displaystyle=e^{-tx}p(0)(p(-y)-p(-x-y)),

\displaystyle J_{3}

\displaystyle=-e^{-tx}p(-y)(p(0)-p(-x)).

But all
J_{i}
can be bounded by
x||p||_{\infty}||p^{\prime}||_{\infty}\wedge 2||p||_{\infty}^{2}
. As
p
and
p^{\prime}
are bounded (a result that can be seen by the Fourier inversion formula) we can thus find constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that
|J_{i}|\leq(c_{1}x)\wedge c_{2}
for
i=1,2,3
. Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3C_{\alpha}^{2}}{p(0)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{-\alpha}y^{-\alpha}(1-e^{-ty})((c_{1}x)\wedge c_{2})\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=\frac{3C_{\alpha}^{2}}{p(0)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{-\alpha}((c_{1}x)\wedge c_{2})\,dx\times\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{-\alpha}(1-e^{-ty})\,dy.

The
x
-integral is finite and independent of
t
, while the
y
-integral is of order
t^{\alpha-1}
(as seen in
2.5
). Thus we have a bound of order
t^{\alpha-1}
.
∎

Proposition 2.7

Fix

\exp(h\widetilde{\sigma}_{t})\mathbb{E}\left[f_{0}(\sigma_{h},\widetilde{\sigma}_{t})\mid\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}\right]

and above by

\exp(h\widetilde{\sigma}_{t})\mathbb{E}\left[f_{\epsilon}(\sigma_{h},\widetilde{\sigma}_{t})\mid\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}\right],

whenever
h\leq\epsilon
.

For
r\geq 0
, the (random) function
x\mapsto f_{r}(x,\widetilde{\sigma}_{t})
is twice continuously differentiable and such that
f_{r}(\cdot,\widetilde{\sigma}_{t})
along with its first and second partial derivatives in
x
are vanishing as
x\to\infty
. By Theorem 31.5 of
[
39
]
,
f_{r}(\cdot,\widetilde{\sigma}_{t})
is in the domain of the generator of
\sigma
, which is the operator
\mathcal{L}
given by

\mathcal{L}f(x)=C_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(y-x)y^{-\alpha}dy.

Hence,

\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{r}(\sigma_{h},\widetilde{\sigma}_{t})\mid\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}\right]=C_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2-x}\exp(rx)\frac{p(-\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}-x)}{p(-\widetilde{\sigma}_{t})}dx.

It then follows that

\liminf_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\left[(\widetilde{\sigma}_{t+h}-\widetilde{\sigma_{t}})^{2}\mid\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}\right]\geq C_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2-x}\frac{p(-\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}-x)}{p(-\widetilde{\sigma}_{t})}dx

and

\displaystyle\limsup_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\left[(\widetilde{\sigma}_{t+h}-\widetilde{\sigma_{t}})^{2}|\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{\alpha}\inf_{\epsilon>0}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2-x}\exp(\epsilon x)\frac{p(-\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}-x)}{p(-\widetilde{\sigma}_{t})}dx

\displaystyle=C_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2-x}\frac{p(-\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}-x)}{p(-\widetilde{\sigma}_{t})}dx,

by bounded convergence. The claimed result (
4
) follows.

Now note that, for each
h>0
,

Q_{t}=\sum_{0\leq s\leq t}(\Delta\widetilde{\sigma}_{s})^{2}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{h}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{s+h}-\widetilde{\sigma}_{s})^{2}ds

and so by Fubini’s theorem and the tower law,

\mathbb{E}\left[Q_{t}\right]\leq\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\left[(\widetilde{\sigma}_{s+h}-\widetilde{\sigma}_{s})^{2}\mid\widetilde{\sigma}_{s}\right]\right]\,ds=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[I_{h}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{s})\right]\,ds.

We would like to apply dominated convergence to the double integral
\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\cdot\right]\,ds
; we will justify this step in a moment. For now, assuming that this application is valid, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[Q_{t}\right]\leq\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[I_{h}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{s})\right]\,ds=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[I(\widetilde{\sigma}_{s})\right]\,ds.

Thus by monotone convergence we get
\mathbb{E}\left[Q_{\infty}\right]\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[I(\widetilde{\sigma}_{s})\right]\,ds
, and so it suffices to show that this integral is finite. This is now a straightforward calculation using
2.8
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[I(\widetilde{\sigma}_{s})\right]\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2-\alpha}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{p(-\widetilde{\sigma}_{s}-x)}{p(-\widetilde{\sigma}_{s})}\right]\,ds\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2-\alpha}\exp(-sx)\frac{p(-x)}{p(0)}\,ds\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{1-\alpha}\frac{p(-x)}{p(0)}\,dx<\infty.

We now return to justifying the application of dominated convergence. We show that there exists some constant
M>0
such that, for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
and all
h\in(0,1)
, we have
I_{h}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{s})\leq M\exp(\widetilde{\sigma}_{s})
. Note that
\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\widetilde{\sigma}_{s})\right]\,ds<\infty
: in fact we can calculate it explicitly by analogous methods to those used in
2.4
.

Using the asymptotic estimate from (
3
) and the fact that
p
is strictly positive we see that there exists a constant
R>0
such that

p(-x)\leq Rx^{\beta}\exp(-C_{2}x^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}),\quad p(-x)\geq\frac{1}{R}x^{\beta}\exp(-C_{2}x^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}})\quad\text{for all $x\geq 0$},

where
\beta=(2-\alpha)/(2\alpha-2)
.

Hence,

\frac{p(-x-y)}{p(-x)}\leq R^{2}\quad\text{for all $x,y\geq 0$}.

(6)

For any
s\geq 0
and any
h\in(0,1)
, we may use
2.3
to obtain

\displaystyle I_{h}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{s})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\left[(\widetilde{\sigma}_{s+h}-\widetilde{\sigma}_{s})^{2}\mid\widetilde{\sigma}_{s}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\left[(\sigma^{\prime}_{h})^{2}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{h}\left(\widetilde{\sigma}_{s}+\sigma^{\prime}_{u}\right)\,du\right)\frac{p(-\widetilde{\sigma}_{s}-\sigma^{\prime}_{h})}{p(-\widetilde{\sigma}_{s})}\ \Bigg|\ \widetilde{\sigma}_{s}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{h}\exp(h\widetilde{\sigma}_{s})\mathbb{E}\left[(\sigma^{\prime}_{h})^{2}\exp\left(h\sigma^{\prime}_{h}\right)\frac{p(-\widetilde{\sigma}_{s}-\sigma^{\prime}_{h})}{p(-\widetilde{\sigma}_{s})}\ \bigg|\ \widetilde{\sigma}_{s}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp(\widetilde{\sigma}_{s})\times\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\left[(\sigma^{\prime}_{h})^{2}\exp(\sigma^{\prime}_{h})\frac{p(-\widetilde{\sigma}_{s}-\sigma^{\prime}_{h})}{p(-\widetilde{\sigma}_{s})}\ \bigg|\ \widetilde{\sigma}_{s}\right].

It now suffices to find a constant
M
such that

\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\left[(\sigma^{\prime}_{h})^{2}\exp(\sigma^{\prime}_{h})\frac{p(-x-\sigma^{\prime}_{h})}{p(-x)}\right]\leq M

for all
x\geq 0
and all
h\in(0,1)
. We split the left-hand side into the sum

\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\left[(\sigma^{\prime}_{h})^{2}\exp(\sigma^{\prime}_{h})\frac{p(-x-\sigma^{\prime}_{h})}{p(-x)}\mathbbm{1}\left(\sigma^{\prime}_{h}\leq R\right)\right]+\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\left[(\sigma^{\prime}_{h})^{2}\exp(\sigma^{\prime}_{h})\frac{p(-x-\sigma^{\prime}_{h})}{p(-x)}\mathbbm{1}\left(\sigma^{\prime}_{h}>R\right)\right]

and find constants
M_{1},M_{2}
bounding each term.

Using (
6
), we can crudely bound the first term by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\left[(\sigma^{\prime}_{h})^{2}\exp(\sigma^{\prime}_{h})\frac{p(-x-\sigma^{\prime}_{h})}{p(-x)}\mathbbm{1}\left(\sigma^{\prime}_{h}\leq R\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq R^{2}\times\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\left[(\sigma^{\prime}_{h})^{2}\exp(\sigma^{\prime}_{h})\mathbbm{1}\left(\sigma^{\prime}_{h}\leq R\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq R^{2}e^{R}\times\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\left[(\sigma^{\prime}_{h}\wedge R)^{2}\right].

This bound does not depend on
x
, is finite for each fixed
h\in(0,1)
and is continuous in
h
on this interval. Moreover, again using the generator, as
h\downarrow 0
the bound converges to
R^{2}e^{R}\int_{0}^{\infty}(y\wedge R)^{2}C_{\alpha}y^{-\alpha}\,dy
, which is also finite. Thus there is some choice of
M_{1}
such that our bound is at most
M_{1}
for all
h\in(0,1)
.

For the second term, we can use the asymptotic estimates for
p
again, this time to obtain the existence of a constant
\widetilde{R}
such that

y^{2}\exp(y)\frac{p(-x-y)}{p(-x)}\leq\widetilde{R}\quad\text{ for all }x,y\geq 0.

Using this, our second term satisfies the bounds

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\left[(\sigma^{\prime}_{h})^{2}\exp(\sigma^{\prime}_{h})\frac{p(-x-\sigma^{\prime}_{h})}{p(-x)}\mathbbm{1}\left(\sigma^{\prime}_{h}>R\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{R}\times\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma^{\prime}_{h}\geq R\right)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{R}\times\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\geq Rh^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\right).

This bound also depends only on
h
, is finite for each fixed
h
and is continuous for
h\in(0,1)
. As
h\to 0
, it converges to a constant (one can calculate this constant by integrating the density in (14.37) of
[
39
]
), and so there exists some
M_{2}
such that the bound is at most
M_{2}
for all
h\in(0,1)
.
∎

2.3
Compactness of the
\mathbb{R}
-tree

With
\widetilde{\sigma}
constructed we may now consider a random
\mathbb{R}
-tree
\mathcal{T}
generated by a line-breaking construction with intensity process
\widetilde{\sigma}
. We already know that
\mathcal{T}
is a metric space; we aim to show that it is also compact. Recall that formally,
\mathcal{T}=(\mathbb{R}^{+},d^{\circ})
: it will be useful to introduce the notation
\mathcal{T}_{x}=([0,x],d^{\circ})
for the subtree at time
x
.

Note that by
2.5
we have
\int_{R}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}\right]}dt<\infty
for all large enough
R
. This condition matches that in the compactness conjecture from
[
18
]
, and our proof will follow a similar structure to the proof given there, although we will also require the variance estimate and a few more auxiliary lemmas.

The first lemma is a variation of Lemma 4.7 in
[
18
]
.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
x\leq z\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Then conditionally given
\widetilde{\sigma}
, the law of
d(z,\mathcal{T}_{x})
is stochastically dominated by an exponential random variable of rate
\widetilde{\sigma}_{x}^{2}/\widetilde{\sigma}_{z-}
.

Proof.

Recall the notation for cut points
Y_{i}
and attachment points
Z_{i}
. We produce a sequence of points
(z_{i},y_{i})_{i\geq 0}\in[0,\infty)^{2}
as follows. First,
z_{0}=z
. Having constructed
z_{i}
, we define an index
k_{i}=\max\{k:Y_{k}<z_{i}\}
, then set
y_{i}=Y_{k_{i}}
and
z_{i+1}=Z_{k_{i}}
. That is,
y_{i}
is the start of the branch containing
z_{i}
, and
z_{i+1}
is the attachment point for this branch (i.e.
y_{i}^{+}=z_{i+1}
). The path
z_{0}\to z_{1}\to\dots
gives the geodesic from
y
back to the root, and terminating upon hitting
\mathcal{T}_{x}
gives the geodesic from
y
to this subtree.

In particular, defining
T
to be the least integer such that
z_{T+1}\leq x
, the distance
d(z,\mathcal{T}_{x})
is given by

d(z,\mathcal{T}_{x})=\sum_{i=0}^{T}(z_{i}-\max\{y_{i},x\})

(note that the maximum is always
y_{i}
unless
i=T
, and when
i=T
it equals
x
only if the geodesic from
z
to
\mathcal{T}_{x}
ends at
x
). Our goal now is to show that the terms in the sum can be stochastically dominated by i.i.d. exponentials with parameter
\widetilde{\sigma}_{x}
, and that
T
can be dominated by a geometric variable with parameter
\widetilde{\sigma}_{x}/\widetilde{\sigma}_{z-}
which is independent of the dominating exponentials.

First of all, note that
(z_{i},y_{i})
forms a Markov chain: indeed given values for
(z_{i},y_{i})
(and
\widetilde{\sigma}
), the law of
(z_{i+1},y_{i+1})
can be described as follows. First
z_{i+1}
is sampled according to the normalisation of
d\sigma|_{[0,y_{i})}
. Then
y_{i+1}
is the rightmost atom to the left of
z_{i}
in the Poisson process of cuts. Running this procedure as
i
increases only reveals atoms of the Poisson process from right to left, and so
y_{i+1}
is conditionally independent of
(z_{0},y_{0}),\dots,(z_{i-1},y_{i-1})
given
(z_{i},y_{i})
.

We prove the stochastic domination via a coupling: let
S\subset(-\infty,z]
be (the atoms of) a Poisson process of constant intensity
\widetilde{\sigma}_{x}
, coupled with the Poisson process of cuts such that
S\cap(-\infty,x]
is independent of the cuts, and
S\cap(x,z]
is a subset of
\{Y_{i}:i\geq 1\}\cap(x,z]
. For
i\geq 0
, let
w_{i}
be the largest element of
S
smaller than
z_{i}
. Let
E_{i}
be a sequence of i.i.d. exponentials of rate
\widetilde{\sigma}_{x}
, independent of everything else, and set

e_{i}=\begin{cases}z_{i}-w_{i},&i\leq T\\
E_{i},&i>T.\end{cases}

Observe that
e_{i}
are all i.i.d. exponentials of rate
\widetilde{\sigma}_{x}
, are independent of
T
, and almost surely we have
z_{i}-\max\{y_{i},x\}\leq e_{i}
for all
i\leq T
. Thus we have

d(z,\mathcal{T}_{x})\leq\sum_{i=0}^{T}e_{i},\quad\text{ a.s.}

Hence it remains only to show
T
is stochastically dominated by a (zero-indexed) geometric random variable with parameter
p=\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}_{x}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{z^{-}}}
. For each
n\geq 0
, let
\mathcal{F}_{n}=\sigma((z_{i},y_{i}):i\leq n,\widetilde{\sigma})
and observe that
T+1
is an
(\mathcal{F}_{n})
-stopping time. Moreover, on the event
\{T+1>n\}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(T+1\leq n+1\ \big|\ \mathcal{F}_{n}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(z_{n+1}\leq x\ \big|\ \mathcal{F}_{n}\right)=\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}_{x}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{y_{n}-}}\geq\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}_{x}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{z-}}.

On the complement of this event, the conditional probability is 1, and so we have

\mathbb{P}\left(T+1\leq n+1\ \big|\ \mathcal{F}_{n}\right)\geq\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}_{x}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{z-}}\mathbbm{1}\left(T+1>n\right)+\mathbbm{1}\left(T+1\leq n\right).

More conveniently we can write

\mathbb{P}\left(T+1>n+1\ \big|\ \mathcal{F}_{n}\right)\leq\left(1-\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}_{x}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{z-}}\right)\mathbbm{1}\left(T+1>n\right),

and then the tower law yields

\mathbb{P}\left(T\geq n\ \big|\ \widetilde{\sigma}\right)\leq\left(1-\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}_{x}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{z-}}\right)^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(T\geq 0\ \big|\ \widetilde{\sigma}\right)=\left(1-\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}_{x}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{z-}}\right)^{n},\quad n\geq 0,

completing the proof.
∎

The strategy to prove compactness is as follows: we take a sequence
\chi_{n}\to\infty
of times and consider the trees
\mathcal{T}_{\chi_{n}}
. These are all compact subsets of the final tree
\mathcal{T}
, and we show that they form a Cauchy sequence with respect to the Hausdorff distance
d_{\mathrm{H}}
in
\mathcal{T}
. The Hausdorff distance yields a complete metric on compact subsets, and thus
\mathcal{T}_{\chi_{n}}\to\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
for some compact
T_{\infty}\subset\mathcal{T}
. We then argue that
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}=\mathcal{T}
, and thus
\mathcal{T}
is itself a compact space.

To this end, define
\chi_{n}=\inf\{t\geq 0:\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}\right]>2^{n}\}
. The asymptotics for the mean imply that
\chi_{n}\sim c\,2^{n/(\alpha-1)}
for some
c>0
. To prove
\mathcal{T}_{\chi_{n}}
is a.s. Cauchy we show the following:

Lemma 2.10
.

Almost surely there exists a (random)
n_{0}\geq 1
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
we have

d_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{T}_{\chi_{n-1}},\mathcal{T}_{\chi_{n}})\leq\frac{18\log(\chi_{n})}{2^{n}}+\chi_{n}^{-1}.

Proof.

A straightforward second-moment argument and Borel–Cantelli reveal that
\widetilde{\sigma}_{\chi_{n}}\sim 2^{n}
a.s. Thus we can find a (random)
n_{1}\geq 1
such that

\widetilde{\sigma}_{\chi_{n}}\in\left[0.9\times 2^{n},1.1\times 2^{n}\right]\quad\text{ for all }n\geq n_{1}-1.

In what follows we work with
n\geq n_{1}
only. For any
t\in[0,\chi_{n}]
, let
E_{n}(t)
be the event that
d(t,\mathcal{T}_{\chi_{n-1}})\geq\frac{18\log(\chi_{n})}{2^{n}}
. By Fubini’s theorem and
2.9
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\chi_{n}}\!\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{n}(t)\right)dt\,\,\Bigg|\,\widetilde{\sigma}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\chi_{n}}\mathbb{P}\left(E_{n}(t)\,\big|\,\widetilde{\sigma}\right)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\chi_{n}}\mathbb{P}\left(\textnormal{Exp}\left(\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}_{\chi_{n-1}}^{2}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{\chi_{n}-}}\right)\geq\frac{18\log(\chi_{n})}{2^{n}}\,\Bigg|\,\widetilde{\sigma}\right)\,dt

\displaystyle=\chi_{n}\exp\left(-\frac{18\log(\chi_{n})\widetilde{\sigma}_{\chi_{n-1}}^{2}}{2^{n}\widetilde{\sigma}_{\chi_{n}-}}\right).

Using
\widetilde{\sigma}_{\chi_{n-1}}\geq 0.9\times 2^{n-1}
and
\widetilde{\sigma}_{\chi_{n}}\leq 1.1\times 2^{n}
and some crude numerical estimates, we see the above quantity is at most
\chi_{n}^{-2}
. Then by Markov’s inequality, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{0}^{\chi_{n}}\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{n}(t)\right)\,dt>\chi_{n}^{-1}\,\Bigg|\,\widetilde{\sigma}\right)\leq\chi_{n}^{-1}

for all
n\geq n_{1}
. Note that
\sum_{n\geq n_{1}}\chi_{n}^{-1}<\infty
a.s. and so, by Borel–Cantelli, we almost surely have

\int_{0}^{\chi_{n}}\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{n}(t)\right)dt\leq\chi_{n}^{-1}\quad\text{ for all }n\geq n_{2},

where
n_{2}
is some (random) finite bound. We claim that the estimate on the Hausdorff distance holds for all such
n
. Indeed, if it does not hold, then there is some point
x\in\mathcal{T}_{\chi_{n}}
with
d(x,\mathcal{T}_{\chi_{n-1}})>\frac{18\log(\chi_{n})}{2^{n}}+\chi_{n}^{-1}
. But then there is a section of the geodesic path from
x
to
\mathcal{T}_{\chi_{n-1}}
of length
d(x,\mathcal{T}_{\chi_{n-1}})-\frac{18\log(\chi_{n})}{2^{n}}>\chi_{n}^{-1}
on which
\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{n}(t)\right)=1
, contradicting the integral bound.
∎

We can now put everything together:

Theorem 2.11
.

The
\mathbb{R}
-tree
(\mathcal{T},d)
built from the line-breaking construction with intensity
\widetilde{\sigma}
is almost surely compact.

Proof.

By
2.10
and the observation that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{18\log(\chi_{n})}{2^{n}}+\chi_{n}^{-1}\right)<\infty
, we see that
(\mathcal{T}_{\chi_{n}})
is almost surely Cauchy with respect to the Hausdorff distance. By completeness, we deduce that a.s. there exists a compact
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}\subset\mathcal{T}
such that
\mathcal{T}_{\chi_{n}}\to\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
a.s. in the Hausdorff metric.

We show that (on the event that
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
exists)
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}=\mathcal{T}
. Suppose not; then there exists some point
x\in\mathcal{T}\setminus\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
. As
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
is compact, it is closed in
\mathcal{T}
and so
x
has positive distance from
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
. Let
r=d(x,\mathcal{T}_{\infty})>0
. It follows that
d(x,\mathcal{T}_{\chi_{n}})\geq r
for all
n
. However, observe that

\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{T}_{\chi_{n}}=\bigcup_{t\geq 0}\mathcal{T}_{t}

and, by construction of
\mathcal{T}
, the latter is a dense subset of
\mathcal{T}
. Thus we may find a sequence
x_{k}\in\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{T}_{\chi_{n}}
such that
d(x,x_{k})\to 0
. But for each
k
there is some
n=n(k)
such that
x_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{\chi_{n}}
, and then

d(x,x_{k})\geq d(x,\mathcal{T}_{\chi_{n(k)}})\geq r

which is a contradiction.
∎

2.4
The mass measure

We now seek to equip this compact metric space with a probability measure. For each
n
, recall that
\mu_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Y_{i}}
is the uniform measure on the leaves of
\mathcal{T}(n)=\mathcal{T}_{Y_{n}}
. We will construct a weak limit of
(\mu_{n})
on
\mathcal{T}
.

For each
n
, let
\pi_{n}:\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}(n)
be the projection map onto
\mathcal{T}(n)
. We are interested in subsets of
\mathcal{T}
which are pre-images under these projection maps.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
n\geq 1
and let
\widetilde{S}\subset\mathcal{T}(n)
be measurable. Set
S=\pi_{n}^{-1}(\widetilde{S})
. Then the sequence
(\mu_{i}(S))_{i\geq n}
converges almost surely to a limit in
[0,1]
.

To prove this lemma, we require the following result about time-dependent Pólya urns (part of which is Theorem 1 of Pemantle
[
36
]
). We defer the straightforward proof to the Appendix.

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
A_{0},M_{0},(m_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be (possibly random) positive real numbers with
A_{0}<M_{0}
. Write
M_{n}=M_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}
for brevity. Conditionally given all of these, let
(A_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be a Markov process such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(A_{i}=A_{i-1}+m_{i}\ \Big|\ (A_{j})_{0\leq j<i},(M_{j})_{j\geq 0}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{A_{i-1}}{M_{i-1}},\quad\text{ and}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(A_{i}=A_{i-1}\ \Big|\ (A_{j})_{0\leq j<i},(M_{j})_{j\geq 0}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{M_{i-1}-A_{i-1}}{M_{i-1}}.

Then we can find a random variable
X_{\infty}
, supported on
[0,1]
, such that the two sequences

\left(\frac{A_{n}}{M_{n}}\right)_{n\geq 1}\quad\text{ and }\quad\left(\frac{\#\{i\leq n:A_{i}\neq A_{i-1}\}}{n}\right)_{n\geq 1}

converge almost surely to
X_{\infty}
.

Proof of
2.12
.

Take
n=n_{0}
and pick
\widetilde{S}\subset\mathcal{T}(n_{0}).
Let
\nu
be the unique measure on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
\nu([0,x])=\widetilde{\sigma}_{x}
for all
x\geq 0
, and note that we can view this as a measure on
\mathcal{T}
such that
\nu(\mathcal{T}(n))=\widetilde{\sigma}_{Y_{n}}<\infty
for each
n
. Now set

A_{i}=\nu(S\cap\mathcal{T}(i))\quad\text{ and }\quad M_{i}=\nu(\mathcal{T}(i))=\widetilde{\sigma}_{Y_{i}}

for
i\geq n_{0}
, also defining
m_{i}=M_{i}-M_{i-1}
for
i>n_{0}
. We note that when going from
A_{i-1}
to
A_{i}
, the value either stays the same (if
Z_{i-1}\not\in S
) or increases by
m_{i}
(if
Z_{i-1}\in S
), and conditionally given everything so far, the probability of the latter is
\frac{A_{i-1}}{M_{i-1}}
. Thus
(A_{i})_{i\geq n_{0}}
and
(M_{i})_{i\geq n_{0}}
form a time-dependent Pólya urn as in
2.13
(up to shifting the indices). It follows that the sequence

\left(\frac{\#\{i\leq n:A_{i}\neq A_{i-1}\}}{n}\right)_{n\geq 1}

almost surely converges to a limit
X_{\infty}
in
[0,1]
. For each fixed
n>n_{0}
, the numerator is counting the number of indices
i
with
n_{0}\leq i\leq n
such that
Z_{i-1}\in S
. For this range of indices we have
Z_{i-1}\in S
iff
Y_{i}\in S
, and so we can estimate

\left|\frac{\#\{i\leq n:A_{i}\neq A_{i-1}\}}{n}-\mu_{n}(S)\right|\leq\frac{n_{0}}{n}\to 0.

We deduce that
\mu_{n}(S)\to X_{\infty}
almost surely.
∎

Corollary 2.14
.

Almost surely the following holds: for any function
g:\mathcal{T}\to\mathbb{R}
which is bounded and continuous, the sequence
(\mu_{n}(g))_{n\geq 1}
converges to a limit in
\mathbb{R}
, where
\mu_{n}(g):=\int_{\mathcal{T}}g(x)\,d\mu_{n}(x).

Proof.

For each
n
, the subspace
\mathcal{T}_{Y_{n}}
is compact, hence totally bounded, so we may partition it into finitely many subsets each with diameter at most
1/n
. Write
I^{n}_{1},I^{n}_{2},\dots,I^{n}_{N}
for this partition, where
N=N(n)
is the number of pieces. Without loss of generality we may take these partitions to be nested, in the following sense: for any
n<k
, the induced partition of
\mathcal{T}(k)
from that of
\mathcal{T}(n)
with pieces
(\pi_{n}^{-1}(I^{n}_{i})\cap\mathcal{T}(k))_{i\leq N(n)}
is coarser than the partition
(I^{k}_{i})_{i\leq N(k)}
. That is, each piece
I^{k}_{i}
is a subset of some
\pi_{n}^{-1}(I^{n}_{i})\cap\mathcal{T}(k)
. Now pick arbitrary elements
x^{n}_{i}\in I^{n}_{i}
for all
n
and
i
.

Write
J^{n}_{i}=\pi_{n}^{-1}(I^{n}_{i})
, and note that for each fixed
n
, the sets
(J^{n}_{i})_{i\leq N(n)}
partition
\mathcal{T}
. By
Theorem 2.11
and
2.12
, the event

\mathcal{G}=\{\mathcal{T}\text{ is compact}\}\cap\{(\mu_{k}(J^{n}_{i}))_{k\geq 1}\text{ converges for all pairs }(n,i)\text{ with }i\leq N(n)\}

has probability 1. We show that the conclusion of the theorem holds on this event. Since
\mathcal{T}
is compact, the Hausdorff distance
d_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}(n))
between
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}(n)
is finite and tends to zero as
n\to\infty
. In particular, we can bound

\delta_{n}:=\max_{1\leq i\leq N(n)}\text{diam}(J^{n}_{i})\leq\frac{1}{n}+2d_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}(n))\to 0.

Write
\mu(J^{n}_{i})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mu_{k}(J^{n}_{i})
. The first step is to show that, for any bounded continuous function
g
, the limit

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)}\mu(J^{n}_{i})g(x^{n}_{i})=:\mu(g)

exists. We will do this by showing the sequence is Cauchy. Fix any
\varepsilon>0
. Then note that by compactness of
\mathcal{T}
,
g
is uniformly continuous, so there exists
\delta>0
such that
d(u,v)\leq\delta
implies
|g(u)-g(v)|\leq\varepsilon
. Now there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\delta_{n}<\delta
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. For any
m>n>n_{0}
, we partition
[N(m)]
into
N(n)
pieces given by

F_{i}=\{j\in[N(m)]:I^{m}_{j}\subset\pi_{n}^{-1}(I^{n}_{i})\cap\mathcal{T}(m)\},\quad\quad 1\leq j\leq N(n).

Now we compute

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)}\mu(J^{n}_{i})g(x^{n}_{i})-\sum_{j=1}^{N(m)}\mu(J^{m}_{j})g(x^{m}_{j})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)}\left|\mu(J^{n}_{i})g(x^{n}_{i})-\sum_{j\in F_{i}}\mu(J^{m}_{j})g(x^{m}_{j})\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)}\left|\sum_{j\in F_{i}}\mu(J^{m}_{j})\left(g(x^{n}_{i})-g(x^{m}_{j})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)}\sum_{j\in F_{i}}\mu(J^{m}_{j})\left|g(x^{n}_{i})-g(x^{m}_{j})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)}\sum_{j\in F_{i}}\mu(J^{m}_{j})\cdot\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\varepsilon,

where in the penultimate line we have used that both
x^{n}_{i}
and
x^{m}_{j}
lie within
J^{n}_{i}
, which has diameter at most
\delta_{n}<\delta
.

We now prove that for any bounded continuous
g
, we have
\mu_{n}(g)\to\mu(g)
, where
\mu(g)
is the limiting value obtained above. For any
m>n
we may estimate

\left|\mu_{m}(g)-\mu(g)\right|\\
\quad\leq\left|\mu_{m}(g)-\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)}\mu_{m}(J^{n}_{i})g(x^{n}_{i})\right|+\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)}\left|\mu_{m}(J^{n}_{i})-\mu(J^{n}_{i})\right||g(x^{n}_{i})|+\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)}\mu(J^{n}_{i})g(x^{n}_{i})-\mu(g)\right|.

(7)

By writing
\mu_{m}(g)=\sum_{i=1}^{N(n)}\mu_{m}(g\mathbbm{1}_{J^{n}_{i}})
, we can crudely bound the first term by
\sup\{|g(x)-g(x^{n}_{i})|:x\in J^{n}_{i},i\leq N(n)\}
. Now given
\varepsilon>0
, we proceed as follows. By uniform continuity of
g
and the diameter bound on
J^{n}_{i}
, we can find
n_{1}
such that, whenever
n\geq n_{1}
, we have
\sup\{|g(x)-g(x^{n}_{i})|:x\in J^{n}_{i},i\leq N(n)\}\leq\varepsilon/3
. By the convergence to
\mu(g)
shown above, we can also find
n_{2}
such that, whenever
n\geq n_{2}
, the third term in (
7
) is at most
\varepsilon/3
. Now take
n=n_{1}\vee n_{2}
. For this choice of
n
, the second term in (
7
) tends to 0 as
m\to\infty
, so there is some
m_{0}
such that the second term is at most
\varepsilon/3
whenever
m\geq m_{0}
. Thus, for all
m\geq m_{0}
we have
|\mu_{m}(g)-\mu(g)|\leq\varepsilon
, which establishes the required convergence.
∎

Theorem 2.15
.

The sequence of measures
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
possesses a weak limit
\mu
which is a probability measure on
\mathcal{T}
.

Proof.

Note that the convergence in Theorem
2.14
occurs for all bounded continuous functions simultaneously — a much stronger statement than only having almost sure convergence for each individual bounded continuous function. On this probability 1 event, we can define a map
\mu
from bounded continuous functions on
\mathcal{T}
to the reals given by

\mu(g)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(g).

This is a positive linear functional and
\mathcal{T}
is both locally compact and Hausdorff, so by the Riesz–Markov representation theorem it is realised by a Borel measure on
\mathcal{T}
. In a slight abuse of notation we will also call this measure
\mu
. It is easy to see that this measure has mass 1, i.e. is a probability measure.
∎

Note that it now follows straightforwardly that

(\mathcal{T}(k),d|_{\mathcal{T}(k)},\mu_{k})\to(\mathcal{T},d,\mu)

(8)

almost surely as

, for the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov distance.

3
Line-breaking constructions for discrete trees

We begin by introducing the Ulam–Harris notation for rooted ordered trees. Set

\mathcal{U}=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{N}^{n},

with the convention that
\mathbb{N}^{0}=\{\varnothing\}
. We write elements of
\mathbb{N}^{n}
as
u=u_{1}\dots u_{n}
, for any
n\geq 1
, and we call
n
the length of
u
, for which we write
n=|u|
. This gives the structure of a rooted ordered tree: indeed,
\varnothing
is the root and any
u=u_{1}\dots u_{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
is a child of
u_{1}\dots u_{n-1}
.

A
(locally finite) rooted ordered tree
is a subset
T\subset\mathcal{U}
such that

(i)

\varnothing\in T
;

(ii)

For any
u\in T
(including
u=\varnothing
) there is an integer
c(u)\geq 0
such that for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
,

ui\in T\iff i\leq c(u).

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0,1,2,\dots\}
. A
Bienaymé tree
with offspring distribution
\mu
is a random rooted ordered tree
T\subset\mathcal{U}
such that all integers
c(u),u\in\mathcal{U}
are i.i.d. with distribution
\mu
. (Note that in this definition we have specified
c(u)
even when
u\not\in T
— this makes the definition easier as we do not need to condition on the event that
u
is a vertex in the tree.) For
\xi
a random variable taking values in
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we will often speak of Bienaymé trees with offspring distribution
\xi
: this is shorthand for a Bienaymé tree with offspring distribution
(p_{k})
, where
p_{k}=\mathbb{P}\left(\xi=k\right)
.

Let
\mathbb{T}_{n}
denote the set of rooted labelled trees with
n
vertices. The Bienaymé trees (conditioned on size) will be our primary model for discrete random trees. It will be convenient for our purposes to think of them as random elements of
\mathbb{T}_{n}
rather than
\mathcal{U}
; we will explain how to do this in Subsection
3.2
below.

3.1
Reverse Prüfer codes

We now present a bijection between
\mathbb{T}_{n}
and
[n]^{n-1}
, the space of sequences of length
n-1
taking values in
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. This is a variant of the classical Prüfer code, and will be useful for constructing random discrete trees. This variant has been studied in
[
40
]
and is closely related to the bijection presented in
[
1
]
.

Given a rooted labelled tree
(t,\rho)
, we produce a codeword by ‘revealing’ the tree one edge at a time. We first reveal the path from the root to the lowest labelled (non-root) vertex, then the path from this branch to the lowest labelled vertex not already included, and so on. Formally, set
C_{0}=1
and
L_{1}=\min\{i\geq 1:i\neq\rho\}
. Say the path from
\rho
to
L_{1}
consists of
C_{1}\geq 2
vertices, namely
\rho=w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{C_{1}-1}
and
L_{1}
appearing in this order. Now inductively for
k\geq 2
, we take

\mathcal{U}_{k-1}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{k-1}\{w_{C_{j-1}},\dots,w_{C_{j}-1},L_{j}\},\quad L_{k}=\min([n]\setminus\mathcal{U}_{k-1})

and consider the path from
\mathcal{U}_{k-1}
to
L_{k}
: suppose its vertices are
w_{C_{k-1}},w_{C_{k-1}+1},\dots,w_{C_{k}-1},L_{k}
in this order (so
w_{C_{k-1}}\in\mathcal{U}_{k-1}
). We repeat this until
\mathcal{U}_{k}=[n]
, i.e. we have exhausted the entire tree. Note that
\#\mathcal{U}_{k}=C_{k}
for each
k\geq 1
, and so if we stop after exploring
B
branches, then
C_{B}=n
. We take as codeword the sequence
(w_{i})_{1\leq i\leq C_{B}-1}
, which has length
n-1
.

We may immediately observe that the multiplicity of a label in the codeword is equal to the out-degree of the corresponding vertex. In particular, the leaves never appear in the codeword.

We can also reverse this construction — given a codeword
(w_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n-1}
, we show how to produce a corresponding tree. We know
w_{1}
is the root. We calculate the values of
L_{j}
and
C_{j}
first: we know
C_{0}=1
and
L_{1}=\min\{i\geq 1:i\neq w_{1}\}
. New branches start whenever the codeword repeats a label or a previously revealed leaf, and so for
k\geq 1
,

C_{k}=\min\left\{i\geq C_{k-1}+1:w_{i}\in\{w_{1},\dots,w_{i-1}\}\cup\{L_{1},\dots,L_{k}\}\right\}

and

L_{k+1}=\min\left\{i\geq 1:i\not\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}\{w_{C_{j-1}},\dots,w_{C_{j}-1},L_{j}\}\right\}

Now given these values, we can form the branches
(w_{C_{k-1}},\dots,w_{C_{k}-1},L_{k})
and glue these together to build a tree.

Write
\psi_{n}:[n]^{n-1}\to\mathbb{T}_{n}
for this mapping. Note that for any vector
\bm{d}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}=n-1
, we can consider the set of codewords

W_{n,\bm{d}}=\{(w_{1},\dots,w_{n-1})\in[n]^{n-1}:\#\{j:w_{j}=i\}=d_{i}\text{ for all }i\in[n]\}

and note that
\psi_{n}|_{W_{n,\bm{d}}}
bijects to labelled rooted trees with out-degree sequence
\bm{d}
(i.e. such that the vertex with label
i
has out-degree
d_{i}
).

We observe that revealing entries in the codeword one at a time gives a way to grow a tree. This is a deterministic construction, but gives a nice way to grow uniformly random trees with a given degree sequence using the fact that the entries of a uniform element of
W_{n,\bm{d}}
(or indeed
[n]^{n-1}
, corresponding to uniformly random trees with no restrictions on individual degrees) have a relatively simple law. Here is an explicit description of this growth process: throughout, we keep track of an ‘active vertex’ where we expand the tree. In the first step, we reveal the root (whose label is
w_{1}
) and mark it as the active vertex. At step
k\geq 2
, we reveal the
k
th entry
w_{k}
in the codeword, and

(i)

if this label has not yet appeared in the tree, we add a new vertex labelled
w_{k}
, connect it to the active vertex, and make
w_{k}
the new active vertex;

(ii)

if the label has previously appeared in the codeword, we add a new vertex, whose label is the lowest index not yet present in the tree, connect it to the active vertex, and then make
w_{k}
the new active vertex;

(iii)

if the label has previously appeared in the tree but not in the codeword, we add a new vertex exactly as in case (ii), then make
w_{k}
the new active vertex.

In this sense, we have a discrete analogue of the line-breaking construction for CRTs: if we think of repeated labels as rare events, then most of the time we are growing a branch, occasionally interrupting the process to move to a new position and start growing a new branch.

3.2
Relation to Bienaymé trees

We now consider randomising the degree sequence
\bm{d}
, and conditionally given
\bm{d}
sampling a uniform element of
W_{n,\bm{d}}
; this will turn out to give a construction of (a labelled, unordered version of) a Bienaymé tree conditioned to have fixed size
n
.

Let
\xi
be a random variable taking values in
\mathbb{N}_{0}
and such that
\mathbb{E}\left[\xi\right]=1
; then
\xi
is the offspring distribution of a critical Bienaymé tree. For simplicity we shall assume that
\gcd\{k:\mathbb{P}\left(\xi=k\right)>0\}=1
. We consider sampling a random vector
\bm{D}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
with the distribution of
n
i.i.d. copies of
\xi
conditioned to have sum
n-1
. Now conditionally given
\bm{D}
, we sample a uniformly random
\bm{w}\in W_{n,\bm{D}}
, and use the bijection
\psi_{n}
to map it to a rooted labelled tree
(T^{n},\rho)
.

We prefer to work with ordered trees, so we use the following random procedure to convert our labelled tree into an ordered tree. First, we map the root
\rho
to
\varnothing
. Now for any vertex
i
of
T^{n}
that is not a leaf, we identify its children and give them a uniformly random ordering, independently of everything constructed so far. Finally, we forget our original labelling. We claim that this gives a conditioned Bienaymé tree with offspring distribution
\xi
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The ordered tree corresponding to
(T^{n},\rho)
has the law of a Bienaymé tree with offspring distribution
\xi
conditioned to have
n
vertices.

Proof.

Fix a rooted ordered tree
t
of size
n
; we study its probability of arising from our construction. It will be helpful to consider the rooted labelled ordered tree
\widetilde{t}
that arises in the process just before forgetting the labelling: there are
n!
possibilities for
\tilde{t}
that give rise to the final tree
t
, so we study the probability that
\tilde{t}
arises instead.

Consider traversing
\tilde{t}
in lexicographical order: let
d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{n}
be the out-degrees of the vertices in this order (so, for example,
d_{1}
is the degree of the root, regardless of the labelling). Let
\sigma
be the permutation of
[n]
such that, for all
i
, the
i
th vertex in lexicographical order has label
\sigma(i)
in
\tilde{t}
. We observe that our construction generates
\tilde{t}
if and only if all three of the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)

The degree sequence
\bm{D}
is chosen so that
D_{i}=d_{\sigma^{-1}(i)}
for all
i
;

(2)

The rooted labelled tree generated by the codeword matches
\tilde{t}
without its ordering;

(3)

The randomly chosen ordering on the labelled tree matches the ordering of
\tilde{t}
.

Condition (1) occurs with probability

\mathbb{P}\left(D_{1}=d_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},\dots,D_{n}=d_{\sigma^{-1}(n)}\right)=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{d_{\sigma^{-1}(i)}}}{\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}=n-1\right)}=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{d_{i}}}{\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}=n-1\right)}.

Given that condition (1) occurs, the codeword is chosen uniformly from a set of size
\binom{n-1}{d_{1}\dots d_{n}}
, and exactly one of these codewords produces the correct labelled tree, so we meet condition (2) with probability
\binom{n-1}{d_{1}\dots d_{n}}^{-1}
. Given that conditions (1) and (2) are met, the ordering matches iff, for each
i
, the permutation of children of the vertex with label
i
matches the ordering in
\tilde{t}
. As the vertex with label
i
has
d_{\sigma^{-1}(i)}
children, the probability that all sets of children are correctly ordered is

\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}d_{\sigma^{-1}(i)}!}=\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}!}.

Putting everything together, the probability that we generate
\tilde{t}
is

\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{d_{i}}}{\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}=n-1\right)}\times\binom{n-1}{d_{1}\dots d_{n}}^{-1}\times\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}!}=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{d_{i}}}{(n-1)!\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}=n-1\right)},

and this quantity is the same for all
\tilde{t}
which produce
t
upon forgetting the ordering. Thus the probability of generating
t
is
n!
times this, which is

\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{d_{i}}}{\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}=n-1\right)}.

By the cycle lemma, the denominator is equal to the probability that a random walk with steps
\xi-1
reaches
-1
for the first time at time
n
, which in turn is the probability that a Bienaymé tree with offspring distribution
\xi
has size
n
. The numerator is the probability of
t
arising from an unconditioned Bienaymé tree, completing the proof.
∎

Let us return to our picture of growing trees one vertex at a time: instead of revealing the entire degree sequence in the beginning, we can consider only revealing degrees of vertices the first time we encounter them. To this end, it is useful to reorder
\bm{D}
based on first appearances of the corresponding labels in the codeword (with all the zero entries appearing at the end, as there are no leaves in the codeword). Formally, letting
M\leq n-1
be the number of distinct entries in the codeword, we define indices
I_{1},I_{2},\dots,I_{M}
inductively by
I_{1}=1
and

I_{k+1}=\min\{i:w_{i}\not\in\{w_{I_{1}},w_{I_{2}},\dots,w_{I_{k}}\}\}.

Then we define an injection
\Sigma:[M]\to[n]
by
\Sigma(k)=w_{I_{k}}
and set

\hat{\bm{D}}=(\hat{D}_{1},\hat{D}_{2},\dots,\hat{D}_{n})=(D_{\Sigma(1)},\dots,D_{\Sigma(M)},0,\dots,0).

Note that
\bm{D}
has
M
nonzero entries which are precisely the first
M
entries of
\hat{\bm{D}}
.

We observe the following property of the reordering
\hat{\bm{D}}
, which we will not prove as it is straightforward:

Proposition 3.2
.

\hat{\bm{D}}
is a size-biased random reordering of
\bm{D}
: that is, conditionally given
\bm{D}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\Sigma(1)=\sigma(1),\dots,\Sigma(k)=\sigma(k)\ \Big|\ \bm{D}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{D_{\sigma(i)}}{n-1-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}D_{\sigma(j)}}

for any
k\leq M
.

Propositions
3.1
and
3.2
show that, provided we have a way to sample
\hat{\bm{D}}
directly, we can run the following algorithm to generate an unlabelled, unordered conditioned Bienaymé tree. (We note that this procedure is also used in
[
2
]
to study Bienaymé trees in the case that
\xi
has finite variance.) Once again we keep track of an active vertex, which we will call
V_{k}
. We will also draw half-edges connected to some of our vertices. Let
H_{k}
be the set of half-edges after the
k
th step. Most of the interesting behaviour of our algorithm is captured by
V_{k}
and
H_{k}
, but for technical reasons we must also consider a set of ‘dormant’ vertices, denoted
Z_{k}
. These correspond to vertices created at the ends of branches, whose labels do not show up in the codeword. We will arrange that the active vertex is never dormant.

We begin with the root as our only vertex, which is marked as active, and with no half-edges or dormant vertices. That is,
V_{0}=\rho
,
H_{0}=\varnothing
and
Z_{0}=\varnothing
. For
k=1,2,\dots
the
k
th step consists of the following:

•

Sample a random element
X_{k}
from
H_{k-1}\cup Z_{k-1}\cup\{V_{k-1}\}
, according to the following rules:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}=e\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n-k}\quad\text{ for all }e\in H_{k-1},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}=u\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n-k-\#H_{k-1}}{(n-k)(n-k+\#Z_{k-1})}\quad\text{ for all }u\in Z_{k-1},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}=V_{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n-k-\#H_{k-1}}{n-k+\#Z_{k-1}}.

(It is easy to see that these quantities sum to 1, so for
X_{k}
to have a well-defined law we only need to check that each quantity is non-negative. For this it is sufficient to show
\#H_{k-1}\leq n-k
, a fact we will show later.)

•

(Branching event) If
X_{k}=e\in H_{k-1}
is a half-edge, then we complete this half-edge by creating a new vertex
v
and connecting it to
e
. The previously active vertex
V_{k-1}
becomes dormant, and the new vertex becomes the active vertex, i.e.
V_{k}=v
;

•

(Growth event) If
X_{k}=V_{k-1}
, we sample the next entry of
\hat{\bm{D}}
, say
\hat{D}_{i}
. We will show that this is always at least 1. Assuming this for now, we add
\hat{D}_{i}-1
half-edges to
V_{k-1}
, then add one full edge by creating a new vertex
v
. The dormant set is unchanged from step
k-1
;

•

(Activation event) If
X_{k}=u\in Z_{k-1}
is a dormant vertex, we sample the next entry of
\hat{\bm{D}}
, say
\hat{D}_{i}
. Once again, we assume for now that
\hat{D}_{i}\geq 1
. We add
\hat{D}_{i}-1
half-edges to
u
, then add one full edge by creating a new vertex
v
. The previously active vertex
V_{k-1}
becomes dormant,
u
ceases to be dormant and the new vertex
v
becomes the active vertex.

To motivate the choices for the law of
X_{k}
, observe that the process
\#Z_{k}
increases by one in every branching event and stays constant in growth and activation events. Thus
\#Z_{k}
is equal to the number of repeats among the first
k
entries of the codeword, and so
n-k+\#Z_{k}
is the number of unused labels in the codeword. Conditionally given that a branching event does not occur (and the probability of this is
\frac{n-k-\#H_{k-1}}{n-k}
), any given dormant vertex becomes active iff the entry in the underlying codeword matches that of the dormant vertex. This codeword entry is conditionally uniform on the set of unused labels in the codeword so far, and so the conditional probability is
\frac{1}{n-k+\#Z_{k}}
.

The following calculation shows both that
X_{k}
has a valid law and that the sampled
\hat{D}_{i}
in any growth event is at least 1: among the first
k-1
steps of the algorithm,
\#Z_{k-1}
of them are branching events, and the other
k-1-\#Z_{k-1}
are growth or activation events. Each branching event removes a half-edge, while each growth or activation event samples the next entry
\hat{D}_{i}
of
\hat{\bm{D}}
and adds
\hat{D}_{i}-1
half-edges. It follows that
\#H_{k-1}=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1-\#Z_{k-1}}(\hat{D}_{j}-1)-\#Z_{k-1}
. In particular,

\frac{n-k-\#H_{k-1}}{n-k}=\frac{n-1-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1-\#Z_{k-1}}\hat{D}_{j}}{n-k}\geq 0,

and since
k-1-\#Z_{k-1}
is the number of entries of
\hat{\bm{D}}
revealed so far, equality holds iff we have already revealed all nonzero entries of
\hat{\bm{D}}
. Thus, conditional on the first
k-1
steps, a growth event at step
k
has positive probability iff there are still positive entries in
\hat{\bm{D}}
to reveal.

Note that
\#Z_{k}\leq k
, and so if
k\ll n
the probability of activation events is negligible. In fact, for
m\ll\sqrt{n}
we see that with high probability no activation events occur during the first
m
steps of the process. For this reason, it will turn out that we can usually work on the event that no activation events occur on the time intervals we are interested in.

3.3
Relation to size-biased distributions

In light of the previous section, we seek to understand the law of the size-biased random reordering
\hat{\bm{D}}
. We will show that this vector is related by a measure change to an i.i.d. sequence
\xi^{*}
of random variables with the size-biased distribution: that is,
\xi_{1}^{*},\xi_{2}^{*},\dots,\xi_{n}^{*}
are i.i.d. with law

\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{1}^{*}=k\right)=\frac{k\mathbb{P}\left(\xi=k\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[\xi\right]}=k\mathbb{P}\left(\xi=k\right).

We will mostly be interested in the first
m
entries of these vectors, where
m=o(n)
as
n\to\infty
. We can only relate the laws on the event
\{N^{n}\geq m\}
, where
N^{n}
is the number of non-zero entries of
\hat{\bm{D}}
, but in our case this event has probability tending to 1. In what follows, write
\Xi_{n}
to denote a sum of
n
i.i.d. copies of
\xi
(note that this is a sum of non-size-biased offspring distributions).

Proposition 3.3
.

For
1\leq m<n
and any bounded continuous test function
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[f(\hat{D}_{1},\dots,\hat{D}_{m})\mathbbm{1}\left(N^{n}\geq m\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[f(\xi_{1}^{*},\dots,\xi_{m}^{*})\Theta_{m}^{n}(\xi_{1}^{*},\dots,\xi_{m}^{*})\right]

where
\Theta_{m}^{n}
is the function given by

\Theta_{m}^{n}(k_{1},\dots,k_{m})=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n-m}=n-1-\sum_{i=1}^{m}k_{i}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n}=n-1\right)}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(\frac{n-i+1}{n-1-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}k_{j}}\right).

Proof.

By linearity it is enough to consider the case
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=\mathbbm{1}\left(x_{1}=r_{1},\dots,x_{m}=r_{m}\right)
, where
r_{1},\dots,r_{m}
are all at least 1 and sum to at most
n-1
. For such a choice of
f
we have

\mathbb{E}\left[f(\hat{D}_{1},\dots,\hat{D}_{m})\mathbbm{1}\left(N^{n}\geq m\right)\right]=\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{D}_{1}=r_{1},\dots,\hat{D}_{m}=r_{m}\right)

and

\mathbb{E}\left[f(\xi_{1}^{*},\dots,\xi_{m}^{*})\Theta_{m}^{n}(\xi_{1}^{*},\dots,\xi_{m}^{*})\right]=\Theta_{m}^{n}(r_{1},\dots,r_{m})\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{1}^{*}=r_{1},\dots,\xi_{m}^{*}=r_{m}\right).

The event
A=\{\hat{D}_{1}=r_{1},\dots,\hat{D}_{m}=r_{m}\}
occurs iff there is an
m
-tuple
\bm{i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})
of distinct indices in
[n]
such that

D_{i_{k}}=r_{k}\text{ for }1\leq k\leq m\quad\text{ and }\quad\Sigma(k)=i_{k}\text{ for }1\leq k\leq m.

Let us call this event
A_{\bm{i}}
. Note that the events
(A_{\bm{i}})
are disjoint, and so
\mathbb{P}\left(A\right)=\sum_{\bm{i}}\mathbb{P}\left(A_{\bm{i}}\right)
. The number of valid index tuples is
\frac{n!}{(n-m)!}
, and by exchangeability the corresponding events all have the same probability. Thus we can consider a fixed index tuple
\bm{i}^{*}=(1,2,\dots,m)
, and now
\mathbb{P}\left(A\right)=\frac{n!}{(n-m)!}\mathbb{P}\left(A_{\bm{i}^{*}}\right)
. For brevity, we will write
A^{*}=A_{\bm{i}^{*}}
.

For the event
A^{*}
to occur, we first require that
D_{1}=r_{1},\dots,D_{m}=r_{m}
and the remaining
n-m
entries sum to
n-1-\sum_{j=1}^{m}r_{j}
. This occurs with probability

\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n-m}=n-1-\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n}=n-1\right)}\prod_{i=1}^{m}p_{r_{i}}.

Conditionally given this, the probability that
\Sigma(1)=1,\dots,\Sigma(m)=m
is
\prod_{i=1}^{m}\frac{r_{i}}{n-1-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}r_{j}}
by
3.2
. It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(A\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n!}{(n-m)!}\cdot\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n-m}=n-1-\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n}=n-1\right)}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{m}p_{r_{i}}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{m}\frac{r_{i}}{n-1-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}r_{j}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{m}(n-i+1)\cdot\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n-m}=n-1-\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n}=n-1\right)}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{m}(r_{i}p_{r_{i}})\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(\frac{1}{n-1-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}r_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n-m}=n-1-\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n}=n-1\right)}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(\frac{n-i+1}{n-1-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}r_{j}}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{m}(r_{i}p_{r_{i}})

\displaystyle=\Theta_{m}^{n}(r_{1},\dots,r_{m})\mathbb{P}\left(\xi_{1}^{*}=r_{1},\dots,\xi_{m}^{*}=r_{m}\right),

completing the proof.
∎

This result gives us a general strategy to study expectations and probabilities associated to our discrete line-breaking construction: first, we use the tower law to reduce the problem to studying a conditional expectation given
\hat{\bm{D}}
. Then we observe that this conditional expectation is some (deterministic) function of
\hat{\bm{D}}
. Finally, using the measure change, we replace
\hat{\bm{D}}
with an i.i.d. sequence of size-biased distributions and insert a factor of
\Theta_{m}^{n}
into the expectation.

As an example, consider the length
Y_{1}^{n}
of the first stick in the construction. (We define the length of a stick to mean the number of edges it contains). We note that
Y_{1}^{n}=C_{1}-1
, so in particular the first stick has length at least
k
(i.e. it consists of
k+1
vertices and
k
edges) iff the first
k
steps in the algorithm are all growth events. Conditionally given
\hat{\bm{D}}
, we see

•

The first step is a growth event with probability 1;

•

Given that the first
j-1
steps are all growth events, we have
\#H_{j-1}=\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}(\hat{D}_{i}-1)
, and so the
j
th step is also a growth event with probability
1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}(\hat{D}_{i}-1)}{n-j}
.

It follows that

\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{1}^{n}\geq k\ \Big|\ \hat{\bm{D}}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}(\hat{D}_{i}-1)}{n-j}\right)

Taking any
m\geq k
, and setting
f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m})=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}(x_{i}-1)}{n-j}\right)
, the measure change result applied to
f
implies that

\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{1}^{n}\geq k,N^{n}\geq m\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}(\xi_{i}^{*}-1)}{n-j}\right)\Theta_{m}^{n}(\xi_{1}^{*},\dots,\xi_{m}^{*})\right].

3.4
Heuristics for scaling limits in the
\alpha
-stable case

In the next section, we will give a full proof of
Theorem 1.1
. We will sketch a proof here in the more restrictive setting where
\mathbb{P}\left(\xi=k\right)\sim Ck^{-\alpha-1}
for some constant
C>0
. Note that then
\xi^{*}
is such that
\mathbb{P}\left(\xi^{*}=k\right)\sim Ck^{-\alpha}
. Following the strategy outlined in the previous section, we seek limits in distribution for various functions involving
\xi^{*}
. Let us begin with the measure change
\Theta_{m}^{n}=\Theta_{m}^{n}(\xi_{1}^{*},\dots,\xi_{m}^{*})
. First we should determine how
m
should grow with
n
. We can study the probability

\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n-m}=n-1-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}^{*}\ \Big|\ \xi^{*}\right)

to get an idea: assuming
m\ll n
we know that
\frac{\Xi_{n-m}-n+m}{n^{1/\alpha}}
converges in distribution to a spectrally positive
\alpha
-stable random variable
L_{1}
, say with density
p
. (Note that this may not be quite the same
L_{1}
defined in
Section 1
— there may be a constant scaling involved. We will deal with this more precisely later). We may write

\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n-m}=n-1-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}^{*}\,\Bigg|\,\xi^{*}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\Xi_{n-m}-n+m}{n^{1/\alpha}}=-\frac{1}{n^{1/\alpha}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\xi_{i}^{*}-1)-\frac{1}{n^{1/\alpha}}\,\Bigg|\,\xi^{*}\right)

and now, by the local limit theorem, if
\frac{1}{n^{1/\alpha}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\xi_{i}^{*}-1)
converges in distribution to some random variable
Z
, then the above probability is asymptotic to
n^{-1/\alpha}p(-Z)
. Meanwhile the probability
\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n}=n-1\right)
is asymptotic to
n^{-1/\alpha}p(0)
, and so the ratio converges to
p(-Z)/p(0)
.

We know that
k^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor tk\rfloor}(\xi_{i}^{*}-1)
converges in distribution as
k\to\infty
to
\sigma_{t}
, an
(\alpha-1)
-stable subordinator (again possibly scaled by a constant). Thus we should choose
m=\lfloor tk\rfloor
, where
k=k(n)
is such that
k^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}=n^{1/\alpha}
. That is,
k=n^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}
, and
m=\lfloor tn^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\rfloor
. Varying
t
will allow us to consider growing trees over time.

With this choice of
m
, let us estimate the product in
\Theta_{m}^{n}
as

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{\lfloor tn^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\rfloor}\left(\frac{n-i+1}{n-1-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\xi_{j}^{*}}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{\lfloor tn^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\rfloor}\left(1-\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}(\xi_{j}^{*}-1)+1}{n-i+1}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor tn^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\rfloor}\log\left(1-\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}(\xi_{j}^{*}-1)+1}{n-i+1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\approx\exp\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor tn^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\rfloor}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}(\xi_{j}^{*}-1)\right)

\displaystyle\approx\exp\left(\frac{1}{n^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor tn^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\rfloor}\left(\frac{1}{n^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}(\xi_{j}^{*}-1)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\approx\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,ds\right).

Putting everything together, we expect that

\Theta_{\lfloor tn^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\rfloor}^{n}\xrightarrow{d}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,ds\right)\frac{p(-\sigma_{t})}{p(0)}.

We will prove a slightly stronger statement in the next section. As the process

t\mapsto\frac{1}{n^{1/\alpha}}\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor tn^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\rfloor}(\xi_{i}^{*}-1)

converges in distribution to
\sigma_{t}
, we then expect the process given by

t\mapsto\frac{1}{n^{1/\alpha}}\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor tn^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\rfloor}(\hat{D_{i}}-1)

to converge to the measure-changed version
\widetilde{\sigma}
of
\sigma
.

Assuming that branching events are rare in the discrete process, the partial sums of
\hat{\bm{D}}
approximately give the number of free half-edges attached to the subtree already created at each step of the construction. Near the beginning of the process, the probability of a branching event is roughly
1/n
times this number of free half-edges, and so
\hat{\bm{D}}
is playing an analogous role to the intensity function in a line-breaking construction. Thus,
\widetilde{\sigma}
acts as the intensity for a line-breaking construction that yields the
\alpha
-stable tree. Indeed, looking back to our first stick length example, if we take
k=\lfloor sn^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\rfloor
for some
s\leq t
, then we see

\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{1}^{n}\geq\lfloor sn^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\rfloor,N^{n}\geq\lfloor tn^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\rfloor\right)\\
=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{\lfloor sn^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\rfloor}\left(1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}(\xi_{i}^{*}-1)}{n-j}\right)\Theta_{\lfloor tn^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\rfloor}^{n}\left(\xi_{1}^{*},\dots,\xi_{\lfloor tn^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\rfloor}^{*}\right)\right]

and by similar calculations to the ones used for
\Theta_{m}^{n}
, the product term should converge to
\exp(-\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{s}\,ds)
. As the
\Theta
term converges to the measure change, the limit of this expectation should be
\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(-\int_{0}^{s}\widetilde{\sigma}_{s}\,ds)\right]
, which is exactly the probability that the first stick has length
\geq s
in the line-breaking construction driven by
\widetilde{\sigma}
.

4
Convergence of discrete trees: a new proof of
Theorem 1.1

Our goal in this section is to show that the discrete trees introduced in
Section 3
converge on rescaling to the continuum tree
(\mathcal{T},d,\mu)
introduced in
Section 1
, thus giving a proof of
Theorem 1.1
.

Recall that we assume the offspring distribution
\xi
is in the domain of attraction of a stable law of index
\alpha
. As in the previous section, we let
\Xi_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}
, where
\xi_{i}
are i.i.d. copies of
\xi
. By Theorem 1 of Kortchemski
[
33
]
, there exists an increasing sequence
a_{n}>0
, regularly varying of index
1/\alpha
, such that
\frac{\Xi_{n}-n}{a_{n}}
converges in distribution to
L_{1}
, where
L
is the spectrally positive
\alpha
-stable Lévy process, and moreover we have the local limit theorem

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|a_{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n}-n=k\right)-p\left(\frac{k}{a_{n}}\right)\right|=0,

where
p
is the density of
L_{1}
. We can also obtain a scaling limit for the random walk with jumps following the size-biased distribution
\xi^{*}
. In what follows, let
m_{n}=n/a_{n}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

For any
t>0
, we have the convergence in distribution

\frac{1}{a_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor}\xi_{i}^{*}\overset{d}{\to}\sigma_{t},

where
\sigma
is an
(\alpha-1)
-stable subordinator with Lévy measure
C_{\alpha}x^{-\alpha}\,dx
,
x>0
. (Note that
C_{\alpha}=\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}
is the same constant appearing in the Lévy measure of
L
. In particular, we have
\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\lambda\sigma_{t}}\right]=\exp(-t\alpha\lambda^{\alpha-1}).
)

Proof.

As
\xi
is in the domain of attraction of an
\alpha
-stable law, there exists a slowly varying function
l(t)
such that
\mathbb{P}\left(\xi\geq t\right)=l(t)t^{-\alpha}
for all
t>0
. By the proof used in Theorem 3.8.2 in
[
25
]
, we also know
n\mathbb{P}\left(\xi\geq xa_{n}\right)\to\int_{x}^{\infty}Ct^{-\alpha-1}\,dt=\frac{C}{\alpha}x^{-\alpha}
for any fixed
x
. Combining these results, we deduce that

l(xa_{n})\sim\frac{Ca_{n}^{\alpha}}{\alpha n}

as
n\to\infty
with
x
fixed. Now we may calculate

\mathbb{P}\left(\xi^{*}\geq t\right)=t\mathbb{P}\left(\xi\geq t\right)+\int_{t}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\xi\geq x\right)\,dx

and so by Karamata’s integral theorem (Proposition 1.5.10 of
[
15
]
) we have
\mathbb{P}\left(\xi^{*}\geq t\right)\sim\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}l(t)t^{-\alpha+1}
. In particular, for fixed
x
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\xi^{*}\geq xa_{n}\right)\sim\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}l(xa_{n})(xa_{n})^{-\alpha+1}\sim\frac{1}{m_{n}}\frac{C}{\alpha-1}x^{-\alpha+1}=\frac{1}{m_{n}}\int_{x}^{\infty}t^{-\alpha}\,dt

and so, following the proof in Theorem 3.8.2 in
[
25
]
, we deduce the desired convergence in distribution.
∎

We can extend this convergence result to a statement about convergence of càdlàg processes: we may define, for each
n
, a càdlàg process
\sigma^{n}(t)
such that

\sigma^{n}(t)\overset{d}{=}\frac{1}{a_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor}(\xi_{i}^{*}-1),\quad 0\leq t\leq T

and now
\sigma^{n}\overset{d}{\to}\sigma
as elements of the Skorokhod space
D([0,T])
. (We could equally have defined
\sigma^{n}
as a sum of
\xi_{i}^{*}
, without subtracting 1 from each, and obtained the same limit, but it is convenient to include the subtraction as it simplifies calculations in the rest of this section.) By the Skorokhod representation theorem we may construct
\sigma^{n}
(for all
n
) and
\sigma
on a common probability space such that
\sigma^{n}\to\sigma
a.s. (with convergence holding in the Skorokhod J1-metric). For the rest of this section we will work in this setting. It will be useful to name the relevant copies
\xi_{i}^{n,*}
of the size-biased distribution, so that

\sigma^{n}(t)=\frac{1}{a_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor}(\xi_{i}^{n,*}-1).

The following lemma on
N^{n}
, the number of vertices of out-degree at least 1, will be helpful when applying the measure change later:

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
T>0
we have
\mathbb{P}\left(N^{n}<\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor\right)\to 0
.

Proof.

If
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
are i.i.d. copies of the (non-size-biased) offspring distribution, then
N_{n}
has the law of
\#\{i\leq n:\xi_{i}\geq 1\}
conditional on
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}=n-1
. Thus we can crudely estimate

\mathbb{P}\left(N^{n}<\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\#\{i\leq n:\xi_{i}\geq 1\}<\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}=n-1\right)}.

By construction
m_{n}\ll n
so the numerator decays exponentially in
n
by e.g. Chernoff bounds for binomial distributions, while the denominator only decays polynomially. Thus we have a bound tending to 0.
∎

4.1
Convergence of the measure change

The key result in this section follows.

Theorem 4.3
.

Fix a finite time horizon
T>0
. Set
M^{n}_{t}=\Theta_{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor}^{n}(\xi_{1}^{*},\dots,\xi_{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor}^{*})
for
0\leq t\leq T
, and let
M_{t}=\exp(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,ds)p(-\sigma_{t})/p(0)
. Then
M^{n}\overset{d}{\to}M
in the Skorokhod space
D([0,T])
.

In what follows, and in all subsequent proofs, we will abuse notation and write
\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n}=Z\right)
as a shorthand for the random variable
\Psi_{n}(Z)
, where
\Psi_{n}
is the probability mass function of
\Xi_{n}
. We can also view this as the conditional probability
\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n}=Z\ \Big|\ Z\right)
with
\Xi_{n}
taken to be independent of
Z
.

Proof.

As discussed earlier, we work in the setting that
\sigma^{n}
and
\sigma
are defined on a common space with
\sigma^{n}\to\sigma
a.s. Define

M_{t}^{n}=\Theta_{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor}^{n}\left(\xi_{1}^{n,*},\dots,\xi_{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor}^{n,*}\right);

we will show that
M^{n}\to M
a.s. in the Skorokhod metric.

We begin by handling the product term: we may write

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor}\left(\frac{n-i+1}{n-1-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\xi_{i}^{n,*}}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor}\left(1-\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}(\xi_{j}^{n,*}-1)-1}{n-i+1}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor}\log\left(1-\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}(\xi_{j}^{n,*}-1)-1}{n-i+1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{\frac{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor}{m_{n}}}m_{n}\log\left(1-\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor sm_{n}\rfloor}(\xi_{j}^{n,*}-1)-1}{n-\lfloor sm_{n}\rfloor}\right)\,ds\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{\frac{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor}{m_{n}}}m_{n}\log\left(1-\frac{a_{n}\sigma^{n}(s)-1}{n-\lfloor sm_{n}\rfloor}\right)\,ds\right).

We would like this to converge to
\exp(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,ds)
and so we will prove that the integrand converges to
-\sigma_{s}
a.s. (again, in the Skorokhod metric). As
\sigma^{n}\to\sigma
a.s. in the Skorokhod metric, it is enough to show that

\left(m_{n}\log\left(1-\frac{a_{n}\sigma^{n}(s)-1}{n-\lfloor sm_{n}\rfloor}\right)+\sigma^{n}(s)\right)_{s\in[0,T]}\to 0\quad\text{ a.s.\ in the Skorokhod metric,}

which amounts to showing uniform convergence to 0. This is easily shown using a Taylor expansion and the observation that, for any
s
,
\sigma^{n}(s)\to\sigma_{s}
a.s. and this quantity is at most
\sigma_{T}
. Now, as integration is a continuous operation on
D([0,T])
, we obtain

-\int_{0}^{t}m_{n}\log\left(1-\frac{a_{n}\sigma^{n}(s)-1}{n-\lfloor sm_{n}\rfloor}\right)\,ds\to\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,ds

almost surely, again with respect to the metric on
D([0,T])
. The left-hand side is not quite of the correct form as the upper limit of integration is slightly wrong (we have
t
instead of the correct
\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor/m_{n}
), but this is easily remedied by showing that the difference tends to 0 uniformly a.s. — that is,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\int_{\frac{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor}{m_{n}}}^{t}m_{n}\log\left(1-\frac{a_{n}\sigma^{n}(s)-1}{n-\lfloor sm_{n}\rfloor}\right)\,ds\right|\to 0\quad\text{ a.s.}

This is again a straightforward calculation, so we omit it. Now we finish by exponentiating (again a continuous operation on
D([0,T])
).

Now we can handle the ratio of probabilities: writing
k=k(t)=\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor
for brevity, we observe that

\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n-k}=n-1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\xi_{i}^{*}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n}=n-1\right)}=\frac{a_{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n-k}-(n-k)=-a_{n}\sigma^{n}(t)-1\right)}{a_{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n}-n=-1\right)}.

By a straightforward application of the local limit theorem the denominator (which is non-random) converges to
p(0)
.

We handle the numerator with another application of the local limit theorem: setting
S^{n}=\sup_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|a_{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n}-n=\ell\right)-p(\ell/a_{n})\right|\to 0
, we have

\left|a_{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n-k}-(n-k)=-a_{n}\sigma^{n}(t)-1\right)-\frac{a_{n}}{a_{n-k}}p\left(-\frac{a_{n}}{a_{n-k}}\sigma^{n}(t)-\frac{1}{a_{n-k}}\right)\right|\\
\leq\frac{a_{n}}{a_{n-k}}S^{n-k}\quad\leq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\{\frac{a_{n}}{a_{n-k(t)}}S^{n-k(t)}\right\},

and similarly

\left|\frac{a_{n}}{a_{n-k}}p\left(-\frac{a_{n}}{a_{n-k}}\sigma^{n}(t)-\frac{1}{a_{n-k}}\right)-p\left(-\frac{a_{n}}{a_{n-k}}\sigma^{n}(t)-\frac{1}{a_{n-k}}\right)\right|\leq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\{\frac{a_{n}}{a_{n-k(t)}}-1\right\}\|p\|_{\infty}.

Hence the processes

\Bigg(a_{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\Xi_{n-k(t)}-(n-k(t))=-a_{n}\sigma^{n}(t)-1\right)\Bigg)_{t\in[0,T]}

and

\left(p\left(-\frac{a_{n}}{a_{n-k(t)}}\sigma^{n}(t)-\frac{1}{a_{n-k(t)}}\right)\right)_{t\in[0,T]}

are a.s. uniformly close to each other. Thus it suffices to show the latter converges a.s. to
p(-\sigma_{t})
, which is clear by continuity of
p
.
∎

Note that
\mathbb{E}\left[|M_{t}^{n}|\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[M_{t}^{n}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left(N^{n}\geq\lfloor Tn^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\rfloor\right)\to 1=\mathbb{E}\left[|M_{t}|\right]
, and so by Scheffé’s lemma we have
\mathbb{E}\left[|M_{n}^{t}-M_{t}|\right]\to 0
in the setting that everything is defined on the same space.

With this convergence established, we can study limits of conditional probabilities given
\hat{\bm{D}}
by replacing the entries
\hat{D}_{i}
with i.i.d. entries
\xi_{i}^{*}
. In an abuse of notation, we will write
\mathbb{P}\left(\cdot\ \Big|\ \hat{\bm{D}}=\xi^{*}\right)
to denote this alternative setting.

The processes
t\mapsto\frac{1}{a_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor m_{n}t\rfloor}\hat{D}_{i}
are obtained as measure changes of
\sigma^{n}
by
M^{n}
. As we can construct a common space on which both
\sigma^{n}\to\sigma
and
M^{n}\to M
almost surely, we deduce that
\left(\frac{1}{a_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor m_{n}t\rfloor}\hat{D}_{i},t\leq T\right)\overset{d}{\to}\left(\widetilde{\sigma}_{t},t\leq T\right)
. As the time horizon
T
was arbitrary, we can extend this to a convergence result on
D([0,\infty))
:

Corollary 4.4
.

As
n\to\infty
,

\left(\frac{1}{a_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor m_{n}t\rfloor}\hat{D}_{i},t\geq 0\right)\overset{d}{\to}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{t},t\geq 0)

for the space of càdlàg functions
D([0,\infty))
endowed with the Skorokhod topology on
[0,\infty)
.

4.2
Convergence of random finite-dimensional distributions

Recall that
C_{1},C_{2},\dots
are the positions of repeated entries, i.e. times of branching events (but not activation events), in the codeword for the discrete construction. We will write
C_{1}^{n},C_{2}^{n},\dots
for clarity. We also introduce the attachment points
J_{1}^{n},J_{2}^{n},\dots
defined as follows: if the branching event at time
C_{i}^{n}
selects a half-edge with vertex
v
, then
J_{i}^{n}
is the first time that the label of
v
appears in the codeword. We aim to show that, for any
k\geq 1
,

\frac{1}{a_{n}}\left(C_{1}^{n},C_{2}^{n},\dots,C_{k}^{n},J_{1}^{n},\dots,J_{k}^{n}\right)\overset{d}{\to}(Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots,Y_{k},Z_{1},\dots,Z_{k})

where
Y_{i}
are the jump times of a Poisson process of intensity
\widetilde{\sigma}
, and conditionally given
\widetilde{\sigma}
and
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k})
, the random variables
(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{k})
are independent and
Z_{i}
has c.d.f.
\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{Y_{i}-}},t\in[0,Y_{i})
. Our strategy of proof is similar to that of Section 4 of
[
2
]
.

As alluded to before, it will be easier to work in the non-measure changed setting — we will show the following analogous result first:

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
k\geq 0
and let
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\leq T
be fixed. Define processes
Q^{n,k}_{t},Q^{k}_{t}
for
t\in[0,T]
by

Q_{t}^{n,k}=m_{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{J_{1}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq s_{1},\dots,\frac{J_{k}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq s_{k},\frac{C_{1}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq t_{1},\dots,\frac{C_{k}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq t_{k},C_{k+1}^{n}=\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor\ \Big|\ \hat{\bm{D}}=\xi^{*}\right),

and

Q_{t}^{k}=\sigma_{t-}\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{u}\,du\right)\int_{\prod_{i=1}^{k}[0,t_{i}]}\mathbbm{1}\left(u_{1}<u_{2}<\dots<u_{k}<t\right)\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(\sigma_{u_{i}^{-}}\wedge\sigma_{s_{i}}\right)\,du_{1}\dots du_{k},

with the convention that the product integral in
Q_{t}^{0}
is equal to 1. Then
Q^{n,k}\overset{d}{\to}Q^{k}
in
D([0,T])
.

Before we prove this lemma, we give some motivation for choosing this statement to prove. If
\eta_{1}<\eta_{2}<\dots
are the jump times of a Poisson process of intensity
\sigma
, then we know

\mathbb{P}\left(\eta_{1}\in du_{1},\dots,\eta_{k}\in du_{k}\,\big|\,\sigma\right)=\mathbbm{1}\left(u_{1}<\dots<u_{k}\right)\sigma_{u_{1}}\dots\sigma_{u_{k}}\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{u_{k}}\sigma_{r}\,dr\right)\,du_{1}\dots du_{k}.

Now adding in attachment points
\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\dots
, again such that conditionally given
\sigma
and
(\eta_{j})_{j\geq 1}
, the random variables
(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\ldots)
are independent and
\zeta_{i}
has c.d.f.
\sigma_{t}/\sigma_{\eta_{i}^{-}},t\in[0,\eta_{i})
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(\eta_{1}\in du_{1},\dots,\eta_{k}\in du_{k},\zeta_{1}\leq s_{1},\dots,\zeta_{k-1}\leq s_{k-1}\,\big|\,\sigma\right)\\
=\mathbbm{1}\left(u_{1}<\dots<u_{k}\right)\sigma_{u_{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\left(\left(\sigma_{s_{i}}\wedge\sigma_{u_{i}^{-}}\right)\frac{\sigma_{u_{i}}}{\sigma_{u_{i}^{-}}}\right)\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{u_{k}}\sigma_{r}\,dr\right)\,du_{1}\dots du_{k}.

Replacing
k
by
k+1
, renaming
u_{k+1}
to
t
and integrating over
0\leq u_{i}\leq t_{i},1\leq i\leq k
yields

\mathbb{P}\left(\eta_{1}\leq t_{1},\dots,\eta_{k}\leq t_{k},\eta_{k+1}\in dt,\zeta_{1}\leq s_{1},\dots,\zeta_{k}\leq s_{k}\right)\\
=dt\,\sigma_{t}\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{u}\,du\right)\int_{\prod_{i=1}^{k}[0,t_{i}]}\mathbbm{1}\left(u_{1}<\dots<u_{k}<t\right)\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(\left(\sigma_{s_{i}}\wedge\sigma_{u_{i}^{-}}\right)\frac{\sigma_{u_{i}}}{\sigma_{u_{i}^{-}}}\right)\,du_{1}\dots du_{k}

which is almost in the form of
Q_{t}^{k}\,dt
, except for the factors of
\sigma_{u_{i}}/\sigma_{u_{i}^{-}}
in the integral. However,
\sigma
has countably many discontinuities, and so
\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\sigma_{u_{i}}}{\sigma_{u_{i}^{-}}}=1
Lebesgue-almost everywhere on
\prod_{i=1}^{k}[0,t_{i}]
. Thus we have exactly

\mathbb{P}\left(\eta_{1}\leq t_{1},\dots,\eta_{k}\leq t_{k},\eta_{k+1}\in[t,t+dt),\zeta_{1}\leq s_{1},\dots,\zeta_{k}\leq s_{k}\right)=Q_{t}^{k}\,dt.

We will need the following analytic lemma in our proof.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
f_{n},\tilde{f}_{n},g_{n},\tilde{g}_{n}
be (deterministic) càdlàg functions
[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
. Suppose that

(a)

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|f_{n}(t)-\tilde{f}_{n}(t)|\to 0
as
n\to\infty
;

(b)

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|g_{n}(t)-\tilde{g}_{n}(t)|\to 0
as
n\to\infty
;

(c)

There exists a constant
C>0
such that
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\tilde{f}_{n}(t)|\leq C
and
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\tilde{g}_{n}(t)|\leq C
for all
n
sufficiently large.

Then

1.

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big|f_{n}(t)g_{n}(t)-\tilde{f}_{n}(t)\tilde{g}_{n}(t)\big|\to 0
as
n\to\infty
;

2.

There exists a constant
C^{\prime}>0
such that all of
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|f_{n}(t)|
,
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|g_{n}(t)|
and
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\tilde{f}_{n}(t)\tilde{g}_{n}(t)|
are at most
C^{\prime}
.

We do not prove
4.6
as it is straightforward, but we will make use of it extensively to prove
4.5
.

Proof of
4.5
.

We will use the Skorokhod representation again and prove almost sure convergence in the Skorokhod metric. We introduce auxiliary processes
R_{t}^{n,k}
, defined analogously to
Q_{t}^{k}
but with
\sigma
replaced by
\sigma^{n}
. With this we know
R^{n,k}\to Q^{k}
in the Skorokhod metric (using the same time change that gives
\sigma^{n}\to\sigma
), and so it is enough to show that as
n\to\infty
, the difference between
Q_{t}^{n,k}
and
R_{t}^{n,k}
tends to 0 uniformly in
t
. We seek to prove this stronger convergence result by induction on
k
.

Dealing with activation events precisely requires handling a great number of cases, but we can exploit the fact that these events are rare in order to effectively ignore them. More precisely, define an auxiliary process
\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{n,k}
by

\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{n,k}=m_{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{J_{1}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq s_{1},\dots,\frac{J_{k}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq s_{k},\frac{C_{1}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq t_{1},\dots,\frac{C_{k}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq t_{k},C_{k+1}^{n}=\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor,\mathcal{E}^{n,k}\ \Big|\ \hat{\bm{D}}=\xi^{*}\right)

where we define the event

\mathcal{E}^{n,k}=\{\text{there are no activation events among the first }\min\{\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor,C_{k+1}^{n}\}\text{ steps}\}.

Note that
1-\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}^{n,k}\right)\leq\frac{(k+1)Tm_{n}}{n-\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor}
for all
n
and
k
, so for all
n
and all
k
we almost surely have the estimate

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{n,k}-Q_{t}^{n,k}\right|\leq O\left(\frac{m_{n}^{2}}{n}\right)=o(1).

We deduce that, for any fixed
k
,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|Q_{t}^{n,k}-R_{t}^{n,k}\right|\to 0\text{ a.s.\ }\iff\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{n,k}-R_{t}^{n,k}\right|\to 0\text{ a.s.\ }.

First consider the case
k=0
, so that

\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{n,0}=m_{n}\mathbb{P}\left(C_{1}^{n}=\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor,\mathcal{E}^{n,0}\ \Big|\ \hat{\bm{D}^{n}}=\xi^{n,*}\right).

The event
\{C_{1}^{n}=l\}\cap\mathcal{E}^{n,0}
occurs if and only if the first
l-1
steps are all growth events and step
l
is a branching event. We know that conditionally given that among the first
j-1
steps there have been
b\geq 0
branching events and no activation events, at step
j
we:

•

branch with probability
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{j-1-b}(\xi_{i}^{n,*}-1)-b}{n-j}
;

•

grow with probability
1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{j-1-b}(\xi_{i}^{n,*}-1)}{n-j+b}.

It follows that

\mathbb{P}\left(C_{1}^{n}=\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor,\mathcal{E}^{n,0}\ \Big|\ \hat{\bm{D}^{n}}=\xi^{n,*}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor-1}\left(1-\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}(\xi_{j}^{n,*}-1)}{n-i}\right)\times\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor-1}(\xi_{j}^{n,*}-1)}{n-\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor}.

By an argument very similar to the one in
Theorem 4.3
, the product is uniformly close to
\exp(-\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{n}(s)\,ds)
. In addition, as
m_{n}/n=1/a_{n}
we have

m_{n}\times\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor-1}(\xi_{j}^{n,*}-1)}{n-\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor}-\sigma^{n}(t-)\to 0

uniformly. Both
\exp(-\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{n}(s)\,ds)
and
\sigma^{n}(t-)
are uniformly bounded in
n
(as they have càdlàg limits in the Skorokhod space), so we may apply
4.6
to get uniform closeness of the products. This establishes almost sure uniform closeness between
\widetilde{Q}^{n,0}
and
R^{0}
, hence also uniform closeness between
Q^{n,0}
and
R^{0}
.

Now assume
k\geq 1
and the uniform closeness holds for the
(k-1)
-dimensional marginals. We observe that conditionally given
\hat{\bm{D}}=\xi^{*}
, all of
(C_{1}^{n},\dots,C_{k}^{n},J_{1}^{n},\dots,J_{k-1}^{n})
and that
\mathcal{E}^{n,k-1}
holds, the random variables
J_{k}^{n}
and
C_{k+1}^{n}
are independent. Letting
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{n}
be the
\sigma
-algebra associated to this conditioning, the conditional c.d.f. of
J_{k}^{n}
is

\mathbb{P}\left(J_{k}^{n}\leq r\ \Big|\ \mathcal{F}_{k}^{n}\right)=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{r-1-B(r)}(\xi^{n,*}_{i}-1)-B(r)}{\sum_{j=1}^{C^{n}_{k}-k}(\xi_{i}^{n,*}-1)-(k-1)},\quad 0\leq r\leq C^{n}_{k}-1

where
B(r)=B_{k-1}^{n}(r)=\#\{1\leq i\leq k-1:J_{i}^{n}\leq r\}
(note in particular that
B(C^{n}_{k}-1)=k-1
). We would like to say that this probability only depends on
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{n}
through
C_{k}^{n}
, but this is not quite true due to the appearance of
B(r)
, a random function defined in terms of
J_{1}^{n},\dots,J_{k-1}^{n}
. However, as
0\leq B(r)\leq k-1
for all
r
in the relevant range, we can essentially ignore it: more precisely, we have estimates

\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{r-k}(\xi^{n,*}_{j}-1)-(k-1)}{\sum_{j=1}^{C^{n}_{k}-k}(\xi_{j}^{n,*}-1)-(k-1)}\leq\mathbb{P}\left(J_{k}^{n}\leq r\ \Big|\ \mathcal{F}_{k}^{n}\right)\leq\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}(\xi^{n,*}_{j}-1)}{\sum_{j=1}^{C_{k}-k}(\xi_{j}^{n,*}-1)-(k-1)}

which depend on
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{n}
only through
C_{k}^{n}
. Thus for any nontrivial
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{n}
-measurable event
\mathcal{A}
with, say,
\mathcal{A}\subset\{C^{n}_{k}=l\}
, we have the almost sure bounds

\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{r-k}(\xi^{n,*}_{j}-1)-(k-1)}{\sum_{j=1}^{l-k}(\xi_{j}^{n,*}-1)-(k-1)}\leq\mathbb{P}\left(J_{k}^{n}\leq r\ \Big|\ \mathcal{A}\right)\leq\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}(\xi^{n,*}_{j}-1)}{\sum_{j=1}^{l-k}(\xi_{j}^{n,*}-1)-(k-1)}.

Importantly, this is true only for
0\leq r\leq l-1
; if
l\leq r
then the probability is trivially equal to 1.

Similarly, we can handle
C_{k+1}^{n}
conditionally given
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{n}
. Conditionally given that
\mathcal{E}^{n,k-1}
holds, we know that

C_{k+1}^{n}=r\text{ and }\mathcal{E}^{n,k}\text{ holds}\iff\\
\text{steps }C_{k}^{n}+1,\dots,r-1\text{ are all growth events and step }r\text{ is a branching event.}

This occurs with probability

\mathbb{P}\left(C_{k+1}^{n}=r,\mathcal{E}^{n,k}\ \Big|\ \mathcal{F}_{k}^{n}\right)=\prod_{i=C_{k}^{n}+1}^{r-1}\left(1-\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{i-1-k}(\xi_{j}^{n,*}-1)}{n-i+k}\right)\times\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{r-1-k}(\xi_{j}^{n,*}-1)-k}{n-r},

which depends on
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{n}
only through
C_{k}^{n}
. Thus for any
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{n}
-measurable event
\mathcal{A}\subset\{C_{k}^{n}=l\}
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(C_{k+1}^{n}=r,\mathcal{E}^{n,k}\ \Big|\ \mathcal{A}\right)=\prod_{i=l+1}^{r-1}\left(1-\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{i-1-k}(\xi_{j}^{n,*}-1)}{n-i+k}\right)\times\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{r-1-k}(\xi_{j}^{n,*}-1)-k}{n-r}.

We are now ready to prove the convergence of
\widetilde{Q}^{n,k}
: we will give almost sure upper and lower bounds for
\widetilde{Q}^{n,k}
which are both uniformly close to
R^{n,k}
. In what follows, it will be helpful to abbreviate

\mathcal{A}_{k-1}=\left\{\frac{J_{1}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq s_{1},\dots,\frac{J_{k-1}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq s_{k-1},\frac{C_{1}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq t_{1},\dots,\frac{C_{k-1}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq t_{k-1}\right\},

so that for example
\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{n,k-1}=m_{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}_{k-1},C^{n}_{k}=\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor,\mathcal{E}^{n,k-1}\ |\ \hat{\bm{D}}=\xi^{*}\right)
. To prove the upper bound, note

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{n,k}

\displaystyle=m_{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}_{k-1},J_{k}\leq\lfloor s_{k}m_{n}\rfloor,C^{n}_{k}\leq\lfloor t_{k}m_{n}\rfloor,C^{n}_{k+1}=\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor,\mathcal{E}^{n,k}\ \Big|\ \hat{\bm{D}}=\xi^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=m_{n}\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor t_{k}m_{n}\rfloor}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}_{k-1},C^{n}_{k}=l,J^{n}_{k}\leq\lfloor s_{k}m_{n}\rfloor,C^{n}_{k+1}=\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor,\mathcal{E}^{n,k}\ \Big|\ \hat{\bm{D}}=\xi^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=m_{n}\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor t_{k}m_{n}\rfloor}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}_{k-1},C^{n}_{k}=l,\mathcal{E}^{n,k-1}\ \Big|\ \hat{\bm{D}}=\xi^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\mathbb{P}\left(J^{n}_{k}\leq\lfloor s_{k}m_{n}\rfloor,C^{n}_{k+1}=\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor,\mathcal{E}^{n,k}\ \Big|\ \mathcal{A}_{k-1},C^{n}_{k}=l,\mathcal{E}^{n,k-1},\hat{\bm{D}}=\xi^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor t_{k}m_{n}\rfloor}\widetilde{Q}_{l/m_{n}}^{n,k-1}\mathbb{P}\left(J^{n}_{k}\leq\lfloor s_{k}m_{n}\rfloor,C^{n}_{k+1}=\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor,\mathcal{E}^{n,k}\ \Big|\ \mathcal{A}_{k-1},C^{n}_{k}=l,\mathcal{E}^{n,k-1},\hat{\bm{D}}=\xi^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor t_{k}m_{n}\rfloor}\frac{\widetilde{Q}_{l/m_{n}}^{n,k-1}}{m_{n}}\left(\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor s_{k}m_{n}\rfloor-1}(\xi_{j}^{n,*}-1)}{\sum_{j=1}^{l-k}(\xi_{j}^{n,*}-1)-(k-1)}\wedge 1\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\prod_{i=l+1}^{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor-1}\left(1-\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}(\xi_{j}^{n,*}-1)}{n-i+k}\right)\times\frac{m_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor-1}(\xi_{j}^{n,*}-1)-m_{n}k}{n-\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor}.

At this point it is helpful to rewrite these in terms of the discrete processes
\sigma^{n}(t)
: we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{n,k}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor t_{k}m_{n}\rfloor}\frac{\widetilde{Q}_{l/m_{n}}^{n,k-1}}{m_{n}}\left(\frac{\sigma^{n}(s_{k}-\tfrac{1}{m_{n}})}{\sigma^{n}(\tfrac{l-k}{m_{n}})-\frac{m_{n}(k-1)}{n}}\wedge 1\right)\prod_{i=l+1}^{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor-1}\left(1-\frac{n\sigma^{n}\left(\tfrac{i-1}{m_{n}}\right)}{(n-i+k)m_{n}}\right)\frac{\sigma^{n}(t-\tfrac{1}{m_{n}})-\frac{km_{n}}{n}}{1-\frac{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor}{n}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t_{k}}\!\!\widetilde{Q}_{u}^{n,k-1}\!\!\left(\frac{\sigma^{n}(s_{k}-1/m_{n})}{\sigma^{n}(u-\tfrac{k}{m_{n}})-\frac{m_{n}(k-1)}{n}}\wedge 1\right)\!\prod_{i=\lfloor um_{n}\rfloor+1}^{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor-1}\!\left(1-\frac{n\sigma^{n}\left(\tfrac{i-1}{m_{n}}\right)}{(n-i+k)m_{n}}\right)\sigma^{n}(t-\tfrac{1}{m_{n}})\,du

\displaystyle\hskip 404.02904pt+o(1).

where the
o(1)
term is a.s. uniform in
t
. We now aim to estimate the integrand for each
u\in[0,t_{k}]
. We know that, almost surely,

(a)

\widetilde{Q}_{u}^{n,k-1}
is uniformly close to
R_{u}^{n,k-1}
(by hypothesis);

(b)

\frac{\sigma^{n}(s_{k}-1/m_{n})}{\sigma^{n}(u-k/m_{n})-\frac{m_{n}(k-1)}{n}}\wedge 1
is uniformly close to
\frac{\sigma^{n}(u-)\wedge\sigma^{n}(s_{k})}{\sigma^{n}(u-)}
;

(c)

\prod_{i=\lfloor um_{n}\rfloor+1}^{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor-1}\!\!\left(1-\frac{n\sigma^{n}\left(\tfrac{i-1}{m_{n}}\right)}{(n-i+k)m_{n}}\right)
is uniformly close to
\exp\left(-\int_{u}^{t}\sigma^{n}(r)\,dr\right)
, by imitating the argument of
Theorem 4.3
;

(d)

\sigma^{n}(t-1/m_{n})
is uniformly close to
\sigma^{n}(t-)
.

In addition, all of these estimates are uniform in
u\in[0,t_{k}]
. The right-hand side of each estimate is uniformly bounded in
n
(as they converge in the Skorokhod metric). Thus by iteratively applying
4.6
we see that the integrand of our estimate on
\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{n,k}
is uniformly close to

u\mapsto R_{u}^{n,k-1}\frac{\sigma^{n}(u-)\wedge\sigma^{n}(s_{k})}{\sigma^{n}(u-)}\exp\left(-\int_{u}^{t}\sigma^{n}(r)\,dr\right)\sigma^{n}(t-),

and moreover this is uniform in
u\in[0,t_{k}]
. Using the uniformity in
u
we may integrate to see that our integral bound on
\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{n,k}
is uniformly (in
t
) close to

t\mapsto\int_{0}^{t_{k}}R_{u}^{n,k-1}\frac{\sigma^{n}(u-)\wedge\sigma^{n}(s_{k})}{\sigma^{n}(u-)}\exp\left(-\int_{u}^{t}\sigma^{n}(r)\,dr\right)\sigma^{n}(t-)\,du=R_{t}^{n,k}.

Thus for all
\varepsilon>0
there is almost surely an
N
such that for all
t\in[0,T]
we have
\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{n,k}\leq R_{t}^{n,k}+\varepsilon
whenever
n\geq N
. The lower bound is completely analogous, and so
\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{n,k}
is uniformly close to
R_{t}^{n,k}
, completing the proof.
∎

By integrating the processes in
4.5
from
0
to
t_{k+1}
and shifting
k
we deduce that

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{C_{1}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq t_{1},\dots,\frac{C_{k}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq t_{k},\frac{J_{1}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq s_{1},\dots,\frac{J_{k-1}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq s_{k-1}\ \Big|\ \hat{\bm{D}}=\xi^{*}\right)\\
\overset{d}{\to}\mathbb{P}\left(\eta_{1}\leq t_{1},\dots,\eta_{k}\leq t_{k},\zeta_{1}\leq s_{1},\dots,\zeta_{k-1}\leq s_{k-1}\ \Big|\ \sigma\right),

where
\eta_{i}
are the atoms of a Poisson process with intensity
\sigma
and
\zeta_{i}
are the attachment points defined conditionally given
\sigma
and
\eta
as before. Now we can introduce the measure change to recover the law of the true rescaled branch times: note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{C_{1}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq t_{1},\dots,\frac{C_{k}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq t_{k},\frac{J_{1}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq s_{1},\dots,\frac{J_{k-1}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq s_{k-1},N_{n}\geq\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{C_{1}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq t_{1},\dots,\frac{C_{k}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq t_{k},\frac{J_{1}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq s_{1},\dots,\frac{J_{k-1}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq s_{k-1}\,\Big|\,\hat{\bm{D}}=\xi^{*}\right)\Theta^{n}_{\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor}(\xi^{*})\right]

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\eta_{1}\leq t_{1},\dots,\eta_{k}\leq t_{k},\zeta_{1}\leq s_{1},\dots,\zeta_{k-1}\leq s_{k-1}\ \Big|\ \sigma\right)\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,ds\right)\frac{p(-\sigma_{t})}{p(0)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\eta_{1}\leq t_{1},\dots,\eta_{k}\leq t_{k},\zeta_{1}\leq s_{1},\dots,\zeta_{k-1}\leq s_{k-1}\ \Big|\ \sigma=\widetilde{\sigma}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{1}\leq t_{1},\dots,Y_{k}\leq t_{k},Z_{1}\leq s_{1},\dots,Z_{k-1}\leq s_{k-1}\right),

where in the third line we have used the Skorokhod representation and the fact that the measure change converges in
L^{1}
in this setting. Thus we have the following convergence in distribution:

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
C_{1}^{n},C_{2}^{n},\dots
be the times of repeats in the codeword used to generate a Bienaymé tree conditioned to have
n
vertices, and
J_{1}^{n},J_{2}^{n},\dots
the first occurrence times of these repeats. Let
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots
be the jump times of a Poisson process of intensity
\widetilde{\sigma}
, where
\widetilde{\sigma}
is the process introduced in
Section 1
, and let
Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots
be the corresponding attachment points. Then

\frac{1}{m_{n}}(C_{1}^{n},\dots,C_{k}^{n},J_{1}^{n},\dots,J_{k-1}^{n})\overset{d}{\to}(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k},Z_{1},\dots,Z_{k-1}).

Proof.

We have already shown that, for
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\leq T
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{C_{1}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq t_{1},\dots,\frac{C_{k}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq t_{k},\frac{J_{1}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq s_{1},\dots,\frac{J_{k-1}^{n}}{m_{n}}\leq s_{k-1},N^{n}\geq\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor\right)\\
\to\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{1}\leq t_{1},\dots,Y_{k}\leq t_{k},Z_{1}\leq s_{1},\dots,Z_{k-1}\leq s_{k-1}\right)

and so it is enough to remove the
N^{n}\geq\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor
from the left-hand side (as then we have pointwise convergence of distribution functions). But we can do this since
\mathbb{P}\left(N^{n}<\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor\right)\to 0
by
4.2
.
∎

4.3
Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov convergence of the trees

We begin by converting the convergence in
Theorem 4.7
into a statement about convergence of subtrees. We can do this as follows: let
T^{n}
be the discrete rooted labelled tree of size
n
as constructed from the algorithm in
Section 3
, and let
T^{n}(k)
be the subtree spanned by the root and vertices labelled
1,2,\dots,k
. On the event that labels
1,2,\dots,k
do not appear in the codeword before time
C^{n}_{k}
(which we can show occurs with probability tending to 1),
T^{n}(k)
precisely matches the tree constructed after time
C^{n}_{k}-1
. Recall that
d^{n}
denotes the graph distance on
V(T^{n})
. The
\mathbb{R}
-tree corresponding to the rescaled discrete tree
(V(T^{n}(k)),\frac{1}{m_{n}}d^{n}|_{T^{n}(k)})
, is a.s. isometric to the
\mathbb{R}
-tree obtained from a line-breaking construction with cut times

y^{n}_{i}=\frac{1}{m_{n}}(C^{n}_{i}-1),\quad 1\leq i\leq k

and attachment times

z^{n}_{i}=\frac{1}{m_{n}}(J^{n}_{i}-1),\quad 1\leq i\leq k-1

taken at time
y_{k}^{n}
. We endow
(V(T^{n}(k)),\frac{1}{m_{n}}d^{n}|_{T^{n}(k)})
with the empirical measure
\mu^{n}_{k}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\delta_{i}
on the vertices labelled
1,2,\ldots,k
.

Analogously, we can consider the tree
\mathcal{T}
constructed from the procedure in
Section 2
, and recall that
\mathcal{T}(k)=\mathcal{T}_{Y_{k}}
is the subtree obtained by terminating the process at time
Y_{k}
. Recall also that
\mu_{k}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\delta_{Y_{i}}
and that, from (
8
), that we have

\left(\mathcal{T}(k),d|_{\mathcal{T}(k)},\mu_{k}\right)\to(\mathcal{T},d,\mu)

almost surely as
k\to\infty
.

For each fixed
k
, we have that

(y_{1}^{n},\dots,y_{k}^{n},z_{1}^{n},\dots,z_{k-1}^{n})\overset{d}{\to}(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k},Z_{1},\dots,Z_{k-1})

and it follows straightforwardly that

\left(V(T^{n}(k)),\frac{1}{m_{n}}d^{n}|_{V(T^{n}(k))},\mu^{n}_{k}\right)\overset{d}{\to}\left(\mathcal{T}(k),d|_{\mathcal{T}(k)},\mu_{k}\right)

(9)

for the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov distance. In other words, the random finite-dimensional distributions converge.

In order to complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we need a tightness argument to deduce that
(V(T^{n}),d^{n}/m_{n},\mu^{n})\overset{d}{\to}(\mathcal{T},d,\mu)
in the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov sense. We follow closely the strategy deployed by Haas and Miermont in Sections 4.3 and 4.4 of
[
28
]
. That paper proves a collection of scaling limit theorems for Markov branching trees, a family of models which includes conditioned Galton–Watson trees. Indeed, Theorem 8 (Case 2) of
[
28
]
is a version of
Theorem 1.1
restricted to the special case of offspring distributions satisfying
\mathbb{P}\left(\xi=k\right)\sim ck^{-\alpha-1}
as
k\to\infty
for some
c>0
. Haas and Miermont’s method for showing convergence of the random finite-dimensional distributions makes use of the theory of fragmentation processes, and is completely different to ours. Their tightness proof, however, adapts well to our situation. Since we wish this paper to be self-contained, we reprove various estimates used in
[
28
]
via our line-breaking approach rather than appealing to the fragmentation perspective.

As argued in Section 4.4 of
[
28
]
, by Proposition 2.4 of
[
26
]
, the convergence in distribution
(V(T^{n}),d^{n}/m_{n})\overset{d}{\to}(\mathcal{T},d)
in the Gromov–Hausdorff sense entails that
(V(T^{n}),d^{n}/m_{n},\mu_{n})
is a relatively compact sequence in the Gromov–Hausdorff–Prokhorov sense. Therefore, it suffices to identify any subsequential limit as
(\mathcal{T},d,\mu)
. But this then follows from the convergence of the random finite-dimensional distributions (
9
). Therefore, in what follows we may ignore the measures and just prove tightness for the Gromov–Hausdorff distance.

In this subsection, let us abuse notation and write
T^{n}(k)
in place of
(V(T^{n}),d^{n}|_{V(T^{n}(k))})
and
T^{n}
in place of
(V(T^{n}),d^{n})
. By the principle of accompanying laws (see e.g. Theorem 3.2 of
[
14
]
), it is enough to show

\lim_{k\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(d_{\mathrm{GH}}(T^{n}(k),T^{n})>\varepsilon m_{n}\right)=0

for any
\varepsilon>0
. Note that
T^{n}\setminus T^{n}(k)
is a forest: indeed, conditionally on the number of vertices in
T^{n}(k)
and the number of edges from
T^{n}(k)
to its complement, it is a forest of i.i.d. Bienaymé trees conditioned on its total size and number of trees. Moreover,
d_{\mathrm{GH}}\left(T^{n}(k),T^{n}\right)
is at most the maximum height of any individual tree in this forest. Write
F_{1}^{n}(k),F_{2}^{n}(k),\ldots
for the sizes of the trees in the forest, and write
H(m)
for the height of a Bienaymé tree with the same offspring distribution conditioned to have size
m
. Then

\mathbb{P}\left(d_{\mathrm{GH}}\left(T^{n}(k),T^{n}\right)>\varepsilon m_{n}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\geq 1}\mathbb{P}\left(H(F_{i}^{n}(k))>\varepsilon m_{n}|F_{i}^{n}(k)\right)\right].

(10)

We adapt Lemma 33 of
[
28
]
to prove the following.

Lemma 4.8
.

For each
q>0
, there exists a universal constant
M_{q}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(H(n)\geq xm_{n}\right)\leq\frac{M_{q}}{x^{q}}

for all
x>0
and all
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

Lemma 33 of
[
28
]
makes use of a hypothesis
(H^{\prime})
, which Haas and Miermont only prove holds in the restricted case
\mathbb{P}\left(\xi=k\right)\sim ck^{-\alpha-1}
as
k\to\infty
for some
c>0
. However, all that is really needed in the proof is that for fixed
\gamma
,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}m_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[1-\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{D}_{1}}\left(\frac{A_{i}}{n}\right)^{\gamma}\right]>0,

(11)

where
A_{1},A_{2},\ldots
are the sizes of the subtrees of the children of the root. We will give a crude bounding argument to show that this condition also holds in our case. Writing
\beta=\gamma-1>0
, we observe that

m_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[1-\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{D}_{1}}\left(\frac{A_{i}}{n}\right)^{\gamma}\right]\geq m_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[1-\left(\frac{A_{*}}{n}\right)^{\beta}\right],

where
A_{*}
is a size-biased pick from
(A_{i})
. Letting
F_{1},F_{2},\dots
be i.i.d. random variables having the same distribution as the size of an unconditioned Bienaymé tree, and setting
S_{n}=\Xi_{n}-n
, where
(\Xi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is the random walk with increments
\xi
as before, we see that the right-hand side of the above is

\displaystyle m_{n}\sum_{d=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\left(1-\left(\frac{j}{n}\right)^{\beta}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\xi=d\right)\cdot d\cdot\frac{j}{n-1}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(F_{1}=j\right)\mathbb{P}\left(F_{1}+\dots+F_{d-1}=n-1-j\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(F_{1}+\dots+F_{d}=n-1\right)}

\displaystyle=m_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\sum_{d=1}^{n-1}\left(1-\left(\frac{j}{n}\right)^{\beta}\right)\frac{(d-1)\mathbb{P}\left(\xi=d\right)}{n-1-j}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(S_{j}=-1\right)\mathbb{P}\left(S_{n-1-j}=-(d-1)\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(S_{n-1}=-d\right)},

using the cycle lemma. For the sake of obtaining a lower bound, we may restrict our attention to
j\in[\frac{n}{3},\frac{2n}{3}]
and
d\in[a_{n},2a_{n}]
. Here, the term
1-\left(\frac{j}{n}\right)^{\beta}
is uniformly bounded away from 0, and
n-1-j=\Theta(n)
, so for sufficiently large
n
we have that (
11
) is bounded below by a constant multiple of

\displaystyle\quad\,\,m_{n}\sum_{j=n/3}^{2n/3}\sum_{d=a_{n}}^{2a_{n}}\frac{1}{n}(d-1)\mathbb{P}\left(\xi=d\right)\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(S_{j}=-1\right)\mathbb{P}\left(S_{n-1-j}=-(d-1)\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(S_{n-1}=-d\right)}

\displaystyle=m_{n}\sum_{j}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[(\xi-1)\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(S_{j}=-1\right)\mathbb{P}\left(S_{n-1-j}=-(\xi-1)\mid\xi\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(S_{n-1}=-\xi\mid\xi\right)}\mathbbm{1}\left(a_{n}\leq\xi\leq 2a_{n}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\frac{m_{n}a_{n-1}}{na_{j}a_{n-1-j}}\mathbb{E}\left[(\xi-1)\frac{a_{j}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{j}=-1\right)a_{n-1-j}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{n-1-j}=-(\xi-1)\mid\xi\right)}{a_{n-1}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{n-1}=-\xi\mid\xi\right)}\mathbbm{1}\left(a_{n}\leq\xi\leq 2a_{n}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\Omega(1)\sum_{j}\frac{m_{n}}{na_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[(\xi-1)\frac{a_{j}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{j}=-1\right)a_{n-1-j}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{n-1-j}=-(\xi-1)\mid\xi\right)}{a_{n-1}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{n-1}=-\xi\mid\xi\right)}\mathbbm{1}\left(a_{n}\leq\xi\leq 2a_{n}\right)\right].

For any fixed
\delta>0
, three applications of the local limit theorem yield that for sufficently large
n
, (
11
) is bounded below by

\Omega(1)\sum_{j}\frac{m_{n}}{na_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[(\xi-1)\frac{\left(p(0)-\delta\right)\left(p\left(\frac{-(\xi-1)}{a_{n-1-j}}\right)-\delta\right)}{p\left(\frac{-\xi}{a_{n-1}}\right)+\delta}\mathbbm{1}\left(a_{n}\leq\xi\leq 2a_{n}\right)\right].

As
(a_{n})
is regularly varying, we can find a constant
R
such that
a_{n-1-j}\geq\frac{a_{n}}{R}
whenever
n
is sufficiently large and
j\in[\frac{n}{3},\frac{2n}{3}]
. For such
n
, we observe that on the event
a_{n}\leq\xi\leq 2a_{n}
we have
\frac{\xi-1}{a_{n-1-j}}\in[0,2R]
and
\frac{\xi}{a_{n-1}}\in[0,2R]
. Thus taking
\delta<\inf_{x\in[0,2R]}p(-x)
, we obtain the existence of a constant
\eta>0
such that

\frac{\left(p(0)-\delta\right)\left(p\left(\frac{-(\xi-1)}{a_{n-1-j}}\right)-\delta\right)}{p\left(\frac{-\xi}{a_{n-1}}\right)+\delta}\geq\eta

whenever
a_{n}\leq\xi\leq 2a_{n}
. Thus we obtain that (
11
) is bounded below by

\displaystyle\Omega(1)\sum_{j=n/3}^{2n/3}\frac{m_{n}}{na_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[(\xi-1)\eta\mathbbm{1}\left(a_{n}\leq\xi\leq 2a_{n}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\Omega(1)\sum_{j=n/3}^{2n/3}\frac{m_{n}}{n}\mathbb{P}\left(a_{n}\leq\xi\leq 2a_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\Omega(1)\times m_{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\xi}{a_{n}}\in[1,2]\right).

By standard theory of Lévy processes we know
m_{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\xi}{a_{n}}\in[1,2]\right)\to\nu_{\alpha}([1,2])
, where
\nu_{\alpha}
is the Lévy measure of the
\alpha
-stable Lévy process
L
. In particular, our lower bound has a strictly positive limit.

The proof now follows as in Lemma 33 of
[
28
]
. We provide a sketch of the argument here: first, without loss of generality we can focus on large values of
q
, specifically
q>\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}
. Pick some
\varepsilon>0
such that
\gamma:=q(\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}-\varepsilon)>1
. Now we can find a new sequence
\widetilde{m}_{n}\sim m_{n}
such that

\widetilde{m}_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[1-\sum_{i=1}^{\widehat{D_{1}}}\left(\frac{A_{i}}{n}\right)^{\gamma}\right]\geq\tilde{M}\text{ for all }n\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{\widetilde{m}_{k}}{\widetilde{m}_{n}}\leq\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}-\varepsilon}\text{ for all }1\leq k\leq n,n\geq N

for some fixed
N
. Now pick
M_{q}
large enough that the following hold:

•

(1-u)^{-q}\leq 1+2qu
for all
u\in[0,M_{q}^{-q}]
;

•

M_{q}\geq(2q/\tilde{M})^{q}
;

•

M_{q}\geq\mathbb{E}\left[H(n)^{q}\right]/{\widetilde{m}_{n}}^{q}
for all
n\leq N
.

We prove the result via a double induction: we prove the main result by induction on
n
, and to prove the result for a given
n
, we prove by induction on
i\geq 1
that

\mathbb{P}\left(H(n)<x\widetilde{m}_{n}\right)\geq 1-\frac{M_{q}}{x^{q}}\text{ for all }x\in\left(0,\frac{i}{\widetilde{m}_{n}}\right).

Markov’s inequality handles the case
n\leq N
(for all
i
), and the right-hand side is negative for small enough values of
x
(and
i
), so we only need to consider
n>N,x>M_{q}^{1/q}
. Since
H(n)
is 1 plus the maximum height across all subtrees rooted at children of the root,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(H(n)<x\widetilde{m}_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{\widehat{D}_{1}}\mathbb{P}\left(H(A_{i})<x\widetilde{m}_{n}-1\ \big|\ A_{i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{\widehat{D}_{1}}\left(1-\frac{M_{q}\widetilde{m}_{A_{i}}^{q}}{(x\widetilde{m}_{n}-1)^{q}}\right)^{+}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[1-\sum_{i=1}^{\widehat{D}_{1}}\frac{M_{q}\widetilde{m}_{A_{i}}^{q}}{(x\widetilde{m}_{n}-1)^{q}}\right].

Note that the first inequality is valid by our inductive hypotheses: on the event that
(A_{i})
is a non-trivial partition, we can use the inductive hypothesis for
n
, and on the event that it is the trivial partition, we can use that
x\widetilde{m}_{n}-1<i-1
, so the inductive hypothesis for
i
is valid. Now we can use the bounds
\frac{1}{(x\widetilde{m}_{n}-1)^{q}}\leq\frac{1}{(x\widetilde{m}_{n})^{q}}\left(1+\frac{2q}{x\widetilde{m}_{n}}\right)
(valid as
x>M_{q}^{1/q}
) and
\left(\frac{\widetilde{m}_{A_{i}}}{\widetilde{m}_{n}}\right)^{q}\leq\left(\frac{A_{i}}{n}\right)^{\gamma}
(valid as
n\geq N
). Some further algebra implies the required bound.
∎

We continue to proceed exactly as in
[
28
]
. Note that since
(m_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is regularly varying of index
1-1/\alpha
, we have that
(m_{n}^{2\alpha/(\alpha-1)}n^{-1})_{n\geq 1}
is regularly varying of index 1 and so we can find a constant
C
such that

\frac{m_{k}^{2\alpha/(\alpha-1)}k^{-1}}{m_{n}^{2\alpha/(\alpha-1)}n^{-1}}\leq C\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}

for all
1\leq k\leq n
. Combining (
10
) and Lemma
4.8
, and taking
q=2\alpha/(\alpha-1)
, we obtain that for any
n
and
k
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(d_{\mathrm{GH}}\left(T^{n}(k),T^{n}\right)>\varepsilon m_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M_{q}}{\varepsilon^{q}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\geq 1}\left(\frac{m_{F_{i}^{n}(k)}}{m_{n}}\right)^{2\alpha/(\alpha-1)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M_{q}}{\varepsilon^{q}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{F_{i}^{n}(k)}{n-|T^{n}(k)|}\frac{m_{F_{i}^{n}(k)}^{2\alpha/(\alpha-1)}/F_{i}^{n}(k)}{m_{n}^{2\alpha/(\alpha-1)}/n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{CM_{q}}{\varepsilon^{q}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{F_{i}^{n}(k)}{n-|T^{n}(k)|}\sqrt{\frac{F_{i}^{n}(k)}{n}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq R(\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{\frac{F_{*}^{n}(k)}{n}}\right],

where
R(\varepsilon)
is a constant depending only on
\varepsilon
and
F_{*}^{n}(k)
is a size-biased pick from the sizes
(F_{1}^{n}(k),F_{2}^{n}(k),\ldots)
of the trees in
T^{n}\setminus T^{n}(k)
. The key result to prove is the following:

Proposition 4.9
.

For each fixed
k
, we have
F_{*}^{n}(k)/n\overset{d}{\to}F_{*}(k)
as
n\to\infty
, where
F_{*}(k)\sim\mathrm{Beta}(1-1/\alpha,k)
and so, in particular,
F_{*}(k)\overset{d}{\to}0
as
k\to\infty
.

Before we prove this proposition, we note that it is sufficient to deduce the desired tightness, as we have

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(d_{GH}\left(T^{n}(k),T^{n}\right)>\varepsilon m_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq R(\varepsilon)\lim_{k\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{F_{*}^{n}(k)/n}\,\right]

\displaystyle=R(\varepsilon)\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{F_{*}(k)}\right]=0.

Proof of
4.9
.

In what follows, let
L=|T^{n}(k)|
be the number of vertices in
T^{n}(k)
, and let
N
be the number of edges from
T^{n}(k)
to
T^{n}\setminus T^{n}(k)
. One can show that conditionally given
L
and
N
, the forest
T^{n}\setminus T^{n}(k)
has the law of
N
i.i.d. Bienaymé trees (with offspring distribution
\xi
) conditioned to have total size
n-L
.

We study the conditional probabilities
\mathbb{P}\left(F_{*}^{n}(k)\geq\lfloor xn\rfloor\ \big|\ L,N\right)
for fixed
x>0
. This probability is zero whenever
x\geq 1
, so we can consider
x\in(0,1)
. Let
F_{1},F_{2},\dots
be i.i.d. random variables, also independent of
L
and
N
, distributed as the size of an unconditioned Bienaymé tree with offspring distribution
\xi
. Then

\mathbb{P}\left(F_{*}^{n}(k)\geq\lfloor xn\rfloor\ \big|\ L,N\right)=\!\!\!\sum_{j=\lfloor xn\rfloor}^{n}\!\frac{\frac{j}{n-L}N\mathbb{P}\left(F_{1}=j\ \big|\ L,N\right)\mathbb{P}\left(F_{2}+\dots+F_{N}=n-L-j\ \big|\ \!L,N\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(F_{1}+\dots+F_{N}=n-L\ \big|\ L,N\right)}.

Taking
S_{n}=\Xi_{n}-n
as before, independently of
L
and
N
, we know by the cycle lemma that, for any fixed
a,b\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}\left(F_{1}+\dots+F_{a}=b\right)=\frac{a}{b}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{b}=-a\right).

Using this, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(F_{*}^{n}(k)\geq\lfloor xn\rfloor\ \big|\ L,N\right)=\sum_{j=\lfloor xn\rfloor}^{n}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(S_{j}=-1\right)\mathbb{P}\left(F_{1}+\dots+F_{N-1}=n-L-j\ \big|\ L,N\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(S_{n-L}=-N\ \big|\ L,N\right)}.

We now wish to apply local limit theorems to this expression. We already have a local limit for
(S_{n})
, namely

a_{n}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{n}=r\right)=p\left(\frac{r}{a_{n}}\right)+o(1)

as
n\to\infty
, uniformly in
r
, but by Theorem 4.2.1 of
[
31
]
we also have a local limit for sums of
F_{i}
: specifically, we can find a sequence
b_{n}
such that
\frac{F_{1}+\dots+F_{n}}{b_{n}}
converges in distribution to a
(1/\alpha)
-stable subordinator at time
1
. Letting
q
be the density of this limit law, we have

b_{n}\mathbb{P}\left(F_{1}+\dots+F_{n}=r\right)=q\left(\frac{r}{b_{n}}\right)+o(1)

as
n\to\infty
, again uniformly in
r
. We note that
b_{n}
is regularly varying of index
\alpha
, and we may further choose the sequence so that
b_{\lfloor a_{n}\rfloor}\sim n
as
n\to\infty
.

Guided by our limits, we write the conditional probability in the more suggestive format

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=\lfloor xn\rfloor}^{n}\frac{a_{n-L}}{a_{j}}\cdot\frac{n}{b_{N-1}}\cdot\frac{a_{j}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{j}=-1\right)\cdot b_{N-1}\mathbb{P}\left(F_{1}+\dots+F_{N-1}=n-L-j\ \big|\ L,N\right)}{a_{n-L}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{n-L}=-N\ \big|\ L,N\right)}

which, via the substitution
j=\lfloor un\rfloor
, can in turn be expressed as the integral
\int_{x}^{1}I_{k}^{n}(u)\,du
, where

I_{k}^{n}(u)=\frac{a_{n-L}}{a_{\lfloor un\rfloor}}\cdot\frac{n}{b_{N-1}}\cdot\frac{a_{\lfloor un\rfloor}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{\lfloor un\rfloor}=-1\right)\cdot b_{N-1}\mathbb{P}\left(F_{1}+\dots F_{N-1}=n-\lfloor un\rfloor-L\ \big|\ L,N\right)}{a_{n-L}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{n-L}=-N\ \big|\ L,N\right)}.

We seek a pointwise limit for
I_{k}^{n}
. Let
Y_{k}
be the time of the
k
th cut in the continuous line-breaking construction. Then we know that

\frac{N}{a_{n}}\overset{d}{\to}\widetilde{\sigma}_{Y_{k}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{L}{m_{n}}\overset{d}{\to}Y_{k}

as
n\to\infty
(with
k
fixed), and these convergences hold jointly. Let us work on a probability space where these convergences hold almost surely. Then, almost surely, for all
u\in(x,1)
the following hold:

•

L\ll n
as
n\to\infty
, so
n-L\sim n
. Thus
\frac{n-L}{\lfloor un\rfloor}\to u^{-1}
;

•

Since the numerator and denominator of
\frac{n-L}{\lfloor un\rfloor}
both tend to
+\infty
and
(a_{n})
is regularly varying of index
1/\alpha
it follows that
\frac{a_{n-L}}{a_{\lfloor un\rfloor}}\to u^{-1/\alpha}
;

•

\frac{a_{n}}{N-1}\to(\widetilde{\sigma}_{Y_{k}})^{-1}
. As
(b_{n})
is regularly varying of index
\alpha
it follows that
\frac{b_{\lfloor a_{n}\rfloor}}{b_{N-1}}\to(\widetilde{\sigma}_{Y_{k}})^{-\alpha}
, and so
\frac{n}{b_{N-1}}
also converges to
(\widetilde{\sigma}_{Y_{k}})^{-\alpha}
.

•

By the local limit results for
X
and
F
, the fraction converges to

\frac{p(0)\cdot q\left((1-u)/(\widetilde{\sigma}_{Y_{k}})^{\alpha}\right)}{p\left(-\widetilde{\sigma}_{Y_{k}}\right)}.

Combining everything, we obtain the pointwise limit

\lim_{n\to\infty}I_{k}^{n}(u)=u^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{Y_{k}})^{-\alpha}\frac{p(0)}{p(-\widetilde{\sigma}_{Y_{k}})}q\left(\frac{1-u}{\widetilde{\sigma}_{Y_{k}}^{\alpha}}\right)=:\mathcal{I}_{k}(u).

By studying the above estimates more closely, we see that there exist constants
R=R(x)\leq x^{-\alpha}+100
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
and all
u\in(x,1)
we have
I_{k}^{n}(u)\leq R\cdot\mathcal{I}_{k}(u)
. In particular, if we can show that
\int_{x}^{1}\mathcal{I}_{k}(u)du<\infty
almost surely, then by dominated convergence it will follow that

\mathbb{P}\left(F_{*}^{n}(k)\geq\lfloor xn\rfloor\ \big|\ L,N\right)\to\int_{x}^{1}\mathcal{I}_{k}(u)\,du.

(12)

To estimate
\mathcal{I}_{k}(u)
we can use the duality relation (14.41) in
[
39
]
between
p
and
q
: we see that

\mathcal{I}_{k}(u)=u^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}(1-u)^{-1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\widetilde{\sigma}_{Y_{k}}\frac{p(0)\cdot p\left(-\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}_{Y_{k}}}{(1-u)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\right)}{p(-\widetilde{\sigma}_{Y_{k}})},\quad u\in[x,1)

and as
u\to 1
we have
\mathcal{I}_{k}(u)\to 0
almost surely (to see this use the stretched exponential decay estimate (
3
) for
p
). Thus
\mathcal{I}_{k}
is the restriction to
[x,1)
of a continuous function on
[x,1]
and so is bounded. We deduce that (
12
) holds. Now we can use the bounded convergence theorem to eliminate the conditioning, and obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(F_{*}^{n}(k)\geq\lfloor xn\rfloor\right)\to\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{x}^{1}\mathcal{I}_{k}(u)\,du\right]=\int_{x}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{I}_{k}(u)\right]\,du,\quad x\in(0,1).

By monotone convergence we also have
\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{I}_{k}(u)\right]\,du\leq 1
, and so we may define a right-continuous non-increasing function
G_{k}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
by

G_{k}(t)=\begin{cases}1&t<0\\
\int_{t}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{I}_{k}(u)\right]\,du&0\leq t<1\\
0&t\geq 1.\end{cases}

It follows that we can construct a random variable
F_{*}(k)
with c.d.f.
1-G_{k}
. We have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(F_{*}^{n}(k)\geq\lfloor xn\rfloor\right)=G_{k}(x)

(13)

for all
x\neq 0
. If
G_{k}(0)=1
then the convergence also holds for
x=0
; if not, then
G_{k}
is discontinuous at 0. It follows that (
13
) holds at all continuity points of
G_{k}
, and so
F_{*}^{n}(k)\overset{d}{\to}F_{*}(k)
.

We now show that
F_{*}(k)\sim\mathrm{Beta}(1-1/\alpha,k)
. If this is the case then

\mathbb{E}\left[F_{*}(k)\right]=\frac{1-1/\alpha}{k+1-1/\alpha}\to 0

as
k\to\infty
, and it follows immediately that
F_{*}(k)\overset{d}{\to}0
as
k\to\infty
.

Recall that
F_{*}(k)
has density function
\phi_{k}(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{I}_{k}(x)\right]
.
Applying the measure change at the stopping time
Y_{k}
allows us to write this density as

\phi_{k}(x)=x^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}(1-x)^{-1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{Y_{k}}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{Y_{k}}\sigma_{s}\,ds\right)p\left(-\frac{\sigma_{Y_{k}}}{(1-x)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\right)\right].

Conditionally given
\sigma
, the stopping time
Y_{k}
has density

\psi_{k}(t\ \big|\ \sigma)=\frac{\sigma_{t}}{(k-1)!}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,ds\right)^{k-1}\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,ds\right),\quad t\geq 0,

and so by the tower law (and Fubini’s theorem) we obtain

\phi_{k}(x)=\frac{x^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}(1-x)^{-1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{(k-1)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{t}^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,ds\right)^{k-1}p\left(-\frac{\sigma_{t}}{(1-x)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\right)\right]\,dt.

By the scaling property of the subordinator
\sigma
, we have the following equality in distribution (as processes):

\left(\sigma_{t},t\geq 0\right)\overset{d}{=}\left((1-x)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\sigma_{t(1-x)^{-1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}},t\geq 0\right).

Thus we may express the density
\phi_{k}(x)
as

\displaystyle\frac{x^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}(1-x)^{-1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{(k-1)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{t(1-x)^{-1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}}^{2}\left((1-x)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s(1-x)^{-1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\,ds\right)^{k-1}\!\!\!p\left(-\sigma_{t(1-x)^{-1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\right)\right]\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}(1-x)^{-1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{(k-1)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{t(1-x)^{-1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}}^{2}\left((1-x)\int_{0}^{t(1-x)^{-1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\sigma_{r}\,dr\right)^{k-1}\!\!\!\!\!p\left(-\sigma_{t(1-x)^{-1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\right)\right]\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}(1-x)^{k-2+\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{(k-1)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{t(1-x)^{-1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}}^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{t(1-x)^{-1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\sigma_{r}\,dr\right)^{k-1}\!\!\!p\left(-\sigma_{t(1-x)^{-1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\right)\right]\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}(1-x)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{u}^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{u}\sigma_{r}\,dr\right)^{k-1}\!\!\!p\left(-\sigma_{u}\right)\right]\,du,

after various changes of variable. The integral term in the last expression does not depend on
x
and thus acts only as a normalising constant for the density. In particular, we can identify this density as that of the
\text{Beta}(1-\frac{1}{\alpha},k)
distribution, and it also follows that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{u}^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{u}\sigma_{r}dr\right)^{k-1}p(-\sigma_{u})\right]du=\frac{\Gamma(k+1-1/\alpha)}{\Gamma(1-1/\alpha)}.\qed

Remark 4.10
.

F_{*}(k)
may clearly be interpreted as a size-biased pick from among the component sizes of the forest
\mathcal{T}\setminus\mathcal{T}(k)
. To the best of our knowledge, its distribution had not previously been explicitly identified in the literature for a general
k
, although it is easy to deduce it from pre-existing results, as we now show. In the case
k=1
, the ranked sequence of component sizes of
\mathcal{T}\setminus\mathcal{T}(1)
forms the
fine spinal mass partition
defined in
[
29
]
. By Corollary 10 of the same paper, the sequence of sizes follows a Poisson–Dirichlet distribution with parameters
(\frac{1}{\alpha},1-\frac{1}{\alpha})
. (See Chapter 3 of Pitman
[
37
]
for more information about the Poisson–Dirichlet distributions.)

Now let
P^{k}=(P_{1}^{k},P_{2}^{k},\dots)
be the ranked sequence of component sizes of
\mathcal{T}\setminus\mathcal{T}(k)
. Conditionally given
P^{k-1}
, we can sample
P^{k}
as follows: we take a size-biased pick from the components of
\mathcal{T}\setminus\mathcal{T}(k-1)
, then reveal the fine spinal mass partition of this component. This has the effect of replacing some
P_{I}^{k-1}
with the collection
\{P_{I}^{k-1}\tilde{P}_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}
, where
I
is a size-biased index,
\tilde{P}
is a copy of
P^{1}
independent of everything else, and finally we reorder the sequence to preserve the decreasing order. By Theorem 3.1 in
[
21
]
, we see that if
P^{k-1}\sim\mathrm{PD}(\frac{1}{\alpha},\theta)
for some
\theta>0
, then
P^{k}\sim\mathrm{PD}(\frac{1}{\alpha},\theta+1)
. It follows by induction that
P^{k}\sim\mathrm{PD}(\frac{1}{\alpha},k-\frac{1}{\alpha})
for every
k\geq 1
. Now
F_{*}(k)
is just a size-biased pick from
P^{k}
, and thus it has law
\text{Beta}(1-\frac{1}{\alpha},k)
.

5
Relation to Wang’s construction

In this section, we show that our construction provides a new perspective on Wang’s construction of the stable tree from
[
41
]
. The key result is Proposition
5.4
below, which connects
\widetilde{\sigma}
and the jumps of a normalised
\alpha
-stable excursion.

For a sequence
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
with a largest element, we will write
(\mathbf{x})^{\downarrow}
for the same sequence with the entries put into decreasing order. Recall that
\Delta_{1}\geq\Delta_{2}\geq\dots\geq 0
are the ordered jumps of
\widetilde{\sigma}
. Let
\Delta_{1}^{n}\geq\Delta_{2}^{n}\geq\dots\geq 0
denote the ordered (out-)degrees in the finite-
n
model, so that
(\Delta_{1}^{n},\Delta_{2}^{n},\ldots,\Delta_{n}^{n})=(D_{1},D_{2},\ldots,D_{n})^{\downarrow}
. For all
m\geq n+1
, let
\Delta^{n}_{m}=0
so that we append an infinite sequence of 0’s to the end of this vector.

Let
\ell_{2}^{\downarrow}=\{(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots):x_{1}\geq x_{2}\ldots\geq 0\}
which we endow with the usual
\ell_{2}
norm. For sequences indexed by an arbitrary countable set
\Gamma
we instead write
\ell_{2}(\Gamma)
for the corresponding set of square summable sequences, and
\ell_{2}^{+}(\Gamma)
when all of the elements of the sequences are strictly positive.

Proposition 5.1
.

As
n\to\infty
,

a_{n}^{-1}(\Delta_{1}^{n},\Delta_{2}^{n},\ldots)\overset{d}{\to}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\ldots)

in
\ell_{2}^{\downarrow}
.

The proof of this result will make use of the theory of size-biased point processes developed by Aldous in Section 3.3 of
[
12
]
. We need a little set-up.

Suppose that
\Gamma
is a countable index-set and we have
\mathbf{Y}=\{Y_{\gamma}:\gamma\in\Gamma\}
such that
\mathbf{Y}\in\ell_{2}^{+}(\Gamma)
. Conditionally on
\{Y_{\gamma}:\gamma\in\Gamma\}
, let
E_{\gamma}
be exponentially distributed with parameter
Y_{\gamma}
, independently for different
\gamma\in\Gamma
. If the sequence
\mathbf{Y}
is summable then it is straightforward to check that putting the values
Y_{\gamma}
in increasing order of their associated exponential random variables has precisely the effect of putting them in size-biased random order. But the construction also makes sense when the sequence is not itself summable, as long as it is square-summable. For
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, define

S(t)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}Y_{\gamma}\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{\gamma}\leq t\right).

Then
S(t)<\infty
a.s. for all
t
. Let
S_{\gamma}=S(E_{\gamma}-)
. The set
\Xi:=\{(S_{\gamma},Y_{\gamma}):\gamma\in\Gamma\}
is called the
size-biased point process
associated with
\mathbf{Y}
. This is an element of the set
\mathcal{M}
of collections of points in
[0,\infty)\times(0,\infty)
such that there are only finitely many points in compact rectangles of the form
[0,a]\times[\delta,1/\delta]
,
a>0,\delta>0
. We endow
\mathcal{M}
with the topology of vague convergence of counting measures on
[0,\infty)\times(0,\infty)
.

Observe we may also construct the process
S(t)
in terms of an i.i.d. sequence of
\textnormal{Exp}\left(1\right)
random variables, say
\{E_{\gamma}^{\prime}:\gamma\in\Gamma\}
, again independent of
\mathbf{Y}
: then we may set

S(t)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}Y_{\gamma}\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{\gamma}^{\prime}\leq Y_{\gamma}t\right).

This is useful for coupling processes
S(t)
across various choices of
\mathbf{Y}
.

A convenient formulation of the result we shall use is given in Proposition 17 of Aldous and Limić
[
5
]
, which we reproduce below.

Proposition 5.2
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{Y}^{(n)}\in\ell_{2}^{+}(\Gamma^{(n)})
and that
\Xi^{(n)}
is the associated size-biased point process, for each
1\leq n<\infty
. Suppose further that

\Xi^{(n)}\overset{d}{\to}\Xi^{(\infty)},

where

1.

\sup\{s:(s,y)\in\Xi^{(\infty)}\text{ for some $y$}\}=\infty
a.s.

2.

if
(s,y)\in\Xi^{(\infty)}
then
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(s^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\Xi^{(\infty)}\\
s^{\prime}<s\end{subarray}}y^{\prime}=s
a.s.

3.

\max\{y:(s,y)\in\Xi^{(\infty)}\text{ for some }s>a\}\overset{p}{\to}0
as
a\to\infty
.

Write
\mathbf{Y}^{\infty}
for the projection of
\Xi^{(\infty)}
onto its second co-ordinate. Then

(\mathbf{Y}^{(n)})^{\downarrow}\overset{d}{\to}(\mathbf{Y}^{(\infty)})^{\downarrow}

in
\ell_{2}^{\downarrow}
.

Proof of
5.1
.

Let
\mathbf{Y}^{(n)}=\{a_{n}^{-1}D_{i}:1\leq i\leq n,D_{i}>0\}
. By construction, the process
(S^{(n)}(t),t\geq 0)
is a random time-change of
(a_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor m_{n}t\rfloor}\widehat{D}_{i},t\geq 0)
and so, in particular, we have

\Xi^{(n)}=\left\{\left(\frac{1}{a_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\widehat{D}_{i},\frac{1}{a_{n}}\widehat{D}_{j}\right):j\geq 1\right\}.

By
4.4
, we have

\left(\frac{1}{a_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor m_{n}t\rfloor}\widehat{D}_{i},t\geq 0\right)\overset{d}{\to}\widetilde{\sigma};

we will use this to show that

\Xi^{(n)}\overset{d}{\to}\Xi^{(\infty)}=\{(\widetilde{\sigma}_{t-},\Delta\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}):\Delta\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}>0,t\geq 0\}.

To prove this, by a strengthening of Theorem 16.16 of
[
32
]
it is enough to prove convergence in distribution of
\Xi^{(n)}(U)
to
\Xi^{(\infty)}(U)
for any open, relatively compact set
U
such that
\Xi^{(\infty)}(\partial U)=0
a.s. Proving this statement is routine but rather long, so we give only a brief sketch here: fix a set
U
and let us work on a common probability space such that the convergence
(\frac{1}{a_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor}\widehat{D}_{i},t\geq 0)\to\widetilde{\sigma}
in
D([0,\infty))
is almost sure. We now work on the (probability 1) event that this convergence does occur and that the limiting point measure puts no mass on the boundary.

Now suppose that
\Xi^{(\infty)}
has precisely
K
atoms
(s_{1},y_{1})\dots,(s_{K},y_{K})\in U
. That is, the process
\widetilde{\sigma}
has jumps whose values go from
s_{i}
to
s_{i}+y_{i}
for each
i
, and that these are the only jumps for which
(\widetilde{\sigma}_{t-},\Delta\widetilde{\sigma}_{t})\in U
. Using the convergence of càdlàg processes we note that, for each
n
, we can identify
K
jumps of the
n
th discrete process, say from
s_{i}^{(n)}
to
s_{i}^{(n)}+y_{i}^{(n)}
, such that
s_{i}^{(n)}\to s_{i}
and
y_{i}^{(n)}\to y_{i}
for all
i
. (Note these jumps need not be distinct, but for sufficiently large
n
they will be.) As
U
is an open set, we conclude that for sufficiently large
n
, all points
(s_{i}^{(n)},y_{i}^{(n)})
lie in
U
and thus
\Xi^{(n)}
has at least
K
atoms in
U
.

It remains to show that for all but finitely many
n
these are the only atoms in
U
. Suppose not, then we can find a subsequence in
n
along which there is an extra atom
(u^{(n)},v^{(n)})\in U
. Using relative compactness of
U
it is straightforward to derive a contradiction: either we find another atom in the limiting measure in
U
or on the boundary, or we find that
\Xi^{(n)}
has a pair of atoms that both converge to a common point (which violates either right continuity or existence of left limits in
\widetilde{\sigma}
).

We now just need to check Conditions 1–3 of
5.2
. Since
\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
, 1 is clear. Condition 2 is a consequence of the fact that
\widetilde{\sigma}
is a right-continuous increasing process which evolves only by jumps. Condition 3 follows immediately from the fact that the jumps of
\widetilde{\sigma}
are a.s. square-summable, which was proved in
2.7
.
∎

We will need one more proposition formalising an intuitive result about convergence of the processes
S^{(n)}
. The proof is deferred to the Appendix.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\mathbf{X}^{(n)},\mathbf{X}
be random elements of
\ell_{2}^{\downarrow}=\ell_{2}^{\downarrow}(\mathbb{N})
, not necessarily defined on the same probability space. Define, for
t\geq 0
,

S^{(n)}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}X_{i}^{(n)}\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq X_{i}^{(n)}t\right)\quad\text{ and }\quad S(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}X_{i}\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq X_{i}t\right),

where
E_{i}\sim\textnormal{Exp}\left(1\right)
are i.i.d. and independent of
\mathbf{X}^{(n)}
and
\mathbf{X}
. If
\mathbf{X}^{(n)}\overset{d}{\to}\mathbf{X}
, then we have
(S^{(n)}(t),t\geq 0)\overset{d}{\to}(S(t),t\geq 0)
in the Skorokhod space
D([0,\infty))
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
\Theta=(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2},\dots)
be the ordered jump sizes of a normalised
\alpha
-stable excursion. Then

(\widetilde{\sigma}_{t})_{t\geq 0}\overset{d}{=}\left(\sum_{i\geq 1}\Theta_{i}\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq\Theta_{i}t\right)\right)_{t\geq 0},

where
E_{1},E_{2},\ldots
are i.i.d.
\textnormal{Exp}\left(1\right)
and independent of
\Theta
.

Proof.

Define a new process
\rho
as follows: first, generate a sequence
(\Delta_{i})_{i\geq 1}\in\ell_{2}^{\downarrow}
distributed as the law of the jumps of
\widetilde{\sigma}
arranged into decreasing order. Independently of these, let
E_{i}\sim\textnormal{Exp}\left(1\right)
be i.i.d. and set

\rho_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Delta_{i}\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq\Delta_{i}t\right).

The main step is to show that
\rho
and
\widetilde{\sigma}
have the same law.

In what follows, write
\widetilde{\sigma}^{n}(t)=\frac{1}{a_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor tm_{n}\rfloor}\widehat{D}_{i}
, and note that
\widetilde{\sigma}^{n}\overset{d}{\to}\widetilde{\sigma}
. (This is not exactly the analogue of
\sigma^{n}
defined in
4.1
, as we have not subtracted 1 from each term in the sum, but as
m_{n}\ll a_{n}
this difference vanishes in the limit and the convergence in distribution still holds.) We will show that
\widetilde{\sigma}^{n}
also converges in distribution to
\rho
in the Skorokhod metric
D([0,T])
, where
T
is an arbitrary time horizon.

By
5.1
and Skorokhod’s representation theorem we can construct
(\Delta_{i}^{n})
on the same space as
\rho
such that
a_{n}^{-1}(\Delta_{1}^{n},\Delta_{2}^{n},\dots)\to(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\dots)
almost surely in
\ell^{2}
. By
5.3
the processes

\rho^{n}(t)=\frac{1}{a_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}^{n}\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq\frac{\Delta_{i}^{n}}{a_{n}}t\right)

converge to
\rho
in probability on the space
D([0,T])
. Moreover, the jump chain of
\rho^{n}
has the same distribution as that of
\widetilde{\sigma}^{n}
. Let
0=\tau_{0}^{n}<\tau_{1}^{n}<\tau_{2}^{n}<\dots<\tau_{n}^{n}
be the jump times of
\rho^{n}
, and let
(\widehat{\Delta}^{n})
be the reordering of
(\Delta^{n})
induced by the jumps of
\rho^{n}
. We see that
\widetilde{\sigma}^{n}(t)\overset{d}{=}\rho^{n}(\lambda^{n}(t))
, where
\lambda^{n}
is the unique piecewise linear function with
\lambda^{n}(\frac{i}{m_{n}})=\tau_{i}
, and (say) such that
\lambda^{n}
is constant beyond time
n/m_{n}
.

We would like to use
\lambda^{n}
as a time change for bounding the Skorokhod distance between
\rho^{n}
and
\sigma^{n}
, but unfortunately it may not fix the endpoint
T
. In order to resolve this issue, we define a modified version
\widetilde{\lambda}^{n}
such that

\widetilde{\lambda}^{n}\left(\frac{i}{m_{n}}\right)=\tau_{i}\wedge T\quad\text{ for }i\leq\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor

and
\widetilde{\lambda}^{n}(T)=T
. We will show that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\widetilde{\lambda}^{n}(t)-t|\overset{p}{\to}0\quad\text{ and }\quad\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\rho^{n}\circ\lambda^{n}-\rho^{n}\circ\widetilde{\lambda}^{n}|\overset{p}{\to}0,

which will establish
\rho^{n}\overset{p}{\to}\widetilde{\sigma}
in the Skorokhod metric. For the first limit, we note that we can bound the supremum by looking only at the endpoints of each piece. Moreover, for all
i\leq\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor
note that
\frac{i}{m_{n}}
is closer to
\tau_{i}\wedge T
than to
\tau_{i}
. Hence we can bound

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\widetilde{\lambda}^{n}(t)-t|\leq\sup_{i\leq\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor}\left|\tau_{i}-\frac{i}{m_{n}}\right|.

By identifying the conditional law of
\tau_{i}-\tau_{i-1}
, we see that the process defined by

Y_{i}=\tau_{i}-\frac{1}{m_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\widehat{\Delta}_{k}^{n}\right)^{-1}

is a mean 0 martingale. We may bound

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{i}\left|\tau_{i}-\frac{i}{m_{n}}\right|>\varepsilon\right)\\
\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{i}|Y_{i}|>\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{i}\frac{1}{m_{n}}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{i}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\widehat{\Delta}_{k}^{n}\right)^{-1}-i\right|>\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\right).

(14)

We bound the first term on the right-hand side by Doob’s inequality and a technical condition as follows. Let
A_{n}
be the event that
\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor}\widehat{\Delta}_{k}^{n}\leq n/2
. We can calculate

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\sup_{i}|Y_{i}|>\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\right\}\cap A_{n}\right)\leq\frac{16}{\varepsilon^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor}^{2}\mathbbm{1}\left(A_{n}\right)\right]\leq\frac{16}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor}\mathbb{E}\left[(Y_{i}-Y_{i-1})^{2}\mathbbm{1}\left(A_{n}\right)\right].

Now for each
i
we can estimate

\mathbb{E}\left[(Y_{i}-Y_{i-1})^{2}\mathbbm{1}\left(A_{n}\right)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[(Y_{i}-Y_{i-1})^{2}\mathbbm{1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}\widehat{\Delta}_{k}^{n}\leq n/2\right)\right].

Conditionally given
(\widehat{\Delta}_{k}^{n})_{k\leq i-1}
, the increment
Y_{i}-Y_{i-1}
has the law of a centered exponential random variable of rate
\beta_{i}=m_{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}\widehat{\Delta}_{k}^{n}\right)
. The conditional second moment of this is
\beta_{i}^{-2}
, and so by the tower law

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(Y_{i}-Y_{i-1})^{2}\mathbbm{1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}\widehat{\Delta}_{k}^{n}\leq n/2\right)\right]

\displaystyle=m_{n}^{-2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(1-\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}\widehat{\Delta}_{k}^{n}\right)^{-2}\mathbbm{1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}\widehat{\Delta}_{k}^{n}\leq n/2\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq m_{n}^{-2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(1-\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n}\times\frac{n}{2}\right)^{-2}\mathbbm{1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}\widehat{\Delta}_{k}^{n}\leq n/2\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq m_{n}^{-2}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{-2}\leq 5m_{n}^{-2}\text{ for sufficiently large $n$. }

Putting this into our initial estimate, we obtain the bound

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\sup_{i}|Y_{i}|>\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\right\}\cap A_{n}\right)\leq\frac{1}{m_{n}}\times\frac{80T}{\varepsilon^{2}}\to 0.

Finally we note that
\mathbb{P}\left(A_{n}\right)\to 1
, and thus
\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{i}|Y_{i}|>\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\right)\to 0
too.

For the second term on the right-hand side of (
14
), we note that the term in the absolute value function is always non-negative, and the supremum is always attained at
i=\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor
. Thus the probability is equal to

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor}\left(\left(1-\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\widehat{\Delta}_{k}^{n}\right)^{-1}-1\right)>\frac{m_{n}\varepsilon}{2}\right).

(15)

On the event
A_{n}
(introduced above) the estimate

\left(1-\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\widehat{\Delta}_{k}^{n}\right)^{-1}\leq 1+C\left(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\widehat{\Delta}_{k}^{n}\right)

holds for every
j
, where
C
is some absolute constant. Working on this event yields that (
15
) is bounded above by

\mathbb{P}\left(C\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor}\left(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\widehat{\Delta}_{k}^{n}\right)>\frac{m_{n}\varepsilon}{2}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(A_{n}^{c}\right)\\
\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor}\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\widehat{\Delta}_{k}^{n}\geq m_{n}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2C}-\frac{T}{n}\right)\right)+o(1).

Since
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor}\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\widehat{\Delta}_{k}^{n}
converges in distribution to
\int_{0}^{T}\widetilde{\sigma}_{s}\,ds
, while
m_{n}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2C}-\frac{T}{n}\right)\to\infty
, this probability tends to zero. This establishes
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\widetilde{\lambda}^{n}(t)-t|\overset{p}{\to}0
.

It remains to bound the distance between
\rho^{n}(\lambda^{n}(t))
and
\rho^{n}(\widetilde{\lambda}^{n}(t))
. Observe that this distance is maximised at
t=T
(we prove this by splitting into the cases
\tau_{\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor}\leq T
and
\tau_{\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor}>T
), where we have

\rho^{n}(\lambda^{n}(T))=\frac{1}{m_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor Tm_{n}\rfloor}\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{n}\quad\text{ and }\rho^{n}(\widetilde{\lambda}^{n}(T))=\frac{1}{m_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{n},

where
I=\sup\{i:\tau_{i}\leq T\}
. For any
\delta>0
the event
B_{\delta}^{n}=\{I\in((T-\delta)m_{n},(T+\delta)m_{n})\}
has probability tending to 1, and on this event we have

|\rho^{n}(\widetilde{\lambda}^{n}(T))-\rho^{n}(\lambda^{n}(T))|\leq\frac{1}{m_{n}}\sum_{i=\lfloor(T-\delta)m_{n}\rfloor}^{\lfloor(T+\delta)m_{n}\rfloor}\widehat{\Delta}_{i}^{n}.

As
n\to\infty
the right-hand side converges in distribution to
\widetilde{\sigma}_{T+\delta}-\widetilde{\sigma}_{T-\delta}
. Thus, by picking
\delta
suitably for each
\varepsilon
, we see that
\mathbb{P}\left(|\rho^{n}(\widetilde{\lambda}^{n}(T))-\rho^{n}(\lambda^{n}(T))|>\varepsilon\right)\to 0
. This proves
\widetilde{\sigma}^{n}\overset{d}{\to}\rho
.

Thus we have identified
\widetilde{\sigma}_{t}\overset{d}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Delta_{i}\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq\Delta_{i}t\right)
. It remains only to identify
(\Delta_{i})
. By
5.1
we know that for any
k
we have
a_{n}^{-1}(\Delta_{1}^{n},\dots,\Delta_{k}^{n})\overset{d}{\to}(\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{k})
, so we will show the same sequence converges to
(\Theta_{1},\dots,\Theta_{k})
. Let
L^{n}:\{0,1,\dots,n\}\to\mathbb{Z}
be the Łukasiewicz path of the discrete tree
T^{n}
of size
n
, and construct a càdlàg function
e^{n}\in D([0,1])
by taking

e^{n}(t)=a_{n}^{-1}L^{n}(\lfloor nt\rfloor).

Then we have
e^{n}\overset{d}{\to}\mathbbm{e}
, where
\mathbbm{e}
is a normalised
\alpha
-stable excursion (this result is implicit in
[
24
]
or
[
33
]
). Now consider a map
\mathfrak{J}:D([0,1])\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
given as follows: for any
f\in D([0,1])
, we identify the
k
largest upward jumps of
f
and arrange them into decreasing order (appending zeros if there are fewer than
k
such jumps). This gives a well-defined continuous map, and applying it to the above convergence gives

\mathfrak{J}(e^{n})\overset{d}{\to}\mathfrak{J}(\mathbbm{e}).

We identify
\mathfrak{J}(e^{n})\overset{d}{=}a_{n}^{-1}((\Delta_{1}^{n}-1)^{+},\dots,(\Delta_{k}^{n}-1)^{+})
and
\mathfrak{J}(\mathbbm{e})\overset{d}{=}(\Theta_{1},\dots,\Theta_{k})
. It follows that
a_{n}^{-1}(\Delta_{1}^{n},\dots,\Delta_{k}^{n})\overset{d}{\to}(\Theta_{1},\dots,\Theta_{k})
and so
(\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{k})\overset{d}{=}(\Theta_{1},\dots,\Theta_{k})
as distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. As
k
was arbitrary, the result follows.
∎

Recall that if
M_{1}\sim\mathrm{ML}(1-1/\alpha,1-1/\alpha)
then

\mathbb{E}\left[M_{1}^{k}\right]=\frac{\Gamma(k+1)\Gamma(1-\tfrac{1}{\alpha})}{\Gamma((k+1)(1-\tfrac{1}{\alpha}))}

for
k\geq 1
and (by Carleman’s condition) the distribution is determined by its moments. So it is sufficient to show that
\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{1}^{k}\right]=\alpha^{-k}\mathbb{E}\left[M_{1}^{k}\right]
.

In what follows, denote by
\Psi_{\alpha}
the characteristic exponent of
L
(the spectrally positive
\alpha
-stable Lévy process such that
L_{1}
has density
p
) and by
\Psi_{\alpha-1}
the characteristic exponent of
\sigma
. We will prove that the result holds for all
k\in(0,\infty)
(including non-integral
k
). We begin by restricting to the case that
(k+1)\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\not\in\mathbb{Z}
.

By the measure change formula we have the identity

\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{1}\geq t\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\widetilde{\sigma}_{s}\,ds}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{p(-\sigma_{t})}{p(0)}\right].

For any
k>0
we can write

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{1}^{k}\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}kt^{k-1}\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{1}\geq t\right)\,dt=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{0}^{\infty}kt^{k-1}e^{-\varepsilon t}\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{1}\geq t\right)\,dt,

using monotone convergence to prove the limit on the right. For each fixed
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}kt^{k-1}e^{-\varepsilon t}\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{1}\geq t\right)\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p(0)}\int_{0}^{\infty}kt^{k-1}e^{-\varepsilon t}\mathbb{E}\left[p(-\sigma_{t})\right]\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi p(0)}\int_{0}^{\infty}kt^{k-1}e^{-\varepsilon t}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\lambda\sigma_{t}}e^{-\Psi_{\alpha}(\lambda)}\,d\lambda\right]\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi p(0)}\int_{0}^{\infty}kt^{k-1}e^{-\varepsilon t}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{i\lambda\sigma_{t}}\right]e^{-\Psi_{\alpha}(\lambda)}\,d\lambda\right)\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi p(0)}\int_{0}^{\infty}kt^{k-1}e^{-\varepsilon t}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-t\Psi_{\alpha-1}(\lambda)}e^{-\Psi_{\alpha}(\lambda)}\,d\lambda\right)\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi p(0)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\Psi_{\alpha}(\lambda)}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}kt^{k-1}e^{-(\varepsilon+\Psi_{\alpha-1}(\lambda))t}\,dt\right)\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi p(0)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\Psi_{\alpha}(\lambda)}\cdot\left(\varepsilon+\Psi_{\alpha-1}(\lambda)\right)^{-k}k\Gamma(k)\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{2\pi p(0)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\varepsilon+\Psi_{\alpha-1}(\lambda)\right)^{-k}e^{-\Psi_{\alpha}(\lambda)}\,d\lambda.

Now if
j\geq 0
is an integer such that
\alpha j-(\alpha-1)k<-1
, we can show that (still for fixed
\varepsilon>0
)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\varepsilon+\Psi_{\alpha-1}(\lambda))^{k}\Psi_{\alpha}(\lambda)^{j}\,d\lambda=0.

Indeed, by contour integration, for every
R>0
we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\varepsilon+\Psi_{\alpha-1}(\lambda))^{k}\Psi_{\alpha}(\lambda)^{j}\,d\lambda=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\varepsilon+\Psi_{\alpha-1}(\lambda+iR))^{k}\Psi_{\alpha}(\lambda+iR)^{j}\,d\lambda

but the modulus of the right-hand side is at most a constant multiple of
\int_{\mathbb{R}}(R^{2}+x^{2})^{\frac{\alpha j-(\alpha-1)k}{2}}\,dx
, which tends to 0 as
R\to\infty
.

This condition on
j
is met for

, where
l=\lfloor(k+1)(1-\frac{1}{\alpha})\rfloor
. It follows that

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{1}^{k}\right]=\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{2\pi p(0)}\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\varepsilon+\Psi_{\alpha-1}(\lambda))^{-k}\left(e^{-\Psi_{\alpha}(\lambda)}-1+\Psi_{\alpha}(\lambda)-\dots-\frac{(-\Psi_{\alpha}(\lambda))^{l-1}}{(l-1)!}\right)\,d\lambda.

We may now apply dominated convergence to obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{1}^{k}\right]=\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{2\pi p(0)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Psi_{\alpha-1}(\lambda)^{-k}\left(e^{-\Psi_{\alpha}(\lambda)}-1+\Psi_{\alpha}(\lambda)-\dots-\frac{(-\Psi_{\alpha}(\lambda))^{l-1}}{(l-1)!}\right)\,d\lambda.

Now we have the relations

\Psi_{\alpha-1}(\lambda)=\alpha(-\Psi_{\alpha}(\lambda))^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\quad\text{ and }\quad-i\Psi_{\alpha-1}(\lambda)=\frac{d}{d\lambda}\left(-\Psi_{\alpha}(\lambda)\right),

which are valid for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. (Some care is required taking fractional powers of complex numbers. For example, given

and
z\in\mathbb{C}
, we can only say
(z^{a})^{b}=z^{ab}
if
a\arg(z)\in(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})
or
b
is an integer. Fortunately, the results above turn out to be true whenever
\lambda
is real). Using these, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{1}^{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{i\Gamma(k+1)}{2\pi p(0)\alpha^{k+1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(-\Psi_{\alpha})^{-(k+1)(1-\frac{1}{\alpha})}\left(e^{-\Psi_{\alpha}}-1+\dots-\frac{(-\Psi_{\alpha})^{l-1}}{(l-1)!}\right)\frac{d}{d\lambda}(-\Psi_{\alpha})\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{i\Gamma(k+1)}{2\pi p(0)\alpha^{k+1}}\int_{\gamma}z^{-(k+1)(1-\frac{1}{\alpha})}\left(e^{z}-1-z-\dots-\frac{z^{l-1}}{(l-1)!}\right)\,dz,

where
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
is the curve given by
\gamma(\lambda)=-\Psi_{\alpha}(\lambda)=(-i\lambda)^{\alpha}
.

Now let
G(z)=z^{-(k+1)(1-\frac{1}{\alpha})}\left(e^{z}-1-\dots-\frac{z^{l-1}}{(l-1)!}\right)
. We evaluate
\int_{\gamma}G(z)\,dz
via contour integration as follows. Note that for each
R>0
,
\gamma(-R)
has negative real part and positive imaginary part, while
\gamma(R)=\overline{\gamma(-R)}
. We can replace
\gamma|_{[-R,R]}
by the union of the following four pieces:

•

The vertical line from
\gamma(-R)
down to
\textnormal{Re}(\gamma(-R))
;

•

The horizontal line from
\textnormal{Re}(\gamma(-R))
to 0 (where we take
\arg(z)=\pi
to deal with the branch cut for

;

•

The horizontal line from 0 to
\textnormal{Re}(\gamma(-R))=\textnormal{Re}(\gamma(R))
(where we take
\arg(z)=-\pi
instead);

•

The vertical line from
\textnormal{Re}(\gamma(R))
down to
\gamma(R)
.

As

\pm e^{\mp i\pi(k+1)(1-\frac{1}{\alpha})}\Gamma\left(1-(k+1)\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\right).

In total, we see that

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma}G(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=\left(e^{-i\pi(k+1)(1-\frac{1}{\alpha})}-e^{i\pi(k+1)(1-\frac{1}{\alpha})}\right)\Gamma\left(1-(k+1)\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-2i\sin\left(\pi(k+1)\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\right)\Gamma\left(1-(k+1)\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{-2i\pi}{\Gamma\left((k+1)\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\right)},

using the reflection formula for the Gamma function to obtain the last line. Finally we have

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{1}^{k}\right]=\frac{i\Gamma(k+1)}{2\pi p(0)\alpha^{k+1}}\times\frac{-2i\pi}{\Gamma\left((k+1)\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\right)}=\alpha^{-k}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma\left((k+1)\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\right)}\times\frac{1}{\alpha p(0)}.

(16)

and is continuous in
k

k\mapsto\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{1}^{k}\right]
is continuous for

. It follows that the left- and right-hand sides of (
16

k\geq 0
. Using that
\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{1}^{0}\right]=1

\frac{1}{\alpha p(0)}=\Gamma(1-\frac{1}{\alpha})
, completing the proof.

By condition (ii) we know
e^{-\Psi_{L}(\lambda)}
is integrable, and so

has a density by Fourier inversion, namely

p(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-i\lambda x}e^{-\Psi_{L}(\lambda)}\,d\lambda.

As discussed in
Section 2

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}(c+\sigma_{s})\,ds\right)p(-c-\sigma_{t})\mathbbm{1}\left(\sigma_{t}<\infty\right)\right]=p(-c).

\sigma

\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma_{t}<\infty\right)=e^{-at}

Using the Fourier representation of the density, the left-hand side of (

\frac{e^{-at}}{2\pi}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(i(c+\sigma_{t})\lambda+\int_{0}^{t}(c+\sigma_{s})\,ds\right)e^{-\Psi_{L}(\lambda)}\,d\lambda\right].

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\exp\left(i(c+\sigma_{t})\lambda+\int_{0}^{t}(c+\sigma_{s})\,ds\right)e^{-\Psi_{L}(\lambda)}\right|\,d\lambda\right]\\

We already know that the integral term is finite, so we just need to prove that the expectation is finite too. We can represent
\sigma_{s}=b^{2}s+J_{s}
, where
J
is a pure jump subordinator. Then we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}(c+\sigma_{s})\,ds\right)\right]=\exp\left(ct+\frac{1}{2}b^{2}t^{2}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}J_{s}\,ds\right)\right].

We can apply Campbell’s formula to the rightmost expectation: let
\Pi\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}

J

J_{t}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(s,x)\in\Pi\\

\int_{0}^{t}J_{s}\,ds=\sum_{s\leq t}(t-s)x

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}J_{s}\,ds\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\left(e^{(t-s)x}-1\right)\,ds\,x\nu(dx)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(e^{tx}-1-tx\right)\,\nu(dx)\right).

This quantity is finite by condition (i). Thus the exchange is valid, and so the left-hand side of (
17

\frac{e^{-at}}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(i\lambda\sigma_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,ds\right)\right]\exp\left(c(t+i\lambda)-\Psi_{L}(\lambda)\right)\,d\lambda.

Once again, we can decompose
\sigma_{t}=b^{2}t+J_{t}

\frac{e^{-at}}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(i\lambda J_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}J_{s}\,ds\right)\right]\exp\left(b^{2}i\lambda t+\frac{1}{2}b^{2}t^{2}+c(t+i\lambda)-\Psi_{L}(\lambda)\right)\,d\lambda.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(i\lambda J_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}J_{s}\,ds\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(e^{i\lambda x}\left(\frac{e^{tx}-1}{x}\right)-t\right)\,x\nu(dx)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(e^{(t+i\lambda)x}-e^{i\lambda x}-tx\right)\,\nu(dx)\right),

noting that all of these integrals are finite by condition (i).
Thus, the left-hand side of (
17
) can be written as

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(-at+\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(e^{(t+i\lambda)x}-e^{i\lambda x}-tx\right)\,\nu(dx)+b^{2}i\lambda t+\frac{1}{2}b^{2}t^{2}+c(t+i\lambda)-\Psi_{L}(\lambda)\right)\,d\lambda.

For brevity, let us denote by
H(t,\lambda)
the expression inside the exponential. It is now useful to separate the drift and Brownian term in
\Psi_{L}
from the (compensated) jumps: write

\Psi_{L}(\lambda)=ai\lambda+\frac{1}{2}b^{2}\lambda^{2}+\Psi_{0}(\lambda),

where

\Psi_{0}(\lambda)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}(e^{i\lambda x}-1-i\lambda x)\,\nu(dx).

Condition (i) implies that
\Psi_{0}
has an analytic continuation to all of
\mathbb{C}
, given by the same formula. We now observe that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(e^{(t+i\lambda)x}-e^{i\lambda x}-tx\right)\,\nu(dx)=\Psi_{0}(\lambda)-\Psi_{0}(\lambda-it),

and so we have

\displaystyle H(t,\lambda)

\displaystyle=-at+\Psi_{0}(\lambda)-\Psi_{0}(\lambda-it)+b^{2}i\lambda t+\frac{1}{2}b^{2}t^{2}+c(t+i\lambda)-\Psi_{L}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=-at-\Psi_{0}(\lambda-it)+b^{2}i\lambda t+\frac{1}{2}b^{2}t^{2}+c(t+i\lambda)-ai\lambda-\frac{1}{2}b^{2}\lambda^{2}

\displaystyle=-ai(\lambda-it)-\frac{1}{2}b^{2}(\lambda-it)^{2}+ic(t-i\lambda)-\Psi_{0}(\lambda-it)

\displaystyle=ic(\lambda-it)-\Psi_{L}(\lambda-it).

In summary, we have shown that

e^{-at}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}(c+\sigma_{s})\,ds\right)p(-c-\sigma_{t})\right]=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(ic(\lambda-it)-\Psi_{L}(\lambda-it)\right)\,d\lambda,

and we would like to show that this common quantity is equal to

. By Fourier inversion we know

p(-c)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(ic\lambda-\Psi_{L}(\lambda))\,d\lambda

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(ic(\lambda-it)-\Psi_{L}(\lambda-it)\right)\,d\lambda=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(ic\lambda-\Psi_{L}(\lambda)\right)\,d\lambda.

To this end, set

\gamma
, we have
\oint_{\gamma}G(z)\,dz=0
— this is by Fubini’s theorem, which is valid due to condition (i). Thus by Morera’s theorem
G
is holomorphic on
\mathbb{C}
, so
\exp(G(z))
is holomorphic too.

Thus for any closed contour
\gamma
in the complex plane, we have
\oint_{\gamma}\exp(G(z))\,dz=0
. We consider the rectangular contour with vertices at
\pm R
,
\pm R-it
and then take
R\to\infty
. Observe that

\oint_{\gamma}\exp(G(z))\,dz=\int_{-R}^{R}\exp(ic(\lambda-it)-\Psi_{L}(\lambda-it))\,d\lambda-\int_{-R}^{R}\exp(ic\lambda-\Psi_{L}(\lambda))\,d\lambda\\
+i\int_{0}^{t}\exp(G(R-is))\,ds-i\int_{0}^{t}\exp(G(-R-is))\,ds,

and so we will obtain (
18
) if we can show that the final two integrals tend to 0 as
R\to\infty
.

To this end, we will show that the integrands of these two integrals tend to 0 uniformly: that is, for
z=\lambda-is
,
s\in[0,t]
we have
\exp(G(z))\to 0
uniformly in
s
as
|\lambda|\to\infty
. As
|\exp(G(z))|=\exp(\textnormal{Re}\left(G(z)\right))
it suffices to show
\textnormal{Re}\left(G(z)\right)\to-\infty
uniformly. It is straightforward to calculate

\textnormal{Re}\left(\Psi_{L}(\lambda-is)\right)=as-\frac{1}{2}b^{2}s^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}b^{2}-\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(e^{sx}\cos(|\lambda|x)-1-sx\right)\,\nu(dx).

Note that the term
as-\frac{1}{2}b^{2}s^{2}
is bounded. We can decompose the integral as

-\int_{0}^{\infty}(e^{sx}\cos(|\lambda|x)-1-sx)\,\nu(dx)\\
=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{sx}(1-\cos(|\lambda|x))\nu(dx)-\int_{0}^{\infty}(e^{sx}-1-sx)\,\nu(dx).

The second term is bounded by condition (i), while the first term is lower bounded by removing the
e^{sx}
term. Hence we have

\textnormal{Re}\left(\Psi_{L}(\lambda-is)\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}b^{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-\cos(|\lambda|x))\,\nu(dx)+O(1)

and now we recognise that

\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}b^{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-\cos(|\lambda|x))\,\nu(dx)=\textnormal{Re}\left(\Psi_{L}(\lambda)\right)\to\infty,

by condition (ii). This completes the proof.
∎

Proof.

Note that it is sufficient to prove the convergence in
D([0,T])
for arbitrary
T>0
. So fix
T>0
. We begin by handling the case that
\mathbf{X}^{(n)}
and
\mathbf{X}
are deterministic, say
\mathbf{X}^{(n)}=x^{(n)}
and
\mathbf{X}=x
. We can construct
S^{(n)}
and
S
on the same probability space by using the same
E_{i}
for all of them. In this setting we will prove that
S^{(n)}\to S
in probability with respect to the Skorokhod metric on
[0,T]
. We introduce an intermediate sequence of processes
R^{(n)}
defined by

R^{(n)}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq x_{i}^{(n)}t\right).

Let
d_{D}
be the Skorokhod distance on
[0,T]
. We will show that
d_{D}(S^{(n)},R^{(n)})\to 0
in probability and
d_{D}(R^{(n)},S)\to 0
in probability.

For the first of these statements, we will show convergence in probability with respect to the uniform norm (which implies the desired result). For any
t\in[0,T]
we have

\displaystyle|S^{(n)}(t)-R^{(n)}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|x_{i}^{(n)}-x_{i}|\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq x_{i}^{(n)}t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|x_{i}^{(n)}-x_{i}|\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq x_{i}^{(n)}T\right).

This bound does not depend on
t
and so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|S^{(n)}(t)-R^{(n)}(t)|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|x_{i}^{(n)}-x_{i}|\left(1-e^{-x_{i}^{(n)}T}\right)

\displaystyle\leq T\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|x_{i}^{(n)}-x_{i}|\cdot x_{i}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\leq T||x^{(n)}-x||_{2}\cdot||x^{(n)}||_{2}.

Now
x^{(n)}
is bounded in
\ell_{2}
and
x^{(n)}-x\to 0
in
\ell_{2}
, so this expectation tends to zero. In particular, by Markov’s inequality we have, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|S^{(n)}(t)-R^{(n)}(t)|>\varepsilon\right)\to 0,

establishing the first convergence in probability.

For the second convergence, some more care is needed. Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta>0
be given. We will show that for sufficiently large
n
we have
\mathbb{P}\left(d_{D}(R^{(n)},S)>\varepsilon\right)<\delta
. We introduce a new notation
||\cdot||_{2,N}
defined by

||u||_{2,N}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty}u_{i}^{2}}.

We can interpret this as the
\ell_{2}
-norm of the projection of
u
onto the subspace of codimension
N
spanned by the
i
th unit vectors for
i>N
. Note that for any
u\in\ell_{2}
we have
||u||_{2,N}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
. For any
N
and any
n
we can estimate

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty}x_{i}\left(\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq x_{i}^{(n)}T\right)+\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq x_{i}T\right)\right)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty}x_{i}\left(\mathbb{P}\left(E_{i}\leq x_{i}^{(n)}T\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(E_{i}\leq x_{i}T\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq T\sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty}x_{i}(x_{i}^{(n)}+x_{i})

\displaystyle\leq T\left(||x||_{2,N}^{2}+||x||_{2,N}\cdot||x^{(n)}||_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq T||x||_{2,N}\left(||x||_{2}+||x^{(n)}||_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C||x||_{2,N},

where
C
is an absolute constant (here we are using the fact that
x^{(n)}
is bounded in
\ell_{2}
). This bound does not depend on
n
, and goes to zero as
N\to\infty
. By Markov’s inequality we can choose
N
large enough such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty}x_{i}\left(\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq x_{i}^{(n)}T\right)+\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq x_{i}T\right)\right)\right|\leq\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-\frac{1}{2}\delta

for every
n\geq 1
. Let
A_{1}^{(n)}
denote this event.

Having chosen
N
, we define truncated processes

\widetilde{R}^{(n)}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq x_{i}^{(n)}t\right)\quad\text{ and }\quad\widetilde{S}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq x_{i}t\right).

Let
B
be the event that
\widetilde{S}
does not jump at time
T
, and note that this has probability 1. Let
K
be the (random) number of times
\widetilde{S}
jumps before time
T
, and let
\pi:[K]\to[N]
be the unique injection such that these jumps occur at times

\frac{E_{\pi(1)}}{x_{\pi(1)}}<\frac{E_{\pi(2)}}{x_{\pi(2)}}<\dots<\frac{E_{\pi(K)}}{x_{\pi(K)}}.

Let
A_{2}^{(n)}
be the event that
\widetilde{R}^{(n)}
also has
K
jumps before time
T
and that these are given by

\frac{E_{\pi(1)}}{x_{\pi(1)}^{(n)}}<\frac{E_{\pi(2)}}{x_{\pi(2)}^{(n)}}<\dots<\frac{E_{\pi(K)}}{x_{\pi(K)}^{(n)}},

using the same permutation
\pi
. We observe that on the full measure event
B
, the events
A_{2}^{(n)}
necessarily occur for all sufficiently large
n
, and so
\mathbb{P}\left(A_{2}^{(n)}\right)\to 1.
On the event
A_{2}^{(n)}
we may construct a time change
\lambda^{(n)}:[0,T]\to[0,T]
as follows: we take the minimal piecewise linear function such that
\lambda^{(n)}(0)=0
,
\lambda^{(n)}(T)=T
and

\lambda^{(n)}\left(\frac{E_{\pi(i)}}{x_{\pi(i)}}\right)=\frac{E_{\pi(i)}}{x_{\pi(i)}^{(n)}}

for
i=1,2,\dots,K
. Observe that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\lambda^{(n)}(t)-t\right|\leq\max_{i\leq K}\left|\frac{E_{\pi(i)}}{x_{\pi(i)}}-\frac{E_{\pi(i)}}{x_{\pi(i)}^{(n)}}\right|

which converges to 0 almost surely (on the event that
\lambda^{(n)}
is defined). Letting
A_{3}^{(n)}
be the event that
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\lambda^{(n)}(t)-t\right|\leq\varepsilon
, we see that
\mathbb{P}\left(A_{3}^{(n)}\right)\to 1
. Note that
\widetilde{R}^{(n)}(\lambda^{(n)}(t))=\widetilde{S}(t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
. In particular, we can compute

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|R^{(n)}(\lambda^{(n)}(t))-S(t)|

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}\left(\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq x_{i}^{(n)}t\right)-\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq x_{i}t\right)\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\widetilde{R}^{(n)}(t)-\widetilde{S}(t)+\sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty}x_{i}\left(\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq x_{i}^{(n)}t\right)-\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq x_{i}t\right)\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty}x_{i}\left(\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq x_{i}^{(n)}t\right)-\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq x_{i}t\right)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty}x_{i}\left(\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq x_{i}^{(n)}T\right)+\mathbbm{1}\left(E_{i}\leq x_{i}T\right)\right)\right|.

On the event
A_{1}^{(n)}
this quantity is at most
\varepsilon
. Thus on the event
A^{(n)}:=A_{1}^{(n)}\cap A_{2}^{(n)}\cap A_{3}^{(n)}
we have
d_{D}(R^{(n)},S)\leq\varepsilon
. Note that
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(A^{(n)}\right)\geq 1-\frac{1}{2}\delta
, and so for sufficiently large
n
we have
\mathbb{P}\left(A^{(n)}\right)\geq 1-\delta
. Thus
R^{(n)}\to S
in probability in the Skorokhod metric.

This completes the proof in the case that
\mathbf{X}^{(n)}
and
\mathbf{X}
are deterministic. For the general case, we use Skorokhod’s representation theorem to construct
\mathbf{X}^{(n)}
and
\mathbf{X}
on the same space such that
\mathbf{X}^{(n)}\to\mathbf{X}
almost surely. Constructing our exponential random variables also on this space, independent of everything else, and using them to define all processes
S^{(n)}
and
S
, we deduce from the previous case that for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(d_{D}(S^{(n)},S)\geq\varepsilon\ \Big|\ \mathbf{X}^{(n)},\mathbf{X}\right)\to 0\text{ almost surely.}

By the bounded convergence theorem we may take expectations of the above to see that

\mathbb{P}\left(d_{D}(S^{(n)},S)\geq\varepsilon\right)\to 0.

As
\varepsilon
was arbitrary we see that
S^{(n)}\to S
in probability for this coupling. In particular, the laws of
S^{(n)}
converge weakly to that of
S
.
∎
