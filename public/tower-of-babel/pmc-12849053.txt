Clinical governance and staffing for healthcare quality: perceptions of nurses, doctors, and senior managers at two tertiary hospitals in Jamaica
Authors: Camelia Thompson, Desmalee Holder Nevins, Dawn Walters, Cameal Chin-Bailey, Elon Thompson, Minerva Thame, Kenneth James
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo describe the characteristics of effective workforce and staffing culture among doctors and nurses at two tertiary hospitals in Jamaica, and to explore perceptions of senior managers relating to the health workforce for the delivery of quality care.MethodsA mixed-methods study was done among 216 nurses and 112 doctors, and 17 senior managers. Data on patient safety culture and the effective workforce component were collected for quantitative component. In-depth interviews were done for the qualitative component. Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics, and qualitative data were thematically analyzed and compared with selected elements of the quantitative data.ResultsStaffing culture for patient safety among doctors and nurses was poor (35% positive score). More nurses (73.6%) than doctors (34.8%) reported working 40–59 h per week. A higher proportion of nurses (85%) than doctors (67.9%) practiced responsibly with consideration of fatigue and human factors (p = 0.001), with a significant difference by institution (Institution A – 75.0%, Institution B – 81.6%; p = 0.009). Fewer than 70% of respondents reported receiving annual performance appraisals. Qualitative findings revealed issues with staff capacity development, performance management, and persistent staffing challenges, which contributed to gaps in staffing culture for patient safety.ConclusionStaffing culture for patient safety was suboptimal among doctors and nurses. Key challenges identified by senior managers include inadequate staffing, limited capacity development, and weak performance appraisal systems. Strategic investment in workforce development, effective performance management, and evidence-based staffing policies is critical to strengthen clinical governance and achieve quality health care delivery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13826-y.Keywords: Clinical governance, Staffing culture, Patient safety, Jamaica

Background

The delivery of quality health care is key to achieving national and global development goals. The United Nations Sustainable Development Goal #3 is geared at achieving ‘good health and well-being.’ Achieving Universal Health Coverage is captured in Target 3.8, and access to quality essential health-care services is a key component [1]. Quality has been broadly defined in many spheres using terms such as conformance to specification/requirements, excellence, value, fitness for use and meeting one’s expectations [2]. Quality is an ever-evolving concept and means different things to different people. The providers, payers and patients all have different expectations of what quality should be. Changes in disease and demographic profiles, changes in technology and new and re-emerging issues influence the expectations of the health system users. Patient factors influence quality such as their knowledge relating to health, access to care and their culture. System-related factors such as poor organizational culture, inadequate infrastructure, lack of a clear organizational mission, system shock such as leadership turnover and change in other operational systems are known to impact quality [2–4]. While the definitions for quality vary, there are common themes that have been encapsulated in the descriptions of quality health care. These include quality health care delivered to patients being safe, effective, people-centered, timely, equitable, and efficient [5, 6].

Staffing and clinical governance are interdependent components of healthcare systems that have a direct impact on the quality of care and patient outcomes. Staffing involves distributing healthcare professionals according to their skills, roles, and the demands of their workloads, whereas clinical governance pertains to the processes through which organizations maintain accountability and facilitate ongoing improvements in service delivery [7, 8]. The concept of clinical governance emerged out of the National Health Services (NHS) United Kingdom (UK), in response to the Bristol heart scandal [9]. The framework of clinical governance from the NHS highlights that the core elements required for achieving quality outcomes are: education and training, clinical audits, clinical effectiveness, research and development, openness and risk management [10, 11]. Other frameworks such as the Australian Capital Territory, posit that to achieve person centered, safe and effective care, there needs to be governance, leadership and culture, clinical performance and effectiveness, patient safety and improvement systems, safe environment for care delivery and partnership with consumers. Monitoring, evaluation and improvement should be ongoing [12]. The ultimate aim of clinical governance is quality improvement. Clinical governance frameworks explicitly recognize human resources as foundational to quality care.

Globally, inadequate health care provider and inequity in their distribution are threats to health care quality and patient safety [13]. The availability of adequate human resources for the delivery of quality care is one of the key building blocks of health systems [14]. In Jamaica, the health worker to population density is 0.43 per 1,000 population for doctors and 1.4 per 1,000 population for nurses [15]. The World Health Organization recommends minimum health worker (doctors, nurses and midwives) density of 4.45 per 1000 population to meet the related Sustainable Development Goals as well as to achieve Universal Health Coverage [16]. The migration of health workers, in particular nurses and those with specialist training, has been prevalent in the Jamaican context [15, 17].

While the number of health workers is critical, skill mix is also important. Lankshear, Sheldon and Maynard (2005) in a systematic review reported that among the studies assessed, better patient outcomes were noted with higher nursing staff, and richer skill mix [18]. In a retrospective longitudinal cohort study, Griffiths et al. (2019) examined the association between patient outcome and nursing staffing levels. Mortality increased by 3% for every day that the nursing staff was below the staffing mean. The staff mix (registered nurses to nursing assistants) was also noted to be an important factor in patient outcome; both low and high levels of nursing assistants were associated with higher mortality rates [19]; the mortality effect of higher level of nursing assistants possibly due to shunting of duties to less experienced/less competent staff. Other studies parallel the aforementioned findings [20, 21], however Cook et al. (2012) did not report improved patient outcome when a minimum nursing staffing level was implemented in the State of California [22]. Quality outcomes are more than simply a function of numbers.

Adequacy of physicians is associated with better patient outcomes [23, 24]. Nevertheless, other physician related factors mediate patient outcomes. Studies have examined physician burnout and medical errors. Although some have found that physician burnout was associated with more medical errors [25–27], others have found no significant associations [28, 29]. Burnout is more commonly seen among physicians [30], but similar associations have been documented among nurses [21, 30–32].

The World Health Organization’s framework on integrated people-centered health services emphasizes investing in the health workforce as a strategic priority [33]. The SDG Target 3.c makes specific reference to health workforce and advocates for substantial increase in health financing and the recruitment, development, training and retention of the health workforce in developing countries, especially in least Developed Countries and Small Island Developing States. Issues with the health workforce may lead to reduced compliance with the systems and structures in place for ensuring quality [1]. It is therefore imperative to examine the health workforce in the context of delivery of quality health care in Jamaica, a developing country setting, given the paucity of data that exist.

Hospitals serve as critical environments for assessing workforce adequacy and governance structures that directly influence patient safety and quality of care. In these settings, where challenges with quality of care often predominate due to high patient acuity, limited resources, and complex service delivery, staffing emerges as a pivotal determinant of healthcare performance [6, 34]. This study sought to describe the characteristics of effective workforce and staffing culture among doctors and nurses at two tertiary hospitals in Jamaica, and to explore perceptions of senior managers relating to the health workforce for the delivery of quality care.

Methods

Between 15/03/2019 to 14/06/2019, a mixed-methods study was conducted, utilizing a convergent parallel design and sought to assess patient safety culture and clinical governance in two tertiary (Type A) hospitals in Jamaica. This design facilitated the simultaneous collection and independent analysis of both quantitative and qualitative data, which were later integrated during interpretation to obtain a comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon studied. The quantitative component utilized a cross-sectional study design and a phenomenological design was used for the qualitative component. Type A hospitals were selected for this study as they represent Jamaica’s highest-level referral institutions, providing the broadest range of secondary and tertiary healthcare services. These facilities also employ the largest complement of healthcare. Nationally, there are three Type A hospitals, two located within the South East Regional Health Authority (SERHA) and one within the Western Regional Health Authority (WRHA). The study included the two institutions in SERHA, as the WRHA facility was not fully operational during the study period. These hospitals typically have a bed capacity ranging from 300 to 500, serving as major teaching and referral centers within the national health system.

For the quantitative component, 328 nurses and doctors were included in the study using a stratified random sampling technique, with sample drawn proportionate to size of nurses and doctors in each institution. The determination of the sample was based on estimates of The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture (AHRQ HSOPSC - one of the tools used in this study) Report 2018, which highlighted a positive percentage score for non-punitive response to errors of 47%; the biggest area for potential improvement of the 12 composites measured among hospitals [35]. The estimated number of doctors and nurses at the institutions studied was 2257. Assuming these figures and applying the usual 95% confidence interval, and 5% margin of error, the minimum required sample was 328 [36]. A stratified random sampling method was used to select doctors (n = 116) and nurses (n = 212) for the sample. Stratification was done by hospitals and by professional groups (doctors and nurses) and the sample was drawn proportionate to size. Twelve potential participants of the 340 approached refused participation in the study and were replaced (refusal rate = 3.5%). Where the participant refused to participate in the study, another eligible participant was randomly selected.

A self-administered questionnaire was used to capture data on the socio-demographic characteristics (age, gender, and marital status) of health workers, training and work history, staff position, area of work and length of time working in respective positions, hospitals and units among nurses and doctors. Data were collected on effective workforce (seven questions) as a key area of clinical governance. The questions were guided by the Victorian Clinical Governance Policy Framework, which has key components for monitoring clinical governance activities [37]. The AHRQ HSOPSC was used with permission to examine patient safety culture. The AHRQ HSPOSC has been examined and has been found to be reliable and psychometrically sound [38]. The staffing composite uses four items to assess whether there are enough staff to handle the workload and if work hours are appropriate to provide the best care for patients (Supplemental File). The questionnaire was pre-tested and used without adaptation to the standardized tools. The psychometric properties of the AHRQ HSOPSC tool used in this study has been assessed and previously published [39].

Quantitative data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) Version 21. Descriptive statistics were used to describe participants’ profile and reporting the frequency of the individual items for effective workforce and the ‘staffing’ composite for patient safety. A median ‘staffing’ score was computed and differences in mean by specific variables were examined using Mann Whitney U and Kruskal Wallis tests as appropriate. ‘Staffing’ score was reduced to a binary variable, with a positive score determined by averaging the positive percent scores received for each item. Neutral responses were not considered in the determination of a percent positive score. To identify independent predictors of a positive ‘staffing’ score, variables with p-value of 0.1 or less in bivariate analyses were entered in a logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of positive ‘staffing’ score. The Chi-squared test was used to determine the association between variables related to effective workforce, by staff position and institution. Where expected values less than five exceeded 20%, the p-value for the Fishers Exact Test was reported.

Seventeen senior managers (working in the position for at least six months) were purposively selected for the qualitative arm of the study. Senior administrators/managers were selected because of their expected knowledge/ability to speak about the systems and structures that were in place to ensure the delivery of quality care to patients. Senior managers have administrative roles and oversight for various categories of workers who are directly or indirectly involved in patient care. A semi-structured interview guide (Supplemental File) was used to direct the qualitative enquiry. Basic demographic and work-related information were obtained from all participants. Managers were also asked to highlight their perspectives relating staffing for the delivery of quality health care and key challenges faced. Probing was done as appropriate. All the interviews were audio-recorded, and field notes were made throughout the process. The average time to complete the interviews was 35 min.

For the qualitative component, recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim then analyzed manually for emergent categories. Data analysis was iterative starting at the point of transcription. The process started with the researcher and one additional person on the research team working independently to read and highlight key words and phrases then categorising and coding into themes; this was done for the first set of five interview scripts. The findings were discussed and then the categories and codes for the rest of the data set were agreed on. On completion of the process, the findings were summarised, and member checking was done with some participants in the study to ensure that the summaries prepared were an accurate representation of the information they shared during the interview. The preliminary findings were also shared with other research participants/peers for corroboration purposes.

Qualitative data were reported thematically. For purposes of confidentiality, only institution name was used when reporting direct quotations. This was done to maintain anonymity of participants as the positions/participants are unique and otherwise may be easily traced. Both quantitative and qualitative data are discussed together.

All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrolment in the study. The researchers obtained consent face-to-face in the presence of an independent witness who was not affiliated with the research. The physical consent form was signed by the researcher, the respondent, and the independent witness. The study was conducted in strict accordance with the ethical principles for research involving human participants as outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All data were handled securely, with no identifying information linked to research records. Ethical approvals were obtained from the Mona Campus Research Ethics Committee and the South-East Regional Health Authority Ethics Committee, Jamaica.

The majority (81%) of participants was female. Nurses constituted the majority of participants (65.9%). Approximately 52% of participants were in the age category 26–35 years. Themes identified were capacity development, performance management and challenges with staffing for the delivery of quality care.

Capacity development was recognized as an important element within the institutions for staff members. Training departments existed in both institutions, but the focus of training is on nursing. Capacity development is facilitated through presentations at monthly meetings, simulation of procedures in a lab setting, and support from the institution to undertake formal training programmes. Nurses and doctors are required to do continuing education as a requirement for re-licensure by their professional regulatory bodies. The institutions do not have a clearly defined structured mechanism in place to support capacity development of staff.

Well for those persons who are required to be registered with their professional bodies, we try to make sure that those persons do their renewals. [Institution B]

There are formal meetings where clinical topics are presented in different forms. So whether it is audits, because we do audits, or whether it is just a didactic sort of presentation of a particular topic… so for example, now, we are actively engaged in sensitization for Ebola and sometimes we co-opt the services of other experts to come in and give us continuing medical education sessions. [Institution A]

It was highlighted that capacity development is not done on a wide scale, and issues with resources were identified as a challenge.

For most of the staff, we try and encourage them to go into the DM [Doctor of Medicine] residency programme so in that way, they can be developed – most certainly junior staff. But certainly, for the senior staff, there is not an official avenue for further training. [Institution A]

For the development of the other staff members, we do offer in-house training. You know, which could be a one day or two days. We don’t have wide scale training, but you know, persons are encouraged to go to other facilities that may offer something that they have an interest in. [Institution B]

So, first things first. We have never received support in our budget through government for training and development. There is limited budgetary support for training. What we do is, via funds that are allocated for/from other sources, so the Panadol money [money to buy a certain pharmaceuticals for example] - we shave off some of it and we put it in training. We do train our staff, we do not have a full training plan. [Institution B]

We are limited by our financial resources [interviewee chuckles at this point] as to the number of persons we can sponsor for courses. [Institution B]

With regard to performance management, appraisals are done annually, according to the report of most managers.

There is an annual review, which usually, their supervisor will do them and that’s with an old form that probably needs some work. But each year, it has to be reviewed and resubmitted at the end of the contract period, and quite a few people are on contract. [Institution A]

Ok, we do annual performance appraisal. Sometimes more often for the temporary staff. For them to continue with their contract, it is done more often. For permanent staff, it is an annual appraisal. [Institution B]

In one institution there was a problem with effecting performance of appraisals for members of one of the professional groups, however, the view was not consistent among managers.

You have a set here that have never done performance appraisal. Doctors! There is no such thing for the doctors. Free for all! It is not a part of the culture, and so we have to get an HR [human resource] person that has some backbone to make this thing happen. [Institution B]

Well we never had [a performance appraisal system] for the doctors initially but now we have. [Institution B]

Issues were also raised about the objectivity of the performance appraisal tool.

The instrument that is used is a very poor instrument, a very subjective instrument. I have articulated that to the HR and asked for training [regarding administering the instrument], but my understanding is that they are about to change the system because it’s from when [long ago], and so they would [then] train the staff. That is another story. [Institution B]

Challenges with staffing for the delivery of quality care focused in adequacy and loss of compassion. There were mixed views on the adequacy of staff. Discussion

Having an effective workforce is critical for the delivery of quality/safe health care. The staffing culture relating to patient safety in the two institutions studied was poor - positive percentage score of 35%. Average positive staffing score in the AHRQ’s 2018 study was 53% and in other studies, staffing scores were also low [40–44]. The majority of participants in this study believed that they did not have enough staff to handle the workload and no difference existed by staff position. By institution though, more staff in Institution A compared to Institution B indicated that they worked longer than is best for patient. Institution A, according to records from the Ministry of Health and Wellness, sees 100% more patients than Institution B and with only half the staff of Institution B [45]. Participants working in the accident and emergency, ICU/operating theatre, and on medicine wards had the lowest median staffing score. The mass migration of nurses, especially specialist nurses, as well as the heavy and intense workload associated with aforementioned units partially explain the low median scores.

Approximately 79% of participants indicated that they always practice responsibly with consideration of fatigue, other human factors and their effect on performance, however, almost 60% agreed that staff in the unit work longer hours than is best for patient care. Doctors in this study worked longer hours compared to nurses. Long working hours among healthcare providers are increasingly recognized as a significant risk factor for compromised quality of care. Numerous studies link extended shifts and chronic overwork to fatigue, decreased cognitive function, and poor decision-making, all of which directly impact patient outcomes. Prolonged work hours, especially shifts exceeding 12 h, have been associated with increased rates of medical errors, adverse events, and lower patient safety standards [46]. Fatigue induced by long hours leads to reduced attention, slower reaction times, and poor clinical judgment, which can result in delayed diagnosis, procedural errors, and patient harm [47]. Long hours not only affect individual performance but also influence team dynamics, handoffs, and communication breakdowns, further increasing the risk of poor-quality care. To mitigate these risks, it is critical for institutions to consider work-hour limitations and shift redesign strategies. Evidence suggests that reducing shift length and allowing for rest periods can improve provider well-being, reduce error rates, and maintain quality standards [48]. Across both professional groups, there is a general shortage of staff. Jamaica has been undergoing a spate of health care worker migration, more commonly seen among nurses.

An effective workforce for the delivery of quality health care relies on ongoing training for staff development. In this study, almost 96% of participants indicated that they develop and maintain skills and competencies appropriate to profession. Yet, in the qualitative component of the study, senior managers highlighted major deficiencies regarding having structured capacity development plans for staff, which was in part due to the unavailability of funding. This may be pointing to the fact that nurses and doctors have to invest heavily in developing their own professional development to support the delivery of quality care. In the Jamaican context, doctors are required to have 30 continuing medical education hours (2 ethics hours included) for the annual professional re-licensure. Nurses are required to renew their license every two years, requiring a total of 30 h (5 non-nursing hours) for a single trained registered nurse, and 40 h (5 non-nursing hours and 10 midwifery hours) for a registered nurse/midwife. The acquisition of continuing education hours may not be adequate to satisfy training required for quality improvement in hospitals, as these hours are typically focused on what is of personal interest to the health professional. Staff training for quality improvement must be embedded as a priority in the strategic plan of hospitals’ quality improvement efforts. Training enhances clinical competency, patient safety, staff engagement, and organizational performance. Training to support quality improvement must be continuous and be based on needs [49].

In this study, less than 70% of participants had performance appraisals done annually. In the qualitative component, managers highlighted that the instrument is outdated, and not very objective. Managers also reported that performance appraisal for doctors is not done routinely. In the context of quality and quality improvement, performance appraisal serves as a structured approach for evaluating employee performance, guiding professional development, and enhancing service delivery [50]. Authors have highlighted challenges with physician appraisals, including theoretical and empirical underestimation of the importance and weight of appraisals, and the lack of reliability of instruments [51]. In a systematic review, Homauni et al. highlighted that the purpose(s) of performance appraisal, the indicators of performance appraisal, the method(s) of performance appraisal and uses of performance appraisal results are common concerns relating to performance appraisals in the healthcare arena [50]. The shortcomings identified in this study suggest the need for modernization of appraisal systems and enhanced human resources oversight.

Staffing issues warrant attention as it is well established that the appropriate number of quality staff with the appropriate skills mix is associated with positive outcomes [18–20, 24]. The World Health Organization in its Global Strategy on Human Resources for Health: Workforce 2030, advocates for optimization of the health workforce through evidence-informed policies, alignment of investment in human resources for health giving consideration to the current and future needs of the population and of health systems, building capacity for effective public policy stewardship, leadership and governance of actions on human resources for health, and strengthening data on human resources for health for monitoring and accountability purposes [52]. Jamaica lacks an overarching Human Resources for Health Strategy. There is need for a clear strategy to address human resources for health, which is a key element for the delivery of quality health care and for achieving global developmental goals. Managers in the study consistently reported on staff shortages, outdated staff cadre/availability of permanent posts, limited funding, and their inability to make decisions regarding staffing based on system constraints – major human resources decisions being left to the government. The health sector in Jamaica would benefit from the development and implementation of a comprehensive human resources for health strategy to address existing gaps, retain skilled staff, and support the achievement of national and global health goals related to the delivery of quality care.

This study has several strengths. It explored the staffing culture and the systems structures in place for staff to support the delivery of quality care. The sample size was large and a validated tool to assess staffing for patient safety culture was used in the study, enabling comparisons with international findings. However, the study focused solely on doctors and nurses, excluding the views of other key healthcare personnel such as patient care assistants, radiographers, and physiotherapists, who also play significant roles in the delivery of patient care. The inclusion of a qualitative component enriched the study by providing a deeper understanding of systems and structures in place to ensure an effective workforce for care delivery. The research was confined to two Type A hospitals within a specific geographic and administrative region. While the findings may reflect conditions in other developing countries, they should not be generalized beyond the context of the study.

Conclusion

This study found that staffing culture for patient safety was poor. Institutional challenges existed with performance appraisal, adequacy of staff and a structured mechanism for capacity development. There is need for strengthening institutional support for workforce development, modernizing performance management systems, and establishing a national human resources for health strategy. Investment in the health workforce is not only a strategic priority for meeting national service delivery demands, but is essential for achieving national and global developmental goals in the pursuit of Universal Health Coverage.
