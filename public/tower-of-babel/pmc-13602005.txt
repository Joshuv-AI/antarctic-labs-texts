In-Hospital Cardiac Resuscitation During Widespread Resident Resignations in South Korea
Authors: Donghyeong Koo, Sulhee Kim, Hyun-Jun Park, Yoon Hae Ahn, Kyung-Eui Lee, Si Mong Yoon, Choon Geun Lee, Hong Yeul Lee, Joo Hae Kim, Sang-Min Lee, Jinwoo Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWas nationwide resignation of residents in South Korea associated with changes in the resuscitation practices for in-hospital cardiac arrest?FindingsIn this cohort study of 383 patients with 469 in-hospital cardiac arrest events at a tertiary teaching hospital in South Korea, the crisis period was associated with fewer cardiopulmonary resuscitation attempts exceeding 35 minutes and a higher hazard of termination without return of spontaneous circulation. Despite this, return of spontaneous circulation rates and overall survival outcomes were not significantly different.MeaningThese findings suggest prolonged workforce shortage may be associated with changes in resuscitation termination decisions, supporting the need for standardized termination of resuscitation decision-making during a health care crisis.

Introduction

Effective management of in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA), including high-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), depends on rapid recognition, coordinated multidisciplinary teams, rapid response systems, and adequate hospital resources.1,2,3 These systems may be particularly vulnerable during periods of severe health care strain. Previous health care crises, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, have demonstrated how sudden workforce and resource shortages can disrupt resuscitation efforts and clinical outcomes after IHCA.4,5

Most prior analyses of IHCA during health care crises have focused on overall outcomes, such as return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) or survival to discharge.6,7,8 Less attention has been given to whether the process of resuscitation itself changes under strained conditions.5,7,8 In particular, the decision to terminate resuscitation (TOR) is a complex clinical judgment. CPR duration and TOR may reflect not only the patient’s likelihood of recovery but also team capacity, staffing constraints, and changing thresholds for clinical futility under institutional pressures.8,9,10 While survival-based outcomes are fundamental, evaluating changes in the resuscitation process is critical to capture the qualitative associations of resuscitation care during a health care crisis.

In South Korea, policy changes in early 2024 triggered an 18-month health care workforce crisis,11,12 beginning with the resignation on February 19, 2024; by July 8, 2024, 12 600 medical residents (92%) maintained their resignations,13 with the resident workforce subsequently recovering by September 2025.14 This disruption was associated with reduced hospital utilization, constrained intensive care unit (ICU) capacity, and an increased clinical burden on the remaining staff.15,16,17 However, its associations with IHCA resuscitation practice remain unknown.

We therefore examined IHCA characteristics, resuscitation trajectories, TOR practices, and clinical outcomes before and during this prolonged health care workforce crisis at a tertiary referral teaching hospital. We hypothesized that the crisis period would be associated with differences in resuscitation practices, particularly during prolonged resuscitation, and examined whether these differences were accompanied by changes in ROSC or survival outcomes.

Methods

Study Design and Population

We conducted a retrospective cohort analysis of patients aged 18 years or older who underwent at least 1 episode of CPR at Seoul National University Hospital between March 1, 2023, and February 28, 2025. Events after a documented prearrest do-not-resuscitate order were excluded. IHCA events were identified through mandatory institutional CPR reports. The study period was divided into a precrisis period (March 2023 to February 2024) and a crisis period (March 2024 to February 2025) following the mass resignation of resident physicians. This study was approved by the institutional review board of Seoul National University Hospital (2409-032-1567), with the requirement for informed consent waived because of the retrospective study design. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.

Changes in Staffing and Resuscitation Team Composition

The workforce crisis substantially changed both the resuscitation team and the rapid response team (RRT). Before the crisis, resuscitations were managed exclusively by a CPR team of 4 ICU-based resident physicians. During the crisis period, however, the mass resignation–induced staffing shortage necessitated their replacement with 4 board-certified faculty physicians trained in advanced cardiac life support. The RRT continued to operate on weekdays from 7 am to 7 pm with trained nurses and intensivists, but without resident participation. General ward and ICU bed capacity was partially reduced, and board-certified faculty physicians assumed direct responsibility for both routine ward care and on-call clinical coverage in place of residents.

Data Collection, Variables, and Outcomes

Baseline demographics and comorbidities were collected, and the Charlson Comorbidity Index was subsequently calculated.18 Event-specific clinical characteristics were obtained, including the location of the IHCA, RRT activation, the highest National Early Warning Score 2 (NEWS2) within 24 hours preceding the IHCA,19 concurrent acute medical conditions, and the provision of organ support therapies. Resuscitation-related characteristics were documented in general accordance with the Utstein resuscitation registry template for IHCA.20 These data comprised the presumed cause of IHCA, witnessed status, electrocardiographic monitoring, initial documented rhythm, time to initiation of resuscitation and code blue activation, epinephrine use, defibrillation, and CPR duration. In cases where extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) was deployed, CPR duration was defined as the time until conventional resuscitation efforts, including chest compressions, were discontinued. To account for potential shifts in inpatient severity during the crisis, the departmental case-mix index (CMI) was collected. Values reported as less than 1.0 were treated as 1.0 for continuous analyses.

Clinical outcomes included ROSC and survival metrics, which were evaluated as favorable neurological status, 24-hour survival, 30-day survival, and survival to discharge. Patients discharged alive before 30 days were considered 30-day survivors. Favorable neurological outcome was defined as maintaining a cerebral performance category (CPC) of 1 to 2 or, for those with a baseline cerebral performance category of 3 to 4, no worsening of their score by 30 days or discharge.20 Prolonged CPR was primarily defined as CPR duration greater than 35 minutes, reflecting the time point in previous literature where the time-dependent probability of survival after IHCA drops below 1%.21 Additional cutoffs of greater than 20 and 30 minutes were examined descriptively.

Statistical Analysis

For patients with multiple IHCAs during a single admission, all events were included in time-to-event analyses with adjustment for within-patient correlation, whereas baseline characteristics were analyzed per patient. Categorical and continuous variables were compared using Fisher exact test, Student t test, or the Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. IHCA incidence rates (per 1000 admissions) were compared using a 2-sample Poisson rate test.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate factors associated with prolonged CPR. The model adjusted for age, sex, hospital period (precrisis vs crisis), overall inpatient severity measured by CMI, Charlson Comorbidity Index, NEWS2, initial documented rhythm, location of IHCA, time from admission to IHCA, prior out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA), witnessed status, and monitored status.

Successful ROSC and TOR without ROSC were analyzed as competing events over CPR duration to characterize the resuscitation process. Unadjusted cumulative incidence functions were compared using the Gray test. After evaluating the proportional hazards assumption (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1), cause-specific Cox models were fitted to estimate the association of the crisis period with each event, adjusting for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, CMI, initial rhythm, IHCA location, NEWS2, time from admission to IHCA, and prior OHCA. To account for within-patient correlation from multiple IHCA events in the same patient, cluster-robust standard errors were calculated at the patient level in both the multivariable logistic regression and cause-specific Cox regression models. To complement the relative hazard estimates from cause-specific Cox models, the parametric G formula (regression standardization) was used to estimate absolute risk metrics, including standardized cumulative incidence and absolute risk differences for ROSC and TOR, with 95% CIs based on 500 patient-level bootstrap samples.

Subgroup analyses were conducted according to the ROSC status, prolonged CPR, and comorbidity burden assessed by the Charlson Comorbidity Index. The optimal cutoff value for the Charlson index was determined using Youden index from receiver operating characteristic analysis for survival to discharge (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1). We considered a 2-tailed P < .05 as statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R version 4.5.0 (R Project for Statistical Computing).

Results

A total of 383 patients who experienced 469 IHCA events were included, comprising 289 IHCA events from 232 patients in the precrisis period and 180 events from 151 patients during the crisis. The median (IQR) age of the patients was 69 (61-79) years, and 238 patients (62.1%) were male (Table 1). Baseline patient characteristics were generally similar between periods. However, the Charlson Comorbidity Index was lower during the crisis compared with the precrisis period (median [IQR], 5 [4-9] vs 7 [4-9]; P = .03), whereas other comorbidities and prior OHCA were comparable. In contrast, the CMI, reflecting overall inpatient severity, did not differ between periods (median [IQR], 1.15 [1.00-1.61] vs 1.17 [1.00-1.64]; P = .17). There was no significant difference in the overall IHCA incidence between the precrisis and crisis periods (2.30 vs 2.50 per 1000 admissions, P = .36) (Figure 1A).

Clinical and Resuscitation Characteristics of IHCA

Time from admission to IHCA was shorter during the crisis than precrisis period (median [IQR], 2 [0-8] vs 4 [1-16] days) (Table 2). Although the prevalence of specific conditions such as sepsis (62 of 180 [34.4%] vs 128 of 289 [44.3%]) and pneumonia (26 of 180 [14.4%] vs 85 of 289 [29.4%]) differed, major organ insufficiencies—including respiratory, kidney, and hemodynamic (hypotension)—were comparable between the crisis and precrisis periods. Prearrest severity also showed no significant difference, as reflected by NEWS2 scores, RRT activation rates, and organ support therapies. Measured early response metrics, witnessed and monitored status, initial rhythm, presumed IHCA cause, and use of ECPR or defibrillation did not differ statistically between periods (Table 3).

Distribution of CPR Duration and Characteristics of Prolonged Events

The median duration of CPR was 8 minutes in both periods. Nevertheless, the upper tail of the CPR duration distribution shifted leftward during the crisis period, with fewer resuscitations exceeding 35 minutes (12 of 180 [6.7%] vs 38 of 289 [13.1%]; P = .03) (Table 3 and Figure 2), a finding that persisted after excluding ECPR cases. In the adjusted multivariable logistic regression model, the crisis period was significantly associated with lower odds of CPR lasting longer than 35 minutes (adjusted odds ratio, 0.48; 95% CI, 0.24-0.96; P = .04).

Despite differences in prolonged resuscitation efforts, overall clinical outcomes did not differ statistically (Table 3). ROSC was achieved in 130 of 180 events (72.2%) during the crisis period and 221 of 289 events (76.5%) before the crisis (P = .33). Neurological outcomes and survival at 24 hours, 30 days, and discharge also showed no significant differences (eg, survival to discharge: 50 [27.9%] vs 82 [28.5%]; P = .92).

Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested that the lower frequency of CPR lasting longer than 35 minutes was concentrated among events without ROSC (eTables 1 and 2 in Supplement 1). In cases where ROSC was not achieved, baseline comorbidity burden remained consistent across both time periods (median Charlson Comorbidity Index of 7 in both groups; P = .75). However, prolonged resuscitations—specifically those exceeding 35 minutes—were less frequent during the crisis period (8 of 50 [16.0%] vs 25 of 68 [36.8%]; P = .01), and a similar pattern was observed among non-ECPR cases (7 of 47 [14.9%] vs 24 of 67 [35.8%]; P = .02). This trend persisted in the subgroup with high comorbidity burden, defined as a Charlson Comorbidity Index of 5 or higher; among IHCA events without ROSC, events with CPR duration exceeding 35 minutes were less frequent during the crisis period (6 of 38 [15.8%] vs 21 of 55 [38.2%]; P = .02), whereas no significant difference was observed among events with ROSC (1 of 69 [1.4%] vs 8 of 158 [5.1%]; P = .28) (eTable 3 in Supplement 1).

Among events with CPR duration greater than 35 minutes, clinical outcomes were poor in both periods (eTable 4 in Supplement 1). ROSC was achieved in 4 of 12 prolonged events (33.3%) during the crisis period compared with 13 of 38 (34.2%) before the crisis (P > .99), and survival to discharge occurred in 1 event in each period. There were no survivors to discharge among patients who underwent prolonged CPR without ECPR.

Competing-Risk Analysis of ROSC and TOR

To account for the competing risks of achieving ROSC vs the termination of resuscitation efforts, cause-specific Cox models were employed (eTable 5 in Supplement 1). The crisis period was not associated with the hazard of ROSC (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 0.93; 95% CI, 0.72-1.19). In contrast, the crisis period was associated with a higher cause-specific hazard of TOR without ROSC (aHR, 1.66; 95% CI, 1.14-2.42).

Standardized cumulative incidence analyses using the G formula showed a similar pattern (eFigure 3D-3F in Supplement 1). The standardized cumulative incidence of ROSC was similar between the precrisis and crisis periods across CPR duration time points, whereas the incidence of TOR without ROSC was higher in the crisis period. The standardized absolute risk difference for TOR without ROSC was 0.027 (95% CI, 0.001-0.101) at 20 minutes and 0.049 (95% CI, 0.002-0.149) at 35 minutes (eFigure 4 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

In this study, we identified changes in IHCA characteristics and resuscitation practices before and during a nationwide resident resignation in South Korea. The crisis period was associated with fewer prolonged resuscitations and a higher cause-specific hazard of TOR without ROSC, while no statistically significant differences were detected in ROSC, neurological outcomes, or survival. These findings suggest that the workforce crisis and related resuscitation-system adaptations may have altered decision-making regarding the cessation of extended resuscitation efforts.

Although longer CPR duration is consistently associated with lower IHCA survival,21 the optimal timing of TOR and the definition of prolonged CPR remain uncertain.22 In line with current American Heart Association guidelines,23 multiple studies have used a 20-minute cutoff,24,25 whereas others have proposed 30-minute22,26 or 35-minute27,28 thresholds. Based on data from the Get With The Guidelines–Resuscitation registry showing that favorable neurologic outcomes and survival after IHCA decline below 1% after 32 and 39 minutes, respectively,21 we adopted a 35-minute threshold, beyond which outcomes in this study were uniformly unfavorable. A recently proposed IHCA TOR rule incorporates variables such as unwitnessed arrest, absence of monitoring, asystole, and resuscitation duration of at least 10 minutes.29 However, direct application of this rule to our cohort was limited because only 14 cases across both periods met the combined criteria of unwitnessed and unmonitored asystole, and 11 of these received at least 10 minutes of CPR, precluding meaningful interperiod comparisons. Two of these 11 patients achieved ROSC, but none had a favorable neurologic outcome or survived to discharge, supporting the poor prognosis associated with these indicators.

Previous studies from the COVID-19 pandemic showed that health care strain may alter the incidence, characteristics, and outcomes of IHCA through resource limitations and hospitalized population shifts.4,8,30,31 In contrast, the recent South Korean health care crisis was characterized primarily by an abrupt shortage of resident physicians, with no major disruptions to other medical resources and minimal impact on other health care personnel. Because resident physicians play a central role in inpatient care in South Korea, their sudden shortage may have contributed to changes in the composition and characteristics of the hospitalized patient population during the crisis.15,16,32 Despite these contextual differences, IHCA resuscitation practices should remain consistent, as resuscitation is a core, life-critical hospital function.3 This aligns with COVID-19 resuscitation guidance emphasizing the preservation of high-quality resuscitation whenever resources permit.33

TOR decisions may be influenced by a wide range of elements, ranging from patient-level characteristics, workforce-related factors, and material resources, to broader contexts including ethical considerations, family communication, and operational pressures or institutional expectations.10,22,34,35,36,37 In our setting, the resident physician resignation primarily affected workforce-related factors, including physician staffing, CPR team composition, overall workload, and supervision structure.17 While characteristics of patients with IHCA differed during the crisis, the crisis period remained independently associated with fewer prolonged CPR attempts and higher TOR without ROSC after adjustment, suggesting that these differences alone do not fully explain the observed change in TOR practice. Nonpersonnel resources, such as resuscitation equipment, remained relatively unchanged, as reflected by the comparable use of defibrillation, epinephrine and ECPR between periods. Nonetheless, ICU operational restructuring may have indirectly affected the decision-making environment.

Trainee physicians, including residents, play important roles in resuscitation teams.38,39,40 Following their resignation, many South Korean hospitals redistributed residents’ clinical responsibilities to attending physicians,16,17,32 potentially resulting in a transition from resident-led to attending-led cardiac arrest teams. However, how these changes may influence TOR decision-making remains poorly understood. Several studies suggest that clinicians’ experience and seniority may influence TOR decisions, with junior physicians tending to continue CPR longer.34,35,36 Given that TOR decisions are high-stakes and effectively irreversible, their potential variation according to team composition, leadership structure, or institutional context carries important clinical implications.10,34,39 Ultimately, structured TOR decision-support frameworks29 and postevent review3 may help reduce unwarranted variation and maintain consistency in decision-making during health care crises.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, we did not exclude patients admitted following OHCA and allowed multiple IHCA events per patient. However, prior OHCA was relatively uncommon (8.1%) and similarly distributed across periods (Table 1), and within-patient correlation was accounted for in the cause-specific Cox and standardized cumulative incidence analyses. Second, because this study had a modest sample size, the absence of statistically significant differences in ROSC, survival, or neurological outcomes should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalent outcomes. Some subgroup analyses, particularly those involving patients with a high comorbidity burden or those who had a shockable initial rhythm, were based on small sample sizes and therefore should be interpreted with caution. Third, because this was a single-center before-and-after observational study, residual confounding and temporal confounding, including the possibility of increasing team experience over time, cannot be excluded. Although monthly IHCA incidence (Figure 1A) and the proportion of prolonged CPR (Figure 1B) did not show a clear seasonal or monotonic temporal pattern, these descriptive findings cannot fully exclude unmeasured temporal associations. Fourth, we could not directly measure detailed indicators of CPR quality, clinician judgment and postresuscitation care, so our interpretation of changes in TOR practice remains inferential. Additionally, because this was a single-center study conducted in a tertiary teaching hospital during a unique nationwide workforce crisis, generalizability may be limited. Multicenter studies are needed to clarify how workforce shortages may be associated with resuscitation practices across diverse hospital settings.

Conclusions

In this single-center before-and-after cohort study of patients with IHCA, the crisis period was associated with fewer CPR attempts exceeding 35 minutes and a higher cause-specific hazard of TOR without ROSC, while no statistically significant differences were detected in ROSC or major survival outcomes. These findings suggest that sustained workforce disruption may alter TOR practices and support the need for optimized and standardized TOR decision-making during health care crises.
