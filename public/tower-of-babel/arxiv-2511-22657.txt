Abstract
. We investigate the minimal free resolutions of closed neighborhood ideals of graphs within the framework of Barile-Macchia (BM) resolutions. 1.
Introduction

The study of minimal free resolutions of monomial ideals is an active area of research that provides deep connections between combinatorics and commutative algebra. Classical constructions such as the Taylor resolution
[
23
]
and Lyubeznik resolutions
[
17
]
always yield free resolutions, although they are typically far from minimal. In contrast, the Scarf complex
[
21
]
rarely supports a resolution, but whenever it does, the resulting resolution is always minimal. Cellular resolutions provide a unifying framework: a monomial ideal
I\subset R=K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is supported on a labeled CW-complex, and consequently, graded Betti numbers, projective dimension, and regularity of the ideal can be extracted from the intrinsic combinatorial structure of the complex. Discrete Morse Theory often offers effective tools for simplifying such complexes; in particular, suitably chosen acyclic matchings on the Taylor complex often produce significantly smaller, and in favorable cases minimal, cellular resolutions
[
4
,
11
]
.

A practical and flexible approach to constructing minimal cellular resolutions originates in the work of Barile-Macchia
[
3
]
, who introduced a Discrete Morse Theory-based method specifically for edge ideals of forests. Their construction imposes some order on the minimal generators and builds a homogeneous acyclic matching on the Taylor complex compatible with that order; the corresponding critical cells support a cellular resolution, and minimality of the resolution is guaranteed when certain subsets of the generating set satisfy a prescribed “bridge” condition. Later, Trung-Kara
[
9
]
generalized this principle to arbitrary monomial ideals by formulating a
bridge-friendly
condition-again expressed as a combinatorial constraint on subsets of the generating set-which characterizes when such Taylor-based matchings yield minimal cellular resolutions in full generality. They referred to the resulting resolution as the
Barile-Macchia resolution
(in short, BM resolution). These methods have since been applied successfully to several classes of graph ideals, including edge ideals of forests
[
3
]
, path ideals
[
7
]
, and certain powers of edge ideals
[
6
]
.

This article focuses on the BM resolution of the
closed neighborhood ideal

NI(G)
of a finite simple graph
G
, with particular attention to the case of trees. The closed neighborhood ideal, introduced by Sharifan and Moradi
[
22
]
, is a square-free monomial ideal whose minimal generators correspond to the closed neighborhoods of the vertices of the graph (see
Section

2.3
). Although the closed neighborhood ideal is relatively recent, its Stanley-Reisner complex, the dominance complex, and their Alexander dual, the neighborhood complex, have been well studied in the literature
[
16
,
18
,
10
]
, highlighting the combinatorial significance of these ideals. Considerable work has explored the relationships between algebraic and homological properties of closed neighborhood ideals and the combinatorial structure of the underlying graph (see, e.g.,
[
15
,
19
,
5
,
14
]
and the references therein). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that focuses on the minimal free resolution of these ideals, particularly in the framework of the BM resolution.

Among the various classes of simple graphs, trees are of particular interest because of their connections to multiple areas of mathematics and computer science, and because several algebraic invariants of their closed neighborhood ideals can be described in terms of combinatorial invariants of the graph. Our first main result in this setting is the following.

Theorem
3.4
.

Let
T
be a tree. Then the ideal
NI(T)
is bridge-friendly. Consequently, the BM resolution of
NI(T)
is minimal.

To establish this result, we consider
T
as a rooted tree and define a lexicographic order on the minimal generators of its closed neighborhood ideal, induced by a chosen ordering of the vertices of
T
. We also explore the relationship between closed neighborhood ideals of trees and three well-studied classes of monomial ideals for which the BM resolution is known to be minimal (
Proposition

3.6
,
Proposition

3.7
, and
Proposition

3.8
).

Two fundamental homological invariants associated with the minimal free resolution of an ideal are the Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity (or simply, regularity) and the projective dimension. The regularity measures the width of the resolution, while the projective dimension measures its length. For closed neighborhood ideals of trees, the regularity is known to equal the matching number of the graph
[
5
, Theorem 3.4]
. By analyzing the critical sets arising from the corresponding acyclic matchings, we are able to establish the following result regarding the projective dimension.

Theorem
3.14
.

Let
T
be a tree with the closed neighborhood ideal
NI(T)
in a polynomial ring
R
. Then the projective dimension

\operatorname{pdim}(R/NI(T))=\alpha(T),

where
\alpha(T)
denotes the independence number of
T
.

In fact,
Algorithm

1
constructs a maximal BM critical set whose size equals the independence number of the tree.

In
Section

4
, we focus on the closed neighborhood ideals of the path graphs
P_{n}
. By applying the Betti splitting criteria for these ideals, we provide an explicit numerical formula for all graded Betti numbers of
NI(P_{n})
in
Corollary

4.7
. Moving on, in
Section

5
, we establish basic results for verifying the bridge, gap, and true gap conditions for certain classes of monomial ideals. Using these results and a correspondence among the critical cells of specific subgraphs of a special class of trees,
Theorem

5.4
expresses the graded Betti numbers of their closed neighborhood ideals in terms of the graded Betti numbers of the corresponding subgraphs. Finally, we conclude
Section

5
with related questions.

2.2.
Minimal graded free resolution

Let

be the polynomial ring in
n

equipped with the usual
\mathbb{N}

\deg(m)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}

\mathcal{F}_{\cdot}:\,\,0\rightarrow F_{l}\xrightarrow{\partial_{l}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\partial_{2}}F_{1}\xrightarrow{\partial_{1}}F_{0}\xrightarrow{\pi}R/I\rightarrow 0,

of

.

Let
\mathcal{F}.
be a minimal graded free resolution of

. Then for each

F_{r}=\bigoplus_{j\in\mathbb{N}}R(-d)^{\beta_{r,d}(R/I)}
. The quantity

in degree

\mathrm{reg}(R/I)=max\{d-r:\beta_{r,d}(R/I)\neq 0\text{ for some }r,d\},

\mathrm{pd}(R/I)=max\{r:\beta_{r,d}(R/I)\neq 0\text{ for some }r,d\}.

2.3.
Closed neighborhood ideal

Let
G
be a graph with the vertex set
V(G)=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
. For each vertex

of

NI(G)=\langle\{m_{(G,w)}:w\in V(G)\}\rangle.

In this article, our primary aim is to describe an explicit minimal graded free resolution of the closed neighborhood ideal of trees and describe its regularity and projective dimension in terms of the combinatorial data associated with the underlying graph. In fact, we show that the recently defined BM resolution is minimal for the closed neighborhood ideal of trees.

3.1.
Comparison with known families of ideals:

In the literature, several classes of monomial ideals are known to admit a minimal BM resolution. Among them, the edge ideals of rooted hypertrees, generic monomial ideals, and monomial ideals with linear quotients form broad families that satisfy this minimality property. Our next goal is to show that the closed neighborhood ideals of trees do not belong to any of these classes.

Next, we turn to the notion of generic monomial ideals. For a monomial

3.2.
Projective dimension from minimal BM resolution:

The fact that the closed neighborhood ideal of a tree admits a minimal BM resolution has several important combinatorial implications. In particular, one can analyze the critical cells in the corresponding acyclic matching to obtain information about the Betti numbers of the ideal. In this subsection, we present such an application. More precisely, we analyze the critical cells to show that the projective dimension of the ideal can be expressed in terms of the independence number of the underlying tree. We begin with some notations that will be useful in the sequel.

Let

For paths

A^{\prime}=\{x\in A:\deg_{T}(y)\neq 1\text{ for all }y\in N_{T}(x)\}\cup\{y\in N_{T}(x):x\in A;\ \deg_{T}(y)=1;\ y<y^{\prime}\text{ for all leaf siblings }y^{\prime}\text{ of }y\}.
Observe that,
|A|=|A^{\prime}|.
Now, define the function
\psi:A^{\prime}\to V_{\sigma}
by,

\psi(v)=\begin{cases}v,&\text{if }v\in V_{\sigma},\\
\min_{<}\{u\in V_{\sigma}:u\text{ is a child of }v\},&\text{if }v\notin V_{\sigma}.\end{cases}

Note that if
v\notin V_{\sigma}
, then by Algorithm
1
, the vertex
v
has at least one child in
V_{\sigma}
. Hence, the function
\psi
is well-defined. To prove that
\psi
is injective, we divide the proof into the following four cases.

Case I:

v_{1},v_{2}\in V_{\sigma}
.

\psi(v_{1})=\psi(v_{2})
implies
v_{1}=v_{2}.

Case II:

v_{1},v_{2}\notin V_{\sigma}
.

If
\psi(v_{1})=\psi(v_{2})
, then there exists a child
u
of
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
. This is a contradiction to the fact that a tree is acyclic.

Case III:

v_{1}\in V_{\sigma}\text{ and }v_{2}\notin V_{\sigma}
.

If
\psi(v_{1})=\psi(v_{2})
, then
v_{1}=\psi(v_{2})
, consequently
v_{1}
is the child of
v_{2}
, contradicting the assumption that
A^{\prime}
is an independent set.

Case IV:

v_{1}\notin V_{\sigma}\text{ and }v_{2}\in V_{\sigma}
.

\psi(v_{1})=\psi(v_{2})
implies
\psi(v_{1})=v_{2}
. This says that
v_{2}
is a child of
v_{1}
, contradicting that
A^{\prime}
is an independent set.

Therefore,
\psi
is an injective function. It follows that,
|A|=|A^{\prime}|\leq|V_{\sigma}|.
As
A
is an arbitrary independent set,
\alpha(T)\leq|V_{\sigma}|.
Moreover, since
V_{\sigma}
itself is an independent set,
\alpha(T)=|V_{\sigma}|.

∎

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
T
be a tree graph. Then,
\mathrm{pdim}(R/NI(T))=|V_{\sigma}|.

Proof.

NI(T)
is bridge friendly by the proof of
Theorem

3.4
. By
Proposition

2.3
and
Lemma

3.11
(ii) and (iv), it follows that
\sigma
is a critical set of
NI(T)
. By
Proposition

2.4

|V_{\sigma}|\leq\mathrm{pdim}(R/NI(T)).
To prove that this is an equality, first recall from
Lemma

3.11
(v) that

\operatorname{lcm}\left(\{m:m\in\sigma\}\right)=\prod_{v\in V(T)}v.

If
\mathrm{pdim}(R/NI(T))>|V_{\sigma}|
, then there exists at least one critical set
\tau
such that
|\tau|=|V_{\sigma}|+1
and
\sigma\subsetneq\tau.
Note that
\operatorname{lcm}(\{m:m\in\tau\})=\prod_{v\in V(T)}v.
Since the BM resolution is a subcomplex of the Taylor resolution of
R/NI(T)
, the differential maps of the BM resolution are the restriction of the Taylor resolution. And the differential maps of the Taylor resolution are the
\pm 1
multiple of the quotient of the lcm’s. Since the lcm of
\sigma
and
\tau
is the same and both of them are critical, it forces the entry of
\pm 1
in the differential matrix of
\partial_{|V_{\sigma}|+1}
. Consequently, the BM resolution will not be minimal, a contradiction. Thus, we have
\mathrm{pdim}(R/NI(T))=|V_{\sigma}|
, as required.
∎

The main theorem of this subsection below follows directly from
Proposition

3.12
and

3.13
.

Theorem 3.14
.

For any tree
T
,
\mathrm{pdim}(R/NI(T))=\alpha(T)
, where
\alpha(T)
is the independence number of
T
.

4.
Betti numbers for path graphs

In this section, we derive an explicit formula for the Betti numbers of the closed neighborhood ideal of path graphs. By
Theorem

3.4
and
Proposition

2.4
, computing the Betti numbers
\beta_{r,d}
reduces to counting the critical cells
\sigma
of size
r
where the degree of the
\mathrm{lcm}
of the elements in
\sigma
is
d
. A convenient approach to determining the number of such critical cells is to relate them to the critical cells of paths with fewer vertices. However, as we discuss in
Section

5
, establishing such recursive relationships becomes significantly more challenging for trees in which the grandparents of leaf vertices do not have any leaf neighbors. For path graphs, we overcome this difficulty by using the fact that their closed neighborhood ideals admit a Betti splitting. The concept of Betti splitting, introduced by Francisco-Hà-Van Tuyl in
[
12
]
, is defined as follows.

Definition 4.1
.

[
12
, Definition 1.1]

Let
I,J
and
K
be monomial ideals such that the minimal generating set of
I
can be expressed as
\mathcal{G}(I)=\mathcal{G}(J)\sqcup\mathcal{G}(K).
Then, the decomposition
I=J+K
is called a
Betti splitting
if, for all integers
r\geq 0,\ d\geq 0
, the following equality holds:

(1)

\beta_{r,d}(I)=\beta_{r,d}(J)+\beta_{r,d}(K)+\beta_{r-1,d}(J\cap K).

The following theorem provides a useful way to find a Betti splitting of an ideal.

Theorem 4.2
.

[
12
, Corollary 2.7]

Let
I\subseteq R=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}]
be a monomial ideal in
R
. Fix a variable
x_{i}
in
R
, and define two monomial ideals
J
and
K
as follows:

\mathcal{G}(J)=\{m\in\mathcal{G}(I):x_{i}\mid m\}\text{ and }\mathcal{G}(K)=\{m\in\mathcal{G}(I):x_{i}\nmid m\}.

Then,
I=J+K
is a Betti splitting if
J
has a linear resolution.

A closely related ideal to the closed neighborhood ideal of path graphs is the
3
-path ideal of path graphs. Recall that if
P_{n}
is the path graph on
n
vertices
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
with edge set
E(P_{n})=\{\{x_{i},x_{i+1}\}:i\in[n-1]\}
, then the set of minimal generators of the
3
-path ideal
J_{3}(P_{n})
is
\{x_{i}x_{i+1}x_{i+2}:i\in[n-2]\}
. A formula for the Betti numbers of
J_{3}(P_{n})
can be deduced from
[
1
, Theorem 4.14]
. For our purposes, however, we employ the following simplified formula.

Proposition 4.3
.

[
2
, Corollary 3.8]

For each
n\geq 3
,
l\geq 0
and
r\geq 0
, the Betti numbers of
J_{3}(P_{n})
are

\beta_{r,d}(J_{3}(P_{n}))=\begin{cases}\binom{n-3l-3}{r-l}\binom{n-2l-r-2}{2l-r+1},&\text{ if }d=r+2l+3,\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

To compute the Betti numbers of
NI(P_{n})
, we first compute the Betti numbers of the ideal
I_{n}
, defined as follows.

Definition 4.4
.

Define the monomial ideal
I_{n}
in the ring
R=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}]
as

\mathcal{G}(I_{n})=\mathcal{G}(NI(P_{r+1}))\setminus\{x_{n-1}x_{n}\}.

We show that the ideals
I_{n}
admit a Betti splitting, and using this fact, we obtain an explicit formula for their Betti numbers. Throughout, we adopt the convention that
\binom{0}{0}=1
and
\binom{n}{-k}=0
for
n,k>0
.

Proposition 4.5
.

For each
n\geq 1
, the Betti numbers can be written as follows:

(2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta_{r,d}(I_{n})=\begin{cases}\binom{n-2l-r-3}{2l-r+1}\binom{n-3l-3}{r-l},&\text{ if }d=r+2l+3,\\
\binom{n-2l-r-4}{2l-r+2}\binom{n-3l-5}{r-l-1},&\text{ if }d=r+2l+4,\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Here
l\geq-1
is an integer.

Proof.

For
l=-1
, we only need to consider the case
d=r+2
,

\beta_{r,d}(I_{n})=\begin{cases}1,\quad&\text{if }(r,d)=(0,2),\\
0,\quad&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

So, from now onwards, we may consider
l\geq 0
. Also, for
r=0
, we only need to consider the case
d=2l+3
for
n\geq 3
,

\beta_{r,d}(I_{n})=\begin{cases}n-3,\quad&\text{if }(r,d)=(0,3),\\
0,\quad&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Thus, from now onwards, we may consider the cases when
l\geq 0,\ r>0
. Let us now write the ideal
I_{n}
as

I_{n}=J_{n}+K_{n},

where

J_{n}=\begin{cases}\langle 0\rangle,\quad&n=1,\\
\langle\{x_{1}x_{2}\}\rangle,\quad&n>1;\end{cases}\text{ and }K_{n}=\begin{cases}\langle 0\rangle,\quad&n\leq 3,\\
\langle\{x_{2}x_{3}x_{4},\dots,x_{n-2}x_{n-1}x_{n}\}\rangle,\quad&n\geq 4.\end{cases}

Observe that, for
n\geq 4
,
K_{n}
is the
3
-path ideal of the path graph on the vertex set
\{x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\}
. Thus
K_{n}=J_{3}(P_{n-1})
for
n\geq 4
. Since the ideal
J_{n}
has a linear resolution, by Theorem
4.2
we see that
I_{n}=J_{n}+K_{n}
is a Betti splitting, and hence, by Definition
4.1
,

\beta_{r,d}(I_{n})=\beta_{r,d}(J_{n})+\beta_{r,d}(K_{n})+\beta_{r-1,d}(J_{n}\cap K_{n}).

Note that,

\displaystyle J_{n}\cap K_{n}=\begin{cases}\langle 0\rangle,\quad&n<3,\\
\langle\{x_{1}x_{2}\}\rangle.I_{n-2},&n\geq 3.\end{cases}

Thus,

\beta_{r-1,d}(J_{n}\cap K_{n})=\begin{cases}0,\quad&n<3,\\
\beta_{r-1,d-2}(I_{n-2}),&n\geq 3.\end{cases}

Moreover,

(3)

\displaystyle\beta_{r,d}(\langle\{x_{1}x_{2}\}\rangle)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{ if }(r,d)=(0,2),\\
0,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Now, we prove by induction on
n
.
A direct computation using
Eq.

2
shows that
\beta_{r,d}(I_{1})=0
for all possible values of

.
Assume that

If

, then

. Therefore, using the induction hypothesis and

\displaystyle\beta_{r,d}(I_{n})

\displaystyle=\binom{n-3l-4}{r-l}\binom{n-2l-r-3}{2l-r+1}+\binom{n-3l-4}{r-l-1}\binom{n-2l-r-3}{2l-r+1}

\displaystyle=\binom{n-2l-r-3}{2l-r+1}\binom{n-3l-3}{r-l},

where the second equality follows from the binomial identity
\binom{a}{b-1}+\binom{a}{b}=\binom{a+1}{b}
. Finally, we take

∎

In the next theorem, we express the Betti numbers of

in terms of the Betti numbers of

obtained in

above.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let

be the path graph with

Proof.

\displaystyle\beta_{r-1,d-2}(NI(P_{n-2}))=

5.
Conclusion

In the final part of this paper, we present some concluding remarks on the Betti numbers of closed neighborhood ideals of trees and examine the bridge-friendly condition for the closed neighborhood ideals of chordal and bipartite graphs.

5.1.
Betti numbers for a class of trees

As shown in
Theorem

3.4
, the closed neighborhood ideals of trees admit minimal BM resolutions and thus, in principle, by
Proposition

2.4
, one may compute their Betti numbers by counting the corresponding critical cells. In practice, however, this counting becomes tedious unless one can identify a correspondence between the critical cells of the original tree and those of certain subtrees. In
Theorem

5.4
, we exploit such correspondences to express the Betti numbers of the closed neighborhood ideal of a class of trees in terms of the Betti numbers of appropriate subtrees.

We begin with some basic results concerning how to verify the bridge, gap, and true gap conditions for certain special classes of monomial ideals. We remark that these results may be useful in future investigations of BM resolutions of arbitrary monomial ideals and their associated critical cells.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
I
be a monomial ideal in
R=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}]
and
A\subseteq\mathcal{G}(I)
such that;

(1)

\mathrm{Supp}(\operatorname{lcm}(\mathcal{G}(I)))=\mathrm{Supp}(\operatorname{lcm}(A))\sqcup\mathrm{Supp}(\operatorname{lcm}(\mathcal{G}(I^{\prime})))
,

(2)

For all
m\in A
, there exists
x_{i}\mid m
and
x_{i}\nmid m^{\prime}
for all
m^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}(I)\setminus\{m\}
,

where
\mathcal{G}(I^{\prime})=\mathcal{G}(I)\setminus A
. Then
\sigma\subseteq\mathcal{G}(I)
has a bridge (resp. a gap) if and only if
\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma\cap\mathcal{G}(I^{\prime})
has a bridge (resp. a gap).

Proof.

Assume
m^{\prime}
is a bridge or a gap of
\sigma
in
I
. By

5.3.
Regularity of the closed neighborhood ideals of trees:

According to
[
5
, Theorem 3.4]
, the regularity of the closed neighborhood ideal of a tree is equal to the matching number of the graph. Moreover, it follows from
Proposition

2.4
that

\mathrm{reg}\left(\frac{R}{NI(G)}\right)=\max\left\{d-r\;:\;\begin{aligned} &\sigma\subseteq\mathcal{G}(NI(G))\text{ is critical},\\
&|\sigma|=r,\ \deg(\operatorname{lcm}(\sigma))=d\end{aligned}\right\}.

Thus, it would be of interest to obtain a combinatorial proof of the fact that the regularity of the closed neighborhood ideal of a tree is the same as its matching number, leveraging the descriptions of the critical cells, analogous to the results presented in
Section

3.2
.
