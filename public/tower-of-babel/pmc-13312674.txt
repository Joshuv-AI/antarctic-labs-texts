Caregiver burden in inflammatory bowel disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Niharika Dadoo, Praisy K. Prabha, Anuraag Jena, Chhagan L. Birda, Sandeep Raut, Sandesh Parajuli, Akhilesh Sharma, Bikash Medhi, Shaji Sebastian, Vishal Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCaregiver burden is increasingly acknowledged in chronic illnesses but its impact in the context of Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) remains underexplored.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted following PRISMA 2020 guidelines to evaluate caregiver burden across physical, emotional, psychological, financial, and social domains. Comprehensive database searches were performed in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and Scopus databases on 3 February 2025. Studies assessing at least one aspect of caregiver burden in IBD were included. Data extraction and bias assessment were carried out independently by reviewers. Pooled prevalence rates for caregiver burden and associated domains were estimated using a random-effects model in R.ResultsOut of 383 records, 25 studies met the inclusion criteria, with 12 eligible for meta-analysis. The pooled prevalence of caregiver burden was 470 per 1000 carers (95% CI: 390–560), depression at 370 per 1000, and anxiety at 400 per 1000. Loss of work productivity and activity impairment were reported in 290 and 300 per 1000 caregivers, respectively. Stress, reduced quality of life, and financial burden were also common. Coping strategies varied, with adaptive strategies (e.g., religious practices) linked to lower burden. Caregiver burden was notably higher in cases of active disease, severe IBD, and among caregivers with additional dependents or personal psychiatric histories.ConclusionThis systematic review underscores a substantial and multidimensional burden on caregivers of IBD patients, affecting mental health, productivity and quality of life. Future interventions should incorporate caregiver-centred support mechanisms and resilience training, emphasising psychosocial care as an integral component of IBD management.The study was registered on Open Science Framework (OSF), Registration https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/YA3NU.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04703-2.

Background

Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBDs) are a group of chronic gastrointestinal diseases, primarily consisting of ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD) that impact individuals all over the world. Globally, the prevalence of IBD is significantly increasing [1]. IBD patients experience detrimental effects on their quality of life in addition to their physical health [2]. Patients experience a wide range of symptoms including bowel urgency, abdominal pain, constipation, rectal bleeding, diarrhoea, faecal incontinence, pain, perianal disease, weight loss, and fatigue, that affect their physical and mental health and reduce the quality of life [3]. IBD's long-term impacts are not only upsetting for the patient but also for their caregivers, those who care for them, such as family and friends. Managing IBD involves costly medications, frequent healthcare visits, and lifestyle adjustments, especially burdensome in developing countries [4]. Patients and caregivers face stress from invasive procedures, complex treatments, and daily medical routines [5]. The financial strain often forces caregivers to reduce work hours or leave jobs, affecting household income. Dietary restrictions, ongoing symptoms, and impaired quality of life add to the emotional and physical toll.

Caregivers are crucial in delivering physical, emotional, social, psychological, and financial support to their patients [6, 7]. Zarit et al. defined caregiver burden as ‘the degree to which a carer’s emotional or physical health, social life or financial status had suffered as a result of caring for their relatives’ [8]. The burden is the degree to which caregivers believe that taking care of their relative impacts their physical and mental health, financial situation, and social life. Caregivers may experience stress or feelings of burden from caring for family members with chronic illnesses due to the need for lifelong treatment [9] which can lower their quality of life [10]. They have to manage several challenges like familial responsibilities, loss of work productivity, and disturbed social life which can take a toll on physical and mental health. In the process of looking after the patient, family members may ignore their well-being which impacts their day-to-day functioning and quality of life. Alternatively, there may also be positive impacts of caregiving, such as a feeling of contentment and satisfaction in the role of caregiving [11], bringing family members closer together, companionship and leading a more fulfilling and meaningful life [12].

A lot of studies have explored the physical and psychological difficulties experienced by those with IBD but there is limited research around the concerns and psychosocial functioning of those caring for their IBD relatives or friends [13].

This study adopts Pearlin et al.’s [14] Stress Process Model to frame caregiver burden as a broad construct emerging from primary and secondary stressors, moderated by coping and social support. Within this model, caregiver burden is treated as a unifying, multidimensional construct arising from caregiving demands, while domains such as depression, anxiety, financial strain, coping, quality of life, and family functioning are conceptualised as related outcomes and downstream manifestations or modifying factors within the stress process.

This framework allows us to systematically integrate diverse domains into a cohesive narrative, rather than treating them as fragmented constructs, and helps explain variability in caregiver experiences across studies. This framework accounts for the heterogeneity in caregiver outcomes by recognising that resources and appraisals dictate how objective caregiving demands manifest as subjective strain [14].

The studies on caregiver burden in IBD have evaluated different domains (caregiver burden, depression, anxiety, stress, financial burden, loss of work productivity, activity impairment, quality of life and coping) and individual studies may not provide information about all the domains that may be impacted as part of the caregiver burden. Therefore, a comprehensive systematic review was performed to explore the role of caregiver functioning within IBD and the multifaceted impact it has on domains like caregiver burden, family functioning, stress, anxiety, depression, financial, and work productivity (absenteeism and presenteeism).

Methods

Systematic review

The systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines. The PRISMA flow diagram is presented in Fig. 1, and the completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided as a supplementary file [15].Fig. 1PRISMA flowchart indicating the Identification of studies via databases and registers

Eligibility criteria

All studies that reported on caregiver burden in patients with inflammatory bowel disease and included at least 10 participants were included. The studies were included irrespective of the study design i.e., observational (both retrospective or prospective) or randomised trials if they provided the relevant data. Studies with reports of at least one domain of ‘caregiver burden’ were included. However, studies that did not provide original data (that is, editorials, systematic reviews, comments, correspondence or narrative reviews, etc.) were excluded. Studies that included caregiver interviews regarding disease management or diet, etc., but did not report caregiver burden, were also excluded.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Scopus and Web of Science on February 3, 2025. The search strategy combined terms related to inflammatory bowel disease (e.g., “ulcerative colitis”, “Crohn’s disease”, “inflammatory bowel disease”) and caregiver outcomes (e.g., “ caregiver burden”, “caregiver stress”, “caregiver burnout”, “caregiver support”) using Boolean operators.

The PubMed search strategy was: (“ulcerative colitis” OR “Crohn’s disease” OR “inflammatory bowel disease”) AND (“ caregiver burden” OR “caregiver stress” OR “caregiver burnout” OR “caregiver support”). Similar search strategies were adapted for other databases. Embase: (‘ulcerative colitis’ OR ‘Crohn disease’ OR ‘inflammatory bowel disease’) AND (‘caregiver burden’ OR ‘caregiver support’ OR ‘caregiver burnout’ OR ‘caregiver strain’). Scopus: TITLE-ABS-KEY (inflammatory AND bowel AND disease OR Crohn’s AND disease OR Crohn AND disease OR ulcerative AND colitis) AND TITLE-ABS-KEY (caregiver AND burden OR caregiver AND stress OR caregiver AND burnout OR caregiver AND support). Web of Science: (“ulcerative colitis” OR “Crohn’s disease” OR “inflammatory bowel disease”) AND (“ caregiver burden” OR “caregiver stress” OR “caregiver burnout” OR “caregiver support”).

Full search strings for all databases are provided in Supplementary Table 1. The search was conducted without any limits of date, language or study type. Additional studies were included using forward and backwards snowballing.

All eligible studies identified through database searches were considered for inclusion. Grey literature, conference abstracts and preprints were not explicitly excluded if they met the inclusion criteria. The protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework, Registration 10.17605/OSF.IO/YA3NU.

Study screening and selection

The titles extracted from various databases were imported into the Zotero software. The removal of duplicates was done manually. The remaining titles were screened initially for title and abstract. At this stage, any article that did not relate to the study question or did not provide original data was excluded and the rest of the titles were taken for full-text screening. The full-text screening was done to identify titles that provided relevant data. The studies that provided any information concerning any of the domains of caregiver burden like caregiver burden, depression, anxiety, stress, financial burden, family functioning, quality of life, coping, work productivity and activity impairment were included. The studies that did not provide relevant information were excluded with reasons for exclusion. Each of these steps was performed manually and independently by two reviewers (ND, PKP). Any disagreement and/or confusion during the selection process was resolved by discussion with other reviewers through consensus (SR, VS). No automation was used in any of the steps including duplicate removal, screening, selection or data extraction.

Data extraction

The data from each of the included studies were extracted manually and independently by two reviewers from the published reports. Where possible the original papers with the largest available data were preferred over abstracts from the same study. The data extraction was done separately and independently by two reviewers (ND, PP) and conflicts were resolved by consensus and discussion with a third reviewer (VS). We extracted information about the reference of the study, the duration of the study, the site of the study, the nature of participants, patient details including the type of disease, definition of caregivers, gender and age of the caregivers and domains in which the caregiver burden was assessed. Where feasible, categorical data regarding the number of caregivers impacted in the particular domain were extracted and included in the quantitative synthesis. The studies that did not provide categorical data, or provided data on temporal changes in various domains were included in the qualitative analysis. For each of the domains, the studies and the outcomes were tabulated separately.

Risk of bias

The risk of bias assessment was done using Joanna Briggs’s critical appraisal tool for cohort and cross-sectional studies [16]. Two of the investigators (AJ and CB) assessed the methodological quality and risk of bias of every study using Joanna Briggs’s critical appraisal tool (Supplementary tables 14 and 15). Any discrepancy between the two authors was settled after discussion with the third author (V.S). The studies were critically assessed for appropriate sample inclusion, adequacy of sample size, study setting description, assessment of confounding factors and method of statistical analysis in the assessment of caregivers’ burden. We planned to assess for publication bias if at least 10 studies were available for a particular quantitative analysis.

Data synthesis

All analyses were performed on R version 4.3.0 using the base package and additional packages of meta and metafor [17, 18]. We planned to calculate the pooled prevalence of caregiver burden and any additional domains assessed if at least three studies provided relevant information. We report only the random effects model because of differences in the underlying population and the definitions of various domains. We used the random intercept logistic regression method after logit transformation to estimate the pooled prevalence rates. The pooled prevalence was reported as per 1000 patients. Since only a limited number of studies were included in the quantitative synthesis, we could not evaluate significant heterogeneity (I2 > 50%) using subgroup analyses or meta-regression.

Results

Study screening and selection

A total of 383 records were retrieved from our search of databases including Embase, Pubmed, Web of Science and Scopus. After removing duplicates, 248 titles were eligible for initial title and abstract screening. Eventually, 45 articles were selected for full-text screening. We excluded 20 titles due to various reasons (Supplementary Table 2). A total of 25 titles were included in the systematic review. Of these 25 titles, 12 studies were included in the quantitative synthesis for various domains (Fig. 1, PRISMA flow for study screening and selection). Supplementary Table 3 depicts the included studies, characteristics of the included population and definition of caregivers and the domains each study assessed.

Risk of Bias

Risk of bias was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools for cross-sectional (n = 23) and cohort (n = 2) studies. Most studies showed low risk in defining inclusion criteria, describing subjects/settings and validly measuring exposures/outcomes with appropriate statistical analysis in the majority.

Overall, most studies demonstrated adequate methodological quality particularly in defining inclusion criteria and using valid and reliable outcome measures. However, common weaknesses included inadequate identification and management of confounding factors, along with occasional lack of clarity in study settings and statistical analyses.

Caregiver burden

Eight studies assessed the caregiver burden [19–26]. All the studies included adult patients (age ≥ 18). Banerjee R, et al., (2024) found the highest caregiver burden in parents or spouses [24]. The caregiver burden was exacerbated for carers of a patient with active disease [19]. The seven studies that reported caregiver burden using the Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI score of ≥ 21) included 1086 participants. The pooled prevalence rate of caregiver burden was 470 per 1000 carers (95% CI, 390–560, I2 = 87%, Fig. 2). Interestingly, Parekh et al. found that participation in religious activities and attendance at a support group were predictive of reduced levels of burden [19]. Depression

Six studies assessed the depression in caregivers for IBD, of which 4 studies (598 participants) provided quantitative data [25–30]. The four studies that reported depression included 598 participants. The pooled prevalence rate of caregiver burden was 370 per 1000 carers (95% CI, 260–490, I2 = 92%, Fig. 3). Overall, all the studies reported significantly higher depression in the caregivers, although severe depression was uncommon. A detailed description of the study findings is tabulated in Supplementary Table 5.Fig. 3Forest plot depicting the pooled prevalence rate of depression per 1000 carers

Anxiety

Four studies assessed anxiety in caregivers (515 participants) [25, 26, 28, 29]. Two studies enrolled adults with IBD while one study enrolled paediatric and adolescent population with IBD. The three studies that reported anxiety included 515 participants. The pooled prevalence rate of caregiver burden was 400 per 1000 carers (95% CI, 220–620, I2 = 95%, Fig. 4). Loss of work productivity

Seven studies assessed the impact of caregiving on work productivity [20, 22, 31–35]. Four studies used the Work Productivity and Activity Impairment (WPAI) questionnaire for assessing the work productivity in terms of absenteeism and presenteeism [20, 22, 33, 35]. The study conducted by Magro et al. found that the ability to work was related to gender (affecting 11.8% of females and 7.5% of males, P < 0.017), level of education (affecting 25.5% caregivers with basic education and 4.6% of those with at least one university degree, P < 0.0001), and age (affecting 15% of those older than 40 years and 4.5% of those younger than 40 years, P < 0.001) [31]. Overall, all the studies reported that caring for IBD patients did have an impact on some aspects of the work function of the caregivers compared to the general population including reduced work hours, absenteeism and presenteeism. The three studies that reported anxiety included 772 participants. The pooled prevalence rate of caregiver burden was 290 per 1000 carers (95% CI, 110–580, I2 = 98%, Fig. 5). Activity impairment

Four studies assessed the impact of caregiver burden on activity impairment [20, 22, 31, 35]. The four studies that reported anxiety included 1001 participants. The pooled prevalence rate of caregiver burden was 300 per 1000 carers (95% CI, 170–480, I2 = 96%, Fig. 6). Stress

Eight studies assessed stress among caregivers [29, 36–42]. Five studies used the Paediatric Inventory for Parents (PIP) to gauge the stress levels among caregivers [36–38, 40, 41], one study used the Perceived Stress Scale 10 [42], one used the distress thermometer for parents (DT-P) [39] while one study used the Impact of Event Scale-Revised (IES-R) to assess PTSD [29]. Carlsen K, et al. 2019 found that the top five most frequently experienced parental difficulties (PIP) were worrying about how friends and relatives interact with my child; disagreeing with a member of the health system; spending a great deal of time in unfamiliar settings; being far away from family and/or friends; and missing important events in the lives of other family members [40]. Detailed information of the included studies is provided in Supplementary Table 9.

Family functioning

Two studies assessed the social implications in adolescents with IBD specifically the impact on family functioning [38, 43]. It reported that families with clinically elevated difficulties in family functioning experienced more stress. A detailed description of the study findings is tabulated in Supplementary Table 10.

Financial burden

Only two studies assessed financial loss in caregivers for paediatric and adolescent population with IBD [32, 33]. Both studies provided absolute numbers of financial loss in caregivers as an estimate of annual productivity costs, which were calculated by the human capital approach. The study conducted by Kahn et al. also suggested that over the course of a patient’s childhood, from age 13.4 years (the average age of diagnosis in this study population) to age 18 years, the accumulated IBD patient caregiver productivity losses were $5161 more than controls [32]. Overall, both the studies reported more loss of annual productivity in the caregivers compared to the general population. A detailed description of the study findings is tabulated in Supplementary Table 11.

Quality of life

Five studies assessed the quality of life in caregivers [19, 24, 27, 28, 33]. Study conducted by Parekh et al. [19] found that factors associated with a lower QOL among IBD caregivers included: presence of a dependent (other than the patient) in the household, caring for patients with active IBD exacerbation and higher disease severity, and a personal history of psychiatric illness. Interestingly, the two factors predictive of a better QOL for caregivers were participation in religious or spiritual activities and attendance at a support group. Also, lower QoL scores were observed in caregivers, having a negative correlation with disease severity. Since all the studies provided categorical data for different aspects of mental health among the caregivers, a meta-analysis was not possible to be performed. A detailed description of the study findings is tabulated in Supplementary Table 12.

Coping

Three studies employed scales to assess the coping strategies being used by caregivers of IBD patients, distinguishing them into adaptive and maladaptive coping strategies [19, 24, 40]. In the study conducted by Parekh et al., 105 of 162 caregivers (64.8%) used predominantly adaptive coping patterns while the remaining 57 (35.2%) employed maladaptive coping patterns [19]. Religion (14.1%) and self-distraction (24.2%) were the most common adaptive and maladaptive coping strategies respectively, according to a study conducted by Banerjee et al. [24]. Detailed description of the study findings is tabulated in Supplementary Table 13.

Discussion

The present systematic review identified a significant caregiver burden among carers of patients with IBD. The studies reported the presence of substantial caregiver burden which impacts all aspects of the life of a caregiver. Further, our findings also suggest that a significant number of carers have substantial financial burdens and that caregiving impacts their work productivity. Zand et al. reported that symptoms of depression and anxiety were reported in the studies as caregivers navigated through the responsibilities of being a caregiver [20]. Their personal time, privacy and ability to engage in hobbies were impacted due to caregiving as observed by Magro et al. [31].

These findings, although well-reported for some of the other chronic diseases including malignancies, are relatively less well-recognised in IBD [13]. The information on coping strategies is also limited; however, the available published literature suggests that a risk of maladaptive strategies also exists in carers for IBD (Fig. 7). Disease severity [29], having an active disease [35], lower annual family income [34], increased other responsibilities of family and work [22] and higher levels of anxiety [25] were some of the factors that heighten caregiver burden, stress and reduced quality of life. Caregiver burden was particularly high in many Asian countries as reported by Banerjee et al. [24] and Dadoo et al. [26], which can be attributed to strong cultural expectations and family-centric values that place the responsibility of caregiving primarily on family members often without access to formal support systems or adequate training.Fig. 7Determinants of Caregiver Outcomes in Inflammatory Bowel Disease: Risk and Protective Factors

Caregiver burden varies across different conditions, with significant levels reported in multiple populations. Among caregivers of cancer patients, 21.38% reported experiencing moderate-to-severe burden [44]. In the context of chronic health conditions, 30.9% of respondents perceived a high burden related to caring for close family members [45]. 52.6% of caregivers of individuals with young-onset dementia reported a high caregiver burden [46]. For caregivers of individuals with schizophrenia, 31.3% felt distressed, 24.7% reported increased social burden, and 26.7% experienced disruptions in their daily routines [47].

Caregiver burden is well recognised as an accompaniment of chronic diseases. High rates of caregiver burden have been reported in many conditions including malignancy, neurological diseases (like stroke, Parkinson’s disease, dementia), cardiac conditions (heart failure), the elderly and others. Unfortunately, there is no literature on various interventions to address caregiver burden in IBD. Outside the setting of IBD, varied interventions have been used to address caregiver burden. These include educational interventions to improve understanding of the disease and coping with it. Other helpful interventions include the provision of dyadic care (targeting family well-being), mindfulness-based interventions, cognitive behavioural therapy, and telephonic support programs [48–50]. The need to study and implement these interventions cannot be overemphasised as there is a possibility of maladaptive coping strategies that may further worsen the caregiver burden. Therefore, future studies should study the impact of various interventions on caregiver burden in carers of IBD patients.

The systematic review has certain limitations: the number of studies assessing each of the domains is relatively small. A pooled estimation could be performed only for some of the domains but could not address the heterogeneity through subgroup analyses due to a smaller number of studies. Formal assessment of publication bias was limited due to the small number of studies and high heterogeneity; however, its presence cannot be excluded. However, the differences in definitions of caregivers and the measures used to assess various domains are some of the obvious sources of heterogeneity. Differences in cultural contexts, healthcare settings, definitions of caregivers, assessment tools and patient characteristics likely contribute to the observed variability across studies and underscore the complexity of interpreting pooled findings. High statistical heterogeneity was anticipated given the diversity of study populations (adult vs. pediatric IBD; Crohn's disease vs. ulcerative colitis), caregiver roles (parent, spouse, adult child), cultural contexts (Asian vs. Western samples), and measurement instruments. In Asian and other collectivist cultures, caregiving is often viewed as a familial obligation which may lead to lower reported burden despite significant caregiving demands. Additionally, caregiver burden may be underreported due to stigma, social desirability, differences in family structures and access to healthcare support systems.

Routine clinical care in IBD should incorporate brief screening of caregiver strain, especially in patients with active or complex disease to enable early identification and support. Future research should focus on developing integrated patient–caregiver interventions, scalable digital supports and culturally sensitive approaches. Importantly, caregiver burden may indirectly affect patient outcomes, including adherence to treatment, care-seeking behaviours and overall disease management, underscoring its clinical relevance.

Further, as pointed out, there is a lack of data on interventions to address the caregiver burden. Since most studies exclude carers with psychiatric comorbidities, we are unable to comment on any additive effects of caregiver burden on underlying psychiatric conditions. However, the present systematic review is the first attempt to systematically study caregiver burden and should raise awareness on this aspect of IBD management.

Conclusion

In conclusion, carers of individuals with IBD experience a substantial caregiver burden, accompanied by increased risks of depression, anxiety, financial strain, and reduced work productivity. Future research should focus on developing and evaluating targeted interventions to alleviate this burden and support caregiver well-being.
