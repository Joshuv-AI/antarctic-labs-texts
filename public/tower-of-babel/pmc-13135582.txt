Engineering L-Arg@ZIF-8 nanoparticles to modulate the immune–osteogenic–angiogenic microenvironment for accelerated bone fracture healing
Authors: Siyu Chen, Taojin Feng, Jiang Liu, Yi Li, Yuqi Wang, Taiqian Gong, Yang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBone fracture healing requires coordinated regulation of osteogenesis, angiogenesis, and immune homeostasis within a dynamically evolving microenvironment. However, current biomaterials rarely integrate these three regulatory dimensions. Herein, we engineered L-arginine–loaded zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (L-Arg@ZIF-8) nanoparticles to remodel the immune–osteogenic–angiogenic microenvironment and accelerate fracture healing. L-Arg@ZIF-8 nanoparticles exhibited uniform morphology, positive surface charge, and stable L-Arg incorporation, enabling efficient cellular uptake and sustained bioactivity. Using two physiologically relevant 3D co-culture spheroid models (osteoblast–macrophage and osteoblast–endothelial), we demonstrated that L-Arg@ZIF-8 promotes osteogenic differentiation, endothelial activation and the upregulation of angiogenic markers, and strengthens osteogenesis–angiogenesis coupling. Bulk transcriptomic profiling further revealed activation of regenerative pathways, including PI3K–Akt and Wnt signaling, along with coordinated modulation of cytokine–receptor interactions and immune-related remodeling programs. In vivo, L-Arg@ZIF-8 markedly accelerated fracture repair in a rat rib fracture model, characterized by enhanced callus formation, increased bone mineral density, greater trabecular thickness, and a significantly elevated mineral apposition rate. Histological and immunofluorescence analyses confirmed upregulation of key osteogenic markers (OPN, OSX) at the fracture site. Together, these findings demonstrate that L-Arg@ZIF-8 functions as a bioengineered microenvironment-modulating nanoplatform that orchestrates immune regulation, osteogenesis, and angiogenesis to promote efficient fracture healing. Introduction

Bone regeneration and repair represent critical challenges in orthopedic medicine, with millions of patients worldwide suffering from bone defects, fractures, and degenerative bone diseases that require therapeutic intervention [1]. The complex process of bone healing involves intricate interactions between multiple cell types, including osteoblasts, immune cells, and vascular endothelial cells, making it a fundamentally important area of biomedical research [2]. Therapeutic strategies that can simultaneously promote osteogenesis, modulate immune responses, and enhance angiogenesis hold tremendous promise for advancing bone regenerative medicine and improving patient outcomes. Consequently, the development of effective bone healing therapies has become a widely accepted research priority due to the aging population and the increasing prevalence of bone-related disorders [1].

Extensive research has focused on identifying bioactive compounds that can enhance bone formation and repair processes, with particular attention to amino acids and their derivatives that play essential roles in cellular metabolism and signaling [3, 4]. L-Arginine (L-Arg), a semi-essential amino acid, has emerged as a promising therapeutic candidate due to its involvement in nitric oxide synthesis, collagen production, and immune modulation. Several studies have demonstrated its positive effects on osteoblast function, bone matrix formation, and angiogenic processes in various experimental models [5–7]. However, the direct clinical application of free L-Arg is often hindered by its rapid metabolism, short half-life, and lack of targeted retention at the injury site. To overcome these limitations, developing an efficient delivery system is imperative. Zeolitic Imidazolate Framework-8 (ZIF-8), a subclass of metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), presents an ideal nanocarrier due to its high porosity, pH-responsive biodegradability, and exceptional loading capacity.

Despite the potential of L-Arg, previous studies have primarily investigated its effects using simplified two-dimensional (2D) culture systems or single cell types, which fail to capture the complex multicellular interactions—specifically the osteo-immune and osteo-angiogenic coupling—that occur during bone healing in vivo [7, 8]. Furthermore, the molecular mechanisms underlying how L-Arg regulates these intercellular communication pathways remain incompletely understood, limiting the translation of these findings into effective therapeutic strategies.

Therefore, our study aimed to comprehensively evaluate the effects of L-Arg-loaded ZIF-8 nanoparticles (L-Arg@ZIF-8) on bone formation and angiogenesis using physiologically relevant three-dimensional (3D) co-culture spheroid models that better recapitulate the cellular complexity of native bone tissue. We employed two distinct 3D models—osteoblast-macrophage and osteoblast-endothelial spheroids—combined with comprehensive transcriptomic profiling to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying L-Arg@ZIF-8’s therapeutic effects. Finally, considering the clinical urgency of treating chest trauma and the high demand for rapid stability in rib injuries, we validated the therapeutic efficacy of this nanoplatform in a rat rib fracture model. Our findings demonstrate that L-Arg@ZIF-8 significantly accelerates fracture healing by enhancing osteogenic differentiation and angiogenesis through the coordinated modulation of multiple signaling pathways (such as PI3K-Akt and Wnt) and the optimization of the immune microenvironment.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of L-Arg@ZIF-8 nanoparticles

To verify the successful loading of L-arginine (L-Arg) into ZIF-8 nanoparticles, L-Arg@ZIF-8 was synthesized via a one-pot method, wherein L-Arg competitively coordinated with Zn2+ against the imidazole ligand. A series of systematic characterizations were then performed.

The morphology of the nanoparticles was observed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Fig. 1A, B). The results showed that the pristine ZIF-8 nanoparticles exhibited a well-dispersed, uniform crystalline morphology with a smooth surface and a typical rhombic dodecahedron structure. After the introduction of L-Arg via competitive coordination, the L-Arg@ZIF-8 nanoparticles largely retained the original polyhedral framework of ZIF-8, but their surfaces became discernibly rougher, providing an initial indication that L-Arg was successfully doped into or encapsulated within the ZIF-8 matrix.

Physicochemical characterization of ZIF-8 and L-Arg@ZIF-8 nanoparticles. A Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and B scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of pristine ZIF-8 and L-Arg@ZIF-8. Scale bar = 100 nm. C Average diameters of ZIF-8 and L-Arg@ZIF-8 nanoparticles. D Zeta potential measurements of ZIF-8 and L-Arg@ZIF-8. E Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra. F X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001, ns: not significant

To further quantify the changes in physicochemical properties before and after loading, their particle size and surface charge were determined. As shown in Fig. 1C, the average diameter of L-Arg@ZIF-8 significantly increased to approximately 105 nm from ~ 93 nm for pristine ZIF-8 (p < 0.05). Concurrently, Zeta potential analysis (Fig. 1D) revealed a significant alteration in surface charge: the incorporation of L-Arg caused the Zeta potential to increase substantially from + 10.2 mV (pristine ZIF-8) to + 22 mV (L-Arg@ZIF-8) (***p < 0.0001). This marked enhancement in positive surface charge strongly confirms the successful modification of the nanoparticle surface with L-Arg, which is rich in positively charged groups (e.g., protonated amino and guanidinium groups).

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) provided direct chemical evidence for the successful loading of L-Arg (Fig. 1E). While completely retaining the characteristic skeletal peaks of ZIF-8, the spectrum of L-Arg@ZIF-8 displayed two new, distinct characteristic absorption bands at 1592 cm−1 and 3133 cm−1. These peaks can be respectively attributed to the bending vibration of the primary amine group (–NH2) or the asymmetric stretching of the carboxylate group, and the N–H stretching vibration of the protonated amine group (–NH3+) from L-Arg.

The crystalline structure of the materials was analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Fig. 1F). The results demonstrated that the XRD pattern of L-Arg@ZIF-8 was highly consistent with that of pristine ZIF-8, with both exhibiting the characteristic diffraction peaks of the ZIF-8 structure. This proves that the loading process of L-Arg did not disrupt the crystalline framework of ZIF-8.

In summary, the characterization results from morphology, particle size, Zeta potential, FTIR, and XRD are mutually corroborative, collectively demonstrating the successful synthesis of a positively charged L-Arg@ZIF-8 nanocomposite that maintains the integral crystalline structure of ZIF-8.

Effects of L-Arg@ZIF-8 on the viability, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation of osteoblast-macrophage co-culture spheroids

To investigate the effects of L-Arg@ZIF-8 on the viability and proliferation of 3D co-culture spheroids composed of osteoblasts and macrophages, Live/Dead staining and EdU incorporation assays were performed at day 1, day 7, and day 14 post-culture.

As shown in Fig. 2A and B, the Live/Dead staining results demonstrated no significant difference in cell viability between the L-Arg@ZIF-8-treated group and the PBS control group at day 1 and day 7. However, a significant increase in dead cell fluorescence was observed in both groups at day 14 compared to earlier time points, indicating time-dependent cell death within the 3D spheroids. Importantly, no significant difference was detected between the PBS and L-Arg@ZIF-8 groups at day 14 (ns), suggesting that L-Arg@ZIF-8 treatment does not adversely affect cell viability and possesses good biocompatibility.

Similarly, Fig. 2C and D illustrates the results of the EdU staining, which revealed no significant difference in proliferative activity between the L-Arg@ZIF-8-treated and control groups at day 1. Notably, the EdU incorporation in both groups significantly declined by day 14 compared to earlier time points, suggesting a reduced proliferative capacity as the 3D co-culture progressed. No significant difference was observed between the treatment groups at day 14, indicating that L-Arg@ZIF-8 does not alter the basal proliferative response of the cells.

Crucially, Fig. 2E and F shows the expression level of the osteogenic marker osteocalcin (OCN) at day 14. Compared with the control group, the spheroids treated with L-Arg@ZIF-8 exhibited a significant increase in OCN fluorescence intensity. This result indicates that L-Arg@ZIF-8 can effectively promote osteogenic differentiation within the 3D osteoblast-macrophage spheroid model without compromising fundamental cellular functions such as viability and proliferation.

To further elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying this enhanced osteogenesis and to distinguish the specific contributions of the cargo versus the carrier, we conducted additional in vitro evaluations using a co-culture system (Fig. S1). Quantitative RT-qPCR analysis of key osteogenic markers (Sp7, Runx2, and Bglap) suggests that while empty ZIF-8 induced only a modest upregulation of osteogenic genes compared to the PBS group—likely attributable to Zn2+ release—the L-Arg@ZIF-8 group exhibited significantly higher expression levels than both the PBS and empty ZIF-8 groups. These findings indicate that the observed enhanced osteogenic activity is primarily attributed to the loaded L-arginine, rather than the carrier alone, thereby corroborating the pro-osteogenic effects observed in the 3D spheroid model.

Transcriptomic analysis reveals that L-Arg@ZIF-8 modulates immune response and bone metabolism pathways in 3D co-culture spheroids

To elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms by which L-Arg@ZIF-8 promotes osteoblast-macrophage interactions, a comprehensive transcriptomic analysis was performed on 3D co-culture spheroids treated with L-Arg@ZIF-8 or PBS for 14 days. Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated a clear separation between the L-Arg@ZIF-8-treated and PBS control groups (Fig. 3A), indicating that L-Arg@ZIF-8 treatment induced a distinct transcriptional profile.

Transcriptomic analysis of 3D osteoblast-macrophage co-culture spheroids after treatment with L-Arg@ZIF-8. A Principal Component Analysis (PCA) plot of samples from the L-Arg@ZIF-8-treated and PBS control groups, showing a distinct separation in their transcriptional profiles. B Volcano plot of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Red dots represent significantly upregulated genes, blue dots represent significantly downregulated genes, and gray dots represent non-significant genes. C KEGG pathway enrichment analysis bubble plot for upregulated DEGs. The size of the bubble corresponds to the gene count, and the color represents the adjusted p value (p.adjust). D KEGG pathway enrichment analysis bubble plot for downregulated DEGs. E and F Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) plots showing the enrichment of gene sets related to (E) bone mineralization and (F) bone remodeling

Differential gene expression (DEG) analysis identified 934 significantly altered genes, with 617 genes upregulated and 317 genes downregulated in the L-Arg@ZIF-8 group compared to the control (Fig. 3B). The volcano plot visualized several highly significant differentially expressed genes, including key osteogenic markers such as Col1a1, Col1a2, and Sp7, as well as critical regulators involved in bone remodeling and osteoclast differentiation, such as Mmp9, Acp5 (encoding TRAP), Tnfsf11 (RANKL), and Tnfrsf11a (RANK). The upregulation of these genes suggests that L-Arg@ZIF-8 actively promotes bone matrix formation and bone metabolic activities at the transcriptional level.

To further explore the biological functions of these DEGs, a Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was conducted on the upregulated genes (Fig. 3C). The results revealed that the most significantly enriched pathways were closely related to immune regulation and bone metabolism. Among them, the Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, Chemokine signaling pathway, and Phagosome pathways were the most prominent. Notably, the Osteoclast differentiation pathway was also significantly enriched, suggesting that L-Arg@ZIF-8 may couple osteogenesis and osteoclastogenesis by modulating the immune microenvironment, thereby promoting bone remodeling.

Conversely, KEGG pathway analysis of the downregulated genes (Fig. 3D) indicated the suppression of pathways such as the Hippo signaling pathway and Glutamatergic synapse, suggesting that L-Arg@ZIF-8 treatment may concurrently modulate specific signaling cascades and amino acid metabolic processes.

Furthermore, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) provided additional evidence for the functional consequences of L-Arg@ZIF-8 treatment. The analysis showed that gene sets related to “Bone mineralization” (Fig. 3E) and “Bone remodeling” (Fig. 3F) were both positively enriched, implying that the entire gene programs involved in bone formation and remodeling processes were holistically enhanced following L-Arg@ZIF-8 treatment.

In summary, the transcriptomic evidence indicates that L-Arg@ZIF-8 promotes bone remodeling through the coordinated modulation of immune–bone interactions. Its mechanism involves the activation of cytokine signaling, osteoclast differentiation pathways, and the initiation of bone mineralization-related gene programs.

L-Arg@ZIF-8 enhances the expression of osteogenic and angiogenic markers without compromising cell viability or proliferation

To further evaluate the modulatory effect of L-Arg@ZIF-8 on the osteogenic and angiogenic microenvironment, a 3D co-culture spheroid model composed of osteoblasts and endothelial cells was established. Cell viability and proliferation were assessed during the early stages of culture, while differentiation markers were evaluated following prolonged culture.

As shown in Fig. 4A and B, Live/Dead staining performed on days 1, 7, and 14 indicated no significant differences in cell viability between the L-Arg@ZIF-8-treated group and the PBS control group throughout the 14-day culture period. Although a modest, time-dependent increase in the dead cell signal was observed in both groups as the culture duration extended (indicated by asterisks spanning time points in Fig. 4B)—consistent with the expected decline in viability within prolonged 3D culture systems—no statistically significant difference was detected between the two groups at day 14 (ns). This confirms the favorable biocompatibility of the material.

Figure 4C and D display the EdU staining results used to assess proliferative activity. Comparable levels of EdU incorporation were observed in both the L-Arg@ZIF-8 and control groups across all time points, suggesting that treatment with the material did not significantly alter cell proliferation within the co-culture spheroids. Consistent with the viability results, a progressive decline in proliferative activity was noted in both groups as the culture duration increased (Fig. 4D).

Notably, after 14 days of culture, immunofluorescence staining revealed a marked upregulation of both osteogenic and angiogenic markers in the L-Arg@ZIF-8-treated group. Specifically, the expression of the key osteogenic marker osteocalcin (OCN) was visibly higher in the L-Arg@ZIF-8 group compared to the control (Fig. 4E). More prominently, the fluorescence intensity of the endothelial marker CD31 exhibited a substantial increase in the L-Arg@ZIF-8 group (Fig. 4F). Quantitative analysis further confirmed (Fig. 4G) that, compared to the PBS group, the L-Arg@ZIF-8-treated spheroids showed a significant increase in OCN expression and a highly significant elevation in CD31 expression. These findings strongly indicate that L-Arg@ZIF-8 significantly promotes both osteogenic differentiation and angiogenesis within the 3D osteoblast–endothelial co-culture environment, thereby potentially enhancing the coupling of osteogenesis and angiogenesis.

Transcriptomic analysis reveals L-Arg@ZIF-8 modulates angiogenic signaling and osteoblast differentiation pathways in 3D osteoblast-endothelial co-culture spheroids

To further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the effects of L-Arg@ZIF-8 on osteoblast-endothelial cell interactions, a comprehensive transcriptomic sequencing analysis was performed on 3D osteoblast-endothelial (C166) co-culture spheroids treated with L-Arg@ZIF-8 or a PBS control for 14 days.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) demonstrated a profound separation between the L-Arg@ZIF-8-treated and PBS control groups (Fig. 5A), with PC1 accounting for an overwhelming 99% of the variance, indicating that the introduction of L-Arg@ZIF-8 triggered a substantial transcriptomic remodeling. Differential gene expression (DEG) analysis identified a total of 4555 significantly altered genes, with 3063 genes upregulated and 1492 genes downregulated in the L-Arg@ZIF-8 group compared to the control (Fig. 5B). The volcano plot revealed a more extensive range of transcriptional changes than observed in the osteoblast-macrophage system, highlighting the critical role of endothelial cells in mediating bone-vascular coupling. Notably, several key genes intimately associated with osteogenesis and angiogenesis were significantly upregulated, including Runx2, Bmp2, Sp7, Spp1, as well as growth factor receptors Fgfr1, Fgfr2, Pdgfra, and the vascular endothelial growth factor Vegfd.

Mechanistic analysis of the transcriptomic profile of 3D osteoblast-endothelial co-culture spheroids treated with L-Arg@ZIF-8. A PCA plot of the L-Arg@ZIF-8-treated and PBS control groups. B Volcano plot of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). C KEGG pathway enrichment bubble plot for upregulated DEGs. D KEGG pathway enrichment bubble plot for downregulated DEGs. E and F Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) plots showing the significant positive enrichment of gene sets related to E osteoblast differentiation and F bone remodeling

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the upregulated genes (Fig. 5C) revealed that the most significantly enriched pathways were crucial for angiogenesis and bone formation. The PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, Regulation of Actin Cytoskeleton, and Cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction were among the top enriched terms. Furthermore, the Wnt signaling pathway, Focal adhesion, and ECM-receptor interaction were also significantly enriched, indicating that L-Arg@ZIF-8 exerts a broad activating effect on the cellular signaling networks governing angiogenesis and osteogenesis.

Conversely, KEGG pathway analysis of the downregulated genes (Fig. 5D) showed that immune and inflammatory-related pathways were predominantly suppressed. The most significantly downregulated pathways included the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway and pathways associated with viral infections (e.g., Herpes simplex virus 1 and Epstein-Barr virus infection). This suggests that L-Arg@ZIF-8 treatment may modulate and optimize the inflammatory immune response within the osteoblast-endothelial co-culture microenvironment, thereby fostering a more favorable milieu for regeneration.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further validated the functional impact of L-Arg@ZIF-8 on bone-related processes. The analysis demonstrated that gene sets for “Osteoblast differentiation” (Fig. 5E) and “Bone remodeling” (Fig. 5F) were both significantly and positively enriched, signifying that the gene expression programs involved in osteoblast maturation and bone matrix remodeling were holistically activated.

Collectively, these transcriptomic findings demonstrate that L-Arg@ZIF-8 significantly enhances both osteogenic differentiation and angiogenic processes by synergistically activating the PI3K-Akt, Wnt, and growth factor signaling pathways, while concurrently suppressing potential inflammatory responses within the 3D osteoblast-endothelial co-culture environment.

L-Arg@ZIF-8 accelerates bone fracture healing in vivo

To validate the therapeutic potential of L-Arg@ZIF-8 in a physiological environment, a rib fracture model was established, and bone regeneration was evaluated using micro-CT imaging and histological analyses.

Micro-CT 3D reconstruction provided a macroscopic view of the fracture callus (Fig. 6A). In the PBS control group, the fracture gap remained distinct with limited callus formation. The ZIF-8 group exhibited partial bridging; however, the L-Arg@ZIF-8 treatment group demonstrated the most robust healing, characterized by a thick, well-connected callus that completely bridged the fracture site. Quantitative analysis of bone microarchitecture parameters corroborated these visual observations (Fig. 6B). The L-Arg@ZIF-8 group showed statistically significant increases in Bone Mineral Density (BMD), Bone Volume fraction (BV/TV), and Trabecular Thickness (Tb.Th) compared to both the PBS and ZIF-8 groups, indicating superior mineralization and bone mass accumulation.

Histological evaluations further confirmed the quality of the regenerated tissue. Masson’s trichrome staining (Fig. 6C) revealed that the fracture gap in the L-Arg@ZIF-8 group was filled with dense, mature collagen fibers and newly formed bone matrix, whereas the control group consisted largely of fibrous connective tissue. Furthermore, Toluidine blue staining (Fig. 6D) highlighted the cellular composition of the callus. Based on these stained sections, quantitative histomorphometry (Fig. 6G) demonstrated a significantly higher number of osteoblasts per bone perimeter (N.Ob/B.Pm) in the L-Arg@ZIF-8 group compared to controls, suggesting that the material actively recruits osteoblasts or promotes their proliferation in situ.

To assess the rate of bone formation, sequential fluorescent labeling was performed (Fig. 6E). The distance between the labeled fronts serves as an indicator of the Mineral Apposition Rate (MAR). As shown in Fig. 6H, the L-Arg@ZIF-8 group exhibited the widest inter-label distance, translating to a significantly higher MAR compared to other groups, thereby confirming accelerated osteogenesis.

The molecular mechanism of bone repair was investigated via immunofluorescence staining for key osteogenic markers (Fig. 6F). The expression levels of Osteopontin (OPN), a matrix protein, and Osterix (OSX), a critical transcription factor for osteoblast differentiation, were markedly elevated in the L-Arg@ZIF-8 group. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 6I) confirmed a significant increase in both the OPN-positive area and the density of OSX-positive cells. Collectively, these in vivo findings demonstrate that L-Arg@ZIF-8 effectively accelerates fracture healing by enhancing osteoblast accumulation, bone matrix deposition, and mineralization.

Discussion

In this study, we successfully developed a multifunctional nanoplatform, L-Arg@ZIF-8, and comprehensively investigated its therapeutic potential for accelerating bone fracture healing. Our findings demonstrate that L-Arg@ZIF-8 not only possesses excellent biocompatibility and physicochemical properties but also significantly enhances osteogenic differentiation and angiogenesis in complex 3D co-culture environments. Through rigorous transcriptomic profiling, we elucidated that these effects are mediated by distinct molecular mechanisms involving the PI3K-Akt and Wnt signaling pathways, as well as the modulation of osteo-immunomodulatory networks. Crucially, we validated these in vitro findings in a rat rib fracture model, providing compelling evidence that L-Arg@ZIF-8 accelerates bone repair in vivo by promoting both osteogenesis and angiogenesis.

The rationale for encapsulating L-Arginine within ZIF-8 nanoparticles stems from the limitations of free amino acids in clinical applications. While L-Arginine’s beneficial effects on bone formation and angiogenesis are well-documented [8–12], its rapid metabolism and short half-life often necessitate high doses that can lead to systemic side effects. Our results confirm that ZIF-8 serves as an efficient nanocarrier, maintaining a stable particle size and providing a positive surface charge that likely facilitates cellular uptake. Furthermore, the beneficial effects observed may be attributed to the combined action of L-Arg and the released zinc ions (Zn2+) from the ZIF-8 framework. As a substrate for nitric oxide synthase, L-Arginine promotes nitric oxide production, which enhances vasodilation and blood flow to bone tissues, thereby improving nutrient and oxygen delivery essential for osteoblast function and bone matrix formation [13]. Concurrently, L-Arginine serves as a precursor for collagen synthesis [14–16], and stimulates the release of growth factors such as IGF-1 [17, 18], supporting the high metabolic demands of differentiating bone cells [13, 19]. Consistent with previous reports, our findings suggest that L-arginine may promote osteogenic differentiation, as indicated by the upregulation of osteogenic markers such as Runx2, Opn, and Sp7 [13].

A key innovation of this study is the use of two distinct 3D spheroid co-culture models to dissect the cellular cross-talk inherent in the bone microenvironment. In the osteoblast-macrophage model, our transcriptomic analysis revealed that L-Arg@ZIF-8 modulates immune responses and bone metabolism pathways. Specifically, the upregulation of cytokine–cytokine receptor interactions suggests that L-Arg@ZIF-8 creates a pro-regenerative immune microenvironment. This aligns with recent studies showing L-Arginine’s ability to induce metabolic reprogramming in immune cells [20], However, unlike studies focusing solely on anti-inflammatory effects, our data indicates a balanced regulation of osteoclastogenesis-related genes (e.g., Rankl/Rank), which is vital for the coupling of bone resorption and formation during the remodeling phase.

Furthermore, in the osteoblast-endothelial model, we observed more extensive transcriptional remodeling, highlighting the critical role of L-Arg@ZIF-8 in promoting osteo-angiogenic coupling. The simultaneous upregulation of OCN and CD31 markers, alongside the enrichment of the PI3K-Akt and Wnt signaling pathways, confirms that L-Arg@ZIF-8 promotes endothelial activation and the upregulation of angiogenic markers [21–23]. This creates a favorable vascularized niche that is indispensable for bone regeneration. While other studies have focused on L-Arginine’s role in general wound healing, our work specifically elucidates its targeted effects on the bone-vascular axis, where osteogenesis and angiogenesis must be tightly coordinated.

It is worth noting that while standard protocols for endothelial cells typically utilize DMEM or specialized endothelial media, our study employed an osteogenic induction medium based on α-MEM to ensure robust osteoblastic differentiation. Although this may not represent the optimal proliferation condition for endothelial cells, our viability assays (Live/Dead) and functional markers (CD31 expression) confirmed that the co-cultured endothelial cells maintained good viability and phenotypic characteristics throughout the 14-day induction period.

Most significantly, the translation of these mechanistic insights into therapeutic efficacy was confirmed in a rat rib fracture model. Rib fractures present a unique clinical challenge requiring rapid stabilization to prevent pulmonary complications. Our in vivo data showed that L-Arg@ZIF-8 treatment significantly accelerated callus formation and mineral apposition rates (MAR). The elevated expression of OPN and OSX at the fracture site corroborates our in vitro findings, suggesting that the molecular pathways identified in our 3D models—specifically the upregulation of key osteogenic transcription factors and enhanced vascularization—are effectively operative in the physiological fracture environment.

Notably, the specific dosing regimen used in this study underscores the significant “dose-sparing advantage” of the L-Arg@ZIF-8 nanoplatform. Given the rapid systemic clearance of free L-arginine, traditional approaches often require daily high-dose systemic administration (e.g., > 100–300 mg/kg/day) [7] to sustain therapeutic levels. By comparison, our results demonstrate that L-Arg@ZIF-8 achieves potent osteogenic effects with a very low total dose (5 mg/kg) and reduced frequency (once every 3 days). Even on a total-mass basis, this represents a reduction of several orders of magnitude relative to conventional free-drug dosing. This high efficiency is primarily attributed to the sustained release and local retention of the ZIF-8 carrier at the fracture site, which maximizes therapeutic efficacy while minimizing potential systemic risks associated with high-dose systemic exposure.

Despite these encouraging findings, this study has several limitations. Although 3D spheroid models better recapitulate native tissue architecture than 2D cultures, they still lack the full systemic complexity of neuro-skeletal interactions. Moreover, while transcriptomic analysis enabled the identification of key signaling pathways, the precise direct molecular targets of L-Arg@ZIF-8 within the nucleus warrant further investigation. Furthermore, given that RNA-seq was performed on mixed co-culture spheroids, it remains challenging to definitively attribute the observed pathway alterations to specific cell types. For instance, the significant enrichment of immune-related pathways may primarily reflect transcriptional changes in macrophages, potentially masking osteoblast-specific responses. Therefore, future studies should employ single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to resolve these cell-type-specific mechanisms with greater precision. Additionally, to minimize experimental variability caused by hormonal fluctuations associated with the estrous cycle, we exclusively used male rats in our in vivo experiments. We acknowledge that this limits the direct translatability of our findings to postmenopausal osteoporosis, a condition predominantly affecting women. Future studies will therefore validate the efficacy of L-Arg@ZIF-8 in ovariectomized (OVX) rat models to better simulate clinical osteoporotic fractures. Finally, long-term biodegradation of ZIF-8 in the thoracic region and the therapeutic potential of this nanoplatform in pathological fracture models—such as those associated with osteoporosis or diabetes—also merit further exploration.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study establishes L-Arg@ZIF-8 as a potent therapeutic agent for bone fracture management. By concurrently optimizing the immune microenvironment and driving enhanced osteogenesis-angiogenesis coupling via PI3K-Akt and Wnt signaling, L-Arg@ZIF-8 offers a robust strategy to accelerate bone healing, holding significant potential for clinical translation in orthopedic trauma.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Zinc nitrate hexahydrate, 2-methylimidazole, methanol, and L-arginine were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Cell culture reagents, including α-MEM, DMEM, fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin/streptomycin, and Trypsin–EDTA, were obtained from Gibco (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Ultra-low attachment culture plates were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific.

For biological assays and staining, the Live/Dead staining kit, EdU incorporation assay kit, 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), DAPI, TRIzol reagent, Masson’s trichrome staining kit, and Toluidine blue were all acquired from Solarbio (Beijing, China). For animal experiments, Isoflurane was obtained from MedChemExpress (MCE, NJ, USA), while sodium pentobarbital, Calcein, and Alizarin Red S were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Primary antibodies used in this study included: anti-Osteocalcin (OCN; 1:400, Cat. No. 16157-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-CD31 (1:200, Cat. No. 11265-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-Osteopontin (OPN; 1:300, Cat. No. 22952-1-AP, Proteintech), and anti-Osterix (OSX; 1:300, Cat. No. sc-393325, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA).

Secondary antibodies included Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor™ 488 (Cat. No. A32731TR, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) used at 4 µg/mL. Additional secondary antibodies were obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK), including: Goat anti-Rabbit Alexa 555 (1:200, ab150078) or Alexa 647 (1:200, ab150079); Goat anti-Mouse Alexa 488 (1:300, ab150113) or Alexa 555 (1:200, ab150114); and Goat anti-Rat Alexa 647 (1:200, ab150167).

Synthesis and characterization of L-Arg@ZIF-8 nanoparticles

L-Arg@ZIF-8 nanoparticles were synthesized via a one-pot competitive coordination method. Briefly, zinc nitrate hexahydrate and 2-methylimidazole were dissolved in methanol, followed by the addition of L-arginine (L-Arg) solution. The mixture was stirred at room temperature to allow competitive coordination and crystal growth. The resulting precipitates were collected by centrifugation, washed with methanol to remove unreacted precursors, and dried under vacuum.

The morphology and size of the nanoparticles were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The crystal structure was analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD), and chemical functional groups were identified using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). The hydrodynamic diameter and Zeta potential were measured using a dynamic light scattering (DLS) analyzer to evaluate particle stability and surface charge.

Cell culture and maintenance

The murine osteoblast cell line (CP-M091) and murine macrophage cell line RAW264.7 were obtained from Procell. The murine endothelial cell line C166 was purchased from WHELAB. Osteoblasts were cultured in α-MEM, while RAW264.7 and C166 cells were maintained in DMEM. All culture media were supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 and passaged upon reaching 80–90% confluence. Cells from passages 3–10 were used for all experiments.

Establishment of 3D co-culture spheroids

Three-dimensional (3D) co-culture spheroids were generated using ultra-low attachment culture plates. Two distinct models were established: (1) Osteoblast-Macrophage spheroids (CP-M091 and RAW264.7) and (2) Osteoblast-Endothelial spheroids (CP-M091 and C166). Cells were seeded at a 1:1 ratio with a total density of 4 × 105 cells per well. Spheroids were allowed to self-assemble for 24 h prior to treatment. Subsequently, the spheroids were treated with PBS (control) or L-Arg@ZIF-8 nanoparticles suspended in osteogenic induction medium at a final concentration of 50 μg/mL. The medium was replenished every 3 days throughout the 14-day culture period. For osteogenic induction, α-MEM complete medium was supplemented with 5 mM β-glycerol phosphate (Cat. No. G9422, Sigma-Aldrich) and 50 μg/mL ascorbic acid (Cat. No. A800295, Macklin, Shanghai, China).

Assessment of viability, proliferation, and differentiation

Cell viability was evaluated on days 1, 7, and 14 using a Live/Dead staining kit (Calcein-AM/Ethidium homodimer-1) and visualized via fluorescence microscopy. Cell proliferation was assessed using a 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation assay according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For differentiation analysis, spheroids were harvested at day 14, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and subjected to immunofluorescence staining. Primary antibodies against Osteocalcin (OCN, osteogenic marker) and CD31 (endothelial marker) were used, followed by incubation with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software. Specifically, for the quantification of OCN and CD31 expression, the positive fluorescence area within each spheroid section was measured after applying a consistent threshold to distinguish specific signals from background. The relative fluorescence expression was then calculated by normalizing the mean positive area of each sample to that of the PBS control group, which was set as 1.0. Consequently, the values for the experimental groups represent the fold change relative to the control.

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

To evaluate osteogenic differentiation at the molecular level, total RNA was extracted from co-cultured cells using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of the isolated RNA were determined using a spectrophotometer. Subsequently, 1 μg of total RNA from each sample was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the HiScript All-in-One RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA Wiper) (Vazyme, China).

Quantitative real-time PCR was performed on a Bio-Rad CFX96 system using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China). The thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Specific primers for the osteogenic markers Sp7 (Osterix), Runx2, and Bglap (Osteocalcin), as well as the housekeeping gene Gapdh, were designed and synthesized The primer sequences used were as follows: for RUNX2, forward 5′-CCAACCGAGTCATTTAAGGCT-3′ and reverse 5′-GCTCACGTCGCTCATCTTG-3′; for SP7, forward 5′-AAGGTGTACGGCAAGGCTTC-3′ and reverse 5′-CGTCAGAGCGAGTGAACCTC-3′; and for BGLAP, forward 5′-CGCTACCTTGGAGCCTCAGT-3′ and reverse 5′-AGGCGGTCTTCAAGCCATAC-3′.

The relative mRNA expression levels of the osteogenic markers (Sp7, Runx2, and Bglap) were quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Ct values were normalized to the internal control gene (Gapdh), and the fold change was calculated relative to the PBS-treated control group. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Transcriptomic profiling and bioinformatics analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the co-culture spheroids (n = 3 per group) after 14 days of treatment using the TRIzol reagent. Following quality control, RNA libraries were constructed and sequenced on an Illumina platform. Raw data were filtered, and clean reads were aligned to the reference genome. Differential expression analysis was performed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the L-Arg@ZIF-8 and PBS groups (adjusted p value < 0.05). Functional enrichment analyses, including Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), were conducted to elucidate the molecular mechanisms governing osteogenesis, angiogenesis, and immune regulation.

Rat rib fracture model and treatment

Adult male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (8 weeks old, weighing 250–300 g) were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., and all procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Under general anesthesia using inhaled isoflurane (induction at 5%, maintenance at 2.5%), a standard transverse fracture was surgically created at the distinct rib shaft. Following surgery, the rats were randomly divided into three groups: PBS control, ZIF-8, and L-Arg@ZIF-8. Treatments were administered via local injection at the fracture site at a dosage of 5 mg/kg every 3 days until the endpoint. To evaluate dynamic bone formation, Calcein and Alizarin Red S were sequentially injected intraperitoneally prior to sacrifice. At the experimental endpoint, animals were euthanized via an overdose of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg), followed by cervical dislocation as a confirmatory measure. The rib samples were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and subjected to Micro-CT scanning (measuring BMD, BV/TV, and Tb.Th) and histological analysis.

Histological and immunofluorescence analysis

Rib samples fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde were decalcified in 10% EDTA (pH 7.4) for 4 weeks at room temperature. The decalcified samples were then dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 5 µm. Histological healing was assessed using Masson’s trichrome and Toluidine blue staining. For molecular evaluation, sections underwent immunofluorescence staining for Osteopontin (OPN) and Osterix (OSX). Images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM). Quantitative analysis was performed to determine the number of osteoblasts per bone perimeter (N.Ob/B.Pm) and the relative expression levels of osteogenic markers.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. Prior to hypothesis testing, the normality of the data distribution was verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Differences between two groups were analyzed using an unpaired Student’s t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
