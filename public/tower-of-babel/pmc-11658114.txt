Exploring the prevalence and symptom presentation of food addiction among Norwegian bariatric surgery patients: associations with depression, dysregulated eating, and postoperative weight loss
Authors: Gro Walø-Syversen, Jon Kristinsson, Inger L Eribe, Øyvind Rø, Camilla Lindvall Dahlgren
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPathological eating and addictive processes are linked to obesity. Food addiction (FA) involves hedonic eating of highly palatable foods, accompanied by addictive symptoms like craving, loss-of-control (LOC) eating, and withdrawal. The main objectives of this study were to assess FA prevalence and symptoms in bariatric surgery candidates, and its relationship with depression, dysregulated eating, and 1- year postoperative weight loss (WL).MethodsData from 69 bariatric surgery patients (74% female, 26% male, ) were analysed. Self-report measures including Yale Food Addiction Scale (YFAS) 2.0, Eating Disorder Examination-Questionnaire (EDE-Q), Repetitive Eating Questionnaire [Rep(eat)-Q] and Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) were administered pre-surgery and at 1-year follow-up.ResultsThe mean preoperative YFAS symptom score was 2.2. (SD = 2.59). 16% of the sample met YFAS diagnostic criteria for FA. Top reported FA criteria were “substance taken in larger amount than intended” (33%), “persisted desire or repeated unsuccessful attempts to quit” (29%), and “use in physically hazardous situations” (23%). The YFAS symptom scores correlated significantly with the frequency of LOC binge eating, depression and repetitive eating scores, but showed no correlation with age or BMI. Preoperative YFAS symptom scores did not significantly predict % WL at 1-year follow-up.ConclusionsOur findings align with previous research, indicating that preoperative FA symptoms is unrelated to preoperative BMI and total %WL, but linked to eating pathology, such as LOC binge eating and grazing, as well as depression in bariatric surgery candidates.Keywords: Food addiction, Bariatric surgery, Grazing, Binge eating, Postoperative weight loss

Background

Pathological eating behaviors and addictive processes have been implicated in the multifactorial development and maintenance of obesity [1]. Food addiction (FA), is defined as hedonic eating behaviors that include the consumption of highly palatable foods [2], and that are associated with addictive psychological and behavioural symptoms such as cravings, altered tolerance, loss-of-control food consumption and withdrawal [3]. Although the concept of FA is widely debated [2, 4], emerging literature has identified some potential points of consensus, e.g., that addictive-like eating patterns do exist, and that mechanisms contributing to substance-related and addictive disorders, e.g. alcohol and substance use disorders are also implicated in excessive eating and obesity [2, 5]. The “food addiction model” for obesity posit that intake of highly processed sugary and fatty food elicits addictive-like processes both at a behavioural and neurobiological level leading to the excessive consumption of food [6–11]. These processes involves alterations in the regulation of food intake at the neurobiological level, where there is a shift from primary control by the normal appetite regulation system to food intake characterized by quantities exceeding homeostatic energy needs, driven by a dysregulated reward system [10].

Highly palatable foods may have addictive qualities that can lead to pathological eating behaviors [6]. A number of studies have shown that FA, operationalized and assessed by various versions of Yale Food Addiction Scale (YFAS) [12, 13] is associated with various forms of eating pathology including dietary restraint, binge eating (consumption of large quantities of food in a short period of time, commonly accompanied by a sense of loss of control over eating), grazing (repetitive and unplanned eating of small amounts of food) [14–17] as well clinical eating disorders (EDs) such as binge eating disorder (BED) [4]) and bulimia nervosa (BN) [18]. Relatedly, studies have also found that FA is related to impulsivity [14, 19, 20], a core feature of loss of control (LOC) binge eating. Overall, recent reviews suggest that FA is related to both anxiety and affective disorders [21–24], poorer quality of life and self-esteem [23]. Further, in bariatric surgery samples specifically, studies have reported that mental health disorders such as depressive, anxiety disorders [25] as well as bipolar disorder [26] are connected to FA.

It is well established that FA, assessed using YFAS, is more prevalent in clinical eating and weight disorder samples, compared to individuals from the general community [27]. A growing number of studies also show that FA is common among bariatric surgery patients [27]. However, reported rates from bariatric surgery samples vary considerably, with a recent meta-analysis compiling data from 272 studies worldwide reporting prevalence rates ranging from 14 to 58% [28]. Most published studies have been conducted in the US, with only 4.0% (11 out of 272) studies in the meta-analysis from Praxedes et al. [28] originating from Northern Europe. Even fewer studies, comprising 0.7% (2 studies) were conducted in the Nordic countries (i.e., Norway, Denmark, Sweden, Finland and Iceland).

In bariatric surgery patients, FA has been shown to be unrelated to preoperative weight, and postoperative weight loss [29, 30]. Moreover, it is uncertain whether FA contributes to weight regain following surgery. A recent meta-analysis [28] revealed a pooled prevalence of FA in bariatric surgery patients of 28%. Preoperative weighted prevalence was 32%, while postoperative prevalence was 15% suggesting a decrease in FA prevalence among those who undergo bariatric surgery. Surgery also appears to correlate with a reduction in self-reported FA [27, 29], yet preoperative FA seems to have little impact on weight outcomes after surgery, both in the short [31] and long term [30]. However, both pre- and post-surgery FA is linked to elevated levels of ED psychopathology, depression, and reduced health related quality of life [27, 30, 32–35].

Given that variations in FA prevalence have been documented across different cultural settings in both clinical [30] and non-clinical populations [36], and considering potential cultural diversity in dietary habits, dietary advice and the bariatric treatment context, combined with the scarcity of studies on the prevalence and correlates of FA in bariatric surgery patients in Northern Europe, particularly in the Nordic countries, it is important to investigate whether existing results can be replicated. Additionally, further research is necessary to confirm its association with post-surgery weight loss and problematic eating behaviors such as grazing and binge eating, along with well-established comorbid conditions like depression. The aims of this study were to assess the prevalence and symptom presentation of preoperative FA in Norwegian bariatric surgery candidates, and to explore its relationship with preoperative depression and dysregulated eating behaviors such as repetitive eating and binge eating. Moreover, we aimed to investigate the association between preoperative FA and post-operative weight loss, as well as differences between patients exhibiting clinical and non-clinical levels of preoperative FA.

Methods

Procedure and sample

The study is based on a subset of data from a prospective, observational study investigating cognitive function in bariatric surgery patients over a 2-year follow-up period, the Oslo Bariatric Surgery Study Cognitive (OBSS-C) [37]. The study recruitment and data collection process are previously reported in Walø-Syversen [38]. Participants (N = 69) in this study includes 51 female (73.9%) and 18 male (26.1%) patients from the Oslo Bariatric Surgery Study (OBSS) Cognitive [39–41]. Patients were recruited from the Centre for Morbid Obesity and Bariatric Surgery (CMOBS) at Oslo University Hospital. In line with international treatment, Norwegian guidelines, and the treatment program at CMOBS, commonly require either severe obesity (BMI ≥ 40), or a BMI of 35 or higher in combination with comorbidity, to be eligible for bariatric surgery. Also, a history of failed non-surgical weight loss attempts is required for surgery approval. Patients are evaluated for suitability by a multidisciplinary team. Due to the potential of reduced adherence, surgery is commonly not advised for individuals with alcohol- and drug-related disorders, severe cognitive deficits, or serious mental illness. All candidates for bariatric surgery are required to complete a mandatory preoperative program that outlines comprehensive treatment strategies for severe obesity, including modifications in diet, exercise, and lifestyle [42]. To minimize the risk of surgical complications, patients are encouraged to achieve a 5–10% weight loss prior to surgery. Starting approximately one month before the procedure, patients must follow a low-calorie diet. Postoperative care recommendations include adherence to the Norwegian national guidelines for physical activity and nutrition, alongside the use of individually prescribed vitamin and mineral supplements. Annual follow-up consultations are conducted for up to five years after surgery.

Exclusion criteria for participation in the OBSS Cognitive study [39–41] was the inability to read, speak, or comprehend Norwegian language, a history of neurological disorder (e.g., dementia or stroke), moderate/severe head injury, past/present history of serious mental illness, past/current alcohol or drug abuse/dependence, history of learning disorder, developmental disability, and impaired sensorimotor function. These criteria partly overlap with the suitability for undergoing surgery.

The recruitment procedure took place within the regular treatment chain at the CMOBS. All patients referred to the treatment centre received oral information about the study. This information was provided at two mandatory information meetings, which were a routine part of the treatment process. The recruitment period ran from June 2016 to May 2017. During a clinical consultation with a surgeon, patients were asked if they were interested in participating in the study and given final notice of being selected for surgery. Surgeons assessed study eligibility based on the exclusion criteria. The study assessments were scheduled 30 days (+/- 5 days) before operation and 1 year (+/- 2 weeks) after surgery. Self-report measures were completed at home prior to the assessments. 69 of 80 patients completed the self-report questionnaires pre-surgery and were included in the study. One-year follow-up assessments were scheduled to occur simultaneously to planned visits at the hospital. Due to attrition only 57 participants completed post-surgery questionnaires at 1 year follow-up. Sample characteristics are reported in Table 1.

Assessment

Weight. Weight was measured on a platform scale SECA 635, III. Participants were lightly dressed and without shoes. Height was measured by an adjustable scale (Soehne Professional) attached to a wall and calibrated. Percentage WL (%WL) was used as weight outcome measure. 1-year %WL was defined as baseline weight minus the weight at 1-year follow-up, divided by the baseline weight, and multiplied by 100.

The Yale Food Addiction Scale (YFAS) 2.0 [13]. The YFAS 2.0 is the most frequently used measure of FA, operationalizing food addiction according to the DSM-V criteria for substance use disorders. The 35-item YFAS 2.0 has two scoring alternatives: a symptom count reflecting the number of endorsed addiction-like criteria (0–11) (e.g. continued use despite adverse consequences, loss of control, withdrawal etc.), and a dichotomous “FA diagnosis” requiring the endorsement of three or more symptoms, in addition to a clinically significant impairment criterion. Both the symptom count, and the dichotomous measures are reported in the current study. The YFAS 2.0 has good internal consistency, convergent, discriminant, and incremental validity [13]. It has been validated in bariatric surgery patients [42, 43] and has several translations [44–48] including a Norwegian version [49]. Cronbach`s alpha for YFAS in the present study was 0.82.

The Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE-Q) [50, 51]. Three items from the EDE-Q were administered and used to assess preoperative binge eating pathology; “Over the past 4 weeks (28 days), how many times have you eaten what other people would regards as an unusually large amount of food given the circumstances?”, “On how many of these times did you have a sense of having lost control over your eating at the time you were eating?” and “Over the past 4 weeks (28 days), on how many days have such episodes of overeating occurred (i.e. you have eaten an unusually large amount of food and have had a sense of loss of control at the time)?”. The item used to indicate binge eating in the current study was item 14, which assesses the frequency (number of episodes) of subjective loss-of-control (LOC) during binge eating the past 28 days. The EDE-Q has shown acceptable psychometric properties both in its original English version [52] and Norwegian translation [51].

Repetitive Eating Questionnaire [Rep(eat)-Q] [53]. The Rep(eat)-Q was used to assess the frequency of grazing (repetitive and unplanned eating of small amounts of food) behaviors. Participants rate their grazing behaviors the preceding 28 days on a 7-point Likert-scale ranging from never (0) to more than once every day (6). The questionnaire has a total score averaging all 12 items and two subscale scores, compulsive grazing (CG) (6 items) and repetitive eating (RE) (6 items). Higher scores indicate a higher frequency of grazing behaviors. A Norwegian translation has shown excellent psychometric properties and a two-factor model which confirmed the original solution [49]. The Cronbach`s alpha for the total scale was 0.93.

Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) [54]. HADS is a 14-item self-report questionnaire measuring symptoms of anxiety and depression using three subscale scores: a total scale score (14 items), an anxiety scale score (7 items) and a depression scale score (7 items). All items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale, and responses range from 3 (“yes, definitely”) to 0 (“no, not at all”). Scale scores are summarized where higher scores indicate higher symptom. The total scale score ranges from 0 to 42, whereas anxiety and depression scale scores range from 0 to 21. For this study, only the depression subscale was used, where a score of ≤ 7 was interpreted as non-clinical score. Depression scores ≥ 11 indicated the presence of depressive symptoms. The HADS has shown consistent and good psychometrically properties [55], and Cronbach`s alphas for the total scale, the depression sub scale and the anxiety sub scale were 0.85,0.76 and 0.85, respectively.

Research ethics

The OBSS Cognitive study was approved by the Regional Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics of South-Eastern Norway (ref. 2015/4012) and the Privacy Ombudsman for Research at Oslo University Hospital. All participants received oral and written information about the study. Informed consent was obtained for all study participants.

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the sample and to investigate food addiction, repetitive eating, binge eating and depression. Histograms and Quantile-Quantile Plots were used to check all continuous variables for normality. The variable binge eating frequency was inversely transformed due to severe skewness, original values are reported due to ease of interpretation. Bivariate correlations (Pearson`s r) were conducted to investigate the relationship between food addiction symptoms and age, BMI, repetitive eating, binge eating and depressive symptoms. Independent samples t-test were performed to compare group of individuals with and without a clinical level of food addiction (i.e. endorsement of the impairment criterion). A hierarchical multiple regression analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between food addiction symptom level and 1-year %WL, while controlling for gender, age, and postoperative BMI. Cohen’s d was used as effect size measure (0.2 = small, 0.5 = medium and 0.8 = large) [56]. A significance level of p ≤ .05 was used. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 for Windows.

Results

Sample characteristics are presented in Table 1. Analyses of preoperative data showed that 16% (n = 11) of participants met criteria for FA, i.e., they exceeded the clinical threshold. The mean FA symptom score was 2.2 (SD = 2.59). For the overall sample, the three most endorsed symptoms were “substance taken in larger amount than intended” (33%), “persisted desire or repeated unsuccessful attempts to quit” (29%) and “use in physically hazardous situations” (23%) (see Table 2 for the frequency of all FA symptoms). At 1 year follow-up, 7% of participants met criteria for FA. None of these participants had reported preoperative FA, and as such, all cases of postoperative FA were de novo (4 individuals). The mean YFAS symptom score at 1 year follow-up was 0.68 (SD = 0.18).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association between preoperative FA and grazing behaviors in a bariatric sample assessed using the Rep[eat]-Q [49], and the first study to assess the prevalence and of pre- and post-bariatric surgery FA in a Norwegian sample. Our analyses revealed several interesting findings, which will be discussed below.

In our study, the prevalence of clinical FA among bariatric surgery candidates was found to be 16%. In the preoperative assessment using the YFAS, the three most prevalent criteria for FA among bariatric surgery candidates were: (1) consumption of the substance in greater amounts than intended, as reported by 33% of individuals; (2) a persistent desire or repeated unsuccessful attempts to control use, cited by 29%; and (3) usage in physically hazardous situations, noted by 23% of the sample. A preoperative FA-prevalence of 16% is on the lower end of the spectrum compared to the range of 14–58% reported in the systematic review by Ivezaj et al. [30]. Furthermore, a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of 40 studies [28] indicated an average preoperative FA prevalence of 32%, while the postoperative prevalence was 15%, implying a decrease in FA following bariatric surgery. Notably, the pre-surgery 16% prevalence in our cohort is substantially below these figures. Cultural factors or aspects specific to Norway may have influenced FA rates in this sample, as variations in FA prevalence have been noted across different cultural settings in both clinical [30] and non-clinical populations [36]. In Norway, a previous study found a marginal shift toward healthier dietary patterns, with increased purchases of fruits, berries and vegetables, and decreased sales of sweet, ultra-processed foods between 2005 and 2013, reflecting national dietary guidelines [57]. Furthermore, in Oslo, where the study sample was primarily recruited, food sales shifted toward less processed options [57], which could potentially help explain our findings.

The total post-operative prevalence of FA was 7%, which is in line with previous studies reporting postoperative FA prevalence [28]. However, participants who reported preoperative FA in the current study did not report postoperative FA. As complete remission was achieved for these individuals at one year, the postoperative prevalence rate reflects the emergence of new cases of FA within the sample. Due to the small sample size, we can only speculate why those who exhibited FA pre-surgery did not show FA post-surgery, as well as the factors contributing to the three reported de novo FA cases. It is possible that maintaining FA behaviors post-surgery is challenging due to physiological changes, such as a reduced stomach pouch and the severe consequences of deviating from post-surgical guidelines regarding portion sizes and food choices. Like the hypothesis that preoperative BED may transition into postoperative grazing [58], it is conceivable that preoperative FA may evolve into postoperative grazing. Indeed, grazing tends to increase following surgery [59] which is concerning given its association with weight regain and poorer adherence to postoperative dietary guidelines [58, 60–62].This may help explain the emergence of new FA cases after surgery but does not fully account for the de novo cases. De novo cases may be a result nutritional deficiency [63] causing excessive hunger and addictive-like eating, or problems adhering to strict dietary guidelines leading to craving cycles and subsequent loss of control of eating. Further studies are needed to explore this in more detail.

Corroborating prior research [30], our findings indicate that preoperative FA diagnosis is not linked to preoperative BMI, nor is preoperative FA symptoms a significant predictor of weight loss one year after surgery. The significant association between FA, binge eating, repetitive eating and compulsive grazing observed in the current study is in line with previous research showing considerable overlap between eating pathology, FA, and depression [21, 32, 42]. The established bidirectional association between eating pathology and depression suggests that each may be a risk factor for the other [64]. In line with this and findings from clinical samples of individuals with BED and obesity [4] our findings indicate a robust correlation between higher FA scores and increased levels of depression within the current sample. However, it is interesting to note that LOC eating, when evaluated as an isolated factor through one specific EDE-Q item, was not associated with depression. Further investigation is needed to understand whether other factors may be influencing the absence of association between LOC eating alone and levels of depression. We hypothesize that the relationship between FA, depression and other indicators of eating pathology, specifically repetitive eating in our study, may collectively point to the presence of a vulnerable subgroup of patients at risk of suboptimal weight loss. However, limitations in our study design and sample size precluded any further analysis of this hypothesis.

In the preoperative comparison between participants with clinical and non-clinical FA scores, those with clinical FA exhibited a significantly higher frequency of LOC binge eating episodes, greater depressive symptoms, and higher total repetitive eating scores. Those with clinical FA scores pre-surgery may be more susceptible to developing pronounced grazing behaviors following bariatric surgery, which, coupled with depression, could complicate weight management efforts. Thus, it seems important that treatment providers are vigilant for the emergence of other forms of addictive-like eating behaviors in the post-operative treatment follow-up phase, as well as for the emergence of new FA cases following surgery. The considerable overlap between FA and BED symptoms [21, 65], which also was indicated in the current study, calls for more and nuanced research, as each condition may require different treatment strategies tailored to their distinct characteristics. Further, a more comprehensive understanding of the role of FA in bariatric surgery, requires studies with larger and more gender-balanced samples than the current study.

The present study also had some limitations. The single-site data collection, convenience sampling, and small sample size likely introduced selection bias and reduced statistical power, weakening the generalizability of the findings. Due to the limited sample size and potential risk of Type II error, we did not perform an overall adjustment of the significance level based on the number of tests conducted, which may have increased the risk of Type I error. Additionally, the reduced sample size necessitated the exclusion of certain predictors of weight loss suggested by the literature, such as surgery type [66] and comorbidity [67] thereby weakening the hierarchical multiple regression model. Furthermore, a more conservative non-parametric test of group differences by FA diagnoses may have been used, thus reducing the risk of Type 1 error. Also, our study had a 26% attrition rate, which may limit the generalizability of findings. It is plausible that social desirability has influenced FA rates in the current sample. In clinical care, bariatric surgery candidates may under-report eating problems and pathology in fear of being deemed ineligible for surgery [68, 69]. Social desirability bias might also influence postoperative findings [69], when considering the importance placed upon adhering to the post-operative treatment guidelines. Although self-report assessments were independent of the pre- and preoperative follow up consultations, data collection took place out at the clinical center, which might have increased patient concerns and unwillingness to disclose certain information.

Conclusions

Our findings align with previous research, indicating that preoperative FA symptoms is unrelated to preoperative BMI and total %WL, but linked to eating pathology, such as LOC binge eating and grazing, as well as depression in bariatric surgery candidates. Additional studies in Northern Europe are needed to investigate whether our findings can be replicated.
