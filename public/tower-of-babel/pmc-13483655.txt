Physical activity, sedentary behaviour, and anthropometric profiles in women with and without insulin resistance: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Katarzyna Wiśniewska-Ślewicz, Karolina Żak-Kowalska, Sebastian Kowalski, Adrian Moskal, Emilia Tęcza, Amir Khorram-Manesh, Ahmed M Al-Wathinani, Mariusz Goniewicz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInsulin resistance (IR) is a multifactorial metabolic condition influenced by lifestyle, body composition, and behavioral factors. Regular physical activity is known to improve insulin sensitivity, yet its associations with anthropometric and socio-demographic variables remain incompletely understood, particularly among young women.MethodsA total of 443 women aged 18–35 years participated in this cross-sectional study, including 301 with medically confirmed IR and 142 metabolically healthy controls. Anthropometric indices, Body Mass Index (BMI), Waist–Hip Ratio (WHR), Abdominal Volume Index (AVI), and Body Adiposity Index (BAI), were self-measured using standardized procedures. Physical activity was assessed via the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ), with total energy expenditure expressed as Metabolic Equivalent of Task (MET). Group comparisons were performed using Mann–Whitney U tests and Generalized Linear Models (GENLIN) with robust covariance estimation.ResultsWomen with IR exhibited significantly higher BMI, WHR, AVI, and BAI values (all p < 0.001) and lower total physical activity (2,615 vs. 3,679 MET; p = 0.01) compared with controls. Sedentary behavior was more prevalent among IR participants (449 vs. 363 MET; p < 0.001). The observed differences were independent of anthropometric covariates. Socio-demographic factors showed nonlinear associations: lower and higher income levels, as well as rural residence, were linked to reduced activity in the IR group. Sleep duration showed no significant effect.ConclusionsPhysical activity, independent of body composition, is a key determinant of metabolic health in women. The results highlight the need for targeted behavioral interventions promoting movement as a core therapeutic and preventive strategy in insulin resistance management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04575-z.Keywords: Insulin resistance, Physical activity, Women’s health, Anthropometry, Metabolic syndrome, GPAQ, Sedentary behavior

Introduction

Physical activity, alongside a balanced diet, constitutes the foundation of a healthy lifestyle and remains essential for maintaining overall health and well-being. In contemporary society, an epidemic of so-called lifestyle diseases, such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes, obesity and overweight, depression, and disorders of lipid and carbohydrate metabolism, has become a major public health challenge. These conditions may ultimately lead to the development of metabolic syndrome. In addition to central (abdominal) obesity, insulin resistance (IR) is regarded as a key determinant in the pathogenesis of metabolic syndrome.

IR is a metabolic condition characterized by a reduced biological response of peripheral tissues, primarily skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, and the liver, to the action of insulin, resulting in impaired glucose uptake and utilization [1]. In the early stages, this diminished insulin sensitivity is often compensated by increased insulin secretion, leading to hyperinsulinemia, which may remain clinically silent for years. Over time, persistent insulin resistance contributes to disturbances in glucose and lipid metabolism, promotes visceral fat accumulation, and substantially increases the risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, and other components of the metabolic syndrome.

Clinically, insulin resistance is most commonly identified using biochemical markers such as fasting insulin and glucose concentrations, the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), and dynamic tests including the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) with concurrent insulin measurements [2]. In the present study, insulin resistance was defined based on a prior medical diagnosis established by physicians using these routinely applied clinical and laboratory criteria. This approach reflects real-world clinical practice and has been widely used in epidemiological and observational research focusing on lifestyle-related determinants of insulin resistance [3, 4].

The development of insulin resistance is multifactorial and influenced by both non-modifiable and modifiable factors [5]. Genetic predisposition plays an important role in individual susceptibility to insulin resistance, affecting insulin signalling pathways, adipose tissue distribution, and metabolic flexibility. In parallel, dietary factors, including macronutrient composition, excessive caloric intake, dietary patterns rich in refined carbohydrates and saturated fats, and high consumption of ultra-processed foods, have been consistently linked to impaired insulin sensitivity and metabolic dysregulation [6].

Although genetic background and diet constitute fundamental determinants of insulin resistance, they are less amenable to rapid modification at the population level. In contrast, lifestyle behaviours such as physical activity and sedentary behaviour represent modifiable factors with substantial preventive and therapeutic potential [7]. Therefore, the present study focuses specifically on the role of physical activity and sedentary behaviour in women with and without insulin resistance, while acknowledging the broader genetic and dietary context of this condition.

Obesity and insulin resistance are closely interconnected, yet they do not represent fully overlapping conditions [8]. Excess adiposity, particularly visceral fat accumulation, is strongly associated with impaired insulin signalling through mechanisms involving chronic low-grade inflammation, altered adipokine secretion, lipotoxicity, and increased free fatty acid flux to insulin-sensitive tissues [8]. Consequently, central obesity has been identified as a key metabolic risk factor, often more strongly linked to insulin resistance than overall body mass alone.

Importantly, metabolic risk varies substantially across individuals with similar levels of adiposity [9]. Distinct metabolic phenotypes have been described, including individuals with obesity but preserved insulin sensitivity, as well as normal-weight individuals presenting with insulin resistance. For this reason, anthropometric indices reflecting both total and central adiposity, such as body mass index (BMI), waist–hip ratio (WHR), abdominal volume index (AVI), and body adiposity index (BAI), are frequently used in epidemiological studies to better characterize obesity-related metabolic risk. However, anthropometric status alone does not fully explain variability in insulin resistance, highlighting the importance of behavioural and lifestyle factors, including physical activity, in modulating metabolic health [10].

Insulin resistance is of particular clinical relevance in women, as its prevalence and metabolic consequences vary across the female life course and are strongly influenced by hormonal and reproductive factors [11]. Periods characterized by profound endocrine changes, such as puberty, pregnancy, the postpartum period, and the menopausal transition, are associated with transient or persistent reductions in insulin sensitivity [11]. Moreover, conditions specific to women, most notably polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), are strongly linked to insulin resistance, even in the absence of overt obesity.

Sex-specific differences in insulin sensitivity are partly mediated by fluctuations in estrogen and progesterone levels, which influence substrate utilization, adipose tissue distribution, and skeletal muscle metabolism [12]. Women of reproductive age therefore represent a particularly relevant population for investigating behavioural determinants of insulin resistance, as metabolic dysregulation during this period may have long-term consequences for cardiometabolic health, fertility, and pregnancy outcomes [12]. These considerations provide a strong rationale for focusing the present study on women and for examining physical activity and sedentary behaviour as potentially modifiable factors influencing insulin resistance in this population.

The beneficial effects of physical exercise, across a spectrum of intensities, on both physical and mental health are well established [13, 14]. Resistance training, in particular, has been shown to influence lipid metabolism by altering the mechanisms through which stored energy is mobilized [15]. Moreover, exercise affects insulin and glucose levels by suppressing the secretion of the former. As energy expenditure increases, insulin’s storage-promoting effect is inhibited, favoring the utilization of available energy substrates [16]. A growing body of evidence demonstrates that regular physical activity enhances tissue sensitivity to insulin, especially within skeletal muscles [17].

Despite extensive evidence supporting the metabolic benefits of physical activity, important gaps remain regarding its role in insulin resistance independent of body composition, particularly among women of reproductive age. While regular physical activity is known to enhance insulin sensitivity, reduce visceral adiposity, and improve glucose regulation, sedentary behaviour has emerged as an independent risk factor for metabolic dysfunction, even among individuals who meet general physical activity recommendations [18].

Importantly, women with insulin resistance may differ from metabolically healthy women not only in anthropometric characteristics, but also in behavioural patterns related to daily movement and sedentary time [19]. However, the extent to which physical activity and sedentary behaviour distinguish women with insulin resistance from those without metabolic impairment, independently of adiposity, remains insufficiently explored.

Therefore, the present study aims to examine differences in physical activity and sedentary behaviour between women with clinically diagnosed insulin resistance and metabolically healthy controls, and to assess how these behaviours relate to anthropometric indices and selected socio-demographic factors. By directly comparing women with and without insulin resistance, this study seeks to clarify the role of physical activity as a key modifiable behavioural determinant of metabolic health in women.

Physical activity encompasses a broad spectrum of behaviours differing in type, intensity, and physiological demands. It includes aerobic activities, resistance-based exercise, and mixed forms, and is commonly classified according to intensity as moderate or vigorous. Current international guidelines recommend that adults engage in at least 150–300 min of moderate-intensity or 75–150 min of vigorous-intensity physical activity per week, with additional benefits observed at higher volumes [20]. Importantly, physical activity prescriptions should be individualized, taking into account health status, metabolic risk, and functional capacity.

Physical activity can be quantified using objective methods, such as accelerometers and wearable sensors, or subjective approaches, including validated self-report questionnaires. While objective measures provide precise estimates of movement patterns, they are resource-intensive and may not fully capture all domains of habitual activity. In large-scale observational studies, standardized questionnaires remain a widely accepted and practical approach for assessing physical activity across diverse populations. These considerations provide the conceptual framework for the assessment of physical activity adopted in the present study.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This study was designed as a cross-sectional observational study conducted using an online questionnaire. To investigate the relationship between physical activity and insulin resistance, as well as to assess how the level of physical activity influences anthropometric parameters, a cohort of women was recruited comprising those with medically confirmed insulin resistance and healthy controls without any chronic diseases. The total study population consisted of 443 women. Both groups were carefully selected using a standardized screening questionnaire. For the control group, special emphasis was placed on confirming the absence of chronic conditions, including metabolic disorders. The inclusion of both the study and control groups was intentional to enable the identification of potential differences in the amount of physical activity and to determine whether these differences translated into distinct anthropometric outcomes.

Recruitment and sampling procedure

Participants were recruited through an online questionnaire distributed via Google Forms. The survey link was shared on Facebook within disease-specific support groups to reach women with a diagnosis of insulin resistance, while the control group was recruited from general, non-health-related community groups unconnected with dietary or physical activity topics. The questionnaire contained conditional logic and filtering mechanisms to ensure that only eligible participants were included.

Key inclusion criteria were age between 18 and 35 years, relevant medical history, and not being pregnant at the time of the study. The survey also incorporated an informed consent form and provided detailed information regarding study duration and procedures. Data collection was conducted between November 2021 and October 2022.

Prior to accessing the questionnaire, participants were presented with detailed study information and were required to provide informed consent by actively selecting a mandatory “I agree to participate” option. Submission of the questionnaire was technically impossible without this confirmation, thereby ensuring explicit participant agreement in accordance with ethical standards for online research.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Women were included in the study group if they had a medically verified diagnosis of insulin resistance based on blood tests, including fasting glucose and insulin levels, HOMA-IR index, and an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) with insulin measurements. The control group consisted of women verified as healthy, without any diagnosed chronic diseases or insulin resistance, confirmed by an absence of symptoms or complaints, normal results of recent laboratory tests, and self-reported good health. Due to the stringent requirement of complete absence of chronic conditions, a considerable number of potential participants were excluded from the control group, resulting in a smaller but metabolically well-defined reference population.

Women aged 18–35 years were recruited via an online questionnaire distributed through Facebook between March and September 2023. After eligibility screening and electronic informed consent, participants were allocated to either the insulin resistance group or the metabolically healthy control group based on predefined clinical criteria.

Anthropometric and physical activity assessment

Anthropometric variables included body weight, waist circumference, hip circumference, BMI, WHR, AVI, and BAI. These indicators were calculated according to the following established formulas: BMI = body weight (kg) / height (m)²; WHR = waist circumference (cm) / hip circumference (cm); AVI = (2 × waist² [cm] + 0.7 × (waist – hip)² [cm]) / 1000; and BAI = (hip circumference [cm] / height [m]^1.5) – 18. Anthropometric indices such as BMI, WHR, AVI, and BAI are recognized as strong correlates of metabolic health and are widely used for evaluating obesity, metabolic syndrome, and insulin resistance, as supported by recent evidence.

To ensure accuracy and reliability, participants were provided with detailed written instructions for self-measurement. The guidance described precise techniques for measuring body weight, height, and waist and hip circumferences under standardized conditions. This approach allowed for the collection of reliable anthropometric data despite the remote, self-reported design of the study.

Physical activity was evaluated using the GPAQ, developed by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 2002 as part of its international STEPS program for the surveillance of noncommunicable disease risk factors. The GPAQ is an internationally validated instrument developed by the World Health Organization, with demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity across diverse adult populations, including European populations [21, 22]. The questionnaire has been widely applied in epidemiological studies conducted in Poland and comparable Central and Eastern European countries, supporting its suitability for assessing physical activity and sedentary behaviour in the population examined in the present study.

The GPAQ comprises nineteen questions assessing activity in three domains, occupational, transportation, and leisure-time, together with sedentary behavior during a typical week. Participants reported the frequency and duration of moderate and vigorous physical activities lasting at least ten consecutive minutes.

Moderate and vigorous activity levels were defined in accordance with the GPAQ protocol to facilitate accurate self-classification. Transportation-related movement was categorized as moderate activity. Each domain was assigned a Metabolic Equivalent of Task (MET) value representing energy expenditure relative to resting oxygen consumption. One MET corresponds to approximately 3.5 mL of oxygen per kilogram of body weight per minute, equivalent to about 1 kcal/kg/hour at rest.

Following WHO guidelines, moderate activity was assigned a value of 4 METs and vigorous activity 8 METs. Weekly energy expenditure was then computed by multiplying the MET value by the number of minutes and days of activity performed within each domain. Sedentary behavior (sitting or lying not related to sleep) was calculated as: minutes × 1 MET × number of days. Moderate activity was expressed as: [work (minutes × 4 MET × days)] + [transportation (minutes × 4 MET × days)] + [recreation (minutes × 4 MET × days)], while vigorous activity was estimated as: [work (minutes × 8 MET × days)] + [recreation (minutes × 8 MET × days)]. The total weekly physical activity level (MET total) was obtained by summing the values for moderate and vigorous activities.

All anthropometric data were self-reported by participants, which may be associated with a risk of measurement bias, including potential underestimation or overestimation of body weight and circumferences. To minimize this risk, participants received detailed written instructions describing standardized measurement procedures, including measurement conditions, body positioning, and the use of non-elastic measuring tapes. Participants who were unable to provide complete anthropometric data were not able to finalize questionnaire submission due to mandatory-response logic and were therefore excluded from the analysis.

Previous research has demonstrated that self-reported anthropometric measurements, while less precise than measurements obtained in clinical or nutritional settings, show acceptable validity and reliability in large-scale epidemiological and observational studies, particularly when standardized instructions are provided. Nevertheless, the limitations associated with self-reported data are acknowledged and considered when interpreting the results of the present study.

Handling of missing data and controlled variables

To minimize missing data, the survey was designed with mandatory response logic, progression through subsequent sections and final submission were possible only after all questions were answered. The online form automatically flagged unanswered items, prompting completion before proceeding. Participants who skipped any question were not able to finalize their participation, ensuring complete datasets for all included cases.

Methodological rationale and confounder adjustment

The target group was intentionally restricted to women aged 18–35 years, representing a critical stage in reproductive and metabolic health. This age range corresponds to the period when women most frequently undergo diagnostic evaluations for hormonal and metabolic disorders, including insulin resistance, which is also known to affect fertility [23]. Women in this demographic are generally free from degenerative musculoskeletal conditions that could confound physical activity assessment and are typically active in the workforce, making them an appropriate population for examining lifestyle factors.

The inclusion of women with medically verified IR, based on recent laboratory tests (fasting glucose, insulin, HOMA-IR, OGTT), and metabolically healthy controls was fundamental to ensure validity. The decision to conduct the study online was deliberate, it allowed wide outreach to both groups and eliminated the high rate of incomplete responses commonly observed in paper-based questionnaires.

The reliability of self-reported anthropometric data was strengthened by providing highly detailed measurement instructions and standardized conditions for all respondents. Physical activity comparisons between the study and control groups were based on the GPAQ, a validated tool for quantifying weekly physical activity. Additional covariates, age, socioeconomic status, and adherence to an insulin-resistance-friendly diet, were also considered to better capture the multidimensional relationship between physical activity and insulin resistance.

Overall, the methodology was designed to rigorously test whether physical activity differs between women with and without insulin resistance and to assess its influence on anthropometric parameters related to metabolic health (BMI, WHR, AVI, and BAI) in a scientifically robust manner.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol received approval from the Bioethics Committee of the Medical University of Lublin, Poland (KE-0254/307/2019). The research adhered to ethical standards established by the Association of Internet Researchers and was conducted with full respect for participants’ rights, including voluntary informed consent and the option to withdraw at any stage.

Participants were informed about the anonymity and confidentiality of their responses and were provided with comprehensive information regarding study procedures, duration, potential benefits, and minimal risks. Honesty in responses was emphasized to ensure data reliability. At the conclusion of the study, participants were offered a complementary educational publication, authored by one of the researchers, covering healthy nutrition, physical activity, and individualized dietary energy needs based on anthropometric indicators. 90% of participants opted to receive this publication.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 29.0.0. The normality of variable distributions was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Given unequal group sizes (χ² < 0.001) and the non-normal distribution of most variables, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for nonparametric comparisons.

Due to violations of assumptions for classical analysis of variance, namely non-homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test, p < 0.05) and non-normality of dependent variables, Generalized Linear Models (GENLIN) with robust covariance estimation were employed. The error distribution was specified as normal with an identity link function.

Initially, interaction terms between group type (insulin resistance vs. control) and all examined covariates were tested, including body weight, waist circumference, hip circumference, BMI, WHR, AVI, and BAI. Due to numerical estimation limitations and model stability considerations, only selected interaction terms were retained in the final models. Specifically, interactions between group type and body weight as well as group type and waist circumference were included in the generalized linear models for total physical activity (Total MET) and sedentary behaviour (Sedentary MET).

Dependent variables included total physical activity level (MET total) and sedentary activity level (MET sedentary). The primary grouping factor distinguished women with insulin resistance (study group) from metabolically healthy women (control group). Covariates included anthropometric parameters, body weight, waist circumference, BMI, WHR, AVI, BAI, and hip circumference. Initially, all interactions between group type and covariates were modeled, however, due to numerical estimation limitations in SPSS, only interactions between Group × Body Weight and Group × Waist/Hip Circumference were retained in the final models.

Results

This section presents a comprehensive account of the findings concerning physical activity among women with a medical diagnosis of IR compared with metabolically healthy women free from any chronic conditions. The analysis explores the effects of physical activity on anthropometric indicators within both groups.

The results reveal detailed differences in waist and hip circumference, body mass, BMI, WHR, AVI, and BAI between the study and control groups, as well as distinct variations in physical activity levels. These findings provide strong empirical support for the study hypothesis that physical activity constitutes a key determinant of metabolic health status. Although in some cases the anthropometric variables did not correlate directly with higher or lower levels of physical activity expressed in MET, the overall trend clearly demonstrates that greater physical activity is associated with more favorable metabolic outcomes.

The statistical analyses further elucidate the relationships between self-reported physical activity and sedentary behaviour, as assessed using the GPAQ, and the presence of insulin resistance. In this study, physical activity outcomes represent questionnaire-derived estimates of weekly energy expenditure (MET-minutes/week) rather than objectively measured daily movement volume.

Demographic characteristics and group comparisons

The study cohort consisted of 443 women, including 301 participants in the insulin-resistance group and 142 in the control group. Both groups were analyzed to assess physical activity levels, anthropometric parameters, and their association with insulin resistance.

Significant differences were observed between the study and control groups across multiple anthropometric variables, including body weight, waist and hip circumference, BMI, WHR, AVI, and BAI. Distinct variations in physical activity levels were also noted between women with insulin resistance and those without chronic diseases. These disparities underscore an essential relationship between physical activity, carbohydrate metabolism, and anthropometric indicators such as body mass and circumferential measurements. The findings further highlight that dietary habits, while fundamental, are not the sole determinants of insulin resistance, rather, overall lifestyle factors, including physical activity, play a pivotal role in metabolic health. As shown in Table 1, significant anthropometric differences were identified between women with insulin resistance and those in the control group, including higher body weight, waist and hip circumferences, and adiposity indices among insulin-resistant participants.

Body mass index and physical activity

BMI was categorized according to the WHO standards to compare total physical activity (total MET) between the study and control groups. According to these guidelines, a BMI value of less than 18.5 kg/m² indicates underweight, 18.5–24.99 kg/m² corresponds to normal weight, 25.00–29.99 kg/m² reflects overweight, and 30.00 kg/m² or higher is classified as obesity. As summarized in Appendix Table A1 (provided in the Supplementary Appendix Materials file), a statistically significant difference in total MET was observed between groups within the overweight category (p = 0.027), with women in the control group achieving higher levels of total physical activity compared to those with insulin resistance. No significant differences were noted among participants classified as underweight, normal-weight, or obese (all p > 0.05). These findings indicate that reduced physical activity among insulin-resistant women becomes particularly evident at the transitional stage between normal weight and obesity. This pattern underscores the importance of early preventive strategies and the promotion of sustained moderate-to-vigorous physical activity as a protective factor against metabolic deterioration.

Waist–hip ratio and physical activity

The relationship between physical activity and the Waist–Hip Ratio (WHR) was analyzed to determine whether central body fat distribution influences total physical activity levels expressed in METs. As summarized in Appendix Table A2, a statistically significant difference in total MET values was observed between the study and control groups among women with a normal WHR (p = 0.033), with the control group achieving higher overall physical activity levels. No significant difference was found among participants with an abnormal WHR (p = 0.187). These findings suggest that the beneficial effects of physical activity on metabolic balance are most evident in women with a normal pattern of fat distribution, whereas in those with central adiposity (elevated WHR), the metabolic advantages of physical activity may be partially diminished by the presence of insulin resistance and altered fat metabolism.

Financial situation and physical activity

The relationship between socioeconomic status and total physical activity levels was examined across three self-reported categories of financial situation, below average, average, and above average. These findings suggest a nonlinear relationship between socioeconomic status and physical activity. The most pronounced differences between metabolically healthy and insulin-resistant women occurred at both ends of the income spectrum. This pattern may reflect contrasting lifestyle constraints and health-related priorities, lower-income women may be more physically active due to occupational or transport-related movement, whereas higher-income women may engage in structured exercise and recreational activities. Overall, women in the control group tended to demonstrate greater physical activity regardless of financial status, emphasizing that behavioral and motivational factors may play a more decisive role in maintaining metabolic health than purely economic determinants.

Discussion

The findings of this study are discussed in relation to current literature addressing the role of physical activity in IR. The primary objective was to compare levels of physical activity, expressed as total MET (the sum of moderate and vigorous activity) and sedentary MET (sitting or lying not related to sleep), between women medically diagnosed with insulin resistance and metabolically healthy controls, while accounting for key anthropometric indicators.

Although previous studies using objective measures such as accelerometry have consistently demonstrated lower physical activity levels among individuals with insulin resistance, the present study extends existing knowledge by showing that differences in physical activity and sedentary behaviour clearly distinguish women with clinically diagnosed insulin resistance from metabolically healthy controls independently of commonly used anthropometric indicators [23, 26]. Importantly, these findings were observed in women of reproductive age within a real-world clinical context, where insulin resistance was defined based on routine medical diagnosis rather than experimental classification. By simultaneously accounting for anthropometric indices and sedentary behaviour, the study highlights behavioural patterns as key discriminators of insulin resistance beyond body size alone.

Insulin is a central anabolic hormone responsible for stimulating glucose uptake, glycogen synthesis in skeletal muscle, and the storage of metabolic substrates. Insulin resistance represents a pathological state characterized by a diminished cellular response, particularly in skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, and the liver, to insulin’s actions. This metabolic impairment also affects lipid and protein metabolism, producing systemic health consequences across multiple domains, including cardiovascular function, endocrine regulation, and reproductive health. The key modifiable factors influencing tissue sensitivity to insulin are an appropriate diet, regular physical activity, maintenance of healthy body weight, adequate sleep, and stress reduction.

Physical activity plays a pivotal role in modulating biomarkers of insulin resistance. Regular, sufficiently intense exercise enhances insulin sensitivity and supports glycemic control, whereas sedentary behavior may aggravate insulin resistance through increased pancreatic strain and reduced secretory efficiency [18]. Moreover, physical activity exerts anti-inflammatory effects, while inactivity is associated with heightened oxidative stress [27].

In this study, physical activity was assessed using the GPAQ, developed by the World Health Organization. The GPAQ, alongside the International Physical Activity Questionnaire, remains one of the most widely used tools for evaluating habitual activity levels in epidemiological research. Alternative approaches, such as accelerometer-based assessments, provide objective movement data but depend on monitoring duration and participant compliance. In previous accelerometer-based studies, activity was tracked over four weeks, revealing that statistically significant differences in sedentary behavior emerged only after the first week of observation, highlighting the importance of long-term monitoring to capture true habitual behavior. Participants aware of being observed may consciously alter their activity patterns, introducing potential bias [28].

Our analyses revealed that group type (study vs. control) was a significant predictor of both total and sedentary activity levels, whereas anthropometric variables did not significantly affect physical activity outcomes. For total MET, women in the control group achieved substantially higher levels of overall physical activity compared with insulin-resistant participants. This difference persisted after adjusting for body weight, waist circumference, BMI, WHR, AVI, BAI, and hip circumference, suggesting that the observed disparities were not directly attributable to body composition but rather to behavioral and lifestyle factors.

Jurczewska et al. [29] reported similar findings, showing that higher physical activity levels were associated with lower HOMA-IR values even among women with insulin resistance secondary to polycystic ovary syndrome. This aligns with the current study, where women in the control group exhibited significantly higher total MET values. Furthermore, the large-scale study by Gao and Lin [30], involving U.S. adults, demonstrated a clear inverse association between MET-based physical activity levels and all-cause mortality. Among participants with insulin resistance (25.8% of 8,460 subjects), greater activity was associated with markedly lower mortality risk, 39% reduction for low activity (MET < 10 h/week), 44% for moderate activity (10–50 h/week), and 57% for high activity (≥ 50 h/week), compared to inactivity. High levels of physical activity have also been linked to lower all-cause mortality (by 21%) and cardiovascular mortality (by 24%) in obese individuals [31], a condition frequently comorbid with insulin resistance.

In our sample, some women reported no structured exercise beyond sedentary activities. MET values were nonetheless estimated following GPAQ guidelines, minutes of moderate activity were multiplied by 4 METs and minutes of vigorous activity by 8 METs. The mean total MET for the insulin-resistance group was 2615.15, reflecting markedly lower engagement in health-promoting physical activity. Prior studies examining anthropometric correlates of insulin resistance in non-diabetic obese populations have demonstrated strong associations between IR and indices such as BMI, WHR, and waist-to-height ratio. Waist circumference alone has been validated as a reliable proxy for metabolic risk [24, 32]. These relationships were echoed in our findings, emphasizing the importance of early detection and preventive interventions targeting high-risk individuals to mitigate progression toward type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome.

Regarding sedentary MET, a significant group effect was also observed, women with insulin resistance spent more time sitting or lying awake than their healthy counterparts. This difference supports previous research linking prolonged sedentary behavior to poorer metabolic profiles and reduced physical fitness [33]. Sedentary time has been positively correlated with adipose-tissue insulin resistance and higher body fat percentage, with this relationship particularly pronounced in women [34]. It has also been shown that even when insulin sensitivity improves, women leading sedentary lifestyles exhibit lower expression of insulin receptor and substrate mRNA, alongside elevated plasma free fatty acid levels [35]. In accelerometer-based studies, each additional 30 min of sedentary time was associated with a 1.80-unit increase in ADIPO-IR (a marker derived from fasting insulin and non-esterified fatty acids), with the strength of this relationship increasing proportionally to BMI. Overall, sedentary behavior has been consistently linked to heightened insulin resistance in individuals at risk of type 2 diabetes as well as in diagnosed patients [36].

Sex-specific physiological mechanisms may further contribute to the observed associations. In women, insulin sensitivity and substrate utilization during physical activity are strongly influenced by hormonal fluctuations, particularly estrogen and progesterone levels, which modulate lipid oxidation, glucose uptake, and skeletal muscle metabolism. These mechanisms may help explain why differences in physical activity and sedentary behaviour were evident between women with and without insulin resistance, even in the absence of pronounced anthropometric differentiation.

Our data showed that none of the anthropometric covariates, body weight, waist and hip circumference, BMI, WHR, AVI, or BAI, significantly predicted physical activity levels, indicating that differences between groups were relatively independent of body composition. Thus, the quantitative amount of adipose tissue does not appear to directly determine the extent of physical activity, women with both lower and higher BMI or WHR values exhibited comparable activity levels. However, the total physical activity (moderate + vigorous MET) was significantly lower among women with insulin resistance. The only significant interaction was observed between group type and body weight in the model for sedentary MET, suggesting that the impact of body weight on sedentary behavior differs between insulin-resistant and healthy women.

This finding should be interpreted with caution, as anthropometric measures such as BMI, WHR, AVI, and BAI represent indirect proxies of adiposity and fat distribution and do not capture body composition in terms of lean mass, fat mass, or ectopic fat deposition. Therefore, the absence of an association with anthropometric indices does not imply that physical activity is unrelated to body composition per se.

AVI and BAI, both increasingly recognized for assessing overweight and obesity-related risks, were significantly higher in the insulin-resistance group, consistent with prior research demonstrating their correlation with IR and excess adiposity [25, 37]. In metabolically healthy individuals, these indices could serve as early indicators of potential insulin-resistance risk, supporting the use of preventive lifestyle interventions such as tailored nutrition and regular physical activity.

The practical implications of these findings are substantial. First, they highlight the need for targeted interventions in women with insulin resistance aimed at reducing sedentary time and promoting daily physical activity. Second, since anthropometric measures did not account for the differences in activity, psychosocial, environmental, and behavioral determinants should be prioritized in public health strategies. Physical activity patterns differed markedly between women with insulin resistance and healthy controls, favoring the latter group, while the former demonstrated significantly higher body weight, waist and hip circumferences, WHR, BMI, AVI, and BAI. Nevertheless, these measures did not predict physical activity behavior directly.

Additional variables explored, place of residence, financial status, and sleep duration, provided further insights. Women with insulin resistance living in smaller towns were less active than healthy women, while differences in larger cities were nonsignificant. Financial status showed a nonlinear pattern, both below-average and above-average income groups displayed higher physical activity in the control sample. Average income was not associated with differences in movement. Sleep duration, although not significantly associated with physical activity in this study, remains a known modulator of insulin sensitivity, shorter, fragmented, or poor-quality sleep is linked to reduced insulin responsiveness [38–42].

An important limitation of the present study is the lack of direct assessment of physical fitness or physical condition, such as cardiorespiratory fitness, muscular strength, or functional performance. Physical fitness represents a construct distinct from physical activity and may mediate the relationship between habitual movement patterns, anthropometric characteristics, and insulin resistance. Future studies should incorporate objective fitness measures alongside physical activity assessment to better elucidate these pathways.

Overall, our findings underscore that physical activity, alongside diet, represents one of the two principal modifiable components of metabolic health. The study contributes to a growing body of evidence confirming that regular movement, independent of anthropometric variation, plays a central role in mitigating insulin resistance and improving metabolic outcomes among women of reproductive age [43, 44].

Taken together, these findings extend beyond the individual metabolic context, highlighting the broader implications for public health. Insulin resistance should be viewed not only as a clinical condition but as a growing societal challenge reflecting modern sedentary lifestyles. Public health strategies that promote daily movement, through urban planning, workplace wellness programs, and education, could markedly reduce the prevalence of insulin resistance and related chronic diseases [45, 46]. Embedding physical activity promotion into primary care and community-based interventions represents a cost-effective and sustainable approach to improving population metabolic health.

Limitations

This study provides valuable insights into differences in physical activity between women with medically diagnosed insulin resistance and metabolically healthy controls. It also highlights significant disparities in anthropometric parameters between groups while demonstrating that these measures did not exert a significant influence on total physical activity levels expressed in MET. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged and considered in future research.

A major limitation stems from the reliance on self-reported physical activity data, which may lead to discrepancies between declared and actual behaviors. Although the questionnaire employed included detailed descriptions intended to minimize interpretation bias, self-assessment inherently carries the risk of overestimating activity frequency or intensity. Future studies should aim to validate self-reported information with objective measurement tools, such as wrist-worn accelerometers, to improve data accuracy and reliability.

However, accelerometer-based methods are not without their own limitations. These devices primarily capture acceleration and general movement patterns and may underestimate energy expenditure from stationary yet strenuous activities such as resistance training or certain occupational tasks. Moreover, measurement precision may vary depending on device placement and posture, for instance, wrist orientation can affect motion detection sensitivity.

Future research should therefore prioritize the development of hybrid assessment protocols that integrate validated self-report instruments with objective motion sensors, thereby enabling a more comprehensive evaluation of total daily activity. Such approaches should encompass both dynamic activities and static but effortful movements performed in a fixed position.

Another potential source of systematic bias arises from participants’ natural tendency to overreport physical activity levels. Although the present study minimized this risk through a structured questionnaire design, including conditional question pathways and built-in consistency checks, some degree of subjective inflation may still persist.

Finally, future studies would benefit from stratifying participants according to the severity of insulin resistance, for example by applying biochemical indices such as HOMA-IR. This would enable a more refined analysis of how varying degrees of metabolic impairment interact with physical activity patterns and anthropometric parameters, providing deeper insight into the pathophysiological dynamics of insulin resistance.

Conclusions

This study provides novel evidence that differences in physical activity and sedentary behaviour clearly distinguish women with clinically diagnosed insulin resistance from metabolically healthy controls, independently of anthropometric status. By directly comparing women with and without insulin resistance, the present findings extend existing knowledge beyond well-established associations between physical activity and metabolic health, highlighting behavioural patterns as key discriminators of insulin resistance in women of reproductive age.

Importantly, although women with insulin resistance exhibited less favourable anthropometric profiles, body composition alone did not determine physical activity levels. This observation underscores the role of modifiable behavioural and lifestyle factors in shaping physical activity patterns, suggesting that reduced activity among insulin-resistant women is not merely a consequence of morphology. The identification of sedentary behaviour as a complementary and independent dimension further strengthens the clinical relevance of these findings.

From a clinical and public health perspective, the results support the need for targeted, behaviour-focused interventions that promote both increased physical activity and reduced sedentary time among women with insulin resistance. Such strategies should be tailored to socio-demographic context and implemented early, before the progression to advanced adiposity and metabolic dysfunction. Future research should incorporate longitudinal designs and objective measures of physical activity to further clarify causal pathways and optimize intervention strategies aimed at preventing and managing insulin resistance in women.
