Effect of concomitant flatfoot on surgical outcomes following isolated talocalcaneal coalition resection in adult patients: a retrospective study
Authors: Zhuo Li, Ke Jie, Xue Li, Guodong Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeA greater hindfoot valgus angle is potentially associated with worse outcomes after talocalcaneal coalition (TCC) resection. However, the impact of concomitant flatfoot on TCC resection outcomes remains controversial. The aim of this study was to discuss the effect of flatfoot on the outcomes of isolated TCC resection.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on patients who underwent isolated resection for symptomatic TCC between 2016 and 2024. Patients were divided into flatfoot (FF, n = 20) and non-flatfoot groups (NF, n = 25) based on the diagnostic criteria for radiographic flatfoot on preoperative weightbearing radiographs: Meary’s angle (MA) > 4°, calcaneal pitch (CP) < 18°, talonavicular coverage angle (TNCA) > 7° and Kite’s angle (KA) > 40°. Preoperative and postoperative visual analogue scale (VAS) scores and American Orthopaedic Foot & Ankle Society (AOFAS) scores were used to evaluate functional outcomes, whereas flatfoot-related angles (CP, MA, TNCA and KA) were measured to assess radiographic outcomes.ResultsForty-four patients (45 feet) were included in this study. The mean age at the time of surgery was 26.7 ± 6.6 years. All cases included were non-osseous coalitions. Twenty feet were in the FF group, and 25 feet were in the NF group. All patients were followed up for 50.6 ± 18.0 months. At the final follow-up, the VAS score was 1.3 ± 0.8, and the AOFAS score was 83.9 ± 9.0 in the FF group. In the NF group, the VAS score was 1.5 ± 0.9, and the AOFAS ankle–hindfoot score was 83.3 ± 6.6. Both groups demonstrated statistically significant improvements in functional outcomes (VAS and AOFAS) compared to preoperative values (p < 0.05). No significant differences in the radiographic measurements of flatfoot were noted between the two groups at the final follow-up.ConclusionIsolated resection is effective for treating symptomatic TCC. Concomitant mild flatfoot did not adversely affect outcomes, suggesting that resection is a viable option for TCC patients with combined flatfoot.

Introduction

Talocalcaneal coalition (TCC) is a common type of tarsal coalition characterized by abnormal fibrocartilaginous or osseous connections within the subtalar joint, with an estimated prevalence of 1%-6% [1]. Middle facet TCCs produce rigid flatfoot, with accompanying symptoms such as hindfoot stiffness or pain in the posteromedial part of the ankle, which may manifest with age or trauma [2–4]. Owing to overlapping osseous structures of the subtalar joint, plain radiographs have limited diagnostic value for TCCs. Thus, computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is recommended when radiographs suggest a TCC [5]. Kumar et al. classified TCCs into three types, namely, osseous, cartilaginous, and fibrous, on the basis of coronal CT morphology [6]. Newman et al. noted that coronal CT effectively depicts osseous coalitions but is less sensitive for non-osseous variants, whereas MRI better visualizes soft-tissue inflammation and bone marrow oedema [7].

TCC resection is indicated after nonoperative management for at least 6 months [8]. For painful cases, early surgical intervention is often necessary. However, optimal surgical management remains debated, particularly for TCCs with concomitant hindfoot valgus. Wilde et al. suggested resection only if the hindfoot angle was less than 16° [9], whereas Luhmann et al. associated worse outcomes when the angle was greater than 21° [10]. In contrast, Khoshbin et al. reported no correlation between hindfoot valgus and resection outcomes [11]. Myerson et al. advocated calcaneal osteotomy over isolated resection for TCCs with flatfoot and stiffness, emphasizing alignment correction [12]. On the other hand, several studies have reported good postoperative outcomes of isolated resection for TCCs associated with hindfoot valgus [12]. In terms of surgical decision-making for TCCs, the controversy has focused mainly on the necessity of hindfoot alignment correction. With respect to patients with TCCs with concomitant flatfoot, surgeons are still debating whether to correct the hindfoot alignment at the same time as the bone coalition is removed or to simply focus on restoring the range of motion of the subtalar joint [12–14]. To the best of our knowledge, no study has reported the role that flatfoot plays in affecting the outcome of resection. We hypothesized that concomitant flatfoot would not negatively compromise the functional and radiographic outcomes following isolated TCC resection. The aim of this study was to discuss the effect of flatfoot on the outcomes of isolated TCC resection.

Methods

This study was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [15]. Approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Foshan Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Clinical Trial Number: KY2025116). All patients provided informed consent for medical record review.

Study design

We retrospectively analysed patients ≥ 18 years who underwent isolated TCC resection between June 2016 and February 2024. The exclusion criteria included concomitant hindfoot realignment, subtalar/triple arthritis or neuromuscular disorders. Five patients underwent subtalar arthrodesis due to subtalar arthritis, 7 patients underwent medializing calcaneal osteotomy due to hindfoot valgus, were excluded. Among the 50 eligible patients, forty-four (45 feet) participated (88% enrolment rate; nonparticipation due to refusal, transportation issues, or disinterest).

The demographic data of all the patients, including gender, age at the time of surgery, body mass index (BMI) and complications, were collected (Table 1). Preoperative weightbearing anteroposterior (AP) and lateral plain radiographs, as well as computed tomography (CT) were performed for diagnosis. To determine the preoperative and postoperative clinical outcomes, the AOFAS scores [16, 17], VAS scores [18] and radiographic data of the patients were evaluated.

Surgical technique

All patients underwent simple resection of the TCC. Our surgical method was modified from that of Scranton and Sobron [21]. Three-dimensional-printed surgical guides were used in some of our surgeries to assist in performing the resection. Before surgery, the CT scan images were exported, and the experts designed a 3D model of the patient’s foot. The modelling data were then imported into the 3D printer to create a customized surgical guide matching the patient’s subtalar joint, which was made from white thermoplastic polylactic acid (PLA). The guide consisted of two 10⋅2.5 mm cylinders and a cutting slot. The cylinders were designed to guide the K-wires so that the K-wires could be drilled accurately into the subchondral bone and fix the position of the resection guide. The guide plate was sterilized and packaged for intraoperative use.

All resections were performed under epidural anaesthesia and nerve block. A 6-cm curvilinear incision was made 1.5 cm inferior to the medial malleolus. After the neurovascular bundle was retracted plantarly and the flexor tendons were retracted dorsally, the coalition was exposed. If the surgical guide was available, the surgeon placed and adjusted the guide at the medial aspect of the coalition to search for a perfect fit. Afterwards, the coalition was excised with a bone chisel along the cutting slot. If the surgical guide was unavailable, a part of the coalition was first removed with bone bite forceps. When the subtalar joint space was essentially visible, a bone chisel was used to cut off the bone block. Finally, two K-wires were drilled into the subchondral bone of the upper and lower articular surfaces of the subtalar joint, and the joint space was fully exposed with a point-type distractor (Fig. 2). Resection adequacy was confirmed by visualization of the subtalar joint cartilage and increased range of motion. Bone wax was applied to the resection margins before incision closure.

Outcome assessment

Preoperative and postoperative pain and function were evaluated using VAS and AOFAS ankle–hindfoot scores. Radiographic parameters (CP, MA, TNCA, and KA) were measured on weightbearing plain radiographs. Assessments were performed preoperatively and at 2 weeks, 8 weeks, and every 3 months post-operatively by a single physician (K.J.).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS 25.0) software. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to verify the normality of the data distribution, and Levene’s test was used to assess the homogeneity of variance. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± SD. The VAS and AOFAS scores were evaluated and compared between groups using independent t tests. The preoperative and postoperative functional outcomes as well as the radiographic outcomes were evaluated and compared using a paired t test. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All study data were collected according to each resection. The functional outcomes in our study were analysed at the level of the individual patient rather than being limited to the assessment of a single foot, according to Menz et al. [22]. In addition, a linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate the relationship between patient age at the time of surgery and postoperative functional outcomes (VAS and AOFAS scores).

Results

The mean age of the patients included in the study was 26.7 ± 6.6 years, the mean follow-up time was 50.6 ± 18.0 months, and the mean BMI was 23.1 ± 3.0 kg/m². All cases included were non-osseous coalitions. The preoperative VAS score was 5.6 (range 4–7) in the FF group and 4.8 (range 3–7) in the NF group, while the postoperative VAS score was 1.3 in the FF group and 1.5 in the NF group. The preoperative AOFAS score was 57.5 (range 39–66) in the FF group and 56.2 (range 38–68) in the NF group. The postoperative AOFAS score was 83.9 (range 71–100) in the FF group and 83.3 (range 69–96) in the NF group. Preoperative pain was significantly more severe for FF group compared with NF group. No further differences were detected with respect to the VAS score or AOFAS score between the 2 groups (Table 2).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study provides clinical evidence that mild flatfoot does not compromise the outcomes of isolated TCC resection. In our study, patients in the FF group reported more severe preoperative pain than those in the NF group did. This may be attributed to​ combined sinus tarsi pain (common in flatfoot) and subtalar joint pain (typical of TCC), ​potentially exacerbating​ perceived pain ​levels. Moreover, Katayama et al. [23] ​propose​ that TCC-related pain ​may stem from​ inflammation caused by mechanical stress between ossified and non-ossified coalition regions, and hindfoot valgus ​would probably increase​ subtalar joint traction, ​potentially worsening​ the symptoms. According to Kothari et al., preoperative ​pain may be correlated with coalition size and hindfoot alignment: when the coalition size is greater than 50% and the hindfoot valgus angle is less than 16 degrees, the ROM of the subtalar joint is restricted by the osseous coalition, thus leading to hindfoot pain; however, pain may be caused by medial arch collapse when the hindfoot valgus angle is greater than 16 degrees [24]. Furthermore, our regression analysis indicated that patient age did not significantly influence postoperative functional outcomes, suggesting that isolated resection remains a viable and effective option for adult patients regardless of age variations within our cohort.

It is generally believed that hindfoot alignment is a crucial factor affecting the outcome of TCC resection. The same rule should be applied to flatfoot, as hindfoot valgus is a typical sign of this condition. In our series, however, we noticed similar postoperative functional outcomes in both groups. These findings offered an alternative perspective to Myerson’s recommendation for combined osteotomy in flatfoot cases [12] while corroborating Khoshbin’s conclusion [11] that isolated resection achieves satisfactory outcomes when hindfoot valgus remains < 21°. Radiographically, no significant differences ​were observed​ in the MA, PA, TNCA or KA before or after surgery, ​indicating that TCC resection ​may not improve flatfoot.

In accordance with our previous experience with TCC resection, identifying the resection plane was difficult for large or irregular coalitions. Therefore, we used 3D-printed surgical guides to assist in the resection of TCCs. Previous studies had revealed that compared with classic resection, 3D-printed surgical guides reduce the median surgical time, which helps improve the surgical efficiency [21, 25, 26]. In addition, this technology may help improve the accuracy of the incision. During classic resections, especially when the bone coalition is irregular, surgeons may start too high while searching for the subtalar joint. This may increase the destruction of the talus, thus affecting the complication rate. The safety of the technique had been reported [21, 25], but there was insufficient clinical evidence to support this claim. Nevertheless, we observed three surgical guide fractures during our surgical process, which may be attributed to the material limitations of the PLA. The limitations of this technique highlight the need for reinforced biomaterials, as proposed by recent engineering studies.​.

In our study, five complications were reported after TCC resection, with the overall complication rate of 11.1%. The higher rate of moderate to severe pain in the flatfoot group may be associated with underlying changes in foot morphology and biomechanics. The case of secondary subtalar arthrodesis in the flatfoot group might reflect disease progression or persistent joint instability. Moreover, the subtalar stiffness observed only in the non-flatfoot group could be related to differences in postoperative adaptation or scar tissue formation. According to previous studies, the complication rate of TCC resection varied from 7% to 8.5% [27–29]. O ‘Neil et al. studied 20 patients, of whom 3 had complications, including 2 with incisional neuroma and 1 with reflex sympathetic dystrophy, which affected the efficacy of surgery [30]. Takakura et al. reported mild residual pain after TCC resection and revealed ossification of the cartilage that was not completely resected [31]. Gantsoudes et al. reported a case of postoperative wound granuloma that healed after debridement [32]. Khoshbin et al. reported a case of early wound infection that was successfully managed with oral antibiotic therapy. The above studies suggest that open resection may lead to a higher rate of wound problems and incomplete resection [11]. In our study, TCC patients with combined flatfoot had a higher incidence of complications, supporting the conclusions of Williams et al. Further observation is warranted to clarify the clinical relevance of these complications [14].

One patient in our study required secondary surgery after TCC resection. With concomitant flatfoot, this patient was readmitted because of pain and limited movement in the medial part of the subtalar joint and required subtalar arthrodesis because of subtalar arthritis. Previous studies have indicated that subtalar arthrodesis or triple arthrodesis might be the first choice for TCCs with pain [33, 34]. Nevertheless, in recent years, some studies have reported new indications for subtalar fusion. An expert consensus in 2020 reported that subtalar arthrodesis is recommended to correct and stabilize the hindfoot if hindfoot stiffness or subtalar arthritis occurs [35]. Ford et al. recommended subtalar arthrodesis if the coalition extended to the posterior articular process or if the posterior articular process was significantly narrowed [36]. Subtalar arthrodesis seems to be helpful for pain relief and midfoot range of motion preservation in patients with large medial facet TCCs. Triple arthrodesis should be considered only in cases of severe deformity or significant arthritis, as it compromises the range of motion of the foot and increases the risk of tibiotalar arthritis in the long term [37]. Despite these advantages, however, as for adolescent patients, excision of the coalition with deformity correction might still be the first choice so as to avoid arthrodesis whenever possible [13].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective study, and comparisons of the ROM of the subtalar joint between groups are lacking because of the difficulty in accurate measurement. Second, the sample size of this study is relatively small, which may have some influence on the study results. Third, the fluoroscopy data were measured by different researchers, and even with the best efforts, unavoidable errors may have occurred; however, we believe that this factor had a limited impact on our study. Fourth, the patients included in this study had mild flatfoot (average angle). It remains a limitation that patients with severe or symptomatic flatfoot may behave differently; thus, our findings should be interpreted cautiously and are only applicable to TCC patients with mild, asymptomatic flatfoot deformities. Although previous literature has emphasized the importance of hindfoot alignment in the context of TCC, this study did not assess hindfoot alignment using a calcaneal axis image. Instead, hindfoot valgus and flatfoot were treated as a single entity, and only flatfoot parameters were measured using AP and lateral radiographs. Fifth, in our cohort, some patients did not undergo MRI preoperatively, resulting in incomplete data concerning the type of coalition. Consequently, an analysis of the impact of coalition types on surgical outcomes could not be conducted. Sixth, the patients included in this study were relatively young (mean age 26.7 years), which may influence the outcomes. Seventh, AOFAS and VAS scores were used to evaluate the surgical outcomes of our study. Although used in many studies on TCC [29, 38–40], these scoring systems still contain subjective components, which may affect the results. Finally, we used 3D-printed surgical guides to assist in TCC resection, but its efficacy has not been observed clinically. Further long-term research is needed to explore its clinical value.

Conclusion

In conclusion, there was no evidence that preoperative radiographic flatfoot was associated with a negative outcome of TCC resection at the midterm follow-up. Therefore, isolated resection is an effective treatment for symptomatic TCC in patients with concomitant mild flatfoot.
