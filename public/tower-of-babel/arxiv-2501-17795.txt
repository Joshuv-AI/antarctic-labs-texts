Dimension of contracting on average Self-Similar Measures
Samuel Kittle, Constantin Kogler

Abstract
. We generalise Hochman’s theorem on the dimension of self-similar measures to contracting on average measures and show that a weaker condition than exponential separation on all scales is sufficient. Our proof uses a technique we call the variance summation method, avoiding the use of inverse theorems for entropy.



1.
Introduction

A central problem in the theory of self-similar measures is to determine their dimension. Significant progress was achieved by Hochman
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
, demonstrating that assuming exponential separation on infinitely many scales, the dimension of a self-similar measure can be expressed in terms of the random walk entropy and the Lyapunov exponent. This paper aims to extend Hochman’s results to contracting on average measures as well as to show that a weaker condition than exponential separation on all scales is sufficient. Our approach employs a technique we call variance summation, replacing the use of inverse theorems for entropy. The variance summation method was initially introduced by the first-named author in
[
9
]
to construct explicit examples of absolutely continuous Furstenberg measures of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and was further developed by the authors in the context of self-similar measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in
[
7
]
.

Denote by
G=\mathrm{Sim}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the group of similarities on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
O(d)
be the group of orthogonal
d\times d
matrices. For each
g\in G
there exists a scalar
\rho(g)>0
, an orthogonal matrix
U(g)\in O(d)
and a vector
b(g)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
g(x)=\rho(g)U(g)x+b(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A similarity is called contracting if
\rho(g)<1
and expanding when
\rho(g)>1
.

Given a probability measure
\mu
on
G
we define the Lyapunov exponent as

\chi_{\mu}=\mathbb{E}_{g\sim\nu}[\log\rho(g)]

whenever it exists. Throughout this paper we use the following terminology.

Definition 1.1
.

If
\chi_{\mu}<0
, we call
\mu

contracting on average
. Moreover, if every
g\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
is contracting, we say that
\mu
is
contracting
.

It is well-known (
[
6
]
,
[
1
]
) that when
\mu
is a finitely supported measure on
G=\mathrm{Sim}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\chi_{\mu}<0
, then there exists a unique probability measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying
\mu*\nu=\nu
. The measure
\nu
is called the self-similar measure of
\mu
. We say that
\nu
is a contracting on average self-similar measure whenever
\mu
is.

To introduce further notation, for a finitely supported probability measure
\mu
on
G
denote the random walk entropy as

h_{\mu}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}H(\mu^{*n})=\inf_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}H(\mu^{*n}),

where
H
is the Shannon entropy. Consider on
G
the metric

d(g,h)=|\log\rho(g)-\log\rho(h)|+||U(g)-U(h)||+|b(g)-b(h)|

for
g,h\in G
,
|\circ|
the euclidean norm and
||\circ||
the operator norm and define

\Delta_{n}=\min\{d(g,h)\,:\,g,h\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu^{*n})\text{ with }g\neq h\}

and

M_{n}=\min\left\{d(g,h)\,:\,g,h\in\bigcup_{i=0}^{n}\mathrm{supp}(\mu^{*i})\text{ with }g\neq h\right\}.

Denote by
U(\mu)
the pushforward of
\mu
under the map
g\mapsto U(g)
. We call
\mu
irreducible if the support of
U(\mu)
acts irreducibly on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, meaning that there are no subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
invariant under all elements in
\mathrm{supp}(U(\mu))
except for the trivial ones
\{0\}
and
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We furthermore say that
\mu
is without a common fixed point if the similarities in the support of
\mu
do not have a common fixed point, as otherwise the Dirac measure at a common fixed point is the self-similar measure.

It is well-established (cf. for example
[
3
]
) that when
\mu
is contracting on average, then
\nu
is exact dimensional, that is there is
\alpha\in[0,d]
such that for
\nu
-almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have
\nu(B_{r}(x))=r^{\alpha+o_{\mu,x}(1)}
as
r\to 0
. The number
\alpha
is called the dimension of
\nu
and denoted as
\dim\nu
. It furthermore holds that

\dim\nu\leq\min\left\{d,\frac{h_{\mu}}{|\chi_{\mu}|}\right\}.

It is conjectured that whenever
\mu
is a finitely supported, contracting on average and irreducible probability measure on
G
without a common fixed point, then
\dim\nu=\min\{d,\frac{h_{\mu}}{|\chi_{\mu}|}\}.
This was proved by Hochman for
d=1
in
[
4
]
and for arbitrary
d
in
[
5
]
under the additional assumptions that
\mu
is contracting, the elements in the support of
\mu
generate a free semi-group and that for some
c>0
it holds that
\Delta_{n}\geq e^{-cn}
for infinitely many
n\geq 1
. In this paper we generalise Hochman’s result to contracting on average measures and we do not assume that the elements in the support of
\mu
generate a free semi-group. We work with
M_{n}
instead of
\Delta_{n}
in order to apply previous results by the authors on the entropy of stopped random walks (
[
8
]
).

Theorem 1.2
.

(Generalisation of Hochman’s theorem)
Let
\mu
be a finitely supported, contracting on average and irreducible probability measure on
G
without a common fixed point. Furthermore assume that there is
c>0
such that
M_{n}\geq e^{-cn}
for infinitely many
n\geq 1
. Then

(1.1)

\dim\nu=\min\left\{d,\frac{h_{\mu}}{|\chi_{\mu}|}\right\}.

We note that Hochman
[
5
]
didn’t assume that
\mu
is irreducible, yet only the weaker condition that on a non-trivial
U(\mu)
-invariant subspace
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
it holds for
\nu
almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that the conditional measure
\nu_{V+x}
on
V+x
satisfies
\dim\nu_{V+x}=\dim V
. For simplicity it is assumed in this paper that
\mu
is irreducible.

Our method also allows us to prove the following weakening of the separation condition that
M_{n}\geq e^{-cn}
for all
n\geq 1
. The reader may observe that in Theorem
1.3
we require information on all scales, which contrasts Theorem
1.2
where information on only infinitely many scales is needed.

Theorem 1.3
.

(Weakening of separation condition on all scales)
Let
\mu
be a finitely supported, contracting on average and irreducible probability measure on
G
without a common fixed point. Furthermore assume that there is
\varepsilon>0
such that for all sufficiently large
n\geq 1
,

(1.2)

\log M_{n}\geq-n\exp((\log n)^{1/3-\varepsilon}).

Then (
1.1
) holds.

We note that condition (
1.2
) is weaker than assuming
M_{n}\geq e^{-n\cdot p(\log n)}
for any real polynomial
p
, yet stronger than assuming
M_{n}\geq e^{-n^{1+\varepsilon}}
for any
\varepsilon>0
. It is well-known that when
\mu
is supported on similarities with algebraic coefficients then for some
c>0
it holds that
\Delta_{n}\geq M_{n}\geq e^{-cn}
for all
n\geq 1
. On the other hand, in
[
5
]
it is shown that the latter holds generically for parametrized families of contracting self-similar measures.

Denote by
\nu_{\lambda}
the Bernoulli convolution of parameter
\lambda
. In recent landmark work, Varjú
[
10
]
proved that
\dim\nu_{\lambda}=1
for all transcendental
\lambda\in(1/2,1)
. Varjú’s proof relies on subtle approximation results of
\lambda
by algebraic numbers and on a result similar yet weaker to Theorem
1.3
, which was contained in Breuillard-Varjú
[
2
]
. Roughly speaking, Breuillard-Varjú proved in
[
2
]
for the Bernoulli convolution
\nu_{\lambda}
, as an important proof step of their main result, that if
\log M_{n}\geq-Cn\log n
for some
C>0
and all
n\geq 1
then (
1.1
) holds. The techniques presented in this paper are more flexible than the ones used in the latter proof step and therefore lead to the strengthening (
1.2
) for especially also inhomogeneous self-similar measures and in arbitrary dimensions.

We proceed with an outline of proofs in section
2.1
followed by setting up relevant notation in section
2.2
. We discuss we few preliminaries in section
3
. Entropy bounds are established in section
4
and we perform the variance summation method in section
5
. The proof of Theorem
1.2
and of Theorem
1.3
is concluded in section
6
.

2.
Outline and Notation

2.1.
Outline of proofs

We give an outline of the proof of Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
. We use techniques from
[
7
]
and the entropy bounds from
[
8
]
. Hochman’s proof
[
5
]
relies on inverse theorems for entropy. We bypass the latter by using a decomposition theory for stopped random walks and by summing the amount of variance we gain at each scale. Indeed, to introduce notation let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots
be independent
\mu
-distributed random variables. For a stopping time
\tau
write
q_{\tau}=\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{\tau}
. Note that if
x
is a sample of
\nu
independent from
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots
then
q_{\tau}x
is also a sample
\nu
. The basic idea of our proof is to decompose
q_{\tau}x
as a sum

(2.1)

q_{\tau}x\approx x_{0}+X_{1}+\dots+X_{n}

of random variables and to construct a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathscr{A}
such that
x_{0}
is
\mathscr{A}
-measurable and the
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are conditionally independent given
\mathscr{A}
. We also require the
X_{i}
to have some variance after conditioning on
\mathscr{A}
.

In order to prove that
\dim\nu=\min\{d,\frac{h_{\mu}}{|\chi_{\mu}|}\}
, our strategy of proof assumes that
\dim\nu<\frac{h_{\mu}}{|\chi_{\mu}|}

from which we will deduce that
\dim\nu=d
. To conclude the latter, we aim to show that for every
C>0
and each sufficiently small scale
r>0
and a suitable stopping time
\tau
we can find a decomposition (
2.1
) such that for all
i\in[n]
it holds that

(2.2)

|X_{i}|\leq C^{-1}r

and with probability
1-C^{-1}
we have

(2.3)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{Var}(X_{j}|\mathscr{A})\geq Cr^{2}I,

where
\mathrm{Var}(X_{j}|\mathscr{A})
is the covariance matrix of
X_{j}
conditional on
\mathscr{A}
and we denote by
\geq
the partial order defined in (
2.18
). Note that
\mathrm{Var}(X_{j}|\mathscr{A})
is an
\mathscr{A}
-measurable random variable.

We may conclude from a Berry-Essen type estimate that if (
2.3
) holds for arbitrarily large
C
, then
\nu
has dimension
d
. Indeed, it will follow that if
\Sigma=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{Var}(X_{j}|\mathscr{A})
, then roughly speaking

\mathcal{W}_{1}(q_{\tau}x|\mathscr{A},x_{0}+\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma))\ll_{d}C^{-1}r,

where
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
is the multivariate Gaussian with mean
0
and variance
\Sigma
and
\mathcal{W}_{1}
is the Wasserstein
L^{1}
-distance. As
C\to\infty
,
\nu
is well-approximated by a smoothened random variable. From the latter it will be straightforward to show that
\dim\nu=d
(see Proposition
3.4
).

From Decomposition on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to Decomposition on
G

Instead of constructing a decomposition (
2.3
) on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we will decompose
q_{\tau}
on
G
into

(2.4)

q_{\tau}=g_{1}\exp(U_{1})g_{2}\exp(U_{2})\cdots g_{n}\exp(U_{n})

for random variables
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}
on
G
and
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}
on the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of
G
and
\exp:\mathfrak{g}\to G
the exponential map. We will construct such a decomposition with
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}
being
\mathscr{A}
-measurable and
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}
being conditionally independent given
\mathscr{A}
. In order to express
q_{\tau}x
as a sum of random variables using (
2.4
), we apply Taylor’s theorem in Proposition
3.8
to deduce

(2.5)

q_{\tau}x\approx g_{1}\cdots g_{n}x+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}(U_{i}),

where

\zeta_{i}=D_{u}(g_{1}g_{2}\cdots g_{i}\exp(u)g_{i+1}g_{i+2}\cdots g_{n}x)|_{u=0}.

For notational convenience denote
g_{i}^{\prime}=g_{1}\cdots g_{i}
. It can then be shown that under suitable assumptions on the
g_{i}
, it holds that

(2.6)

\var(\zeta_{i}(U_{i})|\mathscr{A})\geq c_{1}\rho(g_{i}^{\prime})^{2}\mathrm{tr}(U_{i}|\mathscr{A})=c_{1}\mathrm{tr}(\rho(g_{i}^{\prime})U_{i}|\mathscr{A})I

for some constant
c_{1}>0
depending only on
\mu
and where we denote by
\mathrm{tr}(U_{i}|\mathscr{A})
the trace of the covariance of
(U_{i}|\mathscr{A})
. So in order to achieve (
2.3
), we require that

(2.7)

|U_{i}|\leq\rho(g_{i}^{\prime})^{-1}r\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{tr}(\rho(g_{i}^{\prime})U_{i}|\mathscr{A})\geq C^{3}c_{1}^{-1}r^{2}

for the constant
C
from (
2.3
) and the second bound holding with probability
1-C^{-1}
. Note that to deduce (
2.3
) from (
2.7
) we replace
U_{i}
by
C^{-1}U_{i}
and use (
2.6
).

Entropy Gap and Trace Bounds for Stopped Random Walk

To show (
2.7
) one first establishes suitable entropy gap results to deduce trace bounds at various scales. Indeed, denote for some
a\geq 1
by
H_{a}(q_{\tau};r_{1}|r_{2})
the entropy between scales
r_{1}<r_{2}
as defined in (
2.23
), which measures how much more information
q_{\tau}
has on scale
r_{1}
than on scale
r_{2}
.

We refer to
[
8
]
for a discussion on the entropy between scales. Write for
\kappa>0

\tau_{\kappa}=\inf\{n\geq 0\,:\,\rho(q_{n})\leq\kappa\}.

Then it will follow from
[
8
]
*Theorem 1.2 that under the assumptions of Theorem
1.2
and that
\dim\nu<\frac{h_{\mu}}{|\chi_{\mu}|}
it holds that for infinitely many scales
\kappa_{n}
with
\kappa_{n}\downarrow 0
as
n\to\infty
that

(2.8)

H_{a}(q_{\kappa_{n}};\kappa_{n}^{\beta}|\kappa_{n}^{\delta})\geq\alpha\log\kappa_{n}^{-1}

for suitable constants
\alpha,\beta
and
\delta
depending on
\mu
.

To convert (
2.8
) to a trace bound we use the following notation: For a random variable
g
and a scale
r>0
we denote by

\mathrm{tr}(g;r)

the supremum of all the values
t\geq 0
such that we can express

g=h\exp(U)

for some
\sigma
-algebra
\mathscr{A}
, some
\mathscr{A}
-measurable
G
-valued random variable
h
and such that
U
is a
\mathfrak{g}
-valued random variable satisfying

(2.9)

|U|\leq r\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{tr}(U|\mathscr{A})]\geq tr^{2},

where again
\mathrm{tr}(U|\mathscr{A})
is the trace of the covariance matrix of
(U|\mathscr{A})
. Up to for the purposes of this outline negligible error terms, it is shown in
[
8
]
*Theorem 1.4 that

(2.10)

\mathrm{tr}(g;2ar)\gg_{a}H_{a}(g;r|2r).

Since
H_{a}(g;r|2^{\ell}r)=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}H_{a}(g;2^{i}r|2^{i+1}r)
, by a telescoping sum argument (
[
8
]
*Proposition 1.5) we can deduce from (
2.8
) and (
2.10
) that there exists infinitely many scales
\kappa_{n}
with
\kappa_{n}\downarrow 0
such that for some
r_{n}\in(\kappa_{n}^{\beta},2a\kappa_{n}^{\delta})
we have for an altered constant
\alpha
,

(2.11)

\mathrm{tr}(q_{\tau_{\kappa_{n}}};r_{n})\geq\alpha.

The final part of the proof will be to sum up all the contributions at all scales
r_{n}
, which we will outline below.

In order to prove Theorem
1.3
, we will require sharper entropy and trace bounds. Indeed, up to negligible errors we will be able to show in Proposition
4.9
that if for some
B>0
we have

(2.12)

\log M_{n}\geq-n\exp((\log n)^{B})

then for
\kappa
sufficiently small there is collection of sufficiently separated scales
s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}
such that

s_{i}\in(\kappa^{\exp((\log\log\kappa^{-1})^{B})},2(\log\log\kappa^{-1})^{\frac{B}{2}}\kappa^{\delta}),

as well as

(2.13)

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{tr}(q_{\tau_{\kappa}};s_{i})\geq\frac{\alpha}{(\log\log\kappa^{-1})^{B}}.

for
m\asymp\exp((\log\log\kappa^{-1})^{B})
.

Variance Summation

We will explain how to sum up the contributions at various scales in (
2.11
) and (
2.13
) to conclude that
\dim\nu=d
. As is explained in more detail in section
5
, we denote for
n,K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
A,r>0
with
r\in(0,1)
by

(2.14)

V(\mu,n,K,\kappa,A;r)

the maximal amount of variance obtained in the form (
2.7
) at scale
r
contained in a decomposition (
2.4
) satisfying in essence the following properties:

(i)

Each of the terms
g_{i}\exp(U_{i})
is a product of at least
K
copies of
\gamma_{i}
.

(ii)

\rho(\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{n})\geq\kappa

The parameter
A
is there to ensure that the Taylor expansion (
2.5
) has a controllable error term (see section
5
). The most important property for our purposes is that the variance contributions on different scales are additive. Indeed, as stated in Proposition
5.4
, if
\{\rho(g)\,:\,g\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\}\subset[R^{-1},R]
for some
R>1
then for all suitable parameters and with
M\geq R
we have that

\displaystyle V(\mu,n_{1}+n_{2},K,R^{-1}M^{-1}\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2},A;r)

(2.15)

\displaystyle\geq V(\mu,n_{1},K,\kappa_{1},A;r)+V(\mu,n_{2},K,\kappa_{2},A;M\kappa_{1}^{-1}r).

The variable choice of
M
allows us to sum up information from different scales, provided they are separated by at least
R\kappa_{1}^{-1}
.

Let us assume now that we have a sequence
V_{i}=V(\mu,n_{i},K_{i},\kappa,A;r_{i})
of decompositions (
2.14
). Then we will show in section
6
that if, as
i\to\infty
, it holds that

(2.16)

V_{i}/\log n_{i}\to\infty,\quad\quad\quad\quad K_{i}/\log n_{i}\to\infty

and

(2.17)

\frac{\log r_{i}^{-1}-\log\kappa_{i}^{-1}}{n_{i}}\to\infty

then
\dim\nu=d
. It will become apparent in section
6
how these assumptions arise.

It will be straightforward to construct from (
2.11
) and (
2.1
) sequences such that (
2.16
) and (
2.17
) hold, which concludes the proof of Theorem
1.2
. For Theorem
1.3
we will combine the more subtle bounds (
2.12
) at various scales to conclude the claim (see section
6
).

2.2.
Notation

The same notation as in
[
7
]
and
[
8
]
is used. We write the asymptotic notation
A\ll B
or
A=O(B)
to denote that
|A|\leq CB
for a constant
C>0
. If the constant
C
depends on additional parameters we add subscripts. Moreover,
A\asymp B
denotes
A\ll B
and
B\ll A
.

For an integer
n\geq 1
we abbreviate
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.

Given two positive semi-definite symmetric real
d\times d
matrices
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
we write

(2.18)

M_{1}\geq M_{2}\quad\quad\text{ if and only if }\quad\quad x^{T}M_{1}x\geq x^{T}M_{2}x\quad\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

For a random variable
X
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
we denote by
\mathrm{Var}(X)
the covariance matrix of
X
and by
\mathrm{tr}(X)=\mathrm{tr}\,\mathrm{Var}(X)
the trace of the covariance matrix.

Given a metric space
(M,d)
,
p\in[1,\infty)
and two probability measures
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
on
M
, we define

(2.19)

\mathcal{W}_{p}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}\left(\int_{M\times M}d(x,y)^{p}\,d\gamma(x,y)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

where
\Gamma(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
is the set of couplings of
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
, i.e. of probability measures
\gamma
on
M\times M
whose projections to the first coordinate is
\lambda_{1}
and to the second is
\lambda_{2}
.

Throughout this paper we fix
d\geq 1
and write
G=\mathrm{Sim}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The Lie algebra of
G
will be denoted
\mathfrak{g}
and
\ell=\dim\mathfrak{g}
. For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
consider the map

w_{x}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad\quad u\mapsto\exp(u)x.

Denote by

(2.20)

\psi_{x}=D_{0}w_{x}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

the differential at zero of
w_{x}
.

Note that we can embed
G=\mathrm{Sim}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
into
\mathrm{GL}_{d+1}(\mathbb{R})
via the map

g\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}r(g)U(g)&b(g)\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore we can write
u\in\mathfrak{g}
as
u=(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix})
with
\alpha\in(\mathbb{R}\cdot\mathrm{Id}_{d})\oplus\mathfrak{so}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Thus it follows that
\psi_{x}(u)=u(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
1\end{smallmatrix})=\alpha x+\beta
. With this viewpoint we also use the following notation

(2.21)

ux=\psi_{x}(u)=\alpha x+\beta

We usually consider a fixed probability measure
\mu
on
G
and independent samples
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots
of
\mu
. We write for
\kappa>0

q_{n}=\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{n}\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad\tau_{\kappa}=\inf\{n\geq 1\,;\,\rho(\gamma_{n})\leq\kappa\}.

When
\mu
is a probability measure on
G=\mathrm{Sim}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\nu
is a probability measure
\mathbb{R}^{d}
we denote by
\mu*\nu
the probability measure uniquely characterized by

(\mu*\nu)(f)=\int\int f(gx)\,d\mu(g)d\nu(x)

for
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. When
\mu=\sum_{i}p_{i}\delta_{g_{i}}
is finitely supported, then

(2.22)

\mu*\nu=\sum_{i}p_{i}g_{i}\nu,

where
g_{i}\nu
is the pushforward of
\nu
by
g_{i}
defined by
(g_{i}\nu)(B)=\nu(g_{i}^{-1}B)
for all Borel sets
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Given a random variable
g
on
G
by
H(g)
the Shannon entropy when
g
is discrete and the differential entropy (with respect to a fixed Haar measure on
G
) when
g
is absolutely continuous.

We next define the entropy at a scale and between scales as in
[
8
]
. To do so, we construct a suitable family of smoothing functions. Indeed for given
r>0
and
a\geq 1
, denote by
\eta_{a,r}
a random variable on
\mathfrak{g}
with density function
f_{a,r}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

f_{a,r}(x)=\begin{cases}C_{a,r}e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2r^{2}}}&\text{if }|x|\leq ar,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where

is a normalizing constant to ensure that

integrates to

. We furthermore define

s_{a,r}=\exp(\eta_{a,r}).

We then define the entropy at scale

as

H_{a}(g;r)=H(g;s_{a,r})=H(gs_{a,r})-H(s_{a,r})

and the entropy between scales

as

\displaystyle H_{a}(g;r_{1}|r_{2})

\displaystyle=H(g;s_{r_{1},a}|s_{r_{2},a})=H_{a}(g;r_{1})-H_{a}(g;r_{2})

\displaystyle=(H(gs_{r_{1},a})-H(s_{r_{1},a}))-(H(gs_{r_{2},a})-H(s_{r_{2},a})).

We recall that

3.1.
Smoothing functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

In this paper we will need to smoothen random variables on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with various smoothing functions. Therefore we introduce the following definition and notation.

Definition 3.1
.

A family of independent random variables
A=(A_{r})_{r>0}
is called a
smoothing family
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if
A_{r}
is an absolutely continuous random variable satisfying that for any
r_{1},r_{2}>0
that
r_{1}^{-1}A_{r_{1}}
and
r_{2}^{-1}A_{r_{2}}
have the same distribution.

Given a smoothing family
A=(A_{r})_{r>0}
, we define for a random variable
X
independent of
A
by

(3.1)

H^{A}(X;r)=H(X+A_{r})-H(A_{r}).

We prove the following general lemma that apply to all exact dimensional probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with a polynomial tail decay. We note that by
[
3
]
and Theorem 1.2 of
[
7
]
all contracting on average self-similar measures satisfy the latter. We denote by
B_{R}(x)
the open
R
-ball around
x
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(A_{r})_{r>0}
be a family of smoothing functions and given
\varepsilon,r>0
define
T_{\varepsilon,r}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:d(x,\mathrm{supp}(A_{r})^{C})\geq\varepsilon\}
. Suppose that:

(i)

There exists some
c>0
such that
A_{1}
is supported on
B_{c}(0)
and the density of
A_{1}
is at most
c
on
B_{c}(0)
,

(ii)

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that the density of
A_{1}
is at least
\delta
on
T_{\varepsilon,1}
,

(iii)

A_{1}(T_{\varepsilon,1})\to 1
as
\varepsilon\to 0
(where we view
A_{1}
as a probability measure).

Suppose that
\lambda
is an exact dimensional probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and that there exists some
\alpha>0
such that for every sufficiently large
R>0
we have
\lambda(B_{R}^{C})<R^{-\alpha}
. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists some
r_{0}>0
such that for every
r\in(0,r_{0})
we have

H^{A}(\lambda;r)\in((\dim\lambda-\varepsilon)\log r^{-1},(\dim\lambda+\varepsilon)\log r^{-1}).

Proof.

We prove this by showing that

\liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{H^{A}(\lambda;r)}{\log r^{-1}}\leq\liminf_{r\to 0}\int\frac{\log\lambda(B_{r}(x))}{\log r}\,d\lambda(x)

and

\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{H^{A}(\lambda;r)}{\log r^{-1}}\geq\limsup_{r\to 0}\int\frac{\log\lambda(B_{r}(x))}{\log r}\,d\lambda(x)

and then applying Fatou’s lemma.

Let
f_{r}
be the density function of
\lambda*A_{r}
. Note that
f_{r}(x)=\int A_{r}(u-x)\,d\lambda(u)
and in particular

(3.2)

f_{r}(x)\leq r^{-d}\lambda(B_{cr}(x))

First we find a lower bound for
H(\lambda*A_{r})
. Note by (
3.2
)

\displaystyle H(\lambda*A_{r})

\displaystyle=\int\int-\log f_{r}(x+y)\,dA_{r}(y)\,d\lambda(x).

\displaystyle\geq\int\int-\log\lambda(B_{cr}(x+y))\,dA_{r}(y)\,d\lambda(x)+d\log r^{-1}-\log c

\displaystyle\geq\int-\log\lambda(B_{rc}(x))\,d\lambda(x)+d\log r^{-1}-\log c.

In particular

H(\lambda;r)\geq\int-\log\lambda(B_{rc}(x))\,d\lambda(x)-\log c

and so by Fatou’s lemma and exact dimensionality,

\liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{H(\lambda;r)}{\log r^{-1}}\geq\int\liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{\log\lambda(B_{cr}(x))}{\log r}\,d\lambda(x)=\dim\lambda.

Next we find our upper bound for
H(\lambda*A_{r})
. First let
S_{0}=B_{1}(0)
and for
n\geq 1
let
S_{n}=B_{2^{n}}(0)\backslash B_{2^{n-1}}(0)
. Let
\lambda_{n}:=\lambda|_{S_{n}}
and let
S_{n}^{\prime}:=B_{1}(A_{n})
. By
[
8
]
*Lemma 2.2 we have that

H(\lambda*A_{r})\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}H(\lambda_{n}*A_{r})+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}-\|\lambda_{n}\|_{1}\log\|\lambda_{n}\|_{1}.

Let
f_{n,r}
be the density function of
\lambda_{n}*A_{r}
. We now bound
H(\lambda_{n}*A_{r})
. We have

\displaystyle H(\lambda_{n}*A_{r})

\displaystyle=\int\int-\log f_{n,r}(x+y)\,dA_{r}(y)\,d\lambda_{n}(x)+\|\lambda_{n}\|_{1}\log\|\lambda_{n}\|_{1}.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and choose
\delta>0
such that the density of
A_{1}
is at least
\delta
on
T_{\varepsilon,1}
. Note that for all
x\in A_{n}
and
y\in T_{2\varepsilon,r}
we have

\displaystyle f_{n,r}(x+y)

\displaystyle=\int A_{r}(x+y-u)\,d\lambda_{n}(u)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{B_{\varepsilon r}(x)}A_{r}(x+y-u)\,d\lambda_{n}(u)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{B_{\varepsilon r}(x)}\delta r^{-d}\,d\lambda_{n}(u)

\displaystyle=\delta r^{-d}\lambda_{n}(B_{\varepsilon r}(x))

Therefore

\displaystyle\int_{S_{n}}\int_{T_{2\varepsilon,r}}-\log f_{n,r}(x+y)\,dA_{r}\,d\lambda_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\leq A_{1}(T_{2\varepsilon,1})\int-\log\delta\lambda_{n}(B_{\varepsilon r}(x))\,d\lambda_{n}(x)-A_{1}(T_{2\varepsilon,1})\|\lambda_{n}\|d\log r^{-1}.

Now let
g_{n,r}
be the density function of
\lambda_{n}*(A_{r}|_{T_{2\varepsilon,r}^{C}})
. Clearly
g_{n,r}\leq f_{n,r}
and so

\displaystyle\int_{S_{n}}\int_{T_{2\varepsilon,r}^{C}}-\log f_{n,r}(x+y)\,dA_{r}\,d\lambda_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{n}^{\prime}}-g_{n,r}(u)\log f_{n,r}(u)\,du

\displaystyle\leq\int_{S_{n}^{\prime}}-g_{n,r}(u)\log g_{n,r}(u)\,du

(3.3)

\displaystyle\leq-\|g_{n,r}\|_{1}\log\frac{\|g_{n,r}\|_{1}}{\mathrm{vol}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(S_{n}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=A_{1}(T_{2\varepsilon,1}^{C})\|\lambda_{n}\|\log\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(S_{n}^{\prime})}{A_{1}(T_{2\varepsilon,1}^{C})\|\lambda_{n}\|}

where (
3.3
) follows from Jensen’s inequality. Putting this together we get

\displaystyle H(\lambda_{n}*A_{r})

\displaystyle\leq A_{1}(T_{2\varepsilon,1})\int-\log\lambda_{n}(B_{\varepsilon r}(x))\,d\lambda_{n}(x)-A_{1}(T_{2\varepsilon,1})\|\lambda_{n}\|d\log r^{-1}

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,+A_{1}(T_{2\varepsilon,1})\log\delta^{-1}=A_{1}(T_{2\varepsilon,1}^{C})\|\lambda_{n}\|\log\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(S_{n}^{\prime})}{A_{1}(T_{2\varepsilon,1}^{C})\|\lambda_{n}\|}+\|\lambda_{n}\|\log\|\lambda_{n}\|.

Therefore

\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{H(\lambda_{n}*A_{r})}{\log r^{-1}}\leq A_{1}(T_{2\varepsilon,1})\left[\limsup_{r\to 0}\int\frac{\log\lambda_{n}(B_{\varepsilon r}(x))}{\log r}\,d\lambda_{n}(x)-d\|\lambda_{n}\|_{1}\right]

Clearly
\lambda_{n}
is exact dimensional with the same dimension as
\lambda
and so by Fatou’s lemma we get

\limsup_{r\to 0}\int\frac{\log\lambda_{n}(B_{\varepsilon r}(x))}{\log r}\,d\lambda_{n}(x)\leq\|\lambda_{n}\|_{1}\dim\lambda.

Noting that
\varepsilon
can be arbitrarily small we deduce that

\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{H(\lambda_{n}*A_{r})}{\log r^{-1}}\leq\|\lambda_{n}\|_{1}(\dim\lambda-d).

Finally by Fatou’s lemma we get

\displaystyle\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{H(\lambda*A_{r})}{\log r^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{r\to 0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{H(\lambda_{n}*A_{r})}{\log r^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{H(\lambda_{n}*A_{r})}{\log r^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\|\lambda_{n}\|_{1}(\dim\lambda-d)

\displaystyle=\dim\lambda-d.

The result follows.

∎

.

Suppose that

and that

Proof of Proposition
4.6
.

The proof is similar to Lemma 7.3 from
[
7
]
. First note that the Haar measure on

, which we denote by

, can be written as

\int f(g)\,dm_{G}(g)=\int\int\int f(\rho U+b)\rho^{-(d+1)}\,d\rho dUdb.

In other words it can be expressed as a product measure
\rho^{-(d+1)}d\rho dUdb
. We will find an upper bound on
H(q_{\tau_{\kappa}}s_{\kappa^{\delta},a})
by using
[
8
]
Lemma 2.5. We provide an upper bound on the entropy of

under the natural projections to each of
(\mathbb{R}_{>0},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),\rho^{-(d+1)}d\rho)

(O(d),\mathcal{B}(O(d)),m_{O(d)})
, and
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Note that

is compact so has finite Haar measure and therefore

H(U(q_{\tau_{\kappa}}s_{\kappa^{\delta},a}))\leq O(1).

Clearly providing
\kappa^{\delta}a
is sufficiently small we have
\rho(q_{\tau_{\kappa}}s_{\kappa^{\delta},a})\in[R^{-1}\kappa/2,2\kappa]
. The

so
H(\rho(q_{\tau_{\kappa}}s_{\kappa^{\delta},a}))\leq d\log\kappa^{-1}+O(\log R)+O(1)
.

Finally we need to bound
H(b(q_{\tau_{\kappa}}s_{\kappa^{\delta},a}))
. We introduce the following family of smoothing functions. As in (
3.4
), denote by

the family of smoothing functions where

is the uniform probability measure on

and recall the notation

.

Choose

large enough that
\nu\{x:|x|\geq T\}\leq\varepsilon
and let
\nu_{T}=\nu|_{x:|x|\leq T}
. By
[
8
]
*Lemma 2.1 we have

H((\mu^{*\tau_{\kappa}}*\nu_{T})*\xi_{\kappa})+H((\mu^{*\tau_{\kappa}}*(\nu-\nu_{T}))*\xi_{\kappa})\leq H(\nu*\xi_{\kappa})

By Lemma
3.2
providing
\kappa
is sufficiently small we have
H^{\xi}(\nu;\kappa)\leq(\dim\nu+\varepsilon)\log\kappa^{-1}
and so
H(\nu*\xi_{\kappa})\leq(\dim\nu+\varepsilon)\log\kappa^{-1}+H(\xi_{\kappa})=(\dim\nu+\varepsilon-d)\log\kappa^{-1}
.

Note that
H((\mu^{*\tau_{\kappa}}*(\nu-\nu_{T}))*\xi_{\kappa})\geq\|\nu-\nu_{T}\|_{1}H(\xi_{\kappa})\geq-\varepsilon d\log\kappa^{-1}
. This means

H((\mu^{*\tau_{\kappa}}*\nu_{T})*\xi_{\kappa})\leq(\dim\nu+\varepsilon-d+d\varepsilon)\log\kappa^{-1}

and so

H((\mu^{*\tau_{\kappa}}*\frac{\nu_{T}}{\|\nu_{T}\|_{1}})*\xi_{\kappa})\leq(\dim\nu-d+O_{d}(\varepsilon))\log\kappa^{-1}.

This means

H^{\xi}(\mu^{*\tau_{\kappa}}*\frac{\nu_{T}}{\|\nu_{T}\|_{1}};\kappa)\leq(\dim\nu+O_{d}(\varepsilon))\log\kappa^{-1}

and so by Lemma

H^{\xi}(b(q_{\tau_{\kappa}}s_{\kappa^{\delta},a});\kappa)\leq(\dim\nu+O_{d}(\varepsilon))\log\kappa^{-1}+O(\log(T+a+1)).

In particular providing
\kappa^{\delta}a
is sufficiently small we have

H^{\xi}(b(q_{\tau_{\kappa}}s_{\kappa^{\delta},a});\kappa)\leq(\dim\nu+O_{\mu}(\delta+\varepsilon))\log\kappa^{-1}.

Noting that
H(b(q_{\tau_{\kappa}}s_{\kappa^{\delta},a}))\leq H(b(q_{\tau_{\kappa}}s_{\kappa^{\delta},a})*\xi_{\kappa})
we can therefore conclude

H(b(q_{\tau_{\kappa}}s_{\kappa^{\delta},a}))\leq(\dim\nu+O_{\mu}(\delta+\varepsilon)-d)\log\kappa^{-1}.

Putting these estimates together we get that providing
\kappa^{\delta}a
is sufficiently small

H(q_{\tau_{\kappa}}s_{\kappa^{\delta},a})\leq(\dim\nu+O_{\mu}(\delta+\varepsilon))\log\kappa^{-1}.

The result follows by noting that
H(s_{\kappa^{\delta},a})\geq-O_{\mu}(\delta\log\kappa^{-1})
and that
\delta
and
\varepsilon
can be taken to be arbitrarily small.
∎

5.
Decomposition of Stopped Random Walks and Variance Summation

5.1.
Proper Decompositions

We recall the following from
[
7
]
in order to disintegrate our self-similar measure into measures which are approximately a sum of small independent random variables.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
G
, let
n,K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and let
A,r>0
and
r\in(0,1)
. Then a
proper decomposition
of
(\mu,n,K,A)
at scale
r
consists of the following data

(i)

f=(f_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
and
h=(h_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
random variables taking values in
G
,

(ii)

U=(U_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
random variables taking values in
\mathfrak{g}
,

(iii)

\mathscr{A}_{0}\subset\mathscr{A}_{1}\subset\ldots\subset\mathscr{A}_{n}
a nested sequence of
\sigma
-algebras,

(iv)

\gamma=(\gamma_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
be i.i.d. samples from
\mu
and let
\mathscr{F}=(\mathscr{F}_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
be a filtration for
\gamma
with
\gamma_{i+1}
being independent from
\mathscr{F}_{i}
for
i\geq 1
,

(v)

stopping times
S=(S_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
and
T=(T_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
for the filtration
\mathscr{F}
,

(vi)

m=(m_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
non-negative real numbers,

satisfying the following properties:

A1

The stopping times satisfy

S_{1}\leq T_{1}\leq S_{2}\leq T_{2}\leq\ldots\leq S_{n}\leq T_{n},

S_{1}\geq K
as well as
S_{i}\geq T_{i-1}+K
and
T_{i}\geq S_{i}+K
for
i\in[n]
,

A2

We have
f_{1}\exp(U_{1})=\gamma_{1}\dots\gamma_{S_{1}}
and for
2\leq i\leq n
we have
f_{i}\exp(U_{i})=\gamma_{T_{i-1}+1}\cdots\gamma_{S_{i}}
. Furthermore for each
i
we have that
f_{i}
is
\mathscr{A}_{i}
-measurable,

A3

h_{i}=\gamma_{S_{i}+1}\cdots\gamma_{T_{i}}
and
h_{i}
is
\mathscr{A}_{i}
-measurable,

A4

\rho(f_{i})<1
for all
1\leq i\leq n
,

A5

Whenever
|b(h_{i})|>A
, we have
U_{i}=0
,

A6

For each
1\leq i\leq n
we have

|U_{i}|\leq\rho(f_{1}h_{1}f_{2}h_{2}\cdots h_{i-1}f_{i})^{-1}r,

A7

For each
1\leq i\leq n
, we have that
U_{i}
is conditionally independent of
\mathscr{A}_{n}
given
\mathscr{A}_{i}
,

A8

The
U_{i}
are conditionally independent given
\mathscr{A}_{n}
,

A9

For each
1\leq i\leq n
, it holds

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\var(\rho(f_{i})U(f_{i})U_{i}b(h_{i})|\mathscr{A}_{i})}{\rho(f_{1}h_{1}f_{2}h_{2}\cdots f_{i-1}h_{i-1})^{-2}r^{2}}\,|\,\mathscr{A}_{i-1}\right]\geq m_{i}I.

Note that in
A9
by
\var
we mean the covariance matrix and we are using the ordering given by positive semi-definiteness (
2.18
) and we denote as in (
2.21
) by
U_{i}b(h_{i})=\psi_{b(h_{i})}(U_{i})
.

A proper decomposition as above gives us

(5.1)

\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{T_{n}}=f_{1}\exp(U_{1})h_{1}f_{2}\exp(U_{2})h_{2}\cdots h_{n-1}f_{n}\exp(U_{n})h_{n}

As in
[
7
]
, we briefly comment on the various properties of proper decompositions for the convenience of the reader. We use the parameter
K
and
A1
to ensure that each of the
f_{i}x
and
h_{i}x
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are close in distribution to
\nu
. Properties
A4
,
A5
and
A6
are needed in order to apply Proposition
3.8
. We require
A7
so that we have
\mathrm{Var}(U_{i}|\mathscr{A}_{n})=\mathrm{Var}(U_{i}|\mathscr{A}_{i})
and in particular the latter is a
\mathscr{A}_{i}
-measurable random variable.
A8
is needed so that
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
are conditionally independent given
\mathscr{A}
and therefore we can apply Berry Essen type results to approximate the disintegration of the measure as a normal distribution.

One works with two sequences of random variables
f
and
h
instead of one in order to be able to concatenate proper decompositions as in Proposition
5.4
. Indeed, if we had proper decompositions of the form

\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{T_{n}}=g_{1}\exp(U_{1})g_{2}\exp(U_{2})g_{3}\cdots g_{n}f\exp(U_{n})g_{n+1}

we could show a variant of (
5.3
) and all other results on proper decompositions. However we could not prove anything like Proposition
5.4
, whose flexible choice of the parameter
M
is useful in combining information from various scales.

We next define the
V
function mentioned in the outline of proofs. The additional parameter
\kappa>0
is introduced in order to be able to concatenate the decompositions in a suitable way (Proposition
5.4
).

Definition 5.2
.

Given
(\mu,n,K,A)
and
\kappa,r>0
we denote by

V(\mu,n,K,\kappa,A;r)

the
variance sum
defined as the supremum for
k=0,1,2,\ldots,n
of all possible values of

\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}

for a proper decomposition of
(\mu,k,K,A)
at scale
r
with
\rho(f_{1}h_{1}\cdots f_{k}h_{k})\geq\kappa
almost surely.

It is clear that for any
\kappa^{\prime}>0
with
\kappa^{\prime}\leq\kappa
we have

(5.2)

V(\mu,n,K,\kappa^{\prime},A;r)\geq V(\mu,n,K,\kappa,A;r).

In this section we recall some results from

that are used to show that the variance sum is large. First, we can construct proper decompositions comparing the variance and the trace.

The main result of this section will be to prove Theorems

Proposition 6.1

Let
\mu

Then

This is a corollary of Proposition
3.4
and of the following lemma, which relies on a Berry-Essen-type theorem.

Lemma 6.2

Let
\mu

Proof of Proposition

.

First note that by Proposition
5.4

. By Lemma

we can construct some sample

from
\nu
, some
\sigma

\hat{\mathscr{A}}
, some
\hat{\mathscr{A}}

and some

-measurable random

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\Sigma\geq V_{i}r^{2}I/4\text{ and }\mathcal{W}_{1}(x|\hat{\mathscr{A}},N(x_{0},\Sigma))\leq RC^{n_{i}}\kappa_{i}^{-1}r_{i}r+Cr\right]

\displaystyle\geq 1-O\left(n_{i}\exp\left(-c\min\{K_{i},V_{i}\}\right)\right)

for some constants

which depend only on
\mu
and

. By applying Proposition
3.4
with

It remains to prove Lemma
6.2
. We will need the following quantitative version of Cramer’s theorem.

Lemma 6.3
.

There is some absolute constant
c>0
such that the following is true. Suppose that
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are random
d\times d
symmetric positive semi-definite matrices such that
X_{i}\leq bI
for some
b>0
and

\mathbb{E}[X_{i}|X_{1},\dots,X_{i-1}]\geq m_{i}I.

Suppose that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}=an
. Then

\log\mathbb{P}\left[X_{1}+\dots+X_{n}\leq\frac{na}{4}I\right]\leq-can+O(d\log(b/a))

Lemma
6.3
is a corollary of the following result.

Corollary 6.4
.

(Corollary 7.9 of
[
9
]
)
There is a constant
c>0
such that the following is true for all
a\in[0,1)
and
n\geq 1
. Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be random variables taking values in
[0,1]
and let
m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}\geq 0
be such that we have almost surely

\mathbb{E}[X_{i}|X_{1},\ldots,X_{i-1}]\geq m_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
. Suppose that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}=an
. Then

\log\mathbb{P}\left[X_{1}+\ldots+X_{n}\leq\frac{1}{2}na\right]\leq-cna.

Proof of Lemma
6.3
.

For convenience write
Y_{n}=X_{1}+\ldots+X_{n}
and choose a set
S
of unit vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that if
y
is any unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
then there exists
x\in S
with
\|x-y\|\leq\frac{a}{8b}
. Note that we may choose
S
such that
|S|\leq O((b/a)^{d-1})
.

By Corollary
6.4
we know that for any
x\in S
,

\log\mathbb{P}\left[x^{T}Y_{n}x\leq\frac{na}{2}\right]\leq-can.

Let
A
be the event that there exists some
x\in S
with
x^{T}Y_{n}x\leq\frac{na}{2}
. We have that
\log\mathbb{P}[A]
is at most
-can+\log|S|
. It suffices therefore to show that on
A^{C}
we have
Y_{n}\geq\frac{na}{4}I
.

Indeed let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a unit vector. Choose some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|x-y\|\leq a/8b
. Suppose that
A^{C}
occurs. Note that we must have
Y_{n}\leq bnI
and therefore
||Y_{n}||\leq bn
. This means

\displaystyle y^{T}Y_{n}y

\displaystyle=xY_{n}x+x^{T}Y_{n}(y-x)+(y-x)^{T}Y_{n}y

\displaystyle>\frac{an}{2}-2bn\cdot\frac{a}{8b}=\frac{an}{4}.

and result follows.
∎

From Proposition

Proof of Theorem

.

Assume that

.
Let

\underline{k}_{n}:=\exp(-\exp(n^{3/2})).

Then by Proposition
5.8
with setting
\kappa=k_{n}
and

it follows for
\underline{m}_{n}\asymp\exp(n^{1/2-\varepsilon^{\prime}})

\underline{K}_{n}=\exp(n^{3/4})
as well as

\underline{\kappa}_{n}=k_{n}^{\exp(n^{1/2-2\varepsilon^{\prime}})+o_{\mu}(1)}\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad\underline{r}_{n}=k_{n}^{\delta+\exp(n^{1/2-2\varepsilon^{\prime}})}

for sufficiently small

that

(6.5)

V(\mu,\underline{m}_{n},\underline{K}_{n},\underline{\kappa}_{n},A;\underline{r}_{n})\geq\underline{V}_{n}.

for

5.6

providing
