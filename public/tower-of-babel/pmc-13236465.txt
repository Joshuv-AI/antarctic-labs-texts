Clostridium perfringens in Korean pig farms: antimicrobial resistance and toxin profiles
Authors: Chang-Won Im, Doosung Cheon, Kihong Park, Young-Ho Joo, Wan-Kyu Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ImportanceClostridium perfringens is associated with neonatal piglet diarrhea and economic loss; contemporary national data on toxin gene carriage and antimicrobial susceptibility in Korea are limited.ObjectiveTo characterize toxin gene profiles and antimicrobial resistance among Clostridium perfringens isolated from diarrheic piglets in Korea.MethodsFecal samples (n = 1,627) were collected from 286 farms in 2023–2024. Clostridium perfringens isolates (n = 410) were recovered under anaerobic conditions. Toxin genes (cpa, cpb, cpb2, cpe, etx, and iap) were detected by multiplex real-time polymerase chain reaction. Susceptibility to 18 antimicrobials was assessed by disc diffusion.ResultsType A strains (cpa alone [24.6%] or cpa+cpb2 [46.8%]) predominated; cpb2 and cpe were detected in 73.4% and 19.0% of isolates, respectively. Resistance was highest to bacitracin (94.9%), amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (79.8%), and florfenicol (81.5%).Conclusions and RelevanceIn 2023–2024, type A isolates carrying cpb2 were the predominant toxinotype among Clostridium perfringens recovered from diarrheic piglets in Korea. The high prevalence of resistance to several commonly used agents supports strengthened antimicrobial stewardship and evaluation of alternative control strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Diarrheal diseases caused by pathogenic bacteria impose a substantial economic burden on the global swine industry, particularly in suckling and weaned piglets, where diarrhea reduces farm productivity by causing growth retardation, decreased feed efficiency, and increased mortality [1]. Among bacterial pathogens, Clostridium perfringens is a representative disease-causing bacterium in piglets. This gram-positive, anaerobic, spore-forming bacterium is widely distributed in the environment and may be present as part of the normal intestinal microbiota; however, under specific conditions, it produces multiple toxins that induce severe intestinal diseases, including acute necrotic enteritis and diarrhea, which can lead to rapid clinical deterioration and death, especially in suckling piglets [2345].

C. perfringens produces more than 20 toxins and causes a wide range of systemic and intestinal diseases [4]. Based on the production of major toxins, it was originally classified into five toxinotypes (A–E) and was reclassified in 2018 to include two additional types, F and G, associated with enterotoxin (cpe) and NetB toxin, respectively [67]. Although type C was historically regarded as the primary cause of neonatal piglet diarrhea, type A infections have increased in recent years and are now recognized as a major cause of diarrhea in pig farms worldwide [68]. Notably, type A–associated diarrhea in piglets often shows a high prevalence of the β2 toxin gene (cpb2); however, its pathogenic role remains controversial, as cpb2 has been detected in both diarrheic and non-diarrheic piglets and consistent genotype–pathogenicity correlations have not been established [78].

In South Korea, limited studies have reported the presence of C. perfringens types A and C in piglets, with some isolates carrying the cpe gene; however, nationwide data on toxin gene distribution and antimicrobial resistance remain insufficient [89]. The distribution and clinical relevance of toxinotypes vary considerably by region, time, and production environment, with type C remaining predominant in Europe and North America [1011]. Antimicrobials have traditionally been used to control piglet diarrhea, but antimicrobial resistance has increased despite bans on growth-promoting use. High levels of resistance have been reported in C. perfringens isolates from livestock and meat products in several countries [121314], raising concerns regarding zoonotic transmission and treatment efficacy [15]. Although studies on piglet diarrhea pathogens in Korea have largely focused on Escherichia coli (ETEC, STEC) or viral agents such as porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV) and transmissible gastroenteritis virus [1617], integrated analyses of C. perfringens toxin genes and antimicrobial resistance profiles remain limited [3]. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the toxin gene carriage patterns and antimicrobial resistance characteristics of C. perfringens isolates obtained from diarrheic piglets in Korea during 2023–2024.

METHODS

Sample collection, transportation, and storage

Fecal samples (n = 1,627) were collected from the rectum or intestines of piglets with diarrhea obtained from 286 pig farms in Korea in 2023–2024. The fecal samples were transferred into 50 mL screw-cap tubes under refrigerated conditions. Diarrhea was defined as watery to pasty feces with increased frequency and/or perineal staining, as assessed by attending field veterinarians.

Anaerobic culture and identification of C. perfringens isolates from piglets

Clostridium isolates were anaerobically cultured at the two reference laboratories involved in this investigation based on the shared standard operating protocol for anaerobic culture of C. perfringens from stool. Briefly, fecal samples were inoculated into phosphate-buffered saline (0.01 M, pH 7.2) and plated on Shahidi Ferguson perfringens (SFP) selective media (SFP agar base; Becton, Dickinson Microbiology Systems; Becton, Dickinson and Company, USA) containing 400 µg D-cycloserine (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) as an additional selective agent. The plates were incubated anaerobically in an atmosphere of H2 and CO2 (GasPak, BBL; Becton, Dickinson and Company) for 24 h at 37°C.

Colonies with dark centers and/or diameters larger than 1 mm were systematically selected [12] to eliminate sampling bias and subcultured on SFP plates. They were subsequently subcultured on sheep blood agar, and presence of the typical C. perfringens double-hemolysis zone surrounding the colonies was confirmed through visual inspection. The bacterial suspensions were frozen in brain heart infusion broth with 30% glycerol at −70°C for further analysis.

Determination of the toxinotypes of C. perfringens isolates from piglets

Extraction and isolation of bacterial DNA

Nucleic acids were extracted using a total nucleic acid purification kit (POSTBIO, Korea). Briefly, 200 μL of the bacterial suspension and distilled water were mixed with 200 μL of lysis buffer and 20 μL of proteinase K solution (1 mg/mL) in a 1.5-mL microcentrifuge tube. The mixture was incubated at 56°C for 10 min. The nucleic acids were subsequently purified using an automated nucleic acid extraction instrument according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Bioer Technology, China). Finally, 100 μL of the total nucleic acids was used to detect porcine bacterial gastroenteritis pathogens [1819].

Detection of toxin genes in C. perfringens isolates from piglets

The C. perfringens α-toxin gene (cpa) was detected via real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) using the DNA extracted from the fecal samples. The 410 fecal samples positive for cpa were subjected to multiplex real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to determine the C. perfringens toxinotype based on the presence of the β-toxin gene (cpb), cpb2, cpe, ε-toxin gene (etx), and ι-toxin gene (iap). The qPCR assays were performed using pathogen-specific primers and fluorescent probes (Table 1) following previously published methods [1819].

Antimicrobial susceptibility profiles were matched with toxinotypes on a per-isolate basis to explore potential associations between toxin gene carriage and resistance patterns. Isolates interpreted as intermediate were grouped as susceptible for analysis.

Type C strains were identified in various toxin gene combinations. Strains harboring both cpa and cpb accounted for 3.6% (6/166) of the isolates in 2023, were not detected in 2024, and represented 1.5% (6/410) overall. Type C strains possessing cpa, cpb, and cpb2 accounted for 3.0% (5/166) and 11.5% (28/244) of the isolates in 2023 and 2024, respectively, representing 8.1% (33/410) overall. Furthermore, strains harboring cpa, cpb, cpb2, and cpe accounted for 5.4% (9/166) and 16.4% (40/244) of the isolates in 2023 and 2024, respectively, representing 12.0% (49/410) overall (Table 4).

Type F strains were rarely observed; those possessing cpa and cpe were isolated in only one strain (0.4%) in 2024, representing 0.2% (1/410) overall. Those harboring cpa, cpb2, and cpe accounted for 9.0% (15/166) and 5.3% (13/244) of the isolates in 2023 and 2024, respectively, representing 6.8% (28/410) overall (Table 4).

cpa was detected in all isolates, indicating a 100% detection rate, whereas cpb was detected in 12.1% (20/166) and 27.9% (68/244) of the isolates in 2023 and 2024, respectively, accounting for 21.5% (88/410) overall. cpb2 was detected in 75.3% (125/166) and 72.1% (176/244) of the isolates in 2023 and 2024, respectively, accounting for 73.4% (301/410) overall. cpe was detected in 14.5% (24/166) and 22.1% (54/244) of the isolates in 2023 and 2024, respectively, accounting for 19.0% (73/410) overall.

Antimicrobial resistance profile of C. perfringens isolates from piglets

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed moderate to high resistance rates for most agents, with variations observed between drugs. The resistance rates for bacitracin were 90.9% and 97.4% in 2023 and 2024, respectively. The resistance rates for amoxicillin/clavulanic acid were 81.8% and 78.2%, and those for florfenicol were 78.8% and 83.3% in 2023 and 2024, respectively. The ampicillin resistance rate decreased slightly from 75.8% in 2023 to 67.9% in 2024; the ceftiofur resistance rate decreased markedly from 30.3% to 14.1%. The colistin resistance rates were 45.5% and 43.6% in 2023 and 2024, respectively. The penicillin resistance rates increased slightly from 54.5% in 2023 to 59.0% in 2024. The tetracycline resistance rates were 15.2% and 21.8% in 2023 and 2024, respectively; the corresponding sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim resistance rates were 6.1% and 3.8%, indicating low resistance. The resistance rates for enrofloxacin, kanamycin, lincomycin, tiamulin, and tylosin were very low (0%–3%), indicating relatively well-preserved susceptibility to these agents.

MDR patterns and association with toxinotypes

MDR, defined as resistance to three or more antimicrobials, was observed in 93.4% (383/410) of C. perfringens isolates analyzed in this study. The prevalence of MDR was 89.8% (149/166) among isolates obtained in 2023 and increased to 95.9% (234/244) among those obtained in 2024.

In particular, type A strains, especially those carrying the cpb2 gene, accounted for the majority of MDR isolates, whereas non–type A strains showed a comparatively lower frequency of MDR. The most common MDR patterns involved concurrent resistance to β-lactams, bacitracin, and florfenicol, which were among the most frequently resisted antimicrobials overall. When MDR prevalence was examined in relation to toxinotypes using isolate-level matched data (Table 5), MDR was more frequently observed among type A isolates than among non–type A isolates.

DISCUSSION

We characterized C. perfringens isolates from the feces of diarrheic suckling piglets in Korean pig farms, providing updated epidemiological data and meaningful comparisons with previous studies. C. perfringens is classified into types A–G based on the toxins it produces. The α, β, ε, and ι toxins are the primary toxins, and pathogenicity varies depending on their combinations. Type A, which primarily produces the α toxin, is the most common isolate from suckling piglets with diarrhea. However, the presence of the cpb2 and cpe can influence clinical pathogenicity [20]. Type C, which produces the cpa and cpb, is closely associated with necrotic enteritis in newborn piglets and is traditionally reported as the most lethal form [10]. Types D and E primarily cause problems in ruminants but are rarely reported in pigs. Types F and G were recently added to the classification system, and their pathogenic significance is still under investigation [21].

In our study, type A strains were the most prevalent, accounting for more than 70% of the total isolates; they had either cpa alone or both cpa and cpb2. This finding contrasts with the previous perception that type C was the primary pathogen, but aligns with recent domestic and international findings [222]. The dominance of type A strains has been consistently reported in Korea. Previous studies have reported the isolation of many type A strains from piglets with and without diarrhea, and a significant proportion of these strains harbored cpb2 [222]. However, the presence of these cpb2-carrying strains was not necessarily associated with clinical symptoms. This observation suggests that the pathogenic role of the cpb2 may be limited and that the immune status of the host or complex infections with other pathogens may play a crucial role in actual disease onset. Therefore, the high prevalence of cpb2-positive strains should be interpreted as a potential risk factor rather than a definitive determinant of diarrhea, because cpb2 has also been detected in non-diarrheic piglets in previous studies. Host immunity, management conditions, and co-infection with other enteric pathogens may contribute to clinical disease expression. Although cpb2-positive strains were frequently associated with MDR, the presence of cpb2 should be interpreted as a potential risk factor rather than a definitive determinant of disease, as toxin gene carriage alone does not fully explain clinical outcomes.

Notably, type C has long been recognized as the primary cause of necrotic enteritis in newborn piglets in Europe and North America [1022]. Type C toxoid vaccines for sows are widely used in Europe, reflecting the continued high prevalence of type C infections in the region. Studies from Asian and Middle Eastern countries such as China, Pakistan, and Saudi Arabia have reported a characteristic mix of types A, C, and D, with specific toxin types tending to dominate in different regions [132123]. Our findings indicated that the situation in Korea differs: the frequency of type C was low, and an overwhelming predominance of type A was observed. This difference can be attributed to various factors, including the feeding management system, immune status of sows, antimicrobial usage patterns, and interactions with other enteric pathogens, including bacterial pathogens such as E. coli (ETEC, STEC), Salmonella, and Clostridium difficile. Notably, recent domestic studies have reported the predominant detection of C. difficile ribotype 078 [24]. The presence of this strain, which is emerging as an important pathogen in both humans and animals, alongside the dominance of C. perfringens type A, suggests that the bacteriological profile of piglet diarrhea in Korea is becoming increasingly complex.

Analysis of major toxin genes revealed the presence of cpa in all strains, demonstrating the universality of essential toxin genes. cpb2 was detected in 73.4% of the strains, indicating a very high prevalence. This finding is consistent with the findings of several domestic and international studies and suggests a high correlation between cpb2 and piglet diarrhea [2121322]. However, some studies have reported that cpb2 carriage does not necessarily correlate with clinical symptoms, and the pathogenic role of cpb2 remains controversial. A substantial proportion of type A strains also harbored cpb2. However, whether this is a direct cause of diarrhea outbreaks cannot be definitively established [222]. cpe was detected in 19% of the strains, indicating a risk of zoonotic transmission, as the cpe can cause food poisoning in humans. Therefore, the presence of cpe-carrying strains necessitates consideration of the potential for transmission between pigs and humans [320].

Overall, the distribution of C. perfringens toxin types in Korea is dominated by type A; however, it differs from internationally reported patterns due to the presence of some cpe-carrying strains and lack of correlation between the high frequency of cpb2 and clinical symptoms. Therefore, a tailored approach reflecting the Korean context in preventive strategies for piglet diarrhea, rather than simply applying models from other countries, is needed.

The predominance of type A strains in this study suggests that antimicrobial resistance patterns observed herein largely reflect resistance characteristics of this toxinotype. The antimicrobial resistance patterns observed in this study reflect antimicrobial usage practices within the domestic swine industry. The resistance rates to bacitracin, amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, and florfenicol were extremely high (70%–95%), indicating the frequent use of these agents on farms as feed additives or therapeutics. Resistance to ampicillin and penicillin was also detected in more than half of the isolates, indicating a significant decline in the efficacy of β-lactam antimicrobials that have been widely used to treat piglet diarrhea. Conversely, the rate of ceftiofur resistance decreased from 30.3% in 2023 to 14.1% in 2024, indicating its restricted or reduced use on farms. A resistance rate of approximately 20% was observed for the tetracycline class, whereas that of 55% was observed for the macrolide tilmicosin. However, resistance to enrofloxacin, kanamycin, lincomycin, tiamulin, and tylosin was rare. This finding contrasts with the findings of various domestic and international studies that reported high resistance rates for tetracyclines, macrolides (e.g., erythromycin), and clindamycin [1214]. This resistance pattern may be attributed to changes in the usage patterns of major antimicrobials due to shifts in disease status and the introduction of a veterinary prescription system, despite the frequent use of these antimicrobials as growth promoters or for therapeutic purposes in domestic pig farms.

The predominance of type A isolates in this study provides important context for interpreting the antimicrobial resistance and MDR patterns observed. Because type A strains accounted for the majority of isolates, the overall resistance profile particularly the high resistance to β-lactams, bacitracin, and florfenicol likely reflects resistance characteristics of this toxinotype. The frequent occurrence of MDR among type A, especially cpb2-carrying strains, further underscores the therapeutic challenges associated with C. perfringens–associated diarrhea in Korean pig farms. In line with the high resistance rates observed for several commonly used antimicrobials, MDR was highly prevalent overall (93.4%, 383/410), and was slightly higher in 2024 (95.9%) than in 2023 (89.8%) (Table 5). MDR was particularly frequent among type A isolates, especially cpb2-carrying type A strains (100.0%), suggesting that the predominant toxinotype in Korean pig farms is also a major contributor to the current MDR burden. These findings support the need for antimicrobial stewardship and alternative control strategies tailored to the Korean swine industry.

Similar to a report from Qinghai–Tibet Plateau, China that more than 98% of C. perfringens strains isolated from yaks were multidrug resistant [21], our study and other Korean studies also report an increasing proportion of multidrug-resistant strains. This may reduce the effectiveness of specific antimicrobials, potentially leading to treatment failure and increased disease control costs. Furthermore, the potential for antimicrobial resistance genes to spread horizontally within the gut microbiota to other pathogens (e.g., E. coli and Salmonella) poses a public health threat [1415]. South Korea ranks among the top OECD countries in antimicrobial use, and extensive use in the livestock sector has been identified as a major driver of the spread of resistant bacteria, which has reached critical levels in human clinical settings. The resistance patterns observed for zoonotic bacteria such as C. difficile highlight the need for a One Health approach that considers transmission potential between humans and animals [71424]. Our findings underscore the need to re-examine antimicrobial use policies in domestic pig farms, beyond the issue of C. perfringens resistance. Minimizing antimicrobial use in feed, introducing and researching antimicrobial alternatives, and establishing farm-level resistance monitoring systems are urgently needed.

Vaccination strategies for controlling piglet diarrhea in Korea have primarily targeted enteric pathogens such as enterotoxigenic E. coli, rotavirus, and PEDV [38]. However, the predominance of C. perfringens type A in domestic piglet diarrhea, as demonstrated in this study, suggests that the existing vaccine system may be insufficient. Type C toxoid vaccines have been widely used in Europe and North America through sow vaccination, and they have been effective in preventing neonatal necrotic enteritis [22232526]. However, the prevalence of type C is low in Korea, whereas type A and cpb2-bearing strains are widely distributed. Therefore, the existing type C-focused vaccines are insufficient, and multivalent vaccines that include the cpb2 are required. Recent studies have reported the protective effects of Clostridium vaccines based on multiple toxin antigens in both sows and piglets [232526]. This alternative should be actively considered in Korea.

Antimicrobial replacement strategies are also important. Several alternatives, including bacteriophages, probiotics, prebiotics, organic acids, and plant extracts, can improve piglet gut health and reduce the incidence of diarrhea [15]. Although the use of such alternatives has increased globally since the ban on feed antimicrobials, their practical application in Korea is limited. Piglet diarrhea cannot be controlled using a single measure; combined effects of vaccination, improved hygiene and feeding management, minimized antimicrobial use, and the introduction of antimicrobial alternatives are required. This is an essential strategy for moving beyond simple disease prevention toward a sustainable swine industry.

This study has some limitations. First, only piglets with diarrhea were sampled, and asymptomatic carriers were not included, limiting the interpretation of toxin gene carriage as a direct cause of disease. Second, toxin gene detection was not accompanied by confirmation of toxin protein expression, making it difficult to establish a direct link between gene presence and pathogenicity. Third, antimicrobial resistance was assessed phenotypically, and the underlying resistance genes were not analyzed. Because MDR patterns were derived from isolate-level matched susceptibility profiles, interpretation of subtype-specific MDR estimates for small toxinotype groups should be made cautiously. Future studies should compare piglets with and without diarrhea to clarify causality, assess toxin expression at the protein and in vivo levels, and characterize resistance genes and their transmission potential.

In conclusion, C. perfringens type A strains, especially those carrying cpb2, were the predominant cause of piglet diarrhea in Korea, whereas type C strains were rare. The high prevalence of antimicrobial resistance, especially to bacitracin and β-lactams, reflects domestic antimicrobial usage patterns and poses considerable challenges for treatment. These findings highlight the need for integrated control strategies that combine vaccination, prudent antimicrobial use, and alternative approaches tailored to the Korean swine industry.
