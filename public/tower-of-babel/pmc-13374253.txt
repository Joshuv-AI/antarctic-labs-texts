HAMA microneedles patch loaded with Three-Dimensional exosome and Mupirocin promote diabetic wound healing
Authors: Ying Hu, Yu Wu, Yuansen Luo, Xiangkai Kong, Delong Zhu, Kunrui Wang, Xiang Lan, Lei Zhu, Tao Xu, Kun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetic wound healing impairment poses significant clinical challenges due to infection risks, poor angiogenesis, and chronic inflammation, often leading to amputation or death. Recently, mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (Exos) exhibit potential in enhancing angiogenesis and suppressing inflammation, yet clinical application is limited by short half-life, low bioavailability, and high costs. Three-dimensional (3D) culture technology improves exosome yield and therapeutic efficacy, demonstrating superior performance in tissue regeneration. Concurrently, microneedle (MN) systems enable efficient transdermal drug delivery with minimal invasiveness. This study integrates 3d-Exo with Mupirocin into a hyaluronic acid methacrylate (HAMA)-based MN patch (3d-Exo MN) to enhance diabetic wound healing. The HAMA matrix ensures exosome stability and controlled release, while Mupirocin targets infection. In vitro and in vivo evaluations reveal that 3d-Exo MN significantly promotes cell proliferation, migration, and neovascularization, addressing key limitations of conventional therapies. By synergizing 3d-Exo advantages with MN-mediated delivery, this innovative platform offers a targeted, biocompatible strategy for diabetic wound management, bridging gaps in current treatment paradigms through enhanced drug efficacy and localized action.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-04992-9.

Introduction

Diabetic wound healing impairment represents a growing clinical challenge worldwide [1, 2], particularly in diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs), which are associated with high risks of infection, insufficient angiogenesis, and a chronic inflammatory microenvironment, often leading to severe outcomes such as amputation or death [3]. Conventional therapies (e.g., hypoglycemic drugs, glucose monitoring techniques and insulin pumps) can alleviate the hyperglycemic microenvironment of diabetic but only limited approaches have been established to manage the diabetic wounds [1, 4, 5]. Wound healing can be divided into four chronologically sequential and overlapping phases: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling [6, 7]. Current conventional clinical therapies, including glycemic control, surgical debridement, graft transplantation, wound dressing, wound off-loading, and infection control require lengthy treatment and bring great pains to the patients physically and psychologically [8]. Therefore, new treatments aiming to enhance antiinfection measures and vascular network reconstruction.

In recent years, mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes have shown promise in promoting angiogenesis, suppressing inflammation, and cell proliferation [9–11]. Adipose-[12], umbilical-[13], bone-marrow-[14] and smooth-muscle-derived [15] stem cells had shown a powerful ability to promote diabetes wound healing. Notably, exosomes offer key advantages, including heightened stability, lower immunogenicity and absent tumorigenicity, raising interest in cell-free therapeutic in wound healing research [16]. However, their clinical application is hindered by limitations such as short half-life, inefficient localized delivery, low bioavailability and expensive cost [17–19]. To overcome these challenges, three-dimensional (3D) culture technology has been introduced to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of exosomes. Studies indicate that 3D-cultured MSCs better preserve stem cell potency, and their secreted exosomes (3d-Exo) exhibit superior performance in pulmonary fibrosis [20], spinal cord repair [21], and periodontitis and experimental colitis [22] compared to 2D-derived exosomes. Within this 3D core-shell microfiber fabrication, not only is there an augmentation in exosome yield, but there is also an enhanced expression of pluripotency markers (Oct4, Nanog, Sox2) in MSCs, while preserving their differentiation capabilities [19]. Our study also indicates that 3d-Exo showed better wound healing promotion, which provide a novel strategy for wound healing [23, 24]. Conventional exosome therapies are typically administered via injection, including either a single high-dose injection or multiple low-dose injections [25]. However, a single high-dose injection may lead to wastage of exosomes and increased costs due to excessive concentration. Conversely, multiple low-dose injections involve complex procedures and cause repeated tissue damage [26]. In recent years, certain biomaterials such as hydrogels have been employed to encapsulate exosomes for achieving sustained release [27, 28]. Studies demonstrate that combining hydrogel materials with exosomes preserves the stability of proteins and microRNAs within exosomes, thereby prolonging their release duration [29]. Additionally, Wang indicates that integrating exosomes with hydrogels enables sustained exosome release, which significantly enhances wound healing [30].

As a minimally invasive medical technology, microneedle (MN) systems have garnered increasing interest in biomedical research over recent years. These innovative devices offer multiple distinct advantages such as pain-free administration, patient self-administration capability, and user-friendly operation [31]. Extensive clinical studies have demonstrated the efficacy of MN platforms in facilitating transdermal delivery of both low-molecular-weight compounds and biologics, including therapeutic agents like insulin, vaccine formulations, protein-based medications, and chemotherapeutic agents [32]. Compared to conventional transdermal approaches using bandages or hydrogel matrices, MN-based systems exhibit enhanced drug permeation through deeper epidermal layers while achieving superior delivery efficiency [33]. This technological advancement enables more precise transdermal administration of therapeutic substances while maintaining the inherent benefits of non-invasive delivery methods [34]. Biomaterials such as hyaluronic acid methacrylate (HAMA), known for their rapidly responds to photosensitivity, exhibits fast gelation speed and stable hydrogel performance, have been widely used in the biofabrication [35]. These systems not only protect exosome activity but also prolong therapeutic effects via controlled release mechanisms. For example, HAMA microneedles have successfully achieved localized fibroblast-derived exosomes delivery in senile wound healing models [36]. Additionally, the mechanical properties of microneedles (e.g., pore structure, mechanical strength) can be optimized through material formulation to better adapt to wound microenvironment demands.

In this study, we aimed to design portable MNs exhibiting angiogenic and anti-infection properties in diabetic wounds. A HAMA microneedles patch (3d-Exo MN) loaded with Three-Dimensional exosome and Mupirocin was fabricated. This combination aims to achieve 3d-EXO controlled release, high biocompatibility, and wound healing drug delivery, as depicted in Fig. 1. This study systematically investigates the therapeutic potential of 3d-Exo MN in diabetic wound regeneration. This innovative therapeutic system significantly enhances cells proliferative activity, migratory capacity, and neoangiogenesis across both in vitro and in vivo experimental models, which create an innovative paradigm for targeted intervention in diabetes wound management.

Materials and methods

Human ADSCs culture and 2D conventional cell culture

Human adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) were obtained from Sciencell™ Research Laboratories and cultured in a 150-mm cell culture dish (NEST) at 37℃ and 5% CO2 with 25–30 ml of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco). Serum-free ADSCs culture medium was used as conditioned medium for Exos collection.

3D microfiber fabrication and culture

Fabrication of 3D microfiber was performed according to our previous report [23]. Briefly, the cellular suspension and matrix components were discharged through a dual-lumen coaxial extrusion system. The inner conduit (0.38 mm bore) conveyed a 3 × 10⁷ cells ml− 1 biological suspension, while the outer annular channel (1.1 mm diameter) transported 1.5% sodium alginate solution (Aladdin Biochemicals, China). Controlled infusion rates of 3–10 mL/h and 15–30 mL/h were maintained for core and sheath flows respectively. Cross-linking agent was 3% calcium chloride solution. Following 10-minute crosslinking, the 3D microfiber was immersed sequentially in 0.9% sodium chloride solution (Kelong Chemical, China) for 5 min, then transferred to a 150-mm cell culture dish (NEST) and cultured in the same conditions.

Isolation of ADSC exosome

The experimental protocol involved differential centrifugation of MSC-conditioned culture supernatants derived from both 2D and 3D culture systems. Initial clarification was achieved through sequential centrifugal processing: primary separation at 300 × g (10 min, 4 °C) to sediment viable cells, followed by secondary clarification at 2000 × g (30 min, 4 °C) for cellular debris removal. The clarified supernatant subsequently underwent ultracentrifugation at 10,000 × g for 30 min using a refrigerated benchtop ultracentrifuge (Optima MAX-XP, Beckman Coulter). Post-centrifugation filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane filter (SLGV033RS, Millipore) effectively eliminated vesicular structures and aggregated proteins. The final purification stage employed high-speed centrifugation at 100,000 × g (70 min duration) with intermediate PBS washing steps: initial pelleting followed by resuspension and repeat centrifugation under identical parameters. All processed samples were maintained at cryogenic temperatures (4 °C) throughout the isolation procedure and ultimately reconstituted in appropriate buffers for downstream applications.

HAMA microneedle fabrication

For the formation of HAMA microneedle (MN), HAMA (150 kDa, Engineering For Life, China) and photoinitiator (0.25% (w/v) lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate; Engineering For Life, China) were dissolved in deionized water at concentrations of 5% (w/v). 1 ml of the above solution was poured over a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) mold containing an array of MNs cavities(base radius: 250 μm, height: 500 μm, spacing: 550 μm). The mold with dripping precursor solution was placed into a vacuum drying oven and evacuated at room temperature (negative pressure ≤-0.1 MPa) for 5–10 min. This was repeated 2–3 times until there was no obvious bubble. If the volume of precursor liquid decreases, a small amount of precursor liquid should be added. The defoaming mold was placed in an oven at 30–35℃ and heated for 5–6 h. Heating was repeated 2–3 times until a significant increase in precursor viscosity was observed. After heating, the solution was exposed to UV light (405 nm, 25mW/cm2, 10s) for gel. Finally, the MN arrays were detached from the PDMS mold. The integrity of the microneedles was checked and the broken or deformed microneedles were removed.

To prepare a HAMA microneedle loaded with Mupirocin (Shanghai, China) and 3d-Exos (3d-Exo MN), an appropriate amount of Mupirocin (2 mg/ ml) and 3d-Exos (1 × 1010/ml) was dissolved in a precursor solution.

Scanning electron microscopy analysis

The 3d-Exo MN were immersed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 1 h for fixation. Samples were dehydrated using graded ethanol (50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95% and 100%) and lyophilized in a freeze drier for 12 h to eliminate residual water. All samples were sputter-coated with platinum and imaged with an ULTRA 55 scanning electron microscope (SEM; ZEISS, Germany).

3d-Exo labeling

To determine the loading of exosomes on the microneedles, 3d-Exo were incubated with red fluorescent dye (PKH26, Sigma, USA) for 5 min and the neutralize redundant dye was removed by 0.5% BSA/PBS. Then, the residual dye was removed by centrifugation again to obtain the labeled 3d-Exos.

Controlled release of exosome by 3d-Exo MN

Controlled release of 3d-Exos was performed according to our previous report [24]. Briefly, 3d-Exo MN were put in the transwell upper compartment placed in a 24-well plate at 37℃, and 100 ul dPBS were put in the lower cells. Liquid from the lower chamber were collected from the basal chamber at predefined intervals (0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7days post-initiation) and BCA approach was used to quantitate protein concentration and calculate the amount percentage of released exosomes.

Compressive test

The 3d-Exo MN was fixed on the Compression instrument platform, and the debugging parameters were moved downward at a speed of 0.5 mm/min. When the tip of the needle contacted the horizontal steel plate, the sensor began to record until the patch was destroyed, and the force and displacement changes of the micro-needle were mainly recorded.

Antibacterial activity

The killing rates of staphylococcus aureus by the 3d-Exo MN were determined. A mixture of 100 µL of a bacterial suspension and 100 µL of hydrogel was prepared. Then, the mixture was incubated in an incubator for a duration of 4 h. The suspensions (diluted 10,000 times) were individually plated on BHI blood agar plates, respectively. Antibacterial properties of the 3d-Exo MN were determined by counting bacterial colonies after 24 h (n = 3).

Live–dead assay and cell counting Kit-8 assay

A live–dead cell staining kit (KeyGEN) was used to determine the effect of 3d-Exo MN on Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells (HUVEC), Human immortal keratinocyte line (Hacat) and Human Dermal Fibroblasts (Hdf) viability and mortality according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Images were taken with a fluorescence microscope (Mshot, China) at varying resolutions.

A Cell Counting Kit-8 (Sigma) was used to determine the effect of high glucose environment and exosome on HUVEC proliferation. And absorbance of culture media was measured at 450 nm using a multilabel counter (n = 3).

Cell migration and tube formation assay

For the cell migration assay, HUVEC were seeded at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates and cultured to 100% confluence. The surface of the cells was scraped using a 200µL-shaped suction head, and the free cells were washed off twice using PBS buffer solution. The cells were stimulated with high glucose DMEM (50 mmol/L), 2d-Exo MN culture supernatant, 3d-Exo MN culture supernatant or DMEM (5.56mmol/L). The scratch area was observed and photographed at 0, 24, and 48 h, and the measurements were taken using ImageJ software.

Tube formation was measured using a Matrigel method. A total of 2 × 104 HUVECs were seeded onto Matrigel (BD, 356234) in 96-well plates and incubated in high glucose DMEM (50 mmol/L), 2d-Exo MN culture supernatant, 3d-Exo MN culture supernatant or DMEM (5.56mmol/L) at 37 °C. Tube formation was examined 8 h later. The number of meshes, master segments, master junctions, and total master segment length were measured by ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD).

Wound healing experiments in diabetic animal model

The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. All animal experiments were approved as the “HAMA microneedles patch loaded with Three-Dimensional exosome and Mupirocin promote diabetic wound healing” by the ethical committee of Shenzhen Glorybay Biotech Co Ltd(Approval No.: RW-IACUC-24-0115) on “2024-02-19” and performed following the recommendations of the Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the Ministry of Science and Technology of People’s Republic of China.

Healthy C57 mice (male, 4 weeks) were used in this experiment. Mice were fed a high-fat diet (≥ 60% fat content) for 6 weeks to induce obesity and insulin resistance. B cells were destroyed by intraperitoneal injection of low-dose STZ (30 mg/kg/ day for 3 to 5 days). Blood glucose was measured with a blood glucose monitor. Mice with a blood glucose level below 11.1mmol/L were considered failure models and injected with STZ once again. Mice with blood glucose levels higher than 11.1mmol/L were defined as diabetic mice. A 10-mm full‐thickness cutaneous wound was created on the midline line of the mouse’s back.

The mice were randomly divided into four treatment groups: (1) 3d-Exo MN group (with 3d-Exo, Mupirocin); (2) 3d-Exo group (with only 3d-Exo); (3) MN group (with Mupirocin, without exosome); (4) control group. There were at least six mice in each experimental group. After treatment for 7 and 14 days, some of the mice were killed, and the full layer of back skin was taken for subsequent experiments.

The animal experimentation process involves adequate anesthesia with isoflurane gas. The method of asphyxiation with carbon dioxide was used to euthanize the mice.

Histochemistry and immunohistochemistry

For histochemistry and immunohistochemistry analysis, wound specimens underwent fixation in 4% neutral buffered formaldehyde followed by graded ethanol dehydration. Then, tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned into slices perpendicular to the wound surface. The tissue sections were stained with haematoxylin and eosin (HE) and Masson’s trichrome (MT) following conventional protocols. HE staining was used to examine new tissue formation and infiltrated cells. MT staining was used to detect collagen deposition. An upright optical microscope captured the images.

To assess the angiogenesis of different groups, immunohistochemical staining of CD31 was performed. Briefly, sodium citrate was used to repair tissue antigens for 15 min. The tissue was then blocked with 3% Bull Serum Albumin (BSA) for 30 min and incubated with anti-CD31 (1:100, Abcam) at 4℃overnight. The Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP) -conjugated secondary antibody was added on the second day for 50 min. Then, the nucleus was stained with haematoxylin. Finally, the slices were imaged using an upright optical microscope and analysed by ImageJ.

Statistical analysis

The data from exosome release, the CCK-8 assay, scratch assay, cell tubule formation assay in vitro, and diabetic wound healing in vivo are expressed as the mean ± SD. Student’s t-test was performed for statistical comparisons between the two groups. One-way analysis of variance was performed to statistically compare differences among multiple groups. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To obtain a microneedle that can pass the sustained release of exosomes and mupirocin, we used light-curing HAMA hydrogel. 3d-Exo MN was prepared using a simple micromolding method (Fig. 1).

3d-Exo MN promoted diabetic mice wound healing

To investigate the wound healing efficacy of 3d-Exo MN in vivo, we generated a full-thickness skin defect model in diabetic C57 mice. Figure 5a shown representative images of the diabetic mice wound healing process with different treatments over 14 days. No occurrence of infection or rejection was observed during the experiment. Over time, the wound area gradually decreased, and after implantation for 14 days, 3d-Exo MN showed a better tendency toward wound healing than other groups.

Histological analysis of wound healing

HE staining was used for observing granulation tissue formation and investigate the epithelial formation process. The results show that the epithelial tissue in the 3d-Exo MN group was relatively completed after 7days and completely formed after 14 days of treatment (Fig. 6a). In comparison, there was no evidence of integration in the epidermal structure, with only a thin dermal layer observed in other groups.

Proper collagen deposition and remodeling can enhance tissue tensile resistance and promote wound healing. Tissues in the 3d-Exo MN group obtained the best collagen deposition and oriented arrangement, indicating better wound healing and tissue remodeling outcomes (Fig. 6b).

The 3d-Exo MN enhanced angiogenesis in vivo

Angiogenesis serves as a critical determinant of wound repair efficacy, with the quality and density of neovascularization directly correlating with tissue regeneration outcomes. To evaluate angiogenesis in healing skin, we conducted CD31 immunohistochemical staining for endothelial cell-specific marker detection (Fig. 7a). Quantitative assessments revealed significantly higher numbers of mature vasculature in the 3d-Exo MN group compared to control, MN group and 2d-Exo MN group at 7 days (Fig. 7b). The number of mature blood vessels observed in the experimental group was less than that in the other groups at 14 days, which may be related to the regression of neovascularization in the experimental group (Fig. 7c). This implies that 3d-Exo MN group had moved from the proliferation stage to the tissue remodeling stage.

Discussion

Current epidemiological data from the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) reveals that approximately 10.5% of the global population were affected by diabetes in 2021 [8]. Patients with diabetes are often accompanied by multiple complications, especially chronic wounds, such as diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) [38, 39]. More importantly, diabetic wounds pose a significant clinical challenge due to their complex pathophysiology, which significantly increases morbidity or death worldwide with unaffordable healthcare costs [40]. One of the important factors leading to poor wound healing in diabetes is poor vascular flow. Our study also shown that HUVEC cell proliferation was significantly decreased in high-glucose environment. Numerous studies have highlighted the importance of vascularization in diabetes wound [41]. Long-term exposure to a high-glucose environment leads to endothelial cell dysfunction, such as integrity loss, apoptosis and detachment [42, 43].

Recently, mesenchymal stem cells have shown promising applications in the fields of cell therapy and regenerative medicine, which primarily contribute to their biological functions, especially the exosome secretion function [44, 45]. A large number of studies have shown that exosomes derived from mesenchymal stem cells can secrete a variety of cytokines, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-10 (IL-10), and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), to promote angiogenesis and immune regulation [16, 46]. However, at present, the extraction of exosomes mainly relies on ultracentrifugation purification, which is time-consuming and laborious. The latest groundbreaking advancement lies in the enhanced mimicry of the authentic in vivo environment through 3D culture technology to acquire mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes. Compared to conventional 2D culture, 3D cell culture can produce three orders magnitude more EVs and could save time, labor, and reagents [19]. Our previous study also showed that 3D cell culture could enhancing exosome efficacy [23, 24]. By obtaining cost-effective exosomes with enhanced therapeutic efficacy, the clinical application of exosome therapy becomes more feasible (Supplement 1). We identified 3d-Exo through their morphological structure, particle size, and surface markers (Supplement 2). As shown in Fig. 4, 3d-Exo reversed the decline in endothelial cell migration and tube formation capabilities induced by a high-glucose environment.

The complicated microenvironments in diabetic wounds were mainly occurred in the dermal layer. Prior studies have demonstrated that therapeutic agents require targeted delivery to deeper tissue layers (e.g., the dermal stratum of cutaneous lesions) for optimal therapeutic outcomes [47]. However, current wound dressings predominantly function through superficial wound contact mechanisms, limiting their therapeutic efficacy in penetrating deeper pathological structures [48]. The microneedle tips measuring hundreds of micrometers in length not only provide mechanical traction for tissue regeneration but also enable targeted delivery of therapeutic agents to the dermal layer, thereby significantly enhancing wound healing efficacy [49]. Ryallet al. developed a microneedle patch and evaluated the drug delivery efficacy of dissolved and hydrogel microneedle patches [50]. Results demonstrated that both types of patches effectively penetrated biofilms, with dissolving microneedles exhibiting superior drug delivery efficiency and more pronounced wound-healing promotion, which is likely due to their complete dissolution in the wound and release the drug almost completely.

Herein, we present a novel 3d-Exo MN for diabetes wound healing. Our experimental findings demonstrate that the microneedles exhibited superior mechanical strength, successfully penetrating murine skin tissue and generating aligned microchannels within the dermal layer, thereby demonstrating optimal skin tissue permeability and sufficient tip length (Fig. 2). Quantitative analysis of PKH26 fluorescence-labeled imaging further confirmed the homogeneous distribution of exosomes across the microneedle array structure (Fig. 2e, f). Comprehensive characterization of the microneedle system revealed its dual therapeutic functionality: not only enabling sustained exosome release (Fig. 2h) but also demonstrating controlled antimicrobial agent release capabilities (Fig. 3a). Crucially, cytocompatibility assessments (Fig. 3b) demonstrated negligible cytotoxicity toward normal cells (HUVEC, Hacat, Hdf). In vitro assays (proliferation, migration, and tube formation assay) unveiled a dose-dependent attenuation of endothelial cell viability under hyperglycemic conditions. Notably, comparative analysis established that 3d-Exo rescued the angiogenic ability of HUVECs which had been suppressed by high glucose compared to their 2d-Exo. This investigation further examined their therapeutic effects in a diabetic mouse model, which shown a more significant promotion of wound healing associated with collagen deposition, epithelial regeneration, and angiogenesis. Notably, at Day 14, the number of mature blood vessels reduced, which may be associated with the wound’s transition from the proliferative phase into the remodeling phase. These findings are consistent with our previous finding that 3d-Exos promote wound healing and reduced number of blood vessels at Day 14 in a mouse burn model [23].

Although the substantial progress achieved in current investigations, some limitations persist. Our findings establish that 3d-Exo MN significantly enhance diabetic wound healing efficacy, potentially mediated through exosome-induced pro-angiogenic effects and Mupirocin-induced antibiosis effects. It is well-documented that 3D culture conditions can alter proteins content [19]. 3d-Exos had a greater variety of proteins (1023VS605) compared with 2d-Exo. These bioactive molecules likely activate key signaling pathways such as PI3K/Akt and ERK, which are central to endothelial cell survival and angiogenesis [51]. However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying 3d-EXO-mediated angiogenesis regulation, along with the compositional disparities between 3d-Exo and 2d-Exo, remain to be elucidated through systematic proteomic profiling and functional genomic analyses.

Conclusion

In this study, we have developed a controllable 3d-Exo MN patch loading with exosomes and Mupirocin to promote diabetes wound healing. 3D culture technology improves exosome yield and therapeutic efficacy, while MN systems enable efficient transdermal drug delivery with minimal invasiveness. Within this architecture, we have optimally integrated the advantages of 3d-Exo and MN systems, which enhanced diabetic wound healing by preventing infection and promoting angiogenesis. We anticipate that such 3d-Exo MN patch will have great application prospects in tissue repair and regenerative medicine in the future.
