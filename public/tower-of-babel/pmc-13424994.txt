Potential new targets for rheumatic diseases
Authors: Juergen Braun, Falk Hiepe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
There are now many established and new targets for the treatment of autoimmune diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis and connective tissue diseases. Treatment with conventional (c), biologic (b) and targeted synthetic (ts) disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs has become a daily routine in rheumatologic care, mainly but not only in secondary and tertiary centres. In addition, specialists in oncology, gastroenterology, dermatology, and others are using drugs targeting a whole spectrum of antigens in the immune system. This narrative review presents and shortly discusses several new targets and agents of potential use to treat rheumatic musculoskeletal diseases.

INTRODUCTION

Immune-mediated inflammation is of central significance in autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and this is also the case in other areas such as dermatology (psoriasis), gastroenterology (Crohn's disease), and neurology (multiple sclerosis). There is evidence that it is possible to attack inflammation on different levels. Thus, established targets in RA and partly also in SLE are T and B cells, such as abatacept and rituximab, different cytokines such as tumour necrosis factor (TNF)α, interleukin (IL)-1, IL-6, interferon (IFN)α, and enzymes such as the Janus kinases (JAK). These agents are biologic (b) or targeted synthetic (ts) disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), which means that they are capable of reducing radiographic damage to cartilage and bone of affected joints [1]. The well-established‚ anchor drug for RA, methotrexate (MTX), is a conventional (c)DMARD that works by acting on folic acid metabolism. Less specific anti-inflammatory effects of frequently used drugs such as glucocorticoids (GCs) and nonsteroidal antirheumatic drugs (NSAIDs) are generally considered to be rather symptom modifying, but there are data suggesting DMARD capacity for GC in RA and for NSAIDs in axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA).

The first clearly defined target in rheumatology that finally made it into daily clinical practice was TNF. Almost all TNFs are homologous to the archetypal TNFα, which, primarily produced by activated macrophages, induces inflammation by binding to its receptors on other cells. TNF is a member of the TNF superfamily, a protein family of cytokine receptors characterised by their ability to bind TNF. The TNF superfamily contains 19 members that bind to 29 members of the TNF receptor (TNFR) superfamily [2]. The binding of TNF to TNFR1 and TNFR2 leads to inflammatory signals triggered within the target cells. Most TNFR form trimeric complexes in the plasma membrane, which contain transmembrane domains, but some can be cleaved into soluble forms such as TNFR1. While soluble TNF can only activate TNFR1, transmembrane TNF can activate both TNFR1 and TNFR2. TNFRs are primarily involved in apoptosis and inflammation, but they can also take part in other signal transduction pathways, such as proliferation, survival and differentiation. TNFRs are expressed in a wide variety of tissues, especially in leukocytes. In humans, there are 29 known family members of the TNFR superfamily [2]. Cytokines related to TNF provide a communication network essential for coordinating multiple cell types into an effective host defence system against pathogens and malignant cells. Inhibition of TNF is an established tool for the treatment of RA and other rheumatic musculoskeletal diseases (RMDs). While the fusion protein etanercept mimics the inhibitory effects of naturally occurring soluble TNFRs with higher potency than these, monoclonal antibodies such as infliximab directly target TNF. The therapeutic potential is partly similar but also shows some clinically relevant differences.

This is just one example of an important pathway in the immune system. Members of the TNFR superfamily are also important when potential new targets [3] to treat rheumatic diseases are discussed in the next sections.

POTENTIAL NEW TARGETS

JAK inhibition

There are 4 main subtypes of JAKs, including JAK1, JAK2, JAK3 and tyrosine kinase 2 (Tyk2). By inhibiting JAK, multiple cytokine signals and inflammatory pathways are simultaneously suppressed, reducing inflammation and immune system activation, which is associated with slower progression of the disease [4]. Currently, there are following 5 JAK inhibitors: (i) approved for treating RA [4]: tofacitinib, baricitinib, upadacitinib, filgotinib, and peficitinib. These JAKi vary in their selectivity for different JAK isoforms [4,5]. There is emerging evidence that suggests a role for JAK-signal transducer and activators of transcription (STAT) signalling in the evolution of pain that is unrelated to nociceptive stimuli such as inflammation and joint damage [6]. The biological mechanisms responsible for the widely discussed safety issues related to the use of JAKi in patients at increased cardiovascular risk compared to bDMARDs have been recently discussed in detail [7].

TKs are enzymes responsible for the activation of many proteins by signal transduction cascades. TKs are a group of around 90 enzymes capable of phosphorylating the amino acid tyrosine on another protein, which leads to conformational changes and activation of that protein. They are included in a large family of 518 kinases in the human ‘kinome’. Some TK are cell-surface receptors, whereas the nonreceptor TK serve as intracellular signal transducers, ensuring that binding of a transmembrane receptor for cytokines, growth factors, and others by its cognate ligands leads sequentially activated to transcription in the nucleus [8]. A multitude of pharmaceutical drugs inhibiting TK, named TK inhibitors (TKi), have been developed with the main aim of fighting malignancy. More than 50 TKi have been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) since 2001, when imatinib was first approved. Nintedanib, a small molecule TKi binding to growth factor receptors, inhibits the proliferation of fibroblasts, which is also possibly relevant for rheumatic diseases. Thus, the drug has been approved not only to treat idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, but also for systemic sclerosis-associated [9] and progressive phenotype interstitial lung disease.

Bruton’s TK inhibition

Bruton's TK (BTK), a nonreceptor TK, is a cytoplasmic protein that plays a key role in signalling pathways downstream of diverse surface receptors in B cells and myeloid cells, which include the B-cell receptor (BCR) itself, Fc receptors including FcεRI, toll-like receptors, and chemokine receptors. Thus, BTK is an important component of the BCR signalling pathway, which plays a critical role in B-cell activation [10]. BTKs are approved for chronic lymphatic leukaemia [11]. Fenebrutinib, a selective inhibitor of BTK, has shown efficacy in randomised trials in RA in patients with an inadequate response to MTX. In a subsequent phase II randomized controlled trial (RCT), patients with RA continuing MTX were randomised to receive fenebrutinib, adalimumab or placebo. The results showed comparable efficacy [12]. The drug is not approved for a rheumatic disease.

A novel dual inhibitor that potently blocks BTK and JAK3, named Wj1113, inhibits B-cell activation and suppresses JAK3-dependent STAT5 phosphorylation, shown to reduce proinflammatory cytokine secretion and monocyte chemotaxis in vitro [13].

Spleen tyrosine kinase inhibition

The cytoplasmic nonreceptor TK spleen TK (SyK) plays a pivotal role in the intracellular signalling cascades downstream of immune receptors, particularly within B cells, Fc receptors and integrins. Through these pathways, SyK modulates activation, proliferation and cytokine release of immune cells, suggesting pathogenetic relevance, not only in RA [14]. The orally bioavailable SyK inhibitor fostamatinib, initially developed under the compound name R788, has received FDA approval for the treatment of chronic immune thrombocytopenia. In preclinical models of arthritis, it was shown to work against joint damage due to inflammation. In human immune-mediated disorders, a phase II RCT included patients with RA with inadequate responses to MTX, showing that adjunctive fostamatinib therapy led to reduced disease activity [15], but long-term therapy was associated with safety issues [16]. The drug is not approved for any rheumatic disease to date.

Nonreceptor tyrosine-protein kinase TYK2 has been implicated in IFN-α, IL-6, IL-10, and IL-12 signalling. Deucravacitinib is the first oral TYK2 inhibitor that has been approved for adult patients with moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis after having shown a consistent efficacy and safety profile with a dose of 6 mg/d [17], the drug seems to work also for psoriatic arthritis (PsA) [18].

However, the targeted oral peptide icotrokinra (JNJ-77242113), which selectively binds the IL-23 receptor (200 mg/d), has shown, recently compared with deucravacitinib in psoriasis, superior response rates [19].

NOD-, LRR- and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 inflammasome inhibitors

Inflammasomes are multiprotein signalling platforms that detect molecular patterns, which may be pathogen- or damage-associated, through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs). The NOD-, LRR-, and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome contributes to innate activation of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β [20]. The inflammasome complex consists of a sensor PRR, the adaptor protein named ASC (apoptosis associated speck-like protein containing a caspase recruiting domain CARD) and procaspase-1, which, upon assembly, leads to the proteolytic activation of caspase-1, which drives the processing and secretion of members of the IL-1 cytokine family, such as IL-1β and IL-18, which have both integral regulatory functions for both innate and adaptive immunity. Thus, the NLRP3 inflammasome plays a central role in gout and potentially also in active osteoarthritis. In gout, urate crystals activate NLRP3, hereby driving IL-1β release and arthritis [21]. In a phase IIa RCT, the selective oral NLRP3 inhibitor dapansutrile (OLT1177) has been shown to reduce joint pain and swelling in acute gout flares [22].

Regulatory T cells (Tregs) and immune checkpoints

The discovery of regulatory T cells governed by the gene Foxp3, which play an important role in peripheral immune tolerance, has just been awarded the Nobel Prize in Medicine in 2025. Today, enhancing the function of Tregs or targeting immune checkpoints are major strategies for the modulation of immune tolerance by promoting Tregs and suppressing autoreactive T cells. The programmed death-1 (PD-1) protein is an immune checkpoint that mediates T-cell suppression upon binding to its ligand, PD-L1 [23]. Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis have been successfully used in oncology by enhancing antitumor immunity (Fig 1). However, their use is often accompanied by immune-related adverse events [23]. Indeed, treatment of cancer with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) is frequently associated with musculoskeletal side effects such as arthritis, which occurs in about 10% of patients. In consequence, reverse strategies, namely the agonistic engagement of PD-1 to suppress autoreactive immune activity, have been investigated in RA. Peresolimab, a humanised agonistic mAb targeting PD-1, has been developed to take advantage of this immunoregulatory mechanism. In a phase IIa RCT, patients with RA refractory to c-, b- or ts-DMARDs were treated with 700 mg or 300 mg of peresolimab, or placebo administered intravenously every 4 weeks. The primary endpoint, a change in disease activity score 28-CRP from baseline to week 12, was met in this study [25]. Another PD-1 agonist, Rosnilimab, is in development (Fig 1) [26]. The development of peresolimab has been stopped because of malignancy cases.

CTLA-4 is an inhibitory receptor on T cells that can potentially switch off activation of the immune system. It works by competing with its costimulatory receptor CD28 for binding to B7 proteins such as CD80 or CD86 on other immune cells, which transmits a negative signal to T cells. By binding to its ligands, CTLA-4 downregulates T-cell proliferation by preventing the second signal required for activation of T cells, whereas the first one comes from major histocompatibility complex (MHC) antigens [27]. Abatacept is a fusion protein composed of the Fc region of immunoglobulin IgG1 fused to the extracellular domain of CTLA-4. It binds to CD80 and CD86 molecules, hereby preventing activation of T cells [28]. Abatacept has long been approved for RA and PsA.

Targeting OX40 and CD40

Two other key members of the TNFR superfamily, OX40 (CD134) and its ligand OX40L (CD252), are also critical in regulating immune responses. OX40 is expressed on activated T cells and serves as a costimulatory receptor during T-cell activation. OX40/OX40L signalling contributes to chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation in autoimmune diseases [29], where its role relates to the amplification and persistence, particularly of CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses, including the survival and expansion of autoreactive cells in Th1 and Th17 cell compartments. There are several antagonistic antibodies, such as rocatinlimab, amlitelimab, and telazorlimab, which are now in clinical development, especially for atopic dermatitis [30].

The type I transmembrane protein CD40 found on antigen-presenting cells is required for their activation. CD40 and its ligand CD40L (CD154) are both members of the TNFRs superfamily. Their assembly on T cells activates antigen-presenting cells and induces several downstream effects. The complex is expressed not only on many immune cells but also on stromal and structural cells, including fibroblasts, epithelial and endothelial cells. CD40L is an important costimulatory molecule that induces proinflammatory signals. This seems to play a role in the pathogenesis of SLE, RA, and Sjögren’s disease [31].

A modulator of T-cell–dependent B-cell activation, Dazodalibep (VIB4920), a recombinant fusion protein designed to inhibit the CD40-CD40L interaction, has recently been successfully investigated in Sjögren’s disease [32] and in RA [33].

The mAb dapirolizumab pegol works by targeting and binding to CD40L, which helps to reduce the activation of B cells. It also limits the production of type 1 IFN, a protein with pathogenetic significance in SLE. Indeed, overexpression of a characteristic pattern of IFN-stimulated genes, termed IFN signature, has been detected in SLE. The antibody has been investigated in SLE, but the primary objective was not met [34].

Other antibodies targeting CD40 are the IgG4 mAb bleslumab, and the mAb iscalimab (CFZ533), which has been successfully studied in Sjögren’s disease [35].

Targeted B, plasma cell, and T-cell therapies

Obinutuzumab acts similarly to rituximab and ofatumumab as an anti-CD20 mAb by blocking the activation of B cells. This active ingredient is the first second-generation monoclonal CD20 antibody that has been optimised by use of glycoengineering (glycosylation) technology that leads to a significantly stronger B-cell depletion [36]—with obvious clinical relevance [37]. Obinutuzumab is not only approved for certain types of leukaemia and lymphoma but also for lupus nephritis, since a recent RCT showed significant benefit [38].

The other prominent B-cell marker, CD19, being the coreceptor of the BCR complex, is a major regulator of B-cell activation, and therefore another important target for immune interventions. IgG4-related disease is a multiorgan, relapsing, fibroinflammatory, immune-mediated disorder. The first drug approved for its treatment is the mAb inebilizumab that targets CD19. The drug has been shown to reduce the risk of flares by increasing the likelihood of flare-free complete remission of IgG4-related disease at 1 year. Furthermore, it is indicated for the treatment of patients with neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders who are anti-aquaporin 4 IgG antibody positive and myasthenia gravis [39].

The human IgG1 mAb belimumab that selectively binds soluble B-cell–activating factor (BAFF), hereby preventing binding to BCR, was the first biologic drug approved in SLE, now also for children aged ≥5 years. Studies show that remission or low disease activity, including a reduction in GC dosage, can be achieved more frequently with belimumab, which also seems to prevent the onset of organ damage [40]. It has been intensively studied as a sequential therapy following rituximab in SLE [41]. Although it does not offer additional benefit in patients with active SLE responding to conventional therapy [42], it appears to be a promising option for patients refractory to conventional immunosuppression [43]. Belimumab is now also approved for lupus nephritis [44].

Atacicept is a fully human recombinant fusion protein consisting of the Fc region of human IgG1 and the binding portion of transmembrane activator and calcium-modulator and cyclophilin ligand. The latter, named transmembrane activator (TACI) and calcium modulating ligand (CAML) interactor, also known as TNFR superfamily member 13B, is able to bind the cytokines BAFF (also termed B-lymphocyte stimulator or BlyS) and a proliferation-inducing ligand (APRIL). Atacicept acts as a ‘decoy receptor’ for BAFF and APRIL by binding soluble APRIL, soluble BAFF, and membrane-bound BAFF [45]. Atacicept was recently shown to reduce proteinuria in IgA nephropathy [46].

Telitacicept is a TACI-Fc fusion protein that has already been approved in China [47]. Clinical trials, including a phase 3 study in SLE, have shown the efficacy of the drug [48]. In a phase III RCT, patients with RA with an inadequate response to MTX have been enrolled.

Recent studies have revealed the central role of B cells also in the pathogenesis of Sjögren’s disease [49]. BAFF is a trimeric membrane-bound or soluble factor that binds to 3 receptors: B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA), TACI and the BAFF receptor (BAFF-R)—all expressed on B cells. TACI mediates signalling through BAFF and through the proliferation-inducing ligand APRIL, both members of the TNF superfamily. BAFF and APRIL, sharing TACI and BCMA, promote B-cell maturation and immunoglobulin production. Certain B cells are responsible for producing autoantibodies such as anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB through overactivation of the BAFF/BCR [49].

The mAb Ianalumab works by targeting the BAFF receptor, leading to profound depletion of B cells. In addition, it might prevent reactivation of residual B-cell clones in the target tissues. Based on very recent data [50] showing improvement not only in patients’ global assessment but also in stimulated salivary flow rates and autoantibody levels, which numerically improved in the 300 mg every 4 weeks group, Ianalumab may indeed become the first targeted treatment approved for patients with Sjögren’s disease [50].

Plasma cells are white blood cells originating from lymphoid organs that secrete antibodies in response to the presentation of specific antigens, including autoantigens. The glycoprotein CD38 is highly expressed on plasma cells. Depletion of plasma cells is an important aim of immunosuppressive interventions. This can be achieved with different approaches and techniques. For example, it is definitely possible with daratumumab, a human mAb that targets CD38, a drug that is approved for multiple myeloma. Long-lived plasma cells secreting autoantibodies are known to play a role in the pathogenesis of SLE, but they do not respond to standard immunosuppression. Treatment of 2 patients with life-threatening SLE induced impressive clinical responses, which were maintained with belimumab. A major depletion of long-lived plasma cells and reduction of IFN I activity in these cases was reported [51]. The data were recently confirmed in 10 patients in a single-arm phase 2 trial [52].

Bortezomib is a specific, reversible inhibitor of the 20S subunit of the proteasome, a cellular complex that breaks down unfolded proteins, which works best in malignant cells. The drug is approved for multiple myeloma, but it is not used first-line due to substantial side effects. In some severe cases of SLE, bortezomib has been used with some success [53], especially for renal involvement. The calcineurin inhibitor voclosporin has been recently approved for lupus nephritis [54]. Its effectiveness, metabolic stability, and safety are superior to the long-established drug ciclosporin, which is not much used in rheumatology anymore. Voclosporin is currently not available in Germany and some other European countries.

Chimeric antigen receptor T cells and T-cell engagers

B and T cells are well known to collaborate, which is also relevant for the development of autoimmune diseases. Historical approaches have tried to disturb pathologic B-T-cell interactions by targeting different pathways with drugs such as abatacept and dapirolizumab with limited impact on B-cell depletion, whereas the majority of patients with RA, SLE, and multiple sclerosis have rather benefited from B-cell depletion with anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies such as rituximab, ocrelizumab (approved for multiple sclerosis), or ofatumumab. Refractory courses of autoimmune disease are associated with incomplete B-cell depletion and an increased frequency of IgD-CD27+ switched memory B cells, CD19+CD20- B cells and plasma cells that are not directly targeted by anti-CD20 antibodies, while most lymphoid tissue plasma cells express CD19 [55]. Importantly, any B-T-cell interaction mainly occurs in lymphoid tissues and at inflamed sites, where B-cell depletion may not be sufficient due to limited access to pathogenetically relevant effector cells.

There are 2 novel strategies with promising results, now also in rheumatology (Fig 2): therapies using chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cells and T-cell engagers (TCEs), which recruit T cells to induce B-cell cytotoxicity [[56], [57], [58], [59]]. Promising results have been reported for anti-CD19 [56] and anti-BCMA [57] CAR T-cell therapies, the CD19 TCE blinatumomab and the BCMA TCE teclistamab [58] as well as CD20 TCE such as mosunetuzumab, glofitamab, or epcoritamab in cancer [55,58]. Bispecific TCEs are engineered antibodies that redirect T cells to mediate tumour cell killing by simultaneously binding to CD3 on T cells and tumour-associated antigens [59]. Neutralising IFNγ has been shown to improve safety without compromising the efficacy of CAR T-cell therapy in a mouse model [60].

Taken together, there is increasing evidence that anti-CD19 CAR T-cell and TCE therapies are also effective in managing refractory autoimmune disease. Thus, using T cells as effector cells to profoundly ablate B cells seems a promising strategy for severe autoimmune disease.

However, not only are cell depletion strategies being studied, but there is also potential for tolerance-restoring strategies such as regulatory T-cell-based therapies and the use of tolerogenic dendritic cells.

Targeting the T-cell receptor

Autoimmunity is potentially driven by memory T-cell and B-cell clones inappropriately targeting self-antigens [61]. Autoimmune therapy could work by selective depletion or suppression of self-reactive T cells, if disease-associated T-cell receptors (TCRs) and cognate antigenic epitopes are recognised. Fifty years after the clear association of HLA B27 with ankylosing spondylitis (AS) was described, the arthritogenic peptide model has remained one of the most plausible explanations of this disease [62]. It proposes that AS cytotoxic CD8+ T-cell lymphocytes (CTL) that are cross-reactive to both B27-restricted self-antigen and antigen from an infectious agent, initially sensitised by an infection, are responsible for an autoimmune response against self [62]. This hypothesis was supported by the finding of additional genetic associations of AS with endoplasmatic reticulum aminopeptidase (ERAP)1 and ERAP2 genes whose protein products shape the repertoire of peptides presented by HLA-B27 [63]. Indeed, a TRBV9-containing CD8+ TCR motif was recently reported to be associated with the pathogenesis of AS and anterior uveitis, and cognate HLA-B*27-presented epitopes were identified [64]. After the first case report of human TRBV9+ T-cell elimination in a patient with AS who achieved remission within 3 months and ceased anti-TNF therapy after 5 years of continuous use [65], a large, reportedly successful trial was performed in Russia, published in Russian [66]. The conclusion at this point in time is that these fascinating results need to be independently confirmed [62,64].

Targeting the neonatal Fc receptor

The neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) is a heterodimeric protein that protects immunoglobulin G (IgG) and albumin from intracellular degradation, thereby prolonging their serum half-life. The FcRn binds IgG to maintain its serum levels, including pathogenic autoantibodies. FcRn inhibitors such as the antibody fragment efgartigimod are able to reduce circulating IgG and autoantibody levels, which is clinically effective in antibody-mediated diseases. Efgartigimod is approved for the treatment of patients with anti-acetylcholine receptor antibody-positive myasthenia gravis [67]. It also seems to be promising in other autoimmune diseases such as chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy, myositis, stiff men syndrome, immune thrombocytopenia, and antibody-mediated rejection in renal allograft recipients.

Similar clinical effects can be achieved with drugs that can cleave antibodies. Thus, a cysteine protease derived from the IgG-degrading enzyme of Streptococcus pyogenes, Imlifidase, rapidly degrades IgG. It is approved for the desensitisation treatment of highly sensitised adult kidney transplant patients with positive cross-match results to an available deceased donor [68]. Clinical trials are currently being conducted on Guillain-Barré syndrome, antiglomerular basement disease, and anti-neutrophil cytoplasmatic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis.

Targeting the complement system

The complement system is a central component of the humoral defence of the innate immune system. It consists of >30 soluble protein molecules, which either circulate in the blood or are bound to cell membranes. There are 3 ways to activate this immune process. The alternative complement signalling pathway, which is responsible for most of the actions of the whole complement system, plays a key role in the pathogenesis of ANCA-associated vasculitis [69]. This pathogenic pathway can be blocked by the small molecule avacopan, a human C5aR1/CD88 antagonist. The efficacy and safety of this compound were evaluated in a large phase 3 trial, conducted in 331 patients with newly diagnosed or recurrent granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) or microscopic polyangiitis. Patients were randomised to receive either oral avacopan 30 mg/d two times a day or oral prednisone 60 mg/d tapered over 20 weeks until discontinuation. All patients received induction therapy with either weekly rituximab administered over a period of 4 weeks or cyclophosphamide followed by azathioprine. Patients with worsening disease or reasons unrelated to disease could receive glucocorticoids (GCs) [70]. The study showed that blockade of the alternative complement pathway with avacopan in combination with immunosuppression enables patients with ANCA-associated vasculitis to reduce their GC exposure without compromising efficacy or safety. Subgroup analyses demonstrated benefits for avacopan in patients with a variety of organ involvements, disease stages and age groups. The greatest benefits were seen in patients with renal impairment, pulmonary manifestations and disease relapses. Avacopan is approved for the treatment of ANCA-associated vasculitis [63,64].

Targeting interferons

Anifrolumab, a fully human IgG1 mAb that targets several type I IFNs through binding to the type I IFN receptor subunit 1, is the second biologic that has been approved by the FDA and European Medical Agency for the treatment of SLE. Anifrolumab has been shown to promote low disease activity or remission, while reducing GC use and preventing organ damage [[71], [72], [73], [74]]. Anifrolumab seems to work especially well for lupus-associated skin disease; it is currently being studied in lupus nephritis.

The monoclonal antibody Emapalumab works by neutralising IFNγ. It is the first FDA-approved treatment for primary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, a rare condition characterised by severe inflammation [75,76]. Emapalumab has not been studied in SLE to date. Dazukibart, an IFNβ-specific monoclonal antibody, has been successfully studied in adults with dermatomyositis in a multicentre, double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled, phase 2 trial [77].

The plasmacytoid dendritic cell-specific type II C-type lectin blood dendritic cell antigen (BDCA)-2 is a potent inhibitor of IFN α/β induction [78]. The monoclonal antibody litifilimab targets BDCA2 to prevent the production of IFNs, which may help to reduce symptoms in both cutaneous lupus [79] and SLE [80].

Antigranulocyte–monocyte colony-stimulating factor antibodies

The granulocyte–monocyte colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) plays a critical role in the differentiation and activation of haematopoietic cells, particularly granulocytes and monocytes. GM-CSF has also been associated with immune system dysregulation. The anti-GM–CSF receptor mAb mavrilimumab has demonstrated promising results in clinical trials for RA [81]. Other antibodies targeting GM-CSF, such as namilumab and otilimab, have also been evaluated in phase II studies in RA. None of these has been approved to date.

Mavrilimumab has also been studied in giant cell arteritis (GCA) with some steroid-sparing success [82]. This was also reported for the IL-17a inhibitor secukinumab [83] approved for spondyloarthritides. However, a recent larger study in GCA did not meet the primary endpoint [84]. Nevertheless, successful use of secukinumab after failure of the IL-6R antagonist tocilizumab, which is approved for GCA, has been reported [85]. According to a recent RCT, upadacitinib also seems to be useful in GCA [86], not only in refractory cases (case report) [87].

Inhibition of nuclear factor-kappa B

Iguratimod, a csDMARD approved for RA in Japan in 2012, suppresses B-cell activity by inhibiting proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and IL-17 and downregulates nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signalling [88]. Many clinical trials in RA have demonstrated that iguratimod, either as monotherapy or in combination with MTX, has superior clinical efficacy compared to MTX alone [88]. Iguratimod has also been studied in AS, palindromic rheumatism, and Sjögren’s disease [89].

Cenerimod is a treatment that targets a protein called sphingosine 1-phosphate receptor 1 (S1P1). Blockade of S1P1 reduced the flow of T and B cells into the bloodstream. However, the first RCT has not met the primary endpoint [90]

The tyrosine phosphatase SHP1 is a protein tyrosine phosphatase that contains an SH2 domain and is expressed primarily in haemopoietic cells. The antihuman CD22 monoclonal antibody SM03 operates by enhancing SHP-1 signalling and reducing BCR-driven NF-κB activation [91], hereby limiting B-cell responses. A phase II RCT in patients with RA on MTX showed superior ACR20 responses at 24 weeks in SM03-treated patients compared to placebo [91].

Inhibition of mitochondrial complex I

Leramistat (MBS2320), a small molecule that targets mitochondrial complex I with preferential activity on osteoclasts, takes advantage of a dual mechanism of action, including anti-inflammatory and antiresorptive activity by the modulation of osteoclast metabolism. In preclinical models, preservation of bone architecture and attenuation of arthritis have been reported. A phase II RCT has been conducted to assess leramistat in active patients with RA on MTX with some success [92].

Bispecific antibodies including GC receptors

The development of bispecific antibodies is not new. Combinations of anti-TNF and anti-IL-17 targets have already been studied some time ago [93,94]. However, an innovative approach has recently been developed to combine an anti-TNF-α antibody (adalimumab) with a GC receptor modulator—an attempt to deliver GC specifically to the inflammatory tissue and hereby minimise systemic side effects. Two such antibody-drug conjugates (ABBV-3373 and ABBV-154) have been tested in clinical trials for RA [95,96]. The latter is made up of adalimumab linked to a GC receptor modulator. In a phase 2b study [96] with 472 patients with RA, it was recently shown that significantly more patients achieved the primary endpoint (ACR50) at week 12 (26%-44%) than those on placebo (6.3%).

In another very recent phase 2 randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial with 207 patients with active PsA, treatment with sonelokimab, a nanobody that binds with a similarly high affinity to IL-17A and IL-17F [97], thereby inhibiting all dimers, showed significantly superior efficacy than in patients on placebo regarding most efficacy end points [98].

Bispecific antibodies are being developed that use controlled Fab-arm exchange [99]. In these antibodies, 1 antibody arm binds to a specific disease-relevant antigen, such as carbamylated proteins, while the other arm recruits endogenous complement regulators, such as factor H or C4b-binding protein. This approach seems to be capable of possibly achieving selective local targeted complement inhibition.

Other cell populations

Although we have largely focused on lymphoid and myeloid cells in this review, we would like to add that stromal and tissue-resident cell populations are also important as central contributors to chronic inflammation. This includes synovial fibroblast subsets, tissue-resident macrophages, and fibroblast-myeloid circuits.

Other important mechanisms

Chronic pain is a common problem in patients with RMD. Different mechanisms may play a pathogenetic role. Persistent inflammation leads to nociceptive pain, while neuropathic pain is caused by nerve damage of various origins. In contrast, nociplastic pain is not due to tissue damage or a lesion in the somatosensory nervous system as it is understood today. Nociplastic pain is based on an altered perception of pain through modulation of stimulus processing. The concept of central sensitisation, together with other neurobiological and psychosocial mechanisms, seems to be the best explanation for such pain conditions. The syndrome of fibromyalgia, today considered to be due to central sensitisation mechanisms, plays a major role in rheumatology—both in terms of differential diagnosis and because the management of inflammatory rheumatic diseases can be made more difficult by its simultaneous presence. The syndrome of long COVID has recently raised much interest in neuro-immune pathogenetic mechanisms.

New techniques

There are more potential compounds for intervention in the immune system [100,101].

Some are simultaneously targeting various proteins, such as IL6, TYK2, and CD20. Using a multitarget strategy Rutaecarpine, Hecogenin, Angustine, and Vomicine compounds were selected for simultaneous inhibition of IL6, TYK2, and CD20. While the first 3 inhibited all 3 targets with high affinity and stability, the fourth inhibited only IL-6 and TYK2 but not CD20 [102].

A codelivery system named MACP siTNF-α nanoparticles has been designed for the targeted delivery of drugs to inflamed joint sites. In this system, nanoenzymes and gene therapeutics sequentially modified with guanidinium-based polymers and macrophage membranes were integrated to achieve synergistic therapeutic effects [103]. These novel approaches may well play a role in future treatment strategies.
