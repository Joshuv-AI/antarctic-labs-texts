Introduction

Inequitable and disrespectful healthcare for women and infants is well‐established in many societies, with origins in religion, culture, and political ideology [1, 2]. The issue is controversial, as demonstrated, on the one hand, by progress and, on the other, by regression in the implementation of the Beijing Declaration. The latter is a comprehensive plan for achieving gender equality and women's empowerment, adopted at the Fourth World Conference on Women in 1995 and updated 30 years later at the United Nations Commission on the Status of Women, held in March 2025 [3]. The Declaration outlined 12 areas for action, encompassing aspects of women's rights such as political participation, economic empowerment, education, health, and the elimination of violence. Violation of women's rights and patriarchy have been emphasised by United Nations Secretary‐General António Guterres. Another aspect is the troubling view that equity and respect for women are obstacles to men and boys fulfilling their potential [4]. Such views reinforce restrictive gendered roles, increase polarisation of views, lead to the use of demonising and denigrating language towards women, and ignore their importance for infants.

This confrontational situation was exposed in the April 2025 meeting of the Venice Forum, an academic think‐tank focussing on the health and wellbeing of women and infants. Venice Forum includes a variety of relevant stakeholders, such as physicians (mainly obstetricians, paediatricians, neonatologists), developmentalists, psychologists, sociologists, health law specialists, economists and others. Participants examined equity and respectful care for women and infants from five points of view: the global context; economic issues; healthcare systems; legal and policy issues; and opportunity to benefit from biomedical research. This extended report‐narrative review comprises findings concerning the above issues—often from a UK point of view—opinions and comments on them and related further perspectives.

The Global Context

Maternal and Child Mortality

Disparities in maternal and infant mortality constitute extreme examples of the consequences of inequitable and disrespectful healthcare. Over 30 years ago Thaddeus and Maine described three factors leading to preventable maternal deaths, namely delays in the decision to seek care, ability to access healthcare, and receiving care [5]. These remain the drivers of around 260 000 maternal deaths worldwide in 2023 [6]. Similarly, globally each year, around 2.3 million infants die in the neonatal period, the majority from preventable causes [7].

The roots of this appalling situation are deeply embedded in socio‐cultural, political, and economic realities, and in racism and discrimination. This is also evidenced by higher mortality, higher preterm birth rates, and poorer outcomes in the United States and other high‐income countries, in Black and Asian women and their babies compared to other ethnicities [8].

Progress in reducing maternal, newborn and child mortality in the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) era has slowed substantially and, in some countries, has reversed. Only 30% of low‐ and middle‐income countries are now expected to achieve the SDG 3.1 target, namely, to reduce global maternal mortality to less than 70 per 100 000 live births by 2030 [9]. World Health Organisation (WHO) resolution 77 called for accelerated progress towards reducing maternal, newborn and child mortality in order to achieve SDG 3.1 and 3.2, but this is now unlikely to be realised given the drain on resources from current conflicts, the election of increasingly populist governments in several countries, reductions in development aid, and legislation that reverses previous progress on sexual and reproductive rights [10]. The focus of the WHO and other international agencies on maternal and infant mortality, and short‐term morbidities, as principal measures of success ignores the opportunities presented by recognition of the long‐term, often life‐long and trans‐generational benefits that could be seized by addressing the health and care inequities experienced by women and infants.

Health Policy

An important policy challenge is the promotion of universal healthcare as the primary route to improving population health [11]. It is well established that healthcare accounts for no more than half the variance in health in low‐income countries and substantially less in high‐income settings [12], with the remainder mainly shaped by education, clean water, safety and living conditions. However, this does not render healthcare investment inefficient or inequitable. On the contrary, it is precisely universal healthcare systems—free from commercial incentives—that are best positioned to integrate preventive approaches. Thus, universal healthcare and preventive/promotive measures for population health are not competing priorities but complementary ones. The Nordic model demonstrates that universal, free‐at‐point‐of‐care systems—particularly for maternal, infant and paediatric care—make a significant and measurable contribution to reducing maternal and infant mortality and morbidity.

Although investments in determinants of health are crucially important globally, investing in truly universal healthcare produces documented and notable benefits for pregnant women, infants, and young children and has been deemed the best return on investment strategies economically, compared to later investments in the life course. Given how much of later health is rooted in circumstances during pregnancy and infancy, any state would do well to provide high quality, respectful, culturally sensitive and universal services to pregnant women, infants and young children.

The medical professions have not necessarily risen to the challenge of addressing the inequities faced by mothers and babies: for example, the Lancet Commission on Investing in Health recommends sugar and tobacco taxation and social insurance as principal curbs upon the rising population prevalence of non‐communicable diseases but does not emphasise prevention through tackling adverse health trajectories that are established in fetal life and infancy [13].

Economic Issues: Models Exacerbate Inequities

The evidence that closing the gender health gap would bring wide societal benefit is accumulating. The McKinsey report launched at the World Economic Forum, Davos January 2024, calculated that closing the women's health gap could add $1 trillion to the global economy annually by 2040 [14]. Subsequent analysis indicates that the gender health gap for women costs the UK £36 billion yearly in lost productivity [15].

However, there are other considerations. The current focus of economic policy in most countries is on growth as measured by Gross Domestic Product (GDP). The many problems with this model in relation to health also affect women and infants disproportionately [16]. Thus, for example, women undertake the major proportion of domestic and childcare duties, usually as unremunerated labour which is not factored into GDP and therefore does not benefit from growth‐promoting incentives. Mothers are often further disadvantaged because aspects of childcare such as breastfeeding are also unremunerated and, by restricting their return to work, impose financial and career progression penalties. If women choose to return to work and do not breastfeed, their health and that of their infants are harmed [17]. The majority of countries do not provide adequate paid parental leave, nor the option of sharing leave with the non‐birthing parent. This is because parental leave is not seen as contributing to economic growth even though the positive impacts on infant wellbeing and family cohesion have obvious economic benefits [18]. A further example of the preoccupation with growth as measured by GDP is that policies to increase nursery placements for preschool children are promoted as a means to return parents to the workplace, and not as recognition of the economic—no less societal—value of parent‐delivered childcare [19]. There is growing recognition that incorporation of women's perspectives into economic theory and application can benefit societies [20].

Perversely, GDP includes outputs such as those from the fossil fuel, some highly processed food, and environmental polluting industries that are harmful to human health [21]. The private healthcare industry delivers unnecessary and potentially harmful practices such as elective caesarean section in the absence of clear medical indication and/or informed decision making, frenectomy for so‐called tongue‐tie, and helmet therapy for positional plagiocephaly. Adherence to GDP as a measure of progress is predicated on the assumption that wealth drives health, although this is clearly incorrect for many high‐income countries, most notably the US where increased growth has not prevented recent falling life expectancy [22, 23]. It is likely that continued focus on GDP in its current form will increasingly prove counterproductive to sustainable development. There is a strong case to be made that it is health that will ultimately drive wealth, indicating urgent need to reset political thinking [24].

Healthcare Systems: The UK Paradigm

Maternal and infant healthcare is complex, involving multiple components. There are consequently many leverage points at which improvements can be made. These include healthcare delivery, socioeconomic policy, and cultural attitudes. In many settings, healthcare professionals report low morale, an excess of administrative tasks and targets, and ineffective or openly antagonistic communication between midwives and doctors [25]. In the UK, the problem of inadequate healthcare for women has been recognised for some time in reports such as that from the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists in 2019 (Better for Women), which called for a National Health Service led women's health strategy, the Chief Medical Officer for England (Health of the 51%: women), and the NHS Confederation (Women's Economics: investing in the 51%) [26, 27].

However, these have had little impact and have either not been translated into healthcare policy, clinical guidelines, or practice standards, or have had implementation hindered by funding shortfalls. A 2025 review of the 223 active clinical guidelines from the UK National Institute of Health and Care Excellence (NICE) identified only one which had fully considered sex and gender for a specific subject area [28]. The actions needed at policy level were summarised in the Women's Health Plan in Scotland (2021) and Women's Health Strategy for England (2022) [29, 30] but these too have not been followed by adequate action or longer‐term resource commitment [31]. The rights of infants have received little attention; notable exceptions being the UK House of Lords 2024 report on preterm birth and the 2025 Lancet Child and Adolescent Health Commission on the Future of Neonatology [32, 33] though their impact has yet to be realised. Training healthcare professionals together through coupled programmes would assist in delivering equitable and respectful care, as would greater specificity on metrics of achievement, and clarity on accountability, incentives, sanctions, and legal frameworks. However, what is likely to be more powerful as a force for change is strong societal rejection of inequitable and disrespectful care and building a culture of learning instead of blaming in healthcare systems [34].

Legal and Policy Issues: Equitable and Respectful Care and the Law

Obstetric violence is increasingly recognised in law, policy, and academic literature. This term includes systemic forms of dehumanising, coercive, or neglectful treatment during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period [35].

Equivalent issues arise in relation to infant healthcare, such as unnecessary or potentially harmful practices like routine separation of mothers and babies and surgical procedures like circumcision without pain relief, despite recommendations [36]. However, this has not been recognised in terminology equivalent to “obstetric violence” [37].

While individual criminal liability for intentional harm may be warranted in some cases, a human rights‐based approach centres on state accountability [38]. Various interventions required by human rights bodies include improved resourcing of maternal health services, human rights training for healthcare professionals, public awareness campaigns, legal reforms to ensure free and informed consent, effective accountability mechanisms, and the integration of rights‐based principles into health policy [39].

Opportunity to Benefit From Biomedical Research: Involvement of Women and Infants in Biomedical Research

There is a long history of a predominance of male cell‐lines, animals and human participants in preclinical, translational, and population research. The scientific justification for this simplification is to reduce the complexities introduced by hormonal cycles. The ethical justification is the protection of women and babies from potentially harmful research procedures. A further challenge is fear of expensive litigation. Research participants require protection against potential harms, but interventions that will be used in women and infants also require a solid understanding of efficacy, dose, and safety [40, 41].

Male animals and cell‐lines are 5.5 times more likely than females to be included in preclinical studies [42], less than 30% of participants in phase 1 trials are women [43], and only 2.5% of clinical trials involve neonates [32]. COVID vaccines were withheld from pregnant women and young children despite lack of biological justification [44]. Major knowledge gaps exist in relation to a wide range of common health issues for women and there has been little investigation of sex‐linked effects on the fetus [45, 46]. The situation for infant research is worse with the majority of medications used in neonatal care prescribed off‐label or off‐licence, and only one medicine ever having been developed specifically for a neonatal condition (surfactant for neonatal respiratory distress syndrome) [47]. Most neonatal devices are hand‐me downs from older populations rather than having been developed specifically for newborn needs. The 2025 Lancet Child and Adolescent Health Commission on the Future of Neonatology describes a shocking example of disrespect for newborns, namely the premature closure of a phase 3 trial targeting bronchopulmonary dysplasia, a major cause of preterm morbidity, solely for business reasons [32].

It is unacceptable and short‐sighted that basic questions about the health and care of women and infants remain unaddressed. One approach to reversing this situation is to introduce policies and incentives at each stage of the research pipeline—funding, practice, publication, implementation, evaluation—that ensure that recruitment reflects population diversity and that data are disaggregated and reported by sex, gender and age. There are many examples of how the research pipeline can be improved for women's and infants' healthcare, from technologies such as telemedicine, point of care testing, remote monitoring and counselling, to community health worker training and nationwide provision of mother and baby health hubs [48].

Conclusions and Perspectives on a Complex Problem

Inequitable and disrespectful healthcare for women and infants is well‐established in many societies. Progress in reducing maternal, newborn and child mortality in the Sustainable Development Goal era has reversed in some countries. Although healthcare accounts for no more than half the variance in health in low‐income countries and substantially less in high‐income settings, healthcare investment remains efficient and equitable. GDP in relation to health affects women and infants disproportionately. Women undertake the major proportion of domestic and childcare duties including breastfeeding as unremunerated labour which is not factored into GDP and therefore do not benefit from growth‐promoting incentives. The majority of countries do not provide adequate paid parental leave, nor the option of sharing leave with the non‐birthing parent. Parental leave is not seen as contributing to economic growth even though the positive impacts on infant wellbeing and family cohesion have obvious economic benefits. In the UK, although the problem of inadequate healthcare for women has been recognised, it has not been translated into healthcare policy, clinical guidelines or practice standards, its implementation having been hindered by funding shortfalls. Further, the rights of infants have received little attention. Obstetric violence includes systemic forms of dehumanising, coercive, or neglectful treatment during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period. Though equivalent issues arise in relation to infant healthcare, this has not been recognised in equivalent terminology. Women and infants have much less opportunity than human males to benefit from biomedical preclinical, translational, and population research. Major knowledge gaps exist in relation to a wide range of common health issues for women and only one medicine ever has been developed specifically for a neonatal pathologic condition (surfactant for neonatal respiratory distress syndrome).

Inequity and disrespect to mothers and infants is a barrier to human progress and an example of a complex problem for which both underlying causes and solutions vary across societies and are the consequence of wider societal, political, and ideological attitudes. However, a universal principle applies, namely that multiple actions are needed in each location and domain. Recognition of the problem and the development of policies and legislation to ensure equitable and respectful care and monitor effectiveness are important, but insufficient. There is a need for wider recognition of the economic, societal, and personal consequences of inequitable and disrespectful care for women and infants. Positive messages, good examples, and proactive dissemination of accurate information on the universal benefits that come with the improved health of women and infants are recommended. Above all, there is a need for advocacy to motivate communities to reject the cultural mores that perpetuate these injustices.
