Fatigue Characterization Multimodal Datasets for Stereo Vision in Mixed Reality
Authors: Yan Wu, Chunguang Tao, Qi Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Head Mounted Displays (HMDs) employ mixed reality (MR) technologies across various fields, affording users immersive visual experiences. However, prolonged use of HMDs devices to watch stereoscopic content may lead to visual fatigue. This study explores stereo-visual fatigue induced by viewing MR visual stimuli, focusing on EEG and peripheral physiological signals. We recorded 24-channel EEG signals from 23 healthy participants and combined them with electrocardiogram (ECG), electrodermal activity (EDA), peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2), respiratory, and skin temperature data from 13 participants. These physiological signal characteristics were extracted and combined into multimodal datasets. These datasets have shown significant effects in identifying visual fatigue through experimental verification. The study highlights the need for publicly available datasets to further investigate stereo-visual fatigue in an MR environment.

Background & Summary

Mixed reality (MR) technology has been increasingly utilized in diverse emerging fields, owing to the distinctive fusion of reality and imagination, real-time interactivity, and precise matching1. However, studies have shown that prolonged use of head-mounted displays to view stereoscopic content can lead to visual fatigue2–4. The evaluation of visual fatigue is critical for the advancement of stereo display technologies. The extraction of features plays a pivotal role in identifying fatigue by capturing important information. These features, which are intricately related to the complex physiological and psychological responses5, have not been fully understood and require further investigation and analysis.

In recent years, several non-invasive neuroimaging-based methods have been used to explore fatigue characteristics6–8, such as electroencephalography (EEG) and peripheral physiological signals. Peripheral physiological signals, which include the electrocardiogram (ECG), electrodermal activity (EDA), respiratory rate (RESP), skin temperature (SKT), and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2)9, play a crucial role in monitoring and assessing the physiological state of individuals. EEG-based functional brain connectivity can reveal the brain mechanisms involved in human cognition and neurophysiological disorders10. Most studies have focused more on exploring the effects of brain synchronization on quantifying visual discomfort through EEG measurements11–13. In addition to EEG, an electrocardiogram (ECG) is also an effective signal to quickly understand the state of the autonomic nervous system (ANS), which is responsible for regulating heart activity and monitoring people’s fatigue8. Some studies have used the characteristics of the ECG in the time domain to assess visual fatigue14,15. Nevertheless, it remains a lack of research exploring the connection between various physiological indicators, including SpO2, EDA, heart rate (HR), respiratory rate, among others, and the occurrence of visual fatigue. Generally, integrating these multimodal characteristics is more beneficial for deep learning models to accurately identify fatigue than a single modality16.

There are no publicly available datasets on stereo-visual fatigue. Most studies use EEG to characterize stereo-visual fatigue from conventional displays17–19. Given the increasing portability of head-mounted displays (HMDs), the need for effective methods to assess visual fatigue is undeniable. Therefore, we designed a paradigm to record both EEG and peripheral physiologic signals to detect fatigue states in participants. In this paper, we recorded whole-brain 24-channel EEG signals from 23 healthy participants wearing a Microsoft HoloLens2 device to watch an experimental paradigm based on stereo depth movement. Among them, 13 participants agreed to record peripheral physiological signals including ECG, EDA, RESP, SKT, and SpO2. Thus, the dataset20,21 includes EEG data from 23 participants and peripheral physiological data from 13 participants. Modalities with distinct characteristics, derived from temporal or spatial domains, are employed to construct a variety of multimodal fusion datasets. These datasets are then input into classical neural networks and machine learning models to validate the effectiveness of the features.

Experimental material

The experimental stimuli come from a rotating green gem in the eye-tracking example of the mixed reality toolkit (MRTK). The entire stimulus scene was generated using Unity 3D (https://unity.cn/). Because individual participants had different sensitivities to fatigue triggered by varying speeds, a stereo-depth movement experimental paradigm was designed with six different motion speeds to identify an optimized speed for each participant that would induce visual fatigue. The six motion speeds were 1.30 m/s, 1.95 m/s, 2.60 m/s, 3.25 m/s, 4.45 m/s, and 5.00 m/s22. The participants mentioned above were invited to participate in the preparatory experiment. The preparatory experiment comprised 20 blocks. Participants had a 2-minute break between blocks. Each block included a start scene (Scene 0), preparation scenes (Scene 1), movement scenes (Scene 2), rating scenes (Scene 3 and Scene 4), and a rest scene (Scene 5). Each type of scene required participants to maintain their gaze for a specified duration before proceeding to the next scene in order. The detailed settings are shown in Table 2. Notably, In Scene 2, the gem performs depth movement at one speed randomly selected from the six motion speeds mentioned above, while maintaining a constant rotational speed throughout the scene. In Scene 3, the five-point scales represent, in order: severe, moderate, mild, normal, and relaxed. Similarly, in Scene 4, the five-point scales represent, in order: very tired, fatigued, slightly comfortable, comfortable, and very comfortable. The subjective rating data21 and relevant experimental paradigm code have been uploaded. Based on the results of the preparatory experiments, repeated-measures one-way ANOVA was performed to analyze individuals’ subjective evaluations at different speeds. The results (F = 0.341, p = 0.73) demonstrated that there were no significant differences in subjective evaluations across different speeds. As a result, we selected a moderate speed (3.25 m/s) as the speed parameter for the stereo depth motion experimental procedure.Table 2Scene information.Scene nameScene description informationScene 0Start the preparatory experimentScene 1Remind participants to focusScene 2The green gem rotated at fixed depth in two periodic uniform motion at one of the six random speedsScene 3Participants rated their “EyeStrain” levels using a five-point scaleScene 4Participants rated their “ComfortStrain” levels using a five-point scaleScene 5Remind participants to rest

Experimental procedures

The participants were asked to wear devices required for the experiment, see Fig. 1. Their task was to watch stereoscopic depth movements, with movement speed set at 3.25 m/s. Eye-tracking technology was used to confirm that participants were closely following the experimental material’s movements and to aid in subjective ratings. Specifically, eye-tracking technology was used in each experimental scenario. Participants interacted with the stimulus materials through this technology—only when their gaze stayed within the stimulus area for a preset duration would the interaction be deemed successful, allowing entry to the next scenario. This design ensured participants focused on the stimuli; after the experiment, all participants reported eye fatigue, indicating the experiment achieved its objective.Fig. 1Overview of the experiment. (left) Devices used in the experiment. (middle) Experimental environment. (right) Experimental protocol.

Overview of the experiment. (left) Devices used in the experiment. (middle) Experimental environment. (right) Experimental protocol.

Every participant was mandated to fully view 2 restful scenes and 15 movement scenes. Following each movement scene, a rating scene emerged to evaluate the content watched. Each movement scene consisted of 20 trials of reciprocal periodic movements at a depth of 8m. Participants rated their current comfort level on the same 5-point scale as used in the preparatory experiment (see Table 2, Scene 4). If the participant maintained their gaze on any square for 3 seconds, the square would turn red, indicating that the subjective evaluation was successful, and the experiment would proceed to the next scene. Otherwise, if the participant’s gaze shifted to another square the timer would reset, and the square would turn green. The whole experimental process took about 62 minutes, and both EEG and peripheral physiological signals were recorded. The resting-state data were collected before and after the exhibition of 15 movement scenes and then uploaded to the data sharing platform. See the Data Records section for details.

EEG recording

The NeuSen W Wireless EEG Acquisition System (developed by Neuracle Technology Corporation, Changzhou, China) consists of a 64-electrode cap, a Neuracle amplifier, and a multi-parameter synchronizer (trigger box). In this study, we selected 24 electrodes23 for EEG recording to construct the architecture of the functional brain network: Fp1, Fp2, AF3, AF4, F7, Fz, F8, FC5, FC6 (frontal); FT7, FT8 (temporal); C3, Cz, C4, CP3, CP4 (central); P3, Pz, P4, PO3, PO4 (parietal); O1, Oz, O2 (occipital). The EEG signal is sampled at a rate of 1000Hz, ensuring high-resolution data acquisition and the impedance of the selected electrodes is kept below 5 KΩ. In this study, EOG signals were not recorded. The experimental design focused on analyzing EEG activity from the specifically selected 24 electrodes to construct the functional brain network. Moreover, the experimental paradigm incorporated an eye-tracking interaction procedure to minimize eye-movement-related artifacts in EEG data.

Peripheral physiological signal recording

The peripheral physiological signal acquisition devices used in this study were smart wearable finger sensors and wireless ECG sensors produced by Beijing Jinfa Technology Co. Both the wireless ECG sensors and smart wearable finger sensors used in this study operate with a system sampling rate of 2048 Hz for signal acquisition. The peripheral physiological signals (ECG, EDA, SpO2, respiratory, skin temperature, and other signals) from the smart wearable sensors were transmitted realistically using 2.4 GHz wireless RF transmission technology and displayed in the ErgoLAB software (https://ergolab.cn/). As shown in Fig. 1, regarding the ECG sensor, ECG measurements were conducted utilizing a three-lead configuration: the red electrode (positive) was in the left midclavicular line, the blue electrode (negative) was in the left midaxillary line, and the black electrode (reference) was in the left 5th intercostal space. Electrode fixation was required to ensure that there was no interference with the participant and that the data quality was stable during the exercise. Regarding the finger sensor, after cleaning the skin of the finger, put the finger into the groove of the sensor to ensure that the belly of the finger is in full contact with the electrode, and adjust the strap to the appropriate tightness.

Data preprocessing and characteristic extraction

This experiment collected resting state physiological signals before and after prolonged exposure to stereoscopic depth movements in an MR environment to extract features related to comfort and fatigue. This section details the resting state data pre-processing and feature extraction steps. EEG data preprocessing was conducted using MATLAB R2013b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA), and pre-processing and analysis of peripheral physiological signals were performed utilizing the ErgoLAB wireless human factors physiological recorder.

During the experiment, we only processed data from the comfort and fatigue phases. Based on the 24 selected electrodes, the EEG data were band-pass filtered (0.5-30 Hz) to remove low-frequency noise and high-frequency interference, with a notch filter at 50 Hz to eliminate powerline noise. Subsequently, the signals were downsampled to 250 Hz. This specific sampling rate reduced the burden of subsequent data processing and storage. In addition, it conforms to the principle of minimal preprocessing, leaving sufficient room for researchers to conduct personalized preprocessing according to their own needs. Following downsampling, the data were segmented into 2s epochs with no overlap. The epochs were rigorously quality-checked, with corrupted or poor-quality epochs being discarded, and any electrodes were interpolated to ensure consistency.

An Independent Component Analysis (ICA) algorithm was run to isolate independent components in the data, which were then combined with the ICLABEL version 1.4 toolkit25 to identify and remove artefacts such as Electrocardiography (ECG) and Electromyography (EMG) interference. To further ensure the quality of the data by minimizing noise, abnormal values were identified and used to remove epochs with amplitudes exceeding ±100 μV. Data from all channels were utilized to calculate a common average reference.

EEG characteristic extraction

To analyze the inherent frequency components in the signals of all selected electrodes before and after viewing the stimulus scenes, we applied the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) to the data. This mathematical method converts time-domain signals into frequency-domain signals, thereby uncovering the spectral characteristics present in neural recordings. We defined the frequency bands of interest—Delta waves (0.5-4 Hz), Theta waves (4-8 Hz), Alpha waves (8-12 Hz), and Beta waves (13-30 Hz)—and classified neural oscillations according to their respective frequency ranges for subsequent EEG spectral power analysis.

A brain functional network was constructed for each participant. Our 24-electrode configuration—strategically covering the frontal, temporal, central, parietal, and occipital regions—was used to compute the topological properties of the brain network. As a medium-density setup (20-30 channels), it balances spatial coverage and analytical reliability.

The specific steps for constructing brain networks were as follows: First, the preprocessed EEG data were band-pass filtered, with the selected frequency bands being delta (0.5-4 Hz), theta (4-8 Hz), and alpha (8-13 Hz); Second, Equation (2) for calculating the phase-locking value (PLV) matrix was defined, which contains the functional connections between all possible electrode pairs; Third, the PLV matrix was sparsified by selecting a threshold. Given that there is currently no unified standard for determining thresholds26, previous research27–29 has shown that a sparse brain network composed of the top 20% of all connected electrode pairs typically maximizes the topological efficiency of the network. Therefore, in this study, the top 20% of PLV values were selected for sparsification under both comfortable and fatigued states. By calculating the average PLV values across different frequency bands, the results showed their distribution generally ranged from 0.72 to 0.76; Finally, a brain network was constructed based on the sparsified PLV matrix, where the topological properties of nodes and edges were computed using threshold intervals ranging from 0.72 to 0.76 with a step size of 0.01. Node properties include betweenness centrality (BC)30, clustering coefficient (CC)31, and node efficiency (NE)32, while edge properties include characteristic path length (CPL)33. Data Records

The raw EEG dataset is publicly accessible via the OpenNeuro platform (10.18112/openneuro.ds005416.v1.0.0)20, while other relevant datasets such as brain network datasets and multimodal feature datasets can be obtained through the Figshare platform (10.6084/m9.figshare.30359713)21. Public data is distributed under the the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International Public License (https://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/).

The original EEG dataset20 is structured in accordance with the Electroencephalography Brain Imaging Data Structure (EEG-BIDS) specification34,35—a standardized framework for organizing neuroimaging data. The dataset contains some regular BIDS files and 23 participants’ data folders. A set of standalone files provides a comprehensive overview of the dataset, which is intended to facilitate the reader’s understanding of some information during the data acquisition. For example, dataset_description.json is a JSON file depicting the information of the dataset, such as the name, dataset type and authors; participants.tsv contains participants’ information, such as age, sex and handedness; participants.json describes the column attributes in participants.tsv; README.md contains detailed information about the type of stimulus corresponding to each event.

The other relevant datasets21 contain nine folders: resting_state_eeg, eeg_spectral_characteristics, calculated_plv, brain_network_dataset, ergolab_raw_data, export_erolab_data, corpus, P12data and P36data. The resting_state_eeg folder contains EEG data processed using the full pre-processing pipeline and stored in .set file format. The preprocessing steps can be flexibly adjusted. The calculated frequency spectrum characteristics for each participant are displayed in the folder labeled eeg_spectral_characteristics. The calculated phase-locking values (PLVs) for individual participants and the group average PLVs are presented in the folder titled calculated_plv. The brain_network_dataset folder contains graph-structured datasets in .csv format. Each graph structure comprises 24 nodes (24 electrodes), each with three node properties (BC, CC, NE). The adjacency matrix of each graph represents the network structure at a specific threshold, with each edge possessing a corresponding edge property (CPL). Labels ("comfort” and “fatigue”) are assigned to each graph structure to facilitate subsequent classification using the graph neural network (GNN) model. After processing and analysis, we also provide the raw data of recorded peripheral physiological signals (ergolab_raw_data) and the characteristics of physiological analysis for each modality, such as corpus, P12data, and P36data. Considering that the raw peripheral physiological signals folder erolab_raw_data requires the installation of ErgoLAB software and the use of a dongle to open it, we have exported the results of our analysis to the folder export_ergolab_data. Figure 4 separately displays the raw EEG dataset, the brain network dataset, and the multimodal feature datasets (P12 and P36). The multimodal feature datasets following the format of the PhysioNet Mortality Prediction Challenge 2012 dataset36 includes two types: P12 and P36. The naming convention for P12 and P36 is based on the features included in each dataset. “P12” refers to a dataset that consists of all 12 types of peripheral physiological features. “P36” expands on P12 by incorporating brain network and spectral features from 24 channels, resulting in a dataset that combines both peripheral physiological and EEG spatiotemporal domain features. Subsequently, the multimodal datasets were classified using some classic neural network models: RAINDROP37, Transformer38, GRU-D39, MTGNN40, SeFT41, and DGM242. These models were employed to validate the dataset. As can be seen from the results in Table 4, all EEG indicators showed significant differences across different brain regions; except for Eθ, Eβ and Eθ/α, other indicators also exhibited significant differences before and after stimulus watching. Combined with Fig. 5, it can be observed that the relative energy in the θ frequency band in the fatigued state increased significantly compared with that in the non-fatigued state, and this change was most prominent in the frontal and temporal lobes. In contrast, the relative energy in the α frequency band in the fatigued state showed a significant upward trend in the frontal, temporal, and occipital lobes compared with that in the non-fatigued state. This may be because the subjects in this experiment were given long-term monotonous visual stimulation tasks, which caused overall fatigue in the brain, specifically manifested as an increase in the relative energy of Eθ waves in the prefrontal lobe; however, as the task became boring, local inhibition occurred in specific visual cortices, thereby leading to an enhancement of the relative energy of α waves in these regions. In addition, the relative energy in the Eβ frequency band decreased to a large extent in the parietal lobe after stimulus viewing, that is, the relative energy in this frequency band was lower in the fatigued state than in the non-fatigued state. The relative energy in the δ frequency band in the fatigued state increased significantly in the frontal and central regions compared with that in the non-fatigued state, and this conclusion is consistent with the results obtained in previous studies. Brain network dataset classification results

Four GNN models—GraphSAGE43, GraphConv44, GATConv45, and GINConv46—were used, each with three convolutional layers and a multilayer perceptron (MLP)47 for feature extraction. ReLU activation was applied for nonlinearity. The evaluation used five-fold cross-validation, with training via the Adam optimizer and performance measured by Receiver Operating Characteristic - Area Under the Curve (ROC-AUC) scores for binary classification. To evaluate the model’s accuracy in learning node and edge features, we compared the classification accuracy of GraphSAGE, GINConv, GraphConv, and GATConv. Among them, GraphSAGE and GINConv only consider node features, while GraphConv and GATConv take into account both node and edge features. As shown in Fig. 6, the results indicate that compared to the GraphConv and GATConv models, the GraphSAGE and GINConv models are better at identifying visual fatigue states in the brain under different frequency band combinations. Specifically, the GraphSAGE model outperformed the GINConv model in accuracy classification. Compared to models that evaluate visual fatigue with single-modal features (Fig. 7), the fusion of multimodal features can help the model to deeply explore the correlations between important features, thereby significantly improving the readiness rate for predicting visual fatigue. Since the importance of each modality feature varies, the model can also further explain the neural mechanisms of visual fatigue by learning the associations between important features. Consequently, the multimodal fusion characterization were proved to improve the model’s performance of recognizing visual fatigue compared to unimodal characterization. In addition, integrating brain network and spectral characteristics provides benefits for incorporating more comprehensive critical information, which in turn boosts the model’s predictive accuracy and overall performance.

Usage Notes

Code for EEG and peripheral physiological feature extraction has been uploaded to GitHub to facilitate sharing and utilization, accessible at https://github.com/taochunguang2022/mixed_reality_stereo_vision. Similarly, the code for experiments and multimodal dataset validation is available at https://github.com/taochunguang2022/mixed_reality_stereo_vision_model.

This script code_eeg_characteristic contains the construction of the brain network based on the computed PLV setting reasonable thresholds and extracting four topological properties (BC, CC, NE, CPL). The script code includes code for validating multimodal datasets (P12 and P36) for the classical neural network and machine learning models described above.

After using our dataset20,21, we encourage all users to extract more characteristics and updated samples of the dataset.
