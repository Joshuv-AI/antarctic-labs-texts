Stellar microlensing as a probe of Primordial Black Holes: status and prospects
Anne M. Green

Abstract
Stellar microlensing is a powerful tool for probing dark matter in the form of planetary and stellar mass compact objects (COs), in particular primordial black holes (PBHs). Under standard assumptions, current observations exclude COs in the mass range 10 − 11 ≲ M / M ⊙ ≲ 10 4 10^{-11}\lesssim M/M_{\odot}\lesssim 10^{4} aking up all of the dark matter. We provide an overview, aimed at theorists working on PBHs, of the history, theory, observational status, and future prospects of the field.

1
Introduction and history

Stellar microlensing is the temporary magnification of a star which occurs when a compact object crosses the line of sight. It was first studied by Einstein, who concluded that ’there is no great chance of observing this phenomenon’
Einstein 1936
. However in 1986 Paczyński showed that by monitoring millions of stars in the Magellanic Clouds (MC) it would be possible to probe compact objects (COs) in the Milky Way (MW) halo
Paczyński 1986
.

Various collaborations started microlensing surveys in the early 1990s, and the first MC candidate events were published in 1993 by EROS
Aubourg et al. 1993
, and MACHO
Alcock et al. 1993
. By the late 1990s the MACHO collaboration had observed significantly more events than expected from stars in the MC or MW disk. This indicated that a significant fraction of the Milky Way halo could be in the form of roughly Solar mass COs
Alcock et al. 1997
;
Alcock et al. 2000
. Baryon budget arguments excluded such a large population of astrophysical objects (e.g. white dwarfs)
Fields et al. 2000
. Therefore it was suggested, e.g. Refs.
Yokoyama 1997
;
Jedamzik 1998
, that these events could be due to Primordial Black Holes (PBHs), black holes that may form in the early Universe from large overdensities
Zel’dovich and Novikov 1967
;
Hawking 1971
. However the EROS
Ansari et al. 1996
;
Lasserre et al. 2000
;
Tisserand et al. 2007
and OGLE
Wyrzykowski et al. 2009
;
Wyrzykowski et al. 2011
collaborations did not see a significant excess of events, and instead constrained planetary and stellar mass COs to make up less than of order 10% of the MW halo.

In 2016 the discovery of gravitational waves from mergers of multi-Solar mass PBHs by LIGO-Virgo
Abbott et al. 2016
generated a surge of interest in PBH dark matter (DM)
Bird et al. 2016
;
Clesse and García-Bellido 2017
;
Sasaki et al. 2016
;
Carr et al. 2016
. However microlensing observations over two decades by the OGLE collaboration have failed to find the large,
\gtrsim 10^{2}
, number of events expected (under standard assumptions) if a significant fraction of the MW halo is in the form of stellar mass CO
Mróz et al. 2024
. These long duration observations have also increased the largest CO mass probed to
\sim 10^{4}M_{\odot}
, while high cadence observations by Niikura et al. have decreased the lowest mass to
\sim 10^{-11}M_{\odot}

Niikura et al. 2019
.

We overview how stellar microlensing surveys probe the fraction of COs, such as PBHs, in the MW halo by observing stars in the MC or M31. Our aim is to provide an introduction to this field that is accessible to theorists who are interested PBHs, and specifically the possibility that planetary and stellar mass PBHs make up a significant fraction of the DM. First, in Sec.
2
, we review the theory of stellar microlensing, before focusing on observations in Sec.
3
. We then look at future prospects in Sec.
4
, before concluding with a brief summary in Sec.
5
.

For an overview of the history of PBHs, see Ref.
Carr and Green 2025
. Refs.
Green and Kavanagh 2021
;
Carr et al. 2026
review the formation of PBHs and the full range of observational probes of their abundance.
For a review of the theory of microlensing see, e.g., Ref.
Mao 2012
, while Refs.
Moniez 2010
;
Jetzer 2014
overview the observational status as of the 2010s.

Basics

2.1.1
Individual events

In this subsection we overview the key properties of individual ’standard’ photometric microlensing events where a point source is lensed by a point lens. For more details see, e.g., the original microlensing paper by Paczyński
Paczyński 1986

Mao 2012

A microlensing event is usually defined to occur when

\hat{t}\sim 1\,{\rm yr}\,\sqrt{x(1-x)}\left(\frac{M}{M_{\odot}}\right)^{1/2}\,.

(4)

Because the timescale,

2.2

2.1.2
Optical depth and event rate

The optical depth,
\tau
, is the probability that a source star is being lensed at a given instant, i.e. that it falls within the Einstein radius of a lens:

\tau=\int_{0}^{D_{\rm S}}n(D_{\rm L})\pi R_{\rm E}^{2}(D_{\rm L})\,{\rm d}D_{\rm L}=4\pi G\int_{0}^{D_{\rm S}}\rho(D_{\rm L})\frac{D_{\rm L}(D_{\rm S}-D_{\rm L})}{D_{\rm S}}\,{\rm d}D_{\rm L}\,,

(6)

where
n(D_{\rm L})
and
\rho(D_{\rm L})=n(D_{\rm L})/M
are the number density and (mass) density of lenses respectively.
Note that the dependence on the lens mass,
M
, of the number density and the square of the Einstein radius cancel, and hence the optical depth depends on the total mass density of lenses along the line of sight, but not their individual masses. For a very simple model of the Milky Way (MW) halo with constant density, and a circular speed
v_{\rm c}=\sqrt{GM(<D_{\rm S})/D_{\rm S}}\approx 220\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
,
\tau=v_{\rm c}^{2}/2c^{2}\sim 10^{-6}

Mao 2012
. This small optical depth means that to detect microlensing events it is necessary to monitor a large number, i.e. millions, of stars. Furthermore, it is extremely unlikely that a single star will undergo two independent microlensing events.

A more useful quantity is the rate,
{\Gamma}
, at which events occur, and we summarize the calculation as outlined in Ref.
Mao 2012
. The area,
{\rm d}\tilde{A}
, swept out by a lens in a time interval,
{\rm d}t
, is given by the product of the diameter of the Einstein ring,
2R_{\rm E}
, and the distance traveled,
v_{\rm t}{\rm d}t
:
{\rm d}\tilde{A}=2R_{\rm E}v_{\rm t}{\rm d}t=4R_{\rm E}^{2}{\rm d}t/\hat{t}
. The probability,
{\rm d}\tau
, of an individual star undergoing a new microlensing event is given by

{\rm d}\tau=\int_{0}^{D_{\rm S}}n(D_{\rm L})\,{\rm d}A\,{\rm d}D_{\rm L}=\int_{0}^{D_{\rm S}}n(D_{\rm L})\left(\frac{4R_{\rm E}^{2}}{\hat{t}}\right)\,{\rm d}t\,{\rm d}D_{\rm L}\,.

(7)

The total number of new microlensing events in time
{\rm d}t
is
N_{\star}{\rm d}\tau
, where
N_{\star}
is the number of stars being monitored, and hence the event rate is

\Gamma=N_{\star}\frac{{\rm d}\tau}{{\rm d}t}=N_{\star}\int_{0}^{D_{\rm S}}n(D_{\rm d})\left(\frac{4}{\pi\hat{t}}\right)\pi R_{\rm E}^{2}{\rm d}D_{\rm d}=\frac{4N_{\star}}{\pi}\int\frac{{\rm d}\tau}{\hat{t}}\,.

(8)

Assuming that all events have the same timescale
\hat{t}
, leads to a rough relationship between the event rate and optical depth:

\Gamma\sim\frac{4N_{\star}}{\pi\hat{t}}\tau\,.

(9)

2.2
Differential event rate and parameter constraints

Since the timescale of a microlensing event depends on the CO mass (as well as its position and velocity), the differential event rate,
{\rm d}\Gamma/{\rm d}\hat{t}
, i.e. the rate at which events of timescale
\hat{t}
, occur can be used to probe the CO mass. The standard expression for the LMC differential event rate, for a halo composed entirely of COs (
f=1
, where
f
is the fraction of the total mass of the halo in CO) with mass
M
and a smooth density distribution
\rho(r)
is
Alcock et al. 1997
:

\frac{{\rm d}\Gamma_{\rm MW}}{{\rm d}\hat{t}}=\frac{32D_{\rm S}}{M{\hat{t}}^{4}v_{{\rm c}}^{2}}\int^{1}_{0}\rho(x)R^{4}_{{\rm E}}(x)\exp{\left[-Q(x)\right]}{\rm d}x\,,

(10)

where
Q(x)=4R^{2}_{{\rm E}}(x)/(\hat{t}^{2}v_{{\rm c}}^{2})
. Note that Eq. (
10
) assumes that the velocity distribution,
f({\bf v})
, is Maxwellian

f({\bf v})\,{\rm d}^{3}{\bf v}=\frac{1}{\pi^{3/2}v_{\rm c}^{3}}\exp{\left(-\frac{v^{2}}{v_{\rm c}^{2}}\right)}\,{\rm d}^{3}{\bf v}\,,

(11)

however this assumption is only exact if the density varies with Galactocentric radius
r
as
\rho(r)\propto r^{-2}
for all
r
.

Sahu pointed out that stars (’
\star

Sahu 1994
, a phenomenon often referred to as ’self-lensing’. There are also contributions to the total differential event rate from stars in the MW disc and and COs in the LMC:

\frac{{\rm d}\Gamma}{{\rm d}\hat{t}}=f\frac{{\rm d}\Gamma_{\rm MW}}{{\rm d}\hat{t}}+\frac{{\rm d}\Gamma_{{\rm MW},\star}}{{\rm d}\hat{t}}+\frac{{\rm d}\Gamma_{{\rm LMC},\star}}{{\rm d}\hat{t}}+f\frac{{\rm d}\Gamma_{\rm LMC}}{{\rm d}\hat{t}}\,.

(14)

The self-lensing rate is largest for observing fields in the centre of the LMC, where the number of stars is highest. The MACHO collaboration found, using models from Ref.
Gyuk et al. 2000
, an optical depth
\tau_{{\rm LMC},\star}\sim(1-3)\times 10^{-8}
.
Using the same LMC model with some minor modifications, OGLE III and IV (which has a wider observing field) found 5.7 events expected from stars in the LMC (compared with the
10^{2-3}
expected if all of the halo is in planetary or stellar mass COs)
Mróz et al. 2024
.
The MACHO collaboration found an optical depth for microlensing by stars in the MW disc,
\tau_{{\rm MW},\star}\lesssim 10^{-8}
, lower than their estimate for self-lensing
Alcock et al. 2000
. However more recent studies have found significantly larger MW disc rates, comparable with, or even larger, than the self-lensing rate, in part due to a larger thick disc contribution
Calchi Novati and Mancini 2011
;
Mróz et al. 2024
. For the two MW models they considered
Han and Gould 2003
;
Cautun et al. 2020
, OGLE III and IV found 7.0 and 14.7 expected events respectively from stars in the MW disc
Mróz et al. 2024
. Note, however, that the relative numbers of events from different lens populations depend on the fields observed, and the detection efficiency (see below).

The total expected number of events,
N_{\rm exp}
, is given by

N_{\rm exp}=E\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}\Gamma}{{\rm d}\hat{t}}\epsilon(\hat{t})\,{\rm d}\hat{t}\,,

(15)

where
E
is the exposure (which is equal to the number of stars observed,
N_{\star}
, multiplied by the duration of the survey) and
\epsilon(\hat{t})
is the efficiency, i.e. the probability that a microlensing event with timescale
\hat{t}
is detected (see Sec.
3.1
).
If no events are detected, then the
95\%
confidence limit on the fraction of the MW halo in COs can be calculated as
f=3.0/N_{\rm exp}(f=1)
, where
N_{\rm exp}(f=1)
is the expected number of events if
f=1
. PBHs are expected to have an extended mass function (see e.g. Sec. 2 of Ref.
Carr et al. 2026
). Constraints calculated assuming all CO have the same mass can be translated into constraints for a specific extended mass function using the method presented in Ref.
Carr et al. 2017
.
Surveys with a large exposure expect to observe events due to stars in the MW disc and/or LMC, and in this case constraints on the CO mass and halo fraction are calculated using the likelihood
Alcock et al. 2000
;
Mróz et al. 2024
:

{\cal L}(f,M)=\exp{(-N_{\rm exp})}E\prod_{i=1}^{N_{\rm obs}}\frac{{\rm d}\Gamma}{{\rm d}\hat{t}}(\hat{t}_{i})\epsilon(\hat{t}_{i})\,,

(16)

where
N_{\rm obs}
is the number of observed events and
\hat{t}_{i}
are their timescales.

The MACHO first year results paper
Alcock et al. 1997
studied a very wide range of models for the MW halo, some of which are not consistent with current observations.
They found that there was a significant variation in the differential event rate, and hence the observational constraints on the CO mass and halo fraction. The OGLE collaboration finds that the uncertainty in their constraint is relatively small (see Extended Data Fig. 1 of Ref.
Mróz et al. 2024
), however they only consider two benchmark models (with fixed parameter values) and assume a Maxwellian velocity distribution. Other studies
Hawkins 2015
;
Green 2017
;
Calcino et al. 2018
;
Garcia-Bellido and Hawkins 2024
have found that the uncertainty in exclusion limits can be significant. Ref.
Green 2025
found that the rate of long-timescale events (and hence the constraints on low-mass WIMPs) has a fairly weak dependence on the CO density and velocity distribution. However the rate of short-timescale events (and hence the constraints on high-mass WIMPs) depends sensitively on the local DM velocity distribution. An accurate calculate of the differential event rate (and parameter constraints) for models consistent with current observations, and numerical simulations of Milky Way-like galaxies, is an outstanding issue.

Microlensing observations towards the Small Magellanic Cloud (SMC) could in principle help discriminate halo-lensing from self-lensing, as the visible components of the SMC are elongated along the line of sight.
The self-lensing rate is therefore expected to be larger than for the LMC. Furthermore for halo lenses, the timescales of events towards the LMC and SMC should be similar whereas for self-lensing, events towards the SMC are expected to be longer (due to the larger lens-source separation, and hence Einstein radius)
Sahu and Sahu 1998
. There are, however, significant uncertainties in the SMC’s structure due to its tidal interactions with the LMC. See Refs.
Palanque-Delabrouille 1998
;
Graff and Gardiner 1999
for discussion of the status as of the late 1990s in the context of microlensing, and, e.g., the Introduction of Ref.
Povick et al. 2025
for the current status in a more general context.

Crotts explored the advantages and disadvantages of microlensing observations towards M31 (our nearest neighbour galaxy, also known as Andromeda)
Crotts 1992
.
In particular he pointed out that the high inclination of M31’s disc with respect to the line of sight would lead to a large asymmetry in the microlensing rate from lenses in the halo of M31. The event rate for the far side of the disc would be much higher than that for the near side, due to the larger column density of halo lenses. This would be a potentially powerful tool for discriminating events due to halo lenses from self-lensing or variable stars. Baillon et al.
Baillon et al. 1993
pointed out that
the large number of source stars is another advantage of M31 over the MCs. However individual stars are not resolved, and hence ’pixel’ lensing techniques are required (see Sec.
3.5
). See Refs.
Kerins et al. 2001
;
Baltz et al. 2003
for theoretical studies of microlensing towards M31, including calculations of the differential event rate.

The standard calculation of the halo differential event rate outlined above, Eq. (
10
), assumes that the dark matter distribution is smooth, and fully specified by the density profile,
\rho(r)
. However PBH dark matter is expected to cluster more on sub-galactic scales than particle dark matter. Since PBHs are discrete objects, there are Poisson fluctuations in their distribution and PBH clusters start forming shortly after matter-radiation equality
Afshordi et al. 2003
;
Inman and Ali-Haïmoud 2019
. For PBHs formed from gaussian fluctuations, the clusters are sufficiently diffuse
Jedamzik 2020
that the PBHs still act as individual microlenses. In this case the effect on the microlensing differential event rate, and hence constraints on the PBH mass and halo fraction, is small
Petač et al. 2022
;
Gorton and Green 2022
. Some PBHs are expected to be in binaries which could potentially lead to a source being lensed twice, however the binaries are typically sufficiently wide that the time between such repeating events is expected to be much longer that the duration of microlensing surveys
Petač et al. 2022
. The probability distribution of perturbations that are large enough to produce PBHs which make up a significant fraction of the dark matter, are expected to be non-gaussian, e.g. Ref.
Atal and Germani 2019
, however, and
PBHs formed from non-gaussian fluctuations can form more compact clusters
Young and Byrnes 2020
. If clusters are sufficiently compact that the cluster as a whole acts as a microlens, then microlensing parameter constraints (or allowed regions) will shift to lower PBH masses
Calcino et al. 2018
.

2.3
Non-standard events

Deviations from the ’single point source, single point lens’ assumptions underlying the standard Paczyński light curve, Eq. (
1
), can have observable consequences.
For a more detailed review see Ref.
Mao 2012
. We discuss specific observed non-standard events in Sec.
3.2
.

The standard light curve is significantly modified if the impact parameter of an event is comparable to the ratio of the radius of the source star to the Einstein radius
Witt and Mao 1994
;
Gould 1994
.
In particular
finite source
effects significantly reduce the maximum magnification for compact objects with mass
M\lesssim 10^{-9}M_{\odot}

Sasaki et al. 2018
.

The standard analysis assumes all motions are linear. However this is not the case if the lens or source are binary, and the Earth’s orbit about the Sun also violates this assumption
Gould 1992
. The
parallax
effect, due to the Earth’s orbit, is detectable if the event timescale is not significantly shorter than a year
Gould 1992
. For a
binary source
, orbital motion leads to periodic distortions in the light curve
Griest and Hu 1992
. This is commonly referred to as the ’xallarap’ effect (the inverse of parallax). A
binary lens
can generate complex, multi-peaked light curves with caustics (where the magnification formally diverges)
Mao and Paczynski 1991
.

PBHs with mass
M_{\rm PBH}\lesssim 10^{-11}M_{\odot}
have Schwarzschild radius comparable to the wavelength of light, resulting in diffraction and interference effects. These
wave optics
effects reduce the magnification and place a lower limit on the mass of COs that can be probed by microlensing

Sugiyama et al. 2020
;
Montero-Camacho et al. 2019
.

Some of these effects can break the microlensing degeneracy mentioned above,
and allow the lens mass and/or projected transverse velocity to be measured. The latter is useful as it acts as a diagnostic for the lens location
Boutreux and Gould 1996
. For lenses in the MW halo and LMC, the projected transverse velocity is determined by the velocity dispersion of the MW halo and LMC, which are small and large respectively. For lenses in the MW disc it is determined by the differential rotation of the Sun and disk, which has an intermediate value.

2.4
Astrometric microlensing

The above discussion focuses on photometric microlensing, i.e. the change in the apparent brightness of a source during a microlensing event. This is the observable used by the microlensing surveys to date which we will overview in Sec.
3
. Astrometric microlensing is the change in the apparent position of the source
Miyamoto and Yoshii 1995
;
Walker 1995
, and in the future telescopes which accurately measure the positions of stars (astrometry) will also be able to probe CO DM using astrometric microlensing (see Sec.
4.1
).

The centroid shift of the source,
\delta\theta(u)
, is given by

\delta\theta(u)=\frac{u}{u^{2}+2}\theta_{\rm E}\,,

(17)

where
\theta_{\rm E}
is the angular Einstein radius,
\theta_{\rm E}=R_{\rm E}/D_{\rm L}
.
Using Eq. (
2
),
\theta_{\rm E}
can be expressed in terms of the lens-source parallax,
\pi_{\rm rel}
,

\pi_{\rm rel}=\pi_{\rm L}-\pi_{\rm S}=\frac{1}{D_{\rm L}}-\frac{1}{D_{\rm S}}=\frac{D_{\rm S}-D_{\rm L}}{D_{\rm L}D_{\rm S}}\,,

(18)

in milliarcseconds (mas), as

\theta_{\rm E}^{2}=8.144\,{\rm mas}\left(\frac{M}{M_{\odot}}\right)\left(\frac{\pi_{\rm rel}}{{\rm mas}}\right)\,.

(19)

Therefore if the parallax can also be measured, the lens mass can be determined
Miyamoto and Yoshii 1995
;
Belokurov and Evans 2002
.
Astrometric microlensing signals are detectable for large lens-source separations, so the optical depth is larger than for photometric microlensing
Miralda-Escude 1996
;
Dominik and Sahu 2000
.

3.1
General principles

The first step in analyzing data from a microlensing survey is to generate light curves for individual objects.
Historically this was done using point spread function photometry, e.g. Refs.
Alcock et al. 2001
;
Ansari et al. 1996
. More recently different image analysis (DIA), which is more computationally demanding, but better suited to the crowded stellar fields in the MC, has been used, e.g. Refs.
Zebrun et al. 2001
;
Lasserre et al. 2000
.

Candidate microlensing events are selected by applying a series of cuts to the light curves. These cuts are designed to select light curves which are well fit by standard Paczyński form with a single, achromatic, symmetric variation as described in Sec.
2.1.1
, while rejecting background that can mimic microlensing. Key backgrounds are supernovae in background galaxies and variable stars
Alcock et al. 2000
. A class of variable star that has recurrent bumps in their light curve that mimic microlensing, so called ”blue bumpers”, were in fact discovered by microlensing surveys
Alcock et al. 1996
.
The detection efficiency,
\epsilon(\hat{t})
, is estimated by ascertaining what fraction of simulated events pass the selection cuts, e.g. Ref.
Alcock et al. 2001
. The detection efficiency is necessarily zero for timescales shorter than the interval between observations (i.e. the cadence of the survey). Typically it grows with increasing timescale to a maximum value of order
40\%
, before declining to zero again for timescales longer than the duration of the survey
Alcock et al. 2000
;
Tisserand et al. 2007
;
Mróz et al. 2025
.

As discussed in Ref.
Griest and Thomas 2005
, selection criteria inevitably have to balance false positives (candidate events that are not due to microlensing) and false negatives (microlensing events that are rejected). The detection efficiency calculation takes into account the later but not the former.
Event selection (and detection efficiency estimation) is complicated by the
\sim 10\%
of microlensing events, which are ’exotic’ and don’t follow the standard Paczyński light curve produce by a single point source and lens, e.g. Ref.
Bennett et al. 2005
. In some cases, e.g. MACHO-LMC-9 which we discuss further in Sec.
3.2
, the event is unambiguously microlensing and the features in the light curve provide information about the location of the lens. However in other cases non-microlensing variability can be fit by a non-standard lens model (see discussion in Ref.
Bennett et al. 2005
). As discussed further in Sec.
3.2
, in their final 5.7 year results, the MACHO collaboration used two sets of selection: criteria A designed to detect high-quality simple events, and criteria B designed to also select exotic and lower signal to noise events
Alcock et al. 2000
. EROS-2
Tisserand et al. 2007
and OGLE
Mróz et al. 2025
carried out manual inspections of a subset of their light curves to check for a significant population of overlooked events.

The measured optical depth can be estimated using

\tau=\frac{1}{E}\frac{\pi}{4}\sum_{i}\frac{\hat{t}_{i}}{\epsilon(\hat{t}_{i})}\,,

(20)

where
\hat{t}_{i}
is the estimated timescale of the
i
-th event. The estimated timescales and detection efficiency can also be used in a likelihood analysis, using Eq. (
16
), to probe the CO mass and halo fraction. Note that a single object in a survey can in fact be composed of several, unresolved, stars, a phenomenon known as blending. This affects the determination of the optical depth and event rate in multiple ways. It affects the estimation of the timescales of blended events,
\hat{t}
, and the detection efficiency,
\epsilon(\hat{t})

Smith et al. 2007
. It also means the effective number of stars monitored,
N_{\rm\star}
, is larger than the number of catalogued objects. The impact of blending can be minimized, as is done by the EROS and OGLE collaborations, by only using bright red giant stars. See Ref.
Moniez 2010
for a more detailed overview.

3.2
MACHO

Between 1992 and 1999 the MACHO collaboration monitored over 10 million stars in the LMC. Their first year results contained 3 candidate events
and (assuming all 3 were due to lens in the MW halo) they found a best-fit halo fraction
f=0.2_{-0.1}^{+0.2}
and a MACHO mass
M=0.065^{+0.06}_{-0.03}M_{\odot}

Alcock et al. 1996
. In their 2-year data they found 8 candidate events
Alcock et al. 1997
. MACHO-LMC-2 and 3 from their 1-year data set did not pass the revised event selection cuts used in the 2-year analysis (and one of these two events subsequently brightened again repeatedly and is therefore thought to be a variable star). On the other hand, 3 of the 8 2-year-candidates were in the 1-year data but did not pass the 1-year cuts
Alcock et al. 1997
. MACHO-LMC-9 had a U-shaped light curve, characteristic of a binary lens
and, if the source star is single, the lens is likely to lie in the LMC
Bennett et al. 1996
. Using a sub-sample of 6 events (excluding the binary lens, MACHO-LMC-9, and MACHO-LMC-10 which they deemed a marginal candidate) they found a halo fraction
f=0.5_{-0.2}^{+0.3}
and a MACHO mass
M=0.5_{-0.2}^{+0.3}M_{\odot}
.
A specific search for short timescale events in the 2-year data excluded COs in the range
3\times 10^{-7}\lesssim M/M_{\odot}\lesssim 5\times 10^{-4}
making up all of the MW halo
Alcock et al. 1996
.
A subsequent EROS-MACHO joint analysis tightened this limit to
f<0.10
for this planetary mass range
Alcock et al. 1998

The final MACHO results were based on 5.7-years of observations
Alcock et al. 2000

Alcock et al. 1997
and was designed to select simple microlensing events, with a single, high significance bump in the light curve and a flat baseline. Criteria B was intentionally looser (with higher detection efficiency), and designed to search for exotic and/or low signal-to-noise events. Marginal events suspected of being supernovae in background galaxies were included in set B but rejected from set A.

3.3
EROS

EROS ran from 1990 to 2003. Its first phase, EROS-1 found two candidate LMC events

The final EROS-2 analysis used observations of a subsample of 7 million bright stars in the MC, in order to aid discrimination of variable stars, and minimize the effects of blending

3.4
OGLE

The OGLE project has run for more than thirty year, starting in 1992
Soszyński 2024
.
The first OGLE MC observations (OGLE-II) found two candidate LMC events
Wyrzykowski et al. 2009
. They found that if both were due to COs the halo fraction was
f=0.08\pm 0.06
. However they argued that these events were consistent with expectations from self-lensing, and constrained CO with
0.01\lesssim M/M_{\odot}\lesssim 0.2
to make up less than 10% of the MW halo. OGLE-LMC-02 subsequently underwent multiple additional brightenings and is therefore not a microlensing event
Mróz et al. 2024
.
Their initial observations towards the SMC found a single, unconvincing, candidate and slightly weaker limits
Wyrzykowski et al. 2010
.

In 8 years of observations towards the SMC, OGLE-III found 3 convincing events (OGLE-SMC-2-4)
Wyrzykowski et al. 2011
. The light curve of OGLE-SMC-2 exhibits a clear parallax effect, and also deviates from the simple Paczyński curve around its peak. Follow up observations (including data from the Spitzer space telescope) measured the lens projected velocity and found that the lens is most likely a BH binary in the MW halo, with mass
M\sim 10M_{\odot}

Dong et al. 2007
. Ref.
Wyrzykowski et al. 2011
argued that this event is consistent with BHs making up of order a per-cent of the MW halo. Like MACHO-LMC-5, OGLE-SMC-3 was a jerk-parallax event, with the lens likely a M dwarf in the MW disc
Wyrzykowski et al. 2011
. The combined OGLE-II and III MC limits on the halo fraction were similar to those from EROS2 for
M\gtrsim 0.01M_{\odot}

Wyrzykowski et al. 2011
.

Results from OGLE-III and IV observations of 78.7 million stars in the LMC for up to 20 years were published in 2024
Mróz et al. 2024
. Their automated data pipeline found 13 events, all with Einstein radius crossing times less than one year. A manual, ’by eye’, search found an additional 3 events.
They found that the number of events, and their positions, timescales and parallaxes, are consistent with expectations from stars in the MW disk and the LMC. Using the 13 events identified using the automated data pipeline, they found that COs in the mass range
10^{-4}(10^{-5})\lesssim M/M_{\odot}\lesssim 6(900)
can not make up more than
1(10)\%
of the MW halo (assuming the MW halo has a contracted Navarro Frenk White density profile fitted to Gaia DR2 and other data in Ref.
Cautun et al. 2020

Mróz et al. 2024

Mróz et al. 2025

Niikura et al.
Niikura et al. 2019
used OGLE microlensing observations towards the Galactic bulge to probe planetary mass COs. They found that most events were well-fit by microlensing by stellar populations, and excluded
f>0.1
for
10^{-6}\lesssim M/M_{\odot}\lesssim 10^{-2}
.
There was a distinct population of six ultra-short (
\sim 0.1

From 2022-2024 OGLE carried out a high-cadence survey of the MC, observing 35 million stars up to 21 times per night
Mróz et al. 2024

\sim 90

10^{-8}\lesssim M/M_{\odot}\lesssim 0.01

1\%

3.5
M31

Various collaborations have carried out so-called pixel microlensing surveys towards M31. Stars in M31 are too distant to be resolved individually, so instead microlensing surveys search for variations in the flux of the pixels of the image. Ref.
Calchi Novati 2010
contains a review of pixel microlensing surveys towards M31 as of 2009.

In the early 2000s POINT-AGAPE
Calchi Novati et al. 2005
found 6 events, significantly more than the
\sim 1
they expected from self-lensing. They concluded that if the average mass of COs is
\sim 1(0.01)M_{\odot}
, then at 95% confidence they make up more than
20(8)\%
of the MW halo. Subsequently, MEGA
de Jong et al. 2006
and PLAN
Calchi Novati et al. 2014

(20-30)\%
.

More recently high cadence (2 minute sampling interval) observations of M31 have been carried out using the Hyper Suprime-Cam (HSC) on the Subaru telescope
Niikura et al. 2019
;
Sugiyama et al. 2026
. Initial observations found one candidate event which led to a constraint
f\lesssim 10^{-2}
for
10^{-5}\lesssim M/M_{\odot}\lesssim 10^{-9}

Niikura et al. 2019
;
Niikura et al. 2019
. The finite size of the lensed stars reduces the magnification
Witt and Mao 1994

M\lesssim 10^{-7}M_{\odot}

Niikura et al. 2019
;
Smyth et al. 2020

Sugiyama et al. 2026

Twelve microlensing candidates have been identified, with 4 being classed as secure, high-significance, detections. If these events are due to COs in the MW halo the corresponding mass and halo fraction are
M\sim(10^{-7}-10^{-6})M_{\odot}
and
f\sim 0.01-0.1

4
Future

4.1
Observations

In the near future new telescopes will be able to carry out next generation microlensing surveys. The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope (formerly known as WFIRST) has excellent resolution, which will reduce the effects of blending and allow the measurement of finite source effects. It will carry out high cadence observations, as required to detect short timescale events and probe light CO. The ground-based Vera C. Rubin Observatory (formerly known as the Large Synoptic Survey Telescope) will carry out a long duration wide-field survey. It will have high sensitivity to long timescale events, and hence intermediate and high mass CO.
Telescopes with high spatial resolution, capable of high-precision astrometry, will be able to carry out astrometric microlensing measurements and probe the angular Einstein radius (see Sec.
2.4
).

Following early work in Ref.
Cieplak and Griest 2013
, Ref.
DeRocco et al. 2024
showed that the Roman Space Telescope’s Galactic Bulge Time Domain Survey will be able to to discriminate planetary-mass PBHs from free floating planets, and probe halo fractions
f\sim 10^{-3}-10^{-4}
using the timescales of (photometric) microlensing events.
Ref.
Fardeen et al. 2024
showed that this survey could probe

f\sim 10^{-3}
for
10^{-1}\lesssim M/M_{\odot}\lesssim 10^{2}

if
it is possible to distinguish PBHs and stellar lenses using the joint distributions of the timescales and angular Einstein radii of events. If it is not possible to do this then the sensitivity will be reduced to
f\sim 10^{-1}
, weaker than existing OGLE limits in this mass range
Mróz et al. 2024
.
Similar constraints may also be possible using the Rubin Observatory
Drlica-Wagner et al. 2019
.
With improved understanding of backgrounds, Gaia time-series data could probe halo fractions down to
f\sim 10^{-3}
for multi-Solar mass PBHs
Verma and Rentala 2023
, see also Ref.
Belokurov and Evans 2002
. Thirty-metre class, Extremely Large Telescopes, will also be able to probe PBHs via astrometric microlensing
Bird et al. 2023
.
Combining photometric and astrometric microlensing data would aid the detection and characterization of events
Pruett et al. 2024
. In all cases, correctly identifying events and minimizing the false positive rate will be crucial (as we have seen for previous surveys in Sec.
3
). See Ref.
Crispim Romao et al. 2025
for work in this direction.

4.2
Theory

The key theoretical uncertainty is the distribution of PBHs, specifically their local velocity distribution and also whether PBHs form compact clusters. As discussed in Sec.
2.2
, uncertainties in the DM distribution lead to uncertainties in the differential event rate and hence the constraints on the halo fraction. If the DM is smoothly distributed, the most significant uncertainty is the dependence of the rate of short-timescale events, and hence the constraints on
f
for large masses, on the local velocity distribution
Green 2025
. If PBHs form compact clusters, such that the cluster as a whole lenses, the constraints would change significantly
Calcino et al. 2018
. Understanding the present day spatial distribution of PBHs formed from realistic density perturbation distributions (i.e. how many PBHs are in clusters, and what are their properties? how many PBHs are in binaries, and what are their properties?) is a major outstanding challenge.

5
Summary

We have explored how stellar microlensing surveys probe compact object dark matter, in particular primordial black holes. Early indications from the MACHO collaboration that a significant fraction of the Milky Way halo could be in the form of roughly Solar Mass COs
Alcock et al. 1997
;
Alcock et al. 2000
were not confirmed by EROS
Tisserand et al. 2007
and OGLE
Mróz et al. 2024
. Long duration
Mróz et al. 2024
and high cadence
Niikura et al. 2019
;
Mróz et al. 2024
observations have extended the range of CO masses probed by microlensing. Under the standard assumptions outlined in Sec.
2
, collectively these surveys exclude COs with mass
10^{-11}\lesssim M/M_{\odot}\lesssim 10^{4}
making up all of the DM in the MW, while those in the mass range
10^{-10}\lesssim M/M_{\odot}\lesssim 1
can make up less than one percent. Further theoretical work is required to ascertain how robust the standard assumptions are, in particular regarding the spatial distribution of PBHs. Future observations, in particular astrometric ones using the Roman Telescope and Rubin Observatory, can potentially probe PBHs down to
f\sim 10^{-3}-10^{-4}
. However further work is required to develop techniques for discriminating PBH-induced lensing events from backgrounds (including, but not limited to, lensing events due to astrophysical objects).
