Association of body roundness index with risk of all-cause, cardiovascular disease-cause, and cancer-cause mortality: the role of biological age acceleration
Authors: Pan Ke, Zuxun Lu, Xiaobin Wu, Deliang Lv, Minzhi Xu, Dongsheng Di, Dankang Li, Guangwen Gong, Xiaoxv Yin, Zhiguang Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to explore the association of BRI with all-cause, cardiovascular disease (CVD)-cause, and cancer-cause mortality risk, and the role of biological age acceleration (BAA) in mediating these associations.MethodsWe included 305,406 participants in the UK biobank. BRI was calculated based on waist circumference (WC) and height. BAA was divided into klemera-doubal method biological age acceleration (KDM-BA) and PhenoAge acceleration. The associations of BRI, BAA with outcomes were assessed by Cox proportional hazard models. The role of BAA in this association was analyzed by mediating effect.ResultsCompared with the individuals with the lowest BRI quartile, the individuals with the highest BRI quartile had a higher hazard of death, and the HR and 95% CI of all-cause death, CVD death and cancer death were (HR: 1.43 (95% CI: 1.33, 1.53)), (HR: 1.47 (95% CI: 1.25, 1.72)), (HR: 1.38 (95% CI: 1.26, 1.52)), respectively. The mediation proportion of BAA in associations of BRI with death outcomes were 10.75–42.98% (all p < 0.001).ConclusionBRI is associated with an increased hazard of all-cause death, CVD death, and cancer death, BAA partially mediated these associations. We observed that higher levels of BRI were associated with an increased hazard of death, with biological age acceleration potentially mediating this association. This suggests that maintaining a healthy lifestyle and reducing the inflammatory/immune response and lipid metabolism abnormalities associated with obesity are critical to reducing the burden of chronic disease. The future intervention targets will be maintaining WC at appropriate levels that might contribute to self-health management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-025-01209-2.Keywords: Body roundness index, Biological age acceleration, Mortality, Cohort study

Introduction

Obesity is a global public health problem. In 2021, an estimated 1 billion adult males and 1.11 billion adult females were overweight and obesity in world [1]. In the UK, the obesity rate among adult men rose from 10.7% in 1990 to 26.9% in 2022, and that among adult women increased from 13.8–28.3% [2]. By 2030, the combined healthcare costs of obesity-related conditions will increase by £1.92 billion a year in the UK [3]. The European Heart Association pointed out in its “Clinical Consensus Statement on Obesity and Cardiovascular Disease” that obese patients have a 50–100% increased risk of all-cause mortality, among which 67.5% of the increased risk of mortality could be attributed to cardiovascular disease (CVD) [4]. Hence, effective policies to promote healthy weight also have economic benefits.

Anthropometric measures are considered a simple, low-cost, non-invasive tool for screening and early detection of obesity [5]. In general, obesity is measured according to body mass index (BMI), which is the most commonly used anthropometric index [6]. However, BMI has difficulty with lean body mass and fat mass, and does not distinguish the location of peripheral/central fat [7]. To better represent fat distribution, Thomas et al. created a relatively new anthropometric measure called the body roundness index (BRI) [8]. This is despite studies showing that BRI is associated with all-cause mortality and cause-specific mortality (CVD and cancer) [9–14]. For instance, the result of a study using NHANES shows that when BRI exceeds 6.9, the risk of all-cause mortality increases by 49% [15]. However, no studies have explored the underlying mechanisms of these associations. The development of chronic disease and death are related to aging. Therefore, the ideal biological aging measurement should reflect multiple dimensions of aging. Biological age acceleration (BAA) refers to the state in which an individual’s biological age exceeds their chronological age (CA), indicating faster aging and often associated with a decline in physiological function. Previous studies calculated the BAA of participants using the Klemera-Doubal method Biological age (KDM-BA) and PhenoAge algorithms, and explored its association with CVD, mental health and mediating effects [16–22]. For instance, studies have shown that BAA as the mediating variable, reflects the biological mechanisms of chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, increased insulin resistance and the risk of death from chronic diseases [23]. Given this, we hypothesize that the accelerated process of biological aging itself not only leads to an increased hazard of death, but also mediates the relationship between BRI and death hazard.

Method

Study population

Details about the survey sampling design and data quality of the UK Biobank (UKB) study have been described elsewhere [24]. The UKB sampled 502,401 adults aged 37 to 73 from 2006 to 2010. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Lost to follow up (n = 1293); (2) CVD (n = 11,786) and cancer (n = 30,183) were present before baseline; (3) Missing biological age acceleration indicators (n = 153,346) (Supplementary Table 1); (4) Missing waist circumference (WC) (n = 129) and height (n = 258). Finally, 305,406 participants were included in our analysis (Fig. 1). UKB received ethical approval from the North West Multicenterentre Research Ethics Committee (REC reference: 11/NW/03820). Prior to enrollment in the study, all participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This study followed the STROBE Statement guidelines for observational research.

Data cleaning flow chart

BRI and BAA definition

BRI =, according to the formula developed by Thomas et al. [8]. BAA is divided into KDM-BA acceleration and PhenoAge acceleration. KDM-BA acceleration was computed from forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and seven blood chemical parameters (albumin (ALB), alkaline phosphatase (AKP), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), Serum creatinine (Scr), C-reactive protein (CRP), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), and total cholesterol (TC)); PhenoAge acceleration was calculated based on nine blood chemicals, four of which overlapped with KDM-BA (ALB, AKP, Scr, CRP, glucose (GLU), mean cell volume (MCV), red blood cell distribution width (RDW), white blood cell count (WBC) and lymphocyte ratio). The calculation of KDM-BA acceleration and PhenoAge acceleration adopts the formula given in the study of Kwon D and Xu Gao et al. [18, 25] in Supplementary File (Supplementary Method: Assessment of biological ages and age accelerations).

Ascertainment of outcomes

The date and cause of death are obtained from death certificates held in the National Health Service Information Centre (England and Wales) and the National Health Service Central Register (Scotland). Detailed information about the associated process, please visit https://content.digital.nhs.uk/services. Follow-up time was defined as the time from baseline to the date of death or censoring (September 30, 2021), whichever came first. The outcomes of this study were all-cause death, CVD death, and cancer death, among which the ICD codes of CVD death and cancer death were shown in Supplementary File (Supplementary Method: The Definition of Outcomes and Covariates).

Assessment of covariates

Possible confounding factors (in Supplementary File:Supplementary Method The Definition of Outcomes and Covariates) included sex, age, Townsend deprivation index, BMI, blood lipid (TG (triglyceride), HDL-C (high-density lipoprotein cholesterol), LDL-C (low-density lipoprotein cholesterol)), smoking status (never/ever/current), drinking status (never/ever/current), moderate-to-vigorous intensity physical activity (yes/no), dietary intake (fresh fruit, cooked vegetables, processed meat, cereal), type 2 diabetes (yes/no), loneliness (yes/no), anxiety (yes/no), and assessment centre. The statistical table of missing values of covariates is shown in Supplementary Table 2.

Statistical analyses

Computation of biological age values was conducted using the R package “BioAge“ [25]. Missing values of covariates were subjected to multiple imputation using the R package “mice”. Continuous variables were expressed as the median (interquartile range, IQR), and the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for the difference test between groups. The frequency (%) was used as the categorical variable, and the chi-square test was used for the difference test between groups. BRI, KDM-BA acceleration and PhenoAge acceleration are expressed in quartiles (Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4). To evaluate the potential mediating role of BAA in BRI and adverse outcomes, we considered four pathways: (a) BRI and BAA; (b) BRI and death hazard; (c) BAA and death hazard; (d) the potential mediating role of BAA in the association between BRI and death hazard). For path a, linear regression was analyzed the correlation between BRI and BAA, coefficient (β) and standard error (SE) were documented. For path b and path c, Cox proportional hazard models were applied to examine the associations of BRI, BAA, and death hazard. The proportional hazards assumptions for the independent variables and covariates were satisfied for the primary and secondary end points. Results were reported with hazard ratio (HR) and 95% Confidence Interval (CI). With the lowest quartiles of BRI, KDM-BA acceleration and PhenoAge acceleration as reference, two multivariate models are constructed to account for the potential confounding factors: Model 1 without adjustment; Model 2 adjusted age, sex, Townsend deprivation index, BMI, blood lipid, smoking status, drinking status, moderate-to-vigorous intensity physical activity, fresh fruit intake, cooked vegetables intake, processed meat intake, cereal intake, diabetes, loneliness, anxiety, and assess centre. To estimate the nolinear dose-response relationship between BRI, BAA and death hazard, the restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression model with three knots (5th, 50th and 95th percentiles) was employed. The “rms” package was used for restricted cubic spline analysis. The 50th percentile (median) value of BRI was 4.934. Taking the reference value at HR = 1.0, when exploring its nonlinear relationship with the risk of all-cause mortality, CVD-cause mortality, and cancer-cause mortality. For path d, the role of BAA in this association was analyzed by mediating effect. The “mediation” package was used for mediation effects analysis where we documented average causal mediation effects (ACME), average direct effects (ADE), and proportionately mediated and corresponding 95% CI. We also performed several sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of the results. Firstly, in order to test the robustness of the association between BRI and the hazard of death, without considering BAA, we conducted a correlation analysis on the excluded data. Secondly, some participants who died within the first 2 years after recruitment were excluded to reduce potential reverse causality. Third, the data analysis results of uninterpolated missing values are used. Fourth, we used Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model to explore the relationship between BRI, BAA and CVD death and cancer death, respectively [26]. Fifth, mediating effect analyses were conducted respectively using data that excluded deaths during the first two years of follow-up and data with missing values in covariates. Statistical analyses were conducted by R software Version 4.4.0 (http://www.R-project.org). A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses.

Results

Participants characteristics

Of the 305,406 participants at baseline, the number of BRI corresponding to group Q1, Q2, Q3 and Q4 are 76,352, 76,352, 76,351 and 76,351, respectively (Table 1). Participants with a higher BRI were older, more socioeconomically deprived, have higher BMI, SBP, AKP, BUN, Scr, CRP, HbA1c, GLU, WBC, lower FEV1, higher prevalence of diabetes, loneliness, and anxiety. Meanwhile, as the BRI quartile rises, there is a corresponding augmentation in KDM-BA acceleration, and PhenoAge acceleration. The comparison of various characteristics of the participants with the quartile grouping of BRI showed statistically significant differences (p < 0.001). The median follow-up time was 12.55 years, there were 18,250 all-cause deaths, 3631 CVD deaths, and 9140 cancer deaths, respectively. Associations of BRI with BAA and death (path a and b)

BRI was positively associated with KDM-BA acceleration (β = 0.341, SE = 0.025, p < 0.001), PhenoAge acceleration (β = 0.086, SE = 0.011, p < 0.001) after model adjustment (Table 2). Compared with the BRI in the lowest quartile (Table 3), the highest quartile had the greatest hazard of all-cause death (HR: 1.43, 95% CI: 1.33, 1.53), CVD-cause mortality (HR: 1.47, 95% CI: 1.25, 1.72), and cancer-cause mortality (HR: 1.38, 95% CI: 1.26, 1.52). In dose-response analysis, we found that hazard for all-cause, CVD-cause, and cancer-cause mortality increased monotonically with higher BRI (all p−Nonliner<0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 1A-1 C).

Association of BAA with hazard of death (path c)

Compared with the KDM-BA acceleration and PhenoAge acceleration in the lowest quartile (Supplementary Table 3), the highest quartile had the greatest hazard of all-cause death, corresponding to HR (95% CI) were (1.59 (1.52, 1.66)) and (1.78 (1.70, 1.86)), respectively. Compared with the KDM-BA acceleration in the lowest quartile (Supplementary Tables 4–5), the highest quartile had the greatest hazard of CVD death and cancer death, corresponding to HR (95% CI) were (2.19 (1.98, 2.42)) and (1.24 (1.17, 1.32)), respectively. For PhenoAge acceleration (Supplementary Tables 4–5), the highest quartile had the greatest hazard of CVD-cause and cancer-cause death, corresponding to HR (95% CI) were (2.05 (1.84, 2.29)) and (1.51 (1.41, 1.60)), respectively. The RCS regression indicated a nonlinear relationship between KDM-BA acceleration with the hazard of all-cause, CVD-cause, and cancer-cause mortality (all p−Nonlinear<0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 1D-1 F), whereas PhenoAge acceleration exhibited a linear correlation with both of these outcomes (p−Nonlinear= 0.0710, 0.7779, 0.4861, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. 2A-C).

Mediation analysis (path d)

The proportion (95% CI) of BRI mediated by KDM-BA acceleration and all-cause death, CVD death and cancer death were 42.98% (35.15–54.00%), 38.52% (28.47–53.00%), and 23.88% (17.12–38.00%), respectively (Supplementary Table 6). Whereas, the proportion (95% CI) of BRI mediated by PhenoAge acceleration and all-cause death, CVD death, cancer death were 12.73% (10.08–16.00%), 13.64% (9.46–21.00%), and 10.75% (7.60–18.00%), respectively (Supplementary Table 6).

Sensitivity analysis

Firstly, we analyzed the data without BAA, the results showed that BRI in the highest quantile was closely related to an increased hazard of all-cause mortality and CVD mortality (Supplementary Table 77). Second, we excluded 904 all-cause death events with a follow-up of ≤ 2 years, both BRI and BAA in the Q4 group showed strong associations with all-cause death, CVD death, and cancer death (Supplementary Tables 8–9). Third, we test the robustness of the results by analyzing the data set with uninterpolated missing values (Supplementary Tables 10–11). Fourth, we evaluated CVD deaths and cancer deaths separately using the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model (Supplementary Tables 12–13). Fifth, we conducted a sensitivity analysis of the mediating effect (Supplementary Tables 14–15), and the results showed that the mediating effect of BAA in the association between BRI and mortality remained significant.

Discussion

Key findings

In this large cohort study, compared with the individuals with the lowest BRI quartile, the individuals with the highest BRI quartile had a higher hazard of death, and the HR and 95% CI of all-cause death, CVD death and cancer death were (HR: 1.43 (95% CI: 1.33, 1.53)), (HR: 1.47 (95% CI: 1.25, 1.72)), (HR: 1.38 (95% CI: 1.26, 1.52)), respectively. The mediation proportion of BAA in associations of BRI with death outcomes were 10.75–42.98%. Our study is the first to explore the underlying mechanisms of BRI and adverse outcomes, providing new insights into obesity-related accelerated aging research.

Interpretation of the results and comparison with other studies

Although BRI could estimate the percentage of total and regional fat, the evidence from previous studies on the association between BRI and mortality is controversial. For example, Zhang and Zhou et al. ‘s study observed a U-shaped risk trajectory associated with BRI and all-cause mortality [15, 27]. Another a study found that BRI in the stable group was strongly associated with CVD and risk of death [28]. Our findings suggest that the hazard of death increases significantly as BRI levels rise. The main reason for this inconsistency is the heterogeneity of the included population. The NHANES data used by Zhang and Zhou included adults aged 18 years and older, while our study population ranged from 37 to 69 years. These studies have interpreted the U-shaped results to mean that low levels of BRI may signify frailty and nutritional deficiencies [29], while high levels of BRI indicate the accumulation of visceral fat [30, 31]. However, our study population was mainly middle-aged, which is more likely due to the potential harm brought by higher levels of BRI. In addition, we further included CVD mortality and cancer mortality, as CVD and cancer are the leading causes of the global increase in disability-adjusted life years and mortality [32]. Recent studies have shown that accelerated aging is associated with various diseases [33], such as CVD [20], diabetes [34], chronic lung disease [22], and more. Our study found that accelerated aging is associated with an increased hazard of all-cause death, CVD death, and cancer death.

There are no studies investigating the mediating effects of BAA and BRI on death hazard. Previously, DNA methylation profiles were utilized to evaluate biological age [35]; however, the associated cost was significant. Our study constructs an accelerated aging marker based on clinical biochemical markers to evaluate its association with mortality hazard. The KDM-BA assumes that biological age progresses linearly with time [36], whereas the PhenoAge model posits an exponential increase in biological age over time, making KDM-BA more sensitive to chronological age [19]. Therefore, in our study, the mediating effect is also greater than that of PhenoAge.

The mediating role of accelerated aging in BRI’s association with all-cause mortality, CVD mortality, and cancer mortality might be explained by inflammation/immunity. First, higher levels of WC indicates abdominal obesity [37], which may trigger low-grade chronic inflammation [38], thus accelerating aging. The decline of adipose tissue function during obesity and aging is related to the loss of plasticity [39]. Adipose tissue expands beyond vascular supply, leading to local hypoxia, which inhibits lipogenesis and induces hypertrophic adipocytes to secrete inflammatory cytokines, through apoptosis and to spill lipid in an uncontrolled manner [40]. As a result, fat tissue becomes insulin-resistant, inflamed and fibrotic, further impairing its function [41, 42]. Meanwhile, adipose tissue experiences a higher rate of lipolysis through adipocyte hypertrophy (increase in fat cell size) [43], especially large fat cells, and produces higher levels of inflammatory cytokines, resulting in increased fasting insulin, reduced insulin sensitivity, and elevated blood glucose levels. Secondly, the indicators included in KDM-BA, such as ALB, AKP, BUN, Scr and CRP, could reflect the individual inflammatory situation, while HbA1c and TC could also aggravate the inflammatory. The MCV, RDW, WBC and lymphocyte ratio included in PhenoAge acceleration reflect an individual’s immune status. Inflammatory/immunity status is involved in the progression of CVD death and cancer death. Given the cross-sectional assessment of BRI and BAA, the observed mediation should be interpreted as verifying the hypothesis. The proposed mechanisms (e.g., inflammation/immunity) are speculative and require longitudinal validation.

Another mediating mechanism of accelerated aging could be explained as lipid metabolism disorders BRI→accelerated aging (lipid metabolism)→adverse outcomes. Compared to traditional measures, BRI better reflected the percentage of total and abdominal fat [8, 44]. Abdominal obesity indicates proliferation and enlargement of fat cells [45]. This could lead to changes in the function of fat cells, such as abnormal metabolism of free fatty acids and cell hypoxia [46–48]. The ability of proliferative adipose tissue to expand decreases with aging [49]. Aging-induced progenitor cell deficits in adipocytes include decreased expression of pro-lipogenic transcription factors and impaired proliferative capacity [50–52]. Dysregulation of lipid metabolism is another pathway for CVD death and cancer death [53, 54]. Using another measure of aging, PhenoAge acceleration, we observed similar results, albeit with a weaker mediating effect size, that aging partially mediates the relationship between BRI and death.

The inverse association (HR < 1) observed in the Fine-Gray competitive risk model in supplementary Table 13 is inconsistent with the Cox model results in supplementary Tables 3–5, 9 and 11, mainly due to the time-dependent effect of competitive events. When the BAA significantly increased the hazard of cancer death (such as in the Q2/Q3 group), these individuals died of cancer earlier and were removed from the CVD risk set. In the competitive risk model, these competitive event individuals were retained in the risk concentration (but with reduced weights), resulting in an artificial decrease in the cumulative incidence of CVD in the moderate BAA group, manifested as HR < 1; the Cox model simply regards competitive events as deletions, reflecting the direct biological effect of BAA on CVD (HR ≥ 1). This difference is not a true protective effect but rather a manifestation of the mathematical characteristics of statistical models under the interference of competitive events, highlighting the multi-pathway impact of accelerated biological age on indirectly reducing the burden of observable CVD by increasing the hazard of cancer mortality.

The significant fluctuations in the proportion of mediating effects (from 42.98% in the base model to 81.98% excluding early events and 7.89% without interpolating covariates) mainly result from the sensitivity of mediating analysis to data structure and time frame. Excluding the two years before follow-up that amplified the chronic cumulative effect of accelerated biological age (BAA) and artificially increased its contribution; However, the selection bias and insufficient confounding control introduced by the uninterpolated covariates weakened the observed mediating effect. These differences do not deny the mediating role of BAA (all models show significant non-zero indirect effects), but rather reveal the dynamic nature of the contribution of the mediating effect - its specific values are influenced by research design (such as time window setting), data processing (such as interpolation methods), and model assumptions (such as competition risk), etc.

Public health and clinical implication

In fact, our study has important implications for the hazard of adult death, particularly CVD death and cancer death. We observed that higher levels of BRI increased the hazard of death through accelerated aging. This suggests that maintaining a healthy lifestyle and reducing the inflammatory/immune response and lipid metabolism abnormalities associated with obesity are critical to reducing the burden of chronic disease. From an individual level perspective, future intervention targets will be raising awareness of the health effects of BRI and maintaining WC at appropriate levels that might be contribute to self-health management.

Strength and limitation

Our study is the first to explore underlying mechanisms of the association between BRI and death hazard. However, we should admit some limitations. There may be residual confounding even when we control for multiple covariates. Second, we used measures of aging constructed by combining multi-system blood biomarkers, rather than treating blood biomarkers as a single variable. Third, we did not study the trajectory changes of BRI and BAA, mainly due to the limitations of the original database, which did not repeat measurement and follow-up of the death hazard. Fourth, the cross-sectional measurement of both BRI and BAA precludes causal inferences about the directionality of their association. Although mediation analysis was employed, it cannot establish causality between BRI, BAA, and mortality. Our findings on biological aging as a mediator should be interpreted as exploratory. Fifth, due to different research purposes and the limitation of the article length, we did not compare the specificity of BRI with other obesity indicators (such as WC, WHR, WtHR, visceral obesity index, etc.) in biologically accelerated aging. Finally, due to the intricate nature of aging mechanisms, devising more refined measures of aging and linking them to the processes of “normal aging” is exceptionally challenging. For instance, the prevalence of chronic diseases observed today stems from a multifaceted interplay of risk factors that cannot solely be attributed to accelerated aging. Consequently, in this study, accelerated aging only partially mediates the relationship between BRI and death hazard.

Conclusion

We found that higher levels of BRI and BAA were strongly associated with an increased mortality hazard of all-cause, CVD and cancer in adults. BAA partially mediates these associations. BRI could be used as a new indicator to evaluate mortality hazard and be incorporated into public health practice.
