Estimation Strategies for Causal Decomposition Analysis with Allowability Specifications
John W. Jackson, Ting-Hsuan Chang, Aster Meche, Trang Quynh Nguyen

Abstract
Causal decomposition analysis (CDA) is an approach for modeling the impact of hypothetical interventions to reduce disparities. It is useful for identifying foci that future interventions, including multilevel and multimodal interventions, could focus on to reduce disparities. Based within the potential outcomes framework, CDA has a causal interpretation when the identifying assumptions are met. CDA also allows an analyst to consider which covariates are allowable (i.e., fair) for defining the disparity in the outcome and in the point of intervention, so that its interpretation is also meaningful. While the incorporation of causal inference and allowability promotes robustness, transparency, and dialogue in disparities research, it can lead to challenges in estimation such as the need to correctly model densities. Also, how CDA differs from commonly used estimators may not be clear, which may limit its uptake. To address these challenges, we provide a tour of estimation strategies for CDA, reviewing existing proposals and introducing novel estimators that overcome key estimation challenges. Among them we introduce what we call ”bridging” estimators that avoid directly modeling any density, and weighted sequential regression estimators that are multiply robust. Additionally, we provide diagnostics to assess the quality of the nuisance density models and weighting functions they rely on. We formally establish the estimators’ robustness to model mis-specification, demonstrate their performance through a simulation study based on real data, and apply them to study disparities in hypertension control using electronic health records in a large healthcare system.



1
Introduction

Causal decomposition analysis (CDA) is an approach to understand how an observed outcome disparity would be reduced, and how much would remain, upon hypothetically eliminating the disparity in a point of intervention, a factor hypothesized to causally affect the outcome. CDA can identify factors that interventions to reduce disparities could intervene upon. A key feature of CDA, as developed in
31
, is to explicitly specify which covariate differences are allowable (i.e., fair) for defining disparity in the outcome and in the point of intervention so that the estimands are meaningful and transparent to stakeholders
[
8
]
. For example, measures of disparity in healthcare typically account for differences in clinical need, which are often seen as allowable (i.e., not part of the disparity)
[
14
,
12
]
.

Although CDA was introduced in epidemiology,
[
57
,
28
,
31
]
, it has been extended across the social sciences (see
46
for a summary). However, recent innovations sometimes overlook the issue of allowability, and many estimators, including multiply robust estimators, require correctly modeling the distribution of the variable designated as the point of intervention, limiting their robustness properties. The new estimators require many nuisance components, but few practical diagnostics are available to evaluate their quality. Further, their relevance and connection to traditional statistical decomposition techniques used in the applied research community is unclear, potentially limiting their uptake. Our review and novel contributions aim to overcome these limitations.

1.1
Contributions & Related Literature

We tour estimation strategies for causal decomposition analysis (CDA) that can explicitly incorporate assumptions about which covariates reflect fair sources of outcome differences (outcome-allowability) and which covariates are appropriate for guiding interventions (intervention-allowability). Classifying estimators by whether they do or do not directly model densities for the point-of-intervention variable or covariates, we further organize estimators by whether they focus on re-weighting, outcome modeling, or both. As part of our tour, we generalize the proposals of
28
,
54

45

[
40
,
5
,
15
]
to incorporate assumptions about causality and allowability. Our tour updates the review of
42
that focused on continuous outcomes and ignored allowability, and of
46
that covered allowability but did not examine estimation details.

Our work also develops new estimators that overcome key challenges in CDA, including the need to model densities and the need for robust estimation. We introduce novel ”bridging” estimators that avoid posing models for densities and instead rely on constructing samples that facilitate use of empirical densities or indirect modeling of density ratios. For both modeling and bridging strategies, we introduce novel weighted sequential regression estimators
[
25
,
20
]
that are multiply robust, remaining consistent when certain nuisance components are incorrect. We prove their robustness properties, demonstrate their consistency and robustness through simulation, and show their relation to the influence function, which we derive in the Supplement. They complement recent estimators
[
34
,
59
,
43
]
based on Augmented Inverse Probability Weighting
[
47
,
21
]
that accommodate machine learning tools but, relying on models for densities, are limited to discrete points of intervention. We limit our review of multiply robust estimators to weighted sequential regression estimators because they are intuitive and readily accommodate discrete, continuous, and multiple points of intervention.

Finally, we provide tools to improve the implementation of CDA estimators. Many estimators, even ones that are multiply robust, require a correctly specified model for the density of the point of intervention or covariates, limiting their robustness. We therefore propose diagnostics that evaluate the quality of these density models. Furthermore, many estimators make use of weighting functions that, unlike other estimands in causal inference
[

,
2

29
,
30

Our diagnostics for densities and weighting functions support a design-based estimation process where analysts can evaluate and address the potential quality of their estimator before obtaining the results.

Statistical versus Causal Decomposition Analysis

2.1
Statistical Decomposition Analysis

4
Estimation Strategies for the Intervention Arm

4.1
Overview

Here, we present a range of strategies for estimating
\theta_{g}^{*}
, the outcome mean of the group-specific outcome mean in the intervention arm, used to estimate the disparity in the intervention arm. By definition of the intervention,
\theta_{0}^{*}=\theta_{0}
, so we can estimate
\theta_{0}^{*}
using the approaches described for the observation arm. Hence, in this section, we focus on estimation strategies for
\theta_{1}^{*}
. These include a linear estimator, of interest for its simplicity, and two classes of flexible estimators that employ what we call density-modeling and density-bridging strategies. The modeling estimators model one critical density (e.g.,
\lambda_{0}^{Z}
) and average over the empirical version of the other critical density (e.g.,
\lambda_{1}^{N}
). The bridging strategies avoid the parametric critical density model by exploiting the conditional independence of
Z
and
N
among
G=1
in the intervention arm. Remarkably, this strategy facilitates indirect modeling of the critical density
2
2

2

By ”indirect modeling of the critical density” we mean modeling, as a function of odds ratios, a density ratio with different variables in the conditioning sets of the numerator and denominator densities, where the critical density is the numerator.
or averaging over its empirical version.

We classify the flexible estimators according to whether (a) they use a critical density modeling strategy or a bridging strategy and (b) the modeling or bridging strategy revolves around
Z
or
N
. This leads to four strategies for flexible estimation:
Z-modeling
,
N-modeling
,
Z-bridging
, and
N-bridging
. For each of the four flexible estimation strategies, we present (a) a
Pure Weighting
(PW) estimator that requires no model for the outcome, (b) an
Sequential Expectation (SE)
estimator that only requires sequential models for the outcome and pseudo-outcome
3
3

3

By ”pseudo-outcome”, we mean the predicted value from the previous regression step in the sequential regression procedure.
(c) a
Weighted Sequential Expectation estimator (WSE)
that weights the sequential regressions of the SE estimator to target the covariate space of the intervention arm, and (d) a
Regress-then-Weight
(RW) estimator that combines weighting and outcome regressions (see Supplement).

The linear estimator, PW, RW, and SE estimators will consistently estimate
\theta_{1}^{*}
when all of their nuisance components are correctly specified or consistently esimated. In contrast, the WSE estimators are consistent for
\theta_{1}^{*}
in
2^{J}
ways, where

refers to the estimator’s count of outcome and pseudo-outcome regression steps, permitting some nuisance components to be incorrectly specified or inconsistent. The robustness of each outcome regression step follows from use of a model form with the mean recovery property and weights that target the covariate space of the intervention arm. Under mean recovery, the outcome regression will always recover the mean outcome in the sample space used to fit the model. Thus, when the weights for that regression step are consistently estimated, the intervention arm’s outcome mean is obtained regardless of the outcome regression’s specification. Conversely, when the outcome regression is correctly specified, the intervention arm’s outcome mean is obtained regardless of the weights. The linear estimator and certain PW, RW, and SE modeling estimators generalize existing proposals by accommodating intervention-allowability. To our knowledge, all of the WSE estimators, and the all of the bridging estimators, are novel proposals.

4.2
E-OBD: Linear Estimator

The linear estimator is an extension of the detailed Oaxaca-Blinder Decomposition estimator of

5

\displaystyle\text{E}_{1}[Y|Z,N,A_{z},A_{y}]=\beta_{0}+\beta_{z}Z+\beta_{n}N+\beta_{a_{z}}A_{z}+\beta_{a_{y}}A_{y}.

\displaystyle\theta_{1}^{*}=\frac{\text{E}_{1}[\omega_{1T}^{A_{y}}\times Y]}{\text{E}_{1}[\omega_{1T}^{A_{y}}]}+\beta_{z}\Bigg(\frac{\text{E}_{0}[\omega_{01}^{A_{z}}\times\omega_{0T}^{A_{y}}\times Z]}{\text{E}_{0}[\omega_{01}^{A_{z}}\times\omega_{0T}^{A_{y}}]}-\frac{\text{E}_{1}[\omega_{1T}^{A_{y}}\times Z]}{\text{E}_{1}[\omega_{1T}^{A_{y}}]}\Bigg),

\displaystyle\coloneq\frac{\text{P}_{0}(Z|A_{z},A_{y})\times\text{P}_{1}(N|A_{z},A_{y})}{\text{P}_{1}(Z,N|A_{z},A_{y})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{P}_{\blackdiamond}(Z|N,A_{z},A_{y})}{\text{P}_{1}(Z|N,A_{z},A_{y})}\times\frac{\text{P}_{\blackdiamond}(N|A_{z},A_{y})}{\text{P}_{1}(N|A_{z},A_{y})}\times\frac{\text{P}_{0}(Z|A_{z},A_{y})}{\text{P}_{\blackdiamond}(Z|N,A_{z},A_{y})}\times\frac{\text{P}_{1}(N|A_{z},A_{y})}{\text{P}_{\blackdiamond}(N|A_{z},A_{y})}

(20)

The intuition is revealed in the second equality. The first term morphs the observed distribution of
Z
,
\text{P}_{1}(Z|N,A_{z},A_{y})
, to its convenient distribution,
\text{P}_{\blackdiamond}(Z|N,A_{z},A_{y})
, which the third term morphs to
\lambda_{0}^{Z}
. Thus, the convenient distribution of
\blackdiamond
serves as a bridge to
\lambda_{0}^{Z}
. Because
\text{P}_{\blackdiamond}(Z|N,A_{z},A_{y})=\text{P}_{\blackdiamond}(Z|A_{z},A_{y})
, the third term can be re-expressed as a ratio of odds functions to model
\lambda_{0}^{Z}
indirectly. The second and fourth terms retain
\lambda_{1}^{N}
. The Z-Bridge-PW estimator is consistent for
\theta_{1}^{*}
if
\omega_{1\blackdiamond 1^{*}}^{(Z,N)}
(Eqn.
20
) is consistently estimated.

Z-Bridge-SE: Sequential Expectation

\blackdiamond

\displaystyle\theta_{1}^{*}

\displaystyle=\text{E}_{\text{std}}\big[\text{E}_{1}[\kappa_{1}^{*}(A_{z},A_{y})|A_{y}]\big]

\displaystyle=\text{E}_{\text{std}}\Big[\text{E}_{1}\bigl[\text{E}_{0}[\zeta_{1}^{*}(Z,A_{z},A_{y})|A_{y},A_{z}]\big|A_{y}\big]\Big]

\displaystyle=\text{E}_{\text{std}}\bigg[\text{E}_{1}\Bigl[\text{E}_{0}\bigl[\text{E}_{\blackdiamond}[\mu_{1}(Z,N,A_{y},A_{z})|Z,A_{z},A_{y}]\big|A_{y},A_{z}\big]\Big|A_{y}\Big]\bigg],

where

model). Because

4.5.3

\displaystyle\omega_{\blackdiamond 1^{*}}^{(Z,N)}

\displaystyle\coloneq\frac{\text{P}_{0}(Z|A_{z},A_{y})\times\text{P}_{1}(N|A_{z},A_{y})}{\text{P}_{\blackdiamond}(Z,N|A_{z},A_{y})}

The

-bridging estimators avoid directly modeling either critical density, but are more complex than the

- and

-modeling estimators. The

-bridging estimators also avoid directly modeling

and

but are simpler. They create an alternate artificial sample
\diamond
where

follows a convenient distribution
\text{P}_{\text{c}}(N|\cdot)
that is independent of

given
(A_{z},A_{y})

Definition 4.5

(Convenient Distribution

)
.

The convenient distribution
\text{P}_{\text{c}}(N|\cdot)
is chosen as any distribution of
N
that is independent of
Z
given
(A_{z},A_{y})
and covers
\mathcal{N}
the support of
N
in the
G=1
group.

Definition 4.6

(Artificial Sample
\diamond
)
.

The artificial sample
G=\diamond
is one with a joint distribution
\text{P}_{\diamond}(Z,N,A_{z},A_{y})
where
\text{P}_{\diamond}(N|A_{z},A_{y})\equiv\text{P}_{\text{c}}(N|\cdot)
and
\text{P}_{\diamond}(Z,A_{z},A_{y})\equiv\text{P}_{0}(Z,A_{z},A_{y})
.

When
N
is discrete, we can construct the artificial sample
\diamond
as follows: 1) Determine the set of unique values
N
in the support
\mathcal{N}
of
N
in the
G=1
group; 2) For each person in the
G=0
group, create a clone for each unique value

, where each clone’s

is assigned a unique value of
n
; 3) Retain the original values of
(Z,A_{z},A_{y})
; 4) Stack the clones and discard the original copy. When
N
is continuous or multivariate, we can replace steps 1 and 2 by creating as many clones as computationally feasible and either sampling
N
from the
G=1
group with replacement or drawing
N
from a uniform distribution that covers
\mathcal{N}
the support of
N
in the
G=1
group.

The N-Bridge-PW estimator of

The intuition appears in the second equality. The first term morphs the distribution of

4.7

When no covariates are allowable (i.e., both

and

are empty), the linear estimator (E-OBD; Eqn.

is not), the steps involving

reduce to one. When no covariates are outcome-allowable (i.e.,
A_{y}
is empty but
A_{z}
is not), the steps involving
\eta_{1}^{*}
can be skipped and the weights
\omega_{gT}^{A_{y}}
reduce to one.
\eta_{1}^{*}
is also obviated and
\omega_{1T}^{A_{y}}
reduces to one when
A_{y}
is non-empty but
G=1
is the standard population. When all covariates are intervention-allowable or outcome-allowable (i.e.,
N
is empty but one of
A_{z}
or

is non-empty), the steps involving
\lambda_{1}^{N}
(N-Model estimators) and the steps that involve

or assign or sample values of

\displaystyle=\text{E}_{0}[Y]

\displaystyle\text{E}_{\blacksquare}[h_{1}(N,A_{z},A_{y})]

\displaystyle=\text{E}_{1}[Y]

(28)

for use in

for use in the

-modeling estimators. We want the LHS expectation to return the appropriate observed mean on the RHS. Because the working models

Many of the estimators rely on weighting functions. As density ratios, when consistently estimated, the weights

6.1
Design

We performed a simulation study to examine the consistency, efficiency for
\theta_{1}^{*}
at large (n=5,000) and small (n=500) sample sizes, for continuous and binary outcomes. We also examined the robustness of WSE estimators under mis-specified nuisance models. We omitted estimators for

[

20
]
.

The data generating model (DGM) for the observed data was informed by the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey
[
18
]
and the clinical literature
[
17
]
, adapting the procedure of
8
. We generated systolic blood pressure
Y
at follow-up as a continuous outcome, and dichotomized it as
W

A_{y1}
and sex
A_{y2}

A_{z1}
and diabetes
A_{z2}

N_{1}
and insurance status

and race

depended on systolic blood pressure
A_{z1}
and group membership

\geq

As described in section

, we cast our estimand in a study design where (i) each social group

We specified all estimators’ nuisance models with correct models following the DGM, flexible models including higher order terms and two-way interactions between selected covariates, and incorrect models excluding higher order terms and key covariates

7
Data Application

We analyzed data from 79,898 primary care visits of 25,287 patients seen in the Johns Hopkins Community Physicians health system from 2018 to 2022. The cohort was assembled to emulate a target trial where each trial’s “time zero” was anchored on bi-weekly calendar intervals. Patients could enroll in a trial (including multiple trials) whenever they met the following eligibility criteria: self-reported race as measured in the electronic medical record as “Black” or “White”, current office visit with elevated blood pressure (BP) readings (systolic BP
\geq
140 mm Hg and diastolic BP
\geq
90 mm Hg), no office visit with elevated BP readings in the prior two weeks but 2+ primary care office visits with elevated BP readings in the prior year, were not pregnant or diagnosed with end stage renal disease, liver disease, dementia, or metastatic tumor in the prior year, and all allowable covariates were measured by the time of the current visit defining enrollment into the trial. We considered age and sex as outcome allowable
A_{y}
. We considered body mass index, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, congestive heart failure, baseline numbers of antihypertensive medications and medication classes, current systolic and diastolic BP as intervention-allowable
A_{z}
, as these reflect relevant factors used by clinical guidelines to determine antihypertensive medication regimens
[
58
]
. As non-allowable covariates
N
we considered the number of prior primary care visits, type of health insurance, and the national percentile of the area deprivation index, all measured at baseline. Our point of intervention
Z
was treatment intensification, which was measured as an initiation of or increase in dose or addition of a new class of an antihypertensive medication within the two weeks following the current visit, the date of the visit marking the start of follow-up. We measured our outcomes systolic BP
Y
and controlled hypertension
W
by taking BP measures from the closest visit to the 6-month follow-up mark between 4.5 and 7.5 months. Further details on the dataset and its construction are available in
35
.

7.1
Analysis

We defined the Black population as the historically disadvantaged group
G=1
and the standard population
T=1
, and the White population as the historically advantaged group
G=0
. We used the weighting estimator of section
3
to measure the disparity in antihypertensive treatment intensification, adjusting for all allowable covariates. We estimated
\theta_{1}^{*}
the mean systolic BP and proportion of uncontrolled hypertension for
G=1
in the intervention arm of our target trial using the estimators described in section
4
. We estimated
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{0}
the group-specific mean outcomes of the observation arm using the estimators described in section
3
, and calculated the reduced disparity for each outcome. When implementing these estimators, we included cubic splines for age and systolic BP, and interactions between treatment intensification and (i) treatment status at baseline as well as (ii) number of concomitant treatment classes at baseline. We used multinomial models to estimate the factored joint distribution of the non-allowables, after discretizing the area deprivation index distribution into 10 evenly spaced categories across the response scale. To estimate the average means and disparities over 2018-2022, we adjusted for calendar time using a cubic splines by including it along with outcome-allowable covariates
A_{y}
as discussed in
26
. For the bridging estimators, we created the artificial samples by sampling the marginal distributions of
Z
or
N
. We addressed missing outcomes using the methods described in the Supplement. Because patients could enroll for each trial where they met eligibility, we accounted for within-person correlations by estimating 95% confidence intervals obtained via a non-parametric cluster bootstrap
[
16
]
that sampled persons with replacement and retained all observations of sampled persons. We assed the quality of our nuisance models using the diagnostics described in section
5
.

7.2
Results

Black persons received slightly less treatment intensification than White patients by 1.9% (95% CI 1.2%, 2.7%), after accounting for outcome- and intervention-allowable covariates. At follow up, Black and White patients had an average systolic BP of 142 and 139 mm Hg, respectively, with an observed disparity of 2.8 mm Hg (95%CI 2.4, 3.3), after accounting for outcome-allowable covariates. With respect to uncontrolled hypertension, 53% and 48% of Black and White patients, respectively, had uncontrolled hypertension, with an observed disparity of 5.5% (95%CI 4.3%, 6.6%). Table
3
shows the estimated results and 95% confidence intervals for outcome mean in the intervention arm of the envisioned target trial, along with the reduced disparity and the residual disparity. The diagnostics for the critical density models of
\lambda_{0}^{Z}
and
\lambda_{1}^{N}
and the weights suggested that the nuisance models for the estimators were adequately specified (see Supplement Figures
S1
-
S19
). Interestingly, the varaibility of the
\omega^{(Z,N)}
weights was substantially greater for the forms used in the modeling strategies compared to the forms used in the bridging strategies (see Table

). The results were similar across all estimators, showing outcome means of of 142 mm Hg systolic BP and a proportion with uncontrolled hypertension of 53% among the Black group under the hypothetical intevention, and a negligible reduction in the observed disparity in systolic BP and in uncontrolled hypertension. The estimators’ confidence interval width were wider for the linear and modeling estimators. The small effect size likely stems from the small disparity in treatment intensification. Overall, the results suggest that, in this healthcare system, an intervention to equalize existing disparities in treatment intensification would have little impact on hypertension control disparities.

8
Conclusion

In this paper, we toured estimation strategies for CDA, including existing methods and introduced novel estimators that overcome key challenges, and provided diagnostics for their implementation. We examined estimators that model either the distribution of
Z
(the point of intervention) or
N
(the non-allowable covariates). We also introduced estimators that build ”bridge” samples to facilitate empirical or indirect modeling of the distributions of
Z
or
N
. For each estimator class, we presented or developed pure weighting (PW), sequential regressions (SE), and multiply robust Weighted SE (WSE) estimators (and non-robust Regress-then-Weight estimators, see the Supplement). We showed that these estimators are consistent and that they relate to the influence function of our estimand. We also showed that certain estimators, under simplifying circumstances, reduce to causal versions of statistical decomposition estimators that are widely used in the applied research community.

Here, we provide some guidance for choosing between estimators. The simplicity of the linear estimator is valuable pedagogically, but its assumptions are overly restrictive. Of the more flexible estimators, the Z-Modeling estimators are very intuitive, and require fitting the fewest number of models. They may be advantageous when one has adequate substantive knowledge to correctly model
\lambda_{0}^{Z}
, the interventional distribution of the estimand (the critical density of
Z
), which may be limited to scenarios where
Z
is binary. If chosen, we recommend using the diagnostic for this distribution to support their implementation. We do not prioritize the use of the N-Modeling estimators over their alternatives, as modeling the density of covariates well is both challenging and tedious, as reflected in their worse performance. Both bridging estimators avoid modeling any density directly, but the N-Bridge estimators require fewer models, and in our simulation results they dominated every other estimator in their respective subclass. Of the WSE estimators, the N-Bridging estimator had the best overall performance across scenarios. The N-Bridge estimators therefore offer a very attractive option for routine estimation, especially when dealing with continuous or multivariate points of intervention, but the Z-Briding estimators remain a viable option. Within a given estimation strategy, if the weights can be estimated well and are not highly variable, the WSE estimators could serve as a first-line option. Otherwise, the SE estimators would be the next logical choice. The PW estimators or their RW counterparts can be used when the outcome is difficult to model or one wishes to fit a specialized model such as Cox proportional hazards or quantile regression.

Our multiply robust WSE estimators do not make use of machine learning tools that are advocated for when covariates or their interactions are high-dimensional. They could be incorporated via Cross-validated Targeted Minimum Loss Estimation
[
56
,
13
]
, which satisfies the mean recovery condition for outcome models. Alternatively, augmented balancing weights that leverage covariate balance conditions
[
9
,
3
]
, or emerging techniques for density ratio estimation
[
24
]
, could be used to estimate the weights directly. Each of these proposals can accommodate machine learning but this is left for future work. Importantly, unlike current AIPW-based proposals for CDA (
[
34
,
59
,
43
]
), our WSE estimators readily generalize to continuous and multivariate points of intervention. Furthermore, the sensitivity of machine-learning enabled AIPW estimators to random-seed selection, their performance in finite samples, and the optimal choice of folds in their required sample splitting and cross-validation procedures remains an active area of research
[
10
,
36
,
48
,
37
,
60
,
1
]
. The robustness of our WSE estimators can be enhanced by guiding their implementation with our proposed diagnostics for modeled densities and weighting functions. Moreover, we provided different forms of the
\omega^{(Z,N)}
weights used to fit the initial
\mu_{1}
outcome regressions. Because the various forms rely on different nuisance models, they can be swapped across WSE estimators, offering additional avenues for robustness.

53
discusses proper variable selection for multiply robust estimators. We advise users to include the same respective set of covariates for outcome-allowables
A_{y}
, intervention-allowables
A_{z}
, and non-allowables
N
in whichever nuisance models they appear. If investigators vary
N
, perhaps out of necessity such as in high-dimensional settings, they should ensure that the covariate set for
N
used in the outcome regressions can overcome any biases not addressed by the covariate set for
N
used in the weighting functions and vice versa. See
53
for further details. Sensitivity analysis for unmeasured confounding in CDA is also important avenue to assess robustness to variable selection
[
41
,
49
]
.

Regarding statistical inference, all of our estimators are M-estimators
[
52
]
, and therefore are asymptotically normal and are amenable to bootstrap procedures, including ones developed for clustered data
[
16
]
or very large datasets
[
32
]
. Our simulation results suggest that the standard errors for our multiply robust estimators, when obtained using bootstrap methods or M-estimation theory, may also enjoy a multiple robustness property. They may also achieve the non-parametric efficiency bound, as weighted regression estimators for the average treatment effect do generally
[
20
,
50
]
. Proof of these conjectures for our estimators is left for future work.

A.1
Regress-the-Weight (RW) Estimators

An alternate weighted average targeting
\theta_{1}^{*}
suggests a weighting function that takes covariate data from the
G=0
group and morphs the observed density
\text{P}_{0}(Z,N,A_{z}|A_{y})
to that of
G=1
in the intervention arm
\text{P}_{1}^{*}(Z,N,A_{z}|A_{y})
, and morphs the density of
A_{y}
to that of the standard population, using the pseudo-outcome
\mu_{1}(Z,N,A_{z},A_{y})
:

\displaystyle\theta_{1}^{*}=\frac{\text{E}_{0}\Big[\mu_{1}(Z,N,A_{z},A_{y})\times\frac{\text{P}^{*}_{1}(Z,N,A_{z}|A_{y})}{\text{P}_{0}(Z,N,A_{z}|A_{y})}\times\frac{\text{P}_{\text{std}}(A_{y})}{\text{P}_{0}(A_{y})}\Big]}{\text{E}_{0}\Big[\frac{\text{P}^{*}_{1}(Z,N,A_{z}|A_{y})}{\text{P}_{0}(Z,N,A_{z}|A_{y})}\times\frac{\text{P}_{\text{std}}(A_{y})}{\text{P}_{0}(A_{y})}\Big]}.

This approach forms the basis for many of the regress-then-weight approaches we now present. Unlike the WSE estimators that also combine weighting and outcome regressions, these estimators are only consistent for
\theta_{1}^{*}
when all of their nusiance models are correctly specified and weighting functions are consistently estimated.

A.1.1
Z-Model-RW

One possibility for a Z-Model-RW estimator of
\theta_{1}^{*}
is base it on Eqn.
15
, which takes a weighted mean among the
G=1
group, but replace outcomes
Y
with pseudo-outcomes obtained from a model of
\mu_{1}(Z,N,A_{z},A_{y})
. Alternatively, we can take a weighed mean of the outcome among the
G=0
group:

\displaystyle\theta_{1}^{*}=\frac{\text{E}_{0}[\omega_{01^{*}}^{(Z,N):Z}\times\omega_{0T}^{A_{y}}\times\mu_{1}(Z,N,A_{z},A_{y})]}{\text{E}_{0}[\omega_{01^{*}}^{(Z,N):Z}\times\omega_{0T}^{A_{y}}]},

(30)

where the weighting function
\omega_{01^{*}}^{(Z,N,A_{z}):Z}(Z,N,A_{z},A_{y})
is:

\displaystyle\omega_{01^{*}}^{(Z,N,A_{z}):Z}

\displaystyle\coloneq\frac{\text{P}_{0}(Z|A_{z},A_{y})\times\text{P}_{1}(N|A_{z},A_{y})\times\text{P}_{1}(A_{z}|A_{y})}{\text{P}_{0}(Z,N,A_{z}|A_{y})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{P}_{0}(Z|A_{z},A_{y})}{\text{P}_{0}(Z|N,A_{z},A_{y})}\times\frac{\text{P}_{1}(N|A_{z},A_{y})}{\text{P}_{0}(N|A_{z},A_{y})}\times\frac{\text{P}_{1}(A_{z}|A_{y})}{\text{P}_{0}(A_{z}|A_{y})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{P}_{0}(Z|A_{z},A_{y})}{\text{P}_{0}(Z|N,A_{z},A_{y})}\times\frac{\text{odds}(G=1\text{ vs }0|N,A_{z},A_{y})}{\text{odds}(G=1\text{ vs }0|A_{y})}..

(31)

The first term captures the imposed conditional independence between
Z
and
N
, where
\lambda_{0}^{Z}
appears. The second term shifts the
(N,A_{z}|A_{y})
distribution from the
G=0
to that of the
G=1
group, where
\lambda_{1}^{N}
is modeled indirectly. The Z-Model-RW estimator is consistent for
\theta_{1}^{*}
if the psuedo-outcome model
\mu_{1}
is correctly specified and the weight
\omega_{11^{*}}^{(Z,N):Z}
(Eqn.
16
) (or
\omega_{01^{*}}^{(Z,N,A_{z}):Z}
[Eqn.
31
]) is consistently estimated.

N-Model-RW

\theta_{1}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{P}_{0}(Z|A_{z},A_{y})}{\text{P}_{0}(Z|A_{z},A_{y})}\times\frac{\text{P}_{1}(N|A_{z},A_{y})}{\text{P}_{0}(N|Z,A_{z},A_{y})}\times\frac{\text{P}_{1}(A_{z}|A_{y})}{\text{P}_{0}(A_{z}|A_{y})}

A.2
Extension to Missing Outcome Data

To address missing outcome data, we first assume that (i) the potential outcome
Y(Z)
is independent of outcome missingness status
C
(
1
yes,
0
no) given the point of intervention
Z
, group
G
, and all baseline covariates
(N,A_{z},A_{y})
(conditional exchangeability with respect to missing outcome data) and (ii) there are no covariate strata where all persons are loss to follow-up (positivity with respect to compete data), Under these additional assumptions,
\theta_{g}
and
\theta_{1}^{*}
can be identified by the PW estimators by incorporating the following inverse probability of censoring weights, which are slight modifications of their usual form in other contexts
[
7
,
27
]
:

\displaystyle\omega_{g}^{C}(C,Z,N,A_{z},A_{y})\coloneq\frac{\text{P}_{g}(C=0)}{\text{P}_{g}(C=0|Z,N,A_{z},A_{y})}.

(35)

Under these assumptions,
\theta_{g}
can also be identified as

\displaystyle\theta_{g}

\displaystyle=\text{E}_{\text{std}}[\eta_{g}(A_{y})]

\displaystyle=\text{E}_{\text{std}}[\text{E}_{g}[\mu_{1}(Z,N,A_{z},A_{y},C=0)]|A_{y}],

(36)

where essentially
\mu_{1}
is fit among those without missing outcome data.
\theta_{1}^{*}
can be obtained in SE and WSE estimators by fitting
\mu_{1}
among those without missing outcome data, i.e.,
\mu_{1}(Z,N,A_{z},A_{y},C=0)
rather than
\mu_{1}(Z,N,A_{z},A_{y})
which is unidentified. In the WSE estimator,
\mu_{1}(Z,N,A_{z},A_{y},C=0)
is fit by incorporating
\omega_{g}^{C}
(Eqn.
35
) into the weights. The WSE estimator remains consistent when either the
\mu_{1}
model is correct or the weights (now inclusive of
\omega_{g}^{C}
) are correctly specified, along with its other robustness conditions. For the RW estimators, under these assumptions, it suffices to fit
\mu_{1}(Z,N,A_{z},A_{y},C=0)
rather than
\mu_{1}(Z,N,A_{z},A_{y},C=0)
in the conditional mean outcome model.

A.6.1
Commonly Used Definitions

The proofs rely on the following identities, which express weighting functions:

\displaystyle\omega_{11^{*}}^{(Z,N)}(Z,N,A_{z},A_{y})

\displaystyle\coloneq\frac{\text{P}_{1}^{*}(Z,N|A_{z},A_{y})}{\text{P}_{1}(Z,N|A_{z},A_{y})}

\displaystyle\omega_{\blackdiamond 1^{*}}^{Z,N}(Z,N,A_{z},A_{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{P}_{1}^{*}(Z,N|A_{z},A_{y})}{\text{P}_{\blackdiamond}(Z,N|A_{z},A_{y}}

\displaystyle\omega_{\diamond 1^{*}}^{Z,N,A_{z}}(Z,N,A_{z},A_{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{P}_{1}^{*}(Z,N,A_{z},|A_{y})}{\text{P}_{\diamond}(Z,N,A_{z}|A_{y}}

\displaystyle\omega_{01}^{A_{z}}(A_{z},A_{y})

\displaystyle\coloneq\frac{\text{P}_{1}^{*}(A_{z}|A_{y})}{\text{P}_{0}(A_{z}|A_{y})}

\displaystyle\omega_{gT}^{A_{y}}(A_{y})

\displaystyle\coloneq\frac{\text{P}_{1}^{*}(A_{y})}{\text{P}_{g}(A_{y})}

Note that sometimes we rename
\omega_{11^{*}}^{(Z,N)}(\cdot)
as
\omega_{1\blackdiamond 1^{*}}^{(Z,N)}(\cdot)
or
\omega_{1\diamond 1^{*}}^{(Z,N)}(\cdot)
as they express the same desnity ratio.

The proofs also rely on the following identities, which express sequential expectataions:

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(Z,N,A_{z},A_{y})

\displaystyle\coloneq\text{E}_{1}[Y|Z,N,A_{z},A_{y}]

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}^{*}(Z,A_{z},A_{y})

\displaystyle\coloneq\int\mu_{1}(Z,n,A_{z},A_{y})\text{P}_{1}^{*}(n|Z,A_{z},A_{y})dn

\displaystyle\nu_{1}^{*}(N,A_{z},A_{y})

\displaystyle\coloneq\int\mu_{1}(z,N,A_{z},A_{y})\text{P}_{1}^{*}(z|N,A_{z},A_{y})dz

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}^{*}(A_{z},A_{y})

\displaystyle\coloneq\int\mu_{1}(z,n,A_{z},A_{y})\text{P}_{1}^{*}(z,n|A_{z},A_{y})dzdn

\displaystyle\eta_{1}^{*}(A_{y})

\displaystyle\coloneq\int\mu_{1}(z,n,a_{z},A_{y})\text{P}_{1}^{*}(z,n,a_{z}|A_{y})dzdnda_{z}

A.6.2
Proof of Linear Estimator (E-OBD)

Given the linear causal model

\displaystyle\text{E}_{1}[Y|Z,N,A_{z},A_{y}]=\beta_{0}+\beta_{z}Z+\beta_{n}N+\beta_{a_{z}}A_{z}+\beta_{a_{y}}A_{y},

(
11
)

we have that

\displaystyle\theta_{1}

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{(z,n,a_{z},a_{y})}\text{E}_{1}[Y|Z=z,N=n,A_{z}=a_{z},A_{y}=a_{y}]

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\times\text{P}_{1}(Z=z|N=n,A_{z}=a_{z},A_{y}=a_{y})

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\times\text{P}_{1}(N=n|A_{z}=a_{z},A_{y}=a_{y})

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\times\text{P}_{1}(A_{z}=a_{z}|A_{y}=a_{y})

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\times\text{P}_{\text{std}}(A_{y}=a_{y})

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{(z,n,a_{z},a_{y})}(\beta_{0}+\beta_{z}z+\beta_{n}n+\beta_{a_{z}}a_{z}+\beta_{a_{y}}a_{y})

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\times\text{P}_{1}(Z=z|N=n,A_{z}=a_{z},A_{y}=a_{y})

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\times\text{P}_{1}(N=n|A_{z}=a_{z},A_{y}=a_{y})

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\times\text{P}_{1}(A_{z}=a_{z}|A_{y}=a_{y})

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\times\text{P}_{1}(A_{y}=a_{y})\frac{P_{\text{std}}(A_{y}=a_{y})}{\text{P}_{1}(A_{y}=a_{y})}

\displaystyle=\beta_{0}+\beta_{z}\mathbb{E}_{1}[Z]+\beta_{n}\mathbb{E}_{1}[N]+\beta_{a_{z}}\mathbb{E}_{1}[A_{z}]+\beta_{a_{y}}\mathbb{E}_{1}[A_{y}]

(37)

where for any variable
X
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{1}[X]=\text{E}_{1}[\omega_{1T}^{A_{y}}X]/\text{E}_{1}[\omega_{1T}^{A_{y}}].

Note that we can also re-express
\theta_{1}
as

\displaystyle\theta_{1}

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{a_{y}}\text{E}_{1}[Y|A_{y}]\text{P}_{\text{std}}(A_{y}=a_{y})

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{a_{y}}\text{E}_{1}[Y|A_{y}=a_{y}]\text{P}_{1}(A_{y}=a_{y})\frac{\text{P}_{\text{std}}(A_{y}=a_{y})}{\text{P}_{1}(A_{y}=a_{y})}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{1}[Y]

(38)

where
\mathbb{E}_{1}[Y]=\text{E}_{1}[\omega_{1T}^{A_{y}}Y]/\text{E}_{1}[\omega_{1T}^{A_{y}}]
.

We also have that

\displaystyle\theta_{1}^{*}

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{(z,n,a_{z},a_{y})}\text{E}_{1}[Y|Z=z,N=n,A_{z}=a_{z},A_{y}=a_{y}]

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\times\text{P}_{0}(Z=z|A_{z}=a_{z},A_{y}=a_{y})

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\times\text{P}_{1}(N=n|A_{z}=a_{z},A_{y}=a_{y})

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\times\text{P}_{1}(A_{z}=a_{z}|A_{y}=a_{y})

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\times\text{P}_{\text{std}}(A_{y}=a_{y})

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{(z,n,a_{z},a_{y})}(\beta_{0}+\beta_{z}z+\beta_{n}n+\beta_{a_{z}}a_{z}+\beta_{a_{y}}a_{y})

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\times\text{P}_{0}(Z=z|A_{z}=a_{z},A_{y}=a_{y})

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\times\text{P}_{1}(N=n|A_{z}=a_{z},A_{y}=a_{y})

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\times\text{P}_{1}(A_{z}=a_{z}|A_{y}=a_{y})\frac{\text{P}_{0}(A_{z}=a_{z}|A_{y}=a_{y})}{\text{P}_{0}(A_{z}=a_{z}|A_{y}=a_{y})}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\times\text{P}_{\text{std}}(A_{y}=a_{y})\frac{\text{P}_{1}(A_{y}=a_{y})}{\text{P}_{1}(A_{y}=a_{y})}\frac{\text{P}_{0}(A_{y}=a_{y})}{\text{P}_{0}(A_{y}=a_{y})}

\displaystyle=\beta_{0}+\beta_{z}\mathbb{E}_{0}^{*}[Z]+\beta_{n}\mathbb{E}_{1}[N]+\beta_{a_{z}}\mathbb{E}_{1}[A_{z}]+\beta_{a_{y}}\mathbb{E}_{1}[A_{y}]

(39)

where for any variable
X
,

\mathbb{E}_{1}[X]=\text{E}_{1}[\omega_{1T}^{A_{y}}]/\text{E}_{1}[\omega_{gT}^{A_{y}}X]

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{*}_{0}[X]=\text{E}_{0}[\omega_{01}^{A_{z}}\omega_{0T}^{A_{y}}X]/\text{E}_{0}[\omega_{01}^{A_{z}}\omega_{0T}^{A_{y}}].

Thus,

\displaystyle\theta_{1}^{*}-\theta_{1}=\beta_{z}(\mathbb{E}_{0}^{*}[Z]-\mathbb{E}_{1}[Z]).

Because
\theta_{1}=\mathbb{E}_{1}[Y]
, it follows that

\displaystyle\theta_{1}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{1}[Y]+\beta_{z}(\mathbb{E}_{0}^{*}[Z]-\mathbb{E}_{1}[Z])

(40)

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{y,z,n,a_{z},a_{y}}y\text{P}_{1}(Y|Z,N,A_{z},A_{y})\text{P}_{0}(Z|A_{z},A_{y})\text{P}_{1}(N|A_{z},A_{y})\text{P}_{1}(A_{z}|A_{y})\text{P}_{\text{std}}(A_{y})

A.6.6
Proof for the Influence function for Observation Arm Means

Lemma A.1
.

\textup{E}[A\mid B,D=d]=\textup{E}\left[\frac{\textup{I}(D=d)}{\textup{P}(D=d\mid B)}A\mid B\right]=\textup{E}\left[\frac{\textup{I}(D=d)}{\textup{P}(D=d)}\frac{\textup{P}(B)}{\textup{P}(B\mid D=d)}A\mid B\right].

For proof of Lemma (
A.1
), see Appendix A3 of
39
.

Theorem 1

(Influence function of
\theta_{g}
)
.

The influence function of
\theta_{g}
is given as:

\displaystyle\varphi_{\theta_{g}}(O)=\varphi_{\theta_{g},I}(O)+\varphi_{\theta_{g},II}(O)

where

\displaystyle\varphi_{\theta_{g},I}(O)

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{I}(G=g)}{\text{P}(G=g)}\omega_{gT}^{A_{y}}{\text{P}_{g}(A_{y})}[Y-\eta_{g}(A_{y})],

\displaystyle\varphi_{\theta_{g},II}(O)

\displaystyle=\frac{T}{\text{P}(T=1)}[\eta_{g}(A_{y})-\theta_{1}].

To simplify presentation, we only need to derive the influence function for
\theta_{1}
and then use symmetry to extrapolate to
\theta_{0}
.

Let
O=\{G,A_{y},Y\}
. Assume we have
n
iid copies of
O
.

Our estimand is

\theta_{1}=\text{E}_{\text{std}}\{\underbrace{\text{E}[Y\mid A_{y},G=1]}_{\eta_{1}(A_{y})}\}.

Consider the following factorization of the joint density of
O
:

\displaystyle\text{P}(O)=\text{P}(G,A_{y}){\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\text{P}(Y\mid G=1,A_{y})^{G}}\text{P}(Y\mid G=0,A_{y})^{1-G}.

The observed-data Hilbert space (ie the space of mean-zero finite variance 1-dimensional functions of
O
equipped with the covariance inner product) is the direct sum of three subspaces:

\mathcal{H}={\color[rgb]{1,0,1}\mathcal{H}_{1}}\oplus{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathcal{H}_{2}}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{3},

where

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}\mathcal{H}_{1}}

\displaystyle={\color[rgb]{1,0,1}\{m(G,A):\text{E}[m(G,A)]=0\}},

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathcal{H}_{2}}

\displaystyle={\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\{Gm(Y,A_{y}):\text{E}[m(Y,A_{y})\mid G=1,A_{y}]=0\}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{3}

\displaystyle=\{(1-G)m(Y,A_{y}):\text{E}[m(Y,A_{y})\mid G=0,A_{y}]=0\}.

Consider a parametric submodel (of the nonparametric model) of
O
based on the factorization above:

\displaystyle f_{o}(O,\beta)={\color[rgb]{1,0,1}f_{1}(G,A_{y},\beta_{1})}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}f_{2}(A_{y},Y,\beta_{2})^{G}}f_{3}(A_{y},Y,\beta_{3})^{1-G}.

To proceed, we want to write the implied model
\theta_{1}(\beta)
for
\theta_{1}
, and find the function
\varphi_{\theta_{1}}(O)\in\mathcal{H}
whose covariance with the score of the parametric submodel is equal to the derivative of
\theta_{1}(\beta)
wrt.
\beta
, evaluated at the truth (denoted
\beta^{0}
).

But the specific form of
\theta_{1}(\beta)
depends on what is known about
\text{P}_{\mathrm{std}}(A_{y})
. We consider two cases:

1.

\text{P}_{\mathrm{std}}(A_{y})
is known. In this case,

\theta_{1}(\beta)=\text{E}_{\text{std}}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\int yf_{2}(A_{y},y,\beta_{2})dy}\right],

which is a function of
\beta_{2}
, so the IF of
\theta_{1}
lies in
\mathcal{H}_{2}
.

2.

\text{P}_{\mathrm{std}}(A_{y})
is only known approximately through data, specifically this is the
A_{y}
distribution of a subpopulation marked with
T=1
where
T
is coded using a binary deterministic function
t(G,A_{y})
. In this case,

\theta_{1}=\text{E}[\eta_{1}(A_{y})\mid T=1]=\text{E}\left[\frac{T}{\text{P}(T=1)}\eta_{1}(A_{y})\right]=\frac{\text{E}\{t(G,A_{y})\text{E}[Y\mid A_{y},G=1]\}}{\text{E}[t(G,A_{y})]},

so

\theta_{1}(\beta)=\frac{\int\!\!\int t(g,a_{y})\int y{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}f_{2}(y,a_{y},\beta_{2})}dy\,{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}f_{1}(g,a_{y},\beta_{1})}da_{y}\,dg}{\int\!\!\int t(g,a_{y}){\color[rgb]{1,0,1}f_{1}(g,a_{y},\beta_{1})}da_{y}\,dg},

which involves both
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
, so the IF for
\theta_{1}
includes a term in
\mathcal{H}_{2}
and a term in
\mathcal{H}_{1}
. We call these two terms
\varphi_{\theta_{1},2}(O)
and
\varphi_{\theta_{1},1}(O)
, respectively. It is easy to see that the term
\varphi_{\theta_{1},2}(O)
is exactly the IF in the known
\text{P}_{\mathrm{std}}(A_{y})
case above.

First, we derive
\varphi_{\theta_{1},2}(O)
.
(Throughout, we assume that regularity conditions hold for interchanging derivatives and integrals.)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\theta_{1}(\beta)}{\partial\beta_{2}}\Big|_{\beta=\beta^{0}}

\displaystyle=\text{E}_{\text{std}}\left[\int y\frac{\partial f_{2}(y,A_{y},\beta_{2})}{\partial\beta_{2}}\Big|_{\beta_{2}=\beta_{2}^{0}}dy\right]

\displaystyle=\text{E}_{\text{std}}\left[\int yS_{2}(y,A_{y},\beta_{2}^{0})\text{P}(y\mid A_{y},G=1)dy\right]

\displaystyle=\text{E}_{\text{std}}\left\{\text{E}[S_{2}(Y,A_{y},\beta_{2}^{0})Y\mid A_{y},G=1]\right\}

\displaystyle=\text{E}_{\text{std}}\Big(\text{E}\Big\{S_{2}(Y,A_{y},\beta_{2}^{0})[Y-\eta_{1}(A_{y})]\mid A_{y},G=1\Big\}\Big)~~~~(\text{because}~\text{E}[S_{2}()\mid A_{y},G=1]=0)

\displaystyle=\text{E}_{\text{std}}\left(\text{E}\left\{S_{2}(Y,A_{y},\beta_{2}^{0})\frac{G}{\text{P}(G=1)}\frac{\text{P}(A_{y})}{\text{P}(A_{y}\mid G=1)}[Y-\eta_{1}(A_{y})]\mid A_{y}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\text{E}\left(\frac{\text{P}_{\text{std}}(A_{y})}{\text{P}(A_{y})}\text{E}\left\{S_{2}(Y,A_{y},\beta_{2}^{0})\frac{G}{\text{P}(G=1)}\frac{\text{P}(A_{y})}{\text{P}(A_{y}\mid G=1)}[Y-\eta_{1}(A_{y})]\mid A_{y}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\text{E}\bigg\{S_{2}(Y,A_{y},\beta_{2}^{0})\underbrace{\frac{G}{\text{P}(G=1)}\frac{\text{P}_{\text{std}}(A_{y})}{\text{P}(A_{y}\mid G=1)}[Y-\eta_{1}(A_{y})]}_{\textstyle\varphi_{\theta_{1},2}(O)\in\mathcal{H}_{2}}\bigg\}.

Next, we derive
\varphi_{\theta_{1},1}(O)
.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\theta_{1}(\beta)}{\partial\beta_{1}}\Big|_{\beta=\beta^{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta_{1}}\frac{\int\!\!\!\int t(g,a_{y})\eta_{1}(a_{y})f_{1}(g,a_{y},\beta_{1})da_{y}\,d_{g}}{\int\!\!\!\int t(g,a_{y})f_{1}(g,a_{y},\beta_{1})da_{y}\,d_{g}}\Big|_{\beta_{1}=\beta_{1}^{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta_{1}}\int\!\!\!\int t(g,a_{y})\eta_{1}(a_{y})f_{1}(g,a_{y},\beta_{1})da_{y}\,d_{g}\Big|_{\beta_{1}=\beta_{1}^{0}}}{\text{P}(T=1)}-\theta_{1}\frac{\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta_{1}}\int\!\!\!\int t(g,a_{y})f_{1}(g,a_{y},\beta_{1})da_{y}\,d_{g}\Big|_{\beta_{1}=\beta_{1}^{0}}}{\text{P}(T=1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int\!\!\!\int t(g,a_{y})\eta_{1}(a_{y})\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta_{1}}f_{1}(g,a_{y},\beta_{1})\Big|_{\beta_{1}=\beta_{1}^{0}}da_{y}\,d_{g}}{\text{P}(T=1)}-\theta_{1}\frac{\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta_{1}}\int\!\!\!\int t(g,a_{y})f_{1}(g,a_{y},\beta_{1})da_{y}\,d_{g}\Big|_{\beta_{1}=\beta_{1}^{0}}}{\text{P}(T=1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{E}[S_{1}(G,A_{y},\beta_{1}^{0})T\eta_{1}(A_{y})]}{\text{P}(T=1)}-\theta_{1}\frac{\text{E}[S_{1}(G,A_{y},\beta_{1}^{0})T]}{\text{P}(T=1)}

\displaystyle=\text{E}\Big\{S_{1}(G,A_{y},\beta_{1}^{0})\underbrace{\frac{T}{\text{P}(T=1)}[\eta_{1}(A_{y})-\theta_{1}]}_{\textstyle\varphi_{\theta_{1},1}(O)\in\mathcal{H}_{1}}\Big\}.

(Note that
\frac{T}{\text{P}(T=1)}[\eta_{1}(A_{y})-\theta_{1}]
belongs in
\mathcal{H}_{1}
because it is a function of
G,A_{y}
, and its expectation is zero because
\theta_{1}=\text{E}[\eta_{1}(A_{y})\mid T=1]
.)

Leveraging symmetry, we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{\theta_{g}}(O)=\begin{cases}\varphi_{\theta_{g},2}(O)&\text{in case 1}\\
\varphi_{\theta_{g},2}(O)+\varphi_{\theta_{g},1}(O)&\text{in case 2}\end{cases},

where

\displaystyle\varphi_{\theta_{g},2}(O)

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{I}(G=g)}{\text{P}(G=g)}\frac{\text{P}_{\text{std}}(A_{y})}{\text{P}(A_{y}\mid G=g)}[Y-\eta_{g}(A_{y})],

\displaystyle\varphi_{\theta_{g},1}(O)

\displaystyle=\frac{T}{\text{P}(T=1)}[\eta_{g}(A_{y})-\theta_{1}].

A.6.8
Proof for Robustness of WSE estimators

General reasoning

As this section proves robustness properties of all the WSE estimators, we provide a general reasoning used for all estimators. It involves three steps:

1.

Start with the set of estimating equations that the nuisance estimators and the estimator of
\theta_{1}^{*}
based on them (referred to generically as
\hat{\theta}_{1}^{*}
) solve.

For regression models (models of outcome means), the estimating equation is typically vector-valued. We require that all these models are mean-recovering, and will use only the element of the estimating equation that reflects this mean-recovering feature.

2.

Assume regularlity conditions hold such that the nuisance estimators and
\hat{\theta}_{1}^{*}
converge to certain probability limits (indicated with a
†
superscript), and the estimating equations imply a set of equalities involving the probability limits.

For details on the regularity conditions, see
6
, theorem 7.1. We assume these regularity conditions hold throughout, and will keep this implicit below for conciseness.

3.

Consider different cases where certain regression models are correctly specified and/or certain weighting functions are consistently estimated (replacing their probability limits with the corresponding true functionals) and show that in that case the probability limit
\theta_{1}^{*\dagger}
coincide with the true value (
\theta_{1}^{*}
).

When we say a regression model is correctly specified here, we mean it in a local sense, that the model is correctly specified for the conditional expectation of the probability limit of the dependent variable. For example, regarding a step regressing

, ie
\kappa_{1}^{*}()
is consistently estimated.
