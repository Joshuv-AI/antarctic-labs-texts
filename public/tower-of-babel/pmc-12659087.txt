Progressive post-LASIK myopic retinoschisis: a rare complication and its management
Authors: Karishma Tendulkar, Rupal Kathare, Vishma Prabhu, Prathiba Hande, Ramesh Venkatesh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo report a case of rapid progression of myopic macular retinoschisis (MRS) following femtosecond laser-assisted in situ keratomileusis (LASIK) and its anatomical outcome after pars plana vitrectomy (PPV).Case descriptionA 36-year-old woman with high myopia (right eye: 6/15, -14.75D; left eye: 6/12, -13.5D spherical equivalent) underwent a routine refractive surgery evaluation. Fundus examination revealed pathological myopia with posterior staphyloma and peripapillary atrophy in both eyes, while optical coherence tomography (OCT) demonstrated inner and outer MRS with focal vitreomacular traction in the right eye. Following an uneventful bilateral femtosecond LASIK procedure, the patient presented with visual blurring in the right eye at the two-month follow-up. OCT imaging showed significant progression of MRS with increased vitreomacular traction and central retinal thickness, leading to a decline in visual acuity to 6/24. Given the progressive nature of the pathology, PPV was performed. At 6-week and 4-month follow-ups, OCT demonstrated resolution of MRS with restoration of a normal foveal contour and thickness, and final visual acuity improved to 6/12.ConclusionFemtosecond LASIK in highly myopic eyes with pre-existing tractional macular abnormalities may exacerbate MRS progression. Alternative refractive procedures should be considered in such cases. Early vitrectomy intervention may be beneficial for progressive MRS to achieve anatomical and functional improvement.Keywords: Macular retinoschisis, Pathological myopia, Progression, Refractive surgery, Vitrectomy, Resolution

Introduction

Myopic retinoschisis (MRS) is characterized by retinal splitting into inner and/or outer layers, accompanied by hyporeflective cystoid spaces and columnar structures, and is reported in 9%–34% of highly myopic eyes with posterior staphyloma [1]. It is often undetectable during routine clinical examinations and requires advanced imaging techniques like optical coherence tomography (OCT) for diagnosis [2]. With the increasing prevalence of surgical correction for myopia, particularly laser-assisted refractive procedures, there is potential for vitreous changes that may contribute to retinal and macular damage [3]. Therefore, a thorough fundus evaluation—either through mydriatic examination or ultra-widefield imaging in conjunction with OCT—has become a standard practice before refractive surgery. This report describes a rare case of rapidly progressive myopic MRS following femtosecond LASIK, resulting in acute visual deterioration and successfully managed with pars plana vitrectomy. It highlights the potential for femtosecond LASIK to exacerbate pre-existing vitreomacular interface abnormalities and underscores that even subtle vitreomacular traction in highly myopic eyes may render them unsuitable for LASIK due to suction-induced mechanical stress on the macula.

Case description

A 36-year-old woman presented to the refractive surgery department for consultation regarding refractive error correction and was subsequently referred to the retina clinic for a comprehensive dilated fundus examination. Her best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 6/15 in the right eye (spherical equivalent: -14.75 D) and 6/12 in the left eye (spherical equivalent: -13.5 D). Slit-lamp examination revealed normal anterior segment structures, crystalline lenses, and intraocular pressures in both eyes. Detailed anterior segment measurements are summarized in Table 1.

Dilated fundus examination revealed features of pathological myopia, including posterior staphyloma, peripapillary atrophy, and dull foveal reflexes in both eyes, with normal cup-to-disc ratios. No peripheral retinal degenerative lesions necessitating treatment were identified. Spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (OCT) (Spectralis, Heidelberg Engineering, Germany) demonstrated a convex macular contour consistent with posterior staphyloma, along with both inner and outer myopic macular retinoschisis (MRS). The right eye exhibited vitreomacular traction (VMT) with persistent posterior cortical vitreous attachment at the foveal tip and a central macular thickness of 487 μm, whereas the left eye demonstrated incomplete posterior vitreous detachment (PVD) without foveal attachment or traction. Both eyes exhibited features of MRS (Fig. 1A, B).

Given the high myopic refractive error and pre-existing macular pathology, phakic intraocular lens (PIOL) implantation was recommended. However, the patient declined this option due to concerns regarding its invasiveness and instead opted for bilateral femtosecond LASIK after a thorough discussion of the potential risks. Uneventful femtosecond LASIK was performed in both eyes using the ALCON WAVELIGHT® FS200 laser for flap creation and the WAVELIGHT® EX 500 excimer laser for corneal ablation. Laser parameters included a stromal bed cut spot separation of 8 μm, line separation of 8 μm, side cut bed separation of 5 μm, side cut line separation of 3 μm, bed cut pulse energy of 70 µJ, side cut pulse energy of 70 µJ, and a suction pressure of 80 mmHg for 30 s.

At the one-month postoperative follow-up, BCVA remained stable at 6/15 in the right eye and 6/12 in the left. The manifest refraction was + 0.50 D spherical equivalent in both eyes, indicating satisfactory refractive correction. OCT imaging demonstrated minimal progression of MRS in both eyes, with increased VMT and central macular thickening (518 μm) in the right eye (Fig. 2A), whereas the left eye remained unchanged from baseline. The patient reported no subjective visual disturbances, and no immediate intervention was advised. A follow-up was scheduled in three months or earlier if symptoms developed.

One month later, the patient presented with new-onset blurred near vision in the right eye. BCVA had declined to 6/24 in the right eye, while the left eye remained stable at 6/12. OCT revealed further progression of MRS and increased central macular thickness (715 μm) in the right eye (Fig. 2B). Fundus fluorescein angiography ruled out any retinal vascular leakage. Given the diagnosis of progressive MRS with VMT as the underlying cause of visual deterioration, a 25-gauge pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) was performed the following day. The procedure included complete PVD, Brilliant Blue G-assisted internal limiting membrane peeling over the macula, and intraocular gas endotamponade with 20% sulfur hexafluoride.

At follow-up visits at six weeks and four months postoperatively, the patient reported significant improvement in visual symptoms, with BCVA improving to 6/12 in the right eye. OCT imaging confirmed a substantial reduction in MRS and central macular thickness (351 μm at 6-weeks and 251 μm at 4-months follow-up visits, respectively) and restoration of a normal foveal contour (Fig. 2C, D). The left eye showed no progression of MRS throughout the follow-up period.

The patient provided informed consent for the publication of clinical data and images.

Discussion

In highly myopic eyes (spherical equivalent > − 10.0 D), LASIK is generally contraindicated owing to its limited corrective range and the increased risk of tractional maculopathies [4]. In this case, preoperative OCT demonstrated myopic macular traction with hyperreflective stress lines at the fovea, indicating pre-existing vitreomacular stress. The transient intraocular pressure elevation, acoustic shock waves, and rapid decompression associated with femtosecond LASIK likely exacerbated this traction, resulting in rapid progression of MRS. Femtosecond LASIK has been shown to induce or accelerate PVD more frequently than microkeratome LASIK, attributed to higher suction pressure and longer suction duration [5]. PVD typically occurs within days to weeks after surgery, but may manifest later depending on age and axial length. The delayed worsening in our case likely reflected progressive vitreous liquefaction and persistent focal vitreofoveal adhesion. Similar delayed PVD-related tractional changes have been reported by Osman et al. and Mirshahi et al. [3, 5] To our knowledge, this represents the first reported case of MRS progression following femtosecond LASIK.

Phakic intraocular lens (PIOL) implantation is a non-laser-based surgical alternative for myopia correction, particularly in eyes with high refractive errors, poor excimer laser suitability, deep anterior chambers, healthy corneal endothelium, and appropriate mesopic pupil size [6]. Unlike LASIK, PVD occurrence following PIOL implantation is rare, suggesting a theoretically lower risk of retinal tears, detachment, or MRS progression in these eyes [7]. In this case, PIOL implantation may have been the preferred refractive procedure to mitigate the risk of MRS progression.

Management of MRS involves achieving complete PVD and internal limiting membrane disruption, which may occur naturally over time or be induced through intravitreal injections or PPV. Observation for spontaneous PVD and MRS resolution was initially pursued; however, progressive traction warranted surgical intervention. Current literature advocates a foveal-sparing internal limiting membrane (ILM) peeling technique in MMT to minimize the risk of iatrogenic full-thickness macular hole formation. Studies by Shiraki et al. and Azuma et al. have shown that preserving the ILM over the foveal center maintains structural integrity while still allowing resolution of schitic cavities [8, 9]. Although standard complete ILM peeling was performed in this case without macular hole formation, a foveal-sparing approach may be considered a safer surgical strategy in similar scenarios.

In conclusion, rapid MRS progression following femtosecond LASIK is a rare but potential complication. In highly myopic eyes with pre-existing traction-related macular pathologies, PIOL implantation should be considered as a safer alternative for refractive correction. Furthermore, early vitrectomy may be warranted in cases where MRS progression is evident post-LASIK.
