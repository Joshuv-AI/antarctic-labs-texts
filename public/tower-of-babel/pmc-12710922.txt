HLA class-I genotyping to personalize Bacille Calmette–Guerin immunotherapy in bladder cancer
Authors: Inmaculada Ruiz-Lorente, Lourdes Gimeno, Alicia López-Abad, Pedro López Cubillana, Tomás Fernández Aparicio, Lucas Jesús Asensio Egea, Juan Moreno Avilés, Gloria Doñate Iñiguez, Pablo Luis Guzmán Martínez-Valls, Gerardo Server, Belén Ferri, José Antonio Campillo, Francisco Galindo, María Victoria Martínez-Sánchez, Alfredo Minguela
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacille Calmette-Guerin (BCG) is the immunotherapy of choice for high-risk non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (BC), although recurrence eventually occurs in 50% of patients and 20% progress to advanced stages. This study evaluates the predictive value of human leukocyte antigen class-I (HLA-I) genotyping to guide BCG immunotherapy. HLA-I genotyping and expression of NK cell receptors in circulating T and NK lymphocytes was evaluated at diagnosis in 325 consecutive BC patients (151 treated with BCG and 174 with other therapies), 648 patients with other cancers and 23,250 healthy controls. Proliferation, cytotoxicity, and production of cytokines and intracellular nitric oxide (icNO) was assessed in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from these donors, selected based on their Bw4 genotype, after stimulation in vitro with anti-CD3/CD28 or BCG. HLA-A*11, HLA-B*07 and HLA-B*18 allotypes were associated with favorable outcomes after BCG therapy (longer progression-free and overall survival), whereas HLA-B*44 and other KIR3DL1 Bw4 ligands were associated with unfavorable outcomes. Although Bw4 ligands were associated with better NK cell education in vivo (CD226-upregulation, KIR3DL1 downregulation and stronger NK cytotoxic capacity), they were also associated with weaker NK cell proliferation, and lower IL-1β, IL-6, and icNO production after BCG stimulation in vitro, revealing an inhibitory role of KIR3DL1. Mechanisms by which KIR3DL1/Bw4 interaction interferes with BCG-induced NK cell proliferation and the production of cytokines and icNO, warrant further investigation. HLA-I genotyping should be investigated as a useful biomarker to personalize BCG immunotherapy in BC.

Introduction

The term bladder cancer (BC) encompasses a spectrum of diseases, 70-75% considered non-muscle-invasive BC (NMIBC) and 25-30% muscle-invasive BC (MIBC).1,2 Bacille Calmette-Guerin (BCG) immunotherapy remains the gold standard to treat carcinoma in situ (CIS) and high-risk NMIBC, while blocking PD1 and CTLA4 is emerging as an effective immunotherapy in the treatment of MIBC.3 Unfortunately, the success of these immunotherapies is quite variable. Human leukocyte antigen class-I (HLA-I), including HLA-A, HLA-B and HLA-C, play a crucial dual role in immune responses, presenting tumor peptides to T lymphocytes4 while also acting as ligands for the killer-cell immunoglobulin-like receptors (KIRs) on natural killer (NK) and T cells.5 Since immunotherapy helps immune cells find and attack cancer cells, genetic variability in HLA and KIRs may influence treatment efficacy. For instance, there is less risk of intravesical recurrence after BCG therapy in patients with HLA-B*07 or HLA-B*44 allotypes,6 yet they are associated with a worse outcome following immunotherapy to prevent acute myeloid leukemia recurrence.7 Similarly, HLA-A*03 is associated with worse responses of BC and other tumors to immunotherapy.8

Although BCG stimulates immunosurveillance strongly, only 50-70% of BC patients respond. Intravesical BCG induces granulocyte, macrophage, dendritic cell (DC), and T and NK lymphocyte infiltration into the tumor, cells that secrete IL-1, IL-2, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12, IFN-γ, TNF-α, GM-CSF and sICAM-1, and stimulate: 1) cancer cell death via cytotoxic T and NK cells;4,9 2) inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) activity, producing the nitric oxide (NO) that exerts cytotoxic effects on tumor cells;10 and 3) DCs to present tumor antigens to T lymphocytes.

NK cells appear to play important roles in BCG immunotherapy.11,12 Driven by their ligands on cancer cells, the balance of signals emitted through activating and inhibitory receptors regulates NK cell activity.13 Depending on their KIR genotype, each individual may express different combination of inhibitory (iKIR, KIR2DL1-4, KIR2DL5a/b and KIR3DL1-3) and activating (aKIR, KIR2DS1-5 and KIR3DS1) receptors. Licensing interactions of iKIRs with their cognate HLA-I ligands promote the education of NK cells, and makes them fully competent,13‐15 the KIR2DL1/HLA-C2 (Lys80), KIR2DL2-3/HLA-C1 (Asn80) and KIR3DL1/Bw4 interactions the best characterized.14‐16

The data presented here reveal that certain HLA-I alleles and KIR-ligands are associated with significant variation in the outcome of BCG immunotherapy in BC patients. The immunological mechanisms involved in this variability were studied, findings that may aid the personalization of this immunotherapy.

Patients and methods

Samples and study groups

EDTA-anticoagulated peripheral blood samples from 325 newly diagnosed BC patients, collected prior to any treatment, were used for HLA genotyping as well as for NK and T cell receptor analysis by flow cytometry. BC population consisted of two distinct cohorts, each recruited during separate time frames in different research projects (https://orcid.org/0000-0003-2472-5893): Series-1 2014-2016 (n = 133) and Series-2 2019-2022 (n = 192). Histological classification of BC was performed following the WHO Classification of Tumors of the Urinary System and Male Genital Organs:2 1) non-invasive urothelial neoplasms (NIUN), including CIS and high-grade Ta papillary carcinomas (n = 57); and 2) infiltrating urothelial carcinoma (IUC), including high-grade NMIBC stage T1 (n = 148), and MIBC stages T2 (n = 99) and T3/T4 (n = 21). Patients with low grade NMIBC were excluded. Progression of NIUN was defined as local recurrence at a higher grade or stage, and in IUC, local recurrence at a more advanced stage for IUC and/or development of metastatic disease. Treatments were based on the patients’ condition and tumor histology. For statistical studies, treatments were grouped as BCG or NoBCG (with other treatments). Of the total 205 candidates for BCG therapy (high grade CIS, Ta, and T1), 54 (26.3%) were unable to complete treatment due to side effects, complications, or a shortage of BCG.

To confirm the results were specific to BC, patients with other cancers (OCs): melanoma (n = 308), myeloma (n = 150), pediatric acute leukemia (n = 102) and ovarian cancer (n = 88), as well as 23,791 healthy Caucasian controls (HCs) were also studied.

Sodium-heparin peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from 18 HCs (12 with and 6 without Bw4 ligands) were used to perform proliferation and cytotoxicity assays, and to assess cytokine or intracellular nitric oxide (icNO) production.

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee (IRB-00005712) and written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration.

HLA class-I genotyping

HLA-A, -B, and -C genotyping was performed on DNA using the QIAmp DNA Blood Mini kit (QIAgen, GmbH, Hilden, Germany), as previously described.17,18 HLA-A and HLA-B genotyping allowed us to identify alleles bearing the Bw4 motif according to the amino-acid sequences at positions 77–83 in the alpha1 domain of the HLA class-I heavy chain. Bw4 alleles with threonine at amino acid 80 (Thr80) and higher affinity for KIR3DL1 (HLA-B*05, B*13, B*44) were discerned from those with isoleucine 80 (Ile80) and lower affinity (HLA-A*23, A*24, A*25, A*32 and HLA-B*17, B*27, B*37, B*38, B*47, B*49, B*51, B*52, B*53, B*57, B*58, B*59, B*63 and B*77). HLA-C genotyping allowed the distinction between HLA-C alleles with asparagine 80 (C1-epitope: HLA-C*01, *03, *07, *08, *12, *14, *16:01) and those with lysine 80 (C2-epitope: HLA- C*02, *04, *05, *06, *15, *16:02, *17, *18). Nonetheless, the KIR ligand calculator at https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ipd/imgt/hla/matching/ was used to ascertain Bw4, C1 and C2 epitopes.

Expression of NK cell receptors in peripheral blood lymphocytes

The expression of CD16, CD226 (DNAM1), NKG2A, TIGIT and KIR receptors (KIR2DL1, 2DS1, 2DL2/S2, 2DL3 and 3DL1) on CD56bright and CD56dim NK cells as well as on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells was analyzed simultaneously using an LSR-II or Lyric flow cytometer (BD), following the method previously described.19,20 Briefly, 100 µl of peripheral blood samples were incubated with monoclonal antibodies CD158a-FITC (143211, R&D Systems Inc, which recognized KIR2DL1), CD158a/h-PC7 (EB6B, Beckman Colter, which recognized both KIR2DL1 and 2DS1), CD158b2 (180701, R&D Systems Inc, KIR2DL3), CD226-PE (11A8, Biolegend), CD158e1 (DX9, R&D systems, KIR3DL1), CD16-AlexaFluor700 (3G8, BD), CD8-APC-Cy7 (SK1, BD), TIGIT-BV421 (741182, BD), CD3-BV510 (UCHT1, BD), CD4-BV605 (RPA-T4, BD), CD56 BV711 (NCAM16.2, BD) and CD159a-BV786 (131411, BD) for 10 minutes at room temperature in the dark. Subsequently red cells were lysed, washed and acquired. Hierarchical and logical gating strategy was carried out in order to identify the corresponding cell subsets using DiVA™ Software v9.0 (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA).

In vitro functional assays

To evaluate the effect that Bw4 interaction with KIR3DL1 had on T and NK cell functionality (Figure 1a–c), PBMCs isolated in ficoll density gradients from sodium heparin anticoagulated samples were stained with 0.05 µM carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and simulated in vitro with ImmunoCult™ Human CD3/CD28 T cell activator (StemCell Technologies, Vancouver, Canada) following the manufacturer's instructions or with BCG (Danish 1331, AJVaccines, Copenhagen) at a proportion of 1:1 colony forming units (CFU) to PBMC. CFSE-labeled cells (1x10ˆ6/well) were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator, in 24-well flat-bottomed plates in quintuplicate. After 72 hours 1 well per sample was collected and the supernatant was stored at −80 °C and used for cytokine analysis in Luminex. After 120 hours, 1 well per sample was collected to assessed icNO production. After 144 hours, the harvested cells were either used for cytotoxic assays or stained to analyze proliferation in a Norther-light (NL) flow cytometer (Cytek, Amsterdam, Netherlands).

Functional assays of NK and T cells. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were left unstimulated or stimulated in vitro with anti-CD3/CD28 or BCG during: (a) 120 hours to measure the production of intracellular nitric oxide (icNO) by CD3+ CD4+ (dark green) and CD3+CD8+ (pale green) T lymphocytes, as well as by CD56dim (blue) and CD56high (red) NK cells; (b) for 144 hours to evaluate the proliferation (CFSE low cells) of T and NK cell subsets; and (c) for 144 hours to evaluate the cytotoxicity against K562, T24 and J82 cell lines at different effector (green) to target (pale blue live and dark blue dead) ratios.

Intracellular nitric oxide (icNO) production was assessed as previously described.21 Briefly, harvested cells were labeled with monoclonal antibodies CD3-BV786 (SK7, Becton-Dickinson, BD), CD4-APC (SK3, BD), CD8-BV605 (SK1, BD), CD45-APC-Cy7 (2D1, BD), CD16-V450 (3G8, BD), and CD56-PE-Cy7 (NCAM16.2, BD) for 10 minutes at room temperature. The labeled cells were then transferred to flow cytometry tubes containing 1.5 mL of pre-warmed RPMI medium with 10 μM of a pyrylium probe (mtNOpy) and immediately acquired for 30 minutes at a low flow rate (time recorded). During acquisition, the tubes were incubated in a 37 °C water bath protected from light. The icNO levels were assessed as the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of mtNOpy in the PE channel (586/42 nm) excited by the blue laser (488 nm) using a FACSLyric cytometer and DIVA 9.0 software (BD). Photomultiplier voltages were adjusted beforehand using CS&T beads (BD). The gating strategy used to differentiate NK cells, CD4+ T cells, and CD8+ T cells is illustrated in Figure 1a.

Cell proliferation was evaluated in CFSE labeled cells after 6 days of in vitro expansion by labeling with TIGIT-BV421 (RUO, BD), CD16-V450 (3G8, BD), CD4-cFV505 (DK3, Palex), CD226-BV605 (11A8, Biolegend), CD8-BV570 (RPA-T8, Biolegend), TIM-3-BV711 (7D3, BD), TCRgd-BV750 (11F2, BD), NKG2A-BV786 (131411, BD), HLA-DR-cFB548 (L2D3, Cytek), NKG2C-PE (REA205, Miltenyi Biotec), CD25-cFBYG610 (BC96, Cytek), CD158bj-PE-Cy5 (GL183, Beckman Culter), KIR3DL1-APC (DX9, R&D Systems Inc), CD57-cFR668 (HNK1, Cytek), CD38-cFR685 (HIT2, Cytek), CD3-AF700 (UCHT1, BD), NKG2D-APC-H7 (1D11, Biolegend) and CD45-cFR840 (HI30, Cytek) monoclonal antibodies during 15 min at room temperature. Cells were washed with FACsFlow (BD) and acquired in an NL-flow (Cytek) cytometer and analyzed in Diva software (BD) as shown in Figure 1a,b and Figure S1. Proliferation was estimated as the percentage of CFSE-low cells within each cell subset (CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, CD56dim and CD56bright NK cells).

The cytotoxic activity of effector cells harvested after 6 days of culture (unstimulated, or stimulated with anti-CD3/CD28 or BCG) was evaluated against target cell lines labeled with CellTraceTMViolet (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) at effector/target ratio 5:1 and 15:1 in triplicate. In parallel, target cells were incubated alone to measure basal cell death. Cells were incubated in V-bottom 96-well microplates in a total volume of 150 μL of complete medium for 4 h in a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C. Cell mixtures were then washed in PBS-1% BSA and incubated in the same buffer containing 20 µg/mL 7-amino actinomycin D (7-AAD, Sigma, France) and 0.5 µg/mL DRAQ5 (BD, Canada) for 10 min at 4 °C in the dark. Cells were then washed and acquired right afterwards on a FACSLyric flow cytometer. Hierarchical and logical gating strategy was carried out in order to identify proliferation of the different subsets using and DIVA Software (BD, San Jose, CA, U.S.A) as described in Figure 1c. Mean value of triplicates was used to calculate the percentage of lysis as follows: experimental—spontaneous apoptotic target cells.

Cytokine production in culture supernatant was analyzed using a Procarta Plex Human Immune Monitoring 12 Plex Panel (IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-17A, IL-22, IL-23, IFN-γ, TNF-α, and TGF-β1, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Vienna, Austria), following the manufacturer's instructions, in a Luminex 300 (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, U.S.A), as previously described.20

Statistical analysis

The SPSS package (SPSS 21.0, Chicago, IL, U.S.A) was used to analyze categorical (Chi-squared) and continuous variables (analysis of variance with post hoc tests). Kaplan-Meier and Log-rank tests were used to analyze progression-free (PFS) and overall survival (OS). The time to events (progression or death) was estimated in months from the diagnosis. The outcomes in each group were estimated as the median for PFS or OS ± typical error (TE). Cox regression was used to study the effect of multiple parameters on patient outcome, estimating the Hazard ratio and 95% confidence interval (forward conditional methods were employed to validate the results). P < 0.05 was considered significant, with Bonferroni correction (Pc) and/or false discovery rate (FDR) applied as necessary. HLA haplotypes and linkage disequilibrium (LD) were estimated with Arlequin v. 3.5 (https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1755-0998.2010.02847.x).

Results

Clinical, biological, therapeutic and evolutionary characteristics of the patients

The characteristics of the patients in each study group are summarized in Figure 2. Median OS was not reached by patients with melanoma, pediatric leukemia and myeloma, and it was 103.0 ± 8.3 and 81.0 ± 5.9 months for BC and ovarian cancer patients, respectively (p < 0.001). The median OS for CIS/Ta (n = 57), T1 (n = 148), T2 (99) and T3/T4 (n = 21) BC patients were not-reached, 106.0 ± 9.9, 47.0 ± 5.5 and 26.0 ± 9.1 months, respectively (p < 0.001). BCG therapy was administered to 32 CIS/Ta (56.1%), 117 T1 (78.5%) and 2 T2 cases (2.0%), with a median OS not-reached for BCG and of 56.0 ± 5.6 months for NoBCG groups (p < 0.001).

Association of HLA-I with BC recurrence and patient outcomes

BC recurrence did not appear to be associated with HLA-I allotype, HLA-homozygosity or KIR-ligand genotype, irrespective of whether they were treated with BCG or not (Table 2). However, BC-BCG patients with HLA-A*11 (13.6%) or HLA-B*07 (17.2%) exhibited non-significant trends toward reduced progression rates, while HLA-B*44 carriers showed a comparatively higher progression rate (38.6%) versus other HLA-I allotypes (27.8%). Relative to other HLA-I allotypes (41.4%), the progression rate of BC-NoBCG patients was lower when they had HLA-C*03 (18.5%; p = 0.006), and higher when they were heterozygous (55.4%; p = 0.008) or homozygous for HLA-B*44 (57.1%; p = 0.036).

A lower death rate among the BC-BCG patients was associated with HLA-B*07 (6.9%; p = 0.021), or HLA-C*07 (13.0%; p = 0.009) relative to the other HLA-I allotypes (21.9%), yet it was higher in those heterozygous (34.1%; p = 0.029) or homozygous (50.0%; p = 0.039) for HLA-B*44, or with Bw4 ligands (26.4%; p = 0.003). Given the strong LD between HLA-B and HLA-C (Table S1 and Figure S2), a pairwise analysis was performed to discriminate the allotypes actually associated with BC outcome (Figure 3a). LD was observed for HLA-A*11 and HLA-C*03 (p < 0.001), HLA-B*07 and HLA-C*07 (p < 0.001), and HLA-B*44 and HLA-C*16 (p < 0.001). In BC-BCG patients, only HLA-A*11 and HLA-B*07 but not HLA-C*03 or HLA-C*07 were associated with a favorable OS, while HLA-B*44 but not HLA-C*16 was associated with an unfavorable OS (Figure 3b), regardless of their associated haplotypes (Figure S3).

Interestingly, while the OS in BC patients with HLA-A*11 was initially 100%, it dropped sharply 5 years after starting BCG therapy with an unfavorable evolution thereafter, as in the BC-NoBCG patients. HLA-B*07 was associated with favorable PFS and OS in BC-BCG patients. HLA-B*18 was associated with a favorable PFS and OS in BC-NoBCG patients but not so clearly in the remaining cancer patients. HLA-B*44 was associated with an unfavorable PFS in BC patients irrespective of whether they received BCG therapy or not, yet not in OCs. Nonetheless, HLA-B*44 was only associated with an unfavorable OS in BC-BCG patients (Figure 4). The predictive value of these allotypes to BCG therapy was evident in the two independent series of our study, except for HLA-B*18 which was only observed in the first series (Figure S4).

HLA-B*44 and Bw4 are independently associated with unfavorable outcomes of BCG therapy

We investigated the role of HLA-B*44 as Bw4-ligand of KIR3DL1. Whilst C1/C2 ligands for KIR2DL1-3 were not associated with the OS of BC-BCG patients, Bw4 was clearly associated with a shorter median-OS (120.0 ± 10.6 months, p < 0.009) than in patients without Bw4 (not reached). Patients with HLA-B*44 had an even shorter median-OS (106.0 ± 32.7 months) than patients with other Bw4 allotypes (120.0 ± 10.1 months, p < 0.002: Figure 5a). Thus, Bw4 (OR = 10.04, p = 0.031) and HLA-B*44 (OR = 2.485, p = 0.023) were independently associated with the OS in BC-BCG patients, in addition to age (OR = 1.084, p = 0.001) and tumor stage (OR = 4.810, p = 0.007) (Figure 5b). Cox forward conditional method confirmed the independent predictive value of Bw4 (Figure 5c). To further assess the influence of HLA-B*44 and Bw4 allotypes on bladder cancer, PFS was analyzed (Figure 5e). In this analysis, only Bw4-positive patients carrying HLA-B*44 exhibited a shorter median-PFS (86.0 ± 1.04 months vs. not reached, p < 0.016) compared with patients lacking Bw4 or those with Bw4 not associated with HLA-B*44.

The repertoire of CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes or CD56dim, CD56bright and KIR3DL1+ NK cells in peripheral blood did not differ significantly in patients with HLA-B*44 or other Bw4 allotypes. However, KIR3DL1+ NK cells expressed the co-activating CD226 receptor more strongly (6.64 ± 0.27% and 6.05 ± 0.59 vs. 5.23 ± 0.39 mean fluorescence intensity -MFI- x10,3 p < 0.02) and the KIR3DL1 (10.15 ± 0.4% and 9.88 ± 0.5 vs. 11.55 ± 0.7 MFIx10,3 p < 0.05) more weakly in patients with HLA-B*44 or other Bw4 allotypes relative to those who were Bw4- (Figure 5d). Hence, Bw4 and HLA-B*44 appear to contribute to KIR3DL1+ NK cell education, as indicated previously.22

Bw4 suppresses in vitro proliferation, as well as cytokine and icNO production by NK cells after BCG stimulation

After anti-CD3/CD28 stimulation, the proliferation of NK and T cells was not influenced by the Bw4 genotype, nor was the production of cytokines and icNO. However, after BCG stimulation NK cell proliferation was lower in donors with 1 or 2 copies of Bw4 relative to Bw4- donors (41.6% and 29.6% vs. 43.1%; p > 0.05), and they produced less IL-1β (5.2 ± 0.9 and 1.7 ± 0.5 vs. 7.3 ± 0.3 ng/ml; p < 0.014), IL-6 (11.7 ± 2.2 and 8.29 ± 2.5 vs. 16.0 ± 0.6 ng/ml; p < 0.016), TNFα (4.5 ± 0.5 and 1.98 ± 0.42 vs. 5.6 ± 0.9 ng/ml; p > 0.05), TGFβ1 (5.1 ± 0.8 and 3.26 ± 0.95 vs. 6.2 ± 1.7 ng/ml; p > 0.05) and icNO (202 ± 41 and 179 ± 39 vs. 314 ± 120 MFI; p > 0.05: Figure 6a,b,c). In a representative patient stimulated with BCG (Figure 6d) KIR3DL1+ NK cells proliferated better in the absence rather than the presence of Bw4 ligands (78% vs. 14%), this was clearer in patients with more than one Bw4 allele. Moreover, 2 copies of Bw4 were associated with stronger cytotoxic responses against K562 and T24 cell lines of unstimulated and stimulated NK cells, although these data did not reach statistical significance (Figure 6e).

Discussion

BCG is the immunotherapy of choice in high-risk NMIBC, although up to 50% of patients will recur and 20% progress to MIBC.23 HLA polymorphisms could influence the variability observed in these highly immunogenic tumors, yet few studies have explored the predictive value of HLA in the response to BCG immunotherapy.6 The data here clearly show that HLA-A*11, HLA-B*07, and HLA-B*18 are allotypes associated with favorable responses, while worse responses are associated with HLA-B*44 and Bw4 ligands.

Favorable BCG responses should be understood based on specific T cell responses elicited by tumor peptide presentation, with BCG stimulating HLA expression and antigen presentation.24 Previous studies assigned a protective role to the A*11:01 and B*18:01 allotypes against prostate cancer,25 and although here HLA-A*11 is associated with a favorable OS in the first 5 years post-BCG, there is a sharp drop-off thereafter. Suggesting that if BCG treatment had been repeated after the third year, it might have extended the immunosurveillance induced in these patients. Moreover, it would be interesting to determine if HLA-A*11 patients with MIBC, whose prognosis is unfavorable, might also benefit from BCG therapy before radical cystectomy. Indeed, HLA‐A*11 was independently associated with rapid disease recurrence in patients with high‐risk prostate cancer. The identification of an HLA-A*11:01 restricted neoepitope of the mutated PIK3CA that can elicit tumor-specific T cells26 would further support the extension of BCG treatment for longer periods (or even to MIBC patients), particularly if we consider the extremely high mutation rate of this gene in BC.27

The HLA-B*07 and HLA-B*44 allotypes have also been associated with a lower risk of intravesical recurrence after BCG therapy.6 However, while HLA-B*07 was associated with a longer PFS and OS in our study, HLA-B*44 was associated with a shorter PFS and/or OS in BC, principally in conjunction with BCG therapy. Results that are consistent with reports describing HLA-B*44 as being associated with a worse outcome in acute myeloid leukemia after immunotherapy aimed at preventing recurrence.7 Unfavorable responses associated with HLA-B*44 should be understood in light of its role as a ligand for KIR3DL1. HLA-B*44 interacts with KIR3DL1 with lower affinity than other Bw4 allotypes and it provokes weaker education of KIR3DL1+ NK cells.6,28 Our data in unstimulated PBMCs suggest that both Bw4 and HLA-B*44 do educate KIR3DL1+ NK cells in vivo, inducing stronger NK cell cytotoxicity and higher expression of CD226. Nonetheless, BCG stimulation in patients with Bw4, especially in patients with more than one copy of the Bw4 epitope, is also associated with less in vitro proliferation, and weaker production of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β and IL-6) and icNO by NK cells. These effects reveal a clear inhibitory role of the KIR3DL1/Bw4 interaction in this reactive microenvironment.

Our data suggest that of the two effector functions of NK cells, secretion of factors and cytotoxicity, BCG more strongly stimulates the former over the latter. In fact, NK cells proliferate intensely after BCG stimulation, upregulating CD56 and downregulating CD16 to acquire a CD56high phenotype that mimics that of CD56bright NK cells, cells that specialize in producing soluble factors.9,20 However, the KIR3DL1/Bw4 interaction appears to interfere with NK cell proliferation and differentiation into CD56high, and hence, their effector functions mediated by cytokines and NO. In the absence of Bw4, strong IL-6 production occurs intermittently after each BCG instillation, promoting the anti-tumoral as opposed to the tumorigenic influence of this cytokine.29 IL-6 could dampen the cancer cell proliferation and stimulate the infiltration of cytotoxic effectors into the tumor,29 while also promoting anti-inflammatory cytokine production (e.g. the IL-1 receptor agonist (IL-1ra) and IL-10).30,31 This IL-1ra might counteract the pro-tumoral influence of IL-1β, which also accumulates following BCG stimulation. However, IL-1β can also stimulate IFNγ production by CD56bright NK cells,32 contributing to the Th1, M1 and N1 polarization of cellular immune effectors. Furthermore, strong iNOS-derived NO production by M1 macrophages and Th1 lymphocytes may potentiate immune effector cytotoxicity against tumor cells.33 In addition, the capacity of NK cells to kill tumor cells and eradicate pathogens is in part regulated by their own ability to synthesize NO.34 Therefore, the anti-tumor mechanisms induced by BCG appear to be dampened in patients with HLA-B*44 and other Bw4 allotypes, which could be associated with a worse clinical outcome. For this reason, the use of BCG as the best treatment in patients with non-invasive tumors carrying these genotypes should be questioned.

In summary, the data presented here suggest that HLA-B*44 and Bw4 are unfavorable biomarkers that could counteract the benefits of BCG therapy. Hence, the molecular mechanisms by which the KIR3DL1/Bw4 interaction suppresses BCG-induced NK cell proliferation and production of soluble factors, as well as its unfavorable predictive value, merit further study to identify actionable molecular targets that would improve outcomes in these patients. Moreover, the benefits of extending BCG treatment or applying it to MIBC in patients with favorable HLA biomarkers should be confirmed in well-designed multicenter clinical trials. Predictive biomarkers for BCG immunotherapy could improve the outcome of BC patients, decreasing the burden of unnecessary treatments.
