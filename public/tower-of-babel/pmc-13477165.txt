Development and Validation of a Prediction Model for Early ARDS in Children with Sepsis in the PICU: A Multicenter Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Dilare Aihaiti, Abulaiti Abuduhaer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify early factors associated with pediatric acute respiratory distress syndrome (PARDS) within 48 hours after admission in children with sepsis and to develop and validate a combined prediction model.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 547 children with sepsis admitted to the pediatric intensive care unit between June 2023 and December 2025 as the derivation cohort. Sepsis and PARDS were diagnosed according to the Phoenix and PALICC-2 criteria, respectively. Clinical data obtained within 2 hours of admission were analyzed. Correlation analysis, variance inflation factor assessment, and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression were followed by multivariable logistic regression to construct the final model. Performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), Brier score, calibration assessment, and 1000 bootstrap resamples. An independent external cohort of 100 children, was used only to assess discrimination.ResultsAmong 547 children, 146 (26.7%) developed PARDS within 48 hours. Lower hemoglobin (OR = 0.987, 95% CI: 0.976–0.997), higher LAR (OR = 1.548, 95% CI: 1.042–2.299), septic shock (OR = 4.158, 95% CI: 2.001–8.639), and a higher Phoenix Sepsis Score (OR = 3.438, 95% CI: 2.603–4.540) were independently associated with early PARDS. The model achieved an AUC of 0.911 (95% CI: 0.882–0.939) in the derivation cohort. The apparent and optimism-corrected C-indices were 0.911 and 0.908, respectively, and the corresponding Brier scores were 0.0968 and 0.0995. In the external cohort, the AUC was 0.873 (95% CI: 0.775–0.970).ConclusionElevated LAR, higher Phoenix Sepsis Score, septic shock, and lower HB levels were important risk-related factors for the development of PARDS in pediatric sepsis. Enhanced dynamic monitoring and early interventions should be implemented for high-risk patients to improve clinical outcomes.Keywords: sepsis, acute respiratory distress syndrome, children, risk factors, prediction model

Introduction

Sepsis, defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, remains a major challenge in pediatric critical care worldwide.1,2 According to the Global Burden of Disease study, approximately 25 million children are affected by sepsis annually, with over 3.5 million fatalities, highlighting its substantial global burden in terms of both morbidity and mortality.3,4 Pediatric sepsis is characterized by rapid onset and progression and is frequently complicated by severe organ dysfunction, including acute kidney injury (AKI) and pediatric acute respiratory distress syndrome (PARDS), which may contribute to poor clinical outcomes and mortality.5,6 Among these complications, PARDS serves as a pivotal driver of clinical deterioration in septic children.7 Previous studies have reported that the incidence of PARDS ranges from 26.7% to 57%, with mortality rates oscillating between 20% to 50%.8–10 Therefore, the early identification of septic children at increased risk of early-onset PARDS is important for optimizing clinical monitoring and timely intervention.

ARDS is a heterogeneous syndrome characterized by acute hypoxemic respiratory failure, inflammatory pulmonary injury, and impaired gas exchange.11,12 In clinical practice, conventional imaging findings and oxygenation indices may not fully support early risk stratification, particularly before overt respiratory deterioration occurs.13 Therefore, readily available clinical and laboratory indicators that reflect early pathophysiological changes may be useful for identifying high-risk patients. The lactate-to-albumin ratio (LAR) has recently attracted attention as a composite biomarker that combines lactate, a marker of tissue hypoperfusion and metabolic stress, with albumin, which is associated with systemic inflammation, capillary permeability, and nutritional depletion.14,15 Emerging evidence suggests that elevated LAR is associated with poor prognosis in both adult and pediatric patients with sepsis and may reflect microcirculatory dysfunction and increased disease severity.16

Therefore, this retrospective study investigated pediatric patients with sepsis admitted to our Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) to identify factors associated with the development of PARDS within the initial 48 hours of admission. By evaluating routinely available clinical and biochemical parameters, including LAR, we aimed to determine factors independently associated with early PARDS and provide evidence to support early risk stratification and clinical management in children with sepsis.

Materials and Methods

Study Population: Enrollment and Follow-Up

We conducted a multi-center, retrospective, observational cohort study. Data from 547 pediatric patients with sepsis were extracted from the Jiahe electronic medical record system of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University for model development. Subsequently, the model was externally validated using an independent cohort of 100 pediatric patients with sepsis obtained from the electronic medical record system of Kashgar First People’s Hospital. Inclusion criteria were: (i) age 1–18 years; (ii) fulfillment of the pediatric sepsis diagnostic criteria based on the Phoenix Sepsis Score,2 defined as a suspected or confirmed infection with an increase of ≥2 points in the Phoenix Sepsis Score; (iii) availability of complete clinical data within the first 2 h after admission to the PICU. Exclusion criteria were: (i) PARDS already present at admission; (ii) concomitant malignant disease or recent receipt of antitumor therapy, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or immunotherapy; (iii) hospital stay < 48h without development of PARDS, precluding ascertainment of the primary outcome; and (iv) treatment withdrawal due to treatment abandonment, discharge against medical advice, or other reasons, resulting in inability to determine the outcome.

All eligible patients were enrolled at the time of sepsis diagnosis and PICU admission. The index time was defined as the time of sepsis diagnosis and PICU admission. Follow-up continued for 48h after admission, during which the occurrence of the study outcome was recorded. No deaths occurred during the 48-hour follow-up period in either the derivation cohort or the external validation cohort. The primary outcome was new-onset PARDS within 48h after admission, diagnosed according to the Second International Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Management of Pediatric Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (PALICC-2).17 Patients who developed PARDS within 48h after admission were classified into the PARDS group, whereas those who did not develop PARDS within this time window were classified into the No-early PARDS group (Figure 1).

Patient enrollment and grouping.

External validation cohort: To evaluate the generalizability of the prediction model, we collected an independent external validation cohort from the Kashi First People’s Hospital between June 2023 and December 2025. The same inclusion criteria, exclusion criteria, diagnostic definitions, and data collection protocols were applied. A total of 100 children with sepsis were included, of whom 18 developed early PARDS. The external validation cohort was used solely to test the predictive performance of the final model derived from the derivation cohort.

Definition

In the present study, pediatric sepsis was defined using the Phoenix Sepsis Criteria (2024),2 identified by suspected or confirmed infection with a Phoenix Sepsis Score≥2 points, indicating life-threatening organ dysfunction in pediatric patients. Septic shock was defined as pediatric sepsis with at least 1 cardiovascular point in the Phoenix Sepsis Score. ARDS was diagnosed according to the Second International Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Management of Pediatric Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (PALICC-2).17 New-onset PARDS occurring within 48h after admission was defined as early PARDS. According to whether PARDS developed within 48h after admission, patients were classified into the early PARDS group and the no-early PARDS group. The Pediatric Critical Illness Score (PCIS) was assessed according to the scoring criteria established. The PCIS includes multiple vital signs, laboratory findings, and gastrointestinal manifestations. The total score ranges from 40 to 100 points, with lower scores indicating greater disease severity.18 A score of >80 points was defined as non-critical illness, 71–80 points as critical illness, and ≤70 points as extremely critical illness.

Data Collection

The index time was defined as the time of PICU admission and sepsis diagnosis. All candidate risk-related factors were based on the earliest data obtained before the diagnosis of PARDS. PCIS, Phoenix Sepsis Score, and septic shock status were assessed using clinical information available within the first 2h after PICU admission. Laboratory variables were defined as the first available test results obtained within 2 hours after PICU admission and before fulfillment of any PARDS diagnostic criteria. Vasoactive drug use and blood transfusion were determined according to clinical records within the first 2 hours after PICU admission and before PARDS diagnosis. The following data were extracted: (i) demographics: age and sex; (ii) clinical characteristics: presence of septic shock, use of vasoactive agents, receipt of blood transfusion, PCIS, and Phoenix Sepsis Score; and (iii) laboratory indices: white blood cell count (WBC), platelet count (PLT), neutrophil count (NEU), monocyte count (MON), C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), red cell distribution width (RDW), albumin (ALB), and lactate (LAC). The lactate-to-albumin ratio (LAR) was calculated as serum lactate (mmol/L) divided by serum albumin (g/dL). Early PARDS was assessed during the first 48h after PICU admission based on serial oxygenation parameters and chest imaging findings according to the PALICC-2 criteria.

Statistical Methods

Statistical analysis was performed using R software version 4.3.0. All variables included in the final model had complete data. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables with non-normal distributions are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages [n (%)]. Continuous predictors were assessed for potential nonlinear relationships and transformed when appropriate. Predictor selection was performed using a combination of correlation analysis, variance inflation factor (VIF) assessment, and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression to reduce multicollinearity and identify clinically relevant predictors. Variables selected through this procedure were subsequently entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to develop the final prediction model for early PARDS in children with sepsis. Model performance in the derivation cohort was evaluated in terms of discrimination, calibration, and overall prediction error. Discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Calibration was evaluated using calibration plots, and overall prediction error was quantified using the Brier score. Internal validation was performed using 1,000 bootstrap resamples to estimate optimism-corrected performance, including the corrected C-index and Brier score. For external evaluation, the final model was directly applied to an independent cohort without model refitting or coefficient updating. Because of the limited number of outcome events in the external cohort, external evaluation was restricted to discrimination assessment using the AUC.

Candidate Predictors

Candidate predictors were prespecified based on previous prognostic studies, clinical relevance, and their availability in routine clinical practice. The following predictors measured within the first 2 hours after admission were considered for model development: Age, Sex, WBC, NEU, HB, RDW, PLT, CRP, PCT, FIB, AST, ALT, LAR, PCIS, Septic shock, and Phoenix Sepsis Score. Predictor selection was not based solely on statistical significance in univariable analyses. Continuous predictors were retained in their original continuous form, and potential nonlinear associations with the outcome were evaluated before model fitting.

Sample Size

As this was a retrospective study based on an existing cohort, we assessed the adequacy of the available sample size. Based on a previously developed prediction model for sepsis-associated PARDS, which reported a C-statistic of approximately 0.83,7 the minimum sample size required for developing a multivariable prediction model with a binary outcome was estimated using the method proposed by Riley et al and implemented in the pmsampsize package. The sample size calculation assumed an anticipated outcome incidence of 26.7%, an anticipated C-statistic of 0.83, a corresponding Cox–Snell R2 of 0.2605, and 16 candidate predictor parameters. The estimated minimum sample size was 469 participants, including at least 126 participants with the outcome. The present study included 547 participants, of whom 146 developed PARDS. Therefore, the study met the estimated minimum requirements for both the total sample size and the number of outcome events.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Of the 547 pediatric patients with sepsis in the derivation cohort, 146 (26.7%) developed PARDS within 48h of PICU admission. The baseline characteristics of the PARDS and No-early PARDS groups are summarized in Table 1. There were no significant differences between the groups in age, sex, or nutritional status (P > 0.05). Patients with a higher Phoenix Sepsis Score and a lower PCIS were more likely to develop early PARDS (P < 0.001). Among the 547 pediatric patients with sepsis, 11 laboratory variables differed significantly between the two groups (P < 0.05), including monocyte count (MON), red blood cell count (RBC), hemoglobin (HB), platelet count (PLT), C-reactive protein (CRP), fibrinogen (FIB), lactate (LAC), albumin (ALB), lactate-to-albumin ratio (LAR), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT).

Collinearity Analysis

Pairwise associations among the prespecified continuous and ordinal candidate predictors were assessed using Spearman rank correlation coefficients because several laboratory variables showed markedly skewed distributions. Three pairs of variables showed strong correlations, defined as an absolute correlation coefficient of ≥0.70: WBC and NEU (r = 0.92), AST and ALT (r = 0.73), and Phoenix Sepsis Score and PCIS (r = −0.70), all P < 0.001. The remaining correlations were generally weak to moderate (Figure 2). Multicollinearity was further assessed for all 16 candidate predictors using VIFs and tolerance values. The VIFs ranged from 1.05 to 3.52, and the tolerance values ranged from 0.284 to 0.952, indicating no severe multicollinearity (Table 2). Nevertheless, to reduce redundant information before penalized model fitting, WBC, ALT, and Phoenix Sepsis Score were excluded, whereas NEU, AST, and PCIS, respectively, were retained based on clinical relevance and model interpretability.

RCS and LASSO Regression

To assess the assumption of linearity on the logit scale, the functional forms of the remaining continuous predictors were examined using restricted cubic splines with three knots. Variables with markedly right-skewed distributions, including RDW, CRP, PCT, and AST, were natural-log transformed before model fitting. PLT showed evidence of a nonlinear association with PARDS (P for nonlinearity = 0.026; AIC for the RCS model = 369.98 versus 372.95 for the linear model), and the fitted curve suggested an approximately U-shaped relationship (Supplementary Figure 1). No evidence of nonlinearity was identified for the other assessed continuous predictors (all P for nonlinearity > 0.05), which were therefore modeled as linear terms. PLT was represented by two spline basis terms, resulting in 13 candidate predictors corresponding to 14 candidate parameters. All 14 candidate parameters were subsequently entered into LASSO logistic regression, and the optimal penalty parameter was determined using 10-fold cross-validation. At λ_min = 0.009, 10 parameters had nonzero coefficients, whereas the more parsimonious λ_1se = 0.0413 retained four nonzero parameters (Supplementary Figure 2). The λ_1se solution was therefore selected for subsequent multivariable model development.

Multivariable Logistic Regression Analysis

Following LASSO regression, four predictors—HB, LAR, septic shock, and Phoenix Sepsis Score—were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model. All four variables remained independently associated with early PARDS in pediatric patients with sepsis. Higher HB levels were associated with lower odds of early PARDS (OR = 0.987, 95% CI: 0.976–0.997, P = 0.015). In contrast, higher LAR (OR = 1.548, 95% CI: 1.042–2.299, P = 0.030), the presence of septic shock (OR = 4.158, 95% CI: 2.001–8.639, P < 0.001), and a higher Phoenix Sepsis Score (OR = 3.438, 95% CI: 2.603–4.540, P < 0.001) were associated with increased odds of early PARDS (Table 3).

Model Performance and Validation

The combined prediction model demonstrated good discriminative performance in the derivation cohort, with an AUC of 0.911 (95% CI: 0.882–0.939) (Figure 4). Calibration analysis showed good agreement between the predicted and observed probabilities of early PARDS. The apparent Brier score was 0.0968, indicating a relatively low overall prediction error. After internal validation using 1,000 bootstrap resamples, the optimism-corrected C-index was 0.908, compared with an apparent C-index of 0.911, with an estimated optimism of only 0.003. The optimism-corrected Brier score was 0.0995, compared with an apparent value of 0.0968. These findings suggested limited overfitting and satisfactory internal stability of the model. The final model was subsequently evaluated in an independent cohort from Kashi First People’s Hospital, comprising 100 patients, including 18 who developed early PARDS. The model showed promising discriminative performance, with an AUC of 0.873 (95% CI: 0.775–0.970) (Figure 5).

Discussion

Sepsis remains a major challenge in pediatric intensive care and is characterized by rapid clinical progression, early organ dysfunction, and poor outcomes.19,20 PARDS is one of the most serious complications of sepsis, and its occurrence reflects not only aggravated pulmonary inflammatory injury and impaired gas exchange but also the progression of systemic inflammatory dysregulation and organ dysfunction.21 Consequently, early identification of factors associated with PARDS in septic children is important for clinical monitoring and risk assessment. Using a single-center retrospective cohort, this study identified factors independently associated with early-onset PARDS, defined as PARDS occurring within 48h after admission, in a pediatric septic population. Our findings showed that elevated LAR, higher Phoenix Sepsis Score, and septic shock were associated with increased odds of early PARDS, whereas higher HB levels were associated with lower odds of early PARDS. These readily available clinical and laboratory variables may provide useful information for early risk assessment and closer monitoring during the critical early window of sepsis management.

Among the variables identified in the present study, LAR was a key parameter of particular interest. We found that elevated LAR was independently associated with the early development of PARDS in children with sepsis, with each 1-unit increase in LAR corresponding to a 54.8% increase in the odds of early PARDS. This association is biologically plausible. Lactate is a well-recognized marker of tissue hypoxia and anaerobic metabolism and is commonly elevated in sepsis due to microcirculatory dysfunction and inadequate oxygen delivery.22,23 Albumin, by contrast, not only reflects nutritional status but also acts as a negative acute-phase reactant. During systemic inflammation, albumin synthesis decreases, catabolism increases, and vascular leakage further contributes to hypoalbuminemia.24 Accordingly, an elevated LAR may reflect the coexistence of severe tissue hypoperfusion and systemic inflammatory activation. This pathophysiological state may promote alveolar-capillary endothelial injury, increase vascular permeability, and accelerate pulmonary edema formation, thereby facilitating the onset of ARDS. In addition, hypoalbuminemia itself may reduce plasma colloid osmotic pressure and aggravate pulmonary interstitial edema, further amplifying lung injury. Compared with lactate or albumin alone, LAR may therefore provide a more integrated assessment of perfusion impairment and inflammatory burden.25 Previous studies by Wang et al and Choi et al26,27 reported that each 1-unit increase in LAR was associated with an approximately 1.44- to 1.84-fold increase in mortality among pediatric patients with sepsis. Perez28 further demonstrated that each 1-unit increase in LAR was associated with a 1.31-fold increase in the risk of microcirculatory dysfunction in pediatric sepsis. These findings are broadly consistent with our observation that each 1-unit increase in LAR was associated with a 1.55-fold increase in the odds of early PARDS. Taken together, these results suggest that LAR may not only reflect overall sepsis severity but may also have practical value in identifying children at increased risk of early PARDS. Given that LAR is simple, inexpensive, and readily obtainable, it may be suitable for routine clinical risk assessment.

In addition to LAR, decreased HB was also identified as a factor independently associated with sepsis-associated PARDS in our cohort. Specifically, each 1 g/dL increase in HB was associated with an 12.3% reduction in PARDS risk. Hemoglobin is the principal carrier of oxygen, and anemia may impair systemic oxygen delivery, aggravate tissue hypoxia, and exacerbate lactate accumulation and organ hypoperfusion.29 In the context of sepsis, anemia is often multifactorial and may result from inflammation-related suppression of erythropoiesis, increased erythrocyte destruction, repeated phlebotomy, hemodilution following fluid resuscitation, and potential bone marrow suppression. Therefore, reduced HB may reflect not only impaired oxygen transport but also an underlying state of persistent inflammation and microcirculatory disturbance, both of which are mechanistically linked to lung injury. Previous evidence supports this interpretation. Sheng et al reported that in patients with sepsis, each 1 g/dL increase in hemoglobin was associated with an approximately 12–15% reduction in mortality risk.30 Likewise, a meta-analysis by Zhu et al demonstrated a significant association between HB levels and sepsis prognosis, with higher HB levels associated with lower risk.31 Although these studies focused primarily on prognosis rather than ARDS occurrence, their findings are directionally consistent with our findings. Moreover, Whitney et al reported that biomarkers of endothelial dysfunction were significantly associated with respiratory dysfunction in pediatric sepsis, indirectly supporting the role of endothelial injury in the pathway linking anemia-related hypoxia to lung injury.32 Collectively, these observations suggest that reduced HB may serve as an accessible marker of physiological vulnerability in pediatric sepsis. From a clinical perspective, reduced HB may help identify children who require closer clinical monitoring and comprehensive assessment and may represent an important component of early risk management for patients at high risk of PARDS.

In the present study, the Phoenix Sepsis Score and septic shock were identified as independent clinical indicators associated with early PARDS. Each 1-unit increase in the Phoenix Sepsis Score was associated with a 3.438-fold increase in PARDS. The Phoenix Sepsis Score was developed to quantify life-threatening organ dysfunction in children with suspected infection and incorporates dysfunction across the respiratory, cardiovascular, coagulation, and neurological systems. Its clinical applicability in pediatric sepsis has been supported by previous studies.33 Consistent with our findings, Asiri et al34 reported that, among children with Phoenix-defined sepsis, those who developed PARDS had significantly higher Phoenix total scores than those without PARDS [4 (IQR, 3–6) vs 2 (IQR, 2–3)]. Notably, this result closely parallels the median Phoenix Sepsis Score observed in PARDS patients in our cohort [4 (IQR, 3–5)]. Taken together, these findings highlight the clinical value of the Phoenix Sepsis Score as an indicator of organ dysfunction burden in critically ill children. Septic shock was significantly associated with early PARDS, with an approximately 4.16-fold increase in the odds of early PARDS among children with sepsis. Septic shock represents a state of severe cardiovascular dysfunction, tissue hypoperfusion, endothelial activation, and systemic inflammatory injury. These mechanisms may disrupt the alveolar–capillary barrier, increase pulmonary vascular permeability, and promote interstitial and alveolar edema, thereby facilitating the onset and progression of ARDS.35,36 Prasertsan et al37 reported that children with sepsis-related PARDS had a significantly higher incidence of septic shock, more pronounced organ dysfunction, and higher mortality than those with non-sepsis-related PARDS. Similarly, a meta-analysis by Yin et al38 demonstrated that septic shock was associated with a 2.627-fold increased risk of ARDS compared with non-septic shock conditions. This estimate is broadly consistent with our finding that septic shock was associated with a 4.16-fold increase in the odds of early PARDS in children with sepsis. Overall, these findings indicate that septic shock is not merely a marker of sepsis severity, but may also reflect key pathophysiological processes involved in early lung injury in pediatric sepsis.

Overall, our findings indicate that early PARDS in pediatric sepsis is likely driven by the combined effects of tissue hypoperfusion, inflammatory amplification, impaired oxygen delivery, organ dysfunction, and circulatory failure. The four factors independently associated with early PARDS in this study—elevated LAR, reduced HB, higher Phoenix Sepsis Score, and septic shock —are all available early in the course of hospitalization and are readily applicable in routine practice. Their combined assessment may therefore provide a practical framework for early risk stratification and risk-informed clinical management in children with sepsis.

This study has several limitations. First, as a multicenter retrospective study, the exclusion of patients who withdrew treatment or had a hospital stay of less than 48 hours may have introduced selection bias. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings still requires further validation in prospective studies. Second, all laboratory parameters were measured only within the first 2 hours after admission, without longitudinal dynamic monitoring. Given that the development of PARDS is a dynamic process, serial monitoring of relevant indicators may help improve the accuracy of risk prediction. In addition, the Phoenix Sepsis Score includes a respiratory dysfunction component, whereas the diagnosis of PARDS also relies on respiratory abnormalities; therefore, some degree of informational or temporal overlap may exist between the predictors and the outcome. Third, this study did not include inflammatory cytokine profiles, such as interleukin-6, interleukin-10, and tumor necrosis factor-α, although these biomarkers play important roles in the inflammatory cascade and alveolar-capillary barrier disruption in sepsis-associated PARDS. Future studies should further explore the combined predictive value of inflammatory cytokines and clinical indicators. Moreover, predictor selection in this study was partly data-driven, and the selected predictors and their regression coefficients may therefore be sample-dependent. Fourth, as a retrospective study, we could not fully control for all potential confounders, such as fluid resuscitation volume and mechanical ventilation parameters, both of which may influence the onset and progression of ARDS. Furthermore, the external cohort included only 18 outcome events; therefore, external evaluation was restricted to discrimination, and the findings require further validation in larger independent cohorts. Future prospective studies with dynamic monitoring of multidimensional indicators, including inflammatory cytokines, are warranted to establish more accurate risk prediction models.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this multicenter retrospective study identified that elevated LAR, higher Phoenix Sepsis Score, presence of septic shock, and lower HB levels were predictors independently associated with early PARDS within 48 hours of admission in children with sepsis. These readily available clinical and laboratory indicators may help clinicians identify high‑risk children at an early stage and support timely monitoring and risk‑based management. Given the retrospective design, single-center model development, and limited number of outcome events in the external cohort, these findings should be considered preliminary. Further validation in larger independent, prospective, multicenter cohorts is required before the model can be considered for routine clinical application.
