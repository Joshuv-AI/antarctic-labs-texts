Regularity, Green’s relations and starred Green’s relations

In a semigroup
S
, an element

is said to be

if there is

in
S
such that
a=aba
and
S
is said to be a

When faced with a new type of transformation semigroup, the initial algebraic inquiry typically involves determining the characteristics of its Green’s equivalences. These relations are commonly utilized to categorize elements within a semigroup. For definition of these relations, we recommend that the reader consults Howie
[
12
]
. In semigroup theory, there are five Green’s relations, namely
\mathcal{L,R,D,J\ \text{and }H}
. It is a known fact in finite semigroups that the relations
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{J}
are equivalent (see [
[
12
]
, Proposition 2.1.4]). Therefore, we will focus on characterizing the relations
\mathcal{L,R,D\,\text{and }H}
on the large and small Schröder monoids
\mathcal{LS}_{n}\ \text{and }\mathcal{SS}_{n}
, respectively.

From this point forward in this section, we shall refer to
\alpha
and
\beta
in
\mathcal{LS}_{n}

\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&\ldots&A_{p}\\
a_{1}&\ldots&a_{p}\end{pmatrix}\text{and}\ \beta=\begin{pmatrix}B_{1}&\ldots&B_{p}\\
b_{1}&\ldots&b_{p}\end{pmatrix}\,(1\leq p\leq n)

and
\alpha
and
\beta
in
\mathcal{SS}_{n}

\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&A_{2}&\ldots&A_{p}\\

(11)

Conversely, suppose that

Let

]

Many classes of transformation semigroups have been shown to be either left abundant, right abundant, or abundant; see for example

,
\mathop{\rm ker}\nolimits\,\delta=\mathop{\rm ker}\nolimits\,\gamma
,
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\gamma=\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\beta
. Thus by (i) and (ii) we have
\alpha\mathcal{L}^{*}\delta\mathcal{R}^{*}\gamma\mathcal{L}^{*}\beta
. Hence, by (
[
12
]
, Proposition 1.5.11) it follows that
\alpha\mathcal{D}^{*}\beta
. The proof is now complete.

∎

Lemma 2.13
.

On the Schröder monoids
\mathcal{LS}_{n}
and
\mathcal{SS}_{n}

(
n\geq 3
)
, we have
\mathcal{D}^{*}=\mathcal{R}^{*}\circ\mathcal{L}^{*}\circ\mathcal{R}^{*}=\mathcal{L}^{*}\circ\mathcal{R}^{*}\circ\mathcal{L}^{*}
.

Proof.

The sufficiency follows from the converse of the proof of (iv) in the above theorem, while for the necessity, we have to prove that
\mathcal{L}^{*}\circ\mathcal{R}^{*}\neq\mathcal{R}^{*}\circ\mathcal{L}^{*}
. Take

\alpha=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&2\\
{1}&2\end{array}\right)\text{ and }\beta=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&3\\
{1}&3\end{array}\right).

Now define
\delta=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&3\\
{1}&2\end{array}\right).
Then clearly
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\alpha=\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\delta
and
\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\delta=\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\beta
, and so
\alpha\mathcal{L}^{*}\delta\mathcal{R}^{*}\beta
. i.e.,
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{L}^{*}\circ\mathcal{R}^{*}
.

On the other hand, if we have
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{R}^{*}\circ\mathcal{L}^{*}
, then there must exist
\gamma\in\mathcal{SS}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{LS}_{n}
such that
\alpha\mathcal{R}^{*}\gamma\mathcal{L}^{*}\beta
. However, this means that
\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\alpha=\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\gamma=\{1,2\}
and
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\gamma=\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\beta=\{1,3\}
, which is impossible. The result now follows.
∎

Lemma 2.14
.

On the semigroups
{RLS}_{n}(p)
and
{RSS}_{n}(p)
, we have
\mathcal{D}^{*}=\mathcal{R}^{*}\circ\mathcal{L}^{*}\circ\mathcal{R}^{*}=\mathcal{L}^{*}\circ\mathcal{R}^{*}\circ\mathcal{L}^{*}
.

Proof.

The proof is the same as the proof of the above lemma.
∎

Lemma 2.16

For

\mid\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\alpha\mid\leq\mid\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\beta\mid

Let

Lemma 2.17
.

On the large and small Schröder monoids

∎

Lemma 2.18
.

On the semigroup

∎

Remark 2.19
.

(i)

Now, let

and the number of idempotents in

Lemma 2.20
.

For

, the number of
\mathcal{R}^{*}-
classes in

is

\sum\limits_{r=p}^{n}{\binom{n-1}{r-1}}{\binom{r-1}{p-1}}.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{SS}_{n}
be such that

and
|\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\alpha|=r
for

. Next observe that since

, then we can choose the remaining
r-1

from

\binom{n-1}{r-1}
ways. Moreover, we can partition
\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\alpha
into

convex (modulo
\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\alpha
) subsets in
\binom{r-1}{p-1}
ways. The result follows after multiplying these two binomial coefficients and taking the sum from
r=p
to
r=n
.
∎

Lemma 2.21
.

For
1\leq p\leq n
, we have
\sum\limits_{r=p}^{n}{\binom{n-1}{r-1}}{\binom{r-1}{p-1}}=\binom{n-1}{p-1}2^{n-p}
.

Proof.

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{r=p}^{n}{\binom{n-1}{r-1}}{\binom{r-1}{p-1}}=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{r=p}^{n}{\frac{(n-1)!}{(n-r)!(r-1)!}\cdot\frac{(r-1)!}{(r-p)!(p-1)!}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{r=p}^{n}{\frac{(n-1)!}{(n-r)!(r-p)!(p-1)!}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{r=p}^{n}{\frac{(n-1)!(n-p)!}{(n-r)!(p-1)!(r-p)!(n-p)!}}\,\,\left(\textnormal{multiplying by $\frac{(n-p)!}{(n-p)!}$}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{r=p}^{n}{\frac{(n-1)!}{(p-1)!(n-p)!}\cdot\frac{(n-p)!}{(n-r)!(r-p)!}}\textnormal{ (by spliting and rearranging the fractions)}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{r=p}^{n}{\binom{n-1}{p-1}\binom{n-p}{n-r}}

\displaystyle=\binom{n-1}{p-1}\sum\limits_{r=p}^{n}{\binom{n-p}{n-r}}

\displaystyle=\binom{n-1}{p-1}2^{n-p},

as required.
∎

Let

The result follows from Lemma

from

to

.
∎

3
Rank properties

Let
S
be a semigroup and
A
be a nonempty subset of
S
. The
smallest subsemigroup
of
S
that contains
A
is called the
subsemigroup generated by
A
usually denoted by
\langle A\rangle
. If there exists a finite subset
A
of a semigroup
S
such that
\langle A\rangle
equals
S
, then
S
is referred to as a
finitely-generated semigroup
. The
rank
of a finitely generated semigroup
S
is defined as the minimum cardinality of a subset
A
such that
\langle A\rangle
equals
S
. i.e.,

\text{rank}(S)=\min\{|A|:\langle A\rangle=S\}.

If the set
A
consists exclusively of the idempotents in
S
, then
S
is called
idempotent-generated
(equivalently, a
semiband
), and the idempotent-rank is denoted by
\text{idrank}(S)
.
The monoid
\mathcal{LS}_{n}
first appeared in Ganyushkin and Mazorchuk
[
8
]
, where it was shown that it is idempotent generated. Moreover, the combinatorial properties of the semigroup have been explored in
[
21
]
, where it was shown that the size (or order) of
\mathcal{LS}_{n}
corresponds to the
large
(or
double
)
Schröder number
:

s_{0}=1,\quad s_{n}=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum\limits_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n+1}{n-r}\binom{n+r}{r}\quad(n\geq 1).

It is important to note that Dimitrova and Koppitz
[
2
]
examines the rank of the semigroup of all order-preserving and
extensive
(which means order-increasing) partial transformations on a finite chain, denoted by
\mathcal{POE}_{n}
. This monoid can easily be shown to be isomorphic to the large
Schröder
monoid
\mathcal{LS}_{n}
(see
[
26
]
); thus, the rank of
\mathcal{LS}_{n}
can easily be obtained from [
[
2
]
, Proposition 4.0], by isomorphism. However, we present the result and proof for the sake of completeness and the generating elements. Moreover, Ping
et al.

[
24
]
generalized the results of Dimitrova and Koppitz
[
2
]
, where they obtained the rank of the two-sided ideals of
\mathcal{POE}_{n}
. The rank of the two-sided ideals of
\mathcal{LS}_{n}
can also be obtained through isomorphism from [
[
24
]
, Proposition 2.6]. Furthermore, both articles fail to recognize that each of the objects considered has a minimum generating set (not minimal) since each of the object is an
\mathcal{R}
-trivial semigroup; thus, most of the proofs given in the two articles are belabored. In this section, we provide among other results the proof of the rank properties of these objects.

For a more detailed discussion about ranks in semigroup theory, we refer the reader to
[
13
,
14
]
. Several authors have explored the ranks, idempotent ranks, and nilpotent ranks of various classes of semigroups of transformations. Notably, the works of Gomes and Howie
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
, Howie and McFadden
[
15
]
, Garba
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
, Umar
[
26
,
28
,
29
]
and Zubairu
et. al.,

[
32
]
are here emphasized. The large Schröder monoid
\mathcal{LS}_{n}
has been shown to be idempotent-generated in [
[
8
]
, Theorem 14.4.5], where it first appeared. Our aim is to compute the rank of the two sided ideal
M(n,p)
of the Schröder monoid
\mathcal{SS}_{n}
, thereby obtaining the rank of
\mathcal{SS}_{n}
, as special cases. We first note the following definitions and a well known result about decreasing maps from
[
26
,
30
]
.

Let
f(\alpha)
be the cardinal of

F(\alpha)=\{x\in\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\alpha:\,x\alpha=x\};

the set of fixed points of the map
\alpha
. Then we have the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

For all order decreasing partial maps
\alpha
and
\beta
on
A\subseteq[n]
,
F(\alpha\beta)=F(\alpha)\cap F(\beta)=F(\beta\alpha)
.

Proof.

If
\alpha
or
\beta
is zero (i.e., the empty map), the result follows. Now suppose
\alpha
and
\beta
are nonzero partial order decreasing maps. The proof is the same as the proof of Lemma 2. 1. in
[
30
]
.
∎

However, we initiate our examination with the following result about generating elements of
\mathcal{LS}_{n}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

The large Schröder monoid
\mathcal{LS}_{n}
is idempotent-generated.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{LS}_{n}
be as expressed in (
8
). If
p=0
, then
\alpha
is the empty map which is an idempotent, and the result follows.

Now suppose
1\leq p\leq n
and let
t_{i}=\min A_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq p
. Notice that the maps defined as

\epsilon=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&\cdots&A_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{pmatrix}

and

\epsilon_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&\cdots&a_{i-1}&\{a_{i},t_{i}\}&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\\
a_{1}&\cdots&a_{i-1}&a_{i}&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{pmatrix}\,\,(1\leq i\leq p)

are idempotents in
J^{*}_{p}
.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\epsilon\epsilon_{1}\cdots\epsilon_{p}=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&\cdots&A_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\{t_{1},a_{1}\}&t_{2}&\cdots&t_{p}\\
a_{1}&t_{2}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&\{a_{2},t_{2}\}&t_{3}&\cdots&t_{p}\\
a_{1}&a_{2}&t_{3}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{pmatrix}\cdots\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&\cdots&a_{p-1}&\{a_{p},t_{p}\}\\
a_{1}&\cdots&a_{p-1}&a_{p}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&\cdots&A_{p}\\
a_{1}&\cdots&a_{p}\end{pmatrix}=\alpha.

The result now follows.
∎

Notice that in the proof of the above lemma,
|\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\alpha|=h(\alpha)=h(\epsilon)=h(\epsilon_{i})=p
for all
1\leq i\leq p
. Consequently, we have the following result.

Lemma 3.3
.

Every element in
S\in\{{RSS}_{n}(p),\,{RLS}_{n}(p),\,K(n,p),\,M(n,p)\}
of height
p
can be expressed as a product of idempotents in

, each of height

.

The next result shows that the set of nonzero idempotents in
{RLS}_{n}(p)
(
{RSS}_{n}(p)
) is the minimum generating set of
{RLS}_{n}(p)\setminus\{0\}
(
{RSS}_{n}(p)\setminus\{0\}
).

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\alpha
,
\beta
be elements in
{RLS}_{n}(p)\setminus\{0\}

(
resp.,
\alpha
,
\beta
in
{RSS}_{n}(p)\setminus\{0\}
)
. Then
\alpha\beta\in E({RLS}_{n}(p)\setminus\{0\})

(
resp.,
\alpha\beta\in E({RSS}_{n}(p)\setminus\{0\})

if and only if
\alpha,\,\beta\in E({RLS}_{n}(p)\setminus\{0\})
and
\alpha\beta=\alpha

(
resp.,
\alpha,\,\beta\in E({RSS}_{n}(p)\setminus\{0\})
and
\alpha\beta=\alpha
)
.

Proof.

Suppose

\alpha\beta\in E({RLS}_{n}(p)\setminus\{0\})
(resp.,
\alpha\beta\in E({RSS}_{n}(p)\setminus\{0\})

p=f(\alpha\beta)\leq f(\alpha)\leq|\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\alpha|=p,

p=f(\alpha\beta)\leq f(\beta)\leq|\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\beta|=p.

This ensures that

F(\alpha)=F(\alpha\beta)=F(\beta),

and so
\alpha,\,\beta\in E({RLS}_{n}(p)\setminus\{0\})
and
\alpha\beta=\alpha
(resp.,
\alpha,\,\beta\in E({RSS}_{n}(p)\setminus\{0\})
and
\alpha\beta=\alpha
).

The converse is obvious.
∎

It is evident that necessity ensues, as the result of multiplying a non-idempotent element with any other element does not yield a non-zero idempotent, by Lemma

\mathcal{R}^{*}-

It follows from the fact that there are

\mathcal{R}^{*}-

and each

The next lemma is crucial to determine the ranks of the Schröder monoids

and
\mathcal{SS}_{n}

Lemma 3.7

For

J^{*}_{p}\subset\langle J^{*}_{p+1}\rangle

\alpha\in J^{*}_{p}

It suffices to prove that every idempotent of height

\epsilon\in E(J^{*}_{p})

\epsilon=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&\cdots&A_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{pmatrix},

where
\min A_{i}=t_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq p
.

Now
\epsilon

Case 1.
Suppose
\epsilon
is a full idempotent map of height

|A_{i}|\geq 2
. Thus, there are two subcases to consider.

Subcase i.
Suppose

and
\epsilon_{2}
as:

\epsilon_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&\cdots&A_{i-1}&t_{i}&x_{i_{1}}&A_{i+1}&\cdots&A_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i-1}&t_{i}&x_{i_{1}}&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{pmatrix}

and

\epsilon_{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccccccccc}t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i-1}&\{t_{i},x_{i_{1}}\}&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{j}&y_{j_{1}}&t_{j+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i-1}&t_{i}&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{j}&y_{j_{1}}&t_{j+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{array}\right).

Subcase ii.
Suppose
|A_{i}|>2
, and let
A_{i}=\{t_{i},x_{i_{1}},\,x_{i_{2}}\,\ldots,\,t_{i+1}-1\}
. Thus define
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
as:

\epsilon_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&\cdots&A_{i-1}&t_{i}&\{x_{i_{1}},\,x_{i_{2}}\,\ldots,\,t_{i+1}-1\}&A_{i+1}&\cdots&A_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i-1}&t_{i}&x_{i_{1}}&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{pmatrix}

and

\epsilon_{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccccccccc}t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i-1}&\{t_{i},x_{i_{1}}\}&x_{i_{2}}&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i-1}&t_{i}&x_{i_{2}}&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{array}\right).

Clearly, in either of the subcases, one can easily see that
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
are in
E(J^{*}_{p+1})
, and also in each subcase
\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}=\epsilon
.

Case 2.
Now suppose
\epsilon
is strictly partial. Thus
|(\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\epsilon)^{c}|\geq 1
. Let
q\in(\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\epsilon)^{c}
. Then there are three subcases to consider, i.e., either (i) there exists
x_{i_{k}}
and
x_{i_{k+1}}
in
A_{i}
(for some
1\leq i\leq p
), such that
x_{i_{k}}<q<x_{i_{k+1}}
; (ii) or there exists
1\leq i\leq p-1
, such that
\max A_{i}<q<\min A_{i+1}
; (iii) or
\max A_{p}<q
. Thus, we consider the three cases separately.

Subcase i.
Suppose there exists
x_{i_{k}}
and
x_{i_{k+1}}
in
A_{i}
(for some
1\leq i\leq p
), such that
x_{i_{k}}<q<x_{i_{k+1}}
. Let
A_{i}=\{t_{i},x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{{k}}},x_{i_{k+1}},\ldots\}
. Thus define
\epsilon_{1}
,
\epsilon_{2}
and
\epsilon_{3}
as:

\epsilon_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&\cdots&A_{i-1}&\{t_{i},x_{i_{1}},\,\ldots,\,x_{i_{k}}\}&\{x_{i_{k+1}},x_{i_{k+2}},\,\ldots\}&A_{i+1}&\cdots&A_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i-1}&t_{i}&x_{i_{k+1}}&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{pmatrix};

\epsilon_{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccccccc}t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i}&\{q,x_{i_{k+1}}\}&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i}&q&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{array}\right)

and

\epsilon_{3}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccccccccc}t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i-1}&\{t_{i},q\}&x_{i_{k+1}}&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i-1}&t_{i}&x_{i_{k+1}}&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{array}\right).

Subcase ii.
Suppose
\max A_{i}<q<\min A_{i+1}
. Now either
(a)

|A_{i}|\geq 2
; or
(b)

|A_{i}|=1
and
|A_{i+1}|\geq 2
; or
(c)

|A_{i}|=|A_{i+1}|=1
and
|A_{j}|\geq 2
for some
i\neq j\neq i+1
; or
(d)
all blocks of
\epsilon
are singleton, and so there exists
q^{\prime}\in\,(\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\epsilon)^{c}
such that
\max A_{j}<q<\min A_{j+1}
for some
1\leq j\leq p-1
.

(a.)
Suppose
\max A_{i}<q<\min A_{i+1}
such that
|A_{i}|\geq 2
. Now let
A_{i}=\{t_{i},x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}},\dots\}
. Then define
\epsilon_{1}
,
\epsilon_{2}
and
\epsilon_{3}
as:

\epsilon_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&\cdots&A_{i-1}&t_{i}&\{x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}},\,\ldots,\}&A_{i+1}&\cdots&A_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i-1}&t_{i}&x_{i_{1}}&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{pmatrix};

\epsilon_{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccccccc}t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i}&\{x_{i_{1}},q\}&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i}&x_{i_{1}}&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{array}\right)

and

\epsilon_{3}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccccccccc}t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i-1}&\{t_{i},x_{i_{1}}\}&q&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i-1}&t_{i}&q&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{array}\right).

In either of the two subcases above, one can easily verify that
\epsilon_{1}
,
\epsilon_{2}
and
\epsilon_{3}
are in
E(J^{*}_{p+1})
, and also

\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}\epsilon_{3}=\epsilon.

(b.)
Suppose
\max A_{i}<q<\min A_{i+1}
such that,
|A_{i}|=1
and
|A_{i+1}|\geq 2
. Now, let
A_{i+1}=\{t_{i+1},x_{{(i+1)}_{1}},x_{{(i+1)}_{2}},\dots\}
. Then define
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
as:

\epsilon_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&\cdots&A_{i}&t_{i+1}&\{x_{{(i+1)}_{1}},x_{{(i+1)}_{2}},\,\ldots,\}&A_{i+2}&\cdots&A_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i}&t_{i+1}&x_{{(i+1)}_{1}}&t_{i+2}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{pmatrix}

and

\epsilon_{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccccccc}t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i}&q&\{t_{i+1},x_{{(i+1)}_{1}}\}&t_{i+2}&\cdots&t_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i}&q&t_{i+1}&t_{i+2}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{array}\right).

Clearly
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
in
E(J^{*}_{p+1})
and
\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}=\epsilon
.

(c.)
Suppose
\max A_{i}<q<\min A_{i+1}
such that
|A_{i}|=|A_{i+1}|=1
and suppose there exists
1\leq j\leq p-2
such that
|A_{j}|\geq 2
, where
i\neq j\neq i+1
. Now let
A_{j}=\{t_{j},y_{j_{1}},y_{j_{2}},\,\ldots\}
and we may suppose without loss of generality that
i<j
. Thus
q<y_{j_{1}}
. Now, define
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
as:

\epsilon_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&\cdots&A_{i}&q&A_{i+1}&\cdots&A_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i}&q&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{pmatrix}

and

\epsilon_{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccccccc}t_{1}&\cdots&t_{j+1}&y_{j_{1}}&t_{j+2}&\cdots&t_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{j+1}&y_{j_{1}}&t_{j+2}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{array}\right).

(d.)
Suppose
\max A_{i}<q<\min A_{i+1}
is such that
|A_{i}|=1
for all
1\leq i\leq p
, and suppose there exists
d\in(\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\epsilon)^{c}
, such that
\max A_{j}<d<\min A_{j+1}
for some
1<j<p
. We may suppose without loss of generality that
d<q
.

If
i=j
, then
\max A_{i}<d<q<\min A_{i+1}
. Thus, define
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
as:

\epsilon_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&\cdots&A_{i}&q&A_{i+1}&\cdots&A_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i}&q&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{pmatrix}

and

\epsilon_{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccccccc}t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i}&d&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i}&d&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{array}\right).

However, if
i\neq j
then
j<i
since
d<q
. So define
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
as:

\epsilon_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&\cdots&A_{j}&d&A_{j+1}&\cdots&A_{i}&\cdots&A_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{j}&d&t_{j+1}&\cdots&t_{i}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{pmatrix}

and

\epsilon_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}t_{1}&\cdots&t_{j}&t_{j+1}&\cdots&t_{i}&q&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{j}&t_{j+1}&\cdots&t_{i}&q&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{pmatrix}.

Thus in all the above cases, it can easily be seen that
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
are in
E(J^{*}_{p+1})
, and
\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}=\epsilon
.

Case 3.
If
\max A_{p}<q
, then either (i) there exists
1\leq i\leq p
, such that
|A_{i}|\geq 2
; (ii) or there exists
d\in(\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\epsilon)^{c}
, such that
q<d
.

(i.)
Suppose there exists
1\leq i\leq p
, such that
|A_{i}|\geq 2
. Now let
A_{i}=\{t_{i},x_{i_{1}},\ldots\}
. Then
define
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
as:

\epsilon_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&\cdots&A_{i-1}&t_{i}&\{x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}},\ldots\}&A_{i+1}&\cdots&A_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i-1}&t_{i}&x_{i_{1}}&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{pmatrix}

and

\epsilon_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i-1}&\{t_{i},x_{i_{1}}\}&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}&q\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{i-1}&t_{i}&t_{i+1}&\cdots&t_{p}&q\end{pmatrix}.

(ii.)
Suppose there exists
d\in(\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\epsilon)^{c}
, such that
q<d
. Then define

\epsilon_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&\cdots&A_{p}&q\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{p}&q\end{pmatrix}

and

\epsilon_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}t_{1}&\cdots&t_{p}&d\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{p}&d\end{pmatrix}.

Clearly in (i) and (ii) above,
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
are clearly in
E(JJ^{*}_{p+1})
and also
\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}=\epsilon
.

The proof of the lemma is now complete.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

The above lemma also holds when the large Schröder monoid
\mathcal{LS}_{n}
is replaced by the small Schröder monoid
\mathcal{SS}_{n}
, with a slight modification of the proof by substituting
t_{1}=1
in the definition of
\epsilon
.

Consequently, we have the following (which can equivalently be obtain from [
[
24
]
, Proposition 2.6] by isomorphism).

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
K(n,p)
be as defined in (
4
). Then

\text{rank }K(n,p)=\text{idrank }K(n,p)=|E(J^{*}_{p})|=\sum\limits_{r=p}^{n}{\binom{n}{r}}{\binom{r-1}{p-1}}.

Proof.

Notice that by Lemma
3.7
,
\langle J^{*}_{p}\rangle=K(n,p)
for all
p
. Notice also that,
\langle E({RLS}_{n}(p)\setminus\{0\})\rangle=J^{*}_{p}
. The result now follows from Theorem
3.5
.
∎

We now deduce the following corollary which can also be obtain from [
[
2
]
, Proposition 4] by isomorphism.

Corollary 3.10
.

The
\text{rank }\mathcal{LS}_{n}=\text{idrank }\mathcal{LS}_{n}=2n.

Proof.

Notice that by Lemma
3.7
,
\langle J^{*}_{n-1}\rangle=\mathcal{LS}_{n}\setminus J^{*}_{n}=K(n,n-1)
. Notice also that
J^{*}_{n}
contains only the identity element
1_{[n]}
. Thus
\text{rank }\mathcal{LS}_{n}=\text{idrank }\mathcal{LS}_{n}=\text{idrank }K(n,n-1)+1
. The result now follows from Theorem
3.9
.
∎

Similarly, we also obtain the next theorem and its corollary:

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
M(n,p)
be as defined in (
5
). Then

\text{rank }M(n,p)=\text{idrank }M(n,p)=|E(J^{*}_{p})|=\binom{n-1}{p-1}2^{n-p}.

Corollary 3.12
.

The
\text{rank }\mathcal{SS}_{n}=\text{idrank }\mathcal{SS}_{n}=2n-1.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of the above theorem.
∎
