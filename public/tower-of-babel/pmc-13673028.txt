Manual ventilation with and without a flow limiting device during induction of general anesthesia: a randomized crossover trial
Authors: Mark F Brady, Vendhan Ramanujam, Korinne Dankievitch, Ryan Barnicle, Arthur S Slutsky, Mark C Kendall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•A flow limiting device significantly reduced peak inspiratory pressure during forceful breaths under general anesthesia.•Consistent ventilation performance was observed by providers of varying experience.•A flow limiting device functioned as a safeguard against inadvertent manual overventilation.•Reduced tidal volumes during forceful breaths may impair alveolar ventilation, particularly during CPR, if not corrected.Keywords: Flow limiting valve, Self-inflating resuscitator bag, Manual ventilation, Peak airway pressure

Introduction

Manual ventilation using a self-inflating resuscitator bag (SIRB) remains an important intervention in emergency and critical care settings, particularly when mechanical ventilation is unavailable. Despite its widespread use, manual ventilation is frequently associated with both excessive and insufficient ventilation. Excessive ventilation may result in high tidal volumes and elevated airway pressures, increasing the risk of gastric insufflation, aspiration and lung injury.1, 2 Previous studies have shown that, regardless of a provider's qualifications or experience, manual ventilation commonly produces high peak airway pressures, excessive tidal volumes, and frequent hyperventilation.3, 4, 5 However, inadequate ventilation has also emerged as an important concern, particularly during cardiopulmonary resuscitation, where insufficient tidal volume delivery has been associated with reduced survival following out-of-hospital cardiac arrest.6 These findings highlight the challenge of delivering consistent manual ventilation that achieves adequate ventilation while avoiding excessive airway pressures. This challenge may be amplified during airway emergencies and patient transport, where time constraints and elevated stress increase the likelihood of ventilation errors.

Education and training alone have demonstrated limited effectiveness in mitigating these risks. Recent evidence suggests that traditional training strategies, including didactic instruction, hands‑on practice, and simulation‑based feedback, do not consistently improve manual ventilation performance.3, 7, 8, 9, 10 In contrast, device-based approaches that provide real‑time regulation or feedback of ventilatory parameters have been shown to significantly reduce hyperventilation and excessive airway pressures compared with standard training alone.7, 11, 12, 13, 14 Together, these findings highlight the need for interventions that improve the safety and consistency of manual ventilation.

The need to improve the safety and effectiveness of manual ventilation has led to the development of devices designed to regulate airway flow and pressure. In 2022, the Sotair® device (SafeBVM Corp., USA) received U.S. Food and Drug Administration 510(k) clearance as a Class II medical device.15 Sotair® is a single-use, passive, inline flow limiting valve positioned between the SIRB and the patient connection. The device is actuated by flow rather than pressure: when inspiratory flow exceeds approximately 55 L·min−1 during manual bag compression, a flexible flapper transiently occludes the lumen and interrupts gas delivery. The valve reopens when the provider releases the bag and the pressure differential decreases, typically within 150 ms of activation, allowing delivery of the next breath. However, because the device limits inspiratory flow, it may also reduce delivered tidal volume if providers do not appropriately adjust their ventilation technique. This raises concern for insufficient ventilation in situations where adequate tidal volume delivery is critical. Avoiding hypoventilation therefore requires that providers deliver subsequent breaths at flow rates below the flow limiting threshold.15 Despite its growing clinical adoption, the performance of this device has not been investigated in humans.

The purpose of this study was to compare peak airway pressures generated during intentionally forceful manual ventilation using a SIRB equipped with the flow limiting device (FLD) versus a standard SIRB alone in intubated patients undergoing general anesthesia for non-emergent surgery. This trial was designed as a first-in-human mechanistic characterization of device behavior, specifically to determine whether the flow-actuated occlusion performs as intended and generates measurable feedback under controlled conditions. Assessment of clinical effectiveness or overall ventilation adequacy was beyond the scope of this study.

Methods

This study received full committee review and approval from the Institutional Review Board of Rhode Island Hospital (IRB# 2079951). This trial was prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06261619; May 09, 2024) and is reported following the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials 2025 checklist for reporting of randomized trials.16

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study was conducted at The Rhode Island Hospital, Providence, Rhode Island between May 2024 and March 2025. Adults aged 18–80 years old with an American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status (ASA) classification of I–II who were scheduled for non-emergent surgery requiring general anesthesia were approached to participate. Patients were excluded if they had known oropharyngeal or facial pathology, an ASA classification ≥III, or any condition that could interfere with airway instrumentation or device performance. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Group assignment

Participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups in a randomized crossover design. All patients received manual SIRB ventilation with and without the FLD. In the FLD + SIRB → SIRB group, patients underwent manual ventilation with the flow limiting device for 3 min followed by manual ventilation without the device for an additional 3 min. In the SIRB → FLD + SIRB group, patients received manual ventilation without the device for 3 min followed by manual ventilation with the flow limiting device for 3 min. Each participant served as their own control for a total study duration of 6 min.

Randomization

The order in which the patients received the interventions was randomized. Randomization was performed using a computer-generated list of random numbers in a 1:1 ratio, with group assignments concealed in sequentially numbered, sealed envelopes prepared and provided by research personnel prior to study initiation.

Intraoperative management

All patients underwent standard induction of general anesthesia. After adequate preoxygenation, intravenous propofol with fentanyl and/or lidocaine was administered to induce unconsciousness. After verifying the ability to ventilate using a SIRB, intravenous rocuronium was given to facilitate endotracheal intubation. After successful intubation, a continuous propofol infusion was initiated to maintain anesthesia. Manual ventilation was then performed according to group assignment. After completion of manual ventilation, patients were transitioned to mechanical ventilation prior to the start of surgery.

For each participant, research personnel not involved in patient care performed a pretest evaluation of the individual components of the system including the SIRB (Vyaire® Airlife, Vyaire® Medical, Inc), FLD, flow and pressure sensor (Sensirion SFM 3300-D), and adjustable pressure-limiting valve (NuFLO2, Mercury Medical) which were connected in series to form the complete assembly. This evaluation ensured proper assembly, secure connections, and appropriate system function.

After attaching this assembly to the endotracheal tube, anesthesia providers were instructed by research personnel to deliver one forceful breath every 30 s (a total of five breaths), with non-forceful breaths in between, during each ventilation period. A forceful breath was defined as a firm manual compression of the SIRB sufficient to activate the adjustable pressure-limiting valve, which was set at 35 cm H2O for safety.

Training anesthesia providers

Anesthesia providers assigned to the operating room included attending anesthesiologists, anesthesia residents (PGY-2–PGY-4) and Certified Registered Nurse Anesthetists (CRNAs). Prior to study participation, all providers received standardized training conducted by research personnel, which included review of written instructions, a video demonstration of proper device use, and hands-on familiarization with the device. For each participant, the same anesthesia provider performed both study arms consecutively.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was the mean peak inspiratory pressure during forceful breaths. Secondary outcomes included tidal volume (mL), peak flow (L/min), and peak inspiratory pressure (cm H2O) during non-forceful breaths. Heart rate, blood pressure (mmHg), and oxygen saturation (SpO2, %) were recorded at 1-min intervals and obtained from the anesthesia machine. Device-related malfunction events (flap fails to close/open, manufacture defects, assembly connection failure) events were recorded.

Sample size calculation

We calculated the sample size for a two-period, two-sequence, randomized crossover superiority trial (FLD + SIRB → SIRB group; SIRB → FLD + SIRB group). Assuming a true difference in mean peak inspiratory pressure of forceful breaths between SIRB ventilation alone and SIRB ventilation with the FLD of 7.5 cm H2O (superiority margin, Δ), with a standard deviation of 7.5 cm H2O, a two-sided α of 0.05, and 90% power, 24 participants (12 in each group) were required. To account for potential attrition, the sample size was increased by 25% for a total of 30 participants. The 7.5 cm H2O margin was selected as a conservative, clinically meaningful reduction based on pilot observations showing average decrease of 10 cm H2O and its expected effect on lowering peak inspiratory pressures toward safer levels during manual ventilation. Sample size estimation was performed using Monte Carlo simulations (10,000 replicates) under an analysis of variance model for crossover trials, including terms for treatment, period, and sequence effects, and assuming no carryover effect.17 Power was calculated as the proportion of simulations that resulted in a two-sided p value < 0.05 for the treatment effect.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics. Continuous variables were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed rank test or paired Student’s t test, as appropriate based on data distribution. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Mean differences in the primary outcome are presented with 95% confidence intervals. The primary analysis used generalized linear mixed effects modeling to test differences in average ventilation approach while accounting for the structure of data collection. The primary effect of interest was a dummy code comparing between use of the FLD and SIRB ventilation. Additional fixed effects included time and crossover protocol assignment (FLD first or SIRB ventilation first). Contrasts were used to calculate averages across fixed effects to test the overall benefit of the FLD relative to SIRB ventilation. Random effects modeled the correlations over time as a growth curve model as each individual had many repeated observations over time. Hypothesis testing was limited to the prespecified primary outcome. Hemodynamic, oxygen-saturation, and provider experience analysis were considered secondary and exploratory and are presented descriptively as model-based estimated differences with 95% confidence intervals. The p values are not reported or interpreted for these outcomes. The statistical analysis was performed using SigmaPlot 14.5 (SystatSoftware, Inc), R statistical software (version 4.4.1; R Core Team, 2025), and IBM SPSS Statistics, version 31.

Results

A total of 48 adult patients were assessed for eligibility. Eighteen patients were excluded: seven declined participation, seven had an ASA classification ≥III, two surgical cases were canceled, and 2 cases were excluded due to computer related technical issues. Thirty patients were randomized, with fifteen allocated to the FLD + SIRB → SIRB group and fifteen to the SIRB → FLD + SIRB group. All randomized patients completed the study and were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). No FLD device-related malfunction (i.e. flap fails to close/open, manufacture defects, assembly connection failure) events were observed.

Discussion

Our most important finding was that the flow-limiting device significantly reduced mean peak inspiratory pressure during forceful manual ventilation breaths in endotracheally intubated patients. Elevated airway pressures and excessive tidal volumes are key contributors to barotrauma and volutrauma.18, 19 By limiting these variables, the device may reduce the risk of manual ventilation-induced lung injury. At the same time, manual ventilation in acute and critical care settings carries a dual risk: hyperventilation leading to excessive airway pressures and lung injury, and hypoventilation resulting in insufficient tidal volumes with potential hypoxia and hypercapnia. The FLD directly addresses the former while introducing important considerations regarding the latter.

Previous literature has proposed numerous interventions to improve manual ventilation performance; however, achieving consistent, effective ventilation while limiting iatrogenic harm remains challenging.20, 21 Dodds and colleagues demonstrated substantial variability in bag-valve-mask performance in a mechanical lung model simulating spontaneous breathing, including failure to deliver adequate oxygen concentrations. The authors also showed that increasing tidal volumes may paradoxically reduce effective oxygen delivery due to increased air entrainment.22 Therefore, excessive tidal volumes may not only increase the risk of lung injury but also compromise oxygen delivery, reinforcing the importance of avoiding hyperventilation.

Current resuscitation guidelines from the American Heart Association and European Resuscitation Council recommend adult tidal volumes of approximately 500–600 mL.23, 24 Nevertheless, studies report wide variability in manual ventilation performance, with “acceptable” tidal volumes ranging from 250 to 700 mL.25 In the present study, the FLD selectively limited excessive airway pressures and tidal volumes during forceful breaths while preserving ventilation within clinically acceptable ranges during non-forceful breaths. This selective effect suggests that the device may function as a safety mechanism without impairing effective routine ventilation during non-forceful breaths, and may help to minimize ventilator-induced lung injury, hemodynamic compromise, and hyperventilation.

During forceful breaths, the FLD reduced delivered tidal volume to 96.7 mL [95% CI, 77.6, 115.9], a volume below the typical adult anatomic dead space of approximately 150 mL and therefore insufficient to produce meaningful alveolar ventilation. This is not a shortcoming of the device but represents the intended mechanism of action. When inspiratory flow exceeds the threshold, the resulting forceful breath causes a near-complete transient occlusion, generating back-pressure that the provider perceives as haptic, auditory, and visual feedback. Accordingly, the triggered breath is not intended to provide ventilation; instead, the very low delivered volume signals the provider to reduce compression force on subsequent breaths. This mechanism may underlie the FLD’s superior performance relative to conventional pressure-relief valves, which limit pressure by venting delivered volume to atmosphere and provide negligible tactile feedback to the operator.26 According to ISO 10651-4:2023, a means of overriding the pressure limitation is required when necessary to ventilate patients with low lung compliance or high airway resistance.27 Clinical reports have documented inadequate ventilation and delayed recognition of airway obstruction with open pressure-relief valves.28, 29, 30

The effectiveness of this feedback mechanism depends on whether providers modify their technique for subsequent breaths in response to the occlusion. Because participants in this study were instructed to repeatedly trigger the device, we characterized its flow and pressure limiting properties but not the subsequent behavioral adaptation required to restore adequate ventilation. This distinction has important safety implications. When forceful breaths are intermittent and interspersed with controlled breaths, the device selectively suppresses the unsafe breaths while preserving adequate ventilation, as observed in this study (Table 4). However, if forceful breaths predominate and technique is not adjusted, repeated occlusion could result in sustained hypoventilation with hypoxia and hypercapnia. Prior studies and clinical experience suggest that providers adapt quickly to the device to provide adequate ventilation.12, 31, 32

Unlike conventional pressure-relief (pop-off) valves, which vent gas once a preset pressure is reached, the FLD responds to excessive inspiratory flow by transiently occluding the circuit and generating back pressure, which provides feedback to the provider, facilitating real-time adjustment of ventilation technique, without reliance on external monitors.

Previous human observational and manikin simulation studies have consistently demonstrated marked inter-provider variability in peak airway pressures and tidal volumes during manual ventilation, regardless of provider experience.3, 26 Moreover, well-trained emergency medical service providers frequently overventilate patients.33, 34 In our study, ventilation performance with the FLD was similar across anesthesia-trained clinicians regardless of experience level. This may reflect the clinicians’ baseline airway management expertise rather than an independent effect of the device itself. Consequently, these findings may not extend to providers with different training backgrounds, such as emergency medical technicians or prehospital personnel. Future studies should evaluate the FLD across broader provider groups in emergency and critical care settings, with attention to provider training, adaptation to ventilation technique, adequate alveolar ventilation, and patient centered outcomes. Compression force may be greater in high stress prehospital or resuscitation settings than in the controlled anesthesia environment, so such evaluations should assess whether providers adapt quickly enough to avoid sustained inadequate tidal volumes, particularly during CPR.

Integrated real-time feedback mechanisms may play a critical role in optimizing manual ventilation performance. Simulation studies using ventilation feedback devices have demonstrated significant improvements in adherence to recommended tidal volumes, with improvements exceeding 70% in some simulated settings.4, 35, 36, 37, 38 Although real-time ventilation feedback mechanisms may improve ventilation performance in simulation settings, the 2025 international Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) Consensus on Science with Treatment Recommendations concluded that evidence is insufficient to make a treatment recommendation regarding ventilation-quality feedback devices during CPR.39 Unlike many feedback systems that require visual attention to external displays and serve primarily as alerts,35 the FLD integrates tactile, visual, and auditory feedback directly into the manual ventilation process, using flow limitation as a forcing function, one of the most effective types of human-factors intervention.40 Thus, the observed reductions in peak inspiratory pressure, inspiratory airflow, and tidal volume are consistent with the FLD’s intended design to promote more controlled ventilation.

The device is currently used clinically by fire and emergency medical services departments throughout the United States. A recent qualitative study involving semi-structured interviews with emergency medical technicians, paramedics, training officers, and rescue chiefs reported favorable experiences, citing the device’s simplicity, reduced cognitive burden, and utility as both a clinical and training tool.41 Our findings extend this evidence by demonstrating the physiologic effects of an integrated feedback device in a clinical setting, helping bridge the gap between simulation-based evidence and clinical practice.

Limitations

Our study can only be interpreted within the context of its limitations. First, it was an unblinded study conducted in a controlled operating room environment with a secured airway, which may not fully reflect the dynamic and high-stress conditions encountered in prehospital or emergency settings; however, forceful breaths were used to emulate the inadvertent overventilation common in such conditions. Due to the study design and known pressure-related leaks with face masks and supraglottic airways, manual ventilation was performed through an endotracheal tube rather than in the manner typically used during critical care emergencies. Second, the intervention was evaluated over a short duration, and clinical outcomes were not assessed. Third, the participating providers were anesthesia trained clinicians and trainees whose airway management experience and skill sets differ from those of paramedics, emergency medical technicians, or emergency department personnel. In addition, we did not specifically assess provider perceptions or usability of the device, although informal feedback was favorable. Fourth, the superiority margin was inadvertently omitted from the ClinicalTrails.gov record and was subsequently corrected on July 17, 2026. This administrative correction did not affect study conduct, outcome measures, or data analysis. Finally, this was a single-center study, which may limit generalizability. Larger, multicenter studies are needed to evaluate whether the use of this device improves patient outcomes.

Conclusion

In this randomized crossover trial, the Sotair® flow limiting device reduced excessive peak inspiratory pressures, inspiratory flow, and tidal volumes during forceful manual ventilation while preserving appropriate ventilation during non-forceful breaths in endotracheally intubated patients. However, reduced tidal volumes during forceful breaths raise potential concerns about inadequate alveolar ventilation and potential hypoventilation if the technique is not corrected and the pattern persists over several breaths, particularly during CPR.
