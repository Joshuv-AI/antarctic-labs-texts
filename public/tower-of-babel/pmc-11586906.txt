Genetic Prediction Modeling in Large Cohort Studies via Boosting Targeted Loss Functions
Authors: Hannah Klinkhammer, Christian Staerk, Carlo Maj, Peter M. Krawitz, Andreas Mayr
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPolygenic risk scores (PRS) aim to predict a trait from genetic information, relying on common genetic variants with low to medium effect sizes. As genotype data are high‐dimensional in nature, it is crucial to develop methods that can be applied to large‐scale data (large n and large p). Many PRS tools aggregate univariate summary statistics from genome‐wide association studies into a single score. Recent advancements allow simultaneous modeling of variant effects from individual‐level genotype data. In this context, we introduced snpboost, an algorithm that applies statistical boosting on individual‐level genotype data to estimate PRS via multivariable regression models. By processing variants iteratively in batches, snpboost can deal with large‐scale cohort data. Having solved the technical obstacles due to data dimensionality, the methodological scope can now be broadened—focusing on key objectives for the clinical application of PRS. Similar to most methods in this context, snpboost has, so far, been restricted to quantitative and binary traits. Now, we incorporate more advanced alternatives—targeted to the particular aim and outcome. Adapting the loss function extends the snpboost framework to further data situations such as time‐to‐event and count data. Furthermore, alternative loss functions for continuous outcomes allow us to focus not only on the mean of the conditional distribution but also on other aspects that may be more helpful in the risk stratification of individual patients and can quantify prediction uncertainty, for example, median or quantile regression. This work enhances PRS fitting across multiple model classes previously unfeasible for this data type.

Introduction

Polygenic risk scores (PRS) aim to capture the individual's predisposition to various traits (diseases or clinical outcomes) influenced by genetics. Often, those traits are biologically complex diseases or phenotypes and present specific problems such as polygenicity and heterogeneity that require tailored statistical methods [1, 2]. In particular, PRS are typically estimated based on large‐scale genotype data with both large sample sizes n (hundreds of thousands of participants) and large numbers of variables p (millions of single nucleotide polymorphisms, SNPs), comprising common genetic variants with low to medium effect sizes. In contrast, single variants with a very strong signal are rare. Most traits have a limited genetic component, that is, a low signal‐to‐noise ratio [3]. Another statistical challenge is that closely located genetic variants tend to show high correlations (linkage disequilibrium, LD).

Due to the high‐dimensionality of the data, many common statistical methods to derive PRS such as LDpred [4], PRScs [5] and PRSice [6] are based on summary statistics corresponding to simple univariate association tests for each variant in genome‐wide association studies (GWAS). Additional methods such as Bayesian approaches can be applied to account for the correlation among the SNPs and to combine these univariate effects. From a statistical perspective, to build polygenic prediction models, it is desirable to use multiple regression modeling to jointly estimate multivariable‐adjusted effects for the variants, accounting directly for LD. To do so, we introduced the statistical boosting framework snpboost [7], which works on batches of variants to overcome the computational hurdle of large p and yields sparse linear prediction models while automatically selecting the most informative variants. Previous results suggest that such multivariable methods often yield higher prediction accuracy than methods based on summary statistics [7, 8].

Until now, the majority of PRS methods, including snpboost, have been limited to continuous and binary outcomes, utilizing classical linear and logistic regression, respectively. However, in biostatistical practice, outcomes of interest might not always be continuous or binary but can also represent time‐to‐event data, such as the age of onset of a disease (for an example in the context of PRS, see Li et al. [9]), or count data, such as the number of acute episodes of a chronic illness. In particular, time‐to‐event data usually contain censored observations that demand adequate modeling, while classical linear and logistic regression models do not account for this. Furthermore, outcome data might be skewed or compromised by outliers and measurement errors. This raises important issues, especially as researchers are interested in building individual prediction intervals based on the genetic predisposition. There is hence the need to go one step further in developing PRS as flexible statistical prediction models for different types of outcomes, rather than simple biomarkers constructed as sums of weighted effect alleles.

In this work, we propose using statistical boosting to fit advanced regression models on large‐scale genotype data, enabling flexible modeling of different types of outcomes. In this context, our approach allows, for the first time, the application of multiple model classes for this type of large‐scale genetic data. The modular nature of statistical boosting provides the opportunity to extend the snpboost framework based on targeted loss functions. In particular, we incorporate loss functions for continuous, binary, time‐to‐event, and count data. This allows us to effectively use the most suitable statistical model classes in the context of polygenic risk modeling for the most common types of outcome data. Furthermore, we implement quantile regression in our framework; this includes median regression as a special case, serving as a robust alternative to classical mean regression in the presence of skewed or corrupted data. As an additional feature, quantile regression facilitates the construction of individual prediction intervals to report the uncertainty of point predictions.

In the following, we will describe the snpboost framework and introduce the new loss functions which are provided in an R implementation (https://github.com/hklinkhammer/snpboost). We offer various alternative modeling choices covering accelerated failure time (AFT) models and weighted L2 boosting for time‐to‐event data, as well as Poisson and negative binomial regression for count data. While these model classes and objective functions are well‐known in biostatistics, they have generally not been feasible for handling these extreme data situations with large n and p. We investigate the performance of the proposed loss functions on simulated phenotypes based on genotype data and compare it to a simplified modeling approach by treating the outcomes as continuous or binary. Then, using high‐dimensional data from the UK Biobank [10], we apply the new modeling opportunities by building prediction models for the age of onset of asthma and gout, as well as for the number of sunburn occasions during childhood. Finally, we investigate individual prediction intervals for the body mass index (BMI) and for lipoprotein A.

Methods

For each individual, i=1,…,n we observe the phenotype yi and measure p genetic variants xi,j, for j=1,…,p. Let y=y1,…,ynT denote the observed phenotype vector and X=xi,j∈[0,2]n×p the genotype matrix with the jth column xj corresponding to the jth variant. Our aim is to minimize the expected risk E(ρ(Yη)|X) defined via a pre‐specified loss function ρ with respect to an additive predictor η.

Statistical Boosting for Huge Genotype Data

The concept of boosting emerged from machine learning [11, 12] but was later adapted to fit statistical models for potentially high‐dimensional data while incorporating automated data‐driven variable selection [13, 14].

Statistical boosting estimates the additive predictor η^ in an iterative fashion using so‐called base‐learners. In the genetic context, we currently consider simple linear models as base‐learners for each variant given by hjXj=β0+βjXj,j=1,…,p, leading to a linear predictor η=β0+Xβ. To enable the application of statistical boosting directly on huge and high‐dimensional genotype data, the snpboost algorithm incorporates an additional screening and batch‐building step [7]. To build a batch, the pbatch≪p variants showing the highest absolute Pearson correlations with the current negative gradient of the loss are selected. On this subset of variants, a maximum of mbatch boosting iterations is performed with the corresponding base‐learners. This procedure of batch building and boosting on the batch is sequentially repeated for a maximum number of bmax (possibly overlapping) batches. To prevent over‐fitting, two stopping criteria were incorporated inside and outside of these batches. To avoid over‐fitting within a batch, the largest absolute correlation between a variant outside the batch and the current negative gradient vector is saved when entering a new batch. Boosting on the current batch is then stopped, if all correlations between variants inside the batch and the current gradient are smaller than the saved correlation. Furthermore, as an “outer” stopping criterion for building new batches, simultaneously to the fitting process, the predictive risk (evaluated loss) on an independent validation set is monitored and the snpboost algorithm stops if the risk on the validation set has not decreased for two consecutive batches.

Time‐to‐Event Data

Particularly in biostatistical applications, the interest is often not only on an individual's risk for a disease but also on the time until its occurrence or its course after initial treatment. In oncology, for example, studies typically focus also on the age of onset [15] and on recurrence‐free or metastasis‐free survival times of patients after chemotherapy [16]. In genetic epidemiology, PRS derived from GWAS for disease risk (occurrence as binary outcome) are often directly plugged into multiple regression models to assess genetic contributions regarding time‐to‐event data. From a methodological and statistical perspective, optimizing PRS with respect to the time‐to‐event character of the data (e.g., the hazard rate) rather than solely focusing on the binary outcome of occurrence might be more favorable [17].

To directly estimate PRS models for time‐to‐event data via boosting, we extended the snpboost algorithm by incorporating various new loss functions suitable for survival times. This illustrates the flexibility of our framework which results from the modular nature of the boosting algorithm: new loss functions can be implemented and directly combined with the overall framework, without altering the algorithm itself. For time‐to‐event and count phenotypes, one can now easily try different loss functions and pick the most suitable one depending on the phenotype's characteristics. For time‐related outcomes, the variable of interest is the time Ti until the event occurs (for subject i), which can, for example, be the survival time if the event is death, or the age of onset if the event is the first occurrence of a disease. Furthermore, censoring at time Ci might occur due to the end of the study, competing events or further reasons. The observed time for observation i is then given by T˜i=minCi,Ti. Furthermore, the variable Δi=ITi≤Ci indicates whether the event has been observed.

Weighted L2 Boosting

From an algorithmic point of view, an efficient way to develop PRS for time‐to‐event data is to adapt the classical squared error loss (L2 loss) to account for censoring. To use the L2 loss for time‐to‐event data, we consider the logarithmic survival times yi=logTi and the corresponding logarithmic observed times y˜i=logT˜i. The complete data y1,…,yn could then be modeled using the squared error loss; however, due to censoring, we only have access to the observed incomplete data y˜1,…,y˜n. An effective way to deal with censoring is to incorporate weights [14, 18]. One popular choice is given by inverse probability censoring weights (IPC weights) defined as (1) wi=Δi1G^T˜i where G^(c) is the Kaplan–Meier estimator of the probability of censoring G(c)=P(C>c). Based on the IPC weights, the loss function for weighted L2‐boosting for time‐to‐event data is then given by (2) ρwL2(y˜,η)=1n∑i=1nwiy˜i−ηi2

Accelerated Failure Time Models

An attractive parametric alternative approach that avoids ordering and summing over all individuals (and is therefore computationally much less challenging than, for example, a Cox proportional hazard model), is based on accelerated failure time (AFT) models [19]. As an additional benefit, AFT models have a more intuitive interpretation and do not rely on assumptions like proportional hazards [20]. In these models the logarithmic observed survival times y˜i=logT˜i are modeled via (3) logT˜i=ηi+σZi where σ>0 denotes a scaling parameter and Zi is a random variable with known statistical distribution, for example, Gaussian (“AFT‐Gaussian”), logistic (“AFT‐logistic”) or extreme value distribution (“AFT‐Weibull”). The corresponding loss function for boosting corresponds to the negative log‐likelihood of this model and is given by (4) ρAFT(y˜,η,σ)=−∑i=1nΔilogfy˜i−ηiσ−log(σ)−∑i=1n1−ΔilogSy˜i−ηiσ where f(⋅) and S(⋅) are the probability density function and survival function of Zi, respectively [21]. During the boosting algorithm, the additional unknown nuisance parameter σ is initialized (e.g., σ^(0)=1) and then updated after each boosting iteration by optimizing (5) σ^(m)=minσ>0ρAFTy˜,η^(m),σ with η^(m) being the estimated predictor after m boosting steps.

Cox Proportional Hazard Model and Boosting the C‐Index

A commonly used approach to model time‐to‐event data is the Cox proportional hazard model. [22] Here, the hazard function is assumed to be the product of a baseline hazard and a factor depending on the linear predictor. The model relies on the assumption of proportional hazards. Furthermore, time‐to‐event models are often evaluated using the concordance index (C‐index) C=Pηi>ηj|Ti<Tj corresponding to the probability that a person with a shorter survival time is assigned with a higher risk score and vice versa [23]. The interpretation of the C‐index is similar to that of the area under the receiver operating curve (AUC) for binary data: C=1 indicates perfect discrimination via the risk score, while C=0.5 corresponds to a random assignment of η. In earlier works focusing on low‐dimensional data, statistical boosting has been proposed to directly optimize the linear predictor with respect to the C‐index [24, 25].

We implemented loss functions for both the Cox proportional hazard model and the C‐index in the snpboost framework. As both loss functions and corresponding gradients require ordering and summing over all n observations and predictions in each boosting step, these two approaches are computationally more demanding and limited in practice to small to medium sample sizes (with n≤50000). As our work is focused on the application of statistical boosting for genetic prediction models based on large cohort data, in this study we focus on the proposed alternatives, that is, AFT models and weighted L2 boosting. Further details and results of snpboost based on the Cox proportional hazard model and the C‐index for a reduced subsample with n=50000 individuals can be found in the Appendix A. For a recent lasso‐based approach to estimate PRS via a Cox model on UK Biobank data, we refer to Li et al. [9].

Count Data

In many biomedical applications, the response variable represents count data (e.g., number of cells or number of episodes). In the current literature on genetic risk modeling, count data are typically treated as continuous outcomes, that is, standard linear models are fitted including a PRS that was selected and optimized based on univariate effects. However, it might be more favorable to actually use a model and to optimize the PRS based on a suitable discrete distribution for the count data, particularly if the range of observed values is small or the data is skewed.

Poisson Regression

The most popular regression approach for count data is Poisson regression, which assumes that the outcome yi follows a Poisson distribution with parameter λ(X). We are then trying to predict ηi=log(λ(X)) by minimizing the negative log‐likelihood of a Poisson regression, that is, the loss function is given by (6) ρPoisson(y,η)=∑i=1n−yiηi+expηi.

In practice, when dealing with count data, the Poisson distribution is often too restrictive as it assumes that both expected value and variance are equal to λ(X).

Negative Binomial Distribution

In cases of overdispersed count data, a more flexible negative binomial regression model might be more suitable than Poisson regression [26]. The negative binomial distribution includes an additional parameter σ>0 that allows for skewness in the data. Figuratively speaking, it describes the number of unsuccessful tries needed to reach a given number of successes in a Bernoulli process. The loss function used in statistical boosting again equals the negative log‐likelihood and is given by (7) ρNB(y,η,σ)=−∑i=1nlogΓyi+σΓyi+1Γ(σ)+σlogσexpηi+σ+yilogexpηiexpηi+σ where Γ denotes the Gamma function.

Similar as in AFT models, the additional unknown nuisance parameter σ>0 is first initialized (e.g., σ^(0)=1) and then updated after each boosting iteration by minimizing (8) σ^(m)=minσ>0ρNBy,η^(m),σ with η^(m) being the estimated linear predictor after m boosting iterations.

Quantile Regression

For continuous traits, most often classical mean regression is used to construct PRS, which corresponds to optimizing the L2 loss (or a Gaussian likelihood). However, in some cases the actual interest might not be the center of the distribution but upper and lower quantiles, for example, in the context of over‐ or underweight [27]. Furthermore, for outcomes that are susceptible to measurement errors and outliers, estimation of the conditional median (corresponding to the L1 loss) can be considered as a robust alternative to the conditional mean. Quantile regression, however, can also be used to derive individual conditional prediction intervals for a continuous outcome to quantify the uncertainty of a prediction [28, 29]. Classical prediction intervals, based on standard regression, assume homoscedasticity and therefore yield equidistant interval bounds whose size does not rely on the individuals but on the overall population. To overcome this, a very recent work by Hou et al. [30] propose to calibrate prediction intervals for diverse contexts (subgroups) specified by different covariates. We go one step further, and directly model the bounds of prediction intervals based on the individual genotype information.

In our context, quantile regression aims to predict the individual quantile τ∈(0,1) conditioned on the individual's genetic information. To do so, we use the check function [31] as a loss function which is defined as (9) ρquantile(y,η)=∑i=1nτyi−ηiIηi≤yi+(1−τ)ηi−yiIηi>yi where y is the vector of observed phenotypes and η the vector of corresponding predictions for the quantile τ. To construct prediction intervals for a coverage of (1−α)⋅100%, we hence follow the general approach by Meinshausen and Ridgeway [28] and model the τ=α2 and τ=1−α2 quantiles as bounds of the individual intervals. As quantile regression yields point estimates and does not consider uncertainty in the estimated bounds, those can be refined using conformalized quantile regression [32]. To do so, on a validation set with nval individuals, the conformity scores Ei are calculated as (10) Ei=maxq^α2,i−yi,yi−q^1−α2,i where q^τ,i is the estimated quantile τ of individual i. Then, the lower and upper estimated bounds are corrected by the (1−α)1+1nvalth quantile of E1,…,Enval.

Simulation Study Based On UK Biobank Genotype Data

To assess the performance of our framework in a controlled setting, we conducted a simulation study based on UK Biobank (UKBB) genotype data [10], obtained under application number 81202. For the simulation study, we focused on the imputed genotypes of chromosome 22 and filtered for a minor allele frequency of at least 1% and a genotype rate of at least 90%, resulting in p=124302 genetic variants. By using UK Biobank genotype data from chromosome 22, we ensure to base our simulations on realistic correlation structures among the genetic variants (LD). We randomly sampled n=100000 individuals of which 50%, 20%, and 30% were assigned to training, validation and test sets, respectively. The genotype processing and sampling was conducted in PLINK 2.0 [33, 34]. In total, we sampled 100 different sets of individuals. For all phenotypes, we randomly chose 1200 influential variants, that is, ˜1% of the total number of variants. Based on simulated effect sizes β and a suitable intercept β0, the linear predictor η was defined as η=β0+Xβ. Further details on the simulation of the phenotypes as well as the choices of effect sizes β and intercepts β0 are given in the corresponding subsections. All simulations were run on a small HPC cluster using 30 CPUs with 2 GB RAM each.

Time‐To‐Event Data

Our aim was to simulate realistic time‐to‐event data for the context of PRS, for example, the age of onset of a certain disease that might not occur in every individual. Therefore, we split the model into two components. For both components, the non‐zero effect sizes βj were drawn from a Gaussian distribution with mean μ=0.02 and standard deviation s=0.02, reflecting the small effect sizes of common variants. In the first component, we simulated a binary variable, reflecting if the individual was at risk of developing the disease. While the linear predictor is positively correlated with the age‐of‐onset, it should have the opposite effect on the disease risk (i.e., a longer disease‐free survival is associated with a lower disease risk). Therefore, in the first component, we used an adapted linear predictor ηbin=β0bin−Xβ. The intercept was set to β0bin=13. The binary observations ybin were drawn from a Bernoulli distribution with probabilities pbin=expηbin1+expηbin. Then, in the second component, survival and censoring times were simulated following an AFT model with logistic distribution. Here, the intercept was set to β0=−10. The logarithmic survival times were then drawn from logtsurv=η+0.02z, where z was simulated via a logistic distribution with location parameter of 0 and scale parameter of 1. Finally, the censoring times tcens were drawn from a uniform distribution between 0 and maxtsurv. In our simulations, an individual had an observed event if the binary indicator ybin,i was 1, reflecting that the individual experienced the event, and the simulated survival time was smaller than the random censoring time. This reflects that the event already was observed, for example, the age of onset was smaller than the current age. The observed survival times are hence defined as (11) t˜i=tsurv,iifybin,i=1andtsurv,i≤tcens,itcens,iotherwise and the event indicator as (12) Δi=1ifybin,i=1andtsurv,i≤tcens,i0otherwise.

The resulting phenotypes had a mean censoring rate of 32.8% and a mean observed survival time of 19.5 years. On average, 25.8% of the individuals were modeled to not be at risk for the disease (binary component). Then, we derived PRS via snpboost using AFT loss functions with Gaussian, logistic and Weibull distributions, as well as weighted L2 and binary loss functions, respectively. The results are shown in Figure 1. The weighted L2 boosting showed the best prediction performance in terms of the C‐index, followed by the AFT models with logistic and normal distributions. The AFT model with Weibull distribution performed slightly better than modeling the disease risk as a binary outcome. Additionally, we computed Kendall's τ to assess the agreement between the rankings of model predictions and true survival times. All time‐to‐event loss functions could predict the true ranking better than the binary loss function. Lastly, the weighted L2 model showed an increased number of included variants compared with the other models which also led to longer runtimes. All models included more variants than the truly informative ones, which might be due to the high correlations of variants in the same region (LD).

Count Data

We simulated two types of count phenotypes: one based on the Poisson distribution and the other based on the negative binomial distribution. For the Poisson distributed phenotype, effect sizes β1,…,β1200 were drawn independently from a normal distribution with mean μ=0.04 and standard deviation s=0.01, reflecting again small effect sizes from common variants. Subsequently, the intercept was adaptively chosen via centering the linear predictor to ensure comparable expected values across simulation runs. Finally, observations were drawn from Poisson distributions with parameters λ=exp(η) resulting in phenotypes with an average mean of 1.45 and a median of 1. For a more skewed phenotype following a negative binomial distribution, effect sizes β1,…,β1200 were Gaussian distributed with mean μ=0.03 and standard deviation s=0.01. The intercept was again adaptively chosen and shifted by log(3). Then, observations were drawn from negative binomial distributions with parameters μ=exp(η) and σ=2 (see Equation (7)). The resulting phenotypes had an average mean of 4.97 and a median of 3. We trained and validated three PRS models on both types of count phenotypes using snpboost, employing (i) the L2 loss, (ii) the Poisson loss, and (iii) the negative binomial loss. For each model, we assessed the mean absolute error of prediction (MAEP) and the mean squared error of prediction (MSEP) on the independent test set of n=30000 individuals. Additionally, we also assessed the runtime in minutes and the number of selected SNPs in the PRS of the different models.

The results are illustrated in Figure 2 (negative binomial distributed outcome) and Figure B1 (Poisson distributed outcome). Additionally, as a benchmark reference, we calculated the MAEP and MSEP for the intercept model, using the mean of the training data as the intercept. Our results indicate that for phenotypes following Poisson and negative binomial distributions, using loss functions tailored for count data enhances the prediction performance compared to using the L2 loss, which treats the data as continuous. For Poisson distributed phenotypes, all three PRS models yield a comparable number of selected variants. Regarding the predictive accuracy, the Poisson loss and the negative binomial loss lead to very similar predictive performance. However, as expected, PRS models with the negative binomial loss lead to larger runtimes due to the extra step of optimizing the additional parameter σ. Results are similar for the phenotypes following a negative binomial distribution, but considering the MAEP it is apparent, that the negative binomial loss deals better with an overdispersed phenotype and yields more accurate predictions. In this case, the Poisson model includes considerably less SNPs than the L2, and negative binomial models.

Quantile Regression and Prediction Intervals

For quantile regression, we simulated continuous phenotypes from a linear model with Gaussian distributed noise with standard deviation snoise=2 and effect sizes drawn from a Gaussian distribution with mean μ=0.1 and standard deviation s=0.01. The intercept was set to β0=0. The resulting phenotypes had an average genetic heritability of 52.5% (standard deviation 1.6%). We then applied quantile regression via snpboost to estimate 2.5%, 5%, 10%, 90%, 95%, and 97.5% quantiles. Then, prediction intervals with varying desired coverage rates were constructed; namely 80% (using 10% and 90% quantiles as bounds), 90% (using 5% and 95% quantiles as bounds), and 95% prediction intervals (using 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles as bounds). Finally, the achieved coverage rate defined by the proportion of observed phenotype values that lie within the prediction interval was assessed on the test set consisting of n=30000 individuals.

In 100 simulated phenotypes an average coverage rate of 76.51% (standard deviation 0.29%), 86.99% (standard deviation 0.26%), and 92.80% (standard deviation 0.22%) were achieved for the 80%, 90%, and 95% prediction intervals, respectively. For all three prediction intervals, the achieved coverage is close to, but slightly below, the desired level. These results are expected since the estimated quantiles are point estimates that do not account for estimation uncertainty. However, the estimated prediction intervals can be further refined by using conformalized quantile regression [32] (see Section 2.4). These refined prediction intervals exhibit average coverage rates of 80.04% (standard deviation 0.36%), 90.00% (standard deviation 0.26%), and 94.99% (standard deviation 0.19%), respectively.

UK Biobank Application

We applied our adapted boosting approach, utilizing the new targeted loss functions, to analyze various types of outcome data from the UKBB (application number 81202). This dataset also served as the motivation for our methodological developments. The UKBB is a large‐scale database that comprises extensive phenotype and genotype data of more than 500 000 individuals. Starting in 2006, individuals aged 40 and older were enrolled and data is added and updated consecutively in follow‐up visits and questionnaires. Besides self‐reported traits through questionnaires and data from study visits, the UKBB is linked to electronic health records and death registries through the National Health Service. Additionally, blood samples of all participants were sequenced resulting in large‐scale genotype data. In our applications, we excluded individuals of self‐reported non‐European ancestry as well as related individuals. Furthermore, we restricted the genotype data to genotyped variants, and excluded both imputed variants and those on the sex chromosomes, resulting in p=604967 genetic variants.

Time‐to‐Event Data

Asthma is a highly prevalent respiratory disease influenced by various risk factors including genetic and environmental factors. Genetically, it shows a polygenic architecture and in particular a low age of onset is characteristic for a genetic predisposition [35]. In the UKBB, we identified n=56436 individuals diagnosed with asthma by using the corresponding ICD‐9 (UKBB Field 41271, codes 493, 493[0, 1, 09, 19, 9, 90, 99]) and ICD‐10 codes (UKBB Field 41270, codes J450, J451, J548, J459) as well as self‐reported cases (UKBB Field 22127). Time of diagnosis varied between the year of birth and 84 years of age, with a mean age at time of first diagnosis of 38 years. Additionally, we identified n=339208 controls not diagnosed with asthma. As a second time‐to‐event outcome variable, we considered age at first diagnosis of gout. Gout, a common disease in Western populations, may be influenced by genetic factors, particularly in cases of early onset. Recently, Li et al. [9] found an association of PRS and age at diagnosis in gout. Using again ICD‐10 codes (codes M10[0, 2‐4, 9], M100[0‐9], M102[0‐9], M103[0‐9], M104[0‐9], M109[0‐9], respectively), we found n=7409 individuals diagnosed with gout in the UKBB and n=339202 controls. Age at first diagnosis ranged from 32 years to 84 years, with a mean age of 67 years.

For both of the time‐to‐event outcomes, 50% of the individuals were assigned to the training set and 25% to the validation and test set, respectively. The AFT loss functions as well as the weighted L2 boosting were applied on the complete dataset fitting one PRS with each loss function for both phenotypes using the default values of the snpboost algorithm (batch size pbatch=1000, step length ν=0.1). Additionally, to compare the new loss functions to the most common approach in the PRS field, we fitted a “binary” PRS via snpboost using the occurrence of the disease as an outcome variable. The application of the Cox proportional hazard model and the C‐index on a reduced subsample of n=50000 individuals (also compared to all other time‐to‐event loss functions) is included in Appendix A (Figure A1).

To compare the performance of the various PRS models, we used the C‐index implemented in the function rcorr.cens from the R package Hmisc on the independent test set that was not used for deriving the PRS. The results are illustrated in Figure 3. While all fitted models except for the weighted L2 model for gout were able to detect genetic signal in both outcomes, we found the AFT models to be superior to the other loss functions with respect to the prediction performance. In comparison to the “binary” PRS, the time‐to‐event loss functions yielded sparser models with the same or superior prediction performance. Interestingly, the ranking of the AFT models differ moderately from the binary approach (τ∈(0.68−0.77)) suggesting that the time‐to‐event loss functions capture different information than the binary loss function. As expected due to the reduced performance of the weighted L2 model, the ranking according to this approach differs highly from the other four models.

We then utilized the best performing model, that is, the AFT‐Gaussian model, to stratify individuals into risk groups based on their predicted age of onset. For each outcome separately, we calculated the 10% and 90% quantiles of the combined training and validation set. These quantiles were then used as thresholds to divide all individuals into three risk groups: “bottom 10%,” “10%–90%,” “top 90%.” Figure 4 displays the resulting Kaplan–Meier curves on the independent test set. For both traits, individuals in the tails of the predicted age of onset (PRS) distribution show a decreased or increased disease‐free survival probability compared to individuals in the center of the predicted age of onset (PRS) distribution. Notably, since AFT models yield a PRS predicting the survival times, in this context, a higher PRS is associated with a longer survival time and a smaller lifetime risk. Comparing the rankings of the PRS models, 13.7% of the individuals in the test set would have been assigned to different risk groups based on the binary and the AFT‐Gaussian models for asthma and 4% of the individuals would have been differently assigned for gout.

Figure 5 displays the estimated coefficients for asthma using the different loss functions. While the models differ in the number of included variants, the selected variants are spread throughout the genome for each method. As a sensitivity analysis, we included sex and the first 10 principle components as covariates, which resulted in highly correlated effect estimates (Pearson correlation r>0.99, Figure B3). To assess the potential biological association between the variants included in the PRS models and the predicted phenotype, we performed a variant‐to‐gene mapping using genomic coordinates. Variants were mapped to colocalizing genes via bedtools if located in exons or introns, or to the closest gene within 500 KB if in an intergenic region, to include regulatory variants [36]. In fact, the selected variants might have true mechanistic associations or just be in linkage disequilibrium (LD) with truly associated variants and have no biological effect. Therefore, the selected variants, rather than detecting causal variants, act as proxies for signals in the locus, which could be biologically linked to the mapped genes. The enrichment analysis of the mapped genes using Fisher's Exact Test in Enrichr identified asthma as the top association in the GWAS catalog and showed an odds ratio of 6.34 (adjusted p‐value of 9.43E−48) [37]. This corroborates that the variants included in the PRS capture the genetic signal associated with asthma susceptibility. In line with the enrichment analysis, also the SNP with the highest effect size in all three AFT models, namely rs146597587 in chromosome 9, has been previously associated with asthma [38, 39]. Also SNP rs34210653 in chromosome 17, which is included in all models, has been linked to asthma before [40]. The corresponding figure for gout is included in Appendix B (Figure B4).

Count Data

To demonstrate the application of our boosting approach using targeted loss functions for count data, we considered the number of sunburn occasions during childhood (UKBB Field 1737). We found n=300073 individuals (randomly assigned to 50% training data, 25% validation data, and 25% test data) with a median number of 0 sunburn occasions during childhood (interquartile range (IQR) 0−2 sunburn occasions, maximum 999 sunburn occasions). We fitted three PRS models via snpboost on the training data: (1) using the L2‐loss for quantitative traits, (2) using the Poisson loss and (3) using the negative binomial distribution loss function. The validation set was used for the early stopping of the algorithm. Then, the three models were evaluated on the test set via both the mean squared error of prediction (MSEP) and the mean absolute error of prediction (MAEP). Additionally, we considered a simple intercept model with the median on the training data as the intercept.

The results are shown in Table 2. As expected, due to the additional estimation of σ in each boosting step for the negative binomial model, the fitting of the Poisson model and the L2 model is substantially faster. Even though there is a trend of improved predictions performance from the simplest intercept model toward the more complex negative binomial model, it is only a modest improvement in terms of MSEP and MAEP. This is probably due to a low genetic signal in the considered traits, poor data quality due to retrospective self‐reporting, and other influencing factors such as sun exposure and sun protection.

Quantile Regression

Finally, we applied quantile regression via snpboost on a set of n=262171 unrelated individuals from the UK Biobank (divided into training, validation and test set) to construct prediction intervals for BMI (UKBB Field 21001) and lipoprotein A (UKBB Field 30790). For 78% of the individuals in the test set (n=52551), the observed BMI was covered by individual prediction intervals between the predicted 10% and 90% quantiles that were based on genetic information solely. This could be increased to 80% coverage rate for the conformalized prediction interval. As shown in Figure 6, we found the length of the prediction interval for BMI to be positively correlated with the predicted median as well as the observed BMI. In the observed data, an increased standard deviation is present in both, lower and upper deciles of the BMI (Figure B5). Therefore, the linear increase in the length of the prediction interval indicates that the genetic predisposition might not only have an effect on the level but also on the dispersion parameters, for example, the variance.

Discussion

In this work, we address the challenges presented by large‐scale and high‐dimensional genotype data by proposing solutions that enable for the first time the application of advanced statistical modeling approaches. Specifically, we transfer biostatistical methods to this genetic setting with very large numbers of observations and predictor variables by extending the statistical boosting framework snpboost. Our approach involves the incorporation of targeted loss functions to construct PRS tailored for diverse outcome types, including time‐to‐event data, count data as well as skewed and potentially corrupted data. So far, most GWAS and PRS studies are limited to linear and logistic regression focusing on continuous and binary outcomes, respectively [41, 42]. More recent works considered modeling time‐to‐event data by using Cox proportional hazard models [17, 43, 44]. However, most of these approaches are based on univariate associations. Li et al. [9] proposed a Lasso algorithm, snpnet, to apply penalized Cox regression directly on the individual‐level genotype data. In this work, we introduce the possibility to use statistical boosting to fit different alternative time‐to‐event models, such as AFT models, on individual genotype data. These parametric models are computationally less demanding and do not rely on the proportional hazard assumption. We have shown that such models can be used to effectively stratify the cumulative lifetime risk.

For count data, our approach is able to incorporate loss functions that correspond to Poisson and negative binomial regression. While for RNA sequencing data, the use of a negative binomial regression is a standard approach [45], count data in GWAS is so far mostly modeled using classical Gaussian models. The possibility of actually considering count data models is particularly beneficial for data with limited counts and skewed distributions. This might also be of interest for transcriptome‐wide association studies (TWAS [46]), which consider the expression data of genes as an outcome regressed on genotype data.

Finally, we incorporate quantile regression in our framework as a robust alternative to mean regression models, particularly in the presence of skewed and corrupted outcome data. Quantile regression enables the construction of individual prediction intervals that are often lacking in standard PRS models. Ongoing research focused on the construction of context‐specific prediction intervals which are individualized with respect to covariates like age, sex, and ancestry [30]. Quantile regression provides the possibility to construct individual and possibly asymmetric prediction intervals based on genetic predisposition. To also correct for the uncertainty inherited from estimating the bounds of the intervals via quantile regression, the estimated bounds can be further refined via conformalized quantile regression [32]. Furthermore, quantile regression can help to detect heteroscedasticity.

Interestingly, in our application, we observe only a modest increase in prediction performance when using targeted loss functions for time‐to‐event and count data. Since we have demonstrated a superior performance of the proposed loss functions in simulated data, we hypothesize that this might be due to the nature of the analyzed UK Biobank data. As this is a large population‐based cohort, the number of controls most often greatly exceeds the number of cases, in particular for less prevalent traits. Furthermore, time‐to‐event data are usually noisier than the binary response if a disease was diagnosed or not. From a genetic point of view, we have only investigated the genetic component that can be measured via common variants, that is, we have neglected rare variants that can have potentially highly relevant effects. To improve prediction performance, it might be of interest to integrate rare variants and the derived PRS in one model as has been done in other works [47, 48]. We have also not considered any additional covariates in our prediction models, such as age, sex, or the first principal components of the genotype matrix. There are several approaches to include covariates in the final prediction model, for example, integrating PRS and covariates in a multiple regression model. While the inclusion of those covariates in the final model would most likely improve the prediction performance of our models, we have focused here on detecting the genetic signal based on the individual‐level genotype data. In snpboost, it is also possible to include covariates whose coefficients are then updated in each boosting iteration [7]. In further research, it is of interest to analyze and compare different approaches for covariate correction which could also directly consider covariate‐SNP interactions. Noteworthy, our analysis is only based on individuals with European ancestry. It is known that PRS show decreased performances in out‐of‐target populations, that is, populations of ancestries that are underrepresented in the training data [49]. There is a strong need for more diverse biobanks as well as for the development of methods that effectively include different ancestries to achieve more robust predictions across out‐of‐target populations and admixed individuals. While the proposed loss functions target specific data types, they do not explicitly aim to increase the transferability across populations of different ancestries. In particular, the PRS derived in our UK Biobank application will be biased toward European ancestry since they were only trained on this population. Further research is warranted to extend the snpboost framework by targeted loss functions that can bridge the gap of transferability.

On the methodological side, our approach inherits some limitations from the original statistical boosting framework. Boosting does not provide closed formulas for standard errors for coefficient estimates, that is, statistical inference is not directly possible. Confidence intervals can be constructed via permutation based approaches or bootstrapping, however, the computational demand is not negligible. Additionally, the algorithm includes several potential tuning parameters, including the step length, the batch size, and the stopping iteration. While the stopping iteration is tuned via the independent validation set, the step length and the batch size are typically pre‐specified and kept fixed. While in previous work [7] we found the algorithm to be quite robust with respect to varying batch sizes and step lengths, we have observed that a much larger step length is beneficial for boosting the C‐index. Lastly, we have also incorporated loss functions to derive Cox‐like models and optimize the C‐index. However, those loss functions and their gradients are computationally highly demanding and are therefore limited to small to medium sized cohorts with n≤50000 individuals. In our application, we observed a limited prediction performance in a reduced subsample.

Further research is warranted on how snpboost could provide means to identify SNPs that are associated with the variability of a trait rather than the general level. Such SNPs, which are also referred to as vQTLs [50, 51], could be indicators for gene‐environment interactions. A future approach to identify them could be to extend our framework to location‐scale models in the spirit of generalized additive models for location, scale and shape (GAMLSS [52, 53]), which directly model potential effects of the variants on the variance of a trait. Other model classes that could extend the framework are conditional transformation models [54], as well as multivariate distributional regression models, that allow modeling multiple response variables and their association [55]. However, this requires the simultaneous fitting of several linear predictors which is computationally far more expensive. Additionally, determining the optimal approach for defining different batches of variants for the multiple distribution parameters remains a challenge. Further research could also investigate not only extending the framework to new model classes but also to different types of genetic effects. Currently our framework is focused only on linear base‐learners, following the assumption of most polygenic models in the genetics field. A recent work of Heyne et al. [56] investigates the potential of non‐linear genotype effects. Hence, in future work, it could be of interest to incorporate non‐linear base‐learners to take the dominance structure of the SNPs into account, that is, to allow also for a recessive or dominant rather than an additive effect. The inclusion of additional base‐learners also represents the potential to model gene–gene and gene–environment interactions [57]. With the inclusion of those interactions, new methodological and computational challenges arise, for example, which interactions should be included to ensure biological meaningfulness and avoid computational explosion. For example, gene–environment interactions could be included based on the detection of vQTLs [58] and gene–gene interactions could be considered pathway‐specific [59]. Lastly, snpboost is based on individual‐level genotype data. Despite those becoming increasingly available to the scientific community, many PRS approaches are based on summary statistics from genome‐wide association studies. From a data protection view, summary statistics are generally easier to share compared to individual‐level data and can be combined in a meta‐analysis. To overcome this limitation of individual‐level based approaches, federated learning can be deployed in further research to fit individual‐level based PRS without violation of data sharing agreements.

In summary, we extended the boosting framework for huge genetic data by incorporating various loss functions enabling targeted PRS modeling. This is in the spirit of developing PRS as actual statistical prediction models, rather than summing up and weighting effect alleles as combined biomarkers. In this context, statistical boosting can be applied to individual‐level data to derive outcome‐specific prediction models, which may have the potential to improve the clinical usability of PRS in the future. To the best of our knowledge, many of the proposed model classes are currently not feasible to estimate with these kinds of data. For instance, we introduce the first algorithm to fit quantile regression for PRS on large‐scale individual‐level genotype data, enabling the construction of prediction intervals. Additionally, we demonstrate the modular nature of statistical boosting, which facilitates further extensions of the framework.
