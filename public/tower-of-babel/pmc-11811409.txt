Detection of iridocorneal angle changes after uncomplicated phacoemulsification using automated gonioscopy imaging
Authors: Miguel Santos, Afonso Lima-Cabrita, Rafael Barão, Luís Abegão-Pinto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PURPOSE:Phacoemulsification cataract surgery (PCS) is known to change anterior chamber morphology, widening the iridocorneal angle (ICA) of most patients. This study aims to determine whether automated gonioscopy imaging (AGI) with GS-1® (NIDEK CO., Japan) can detect changes in ICA morphology and pigmentation after PCS.METHODS:A prospective, observational study including patients who underwent PCS at Hospital Santa Maria, Lisbon, from March to October 2022. AGI was done at the preoperative visit and 6-weeks postoperative. The images were analyzed using NAVIS-EX Software (NIDEK CO., Japan) by a masked observer. Each eye was divided into four quadrants and their morphology and pigmentation was graded according to the Shaffer and modified Scheie classifications, respectively.RESULTS:Twenty-two eyes from 21 patients (62% female) were included, with a mean age of 74.5 ± 7.9 years. Baseline AGI identified 7 (33%) patients with angle closure. PCS led to all patients achieving open angle status and lower preoperative Shaffer grades achieved greater postoperative improvement. Statistically significant ICA widening was found in the superior (P = 0.004), inferior (P = 0.008), and temporal (P = 0.023) quadrants, but not the nasal quadrant (P = 0.21). Similarly, a statistically significant pigmentation increase was found in the superior (P = 0.008), inferior (P = 0.002), and temporal (P = 0.016) quadrants and less pigmented baseline quadrants showed a greater gain. The most significant pigmentation gain was in the inferior quadrant.CONCLUSION:Unlike other imaging modalities, AGI with GS-1 can detect changes in ICA morphology and pigmentation after routine cataract surgery, mainly in the superior, inferior, and temporal quadrants.

INTRODUCTION

Evaluation of the iridocorneal angle (ICA) using gonioscopy is an essential part of the evaluation of glaucoma patients. Adequate visualization of ICA structures is crucial to classify glaucoma as open angle or as angle closure, which determine differing treatment algorithms.[1] In addition, the documentation of ICA pigmentation or the presence of pathologic findings such as neovascularization, angle recession, or peripheral anterior synechiae (PAS) can also significantly alter patient management and follow-up.[2]

In recent years, attempts to facilitate ICA evaluation have led to the development of imaging techniques such as Automated Gonioscopy Imaging (AGI) that allow for easy ICA visualization, documentation, and analysis. The GS-1 Gonioscope (NIDEK Co., Gamagori, Japan) is an AGI device that uses a 16-facet optical contact mirror prism to quickly capture 16 true color photographs of contiguous sections of the ICA in <30 s.[3] The device can automatically adjust focus and takes several pictures per quadrant with different focal lengths.[4] The captured images are then promptly available to the clinician, who can use built-in software to reconstruct linear or circular stitching images that result in a full 360° composite image of the ICA [Figure 1].[4] Reasonable agreement between GS-1 AGI and manual indirect gonioscopy (MIG) has been shown in terms of angle status (i.e., open or closed) and trabecular pigmentation.[56] Phacoemulsification cataract surgery (PCS) is known to change anterior chamber morphology, leading to ICA widening (which can occur even in open-angle subjects), and is the reason why PCS can be used to treat primary angle closure.[17] Trabecular pigmentation, which is usually more marked in the inferior sector (due to gravity and it being the more functionally active sector),[8] can be used to infer functional changes in the ICA. It is expected that by increasing trabecular outflow PCS will increase ICA pigmentation. This change in pigmentation cannot be detected using imaging methods such as anterior segment optical coherence tomography or ultrasonic biomicroscopy. It thus follows that imaging techniques aimed at studying ICA anatomy should be able to detect these structural and functional changes after PCS. With the advent of minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS), which usually involves ICA manipulation, the potential applications of a device that can detect and document postoperative ICA changes are substantial.

This study aims to determine whether AGI with GS-1 can detect ICA widening and increased pigmentation after uncomplicated PCS.

METHODS

A prospective, observational study was undertaken. Consecutive patients who were to undergo routine PCS at a tertiary teaching hospital from March to October 2022 were included. Reflecting a real-world setting, all consenting patients were included, with no exclusions due to cataract grade or other anterior or posterior segment anatomic factors.

AGI with GS-1 was performed at the preoperative visit and at the 6-week postoperative visit by the same two trained technicians, in scotopic lighting conditions. The standard GS-1 exam protocol was adapted to capture 15 frames in each of the 16 mirrors to increase overall image quality, as per manufacturer instructions. MIG was not performed in any patient.

The images were then analyzed by a masked observer using NAVIS EX Software (NIDEK CO., Japan). This observer had no knowledge of which patient was being analyzed or whether the exam was executed pre- or postoperative. For the purposes of this analysis, each eye was divided into superior, inferior, temporal, and nasal quadrants.

The primary outcome was determined to be the AGI detection of ICA widening after PCS. ICA morphology was graded for each quadrant of all pre- and postoperative eyes according to the Shaffer classification (0–4).[9] Angle closure was defined according to the ISGEO classification as >180° of iridotrabecular contact (i.e., two quadrants graded as Shaffer 2 or less).[10]

The secondary outcome was the AGI detection of increased ICA pigmentation after PCS. Each quadrant of all pre- and postoperative eyes was graded according to a modified Scheie (mScheie) classification where Scheie 0/I = mScheie 0, Scheie II = mScheie 1, and Scheie III/IV = mScheie 2.[6]

The study protocol was submitted to the local ethics committee and informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Prism GraphPad® version 8 was used for statistical analysis. Wilcoxon’s matched-pairs signed rank test was used to access statistically significant differences between the pre- and postoperative data collected. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Twenty-two eyes from 21 patients (62% females) with a mean age of 74.5 ± 7.9 years were analyzed. Two eyes from two patients were excluded from the analysis, one due to intraoperative complications (posterior capsule rupture) and one due to inability of the patient to cooperate with AGI in the postoperative examination.

One patient had glaucoma in the studied eye. The range of implanted intraocular lens powers was 10.50D – 25.00D. Mean axial length and anterior chamber depth were 23.12 ± 1.16 mm and 3.16 ± 0.21 mm, respectively. Mean intraocular pressure was 17.2 ± 4.3 in preoperative and 15.1 ± 2.9 mmHg in postoperative visits, a change which was not statistically significant (P = 0.07).

Baseline AGI identified seven (33%) patients with angle closure. PCS led to angle opening, as all patients achieved open angle status. An increase in mean Shaffer grade was observed in all quadrants. As expected, this ICA opening was uneven, with lower preoperative Shaffer grades achieving greater postoperative improvement. Angle widening by 1 Shaffer grade was observed in 47% of quadrants initially graded Shaffer 3 while this was observed in 70% of those graded Shaffer ≤2 preoperatively (with 30% of these widening by 2 or more grades). PAS were observed in 4 quadrants preoperatively (mainly superiorly) with only 1 quadrant maintaining PAS after surgery.

An increase in mean ICA pigmentation was also observed in all quadrants. Descriptive statistics, including mean and standard deviation, for the pre- and postoperative evaluation of ICA morphology and pigmentation can be seen in Figures 2 and 3, respectively.

When the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed–rank test was used to evaluate the effects of PCS on the ICA morphology of each quadrant, a statistically significant angle opening was found in the superior (P = 0.004), inferior (P = 0.008), and temporal (P = 0.023) quadrants. However, no difference in ICA morphology was found in the nasal quadrant (P = 0.21). PCS allowed the gonio identification of at least one more ICA structure (i.e., an angle opening change of at least 1 Shaffer grade) in 66% of all quadrants included in this analysis. An example of this is shown in Figure 4. Only 1 quadrant in one patient (1.1%) was graded as exhibiting ICA narrowing after PCS. Similarly, when analyzing postoperative changes in ICA pigmentation, the Wilcoxon test yielded a statistically significant increase in pigment density in the superior (P = 0.008), inferior (P = 0.002), and temporal (P = 0.016) quadrants, but not in the nasal quadrant (P = 0.09). Forty-two percent of all baseline quadrants with low pigmentation scores (i.e., graded preoperative as <2 mScheie) showed a postoperative increase in pigment density of 1 or more mScheie points, with the most significant pigmentation gain in the inferior quadrant. DISCUSSION

Reports by Teixeira et al. and Correia Barão et al., have validated the use of GS-1 AGI for imaging the ICA and determined there was reasonable agreement between GS-1 and MIG concerning angle morphology status (i.e., Open vs. angle-closure).[25] Matsuo et al. documented the ability of GS-1 to detect ICA pigmentation, also with reasonable agreement with MIG.[6] Although AGI does not attempt to substitute dynamic MGI, the gold-standard for ICA evaluation, the global increase in the use of MIGS procedures has created the need for documentation of postsurgical angle changes. This study attempted to determine whether the GS-1 can, in a clinical setting, detect ICA changes in morphology and pigmentation after phacoemulsification.

We have demonstrated that AGI can detect the expected primary outcome of ICA opening after phacoemulsification as all patients in this study achieved open-angle status as measured via this method. Furthermore, statistically significant changes in objective angle grading were observed in three of the four quadrants studied: the superior, inferior, and temporal quadrants. We have also demonstrated that AGI can detect the expected increase in ICA pigmentation after phacoemulsification with statistically significant changes in objective angle pigmentation observed in three of the four quadrants studied (superior, inferior, and temporal). Furthermore, this gain in pigmentation was greatest in the inferior sector, meaning that the results are congruent with the expected changes in ICA physiology. We are however aware that PCS fluidics is a unique event in anterior chamber physiology which could lead to different patterns of pigment deposition, especially in the short term, as was observed here.

Despite not obtaining statistically significant results in the nasal quadrant for both ICA widening and pigmentation, a mean increase in both these parameters was nonetheless detected. The available literature suggests that the angle width in the nasal quadrant does indeed increase after phacoemulsification.[11] We cannot at this time determine with certainty the cause for this statistical insignificance though we hypothesize that suboptimal patient head positioning or device-related hardware or software factors could have been at play. It must be noted that MIGS are usually executed in the nasal quadrant, thus the suboptimal results obtained in this study in the nasal quadrant impose the need for future studies directed at the detection of ICA changes after cataract surgery combined with MIGS. In any case, when planning MIGS, careful preoperative gonioscopic evaluation is warranted to ensure the nasal ICA quadrant is sufficiently open. This is particularly important in clinical practice as it may avoid device placement in narrower angles than assumed when the anterior chamber is filled with viscoelastic intraoperatively.

One patient was excluded from the study due to intraoperative complications, namely posterior capsule rupture. It is important to note that some degree of mobility and patience is required by the patient to be able to fully cooperate in the execution of the AGI examination and one further patient that had been recruited was excluded due to inability to cooperate with the exam.

This study has several limitations. One is the short-term postoperative follow-up. A second postoperative evaluation at 3–6 months would have permitted corroboration of the results as well as the determination of whether ICA pigmentation remains increased long-term after PCS or if it returns to baseline levels. Another limitation of this study was that Lens Opacity Classification System III grading for the cataract was not recorded, although it must be stated that the only criteria for surgery was a visually significant cataract and no clear-lens extractions due to angle closure were performed. Furthermore, limiting this study is the fact that MIG was not done systematically as the objective of this paper was to determine if GS-1 could detect any changes in angle anatomy and previous studies by our group (i.e., Correia Barão et al., Teixeira et al.) and others (Matsuo et al.) had already established the agreement between AGI with GS-1 and MIG. The subjective nature of pigment evaluation employed here could also be optimized in future studies using imaging software capable of a quantitative pigment analysis. The small sample size is also a limitation although we were constrained by the prospective nature of the study.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrates that unlike other imaging modalities, AGI with GS-1 can detect anatomical and functional ICA changes after routine cataract surgery, namely angle widening and increased pigmentation in the superior, inferior, and temporal quadrants. In the age of MIGS, AGI could become a crucial tool for postoperative follow-up.
