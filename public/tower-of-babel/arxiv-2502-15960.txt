A new proof of Chen’s theorem for Markoff graphs Thanks: This research is supported by NSF grant 2336000.
Daniel E. Martin

Abstract
. In 2021, Chen proved a congruence for the degree of a certain map on the space of covers of elliptic curves. He concluded as a corollary that the size of any connected component of the Markoff mod p p graph is divisible by p p . In combination with the work of Bourgain, Gamburd, and Sarnak, Chen’s result proves a conjecture of Baragar for all but finitely many primes: the Markoff mod p p graph is connected. In this note, we provide an alternative proof for the Markoff corollary of Chen’s theorem.

Primary: 11D25. Secondary: 05C25.

1.
Introduction

Integer solutions to the equation

x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}=3x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}

(1.1)

are known as Markoff triples. Given a prime number
p
, every Markoff triple reduces to a solution
\text{mod}\,p
, but it is not clear if every solution
\text{mod}\,p
lifts to a solution in
\mathbb{Z}
. In 1991, Baragar conjectured this to be true
[
1
]
.

Fixing
x_{2}
and
x_{3}
makes (
1.1
) a quadratic equation in
x_{1}
. The sum of the two solutions to this quadratic equation is
3x_{2}x_{3}
. In other words, the map

(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\mapsto(3x_{2}x_{3}-x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}),

(1.2)

which is called a Vieta involution, preserves Markoff triples. The second- and third-coordinate Vieta involutions are defined analogously. The Markoff graph is defined to have one vertex for each nonzero triple (in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{3}
for our purpose, though any ring works) that solves (
1.1
), with an edge connecting two vertices if one triple is obtained from the other via a Vieta involution. Markoff proved that the graph obtained from integer solutions is a tree
[
8
]
. In particular, it is connected, which means Baragar’s conjecture can be restated as follows. We use
\mathcal{G}_{p}
to denote the Markoff graph over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

Conjecture 1.1

(Baragar
[
1
]
)
.

\mathcal{G}_{p}
is connected for any prime
p
.

Significant progress toward resolving this conjecture was made in 2016 by Bourgain, Gamburd, and Sarnak, who proved the following.

Theorem 1.2

(Bourgain–Gamburd–Sarnak
[
2
,
3
]
)
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. For sufficiently large
p
, there is a connected component
\mathcal{C}_{p}
of
\mathcal{G}_{p}
for which

|\mathcal{G}_{p}\backslash\mathcal{C}_{p}|<p^{\varepsilon}
. Furthermore, the set of primes for which
\mathcal{G}_{p}
is connected has natural density 1.

Then in 2021, a result of Chen provided the missing piece.

Theorem 1.3

(Special case/corollary of Chen
[
5
]
)
.

The number of vertices in a connected component of
\mathcal{G}_{p}
is divisible by
p
. In particular, Baragar’s conjecture holds for all but finitely many primes.

Using notation from Theorem
1.2
, if
\mathcal{G}_{p}
has a connected component disjoint from
\mathcal{C}_{p}
, call it
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{p}
, then we have
|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{p}|<p^{\varepsilon}
for some
\varepsilon
provided
p
large enough (depending on
\varepsilon
). On the other hand, Theorem
1.3
says
|\mathcal{C}_{p}^{\prime}|\geq p
. So setting
\varepsilon=1
provides a contradiction for large
p
. By making the bounds in
[
2
]
effective, and by incorporating some new arguments involving so-called maximal divisors, Eddy, Fuchs, Litman, Tripeny, and the author found that
p>3.489\cdot 10^{392}
suffices for Theorem
1.2
to hold with
\varepsilon=1

[
7
]
. (The bound is too large for a computer to fully verify Baragar’s conjecture. Data on connectivity for much smaller primes can be found in
[
6
]
and
[
4
]
.)

Our aim is to provide a second proof of Theorem
1.3
.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

Proof.

Fix a prime
p>3
. To each nonzero
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{3}
that solves (
1.1
), we associate a new triple
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})
defined by

\mathbf{y}=\begin{cases}\left(\tfrac{x_{1}}{3x_{2}x_{3}},\tfrac{x_{2}}{3x_{1}x_{3}},\tfrac{x_{3}}{3x_{1}x_{2}}\right)&x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\neq 0\\
\left(0,\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}\right)&x_{1}=0\\
\left(\tfrac{1}{2},0,\tfrac{1}{2}\right)&x_{2}=0\\
\left(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2},0\right)&x_{3}=0.\end{cases}

(2.1)

(As Dylan Thurston has pointed out, the case
x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\neq 0
above is known as Penner coordinates, first introduced by Robert Penner in
[
9
]
.) By scaling either side of (
1.1
) by
\tfrac{1}{3x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}}
when
x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\neq 0
, we see that

y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{3}=1

(2.2)

for any
\mathbf{y}
associated to a nonzero Markoff triple.

Now, consider two triples
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},x_{3}^{\prime})
that are connected by an edge in
\mathcal{G}_{p}
from, say, the first-coordinate Vieta involution in (
1.2
). In particular,
x_{2}^{\prime}=x_{2}
and
x_{3}^{\prime}=x_{3}
. If
x_{2}x_{3}\neq 0
, we see from the first two cases of (
2.1
) that
y_{1}=\tfrac{x_{1}}{3x_{2}x_{3}}
regardless of whether
x_{1}=0
. Also
y_{1}^{\prime}=\tfrac{x_{1}^{\prime}}{x_{2}x_{3}}
, so (
1.2
) gives

y_{1}+y_{1}^{\prime}=\frac{x_{1}}{3x_{2}x_{3}}+\frac{x_{1}^{\prime}}{3x_{2}x_{3}}=\frac{x_{1}}{3x_{2}x_{3}}+\frac{3x_{2}x_{3}-x_{1}}{3x_{2}x_{3}}=1.

(2.3)

Similarly (and still assuming
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
are connected by the first-coordinate Vieta involution) if
x_{2}=x_{2}^{\prime}=0
we see from (
2.1
) that

y_{1}+y_{1}^{\prime}=\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}=1.

(2.4)

Of course the same holds if
x_{3}=0
. In any case,
y_{1}+y_{1}^{\prime}=1
. Thus if
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, then
y_{1}+y_{1}^{\prime}
counts half the number of vertices connected by this particular edge. Similarly, if
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
then
y_{1}=y_{1}^{\prime}
, so (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) show that
y_{1}=\tfrac{1}{2}
; again half the number of vertices connected by the edge. We conclude that if
\mathcal{C}
is a subgraph of
\mathcal{G}_{p}
that contains either both or neither of any two (not necessarily distinct) vertices connected by a first-coordinate Vieta involution, we have

\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{C}}y_{1}=\tfrac{1}{2}|\mathcal{C}|,

(2.5)

where
|\mathcal{C}|
is reduced
\text{mod}\,p
.

Now suppose
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{G}_{p}
is a connected component. Then
\mathcal{C}
contains all three neighboring vertices for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{C}
, so (
2.5
) holds, and it holds just as well for the second or third coordinate. Thus

\displaystyle|\mathcal{C}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{C}}1=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{i=1}^{3}y_{i}\hskip 20.0pt\text{by (\ref{eq:relate1})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{C}}y_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\tfrac{1}{2}|\mathcal{C}|\hskip 20.0pt\text{by (\ref{eq:relate2})}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{3}{2}|\mathcal{C}|.

Comparing either end, we see that
\tfrac{1}{2}|\mathcal{C}|\equiv 0\,\text{mod}\,p
, which completes the proof.∎
