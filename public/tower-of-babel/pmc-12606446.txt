Perspectives on Clinical Biomarkers Based on the Pathophysiology of Arrhythmias
Authors: Tomoya Hara, Masataka Sata
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTArrhythmias are diagnosed using electrocardiograms (ECGs). Particularly for paroxysmal arrhythmias, an ECG recorded during an episode is essential for definitive diagnosis. Meanwhile, biomarkers assessable through blood tests do not directly diagnose arrhythmias, but are considered useful for evaluating the prevalence of paroxysmal arrhythmias, predicting their onset, assessing severity, and evaluating the risk of complications. While most biomarker research in the arrhythmia field focuses on atrial fibrillation, several studies have also reported on the prediction of ventricular arrhythmias and sudden death. This paper defines biomarkers as substances measurable and evaluable through blood tests and primarily discusses their relationship with atrial fibrillation and its complications.

Introduction

Diagnosis of arrhythmia is made by electrocardiogram. In particular, for paroxysmal arrhythmia, an electrocardiogram during an attack is essential for a definitive diagnosis [1, 2, 3]. In contrast, biomarkers that can be evaluated by blood tests do not directly diagnose arrhythmia, but they are thought to be useful for predicting the prevalence and onset of paroxysmal arrhythmia, assessing the severity, and assessing the risk of complications [4, 5]. While most biomarker research in the field of arrhythmia focuses on atrial fibrillation, several studies have also been reported on predicting ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death [6, 7]. In this article, we define biomarkers as substances that can be measured and evaluated by blood tests, and we will mainly discuss their relationship with atrial fibrillation and its complications.

Overview of Biomarkers in Atrial Fibrillation

Biomarkers Causing Atrial Fibrillation

Atrial fibrillation is an arrhythmia disease with a large population and is of great clinical importance [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. In general, atrial remodeling is recognized as a key mechanism in the progression of atrial fibrillation [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Atrial remodeling progresses due to various risk factors such as aging, hypertension, obesity, alcohol consumption, and sleep apnea syndrome [20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. It can be divided into electrical remodeling caused by changes in ion channel expression and function, and structural remodeling involving atrial myocardial fibrosis and loss of myocardial cells [25]. In either case, it leads to atrial conduction disturbances and becomes a substrate for the onset of atrial fibrillation or structural remodeling due to loss of cardiomyocytes. In either case, it leads to conduction disturbances in the atrium, becoming a substrate that triggers atrial fibrillation [26].

If substances released from the atrium during atrial remodeling can be detected in the blood, they could serve as biomarkers for atrial fibrillation. Additionally, substances released from the heart due to underlying cardiac diseases complicating atrial fibrillation, or substances released from other organs due to systemic underlying diseases, can also be measured as biomarkers. These are considered biomarkers for the cause of atrial fibrillation. They can be detected independently of atrial fibrillation episodes or even before the onset of atrial fibrillation, and are thought to increase as atrial remodeling progresses. Furthermore, in atrial remodeling, it is widely known that atrial fibrillation episodes themselves promote atrial remodeling [27]. Therefore, it is assumed that repeated atrial fibrillation episodes lead to the progression of atrial remodeling and an increase in the blood concentration of biomarkers (Figure 1, left side). These are biomarkers reflecting the cause of atrial fibrillation. They can be used for predicting the onset of atrial fibrillation and are also thought to reflect the progression of atrial fibrillation itself.

Overview of atrial fibrillation and biomarkers. The overall picture to consider when examining biomarkers in atrial fibrillation. Broadly categorized in relation to the pathophysiology of atrial fibrillation, these factors fall into two main groups: Those that cause or trigger atrial fibrillation, and those that result from atrial fibrillation. Representative examples of the former include markers reflecting inflammation, such as inflammatory cytokines. Representative examples of the latter are markers of heart failure and myocardial injury. Nucleic acids released into the extracellular space and extracellular vesicles mediate complex intercellular/inter‐tissue communication and are deeply involved in the pathophysiology of atrial fibrillation.

Biomarkers Resulting From Atrial Fibrillation

Atrial fibrillation involves a high atrial excitation rate of 400–600 beats per minute, leading to greater changes in atrial myocardial cells compared to other arrhythmias. Furthermore, atrial fibrillation differs from other tachyarrhythmias (such as supraventricular tachycardia or ventricular tachycardia) in that it can progress from paroxysmal to persistent forms, with longer duration as the disease advances. Substances released from the heart into the bloodstream due to this electrical excitation of the atrial myocardium, or substances released from other organs into the bloodstream under the pathophysiology of atrial fibrillation, can be measured as biomarkers of atrial fibrillation. Although these biomarkers result from atrial fibrillation episodes, their clearance (half‐life in blood) allows detection even outside episodes. Repeated episodes are expected to increase blood concentrations (Figure 1, right side). Therefore, they are not only potentially useful for detecting so‐called asymptomatic atrial fibrillation but also are thought to be capable of predicting the progression of atrial fibrillation.

Concerns When Assessing and Interpreting Atrial Fibrillation Biomarkers

When clinical studies show fluctuations in the blood levels of certain biomarkers in patients with atrial fibrillation, the question arises as to whether they are the cause or the result of atrial fibrillation. However, unless the mechanism of biomarker production is elucidated, it is not possible to clearly determine which factor is more important. In either case, however, it is highly likely that the prevalence and progression of atrial fibrillation can be predicted with some degree of accuracy.

When considering biomarkers for atrial fibrillation, it is important to note that atrial fibrillation is not merely a simple arrhythmia. It is a disease that develops on the basis of aging and other underlying conditions, leading to systemic complications. In other words, atrial fibrillation can be considered the electrical phenotype in the atria of systemic changes primarily rooted in aging. Therefore, when biomarker changes are observed in atrial fibrillation patients, it is crucial to determine whether they are directly related to atrial fibrillation itself or reflect underlying factors such as aging. Biomarker research in atrial fibrillation must therefore evaluate these changes while accounting for these background factors as much as possible. Furthermore, atrial fibrillation itself originates in the localized site of the atrium. It is important to interpret that a dissociation may occur between localized changes and biomarkers measured in peripheral blood.

Biomarkers that Reflect Inflammation in the Atrial Cardiomyocyte

Pathophysiology of Atrial Myocardial Inflammation

It is widely known that inflammation of the atrial myocardium is one of the underlying mechanisms for the onset of atrial fibrillation [28]. From the perspective of acute inflammation, reports indicate a high incidence of atrial fibrillation following open‐heart surgery or lung surgery. Additionally, studies suggest that elevated levels of C‐reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) in the blood are associated with the onset of atrial fibrillation. Furthermore, reports indicate that anti‐inflammatory treatment suppresses such postoperative onset of atrial fibrillation. Alternatively, conditions like metabolic syndrome and obesity induce systemic chronic microinflammation originating from adipose tissue inflammation. A link between this microinflammation and atrial fibrillation is also being considered [29]. In atrial fibrillation, research has particularly focused on the association with epicardial fat. Inflammatory responses originating from epicardial fat are thought to induce myocardial fibrosis, thereby triggering atrial fibrillation [30]. Furthermore, pressure overload due to hypertension, volume overload in heart failure, and decreased intrathoracic pressure caused by obstructive sleep apnea all constitute mechanical stimulation to the atrial myocardium. This mechanical stress induces macrophage migration into the atrial myocardium, leading to inflammation. These findings support the perspective that inflammation is a cause of atrial fibrillation and reinforce the concept that, within the mechanism of atrial remodeling, inflammation subsequently induces fibrosis and structural remodeling.

Furthermore, there is a theory that atrial fibrillation itself induces inflammation. Clinical studies have shown that blood samples from atrial fibrillation patients exhibit higher levels of CRP and IL‐6 compared to samples taken during sinus rhythm. Additionally, animal experiments have demonstrated that high‐frequency atrial pacing in rats induces TNF‐α expression in the liver. These findings suggest that atrial fibrillation itself induces inflammation. Regarding the mechanism by which atrial fibrillation directly induces inflammation, recent reports indicate that extracellularly released DNA is involved [31]. Culturing atrial myocytes and applying high‐frequency electrical stimulation revealed a time‐dependent increase in DNA concentration in the culture medium, confirming that cardiomyocytes release DNA into the extracellular space. This extracellularly released free DNA is termed cell‐free DNA (cfDNA). When macrophages internalize mitochondrial‐derived unmethylated DNA, it is recognized and processed by TLR9 within endosomes, inducing the expression of IL‐6 and IL‐1β. High‐frequency excitation of atrial myocytes puts mitochondria into an overload state. Dysfunctional mitochondria that cannot be completely processed by intracellular mitophagy are released outside the cell as extracellular vesicles [32, 33]. It is believed that the cfDNA contained within these extracellular vesicles, particularly that derived from mitochondria, induces inflammation (Figure 2).

Mechanism by which atrial fibrillation itself induces inflammation. In the innate immune system of mammals, various pattern recognition receptors recognize foreign invaders such as bacteria and viruses, triggering an immune response. While most of our host genomic DNA is methylated, genomes derived from bacteria and viruses are largely unmethylated. The Toll‐like receptor (TLR9), expressed on macrophage endosomes, recognizes this unmethylated DNA as a foreign invader, transmits signals downstream, and triggers an inflammatory response. In atrial fibrillation, the atrial muscle undergoes abnormally high‐frequency excitation at 400–600 times per minute, causing immense mitochondrial stress within cardiomyocytes. Dysfunctional mitochondria are processed via mechanisms like mitophagy. However, when this processing capacity is exceeded, they are released outside the cell as exophores. Mitochondria are organelles that originated when ancient aerobic bacteria, which utilized oxygen to produce energy, were incorporated into the ancestral eukaryotic cell and established a symbiotic relationship. Consequently, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is unmethylated, similar to bacterial or viral DNA, and stimulates TLR9, triggering an inflammatory response. These pathways are thought to be one mechanism by which atrial fibrillation itself induces myocardial inflammation. Common Inflammatory Cytokines

Thus, inflammation and atrial fibrillation have a bidirectional relationship, and inflammation‐related biomarkers are elevated in patients with atrial fibrillation. A representative example is serum high‐sensitivity CRP (hsCRP), a widely used inflammation marker. Many studies have shown elevated CRP levels in patients with atrial fibrillation. Furthermore, elevated CRP has been associated with recurrence after electrical cardioversion and recurrence after catheter ablation [34]. Other inflammation‐related markers reported to be associated with atrial fibrillation include IL‐6 and free immunoglobulin light chains [35].

Cell‐Free DNA

Furthermore, circulating Cell‐Free DNA (cfDNA), particularly mitochondrial DNA, is useful as a biomarker for the onset of atrial fibrillation and the frequency of progression to persistent atrial fibrillation. It has also been reported that cfDNA levels are elevated in patients with a history of cerebral infarction, raising expectations for its potential as a marker for complications of atrial fibrillation [36].

Biomarkers of Heart Failure and Myocardial Damage in Atrial Fibrillation

Brain Natriuretic Peptides

In atrial fibrillation, high‐frequency atrial excitation places a load on the atrial myocardium. Additionally, heart failure may develop due to factors such as tachyarrhythmic atrial fibrillation or loss of atrial contractility, which can also place a load on the ventricular myocardium. Consequently, brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) and NT‐proBNP may be elevated in atrial fibrillation, and their usefulness as biomarkers for atrial fibrillation has been reported [37]. BNP is a marker of heart failure and has low specificity for atrial fibrillation alone. However, when BNP is elevated and other factors that could explain this are ruled out, the possibility of paroxysmal arrhythmia disorders, including atrial fibrillation, should be considered.

Atrial Natriuretic Peptides

In patients without cardiac disease, the tissue distribution of BNP has been reported to be 23% atrial and 77% ventricular. Therefore, in atrial fibrillation, BNP is secreted from the atria due to the stress of high‐frequency atrial activation, and BNP is secreted from the ventricles due to the stress of the ventricles. On the other hand, the tissue distribution of ANP is more atrial‐specific, with 97% atrial and 3% ventricular. While ANP elevation is observed in atrial fibrillation patients, it is known that as atrial fibrillation persists and atrial remodeling progresses, ANP secretion decreases due to mechanisms such as atrial myocyte apoptosis. Utilizing this, studies have shown that the ANP/BNP ratio can predict the progression of atrial remodeling and the potential for reverse remodeling following catheter ablation therapy [38].

Cardiac Troponin

It has been reported that cardiac troponin, a marker reflecting myocardial damage, is elevated in patients with atrial fibrillation [39]. In this case, the troponin elevation is not thought to result from the release of troponin from necrotic cells, as seen with typical myocardial necrosis markers. Instead, mechanisms such as leakage from the cytoplasm due to increased cell membrane permeability following intracellular breakdown, or detection of troponin contained within extracellular vesicles released from the myocardium, are considered likely [40].

Other Biomarkers in Atrial Fibrillation

Extracellular Nucleic Acids as Biomarkers for Atrial Fibrillation

Extracellular nucleic acids are biomarkers that have attracted attention in recent years. The aforementioned cfDNA and microRNAs (miRNAs) have been the main focus of research. miRNAs are a type of non‐coding RNA that is not translated into amino acids and are short, consisting of approximately 19–25 bases. miRNAs bind to target messenger RNAs (mRNAs) and suppress their expression by inhibiting mRNA translation or causing mRNA degradation [41, 42]. It has been reported that the regulation of the expression levels of target molecules through changes in intracellular miRNA expression is involved in various pathological conditions in many diseases, and there have also been many reports of the involvement of miRNA in the progression of atrial remodeling. For example, the involvement of miR‐1 [43] and miR‐133 [44], which are highly expressed in cardiac myocytes and contribute to the regulation of the expression of many genes, including ion channels, and miR‐27b [45], which increases with a high‐fat diet and suppresses the expression of connexin 40, has been reported. However, the types of miRNAs identified as biomarkers for atrial fibrillation in many studies vary, and this is thought to be due to differences in background factors, racial differences, RNA extraction methods, etc.

Extracellular Vesicles in Atrial Fibrillation

Extracellular vesicles are small vesicles composed of lipid bilayers and measuring 100–1000 nm in diameter. They are released into the extracellular fluid and bloodstream from many cells, including cardiomyocytes. Extracellular vesicles are broadly divided into three types based on their production mechanisms: exosomes, microvesicles, and apoptotic bodies [46]. These extracellular vesicles are taken up by other cells and their contents are transferred, contributing to intercellular communication [47]. Regarding the aforementioned miRNA, it has been reported that quantifying miRNA contained in extracellular vesicles is useful as a biomarker for atrial fibrillation [48]. Meanwhile, there have also been reports that the blood concentration of extracellular vesicles themselves is elevated, so quantifying extracellular vesicles may also be a useful tool. However, measuring extracellular vesicles requires the extraction of small vesicles using methods such as ultracentrifugation, which is a tedious process and has the problem of reduced quantitative accuracy due to losses during the extraction process. A solution to this problem is required to utilize extracellular vesicles as clinically useful biomarkers.

Other Candidate Proteins for Atrial Fibrillation Biomarkers

ANGPT2, BMP10, FGF‐23, and GDF‐15 are examples of proteins identified and validated as potential biomarkers associated with atrial fibrillation through comprehensive analyses of RNA or proteins [37]. ANGPT2 is a protein that serves as a ligand for receptor tyrosine kinases and is involved in angiogenesis [49]. BMP10 is a molecule belonging to the TGFβ superfamily and is involved in processes such as cardiac formation [50]. It has been reported that lower levels of these biomarkers during rhythm control therapy for atrial fibrillation are associated with fewer recurrences [51]. FGF‐23 and GDF‐15 have also been reported to be elevated in atrial fibrillation patients [52]. However, since they also increase in other cardiac diseases, they are considered to be a type of cytokine expressed in response to stress [53]. In addition to atrial fibrillation, associations with heart failure and acute coronary syndrome have been reported [54].

Biomarkers for Ventricular Arrhythmias and Sudden Cardiac Death

Sudden cardiac death is a very serious concern of pathological significance, and despite its importance, its accurate prediction remains difficult [55, 56, 57, 58, 59, 60]. Several studies have been conducted to attempt to predict ventricular tachycardia and sudden cardiac death, including using biomarkers [61, 62, 63, 64]. Due to the nature of these diseases, biomarker research is exploring biomarkers that reflect the underlying causes of these arrhythmias, rather than those that result from ventricular tachycardia or sudden cardiac death. This overlaps with biomarkers for atrial fibrillation. Several studies showing that elevated CRP and IL‐6 as inflammatory markers are associated with the risk of sudden cardiac death have been reported in cohorts of the general public and in populations undergoing implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator (ICD) implantation [65]. There have also been many similar reports on BNP as a marker of heart failure, and troponin as a marker of myocardial damage has also been reported to be associated with sudden cardiac death [66]. These may reflect the severity of underlying heart disease, but their specificity for arrhythmogenicity remains unclear. Regarding miRNAs, one report indicates that miR‐320 levels are lower in patients with arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy compared to healthy individuals and those with idiopathic ventricular tachycardia, suggesting potential utility as an adjunctive diagnostic tool [67].

The Significance of Biomarker Testing in Arrhythmia Management

When using biomarkers in cardiac arrhythmia management, it is important to keep in mind that they are essentially nonspecific markers. CRP and BNP, among others, are easy to measure clinically but have low specificity [67, 68, 69, 70]. Atrial fibrillation is an example of a disease in which biomarkers may play an important role in clinical practice in the future. However, they are not used for definitive diagnosis, but rather as an auxiliary test to predict the presence of the condition when a diagnosis has not been made. When there is no other pathological condition causing inflammation and a slight elevation in CRP persists, or when an elevation in BNP is observed with no clear cause, it is useful to suspect the presence of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation and search for it. There are also reports that biomarkers can assess the risk of atrial fibrillation recurrence and complications, and a certain degree of usefulness can be expected, but treatment selection should be based primarily on traditional risk assessment.

Conclusion

This review summarizes the knowledge and potential of biomarkers for arrhythmias, focusing on atrial fibrillation. Arrhythmia biomarkers can be broadly divided into factors that cause arrhythmias and factors that increase or decrease as a result of arrhythmias. Much research is ongoing, and it is very promising. However, no biomarkers have yet been established that can provide concrete information for clinical decision‐making, and further research is needed.
