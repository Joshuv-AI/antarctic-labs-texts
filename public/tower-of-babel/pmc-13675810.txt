A new three-layer metapopulation networks model coupling online-offline social interactions with epidemic spread
Authors: Nan Chen, Liang’an Huo
Published: 2027

ABSTRACT
The dissemination of epidemic-related information can effectively enhance public awareness of preventive measures and decrease infection risks. However, information dissemination through multiple channels, including both online and offline sources, and the dynamics of information dissemination across these channels are influenced by individual mobility. To address this gap, this study develops a three-layer metapopulation network coupled model that integrates online information dissemination, epidemic spreading, and offline information dissemination. The proposed model systematically investigates the patterns of information dissemination through both online and offline channels, as well as the epidemic reaction–diffusion process driven by individual migration. Furthermore, the social interactions dynamics of both information channels are influenced by migration, manifested as migrated individuals acquire offline information in destination patches and establish online connections with individuals residing there. Theoretical analysis using the Microscopic Markov Chain Approach (MMCA) derives epidemic thresholds, while Monte Carlo (MC) simulations validate the model and examine factors influencing propagation dynamics. Results indicate that migration enhances individuals’ offline information acquisition, and accelerates cross-patch online information dissemination. Both online and offline information interactions exert inhibitory effects on epidemic spread, effectively increasing the infection threshold and reducing the outbreak scale. Furthermore, increasing the connectivity and dissemination rate of online information, particularly the information dissemination via weak relationships between patches, can effectively inhibit epidemic outbreaks. Notably, when the suppression effect of information on the epidemic is weak, population migration facilitates epidemic spread. In contrast, when the suppression effect is strong, migration-driven information dissemination significantly reduces the probability of infection, thereby further suppressing the outbreak.

Introduction

The epidemics have long posed a substantial threat to global public health and socioeconomic stability, such as the global spread of COVID-19, which has had an enormous impact on the safety of human life and the world economy since its outbreak (Morens et al., 2004; Tonnelier et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2026). Accelerated urbanization and intensified human activities have significantly altered epidemic transmission patterns, propelling complex network theory as a paradigm-shifting tool for epidemiological research. A large number of scholars have extensively studied the mechanisms of epidemic transmission through complex network theory, modeling and exploring the important factors influencing the spread of epidemics (Al-Amery et al., 2025; Iacopini et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2019).

The spread of epidemic is not only influenced by biological factors (e.g., viral infectivity, host immunity), but is also closely related to human behavior and social factors (Elsonbaty et al., 2026; Liu, Geng, et al., 2026; Wu & Huo, 2025). Studies have shown that information dissemination can significantly affect the dynamics of epidemic transmission by altering the individuals’ sense of protection (Liu, Peng, et al., 2026; Masoomy et al., 2023; Yu & Huo, 2025). It is well known that the spread of an epidemic may lead to the diffusion of epidemic-related information (Hong et al., 2022). In turn, individuals who are aware of epidemic-related information will take self-protective measures to avoid contracting the epidemic (Fan et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024). Therefore, the multi-layer network of information and epidemic coupling becomes an important framework for modeling the spread of epidemics (Chen et al., 2023; Granell et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2025; Zhang, Zhu, et al., 2025). For example, Ma et al. (Ma & Wang, 2025) proposed a UAU-SIS model based on a two-layer community structure and analyzed the effect of community structure on the spread of epidemic in a multi-layer network. Zhang et al. (Zhang et al., 2023) proposed an activity-driven multiplex framework with partial node mapping between layers and simplicial-complex interactions, showing that higher-order information interactions and interlayer mapping patterns can substantially affect epidemic spreading. Sagar et al. (Sagar et al., 2018) investigated the effect of time-varying transmission rates on the co-evolution of epidemics on a two-layer multiplexed network, where the infection rate in the epidemic layer depends on its perception probability in the awareness layer, and the information transmission rate in the awareness layer depends on its infection probability in the epidemic layer.

In the real world, information related to epidemics is diverse (Gallotti et al., 2020). For example, diversified information access channels, including offline information dissemination (Liu et al., 2022), online information dissemination (Saggu & Sinha, 2020), and mass media information dissemination (Granell et al., 2014), among others. Different information dissemination mechanisms have different impacts on epidemic transmission. Du et al. (Du & Fan, 2024) introduced the SHI1I2R information dissemination model for investigating the interaction dynamics of information in two-layer networks, online and offline, in emergency situations, and showed that information spreads faster and more widely in two-layer networks than in single-layer networks. Sun et al. (Sun et al., 2024) examined the SIRS-UAU disease-awareness transmission model incorporating the influence of mass media, noting that the implementation of mass media broadcasts contributed to an increase in the incidence of awareness and a decrease in the proportion of recovered individuals. In addition, during the epidemic spread, relevant information spreads not only positive information that is useful for epidemic prevention and control, but also negative information (Chen et al., 2022). Bao et al. (Bao & He, 2025) developed an epidemic model with an awareness cascade of positive and negative information on a time-delay multiplex networks, and investigated the effect of the awareness cascade of positive and negative information on the model. There may also be competition (Fang et al., 2023) or cooperation (Zhang et al., 2022) between the various types of information. Xiao et al. (Xiao et al., 2019) proposed the MM-SIS (Multiple information and Multiplex network-SIS) model to explore the propagation mechanism of multiple information in multiplexed networks. Meanwhile, the concept of influence factor is introduced to describe the complex interaction between different information.

Complex network theory provides an analytical tool for the spatial heterogeneity of epidemic transmission. Traditional contact models assume that epidemic transmission is characterized by significant spatial clustering through interactions between individuals, and in recent years, research has been further extended to metapopulation networks (Vikram et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2022). In metapopulation network models, where nodes represent different patches (e.g., regions and cities) and links represent migration routes between these patches, metapopulation models have been widely used to explore epidemiologic trends that depend on spatial structure (Tanaka et al., 2014; Valgañón et al., 2022). Colizza et al. (Colizza et al., 2007) first proposed a reaction-diffusion (RD) model in metapopulation networks, analyzing the effects of individual mobility and network heterogeneity on transmission. Gómez et al. (Gómez-Gardeñes et al., 2018) developed a Migration-Interaction-Return (MIR) framework based on reaction-diffusion model that takes into account the recurrent mobility patterns of human movement in response to underlying epidemiological processes, spatial distribution of populations, and commuting mobility patterns. Recent studies have further considered the spread of epidemics in multiple metapopulation networks (Han et al., 2025; Soriano-Paños et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2025). Zhang et al. (Zhang, Hong, et al., 2025) in order to explore the co-effects of herd awareness and resource allocation on the spatial spread of epidemics, a three-layer metapopulation network model was proposed to characterize the complex interactions between information dissemination, resource allocation, and epidemic spreading. From the perspective of considering individual behavior (e.g., individual mobility), Zhou et al. (Zhou et al., 2024) proposed a two-layer network framework, with an individual contact network at the information layer and a reaction-diffusion metapopulation network at the disease layer, and explored the coupling mechanism between information dissemination and epidemic spreading.

Existing studies have investigated the coupled dynamics of online information dissemination and physical epidemic spread. However, offline social interaction remains a crucial channel for information dissemination, as individuals continue face-to-face communication or receive broadcasts within their residential patches. Moreover, within metapopulation networks, individual mobility redistributes populations across regions through reaction–diffusion processes, thereby changing individuals’ physical locations. Consequently, migrating individuals shifted their information sources from their residential patches to their destination patches in the offline information dissemination channel, while simultaneously adjusting their connections in online information channels—for instance, by forming new virtual connections with individuals in their destination patches. The physical movement of individuals introduces time-varying characteristics to information environments and connections, thereby facilitating cross-patch information exchange—features that existing network frameworks fail to capture.

To bridge these research gaps, we propose a novel three-layer network framework that integrates an epidemic reaction-diffusion process in metapopulation network, an offline information propagation network, and an online information contact network. Considering the interplay between individual migration and dual-channel information dissemination, the model systematically investigates how the offline information environment in residential and destination patches, together with migration-driven time-varying online information connections, co-evolve with epidemic transmission. The main innovations of this study are as follows:(1)We propose a three-layer coupled metapopulation network model consisting of an epidemic spread layer, an offline information layer, and an online information layer. The epidemic spread layer represents the reaction-diffusion process of epidemic spread across patches; the offline information layer represents the offline information dissemination process in the metapopulation network; while the online information layer represents the online information dissemination process in the individual’s virtual social network.(2)The model examines the effect of individual migration on offline information dissemination. During movement from their residential patch to a destination patch, individuals exclusively access and adapt to the offline information environment of their new location. Specifically, the individuals’ source of offline information switches from the offline information dissemination in their residential patch to those in the destination patch.(3)The model further captures the effect of individual migration on online information dissemination. Upon arriving at the destination patch, increased offline interaction with local residents motivates migrants to establish new online informational weak relationships with a certain probability, thereby promoting cross-patch information exchange.

A coupling three-layer metapopulation network model

Model description

During epidemics, individuals acquire epidemic-related information through multiple channels. Traditionally, they are exposed to offline dissemination channels in their physical environment, such as community bulletins and broadcast announcements. However, with the rapid development of the internet, they engage in online information exchange with friends and family through digital devices and internet platforms. Furthermore, convenient transportation systems enable frequent population mobility across cities and regions, including commuting and travel. When an individual moves to a new area, they encounter local offline epidemic prevention publicity, which reshapes their immediate information environment. Simultaneously, migration prompts individuals to establish new online connections with local residents through social media and other digital platforms, facilitating additional information exchange (Zhou et al., 2024). Such online connections established between migrating individuals and local residents at their destinations are temporary and unstable, and are defined as online informational weak relationships (such as nodding acquaintances or temporary partners) (Feng et al., 2024). Consequently, human mobility facilitates cross-regional information flow and integration.

In this paper, we propose a three-layer network model to characterize the coupled interactions among epidemic spreading, offline information dissemination, and online information dissemination, as shown in Fig. 1. The epidemic spread layer (L) represents the epidemic reaction-diffusion metapopulation network, where nodes correspond to patches, and a homogeneous mix of individuals within the patches. Within each patch, individuals are homogeneously mixed, with epidemic transmission governed by intra-patch reaction processes and inter-patch diffusion through individual migration. The offline information layer (V1) represents the offline information dissemination network, with nodes as patches (one-to-one correspondence with the patches in the epidemic spread layer), and individuals within patches accessing information through their residential patches or neighboring patches. The online information layer (V2) represents the online information dissemination network, where the nodes are individuals (one-to-one correspondence with the individuals in the epidemic spread layer), and information diffuses through connections between individuals. The L layer describes the epidemic spread process, employing the Susceptible-Infected-Susceptible (SIS) model. There are two possible states of an individual: susceptible (S) or infected (I). Simultaneously, the reaction-diffusion process is used to characterize the spatial spread of the epidemic between patches. Following the reaction-diffusion framework introduced by (Gómez-Gardeñes et al., 2018) in the context of epidemic transmission dynamics, which describes periodic recurrent migration patterns, the proposed model consists of three steps at each time step: migration, interaction and return. As shown in Fig. 2(a).(1)Migration (M): first, any individual i selects to migrate out of their residential patch N with migration rate p, and determines the destination patch N′ through the inter-patch transition matrix W, WNN′=ωNN′/∑N″ωNN″, where ωNN′ denotes the weight of the edges between patch N and patch N′.(2)Interaction (I): after migration, epidemic interactions occur in each patch. Susceptible individuals (S) acquire infection through contact with infected counterparts (I) at a transmission rate β, while infected individuals (I) spontaneously revert to the susceptible state with a recovery rate μ.(3)Return (R): finally, migrating individuals return to the residential patches after updating their status and the next time step begins.

The information dissemination layers capture the propagation dynamics of epidemic-related information, employing the unaware-aware- unaware (UAU) model. Each individual may receive information and become aware of the epidemic, so the individual may be in one of two states: unaware (U) or aware (A).

The V1 layer describes the offline dissemination dynamics of information. Typically, unaware individuals become more alert to the epidemic and gradually become aware of the risk by interacting with aware individuals or information campaigns within the patch. It is reasonable to assume that the offline information dissemination rate exhibits a positive correlation with the local awareness density. Furthermore, for an individual migrating at time t, the recurrent migration pattern leads to movement from the residence patch to the destination patch. The V2 layer describes the online dissemination dynamics of information. Individuals also interact with each other online through virtual socialization, and it is assumed that individuals have stable online friendships with their relatives and friends, i.e., online information strong relationships (Friedkin, 1982). These strong relationships persist over time and are not disrupted by individual migration. The online information strong relationship adjacency matrix between individuals is defined as A,aij∈A, and aij=1 indicates that there is an online information strong relationship between individual i and individual j; aij=0 indicates that there is no online information strong relationship between individual i and individual j.

For migrated individuals, their offline interactions with destination patches become closer, thus affecting online socialization. Individuals have the probability to establish online informational weak relationships (Larson, 2017) (connecting edges with low frequency of interactions or weak relationships, such as nodding acquaintances or temporary partners) with individuals in the patch where they are located after migration is completed, as shown in Fig. 2(c). Specifically, after an individual migrates offline to a destination patch, the individual’s online information social network also has a probability of generating an information weak connection with an individual within that destination patch, with a connection rate δ. For offline information dissemination, individuals are affected by migration only in relation to the migration status of the individual at the current time step, and are not affected by previous time steps, i.e., migrating individuals will no longer receive offline information transfers from the migrating patch once they return to the residential patch.

Simulation results

In reality, due to the complexity and uncertainty of epidemic propagation, it is difficult to obtain the detailed propagation form of information dissemination during an epidemic outbreak. Therefore, in order to reveal how information dissemination changes the dynamics of an epidemic, we simulate the co-evolution of offline and online information dissemination-epidemic spreading on a synthetic three-layer network. In particular, the form of information dissemination is complex and usually has a heterogeneous network structure. The online network considers two network structures: the BA network and the ER network, with 4000 individuals and an average degree of 10. There were 20 patches in the offline network with a total number of 4000 individuals. For comparison, we consider two population distributions in the offline layer: homogeneous distribution (HOD) and heterogeneous distribution (HED). Under HOD, each patch contained an average of 200 individuals, reflecting a uniform distribution of the population across patches. Under HED, the number of individuals in each patch is proportional to the sum of its edge weights WNN′=ωNN′/∑N″ωNN″, which is determined by the physical network structure.

The MMCA starts with 0.1% infected individuals as seeds in each patch. The same initial condition is applied to the MC simulations for comparison. Epidemic prevalence is a commonly used measure of the extent of disease transmission, defined as ρI, ρI=∑NρNInN∑NnN, which represents the proportion of infected individuals in the population as a whole. Unless otherwise stated, the parameters μ, γ, α, and β were set to 0.2, 0.3, 0.5 and 0.4, respectively. In order to gain a comprehensive understanding of the combined effects of information dissemination and individual mobility on epidemic spread, we next analyzed the effects of key parameters, including offline information dissemination rate λ1, online information dissemination rate λ2, information suppression rate θ, and migration rate p. In the next sections, we explore the specific impact of these parameters on the spread of the epidemic. For each parameter set, we performed 50 mutually independent MC realizations and calculated the average result. Each realization was run for 200 time steps, and the result was recorded after confirming that the system had reached a stable state.

Conclusion

This study develops a novel three-layer metapopulation network framework to investigate the coupled dynamics of epidemic spreading and dual-channel information dissemination under individual mobility. By integrating epidemic migration-interaction-return processes, offline information dissemination, and online information dissemination into a unified structure, the model reveals how migration-driven shifts in physical environments and adjustments in virtual social connections influence the information-epidemic interplay. Key findings and contributions are summarized as follows:(1)In this study, we have examined a dual-channel information dissemination mechanism: offline information dissemination through physical metapopulation networks, where individuals have acquired awareness through local interactions within residence/destination patches; online information dissemination through a virtual social network between individuals. The results have shown that the dual-channel information dissemination is effective in suppressing epidemic, but enhancing online information dissemination has proved a more pronounced impact on reducing the epidemic scale. (2) Furthermore, we have explored the migration-information coupling mechanisms. For offline information dissemination, individual migration expands the scope of information dissemination through cross-patch exposure to offline information environments. For online information dissemination, the weak relationships formed between migrants and residents in destination patches accelerate the dissemination of epidemic-related information across patches. Simultaneously, the strength and stability of these relationships influence the final infection scale. (3) We have also investigated the complex relationship between the inhibitory effects of information on epidemic and individual mobility. The results have indicated that when information has only a weak suppressive effect, migration promotes epidemic transmission; in contrast, when information suppression is strong, migration inhibits epidemic spread. Epidemic control measures targeting population mobility need to take into account the specific strength of information’s suppressive effect on epidemic. This study assumed homogeneous mixing within patches and simplified online connection rules. Future research could incorporate dynamic network rewiring mechanisms, heterogeneous individual responses, and real-world mobility data. Extending the model to competitive information (e.g., misinformation vs. official guidance) and multi-pathogen scenarios would further enhance its applicability.
