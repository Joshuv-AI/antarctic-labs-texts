Purulent appendicular peritonitis revealing an appendiceal mucocele: a case report
Authors: Ouadi Yacine, Ben Brahim Maryem, Ben Mahmoud Ahmed, Atri Souhaib, Haddad A, Jouini M
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Appendiceal mucoceles are rare entities, often discovered incidentally or presenting with chronic, nonspecific abdominal symptoms. Acute purulent peritonitis, as an initial presentation, is exceptionally uncommon. Recognizing this atypical presentation is essential, as it may reveal an underlying appendiceal mucinous neoplasm with important therapeutic and prognostic implications.Case presentation:We report the case of a 26-year-old man with a 1-year history of intermittent abdominal pain and diarrhea who presented with an acute abdomen. Laboratory investigations showed elevated inflammatory markers. Abdominal CT scan revealed a dilated appendix with mural calcifications and associated intraperitoneal purulent fluid. Emergency laparotomy demonstrated generalized purulent peritonitis and a perforated, distended appendix with a distal cystic mass. An appendectomy was performed. Histopathological examination confirmed a ruptured low-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasm without evidence of invasive carcinoma or malignant peritoneal cells.Conclusion:Purulent appendiceal peritonitis may rarely be the initial manifestation of an underlying mucinous neoplasm. This case highlights the importance of considering this diagnosis in atypical presentations of the acute abdomen and underscores the role of histopathological assessment and multidisciplinary management in guiding optimal treatment and follow-up.

Introduction

Acute appendiceal peritonitis is a common surgical emergency, usually caused by perforated appendicitis. Rarely, it may reveal an underlying appendiceal neoplasm. Appendiceal mucocele is an uncommon condition, representing less than 1% of appendectomy specimens. Low-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasm (LAMN) is the most frequent neoplastic subtype. It usually presents with chronic symptoms or is discovered incidentally. Acute purulent peritonitis is an unusual mode of presentation. We report a case of a ruptured LAMN presenting with acute purulent peritonitis in a young patient. This case report is presented in accordance with the SCARE checklist[1]

Case report

A 26-year-old male presented to the emergency department with a 1-year history of intermittent abdominal pain associated with diarrhea. The pain had significantly increased over the previous 24 hours. On physical examination, the patient was afebrile. Abdominal examination revealed guarding in the right iliac fossa and hypogastrium. The remainder of the abdomen was tender. Laboratory investigations showed elevated inflammatory markers. The CRP was 200 mg/L, and the white blood cell count was 17 610/mm3. A CT scan was performed. It showed a dilated appendix measuring 18 mm in diameter with parietal calcifications, suggestive of an appendiceal mucocele. Significant periappendiceal fat stranding was noted, and intraperitoneal fluid was present. No peritoneal nodules or mucinous implants were identified (Fig. 1). The patient underwent emergency surgery by an attending at our teaching hospital through a midline laparotomy. Intraoperatively, a purulent intraperitoneal fluid was found. Exploration revealed a distended appendix perforated at the body. A cystic mass was present at the distal tip of the appendix (Figures 2–3). No mucinous peritoneal implants were observed. Peritoneal lavage was performed using abundant warm saline solution without antiseptic additives, and an appendectomy was carried out. The appendiceal base was resected using an 80-mm linear cutting stapler. Bacteriological examination of the peritoneal fluid was positive for Escherichia coli. Cytological analysis did not reveal malignant cells. The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 5 and received a total of 7 days of a combination of amoxicillin and clavulanic acid. Histopathological exam showed a 7-cm enlarged appendix. It was perforated at the distal body. The tip contained a cystic lesion filled with mucin. Mucin was present within the wall. The surrounding tissue showed inflammatory changes. No malignant cells were found. The base of the appendix was not infiltrated by mucin. The final diagnosis was a ruptured LAMN without evidence of malignancy. The case was discussed in a multidisciplinary tumor board meeting. Considering the diagnosis of a ruptured LAMN without malignant cells and without peritoneal implants, the decision was close surveillance. The patient was scheduled for regular clinical and radiological follow-up. We are currently at 9 months from the surgery. The patient is asymptomatic and compliant with the surveillance regimen. Discussion

Acute purulent appendiceal peritonitis is most commonly caused by perforated acute appendicitis. However, in rare cases, it may reveal an underlying appendiceal neoplasm such as an LAMN. Appendiceal mucoceles account for less than 1% of appendectomy specimens, and LAMN represents the most frequent neoplastic subtype[2]. LAMN is characterized by low-grade cytologic atypia and mucin production. Its clinical course is usually indolent. Many patients present with chronic or intermittent iliac fossa pain, while others are asymptomatic[3]. Acute presentation generally mimics uncomplicated or complicated appendicitis. Our case, presenting as generalized purulent peritonitis, is uncommon and may delay suspicion of an underlying neoplasm[4]. In most reported cases of ruptured LAMN, perforation leads to mucinous ascites and peritoneal dissemination rather than purulent contamination[3,5]. The presence of purulent peritonitis, as observed in our patient, suggests secondary infection of the obstructed appendix followed by perforation. In such cases, the inflammatory process may mask the mucinous lesion, and the diagnosis is often made intraoperatively or on histopathological examination. Preoperative imaging plays an important role. A dilated appendix greater than 15 mm with mural calcifications strongly suggests a mucocele rather than simple appendicitis[3]. Inflammatory signs such as fat stranding and intraperitoneal fluid may indicate superinfection or perforation. In emergency settings, however, the radiological appearance may be misinterpreted as complicated appendicitis, especially in young patients[4]. The main concern in cases of ruptured LAMN is the risk of pseudomyxoma peritonei (PMP). Perforation allows mucin and epithelial cells to disseminate into the peritoneal cavity. However, the risk depends on the presence of viable neoplastic cells within the peritoneal cavity[5]. In our case, although perforation was confirmed, no mucinous implants were observed intraoperatively, and cytology of the peritoneal fluid was negative for malignant cells. Surgical management in the setting of purulent peritonitis must balance oncologic safety and emergency control of sepsis. When the lesion is confined to the appendix and the base is free, appendectomy with safe resection margins is considered adequate treatment[3,6]. Right hemicolectomy is reserved for invasive adenocarcinoma, positive margins, or lymph node involvement[6]. Recent literature suggests that routine right hemicolectomy does not improve survival in the absence of invasive disease[6]. Postoperative management after perforated LAMN remains controversial. In patients without peritoneal implants or invasive carcinoma, close surveillance with periodic imaging is widely accepted[5]. Multidisciplinary discussion is essential to individualize management. In our patient, given the absence of malignant cells and macroscopic dissemination, surveillance was considered appropriate. Unique features of this case include the young age of the patient, the misleading presentation as generalized purulent peritonitis, and the absence of PMP despite appendiceal rupture. This case highlights that purulent appendiceal peritonitis in a young patient does not exclude an underlying mucinous neoplasm. Chronic symptoms preceding acute deterioration should raise suspicion. Careful intraoperative exploration, avoidance of mucin spillage, and thorough pathological assessment are essential to guide prognosis and follow-up.

Conclusion

Purulent appendiceal peritonitis may rarely reveal an underlying LAMN. In emergency settings, inflammation may mask the diagnosis. Careful surgical management and histopathological examination are essential. When no peritoneal implants or malignant cells are identified, close surveillance after a multidisciplinary discussion is an appropriate strategy.
