Enhancing early Alzheimer's disease clinical trials through prognostic score covariate adjustment
Authors: Bruno T Scodari, Roland Brown, Xiaotong Jiang, Changyu Shen, Kyle Ferber, Shuang Wu, Feng Gao, Szofia Bullain, Brian Millen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONA prognostic score (PS) summarizes a patient's expected disease progression and can increase the statistical efficiency of clinical trials when included as an analysis covariate.METHODSWe pooled patient data from observational studies and randomized trials for early Alzheimer's disease (AD) and trained PS candidates to predict 18‐month changes in the Clinical Dementia Rating Scale – Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB) score. The efficiency gains achieved through covariate adjustment were evaluated in a held‐out trial (N = 650).RESULTSA machine learning PS achieved a Pearson correlation of 0.48 between predicted and observed CDR‐SB changes in an internal test set (N = 398). Adjusting for this PS in the held‐out trial increased power from 80% to 87.9% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 85.5%–90.2%) with the original sample size. Alternatively, this approach could reduce the required sample size by 19.7% (95% CI: 13.7%–25.7%) while maintaining 80% power.DISCUSSIONOur findings support the use of PS adjustment for enhancing the efficiency of early AD trials.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, covariate adjustment, machine learning, power, prognostic score, required sample size, statistical efficiency

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a disabling neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the build up of beta‐amyloid in the brain and subsequent cognitive impairment. 1 It is the most common form of dementia, impacting one in nine Americans aged > 65 years and ranking as the sixth leading cause of death in the U.S. 1 , 2 The prevalence of AD is projected to double in the United States and in Europe by 2050, with other countries realizing up to a three‐fold increase. 3 , 4 Given this growing public health burden, there is a large unmet need for additional disease‐modifying therapies that slow AD progression. 5 , 6

Historically, clinical development in AD has been challenging largely due to heterogeneity in disease pathophysiology and progression, coupled with highly variable outcomes/endpoints. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 As an additional challenge, AD trials are inherently expensive due to the long treatment durations and large sample sizes required to detect clinically relevant treatment effects. 11 , 12 Given these challenges, increasing the statistical efficiency of AD trials is critical for addressing this public health burden.

One promising approach accepted by regulatory agencies for increasing trial efficiency is prognostic score (PS) covariate adjustment (herein referred to as PSA). 13 , 14 A patient's PS is broadly defined as their predicted outcome under the control condition, therefore representing baseline “disease‐risk”. 15 In our specific use case, the PS represents an individual's predicted change in Clinical Dementia Rating Scale ‐ Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB) score from baseline to month 18 in the absence of treatment. 16 , 17 Such predictions are estimated as a function of baseline patient attributes by fitting a statistical or machine learning model, sometimes referred to as a digital twin, 18 , 19 to predict future disease progression. 20 In the primary analysis of clinical trials, adjusting for a PS that is correlated with true disease progression as a covariate can reduce unexplained variance, resulting in improved precision of treatment effect estimates (e.g., difference in mean CDR‐SB change from baseline to month 18 between treatment vs. placebo). 21 While most recent work has focused on the development of PS models for patients with AD, 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 few studies have evaluated the precision gains attributable to PSA in independent AD trials. Such research is critical for understanding the efficacy and generalizability of PSA, which could be instrumental for improving the overall efficiency of AD trials.

In this study, we aimed to (1) develop PS models for patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) due to AD and mild AD (herein referred to as early AD) using data from observational studies and randomized controlled trials (RCTs), and (2) evaluate the precision gains attributable to PSA in an independent, held‐out RCT. The scope of our work focuses on patients with early AD given the abundance of data from recent trials, but the principle of these methods can be extended to more advanced AD populations and other therapeutic areas.

METHODS

Data sources

We harmonized data for patients with early AD from non‐interventional observational studies (Supporting Information), including the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), 27 Swedish BioFINDER study, 28 and National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC), 29 along with data from the placebo arm of aducanumab RCTs—PRIME (NCT01677572), 30 ENGAGE (NCT02477800), 31 EMERGE (NCT02484547) 32 —and the gosuranemab Phase 2 TANGO trial (NCT03352557). 33 To ensure generalizability to future AD trials, we restricted the harmonized data to include patients who met the inclusion criteria for contemporaneous AD trials; however, we did not impose strict age or amyloid positivity criteria for individuals from the observational sources to maximize the size of the training data (Table S1). All studies were approved by the institutional review boards of the participating institutions, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Outcome

The outcome of interest was change in CDR‐SB score from baseline to month 18, as this was the primary endpoint in the held‐out TANGO trial. The CDR scale is evaluated across six cognitive and functional domains—memory, orientation, judgement and problem solving, community affairs, home and hobbies performance, and personal care—and the scoring of each domain ranges from 0 to 3 (0, no impairment; 0.5, questionable impairment; 1, mild impairment; 2, moderate impairment; and 3, severe impairment, with the personal care domain omitting the 0.5 score). 16 , 17 The CDR‐SB score sums the six individual domains, providing a value ranging from 0 to 18 with higher scores indicating greater disease severity. It has been widely used as the primary endpoint for clinical efficacy in contemporaneous AD trials. 34 , 35 , 36 , 37

Patient features

Our harmonized data comprised 66 baseline patient features that were used to predict changes in CDR‐SB. This included information on patient demographics, clinical diagnosis, health status, familial history of disease, and the total scores and sub‐scores of clinical scales (i.e., CDR‐SB, Mini‐Mental State Examination [MMSE], 38 Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale‐Cognitive [ADAS‐Cog] Subscale 13). 39 The baseline data also contained brain region volume features derived from structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The comprehensive list of patient features is included in Table S2.

Model development

We applied a three‐step process to train PS candidate models (Figure 1).

PS training pipeline. A three‐step approach was used to train potential PS candidates. In step 1, we partitioned the data into train and test sets using a 75/25% split. In step 2, we coerced all features to numeric values and imputed missing features using the missForest algorithm, using the same imputer object to impute missingness across the train and test sets. Imputed features were normalized using a Z‐score transformation before fitting any models, making sure to retain and apply the same means and standard deviations on the test sets. In step 3, we trained machine learning models within a five‐fold cross‐validation framework. A randomized grid search was used to select the best‐fit parameters that maximized for Pearson's r. Final models with best‐fit parameters were retrained on the entire train data and used to generate predictions on the unseen test data for performance evaluation. PS, prognostic score.

Train–test split

We used ADNI, NACC, BioFinder, ENGAGE, EMERGE, and PRIME sources to develop our PS models. TANGO was not used for model development and was held out as an external test set, as we expect this cohort to closely resemble the target population of future AD trials. In step 1, patients from the six model development sources were partitioned into train and internal test sets using an approximate 75/25% split, where the internal test set was frequency matched to key TANGO characteristics: baseline diagnosis, age, education, and apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotype. We assessed model performance on the internal test set to inform decisions related to various data and modeling tasks (e.g., missing data imputation strategy).

Preprocessing

In step 2, we coded all features as numeric values for simplicity, and we imputed missing observations (Table S3) using the missForest algorithm, given its strong performance across similar applications, as well as its ability to model non‐linear relationships and incorporate stochasticity. 40 Next, we fit a missForest object to the train set and used the same object to impute missingness across the train set, internal test set, and external test set. 41 We normalized the imputed features using a Z‐score transformation before fitting any models, making sure to retain and apply the identical transformation on the test sets.

Training

In step 3, we trained a series of machine learning models to predict CDR‐SB changes. These models included ordinary least squares (OLS) regression, Elastic Net, K‐nearest Neighbors, Support Vector Regression with a radial basis function, Random Forest, and XGBoost. Each of these models—except for OLS (no tunable parameters)—was hypertuned within a five‐fold cross validation framework, using a randomized grid search to select the parameters that maximized Pearson's correlation coefficient (r). We optimized for Pearson's r due to its direct relationship with sample size and statistical power when evaluating treatment effects on continuous outcomes. 42 As a more complex model, we used “stacking” to train an ensemble of the aforementioned models, which is a common approach for combining the strengths of individual learners to improve model generalization and performance on unseen data. 43 , 44 Details of our stacked ensemble architecture are described in Figure S1, and example code on how to fit such a model is provided in the Supporting Information.

Internal evaluation

PS candidates

For each model, we generated predictions on the internal test set and computed Pearson's r between predicted and observed CDR‐SB changes at month 18. We selected the best‐performing model as our primary candidate for external evaluation in TANGO. As a secondary candidate, we trained a simple linear regression model with a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) penalty. The LASSO regression approach selects the most important variables, resulting in a parsimonious model that utilizes a subset of features. 45 Lastly, we treated baseline ADAS‐Cog as the third candidate, as it was the most prognostic individual predictor (i.e., highest correlation with 18‐month CDR‐SB changes) in TANGO but has not always been included as a covariate when analyzing CDR‐SB endpoints in previous AD trials. The two model‐based PS candidates were retrained on the combined train and internal test sets prior to generating PS predictions for the TANGO cohort.

Feature importance and stability

Within a bootstrapped framework (1000 iterations), we refit the LASSO model (with the optimized penalty parameter) to estimate the variability of coefficient estimates. Relative feature importance was assessed as the proportion of the summed absolute coefficient values explained by each predictor, while predictor stability was measured as the proportion of times each predictor was included in the bootstrapped model (i.e., had a non‐zero coefficient).

External evaluation

Treatment effect estimates

We used mixed models for repeated measurements (MMRMs) to evaluate the efficiency gain (variance reduction) attributable to PSA in TANGO. To do so, we fit the originally specified primary analysis MMRM with CDR‐SB change as the outcome, and fixed effects for treatment (placebo, low‐dose gosuranemab [125 mg once every 4 weeks (q4w) or 375 mg q12w], intermediate‐dose gosuranemab [600 mg q4w], high‐dose gosuranemab [2,000 mg q4w]), visit (week 28 [month 6], week 54 [month 12], week 78 [month 18]), interaction between treatment and visit, baseline CDR‐SB, interaction between baseline CDR‐SB and visit, baseline MMSE score, region (U.S., Europe, Poland, Australia/Japan), disease stage (mild AD or MCI), and baseline use of AD symptomatic medications (yes or no). An unstructured covariance matrix was used, along with the Kenward–Roger degrees of freedom approximation. 46 For each PS candidate, we used the same model specifications but included additional fixed effects for the PS and its interaction with visit. We extracted the difference in means between gosuranemab and placebo arms at month 18, comparing the point estimates and variances among the original and PS‐adjusted cases.

To quantify the efficiency gain attributable to PSA, we computed relative efficiency (RE), which provides a comparison of the variance of treatment effect estimates before and after PSA:

The denominator, Var(β^TANGO), denotes the variance of a treatment effect estimate from the pre‐specified TANGO analysis while the numerator, Var(β^PSA), denotes the variance of the PS‐adjusted estimate. An RE value less than one indicates that PSA successfully reduced variance and increased statistical efficiency.

Performance metrics

Our assessment of PSA performance in TANGO focused on efficiency gains in estimating the difference in mean CDR‐SB changes between the high‐dose gosuranemab and placebo group at month 18. Using the RE statistic for the high‐dose treatment effect estimate, we calculated two performance metrics that more directly quantify the impact of PSA 47 :

For each performance metric, we constructed 95% bootstrapped confidence intervals using the percentile method.

Intermediate visits

To interrogate the benefits of PSA at intermediate visits, we trained separate PS models to predict 6‐ and 12‐month CDR‐SB changes. We included the resulting visit‐specific predictions as the sole PS covariate in separate MMRM analyses, estimated treatment effects at the corresponding visit, and calculated the associated performance metrics using the same methodology outlined in Sections 2.6.1 and 2.6.2.

Cross‐trial evaluation

We repeated the analyses outlined in Sections 2.6.1 and 2.6.2 in ENGAGE and EMERGE (using placebo and treatment arms) to further assess the efficiency gains attributable to PSA across trials. Details of the original study designs and primary analysis model specifications are found in the original Haeberlein et al. (2022) publication. 48

Software

All analyses were performed using version 4.3.0 of the R statistical software. Specifically, we used the dplyr (version 1.1.4) package for data wrangling, the missForest (version 1.5) and missForestPredict (version 1.0) packages for imputation, the caret (version 7.0‐1) package for PS model development, and the mmrm (version 0.3.12) and emmeans (version 1.10.3) packages for fitting treatment effect models and analyzing the efficiency gains attributable to PSA.

RESULTS

We identified 6075 patients with early AD from the harmonized data with observed CDR‐SB changes at month 18. Of this harmonized data, 1549 patients matched the selection criteria outlined in Table S1. From these 1549 patients, we allocated 1151 to the train set and 398 to the internal test set based on an approximate 75/25% split, using frequency matching to ensure comparability among patients in the internal test set and TANGO with respect to baseline diagnosis, age, education, and APOE genotype. The 650 patients in TANGO were held out as an external test set, of whom 533 had CDR‐SB scores at month 18 (Figure 2).

Baseline patient characteristics

Of the 1151 patients in the training data, 560 (48.7%) were included from observational studies and 591 (51.3%) were included from previous RCTs (Table 1). A higher proportion of patients in the observational data were older, male (vs. female), White (vs. minority), and more educated than those in the RCT data. Related to diagnosis, a higher proportion of patients in the observational data had mild AD (vs. MCI due to AD), were amyloid negative (vs. positive), and had e2/other and e3/e3 APOE alleles (vs. e3/e4 and e4/e4) than those in the RCT data. Patients in the observational data had lower baseline scores for CDR‐SB (Mean [SD]: 1.71 [1.3] vs. 2.38 [1.0]) and ADAS‐Cog (17.4 [7.2] vs. 22.0 [6.2]), but higher scores for MMSE (27.3 [2.1] vs. 26.4 [1.7]) than those in the RCT data.

Internal evaluation

Machine learning methods were effective in predicting CDR‐SB changes at month 18 (Table 2). Individual models produced internal test set predictions that were moderately correlated with observed CDR‐SB changes (r = 0.422–0.477). Generally, the more complex models (e.g., Random Forest and XGBoost) were prone to overfitting, yielding higher correlations between the train set predicted and observed changes, but lower correlations between the internal test set predicted and observed changes. By combining the strengths of each individual model in the stacked ensemble, we minimized overfitting and boosted Pearson's r in the internal test set to 0.480. Based on these statistics, we selected the ensemble model as our primary candidate for evaluation in TANGO.

External evaluation

For the TANGO cohort (N = 533 with CDR‐SB changes at month 18), we generated PSs for each candidate (stacked ensemble, LASSO regression, and ADAS‐Cog). Of these patients, 174 (32.6%) were originally randomized to high‐dose gosuranemab, 91 (17.1%) to intermediate‐dose gosuranemab, 98 (18.4%) to low‐dose gosuranemab, and 170 (31.9%) to placebo. At month 18, the average change in baseline CDR‐SB was 1.76 (SD: 2.04) for the high‐dose group, 2.23 (SD: 2.99) for the intermediate‐dose group, 2.10 (SD: 2.38) for the low‐dose group, and 1.77 (SD: 2.38) for the placebo group. The distributions of prespecified covariates were balanced across the treatment arms, while the distributions for PS candidates were slightly imbalanced across the treatment arms (Table S4). Furthermore, the PS candidates appeared to generalize well to external data, as their correlations with patient disease progression in TANGO were comparable to the correlations observed in the internal test set (Figure S2).

Treatment effect estimates

For each TANGO treatment effect, PSA resulted in slightly different point estimates but significantly lower variances across all three candidates (Table 3). Importantly, we note that perturbations in the point estimates do not reflect statistical bias, 49 , 50 but rather a correction of the PS imbalance across study arms. We found that the ensemble candidate was most effective in reducing variance compared to the prespecified MMRM model, lowering the variance of treatment effect estimates by 19.6%–19.9% and achieving relative efficiencies of 0.801–0.804. We found that the LASSO candidate achieved similar results, lowering the variance of treatment effect estimates by 18.6%–18.8% and achieving relative efficiencies of 0.812–0.814. The ADAS‐Cog candidate was the least effective, lowering the variance of treatment effect estimates by 14.2%–14.4% and achieving relative efficiencies of 0.856–0.858.

Performance metrics

When focusing specifically on the high‐dose gosuranemab versus placebo group (Table 4), PSA with the ensemble candidate achieved absolute power increases of 7.9% (5.5%–10.2%) and 5.1% (3.7%–6.4%) from 80% and 90% baselines, respectively, compared to analysis without PSA. Alternatively, PSA with the ensemble candidate could lead to a relative sample size reduction of 19.7% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 13.7%–25.7%) while maintaining 80% power. Similarly, PSA with the LASSO candidate achieved absolute power increases of 7.4% (5.3%–9.8%) and 4.9% (3.6%–6.2%) from 80% and 90% baselines, respectively, compared to analysis without PSA. Alternatively, PSA with the LASSO candidate could lead to a relative sample size reduction of 18.6% (95% CI: 13.3%–24.6%) while maintaining 80% power. The LASSO performance metrics were robust to a simpler imputation strategy where missing observations in TANGO were imputed using the training medians instead of the missForest object. In this analysis, the LASSO model coefficients were static, indicating that the results were insensitive to the method used to impute only the new data (Table S5). Lastly, PSA with baseline ADAS‐Cog achieved absolute power increases of 5.7% (3.6%–7.7%) and 3.8% (2.5%–5.0%) from 80% and 90% baselines, respectively, compared to analysis without PSA. Alternatively, PSA with baseline ADAS‐Cog could lead to a relative sample size reduction of 14.2% (95% CI: 8.9%–19.4%) while maintaining 80% power.

Intermediate visits

High‐dose gosuranemab versus placebo estimates with and without PSA are visualized by study visit and faceted by PS candidate in Figure 4. As expected, variances increased as a function of follow‐up time due to heterogeneity in disease progression. While the ensemble candidate was most effective in reducing variance at month 18, the LASSO candidate was most effective at both intermediate visits. DISCUSSION

In this study, we harmonized observational and RCT data to develop PS models for patients with early AD. We evaluated the performance of three PS candidates on the gosuranemab Phase 2 trial and found that PSA resulted in significant precision gains, which could have retrospectively increased the trial's efficiency.

Our results are most comparable to recent research conducted by Devanarayan et al., 10 , 22 who developed a PS model for predicting 18‐month CDR‐SB changes with placebo data from elenbecestat trials (NCT02956486 and NCT03036280 [N = 934]) 51 and evaluated PS‐adjusted treatment effect estimates in the lecanemab Study 201 trial (NCT01767311 [N = 587]). 52 Similarly, they found that a machine learning ensemble performed the best during model development and reported a Pearson correlation of 0.54 between predicted and observed changes in a validation set, which was similar to our performance in the internal test set (Table 2). In addition, they identified an overlapping set of influential features, including baseline ADAS‐Cog scores, MMSE scores, and structural brain predictors derived from MRI measurements. 22 The similarity of our feature importance results, despite differences in data sources, provides a high level of confidence that the prognostic signals we observed are robust and reproducible across patient populations with early AD.

On the other hand, there are some noticeable differences between our PSA evaluation in TANGO compared to their PSA evaluation in Study 201. 10 First, we only observed marginal shifts in the treatment effect point estimates when applying PSA (Table 3, Figure 4), whereas they reported more sizeable shifts. Larger shifts in their point estimates likely reflect greater imbalances in the distribution of their PS across the Study 201 arms compared to the distribution of our PS across the TANGO arms. Additionally, our PS performance measures were computed directly from the observed relative efficiency for the high‐dose gosuranemab versus placebo comparison in TANGO, whereas they used the correlation between the placebo arm predicted and observed changes in Study 201 to simulate variance explained by the PS and estimate performance measures. Both approaches are statistically valid, but the latter assumes that the proportion of variance explained by adjusting for the PS is equal across placebo and treatment arms, 19 which need not hold in practice. Lastly, we developed two alternate PS candidates with solid performance that are more interpretable, easier to operationalize, and simpler to communicate to regulatory agencies and other stakeholders. Of particular interest is the LASSO candidate—a weighted sum of just 11 predictors—which was almost as effective as the ensemble at the month 18 visit, as well as the most performant at intermediate visits. A possible explanation is that the relationship between the baseline features and CDR‐SB changes may be sufficiently described by a linear model with a subset of variables in the short‐term (i.e., < 1 year), but explaining this relationship at later timepoints may require more complex approaches due to an increase in the heterogeneity of progression and treatment response. However, future research is needed to confirm the validity of this hypothesis.

Irrespective of which PS is selected, the efficiency benefits of PSA are clear. In the analysis of the TANGO trial, we demonstrated significant variance reduction in the treatment effect estimates due to PSA, which enabled sizeable absolute power increases. Given the high inherent risk of conducting AD trials, even modest power increases can effectively derisk a trial by increasing the likelihood of detecting an effect. 53 The reported 7.9% power increase (from 80% baseline) is sizeable, as such a power gain would typically require recruiting many more patients due to diminishing power increases per patient beyond the 80% threshold. 54

The precision gains attributable to PSA can also be used to reduce the sample size while maintaining the planned study power. With a 19.7% relative sample size reduction in TANGO, approximately 128 patients could have theoretically been omitted while maintaining 80% power. Reducing recruitment counts by this magnitude would yield considerable resource and time savings, 55 but fixed sample size designs increase the risk of an underpowered trial if the PS does not perform as expected. Two approaches have been proposed to mitigate this risk while still realizing PSA‐derived resource savings from lower required sample sizes. Blinded sample size re‐estimation can estimate the degree of PSA performance during the trial—via residual variance or relative efficiency measures—and adjust the required sample size accordingly. 56 , 57 Alternatively, continuous information‐adaptive designs use information accrual rather than exact sample size to define stopping rules, and PSA‐derived efficiency gains can be incorporated into the information accrual measures to stop recruitment when the PS‐adjusted estimator obtains the required power. 58 These approaches have the additional benefit of enabling further sample size reduction if the PS outperforms expectations in the target trial.

Our approach has several limitations. First, our external evaluation was limited to a single Phase 2 AD trial that was comprised of predominantly white participants. 46 It is possible that the underlying population characteristics in future AD trials may differ from those in TANGO (i.e., “data drift”), which could attenuate the benefit of our PS. While we observed similar performance in ENGAGE and EMERGE, further exploration in fully independent early AD trials would be helpful for confirming generalizability. Second, because TANGO was a negative trial, our PS predictions were similarly prognostic across study arms. In a positive trial and depending on the nature of the treatment effect, the PS could be less prognostic in the treatment arm(s), resulting in a lower degree of efficiency gain. Finally, our current PS models were developed and evaluated for patients with early AD and, therefore, may not accurately predict outcomes for patients at earlier or more advanced disease stages. However, the underlying analytical principles could be generalized to other stages of AD or even other therapeutic areas provided the appropriate data are available.

In conclusion, we developed PS candidates for patients with early AD and found that PSA resulted in reduced variance in a held‐out trial. We also demonstrated that simpler PS models, such as LASSO regression, can achieve comparable efficiency gains while being more interpretable and easier to operationalize. Future directions include confirming the generalization of these results in other trials and incorporating multimodal features (e.g., fluid biomarkers and unstructured imaging data). Our findings support the application of PSA in early AD trials and highlight its potential for use in other therapeutic areas.
