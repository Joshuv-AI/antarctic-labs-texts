Upper bound solution to the support pressure of tunnels excavated in weak Hoek–Brown rock masses
Authors: Mehdi Bahrami, Meysam Imani, Ahmad Fahimifar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Determining a reliable value of the support pressure is necessary to prevent tunnel instability during excavation in weak rock masses. There are a few appropriate analytical solutions, most focused on tunnels excavated in soils. The present study proposes a method for determining the support pressure required to stabilize tunnels excavated in weak rock masses. The upper bound limit analysis method was applied for a rock mass obeying the Hoek–Brown failure criterion. Based on the findings, improving the strength of the rock masses results in decreasing the required support pressure. Increasing the Hoek–Brown parameters mi from 5 to 20, GSI from 10 to 25, and σci from 1 to 10 MPa, results in a maximum reduction of the tunnel support pressure by 62%, 56%, and 70%, respectively. In addition, increasing the ratio of horizontal to vertical insitu stresses from 0.4 to 1.2 reduces the maximum support pressure by about 59%. Moreover, increasing the disturbance factor from zero to 1 results in an increase of about 135% in the support pressure, while by increasing the tunnel width and the unit weight of the rock mass surrounding the tunnel, the maximum increase of 181% and 93% were observed in the support pressure.

Introduction

The support pressure is an important issue in excavating underground structures, mainly road tunnels. Extensive studies have been conducted using experimental, analytical, and numerical methods to determine the support pressure of tunnels. Using 1g tests, Atkinson et al.1 investigated the support pressure and failure mechanism developed around the tunnels excavated in dense sand. Hagiwara et al.2 used centrifuge tests to evaluate the effect of support pressure on the undrained stability of tunnels in over-consolidated clays. Further, using a centrifuge test, Kamata et al.3 investigated the face stability of reinforced tunnels. Along with the mentioned experimental methods, various analytical studies have been presented mainly based on limit equilibrium and limit analysis methods to determine tunnel support pressure. Yang et al.4 studied the support pressure of shallow circular tunnels in cohesive-frictional soils utilizing the upper bound method of limit analysis, considering two different failure mechanisms. The Mohr–Coulomb failure criterion and the associated flow rule were applied to this aim. The support pressure was assumed to be exerted uniformly on the tunnel roof and wall. Based on the results, an increase in the lateral earth pressure coefficient decreases the support pressure in the tunnel roof while increasing the support pressure of the tunnel walls. In other studies, Yamamoto et al.5,6 and Yamamoto et al.7 investigated the support pressure of a shallow single circular tunnel and single and twin square tunnels in cohesive-frictional soils using the upper and lower bound methods. Based on the upper bound solution proposed by Zhao et al.8, the lateral earth pressure coefficient of rock masses affects the tunnel support pressure for shallow circular tunnels in cohesive-frictional soils. In addition, Yang et al.9 analyzed the support pressure of circular tunnels in Hoek–Brown rock masses using the upper bound method. They assumed that the support pressure exerted on the tunnel roof and wall be uniform. They have also considered a constant cohesion and friction angle in their failure mechanism, which is not consistent with the behavior of a rock mass. The results indicated that increasing the Hoek–Brown parameters of the rock mass decreases the support pressure and the expansion of the failure mechanism around the tunnel. Moreover, Aboo et al.10 assessed the support pressure of wide rectangular tunnels in Tresca soils using the finite element limit analysis method. They investigated the effect of different tunnel dimensions, depth, and soil unit weight and cohesion on the support pressure. Furthermore, Wang et al.11 examined the support pressure of shallow horseshoe tunnels in layered jointed rock strata using the upper bound method. By considering various magnitudes of the tunnel depth and the properties of the surrounding rock, they performed several numerical analyses to obtain the failure mechanism needed in the upper bound calculation. In another study, Wilson et al.12 investigated the effect of the distance between twin square tunnels on the undrained stability of the tunnels using the limit analysis method. Yang et al.13 studied the stability of an oval-shaped tunnel in cohesive-frictional soils by applying the finite element limit analysis. Wilson et al.14 discussed the stability of rectangular tunnels in normally consolidated clay using the upper bound of limit analysis. They showed that the tunnels in which the height is greater than the width are more stable than those in which the width is greater than the height. In another study, Zhang et al.15 proposed a limit equilibrium solution for the stability of a bolt-reinforced tunnel face in homogeneous soil. Based on the results, the optimal length of the bolts is about 90% of the tunnel diameter. Further, Xiao et al.16 assessed the stability of rectangular tunnels in Hoek–Brown rock masses by applying the limit analysis method. They performed a parametric study on the effect of Hoek–Brown parameters and the tunnel depth, width, and height. Furthermore, Seghateh Mojtahedi et al.17 presented an upper bound solution to determine the face pressure of deep and shallow circular tunnels excavated in weak Hoek–Brown rock masses. Pengfei et al.18 focused on theoretical analytical simulations of limit support pressure and vertical earth pressure on the cutting face for tunnels considering the arching effect.

As explained, most previous studies have focused on the stability of tunnels in soil medium considering the Mohr–Coulomb failure criterion. Although many tunnel engineering projects are constructed in Hoek–Brown rock masses, few studies have been conducted on the stability of tunnels in such environments. Among others, the method presented by Yang et al.9 can be mentioned. However, as noted previously, considering a constant cohesion and friction angle for the rock mass is an important shortage in this method. The present paper aims to improve the lack of studies regarding the support pressure of tunnels excavated in Hoek–Brown rock masses and also improve the accuracy of the existing methods, namely Yang et al.9, by considering different values of cohesion and friction angle in different parts of the rock mass surrounding the tunnel. The upper bound method of limit analysis was applied in this study, and the Hoek–Brown rock mass parameters were considered in the order that the rock mass can be assumed as “weak”. The ground surface was considered horizontal, while groundwater’s presence was ignored. A horseshoe cross section was considered for the tunnel which was approximated by a rectangular section for simplification. In addition, the support pressure distribution on the tunnel roof and wall was assumed to be uniform.

Replacing the non-linear Hoek–Brown criterion with only one tangent line reduces the accuracy of the results significantly. Steps of determining the support pressure

The failure mechanism

Fig. 2 shows the failure mechanism considered in this study and the corresponding velocity hodograph. Only half of the mechanism was illustrated due to the symmetry. W, H, and h represent the width, height, and depth of the tunnel, respectively. To simplify the calculations, the actual shape of the tunnel section was approximated by a rectangular shape, and the support pressures on the tunnel roof and wall were assumed to apply uniformly. Previous researchers have also assumed such a simplification to facilitate the calculation (Wang et al.11; He et al.23; Lei et al.24).

Fig. 2(a) The failure mechanism and (b) the velocity hodograph.

(a) The failure mechanism and (b) the velocity hodograph.

The rock mass above the tunnel roof was divided into two triangular wedges, named ABC and CMG, m triangular wedges in part ACAm and the quadrilateral wedge CGAm+1Am which extends to the region below the tunnel crown. In the rest of the rock mass below the tunnel crown, i.e., part FGAm+1, n-1 triangular wedges were considered. The absolute velocity of the wedges was shown as Vi, while the relative velocity of two adjacent wedges was displayed as Vi-1,i. The angle of each velocity vector with respect to the corresponding velocity discontinuity line equals the internal friction angle of the rock mass (Chen25. As explained previously, the main contribution of the present study is to use the multi-tangential technique, which results in unique tangential friction angles in each discontinuity line. The magnitude of these angles is unknown, which were determined during the optimization of the objective function, i.e., the formula of the support pressure developed in the following parts of this paper. In addition to the tangential friction angles, the angles αi, βi, and θi are other unknowns of the problem, whose values were determined through optimization. Furthermore, q and e = Kq indicate the uniform vertical and horizontal support pressures on the tunnel roof and wall, respectively. K is regarded as the value of the lateral earth pressure coefficient (the ratio of horizontal to vertical support pressure), which does not necessarily equal the at-rest lateral earth pressure coefficient (K0). Therefore, various magnitudes of K were considered in the present study to investigate its effect on the support pressure.

Determining the optimal number of wedges

Any number of wedges, i.e., m and n, can be considered in the upper bound equations developed in the current study. However, an optimal number of wedges should be considered to achieve the best value of the support pressure in a reasonable run time. For simplicity, it was assumed that m = n in the present study. Considering three different rock masses with the properties presented in Table 1, the optimization of the objective function, i.e., Eq. (9), was performed considering different values of m = n. As shown in Fig. 3, by increasing m and n to 3, q increases significantly. Then, the rate of increase decreases significantly so that it becomes less than 0.5% from m = n = 4 to m = n = 5. Therefore, the optimal values of m and n were selected equal to 5, which resulted in 24 rigid blocks and 46 unknowns in the whole mechanism. It is worth noting that Wang et al.11 also considered the number of wedges for each side of the failure mechanism as m = n = 5 when calculating horseshoe tunnels’ support pressure.

Comparison of the results

Comparing with the Mohr–Coulomb-based methods

Reviewing literature shows that most existing methods for determining tunnel support pressure have been presented for soil media considering the Mohr–Coulomb failure criterion. Among others, the method presented by Yang et al.4 which was related to the support pressure of tunnels excavated in soil medium, was selected for comparison with the results of the present paper. Yang et al.4 utilized the upper bound method and considered two failure mechanisms A and B, for the soil surrounding the tunnel. As another comparison, the present paper was compared with the upper bound method proposed by Zhao et al.8 for rock masses following the Mohr–Coulomb failure criterion. It should be noted that both the Yang et al.4 and Zhao et al.8 methods are based on the Mohr–Coulomb failure criterion, while the present paper deals with a rock mass obeying the Hoek–Brown failure criterion. To compare these methods, first, a set of Hoek–Brown properties were assumed. Then, using the RocLab program, the equivalent c and ϕ for the assumed Hoek–Brown properties were determined. These c and ϕ were used in the Yang et al.4 and Zhao et al.8 equations to obtain the support pressure. Considering a tunnel with W = H = 10 m and h = 20 m, excavated in a rock mass with σci = 1 MPa, mi = 7, GSI = 10, D = 0, and γ = 20 kN/m3, the equivalent Mohr–Coulomb parameters using RocLab program are c = 10 kPa and ϕ = 18°. The obtained c and ϕ parameters were applied to the Yang et al.4 and Zhao et al.8 methods, while the assumed Hoek–Brown parameters were used in the upper bound formulation of the present paper. As shown in Fig. 4, the support pressure obtained from the present study is at most 15% and 14% larger than those of mechanisms A and B proposed by Yang et al.4, while a maximum difference of about 21% was seen between the present study and the Zhao et al.8 method. The same variation trend was observed among the considered methods.

Comparing with the Hoek–Brown-based method

As another comparison, the results of the present paper were compared with that presented by Yang et al.9 for Hoek–Brown rock masses. In their study, these researchers applied the upper bound method and considered two failure mechanisms, M1 and M2. Considering a tunnel with H = W = 10 m and h = 20 m excavated in a rock mass with D = 0.5, K = 0.6, and γ = 20 kN/m3, Fig. 5 compares the results of these two methods. The trend of variation of q in all cases is similar, and a maximum difference of about 28%, 15%, and 8% were seen among the results in Fig. 5a, b and c, respectively. It is important to note that the support pressure obtained from the present paper is larger (better) than that obtained from Yang et al.9 method which is more valuable in practical applications and shows the effectiveness of the approach developed in the current study.

Conclusion

An equation was developed for determining the support pressure of tunnels excavated in weak rock masses obeying the Hoek–Brown failure criterion. The obtained results show that increasing the parameters σci, mi, and GSI decreases the support pressure due to improving the rock mass conditions and getting it closer to the strength of the corresponding intact rock. For large values of the mentioned parameters, the support pressure becomes zero, which means that the tunnel is stable and there is no need to install a support system. Regarding the magnitude of K, since it is variable in different ground conditions, several values in the range of 0.4 to 1.2 were considered. The results show that increasing K increases the required support pressure for tunnel walls while the required support pressure for the tunnel roof decreases. Also, increasing the rock mass unit weight and the tunnel width increases the required support pressure due to greater forces exerted on the tunnel excavation surface from the surrounding rock. The percentage of this increase is more sensible in the high values of K, i.e., K = 1.2.

In very weak rock masses, pre-support methods such as spiling and forepoling may be required to enable stable tunnel excavation prior to the installation of temporary support system. This aspect has been omitted in the present study and is acknowledged as a limitation of the current research. Considering such pre-support techniques in determination of tunnel support pressure could serve as a valuable subject for future research.
