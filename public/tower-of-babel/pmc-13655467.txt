Clinical Practice Recommendations for the Treatment of Candida auris Infection in Korea
Authors: Su-Yeon Lee, Jae-Hoon Ko, Nam Yi Kim, Tae Hyong Kim, Jae Woo Park, In Young Yoo, Mi-Kyung Lee, Seung Gul Lee, Tong Ryoung Jung, Yong Pil Chong, Joon-sik Choi, Chang Young Ha, Dong-Gun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCandida auris is an emerging multidrug-resistant fungal pathogen associated with invasive infections and high mortality. In Korea, C. auris infections have historically been caused predominantly by Clade II isolates; however, recent reports of invasive infections due to Clade I indicate an evolving epidemiology with potential implications for antifungal resistance and treatment strategies. To provide practical guidance for clinicians, we developed evidence-based recommendations for the management of C. auris infections in Korea through review of current international guidelines and available epidemiologic and clinical data, with multidisciplinary expert consensus. Antifungal therapy is not recommended for asymptomatic colonization. Echinocandins are recommended as first-line therapy for suspected or confirmed invasive C. auris infection, whereas amphotericin B deoxycholate is preferred for neonates and young infants. Antifungal susceptibility testing should be performed whenever invasive infection is suspected, although initiation of empirical therapy should not be delayed while awaiting results. Treatment should be reassessed according to susceptibility results, infection sites, and clinical response, with specialist consultation for refractory or complicated infections. These recommendations are based on the currently available evidence and epidemiology, warranting continued surveillance of epidemiologic changes and emerging clinical data.Keywords: Candida auris, Practice guideline, Treatment, Echinocandin, Antifungal susceptibility testing

Background

Candida auris, recently reclassified as Candidozyma auris, is an emerging multidrug-resistant fungal pathogen with limited therapeutic options that causes invasive infections associated with substantial morbidity and mortality [1,2]. The major phylogenetic clades exhibit distinct geographic distributions and differ in their epidemiologic characteristics and antifungal resistance profiles (Table 1) [1,2,3,4]. Since C. auris was first reported in Korea in 2009, sporadic cases have predominantly involved Clade II isolates, which are commonly associated with colonization of the external auditory canal [5]. More recently, however, invasive infections caused by Clade I isolates, including severe multidrug-resistant cases, have been reported [6,7], indicating that the epidemiology of C. auris in Korea is evolving. This shift is clinically important because different clades may exhibit distinct antifungal susceptibility profiles [4]. Although epidemiologic data regarding transmission routes, disease burden, clinical manifestations, and antifungal resistance patterns in Korea remain limited, the increasing emergence of invasive disease highlights the need for practical guidance to support timely diagnosis and appropriate antifungal therapy. Given the multidrug-resistant nature of C. auris, particularly its high rates of azole resistance, echinocandins are recommended as empirical first-line therapy, consistent with current international recommendations [8,9,10,11]. These recommendations are intended to provide standardized guidance for the management of C. auris infections, particularly in healthcare settings where infectious diseases expertise may not be readily available. The initial recommendations were drafted based on relevant international guidelines and Korean epidemiological and clinical literature identified through a targeted PubMed search conducted from database inception through January 2026 using the terms “Candida auris” AND “Korea”. They were subsequently refined through iterative review and discussion by a multidisciplinary expert panel until consensus was reached. Although they primarily reflect the Korean healthcare setting, the general treatment principles may also be applicable to similar settings. As the epidemiology and antifungal susceptibility patterns of C. auris continue to evolve, these recommendations should be revised periodically to incorporate emerging clinical evidence. This document was finalized on March 24, 2026, and was developed in consultation with, and endorsed by, relevant academic societies and professional organizations, including the Korean Society of Infectious Diseases, Korean Society of Pediatric Infectious Diseases, Korean Convalescent Hospital Association, Korean Society of Clinical Microbiology, Korean Society for Laboratory Medicine, and Korean Society of Antimicrobial Therapy. Recently published guidance from the World Health Organization, the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the UK Health Security Agency, and European Confederation for Medical Mycology were reviewed and considered in the development of the document [8,9,10,11]. A concise Korean-language brochure summarizing these recommendations for healthcare professionals is available for download from the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency website [12], and an English-language version is provided as Supplementary Material. Given the continued widespread use of the name C. auris in clinical and public health settings, the former nomenclature is retained throughout these recommendations to minimize confusion in clinical practice. The terminology will be updated once the revised nomenclature is broadly adopted by the international scientific community and relevant professional societies.

Indications for antifungal therapy and initial treatment recommendations

C. auris, like other Candida species, may colonize the human body without causing clinical infection. Antifungal therapy is not recommended when C. auris is isolated from the urinary tract, skin, external auditory canal, upper respiratory tract, or other nonsterile sites in the absence of clinical signs or symptoms of infection [11]. However, antifungal therapy should be promptly initiated when invasive infection is suspected or when compatible clinical manifestations are present. This is particularly important in patients with confirmed or strongly suspected invasive C. auris infections, including candidemia, infective endocarditis, abscesses, or osteomyelitis. Whenever feasible, treatment decisions should be made in consultation with an infectious diseases specialist.

Echinocandins are recommended as first-line therapy for invasive C. auris infections based on the current epidemiology and antifungal susceptibility patterns in Korea. However, because echinocandins have limited penetration into the urinary tract and central nervous system, the choice of antifungal agent should be guided by the site of infection. Initial treatment with an echinocandin is recommended for adults and children aged ≥2 months, though the recommended age for pediatric patients varies from ≥1 to ≥4 months depending on the specific agent. For neonates and infants aged <2 months, amphotericin B deoxycholate (1 mg/kg/day) is recommended. Although recent international guidelines include liposomal amphotericin B and echinocandins as alternative treatment options for neonates [11], amphotericin B deoxycholate is preferred in this document because echinocandins are not approved in Korea for neonates or young infants, amphotericin B deoxycholate is reported to be less nephrotoxic in neonates and young infants [13], and a previous observational study reported higher mortality with lipid formulations of amphotericin B than with amphotericin B deoxycholate in neonates [14,15]. Antifungal therapy should be reassessed during treatment according to antifungal susceptibility testing (AFST) results, the site of infection, and the patient's clinical response. When appropriate, therapy may be modified or switched to an alternative antifungal agent, including amphotericin B formulations. Antifungal susceptibility testing and treatment considerations

AFST should be performed in patients with suspected invasive C. auris infection because antifungal resistance is common [8,9]. Empiric antifungal therapy should not be delayed while awaiting AFST results. Once susceptibility results become available, antifungal therapy should be reassessed accordingly, particularly in patients with a suboptimal or delayed clinical response. Laboratory methods and interpretation of AFST results should be guided by the recommendations of the US CDC and other relevant professional guidelines [16,17,18].

Antifungal monotherapy is the standard treatment for C. auris infection [11]. In particular, uncomplicated candidemia without evidence of metastatic organ involvement can generally be managed effectively with a single antifungal agent. In patients with C. auris candidemia, removal of central venous catheters should be considered whenever clinically feasible. A dilated ophthalmologic examination should also be considered to evaluate for ocular involvement, and follow-up blood cultures should be obtained until clearance of fungemia is documented. The duration of antifungal therapy should be individualized according to the site of infection, the patient's immune status, clinical response, and the timing of documented blood culture clearance. For candidemia without metastatic complications, antifungal therapy should be continued for at least 2 weeks after the first documented negative blood culture, provided that clinical signs and symptoms have resolved [11]. For patients with a poor clinical response, persistent candidemia, confirmed or strongly suspected antifungal resistance, or severe or complicated infections (e.g., central nervous system infection), combination antifungal therapy or alternative antifungal agents may be considered in consultation with an infectious diseases specialist.

Limitations and future directions

This document was developed based on currently available international guidelines and the best available evidence at the time of writing. As new evidence emerges and the epidemiology of C. auris continues to evolve, these recommendations should be interpreted in the context of changing clinical circumstances. Although not yet available in Korea, several novel antifungal agents, including ibrexafungerp and fosmanogepix, have shown promising activity against C. auris and may expand future therapeutic options, particularly for infections caused by multidrug-resistant isolates. This document will be updated as needed to reflect newly available antifungal agents, evolving epidemiology, and changes in antifungal resistance patterns.
