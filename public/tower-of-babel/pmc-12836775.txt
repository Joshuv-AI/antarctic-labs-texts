Effect of fiber reinforcement on the fracture resistance of bulk-fill composite restorations in endodontically treated premolars: an in vitro study
Authors: Ismail Ozkocak, Fatma Aytac Bal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis in vitro study aimed to evaluate the fracture resistance of endodontically treated premolars restored with different bulk-fill composites with and without polyethylene fiber reinforcement.MethodsA total of 144 extracted premolars were prepared with standardized mesococclusodistal cavities and allocated to 12 groups (n = 12). Root canal treatment was performed in all but the intact control group. Restorations were carried out via a nanohybrid composite (Filtek Z250) or one of four bulk-fill composites (SDR, SonicFill 2, Tetric EvoCeram Bulk Fill, Filtek Bulk Fill Posterior), each with or without polyethylene fiber reinforcement. After 24 h of water storage at 37 °C, the samples were loaded axially with a steel sphere until fracture. Fracture resistance values (mean ± standard deviation, N) were recorded, and the data were analyzed via one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s test (α = 0.05).ResultsIntact teeth presented the highest fracture resistance (1278.82 ± 124.19 N), whereas unrestored teeth presented the lowest fracture resistance (255.88 ± 59.17 N). Among the restored groups, Filtek Bulk Fill Posterior (996.77 ± 55.90 N) provided the greatest resistance, approaching intact values. Fiber reinforcement did not consistently increase fracture resistance and significantly reduced it in the Filtek Bulk Fill Posterior group (p < 0.05).ConclusionsWithin the limitations of this in vitro study, high-viscosity bulk-fill composites, particularly Filtek Bulk Fill Posterior, effectively restored the fracture resistance of premolars to levels approaching intact teeth. The routine use of polyethylene fiber reinforcement does not provide consistent benefits and may compromise performance.Keywords: Bulk-Fill composite resins, Endodontically treated teeth, Fiber reinforcement, Fracture resistance, Maxillary premolars

Background

The structural integrity of endodontically treated teeth (ETT) is critical for long-term clinical success, particularly in maxillary premolars, which are prone to fracture owing to their narrow crown morphology and the weakening effect of mesio-occluso-distal (MOD) cavity preparations [1–3].

Adhesive composite restorations are commonly used to rehabilitate ETT, yet the conventional incremental technique is time-consuming and technique-sensitive, predisposing restorations to interfacial defects and incomplete polymerization [4, 5]. Bulk-fill composites, which allow placement in increments of up to 4–5 mm, were introduced to overcome these limitations. They offer improved depth of cure, reduced polymerization shrinkage stress, and enhanced clinical efficiency, with favorable clinical and laboratory outcomes reported [4, 6, 7].

Fiber reinforcement, particularly with polyethylene and glass fibers, has also been proposed to improve the biomechanical behavior of structurally compromised teeth [8, 9]. However, evidence remains inconsistent: while some studies report improved fracture resistance, others indicate minimal or even adverse effects depending on fiber type, bonding capacity, and placement technique [10–12].

Given these discrepancies, the combined use of bulk-fill composites and fiber reinforcement remains controversial, especially in MOD-prepared premolars that are subjected to high occlusal stresses. Further investigation is needed to clarify whether polyethylene fiber reinforcement provides additional mechanical benefits when it is used with bulk-fill composites.

The null hypothesis of this study was that neither the type of composite resin (conventional nanohybrid or bulk-fill) nor the use of polyethylene fiber reinforcement would significantly influence the fracture resistance of endodontically treated maxillary premolars.

Methods

Specimen selection and preparation

A total of 144 extracted, intact, noncarious, single-rooted human maxillary premolars of similar dimensions were selected. All teeth were extracted for orthodontic or periodontal reasons, unrelated to this research. After extraction, the teeth were stored in 0.1% thymol solution at room temperature until use. All the samples were examined under 10× magnification with a stereomicroscope (Stemi 2000-C, Carl Zeiss MicroImaging, Göttingen, Germany) to exclude cracks or structural defects. Teeth with similar crown dimensions (± 10% variation in buccolingual and mesiodistal widths) were selected to ensure standardization. Measurements were performed using a digital caliper. All root canal treatments were performed by a single experienced endodontist (IO), and all cavity preparations and restorative procedures were carried out by a single operator (FAB) to standardize the protocol and eliminate inter-operator variability. Standardized MOD cavities were prepared with an isthmus width of 3 mm, depth of 5 mm, and remaining wall thickness of 2.5 mm using a high-speed flat-end fissure diamond bur (#856, Komet, Germany). Endodontic access was performed with a round diamond bur (#801, Komet, Germany). All procedures were completed under copious water cooling. The remaining 12 teeth served as the intact control.

Root canal treatment

The root canals were prepared with ProTaper Universal rotary instruments (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) up to size F3. Irrigation was performed with 2 mL of 5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) after each file. The final irrigation consisted of 5 mL of 5% NaOCl, 5 mL of 15% EDTA (Imicryl, Konya, Turkey), and 5 mL of 5% NaOCl. Canals were dried with paper points and obturated with gutta-percha and an epoxy resin–based sealer (2Seal, VDW, Munich, Germany). The orifices were sealed with a 1 mm-thick layer of glass ionomer (Ionofil U, Voco, Cuxhaven, Germany). The samples were stored at 37 °C and 100% humidity for seven days to allow complete sealer setting before restoration.

Storage and fracture resistance testing

After restoration, the samples were stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h. No thermocycling or mechanical loading was applied to evaluate the initial fracture resistance without the influence of aging effects. Each tooth was embedded vertically in self-curing acrylic resin up to 2 mm below the cementoenamel junction using cylindrical molds to ensure standardized positioning. Fracture resistance was measured in a universal testing machine (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) by applying an axial compressive load with a 5-mm stainless-steel sphere at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min until failure. The maximum load at fracture (N) was recorded.

Statistical analysis

Normality was tested with the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity was tested with Levene’s test. The data were analyzed via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. The level of significance was set at α = 0.05. The effect size (η²) was calculated. An a priori power analysis (G*Power 3.1.9.7) determined that a minimum of 12 specimens per group (total N = 144) would provide > 0.80 statistical power to detect medium effect sizes (f = 0.25) at α = 0.05.

Results

The intact teeth (INT) presented the highest fracture resistance (1278.82 ± 124.19 N; mean ± standard deviation (SD)), whereas the endodontically treated but unrestored teeth (UR) presented the lowest fracture resistance (255.88 ± 59.17 N; p < 0.001). Compared with the plants in UR group, those in the restored groups presented significantly greater resistance.

Among the restored groups, Filtek Bulk Fill Posterior (FBP) demonstrated the highest resistance (996.77 ± 55.90 N), which was significantly lower than that of intact teeth but closest to their performance. The lowest resistance among the restored groups was observed in Filtek Z250 (Z250) (3: 635.18 ± 83.12 N), whereas SDR base + Filtek Z250 occlusal (SDR-Z250), SonicFill 2 (SF2), and Tetric EvoCeram Bulk Fill (TEC) presented intermediate values.

Polyethylene fiber reinforcement had an inconsistent effect. Its addition significantly reduced fracture resistance in the FBP group (Group 12: 759.83 ± 68.43 N vs. Group 11: 996.77 ± 55.90 N; p < 0.05). For the other composites (Z250 group compared with Z250-F group, SDR group compared with SDR-F group, and TEC group compared with TEC-F group), no significant differences were observed.

One-way ANOVA confirmed significant differences among the groups (p < 0.001), with a large effect size (η² = 0.88), indicating that 88% of the variance in fracture resistance was explained by the type of restoration. Discussion

The present study confirmed that, compared with a conventional nanohybrid composite, bulk-fill composites demonstrated superior mechanical performance. Given the significant differences observed among the restorative materials and between fiber-reinforced and non-reinforced groups, the null hypothesis stating that neither composite type nor fiber reinforcement would affect fracture resistance was rejected. This finding is consistent with previous in vitro evidence attributing the improved behavior of bulk-fill systems to optimized filler loading, reduced polymerization shrinkage, and more favorable stress distribution [13]. Among the tested materials, FBP group exhibited the highest fracture resistance, performing closer to intact teeth than the other restorative protocols did. These results support earlier reports that high-viscosity bulk-fill composites are particularly advantageous for posterior teeth under heavy occlusal loading [14, 15]. Previous studies have demonstrated that bulk-fill composites differ considerably in their mechanical behavior, with high-viscosity formulations showing superior stress distribution under occlusal loading [13–15]. Our results align with these findings, as Filtek Bulk Fill Posterior outperformed the other materials, consistent with reports by Hegde and Sali [14] and Al-Nahedh and Alawami [15]. Conversely, the inconsistent effect of polyethylene fibers agrees with the heterogeneous outcomes reported in systematic reviews, which emphasize technique sensitivity and placement-dependent performance [16–18]. Some studies reported improved fracture resistance with glass or polyethylene fibers [8, 9], whereas others demonstrated neutral or even adverse effects depending on fiber orientation, polymerization, or bonding limitations [11, 12]. Such discrepancies may explain why fiber reinforcement significantly reduced the performance of Filtek Bulk Fill Posterior in the present study, suggesting that fiber placement may interfere with light transmission or stress distribution, as also discussed by Garoushi et al. [18].

This study also provided one of the few direct comparisons of multiple bulk-fill composites with and without polyethylene fiber reinforcement in standardized MOD-prepared maxillary premolars. While bulk-fill materials consistently improved fracture resistance, polyethylene fibers produced inconsistent outcomes, sometimes significantly reducing resistance. This observation underscores the technique sensitivity of fiber reinforcement and highlights the need for further research to establish protocols that optimize its clinical effectiveness [16, 17]. The unexpected reduction in resistance contrasts with the theoretical benefits of fiber reinforcement, which are thought to derive from enhanced crack deflection and toughness [8]. The inconsistent performance of polyethylene fibers observed in the present study aligns with previous findings reporting technique-dependent or unfavorable effects depending on fiber location, hydrophobicity, impaired interfacial bonding, and reduced light transmission beneath the fiber layer [11, 12, 18, 19]. Possible explanations include the hydrophobic nature of polyethylene fibers and impaired polymerization beneath the fiber layer due to reduced light transmission [11, 12, 18].

Not all the bulk-fill composites performed equally. Compared with high-viscosity systems, the flowable SDR composite exhibited inferior resistance, which can be attributed to its lower filler loading and reduced modulus of elasticity [19]. These findings emphasize that bulk-fill composites do not form a homogeneous category and that high-viscosity formulations are better suited for posterior restorations subjected to high occlusal demands. Moreover, the efficiency of fiber reinforcement depends strongly on its position and orientation, with recent studies showing more favorable results when fibers are placed closer to the occlusal surface or combined with cusp reduction [20, 21]. Additional factors, such as adequate polymerization and surface pretreatment of fibers, also appear critical [22].

Although all tested restorative strategies provided fracture resistance values above average physiological masticatory forces [23, 24], high-viscosity bulk-fill composites clearly demonstrated superiority. From a clinical perspective, the present results suggest that high-viscosity bulk-fill composites should be preferred when restoring endodontically treated maxillary premolars exposed to high occlusal loads. Polyethylene fiber reinforcement, although theoretically beneficial, does not consistently increase fracture resistance and may even reduce fracture resistance in certain configurations. Therefore, its application should be restricted to carefully selected clinical scenarios and applied under well-controlled protocols [16, 18]. As all tested restorations exceeded functional thresholds, failures in practice are more likely to result from fatigue or parafunctional stresses, underscoring the importance of patient-specific occlusal evaluation in treatment planning [23–25]. These recommendations are consistent with clinical evidence showing satisfactory long-term performance of bulk-fill composites in posterior teeth [25, 26] and with randomized clinical trial results confirming their reliability in Class II restorations [27].

This in vitro study presents several methodological limitations that should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings. First, only static load-to-fracture testing was performed, without the use of thermocycling, mechanical fatigue loading, or long-term aging procedures. The absence of such protocols limits the extrapolation of the results to the dynamic and thermomechanical conditions of the oral environment. In addition, axial compressive loading was used, whereas oblique forces may more accurately represent functional masticatory loads.

The study also did not evaluate fracture modes; therefore, distinctions between restorable (supracervical) and non-restorable (subcervical) failures could not be identified. This restricts the clinical interpretability of the fracture resistance values, particularly for fiber-reinforced groups, where differences in crack propagation patterns may be expected.

Periodontal ligament simulation was not included, which may have altered stress distribution during loading. Only one type of polyethylene fiber and a single placement orientation were tested, and no assessment was made regarding the polymerization efficiency beneath the fiber layer—an aspect that may influence its mechanical contribution due to potential light attenuation.

Furthermore, the restorative and fiber-reinforcement techniques were limited to specific materials and protocols, and the findings can not necessarily be generalized to other bulk-fill systems, adhesive strategies, or fiber designs. Although teeth were standardized dimensionally, natural anatomical variability may still have influenced fracture behavior.

Considering these limitations, future research should incorporate cyclic fatigue loading, thermal aging, periodontal ligament simulation, evaluation of multiple fiber orientations and materials, and detailed fracture mode classification to enhance clinical relevance.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, high-viscosity bulk-fill composites demonstrated superior fracture resistance compared with conventional nanohybrid composites. FBP was performed most closely to intact teeth, supporting its reliability for restoring endodontically treated premolars. Polyethylene fiber reinforcement did not provide consistent benefits and, in some cases, reduced fracture resistance, emphasizing its technique-sensitive nature. Therefore, high-viscosity bulk-fill composites can be recommended as the preferred restorative option in clinical practice, whereas fiber reinforcement should be reserved for carefully selected and well-controlled applications.
