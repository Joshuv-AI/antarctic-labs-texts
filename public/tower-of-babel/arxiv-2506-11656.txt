Abstract
. In this paper we prove existence and uniqueness of energy solutionns for singular problems with absorption driven by local-nonlocal operators. Moreover, we establish a comparison principle à la Talenti, leading to a gain of summability result for the solutions of these problems.

1.
Introduction

Local-nonlocal operators have been studied in connection with the validity of maximum principles since the ’60s, see e.g.
[
23
,
28
]
. In the last twenty years there has been a restored interest thanks to the their natural appearance as stochastic processes combining a Brownian motion and a Lévy-type process, see e.g.
[
33
]
and the references therein. In this paper we are interested in working with the simplest possible realization of such operators, namely

\mathcal{L}:=-\Delta+(-\Delta)^{s},\quad s\in(0,1),

where
(-\Delta)^{s}
denotes the fractional Laplacian. We note that the above operator falls in the general framework considered in
[
22
]
thanks to the Green function estimates proved in
[
33
]
. Despite being linear, the operator
\mathcal{L}
exhibits a natural lack of homogeneity which may cause significant differences w.r.t. to
-\Delta
or
(-\Delta)^{s}
: for example, this is the case in critical problems, see e.g.
[
10
,
12
,
13
]
.
In the last five years, a more analytical approach towards regularity properties of
\mathcal{L}
(and its quasilinear variants) has been developed, see e.g.
[
9
,
1
,
46
,
47
,
37
,
64
,
16
,
26
,
39
,
3
]
. We stress in particular that in
[
65
]
it has been recently showed that natural
C^{2,\alpha}
Schauder estimates up to the boundary may fail. We finally mention that operators like
\mathcal{L}
may appear in biomathematical models, see e.g.,
[
40
,
41
]
and the references therein.

We are now ready to describe the problem we are interested in.
Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open and bounded set with smooth enough boundary
\partial\Omega
.
We consider the following local-nonlocal problem:

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathcal{L}u+g(u)=h(u)f&\textrm{ in }\Omega,\\
u>0&\textrm{ in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\textrm{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega.\end{array}\right.

To be more precise, we are interested in proving existence of finite energy solution to (
1.1
) according to the summability of
f
. We postpone for the moment the precise assumptions on the semilinear terms
g
and
h
appearing in (
1.1
).

The study of regularity properties of solutions to elliptic PDEs depending on the summability of the source datum is a pretty classical subject that dates back to results by Calderon and Zygmund
[
27
]
and Stampacchia
[
62
]
. The first classical instance is provided by the classical Dirichlet problem

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\Delta u=f(x)&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

with
f\in L^{m}(\Omega)
for some
m\geq 1
. In particular, see also
[
18
]
, the following holds:

•

if
m>\tfrac{n}{2}
, then
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
;

•

if
\tfrac{2n}{n+2}\leq m\leq\tfrac{n}{2}
, then
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\cap L^{m^{\ast\ast}}(\Omega)
with
m^{\ast\ast}:=\tfrac{nm}{n-2m}
;

•

if
1<m<\tfrac{2n}{n+2}
, then
u\in W^{1,m^{\ast}}_{0}(\Omega)
with
m^{\ast}:=\tfrac{nm}{n-m}
;

•

if
m=1
, then
u\in W^{1,t}_{0}(\Omega)
for every
t<\tfrac{n}{n-1}
.

We stress that the notion of solution has to be modified accordingly to the case considered. We refer to
[
59
]
for a more general singular term and we also refer to
[
53
]
for the appropriate analogous results in the fractional case, and to
[
5
]
for the local-nonlocal case.

In
[
20
]
, Boccardo and Orsina considered the following singular problem

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\Delta u=\dfrac{f(x)}{u^{\gamma}}&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

with
0\leq f\in L^{m}(\Omega)
for some
m\geq 1
and with
\gamma>0
. They proved that the solution has an improvement in terms of Sobolev regularity w.r.t. the nonsigular case
\gamma=0
. Precisely, they proved that

•

if
\gamma=1
and
m=1
, then
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
;

•

if
\gamma<1
and
m=\tfrac{2n}{n(1+\gamma)+2(1-\gamma)}
, then
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
;

•

if
\gamma<1
and
m<\tfrac{2n}{n(1+\gamma)+2(1-\gamma)}
, then
u\in W^{1,t}_{0}(\Omega)
with
t=\tfrac{nm(1+\gamma)}{n-m(1-\gamma)}
.

We omit here the details concerning the case
\gamma>1
because this is out of our interest, since this leads to solutions with possibly infinite energy. Once again, similar results have already been proved in
[
7
,
69
]
for the fractional case and in
[
5
]
in the local-nonlocal case. We notice here that other singular perturbations, e.g. with Hardy-type potentials, lead to quite different results, see
[
21
]
and
[
11
]
.

A similar regularizing effect in terms of Sobolev regularity can also be obtained perturbing (
1.2
) with a power-type absorption term as in
[
19
,
34
]
. Just to give a glance at the situation occurring in presence of an absorption term, we recall that if
f\in L^{1}(\Omega)
and
q>\tfrac{n}{n-2}
, there exists a solution
u
to

(1.4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\Delta u+u^{q}=f(x)&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

such that
u\in W^{1,t}_{0}(\Omega)
for every
t<\tfrac{2q}{q+1}
, yielding an improvement w.r.t. the solution of (
1.2
). It is worth to stress that we are not aware of any other contribution in this direction neither in the purely nonlocal case, nor in the local-nonlocal one.

It is therefore natural to wonder whether the combination of both the regularizing effects, i.e. absorption and singular term, may provide an even stronger regularization. This question has been addressed in
[
36
]
and then extended to more general absorption and singular terms in the case of the
p
-Laplacian in
[
57
]
. We notice that Oliva in
[
57
]
considered also the case
\gamma>1
which leads to solutions with locally finite energy. We will not deal with this case in this paper.
Our interest is therefore only to find energy solutions to (
1.1
).

Let us now move back to problem (
1.1
), and let us begin
by fixing all the
standing assumptions
on the
absorption term
g
, on the
singular term
h
and on
f
.

Standing assumptions:

Throughout what follows, we make
the following
structural assumptions
on the functions
g,h
and
f

involved in problem (
1.1
).

(H)
h

h:[0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty]
is continuous, possibly singular, finite outside the origin and such that
h(0)\neq 0
. Moreover,

(h1)

\exists\,\,\gamma\in[0,1],\,\underline{C},\,\underline{s}>0:h(s)\leq\dfrac{\underline{C}}{s^{\gamma}},\textrm{ for every }s\leq\underline{s},

and

(h2)

\exists\,\,\theta\geq 0,\,\overline{C},\,\overline{s}>\underline{s}:h(s)\leq\dfrac{\overline{C}}{s^{\theta}},\textrm{ for every }s\geq\overline{s}.

(H)
f

f:\Omega\to[0,+\infty)
is a measurable function such that

(1.5)

|\{x\in\Omega:\,f(x)=0\}|=0\quad\textrm{for }1/2<s<1,

(where
|A|
denotes the Lebesgue measure of a measurable set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
);
moreover, we suppose that

\mathrm{i)}\,\,\text{$f\in L^{1}(\Omega)$ if $\theta\geq 1$}\quad\text{or}\quad\mathrm{ii)}\,\,\text{$f\in L^{m}(\Omega)$ for some $m>1$ if $\theta<1$}.

(H)
g

g:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
is a continuous function such that
g(0)=0
; moreover, in the case when
\theta<1
, we further suppose that

(g1)

\exists\,\,q\geq\dfrac{1-m\theta}{m-1},\,\,\exists\,\,\nu,s_{1}>0:g(s)\geq\nu\,s^{q},\textrm{ for every }s\geq s_{1}.

We notice that the choices
h(s)=s^{-\gamma}
(with
\gamma\geq 0
),
g(s)=s^{q}
are both admissible, provided that
q
satisfies the following bound

q\geq\frac{1-m\gamma}{m-1}

(as, in this case, we have
\theta=\gamma
). With this choice of

g
and
h
, problem (
1.1
) reduces to the following one,
which we will call the
model problem
:

(1.6)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\Delta u+(-\Delta)^{s}u+u^{q}=\dfrac{f(x)}{u^{\gamma}}&\textrm{ in }\Omega,\\
u>0&\textrm{ in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\textrm{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega,\end{array}\right.

Before entering into the details of the content of the paper, let us introduce the notion of solution we will work with.

Definition 1.1
.

Suppose that (H)
h
, (H)
f
and (H)
g
hold. A function
u\in W^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a
distributional solution
of problem (
1.1
) if

i)

u|_{\Omega}\in W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega)
and
u\equiv 0
a.e. on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
;

ii)

u>0
in
\Omega
;

iii)

g(u),h(u)f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
;

iv)

for every
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
it holds that

(1.7)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx

\displaystyle+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{(u(x)-u(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dxdy+\int_{\Omega}g(u)\varphi\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}h(u)f\varphi\,dx.

While we refer to Section
2
for a detailed discussion of the well-posedness of the above definition (and for the introduction of an adequate functional setting for the operator
\mathcal{L}
), here we limit ourselves to point out that the need of a
globally defined
function

u
in Definition
1.1
is motivated by the non-local nature of
\mathcal{L}
.

On the other hand, the assumed
global regularity
of
u

(namely,
u\in W^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
) is a simple consequence of the fact that

\text{($u|_{\Omega}\in W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega)$ and $u\equiv 0$ a.e.\,in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$})\,\,\Longrightarrow\,\,u\in W^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

We are now ready to state or main results: the first is an existence result, while the second concerns the uniqueness of the solutions.

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that
(H)
h
,
(H)
f
and
(H)
g
hold. Then, there exists a solution
u

(
in the sense of Definition
1.1
)
of (
1.1
) such that

u|_{\Omega}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\quad\textrm{and}\quad g(u)u\in L^{1}(\Omega).

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose that
(H)
h
,
(H)
f
and
(H)
g
hold. Moreover, assume that

i)

h
is non increasing;

ii)

g
is non decreasing.

Then there exists at most one solution (in the sense of Definition
1.1
) of (
1.1
) such that

u|_{\Omega}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\quad\textrm{and}\quad g(u)\in L^{1}(\Omega).

We explicitly highlight that, by applying Theorems
1.2
-
1.3

to the
model problem
(
1.6
),
we obtain the following
existence and uniqueness
result.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
0\leq\gamma\leq 1
, and let
f
satisfy

(H)
f
(with
\theta=\gamma
). According to assumption
(H)
g
, let
q\in\mathbb{R}
be such that

q\geq\max\{0,q_{\gamma,m}\},\quad\text{where $q_{\gamma,m}=\frac{1-m\gamma}{m-1}$}

(with the convention that
q_{m,\gamma}=-1
if
\gamma=1
).

Then, there exists a unique solution
u
of problem (
1.6
) such that

u|_{\Omega}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\quad\text{and}\quad u\in L^{q+1}(\Omega).

Let us now comment on the proof of Theorem
1.2
. We follow a quite classical scheme as already performed in
[
57
]
with a few significant issues. We first consider a family of approximating problems (see (
3.2
)). Usually, the existence of a weak solution to such problems descend from classical Leray-Lions theory
[
54
]
, which cannot be used for free in our case due to the presence of the nonlocal term. This is the reason why we show that the functional analytic properties of the operator
\mathcal{L}
are anyway enough to mimic the classical Leray-Lions existence theorem, see Theorem
2.6
. The next step in the approximation scheme consist in proving suitable apriori estimates, and this is done in Lemma
3.2
. The final step is a limiting procedure. Most of the terms can be treated as in
[
57
]
except for one, see (
3.32
). In order to show the validity of (
3.32
), we test the equation with a suitable function and therefore the nonlocal part comes heavily into play. We stress here that this approach has never been used in the purely nonlocal case and indeed we reach the desired conclusion with the help of an interpolation inequality which work only because of the presence of the local term. We also stress that in the case
s\in(1/2,1)
we also need the assumption (
1.5
) to let the argument work.

It is worth to spend a further comment on the line of proof we chose to follow. In
[
36
]
, the authors avoided the use of the test-function argument described before, by using a strong maximum principle for distributional solutions proved in
[
68
]
. An analogous result is currently not available for local-nonlocal operators. Nevertheless, we showed in the Appendix
A
that paying a price on the growth of the absortion term, and asking for more regularity on the datum
f
, we can deal with classical solutions and then prove a strong maximum principle. We stress that the at most critical growth asked on the absorption term is kind of natural if one looks for classical solutions, at least in the case of
-\Delta
, thanks to a famous result by Brezis and Kato
[
25
]
.

The last result of the paper concern the model problem (
1.6
). In this case, by adapting an idea found in
[
24
]
, we first establish a pointwise comparison à la Talenti and then we use it to provide a gain of summability of the solutions which is the content of the following

Theorem 1.5
.

Let the assumptions of Corollary
1.4
be satisfied, and let
u
be the unique solution of problem (
1.6
).
Then, the following assertions hold.

(
i
)

if
1<m<\frac{n}{2}
, then we have

(1.8)

\begin{split}\|u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}&\leq\left(\frac{\gamma+1}{n^{2}\omega_{n}^{2/n}}\right)^{\gamma+1}\left(\frac{n(m-1)}{n-2m}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{p(\gamma+1)}}\times\\
&\qquad\times\left(\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2m}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{nm-n+2m}{2m}\right)}\right)^{\frac{2}{n(\gamma+1)}}\|f\|_{L^{m}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}},\end{split}

where
p=\frac{nm(\gamma+1)}{n-2m}
;

(
ii
)

if
m>\frac{n}{2}
, then we have

(1.9)

\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\left(\frac{\gamma+1}{n^{2}\omega_{n}^{2/n}}\Big[\frac{m}{m-1}\left(\frac{n(m-1)}{2m-n}\right)^{\frac{m-1}{m}}\Big]|\Omega|^{\frac{2m-n}{nm}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}\|f\|_{L^{m}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}\,;

(

)

if

, then

belongs to the Orlicz space

and

(1.10)

\sup_{s\in(0,|\Omega|)}\frac{u^{\ast}(s)}{\left(\log\frac{|\Omega|}{s}\right)^{\frac{n-2}{n(\gamma+1)}}}\leq\left(\frac{\gamma+1}{n^{2}\omega_{n}^{2/n}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}\|f\|_{L^{n/2}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}.

.

Let the assumptions of Corollary
1.4
be satisfied

Taking for granted all the notation listed above, we now turn to spend a few words on the
natural functional setting
associated with the mixed operator

\mathcal{L}=-\Delta+(-\Delta)^{s}.

Actually, since a key ingredient in our approach will be a
Leray-Lions-type
result for
\mathcal{L}
, and since this result naturally applies
to
quasilinear operators
, we consider the quasilinear
generalization of
\mathcal{L}
, that is,

\mathcal{L}_{p}=-\Delta_{p}+(-\Delta)_{p}^{s},

where
-\Delta_{p}u=\mathrm{div}(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u)
is the usual
p
-Laplace operator.

1) The fractional
p
-Laplacian.
Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and
s\in(0,1)
be fixed

once and for all
. If
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function, the
fractional
p
-Laplacian
(of order
s
) of
u

at a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined (up to a

normalization constant

C_{n,s,p}>0
that we neglect, and setting
B_{p}(a)=|a|^{p-2}a
) as follows

\displaystyle(-\Delta)_{p}^{s}u(x)

\displaystyle=2\,\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{B_{p}(u(x)-u(y))}{|x-y|^{n+ps}}\,dy

\displaystyle=2\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\{|x-y|\geq\varepsilon\}}\frac{B_{p}(u(x)-u(y))}{|x-y|^{n+ps}}\,dy,

provided that the above limit
exists and is finite
.
As it is reasonable to expect, for
(-\Delta)_{p}^{s}u(x)
to be well-defined one needs
to impose suitable
growth conditions
on the functions
u
, both when
y\to\infty
and
when
y\to x
.
In this perspective we have the following
proposition, where we employ the notation

(2.6)

{L}^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):=\Big\{f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\,\|f\|_{p,s}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|f(x)|^{p-1}}{1+|x|^{n+ps}}\,dx<\infty\Big\}.

Proposition 2.1

(See
[
56
, Lemmas 3.6 and 3.8]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set, and
let
u\in\mathcal{L}^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C^{2}(\mathcal{O})
.
Then, if we assume that
sp<p-1
, we have

\exists\,\,(-\Delta)_{p}^{s}u(x)=2\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{B_{p}(u(x)-u(y))}{|x-y|^{n+ps}}\,dy\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathcal{O}$}.

Moreover,
(-\Delta)_{p}^{s}u\in C(\mathcal{O})
.

Even if Proposition
2.1
provides an easy
sufficient condition for the well-posedness of the
pointwise definition

of
(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}u(x)
, it is well-known
that the develop of a
pointwise theory
for (local or nonlocal)
quasilinear operators presents intrinsic difficulties
(in particular when
1<p<2
); for this reason,
we now review some basic
facts concerning the
Weak Theory
of
(-\Delta)_{p}^{s}
.

To begin with we recall that, if
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an arbitrary open set,
the fractional
p
-Laplacian
(-\Delta)_{p}^{s}
is related
(essentially via the Euler-Lagrange equation)
to the
fractional Sobolev space

W^{s,p}(\mathcal{O})
, which is defined as follows:

W^{s,p}(\mathcal{O}):=\Big\{u\in L^{p}(\mathcal{O}):\,[u]^{p}_{p,s,\mathcal{O}}=\iint_{\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{O}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{n+ps}}\,dx\,dy<\infty\Big\}.

While we refer to
[
52
]
for a detailed introduction
on fractional Sobolev spaces, here we list the few basic properties of
W^{s,p}(\mathcal{O})

we will exploit in this paper.

a)

W^{s,p}(\mathcal{O})
is a real Banach space, with the norm

\|u\|_{s,p,\mathcal{O}}:=\Big(\|u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathcal{O})}+\iint_{\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{O}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{n+ps}}\,dx\,dy\Big)^{1/p}\qquad(u\in W^{s,p}(\mathcal{O})).

b)

C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})
is a
linear subspace of
W^{s,p}(\mathcal{O})
; in addition,
in the particular case when
\mathcal{O}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have that

C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is
dense
in
W^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

c)

If
\mathcal{O}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
or if
\mathcal{O}
has
bounded boundary
\partial\mathcal{O}\in C^{0,1}
,
we have the
continuous embedding

W^{1,p}(\mathcal{O})\hookrightarrow W^{s,p}(\mathcal{O})
,
that is,

(2.7)

\iint_{\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{O}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{n+ps}}\,dx\,dy\leq\mathbf{c}\,\|u\|^{p}_{W^{1,p}(\mathcal{O})}\quad\text{$\forall\,\,u\in W^{1,p}(\mathcal{O})$},

for some constant
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{c}(n,p,s)>0
.
In particular, if
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a

bounded open set
(with no regularity assumptions on
\partial\mathcal{O}
) and
if
u\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\mathcal{O})
, setting
\hat{u}=u\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{O}}\in W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we have

(2.8)

\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{|\hat{u}(x)-\hat{u}(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{n+ps}}\,dx\,dy\leq\beta\,\int_{\mathcal{O}}|\nabla u|^{p}\,dx,

where
\beta>0
is a suitable constant depending on
n,s
and on
|\Omega|
. Here
and throughout,
|\cdot|
denotes the
n
-dimensional Lebesgue measure.

As usual, we also say that
u\in W^{s,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{O})
if

\text{$u\in W^{s,p}(\mathcal{V})$ for every open set $\mathcal{V}\Subset\mathcal{O}$}.

The relation between the space
W^{s,p}(\mathcal{O})
and the fractional
p
-Laplacian
(-\Delta)_{p}^{s}

is rooted in the following
fractional integration-by-parts formula
:
assuming for a moment that
sp<p-1
,
let

\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set (bounded or not), and let

u\in{L}^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C^{2}(\mathcal{O});

given any test function
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})
,
we then have

(2.9)

\begin{split}\int_{\mathcal{O}}(-\Delta)_{p}^{s}u\,\varphi\,dx&=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{B_{p}(u(x)-u(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy,\end{split}

and the double integral in the right-hand side of the above
(
2.9
)
is finite

(notice that, since
u\in{L}^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C^{2}(\mathcal{O})

and since
sp<p-1
, from
Proposition
2.1
we
know that
(-\Delta)_{p}^{s}u
is well-defined pointwise in
\mathcal{O}
,
and
(-\Delta)_{p}^{s}u\in C(\mathcal{O})
).

On the other hand, it is not difficult to recognize that
the finitness of such a double integral
is guaranteed
even if
sp\geq p-1
, and if we only assume that

u\in{L}^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap W_{\mathrm{loc}}^{s,p}(\mathcal{O})

(notice that this clearly holds if
u\in{L}^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C^{2}(\mathcal{O})
,
thanks to identity (
2.7
)):
indeed, under this more general assumption,
if
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})
and if
\mathcal{V}\Subset\mathcal{O}
is an open
set such that
K=\mathrm{supp}(\varphi)\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, we have

(2.10)

\begin{split}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}&\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{p-1}|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|}{|x-y|^{n+ps}}\,dx\,dy\\
&\qquad=\iint_{\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{p-1}|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\\
&\qquad\quad-2\,\iint_{(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{V})\times K}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{p-1}|\varphi(y)|}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\\
&\qquad\leq C\big(1+\mathrm{dist}(K,\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{V})^{-ps}\big)\|\varphi\|_{s,p,\mathcal{V}},\end{split}

(here, the constant
C>0
depends on
n,s,p
and on
u
).

As a consequence of this fact, it is thus natural to define the

fractional
p
-Laplacian

(-\Delta)_{p}^{s}u

of a function
u\in{L}^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap W_{\mathrm{loc}}^{s,p}(\mathcal{O})
as the

linear functional
acting on the space
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})
as follows

(2.11)

\begin{split}\langle(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}u,\varphi\rangle&=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{B_{p}(u(x)-u(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{n+ps}}\,dx\,dy.\end{split}

On account of (
2.10
) (and observing that

\|\varphi\|_{s,p,\mathcal{V}}\leq c\,\|\varphi\|_{C^{1}(\overline{\mathcal{V}})}
for some absolute constant
c>0
,
see (
2.8
)),
we easily see that

(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}u\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathcal{O}).

In particular, if
u\in W^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subseteq L^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
by the density of
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

in
W^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we derive that

W_{0}^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=W^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

and thus

(-\Delta)_{p}^{s}u\in(W^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))^{\prime}.

In this case, we also have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big|_{t=0}\Big(\frac{1}{p}\,[u+tv]^{p}_{s,p,\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big)=(-\Delta)_{p}^{s}u(v)\quad\text{for all $v\in W^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$}.

Remark 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set (bounded or not), and let

u\in{L}^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap W_{\mathrm{loc}}^{s,p}(\mathcal{O});

moreover, let
(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}u\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathcal{O})

be the distribution on
\mathcal{O}
defined by formula
(
2.11
).

Given
any bounded open set
\Omega
such that
\Omega\Subset\mathcal{O}
,
by combining
(
2.10
) (where we choose the open set
\mathcal{V}

in such a way that
\Omega\Subset\mathcal{V}\Subset\mathcal{O}
)
with the continuous embedding in (
2.8
), we derive that

\begin{split}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}&\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{p-1}|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|}{|x-y|^{n+ps}}\,dx\,dy\\
&\qquad\leq C\big(1+\mathrm{dist}({\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{V})^{-ps}\big)\Big(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi|^{p}\,dx\Big)^{1/p},\end{split}

for every function
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
(and for some constant
C>0

depending on
n,s,p
and on
u
). As a consequence, we deduce that
(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}u

can be extended to a
linear and continuous operator on
W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
, that is,

(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}u\in W^{-1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega)=\big(W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\big)^{\prime}.

More precisely, we have

\displaystyle\langle(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}u,\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{B_{p}(u(x)-u(y))(\hat{\varphi}(x)-\hat{\varphi}(y))}{|x-y|^{n+ps}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}}\frac{B_{p}(u(x)-u(y))(\hat{\varphi}(x)-\hat{\varphi}(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad\quad-2\,\iint_{(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{V})\times\Omega}\frac{B_{p}(u(x)-u(y))\hat{\varphi}(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy,

for every
\varphi\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
(here we have set, as above,

\hat{\varphi}=\varphi\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}\in W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
).

2) The space
\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega)
.
Now we have properly defined the fractional
p
-Laplacian
of a
non-regular function
(as a distribution),
and taking into account Remark
2.2
, we
introduce a suitable
function space
which will allow us to define an
adequate
Weak Theory
for
\mathcal{L}_{p,s}

(and for the study of problem (
1.1
)).

Let then
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded open set. In view of the usual definition of the (local) Sobolev space
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
,
and taking into account (
2.11
), we
define

\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega)
as the completion
of
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
w.r.t. the
global norm

\rho_{p}(u):=\left(\||\nabla u|\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+[u]^{p}_{s,p,\mathbb{R}^{n}}\right)^{1/p},\qquad u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

Concerning this space
\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega)
we first observe that,
since we assuming
1<p<\infty
, we clearly have that it is a
Banach space
; most importantly, since
\Omega
is bounded, by combining
(
2.7
) with the Poincaré
inequality we infer that

\vartheta^{-1}\|u\|_{W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\rho_{p}(u)\leq\vartheta\|u\|_{W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\qquad\text{for every $u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)$},

where
\vartheta>1
is a suitable constant depending on
n,s,p
and on
|\Omega|
.
Thus,
\rho_{p}(\cdot)
and the full
W^{1,p}
-norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

are
actually equivalent
on the space
C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
, so that

(2.12)

\begin{split}\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega)&=\overline{C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{\,\,\|\cdot\|_{W^{1,p}\mathbb{R}^{n})}}\\
&=\{u\in W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\,\text{$u|_{\Omega}\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)$ and
$u\equiv 0$ a.e.\,in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$}\}.\end{split}

As a consequence, the following properties hold:

(2.13)

\displaystyle(1)\,\,

\displaystyle\text{If $u\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)$, then $u\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega)$};

(2.14)

\displaystyle(2)\,\,

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega)\subseteq W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subseteq L^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap W^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Since throughout this paper we are mainly interested in the
case
p=2
, we collect in the next remark some properties of the space
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
which will be repeatedly used in the sequel.
To avoid cumbersome notation, we simply set

\rho(u)=\rho_{2}(u)\quad\text{and}\quad[u]_{s,\mathbb{R}^{n}}=[u]_{s,2,\mathbb{R}^{n}}.

Remark 2.3

(Properties of the space
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
)
.

The following assertions hold.

1)

Since both
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
are
closed
under the
maximum/minimum operation, it is readily seen that

u^{\pm}\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)\quad\text{for every $u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)$},

where
u_{+}
and
u_{-}
have been defined in (
2.1
).

2)

On account of (
2.8
), for every

u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
we have

(2.15)

[u]_{s,\mathbb{R}^{n}}^{2}=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\leq\beta\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx.

As a consequence,
the norm
\rho
is
globally equivalent
on
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
to the

H_{0}^{1}
-norm: in fact, by (
2.15
) there exists a constant
\Theta=\Theta_{n,s}>0
such that

(2.16)

\||\nabla u|\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\rho(u)\leq\Theta\||\nabla u|\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\quad\text{for every $u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)$}.

3)

By the (local) Sobolev inequality, for every
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)

we have

\displaystyle S_{n}\|u\|_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle=S_{n}\|u\|_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx\leq\rho(u)^{2}.

This, together with Hölder’s inequality
(recall that
\Omega
is
bounded
), proves the
continuous embedding

\text{$\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{m}(\Omega)$ for every $1\leq m\leq 2^{\ast}$}.

4)

By combining (
2.16
) with the
compact embedding
of

H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{m}(\Omega)
(holding true for every
1\leq m<2^{\ast}
),
we derive that also the embedding

\text{$\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{m}(\Omega)$ is compact
for every $1\leq m<2^{\ast}$}.

As a consequence, if
\{u_{k}\}_{k}
is a bounded sequence in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, it is possible
to find a (unique) function
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
such that (up to a sub-sequence)

a)

u_{n}\to u
weakly in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
;

b)

u_{n}\to u

strongly
in
L^{m}(\Omega)
for every
1\leq m<2^{\ast}
;

c)

u_{n}\to u
pointwise a.e. in
\Omega
.

Clearly, since both
u_{n}
(for all
n\geq 1
) and
u

identically vanish

out of
\Omega
, we can replace

\Omega
with
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the above assertions b)-c).

As already anticipated, the space
\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega)
just introduced
is naturally associated with the
weak realization
of
(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}

via Remark
2.2
. Indeed, let
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an arbitrary open set, and let

u\in L^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap W^{s,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{O})
.
If
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
bounded open set
such that

\Omega\Subset\mathcal{O}
, from Remark
2.2

we know that
the distribution

(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}u\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathcal{O})

can be
extended to a linear and continuous operator on
W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
by setting

\displaystyle\langle(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}u,\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{B_{p}(u(x)-u(y))(\hat{\varphi}(x)-\hat{\varphi}(y))}{|x-y|^{n+ps}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}}\frac{B_{p}(u(x)-u(y))(\hat{\varphi}(x)-\hat{\varphi}(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad\quad-2\,\iint_{(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{V})\times\Omega}\frac{B_{p}(u(x)-u(y))\hat{\varphi}(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy,

where

\hat{\varphi}=\varphi\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}\in W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Hence, since
\mathcal{K}=\big\{\hat{\varphi}:\,\varphi\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\big\}

coincides with our space
\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega)
,
and since
\rho_{p}(\cdot)\geq\|\cdot\|_{W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)}
, we get

(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}u\in(\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega))^{\prime};

moreover, for every function
\varphi\in\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega)
we have

(2.17)

\begin{split}\langle(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}u,\varphi\rangle&=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{B_{p}(u(x)-u(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{n+ps}}\,dx\,dy\\
&=\iint_{\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}}\frac{B_{p}(u(x)-u(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\\
&\qquad\quad-2\,\iint_{(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{V})\times\Omega}\frac{B_{p}(u(x)-u(y))\varphi(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy.\end{split}

In particular, if we also have that
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega)
(whence,

u\in L^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap W^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, see (
2.14
)), for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega)

we have the estimate

(2.18)

\begin{split}|\langle(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}u,\varphi\rangle|&\leq\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{p-1}|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\\
&\leq[u]_{s,p,\mathbb{R}^{n}}[\varphi]_{s,p,\mathbb{R}^{n}}\leq\rho_{p}(u)\,\rho_{p}(\varphi),\end{split}

and thus
u\mapsto(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}u
is continuous from
\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega)

into its dual.

3) The mixed operator
\mathcal{L}_{p}
and problem (
1.1
).

Taking everything said so far into account,
we can introduce the
weak realization
of
\mathcal{L}_{p}

we are going to use in this paper (mainly in the case
p=2
), and
give some comments on Definition
1.1
.

To begin with we recall that, if

\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an arbitrary open set (bounded or not)
and if
u\in W^{1,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{O})
, the
p
-Laplacian

-\Delta_{p}u

of
u

is defined as the linear operator

-\Delta_{p}u:C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})\to\mathbb{R},\qquad\langle-\Delta_{p}u,\varphi\rangle=\int_{\mathcal{O}}|\nabla u|^{p-2}\langle\nabla u,\nabla\varphi\rangle\,dx.

Clearly, such an operator is in fact a distribution on
\mathcal{O}
. Moreover, for any bounded open set
\Omega\Subset\mathcal{O}
, the purely local nature of
-\Delta_{p}
ensures that it can be extended (with the same expression)
as a linear and continuous operator on
\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega)
.

In view of these facts, and since
W^{1,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{O})\subseteq W^{s,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{O})

(see (
2.7
)),
for any given function
u\in L^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap W^{1,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{O})
,
we can thus
define
\mathcal{L}_{p}u

as the linear operator
on the space
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})
given by the sum between
-\Delta_{p}u
and
(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}u
, that is,

(2.19)

\begin{split}\langle\mathcal{L}_{p}u,\varphi\rangle&=\langle-\Delta_{p}u,\varphi\rangle+\langle(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}u,\varphi\rangle\\
&=\int_{\mathcal{O}}|\nabla u|^{p-2}\langle\nabla u,\nabla\varphi\rangle\,dx\\
&\qquad+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{B_{p}(u(x)-u(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{n+ps}}\,dx\,dy.\end{split}

In particular, we see that

1)

\mathcal{L}_{p}u
is a distribution on
\mathcal{O}
, that is,
\mathcal{L}_{p}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathcal{O})
;

2)

given any
bounded open set

\Omega\Subset\mathcal{O}
, the distribution

\mathcal{L}_{p}u
can be extended

with the same expression
as a linear and continuous operator on
\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega)
.

Remark 2.4
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded open set, and let

u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega)
. On account of (
2.14
),
we know that
u\in W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subseteq L^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
;
as a consequence,

\mathcal{L}_{p}u\in(\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega))^{\prime}

Moreover, owing to (
2.19
)
(see also (
2.18
)), we have

\displaystyle|\langle\mathcal{L}_{p}u,\varphi\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\||\nabla u|\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\||\nabla\varphi|\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+[u]_{p,s,\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,[\varphi]_{p,s,\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\rho_{p}(u)\,\rho_{p}(\varphi)\quad\text{for every
$\varphi\in\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega)$}.

and thus we can think of
\mathcal{L}_{p}
as an operator
from
\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega)
into
(\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega))^{\prime}
.

Thanks to the preceding discussion (which provides a precise definition of

\mathcal{L}_{p}u

under mild assumptions on
u
),
we spend a few words on Definition
1.1
.

1)  First of all we observe that, if
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a solution of problem (
1.1
) (in the sense of Definition

1.1
), we
cannot guarantee

that
u\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
, and thus
\mathcal{L}u

is not well-defined
(as a distribution on
\Omega
);
in other words, we
cannot say
that the first two terms in the left-hand side of (
1.7
) do coincide with

\langle\mathcal{L}u,\varphi\rangle
.
Despite this fact, it is not difficult to see that all the integrals
appearing in (
1.7
)
are finite.

Indeed, given any

\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, since
g(u),\,h(u)f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
, we clearly have

\int_{\Omega}g(u)|\varphi|\,dx<+\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\Omega}h(u)f\,|\varphi|\,dx<+\infty.

Moreover, since
u|_{\Omega}\in W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega)
, we also have

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi|\,dx\leq\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla\varphi|\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|\,dx<+\infty.

Finally, we turn to the
non-local term
in (
1.7
). To this end
we first observe that, since
u|_{\Omega}\in W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega)

and since

u=0
a.e. on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
we have

u\in W^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n});

as a consequence, since Remark
2.3
-c) holds also
for
p=1
, we obtain
u\in W^{s,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

4) A Leray-Lions-type Theorem.

We now state the announced Leray-Lions-type theorem for our mixed operator
\mathcal{L}_{p}

which will be used in the sequel.

To this end we first recall that,
if
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded
open set, by Remark
2.4

we know that
the following map

u\ni\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega)\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{p}u\in(\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega))^{\prime}

is well-defined and continuous; moreover, it is not difficult to see that
this map, still denoted by
\mathcal{L}_{p}
, is monotone, coercive and hemicontinuous (see e.g.
[
47
, Section 3]
), and therefore it is surjective (see
[
61
, Corollary 2.2]
). Finally,
\mathcal{L}_{p}
is pseudomonotone (see
[
61
, Proposition 2.3]
).

Hence, we can have the following theorem.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
p\in(1,+\infty)
and
s\in(0,1)
. Let
a:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
F:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be Carathéodory functions satisfying the following properties:

•

there exists
\beta>0
such that
|a(x,\sigma,\xi)|\leq\beta\,(|\sigma|^{p-1}+|\xi|^{p-1})
;

•

there exists
\alpha>0
such that
a(x,\sigma,\xi)\cdot\xi\geq\alpha|\xi|^{p}
, for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
;

•

(a(x,\sigma,\xi)-a(x,\sigma,\eta)\cdot(\xi-\eta)>0
for every
\xi\neq\eta
;

•

there exists
f\in L^{p^{\prime}}(\Omega)
such that
|F(x,\sigma,\eta)|\leq f(x)
.

Then there exists a solution
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega)
of

(mLL)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\mathrm{div}(a(x,u,\nabla u))+(-\Delta)_{p}^{s}u=F(x,u,\nabla u)&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\textrm{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega.\end{array}\right.

In absence of the nonlocal part
(-\Delta)_{p}^{s}
, Theorem
2.6
has been proved in
[
54
]
as an application of the surjectivity theorem. The crucial part of this proof consists in showing that the operator

(2.24)

W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\ni u\mapsto A(u)-\mathrm{div}(a(x,u,\nabla u))-F(x,u,\nabla u)\in(W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega))^{\prime}

is pseudomonotone and coercive.
On the other hand,
since we know that the operator

u\mapsto(-\Delta)_{p}^{s}u
is
continuous from
\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega)
to its dual,
see (
2.18
),
and since it is not difficult to recognize that

\displaystyle(\ast)

\displaystyle\,\,\langle(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}u-(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}v,u-v\rangle\geq 0

\displaystyle(\ast)

\displaystyle\,\,\langle(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}u,u\rangle=[u]_{s,p,\mathbb{R}^{n}}\geq 0

for every
u,v\in\mathcal{X}^{1,p}(\Omega)
(see
(
2.17
) and recall (
2.12
)),
the mixed operator

A^{\prime}=A+(-\Delta)^{s}_{p}

we are considering satisfies the assumptions of the surjectivity theorem as well.

We explicitly point out that the argument outlined before shows that one is allowed to perturb the original
Leray-Lions operator
with a more general nonlocal operator with
p
-growth provided it is pseudomonotone and non-negative when defined on the appropriate reflexive Banach space.

4) Rearrangements.

Finally, we end this section by recalling some basic notions and results about rearrangements that will be needed in Section
4
. For an exhaustive treatment on this topic see e.g.
[
66
,
67
]
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open and bounded set and let
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function. We denote by
\mu_{u}
the distribution function of
u
, that is

\mu_{u}(t):=|\{x\in\Omega:|u(x)|>t\}|,\quad\text{for }t\geq 0

and by
u^{*}
its
decreasing rearrangement
as the generalized inverse of
\mu_{u}
, that is

u^{\ast}(s):=\sup\left\{t\geq 0:\mu_{u}(t)>s\right\},\quad\text{for }s\in(0,|\Omega|).

The radially symmetric, decreasing rearrangement of
u
, also known as the
Schwarz decreasing rearrangement
of
u
, is hence defined as

u^{\sharp}(x)=u^{\ast}(\omega_{n}|x|^{n})\qquad x\in\Omega^{\sharp},

where
\omega_{n}
is the measure of the unitary ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\Omega^{\sharp}
is the ball (centered at the origin) having the same measure as
\Omega
.
From the definitions given above we can easily deduce that
u
,
u^{\ast}
and
u^{\sharp}
are equi-distributed, that is

\mu_{u}=\mu_{u^{\ast}}=\mu_{u^{\sharp}}.

Moreover, from the definition, we can easily deduce the following properties that will be useful in the sequel:

i)

if
|u(x)|\leq|v(x)|
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
, then
u^{*}(s)\leq v^{*}(s),s\in(0,|\Omega|)
;

ii)

for any
c\in\mathbb{R},(u+c)^{*}(s)=u^{*}(s)+c,s\in(0,|\Omega|)
;

iii)

if
u\in L^{p}(\Omega),1\leq p\leq\infty
, then
u^{*}\in L^{p}(0,|\Omega|)
and
\|u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=\left\|u^{*}\right\|_{L^{p}(0,|\Omega|)}
;

iv)

if
u,v\in L^{p}(\Omega),1\leq p\leq\infty
, then

\left\|u^{*}-v^{*}\right\|_{L^{p}(0,|\Omega|)}\leq\|u-v\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}.

Moreover, the following inequality, known as Hardy-Littlewood inequality, holds true

\int_{\Omega}|u(x)v(x)|\,dx\leq\int_{0}^{|\Omega|}u^{*}(s)v^{*}(s)\,ds.

3.
Existence and uniqueness via approximating problems

In this section we provide the proofs of Theorems

and
1.3
.

3.1.
Existence

For every
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
, we use the truncation functions

(defined in (
2.3
)) and

(defined in (

(3.1)

\begin{gathered}f_{n}:=T_{n}(f),\qquad g_{k}(s):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}T_{k}(g(s))&s\geq 0,\\

We then consider the following family of approximating problems

(3.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathcal{L}u_{n,k}+g_{k}(u_{n,k})=h_{n}(u_{n,k})f_{n}&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\

The weak formulation of problem (
3.2
) reads as follows:

(3.3)

\mathcal{B}(u_{n,k},\varphi)+\int_{\Omega}g_{k}(u_{n,k})\varphi=\int_{\Omega}h_{n}(u_{n,k})f_{n}\varphi,\quad\varphi\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega).

We explicitly observe that we allowed to use
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)

as test - function space in (
3.3
) since, by definition,
g_{k},h_{n}
and
f_{n}
are globally bounded.

Lemma 3.1
.

The approximating problem (
3.2
) admits a weak solution
u_{n,k}\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
such that

i)

u_{n,k}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
;

ii)

u_{n,k}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
uniformly in
k\in\mathbb{N}
;

iii)

u_{n,k}
is bounded in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
uniformly in
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

First, we notice that Theorem
2.6
provides the existence of a
weak solution
u_{n,k}\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
for every
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
fixed. Moreover, exploiting
[
9
]
, one immediately obtains that
u_{n,k}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

Concerning i), recalling Remark
2.3
-1), we can test the weak formulation (
3.3
) with
\varphi=u_{n,k}^{-}
, getting

(3.4)

0\geq\mathcal{B}(u_{n,k},u_{n,k}^{-})+\int_{\Omega}g_{k}(u_{n,k})u_{n,k}^{-}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}h_{n}(u_{n,k})f_{n}u_{n,k}^{-}\,dx\geq 0,

where the last inequality is a consequence of the nonnegativity of
h_{n}(u_{n,k})f_{n}u_{n,k}^{-}
. On the other hand, concerning the first inequality, it holds that

0=g_{k}(u_{n,k}(x))u_{n,k}^{-}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}g_{k}(u_{n,k})\cdot 0,&x\in\{u_{n,k}\geq 0\}\\
0\cdot(-u_{n,k}),&x\in\{u_{n,k}<0\},\end{array}\right.

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(u_{n,k},u_{n,k}^{-})

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{n,k}^{-}|^{2}\,dx+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{(u_{n,k}(x)-u_{n,k}(y))(u_{n,k}^{-}(x)-u_{n,k}^{-}(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dxdy

\displaystyle\leq-\mathcal{B}(u_{n,k}^{-},u_{n,k}^{-})\leq 0,

where we used that

\displaystyle(LHS)

\displaystyle:=(u_{n,k}(x)-u_{n,k}(y))(u_{n,k}^{-}(x)-u_{n,k}^{-}(y))

\displaystyle\leq-(u_{n,k}^{-}(x)-u_{n,k}^{-}(y))^{2}=:(RHS),

for every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Indeed:

•

if
x,y\in\{u_{n,k}\geq 0\}
, then
(LHS)=0=(RHS)

•

if
x\in\{u_{n,k}\geq 0\}
and
y\in\{u_{n,k}<0\}
, then

(LHS)=u_{n,k}(x)u_{n,k}(y)-u_{n,k}^{2}(y)\leq-u_{n,k}^{2}(y)=(RHS);

•

if

and

, then

•

if
x,y\in\{u_{n,k}<0\}
, then

(LHS)=-(u_{n,k}(x)-u_{n,k}(y))^{2}=(RHS).

Therefore, going back to (
3.4
), we find

and this implies that

in
\Omega
and thus

a.e. in
\Omega
.

Regarding ii), for any given

; this, together with the above property c), allows us to use
the Dominated Convergence Theorem, from which we easily derive that

\int_{\Omega}g_{k}(u_{n,k})\varphi\,dx\to\int_{\Omega}g(u_{n})\varphi\,dx\quad\text{as $k\to+\infty$}.

Finally, again by Lemma
3.1
-ii) and the definition of
T_{n}
, we have

0\leq h_{n}(u_{n,k})f_{n}=T_{n}\big(h(u_{n,k})\big)\,T_{n}(f)\leq n^{2}\quad\text{for every $k\geq 1$};

hence, using property c) and again the Dominated Convergence Theorem, we get

\int_{\Omega}h_{n}(u_{n,k})f_{n}\varphi\,dx\to\int_{\Omega}h_{n}(u_{n})f_{n}\varphi\,dx\quad\text{as $k\to+\infty$}.

Gathering all these facts, from (
3.3
) we then infer that

(3.11)

\mathcal{B}(u_{n},\varphi)+\int_{\Omega}g(u_{n})\varphi\,dx=\int_{\Omega}h_{n}(u_{n})f_{n}\varphi\,dx,\quad\varphi\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega).

We explicitly notice that also in the above (
3.11
)
we can use
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
as a test-fun-ction
space (since
g(u_{n})\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, with a norm depending on
n
).

The following Lemma provides the necessary apriori estimates to further pass to the limit (as
n\to+\infty
) in (
3.10
). This an almost verbatim generalization of
[
57
, Lemma 4.1]
to the local-nonlocal case.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
u_{n}
be a weak solution to (
3.10
).
Let
h
satisfy
(h1)
with
\gamma\leq 1
and
(h2)
. If one of the following holds

i)

\theta\geq 1
and
f\in L^{1}(\Omega)
;

ii)

\theta<1
,
f\in L^{m}(\Omega)
with
m>1
, and
g
satisfies
(g1)
,

then there exists a positive constant
C>0
independent of
n
such that

(3.12)

\rho(u_{n})^{2}+\|g(u_{n})u_{n}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C.

Moreover, there exists a positive constant (still denoted by
C
) independent of
n
such that

(3.13)

\int_{\Omega}h_{n}(u_{n})f_{n}\varphi\,dx\leq C+\rho(\varphi)^{2}+C\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)},

for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Firstly we test (
3.10
) with the solution
u_{n}
itself, finding

(3.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(u_{n},u_{n})+\int_{\Omega}g(u_{n})u_{n}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}h_{n}(u_{n})f_{n}u_{n}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\underline{C}\int_{\{u_{n}<\underline{s}\}}f_{n}u_{n}^{1-\gamma}\,dx+\int_{\{\underline{s}\leq u_{n}\leq\overline{s}\}}h_{n}(u_{n})f_{n}u_{n}\,dx+\overline{C}\int_{\{u_{n}>\overline{s}\}}f_{n}u_{n}^{1-\theta}\,dx,

where
\underline{C}
,
\gamma
and
\underline{s}
come from (h1) while
\overline{C}
,
\theta
and
\overline{s}
from (h2).
Now, in both cases i) and ii), we have that

(3.15)

\displaystyle\underline{C}\int_{\{u_{n}<\underline{s}\}}f_{n}u_{n}^{1-\gamma}\,dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\{\underline{s}\leq u_{n}\leq\overline{s}\}}h_{n}(u_{n})f_{n}u_{n}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\left(\underline{C}\,\underline{s}^{1-\gamma}+\max_{s\in[\underline{s},\overline{s}]}(h(s)s)\right)\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}

In case i) holds, we can simply estimate the very last term in the right hand side of (
3.14
) as follows

(3.16)

\overline{C}\int_{\{u_{n}>\overline{s}\}}f_{n}u_{n}^{1-\theta}\,dx\leq\overline{C}\overline{s}^{1-\theta}\,\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}.

Combining (
3.14
) with (
3.15
) and (
3.16
) we prove (
3.12
) in case i) holds for

C:=\left(\underline{C}\,\underline{s}^{1-\gamma}+\max_{s\in[\underline{s},\overline{s}]}(h(s)s)+\overline{C}\overline{s}^{1-\theta}\right)\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)},

which is independent of
n
.

Let us now consider the case in which ii) is in force. Starting from (
3.14
), we can use Young’s inequality (with exponents
m
and
\tfrac{m}{m-1}
) on the very last term finding

(3.17)

\overline{C}\int_{\{u_{n}>\overline{s}\}}f_{n}u_{n}^{1-\theta}\,dx\leq C_{\varepsilon}\|f\|^{m}_{L^{m}(\Omega)}+\varepsilon\int_{\{u_{n}>\overline{s}\}}u_{n}^{\tfrac{(1-\theta)m}{m-1}}\,dx,

for every
\varepsilon>0
and for some positive constant
C_{\varepsilon}>0
depending on
\varepsilon
. From now on, we can assume without loss of generality, that
s_{1}\leq\overline{s}
.
Now, if
q=\tfrac{1-m\theta}{m-1}
in (g1), it follows that

q+1=\tfrac{(1-\theta)m}{m-1}
and therefore

(3.18)

\varepsilon\int_{\{u_{n}>\overline{s}\geq s_{1}\}}u_{n}^{q+1}\,dx\leq\dfrac{\varepsilon}{\nu}\int_{\Omega}g(u_{n})u_{n}\,dx,

which can reabsorbed for
\varepsilon>0
small enough (e.g.
\varepsilon=\tfrac{\nu}{2}
). Summarizing, in case ii) with
q=\tfrac{1-m\theta}{m-1}
, combining (
3.14
), (
3.15
), (
3.17
) and (
3.18
), we get

\displaystyle\rho(u_{n})^{2}

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}g(u_{n})u_{n}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C:=\left(\underline{C}\underline{s}^{1-\gamma}+\max_{s\in[\underline{s},\overline{s}]}(h(s)s)+\overline{C}\overline{s}^{1-\theta}\right)\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+C_{\varepsilon}\|f\|^{m}_{L^{m}(\Omega)},

which is independent of
n
and the latter in turn gives

\rho(u_{n})^{2}+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}g(u_{n})u_{n}\,dx\leq 3C.

The case ii) with
q>\tfrac{1-m\theta}{m-1}
can be treated similarly applying once again Young’s inequality with exponents
\left(\tfrac{(q+1)(m-1)}{(1-\theta)m},\tfrac{(q+1)(m-1)}{(q+1)(m-1)-(1-\theta)m}\right)
on the last term on the right hand side of (
3.17
). This choice of exponents, combined with (g1), gives

(3.19)

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle\int_{\{u_{n}>\overline{s}\}}u_{n}^{\tfrac{(1-\theta)m}{m-1}}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{\varepsilon(1-\theta)m}{(q+1)(m-1)}\int_{\{u_{n}>\overline{s}\geq s_{1}\}}u_{n}^{q+1}\,dx+\dfrac{\varepsilon[(q+1)(m-1)-(1-\theta)m]}{(q+1)(m-1)}|\Omega|

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{\varepsilon(1-\theta)m}{\nu(q+1)(m-1)}\int_{\Omega}g(u_{n})u_{n}\,dx+\dfrac{\varepsilon[(q+1)(m-1)-(1-\theta)m]}{(q+1)(m-1)}|\Omega|.

Arguing as before, we can complete the proof of (
3.12
) in case ii) with
q>\tfrac{1-m\theta}{m-1}
.

Once (
3.12
) is proved, we are allowed to test (
3.10
) with
\varphi\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
finding

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}h_{n}(u_{n})f_{n}\varphi\,dx

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}(u_{n},\varphi)+\int_{\Omega}g(u_{n})\varphi\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{\rho(u_{n})^{2}}{2}+\dfrac{\rho(\varphi)^{2}}{2}+\|g(u_{n})\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

Combining the latter with (
3.12
) we get (
3.13
). This closes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to prove the existence result.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We consider a weak solution
u_{n}\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
to problem (
3.10
).
By (
3.12
), we have that
\rho(u_{n})
is uniformly bounded (i.e.,
u_{n}
is uniformly bounded in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
); hence, by Remark
2.3
- 4) there exist a (unique)
function
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
such that
(by choosing a sub-sequence if needed, and as
n\to+\infty
)

a)

u_{n}\to u
weakly in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
;

b)

u_{n}\to u
strongly in
L^{r}(\Omega)
for every
1\leq r<2^{\ast}
;

c)

u_{n}\to u

pointwise a.e. in
\Omega
(hence, in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
).

Moreover, by passing to the limit as
n\to+\infty
in (
3.12
) with the aid
of the Fatou Lemma, we derive that

(3.20)

g(u)u\in L^{1}(\Omega),

as requested. We explicitly stress that, owing to
(
3.20
) (and recalling that
g
is non-negative
and continuous on
[0,+\infty)
), we also derive that

g(u)\in L^{1}(\Omega).

We now fix a test function
\varphi\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega),\varphi\geq 0
in
\Omega
,
and we turn to show that
it is possible to pass to the limit as
n\to+\infty
in (
3.11
)
, thus obtaining

(3.21)

\mathcal{B}(u,\varphi)+\int_{\Omega}g(u)\varphi\,dx=\int_{\Omega}h(u)f\varphi\,dx.

Due the arbitrariness of
\varphi
, this proves that
u
is a weak solution of
(
1.1
). To ease the readability,
we study separately the three terms appearing in the cited (
3.11
).

-
The operator part.

Thanks to b), we immediately have that

(3.22)

\mathcal{B}(u_{n},\varphi)\to\mathcal{B}(u,\varphi)\quad\textrm{as }n\to+\infty.

-
The absorption term
.
Regarding the absorption term, our goal is to show that

\int_{\Omega}g(u_{n})\varphi\,dx

is equi-integrable (with respect to
n
). To this aim, we first test (
3.10
) with the function
S_{\eta,k}(u_{n})
(defined in (
2.5
) with
\eta,k>0
being fixed), finding

(3.23)

\mathcal{B}(u_{n},S_{\eta,k}(u_{n}))+\int_{\Omega}g(u_{n})S_{\eta,k}(u_{n})\,dx=\int_{\Omega}h_{n}(u_{n})f_{n}\,S_{\eta,k}(u_{n})\,dx.

Now,

(3.24)

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{n}\cdot\nabla S_{\eta,k}(u_{n})\,dx=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{n}|^{2}S^{\prime}_{\eta,k}(u_{n})\,dx,

while we claim that

(3.25)

\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{(u_{n}(x)-u_{n}(y))(S_{\eta,k}(u_{n}(x))-S_{\eta,k}(u_{n}(y)))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\geq 0.

Indeed, since the map
z\mapsto S_{\eta,k}(z)
is non-decreasing, we have

(u_{n}(x)-u_{n}(y))(S_{\eta,k}(u_{n}(x))-S_{\eta,k}(u_{n}(y)))\geq 0\quad\text{for a.e.\,$x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}.

Combining (
3.23
) with (
3.24
) and (
3.25
), we further get

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{n}|^{2}S^{\prime}_{\eta,k}(u_{n})\,dx+\int_{\Omega}g(u_{n})S_{\eta,k}(u_{n})\,dx\leq(\sup_{s\in[k,+\infty)}h(s))\int_{\Omega}f_{n}S_{\eta,k}(u_{n})\,dx.

From now on we can follow verbatim
[
57
, Proof of Theorem 3.4]
, giving

(3.26)

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\int_{\Omega}g(u_{n})\varphi\,dx=\int_{\Omega}g(u)\varphi\,dx.

-
The singular term.

Finally, we turn to study the last term of (
3.11
), that is,

\int_{\Omega}h_{n}(u_{n})f_{n}\varphi\,dx

To this end we preliminary notice that, if
h(0)<+\infty
, an application
of the Dominated Convergence Theorem (based on (
h2
)) easily shows that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\int_{\Omega}h_{n}(u_{n})f_{n}\varphi\,dx=\int_{\Omega}h(u)f\varphi\,dx,

and the proof is complete in this case; hence, we assume that

(3.27)

h(0)=+\infty,

and we closely follow the argument in the proof
of
[
57
, Theorem 3.4]
.

First of all we first observe that,
by passing to the limit as
n\to+\infty

in (
3.13
) with the aid of the
Fatou Lemma, we get

(3.28)

\int_{\Omega}h(u)f\varphi\,dx\leq C,

for some
C>0
independent of
n
. In particular, by
(
3.27
) - (
3.28
),
we have

(3.29)

\text{$\{u=0\}\subseteq\{f=0\}$ (up to a set of zero Lebesgue measure)}.

We then fix

\delta>0
in such a way that
|\{u=\delta\}|=0

(notice that this is certainly possible, since
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)\subseteq L^{2}(\Omega)
ensures that
\mathcal{E}=\{\delta:\,|\{u=\delta\}|>0\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is at most countable, see
[
55
, Lemma 2.8]
), and we write

(3.30)

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}h_{n}(u_{n})f_{n}\varphi\,dx&=\int_{\{u_{n}>\delta\}}h_{n}(u_{n})f_{n}\varphi\,dx+\int_{\{u_{n}\leq\delta\}}h_{n}(u_{n})f_{n}\varphi\,dx\\
&=A_{n,\delta}+B_{n,\delta}.\end{split}

Taking into account (
3.30
), we now turn to show that it is possible
to pass to the limit
as
n\to+\infty
and as
\delta\to 0^{+}
in both terms

A_{n,\delta},B_{n,\delta}
.

-
The term
A_{n,\delta}
. As regards this term,
we can proceed exactly as in the proof
of
[
57
, Theorem 3.4]
(where the same term is considered,
and since the
operator term
does not play any role): owing to the properties of
h

(see, in particular, property (
h2
)),
and taking into account (
3.28
), we derive that

(3.31)

\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\lim_{n\to+\infty}A_{n,\delta}=\int_{\Omega}h(u)f\varphi\,dx.

-
The term
B_{n,\delta}
. As regards this term, instead, we claim that

(3.32)

\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\lim_{n\to+\infty}B_{n,\delta}=0.

Taking this claim for granted for a moment, we can immediately complete the proof
of the theorem. Indeed, by combining (
3.31
) - (
3.32
),
from (
3.30
) we deduce that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\int_{\Omega}h_{n}(u_{n})f_{n}\varphi\,dx=\int_{\Omega}h(u)f\varphi\,dx;

this, together with (
3.22
) - (
3.26
), finally shows (
3.21
).

Hence, we are left to show (
3.32
). To this end
we first observe that, by using the function
v_{n}=V_{\delta,\delta}(u_{n})\varphi\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
as
a test function in (
3.10
) (here,
V_{\delta,\delta}
is the
function defined in (
2.4
) with
k=\delta
), we get

(3.33)

\begin{split}&0\leq\int_{\{u_{n}\leq\delta\}}h_{n}(u_{n})f_{n}\varphi\,dx\leq\int_{\Omega}h_{n}(u_{n})f_{n}V_{\delta,\delta}(u_{n})\varphi\,dx\\
&\qquad=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{n}\cdot\nabla v_{n}\,dx+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{(u_{n}(x)-u_{n}(y))(v_{n}(x)-v_{n}(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\\
&\qquad\qquad+\int_{\Omega}g(u_{n})v_{n}\,dx\\
&\qquad(\text{since $V^{\prime}_{\delta,\delta}(s)\leq 0$ for a.e.\,$s\geq 0$})\\
&\qquad\leq\int_{\Omega}V_{\delta,\delta}(u_{n})\nabla u_{n}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{(u_{n}(x)-u_{n}(y))(v_{n}(x)-v_{n}(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\\
&\qquad\qquad+\int_{\Omega}g(u_{n})v_{n}\,dx.\end{split}

Moreover, by arguing exactly as in the proof of
[
57
, Theorem 3.4]

(where the
very same terms
are considered), we have

(3.34)

\begin{split}\mathrm{i)}&\,\,\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\lim_{n\to+\infty}\int_{\Omega}V_{\delta,\delta}(u_{n})\nabla u_{n}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx=\int_{\{u=0\}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx=0;\\
\mathrm{ii)}&\,\,\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\lim_{n\to+\infty}\int_{\Omega}g(u_{n})v_{n}\,dx=\int_{\{u=0\}}g(u)\varphi\,dx=0.\end{split}

Hence, we are left to study the
nonlocal part
in (
3.33
), whose presence is
the main different with respect to the case considered in
[
57
]
.

To begin with we observe that, by combining the
global interpolation
inequality
for fractional Sobolev spaces in

[
52
, Theorem 7.45]

(applied here with
p_{1}=p_{2}=2
and
\theta=1-s
) with
the properties of the sequence

\{u_{n}\}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, we get

(3.35)

\begin{split}[u_{n}-u]_{s,\mathbb{R}^{n}}&\leq C\,\|u_{n}-u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{\theta}\,\|\nabla(u_{n}-u)\|^{1-\theta}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\\
&\leq C\,\|u_{n}-u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{\theta}\,\rho(u_{n}-u)^{1-\theta}\\
&(\text{by property b), and since $\rho(u_{n})$ is uniformly bounded})\\
&\leq C\,\|u_{n}-u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{\theta}\to 0\quad\textrm{ as }n\to+\infty.\end{split}

Moreover, it is readily seen that also the sequence
\{v_{n}\}_{n}
is bounded in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
:
indeed, taking into account (
2.15
), for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

(3.36)

\displaystyle\rho(v_{n})^{2}\leq C\,\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v_{n}|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left|V^{\prime}_{\delta,\delta}(u_{n})\varphi\nabla u_{n}\chi_{\{\delta<u_{n}<2\delta\}}+V_{\delta,\delta}(u_{n})\nabla\varphi\right|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{n}|^{2}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}\,dx\right)\leq C.

As a consequence, by Remark
2.3
- 4) (and recalling that

u_{n}\to u
a.e. on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
), we deduce that (by passing to a sub-sequence if needed)

\text{$v_{n}\to v=V_{\delta,\delta}(u)\varphi$
strongly in $L^{2}(\Omega)$ (and pointwise a.e.\,in $\Omega$)}.

From this,
again by using the
global interpolation
inequality
for fractional Sobolev spaces in

[
52
, Theorem 7.45]
, we obtain

(3.37)

\begin{split}[v_{n}-v]_{s,\mathbb{R}^{n}}&\leq C\,\|v_{n}-v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{\theta}\,\|\nabla(v_{n}-v)\|^{1-\theta}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\\
&\leq C\,\|v_{n}-v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{\theta}\,\rho(v_{n}-v)^{1-\theta}\\
&\leq C\,\|v_{n}-v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{\theta}\to 0\quad\textrm{ as }n\to+\infty.\end{split}

Gathering (
3.35
) - (
3.37
)
(and using Hölder inequality),
we can then pass to the limit as
n\to+\infty
in the nonlocal
part in (
3.33
), obtaining

\displaystyle\Big|\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{(u_{n}(x)-u_{n}(y))(v_{n}(x)-v_{n}(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\Big|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{|(u_{n}-u)(x)-(u_{n}-u)(y)||v_{n}(x)-v_{n}(y)|}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{|u(x)-u(y)||(v_{n}-v)(x)-(v_{n}-v)(y)|}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad\leq[u_{n}-u]_{s,\mathbb{R}^{n}}[v_{n}]_{s,\mathbb{R}^{n}}+[u]_{s,\mathbb{R}^{n}}[v_{n}-v]_{s,\mathbb{R}^{n}}\to 0\quad\text{as $n\to+\infty$}.

In other words, we have

(3.38)

\begin{split}&\lim_{n\to+\infty}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{(u_{n}(x)-u_{n}(y))(v_{n}(x)-v_{n}(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\\
&\qquad=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{(u(x)-u(y))(V_{\delta,\delta}(u)(x)\varphi(x)-V_{\delta,\delta}(u)(y)\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy.\end{split}

Finally, we turn to pass to the limit as
\delta\to 0^{+}
in the right-hand side of
(
3.38
). To this aim, we distinguish the following two cases (according
to the values of
s
).

-
Case I)
0<s<1/2
.
In this first case, we claim that it is possible to
apply the Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem to show that

(3.39)

\begin{split}&\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{(u(x)-u(y))(V_{\delta,\delta}(u)(x)\varphi(x)-V_{\delta,\delta}(u)(y)\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\\
&\qquad=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{(u(x)-u(y))((\chi_{\{u=0\}}\varphi)(x)-(\chi_{\{u=0\}}\varphi)(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy.\end{split}

Indeed, by the very definition of
V_{\delta,\delta}(\cdot)
we readily see that

\text{$\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}V_{\delta,\delta}(u)=\chi_{\{u=0\}}$ a.e.\,in $\Omega$}.

Moreover, since
0\leq V_{\delta,\delta}(\cdot)\leq 1
and since
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we have

\begin{split}&\Big|\dfrac{(u(x)-u(y))(V_{\delta,\delta}(u)(x)\varphi(x)-V_{\delta,\delta}(u)(y)\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\Big|\\
&\qquad\qquad\leq 2\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\dfrac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}=\mathfrak{g}(x,y)\end{split}

and
the function
\mathfrak{g}
is integrable on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. To see this fact it suffices
to notice that,
since
0<s<1/2
, by arguing as in
[
38
, Proposition 2.2]

(and by recalling the explicit expression of the space
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
given in
(
2.12
)) we get

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\mathfrak{g}(x,y)\,dx\,dy\leq C\Big(\int_{\{|x-y|\leq 1\}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy+\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq C\Big\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big(\int_{B_{1}(0)}\frac{|u(x)-u(x+z)|}{|z|}\cdot\frac{1}{|z|^{n+(2s-1)}}\,dz\Big)dx+\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\Big\}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq C\Big\{\||\nabla u|\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\int_{B_{1}(0)}\frac{1}{|z|^{n+(2s-1)}}\,dz+\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\Big\}<+\infty,

where
C>0
is a suitable constant depending on
\varphi,n
and
s
.

Gathering all these facts, we are then entitled to apply the
Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem in (
3.38
),
which yields (
3.39
). Summing up, we have

(3.40)

\begin{split}&\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\lim_{n\to+\infty}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{(u_{n}(x)-u_{n}(y))(v_{n}(x)-v_{n}(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\\
&\qquad=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{(u(x)-u(y))((\chi_{\{u=0\}}\varphi)(x)-(\chi_{\{u=0\}}\varphi)(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy.\end{split}

With (
3.40
) at hand, we can finally complete

-

3.2.
Uniqueness

Finally, we provide the

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Suppose by contradiction that there exist two different solutions of (
1.1
)
u_{1},u_{2}
such that
g(u_{1}),g(u_{2})\in L^{1}(\Omega)
and

\text{$u_{1}|_{\Omega},u_{2}|_{\Omega}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)$
(hence, $u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)$}).

Owing to Lemma
2.5
, we can use

\varphi=T_{k}(u_{1}-u_{2})
as a test function either for the equations solved by

, either for the one solved by
u_{2}
. Taking the difference yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla(u_{1}-u_{2})\cdot\nabla T_{k}(u_{1}-u_{2})\,dx

\displaystyle+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{((u_{1}-u_{2})(x)-(u_{1}-u_{2})(y))(T_{k}(u_{1}-u_{2})(x)-T_{k}(u_{1}-u_{2})(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy

Theorem 4.1
.

Let the assumptions of Corollary
1.4
, and let

be the unique solution to the model problem

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\Delta u+(-\Delta)^{s}u+u^{q}=\dfrac{f(x)}{u^{\gamma}}&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
u>0&\text{ in }\Omega,\\

Then

(4.2)

u^{\ast}(\tau)\leq\left((\gamma+1)v^{\ast}(\tau)\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}},\quad\text{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}} for a.e. }\tau\in(0,|\Omega|),

where

is the unique radial
solution to the problem

(4.3)

\begin{cases}-\Delta v=f^{\sharp}&\text{ in }\Omega^{\sharp}\\

Proof.

We split the proof into different steps.

-
Step I): Approximating problems.
For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and

we consider the following nonsingular approximating problems

(4.4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u_{k}+(-\Delta)^{s}u_{k}+g_{k}(u_{k})=\dfrac{f_{k}}{(u_{k}+\frac{1}{k})^{\gamma}}&\mbox{in }\Omega\\
u_{k}>0&\mbox{in }\Omega\\
u_{k}=0&\mbox{on }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega,\end{array}\right.

where, recalling (
2.2
) and exploiting that
f\geq 0
, we have set

\displaystyle\mathrm{i)}\,\,

\displaystyle f_{k}=T_{k}(f)=\min\{f,k\};

\displaystyle\mathrm{ii)}\,\,

\displaystyle g_{k}(s)=\begin{cases}T_{k}(g(s))&\text{if $s\geq 0$}\\
0&\text{if $s<0$}\end{cases}\,\,\,=\begin{cases}\min\{s^{p},k\}&\text{if $s\geq 0$}\\
0&\text{if $s<0$}\end{cases}

By Lemma
3.2
, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
problem (
4.4
) has a unique nonnegative solution belonging to
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Setting
\tilde{u}_{k}:=u_{k}+\frac{1}{k}
, problem (
4.4
) reads as

\begin{cases}-\Delta\tilde{u}_{k}+(-\Delta)^{s}\tilde{u}_{k}+g_{k}\left(\tilde{u}_{k}-\frac{1}{k}\right)=\dfrac{f_{k}}{\tilde{u}_{k}^{\gamma}}&\text{ in }\Omega\\
\tilde{u}_{k}>\frac{1}{k}&\text{ in }\Omega\\
\tilde{u}_{k}=\frac{1}{k}&\text{ on }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega.\end{cases}

In particular,

(4.5)

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\tilde{u}_{k}(x)\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)\,dx&+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{(\tilde{u}_{k}(x)-\tilde{u}_{k}(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\\
&+\int_{\Omega}g_{k}\left(\tilde{u}_{k}(x)-\frac{1}{k}\right)\varphi(x)\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\frac{f_{k}(x)}{\tilde{u}_{k}(x)^{\gamma}}\,\varphi(x)\,dx\end{split}

for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
.

Let us fix
\frac{1}{k}<t<\|u_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
and
h>0
. We consider the following test function

\varphi(x)=\mathcal{G}_{t,h}(\tilde{u}_{k}(x)),

where
\mathcal{G}_{t,h}(\theta)
is defined by

(4.6)

\mathcal{G}_{t,h}(\theta)=\begin{cases}h\qquad&\mbox{if }\theta>t+h\\
\theta-t&\mbox{if }t<\theta\leq t+h\\
0&\mbox{if }\theta\leq t.\end{cases}

We note that
\mathcal{G}_{t,h}(\tilde{u}_{k})\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, so we can use it the weak formulation of solution (
4.5
), obtaining

(4.7)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla\tilde{u}_{k}(x)

\displaystyle\cdot\nabla\mathcal{G}_{t,h}(\tilde{u}_{k}(x))\,dx

\displaystyle+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{(\tilde{u}_{k}(x)-\tilde{u}_{k}(y))\left(\mathcal{G}_{t,h}(\tilde{u}_{k}(x))-\mathcal{G}_{t,h}(\tilde{u}_{k}(y))\right)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}g_{k}\left(\tilde{u}_{k}(x)-\frac{1}{k}\right)\mathcal{G}_{t,h}(\tilde{u}_{k}(x))\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{f_{k}(x)}{\tilde{u}_{k}(x)^{\gamma}}\,\mathcal{G}_{t,h}(\tilde{u}_{k}(x))\,dx.

We first deal with the left-hand side in (
4.7
). To this end we observe that, setting

\mathcal{H}:=(\tilde{u}_{k}(x)-\tilde{u}_{k}(y))\left(\mathcal{G}_{t,h}(\tilde{u}_{k}(x))-\mathcal{G}_{t,h}(\tilde{u}_{k}(y))\right),

by the monotonicty of
\mathcal{G}_{t,h}
we see that

\mathcal{H}\geq 0
.
Therefore,

\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{(\tilde{u}_{k}(x)-\tilde{u}_{k}(y))\left(\mathcal{G}_{t,h}(\tilde{u}_{k}(x))-\mathcal{G}_{t,h}(\tilde{u}_{k}(y))\right)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\geq 0.

Moreover, since
g_{k}\left(\tilde{u}_{k}-\frac{1}{k}\right)\geq 0
and
\mathcal{G}_{h,t}(\tilde{u}_{k})\geq 0

\int_{\Omega}g_{k}\left(\tilde{u}_{k}-\frac{1}{k}\right)\mathcal{G}_{t,h}(\tilde{u}_{k}(x))\,dx\geq 0,

thus

(4.8)

\int_{\Omega}\nabla\tilde{u}_{k}(x)\cdot\nabla\mathcal{G}_{t,h}(\tilde{u}_{k}(x))\,dx\leq\int_{\Omega}\frac{f_{k}(x)}{\tilde{u}_{k}(x)^{\gamma}}\,\mathcal{G}_{t,h}(\tilde{u}_{k}(x))\,dx.

Dividing by
h>0
the right-hand side of (
4.8

4.6

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{h}\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{f_{k}(x)}{\tilde{u}_{k}(x)^{\gamma}}\,\mathcal{G}_{t,h}(\tilde{u}_{k}(x))\,dx=

\displaystyle\int_{\{\tilde{u}_{k}>t+h\}}\dfrac{f_{k}(x)}{\tilde{u}_{k}(x)^{\gamma}}\,dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\{t<\tilde{u}_{k}\leq t+h\}}\dfrac{f_{k}(x)}{\tilde{u}_{k}(x)^{\gamma}}\left(\frac{\tilde{u}(x)-t}{h}\right)\,dx

which implies,

(4.9)

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\dfrac{1}{h}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{f_{k}(x)}{\tilde{u}_{k}(x)^{\gamma}}\,\mathcal{G}_{t,h}(u_{k}(x))\,dx=\int_{\{\tilde{u}_{k}>t\}}\dfrac{f_{k}(x)}{\tilde{u}_{k}(x)^{\gamma}}\,dx

While for the left-hand side of (

\int_{\Omega}\nabla\tilde{u}_{k}(x)\cdot\nabla\mathcal{G}_{t,h}(\tilde{u}_{k}(x))\,dx=\int_{\{t<\tilde{u}_{k}\leq t+h\}}|\nabla\tilde{u}_{k}(x)|^{2}\,dx

and dividing by

(4.10)

\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{1}{h}\int_{\{t<\tilde{u}_{k}\leq t+h\}}|\nabla\tilde{u}_{k}(x)|^{2}\,dx=-\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\{\tilde{u}_{k}>t\}}|\nabla\tilde{u}_{k}(x)|^{2}\,dx.

(4.12)

\displaystyle-\dfrac{d}{dt}

\displaystyle\int_{\{\tilde{u}_{k}>t\}}|\nabla\tilde{u}(x)|\,dx=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{1}{h}\int_{\{t<\tilde{u}_{k}\leq t+h\}}|\nabla\tilde{u}_{k}(x)|\,dx

Thus, combing (

Finally, from (
4.8

4.9

4.10

4.13

n^{2}\omega_{n}^{2/n}\dfrac{\mu_{\tilde{u}_{k}}(t)^{2-2/n}}{-\mu_{\tilde{u}_{k}}^{\prime}(t)}\leq\int_{\{\tilde{u}_{k}>t\}}\dfrac{f_{k}(x)}{\tilde{u}_{k}(x)^{\gamma}}\,dx\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in\Big(\frac{1}{k},\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\Big)

Then, recalling Hardy-Littlewood inequality and
that facts that

and

n^{2}\omega_{n}^{2/n}\frac{\mu_{\tilde{u}_{k}}(t)^{2-2/n}}{\left(-\mu_{\tilde{u}_{k}}\right)^{\prime}(t)}<\frac{1}{t^{\gamma}}\int_{0}^{\mu_{\tilde{u}_{k}}(t)}f_{k}^{\ast}(\sigma)\,d\sigma\leq\frac{1}{t^{\gamma}}\int_{0}^{\mu_{\tilde{u}_{k}}(t)}f^{\ast}(\sigma)\,d\sigma

-
Step 2): Local symmetrized nonsingular problems.

).

To this end, for every
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
, we start considering the truncated problem

(A.4)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}u+g_{k}(u)=\dfrac{f_{n}}{(u+1/n)^{\gamma}}&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
u\geq 0&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\textrm{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega,\end{cases}

where (as in the general case) we have set

\displaystyle\mathrm{i)}\,\,

\displaystyle f_{n}=T_{n}(f);

\displaystyle\mathrm{ii)}\,\,

\displaystyle g_{k}(s)=\begin{cases}T_{k}(g(s))&\text{if $s\geq 0$}\\
0&\text{if $s<0$}\end{cases}\,\,\,=\begin{cases}\min\{s^{p},k\}&\text{if $s\geq 0$}\\
0&\text{if $s<0$}.\end{cases}

Since the ‘regularized’ function
\mathcal{R}_{n}(t)=(t+1/n)^{\gamma}
(with
t\geq 0
)
clearly satisfies the same ‘qualitative’ properties of the truncation
h_{n}
defined in
(
3.1
), namely

(A.5)

\text{$\mathcal{R}_{n}\geq 0$ and $\mathcal{R}_{n}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)$},

we can certainly exploit Lemmas
3.1
-
3.2

also in this context (notice that the proof of such lemmas only relies on
(
A.5
),
and not on the explicit definition of
h_{n}
). Thus, for every fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
we can
find a function
u_{n}\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, obtained
as the limit as
k\to+\infty
of
u_{k,n}
(the latter being a solution
of
(
A.4
)),
such that

(P1)

u_{n}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
;

(P2)

u_{n}
is a weak solution (in the sense of Definition
1.1
) of

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}u+u^{q}=\dfrac{f_{n}}{(u+1/n)^{\gamma}}&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
u\geq 0&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\textrm{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega,\end{cases}

that is,
u_{n}\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
and

(A.6)

\mathcal{B}(u_{n},\varphi)+\int_{\Omega}u_{n}^{q}\varphi\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{f_{n}}{(u_{n}+1/n)^{\gamma}}\varphi\,dx\quad\text{for every $\varphi\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)$}.

(P3)

there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
n
, such that

(A.7)

\displaystyle\rho(u_{n})^{2}+\|u_{n}^{q+1}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C;

(A.8)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{f_{n}}{(u_{n}+1/n)^{\gamma}}\varphi\,dx\leq C+\rho(\varphi)^{2}+C\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

In view of these facts, we can then proceed exactly as in the
incipit
of the proof of Theorem

1.2
, and find a function

u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
such that (up to a subsequence)

a)

u_{n}\to u
weakly in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
;

b)

u_{n}\to u
strongly in
L^{r}(\Omega)
for every
1\leq r<2^{\ast}
;

c)

u_{n}\to u
pointwise a.e. in
\Omega
(hence, in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
);

d)

g(u)=u^{q}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
.

In order to pass to the limit as
n\to+\infty
in
(
A.6
) (and thus to prove
that
u
solves problem (
1.6
)), instead, we rely on the following lemma.

Lemma A.1
.

The following assertions hold.

(1)

the sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n}
is non-decreasing in
\Omega
;

(2)

for every compact set
K\subseteq\Omega
there exists
c=c_{K}>0
such that

(A.9)

\text{$u_{n}\geq c_{K}$ a.e.\,in $K$}.

Proof.

We prove the two assertions separately.

(1)  Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed, and let
v_{n}=u_{n+1}-u_{n}
.
First of all,
by using

\varphi=v_{n}^{-}=(u_{n+1}-u_{n})^{-}\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)

as a test function in the weak formulation (
A.6
)
for
u_{n}
and
u_{n+1}
, respectively,
and by subtracting the corresponding identities, we derive

(A.10)

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(u_{n+1}-u_{n})^{-}|^{2}\,dx-\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{(v_{n}(x)-v_{n}(y))(v_{n}^{-}(x)-v_{n}^{-}(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\\
&\quad=\mathcal{B}(u_{n},\varphi)-\mathcal{B}(u_{n+1},\varphi)\\
&\quad=\int_{\{u_{n}\geq u_{n+1}\}}\big(u_{n}^{q}-u_{n+1}^{q}\big)v_{n}\,dx\\
&\quad\quad-\int_{\{u_{n}\geq u_{n+1}\}}f_{n}\Big(\frac{1}{(u_{n}+1/n)^{\gamma}}-\frac{1}{(u_{n+1}+1/(n+1))^{\gamma}}\Big)v_{n}\,dx.\end{split}

We then observe that, by the computations in Lemma
3.1
, we get

(A.11)

\begin{split}&-\big(v_{n}(x)-v_{n}(y)\big)\big(v_{n}^{-}(x)-v_{n}^{-}(y)\big)\geq(v_{n}(x)-v_{n}(y))^{2}\geq 0.\end{split}

Moreover, since the map
s\mapsto g(s)=s^{q}
is increasing in
(0,+\infty)
, we have

(A.12)

\begin{split}\big(u_{n}^{q}-u_{n+1}^{q}\big)v_{n}=(g(u_{n})-g(u_{n+1}))(u_{n+1}-u_{n})\leq 0.\end{split}

Finally, since the map
s\mapsto\mathcal{R}_{n}(s)=(s+1/n)^{-\gamma}
is decreasing
in
(0,+\infty)
, and since (by definition)
f_{n}=T_{n}(f)\geq 0
in
\Omega
,
for a.e.
x\in\{u_{n}\geq u_{n+1}\}
we also have

(A.13)

\begin{split}&-f_{n}\Big(\frac{1}{(u_{n}+1/n)^{\gamma}}-\frac{1}{(u_{n+1}+1/(n+1))^{\gamma}}\Big)v_{n}\\[5.69046pt]
&\qquad(\text{since $v_{n}=u_{n+1}-u_{n}\leq 0$})\\
&\qquad\geq f_{n}\Big(\frac{1}{(u_{n}+1/n)^{\gamma}}-\frac{1}{(u_{n+1}+1/n)^{\gamma}}\Big)(u_{n}-u_{n+1})\\
&\qquad=f_{n}\big(\mathcal{R}_{n}(u_{n})-\mathcal{R}_{n}(u_{n+1})\big)(u_{n}-u_{n+1})\leq 0.\end{split}

Gathering (
A.11
)-(
A.13
), from
(
A.10
) we thus obtain

\displaystyle 0\leq\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(u_{n+1}-u_{n})^{-}|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(u_{n+1}-u_{n})^{-}|^{2}\,dx-\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{(v_{n}(x)-v_{n}(y))(v_{n}^{-}(x)-v_{n}^{-}(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\leq 0.

As a consequence, we conclude that

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(u_{n+1}-u_{n})^{-}|^{2}\,dx=0,

and this shows that
u_{n+1}\geq u_{n}
a.e. in
\Omega
, as desired.

(2)  First of all we observe that, since
we have already proved

that
\{u_{n}\}_{n}
is non-decreasing in
\Omega
,
it suffices to prove (
A.9
) for
u_{1}
.

To this end, we claim that there exists

\alpha\in(0,1)
such that

(A.14)

u_{1}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\cap C^{2,\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega).

Indeed, by property (P2) we have that
u_{1}\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
is a solution of

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}u=\mathcal{F}(x,u)&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u=0&\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$}\end{cases}\qquad\text{where $\mathcal{F}(x,t)=\frac{f_{1}(x)}{(|t|+1)^{\gamma}}-|t|^{q}$}.

Thus, since

\mathcal{F}
satisfies the following growth estimate (by the assumption on
q
)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}(x,t)|

\displaystyle\leq|f_{1}(x)|+t^{q}=T_{1}(f)+|t|^{q}

\displaystyle(\text{since $T_{1}(f)=\min\{f,1\}$})

\displaystyle\leq 1+|t|^{q}\leq 2(1+|t|^{2^{\ast}-1}),

we derive from
[
65
, Theorem 1.3]

that

(A.15)

u_{1}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
for a suitable
\alpha\in(0,1)

(notice that here we are also using the fact that
\partial\Omega
is smooth).

On the other hand, since
f\in C^{\delta}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
,
it is easily seen that
\mathcal{F}
is locally Hölder-continuous on
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}

(for a suitable exponent depending on
\gamma
and
\delta
). Thus,
since
u_{1}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
(see property I)), we can apply also

[
65
, Theorem 1.5]
, obtaining

(A.16)

u\in C^{2,\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)

(up to shrinking
\alpha
if needed). Gathering (
A.15
)-(
A.16
), we then
get (
A.14
).

Now we have proved (
A.14
), we can easily conclude
the demonstration of (
A.9
). Indeed, using (
A.14
),
we see that
u_{1}
is a
classical solution
of

(A.17)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}u+u^{q}=\dfrac{f_{1}}{(u+1)^{\gamma}}&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
u\geq 0&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\textrm{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega,\end{cases}

In particular, we have
u_{1}=0
pointwise in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
, and
u_{1}\gneqq 0

pointwise in
\Omega
(notice that we necessarily have
u_{1}\not\equiv 0
, since
the zero function
does not solve
(
A.17
)).

We then argue by contradiction, supposing that there exists
x_{0}\in\Omega
such that
u_{1}(x_{0})=0

(so that
x_{0}
is an minimum point for
u_{1}
in
\Omega
). Thus, we have

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}u(x_{0})\geq-\Delta u(x_{0})+(-\Delta)^{s}u(x_{0})

\displaystyle\qquad=-u(x_{0})^{q}+\frac{f_{1}(x_{0})}{(u_{1}(x_{0})+1)^{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{f_{1}(x_{0})}{(u_{1}(x_{0})+1)^{\gamma}}\geq 0;

on the other hand, we also have

0\leq 2\,\mathrm{P.V.}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{u(x_{0})-u(y)}{|x_{0}-y|^{n+2s}}\,dy\leq 0,

and hence
u(y)=u(x_{0})=0
for every
x\in\Omega
, which is a contradiction.

Summing up, we have proved that
u_{1}>0
pointwise in
\Omega
, and
this immediately implies the desired (
A.9
)
(in view of the regularity of
u_{1}
in
\Omega
).
∎

As anticipated, by exploiting Lemma
A.1
we can easily prove
that the function
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, obtained
as the limit as
n\to+\infty
of the sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n}
(which, in this case,
exists without the need
of passing to a sub-sequence, see Lemma
3.2
-(1))
is a weak solution of the ‘model’ problem (
1.6
).

Indeed, we already know from a) and d) that
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
and

u^{q}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
. Moreover, given any

\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that

\varphi\geq 0
in
\Omega
, the weak convergence
of
u_{n}
to
u
in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
gives (as in the
proof of Theorem
1.2
)

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\mathcal{B}(u_{n},\varphi)=\mathcal{B}(u,\varphi).

We now observe that, as regards the absorption term, a direct application
of the Fatou Lemma based on Lemma
3.2
-(1) gives

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\int_{\Omega}u_{n}^{q}\varphi\,dx=\int_{\Omega}u^{q}\varphi\,dx.

Furthermore, by Lemma
3.2
-(2) we get

\frac{f_{n}}{(u_{n}+1/n)^{\gamma}}\varphi\leq\frac{f_{n}}{u_{n}^{\gamma}}\varphi\leq c_{\mathrm{supp}(\varphi)}^{-\gamma}f\varphi;

thus, we can apply the Dominated Convergence Theorem, obtaining

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\int_{\Omega}\frac{f_{n}}{(u_{n}+1/n)^{\gamma}}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\frac{f}{u^{\gamma}}\,dx

(where we have also used the fact that
f_{n}=T_{n}(f)\to f
a.e. in
\Omega
).

Gathering all these facts, we can then pass to the limit as
n\to+\infty

in (
A.6
),
thus concluding that
u
is a weak solution of problem (
1.6
),
as desired.

We explicitly notice that, since
\{u_{n}\}_{n}
is non-decreasing, by Lemma
3.2
-(2)
(applied here with
n=1
) we derive that
u\geq u_{1}>0
a.e. in
\Omega
.

Remark A.2
.

It is worth highlighting that the above computations can be carried out also for the more general problem (
1.1
), provided that

g
and
h
fulfill, together with (H)
h
and (H)
g
, the following
additional assumptions
:

(1)

g
is
non-decreasing
and locally Hölder-continuous on
[0,+\infty)
; moreover, there exists a positive constant
C>0
such that

0\leq g(s)\leq C(1+s^{2^{*}-1})\quad\text{for all $s\geq 0$}.

(2)

h
is
non-increasing
and locally Hölder-continuous on
(0,+\infty)
.

Under these assumptions, one can then prove
the existence of a solution of problem (
1.1
) without requiring (
1.5
) on the function
f
.
