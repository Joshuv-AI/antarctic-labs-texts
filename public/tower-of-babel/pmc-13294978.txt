Master Class: Writing a Case Report – Reflections on a Recently Published Case Report
Authors: Jennifer McEachron, Susan C Modesitt, Jamie N Bakkum-Gamez
Published: 2026

Gynecologic Oncology Reports began as Gynecologic Oncology Case Reports (2011–2014), a journal uniquely dedicated to case reports relevant to the practice of gynecologic oncology. In 2014, the journal's name change reflected the scope transitioning to also include original research articles, review articles, and other types of articles (Elsevier; Science Direct, 2026; Modesitt et al., 2022). Case reports remain critical in reporting the earliest in innovative managements, novel therapeutic approaches, and remarkable, unique outcomes among people with or at risk for gynecologic cancers. Case reports are also often the first experience emerging academic authors have in publishing success. Editors of Gynecologic Oncology Reports, in collaboration with Elsevier Researcher Academy, held a live webinar on May 10, 2026, titled “Master Class: Writing a Case Report” with the goals of demystifying the approach to writing a case report and providing education on overarching writing and publishing etiquette for the budding academic author (Elsevier, Science Direct, 2026). In this issue of Gynecologic Oncology Reports, we complement the webinar by reflecting upon a select case report (Bashi et al., 2026) published in this month's issue that exemplifies the case selection, manuscript design, and execution of publishing an impactful case report that also contextualizes the authors' findings within the current literature. Readers can also reference the recording of the Gynecologic Oncology Reports webinar, “Master Class: Writing a Case Report” (Elsevier, Science Direct, 2026), for additional guidance as they begin or refine their journey in academic publishing across the scope of gynecologic oncology.

Selecting a case that meaningfully contributes new knowledge to the literature is a first step to publishing a successful case report. The case reported by Bashi et al. (Bashi et al., 2026) presents a rare gynecologic malignancy and a novel therapeutic strategy for this disease. There are multiple learning points that contribute new information – in particular the use of the emerging tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy in a case of metastatic vulvar melanoma with a resulting favorable treatment outcome. The authors discuss hypotheses as to why TIL therapy was successful and suggest future study of TIL therapy is warranted in vulvar melanoma. Journal selection is an important piece of the publishing puzzle that should be consider at inception of the project with selection of the case and Bashi et al. selected a case well suited for Gynecologic Oncology Reports.

The manuscript writing process can feel daunting, especially among those new to academic writing, and mentors and co-authors are essential. Getting started on the writing process can still be a hurdle. As suggested in the Gynecologic Oncology Reports webinar, “Master Class: Writing a Case Report” (Elsevier, Science Direct, 2026), one strategy for those new to the process can be drafting the case first. This allows the authors to organize the important parts of the case that will guide the literature search, build the discussion, and teaching points. Regardless of the writing strategy employed, Bashi et al. (Bashi et al., 2026) deliver a fluid case presentation with an appropriate level of detail. The case presentation starts with the patient's initial presentation including history of their symptoms, physical examination, tissue biopsy results, and imaging findings. This leads to a discussion of first line therapy for vulvar melanoma and the complications that led to discontinuation. Next, a detailed discussion around the rationale for TIL therapy is developed. This section requires more detail as it describes a treatment regimen that may be new to the readership of Gynecologic Oncology Reports. The case then evolves to discuss the patient's response to this novel therapy and finally describes the patient's long-term outcome.

After conquering the case presentation, the webinar details a suggested approach for newcomers to next address the introduction, followed by the discussion and lastly construct an abstract that reflects all headings in the manuscript. Impactful introductions deliver key information that draws the reader in. Bashi et al. (Bashi et al., 2026) first describe the rarity and demographics of vulvar melanoma, then transition into a discussion of the treatment modalities and prognosis. The authors detail typical standard systemic therapies for vulvar melanoma and the limited response rates, which segues into a discussion of emerging therapies, including TIL therapy. The introduction culminates in a summary statement describing the current case and highlighting the success of this novel treatment all in one sentence.

An effective discussion addresses pertinent details of the case and refers to the current literature as well as highlights teaching points and hypotheses the authors have developed. Bashi, et al. (Bashi et al., 2026) craft their discussion into seven paragraphs. The first paragraph reminds the reader as to why this case is worthy, describing factual information and statistics about vulvar melanoma without being overly redundant. Here, the authors elaborate more on standard treatment options and including immunotherapy. The authors reference reliable literature sources here and give accurate expected response rates. This leads smoothly to a discussion of TIL therapy including basic background information as well as the process and necessary steps to perform TIL therapy. The last paragraph functions as a summary by clearly stating the key take away points and emphasizing what the authors' work adds to the literature.

Many journals, including Gynecologic Oncology Reports, allow two styles of abstracts in the setting of case reports: structured and unstructured. Structured abstracts will often reflect similar sections as to the headings featured in the manuscript body. Conversely, unstructured abstracts are one flowing paragraph without separate sections. Bashi, et al. features a structured abstract which briefly highlights each heading in the manuscript body: introducing the case by describing the rarity of vulvar melanoma and the limited treatment options, followed by highlighting the key details of the case including the diagnosis, presentation and initial failed therapy, and then a brief conclusion sentence summarizing the benefit a non-standard treatment provided in a disease with limited treatment options and poor response rates.

Following a similar template can simplify the writing process for those beginning their publishing journeys. It allows for organization of thought and breaks the manuscript down into manageable segments, all the while focusing on the case as the center point around which to build the manuscript. For an in-depth discussion of tips, tricks and rules of manuscript writing etiquette for the newcomer in academic publishing, reference the recording of the Gynecologic Oncology Reports webinar, “Master Class: Writing a Case Report” (Elsevier, Science Direct, 2026). Learn more about authorship, avoiding plagiarism, rules governing the use of artificial intelligence in publishing, as well as the submission and peer-review process, from experienced authors to guide new authors towards successful publishing of their valuable original research.
