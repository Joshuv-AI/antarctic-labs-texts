Abstract
3D Point clouds (PCs) are commonly used to represent 3D scenes. They can have millions of points, making subsequent downstream tasks such as compression and streaming computationally expensive. PC sampling (selecting a subset of points) can be used to reduce complexity. Existing PC sampling algorithms focus on preserving geometry features and often do not scale to handle large PCs. In this work, we develop scalable graph-based sampling algorithms for PC color attributes, assuming the full geometry is available. Our sampling algorithms are optimized for a signal reconstruction method that minimizes the graph Laplacian quadratic form. We first develop a global sampling algorithm that can be applied to PCs with millions of points by exploiting sparsity and sampling rate adaptive parameter selection. Further, we propose a block-based sampling strategy where each block is sampled independently. We show that sampling the corresponding sub-graphs with optimally chosen self-loop weights (node weights) will produce a sampling set that approximates the results of global sampling while reducing complexity by an order of magnitude. Our empirical results on two large PC datasets show that our algorithms outperform the existing fast PC subsampling techniques (uniform and geometry feature preserving random sampling) by 2 2 dB. Our algorithm is up to 50 50 times faster than existing graph signal sampling algorithms while providing better reconstruction accuracy. Finally, we illustrate the efficacy of PC attribute sampling within a compression scenario, showing that pre-compression sampling of PC attributes can lower the bitrate by 11 % 11\% while having minimal effect on reconstruction.



I
Introduction

Point clouds (PCs) have become a popular data format to represent 3D objects and scenes. A PC is comprised of a list of unordered points in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
{\cal P}=\{\mathbf{p}_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i},z_{i})\}
, representing the geometry, and their corresponding attributes such as color, intensity, or surface normals. A typical PC may contain millions of points. As a result, subsequent downstream tasks, such as compression and real-time streaming in telepresence, become computationally costly
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. To alleviate the computational load, we can employ PC sampling (selecting a subset of representative points), compress and stream a low-resolution PC, and reconstruct the original high-resolution PC. A similar technique is employed in image and video compression, i.e., chroma sub-sampling, where chrominance channels are sampled and encoded at a lower resolution
[
4
]
.

Existing PC sampling frameworks mainly focus on preserving geometry features (e.g., edges, corners)
[
5
,
6
]
, which can be suboptimal when the goal is to accurately represent the attributes, as shown in our experiments (
Section V
).
This work focuses on scenarios where only attributes are sampled, while geometry information is not sampled (i.e., all point coordinates are preserved). For example,
in PC compression applications, geometry is available at both the encoder and the decoder and can be used to compress the color attributes. Thus, some color channels (chrominance) can be sampled at the encoder and reconstructed/interpolated to the original higher resolution at the decoder
[
7
]
.
PC attribute sampling is also crucial in immersive communication requiring low latency
[
1
]
or rendering at varying resolutions
[
2
,
3
]
.

Signal sampling and reconstruction are fundamental operations in signal processing. For 1D signals and images
we can employ regular sampling patterns (e.g., selecting even samples). In our preliminary work, we proposed an
uniform sampling
algorithm, which selects points in the PC belonging to a regular 3D grid
[
7
]
.
While this approach is computationally inexpensive, its performance suffers due to several factors: 1) the sampling algorithm is unaware of the reconstruction algorithm, 2) sampling can only be performed at certain fixed rates, e.g.,
50\%
,
25\%
,
12.5\%
, etc., 3) regular sampling is sub-optimal for PCs with irregular point distribution and non-uniform point densities (e.g., in LiDAR PCs
[
8
,
9
]
). All these factors make 3D PC sampling challenging.

In this paper, we formulate the problem of sampling set selection with two goals.
First, the sampling set should be optimized for both the reconstruction algorithm and the sampling rate.
Second, the sampling algorithm should scale to PCs with millions of points, often encountered in practice
[
10
,
11
]
.

3D PCs can be represented and processed using graphs, with nodes
corresponding to points, edge weights chosen based on the relative positions of the points, and PC attributes are treated as graph signals
[
12
,
13
]
.
This has led to the development of graph signal processing (GSP) techniques for PC denoising
[
14
]
, attribute compression
[
15
,
16
]
and sampling
[
17
,
18
]
.
However, even existing fast (eigendecomposition-free) graph signal sampling algorithms
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
can generally only be applied to small/medium graphs (1000-10,000 nodes)
[
20
]
and are optimized for bandlimited graph signal reconstruction.

This work proposes a computationally efficient
reconstruction-aware global sampling (RAGS) algorithm
for PC attributes.
Our RAGS algorithm is optimized for a popular and scalable graph Laplacian regularized (GLR) reconstruction method

[
25
,
24
,
26
]
.
Similar to
[
19
,
20
]
, we choose samples such that certain interpolating vectors at the sampled locations are as close to orthogonal as possible. In
[
19
,
20
]
, interpolators based on spectral filters are used, for which it is necessary to estimate the cut-off frequency.
Instead, in our work, we construct polynomial interpolators directly in the vertex domain without requiring graph frequency analysis.
Unlike prior work, our proposed interpolators
adapt to the sampling rate
by means of their polynomial degree,
p
.
This allows us to select highly localized (small
p
) or less localized (large
p
) interpolators depending on whether the sampling rate is high (e.g., over 25%) or low. We propose a method to choose the localization parameter
p
as a function of the sampling rate, which allows our RAGS algorithm to handle large PCs, even at higher sampling rates.
Our proposed sampling algorithm has a complexity of
{\cal O}((\bar{d}^{3p}+s)N)
, where
\bar{d}-1
is the maximum unweighted graph degree,
s
is the number of samples and
N
is the total number of points.

Since PCs can be partitioned into non-overlapping blocks using the octree structure with
\mathcal{O}(N\log(N))
complexity
[
27
,
28
]
, we propose a
reconstruction-aware block sampling (RABS) algorithm
, which samples each block independently (see
Figure 1
).
Because independent block sampling ignores connections between points across block boundaries, we propose to add node weights (self-loops) to those points. We show that RABS on graphs with self-loops reduces the sampling complexity and the runtime by an order of magnitude compared to the RAGS with minimal loss in reconstruction quality.

Our main contributions are:

•

A global sampling set selection algorithm optimized for a graph Laplacian-based reconstruction(
Section III
).

•

Methods to select the interpolation localization parameter
p
as a function of the sampling rate (
Section III-D
).

•

A block-based graph sampling framework where self-loops are added to block-boundary nodes (
Section IV
).

•

A comprehensive evaluation of our algorithms on two large PC datasets and their application to
PC attribute compression through chroma subsampling
(
Section V
).

A graph

The combinatorial Laplacian,

The random walk Laplacian is defined as

[
13
]
.
The generalized graph Laplacian of a graph

with self-loops is

, where

III
Sampling algorithm development

Given a set of points
{\cal P}=\{{\bf p}_{1},\cdots,{\bf p}_{N}\}
, and its corresponding attribute (signal)
{\bf f}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we aim to obtain a sampling set,
{\cal S}\subset\{1,2,\cdots,N\}
, such that we can accurately reconstruct
{\bf f}_{{\cal R}}
from the sampled signal
{\bf f}_{{\cal S}}
, where
{\cal R}={\cal V}\setminus{\cal S}
.

III-A

Overview of proposed PC attribute sampling framework

Throughout this paper, we will perform signal reconstruction by minimizing the graph Laplacian quadratic form with sample consistency constraints
[
31
,
25
,
24
,
17
]
. Given a sampling set
{\cal S}
, the reconstructed signal
\hat{{\bf f}}
is

\hat{{\bf f}}={\hbox{arg}}\min_{{\bf x}}{\bf x}^{\top}{\bf L}{\bf x}\quad\textrm{s.t.}\quad{\bf x}_{{\cal S}}={\bf f}_{{\cal S}}.

(2)

\hat{{\bf f}}
is the smoothest graph signal consistent with the samples
{\bf f}_{{\cal S}}
.
There exist various efficient algorithms to solve (
2
) (e.g.,
[
25
]
and
[
31
, Alg. 11.1]
) based on sparse matrix-vector products.

For a desired sampling rate
\alpha
(
s/N\leq\alpha
), our goal is to obtain a sampling set
{\cal S}^{*}
of size
|{\cal S}^{*}|=s
that gives the best reconstruction according to (
2
).
While reconstruction algorithms to solve (
2
) can scale to large PCs, traditional graph-based sampling set selection algorithms are often too complex to be used in the PCs considered in this work.

Two main ideas allow our approach to scale to large graphs.
First, similar to
[
19
,
20
]
, we compute interpolating vectors for each node, but we avoid bandwidth estimation by using spatially-localized interpolators designed for reconstruction with (
2
). This approach is our Reconstruction Aware Global Sampling (
RAGS
), which is developed later in this section. Second, we propose using a block-based sampling strategy whereby the PC is divided into non-overlapping blocks as shown in
Figure 1
, and each block is sampled independently.
The proposed Reconstruction Aware Block Sampling (
RABS
) can be performed using information local to each block, leading to significant complexity reductions in the complexity of computing the interpolating vectors and selecting the sampling set.
In RABS,
the point set
{\cal P}
is partitioned into
L
non-overlapping blocks (subsets of points) denoted by
\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2},\dots\mathcal{B}_{L}
such that
{\cal P}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{L}\mathcal{B}_{j}
, with
\lvert\mathcal{B}_{j}\rvert=b_{j},\forall j
. Let
\{\mathcal{G}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{L}
be the subgraphs of
\mathcal{G}
associated with the points in blocks
\{\mathcal{B}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{L}
.
Our algorithm obtains sampling sets
\{{\cal S}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{L}
for each of the subgraphs
\{\mathcal{G}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{L}
, which are aggregated to form a global sampling set
\bigcup_{j=1}^{L}{\cal S}_{j}
.
We sample
s_{i}
points from the
i
th block so that
\frac{s_{i}}{b_{i}}\leq\alpha
, and the global sampling rate is
\alpha
.

III-B

From signal reconstruction to sampling

To design a sampling set selection algorithm, first, from
[
31
,
25
]
, we note that the closed-form solution to (
2
) is:

\hat{{\bf f}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf f}_{{\cal S}}\\
-{\bf L}_{{\cal R}{\cal R}}^{-1}{\bf L}_{{\cal R}{\cal S}}{\bf f}_{{\cal S}}\end{bmatrix}={\bf M}({\cal S}){\bf f}_{{\cal S}}.

(3)

Since the interpolation matrix
{\bf M}({\cal S})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times\lvert{\cal S}\rvert}
is a function of
{\cal S}
, we are not able to use efficient ED-free sampling set selection methods such as
[

20

rather than

{\bf Z}:=\frac{1}{2}({\bf I}+{\bf D}^{-1}{\bf A})={\bf I}-\frac{1}{2}{\bf L}_{rw},

(6)

Objective function and sampling algorithm

Starting from the approximation in (

From
[

20
]
,
the sampling set should be selected such that the interpolators are close to orthogonal. This can be achieved if the overlap between the

-hop neighborhoods of two selected interpolating vectors

,
{\bf q}_{j}^{(p)}

i,j\in{\cal S},i\neq j
is minimized.
This problem can be approximated by maximizing the volume of parallelepiped formed by the vectors
\{{\bf q}_{i}^{(p)}\}_{i\in{\cal S}}
, i.e.,
{\bf Q}(p)_{{\cal V}{\cal S}}

[
20
, Section 4]
, which is equivalent to
[
20
,
35
]

{\cal S}^{*}=\argmax_{{\cal S}:|{\cal S}|\leq s}\text{det}({\bf Q}(p)_{{\cal V}{\cal S}}^{\top}{\bf Q}(p)_{{\cal V}{\cal S}}).

(13)

We adopt the greedy algorithm proposed in
[

to approximately solve (

that solves:

x^{*}=\argmax_{x\in{\cal R}}\lVert{\bf q}_{x}^{(p)}\rVert_{2}^{2}-\sum_{y\in{\cal S}}\frac{\langle{\bf q}_{y}^{(p)},{\bf q}_{x}^{(p)}\rangle^{2}}{\lVert{\bf q}_{y}^{(p)}\rVert_{2}^{2}}.

By minimizing (

\lVert{\bf q}_{x}^{(p)}\rVert_{2}^{2}
is large, while the overlap with the already selected points, i.e.,
\sum_{y\in{\cal S}}\langle{\bf q}_{y}^{(p)},{\bf q}_{x}^{(p)}\rangle^{2}/\lVert{\bf q}_{y}\rVert^{2},\forall x\in{\cal R},y\in{\cal S}
is small.
The proposed reconstruction aware global sampling (RAGS) is presented as
Algorithm 1
, and complexity is discussed in
Section IV-C
.

We now revisit the approximation proposed in (
10
) given that the sampling set is chosen according to (
13
).
The overlap between the selected interpolators is dominated by the support of the
p
th order terms of
{\bf Q}(p)_{{\cal V}{\cal S}}
. If we compare it with the
p
th order term in
{\bf M}({\cal S})
we get

{\bf E}(p)={\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z}^{p}{\bf P}_{{\cal S}}-({\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z})^{p}{\bf P}_{{\cal S}}.

(15)

The error
{\bf E}(p)
is zero when
p=1
. In general, when the samples are far apart, i.e., no two samples in
{\cal S}
have common nodes in their
p
-hop neighborhood, the terms
{\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z}^{p}{\bf P}_{{\cal S}}
and
({\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z})^{p}{\bf P}_{{\cal S}}
will have the same sparsity pattern leading to small approximation error in (
15
).
Although a smaller
p
, in general, reduces the error in (
15
), it limits the neighborhood information for sampling. For example, for low sampling rates (small
\alpha
) and small
p
, many pairs of interpolators are nearly orthogonal so that two nodes that are relatively close can both be selected for sampling. This can lead to highly non-uniform sampling. Thus, for small
\alpha
, we need a higher value of
p
for better signal reconstruction.

III-D

Choosing the parameter
p
based on sampling rate
\alpha

From previous discussions, and as will be confirmed by our experiments (
Section V
), using a larger
p
leads to sampling sets with better signal reconstruction.
However, complexity and runtime also increase with
p
(see
Section IV-C
). In this section, we investigate the problem of selecting
p
to achieve a good trade-off between complexity and reconstruction accuracy. In particular, we propose to choose
p
to be inversely proportional to the sampling rate
\alpha
.

To understand the interplay between
p
and
\alpha
, consider the approximate reconstruction using interpolators in (
7
). Assuming
{\cal S}
is known, the parameter
p
in (
7
) represents the minimum number of hops required to interpolate points in
{\cal R}
. When
\alpha
is small, points in
{\cal R}
will be far away from sampled points, and thus a higher-order interpolator will be required. In general, the optimal
p
depends on
{\cal S}
and
{\bf Z}
, and thus, it cannot be computed before sampling. In what follows, we propose two strategies to choose
p
for a given
\alpha
: 1) when
{\cal S}
is obtained by uniform sampling on a regular grid graph, and 2) when
{\cal S}
is obtained by random sampling on any arbitrary graph.

III-D
1
Heuristic for uniform sampling of regular graphs

Consider a uniform sampling set on a line graph (
4(a)
).
When
\alpha=0.5
, at least 1 hop is required to interpolate the points in
{\cal R}
from those in
{\cal S}
. But when
\alpha=0.33
, a minimum of 2 hops is required. This is true in the case of 3D grid graphs where samples are uniformly selected in each dimension as shown in
4(b)
.
Therefore, we propose selecting
p
as the smallest number of hops required for interpolation, that is,

p_{\text{uni}}=\left\lceil\frac{1}{2\alpha}\right\rceil,

(16)

where
\lceil x\rceil
is the smallest integer satisfying
x\leq\lceil x\rceil
.
This choice of
p
is a good heuristic for dense PCs representing a smooth surface, such as those used in AR/VR applications.

III-D
2
Choosing
p
based on random sampling

We propose a method to choose
p
, that is valid for arbitrary graphs under a random sampling strategy. In random sampling, a node is added to the sampling set with probability
\alpha
.

We study the error of the approximately reconstructed signal
\hat{{\bf f}}^{(p)}
from (
7
) with respect to
{\bf f}
. We show that part of this error depends only on
p
and
\alpha
(
Lemma III.2
). Later we show that by setting
p
as a function of
\alpha
, we can control this error to any given tolerance (
Theorem III.1
), and thus a good trade-off between complexity (small
p
) and reconstruction accuracy can be achieved. Based on these results, we propose using

p_{\text{rand}}=\left\lceil-\frac{1}{2\log(1-\alpha)}\right\rceil.

(17)

In the rest of this sub-section, we describe the mathematical derivation used to arrive at (
17
) (see Appendix for proofs).

Lemma III.2
.

If each node
i\in{\cal V}
is assigned independently to the sampling set
{\cal S}
with probability
\alpha
, then

\displaystyle\mbox{\bb E}\left[\frac{\|\hat{{\bf f}}^{(p)}-{\bf f}\|}{\|{\bf f}\|}\right]\leq\mbox{\bb E}\|{\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z}\|^{p}+

(18)

\displaystyle\left(1+\left(\mbox{\bb E}\|{\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z}\|^{2p}\right)^{1/2}\right)\left(\mbox{\bb E}\left[\frac{\|\hat{{\bf f}}-{\bf f}\|^{2}}{\|{\bf f}\|^{2}}\right]\right)^{1/2},

(19)

where the expectation is taken with respect to
{\cal S}
.

The first term depends on
p
and
\alpha
and the norm
\|{\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z}\|^{p}
converges to zero as
p\rightarrow\infty
(due to
Lemma .3
). In addition since
\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\hat{{\bf f}}^{(p)}=\hat{{\bf f}}
, the second term is dominated by
\mbox{\bb E}\left[{\|\hat{{\bf f}}-{\bf f}\|^{2}}/{\|{\bf f}\|^{2}}\right]
, which is independent of
p
and represents how good the signal reconstruction with (
3
) is, on average, for random sampling sets of expected size
\mbox{\bb E}[|{\cal S}|]=\alpha N
.
The trade-off between
p
and the sampling rate
\alpha
is characterized by
\mbox{\bb E}[\|{\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z}\|^{p}]
in the next theorem.

Theorem III.1
.

If
p\geq 2
and the sampling rate is
\alpha
, then

\sqrt{1-\alpha}\leq\left(\mbox{\bb E}\|{\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z}\|^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq\sqrt{1-\alpha}+\sqrt{\sigma_{\frac{p}{2}}(\alpha)},

(20)

where

\sigma_{\frac{p}{2}}^{\frac{p}{2}}(\alpha)=\mbox{\bb E}\|{\bf Z}^{\top}{\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z}-(1-\alpha){\bf Z}^{\top}{\bf Z}\|^{\frac{p}{2}}

(21)

is the centered moment of order
p/2
, since
\mbox{\bb E}[{\bf P}_{{\cal R}}]=(1-\alpha){\bf I}
.

This result shows that
\mbox{\bb E}\|{\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z}\|^{p}
concentrates around
\left(\sqrt{1-\alpha}\right)^{p}
. We choose
p
as the smallest value that ensures
\left(\sqrt{1-\alpha}\right)^{p}\leq\delta
for some
\delta>0
. This can be obtained if

p\geq{2\log({\delta})}/{\log(1-\alpha)}.

(22)

Our proposed practical rule in (
17
) is obtained after setting
\log(1/\delta)=1/4
and rounding up (
22
) to an integer.
The choice
\log(1/\delta)=1/4
is justified by following inequality due to Taylor’s series approximation

-\frac{1}{2\log(1-\alpha)}=\frac{1}{2(\alpha+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}+\frac{\alpha^{3}}{3}+\cdots)}\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha},

(23)

which implies that for any
\alpha
we have

p_{\text{rand}}\leq p_{\text{uni}}.

(24)

The comparison between (
16
) and (
17
) for different sampling rates
\alpha
is shown in
Figure 5
.
When
\alpha
is high (
0.3-0.5
),
p
will be
1
or
2
, and the interpolators will be extremely localized and sparse, which can be exploited for efficient inner product computation in
RAGS
, thus allowing us to sample efficiently at higher rates.

In the next section, we introduce our block-based sampling algorithm. We also include a complexity analysis of both algorithms, which illustrates the effect of
p
on complexity.

IV
Block-based PC subsampling using sub-graphs with self-loops

So far, we have developed a sampling algorithm (
RAGS
) that acts on the whole point cloud. As will be discussed in
Section IV-C
, the complexity of computing the interpolating vectors and sampling set selection scale significantly with the number of points
N
, even if graph sparsity is exploited.
We propose to employ a block-based sampling approach, reconstruction-aware block sampling (
RABS
) to further reduce the complexity of
RAGS
when
N
is large (e.g.,
N>10^{6}
).In We show that localizing the interpolating vectors to each block,
reduces their dimension,
and thus the overall complexity.
By performing sampling independently in each block, we also reduce the number of comparisons.
Note that during block partitioning, the graph edges across the block boundaries are removed.
To account for this loss of information while computing the block-wise interpolators, we propose adding self-loops (node weights) to the points along block boundaries.

IV-A

Problem formulation

We wish to obtain local sampling sets
\{{\cal S}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{L}
on subgraphs
\{{\cal G}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{L}
such that
\bigcup_{j=1}^{L}{\cal S}_{j}
is as close as possible to the global sampling set
{\cal S}^{*}
obtained for the entire graph
{\cal G}
.
The point set
{\cal P}
is partitioned into
L
non overlapping blocks (subsets of points) denoted by
{\cal B}_{1},{\cal B}_{2},\dots{\cal B}_{L}
such that
{\cal P}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{L}\mathcal{B}_{j}
, with
\lvert\mathcal{B}_{j}\rvert=b_{j},\forall j
. The corresponding vertex sets are denoted by
{\cal V}_{1},{\cal V}_{2},\dots{\cal V}_{L}
. We sample
s_{i}
points from the
i
th block such that
s_{i}/b_{i}\leq\alpha
, thus keeping the global sampling rate below
\alpha
. The parameter
p
is the same for all blocks.

We first consider the case of two blocks
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
and show the modifications that need to be made to
RAGS
for block-wise sampling. At the end of this section, we extend our solution to
L>2
blocks.

We denote
{\bf Q}^{{\cal G}}(p)
the matrix of interpolating vectors for graph
{\cal G}
.
Consider sub-graphs
{\cal G}_{1},{\cal G}_{2}
, with corresponding interpolator matrices
{\bf Q}^{{\cal G}_{1}}(p)
and
{\bf Q}^{{\cal G}_{2}}(p)
, which will be explicitely determined later in this section. Then, rewriting the objective (
13
) for
m\in\{1,2\}
,
{\cal S}_{m}
is the solution to

\displaystyle\max_{{\cal S}_{m}:|{\cal S}_{m}|\leq s_{m}}{\hbox{det}}\left(({\bf Q}^{{\cal G}_{m}}(p))_{{\cal V}_{m}{\cal S}_{m}}^{\top}({\bf Q}^{{\cal G}_{m}}(p))_{{\cal V}_{m}{\cal S}_{m}}\right).

(25)

The number of samples
s_{m}
is chosen so that
s_{m}/b_{m}\leq\alpha
and
s_{1}+s_{2}=s
.
While independent sampling using subgraphs
{\cal G}_{1}
and
{\cal G}_{2}
can reduce overall computational complexity, in general,
{\cal S}_{1}\cup{\cal S}_{2}\neq{\cal S}^{*}
if the interpolators and sub-graphs are computed naively. In particular, if the subgraphs are obtained by removing connections across block boundaries, (
25
) tends to oversample points near the block boundaries. A comparison of global sampling with this naive approach is depicted in
6(a)
and
6(b)
.

IV-B

Computing local interpolating vectors on sub-graphs

A solution to the poor sampling caused by block-wise graphs would be to truncate the interpolators computed based on
{\cal G}
so that they only have values in
{\cal G}_{m}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\bf Q}^{{\cal G}_{m}}(p)=({\bf Q}^{{\cal G}}(p))_{{\cal V}_{m}{\cal V}_{m}},

(26)

for
m=1,2
.
However, this still requires computing the global interpolating vectors, which is computationally expensive.

Instead, we propose to identify modified block-wise graphs,
\tilde{{\cal G}_{1}}
, whose local interpolators approximate the truncated interpolator of (
26
), i.e., such that

\min_{\tilde{{\cal G}}_{1}}\|{\bf Q}^{\tilde{{\cal G}}_{1}}(p)-({\bf Q}^{{\cal G}}(p))_{{\cal V}_{1}{\cal V}_{1}}\|_{F}^{2}.

(27)

From (
9
), we can equivalently write (
27
) in-terms of
{\bf Z}
as

\displaystyle J(\tilde{{\bf Z}}_{\tilde{{\cal G}}_{1}})=\left\lVert\sum_{l=1}^{p}\left(\begin{bmatrix}{\bf Z}_{{\cal V}_{1}{\cal V}_{1}}&{\bf Z}_{{\cal V}_{1}{\cal V}_{2}}\\
{\bf Z}_{{\cal V}_{2}{\cal V}_{1}}&{\bf Z}_{{\cal V}_{2}{\cal V}{2}}\end{bmatrix}^{l}\right)_{{\cal V}_{1}{\cal V}_{1}}-\sum_{l=1}^{p}(\tilde{{\bf Z}}_{\tilde{{\cal G}}_{1}})^{l}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}.

(28)

When
p=1
, it is easy to notice that minimizing
(
28
) with respect to graph operator
\tilde{{\bf Z}}_{\tilde{{\cal G}}_{1}}
for the graph
\tilde{{\cal G}}_{1}
will result in,

\tilde{{\bf Z}}_{\tilde{{\cal G}}_{1}}={\bf Z}_{{\cal V}_{1}{\cal V}_{1}}

But,
{\bf Z}_{{\cal V}_{1}{\cal V}_{1}}=\frac{1}{2}({\bf I}+{\bf D}_{{\cal V}_{1}{\cal V}_{1}}^{-1}{\bf A}_{{\cal V}_{1}{\cal V}_{1}})
, where
{\bf A}_{{\cal V}_{1}{\cal V}_{1}}
is the adjacency matrix of subgraph
{\cal G}_{1}
. The degree corresponding to the points
i\in{\cal V}_{1}
according to
{\bf D}_{{\cal V}_{1}{\cal V}_{1}}
can be written as,

[{\bf D}_{{\cal V}_{1}{\cal V}_{1}}]_{ii}=\begin{cases}\sum_{j\in{\cal V}_{1}}w_{ij}+\sum_{k\in{\cal V}_{2}}w_{ik},&\text{if }{\cal N}(i)\subset{\cal V}_{2}\\
\sum_{j\in{\cal V}_{1}}w_{ij},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(29)

where
\sum_{k\in{\cal V}_{2}}w_{ik}
is the node weight (self-loop weight) on the point that shares edges with the neighboring blocks.
Therefore, the optimal sub-graph
\tilde{{\cal G}}_{1}
when
p=1
is the sub-graph
{\cal G}_{1}
with node set
{\cal V}_{1}
and weighted self-loops (node weights) given by,

\hbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{ii}=\sum_{k\in{\cal V}_{2}}w_{ik}.

(30)

We can write the interpolating vectors for
p=1
with respect to the optimized sub-graph
\tilde{{\cal G}}_{1}
as,

{\bf Q}^{\tilde{{\cal G}}_{1}}(1)=\frac{1}{2}({\bf I}+({\bf D}_{{\cal G}_{1}}+\hbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{{\cal G}_{1}})^{-1}{\bf A}_{{\cal G}_{1}}),

(31)

where
{\bf A}_{{\cal G}_{1}}={\bf A}_{{\cal V}_{1}{\cal V}_{1}}
and
{\bf D}_{{\cal G}_{1}}
are the adjacency matrix and degree matrix of sub-graph
{\cal G}_{1}
respectively.
\hbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{{\cal G}_{1}}
is the diagonal self-loop matrix with weights given in (
30
). Thus, when
p=1
, minimizing the objective in (
28
) does not require computing the interpolating vectors of the entire graph
{\cal G}
. Instead, we can compute vectors independently on the sub-graphs of
{\cal G}
using (
31
) while satisfying (
26
).
However, for
p>1
, the truncation in (
26
) requires calculating interpolating vectors of the entire graph
{\cal G}
. To circumvent this, we obtain the sub-graph interpolators recursively from (
31
) using

{\bf Q}^{\tilde{{\cal G}}_{1}}(p)={\bf Z}_{{\cal V}_{1}{\cal V}_{1}}{\bf Q}^{\tilde{{\cal G}}_{1}}(p-1)+{\bf Q}^{\tilde{{\cal G}}_{1}}(1)=\sum_{l=1}^{p}({\bf Z}_{{\cal V}_{1}{\cal V}_{1}})^{l},

(32)

where
{\bf Q}^{\tilde{{\cal G}}_{1}}(1)={\bf Z}_{{\cal V}_{1}{\cal V}_{1}}

from (
31
). It is important to note that while (
32
) is not an optimal solution to (
27
) for
p>1
, it is a computationally efficient approximation. The same steps can be repeated to obtain interpolators for the sub-graph on
{\cal V}_{2}
.

The effect of self-loops in block-based sampling is demonstrated through a toy example in
Figure 6
.

shows
{\cal S}^{*}
obtained from sampling the entire graph using RAGS with
p=3
.
6(b)
shows the results of sampling sub-graphs independently without self-loops while

6(c)
shows the results of proposed RABS with self-loops. We can observe that
\tilde{{\cal S}}_{1}\cup\tilde{{\cal S}}_{2}
is closer than
{\cal S}_{1}\cup{\cal S}_{2}
to
{\cal S}^{*}
.

Next, we extend our approach to
L>2
blocks by leveraging PC partitioning with octrees
[
27
]
.
Initially, a
{\cal P}
is divided across the
x
,
y
, and
z
axes,
resulting in a total of
8
subsets (PC blocks) after one level of octree partitioning (refer to
[
28
]
). This process is repeated recursively for non-empty subsets until the desired resolution level (or block size) is reached.
If there are
L
blocks after the octree sub-division, then the self-loop weights for points in block
{\cal B}_{m}
can be calculated by simply considering the edges across the block boundaries, i.e.,

\hbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{ii}=\sum_{k\in{\cal N}({\cal B}_{m})}w_{ik}
, where
i\in{\cal B}_{m}
and
{\cal N}({\cal B}_{m})
are the points in the neighboring blocks. Therefore, for each block, we find the boundary points that share edges with the points in neighboring blocks and compute the edge weight for each point using (
30
). We can obtain self-loop matrix for the block
{\cal B}_{m}
by a sparse matrix-vector product given by,

\hbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{{\cal G}_{m}}={\hbox{diag}}({\bf A}_{m}{\bf c}^{(m)}),

(33)

where
{\bf A}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{b_{m}\times N}
is constructed by keeping the rows indexed by
{\cal V}_{m}
of the adjacency matrix of
{\cal G}
.
{\bf c}^{(m)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a vector defined as
{\bf c}^{(m)}_{i}=1,\forall i\notin{\cal V}_{m}
and zero otherwise.

IV-C

Complexity

In this section, we compare the run-time complexity of the proposed
RAGS
and
RABS
algorithms with existing graph signal sampling algorithms (see
Table I
).

IV-C
1
Complexity of RAGS

This algorithm has two main stages: preparation and sampling. The preparation step consists of obtaining the interpolators
\mathbf{q}_{i}^{(p)}
(i.e., the columns of
{\bf Q}(p)
) and the inner products
\langle\mathbf{q}^{(p)}_{i},\mathbf{q}^{(p)}_{j}\rangle
,
\forall i,j\in\{1,\cdots,N\}
. The sampling step implements the greedy update (
14
). An important quantity appearing in our complexity analysis is the maximum number of non-zero entries of each row and column of
{\bf Z}
, which is denoted by
\bar{d}
. For KNN graphs
\bar{d}=k+1
, the unweighted degree of
{\cal G}
is
\bar{d}-1
.
The complexity of the preparation step is dominated by the computation of the inner products
\langle\mathbf{q}^{(p)}_{i},\mathbf{q}^{(p)}_{j}\rangle
resulting in a total cost of
{\cal O}(\bar{d}^{3p}N)
. Once the inner products are available, the complexity of the greedy update is
{\cal O}(sN)
, which is a result of the number of additions and comparisons per iteration and the total number of iterations. See Appendix
-A
for a detailed explanation.

IV-C
2
Complexity of RABS

Because
RABS
is based on
RAGS
applied to each block independently, we can estimate its complexity using similar arguments. Since the graphs in each block are sub-graphs of the original graph, the parameter
\bar{d}
estimated for the whole graph can be used as an upper bound for each sub-graph.
Because the interpolator vectors have lower dimensions, the complexity of the preparation stage can be reduced to
{\cal O}(\bar{d}^{p}b_{i}^{2})
for the
i
th block (see
Remark 1
in the Appendix). Thus, given that
N\leq\sum_{i=1}^{L}b_{i}^{2}\leq\bar{B}N
, where
\bar{B}=\max_{i}b_{i}
is the maximum number of points in a block, the overall complexity of interpolator and inner product computations is
{\cal O}(\bar{d}^{p}\sum_{i=1}^{L}b_{i}^{2})={\cal O}(\bar{B}\bar{d}^{p}N)
.
Block partitioning using octree has complexity
{\cal O}(N\log(N))
, which is also part of the preparation step. Greedy sampling of each block runs in
{\cal O}(s_{i}b_{i})
time, resulting in total complexity of sampling equal to
{\cal O}(\sum_{i=1}^{L}s_{i}b_{i})
. If
s_{i}={\alpha b_{i}}
and
\alpha=s/N
we have that

\sum_{i=1}^{L}s_{i}b_{i}\leq s\max_{i}b_{i}=s\bar{B},

(34)

which can be much smaller than
{\cal O}(sN)
, especially when the number of points per block or the size of the sampling set is small compared to
N
. In our experiments,
N
can be in the order of hundreds of thousands, while
\bar{B}
can be a few hundred points, resulting in orders of magnitude runtime reduction.
Note that this complexity reduction with respect to global sampling is not due to parallelization.

IV-C
3
Comparison with AVM and BS-GDA

We now compare the complexity of the proposed
RAGS
and
RABS
with the existing ED-free graph signal sampling algorithms - approximate volume maximization (AVM)
[
20
]
and BS-GDA
[
24
]
(see
Table I
). The overall complexity is divided into two parts: 1) preparation and 2) sampling set selection. In the sampling step, most algorithms adopt a greedy selection strategy.

For sparse KNN graphs, since
|{\cal E}|=kN
, the preparation step of the four approaches scales near linearly with
N
, with different factors depending on their specific parameters.
The main difference is observed in actual runtimes (see
Section V
), where the runtime of AVM is dominated by
the estimation of cut-off frequencies through graph filtering, and for BS-GDA complexity is dominated by Gershgorin disk alignment of all nodes.
In the proposed methods, complexity is dominated by inner product computation, where the complexity depends exponentially on
p
. This highlights the importance of choosing
p
as small as possible, which is achieved using the methods from
Section III
. The dependence on
p
is also improved by RABS from
3p
to
p
, due to the use of more localized interpolators.

The sampling stage of AVM involves computing interpolators through spectral domain filtering in every iteration, and BS-GDA performs
\mathcal{O}(\log_{2}\frac{1}{\epsilon})
binary searches in every iteration. The sampling stage of RAGS and RABS does not involve additional computation apart from evaluating the objective function from the precomputed inner products.
While block sampling can be combined with AVM and BS-GDA, the resulting runtime would still be high because of the expensive preparation stage, which involves graph frequency analysis (see
Section V-C
).

VI
Conclusion

We have proposed scalable graph-based sampling algorithms for PC attributes.
We first proposed a reconstruction-aware global sampling set selection algorithm based on a fast reconstruction method. We showed how to construct interpolators that can be used for sampling. The proposed interpolators can be efficiently implemented without sacrificing performance by exploiting the interplay between the localization (sparsity) of the interpolators and the sampling rate. We proposed a method to select the suitable localization parameter for sampling.
To further reduce the sampling complexity, we proposed a block-based algorithm, where we sample blocks independently. We proposed a method to locally compute interpolators by adding self-loops to the sub-graphs, leading to an order of magnitude reduction in complexity and runtime, while maintaining the good performance of global sampling.
We evaluated the proposed global and block sampling algorithms on large PCs. We showed that our proposed sampling algorithms are up to
50
times faster than existing graph-based sampling algorithms by ensuring similar or better reconstruction. Our sampling algorithm outperforms commonly used PC sampling techniques - uniform and geometry feature preserving sampling with respect to reconstruction accuracy. Additionally, we showcased a practical application in PC attribute compression through chroma subsampling, which resulted in bitrate savings of up to
11\%
.

Complexity of RAGS

Since
{\bf Q}(1)={\bf Z}
, we can compute
{\bf Q}(p)
using the recursive formula
{\bf Q}(t)={\bf Z}+{\bf Q}(t-1){\bf Z}
, and thus the complexity of computing
{\bf Q}(p)
depends on the cost of sparse matrix product and sparse matrix addition. For
p=2
, note that the matrix
{\bf Z}^{2}
has at most
{\cal O}(\bar{d}^{2}N)
entries, and each of its entries is computed by the inner product of two
\bar{d}
-sparse vectors, thus the total complexity of computing
{\bf Z}^{2}
is
{\cal O}(\bar{d}^{3}N)
. The matrix addition has complexity proportional to the number of entries in
{\bf Z}^{2}
, which is
{\cal O}(\bar{d}^{2}N)
. In general for
t\geq 2
, the product
{\bf Q}(t-1){\bf Z}
has
{\cal O}(\bar{d}^{t}N)
non-zero entries and each entry is the inner product of a
\bar{d}
-sparse vector with a
\bar{d}^{t-1}
-sparse vector, which has cost
{\cal O}(\bar{d}^{t-1})
per inner product, thus the computation of the product
{\bf Q}(t-1){\bf Z}
has cost
{\cal O}(\bar{d}^{2t}N)
. The complexity of the matrix addition is dominated by the number of entries of the densest matrix, which is
{\cal O}(\bar{d}^{t}N)
. Because the recursion must be applied
p-1
times, the total complexity of computing
{\bf Q}(p)
is dominated by the cost matrix multiplication applied
p
times, which is given by
{\cal O}(p\bar{d}^{2p}N)
.
To compute the inner products between the columns of
{\bf Q}(p)
, we can compute the product
({\bf Q}(p))^{\top}{\bf Q}(p)
. This matrix has
\bar{d}^{2p}N
non-zero entries, and each of them is computed by an inner product between
\bar{d}^{p}
-sparse vectors, resulting in total complexity of
{\cal O}(\bar{d}^{3p}N)
.
The complexity of the preparation step is thus dominated by the computation of
({\bf Q}(p))^{\top}{\bf Q}(p)
over the computation of
{\bf Q}(p)
, which results in a total complexity of
{\cal O}(\bar{d}^{3p}N)
.

Remark 1
.

Note that if
\bar{d}
is large (e.g., for irregular graphs) or if
p
is large,
{\cal O}(\bar{d}^{2t}N)
may significantly overestimate the number of entries in
{\bf Q}(t)
, in which case we say that the number of entries is
{\cal O}(N^{2})
, resulting in a worst-case complexity
{\cal O}(\bar{d}^{p}N^{2})
. In this work, we can assume that
\bar{d}
is relatively small with respect to
N
since the point clouds are dense and points belong to the 3D integer grid, and
p
can be kept small by using techniques developed in
Section III
.

Regarding sampling, since the inner products between columns of
{\bf Q}(p)
are already computed, the greedy sampling set update only involves adding
{\cal O}(N)
inner products and solving the maximization problem in (
14
). This has
\mathcal{O}(N)
cost per iteration. Since we have to repeat this step
s
times, the overall complexity of the sampling set update is
\mathcal{O}(sN)
.

Proof of
Lemma III.1

We divide the proof in two Lemmas, one for each equality.

Lemma .1
.

The closed form solution (
3
) for reconstruction can be expressed equivalently in terms of
{\bf Z}
defined in (
4
)

\hat{{\bf f}}=({\bf I}-{\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z})^{-1}{\bf P}_{{\cal S}}{\bf f}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf f}_{{\cal S}}\\
-{\bf L}_{{\cal R}{\cal R}}^{-1}{\bf L}_{{\cal R}{\cal S}}{\bf f}_{{\cal S}}\end{bmatrix}.

(37)

Proof.

Using
{\bf P}_{{\cal S}}{\bf f}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf f}_{{\cal S}}^{\top}&{\bf 0}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
, and the block matrix inverse formula
[
40
]
we can show that

({\bf I}-{\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z})^{-1}{\bf P}_{{\cal S}}{\bf f}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf I}_{{\cal S}{\cal S}}\\
({\bf I}_{{\cal R}{\cal R}}-{\bf Z}_{{\cal R}{\cal R}})^{-1}{\bf Z}_{{\cal R}{\cal S}}\end{bmatrix}{\bf f}_{{\cal S}}.

(38)

It is easy to check that
({\bf I}_{{\cal R}{\cal R}}-{\bf Z}_{{\cal R}{\cal R}})^{-1}{\bf Z}_{{\cal R}{\cal S}}=-{\bf L}_{{\cal R}{\cal R}}^{-1}{\bf L}_{{\cal R}{\cal S}}
, which combined with (
3
) produces the desired result.
∎

Lemma .2
.

We have the identity

({\bf I}-{\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z})^{-1}{\bf P}_{{\cal S}}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}({\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z})^{l}{\bf P}_{{\cal S}}.

(39)

Proof.

The truncated interpolator matrix is given by

\sum_{l=0}^{k}({\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z})^{l}{\bf P}_{{\cal S}}.

(40)

This sum will converge if and only if
\|{\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z}\|<1
, which is proven in
Lemma .3
. Therefore, from Neumann series for matrices (Th. 5.6.12 of
[
40
]
) we have that
\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{l=0}^{i}({\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z})^{l}=({\bf I}-{\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z})^{-1}
.
∎

Lemma .3
.

The operator
{\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z}
is strictly non-expansive, that is,
\|{\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z}{\bf x}\|<1
for all
{\bf x}
.

Proof.

Note that
\|{\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z}{\bf x}\|^{2}=\sum_{i\in{\cal R}}(\langle{\bf b}_{i},{\bf x}\rangle)^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\langle{\bf b}_{i},{\bf x}\rangle)^{2}=\|{\bf Z}{\bf x}\|^{2}\leq\|{\bf x}\|^{2}
, where
{\bf b}_{i}
is defined in Appendix
-D
.
The matrices
{\bf Z}
and
{\bf P}_{{\cal R}}
are both non expansive. In fact, for connected graphs
\|{\bf Z}{\bf x}\|=\|{\bf x}\|
if and only if
{\bf x}=\alpha\cdot\mathbf{1}

[
40
]
.
If
{\bf x}=\alpha\mathbf{1}
for some
\alpha\neq 0
, the first inequality becomes strict because
{\bf Z}
is a non negative irreducible matrix. If
{\bf x}\neq\alpha\mathbf{1}

the second inequality becomes strict.

Proof of
Lemma III.2

We consider the error vector between the
p^{th}
order approximation of the reconstructed signal and the original signal
{\bf e}^{(p)}=\hat{{\bf f}}^{(p)}-{\bf f}
.
Using the triangular inequality, we arrive at

\lVert{\bf e}^{(p)}\rVert\leq\lVert\hat{{\bf f}}^{(p)}-\hat{{\bf f}}\rVert+\lVert\hat{{\bf f}}-{\bf f}\rVert.

(41)

We will focus only on the first term (
41
), which depends on
p
. From the definition of
\hat{{\bf f}}^{(p)}
we can show that

\hat{{\bf f}}^{(p)}-\hat{{\bf f}}={\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z}(\hat{{\bf f}}^{(p-1)}-\hat{{\bf f}})=({\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z})^{p}(\hat{{\bf f}}^{(0)}-\hat{{\bf f}}),

(42)

which implies the error bound

\lVert\hat{{\bf f}}^{(p)}-\hat{{\bf f}}\rVert\leq\lVert{\bf P}_{{\cal R}}{\bf Z}\rVert^{p}\lVert\hat{{\bf f}}^{(0)}-\hat{{\bf f}}\rVert.

(43)

We can bound the error of the initialization
by

\lVert\hat{{\bf f}}^{(0)}-\hat{{\bf f}}\rVert\leq\|\hat{{\bf f}}^{(0)}-{\bf f}\|+\|{\bf f}-\hat{{\bf f}}\|\leq\|{\bf f}\|+\|{\bf f}-\hat{{\bf f}}\|,

(44)

where the second inequality is due to
\|\hat{{\bf f}}^{(0)}-{\bf f}\|=\|{\bf f}_{{\cal R}}\|\leq\|{\bf f}\|
. Replacing back into (
41
