Osteoporosis screening and management framework for community-based primary care in Hong Kong: A Delphi consensus study
Authors: Ching-Lung Cheung, Hing-Sun Chan, Pak-Kin Chan, Alvina Wing-Sze Chau, Cindy Ming-Wah Choi, Dicky Ka-Chun Chow, Dilys Yan-Wing Chow, Hoi-Yan Chung, Bonnie So-Kuen Lam, Wing-Wo Lam, Bonnie Leung, Yuen-Lok Ma, Janus Chi-Keung Wong, Sze-Hung Wong, Kenny Wai-Ming Wong, William Ho-Ching Yuen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesOsteoporosis poses a significant public health challenge as it leads to an increased incidence of fractures. This can result in considerable disability and substantial financial burdens. With the rapidly aging population in Hong Kong, the incidence and impact of osteoporotic fractures are expected to rise, underscoring the need for a coordinated, community-based care model to identify at-risk older adults and offer timely management.MethodsThis study adopted a Delphi methodology to develop consensus statements and a structured framework for community osteoporosis screening and management in Hong Kong. A multidisciplinary panel of 15 experts with experience in osteoporosis care in the primary care setting participated in three iterative rounds of surveys that collected anonymized ratings and feedback.ResultsA total of 31 consensus statements were established covering risk assessment, diagnosis, referral, and the care pathway of osteoporosis in the primary care setting. The statements provide practical recommendations for a systematic framework that streamlines workflow and delineates roles and responsibilities for primary care professionals and carers. This guidance is intended for primary care services, including community pharmacies, district health centers, and elderly service centers engaged in osteoporosis screening and management of individuals diagnosed with the condition.ConclusionsThe consensus recommendations advocate for the development of a sustainable, community-driven service framework that promotes routine risk assessment, timely initiation of treatment and coordinated long-term management. Implementation of this model has the potential to improve patient outcomes, reduce fracture-related morbidity, and alleviate the overall healthcare burden of osteoporosis in Hong Kong.

Introduction

Fragility fractures are associated with osteoporosis, a chronic disorder characterized by low bone density and deteriorated bone microarchitecture. These fractures can lead to chronic pain, reduced mobility and independence, increased comorbidities, and higher mortality rates, and also impose a substantial financial burden on the healthcare system, with costs primarily stemming from surgical treatment, hospitalization, rehabilitation, and long-term disability [[1], [2], [3]]. As the global population continues to age rapidly [4], the incidence and associated healthcare costs of osteoporosis-related fractures are projected to rise alarmingly.

According to the International Osteoporosis Foundation (IOF), one in three women and one in five men aged 50 years or above may experience a fragility fracture in their lifetime [5]. In Hong Kong, the number of fragility fractures surged by 51.2% over 14 years—from 5596 cases in 2004 to 8465 in 2018 [6]. The most severe type of fracture is hip fracture, which requires immediate surgical intervention. The incidence rose by 30.9% during the same period [6]. Without proactive prevention strategies, the number of hip fractures is expected to double, reaching around 14 500 by 2040, and more than triple, reaching over 27 000 by 2050 [3,7]. Compounding the issue, patients with a history of a fragility fracture are at high risk of subsequent fractures. Nearly half (49.5%) of those who experience an initial fracture will sustain another within just two years, a period known as the “imminent fracture” window, during which patients are especially vulnerable to repeated, debilitating injuries [6], increased disability, and a significant loss in societal productivity [3]. The direct cost of hip fracture is projected to rise from HKD 600 million in 2018 to HKD 1.9 billion by 2050 [7]. Accordingly, establishing a feasible, structured model for osteoporosis screening, diagnosis, and management is essential to facilitate early detection, prevent fragility fractures, and reduce the burden on patients and the healthcare system.

Emerging evidence highlights the importance of osteoporosis screening and management within primary care settings to prevent osteoporotic fractures. A meta-analysis of three randomized controlled trials involving over 40 000 participants revealed that implementing osteoporosis screening in primary care settings led to a 6% reduction in major osteoporotic fractures and a 17% reduction in hip fractures [8]. These findings underscore the potential of early identification and timely intervention to alleviate the growing burden of osteoporosis in aging populations. Notably, osteoporosis has been included in Hong Kong's Primary Care Reference Framework, published by the Health Bureau to provide recommended standards of care in osteoporosis [9]. This inclusion indicates that osteoporosis is recognized as a priority disease for management in the primary care setting.

Primary care serves as the first point of care for chronic disease management by enhancing screening, diagnosis, and referral to appropriate family doctors through community-based prevention strategies to address the rising prevalence of chronic conditions. Therefore, it is increasingly important to have an established framework within the primary care setting for screening and the care pathway for osteoporosis, a major cause of fractures in the aging population. Through a structured Delphi process involving a multidisciplinary expert panel, 31 consensus statements were developed to form the foundational framework for community-based osteoporosis screening, referral and management, with the aim of improving early detection and optimizing care pathways.

Methods

An osteoporosis working group was established under the leadership of the Osteoporosis Society of Hong Kong and the Department of Pharmacology and Pharmacy of The University of Hong Kong. Purposive sampling was employed to identify 15 multidisciplinary experts for the establishment of the working group [10,11]: primary and specialty care physicians (N = 4), allied health professionals (eg, pharmacists and nurses) from community health centers and community service centers (N = 3), service planners from community health centers and community service centers, including elderly centers, community pharmacies, and District Health Centers (DHCs) (N = 7), and public health policy makers (N = 1). A total of 15 recruited experts would be sufficient to achieve a higher response rate and improve the validity of consensus outcomes [11]. The selection criteria of experts included a minimum of eight years of professional experience in either clinical practice for healthcare professionals, or service management or research in elderly care for non-healthcare professionals, to ensure representation across key disciplines.

Prior to the development of the consensus statements, a literature review was conducted in PubMed to identify evidence on osteoporosis screening, referral pathways for diagnosis and long-term management. The review also examined international practices and recommendations from clinical guidelines on osteoporosis screening, diagnosis, and management to ensure alignment with current standards of care. Following the literature review, an investigative questionnaire was developed for 35 community health centers and community service centers to assess current osteoporosis screening practices in Hong Kong and to identify the service gaps and operational challenges.

Delphi method was adopted to facilitate structured consensus-building. Panelists were invited to participate in three iterative rounds of voting and commentary through an online survey. Responses were anonymized to ensure unbiased feedback. Two outcomes were assessed:(1)Implementability (primary outcome). Implementability was rated on a 4-point scale from a practical perspective, reflecting the perceived likelihood that the item can be executed in real-world settings (strongly agree, agree, disagree, or strongly disagree). It was designated as the primary outcome because the expert panel was expected to broadly recognize, in principle, the clinical value and importance of osteoporosis screening and management. Based on prior experience and existing evidence, the primary challenge in advancing community-based osteoporosis services lies not in conceptual acceptance, but in the feasibility of execution within real-world healthcare settings. Therefore, prioritizing implementability allowed the study to focus on identifying service components that experts considered practically achievable and actionable in routine practice.(2)Relevance (secondary outcome). Relevance was rated on a 3-point scale from a priority perspective, indicating the perceived priority for inclusion in the service framework: primary goal (very important and must be included in the service framework), secondary goal (important and worthy of inclusion but not critical), and no goal (not important and irrelevant to the service framework). It was assessed as a secondary outcome to confirm that the proposed statements were clinically meaningful and aligned with current service priorities.

For each statement, agreement on the primary outcome of implementability was expressed as a percentage (%). With reference to the agreement standards adopted in previous studies, the consensus cutoff level of this study was set at 70% [12]. Thus, a statement reached positive consensus if ≥70% of experts rated “agree” or “strongly agree” on the 4-point Likert-type scale, while negative consensus was attained when ≥70% rated “disagree” or “strongly disagree.” Positive consensus statements in the Round 1 Delphi were considered recommendations for implementation in the service framework. For statements without consensus, qualitative comments were summarized and presented for further evaluation in Round 2.

In addition, assessment results for the secondary outcome of relevance were expressed as percentages, based on the proportion of experts making a specific rating. If any expert rated “no goal” (>0%) for a given statement in Round 1 Delphi, the statement was also refined based on feedback and further evaluated in Round 2.

In Round 2 Delphi, the same panel of experts re-assessed the statements that had not reached positive or negative consensus in Round 1. Delphi survey package for this round included: (1) experts’ percentage agreement on the primary outcome of implementability; (2) anonymous qualitative comments; and (3) assessment results on the secondary outcome of relevance from all experts. Data collection and analysis followed the same procedures as in Round 1.

Statements neither reaching positive nor negative consensus in Round 2 were further evaluated in Round 3. Qualitative comments were summarized for each statement, and the statements were further refined based on the experts’ suggestions to improve clarity and feasibility. Final consensus status was determined in Round 3 using the same predefined consensus threshold. Statements that ultimately achieved positive consensus were incorporated into the final community-based osteoporosis screening and management framework.

Consensus statements

Service framework of community-based osteoporosis screening and management in Hong Kong

Primary healthcare in Hong Kong has undergone substantial development in recent years. In response to an aging population and the growing burden of chronic diseases, the government introduced the Primary Healthcare Blueprint [13], outlining a transition from a treatment-centric to a prevention-oriented model. Key initiatives have been progressively launched with encouraging early results, including (1) the establishments of DHCs and community-based medical facilities providing screening, health coaching, and chronic disease management, (2) promotion of family doctors’ engagement for enhancing continuity of care and improving care of chronic diseases through regular check-ups and ongoing support in the community, and (3) transformation of community pharmacy, expanding their role beyond medication dispensing to the provision of comprehensive health services that enhance community well-being, improve accessibility to affordable medications, and support preventative care.

Riding on this momentum, there remains a critical unmet need in fracture prevention among a rapidly aging population that can be effectively addressed through osteoporosis screening [8]. This creates an opportune moment to explore how best to coordinate efforts between primary care and the community. Following three rounds of Delphi voting among 15 representative experts, consensus has been reached on 31 statements defining a service framework of community-based osteoporosis screening and management suitable for implementation in Hong Kong. These fell into three key areas: (1) the needs for community osteoporosis screening and management service, (2) definition of service scope and workflow of osteoporosis screening and management, and (3) strategic imperatives for establishing sustainable community-based osteoporosis service framework. Unlike the conventional physician-led osteoporosis screening model, this service framework is primarily community-driven, with community health centers and community service centers playing a key role in identifying individuals at high risk of osteoporosis and/or fragility fractures. The model emphasizes close collaboration among community health centers and community service centers, primary care physicians, and specialists, with clearly defined roles and responsibilities (Table 2). Recognizing the variability in operational models across community settings, two complementary service workflow models are proposed to support implementation in a stepwise approach and more flexible frontline settings. Need for community osteoporosis screening and management service

The panelists reached consensus on several critical points related to osteoporosis in Hong Kong, acknowledging that it is a growing yet under-recognized public health issue among the aging population. The condition significantly contributes to increased fracture risks and imposes significant healthcare and economic burdens [7]. As fragility fractures are largely preventable through early detection and timely management of osteoporosis [3], there is an urgent need to develop and expand accessible community-based osteoporosis screening and management services. Implementing these services not only meets critical healthcare needs but also enhances the overall effectiveness in preventing fragility fractures.

Definition of service scope and workflow of osteoporosis screening and management

The consensus outlines a comprehensive two-step approach for community osteoporosis screening in Hong Kong, emphasizing the use of validated risk tools to identify high-risk individuals, followed by confirmation with a dual-energy absorptiometry (DXA) scan. The first step involves integrating the identification of high-risk individuals into community health services, such as health education talks, public awareness campaigns, and community screening events, to enhance both efficiency and accessibility.

Risk factors for early identification

Understanding the risk factors associated with osteoporosis is crucial for effective screening and enables healthcare providers to implement timely interventions. Chinese osteoporosis screening algorithm (COSA) is a validated screening tool for individuals aged ≥50 years, and those classified as high risk have been shown to have approximately nine-fold higher fracture risk compared with low-risk individuals [14,15]. The experience to date with COSA in community settings has been positive according to the internal local survey of 35 community services centers. Both community centers and screening participants considered COSA an easy-to-use, credible, and reliable risk assessment tool (unpublished observation).

Based on the operational design of primary care services, two service workflow models are proposed. Model 1 adopts a stepwise approach screening focused on high-risk individuals: first identifying the targeted population who meet predefined very high-risk criteria, with those who do not meet very high-risk criteria subsequently undergoing COSA screening according to the defined priority groups. Model 2 provides a more flexible approach for frontline workers, allowing either COSA screening irrespective of age or risk profile, or initial identification of very high-risk factors prior to COSA, depending on the clinical context and communication capacity of the individual.

Model 1: Stepwise approach screening with osteoporosis assessment for targeted population with very high-risk criteria followed by COSA

The primary objective of this model is to maximize the yield of screening by prioritizing individuals at relatively high risk of osteoporosis. Model 1 therefore emphasizes targeted screening based on predefined priority groups, followed by a risk-stratified management pathway. Community health centers and community service centers should refer individuals with established “very high-risk” factors for osteoporosis directly to medical consultation, bypassing the need for additional risk assessment tools (Fig. 1). Nevertheless, three groups of individuals were recommended to prioritize for osteoporosis risk assessment using COSA as follows.Group 1:All women aged 65 years and older and men aged 70 years and older should be prioritized for osteoporosis screening with risk tool.

All women aged 65 years and older and men aged 70 years and older should be prioritized for osteoporosis screening with risk tool.

Age is a significant risk factor for osteoporosis, and both international and local guidelines advocate for prioritizing older adults for screening [1,8]. Delaying screening until age 75 or 80 years could diminish the effectiveness of early intervention and hinder efforts to reduce the disease burden of osteoporosis. The working group agreed that these age groups should constitute the highest-priority cohort for COSA.Group 2:Middle-aged adults (females aged 50–64 years, males aged 50–69 years) with any one of the following risk factors should also be offered osteoporosis screening with risk tool.

Middle-aged adults (females aged 50–64 years, males aged 50–69 years) with any one of the following risk factors should also be offered osteoporosis screening with risk tool.

Risk factors include: (1) family history of osteoporosis or fragility fractures, (2) previous fragility fracture, (3) low body weight (or low BMI <18.5 kg/m2), (4) height loss (compared to young adulthood, or loss >2 cm within three years), (5) early menopause (≤45 years), (6) presence of diseases that can lower bone density or increase fracture risk, (7) low calcium intake (<500 mg/day), eg, lack of dairy product/tofu/calcium supplements, (8) low vitamin D intake, (9) smoker or past smoker, (10) prolonged immobility, and (11) history of falls.

A risk-stratified extension of screening should be applied to middle-aged adults whose absolute fracture risk is substantially increased by specific clinical or lifestyle factors, despite being younger than the highest-priority age group in Group 1. The listed risk factors are well-recognized correlates of low bone mineral density and fragility fracture taking reference from the international guidelines [16,17] and thus justify earlier assessment where resources permit.

The panel considered this group a secondary priority for COSA. Routine blood testing (eg, serum vitamin D) is generally impractical in community settings. Instead, structured enquiry about habitual intake of calcium- and vitamin D-rich foods and supplements was endorsed as feasible alternative to identify at-risk individuals and to guide preventive counselling.Group 3:Eligible middle-aged adults (women aged 50–64, men aged 50–69) without the above risk factors may also be considered for osteoporosis screening with risk tool, subject to the availability of resources.

Eligible middle-aged adults (women aged 50–64, men aged 50–69) without the above risk factors may also be considered for osteoporosis screening with risk tool, subject to the availability of resources.

Extending the use of COSA to middle-aged adults without established risk factors might help identify further high-risk cases in primary care or community settings. Nevertheless, this group was regarded as the lowest priority for proactive, service-initiated risk assessment. Active screening with COSA in this population should be pursued only when community centers have adequate staffing and resources. A patient-led approach may also be considered, for instance, by encouraging eligible adults to self-complete the COSA via a web-based format, with those identified as high risk then encouraged to contact community centers for further evaluation and management.

Where human resources and service capacity permit, screening services may be broadened to include younger or low-risk populations, with the aim of improving early identification of osteoporosis risk.

Model 2: Flexible approach screening with either osteoporosis assessment based on very-high-risk criteria or COSA regardless of risk criteria

Under this model, frontline workers may initiate COSA screening irrespective of an individual's age or baseline risk profile, or may first assess the presence of very-high-risk factors (Fig. 2). This approach allows timely escalation of care while accommodating variability in individuals' ability to articulate clinical risk factors. In practice, COSA may be more straightforward and efficient to implement than assessment of very high-risk factors, particularly in busy community settings. For example, COSA screening may be particularly advantageous for elderly individuals who have difficulty articulating their medical history or recognizing very high-risk factors. In contrast, direct identification of very high-risk factors may be more efficient in individuals with apparent clinical features such as recent fragility fractures or prolonged steroid use.

In line with international guidelines [16,17], individuals with a history of osteoporosis (T-score ≤–2.5) or a fragility fracture, regardless of recency, should be offered prompt initiation of pharmacological treatment, which may be managed in primary care when clinical complexity is low. Individuals with suspected secondary osteoporosis, multiple comorbidities, or recurrent or recent fragility fractures should be referred directly to specialist services for further evaluation and management.

Recommended care pathway for individuals identified high-risk by risk tool

For individuals identified as high risk by the risk assessment tool, a structured four-step care pathway is recommended. This pathway comprises: (1) risk communication to support patient making informed decision of next step management; (2) referral for DXA scan and/or medical evaluation to confirm osteoporosis diagnosis; (3) osteoporosis diagnosis and initial management; and (4) community follow-up and monitoring to maintain care continuity and promote long-term bone health.Step 1:Risk communication to support patient making informed decision of next step management

Risk communication to support patient making informed decision of next step management

Clear and structured risk communication should be provided to ensure that high-risk individuals understand their personal risk and the need for timely diagnosis and treatment. Appropriately designed patient educational materials can support discussions and facilitate effective risk communication. Where resources permit, non-physician counselling and fall-risk assessments may be considered to further reinforce risk awareness and the urgency of subsequent management.Step 2:Referral for DXA scan and/or medical evaluation to confirm osteoporosis diagnosis

Referral for DXA scan and/or medical evaluation to confirm osteoporosis diagnosis

Prompt referral for DXA scans is essential for timely diagnostic confirmation of osteoporosis. To facilitate efficient referrals and patient engagement, providing simple, clear referral guides is recommended. These materials can facilitate communication between high-risk individuals and their general practitioners and to assist patients in navigating the referral process. Effective tracking of referrals and assessments is critical to maximize the benefits of early management of osteoporosis. DHCs, elderly centers and community pharmacies are recommended to remind high-risk individuals to complete their assessments promptly following their identification, particularly if assessments have not yet been completed, thereby reinforcing continuity of care and timely intervention.

Maintaining continuity of care and minimizing loss to follow-up care are critical to achieving the full benefits of osteoporosis risk assessment. However, systematic and proactive tracking of all referrals would substantially increase workload in resource-limited community settings with constrained staffing. The panel therefore endorsed a pragmatic approach: DHCs, elderly centers and community pharmacies should provide opportunistic reminders to high-risk individuals—when they become aware that the recommended DXA scan or clinical assessment has not been completed—thereby balancing coordinated care with operational realities. This strategy also reinforces patient responsibility by encouraging individuals to take an active role in managing their bone health.Step 3:Osteoporosis diagnosis and initial management

Osteoporosis diagnosis and initial management

For high-risk individuals who are further confirmed to have an osteoporosis diagnosis via DXA scan, the panel reached consensus on initiating tailored management plans based on T-scores. High-risk individuals and those with confirmed osteoporosis are encouraged to engage with available community resources. The service scope was refined since most of the panelists emphasized that the primary role of community centers and community pharmacies is to support long-term bone health. This is achieved through patient education, health-promotion activities, reinforcement of lifestyle recommendations from healthcare professionals, promotion of adherence to recommended management plans, and encouragement of sustained bone health behaviors—ultimately contributing to better long-term outcomes and enhanced quality of life.

Primary care physicians should lead the management of uncomplicated osteoporosis, while timely referral to specialist services is recommended for individuals with secondary causes, significant comorbidities, or recurrent fractures despite treatment. For individuals who have sustained a major osteoporotic fracture within the preceding two years, anabolic therapy may be considered in accordance with current guidelines.Step 4:Community follow-up and monitoring to maintain care continuity and promote long-term bone health

Community follow-up and monitoring to maintain care continuity and promote long-term bone health

Continued follow-up and monitoring in the community plays an important role in supporting ongoing osteoporosis management and long-term bone health. Educational and exercise programs focusing on bone health, muscle strengthening, and fall prevention are recommended for individuals identified as high risk or with confirmed osteoporosis Where feasible, periodic reminders for follow-up medical consultations and repeat DXA assessments may be provided to support ongoing monitoring in cases of complex osteoporosis, including secondary causes, refractory disease, or persistently high fracture risk, general practitioners may consider referral to specialist services for further evaluation and advanced management.

In line with the established primary care framework, primary care physicians are responsible for leading the management of confirmed osteoporosis. This includes initiating and monitoring treatment, assessing treatment response, and coordinating referrals to specialists for complex or refractory cases. While community centers and pharmacies serve as key partners in maintaining continuity of care, systematic follow-up of all patients is not expected due to limited resources. However, where feasible, these centers may periodically remind patients to attend scheduled medical follow-ups and repeat DXA scans as recommended by their doctors. This shared-care approach acknowledges the complementary roles of clinical and community services. By offering timely reminders during routine contacts or program participation, community centers and pharmacies can help reduce loss to follow-up, support adherence to long-term management plans, and reinforce patients’ engagement with their own bone health, without assuming responsibilities that require direct clinical oversight.

Recommended care pathway for individuals identified low-risk by risk tool

For low-risk individuals identified through the COSA risk assessment, ongoing education and periodic reassessments are essential, especially when there are changes in individuals’ health status. Community centers and community pharmacies should emphasize lifestyle modifications and sustained awareness of bone health.

It is noteworthy that the panel recommended a more flexible reassessment interval with a risk assessment tool for low-risk osteoporosis individuals, rather than a fixed follow-up interval of every two years, due to limited staffing and resources. In Hong Kong, routine DXA retesting for low-risk patients every five years, as per international guidance [18], is unlikely to be feasible at the population level. Periodic reassessment using a simple risk tool such as COSA, which can be completed in less than 1 min, offers a pragmatic alternative. This helps maintain awareness of bone health, facilitates timely identification of individuals whose risk status may have changed and is more scalable within community-based services.

Strategic imperatives for establishing sustainable community-based osteoporosis service framework

The successful implementation of a comprehensive osteoporosis care pathway necessitates establishing robust referral networks, forging of multidisciplinary partnerships, delivering structured training for healthcare professionals, standardizing protocols and materials, securing sustainable funding and resources, and promoting co-payment models to enhance service affordability. Two key elements from the survey pertaining to a sustainable community-based osteoporosis framework are as follows:(1)Community centers and community pharmacies are encouraged to explore partnerships with multidisciplinary organizations such as universities, government departments, industry partners and different local organizations to develop diverse and adaptable bone health promotion programs that strengthen preventive efforts and support long-term care. Collaboration among community centers or pharmacies is currently uncommon, and this was recognized as a practical constraint. The panel therefore recommended that these centers or pharmacies seek external partnerships with diverse stakeholders, such as universities, government agencies, non-governmental organizations, and industry partners. Project-based collaborations offer greater flexibility, enabling different stakeholders to contribute complementary expertise, such as clinical knowledge, research capabilities, and communication platforms. By leveraging these broader networks, community centers and pharmacies can develop more diverse, adaptable, and sustainable bone health promotion programs for the public.(2)Co-pay models, such as the elderly health care voucher (EHCV), should be promoted or considered to fund DXA scans and/or subsequent treatment management. Costs represent a significant barrier to osteoporosis screening and treatment among older adults, particularly those with low income, who may forgo DXA scans and other preventive services. Increased utilization of existing co-payment mechanisms could help reduce out-of-pocket expenses and improve access to necessary screenings. The panel experts believed that promoting use of the established EHCV scheme on osteoporosis assessment and treatment would be more effective than pursuing a new funding model. The EHCV, including its pilot reward component for designated primary care services, provides a practical platform to support DXA screening in community settings. However, most of the elderly prioritize the use of EHCV in treating acute conditions [19]. Increasing public awareness of the EHCV on osteoporosis management may enhance the uptake of DXA scans, thus facilitating early diagnosis and treatment initiation.

Discussion

Building on the 31 consensus statements developed through a structured, multi-round voting process, the panelists have proposed an integrated framework for community-based osteoporosis screening and management tailored to the context of Hong Kong. Fig. 1 illustrates the service pathway from initial risk assessment to diagnostic confirmation and ongoing management, while Table 2 outlines the roles and responsibilities of stakeholders and details how community organizations, primary care providers and specialist service units collaborate along this pathway. This service framework provides an operational blueprint to enhance systematic case-finding and improve coordination in osteoporosis management across various settings.

The framework strategically transitions from opportunistic, clinic-based detection to a proactive, community-driven approach for identifying and managing osteoporosis. This is supported by simple risk assessment tools such as COSA that can be effectively employed by non-healthcare professionals. High-risk individuals can then be referred in a timely manner to primary care physicians for DXA assessment, diagnosis and treatment initiation, while low-risk individuals can benefit from lifestyle advice and periodic reassessments.

Community health centers and community service centers, including DHCs, elderly centers, and community pharmacies, can serve as important community touchpoints for osteoporosis services. Beyond identifying high-risk individuals, these centers can provide follow-up support for those at risk for osteoporosis, particularly when resources allow. Key roles may include communicating osteoporosis risk factors to high-risk individuals, delivering education on bone health, and encouraging periodic reassessment and follow-up as feasible. The integration of community services with primary care not only raises public awareness of osteoporosis and fosters self-management in community settings, but also streamlines referrals to primary care physicians and specialists, thereby contributing to improved osteoporosis management outcomes.

Effective implementation of this framework relies on strengthening referral pathways among community health/service centers, primary care and specialist services, as well as integrating workflows into existing primary care service models. Additionally, raising public and professional awareness of osteoporosis as a preventable and treatable disease is crucial. While implementation will require coordination and ongoing evaluation, the framework presents a coherent starting point for a more systematic, community-driven approach to osteoporosis care in Hong Kong.

Conclusions

The framework, derived from consensus statements, provides the first structured model for community-oriented osteoporosis screening and management services in Hong Kong, translating expert opinion into a practical service pathway that links community services, primary care and specialist services. By facilitating risk assessment closer to where older adults live and interact, and by clearly delineating referral and management responsibilities, the framework aims to enable earlier identification of individuals at high risk of osteoporosis, prompt intervention and improved long-term follow-up care. If implemented at scale, the proposed service framework has the potential to increase osteoporosis screening uptake and promote early diagnosis and timely treatment, ultimately lowering both healthcare and economic burden associated with fragility fractures. Moreover, the framework lays a solid foundation for policymakers, service planners and clinicians seeking to strengthen primary and continuity care for osteoporosis and to support healthy aging in community settings in Hong Kong.
