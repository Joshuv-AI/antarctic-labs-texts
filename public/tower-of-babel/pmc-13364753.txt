High Cardiometabolic Risk Burden Predicts Adverse Outcomes in Patients with MASLD-Related Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Yeonhong Do, Young Hyeon Ahn, Young Chang, Jeong-Ju Yoo, Sae Hwan Lee, Soung Won Jeong, Sang Gyune Kim, Jae Young Jang, Young Seok Kim, Hong Soo Kim, Keungmo Yang, Tom Ryu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimsMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a rapidly growing cause of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC); however, the prognostic implications of the cumulative cardiometabolic risk factor (CMRF) burden remain unclear.MethodsIn this single-center retrospective cohort, we identified 94 patients with MASLD-related HCC after excluding other etiologies of liver disease. The modified CMRF count (based on eight routinely available variables) was calculated, and the patients were stratified according to the count (<5 or ≥5). In addition to baseline data, survival was assessed by the Kaplan-Meier log-rank test, and prognostic factors were evaluated by Cox regression.ResultsA higher CMRF burden was associated with older age, a greater cumulative tumor diameter, and a higher level of protein induced by vitamin K absence or antagonist-II (PIVKA-II), but not with the level of alpha-fetoprotein. Patients with a CMRF count ≥5 had significantly worse overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS). In the Cox models, a CMRF count ≥5 was associated with poor OS; the association with RFS was evident in univariate analysis but was attenuated after adjustment. Among individual factors, hypertension was independently associated with OS, whereas diabetes lost significance after adjustment. The level of triglycerides was not a prognostic factor, while higher high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels were associated with better OS and RFS by the Kaplan-Meier method.ConclusionsThe cumulative metabolic burden, represented by a modified CMRF count, was associated with larger tumors, a higher PIVKA-II level, and inferior survival in patients with MASLD-related HCC.

INTRODUCTION

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has recently emerged as a redefined disease entity replacing nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, reflecting the critical role of metabolic dysfunction in the development and progression of fatty liver disease.1 MASLD is diagnosed when hepatic steatosis is present in conjunction with one or more cardiometabolic risk factors (CMRFs), including overweight or obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, or related metabolic abnormalities.2 This new nomenclature emphasizes that fatty liver disease is not merely a hepatic disorder but a systemic condition driven by metabolic derangements.

The clinical burden of MASLD is rapidly increasing worldwide, paralleling the epidemics of obesity, diabetes, and hypertension.3 It is estimated that nearly one in four adults globally is affected, with prevalence rising further in Asian populations where metabolic risks frequently manifest at lower body mass index (BMI) thresholds.4 Beyond its hepatic consequences, MASLD is closely associated with cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and systemic metabolic complications.5 Importantly, MASLD has now been recognized as an increasingly important etiology of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), even in the absence of cirrhosis.6 Epidemiologic studies suggest that patients with MASLD-related HCC often present at older ages, with larger tumors and fewer symptoms at diagnosis, leading to challenges in surveillance and management.7,8

The pathophysiological mechanisms connecting MASLD to hepatocarcinogenesis are complex and multifactorial. Chronic low-grade inflammation, lipotoxicity, oxidative stress, and activation of pro-oncogenic signaling pathways in the setting of metabolic dysfunction are thought to induce malignant transformation.9 Moreover, systemic metabolic disorder could enhance tumor progression by altering immune surveillance, promoting angiogenesis, and creating a tumor-permissive microenvironment.10 Despite the recognized association between metabolic dysfunction and HCC, the prognostic impact of the cumulative burden of CMRFs in MASLD-related HCC remains poorly defined.

Prior studies have examined individual metabolic risk factors in the context of chronic liver disease and HCC outcomes. For example, hypertension and diabetes mellitus have been consistently associated with adverse liver-related and cardiovascular outcomes, while dyslipidemia and low high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol have emerged as important predictors of cancer progression and mortality.11,12 Nevertheless, patients often present with multiple coexisting risk factors, and the synergistic effects of these risks on HCC biology and prognosis have not been systematically investigated. Understanding how the cumulative number of metabolic risk factors influences tumor characteristics and clinical outcomes in MASLD-related HCC would be essential to modify risk stratification and guide surveillance and treatment strategies.

In this context, we introduced a modified CMRF scoring system, including eight routinely available clinical and laboratory variables: elevated BMI, fasting glucose, glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), diagnosis of diabetes, diagnosis of hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia, low HDL cholesterol, and statin use. By counting the number of abnormal parameters, we aimed to capture the overall metabolic burden in each patient. This approach would offer a clinically relevant method of quantifying metabolic dysfunction in MASLD-related HCC.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the prognostic significance of cumulative metabolic burden in patients with MASLD-related HCC. We hypothesized that a higher modified CMRF count would be associated with larger tumor size, elevated tumor markers, and poorer survival outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study design and population

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted at Soonchunhyang University Seoul Hospital (IRB number: SCHUH 2025-09-011). Given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de-identified clinical data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board. We reviewed the medical records of patients who were newly diagnosed with HCC between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2024, based on radiologic examinations including computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging, or histologic confirmation. From an initial cohort of 787 patients, we excluded those with insufficient clinical records (n=114), viral hepatitis (hepatitis B or D, n=393; hepatitis C, n=72), alcohol-associated liver disease (n=105), autoimmune hepatitis (n=3), or the absence of any CMRFs despite evidence of hepatic steatosis (n=6). After these exclusions, 94 patients with MASLD-related HCC were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

2. Definitions

MASLD was defined as the presence of hepatic steatosis on abdominal ultrasound in the absence of other known etiologies of liver disease, accompanied by at least one CMRF.2 Modified CMRFs in this study included eight variables: (1) BMI ≥25 kg/m2, (2) fasting glucose ≥100 mg/dL, (3) HbA1c ≥5.7%, (4) diagnosis of diabetes mellitus, (5) diagnosis of hypertension, (6) serum triglycerides ≥150 mg/dL, (7) statin use, and (8) low serum HDL cholesterol (<40 mg/dL for males, <50 mg/dL for females). The total number of CMRFs present in each patient was counted and used as a measure of cumulative metabolic burden. For the main analysis, patients were stratified into two groups according to CMRF count (<5 vs ≥5). All CMRF variables were collected at the time of HCC diagnosis and defined based on baseline clinical, laboratory, and medication records. For additional analysis restricted to patients receiving curative treatments (surgical resection, radiofrequency ablation, or liver transplantation), recurrence-free survival (RFS) was defined as the time from curative therapy to radiologically confirmed tumor recurrence or death. Liver cirrhosis was defined by typical ultrasonographic features and/or liver stiffness corresponding to advanced fibrosis (F4) on vibration-controlled transient elastography, when available.

3. Data collection

Demographic characteristics (age, sex, height, weight, and BMI), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus), laboratory data (hemoglobin, platelet count, creatinine, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, total bilirubin, albumin, international normalized ratio, triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, alpha-fetoprotein [AFP], protein induced by vitamin K absence or antagonist-II [PIVKA-II]), and imaging findings were obtained from the electronic medical record system. The sum of tumor diameters was determined from radiologic reports at the time of diagnosis. Child-Pugh class (A vs B or C), tumor multiplicity (≥2 masses), and vascular invasion were additionally analyzed. Treatment modalities, including surgical resection, radiofrequency ablation, liver transplantation, radiation therapy, transarterial chemoembolization, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, and atezolizumab–bevacizumab, were also recorded. Clinical outcomes were assessed through follow-up records, including recurrence and mortality. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from HCC diagnosis to death from any cause, while RFS was defined as the time to either tumor recurrence or death, whichever occurred first.

4. Endpoints

The primary endpoint of this study was to determine whether modified CMRF count was associated with OS and RFS in MASLD-related HCC. The secondary endpoint was to evaluate the association between CMRF count and tumor characteristics at baseline, including sum of tumor diameter and tumor markers, such as AFP and PIVKA-II, and to examine the prognostic role of individual metabolic risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, triglycerides, and HDL cholesterol.

5. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean±standard deviation or median with interquartile range, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Differences between groups were analyzed using the Student t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Correlations between CMRF count and tumor-related variables (sum of tumor diameter, AFP, and PIVKA-II) were evaluated using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients. Survival curves for OS and RFS were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to identify independent prognostic factors. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and two-tailed p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

1. Baseline characteristics of the population

Among the 94 patients who met the inclusion criteria for MASLD-related HCC after exclusion of those with insufficient records, viral hepatitis, alcohol-associated liver disease, autoimmune hepatitis, or absence of any CMRF, patients were stratified into two groups according to modified CMRF count: <5 (n=48) and ≥5 (n=46). The median follow-up duration was 10 months (interquartile range, 2.5 to 38 months). Regarding individual components of the modified CMRF, BMI ≥25 kg/m2 was present in 51 patients (54.3%), fasting glucose ≥100 mg/dL in 75 (79.8%), HbA1c ≥5.7% in 67 (71.3%), diabetes mellitus in 56 (59.6%), hypertension in 65 (69.1%), serum triglycerides ≥150 mg/dL in 29 (30.9%), statin use in 25 (26.6%), and low HDL cholesterol in 47 (50.0%).

Demographic features, including age (median 75.0 vs 72.5 years; p=0.706) and sex (male 75.0% vs 77.3%; p=0.992), were comparable between the groups. In contrast, hypertension and diabetes mellitus were significantly more prevalent in the CMRF ≥5 group compared with the group with <5 CMRFs (90.9% vs 47.9%, p<0.001; and 90.7% vs 31.2%, p<0.001, respectively). Laboratory analyses revealed that patients with higher CMRF burden had lower hemoglobin levels (11.5 g/dL vs 12.6 g/dL; p=0.032), higher triglyceride levels (138.0 mg/dL vs 96.0 mg/dL; p=0.002), and lower HDL cholesterol (36.0 mg/dL vs 43.5 mg/dL; p=0.003). Other biochemical parameters, including platelet count, creatinine, albumin, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, international normalized ratio, and total bilirubin, were not significantly different between the groups. There were no significant differences between the groups in terms of liver cirrhosis prevalence or modified Union for International Cancer Control stage distribution at diagnosis. Regarding additional HCC-related prognostic factors, there were no significant differences between the two groups in Child-Pugh class or tumor multiplicity. In contrast, vascular invasion was significantly more frequent in patients with a modified CMRF count ≥5 compared with those with a count <5 (p<0.001). Initial treatment modalities did not differ significantly (Table 1).

2. Correlation analysis between CMRF count and characteristics of tumor

Correlation graphs demonstrated significant associations between the CMRF count and several clinical parameters. The number of CMRFs showed a positive correlation with age (r2=0.085, p=0.044), sum of tumor diameter (r2=0.167, p=0.003), and PIVKA-II levels (r2=0.188, p=0.003). In contrast, AFP levels were not correlated with CMRF burden (r2=0.000, p=0.929) (Fig. 2).

3. Impact of CMRF burden on survival of patients with HCC

Patients with CMRF ≥5 had significantly worse OS in univariate analysis (HR, 6.57; 95% CI, 2.43 to 17.78; p<0.001), which remained independently associated in the multivariate model (HR, 3.79; 95% CI, 1.30 to 11.06; p=0.015) (Table 2). The same group also showed worse RFS in univariate analysis (HR, 2.85; 95% CI, 1.24 to 6.57; p=0.013), although this association attenuated after adjustment (HR, 1.67; 95% CI, 0.64 to 4.40; p=0.298) (Table 3). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed significantly poorer OS (log-rank p<0.001) (Fig. 3A), while no significance was observed in RFS (log-rank p=0.212) (Fig. 3B).

4. Subgroup analysis: impact of hypertension and diabetes mellitus on survival of patients with HCC

Subgroup analyses showed the role of individual metabolic factors. In univariate Cox analysis, hypertension was significantly associated with increased mortality (HR, 11.20; 95% CI, 2.45 to 51.12; p<0.001), and this remained significant after multivariable adjustment (HR, 6.48; 95% CI, 1.31 to 32.12; p=0.022) (Table 2). Kaplan-Meier curves partly supported this finding, showing shorter OS in hypertensive patients (log-rank p=0.005) (Supplementary Fig. 1A), although no difference was observed for RFS (log-rank p=0.202) (Supplementary Fig. 1B). In contrast, diabetes mellitus was associated with poor OS in univariate analysis (HR, 3.29; 95% CI, 1.23 to 8.77; p=0.015), but the significance was attenuated in the adjusted model (HR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.17 to 2.89; p=0.614). Kaplan-Meier analyses similarly showed no significant difference in OS (p=0.265) or RFS (p=0.929) by diabetes status (Supplementary Fig. 1C and D).

5. Subgroup analysis: impact of dyslipidemia on survival of patients with HCC

Triglyceride levels were not associated with outcomes in either univariate or multivariate Cox models (for OS: HR, 1.80; 95% CI, 0.72 to 4.52; p=0.205; for RFS: HR, 1.33; 95% CI, 0.55 to 3.23; p=0.527) (Tables 2 and 3). Kaplan-Meier analysis also demonstrated no prognostic relevance for triglycerides with respect to OS (log-rank p=0.968) or RFS (log-rank p=0.986) (Supplementary Fig. 2A and B).

In contrast, HDL cholesterol showed a survival benefit in Kaplan-Meier analysis, with patients having higher HDL levels experiencing significantly longer OS (log-rank p=0.028) and RFS (log-rank p=0.043) (Supplementary Fig. 2). However, Cox regression did not confirm that HDL cholesterol was independently associated with either OS (HR, 1.76; 95% CI, 0.73 to 4.25; p=0.208) or RFS (HR, 1.35; 95% CI, 0.60 to 3.06; p=0.471) (Table 2).

6. Survival analysis for patients with curative treatment

To address the interpretability of survival in patients receiving non-curative treatments, we performed analyses restricted to patients with curative treatment, including surgical resection, radiofrequency ablation, and liver transplantation. In this subset, no significant differences in OS or RFS were observed between patients with modified CMRF counts <5 and ≥5 (OS, p=0.748; RFS, p=0.098) (Supplementary Fig. 3).

When individual CMRFs were examined, the presence of hypertension was not associated with significant differences in OS or RFS (OS, p=0.074; RFS, p=0.303) (Supplementary Fig. 4A and B), nor was the presence of diabetes mellitus (OS, p=0.315; RFS, p=0.698) (Supplementary Fig. 4C and D). Similarly, elevated triglyceride levels were not significantly associated with OS or RFS (OS, p=0.061; RFS, p=0.259) (Supplementary Fig. 5A and B). In contrast, patients with higher HDL cholesterol levels demonstrated significantly better OS and RFS compared with those with lower HDL levels (OS, p=0.020; RFS, p=0.025) (Supplementary Fig. 5C and D).

DISCUSSION

In this retrospective cohort study, we demonstrated that the cumulative number of CMRFs had a significant impact on the clinical course of patients with MASLD-related HCC. Patients with a modified CMRF count ≥5 exhibited significantly poorer OS compared to those with fewer metabolic risks, even after adjustment for selected covariates. Although RFS was also inferior in the high-CMRF group, the association attenuated after multivariate adjustment, suggesting that some of the observed risk might be mediated by overlapping clinical variables. In addition, baseline analyses revealed that higher CMRF burden was correlated with larger tumor size and elevated PIVKA-II levels, whereas AFP showed no association. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the prognostic significance of metabolic dysfunction in MASLD-related HCC and suggest that the accumulation of metabolic risks shapes both tumor biology and long-term outcomes.

Our study expands on prior reports that MASLD-related HCC tends to present with distinct clinical features compared to viral or alcohol-related HCC. In particular, tumors induced by MASLD are often larger at diagnosis and detected later due to less consistent surveillance in this patient population.8 We observed that increasing CMRF count correlated with greater tumor diameter and higher PIVKA-II levels, both of which are established markers of aggressive tumor biology. The absence of correlation with AFP suggests that AFP might be a less reliable biomarker in MASLD-related HCC, consistent with a previous study reporting lower AFP sensitivity in metabolic etiology.13 These findings highlight the requirement for tailored surveillance strategies and biomarker development specific to MASLD-related HCC.

Among individual metabolic factors, hypertension emerged as a particularly prominent predictor of OS in both univariate and multivariate analyses. These results are consistent with prior evidence that systemic hypertension contributes to endothelial dysfunction, vascular inflammation, and pro-oncogenic signaling, which might accelerate HCC progression.14,15 Diabetes mellitus was also associated with poor survival in univariate analysis, though this effect diminished after adjustment. These findings suggest that the effect of diabetes mellitus may be mediated by its association with other metabolic parameters such as HbA1c and obesity. The discrepancy between Kaplan-Meier curves and multivariate Cox regression for these variables further illustrates the complex interplay between metabolic risk factors and their coexisting contributions to prognosis.

In our analyses, triglyceride levels showed no significant association with survival, whereas higher HDL cholesterol was consistently associated with improved OS and RFS. This distinction is biologically explained, as HDL exerts anti-inflammatory and antioxidative effects and has been associated with reduced HCC risk, while triglyceride levels are highly variable and less reliable as prognostic markers.16-18 These findings suggest that HDL, rather than triglycerides, would represent a more stable and clinically meaningful biomarker in MASLD-related HCC. Although higher HDL cholesterol was associated with improved OS and RFS in Kaplan-Meier analyses, this association was not retained in Cox regression models. This discrepancy might reflect confounding by other CMRFs and limited statistical power in this modest-sized cohort.

Our findings support the concept that metabolic burden is not only a background comorbidity but also an active determinant of cancer outcomes in MASLD. The cumulative CMRF count, which integrates multiple routinely available parameters, provides a simple approach to quantify this burden. Clinically, incorporating CMRF burden into prognostic models might improve risk stratification beyond traditional tumor-related variables such as size, number, and stage. Moreover, aggressive management of metabolic dysfunction, which includes blood pressure control, glycemic management, and lipid modification, would represent another avenue to improve outcomes in MASLD-related HCC. Although causality cannot be established from retrospective data, our results suggest that multidisciplinary care addressing both oncologic and metabolic domains might be required.

Several recent studies have emphasized the rising importance of MASLD as an etiology of HCC and hepatic fibrosis, and its association with worse clinical outcomes.19,20 Several studies reported that the presence of multiple metabolic risk factors was strongly associated with advanced hepatic fibrosis and poor outcomes in MASLD patients.11,21 Our study extends these findings by demonstrating that the cumulative CMRF burden also influences tumor characteristics and survival in established MASLD-related HCC. Recent studies further showed that MASLD-related HCC might occur both with and without cirrhosis, suggesting that metabolic dysfunction itself may drive carcinogenesis independent of fibrosis stage.13,22 In this context, our findings support the hypothesis that systemic metabolic stress is a critical enhancer of tumor biology in MASLD-related HCC.

The strengths of this study include the focus on a well-defined MASLD-related HCC cohort, rigorous exclusion of viral, alcohol-related, and autoimmune etiologies, and the use of a simple CMRF count applicable in routine clinical settings. We also employed multiple analytic approaches, including correlation analyses, survival curves, and multivariate Cox models, to analyze both baseline tumor biology and long-term outcomes.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective, single-center design would limit generalizability and introduce selection bias. Given the retrospective design, residual misclassification of underlying liver disease etiology cannot be entirely excluded. Second, although we attempted to control for confounding, residual confounders such as unmeasured lifestyle factors or medication adherence cannot be excluded. Third, the modified CMRF definition used in this study was tailored to reflect the diabetes-dominant metabolic phenotype of MASLD-related HCC and should be interpreted as an exploratory metric rather than a standardized risk score.23 Fourth, our sample size was modest, and some subgroup analyses might have shown limited statistical power, which could explain discrepancies between Kaplan-Meier and multivariate results. Fifth, the higher prevalence of vascular invasion in patients with greater CMRF burden presents the need for cautious interpretation. Sixth, the multivariable models adjusted for selected covariates and might not adequately account for all established prognostic factors in HCC.

Future research should validate these findings in larger, multicenter cohorts, ideally including diverse ethnic populations where MASLD prevalence and metabolic risk patterns might differ. Integrating CMRF burden into established prognostic models such as the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer staging system could alter patient stratification. Mechanistic studies are also required to elucidate how hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia biologically influence HCC progression in MASLD, potentially through chronic inflammation, immune dysregulation, and altered lipid metabolism. Finally, interventional studies evaluating whether aggressive metabolic risk management could improve oncologic outcomes in MASLD-related HCC would provide further evidence for clinical practice.

This study highlights the prognostic importance of cumulative metabolic burden in MASLD-related HCC. A higher modified CMRF count was associated with larger tumors, elevated PIVKA-II levels, and inferior OS. Among individual factors, hypertension and low HDL cholesterol were identified as particularly strong determinants of prognosis. These findings suggest the need to integrate metabolic risk assessment into the clinical management of MASLD-related HCC and to analyze metabolic interventions as potential strategies to improve outcomes in this rapidly growing patient population.
