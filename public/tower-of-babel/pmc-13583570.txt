PaO2/FiO2 Ratio As an Exploratory Indicator of Oxygenation Changes in Acute Decompensated Heart Failure Treated with Intravenous Tolvaptan
Authors: Hiroki Hoya, Yodo Gatate, Keisuke Matsuo, Michiru Nomoto, Takahide Arai, Shintaro Nakano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary congestion leading to dyspnea is the main cause of hospitalization in patients with acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF). Intravenous tolvaptan (TLV) is an option for acute decongestion, but its effects on pulmonary congestion remain unclear, due to limited objective tools. The arterial oxygen partial pressure to inspired oxygen fraction (PaO2/FiO2 [P/F]) ratio is a practical index of oxygenation and may serve as an exploratory indicator of oxygenation changes in this setting. This exploratory study evaluated whether adding intravenous TLV to furosemide improves the 24-hour P/F ratio and factors associated with improved oxygenation.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed adults hospitalized for ADHF between January 2022 and December 2023. Patients receiving TLV plus furosemide (TLV+F) were compared with those receiving furosemide (F alone). The primary endpoint was the 24-hour P/F ratio; secondary endpoints were urine output and safety (serum sodium, potassium, creatinine, and hemodynamics). Logistic regression identified factors associated with improved oxygenation. Subgroup analyses were by left ventricular ejection fraction (< 40% vs ≥ 40%).ResultsAmong 120 patients (TLV+F n = 48; F alone n = 72), the TLV+F group achieved a higher 24-hour P/F ratio (340 ± 89 vs 281 ± 87; P < 0.001) and greater urine output, both in absolute terms (3925 ± 1890 vs 3097 ± 1181 mL; P = 0.004) and when indexed to body weight (63.3 ± 24.3 vs 52.3 ± 21.1 mL/kg; P = 0.01). Serum sodium level rose modestly, with no apparent short-term renal or hemodynamic differences. TLV use independently predicted improved oxygenation. In subgroup analyses by left ventricular ejection fraction, no significant interaction was observed (P = 0.38).ConclusionsIn ADHF, use of adjunctive intravenous TLV was associated with improved early oxygenation and diuresis without an apparent increase in short-term adverse events. These exploratory findings suggest that intravenous TLV may be a useful component of acute decongestive therapy, warranting prospective validation.Keywords: acute decompensated heart failure, intravenous tolvaptan, pulmonary congestion, arterial oxygen partial pressure to inspired oxygen fraction ratio

The number of patients with heart failure (HF) is increasing in developed countries, contributing to a growing burden on public health and annual increases in treatment costs.1, 2, 3 Among clinical presentations of HF, acute decompensated HF (ADHF) represents the most severe condition, often manifesting as worsening dyspnea due to pulmonary congestion, and it frequently results in emergency visits or hospital readmission.4

Pulmonary congestion in ADHF is a key determinant of dyspnea, making appropriate assessment essential. Nevertheless, the methods for such assessment remain insufficiently established. Many previous studies have relied on systemic congestion markers or symptom-based scales rather than direct assessment.4, 5, 6, 7 The arterial oxygen partial pressure to inspired oxygen fraction (PaO2/FiO2 [P/F]) ratio is a widely used indicator of pulmonary oxygenation, and a decrease in this ratio reflects impaired gas exchange caused by pulmonary congestion.8 Given its simplicity and availability in clinical practice, the P/F ratio may serve as an exploratory indicator of oxygenation changes that may be consistent with pulmonary congestion. However, its validity in this context has not been confirmed adequately, and its use in ADHF therefore should be regarded as exploratory.

In addition to the lack of well-established methods for assessing pulmonary congestion, effective decongestive therapy remains a critical challenge in the management of ADHF. Diuretics are used to treat pulmonary congestion, with loop diuretics being the first-line agents.3,4 In contrast, tolvaptan (TLV) is a vasopressin type 2 receptor (V2 receptor) antagonist that promotes aquaresis through a mechanism different from that of loop diuretics.9 In the SMILE study, a large post-marketing surveillance analysis in Japan, TLV increased 24-hour urine volume in 76.9% of patients by day 2 and was associated with significant reductions in body weight and improvement in congestive symptoms, without serious safety concerns such as central pontine myelinolysis.10 Recently, an intravenous formulation of TLV has become available, offering a promising option for rapid decongestion, particularly in the acute phase of ADHF when oral absorption may be impaired.11,12

Therefore, in this retrospective observational study, we evaluated the efficacy of intravenous TLV for congestion relief in ADHF patients, focusing on pulmonary oxygenation and associated adverse effects. Although the P/F ratio is influenced by various factors and is not specific to pulmonary congestion, we explored its potential as a practical exploratory indicator of oxygenation changes in this setting.8,13 We also assessed changes in oxygenation, electrolytes, renal function, and hemodynamic parameters following initial therapy. Given that the effect of TLV may vary with left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), we performed subgroup and interaction analyses to examine potential heterogeneity by systolic function.14,15

Material and Methods

Study design and subjects

We retrospectively analyzed 340 consecutive patients admitted from the emergency room for ADHF at Saitama Medical University International Medical Centre between January 2022 and December 2023. Patients with anuria or hypernatremia were excluded. ADHF was diagnosed according to established definitions.2,16

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) absence of initial intravenous furosemide administration after admission; (ii) requirement for immediate invasive treatment; (iii) critical cardiovascular condition unamenable to diuretics; (iv) contraindication to TLV use; and (v) lack of essential information on the outcome.

The study was approved by the institutional review board (KOKU2024-025) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for written informed consent was waived with public disclosure and an opt-out option.

Clinical data

Patients’ baseline characteristics data, including sex (male or female), age, body mass index, New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification III or IV, and etiology of ADHF were collected. Additionally, we evaluated each patient’s medical history for the presence or absence of hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, ADHF hospitalization within the past year, atrial fibrillation, and the placement of a cardiac implantable electronic device. We also recorded systolic and diastolic blood pressures, as well as heart rate, at the time of admission. Laboratory data comprised serum creatinine, uric acid, estimated glomerular filtration rate, hemoglobin, blood urea nitrogen, serum sodium, serum potassium, and plasma N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide, in accordance with previous studies.10,17 Cardiac ultrasonography confirmed LVEF measured by the modified Simpson’s method, left ventricular (LV) dimension in diastole, LV dimension in systole, and left atrial dimension. Regarding preadmission medications, we documented the use of renin-angiotensin system inhibitors, such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers, and angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitors, beta-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, and loop diuretics. Arterial blood gas analysis was performed according to routine clinical practice, and repeat measurements were obtained at the discretion of the treating physician. The P/F ratio was calculated using arterial partial pressure of oxygen obtained from arterial blood gas analysis, and FiO2 was estimated based on oxygen delivery modality and flow rates, in accordance with a previous study.13 pH and arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide data were also collected. Oxygen delivery modality and FiO2 at admission and at 24 hours after treatment initiation were recorded when available. Oxygen delivery modalities were categorized as room air, nasal cannula, simple face mask, reservoir mask, noninvasive positive pressure ventilation (NPPV), or invasive mechanical ventilation. Changes in oxygen delivery modality within the first 24 hours were classified as escalation or de-escalation according to increased or decreased intensity of respiratory support. Oxygen titration and selection of delivery modality were performed at the discretion of the treating physicians, as no standardized institutional protocol was applied.

Acute decongestion therapy

Our study evaluated 2 groups created on the basis of different initial decongestion strategies: patients who received intravenous TLV in addition to furosemide (TLV + F group) and those who received intravenous furosemide alone (F-alone group). In our institutional routine practice, intravenous furosemide (20 mg/dose) was administered after a rapid survey that comprised a physical examination, electrocardiography, echocardiography, chest radiograph, and blood gas analysis, with consideration of vasodilator and inotrope use unless contraindicated, in accordance with clinical guidelines.3

After initial intravenous furosemide administration, patients were reassessed within approximately 1 hour, and escalation of decongestion therapy with intravenous TLV or additional furosemide was considered at the discretion of the treating physician. This decision reflected the overall clinical assessment, including respiratory status, urine output, oxygen requirement, and residual signs of congestion when available, and no predefined objective institutional criteria were used to mandate TLV initiation. In the TLV group, the median time from furosemide administration to TLV initiation was 3.0 [2.1-4.5] hours (range, 1.2-12.8 hours), reflecting routine clinical practice rather than a protocol-driven approach.

Outcome measures

Given the rapid onset of action expected with intravenous TLV, outcomes were primarily assessed at 24 hours to capture early decongestive effects during the acute phase. The primary endpoint was the P/F ratio as a continuous variable at 24 hours after admission. The 24-hour time point was selected to capture early physiological effects of intravenous TLV in the acute phase of ADHF, given its rapid aquaretic action and in contrast to prior studies of oral TLV, which primarily assessed outcomes at later time points, such as 48 hours.10 For interpretive and exploratory analyses, a cutoff of P/F ≥ 300—based on the Berlin Definition for acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)—was regarded as indicating normal oxygenation.18 Although this threshold has not been directly validated for pulmonary decongestion in ADHF, previous studies using lung ultrasound suggest that improved lung congestion parallels better oxygenation.19,20 Accordingly, P/F ≥ 300 was used only to support multivariable and subgroup analyses as a pragmatic exploratory indicator of oxygenation changes consistent with decongestion.3,10,18 Secondary endpoints were 24-hour urine output and safety indices, including serum sodium, potassium, creatinine, and hemodynamic parameters at baseline and 24 hours. Urine output was additionally normalized to body weight and expressed as mL/kg. The exploratory endpoint was in-hospital mortality. Subgroup analyses also were performed based on LVEF, comparing patients with LVEF < 40% (HF with reduced ejection fraction [HFrEF]) and those with LVEF ≥ 40% (HF with preserved ejection fraction [HFpEF]) to evaluate potential LVEF-dependent heterogeneities. To further assess potential effect modification by systolic function, we conducted an interaction analysis by including an interaction term between intravenous TLV use and LVEF group (HFrEF vs HFpEF) in the multivariable logistic regression model.

Statistics

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median [first–third quartile], and categorical variables are expressed as number (%). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the data distribution.

Intergroup comparisons (TLV+F vs F-alone groups) were performed using the t-test for normally distributed data, and the Wilcoxon rank sum test was used for non-normally distributed data. For categorical variables, comparisons were performed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test when expected patient numbers were low.

To evaluate the independent association between intravenous TLV use and improvement in oxygenation, we performed a multivariable logistic regression analysis with a binary outcome of P/F ratio ≥ 300 at 24 hours after admission, as defined in the Outcome Measures section. The number of events was sufficient for multivariate logistic regression analysis, and the event ratio per variable was set so as not to exceed generally accepted thresholds. The following covariates were initially considered based on clinical relevance and prior literature: age, body mass index, LVEF, serum creatinine, serum sodium, systolic blood pressure (SBP), and baseline P/F ratio.3 Age and body mass index were ultimately excluded from the final model to avoid overfitting due to the limited number of events. To mitigate confounding, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied using a propensity score including age, sex, SBP, LVEF, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), sodium, baseline P/F ratio, and atrial fibrillation. Stabilized IPTW with 1st-99th percentile trimming was used. Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (< 0.10 considered adequate) and visualized in a Love plot. Weighted logistic regression with robust standard errors was performed, and a doubly robust model (IPTW + covariate adjustment) served as sensitivity analysis.

In an exploratory analysis, the association between changes in the P/F ratio (ΔP/F) and 24-hour urine output was assessed using Spearman rank correlation analysis.

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.3 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria, www.r-project.org).

Results

Patients’ characteristics

During the study period, 340 patients presented to our emergency hospital and subsequently were admitted with a diagnosis of ADHF. Of these, 220 patients were excluded for the following reasons: (i) 118 did not have initial intravenous furosemide; (ii) 4 required immediate invasive treatment; (iii) 12 were in critical condition unamenable to diuretics (such as patients with hemodialysis); (iv) 23 had contraindications to TLV use; and (v) 63 had missing essential information on outcomes (Fig. 1). Patients requiring immediate invasive treatment, such as mechanical circulatory support for cardiogenic shock, were excluded. Patients excluded due to missing outcome data primarily lacked arterial blood gas measurements within 24 hours. This lack of data was largely attributable to clinical judgment that repeat measurements were unnecessary, admission to general wards without arterial line placement, or removal of arterial lines because of patient-related factors such as agitation. Data for the remaining 48 patients in the TLV+F group and 72 patients in the F-alone group were analyzed.

Primary and secondary endpoint: overall cohort

The primary endpoint is summarized in Figure 2. The P/F ratio at admission was similar between groups (254.4 ± 115.7 vs 273.5 ± 143.8; P = 0.44; Table 1), but it was significantly higher at 24 hours in the TLV+F group than in the F-alone group (340.3 ± 88.9 vs 280.7 ± 87.1; P < 0.001). In addition, the change in P/F ratio from baseline to 24 hours (ΔP/F) was also greater in the TLV+F group (85.9 ± 102.3 vs 7.2 ± 117.4; P < 0.001; Supplemental Table S4).

The results of the analysis of the secondary endpoints are summarized in Figure 2. The 24-hour urine output was significantly higher in the TLV+F group than in the F-alone group (3925 ± 1890 mL vs 3097 ± 1181 mL; P = 0.004). When normalized to body weight, 24-hour urine output remained significantly higher in the TLV+F group than in the F-alone group (63.3 ± 24.3 vs 52.3 ± 21.1 mL/kg; P = 0.01). To assess early diuretic response before intravenous TLV administration, urine output during the first hour after initial intravenous furosemide administration was compared between the 2 groups. No significant difference was observed between the TLV+F group and the F-alone group (median [interquartile range], 250 [143-445] mL vs 300 [160-500] mL, P = 0.48).

Serum sodium increased modestly but significantly with TLV+F (3.0 ± 3.9 vs 0.0 ± 2.5 mmol/L; P < 0.001), with no cases of symptomatic hypernatremia (Table 2). At 48 hours, serum sodium level remained slightly but significantly higher in the TLV+F group, compared with the F-alone group, and potassium and creatinine levels remained comparable between groups (Supplemental Table S5). No significant correlation was observed between the change in P/F ratio (ΔP/F) and 24-hour urine output (Spearman ρ = −0.12, P = 0.18).

Exploratory endpoint

The in-hospital mortality rate did not differ significantly between the 2 groups, with 3 of 48 deaths (6.3%) in the TLV+F group and 5 of 72 deaths (6.9%) in the F-alone group (P = 0.88). Hospital length of stay did not significantly differ between the 2 groups.

Subgroup analyses on the basis of LVEF

In patients with HFrEF (n = 67), the TLV+F group showed a significantly higher 24-hour P/F ratio than the F-alone group (356.8 ± 84.5 vs 302.9 ± 81.4; P = 0.01) and greater urine output (3975 ± 1620 mL vs 3142 ± 1191 mL; P = 0.02; Fig. 3). In contrast, among those with HFpEF (n = 53), no significant differences were observed in either the 24-hour P/F ratio (305.9 ± 93.1 vs 266.7 ± 90.9; P = 0.15) or urine output (3843 ± 2322 mL vs 3079 ± 1191 mL; P = 0.12; Fig. 3). To further assess whether the effect of TLV on oxygenation differed by left ventricular function, we performed an interaction analysis between TLV use and LVEF category. An interaction term (TLV × LVEF group) was included in the logistic regression model predicting P/F ratio ≥ 300 at 24 hours. No significant TLV × LVEF interaction occurred (OR 0.52, 95% CI 0.10-2.76; P = 0.38), indicating no evidence of effect modification by LVEF.

The same analysis as that shown in Figure 2 was performed separately in the heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF; left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF] < 40%, [A] and [B]) and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF; LVEF ≥ 40%, [C] and [D]) groups. In the HFrEF subgroup, (A) the arterial partial pressure of oxygen/fraction of inspired oxygen ratio (P/F ratio) 24 hours after admission was significantly higher in the tolvaptan (TLV) + furosemide (F) group, compared with the F-alone group (P = 0.01), and (B) 24-hour urine output was significantly greater (P = 0.02). However, in the HFpEF group, there were no significant differences in (C) the P/F ratio 24 hours after admission between the treatment groups (P = 0.15) or (D) 24-hour urine output (P = 0.12).

Discussion

Our study is the first to explore the role of intravenous TLV in patients with ADHF, focusing on oxygenation changes assessed by the P/F ratio. We demonstrated that the addition of intravenous TLV to furosemide was associated with improved oxygenation, as reflected by higher P/F ratios at 24 hours and a larger ΔP/F from admission to 24 hours, consistent with the rapid onset of action expected from intravenous administration in the acute phase of ADHF. In addition, multivariable analysis identified TLV use and lower SBP as independent predictors of achievement of a P/F ratio ≥ 300, but interaction analysis did not demonstrate statistical significance. In terms of safety, intravenous TLV was associated with greater urine output without an increase in clinically significant adverse events, supporting its utility in acute management. These results are in line with contemporary registry data describing the characteristics and outcomes of acute HF patients treated with TLV and extend those findings by providing early physiological evidence of improved oxygenation with use of intravenous TLV.21 Collectively, these findings suggest the potential value of intravenous TLV use as part of enhanced decongestion therapy in ADHF.

Effect of intravenous TLV on pulmonary congestion and predictors of response

Persistent pulmonary congestion in ADHF not only leads to long-term adverse outcomes but also poses an immediate risk of life-threatening respiratory compromise due to dyspnea.2,4 Addressing this issue, our study demonstrated that intravenous TLV use was associated with early improvement in oxygenation, as reflected by significant improvement in the P/F ratio, consistent with decongestion. This rapid improvement may be attributed partly to the pharmacokinetic advantage of using intravenous TLV, which ensures immediate systemic exposure even when oral absorption is impaired in the acute phase of ADHF.22 Mechanistically, TLV antagonizes the vasopressin V2 receptor, promotes aquaresis, and mobilizes interstitial fluid into the vascular space, potentially alleviating pulmonary interstitial congestion.9 Unlike previous studies that relied mainly on symptom-based assessment, our findings suggest that the P/F ratio may serve as an exploratory indicator of oxygenation changes consistent with decongestion.9,17,23

To further substantiate these physiological findings, we conducted multivariable analyses to examine whether the observed oxygenation improvement remained independent of confounding factors. Multivariable logistic regression was performed using a clinically relevant cutoff of P/F ≥ 300, which has been applied in studies of acute lung injury and ARDS, although its use in ADHF remains exploratory.24 After adjustment for major confounders, including LVEF, baseline renal function, and initial P/F ratio, intravenous TLV use remained independently associated with improved oxygenation (Table 3).

In addition, multivariable analysis demonstrated that lower LVEF and lower SBP were associated with greater improvement in the P/F ratio. These findings likely reflect the pathophysiological background of volume-overloaded HF phenotypes. Patients with reduced LVEF often exhibit chronic fluid retention due to persistent neurohormonal activation (renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, sympathetic nervous system, and vasopressin), which promotes sodium and water retention despite ongoing diuretic therapy.14 Similarly, low SBP in ADHF generally indicates impaired forward flow and reduced renal perfusion, both of which further limit sodium excretion and accelerate fluid accumulation.15 Although the underlying mechanisms differ—chronic neurohormonal-driven retention in HFrEF vs acute hemodynamic-driven retention in low SBP—both phenotypes converge on the pathophysiology of marked fluid overload. This shared background may explain the greater responsiveness to TLV, the aquaretic effect of which directly counteracts fluid accumulation.

Taken together, our findings suggest that TLV may exert its effects preferentially in patients with substantial volume overload, highlighting the potential value of early initiation guided by clinical signs of congestion. To address potential confounding and indication bias, we applied IPTW using clinically relevant covariates. Post-weighting balance improved, and sensitivity analyses showed consistent results; however, residual treatment-selection bias cannot be excluded in this observational design.

Subgroup analysis and mechanistic insights

Although current guidelines do not recommend differentiating diuretic therapy based on ADHF phenotype,2,25 no significant interaction was observed between TLV use and LVEF category, and these subgroup findings therefore should be considered exploratory. Previous reports likewise suggest that the efficacy of TLV in ADHF depends primarily on the degree of congestion rather than on systolic function. For example, recent evidence from the Decongestion duRing therapY for acute decOmpensated heart failure in HFpEF vs HFrEF (DRY-OFF) study demonstrated that congestion phenotypes differ within both reduced- and preserved-EF populations, and that decongestion response is better explained by congestion status than by EF class itself.14 Taken together, TLV may provide benefit across ADHF phenotypes characterized by substantial fluid overload. In addition, early urine output during the first hour after initial intravenous furosemide administration did not differ significantly between groups, suggesting that the greater 24-hour urine output observed in the TLV+F group was unlikely to be explained solely by differences in immediate response to loop diuretics before TLV initiation.

To further explore the mechanism underlying the observed improvement in oxygenation, we examined the relationship between change in the P/F ratio and 24-hour urine output and found no significant correlation. Oxygenation in ADHF is influenced by multiple physiological factors beyond diuresis alone, including pulmonary vascular congestion, redistribution of lung fluid, and variability in oxygen delivery settings. Therefore, improvements in oxygenation may not necessarily correlate directly with urine volume. These findings suggest that the improvement in oxygenation associated with TLV use may not be explained solely by increased urine output and could involve more-complex hemodynamic and pulmonary mechanisms.

Safety profile and role in enhanced decongestion therapy

In this study, the addition of intravenous TLV to furosemide increased urine output and improved oxygenation without inducing clinically significant adverse events, including symptomatic hypernatremia, hypotension, hyperkalemia, and worsening renal function. In-hospital mortality did not differ between the 2 groups, further supporting the short-term safety of use of intravenous TLV in this setting.

Consistent with previous studies, this favourable profile supports its use as part of enhanced decongestion therapy.24 Serum sodium level at 24 hours was higher with TLV+F (142 [140-144] mmol/L] than with furosemide alone (140 [138-142] mmol/L; P = 0.013); the 0-24 hours change was 2 vs 0 mmol/L (P < 0.001). No patient exceeded 150 mmol/L in either group; counts at ≥ 147 and ≥ 145 mmol/L were 5 of 48 vs 5 of 72 and 9 of 48 vs 6 of 72, respectively (Supplemental Table S6). At 48 hours, serum sodium level remained within a clinically acceptable range despite being statistically higher in the TLV+F group, and no cases of symptomatic hypernatremia were observed.

By comparison, other intensified decongestion strategies, including high-dose loop diuretics, thiazides, acetazolamide, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, and ultrafiltration have been explored in ADHF.5,6,23,26, 27, 28, 29 However, their long-term benefits remain uncertain, and several approaches are associated with renal dysfunction, hypotension, and electrolyte disturbances.5,6,23,27, 28, 29 In our routine clinical practice, loop diuretics often are used at relatively modest doses, particularly when concerns are present regarding worsening renal function, hypotension, or electrolyte imbalance. In this context, adjunctive intravenous TLV may be selected when additional decongestion is desired, without simply escalating the loop diuretic dose.

Overall, our findings suggest that TLV may be a useful component of acute decongestion therapy in ADHF. However, given the retrospective design and limited observation period, these findings primarily support short-term safety, and conclusions regarding longer-term safety should be made with caution. Intravenous TLV may therefore be considered as part of enhanced decongestion therapy in selected patients with ADHF, particularly when rapid improvement in oxygenation consistent with decongestion is desired and oral absorption may be unreliable.

Limitations

First, this was a single-centre, retrospective observational study with a limited sample size, which may have underpowered the analysis, particularly for in-hospital mortality and subgroup comparisons. Patients without initial furosemide therapy were excluded, which may have introduced selection bias by omitting less-severe cases. In addition, because intravenous TLV was initiated after reassessment of the early clinical course and at the discretion of the treating physician, patients with persistent dyspnea, ongoing oxygen requirement, perceived inadequate early diuretic response, or residual signs of congestion may have been more likely to receive TLV. No predefined objective institutional criteria were used to guide this treatment decision. Therefore, treatment selection may have been influenced by both measured and unmeasured factors, raising the possibility of confounding by indication and treatment-selection bias. Although IPTW and multivariable adjustment mitigated imbalance in measured covariates, they cannot eliminate bias arising from unmeasured factors or physician judgment in an observational design. In addition, 63 patients were excluded because P/F ratio data were unavailable within the predefined assessment window. Baseline data could be retrieved for 51 of these excluded patients, and comparison with the analyzed cohort showed differences in several baseline characteristics, including age, admission SBP, LVEF, oxygen support, and prior HF hospitalization. Because the direction of severity was not uniform, the pattern of missingness appeared heterogeneous; however, selection bias related to missing outcome data cannot be excluded. In addition, because the timing of intravenous TLV initiation was not standardized and varied according to clinical reassessment in routine practice, heterogeneity in treatment timing may have influenced the observed physiological response.

Second, the P/F ratio is affected by extrapulmonary factors, including FiO2 measurement inaccuracies during noninvasive support and ventilation–perfusion mismatch.13,17 Although these factors may reduce measurement precision, the P/F ratio remains an objective measure of oxygenation in routine practice, and our use of ΔP/F as a supplementary analysis provided complementary support for the primary findings, although residual bias cannot be excluded.

Third, we did not collect urine osmolality data, which could have been a key factor in explaining the advantages of adding TLV to furosemide, compared with use of F alone. However, considering TLV’s mechanism of action, a mechanistic link between increased urine output and subsequent improvement in the P/F ratio may be plausible.

Finally, although we selected the P/F ratio as the primary endpoint due to its objectivity and clinical familiarity, it is not yet validated as a standard marker in ADHF. The cutoff of 300, derived from ARDS studies, also requires further validation in this population. Nonetheless, multivariable analysis confirmed an independent association between TLV use and improved oxygenation, supporting the relevance of the P/F ratio as an exploratory indicator in the acute setting. Given its single-centre, retrospective nature, this study should be considered hypothesis-generating, and the findings warrant validation in larger prospective cohorts.

Conclusion

The addition of intravenous TLV to furosemide in patients with ADHF was associated with improved oxygenation, as assessed by the P/F ratio, and greater urine output at 24 hours, without an apparent increase in hypotension, renal dysfunction, or clinically significant electrolyte disturbances. In-hospital mortality was comparable between the 2 groups, supporting an acceptable short-term safety profile of intravenous TLV as part of enhanced decongestion therapy. Our findings also suggest that the P/F ratio may serve as an exploratory indicator of oxygenation changes consistent with decongestion in ADHF. Given the retrospective design and the unestablished validity of this measure in this setting, these results should be considered hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in larger prospective studies.
