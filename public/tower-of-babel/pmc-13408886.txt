Spectroscopic investigation of the photochemical transformations of metoclopramide hydrochloride in the presence of alkaline and oxidative conditions
Authors: Radu Cercel, Andreea Androne, Cristina Bartha, Catalin Negrila, Mihaela Baibarac, Stefano Bellucci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the photodegradation of metoclopramide hydrochloride (MET) in alkaline conditions and in the presence of oxidative agents such as hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). The degradation process was analyzed using UV–VIS spectroscopy, photoluminescence (PL), Raman scattering, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), thermogravimetry, and mass spectrometry. Hydrolysis of MET in alkaline solutions led to the emergence of three isosbestic points at 242, 258, and 326 nm in UV–VIS spectra, accompanied by a decrease in PL intensity at 363 nm. These spectral changes are attributed to the formation of reaction products, specifically 4-amino-5-chloro-2-methoxybenzoic acid sodium salt and 2-(diethylamino)-ethyl ammonium chloride. In contrast, MET interaction with H2O2 resulted in a hypsochromic shift of the 270 nm band to 260 nm and a bathochromic shift of the 280 nm band to 296 nm, along with the appearance of a single isosbestic point at 314 nm and a concurrent reduction in PL intensity around 365 nm. Raman, FTIR, thermogravimetry, and mass spectrometry studies confirmed the generation of 4-amino-5-chloro-2-hydroxybenzoic acid and 2-(diethylamino)-ethyl-hydroxylamine as degradation products.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54111-0.

Introduction

Metoclopramide hydrochloride (MET), chemically known as 4-amino-5-chloro-N-[2-(diethylamino)ethyl]-2-methoxybenzamide hydrochloride, is widely used to treat nausea, vomiting, and gastroparesis1–6. It is an active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) in various drug formulations, including Metoclopramid, Antigram, Anagraine, Pangastren, and Talex. Due to its molecular structure containing polar functional groups (primary amine, tertiary amine, and amide), MET is highly susceptible to environmental factors such as hydrolysis and oxidation7,8.

Since 2001, various studies have investigated MET’s structural and optical properties using techniques such as X-ray diffraction (XRD), UV–VIS and FTIR spectroscopy, Raman scattering, proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1 H NMR), thermogravimetry (TG), derivative thermogravimetry (DTG), differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), and photoluminescence (PL)8–11. These studies have provided insights into MET’s polymorphism12, phase transformations13, pharmaceutical formulations9, and adsorption behavior on colloidal silver via surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS)14. With increasing concern over pharmaceutical contaminants in water bodies, MET has been identified in wastewater from hospitals, pharmaceutical production sites, and treatment plants15. Consequently, two main strategies have emerged for MET monitoring: (i) electrochemical detection via cyclic voltammetry16,17 and spectrophotometry18,19, and (ii) spectrophotometric methods for pollutant monitoring19. However, limited research has addressed the impact of light exposure and alkaline or oxidative conditions on MET degradation14,20. This study aims to bridge that gap by investigating MET photodegradation using a combination of UV–VIS spectroscopy, PL, Raman scattering, FTIR spectroscopy, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), thermogravimetry, and mass spectrometry. The photodegradation of the pharmaceutical product Metoclopramide tablets (METO), containing 10 mg MET each tablet, along with excipients such as lactose monohydrate and anhydrous lactose, will also be studied.

The purpose of this paper is to understand the transformations that occur upon exposure of MET to UV light, in the presence of alkaline or oxidative environments. These studies are crucial for the stability and efficacy during the shelf life of the drug and the choice of appropriate packaging for the pharmaceutical product. The immediate impact of this study will be related to preventing the loss of therapeutic efficacy of METO by packaging in opaque containers. Knowledge of the new compounds resulting from the photodegradation of MET in alkaline media may allow for the subsequent evaluation of the toxicity of these compounds, with consequences in the METO dosage administered in therapeutic regimens. Last but not least, the identification of the photodegradation products of MET in oxidative media may have an important role in the evaluation of adverse reactions triggered during METO treatment, with consequences in the dosage administered to patients in view of their safety and/or the development of optimized pharmaceutical formulations containing antioxidants.

Results and discussion

Figure 1a highlights, in the UV–VIS spectrum of the MET aqueous solution, three bands at 214, 270, and 308 nm, which can be assigned to the electronic transitions: (i) π-π* in the benzene ring and n-π of the amide group, (ii) n-π of the amine groups present in substituted benzene ring, and (iii) substituted benzene ring, respectively. The UV–VIS spectra of the METO aqueous solution show the three bands, which are hypsochromic shifted at 204, 264 and 300 nm (Fig. 1b). Focusing attention on the PLE spectra of Fig. 7, two bands at 331 and 292 nm, with high and low intensity, are observed. The PLE band at 331 nm originates from the π → π* transitions belonging to substituted aromatic molecules with electron-donating groups such as –N(CH2CH3)3 and electron-withdrawing groups such as –Cl, –CONH– 22. The higher intensity of the PLE band at 331 nm can be explained by taking into account that π → π* transitions are usually electrically allowed, having the highest probability. The PLE band at 292 nm originates from n → π* transitions, which involve the unpaired electrons of the carbonyl oxygen or nitrogen from the amide groups to the π* orbitals22. The lower intensity of the PLE band at 292 nm can be explained on the basis that the n → π* transitions are partially electrically forbidden and have a reduced transition moment. The ratio between the intensities of the PLE bands at 292 and 331 nm varies for METO samples in PBS as the pH increases from 6.4 to 7 and 8, from 0.2 to 0.22 and 0.27, respectively. This variation can be explained by considering the chemical processes of METO in the presence or absence of protons. In the case of the samples of METO in PBS with pH equal to 6.4, the protonation reduces the electron-donating character of the –N(CH2CH3)3 group and stabilizes the π → π* transition, the PLE band at 331 nm being more intense than in the case of the samples of METO in PBS with pH equal to 7 and 8. In the case of the METO samples in PBS with pH equal to 7 and 8, the intensity of the PLE band at 331 nm decreases, as a consequence of an internal charge transfer process between the substituted aromatic ring and the rest of the METO molecule. This induces a decrease in the probability of the π → π* transition, when a possible favoring of intersystem transitions between singlet and triplet states (S₁→T₁) occurs. Upon alkaline hydrolysis, the relative intensity of the 292 nm band decreases, while the excitation profile becomes dominated by the 311–331 nm contribution. This behavior is consistent with cleavage of the amide bond and formation of two products: 4-amino-5-chloro-2-methoxybenzoic acid (or its sodium salt in NaOH medium) and 2-(diethylamino)-ethyl ammonium chloride. Since one amide-containing chromophore is consumed per MET molecule, the decrease of the n→π* contribution at ~ 292 nm is in agreement with the 1:1 stoichiometry proposed for the hydrolysis products.

The interaction of MET with NaOH in the case of the MET : NaOH 1:2 sample has induced in: (i) the XPS C1s (Figure S2a2), highlighting four maxima located at 284.8, 286, 288.3 and 289.2 eV, which were attributed to the C = C/C-C/C-H, C-N, C = O and O = C-O bonds33,34,36,38; (ii) the XPS N1s (Figure S2b2), the presence of three peaks at 399.5, 400.8 and 403.1 eV, belonging to the bonds N-C, N-H and quaternary nitrogen36; and (iii) the XPS Cl2p (Figure S2c2), noting only four peaks located at 198.2, 199.4, 199.8 and 201 eV, which were assigned to the first three to Cl− anion which compensates for the positive charge of the quaternary nitrogen and the last one attributed to the C-Cl covalent bond35.

Summarizing these results, the main arguments concerning reaction 3 in Fig. 4 are: (i) the high intensity of the Raman line at 1628 cm−1 and the presence of two new Raman lines at 1063 and 1365 cm−1 which are specific to compounds containing carboxylic groups and triethylamine-type entities; (ii) the increase in absorbance of the IR bands located in the spectral range 1400–1500 cm−1 specific to ammonium-type compounds and the presence of a new IR band at 1057 cm−1 specific to carboxyl-type functional groups; (iii) the presence of new peaks in the XPS spectra located at 289.2, and 403.1 eV specific to the O = C-O bond and quaternary nitrogen, which suggest the generation of 4-amino-5-chloro-2-methoxy benzoic acid sodium salt and 2-(diethylamino)-ethyl ammonium chloride.

Thermal analysis of MET before and after interaction with NaOH and H₂O₂

Figure S4 shows the analysis by TG-DSC and MS of the samples of MET, MET : NaOH, and MET : H2O2. All three samples thermally decompose at low temperatures, although the process varies depending on their synthesis methods. The most stable sample is metoclopramide, which shows a mass loss of about 14.25% up to 400 °C. The DSC curve for metoclopramide shows a series of small endothermic peaks in this temperature range, with maxima at 83.2 °C, 285.2 °C, and 369.2 °C. These peaks are related to dehydration, followed by thermal decomposition and evaporation of the reaction products, as evidenced by the mass spectra. This behavior deviates slightly from what is reported in the literature, where metoclopramide is known to melt around 184–185 °C 43,44. The thermal behavior of metoclopramide is significantly modified when it interacts with different compounds, namely H2O2, and NaOH, respectively. In all cases, the thermal stability of metoclopramide decreases, the decomposition taking place in a single step without the formation of intermediate phases. These processes are accompanied by a single endothermic peak of high intensity visible on the associated DSC curves, with maxima at 173.1 °C for sample MET: H2O2, and 149.8 °C for sample MET : NaOH. The differences in recorded mass losses, i.e., 14.25% for MET, 54.13% for MET : NaOH, and 67.47% for MET: H2O2 are also confirmed by the mass spectra presented in Fig. S4. According to the mass spectra, MET: NaOH and MET: H2O2, which decompose at lower temperatures (149.8 °C and 173.1 °C, respectively), exhibit a higher abundance of specific fragments during the decomposition process compared to MET. This indicates that each sample follows a distinct decomposition mechanism, which is difficult to identify due to its complexity45,46. The originality of this study derives from the mass spectrometric analysis that provides a quantification of gases/molecular fragments resulting from thermal decompositions. Although mass spectrometry has started to be increasingly used in the developing pharmaceutical industry47 as an analytical method of evaluation, there are no reported data on this type of material yet.

Although the combined spectroscopic and thermal analyses strongly suggest the occurrence of the reactions illustrated in Figs. 4 and 13, these techniques alone do not unambiguously determine the molecular structures of the degradation products. Conclusions

This study presents new insights into the photochemical reactions of MET and METO in the presence of alkaline solutions and H2O2 as an oxidant. Through a combination of UV–VIS spectroscopy, photoluminescence, Raman scattering, FTIR spectroscopy, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), TG-DSC, and mass spectrometry, we gained preliminary insight into the chemical transformations that MET may undergo under oxidative conditions. Key conclusions include: (a) Photochemical Transformation of MET by H2O2: a1) UV–VIS spectroscopy revealed significant hypsochromic shifts and the appearance of isosbestic points, confirming the formation of oxidation products; a2) Photoluminescence measurements demonstrated spectral shifts and intensity reductions, suggesting modifications in molecular structure and electronic states; (b) Oxidation Pathway and Structural Modifications: b1) Raman scattering and FTIR spectroscopy suggest possibles structural alterations, with shifts in characteristic vibrational modes and the appearance of new functional groups, which indicates that the interaction of MET with H2O2 leads to the formation of oxidation products such as 4-amino-5-chloro-2-hydroxy benzoic acid, 2-(diethylamino)-ethyl-hydroxylamine, and methanol, consistent with the proposed reaction mechanism; correlated studies by Raman scattering, FTIR spectroscopy and XPS have suggested that the interaction of MET with NaOH results in the formation of the potential reaction products 4-amino-5-chloro-2-methoxy benzoic acid sodium and 2-(diethylamino)-ethyl ammonium chloride; (c) Thermal Stability and Decomposition Analysis: c1) TG-DSC and MS analyses demonstrated that the thermal stability of MET decreases upon interaction with H2O2 and NaOH, with decomposition occurring at lower temperatures; c2) MET : NaOH and MET : H2O2 samples showed significantly higher mass loss and different fragmentation patterns, indicating distinct decomposition pathways; (d) Implications for Pharmaceutical Stability and Environmental Considerations: d1) The oxidation of MET by H2O2 suggests potential degradation pathways relevant to pharmaceutical formulation and stability; d2) The identification of oxidation products provides insights into potential metabolites and degradation byproducts, which may have implications for drug efficacy and environmental impact. Overall, this study advances the understanding of metoclopramide oxidation mechanisms, providing valuable insights for pharmaceutical sciences, environmental chemistry, and analytical chemistry applications.

Methods

MET, NaOH, H₂O₂ (30 wt%), Na₂HPO₄, and NaH₂PO₄ were procured from Sigma-Aldrich. Metoclopramide tablets (METO), each containing 10 mg MET along with excipients such as lactose monohydrate and anhydrous lactose, were obtained from Terapia (Romania). Phosphate buffer solutions (PBS) with pH values of 6.4, 7, and 8 were prepared using Na₂HPO₄ and NaH₂PO₄.

Aqueous solutions of MET and METO were prepared at concentrations of (a) 0.03 mg/mL for UV–VIS spectroscopy and (b) 1 mg/mL for PL and photoluminescence excitation (PLE) studies. To assess alkaline influence, MET solutions were mixed with NaOH solutions (60 mg/mL) at volumetric ratios of 2:1, 1:1, and 1:2. Similarly, interactions with oxidative agents were studied using MET solutions mixed with H₂O₂ (300 mg/mL) at the same volumetric ratios. Photodegradation experiments were conducted using a 350 W mercury-vapor lamp endowed with an optical filter UG5 to select the 200–380 nm spectral range, where the most intense spectral line of Hg lamp is at 253 nm.

UV–VIS spectra of MET before and after interaction with the alkaline media and oxidative agents were recorded with a Perkin Elmer Lambda 950 UV–VIS–NIR spectrophotometer.

The photoluminescence excitation (PLE) and photoluminescence (PL) spectra of MET before and after interaction with the alkaline media and oxidative agents were obtained using a Horiba Jobin Yvon Fluorolog-3 FL 3–22 spectrophotometer, equipped with a 450 W Xe lamp. PL spectra of MET before and after interaction with the alkaline media and oxidative agents were recorded at an excitation wavelength of 275 nm, while for recording of the PLE spectra, an emission wavelength equal to 380 nm was used.

The Raman spectra of MET before and after the interaction with the alkaline medium and the oxidizing agents were collected using a Bruker FTRaman spectrophotometer, MultiRaman model, equipped with a YAG: Nd laser, the analyses being performed in backscattering geometry, using a laser power of 20 mW, the number of scans being equal to 200. The preparation of each sample for Raman analyses involved the use of 2 ml of Au nanoparticles with an average size of 10 nm, prepared according to the protocol previously published by us 48,49, to which 100 µl of MET was added before and after the interaction with the alkaline medium and the oxidizing agents.

IR spectra of MET before and after interaction with the alkaline media and oxidative agents were recorded with a Bruker FTIR spectrophotometer, Vertex 80 model, in the Attenuated Total Reflection (ATR) geometry, with a scan number equal to 64. Using rough Au supports, prepared according to the previously reported protocol50, 200 µl of MET before and after interaction with the alkaline medium and with the oxidizing agents were deposited on an Au support with an area of 1 cm2 in order to obtain films by vacuum evaporation of solvent, for 2 hours at 70 °C.

The complex thermal investigations were performed with a Netzsch STA 449 F3 Jupiter STA 449 F3 simultaneous thermal analyzer in TG-DSC mode coupled with a mass spectrometer, QMS 301 from OMNISTAR PFEIFFER. Three samples with masses of approximately 15 mg were analyzed in an inert argon atmosphere from room temperature to 500 °C. A heating rate of 10 °C/min and a gas flow rate of 20 ml/min were used. The gases resulting from the thermal degradation of the samples were monitored by a mass spectrometer. MS data were recorded in MID (multiple ion detection) mode through calibration of the ambient air species (argon, nitrogen, and oxygen). In MID mode, the gases were calculated by multiplying the intensities by previously determined calibration factors51.

The XPS spectra of the samples labeled as MET and MET: NaOH 1:2 were performed with a SPECS spectrometer endowed with a monochromatic X-ray source of the type Al Kα 1486.74 eV and a Phoibos 150 electron energy analyser.
