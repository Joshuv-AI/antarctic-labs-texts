ToxSyn-PT: A Large-Scale Synthetic Dataset for Hate Speech Detection in Portuguese
Iago A. Brito, Julia S. Dollis, Fernanda B. Färber, Diogo F. C. Silva, Arlindo R. Galvão Filho

Abstract
We present ToxSyn-PT, the first large-scale Portuguese corpus that enables fine-grained hate-speech classification across nine legally protected minority groups. The dataset contains 53,274 synthetic sentences equally distributed between minorities groups and toxicity labels. ToxSyn-PT is created through a novel four-stage pipeline: (1) a compact, manually curated seed; (2) few-shot expansion with an instruction-tuned LLM; (3) paraphrase-based augmentation; and (4) enrichment, plus additional neutral texts to curb overfitting to group-specific cues. The resulting corpus is class-balanced, stylistically diverse, and free from the social-media domain that dominate existing Portuguese datasets. Despite domain differences with traditional benchmarks, experiments on both binary and multi-label classification on the corpus yields strong results across five public Portuguese hate-speech datasets, demonstrating robust generalization even across domain boundaries. The dataset is publicly released to advance research on synthetic data and hate-speech detection in low-resource settings.

Introduction

Detecting hate speech online has become essential across a variety of environments, from social-media platforms to emerging virtual reality interfaces
Albladi et al. 2025
;
Weerasinghe et al. 2025
. Yet, most progress hinges on English-language datasets harvested from informal social feeds
(
Jahan and Oussalah 2023
)
, leaving other languages without equally robust resources. Models trained predominantly on English data exhibit limited cross-linguistic transfer
Kargaran et al. 2024
, especially in low and mid-resource settings like Portuguese, where high-quality annotated corpora are scarce. This gap is particularly acute in domains beyond social media (e.g. transcript of a spoken message), where the register differs markedly from comment threads and where reliable hate-speech detection is still unresolved.

Recent progress on large language models (LLMs) has revealed powerful capabilities for controlled text generation, making them well-suited for augmenting scarce training data
(
Team et al. 2024
;
Grattafiori et al. 2024
)
. For instance,
Hartvigsen et al. 2022
and
Hui et al. 2024
have demonstrated that LLMs can synthesize toxic examples with fine-grained control over attributes like target group, severity, and discourse style. Despite these advances, these synthetic corpora are confined to English, perpetuating a linguistic bias that excludes languages like Portuguese from benefiting equally. Consequently, there is a pressing need to develop analogous datasets to ensure equitable coverage of underrepresented minority targets.

Compounding this linguistic and domain mismatch, nearly all existing hate-speech datasets adopt a simple toxic/non-toxic scheme that overlooks crucial distinctions in tone and severity level. Moreover, Portuguese corpora are typically small, limited to noisy social-media comments, and lack multi-label annotations for protected classes
(
Vargas et al. 2022
)
. Critically, the few corpus that annotates minority-targeted examples only achieves a small number of samples and does not label positive texts referring to minorities, making it impossible to train models that accurately identify which community is offended. Consequently, current Portuguese classifiers fail to capture the nuanced manifestations of hate, especially in formal or non-social-media contexts, undermining efforts to build truly context-aware and robust detection systems.

To address this gap, in this work we introduce ToxSyn-PT, a large-scale synthetic dataset for Portuguese hate speech detection that enables both binary classification and fine-grained identification of targeted minority groups. Comprising 53,274 LLM-generated samples annotated for toxicity, discourse type, and target group, ToxSyn-PT spans nine legally protected communities, including racial, religious, gender, and ability-based categories, currently underrepresented in Portuguese resources. To our knowledge, it is the first corpus in Portuguese explicitly designed to support multi-label hate-speech classification across minority targets. The dataset is produced via a controlled generation four-stage pipeline as illustrated in Figure
1
, ensuring broad coverage of both overt and subtle hate expressions while balancing label frequencies and rhetorical variation.

We assess the effectiveness of ToxSyn-PT by fine-tuning established classifiers on the dataset and evaluating them on both binary toxicity detection and multi-label group classification tasks. Despite being trained exclusively on toxicity directed at minority groups, the models generalize robustly to broader forms of hate speech, including general insults and offensive content unrelated to demographic identity. This suggests that the linguistic patterns learned from minority-targeted hate extend to more universal expressions of toxicity. The dataset is publicly available at
Hugging Face
to facilitate further research on synthetic data and low-resource hate speech detection.

The main contributions of this work are:

1.

We introduce ToxSyn-PT, the first Portuguese publicly available corpus designed to support hate-speech classification across multiple minority targets, comprising over 50K synthetic instances annotated with toxicity, target group, and discourse-type labels

3.

We evaluate the utility of ToxSyn-PT across multiple classification settings, including binary and multi-label toxicity detection, demonstrating strong generalization to diverse toxic language scenarios.

Related Works

Synthetic corpora have been explored as a means to augment limited hate speech datasets. ToxiGen
(
Hartvigsen et al. 2022
)
combines a classifier-in-the-loop framework with GPT-3
Brown et al. 2020
to adversarially generate over 250,000 toxic examples across 13 demographic targets, yielding measurable improvements in classification performance. ToxiCraft
(
Hui et al. 2024
)
extends this line of work by using GPT-4
(
Achiam et al. 2023
)
with structured prompting and self-evaluation to generate text with greater controllability. Despite their technical sophistication, both approaches are constrained to English, which limits their applicability to languages that differ in sociolinguistic norms and expressions of toxicity.

In the context of Portuguese, HateBR
(
Vargas et al. 2022
)
provides a multilabel dataset of 7,000 Instagram comments, annotated for binary offensiveness, severity (in three levels), and nine hate speech categories. Although the annotation schema is comprehensive, the category distribution is highly uneven: only 727 category labels are assigned in total, with 496 corresponding to partyism and fewer than 100 for any other class (e.g., just two antisemitic samples and a single example of xenophobic text). This imbalance limits the dataset’s utility for training models with generalization capacity.

Several additional Portuguese-language corpora have been proposed, but often suffer from limited scale and class coverage. OFFCOMBR-3
(
De Pelle and Moreira 2017
)
includes 1,250 comments annotated across six categories by three annotators. However, only 19.5% of the samples are toxic, and most categories have very few labeled instances marking prejudice against a minority group. ToLD-BR
(

)
provides annotations for four minority classes but includes less than 30 examples per class. OLID-BR
(
Trajano et al. 2024
)

Although TuPy-E’s multilabel schema spans nine categories (e.g., ageism, aporophobia, capacitism, LGBT-phobia), its utility is undermined by severe class imbalance: some categories, such as ageism (57 examples) or aporophobia (66), are represented by fewer than one hundred instances, whereas misogyny comprises 1,675 samples. Existing Portuguese corpora exhibit an additional blind spot: they annotate only hostile mentions of protected groups and omit neutral or supportive references. Consequently, a sentence that praises Black resilience or affirms LGBTQIA+ rights is either absent or, at best, indistinguishable from non-minority content. This omission prevents classifiers from learning the difference between the absence of hate and the presence of endorsement, limiting applications such as allyship detection and fine-grained content moderation in both formal and informal domains. Filling this gap is essential for building context-aware systems that recognise not just toxicity, but also positive engagement with minority communities.

3
ToxSyn-PT

This section describes the construction of ToxSyn-PT. The dataset was generated with an emphasis on controlled coverage of minority groups and variation in both toxic and non-toxic linguistic expressions. The process comprises four stages: (1) manual creation of a seed dataset; (2) prompt-based expansion across minority groups; (3) paraphrasing-based augmentation; and (4) enrichment with implicit-hate templates. Additionally, neutral content unrelated to minority identity was incorporated to promote topic diversity and reduce overfitting to group-specific patterns.

3.1
Seed Dataset

We begin with a manually curated seed of 160 Portuguese sentences focused on two minority groups: Black people and women. Concentrating on a smaller set of targets allows us to explore a broader range of perspectives, lexical choices, and syntactic variations for each group. These groups were selected due to their prevalence in toxic online discourse and their linguistic diversity. By crafting 40 toxic and 40 non-toxic statements per group that systematically vary tone, grammatical person, and topic, we maximize intra-group diversity. This approach ensures that each minority target is represented by richly varied examples, which serve as strong in-context demonstrations for subsequent LLM prompting.

We hypothesize that by exposing the LLM to diverse expressions of toxicity and neutrality for these two well-represented groups, the model will learn underlying linguistic patterns that generalize effectively to other minority targets. In other words, this compact yet varied seed set provides the foundation for downstream synthetic generation: the LLM can extrapolate what it has learned about how hate manifests and about how neutrality is expressed more broadly, enabling it to produce high-quality synthetic samples for additional minority groups without needing explicit examples for each one.

3.2
Expansion

In the expansion phase, we employ GPT-4o Mini
(
Achiam et al. 2023
)
in a few-shot prompting setup to synthesize new samples conditioned on one of nine minority groups: Afro-descendants, Jews, Muslims, Indigenous Brazilians, women, LGBTQIA
+
individuals, elderly people, and people with disabilities. These groups were selected for three complementary reasons. First, they map directly onto classes protected by Brazilian anti-discrimination statutes and legislations, ensuring our corpus targets communities at legally recognized risk of hate speech. Second, together they span multiple demographic axes (race, religion, gender, sexual orientation, age, and ability), providing broad cultural coverage and enabling the study of intersectional toxicity. Third, several of these categories (e.g., Indigenous Brazilians, Muslims and the elderly) has few samples or are totally absent from existing Portuguese hate-speech datasets; including them addresses critical blind spots and supports equitable model performance across under-represented groups.

Each generation prompt supplies 20 curated seed sentences and specifies whether the model should produce toxic or non-toxic continuations. Sampling is balanced across groups and toxicity classes, with every API call returning 20 samples. To encourage discourse variation, we design 26 controllable templates (14 non-toxic and 12 toxic) that vary ideological frame and tone; a randomized length tag (short, medium, long) further diversifies output. Illustrative templates include
Real Problems Minimization
(toxic), which downplays the hardships faced by a minority group, and
Problem Acknowledgment
(non-toxic), which positively frames real challenges while proposing constructive support. Appendix
A
contains explanations of each generation strategy. We conclude this phase with 10,790 unique sentences, supplying a rich, legally grounded, and demographically diverse foundation for downstream training and evaluation.

3.3
Enrichment

In the final generation round we repeat the few-shot procedure from Section
3.2
, but draw our in-context examples from the 31,589 sentences produced in the paraphrasing stage rather than from the original seed dataset. By exposing GPT-4o Mini to this richer demonstration pool, already populated with a spectrum of explicit and implicit rhetoric, we encourage the model to imitate a broader range of discourse styles while preserving the label fidelity established earlier. Prompts remain balanced across the nine protected groups and toxicity classes, and each API call again returns twenty samples.

To further broaden the corpus’s coverage of implicit hate speech, we extend the template catalogue from 26 to 28 by adding two new toxic patterns:
Ambiguous Prejudice
(double-meaning language to convey prejudice in an implicit but perceptible way) and
Justification Prejudice
(frames discriminatory attitudes as ostensibly reasonable, presenting bias as a matter of common sense, economic necessity, or cultural tradition). Although earlier stages included implicit hate, these two templates diversify the rhetorical tactics used to conceal prejudice, producing harder-to-classify examples that increase the dataset’s linguistic complexity.

Applying all 28 generation templates to the enriched prompt pool yields a final minority-targeted corpus of 50,074 sentences, in which 24,707 are toxic and 25,367 are non-toxic examples. Every sample is labeled with its toxicity status, specific target group, and the template strategy employed. This comprehensive collection captures a continuum of hateful expression, from overt slurs to subtle and covert prejudice, providing a richly annotated dataset for Brazilian Portuguese hate-speech research.

3.4
Augmentation

To diversify lexical choice and pragmatic structure, every sentence generated in the expansion stage is paraphrased twice: once with a toxic and once with a non-toxic transformation. The transformation was selected at random from predefined strategy pools. Toxic transformations operate by (i) victim-blaming, which shifts culpability onto the minority group; (ii) doubt/ambiguity, which casts uncertainty on the legitimacy of the group’s protests; (iii) appeal-to-authority, which cites purported experts or statistics to legitimize prejudice; and (iv) hyperbole/exaggeration, which intensifies negative stereotypes without necessarily using overt slurs. Non-toxic transformations include (i) questioning, which recasts statements as reflective prompts; (ii) contrastive emphasis, which highlights unexpected positive facts; (iii) nuanced ambiguity, which introduces multi-faceted readings that remain non-prejudicial; and (iv) positive negation, which reverses a negative assertion to achieve a neutral or approving stance.

Because these transformations are applied symmetrically (i.e., non-toxic transformation patterns rewrite toxic inputs and vice versa), we observe cases where a toxic sentence is effectively detoxified yet retains residual ambiguity. This controlled ambiguity adds further realism and encourages models to reason beyond surface cues, playing an essential step on dataset generation pipeline.

In this augmentation phase, paraphrasing enables systematic swapping of the original minority target across our nine defined groups. However, LLM outputs occasionally hallucinate unsupported targets or use non-standard labels. To enforce consistency, we apply a regular-expression–based normalization step, mapping all variants to our nine canonical labels (e.g., mapping all “afro-*” variants to "negros") and discarding approximately 8% of sentences that lack a valid mapping. This process yields 20,799 additional paraphrases; when merged with the original samples, the corpus comprises 14,521 toxic and 17,068 non-toxic instances, a total of 31,589 exemplars. These steps substantially broaden ToxSyn-PT’s stylistic and rhetorical diversity while preserving precise, minority-target annotations.

3.5
Neutral Samples

To prevent over-fitting to minority-centric patterns and improve generalization to content where demographic cues are absent, we design a two-phase neutral-text augmentation module. In Phase 1, we curate 60 fully neutral seed sentences and expand each via a five-shot prompting routine using four randomly chosen domain templates (conversational, news, policy memo, academic). Each five-shot input return five new samples as output, resulting in 300 domain-varied variants at final of phase 1.

In Phase 2, we further diversify those 300 sentences through 20 distinct style-transformation prompts, ranging from social-media vernacular to formal editorials, resulting on 6,000 neutral candidates. To maintain emphasis on minority-targeted hate speech, we stratify this pool by seed, domain, and style, then uniformly sample 3,200 sentences for integration. This procedure injects rich topical and stylistic diversity while preserving the dataset’s core focus on protected-group samples.

3.6
Final Dataset

The final ToxSyn-PT corpus comprises 53,274 synthetic sentences, including 24,707 toxic examples, 25,367 non-toxic sentences referencing minority groups, and 3,200 neutral utterances. This results in a near-balanced split (46% toxic vs. 54% non-toxic), with each of the nine protected groups represented by at least 4,900 instances (see Table

We evaluate the effectiveness of the ToxSyn-PT dataset in two classification settings: (i) binary classification (toxic vs. non-toxic), and (ii) multi-label classification of targeted minority groups. 4.3
Classification Experiments

We fine-tuned BERTimbau
Souza et al. 2020
using the ToxSyn-PT training split for both tasks. In the binary setup, the model was trained using all examples without considering target group labels. For the multi-label setting, we constructed group-specific training sets: for each group, we retained all toxic samples targeting that group and sampled 3,000 counterexamples, consisting of 1,000 non-toxic samples from the same group, 1,000 toxic samples from other groups, and 1,000 non-toxic samples from other groups or neutral content.

Due to class imbalance, we report macro-averaged recall (to measure per-class sensitivity) and ROC AUC (to assess ranking quality).

5.3
Discussion

The results indicate that ToxSyn-PT enhances model performance in both binary and multi-label hate speech classification for Portuguese. In binary tasks, synthetic fine-tuning narrows performance gaps on underrepresented benchmarks. In the multi-label setting, the combination of balanced sampling and controlled generation supports strong detection performance across mid-resource groups. However, performance remains lower for categories with sparse original examples, suggesting that further data augmentation may be required.

6
Conclusion

We have introduced ToxSyn-PT, the first Portuguese hate-speech corpus designed for fine-grained, multi-label classification across nine legally protected minority groups. Generated through a controlled four-phase pipeline (seed curation, LLM-driven expansion, paraphrase augmentation, and implicit-hate enrichment), ToxSyn-PT delivers 53,274 balanced samples annotated for toxicity, discourse type, and target group. This resource overcomes the critical scarcity of minority-focused and multi-label data in Portuguese, enabling the training of classifiers that can distinguish which community is being targeted.

Our experiments demonstrate that models fine-tuned on ToxSyn-PT achieve robust performance in both binary toxicity detection and minority-target prediction, even under out-of-domain conditions. By releasing the dataset publicly, we provide a template for synthetic corpus construction in other low-resource languages, paving the way for more equitable and context-aware hate-speech detection systems. Future work will explore integrating severity tiers, intersectional annotations, and large-scale human validation to further enhance realism and applicability.

7
Future Work

Future work includes extending the implicit hate component by designing additional generation templates and incorporating human-in-the-loop validation. This would enable the synthesis of more subtle forms of toxicity, such as sarcasm, microaggressions, and coded language. The proposed methodology could also be adapted to other low-resource languages using cross-lingual prompting and transfer learning, enabling broader applicability in multilingual moderation settings.

In addition to data generation, further research is needed to assess model fairness and robustness. Directions include adversarial evaluation of classifiers trained on ToxSyn-PT, integration of active learning to update the corpus with real-world data, and user-centered studies to assess moderation effectiveness in practical deployments. Other extensions may involve multimodal hate speech detection by incorporating visual context, as well as finer-grained annotations such as severity and discourse function. Finally, ToxSyn-PT could support the creation of synthetic datasets for training LLM safety guardrails in Portuguese, addressing an unmet need in instruction alignment and content filtering.

Limitations

While ToxSyn-PT substantially advances Portuguese hate-speech resources, it carries several caveats. First, each sentence is annotated with a single toxicity label and one target group, precluding intersectional or multi-target cases; overlapping abuses (e.g., simultaneously sexist and racist language) thus remain unmodeled. Second, the corpus lacks severity tiers—mild versus severe toxicity, which limits studies requiring fine-grained harm assessment. Third, our four-stage generation pipeline relies on a fixed set of prompts and normalization rules; although this ensures control and class balance, it may constrain the emergence of novel or context-specific hate-speech patterns.

Moreover, as a fully synthetic dataset, ToxSyn-PT inherits biases from the underlying LLM, including repetitive phrasing and distributional artifacts that may diverge from authentic language use. Finally, while prompt templates and seed examples were manually reviewed, the remainder of the corpus has not undergone large-scale human validation, leaving potential labeling noise unquantified. Addressing these limitations by integrating multi-label intersectional annotations, severity scales, more flexible generation strategies, and extensive human review constitutes important directions for future work.

Ethics Statement

The use of generative models to create synthetic toxic data must be approached with great caution to prevent harmful applications, such as deliberately offending minority groups or training language models to produce hate speech. Nevertheless, responsible use of this methodology can significantly enhance online communication by improving the identification of toxic content and specifying the particular minority groups being targeted. To ensure ethical deployment, a multidisciplinary team should oversee and validate the application of ToxSyn-PT, providing critical guidance on responsible usage and preventive measures for potential misuse.

Appendix A
Pipeline Strategies Description

In this subsection, we catalogue the controlled templates used to generate non-prejudiced sentences. Each strategy is designed to produce neutral or positive language about minority groups—ranging from historical context to explicit allyship—thereby ensuring a diverse set of unoffensive exemplars for downstream training and evaluation.

A.1
Generating Non-Prejudiced Texts

History

Create historical sentences about minority-related events that, while not prejudiced, can be interpreted negatively if taken out of context, maintaining an informative tone.

Inspirational Figures

Celebrate the achievements and legacies of historical figures from minority groups, highlighting their positive impact on society.

Cultural Appreciation

Emphasize the cultural contributions of different minority communities, showcasing the diversity and richness each group brings to society.

Community Strength

Highlight the resilience and solidarity of minority communities, celebrating cooperation, mutual support, and progress achieved.

Positive Inclusivity

Promote acceptance and respect for individuals from all minority groups, addressing sensitive topics in an explicitly positive manner and encouraging equality.

Equal Rights Advocacy

Advocate for equal rights and respect for all groups by underscoring the importance of recognizing and guaranteeing equal rights, thereby fostering inclusion and social justice.

Problem Acknowledgment

Acknowledge and validate the challenges faced by a specific group, promoting solutions and support in a positive and constructive way.

Humanization

Emphasize shared humanity by highlighting common qualities and experiences that unite individuals, fostering empathy and mutual respect.

Thought Provoking Statements

Craft factually correct statements about minority groups that may be misinterpreted if not read carefully, challenging preconceptions.

Extremely Positive

Create unambiguously positive narratives that celebrate a specific group’s contributions and importance in a clear, direct manner.

Positive Stereotypes

Employ common stereotypes explicitly but subvert them to promote acceptance and respect for the group, thereby disarming prejudice.

Economic Development

Demonstrate how the inclusion of minority groups drives economic growth and sustainable development, underscoring their crucial role in society.

Supportive Allyship

Actively support and defend the rights of a specific group, promoting solidarity and amplifying their voices to foster inclusion and justice.

Historical Documentaries

documentary-style scripts that narrate the history and contributions of minority groups in an engaging, educational, and respectful manner.

A.2
Generating Prejudiced Texts

Violence

Generate text that explicitly incites violence against a specific group, promoting aggressive behavior in a clear and direct form.

Hostile

Create a heated debate narrative expressing intensely negative, prejudiced, and toxic views against a group, reflecting strong hostility and biased arguments.

Hate Speech

Compose fictional accounts of hate speech directed at a specific group in a creative and detailed way that reflects the intensity of the prejudice.

Harassment

Develop scenarios of workplace harassment targeting a specific group, illustrating persistent behaviors of humiliation or marginalization.

Authoritative Argument

Formulate arguments that cite alleged authorities, studies, or distorted statistics (even if false) to justify prejudiced viewpoints, lending a veneer of legitimacy to discriminatory statements.

Moral Superiority

Criticize a specific group based on a supposed ethical or moral superiority, suggesting that the group is inherently inferior or corrupt.

Legal Discrimination

Defend arguments that use legal reasoning or formal language to justify discrimination against a group, framing biased claims as legitimate under the law.

Historical Revisionism

Reinterpret historical events to belittle or criticize a group, suggesting that the group was less important or even harmful in certain contexts.

Equal Rights Denial

Argue against granting equal rights to a specific group, promoting inequality as natural or necessary.

Dehumanization

Create dehumanizing comparisons that reduce a group to something less than human, using metaphors or degrading language.

Segregation

Defend the idea of separating a group from society, suggesting that exclusion is necessary to protect social values without explicitly using the word “separation.”

Real Problems Minimization

Downplay the genuine difficulties faced by a group by portraying their struggles as exaggerated or irrelevant, thereby devaluing their experiences.

Ambiguous Prejudice

Use double meanings and coded language to convey prejudice implicitly but perceptibly, making hostility less overt yet still recognizable. Strategy added only in phase three of our pipeline.

Justification Prejudice

Create sentences that normalize prejudiced attitudes by presenting them as justifiable or socially acceptable in a subtle, implicit manner. Strategy added only in phase three of our pipeline.

A.3
Non-Toxic Rewriting Strategies

Contrast Emphasis

Rewrite sentences to highlight apparent contradictions that initially seem conflicting but ultimately underscore positive aspects of the mentioned group.

Question Format

Transform statements into rhetorical or direct questions that emphasize positive aspects of a group, engaging the reader interactively.

Nuanced Ambiguity

Reformulate sentences to introduce ambiguity, allowing multiple interpretations that might seem prejudiced at first glance but in fact carry no bias.

Positive Negation

Rewrite sentences by negating negative assertions, resulting in neutral or affirming statements that avoid prejudice.

A.4
Toxic Rewriting Strategies

Victim Blaming

Reformulate texts to blame the minority group itself for the problems it faces, insinuating that they are responsible for their own discrimination or hardships.

Doubt or Ambiguity

Rewrite toxic texts to insinuate prejudice ambiguously or suggest doubt, making the offense less direct while remaining perceptible.

Appeal to Authority

Reformulate toxic texts by citing supposed authorities, studies, or statistics (even when fabricated) to legitimize discriminatory claims.

Hyperbole or Exaggeration

Rewrite toxic texts to intensify or exaggerate prejudice in an overt manner, using hyperbole and amplification without resorting to explicit slurs.
