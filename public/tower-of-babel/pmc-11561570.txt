Countermeasures and follow-up actions in response to information on thyroid cancer, which possibly provoked misunderstanding and unfounded rumour, issued by five former Japanese prime ministers
Authors: Isamu Amir, Naomi Ito, Masaharu Tsubokura
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study summarises the responses to information on thyroid cancer, which possibly provoked misunderstanding and unfounded rumour, described in a letter issued by five former Japanese Prime Ministers on 27 January, 2022. Fukushima Medical University (FMU) implemented countermeasures and follow-up in three phases in response to the letter. In Phase 1, FMU shared scientific facts on the relationship between radiation exposure and thyroid cancer, as well as the response of FMU towards those who read the letter and were concerned about the description. Furthermore, FMU organised lectures and workshops to facilitate peer support among medical and non-medical staff, which were publicised through a newspaper article. In Phase 2, the peer support programme targeting patients with thyroid cancer was launched. In Phase 3, FMU participated in a TV programme to explain the medical aspects of thyroid cancer and the importance of providing peer support to patients with cancer, featuring individuals with experiences as medical staff and survivors.

Introduction

Discrimination and prejudice based on race, sex and disabilities persist in our society. Similarly, there is a concerning trend of discrimination and prejudice related to radiation exposure and its health effects, particularly genetic effects. These issues can lead to significant social arguments.

On 27 January 2022, five former Japanese prime ministers including Jun-ichiro Koizumi, issued a letter (the letter) to President of the European Commission, Dr. Ursula von der Leyen, titled ‘Pursuing a Nuclear-Free and Carbon-Free Society is Possible.’ The letter included the sentence ‘many children are suffering from thyroid cancer,’ as one of the tragedies of Fukushima Dai-ichi Nuclear Power Station (FDNPS) accident(1).

From a scientific standpoint, there is no recognised causal relationship between thyroid cancer and radiation exposure from the FDNPS accident(2). However, among the young generation in Fukushima who read the letter, particularly those who are eligible for thyroid screening and their families, this expression triggered anxiety over the potential development of thyroid cancer in the future. In response, a local newspaper, ‘Kahoku Shimpo,’ issued an article on 9 February 2022, including voices from patients with thyroid cancer and their families, describing their disappointment, despair, and loneliness(3).

The letter sparked concerns about potential anxiety, discrimination, prejudice, stigma or ‘unfounded rumour’ towards those from Fukushima Prefecture in Japan.

Regarding stigma following the FDNPS accident, Sawano et al.(4) highlighted the misinterpretation of hospital insurance claims data, which has led to the development of stigma in the region. They emphasised the need to establish accurate disease databases and to improve local people’s literacy to prevent such issues. Furthermore, Tani et al. (5) focused on the political use of aspects related to the FDNPS accident in a 10-day follow-up after the letter’s release. This article strongly stressed the importance of disseminating authoritative scientific information to prevent unfounded rumour including discrimination and prejudice.

Based on these previous reports, this study aimed to explore how academic and medical institutions near nuclear accident sites should promptly address the spread of uncertain information, which may possibly cause misunderstanding, discrimination and prejudice. Herein, strategies for supporting individuals who might face such a situation after the event of another nuclear disaster are discussed.

This study outlines the responses of Fukushima Medical University (FMU), as an academic and medical institution, in three phases and summarises the actions taken against the letter issued by five former Japanese prime ministers, which contained information about thyroid cancer, which possibly provoked misunderstanding and subsequent unfounded rumour.

Timeline

Following the letter’s issuance on 27 January 2022, FMU recognised the extreme seriousness of the situation. FMU took various actions as an academic and medical institution located in Fukushima Prefecture which was responsible for the Fukushima Health Management Survey (FHMS) initiated after the FDNPS accident in 2011(6). Actions

After the issuance of the letter, FMU promptly took an action, given its role as a medical institution dedicated to supporting the health of local Fukushima Prefecture residents. Actions included not only the provision of scientific facts but also the empathetic alleviation of the concerns of those who were anxious. Outcomes of the actions executed by FMU were divided into following three phases:

(1) Phase 1 (Countermeasures toward the letter from the beginning of February to the end of May 2022)

On 4 February, shortly after the letter’s issuance, the Radiation Medical Science Center (RMSC) of FMU released a statement towards prefectural residents. The declaration summarised scientific findings from the Fukushima Health Management Survey (FHMS)(7). Local newspapers covered this declaration.

The message was composed based on two reports about ‘suspected thyroid cancer’ discovered during FHMS thyroid screening. One report came from the United Nations Scientific Committee on the Effects of Atomic Radiation (UNSCEAR) 2020/2021 report(2). The other was extracted from the ‘Interim Summary’ of FHMS Review Committee (8).

On 17 February, RMSC issued a second statement for those eligible for FHMS thyroid screening and their parents/guardians, focusing on two key points(9):

(1) RMSC has responded for individuals and families with enquiries and concerns related to the thyroid screening of FHMS.

(2) The statement re-announced RMSC’s provision of support for those eligible for second thyroid screening, medical treatment, and follow-up.

In the concluding paragraph, RMSC expressed its intention to enhance these supportive efforts, to provide the latest information, and to be sensitive to the feelings of prefectural residents. Specifically, RMSC sought to dispel misunderstandings and prejudice about the health effects of radiation.

This statement represents a different approach, considering not only the scientific content but also the emotions and anxieties of those eligible for thyroid screening and their parents/guardians.

Local newspapers covered these declarations the following day, with RMSC’s contact number for prefectural residents who were seeking consultation.

Furthermore, on 24 February, the Ministry of the Environment (MOE) launched a dedicated webpage on their site offering a ‘Q&A on Thyroid Screening’ with the contact information for FMU provided in the last section of the Q&A page(10).

On 5 March, during the International Symposium on the FHMS, RMSC issued another statement announcing the initiation of ‘peer support,’ a programme excluding screening or medical aspects. The Mental Health Commission of Canada defines peer support as ‘a supportive relationship between people who have a lived experience in common’(11). In this context, patients can share their ‘life challenges’ with those who have been diagnosed with the same disease before, which helps to mitigate their anxiety with advice from former patients. The stated programme is designed to support individuals diagnosed with thyroid cancer in their ‘current lives,’ and address concerns and worries about their ‘future lives,’ including higher education, employment, marriage and more. The statement also underscores the importance of the interactions among family members in addition to those of the diagnosed individuals.

Following the release of statements on peer support, FMU took a thoughtful approach by inviting an expert, Ms. Naomi Sakurai, a breast cancer survivor from the Adolescents and Young Adults generation (AYA: aged 15–39 years old), who is currently engaged in peer support activities for patients and their families at an organisation, to participate in the programme(12). An essential aspect of peer support is to emphasise the challenges patients and survivors face, rather than solely problem-solving.

Two study sessions were conducted for both medical and non-medical FMU staff members. The first session was carried out online with a limited number of participants, while the second session accommodated ~50 participants and was held both onsite and online (Figure 1).

In the second study session, Ms. Sakurai, the lecturer, covered the definition and significance of peer support, stressed the importance of establishing rules, explained the distinctions between support during treatment and peer support, and addressed its limitations.

This session helped medical professionals, such as doctors and nurses, understand the differences between medical support and peer support. A local newspaper reported on this study session.

(2) Phase 2 (follow-up from the end of June to August 2022)

Following the sessions in Phase 1, the inaugural peer support programme commenced on 28 June. It targeted individuals, aged 18 to 39, diagnosed with thyroid cancer who visited FMU as outpatients.

Furthermore, a peer support workshop took place as part of a public lecture on 9 August, in conjunction with the ‘GU-GU-RU’ project initiated by MOE. Ms. Sakurai was also a guest lecturer for this event.

MOE launched the ‘GU-GU-RU’ project in July 2021, aiming to provide updated information on health effects, particularly genetic effects, of radiation to dispel misunderstanding, prejudice and discrimination. This is crucial because ~40% of the Japanese population holds inaccurate information that current radiation exposure from the FDNPS accident could be inherited by the next generation(13).

During the public lecture, the participants were divided into three groups, each comprising five to six individuals. Two individuals within each group were selected to role-play as a patient and a peer supporter taking turns in their roles during the practice. In every role-play scenario, the patient’s condition/situation were changed, and the supporter endeavoured to communicate with them. Other group members observed the interaction between the two, recorded their observations and opinions, and provided feedback afterwards.

As per Ms. Sakurai, a key aspect of peer support is to ensure that the patient is not left alone. To accomplish this, it is crucial to highlight that the peer supporter is standing by the patient’s side. Local newspapers also covered this workshop.

On 18 February 2023, Fukushima Central Television aired a TV programme titled, ‘Radiation Times’ as part of the ‘GU-GU-RU’ project. The programme was composed of following three parts(14):

For (3), the programme consisted of two parts. In the former part, characteristics of thyroid cancer were explained. It also featured a lounge where general cancer peer support was provided irrespective of age or disease site. For the latter part, Professor Shigehira Saji from the Department of Medical Oncology at FMU highlighted the significance and importance of peer support from a physician’s perspective. In addition, Ms. Sakurai conveyed the effectiveness of peer support from the patient’s standpoint. The programme was broadcast in Fukushima prefecture on Saturday evening and achieved a viewership rating of 3.4%.

Discussion

The impact of the three phases executed by FMU after the letter’s issuance should be examined.

For phase 1, the statements made by RMSC representing a swift and empathetic response to the letter, stand out. The first declaration was released on 4 February, a week after the letter’s issuance. Unlike the usual delay seen in declarations from administrative or academic institutions due to internal checks, FMU efficiently compiled and shared scientific information on this occasion. They continued to release statements every 2 weeks as an academic institution, emphasising a prompt response objecting to the information which possibly provoked misunderstanding and prejudice. This approach appears to reflect lessons learned from the FDNPS accident, where FMU was unable to share appropriate information during the chaotic situation.

The statements were also intriguing when viewed through the lens of empathy. The messages issued by RMSC on 17 February and 5 March significantly differ from previous ones. FMU, as an academic and a medical institution, does not simply present scientific facts but expresses solidarity with the feelings of prefectural residents who read the letter.

While no scientific link has been established between radiation exposure from the FDNPS accident and thyroid cancers detected in FHMS thyroid screenings, it is a fact that thyroid cancer cases have emerged among prefectural residents through the screenings.

Therefore, the messages were targeted at individuals, and their families, concerned about thyroid screenings or their results, as well as those diagnosed with thyroid cancer, and their families. Statements from FMU conveyed empathy while aligning with their perspective. The approach of RMSC, particularly regarding empathy, reflects the lessons learned about the importance of standing with prefectural residents and their viewpoints.

Following the statement publicised on 5 March, FMU began the preparation of a peer support programme for individuals diagnosed with thyroid cancer. While FMU already provides non-medical support to patients with cancer, including the establishment of the Cancer Counselling Support Centre, support specifically tailored to patients with cancer discovered after the FDNPS accident, has not been sufficient.

In preparation, FMU held training sessions not only for patients with thyroid cancer but also for other patients with cancer in the AYA generation. The goal was to provide tangible support and make patients aware of messages that they have a place to consult about the challenges they may face, which meant they were not alone. The intent of the training was for all participants to know what peer support entails and to experience the perspectives of both patients and peer supporters.

While peer support is not the sole non-medical support option for patients with cancer, various forms of support exist, and the most suitable approach may vary for each individual. Nonetheless, the crucial element is to communicate ‘peer support is available at FMU’ as one of their choices. This can help alleviate patients’ anxiety by providing an additional option for them to consider.

The newspaper coverage of the training sessions was vital to reach the public and showcase activities of FMU to prefectural residents, as effective information sharing through the media.

For phase 3, using TV programmes for communication remained effective, even in an age of thriving social networking services. The broadcast of a special programme on a Saturday evening, which explained thyroid cancer and the peer support programme, was successful.

The programme structured its content to highlight the characteristics of thyroid cancer, and to provide an overview of peer support including insights from both medical professionals and patients with peer support experiences. The success is evident in the viewer ratings, which reached 3.4%, underscoring the importance of using television to disseminate information to prefectural residents. Some of these videos are available on the ‘GU-GU-RU’ project YouTube channel. Future information dissemination strategies, like utilizing social media, are under consideration.

Examining actions executed by FMU, phases 1, 2 and 3 had distinct content, yet they were interconnected over time, collectively serving to disseminate information to prefectural residents. As stated in RESULTS(3), peer support is one option of support for patients with cancer, and as a Fukushima-based medical institution, FMU can support residents by offering opportunities like peer support.

This experience underscores the significance of timely and sequential responses to information which possibly provoked misunderstanding and subsequent unfounded rumour. Follow-up actions and the dissemination of such activities across various media are also crucial.

Conclusion

The letter with information that possibly provoked misunderstanding and unfounded rumour, from five former Japanese prime ministers, issued in late January 2022, prompted FMU to take countermeasures and follow-up actions in three phases, which emphasised the significance of ‘promptness’ and ‘empathy’ in the initial phase. For the second and third phases, the use of various media for showing activities conducted by FMU was found to be effective.

The lesson here extends beyond radiation and health effects; it highlights the necessity of implementing prompt and sequential countermeasures against uncertain information, providing accurate data and showing empathy. This could be applicable to various situations in the future, including the spread of unknown infectious diseases.
