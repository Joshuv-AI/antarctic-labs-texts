Finding clonal hematopoiesis before sequencing
Authors: Nancy Gillis, Jeffrey West
Published: 2026

In this issue of Blood Advances, Batchi-Bouyou et al1 describe clonal hematopoiesis (CH)–Predict, a predictive model that uses routine clinical data to identify individuals likely to harbor gene-specific CH, substantially reducing the number of people who need to be sequenced to find CH carriers.

The field of CH has progressed from discovery to intervention, but one challenge remains unchanged: efficiently identifying individuals who harbor CH. CH is linked to risk for hematologic malignancies, cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, and other age-related conditions.2 To mitigate these risks, several ongoing clinical trials target specific CH mutations and pathways.3 These advances have created a practical challenge: how do we efficiently find people with CH when CH is not routinely screened for in clinical practice? Batchi-Bouyou et al tackle this problem by asking whether routinely available demographic and blood count data can identify individuals most likely to have CH. The authors developed CH-Predict using data from >450 000 UK Biobank participants and validated the model using the All of Us research database and a subset of the MSK-IMPACT cohort. The model combines age, sex, race, smoking history, and standard blood count parameters to estimate the likelihood of overall and gene-specific CH.

This study provides insights into both what drives CH prediction and how it can be applied in practice. Age remained the strongest predictor for most CH subtypes, reinforcing the central role of aging in CH biology. However, age was not the whole story. Blood count parameters added meaningful predictive value for multiple genes, with platelet count improving the identification of JAK2-CH, red cell distribution width (RDW) improving the prediction of U2AF1-CH, and both RDW and neutrophil count contributing to the prediction of IDH1/2-CH. This pattern is biologically plausible because mutations in JAK2, U2AF1, and IDH1/2 may produce more distinct hematopoietic phenotypes than common age-associated drivers (eg, DNMT3A and TET2), making them more detectable through routine blood count parameters.4 By incorporating these signals, CH-Predict may help identify individuals who might otherwise be missed by age-based screening alone.

These gene-specific improvements in prediction also highlight important features of CH biology. Hematopoiesis is a biological process subject to somatic evolution, in which mutations accumulate during aging. Indeed, CH prevalence increases with age in all 3 cohorts considered, but mutational accumulation alone cannot explain prolonged periods of stability during which clones remain at low levels before rapid expansion is triggered by selective pressures such as inflammatory insults or cancer treatment.5 Other recent work has aimed to disentangle the 2 competing effects of somatic evolution: the selective advantage of mutations (clonal expansion) and the malignant risk posed by these clones (eg, the rate of transformation), both of which are typically accelerated with age.6 The observation that routine blood count parameters improve prediction for specific CH genotypes suggests that clinical variables may capture some of the biological consequences of these evolutionary processes, providing information beyond age alone.

Much of the research in the field has used longitudinal sequencing data as a biomarker to track and predict disease progression. However, the most important contribution of CH-Predict may not be its discrimination performance but its ability to enrich for CH carriers before sequencing. When the authors used CH-Predict to prioritize individuals for sequencing, they reduced the number of samples needed to identify CH carriers by twofold to sixfold compared with an agnostic approach (see figure). More stringent risk thresholds produced even greater enrichment. These gains could substantially reduce the cost and logistical burden of recruiting participants for observational studies and mutation-specific clinical trials. CH-Predict offers a novel strategy: use information already available in the electronic health record to identify individuals at the highest risk and then deploy sequencing where it is most likely to be informative. This approach may be especially important for studies seeking less common but potentially actionable CH mutations, for which untargeted sequencing can quickly become impractical. Importantly, CH-Predict is publicly available (https://github.com/kbolton-lab/ch-prediction), allowing investigators to readily test and apply the model in their own studies.

Several questions remain. Although blood counts improved prediction for selected genes, age still accounted for much of the predictive signal, raising the question of whether more sophisticated models can substantially improve upon age-based risk stratification. Moreover, individuals identified as high risk may not exclusively harbor CH but could also have clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance, undiagnosed myeloid neoplasms, or other clonal hematopoietic states. Consistent with these limitations, observed CH prevalence often exceeds model predictions despite strong predictive accuracy, suggesting that some important CH-promoting factors may not be captured by routinely collected clinical data. Finally, although the inclusion of the All of Us cohort broadened validation beyond the predominantly White UK Biobank population, future studies should assess whether performance remains consistent across racial and ethnic groups.

The oncology setting raises additional considerations. Patients with cancer differ from population-based cohorts because therapy exposure alters blood counts, influences clonal selection, and enriches for DNA damage response mutations, such as PPM1D and TP53.7,8 Deeper sequencing may also detect smaller clones and shift the spectrum of mutations identified. Notably, the prediction of ASXL1-CH in the MSK-IMPACT cohort improved beyond age alone but did not in the UK Biobank cohort, providing evidence that the relative importance of clinical predictors may differ in oncology populations. Future studies should explore whether models tailored to cancer populations can leverage these differences to further improve performance.

Rather than replace sequencing, CH-Predict helps identify the right people to sequence. By leveraging data collected in routine clinical care, Batchi-Bouyou et al provide a practical framework for identifying individuals at the highest risk for CH. As the field moves toward prevention and intervention studies, tools such as CH-Predict may help bridge the gap between large populations at risk and the smaller groups most likely to harbor CH. Whether such risk-enrichment strategies ultimately have a role beyond research recruitment and clinical trial enrollment will depend on demonstrating that identifying CH carriers leads to interventions that improve outcomes.
