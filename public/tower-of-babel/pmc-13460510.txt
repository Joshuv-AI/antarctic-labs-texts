Peri‐ and Post‐Menopausal Hormone Replacement Therapy and Voice Disorder Risk: A TriNetX Study
Authors: David Kayekjian, Warren B. Chun, Shaun A. Nguyen, Ashli K. O'Rourke, Kirsten D. Meenan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThe aim of this study was to elucidate the risk of developing voice disorders among peri‐ and post‐menopausal female hormone replacement therapy (HRT) users.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network. Females aged 40–60 years old were included and stratified into two cohorts: HRT users (n = 16,586) and HRT non‐users (n = 248,725) while excluding for head and neck radiation/neoplasms, smoking, benign laryngeal lesions, thyroid disorders, gender dysphoria, and any other systemic hormone use. Voice and resonance disorders (VRD) and dysphonia were separately assessed at 3‐month intervals post‐HRT initiation after propensity score‐matching for age, sex, race, and ethnicity. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and risk differences (RD) were generated to compare outcomes.ResultsHRT users had significantly higher odds of dysphonia, but not VRD, within 0–9 months (OR 1.72; 95% CI (1.01–2.95), OR 1.66; 95% CI (0.99–2.79), respectively). For HRT users with elevated BMI, the 1‐year VRD incidence was not significantly different than non‐HRT users (RD 0.19%, p = 0.069). Overall, the incidence of VRD and dysphonia within 1 year in both groups was < 0.5% and showed no significant difference at most times between groups.ConclusionsHRT may not have any significant clinical impact on the peri‐ and post‐menopausal voice. Although higher odds were noted earlier after treatment, HRT users did not have higher odds of voice issues after 1 year. This study highlights the lack of consensus in literature and urges future research to fully understand the impact of HRT on peri‐ and post‐menopausal voice.Level of Evidence3.

Introduction

Menopause is defined as the permanent cessation of menstruation for 12 consecutive months, resulting from the natural or surgically induced decline in reproductive hormone levels [1]. In the United States (US), the average age of menopause is 51–52 years, with a range of 45–56 [2, 3]. Symptoms can begin up to 6 years before the final menstrual cycle, during the perimenopausal phase [2, 3]. Annually in the US, about 1.3 million women enter menopause [2]. This transition brings physical, vasomotor, psychological, and urogenital symptoms that commonly include hot flushes, night sweats, decreased bone mineral density, sexual health changes, and an increased cardiovascular disease risk [4]. Approximately 80% of women experience a combination of these symptoms during menopause, and up to 30% may endure debilitating effects [3].

Although these symptoms are well‐recognized, less attention has been given to the impact of hormonal decline on the larynx and pharynx despite potential effects on quality of life. Both structures contain estrogen and progesterone receptors [5, 6]. For example, cytologic changes in the cervix during menopause also occur in the vocal folds due to loss of estrogen's hypertrophic and proliferative effects, including changes in intercellular space, reduced epithelial thickness, and mucosal atrophy [5]. Similar hormone‐dependent alterations occur in the pre‐menstrual phase, where the larynx may experience increased acidity, dryness, vocal cord edema, and venous dilation [5]. Additionally, menopause has been associated with muscular atrophy and reduced tone of vocal folds, thinning of vocal fold mucosa, and decreased glandular secretions, which become thicker and less abundant. These factors can contribute to dysphonia, vocal fatigue, and reduced fundamental frequency (F0) [5, 7].

Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) remains the most effective intervention for alleviating menopausal symptoms, with the potential of improving up to 80% of symptoms [8, 9]. HRT is the use of exogenous hormones such as estrogen, progesterone, and/or testosterone to replace declining endogenous hormone levels [3]. Typically, HRT can involve estrogen‐only therapy in females with hysterectomy or combined estrogen‐progesterone in females with a uterus to prevent endometrial malignancy and hyperplasia [3]. Common estrogen formulations include oral 17β‐estradiol (0.5‐2 mg daily) or conjugated estrogens (0.625–1.25 mg daily), transdermal estradiol (0.014–0.15 mg daily), and vaginal low‐dose estradiol creams, rings, or tablets specific for genitourinary symptoms [10]. Common progesterone formulations include micronized progesterone (100 mg daily) and synthetic progestins like medroxyprogesterone acetate (2.5‐10 mg daily) [3]. FDA‐approved combinations include oral pills such as conjugated estrogens and medroxyprogesterone or transdermal patches such as estradiol and levonorgestrel [10]. In general, the lowest effective doses for the shortest possible duration are often prescribed to reduce the risk of side effects. Testosterone therapy for postmenopausal women is currently not yet FDA approved but is used off‐label most commonly for hypoactive sexual desire disorder [11]. Transdermal formulations, such as a short‐term 300 mcg matrix patch, are preferred over oral to prevent androgen excess side effects like acne and hirsutism [11].

Despite its efficacy, HRT prescription rates declined sharply in the 2000s following suspension of the Women's Health Initiative (WHI) clinical trial. In 2002, its interim analysis resulted in widespread concerns that HRT use increased risks of breast cancer, pulmonary embolism, cerebrovascular accidents, and heart disease [12]. Consequently, HRT use declined from 26.9% in 1999 to 4.7% in 2020 [12]. However, subsequent WHI analyses revealed that for women aged 50–59 or within 10 years of menopause onset, HRT with conjugated estrogen alone or combined with medroxyprogesterone relieved vasomotor symptoms without increasing cardiovascular disease risk or all‐cause mortality [13, 14]. Additionally, a meta‐analysis including 25,000 patients across 30 trials found no significant effect on cardiovascular or cancer mortality and even noted reduced mortality in HRT users under 60 years of age [15]. In response, the FDA has initiated removal of HRT's black box warning, reinforcing HRT as the mainstay treatment for symptomatic menopause [16].

Due to increasing acceptance of HRT, understanding its broader risks and benefits, including its effects on voice, becomes essential [17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. A recent meta‐analysis reported a significantly higher F0 in postmenopausal women using HRT compared to nonusers (185.9 vs. 174.6 Hz, (95% CI, 7.35–16.36 Hz)); however, only four case–control studies were included and no significant differences were found in other acoustic variables [22]. These findings highlight a gap in large‐scale, longitudinal data regarding the impact of HRT on voice. To address this, the present study uses the TriNetX research network to examine the incidence of voice disorders in peri‐ and postmenopausal HRT users versus nonusers.

Methods

A retrospective cohort study was performed utilizing the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network, a federated research platform that provides real‐time access to de‐identified electronic health records from healthcare organizations (HCOs) internationally. As of September 5, 2025, the Global Collaborative Network included 156 HCOs and 178,474,164 patient records. TriNetX de‐identifies data in accordance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act Privacy Rule; therefore, Institutional Review Board approval was not required.

Baseline Cohorts

Adult females aged 40–60 years (peri‐ to post‐menopausal) were included. All cohorts were established using Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes, Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) codes, and International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐10‐CM) codes for cohort inclusion and exclusion criteria and outcomes as appropriate. Two cohorts were created based on the index event: (1) initiation of HRT (ICD‐10‐CM: Z79.890) following an office visit coded for menopausal and other perimenopausal disorders (ICD‐10‐CM: N95), or (2) no such initiation following a similarly coded visit. To ensure that HRT codes reflected therapy for menopausal symptoms rather than other hormone indications, both cohorts excluded any coded prescriptions for systemic steroids (ICD‐10‐CM: Z79.52 and RxNorm H02), pituitary and hypothalamic hormones and analogues, thyroid hormones, pancreatic hormones, and calcium regulation hormones (RxNorm: H01, H03‐05, respectively). Additionally, patients with any history of tobacco or nicotine use, head and neck neoplasms, head and neck radiation, thyroid disorders, and gender identity disorders were also excluded from both cohorts.

Primary Outcomes Analyses

The primary outcomes were the incidence of new diagnoses of voice and resonance disorders (VRD) (ICD‐10‐CM: R49) or dysphonia specifically (ICD‐10‐CM: R49.0) at follow‐up intervals of 0–3, 0–6, 0–9, 0–12, and 6–12 months after the index event. The R49 code is an umbrella diagnostic code which includes dysphonia, aphonia, hyper‐ and hyponasality, other VRD, and unspecified VRD. This code was used to capture the full range of HRT effects on voice with increased event counts for greater statistical power. Dysphonia was assessed separately as a narrower, clinically meaningful outcome, as it is the most commonly coded voice disorder [23]. Patients with a prior diagnosis of VRD or dysphonia before the index event were excluded. Sub‐analyses assessing the independent effects of estrogen, progesterone, or testosterone separately could not be conducted due to TriNetX's privacy safeguards. When cohort outcomes do not meet minimum network thresholds, analyses cannot be run to protect patient privacy and reduce potential identification risks. Thus, the HRT cohort included patients collectively on estrogen, progesterone, and/or testosterone.

Biostatistics

All analyses were performed within the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network using its integrated tools. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and risk differences (RD) were generated to compare outcomes. Propensity score matching on age, sex, race, and ethnicity was used to reduce confounding across cohorts. A p value of < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Baseline Cohort Characteristics and Incidence

At baseline, 16,586 peri‐ and post‐menopausal women were included in the HRT cohort and 248,725 in the non‐HRT cohort (Table 1). The mean age at index was higher in HRT users than in non‐users (51.2 vs. 49.9 years). Significant differences in racial and ethnic distribution were observed between groups, with the HRT cohort comprising a greater proportion of white patients and the non‐HRT cohort including higher proportions of Black or African American, Asian, Hispanic or Latino, and Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander patients. The proportion of American Indian or Alaska Native did not differ significantly between cohorts. After propensity score matching on age, sex, race, and ethnicity, the HRT and non‐HRT cohorts were balanced for these variables, and the matched cohorts were used for all subsequent analyses.

Primary Outcomes: VRD

Following propensity score matching, the cumulative incidence of new VRD in the HRT cohort increased from 0.062% at 0–3 months to 0.30% at 0–12 months post‐HRT initiation (Table 2). Over the same intervals, incidence in the non‐HRT cohort ranged from 0.068% to 0.20%. At all time intervals evaluated (0–3, 0–6, 0–9, 0–12, and 6–12 months), there was no statistically significant difference in VRD incidence between HRT users and non‐users (all, p > 0.05). At 12 months, VRD incidence was 0.30% in the HRT cohort versus 0.20% in the non‐HRT cohort, corresponding to a risk difference of 0.10% (95% CI −0.008 to 0.21) and an odds ratio of 1.51 (95% CI 0.97–2.36; p = 0.070) (Table 2). Across all intervals, the absolute incidence of VRD remained below 0.5% in both cohorts.

Primary Outcomes: Dysphonia

When dysphonia was examined separately, event rates were low in both cohorts, and outcomes for the 0–3 month interval could not be reported because of small counts that did not meet network privacy thresholds (Table 3). From 0 to 6 months, dysphonia incidence was 0.16% in the HRT cohort and 0.09% in the non‐HRT cohort (OR 1.74, 95% CI 0.92–3.23; RD 0.068%, 95% CI −0.009 to 0.15; p = 0.083). The only statistically significant association was observed in the 0–9 month interval, during which dysphonia incidence was 0.22% in HRT users versus 0.13% in non‐users (OR 1.72, 95% CI 1.01–2.95; RD 0.093%; p < 0.005). By 12 months, dysphonia incidence was 0.28% among HRT users and 0.18% among non‐users, yielding an OR of 1.54 (95% CI 0.97–2.45) and a risk difference of 0.099% (95% CI −0.005 to 0.20; p = 0.063). No significant differences were detected in the 6–12‐month interval (OR 1.34, 95% CI 0.69–2.62; RD 0.031%, 95% CI −0.04 to 0.102; p = 0.39). Despite the transiently higher odds at 0–9 months, the absolute incidence of dysphonia remained under 0.3% in both groups at all time points.

Comparative Sub‐Analyses Stratified by BMI

In BMI‐stratified analyses after propensity score matching, HRT use was not significantly associated with VRD in either normal or elevated BMI categories (Table 4). Among patients with normal BMI (20–25 kg/m2), VRD incidence over 3 years was 0.79% in both HRT and non‐HRT users (OR 1.005, 95% CI 0.48–2.12; RD 0.004%, 95% CI −0.58 to 0.59; p = 0.99). For patients with elevated BMI (> 25 kg/m2), the 1‐year VRD incidence was 0.41% in the HRT cohort compared with 0.22% in the non‐HRT cohort (OR 1.86, 95% CI 0.94–3.63; RD 0.19%, 95% CI −0.02 to 0.40; p = 0.069). Although the point estimate suggested higher odds of VRD in HRT users with elevated BMI, this association did not reach statistical significance.

Discussion

In this global, retrospective cohort study of over 240,000 peri‐ and postmenopausal women in the TriNetX network, initiation of HRT was evaluated for its association with new diagnoses of voice disorders compared with matched nonusers. To date, this is the largest study examining the relationship between menopausal HRT and clinically coded voice outcomes, adding population‐level data to literature that has predominantly focused on small acoustic series and short‐term physiologic measures. In the current study, after propensity‐score matching, HRT use was associated with increased odds of dysphonia only in the 0–9‐month interval after initiation, with no significant differences at other time intervals. There were no significant differences at any time interval for VRD codes, and absolute event rates remained low in both cohorts over 1 year.

Current literature evaluating the effects of HRT on voice in peri‐ and postmenopausal women remains inconclusive, with no clear consensus. Some studies suggest that HRT may mitigate menopausal‐related vocal changes, indicating protective effects on speaking F0 and vocal instability [24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. A recent meta‐analysis pooling four small case–control studies (n = 308 total) reported significantly higher mean F0 among age‐matched HRT (estrogen or estrogen + progesterone) users than nonusers (185.9 (8.6) Hz vs. 174.6 (6.6) Hz; p < 0.001; I 2 = 0%), suggesting a protective effect of HRT use [22]. However, pooled analyses of jitter and shimmer in these case–control studies found no differences between HRT users and nonusers, and one study using the Voice Handicap Index‐10 reported no deterioration of scores in non‐HRT users compared with HRT users [28]. Together, these findings suggest that while subtle acoustic differences may be measurable, they may not reliably translate into clinically significant or patient‐perceived disability.

The meta‐analysis also evaluated the impact of elevated BMI on F0. While patients with normal BMI had a higher F0 if taking HRT than nonusers, patients with elevated BMI had similar F0 regardless of HRT status [22]. This may be explained by higher endogenous estrogen levels from adipose tissue in individuals with elevated BMI [28]. In contrast, the present TriNetX study did not identify differences in VRD incidence by BMI category, with similar low rates of coded diagnoses in both normal and elevated BMI strata. This divergence between acoustic‐based and diagnosis‐based studies underscores the distinction between measurable changes in vocal parameters and the threshold at which such changes prompt clinical evaluation or coding.

While prior studies suggest a possible protective effect of HRT (specifically estrogen or estrogen + progesterone) on vocal parameters such as F0, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. The supporting meta‐analysis included 5 small case–control studies, with many groups enrolling fewer than 30 patients, in contrast to more than 250,000 peri‐ and post‐menopausal patients analyzed in this TriNetX cohort [22]. Moreover, it remains challenging to conclude that HRT is truly protective for the voice given the absence of randomized controlled trials, heterogeneity in hormone formulations, inconsistency in administration routes, and substantial variation in dosing regimens across studies [29].

The current large‐scale analysis did not demonstrate sustained improvement in voice outcomes in HRT users, including in BMI‐stratified cohorts, despite controlling for age, ethnicity, race, smoking status, head and neck cancer and radiation, and other systemic hormone therapies. The significant RD and OR for the incidence of dysphonia between groups at 0–9 months should be interpreted cautiously due to the small absolute risk difference and low number of diagnoses per cohort. This suggests voice disorders may not be a primary issue in the peri‐ and postmenopausal population. Though some studies showed objective HRT effects on F0, others showed no subjective changes. This raises the possibility that subclinical changes in voice may only be appreciated by acoustic testing, contributing to the low number of physician‐coded diagnoses in the TriNetX database [28, 30]. In two epidemiologic studies assessing the prevalence of voice disorders and treatment‐seeking behaviors in the general population, only 19.7%–22.1% of patients with a voice disorder sought professional treatment [31, 32]. Common barriers included clinicians not inquiring about voice changes, lack of awareness of treatment options, and assumptions that symptoms were transient [32]. Thus, multiple barriers prevent patients with voice disorders from seeking treatment, which may explain why voice disorders are not commonly coded for diagnosis. Furthermore, a healthy user effect may influence these findings, as patients initiating HRT, especially in the context of evolving safety perceptions with the removal of the FDA black box warning, may be more health‐conscious and seek evaluation for subtle vocal changes, potentially inflating early dysphonia diagnoses before any pharmacologic effect occurs [33, 34].

Additionally, it is important to consider the effects of testosterone HRT on the voice, which the previously mentioned studies did not include. This TriNetX study could not exclusively assess testosterone effects because patient outcomes fell below TriNetX privacy thresholds after applying exclusion criteria. Therefore, patients on testosterone were captured within the larger HRT cohort. In a prospective randomized trial of post‐hysterectomy females aged 41–62 examining intramuscular testosterone‐HRT, a dose‐ and concentration‐dependent decrease in F0 was found in higher dose groups after 24 weeks [30]. Despite objective pitch changes, participants did not report subjective voice changes, further confirming that HRT‐related acoustic changes may not produce perceptible voice changes [30].

Strengths of this study include its large, population‐level sample size and rigorous propensity‐score matching, which enhances cohort comparability while maintaining generalizability. Strict exclusion criteria for smoking, head and neck neoplasms, radiation, thyroid disease, and other systemic hormone therapies further reduce confounding and help isolate the association between menopausal HRT and coded voice outcomes. Limitations include reliance on diagnostic ICD coding in a retrospective fashion, rather than prospective and objective assessments of laryngeal function. The analysis depends on clinician recognition and accurate coding of voice disorders, and gradually manifesting or mild voice changes may be underdiagnosed or inconsistently coded. Secondly, through the TriNetX global health research network, we could not account for routes of administration, dosing, formulations, and adherence, resulting in substantial cohort heterogeneity that may obscure regimen‐specific effects. ICD coding also depends on patients seeking medical care, and perceptions of HRT safety may alter care‐seeking behavior and diagnostic thresholds over time. Additional limitations inherent to TriNetX include the inability to verify individual‐level outcomes or perform chart review because of anonymized, pooled data, the lack of information on symptom severity, absence of laboratory or imaging results, and potential variability in coding practices across institutions. Furthermore, temporal accuracy of diagnosis coding and medication exposure cannot be confirmed, raising the possibility of misclassification. Finally, as a retrospective study, these results cannot imply causation due to unaccountable confounders such as aging voice, undiagnosed laryngeal lesions, vocal health habits, lifestyle choices, and occupational status.

Nevertheless, these findings underscore the lack of clear consensus and the need for long term, prospective trials evaluating voice outcomes before and after HRT initiation with standardized treatment duration, dosing, and hormone formulations [29]. With the nationwide shift in perspective regarding HRT with the FDA's updated stance, and approximately 1.3 million new menopausal females each year in the US, new trials are necessary for primary care providers, gynecologists, and otolaryngologists to counsel patients on both established and lesser‐known effects and benefits of HRT.

Conclusion

In this large global, retrospective cohort study, HRT did not appear to have substantial clinical impact on the peri‐ and post‐menopausal voice over the first year after initiation. Although higher odds of dysphonia were observed early after treatment, this association was transient and not accompanied by a sustained increase in voice disorders at 12 months. In the context of evolving perspectives on menopausal hormone therapy and removal of the FDA black box warning, these findings suggest that concerns about overt, clinically recognized voice disorders should not, in isolation, preclude appropriate HRT use. At the same time, the study underscores persistent uncertainty in the literature and supports the need for prospective, laryngology‐focused future research to clarify the nuanced effects of HRT on both objective voice measures and patient‐reported vocal function.
