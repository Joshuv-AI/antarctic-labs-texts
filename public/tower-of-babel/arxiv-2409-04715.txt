Monoidal categorification of open Richardson varieties
Yingjin Bi

Abstract
. Let G G be a simply connected complex semisimple group of finite simply-laced type, and let v ≤ w v\leq w be Weyl group elements. We construct a monoidal categorification of open Richardson varieties in this setting. The construction realizes the cluster variables of Ménard’s seed by real simple modules in the quiver Hecke category 𝒞 w , v \mathscr{C}_{w,v} , by comparing their truncated Lusztig parameters with the Δ \Delta -vectors at every step of Ménard’s mutation algorithm. Every mutation in a retained direction is realized inside 𝒞 w , v \mathscr{C}_{w,v} . After localizing at the determinantial modules M ⁡ ( w ​ ϖ i , v ​ ϖ i ) M(w\varpi_{i},v\varpi_{i}) , the resulting category is a monoidal categorification of the coordinate ring of the open Richardson variety ℬ ̊ v , w \mathring{\mathcal{B}}_{v,w} . In particular, cluster monomials without negative frozen exponents are represented by real simple modules in 𝒞 w , v \mathscr{C}_{w,v} ; arbitrary frozen Laurent factors are realized in its localization.



1.
Introduction

Cluster algebras connect the geometry of flag varieties with
representation theory
[
3
]
. A monoidal
categorification realizes a cluster algebra as the Grothendieck
ring of an abelian monoidal category, with cluster monomials
represented by real simple objects
[
6
]
.
Here a simple object is called
real
if its monoidal square
is simple. For quantum unipotent coordinate rings, such
categorifications are provided by categories of finite-dimensional
graded modules over symmetric quiver Hecke algebras

[
5
,
8
]
.

Let
G
be of finite type
ADE
, and let
W
be its Weyl group,
with longest element
w_{0}
and length function
\ell
.
For
v\leq w
in the Bruhat order, denote by
\mathring{\mathcal{B}}_{v,w}
the open
Richardson variety obtained by intersecting the Schubert cell
indexed by
w
with the opposite Schubert cell indexed by
v
.
Kashiwara–Kim–Oh–Park introduced a monoidal subcategory

\mathscr{C}_{w,v}\subset\mathscr{C}_{w}
, where
\mathscr{C}_{w}
is
the category of quiver Hecke algebra modules categorifying the
quantum unipotent coordinate ring associated with
w
.
They identified the graded Grothendieck ring of

\mathscr{C}_{w,v}
with a quantum doubly invariant algebra

[
9
]
. After specialization at
q=1

and localization at suitable minors, this algebra becomes

\mathbb{C}[\mathring{\mathcal{B}}_{v,w}]
.

On the additive side, Leclerc constructed a Frobenius category
of modules over a preprojective algebra and an associated
cluster structure
[
13
]
. Ménard subsequently
gave an explicit mutation algorithm for an initial seed of this
structure
[
15
]
. The comparison with
braid-variety seeds identifies the resulting cluster algebra,
after inversion of its frozen variables, with the full coordinate
ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathring{\mathcal{B}}_{v,w}]

[
2
, Theorem 10.1]
.
The purpose of this paper is to construct a monoidal
categorification of this cluster structure by realizing
Ménard’s mutation sequence inside
\mathscr{C}_{w}
.

The main step is a comparison between two families of integer
vectors: Lusztig parameters on the quantum unipotent side and

\Delta
-vectors on the additive side. The former describe
dual canonical basis elements through their
Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt (PBW) parametrization, whereas the
latter record filtration multiplicities of preprojective-algebra
modules. We prove that the coordinates relevant to
v
agree
throughout Ménard’s mutation sequence. Their vanishing at
the final step then places the corresponding simple modules
in
\mathscr{C}_{w,v}
.

To state this comparison precisely, fix a reduced expression

\mathbf{i}
of
w
and the reduced subexpression for
v
selected
by Ménard’s algorithm, using the conventions of
Section
2
. Put

N=\ell(w_{0})
and
d=\ell(v)
, and choose a completion

\mathbf{j}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{N})
to a reduced expression of
w_{0}
,
with the chosen expression of
v
occupying the first
d

positions. Write
\dot{v}=(j_{N},\ldots,j_{1})
for the display
of this completed word in the additive conventions.
Thus
\dot{v}
denotes the full word of length
N
.

For a quantum cluster variable
X
, let

\mathbf{a}^{\mathbf{j}}(X)=\bigl(a^{\mathbf{j}}_{1}(X),\ldots,a^{\mathbf{j}}_{N}(X)\bigr)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{N}

be its
Lusztig parameter
with respect to
\mathbf{j}
.
This is the exponent vector of the distinguished leading
dual PBW term in the triangular expansion of the corresponding
normalized dual canonical basis element, with the PBW
conventions fixed in Section
2
.
On the additive side, for a preprojective-algebra module
R
,
write

\Delta_{\dot{v}}(R)=\bigl(\Delta_{\dot{v},1}(R),\ldots,\Delta_{\dot{v},N}(R)\bigr)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{N}

for its
\Delta
-vector
. Its coordinates record the
multiplicities of the ordered layer modules in the filtration
associated with
\dot{v}
; these layer modules and the filtration
are recalled in Section
2
.
Both vectors are indexed by the same positions

1,\ldots,N
, with the additive positions read from right
to left in the displayed word
\dot{v}
.
We compare their first
d
coordinates using the projection

\pi_{v}:\mathbb{Z}^{N}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{d},\qquad(c_{1},\ldots,c_{N})\longmapsto(c_{1},\ldots,c_{d}).

Theorem 1.1

(Comparison of truncated parameters)
.

Apply Ménard’s mutation sequence to the corresponding
additive and quantum unipotent seeds. At a step
t
, let

R_{k}^{(t)}
be the indecomposable summand of the additive
seed and
X_{k}^{(t)}
the quantum cluster variable carrying
the same position label
k
in the full seeds. Then

\pi_{v}\!\left(\mathbf{a}^{\mathbf{j}}(X_{k}^{(t)})\right)=\pi_{v}\!\left(\Delta_{\dot{v}}(R_{k}^{(t)})\right)

for every step
t
and every label
k
, including labels
omitted from the auxiliary cut seeds used in the proof.
In particular, every variable retained in the final
Richardson seed has its first
d
Lusztig coordinates
equal to zero.

Theorem
4.3
establishes this
comparison by combining PBW leading parameters with
Ménard’s additive mutation formulas. The resulting
vanishing condition shows that the real simple modules
representing the retained variables belong to

\mathscr{C}_{w,v}
. The support formula for convolution,
which is the monoidal product of quiver Hecke algebra
modules, then ensures that subsequent mutations of the
Richardson seed are also realized within this category.
Localization at determinantial modules completes the
categorification.

More precisely, let
I
index the simple roots of
G
, and
let
\varpi_{i}
be the fundamental weight corresponding to

i\in I
. Denote by
M(w\varpi_{i},v\varpi_{i})
the
determinantial simple module corresponding to the quantum
minor with extremal weights
w\varpi_{i}
and
v\varpi_{i}
.
Write
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{w,v}
for the monoidal
localization of
\mathscr{C}_{w,v}
in which these modules
are made invertible. Let
\Sigma_{w,v}
be Ménard’s
initial Richardson seed, let
J_{\mathrm{fr}}
be its set of frozen
indices, and write
x_{a}
for the frozen variable indexed
by
a\in J_{\mathrm{fr}}
. We use
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma_{w,v})
for the
associated cluster algebra over
\mathbb{C}
before inversion
of the frozen variables.

For a graded monoidal category
\mathscr{C}
, define its
classical Grothendieck ring by

K_{\mathrm{cl}}(\mathscr{C}):=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}[q,q^{-1}]}K_{0}(\mathscr{C}),\qquad q\longmapsto 1,

where
q
acts on
K_{0}(\mathscr{C})
by grading shift.
We obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

For
G
of finite type
ADE
and
v\leq w
, the category

\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{w,v}
is a monoidal
categorification of
\mathbb{C}[\mathring{\mathcal{B}}_{v,w}]
equipped with Ménard’s
cluster structure. More precisely, there are algebra
isomorphisms

K_{\mathrm{cl}}(\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{w,v})\simeq\mathcal{A}(\Sigma_{w,v})[x_{a}^{-1}\mid a\in J_{\mathrm{fr}}]\simeq\mathbb{C}[\mathring{\mathcal{B}}_{v,w}].

Every cluster monomial with nonnegative frozen exponents
is the class of a real simple object of

\mathscr{C}_{w,v}
. Cluster monomials with arbitrary
integer frozen exponents are represented by real simple
objects of
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{w,v}
.

The ring isomorphisms in Theorem
1.2

are established after specialization at
q=1
and
determinantial localization. At the quantum level, the
construction gives an inclusion of the quantum cluster
algebra with nonnegative frozen exponents into

K_{0}(\mathscr{C}_{w,v})
, after the standard extension of
scalars allowing half-integral grading normalizations.
Equality with the unlocalized quantum Grothendieck ring
is not asserted.

Section
2
fixes the conventions and
recalls the geometric, additive, and PBW constructions.
Section
3
computes the initial
truncated parameters, and Section
4

compares them along Ménard’s mutation sequence.
Finally, Section
5
constructs
the monoidal realization and proves the localization
theorem.

2.
Preliminaries

We fix the root and word conventions, relate the quantum and
classical invariant algebras, and recall the geometric
localization and PBW parametrizations used below.

2.1.
Root and word conventions

Throughout,
G
is a simply connected complex semisimple group of
finite simply-laced type. Fix opposite Borel subgroups
B^{+},B^{-}
,
their common maximal torus
H
, and their unipotent radicals
N,N^{-}
.
Fix a pinning, with simple root subgroups
x_{i}(t)\in N
and

y_{i}(t)\in N^{-}
. Let
I
index the simple roots
\alpha_{i}
and
coroots
\alpha_{i}^{\vee}
, and let

C=(c_{ij})_{i,j\in I}
be the Cartan matrix, where

c_{ij}=\langle\alpha_{i}^{\vee},\alpha_{j}\rangle
.
Write
\Delta_{+}
and
\Delta_{-}=-\Delta_{+}
for the positive and
negative roots, respectively. Set

Q_{+}=\sum_{i\in I}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\alpha_{i},\qquad Q_{-}=-Q_{+},\qquad P_{+}=\sum_{i\in I}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\varpi_{i},

where
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
\varpi_{i}
is the fundamental
weight corresponding to
i
. Let
W
be the Weyl group, with
simple reflections
s_{i}
, length function
\ell
, and longest
element
w_{0}
.

For a reduced expression
w=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{r}}
, where

r=\ell(w)
, write
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r})
and use the
quiver Hecke conventions

(2.1)

w_{\leq k}=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{k}},\qquad\beta_{k}=w_{\leq k-1}(\alpha_{i_{k}}),\qquad w_{\leq 0}=e.

Thus
\{\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{r}\}=\Delta_{+}\cap w\Delta_{-}
.
The reversed tuple
\overline{w}=(i_{r},\ldots,i_{1})
is a reduced
expression of
w^{-1}
and is the displayed word used in the
conventions of Leclerc and Ménard. Accordingly, our pair

(v,w)
corresponds to their pair
(v^{-1},w^{-1})
.
We retain the position labels
1,\ldots,r
from the original
word, so that these labels increase from right to left in

\overline{w}
.

For
k\in[1,r]
, let
k^{+}
and
k^{-}
be the next and previous
positions of the same color
i_{k}
, with boundary values

r+1
and
0
, respectively. Write
k^{t+}
and
k^{t-}

for their
t
-fold iterates, with
k^{0+}=k^{0-}=k
;
iterations remain at the boundary once it is reached.
Also put

k_{\min}=\min\{a\mid i_{a}=i_{k}\},\qquad k_{\max}=\max\{a\mid i_{a}=i_{k}\}.

When several words occur, successors and predecessors are
computed in the word specified by the superscript or the
surrounding notation.

2.2.
Quantum coordinate rings and unipotent minors

Put
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{Z}[q,q^{-1}]
and

\mathfrak{g}=\operatorname{Lie}(G)
,

\mathfrak{n}=\operatorname{Lie}(N)
.
Let
U_{q}^{+}(\mathfrak{g})
be the positive part of the quantum
group
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
. Its restricted graded dual

A_{q}(\mathfrak{n})=\bigoplus_{\beta\in Q_{+}}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Q}(q)}\bigl(U_{q}^{+}(\mathfrak{g})_{\beta},\mathbb{Q}(q)\bigr)

carries the standard quantum coordinate-ring multiplication.
Denote by
A_{q}(\mathfrak{n})_{\mathbb{A}}
the integral form
dual to the divided-power integral form of
U_{q}^{+}(\mathfrak{g})
.
The left and right actions are specified by

(x\cdot f\cdot y)(z)=f(yzx),\qquad x,y,z\in U_{q}^{+}(\mathfrak{g}).

Define

(2.2)

A_{w,v}:=\left\{f\in A_{q}(\mathfrak{n})_{\mathbb{A}}\ \middle|\ \begin{aligned} U_{q}^{+}(\mathfrak{g})_{\beta}\cdot f&=0&&\text{for all }\beta\in(Q_{+}\cap wQ_{+})\setminus\{0\},\\
f\cdot U_{q}^{+}(\mathfrak{g})_{\gamma}&=0&&\text{for all }\gamma\in(Q_{+}\cap vQ_{-})\setminus\{0\}\end{aligned}\right\}.

This is the quantum doubly invariant subalgebra of
Kashiwara–Kim–Oh–Park

[
9
, Section 2.2]
.

For
\lambda\in P_{+}
, let
V_{q}(\lambda)
be the simple
highest-weight module, with normalized extremal vectors

u_{\mu}
for
\mu\in W\lambda
and the standard contravariant
form
(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,)_{\lambda}
.
The
unipotent quantum minor

D(\mu,\nu)
is the
matrix coefficient

(2.3)

D(\mu,\nu)(x)=(u_{\nu},xu_{\mu})_{\lambda},\qquad x\in U_{q}^{+}(\mathfrak{g}),\qquad\mu,\nu\in W\lambda.

The form is normalized by
(u_{\lambda},u_{\lambda})_{\lambda}=1
.
These minors belong to
A_{q}(\mathfrak{n})_{\mathbb{A}}
.
Writing
\Delta_{\nu,\mu}
for the corresponding
classical generalized minor restricted to
N
, we have

(2.4)

\left.D(\mu,\nu)\right|_{q=1}=\Delta_{\nu,\mu}.

In particular, the two weight labels occur in reversed order
in the quantum and classical notation; see

[
9
, Definition 1.5]
.

The upper global basis of
A_{q}(\mathfrak{n})_{\mathbb{A}}

is denoted by
\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{up}}
.
A minor
D(\mu,\nu)
is nonzero precisely when there exist

u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in W
such that

\mu=u^{\prime}\lambda
,
\nu=v^{\prime}\lambda
, and
v^{\prime}\leq u^{\prime}
;
in that case it belongs to
\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{up}}

[
9
, Lemma 1.6]
.
Thus nonvanishing depends on the orbit weights, and

v\leq u
is sufficient for
D(u\lambda,v\lambda)\neq 0
.
We also have
D(\mu,\mu)=1
.

2.3.
Conventions and the geometric localization

Choose representatives
\dot{u}\in N_{G}(H)
of Weyl group
elements
u\in W
. For
v\leq w
, set

\mathring{\mathcal{B}}_{v,w}:=(B^{-}\dot{v}B^{+}/B^{+})\cap(B^{+}\dot{w}B^{+}/B^{+}).

Define

N(w):=N\cap\dot{w}N^{-}\dot{w}^{-1},\qquad N^{\prime}(w):=N\cap\dot{w}N\dot{w}^{-1},

and specify the classical invariant ring by

(2.5)

\mathcal{I}_{w,v}:=\{f\in\mathbb{C}[N]\mid f(anb)=f(n)\text{ for all }a\in N(v),\ b\in N^{\prime}(w),\ n\in N\}.

With the actions used in
(
2.2
), specialization gives

\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{A}}A_{w,v}\simeq\mathcal{I}_{w,v},\qquad q\longmapsto 1;

see
[
9
, Remark 2.19 and Theorem 2.20]
.

Let
\Lambda
be the preprojective algebra of the Dynkin diagram,

S_{i}
its simple module at
i
, and
Q_{i}
the injective envelope
of
S_{i}
. For a sequence
(j_{1},\ldots,j_{t})
, the iterated socle

\operatorname{soc}_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{t})}(M)
is constructed by successively
taking the
S_{j_{a}}
-isotypical socle in the quotient by the
submodule already obtained. The functor
\mathcal{E}_{i}

removes the
S_{i}
-isotypical head, while

\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\dagger}
takes the quotient by the

S_{i}
-isotypical socle. Their braid relations give well-defined
functors

\mathcal{E}_{u}=\mathcal{E}_{a_{1}}\cdots\mathcal{E}_{a_{t}},\qquad\mathcal{E}_{u}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{E}_{a_{1}}^{\dagger}\cdots\mathcal{E}_{a_{t}}^{\dagger}

for a reduced expression
u=s_{a_{1}}\cdots s_{a_{t}}
;
see
[
13
, Section 3.2]
.

Leclerc’s categories are

\mathcal{C}_{b}=\operatorname{Fac}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{b^{-1}w_{0}}\left(\bigoplus_{i\in I}Q_{i}\right)\right),\qquad\mathcal{C}^{a}=\operatorname{Sub}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{a^{-1}}^{\dagger}\left(\bigoplus_{i\in I}Q_{i}\right)\right),

where
\operatorname{Fac}
and
\operatorname{Sub}
denote factor modules and submodules
of finite direct sums, respectively. Put

\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{Lec}}_{a,b}=\mathcal{C}_{b}\cap\mathcal{C}^{a}
.
In our conventions, the relevant additive category is

\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{add}}_{w,v}:=\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{Lec}}_{v^{-1},w^{-1}}.

This is a Frobenius category with a cluster structure

[
13
, Corollary 3.11]
.
The rightmost reduced subexpression for
v^{-1}
in

(i_{r},\ldots,i_{1})
corresponds to the leftmost reduced
subexpression for
v
in
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r})
, with the same
position labels
p_{1}<\cdots<p_{\ell(v)}
.

Let
g\mapsto g^{\mathsf{T}}
be the transpose
anti-automorphism determined by

x_{i}(t)^{\mathsf{T}}=y_{i}(t),\qquad y_{i}(t)^{\mathsf{T}}=x_{i}(t),\qquad h^{\mathsf{T}}=h\quad(h\in H).

It induces an isomorphism

(2.6)

\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{Lec}}_{v^{-1},w^{-1}}\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\mathring{\mathcal{B}}_{v,w},\qquad B^{-}g\longmapsto g^{\mathsf{T}}B^{+}.

Indeed, transpose exchanges
B^{-}
and
B^{+}
, reverses
products, and sends a representative of
u
to a
representative of
u^{-1}
. Moreover,

N_{\mathrm{Lec}}(v^{-1})=N(v)
and

N^{\prime}_{\mathrm{Lec}}(w^{-1})=N^{\prime}(w)
.
Hence Leclerc’s invariant ring for
(v^{-1},w^{-1})

is precisely
\mathcal{I}_{w,v}
, with no additional
involution on
\mathbb{C}[N]
.

Let
\varphi
be the cluster character for
preprojective-algebra modules. For

V_{k}=\operatorname{soc}_{(i_{k},\ldots,i_{1})}(Q_{i_{k}})\in\mathcal{C}_{w^{-1}},

its normalization is

\varphi(V_{k})=\Delta_{\varpi_{i_{k}},w_{\leq k}\varpi_{i_{k}}}=\left.D(w_{\leq k}\varpi_{i_{k}},\varpi_{i_{k}})\right|_{q=1}.

Thus the common position labels identify the additive
initial cluster variables with the classical
specializations of the quantum unipotent initial
variables. Applying the same mutation sequence preserves
this identification.

We next explain why the frozen variables are independent
of the categorical seed. Let
T
be a basic cluster-tilting
object of
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{add}}_{w,v}
, and let
P

be projective-injective in this category. Since

\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{\Lambda}(T,P)=0
, the cluster-tilting
property implies
P\in\operatorname{add}(T)
.
Here
\operatorname{add}(T)
consists of direct summands
of finite direct sums of copies of
T
.
Consequently, every indecomposable projective-injective
occurs, up to isomorphism, exactly once in every basic
cluster-tilting object. Categorical mutation replaces only
non-projective-injective summands, so these common
summands determine the frozen variables in every
categorical seed of
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{add}}_{w,v}
.

Choose representatives
P_{a}
,
a\in J_{\mathrm{fr}}
, of
these indecomposable projective-injectives, and put

x_{a}=\varphi(P_{a})\in\mathcal{I}_{w,v}
.
The inclusion follows from

[
13
, Proposition 4.2]
.
The following proposition relates this common frozen set
to the minors defining the geometric localization.

Proposition 2.1
.

For
i\in I
, set

P_{i}^{\mathrm{det}}:=\mathcal{E}_{v}^{\dagger}\mathcal{E}_{ww_{0}}(Q_{i}),\qquad d_{i}:=\Delta_{v\varpi_{i},w\varpi_{i}}=\left.D(w\varpi_{i},v\varpi_{i})\right|_{q=1}.

Then

\operatorname{add}(\bigoplus_{i\in I}P_{i}^{\mathrm{det}})

is the subcategory of projective-injective objects of

\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{add}}_{w,v}
, and

\varphi(P_{i}^{\mathrm{det}})=d_{i}
.
Writing

P_{i}^{\mathrm{det}}\simeq\bigoplus_{a\in J_{\mathrm{fr}}}P_{a}^{\oplus m_{ia}},\qquad m_{ia}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},

we obtain

d_{i}=\prod_{a\in J_{\mathrm{fr}}}x_{a}^{m_{ia}}.

Every
x_{a}
occurs with positive exponent in at least one
of these products. In particular,

\mathcal{I}_{w,v}[d_{i}^{-1}\mid i\in I]=\mathcal{I}_{w,v}[x_{a}^{-1}\mid a\in J_{\mathrm{fr}}]\simeq\mathbb{C}[\mathring{\mathcal{B}}_{v,w}].

Proof.

By
[
13
, Lemma 3.15 and Proposition 4.1]
,
applied to
(v^{-1},w^{-1})
, the objects

\mathcal{E}_{v}^{\dagger}\mathcal{E}_{ww_{0}}(Q_{i})
generate
the projective-injective subcategory, and their cluster
characters are
\Delta_{v\varpi_{i},w\varpi_{i}}
.
Their indecomposable summands are therefore precisely
the objects
P_{a}
that occur as frozen summands in every
categorical seed.

The stated factorizations now follow from

\varphi(M\oplus M^{\prime})=\varphi(M)\varphi(M^{\prime})
.
Since the
P_{i}^{\mathrm{det}}
generate the
projective-injective subcategory, for every
a
there is
some
i
with
m_{ia}>0
. For such an
i
,

x_{a}^{-1}=d_{i}^{-1}x_{a}^{m_{ia}-1}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in J_{\mathrm{fr}}\\
b\neq a\end{subarray}}x_{b}^{m_{ib}}.

Hence inverting the
d_{i}
makes every
x_{a}
invertible.
Conversely, inverting all
x_{a}
makes every
d_{i}

invertible, proving equality of the two localizations.

Finally, the geometric localization theorem

[
13
, Theorem 2.12]
, together with
(
2.6
), identifies

\mathcal{I}_{w,v}[d_{i}^{-1}\mid i\in I]

with
\mathbb{C}[\mathring{\mathcal{B}}_{v,w}]
.
∎

Thus localization at the determinantial minors agrees
with localization at the common frozen variables of the
categorical seeds. This statement concerns the invariant
ring and its localization; equality between

\mathcal{I}_{w,v}
and the cluster algebra before
inversion of frozen variables is not asserted.

2.4.
Interval minors and PBW parameters

We now describe the minors attached to subintervals of

\mathbf{i}
and the PBW parameters used to compare additive
and monoidal mutations. All quantum minors use the
normalization of (
2.3
).

An
i
-box
in
\mathbf{i}
is an interval
[a,b]
with

1\leq a\leq b\leq r
and
i_{a}=i_{b}=i
. Set

(2.7)

D^{\mathbf{i}}[a,b]:=D(w_{\leq b}\varpi_{i},w_{\leq a-1}\varpi_{i}).

Since all letters strictly between
a^{-}
and

have
color different from

, their simple reflections fix

. Hence

w_{\leq a-1}\varpi_{i}=w_{\leq a^{-}}\varpi_{i}
,
where
w_{\leq 0}=e
if
a^{-}=0
.
For an empty interval, we set
D^{\mathbf{i}}[a,b]=1
.
In particular, the initial minors are

D_{k}:=D(w_{\leq k}\varpi_{i_{k}},\varpi_{i_{k}})=D^{\mathbf{i}}[k_{\min},k],\qquad 1\leq k\leq r.

A singleton interval
D^{\mathbf{i}}[a,a]
is the dual root
vector associated with
\beta_{a}
in the normalization
used below; it is not the empty-interval minor.

Put
L=\ell(w_{0})
and choose a completion

\widehat{\mathbf{i}}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{L})
of
\mathbf{i}
to a reduced
expression of
w_{0}
. Extend the definition of
\beta_{t}

in (

) to
1\leq t\leq L
.
We suppress the hat in the PBW notation associated with
this fixed completion. Let
E_{\mathbf{i}}^{*}(m\beta_{t})
denote
the normalized dual divided-power root vector, with

E_{\mathbf{i}}^{*}(0)=1
and

E_{\mathbf{i}}^{*}(\beta_{t})=D^{\widehat{\mathbf{i}}}[t,t]
.

We use the decreasing-root PBW convention, corresponding
to
e=-1
in
[
12
, Section 4.3]
.
In particular, both the root vectors and the product
order are taken in this convention.
For
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{L})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{L}
, write

P_{\mathbf{i}}(\mathbf{a})=q^{\kappa_{\mathbf{i}}(\mathbf{a})}E_{\mathbf{i}}^{*}(a_{L}\beta_{L})\cdots E_{\mathbf{i}}^{*}(a_{1}\beta_{1}).

Here
\kappa_{\mathbf{i}}(\mathbf{a})
is the scalar
normalization exponent, chosen so that the transition
from the upper global basis to this dual PBW basis has
diagonal coefficients equal to
1
.
Let
G_{\mathbf{i}}^{\mathrm{up}}(\mathbf{a})
be the upper
global basis element with Lusztig parameter
\mathbf{a}
.

We order
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{L}
lexicographically in increasing
coordinate order:
\mathbf{b}<_{\mathrm{lex}}\mathbf{a}

if, at the first coordinate where they differ, the
entry of
\mathbf{b}
is smaller.
With the preceding conventions, PBW triangularity gives

(2.8)

G_{\mathbf{i}}^{\mathrm{up}}(\mathbf{a})=P_{\mathbf{i}}(\mathbf{a})+\sum_{\mathbf{b}<_{\mathrm{lex}}\mathbf{a}}c_{\mathbf{b},\mathbf{a}}(q)P_{\mathbf{i}}(\mathbf{b}).

The sum is finite, and the coefficients belong to

\mathbb{A}
. The triangularity and multiplication
statements of

[
12
, Theorem 4.26 and Proposition 4.28]

apply in this decreasing-root convention by transport
through the standard anti-involution.

For a nonzero element with PBW expansion

f=\sum_{\mathbf{a}}f_{\mathbf{a}}(q)P_{\mathbf{i}}(\mathbf{a})
,
define its
leading PBW parameter
by

\mathbf{a}^{\mathbf{i}}(f):=\max_{\mathrm{lex}}\{\mathbf{a}\mid f_{\mathbf{a}}(q)\neq 0\}.

For an upper global basis element, this is its Lusztig
parameter by (
2.8
).
The PBW multiplication formula yields

(2.9)

\mathbf{a}^{\mathbf{i}}(fg)=\mathbf{a}^{\mathbf{i}}(f)+\mathbf{a}^{\mathbf{i}}(g)\qquad(f,g\neq 0).

Indeed, PBW straightening is triangular for an order
compatible with addition, and the product of the leading
terms has a nonzero leading coefficient.
When quantum seed normalizations require half-integral
powers of
q
, we extend scalars to
\mathbb{Q}(q^{1/2})
.
The same definitions and formulas apply, and multiplying
an element by a power of
q^{1/2}
does not change its
leading parameter. We use the same notation

\mathbf{a}^{\mathbf{j}}
for any other reduced expression

\mathbf{j}
of
w_{0}
.

For the interval minor in (
2.7
),

[
9
, Proposition 4.6 and Corollary 4.7]

give

(2.10)

a_{t}^{\mathbf{i}}(D^{\mathbf{i}}[a,b])=\begin{cases}1,&a\leq t\leq b\text{ and }i_{t}=i_{a},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad 1\leq t\leq L.

In particular, all coordinates beyond
r
vanish.
Thus the parameter may be regarded as a vector in

\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r}
, independent of the chosen completion, and then
extended by zero to
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{L}
. For
a=b
, it is the standard
unit vector at position
a
; for an empty interval, it
is the zero vector.

We will use the following consequence of
(
2.9
) for quantum mutations.

Lemma 2.2

(Leading parameters in an exchange relation)
.

Let
(X_{i})_{i\in J}
be the variables of a quantum seed
obtained by mutations from a quantum unipotent seed,
with frozen variables not inverted, and let

\widetilde{B}=(b_{ik})
be its extended exchange matrix.
Fix a reduced expression
\mathbf{j}
of
w_{0}
.
If
k
is mutable and
X_{k}^{\prime}
is the variable obtained by
mutation at
k
, then

(2.11)

\mathbf{a}^{\mathbf{j}}(X_{k})+\mathbf{a}^{\mathbf{j}}(X_{k}^{\prime})=\max_{\mathrm{lex}}\left\{\sum_{i\in J}[b_{ik}]_{+}\mathbf{a}^{\mathbf{j}}(X_{i}),\quad\sum_{i\in J}[-b_{ik}]_{+}\mathbf{a}^{\mathbf{j}}(X_{i})\right\},

where
[m]_{+}=\max\{m,0\}
.
The maximum is taken in the specified lexicographic
order, not coordinatewise.

Proof.

By the monoidal categorification of quantum unipotent
coordinate rings
[
8
]
, the quantum
cluster variables and normalized cluster monomials
belong to
\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{up}}
up to the prescribed
powers of
q^{1/2}
. Their leading PBW coefficients are
therefore powers of
q^{1/2}
with positive sign.

Write the exchange relation as

X_{k}X_{k}^{\prime}=q^{s_{+}}M_{+}+q^{s_{-}}M_{-},\qquad s_{+},s_{-}\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z},

where
M_{+}
and
M_{-}
are the normalized cluster
monomials with exponent vectors

([b_{ik}]_{+})_{i\in J}
and

([-b_{ik}]_{+})_{i\in J}
, respectively.
By (
2.9
), their leading
parameters are the two sums on the right-hand side
of (
2.11
).

If these parameters differ, the larger one is the
leading parameter of the exchange sum. If they agree,
the leading coefficients add without cancellation,
since each is a Laurent monomial in
q^{1/2}
with
coefficient
1
.
Thus the leading parameter of the right-hand side
is their lexicographic maximum.
Applying (
2.9
) to
X_{k}X_{k}^{\prime}

proves the formula.
∎

2.5.
Seeds and frozen variables

An ice seed has a vertex set
J=J_{\mathrm{ex}}\sqcup J_{\mathrm{fr}}
and an
extended exchange matrix
B=(b_{ij})_{i\in J,j\in J_{\mathrm{ex}}}
.
For a drawn quiver we use

b_{ij}=\#\{i\to j\}-\#\{j\to i\}
.
Throughout,
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
denotes the cluster algebra with

noninvertible
frozen variables. We distinguish it from

\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{loc}}(\Sigma):=\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)[x_{a}^{-1}\mid a\in J_{\mathrm{fr}}].

The same distinction is made for quantum seeds. Classical cluster
algebras are taken over
\mathbb{C}
; quantum ones use the standard

\mathbb{Z}[q^{\pm 1/2}]
coefficient extension when needed.

The ambient unipotent seed
\mathcal{S}
associated with
\mathbf{i}
has variables

D_{1},\ldots,D_{r}
and frozen positions

J_{\mathrm{fr}}^{\mathrm{amb}}=\{k\in[1,r]\mid k^{+}=r+1\}.

Its exchange matrix and compatible commutation matrix are those
of the unipotent seed in

[
5
,
8
]
. One may draw its quiver
with horizontal arrows
k\to k^{-}
and ordinary arrows

s\to t
for

s<t<s^{+}\leq t^{+},\qquad c_{i_{s},i_{t}}=-1,

using the corresponding sign convention for
B
.
This is the opposite of Ménard’s endomorphism quiver. A
simultaneous reversal of all arrows preserves the mutation labels
and the unordered pair of exchange monomials.
Arrows between two frozen vertices do not enter the extended
exchange matrix.

Under
q=1
, the initial quantum minor
D_{k}
becomes

\Delta_{\varpi_{i_{k}},w_{\leq k}\varpi_{i_{k}}}|_{N}
.
The cluster algebra associated with this classical seed is

\mathbb{C}[N(w)]
.
On the additive side, the same function is the GLS cluster
character of the module associated with the reversed tuple

\overline{w}
; this identification will be used in
Section
4
.

Lemma 2.3

(Freezing and deletion)
.

Let
E
be a set of vertices to be deleted from a seed. Freeze
every retained mutable vertex that is joined to a vertex of
E
,
and retain every original frozen vertex not in
E
.
For any remaining mutable vertex
k
, the entries
b_{ek}
vanish
for all
e\in E
. This vanishing is preserved by every sequence of
mutations in the remaining mutable directions. Such mutations
therefore agree with the corresponding ambient mutations after
deletion of
E
.

Proof.

The initial vanishing is exactly the freezing rule. If
k
is
mutated and
j
is another retained mutable vertex, the matrix
mutation formula changes
b_{ej}
by an expression containing

b_{ek}=0
. It also changes
b_{ek}
to its negative, which is still
zero. Induction proves the assertion. The exchange relations then
involve only retained variables at every step.
∎

3.
Lusztig parameters and the Richardson seed

3.1.
Lusztig parameters of determinantial minors

Throughout this section, we work in finite simply-laced type and retain the word convention in
(
2.1
). For compatibility with the reversed
displayed words, we also write

D^{\overline{w}}\{0,k]:=D_{k},\qquad\mathbf{a}^{\overline{w}}:=\mathbf{a}^{\mathbf{i}}.

Thus the displayed word is reversed, but the coordinates are
always read in increasing position order.

Put
n_{0}:=\ell(w_{0})
. We use stable MV polytopes, that is,
MV polytopes modulo translation, in the root realization
compatible with our PBW convention. Each translation class is
normalized by placing its lowest vertex at
0
.
A reduced word of
w_{0}
determines a path whose successive
edge directions are the corresponding ordered positive roots.
The edge lengths, with zero lengths allowed, form its Lusztig
datum.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
7
, Theorem 7.2]
)
.

The crystal labels of the upper global basis are parametrized
by stable MV polytopes. For every reduced word of
w_{0}
, the
Lusztig parameters of a basis element are the edge lengths
along the corresponding path in its normalized MV polytope.

Choose a completion

\dot{w}=(i_{n_{0}},\ldots,i_{r+1},i_{r},\ldots,i_{1}),\qquad s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{n_{0}}}=w_{0}.

For a fixed
1\leq k\leq r
, put

u_{k}:=s_{i_{n_{0}}}\cdots s_{i_{k+1}}=w_{0}w_{\leq k}.

Let
\dot{x}=(j_{n_{0}},\ldots,j_{1})
be another reduced word of
w_{0}
,
again displayed in reverse order. We write

\mathbf{a}^{\dot{x}}:=\mathbf{a}^{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{n_{0}})}
;
thus coordinates are indexed by the forward order.
Write

1\leq q_{1}<\cdots<q_{{n_{0}}-k}\leq{n_{0}}

for the positions of the leftmost reduced subexpression of
u_{k}

in this displayed word, so that

u_{k}=s_{j_{q_{{n_{0}}-k}}}\cdots s_{j_{q_{1}}}.

Denote its complementary positions by

\{r_{1}<\cdots<r_{k}\}=\{1,\ldots,{n_{0}}\}\setminus\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{{n_{0}}-k}\}.

If
k={n_{0}}
, the
q
-sequence is empty and the complementary sequence
is
(1,\ldots,{n_{0}})
.

Definition 3.2
.

For the fixed minor index
k
, define

\xi_{t}^{\dot{x},k}
,
0\leq t\leq{n_{0}}
, by

(3.1)

\xi_{0}^{\dot{x},k}:=\varpi_{i_{k}},\qquad\xi_{t}^{\dot{x},k}:=\begin{cases}s_{\beta_{t}^{\dot{x}}}\xi_{t-1}^{\dot{x},k},&t\in\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}\},\\
\xi_{t-1}^{\dot{x},k},&t\in\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{{n_{0}}-k}\},\end{cases}

where

\beta_{t}^{\dot{x}}=s_{j_{1}}\cdots s_{j_{t-1}}(\alpha_{j_{t}})
.

The following is the extremal-minor form of the MV-polytope
calculation in
[
16
, §4.1]
; the corresponding
preprojective-module calculation and its identification with
Lusztig data are described in

[
1
, Example 5.14, Proposition 5.24 and Remark 5.25(ii)]
. See also

[
15
, Definition 5.20 and Proposition 5.21]

for the reverse-word notation used here.

Proposition 3.3
.

For
1\leq t\leq{n_{0}}
, define
n_{t}^{\dot{x},k}
by

\xi_{t-1}^{\dot{x},k}-\xi_{t}^{\dot{x},k}=n_{t}^{\dot{x},k}\beta_{t}^{\dot{x}}.

Then
n_{t}^{\dot{x},k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and

a_{t}^{\dot{x}}(D^{\overline{w}}\{0,k])=n_{t}^{\dot{x},k}.

Proof.

Put
N=n_{0}
and
\lambda=\varpi_{i_{k}}
, and write

D_{k}:=D^{\overline{w}}\{0,k]=D(w_{\leq k}\lambda,\lambda).

All positions below are numbered in the forward order

j_{1},\ldots,j_{N}
, although
\dot{x}
is displayed in reverse
order.

Step 1: a formula for the recursively defined weights.

Let
\mathcal{Q}=\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{N-k}\}
and put

h_{t}:=s_{j_{1}}\cdots s_{j_{t}},\qquad z_{t}:=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\in\mathcal{Q}\\
q\leq t\end{subarray}}s_{j_{q}},\qquad h_{0}=z_{0}=e,

where the factors in
z_{t}
are taken in increasing order of
q
.
Then
z_{N}=u_{k}^{-1}
.
The leftmost reduced subexpression in the reverse display
is the rightmost reduced subexpression in the forward word.
Consequently, its partial products are obtained by the
backward greedy rule

(3.2)

z_{t-1}=\begin{cases}z_{t}s_{j_{t}},&\ell(z_{t}s_{j_{t}})<\ell(z_{t}),\\
z_{t},&\ell(z_{t}s_{j_{t}})>\ell(z_{t}).\end{cases}

The first case occurs precisely when
t\in\mathcal{Q}
.

We claim that

(3.3)

\xi_{t}^{\dot{x},k}=h_{t}z_{t}^{-1}\lambda\qquad(0\leq t\leq N).

This is clear for
t=0
. If
t\in\mathcal{Q}
, then

z_{t}=z_{t-1}s_{j_{t}}
, and hence

h_{t}z_{t}^{-1}=h_{t-1}s_{j_{t}}s_{j_{t}}z_{t-1}^{-1}=h_{t-1}z_{t-1}^{-1}.

If
t\notin\mathcal{Q}
, then
z_{t}=z_{t-1}
and

h_{t}z_{t}^{-1}=\bigl(h_{t-1}s_{j_{t}}h_{t-1}^{-1}\bigr)h_{t-1}z_{t-1}^{-1}=s_{\beta_{t}^{\dot{x}}}h_{t-1}z_{t-1}^{-1}.

These are exactly the two cases of (
3.1
), proving
(
3.3
).

Step 2: nonnegativity.

The reflection formula and
(
3.3
) give

(3.4)

n_{t}^{\dot{x},k}=\begin{cases}\langle z_{t-1}\alpha_{j_{t}}^{\vee},\lambda\rangle,&t\notin\mathcal{Q},\\
0,&t\in\mathcal{Q}.\end{cases}

Indeed, in the first case,

\langle(\beta_{t}^{\dot{x}})^{\vee},\xi_{t-1}^{\dot{x},k}\rangle=\langle h_{t-1}\alpha_{j_{t}}^{\vee},h_{t-1}z_{t-1}^{-1}\lambda\rangle=\langle z_{t-1}\alpha_{j_{t}}^{\vee},\lambda\rangle.

When
t\notin\mathcal{Q}
, the greedy rule says that

z_{t-1}=z_{t}
and
\ell(z_{t}s_{j_{t}})>\ell(z_{t})
.
Thus
z_{t-1}\alpha_{j_{t}}
is a positive root.
Since
\lambda
is dominant integral,
(
3.4
) proves

n_{t}^{\dot{x},k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

Step 3: compatibility with braid moves.

Consider a three-term braid move in the forward word,
replacing the letters in positions
m+1,m+2,m+3
by

(i,j,i)\longleftrightarrow(j,i,j),\qquad c_{ij}=c_{ji}=-1.

The backward greedy algorithm processes the same suffix
in both words, so its residual
z_{m+3}
is the same.
Take the right parabolic decomposition

z_{m+3}=zy,\qquad y\in W_{\{i,j\}},\qquad z\alpha_{i},z\alpha_{j}\in\Delta_{+}.

Here
z
is the minimal representative of its right

W_{\{i,j\}}
-coset, so

\ell(zy^{\prime})=\ell(z)+\ell(y^{\prime})
for every

y^{\prime}\in W_{\{i,j\}}
.
The greedy decisions inside the braid interval therefore
depend only on
y
. Processing either reduced expression
of the longest element of
W_{\{i,j\}}
removes
y
and
leaves
z
. In particular,
z_{m}=z
for both words, and
the selected positions outside the interval agree.

Set

A:=\langle z\alpha_{i}^{\vee},\lambda\rangle,\qquad B:=\langle z\alpha_{j}^{\vee},\lambda\rangle.

Both numbers are nonnegative. Write
1
for a selected
position in
\mathcal{Q}
and
0
for a complementary
position. The six possible elements
y\in W_{\{i,j\}}

give the following exhaustive table. The coefficient
triples are computed from
(
3.4
), using

s_{i}\alpha_{j}=\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}
and

s_{j}\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}
:

\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c}\text{selection in }iji&\text{selection in }jij&\text{coefficients for }iji&\text{coefficients for }jij\\
\hline\cr 000&000&(A,B,A)&(B,A,B)\\
001&010&(A,B,0)&(B,0,A+B)\\
010&001&(A,0,A+B)&(B,A,0)\\
011&110&(A,0,0)&(0,0,A)\\
110&011&(0,0,B)&(B,0,0)\\
111&111&(0,0,0)&(0,0,0)\end{array}

For example, the selection
010
gives successive
selected partial products

z,z,zs_{j},zs_{j}
, and hence the coefficients

(A,0,A+B)
.

Since
A,B\geq 0
, every row satisfies

(3.5)

(a,b,c)\longmapsto(b+c-p,p,a+b-p),\qquad p=\min\{a,c\}.

For
t\leq m
and
t\geq m+3
, both
h_{t}
and
z_{t}

agree for the two words. Hence
(
3.3
) shows that the weights
at these positions agree, and all coefficients outside the
braid interval are unchanged.

For a commuting move, the same argument applies to the
rank-two parabolic subgroup of type
A_{1}\times A_{1}
.
The two roots are orthogonal, so the affected
coefficients are interchanged and all other coefficients
remain unchanged.

Thus the vectors
(n_{t}^{\dot{x},k})_{t=1}^{N}
transform
under every braid move by the usual Lusztig transition
maps; see

[
1
, Propositions 5.26 and 5.27]
.

Step 4: identification with the minor parameters.

For the original completion
\dot{w}
, the chosen leftmost
subexpression of
u_{k}
occupies positions
k+1,\ldots,N
.
Consequently,

\xi_{t}^{\dot{w},k}=\begin{cases}w_{\leq t}\lambda,&0\leq t\leq k,\\
w_{\leq k}\lambda,&k\leq t\leq N.\end{cases}

It follows that

n_{t}^{\dot{w},k}=\begin{cases}\langle\alpha_{i_{t}}^{\vee},\lambda\rangle=\delta_{i_{t},i_{k}},&t\leq k,\\
0,&t>k.\end{cases}

By (
2.10
), these are precisely
the Lusztig parameters of
D_{k}
in the word
\dot{w}
.

Any two reduced expressions of
w_{0}
are connected by
commuting and three-term braid moves.
The recursion coefficients and the Lusztig parameters
of the fixed minor
D_{k}
agree for
\dot{w}
and undergo
the same transition at each such move.
They therefore agree for every
\dot{x}
, proving

a_{t}^{\dot{x}}(D^{\overline{w}}\{0,k])=n_{t}^{\dot{x},k}\qquad(1\leq t\leq N).

∎

The corresponding normalized MV path has vertices

\mu_{t}=\varpi_{i_{k}}-\xi_{t}^{\dot{x},k},\qquad\mu_{t}-\mu_{t-1}=n_{t}^{\dot{x},k}\beta_{t}^{\dot{x}}.

In particular,
\mu_{0}=0
and

\mu_{n_{0}}=\varpi_{i_{k}}-w_{\leq k}\varpi_{i_{k}}
.
This explains the sign in the weight recursion.

3.3.
The initial coordinates for the completed word

Proposition 3.6
.

For

and

a_{m}^{\dot{v}}(D_{k})=a_{p_{m}}^{\dot{w}}(D_{k})=\begin{cases}1,&p_{m}\leq k\text{ and }i_{p_{m}}=i_{k},\\

\mathbf{a}^{\overline{v}}(D_{k})

\{1\leq m\leq d\mid f_{\min}(k)\leq m\leq f(k),\ i_{p_{m}}=i_{k}\}.

Proof.

Theorem 4.3

The initial equality follows from

and

5.
Monoidal realization and localization

5.1.
Quiver Hecke categories and support

Let
R=\bigoplus_{\nu\in Q_{+}}R(\nu)
be a symmetric
quiver Hecke algebra of the same finite simply-laced type
over
\mathbb{C}
, with the standard geometric choice of
parameters. For example, for an orientation of the Dynkin
diagram one may take

Q_{ij}(u,v)=(v-u)^{m_{ij}}(u-v)^{m_{ji}}\qquad(i\neq j),

where
m_{ij}
is the number of arrows from
i
to
j
.
We work with finite-dimensional graded modules.
Write
qM
for grading shift,
M^{*}
for graded duality,
and
M\circ N
for convolution.

A simple module
M
is
real
if
M\circ M
is simple.
Two simple modules
strongly commute
if their
convolution is simple. We use self-dual representatives
of simple modules. For a pairwise strongly commuting
family of real self-dual modules,
\bigodot
denotes the
self-dual grading normalization of their convolution.

Recall that
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{Z}[q,q^{-1}]
, and write

K_{\mathrm{cl}}(\mathscr{C}):=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{A}}K_{0}(\mathscr{C}),\qquad q\longmapsto 1.

For classical cluster algebras, monoidal categorification
requires an isomorphism with this specialized Grothendieck
ring under which cluster monomials are represented by
real simple objects. We will also realize every successive
seed mutation by exchange short exact sequences.

For a nonzero
R(\nu)
-module
M
, define

\displaystyle\mathrm{W}(M)

\displaystyle:=\{\gamma\in Q_{+}\mid e(\gamma,\nu-\gamma)M\neq 0\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{W}^{*}(M)

\displaystyle:=\{\gamma\in Q_{+}\mid e(\nu-\gamma,\gamma)M\neq 0\}.

Here
e(\gamma,\delta)
is the sum of the word idempotents
whose first block has root degree
\gamma
and whose
second block has root degree
\delta
; it is zero unless

\gamma,\delta\in Q_{+}
and
\gamma+\delta=\nu
.
For modules with several root degrees, take the union of
the supports of their homogeneous summands.
Set
\mathrm{W}(0)=\mathrm{W}^{*}(0)=\varnothing
.
The positive root degree is
\nu
, while the usual
representation-theoretic weight is
-\nu
.

Define the full subcategories of
R\text{-gmod}
by

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{w}

\displaystyle:=\{M\mid\mathrm{W}(M)\subset\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}(\Delta_{+}\cap w\Delta_{-})\},

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{*,v}

\displaystyle:=\{M\mid\mathrm{W}^{*}(M)\subset\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}(\Delta_{+}\cap v\Delta_{+})\},

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{w,v}

\displaystyle:=\mathscr{C}_{w}\cap\mathscr{C}_{*,v}.

These categories are closed under subquotients,
extensions, convolution, and grading shifts

[
9
, Proposition 2.16]
.
The algebra
A_{w,v}
retains the definition in
(
2.2
).

Proposition 5.1
.

Quiver Hecke categorification identifies

K_{0}(R\text{-gmod})
with

A_{q}(\mathfrak{n})_{\mathbb{A}}
, sending self-dual simple
modules to the upper global basis.
It restricts to a based identification

K_{0}(\mathscr{C}_{w,v})\simeq A_{w,v}

and a classical isomorphism

\Phi_{\mathrm{cl}}:K_{\mathrm{cl}}(\mathscr{C}_{w,v})\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathcal{I}_{w,v}.

The specialized classes of self-dual simple modules form
a
\mathbb{C}
-basis of
K_{\mathrm{cl}}(\mathscr{C}_{w,v})
.

Proof.

The ambient identification and its assertion about bases
are
[
8
, Theorems 2.1.2 and 2.1.4]
.
Its restriction is

[
9
, Theorem 2.20]
, and the
classical invariant algebra is identified in

[
9
, Remarks 2.19 and 2.21(ii)]
,
with the actions fixed in
(
2.2
).
Finally, the self-dual simple classes form an

\mathbb{A}
-basis before specialization, and hence a

\mathbb{C}
-basis after specialization.
∎

Write
\mathbf{j}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{n_{0}})
for the forward reading
of the fixed completion
\dot{v}
.
The cuspidal multiplicities of a simple module
L
for
this convex order equal the Lusztig parameters of its
upper global basis element

[
17
]
.
We denote this common vector by

\mathbf{a}^{\dot{v}}(L)=\mathbf{a}^{\mathbf{j}}(L)
and use

\mathbf{a}_{v}(L)
for its truncation.

Lemma 5.2
.

For a simple module
L\in\mathscr{C}_{w}
,

L\in\mathscr{C}_{w,v}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathbf{a}_{v}(L)=0.

For

, the coordinate condition is empty.

Proof.

The roots attached to the first
d
letters of
\mathbf{j}

are precisely
\Delta_{+}\cap v\Delta_{-}
.
By
[
9
, Proposition 2.18]
,

L\in\mathscr{C}_{*,v}
if and only if all its nonzero
cuspidal multiplicities occur after these positions.
The assertion follows.
∎

Lemma 5.3

(Closure under convolution factors)
.

Let
M,N
be nonzero finite-dimensional graded modules.
If
M\circ N\in\mathscr{C}_{w,v}
, then

M,N\in\mathscr{C}_{w,v}
.

Proof.

By
[

, Lemma 2.2]
,

\mathrm{W}(M\circ N)=\mathrm{W}(M)+\mathrm{W}(N),\qquad\mathrm{W}^{*}(M\circ N)=\mathrm{W}^{*}(M)+\mathrm{W}^{*}(N).

Each factor support contains

, so it is contained in
the corresponding convolution support.
The defining cone conditions therefore hold for both factors.
∎

Together with the restricted exchange and commutation
matrices, these modules define a monoidal seed

\mathscr{T}_{w,v}
lifting
\Sigma^{q}_{w,v}
.

We next verify that freezing and deletion remain
compatible with all subsequent retained mutations.

Lemma 5.4
.

Every mutation sequence in the mutable directions

J_{\mathrm{ex}}:=J\setminus J_{\mathrm{fr}}
of

\Sigma^{q}_{w,v}
is the restriction of the same mutation
sequence in the full ambient quantum seed.
At every step, the deleted rows of the ambient exchange
matrix vanish in all columns indexed by
J_{\mathrm{ex}}
.

Proof.

The freezing rule gives
b_{ek}=0
for
e\in E
and

k\in J_{\mathrm{ex}}
.
After mutation at
\ell\in J_{\mathrm{ex}}
,

b^{\prime}_{e\ell}=-b_{e\ell}=0
, and for
k\neq\ell
,

b^{\prime}_{ek}=b_{ek}+[b_{e\ell}]_{+}[b_{\ell k}]_{+}-[-b_{e\ell}]_{+}[-b_{\ell k}]_{+}=0.

This vanishing therefore persists.
The exchange monomials involve only retained variables,
and matrix mutation commutes with restriction.
The quantum toric frames consequently restrict as well.
Compatibility with the restricted commutation matrix
follows by omitting the zero deleted-row contributions
from the ambient compatibility identity.
∎

Proposition 5.5
.

Every mutation sequence in the mutable directions of

\mathscr{T}_{w,v}
is realized by monoidal seeds in

\mathscr{C}_{w,v}
.
Consequently, every cluster monomial of

\mathcal{A}(\Sigma_{w,v})
, with nonnegative frozen exponents,
is the specialized class of a real simple module in

\mathscr{C}_{w,v}
.

Proof.

We induct on the number of mutations.
The initial modules belong to
\mathscr{C}_{w,v}
by
(
5.1
).
Suppose the assertion holds for the current seed and
mutate at
k\in J_{\mathrm{ex}}
.
Lemma
5.4
identifies this with
the corresponding ambient mutation, whose exchange
monomials involve only retained variables.

Ambient monoidal categorification supplies a real simple
module
L_{k}^{\prime}
and an exchange sequence

0\longrightarrow q^{a}M_{+}\longrightarrow q^{b}(L_{k}\circ L_{k}^{\prime})\longrightarrow q^{c}M_{-}\longrightarrow 0,

where
M_{+}
and
M_{-}
are normalized convolution
monomials in the current retained seed modules.
Both end terms belong to
\mathscr{C}_{w,v}
by induction
and convolution closure. Extension closure places

L_{k}\circ L_{k}^{\prime}
in the same category, and
Lemma
5.3
gives

L_{k}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}_{w,v}
.
The opposite exchange sequence and all strong
commutation properties are inherited from the ambient seed.
This completes the induction.

Normalized convolution monomials in any resulting seed
are simple. Applying this to twice the exponent vector
shows that they are real.
Their specialized classes are the required cluster monomials.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

The construction realizes all successive mutations in

\mathscr{C}_{w,v}
before any equality between its
Grothendieck ring and a cluster algebra is used.
It also embeds the quantum cluster algebra with

is asserted.

5.3.
Determinantial and frozen localizations

For
i\in I
, put

\mathsf{D}_{i}:=M(w\varpi_{i},v\varpi_{i}).

Its specialized class maps under
\Phi_{\mathrm{cl}}

to the minor

d_{i}=\Delta_{v\varpi_{i},w\varpi_{i}}

from Proposition
2.1
.

We first account for the full-support hypothesis in

[
11
, Section 4 and Theorem 4.4]
.
If
w=e
, then
v=e
and the assertions reduce to graded
vector spaces. Otherwise put
I^{\prime}=\operatorname{supp}(w)
.
The subword criterion gives

\operatorname{supp}(v)\subseteq I^{\prime}
.
Every root degree in
\mathscr{C}_{w}
belongs to

Q_{I^{\prime},+}
, so
\mathscr{C}_{w}
and
\mathscr{C}_{w,v}

identify with the corresponding categories for the
restricted Cartan datum on
I^{\prime}
.
For
i\in I^{\prime}
, restriction of the fundamental weight
gives the same determinantial module.
For
i\notin I^{\prime}
, both
w
and
v
fix
\varpi_{i}
, and

\mathsf{D}_{i}\simeq\mathbf{1}
.

The cited theorem thus makes
(\mathsf{D}_{i})_{i\in I}

a real commuting family of graded central objects of

\mathscr{C}_{w,v}
. Here graded centrality means that
for homogeneous
M
of positive root degree
\gamma

there are natural isomorphisms

\mathsf{D}_{i}\circ M\simeq q^{\chi_{i}(\gamma)}M\circ\mathsf{D}_{i},

compatible with convolution, where
\chi_{i}
is an
integer-valued linear function of the root degree.
Since determinantial modules are real and admit affinizations,
these isomorphisms imply that the
\mathsf{D}_{i}
strongly
commute with every simple object of
\mathscr{C}_{w,v}
;
see
[
10
, Lemma 3.20 and Theorem 3.26]
.
We may therefore form

\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{w,v}:=\mathscr{C}_{w,v}[\mathsf{D}_{i}^{\circ-1}\mid i\in I],\qquad\mathsf{Q}:\mathscr{C}_{w,v}\longrightarrow\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{w,v}.

By
[
10
, Proposition 2.13]
,
this is an abelian monoidal category with exact
convolution, and
\mathsf{Q}
is exact.
Every localized object is, up to grading shift, of the
form
\mathsf{Q}(M)\circ\mathsf{Q}(D)^{\circ-1}
,
where
D
is a convolution product of the
\mathsf{D}_{i}
.

For a simple
S\in\mathscr{C}_{w,v}
, each
D\circ S

is simple. Clearing denominators in a nonzero morphism
to
\mathsf{Q}(S)
represents it by a nonzero morphism
to such a simple module, which is an epimorphism.
Exactness of
\mathsf{Q}
then shows that

\mathsf{Q}(S)
is simple.
It is nonzero because convolution with a nonzero
denominator cannot annihilate a nonzero module.
Applying
\mathsf{Q}
to composition series and then
tensoring with invertible objects also shows that

\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{w,v}
has finite length.

Lemma 5.7
.

For
a\in J_{\mathrm{fr}}
, let

be the frozen

\mathscr{T}_{w,v}
and put

d_{i}=\prod_{a\in J_{\mathrm{fr}}}x_{a}^{m_{ia}},\qquad\mathsf{D}_{i}\simeq\bigodot_{a\in J_{\mathrm{fr}}}L_{a}^{\odot m_{ia}},

Proof.

The common ambient mutation sequence identifies the

2.1

therefore gives the first factorization, the occurrence
of every frozen factor, and the equality of commutative

The normalized product

Its specialized class has the same image

as that
of the self-dual simple module

, these

Fix

Moving one factor

∎

Theorem 5.8
.

Assume finite simply-laced type and let
v\leq w
.
The localized category

\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{w,v}
is a monoidal
categorification of

\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{loc}}(\Sigma_{w,v})\simeq\mathbb{C}[\mathring{\mathcal{B}}_{v,w}]
.
More precisely, there is an isomorphism

K_{\mathrm{cl}}(\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{w,v})\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathbb{C}[\mathring{\mathcal{B}}_{v,w}]

under which every cluster monomial with arbitrary
integer frozen exponents is represented by a real
simple object of
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{w,v}
.
For nonnegative frozen exponents, such an object can
already be chosen in
\mathscr{C}_{w,v}
.

Proof.

Proposition
5.5
realizes every
retained mutation and every cluster monomial with
nonnegative frozen exponents in
\mathscr{C}_{w,v}
.

By Proposition
5.1
,

K_{0}(\mathscr{C}_{w,v})
embeds into the domain

A_{q}(\mathfrak{n})
.
Thus the classes
[\mathsf{D}_{i}]
are regular.
Graded centrality makes them normal, with

[\mathsf{D}_{i}][M]=q^{\chi_{i}(\gamma)}[M][\mathsf{D}_{i}]\qquad\bigl(M\in\mathscr{C}_{w,v}\cap R(\gamma)\text{-gmod}\bigr).

Their products therefore form an Ore denominator set,
allowing powers of
q
.
Fraction representatives and clearing common
denominators give

K_{0}(\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{w,v})\simeq K_{0}(\mathscr{C}_{w,v})\bigl[[\mathsf{D}_{i}]^{-1}\mid i\in I\bigr],

by the same argument as in the proof of

[
10
, Corollary 5.4]
.
Indeed, fraction representatives give surjectivity;
preservation of simple objects and the isomorphism
criterion after clearing denominators give injectivity.

All commutation factors specialize to
1
.
The universal property of localization consequently gives

K_{\mathrm{cl}}(\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{w,v})\simeq\mathcal{I}_{w,v}[d_{i}^{-1}\mid i\in I]\simeq\mathbb{C}[\mathring{\mathcal{B}}_{v,w}],

2.1
.
By Lemma
5.7
, this
is also localization at the frozen classes.
Theorem
3.8
identifies the same
functions with

\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{loc}}(\Sigma_{w,v})
.

The exact functor
\mathsf{Q}
sends all exchange
sequences from Proposition
5.5

to exchange sequences in the localized category.
The seed objects remain nonzero real simple, and the
frozen objects are invertible.

Finally, write a cluster monomial with integer frozen
exponents as a monomial with nonnegative exponents
times an inverse frozen monomial.
The first factor is represented by a real simple object

.
The second is represented by an invertible object that
commutes with

∎
