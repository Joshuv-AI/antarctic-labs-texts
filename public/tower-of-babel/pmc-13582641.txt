Association between age at Parkinson's disease onset and divorce: An exploratory analysis from the fox insight cohort
Authors: Katarzyna Smilowska, Orsolya Makos, Alay Rangel Cristales, Raja Mehanna, Jori Fleisher, Kishore R Kumar, Roy N Alcalay, Mehri Salari, Victor McConvey, Baorong Zhang, Rodolfo Savica, Daniel J Van Wamelen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParkinson's disease (PD) typically presents after age 60; however, approximately 10% of individuals experience early-onset PD (EOPD), with symptoms beginning between ages 21 and 50. Beyond motor and non-motor symptoms, PD can impact personal relationships through stigma, social isolation, and emotional burden. This study aims to examine the influence of PD on relationship outcomes such as divorce or separation.MethodsThis exploratory study analyzed data from the Fox Insight cohort, an online longitudinal research initiative supported by The Michael J. Fox Foundation. We included 470 individuals with self-reported PD who had complete data on marital status (“married” or “divorced”) and age at PD onset. Participants were categorized as having EOPD (<50 years) or late-onset PD (≥50 years). Chi-square tests assessed the association between PD onset group and marital status. A binary logistic regression model further evaluated gender and onset age as predictors of marital status in a subset of 330 participants.ResultsA significant association was found between PD onset group and marital status, with a higher divorce rate among the EOPD group (17.5%; 7/40) compared to late-onset PD (6.1%; 26/430) (p = 0.017). Logistic regression revealed no statistically significant predictors, though trends suggested lower odds of divorce in men and those with late-onset PD.ConclusionThis exploratory analysis identified a potential association between early PD onset and higher divorce rates; however, with only 7 events in 40 EOPD participants, this finding is inherently unstable and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating only. Confirmation in larger, longitudinal cohorts is required before any conclusions can be drawn.Keywords: Parkinson's disease, relationship, EOPD, marital status, stigma

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by both motor and non-motor symptoms. 1 As the fastest-growing neurological disorder worldwide, it now affects over 8.5 million people across the globe. 2 While PD typically develops in individuals in their 60 s, approximately 10% of cases are classified as early-onset Parkinson's disease (EOPD), with symptoms onset after the age of 21 and before 50. 3

Age at onset may play a meaningful role in shaping social, relational, and psychosocial outcomes. Existing research suggests that people with PD, regardless of onset age, experience substantial challenges related to sexual dysfunction, reduced relationship satisfaction, caregiver burden, stigma, and social isolation. 4 Stigma arises from external negative perceptions and is shaped more by societal attitudes. On the other hand, shame is an internalized feeling of inadequacy that can further intensify the difficulties faced by those with PD. 5 These challenges can increase the burden of the condition, reduce quality of life, and significantly impact personal relationships. 6 Data on the prevalence of long-lasting relationships, divorces, and breakups in people with PD remains limited. Yet little is known about how these relationship stressors differ between EOPD and late-onset PD (LOPD), and whether younger age at diagnosis confers additional vulnerability.

Evidence from other chronic illnesses diagnosed at a younger age suggest that they may disrupt established life plans, career paths, and family dynamics—factors that can challenge relationship resilience. In addition, factors such as sexual dysfunction, caregiver strain, anxiety and depression, communication difficulties, and medication-induced compulsive behaviors may also contribute to the vulnerability of intimate relationships in the context of EOPD. 7 Similar relationship challenges have been documented in other chronic conditions, particularly those with early onset or progressive trajectories. For example, studies in multiple sclerosis have shown that early diagnosis can lead to significant strain on intimate relationships due to unpredictability, role changes, and caregiver burden, especially when diagnosed during peak career or parenting years.8,9 Similarly, in epilepsy, particularly among young adults, seizure unpredictability and stigma have been associated with relationship instability and lower marriage rates.10,11 In traumatic brain injury, early-onset cognitive and behavioral changes—especially those affecting emotional regulation and empathy—have been linked to elevated divorce rates and relationship breakdown. 12 These conditions help contextualize how chronic disease can disrupt personal and relational stability. However, Parkinson's disease may contribute in more specific ways, notably through impaired social cognition, including difficulties with emotion recognition, theory of mind, and empathy. 13 These deficits can uniquely affect communication and intimacy within relationships and may differentiate the psychosocial impact of PD from other neurological or chronic diseases.

The aim of this analysis is to investigate the prevalence of relationship outcomes, such as divorce and breakups, among people with PD and to compare these outcomes between EOPD and late-onset Parkinson's disease (LOPD). Additionally, we examined how relationship dynamics are affected by the challenges associated with PD, providing insights to inform clinical care and support services.

Methods

This analysis used data from the Fox Insight study, a large-scale, longitudinal, online research initiative sponsored by The Michael J. Fox Foundation for Parkinson's Research, and accessed through the Fox Insight Data Exploration Portal (https://foxden.michaeljfox.org/insight/explore/insight.jsp) on 28 October 2024. Fox Insight collects comprehensive self-reported data from individuals with and without PD through a series of standardised online assessments.

As this was an exploratory analysis, we only included a limited set of variables based on availability in the Fox Inisght database. Several demographic and clinical variables were extracted and merged to construct a dataset suitable for statistical analysis, and included age at PD onset, gender, and marital status. Participants were included in the final analytic samples only if they had complete data for the relevant variables in each analysis. To determine age at PD onset, data were merged from three different variables: PDMBAgeYrs, FIVEDiagAge, and InitPDDiagAge. In instances where values differed between variables for a given participant, the value from InitPDDiagAge was selected for analysis. Participant gender was compiled by merging five separate variables: CPEPDGender, PDMBSex, SexAtBirth, FemMenoSex, and FIVESex. These variables represented overlapping and identical definitions of sex and gender identity and no cases of discrepancy were identified. Marital status was constructed using data from three variables: PDMBMartStat, FIVEMarital, and TherapyMaritalStat. These sources were highly consistent across participants, and no mismatches were identified. For the purposes of this study, only participants who identified as either “married” or “divorced” were included. Participants reporting other marital statuses—such as single, widowed, separated, or never married—were excluded to reduce heterogeneity and allow clearer comparison between individuals currently in a marriage and those whose marriages had ended in divorce.. Participants with unresolved demographic mismatches or ambiguous marital status were excluded from final analyses to ensure data integrity. To ensure methodological transparency, detailed descriptions of how each variable was defined, measured, and harmonized across assessments are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Additionally, we created a cohort of healthy volunteers to serve as a control group for marital status. For this, we extracted all participants who replied ‘no’ to questions pertaining to having received an official diagnosis of PD. From a total of 6028 participants, only 2616 had information available regarding sex. Following matching for age and sex to the cohort of people with PD, a final cohort of 269 healthy volunteers was created. However, due to the structure of the Fox Insight database, it was not possible to confirm whether participants who reported no PD diagnosis were entirely free of other neurological diagnoses or did not have a pending PD diagnosis.

A chi-square test of independence was conducted to examine whether there was a significant association between marital status (married or divorced) and PD onset. For these purposes, two groups were created: 1) participants with PD onset before the age of 50 (EOPD), and 2) participants with PD onset at age 50 or later (LOPD). This analysis included a total of 470 participants who had complete data on both marital status (restricted to married or divorced) and age at onset of Parkinson's disease. Participants with missing or excluded marital status categories were not included in this analysis. To assess whether gender was associated with PD onset in the context of marital status, a binary logistic regression analysis was performed. This analysis included 425 participants who had complete data on marital status (married or divorced), age at onset of PD, and gender. The logistic regression model allowed for the evaluation of potential effects of gender on the odds of being in the married versus divorced category while accounting for PD onset age.

All analyses were conducted using R version 2025.05.0 + 496. Data are presented as number (percentage), unless otherwise specified.

Results

A total of 470 participants with complete data on both marital status (married or divorced) and PD onset group were included in a chi-square test of independence to assess the association between these variables (Table 1). Gender data were available for 425 individuals, of whom 260 (61%) were male and 165 (39%) female. Ethnicity was recorded for 468 participants, the majority of whom were of White ethnicity (n = 463, 99%). Educational data were available for 467 participants: 239 (51%) held or were pursuing graduate or professional degrees, while the remainder had completed associate (7%), bachelor's (39%), or high school (3%) qualifications.

The chi-square analysis indicated a statistically significant association between PD onset group and marital status (p = 0.017; Figure 1). Specifically, participants in the early onset group had a higher divorce rate (17.5%) compared to those in the late onset group (6.1%). In addition, 94.0% of individuals with LOPD reported being married compared to 82.5% of those with EOPD. Due to small expected cell counts, a Fisher's exact test was conducted instead of a chi-square test. The test confirmed a significant association between EOPD and divorce (7 of 40 early-onset participants divorced vs. 26 of 427 LOPD; p < 0.05), although the effect size was small (OR = 3.28, 95% CI [1.12, 8.55]). Compared to the group of presumed healthy volunteers, no differences were observed in divorce rates compared to EOPD participants (p = 0.086) nor LOPD participants (p = 0.225) (Figure 2).

Regression models examining interactions between PD onset and gender (n = 425), education level (n = 467), employment status (n = 470), and UPDRS motor scores (n = 460) did not show significant effects on marital status (all p > 0.05), except for number of motor symptoms, which showed a significant effect (p = 0.039). Due to low representation, race was excluded from regression analyses. Similarly, caregiver burden, assessed using data from spouses who were the primary caregivers, was only associated with marital status due to limited available data when stratified by PD onset.

To further explore the role of gender in the relationship between marital status and PD onset, a binary logistic regression was performed using a subset of 330 participants who had full data on marital status and gender. Within the model, none of the predictors reached statistical significance (p ≥ 0.256) and we observed a lower, yet non-significant, likelihood of being divorced for participants with LOPD (OR = 0.387), and for males compared to females (OR = 0.526) (Figure 2).

Discussion

Social connection and emotional support are critical determinants of quality of life and functional outcomes in PD. Therefore, exploring the relationship between marital status and PD onset is particularly important. 14 In a cross-sectional study of 178 PD patients, over 64% reported high levels of loneliness, and subjective functional impact, depression, and social network size were identified as the strongest predictors of this loneliness, pointing out that the social and emotional context in which PD unfolds is central to patient well-being, with men potentially experiencing more emotional loneliness due to a loss of close confidants.15,16

In our results, the association observed between earlier PD onset and higher likelihood of divorce should be considered cautiously as this finding was driven by a limited number of divorce events in the early-onset group. This is also reflected by the modest effect size, which may partly reflect an imbalance between EOPD (n = 40) and LOPD (n = 430) groups, which increases the risk of unstable estimates. Additionally, disease duration may mediate the observed relationship, as individuals with earlier onset have lived longer with PD and potentially faced more prolonged strain on relationships. The Fox Insight database does not capture information on the timing, context, or reasons for marital dissolution, nor does it indicate whether divorce occurred before or after PD onset or diagnosis. Therefore, it is not possible to determine whether divorce was directly related to PD, its symptoms, or unrelated personal or social factors, and these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal relationship. Consequently, it is not possible to determine whether divorce occurred before or after the development of PD symptoms. As a result, this study cannot directly evaluate whether PD contributes to divorce risk or whether other factors unrelated to the disease may explain the observed association.

This should also be compared with divorce rates in the general population, which vary significantly by age and subsequently expected to be also different in LOPD, with historically higher rates observed among younger adults. 17 Individuals between the ages of 25 and 39 account for approximately 60% of all divorces, making this the group with the highest divorce rate. However, recent trends show a marked increase in divorces among older adults—a phenomenon often referred to as “gray divorce.” In 1990, only about 8.7% of divorces involved adults aged 50 and older, but by 2019, this figure had risen to 36%. Among those aged 65 and older, the divorce rate has tripled since the 1990s, reaching 15% in 2022.17,18 These evolving patterns highlight shifting marital dynamics and indicate that while younger individuals continue to experience higher divorce rates overall, older adults are increasingly affected by late-life relationship dissolution.

Other factors that might impact disparities in divorce rates between EOPD and LOPD people with PD. For example, recent studies have highlighted certain gender-specific experiences of living with PD where women with PD may experience unique psychosocial challenges, including altered self-image, stigma related to visible symptoms, and disparities in access to care.19,20 This might lead to lower divorce rates in women with PD compared to men with PD, although this did not reach statistically significant thresholds in our analyses. Nonetheless, stigma and social withdrawal have increasingly been recognized as important determinants of quality of life and may influence interpersonal relationships and caregiving dynamics.21,22

Furthermore, younger generations are less likely to formalize partnerships through marriage and more likely to cohabit or remain unmarried. This means that “divorce” may underestimate relationship instability in younger adults, including those with EOPD and highlights the importance of including non-marital partnerships in future analyses.

This study has several limitations. First, as a cross-sectional, exploratory analysis, causal inferences cannot be drawn. Beyond the low number of participants with EOPD, the observed associations could be influenced by unmeasured confounders such as disease severity, duration since diagnosis, financial strain, caregiver burden, childrearing responsibilities, premorbid relationship strain, or mental health status—all of which may independently impact marital stability. Second, the use of self-reported data introduces potential biases, including recall bias and social desirability bias. Third, by limiting our marital status variable to only “married” and “divorced” individuals, we excluded potentially relevant comparison groups (e.g., single, widowed, separated, unmarried but in a long-term relationship). This approach may disproportionately affect younger participants, who are less likely to be married and more likely to cohabit, potentially underestimating broader relationship instability in individuals with EOPD.. While this choice was made to reduce heterogeneity and strengthen internal validity, it also limits generalizability. In addition, when gender and interaction effects were included in the logistic model, no statistically significant predictors of marital status were identified. Although these findings show a potential association between marital status and age at onset of PD, the effect appears small and may be influenced by additional, unmeasured factors which deserve further exploration. Furthermore, the EOPD group in our analysis is clinically heterogeneous, encompassing individuals with monogenic forms (e.g., Parkin-related disease) alongside idiopathic EOPD. These subgroups likely differ in disease trajectory, motor progression, and psychosocial burden, yet cannot be distinguished within the Fox Insight dataset. This heterogeneity may obscure subgroup-specific associations and should be addressed in future research using genetically characterised cohorts. A critical limitation of this analysis is that it does not account for non-motor symptoms, which are arguably the most important mediators of relationship breakdown in PD. The Fox Insight database contains data on impulse control behaviours (QUIP-RS), depression, apathy, sexual dysfunction, and sleep disturbance (PDSS-2). However, these domains were not included in the primary analysis due to substantially lower rates of data completeness across instruments, which would have further reduced statistical power in an already small EOPD cohort. It is possible, and indeed clinically plausible, that the observed association between EOPD and divorce is entirely or partly mediated by non-motor factors such as impulse control disorders, which are disproportionately prevalent in EOPD due to longer cumulative dopamine agonist exposure, or by depression and sexual dysfunction. Future analyses should incorporate these domains as both covariates and potential mediators. Finally, it should be noted that the number of participants in the EOPD group was relatively small (n = 40), which limits statistical power. Moreover, because this cohort had 99% of participants from a White background, our findings may not generalize to people with PD from other ethnic backgrounds.

Future research should consider longitudinal designs to better understand how marital status evolves over the course of Parkinson's disease and whether certain interventions might support relationship stability. Incorporating qualitative methods, such as interviews with patients and partners, could provide richer contextual understanding of the interpersonal challenges that arise following an early PD diagnosis. Additionally, future analyses should explore the role of disease duration, severity, and comorbid conditions in marital outcomes, and should aim to include a more diverse range of relationship statuses and sociodemographic backgrounds. Finally, examining larger and multi-ethnic cohorts may be helpful, as exploring cultural differences in relationship expectations and caregiving norms across global Fox Insight participants could uncover important moderating factors influencing marital outcomes in PD populations. Future studies should prospectively examine the role of non-motor symptoms, particularly impulse control disorders, depression, apathy, and sexual dysfunction, as mediators of the relationship between PD onset age and marital outcomes. Given the well-documented disproportionate burden of ICDs in EOPD, mediation analyses in larger cohorts with systematic non-motor assessments are a priority.

Conclusion

This exploratory analysis showed a potential association between age at PD onset and marital status, with individuals diagnosed before age 50 being more likely to be divorced. While this relationship may reflect the broader social and emotional challenges faced by those with early onset PD, no significant effects were found when gender and interaction terms were included in the regression model. These findings highlight the need for further research using longitudinal and qualitative approaches to better understand the complex interplay between relationship dynamics and the lived experience of Parkinson's disease. Importantly, this line of research could help inform the development of tailored resources to support individuals with EOPD, such as relationship counseling, peer support groups, and other interventions designed to strengthen coping and resilience.
