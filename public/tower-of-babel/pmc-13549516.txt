Risk evaluation of neck extension in patients with degenerative or ossified cervical spine undergoing extension-dependent surgical procedures
Authors: Ye Won Lee, Jong Beom Lee, Min Jai Cho, Hyoung Soo Byoun, Hong Rye Kim, Kyung Soo Min, Mou Seop Lee, Byeong Ho Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to establish a structured clinical flow for evaluating the risk of iatrogenic spinal cord injury in patients with cervical spine pathology undergoing surgeries requiring neck extension. We retrospectively reviewed 152 patients who underwent anterior neck surgeries requiring cervical extension between March 2022 and February 2024. All patients received preoperative cervical computed tomography (CT) to assess for ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament, degenerative stenosis, and other compressive pathologies. Patients with radiologic risk underwent symptom evaluation and were recommended cervical magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Based on CT, MRI, and clinical findings, patients were stratified into risk groups, and cleared for extension, underwent intraoperative neck position modulation, or received preventive surgery. Radiologic pathology was identified in 27 patients (17.8%). MRI was performed in 10 patients; 3 showed T2 signal changes. Five patients met high-risk criteria, of whom 1 underwent preventive decompression and 4 received intraoperative position modulation. Neck extension was permitted without restriction in 134 patients (88.2%). No postoperative neurological deterioration occurred in any patient. Our proposed evaluation flow, integrating CT, MRI, and symptom-based assessment, enables safe and selective surgical planning for neck extension. Intraoperative neck position modulation may serve as a practical alternative to preventive decompression in patients with moderate risk, thereby avoiding unnecessary surgery without compromising neurological safety.

1. Introduction

Cervical neck extension is a common intraoperative positioning technique employed in various anterior neck surgeries, including thyroidectomy, parathyroidectomy, and anterior neck mass excision. While generally considered safe, this position may pose a risk of iatrogenic spinal cord injury (SCI) in patients with underlying cervical spinal pathology. Although rare, such injuries can lead to devastating neurological outcomes, ranging from transient weakness to permanent quadriparesis, thereby negating the primary purpose of otherwise non-neurosurgical procedures.[1,2]

The biomechanical vulnerability of the cervical spinal cord during neck extension is particularly significant in patients with preexisting structural compromise. Conditions such as ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL), degenerative cervical spinal stenosis, and other space-occupying lesions can reduce the available canal space. Extension of the neck further narrows the spinal canal due to posterior buckling of the ligamentum flavum and anterior impingement from osteophytes or ossified masses, resulting in dynamic spinal cord compression.[3–5]

Despite the potential risks, there is currently no widely accepted clinical flow for assessing these patients preoperatively. Surgeons often rely on routine clinical judgment without a systematic approach to evaluating spinal pathology or stratifying risk. Performing preventive spinal surgery in all patients with cervical pathology is not only impractical but may lead to unnecessary interventions and complications. Conversely, the absence of proper preoperative screening could result in avoidable neurological injury during otherwise benign procedures.[6–8]

Therefore, this study aims to establish a structured clinical flow for evaluating the risk of iatrogenic SCI in patients undergoing surgeries that require neck extension. By incorporating radiologic findings (e.g., OPLL, degenerative stenosis, cord compression) and clinical symptoms (e.g., myelopathy, radiculopathy), we propose a stratified approach to identifying high-risk individuals. Furthermore, we assess the role of intraoperative neck position modulation and the selective use of preventive spinal surgery as tailored interventions according to risk level.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and patient selection

This retrospective observational study included patients who underwent anterior neck surgeries requiring cervical extension (e.g., thyroidectomy, parathyroidectomy, anterior neck mass excision) between March 2022 and February 2024. All patients scheduled for these surgeries were routinely referred to the Department of Neurosurgery by the Department of Surgery for preoperative cervical spine clearance. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chungbuk National University Hospital (IRB No. 2025-05-001), which waived the requirement for informed consent because of the retrospective nature of the study.

In addition, cervical range of motion was evaluated, and a sustained passive neck extension test was conducted. Patients were placed in a supine position with the neck fully extended and held in this position for up to 5 minutes, unless symptoms emerged earlier. The test was considered positive if patients developed new or worsening neurological symptoms (e.g., paresthesia, weakness, clumsiness), indicating potential dynamic spinal cord or nerve root compression. In patients with severe baseline myelopathy or who could not tolerate the extension posture, the test was deferred.

2.4. Magnetic resonance imaging evaluation and risk stratification

For patients with radiologic risk factors, cervical magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was recommended. If MRI was declined or not feasible, decisions were guided by clinical findings. T2-weighted images were reviewed for intramedullary high signal intensity.

2.5. Preventive management decision flow

High-risk patients were advised to undergo preventive decompression surgery. If the patient declined or surgery was deemed infeasible, intraoperative neck position modulation was performed.

The standard intraoperative neck extension position involved placing a cushion beneath the shoulders and cervical spine, thereby inducing full neck extension with chin elevation. In contrast, for patients in whom position modulation was indicated, the cushion height was minimized, and the chin was gently retracted. This allowed for a reduction in cervical extension while still preserving chin elevation, thereby ensuring optimal surgical exposure for thyroid or neck procedures while minimizing cervical cord injury (Fig. 1). 2.6. Outcome assessment

Postoperative neurological status was reviewed. Outcomes of interest included new or aggravated neurological symptoms and their correlation with preoperative imaging and the use of preventive strategies.

2.7. Statistical analysis

Due to the descriptive nature of the study and the limited number of high-risk patients, no inferential statistical comparisons were performed. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics and clinical characteristics. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations or medians with ranges, as appropriate. Statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk).

3. Results

3.1. Patient characteristics

A total of 152 patients scheduled for anterior neck surgeries requiring cervical extension between March 2022 and February 2024 were included in the study. None had a history of prior cervical spinal surgery.

3.2. Risk stratification according to clinical flow

Based on the proposed risk evaluation flow, the majority of patients (n = 125, 82.2%) were classified as low risk, having no evidence of pathology or cord compression on preoperative cervical CT, and thus allowed standard neck extension.

An additional 14 patients (9.2%) did not undergo MRI but were asymptomatic and likewise permitted neck extension without modification.

In contrast, 3 patients (2.0%) who declined MRI but exhibited symptoms underwent intraoperative neck position modulation.

Among patients who underwent MRI, 5 patients (3.3%) exhibited neither T2 signal changes nor symptoms and were managed with standard neck extension, while another 5 patients were classified as high-risk based on the presence of symptoms and/or T2 signal changes.

Of these high-risk patients, 4 underwent intraoperative neck position modulation, and 1 patient underwent preventive decompression surgery prior to the primary anterior neck procedure.

4. Discussion

Neck extension is essential for optimal exposure in anterior neck surgeries, including thyroidectomy and parathyroidectomy. However, in patients with cervical spinal pathology such as OPLL or degenerative stenosis, this positioning may lead to dynamic compression of the spinal cord and potential iatrogenic SCI. Although rare, cases of postoperative quadriparesis and tetraplegia following thyroid or parathyroid surgeries have been reported, often in the context of unrecognized cervical pathology.[9–12] This study proposes and validates a structured, risk-based preoperative evaluation flow that safely guides intraoperative positioning decisions and minimizes the risk of SCI.

Among 152 patients, 27 (17.8%) exhibited radiologic spinal pathology. Clinical symptoms or T2 signal changes identified 5 high-risk patients, 4 of whom were managed with intraoperative neck position modulation and 1 with preventive decompression surgery. Notably, no postoperative neurological deterioration occurred, supporting the efficacy and safety of this risk-adapted approach. Conventional neck extension involves full cervical extension via posterior neck support and shoulder elevation. In this study, high-risk patients underwent position modulation – minimizing posterior cervical support and gently retracting the chin, thereby preserving chin elevation while limiting cervical lordosis. This technique provided adequate surgical exposure while minimizing dynamic cord strain and proved effective in preventing neurological injury.

Previous literature has focused on radiographic predictors of cervical instability during extension,[2,3,5], dynamic MRI findings,[4] and motion-based risk stratification.[6,7] However, few have translated these into clinical decision-making algorithms. Our flow integrates imaging and symptom-based risk assessment and provides a practical intraoperative solution. It expands upon studies emphasizing difficult airway risks in cervical pathology[13–15] and aligns with recent calls for individualized, conservative spinal care.[1,8,16]

This approach has implications beyond neurosurgery. Cervical pathology can complicate intubation,[13] increase the risk of central cord syndrome during anesthesia,[14] and lead to catastrophic neurological outcomes in nonspinal surgeries.[9,11,12] Structured risk assessment and intraoperative caution may thus reduce cross-disciplinary complications.

SCI imposes a significant long-term economic burden. In Australia and the United Kingdom, the cost per case of SCI is substantial, especially when injury occurs perioperatively in otherwise healthy individuals.[17,18] Preventing even a single iatrogenic SCI via flow-based planning can yield major cost and quality-of-life benefits. Managing cervical spine risk in patients undergoing anterior neck surgeries presents a clinical dilemma. Although ideal risk elimination would involve routine MRI evaluation or preventive spinal surgery in all patients with cervical pathology, such an approach is neither practical nor feasible due to resource limitations, patient factors, and surgical urgency. Recognizing this constraint, our study proposes a pragmatic risk stratification flow that minimizes iatrogenic SCI risk without necessitating universal MRI or prophylactic decompression. Importantly, we introduce neck position modulation as a conservative intraoperative strategy for moderate-risk patients. By reducing cervical extension while maintaining sufficient chin elevation for surgical exposure, this technique offers a compromise between complete risk elimination and operative feasibility. Our findings demonstrate that individualized application of this flow enables safe surgical positioning even in the presence of cervical spinal pathology, balancing risk management with clinical practicality.

5. Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design introduces potential selection bias. Second, being a single-institution study, it limits external validity. Third, the small number of high-risk patients (n = 5) limits statistical power and generalizability. Methodological limitations include a lack of inter-rater reliability assessment for imaging interpretation, subjective clinical evaluation criteria, and nonvalidated neck extension testing. Future research should include multicenter prospective cohort studies to establish causality between risk factors and outcomes. Quantitative metrics for risk assessment (e.g., spinal cord compression ratios, critical spinal cord diameter thresholds) should be developed using advanced imaging techniques such as dynamic MRI. Finally, quantitative thresholds defining “safe angular ranges” for individual patients could provide valuable clinical guidance.

6. Conclusion

Our flow-based risk stratification using cervical CT, MRI, and symptom-based assessment enables safe surgical positioning in patients with cervical pathology undergoing extension-dependent procedures. Among 152 patients, 27 (17.8%) had radiologic risk factors, yet with proper evaluation and management, no neurological complications occurred. For moderate-risk patients, intraoperative neck position modulation proved a safe, noninvasive alternative to preventive decompression surgery.

This systematic approach offers several advantages: it avoids unnecessary prophylactic surgery while maintaining neurological safety, provides clear decision-making criteria for surgeons, and can be readily implemented across various surgical disciplines requiring neck extension. Our findings support the adoption of this protocol as a practical strategy to reduce iatrogenic SCI risk in patients with cervical pathology. However, further multicenter prospective studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate the clinical utility and generalizability of this flow-based risk stratification approach.
