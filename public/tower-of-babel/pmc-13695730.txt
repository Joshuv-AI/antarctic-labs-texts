International Comparison of Ambulance Time Intervals for Suspected Stroke/Transient Ischemic Attack Across 7 Emergency Medical Services Before and During the COVID‐19 Pandemic
Authors: Edel Burton, Rory Quinn, Emma Boseley, Conor Deasy, Heather Hutchinson, Mario Krammel, Adam Lloyd, Wayne Loudon, Terence Quinn, Siobhán Masterson, Neelesh C. Mupparapu, Cathal O'Donnell, Jerry Overton, Harm Van de Pas, David Willis, Julia H. Wolfe, Kieran Crosbie‐Staunton, Vera J. C. McCarthy, Áine Merwick, Patricia M. Kearney, Claire M. Buckley, Prehospital Care of Stroke (PHoCoS) Consortium
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInternational reports highlight the COVID‐19 pandemic's wide‐ranging impacts on time‐sensitive conditions, including stroke/transient ischemic attack (TIA). Comparing ambulance time intervals globally offers insights into COVID‐19's effects on different health care systems. This study aimed to compare ambulance time intervals for adults with suspected stroke/TIA across 7 services before and during the COVID‐19 pandemic.MethodsWe conducted a quasi‐experimental before‐and‐after study examining the impact of COVID‐19 on 5 ambulance time intervals and call volume for suspected stroke/TIA cases. International ambulance services using the Advanced Medical Priority Dispatch System provided data on all suspected stroke/TIA calls. Outcomes were compared between the pre‐COVID‐19 period (January 1, 2018, to the week before first identified case) and the COVID‐19 period (first identified case to December 31, 2021) using descriptive, time‐series, and regression analyses.ResultsFour national (Republic of Ireland, New Zealand, Northern Ireland, and Scotland) and 3 regional (Queensland, Tilburg, and Vienna) services provided data on 273 362 suspected stroke/TIA cases. No consistent effect was observed on allocation performance, mobilization performance, or conveyance time. Mean response time increased significantly in all services (0.5–9.13 minutes) except New Zealand, where it decreased by 1.57 minutes. Mean on‐scene time increased significantly across all services (1.05–3.78 minutes). Mean monthly call volume per 1000 emergency calls increased significantly in 6 services (16–40.5 cases), whereas the Netherlands showed no change.ConclusionsAligning time interval definitions across 7 ambulance services highlighted response and on‐scene times as key contributors to prehospital delays during the pandemic. Future interventions should focus on these prehospital phases, particularly during times of increased pressure on the health care system.

Stroke and transient ischemic attack (TIA) are time‐sensitive conditions, as optimal outcomes depend on rapid access to specialist care. 1 Minimizing the time from symptom onset to treatment initiation can be crucial in determining patient prognosis. 1 , 2 , 3 Although the urgency of response differs between stroke and TIA, distinguishing between the 2 conditions in the prehospital phase is challenging. Therefore, prompt symptom recognition and appropriate action are essential in both cases. 1 , 2 , 3 As a result, efforts have expanded beyond hospital treatment times to encompass the entire stroke care pathway, from symptom onset to intervention. 1 , 4 , 5 Internationally, health care systems have developed comprehensive care models that engage the public, emergency medical services (EMS), and referral hospitals to reduce prehospital time intervals and improve overall system efficiency. 1 , 4 , 5

Prehospital care can be broadly defined as “emergency medical care given to patients before arrival at hospital after activation of EMS”. 6 The composition of the prehospital workforce varies internationally, with paramedic‐ and nurse‐led ambulance services being the most common, whereas physician‐led EMS models are prevalent in parts of Europe. 6 , 7 , 8 As the first point of contact in the stroke chain of survival, an internationally recognized framework guiding acute stroke care, prehospital care practitioners play a key role in early intervention and expediting treatment. 1 , 2 , 3

However, the COVID‐19 pandemic had a profound impact on stroke services worldwide, disrupting acute care because of changes in service delivery and health care–seeking behavior. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 The World Stroke Organisation reported widespread consequences, including an initial decline in stroke presentations. 13 , 14 Prehospital care services experienced reduced call volumes and longer ambulance response intervals during the early pandemic phase for stroke/TIA cases. 15 Acute stroke services were reorganized to accommodate COVID‐19 cleaning protocols and personal protective equipment requirements, whereas many stroke team members and intensive care unit beds were reallocated to patients with COVID‐19. 1 , 3 , 13 , 16 , 17 Furthermore a noticeable decrease in patient presentation of less severe strokes was observed. 13 , 15 , 16 , 17 We previously reported the impact of COVID‐19 on the stroke survival chain of survival in the prehospital phase in the Republic of Ireland. 18 We observed prolonged time intervals and fewer patients falling within the thrombolysis window, alongside dips in call volume during the initial COVID‐19 lockdown and subsequent surges in COVID‐19 cases. 18 During later pandemic waves, there was a significant increase in call volume for stroke/TIA. 18

In this present study, we aimed to compare ambulance time intervals for adults with suspected stroke/TIA across 7 international ambulance services. This allowed us to explore the impact of the COVID‐19 pandemic on countries with different health care systems and contrasting pandemic responses.

METHODS

The authors declare that all supporting aggregate data are available within the article and its online Supplementary Files.

Study Design

We conducted a secondary data analysis, with a quasi‐experimental before‐and‐after study design, using data from 7 international ambulance services. In reporting this study, we adhered to the Transparent Reporting of Evaluations With Nonrandomized Designs checklist. 19

Source Population

All countries eligible for inclusion in this study are members of the Prehospital Care of Stroke Consortium. This is an international group of prehospital care, emergency medicine, and neurology clinicians and researchers established by the first author. The key inclusion criteria for services in this study was use of the Advanced Medical Priority Dispatching System (AMPDS). 20 As a result, the AMPDS code for “suspected stroke/TIA” (code 28) and other related variables are comparable across services. Services included in the Prehospital Care of Stroke Consortium have previously aligned ambulance times and intervals definitions. 21 Included countries had a range of prehospital service models, population sizes, and COVID‐19 management strategies.

Data Source

Data used in this study were extracted from electronic ambulance dispatch systems for each ambulance service. AMPDS offers a scripted caller interrogation protocol to prioritize calls and allocate EMS resources to incidents through the integrated ProQA Priority Dispatch software system. 22 , 23 A resource in this context is a prehospital practitioner who attends the patient. A code is generated during the initial triage of the call, based on the answers provided to specific questions asked by the call‐taker.

Each included service provided information on emergency call volumes and ambulance time intervals for all suspected stroke/TIA calls. The times and time intervals are accurately captured using Geotagging software. 24 Geotagging involves adding geospatial identification metadata to various media types. 24

Data Access and Ethical Approval

Data Access

The processes for accessing data for research purposes was completed for each ambulance service. A data request form was completed and submitted to the relevant team within each included service for review and approval.

For this study, we requested data sets from the resource perspective, including all adults with suspected stroke/TIA (AMPDS 22 code 28) during the period from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2021. Within the initial data set, each row of data represented an EMS resource.

Ethical Approval

We followed each of the services, or their respective country's procedure for ethical approval. Austria: ethical approval not required. Australia: Townsville Hospital and Health Service Human Research Ethics Committee; March 23, 2023 (HREC/QTHS/94281). New Zealand: Northern B Health and Disability Ethics Committee; November 10, 2022 (2022 AM 13415). Northern Ireland: ethical approval not required. Scotland: ethical approval not required. The Netherlands: waiver of ethical approval. The Republic of Ireland: Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Cork Teaching Hospital; ECM 4 (h) 05/04/2022 and ECM 3 (v) 28/03/2023. The requirement for individual informed consent was waived as anonymized data, focused on service operation was used in this study.

Outcome Variables

Outcomes

Primary outcomes included: ambulance time intervals from assignment of AMPDS triage code 8 to arrival at hospital (allocation performance, mobilization performance, response time, on‐scene time, and conveyance time).

Secondary outcome included: emergency call volume for suspected stroke/TIA per 1000 emergency calls.

Ambulance Time Intervals

An ambulance time is defined as “a specific event within the EMS response.” 25 Whereas an ambulance time interval is defined as “the temporal distance between 2 times.” 25 We defined 5 ambulance time intervals based on the terminology and definitions used in use in the Republic of Ireland 26 (Figure 1, Table S1). These time intervals align with the Ulstein recommendations for emergency stroke care. 1

AMPDS indicates Advanced Medical Priority Dispatching System.

Ambulance time intervals were derived from a sequence of discrete ambulance time points and are therefore partially overlapping. Each interval represents a specific operational phase of the prehospital care pathway; however, several intervals share common time stamps and should not be interpreted as independent measures. Rather, they reflect interrelated components of the same patient journey from emergency call triage to hospital arrival (Figure 1, Table S1).

To ensure accurate comparison amid cross‐country heterogeneity in definitions for ambulance time intervals, 21 constituent variables from each service were mapped to the time interval definitions used in the Republic of Ireland 26 (Figure 1, Table S1). E.B. created a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet with these definitions, corresponding ambulance times, and interval explanations, which was then provided to service representatives for data extraction and standardization.

Call Volume for Suspected Stroke/TIA

We defined call volume as the number of distinct suspected stroke/TIA cases per month. The monthly total was then divided by the monthly total of emergency calls for any purpose to standardize the rate of suspected stroke/TIA calls per 1000 emergency calls across services.

Covariates

Demographic factors: gender (male, female, or missing) and age.

Geographic factors: location of the incident. The classification system for this variable varied between countries. The scales used by all countries include levels of rurality and urbanism (Table S2). The location variable categories were collapsed into rural and urban for the purpose of the regression analysis.

Service factors: Included monthly total emergency calls, hour call start, and time status. We categorized the start times of calls into 4 segments: early morning (00:00–5:59); late morning (06:00–11:59); early evening (12:00–17:59); and late evening (18:00–23:59). Time status (time since patient last known well) was classified in the Republic of Ireland, New Zealand, and Northern Ireland as >4 hours, <4 hours, and unknown. In the Austrian data set, the variable is coded as >5 hours, <5 hours, and unknown. In the Netherlands, >24 hours, <24 hours, and unknown are used. In Queensland >3 hours, <3 hours, and unknown are present. Time status was not available for Scotland.

Clinical variables: The 13 determinant descriptors from the AMPDS 22 code stroke protocol were recategorized into 5 groups by grouping common determinants together, such as those related to breathing or history of stroke/TIA. These categories were: not alert, other, breathing, sudden onset of stroke/TIA symptoms, and history of cerebrovascular disease (Table S3). These five categories were established to aid in the regression analysis. This decision was based on feedback from members of the Prehospital Care of Stroke Consortium. This method has been successfully used by consortium members in past projects. 18

COVID‐19

For the overall study, January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2019, was defined as the pre‐COVID‐19 period and January 1, 2020, to December 31, 2021, as the COVID‐19 period. The beginning of the COVID‐19 period for each country was the week where the first case of COVID‐19 was reported in that region/country.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive Statistics

Categorical variables are displayed as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables are shown as mean±SD or median (with interquartile range). We conducted χ2 tests, t‐tests, z‐tests, and Mann–Whitney U tests to examine differences in the sample characteristics, time intervals, and call volume between the COVID‐19 and pre‐COVID‐19 periods. We regarded P<0.05 as statistically significant. We conducted all analysis using Stata version 16.2.

Regression Analyses

Ambulance Time Intervals

We performed multivariable ordinary least squares linear regression on the 5 ambulance time intervals to estimate the difference in mean interval duration between the pre‐COVID‐19 and COVID‐19 periods. Consequently, the dependent variable in each regression was a numeric value in minutes. The independent variable was COVID‐19.

We included time of call, call location, AMPDS dispatch determinant, and time status (time since last known well) as covariates in the model.

Suspected Stroke/TIA Call Volume per 1000 Emergency Calls

We performed multivariable ordinary least squares linear regression on the monthly suspected stroke/TIA call volume per 1000 emergency calls. The independent variable was COVID‐19.

Time Series Analysis

To test whether suspected stroke/TIA call volume per thousand calls increased over the time period studied (January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2021), we performed a 1‐sided Mann‐Kendall trend test with continuity correction for the monthly call volume data for each country.

Additionally, we performed a 1‐sided Mann‐Kendall trend test with continuity correction for the monthly call volume data for each country, using the absolute number of suspected stroke calls over the study period (2018–2021) for each EMS service.

Patient and Public Involvement

We engaged with a public and patient involvement panel consisting of 13 stroke and TIA survivors along with their family members. This group was involved from refining the research question to disseminating findings. We provide a detailed description of public and patient involvement using the Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public 2 checklist (short version) 27 in Data S1.

Median age decreased in Austria and the Republic of Ireland (both P<0.001) but increased in Australia (P<0.01) and New Zealand (P=0.02).

DISCUSSION

This analysis of 7 international ambulance services had 3 main findings. First, the presence and level of impact of the COVID‐19 pandemic on ambulance time intervals varied across the 7 included services. Second, monthly suspected stroke/TIA calls per 1000 emergency calls increased during COVID‐19 for all services, except the Netherlands, which saw no change. Finally, among the 5 services reporting data on “time since last known well,” Austria, New Zealand, and the Republic of Ireland saw more calls outside the thrombolysis window during COVID‐19, whereas the Netherlands saw fewer. In Northern Ireland, the proportion of calls with unknown “times since last known well” increased during the COVID‐19 period.

Overall, the analysis demonstrated heterogeneous effects on the 5 ambulance time intervals, with on‐scene time and response time interval being the most frequently implicated in prehospital delays during COVID‐19. The factors influencing these delays varied across different services, reflecting structural, operational, and policy‐driven differences in pandemic response. The extended time intervals observed can be attributed to several COVID‐19–related factors, including prolonged hospital handover periods because of emergency department protocols and COVID‐19 care pathways. 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 These systemic adaptations, although necessary for infection control, inadvertently contributed to longer prehospital care phases. Additionally, enhanced ambulance cleaning protocols extended vehicle turnaround times, affecting resource allocation and mobilization efficiency. 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 The use of personal protective equipment further complicated response efforts, particularly impacting response and on‐scene times. 39 Furthermore, international reports indicate a general increase in all emergency calls during the COVID‐19 period, exacerbating system pressures and contributing to delays. Staffing shortages attributable to COVID‐19 infections, isolation requirements, or redeployment further strained resources. 3 , 40 , 41 A reduction in workforce availability inevitably decreased ambulance capacity, leading to increased prehospital intervals as demand outpaced supply. 3 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 However, these effects were not uniform across all services. Nawabi et al, 45 in a meta‐analysis of stroke response times, identified that on‐scene time and ambulance response time were most frequently affected. These delays were linked to pandemic‐induced operational changes, such as enhanced personal protective equipment protocols, prolonged cleaning requirements, emergency department bottlenecks attributed to COVID‐19 triage, and staffing shortages from illness or redeployment. 45 Such systemic pressures are likely to have disproportionately impacted services already operating near capacity, leading to clinically relevant delays in thrombolysis or thrombectomy initiation. 45 , 46 , 47

The structure of a health care system and the strengths and weaknesses of its components seemed to influence the system's ability to respond during a crisis. The Australian service experienced increases in all 5 time intervals on the stroke/TIA pathway during COVID‐19. Whereas services in the Republic of Ireland, Scotland, and Northern Ireland experience delays in 4 of the 5 time intervals investigated. These services operate nationally or statewide, unlike Austria and the Netherlands, which have regional services. This suggests that a greater pool of shared resources, including staff and vehicles, may provide more resilience during increased pressure. Furthermore, New Zealand, which had the strictest COVID‐19 management strategy, elimination, 48 saw improved efficiency in entry to the prehospital pathway and review by practitioners during the pandemic. This strict COVID‐19 management strategy likely enhanced its capacity to respond to suspected stroke/TIA calls more effectively than other services. Services with the greatest number of increased time intervals were found in countries with either national health care systems or COVID‐19 management strategies other than elimination. As a result, these aspects of the health care systems in affected countries may represent appropriate targets for “futureproofing” interventions to enhance resilience in future public health crises.

A decline in suspected stroke/TIA call volume was observed in 5 of the 7 services shortly after the pandemic's onset, a trend consistent with findings from recent systematic reviews of prehospital care and hospital stroke admissions. 17 , 49 Similar reductions were reported in Canada, where ischemic stroke hospitalizations declined during the first pandemic wave and did not return to prepandemic levels. 11 Recent literature also shows declines in hospital admissions for ischemic stroke and TIA during the pandemic's early stages, potentially linked to altered health‐seeking behaviors and fear of contagion. In our data, most of the 7 services reported a reduction in suspected stroke/TIA call volume shortly after the pandemic began, echoing this broader trend. Such reductions may delay diagnosis and intervention for time‐critical events, impacting recovery and survival. 50 , 51 These declines may be linked to altered health care–seeking behaviors, with individuals delaying or avoiding emergency care because of fears of COVID‐19 exposure. This may reflect differences in public health messaging and hospital presentation procedures for stroke/TIA during the pandemic across countries.

Conversely, overall suspected stroke/TIA call volume increased during the broader COVID‐19 period in all services except the Netherlands. This increase could reflect a shift in emergency health care use, with individuals opting for EMS transport rather than self‐transport because of infection concerns. 39 , 49 Additionally, a reduction in TIA hospital admissions, as seen in Canadian data, could have contributed to an increased number of suspected stroke/TIA calls, given that standard TIA management protocols aim to prevent subsequent strokes. 11 , 52

All services apart from the Netherlands, reporting on “time since last known well,” observed an increased proportion of cases presenting beyond the thrombolysis window, indicating potential delays in seeking care. Hesitancy in calling emergency services, possibly because of fear of contracting COVID‐19 in health care settings, may have contributed to this trend. 50 , 51 , 53 , 54 , 55 Public health restrictions also limited visitors, reducing the likelihood of a witness recognizing stroke symptoms and calling for emergency assistance. Both factors could have led to more cases exceeding the critical 4‐ and 24‐hour treatment windows, ultimately affecting clinical outcomes. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 International evidence indicates that the early containment phase of COVID‐19 was associated with a reduction in individuals presenting within 4.5 hours of symptom onset and a decrease in TIA presentations, both of which are critical windows for intervention. 50 , 51 , 53 , 54 , 55 Given the time‐sensitive nature of stroke/TIA management, any delays in the prehospital phase may have significant consequences for patient recovery and prognosis. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 The unchanged “time since last known well” in the Netherlands may be attributable to its stable suspected stroke/TIA call volume during COVID‐19. With demand never exceeding capacity, the service maintained its ability to respond within the thrombolysis window where applicable.

Strengths and Limitations

The key strength of this study is its ability to accurately compare key outcomes in prehospital care across 7 international ambulance services. This allows for intercountry learnings on lessons learned from the COVID‐19 pandemic, and how these could be applied to future stroke/TIA care. The process of aligning the definitions of the global services against the Republic of Ireland definitions facilitated appropriate comparison. 21 Furthermore, the services selected used the AMPDS triaging system. 20 Thus, similar and consistent data from the triage perspective are collected. Analyzing monthly stroke call volume using both absolute numbers of stroke calls and values per 1000 emergency calls was a further strength. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that suspected stroke call volumes increased both in absolute terms and relative to overall EMS activity, strengthening confidence that the observed trends reflect true changes in demand rather than being driven solely by fluctuations in call volume related to COVID‐19 or other conditions.

However, limitations exist. The study covers 2 years pre‐COVID‐19 and during COVID‐19 but does not analyze results by pandemic wave because of varying wave patterns across countries. For example, the Republic of Ireland is one of the only countries worldwide that experienced 5 pandemic waves. 18 Differences in reporting call volumes for suspected stroke/TIA and emergency calls also pose a challenge, as some services count only attended calls, whereas others include all calls. Additionally, reliance on control center data means stroke diagnoses are based on emergency call assessments rather than clinical confirmation. Despite this, AMPDS's universal implementation suggests a consistent rate of overtriage or undertriage across regions. Ambulance time intervals examined in this study vary substantially in duration, ranging from seconds to minutes. Consequently, observed effects may be largely driven by changes in longer intervals, such as response time and on‐scene time, whereas statistically significant differences in shorter intervals (eg, allocation or mobilization performance) may have limited influence on total prehospital delay. As the intervals are sequential and partially overlapping, changes in longer phases of care may dominate overall system‐level effects and should be interpreted accordingly.

Finally, the multivariable regression models demonstrated modest R2 values, indicating that only a small proportion of the variance in ambulance time intervals was explained by the included covariates. As per previous literature, this was anticipated given the complex, multifactorial nature of prehospital care processes and the use of routinely collected dispatch‐level data. 5 , 7 , 25 , 29 , 37 , 51 Key determinants of ambulance time intervals identified in the literature, such as traffic conditions, distance to scene and receiving hospital emergency department crowding, ambulance availability, staffing levels, weather conditions, and patient‐level clinical severity, 5 , 7 , 25 , 29 , 37 , 51 were not available in a consistent or standardized format across all 7 international services and therefore could not be included. As a result, a substantial proportion of unexplained variance remains. Importantly, the primary aim of the regression analyses was not prediction but estimation of the independent association between the COVID‐19 period and ambulance time intervals, and the direction and statistical significance of these associations were consistent across services despite the low R2 values.

Future Research

First, this study offered insights into 2 key prehospital outcomes during the pre‐COVID‐19 and COVID‐19 periods. Future work may consider developing this study further by including an analysis during a post‐COVID‐19 period. This would allow for investigation of health system resilience and responsiveness after crisis. Furthermore, this analysis indicated that health care–seeking behavior may have changed during the COVID‐19 pandemic internationally. Thus, research investigating the long‐term impact of the pandemic on delayed care should be considered.

CONCLUSIONS

This international comparative study of 7 ambulance services highlights the varied impact of the COVID‐19 pandemic on ambulance service performance, shaped by differences in public health strategies, service structures, and operational adaptations. Understanding these variations provides valuable insights for optimizing prehospital stroke care and enhancing system resilience in future health crises.
