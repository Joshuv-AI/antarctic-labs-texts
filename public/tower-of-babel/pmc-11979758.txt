Control of iron acquisition by multiple small RNAs unravels a new role for transcriptional terminator loops in gene regulation
Authors: Eugenio Solchaga Flores, Jonathan Jagodnik, Fanny Quenette, Alexey Korepanov, Maude Guillier
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall RNAs (sRNAs) controlling gene expression by imperfect base-pairing with mRNA(s) are widespread in bacteria. They regulate multiple genes, including genes involved in iron homeostasis, through a wide variety of mechanisms. We previously showed that OmrA and OmrB sRNAs repress the synthesis of the Escherichia coli FepA receptor for iron–enterobactin complexes. We now report that five additional sRNAs, namely RprA, RybB, ArrS, RseX and SdsR, responding to different environmental cues, also repress fepA, independently of one another. While RprA follows the canonical mechanism of pairing with the translation initiation region, repression by ArrS or RseX requires a secondary structure far upstream within the long fepA 5′ untranslated region. We also demonstrate a dual action of SdsR, whose 5′-part pairs with the fepA translation initiation region while its 3′-end behaves like ArrS or RseX. Strikingly, mutation analysis shows a key role for the loops of these sRNAs’ intrinsic terminators in the regulation. Furthermore, regulation depends on both the Hfq chaperone and the RNase E endonuclease. Overall, our data strongly suggest that FepA levels must be tightly controlled under a variety of conditions and highlight the diversity of mechanisms that underly the regulation of gene expression by sRNAs in bacteria.

Introduction

Bacteria have the ability to adapt to a wide diversity of environments by regulating their gene expression in response to a multitude of internal and external signals. This genetic control relies on protein and RNA regulators that can target the different stages of gene expression. Among these, small RNAs (sRNAs) acting as post-transcriptional regulators via imperfect pairing to target mRNAs have been extensively studied. They exist in virtually all bacteria, and studies in model organisms revealed some key aspects of their biological functions and modes of action (1). Their pairing to target mRNA(s) typically affects the translation and/or the stability of the mRNA, either positively or negatively. In some cases, often species dependent, their action requires protein chaperones such as Hfq or ProQ. Importantly, sRNAs can be primary transcripts or processing products originating from an mRNA (or even from a longer isoform of an sRNA) and they often respond to environmental changes because their synthesis, or that of their precursors, is controlled by transcription factors. As a result, bacterial gene expression is controlled via complex regulatory circuits integrating transcriptional and post-transcriptional controls, frequently mediated by proteins and sRNAs, respectively (2).

A single sRNA typically targets multiple mRNAs, which is facilitated by imperfect base-pairing interactions, and thereby controls the expression of many genes. Hence, sRNAs can be involved in multiple pathways, ranging from central carbon metabolism to membrane stress responses. Among those processes, metal homeostasis, in particular iron homeostasis, has been repeatedly found to be controlled by sRNAs in a wide range of bacterial species, sometimes in conjunction with the Fur transcriptional repressor (3–5). In Escherichia coli (E. coli), for instance, the Fur-controlled RyhB sRNA was shown early on to repress several genes encoding iron storage or iron-containing proteins (6). These regulations not only expand the Fur regulon but also result in indirect gene activation by the Fur repressor. RyhB also activates the synthesis of enterobactin (7), the most potent iron siderophore. In addition, later studies demonstrated a role for RyhB and other sRNAs in controlling the synthesis of several iron-siderophore receptors, such as CirA, Fiu, FecA or FepA (8–11). Of note, fepA encodes the receptor for iron-enterobactin complexes, and therefore plays a key role in iron acquisition. This sRNA control of metal homeostasis is likely important for bacterial virulence, as both metal sequestration and intoxication can be encountered by bacteria during host infection (12).

From a mechanistic standpoint, control by sRNAs most often relies on their pairing with the translation initiation region (TIR) of the target-mRNAs. This interaction impedes, or more rarely favors, the binding of the 30S ribosomal subunit and therefore impacts translation initiation. However, this canonical mechanism is far from being the only one and many others have already been described. One illustration of sRNAs pairing outside of the TIR was provided by the regulation of fepA by the sRNAs OmrA and OmrB. These two partly redundant sRNAs pair with the coding sequence of fepA, more than 20 nucleotides (nts) downstream of the start codon, and impede the formation of a secondary structure that activates translation initiation (10). Consistent with this model, the long fepA 5′ untranslated region (UTR) was shown to be mostly dispensable for control by OmrA and OmrB.

Intriguingly however, other mRNAs with long 5′UTRs, such as flhDC or csgD, are known to be regulated by multiple sRNAs (13–20). Similarly, the > 140-nt fepA 5′UTR could thus serve as a hub for post-transcriptional regulation by actors other than OmrA or OmrB. This prompted us to search for additional sRNAs regulating fepA. Our results show that at least seven sRNAs repress fepA expression, and their action depends on different regions of the fepA mRNA. Furthermore, in at least three cases, the regulation relies on the terminator loop of the sRNAs rather than on the previously described base-pairing regions. Overall, this study reveals that sRNA regulation can act in yet undescribed ways and unravels new connections between sRNAs and iron uptake.

Fluorescence measurements

The fluorescence of mScarlet-I fusions was assessed as described previously (22). Briefly, cells were grown in minimal A medium supplemented with 0.25% casamino acids, 0.5% glycerol and 1 mM MgSO4 (CAG medium) with tetracycline overnight, diluted 200-fold in fresh medium supplemented with 250 μM IPTG, and optical density at 600 nm (OD600) and fluorescence using an excitation wavelength of 560 nm and an emission wavelength of 600 nm with a bandwidth of 15 nm were followed for 16 hours. The value of fluorescence at the point where OD600 is closest to 0.4 was used to calculate the normalized fluorescence (i.e. fluorescence/OD600) for each culture. Values of fluorescence shown in the figures correspond to the average normalized fluorescence of three independent replicates (2 replicates for the screen of Figure 1A) and the error bars indicate the standard deviations. The statistical significance of the results was systematically assessed with a bilateral heteroscedastic Student t-test (****: P-value < 5 × 10–5; ***: P-value < 5 × 10–4; **: P-value < 0.005; *: P-value < 0.05). For the screen of Figure 1A, strains transformed with pDicF, pCyaR, pIS118, pMicL or pSdsN137 did not grow enough in 96-wells plate to allow fluorescence measurement.

β-galactosidase assays

Strains were grown in LB medium containing antibiotics and IPTG as needed, and the β-galactosidase activity was measured from cells in mid-exponential phase unless otherwise indicated. Miller assays were performed as previously described (26) using chloroform and SDS to lyse the cells. For the experiments with a large number of samples (Figure 5 and Supplementary Figure S7), cells were diluted 200-fold from saturated overnight cultures in 200 μl medium in 96-wells plates and grown with shaking for 4.5 h. The optical density at 600 nm was measured, and 100 μl of cells were mixed with 50 μl lysis buffer containing polymyxin B and DTT (as in (27)), incubated at room temperature for 30 min, and 50 μl ONPG 4mg/ml were added. Samples were covered with 50 μl mineral oil and incubated at 28°C. The absorbance at 420 and 550 nm was followed and the β-galactosidase was calculated with the Miller formula after 75 min incubation. All β-galactosidase data are shown as average of three independent replicates with error bars indicating standard deviations. The statistical significance of the results was systematically assessed with a bilateral heteroscedastic Student t-test (****: P-value < 5 × 10–5; ***: P-value < 5 × 10–4; **: P-value < 0.005; *: P-value < 0.05).

RNA extraction, northern blots and primer extension

RNA was extracted using the hot-phenol method mostly as in (9). 650 μl of cell cultures in LB were directly mixed with phenol, while 2 ml of cultures in CAG were first centrifuged at 4°C and the cell pellets resuspended in 650 μl PBS1X prior to be mixed with phenol. After phenol extractions, RNA was ethanol-precipitated and equal amounts of total RNA were separated by electrophoresis. For the analysis of sRNAs or mRNAs, 3–8 μg of total RNA were loaded on denaturing polyacrylamide gels, or 5 μg of RNA were loaded on agarose gels, respectively. After migration, RNAs were transferred to Hybond-N+ membranes, and specific RNAs were detected using specific biotinylated probes and the same detection procedure described in (25).

For primer extension analysis, 5 μg of total RNA were mixed with 2 pmol of a Cy5-labeled probe, denatured at 80°C for 3 min, flash-frozen in dry ice-cold ethanol and thawed on ice. After 5 min of incubation at 37°C, primer extension was performed with Superscript III (Invitrogen) following manufacturer's instructions. cDNAs were separated on a denaturing 6% polyacrylamide sequencing gel and visualized with a Typhoon fluorescent scanner setup for Cy5 detection.

Western blot

Protein samples preparation and western blotting were performed mostly as described previously (23), with proteins being separated on Mini-protean TGX precast gels 4–15% (Biorad) or on a 12% SDS-polyacrylamide handcast gel when a larger number of samples was required (Figure 7C and Supplementary Figure S12). Transfer was done with the Transblot Turbo RTA transfer kit Nitrocellulose (Biorad) and detection was performed with the Clarity Max Western substrate (Biorad) following manufacturer's instructions. The anti-FepA is a gift from Kathleen Postle (28) and was used overnight at a 1:5000 dilution in PBS-Tween 0.01% with 2.5% non-fat milk at 4°C; the antibody against EF-Tu, given by Valérie Heurgué (29), was used at a dilution of 1:10 000 in the same solution.

In vitro transcription and RNA structure probing

In vitro RNA synthesis was done with the T7 RNA polymerase using PCR products as templates and the T7 Megascript kit (Ambion) following manufacturer's instructions. Around 2 or 3 G nts were added downstream of the T7 promoter to enhance efficiency of transcription for fepA, but they were omitted for the sRNAs.

Probing using DMS (dimethyl sulfate) or CMCT (1-cyclohexyl-3-(2-morpholinoethyl)carbodiimide metho-p-toluene sulfonate) was done as described in (10). For lead-acetate probing, 200 nM mRNA and 1 μM sRNA were mixed, denatured at 80°C for 3 min and flash-frozen in dry ice-cold ethanol. They were then equilibrated in 10 mM MgCl2 and incubated in a ‘structure buffer’ (final composition is 7.5 mM Tris-HCl pH7.5; 15 mM NH4Cl; 0.15 mM EDTA pH8.0; 15% glycerol and 0.03% Triton) in the presence or absence of 600 nM Hfq (a gift from Céline Gracia and Eliane Hajnsdorf, (30)) at 37°C for 10 min. Lead-acetate was then added at the final concentration of 5 mM and incubation continued for 3 min at 37°C. Reactions were stopped with 50 mM EDTA, and RNA was phenol-extracted and ethanol-precipitated with 2 μl GlycoBlue (Thermo). After resuspension in water, RNA was mixed with 2 pmol of a Cy5-labeled probe and subjected to primer extension as described above.

Results

At least seven sRNAs repress the expression of fepA in E. coli

To identify putative sRNA regulators of fepA other than OmrA/B, we took advantage of a plasmid library developed by Mandin and Gottesman (24), allowing the overproduction of multiple Hfq-dependent sRNAs. We added to this library plasmids overproducing the more recently described sRNAs McaS (19), ArrS (31), CpxQ (32), DapZ (33), NarS (34) and the ProQ-dependent RaiZ (35) and the CsrA-binding CsrC (36) sRNAs, for which we verified sRNA overexpression by Northern Blot (Supplementary Figure S1). To study the effect of these different sRNAs on fepA expression, we measured the fluorescence of a fepA-mScarlet-I (fepA-mSc) translational reporter fusion in cells transformed by the 30 plasmids. This fepA-mSc construct carries the nts −146 to +45 of fepA mRNA relative to its start codon fused in frame with the mSc coding sequence, so that the 5′-end of the fepA-mSc mRNA corresponds to the predominant 5′-end of fepA detected by primer extension in exponentially growing cells (Supplementary Figure S2). The fusion is furthermore expressed from a PLtetO-1 promoter (hereafter referred to as Ptet, (37)) and is thus independent of control exerted on the fepA promoter, such as repression by Fur.

In this experiment, 5 out of the 30 sRNAs tested affected the fluorescence of fepA-mSc more than 3-fold, always negatively: RprA, RseX, RybB, ArrS and SdsR (Figure 1A). Surprisingly, the sRNAs OmrA and OmrB that target a translation activating stem-loop (TASL) in the early coding sequence of fepA mRNA (10) had no significant effect in this experiment. This is most likely because the TASL can be extended in the context of this fepA-mSc construct and could be refractory to OmrA/B regulation and/or have lost its function in translation activation (Supplementary Figure S3).

To validate these five novel regulators of fepA, we then looked at their effect on the expression of both a fepA-lacZ translational fusion (again transcribed from the Ptet promoter), and the fepA endogenous gene. All five plasmids, as well as pOmrA and pOmrB, efficiently repressed the expression of fepA-lacZ, decreasing the β-galactosidase activity from 2.8- to more than 20-fold (Figure 1B) and induced a clear reduction in fepA mRNA and FepA protein levels (Figure 1C). This control of fepA-lacZ and endogenous fepA by OmrA and OmrB confirms that the lack of regulation of fepA-mSc by these sRNAs is context dependent and that fepA-mSc is not a good reporter for regulations targeting the TASL. Overall, these results show that at least seven sRNAs repress fepA expression.

As FepA is the receptor for iron-enterobactin complexes and enterobactin is the most potent iron siderophore used by E. coli, FepA plays a major role in iron acquisition. Hence, fepA-deleted cells display a severe growth defect in iron-depleted conditions, e.g. in the presence of an iron chelator such as 2–2′-dipyridyl (DIP, Figure 1D). We thus tested the effect of some of the sRNAs repressing fepA under these conditions and found that overproduction of either SdsR or ArrS inhibited growth of iron-starved cells (Figure 1D). This is consistent with the observation that these two sRNAs provide the strongest fepA repression in several experiments (Figure 1A–C).

Independent action of the seven sRNAs, and of Hfq, to repress fepA

We next checked whether the effect of some of these seven sRNAs could be mediated by one (or more) of the other six sRNAs, or whether they act independently to repress fepA. To address this question, we analyzed the control of fepA by the different plasmids in cells deleted for the chromosomal copies of the omrA, omrB, rprA, rybB, arrS, sdsR and rseX genes (Δ7 strain, Figure 2A). This was done using the translational fepA-lacZ fusion as a reporter, transcribed here from a modified version of the Ptet promoter (lowPtet) to decrease its expression and potentially be able to detect up-effects of the sRNAs deletions. Repression of fepA by all plasmids was similar in the wt and Δ7 strains, clearly showing that the action of each of these seven sRNAs is independent of the six others (Figure 2A).

Interestingly, this experiment also showed that the deletion of the seven sRNAs significantly upregulated fepA expression by a factor of about 1.4-fold (compare wt and Δ7 in presence of the vector control). This effect was similar in the absence of plasmids in exponential phase (1.5-fold increase in the Δ7 strain at OD600= 0.4; Figure 2B), but was no longer visible at the onset of stationary phase (OD600= 2, Figure 2B). This is surprising as Northern blot analysis showed that at least four of these sRNAs, namely SdsR, RprA, OmrA and RybB, are more abundant at OD600= 2 than OD600= 0.4, consistent with published data (e.g. (38–40), also suggesting that this is true for OmrB), while the other two sRNAs, RseX and ArrS, were not or barely detected by Northern blot in either condition (Figure 2C). It is possible that (an)other factor(s) are involved in repressing fepA expression in the Δ7 strain at OD600= 2. Lastly, this experiment showed that the deletion of hfq had a greater impact than deletion of the seven sRNAs, since fepA expression was increased in the Δhfq strain by 2.1- and 2.5-fold at OD = 0.4 and OD = 2, respectively (Figure 2B). These data strongly suggest that Hfq also plays an independent role in fepA repression in addition to the repression by the seven sRNAs. To study this further, we then used mutants of three different RNA binding surfaces of the Hfq protein that have been described previously: the proximal face (Q8A mutant), the distal face (Y25D) or the rim (R16A) (41–43). The proximal face recognizes U-rich sequences, and interacts with a predominant class of Hfq-binding sRNAs termed Class I sRNAs, while the distal face recognizes successive (AAN) motifs, often found in mRNAs that are targeted by Class I sRNAs. The lateral rim surface makes additional contact with different RNA substrates (either sRNAs or mRNAs) via a patch of arginine residues; these residues are important for the RNA annealing activity of Hfq, at least in vitro (43). When following fepA expression in these three Hfq point mutants, only the Y25D allele mimicked the effect of the hfq deletion, although at a lower level (activation of 1.8-fold for hfqY25D compared to >2-fold for Δhfq, Figure 2D). In contrast, fepA activation was lost or reduced to only 1.2-fold in the Q8A and R16A mutants, respectively. This clearly indicates that most of the repression of fepA by Hfq is likely independent of sRNAs.

RprA and SdsR repress fepA via direct binding to the TIR

To identify possible mechanisms of regulation by the five newly identified fepA repressors, we searched for potential base-pairing interactions between RprA, RybB, RseX, ArrS or SdsR sRNAs and the (−146 +45) region of the fepA mRNA using IntaRNA 2.0 (44). The most convincing predictions were found for the RprA and SdsR sRNAs, and the pairing is predicted within the fepA TIR in both cases (Figure 3). We tested these putative interactions by introducing compensatory changes in either the sRNA and/or the fepA-lacZ fusion. For RprA, mutating either the sRNA or the mRNA within the predicted pairing site strongly impaired regulation, while restoring the interaction by mutating both restored control (2.5- and 2.1-fold repression in the wt/wt and mutant/mutant context, respectively; Figure 3A). For SdsR, control of the fepA-146+45-lacZ fusion was also more efficient in the wt/wt (36-fold repression) or mutant/mutant (24-fold) context than when pairing was disrupted with mutation in either SdsR or in fepA-lacZ (Figure 3B). These data support the predicted interactions of the fepA TIR with RprA or SdsR, which presumably impede 30S binding and thus inhibit translation initiation. For SdsR however, repression is still visible in the presence of these mutations that are expected to disrupt the SdsR-fepA pairing (12-fold repression of wt fepA-lacZ by SdsRmutβ, and 15-fold for fepAmutβ-lacZ by wt SdsR, Figure 3B). This suggests that the pairing of SdsR with the fepA Shine–Dalgarno (SD) region only explains part of the observed regulation, and that a second action of SdsR, involving another mechanism, contributes to the full regulation. This could explain the strong effect of SdsR on fepA expression, which is rather unusual for an sRNA, even when overproduced.

To go further, we next analyzed the same set of compensatory mutations in SdsR and fepA using the Ptet-fepA-26+45–lacZ fusion (Figure 3B). This simplified version of the fepA reporter is devoid of most of fepA 5′UTR and was previously shown to allow similar translational regulation to the full-length fepA–lacZ fusion, at least by sRNAs affecting fepA translation initiation, such as OmrA/B ((10) and see below). Repression of this shorter fusion by pSdsR was about 13-fold, which is reduced compared to the 36-fold repression of the fusion with the full-length 5′UTR. In addition, mutations SdsRmutβ or fepAmutβ-lacZ completely abolish repression, while combining these mutations to restore pairing also restored control (11-fold repression, Figure 3B). This fully confirms that SdsR sRNA pairs to fepA TIR but also that full repression of fepA by SdsR requires an additional regulatory event that is dependent on a longer fepA 5′UTR. In summary, our data suggest that SdsR exerts a dual regulation on fepA expression, presumably through two distinct mechanisms.

ArrS, RseX and SdsR are dependent on the long 5′UTR of fepA to control its expression

The above results prompted us to dissect the role of the long fepA 5′UTR in the control by sRNAs in more detail. To this end, we first determined its secondary structure using DMS and CMCT chemical probing, and an RNase A and T1 protection assay (Supplementary Figure S4). The reactivities of the different nts are summarized in Figure 4A. The inferred structure is also supported by the cleavage pattern of fepA by lead-acetate, which is more prominent in all single-stranded regions (Figure 4B). Briefly, several secondary structures are present in the fepA 5′UTR: a 5-base pairs (bp) stem-loop at the very 5′-end (stem-loop I) and two imperfect stem-loops (II-b and II-c) forming a three-way junction with the 6-bp stem II-a (Figure 4A). In contrast, the fepA TIR is very reactive to the different probes and thus appears mostly unstructured, consistent with our previous in vitro probing data (10).

We next sought to assess the importance of the different structural elements in fepA 5′UTR for the control by sRNAs. To do so, we measured the repression of a set of fepA-lacZ fusions carrying either the full-length 5′UTR or truncated versions with only the last 120, 109, 92, 39 and 26 nts (Figure 5A). These truncated constructs are expected to remove stem-loop I (construct -120), I and II-a (-109), I, II-a and II-b (-92), or all stem-loop structures in the 5′UTR (−39 and −26). None of these truncations significantly affected the expression of the fusion (Supplementary Figure S5A), and the predicted structure of the fepA mRNA region of each construct is shown in Supplementary Figure S6. As expected, OmrA and OmrB sRNAs repressed fepA regardless of the length of its 5′UTR (Figure 5A). The effects of RprA and RybB also appear largely independent of the long fepA 5′UTR. Indeed, RybB efficiently repressed all fusions, with a much higher repression factor for the (−26 +45) version (>8-fold repression, compared to 2- to 4-fold for other fusions), suggesting that RybB directly or indirectly targets fepA TIR or early coding sequence. RprA similarly repressed fusions −146, −109, −92 and −39, but failed to repress the −120 and the −26 variants (Figure 5A, left panel). This is easily explained for the −26 fusion, as this construct lacks part of the pairing region to RprA (Figure 3A), and the −120 fusion may escape regulation due to a different accessibility of the RprA pairing region in this variant. The fepA 5′UTR is thus mostly dispensable for the regulation by OmrA, OmrB, RybB and RprA.

The situation is completely different for the 3 other sRNAs repressing fepA. Indeed, control by ArrS, RseX or SdsR was reduced (roughly by half) for the −120 and −109 versions, and further reduced (for SdsR) or even abolished (for RseX or ArrS) when the 5′UTR was 92 nt long or shorter (Figure 5A, right panel). The demonstrated pairing of SdsR with the fepA TIR is likely responsible for the remaining repression of the fepA-lacZ fusions −92, −39 or −26 by SdsR. We thus also measured repression of the full set of fusions by SdsRmutβ, i.e. the SdsR variant carrying the mutation that disrupts pairing to the fepA TIR (Figure 3B). In this case, the pattern of regulation was highly similar to those observed with RseX and ArrS, as regulation was abolished when the 5′UTR of fepA was 92 nts or shorter (Figure 5A). This confirms that SdsR represses fepA both via pairing to the mRNA TIR and via an independent regulatory process that requires the upstream portion of the fepA 5′UTR. For simplicity, we will distinguish between these two control mechanisms as far5’UTR-independent and far5’UTR-dependent, respectively, for the rest of the manuscript.

Our results clearly show a role for the promoter-proximal portion of the fepA 5′UTR in the repression by SdsR, RseX and ArrS, and we therefore wondered whether it was sufficient for these control processes or not. To address this question, we used another set of fepA–lacZ fusions, truncated this time from the 3′-end of the fepA moiety and where lacZ is fused in frame after nt +30 or +3 of fepA, or fused after nt -26 and carrying the lacZ TIR in this case (Supplementary Figure S7A; the basal expression of these fusions and the predicted structure of the fepA region are shown in Supplementary Figures S5A and S6, respectively). None of the far5’UTR-dependent or -independent sRNAs was able to significantly control the (−146–26) fusion, clearly showing that the fepA TIR is required for all these control mechanisms (Supplementary Figure S7A). Moreover, despite a lower basal expression (Supplementary Figure S5A), the fusion ending at fepA nt + 3 was controlled to varying degrees by most of the far5’UTR-dependent sRNAs (Supplementary Figure S7A). Hence, the essential elements for control by these sRNAs are fepA 5′UTR and start codon, while the early ORF region is required for optimal regulation. Finally, the fusion with fepA ending at + 30 was still controlled by the different sRNAs, except for OmrA and OmrB, as expected since this construct removes the 3′ side of the translation activating structure that is the target of these sRNAs (10).

Together, these data show that, of the seven sRNAs that repress fepA, the three that had the strongest effect when overproduced are dependent on the upstream portion of the fepA 5′UTR. At this stage, however, their mode of action on fepA was still unclear, and we focused on this question.

Repression of fepA by the 3′-ends of SdsR or RseX sRNAs

SdsR is transcribed as a 104 nts long sRNA in E. coli (103 nts in Salmonella, 45) and can be processed by RNase E, producing a shorter sRNA devoid of the first 31 nts (45,46). This processed form is hereafter referred to as SdsR+32. SdsR was shown to target multiple mRNAs, including ompD in Salmonella (45), mutS (47), tolC (48) and two pairing regions have been identified within this sRNA (46). Interestingly, the first pairing region of SdsR is the one that interacts with the fepA TIR, and it is absent from the processed SdsR+32 (Figure 6A). As data from Figures 3 and 5 show a repressive effect of SdsR on fepA independently of its pairing to the TIR, we asked whether SdsR+32 could repress fepA. Using the Ptet-fepA-146+45–lacZ fusion as a reporter, we found that SdsR+32 down-regulated fepA 16-fold. As expected, this is lower than the repression by full-length SdsR (22-fold, Figure 6B–C), as the pairing to the TIR is lost with SdsR+32. To test the involvement of the second pairing region of SdsR in fepA regulation, we constructed a second truncated version of SdsR, namely SdsR+44, that lacks both previously identified pairing regions (Figure 6A) and found that it still efficiently repressed fepA, independently of the other sRNAs (Figure 6B–E). Finally, we also tested these two short forms of SdsR on the set of fepA-lacZ reporters with different 5′UTR lengths. Their regulation pattern was strikingly similar to that of SdsRmutβ, with impaired or abolished repression of fusions with shorter 5′UTR (Figure 5A). These data demonstrate that the last 61 nts of SdsR are necessary and sufficient for the regulation of fepA that is independent of the TIR-pairing.

Since control of fepA by RseX and ArrS also displayed a profile similar to that of SdsRmutβ, SdsR+32 and SdsR+44 (Figure 5A), we wondered whether the 3′-end of these sRNAs could be responsible for fepA regulation as well. This was tested for RseX, by using a short form of this sRNA devoid of the first 51 nts, hereafter named RseX+52. Surprisingly, repression of fepA-lacZ by this variant increased to 23-fold, compared to 7-fold for wt RseX (Figure 6A–C). As SdsR+44, RseX+52 acts independently of the other sRNAs repressing fepA (Figure 6D) and strongly decreased the FepA protein levels (Figure 6E). Furthermore, similar to the other far5’UTR-dependent sRNAs (SdsRmutβ, SdsR+32, SdsR+44, ArrS and RseX), reducing the 5′UTR to 92 nts or less completely abolished control by RseX+52 (Figure 5A). Thus, the 3′-ends of both SdsR and RseX are responsible for the far5’UTR-dependent control of fepA. As for ArrS, we were unable to determine if a shorter version of this sRNA could still repress fepA as this construct was poorly produced in vivo, presumably because of stability defects. Overall, the unexpected results obtained with the far5’UTR-dependent sRNAs raise several intriguing questions, in particular, which common feature(s), if any, of SdsR(+44), RseX(+52) and ArrS mediate fepA control, and by which mechanism.

SdsR, RseX and ArrS transcriptional terminator loops drive fepA regulation, likely without base-pairing

The most obvious common element between SdsR+44, RseX+52 and ArrS is their Rho-independent transcriptional terminator, and we thus wondered whether it could mediate fepA control. As all sRNAs tested in our original screen (Figure 1A), including the ones that did not regulate fepA expression, also possess such terminators, it is highly unlikely that the stem-loop structure or the stretch of Us could explain the effect of SdsR+44, RseX+52 and ArrS. For this reason, we focused on the exposed nts of their terminator loop and tested their importance in fepA control by mutating some of them in SdsR+44 (mutant SdsR+44mut), RseX+52 (mutant RseX+52mut) and ArrS (ArrSmut). Strikingly, these mutant sRNAs strongly impaired fepA regulation, even though they accumulated to a comparable level to the un-mutated SdsR+44 RseX+52 and ArrS (Figure 6, panels A–C and F). This indicates that the loops of the intrinsic terminators of these three sRNAs are involved in gene regulation, which is rather unusual.

We were not able to predict a convincing base-pairing interaction between SdsR+44, RseX+52, or ArrS, and the fepA mRNA. Nonetheless, we could not rule out such a pairing on this basis alone. To test for a possible direct interaction, we analyzed the lead cleavage pattern of an in vitro transcribed fepA mRNA fragment encompassing the 5′UTR and the early coding sequence, in the presence of several sRNAs: wt SdsR, SdsRmutβ, SdsR+44, SdsR+44mut and wt RseX (Figure 4B). The addition of wt SdsR protected the (−22–11) region of fepA mRNA, and the SdsRmutβ variant largely lost this protection pattern, in complete agreement with the pairing shown in Figure 3B. None of the other sRNAs tested modified the lead cleavage pattern of fepA (Figure 4B). As the Hfq chaperone is known to promote several sRNA–mRNA interactions, we also performed this experiment in the presence of Hfq. Even under these conditions, the far5’UTR-dependent sRNAs did not change the cleavage pattern of fepA, further indicating that they do not base-pair to fepA mRNA. However, Hfq itself modified the fepA digestion pattern (Figure 4B). More specifically, several regions including the II-b stem, the II-c loop and the TIR appeared protected by Hfq, while the II-b loop appeared more exposed. Altogether, these data suggest that repression of fepA by RseX or SdsR is likely to involve additional factor(s) and may not rely only on a classical base-pairing mechanism. Furthermore, they strongly support a direct interaction between Hfq and the fepA mRNA.

Stem-loop II-b of the fepA 5′UTR is required for the control by SdsR, RseX and ArrS sRNAs

We next wondered which feature of the fepA mRNA is involved in the control by the far5’UTR-dependent sRNAs. As these sRNAs can still repress the −109 fepA-lacZ fusion, but not the −92 or shorter fusions (Figure 5A), we reasoned that the stem-loop II-b most likely plays an important role. This was tested by introducing mutations in the exposed nts −95 to −88 of the apical loop (mutations Loop1 and Loop2) or in the upper part of the stem (mutations Stem5’ and Stem3’ to open this structure, and the compensatory mutation StemComp) (Figure 5B; the predicted structures of the different mutants are shown in Supplementary Figure S9). The relative expression of the resulting fusions is displayed in Supplementary Figure S5B, showing mild negative effects of Stem5’, Stem3’ and Loop2 mutants on fepA expression, hinting at the importance of this region for fepA expression. Strikingly, the Loop1, Stem5’ and Stem3’ mutations completely abolished the regulation of fepA by all the far5’UTR-dependent sRNAs, confirming the key role of stem-loop II-b. Furthermore, the restoration of the upper stem structure with the StemComp variant fully restored control by ArrS, and partially by SdsR+32 or SdsR+44, showing the importance of the secondary structure rather than the sequence of the stem for these sRNAs (Figure 5B). In contrast, RseX or RseX+52 barely affected the expression of the StemComp fusion, suggesting there may be contextual differences between the modes of action of RseX, SdsR and ArrS. Lastly, the Loop2 change had no noticeable effect at all, contrasting with the major effect of the Loop1 mutation. This suggests that only specific nts of the apical loop II-b are important for sRNA control. In full agreement with the previous data, the control by SdsR followed the same pattern as SdsR+32 or SdsR+44, except that the regulation was reduced, and not abolished, in the Loop1, Stem5’ or Stem3’ mutants, consistent with the fact that the pairing of SdsR with the TIR is not affected in these 5′UTR mutants. Similarly, we found that the far5’UTR-independent sRNA OmrA repressed the wt and the different mutants of fepA equally efficiently (Figure 5B).

We also tested the importance of other elements of fepA mRNA, and in particular the stem-loop II-c, by deleting it in the fepA-lacZ reporter fusion (ΔII-c construct, Figure 5B), as well as the sequence around the SD and AUG start codon by using the mutβ and mutγ variants (Supplementary Figure S7B). These changes impaired control by some of the far5’UTR-dependent sRNAs, such as SdsR+32, SdsR+44, RseX, RseX+52 or ArrS, but never as drastically as changes in the stem-loop II-b (Figure 5B and Supplementary Figure S7B).

In conclusion, stem-loop II-b is a major determinant of the fepA mRNA for the control by these sRNAs that are likely to act on the fepA 5′UTR independently of a direct base-pairing interaction. Apart from minor differences between them (for instance, with the StemComp variant), the similar regulation patterns of the different stem-loop II-b mutants suggests that (a) common factor(s) and a common regulatory mechanism likely mediate the effects of the far5’UTR-dependent sRNAs on fepA.

Hfq and RNase E are required for fepA control by ArrS, RseX and SdsR

RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) are obvious candidates for factors involved in gene control by sRNAs, and several of them, among which Hfq, ProQ, CsrA or different ribonucleases were previously found to be essential for sRNA action (e.g. 35,36,49–51). Hence, we tested whether the different sRNAs still control fepA in strains mutated for some RBPs. The control of fepA was not affected in mutants of the genes for ProQ, CsrA, RNase III or PNPase (Supplementary Figure S10). In contrast, we found that Hfq and the RNase E were involved in these controls.

To study the role of Hfq, we used a deletion of the hfq gene as well as mutants of the proximal face (Q8A mutant), the distal face (Y25D) or the rim (R16A) described above (41–43). Because the deletion of hfq or the point mutations Q8A or R16A drastically decreased the levels of most of the sRNAs used in this study (Supplementary Figure S11A), we focused on the distal face mutant Y25D. In these experiments, we assessed the expression of fepA using the Ptet-fepA-mSc fusion, and we found that only the full-length SdsR repressed fepA in the hfqY25D mutant (Figure 7A). This is consistent with the in vitro observation that the pairing of SdsR to the SD region of fepA exists in the absence of any other factor (Figure 4B). In contrast, repression by all other sRNAs, including SdsR+44, was abolished in the hfqY25D context, despite the fact that all sRNAs accumulate to higher levels in this mutant background compared to the wt strain (Figure 7B).

Expression of fepA was clearly up-regulated in the Δhfq or hfqY25D mutant (Figures 2B, 2D and 7A, vector lanes), and one possibility to explain the effect of the 3′-ends of RseX or SdsR is that they would generally enhance Hfq action on mRNA-targets. Since the expression of the mutS mRNA is also strongly up-regulated (about 10-fold) in either Δhfq or hfqY25D strains (52), we analyzed the impact of ArrS, RseX, SdsR and their short forms on a mutS-lacZ fusion. Consistent with previous reports (52), this fusion was repressed 3-fold by ArcZ, used here as a positive control, and, to a lesser extent, by SdsR (1.8-fold repression; note that this fusion does not carry the pairing site shown in (47)). However, we found that its expression is barely modulated by SdsR+44 (1.2-fold repression) and completely insensitive to ArrS, RseX or RseX+52 overproduction (Supplementary Figure S11B). Thus, these sRNAs do not have a general effect on the expression of Hfq-regulated genes.

We also studied a possible involvement of the essential RNase E endonuclease in fepA control. For this, using cells grown in iron-supplemented LB to limit fepA transcription (Supplementary Figure S12A), we inactivated the thermosensitive RNase E enzyme encoded by the rnets allele by shifting the culture to non-permissive temperature for 15 minutes. At the same time, transcription of endogenous fepA mRNA and of the different plasmid-borne sRNAs, SdsR+44, RseX+52 and ArrS, was induced by addition of DIP and IPTG, respectively. As controls, the effect of sRNAs was also analyzed at permissive temperature, and the same experiment was performed with rne+ cells. Proteins and RNAs were then extracted and analyzed by Western or Northern blots, respectively (Figure 7C). With this experimental set-up, we observed clear regulation of FepA levels by ArrS, SdsR+44 and RseX+52 in wt cells, as well as in the rnets cells at permissive temperature. In contrast, when RNase E was inactivated by the shift at 44°C, regulation by all three far5’UTR-dependent sRNAs was strongly impaired. In a similar experiment, we also analyzed the requirement for RNase E for fepA regulation by OmrA and the full-length SdsR and RseX sRNAs (Supplementary Figure S12B). In this case, cells were grown in LB, and the basal level of FepA was analyzed for each plasmid using samples that were kept at low temperature and were not treated with DIP or IPTG (‘-’ lanes). More modest regulations of fepA, for instance by OmrA or wt RseX, were not easily assessed, even in the wt strain, and deciphering the role of RNase E in these cases would require to adjust the experimental conditions (see for instance (10) for OmrA). As for the wt SdsR sRNA, it clearly prevented the synthesis of FepA even in the rnets samples at non-permissive temperature, in contrast to ArrS, SdsR+44 or RseX+52. This clearly shows that the control of FepA protein levels by SdsR through TIR base-pairing does not require the catalytic activity of the RNase E. Consistent with this pairing affecting fepA translation, the impact of SdsR on fepA mRNA levels was lost upon RNase E inactivation, despite the control at the protein level (Supplementary Figure S12B, compare SdsR effect on FepA Western and fepA Northern blots). Together, these data clearly point to a role for both Hfq and the RNase E in the control of fepA by the far5’UTR-dependent sRNAs.

Discussion

This work reveals that at least five sRNAs, in addition to the previously studied OmrA and OmrB, repress the expression of the fepA gene in E. coli. Even more sRNAs could be involved in fepA control, either because they were not present in the plasmid library used in this study, or because, as seen for OmrA and OmrB, the fepA-mSc reporter that was used here to identify them is not a suitable read-out for their action (Figure 1 and Supplementary Figure S3). In this regard, it is interesting that different RNA-RNA interactome studies such as RIL-seq following co-immunoprecipitation with Hfq (53–55) detected SdsR as fepA partner, as well as a few other (s)RNAs such as MicF, raiA (RaiZ precursor), 5′rnk or the ybiJ-ybiC intergenic region. However, SdsR was identified in almost all tested conditions, while the others were found mostly under iron starvation conditions (53), in which fepA is expected to be the most abundant. Some of these other sRNAs are absent from the library used in Figure 1 and it would be interesting to analyze their effect on fepA expression. In contrast, with the exception of SdsR, the sRNAs shown here and previously (10) to repress fepA were not found in the RIL-seq, even for the direct base-pairing OmrA/B or RprA sRNAs. This highlights the complementarity of the approach used here with large scale interaction studies such as RIL-seq.

The repression of fepA by SdsR was recently confirmed in a proteomics analysis comparing wt and ΔsdsR in long-term nitrogen-starved cells, a condition under which SdsR was found to importantly contribute to sRNA-mRNA interactions (55). Interestingly, FepB and FepC proteins, both involved in ferric enterobactin transport were also more abundant in ΔsdsR cells in this study, suggesting a wider role for this sRNA in iron acquisition. Under our experimental conditions, we did not observe an effect of ΔsdsR alone, or of any single deletion of the other six sRNAs, on fepA expression when using the lowPtet-fepA-lacZ read-out (data not shown). However, activity of the fusion was increased in the Δ7 strain (Figure 2B), confirming that chromosomal expression of at least some of these sRNAs is sufficient to impact FepA synthesis.

Except for OmrA and OmrB which are at least partially redundant, the seven sRNAs repressing fepA respond to different environmental cues via distinct transcriptional regulators (Figure 8 and references therein (77–82)). They can nonetheless be co-expressed, for instance when cells reach stationary phase (Figure 2C). These results thus suggest that it is beneficial for the cell to limit FepA levels under different conditions. Intuitively, these regulatory mechanisms could be more efficient when fepA transcription is less abundant, such as under iron-replete conditions, and they could participate to limit iron toxicity. They could also be important during a switch to iron limitation or, in contrast, recovery from iron limitation. Alternatively, these sRNAs could limit fepA de-repression by Fur under conditions combining iron starvation and FepA toxicity, such as those induced by phage H8 (56) or colicins B or D (57). Further investigation could reveal whether any of these sRNAs itself respond to iron, phages and/or colicins, and how these regulations articulate with the Fur-mediated transcriptional control of fepA. It would also be interesting to determine if this repression of fepA by multiple sRNAs participates to the different processes that are controlled by changes in iron levels, among which antibiotics resistance (58,59) or the iron-based memory impacting swarming motility (60).

Another key finding made in this study is that the intrinsic transcriptional terminators of several sRNAs are required for their regulatory action. These terminators were already known to be strong Hfq-binding sites via their long U stretches (61,62), but we report here that the sequence of the exposed nts can have an important role as well (Figure 6). This is reminiscent of what was shown for the OxyS sRNA in the control of the gene encoding the FhlA transcriptional activator, but with the major difference that the terminator loops of the sRNAs apparently act via different mechanisms to control fhlA or fepA. Indeed, the exposed nts of the OxyS terminator loop are directly involved in the pairing with the mRNA-target as they form a kissing complex with the fhlA TIR, while a second kissing loop interaction is made between another loop of OxyS and the fhlA coding sequence (63,64). In contrast, our results do not support a direct interaction of SdsR, RseX or ArrS terminator loops with the fepA mRNA (Figure 4), but they could pair instead to another sRNA or mRNA, whose regulation would in turn affect fepA expression.

Alternatively, they could be involved in the interaction with one or several protein(s), including Hfq itself. However, because the mutants of the terminator loop (RseX+52mut, SdsR+44mut and ArrSmut), accumulate to levels comparable with their un-mutated counterparts (RseX+52, SdsR+44 and ArrS), it is unlikely that they are strongly affected in their ability to bind Hfq. Furthermore, the effect of the far5’UTR-dependent sRNAs does not appear to be simply due to a general effect on Hfq as the known Hfq target mutS does not respond to these sRNAs (Supplementary Figure S11B).

In addition to its RNA binding activities, Hfq is involved in numerous protein–protein interactions, many of which are RNA dependent ((65) and references therein). Hence, one can hypothesize that mutating the exposed nts of RseX+52 or SdsR+44 changes the ability of Hfq to interact with its other partners. Of note, RNase E is one of the proteins that interacts with Hfq in an RNA-dependent manner (65), and it would be interesting to determine if RseX+52, SdsR+44 or ArrS somehow affects the formation of Hfq-RNase E complexes. More generally, understanding how Hfq and RNase E, as well as other potential factors, are involved in the sRNA control of fepA will be useful to explain the specificity of these regulatory mechanisms for which no sRNA-mRNA pairing could be demonstrated. This situation is indeed different from the other examples of RNase E-dependent control by sRNAs reported previously for which target specificity was provided by sRNA-mRNA pairing (49,66,67). At this stage, we cannot exclude that some of the effects that we observe on fepA are due to an overproduction of the sRNAs. Even if it were the case, understanding the underlying mechanism is likely to bring important insight into the post-transcriptional regulation of this important gene for iron acquisition.

Being the receptor of iron-siderophore complexes, FepA belongs to two classes of proteins whose synthesis has been repeatedly found to be subjected to sRNA control: the outer membrane proteins and the proteins involved in iron homeostasis. Some of the sRNAs involved in these controls of outer membrane proteins or iron-related genes were notably RybB (68,69), SdsR (45,46,48), RseX (70), and OmrA or OmrB (9,10,71). The findings that they also repress fepA expression seem thus consistent regarding their other targets. Furthermore, the fepA mRNA possesses a long 5′UTR, which is also the case of other mRNAs controlled by multiple sRNAs such as csgD, flhDC or the rpoS mRNA, whose 5′UTRs are 148-, 198- and 567-nt long, respectively. Again, some of the fepA repressors, namely OmrA, OmrB, RybB and RprA, are also regulators of csgD (14–16), flhDC (18) or rpoS (72). The interaction between Hfq and the exceptionally long rpoS 5′UTR was analyzed in detail and Hfq was found to bind both an AAN repeated motif and a U-rich loop of the rpoS leader, resulting in a compact structure of the mRNA when in complex with Hfq and the exposure of the sRNA binding-site (73). Interestingly, fepA 5′UTR also presents both a U-rich loop in the vicinity of stem-loop II-b and successive (AAN) motifs in the TIR, i.e. in two regions that are required for control by the far5’UTR-dependent sRNAs (Figure 5 and S7). Hence, an exciting hypothesis is that Hfq could similarly contact these two sites of the fepA leader and, if true, understanding the outcomes for the sRNA regulation will be of great interest. Furthermore, studying in detail the interaction between Hfq and the RseX sRNA also seems appealing given the presence of U stretches on both the 5′ and 3′ sides of the RseX transcriptional terminator (Figure 6A), which could both play a role in contacting Hfq.

The csgD, flhDC, rpoS and fepA mRNAs thus provide examples of long 5′UTRs allowing control by multiple sRNAs. While there are numerous examples of mRNAs with shorter 5′UTRs targeted by sRNAs at their TIR and/or within their coding sequence downstream of the TIR, e.g. the OmpD porin gene in Salmonella (45,67,68,74,75), fepA, csgD and flhDC are so far the mRNAs with the most numerous sRNA regulations described in bacteria. In particular, fepA 5′UTR provides a functional and efficient hub for post-transcriptional regulation, in addition to the fepA TIR and coding region. Overall, this raises the question of whether mRNAs with long 5′UTRs are more represented among sRNA-regulated genes, or among genes with specific functions that require multiple regulatory pathways, such as membrane proteins genes for instance. More generally, whether a correlation exists between the length of mRNA 5′UTRs and the number of post-transcriptional regulators remains to be determined.
