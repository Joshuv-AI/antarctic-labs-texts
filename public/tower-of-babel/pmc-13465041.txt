TRIM71 suppresses cervical cancer progression by inhibiting Nectin4-mediated Wnt/β-catenin signaling
Authors: Tianyu Liu, Jian Chen, Bao Dai, Diqiao Du, Min Chen, Na Zhao, Huaxi Mai, Haolin Fan, Lin Lin, Yanchun Liang, Shuzhong Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTripartite motif-containing 71 (TRIM71), an RNA-binding E3 ubiquitin ligase, plays essential roles in malignant progression, but its function in cervical cancer (CC) remains unclear.MethodsTRIM71 expression was assessed in CC cell lines and clinical specimens using qRT-PCR, Western blotting, and immunohistochemistry. The clinical significance of TRIM71 was evaluated through correlation analyses and survival models. Functional assays in vitro and xenograft models in vivo were used to determine the biological roles of TRIM71. RNA-sequencing, RIP-sequencing, luciferase reporter assays, actinomycin D decay assays, and co-immunoprecipitation assays were performed to elucidate underlying mechanisms.ResultsTRIM71 expression was significantly reduced in CC cell lines and tumor tissues, and its low expression correlated with aggressive clinicopathologic features and poorer survival, identifying it as an independent prognostic factor. Functionally, TRIM71 overexpression impaired CC cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and cytoskeletal remodeling, whereas TRIM71 loss enhanced these malignant behaviors. In vivo, TRIM71 suppressed tumor growth, lung metastasis, and angiogenesis. Transcriptome analysis and molecular assays showed that TRIM71 inhibited Wnt/β-catenin signaling and Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). RIP-seq revealed Nectin4 as a direct TRIM71 target. TRIM71 bound the Nectin4 3’-UTR and reduced its mRNA stability without affecting ubiquitination. Rescue experiments demonstrated that Nectin4 was essential for TRIM71-mediated repression of Wnt/β-catenin signaling, as Nectin4 restoration or pathway activation reversed TRIM71’s inhibitory effects, whereas Nectin4 silencing or pathway inhibition negated the impact of TRIM71 knockout.ConclusionsTRIM71 may function as a tumor suppressor in CC by regulating Nectin4 expression and influencing Wnt/β-catenin signaling, EMT, tumor progression, and angiogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-026-00843-y.

Introduction

Cervical cancer (CC) remains a major global health challenge, ranking as the fourth most common malignancy and cause of cancer-related mortality among women worldwide [1, 2]. Although persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) is the primary etiological factor, HPV alone is insufficient to drive full malignant transformation [3]. Instead, cervical carcinogenesis, tumor progression, and therapeutic resistance arise through a complex interplay of genetic and epigenetic alterations, dysregulation of oncogenic signaling pathways, and aberrant expression of oncogenes and tumor suppressors [4–6]. Multiple signaling pathways become disrupted during CC progression. The HPV E6 and E7 oncoproteins are pivotal: E6 targets p53 for degradation, impairing DNA repair and apoptosis, whereas E7 inactivates pRb, leading to uncontrolled cell-cycle progression [7]. In addition, genetic variation within HPV oncogenes may influence cervical carcinogenesis and disease progression. Recent genomic analyses have revealed that specific HPV16 variants harbor polymorphisms within the E6 and E7 regions that are associated with differences in oncogenic potential and disease susceptibility [8]. Additional pathways frequently activated in CC include the PI3K/Akt/mTOR axis, which enhances proliferation and survival; the MAPK/ERK cascade, which fosters oncogenic signaling; and Wnt/β-catenin, Notch, Hippo, and JAK/STAT, which collectively promote proliferation, stemness, migration, and immune evasion [9]. Despite significant advances in screening, vaccination, and treatment, a substantial proportion of patients, particularly those with early-stage disease, still develop recurrence or metastasis. Therefore, defining novel molecular regulators and prognostic biomarkers remains imperative for improving the clinical management of CC.

Recent studies have highlighted the role of noncoding RNAs in CC progression. Circular RNAs, in particular, have emerged as important regulators of tumor development. CircRNA hsa_circ_0000021 promotes cervical cancer progression by modulating the miR-3940-3p/KPNA2 regulatory axis [10]. In addition, integrative genomic analyses have identified several candidate biomarkers and therapeutic targets in CC, including metabolism-related genes and their regulatory miRNAs [11]. In addition to molecular regulators, emerging studies have explored bioactive molecules derived from natural sources with potential anticancer activity against cervical cancer cells, suggesting alternative therapeutic strategies under investigation [12].

The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is essential for embryonic development and adult tissue homeostasis [13]. The pathway is activated when Wnt ligands engage Frizzled/LRP receptors, resulting in β-catenin stabilization and its subsequent nuclear translocation. In the nucleus, β-catenin drives transcription of genes that regulate cell proliferation, migration, and differentiation [14]. In CC, persistent high-risk HPV infection further alters this pathway. HPV16 E6 cooperates with the E3 ligase E6AP to stabilize β-catenin and enhance Wnt target gene transcription. E6AP alone can also stimulate Wnt signaling in an E3-dependent manner [15]. Although less well studied, HPV16 E7 may contribute to β-catenin accumulation through inhibition of the PP2A catalytic subunit, reducing β-catenin degradation [16]. Although Wnt/β-catenin signaling is frequently upregulated in CC, the upstream mechanisms controlling its activation are incompletely understood.

Tripartite motif-containing 71 (TRIM71), also known as LIN41, is a highly conserved member of the TRIM protein family and contains an NHL domain with distinct RNA-binding activity [17]. TRIM71 plays essential roles in embryonic development, stem cell maintenance, neural differentiation, and post-transcriptional regulation [18]. TRIM71 also plays important roles in human cancers, where its function appears to be highly context-dependent. In several malignancies, including non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), TRIM71 is frequently upregulated and associated with advanced disease and poor prognosis. TRIM71 can promote tumor growth by activating oncogenic pathways such as NF-κB in NSCLC, or by stabilizing let-7 target mRNAs in HCC through interactions with AGO2, thereby counteracting the tumor-suppressive let-7 miRNA family [18]. Conversely, in ovarian carcinoma, where TRIM71 is commonly downregulated, it functions as a tumor suppressor. TRIM71 curbs cellular proliferation and invasion via regulation of the Lin28B-let-7-HMGA2 axis and additionally mitigates tumor progression by facilitating the degradation or inactivation of mutant p53 and its associated target genes [19]. Although TRIM71 expression is significantly diminished in cervical and several other malignancies, its contribution to CC biology has not been defined [19].

In this study, we demonstrate that TRIM71 is significantly downregulated in CC and that its loss promotes tumor growth, metastasis, angiogenesis, and EMT through aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Mechanistically, we identify Nectin4 as a direct RNA target of TRIM71 and show that TRIM71 suppresses Nectin4 expression by binding its 3’-UTR, thereby restraining Nectin4-driven Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Functionally, restoring Nectin4 or activating Wnt signaling rescues the inhibitory effects of TRIM71, whereas Nectin4 silencing reverses the pro-tumorigenic phenotype induced by TRIM71 loss. Together, these findings uncover TRIM71 as a previously unrecognized suppressor of CC progression and suggest the TRIM71-Nectin4-Wnt axis as a potential therapeutic target for improving outcomes in CC.

Materials and methods

Patients and tissue specimens

A total of 118 patients with cervical squamous cell carcinoma who underwent radical hysterectomy and lymphadenectomy at the Department of Gynecology and Obstetrics, First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, between November 2000 and September 2010 were initially identified. After re-evaluation by two independent pathologists, 12 cases were excluded (7 due to damaged specimens and 5 due to initial misclassification), resulting in 106 early-stage cases (FIGO stage Ia2–IIa2) included in the final cohort. Comprehensive clinical and pathological data were prospectively collected and maintained in a dedicated database. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the interval from diagnosis to CC–related death or last follow-up, while disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to the first documented recurrence. Fresh normal cervical tissues and early-stage tumor specimens were obtained with written informed consent, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analyses. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, and all procedures adhered to REMARK guidelines for prognostic biomarker studies.

To further validate the clinical associations, survival analyses were performed using Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), in which 183 cases of stage Ia1–IIa2 CC were included.

Cell lines and cell culture

Seven human CC cell lines, including SiHa, CaSki, MS751, ME180, C33A, HeLa, and HeLa229, were obtained from ATCC. SiHa, HeLa, and ME180 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM); CaSki and HeLa229 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640; and C33A and MS751 cells were grown in Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (MEM). All media (Gibco BRL, Rockville, MD) were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (HyClone, Logan, UT) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (100 U/ml penicillin, 100 µg/ml streptomycin). Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.

RNA Isolation and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol® Reagent (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was synthesized from purified RNA using the Universal cDNA Synthesis Kit (Toyobo, Japan). qRT-PCR was performed on a 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) with the SYBR Green PCR Kit (TaKaRa, Japan). Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 1. β-actin served as the internal control, and all reactions were run in triplicate. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the comparative Ct (2−ΔΔCt) method for both tissue samples and cell lines.

Western blot

Cells and tissue samples were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, China). Protein concentrations were measured using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Roche Life Sciences, Switzerland). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA) and imaged using a ChemiDoc detection system (Bio-Rad, USA). GAPDH and β-actin served as the loading control. Each experiment was performed at least in triplicate. All antibodies were listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Immunohistochemistry

4 μm paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. After antigen retrieval, slides were blocked with 10% goat serum and incubated with the primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The following day, sections were treated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 30 min at room temperature. Signal detection was performed using DAB, and the reaction was terminated in distilled water once a brown coloration appeared. Immunohistochemical staining was evaluated using a semi-quantitative scoring system according to a previously validated protocol [20, 21]. Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted. IHC staining was evaluated based on both staining intensity (0 = none, 1 = weak, 2 = moderate, 3 = strong) and the percentage of positive cells (0 = none, 1 ≤ 30%, 2 = 30–60%, 3 = 60–100%). The sum of these two parameters yielded an immunoreactivity score ranging from 0 to 6. A score of < 4 was defined as low expression, whereas ≥ 4 indicated high expression. All antibodies were listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Generation of TRIM71-overexpressing and TRIM71-knockout cell lines

Lentiviral particles encoding full-length human TRIM71 and the corresponding negative control vector were purchased from GeneChem (Shanghai, China). Cells were transduced following the manufacturer instructions, and stable TRIM71-overexpressing clones were selected using puromycin (2 µg/mL).

TRIM71-knockout cell lines were generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. The lentiviral plasmids pLenti-Cas9-P2A-blast (Addgene #52962) and pLenti-sgRNA-Hygro (Addgene #62205), expressing spCas9 and sgRNA respectively, were obtained from the laboratories of Feng Zhang and Rene Maehr. The spacer of sgRNA targeting human TRIM71 is identical with previous report (NTERA-2 KO #1 gRNA 1) [22], and cloned into pLenti-sgRNA-Hygro.

Lentiviral particles were produced in HEK293T cells via co-transfection of pLenti-Cas9-blast or pLenti-sgRNA-Hygro with the packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pVSV-G using Lipofectamine 2000. Medium was replaced with Opti-MEM after 6 h, and viral supernatants were collected at 48 h, cleared by centrifugation, filtered (0.45 μm), and stored at − 80 °C.

Target cells expressing Cas9 and a TRIM71-targeting sgRNA were generated by sequential lentiviral transduction. First, cells were transduced with a lentivirus (pLenti-Cas9-P2A-blast) and selected with blasticidin (10 µg/ml, Solarbio, China), followed by transduction with pLenti-gTRIM71-Hygro and selection with hygromycin B (400 µg/ml, APExBIO). After selection, cells were cultured for one week to allow for maximal cleavage efficiency. To obtain monoclonal knockout cell lines, single-cell cloning was performed by limiting dilution (5 cells/mL) into 96-well plates. Wells containing a single colony, confirmed by microscopic inspection, were expanded. Genomic DNA from clonal lines was isolated using a commercial kit (Thermo Scientific). PCR amplicons were purified with VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme), and deep sequencing libraries were constructed and sequenced on an Illumina Nova 6000 platform (Annoroad, Beijing). CRISPR editing efficiency and indel profiles were analyzed using CRISPResso2 (v2.0.30). Candidate TRIM71-knockout clones were selected according to the following criteria: (1) confirmed single-cell origin; (2) biallelic disruptive indels or mutations predicted to generate frameshifts or premature stop codons; and (3) loss of TRIM71 expression verified by Western blot [23, 24]. All sgRNA sequences used in this study are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Cell viability and colony formation assays

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 1,000 cells per well. Three replicate wells were measured every 24 h, and absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a microplate reader.

For the colony formation assay, indicated cells were plated into 6-well plates at 500 cells per well and cultured for approximately two weeks. Formed colonies were fixed and stained with crystal violet, and the number of positive colonies in each well was counted and compared across groups.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Cells were pretreated with mitomycin-C (10 µg/mL) for 1 h at 37 °C to inhibit proliferation. For migration assays, 2 × 10⁴ cells suspended in 100 µL serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber of 8-µm pore transwell inserts (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ). After incubation, cells that migrated to the underside of the membrane were fixed with methanol for 15 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (KeyGEN Biotech) for 30 min.

For invasion assays, 2 × 10⁵ cells in 100 µL serum-free medium were added to Matrigel-coated inserts (50 µL Matrigel diluted 1:4 in serum-free medium; BD Biosciences, Bedford, MD). After 24 h at 37 °C, non-invading cells and Matrigel were gently removed. Invaded cells were then fixed and stained as described above. Cells in five randomly selected fields were counted using a DMI4000B inverted microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Xenograft tumor formation and metastasis models

For the subcutaneous xenograft model, 5 × 10⁶ cells were injected into the left dorsal flank of female BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old), maintained under SPF conditions. After 30 days, mice were euthanized and tumors were excised. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: Volume = (length × width²) / 2.

To evaluate metastatic potential, luciferase-labeled stable cell lines (MS751-Vec-luc vs. MS751-TRIM71-luc; SiHa-WT-Vec-luc vs. SiHa-KO-TRIM71-luc) were used to establish an intravenous metastasis model. Each mouse received 2 × 10⁶ cells in 150 µL via tail-vein injection. Beginning one week post-injection, metastatic progression was monitored weekly using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS). Mice were administered D-luciferin (0.15 mg/g, intraperitoneally) prior to imaging, and bioluminescent signals were captured using an IVIS Lumina II system (Xenogen, Hopkinton, MA).

After six weeks, mice were sacrificed, and lungs were harvested, fixed in 3.7% formaldehyde, and processed for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Pulmonary metastatic foci were evaluated and quantified by experienced pathologists.

RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (RIP-seq)

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) was performed using RNA Immunoprecipitation Kit (Merck Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. MS751-TRIM71-HA cells were lysed in the provided buffer, and 20% of the lysate was reserved as the input fraction. The remaining 80% was incubated with anti-HA antibody (10¹¹) to immunoprecipitate TRIM71-associated RNA (IP fraction). RNA from both input and IP samples was purified, and strand-specific RNA-seq libraries were generated. The libraries were enriched, quantified, and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform by LC Sciences (Hangzhou, China).

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed by BGI (Service ID:

F21FTSSCKF2175, Huada Gene, Wuhan, China). Total RNA was fragmented and mRNA was enriched using oligo(dT) magnetic beads, followed by first- and second-strand cDNA synthesis. The resulting double-stranded cDNA was purified, PCR-amplified to generate sequencing libraries, and subsequently sequenced on the BGIseq-500 platform.

Data analysis of RIP-seq and RNA-seq

Quality control of raw RIP-seq and RNA-seq reads was performed using standard preprocessing pipelines, including adapter trimming and removal of low-quality bases. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38), and gene-level counts were generated using the corresponding annotation files. Differential enrichment analysis for RIP-seq and differential expression analysis for RNA-seq were performed based on normalized read counts. For RIP-seq, significantly enriched transcripts were defined using an adjusted P-value < 0.05 and |log₂FC| ≥ 2 when comparing RIP samples with the corresponding input controls. For RNA-seq, differentially expressed genes were identified using an adjusted P-value < 0.05 and an absolute log₂ fold change (|log₂FC|) ≥ 2. The resulting gene lists were used for subsequent Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG enrichment analyses. KEGG pathway and GO enrichment analyses were performed by BGI using Dr. TOM, an in-house customized data mining and visualization platform. Dr. TOM integrates functional annotation, statistical testing, clustering, and pathway analysis to generate high-confidence enrichment results. Significantly enriched pathways and GO categories were identified according to BGI’s analytical criteria.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Luciferase reporter constructs (GeneChem, Shanghai, China) were generated containing either the wild-type Nectin4 3′-UTR fragment or a corresponding mutant sequence. These plasmids were transfected into HEK293T or MS751 cells alongside TRIM71-overexpression or control vectors. After 48 h of incubation, luciferase activity was quantified using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The specific WT and Mut fragment sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Immunofluorescence staining

For cytoskeletal visualization, F-actin staining was performed using rhodamine–phalloidin. Cells grown on glass coverslips were washed twice with pre-warmed PBS (37 °C) for 5 min each, fixed in 3.7% formaldehyde for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min, and blocked with 1% BSA for 15 min. F-actin was labeled with rhodamine–phalloidin (Ghost Pen Ring Peptide, Kaiji Biotech, China), followed by several PBS washes to remove unbound dye. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Roche Life Sciences, Switzerland).

For nuclear translocation analysis, cells were fixed and permeabilized as described above and incubated with primary antibodies against YAP or non-phospho-β-catenin (Proteintech, Wuhan, China), followed by Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antibodies (Proteintech, Wuhan, China).

For EMT marker detection, E-cadherin was examined by immunofluorescence. Cells were fixed and permeabilized as above, incubated with anti–E-cadherin antibody (Proteintech) overnight at 4 °C, and subsequently treated with Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antibody.

Fluorescence images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 900 laser scanning confocal microscope equipped with a 63× oil-immersion objective. Z-stack images were processed and analyzed using ZEN software.

Immunoprecipitation and ubiquitination assay

Cells were transfected with HA-ubiquitin (HanYi Biotech, China), myc-Nectin4, and/or flag-TRIM71 plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher). To inhibit proteasomal activity, cells were treated with MG132 (10 µM; Selleck) for 6 h prior to collection.

Cell lysates were prepared using IP lysis buffer (P0013, Beyotime) supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitors and 10 mM N-ethylmaleimide. After centrifugation, equal amounts of protein were incubated with anti-myc antibody (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) and Protein A/G magnetic beads (Beyotime) for immunoprecipitation. Immunoprecipitates and input samples were resolved by SDS–PAGE, transferred onto PVDF membranes, and probed with anti-HA, anti-myc, and anti-FLAG antibodies (Proteintech), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein signals were detected using ECL substrate (Beyotime) and quantified with ImageJ. All experiments were performed independently in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 20.0 (Chicago, IL, USA). Associations between TRIM71 expression and clinicopathological parameters were evaluated using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Prognostic factors for OS and DFS were assessed using Cox proportional hazards regression, with variables showing P < 0.05 in univariate analysis included in the multivariate model. Continuous data are presented as mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments, and differences between groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared by the log-rank test. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

TRIM71 is down-regulated in human cervical cancer tissues and cells

To explore the protein and mRNA expression of TRIM71 in CC, we first performed qRT-PCR and western blot analyses on seven human CC cell lines, including SiHa, CaSki, MS751, ME180, C33A, HeLa, and HeLa229. TRIM71 mRNA was significantly down-regulated in all CC cell lines compared with normal cervical tissues (NCT) (Fig. 1A). Consistent with this finding, TRIM71 protein was absent or significantly decreased in these CC cell lines, including several with strong invasive or metastatic traits such as MS751, CaSki, and ME180 (Fig. 1B).

We next assessed whether TRIM71 was also reduced in clinical CC samples. qRT-PCR analysis of 15 normal cervical tissues and 30 early-stage CC tissues showed significantly lower TRIM71 mRNA levels in tumors (Fig. 1C). Western blotting of twelve samples, which included six normal tissues and six early-stage CC tissues, also confirmed that TRIM71 protein was reduced in carcinoma types compared with normal tissues (Fig. 1D). Immunohistochemistry of 106 early-stage CC cases further demonstrated a significant decrease in TRIM71 expression in cervical squamous cell carcinoma relative to normal tissue (Fig. 1E; Table 1).

We next analyzed the GENT public bioinformatics database (http://gent2.appex.kr/gent2/), which also confirmed that TRIM71 (Affymetrix ID: 231310_at) was downregulated in CC tissues compared with normal cervical tissues (Fig. S1). Together, these findings showed that TRIM71 expression was consistently reduced in CC at both the mRNA and protein levels.

Low TRIM71 expression correlates with adverse clinical features and poor prognosis in early-stage cervical cancer

Early-stage CC patients were classified into either a low-expression group (IHC score < 4) or a high-expression group (IHC score ≥ 4) based on the expression of TRIM71. We then examined the relationship between TRIM71 expression and clinicopathologic characteristics. Low TRIM71 expression was significantly associated with several adverse features, including FIGO stage (P = 0.045), tumor size (P = 0.038), lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI) (P = 0.030), and pelvic lymph node metastasis (PLNM) (P = 0.029) (Table 1). To determine whether TRIM71 served as an independent prognostic factor, we performed univariate and multivariate analyses. TRIM71 expression (P = 0.002) and FIGO stage (P = 0.013) were both identified as independent predictors of OS (Supplementary Table 5). For DFS, TRIM71 expression (P = 0.001) and FIGO stage (P = 0.011) were also independent prognostic factors (Supplementary Table 6). Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests further showed that patients with low TRIM71 expression had shorter OS and DFS compared with those in the high-expression group (P = 0.021 and P = 0.016, respectively) (Fig. 1F). Consistent with these findings, survival analysis using the TCGA dataset also demonstrated that low TRIM71 expression was associated with poorer survival in early-stage CC (Fig. 1G). Altogether, these findings showed that TRIM71 expression was strongly associated with patient outcomes and could serve as a potential prognostic biomarker for early-stage CC.

TRIM71 inhibits proliferation, migration and invasion of cervical cancer cells

We further investigated the function of TRIM71 in CC cells. TRIM71 was overexpressed in MS751 cells using a lentiviral vector, with empty vectors as controls. Overexpression efficiency was verified by qRT-PCR and Western blot (Fig. S2A). We also generated TRIM71-knockout cells in another CC cell line with high endogenous TRIM71 using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. Successfuly knockout was confirmed by Western blot and next-generation sequencing, which revealed a high editing efficiency with an indel frequency of 98.62% (25,030 total reads) at the target locus (Fig. S2B-D).

CCK-8 and colony formation assays were used to evaluate the effect of TRIM71 on CC cell proliferation. MS751 cells overexpressing TRIM71 showed a markedly reduced proliferation rate and formed fewer colonies compared with MS751-Vec control cells. In contrast, SiHa-WT-Vec cells displayed lower proliferation and fewer colonies than SiHa-KO-TRIM71 cells, indicating that loss of TRIM71 enhanced cell growth (Fig. 2A and B).

To examine whether TRIM71 influenced the migratory and invasive abilities of CC cells, we performed transwell migration and invasion assays. In both assays, MS751 cells with TRIM71 overexpression showed a marked reduction in migration and invasion compared with vector-control cells. In contrast, TRIM71-knockout SiHa cells displayed substantially higher migration and invasion than their wild-type counterparts (Fig. 2C).

We further examined cytoskeletal organization using rhodamine-phalloidin staining. MS751-Vec and SiHa-KO-TRIM71 cells exhibited an elongated, spindle-like morphology with abundant, stretched F-actin fibers—features that resemble a mesenchymal phenotype. By comparison, MS751-TRIM71 and SiHa-WT-Vec cells displayed a more rounded, cobblestone-like appearance with reduced F-actin bundles, consistent with an epithelial-like phenotype (Fig. 2D). Together, these findings indicate that TRIM71 suppresses the proliferation, migration and invasion potentialities of CC cells.

TRIM71 inhibits tumor growth, metastasis, and angiogenesis in cervical cancer

To evaluate the effect of TRIM71 on tumor growth in vivo, we established xenograft models using MS751 cells overexpressing TRIM71 and SiHa cells with TRIM71 knockout in female BALB/c nude mice. After 30 days of continuous monitoring, tumors derived from the TRIM71-high groups (MS751-TRIM71 and SiHa-WT-Vec) grew markedly slower than those from their respective control groups (MS751-Vec and SiHa-KO-TRIM71). Consistently, the mean tumor volumes in the TRIM71-high groups were significantly smaller than those in the TRIM71-low groups (Fig. 3A and B).

To further investigate the role of TRIM71 in metastasis, a xenograft metastatic mouse model was generated, and lung metastases were monitored weekly by in vivo imaging. After 6 weeks, the lungs were harvested and processed for H&E staining. MS751-TRIM71 cells formed substantially smaller metastatic lesions compared with MS751-Vec controls (Fig. 3C). Histological evaluation also showed reduced pulmonary metastatic incidence in the TRIM71-high groups (MS751-TRIM71 and SiHa-WT-Vec), whereas the TRIM71-low groups (MS751-Vec and SiHa-KO-TRIM71) displayed more frequent and extensive lung metastases (Fig. 3D-E).

Together, these results indicate that TRIM71 overexpression suppresses both tumor growth and metastasis of CC in vivo.

To explore a possible association between TRIM71 and tumor angiogenesis, we examined microvessel density in CC tissues. Twenty tumors with high TRIM71 expression and twenty with low expression were selected. Immunohistochemistry for CD34 demonstrated that tumors with high TRIM71 showed significantly lower microvessel density than those with low TRIM71 (Fig. 3F), suggesting that TRIM71 may inhibit angiogenesis. To further investigate the angiogenic potential regulated by TRIM71, conditioned medium from MS751-TRIM71 or MS751-Vec cells was applied to HUVECs. conditioned medium from MS751-TRIM71 cells significantly inhibited HUVEC migration and impaired tube formation, producing fewer branches and shorter tube networks compared with conditioned medium from control cells (Fig. 3G–H). Together, these results demonstrate that TRIM71 suppresses CC tumor growth and angiogenesis and reduces the pro-angiogenic activity of tumor-derived secreted factors.

TRIM71 inhibits activation of Wnt/β-Catenin signaling and EMT in cervical cancer

To further explore the molecular mechanisms through which TRIM71 exerts its biological effects in CC, RNA-Seq analysis was performed on MS751 cells stably overexpressing TRIM71 (MS751-TRIM71) and their corresponding control cells (MS751-Vec). Differential expression analysis revealed a distinct transcriptional profile between the two groups (Fig. S3A). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses showed that the differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in pathways related to cell signaling, including the Wnt signaling pathway (Fig. S3B-F).

To determine whether TRIM71 regulates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in CC, we examined the expression of key pathway components in MS751-TRIM71 and SiHa-KO-TRIM71 cells. TRIM71 overexpression reduced the levels of active β-catenin, total β-catenin, and downstream targets including MMP-7 and Cyclin D1 [25], whereas TRIM71 knockout enhanced the expression of these proteins (Fig. 4A). These findings indicate that TRIM71 negatively regulates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. We next investigated whether TRIM71 also influences EMT. Western blot analysis showed that TRIM71 overexpression upregulated epithelial markers such as E-cadherin and ZO-1 while downregulating mesenchymal markers, including Twist, Snail, and N-cadherin. Conversely, TRIM71 knockout reversed these changes, promoting a mesenchymal-like phenotype (Fig. 4B). Immunofluorescence staining further supported these observations. MS751-TRIM71 and SiHa-WT-Vec cells exhibited reduced nuclear β-catenin accumulation compared with their corresponding control groups (Fig. 4C). In addition, higher E-cadherin membrane expression was observed in TRIM71-overexpressing cells, whereas TRIM71-deficient cells showed diminished E-cadherin staining (Fig. 4D). Together, these results indicate that TRIM71 suppresses Wnt/β-catenin signaling activity and inhibits EMT in CC cells.

TRIM71 directly binds and transcriptionally suppresses Nectin4 mRNA

TRIM71 possesses a C-terminal NHL domain known for its RNA-binding capacity [26]. To identify RNAs directly associated with TRIM71 in CC cells, RIP-Seq was performed in MS751 cells. The RIP-Seq data revealed that TRIM71-bound transcripts were predominantly protein-coding mRNAs, although lncRNAs and other RNA species were also detected (Fig. S4A). Gene Ontology and KEGG enrichment analyses demonstrated that TRIM71-associated RNAs were significantly enriched in pathways involved in RNA metabolic processes, mRNA processing, transcriptional regulation, and several cancer-related signaling pathways (Fig. S4B-C).

Mapping of TRIM71 RIP reads showed strong enrichment along the 3’-UTR region of Nectin4 mRNA, suggesting that TRIM71 directly regulates Nectin4 (Fig. 5A). Western blot analysis showed that TRIM71 overexpression markedly reduced Nectin4 protein levels in MS751 cells, whereas TRIM71 knockout increased Nectin4 expression in SiHa cells (Fig. 5B). Consistent with these protein-level changes, qRT-PCR results demonstrated that TRIM71 overexpression significantly decreased Nectin4 mRNA levels, while TRIM71 depletion significantly increased Nectin4 mRNA (Fig. 5C).

To further explore whether TRIM71 directly modulates Nectin4 mRNA stability, luciferase reporters containing either the wild-type Nectin4 3’-UTR or a mutated version were constructed (Supplementary Table 4). TRIM71 significantly suppressed luciferase activity of the wild-type 3’-UTR reporter but showed no effect on the mutant construct, suggesting a direct interaction with the Nectin4 3’-UTR (Fig. 5D). To further evaluate whether TRIM71 affects Nectin4 mRNA stability, Actinomycin D was used to block new mRNA synthesis. Actinomycin D treatment reduced Nectin4 mRNA levels, and this decrease was more pronounced in cells with higher TRIM71 expression (Fig. 5E), indicating that TRIM71 suppresses Nectin4 at the transcriptional level. To determine whether TRIM71 regulates Nectin4 through ubiquitin-mediated degradation, we performed a ubiquitination assay in 293T cells co-transfected with myc-tagged Nectin4 and HA-tagged ubiquitin. Nectin4 was immunoprecipitated using an anti-myc antibody, and ubiquitinated Nectin4 was detected using an anti-HA antibody. Overexpression of TRIM71 did not increase the ubiquitination of Nectin4, either in the presence or absence of the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (Fig. 5F). Taken together, these results indicate that TRIM71 reduces Nectin4 expression through a transcriptional regulatory mechanism, rather than through ubiquitin-proteasome–mediated degradation.

TRIM71 regulates Wnt/β-Catenin activity through Nectin4

To determine whether Nectin4 mediates the regulatory effects of TRIM71 on the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, we performed rescue experiments in MS751 and SiHa cells. Western blot analysis showed that TRIM71 overexpression in MS751 cells reduced active β-catenin and Nectin4 levels (Fig. 6A). However, co-overexpression of Nectin4 restored β-catenin activity suppressed by TRIM71. Likewise, treatment with the Wnt/β-catenin activator BML-284 also reversed the inhibitory effect of TRIM71 on the pathway. In contrast, TRIM71 knockout in SiHa cells increased β-catenin activation and Nectin4 expression. Knockdown of Nectin4 in TRIM71-deficient cells re-suppressed β-catenin activity, indicating that the pathway activation driven by TRIM71 loss depends on Nectin4. The Wnt/β-catenin inhibitor IWR-1 produced a similar inhibitory effect, while DMSO had no impact.

These molecular changes were reflected in cell behavior. In MS751 cells, TRIM71 overexpression significantly reduced cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Co-overexpression of Nectin4 rescued these phenotypes, restoring growth and motility. Activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway with BML-284 produced a similar rescue effect, whereas DMSO had no influence (Fig. 6B-C). In SiHa cells, TRIM71 knockout enhanced proliferation, migration, and invasion. Silencing Nectin4 in TRIM71-deficient cells abolished this increase, bringing cell behavior closer to baseline. Inhibition of Wnt/β-catenin signaling with IWR-1 produced the same suppressive effect, while DMSO treatment did not alter the phenotype (Fig. 6B-C). Together, these findings demonstrate that Nectin4 contributes to TRIM71-mediated regulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and the resulting changes in proliferation, migration, and invasion in CC cells.

Discussion

In this study, we identified TRIM71 as a previously unrecognized tumor suppressor in CC and described a regulatory mechanism in which TRIM71 may restrict tumor progression by modulating Nectin4-associated activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. We demonstrated that TRIM71 expression is markedly reduced in CC tissues and cell lines, and this downregulation strongly correlates with multiple adverse clinicopathologic features. Importantly, low TRIM71 expression independently predicted shorter overall and disease-free survival, indicating that TRIM71 may serve as a clinically relevant prognostic biomarker in early-stage CC.

Functional studies demonstrate that TRIM71 exerted robust tumor-suppressive effects in vitro and in vivo. TRIM71 overexpression inhibited CC cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and cytoskeletal remodeling, whereas loss of TRIM71 produced the opposite phenotypes. In ovarian tumors with mutant p53, TRIM71 acts as an E3 ligase that ubiquitinates and destabilizes the mutant protein, which neutralizes its oncogenic activity and limits cancer cell proliferation and invasion. This effect is specific to mutant p53 and does not occur in cells with wild-type p53 [27]. TRIM71 has also been shown to suppress tumorigenesis through the Lin28B-let-7-HMGA2 pathway, a conserved regulatory axis in development and cancer. Overexpression of TRIM71 counteracts Lin28B-induced transformation, decreases proliferation and invasion in NSCLC cells, and requires intact ubiquitin transfer activity to exert these effects [19]. However, TRIM71’s function is context dependent. In NSCLC, TRIM71 can also act as a tumor promoter, where it is upregulated, activates the NF-κB pathway, and correlates with larger tumors, lymph node metastasis, and poor prognosis [28]. In CC, our data support a predominantly tumor-suppressive function shaped by the unique molecular landscape of this disease. This context-dependent behavior may reflect the unique molecular environment of cervical cancer, which is strongly influenced by persistent HPV infection and viral oncoproteins such as E6 and E7 [29]. These viral factors extensively remodel host transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulatory networks, including p53- and pRb-dependent pathways, thereby altering the cellular targets and functional outcomes of RNA-binding proteins [29, 30]. In this setting, TRIM71 may preferentially regulate transcripts that restrain oncogenic signaling rather than promote it. Such HPV-driven molecular rewiring may help explain why TRIM71 exhibits tumor-suppressive activity in cervical cancer, in contrast to its oncogenic role reported in several other malignancies.

The Wnt/β-catenin pathway is a major driver of CC progression. Its activity can be enhanced by high-risk HPV infection, epigenetic silencing of APC, or disruption of the E-cadherin/β-catenin complex, and is consistently associated with aggressive disease and poor prognosis [31, 32]. Recent study also shows that lncRNA CALML3-AS1 promote CC growth and metastasis by upregulating β-catenin signaling [33]. Consistently, a comprehensive analysis of 109 CC patients revealed widespread dysregulation of the Wnt/β-catenin network, with many pathway components aberrantly expressed. The study also showed that while inhibitor C59 effectively suppress pathway activity, others inhibitor ICRT14 fail due to lncRNA-mediated resistance, such as HOTAIR binding to β-catenin and sustaining pathway activation [34]. In our study, transcriptomic analysis and subsequent biochemical validation revealed that TRIM71 negatively regulates this pathway. TRIM71 overexpression reduced active β-catenin and downregulated downstream targets including Cyclin D1 and MMP-7 [25], while reversing EMT marker expression. These findings indicate that TRIM71 suppresses CC progression, at least in part, by inhibiting Wnt/β-catenin signaling.

Nectin4 is an oncogenic cell adhesion molecule widely overexpressed in multiple cancers, where it promotes tumor growth, metastasis, EMT, angiogenesis and cancer stemness [35–38]. In NSCLC, Nectin4 enhances lamellipodia formation and Rac1 activation and serves as a highly sensitive serum and tissue biomarker associated with poor prognosis [39]. In gallbladder cancer, Nectin4 overexpression correlates with advanced stage, lymph node metastasis, and reduced survival, and promotes proliferation and motility through PI3K/Akt-dependent activation of Rac1 [40]. Nectin4 binds Nectin1 to promote intercellular adhesion and tumor cell clustering, and functions as a high-affinity ligand for the immune checkpoint receptor TIGIT, enabling cancer cells to suppress T-cell and NK-cell activity [41]. It also undergoes homophilic binding and, when cleaved by ADAM17, its soluble ectodomain activates endothelial integrin-β4 to stimulate angiogenesis [37]. In addition, Nectin4 has been reported to has been reported to form a cis-complex with the prolactin receptor, thereby enhancing JAK2 signaling through relief of SOCS1-mediated inhibition, further indicating its capacity to modulate diverse oncogenic signaling pathways [42]. Intracellularly, Nectin4 engages afadin to link adhesion complexes to the actin cytoskeleton and activate pathways such as Rac1 and PI3K/AKT that promote proliferation, migration, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in gallbladder cancer cells [40]. Nectin4 is aberrantly expressed in a subset of cervical cancers and has emerged as a promising antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) target, with large population-based analyses of 525 patients showing frequent and specific upregulation, particularly in squamous tumors [43]. Nectin4 is also the therapeutic target of the FDA-approved ADC enfortumab vedotin in urothelial carcinoma. Although most primary bladder tumors show high Nectin4 expression, recent evidence indicates that its levels can decline during metastatic progression, and reduced Nectin4 expression in metastatic lesions is associated with shorter progression-free survival on enfortumab vedotin [44]. A recent phase 1/2a study of the Nectin4-targeting ADC 9MW2821 further underscores the clinical importance of this molecule. Among 260 patients with Nectin4-positive solid tumors, 9MW2821 demonstrated good tolerability (RP2D 1.25 mg/kg) and promising antitumor activity, achieving a 35.3% objective response rate and 78.4% disease control rate in heavily pretreated urothelial, cervical, esophageal, and triple-negative breast cancers [45]. However, the functional role of Nectin4 in CC biology remains poorly defined. Our study identifies Nectin4 as a critical downstream effector through which TRIM71 suppresses CC progression.

Mechanistically, our RIP-seq analysis reveals that TRIM71 directly binds the 3’-UTR of Nectin4 mRNA, reducing its stability and protein expression. TRIM71 is known to regulate mRNA fate in a context-dependent manner. It can promote mRNA decay and translational repression by cooperating with factors such as those involved in nonsense-mediated decay, as seen with targets like CDKN1A [46]. Recent study shows that TRIM71 is a multifunctional RNA-binding protein capable of either repressing or stabilizing transcripts, depending on the target. Global profiling has identified key RNA targets, including MBNL factors, LIN28B, MDM2, and TCF7L2, and revealed that TRIM71’s regulatory versatility is shaped by its interaction partners, such as the stabilizing factor IMP1. Its RNA-binding NHL domain is essential for proper target recognition, as both domain deletion and disease-associated mutations disrupt binding [47]. In CC cells, TRIM71 acts as an mRNA repressor of Nectin4. TRIM71 overexpression reduces Nectin4 mRNA stability, whereas TRIM71 loss increases its expression, and luciferase assays confirm that this regulation is mediated specifically through the Nectin4 3’-UTR rather than through proteasomal degradation. These findings establish TRIM71 as a post-transcriptional suppressor of Nectin4 in CC.

Functionally, nectin4 appears to play an important role in TRIM71-associated regulation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling and tumor behavior. Restoring Nectin4 reversed TRIM71-induced inhibition of proliferation, migration, invasion, and β-catenin activity, whereas Nectin4 knockdown abolished the pro-tumorigenic effects of TRIM71 loss. Emerging evidence shows that Nectin4 promotes tumor progression in part by activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Although first identified as a junction protein, Nectin4 is now recognized as a marker of breast cancer stem cells, where it drives self-renewal, EMT, invasion, and metastasis through PI3K/Akt-dependent activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling [48]. Together, our results suggest that TRIM71 may function as a post-transcriptional regulator of Nectin4 expression and reveal the potential importance of the TRIM71-Nectin4-Wnt/β-catenin axis in CC progression.

This study has several limitations. The clinical cohort included only cervical squamous cell carcinoma samples, and cervical adenocarcinoma was not represented. Because these two histological subtypes can exhibit distinct molecular characteristics, the relevance of the TRIM71-Nectin4 regulatory axis in cervical adenocarcinoma remains to be determined. Future studies that include multiple histological subtypes of cervical cancer will help clarify the broader clinical significance of this pathway.

Our findings indicate that TRIM71 may suppress CC progression and suggest a regulatory framework in which TRIM71 limits oncogenic processes, at least in part, through the modulation of Nectin4 and Wnt/β-catenin signaling (Fig. 7). Given the growing clinical interest in Nectin4-targeted ADCs and the emerging recognition of dynamic Nectin4 expression as a mechanism of drug resistance, the TRIM71-Nectin4 axis may hold substantial translational relevance. Restoring TRIM71 activity or targeting the Nectin4-Wnt signaling may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for CC, particularly in tumors characterized by reduced TRIM71 and elevated Nectin4 expression.
