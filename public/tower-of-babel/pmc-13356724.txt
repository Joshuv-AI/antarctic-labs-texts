Microsurgical treatment of lower extremity lymphedema: An umbrella systematic review supporting the American Venous Forum and the American Vein and Lymphatic Society clinical practice guidelines on management of lymphedema
Authors: Peter Gloviczki, Monika Lecomte Gloviczki, Balazs Mohos, Alisha Oropallo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLymphovenous anastomosis (LVA) and vascularized lymph node transfer (VLNT) are established microsurgical options for chronic lymphedema. This umbrella systematic review synthesizes evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses to define indications for lymphatic microsurgery and inform forthcoming practice guidelines of the American Venous Forum and the American Vein and Lymphatic Society.MethodsA systematic search of multiple electronic databases identified English-language systematic reviews and meta-analyses published between 2010 and 2025 addressing microsurgical treatment or prevention of lower-extremity lymphedema (LEL). Eligible studies reported objective and/or patient-reported outcomes and addressed clinical questions prioritized by the American Venous Forum/the American Vein and Lymphatic Society Guideline Committee.ResultsTwenty-six systematic reviews, including 14 meta-analyses, were analyzed; no randomized controlled trials were identified. Twenty reviews evaluated treatment of LEL; 16 also included upper-extremity lymphedema. Six assessed immediate lymphatic reconstruction (ILR) following tumor excision with inguinal lymphadenectomy. Conclusions were primarily driven by four moderate-quality treatment reviews and one high-quality prevention review that satisfied all critical A MeaSurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews 2 domains. Due to substantial overlap of primary studies across reviews, findings were synthesized qualitatively, and effect estimates were not pooled to avoid double counting. Both LVA and VLNT were effective. LVA was more commonly performed in earlier disease stages. Limb volume/circumference reduction ranged from 34.16% [95% confidence interval (CI), 23.93-44.40] to 46.8% (95% CI, 43.2-50.4), with follow-up from 1 month to 8 years (Evidence C, low- to very-low quality). Postoperative cellulitis, reported in 12 reviews, decreased by 2.1 episodes/year after VLNT (95% CI, −2.7 to −1.4) and from an annual incidence of 0.84 to 0.07 following LVA (Evidence B, moderate quality). Symptoms or quality of life improved in 50% to 100% of patients across 17 reviews. No deaths or major complications were reported; minor complications were more frequent after VLNT. ILR reduced the risk of LEL by 30.3 per 100 patients treated compared with no ILR (risk difference, −30.3%; 95% CI, −46.5% to −14%; P<.001). Low-quality evidence from one review suggested possible association between ILR and worse oncologic outcomes in lower-extremity melanoma and squamous cell carcinoma.ConclusionsEvidence supports LVA for both primary and secondary LEL when functional lymphatic channels are identified, irrespective of disease stage. VLNT may provide greater benefit in advanced disease but is associated with higher morbidity. ILR is supported for the prevention of LEL following pelvic or truncal oncologic surgery with iliofemoral lymphadenectomy but should likely be avoided in extremity dermatologic malignancies. Guideline recommendations should emphasize shared decision-making and incorporate patient preferences, procedural cost, available technology, and surgeon expertise.Keywords: Lymphedema, Microsurgery, Lymphovenous anastomosis, Vascularized lymph node transfer, Lower extremity, Indocyanine green lymphography, Quality of life, Cellulitis, Prevention

Take Home Message: Microsurgical treatment and prevention of lower-extremity lymphedema are effective, safe, and durable. The evidence synthesized in this review informs the American Venous Forum and American Vein and Lymphatic Society Guideline Committee in the development of clinical recommendations for lymphedema management.

Lymphedema is a chronic, progressive, and debilitating disease that develops when interstitial fluid filtration exceeds the transport capacity of the lymphatic system. Contemporary evidence supports a broader and clinically consequential concept: any edema persisting beyond 3 months constitutes lymphedema, as lymphatic drainage is relatively insufficient in all cases of sustained tissue fluid overload.1,2 This paradigm shift has had profound implications for diagnosis, classification, and management of chronic edema.

Primary lymphedema affects approximately 1.15 per 100,000 individuals under 20 years of age3 and may be congenital or present later in life as lymphedema praecox or tarda.4 Affected patients harbor genetic or idiopathic functional, and/or structural abnormalities of lymphatic vessels or the lymph conducting elements of lymph nodes. Secondary lymphedema develops following a defined insult to the lymphatic system, including malignancy, lymphadenectomy, radiation, infection, trauma, or parasitic disease. Worldwide, filariasis is the most common cause, with an estimated 14 million individuals affected with secondary lymphedema; however, its global prevalence has declined substantially in recent decades due to effective eradication programs.5,6 In oncologic populations, approximately 14.9% of patients undergoing lymphadenectomy develop late-onset lymphedema.5 Among 26,902 patients with chronic edema, cancer was the leading etiology (32.1%), followed by chronic venous insufficiency (CVI, 10.4%); however, CVI was the most frequent cause of lower-extremity lymphedema (LEL).7

Current guidelines recommend conservative therapy as first-line treatment of lymphedema.2,4,8 This includes complex decongestive therapy (CDT) using compression bandages, garments or adjustable compression wraps, skin care, exercise, manual lymphatic drainage,9,10 and sequential pneumatic compression.11

Surgical strategies may be broadly categorized as excisional procedures, including suction-assisted lipectomy (SAL), and physiologic reconstructions.12,13 The concept of physiologic lymphatic reconstruction dates to 1955, when Brewer first performed lymphovenous anastomosis (LVAs) for a chylous leak following tumor excision.14 Early clinical success with lymph node-to-vein anastomosis was reported by Nielubowicz and Olszewski in 1968.15 The advent of microsurgery, pioneered by Jacobson in the 1960s, enabled experimental and clinical application of microsurgical LVAs.16, 17, 18, 19, 20 In 1969, Yamada21 reported successful LVA using an operating microscope. Subsequently, O'Brien et al19,22 published on extensive experience and Gloviczki et al23 demonstrated clinical improvement in 57% of patients with secondary lymphedema. Supermicrosurgery is characterized by high-magnification optics, refined instrumentation, ultrafine sutures, intraoperative lymphatic imaging and involves the anastomosis of vessels with a lumen below 0.8 mm in diameter. It was pioneered by Koshima et al24,25 and led to renewed interest in lymphatic reconstructions.26, 27, 28, 29

Over the past two decades, supermicroscopic lymphatic surgery has undergone a substantial renaissance, expanding the safety, precision, and clinical applicability of both LVA30,31 and vascularized lymph node transfer (VLNT).32, 33, 34, 35 These advances have not only enhanced therapeutic efficacy but have also enabled preventive strategies for lymphedema, like using the lymphatic microsurgical preventive healing approach (LYMPHA).36, 37, 38, 39, 40 Robotic microsurgery is poised now to further transform the surgical management paradigm of lymphedema.39

In this umbrella systematic review, we synthesize contemporary evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses to define current indications for microsurgical treatment and prevention of LEL.

Methods

An umbrella systematic review is a relatively recent methodological approach that provides a comprehensive overview and rapid synthesis of evidence derived from multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses on a broad topic.41, 42, 43 It represents the highest level in the hierarchy of evidence synthesis methods.41,44 This umbrella systematic review was conducted under a prespecified protocol developed by the guideline committee of the American Venous Forum and the American Vein and Lymphatic Society to directly inform clinical practice guidelines for the management of lymphedema. The study is exempted from Institutional Review Board approval because it does not involve the participation of human beings. The review was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 statement,45 it was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews database (CRD420251274447).46

The primary outcomes for LEL treatment were change in limb volume and/or circumference and change in episodes of cellulitis/lymphangitis. Percentage reductions refer to reductions in excess limb volume or circumference relative to baseline or the contralateral limb, unless otherwise specified. Secondary outcomes included patient-reported outcomes, including pain and validated general or disease-specific quality of life (QoL) measures, major and minor complications, and postoperative need for compression garments. Comparative outcomes between LVA and VLNT, outcomes in patients with cancer related, primary and post-traumatic lymphedema and effectiveness to prevent LEL with immediate lymphatic reconstructions (ILRs) following tumor excision and lymphadenectomy were also assessed. Pediatric patients were excluded from the analysis.

The primary outcome for prevention of lymphedema was the incidence of lymphedema after ILR. The secondary oncological outcomes were distant-metastasis-free survival (DMFS), recurrence-free survival (RFS), and overall survival (OS).

Literature review and search Strategy

PubMed, Embase, Medline (Ovid), Clinical Trials.gov, and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews were searched between 2010 and 2025. Studies found among references of reviews were added. Manuscripts were systematically identified, screened, and English-language systematic reviews and meta-analyses evaluating LVA and/or VLNT for LEL were selected.

Quality assessment

Heterogeneity, risk of bias, and publication bias were evaluated using multiple methods reported in the systematic reviews. We extracted if heterogeneity was quantified with the I2 statistic, with values >50% indicating substantial heterogeneity. We also extracted whether risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale,50 the Risk of Bias in Nonrandomized Studies of Interventions,51 the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine,52 or Methodological Quality Assessment Tool.53 We audited if publication bias was assessed using funnel plot inspection and Egger's regression test.54

Methodological quality and confidence in the reported outcomes were assessed using A MeaSurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews 2 (AMSTAR 2).55 Overall confidence was rated as high, moderate, low, or critically low based on the critical methodological domains. Risk of bias due to missing results was assessed using AMSTAR-2 item 15. Overlap of primary studies across systematic reviews was evaluated with a citation matrix and calculated with the corrected-covered area.56 Overlap was classified as slight (0%-5%), moderate (6%-10%), high (11%-15%), or very high (>15%). Our a priori criteria for prioritizing effect estimates included the highest AMSTAR-2 rating and LEL-only data.

Evidence grading

Evidence grading was based on systematic reviews and conducted in accordance with the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation methodology, consistent with Society for Vascular Surgery guidelines.57,58 Evidence quality was classified as A (high), B (moderate), or C (low to very low), reflecting the level of confidence in the estimated treatment effect. High-quality (Grade A) evidence indicates strong confidence in the effect estimate, with further research unlikely to change conclusion, moderate-quality (Grade B) evidence indicates that further research will likely impact confidence in estimate of effect, whereas low-quality (Grade C) evidence indicates substantial uncertainty, with further research very likely to influence the estimate. Systematic reviews of Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs) represent high level of evidence, and reviews based on observational studies or case series are rated as moderate, low, or very low quality.

Overlap of primary studies on treatment across the reviews was slight (5.9%), bordering on moderate, but it was very high among those performed with strict methodological rigor (Supplementary Table II, online only). Overlap between Ward et al32 and Meuli et al69 was 78%, 37% between Kong et al68 and Meuli et al,69 and 38% between Hahn et al72 and Meuli et al.69 Overlap of 21 primary studies on prevention was very high (20%) (Supplementary Table III, online only). Given the substantial overlap and the lack of assessments of risk of bias in several studies, findings were interpreted qualitatively, with priority given to reviews with higher methodological confidence and a narrower clinical focus. To avoid double counting, effect estimates across systematic reviews were not pooled. Data of the included reviews had high heterogeneity when volume or circumference was measured (Table III). Results were analyzed based on seven PICO questions.

Question 1. Outcomes of microsurgical treatment compared with conservative management of LEL

No RCTs were identified in the systematic reviews on microsurgical treatment for LEL. The 20 reviews included 840 studies on both UEL and LEL. (Tables II and III). There were 278 primary studies on LEL (Supplementary Table II, online only).

Patient demographics

Table II displays the demographic data, where reported. The largest systematic review by Meuli et al69 included 150 primary studies with 6496 patients with lymphedema, 52% patients with LEL.69 Another review31 included 6260 patients and 2554 LELs. Mean age of the patients was 54.13 years (range: 22.6-76.1).31 In our umbrella review, age ranged from 18 to 94 years. Most patients with secondary UEL were females, the ratio of females in our LEL cohort ranged from 42% to 91%.

Disease characteristics

Two systematic reviews were only on LEL, 18 analyzed data in both UEL and LEL (Tables II and III). Secondary LEL was included in 18 reviews, two of these dealt with cancer-related lymphedema only,32,60 another with post-traumatic lymphedema.71 Two systematic reviews focused on primary lymphedema,67,70 but primary lymphedema patients were included in 15 other systematic reviews.

Patient selection for surgical treatment

Preoperative and intraoperative near-infrared fluorescence imaging using indocyanine green (ICG) lymphangiography has become the imaging modality of choice for identifying functioning lymphatics suitable for LVA.93,94 ICG lymphangiography was used to detect the lymph ducts in 75% of the cases reviewed by Verhey et al.31 Near-infrared light (approximately 780 nm) penetrates up to 2 cm into the subcutaneous tissue, and the technique allows visualization of lymphatic vessels and nodal basins and demonstrates active lymphatic contractility.93 In earlier studies, isotope lymphoscintigraphy was used most frequently for assessing the lymphatic system, as reported by Mihara et al95 and Gloviczki et al.23 In the systematic review by Dahl et al,71 preoperative imaging included ICG lymphangiography in 11 studies, magnetic resonance lymphangiography in two studies, and isotope lymphoscintigraphy in two studies. Intraoperatively, eight studies used ICG lymphography, and one study used high-frequency ultrasound study (18 Hz) to identify lymphatic vessels.71

Disease stage

Two classification systems were used mostly to define clinical stage (Table I). Considerable heterogeneity was observed across studies (Table II). Most patients undergoing LVA had early International Society of Lymphology (ISL) Stage I or Stage IIa disease. Patients treated with VLNT more commonly had advanced Stage IIb or Stage III disease. Patients undergoing VLNT frequently required additional excisional procedures or SAL to address fibrotic and adipocutaneous tissue, either concomitantly or as staged procedures.85

Microsurgical techniques

The supermicrosurgical techniques of LVAs were described previously.25,26,28 Procedures are typically performed under local anesthesia. ICG or blue dye is injected subdermally distal to the planned incision sites to visualize lymphatic vessels. Small transverse incisions are made along the limb to dissect lymphatic vessels and adjacent subdermal veins. Depending on the vascular anatomy, end-to-end, end-to-side, side-to-end, or side-to-side anastomoses are performed under high magnification (×20 to ×30) using 11-0 or 12-0 monofilament sutures. Additional anastomosis types, such as Y-shaped, K-shaped, λ-shaped, π-shaped or “double barrel” LVAs have also been described.13,96 An intravascular stent may be used to facilitate anastomosis and is removed before placement of the final suture.26

VLNT consists of a free flap containing autologous lymph nodes transplanted to the lymphedematous limb to enhance lymphatic drainage and stimulate lymphangiogenesis.97 For LEL, donor sites include the submental, supraclavicular, lateral thoracic, or omental regions.32,62 The recipient site can be proximal, at the groin, or distal, at the level of the ankle.13

Primary outcomes

Volume/circumference outcomes were reported in 18 reviews, all demonstrating significant improvement (Table III). Meuli et al69 synthesized the contemporary literature on microsurgical treatment of lymphedema. This meta-analysis included 1002 patients from 29 studies, comprising 20 studies evaluating VLNT, 8 evaluating LVA, and 1 combining both techniques.69 The pooled reduction in excess circumference was −35.6% (95% CI, −30.9 to −40.3), with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 44.3%; 95% CI, 13.5%-64.1%). The pooled reduction in excess limb volume, reported in 587 patients across 12 studies, was −32.7% (95% CI, −19.8 to −45.6).

Hahn et al72 noted comparable results for LEL, with pooled clinical improvement (excess limb volume or circumference) of 34.16% (95% CI, 23.93-44.40). Ward et al.32 reported circumferential reduction rate (CRR) of 46.8% (95% CI, 43.2-50.4) at a mean follow-up of 19.2 months. Superior outcomes were observed when gastroepiploic lymph nodes were used as donor flaps compared with submental donor sites and when patients adhered to a postoperative compression protocol.72 Verhey et al31 analyzed outcome in 2554 patients with LEL who underwent LVA. Rates of improvement ranged from 23.3% to 100%, with a mean limb volume reduction of 22.67% and excess volume reduction of 45.52%.

Postoperative cellulitis incidence was assessed in 12 systematic reviews, all demonstrating improvement (Table III). Ward et al32 reported a mean reduction of 2.1 cellulitis episodes/year (95% CI, −2.7 to −1.4). Verhey et al31 observed that the annual incidence decreased from 0.84 to 0.07 following LVA. Kong et al68 reported that 96% of the patients were free from postoperative cellulitis. A meta-analysis of eight studies (five VLNT and three LVA) by Meuli et al69 found that the pooled reduction in cellulitis was −1.9 episodes/year (95% CI, −1.4 to −2.3), with no observed heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%; 95% CI, 0.0-67.6%). This review reported on data combined for UEL and LEL.

Secondary outcomes

PROMs were reported in 15 reviews, with substantial heterogeneity across instruments and outcome domains (Table III).63,66 The most frequently used questionnaires were the Lymphedema Quality of Life Measure for Limb Lymphedema (LYMQOL)98 and the Lymphoedema Functioning, Disability and Health Questionnaire for Lower Limb Lymphoedema.99 LYMQOL, which demonstrates the strongest psychometric properties was used by Grunherz et al.66 Improvement in QoL was reported in six studies, although the magnitude of change varied widely. Mean preoperative-to-postoperative differences ranged from 2.0 to 5.3 for overall score, with large standard deviations across pooled estimates. Greater QoL improvements were observed in patients undergoing VLNT (range: 4.1-5.3) vs LVA (range: 2.0-2.6).

The largest gains in function, symptoms, and mood were reported in 10 patients by Ciudad et al85 following double gastroepiploic VLNT. LYMQOL demonstrated a 2.7-fold improvement in overall QoL (P < .01),85 whereas most patients continued some form of conservative therapy after intervention, in one meta-analysis, 63% of the patients discontinued CDT after surgery.68 In another meta-analysis by Basta et al,59 postoperative compression was not recommended in 7 out of 21 studies and ultimately 64.8% of patients discontinued using compression garments.

Complications were infrequent, and no deaths or major complications were reported (Table III). Minor complications included seroma, lymphatic leak, wound infection, or dehiscence, partial skin graft or flap loss, transient ileus, skin paddle congestion, and venous thrombosis.32 In one review, the incidence of minor complications was 1% following LVA and 13% following VLNT.70

Question 2. Outcomes of microsurgical treatment compared with conservative management of cancer-related LEL

Most patients in 17 systematic reviews had secondary LEL after cancer treatment (Tables II to III). Ward et al.32 reported outcomes in 105 LELs following gynecologic (n = 69), urologic (n = 6), or skin cancer treatment (n = 14), (not specified in 16), all treated with VLNT. At a mean follow-up of 19.2 months (standard deviation 12.3), the above-knee CRR was 46.8% (95% CI, 43.2-50.4; I2 = 92.4). VLNT reduced the mean number of cellulitis episodes by 2.1 episodes/year (95% CI, −2.7 to −1.4; I2 = 81.3) and significantly improved lymphedema-specific QoL outcomes, with a mean increase of 4.26 points in the LYMQOL overall domain.

Question 3. Outcomes of microsurgical treatment compared with conservative management of post-traumatic LEL

A focused systematic review included 112 patients with post-traumatic lymphedema, 73 had LEL.71 Ten patients were treated with LVA, 29 with VLNT. All surgically treated patients experienced resolution of symptoms, reduction in excess limb volume, and reduced number of postoperative cellulitis episodes. There was significant heterogeneity among the studies.

Question 4. Outcomes of microsurgical treatment compared with conservative management of primary LEL

Seventeen systematic reviews included patients with primary lymphedema, almost all had LEL (Tables II and III), two focused exclusively on primary disease.67,70 In one review, 177 patients underwent LVA and 82 patients underwent VLNT.70 All but one study reported improvement in clinical outcomes. Both LVA and VLNT were associated with symptomatic improvement and enhanced QoL in up to 90% of patients, with a mean circumference reduction of approximately 61%. Postoperative cellulitis rates decreased by 25% after LVA and 40% after VLNT. Most included studies were case series or retrospective cohort studies with low-to-moderate risk of bias. Fallahian et al67 evaluated 357 microsurgical procedures (88% were LVAs) in patients with primary lymphedema. Regardless of the type of intervention, all studies demonstrated significant improvement in lymphedema outcomes, including reductions in limb circumference or edema volume, decreased postoperative cellulitis, and improved QoL.

Question 5. Comparative outcomes of LVA vs VLNT for LEL

No RCT has directly compared outcomes between LVA and VLNT (Table VI). In a systematic review Hahn et al.72 compared outcomes in 775 patients with LEL, who underwent LVA with outcomes in 72 patients, who had VLNT. Improvement was 31.87% (95% CI, 18.60-45.14) for LVA and it was 39.53% (95% CI, 19.37-59.69) for VLNT (P = .59). However, lower extremities reconstructed with gastroepiploic lymph node flaps (vascularized omentum lymph node transfer) demonstrated greater clinical improvement (43.48%, 95% CI, 20.43-66.52) vs those reconstructed using submental lymph node flaps (19.80%, 95% CI, 16.75-22.85; P = .005). There was substantial heterogeneity across studies (I2 = 100%, P < .001). No differences in outcomes were observed based on lymphedema severity or the number of LVAs performed.

Question 6. Outcomes of ILR for prevention of LEL following tumor excision and lymphadenectomy.

Brown et al38 analyzed data of 460 patients who underwent ILR following tumor excision and lymphadenectomy. The mean follow-up was 20.6 ± 4.8 months. ILR was associated with a significantly lower incidence of lymphedema compared with no ILR (9%; 95% CI, 1%-16% vs 29%; 95% CI, 11%-46%). Patients who underwent ILR had 69% lower odds of developing lymphedema than those who did not [odds ratio (OR), 0.31; 95% CI, 0.19-0.51). However, two RCTs included in this review evaluated upper-extremity procedures only, with ILR performed in patients with postmastectomy.37,100

For prevention of LEL, ILR was performed in 67 patients in one prospective study with retrospective controls101 and in three retrospective studies.102, 103, 104 Boccardo et al.102 treated 11 patients with vulvar cancer and 16 patients with truncal melanoma with the LYMPHA technique with multiple LVAs. One melanoma patient developed transient lymphedema, another with vulvar cancer developed permanent lymphedema. The controlled study by Alarcón et al101 also supported ILR for reducing the risk of postlymphadenectomy lymphedema. In their study, lymphedema developed in 37.0% (10/27) of patients in the retrospective control group compared with 10.5% (2/19) in the prospective ILR group (P = .029) at a median follow-up of 14.2 months (range: 6-33 months). Ciudad et al.75 reported that ILR reduced the risk of LEL by 30.3 cases per 100 patients treated compared with no ILR (risk difference, −30.3%; 95% CI, −46.5% to −14%; P < .001).

Question 7. Outcomes of ILR for prevention of LEL following excision of extremity dermatologic malignancies and lymphadenectomy

A systematic review by Zaidi et al77 focused on the oncologic outcomes of ILR. One included primary study by Chungsiriwattana et al105 was a retrospective review of 29 patients with melanoma or squamous cell carcinoma of the lower extremities who underwent tumor resection with groin lymph node dissection. Seven patients underwent ILR, 22 served as controls, with a median follow-up of 7 years. LEL developed in three patients after ILR and in 12 controls (P = .68). At 5 years, the ILR group had lower OS (14.3% vs 54.5%; P = .035), lower RFS (28.6% vs 68.2%; P = .013), and distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS) (0% vs 70%; P = .003). In contrast, a retrospective study of prospectively collected data by Xia et al106 included 23 patients who underwent LVA and 22 controls, all with cutaneous melanoma. After a follow-up of ≥2 years, no differences were observed between groups in OS, DMFS, or RFS.

Discussion

Evaluation and treatment of chronic lymphedema have advanced substantially over the past decades, driven by improvements in imaging,107,108 conservative therapy, and supermicrosurgical techniques.13 During the last decades near-infrared fluorescence imaging with ICG lymphangiography93 and high or ultrahigh-frequency ultrasound study109 have enabled accurate identification of functional lymphatics and optimized patient selection for supermicrosurgical reconstruction.

Cohort studies, systematic reviews, and guidelines recommend CDT as first-line therapy to reduce limb volume, lower cellulitis frequency, and improve QoL.9, 10, 11,110,111 A Delphi consensus suggested that surgery should be considered for patients with higher than the second ISL stage only if conservative treatment failed.2 A minimum of 3 to 6 months of supervised conservative therapy has been the generally accepted period by most centers before surgical treatment (Table VI). Failure of CDT or optimization of conservative therapy were included as indications of surgery in most studies (Tables II and VI).

Although several consensus statements2,4,65 and practice guidelines112, 113, 114 addressing lymphedema management have been published, a systematic review by O'Donnell et al115 demonstrated that the methodological quality of many existing guidelines113,114,116,117 was low. In response, the American Venous Forum and the American Vein and Lymphatic Society collaborated to develop new evidence-based practice guidelines. To directly inform these recommendations, this umbrella systematic review applied prespecified PICO questions to synthesize evidence on clinically meaningful outcomes following microsurgery for the treatment and prevention of LEL (Table VII).

Main findings

No RCTs were identified in the 20 systematic reviews, and studies with direct comparison of surgical vs conservative treatment are limited (Table VI). Most major series, however, either explicitly required, or strongly implied optimized conservative therapy before microsurgery. Many primary studies include the minimum period of supervised conservative therapy before surgery, ranging from 1-2 weeks to ≥2 years (Table VI.) Baseline measurements after CDP and before surgery served as self-controls after a period of supervised conservative therapy that were compared to postsurgical measurements.

Based on this umbrella review, microsurgical lymphatic reconstructions provide clinically meaningful benefit [volume/circumference reduction or decrease of cellulitis episodes or improved QoL in patients with LEL (Level of Evidence: B, moderate)]. Evidence is moderate for decrease of cellulitis and low to very low for volume/circumference measurements and QoL. Certainty was downgraded due to the absence of RCTs and the low number of direct comparative studies with conservative therapy (Table VI), substantial heterogeneity, limited risk-of-bias assessment, and high overlap among moderate-quality reviews. Our umbrella review found, however, that 18 of 20 systematic reviews consistently reported that LVA and VLNT are safe, effective, and durable, with meaningful reductions in limb volume or circumference. Significant decrease of cellulitis was recorded in 11 systematic reviews.

Moderate-to-low quality evidence supports our conclusions. Meuli et al.69 reported mean reductions of 33% in excess limb volume and circumference, and a decrease of cellulitis per year by − 1.9 (95% CI, −1.4 to −2.3), with an estimated between-study heterogeneity variance at τ2 = 0, and an I2 value of 0.0% (95% CI, 0.0%-67.6%)]. Verhey et al,31 analyzing 2554 patients with LEL, reported improvement rates after LVA ranging from 23.3% to 100%, with mean limb volume and excess volume reductions of 23% and 45%, respectively. Kong et al.68 demonstrated that at a mean follow-up of 31 months, LVA or VLNT reduced limb circumference by 45%, enabled 63% of patients to discontinue physiotherapy, and resulted in 96% of patients remaining free from cellulitis. Forte et al.30 confirmed improvement in limb volume or circumference in 57 of 58 studies following LVA, with greater reductions observed in patients undergoing five to eight anastomoses. Favorable outcomes after vascularized omentum lymph node transfer were also reported.25 Clinical stage did not adversely affect outcomes in a primary study by Mihara et al.95 A retrospective study of Shimbo et al89 compared outcomes of conservative treatment in 53 limbs with LVA in 19 (Table VI). The LEL index at 2 years decreased by 16.2% (P=.019) after surgery, the change in the conservative group was nonsignificant (+3.1%; P = .299). In a similar comparative study Koshima et al24 noted that at 1.5 years leg circumference decreased in 8 of 13 patients after LVA, by a mean 4.7 cm and by 0.6 cm only after conservative treatment, in 1 of 12 patients (P < .05) (Table VI). The reduction in postoperative cellulitis reported across systematic reviews31,32,68 was recently confirmed in an RCT not included in these reviews.91 At 6 months, cellulitis incidence was significantly lower in 336 patients treated with LVA plus conservative therapy compared with conservative therapy alone.91

Most studies emphasize the need for continuing postoperative compression using bandages, compression garments, manual lymph drainage and massage, with exercise and skin care.22,27,118 Compression therapy in general is initiated 1 month after surgery, and it is supervised at least for 3 months. Hahn et al72 reported on significantly larger clinical improvement in patients who followed a prespecified protocol of compression therapy or manual lymphatic drainage after surgery than in those who had a restrictive protocol prohibiting patients from actively receiving therapy [34.23% (95% CI, 21.13-47.33) vs 19.80% (95% CI, 16.75-22.85)] (P < .0001). Akita et al80,81 found that in 44.8% of patients with LVA with early-stage lymphedema daily compression therapy was not required after 6 months, but in a study of 64 patients with advanced stage lymphedema, all patients with LVA needed long-term compression therapy (Table VI).

Patients with cancer-related LEL benefit from microsurgical treatment compared with conservative management (Level of Evidence: B, moderate). Of the clinically meaningful outcomes, effect on cellulitis has moderate, on volume/circumference, low to very low evidence. In 17 of 20 systematic reviews, most included patients had secondary lymphedema following cancer therapy. Ward et al32 demonstrated significant clinical benefit after VLNT in this population, including a 47% CRR, a reduction in postoperative cellulitis, and improvement in QoL.

Patients with post-traumatic LEL also appear to benefit from lymphatic microsurgery. Reported outcomes include reductions in limb swelling, symptom burden, and cellulitis episodes. However, the available evidence is very low and derived from 18 retrospective primary studies with substantial heterogeneity, without direct comparison to conservative management.71

Patients with primary LEL119 may also benefit from lymphatic reconstruction. Two systematic reviews67,70 provided low-quality evidence supporting the use of LVA or VLNT in primary lymphedema, reporting reductions in limb volume or circumference, fewer episodes of cellulitis, and improved QoL.

Both LVA and VLNT are effective treatment options for patients with LEL (Level of Evidence: B, moderate). No RCT has directly compared outcomes between LVA and VLNT. Direct comparison is inherently challenging because patient populations differ substantially. LVA requires identification of patent, functioning lymphatic vessels and is therefore most performed in patients with early ISL Stage I or IIa disease. In contrast, VLNT is typically reserved for patients with more advanced stages of secondary LEL or in those with primary lymphedema, in whom functional lymphatic channels are absent.

Hahn et al72 compared outcomes of LVA and VLNT in retrospective studies including 847 patients with LEL. In meta-analysis, clinical improvement was numerically higher following VLNT (39%) than LVA (32%), although this difference was not statistically significant, likely due to high heterogeneity. The study reporting the greatest circumference reduction (68%) included a concomitant debulking procedure in addition to VLNT.85 After excluding this study in sensitivity analysis, the pooled mean clinical improvement following VLNT was 34%, similar to LVA. Lower extremities receiving gastroepiploic lymph node donor flaps demonstrated significantly greater improvement than those receiving submental donor nodes.

Patients undergoing tumor therapy with iliofemoral lymphadenectomy benefit from ILR (Level of Evidence: B, moderate). The concept of preventing lymphedema through lymphatic reconstruction at the time of lymph node dissection was reported by Orifice et al120 and Boccardo et al36 introduced the LYMPHA approach.121 Five systematic reviews support the use of LVA or VLNT at the time of lymphadenectomy,38,40,74, 75, 76 including one high-methodological-quality review by Brown et al38 (Tables VI and V). As previously reported by Chang et al,65 the level of evidence for LEL prevention was downgraded due to the absence of RCTs specific to LEL.

In patients with lower-extremity dermatologic malignancies (melanoma or squamous cell carcinoma) undergoing tumor resection and iliofemoral lymphadenectomy, ILR may increase the risk of tumor recurrence (Level of Evidence: C, low to very low). A systematic review by Zaidi et al,77 based on low-quality data and very low certainty of evidence (Table V), raised concerns regarding oncologic safety in this population. Although Xia et al106 found no increase in recurrence at 2 years in patients with extremity melanoma, Chungsiriwattana et al105 reported lower 5-year OS, RFS, and DMFS after ILR in four patients with melanoma and three with squamous cell carcinoma. Current consensus recommendations therefore advise against prophylactic LVA in patients with extremity malignancies.65

Study limitations

Our study has several limitations. Only 5 of the 26 included systematic reviews fulfilled all critical domains of the AMSTAR-2 framework. There was substantial overlap among high-quality reviews; therefore, pooling effect estimates across meta-analyses would have resulted in double counting of data from the same core evidence base. Inclusion of lower-quality systematic reviews may have influenced the overall assessment of treatment effects.

The primary studies exhibited a high risk of bias and substantial heterogeneity, driven by variability in preoperative and intraoperative imaging protocols, surgical techniques, postoperative care, and inconsistent reporting of oncologic outcomes in patients undergoing lymphatic microsurgery for cancer-related lymphedema. Outcome assessment was further limited by the lack of RCTs, the low number of direct control studies with conservative therapy and the high heterogeneity in reporting methods. Volume and circumference were reported using excess or absolute circumference or volume, the LEL index, perometry, bioelectrical impedance analysis, magnetic resonance volumetry, body weight or body composition analysis, and computed tomography-based measurement of subcutaneous fat thickness.

Patient-reported outcomes were also inconsistently measured. Although six validated QoL instruments are available, LYMQOL demonstrated the most complete and highest-quality evidence for validity, reliability, and responsiveness.63,66

Future research should prioritize well-designed RCTs conducted, according to internationally accepted reporting standards. Long-term follow-up data from the only published RCT in LEL are still needed,91 and results from an ongoing RCT registered in ClinicalTrials.gov are awaited.122

Conclusions

Our umbrella systematic review of systematic reviews and meta-analyses found moderate-to-low quality evidence supporting the clinically meaningful benefits of lymphatic microsurgery, including LVA and VLNT, for patients with chronic lymphedema, irrespective of disease type (primary or secondary) or clinical stage. The certainty of evidence was downgraded due to the absence of RCTs and high heterogeneity, evaluating surgical treatment or prevention of LEL. Guideline recommendations should emphasize that lymphatic microsurgery requires shared decision-making, a multidisciplinary team, pre- and postoperative protocol, and must be individualized based on patient anatomy, physiology, and preferences, as well as surgeon expertise, available technology, and procedural cost.
