Non-ampullary duodenal mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasm initially diagnosed as adenocarcinoma: a case report
Authors: Maho Takuwa, Yukinori Yamagata, Toshihiro Haga, Hirokazu Shoji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasms (MiNENs) are rare tumors composed of neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine components. Their occurrence in the duodenum is extremely uncommon. Preoperative diagnosis is difficult because the neuroendocrine component may not be detected in biopsy specimens. We report a rare case of non-ampullary duodenal MiNEN initially diagnosed as adenocarcinoma.Case presentation:A 56-year-old man was found to have a tumor in the duodenal bulb during routine endoscopic examination. Biopsy revealed moderately to poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma, and computed tomography demonstrated bulky regional lymph node metastasis without distant disease. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy with S-1 plus oxaliplatin was administered based on treatment strategies used for advanced gastric cancer in Japan. Post-treatment evaluation showed stable disease, and pancreaticoduodenectomy with regional lymph node dissection achieved R0 resection. Histopathological examination revealed both adenocarcinoma and neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) components, leading to a diagnosis of MiNEN. Lymph node metastases demonstrated component-specific heterogeneity, with NEC involvement in station No. 8a and adenocarcinoma in the remaining metastatic nodes. Postoperative platinum-based chemotherapy was administered, and the patient remains recurrence-free at 16 months after surgery.Discussion:Duodenal MiNEN presents diagnostic and therapeutic challenges because biopsy specimens may fail to detect the neuroendocrine component preoperatively. In the present case, component-specific lymph node metastases suggested biological heterogeneity and may have influenced treatment selection.Conclusion:Non-ampullary duodenal MiNEN is a rare and diagnostically challenging disease. This case highlights the importance of accurate pathological evaluation and multidisciplinary management.

Background

Mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasms (MiNENs), as defined by the World Health Organization, are tumors composed of both neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine components, each accounting for at least 30% of the lesion. In the gastrointestinal tract, MiNENs are rare, and their occurrence in the duodenum – particularly outside the ampulla – is extremely uncommon[1]. The term MiNEN encompasses tumors composed of neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine components, including those with neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC), which is generally associated with aggressive clinical behavior and a poor prognosis[2,3].

A major clinical challenge in MiNEN is the difficulty of preoperative diagnosis. The neuroendocrine component is often located in the deeper layers of the tumor and may not be detected in biopsy specimens, resulting in an initial diagnosis of adenocarcinoma[4]. Consequently, treatment strategies are frequently determined based on incomplete pathological information.

In addition to diagnostic challenges, the optimal management of duodenal NEC or MiNEN remains unclear. Platinum-based chemotherapy is generally recommended for NEC, based on treatment strategies for small cell lung cancer and high-grade gastrointestinal NEC[5]; however, the role of surgery and perioperative management remains controversial, particularly in localized disease.

We report a rare case of non-ampullary duodenal MiNEN that was preoperatively diagnosed as adenocarcinoma and treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy, highlighting the diagnostic limitations, therapeutic challenges, and biological heterogeneity of this disease. This case report has been prepared in accordance with the SCARE criteria.

Case presentation

A 56-year-old man underwent esophagogastroduodenoscopy during a routine health check-up, which revealed a tumor on the posterior wall of the duodenal bulb (Fig. 1A). Biopsy demonstrated moderately to poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma (Fig. 1B). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed a 3-cm bulky lymph node at station No. 8a, with no evidence of distant metastasis (Fig. 1C,D). Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with cT4N1M0, clinical stage IIIA duodenal cancer according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer staging system[6]. The patient was referred to our institution for further management. Because bulky nodal disease around station No. 8a raised concern regarding advanced locoregional disease and potential systemic spread, neoadjuvant chemotherapy was selected to achieve disease stabilization before surgery. Although evidence supporting neoadjuvant chemotherapy for duodenal cancer remains limited, treatment strategies were adapted from advanced gastric cancer because of the anatomical proximity and the presence of bulky regional nodal disease[7]. The patient received three courses of SOX [S-1 (tegafur/gimeracil/oteracil) plus oxaliplatin] at the referring hospital. Although fluorouracil, leucovorin, and oxaliplatin (FOLFOX) is commonly used for small intestinal adenocarcinoma, SOX was selected as a fluoropyrimidine-platinum regimen widely used in Japan and was considered feasible in this clinical setting.

Post-treatment evaluation demonstrated stable disease on both endoscopy and CT (Fig. 1E–G). He subsequently underwent curative resection at our institution, with a diagnosis of ycT4N1M0, ycStage IIIA[6]. A pancreaticoduodenectomy with regional lymph node dissection was performed, achieving an R0 resection (Fig. 2A). Histopathological examination revealed a tumor composed of both adenocarcinoma and NEC components. Immunohistochemical staining showed positivity for chromogranin A, synaptophysin, and insulinoma-associated protein 1, confirming the presence of the NEC component (Fig. 3). The Ki-67 labeling index of the NEC component was approximately 30–40% in the hot spot area. The adenocarcinoma and NEC components accounted for approximately 40% and 60% of the tumor, respectively, leading to a diagnosis of MiNEN. The adenocarcinoma component was predominantly distributed around the NEC component (Fig. 2B). Lymph node metastases were identified in stations No. 3b, 4d, 6, and 8a, as well as in nodes attached to the specimen (14/133 nodes). Notably, metastasis in station No. 8a consisted of the NEC component, whereas metastases at the other stations were composed of adenocarcinoma. Differential pathological responses between the adenocarcinoma and NEC components could not be clearly quantified because the overall histological response was limited.

The final pathological stage was ypT4N2M0 (ypStage IIIB[6]). Postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy with cisplatin plus etoposide was administered for six courses. The patient underwent surveillance with contrast-enhanced CT every 3–4 months and remains recurrence-free at 16 months after surgery.

Discussion

MiNENs of the duodenum are extremely rare, particularly in non-ampullary locations, with only a limited number of cases reported in the literature[1]. The present case adds to the accumulating evidence regarding the clinical characteristics and management of this rare entity.

A major clinical challenge in MiNEN is the difficulty of preoperative diagnosis[5]. Because the neuroendocrine component is often located in the deeper layers of the tumor, biopsy specimens frequently fail to detect it, leading to an initial diagnosis of adenocarcinoma[4]. As demonstrated in the present case, this diagnostic limitation can directly influence treatment selection.

A review of previously reported cases of non-ampullary duodenal NEC or MiNEN is summarized in Table 1[8–12]. Cases were identified through a PubMed search of English-language articles published between 2015 and 2025 using the keywords “duodenum,” “neuroendocrine carcinoma,” “MANEC,” and “MiNEN,” and only cases with a definitive pathological diagnosis were included. Most reported cases were initially diagnosed as adenocarcinoma or were difficult to diagnose preoperatively. Pancreaticoduodenectomy was the main surgical procedure, and perioperative treatment strategies varied considerably. Notably, neoadjuvant chemotherapy has been rarely reported, and early recurrence or poor prognosis were observed in several patients. In contrast, the present case received component-oriented multidisciplinary treatment, including neoadjuvant chemotherapy and postoperative platinum-based chemotherapy, and has remained recurrence-free to date. Another important issue is the biological heterogeneity of MiNEN. In the present case, lymph node metastases demonstrated component-specific heterogeneity: whereas most metastatic nodes consisted of adenocarcinoma, the metastasis at station No. 8a was composed of NEC. This finding suggests that both tumor components may independently contribute to disease progression and may require different therapeutic considerations. Whether this finding reflects clonal divergence, differential metastatic potential, or simply intratumoral heterogeneity remains uncertain. Further molecular studies are required to clarify the biological relationship between the adenocarcinoma and NEC components in MiNEN.

The present case also illustrates the complexity of treatment selection in localized duodenal MiNEN. Because bulky nodal disease around station No. 8a raised concern regarding advanced locoregional spread and potential systemic disease, neoadjuvant chemotherapy was selected before surgery. As the preoperative biopsy demonstrated adenocarcinoma without evidence of a neuroendocrine component, treatment selection was based on the contemporary management of locally advanced duodenal adenocarcinoma. Although evidence supporting neoadjuvant chemotherapy in duodenal cancer remains limited, treatment strategies were adapted from advanced gastric cancer because of the anatomical proximity and bulky nodal involvement[7]. Although FOLFOX is commonly used for small-bowel adenocarcinoma, SOX was selected because this patient presented with bulky regional nodal disease resembling advanced gastric cancer with bulky nodal metastases, for which SOX-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy has been widely adopted in Japan. Had the neuroendocrine component been recognized preoperatively, platinum–etoposide–based chemotherapy would also have been considered.

Although the histopathological response was limited (Grade 1a), no disease progression was observed during treatment, and curative resection was achieved. Differential pathological responses between the adenocarcinoma and NEC components could not be clearly evaluated because of the limited overall treatment effect. Nevertheless, the predominance of adenocarcinoma in most metastatic lymph nodes and NEC involvement in station No. 8a may partly reflect the biological heterogeneity of this tumor.

Postoperative platinum-based chemotherapy was administered following NEC-oriented treatment strategies[13]. Although evidence supporting adjuvant chemotherapy after curative resection for duodenal NEC remains limited, the presence of metastatic NEC components and the aggressive biological behavior generally associated with NEC supported this treatment approach in the present case.

Taken together, the present case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of duodenal MiNEN. When MiNEN cannot be diagnosed preoperatively, treatment strategies based on adenocarcinoma may represent a pragmatic initial approach. Once NEC is identified, postoperative management should be reconsidered according to the biological characteristics of the tumor.

Further accumulation of cases is warranted to establish optimal treatment strategies for this rare disease.

Conclusion

Non-ampullary duodenal MiNEN is a rare and diagnostically challenging disease. Adenocarcinoma-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy may be a reasonable treatment option in selected patients when MiNEN cannot be diagnosed preoperatively. However, this observation is based on a single case and should be interpreted with caution. Further accumulation of cases is required to establish the optimal treatment strategy for this rare disease.
