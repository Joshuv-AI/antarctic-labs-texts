Power Cable Radiation: A Novel Approach to Underground Mining Connectivity
Siphiwe Shandu, Thabiso Moropa, Alain R. Ndjiongue

Abstract
This letter investigates contactless power line communications (CPLC) for underground mining by modeling power wires as long-wire antennas. A system-level framework is developed, comprising a cascade of RF and power line channels. The model accounts for multipath propagation, frequency-dependent attenuation, and Rician fading. Simulations from 1-20 GHz reveal that the length of the wire significantly affects radiation, directivity, and input impedance. The findings show that CPLC transmits electromagnetic waves without direct electrical contact, offering a robust, cost-effective solution that enhances mobility, reduces maintenance, and ensures compatibility with existing mining power infrastructure.



I
Introduction

Underground mining environments present unique challenges for communication systems due to dynamic conditions such as shifting topologies, high humidity (greater than 90%), corrosive water, dust, and electromagnetic interference from heavy machinery, all of which degrade signal reliability
[
1
,
2
]
. In addition to these harsh physical conditions, the potential presence of explosive gases demands the use of robust and explosion-proof equipment to ensure safe operation
[
1
]
. In these environments, effective communication is critical for safety, allowing emergency alerts and evacuation coordination, and for operational efficiency through real-time orchestration. Traditional wired systems, such as magnetophones, offer simplicity but lack mobility and require frequent maintenance due to cable wear
[
1
]
. Radio-based systems, including through-the-earth communication and wireless networks, improve mobility, but suffer from
line of sight (LoS)
limitations and signal attenuation in complex mine topologies
[
2
]
. Other technologies, such as leaky feeders, are susceptible to cable damage and require power at the interfaces. A potential technology to be used in this environment is
power line communication (PLC)
that exploits power cables for data transmission, but is limited by the need for direct electrical connections
[
3
]
.

In this work, we present a novel
contactless PLC (CPLC)
model that leverages radiative coupling to transmit signals through electromagnetic fields, treating power cables as antennas
[
3
,
4
]
. This approach eliminates direct electrical contact, enhancing mobility for equipment and personnel in mines where
LoS
issues and attenuation are prevalent
[
1
,
2
]
. By leveraging existing power infrastructure,
CPLC
reduces deployment costs and can serve as an environmental sensor for monitoring gas levels, temperature, and pressure. More specifically, this letter develops a theoretical framework for
CPLC
by modeling power cables as long-wire antennas, applying antenna theory principles, and simulating their performance in a mining environment, considering carrier signals with frequencies from 1-20 GHz. We focus on a
\kappa
-wire system coupled to a
radio frequency (RF)
source. To this end, the primary contributions of this paper are: (
i
) The evaluation of the radiation characteristics of an indoor long-wire antenna in a lossy environment; (
ii
) An analysis of the directivity characteristics of an indoor long-wire antenna in a mining tunnel; (
iii
) Modeling of an indoor power wire antenna at specific frequencies based on the gain and directivity profile; (
iv
) We also characterize the three-phase power cable typically used in underground mining applications; (
v
) Finally, we develop a system and channel model for indoor long-wire antenna and application to underground mining communications.

(1)

where
\mu_{0}=4\pi\times 10^{-7}\,\text{H/m}

Radiation Pattern

It describes the angular distribution of the radiated power. For a long-wire antenna, the far-field also known as the Fraunhofer region of the electric field in spherical coordinates can be given as
[
6
]

E_{\theta}=j\eta\frac{kI_{0}e^{-jk\mathbf{r}}}{4\pi\mathbf{r}}\sin\theta\int_{-L_{w}/2}^{L_{w}/2}e^{jkz\cos\theta}dz,

(2)

where
\eta=120\pi\,\Omega
represents the free-space impedance,
I_{0}
denotes the peak current,
L_{w}
expresses the wire length,
\mathbf{r}
stands for the radian distance to the observation point, and
\theta

F(\theta)=\frac{\sin\left(\frac{\pi n\cos\theta}{2}\right)}{\sin\theta},\quad n=\frac{L_{w}}{\lambda},

(3)

where

A long-wire antenna, defined as having a length

[Z][I]=[V]

7

Simulation Results and Analysis

Figure

5

The multiple lobes observed typically arise due to the electrical length of the wire relative to the operating wavelength. When a wire antenna is much longer than a quarter wavelength, it no longer behaves like a simple monopole. Instead, it starts to exhibit standing wave patterns, leading to multiple lobes in the radiation pattern. These lobes are areas of constructive interference where the radiated signal is stronger. The behavior of the indoor long-wire antenna depends heavily on the current distribution, and as such, in a short monopole, the current distribution is roughly triangular, peaking at the feed-point. However, in a long-wire, multiple current maxima and minima form along the wire, creating multiple radiation zones. Each of these zones contributes to a lobe in the far-field pattern.

IV
Conclusion

This research developed a framework for using antenna radiation theory in CPLC systems for underground mining. It confirmed that mining power cables can act as long-wire antennas, with their length having a significant impact on signal directivity. The proposed model captured key effects like frequency-dependent attenuation, demonstrating that longer cables produce multi-lobed radiation patterns. This CPLC system could improve mobility and leverage existing infrastructure, overcoming limitations of current systems. Future work should investigate the mutual coupling effects of multiple active wires.
