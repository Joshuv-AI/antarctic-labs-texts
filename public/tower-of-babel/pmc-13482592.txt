Intravesical mycobacteria reshape bladder and gut microbiota in a murine bladder cancer model
Authors: Víctor Campo-Pérez, Sandra Guallar-Garrido, Raúl Cabrera-Rubio, Paula Herrero-Abadía, Arnau Seguí-Moll, Manuela Torres-Puente, Pablo Cano-Jimenez, Iñaki Comas, Eduard Torrents, Esther Julián
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The bladder microbiota may influence tumor progression and responses to intravesical therapies, yet the effects of Mycobacterium bovis BCG instillations on bladder and gut microbiota remain unexplored. This study evaluates, for the first time, the impact of M. bovis BCG and Mycobacterium brumae, a non-pathogenic immunomodulator, on microbiota composition in bladder tissue and cecal stool of healthy and tumor-bearing mice. Mycobacterial instillations increased gut microbial richness but reduced Actinobacteria. In tumor-bearing mice, Muribaculaceae and Bacteroidaceae were enriched, while M. bovis BCG reduced Muribaculaceae and increased Rikenellaceae. In the bladder, M. brumae instillations favored Staphylococcaceae in healthy mice, while Enterococcaceae dominated most groups except M. bovis BCG-treated tumor-bearing mice. Escherichia/Shigella decreased in mycobacteria-treated tumor-bearing mice, while Burkholderia was more prevalent in untreated bladders. Notably, M. brumae increased stool alpha diversity, whereas M. bovis BCG reduced bladder richness. These findings reveal that intravesical mycobacterial therapy modulates microbiota composition across body sites, underscoring the complex host-tumor-immunotherapy interplay.

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BC) is the tenth most common malignancy worldwide, with approximately 600,000 new cases and over 200,000 deaths reported annually1. At diagnosis, ~75% of patients are present with non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC), while the remaining 25% have muscle-invasive disease. High-risk recurrence and progression NMIBC cases are commonly treated with surgical resection of the tumor followed by weekly intravesical instillations of Mycobacterium bovis Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) immunotherapy2. Despite its proven efficacy, M. bovis BCG immunotherapy treatment fails in ~25– 45% of cases3. The efficacy variations have been related to many possible factors such as tumor development stage and heterogeneity, tumor microenvironment, genetic background, the immunological status of the patient, and intolerance to M. bovis BCG instillations4, or even the patient microbiota, as recently suggested by observations in urine samples5,6. In addition, M. bovis BCG therapy is not without risk: it can lead to local and, in rare cases, systemic infections. Therefore, alternative treatments are necessary. Mycobacterium brumae, a non-pathogenic environmental mycobacterium, achieves NMIBC antitumoral effects comparable to M. bovis BCG in vitro and in vivo without causing infections7–9, emerging as a safe immunotherapy agent.

The long-standing notion of the urinary tract as a sterile environment has been overturned by the advent of high-throughput sequencing technologies, particularly 16S ribosomal RNA (16S rRNA) gene sequencing, which has enabled the identification of a resident urinary microbiota10. This revelation has sparked a revolution in urological research, reshaping our understanding of the field11. The evidence of bladder microbiota may affect (bladder) tumor progression and response to therapies, including M. bovis BCG instillations, or may be potentially used as a noninvasive predictive response to treatment biomarkers12. Furthermore, urinary microbiota profiles are being explored as potential noninvasive biomarkers for predicting treatment response.

Despite these advances, the nature of the interaction between bladder microbiota and M. bovis BCG treatment remains poorly understood. Proposed mechanisms include M. bovis BCG inactivation or elimination by resident microbes and competition for fibronectin binding sites on the bladder mucosa surface, which are essential for M. bovis BCG internalization and immunogenicity13,14. Several studies have revealed differences in urinary microbiota composition between healthy individuals and those with BC15–18, yet findings are inconsistent, and no definitive microbial signature has been established. It has been suggested that this variability among studies is related to differences depending on patient sex, the sample type (urine vs. bladder tissue) and collection methods11,19. It is challenging to compare urinary microbiota across studies due to the lack of standardized approaches20. Further standardized and comprehensive studies are required to address these limitations and clarify the role of the bladder microbiota in BC21.

The role of the gut microbiota has been highlighted in recent years due to its impact on the carcinogenesis, evolution, and response to immunotherapy of different types of cancer22, usually associated with the development of resistance to chemo drugs or immune checkpoints inhibitors23. In this sense, the modulation of the gut microbiota is considered a future key factor in precision medicine cancer treatment. Although anatomically separated, the gut microbiota may exert systemic effects on the urinary tract. Notably, probiotic intake (particularly Lactobacillus-based formulations) has been associated with reduced risk of recurrence in BC24,25, and gut microbiota alterations have been identified in BC patients26. However, the physiopathology mechanisms involved in these correlations between gut microbiota and BC remain unclear.

Understanding and characterizing the recently described bladder microbiota, as well as the implications of the gut microbiota on BC, is essential for the development of predictive and personalized strategies to obtain a more effective treatment. In this study, we collected bladder and cecum stool samples from a mouse orthotopic BC model to characterize the microbiota, marking the first analysis of the mouse bladder microbiota. Both healthy and tumor-bearing mice were treated with either M. bovis BCG immunotherapy, the current clinical standard, or M. brumae to assess microbiota alterations in response to treatment. This design included healthy treated control groups and therefore allowed discrimination between microbiota changes associated with intravesical mycobacterial treatment itself, those associated with tumor presence, and those related to their interaction.

Methods

Bacterial strains and cell lines

M. bovis BCG Connaught (ATCC 35745) and M. brumae (ATCC 51384T) were grown on Middlebrook 7H10 agar medium (Difco Laboratories, Detroit, Michigan) supplemented with 10% oleic-acid-dextrose-catalase (OADC) enrichment and incubated at 37 °C for 3 or 1 week, respectively. M. bovis BCG and M. brumae cells were resuspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and adjusted to a 1.0 McFarland turbidity standard as previously described27 to achieve concentrations of 5×105 CFU/mL or 5 × 106 CFU/mL, respectively. Finally, bacterial suspensions were centrifuged at 1300 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C and diluted in PBS at the desired concentration. Inoculums were corroborated by plating the solution in 7H10 Middlebrook plates. CFU/mL were obtained after incubating M. bovis BCG plates at 37 °C for 3 weeks and M. brumae plates for 1 week.

The murine high-grade BC cell line MB49 was maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) with stable L-glutamine (Gibco BRL) and supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Lonza, Basel, Switzerland), 100 U/mL penicillin G (Laboratorios ERN, Barcelona, Spain), and 100 μg/ml streptomycin (Laboratorio Reig Jofré, Barcelona, Spain).

Orthotopic model of bladder cancer and intravesical treatment

Animal experiments were approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee (reference number CEEAH 5263) at the Autonomous University of Barcelona in accordance with ethical and legal requirements, authorized by the Government of Catalonia (project number 11185), and conducted according to the approved ethical guidelines. All animals were initially acclimatized for 1 week under quarantine conditions before study initiation. During the experiment, all animals were housed with a maximum of six animals per cage with food and water ad libitum in an enriched environment in a Biosafety Level (BSL) 2 facility. The animals were evaluated daily for their general aspect and behavior and were euthanized when required to avoid unnecessary suffering. The orthotopic murine model of BC was developed as described previously4,7,28–30). Briefly, 8 C57Bl/6 female mice (6- to 8-week-old, Charles River Laboratories, Barcelona, Spain) were stratified by body weight and then randomly assigned to groups using a randomization list to ensure comparable weight distributions across groups. Sample size was consistent with the principle of Reduction within the 3Rs framework. Two days post-randomization, bladders were initially emptied, and chemical lesions on the urothelium were induced by intravesical instillation of poly-L-lysine solution (PLL, Sigma), which remained in the bladder for 15 min before removal. Subsequently, 100 µL of DMEM without FBS containing 1 × 105 MB49 cells were intravesically instilled and maintained in the bladder for 1 h to allow tumor cells adhesion. The day after tumor induction (day 1), mice received 100 µL of PBS (positive control group), 100 µL of PBS containing 2 × 107 CFU of M. brumae, or 100 µL of PBS containing 2 × 106 CFU of M. bovis BCG, with a 1-h intravesical dwell time. Intravesical treatment was initiated at day 1 to model an early therapeutic intervention, following previously established orthotopic BC procedure4,7,28–30. Mycobacterial doses were selected according to previously optimized conditions reported for this orthotopic BC model7. Tumor implantation was subsequently monitored during follow-up and was considered established between days 7 and 10 based on the presence of hematuria. All intravesical instillations were carried out under inhaled anesthesia, 1.5–2% isoflurane in pure O2. Treatments were repeated weekly on days 8, 15, and 22 after tumor induction (four total instillations).

In parallel, control groups were also included: mice received the PLL instillation, but PBS was used instead of BC tumor cells. Thus, the study comprised six groups: healthy + PBS, healthy + M. brumae, healthy + M. bovis BCG, tumor-bearing + PBS, tumor-bearing + M. brumae, and tumor-bearing + M. bovis BCG mice. This experimental design was intended to distinguish the independent effects of intravesical mycobacterial treatment from tumor-associated microbiota changes. The final analysis, conducted blindly by a researcher, included all animals that completed the treatment schedule, with no exclusions.

Sampling procedure

Thirty days after tumor induction, surviving animals were euthanized via cervical dislocation under terminal inhalation anesthesia to collect bladders and fecal content from the cecum. Mice skin was cleaned with 70% ethanol, and then an abdominal incision was carried out to access the abdominal cavity aseptically. Sterilized surgical instruments were used to aseptically remove the bladder, avoiding the adjoining part of the urethra and crossover contamination31. The cecum was located to obtain the stool samples, and a cross-section was carried out aseptically, pouring the fecal content into a cryovial. Both stool and bladder samples were recollected in 2 mL sterile cryovials and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, then stored at -80°C until microbial DNA extraction.

Microbial DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene amplification

Microbial DNA from the cecum samples was extracted using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Barcelona, Spain) following the kit instructions for “isolation of DNA from stool for pathogen detection” under strict sterile conditions. DNA was eluted and stored at −80 °C until use.

Each bladder was cut into small portions using sterile blades and placed in a 15 mL Falcon tube (Fisher Scientific, Hampton, USA) with 0.5 mm glass beads (dDBiolab, Barcelona, Spain). Then, 3 mL of PBS (pH 7.4) (Fisher Bioreagents, Hampton, USA) were added, and bladder tissue was homogenized by vortexing for 5 min, the consequent suspension was recovered into a new 15 mL falcon tube, and the same process was repeated, obtaining a 6 mL final disrupted bladder tissue suspension. This final suspension was centrifuged at 13,000×g for 5 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was applied to the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Barcelona, Spain) using the spin protocol procedure to isolate the microbial DNA. The microbial DNA from bladders was subsequently concentrated up to a final volume of 10 μL, using a DNA concentrator 5301 (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) at 42°C. Both cecum and bladder microbial DNA isolated were amplified by PCR (35 cycles and 55 °C annealing temperature) using primers targeting the V3 and V4 regions of the ribosomal RNA adapted to Illumina sequencing, tailored with the end of Illumina adapters (underlined): 16S_V3V4F (TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGCCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG) and 16S_V3V4R (GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGGACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC). The PCR products of around 464 bp of all the samples were run in a 2% agarose gel with a 100 bp DNA ladder (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). The amplified products were purified using the Monarch® DNA Gel Extraction Kit (New England BioLabs, Ipswich, USA) following the manufacturer’s recommendations. The purified DNA samples were stored at −80 °C until Illumina sequencing.

Illumina library preparation and sequencing

Illumina libraries were prepared by 8-cycle index PCR using KAPA HiFi HotStart PCR Kit as follows: 1X KAPA HiFi Fidelity Buffer, 0.5 mM MgCl2, 0.3 mM KAPA dNTP Mix, 0.3 μM primers, 0.5 U KAPA HiFi HotStart DNA Polymerase, and 5 μL of one combination of i7 and i5 indexes per sample. Library products were purified using Agencourt AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA). The final DNA concentrations of the purified products were measured using a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and validated with the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The purified products were diluted to a final concentration of 4 nM and pooled. The 16S rRNA gene libraries were sequenced with 2 × 300 bp paired-end reads in an Illumina MiSeq System.

Bioinformatics and statistical analyses

Raw reads were quality-filtered (Quality; 25 and length; 125 base-pairs (bp)) using trimGalore (v0.6.4_dev; https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore). Paired-end reads with a minimum overlap of 30 bp were joined using Fastq-join32. Sequences of primers and distal bases were trimmed, and singletons were removed with USEARCH v1133. The resulting reads were denoised, and chimeras were filtered with UNOISE332. The zOTUs (zero-radius operational taxon units) mapping to the human genome (GRCh38) using the Burrow-Wheeler Aligner in Deconseq v0.4.3 were filtered out.

Taxonomic assignment of zOTUs was performed in QIIME 2 v2018.234 using the QIIME 2 feature classifier plugin35 and the Ribosomal Database Project (RDP)36. The zOTUs were aligned with MAFFT37 to make a midpoint-rooted phylogenetic tree with FASTTREE38.

Statistical analyses were performed with R version 4.1.0, and figures were drawn with the “ggplot2” R package39. Venn diagrams were drawn to analyse group-specific bacterial taxa using the R package “VennDiagram”40. The Chao1 and Shannon indices were calculated for each individual sample using the phyloseq R package41, and differences by group were assessed by analysis of variance tests. The PERMANOVA test (adonis2; terms added sequentially) from the vegan R package (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=vegan) evaluated overall differences in microbiota structure using a Bray–Curtis matrix of normalization data (log ratio) from abundance data of zOTUs and vegan distance analysis. Between-sample variability within and across experimental groups was explored using alpha-diversity metrics, Bray–Curtis-based beta-diversity analysis, and visual inspection of sample-level taxonomic composition plots at the genus level. Rarefied was not performed, but a filter of 0.01% relative abundance in a minimum at 25% of the samples was established to perform the following analyses. The linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was performed to discover specific bacterial taxa differentially associated with health and disease states. To account for multiple comparisons, p values were adjusted using a false discovery rate (FDR) correction, and only taxa remaining significant after FDR adjustment were considered for interpretation.

Multimodal mediation analysis was conducted using the multimedia R package (version 0.2.142), which enables integrated modeling of direct and indirect microbial effects across multiple mediators. The approach employed a combination of linear log-ratio models (LNM), sparse penalized regression (via glmnet) and Bayesian inference to account for both parametric and non-parametric relationships. Mediators of interest included bacterial treatment (e.g., M. brumae and M. bovis BCG) and tumor status, while pretreatment covariates (e.g., tissue origin) were controlled in the model specification. Direct and indirect effects were estimated across all microbial genera, and false discovery rates were controlled using synthetic null contrasts. This analysis was used as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating statistical framework to identify microbial taxa associated with tumor status, rather than as proof of definitive biological causality.

Results

M. brumae treatment improves the survival of tumor-bearing mice

In the orthotopic murine BC model, all healthy animals survived until the end of the study (day 30), regardless of whether they received M. brumae, M. bovis BCG, or PBS treatment (Supplementary Fig. 1). In contrast, survival rates differed by treatment group among tumor-bearing mice. Humane endpoints were applied as needed across all tumor-induced groups. On day 30, 83% of the M. brumae-treated group survived, while 58 and 69% of M. bovis BCG- and PBS-treated mice did survive. The body weight progression of animals in each treatment group throughout the study is shown in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Phylum and family level microbiota modifications depending on bladder tumor presence and mycobacteria treatment

Since the study included healthy mice treated with PBS, M. brumae, or M. bovis BCG, as well as the corresponding tumor-bearing groups, comparisons could be made to distinguish treatment-associated effects from tumor-associated microbiota shifts. When the cecal stool microbiota was analyzed in healthy and tumor-bearing mice upon M. bovis BCG or M. brumae treatments, the most abundant phyla were Firmicutes (between 50 and 90%), Proteobacteria (between 5 and 30%) and Actinobacteria (between 5 and 20%) for all experimental groups (Fig. 1A). The phyla Bacteoidetes and Deinococcus-Thermus provided less than 10% of the zOTUs identified in the stool cecum. Remarkably, tumor-bearing animals reported higher percentages of Firmicutes at the expense of Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria. In contrast, healthy animals instilled with M. brumae reported a higher rate of Firmicutes than their tumor-bearing mouse counterparts (Fig. 1A). At the family level, Lachnospiraceae (Firmicutes) is the most abundant family in all experimental groups, accounting for between 40 and 50% of the relative abundance, without significant differences among groups. M. brumae instillations did not alter the family abundance profiles compared to non-instilled animals. However, healthy mice instilled with M. bovis BCG presented a lower percentage of Lachnospiraceae and a higher percentage of Rikenellaceae (Bacteroidetes) and Akkermansiaceae (Verrucomicrobia) relative abundance than PBS- or M. brumae instilled healthy mice (Fig. 1B).

In tumor-bearing mice, cecum stools from M. bovis BCG-treated animals reported a lower abundance of Muribaculaceae (Bacteroidetes) and higher Rikenellaceae families compared with nontreated and M. brumae-treated mice. Ruminoccaceae (Firmicutes) was the fourth most represented family in all tumor-bearing groups, without significant difference among them (Fig. 1B). Similarly, the Bacteroidaceae (Bacteroidetes) family clearly appeared in greater abundance in the experimental groups of tumor-bearing mice than in their healthy counterparts. Akkermansiaceae and Deferribacteraceae (Deferribacteres) families were also detected, though only slight differences in relative abundance were observed between groups. Detailed intragroup variation in stool microbiota composition at the family level is shown in Supplementary Fig. 3A.

Tumor presence and mycobacteria treatments modify cecum stool alpha and beta diversity

The richness (number of zOTUs observed) in cecum stool samples was, on average, around 700 zOTUs in all experimental groups, although differences emerged between the different experimental groups (Fig. 1C). M. brumae instillation, regardless of bladder tumor presence, significantly increased richness in cecum stool samples compared to untreated healthy mice, as indicated by the Shannon and Simpson alpha diversity indices (Fig. 1D, E). No significant alpha diversity differences were reported between the cecum stool samples from healthy and tumor-bearing mice nontreated or treated with M. bovis BCG.

Regarding beta diversity, non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) analysis revealed a significant difference in microbiota composition between healthy M. brumae instilled mice and tumor-bearing M. bovis BCG-treated mice (R2 = 0.1418; p value = 0.03) (Fig. 1F). Additionally, the microbiota of tumor-bearing mice treated with M. bovis BCG was the most dissimilar from the other experimental groups, although this trend was not statistically significant. PCoA Bray–Curtis dissimilarity and Unweighted Unifrac distance analysis showed overlapping group profiles, suggesting microbial community composition in the cecal stool was not sufficiently distinct to separate groups (Fig. 1G, H).

The Staphylococcaceae (Firmicutes) family reported the most significant relative abundance to the microbiota of M. brumae-instilled healthy mice (90%) and M. bovis BCG-treated tumor-bearing mice (70%). However, the Enterococcaceae (Firmicutes) family was the more abundant in the rest of the experimental groups, contributing between 25-65% of the relative abundance (Fig. 2B). Microbiota richness is compromised upon tumor and M. bovis BCG instillations

Bladder microbiota richness (number of zOTUs observed) was analyzed among the groups, an approximate average of 125 zOTUs across all groups. However, richness in M. bovis BCG-treated tumor-bearing mice was significantly lower than in their healthy counterparts (Fig. 2C). Similarly, both Shannon and Simpson indices also revealed reduced significant richness differences between the M. bovis BCG-treated mice compared with untreated and M. bovis BCG-instilled healthy mice counterparts (Fig. 2D, E). While no statistically significant differences were detected, healthy animals, both M. bovis BCG-treated and untreated, reported higher richness values than those observed in their BC tumor homologous groups. Remarkably, the richness values of bladder microbiota from M. brumae-instilled mice were similar between tumor-bearing and healthy mice (Fig. 2C).

Concerning beta diversity, NMDS, PCoA Bray–Curtis and PCoA Unweighted Unifrac analysis showed no significant divergence among experimental groups (Fig. 2F–H). This indicates that bladder microbiota samples did not exhibit distinctive groupings or patterns that differentiate one group from another. This may be due to the variability among samples within the same group and the small sample sizes in certain groups (n = 3–6). The NMDS analysis revealed that the tumor-bearing animals treated with M. bovis BCG and the healthy animals treated with M. brumae exhibited slight separation from the remaining groups, but not statistically significant (Fig. 2F).

Mycobacteria treatment drives genus-level shifts in bladder microbiota of tumor-bearing mice

Bladder microbiota was analyzed at the genera level, as represented in the heatmap distinguishing each experimental group independently (Fig. 6). Enterococcus and Staphylococcus are the most abundant genera in all samples. However, Enterococcus was identified with lower abundance in M. bovis BCG-treated tumor-bearing mice bladders. Very low-abundance reads assigned to the genus Mycobacterium were also detected in some untreated bladder samples (Fig. 6). However, given the limitations of 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing, this finding was not interpreted as evidence of strain-specific colonization or constitutive endogenous presence in the bladder microbiota. Remarkably, Escherichia and Shigella genera were not identified in mycobacteria-treated tumor-bearing mice (both M. brumae and M. bovis BCG), while they were present in the rest of the groups. In bladder samples, genera with significant positive direct effects included Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Corynebacterium, Mycobacterium, and Clostridium sensu stricto, suggesting a statistical association consistent with a potential role in tumor-associated host–microbe interactions. Indirect effects were also detected, particularly for Finegoldia, Campylobacter, and Turicimonas, suggesting that these taxa may be indirectly associated with tumor status through interactions with other members of the bladder microbial network.

In cecal stool microbiota, positive direct effects were observed for Akkermansia, Roseburia, Escherichia/Shigella, Mycobacterium, Streptococcus, and Facklamia, highlighting them as candidate taxa associated with tumor status in the intestinal microbiota. Positive indirect effects were detected for Alistipes, Akkermansia, Turicibacter, and Butyricicoccus, suggesting that these taxa may also be indirectly associated with pro-tumor related microbial patterns through ecological interactions.

Discussion

The involvement of both local and gut microbiota in oncogenesis, tumor progression, and response to cancer therapy has gained significant attention in recent years. Although the mechanisms mediating between the microbiota and cancer are mostly unknown, the tumor tissue microenvironment and gut microbiome signatures characterization could predict clinical outcomes, prognosis, and responses to immunotherapy43. In this study, we investigated the effects of mycobacteria intravesical instillations on the gut and bladder microbiota compositions in both healthy and BC tumor-bearing mice.

The cecum stool microbiota composition was primarily dominated by Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteria, consistent with previous reports on murine intestinal microbiota44. The observed increase in Firmicutes and decrease in Proteobacteria in BC tumor-bearing mice suggests a shift in gut microbiota balance comparable to that reported in colorectal cancer, where a similar dysbiosis has been associated with tumor development and progression45. The reduction of Actinobacteria in all treatment groups, compared to untreated controls, suggests that both mycobacteria may influence the gut microbiota composition. One plausible explanation is that intravesical mycobacterial instillation induces host immune or inflammatory responses that secondarily affect the intestinal microbial ecosystem29 rather than direct colonization of the intestinal niche by the instilled mycobacteria. This aligns with findings showing that the gut microbiome influences immunotherapy efficacy, particularly anti-PD-1, while immunotherapy itself can also reshape the microbiome, affecting treatment outcomes46,47. In addition, the different microbiota patterns observed between healthy treated and tumor-bearing treated mice, together with the distinct response observed in healthy M. brumae-instilled mice compared with their tumor-bearing counterparts, suggest that these effects may depend on tumor context and host physiological or metabolic status. However, because immune parameters were not directly assessed in parallel with microbiota profiling in the present study, these changes should be interpreted cautiously, and immune response-mediated secondary modulation remains a plausible, but not directly demonstrated, mechanism48. At the family level, the dominance of Lachnospiraceae across all groups (40–50%) underscores its key role in gut homeostasis, as this family is known for its beneficial effects on gut health, including short-chain fatty acid production and mucosal integrity maintenance49. M. bovis BCG instillation in healthy mice was associated with a lower percentage of Lachnospiraceae, accompanied by an increased abundance of Rikenellaceae and Akkermansiaceae. Rikenellaceae has been associated with immune activation, suggesting that this taxon might be associated with enhanced migration and adhesion of immune cells to sites of inflammation50 and may reflect a shift toward a more immunomodulatory microbiota following M. bovis BCG exposure. Akkermansiaceae, particularly Akkermansia muciniphila, is recognized for its role in maintaining gut barrier integrity and has been associated with improved responses to immunotherapy in non-small-cell lung cancer51.

In tumor-bearing mice, distinct microbial shifts were observed in response to M. bovis BCG instillations. A lower abundance of Muribaculaceae, a family associated with carbohydrate metabolism and gut homeostasis52, along with an increase in Rikenellaceae, suggests an altered gut environment in response to both tumor burden and M. bovis BCG treatment. Interestingly, Ruminococcaceae, Akkermansiaceae, and Bacteroidaceae were enriched in all tumor-bearing compared to healthy mice groups, suggesting a potential link between gut microbiota composition and bladder tumor presence. An increased abundance in Ruminococcaceae has been linked with increased levels of CD4+ and CD8+ effector T cells and responsiveness to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy in melanoma patients53 and improve responses in other cancer types as unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma54 and colorectal cancer55. The variations in the abundance of Bacteroidaceae in tumor-bearing mice support the notion that members of this family are involved in tumor-associated microbiota shifts, as observed in colorectal cancer56.

The alterations in alpha and beta diversity in cecum microbiota due to tumor presence and mycobacterial treatments suggest an influence of both cancer progression and immunotherapy on gut microbiota composition. The significant increase in richness after M. brumae instillation, independently of bladder tumor presence, indicates its role in promoting microbial diversity, a feature often associated with a healthier gut microenvironment57. Previous studies have shown that immune system alterations influence gut microbial diversity58. In this context, immune-modulating mycobacteria like M. brumae, efficacious for NMIBC treatment7, may enhance gut microbiota diversity, potentially promoting beneficial immunological effects. In contrast, no significant alpha diversity changes in the gut microbiota were observed for M. bovis BCG treatment compared to the untreated mice. This aligns with previous findings showing that M. bovis BCG vaccination did not significantly alter cecum microbiota alpha diversity index59. NMDS analysis revealed a distinct microbial composition in M. brumae-treated healthy mice compared to tumor-bearing M. bovis BCG-treated mice, suggesting differential microbial shifts based on treatment and disease status and reinforcing the idea that host–microbe interactions in the gut are influenced by both immunotherapy and tumor-induced changes60. However, Bray–Curtis and Unweighted UniFrac PCoA results did not reveal any significant differences among the experimental groups, and further studies are needed to elucidate whether these microbial alterations contribute to therapeutic outcomes or instead reflect secondary effects related to tumor burden and/or host immune responses induced by intravesical treatment.

At the genus level, tumor-bearing mice showed increased abundance of Phocaeicola and Ruminococcus compared to healthy mice, regardless of the treatment administered. Increased presence of Ruminococcus in the gut microbiota has been associated with greater tumor progression in prostate cancer61 and has also been observed in patients with thyroid cancer, endometrial cancer, and renal cell carcinoma62. The increased abundance of Lactobacillus in tumor-bearing mice, particularly those treated with M. bovis BCG, suggests a potential immunomodulatory role of these gut bacteria in BC. Lactobacilli are known for their protective effects, and Lactobacillus-based probiotics consumption in BC treatment have been previously explored, enhancing therapeutic outcomes by preventing tumor recurrence24,25.

LEfSe results identified significantly higher levels of Alistipes, Bacteroides and Enterocloster in the cecum stool microbiota from M. bovis BCG-treated tumor-bearing mice. The genus Alistipes has been implicated as an opportunistic pathogen in colorectal cancer; however, its role in other cancers appears to be more complex, with evidence suggesting potential immunomodulatory benefits63. Alistipes species interact with the immune system via TLR-4, triggering TNF-α production, and their reduction has been linked to decreased immunotherapy efficacy in a murine subcutaneous tumor model, suggesting a role in modulating the tumor microenvironment and treatment response64. In the case of Bacteroides, a study comparing the gut microbiota of healthy individuals and BC patients found a significantly higher abundance of this genus in healthy individuals, suggesting a potential protective role26. Conversely, the presence of the Enterocloster genus in all experimental groups, except for M. brumae-treated tumor-bearing mice, suggests a potential interaction between this bacterial genus and the immune-modulating effects of M. brumae. Enterocloster was first described in 202065 and has been associated with reduced efficacy of checkpoint inhibitor-based immunotherapy in lung, kidney and bladder cancers66. Its absence in M. brumae-treated tumor-bearing mice could indicate a shift in the gut microbiota toward a more immunostimulatory profile, potentially enhancing the therapeutic response. Finally, the significant enrichment of Paramuribaculum in M. brumae-treated tumor-bearing mice suggests a potential, yet unexplored, relationship between this genus and the M. brumae treatment. Since Paramuribaculum was recently described67, there is currently no literature linking it to cancer or immunotherapy outcomes, highlighting the need for further investigation into its possible immunomodulatory effects. On the other hand, Muribaculum was also found in higher abundance in cecum stools from tumor-bearing animals. While limited research has explored its role in cancer, a study reported that treatment with lactulose in a colorectal cancer model increased Muribaculum abundance, which was associated with reduced levels of inflammation and tumor development68. Kineothrix, present in M. brumae-instilled healthy animals, helps distinguish this group from others. First described in 201769, Kineothrix has since been linked to improvements in gut health and lipid metabolism within the gut-liver axis70. Finally, the genus Akkermansia was predominantly found in tumor-bearing mice, and emerging research suggests its potential role in enhancing the efficacy of chemotherapy or immunotherapy, ultimately improving long-term survival across various cancer types71. This genus influences metabolite production from host nutrients, helps maintain the intestinal mucosal barrier, and plays a key role in immunomodulation, all of which can affect immune responses at tumor sites72.

To further contextualize these findings, multimodal mediation analysis was performed to disentangle direct and indirect microbial contributions to BC across bladder and gut niches42. This analysis was intended as a hypothesis-generating statistical approach to explore genera associated with BC status across tissues, rather than as definitive evidence of biological causality. Given that a definitive causal relationship between the bladder microbiome and bladder carcinogenesis has not been established, these mediation-based findings should be interpreted as exploratory statistical associations that may help prioritize candidate taxa for future mechanistic studies. This approach revealed distinct and shared taxa statistically associated with tumor-related or healthy microbial patterns depending on tissue context (Fig. 8). Positive direct associations were observed for genera such as Mycobacterium, Escherichia/Shigella, and Streptococcus across both tissues, consistent with prior evidence implicating these taxa in cancer-associated microbiota73,74. Conversely, Bacteroides consistently displayed negative direct effects, supporting its protective role previously noted in colorectal and BC contexts75,76. Interestingly, Enterococcus demonstrated tissue-dependent behavior, showing a negative direct association in the bladder but an indirect association with tumor-related microbial patterns in the cecum, aligning with observations in urinary tract infections and gut dysbiosis77,78. Moreover, Akkermansia exhibited combined direct and indirect positive associations in the gut, supporting its candidacy as a taxon of interest in immune modulation and tumor-associated microbiota patterns, as described in studies of immunotherapy responsiveness51,79.

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first evaluation of the microbiota within mouse bladder tissue. Although the relatively small sample size per group represents a limitation, this constraint is largely attributable to the inherently low microbial biomass of the bladder and the technical challenges associated with extracting microbial DNA from this tissue. Despite this limitation, the findings remain highly relevant, as they provide novel insights into the potential role of the microbiota in BC progression and response to treatment. While our results provide valuable insights, several limitations should be considered. First, sample sizes, particularly in bladder microbiota analyses (n = 3–6 per group), were limited, potentially reducing the statistical power to detect subtle differences, especially in beta diversity. Accordingly, intragroup inter-sample variation could only be assessed in an exploratory and descriptive manner, based on individual sample alpha-diversity values, beta-diversity analyses, and sample-level taxonomic composition profiles. Second, the study used 16S rRNA gene sequencing, which lacks the resolution of shotgun metagenomics and provides no direct information on microbial gene content or functional potential. In addition, genus-level assignments based on 16S rRNA sequencing should be interpreted cautiously, particularly for low-abundance taxa such as Mycobacterium, as these data do not allow strain-level discrimination. Although tools such as PICRUSt2 can provide exploratory predictions of putative functional potential from 16S rRNA gene data, these approaches do not directly measure microbial gene content or activity and therefore add an additional inferential layer to the interpretation of taxonomic shifts. For this reason, we chose not to include prediction-based functional analyses in the present work. Future studies incorporating functional metagenomics, transcriptomics, or metabolomics would better define the biological consequences of the observed shifts. Third, because host immune parameters were not measured in parallel with microbiota analyses, the study cannot distinguish whether the observed microbial shifts were directly treatment-driven or indirectly mediated through immune or inflammatory responses. Likewise, the mechanism linking intravesical mycobacterial treatment to intestinal microbiota modulation remains hypothetical and requires dedicated mechanistic studies. In addition, mediation-based analyses in the present study should be interpreted as exploratory statistical approaches to identify candidate taxa associated with tumor status, rather than as evidence of definitive causal microbial mediation. Fourth, the cross-sectional design of the study prevents temporal evaluation of microbiota changes and their dynamic relationship with tumor progression or therapeutic response. Longitudinal sampling would offer more detailed insights into causality. Fifth, evaluation of mycobacterial treatments (four weekly instillations) in the orthotopic murine model of BC is only possible in female mice, that did not permit evaluate plausible sex differences in microbiota composition. Lastly, species-specific differences between mouse and human microbiota caution against direct clinical extrapolation.

However, our findings in mice show notable similarities to human bladder microbiota composition, particularly in the relative abundance of dominant phyla. Firmicutes were the most abundant, comprising 41.46% in human BC tissue and between 50 and 70% in mice, while Bacteroidetes accounted for 28.23% in humans and 25–40% in mice80. Additionally, Proteobacteria was present in both groups, representing 22.78% in human cancer tissue and 5–15% in mice80, further supporting the consistent presence of these phyla across species. The observed differences between healthy and tumor-bearing mice, particularly the dominance of Staphylococcaceae in M. brumae-instilled healthy mice and Moraxellaceae in M. bovis BCG-treated animals, suggest that these mycobacterial therapies play a role in reshaping the bladder microbiome. The absence of Enterococcaceae in M. bovis BCG-treated tumor-bearing mice further highlights the potential effects in the microbiota of intravesical therapy. Moreover, the reduction of Pseudomonadaceae in M. brumae-treated animals is particularly relevant, given that members of this family have been linked to bladder inflammation and tumor recurrence14. The findings of this study reveal that M. bovis BCG treatment significantly reduces the bladder microbiota richness, as evidenced by the significantly lower number of observed zOTUs. This finding aligns with previous studies showing a reduction in microbiota richness from catheterized BC patients following M. bovis BCG intravesical therapy5. Interestingly, M. brumae-instilled mice displayed similar alpha diversity values in both tumor-bearing and healthy mice, suggesting that this mycobacterium might have a less profound impact on bladder microbiota richness. In contrast to the significant alpha diversity differences observed with M. bovis BCG, the lack of significant changes in beta diversity, as shown by NMDS, PCoA Bray–Curtis dissimilarity, and PCoA Unweighted UniFrac indices, suggests that the overall microbiota structure in the bladder might be influenced by individual animal variability rather than consistent group-level shifts. Nonetheless, these preclinical models provide an essential framework for understanding host-microbiota-tumor interactions and generating hypotheses for translational research.

Because 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing does not provide strain-level resolution, the detection of low-abundance reads assigned to the genus Mycobacterium in untreated samples should be interpreted cautiously and not as direct evidence of colonization by the instilled strains or stable endogenous persistence. In the detailed genus-level bladder microbiota characterization, Burkholderia was predominantly found in the bladders of tumor-bearing mice, aligning with previous research that indicates higher abundances of this genus in bladder neoplastic tissue81,82. In literature, other genera such as Pseudomonas, Escherichia-Shigella, and Acinetobacter are typically associated with bladder cancerous tissues14,18. Notably, M. bovis BCG and M. brumae instillations in tumor-bearing mice led to lower relative abundances of Escherichia-Shigella and Micrococcus, suggesting a treatment-specific shift in microbiota composition. Interestingly, a significant increase in Micrococcus relative abundance has previously been reported in the bladder microbiota of BC patients83. Similarly, M. bovis BCG-treatment in tumor-bearing mice also reduces the relative abundance of Enterococcus, a genus usually associated with urinary tract infections84. On the contrary, the genera Roseomonas and Bradyrhizobium were exclusively detected in the bladders of healthy mice. Previous studies have reported a decrease in the abundance of Roseomonas in the presence of bladder tumors, further supporting the findings in our study14,85. Both Roseomonas and Bradyrhizobium reported significant higher relative abundances in the urine of patients with primary compared to recurrent BC, suggesting a potential association between those genera abundance decrease and cancer recurrence86. Overall, genera such as Lactobacillus, Prevotella, and Ruminococcus are typically reported as more abundant in healthy bladder tissue compared to tumor18; however, in our study, these genera did not show significant differences between groups.

This study demonstrates that intravesical instillations of M. bovis BCG and M. brumae can significantly influence both bladder and gut microbiota in healthy and BC tumor-bearing mice. In healthy animals, microbial shifts were modest but indicated that even in the absence of disease, immunotherapy can subtly alter host microbial communities. In tumor-bearing mice, more pronounced microbiota changes were observed, particularly in untreated animals, which displayed microbial patterns commonly associated with cancer-related dysbiosis. Both mycobacterial treatments modulated these altered profiles, with each therapy inducing distinct microbial signatures in the bladder and gut. Notably, M. brumae and M. bovis BCG appeared to induce distinct patterns of microbiota remodeling, suggesting they may exert unique immunomodulatory effects. Overall, this microbiota modulation may contribute to treatment efficacy and immune response, offering new perspectives on the interplay between microbial communities and cancer mycobacterial immunotherapy. Moreover, mediation-based exploratory analysis identified genera such as Akkermansia, Mycobacterium, Escherichia/Shigella, and Streptococcus as candidate taxa statistically associated with tumor status across tissues, while Bacteroides and Ruminococcus were more frequently associated with healthy patterns or protective taxa. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating associations rather than evidence of definitive causal mediation. These insights integrate compositional patterns and mediation-based associations across tissues, highlighting candidate microbial taxa as potential targets for future biomarker development and therapeutic modulation in BC.
