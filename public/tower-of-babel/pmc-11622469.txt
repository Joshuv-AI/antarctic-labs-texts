Impact of porcine respiratory disease complex on carcass weight and meatiness: quantitative insights from a mixed-model analysis
Authors: Paulina Przyborowska, Renata Lewko-Wojtowicz, Piotr Cybulski, Dominiek Maes, Dawid Tobolski
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPorcine respiratory disease complex (PRDC) significantly impacts the swine industry worldwide, leading to economic losses due to poor growth performance, reduced feed efficiency, higher medication costs, and adversely affecting pig welfare by causing clinical symptoms such as fever, cough, loss of appetite, lethargy, and dyspnea. Cranio-ventral pulmonary consolidation (CVPC) and pleuritis are the most frequent macroscopic lung lesions observed in PRDC and are indicators of decreased animal welfare. This study aimed to quantify the effects of CVPC and pleurisy on carcass weight, meatiness, and average daily carcass weight gain (ADCWG) in fattening pigs, thereby assessing their impact on both production and welfare. A total of 679 slaughtered pigs from seven batches (farms) were evaluated for lung lesions at slaughter. We employed a mixed-model analysis to assess the correlation between lung lesions and production parameters across the farms.ResultsThe mean prevalence of lesions was 23.86% for CVPC and 15.46% for pleurisy, indicating a significant presence of respiratory disease affecting animal welfare. Pigs with severe lung lesions (≥ 15.1%) exhibited significantly lower ADCWG compared to pigs without lesions (0.951 kg/day vs. 0.997 kg/day, p = 0.024), reflecting reduced growth performance and welfare. The mixed-effects model revealed that lesions in the right apical lobe and dorso-caudal pleurisy were associated with significant reductions in carcass weight (− 2.77 kg and − 2.29 kg, respectively) and carcass meat (− 1.76 kg and − 1.43 kg, respectively). An economic analysis under average market price conditions demonstrated that severe lung lesions could lead to financial losses of up to 11.53 EUR per 100 kg of meat, emphasizing the economic impact of compromised welfare due to respiratory diseases.ConclusionsThis study provides quantitative evidence of the negative impact of CVPC and pleurisy on carcass weight and meatiness in fattening pigs. The findings underscore the importance of effective respiratory disease management in swine production, highlighting potential areas for targeted interventions to improve animal health and economic outcomes.

Background

Porcine respiratory disease complex (PRDC) significantly impacts the global pig industry, primarily by affecting growing and fattening pigs. This multifaceted syndrome, characterized by a combination of pathogens and environmental conditions affecting the respiratory system. Several bacteria and viruses are involved in the development of PRDC. Mycoplasma hyopneumoniae (M. hyopneumoniae), Pasteurella multocida (P. multocida), Actinobacillus pleuropneumoniae (A. pleuropneumoniae), swine influenza viruses (SIV) and porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV) [1].

Primely, PRDC leads to decrease animal welfare by the slow growth and manifestation of clinical symptoms of disease as fever, cough, loss of appetite, lethargy and dyspnea. Secondly, PRDC is associated with substantial economic losses through decreased growth rates, increased feed conversion and mortality, and higher costs for veterinary treatments and management [1]. Pleurisy and cranio-ventral pulmonary consolidation (CVPC) are the most frequently diagnosed lesions in slaughter-age pig lungs at the abattoir, and they are strongly associated with PRDC [2].

In compliance with legislation [3], all pig carcasses undergo routine meat inspection at abattoirs to ensure food safety and animal welfare. However, the data collected during this process holds value beyond immediate health and safety concerns [4]. It provides crucial information for estimating the prevalence of specific conditions, informing herd health advisory services, and developing more effective disease management and prevention strategies [4–8].

Abattoir evaluation of lung lesions plays a pivotal role in assessing the prevalence and severity of respiratory diseases in swine herds [9–12]. These evaluations are instrumental in detecting subclinical states, providing insights into ongoing respiratory issues, and assessing risk factors and vaccine efficacy at farm level [7, 13]. The presence of lung lesions, particularly CVPC and pleurisy, is often associated with reduced animal productivity, such as decreased and uneven weight gain and reduced feed conversion efficiency [4, 14–16].

While the negative impact of respiratory conditions such as CVPC and pleurisy on fattening pigs is well-documented at the farm level [17], most studies primarily quantify these impacts in terms of live animal performance metrics, rather than focusing on meat quality or carcass traits [14, 15]. Assessing the economic impact associated with lung lesions presents unique challenges, as these losses are multifaceted and span the entire production chain, from farm to abattoir. At the farm level, respiratory conditions can contribute to poorer feed conversion and slower growth rates, indirectly influencing overall production efficiency. At the abattoir level, lung lesions are a major cause of lung condemnations and can lead to slower slaughter line speeds due to the need for additional carcass trimming. CVPC alone accounts for approximately 50% of lung rejections, indicating its substantial effect on processing efficiency. These factors incur additional processing costs at abattoirs, including increased labor requirements, and while they are substantial, their full economic impact is often underestimated or difficult to quantify.

Moreover, economic losses associated with lung lesions depend on various factors, including regional market prices, farm cost structures (e.g., feed costs), and specific management practices [18, 19]. Despite the significance of CVPC and pleurisy within the global pig industry, the precise scale of economic losses due to these conditions remains inadequately detailed in current literature. There is limited research that comprehensively assesses the impact of lung lesions on both meat productivity and economic revenue, particularly at the abattoir level (Fig. 1).

This study aims to quantify the impact of CVPC and pleurisy on the production and economic performance of fattening pigs. Specifically, we seek to: (I) Assess the prevalence of CVPC and pleurisy in slaughter-age pigs from multiple farms. (II) Determine the relationship between lung lesions and key production parameters, including carcass weight, meatiness, and average daily carcass weight gain (ADCWG). (III) Evaluate the economic consequences of these respiratory conditions under various market price scenarios. By integrating data from abattoir inspections with farm-level production information, this research aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of how these common respiratory issues affect both animal performance and final product quality. The findings from this study will contribute to the development of more effective herd health management strategies and help improve the economic efficiency of pig production.

Results

The production characteristics of the fattening pigs from the seven different swine batches showed notable variability. The number of pigs per batch was mostly consistent at 100, except for batch 4 and 5, which had 99 and 80 pigs, respectively. Average fattening liveweight ranged from 115.73 kg in batch 5 to 122.66 kg in batch 3. The fattening period varied, with the shortest period being 86.60 days in batch 2 and the longest being 99.40 days in batch 7. Average daily gain (ADG) was highest in batch 2 (982.00 g/day) and lowest in batch 1 (925.40 g/day). Carcass weight also differed among the batches; it was highest in batch 7 (94.10 kg) and the lowest (84.40 kg) in batch 1. Meatiness percentages ranged from 58.86% in batch 7 to 60.82% in batch 2, indicating variations in the proportion of the carcass composed of meat among the different batches (Table 1).

Assessment of macroscopic lung lesions

The prevalence of lesions among different swine batches showed significant variability, as indicated by the Chi-square test results (χ² = 96.387, p < 0.001) (Table 2). Batch 1 had the highest overall prevalence of lesions (55%), with 45% of pigs having no lesions, 17% with 0.1%-5% lesions, 26% with 5.1%-15% lesions, and 12% greater than or equal to 15.1% lesions. In contrast, Batch 2 had the lowest overall prevalence (11%), with 89% of pigs having no lesions, 6% with 0.1%-5% lesions, 4% with 5.1%-15% lesions, and only 1% greater than or equal to 15.1% lesions. Batch 5 had an intermediate prevalence of lesions, with 67.50% having no lesions, 17.50% with 0.1%-5% lesions, 13.75% with 5.1%-15% lesions, and 1.25% greater than or equal to 15.1% lesions.

Weighing of carcasses, assessment of meatiness, and average daily carcass weight gain

The analysis of variance for carcass weight, carcass meat, and ADCWG across different categories of lesions revealed significant differences (Table 3), particularly between group 1 and 4. Pigs with no lesions (group 1) had the highest values, with an average carcass weight of 92.70 kg (SD = 6.69), carcass meat of 55.57 kg (SD = 4.12), and an ADCWG of 0.997 kg/day (SD = 0.08). In contrast, pigs with greater than or equal to 15.1% lesions (group 4) had the lowest values, with an average carcass weight of 91.32 kg (SD = 7.37), carcass meat of 54.60 kg (SD = 3.73), and an ADCWG of 0.95 kg/day (SD = 0.07, p = 0.024).

Specific lung regions were more frequently affected by lesions independently of other regions e.g. the majority of lesions were located in the dorso-caudal pleurisy area with 105 cases (15.5% of the total population of 679 pigs). The intermediate and right cardiac lobes were also frequently affected namely 80 cases (11.8%) and 81 cases (11.9%), respectively (Fig. 2).

Significant co-occurrences between different lung regions were identified, highlighting strong associations between lesion occurrences in certain areas. The right diaphragmatic lobe and the intermediate lobe had the highest co-occurrence with 42 cases (6.2%), followed closely by the right diaphragmatic lobe and the right cardiac lobe with 39 cases (5.7%). The right cardiac lobe and the intermediate lobe had 37 cases (5.4%) of concurrent lesions, suggesting these regions are commonly affected together. Other notable co-occurrences include 33 cases (4.9%) between the intermediate lobe and the left cardiac lobe, and 33 cases (4.9%) between the right cardiac lobe and the right apical lobe. The right diaphragmatic lobe and the right apical lobe had 32 cases (4.7%) of concurrent lesions, while the right cardiac lobe and the left cardiac lobe co-occurred in 30 cases (4.4%). Finally, there were 27 instances (4.0%) of co-occurrence between the left cardiac lobe and the right apical lobe, and 26 cases (3.8%) each between the intermediate lobe and the right apical lobe, and the right diaphragmatic lobe and the left cardiac lobe (Fig. 2).

Economic analysis

The economic loss analysis revealed significant financial impacts associated with lung lesions. The analysis considered three price scenarios: minimum (201.86 EUR/100kg), average (221.77 EUR/100kg), and maximum (235.59 EUR/100kg) observed prices.

The results showed that pigs with 0.1-5% lesions incurred losses of 2.62, 2.88, and 3.06 EUR/pig under the minimum, average, and maximum price scenarios, respectively, compared to pigs with no lesions. Pigs with 5.1–15% lesions showed slightly higher losses of 2.83, 3.10, and 3.30 EUR/pig. The most substantial economic impact was observed in pigs with greater than or equal to 15.1% lesions, with losses of 10.50, 11.53, and 12.25 EUR/pig under the three price scenarios. The maximum loss relative to the group with no lesions was 11.53 EUR/pig under the average price scenario, highlighting the significant economic consequences of severe lung lesions (Fig. 3).

For carcass weight and carcass meat, significant negative associations were found with lesions in the right apical lobe and dorso-caudal pleurisy (Tables 4 and 5). Specifically, lesions in the right apical lobe were associated with a decrease in carcass weight by 2.77 kg (p = 0.01), while dorso-caudal pleurisy was associated with a decrease of 2.29 kg (p < 0.001). These reductions in carcass weight translate to significant economic losses across different price scenarios. At the minimum price of 201.86 EUR/100kg, lesions in the right apical lobe resulted in losses of 5.59 EUR/pig, while dorso-caudal pleurisy caused losses of 4.62 EUR/pig. Under the average price scenario of 221.77 EUR/100kg, these losses increased to 6.14 EUR/pig for right apical lobe lesions and 5.08 EUR/pig for dorso-caudal pleurisy. In the maximum price scenario of 235.59 EUR/100kg, the losses further rose to 6.53 EUR/pig and 5.39 EUR/pig for right apical lobe lesions and dorso-caudal pleurisy, respectively.

Discussion

This study focused on assessing the impact of CVPC and pleurisy changes in lungs on production and economic parameters of fattening pigs. Our findings reveal significant implications for the swine industry, highlighting the importance of respiratory health in maintaining optimal production and economic outcomes.

The mean prevalence of lesions observed in our study was 23.86% for CVPC and 15.46% for pleurisy, both lower than those reported in previous Polish studies, where a 45.10% prevalence of macroscopic lung lesions for CVPC was observed at the slaughterhouse [18]. Several factors may explain the discrepancy between our findings and prior Polish data. Firstly, our study focuses specifically on the prevalence of CVPC and pleurisy as distinct disease entities, excluding any pigs exhibiting additional pathological conditions. Secondly, the previous study included batches from multiple farms (n = 102) with different management strategies and examined a significantly larger sample size (n = 3060) [18]. It is important to emphasize here that our study was designed with sufficient statistical power to assess the impact of lung lesions on carcass quality according to the experimental framework.

Moreover, differences in prevalence may reflect the presence of endemic pathogens within each herd [1] as well as seasonal variations. Studies have shown a seasonal correlation with respiratory tract infections and lung lesion prevalence in pigs [19–21]. While the previous study was conducted from January to March [18], our study spanned from March to May. According to Cobanovic et al. (2020), the highest prevalence of pneumonia-related lesions in slaughtered pigs was recorded during winter (58.33%) and autumn (56.67%), while the spring and summer prevalence rates were considerably lower, at 26.67% and 20.00%, respectively [19], which aligns with our findings. Interestingly, the study found no statistically significant seasonal differences in the prevalence of pleurisy [19].

The prevalence rates in our study fall within the range reported in other European countries, which varies widely from 24 to 73%, depending on the source [1, 7–9, 11, 22–32]. For example, the lowest CVPC prevalence was recorded in Belgium (24%) [25] and the United Kingdom (27%) [27], while the highest was observed in Germany (73%) [28]. Data from France showed CVPC prevalence rates of 72% in 2005, 58% in 2007, and 69% in 2012 [1, 29, 33]. Monitoring of pleurisy prevalence in Italy revealed rates of 48% in 2012 [24], 50% in 2019 [30], and 42% in 2020 [31]. In countries with continuous monitoring, such as France and Italy, no linear decline in lesion prevalence has been observed over time. Based on the available data, despite improvements in herd management practices, vaccination programs, and biosecurity measures, the prevalence of lung lesions has not significantly decreased. This suggests that CVPC and pleurisy continue to be major concerns impacting swine productivity, as also highlighted by Petri et al. (2023) [32].

Our study provides strong evidence for the association between lung lesions and decreases in productivity, as expressed by various production characteristics. This relationship has been consistently reported in previous studies [16, 17, 34, 35]. The impact of lesions on the performance was particularly evident in batches 2 and 5. Batch 2, which had the lowest overall prevalence of lesions (11%), demonstrated the highest ADG and meatiness, and the shortest fattening period among the investigated population. This observation aligns with the general understanding that healthier lungs contribute to better feed conversion and growth rates [16]. Our findings are consistent with previous research reporting that the ADG of animals with CVPC was reduced by 7–9% during the fattening period [36]. Our results also support the more detailed findings of Hill et al. (1992) [37], who reported that ADG decreased by 41.1 g for every 10% of the pig’s lung surface affected by CVPC. Regarding pleurisy, our observations align with those of Pagot et al. (2017) [29], who found that pleurisy at slaughter was associated with an ADG decrease of 39 g.

Our study focused on calculating ADCWG, carcass weight, and meat weight as critical indicators of pig performance affected by respiratory lesions. The results demonstrated that pigs with more severe lung lesions had significantly lower daily carcass weight gain compared to pigs without lesions. The substantial difference in ADCWG (p = 0.024) among lesion severity groups suggests that lung lesions negatively impact overall production efficiency. The higher carcass weight observed in pigs from Groups 2 and 3, despite the presence of lung lesions, is likely due to an increased proportion of connective tissue and fat in these carcasses. This effect can be attributed to management practices on farms with a high prevalence of CVPC, where animals had to be kept for an extended fattening period to reach the target slaughter weight. As a result, the prolonged fattening phase led to higher carcass weights in Groups 2 and 3, despite the reduced ADCWG. In this context, metrics such as carcass meat per kilogram per animal and ADCWG serve as valuable parameters to assess carcass composition and efficiency in relation to feeding days. This nuanced finding indicates that CVPC may lead to higher carcass weights in affected groups due to extended fattening, though at the cost of daily gain efficiency.

Furthermore, the observed relationship is supported by a statistically significant negative correlation (r=-0.191, p = 0.015) between ADCWG and lesion severity. Although this correlation is weak, it reveals a clear trend: as lung lesion severity increases, daily carcass weight gain decreases. This finding holds critical implications for the swine industry by quantifying the direct impact of respiratory health on a vital production metric.

The ADCWG value represents the carcass mass gain per day (may accurately reflect production efficiency), aligning with changes in ADG and feed conversion ratio (FCR). However, ADCWG is a more specific indicator for assessing carcass impact than ADG or FCR, as it focuses on carcass weight rather than live weight gain, thus offering more precise insight into production efficiency. Additionally, ADCWG allows for individual animal assessments, whereas ADG and FCR are often applied on a group level in swine production. ADCWG also serves as a versatile measure for evaluating the secondary effects of production declines caused by welfare issues, health conditions, or improvements in rearing and breeding systems. While ADG and FCR provide meaningful data, the conversion of feed energy to carcass weight is more directly evaluated by ADCWG.

In cattle, studies such as Kelly et al. (2019) have explored alternative definitions of residual energy intake (REI) as a measure of feed efficiency, recommending REI calculations that consider carcass weight and fat as energy metrics (REICWF) [38]. Producers fattening cattle for slaughter receive payment based on carcass weight rather than live weight. Kelly et al.‘s study found that REICWF produced a correlation of 0.36 with REI traits, compared to a 0.50 correlation for traditional REI. Selecting REICWF over traditional REI can thus better reflect production value [38]. This approach supports the adoption of carcass-focused metrics like ADCWG in swine, as they better describe production efficiency and economic value by focusing on the final product rather than intermediate growth metrics.

Our productivity analysis indicated that CVPC and dorso-caudal pleurisy could result in significant carcass weight losses of 2.77 kg and 2.29 kg per animal with lesions, respectively. These results are consistent with previous studies linking lung lesion severity to reduced carcass weight [18, 34, 39–41]. The alignment of our findings with multiple studies across different regions highlights the global importance of respiratory health for swine productivity.

It is important to note that our findings diverge from those of Escobar et al. (2004) [42], who reported no consistent effect of CVPC severity on carcass weight. This discrepancy may arise from differences in study conditions, as Escobar et al. conducted their research under controlled experimental infections, which may not fully represent real-world farm conditions. Our study, conducted under typical production settings, may therefore offer a more accurate assessment of how lung lesions impact carcass characteristics in actual swine production environments.

The economic analysis demonstrates the significant financial impact of lung lesions on pork production. By considering three price scenarios, we quantified the economic losses associated with different severities of lung lesions. The most substantial impact was observed in pigs with severe lesions (≥ 15.1%), with losses reaching up to 12.25 EUR per pig in the maximum price scenario. Even mild lesions result in noticeable economic losses, while severe lesions can lead to losses exceeding 10 EUR per pig. When extrapolated to the scale of large commercial pig operations, these losses can amount to substantial sums, potentially affecting the profitability and sustainability of pig farming enterprises. These findings highlight the considerable economic burden that respiratory diseases place on the swine industry, emphasizing the potential for substantial cumulative losses in large-scale operations.

Moreover, our study provided detailed calculations of meat weight impacted by lung lesions. Since revenue depends on carcass weight and meatiness, and the percentage of meat tissue in the carcass defines its quality class, the reduction of meat weight caused by lung lesions is of economic significance. We found substantial losses in carcass meat weight correlated with CVPC and dorso-caudal pleurisy, with declines of 1.76 kg and 1.43 kg, respectively.

The findings of our study have significant implications for herd health management in the swine industry, underscoring the vital importance of respiratory disease prevention and control in optimizing production efficiency and achieving favorable economic outcomes. These results emphasize the need for comprehensive respiratory health programs that integrate early detection, targeted interventions, and continuous monitoring to maintain respiratory health across herds. Given the observed variability in lesion prevalence and severity across different batches, it is evident that farm-specific factors play a crucial role, highlighting the importance of tailored approaches to disease prevention and control.

Our economic analysis provides strong support for investing in respiratory health initiatives, demonstrating that the cost savings from preventing severe lung lesions can justify substantial expenditures on preventive measures. A deeper understanding of lesion location and co-occurrence may also inform more focused intervention strategies, particularly in high-prevalence areas like the dorso-caudal pleurisy region, enabling more precise targeting of resources.

In addition, investigating the effectiveness of various intervention strategies to reduce the prevalence and severity of lung lesions could offer swine producers practical and actionable guidance. Effective strategies may include targeted vaccination programs against prevalent respiratory pathogens, such as M. hyopneumoniae and PRRSV, strengthening biosecurity measures to limit disease introduction and spread, and optimizing environmental factors like ventilation to reduce stress and control airborne pathogens. Research demonstrates that these measures can significantly lower the prevalence and severity of lung lesions, leading to better herd health and increased productivity. Together, these insights underscore the value of robust respiratory health management as a means to bolster both animal welfare and economic resilience within the swine industry [43, 44].

While our study provides valuable insights into the impact of lung lesions on swine production, it is important to acknowledge its limitations. The sampling of herds was not done randomly, which may have introduced bias into our estimated prevalence of lesions. Our original intention to include only lungs from carcasses deemed fit for human consumption may have led to an underestimation of the true prevalence and severity of lung lesions in the overall swine population. The limited number of farms in our study may restrict the generalizability of our findings.

Future research could address these limitations by incorporating larger, randomly selected samples from a wider range of farms. Longitudinal studies tracking pigs from birth to slaughter could provide more detailed insights into the development of lung lesions and their impact on growth and production parameters over time. Additionally, studies investigating the effectiveness of various intervention strategies in reducing the prevalence and severity of lung lesions could provide valuable practical guidance for swine producers. Moreover, future research could explore the potential synergistic effects of different types of lung lesions and their combined impact on production parameters. The co-occurrence of lesions in certain lung regions observed in our study suggests that such interactions may be significant and warrant further investigation.

Conclusion

This study highlights the substantial negative impact of CVPC and dorso-caudal pleurisy on swine carcass weight and meatiness, underscoring the need for enhanced respiratory disease management in pig farming. The findings emphasize the value of utilizing abattoir data to develop precise health strategies and tailored interventions specific to each farm. By addressing these respiratory health challenges, the swine industry can improve productivity, economic efficiency, and overall animal welfare, fostering more sustainable and profitable farming practices.

Methods

Animal management, housing conditions, and experimental framework

The study was conducted from March to May 2023 as part of a project by Ceva Animal Health Poland (Warsaw, Poland), which provided a diagnostic package to pig producers facing persistent respiratory disease issues in their herds. Data were collected from inspections conducted at a single abattoir, involving fattening pigs from seven pig farms owned by the same producer. Although all farms practiced the same management protocols, each was located separately and operated by different staff members.

All pigs were of the Polish Great White breed. From each farm, one batch of approximately 100 pigs (sex not considered) was evaluated. Inspections included a visual assessment of carcasses as well as red and white offal. The abattoir’s slaughtering capacity was approximately 150 pigs per hour. A random sample of 100 lungs was taken from each batch to provide a reliable and accurate estimate of lung lesion severity at the herd level [45]. To reduce the potential influence of other disease conditions, animals displaying additional macroscopic pathological changes beyond lung lesions were excluded from the random selection process. Two trained lung investigators performed lung inspections. Livestock production data for each batch, including fattening duration, ADG, and mortality rate, were provided by the pig producer.

Assessment of macroscopic lung lesions

Lung evaluations focused on the left apical lobe, left cardiac lobe, left diaphragmatic lobe, intermediate lobe, right diaphragmatic lobe, right cardiac lobe, right apical lobe, and regions affected by cranial pleurisy, dorso-caudal pleurisy, and scar presence.

CVPC were scored using a method adapted from Madec and Kobisch (1982) [46]. Each lobe was scored on a scale from 0 to 4, based on the percentage of the lobe affected by lesions, with scores as follows: 0 for no lesions, 1 for less than 25% affected, 2 for 26–50% affected, 3 for 51–75% affected, and 4 for more than 76% affected. To account for the different volumes each lobe contributes to the total lung, the scores were adjusted according to the method described by Christensen et al. (1999) [47]. The percentage of the affected area for each lobe was multiplied by its relative weight contribution to the total lung volume: right apical lobe (11%), right cardiac lobe (10%), right diaphragmatic lobe (34%), left apical lobe (5%), left cardiac lobe (6%), left diaphragmatic lobe (29%), and intermediate lobe (5%) (Fig. 3). Based on the extent of the macroscopic lung lesions, the pigs were categorized into four groups: Group 1 – pigs without macroscopic lesions; Group 2–0.1–5% of lesions; Group 3–5.1–15% of lesions; Group 4 – greater than or equal to 15.1% of lesions.

Pleurisy in the dorso-caudal lobes was scored using a modified version of the Slaughterhouse Pleurisy Evaluation System (SPES) developed by Dottori et al. (2007) [48]. Cranial pleurisy, characterized by adhesions between lobes or between the lung and heart, and the presence of scars indicating previous pneumonic lesions were recorded as either present or absent for further analysis. Data collection was facilitated by the digital Ceva Lung Program (Ceva Animal Health, France), where data were recorded at the line operating speed, ensuring consistency and accuracy in the evaluation process.

Weighing of carcasses, assessment of meatiness, and average daily carcass weight gain

The animals were weighed at the beginning of the fattening phase (at 11 weeks of age) to establish their initial weights, the mortality rate for each group was involved in the calculation of initial weight. After slaughter, the carcasses were weighed to determine their final weights. Additionally, the meatiness of the carcasses was assessed and expressed as a percentage, representing the proportion of the carcass composed of meat, as opposed to fat and bone.

Abattoir production data, including carcass weight and meatiness, were provided by the meat producer. The carcass was weighed immediately after splitting and final washing to obtain the carcass weight. Meatiness was measured with a portable Sydel CGM Fat Lean Sensor device (Syleps, France) on the musculus longissimus dorsi, pars lumbalis, on the left half of the carcass, at the level of the 10th and 11th ribs following applicable legislation (Regulation (EU) 2017/1182) [49].

Data analysis and statistical methods

The statistical analysis was structured to ensure the reliability and validity of the results. Data were collected and stored in text files and MS Excel spreadsheets for efficient organization and accessibility. The analysis was completed using Python 3.12.3 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA, 2023), R 4.3.3 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria, 2023), and JupyterLab (Project Jupyter, Berkeley, CA, USA, 2023), providing the necessary tools for managing statistical models and data visualization.

The normality of the data distribution was confirmed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, with a significance level set at 0.05. Additionally, we examined the homogeneity of variances using Levene’s test to validate the assumptions required for parametric analysis. These steps were crucial for the application of parametric statistical methods and for ensuring the accuracy of inferential statistics.

Data from the abattoir inspections of the seven pig batches, comprising of 679 pigs, were analyzed using regression and mixed-model analyses, incorporating the batch as fixed variable. Statistical analyses were conducted with individual pigs as the experimental units.

The differences in lesion prevalence among the swine farms were statistically analyzed using the Chi-square test. Following the Chi-square test, a post hoc test with z-score corrections was conducted to identify specific farms that showed significant differences. The Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons and control the family-wise error rate.

A regression model was developed using Python 3.12.3 to estimate the linear relationship between the weighted macroscopic lung lesion scores, as measured at the slaughterhouse, and the ADCWG of pigs during the fattening phase. The lesion scores were calculated by multiplying the percentage of the affected area of each lobe by its relative weight contribution to the total lung volume. This model included pigs with lesions categorized into groups 2 to 4. Additionally, an analysis of variance was employed to estimate and compare the carcass weight, carcass meat, and ADCWG associated with each group of lesions (1, 2, 3 and 4). All multiple pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni corrections to control for Type I errors. The mean and standard deviation (SD) of lesion score groups were calculated and presented in a Table 3.

Additionally, a mixed-effects model was employed to estimate and compare the effects of different types of lesions on carcass weight, carcass meat, and ADCWG. The lesions considered in the model included those in the left apical lobe, left cardiac lobe, left diaphragmatic lobe, intermediate lobe, right diaphragmatic lobe, right cardiac lobe, right apical lobe, cranial pleurisy, dorso-caudal pleurisy, and the presence of scars.

The mixed-effects model was structured to account for the random effects of the farm and other potential confounders while focusing on the fixed effects of the specific lung lesions on the measured outcomes.
