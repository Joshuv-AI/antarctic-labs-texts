Therapeutic drug monitoring uncovering incorrect antiretroviral drug intake in a patient with swallowing difficulties
Authors: Giorgia Carrozzo, Dario Cattaneo, Andrea Giacomelli, Andrea Gori, Anna Lisa Ridolfo, Cristina Gervasoni, Maria Vittoria Cossu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA woman living with HIV reported swallowing difficulties that interfered with oral antiretroviral therapy. Treatment with darunavir/cobicistat/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide (Symtuza®) was initiated, with the patient choosing to chew the tablet before swallowing because of her swallowing difficulties. Therapeutic drug monitoring, performed because of this non-standard administration, revealed unexpectedly low darunavir trough concentrations despite reported adherence and maintained virological suppression. A structured pharmacological assessment revealed that the patient had been consistently taking the medication in the fasting state, despite initial counselling to take it with food. After correction of the drug intake conditions, darunavir exposure increased to therapeutic levels. This case highlights the clinical value of therapeutic drug monitoring in identifying medication intake errors and optimising antiretroviral therapy in patients with swallowing difficulties.Keywords: Therapeutic drug monitoring, Antiretroviral therapy, Darunavir, Medication administration, Long-acting injectable therapy

Introduction

Swallowing difficulties are a relatively common yet often underestimated issue in both the general population and among people with HIV (PWH) [1,2]. These difficulties may significantly compromise adherence and drug exposure, particularly when fixed-dose combinations are not designed to be split, crushed, or chewed [3,4]. Management of such patients often requires individualized strategies, including alternative formulations or administration strategies [5]. Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) may play a crucial role in identifying pharmacokinetic variability related to non-standard drug administration and in guiding individualized pharmacotherapy [4,6,7].

Case presentation

A 44-year-old Caucasian woman with no relevant comorbidities was diagnosed with HIV infection in August 2023 following her husband’s positive test. She was not taking any concomitant medications or supplements. At baseline, HIV RNA was undetectable (<20 copies/mL) and CD4 cell count was 1365 cells/mm³. Given her favourable viro-immunological profile, she was initially reluctant to initiate antiretroviral therapy (ART). In June 2025, HIV RNA increased to 52 copies/mL, rising to 484 copies/mL the following month and to 1700 copies/mL two months later. ART was initiated with the fixed-dose bictegravir/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide. However, after a few days, the patient reported inability to swallow the tablet and discontinued the regimen.

During the clinical assessment, she reported a lifelong difficulty specifically with swallowing tablets and capsules, with no difficulty swallowing food or liquids. No signs of oral candidiasis were present; oesophageal candidiasis was not specifically investigated given the absence of dysphagia for food or liquids and her high CD4 cell count. Although dissolution of the tablet in water is indicated by the EACS guidelines as a possible alternative when it cannot be swallowed whole [8], this option was not attempted and, given the limited clinical experience with this administration strategy, we preferred to switch to an alternative antiretroviral regimen. The EACS guidelines report that crushing darunavir/cobicistat/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide (Symtuza®) has no significant effect on the pharmacokinetics of its components [8]. The patient did not attempt to crush the tablet and, for practical reasons, preferred to chew it before swallowing. Alternative formulations, including liquid darunavir and ritonavir sachets, were considered but were not available through our hospital pharmacy at the time. The patient was advised to take the medication with food. One month after ART initiation, given the non-standard administration of the tablet (chewing before swallowing), therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) was requested in consultation with the clinical pharmacology service to evaluate darunavir exposure. Darunavir trough plasma concentrations, collected 24 h after the previous dose, were unexpectedly low (414 ng/mL: protein-adjusted 50% effective concentration (PA-EC₅₀) of darunavir against protease inhibitor-resistant HIV-1 strains: 550 ng/mL [9,10]), yet HIV RNA was < 20 copies/mL. At the subsequent monthly assessment, darunavir trough levels further decreased to 203 ng/mL, while virological suppression was maintained. The patient reported optimal adherence, as confirmed by pharmacy refill records. In collaboration with the clinical pharmacologist, a structured assessment explored other potential determinants of reduced drug exposure, focusing on administration conditions, food intake, and drug–drug interactions. This evaluation revealed that, despite the initial counselling, the patient had consistently taken the chewed tablet in the fasting state, having forgotten the recommendation to take the drug with food. The patient was provided with additional counselling on correct drug intake and at repeat TDM assessment darunavir trough concentration increased to 723 ng/mL.

Given the swallowing difficulties and the patient’s preference to avoid daily oral therapy, the patient was switched to long-acting injectable (LAI) cabotegravir plus rilpivirine. As she was already virologically suppressed (HIV RNA <20 copies/mL), she met the eligibility criteria for this regimen. Virological suppression was maintained at follow-up.

Discussion

Crushing or chewing of Symtuza® is not recommended in the product information [9]. However, in a phase 1 randomized, open-label, three-period, three-treatment crossover study in healthy volunteers, Brown et al. demonstrated that the pharmacokinetic profiles of darunavir/cobicistat/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide administered with food were not significantly modified when the tablet was crushed or split compared with the whole formulation [11]. Administration of a crushed tablet resulted only in a modest decrease in tenofovir alafenamide exposure, unlikely to be clinically significant. These findings suggest that, at least in healthy volunteers, modification of tablet integrity per se may not substantially impair bioavailability when the drug is taken under appropriate conditions, although real-world data remain limited.

At first glance, our case appears to contrast with these findings, as low darunavir exposure was observed despite reported adherence and absence of drug–drug interactions. However, the key determinant was ultimately identified as drug intake in the fasting state, a well-established factor significantly reducing darunavir bioavailability. Therefore, the reduced exposure was not primarily attributable to chewing the tablet but rather to incorrect intake conditions. Notably, the relatively low viral load at ART initiation (1700 copies/mL) likely contributed to the rapid achievement and maintenance of virological suppression despite subtherapeutic darunavir concentrations. In this context, the residual antiviral activity of the regimen, together with a limited viral burden, may have been sufficient to achieve viral suppression, potentially masking the underlying pharmacokinetic issue.

This case highlights the clinical value of TDM not only as a tool for quantifying drug exposure but also for uncovering hidden determinants of pharmacokinetic variability in real-world practice, particularly when adherence appears optimal. In our patient, interpretation of the TDM results prompted a careful reassessment of drug intake conditions, ultimately identifying the cause of the reduced exposure. This observation also underscores that adherence should not be defined solely as regular drug intake, but also as correct administration according to recommended conditions. Close collaboration between clinicians and clinical pharmacologists is therefore essential for the appropriate interpretation of TDM results and for identifying correctable causes of altered drug exposure.

Finally, long-acting injectable (LAI) antiretroviral therapies, such as cabotegravir plus rilpivirine, may represent an additional option for selected patients with barriers to daily oral therapy, including swallowing difficulties [12,13]. However, individualized management remains essential to optimize treatment in this setting, ideally supported by close collaboration between clinicians and clinical pharmacologists.
