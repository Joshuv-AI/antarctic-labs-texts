An in vitro study of a novel intraoperative 3D-printed device for intentional replantation
Authors: Fangzhe Li, Fushi Wang, Ying Zhang, Yeyu Lin, Wenhui Tang, Liuyan Meng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo improve the accuracy and efficiency of intentional replantation, we designed a novel intraoperative three-dimensionally printed device. This study aimed to evaluate its assistance in intentional replantation by comparing the accuracy and efficiency of the procedure performed with and without the device in extracted premolars and molars.MethodsTwenty-two mandibular premolars and 18 mandibular molars were collected and equally divided into two comparable groups according to tooth type. In the experimental group, a novel three-dimensionally printed intraoperative device was used to enhance tooth stabilisation, and improve root-end resection and preparation accuracy. In the control group, a saline-soaked gauze was used to hold the crown. Accuracy was assessed by measuring the length and angle deviation of the root-end resection, and the angle deviation of the root-end preparation. Efficiency was evaluated based on operation time. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS software, with comparisons made using the Satterthwaite t-test and Mann–Whitney U test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.ResultsThe angle deviations for root-end resection with premolars and molars in experimental group were significantly lower compared with those in control group (P < 0.05). Analysis of the length deviations for root-end resection, the angle deviations for root-end preparation and the operation time between two groups revealed no significant differences with both tooth types.ConclusionsThe novel intraoperative device effectively supported intentional replantation without reducing operational efficiency. Furthermore, it improved root-end resection angle accuracy in both premolars and molars.Keywords: Accuracy, Efficiency, Guided endodontics, Intentional replantation, Root-end resection

Background

Intentional replantation is an endodontic surgical procedure commonly indicated for refractory apical periodontitis [1–5] with reported success rates up to 80.2% [6]. Compared with apical microsurgery, it offers several advantages, including the ability to fully inspect the entire root surface [2, 4, 7], improved management of complex conditions such as dens invaginatus and root resorption [8, 9], greater operative efficiency, and reduced risk of damage to critical anatomical structures [1, 10, 11].

The procedure involves atraumatically tooth extraction, root-end resection, root-end preparation, root-end filling, and replantation [12, 13]. Conventional extraoral handling techniques such as using saline-soaked gauze or dental forceps to grip the crown [4, 14] present operational challenges. Maintaining tooth stability to avoid accidental periodontal ligament damage due to slips requires heightened concentration. During the root-end resection phase, it is recommended to resect 3 mm of the root end, a practice shown to remove 98% of apical ramifications and 93% of lateral root canals [12, 15]. Additionally, resecting the root end at a 90° angle to its long axis minimises root damage and limits dentinal tubule exposure [15]. The ideal preparation is a class I cavity with a depth of 3 mm and a cavity wall parallel to the corresponding anatomic root canal wall [12, 16]. To preserve the vitality of periodontal ligament cells and improve prognostic outcomes, most experts recommend minimising the extraoral operation time, ideally to less than 15 min [7, 17]. However, adhering to these modern endodontic surgical principles [12] while avoiding accidental damage to the tooth remains technically demanding under time constraints.

To address these issues, we developed a novel three-dimensional (3D)-printed intraoperative device using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), optical scanning, digital design, and 3D printing technologies, inspired by the concept of the static guide system. Like other surgical guides, its purpose is to improve surgical accuracy and minimize tissue trauma by controlling bur positioning. The device consisted of a base and a cover. The base secured the tooth and contains Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS) to maintain stability and preserve periodontal ligament cell vitality [18]. The cover provided guidance for precise root-end resection and preparation, thereby promoting greater accuracy during the procedure. The patent for the new device was under application.

Currently, few devices have been developed to digitally assist intentional replantation, and research on improving the accuracy of root-end resection and preparation is limited. This study aimed to evaluate the performance of this novel 3D-printed intraoperative device during intentional replantation, and compare the accuracy and efficiency of procedures performed on premolars and molars with and without its assistance. The null hypothesis was that there would be no significant difference in the accuracy or efficiency between the device-assisted and conventional groups.

Methods

Extracted teeth and root canal treatment

Molars and premolars, the most commonly treated teeth in intentional replantation, were selected for this study [6]. To minimise potential bias, only intact or minimally carious mandibular premolars with a single root, and mandibular molars with one mesial root and one distal root were included. The premolars had a single root canal. In the molars, the mesial root had two independent root canals and the distal root had a single canal. The Schneider angle of curvature for all root canals was classified as straight or moderate (< 25°) [19, 20]. Teeth with prior restorations, root canal treatment, cracks, fractures, or extensive decay beyond repair were excluded. Sample size was determined based on pilot studies using G*Power software (v3.1, University of Düsseldorf, Germany) with α-type error = 0.05 power, β = 0.95, and a size effect of 1.49° root-end resection angle for premolars and 1.66° for molars, yielding a required sample size of 22 premolars (11 per group) and 18 molars (nine per group). In the device-assisted group, the novel intraoperative device was used to assist in the operation, whereas in the conventional group the traditional method of using saline-soaked gauze to hold the crown was adopted. Extracted teeth were selected as experimental samples, which were sterilized using steam autoclave and kept hydrated in distilled water.

In the preoperative root canal treatment, a K-file size #15 (Dentsply Sirona, Ballaigues, Switzerland) was used to explore patency under a digital operating microscope (DOM) after accessing the cavity, and the working length of the root canal was determined to be 0.5 mm short of the visual apical foramen. The canal preparation was performed using a crown-down technique with M3 Pro Classic file systems (United Dental Group, Changzhou, China), reaching size #25/0.06, with 5 mL of 2.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) for irrigation between files. The canal was then rinsed with 5 mL of 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), left for 1 min, and refreshed three times for a total of 3 min. Subsequently, 5 mL of 2.5% sodium hypochlorite was used for irrigation, followed by a final rinse with 5 mL of saline solution and drying with #25, 0.06 taper paper points (Dentsply Sirona). Thorough intermediate rinsing with saline was performed after each irrigation with EDTA and NaOCl. Root canal obturation was completed using a size #25/0.06 gutta-percha point (Dentsply Sirona) and AH Plus sealer (Dentsply DeTrey GmbH, Konstanz, Germany) with a single-cone technique. The access cavity was sealed with resin (Dentsply Sirona) to restore the original crown morphology.

Design and 3D-printing of the intraoperative device

After root canal treatment, the premolars and molars were randomly assigned to either the device-assisted or conventional group. The teeth were embedded in an alginate impression material (Haixin, Beyzen, China), and a CBCT scan (Morita 3D Accuitomo type F17, Japan) was performed with a field of view of 80 × 80 mm and a voxel size of 0.16 mm. Crown morphology was captured using 3D scanning (Trios 2, 3Shape, Denmark) and root morphology was reconstructed using CBCT. Preoperative 3D reconstruction models of the teeth in both groups were generated using Mimics Medical software (v21.0, Materialise, Belgium), 3-Matic Medical software (v13.0, Materialise), and Magics software (v21.0, Materialise) in the same way for analysis. The novel intentional replantation intraoperative devices were three-dimensionally fabricated according to the design specifications.

The intraoperative device (Fig. 1) comprised two main parts: a base and a cover. The base featured a custom-shaped recess that corresponded to the crown morphology, ensuring stability during the procedure (Fig. 1-D). The cover enclosed the base with a hole in the centre of the upper plane to expose the root end for a precise 3-mm resection of length and angle (Figs. 1-I, 1-J and 1-L). Additionally, root canal directional markers were integrated into the sidewalls of the cover to guide bur angulation during root-end preparation (Figs. 1-I, 1-K and 1-M). The device was designed to maintain periodontal ligament cell viability—a key factor for successful intentional replantation [10, 21–23]. The assembled device was in a secure state to prevent physical damage and adherence of debris to the root surface, and it could be filled with HBSS to preserve periodontal ligament cells [24, 25]. Fabrication was carried out using a 3D printer (UltraCraft A2D, Heygears, China) with a high-precision resin (Model HP UV 2.0, Heygears), following the design scheme. The printing error was 50 μm. Any model with a printing deviation exceeding 50 μm was deemed ineligible and excluded from the study.

Simulated operation of intentional replantation

Prior to performing the simulated surgical procedure, the operator was permitted unlimited time to evaluate root and canal morphology using CBCT images. The teeth, embedded in alginate impression material, were extracted using extraction forceps, followed by root surface staining and inspection under the DOM. The subsequent procedural steps included root-end resection, preparation, and filling, followed by reimplantation. Since all extracted teeth were free of cracks, methylene blue staining and microscopic inspection were performed to standardize the experimental procedures and better simulate clinical surgical operations.

In the device-assisted group, the tooth was placed in the intraoperative device, which was assembled later after staining and inspection of the root surface. A specially lengthened bur (HX-S5, Walrus, China) was used to perform a 3-mm root-end resection guided by the upper plane of the cover, under continuous irrigation with HBSS. The resected root surface was stained and re-inspected under the DOM. Root-end preparation was then performed using a 0.8 mm diameter bur (HX-62, Walrus, China) to create a 3-mm-deep class I cavity with the angulation guidance of the sidewall’s root canal directional markers. Bioceramic material (iRoot BP Plus, Innovative BioCeramix Inc., Canada) was rolled into 0.5-mm cones to fill the cavity. Upon completion of these steps, the device was disassembled and teeth were removed.

In the conventional group, saline-soaked gauze was used to hold the crown of tooth. Root-end resection was performed after determining the 3-mm root end using a periodontal probe, and the direction of preparation was perpendicular to the resection surface.

After root-end filling, all teeth of both groups were replanted into alginate impression material. All procedures were performed by a third-year resident endodontist who had received prior standardized training of intentional replantation techniques, both with and without device assistance. The training protocol included nine practices in each group. After completing the procedures, all teeth underwent CBCT scanning with the same acquisition parameters as baseline, and 3D reconstruction was completed in the identical manner for further comparative analysis.

Accuracy and efficiency of measurements

The accuracy of the procedure was evaluated by superimposing preoperative and postoperative 3D reconstructions of the teeth and root canals using Mimics and 3-matic software. The length deviation of root-end resection was measured using two metrics: distance between the highest point of the root resection surface from the designed plane, and distance between the lowest point of the root resection surface from the designed plane (Figs. 2-E, 2-F and 2-G). The angle deviation of the root-end resection was determined by measuring the angle between the designed plane and the postoperative resection datum plane, generated by manually marking the resection surface using 3-matics software (Figs. 2-E, 2-F and 2-H). Deviation in root-end preparation was measured as the 3D angle between the preoperative 3-mm segment of the root canal (to be prepared) and the same segment postoperatively (Figs. 2-I, 2-J and 2-K). These three deviations collectively constituted the accuracy assessment. All measurements were performed by a technician who was blinded to the group allocation and not involved in the preoperative design or intraoperative procedures. Efficiency was evaluated by measuring the operation time using a stopwatch. The stopwatch started when the tooth was extracted from the alginate impression material and stopped when it was replanted in the socket. The assembly and disassembly time of the device was included in the total time recorded for the device-assisted group.

Data analysis

All deviation values related to accuracy were recorded as absolute values. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation and calculated using SPSS Software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The homogeneity of variance was tested using Levene’s variance equality test. The Satterthwaite t-test and Mann–Whitney U test were used to compare values between the two groups. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. The data from homonymous roots and root canals in the molars were compared.

Results

The experimental results confirm that the device effectively provided the intended assistance. As summarized in Tables 1 and 2, the root-end resection angle deviations were significantly smaller in the device-assisted group than in the conventional group across all measured sites—premolars, molar mesial roots, and molar distal roots (P < 0.05). In contrast, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of root-end resection length deviations, root-end preparation angle deviation or total operation time for either tooth type.

Discussion

Research indicates that root-end resection should be 3 mm long and performed at 90° [15] to optimize surgical stability and infection control, ultimately improving prognosis [14, 26, 27]. While static guides have been extensively studied for their precision in controlling length and angular deviation during root-end resection in endodontic microsurgery [14, 28, 29], data on the accuracy of intentional replantation are limited. Therefore, in order to align the procedure of intentional replantation more closely with the aforementioned research requirements, achieve precise control of root resection length and angle, and simultaneously address the existing gap in the relevant research field, we designed a novel digital intraoperative device, and evaluated its accuracy and efficiency in vitro. The device effectively supported the entire surgical procedure. Based on the results presented above, the null hypothesis regarding the angle accuracy of root-end resection with both premolars and molars was rejected.

The extracted teeth comprised the experimental samples, and a CBCT scan with a view field of 80 × 80 mm was used to closely replicate clinical conditions. This wide field of view is necessary to accommodate a sufficient number of teeth (at least seven) to register the CBCT and oral scan data [30, 31]. A specially designed bur (HX-S5), featuring a 6 mm working length and 32 mm total length, was employed for root resection along the upper plane of the device cover. This design ensured control of the length and angle without interference between the handpiece and lateral wall of the device. For root-end preparation, a bur with a 0.8 mm tip diameter was used instead of an ultrasonic instrument, guided by the sidewall directional markers of the device, to enhance efficiency [2, 12].

As shown in Table 1, the length deviations recorded in this study were similar between the device-assisted and conventional groups, and comparable to those reported for static guides in microsurgery trials [14, 29, 32]. This consistency may be attributed to the operator’s ability to accurately identify the 3 mm resection length on extracted teeth without obstruction from buccal bone. In addition, this accurate determination led to a small difference in length deviation between the two groups, considering the minimal design and printing errors of the device. However, maintaining the correct root orientation of the long axis and stabilizing the bur direction were proved difficult without device assistance, resulting in significantly smaller angular deviations in the device-assisted group for both premolars and molars. In the device-assisted group, the mean angle deviation with premolars, molar mesial root, and molar distal root was 6.58°, 4.41°, and 5.62°, respectively, well below the commonly recommended 10° bevel angle [15].

Although directional markers were present around the cover of the root-end preparation segment, operators faced challenges in achieving a more precise 3D preparation based on four two-dimensional indications. Additionally, real-time guidance akin to static guides or dynamic navigation was lacking during the procedure. Consequently, while the mean angular values in the device-assisted group were lower than those in the conventional group, there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups for either premolars or molars.

Crown coverage area was a critical factor influencing tooth stabilization within the device. Insufficient coverage led to instability during root-end resection, compromising the accuracy. Conversely, extending the coverage to the undercut line improved the stability, as confirmed by previous studies [33]. However, excessive coverage under the undercut line made tooth placement challenging because of the inflexibility of the printing material. Further exploration was required to balance precision and ease of placement. Most studies recommend minimising the extraoral time, ideally under 15 min [7, 34] or as short as possible [11], to preserve periodontal ligament cell vitality. In the device-assisted group, mean extraoral time was 266.63 s (4.44 min) for premolars and 449.62 s (7.49 min) for molars; well within the 15-minute threshold and shorter than most reported clinical cases. Device assembly and disassembly might have a minimal impact on the overall operation time, as reflected by the comparable results between the two groups.

The main advantages of this device include improved angular accuracy in root-end resection, enhanced operational stability, reduced physical damage and gutta-percha debris on the root surface, and preservation of periodontal ligament cells by HBSS. Nevertheless, several limitations must be addressed in clinical practice. Preoperative design and printing required additional time and expense compared with conventional methods. Moreover, fixation stability may be reduced because it depended on crown morphology obtained via optical scanning, which in clinical settings only captured the buccal, lingual, and occlusal surfaces, unlike the full crown representation available in this in vitro study. In addition, because of its reliance on the scanned morphology, if the crown restoration was lost during extraction, the tooth might not fit securely in the device. Although the dental surface image registration was used in this study, a more accurate fiducial marker-based registration could be implemented in future clinical applications [35]. Additionally, since the optimal temperature for HBSS preservation of periodontal ligament cells was 4 °C [36], the temperature fluctuations during irrigation remained to be solved. Therefore, further refinement and clinical validation were essential to address these issues and confirm the ability of the device to better preserve periodontal ligament cell vitality.

Conclusion

We designed a novel digital intraoperative device, and successfully validated its functionality during intentional replantation operational procedures. It demonstrated the ability to improve the accuracy of root-end resection by reducing angle deviations in both premolars and molars without reducing operational efficiency. Despite its advantages, certain limitations were observed regarding its clinical application, highlighting the need for further refinement and verification in clinical trials.
