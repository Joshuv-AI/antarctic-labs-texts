Infinite Trees
Alexandre Goy

Abstract
A few notes about infinite trees in a descriptive set-theoretic setting.



1
Basics on Languages

This section introduces notation for manipulating words and languages.

Let
\omega=\{0,1,\ldots\}
be the first infinite ordinal, identified with
\mathbb{N}
. Let
\Sigma
be a set called the
alphabet
. Let
\Sigma^{*}=\bigcup_{n\in\omega}\Sigma^{n}
be the set of finite words on
\Sigma
,
\Sigma^{\omega}
be the set of infinite words on
\Sigma
, and
\Sigma^{\infty}=\Sigma^{*}+\Sigma^{\omega}=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\omega+1}\Sigma^{\alpha}
be the set of all words. Letters in a word are numbered starting from
1
. The length of a word
w
is denoted by
|w|
, defining a length map
|-|:\Sigma^{\infty}\to\omega+1
. Words will be typically written

\displaystyle w=w_{1}w_{2}\ldots w_{n}

\displaystyle\text{if }w\in\Sigma^{n}

\displaystyle\text{ i.e. }|w|=n

\displaystyle w=w_{1}w_{2}\ldots

\displaystyle\text{if }w\in\Sigma^{\omega}

\displaystyle\text{ i.e. }|w|=\omega

There is a unique word of length
0
called the empty word and denoted by
\varepsilon
. Words of length
1
and letters are identified, i.e.,
\Sigma^{1}\cong\Sigma
.

Concatenation.

Concatenation of words has type
\Sigma^{\infty}\times\Sigma^{\infty}\to\Sigma^{\infty}
and is denoted by juxtaposition. With the convention that left-concatenating an infinite word overwrites whatever is on the right, concatenation is associative with neutral element
\varepsilon
. For instance,
a^{\omega}b^{\omega}=a^{\omega}
. Fixing the first argument
u\in\Sigma^{\infty}
yields a map
u:\Sigma^{\infty}\to\Sigma^{\infty}
. Applying covariant and contravariant powerset to this map generates

\displaystyle u(\cdot)\colon

\displaystyle P(\Sigma^{\infty})\to P(\Sigma^{\infty})

\displaystyle u^{-1}(\cdot)\colon

\displaystyle P(\Sigma^{\infty})\to P(\Sigma^{\infty})

\displaystyle L\mapsto\{uv\mid v\in L\}

\displaystyle L\mapsto\{v\in\Sigma^{\infty}\mid uv\in L\}

Note that these maps are degenerate when
u
is an infinite word, as in that case
uL=\{u\}
if
L\neq\emptyset
,
\emptyset
else and
u^{-1}L=\Sigma^{\infty}
if
u\in L
,
\emptyset
else.

.

For all
u,v\in\Sigma^{\infty}
and

1.

u(vL)=(uv)L

2.

u^{-1}(v^{-1}L)=(vu)^{-1}L

3.

uu^{-1}L=L\cap u\Sigma^{\infty}

4.

if

,

,
\Sigma^{\infty}

Sketch:

1.

This is functoriality of the covariant powerset.

2.

This is functoriality of the contravariant powerset.

3.

We have
uu^{-1}L=\{uv\mid uv\in L\}=L\cap u\Sigma^{\infty}
.

4.

We have
u^{-1}uL=\{v\in\Sigma^{\infty}\mid uv\in uL\}
. If
u\in\Sigma^{*}
,
uv\in uL\iff v\in L
whence the result, else and if
L\neq\emptyset
,
uv\in uL\iff u\in\{u\}
whence the result.

∎

Concatenation of languages
P(\Sigma^{\infty})\times P(\Sigma^{\infty})\to P(\Sigma^{\infty})
is also denoted by juxtaposition.

Restriction and Co-Restriction.

The restriction of a word
w\in\Sigma^{\infty}
to a finite length
n\in\omega
is defined by
w_{\mid n}=w_{1}w_{2}\ldots w_{n}
if
n\leq|w|
,
w_{\mid n}=w
else. For instance,
w_{|0}=\varepsilon
is the empty word. The co-restriction of a word
w\in\Sigma^{\infty}
to a finite length
n\in\omega
is the unique word
w^{\mid n}\in\Sigma^{\infty}
such that
w=w_{\mid n}w^{\mid n}
. For instance,
w^{\mid 0}=w
and
w^{\mid n}=\varepsilon
for any
n\geq|w|
. Note that
w^{\mid n}=w_{n+1}w_{n+2}\ldots
for infinite words. Furthermore, for every
w\in\Sigma^{\infty}
and
p,q\in\omega
the equality

w_{\mid p+q}=w_{\mid p}(w^{\mid p})_{\mid q}
holds.

Prefixing.

The map
\mathsf{Pref}:\Sigma^{\infty}\to P(\Sigma^{*})
assigns to a word the set of its finite prefixes, i.e.,
\mathsf{Pref}(w)=\{w_{\mid n}\mid n\in\omega\}
. It is extended to
\mathsf{Pref}:P(\Sigma^{\infty})\to P(\Sigma^{*})
by union. We have

\mathsf{Pref}(L)=\{w_{\mid n}\mid w\in L,n\in\omega\}=\{u\in\Sigma^{*}\mid L\cap u\Sigma^{\infty}\neq\emptyset\}

Proposition 2
.

For any
L,M\in P(\Sigma^{\infty})
,

1.

L\cap\Sigma^{*}\subseteq\mathsf{Pref}(L)

2.

L\subseteq M\Rightarrow\mathsf{Pref}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{Pref}(M)

3.

\mathsf{Pref}(\mathsf{Pref}(L))=\mathsf{Pref}(L)

4.

\mathsf{Pref}(LM)=\mathsf{Pref}(L)\cup L\mathsf{Pref}(M)
if
M\neq\emptyset

Proof.

The first three statements are obvious. Note that this makes the restriction
\mathsf{Pref}:P(\Sigma^{*})\to P(\Sigma^{*})
a closure operator. For the last one:

4.

A prefix of a word in
LM
is either a prefix of a word in
L
, or a word in
L
then a prefix of a word in
M
, and conversely because
M
is non-empty.

∎

Example 3

(The binary alphabet)
.

The
binary alphabet
is
\Sigma=\{a,b\}
where
a
is interpreted as ’going left’ and
b
as ’going right’. The map
(-)^{\perp}:\Sigma\to\Sigma
reverses directions as
a\mapsto b
and
b\mapsto a
. It is extended letterwise to a map
(-)^{\perp}:\Sigma^{\infty}\to\Sigma^{\infty}
, for instance
(abba)^{\perp}=baab
.

2
Basics on Trees

This section introduces trees as they are usually defined in descriptive set theory. These trees can have finite or infinite depth, and they can have finite or infinite width depending on the alphabet
\Sigma
.

A language
L\in P(\Sigma^{\infty})
is
prefix-closed
if
\mathsf{Pref}(L)=L
.

Definition 4

(Tree)
.

A
tree
is a prefix-closed subset of
\Sigma^{*}
. The set of trees over
\Sigma
is denoted by
T(\Sigma)
.

Proposition 5
.

The image of
\mathsf{Pref}:P(\Sigma^{\infty})\to P(\Sigma^{*})
is
T(\Sigma)
.

Proof.

As
\mathsf{Pref}(\mathsf{Pref}(L))=\mathsf{Pref}(L)
, the image is included in
T(\Sigma)
. Conversely, let
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
be prefix-closed, then
L=\mathsf{Pref}(L)
is in the image of
\mathsf{Pref}
.
∎

Definition 6
.

Given a tree
T\in T(\Sigma)
, its set of infinite branches is

[T]=\{w\in\Sigma^{\omega}\mid\forall n\in\omega,w_{\mid n}\in T\}=\{w\in\Sigma^{\omega}\mid\mathsf{Pref}(w)\subseteq T\}

Proposition 7
.

For a tree
T\in T(\Sigma)
,
[T]=\{w\in\Sigma^{\omega}\mid\mathsf{Pref}(w)\cap T\text{ is infinite}\}
.

Proof.

We must show that for a tree
T
and a word
w\in\Sigma^{\omega}
,
\mathsf{Pref}(w)\subseteq T
iff
\mathsf{Pref}(w)\cap T
is infinite. The left-to-right direction is obvious as
\mathsf{Pref}(w)
is infinite. For the other direction, assume
\mathsf{Pref}(w)\cap T
is infinite and let
n\in\omega
, we must show
w_{\mid n}\in T
. As
\mathsf{Pref}(w)\cap T
is infinite, there is
N\geq n
such that
w_{\mid N}\in T
. As
T
is prefix-closed and
n\leq N
,
w_{\mid n}=(w_{\mid N})_{\mid n}\in T
, whence the result.
∎

Proposition 8
.

On the binary alphabet, taking infinite branches commutes with reversing directions:
[T^{\perp}]=[T]^{\perp}
.

Proof.

Compute

\displaystyle[T^{\perp}]

\displaystyle=\{w\in\Sigma^{\omega}\mid\forall n\in\omega,w_{\mid n}\in T^{\perp}\}

\displaystyle=\{w\in\Sigma^{\omega}\mid\forall n\in\omega,w_{\mid n}^{\perp}\in T\}

\displaystyle=\{w^{\perp}\mid w\in\Sigma^{\omega},\forall n\in\omega,w_{\mid n}\in T\}

\displaystyle=[T]^{\perp}

∎

Proposition 9
.

Taking infinite branches commutes with taking arbitrary intersections:
[\bigcap_{i\in I}T_{i}]=\bigcap_{i\in I}[T_{i}]
.

Proof.

For any
w\in\Sigma^{\omega}
,
\forall n.\forall i.w_{\mid n}\in T_{i}\iff\forall i.\forall n.w_{\mid n}\in T_{i}
.
∎

Concatenation and Infinite Branches.

Proposition 10
.

For any
T\in T(\Sigma)
,
u\in\Sigma^{*}
,
a\in\Sigma
,

1.

u[T]=[\mathsf{Pref}(u)\cup uT]

2.

a[T]=[\{\varepsilon\}\cup aT]

3.

u^{-1}[T]=[u^{-1}T]

Proof.

Let us prove the statements.

1.

First check that
\mathsf{Pref}(u)\cup uT
is a tree. We can assume
T
is non-empty. Then
\mathsf{Pref}(uT)=\mathsf{Pref}(u)\cup u\mathsf{Pref}(T)=\mathsf{Pref}(u)\cup uT
because
T
is a tree, hence
\mathsf{Pref}(u)\cup uT
is a tree. Now let us prove the wanted equality by double inclusion. Let
uw\in u[T]
, where
w\in[T]
. Then prefixes of
uw
are, depending of their length, either in
\mathsf{Pref}(u)
, or in
uT
, so that
uw\in[\mathsf{Pref}(u)\cup uT]
. Conversely, let
w\in[\mathsf{Pref}(u)\cup uT]
. Let
n=|u|
. As
w_{\mid n}\in\mathsf{Pref}(u)\cup uT
and the only element of length
n
of that set is
u
itself, we have
w_{\mid n}=u
. Furthermore, for length reasons
w_{\mid n+m}\in uT
for every
m\in\omega
, therefore
w^{\mid n}\in[T]
. Whence
w=w_{\mid n}w^{\mid n}=uw^{\mid n}\in u[T]
.

2.

This is a special case of the previous point.

3.

First check that
u^{-1}T
is a tree. We have

\displaystyle\mathsf{Pref}(u^{-1}T)

\displaystyle=\{w_{\mid n}\mid w\in u^{-1}T\text{ and }n\in\omega\}

\displaystyle=\{w_{\mid n}\mid uw\in T\text{ and }n\in\omega\}

\displaystyle\subseteq\{w\mid uw\in\mathsf{Pref}(T)\}

\displaystyle=u^{-1}\mathsf{Pref}(T)=u^{-1}T

Now let us prove the wanted equality by double inclusion. We have

\displaystyle u^{-1}[T]=\{w\mid uw\in[T]\}=\{w\mid\mathsf{Pref}(uw)\subseteq T\}

\displaystyle[u^{-1}T]=\{w\mid\mathsf{Pref}(w)\subseteq u^{-1}T\}=\{w\mid u\mathsf{Pref}(w)\subseteq T\}

As
\mathsf{Pref}(uw)=\mathsf{Pref}(u)\cup u\mathsf{Pref}(w)
and
T
is prefix-closed,
\mathsf{Pref}(uw)\subseteq T
iff
u\mathsf{Pref}(w)\subseteq T
, whence the result.

∎

Note that, given
u\in\Sigma^{*}
, the map
u^{-1}(\cdot):T(\Sigma)\to T(\Sigma)
recovers the sub-tree rooted at the node
u
. In particular

•

The tree
u^{-1}T
is the sub-tree rooted at
u
extracted from
T
.

•

The language (usually not tree)
uu^{-1}T
is that sub-tree viewed in its place inside
T
.

•

The tree
\mathsf{Pref}(u)\cup uu^{-1}T
is the sub-tree of
T
given by keeping only the finite branch
u
and the sub-tree rooted at
u
.

2.1
Construction and Destruction of Trees

There are two remarkable viewpoints on a tree: lying down and staring at what is around the root, or climbing along a branch.

2.1.1
Around the Root

Destructively speaking, a non-empty tree is the union of the root and its children subtrees.

Proposition 14
.

For all
T\in T(\Sigma)\setminus\{\emptyset\}
,
T=\{\varepsilon\}\cup\biguplus_{a\in\Sigma}aa^{-1}T
.

Proof.

We have
T=\{\varepsilon\}\cup\biguplus_{a\in\Sigma}(T\cap a\Sigma^{*})=\{\varepsilon\}\cup\biguplus_{a\in\Sigma}aa^{-1}T
.
∎

Constructively speaking, given a
\Sigma
-indexed family of trees
(T_{a})_{a\in\Sigma}
, one can define

T=\{\varepsilon\}\cup\biguplus_{a\in\Sigma}aT_{a}

This yields a tree whose infinite branches are obtained from those of the children subtrees.

Proposition 15
.

If
(T_{a})_{a\in\Sigma}\in T(\Sigma)
is a family of trees, then

1.

\{\varepsilon\}\cup\biguplus_{a\in\Sigma}aT_{a}\in T(\Sigma)

2.

[\{\varepsilon\}\cup\biguplus_{a\in\Sigma}aT_{a}]=\bigcup_{a\in\Sigma}a[T_{a}]

Proof.

Let
T
be the set
\{\varepsilon\}\cup\biguplus_{a\in\Sigma}aT_{a}
.

1.

Let us show that
T
is prefix-closed. Let
u\in T
, the case
u=\varepsilon
is obvious so we can assume
u=av
for some
a\in\Sigma
and some
v\in T_{a}
. As
T_{a}
is a tree,
\mathsf{Pref}(v)\subseteq T_{a}
hence
a\mathsf{Pref}(v)\subseteq aT_{a}\subseteq T
. Therefore
\mathsf{Pref}(u)=\{\varepsilon\}\cup a\mathsf{Pref}(v)\subseteq T
, so that
T
is prefix-closed.

2.

Let
w\in\Sigma^{\omega}
. Assume
w\in[T]
. We have for every
n\in\omega
,
w_{\mid n}\in\{\varepsilon\}\cup\biguplus_{a\in\Sigma}aT_{a}
. If
n
is non-zero, the only possibility is that
w_{\mid n}\in w_{1}T_{w_{1}}
. Therefore, every prefix of
w^{\mid 1}
is in
T_{w_{1}}
, whence
w^{\mid 1}\in[T_{w_{1}}]
so that
w\in w_{1}[T_{w_{1}}]\subseteq\bigcup_{a\in\Sigma}a[T_{a}]
. Conversely, let
w\in\bigcup_{a\in\Sigma}a[T_{a}]
, then there is
a\in\Sigma
such that
w\in a[T_{a}]
. For every
n\in\omega
, either
n=0
and
w_{\mid 0}=\varepsilon\in T
, or
n>0
and
w_{\mid n}=au
for some
u\in T_{a}
, therefore
w_{\mid n}\in aT_{a}\subseteq T
. This shows that
w\in[T]
.

∎

And as expected, we then have

Proposition 16
.

If
T=\{\varepsilon\}\cup\biguplus_{a\in\Sigma}aT_{a}
and
a\in\Sigma
, then
T_{a}=a^{-1}T
.

Proof.

As
a^{-1}aL=L
and
a^{-1}bL=\emptyset
for
b\neq a
, we have

a^{-1}T=a^{-1}\left(\{\varepsilon\}\cup\biguplus_{b\in\Sigma}bT_{b}\right)=a^{-1}\{\varepsilon\}\cup\biguplus_{b\in\Sigma}a^{-1}bT_{b}=T_{a}

∎

In particular, for any non-empty tree
T\in T(\Sigma)
we have

\displaystyle T=\{\varepsilon\}\uplus\biguplus_{a\in\Sigma}aa^{-1}T

\displaystyle[T]=\biguplus_{a\in\Sigma}aa^{-1}[T]

2.1.2
Along a Branch

Destructively speaking, a tree with at least one infinite branch is the union of the subtrees plugged along that branch.

Given a infinite word
w\in\Sigma^{\omega}
, the set of
off-words
of
w
is defined as
w^{\circ}=\{w_{\mid n}a\mid n\in\omega,a\in\Sigma\setminus\{w_{n+1}\}\}\in P(\Sigma^{*})
.

Proposition 17
.

For all
T\in T(\Sigma)
and
w\in[T]
,

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Pref}(w)\cup\biguplus_{u\in w^{\circ}}uu^{-1}T

\displaystyle=\biguplus_{n\in\omega}w_{\mid n}\left(\{\varepsilon\}\cup\biguplus_{a\in\Sigma\setminus\{w_{n+1}\}}a(w_{\mid n}a)^{-1}T\right)

Proof.

The set
\{\mathsf{Pref}(w)\}\cup\{u\Sigma^{*}\mid u\in w^{\circ}\}
is clearly a partition of
\Sigma^{*}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle T=T\cap\Sigma^{*}

\displaystyle=(T\cap\mathsf{Pref}(w))\cup\biguplus_{u\in w^{\circ}}(T\cap u\Sigma^{*})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Pref}(w)\cup\biguplus_{u\in w^{\circ}}uu^{-1}T

\displaystyle=\biguplus_{n\in\omega}\left(\{w_{\mid n}\}\cup\biguplus_{u\in w^{\circ}\cap\Sigma^{n+1}}uu^{-1}T\right)

\displaystyle=\biguplus_{n\in\omega}\left(\{w_{\mid n}\}\cup\biguplus_{a\in\Sigma}w_{\mid n}a(w_{\mid n}a)^{-1}T\right)

\displaystyle=\biguplus_{n\in\omega}w_{\mid n}\left(\{\varepsilon\}\cup\biguplus_{a\in\Sigma\setminus\{w_{n+1}\}}a(w_{\mid n}a)^{-1}T\right)

∎

Constructively speaking, given a countable family of non-empty
\Sigma
-trees
(T_{n})_{n\in\omega}
and an infinite word
w\in\Sigma^{\omega}
, one can define

T=\bigcup_{n\in\omega}w_{\mid n}T_{n}

This yields a tree whose infinite branches are the main branch
w
and the ones obtained from the subtrees
T_{n}
.

Proposition 18
.

If
w\in\Sigma^{\omega}
and
(T_{n})_{n\in\omega}\in T(\Sigma)
is a countable family of non-empty trees, then

1.

\bigcup_{n\in\omega}w_{\mid n}T_{n}\in T(\Sigma)

2.

\left[\bigcup_{n\in\omega}w_{\mid n}T_{n}\right]=\{w\}\cup\bigcup_{n\in\omega}w_{\mid n}[T_{n}]

Proof.

In the whole proof, let
U=\bigcup_{n\in\omega}w_{\mid n}T_{n}
.

1.

Let
u\in w_{\mid n}T_{n}
for some
n\in\omega
. Then
u=w_{\mid n}v
for some
v\in T_{n}
. Prefixes of
u
are either prefixes of
w
, which belong to
U
because every
T_{i}
contains
\varepsilon
, or of the form
w_{\mid n}v^{\prime}
for some
v^{\prime}
prefix of
v
. As
v\in T_{n}
and
T_{n}
is prefix-closed,
v^{\prime}\in T_{n}
, hence
w_{\mid n}v^{\prime}\in w_{\mid n}T_{n}
is in
U
as well.

2.

\boxed{\supseteq}
First consider
w
. For all
n\in\omega
, as
\varepsilon\in T_{n}
we have
w_{\mid n}\in w_{\mid n}T_{n}
, therefore
w
is an infinite branch of
U
. Now let
x\in[T_{n}]
for some
n\in\omega
, let us show that
w_{\mid n}x
is an infinite branch of
U
as well. Every prefix of
w
is in
U
, so consider
w_{\mid n}u
where
u
is a prefix of
x
. As
x\in[T_{n}]
we have
u\in T_{n}
therefore
w_{\mid n}u\in U
, which completes the proof.

\boxed{\subseteq}
Let
b\in\left[\bigcup_{n\in\omega}w_{\mid n}T_{n}\right]\setminus\{w\}
. Let
n_{0}\in\omega
be such that
b_{\mid n_{0}}\neq w_{\mid n_{0}}
and minimal with that property. We know that for all
m\in\omega
,
b_{\mid m}\in\bigcup_{n\in\omega}w_{\mid n}T_{n}
, henceforth for all
m\geq n_{0}
,
b_{\mid m}\in\bigcup_{n<n_{0}}w_{\mid n}T_{n}
. Pick
n_{1}<n_{0}
such that there are infinitely many
b_{\mid m}
(
m\geq n_{0}
) in
w_{\mid n_{1}}T_{n_{1}}
. Therefore, there are infinitely many prefixes of
b^{\mid n_{1}}
in
T_{n_{1}}
. As
T_{n_{1}}
is prefix-closed, this yields
b^{\mid n_{1}}\in[T_{n_{1}}]
. Hence
b=b_{\mid n_{1}}b^{\mid n_{1}}=w_{\mid n_{1}}b^{\mid n_{1}}\in w_{\mid n_{1}}[T_{n_{1}}]
, which completes the proof.

∎

The aforementioned way to construct trees along an infinite branch is not very handy, because the union may not be disjoint as trees
T_{n}
can ’overflow’ along the branch

(T_{u})_{u\in w^{\circ}}
parametrised by the off-words of

T\in T(\Sigma)

2.

[T]=\{w\}\cup\biguplus_{u\in w^{\circ}}u[T_{u}]

1.

The

2.

Proposition 20
.

If

The Cantor-Bendixson Derivative

This section introduces the Cantor-Bendixson derivative, an operation on topological spaces which consists in deleting isolated points. We will then see that trees themselves are topological spaces and how derivation works on them.

3.1
Derivating Topological Spaces

We assume familiarity with elementary topology and only give some of the basics definitions to fix notation.

A topological space is a set
X
equipped with a set
\Omega(X)\subseteq P(X)
such that
\emptyset,X\in\Omega(X)
and
\Omega(X)
is closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections. Elements of
\Omega(X)
are called the
open sets
of
X
. Their complements
C(X)
are called
closed sets
of
X
.

Given a subset
Y\subseteq X
of a topological space
X
, the subset topology is defined as

\Omega(Y)=\{U\cap Y\mid U\in\Omega(X)\}

A point
x\in X
is
isolated
if
\{x\}\in\Omega(X)
.

Definition 21

(Cantor-Bendixson derivative)
.

The
derivative
of a topological space
X
is the set

X^{\prime}=X\setminus\{x\in X\mid x\text{ is isolated}\}

equipped with the subset topology inherited from
X
.

As
X\setminus X^{\prime}
is the union of its open singletons, the set
X^{\prime}
is closed in
X
.

Lemma 22
.

Let
Z
be a topological space and
X\subseteq Y\subseteq Z
with the subset topologies. For any
x\in X
, if
x
is isolated in
Y
, then
x
is isolated in
X
.

Proof.

Let
x\in X
. Assume
x
is isolated in
Y
, then
\{x\}\in\Omega(Y)
so there is
U\in\Omega(Z)
such that
\{x\}=U\cap Y
. As
x\in X
,
\{x\}=\{x\}\cap X=U\cap Y\cap X=U\cap X\in\Omega(X)
so that
x
is isolated in
X
.
∎

As a consequence, the Cantor-Bendixson derivative is monotone.

Corollary 23
.

Let
Z
be a topological space and
X\subseteq Y\subseteq Z
with the subset topologies. Then
X^{\prime}\subseteq Y^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
x\in X^{\prime}
. As
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
we have
x\in X
. If
x
is isolated in
Y
, then by Lemma
22
,
x
is isolated in
X
which is contradicting
x\in X^{\prime}
. So
x
is not isolated in
Y
, that is,
x\in Y^{\prime}
.
∎

The Cantor-Bendixson derivative preserves the fact of being homeomorphic.

Proposition 24
.

Let
X
and
Y
be homeomorphic topological spaces. Then
X^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime}
are homeomorphic.

Proof.

Let
f:X\to Y
be a homeomorphism. We show that it restricts to a homeomorphism
f^{\prime}:X^{\prime}\to Y^{\prime}
.

•

First see that if
f(x)\in Y
is isolated, then
f^{-1}(\{f(x)\})=\{x\}
is open, hence
x\in X
is isolated. This proves that the restriction
f^{\prime}:X^{\prime}\to Y^{\prime}
is well-defined.

•

Similarly, the restriction
(f^{-1})^{\prime}:Y^{\prime}\to X^{\prime}
is well-defined. It is straightforward to check that
f^{\prime}
and
(f^{-1})^{\prime}
are inverse to each other, showing that
f^{\prime}
is a bijection.

•

Let
U\cap Y^{\prime}\in\Omega(Y^{\prime})
, where
U\in\Omega(Y)
. Then
f^{\prime-1}(U\cap Y^{\prime})=f^{-1}(U)\cap X^{\prime}\in\Omega(X^{\prime})
, whence

is continuous.

•

Similarly,
(f^{-1})^{\prime}
is continuous, hence

is a homeomorphism.

∎

The Cantor-Bendixson Rank

The Cantor-Bendixson derivative can be transfinitely iterated. The derivating process always stabilises at some ordinal

4.1
On Topological Spaces

One can transfinitely iterate the topological derivative as follows. For a topological space

, let

\displaystyle X^{(0)}=X

\displaystyle X^{(\alpha+1)}=(X^{(\alpha)})^{\prime}

\displaystyle X^{(\lambda)}=\bigcap_{\beta<\lambda}X^{(\beta)}

\displaystyle\lambda\text{ limit ordinal}

The sequence
X^{(\alpha)}
is decreasing, hence stationary.

Definition 32

(Cantor-Bendixson rank)

For any topological space

such that
X^{(\alpha)}=X^{(\alpha+1)}

of

and is denoted as
\alpha=\mathsf{CB}(X)
.

By definition, the space

is

in the sense that it has no isolated points. It is called the
perfect kernel
of
X
.
