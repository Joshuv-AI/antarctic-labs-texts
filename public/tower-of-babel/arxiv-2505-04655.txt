Integration of Large Language Models and Traditional Deep Learning for Social Determinants of Health Prediction
Paul Landes, Jimeng Sun, Adam Cross

Abstract
Social Determinants of Health are economic, social and personal circumstances that affect or influence an individual’s health status. Social Determinants of Healths have shown to be correlated to wellness outcomes, and therefore, are useful to physicians in diagnosing diseases and in decision-making. In this work, we automatically extract Social Determinants of Healths from clinical text using traditional deep learning and Large Language Models to find the advantages and disadvantages of each on an existing publicly available dataset. Our models outperform a previous reference point on a multilabel Social Determinants of Health classification by 10 points, and we present a method and model to drastically speed up classification (12X execution time) by eliminating expensive Large Language Model processing. The method we present combines a more nimble and efficient solution that leverages the power of the Large Language Model for precision and traditional deep learning methods for efficiency. We also show highly performant results on a dataset supplemented with synthetic data and several traditional deep learning models that outperform Large Language Models. Our models and methods offer the next iteration of automatic prediction of Social Determinants of Healths that impact at-risk patients.

Social Determinants of Health greatly affect health, well-being and quality of life. Safe housing, job opportunities, discrimination, and environmental factors are just a few examples Social Determinants of Health types. Adverse Social Determinants of Healths have immediate and profound affects on patient’s health, such as air quality’s negative affect on one’s respiratory system or access to healthcare for those without income stability. 2.4
Error analysis

Our traditional deep learning multilabel classifiers failed for labels
Housing
and
Transportation
on the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III dataset. These labels have only three occurrences each (see
Table
8
). The two-step classifier gets the single
Housing
instance correct, which means the binary traditional classifier learned that it was Social Determinants of Health positive and the Large Language Model correctly classified it but still failed on the
Transportation
label. However, all of our Large Language Models achieved a non-zero score on all labels and outperformed the
[
7
]

[
7
]
reference models for most labels. Furthermore, our models perform better on every label on the Amended dataset.

The method of parsing of the Large Language Model output might negatively affect performance. A complex regular expression was needed to parse the noisy Large Language Model output. Models hallucinated in the few-shot setting (output on the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III dataset was more noisy than on the Amended), but were more consistent on the fine-tuned models. Of course, this was not an issue for the traditional deep learning model as its output layer directly predicted each label. However, coupling traditional models with Large Language Models can have consequences, such as with the two-step classifier.

The two-step classifier uses the binary classifier to predict if a Social Determinants of Health is present in a sentence. When it predicts the presence of one or more Social Determinants of Healths, it uses the Llama 3.1 8B Instruct model to assign labels. As noted in
Section
2.3
, the binary classifier has a lower recall than precision, but the Llama 3.1 8B Instruct model’s recall is significantly higher
Table
1
. This could be propagation error from false negative Social Determinants of Health classifications. However,
Table
3
shows a very similar macro precision and recall, so the Large Language Model might not assign any labels since the classifier was trained with
No SDoH
as a label. The two-step classifier may perform better using a Large Language Model trained without negative Social Determinants of Health labels.

The traditional deep learning multilabel model was trained using the same features as the binary model described in the

Furthermore, we observe the traditional model’s ability at detecting Social Determinants of Healths with a higher macro recall (0.49) than precision (0.6). However, the converse is true with all Large Language Models on the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III dataset (this dataset includes sentences with no Social Determinants of Health). 3.2
Two-step and binary classifiers

The binary classifier achieved a F1 of 0.997 on our test split of the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III dataset. The stratified dataset contains 992 negative Social Determinants of Health labels and 73 positive labels. This dataset imbalance explains the lower F1 score of 0.767 on the positive labels. However, it is 73 times faster compared to the Llama 3.1 8B Instruct fine-tuned model.

Our experiments show that the binary classifier used as the first component in the two-step classifier is relatively close in performance to the fine-tuned Large Language Models. The two-step classifier yields a macro F1 of 0.62, which is only two macro F1 points lower than the Llama 3.3 70B Instruct model (see
Table
1
). Considering the two-step classifier is 12 times faster than the Llama 3.1 8B Instruct classifier, we believe it shows the best performance trade off for real-world clinical application.

We trained two types of models: binary (determines if a Social Determinants of Health exists) and a multilabel that classified zero or more Social Determinants of Healths. These models included:

•

Supervised-fine tuned Large Language Model: classifies zero or more Social Determinants of Health labels

•

Traditional deep learning multilabel: classifies zero or more Social Determinants of Health labels

•

Traditional deep learning binary: predicts whether a sentence has at least one Social Determinants of Health

•

Two-step that integrates the traditional binary classifier with a Large Language Model

The traditional deep learning and LLM models were trained and developed on the same dataset.

4.4.2
Concept features

The Unified Medical Language System is a large graph based medical taxonomy and terminology data source
[
21
]
. Each node in the Unified Medical Language System graph represents a concept unique identifier. Each concept unique identifier has many properties, two of which are the
preferred name
(a common name for the concept) and a
definition
of the concept, which can be exploited to provide more context to the model for each token.

We considered two methods creating additional features using concept unique identifiers found in the clinical text. The first was simply to use embeddings generated from the concept unique identifier’s preferred name and definition. We also considered adding cui2vec
[
22
]
embeddings, which are 500D vectors trained from clinical text using the word2vec algorithm
[
23
,
24
]
. However, cui2vec feature sparsity was a concern as its vocabulary is a subset of Unified Medical Language System, which is then further conditioned by a less than perfect recall by the entity linker.

An example of a sentence processed through the traditional multilabel model. The clinical text input is encoded by a RoBERTa, an entity linker and several linguistic taggers. Each of these components’ output is concatenated, passed through a 1D convolutional neural network and decoded as zero or more labels.

To make an informed decision, we computed a concept unique identifier and cui2vec in-vocabulary distributions from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III dataset. concept unique identifiers were extracted using The MedCAT
[
25
]
entity linker and were then assigned a numerical count per sentence. As shown in the first bar in
Figure
2
, the number of sentences with no concept unique identifier is 3,434 (65% of the dataset) and the cui2vec embeddings available for those are even more scarce. However, given that 1,887 sentences (35% of the dataset) were found to have at least one concept unique identifier, we opted for a middle-ground solution to encode the concept unique identifier properties and leave the implementation of the cui2vec features for future work.

To encode of the concept unique identifier properties, a Sentence-BERT (SBERT) model
[
26
]
was used to create embeddings. We give the model a way to relate from medical concepts to Social Determinants of Healths semantically as SBERT models embed text in Euclidean space. Embedding the concept unique identifier with RoBERTa would add little or add redundancy in cases where the properties are close or identical to the text.

A clinically trained SBERT model
[
27
]
was used for the concept unique identifiers’ embedding. Only the static embeddings from a forward inference were used due to memory constraints of fine-tuning two (the SBERT and RoBERTa) models in parallel. Text in the form
<preferred name>:<definition>
was used as input to the SBERT model and repeated (stacked) the embeddings across all wordpieces
[
28
]
(token sub-units with associated vectors and provided by the model’s
tokenizer) tagged by the entity linker for each concept. Next, we explain how concept unique identifiers are vectorized and used with examples.

4.4.3
Neural architecture

The traditional deep learning multilabel and binary models use the same neural network architecture. Only the output layer differs in the neuron cardinality: the multilabel model has one for each Social Determinants of Health label (6) and the binary model has one.

Figure

shows the three components that take the sentence as input:

•

RoBERTa
(base) model’s last layer
: The sentence is first tokenized and applied to the model.

•

concept unique identifier

).

•

POS
,
Dep
,
Ent
,
MedEnt
and
TokSDoH
(see

Each of these components take the sentence as input and are used in parallel to create enriched embeddings for each wordpiece. The components’ output are then concatenated so that each wordpiece has the RoBERTa last layer embedding, last layer SBERT embedding, and the one-hot encode vectors. The concatenated tokens and features are then passed through a two layer 1D convolutional neural network. Finally, the last fully connected linear layer learns to decode the convolutional to Social Determinants of Health label. A threshold for each neuron determines if the output is considered as present.

Our results report the performance of the traditional deep learning binary model with the Llama 3.1 8B Instruct on the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III dataset. We believe the results of the two-step on the Amended dataset would be higher, but leave this as a future work.
