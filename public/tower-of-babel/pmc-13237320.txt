Real-World Cutaneous Immune-Related Adverse Events of Immunotherapy in Melanoma: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Karolina Zarańska, Grażyna Kamińska-Winciorek, Alexander Jorge Cortez, Grażyna Wąsik, Maksymilian Gajda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDermatologic toxicities represent a broad and heterogeneous group of immune-related adverse events (irAEs) in patients with melanoma treated with immunotherapy, ranging from mild, transient, and self-limiting reactions to severe and potentially life-threatening conditions. Certain dermatologic toxicities have been shown to reflect immune system activation and may influence decisions on treatment continuation. The objective of this study was to comprehensively analyse real-world evidence in order to characterise the frequency, time to onset, and spectrum of dermatologic toxicities in patients with melanoma treated with immunotherapy.Materials and MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted in the PubMed and EBSCO (MEDLINE Complete) databases to identify relevant reports published between January 2014 and December 2024. Case reports and letters describing skin toxicities in adult patients with melanoma undergoing immunotherapy were included.ResultsA total of 18 patients with melanoma who had experienced dermatologic immune-related adverse events (d-irAEs) were identified. Breslow thickness ranged from 0.85 to 5.1 mm. The most frequently administered treatment was anti-PD-1 monotherapy. All reported cases involved metastatic disease (18/18, 100%; 95% CI 81.47–100%). Autoimmune bullous disorders were the most common toxicities (27.8%; 95% CI 9.69–53.48%). The median time to onset was 14 weeks (IQR 23.5 weeks; Q1 3.75 weeks; Q3 27.3 weeks). Disease progression or patient death occurred in 12 of 18 cases (66.7%; 95% CI 40.99–86.66%).ConclusionThe increasing prevalence of immunotherapy is accompanied by a paucity of real-world data, particularly with regard to mild-to-moderate skin toxicities. This underreporting emphasises the necessity for systematic documentation to more accurately define the true clinical burden of these events and to provide more personalised management strategies for patients with melanoma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-026-01780-4.Keywords: Skin toxicity, Immunotherapy, Melanoma, Meta-analysis, Systematic review

Key Summary Points

Dermatologic immune-related adverse events (d-irAEs) in patients with melanoma treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors encompass a broad and heterogeneous spectrum, ranging from mild, self-limiting eruptions to rare, potentially life-threatening dermatologic conditions.The onset and severity of skin toxicities exhibit significant variability, with the potential to occur at any stage of treatment. This underscores the necessity for continuous dermatological surveillance, with regular assessment by a dermatologist during immunotherapy. A multidisciplinary approach, integrating both dermatological and oncological expertise, is fundamental for the early detection and appropriate management of skin toxicities.Real-world d-irAEs, particularly mild-to-moderate manifestations, remain markedly underreported despite widespread immunotherapy use.A systematic approach to reporting real-world d-irAEs is crucial to enhance the personalised management of patients.

Introduction

The incidence of malignant melanoma increases with age, with the highest prevalence observed among fair-skinned older populations with a history of excessive ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure [1, 2]. Additionally, key risk factors for melanoma development include a high number of benign melanocytic nevi, the presence of atypical nevi, multiple solar lentigines, significant sun-induced skin damage, and a family history of melanoma, with mutation in the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A (CDKN2A) gene being the most commonly identified, accounting for up to 40% of hereditary cases [2, 3].

Arnold et al. predict that the global burden of melanoma will increase by 50%, reaching 510,000 new cases by 2040, compared to 325,000 new cases in 2020. Furthermore, the number of melanoma-related deaths is expected to rise by 68% to 96,000 by 2040, compared to 57,000 in 2020 [1].

Genetic alterations play a critical role in melanoma pathogenesis, with activating mutations in the BRAF gene detected in approximately 40–50% of cases. Among these, the V600E variant is the most prevalent, accounting for roughly 80% of BRAF mutations [4].

Since 2011, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have become a cornerstone in the treatment of advanced melanoma, representing the first class of therapies approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for this indication [5]. These agents enhance the T cell-mediated antitumour response by targeting immune checkpoint proteins, including cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4), programmed cell death receptor 1 (PD-1), and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) [6, 7].

Recent studies have confirmed the efficacy of ICIs in neoadjuvant, adjuvant, and metastatic melanoma treatment settings [8].

Ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4) was the first ICI evaluated in patients with completely resected stage III melanoma in a randomised phase III trial (EORTC 18071) [9]. The trial demonstrated significant improvements in both recurrence-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS) compared with the placebo group, resulting in FDA approval of ipilimumab in 2015 [9].

In the CheckMate 238 trial, a randomised phase III study, nivolumab (anti-PD-1) was compared with ipilimumab in patients with resected stage IIIB, IIIC, or IV melanoma [10]. A 12-month RFS rate of 70.5% was observed with nivolumab, compared with 60.8% with ipilimumab. On the basis of these findings, nivolumab received FDA approval in 2017 for the adjuvant treatment of melanoma [8]. Similarly, in the phase III EORTC 1325/KEYNOTE-054 trial, patients with resected stage III melanoma were randomised to receive either pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1) or a placebo [11]. The study reported a 12-month RFS of 75.4% with pembrolizumab versus 61.0% with placebo, leading to FDA approval of pembrolizumab for the adjuvant setting in 2019 [8].

More recently, two large, randomised, placebo-controlled phase III trials have extended this approach to high-risk stage II melanoma. In the KEYNOTE-716 trial, pembrolizumab significantly improved RFS compared with placebo in patients with stage IIB/IIC melanoma [12, 13]. This led to FDA approval in 2021 [14] and European Medicines Agency (EMA) approval in 2022 [15]. Similarly, in the CheckMate 76K trial, nivolumab was shown to substantially reduce the risk of recurrence or death compared with placebo, resulting in FDA approval in 2023 [16] and EMA approval in 2024 [17].

Together, these studies have firmly established anti-PD-1 immunotherapy as the current standard of care for patients with resected stage IIB or higher melanoma, providing long-lasting clinical benefits and a more favourable safety profile than CTLA-4 blockade.

In the management of patients with high-risk, surgically resectable melanoma, the role of neoadjuvant immunotherapy is becoming increasingly important. Studies have shown that it provides superior clinical outcomes compared with adjuvant therapy alone [8].

In the SWOG S1801 trial, the combination of neoadjuvant and adjuvant pembrolizumab treatment significantly improved event-free survival (EFS; 72% vs 49%), confirming the benefit of early immune activation [18].

The phase III NADINA trial provided further evidence that neoadjuvant nivolumab and ipilimumab together achieved a markedly higher EFS (83.7% vs 57.2%) than adjuvant nivolumab alone, with benefits observed in patients with both BRAF-mutant and BRAF-wild-type melanoma [19].

In patients with metastatic melanoma, the CheckMate 067 trial demonstrated that OS was significantly longer with nivolumab plus ipilimumab combination therapy or nivolumab monotherapy than with ipilimumab alone [20]. Similarly, the 10-year follow-up of the KEYNOTE-006 study confirmed the durable long-term survival benefit of pembrolizumab in advanced melanoma, with an OS rate of 34.0% in the pembrolizumab group compared with 23.6% in the ipilimumab group, reinforcing the sustained efficacy of PD-1 blockade in metastatic disease [21].

While ICIs have demonstrated significant clinical efficacy in both monotherapy and combination regimens, this success is accompanied by a notable risk of immune-related adverse events (irAEs), including cutaneous manifestations. IrAEs can affect multiple organ systems, with the highest incidence reported in the skin, gastrointestinal tract, liver, lungs, and endocrine system [22]. In the available literature, the incidence of any irAEs in patients with advanced melanoma undergoing ICIs treatment varies widely, ranging from 54% to 96% [22]. Among all irAEs, dermatologic adverse events are the most prevalent, reported in 30–40% of patients receiving anti-PD-1 monotherapy, with both incidence and severity increasing when ICIs are used in combination [23–25].

According to the European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology (EADV) Task Force on Dermatology for Cancer Patients, dermatologic immune-related adverse events (d-irAEs) occur in approximately 30–40% of patients treated with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and in up to 50% of those receiving CTLA-4 inhibitors [26]. These reactions most commonly present as maculopapular, psoriasiform, lichenoid, or eczematous eruptions, but may also include autoimmune bullous diseases, pigmentary alterations, pruritus, oral mucosal lesions, and changes in hair or nails, as well as a small subset of rare but potentially life-threatening toxicities [26]. The broad range of clinical patterns can vary depending on the type of ICI used [26]. Pruritus, maculopapular rash, and vitiligo-like depigmentation are the most commonly reported d-irAEs in patients receiving ICIs therapy [25–28]. Approximately 50% of individuals develop their first skin toxicity within 12 months, while one-third experience it within the first 6 months of immunotherapy [29].

ICI-related skin toxicities can range from mild to severe and life-threatening events. According to the National Cancer Institute’s Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI-CTCAE), these toxicities are primarily classified as grade 1 or 2 and typically do not require discontinuation of immunotherapy [26, 27]. However, correct classification and grading can be challenging, as skin lesions during ICIs therapy may evolve rapidly. The pathophysiology underlying ICI-induced dermatologic toxicities remains incompletely understood. Several hypotheses have been proposed, including the reactivation of tissue-resident memory T cells, leading to localized and systemic high-level proinflammatory cytokine secretion [30]. Additional mechanisms include cross-antigen reactivity, disruption of self-tolerance, and cytokine dysregulation [31, 32]. Furthermore, off-target immune activation affecting organs expressing checkpoint receptors, tumour mutational burden, and alterations in the gut microbiome have also been implicated in irAE development [32–34].

Interestingly, the occurrence of dermatologic toxicities has been correlated with improved survival and enhanced tumour response, suggesting a potential biomarker role for these adverse events in predicting treatment efficacy. This phenomenon is hypothesized to reflect a more pronounced systemic immune activation induced by ICIs, which may concomitantly trigger both antitumour effects and off-target immune cutaneous reactions [35–37]. This finding is consistent with studies that have reported the development of vitiligo-like depigmentation and lichenoid skin reactions being linked to better OS and more favourable treatment outcomes [37, 38]. These observations indicate the possibility of d-irAEs serving as clinical biomarkers of treatment response. However, the prognostic value of specific d-irAEs phenotypes remains uncertain, as current evidence is limited and heterogeneous, with inconsistent findings across studies [26, 39].

As a result of the risk of irAEs, especially dermatologic toxicities, clinicians have to carefully evaluate the risk–benefit ratio when choosing between monotherapy and combination immune checkpoint blockade. This is particularly relevant for patients with stage IIA melanoma, who typically have favourable RFS even without adjuvant therapy. In contrast, stage IIB/C melanoma carries a 5-year recurrence risk of 35–43% and a 5-year melanoma-specific survival rate of 77–87%, reflecting a prognosis similar to stage IIIA/B disease [40]. These observations highlight the importance of making treatment decisions that are tailored to the individual patient and take into account both disease stage and the potential burden of irAEs.

Despite recent advances, several key questions remain regarding optimal therapeutic combinations and identification of predictive biomarkers that could lead to improved outcomes together with better immunotherapy tolerability for patients with high-risk melanoma [41]. Currently, there are no clinically validated biomarkers available that can accurately predict patient prognosis, immunotherapy response, or occurrence of d-irAEs. Further research integrating genomic and immune biomarkers is essential for advancing personalised benefit–risk assessment.

Study Aim

The aim of this study was to systematically review and characterize d-irAEs associated with immunotherapy in adult patients with melanoma. To ensure data homogeneity and enhance clinical interpretability, the analysis was deliberately restricted to individual case reports and letters concerning adults (≥ 19 years). These publication types provide detailed, patient-level information on clinical presentation, timing, management, and outcomes of skin toxicities, which are often underrepresented or aggregated in larger clinical studies. Focusing exclusively on adult case reports also minimized heterogeneity related to differences in immune response, treatment regimens, and toxicity profiles between pediatric and adult populations. This approach enabled a more consistent and nuanced synthesis of evidence regarding the spectrum, onset, and incidence of skin toxicities in real-world settings, thereby complementing existing data derived from clinical trials and broader cohort analyses.

Methods

Literature Search Strategy

The systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [42]. A systematic literature search was performed in the PubMed and EBSCO (MEDLINE Complete) databases to identify relevant reports published between January 2014 and December 2024. Access to MEDLINE Complete was obtained through EBSCOhost Research Platform.

The following search strategy was applied using both free-text terms and Boolean operators: ‘skin toxicity’ AND ‘immunotherapy’ AND ‘melanoma’. Detailed search strategies are presented in Supplementary Material (Tables S1–S3).

Eligibility Criteria

The purpose of this study was to summarize the incidence, time to onset, and spectrum of skin toxicities in patients with melanoma treated with immunotherapy. Accordingly, the inclusion criteria were as follows: documented occurrence of d-irAEs, English-language full-text case reports and letters concerning adults (≥ 19 years) receiving immunotherapy for melanoma were included.

The following types of records were excluded: abstracts (without full-text availability), review articles, original studies (including experimental and observational studies), animal studies, molecular targeted therapies including accelerated fibrosarcoma B-type (BRAF) and mitogen-activated extracellular signal-regulated kinase (MEK) inhibitors; as well as records describing other non-immunotherapeutic treatments for melanoma.

Study Selection Process

Following the removal of duplicate records, the remaining studies were screened for eligibility. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion among three independent authors (G.K.-W., M.G. and K.Z.) until consensus was reached. Only studies meeting all predefined inclusion criteria were included into the final review. Figure 1 provides a flowchart of the selection process of the reviewed cases.

Data Selection and Extraction

From the included records, data were extracted on patient characteristics, including age, sex, and comorbidities, with particular focus on autoimmune disorders. When available, information regarding Breslow thickness, tumour, nodes and metastases (TNM) final staging, and BRAF mutation status was also collected. The type of ICIs administered was documented, including PD-1 inhibitors (nivolumab, pembrolizumab), PD-L1 inhibitors (durvalumab), and CTLA-4 inhibitors (ipilimumab). The time interval from initiation of ICIs to the onset of the first irAEs was carefully documented. Additionally, therapeutic interventions were analysed, including the need for temporary or permanent discontinuation of immunotherapy.

Risk of Bias Assessment

The methodological quality of included case reports [43–57] was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Reports [58]. Each publication was evaluated independently by two reviewers (G.K.-W. and K.Z.) across eight predefined domains addressing clarity of patient description, diagnostic reporting, intervention, outcome and clinical relevance. The JBI checklist is based on eight items and each of which was rated as “Yes”, “No” or “Unclear or not applicable”.

The total score obtained of each study was presented in a descriptive manner and each study was categorized according to different levels of case report quality/risk of bias (high quality (low risk of bias) if 6–8 items scored yes, moderate quality (moderate risk of bias) if 4–5 items scored yes, and low quality (high risk of bias) if 0–3 items scored yes as per JBI checklist). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. The results of the appraisal were summarised in a descriptive manner and are presented in a supplementary risk of bias table; additionally, they were visualized with the Risk-Of-Bias VISualization (robvis) tool (v. 0.3.0) [59] (Supplementary Material).

Statistical Analysis

Data were extracted from selected case reports and letters and entered into a structured Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. The analytic dataset consisted of the following predefined variables: age (years); sex (male/female); primary melanoma location (cutaneous/mucosal/ocular/nail); Breslow thickness [mm] and final stage; presence of BRAF mutation; metastatic status (non-metastatic/metastatic locoregional or distant or unspecified) and location of metastases according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system (distant skin, subcutaneous, nodal refers to M1a/lung refers to M1b/other visceral organs refers to M1c/central nervous system refers to M1d/in-transit and satellite(s)/regional lymph node(s) or unspecified); type of immunotherapy (anti-PD-1/anti-CTLA-4/anti-PD-1 plus anti-CTLA-4 combination therapy); treatment with sequential BRAF and MEK inhibitors; administered doses of pembrolizumab (2 mg/kg or other dose or unspecified), nivolumab (1 mg/kg or 3 mg/kg or other dose or unspecified) and ipilimumab (3 mg/kg or other dose or unspecified); d-irAEs were classified according to European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology (EADV) Task Force of Dermatology for Cancer Patients, as described by Apalla et al. [26]. Twelve categories were defined: eczematous rash, pruritus, lichen planus-like rash, psoriasis-like rash, vitiligo-like rash, autoimmune bullous disorders, hair irAEs, nail irAEs, oral mucosal irAEs, rare d-irAEs including cutaneous granulomatous/sarcoid-like eruptions, both with acantholytic dermatoses, scleroderma/scleroderma-like rash, immune-related eosinophilic fasciitis and immune-related cutaneous vasculitis, as well as potentially life-threatening d-irAEs so-called SCARs (severe cutaneous drug adverse reactions) including Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS), toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), drug reactions with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), and acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP). The remaining included data were presence or absence of histopathological confirmation; time from start of the therapy to d-irAE onset (weeks); presence of progression/recurrence (present/absent or death of patient); and follow-up (months).

Missing data (coded as ‘N/A’) were treated as missing at random. Analyses were performed using available case data only. Given the descriptive, case-report nature of the dataset, no imputation or data substitution was performed.

Quantitative synthesis was conducted in the form of a meta-analysis of pooled proportions for the frequency of reported d-irAEs. As a result of the exploratory nature of the study and the absence of control groups, classical effect-size meta-analyses were not applicable. All analyses were performed using the R environment for statistical computing version 4.4.2 “Pile of Leaves” released October 31, 2024 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, http://www.r-project.org).

Ethical Approval

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Results

Methodological Quality of Included Studies

Methodological quality of the included case reports [43–57] was assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist. Overall, the quality ranged from low to high. Eight reports were classified as high quality (≥ 6 “Yes” responses), nine as moderate quality (4–5 “Yes”) and one as low quality (≤ 3 “Yes”).

The most consistently reported domains included the diagnostic methods used to confirm the condition (D4), the clinical condition of the patient post intervention (D6), and the presentation of key takeaway lessons (D8), which were adequately described in 100% of case reports. In contrast, patient demographic characteristics (D1), medical history (D2), and current clinical condition of the patient (D3) were reported less consistently across the included publications. A detailed summary of the quality assessment is presented in Supplementary Material.

Cohort Group Studied Overview

Our literature search identified 18 patients with melanoma who developed reported d-irAEs during immunotherapy [43–57].

In the studied cohort, sex was reported for all patients (18/18, 100%), with the majority being male (72.2%), corresponding to a male-to-female ratio of 13:5. The median age was 63 years, with half of the cohort aged between 50 and 74 years (interquartile range, IQR 50–74), and the overall age range spanning from 38 to 81 years (Fig. 2).

Cohort overview. Distribution of demographic features among 18 patients with melanoma who experienced dermatologic immune-related adverse events (d-irAEs) during immunotherapy. a Age distribution presenting the median age (63 years), the interquartile range (50–74 years) and the overall age range (38–81 years). b Sex distribution demonstrating male predominance (13 male and 5 female, with a ratio of 13:5)

The most frequently administered immunotherapy was anti-PD1 monotherapy, received by 12 out of 18 patients (66.7%) [43–51]. Pembrolizumab was the treatment of choice for six patients (6/18, 33.3%) [43, 45, 47, 48, 50], whereas nivolumab for five patients (5/18, 27.8%) [44, 46, 49–51] and durvalumab for single patient (1/18, 5.5%) [51].

Combination therapy with anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1 ICIs (ipilimumab plus nivolumab) was administered in 3 of the 18 cases (16.7%) [52–54].

Skin toxicities were also reported in two patients (2/18, 11.1%) treated with anti-CTLA-4 monotherapy (ipilimumab) [55, 56], as well as in one patient (1/18, 5.5%) who received a non-standard combination of ipilimumab and pembrolizumab [57].

Within the pembrolizumab-treated subgroup (6/18, 33.3%), the most common dose was 2 mg/kg in four of six cases [45, 47, 48, 50]. One patient received a higher dose of 10 mg/kg [50], while in one case the dose was not specified [43].

Among nivolumab-treated patients (5/18, 27.8%), a dose of 3 mg/kg was reported in three of five cases [46, 49, 50]; in the remaining two cases, the dose was not specified [44, 51].

For patients receiving combined anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1 therapy (3/18, 16.7%), the dosing regimen was consistent across all cases: nivolumab 1 mg/kg plus ipilimumab 3 mg/kg [52–54].

An equivalent ipilimumab dose of 3 mg/kg was administered in one of the two patients treated with ipilimumab monotherapy (2/18, 11.1%) [55], while dosage information was unavailable for the second case [56].

Frequencies of d-irAEs: Pooled Analysis of Published Case Reports

The most common d-irAEs were autoimmune bullous disorders, reported in 5 out of 18 cases (27.8%; 95% CI 9.69–53.48%) [47, 50, 51], followed by potentially life-threatening d-irAEs, also observed in 5 out of 18 cases (27.8%; 95% CI 9.69–53.48%) [44, 49, 50, 52, 53]. Notably, three patients experienced a potentially life-threatening d-irAEs, manifesting as a severe, generalised bullous pemphigoid-like reactions [47, 50] and DRESS [44], had previously received sequential therapy with BRAF/MEK inhibitors.

Pruritus [45, 47], lichen planus-like rash [43, 48], psoriasis-like rash [45, 50], vitiligo-like rash [46, 55], rare d-irAEs [46, 57] and non-classified d-irAEs [54, 56] were each observed in 2 of 18 patients (11.1%; 95% CI 1.37–34.71%), whereas eczematous rash [51] was reported in a single patient (5.6%; 95% CI 0.14–27.29%).

Rare d-irAEs included a case of extragenital lichen sclerosus during nivolumab therapy [46], and a case of interstitial granulomatous dermatitis during combination therapy with pembrolizumab and ipilimumab [57].

Cases such as pseudolymphoma following nivolumab and ipilimumab therapy [54], and a delayed, severe drug-related dermatologic reaction secondary to ipilimumab [56] were classified as non-classified d-irAEs according to the EADV Task Force of Dermatology for Cancer Patients [26].

Considering the aforementioned search criteria, no cases with hair, nail or oral mucosal irAEs were reported (Fig. 3).

The most frequent d-irAEs were autoimmune bullous disorders and potentially life-threatening d-irAEs (both n = 5), followed by pruritus, lichen planus-like rash, psoriasis-like rash, vitiligo-like rash, rare d-irAEs and non-classified d-irAEs (each n = 2). One case of eczematous rash was reported, while no hair, nail, or oral mucosal irAEs were published

Histopathological confirmation of specific d-irAEs was available for 15 out of 18 cases (83.3%; 95% CI 58.58–96.42%).

Time to Onset of d-irAEs in the Cohort Group Studied

Analysis of time to onset of d-irAEs among 18 patients with melanoma included 20 observations. This was because, as in three cases, two distinct and temporally unrelated skin toxicities occurred in the same patient [46, 50, 51]. In one case, the exact time at which the d-irAE occurred was not specified [44].

The median time to onset was 14 weeks (IQR 23.5 weeks; Q1 3.75 weeks; Q3 27.3 weeks), with a range from 0.1 to 104 weeks.

Most d-irAEs occurred relatively early, with 50% developing within the first 14 weeks and 75% within approximately 27 weeks of initiating of ICIs. A subset of late-onset toxicities was observed after more than 90 weeks of treatment (with onset up to 104 weeks).

Predictive Factors and Prognosis in the Cohort Group Studied

All reported cases involved metastatic disease (18/18, 100%; 95% CI 81.47–100%), including 9 cases (50%; 95% CI 26.02–73.98%) with confirmed distant metastases [43–45, 47, 49, 53–55, 57]. Locoregional in-transit metastases were observed in one patient (1/18, 5.5%; 95% CI 0.14–27.29%) [52]. In the remaining 8 out of 18 cases, the disease was described only as ‘metastatic’ without further specification [46, 48, 50, 51, 56].

According to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system, among patients with distant metastases, one case involved pulmonary metastases (classified as M1b) [45], six involved metastases to other visceral organs (classified as M1c) [44, 47, 49, 53, 54, 57], and one presented with metastases to the central nervous system (CNS) (classified as M1d) [55].

Disease progression or patient death during or after discontinuation of immunotherapy was reported in 12 out of 18 cases (66.7%; 95% CI 40.99–86.66%).

Primary melanoma was predominantly cutaneous, occurring in 10 out of 18 cases (55.5%; 95% CI 30.76–78.47%) [44, 45, 47–50, 52, 54, 55, 57]. In 2 out of 18 cases (11.1%; 95% CI 1.37–34.71%), the primary site was ocular [46, 53]. In 6 of the 18 cases (33.3%; 95% CI 13.34–59.01%), the primary site was not reported [43, 50, 51, 56].

Breslow thickness ranged from 0.85 mm [57] to 5.1 mm [55], but was reported in only 5 out of 18 cases (27.8%; 95% CI 9.69–53.48%) [44, 48, 49, 55, 57]. A BRAF mutation was confirmed in 7 out of 18 cases (38.9%; 95% CI 17.30–64.25%) [43, 44, 47, 48, 50, 54, 55].

As a result of limited availability, the potential influence of comorbidities (including autoimmune diseases), ulceration of the primary tumour and other melanoma-specific mutations on the occurrence of skin toxicities was not included in the statistical analysis.

Discussion

Given that ICIs enhance systemic cytotoxic CD4+ and CD8+ T cell activity against tumour antigens, therapies targeting CTLA-4, PD-1, or PD-L1 are associated with a wide spectrum of irAEs. Among these, cutaneous manifestations are the most frequent, occurring in up to 70% of patients undergoing immunotherapy, and they often represent the earliest clinical sign of immune activation [60–62]. According to Geisler et al. 50% of patients receiving ipilimumab, and 30–40% receiving nivolumab or pembrolizumab will experience dermatologic complications [63].

The EADV Task Force ‘Dermatology for Cancer Patients’ emphasised that d-irAEs present with variable clinical phenotypes that differ depending on both the type of ICI administered and the underlying malignancy [26]. With regard to the potential impact of tumour histopathology on the development of d-irAEs, Wan et al. demonstrated that patients diagnosed with cutaneous malignancies have a higher probability of developing dermatologic toxicities compared with those receiving ICI therapy for non-cutaneous cancers [64]. The highest incidence was observed among patients with cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, affecting 38.7% of the study population, followed by melanoma, in which skin toxicities occurred in 30.9% of individuals [64].

The most recent version of the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) 6.0, published in 2025, provides an updated, and standardised framework for grading the severity of irAEs based on body surface area (BSA) involvement, symptom severity, and interference with activities of daily living [65]. Comprehensive consensus statements from the European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO), National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), Society for Immunotherapy of Cancer (SITC), and American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) provide recommendations for the early recognition, monitoring, and management of irAEs in patients with melanoma [66–69].

According to the updated guidelines, grade 1 skin toxicities (affecting less than 10% of the BSA and presenting with mild symptoms) should be managed with topical corticosteroids, emollients and oral antihistamines, while immunotherapy may be safely continued. Grade 2 reactions (affecting 10–30% of the BSA or presenting with moderate symptoms) may require temporary interruption of ICI therapy, and initiation of treatment with potent or super-potent topical corticosteroids or short-term systemic corticosteroids. Grade 3 toxicity (> 30% BSA or severe symptoms) warrants withholding ICI therapy and initiating high-dose systemic corticosteroids, with gradual tapering over several weeks once clinical improvement is observed. Grade 4 events require permanent discontinuation of immunotherapy and multidisciplinary management [65–69].

Severe reactions (grade ≥ 3) are uncommon, affecting less than 3% of patients receiving anti-PD-1 monotherapy, compared with 4–10% of patients treated with combined CTLA-4/PD-1 blockade [70].

Studies have shown that patients with a history of autoimmune diseases are more likely to experience irAEs, including skin toxicities [71, 72]. Therefore, patients with pre-existing chronic dermatological conditions such as psoriasis or atopic dermatitis are at increased risk of disease exacerbation during immunotherapy, warranting heightened clinical vigilance, particularly in the early phase of treatment, when most adverse events typically emerge [71, 72].

The etiopathogenic mechanisms underlying d-irAEs have not yet been fully elucidated. Recent studies have highlighted the growing importance of predictive biomarkers in estimating the likelihood of irAE development. Açar et al. (2025) identified a single-nucleotide polymorphism in the IL7 gene (rs16906115), together with alterations in peripheral lymphocyte kinetics, as factors that may increase predisposition to irAEs [73]. Similarly, Manger et al. (2025) demonstrated that the HLA-A*02:01 allele was associated with both an increased risk of treatment-induced adverse events, including dermatological manifestations, as well as an improved therapeutic response [74]. Furthermore, Mallardo et al. (2025) proposed a gene expression profile as a potential early-stage prognostic biomarker for identifying patients at increased risk of autoimmune toxicity [75].

On the basis of the available literature, pruritus, either as an isolated symptom or occurring alongside other skin toxicities (most frequently a maculopapular rash), is the most prevalent d-irAEs reported in patients undergoing ICI therapy [25–28, 76, 77]. A meta-analysis by Belum et al. indicated that pruritus of any grade occurred in 13% of patients treated with nivolumab and in 20% of those receiving pembrolizumab [39]. A higher incidence has been observed in patients treated with anti-CTLA-4 agents (25–36%), with the highest rates reported among those receiving combination anti-CTLA-4/PD-1 therapy (33–47%) [78, 79]. In general, pruritus represents an early-onset d-irAE, usually appearing within the first 3 to 10 weeks of ICIs therapy [26, 77].

By comparison, a maculopapular rash occurs in 49–68% of patients treated with anti-CTLA-4 agents, compared to 20% of patients receiving anti-PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy [78, 79]. It typically manifests between 3 and 6 weeks after the initiation of ICIs treatment [26, 77]. However, cases have also been reported in which the eruption developed either a few days after the first dose or several months into therapy [79].

Unfortunately, the data summarised in the present review do not support these observations. Only one case of ipilimumab-induced eczematous rash (5.5%) was identified [51], together with two cases of pruritus (11.1%): one reported in the context of a psoriasis-like rash during pembrolizumab therapy, and the other representing bullous pemphigoid associated with sequential treatment consisting of first-line ipilimumab followed by second-line pembrolizumab [45, 47]. This discrepancy is most likely attributable to the fact that the vast majority of cases observed in routine clinical practice are not life-threatening, tend to be self-limiting, and typically present with mild-to-moderate severity (grade 1–2 according to CTCAE 6.0). Treatment with topical corticosteroids in combination with oral antihistamines usually results in clinical improvement. Grade 3 pruritus is exceptional, occurring in fewer than 1% of cases [80]. In a study by Phillips et al. patients with ICI-induced pruritus were found to have higher ItchyQoL (Itchy Quality of Life) scores than individuals undergoing haemodialysis for uremic disease [81].

Psoriasis may develop de novo in patients receiving ICIs, but these agents can also trigger a flare-up or a significant progression of a pre-existing condition. Both clinical scenarios have been well documented in association with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy and, less frequently, with anti-CTLA-4 agents [82, 83]. Interestingly, the similar frequency of de novo and exacerbation-type presentations suggests that they may result from overlapping pathophysiological mechanisms, most likely related to ICI-induced dysregulation of T cell activity and enhanced activation of Th1/Th17-driven inflammatory pathways [83, 84]. De novo psoriasis usually develops between 5 and 12 weeks after initiation of ICI therapy [83], whereas exacerbation of pre-existing disease tends to occur earlier [70]. Psoriasis-like rash generally tend to be low grade and can be managed with standard antipsoriasis therapies. It rarely causes discontinuation of immunotherapy [85]. In a systematic review, Cutroneo et al. reported that ICIs—most commonly anti-PD-1 and less frequently anti-CTLA-4 agents—were permanently discontinued in 13.6% of patients, while temporary interruption was required in 15.5% of cases presenting with psoriasis-like eruptions [86].

In our cohort, both patients presenting with a psoriasis-like rash (11.1%) were treated with pembrolizumab. Psoriasis developed de novo in one patient [45] at week 12 of therapy, whereas the other had a prior history of psoriasis that was exacerbated by pembrolizumab at week 3 of treatment [50].

According to Watanabe and Yamaguchi, lichen planus-like eruptions affect approximately 0.5–6% of patients undergoing immunotherapy, although some single-institution studies have reported rates as high as 17% [28]. In line with the EADV Task Force, as outlined by Apalla et al., the overall frequency is typically reported as below 10% [26]. Published data indicate that lichen planus-like rash usually has a delayed onset, occurring several weeks to months after the initiation of ICIs therapy [76–79]. However, Shi et al. reported that lesions may appear much earlier, with some cases presenting as soon as 3 days after treatment initiation [87]. Lichen planus-like eruptions are observed more frequently in patients receiving anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy than in those treated with anti-CTLA-4 agents [88]. These lichenoid reactions may present across a broad clinical spectrum, ranging from classic lichen planus with flat-topped papules and characteristic Wickham striae, to hypertrophic or papulosquamous variants [79]. Most lichenoid mucocutaneous eruptions are mild to moderate in severity and can be effectively managed with topical corticosteroids. Systemic therapy or permanent discontinuation of ICIs is rarely required [87].

In our cohort, two patients treated with pembrolizumab developed a lichen planus-like rash (11.1%). Notably, the timing of onset varied substantially—one patient developed the rash at week 32 of immunotherapy [43], whereas the other experienced a sudden onset of a pembrolizumab-induced lichenoid drug reaction as early as day 1 of treatment initiation [48]. In both cases, the lesions were limited to the skin. However, rare cases of ICI-induced lichen planus with predominant hair or nail involvement, as well as palmoplantar, mucosal or inverse presentations, have also been reported [79, 87, 89–92].

A vitiligo-like rash indicates a strong predisposition to melanoma and is often associated with favourable tumour responses, reducing the risk of disease progression and mortality by twofold and fourfold, respectively [72, 93]. It predominantly occurs in association with anti-PD-1 and anti-PD-L1 therapies [77]. Meta-analyses estimate the incidence of this condition at 8.3% in patients treated with pembrolizumab and 7.5% in patients treated with nivolumab [26, 39, 93], with much lower frequency observed during anti-CTLA-4 therapy [26, 93]. Vitiligo-like rash represents a distinct entity from classic vitiligo, both clinically and immunologically [94]. Unlike classic vitiligo, ICI-induced vitiligo-like depigmentation does not exhibit the Koebner phenomenon and initially appears as asymmetrical, spot-like patches that gradually merge into larger areas of depigmentation in sun-exposed regions [93, 94]. Depigmentation of hair and poliosis has also been observed [80]. Vitiligo-induced by immunotherapy shows a predominance of cytotoxic, tumour-associated CD8+ T cells and, unlike classic vitiligo, it is not triggered by autoimmune destruction of melanocytes [94]. It typically has a delayed onset, occurring several months after the initiation of ICIs and often does not resolve following cessation of immunotherapy [93, 94]. Notably, treatment involving a combination of anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 has been associated with earlier onset and more widespread manifestation of vitiligo-like depigmentation [80, 93]. Unlike many other d-irAEs, the development of vitiligo-like rash does not require the discontinuation or temporary interruption of ICIs therapy, nor specific treatment other than sun protection and cosmetic cover-up [63, 94, 95].

Two patients in our cohort developed vitiligo-like toxicity (11.1%). One patient receiving ipilimumab presented with late-onset depigmentation, emerging after 104 weeks of therapy [55], whereas in the other patient, a vitiligo-like eruption appeared after 16 weeks of nivolumab treatment [46].

Analysis of case reports and letters published between 2014 and 2024 revealed that the most frequently described d-irAEs were autoimmune bullous disorders (27.8%) and severe, potentially life-threatening events (27.8%). All reported cases of autoimmune bullous diseases involved bullous pemphigoid (BP) [47, 50, 51]. Life-threatening reactions included three cases of TEN [49, 52, 53], one case of bullous erythema multiforme [50], and one case of DRESS syndrome [44]. The apparent prevalence of these entities is likely affected by reporting bias as researchers are more inclined to publish cases that are clinically significant, require intensive management, or result in temporary or permanent discontinuation of ICIs therapy.

According to current evidence, autoimmune bullous diseases are most frequently associated with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapies, with BP representing the dominant clinical presentation [78, 79]. The overall incidence of bullous dermatoses in patients treated with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 agents is estimated to be around 1% [96]. However, studies focusing specifically on anti-PD-L1 inhibitors report higher rates, ranging from 1.3% to 5% [97]. Other types of autoimmune bullous diseases in patients with melanoma undergoing ICIs therapy, such as mucous membrane pemphigoid, linear IgA bullous disease and epidermolysis bullosa acquisita, have only been reported rarely [98–100].

Clinically, lesions may appear abruptly or with delayed onset, emerging within days or several months of immunotherapy initiation, with an average onset reported at around 14 weeks [79, 80, 101]. In our cohort, the onset of bullous skin lesions occurred within a broad time frame, ranging from 16.1 to 44.1 weeks after the initiation of treatment.

Primary symptoms are typically pruritic, eczematous or urticarial, and may precede the development of tense bullae, which can delay diagnosis [78, 79, 101]. From a therapeutic standpoint, ICI-induced BP tends to be more refractory than idiopathic BP and often requires high-dose systemic corticosteroids alongside immunosuppressants [101, 102]. However, moderate disease can often be managed without definitively discontinuing immunotherapy. Treatment interruption is generally reserved for severe cases involving steroid-resistant, extensive blistering or mucosal involvement [80, 95].

Drug-induced BP can present in two distinct clinical patterns: an acute, self-limiting form that resolves upon discontinuation of the offending agent, and a chronic, long-term form that persists despite drug withdrawal and often requires prolonged immunosuppressive treatment, as observed in ICI-related BP [51, 102]. This persistent activity likely reflects sustained immune stimulation, which may contribute to ongoing antitumour effects [51]. Additionally, mucosal involvement appears to be more prevalent in immunotherapy-induced BP, reported in up to 40% of cases, compared with approximately 19% in primary BP [102, 103]. Lopez et al. reported that immunotherapy had to be discontinued in 76% of patients who developed BP-like reactions because of the severity of their symptoms [101].

In contrast, the true prevalence of SCARs remains difficult to determine, as most available data originates from case reports or small case series [104]. SCARs occurring during ICI therapy typically exhibit a delayed onset, with early manifestations often resembling nonspecific maculopapular or urticarial eruptions [96, 104]. Although the RegiSCAR (Registry of Severe Cutaneous Adverse Reactions) and EuroSCAR (European Registry of Severe Cutaneous Adverse Reactions) diagnostic criteria for DRESS and AGEP can support clinical assessment, it remains unclear whether these frameworks can be fully applied in the context of ICI-related SCARs [26, 104]. Mucosal involvement may also occur, affecting the oral, gastrointestinal, respiratory or genital tracts [70].

The latency period between the initiation of ICIs and the development of SCARs ranges from 1 to 20 weeks [63]. The mean time to onset of DRESS has been reported to range from 40 to 164 days [26]. AGEP and SJS/TEN can present with substantially delayed onset, sometimes up to 12 weeks after the initiation of ICIs, with the longest documented latencies reaching 197 days for TEN and 251 days for SJS [26, 105]. In our cohort, TEN was reported between 3 and 7 weeks after the start of immunotherapy.

The mortality rate for SJS, SJS–TEN overlap, and TEN in non-ICI-related cases is approximately 10%, 30%, and 50%, respectively [106]. Systematic reviews report mortality rates of 20–30% among patients who develop ICI-induced SJS/TEN, comparable to those of classic drug-induced forms [107, 108]. As a result of their high potential lethality, these reactions require prompt recognition and aggressive management. Symptoms are usually reversible, following discontinuation of immunotherapy and initiation of high-dose systemic immunosuppressive therapy [51, 104, 105, 109].

Rare d-irAEs encompass granulomatous, sclerodermoid, and neutrophilic reaction patterns, each occurring in < 1% of patients treated with ICIs. Data and treatment guidelines for these rare d-irAEs are based on limited published literature and ‘authors’ personal observations [26]. Rare skin toxicities tend to present with a delayed onset. Granulomatous reactions (most often sarcoid- or granuloma annulare-like) usually develop within weeks to months of initiating ICI therapy [110]. Sclerodermoid changes (morphea-like, systemic sclerosis-like, or eosinophilic fasciitis-like) may develop after 3 to 12 months of therapy [111]. From a therapeutic perspective, most reported cases of granulomatous irAEs are mild and can be managed with topical or systemic corticosteroids while continuing immunotherapy, except in the presence of significant organ involvement [110]. By contrast, sclerodermoid reactions and eosinophilic fasciitis-like syndromes more often lead to permanent discontinuation of immunotherapy due to functional disability and the risk of internal organ involvement [111]. However, current evidence is primarily based on small case series and may be subject to reporting bias.

Several cases of ICI-induced dermatomyositis [112], various forms of vasculitis with or without systemic involvement [113], and acantholytic dermatoses such as Grover’s disease [114] have been reported.

Although toxicities involving hair and mucous membranes are uncommon, they can have a significant impact on patients’ quality of life.

Alopecia areata represents the most frequently reported hair-related adverse event, affecting 1–2% of individuals receiving ICIs [115]. The development of alopecia has been linked to a favourable antitumour response [115].

Xerostomia and lichenoid reactions are the most commonly documented oral mucosal toxicities in the literature. Nonspecific stomatitis, periodontal disease, and mucosal inflammation have also been reported in patients undergoing anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-L1 therapy. In contrast, the incidence of oral side effects related to anti-CTLA-4 treatment remains poorly characterised [116, 117].

Most d-irAEs can be diagnosed based on their clinical morphology. Nevertheless, histopathological assessment is crucial in severe cases, atypical presentations, or for lesions that do not respond to standard therapy. Current European recommendations emphasise that histopathological examination should be performed in cases of bullous diseases, as well as in severe cutaneous adverse reactions [26].

Overall, d-irAEs represent a heterogeneous spectrum of reactions, ranging from mild, self-limiting eruptions to severe, life-threatening conditions requiring urgent intervention. In the context of melanoma treatment, identifying skin toxicities is particularly important, as they may serve not only as markers of immune activation but also as determinants of treatment continuation or cessation. As immunotherapy becomes the cornerstone of melanoma therapy, the ability to promptly identify and effectively manage skin toxicities will remain a key component of multidisciplinary dermato-oncological practice.

Limitations

A potential limitation of this study is that the literature search was restricted to two databases, PubMed and EBSCO (MEDLINE Complete), which may have reduced overall search sensitivity. Further limitations are imposed by the paucity of reported cases in the literature, which may impact the generalisability of the findings.

Conclusions

Our study illustrates the broad spectrum of d-irAEs in patients with melanoma treated with ICIs, ranging from common mild, self-limiting eruptions to rare but potentially life-threatening conditions. Considerable heterogeneity in morphology, timing of onset, and severity was observed even within our limited cohort, underlining the need for a high vigilance level and routine dermatological assessment throughout the course of immunotherapy. Despite the increasing use of ICIs, the number of published case reports, letters, and small case series remains surprisingly low compared with real-world prevalence, particularly with regard to mild-to-moderate skin toxicities.

The authors wish to highlight this significant underreporting gap and emphasise that mild-to-moderate dermatological adverse events remain insufficiently represented in the literature. The need for more systematic reporting of skin toxicities better reflecting the true real-world clinical burden is evident. In light of the growing importance of immunotherapy in the treatment of melanoma, advancing our understanding of d-irAEs and their implications for both efficacy and safety is crucial. Combining ongoing research with comprehensive real-world documentation may ultimately lead to more personalised management algorithms in the future.
