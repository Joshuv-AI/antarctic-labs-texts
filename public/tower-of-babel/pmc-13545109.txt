Comparison of different cleaning techniques for the removal of root canal sealer from dentine surfaces: An SEM-EDX analysis
Authors: Savas Sagmak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo evaluate and compare the effectiveness of different cleaning protocols for removing AH Plus root canal sealer remnants from coronal dentine surfaces before adhesive restorative procedures using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). Forty-nine dentine specimens prepared from extracted human molars were randomly allocated to seven groups according to the cleaning protocol: negative control, positive control, dry cotton pellet, ethanol with cotton pellet, ethanol followed by 37% phosphoric acid, ethanol followed by air abrasion (53 μm Al₂O₃), and ethanol followed by air abrasion and 37% phosphoric acid. Residual sealer contamination was quantified by measuring zirconium (Zr) weight percentages using EDX, while dentine surface morphology was evaluated by SEM. Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s post hoc test (p < 0.05). Significant differences were observed among the cleaning protocols (p < 0.001). The positive control group exhibited the highest zirconium content, whereas no zirconium was detected in the negative control group. Ethanol cleaning significantly reduced residual sealer compared with dry cotton pellet cleaning. Protocols incorporating phosphoric acid and/or air abrasion resulted in significantly lower zirconium levels than ethanol alone; however, no statistically significant differences were observed among these advanced cleaning protocols. The combined ethanol, air-abrasion, and phosphoric acid protocol yielded the numerically lowest residual zirconium content (2.64 ± 0.10 wt%), corresponding to a descriptive reduction of 86.4% relative to the positive control group. Ethanol alone was insufficient for the complete removal of AH Plus sealer remnants. Phosphoric acid and/or air abrasion following ethanol cleaning resulted in significantly lower residual zirconium levels than ethanol cleaning alone, although no statistically significant differences were observed among the advanced cleaning protocols. Further studies are needed to determine whether these improvements in surface cleanliness enhance adhesive performance and clinical outcomes.Keywords: Adhesion, Air abrasion, Dentine, Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX), Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Introduction

Successful endodontic treatment depends not only on effective root canal obturation but also on the quality of the subsequent coronal restoration [1, 2]. Root canal sealers play a critical role in achieving a fluid-tight seal by filling the irregularities between dentine walls and core filling materials [3, 4]. However, contamination of dentine surfaces with root canal sealers during obturation procedures is inevitable, particularly in the access cavity and coronal dentine [1].

Residual sealer on dentine surfaces has been shown to adversely affect adhesive procedures by interfering with resin infiltration, reducing bond strength, increasing microleakage, and ultimately compromising the long-term success of coronal restorations [1, 6, 9]. Therefore, complete removal of sealer remnants is a critical step prior to adhesive restorative procedures [5, 8].

Numerous methods have been proposed to remove root canal sealers from dentine surfaces. These include mechanical methods such as dry cotton pellets, ultrasonic devices, and air abrasion, as well as chemical approaches involving ethanol, acetone, organic solvents, and phosphoric acid [5, 7, 9]. Among these, ethanol is widely used due to its low toxicity, cost-effectiveness, and availability in clinical settings. However, several studies have reported that ethanol alone is insufficient for complete removal of epoxy resin-based sealers, such as AH Plus [5, 6].

Mechanical cleaning methods, particularly air abrasion, have gained attention due to their ability to physically remove contaminants and modify surface characteristics. Previous studies have demonstrated that air abrasion may provide superior cleaning efficiency compared to conventional methods, although standardization of parameters and potential clinical limitations remain concerns [9]. Phosphoric acid, commonly used in adhesive dentistry, has also been suggested as a cleaning agent due to its ability to alter the smear layer and enhance surface conditions [7, 8].

Most previous investigations have evaluated cleaning effectiveness indirectly through bond strength measurements [6, 8, 9]. Although bond strength is clinically relevant, it may be influenced by several factors unrelated to surface cleanliness, including the adhesive system, restorative material, specimen preparation, and testing conditions. Therefore, bond strength measurements do not necessarily indicate the actual amount of sealer remaining on the dentine surface. In contrast, SEM provides direct morphological visualization of residual deposits, while EDX enables objective elemental identification and quantitative measurement of sealer-specific components, such as zirconium in AH Plus [5, 10, 11]. The combined use of SEM and EDX may therefore provide a more direct and surface-specific assessment of sealer removal effectiveness.

Despite the increasing number of studies on sealer removal protocols, there is limited information regarding the direct quantification of residual AH Plus sealer following different cleaning techniques, particularly when comparing combined mechanical and chemical approaches. Therefore, the aim of this study was to compare the effectiveness of different cleaning protocols—including ethanol, air abrasion with 53 μm aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) particles, and phosphoric acid—in removing AH Plus sealer from dentine surfaces using SEM-EDX analysis.

This study tested the null hypothesis that there would be no difference between different cleaning methods in their effectiveness at removing root canal sealer residues from dentine surfaces.

Materials and methods

Study design

This in vitro experimental study was designed to evaluate and compare the effectiveness of different cleaning techniques in removing AH Plus sealer from dentine surfaces using SEM–EDX analysis. The sample size was determined by an a priori power analysis performed using G*Power version 3.1.2 (Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) for a fixed-effects one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA; omnibus test). Based on an effect size of f = 0.57, seven experimental groups, an alpha error probability of 0.05, and a statistical power of 80%, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 49 dentine specimens (n = 7 per group). Twenty-five extracted human molar teeth meeting the inclusion criteria were selected. Teeth with cracks, caries extending into the dentine, previous endodontic treatment, internal or external resorption, or other structural defects were excluded. Following extraction, soft-tissue remnants and calculus deposits were removed using hand curettes, and the teeth were stored in 0.1% thymol solution at 4 °C until use. Two dentine specimens were obtained from each tooth, yielding 50 specimens. Of these, 49 specimens, as determined by the power analysis, were randomly allocated to the seven experimental groups (n = 7 per group). All specimen preparation, contamination, cleaning, and evaluation procedures were performed by a single operator to ensure standardization.

Specimen selection and preparation

A total of 49 dentine specimens were prepared for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) analyses. The enamel layer was removed using a green-ring diamond bur under continuous water irrigation to expose the underlying dentine surface. Complete enamel removal was confirmed under 30× magnification using a Stemi C-2000 stereomicroscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

A Micracut 152 precision cutting device (Metkon, Bursa, Türkiye) was used to prepare the specimens. Each tooth was sectioned parallel to the buccal surface under continuous water cooling to expose the coronal dentine and obtain specimens simulating the axial dentine wall of an endodontic access cavity. To reproduce the chemical conditions typically encountered during endodontic treatment, the exposed dentine surfaces were sequentially treated with 3% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl), 0.9% sodium chloride (NaCl), 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), and a final rinse with 0.9% sodium chloride. Each solution remained in contact with the dentine surface for 1 min. Upon completion of the irrigation regimen, the specimens were thoroughly air-dried using an air–water syringe, as previously described [13].

The 49 specimens were randomly allocated to the seven groups (n = 7 per group) by drawing lots (Fig. 1).

Sealer application

AH Plus sealer (Dentsply DeTrey, Konstanz, Germany) was mixed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. An equal amount of AH Plus was prepared for each experimental group according to the manufacturer’s instructions and applied uniformly to completely cover the dentine surfaces using a microbrush. All applications were performed by the same operator under identical conditions. The specimens were left undisturbed for 5 min to simulate clinical contamination conditions [14].

Experimental groups and surface cleaning protocols

The specimens were randomly assigned to seven groups according to the contamination and dentine surface cleaning protocols:

Group 1 (Negative Control): No sealer contamination was applied, and no surface cleaning procedure was performed.

Group 2 (Positive Control): The bulk sealer was gently removed using an excavator, without rubbing or scraping the dentine surface and without any subsequent surface-cleaning procedure. This step was intended only to remove gross sealer excess rather than to decontaminate the dentine surface.

Group 3 (Dry Cotton Pellet): Sealer-contaminated dentine surfaces were cleaned using a dry cotton pellet with gentle mechanical pressure until the surface appeared visually clean.

Group 4 (Ethanol): Sealer-contaminated dentine surfaces were cleaned by gently rubbing the surface with a cotton pellet saturated with ethanol for 10 s.

Group 5 (Ethanol + Phosphoric Acid): Following ethanol cleaning, 37% orthophosphoric acid was applied to the dentine surface for 15 s. The surface was then rinsed with distilled water for 10 s and gently air-dried.

Group 6 (Ethanol + Air Abrasion): After ethanol cleaning, the dentine surfaces were subjected to air abrasion using 53 μm aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) particles delivered with an AquaCare Single air-abrasion device (Velopex, London, UK) at a pressure of 2.5 bar, in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommended settings, under standardized conditions (distance: 10 mm, angle: 90°, duration: 10 s).

Group 7 (Ethanol + Air Abrasion + Phosphoric Acid): Following ethanol cleaning, air abrasion was performed using 53 μm Al₂O₃ particles with an AquaCare Single device (Velopex, London, UK) at a pressure of 2.5 bar under the standardized conditions (distance: 10 mm, angle: 90°, duration: 10 s). Subsequently, 37% orthophosphoric acid was applied for 15 s, after which the surfaces were rinsed with distilled water for 10 s and gently air-dried.

SEM and EDX evaluation

Residual AH Plus root canal sealer on dentine surfaces was evaluated using a Hitachi SU-1500 scanning electron microscope equipped with an integrated energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy system (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). The specimens were randomly allocated to the experimental groups according to the surface-cleaning protocols. In addition to the negative control group, a sealer-contaminated positive control group subjected only to gross manual removal of the sealer with an excavator, without any subsequent surface-cleaning protocol, was included as the reference contamination group.

Prior to analysis, the specimens were dehydrated, freeze-dried, and sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold to provide electrical conductivity. SEM examinations were carried out at magnifications of ×500, ×1000, ×3000, and ×5000 to evaluate dentine surface morphology and the presence of residual sealer deposits. For each specimen, images were obtained from three randomly selected locations on the dentine surface.

EDX analyses were performed on the same areas examined by SEM. Spot measurements were obtained from three randomly selected locations on each specimen under standardized operating conditions using an accelerating voltage of approximately 20 kV and a constant working distance. The acquisition time was kept constant for all measurements to ensure comparable signal quality. Elemental composition was recorded as weight percentages (wt%). The mean of the three measurements was calculated to obtain a single zirconium value for each specimen, which was used in the statistical analyses.

Particular attention was given to zirconium (Zr), which was used as the specific marker of residual AH Plus contamination. Because zirconium is not naturally present in dentine and is a constituent of AH Plus sealer, it was used as the primary indicator of residual sealer contamination on the dentine surface. The amount of residual sealer remaining after each cleaning procedure was determined based on the detected zirconium content.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (Version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. Intergroup differences in zirconium (Zr) content were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When significant differences were detected, pairwise comparisons were performed using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post hoc test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The mean zirconium (Zr) weight percentages for each group are presented in Table 1; Fig. 2. A statistically significant difference in residual zirconium levels was observed among the seven groups [F = 319.33, p < 0.001]. The Positive Control group exhibited the highest Zr level (19.39 ± 2.31 wt%), indicating substantial residual sealer contamination, whereas no zirconium was detected in the Negative Control group. The Dry Cotton Pellet group also showed a high residual Zr level (14.53 ± 0.65 wt%), while ethanol cleaning reduced it to 7.12 ± 1.12 wt%. Further reductions were observed with the adjunctive phosphoric acid and/or air-abrasion protocols, with the lowest mean Zr level detected in the Ethanol + Air Abrasion + Phosphoric Acid group (2.64 ± 0.10 wt%). Tukey HSD analysis showed that the Positive Control group had significantly higher Zr levels than all other groups (all p < 0.001), while the Dry Cotton Pellet group had significantly higher levels than the ethanol and adjunctive cleaning groups (all p < 0.001). Compared with ethanol alone, all adjunctive protocols resulted in significantly lower residual Zr levels (all p < 0.001). However, no statistically significant differences were observed between Ethanol + Phosphoric Acid and Ethanol + Air Abrasion (p = 0.858), between Ethanol + Phosphoric Acid and Ethanol + Air Abrasion + Phosphoric Acid (p = 0.654), or between Ethanol + Air Abrasion and Ethanol + Air Abrasion + Phosphoric Acid (p = 0.075).

Discussion

The present study evaluated the effectiveness of different cleaning protocols for the removal of AH Plus sealer remnants from dentine surfaces using SEM-EDX analyses. The findings demonstrated significant differences among the tested cleaning methods. Ethanol-based cleaning protocols combined with phosphoric acid application and/or air abrasion resulted in substantially lower residual zirconium levels than conventional mechanical cleaning methods. Although the combination of ethanol, air abrasion, and phosphoric acid produced the lowest zirconium content, no significant differences were observed among the phosphoric acid, air abrasion, and combined treatment groups.

Residual root canal sealer on dentine surfaces may adversely affect subsequent adhesive procedures by reducing dentine wettability, impairing resin infiltration, and compromising bond strength [1, 6, 12, 13]. Therefore, effective removal of sealer remnants is essential to optimize the clinical performance and longevity of restorative procedures following endodontic treatment [1, 8].

Previous SEM–EDX studies have similarly reported that reductions in characteristic elemental markers are associated with improved cleaning efficiency [5, 12, 13]. Consistent with these findings, the present study demonstrated lower zirconium levels following the adjunctive cleaning protocols than following ethanol alone, indicating reduced residual sealer contamination.

Tian et al. reported that cleaning sealer-contaminated dentine surfaces with 70% ethanol or a surfactant-based cleaner left fewer residues than cleaning with a dry cotton pellet. SEM–EDX analysis also revealed lower zinc and zirconium levels after these protocols, indicating more effective decontamination [15]. Similarly, Çilingir et al. found that Endosolv E provided the highest microtensile bond strength and improved surface cleanliness after contamination with a calcium silicate-based bioceramic sealer. They also observed that a two-step self-etch adhesive produced higher bond strength than a universal adhesive in several groups, suggesting that both the cleaning protocol and adhesive system may influence subsequent bonding [16].

For epoxy resin-based sealer residues, Zaniboni et al. demonstrated by SEM that amyl acetate and xylol provided better surface cleaning than 95% ethanol. However, these differences did not significantly affect the bond strength of the universal adhesives tested, indicating that greater residue removal may not necessarily translate into improved bonding performance [17]. In contrast, Bek Kurklu and Yoldas reported that dry or wet cotton pellets, water spray, and ultrasonic irrigation effectively removed a calcium silicate-based bioceramic sealer, with no significant differences in either residual sealer or microtensile bond strength [18]. The present study, however, identified significant differences among cleaning protocols, with ethanol followed by phosphoric acid and/or air abrasion producing markedly lower zirconium levels than conventional cleaning methods. This discrepancy may be related to differences in sealer composition and removal characteristics, particularly between calcium silicate-based bioceramic sealers and epoxy resin-based sealers such as AH Plus.

In the present study, cleaning with a dry cotton pellet showed limited effectiveness, as relatively high zirconium levels remained on the dentine surface. Ethanol cleaning significantly reduced residual sealer compared with dry mechanical cleaning; however, complete removal was not achieved. This finding is in agreement with previous studies reporting that epoxy resin-based sealers such as AH Plus exhibit considerable chemical stability and resistance to dissolution [5, 6]. Although ethanol may facilitate the removal of superficial contaminants, its ability to eliminate firmly attached resin-based sealer residues appears to be limited [5, 13].

The application of 37% phosphoric acid following ethanol cleaning further reduced zirconium levels, suggesting enhanced removal of sealer remnants from the dentine surface. Phosphoric acid may contribute to the elimination of residual contaminants through superficial demineralization and modification of the smear layer, thereby exposing a cleaner dentine substrate [12, 19]. Similarly, air abrasion with 53 μm aluminum oxide particles resulted in a marked reduction in residual zirconium content compared with ethanol cleaning alone. The mechanical action of airborne-particle abrasion may facilitate the disruption and removal of adherent sealer remnants from the dentine surface [20, 21].

Karadayi et al. evaluated different post-endodontic access cavity cleaning techniques for the removal of residual AH Plus sealer under clinical conditions. The authors reported that mechanically assisted cleaning protocols, particularly air abrasion, ethanol-saturated microbrushes, and ethanol combined with air polishing, achieved the highest sealer removal rates. In contrast, conventional approaches such as dry cotton pellets and short-term ethanol application showed substantially lower cleaning efficacy. These findings are consistent with the results of the present study, in which air abrasion significantly reduced residual zirconium levels compared with ethanol alone. Both studies suggest that mechanical cleaning approaches may enhance the removal of epoxy resin–based sealer remnants more effectively than chemical cleaning methods used alone [20].

Mine et al. emphasized that effective decontamination of dentine surfaces is essential for restoring optimal adhesive performance and that the success of cleaning procedures depends on both the nature of the contaminant and the cleaning strategy employed [21]. The lowest zirconium values were observed in the group receiving ethanol cleaning, air abrasion, and phosphoric acid treatment. However, this protocol did not demonstrate a statistically significant advantage over phosphoric acid or air abrasion alone following ethanol cleaning. This finding suggests that both phosphoric acid application and air abrasion are effective approaches for reducing AH Plus sealer contamination and that combining these procedures may provide only limited additional benefit under the conditions of the present study.

Air abrasion significantly improved cleaning efficiency compared with ethanol cleaning alone. This finding is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that mechanical cleaning approaches are more effective than chemical methods in removing sealer remnants from dentine surfaces [20, 21]. The improved performance of air abrasion may be attributed to its ability to mechanically disrupt and remove adherent resin-based sealer particles rather than relying solely on chemical dissolution [20].

Similarly, the application of 37% phosphoric acid following ethanol cleaning resulted in a substantial reduction in residual zirconium levels. Phosphoric acid may contribute to the removal of sealer remnants by modifying the smear layer and increasing dentine surface cleanliness through superficial demineralization [12, 19]. Although the phosphoric acid and air abrasion groups exhibited numerically different zirconium values, no statistically significant differences were observed between these protocols. This suggests that both methods may provide comparable cleaning efficacy when used following ethanol application.

The combined ethanol, air-abrasion, and phosphoric acid protocol yielded the numerically lowest zirconium value. However, no statistically significant differences were observed among the advanced cleaning protocols. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted solely as a numerical trend rather than evidence of an additive or synergistic effect.

A major distinction between the present study and many previous investigations is the method used to evaluate cleaning effectiveness. Numerous studies have relied on bond strength testing as an indirect indicator of surface cleanliness [8, 9]. However, bond strength measurements do not necessarily reflect the actual amount of residual sealer present on dentine surfaces. In contrast, EDX analysis enables direct identification and quantification of sealer-related elements, such as zirconium, providing a more objective assessment of residual contamination [12]. The use of zirconium as a tracer element represents a particular advantage when evaluating AH Plus sealer remnants because zirconium is not naturally present in dentine [11].

From a clinical perspective, reducing residual sealer contamination may provide a more favorable dentine substrate for subsequent adhesive procedures. Sealer remnants may impair dentine wettability and resin infiltration, potentially compromising interfacial integrity and the durability of post-endodontic restorations [1, 6, 8]. Therefore, the lower zirconium levels observed after phosphoric acid and/or air abrasion may indicate improved surface decontamination. Nevertheless, the present study did not evaluate bond strength, interfacial durability, or clinical outcomes; thus, a direct improvement in adhesive performance cannot be inferred from these findings.

The findings of the present study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, the study was conducted under laboratory conditions that may not fully reproduce the complex clinical environment. Second, only one epoxy resin–based sealer (AH Plus) was evaluated. Other sealers, particularly calcium silicate–based bioceramic materials, may exhibit different adhesion and removal characteristics; therefore, the present findings cannot be directly generalized to these materials [15–17]. Third, the study focused exclusively on surface cleanliness and did not assess whether residual sealer affected adhesive performance or bond strength. Furthermore, air abrasion with aluminum oxide particles may leave abrasive residues on the dentine surface, potentially influencing subsequent adhesive bonding. Because zirconium was used as the specific marker of residual AH Plus contamination, residual aluminum was not quantified in the present study. Future studies should investigate the relationship between residual sealer contamination and adhesive bonding performance, evaluate residual aluminum oxide particles and their effects on bond strength and interfacial durability, and compare different cleaning protocols across a broader range of root canal sealers under clinically relevant conditions.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, phosphoric acid and/or air abrasion following ethanol cleaning resulted in lower residual zirconium levels than ethanol cleaning alone. No statistically significant differences were observed among the advanced cleaning protocols. These approaches may improve the removal of AH Plus remnants from coronal dentine surfaces under in vitro conditions; however, future studies evaluating adhesive performance, followed by in vivo and clinical investigations, are required to determine whether these improvements translate into better restorative outcomes.
