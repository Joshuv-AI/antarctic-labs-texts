Female Sex, Higher Outerbridge Grade, and Preoperative Tibial Edema Are Associated With Failure After Arthroscopic Posterior Medial Meniscus Root Repair
Authors: Morgan C Wynveen, Antonella M Henson‐Vendrell, Mark A Glover, Alexis Restrepo, Andrew J Recker, Nicholas A Trasolini, Brian R Waterman, Jelle P van der List
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo characterize the prevalence of arthroscopic surgical revisions, postsurgical conversion to arthroplasty, and patient‐reported outcomes of medical meniscus posterior root tear repairs at 1‐ and 2‐year follow‐up timepoints, with a focus on identifying predictors of treatment failure.MethodsA retrospective case series study of patients who underwent primary arthroscopic medical meniscus posterior root tear repair performed at a tertiary referral center between 2017 and 2023, with a minimum of 1‐year follow‐up. Demographics, radiographic findings, intraoperative variables, and surgical outcomes were analyzed, and patients were contacted to further assess outcomes. Failure was defined as meniscal retear and/or conversion to knee arthroplasty, as confirmed by clinical examination and radiographic imaging by an orthopaedic surgeon. Univariate analyses used χ2 or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables and t‐tests for continuous variables.ResultsFifty‐three patients were included (mean age 54 ± 12 years, mean body mass index 33 ± 8 kg/m2, 69.8% women). Twelve patients (22.6%) met criteria for failure with a minimum of 1‐year follow‐up. Failure was associated with female sex (92% vs 63%, P = .033), preoperative tibial bone edema (58% vs 12%, P = .001), and higher medial compartment Outerbridge grade (2.8 ± 0.9 vs 1.9 ± 1.1, respectively, P = 0.015). Age, body mass index, fixation method, and preoperative extrusion were not associated with failure. Patients with successful outcomes showed significant improvement in pain and function, with mean visual analog scale score decreasing from 4.9 ± 3.1 to 2.1 ± 2.2 and Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation increasing from preoperative 51.7 ± 28.1 to 80.8 ± 18.2 at 2‐year follow‐up (P < .001 for both).ConclusionsIn this small cohort, predictors of failure included female sex, preoperative tibial bone marrow edema, and higher Outerbridge classification, whereas age, body mass index, and preoperative extrusion were not associated with failure. Overall, arthroscopic medical meniscus posterior root tear repair provided meaningful symptomatic and functional improvements in most patients at 2‐year follow‐up.Level of EvidenceLevel IV, retrospective case series.

Ameniscal root tear is a complete radial lesion or an avulsion in the posterior insertion zone of the meniscus within 10 mm of the tibial insertion. 1 , 2 , 3 Medial meniscus posterior root tears (MMPRTs) often occur in knees with mild to moderate degenerative changes and are frequently associated with obesity and varus knee alignment in middle‐aged and older individuals. 2

Traditional mainstays of MMPRT treatment, conservative management and meniscectomy, are associated with poor clinical outcomes, progression to osteoarthritis, and a high rate of conversion to total knee arthroplasty (TKA). In contrast, recent literature has shown MMPRT repairs leading to superior clinical outcomes and lower rates of osteoarthritis progression and conversion to TKA. 1 , 4 , 5 , 6 Multiple studies have evaluated how risk factors such as age, body mass index (BMI), chondral damage and radiographic evidence of osteoarthritis, and sex may influence outcomes after MMPRT repairs, yet their predictive value has been variable. Although some reviews have identified older age, female sex, higher BMI, and advanced chondral damage as predictors of poorer outcomes after repair, their independent predictive value when analyzed together remains unclear. 7 , 8 , 9 Therefore, further investigation at defined timepoints and multivariable analysis of failure predictors is needed to optimize patient selection and surgical decision‐making for MMPRT repair.

Understanding factors that may be associated with MMPRT repair treatment success or failure, such as age, gender, BMI, or cartilage status, is essential for optimizing patient selection and surgical decision‐making. The purpose of this study was to characterize the prevalence of arthroscopic surgical revisions, postsurgical conversion to arthroplasty, and patient‐reported outcomes (PROs) of MMPRT repairs at 1‐ and 2‐year follow‐up timepoints, with a focus on identifying predictors of treatment failure. It was hypothesized that female sex, worse chondral involvement, BMI, and older age would be associated with failure.

METHODS

Patient Selection

After institutional review board approval, a retrospective chart review was performed to identify all consecutive patients, regardless of their comorbidities, who underwent arthroscopic MMPRT repair with transtibial pullout technique by 4 orthopaedic surgeons (including authors B.R.W., N.A.T, J.P.v.d.L.) at a tertiary referral center between 2017 and 2023. Patients who underwent meniscal root tear repair, indicated for medial meniscal root tears confirmed by clinical assessment and radiographic assessment by an orthopaedic surgeon, with a minimum of 1‐year follow‐up, were included in the study. Concomitant procedures included chondroplasty, abrasion arthroplasty, or limited lateral meniscal debridement. Patients were excluded for concomitant injuries, osteotomies, or surgeries to the anterior cruciate ligament, posterior cruciate ligament, and other knee ligaments. Patient characteristics and preoperative PROs, including visual analog scale (VAS) and Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation (SANE) scores, were collected from the electronic medical record as documented by the treating provider at respective preoperative and postoperative office visits or phone visit surveys for missing scores.

Surgical Technique

Under general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the supine position with knee flexed to 90° and supported by a lateral post. Anteromedial and anterolateral portals were created, and diagnostic arthroscopy was performed. After the MMPRT was identified, the posterior root meniscal footprint was decorticated using a shaver. A scorpion suture passer (Arthex, Naples, FL) was used to pass 2 sutures through the meniscus root in a luggage tag configuration. Then, a guide pin was drilled using an anterior cruciate ligament guide set to 55° from the anterolateral tibia into the footprint. The core of the guide pin was removed, and a nitinol wire was inserted into the joint and retrieved through the anteromedial portal along with the luggage tag sutures. These sutures are then shuttled through the tibial tunnel out of the tibia. A 4.75 mm SwiveLock anchor (Arthrex, Naples, FL), cortical button, or bone bridge was then used to fix the meniscus luggage tag sutures with tension, whereas the arthroscope is used to assess reduction. Tension of the repair construct is confirmed. In 5 patients (9.4%), a SutureLoc (Arthex, Naples, FL) was used as has been described previously. 10 The other 48 cases used non‐SutureLoc fixation methods (Table S1). In 2 patients with significant bone marrow edema, intraosseous bioplasty (Arthex, Naples, FL) was performed using Autologous Conditioned Plasma platelet‐rich plasma (Arthex, Naples, FL) along with AlloSync (Arthex, Naples, FL). 10 Standard closure in layers was performed.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

For weeks 0 to 4, patients were toe‐touch weight‐bearing in a hinged knee brace in extension with crutches while in physical therapy for active/passive 0° to 90° knee range of motion and quadricep strengthening. At 4 to 6 weeks, patients progressed to full weight‐bearing as tolerated with locked braces and to full range of motion, except for no weight‐bearing with knee flexion beyond 90°. The rehabilitation program advanced at the following postoperative timepoints: 6 to 8 weeks to weight‐bearing without brace or crutches as quadriceps strength returns and gait normalizes; 8 to 12 weeks to closed‐chain exercises, proprioception exercises, stationary bike, and 0° to 90° lunges and leg press; and 12 to 16 weeks to progressive strength training with single‐leg focus, running, and sports‐specific exercises.

Data Collection

Electronic medical records were reviewed for patient demographics, radiographic findings (including meniscal extrusion and bone edema), intraoperative Outerbridge grades of chondral lesions (from the medial and lateral tibiofemoral and patellofemoral compartments), and surgical outcomes including preoperative and postoperative VAS scores, SANE scores, and the occurrence of postoperative complications including infection, stiffness, or recurrent pain, reinjury, or secondary operations. The Outerbridge classification was determined arthroscopically and categorized by the highest grade observed in the femur and tibia or patella and trochlea. Patients were contacted at 1 and 2 years post initial repair for any missing surgical outcomes including VAS and SANE scores, or postoperative complications, if they were not previously documented in the electronic medical record. Data was collected and stored in Microsoft Excel.

The primary outcome was clinical failure of the meniscal root repair, defined as either a retear of the meniscus based on a standardized clinical knee examination and confirmation on magnetic resonance imaging or conversion to arthroplasty, by an orthopaedic surgeon (B.R.W., N.A.T., J.P.v.d.L.). The patients were then subdivided into success and failure groups based on this definition.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA). Descriptive statistics were reported as means and standard deviations for continuous variables and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. For inferential comparisons, univariate analyses were performed using χ 2 or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables and independent‐sample t‐tests for continuous variables. A P Value < .05 was considered statistically significant. A statistical fragility index was calculated for significant categorical variables to assess the robustness of associations. Cohort‐specific minimal clinically important difference (MCID) analysis was calculated using a distribution‐based method (0.5 times the standard deviation of the delta score) at 1 and 2 years. Delta scores were defined as preoperative VAS minus follow‐up VAS (improvement in pain) and follow‐up SANE minus preoperative SANE (functional improvement).

Failures

Of 53 patients, 12 patients (22.6%) were considered failures based on documentation of a retear or conversion to TKA in the electronic medical record and/or phone call survey responses. Among the failure group, 5 patients (45.5%) had a retear of their meniscus at 7 ± 3 months of initial repair. Two retears resulted from traumatic injuries, occurring at a mean of 5 months of initial repair and managed with revision surgery. The remaining 3 retears were deemed degenerative in nature, occurring at a mean of 10 months from the initial procedure and managed nonoperatively. Additionally, 9 patients (75.0%) in the failure group were referred for arthroplasty within 16.0 ± 11 months of initial repair. Of these, 7 underwent TKA, 1 underwent unicompartmental knee arthroplasty, and another opted for conservative management owing to their young age (<50 years old).

As summarized in Table 2, patients with successful outcomes showed significant improvement in pain and function from preoperative (preop) to postoperative (postop) follow‐up. VAS scores from preoperative to 1‐year follow‐up improved from 4.9 ± 3.1 (n = 27) to 1.6 ± 2.0 (n = 22) at 1 year and 2.1 ± 2.2 at 2 years (n = 29), both P < .001. This improvement was also seen in SANE scores from preoperative 51.7 ± 28.1 (n = 17) to 84.1 ± 16.1 at 1‐year follow‐up (N = 20) and 80.8 ± 18.2 (N = 30) at 2‐year follow‐up, both with P < .001. At 1 year, the cohort‐specific MCID was 1.61 points for VAS and 13.41 points for SANE; 7/18 (38.9%) achieved VAS MCID, and 9/13 (69.2%) achieved SANE MCID. At 2 years, the MCID was 1.70 points for VAS and 13.58 points for SANE; 11/20 (55%) achieved VAS MCID, and 7/10 (70%) achieved SANE MCID.

Predictors

Patients who failed treatment were more often women (92% vs 63%, P = .033) and had a higher prevalence of tibial bone edema on preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (58% vs 12%, P = .001). Mean age did not differ between the failure and success groups (55 ± 7.4 vs 54 ± 12.6 years, P = .68). The mean medial compartment Outerbridge grade was significantly higher in the failure groups (2.8 ± 0.9 vs 1.9 ± 1.1, P = .015), and the proportion of grade 3 to 4 chondral lesions was statistically greater among the failure group (67% vs 37%, respectively, P = .039). No differences were observed for grade 3 to 4 chondral lesions in the lateral (17% vs 10%, P = .066) or patellofemoral (8% vs 22%, P = .17) compartments. Preoperative meniscal extrusion did not differ between groups (3.3 ± 1.2 vs 3.1 ± 1.3 mm, respectively, P = .617). Finally, there were no differences in the rate of intraosseous bioplasty (5% vs 0%, P = .30) (Table 3).

Postoperative Complications

Postoperative complications were uncommon. No postoperative infections or isolated bleeding events were identified in either cohort. Complications were observed in 19 of 53 patients (35.8%). The most common complication was persistent pain or stiffness for longer than 6 months, observed in 18 total patients, 7 of which were in the failure group. One patient from the failure group experienced both persistent pain or stiffness and postoperative bleeding.

DISCUSSION

The principal finding of this study was that 22.6% of patients experienced failure of medial meniscus posterior root repair at short‐term follow‐up, with female sex, preoperative tibial bone marrow edema, and medial compartment chondromalacia identified as predictors of failure, whereas age, BMI, and meniscal extrusion were not associated with failure. Furthermore, age, BMI, and meniscus extrusion were not predictors of failure. These findings suggest that arthroscopic MMPRT repair can be performed regardless of age, BMI, and preoperative meniscal extrusion but that there is a higher association of failure in female patients and once tibial bone marrow edema develops.

The findings of this study show that the female sex may be a potential predictor of medial meniscal root repair failure, although this finding varies across other studies. Although the prevalence of MMPRT is higher among the female sex, 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 no studies in the current literature have identified female sex as an independent risk factor for success or failure after MMPRT repair. 2 Chung et al. evaluated prognostic factors in MMPRT pullout fixation results and reported no significant difference in meniscal repair failure rates between men and women at a mean follow‐up of 5.9 years. 15 A retrospective review by Krych et al. concluded that MMPRTs are more common in older, female patients with a higher BMI and greater chondral generation, but they also found no significant association between sex and medial meniscus root tear repair outcomes. 16 Collectively, these findings suggest that factors more prevalent among female patients, particularly greater cartilage degeneration, may contribute to poorer surgical outcomes and thus higher rates of repair failure. Although female sex was significantly associated with repair failure in our cohort and may be a potential predictor of poorer outcomes, this association between them was statistically fragile, with a fragility index of 1. This indicates that a change in outcome for a single female patient would rend the result nonsignificant (P ≥ .05). Therefore, although this result suggests a possible sex‐based difference in outcomes, it should be interpreted cautiously. The higher association of failure in the female sex may not generalize to all populations, and larger prospective studies are needed to clarify whether female sex independently contributes to poorer outcomes or whether this effect is confounded by comorbidities such as cartilage degeneration and obesity. Although the specific mechanism independently linking female sex to root repair failure remains controversial, the association between female gender and known risk factors for osteoarthritis, in addition to baseline anatomic and degenerative factors and biomechanical loading differences related to body habitus and alignment, suggests that sex‐related differences in knee biomechanics could compromise root repair integrity and healing. 8 Our evidence suggests that extra consideration should be made when considering meniscal root repair surgery for women. Additionally, this study assessed sex as documented in medical records and did not capture gender identity, which may limit interpretation of sociocultural influences on outcomes.

The presence of preoperative tibial bone edema was a predictor of failure. This is supported by previous literature such as that done by Kaplan et al. who evaluated radiographic outcomes with magnetic resonance imaging and found that patients with increased tibial bone edema had worse outcomes in terms of meniscal extrusion and cartilage degeneration postoperatively. 17 This suggests that preoperative tibial bone edema may indicate a more severe underlying pathology, potentially increasing the risk of repair failure. In another study conducted to determine long‐term predictors of clinical failure after MMPRT pull‐out repairs at a minimum of 10‐year follow‐up, it was identified that preoperative varus alignment and increased postoperative meniscal extrusion were significant predictors of clinical failure in the long term, which included the need for conversion to TKA. Coronal knee malalignment, specifically greater than 5°, has been identified as a poor prognostic factor after medial meniscus posterior root repair in several other short‐term and midterm follow‐up studies. 9 , 18 , 19

In this study, patients who failed after repair often had Outerbridge grade chondral lesions greater than or equal to 3, especially in the medial compartment. This finding is supported in the literature because previous studies have reported Outerbridge grade 3 or 4 chondral lesions, indicating significant cartilage damage, to be negative prognostic factors after MMPRT repair, including resulting in PROs after multivariate analysis. 9 , 15 , 16 , 18 , 19 , 20 Additionally, biomechanical studies have shown that meniscal root tear repair may restore or reduce elevated peak tibiofemoral contact pressures toward normal values. This improvement in joint biomechanics may delay the development of early degenerative joint disease, further benefiting patients with baseline higher chondral defect. 21 , 22 A multicenter study also showed no observable progression in cartilage degeneration 6 months after meniscal root tear repair. 23

Although the SutureLoc (knotless) technique showed a higher proportion of failures compared with the non‐SutureLoc fixation techniques (60.0% vs 18.8%), the difference did not reach statistical significance. Given the small number of SutureLoc cases and overall sample size, this study was underpowered to determine whether the fixation method independently contributes to surgical failure. First described in 2020 by Prado‐Novoa et al. in porcine models, the knotless anchor repair showed superior biomechanical properties compared with the traditional transtibial knot repair for complete posterior meniscal root tears. 24 In cadaveric models, Bachmaier et al. further showed that knotless suture fixation resulted in reduced cyclic displacement over 100,000 load cycles compared with conventional techniques. 25 Subsequent studies have detailed the clinical use of this technique in patients. 26 , 27 Although the biomechanical advantages are promising, the current clinical evidence remains limited. Thus, these results should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating, and further investigation with larger, prospective studies is warranted to evaluate whether fixation method influences long‐term outcomes after MMPRT repair.

The VAS and SANE scores were recorded to assess patient‐reported pain and functional outcomes after repair. Patients in the success group reported significant improvement in their VAS and SANE scores from preoperatively to 1 and 2 years postoperatively. Studies have shown that meniscal root repair is associated with improved PROs compared with other treatment options such as partial meniscectomy or nonoperative management. Multiple studies identified MMPRT repairs resulted in superior radiological and clinical outcomes, including significant improvements in International Knee Documentation Committee, Lysholm, Tegner, and various subscales of the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome scores. 20 , 23 , 28 , 29 , 30 A clinically meaningful improvement was achieved by a substantial proportion of patients, with 39% to 55% meeting cohort‐specific MCID for pain (VAS) and about 70% meeting cohort‐specific MCID for function (SANE) at 1 to 2 years.

Although obesity has been consistently associated with an increased risk of MMPRTs, evidence regarding its impact on outcomes after repair remains conflicting. Higher BMI has been linked to increased odds of MMPRT and a significant risk factor for meniscal extrusion; 31 , 32 however, evidence on the impact of BMI on postoperative healing and clinical results is inconsistent. 15 , 33 Although some studies suggest that severe obesity (BMI > 35 kg/m2) and advanced age may be associated with poorer outcomes, 16 , 34 others including the present study found no significant correlation between BMI, age, and surgical failure. 15 Similarly, age alone has not been shown to consistently predict worse outcomes, 15 and older patients with moderate osteoarthritis may still benefit from repair over nonoperative management. 35 Overall, although BMI and age are important preoperative considerations, they should not automatically exclude patients from meniscal root repair.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. As a retrospective case‐control study, the relatively small sample size limits the generalizability of the findings, and larger, prospective studies are needed to validate these results and determine causality. This study did not account for the potential influences of concomitant procedures, including chondroplasty, abrasion arthroplasty, or limited lateral meniscal debridement, on outcomes. Additionally, VAS and SANE scores were collected by phone call surveys at 1‐ and 2‐year follow‐up if not originally documented during routine follow‐up office visits. As subjective measures, these scores are inherently variable and may fluctuate with day‐to‐day changes in patient perception, potentially introducing response bias. Their assessment was also incomplete because of limited pre‐ and postoperative score results recorded during follow‐up visits. Selection bias may also be present because of incomplete PRO data, which could also affect the representativeness of the data. Anchor questions were not collected; therefore, Patient Acceptable Symptom State/Substantial Clinical Benefit could not be calculated. Furthermore, some failures may have been missed among patients who could not be contacted. The follow‐up period of 1 to 2 years is another limitation because the development of end‐stage arthritis requiring arthroplasty often occurs several years after meniscal root repair. Asymptomatic retears or progressive degenerative changes may develop between 4 and 11 years postoperatively, with most conversions to TKA reported between 4 and 7 years after repair. 6 Additionally, because of the variety of surgical fixation techniques used and the limited number of cases in each fixation subgroup, the study was likely underpowered to detect significant differences between surgical fixation methods. Finally, certain statistically significant findings—such as the association between female sex and repair failure—were found to be statistically fragile, reflecting the limited sample size and the need for larger studies to confirm these associations.

CONCLUSIONS

In this small cohort, predictors of failure included female sex, preoperative tibial bone marrow edema, and higher Outerbridge classification, whereas age, BMI, and preoperative extrusion were not associated with failure. Overall, arthroscopic MMPRT provided meaningful symptomatic and functional improvements in most patients at 2‐year follow‐up.
