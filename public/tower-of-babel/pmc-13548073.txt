Rhapontigenin Alleviates Sepsis‐Associated Acute Kidney Injury and Is Accompanied by Modulation of Ferroptosis, Inflammation, and NF‐κB Signaling
Authors: Jing Kuang, Xiaoyun Zhu, Jun Fang, Shuli Hu, Wei Dai, Xuepeng Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSepsis‐associated acute kidney injury (SA‐AKI) is a common complication in critically ill patients and is associated with high mortality. Evidence suggests that ferroptosis‐related lipid peroxidation and inflammatory responses contribute to renal injury. The potential effects of rhapontigenin (Rha), a natural compound with antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties, have not been directly examined in SA‐AKI. HK‐2 cells were pretreated with Rha for 3 h, followed by stimulation with 10 ng/mL LPS for 24 h to establish an in vitro sepsis‐associated injury model. Cell viability was assessed by CCK‐8. Ferroptosis‐related changes were evaluated by measuring GPX4 and xCT expression, intracellular Fe2+, ROS, MDA, and BODIPY‐C11‐detected lipid ROS. Erastin and ferrostatin‐1 (Fer‐1) were used for pharmacological intervention. Inflammatory cytokines (IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α) were quantified by ELISA. NF‐κB activation was assessed by the p‐NF‐κB/NF‐κB and p‐IκBα/IκBα ratios and p65 nuclear localization, with RANKL used as an NF‐κB activator. In vivo, male C57BL/6J mice underwent cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) to induce SA‐AKI. The effects of Rha on kidney histology, renal injury markers, ferroptosis‐related indices, inflammatory cytokines, and NF‐κB signaling were evaluated. The IC50 of Rha was not reached within the tested concentration range of 0–80 μM. Rha at 5–40 μM did not significantly affect basal HK‐2 cell viability, whereas 80 μM caused a slight decrease. Rha treatment at 20 and 40 μM increased cell viability, decreased intracellular Fe2+, ROS, MDA, and lipid ROS levels, and increased GPX4 and xCT expression in LPS‐treated HK‐2 cells. Rha also reduced erastin‐induced Fe2+ accumulation, while Fer‐1 decreased Fe2+ and ROS levels and increased GPX4 and xCT expression in LPS‐treated cells. In addition, Rha lowered IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α levels and reduced NF‐κB phosphorylation and p65 nuclear localization. RANKL partially reversed the effects of Rha on p65 nuclear localization, cell viability, Fe2+ accumulation, ROS production, and inflammatory cytokine levels. In CLP mice, Rha at 25 and 50 mg/kg reduced serum NGAL, KIM‐1, Scr, and BUN levels and decreased renal Fe2+, MDA, and inflammatory cytokine levels. The kidney injury score was significantly reduced at 50 mg/kg. Rha alleviated experimental SA‐AKI, accompanied by reductions in ferroptosis‐related changes, inflammatory responses, and NF‐κB activation.Keywords: ferroptosis, inflammation NF‐κB signaling, rhapontigenin, sepsis‐associated acute kidney injury

Introduction

Sepsis‐associated acute kidney injury (SA‐AKI) is a common and severe complication in critically ill patients, associated with high mortality [1]. In the intensive care unit (ICU), around 30% to 50% of septic patients develop AKI, with incidence rates exceeding 60% in those with septic shock [2, 3]. Current treatment remains largely supportive, including infection control, fluid resuscitation, and organ support [4, 5]. In SA‐AKI, ferroptosis contributes to renal tubular injury through iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation, while excessive inflammation aggravates renal damage by promoting the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines. However, few interventions directly target these processes, highlighting the need for new experimental therapies.

The development of SA‐AKI is multifactorial and involves both direct and indirect effects of infection [6]. Among the mechanisms implicated in kidney injury, ferroptosis is an iron‐dependent form of cell death associated with oxidative stress and has been increasingly implicated in sepsis‐induced kidney damage [7, 8]. This process is characterized by intracellular iron accumulation and ROS generation, leading to lipid peroxidation and membrane damage [9, 10]. Recent studies have reported increased iron and lipid peroxide levels in both experimental models and patients with AKI, suggesting a role for ferroptosis in SA‐AKI [9, 11]. In sepsis models such as cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)‐induced mice, ferroptosis‐related markers, including GPX4 and xCT, are reduced, whereas iron accumulation and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels increase in parallel with worsening kidney injury [12, 13, 14]. Inhibition of ferroptosis in these models has been associated with reduced kidney damage and improved renal function, supporting further investigation of this process in SA‐AKI.

Inflammation is also involved in the progression of SA‐AKI [15, 16]. Activation of the NF‐κB pathway is a key event in the inflammatory response triggered by sepsis [17, 18]. NF‐κB promotes the expression of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, thereby amplifying systemic inflammation and aggravating kidney injury [19, 20]. Persistent activation of NF‐κB in sepsis has been associated with sustained inflammation, worsening renal function, and more severe tissue damage [19, 21, 22, 23]. Preclinical studies have shown that modulation of NF‐κB activity can attenuate kidney injury [24], suggesting that this pathway may be relevant to SA‐AKI.

Despite the documented involvement of ferroptosis and inflammation in SA‐AKI, effective therapies directed at these processes remain limited. Rhapontigenin (Rha), a naturally occurring stilbenoid and the aglycone metabolite of rhaponticin, has shown antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory activities in several experimental models [25, 26, 27, 28]. For example, Rha attenuated H2O2‐induced oxidative injury and lipid peroxidation, and was also shown to suppress inflammatory responses in other disease models [27]. The effects of Rha on ferroptosis in the context of SA‐AKI have not been directly examined.

In this study, using in vitro lipopolysaccharide (LPS)‐treated HK‐2 cells and in vivo CLP‐induced sepsis mouse models, we investigated whether Rha could alleviate sepsis‐associated kidney injury and modulate ferroptosis, inflammation, and NF‐κB signaling. These experiments aimed to clarify the cellular and molecular effects of Rha in a sepsis‐associated kidney injury model.

Materials and Methods

Rha Preparation

Rha was purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) with a purity of ≥ 98%. A stock solution was prepared in DMSO and diluted with culture medium to the required concentrations before use.

Cell Culture and Treatment

HK‐2 cells (ATCC) were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, MA, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) at 37°C with 5% CO2. To evaluate the effect of Rha alone on cell viability, HK‐2 cells were treated with 0, 5, 10, 20, 40, or 80 μM Rha for 24 h without LPS stimulation. Because 5–40 μM Rha did not significantly affect basal cell viability, whereas 80 μM caused a slight decrease, 10, 20, and 40 μM Rha were selected for the subsequent experiments. To establish an in vitro sepsis‐associated kidney injury model, HK‐2 cells were pretreated with 10, 20, or 40 μM Rha for 3 h, followed by stimulation with 10 ng/mL LPS (Sigma‐Aldrich) for 24 h. The main experimental groups were Control, LPS, LPS + 10 μM Rha, LPS + 20 μM Rha, and LPS + 40 μM Rha. For the study of RANKL effects, HK‐2 cells were pretreated with 40 μM Rha for 3 h and then stimulated with LPS in the presence or absence of 100 ng/mL RANKL [29, 30]. NF‐κB p65 nuclear localization was examined 60 min after RANKL addition, whereas cell viability, Fe2+, ROS, and inflammatory cytokines were assessed after 24 h. The final experimental groups included Control, LPS, LPS + 40 μM Rha, and LPS + 40 μM Rha + RANKL groups. For the ferroptosis intervention experiments, cells assigned to the Rha groups were pretreated with 40 μM Rha for 3 h. Fer‐1 (1 μM; MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) was added 30 min before LPS stimulation, whereas erastin (10 μM; MedChemExpress) was added together with LPS. Cells were then incubated for 24 h. For Fe2+ measurement, the groups were Control, LPS, LPS + 40 μM Rha, LPS + erastin, LPS + erastin + 40 μM Rha, LPS + Fer‐1, and LPS + Fer‐1 + 40 μM Rha. For ROS detection and Western blot analysis, the groups were Control, LPS, LPS + 40 μM Rha, LPS + Fer‐1, and LPS + Fer‐1 + 40 μM Rha. All cell experiments were independently repeated three times. Plate‐based assays were performed in three technical wells in each independent experiment, and the mean of the technical wells was used as one biological replicate for statistical analysis.

CCK‐8

HK‐2 cells (1 × 104 cells/well) were plated in 96‐well plates overnight. After the indicated treatments, CCK‐8 reagent (10 μL; Dojindo, Japan) was applied. After 1 h at 37°C, absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA).

Fe2+ Detection

Intracellular Fe2+ concentrations were determined with an iron detection kit (Beyotime Biotechnology). Briefly, after 24 h treatment, cell lysates were prepared and analyzed according to the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance was analyzed at 562 nm using a microplate reader. Fe2+ levels were calculated based on a standard curve constructed using known concentrations of ferrous sulfate (Sigma‐Aldrich). For renal Fe2+ measurement, kidney tissues were weighed, homogenized in the assay buffer supplied with the kit, and centrifuged. The resulting supernatants were analyzed using the same procedure, and renal Fe2+ levels were normalized to tissue weight.

ROS Detection

ROS production was evaluated using DCFH‐DA (Sigma‐Aldrich). After the indicated treatments, HK‐2 cells were incubated with 10 μM DCFH‐DA at 37°C for 30 min. After washing, fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Images from all groups were collected using identical exposure settings. Fluorescence intensity was quantified from five randomly selected, non‐overlapping fields per biological replicate using ImageJ software (version 1.53; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) and normalized to the Control group.

Lipid ROS Detection

Lipid ROS levels were measured using BODIPY 581/591 C11 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). After 24 h of treatment, HK‐2 cells were incubated with 2 μM BODIPY 581/591 C11 at 37°C for 1 h in the dark. The cells were then washed with PBS, collected, and resuspended in PBS. The oxidized BODIPY‐C11 signal was detected in the FITC channel using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). At least 10,000 events were collected for each sample, and the data were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10; BD Biosciences). Relative lipid ROS levels were quantified using the mean fluorescence intensity of the oxidized BODIPY‐C11 signal in the FITC channel.

MDA Detection

MDA levels were determined with an MDA assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology). Cell lysates or renal tissue homogenates were prepared according to the manufacturer's instructions. The samples were incubated with MDA detection reagent, and absorbance was monitored using a microplate reader. MDA levels were normalized to the protein concentration of each sample and expressed as nmol/mg protein.

ELISA

Cell culture supernatants were collected for the measurement of IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α. Kidney tissue homogenates were used to determine renal IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α levels, whereas serum samples were used to measure NGAL and KIM‐1. Commercial ELISA kits (Abcam, UK) were used according to the manufacturer's instructions. Samples (50 μL) were added to precoated plates, incubated with detection antibodies, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Western Blot Analysis

Proteins were extracted from HK‐2 cells or kidney tissues using RIPA buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology). After quantification, protein (30 μg) was separated by SDS‐PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). After blocking, membranes were treated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against GPX4 (CST, #6313), xCT (CST, #80333), GAPDH (CST, #5174), p‐NF‐κB p65 (CST, #3033), NF‐κB p65 (CST, #8242), p‐IκBα (CST, #2859), and IκBα (CST, #4812), followed by secondary antibodies (Abcam) for 1 h after washing. Protein bands were revealed with ECL reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software. GPX4 and xCT expression was normalized to GAPDH. The levels of p‐NF‐κB and p‐IκBα were normalized to total NF‐κB and IκBα, respectively.

Immunofluorescence (IF) Staining

HK‐2 cells were seeded on coverslips and subjected to the indicated treatments. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‐100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin for 1 h. The cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with an anti‐NF‐κB p65 antibody (CST, #8242; 1:200), followed by an Alexa Fluor 488‐conjugated secondary antibody (1:500) for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus) under identical imaging settings. Five randomly selected, non‐overlapping fields were analyzed for each biological replicate. The percentage of cells showing predominantly nuclear NF‐κB p65 staining was calculated as the number of cells with nuclear p65 staining divided by the total number of cells × 100%.

Animal Experiments

Male C57BL/6J mice (8–10 weeks old, 25–30 g) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Wuhan No.1 Hospital. The animals were maintained under a 12 h light/dark cycle at 22°C–24°C and 50%–60% humidity. Animals were acclimatized for 1 week before the experiments and had free access to food and water. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Wuhan No.1 Hospital (approval no. 2025012) and carried out according to the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, while also meeting the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. The group size of six mice was selected with reference to previous studies using comparable CLP‐induced acute kidney injury models [31, 32].

CLP Model Establishment and Drug Administration

Sepsis was induced in male C57BL/6J mice by performing CLP as previously described [33, 34, 35]. Mice were anesthetized with 3% pentobarbital sodium (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and a midline abdominal incision was made under sterile conditions. The cecum was exposed and ligated at approximately one‐quarter of its length from the tip, followed by a single puncture with a 20‐gauge sterile needle. A small amount of fecal material was gently extruded, and the cecum was returned to the abdominal cavity. The abdomen was then closed in layers, and normal saline (Otsuka Pharmaceutical, Tokyo, Japan) was administered subcutaneously after surgery. Sham‐operated mice underwent the same procedure without ligation or puncture. All CLP procedures were performed by the same trained investigator using the same ligation position, needle size, puncture number, fecal extrusion procedure, and postoperative fluid replacement. Mice were randomly assigned to the following groups: sham, CLP, CLP + 12.5 mg/kg Rha, CLP + 25 mg/kg Rha, and CLP + 50 mg/kg Rha, with six mice in each group. Group allocation was performed using a computer‐generated random sequence. An additional sham‐operated group receiving 50 mg/kg Rha alone (n = 6) was included for the renal histological assessment. Rha (Sigma‐Aldrich) was administered intraperitoneally 20 min after CLP surgery and again 12 h later. The Rha‐alone group received 50 mg/kg Rha using the same dosing schedule. The 25 and 50 mg/kg doses were selected with reference to a previous in vivo study in which Rha was administered intraperitoneally at these doses [36], while the 12.5 mg/kg dose was included based on our preliminary dose‐ranging experiments. Rha was first administered 20 min after CLP to assess early post‐injury treatment, and a second dose was given 12 h later as part of the predefined 24 h treatment protocol. Mice in the sham and CLP groups received the same volume of vehicle. No animals died before the 24 h endpoint, and no animals were excluded from the analysis. At 24 h after CLP, mice were deeply anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital at 100 mg/kg. Blood was collected by cardiac puncture, after which euthanasia was completed by cervical dislocation under deep anesthesia. Death was confirmed by the absence of respiration and heartbeat before tissue collection. Kidney tissues were collected for histopathological analysis, biochemical assays, and protein extraction, and serum samples were collected for ELISA and biochemical assays.

H&E Staining

Kidney tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, China) for 24 h and embedded in paraffin (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA). Sections (4 μm) were cut using a microtome (Leica, Germany), deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated. Sections were then stained with hematoxylin for 5 min and eosin for 1 min, followed by dehydration, clearing in xylene, and mounting with a mounting medium (Abcam, UK). Kidney injury was evaluated in 10 randomly selected, non‐overlapping cortical fields per section at ×200 magnification. Tubular dilation, epithelial swelling or disruption, cast formation, and luminal debris were scored according to the percentage of affected tubules: 0, no detectable injury; 1, < 10%; 2, 10%–25%; 3, 25%–50%; 4, 50%–75%; and 5, > 75%. The overall percentage of injured tubules across the 10 fields was used to assign one injury score to each mouse. Histological images were coded before evaluation. The kidney injury scoring was performed by blinded observers.

Serum Scr and BUN Measurement

At the end of the experiment, blood samples were collected by cardiac puncture under deep anesthesia, and serum was separated by centrifugation. Serum Scr was measured using the Serum Creatinine Detection Kit (Arbor Assays, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. BUN levels were determined using a mouse BUN ELISA kit (Sunlong Biotech, China). Absorbance values were recorded on a microplate reader, and concentrations were calculated from standard curves.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data from cell experiments are presented as mean ± SD from three independent biological experiments (n = 3). Technical replicates within each independent experiment were averaged and were not treated as independent observations. Continuous animal data are presented as mean ± SD, with six mice per group, whereas kidney injury scores are presented as the median and interquartile range. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using the Brown–Forsythe test. Comparisons among groups were performed using one‐way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's multiple‐comparisons test. Kidney injury scores were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's multiple‐comparisons test. All statistical tests were two‐sided. Exact adjusted p values are shown in the figures, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Rha Alleviated LPS‐Induced Changes in Cell Viability and Ferroptosis‐Related Parameters in HK‐2 Cells

The chemical structure and molecular information of Rha were shown in Figure 1A. We first evaluated whether Rha itself affected HK‐2 cell viability using the CCK‐8 assay. Concentrations from 5 to 40 μM did not alter viability compared with 0 μM, whereas 80 μM caused a slight but significant decrease (Figure 1B). Based on these results, concentrations up to 40 μM were selected for subsequent experiments. LPS exposure reduced HK‐2 cell viability, and Rha treatment partially restored viability in LPS‐exposed HK‐2 cells, with no significant change at 10 μM but significant improvement at 20 and 40 μM relative to the LPS group (Figure 1C). In HK‐2 cells, intracellular Fe2+ levels were elevated after LPS stimulation, and this increase was not altered by 10 μM Rha, whereas 20 and 40 μM reduced Fe2+ levels compared with LPS alone (Figure 1D). GPX4 and xCT protein expression was decreased following LPS treatment in HK‐2 cells; 10 μM Rha did not produce a significant change, while 20 and 40 μM increased the expression of both proteins relative to the LPS group (Figure 1E). MDA levels were also elevated in the LPS group and were reduced by 20 and 40 μM Rha, whereas 10 μM showed no significant effect (Figure 1F). ROS fluorescence intensity was increased after LPS exposure in HK‐2 cells, with no significant reduction at 10 μM but a significant decrease at 20 and 40 μM under Rha treatment (Figure 1G). BODIPY‐C11 flow cytometry further showed that lipid ROS levels were increased after LPS stimulation. Treatment with 20 and 40 μM Rha reduced lipid ROS levels, whereas 10 μM Rha produced no significant change (Figure 1H). To further evaluate ferroptosis‐related changes, intracellular Fe2+ levels were examined after treatment with erastin or Fer‐1. Erastin increased Fe2+ levels in LPS‐treated cells, whereas the addition of 40 μM Rha reduced this increase. Fer‐1 also decreased Fe2+ levels compared with the LPS group (Figure S1A). In separate experiments, ROS levels were reduced by either 40 μM Rha or Fer‐1, and combined treatment produced a further decrease compared with Fer‐1 alone (Figure S1B,C). GPX4 and xCT expression was increased after treatment with either 40 μM Rha or Fer‐1 compared with the LPS group (Figure S1D).

Rha Reduced LPS‐Induced Inflammation in HK‐2 Cells

Inflammatory cytokines were then analyzed in HK‐2 cells after LPS stimulation. IL‐1β levels were increased, and Rha reduced IL‐1β in LPS‐treated cells, with no significant change at 10 μM but significant decreases at 20 and 40 μM relative to the LPS group (Figure 2A). IL‐6 levels were also elevated after LPS exposure, and this increase was not altered by 10 μM Rha, whereas 20 and 40 μM lowered IL‐6 compared with LPS alone (Figure 2B). TNF‐α showed a similar response to LPS treatment (Figure 2C).

Rha Modulated NF‐κB Signaling and Related Cellular Changes in LPS‐Induced HK‐2 Cells, and These Effects Were Attenuated by RANKL

In LPS‐induced HK‐2 cells, treatment with Rha decreased NF‐κB pathway phosphorylation, as indicated by lower p‐NF‐κB/NF‐κB and p‐IκBα/IκBα ratios; this reduction was not evident at 10 μM but became significant at 20 and 40 μM (Figure 3A). IF staining showed that LPS increased NF‐κB p65 nuclear localization, whereas 40 μM Rha attenuated this increase. The addition of RANKL partially restored NF‐κB p65 nuclear localization compared with the Rha‐treated group (Figure 3B,C). Under the same LPS condition, 40 μM Rha improved cell viability, whereas the addition of RANKL reduced viability compared with the Rha‐treated group (Figure 3D). Rha also diminished intracellular Fe2+ accumulation, and Fe2+ levels increased again after RANKL exposure (Figure 3E). A comparable pattern was observed for ROS, with decreased fluorescence intensity following Rha treatment and a rise after adding RANKL (Figure 3F). IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α showed a similar pattern, with lower levels after Rha treatment and a partial increase after RANKL exposure (Figure 3G–I).

Rha Attenuated CLP‐Induced Renal Injury in Mice

H&E staining further revealed the morphological differences among the groups. No obvious histological abnormality was observed in kidneys from mice treated with 50 mg/kg Rha alone compared with the sham group (Figure 4A). In the CLP group, tubular dilation, epithelial swelling, and accumulation of luminal debris were observed. Rha treatment alleviated these histological changes. The effect was limited at 12.5 mg/kg, whereas better preservation of tubular structure was observed at 25 and 50 mg/kg, along with less epithelial disruption and reduced intraluminal obstruction (Figure 4F). CLP surgery increased serum NGAL and KIM‐1 levels. Rha treatment lowered both markers in CLP mice, with little change at 12.5 mg/kg and more apparent reductions at 25 and 50 mg/kg (Figure 4B,C). Scr and BUN concentrations were also elevated after CLP and declined following Rha treatment, reaching statistical significance in the two higher‐dose groups (Figure 4D,E). Kidney injury scores reflected the histological findings. The score was significantly lower in mice receiving 50 mg/kg Rha, whereas the reduction in the 25 mg/kg group did not reach statistical significance (Figure 4F).

Rha Reduced Ferroptosis‐Related Indices, Inflammatory Cytokines, and NF‐κB Activation in CLP Mice

In CLP mice, renal Fe2+ and MDA levels were higher than those in sham animals, and Rha administration lowered both parameters, with only slight changes at 12.5 mg/kg but significant reductions at 25 and 50 mg/kg (Figure 5A,B). Renal IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α levels showed a similar increase after CLP surgery but decreased following Rha treatment, reaching statistical significance in the two higher‐dose groups (Figure 5C−E). NF‐κB signaling was also altered in CLP mice, as reflected by increased p‐NF‐κB/NF‐κB and p‐IκBα/IκBα ratios, and these ratios were reduced after Rha administration, with evident changes at 25 and 50 mg/kg (Figure 5F).

Discussion

Current studies on Rha have mainly examined its antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory activities in different cell and animal models [28, 37]. In vitro work has reported reduced ROS levels and protection against oxidative injury after Rha treatment [27]. In some inflammatory disease models, Rha was also associated with lower pro‐inflammatory responses and improved pathological changes [28]. In a mouse model of MRSA‐induced acute lung injury, Rha improved bronchoalveolar lavage protein levels, inflammatory cytokines, and endothelial injury markers, an effect linked to CYP1A1 inhibition [38]. Despite these findings, the role of Rha in SA‐AKI has remained unclear. In the present study, Rha treatment was associated with changes in several injury‐related readouts in both LPS‐exposed HK‐2 cells and CLP mice, involving indices related to oxidative stress, inflammation, ferroptosis, and NF‐κB signaling.

Ferroptosis is a form of regulated cell death driven by iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation [39]. It has been increasingly recognized as an important mechanism in several forms of kidney injury, including SA‐AKI [9, 40]. Previous studies have linked ferroptosis in septic kidney injury to disturbed iron homeostasis, oxidative stress, and lipid peroxidation [41, 42, 43]. Although the effects of Rha on ferroptosis in SA‐AKI have not been directly examined, a previous study reported that Rha inhibited erastin‐induced ferroptosis in bone marrow‐derived mesenchymal stem cells [44]. Rha has also been shown to protect cells against oxidative injury through its antioxidant activity [27]. In the present study, Rha reduced intracellular Fe2+, ROS, MDA, and BODIPY‐C11‐detected lipid ROS levels and increased GPX4 and SLC7A11/xCT expression in LPS‐treated HK‐2 cells. Rha also reduced erastin‐induced Fe2+ accumulation, while Fer‐1 decreased Fe2+ and ROS levels and increased GPX4 and xCT expression. These findings are in line with previous studies showing that suppression of ferroptosis can reduce renal injury in experimental SA‐AKI. Previous work has examined different compounds and signaling pathways, while the present study focused on the effects of Rha and did not determine its direct molecular target. In addition, the ferroptosis intervention experiments were performed only in cultured cells, and the findings require further validation in vivo.

Inflammatory activation is a well‐recognized feature of SA‐AKI and has been closely linked to the progression of renal injury [45]. Increased IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α levels have been reported in experimental sepsis models and in patients with SA‐AKI, and are associated with aggravated renal injury [46, 47]. Among the pathways involved, NF‐κB has been studied extensively because of its role in cytokine production and amplification of local inflammatory responses in injured renal tissue [47]. Evidence from other disease models also supports an anti‐inflammatory role for Rha. In a Parkinson's disease model, Rha attenuated neuroinflammation by suppressing the mtDNA‐cGAS‐STING‐NF‐κB axis, and this effect was linked to enhanced PINK1/DRP1‐dependent microglial mitophagy [48]. In osteoarthritis chondrocytes, Rha attenuated IL‐1β‐induced inflammation and senescence by reducing INHBA expression and inhibiting AKT/FoxO1 phosphorylation [28]. In the present study, Rha reduced inflammatory cytokine levels and NF‐κB pathway phosphorylation in both LPS‐treated HK‐2 cells and CLP mice. Rha also reduced NF‐κB p65 nuclear localization in HK‐2 cells, whereas RANKL partially reversed its effects on p65 nuclear localization, cell viability, Fe2+ accumulation, ROS production, and inflammatory cytokine levels. These findings support a role for NF‐κB signaling in the cellular response to Rha, although they do not establish NF‐κB as the only pathway involved. Taken together, these observations suggest that the anti‐inflammatory action of Rha may also be relevant in SA‐AKI, although its role in this setting has not been fully defined.

The in vivo findings were broadly consistent with the cell‐based observations and extended them to the CLP model. In SA‐AKI, tubular injury is commonly reflected by histological abnormalities together with increases in injury‐related biomarkers and renal function indices [49]. Similarly, Bora et al. reported that tibolone alleviated renal injury together with oxidative and inflammatory changes in a rat model of sepsis‐induced AKI [50]. In the present study, Rha treatment was accompanied by improvement in these readouts, suggesting that the changes observed in vitro were also reflected in vivo. Together, these results indicate a similar pattern of response in the cell and animal models. However, current evidence for Rha comes mainly from non‐renal or non‐septic disease models, and direct studies in SA‐AKI remain scarce [38, 48]. The present in vivo data therefore provide preliminary evidence for the effects of Rha in experimental SA‐AKI, although further work is still needed to clarify the extent and basis of these effects.

A dose‐related pattern was observed in both the cell and animal experiments, with more apparent effects at the higher concentrations or doses tested. No obvious renal histological abnormalities were observed at 24 h in mice treated with 50 mg/kg Rha alone. However, this was a short‐term, organ‐specific assessment and does not exclude systemic or longer‐term toxicity. Although the pharmacokinetics of Rha and rhaponticin have been examined in rats [51, 52], systemic Rha exposure was not measured in the present study. Further studies are needed to define the relationships among dose, exposure, tolerability, and duration of response.

The link between ferroptosis‐related changes and inflammation is also relevant in SA‐AKI [53, 54]. Available evidence indicates that these processes are closely connected [53, 55]. Oxidative stress and iron‐dependent lipid damage may enhance inflammatory signaling, while inflammatory activation may, in turn, worsen redox imbalance and cellular injury [56]. In our present study, changes in ferroptosis‐related indices were accompanied by changes in inflammatory markers after Rha treatment, suggesting that these responses may be linked in this model. However, the direction and basis of this association cannot be determined from the current data. Further work will be needed to clarify how these processes interact and whether their overlap contributes to the response observed with Rha.

Several limitations should be considered. Although the CLP model is widely used to study septic kidney injury, it does not fully reflect the heterogeneity of human sepsis, and the findings were not validated using human samples. The study was limited to a 24 h endpoint and did not assess survival or longer‐term renal outcomes. In addition, the ferroptosis intervention and RANKL experiments were performed only in HK‐2 cells, without corresponding intervention studies in vivo. Further studies in other models of SA‐AKI, together with survival assessment, longer‐term follow‐up, and validation using human samples, are needed.

Conclusion

Overall, Rha treatment was associated with attenuation of injury‐related changes in cell and animal models of SA‐AKI, accompanied by alterations in ferroptosis‐related indices, inflammatory responses, and NF‐κB signaling. These findings support further investigation of Rha in experimental SA‐AKI. Additional studies are needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying these changes, establish an appropriate dose range, and determine whether the observed effects are sustained over time.
