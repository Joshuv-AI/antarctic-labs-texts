Håkon Noren Myhr, Sølve Eidnes

Abstract
. Discrete gradient methods are a class of numerical integrators producing solutions with exact preservation of first integrals of ordinary differential equations. In this paper, we apply order theory combined with the symmetrized Itoh–Abe discrete gradient and finite differences to construct an integral-preserving fourth-order method that is derivative-free. The numerical scheme is implicit and a convergence result for Newton’s iterations is provided, taking into account how the error due to the finite difference approximations affects the convergence rate. Numerical experiments verify the order and show that the derivative-free method is significantly faster than obtaining derivatives by automatic differentiation. Finally, an experiment using topographic data as the potential function of a Hamiltonian oscillator demonstrates how this method allows the simulation of discrete-time dynamics from a Hamiltonian that is a combination of data and analytical expressions.

1.
Introduction

For an ODE

\frac{d}{dt}x=f(x),\quad f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n},

(1.1)

a first integral is a function
G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that remains constant along a solution trajectory
x(t)
of (
1.1
), i.e.
G(x(t))=G(x(t_{0}))
for all
t>t_{0}
. A first integral could represent conservation laws for physical quantities, such as the energy and angular momentum in the Kepler problem
[
8
, Ch. I.2]
or the total mass in a chemical reaction
[
8
, Ch. IV.1]
. A particular example of a first integral is the Hamiltonian
H:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of a canonical Hamiltonian system, which is a system of the form

\dot{x}=S\nabla H(x),\quad\text{where}\;S:=\begin{bmatrix}0&I\\
-I&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times 2d},

(1.2)

with
I
being the
d\times d
identity matrix.
That the Hamiltonian is a first integral can be seen by calculating

\frac{d}{dt}H(x)=\nabla H(x)^{T}S\nabla H(x)=0.

(1.3)

This is true for any system
\dot{x}=S\nabla H(x)
where
S
is a skew-symmetric matrix, and not only for canonical Hamiltonian systems. In physics, one could think of
H(x)
as representing the total energy of the system and thus canonical Hamiltonian systems could be used to represent systems with energy conservation. Hamiltonian systems are also used in the setting of computational statistics when sampling from a probability distribution
[
19
]
. Here, the volume preservation (symplecticity
[
8
, Ch. VI]
) of the Hamiltonian dynamics simplifies the sampling procedure and allows for more efficient sampling algorithms called Hamiltonian Monte Carlo
[
19
]
.

In this paper, we will study and construct methods that preserve first integrals such as
H(x)
under discretization in time, while not relying on derivatives of
H
.

1.1.
Discrete gradient methods

In some cases, first integrals are preserved by Runge–Kutta methods. For instance, any Runge–Kutta method preserves linear first integrals
[
21
]
and symplectic Runge–Kutta methods preserve quadratic invariants:
H(x)=x^{T}Ax
when
A
is square and symmetric matrix
[
2
]
. However, for
n>2
, no Runge–Kutta method can preserve all polynomial invariants of degree
n

[
1
]
. Discrete gradient methods, on the other hand, are numerical integrators specifically constructed to preserve first integrals of a general form. They were defined formally for the first time in
[
5
]
. A discrete gradient (DG) of a first integral
H
is a function
\overline{\nabla}H:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
that satisfies the properties

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla}H(x,\hat{x})^{T}(\hat{x}-x)

\displaystyle=H(\hat{x})-H(x),

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla}H(x,x)

\displaystyle=\nabla H(x).

Note that, as remarked in
[
4
]
, the second property follows from the first if we require the discrete gradient to be differentiable in the second argument. A discrete gradient method (DGM) is a time-stepping scheme for a skew-gradient system, providing approximations
\hat{x}\approx x(t+h)
given
x\approx x(t)
by

\hat{x}=x+hS\overline{\nabla}H(x,\hat{x}).

(1.4)

DGMs preserve invariants since, by the skew-symmetry of
S
, we have that

\displaystyle H(\hat{x})-H(x)

\displaystyle=\overline{\nabla}H(x,\hat{x})^{T}(\hat{x}-x)

\displaystyle=h\overline{\nabla}H(x,\hat{x})^{T}S\overline{\nabla}H(x,\hat{x})=0,

which could be seen as a discrete version of (
1.3
).

These methods rely on approximations of the gradient
\overline{\nabla}H\approx\nabla H
, for which there exist several explicitly defined examples. The most common are the midpoint
[
5
]
and average vector field (AVF)
[
9
]
discrete gradients, which are second-order approximations of the gradient, and the Itoh–Abe discrete gradient
[
10
]
, which is of first order.

1.2.
Derivative-free numerical algorithms

Derivative-free numerical algorithms are useful in the context of optimization when seeking to minimize a function even if the gradient is not available. In optimization research, multiple authors have argued that using finite-difference approximations to replace exact derivatives is prohibitively expensive and sensitive to noise. However, the authors of
[
22
]
demonstrate that finite-difference approximations replacing derivatives within for instance the L-BFGS algorithm are competitive to well-known derivative-free optimization algorithms. Furthermore, optimization libraries such as the Python-based SciPy library
[
24
]
apply finite-difference approximations to approximate Jacobian and Hessian matrices when these are not supplied by the user and needed in specific algorithms. Specifically, finite-difference approximations used to approximate the Jacobian in Newton’s method are discussed in
[
12
,
3
]
. In the setting of numerical integration and preservation of first integrals, the Itoh–Abe discrete gradient represents a derivative-free approximation to the gradient of
H(x)
. Indeed, it is observed by
[
17
]
that the Itoh–Abe discrete gradient method for gradient systems is equivalent to the derivative-free optimization method, coordinate descent, when applied to solve linear systems. Considering the issue of obtaining samples from probability distributions where direct sampling is difficult, Itoh–Abe discrete gradients have been leveraged within the Hamiltonian Monte Carlo scheme to enable a derivative-free approach
[
14
]
.

2.
Derivative-free discrete gradient methods

The original discrete gradient method (
1.4
) is restricted to have the same order as the discrete gradient
\overline{\nabla}H
, but higher-order extensions have also been developed
[
16
,
20
]
. A general order theory to construct discrete gradient methods of arbitrary order was presented in
[
4
]
. Replacing
S
in (
1.4
) with an approximation
\overline{S}(x,\hat{x},h)
allows for the construction of higher-order schemes, even though discrete gradients can at most be second-order approximations of the gradient
[
15
]
. The approximation
\overline{S}(x,\hat{x},h)
is required to be skew-symmetric and to satisfy
\overline{S}(x,x,0)=S(x)
.

The construction of
\overline{S}(x,\hat{x},h)
requires potentially higher-order derivatives of the first integral
H(x)
and of the discrete gradient
\overline{\nabla}H(x,\hat{x})
, which might be expensive or infeasible to compute. Automatic differentiation is an increasingly common approach to obtain derivatives of functions, especially in machine learning settings where functions may be outputs of neural networks, but this requires the function to be implemented in a programming language that supports automatic differentiation and could be computationally expensive.

In this paper, we propose a fourth-order derivative-free integral-preserving method. This uses the symmetrized Itoh–Abe discrete gradient and an
\overline{S}(x,\hat{x},h)
that relies on finite difference approximations of derivatives of the discrete gradient
\overline{\nabla}H(x,\hat{x})
and the Hessian of
H(x)
. We will first present a general fourth-order discrete gradient method, derived using the order theory of
[
4
]
. Then we specify the scheme by choosing to use the symmetrized Itoh–Abe discrete gradient and present appropriate finite difference approximations that ensure that the order is kept while the scheme becomes derivative-free.

(Fourth-order DGM)

\overline{\nabla}H

\displaystyle S_{4}(x,\hat{x},h)=S

Remark 2.2
.

It should be noted that discrete gradient methods can also be applied to dissipative systems, in which case the skew-symmetric matrix
S
in (
1.2
) is replaced by a negative semi-definite matrix, and preserve the dissipation property
[
15
]
. However, in this case the higher-order schemes derived using the theory of
[
4
]
will not lead to dissipative discrete gradient methods in general. For instance,
S_{4}
given by (
2.1
) is not guaranteed to be negative semi-definite if
S
is.

If the discrete gradient is the AVF discrete gradient, then
Q(x,\hat{x})=0
for any
x,\hat{x}

[
4
]
, and (
2.3
) becomes the fourth-order method of Quispel and McLaren
[
20
]
.
We aim at constructing a derivative-free, fourth-order DGM. This could for instance be achieved by starting with the first-order Itoh–Abe DG, which is defined below:

Definition 2.3

(Itoh–Abe discrete gradient)
.

We use the following short-hand notation for the various vector concatenations or shifts along dimension
m
:

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{m}

\displaystyle:=[\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2},\dots,\hat{x}_{m},x_{m+1},\dots,x_{n}]^{T},

(2.6)

\displaystyle X_{m}

\displaystyle:=[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m},\hat{x}_{m+1},\dots,\hat{x}_{n}]^{T}.

Then the Itoh–Abe discrete gradient
[
10
]
is defined for a Hamiltonian
H:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, by

\overline{\nabla}_{\text{IA}}H(x,\hat{x}):=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{H(\hat{x}_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\dots,x_{n})-H(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\dots,x_{n})}{\hat{x}_{1}-x_{1}}\\
\frac{H(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2},x_{3},\dots,x_{n})-H(\hat{x}_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\dots,x_{n})}{\hat{x}_{2}-x_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
\frac{H(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2},\dots,\hat{x}_{n-1},\hat{x}_{n})-H(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2},\dots,\hat{x}_{n-1},x_{n})}{\hat{x}_{n}-x_{n}}\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{H(\hat{X}_{1})-H(\hat{X}_{0})}{h_{1}}\\
\frac{H(\hat{X}_{2})-H(\hat{X}_{1})}{h_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
\frac{H(\hat{X}_{n})-H(\hat{X}_{n-1})}{h_{n}}\\
\end{bmatrix},

(2.7)

assuming
x_{i}\neq\hat{x}_{i}
and letting
h_{i}=\hat{x}_{i}-x_{i}
. In the event that
x_{i}=\hat{x}_{i}
we define the
i
-th element of the discrete gradient to be given by
(\overline{\nabla}_{\text{IA}}H(x,\hat{x}))_{i}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}H(\hat{X}_{i})
.

This is a first-order approximation of the gradient. However, by computing the average of the Itoh–Abe discrete gradient and its adjoint (switching
x
and
\hat{x}
), we obtain a symmetric discrete gradient of second order, due to
[
8
, Th. II.3.2]
. We call this the symmetrized Itoh–Abe (SIA) discrete gradient. It is defined by

\overline{\nabla}_{\text{SIA}}H(x,\hat{x}):=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\overline{\nabla}_{\text{IA}}H(x,\hat{x})+\overline{\nabla}_{\text{IA}}H(\hat{x},x)\bigg)\!=\!\begin{bmatrix}\frac{H(\hat{X}_{1})-H(\hat{X}_{0})-H(X_{0})+H(X_{1})}{h_{1}}\\
\frac{H(\hat{X}_{2})-H(\hat{X}_{1})-H(X_{1})+H(X_{2})}{h_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
\frac{H(\hat{X}_{n})-H(\hat{X}_{n-1})-H(X_{n-1})+H(X_{n})}{h_{n}}\end{bmatrix}.

(2.8)

These discrete gradients are derivative-free, but only of first and second order; they will thus give first- and second-order integrators if used in the scheme (
1.4
) with
\overline{S}(x,\hat{x},h)=S
. We will now show how to construct a fourth-order derivative-free discrete gradient method, by combining the second-order Itoh–Abe discrete gradient with the fourth-order discrete gradient method in Theorem
2.1
. Since the fourth-order DGM requires the Hessian of
H
,
\nabla^{2}H
, and the derivative of the discrete gradient with respect to the second argument,
D_{2}\overline{\nabla}H(x,\hat{x})
, these quantities must be approximated in a derivative-free manner.

3.
Finite difference approximations

3.1.
Hessian approximation

Let
e_{i}
denote the
i
-th vector in the canonical basis, so that e.g.
e_{2}=[0,1,0,\dots,0]^{T}
, and let
\text{1}:=[1,\dots,1]\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We define the following difference operators:

\displaystyle[\Delta_{1}(H)(x)]_{i}

\displaystyle:=H(x+\tau e_{i})+H(x-\tau e_{i}),

\displaystyle[\Delta_{2}(H)(x)]_{ij}

\displaystyle:=H\big(x+\tau(e_{i}+e_{j})\big)+H\big(x-\tau(e_{i}+e_{j})\big).

By computing the average of the forward and backward difference approximations, we obtain the following second-order approximation of the Hessian:

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}H(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\tau^{2}}\bigg(2\cdot\text{1}\text{1}^{T}H(x)+\Delta_{2}(H)(x)-\Delta_{1}(H)(x)\text{1}^{T}-\text{1}\Delta_{1}(H)(x)^{T}\bigg)+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2})

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}_{\tau}H(x)+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2}),

(3.1)

where
\nabla^{2}_{\tau}H(x)
denotes the Hessian approximation. The order is easily verified by Taylor expansion, and the cost of this approximation in terms of evaluations of
H(x)
is
n^{2}+3n+1
. If
n>2
, this is less than directly applying the midpoint finite difference rule, which would require
2n^{2}+1
function evaluations.

3.2.
Derivative of the SIA discrete gradient

The symmetrized Itoh–Abe discrete gradient is given element-wise by

[\overline{\nabla}_{\text{SIA}}H(x,\hat{x})]_{i}=\frac{1}{h_{i}}\bigg(H(\hat{X}_{i})-H(X_{i})-H(\hat{X}_{i+1})+H(X_{i+1})\bigg).

(3.2)

By considering which variables depend on
\hat{x}_{j}
following the definitions of
X_{m}
and
\hat{X}_{m}
in (
2.6
) we obtain the following expression for the derivative of the SIA discrete gradient:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{x}_{j}}[\overline{\nabla}_{\text{SIA}}H(x,\hat{x})]_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{h_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{x}_{j}}\bigg(H(\hat{X}_{i+1})-H(\hat{X}_{i})\bigg)\quad&\text{if }i>j,\\
\frac{1}{h_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{x}_{j}}\bigg(H(X_{i})-H(X_{i+1})\bigg)\quad&\text{if }i<j,\\
\frac{1}{h_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{x}_{i}}\bigg(H(X_{i})+H(\hat{X}_{i+1})\bigg)-\frac{1}{h_{i}}[\overline{\nabla}_{\text{SIA}}H(x,\hat{x})]_{i}\quad&\text{if }i=j.\end{cases}

(3.3)

where the equation when
i=j
follows from the product rule. Approximating derivatives of
H(x)
with the midpoint rule we obtain

\frac{1}{h_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{x}_{j}}H(X_{i})=\frac{1}{2h_{i}\tau}\bigg(H(X_{i}+e_{j}\tau)-H(X_{i}-e_{j}\tau)\bigg)+\mathcal{O}(\frac{\tau^{2}}{h}),

(3.4)

and similar for
X_{i+1}
,
\hat{X}_{i}
and
\hat{X}_{i+1}
. Let us denote the total midpoint approximation of the SIA discrete gradient by
D_{2}^{\tau}\overline{\nabla}_{\text{SIA}}H(x,\hat{x})
such that

D_{2}\overline{\nabla}_{\text{SIA}}H(x,\hat{x})=D_{2}^{\tau}\overline{\nabla}_{\text{SIA}}H(x,\hat{x})+\mathcal{O}(\frac{\tau^{2}}{h}).

(3.5)

Computing
D_{2}^{\tau}\overline{\nabla}_{\text{SIA}}
requires
4(n^{2}+n)
evaluations of
H(x)
which is reduced to
4(n^{2}-n)
when the diagonal is not needed, which is the case when computing
Q_{\tau}(x,\hat{x})
due to its skew-symmetry. The derivative approximation of the first order Itoh–Abe method
D_{2}^{\tau}\overline{\nabla}_{\text{IA}}
requires
2(n^{2}+n)
evaluations.

3.3.
Finite precision in difference schemes

Due to limited machine precision, error from numerical methods or noise, it is often the case that a function
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
only can be evaluated to a finite precision. Let us denote this function by
f_{\epsilon}(x):=f(x)+\epsilon(x)
for some bounded error
\epsilon(x)\leq\overline{\epsilon}
. Computing a finite difference approximation needs to take the finite precision into account when choosing the discretization step size
\tau
, as discussed for instance in
[
12
,
22
]
. For the midpoint difference scheme, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f_{\epsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\tau}\bigg(f(x+\tau)-f(x-\tau)+\epsilon(x+\tau)-\epsilon(x-\tau)\bigg)+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\tau}\bigg(f(x+\tau)-f(x-\tau)\bigg)+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\tau^{2}+\frac{\overline{\epsilon}}{\tau}\bigg).

(3.6)

For
\tau>0
, the leading order term
\tau^{2}+\frac{\overline{\epsilon}}{\tau}
is convex and thus minimized by
\tau=\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{3}}
. For the Hessian approximation, we use a second-order approximation for the second derivative and find that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}f_{\epsilon}(x)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\tau^{2}}\bigg(f(x)-2f(x+\tau)-2f(x-\tau)+f(x+2\tau)+f(x-2\tau)\bigg)

(3.7)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\tau^{2}+\frac{\overline{\epsilon}}{\tau^{2}}\bigg).

By a similar argument as above, the leading order term is minimized by
\tau=\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{4}}
.

3.4.
Derivative-free fourth-order DGM

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
S_{4}^{\tau}
be given by the same form as
S_{4}
in Definition
2.1
with the following substitutions:

\displaystyle D_{2}\overline{\nabla}_{\text{SIA}}H(x,\hat{x})

\displaystyle\rightarrow D_{2}^{\tau_{1}}\overline{\nabla}_{\text{SIA}}H(x,\hat{x})\quad\text{in $Q(x,\hat{x})$},

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}H(\bar{x})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\nabla^{2}_{\tau_{2}}H(\bar{x}).

Here
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
are the finite difference step sizes for the derivative of the SIA DG approximated by (
3.5
) and the Hessian approximated by (
3.1
), respectively. Assume that the Hamiltonian can only be evaluated to a finite precision
H(x)+\epsilon(x)
, where the error
\epsilon(x)
is bounded by
\overline{\epsilon}
. Then

S_{4}^{\tau}(x,\hat{x},h)=S_{4}(x,\hat{x},h)+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\tau_{1}^{2}+\frac{\overline{\epsilon}}{\tau_{1}}+h^{2}(\tau_{2}^{2}+\frac{\overline{\epsilon}}{\tau_{2}^{2}})\bigg).

(3.8)

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle Q_{\tau_{1}}(x,\hat{x}):=\frac{1}{2}(D_{2}^{\tau_{1}}\overline{\nabla}H(x,\hat{x})^{T}-D_{2}^{\tau_{1}}\overline{\nabla}H(x,\hat{x})).

Then

\displaystyle Q_{\tau_{1}}(x,\hat{x})=Q(x,\hat{x})+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{1}{h}(\tau_{1}^{2}+\frac{\overline{\epsilon}}{\tau_{1}})\bigg),

due to the finite difference error in (
3.5
) and the finite precision error in (
3.6
). Similarly, we find

\displaystyle S\nabla^{2}_{\tau_{2}}H(\bar{x})S\nabla^{2}_{\tau_{2}}H(\bar{x})S

\displaystyle=S\nabla^{2}H(\bar{x})S\nabla^{2}H(\bar{x})S+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\tau_{2}^{2}+\frac{\overline{\epsilon}}{\tau_{2}^{2}}\bigg),

due to (
3.1
) and (
3.7
). In total, we arrive at

\displaystyle S_{4}^{\tau}(x,\hat{x},h)=S

\displaystyle+\frac{h}{2}S\bigg[Q_{\tau_{1}}(x,\frac{x+2\hat{x}}{3})-Q_{\tau_{1}}(\hat{x},\frac{2x+\hat{x}}{3})\bigg]S

\displaystyle-\frac{h^{2}}{12}\,S\nabla^{2}_{\tau_{2}}H(\bar{x})S\nabla^{2}_{\tau_{2}}H(\bar{x})S

\displaystyle=S

{}_{4}(x,\hat{x},h)+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\tau_{1}^{2}+\frac{\overline{\epsilon}}{\tau_{1}}+h^{2}(\tau_{2}^{2}+\frac{\overline{\epsilon}}{\tau_{2}^{2}})\bigg).

∎

Remark 3.2
.

Selecting
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
to minimize the leading order terms, following the discussion above, we find that

S_{4}^{\tau}(x,\hat{x},h)=S_{4}(x,\hat{x},h)+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{2}{3}}+h^{2}\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg).

(3.9)

Since
\overline{\nabla}_{\text{SIA}}H
is a symmetric discrete gradient and does not require derivatives, the combination with the gradient-free skew-symmetric
S_{4}^{\tau}
gives a fourth-order derivative-free method by

x_{n+1}=x_{n}+hS_{4}^{\tau}(x_{n},x_{n+1},h)\overline{\nabla}_{\text{SIA}}H(x_{n},x_{n+1}).

(3.10)

This yields

\displaystyle x_{n+1}

\displaystyle=x_{n}+hS_{4}(x_{n},x_{n+1},h)\overline{\nabla}_{\text{SIA}}H(x_{n},x_{n+1})+\mathcal{O}\bigg(h\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{2}{3}}+h^{3}\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=x(t_{n}+h)+\mathcal{O}\bigg(h\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{2}{3}}+h^{3}\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}+h^{4}\bigg),

assuming that
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
are chosen optimally regarding error from the finite precision evaluation of
H(x)
. Since
x_{n+1}
is defined implicitly, Newton’s method will be utilized to enable the solution of initial value problems.

4.
Convergence of Newton’s method

Newton’s method could be utilized to find the root
x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
F(x^{*})=0
of a differentiable function
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
F^{\prime}(x):=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}F(x)
be the Jacobian, which we assume is invertible for all
x\in D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The iterations of Newton’s method are then given by

x^{(i+1)}=x^{(i)}+F^{\prime}(x^{(i)})^{-1}F(x^{(i)}).

(4.1)

As proved in Theorem 5.1.1 in
[
12
]
, assuming that
F(x)=0
has a solution
x^{*}
and that
F^{\prime}(x)
is Lipschitz continuous for all
x\in D
, the iterations exhibit quadratic convergence; i.e., for some constant
\tilde{C}>0
we have that

\|x^{(i+1)}-x^{*}\|\leq\tilde{C}\|x^{(i)}-x^{*}\|^{2}.

Consider first using the derivative-dependent scheme (
2.3
) with the SIA DG to solve initial value problems on Hamiltonian form. That is, assuming
x:=x(t_{n})
we aim to find
\hat{x}\approx x(t_{n+1})
such that
F(\hat{x})=0
, where

\displaystyle F(\hat{x}):=

\displaystyle\hat{x}-x-hS_{4}(x,\hat{x},h)\overline{\nabla}_{\text{SIA}}H(x,\hat{x}),

(4.2)

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad F^{\prime}(\hat{x})=

\displaystyle I-h\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{x}}S_{4}(x,\hat{x},h)\overline{\nabla}_{\text{SIA}}H(x,\hat{x})-hS_{4}(x,\hat{x},h)\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{x}}\overline{\nabla}_{\text{SIA}}H(x,\hat{x}).

Considering then the gradient-free method of (
3.10
), we replace
S_{4}
by
S_{4}^{\tau}
. Also ignoring the second term of
F^{\prime}
, we arrive at approximations of
F
and
F^{\prime}
given by

\displaystyle F_{\tau}(\hat{x}):=

\displaystyle\hat{x}-x-hS^{\tau}_{4}(x,\hat{x},h)\overline{\nabla}_{\text{SIA}}H(x,\hat{x}),

(4.3)

\displaystyle\quad F_{\tau}^{\prime}(\hat{x}):=

\displaystyle I-hS^{\tau}_{4}(x,\hat{x},h)D_{2}^{\tau}\overline{\nabla}_{\text{SIA}}H(x,\hat{x}),

where
S^{\tau}_{4}(x,\hat{x},h)
is defined in Lemma
3.1
and
D_{2}^{\tau}\overline{\nabla}_{\text{SIA}}H(x,\hat{x})
is the approximation found in Equation (
3.5
). This begs the question of how the error introduced by
F_{\tau}
and
F^{\prime}_{\tau}
affects the convergence of Newton’s method, compared to the solution obtained by (
4.2
). Theorem 5.4.1 in
[
12
]
presents a general convergence proof for Newton iterations under inexact evaluation of
F
and
F^{\prime}
. The following theorem considers the special case in which the inexact iterations are given by the approximations in (
4.3
).

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
x^{*}
satisfy
F(x^{*})=0
, where
F
is defined as in (
4.2
). Consider the inexact Newton iterations

x^{(i+1)}=x^{(i)}+F_{\tau}^{\prime}(x^{(i)})^{-1}F_{\tau}(x^{(i)}),

(4.4)

using
F_{\tau}
and
F_{\tau}^{\prime}
given by (
4.3
). Assume that the Hamiltonian can be evaluated to finite precision
H(x)+\epsilon(x)
, with
\epsilon(x)\leq\overline{\epsilon}
. Let the finite difference step size for the derivative
D_{2}^{\tau_{1}}
be given by
\tau_{1}=\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{3}}
and for the Hessian
\nabla^{2}_{\tau_{2}}
be
\tau_{2}=\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{4}}
. Then

\|x^{(i+1)}-x^{*}\|\leq C\bigg(\|x^{(i)}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\big(\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{2}{3}}+h^{2}+h^{3}\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}\big)\|x^{(i)}-x^{*}\|+h\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{2}{3}}+h^{3}\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg).

(4.5)

Proof.

For inexact iterations by

\displaystyle\tilde{F}(x)

\displaystyle=F(x)+\epsilon(x)

\displaystyle\tilde{F}^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=F^{\prime}(x)+\Delta(x)

Theorem 5.4.1 in
[
12
]
states that the convergence of the iterations are determined by

\|x^{(i+1)}-x^{*}\|\leq C\bigg(\|x^{(i)}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\|\Delta(x^{(i)})\|\|x^{(i)}-x^{*}\|+\|\epsilon(x^{(i)})\|\bigg).

In this specific case the exact
F(\hat{x})
is given by (
4.2
), and from Lemma
3.1
and (
3.10
) it is clear that

F_{\tau}(\hat{x})-F(\hat{x})=\mathcal{O}\bigg(h\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{2}{3}}+h^{3}\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg),

(4.6)

meaning it is possible to find a constant
C_{1}\geq 0
such that

\|F_{\tau}(\hat{x})-F(\hat{x})\|\leq C_{1}(h\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{2}{3}}+h^{3}\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}).

To simplify the notation, let us omit the arguments for
S_{4}
and
H
and write
D_{2}
to represent
D_{2}\overline{\nabla}_{\text{SIA}}H
and similarly
D_{2}^{\tau}
for the finite difference approximation. For the Jacobian, we observe that

\displaystyle\|F_{\tau}^{\prime}(\hat{x})-F^{\prime}(\hat{x})\|\leq

\displaystyle\;h\big\|S^{\tau}_{4}D_{2}^{\tau}-S_{4}D_{2}\big\|+h\|\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{x}}S_{4}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;h\big\|S^{\tau}_{4}D_{2}^{\tau}-S_{4}D_{2}\big\|+\mathcal{O}(h^{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;h\|(S^{\tau}_{4}-S_{4})D_{2}\big\|+h\|S_{4}^{\tau}(D_{2}^{\tau}-D_{2})\big\|+\mathcal{O}(h^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\mathcal{O}(h\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{2}{3}}+h^{3}\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{2}{3}}+h^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\mathcal{O}(\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{2}{3}}+h^{2}+h^{3}\overline{\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{2}}).

The first equality follows from

\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{x}}S_{4}=\frac{h}{2}S\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{x}}\big(Q(x,\frac{1}{3}x+\frac{2}{3}\hat{x})-Q(\hat{x},\frac{2}{3}x+\frac{1}{3}\hat{x})\big)S+\mathcal{O}(h^{2}).

To derive the second inequality, we have used that for matrices
A,B
and
\hat{A},\hat{B}
,

\displaystyle\hat{A}\hat{B}-AB

\displaystyle=\hat{A}\hat{B}-\hat{A}B+\hat{A}B-AB

\displaystyle=\hat{A}(\hat{B}-B)+(\hat{A}-A)B.

The leading order terms of
S_{4}^{\tau}-S_{4}
are due to (
3.9
), while those for
D_{2}^{\tau}-D_{2}
are due to (
3.5
) and (
3.6
).

∎

5.3.
Implementation details

For the discrete gradients, we have that
\overline{\nabla}H(x,x)=\nabla H(x)
. Hence, for constructing an algorithm that is gradient-free, we need to initialize the Newton iterations with
x_{n+1}^{(0)}\neq x_{n}
. Integrating a trajectory
x_{n}\approx x(t_{n})
for
n=0,\dots,N
, we initialize the iterations with

\displaystyle x_{n+1}^{(0)}=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x_{n}+h\delta\quad&\text{if}\;n=0\\
x_{n}+x_{n}-x_{n-1}\quad&\text{else}.\\
\end{cases}

Here,
\delta
is a sample from a standard normal distribution
\delta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
. For
n\neq 0
, using
x_{n+1}^{(0)}=x_{n}+x_{n}-x_{n-1}
is equivalent with assuming that the vector field
f(x(t))
is constant for
t\in[t_{n-1},t_{n}]
.

The Newton iterations are terminated when
\|F(x_{n+1}^{(i)})\|\leq\text{TOL}=10^{-11}
or the number of iterations satisfies
i>20
. The code for the implementation of the numerical experiments can be found on GitHub.

To demonstrate how the derivative-free DGM allows for the numerical integration of Hamiltonians where exact differentiation might be more involved or inaccessible, we will consider the following Hamiltonian with a potential that is the interpolation of topographic (altitude) data. Let
q\in D:=[-1,1]\times[-1,1]\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

p\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

maps coordinates

is chosen such that

,
