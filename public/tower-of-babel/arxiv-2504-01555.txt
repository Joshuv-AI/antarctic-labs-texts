Bifurcation from multiple eigenvalues of rotating traveling waves on a capillary liquid drop
Pietro Baldi, Domenico Angelo La Manna, Giuseppe La Scala

Abstract




Abstract.

We consider the free boundary problem for a liquid drop of nearly spherical shape with capillarity,
and we study the existence of nontrivial (i.e., non spherical) rotating traveling profiles
bifurcating from the spherical shape, where the bifurcation parameter is the angular velocity.
We prove that every eigenvalue of the linearized problem is a bifurcation point,
extending the known result for simple eigenvalues
to the general case of eigenvalues of any multiplicity.
We also obtain a lower bound on the number of bifurcating solutions.

The proof is based on the Hamiltonian structure of the problem
and on the variational argument of constrained critical points
for traveling waves of Craig and Nicholls (2000, SIAM J. Math. Anal. 32, 323-359),
adapted to the nearly spherical geometry;
in particular, the role of the action functional is played here by the angular momentum with respect to
the rotation axis.

Moreover, the bifurcation equation presents a 2-dimensional degeneration,
related to some symmetries of the physical problem.
This additional difficulty is overcome
thanks to a crucial transversality property,
obtained by using the Hamiltonian structure
and the prime integrals corresponding to those symmetries by Noether theorem,
which are the fluid mass
and the component along the rotation axis of the velocity of the fluid barycenter.

Sketch of the proof.

A fundamental ingredient in Craig-Nicholls approach

More precisely, in Section

and

Next, we show some consequences of the geometric nature of the problem:
in Section

we use some geometric considerations to
find symmetries of the problem
and we follow the ideas of Noether Theorem to find the corresponding conserved quantities. An important tool that we also study in Section
4
is the operator
\mathcal{T}_{\theta}

in (
4.2
), which is the natural torus action of the problem,
for which we prove some key invariance properties, see Lemma
4.8
.
At this point, we are ready to start the study of equation (
1.26
).

In Section
5
we
prove some orthogonality (Lemma
5.2
)
and conjugation (Lemma
5.3
) properties
of the nonlinear operator in (
1.26
).
These two simple lemmas will have important consequences:
orthogonality will be used at the end of the proof of Theorem
1.1
,
see Lemma
12.1
, and it will be essential
in order to show the existence of at least two solutions
to (
1.26
),
while the conjugation property will be used to obtain
n
orbits of solutions,
see Lemma
11.6
.

In Section
6
we linearize the system at the origin
and list some properties of the linearized operator,
building the appropriate set up to implement the Lyapunov-Schmidt decomposition
in Section
7
.
The original problem is then reduced to a finite-dimensional one
by solving the range equation by the implicit function theorem, see Lemma
7.1
.
It is important to observe that the reduced problem is now an equation in even dimensional space;
more precisely, we are left to solve the bifurcation equation in a vector space of dimension
2n+2
.

Following
[
24
]
and the classical methods described in

[
22
,
40
,
52
]
,
we note that, even after performing the Lyapunov Schmidt decomposition,
the bifurcation equation
has still a variational structure, see Lemma
11.3
.
Hence, to solve the bifurcation equation, we look for critical points
of the reduced functional
\mathcal{E}_{V_{N}}
.
To this purpose, we introduce the level set of the angular momentum
as a constraint where to look for constrained critical points.

A close inspection of the constraint
\mathcal{I}(u)=a
makes clear the necessity of a further reduction,
as the level sets of the angular momentum resemble a cylinder of the type

\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{2n-1}
(see Lemma
10.3
).
Hence, in Section
8

we further decompose the problem separating the two
degenerate
directions in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

from the other
2n

nondegenerate
directions on
{\mathbb{S}}^{2n-1}
.
Note that the formula of the main order
\mathcal{I}_{0}(v)
in the Taylor expansion
of the reduced functional
\mathcal{I}_{V_{N}}(v)
in Lemma
10.3

does not depend on the degenerate variables.

Then, in Section
9
,
we choose the angular velocity parameter
\omega
in an efficient way
as a function of the nondegenerate component of the kernel
(Lemma
9.4
).
This choice of
\omega
guarantees that constrained critical points of the reduced functional
are, in fact, critical points, and therefore solutions of the nondegenerate bifurcation equation
(Lemma
11.4
). Thus the level sets of the reduced angular momentum functional

\mathcal{I}_{V_{N}}(v)=a
become
natural constraints
(in the sense of
[
5
]
)
for the reduced functional
\mathcal{E}_{V_{N}}(v)
.
Actually, we have that critical points of the restricted functional
(defined on a
2n
dimensional space)
invariant under the group action
\mathcal{T}_{\theta}
on a manifold
diffeomorphic to
{\mathbb{S}}^{2n-1}

are solutions to the bifurcation equation.
Hence, by using classical theorems of critical point theory
(Benci
{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
-index), we infer the existence of
n
distinct orbits of solutions,
see Lemma
11.6
.

Finally, in Section
12
, we solve the bifurcation equation
also in the two degenerate directions, thanks to the orthogonality property
of Lemma
5.2
.

Difficulties in the proof.

Although the strategy of the proof is well established,
we encounter some nontrivial challenges in the way to prove Theorem
1.1
,
which seem to be peculiar of the problem of the drop with its spherical geometry,
in the sense that they are not present for the more studied case
of traveling waves on a flat reference manifold (the ocean).
We try to explain this point.

In the problem of traveling gravity-capillary waves on a flat 2-dimensional lattice
considered by Craig and Nicholls
[
24
]
,
there is one degenerate direction, which corresponds to the zeroth Fourier coefficient

\hat{\psi}_{0}
of the velocity potential unknown
\psi
.
This degeneration is easily removed in
[
24
]

by restricting all the analysis to the
linear
invariant subspace of functions

(h,\psi)
where
\psi
has zero average over the lattice, i.e.,
\hat{\psi}_{0}=0
.
Also, the conservation of the fluid mass in
[
24
]

corresponds to the
linear
identity
\hat{h}_{0}=
constant.
The same happens, for example, also in the recent paper
[
10
]
with constant vorticity.
In fact, this can also be achieved for the drop:
the first orthogonality relation in (
5.7
)
(that is, the first orthogonality relation in (
5.10
))
gives an invariant
linear
subspace,
while the mass conservation, despite its cubic expression (
3.3
)
in the elevation function
h
,
could become the
linear
identity
\hat{h}_{0}=
constant
by means of a simple change of variable, as explained in Lemma 3.4 in
[
9
]
.

However, the problem for the drop contains
another
degenerate direction,
which corresponds to the conservation of the
x_{3}
-component
\mathcal{B}_{3}

of the barycenter velocity
\mathcal{B}
, see (
4.7
).
To better explain, let us describe the simple action of moving the barycenter of the drop
along the
x_{3}
axis.
Geometrically and physically, this is probably the simplest operation one can perform
and yet the reparametrization of a translated drop is an implicitly defined nonlinear diffeomorphism.
From a physical point of view, it is clear that if a drop rotating around the

x_{3}
axis is a solution to (
1.6
),
any other drop that rotates in the same fashion and translates along
x_{3}

with constant velocity is still a solution.

Now, unlike the previous degeneration, the conservation of
\mathcal{B}_{3}

is not linear, and it is hard, if not even impossible,
to find a change of variable that simultaneously turns both the conservation relations
into linear ones.
This would force one to work over infinitely-dimensional invariant
manifolds

of codimension 2.
This technical issue is not present in the flat case.
At a linear level, this is related to the fact that the Laplace-Beltrami operator

-\Delta_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}
has eigenvalues
\ell(\ell-1)
,
which vanishes for
\ell=0
and also for
\ell=1
,
in contrast with
-\Delta_{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}
.

From a technical point of view, we encounter the degeneracy
in Section
8
,
where, after performing the Lyapunov-Schmidt decomposition,
following the method of
[
22
,
24
,
40
,
52
]
,
we have to fix the bifurcation parameter
\omega
as a function
of the kernel component
v
by implicit function theorem,
and we face the fact that the dominant term (
9.8
)
depends nontrivially on all the components of
v
but two,

\hat{v}_{0,0}
and
\hat{v}_{1,0}
, which are the degenerate directions.

In fact, we choose not to perform any change of variable
to make the mass conservation a linear expression of
h
,
and we also choose not to work on infinitely-dimensional invariant manifolds.
Instead, the strategy that we find more convenient to overcome this obstacle
is to exploit the two orthogonality conditions
(
5.7
) (that is, (
5.10
))
coming from the Hamiltonian structure of our problem and Noether Theorem.
While the first relation in (
5.10
) is a simple linear expression,
the second one is not, but it gives a geometrical
transversality condition

in the vicinity of the bifurcation point.
This transversality property is sufficient to cancel out the Lagrange multipliers
related to the two degenerate directions in the proof of Lemma
12.1
.

Another nontrivial aspect of working on
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}

concerns the compatibility of the Lyapunov-Schmidt decomposition
with the torus action
\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}
, which does not seem to be a priori obvious,
because of the nontrivial algebraic expression of the spherical harmonics.
This compatibility is proved in Lemma
6.2
.

Tangential gradient
,
tangential Laplacian
.
Given a function
f:{\mathbb{S}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
we denote by
\mathcal{E}_{0}f
its 0-homogenous extension to
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus\{0\}

\mathcal{E}_{0}f(x)=f\Big(\frac{x}{|x|}\Big),\quad\ x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus\{0\}.

Using the extension operator
\mathcal{E}_{0}
, we define
the tangential gradient
\nabla_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}f

and the tangential Laplacian
\Delta_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}f

of a function
f:{\mathbb{S}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
as
the classical gradient and Laplacian of
\mathcal{E}_{0}f
,

\nabla_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}f=\nabla\mathcal{E}_{0}f,\quad\ \Delta_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}f=\Delta\mathcal{E}_{0}f.

(2.1)

By using the definition of tangential gradient in (
2.1
),
it also holds that, if
\tilde{f}
is any diffentiable extension of
f
in a neighborhood of
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, then

\nabla_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}f=\nabla\tilde{f}-\langle\nabla\tilde{f},x\rangle x.

(2.2)

Mean curvature
.
The mean curvature term
H(h)
in equation (
1.8
) is given by

H(h)=-\frac{\Delta_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}h}{(1+h)J}+\frac{2}{J}+\frac{\langle(D_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}^{2}h)\nabla_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}h,\nabla_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}h\rangle}{(1+h)J^{3}}+\frac{|\nabla_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}h|^{2}}{J^{3}},

(2.3)

where

J=\sqrt{(1+h)^{2}+|\nabla_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}h|^{2}},\quad\ \langle(D_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}^{2}h)\nabla_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}h,\nabla_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}h\rangle=\langle(D^{2}\mathcal{E}_{0}h)\nabla\mathcal{E}_{0}h,\nabla\mathcal{E}_{0}h\rangle,

(2.4)

and
D^{2}\mathcal{E}_{0}h
is the Hessian matrix of
\mathcal{E}_{0}h
.
Formula (
2.3
) is proved in Lemma 2.3 of
[
9
]
.

Divergence Theorem on
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
.
Given a vector field
F
on
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, one has

\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}\mathrm{div}\,_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}F\,d\sigma=2\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}\langle F,x\rangle\,d\sigma,

(2.5)

where
d\sigma
is the 2-dimensional Hausdorff measure, and
the tangential divergence is

\mathrm{div}\,_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}F=\mathrm{div}\,\mathcal{E}_{0}F.

Spherical harmonics
.
The real Hilbert space
L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2},{\mathbb{R}})
admits orthonormal Hilbert basis
made of real spherical harmonics.
In particular, we consider the
L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2},{\mathbb{R}})
orthonormal Hilbert basis

\{\varphi_{\ell,m}:(\ell,m)\in T\},\quad\ T:=\{(\ell,m)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\ell\geq 0,\ m=-\ell,\ldots,\ell\},

(2.6)

where
\varphi_{\ell,m}(x)
,
m=-\ell,\ldots,\ell
,
are the Legendre real spherical harmonics
on
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
of degree
\ell
written in Cartesian coordinates
x\in{\mathbb{S}}^{2}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
,
namely

\displaystyle\varphi_{\ell,0}(x)

\displaystyle:=c_{\ell}^{(0)}P_{\ell}(x_{3}),

\displaystyle\varphi_{\ell,m}(x)

\displaystyle:=c_{\ell}^{(m)}P_{\ell}^{(m)}(x_{3})\mathrm{Re}\,[(x_{1}+ix_{2})^{m}],\quad m=1,\ldots,\ell,

\displaystyle\varphi_{\ell,-m}(x)

\displaystyle:=c_{\ell}^{(m)}P_{\ell}^{(m)}(x_{3})\mathrm{Im}\,[(x_{1}+ix_{2})^{m}],\quad m=1,\ldots,\ell,

(2.7)

where
P_{\ell}
is the ordinary Legendre polynomial of degree
\ell
,

P_{\ell}^{(m)}
is its
m
th derivative,
and
c_{\ell}^{(m)}\in{\mathbb{R}}
is a normalizing coefficient;
see, e.g.,
[
8
]
, Example 2.48 in Section 2.11,
and
[
9
]
, Section 6.1.
For all
(\ell,m)\in T
, one has

-\Delta_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}\varphi_{\ell,m}=\ell(\ell+1)\varphi_{\ell,m}.

Sobolev spaces
.
Given any function
u\in L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2},{\mathbb{R}})
, one has

u=\sum_{(\ell,m)\in T}\hat{u}_{\ell,m}\varphi_{\ell,m},\quad\ \hat{u}_{\ell,m}=\langle u,\varphi_{\ell,m}\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}u\varphi_{\ell,m}\,d\sigma.

For any real
s\geq 0
, we define the Sobolev space
H^{s}({\mathbb{S}}^{2},{\mathbb{R}})
as

H^{s}({\mathbb{S}}^{2},{\mathbb{R}})=\{u\in L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2},{\mathbb{R}}):\|u\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}<\infty\},\quad\ \|u\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}^{2}=\hat{u}^{2}_{0,0}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\ell,m)\in T\\
\ell\geq 1\end{subarray}}\ell^{2s}\hat{u}^{2}_{\ell,m}.

(2.8)

Sometimes, we shortly write
L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2}),H^{s}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})

instead of
L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2},{\mathbb{R}}),H^{s}({\mathbb{S}}^{2},{\mathbb{R}})
.

\varphi_{0,0}\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\ell,m)\in S\\
\ell\geq 1\end{subarray}}\lambda_{\ell,m}\begin{pmatrix}\ell\varphi_{\ell,m}\\
-\omega_{0}m\varphi_{\ell,-m}\end{pmatrix}:\lambda_{\ell,m}\in{\mathbb{R}}\bigg\}.

(6.5)

Its orthogonal complement in
L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})\times L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
is the vector space

W:=\bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}\eta\\
\beta\end{pmatrix}=\lambda_{0,0}\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{0,0}\\
0\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\ell,m)\in S\\
\ell\geq 1\end{subarray}}\lambda_{\ell,m}\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{0}m\varphi_{\ell,m}\\
\ell\varphi_{\ell,-m}\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{(\ell,m)\in T\setminus S}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\eta}_{\ell,m}\varphi_{\ell,m}\\
\hat{\beta}_{\ell,m}\varphi_{\ell,m}\end{pmatrix}\\
:\lambda_{\ell,m},\hat{\eta}_{\ell,m},\hat{\beta}_{\ell,m}\in{\mathbb{R}},\ (\eta,\beta)\in L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})\times L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})\bigg\}.

The range of
L_{\mathcal{F}_{0},\omega_{0}}
is contained in

R_{\mathcal{F}_{0}}:=\bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}f\\
g\end{pmatrix}=\hat{g}_{0,0}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\varphi_{0,0}\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\ell,m)\in S\\
\ell\geq 1\end{subarray}}\hat{f}_{\ell,-m}\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{\ell,-m}\\
-\omega_{0}m\ell^{-1}\varphi_{\ell,m}\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{(\ell,m)\in T\setminus S}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{f}_{\ell,m}\varphi_{\ell,m}\\
\hat{g}_{\ell,m}\varphi_{\ell,m}\end{pmatrix}\\
:\hat{f}_{\ell,m},\hat{g}_{\ell,m}\in{\mathbb{R}},\ (f,g)\in L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})\times L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})\bigg\}.

The orthogonal complement of
R_{\mathcal{F}_{0}}
with respect to the scalar product of

L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})\times L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
is the finite-dimensional space

Z_{\mathcal{F}_{0}}:=\bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}f\\
g\end{pmatrix}=\lambda_{0,0}\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{0,0}\\
0\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\ell,m)\in S\\
\ell\geq 1\end{subarray}}\lambda_{\ell,m}\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{0}m\varphi_{\ell,-m}\\
\ell\varphi_{\ell,m}\end{pmatrix}:\lambda_{\ell,m}\in{\mathbb{R}}\bigg\}.

(6.6)

Define

W^{s}:=W\cap(H^{s+\frac{3}{2}}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})\times H^{s+1}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})),\quad\ R_{\mathcal{F}_{0}}^{s}:=R_{\mathcal{F}_{0}}\cap(H^{s}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})\times H^{s-\frac{1}{2}}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})).

(6.7)

The linear map
L_{\mathcal{F}_{0},\omega_{0}}|_{W^{s}}:W^{s}\to R_{\mathcal{F}_{0}}^{s}
is bijective.
Its inverse
(L_{\mathcal{F}_{0},\omega_{0}}|_{W^{s}})^{-1}:R_{\mathcal{F}_{0}}^{s}\to W^{s}
is bounded, with

\|(L_{\mathcal{F}_{0},\omega_{0}}|_{W^{s}})^{-1}(f,g)\|_{H^{s+\frac{3}{2}}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})\times H^{s+1}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}\leq C_{s}\|(f,g)\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})\times H^{s-\frac{1}{2}}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}

(6.8)

for all
(f,g)\in R_{0}^{s}
.
The constant

depends on
\sigma_{0},\ell_{0},s
.

We are interested in the operator
L_{\omega_{0}}=J_{0}^{-1}L_{\mathcal{F}_{0},\omega_{0}}
.
Observe that
J_{0}^{-1}
acts as a rotation on the arrival space of the operator, leaving the domain unchanged.
Thus,

\operatorname{ker}(L_{\omega_{0}})=\operatorname{ker}(L_{\mathcal{F}_{0},\omega_{0}})=V.

The range of

satisfies

R:=\operatorname{Range}(L_{\omega_{0}})=J_{0}^{-1}\operatorname{Range}(L_{\mathcal{F}_{0},\omega_{0}}),

whence

R=\bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}f\\
g\end{pmatrix}=\hat{f}_{0,0}\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{0,0}\\
0\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\ell,m)\in S\\
\ell\geq 1\end{subarray}}\hat{f}_{\ell,-m}\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{0}m\varphi_{\ell,m}\\
\ell\varphi_{\ell,-m}\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{(\ell,m)\in T\setminus S}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{f}_{\ell,m}\varphi_{\ell,m}\\
\hat{g}_{\ell,m}\varphi_{\ell,m}\end{pmatrix}\\
:\hat{f}_{\ell,m},\hat{g}_{\ell,m}\in{\mathbb{R}},\ (f,g)\in L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})\times L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})\bigg\}.

(6.9)

We also define

R^{s}:=J_{0}^{-1}R_{\mathcal{F}_{0}}^{s}=R\cap(H^{s-\frac{1}{2}}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})\times H^{s}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})),\quad\ Z:=J_{0}^{-1}Z_{\mathcal{F}_{0}}.

Thus

is the orthogonal complement of

in
L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})\times L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
.
We also note that

Z=V,\quad\ R=W,

(6.10)

while, for any real

, the spaces

and

are not equal
(if

, then both

and

,
which is impossible);
on the contrary,
Z=V
because they are finite-dimensional vector spaces,
and
R=W
as subspaces of
L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})\times L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
.
Since
V
is finite-dimensional, we denote

|v|:=\|v\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})\times L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}

(6.11)

for all

.
Also, we introduce the notation

\|(\eta,\beta)\|_{W^{s}}:=\|\eta\|_{H^{s+\frac{3}{2}}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}+\|\beta\|_{H^{s+1}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})},\quad\ \|(f,g)\|_{R^{s}}:=\|f\|_{H^{s-\frac{1}{2}}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}+\|g\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})},

(6.12)

for all
(\eta,\beta)\in W^{s}
, all
(f,g)\in R^{s}
.
We denote by

\Pi_{R}\colon R\oplus Z\to R,\quad\ \Pi_{Z}\colon R\oplus Z\to Z

the projection maps onto
R
and
Z
,
and we also write
\Pi_{V}=\Pi_{Z}
,
\Pi_{W}=\Pi_{R}
.
To keep track of the invariance property under the group action
\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}
,
we make the following observations.
Recall that
T
is the set of indices defined in (
2.6
).

Lemma 6.2
.

For all
(\ell,m)\in T
with
m\geq 0
,
for all
\vartheta\in{\mathbb{T}}
, one has

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}\varphi_{\ell,m}

\displaystyle=\cos(m\vartheta)\varphi_{\ell,m}-\sin(m\vartheta)\varphi_{\ell,-m},

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}\varphi_{\ell,-m}

\displaystyle=\sin(m\vartheta)\varphi_{\ell,m}+\cos(m\vartheta)\varphi_{\ell,-m}.

(6.13)

As a consequence, for all
(\ell,m)\in T
,
the linear space generated by
\varphi_{\ell,m},\varphi_{\ell,-m}
is invariant for
\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}
.
Moreover,
V
and
W
are also invariant for
\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}
, namely

\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}(V)=V,\quad\ \mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}(W)=W,\quad\ \mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}\Pi_{V}=\Pi_{V}\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta},\quad\ \mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}\Pi_{W}=\Pi_{W}\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}.

(6.14)

Proof.

Let
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and
y:=R(\vartheta)x
, where
R(\vartheta)
is in (
1.13
).
Then the complex number
y_{1}+iy_{2}
is equal to the product
e^{i\vartheta}(x_{1}+ix_{2})
,
while
y_{3}=x_{3}
. Therefore, for any integer
m\geq 0
, one has

(y_{1}+iy_{2})^{m}=e^{im\vartheta}(x_{1}+ix_{2})^{m}

and, by (
2.7
) and (
4.2
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}\varphi_{\ell,m}(x)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{\ell,m}(R(\vartheta)x)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{\ell,m}(y)

\displaystyle=c_{\ell}^{(m)}P_{\ell}^{(m)}(y_{3})\mathrm{Re}\,[(y_{1}+iy_{2})^{m}]

\displaystyle=c_{\ell}^{(m)}P_{\ell}^{(m)}(x_{3})\mathrm{Re}\,[e^{im\vartheta}(x_{1}+ix_{2})^{m}]

\displaystyle=c_{\ell}^{(m)}P_{\ell}^{(m)}(x_{3})\{\cos(m\vartheta)\mathrm{Re}\,[(x_{1}+ix_{2})^{m}]-\sin(m\vartheta)\mathrm{Im}\,[(x_{1}+ix_{2})^{m}]\},

which is the first identity in (
6.13
).
The second identity in (
6.13
) is proved similarly.
By (
6.13
), for
(\ell,m)\in T
, with
m\geq 0
, one has

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}\begin{pmatrix}\ell\varphi_{\ell,m}\\
-\omega_{0}m\varphi_{\ell,-m}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\cos(m\vartheta)\begin{pmatrix}\ell\varphi_{\ell,m}\\
-\omega_{0}m\varphi_{\ell,-m}\end{pmatrix}-\sin(m\vartheta)\begin{pmatrix}\ell\varphi_{\ell,-m}\\
\omega_{0}m\varphi_{\ell,m}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}\begin{pmatrix}\ell\varphi_{\ell,-m}\\
\omega_{0}m\varphi_{\ell,m}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\cos(m\vartheta)\begin{pmatrix}\ell\varphi_{\ell,-m}\\
\omega_{0}m\varphi_{\ell,m}\end{pmatrix}+\sin(m\vartheta)\begin{pmatrix}\ell\varphi_{\ell,m}\\
-\omega_{0}m\varphi_{\ell,-m}\end{pmatrix}.

(6.15)

Hence, recalling the definition (
6.5
) of
V
,
we deduce that
\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}v\in V
for all
v\in V
.
This means that
\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}(V)\subseteq V
for all
\vartheta\in{\mathbb{T}}
.
Applying
\mathcal{T}_{-\vartheta}
to this inclusion, we get
V\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{-\vartheta}(V)

for all
\vartheta\in{\mathbb{T}}
, whence
V=\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}(V)
for all
\vartheta
.
Now let
w\in W
, and consider
\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}w
, which is the sum
\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}w=v_{1}+w_{1}

for some
v_{1}\in V
,
w_{1}\in W
. One has

\langle\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}w,v_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}=\langle w,\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}^{\,T}v_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}=\langle w,\mathcal{T}_{-\vartheta}v_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}=0

because
\mathcal{T}_{-\vartheta}v_{1}\in V
and
w\in W
.
On the other hand, one also has

\langle\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}w,v_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}=\langle v_{1}+w_{1},v_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}=|v_{1}|^{2},

whence
v_{1}=0
. Hence
\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}w=w_{1}\in W
.
This proves that
\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}(W)\subseteq W
for all
\vartheta\in{\mathbb{T}}
.
Applying
\mathcal{T}_{-\vartheta}
to this inclusion gives
W\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{-\vartheta}(W)

for all
\vartheta\in{\mathbb{T}}
, whence
\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}(W)=W
.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

Concerning the definitions (
5.1
) and (
5.5
),
one could wonder why introducing
\mathcal{F}
should be more convenient
than just keeping
\mathcal{F}_{0}
, which is already studied in
[
9
]
.
The reason is that, with
\mathcal{F}_{0}
, the matrix
(L_{0})_{(\ell,m)}
is a Jordan block for
(\ell,m)=(0,0)

and
(1,0)
, while using
\mathcal{F}
the corresponding matrices are diagonal, with one zero eigenvalue.
Jordan blocks create troubles proving that the bifurcation equation
is still a variational problem, but this difficulty is
easy to overcome, as it can be removed by

J_{0}
in (
5.5
).
This problem is not visible in
[
9
]
,
both because there the variational structure is not explicitly used,
and because the frequencies
(0,0)
and
(1,0)
are removed by considering
invariant subspaces of even/odd functions.
On the contrary, Theorem
1.1
does not rely on any symmetry assumption for the unknown functions.
∎

7
Lyapunov-Schmidt decomposition and solution of the range equation

We perform the Lyapunov-Schmidt decomposition.
Decomposing any
u\in H^{s+\frac{3}{2}}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})\times H^{s+1}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})

as the sum
u=v+w
where
v\in V
and
w\in W^{s}
,
the equation
\mathcal{F}(\omega,u)=0
can be reformulated as

\displaystyle\Pi_{R^{s}}\mathcal{F}(\omega,v+w)=0,

(7.1)

\displaystyle\Pi_{Z}\mathcal{F}(\omega,v+w)=0.

(7.2)

Here (
7.1
) is the range equation,
while (
7.2
) is the bifurcation equation.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, recalling the notation in (
6.11
), denote

\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}:=\{(\omega,v)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times V:|\omega-\omega_{0}|<\varepsilon,\ |v|<\varepsilon\}.

(7.3)

In the next lemma we observe that the range equation can be solved
by the implicit function theorem.

Lemma 7.1

(Solution of the range equation)
.

There exist
\varepsilon_{0}>0
,
C>0
, and an analytic function
\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon_{0}}\to W^{s}
,

(\omega,v)\mapsto w(\omega,v)
such that

\Pi_{R^{s}}\mathcal{F}(\omega,v+w(\omega,v))=0

(7.4)

for all
(\omega,v)\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon_{0}}
, and the only solution
(\omega,v,w)

of the range equation (
7.1
)
with
(\omega,v)\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon_{0}}
and
\|w\|_{W^{s}}<C\varepsilon_{0}^{2}

is the triplet
(\omega,v,w(\omega,v))
.
Moreover, for all
(\omega,v)\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon_{0}}
, all
\vartheta\in{\mathbb{T}}
, one has

\displaystyle w(\omega,0)

\displaystyle=0\quad\forall|\omega-\omega_{0}|<\varepsilon_{0},\qquad\partial_{v}w(\omega_{0},0)=0,

(7.5)

\displaystyle\|w(\omega,v)\|_{W^{s}}

\displaystyle\leq C(|v|^{2}+|v||\omega-\omega_{0}|),

(7.6)

\displaystyle w(\omega,\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}v)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}w(\omega,v).

(7.7)

Proof.

The operator in the left-hand side of (
7.1
) is analytic in
\omega,v,w

(see Lemma
5.1
), and its linearization with respect to
w

at the point
(\omega,v,w)=(\omega_{0},0,0)
is the operator
L_{\omega_{0}}
,
which is a linear homeomorphism of
W^{s}
onto
R^{s}
.
Hence the existence and uniqueness in the statement comes from the implicit function theorem
for analytic operators in Banach spaces.
The first identity in (
7.5
) holds by the uniqueness of the solution
and because
\mathcal{F}(\omega,0)=0
for all
\omega
.
The derivative of identity (
7.4
) with respect to
v

in any direction
\tilde{v}\in V
at
(\omega,v)=(\omega_{0},0)
gives

0=\Pi_{R^{s}}(\partial_{u}\mathcal{F})(\omega_{0},0)\big[\tilde{v}+\partial_{v}w(\omega_{0},0)[\tilde{v}]\big]=\Pi_{R^{s}}L_{\omega_{0}}|_{W^{s}}\big[\partial_{v}w(\omega_{0},0)[\tilde{v}]\big],

which implies the second identity in (
7.5
).
Then (
7.6
) follows by (
7.5
) and the Taylor expansion of
w(\omega,v)

around
(\omega_{0},0)
.
Applying
\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}
to identity (
7.4
),
and using the third identity in (
6.14
)
and the last one in (
5.11
),
one has

0=\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}\Pi_{R^{s}}\mathcal{F}(\omega,v+w(\omega,v))=\Pi_{R^{s}}\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}\mathcal{F}(\omega,v+w(\omega,v))=\Pi_{R^{s}}\mathcal{F}(\omega,\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}v+\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}w(\omega,v)).

On the other hand, (
7.4
) applied with
\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}v
in place of
v
gives

\Pi_{R^{s}}\mathcal{F}(\omega,\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}v+w(\omega,\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}v))=0.

Thus (
7.7
) follows from the uniqueness property of the implicit function.
∎

8
Degenerate and non-degenerate decomposition

For notation convenience, recalling (
6.5
), we define

\mathtt{v}_{0,0}:=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\varphi_{0,0}\end{pmatrix},\quad\ \mathtt{v}_{\ell,m}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell^{2}+\omega_{0}^{2}m^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}\ell\varphi_{\ell,m}\\
-\omega_{0}m\varphi_{\ell,-m}\end{pmatrix},\quad\ (\ell,m)\in S,\ \ell\geq 1.

(8.1)

Since each
\varphi_{\ell,m}
has unitary

norm, one has

|\mathtt{v}_{\ell,m}|=1\quad\forall(\ell,m)\in S,

(8.2)

where notation (
6.11
) is used.
Thus the vectors in (
8.1
) form an orthonormal basis of
V

with respect to the

We split the set
S
defined in (

)
into its “degenerate” and “non-degenerate” part,

S_{D}:=\{(0,0),(1,0)\},\quad\ S_{N}:=\{(\ell,m)\in S:\ell>1\},

and we decompose

Thus,
S=S_{D}\cup S_{N}
and

.
Since
Z=V
, we also define
Z_{D}:=V_{D}
,
Z_{N}:=V_{N}
.
From Lemma
6.2
it follows that

V_{D}
and
V_{N}
are invariant spaces for
\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}
; more precisely, one has

\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}|_{V_{D}}=\mathrm{id},\quad\ \mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}(V_{N})=V_{N}.

(8.5)

By (
6.15
) and (
8.1
),
one has

\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}\mathtt{v}_{\ell,m}=\cos(m\vartheta)\mathtt{v}_{\ell,m}-\sin(m\vartheta)\mathtt{v}_{\ell,-m}

(8.6)

for all
(\ell,m)\in S_{N}
; note that (
8.6
)
holds not only for
m
positive, but also for
m
negative.
By (

),
for any
v=\sum_{(\ell,m)\in S_{N}}\hat{v}_{\ell,m}\mathtt{v}_{\ell,m}\in V_{N}
one has

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}v

\displaystyle=\sum_{(\ell,m)\in S_{N}}\hat{v}_{\ell,m}\cos(m\vartheta)\mathtt{v}_{\ell,m}-\sum_{(\ell,m)\in S_{N}}\hat{v}_{\ell,m}\sin(m\vartheta)\mathtt{v}_{\ell,-m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(\ell,m)\in S_{N}}\hat{v}_{\ell,m}\cos(m\vartheta)\mathtt{v}_{\ell,m}-\sum_{(\ell,m)\in S_{N}}\hat{v}_{\ell,-m}\sin(-m\vartheta)\mathtt{v}_{\ell,m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(\ell,m)\in S_{N}}\{\cos(m\vartheta)\hat{v}_{\ell,m}+\sin(m\vartheta)\hat{v}_{\ell,-m}\}\mathtt{v}_{\ell,m},

where we have made a change of summation variable in the second sum.
In other words, if
S_{N}=\{(\ell_{1},m_{1}),(\ell_{1},-m_{1}),\ldots,(\ell_{n},m_{n}),(\ell_{n},-m_{n})\}
,
and
\hat{v}=(\hat{v}_{\ell_{1},m_{1}},\ldots,\hat{v}_{\ell_{n},-m_{n}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}

is the vector of the coefficients of
v
with respect to the basis

\{\mathtt{v}_{\ell_{1},m_{1}},\ldots,\mathtt{v}_{\ell_{n},-m_{n}}\}
of

,

\mathcal{T}_{\vartheta}:V_{N}\to V_{N}

is represented by the block-diagonal matrix
M=\mathrm{diag}(M_{1},\ldots,M_{n})
, where
the
j
-th block is

M_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(m_{j}\vartheta)&\sin(m_{j}\vartheta)\\
-\sin(m_{j}\vartheta)&\cos(m_{j}\vartheta)\end{pmatrix},\quad\ j=1,\ldots,n.

\omega(v)

.

The functions

.

Lemma 9.2
.

One has

\displaystyle f(\omega,0)

\displaystyle=0,\quad\partial_{v}f(\omega_{0},0)=0,\quad\partial_{\omega v}f(\omega_{0},0)=-\Pi_{Z_{N}}J_{0}\mathcal{M},

\displaystyle F(\omega,0)=0,\quad\ \partial_{v}F(\omega,0)=0,\quad\ \partial_{vv}F(\omega_{0},0)=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{\omega vv}F(\omega_{0},0)[\tilde{v},\tilde{v}]=-|\Pi_{Z_{N}}J_{0}\mathcal{M}\tilde{v}|^{2}\quad\forall\tilde{v}\in V.

The second identity in (

\omega

\partial_{v}f(\omega,0)[\tilde{v}]=\Pi_{Z_{N}}\partial_{u}\mathcal{F}(\omega,0)[\tilde{v}+\partial_{v}w(\omega,0)[\tilde{v}]].

,

.
From (

Remark 9.5
.

In general, even if the function

in the proof of Lemma

,
the function

is not analytic around

For example, consider the variable

,
