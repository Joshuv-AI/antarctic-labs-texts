The Keychain Problem: On Minimizing the Opportunity Cost of Uncertainty
Ramiro N. Deo-Campo Vuong, Robert Kleinberg, Aditya Prasad, Eric Xiao, Haifeng Xu

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce a family of sequential decision-making problems, collectively called the Keychain Problem , that involve exploring a set of actions to maximize expected payoff when only a subset of actions are available in each stage. In an instance of the Keychain Problem, a locksmith faces a sequence of choices, each of which involves selecting one key from a specified subset (a keychain) to attempt to open a lock. Given a Bayesian prior on the effectiveness of keys, the locksmith’s goal is to maximize the expected number of rounds in which the lock is opened – or equivalently, minimize the opportunity cost which is the expected number of rounds in which the chain has a correct key but our selected key is incorrect. We investigate Keychain Problems under three assumptions on the order in which keychains are tested by the locksmith: a fixed, known order; a random order sampled from a known distribution on a set of “scenarios”; or an order selected by the locksmith themself. We present an exact algorithm for the simplest of these settings, and we present approximation algorithms and hardness results for the others. In the Probabilistic Scenarios setting, our approximation algorithm is based on a novel connection between combinatorial auctions and policy design for sequential decision-making problems. To illustrate the generality of this technique, we apply the same ideas to obtain Philosopher Inequalities for Online Bipartite Matching and some of its extensions.

Introduction

Designing experimentation policies to
optimally explore a space of alternatives
has been a fruitful theme for algorithms research,
encompassing influential models such as multi-armed bandits

[
24
,
36
,
52
]
and Pandora’s problem
[
34
,
51
,
53
]
. An aspect of optimal
exploration problems that has been under-examined in prior
work is
limited availability
: the set of alternatives
available for exploration may vary over the course of the
exploration process. This is captured by the

sleeping bandits
problem
[
11
,
32
]
in prior-independent settings where the goal is to
minimize regret. In Bayesian settings where the goal is to
maximize expected reward under a given prior distribution,
we are not aware of any prior work addressing the
design and analysis of optimal or approximately optimal
experimentation policies under limited availability.
This paper seeks to fill that gap.

We introduce and study a problem of sequential decision-making
under uncertainty that we call the
Keychain Problem
.
In this setting, there is a locksmith
tasked with opening a lock.
At her disposal is a sequence of keychains.
In an online fashion, the locksmith irrevocably selects
at most one key
from the next keychain in the sequence. She observes if the selected
key opens the lock, and receives a reward if it does.
The goal of the locksmith is to quickly identify correct
keys so she can recover rewards on subsequent keychains.
We measure the performance of the locksmith against the
performance of a Bayes optimal policy.

There are essentially two reasons why optimal experimentation
in the face of limited availability is difficult. First, since
the set of actions available varies over time, one must seize
opportunities to reduce uncertainty about the identity of the
best action in light of the choices one will face in the
future. (See the concrete example in

Section

1.1
.)
Second, since the best action may not be available
at all times, one must also work to identify
high-performing alternatives for the
times when it is unavailable. The Keychain Problem
is designed to distill the first of these challenges
while rendering the second one moot by equalizing the values
of all suboptimal actions.

Similar to Pandora’s Problem
[
53
]
,
the Keychain Problem admits a basic version whose
optimal solution is algorithmically
tractable (see
Section

2.1
)
and a wealth of versions that are generally
computationally hard. Our paper is mainly devoted to
exploring the rich landscape of approximation algorithms
and hardness results for the Keychain Problem
under varying modeling assumptions.
This exploration is far from exhaustive,
but we hope it constitutes an enticing invitation
for researchers to study the problem further.
We conclude the paper by listing
what we perceive to be some of the most appealing
directions for future investigation.

1.1
Example: Advisor Search

Consider the problem of finding an advisor as a first-year graduate student.
Lily (locksmith) is a new theoretical computer science student at her dream university.
There are three rotational periods (keychains) in her first year.
For each rotation, she adaptively selects a single professor to collaborate with.
After working with a professor during a rotation, she learns if the professor is a good advising fit and may continue to work with the same professor for future rotations.
To remain in good standing with the university, Lily must identify an advisor and partake in successful collaborations during this rotational period.
There are three professors (keys) Lily is interested in working with: Alice, Bob, and Carol.
The professors have very different advising styles and interests, so one and only one professor will make a good advisor for Lily.
Based on the professors’ previous work and accounts from other students, Lily estimates that Alice, Bob, and Carol will be a good advisor fit with probabilities of
3/7
,
2/7

2/7

Bob and Carol are available for every rotation.
In contrast, Alice is considering a short sabbatical period and will not be available to collaborate during the second rotation with probability
2/3

Notably, Lily will not learn of Alice’s realized availability for the second rotation before choosing who to work with in the first rotation.
If Lily’s goal is to maximize her expected number of rotations with the professor who would make a good advisor, whom should she choose to work with at each rotational period?

Let us consider two polices.
The first greedy policy selects the professor that yields the largest number of expected successful future rotations, conditioned on past observations.
The second policy strikes a balance between greedy selection and exploring alternatives to reduce uncertainty in future rotational periods.
For the sake of brevity, we leave explicit calculations for this example in Appendix
A
.

The Greedy policy first collaborates with Alice because she yields the greatest number of successful future rotations in expectation.
In expectation, choosing to collaborate with Alice first yields
1
successful future rotation, whereas collaboration with Bob or Carol first only offers
6/7
successful future rotations.
If collaboration with Alice is successful during the first rotation, then Lily should continue to collaborate with Alice on subsequent rotations.
Otherwise, she should choose either Bob or Carol on the second rotation, and the other one on the third rotation, if the second-rotation collaboration is unsuccessful.
Lily can expect to have
13/7
successful rotations by greedily selecting collaborators.

It turns out the greedy policy is not optimal in Lily’s situation.
Counterintuitively, Lily should collaborate with Bob (or Carol by symmetry) for her first rotation.
The interesting event is when Bob is not the right advisor for Lily, and Alice goes on sabbatical. By ruling out Bob as the correct advisor on the first rotation, Lily can rotate with Carol next to learn if she is the correct advisor at no opportunity cost (since Carol is the only available advisor on the second rotation who could be a good fit).
This reduction in opportunity cost turns out to outweigh the benefit of greedily collaborating with Alice first.

We now analyze this optimal policy, which adapts to Alice’s availability on the second rotation.
Conditioned on Alice taking a sabbatical period, Lily will first choose to collaborate with Bob, and Carol second if Bob is not the right advisor.
If neither Bob nor Carol is are good advising fit, then Lily will choose to collaborate with Alice for the third rotation.
Lily can expect
13/7
successful rotations conditioned on Alice going on sabbatical.
Now we condition on the event that Alice is available for the second rotation.
In this event, Lily again chooses to collaborate with Bob for the first rotation.
On the second rotation, she should choose to work with Alice if Bob is not a good advising fit.
Finally, if neither Alice nor Bob is a good advising fit, she should collaborate with Carol for the last rotation.
Lily can expect
2
successful rotations conditioned on Alice being available.
Finally, by the law of total expectation, Lily’s expected number of successful rotations with her second policy is
40/21
, which exceeds the
13/7
expected successful rotations yielded by the greedy policy.

1.2
Settings, Techniques, and Results

We consider three different variants of the Keychain Problem that differ in how the sequence of keychains is generated.

1.2.1
Fixed, Known Order

As a warm-up and to establish a useful baseline for subsequent analyses, we present an
easy result: when the order of keychains is fixed and known to the locksmith,
if there is only one correct key then the Bayes optimal key-testing policy is easy to compute.

Proposition

1
(Informal):
Given any Keychain Problem instance with known keychain order and one correct key, there is a polynomial-time algorithm that computes a Bayes optimal solution.

The optimal policy for this special case of the problem, which we analyze in
Section

2.1
,
works by reducing the problem to maximum-weight bipartite matching on the graph of keys and keychains.
The weight of an edge
(i,j)
is the expected value to be gained by testing key
i
on keychain
j
,
given that
i
will be used on all subsequent chains to which it belongs, if the test is successful.
Policies with this property are called
exploitative
because they reuse keys identified to be correct.
In the case of one correct key, it is not difficult to see that optimal policies
can without loss of generality be exploitative (see
1
).

However, the same Keychain Problem
becomes much harder when there can be multiple correct keys, still with a fixed, known order of keychains. First of all,
counterintuitively, the observation that optimal policies are exploitative
no longer holds; we give a counterexample in
1
of
Section

2.2
.
Worse yet, the problem of designing an optimal key-testing policy becomes computationally
hard.

Proposition

2
(Informal):
For the Keychain Problem with known keychain order
and many correct keys, it is APX-hard to compute a Bayes optimal policy, even in the special case when the keys are partitioned into disjoint two-element sets and the set of correct keys
is obtained by sampling one element uniformly at random from each of these pairs.

The proof proceeds by reducing from Vertex Cover, using two keys for each node in the Vertex Cover
instance.
In each of these pairs, there is exactly one correct key, sampled uniformly at random, independently across key-pairs.
Corresponding to each edge that must be covered, there is a keychain composed of the two key-pairs associated with the nodes at the endpoints of the edge. The analysis of the reduction is based on the
observation that for each key-pair, the first time an element of that key-pair is
tested the probability of failing to open the lock is
\frac{1}{2}
.
However, as soon as either element of the key-pair has been tested at least once, the locksmith knows which key of the pair is correct and will never make another mistake using that
pair.
Hence, designing an optimal key-testing policy is equivalent to minimizing the number of key-pairs that are used at least once, which is equivalent to selecting a minimum set cover.

We leave open the question whether the Keychain Problem with multiple correct keys can be solved exactly when the algorithm’s running time is allowed to depend polynomially on the support size of the distribution of correct key sets.

(Note that the distribution constructed in our reduction has exponential
support size, despite having a very succinct description.)

For the remaining results in this paper, we focus exclusively
on the case when there is one correct key.

Random Order

Section

1.1
is an instance of the
Probabilistic Scenarios
setting.
In section
3
, we describe this variant of the Keychain Problem, in which the correct key and the keychain sequence are drawn from a known prior.
Each realization of the environment is called a
scenario

We provide a
(1-1/e)
-approximation

algorithm.
We complement our algorithms with a hardness-of-approximation result.
Notably, the techniques we use in the Probabilistic Scenarios problem apply to the setting of online weighted bipartite matching (and one-sided

Theorem
1
and Proposition
3
(Informal):

There is a
(1-1/e)
-approximation algorithm for the Probabilistic Scenarios setting of the Keychain Problem that runs in polynomial time in the number of keys, maximum keychain sequence length, and the number of scenarios.
The algorithm requires an explicit description of the prior or sample access to the prior; sampling yields a high probability guarantee with small additive error.

These results constitute the most innovative technical contribution of the paper
. Below we provide an overview of our proof sketch, though the interested reader may skip directly to
Section

3
for the details of how we obtain them.

Our
(1-1/e)
-approximations are proved through a series of polynomial-time and approximation-preserving reductions in Lemmas
1
and
2
.
A self-contained algorithm that bypasses these reductions is available in Appendix
D
.
We first simplify our representation of policies by observing that deterministic and
exploitative
polices are optimal;
exploitative policies always select the correct key once it has been identified.
These observations reduce the Probabilistic Scenarios setting to determining when to test a key for the first time, as the expected reward of exploiting a correctly tested key can be precomputed with an explicit prior or estimated via sampling.
We represent deterministic and exploitative policies as a map from prefixes of keychain sequences, called
information sets
, to a key to test for the first time.
Admissible policies map to a key at most once on the information sets associated with a scenario.

The first reduction is to Maximum Weight Laminar Matching (
MWLM
), a generalization of Maximum Weight Bipartite Matching.

MWLM
is defined on a weighted bipartite graph, in which each right node has an associated set of types that collectively form a laminar family.
The goal is to compute a maximum weight edge set for which every left node has at most one neighbor of each type, and every right node has at most one neighbor.
In our reduction, keys and information sets are modeled as left and right nodes, respectively.
An edge from a left node (key) to a right node (information set) in the laminar matching corresponds to a policy selecting said key at that information set.
By assigning the type set of a right node (information set) to be the collection of scenarios in which the information set will be observed, we enforce the constraint that a key is selected at most once on the information sets presented in a scenario.
Notably, when each scenario presents the same information set, the laminar matching constraint coincides with the standard matching constraint.
In this special case, the Probabilistic Scenarios setting reduces to Maximum Weight Bipartite Matching.

The second reduction is from
MWLM
to Combinatorial Auctions with XOS valuations.
In the Combinatorial Auction problem
[
17
,
18
]
, an auctioneer is tasked with allocating items to bidders.
Each bidder has an associated XOS valuation function (see
Definition

9
)
encoding their utility for every bundle of items they may receive.
The goal of the auctioneer is to compute an allocation of items to bidders that maximizes social welfare.
In our reduction from
MWLM
to XOS combinatorial auctions, each left node is represented by a bidder, and each right node is an item.
The valuation of a bidder (left node) for a bundle of items (set of right nodes) is the maximum weight subset of those items with disjoint type sets.
We call these valuations
antichain set functions
, as they correspond to finding a maximum weight antichain on the forest induced by the laminar type sets.
An item (right node) allocated to a bidder (left node) with a positive supporting price encodes an edge between the respective left and right node.
Further, allocations surjectively map to laminar matchings, and the social welfare of an allocation equals the weight of the corresponding matching.
We show that antichain functions are XOS, monotone, and normalized.
In addition, they admit efficient value, demand, and supporting price oracles via dynamic programming, which allows us to employ the
(1-1/e)
-approximation presented in
[
18
]
.

On the negative side, we present a result on the hardness of approximation.

Theorem
2
(Informal):

No polynomial-time algorithm guarantees a
\left(\frac{4063}{4064}+\epsilon\right)
-approximation for the Probabilistic Scenarios setting of the Keychain Problem for any
\epsilon>0
, assuming
P\neq NP
.

To prove the approximation hardness of the Probabilistic Scenarios setting, we reduce from a hard-to-approximate special case of 3-SAT
[
7
]
.
In this reduction, we instantiate a key for each literal and scenarios for each clause.
Each of the first keychains each contain a pair of keys (literals) associated with a variable.
By selecting keys on these keychains, the locksmith commits to an assignment of variables for the 3-SAT instance.
The final keychain contains the keys (literals) in a clause drawn uniformly at random from the 3-SAT instance.
If the locksmith previously played keys corresponding to a variable assignment satisfying the clause, she may select an untested key to probabilistically receive a reward.
If her assignment does not satisfy the clause, then she cannot test a new key and will receive no reward.
Thus, an algorithm approximating the Probabilistic Scenarios setting will also approximate the special case of 3-SAT.

Application: Online Bipartite Matching
.
We apply our techniques to the problem of Weighted Online Bipartite Matching to derive Philosopher Inequality guarantees when there are few realizable edge sets.
The series of
MWLM
and XOS combinatorial auction reductions similarly give rise to
a
(1-1/e)
-approximation algorithm (Proposition
4
).
Our hardness reduction also applies to derive a
(4063/4064)
-approximation hardness result (Proposition
5
).
Finally, in Appendix
F
, we show that our techniques can be generalized to prove approximations under one-sided
b
-matching constraints.

Our results leave open the intriguing question of how to provide approximation guarantees for instances with exponentially many scenarios.
We believe access to a
future scenario sampling
oracle may facilitate the design of a
(1-1/e)
-approximation algorithm.
A future scenario sampling oracle takes as input an information set
o
and outputs a randomly sampled scenario from the prior conditioned on the occurrence of
o
(i.e., it samples a realization of the future consistent with past observations).

Open Question (Section
5.1
):
Given access to a future scenario sampling oracle, is there a polynomial time
(1-1/e)
-approximation algorithm even for instances with exponentially many scenarios?

1.2.3
Keychain Order Selection

Section
4
provides our analysis of the Keychain Order Selection setting.
In this variant of the Keychain problem, a single correct key is drawn from a known prior.
A set of keychains is available to the locksmith, which she may enumerate in an adaptively chosen order.
We provide a
(1/2)
-approximation as well as a hardness result for the order selection problem.

Proposition
6
(Informal):
There is a polynomial time
(1/2)
-approximation for the Keychain Order Selection setting.

Like the Probabilistic Scenarios problem, we observe that exploitative and deterministic policies are optimal in the Keychain Order Selection setting.
We represent such policies as two assignments or matchings.
The first matching decides the ordering of the keychains by assigning keychains to rounds.
The second matching assigns a key to select on each keychain.
The expected reward of such a policy can be precomputed using an explicit description of the prior on correct keys.

Our
(1/2)
-approximation algorithm is the best of two different solutions.
The algorithm considers an arbitrary ordering of keychains and the reverse ordering.
Then, on each fixed ordering of the keychains, it computes the optimal assignment of keys to keychains using Theorem
1
; the better of the two solutions is returned.
Our proof of correctness hinges on the observation that for an optimal assignment of keys to keychains, one of the orderings provides at least half of the expected reward of a Bayes optimal solution.
A similar observation is made in the
(1/2)
-approximation algorithm for Maximum Acyclic Subgraph
[
26
]
.
Thus, by considering the best assignment of keys to keychains on both orderings, we guarantee a
(1/2)
-approximation.

Theorem
3
(Informal):
There is no polynomial time algorithm that computes a Bayes optimal solution for the Keychain Order Selection Problem, assuming
P\not=NP
.

We reduce from the following
NP
-complete problem
[
21
,
23
]
: given a square binary matrix
M
, determine if there are independent row and column permutations that result in an upper triangular matrix.
A matrix is upper triangular when all
1
entries occur on or above the main diagonal.
If this is true, we say that
M
is permute-equivalent to an upper triangular matrix.
It is not too difficult to see that this problem is inter-reducible to the task of determining if a binary matrix
A
is permute equivalent to a matrix with only
1
s in the upper triangle (along or above the main diagonal).

In our reduction, we instantiate a keychain for each column and a key for each row; a key is in a keychain when there is a
1
in the corresponding entry of the matrix.
The prior is set to be the uniform distribution over keys.
We interpret the ordering of keychains as the ordering of columns.
The
i
th row is set to be the row counterpart of the key selected on the
i
th keychain.
In this reduction, the matrix permutation has a positive answer if and only if there is a policy that tests a new key on each keychain, and tested keys appear on every subsequent keychain.

En route to our results, we uncover an intimate connection between the Keychain Order Selection setting and quadratic assignment problems
[
3
]
, such as the Maximum Acyclic Subgraph problem.
These connections suggest several open questions.
First, as Maximum Acyclic Subgraph is hard to approximate by a factor greater than
1/2
assuming the Unique Games Conjecture
[
26
]
, we ask if there is a similar hardness result for the Keychain Order Selection problem:

Open Question (Section
5.2
):
Is it hard to approximate the Keychain Order Selection setting by a factor greater than
1/2
?

Since
[
3
]
presents a PTAS for dense instances of quadratic assignment problems, we believe such a PTAS may also exist for the Keychain Order Selection setting.
Unfortunately, it is not clear that we can directly apply the results of
[
3
]
due to the constraint that keys may only be assigned to specific keychains.

Open Question (Section
5.2
):
Is there a PTAS for dense instances of the Keychain Order Selection problem?

1.3
Applications

We foresee several useful applications of the Keychain Problem in addition to advisor search.
One such application is planning the deployment of camouflage technology.
Consider the planning of monthly shipments of critical cargo across contested waters that must avoid being detected by adversarial parties.
Each shipment vessel (keychain) has a set of compatible camouflage technologies (keys) that it may deploy.
Initially, the capabilities of these camouflage technologies are unknown.
To avoid detection by adversarial parties (open a lock), the shipment planner would like to select a camouflage technology (key) to equip each vessel.
A good policy will use feedback about the effectiveness of previously deployed camouflage to quickly identify a technology that avoids detection.

A more dubious application is the goal of password cracking.
There is a hacker who wants access to Bob’s online accounts, and unfortunately, like many of us, Bob uses the same password for all of his accounts.
Suppose the hacker obtains a database of passwords for compromised websites, but they do not know the mapping of passwords to user accounts.
Bob only has an account on a subset of the compromised websites (keychains) in the database, and the hacker may choose one password
(key) to test on a website before being locked out.
The goal of the hacker is to identify Bob’s password while getting banned from the fewest number of websites.

1.4
Related Work

The theme of exploring a space of actions subject to constraints
on the available actions in each round connects our work to the

Sleeping Bandits
problem
[
11
,
32
]
.
While sharing similar motivation, the two problem formulations
are very different: in the Keychain Problem one has a prior
distribution on the identity of the correct key, and once a
key is tested it is known to be correct (in which case it always
opens the lock) or incorrect (in which case it never does).
In the sleeping bandits problem there is no prior distribution
and the reward obtained from using an arm may vary according to
the round in which it is used. Accordingly, the two problems are
solved using very different techniques, and different modes of
analysis are used to evaluate algorithms: Keychain algorithms are
evaluated according to their approximation ratio, Sleeping Bandit
algorithms are evaluated according to their regret.

In stochastic search and optimization it is rare for the
Bayes optimal procedure to be computationally efficient.
One of the earliest examples of an efficiently solvable stochastic
search problem is Pandora’s Problem
[
53
]
, formulated by Martin
Weitzman in 1979. In this problem there are
n
boxes containing
independent random rewards sampled from known distributions.
Each box also has a known cost that must be paid to open it.
Upon opening a box the searcher learns the value of the reward
inside. A search policy must decide, at every stage, whether
to stop and claim the largest prize uncovered so far or to
open one more box and, if so, which box to open. Weitzman
showed that a remarkably simple “index policy” is Bayes optimal.
Subsequent work in theoretical computer science
built upon Weitzman’s ideas to obtain approximately
optimal policies for combinatorial generalizations
of Pandora’s Problem in which more than one prize may
be claimed
[
51
]
, decision-theoretic
generalizations in which there is more than one way
to open a box
[
8
,
9
,
12
,
15
,
22
,
25
,
49
]
,
and strategic generalizations in which boxes are under
the control of selfish agents
[
31
,
33
]
.
Our Keychain Order Selection problem bears a resemblance to
Pandora’s Problem in that the searcher has freedom, in both
models, to decide on the order in which to explore
alternatives. However, in Keychain Order Selection this
freedom is not absolute; it is limited by the structure
of the keychains, which inhibits the application of
techniques based on Weitzman indices and their generalizations.

1.5.2
Approximation Guarantees

In many of our settings, it is intractable to compute a Bayes optimal solution.
In these cases, we consider multiplicative and mixed multiplicative-additive approximations.
An algorithm is said to be an

\displaystyle\textsc{ALG}\geq\alpha\cdot\textsc{OPT}.

\displaystyle\textsc{ALG}\geq\alpha\cdot\textsc{OPT}-\epsilon.

The Keychain Problem with Known Keychain Order

Let us start by formally defining the
Keychain Problem
.
A
locksmith
needs to find the correct keys to open a lock.
There is a set of
keys

[n]
.
The locksmith is presented with a sequence of
keychains

C_{1},\dots,C_{m}
, each being a subset of
[n]
.
There could be one or multiple
correct keys
(i.e., lock-opening keys) from
[n]
to open the lock.
The locksmith does not know which keys are correct, but possesses some prior knowledge that is modeled as a prior distribution
p
on the probability of keys’ correctness (possibly jointly with other setting scenarios).
The tuple
\langle n,\{C_{t}\}_{t=1}^{m},p\rangle
hence consists of the input to a Keychain Problem.

Except for Subsection
2.2
, all other parts of this paper will focus on the situation with only a single correct key.
In this case, the locksmith’s prior knowledge encodes a distribution
p=(p_{1},\cdots,p_{n})\in\Delta([n])
in which
p_{i}
is the probability that key
i
is the correct key; let
k^{*}
denote the correct key, drawn from the distribution
p
.
In the Probabilistic Scenarios setting that we consider in
Section

3
, the locksmith’s
prior knowledge also incorporates a distribution over the sequence of keychains, and this may be correlated
with the identity of the correct key. By abuse of notation, we will continue using
p
in that section to
denote all of the locksmith’s prior knowledge, i.e. a joint distribution over (correct key, keychain sequence) pairs.
Given her prior knowledge, the locksmith must design a
policy

\pi
that irrevocably selects a key
k_{t}\in C_{t}\cup\{\perp\}
at time step
t=1,\dots,m
.
She then observes whether or not
k_{t}=k^{*}
and receives a reward
\mathbf{1}\left\{k^{*}=k_{t}\right\}
, which will affect how the policy
\pi
selects the next keys.
In the applications we envision, a keychain captures an opportunity with multiple options (i.e., the keys); hence, the keychain will be discarded once one of its keys is tested.
For mathematical convenience, the locksmith is allowed to discard the keychain without trying any key as well, i.e., setting
k_{t}=\perp
.
The goal of the locksmith is to maximize her expected reward:

\displaystyle r(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{p,\pi}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}\left\{k_{t}=k^{*}\right\}\right]

where the expectation is taken with respect to the randomness of the environment described by prior distribution
p
and any randomness of the locksmith’s policy
\pi
.

We make two useful observations that will simplify our search for the optimal policy
\pi
that solves the above problem. These observations are intuitive, hence we defer the detailed arguments to Appendix
B
for ease of presentation.
Notably, these observations apply to the general Keychain Problem studied in the remainder of this section (excluding the case of multiple correct keys,
Section

2.2
), and in Sections
3
,
4
.

The first observation is that there is a deterministic and Bayes optimal policy that always tries to play the correct key once it is identified. We call such policies
exploitative
.
The property that optimal policies are exploitative is the result of a simple exchange argument.

Definition 1

(Exploitative Policies under One Correct Key)
.

In situations with only one correct key
k^{*}
, we say a policy is exploitative if
k_{t}=k^{*}
implies that the policy plays
k_{\tau}=k^{*}
whenever
k^{*}\in C_{\tau}
for all rounds
\tau\geq t
.

The keychain sequence decision process is a finite time horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP) with exponentially many states.
The states of the game are keys and keychains tested in the past, and the correct key if it has been identified.
The transition probabilities depend on the locksmith’s action of selecting which key to test, the keychain on the current round, and her observations.
2
2

2

In the keychain order selection regime, transition probabilities additionally depend on the keychain played by the locksmith.

Since there is always a deterministic and optimal solution for MDPs
[
45
]
, there must always be a Bayes optimal policy that is deterministic. For the remainder of this paper, we will restrict our analysis to such deterministic policies.

Observation 1

(Exploitation and Determinism)
.

In instances of the Keychain Problem with One Correct Key, there is a Bayes optimal policy that is deterministic and exploitative.

Observation
1
is quite intuitive.
What is surprising, however, is that this same insight ceases to be true when there are multiple correct keys – we present such a counterexample in
Section

2.2
, which also hints at the non-triviality of settings with many correct keys.

As it turns out, a crucial factor that governs the intricacy of the problem is whether the locksmith knows the order of the keychains or not.
In the remainder of this section, we focus on the case where the chain order is fixed and known.
We shall see that the number of correct keys can determine the problem’s tractability in this case.

An Algorithm for Computing the Bayes Optimal Policy.

To gain some intuition about the Keychain Problem, we now illustrate how the Bayes optimal solution can be found via a reduction to
Maximum Weight Bipartite Matching

Notice that the injective function
\pi

By setting the weights of this graph to be the expected future reward of testing keys, we produce a bipartite graph in which the weight of any matching is equal to the expected value of the corresponding policy.

Proposition 1
.

Given any Keychain Problem instance
\langle n,\{C_{t}\}_{t=1}^{m},p\rangle
with known keychain order and one correct key, there is a polynomial-time algorithm that computes a Bayes optimal solution.

Proof (via a reduction to Maximum Weight Bipartite Matching). .

From an instance of the fixed keychain sequence setting, we compute an instance of Maximum Weight Bipartite Matching as follows.
There is a left node for each key and a right node for each chain.
The bipartite graph is complete, and the edge weight from left node (key)

\displaystyle w(M)=\sum_{(k,t)\in M}w_{k,t}=\sum_{k,t}r_{k,t}\mathbf{1}\left\{\pi(t)=k\right\}=r(\pi)

2.2
Known Keychain Order and Many Correct Keys: APX-hardness

We now investigate the case with many correct keys that opens the lock.
This turns out to be a much more challenging situation; not only is computing a Bayes optimal policy APX-hard, but the natural exploitation property of Observation
1
also ceases to hold.

The setting with many correct keys is the same as described at the beginning of this section, except that now there is a set
K^{*}\subseteq[n]
of correct keys and the locksmith gets reward
\mathbf{1}\left\{k_{t}\in K^{*}\right\}
.
To study the problem’s computational complexity, we shall consider settings with a succinct polynomial-size representation of the prior distribution of correct keys.

Succinct prior with dueling key pairs.

To show the hardness, we consider a simple case with dueling key pairs. Similar to the standard setup, let there be
m
keychains
C_{1},\dots,C_{m}\subseteq[n]
. Suppose the number of keys
n
is even. The prior distribution about correct keys is such that one and only one key among
2i-1
and
2i
can open the lock, and
2i-1
is the correct key with probability
p_{i}\in[0,1]
(hence
2i
is correct with probability
1-p_{i}
). These events are independent for different
i
’s, so there are precisely
n/2
correct keys. An input to such an instance is thus
\langle n,\{C_{t}\}_{t=1}^{m},\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n/2}\rangle
.

Proposition 2

(Many Correct Keys APX-Hardness)
.

It is APX-hard to compute a Bayes optimal policy for the Keychain problem with many correct keys and a succinct prior with dueling key pairs, as described above.

The proof of the proposition can be found in
Section

B.3
.
To further illustrate the challenge with many successful keys, next we give an example to show that exploitative policies may not be optimal. In our example, even when one of the correct keys is identified, the optimal policy needs to intentionally avoid playing that correct key despite its availability in the current chain.

Fact 1
.

There exist instances of the Keychain Problem with Many Correct Keys in which exploitative policies are suboptimal. This result holds even when the events that each key opens the lock are mutually independent.

We leave the explicit construction for Fact
1
in Appendix
B.4
, but we provide the reader with an intuition for why we might expect for such a statement to be true.
When there are multiple correct keys, there is
an opportunity cost to exploiting: namely, we lose out on the ability to try a previously untested key and reduce uncertainty in the future.
This stands in contrast to settings in which there is only one correct key, where once the correct key is identified, exploiting is best as no other key can work.
Our construction leverages this fact so that exploitative strategies suffer more losses in the future than they gain in present reward.

3.2.1
Proof Step 1: Reduction to Maximum Weight Laminar Matching

We define Maximum Weight Laminar Matching (MWLM) and the approximation-preserving reduction from the Keychain Problem to MWLM.
MWLM is defined on an edge-weighted bipartite graph and a set of types associated with each right node.
The goal is to find a maximum weight laminar matching: an edge set in which each right node has at most one neighbor and each left node has at most one neighbor of each type.

In our reduction, keys correspond to left nodes, information sets correspond to right nodes, and types correspond to scenarios.
The types associated with a right node (information set) are exactly the set of scenarios consistent with the information set.
We define a bijection between policies and laminar matchings, in which an edge from a left node (key)
k
to a right node (information set)
o
encodes that the policy selects key
k
on information set
o
(e.g.
\pi(o)=k
).
Finally, by carefully selecting edge weights, the weight of any laminar matching is equivalent to the expected reward of the corresponding policy.

We now formally define laminar matchings and the MWLM Problem.

Definition 2

(Laminar Matching)
.

Given a bipartite graph
G=(L\cup R,E)
and a collection of type sets
\{T_{j}\}_{j\in R}
on right nodes that form a laminar family, a laminar matching is an edge set
M\subseteq E
such that

•

Right nodes have at most one neighbor:
|N_{M}(j)|\leq 1,\quad\quad\quad\forall j\in R

•

Left nodes have at most one neighbor of each type:
|N_{M}(i)\cap R(t)|\leq 1,\quad\quad\forall i\in L,
types
t

where
R(t)=\{j\in R\mid t\in T_{j}\}
is the set of right nodes with type
t
and
N_{M}(v)=\{u\in V\mid(u,v)\in E\}
is the set of nodes neighboring
v
on the graph induced by edges
M
.

Definition 3

(Maximum Weight Laminar Matching)
.

In the Maximum Weight Laminar Matching (MWLM) problem one is given a bipartite graph
G=(L\cup R,E)

\{T_{j}\}_{j\in R}
on right nodes that form a laminar family. The goal is find a laminar matching
M
with maximum weight
w(M)=\sum_{(i,j)\in M}w_{i,j}
.

We now present a polynomial-time and approximation-preserving reduction from Probabilistic Scenarios to MWLM.

Lemma 1

(Reduction to Maximum Weight Laminar Matching)
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
\epsilon\geq 0
, if there is a polynomial time
(\alpha,\epsilon)
-approximation algorithm for the Maximum Weight Laminar Matching problem then there is a polynomial time
(\alpha,\epsilon)
-approximation algorithm for the Probabilistic Scenarios problem.

Proof.

Given an instance of the Probabilistic Scenarios problem
\left\langle n,\mathcal{S},p\right\rangle
, we construct an instance of MWLM
\left\langle G=(L\cup R,E),w,T\right\rangle
.
The set of left nodes in the bipartite graph corresponds with keys
L=[n]
.
Right nodes correspond with information sets
R=\mathcal{O}
.
The bipartite graph is complete (e.g.
E=L\times R
).
The weight of edge
(k,o)\in L\times R
is the expected reward of picking key
k
upon observing information set
o
.
Specifically, we assign weight
w_{k,o}=p_{o}r_{k,o}
.

We now describe a bijection between admissible policies
\pi
and laminar matchings, in which the expected reward of a policy is equivalent to the weight of the corresponding laminar matching.
Admissible policy
\pi
maps to the matching
M=\{(k,o)\in E\mid\pi(o)=k\}
.
This mapping is bijective.
Moreover, the expected reward of admissible policy
\pi
and its corresponding laminar matching
M
satisfy:

\displaystyle r(\pi)=\sum_{k,o}p_{o}r_{k,o}\mathbf{1}\left\{\pi(o)=k\right\}=\sum_{(k,o)\in M}w_{k,o}=w(M)

The reduction takes polynomial time.
Thus, a polynomial time
(\alpha,\epsilon)
-approximation algorithm for the Probabilistic Scenarios problem is implied by the existence of a polynomial time
(\alpha,\epsilon)
-approximation algorithm for Maximum Weight Laminar matching.
∎

3.2.2
Proof Step 2: Reduction to XOS Combinatorial Auctions

In this section, we present a polynomial-time and approximation-preserving reduction from MWLM to Combinatorial Auctions with XOS valuations.
The Combinatorial Auction problem is defined on a set of buyers and items.
Each buyer has a valuation function that specifies the buyer’s utility for every allocation of goods they could receive.
The goal of the Combinatorial Auction problem is to assign disjoint bundles of goods to each bidder, as to maximize social welfare (the sum of all the buyers’ utilities).
When the valuations of the buyers are XOS, normalized, and monotone, there are known
(1-1/e)
-approximation algorithms for the Combinatorial Auctions problem
[
17
,
18
]
.

In our reduction, each left node is a buyer and each right node is an item.
An assignment of bundles corresponds with a laminar matching.
The constraint that each item is allocated once in an assignment guarantees that each right node has at most one neighbor in the laminar matching.
We use the valuations of buyers to encode the constraint that each left node is matched to at most one neighbor of each type in the laminar matching.
Specifically, the valuation of a buyer (left node) for a bundle of goods is the maximum weight subset of items (right nodes) in the bundle that do not share a type.
We call such valuations
antichain
functions.
Finally, we specify a surjective map from allocations to laminar matchings in which the social welfare admitted by the allocation is equivalent to the weight of the corresponding laminar matching.

Preliminaries:
Our reduction necessitates background about set functions.
We assume familiarity with previously studied XOS functions, monotone functions, normalized functions, value oracles, demand oracles, and supporting price oracles.
The definition of each of these objects is provided in Appendix
C.1
for completeness.

We introduce a special class of XOS functions called antichain functions.
To remain brief, we summarize facts about antichain functions.
Formal proofs of all claims related to antichain functions are implied by results in Appendix
C.2
for a more general class of set functions.

Definition 4

(Antichain Set Function)
.

A set function
f:2^{[n]}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is an antichain function if there are weights
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\geq 0
and laminar sets
T_{1},\dots,T_{n}\subseteq T
such that on any input
S\in 2^{[n]}
we have:

\displaystyle f(S)=\max_{A\in\textsc{AC}(S)}w(A)

where
\textsc{AC}(S)=\{A\subseteq S\mid T_{i}\cap T_{j}=\emptyset\text{ for all }i,j\in A\text{ with }i\neq j\}
is the collection containing all subsets of
S
that only contain incomparable items.

To apply the
(1-1/e)
-approximation results of
[
18
]
to combinatorial auctions, we require that valuations are XOS, monotone, and normalized.
Straightforward algebraic manipulations show that every antichain function satisfies these properties.

Fact 2

(XOS, Monotonicity, and Normalized)
.

If
f
is an antichain function, then
f
is XOS, monotone, and normalized.

The last fact we require is that antichain functions admit efficient valuation, demand, and supporting price oracles.
Each of these oracles reduces to finding a maximum-weight antichain on a vertex-weighted forest.
We specifically consider forests induced by a laminar type sets
\{T_{i}\}_{i\in S}
; these graphs have vertex set
S
and require that every
i,j\in S
with
T_{i}\subset T_{j}
has
i
as an ancestor of
j
.
Such forests can be computed efficiently.

In the case of value and supporting price queries on input
S\in 2^{[n]}
, the maximum weight antichain on the forest induced by laminar types
\{T_{i}\}_{i\in S}
with vertex weights
w
encodes a maximizing XOS clause.
For demand queries, the bundle of items in demand is a maximum weight antichain on the forest induced by laminar types
T_{1},\dots,T_{n}
with vertex weights
w_{i}-p_{i}
for each vertex
i
.

We use dynamic programming to compute maximum weight antichains.
There is an entry
\textsc{OPT}[i]
in our dynamic programming table for each node
i
.
The value of entry
\textsc{OPT}[i]
is the maximum weight of an antichain in the subtree rooted at
i
.
There are two cases we consider in our recursion at node
i
.
First, the maximum weight antichain is
\{i\}
(note that the inclusion of any descendant of
i
breaks the antichain property).
Second, the maximum weight antichain is not
\{i\}
but some collection of descendants of
i
.
This yields the following recursive definition:

\displaystyle\textsc{OPT}[i]=\max\left\{w_{v},\sum_{j\in\textsc{CHILD}(i)}\textsc{OPT}[j]\right\}

where
\textsc{CHILD}(i)
is the immediate children node of
i
in the specified forest.
Finally, backtracking recovers the maximum weight antichain in the vertex-weighted forest in polynomial time.

Fact 3

(Efficient Oracles)
.

An antichain function admits polynomial-time implementations of value, demand, and supporting price oracles.

Having established the required background, we turn our attention to the Combinatorial Auction problem and our reduction from
MWLM
.

Definition 5

(Combinatorial Auctions with XOS Valuations)
.

The Combinatorial Auctions problem is defined on a set of buyers
[n]
and a set of items
[m]
.
Each buyer has an associated normalized, monotone, and XOS valuation
v_{i}:[m]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
;
it is assumed that the auctioneer has access to a value, demand, and supporting price oracle for each valuation function.
The goal is to compute an allocation (partition on items)
S_{1},\dots,S_{n}
that maximizes
v(S_{1},\dots,S_{n})=\sum_{i\in[n]}v_{i}(S_{i})
.

Lemma 2

(Reduction to Combinatorial Auctions)
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
\epsilon\geq 0
, a polynomial time
(\alpha,\epsilon)
-approximation algorithm for the Combinatorial Auctions problem implies a polynomial time
(\alpha,\epsilon)
-approximation algorithm for the Maximum Weight Laminar Matching problem.

Proof.

Given an instance of MWLM
\left\langle G=(L\cup R),w,T\right\rangle
, we construct an instance of Combinatorial Auctions
\left\langle n,m,\{v_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}\right\rangle
.
Each left node corresponds with a buyer
[n]=L
.
Each right node has an associated item
[m]=R
.
The valuation
v_{i}
of buyer
i
for a bundle
S\subseteq[m]
of goods is the maximum weight subset of right nodes with distinct types.
For a bundle
S
, recall that
\textsc{AC}(S)=\{A\subseteq S\mid T_{j}\cap T_{j^{\prime}}=\emptyset\text{ for all distinct }j,j^{\prime}\in A\}
is the collection of subsets containing at most one item of each type.

\displaystyle v_{i}(S)=\max_{A\subseteq\textsc{AC}(S)}\sum_{j\in A}w_{i,j}

Notice that
v_{i}
is an antichain function.
Thus, it admits a linear time value, demand, and supporting price oracles.

We now create a surjective map from allocations to laminar matchings.
Allocation
S_{1},\dots,S_{n}\subseteq[m]
maps to the matching in which left node
i
has an edge to right node
j
when the supporting prices of
i
on allocation
S_{i}
has non-zero price on item
j
:

\displaystyle M=\left\{(i,j)\in E\,\middle|\,j\in A\text{ for antichain }A=\arg\max_{A^{\prime}\in\textsc{AC}(S_{i})}\sum_{j\in A^{\prime}}w_{i,j}\right\}

where the argmax operation breaks ties arbitrarily, but consistently.
The mapping is surjective.
For an arbitrary laminar matching
M
, the allocation
S_{1},\dots,S_{n}
where
S_{i}=\{j\in R\mid(i,j)\in M\}
is associated with
M
.

Finally, we show that the value of an allocation is equal to the weight of its associated laminar matching.
Let
A_{i}\in\arg\max_{A\in\textsc{AC}(S_{i})}\sum_{j\in A}w_{i,j}
be the set of items allocated to buyer
i
with non-zero supporting price for their allocation
S_{i}
(break ties arbitrarily but consistently).

\displaystyle\sum_{i}v_{i}(S_{i})=\sum_{i}\sum_{j\in A_{i}}w_{i,j}=\sum_{(i,j)\in M}w_{i,j}=w(M)

The reduction requires polynomial time.
A polynomial time
(\alpha,\epsilon)
-approximation for combinatorial auctions implies a polynomial time
(\alpha,\epsilon)
-approximation for the MWLM Problem.
∎

3.3
Hardness of Approximation

We prove that the Probabilistic Scenarios problem is hard to approximate to a factor greater than
\frac{4063}{4064}+\epsilon\approx 0.999754
.
We reduce from a special case of 3-SAT called Max-
(3,2B)
-SAT
[
7
]
.
In this special case of 3-SAT, each literal appears exactly twice among all the clauses, and each clause contains three distinct literals.

Definition 6

(Max-
(3,2B)
-SAT)
.

Given an instance of
3
-SAT with
n
variables and
m
clauses in which each literal appears twice among all clauses (balance) and each clause has three unique literals (uniqueness), find an assignment of variables that maximizes the number of satisfied clauses.

Note that the number of variables (
n
) and clauses (
m
) appearing in a
Max-
(3,2B)
-SAT formula must satisfy
4n=3m
, since the left side counts the
total number of literals (with multiplicities) when each of
2n
literals appears
twice in the formula, while the right side counts the total number of literals
when each of
m
clauses contains exactly three literals.

Fact 4

(
(3,2B)
-SAT Hardness of Approximation due to
[
7
]
)
.

In a
(3,2B)
-SAT instance with
m
clauses, it is NP-hard to distinguish between the following cases for every
\epsilon\in(0,1/4)
:

•

Complete: there is an assignment satisfying
\left(\frac{1016-\epsilon}{1016}\right)m
of the clauses.

•

Sound: every assignment satisfies at most
\left(\frac{1015+\epsilon}{1016}\right)m
of the clauses.

In our reduction, there is a key for each literal.
The possible sequences of chains that could be realized correspond with clauses.
The main gadget of our reduction is a sequence of
n
keychains, which is common to all scenarios.
Each of these first
n
chains contains the positive and negative literal of a variable.
By selecting keys from these chains, a policy commits to a variable assignment.
The final keychain contains the keys corresponding to the literals of a clause selected uniformly at random.
A policy may receive a reward on the final chain only if its variable assignment specified in the first
n
rounds satisfies the clause.

Theorem 2

(Probabilistic Scenarios Hardness of Approximation)
.

Assuming
P\neq NP
, there is no polynomial time algorithm for the Probabilistic Scenarios problem that guarantees a
\left(\frac{4063}{4064}+\delta\right)
-approximation for any
\delta\in(0,1/4)
.

Proof.

We begin by describing how to construct an instance of the Probabilistic Scenarios problem given an instance of Max-
(3,2B)
-SAT.
For each literal, we construct a key.
For each clause, we construct three scenarios: this yields
3m
total scenarios.
Consider the clause
\ell_{1}\vee\ell_{2}\vee\ell_{3}
.
The scenarios
s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}
associated with this clause all have the same sequence of keychains.
The first
n
keychains contain the pairs of literals corresponding to a variable:
C_{t}=\left\{x_{t},\neg x_{t}\right\}
for all
t\in[n]
.
The last chain contains the literals in the clause:
C_{n+1}=\left\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}\right\}
.
The correct keys for the three scenarios are
k^{*}(s_{1})=\ell_{1}
,
k^{*}(s_{2})=\ell_{2}
, and
k^{*}(s_{3})=\ell_{3}
.
Finally, the prior
p
is the uniform distribution over all scenarios.

We now define a surjective map from admissible policies to
(3,2B)
-SAT assignments, in which the expected reward of the policy is determined by the number of clauses satisfied by the corresponding assignment.
First, observe that for any admissible policy, there is an admissible policy that always plays an untested key when possible that yields at least as much expected reward.
To further simplify our arguments, we focus on deterministic policies.
The reduction for randomized policies follows from taking convex combinations of deterministic policies.
Now consider a policy
\pi
that selects keys/literals
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{n}
from the first
n
chains.
We map this policy to the assignment
x_{t}=\neg\ell_{t}
for each
t\in[n]
.
This map from policies to assignments is surjective.

Finally, we relate the expected reward of a policy to the number of clauses satisfied
by its corresponding variable assignment. The expected reward of policy
\pi
may be divided into three shares:

1.

r_{1}(\pi)=
the expected number of locks opened in time steps
1,\ldots,n
.

2.

r_{2}(\pi)=
the expected number of locks opened in step
n+1
using a key that was
already tested in one of the first
n
times steps.

3.

r_{3}(\pi)=
the expected number of locks opened in step
n+1
using a
previously untested key.

We will show that the first two of these terms are constants having no dependence on the policy,
and that the third one depends on the number of clauses satisfied by the corresponding variable
assignment.

For any policy
\pi
, one can simulate
\pi

to precompute the set
S_{\pi}
of keys that will be tested on the first
n
keychains
assuming that none of the tested keys opens the lock.
Note that the contents of the first
n
keychains are
the same in all scenarios, so
S_{\pi}
is a fixed set
of
n
keys that depends on
\pi
but has no dependence
on the scenario
s
.
Recall that the total number of scenarios is
3m=4n
. By the balance property of
Max-
(3,2B)
-SAT, each of the
2n
keys is correct in exactly 2 of these scenarios.
Since
S_{\pi}
has
n
keys, the probability that the correct key belongs to

S_{\pi}
is
\frac{2n}{4n}=\frac{1}{2}
,
irrespective of the policy
\pi
. The number of locks opened during
the first
n
time steps is 1 if the correct key belongs to
S_{\pi}

and 0 otherwise, so
r_{1}(\pi)=\frac{1}{2}
. Since the correct key always
belongs to
C_{n+1}
and
\pi
is exploitative, the event that a lock
is opened in step
n+1
using a key that was previously tested coincides
with the event that the correct key belongs to
S_{\pi}
, so

r_{2}(\pi)=\frac{1}{2}
.

Finally, to calculate
r_{3}(\pi)
, let
k_{n+1}(\pi)
denote the key
that
\pi
selects from
C_{n+1}
assuming the first
n
keys tested
are not correct. Again, since the first
n
chains are the same in all
scenarios, the identity of the key
k_{n+1}(\pi)
depends on the policy

\pi
but not the scenario
s
. We can assume without loss of generality

\pi
will always select an untested key if possible. The only scenarios
in which this is impossible are those in which the three keys on chain
C_{n+1}

belong to
S_{\pi}
. By our construction of the truth assignment
corresponding to
\pi

truth assignment, which we denote by
\tau(\pi)
, fails to satsfy the clause
associated with the scenario, which we denote by
\text{cl}(s)

The event that
\pi

We have seen that

\Pr(k^{*}(s)=k_{n+1}(\pi)\,|\,k_{n+1}(\pi)\not\in S_{\pi})=\frac{1}{3}

Putting these observations together, we find that

where

due to
[

4.3
NP-Hardness

When each keychain can contain an arbitrary number of keys and the locksmith can pick the order of the keychains, we show that it is NP-Hard to determine the optimal strategy for the locksmith via a reduction from the
Upper Triangular Matrix Permutation
(UTMP) problem.
For the remainder of this section, we take

To describe the UTMP problem and our reductions, it will be useful to define a few matrix-related notations.
For an

We say that a binary matrix

).
Notably, a matrix with a full upper triangle is not necessarily upper triangular, nor is the reverse true.
Finally, we say that a matrix

To reduce it to the

Next, we observe that any binary matrix

We begin with a few observations about the Keychain Problem.

Observation 2

Proof.

Further, observe that if it is possible to achieve the theoretical upper bound reward of

Open Questions and Future Directions

5.1
Approximation with Exponentially Many Probabilistic Scenarios

Our algorithms for the Probabilistic Scenarios setting (
Section

3
) run polynomially in the number of scenarios.
We ask the intriguing question: Is there an efficient
(1-1/e)
-approximation for instances with succinct priors over exponentially many scenarios?
Specifically, we consider prior distributions that admit an efficient future scenario sampling oracle.
This oracle takes as input an information set
o
and returns a scenario
s
sampled from the prior conditioned on the occurrence of
o

s
is returned with probability
(p_{s}/p_{o})\cdot\mathbf{1}\left\{s\in C(o)\right\}
.

Open Question 1

(Exponentially Many Scenarios)
.

Given a future scenario sampling oracle, is there a polynomial-time
(1-1/e)
-approximation algorithm for the probabilistic scenarios problem?

The main observation giving hope that such an algorithm is possible is the fact that the locksmith does not need an entire description of her policy
\pi
.
She only needs the output of her policy
\pi
restricted to the set of realized information sets, which is observed in an online fashion.
We foresee the future scenario sampling oracle being applied to each observed information set to learn which available key should be tested.

In addition to the instance in which each of the
m
keychains is drawn i.i.d. from a known distribution (analogous to the online bipartite matching setting investigated in
[
43
,
13
,
14
]
), this future sampling algorithm could be applied to much more intricate stochastic models.
The most natural of these instances has a prior that presents a sequence of known keychains whose ordering is chosen uniformly at random.

5.2
Open Directions in the Keychain Order Selection Setting

Both the approximation algorithm and the NP-Hardness result in Section
4
point towards connections between the Keychain Order Selection problem and quadratic assignment problems
[
40
]
.
Indeed, the (1/2)-approximation is very similar to the known folklore (1/2)-approximation of Maximum Acyclic Subgraph (MAS), in which either an ordering of the nodes or its reverse satisfies at least 1/2 the edges.
Similarly, the hardness reduction from the UTMP problem suggests a deeper connection to quadratic assignment problems in general.

However, an approximation-preserving connection between the two problems remains elusive: in UTMP, the objective sums up the number of 1 entries in the upper triangle.
The Keychain Order Selection problem has
almost
the same objective except that it only counts 1 entries in rows of the upper triangle such that the main diagonal entry of that row is a 1 (
A_{i,i}=1
).
We refer to this as the diagonal constraint.

The diagonal constraint is not easily encoded in either MAS or the UTMP objectives, whose focus is simply to maximize the number of “satisfied” (in some way) edges.
A high value solution to MAS or UTMP can have potentially zero value in the Keychain Order Selection problem if all the diagonal entries are 0.
Conversely, a high value solution for the Keychain Order Selection problem may not be the highest value solution in MAS or UTMP (although it
will
retain the same value), as it may be dwarfed by a solution that disregards the diagonal constraint entirely.

We leave open multiple directions for the Keychain Order Selection problem.
If the diagonal constraint issue can be navigated properly, then steps could be made towards a proper hardness of approximation result.
It would be interesting to learn whether known hardness results in MAS
[
26
]
generalize to the Keychain Order Selection problem.

Open Question 2

(Hardness of Approximation)
.

Assuming standard complexity theory assumptions (
P\neq NP
or the Unique Games Conjecture), is it hard to approximate the Keychain Order Selection problem past 1/2?

This would imply a deep and concrete connection to MAS and showing a hardness of approximation past 1/2 would imply that Algorithm
6
is optimal.
However, a direct approximation-preserving reduction from MAS seems difficult as it is unclear how to incorporate the diagonal constraint into the MAS objective.
On the other hand, and perhaps more interestingly: if additional structure of the Keychain Order Selection problem can be leveraged properly, it may be possible to break the
\frac{1}{2}
OPT barrier.

Another promising direction is in developing a PTAS when the problem is
dense
: settings in which the value of the optimal solution is guaranteed to be at least
c\cdot m
for some constant
c>0
.
Loosely speaking, it is easy to identify ‘‘good’’ keys to try in these settings: either each relevant key shows up many
5
5

5

By many here, we mean roughly
\Omega(m)
times.
times (opportunity cost of skipping is low), or the key has a high probability of opening the lock (the opportunity cost of skipping is recognizably high).

Open Question 3

(PTAS for Dense Instances)
.

Does the Keychain Order Selection problem admit an additive or multiplicative PTAS for dense instances?

One possible approach for dense settings is to use the techniques of
[
3
]
, which could possibly be generalized to incorporate the conditional nature of the diagonal constraint.
However, a direct application fails as the diagonal constraint eventually manifests as a linear constraint that needs to be satisfied
exactly
in any solution, but
[
3
]
only satisfies linear constraints approximately.

5.3
Known Keychain Order and Many Correct Keys

There are a wealth of enticing directions moving forward in the Multiple Correct Key setting with known keychain order.
We offer two natural settings which are largely unexplored.

In the first setting, the prior on correct keys has polynomial size support.
Much like small priors in the Probabilistic Scenarios setting facilitated the design of efficient algorithms, we question if small priors in the Multiple Correct Key setting allows that design of similar efficient algorithms.

In other words, we suggest looking into the Multiple Correct Key setting when the correctness of keys is mutually independent.

\displaystyle=\Pr[A]\cdot\mathbb{E}[N_{A}(1,2,3)]+\Pr[B]\cdot\mathbb{E}[N_{B}(2,3)]+\Pr[C]\cdot\mathbb{E}[N_{C}(3)]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{3}{7}\right)\left(\frac{7}{3}\right)+\left(\frac{2}{7}\right)\left(2\right)+\left(\frac{2}{7}\right)\left(1\right)=\frac{13}{7}

We now analyze the second optimal policy.

Lily will first choose to collaborate with Bob, and Carol second if Bob is not the right advisor.
If neither Bob nor Carol is are good advising fit, then Lily will choose to collaborate with Alice for the third rotation.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\text{successful rotations of optimal}\mid S]

\displaystyle=\Pr[A\mid S]\cdot\mathbb{E}[N_{A}(3)\mid S]+\Pr[B\mid S]\cdot\mathbb{E}[N_{B}(1,2,3)\mid S]+\Pr[C\mid S]\cdot\mathbb{E}[N_{C}(2,3)\mid S]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{3}{7}\right)\cdot 1+\left(\frac{2}{7}\right)\cdot 3+\left(\frac{2}{7}\right)\cdot 2=\frac{13}{7}

\displaystyle\Pr[S]\cdot\mathbb{E}[\text{successful rotations of optimal}\mid S]+\Pr[\bar{S}]\cdot\mathbb{E}[\text{successful rotations of optimal}\mid\bar{S}]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)\left(\frac{13}{7}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)\left(\frac{14}{7}\right)=\frac{40}{21}>\frac{13}{7}

C.2
Antichain Set Functions

Vital to our combinatorial auction reductions are antichain functions and disjoint antichain functions, more generally.
Specifically, these set functions serve as the valuations of bidders.
In this section, we explicitly work with disjoint antichain functions.
All properties of disjoint antichain functions apply to the subsumed class of antichain functions.

Disjoint antichain functions output the maximum weight of at most
k
disjoint antichains on a vertex-weighted forest.
We apply disjoint antichain functions to the forest representation of laminar families to find maximum weight subsets with few comparable elements.
We begin by showing that disjoint antichain functions are XOS, but not submodular nor supermodular.
To ensure that we can use disjoint antichain functions as valuations in combinatorial auctions, we also prove that all such functions are monotone and normalized.
Finally, to facilitate our reductions, we prove that this special class of set functions admits polynomial-time value, demand, and supporting price oracles.

Appendix D
An Implementable Algorithm for Randomized Scenarios

We provide a self-contained algorithm for the Randomized Scenario setting that bypasses the reduction presented in Lemmas

2

This

-approximation algorithm is a simplification of the XOS Combinatorial Auction algorithm presented in
[
18
]
, and the rounding scheme we provide closely resembles that of
[
13
]
.
There are two main components of the algorithm.
The first component uses a linear program to construct a fractional policy
\pi
, which may select a fractional amount of a key on an information set.
The second part of the algorithm rounds the fractional solution to an integral one.
We assume familiarity with the notation in Section
3
.

Theorem 4

(Randomized Scenarios Approximation Algorithm)
.

Algorithm

(1-(1-1/n)^{n})
-approximation to the Randomized Scenarios Setting.

We begin by describing the linear program (LP), which is a continuous extension of an integer linear program whose solution is a Bayes optimal policy.
In the LP, the variable
x_{k,o}
indicates that the corresponding policy
\pi
plays key
k
upon observing information set
o\in\mathcal{O}
.
There are two non-trivial constraints in the LP.
The first constraint requires that the policy
\pi
maps each information set to at most one key (or at most one fractional key).
The second constraint ensures that each key is selected at most once, among the information sets associated with a scenario.
We use
\textsc{LP}(p,\mathcal{S})
to denote the collection of optimal solutions to the following LP.

\displaystyle\max\sum_{k,o}p_{o}r_{k,o}x_{k,o}

(6)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\sum_{k}x_{k,o}\leq 1,

\displaystyle\text{for all }o\in\mathcal{O}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\sum_{o\in P(s)}x_{k,o}\leq 1,

\displaystyle\text{for all }k\in[n],s\in\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle\quad\quad x_{k,o}\geq 0,

\displaystyle\text{for all }k\in[n],o\in\mathcal{O}

The remainder of the algorithm rounds a fractional solution to LP
6

In summary, when faced with scenario

The preallocation yields total reward equal to the objective value of the fractional solution

To resolve conflicting assignments for information set

k
preallocated

r_{k,o}
value.
The
(1-(1-1/n)^{n})
factor is a product of handling conflicts caused by independent preallocations of an information set to many keys.

We now focus on several supporting lemmas and observations related to algorithm

\mathcal{K}
of feasible priors?
For instance,
\mathcal{K}
could be the set of priors statistically close to a specified reference prior.
Using black-box access to
\alpha
-approximation algorithms for the Probabilistic Scenarios setting, we compute an approximate best response policy to any prior
p\in\cal\mathcal{K}

Similar to
[
44

\mathcal{A}

We show that using a low-regret policy implies a fast convergence to a policy that performs well against any prior in
\mathcal{K}

Before formalizing our claims, we introduce some necessary notation and specify some assumptions.
All definitions consider a fixed set
\mathcal{S}
of scenarios.
To denote the reward/utility of an admissible policy
\pi:\mathcal{O}\to[k]
in scenario
s

\displaystyle u_{s}(\pi)=\sum_{k}\left(\sum_{o\in P(s)}r_{k,o,s}\mathbf{1}\left\{\pi(o)=k\right\}\right)

\pi

In our algorithm, we assume black-box access to a possible randomized
\alpha
-approximation oracle
\mathcal{A}
for the Probabilistic Scenarios Problem on scenarios

.
Specifically, we assume that a policy sampled from this algorithm yields an approximately optimal best response in expectation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim\mathcal{A}(p)}\left[u(p,\pi)\right]\geq\alpha\max_{\pi}u(p,\pi)

\mathcal{K}

The goal of this algorithm is to compute a policy

In this section, we describe our representation of a

We assume familiarity with Section

WOBM

Each state is a prefix of observed weight sequences, called information sets, and the previously selected edges.
By
[

Such deterministic policies implicitly encode previously selected edges in this MDP.
Thus, a state of the MDP is simply an information set.
We represent deterministic policies as mappings from information sets to an offline node to select.
A policy is admissible only if it allocates at most
b_{i}

i
.

We introduce some notation to formalize our policy characterization.
The collection of information sets is denoted as
\mathcal{O}=\{w_{:,1:j}\}_{j\in[m],w\in\textsc{supp}(p)}
.
For information set

to denote the weight of matching offline node
i
to the online node
j
that arrived to yield information set

.
We call a realization of edge weights a scenario and use
\mathcal{S}=\textsc{supp}(p)
to denote the set of all scenarios.
The realized weights in scenario

w^{s}

We use

to denote that information set
o\in\mathcal{O}
is observed in scenario
s
.
A scenario

is said to be consistent with information set

when

s
; we use

to denote that
s

o

The probability of a scenario being realized is

and the probability that an information set

p_{o}=\sum_{s\in C(o)}p_{o}
.
A deterministic

\pi:\mathcal{O}\to[n]
.
A policy is admissible if
|\{o\in P(s)\mid\pi(o)=i\}|\leq b_{i}
for all scenario

.
Finally the reward of a policy is:

Reduction: Maximum Weight Laminar b-Matching

F.4
Reduction: XOS Combinatorial Auctions

In this section, we reduce

We assume familiarity with combinatorial auctions and disjoint antichain functions.
See Section

In this reduction, each left node is a bidder and each right node is an item.
The valuation of each bidder is a disjoint antichain function, where the number of antichains is set to the capacity of the corresponding left node.
By ignoring allocated items with supporting price equal to
0

By carefully selecting the weights of the disjoint antichain function, we can ensure an allocation has social welfare equal to the weight of its corresponding laminar matching.

Lemma 8

(Reduction to Combinatorial Auctions)

We convert an instance of

i

\displaystyle v_{i}(S)=\max_{A_{1},\dots,A_{k}\in\textsc{AC}(S)}\hat{w}_{i}(A_{1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{k})

Let

times because
