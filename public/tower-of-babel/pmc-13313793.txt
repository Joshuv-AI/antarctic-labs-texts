Gender difference in health profession education among undergraduate medical students: Importance of culture and simulation in women’s health
Authors: Shahid Akhund, Hassan Shaibah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pakistan has one of South Asia’s highest maternal mortality ratios, a problem exacerbated by cultural sensitivities that influence health professions education (HPE) in obstetrics and gynecology (O&G) clerkships. These factors may ultimately affect women’s health outcomes. This study aimed to evaluate gender-based differences in health professions education during O&G clerkships in Pakistan. This retrospective cross-sectional study analyzed examination records of 1572 medical students (795 males, 777 females) from Liaquat University of Medical and Health Sciences, Pakistan. The written, practical/clinical skills, and overall O&G clerkship scores were compared using independent-samples t tests. Male students achieved significantly higher scores in written O&G examinations (70.46 ± 6.32 vs 68.69 ± 7.28; P < .001), while female students performed better in practical/clinical skills assessments (69.05 ± 10.39 vs 65.98 ± 10.47; P < .001). No significant differences were observed in total O&G scores (68.23 ± 6.79 vs 68.40 ± 7.47; P = .64). Male students performed better in written O&G examinations, while female students excelled in practical assessments; however, overall scores were comparable. This study highlights differences in educational outcomes, which may be influenced by cultural perceptions of gender within the medical specialty.

1. Introduction

Maternal mortality is one of Pakistan’s most concerning public health challenges. Pakistan also has South Asia’s highest maternal mortality ratio (MMR), a measure that is extremely closely linked with cultural practices that determine the accessibility of care for women and the structure of medical education.[1] Gender segregation and the use of purdah (covering) by way of cultural sensitivities also restrict male–female interactions and reinforce strict gender roles.[2,3] In the clinical environment, those same practices also affect patient preference such that patients overwhelmingly prefer consultation by and procedures at the hands of female doctors if intimate clinical examinations are required.[4,5] Though the preference may earn women wider access to female caregivers, an unforeseen byproduct is a reduction of learning experiences for male medical students, particularly those in obstetrics and gynecology (O&G).

The Pakistani cultural environment is strongly patriarchal, with feudal-based sociopolitics and religious understandings that stress gender segregation.[3,6,7] Complying with purdah is sometimes seen as a family matter of honor,[2] influencing community conduct as well as utilization of healthcare services.[8-10] Males in O&G clerkships often have restricted access to women patients, with consequent lower clinical exposure, lower confidence, and lack of enthusiasm for pursuing the specialty.[11-13] Gender bias has long-term effects by perpetuating fewer male O&G specialists in Pakistan as well as by possibly fueling vacancies in the provision of maternal healthcare services.[3,11,12]

The Pakistan Medical and Dental Council (PMDC) sets out standardized learning objectives for O&G clerkships such as clinical diagnosis, minor procedures, effective communication, ethical issues of medicine, and public health issues awareness.[14] Achieving these objectives demands routine interaction with patients and hands-on exposure, of which male students are deprived because of cultural taboos. Although female students enjoy privileged interaction with patients, the unequal status defeats the purpose of equal medical training and threatens male graduates’ competence in providing women’s health care.[11,15]

Overseas evidence reflects these issues. For example, nursing school studies reflect how male students on women’s health placements experience role stereotyping, restricted access to patients, and an exclusionary atmosphere on clinical teams.[13,16,17] Correspondingly, an Angolan study focused on the issues of insufficient infrastructure and poor teaching resources, leading to poor clinical training results that also exacerbate maternal health issues.[18] These results imply that gender is by no means the only determinant of training disparity; structural problems like poor clinical supervision, poor facilities, and resource inadequacies also play large roles.[15]

One of the potential strategies for mitigating such inequalities is applying simulation-based medical education. Simulation offers learners a chance to gain technical proficiency as well as effective communication in a sterilized environment, free of cultural barriers that may deter actual application in the real world.[17,19,20] Simulation has proved to enhance learners’ levels of confidence, training standardization, as well as technical proficiency in the specialty of O&G.[21,22] Through clinical exposure gap closures, simulation provides equal opportunities for male and female learners such that each graduate attains the competencies necessary for effective maternal health management.

Because of cultural sensitivities and structural limitations that exist across Pakistan, gender differences impacting the abilities of medical students performing in O&G clerkships need to be comprehended. This study aimed to evaluate gender-based differences in health professions education (HPE) during O&G clerkships in Pakistan. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study in Pakistan has examined this topic with a comparable level of analytical detail. This research adds to the existing literature by providing empirical evidence that cultural sensitivities in Pakistan influence not only social interactions but also HPE, as reflected by performance differences within the O&G clerkship environment. Furthermore, the findings support the need for gender-neutral HPE interventions to ensure that all medical graduates, regardless of gender, acquire the practical competencies required to improve maternal health outcomes in culturally conservative settings.

2. Methodology

2.1. Study design and setting

This research used a retrospective cross-sectional study design. The data were obtained from Liaquat University of Medical and Health Sciences (LUMHS).

2.2. Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Formal ethical approval was not deemed necessary because the study utilized retrospectively collected, de-identified secondary data from archived institutional records. The data consisted of student performance metrics from previous batches, and no active intervention or direct contact with students occurred. All personal identifiers were removed prior to data sharing and analysis to ensure confidentiality. Administrative permission to utilize these records for research purposes was formally granted by the Vice-Chancellor of LUMHS, the custodian of the data.

2.3. Study population

The study participants were undergraduate medical students who had cleared the end-of-clerkship exams of O&G and internal medicine from 2018 up to 2021. The inclusion criteria were: medical students officially registered at LUMHS; students who completed both the theory and clinical skills components of the O&G clerkship examination; and students with complete assessment records. The exclusion criteria were: students who did not attempt both the theory and clinical skills components of the O&G clerkship examination and students with incomplete assessment records. A total of 1572 students (795 males and 777 females) fulfilled the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis.

2.4. Data collection

De-identified examination records were accessed from the academic office of LUMHS after institutional permission. The assessment formats for all cohorts included in the study were consistent and conducted in accordance with the regulations of the Pakistan Medical and Dental Council (PMDC). Each assessment comprised both written and practical/clinical components. The written component evaluated theoretical knowledge, while the practical/clinical component assessed competencies such as communication proficiency, patient interaction, and clinical examination skills.

2.5. Variables of interest

Student gender was the primary independent variable and was operationalized as male or female. Dependent variables included written test scores, practical/clinical test scores, and aggregate total score sums for O&G clerkships. These were measured to assess whether any observed gender-based differences appeared in the O&G clerkship.

2.6. Statistical analysis

Statistical tests were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 30; IBM Corp.). Skew statistics assisted in testing the distribution of scores for normality. Given the large sample size (n = 1572), the application of parametric tests is further supported by the Central Limit Theorem, which indicates that the sampling distribution of the mean tends toward normality. In addition, the observed skewness values for the analyzed components were −0.75 (written), −0.29 (practical/clinical skills), and −0.43 (overall scores), all of which fall within the acceptable range of −1 to +1, indicating approximate normal distribution with slight negative skewness.

Furthermore, bootstrapping with 2000 resamples was conducted, and the resulting 95% confidence intervals (written: −1.10 to −0.48; practical/clinical skills: −0.46 to −0.13; overall scores: −0.60 to −0.25) were consistent with the parametric estimates, providing additional support for the appropriateness of the selected analytical approach.

Descriptive statistics, including mean values and standard deviations, were calculated for male and female students. Inferential analysis was conducted using independent-samples t tests to compare male and female learners’ achievement on diverse components of assessment. A 95% confidence interval (CI) was adopted, while a P-value of <.05 was treated as statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Data distribution and statistical assumptions

Prior to analysis, the dataset was screened for normality. The distribution of the scores fulfilled assumptions necessary for parametric testing as mentioned before, validating the appropriateness of the independent-samples t test for comparisons across groups. As noted in the literature, there exists ongoing debate over whether parametric or nonparametric tests are appropriate for use in educational research,[23] but assumptions of parametric methods were fulfilled for the current study.

3.2. Descriptive findings

Descriptive data in Table 1 show the gender distribution and achievement scores of the sample (795 male and 777 female students). These students graduated over 4 batches of MBBS courses and undertook O&G clerkships during their clinical years. Their mean percentage written scores, practical/clinical skills scores, and total scores on end-of-year clerkship examinations were analyzed. Figure 1 shows the scores on the written and practical/clinical skill tests and the overall scores of the students. Male students scored higher on average on the written tests, whereas female students scored higher on the practical/clinical skill tests, and both attained almost the same total scores. As presented in Table 1, in the written tests, the mean score obtained by male students was 70.46 ± 6.32%, and female students obtained 67.34 ± 7.7%. In the practical/clinical skill tests, male students scored 65.98 ± 10.47% and female students scored 69.05 ± 10.39%. The total score obtained by male students was 68.15 ± 7.12% and by female students was 68.19 ± 7.30%.

3.3. Inferential findings

The hypothesis that male students underperform compared with female students in O&G clerkships was tested using independent-samples t tests. Results confirmed significant gender differences in component scores.

Written examinations: Male students scored significantly higher than females (t(1570) = 5.15, P < .001, 95% CI = 1.10–2.45; Table 1). Practical examinations: Female students outperformed males, and the difference was statistically significant (t(1570) = –3.93, P < .001, 95% CI = –3.16 to–1.05; Table 1). Total scores: No significant difference was observed between male and female students (t(1570) = –0.47, P = .64, 95% CI = –0.87 to 0.54).

4. Discussion

The present study investigated gender-related differences in the HPE during O&G clerkships at a public medical university in Pakistan. The results demonstrated that male students outperformed in written tests, indicative of superior cognitive knowledge, while female students did better on practical/clinical skills tests, indicative of superior clinical and interpersonal abilities. However, the total scores did not differ substantially across gender. These differences occurred during O&G, were specific, and related to factors that are exclusive to women’s health during undergraduate HPE. These findings align with previous studies[12,13,16] that have recorded gender-differentiated difficulties during O&G clerkships. Male students tend to record minimal clinical exposure, lower patient acceptability, and lower self-confidence while conducting intimate clinical examinations. Female students, on the other hand, benefit from greater patient accessibility and trust, usually more pronounced against the backdrop of conservative cultural milieux wherein female patients overwhelmingly prefer female doctors.[4,5] The present study findings thus confirm the proposition that gender bias in HPE has its genesis not in academic competence but rather in structural barriers as well as cultural bias.

Cultural sensitivity seems the most powerful contributor to these gaps. In Pakistan, healthcare delivery is strongly influenced by sociocultural practices like the purdah that inhibit interaction between men and women and perpetuate gender segregation.[2,3,7,24] For male students, this translates to fewer opportunities for carrying out patient interviews, pelvic examinations, or attending delivery, all essential procedures of O&G training. As suggested by Flores[9] and Bussey-Jones and Genao,[8] culture not only influences the preference of patients but also delineates the physician–patient relationship, upon which quality of care as well as training breadth depend. The present study mirrors that experience: male students underperformed in practical/clinical skills O&G tests. Similar issues have been observed internationally. Mendes et al,[18] reported that systemic issues such as substandard infrastructures, inadequacy of supervision, and resource shortages could also compromise clinical education in middle- and low-income countries.

These systemic flaws coupled with cultural constraints create a double challenge for male undergraduate medical students engaged in O&G clerkships. As Kerssens et al[5] suggested, female patients prefer female doctors, particularly in clinical situations where intimate clinical examinations are required. This study highlighted similar findings. In addition to the many challenges highlighted by Pradhan et al,[11] the interaction of beliefs, principles, and cultural demands[25] appears to have a greater effect on the clinical skills, learning, and performance of male students in O&G clerkships. The result is a perpetuation of gender stereotyping with males withdrawing progressively from women’s health specialties, thus contributing to the shortage of trained gynecologists and obstetricians available in Pakistan.[11,12]

In highly conservative societies such as Pakistan,[3,7,24] it is difficult to address such sensitive issues by changing the mindset of cultural groups. To address this teaching and learning issue in HPE, the incorporation of simulations into O&G teaching and assessment may be a way forward. This suggestion was also identified in the literature as providing students with valuable real-time feedback.[17] Levine et al[20] and McGaghie et al[19] explained the benefits of incorporating simulation-based learning exercises to standardize medical education globally.

One such potential strategy for reducing these inequalities is the inclusion of simulation-based learning within undergraduate O&G training. Simulation offers standardized exposure for every student to essential clinical skill practice irrespective of cultural sensitivities or individual patient preference. Earlier studies have indicated that simulation develops baseline knowledge, refines technical skill, and enhances confidence amongst learners.[17,19-21] Everett et al[22] also noted its practicability in large student cohorts at the faculty of medicine level by showing that skill acquisition could be enhanced with quality maintained. In Pakistan’s case, cultural restrictions are unlikely to diminish in the near future, simulation provides an achievable culturally acceptable solution.

4.1. Limitations

The current study has few limitations. First, data were obtained from only 1 institution, which could reduce the generalizability of the results across Pakistan. Second, the study was based on test scores only and did not incorporate qualitative information from students on their learning opportunities, barriers, and survival strategies. Future studies should adopt a mixed-method approach to capture both the objective measure of performance and the subjective phenomenon. In addition, broadening the study across other gender-stereotypic specialties like surgery, commonly male-dominated, may offer a balanced understanding of gender norms in medical education across the specialties.

5. Conclusion

The HPE is not culturally immune, especially in conservative societies. Its effects could be seen in the difference in academic performance between male and female students. This difference could be rooted in cultural sensitivities rather than academic acumen. This study found that male students performed better in the written component of O&G examinations, while female students excelled in practical/clinical skills assessments; however, overall scores were comparable.

The findings of this study highlight the need for curricular reforms in O&G clerkships, particularly in culturally conservative settings such as Pakistan. Integrating simulation-based training can provide male students with equitable opportunities to acquire essential clinical skills that are otherwise restricted by patient preferences and gender norms. Such interventions not only promote fairness in medical education but also help address the shortage of skilled obstetricians and gynecologists, ultimately contributing to improved maternal healthcare outcomes.

Simulation-based education may offer an effective strategy to mitigate the impact of cultural barriers in HPE. In the Pakistani context, it can serve as a pedagogical intervention that promotes equity in training. Medical institutions are encouraged to establish specialized simulation centers equipped with appropriate resources to support learning in common health issues, including women’s health. Such learning environments may enhance student confidence and help bridge educational gaps arising from gender-based biases. Incorporating simulation-based education into HPE may represent a future strategy to address cultural barriers and promote equitable skill development, particularly for male students, thereby strengthening maternal healthcare training in Pakistan. However, this approach requires further validation through dedicated action research.
