Artificial Intelligence Scribes in Orthopaedic Surgery: A Narrative Review
Authors: Fatumastar O. Adan, Kehinde S. Agoro, Venkat Rao, Brian P. Cunningham, Allison J. Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:The growing applications of artificial intelligence (AI) is transforming the healthcare landscape by reshaping diagnostics, workflow operations, and clinical decision making. Among its most promising application in surgery is the development of AI scribes designed to reduce the burden of documentation. This review examines standard and emerging documentation tools with a focus on orthopaedic surgery.Methods:A narrative review of the applications of AI and AI scribes in orthopaedic surgery was done. Literature searches were conducted using PubMed and Google Scholar through July 2025. Keywords included AI, documentation, scribes, AI scribes, and electronic health record. Articles were included if they focused on clinical documentation with particular relevance to orthopaedic surgery. In addition, a targeted review of commercially available AI scribe platforms was conducted, extracting advertised features including orthopaedic note tailoring, EHR integration, security policies, cost, strengths, and potential areas of consideration.Results:The standard documentation approaches, including human transcription services, medical scribes, and frontend speech recognition software, provide partial relief from documentation burden but remain limited by cost, scalability, and workflow integration. Although human transcription services and medical scribes can capture nuanced clinical information and provide real-time support, they are constrained by turnaround time, financial burden, and scalability challenges. By contrast, emerging AI scribe platforms offer EHR-integrated, ambient documentation solutions but vary widely in cost, scalability, and orthopaedic-specific customization, highlighting ongoing challenges in addressing the unique documentation demands of orthopaedic practice.Discussion:AI scribes represent a promising evolution in clinical documentation for orthopaedic surgery. Although early results are encouraging, further specialty-specific validation is needed to address orthopaedic workflows, ensure accuracy, and define optimal implementation strategies; however, ethical considerations and patient privacy protections remain essential to safe and responsible implementation.

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is rapidly transforming medicine, with its application ranging from diagnostic and imaging analysis to workflow organization, and decision-making input.1-6 The use of AI in healthcare is still unfolding, and one of its most promising and clinically relevant application in surgery is the development of AI-powered scribes. This use case of AI is well suited to solve the real problem of physician burnout caused by documentation burden.

The administrative strains of health care, especially documentation requirements, have been consistently linked to physician burnout and well documented in the literature.7-11 Burnout is a well-established risk factor for decreased quality of care, increased medical errors, reduced patient satisfaction, and high provider turnover.9,12,13 Documentation burden not only affects provider well being but can also often interfere with direct patient care.11,14 One study reported that physicians spent only 27% of their time in direct clinical interaction with patients, whereas 49% is spent on Electronic Health Record (EHR) and desk work.14 Even during in-person encounters, nearly 37% of their time is spent on the EHR.14 In addition to reduced quality of care, burnout compromises patient safety; affected clinicians are up to 2.2 times more likely to commit errors.15 The economic toll is also notable, with an estimated $4.6 billion lost annually in the United States due to physician turnover and reduced clinical hours.12

To reduce the documentation burden, many healthcare systems have turned to transcription services, voice recognition software, and human scribes. However, ambient AI scribes can passively capture clinical patient dialogue and generate structured notes reducing these limitations. These tools hold promise for improving both provider well being and patient care.16-18 Orthopaedic surgery is unique because of subspeciality-specific terminology, imaging-driven encounters, complex implant nomenclature, and need to meet insurance requirements for approval of surgery. This review will critically examine standard scribe options and the current state of AI scribes, with a particular attention to orthopaedic requirements.

Methods

We did a narrative review of the applications of AI and AI scribes in orthopaedic surgery. Literature searches were conducted using PubMed, and Google Scholar through July, 2025. We entered keywords directly into search field using a combination of AI, documentation, scribes, AI scribes, and EHR. The search was limited to peer-reviewed studies published in English.

Articles were included if they focused on clinical documentation with a particular focus on orthopaedic surgery. Studies that examined other AI applications, such as imaging, decision making, and risk management, were noted, but the review concentrated on tools directly related to documentation. We included reports of human scribes, transcription services, and frontend speech recognition software to provide context for how AI scribe compared with existing documentation methods.

In parallel, we also did a targeted review of commercially available AI scribe platform and transitional documentation services. We extracted each platform's advertised features, including orthopaedic note tailoring, EHR integration, security and data policies, cost, strength, and possible weakness.

Results

Standard Documentation Approaches and Their Limitations

Standard approaches to reducing documentation burden generally fall into three categories: (1) human transcription services, which convert recorded clinic encounters into notes; (2) in-person or remote medical scribes, who document visits in real time; and (3) frontend speech recognition software, which enables providers to dictate notes directly into the electronic health record (EHR). Each of these methods offers potential benefits, but scalability, cost, and workflow integration remain a challenge.

Human Transcription Services

One widely used example is iDictate, a US-based transcription company founded in 1999 that provides 100% human transcription with rapid turnaround.19 These kinds of services have the ability to handle nuanced and complex audio that AI tools may miss. iDictate advertises free setup and storage with a $5 trial credit.19 The transcription costs start at about two cents per word for regular medical services and can increase with greater complexity or speed, with expedited services priced at five cents per word for the fastest turnaround.19 It is able to integrate with EHR systems and is HIPAA compliant.19 The focus predominantly lies on general medical transcription, with potential applicability across various specialties. Nonetheless, generalized transcription services may lack precision for subspecialty-specific terminology. Furthermore, although the service is economically viable for limited use, it can become rapidly expensive when scaled, and a notable limitation is the turnaround time, which may impede real-time accessibility.

In-Person or Remote Medical Scribes

Another long-standing solution to the documentation burden has been the use of medical scribes—paraprofessional staff who shadow the doctor in person or remotely through video to document visits in real time.20 ScribeAmerica established in 2003 is the largest medical scribe company in the United States. Although the concept of medical scribe utilization has been anecdotally reported since the 1970s, ScribeAmerica is widely credited with transforming the practice into a national industry.20 A human scribe provides true real-time on-site assistance, navigating the EHR for the physician, and entering notes directly.20 Although the average medical scribe earns approximately $41,492 per year, costs to organizations are higher due to training, benefits, and administrative overhead.21 Scribes maintain HIPAA compliance, achieve seamless integration with healthcare systems' electronic health records, and can be proficiently trained in orthopaedic terminology. However, the financial implications and scalability of this method present considerable challenges. High turnover rates are prevalent among medical scribes, as many are temporary employees pursuing further educational opportunities, resulting in recurrent cycles of hiring and training. In addition, certain patients may experience discomfort in disclosing sensitive information in the presence of a third party.

Frontend Speech Recognition Software

Dragon Medical One (a Microsoft health solutions product) was first announced in 2016. It is a cloud-based clinical documentation tool that enables clinicians to dictate encounter notes, navigate EHRs using voice, and automate documentation task without needing voice-profile training.22 We are attempting to say that dragon medical one intergrates with more than 200 electronic health record system, and supported approximately 90 medical specialties.22 It offers various plans with differing costs with pricing structure at $99 per month per provider for a 1-year plan, $89 per month for a 2-year plan, and $79 per month for a 3-year plan, accompanied by a one-time setup and training fee of $525.22 Nonetheless, several limitations warrant consideration, such as its compatibility being restricted to Windows, a single-user licensing framework, and the learning curve associated with advanced functionalities. Furthermore, dictation may interfere with patient-provider interactions, unless conducted post clinical encounters; although it may be more expedient than typing, it could necessitate additional time for dictation.

Given the limitations of transcription services, medical scribes, and voice recognition software, there has been an increasing interest in the utilization of AI scribe systems and ambient documentation technologies. These tools aim to reduce clerical burden by automatically transcribing and structuring clinical encounters using advanced natural language processing (NLP). Unlike human scribes, AI scribes operate passively and asynchronously, thereby reducing interruptions to workflow and patient care.

Artificial Intelligence and Ambient Technologies

AI scribes have shown promise in reducing clinical documentation burden and have been well studied in primary and outpatient settings. In orthopaedic surgery, early application of AI has focused on clinical decision making, image interpretation, and outcome predictions.1,4,6 Several studies have evaluated the accuracy of predicting postoperative outcomes in joint arthroplasty and spine surgery, with models showing strong performance in predicting complications, readmissions, and recovery.2,23 Other work has highlighted the role of AI in imaging analysis, showing that deep learning systems can reliably detect fractures, classify osteoarthritis, and accurately measure alignments and implant positioning compared with expert clinicians.1,6 These applications highlights AI's potential to handle complex orthopaedic data. Despite advances in radiographic analysis and outcome prediction, little work has addressed the documentation burden unique to orthopaedics.

Several commercial platforms have emerged to address documentation burden, although most share core capabilities such as HIPAA or SOC-2 compliance, and EHR integration. For orthopaedic practice, however, AI scribes must also reliably capture unique fields that are included in notes such as mechanism of injury, physical examination maneuvers, imaging findings, implant nomenclature, coding specificity, and insurance requirements for surgical authorization. This gap highlights an opportunity for ambient AI scribes to improve efficiency and accuracy in orthopaedic settings.

Commercial AI Scribe Systems

Abridge

Abridge was founded in 2018 and develops AI solutions for healthcare communications. This platform uses a contextual reasoning engine to convert speech to test, then applies AI summarization to generates structured notes.24 It is multilingual and has been deployed in large institutions, such as Kaiser Permanente.24 Although it can be used in many care settings, it does not advertise specialty-specific templates. Pricing is not listed publicly; however, estimates suggest approximately $250 per provider per month.24 Some potential limitations are that without specialty tailoring, some niche surgical workflows may require manual editing.

DeepScribe

DeepScribe, founded in 2017, uses deep learning speech-to-text and medical natural language processing in a human-in-the-loop model.25 The system automatically generates notes, and human reviewers can perform quality checks for accuracy. It supports patient conversations in more than 25 languages.25 The company specifically highlights services to three specialties oncology, cardiology, and orthopaedic surgery, offering SOAP notes tailored for each field, along with real-time coding capture.25 Third-party sources estimate costs of $300+ per provider per month. It offers a 14-day trials.25 Human reviews may introduce a slight delay before final notes are available, although the benefit for higher accuracy can outweigh the slight delay.

Nabla

Nabla is a French-origin cloud-based AI scribe that was founded in 2018 that transcribes encounters into structured SOAP notes in real time and includes automated coding features.26 It advertises that it can create SOAP notes in less than 20 seconds and applies a zero-storage policy meaning that clinician-patient conversations are never stored on the company servers.26 This platform costs approximately $119–$120 per provider per month with available free trial, making it a more affordable option with lower barrier to entry for smaller clinics.26 Although it works across specialties, it lacks deep customization for fields, such as orthopaedic surgery. As a smaller company, customer support may be less robust than that of its larger competitors.

Nuance DAX

Nuance was founded in 1992, and acquired by Microsoft in 2022, and is a leader in speech recognition solutions. Nuance Dax (Dragon Ambient eXperience) uses the Dragon Medical Engine with AI and machine learning to transcribe and summarize clinical encounters.27 Earlier versions included human review for quality control, but the current system is fully AI driven.27 It is designed for large-scale deployment across multiple specialties and large institutions. Reported pricing range from $500 to $800 per provider per month.27 Although it can be used across many specialties, its strength lies in large-scale deployment, which can be helpful in larger institutions or busy training centers. Some of limitation is its high expense and steep learning curve, which may be challenging for smaller, independent clinics.

OrthoScribe

OrthoScribe was developed in 2023 by an orthopaedic surgeon in partnership with Hannibal AI. It is a specialty AI scribe for orthopaedic surgery that provides musculoskeletal-specific templates, device-agnostic access, and even Apple Watch integration for hands-free recording.28 The platform develops automated billing capture to help reduce missed charges and streamline coding.28 It uses voice AI developed in partnership with Hannibal AI to structure notes according to orthopaedic workflows, such as imaging findings or surgical steps.28 OrthoScribe costs approximately $500 per provider per month.28 Although it is highly tailored to orthopaedics, it is less suited to practices outside this specialty.

Suki Assistant

Suki AI was founded in 2017 in California, and it builds AI-powered voice assistances for health care.29 It provides an AI-powered voice assistant that provides ambient documentation and voice-activated commands for EHR navigation.29 Suki works across multiple specialties, offering note templates for primary care, internal medicine, pediatrics, and specialty care.29 Although it is unclear on the website the types of specialty cares. It supports English-only functionality, focusing on US-based clinical use cases. The cost was reported to be approximately $199 per provider per month.29 Some possible limitations include its smaller footprint in large hospital systems compared with competitors, and the lack of advanced specialty-specific customization for fields such as orthopaedic surgery.

Taken together, these systems demonstrate the rapid growth of AI scribes across medicine. Among these different AI scribes, Nabla had the cheaper monthly cost, Nuance Dax was best suited for large scale deployment, and OrthoScribe was the most specific to orthopaedic surgery (Table 1). These tool shares some similarities; however, their costs, scalability, and limited specialty tailoring highlights the importance of carefully evaluating which tools best fit into specific orthopaedic institutions and their needs.

Discussion

Implementation of AI Scribes: Practical Considerations

The adoption of AI scribes in orthopaedic practice requires careful planning to ensure smooth integration into existing workflows. These systems are designed to connect directly into EHRs capturing conversations in real time and producing structured notes for clinicians to review. Studies in ambulatory settings have shown reduction in after-hour documentation, improved same-day note completion, and greater time available for patient interactions.30-33 For example, a quality-improvement study that was conducted at Stanford Health Care involving 49 physicians over 3 months demonstrated a notable reduction in task load, burnout, along with moderate improvement in usability.32 Similarly, in a prospective study across eight ambulatory specialties, 45 physicians used an ambient AI scribe in more than half of their encounters. Objective EHR metrics showed reductions in note-writing time, daily documentation time, after-hours work, and overall EHR, underscoring the measurable time savings these systems can deliver at scale.31 It is important to highlight that most of the evidence comes from outpatient or primary care setting rather orthopaedic surgery specifically. Despite these benefits, implementation presents with several challenges such as consent and privacy policies, workflow design, maintaining of accuracy, billing and economics, training, and evaluation metrics that need to be considered carefully.

Consent and Privacy

The use of ambient AI scribes raises important ethnical concerns, such as privacy, transparency, algorithmic bias, and professional accountability. Although most of the commercial platforms highlight HIPAA and SOC-2 compliance as a safeguard measure, patients may still have reservations about the way the data are used and stored. A recent quality-improving study found that most patients expressed comfort with ambient documentation.34 However, willingness to consent decreased with more details about AI features, data storage, and corporate involvement was disclosed.34 In this study, participants highlighted the importance of transparency, opt-out options, and clear policies on data handling.34,35 Thus, addressing and recognizing these potential ethical concerns is critical.

The first ethnical concerns involved informed consent, especially in the setting of complex and difficult to understand AI systems.36 AI technologies challenged the standard informed consent processes because providers themselves may not fully understand how these tools generate outputs. This complicates disclosure of risks, error rates, and system limitation to patients.36,37 A second concern relates to privacy and data governance. AI scribes rely on continuous audio capture and cloud-based processing, and this intensifies privacy and data consent concerns, especially when large amounts of patient data are collected, stored, and processed.38 Transparency must address whether audio recordings are stored or deleted after processing, who owns the data, and what safeguards are in place. Kiseleva et al39 frame transparency as a multilayered system of accountability rather than surface-level disclosure, requiring institutional governance beyond vendor assurances. Another ethical concern is algorithmic bias, which is defined as the compounding of existing inequities through algorithmic deployment in health systems.40 In other words, if ambient AI systems are trained on nonrepresentative speech patterns, accents, or communication styles, documentation accuracy will vary across racial, linguistics, and socioeconomic groups. This may result in inaccurate transcription, potentially affecting disproportionally underrepresented populations, which could have downstream effects such as coding and medicolegal.38,40 Finally, professional accountability remains another central issue. Even when AI scribes assist in notes, physicians hold ultimate responsibility for documentation accuracy. Overreliance on automated summaries may risk erosion of clinical vigilance and increased transcription errors. Ethical deployment therefore requires preserving clinician oversight as the final safeguard.41

Effectively communicating these various concerns to patients is paramount, and communication best practices will continue to evolve as the AI landscape advances. In the current literature, recommendations include clearly disclosing AI use to patients, delineating AI's note in documentation, and the clinician's role in reviewing, editing, and validating those notes.36,39 Patient should receive transparency about where their data go, and what happens to it after the visit. Risks such as transcription errors and privacy concerns should be disclosed, and finally, patients should have the opportunity to opt out.36,39 Communicating this information should not only be done through intake forms but also through verbal disclosure.36,39 Informing patients of these risks is important to enhance trust and protect privacy.36-41

Workflow Design

The integration of AI scribes into busy clinical spaces is another key issue that needs consideration. Institutions must decide when recording starts and who initiates it, for example, medical assistants versus physicians. In addition, there needs to be a system in place for reviewing notes before finalizing them into the EHRs. Orthopaedic surgery, in particular, must account for high patient volumes, image-driven encounters, and presurgery authorization steps needed for insurance.

Accuracy

Maintaining documentation accuracy remain an important challenge. Some recent studies found that 70% of AI-generated notes contained errors, most commonly omissions reinforcing the need for human oversight.42-45 Providers and medical administration must also guard against automation bias, where clinicians may overrely on AI outputs simply because they appear correct. Without a robust system of checks and balances, inaccurate or incomplete notes can affect or compromise downstream clinical decision making, billing, and compliance with medicolegal standards.

Billing and Coding

Documentation accuracy is also important because it is essential for coding specificity and reimbursement. Although some AI scribe platforms incorporate automated coding support, this can vary across states and institutions. In orthopaedic surgery, this is particularly important given the complexity of implant documentation, procedural coding, and insurance authorization requirements. Documentation accuracy is essential not only for clinical communication but also for reimbursement and regulatory compliance. In a recent retrospective study of 500 surgical cases, final coder review resulted in a 161% increase in total CPT code submission compared with surgeon draft coding.46 These findings suggest that incomplete or nonspecific surgical documentation may contribute to substantial revenue leakage and administrative inefficacy. Furthermore, administrative costs accounts for approximately 25 to 31% of total healthcare spending, with the majority attributable to billing and insurance related takes.47 A recent study evaluating AI technologies for CPT generation demonstrated that AI systems can generate correct or partially correct CPT codes in large proportion of cases, suggesting that AI scribes with these features can be used as adjunctive tools for improving billing and coding completeness.47

Economics

The economic implications of AI scribes are notable. Subscription costs often exceed several hundred dollars per provider per month, and upfront training and system integration adds to the financial burden. Depending on the size and resources available across institutions, sustainability might vary. Financial feasibility will ultimately depend on balancing these costs against the potential gains of AI scribes in efficiency, reduced provider burnout, and longer clinician retention.

When evaluating cost, ambient AI scribes must be compared against existing documentation modalities rather considering them in isolation. The standard human transcript services operate on a per-word pricing model, usually beginning at appropriately $0.02 per word and increasing to 0.05 per word for expedited turnaround.19 Assuming an average orthopaedic clinic note of 1000 words, this translates to $20 per encounter for the standard rate and $50 per encounter for the expedited service. For a surgeon seeing 20 patient per day, transcription costs may reach $400 to $1000 per day. Over three clinic days per week, and 48 working weeks per year, this may total approximately to $57,600 annually at the standard rate and up to $144,000 annually at the expiated rate. Although these transcription serves provide high linguistic accuracy and HIPAA compliance, the per word structure becomes financially substantial in high-volume practices, especially when scaled across departments.

Furthermore, in person or remote medical scribes represent another potential solution. The average medical scribe salary is approximately $41,492 per year.21 In addition to the salary, there are onboarding, training, and administrative overhead costs, and this cost exceeds the annual subscription price of most ambient AI platforms. In a 10-surgeon department, multiple scribes may be required, adding to the overall annual costs. Scribes provide real-time EHR navigation and can be trained in orthopaedic terminology; however, high turnover rates and recurring recruitment cycles can create additional operation strain.

Frontend speech recognition software offers a lower cost alternative. Subscription pricing can range from $79 to $99 per month, with a one-time setup fee of $525.22 Annual costs therefore range from approximately $948 to $1,188, with first-year expenses between $1473 and $1713 when setup is included. Although this option may be financially accessible, these platforms rely entirely on physicians to generate dictation and do not automate summarization or ambient capture of encounters. As a result, documentation efforts remain physician dependent, and cognitive workload may not meaningfully decrease despite lower subscription costs.

By contrast, commercial ambient AI scribe platforms operate within a broader subscription range. Based on current estimates, annual costs per provider range from approximately $1440 (for lower costing systems around $120 per month) to $9600 (for enterprise platforms costing up to $800 per month).24-29 Midrange platforms fall between $2388 and $6000 annually depending on vendor selection.24-29 For a 30-surgeon orthopaedic department, total annual costs therefore range from approximately $43,200 to $288,000. Although not insignificant, these figures remain lower compared with modeled transcriptions costs at scale and may be comparable to or lower than employing multiple full-time medical scribes (Table 2).

Direct subscription costs represent only part of the economic equation. Orthopaedic surgery carries substantial documentation demands, including imaging interpretation, procedure planning, surgical reports, and postoperative follow-up. Burnout prevalence among orthopaedic surgeon approaches 49.8%.48 Documentation burden and cognitive load are well-recognized contributors to emotional exhaustion. Ambident AI documentation has shown measurable reduction in cognitive load. A recent prospective study found that ambient AI platform was associated with 60.7% reduction in composite cognitive load, with notable decreases in effort, mental demand, and temporal demand.49 Given the well-known association between cognitive load and burnout risk, these finding provide a plausible mechanism by which documentation focused AI tools may improve provider well being.

Ultimately, the financial justification of ambient AI scribes is context dependent. In high-volume orthopaedic centers currently relying on per-word transcription or dedicated scribes, subscription-based AI system may represent a comparatively lower annual cost while improving workflow efficiency. Conversely, in lower-volume settings where frontend dictation is sufficient, incremental benefits may be smaller. Institution leaders and stakeholders should evaluate adoption based on documentation costs, clinic volume, burnout prevalence, and turnover risk. Ambident AI scribes should therefore be viewed not solely as documentation tools but as workflow optimization technologies.

Training and Education

Effective training is critical for both clinician and support staff in the implementation and daily use of AI scribes. An additional factor to consider in orthopaedic practice is the role of trainees. Although AI scribes may alleviate the administrative workload for residents and medical students, excessive dependence could potentially hinder their learning experience. Institution and learning centers must balance efficiency with educational value, ensuring that trainees are still gaining those important skills associated with documentation such as effective communication and clinical reasoning.

Evaluation Metrics

The implementation of AI scribes should be followed with both objective and subjective metrics to evaluate its impacts and usefulness. Having quality-improvement assessment examines standard outcomes such as documentation time, after-hour work, note completeness, and quality. In addition, patient experiences, trainee perspectives, and medicolegal review of documentation accuracy are all critical measures. A comprehensive evaluation strategy will help determine whether AI scribes improved clinical experience for physicians, trainees, and patients. Ultimately, effective implementation of AI scribes in orthopaedics will depend on balancing the benefits of efficiency with safeguards that ensure accuracy, maintain clinician oversight, and preserve patient trust.

Future Directions and Research Priorities

AI scribes are already being used in multiple specialties, yet there remains a notable need for research in orthopaedic surgery. Currently published studies have focused on primary care or general hospital settings, leaving a gap in understanding how these tools perform with musculoskeletal language, operative note documentation, and imaging-driven visits. Future studies should begin with pilot projects in orthopaedic clinics and operating rooms, followed by large multi-institutional evaluations assessing accuracy, completeness, and workflow impacts.

Furthermore, there are economic considerations that require future exploration such as comparative analyses of AI scribes versus specialty-trained medical scribes could help clarify whether these tools serve as supplement or a full replacement for standard scribes services. In addition, cost-effectiveness studies are especially warranted given the wide range of platforms available and the diversity in orthopaedic practices. Research should also address scalability and equitable implementation in smaller practices with fewer resources.

Finally, ethical and regulatory questions must be prioritized. Transparency in AI use, and liability in the event of documentation errors both require the development of clear governance structures and systems to maintain clinical trust and patient safety. There should be longitudinal studies that focus on the downstream effects of AI scribes on patient outcomes, trainee education, and provider well being, and these will shape sustainable integration into orthopaedic care.
