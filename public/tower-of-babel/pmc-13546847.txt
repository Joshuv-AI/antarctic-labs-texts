Effects of calcium-regulating pulsed electromagnetic field combined with low-load blood flow restriction training on strength and performance adaptations in adolescent sprinters
Authors: Wenhao Li, Ran Huo, Kun Yan, Fucheng Wang, Xiupeng Li, Jiyuan Suo, Jiakai Tang, Lupei Ding, Xinran Du, Shiqiang Sun, Jiacheng Chen, Zhongshan Li, Mingyue Yin, Weiqian Kong, Shi Bai, Olivier Girard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhen recovery capacity is limited during the competitive season, low-load blood flow restriction training (LL-BFR) is increasingly used as an alternative to high-load resistance training (HL-RT). This study examined whether combining calcium-regulating pulsed electromagnetic field (Ca-R-PEMF) with LL-BFR further enhances maximal strength and its transfer to jumping and anaerobic performance.MethodsForty-six adolescent sprinters were randomly assigned to high-load resistance training [80% one-repetition maximum (1RM)], LL-BFR [30% 1RM; 30-15-15-15 repetitions; cuff pressure = 80% limb occlusion pressure], or Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR. All groups completed three sessions per week for four weeks. The primary outcome was 1RM, and secondary outcomes included lower-limb maximal voluntary isometric contraction (MVIC), jumping performance, and 30-s Wingate-derived outcomes. Between-group effects were analyzed using baseline-adjusted ANCOVA.ResultsAfter baseline adjustment, a significant between-group effect was observed for 1RM. HL-RT produced greater gains than LL-BFR (Adjusted Mean Difference = 12.20 kg, 95% CI: 4.11 to 20.29; p = 0.002), while Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR did not differ statistically from HL-RT (p = 0.243). For knee extensor MVIC, Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR exceeded LL-BFR (p = 0.033) and did not differ statistically from HL-RT; for plantar flexor MVIC, Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR exceeded HL-RT (p = 0.005). Squat jump height improved more with Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR than LL-BFR (p = 0.017), whereas no clear between-group effects were observed for countermovement jump height or standing broad jump distance. Wingate peak power was higher with Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR than LL-BFR (p = 0.011), while both BFR conditions showed lower fatigue indices than HL-RT.ConclusionOver four weeks, Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR did not differ statistically from HL-RT for 1RM, and improved selected isometric strength measures, jump, and 30-s Wingate-derived outcomes compared with LL-BFR alone, without increasing external load in adolescent sprinters.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2300078947). Ethics approval was granted by the Bioethics and Medical Ethics Committee of Northeastern University (NEU-EC-2025B069).Keywords: Sprinting, Blood flow restriction, Pulsed electromagnetic field stimulation, Adolescent sprinters, Maximal strength, In-season load management

Introduction

Maximal sprint velocity depends on the ability to generate substantial ground reaction forces within very brief ground contact times.1,2 These neuro-mechanical capabilities are largely underpinned by well-developed maximal lower-limb strength, particularly through the effective recruitment of prime movers.1,3,4 In most training contexts, these adaptations are achieved through high-force contractions and substantial neural drive.5 However, in adolescent athletes, dense competition schedules and accumulated in-season fatigue often limit recovery, increasing the likelihood of a mismatch between training stimulus and recovery capacity.4 Under such circumstances,6,7 further increases in external resistance-training load may impair speed development and elevate injury risk.8, 9, 10 Therefore, identifying strategies that enhance strength and neuromuscular performance without a corresponding increase in external load represents a key challenge in in-season load management.

Blood flow restriction (BFR) is commonly regarded as an alternative when external training loads are constrained. Low-load BFR (LL-BFR) induces substantial metabolic stress and local hypoxia, even at relatively low external loads, thereby promoting high-threshold motor unit recruitment.11,12 Consequently, LL-BFR generally produces greater strength gains than conventional low-load resistance training,13 nevertheless, systematic reviews indicate that LL-BFR remains inferior to high-load resistance training (HL-RT) for maximal strength and explosive power outcomes,14, 15, 16, 17 likely because HL-RT requires higher-force contractions and greater neural drive than LL-BFR.18 In contrast, LL-BFR relies primarily on accentuated metabolic stress to boost the training stimulus. Although this may enhance motor unit recruitment as fatigue develops, it may not fully replicate the high-force contractions and corresponding neural drive associated with HL-RT.19,20 Moreover, some studies in team-sport athletes report limited improvements in sprint or jumping performance with LL-BFR.21 Therefore, enhancing the transfer of LL-BFR adaptations to sport performance remains a major challenge when external load is restricted. To address this limitation, adjunctive physical modalities have been explored.22, 23, 24, 25

Among these approaches, calcium-regulating pulsed electromagnetic field (Ca-R-PEMF) is a form of pulsed electromagnetic field stimulation applied non-invasively via an external coil over the target limb.26,27 Its non-invasive, imperceptible, and tissue-penetrating characteristics have generated interest as a potential tool to enhance skeletal muscle recovery and function.28 Prior experimental evidence has reported that parameter-specific PEMF exposure (1.5 mT, 3300 Hz) may influence calcium (Ca2+)-related signaling, including transient receptor potential canonical 1 (TRPC1)-mediated Ca2+ handling.28,29 These responses have also been linked to mitochondrial regulation and post-exercise adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and phosphocreatine resynthesis (PCr) recovery.30,31 In this context, Ca-R-PEMF may serve as a potential adjunct to LL-BFR through putative Ca2+-related processes, potentially contributing to the management of transient energetic and contractile limitations associated with metabolically demanding LL-BFR sessions.32,33 Given the relevance of rapid ATP and PCr resynthesis to short-duration sprint performance,34,35 Wingate-derived outcomes provide a practical laboratory-based assessment of these capacities.36,37

Previous studies suggest that PEMF exposure may improve muscle mitochondrial bioenergetics during post-surgical recovery,31 increase elbow flexor maximal voluntary isometric contraction (MVIC) and favorable morphological adaptations in healthy adults,38 and modulate adenosine triphosphate-related and force-transmission pathways in an animal model of tendinopathy.39 However, these findings are mainly derived from rehabilitation settings, adult or older-individual populations, and animal models, which limits their transferability to adolescent performance training.38, 39, 40 Specifically, it is unknown whether adding Ca-R-PEMF to an otherwise identical LL-BFR stimulus provides benefits beyond LL-BFR alone for maximal strength, jumping performance, and anaerobic power, and whether any such responses approach those achieved with HL-RT.

Accordingly, this 4-week randomized training intervention compared Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR, LL-BFR alone, and HL-RT in adolescent sprinters. Outcomes included maximal strength (1RM), lower-limb isometric strength measures, jumping performance, and Wingate-derived anaerobic power indices. We hypothesized that, under equivalent low-load conditions, adding Ca-R-PEMF to LL-BFR would produce greater improvements than LL-BFR alone and elicit responses in selected outcomes not significantly different from HL-RT.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited from middle-school sprint squads (100 m and 200 m) through coach referral and team-based screening. Based on the framework of McKay et al.,41 and their structured sprint-specific training and competitive involvement, they were classified as Tier 2-3 athletes. Inclusion criteria were: (1) post-peak height velocity adolescent sprinters meeting Chinese National Level II standard or above (200 m < 22.35 s; 100 m < 11.24 s); (2) regular resistance training experience (≥2 sessions/week for ≥ 3 years); (3) no current lower-limb injury and musculoskeletal injury within the previous 3 months; and (4) no previous exposure to magnetic field or blood flow restriction training. Exclusion criteria were: (1) lumbar, cervical, or joint injury within the previous 3 months, history of magnetic vertigo, or recent antibiotic use; and (2) presence of implantable devices or metallic foreign bodies (i.e., pacemakers, stents) that could be affected by magnetic fields.

Before participant recruitment, an a priori sample-size calculation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7) for a one-way ANOVA for three-group comparisons (α = 0.05, power = 0.80, and Cohen's f = 0.50). The assumed effect size was based on prior training-intervention evidence in athletic populations,16,17 while confirmatory trial analyses were later performed using ANCOVA with baseline adjustment. The target sample size was 42 participants (14 per group). To account for potential attrition, 54 adolescent sprinters were initially recruited. After medical screening, 46 met the inclusion criteria and completed baseline testing (n = 3 excluded for lower-limb musculoskeletal injury; n = 5 for illness or refusal). All participants were fully informed of study procedures, risks, and their right to withdraw at any time.

Written informed consent was then obtained from both the participants and their guardians before randomization and intervention. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, received institutional ethics approval (NEU-EC-2025B069), and was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2300078947).

Study design and blinding

This three-arm randomized comparative intervention trial evaluated the effects of Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR, compared with LL-BFR alone and HL-RT on lower-limb neuromuscular performance and anaerobic power-related outcomes in adolescent sprinters. An independent researcher generated the allocation sequence using computer-based randomization, with allocation concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes managed by a third party. Participants were assigned to three groups: HL-RT, LL-BFR + sham Ca-R-PEMF (LL-BFR), and Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR. Intervention protocols and testing procedures are shown in Fig. 1. Blinding of participants and coaches was not feasible due to differences in training mode and external load between HL-RT and LL-BFR. However, outcome assessors and the statistician remained blinded. For the magnetic stimulation component, participants in the LL-BFR and Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR groups used identical devices (e.g., appearance, interface, coil structure, indicator lights, and operating sounds) and followed the same procedures (i.e., supine position, lower limbs centered within the coil, remaining still for 15 min). The Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR group received active magnetic stimulation, whereas the LL-BFR + sham Ca-R-PEMF group received sham stimulation where the coil remained inactive (effective field output = 0 mT). Device settings were configured and concealed by an independent researcher not involved in testing or statistical analysis, ensuring that both participants and assessors remained unaware of group allocation for the stimulation condition.

Intervention protocols

Across all resistance-training conditions, the squat exercise was standardized as a full-squat movement, and the same range-of-motion criterion was used during both 1RM testing and training. This full-squat variation was selected to maintain consistency across loading conditions and because previous evidence indicates that full-squat can elicit greater neuromuscular, functional, strength, and lower-limb hypertrophy adaptations than partial-squat or partial-range-of-motion training.42,43

Low-load blood flow restriction training

LL-BFR consisted of loaded full-depth squats performed at 30% 1RM for four sets (30-15-15-15; 60 s rest). A dedicated pneumatic blood-flow restriction training device (KAATSU Master, Sato Sports Plaza, Tokyo, Japan) with 8-cm-wide cuffs were applied bilaterally to the proximal thighs (upper third of each thigh, perpendicular to the long axis of the femur). Cuffs were inflated before each set, released during inter-set recovery, and re-inflated before subsequent sets. All sessions were supervised by the same strength and conditioning coach to ensure consistent technique, tempo, and rest intervals.

Lower-limb occlusion pressure (LOP) was determined during baseline testing using the same automated pneumatic BFR device equipped with built-in LOP detection, 8-cm-wide cuffs, and proximal-thigh cuff placement as those used during training. Participants were assessed in a standardized supine position after 5 min of quiet rest, with both lower limbs fully relaxed and extended. The device automatically increased cuff pressure in stepwise increments according to the manufacturer's LOP assessment algorithm until complete arterial occlusion was detected based on arterial pulse characteristics within the cuff. The minimum pressure required to achieve complete arterial occlusion was recorded as each participant's individualized LOP. During training, cuff pressure was set at 80% of each participant's individualized LOP to provide a standardized relative BFR stimulus across participants.44 Individual LOP values and the corresponding training cuff pressures are presented in Table 2.

Calcium-regulating pulsed electromagnetic field

Ca-R-PEMF was delivered using a low-frequency pulsed magnetic therapy device (DM-MED1, Shenyang Dami Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Shenyang, China; Fig. 2A). In the combined-intervention group, stimulation was applied immediately after each LL-BFR session. Sessions lasted 15 min, were performed three times per week for four weeks with approximately 48 h between sessions, and used the manufacturer's preset Ca-R-PEMF program. The nominal magnetic field intensity was 1.5 mT at 3300 Hz, consistent with previous work.39 This parameter-specific protocol was selected based on prior Ca-R-PEMF evidence suggesting potential effects on Ca2+-related signaling and post-exercise energy-restoration processes.26, 27, 28 The coil system had an inner diameter of 800 mm and a centre-to-centre spacing of 400 mm, allowing both lower limbs to be positioned within the central stimulation region. The magnetic field distribution and coil configuration are illustrated in Fig. 2B and C. Participants lay supine with both lower limbs centered within the coil and remained still throughout the session.

Ca-R-PEMF device, coil configuration, and magnetic field distribution.

According to the manufacturer's technical specifications, the pulsed output had a single-pulse duration of 300 μs, a pulse-train duration of 6 ms, and a cycle period of 66 ms, corresponding to an approximate duty cycle of 9.1%. Magnetic field output and waveform characteristics were checked using a Gaussmeter connected to an oscilloscope, and magnetic field intensity was converted from the voltage signal using the manufacturer-specified ratio of 100 mV = 1 mT. The selected parameters were within established safety ranges.26, 27, 28 No intervention-related adverse events were reported, and supervised attendance records indicated >95% compliance.

To preserve participant blinding, sham stimulation followed the same procedures, including the same device, body position, coil placement, indicator lights, operating procedures, and session duration, but delivered no effective electromagnetic output (0 mT). Because the stimulation was non-invasive and imperceptible, this sham procedure was designed to minimize differences in treatment experience between the active and sham stimulation conditions. No formal post-intervention assessment of blinding success was conducted.

High-load resistance training

The HL-RT protocol consisted of barbell squats at 80% 1RM (4 sets of 8 repetitions, 3 min rest).44 Participants placed the barbell across the upper back, kept a neutral head position and forward gaze, and stood with feet shoulder-width apart. Full-depth squats were performed using a standardized full range of motion, followed by a rapid concentric phase involving simultaneous hip and knee extension.

Standardization of concomitant training

To isolate the effects of the assigned strength intervention, all athletes followed the same coach-prescribed in-season sprint-training and recovery schedule throughout the 4-week intervention. The only between-group difference was the allocated modality: HL-RT, LL-BFR with sham stimulation, or Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR.

The weekly program included six training days with two sessions per day (12 sessions per week), comprising eight sprint-specific sessions, one technical session, and three strength sessions aligned with group allocation. Training exposure and adherence were monitored throughout the intervention and are summarized in Table 1. All groups completed a comparable training exposure of approximately 18 h/week, with resistance-training adherence exceeding 95% and running/sprint-training adherence exceeding 90% across groups. Ca-R-PEMF active and sham stimulation adherence exceeded 95% and was comparable between the two low-load BFR groups.

The resistance-training protocols were not performed to task failure; sets were completed with proper technique and discontinued if range of motion or technical quality could not be maintained. No intervention-related adverse events were reported, and participants were instructed to avoid additional structured lower-limb resistance or high-intensity training while maintaining their usual diet and sleep routines.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was 1RM. Secondary outcomes included MVIC, jumping performance, and Wingate-derived outcomes. All outcomes were assessed at baseline and post-intervention in a fixed order using standardized procedures. Before each test session, participants completed the corresponding standardized warm-up. Testing was conducted over three separate days at both time points: 1RM and jumping performance on Day 1, the 30-s Wingate test on Day 2, and lower-limb MVIC testing on Day 3. This order was used to reduce fatigue carryover between maximal tests and was kept identical at baseline and post-intervention. For each participant, baseline and post-intervention assessments for the corresponding test day were conducted at the same time of day. Assessments were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions (20°C; 50% relative humidity). Participants were instructed to avoid strenuous exercise and to refrain from caffeine and alcohol for 24 h before testing.

Maximal strength assessments

Maximal dynamic strength was assessed using 1RM testing following NSCA recommendations.5 After a progressive warm-up 10 repetitions with a light load (1 min rest), followed by 3-5 repetitions (2 min rest), and then 2-3 near-maximal repetitions (2-4 min rest) participants then performed single 1RM attempts with 2-4 min rest. Load was progressively increased (∼10-20% initially, smaller increments near maximum) until the heaviest successful lift was achieved and recorded as 1RM.

MVIC torque (N·m) of the quadriceps, hamstrings, and plantar flexors was measured using an isokinetic dynamometer (Biodex System 4 Pro, Biodex Medical Systems, Shirley, NY, USA). After a standardized warm-up (5 min cycling, 3 min dynamic stretching), participants completed three 5-s maximal efforts for each muscle group. Quadriceps was tested seated with the hip flexed to ∼90°and the knee flexed at 60° (0° = full extension). The dynamometer axis was aligned with the lateral femoral condyle, and the lower leg was secured above the ankle. Participants performed three 5-s maximal knee extensor efforts, separated by 2-3 min of rest. For hamstrings, participants lay prone with the pelvis and thigh stabilized, and the knee flexed to 60°. Three 5-s maximal voluntary knee flexion efforts were performed, with 2-3 min of rest between trials. For plantar flexors, participants were seated with the knee flexed to 90° and the ankle held in a neutral position (90°). The dynamometer axis was aligned with the lateral malleolus. Participants performed three 5-s maximal plantarflexion efforts with at least 1 min rest between trials. Standardized verbal encouragement was provided throughout, and gravity correction was applied. The highest peak torque was retained for analysis.

Vertical and horizontal jumping performance

Vertical jumping performance was assessed using dual force plates (FD 4000, Vald Performance, Australia), with jump height calculated from flight time. After two practice trials, participants completed three maximal attempts with 3 min rest; the best value was retained. During the CMJ, participants kept their hands on their hips, rapidly descended to ∼90° knee flexion, and immediately performed a maximal vertical jump. During the SJ, participants held a static ∼90° knee flexion for 2 s before take-off to minimize countermovement contribution. Horizontal jumping performance was assessed using the standing broad jump (SBJ) on a standard mat (Livex, China). Participants stood with feet parallel and executed a maximal forward jump with an arm swing, stabilizing the landing for ≥2 s. Three trials were completed with 1 min rest. Trials were considered invalid if balance was lost or landing was not on both feet. The longest valid distance was used for analysis.

Wingate test

Anaerobic performance was assessed with a 30-s Wingate test on a cycle ergometer (Monark 894E, Sweden), with resistance set at 7.5% of body mass. Following a 5-min unloaded warm-up and system calibration, participants were instructed to pedal maximally, with resistance applied automatically once the target cadence was reached. Standardized verbal encouragement was provided throughout. Outcomes included peak power (PP), minimum power (MinP), and fatigue index (FI), calculated as: FI = (PP − MinP)/PP × 100.37

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 24.0). Tests were two-sided, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Within-group pre-to-post changes were assessed using paired t-tests (normally distributed data) or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (normality assumption not met), with effect sizes reported as Cohen's d. Between-group effects were examined using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), with post-intervention values entered as the dependent variables, group as the fixed factor, and baseline values as covariates. Homogeneity of regression slopes was tested via the Group × Baseline interaction. When a significant group effect was detected, Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons were performed, reporting adjusted mean differences (AMD), 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), adjusted p values, and partial eta squared (ηp2). Residual normality was checked for model diagnostics. Percentage change was calculated as Δ% = [(Post − Pre)/Pre] × 100. Exploratory within-group associations were examined using baseline-adjusted (residualized) percentage change scores. Pearson correlations were performed with false discovery rate correction applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Separate ordinary least-squares regression models were fitted within each group, with Δ%PP or Δ%SJ as dependent variables, Δ%1RM as the predictor, and the corresponding baseline value included as a covariate.

Results

Participant characteristics and adherence

A total of 54 athletes were recruited. After screening, 8 were excluded before randomization, leaving 46 participants randomly allocated to HL-RT (n = 15), LL-BFR (n = 15), or Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR (n = 16) (see Fig. 3). Training attendance was documented in the training logs, with no intervention-related adverse events or injuries reported. Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 2. No significant group × baseline interactions were observed for lower-limb MVIC, 1RM, jumping performance (CMJ, SJ, and SBJ), or anaerobic power indices (PP, MinP, and FI) (all p > 0.05), supporting the homogeneity of regression slopes assumption for ANCOVA.

Changes in maximal strength

After baseline adjustment, 1RM showed a significant between-group effect (p = 0.002, ηp2 = 0.252; Table 3). HL-RT elicited greater increases than LL-BFR (AMD = 12.20 kg, 95% CI 4.11-20.29; p = 0.002), while no difference was noted between HL-RT and Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR (p = 0.243). Within-group increases in 1RM were significant in HL-RT (p = 0.003, d = 1.325) and Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR (p = 0.002, d = 0.939).

Changes in vertical and horizontal jumping performance

Jumping performance outcomes are summarized in Table 3. After baseline adjustment, no clear between-group effect was observed for CMJ (p = 0.094, ηp2 = 0.106; Table 3). Within-group analyses showed significant increases in CMJ in HL-RT (p = 0.004, d = 1.183) and Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR (p = 0.005, d = 0.976), but not in LL-BFR group (p = 0.104, d = 0.480).

For SJ, a significant between-group effect was observed after baseline adjustment (p = 0.021, ηp2 = 0.169), with greater improvements for Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR than LL-BFR (p = 0.017; Table 3). Consistent with this, SJ improved in HL-RT (p = 0.021, d = 0.810) and Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR (p < 0.001, d = 1.379), but not in LL-BFR (p = 0.207, d = 0.342).

No significant between-group effect was found for SBJ (p = 0.391, ηp2 = 0.044; Table 3). Although scores increased numerically in all groups, only Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR demonstrated a significant within-group increase (p = 0.020, d = 0.648). The change in HL-RT approached significance (p = 0.051, d = 0.550), while no significant change was observed for LL-BFR (p = 0.478, d = 0.188). Discussion

To our knowledge, this is one of the first randomized studies to examine whether Ca-R-PEMF applied after LL-BFR enhances strength and performance adaptations beyond LL-BFR alone, and whether these responses approach those achieved with HL-RT in adolescent sprinters. The main finding was that, without increasing external load, Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR produced 1RM improvements that were not significantly different from HL-RT and more favorable responses than LL-BFR alone for selected secondary outcomes, including SJ and Wingate-derived outcomes. From an applied perspective, this combined approach may offer an effective lower external load strategy for in-season strength development when additional HL-RT is not feasible.

Maximal strength

These strength-related outcomes align with prior evidence indicating that HL-RT generally produces greater maximal-strength adaptations than LL-BFR,12,14,18,32 whereas LL-BFR remains capable of improving strength under low-load conditions through metabolic stress and a hypoxia-like environment.16,17,57,58 In athletic populations, BFR-based resistance training appears to improve lower-limb maximal strength, but its transfer to jumping and sprint performance is less consistent.21,57 Against this background, the present findings suggest that HL-RT produced greater 1RM improvements than LL-BFR, whereas the Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR condition was not significantly different from HL-RT, it suggests that adding Ca-R-PEMF to LL-BFR may partially reduce the strength-adaptation gap commonly observed between LL-BFR and HL-RT. For knee extensor MVIC, the combined intervention also showed a response not significantly different from HL-RT, whereas plantar flexor MVIC produced greater gains in the combined intervention than in HL-RT, possibly reflecting the sensitivity of the plantar flexor musculature to the metabolic and electromagnetic stimuli delivered by the combined protocol.26, 27, 28, 29,31,38 In contrast, knee flexor MVIC showed no between-group differences despite a within-group improvement in the combined group. These muscle-group-specific responses may relate to differences in fiber-type composition, vascular characteristics, or the relative contribution of each muscle group to the squat exercise, which warrants further investigation.12,23,32,43 Taken together, applying Ca-R-PEMF during the recovery period following LL-BFR may enhance the strength adaptations achievable with low-load training.

These selected strength-related responses occurred without increased external loading, suggesting that the combined intervention may have involved recovery- or contractile-related processes beyond the loading stimulus itself. Indirect evidence may help explain these findings. Ca-R-PEMF has been proposed to modulate skeletal muscle function by activating ion channels and promoting intracellular calcium influx.28,59 Although the present study primarily relied on macroscopic performance assessments and did not involve muscle biopsy analyses, this interpretation is supported by existing biological evidence.28,47,60, 61, 62 Based on this evidence, we cautiously speculate that Ca-R-PEMF applied after LL-BFR may have supported selected strength-related adaptations through putative Ca2+-related and anabolic pathways, including TRPC1-mediated Ca2+ handling.28,39,45, 46, 47,59, 60, 61, 62 Collectively, modulation of intracellular Ca2+-signaling networks may enhance the molecular environment for skeletal muscle adaptation following LL-BFR, offering a plausible explanation for the improvements in MVIC observed in the combined intervention. However, this interpretation remains literature-informed and should be tested directly in future studies using molecular, neuromuscular, architectural, and bioenergetic measurements.

Transfer to jumping performance

Despite improvements in maximal strength across groups, jump-related responses were not uniform across tasks. The clearest effect was observed in SJ, which suggests that the combined intervention favoured the expression of force in a concentric explosive task initiated from a static position. This is mechanistically plausible because SJ is initiated from a static position and depends primarily on rapid concentric force production without stretch-shortening cycle contribution.63 In this context, based on prior biological evidence, we cautiously speculate that Ca-R-PEMF may have facilitated Ca2+ influx through TRPC1-sensitive pathways and supported excitation-contraction processes relevant to concentric force production (see Fig. 9). This may have partly contributed to the clearer SJ response in the combined intervention, although this interpretation remains inferential. By contrast, CMJ and SBJ rely more heavily on elastic energy utilization, inter-joint coordination, and task-specific movement strategy,63, 64, 65 which are less likely to change over a 4-week period without additional plyometric or sprint-specific stimuli. This pattern is consistent with prior BFR-based work showing inconsistent transfer to jumping performance. Jump training with BFR did not improve SJ or CMJ under similar exercise intensity, whereas supplementary low-load BFR in team-sport athletes increased squat strength and muscular endurance without clear improvements in sprinting or jumping tasks.19,21 In a recent athlete-focused meta-analysis, BFR-RT improved lower-limb maximal strength but did not show clear additional benefits for jumping ability or sprint performance.57,66,67 In practical terms, these findings suggest that Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR may produce a task-specific improvement in jumping performance, primarily reflected in SJ rather than in jump tasks that rely more heavily on stretch-shortening cycle contribution. This explanation remains inferential, as no direct measures of neural drive or muscle activation (i.e., using surface electromyographic activity) were obtained.68,69

Wingate anaerobic performance

Sprint-specific performance depends not only on rapid maximal power production during acceleration but also on the ability to sustain high-intensity power output under fatigue during speed-endurance demands.70 In this context, the 30-s Wingate test provides a standardized laboratory index of anaerobic power production and fatigue tolerance by capturing maximal power output, short-term power maintenance, and power decline during all-out exercise.71 Accordingly, the Wingate findings suggest that the combined intervention did not only preserve the favorable fatigue profile typically associated with BFR but also enhanced anaerobic power.70,71 Specifically, Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR showed higher PP than LL-BFR and higher MinP than HL-RT, whereas both BFR conditions showed lower FI than HL-RT. This pattern may be partly related to fatigue-resistance adaptations associated with repeated BFR-related metabolic and ischemic stress.55 A similar Wingate-related pattern has been reported after four weeks of BFR-RST in boxers, with improvements in PP and MP and a reduction in power decrement.56 Against this background, the combined group also showed higher PP than LL-BFR and higher MinP than HL-RT. Taken together, these observations suggest that adding Ca-R-PEMF to LL-BFR preserved the fatigue-related benefits of BFR while providing additional benefits for peak power expression and short-term power maintenance; although these Wingate-derived outcomes should be interpreted as laboratory-based performance indices rather than direct evidence of improved sprint performance.

One plausible mechanism is that Ca-R-PEMF may have influenced recovery-related Ca2+ handling. During repeated high-intensity contractions, extracellular Ca2+ entry may help restore sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ availability and maintain excitation-contraction coupling.72,73 Ca-R-PEMF has been proposed to induce controlled Ca2+ influx through TRPC1 channels and influence mitochondrial regulation.28,47,74 This may have helped attenuate acidosis- and fatigue-related impairments in contractile function, thereby supporting peak power expression.72, 75 Additionally, Ca-R-PEMF likely influenced recovery-related bioenergetic processes. Calcium-dependent signaling pathways linked to PGC-1α may contribute to mitochondrial adaptations,25,26,47 supporting adenosine triphosphate production and phosphocreatine resynthesis during recovery.48, 49, 50 These effects may contribute to the more favorable MinP response observed in the combined group while preserving the fatigue-related advantages of BFR. These mechanisms remain speculative, as no direct molecular or metabolic measures were obtained in the present study.

Exploratory associations

Exploratory analyses provided preliminary evidence that changes in local strength may be related to task-level performance across intervention contexts, although the observed associations varied across intervention contexts. More specifically, in both HL-RT and Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR, changes in quadriceps MVIC were positively associated with changes in 1RM, whereas this relationship was not evident in LL-BFR alone. This exploratory pattern may indicate a potential link between local knee-extensor force-generating capacity and overall strength expression under the combined condition, typically observed with higher-load training. This interpretation is compatible with our previous work in healthy adults, where short-term Ca-R-PEMF exposure was accompanied by concordant increases in muscle cross-sectional area, pennation angle, muscle stiffness, and strength,38 and with evidence linking greater muscle stiffness to more effective force transmission within the muscle-tendon unit, which may facilitate the translation of local force-generating capacity into whole-movement strength expression.52, 53, 54 A similar pattern was seen for performance outcomes. In the combined group, gains in 1RM were more strongly associated with gains in SJ, suggesting a potential link between overall strength adaptation and concentric explosive performance under the combined group. This is relevant because SJ is initiated from a static position and relies primarily on rapid concentric force expression with no stretch-shortening cycle contribution. Changes in 1RM were also associated with changes in Wingate PP in the combined group, which may indicate that greater strength gains coincided with greater laboratory-derived high-intensity power output. These findings should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously. The analyses were exploratory and correlational, and no direct measures of muscle recruitment, muscle architecture, or molecular signaling were obtained.

Practical implications

These findings suggest that Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR may represent a viable low-load training strategy for adolescent sprinters during the competitive season, particularly when tolerance to high mechanical loads is a concern. In this study, the Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR group was not significantly different from HL-RT for 1RM under the present study conditions. BFR-based conditions also showed lower Wingate FI values than HL-RT, suggesting that the combined approach may support strength development while preserving fatigue resistance. For practitioners, this approach can support ongoing strength development while reducing the need for frequent high-load resistance training during periods of dense sprint and technical work, thereby facilitating the integration of strength work within concurrent sprint training.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. The 4-week intervention period captures only short-term adaptations and cannot determine whether the observed responses would persist, increase, or diminish over a longer training cycle or an entire competitive season. In addition, although the three-group design allowed direct comparisons, the absence of a time-matched non-exercise control group, a trained sprint-only control group, a work-matched low-load non-BFR comparator, and a Ca-R-PEMF-only condition limits the separation of the independent contributions of resistance training, BFR, and Ca-R-PEMF. This should be considered when attributing training-induced adaptations or interpreting individual response patterns in trained adolescent athletes.76,77

Another limitation concerns the comparability of post-exercise exposure between conditions. In the present study, the Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR group received an additional 15-min recovery period compared with HL-RT. This difference does not undermine the applied comparison between interventions, but it should be considered when interpreting recovery-related differences between these conditions. In the same applied context, although all participants followed the same sprint-training schedule, we did not collect objective data on accumulated training load, session-RPE, internal load responses, nutritional intake, or sleep behavior.

In addition, the absence of metabolic measures, such as blood lactate, insulin-like growth factor-1, vascular endothelial growth factor, ATP-PCr kinetics, and mitochondrial function, as well as histological, neuromuscular, architectural, and molecular assessments, limits mechanistic interpretation, which remains speculative and based on indirect evidence. Furthermore, no separate test–retest reliability assessment was conducted for the present cohort; therefore, study-specific ICC, CV, and SEM values for the outcome measures could not be reported. Moreover, although the study was adequately powered for the primary outcome, the relatively small sample size and multiple secondary analyses warrant cautious interpretation of the exploratory findings. The sample was restricted to male adolescent sprinters, limiting generalizability to female athletes, in whom hormonal and neuromuscular differences may influence responses. In addition, the present study used the 30-s Wingate test to assess anaerobic performance but did not include direct sprint-performance outcomes, acceleration measures, maximal sprint velocity, sprint kinetics, or reactive strength index. Therefore, the translation of these findings should be interpreted with caution due to biomechanical differences between cycling and overground sprinting.

Conclusion

In male adolescent sprinters during the competitive season, adding Ca-R-PEMF to LL-BFR during a 4-week intervention produced more favorable responses than LL-BFR alone in selected strength, jumping, and selected Wingate-derived outcomes, without increasing external mechanical load. The combined condition was not significantly different from HL-RT for 1RM under the present study conditions, suggesting that Ca-R-PEMF + LL-BFR may represent a useful lower-load alternative when additional high-load training is undesirable. These findings support its practical use for in-season load management, particularly when recovery resources are constrained.
