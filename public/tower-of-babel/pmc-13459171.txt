Engaging patients in the decarbonisation of primary care: findings from a cross-sectional survey in England
Authors: Naomi Klepacz, Olivia Geddes, Frederik Dahlmann, Abi Eccles, Jenny Earle, Michael Gregg, Florence Karaba, Rachel Spencer, Jeremy Dale, Ana Raquel Nunes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesPrimary care accounts for almost a quarter of the National Health Service (NHS) carbon footprint. While general practices are expected to contribute to NHS decarbonisation efforts, little is known about how patients perceive these activities.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted as part of the General Practice Net Zero (GPNET-0) study across twelve general practices in three Integrated Care Boards (ICBs) in England. Adult patients who had recently attended their general practice completed an online or paper survey assessing: awareness of climate change and health, perceptions of general practice responsibility for reducing emissions, willingness to engage in sustainability-aligned behaviours if suggested by practice staff; and perceived barriers to action. Quantitative data were analysed descriptively overall and by ICB.ResultsA total of 252 patients responded (response rate 12%). Only 6.4% agreed that their general practice has a key role in reducing greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, and 5.0% felt their general practices should take greater action. Most (82.0%) disagreed that the government should do more to incentivise practice-level decarbonisation. Few respondents felt well informed about climate change (14.0%) or its health impacts (1%). Awareness of practice-level actions such as electronic communication and medicine-return schemes was moderate, but these were rarely recognised as decarbonisation measures. Around one in four reported willingness to engage in a medication review or increase active travel if suggested by practice staff. Concerns that environmental actions might compromise the quality of their care were common (72%).ConclusionsDecarbonisation is not currently a core expectation that patients hold for general practice. Limited awareness and engagement highlight the need to frame sustainability as supporting, rather than competing with, care quality. Embedding decarbonisation within routine service improvement may be more acceptable than positioning it as a separate priority.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14807-5.

Introduction

The National Health Service (NHS) accounts for an estimated 4–5% of the UK’s greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, making it a significant contributor to the country’s carbon footprint [1, 2]. In 2020, the NHS became the world’s first health system to commit to achieving net zero emissions by 2040 [2, 3]. This commitment, set out in the Greener NHS strategy [2] and underpinned by the Health and Care Act 2022 [4], provides a clear framework for decarbonising healthcare delivery.

Primary care accounts for around 23% of NHS emissions through building energy use, prescribing (notably metered-dose inhalers), procurement, and staff and patient travel [5, 6]. The Royal College of General Practitioners (RCGP) has identified the climate emergency as a strategic priority [7], and Integrated Care Boards (ICBs) have, since 2020, been required to publish “Green Plans” outlining local strategies for decarbonisation (e.g [8]).

Patients have the potential to play a key role in decarbonising general practice. Their travel, medication use, and lifestyle behaviours account for a substantial proportion of practice-related emissions [9]. Many low-carbon actions – such as switching to lower-emission inhalers, opting for remote consultations, or returning unused medicines – depend directly on patients’ willingness and engagement. Evidence suggests that patients may be receptive to sustainability discussions when linked to personal health benefits, but less engaged when messaging feels politicised [10]. Understanding the factors that facilitate or hinder environmentally sustainable initiatives in this setting is therefore essential. Given its scale and reach, general practice has the potential to drive meaningful environmental change, provided this is pursued alongside delivery of high-quality patient-centred care [11].

Despite this, research on decarbonisation in general practice has largely focused on staff views rather than on patient views [10, 12–14]. More broadly, patient engagement in service transformation is associated with improvements in quality, safety and experiences [15], yet evidence on how to involve patients in general practice-level change remains limited [16, 17]. Patient participation groups (PPGs) provide an existing mechanism for involvement, facilitating dialogue on local priorities and service development [18]. Engaging patients in decarbonisation may therefore support the NHS’s statutory net zero commitments and the delivery of trusted and equitable care [19].

This study examines patients’ perceptions of general practice responsibilities in reducing emissions, their views on the links between climate change and health, and their willingness to engage in practice-led sustainability initiatives. The findings aim to inform strategies for embedding patient-centred, decarbonisation within routine general practice.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a cross-sectional survey of adult patients registered at 12 diverse general practices spanning 3 ICB areas in England, selected to reflect a range of rural and urban settings and levels of deprivation (see Table 1). This survey formed part of the General Practice Net Zero (GPNET-0) study, which examines the implementation and sustainability of decarbonisation actions in primary care [20].

Survey development

The survey design was informed by our systematic review [12], our survey of general practice staff views on decarbonisation [21], and previous research on patient perspectives [9–11]. The questionnaire was designed to be completed in under ten minutes to minimise participant burden. Eight items collected sociodemographic information (age, gender, ethnicity, education, health status and employment). Four sets of Likert-scale questions assessed climate change awareness, perceived responsibility of general practice for GHG reduction, and willingness to engage in pro-environmental behaviours relating to health and care delivery in general practice. Two multiple-choice items assessed awareness of general practice-level decarbonisation actions and perceived barriers to change, and one open-ended question invited additional comments. The full questionnaire is provided in Supplementary File S1.

Three items were adapted from the Yale Climate Opinion maps [22] to capture climate-related knowledge and attitudes to UK government intervention. Items were mapped to domains of the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) [23] and Normalisation Process Theory (NPT) [24], ensuring theoretical coverage of both individual-level behavioural determinants (e.g., knowledge, beliefs about consequences, social influences) and system-level implementation processes (e.g., coherence, cognitive participation). This approach reflected the wider GPNET-0 study design [20], which is informed by these frameworks to link behaviour-change theory and implementation sciences.

The draft instrument was reviewed by members of the study’s patient and public involvement (PPI) panel to assess comprehensibility, acceptability, and respondent burden. Feedback led to minor refinements to wording, question ordering and layout.

Recruitment and sampling

Each practice was asked to systematically sample and invite 175 patients (2,100 patients in total) (≤18 years) who had attended a clinical appointment (online, over the phone or in-person) at the practice within the previous five working days. General practitioners at each participating practice screened the identified patients to exclude individuals who were ineligible for ethical or clinical reasons, including: patients who had previously opted out of research, at end of life, severely unwell, or with significant cognitive impairment. Carers could complete the questionnaire on behalf of the person they cared for.

The response rate was anticipated as 20% equivalent to 30–35 patient from each practice completing the questionnaire to give a total sample of 400–420, consistent with comparable primary care patient surveys [25]. To enhance inclusion of perspectives from underserved groups, recruitment included engagement with community and faith organisations located near participating practices to encourage their members to participate in the survey. Groups were identified through practice staff, input from patient participation groups, and researcher-led identification. In a small number of practices with very low initial response, recruitment methods were broadened to include repeat survey mailouts and the use of practice-based flyers and waiting room posters inviting eligible patients to participate.

Survey administration

The survey was hosted on Qualtrics XM [26] and open from December 2024 to May 2025. Participants could complete the survey online via a secure link or QR code or request a paper version in English or translated format. Invitations were distributed by post. All invitations included a participant information sheet designed, reviewed by members of the PPI panel, and written in plain language with visual cues to support accessibility. A telephone number was provided for requesting alternative formats; no translated versions were requested, but 11 participants requested and returned paper copies.

To encourage participation, a collective incentive was offered: if ≥30 patients from a practice completed the survey, ten trees would be planted by NHS Forest on behalf of that practice. This practice-level incentive was intended to promote engagement without influencing individual responses. Completion of the survey constituted informed consent. No identifiable data were collected unless participants voluntarily provided contact details for follow-up interviews (published elsewhere); these were stored separately from survey data.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participant characteristics and survey responses overall and by ICB. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means and standard deviations. Exploratory comparisons between ICBs were undertaken for selected survey items using chi-squared tests, with Likert-scale responses collapsed into broader categories to ensure adequate cell sizes. Data completeness was assessed prior to analysis. At one practice, education level and appointment frequency were not available and were therefore included in descriptive summaries but excluded from analyses involving those variables. For other items, missingness was low and analyses were conducted on available cases.

Optional free-text responses were analysed descriptively. Comments were reviewed and grouped into broad descriptive categories to support reporting and illustration of survey findings. This process was intended to summarise the scope and nature of issues raised by respondents rather than to generate explanatory or theory-driven qualitative insights. Results presented in this paper focus on survey items aligned with the study aims and with sufficient completeness for interpretation.

Patient and public involvement

Members of the study’s Patients and public involvement (PPI) panel contributed to identifying priority domains, piloting the survey instrument, and refining item wording and layout. The PPI panel also advised on recruitment approaches to support inclusion of underserved groups and contributed to interpretation of findings through review and discussion of summary results. Two PPI co-applicants are co-authors on this paper. Reporting of PPI activities follows GRIPP2 short-form guidance [27].

Results

General practice, ICB and participant characteristics

A total of 252 patients completed the survey across three ICBs: Coventry and Warwickshire (CW, n = 140), Birmingham and Solihull (BS, n = 62), and South Yorkshire (SY, n = 28). Across the 12 participating practices, response rates varied widely, ranging from 0.6% to 34.9%. Practice-level response rates were not associated with socioeconomic deprivation, as measured by Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) score (Spearman’s ρ = 0.007, P = 0.98). Characteristics of participating practices and survey respondents ICB

Survey respondents were predominantly older adults, with almost one-third aged ≥ 75 years (n = 76; 30.2%) and a further 26% aged 65–74 years. More than half identified as female (n = 135/252; 53.6%) and over half were fully retired (n = 112/218; 51.4%). Just over half reported at least one long-term condition (n = 117/217; 53.9%). In terms of appointment frequency, most attended or contacted their practice often (2–4 times per six months; n = 109/251; 43.4%). Educational attainment was relatively high, with 51% (n = 112/218) holding a degree or higher qualification. The sample was predominantly White British (n = 220/246; 89.4%) (See Supplementary Table S2).

Although overall patterns were similar across ICBs, some differences reflected the characteristics of participating practice. Respondents in CW tended to be older and more frequently retired; those in BS were younger and more likely to hold a degree; and respondents in SY were drawn from practices serving less ethnically diverse populations.

Perceived role of general practice in reducing GHG emissions

Participants expressed mixed views about the role of general practice in reducing carbon emissions. A small proportion (6.4%) agreed that their practice had a key role, whereas 46.8% disagreed and around one-third were either neutral (28.8%) or reported not knowing (18.0%). Nearly half of respondents (49.0%) were uncertain whether their practice was already doing enough to reduce emissions, with only 4% agreeing that it was. Views on government incentivisation of general practice decarbonisation were more polarised: 11.3% agreed, 70.2% disagreed and 14.1% were neutral. Patterns were similar across ICBs, with no statistically significant differences observed.

Awareness of decarbonisation actions implemented by general practice

Most participants (87%) recognised at least one sustainability action undertaken by their practice during the past year, selected from a predefined list of actions. As shown in Table 2, the most frequently identified were electronic communication with patients (82.5%), encouragement to return unused medication (38.5%), and promotion of active travel (28.2%). About one-fifth of respondents (21.4%) reported not being aware of any actions undertaken by their practice. Recognition patterns were broadly similar across ICBs. Awareness of email or text-message communication was high in all regions (CW 80.7%, BS 85.5%, SY 84.2%; p = 0.68). Awareness of initiatives encouraging the return of unused medicines was somewhat higher in CW (42.1%) than in BS (33.9%) or SY (26.3%), although these differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.16).

Willingness to adopt environmentally sustainable behaviours if advised by a general practice healthcare professional

When asked how likely they would be to undertake specific environmentally sustainable behaviours if suggested by practice staff, selected from a list of examples, willingness varied. Highest levels of willingness (likely or very likely) were reported for medication reviews (25.8%), increasing active travel (24.4%), and engaging in social-prescribing alternatives (18.2%). Few participants expressed willingness to change medications (< 2%), discuss climate change with staff (2.0%), or modify diet towards plant-based foods (9.3%). Regional differences were modest: willingness for active travel and medication reviews was slightly greater in CW than in other ICBs, although these differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.06–0.09 after Holm adjustment).

Perceived barriers to individual engagement in decarbonisation actions

Most respondents (92.7%) reported at least one perceived barrier to engaging in general practice-led low-carbon behaviours, selected from a predefined list of barriers (Table 2). The two most commonly identified barriers were concern that such changes might compromise quality of care (72.4%) and lack of trustworthy information (30.1%). Only 4.9% of respondents selected “don’t know”, and 2.4% reported no specific barrier. The pattern of barriers was consistent across ICBs, with concern about impact on quality of healthcare dominant in all regions (SY 82.9%, BS 77.0%, CW 73.9%; p = 0.56).

Climate change knowledge and perceived health impacts

Few respondents felt well informed about climate change (10.8%) or recognised its links with health (1.2%). Only small proportions agreed that climate change was harming people in the UK (2.4%) or people in developing countries (2.0%). Just 9.6% reported that climate change in general (outside a healthcare context) had arisen in informal conversations with family or friends. Patterns were similar across ICBs, with no evident regional differences.

Attitudes towards responsibility for reducing GHG emissions across societal levels

Agreement with statements that different societal actors should do more to reduce GHG emissions was consistently low across all groups (Table 3). Only small proportions of respondents agreed that individuals or communities should do more (3.1%), that general practices should take greater responsibility (5.0%), or that the NHS should do more (5.6%). Most respondents disagreed that the government should do more to reduce GHG emissions (94.1%). Patterns were similar across ICBs.

Free-text comments on general practice and decarbonisation actions

Seventy-four respondents provided optional free-text comments (Table 4). Comments reflected a range of views on general practice involvement in decarbonisation, including support, scepticism, and conditional acceptance. Many comments focused on the visibility and communication of actions already underway, with respondents noting uncertainty about how changes such as electronic communications related to environmental impact. Other suggest practical, local actions, particularly around waste reduction and operational practices, while some raised concerns about access, workload, or unintended impacts on care. A smaller number of comments questioned whether decarbonisation should be led at practice level or addressed through wider NHS or government action. Overall, free-text responses provided illustrative examples of how patients interpreted and reacted to the issues raised in the survey.

Discussion

This study provides exploratory insights into patients’ perceptions of general practice-led decarbonisation initiatives, revealing low awareness, limited salience, and concerns about potential trade-offs with care quality. Despite the NHS’s statutory commitment to net-zero emissions [2], most respondents did not perceive general practice as having a key role in reducing GHG emissions, and support for government incentives for practice-level action was low. These findings point to a gap between national sustainability ambitions and patient expectations of general practice.

To our knowledge, this is the first multi-site study in England to examine general practice decarbonisation directly from a patient perspective. Existing research suggests that patient views on sustainability in healthcare are diverse and shaped by context, framing, and perceived relevance to care [10, 12–14, 28–30]. Our findings extend this evidence by showing that, while patients may accept changes within general practice that may support decarbonisation, these actions are not consistently understood or recognised by patients as environmental initiatives. This highlights an important distinction between acceptability and attribution, with implications for how sustainability is communicated and implemented in general practice.

Notably, commonly recognised practice activities – such as remote consultations and medicine-return schemes – were rarely linked by patients to decarbonisation. This suggests that sustainability benefits may be perceived as secondary or incidental to core service goals. Environmental initiatives introduced primarily to improve access, efficiency, or safety may therefore be more readily accepted by patients, regardless of whether their climate benefits are explicitly communicated. In this context, sustainability activities embedded within routine service improvement may be more acceptable than actions framed primarily around decarbonisation, particularly where care quality or convenience benefits are visible.

Respondents also expressed willingness to undertake certain actions if suggested by general practice staff. While this does not imply causality, it indicates that some decarbonisation-aligned behaviours may be considered acceptable when aligned with familiar clinical activities. Importantly, patients may accept sustainability-aligned change within general practice without viewing decarbonisation as a distinct or explicit professional responsibility for general practice.

The combination of low climate-health literacy and strong views about responsibility for emissions reduction suggests that attitudes may be shaped more by broader narratives about healthcare pressures and government roles than by detailed understanding of decarbonisation in healthcare. Free-text comments reinforced this interpretation, highlighting sensitivity to visible and tangible sources of waste, alongside concerns that sustainability efforts should not compromise access or care quality. Together, these findings point to the importance of visible, credible, and locally meaningful actions that align with patient priorities.

A key strength of this study is its multi-site design, spanning 12 contrasting general practices across three contrasting ICB regions, and its focus on patients who had recently used general practice services. The survey was co-designed with patients and included free-text responses, enabling contextualisation of quantitative findings.

However, several limitations should be noted. First, the sample size was modest and the response rates were lower than anticipated, with substantial variation between practices. Respondents were disproportionately older, White, and well educated, indicating that the sample was not representative of the wider population of NHS general practice users. Small per-practice numbers and missing data limited some comparisons, and the cross-sectional, self-reported design precludes causal inference. Hence, the quantitative data were interpreted descriptively, with the level of quantitative detail reported reflecting analytic transparency rather than statistical generalisability. Second, recruitment focused on patients who had recently attended general practice. While this added strength to the relevance of responses to current care experiences, it may have limited the inclusion of perspectives from less frequent users and those who have difficulty accessing care. Despite efforts to engage with community and faith groups, underserved populations remained under-represented in the sample. Findings should therefore be interpreted with caution in relation to wider patient views.

Low engagement with the survey also highlights the challenge of engaging patients with sustainability-related topics in the context of routine care. Developing approaches that better engage and include less-heard perspectives is an important area for future research. Finally, while the inclusion of free-text comments helped contextualise survey responses, the analysis of these data was descriptive in nature and not intended to generate in-depth qualitative explanations.

The study’s findings suggest that efforts to decarbonise general practice should prioritise patient-facing engagement alongside operational change. Communication strategies should emphasise how sustainability initiatives enhance, rather than compete with, care quality, highlighting tangible co-benefits such as safer prescribing, and reduced waste and travel burden. PPGs may provide a practical route for co-designing locally meaningful approaches, and alignment with ICB Green Plans and Greener NHS programme could strengthen coherence between local and national action. Equity must underpin all engagement efforts to ensure participation in the net-zero transition is inclusive and fair.

In conclusion, this study suggests that patients are broadly accepting of sustainability-aligned changes in general practice when these are perceived to support care quality, access, or efficiency, but do not necessarily view decarbonisation as an explicit or separate responsibility of general practice. This underscores the importance of embedding environmental sustainability within routine service improvement, rather than positioning it as a competing priority. However, it is important that this study’s conclusions be viewed as exploratory, and the insights generated point to important questions about how patients interpret, prioritise, and respond to decarbonisation efforts in general practice. There is a need for further research using larger and more representative samples to assess how widespread these views are across the UK population.
