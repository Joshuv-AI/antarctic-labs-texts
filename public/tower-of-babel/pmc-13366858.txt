Association between mid-upper arm circumference and weight for height with long-term survival in South Kivu, eastern Democratic Republic of Congo: Lwiro cohort
Authors: Gaylord Ngaboyeka, Pacifique Mwene-Batu, Jean Claude Zaluka, Armand Mulume, Didier Lwashiga, Lembebu Corneille, Emmanuel Ongezi, Christine Chimanuka, Joelle Mwalungwe, Richard Kambale, Michèle Dramaix, Ghislain Bisimwa, Philippe Donnen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionLittle information is available on the relationship between mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) and weight-for-height Z-score (WHZ) with long-term survival. This study explores the association between MUAC and WHZ of former children hospitalized for severe acute malnutrition (SAM) with their long-term survival after nutritional rehabilitation.MethodsThis was a cohort of adults followed in childhood for SAM between 1988 and 2007 at the Lwiro Pediatric Hospital (HPL) and traced 11 to 30 years after their nutritional rehabilitation in South Kivu, DRC. Childhood data included anthropometry, clinical characteristics, and nutritional edema. Anthropometric indicators (WHZ, MUAC, HAZ, and WAZ) were classified according to WHO thresholds. The dependent variable was long-term survival. Associations between admission WHZ, MUAC, and survival were assessed using Cox regression, with indicators analyzed separately and in combination. Reference groups were defined based on WHO thresholds (MUAC ≥ 125 mm, WHZ ≥ -2, and, for combined analyses, absence of both WHZ < -3 and MUAC < 115 mm).ResultsWe sampled 670 participants, 461 of whom were traced to adulthood, of whom 12.2% had died. The median age of living participants was 26 years, with a mean age at admission of 32 months during childhood. Among them, 71.1% had nutritional edema, 88.5% had stunting, 14.5% had WHZ < -3, and 22.3% had MUAC < 115 mm. Children with MUAC < 115 mm or WHZ < -3 had a higher, although non-significant, risk of long-term death compared to those without SAM as defined by these criteria. In univariate analyses, children with edema, including those with concurrent anthropometric deficits, showed an increased but non-significant risk of mortality. After adjustment for multiple factors, only WHZ < -3, when analyzed independently, was significantly associated with long-term mortality [adjusted HR (95% CI) = 2.7 (1.1–6.8), p = 0.03].ConclusionWHZ < -3 was associated with increased long-term mortality. These findings highlight the need to strengthen post-discharge follow-up and long-term care strategies to improve survival in this high-risk group.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-026-01372-7.

Context

Severe acute malnutrition (SAM) represents a major critical public health problem in many low- and middle-income countries, despite ongoing international efforts to reduce it.

Malnutrition, in all its forms, is estimated to contribute to approximately 45% of deaths in children under five [1]. Among its manifestations, severe acute malnutrition (SAM) has immediate consequences on child mortality and long-term impacts on health and development [2]. In the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), particularly in the South Kivu province, malnutrition has been a chronic problem since the 1960s and continues to represent a major public health concern affecting child survival [3].

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines SAM in children aged 6–59 months as the presence of any of the following: a mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) < 115 mm, a weight-for-height Z-score (WHZ) < -3, or bilateral nutritional edema, reflecting different clinical and physiological forms of acute malnutrition [4]. However, although MUAC and WHZ are commonly used as independent diagnostic criteria, many studies have demonstrated that these two indicators identify different cases of SAM, as shown by their poor concordance, and are associated with different mortality risks [5–8]. These differences vary across cohort studies, reflecting heterogeneity in study populations and contexts.

Recent research suggests the joint use of these criteria to improve the effectiveness of interventions in SAM management programs [3, 9–10]. However, in humanitarian contexts, MUAC is preferred due to its ease of use. Measure using a color-coded tape requires minimal training or equipment, making it ideal for resource-limited or emergency settings [5, 11].

Studies increasingly highlight the long-term health effects of SAM, emphasizing its impact on survival, cognitive development, and the onset of chronic diseases in adulthood [12–15].

In South Kivu, research comparing children who survived SAM with community controls without a history of SAM has shown persistent negative consequences on cognitive function, academic achievement, and predisposition to non-communicable diseases in adulthood, including reduced lean body mass and hematological imbalances [2, 16, 17]. However, studies directly comparing MUAC and WHZ criteria in terms of long-term survival are limited, particularly in the DRC context. A multi-country pooled analysis showed that MUAC and WHZ had similar associations with the risk of mortality during an average follow-up period of 10.1 months [18]. Our study addresses this gap by exploring the relationship between these diagnostic criteria and long-term mortality in a cohort of children treated for SAM in South Kivu.

Methodology

Study design and population

Our study population consisted of individuals who had been hospitalized as children at the Lwiro Pediatric Hospital (HPL) for SAM between 1988 and 2007. At that time, the diagnosis of SAM was based on weight-for-height percentiles (WPP) derived from a local growth reference curve developed by DeMaeyer in 1959 (unpublished, as cited by Lemonnier and Ingenbleek, 1989) [19], in combination with clinical criteria including the presence of nutritional edema and biological assessment based on serum albumin levels [12]. The participants were identified using electronic files and medical records archived at the Natural Sciences Research Center (CRSN) and were subsequently traced to their village of origin. They were classified into four categories: found (living in the village or its surroundings), deceased, displaced, or lost to follow-up.

Participants included in the present analysis were children aged 6 to 59 months who had anthropometric parameters collected at admission (weight, height and MUAC), data on nutritional edema and survival status at hospital discharge. They were followed up between 11 and 30 years after their nutritional rehabilitation, depending on the year of admission [12, 20].

A total of 670 participants were included from 1,981 records, of whom 461 were successfully traced and 209 were lost to follow-up. Among those traced, 87.8% were alive and 12.2% had died (Fig. 1). Over a median follow-up of 20 years (P25–P75: 17–23 years), the long-term mortality rate was 12.2%. The median age, interquartile range at death was 7.5 years (P25–P75: 4.5–11.0), while the median age of surviving participants was 26 years (P25–P75: 24–29 years).

Type of study and study framework

This is a cohort of former children admitted between 1988 and 2007 to the HPL for the management of SAM. The HPL is one of the first structures involved in the management of malnutrition in the DRC. It is located in the Miti-Murhesa health zone [21].

Within the Miti-Murhesa health zone, in Lwiro, the CRSN has been operating since 1947, organizing its activities into four research departments: documentation, geophysics, biology, and nutrition. Many research activities have been carried out in the nutrition department, which had between five and seven doctors. Nutritional data from care centers (HPL and other health centers) are analyzed within the CRSN.

The main economic activities of the population of these two areas are agriculture, livestock, fishing and small trade. The various conflicts that have caused insecurity have led to a disruption of these different economic activities, especially agriculture and livestock, which constitute nearly 70% of the income of the population. This has been the basis for the perpetuation of food insecurity in this region [2, 12].

Study variables

The main variable of interest was the long-term survival status of the hospitalized child, a dichotomous variable (survivor or deceased). Long-term mortality includes deaths, whatever the cause, occurring after discharge cured from hospitalization for SAM. “Cured” refers to children discharged from hospitalization for severe acute malnutrition (SAM) after meeting recovery criteria. These criteria typically include adequate anthropometric thresholds, such as MUAC ≥ 125 mm or WHZ ≥ -2, resolution of medical complications, return to normal appetite, an albumin level of at least 3 g/dL, and resolution of edema.

Variables gathered during the childhood hospitalization period were age (months), sex, weight (kg), height (cm), MUAC (mm), presence or absence of generalized nutritional edema, vaccination status, SAM relapse, duration of hospitalization, therapeutic failure and infectious diagnosis. A case of therapeutic failure was defined as a failure to respond to treatment after 45 days of hospitalization despite appropriate monitoring, based on clinical and nutritional assessment, including lack of improvement in nutritional status and/or persistence of edema.

Anthropometric indicators measured at admission included WHZ, MUAC, height-for-age z-score (HAZ), and weight-for-age z-score (WAZ). WHZ and MUAC were classified into three categories according to WHO cutoffs: (1) normal, (2) moderate acute malnutrition (MAM), and (3) SAM, with the presence of nutritional edema also considered in the definition of SAM. HAZ and WAZ were used to define stunting and underweight, respectively, based on WHO criteria.

Children without edema but with MUAC < 115 mm and/or WHZ Z-score < -3 were defined as having SAM. Underweight was defined as a WAZ < -2 and stunting as a HAZ < -2.

To associate the parameters with mortality, we defined a combined WHZ-MUAC variable, which allowed us to classify children into the following categories: MUAC < 115 mm alone (with WHZ ≥ -3), WHZ < -3 alone (with MUAC ≥ 115 mm), MUAC < 115 mm and WHZ < -3 together, and neither MUAC < 115 mm nor WHZ < -3.

Data collection

We used computerized databases and medical records in the CRSN archives room for the childhood period. During the follow-up phase, these former cases were traced in their village of origin between December 2017 and November 2018 in the Lwiro cohort as part of a research project on the long-term effects of childhood malnutrition [12].

Anthropometric measurements taken in adulthood were weight and height. Weight was measured by a scale to the nearest 100 g; height was taken using a SECA measuring device with the participant standing barefoot, to the nearest 0.1 cm; MUAC was measured using a non-stretchable tape on the left arm at the midpoint between the acromion and olecranon, to the nearest millimeter. Anthropometric measurements were performed using standard equipment (calibrated scale, stadiometer, and non-stretchable measuring tape) by trained healthcare professionals following standardized procedures.

Data analysis

Data analysis were performed using Stata 16 software. Frequencies and proportions were used to summarize categorical variables, median with interquartile range or mean with standard deviation were used for the description of quantitative variables depending on the shape of their distribution.

For survival analysis, time 0 was the date of discharge from the HPL. Patient follow-up time was calculated in years by subtracting the discharge date from the date of final outcome (classified as alive or dead). Displaced patients were included among those alive, as their status was known, while patients lost to follow-up were excluded from the follow-up time calculation. A Cox regression analysis was conducted to evaluate the proportional hazards associated with long-term mortality. The model was built progressively, following a step-by-step approach. Anthropometric indicators, such as MUAC and WHZ, were included initially. For all analyses, reference groups were defined according to WHO anthropometric thresholds, depending on whether MUAC, WHZ, or their combination was considered. For MUAC analyses, the reference group was defined as MUAC ≥ 125 mm; for WHZ analyses, as WHZ ≥ -2; and for combined indicators, as the absence of both WHZ < -3 and MUAC < 115 mm.

Subsequently, clinically relevant variables or those with an association at p < 0.20, such as therapeutic failure and vaccination status, were added successively. Other variables, including age at admission, sex, infectious diagnosis, albumin level, history of transfusion, history of infection, underweight and stunting, were integrated step by step. Only variables with a significant p-value (p < 0.05) or deemed clinically relevant were retained in the final model, in line with the principle of parsimony. The hypothesis of proportional hazards (proportional Hazards = PH model) was assessed using two approaches. For categorical variables, ln(-ln(S(t)) curves were plotted to visually verify the parallelism of survival curves. For the quantitative variables, Schoenfeld residuals were analyzed to evaluate the proportional hazards assumption. The adjusted HR (Hazard Ratio) and its 95% confidence interval is reported alongside the p-value of the Wald test (Appendix 1). For all these analyses, we set a type I error threshold (alpha) of 5%.

Ethical considerations

Our study obtained approval from the ethics committee of the Catholic University of Bukavu (approval number: UCB/CIE/NC/022/2016) to begin this study. Participation in the study was preceded by the signing of informed consent by the participant if he/she was an adult, or by a parent or guardian if the participant was a minor. Assent was also obtained from adolescents where appropriate.

Results

General characteristics of the population

The participants selected for the study had a mean (SD) age at admission of 32.3 (13.6) months, 58.3% were male and 22.9% had an incomplete or no vaccination status. The infectious diagnosis was made in 81.1% and among them malaria, gastroenteritis and pneumonia were the most predominant with respectively 46.8. %, 16.0% and 13.6%. SAM relapse was observed in 14.5% of participants, and the median duration of hospitalization was 22 days. In addition, 71.1% of the children were classified as having SAM based on the presence of nutritional edema. (Table 1)

Long-term follow-up of children admitted for SAM: outcomes in 2018

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed significant differences in long-term survival according to the period of admission (log-rank p = 0.024) (Fig. 2). Patients admitted during earlier periods (1987–1994 and 1994–1996) exhibited higher mortality compared to those admitted in more recent years (1996–2007), who demonstrated better long-term survival.

Kaplan–Meier survival curves stratified by combined anthropometric status showed a trend toward lower survival among children with combined or single deficits; however, the differences were not statistically significant (log-rank p = 0.311) (Fig. 3).

Therapeutic failure was the only factor significantly associated with long-term mortality, with children who experienced treatment failure during the initial episode of SAM having a threefold increased risk of death later in life (crude HR = 3.1; 95% CI: 1.6–6.2; p = 0.001).

In contrast, other factors such as age, vaccination status, transfusion history, nutritional edema, stunting, and infectious diagnosis were not significantly associated with long-term mortality (Table 3).

When considered separately, mortality was 19.4% among children with WHZ < -3 and 13.6% among those with a MUAC < 115 mm. When combining these criteria, mortality was 20.7% among children identified by WHZ < -3 only, 10.8% among those identified by MUAC < 115 mm only, and 18.4% among those meeting both criteria. Mortality among children who met neither criterion was 10.9% (Table 4).

In the univariate analysis, none of the indicators, whether considered alone or in combination, was significantly associated with long-term mortality. After adjustment, neither WHZ nor MUAC taken independently was significantly associated with mortality. However, in the combined analysis, children identified solely by WHZ < -3 had a 2.7-fold increased risk of long-term death compared to those without severe anthropometric deficits as defined by both indicators (Table 4).

Discussion

This study follows a cohort of individuals admitted for SAM during childhood between 1988 and 2007 and traced 11 to 30 years after nutritional rehabilitation in South Kivu, DRC. The long-term mortality observed in our cohort was 12.2%. Causes of death were determined based on information collected from relatives and cross-checked with available medical records [12], suggesting that malaria, kwashiorkor, respiratory infections, and diarrheal diseases were the most common contributors.

The observed differences in survival across admission periods suggest that improvements in SAM management over time may have contributed to better long-term outcomes. The transition from locally adapted treatments to standardized WHO protocols (F-75 and F-100), along with advances in clinical care, likely played a key role in improving survival.

Our findings show that, although both MUAC < 115 mm and WHZ < -3 were associated with increased mortality risk in univariate analyses, only WHZ < -3 remained significantly associated with long-term mortality after adjustment for confounders. These results are consistent with previous findings from the same cohort, which demonstrated a stronger association between WHZ and mortality during hospitalization [3].

Previous studies have reported varying associations between MUAC, WHZ, and mortality risk across settings [18, 22–37]. In contrast to our findings, several studies have reported MUAC as a stronger predictor of mortality, particularly in high-burden settings and short-term follow-up contexts. For instance, studies in Niger, Cameroon, Bangladesh, India, and The Gambia have consistently shown higher predictive performance of MUAC compared to WHZ [23, 24, 30–32]. A multicountry analysis including the DRC found similar associations between MUAC and WHZ over a short follow-up period (mean 10.1 months) [18], while meta-analyses have suggested that combining indicators (e.g., MUAC < 115 mm or WAZ < -3) improves sensitivity and detection of high-risk children [27, 28, 33]. A recent large-scale study conducted in Nigeria also reported that children identified by WHZ criteria had a significantly higher risk of death compared to those identified by MUAC alone, with an even greater risk observed in children presenting both WHZ deficits and oedema [38]. These findings are consistent with our results and further highlight the limitations of relying solely on MUAC to identify children at high risk of mortality.

However, most of these studies were conducted over relatively short follow-up periods (ranging from 1 to 24 months), which limits direct comparison with our long-term findings. Our study, with a median follow-up of 20 years, provides a different perspective on the long-term consequences of early-life malnutrition. In this context, WHZ < -3 appeared to be more strongly associated with mortality than MUAC, both during hospitalization and over extended follow-up [3, 8, 39].

These discrepancies may be explained by differences in study design, population characteristics, and follow-up duration. MUAC is strongly correlated with age, similar to absolute weight and height, and thus reflects both nutritional status and underlying biological vulnerability. In our study, after adjustment for age and other confounders, only WHZ < -3 remained significantly associated with long-term mortality. This suggests that the apparent superiority of MUAC observed in previous studies may partly reflect age-related vulnerability rather than a specific effect of acute malnutrition [40]. This is further supported by a recent multi-country pooled analysis from the WHO Child Mortality Risk Stratification Working Group, which showed that mortality risk is strongly influenced by a combination of factors including age, anthropometric deficits, and early-life conditions, with additive effects across risk factors [41]. Several contextual and physiological mechanisms may explain the stronger association between WHZ and mortality. In settings where MUAC is primarily used for community screening, children with low MUAC are often detected earlier and treated before complications arise, whereas those with WHZ < -3 but normal MUAC may be identified later, often at a more advanced stage of illness. In addition, in populations with a high prevalence of stunting, WHZ may reflect more advanced tissue depletion, while MUAC may preferentially identify younger or chronically undernourished children due to its strong correlation with age.

Longer-term follow-up studies have also highlighted persistent vulnerability after discharge from nutritional programs. Studies conducted in Bihar, Niger, and Bangladesh have shown that children discharged with low MUAC remain at high risk of death [30, 42, 43], while others have reported strong predictive performance of MUAC for short-term mortality [31, 32]. However, these findings remain difficult to compare directly with long-term outcomes such as those assessed in our study.

Several studies have suggested that combining MUAC and WHZ improves the identification of children at high risk of death, given the limited overlap between these indicators [44]. However, evidence also indicates that children identified solely by WHZ < -3 may remain at high risk and could be missed when screening relies only on MUAC thresholds [45]. This highlights the importance of maintaining both indicators in screening strategies. Furthermore, Briend et al. demonstrated that MUAC maintained its predictive value over time, whereas the predictive performance of WHZ declined [46]. This finding highlights the importance of considering follow-up duration when interpreting the relative performance of anthropometric indicators.

The biological mechanisms underlying these findings may relate to differences in body composition and metabolic adaptation. WHZ reflects generalized wasting affecting both fat and lean mass, whereas MUAC primarily reflects peripheral muscle and subcutaneous fat. Children with low WHZ may experience more severe depletion of central fat reserves, which are critical for immune and metabolic functions [47]. This is supported by studies showing lower leptin levels among children with WHZ deficits, particularly those with combined deficits [48, 49].

Severe acute malnutrition also induces long-term metabolic and physiological alterations, including increased susceptibility to chronic diseases such as diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disorders [50–52]. Early-life nutritional insults may lead to metabolic programming, whereby adaptations that promote short-term survival result in long-term health consequences [51, 53–55].

Beyond metabolic effects, early malnutrition increases the risk of infections and contributes to long-term health vulnerability, including impaired physical capacity, reduced cognitive development, and lower socioeconomic outcomes, all of which may indirectly influence long-term mortality [12, 56, 57]. These effects may also be linked to alterations in immune function and energy metabolism, including changes in fat distribution and inflammatory responses [58–60].

Limitations

It is important to recognize that, despite the valuable results of our study, several limitations remain. Survival bias is a major limitation, as 209 participants were lost to follow-up. However, a key strength is that the admission characteristics of these individuals were similar to those of participants retained in the analysis, which gives us reasonable confidence that this loss did not substantially influence our findings (Table Annex 2).

Secondly, we lacked data on key health factors in young children, including birth weight, growth trajectories during the first two years of life, HIV status, and detailed information on participants’ progress from hospital discharge to the end of the study. These variables could represent potential confounding factors, as they are closely linked to both malnutrition and its long-term adverse effects. Nevertheless, we were able to adjust for other childhood factors in our analyses.

Another limitation of this study is that the reference group did not consist of well-nourished children, but rather of children admitted for SAM who were classified as “normal” according to WHO anthropometric thresholds after retrospective reclassification. This group may therefore include both children with nutritional edema and children without anthropometric deficits, some of whom may be truly normal according to current SAM criteria (i.e., absence of edema and normal anthropometry). To mitigate this potential heterogeneity, all analyses were adjusted for the presence of nutritional edema.

Conclusion

Our study shows that WHZ < -3 is significantly associated with long-term mortality among children treated for severe acute malnutrition. Particular attention should therefore be given to children identified by low WHZ, as they may remain at high risk of death even after nutritional rehabilitation. Tailored follow-up and intervention strategies are needed to improve their long-term survival.
