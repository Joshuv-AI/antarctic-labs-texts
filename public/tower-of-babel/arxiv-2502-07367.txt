Extriangulated length categories: torsion classes and τ \tau -tilting theory
Li Wang, Jiaqun Wei, Haicheng Zhang and Panyue Zhou

Abstract
. This paper introduces the notion of extriangulated length categories, whose prototypical examples include abelian length categories and bounded derived categories of finite dimensional algebras with finite global dimension. We prove that an extriangulated category 𝒜 \mathcal{A} is a length category if and only if 𝒜 \mathcal{A} admits a simple-minded system. Subsequently, we study the partially ordered set tor Θ ​ ( 𝒜 ) \mbox{\rm tor}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A}) of torsion classes in an extriangulated length category ( 𝒜 , Θ ) (\mathcal{A},\Theta) from the perspective of lattice theory. It is shown that tor Θ ​ ( 𝒜 ) \mbox{\rm tor}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A}) forms a complete lattice, which is further proved to be completely semidistributive and algebraic. Moreover, we describe the arrows in the Hasse quiver of tor Θ ​ ( 𝒜 ) \mbox{\rm tor}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A}) using brick labeling. Finally, we introduce the concepts of support torsion classes and support τ \tau -tilting subcategories in extriangulated length categories and establish a bijection between these two notions, thereby generalizing the Adachi-Iyama-Reiten bijection for functorially finite torsion classes.

1.
Introduction

The concept of abelian categories, introduced and thoroughly explored by Grothendieck, serves as a unified framework that elegantly generalizes both module categories over rings and categories of sheaves over schemes. Among these, abelian length categories
[
11
]
, which are abelian categories with the length of every object finite, play a pivotal role in representation theory. Their importance stems from their structural richness and their applications in various mathematical domains, including

\tau
-tilting theory
[
9
]
, lattice theory
[
3
]
, and the study of strongly unbounded types
[
16
]
. Schur’s lemma underscores their significance by showing that the set of isomorphism classes of simple objects in an abelian length category forms a semibrick, which is a combinatorial object with rich mathematical properties.

In triangulated categories, the notion of a simple-minded system generalizes the role of simple objects in abelian length categories. This concept, introduced by Koenig and Liu
[
15
]
in the study of stable module categories, has been further investigated, particularly in relation to mutations
[
7
]
. Dugas
[
7
]
proved that bounded derived categories of finite dimensional algebras with finite global dimension admit simple-minded systems. This result allows us to interpret these derived categories as analogs of abelian length categories in the triangulated framework, thereby establishing a meaningful connection between the two contexts.

Recently, Nakaoka and Palu
[
17
]
proposed the framework of extriangulated categories, which synthesizes and extends properties from both triangulated and exact categories. Within this broader framework, it becomes natural to ask how the notion of abelian length categories can be extended. To address this, we introduce the concept of extriangulated length categories, which formalizes the idea of abelian length categories in the setting of extriangulated categories.

An extriangulated length category is defined as a pair

(\mathcal{A},\Theta)
, where
\mathcal{A}
is an extriangulated category and

\Theta:\mbox{\rm Iso}\,(\mathcal{A})\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
is a length function that ensures every morphism in

\mathcal{A}
satisfies the
\Theta
-admissibility condition (see Definition
3.8
). This definition preserves the essential characteristics of abelian length categories while adapting them to the more flexible extriangulated setting. Our first main result, stated below, establishes a foundational theorem for extriangulated length categories and opens the door to further theoretical exploration and applications.

Theorem A
.

(see Theorems
3.9
,
3.14
for details)
Let
\mathcal{A}
be an extriangulated category.

(1)
If
\mathcal{X}
is a simple-minded system in
\mathcal{A}
, then
(\mathcal{A},l_{\mathcal{X}})
is an extriangulated length category, where
l_{\mathcal{X}}
is the minimal length of
\mathcal{X}
-filtrations.

(2)

\mathcal{A}
is an extriangulated length category if and only if
\mathcal{A}
has a simple-minded system.

An important source of examples for extriangulated length categories comes from the notion of
length wide subcategories
(see Definition
3.15
). These subcategories extend the concept of wide subcategories from abelian length categories to the extriangulated setting, thereby enriching the structural landscape of extriangulated categories. Analogous to their counterparts in abelian categories, length wide subcategories can be generated by semibricks, a combinatorial structure that captures key categorical properties.
This connection not only deepens our understanding of the role that semibricks play in categorical frameworks but also allows us to generalize classical results into the realm of extriangulated categories. In particular, we extend the celebrated result of Ringel
[
19
]
, which describes the structure and generation of certain subcategories in abelian length categories. Our generalization shows that the underlying principles of Ringel’s theorem hold in this broader context, offering new insights and potential applications. More specifically, we establish the following theorem, which serves as a cornerstone of our study.

Theorem B
.

(see Theorem
3.18
for details)
Let
\mathcal{A}
be an extriangulated category. Then there exists a bijection between the following two sets.

(1)
The set of semibricks
\mathcal{X}
in
\mathcal{A}
.

(2)
The set of length wide subcategories
(\mathcal{C},\Theta)
in
\mathcal{A}

The mutually inverse maps are given by

\mathbf{Organization.}
This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we summarize the fundamental definitions and key properties of extriangulated categories and filtration categories, and provide the foundational concepts required for subsequent discussions. Section 3 introduces the notion of extriangulated length categories and establishes several basic properties that will be utilized throughout the paper. In Section 4, we develop a lattice-theoretical framework for studying the partially ordered sets of torsion classes within extriangulated length categories, offering a structured approach to analyzing their relationships. Finally, in Section 5, we introduce the concepts of support torsion classes and support

-tilting theories to the framework of extriangulated categories.

3.
Extriangulated length categories

In this section, we introduce the concept of extriangulated length categories and establish their fundamental properties. We prove that extriangulated length categories correspond precisely to
those categories arising from simple-minded systems. Notably, many significant categories in representation theory fall within the framework of extriangulated length categories, highlighting their broad applicability and importance. Throughout the remainder of this section, we fix an extriangulated category

(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
as the basis for our discussion.

3.1.
Definitions and basic properties

We start with introducing the following notations.

Definition 3.1
.

We say that a map
\Theta:\mbox{\rm Iso}\,(\mathcal{A})\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
is a
length function
on
\mathcal{A}
if it satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

\Theta(X)=0

X\cong 0

(2)

For any
\mathbb{E}

in
\mathcal{A}
, we have

. In addition, if
\delta=0
, then
\Theta(L)=\Theta(X)+\Theta(M)
.

A length function
\Theta
is said to
stable
if

\mathbb{E}
-triangle

\mathcal{A}
.

When we say that an extriangulated category

Let

\mathcal{A}
is a length category.

(2)
The category
\mathcal{A}

Proof..

This follows from Theorem

3.3.
Extriangulated length subcategories and semibricks

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
be an extension-closed subcategory. We define
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}}
by the restriction of
\mathbb{E}
onto
\mathcal{C}^{\rm op}\times\mathcal{C}
and define
\mathfrak{s}_{\mathcal{C}}
by restricting
\mathfrak{s}
. Then
(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}},\mathfrak{s}_{\mathcal{C}})
is an extriangulated category (cf.
[
17
, Remark 2.18]
).

Definition 3.15
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
be an extension-closed subcategory.

(1)
We say that
(\mathcal{C},\Theta)
is a
length subcategory in
\mathcal{A}
if
((\mathcal{C},\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}},\mathfrak{s}_{\mathcal{C}}),\Theta)
is a length category.

(2)
We say that a length subcategory
(\mathcal{C},\Theta)
in
\mathcal{A}
is
length wide
if
\Theta_{1}=\Theta_{\infty}
.

We say that a subcategory
\mathcal{C}
is length (wide) if there exists a length function
\Theta
such that
(\mathcal{C},\Theta)
is a length (wide) subcategory.

Remark 3.16
.

(1) Let
\mathcal{X}
be a semibrick in
\mathcal{A}
. Using Theorem
3.9
together with Remark
2.4
, we can easily check that
(\mbox{\rm{Filt}}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{X}),l_{\mathcal{X}})
is a length wide subcategory in
\mathcal{A}
. Now, let
(\mathcal{C},\Theta)
be a length subcategory in
\mathcal{A}
. By Proposition
3.13
,
(\mathcal{C},l_{\Theta_{\infty}})
is a length wide subcategory. By this fact, a subcategory
\mathcal{C}
is length if and only if
\mathcal{C}
is length wide.

(2)
Let
\mathcal{C}
be a length subcategory in an exact category. Combining
[
5
, Exercise 8.6]
with Theorem
3.13
, we obtain that
\mathcal{C}
is an abelian length category with the natural exact structure. Then by (1), it is easy to see that length subcategories in an exact category are just length wide subcategories in the sense of
[
8
, Definition 2.4]
.

Let
(\mathcal{C},\Theta)
be a length subcategory of
\mathcal{A}
. A non-zero object
M
in
\mathcal{C}
is called a
simple
object if there does not exist a
\Theta
-stable
\mathbb{E}
-triangle
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\rightarrowtail}}M\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\twoheadrightarrow}}B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\dashrightarrow}}
in
\mathcal{C}
such that
A,B\neq 0
. Let
\mbox{\rm sim}(\mathcal{C})
be the set of isomorphism classes of simple objects in
(\mathcal{C},\Theta)
. We have the following basic observation.

Lemma 3.17
.

Let
(\mathcal{C},\Theta)
be a length subcategory in
\mathcal{A}
.

(1)

\Theta_{1}=\Theta_{\infty}
if and only if
\Theta=l_{\Theta_{1}}
.

(2)
If
(\mathcal{C},\Theta)
is length wide, then
\Theta_{1}=\mbox{\rm sim}(\mathcal{C})
.

(3)

(\mathcal{C},\Theta)
is length wide if and only if
\Theta=l_{{\rm sim(\mathcal{C})}}
.

Proof..

(1) Suppose that
\Theta_{1}=\Theta_{\infty}
. Then
\Theta_{1}
is a simple-minded system in
\mathcal{A}
by Theorem
3.13
. Take an object
M\in\mathcal{A}
with
\Theta(M)=m
. The case of
m=0
is trivial. If
m=1
, then
M\in\Theta_{1}
and thus
l_{\Theta_{1}}(M)=1
. Now consider the general case. We note that
\Theta_{1}^{\perp}\bigcap{{}^{\perp}}\Theta_{1}=\emptyset
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that there exists a non-zero morphism
f:X\rightarrow M
such that
\Theta(X)=1
. By Lemma
3.11
,
f
is a
\Theta
-inflation and thus
\Theta(\mbox{\rm Cone}(f))=m-1
. On the other hand, by Proposition
2.3
, we have
l_{\Theta_{1}}(M)=1+l_{\Theta_{1}}(\mbox{\rm Cone}(f))
. By induction hypothesis, we have
\Theta(\mbox{\rm Cone}(f))=l_{\Theta_{1}}(\mbox{\rm Cone}(f))
and conclude that
\Theta(M)=l_{\Theta_{1}}(M)
. Conversely, if
\Theta=l_{\Theta_{1}}
, then
\Theta_{1}^{\perp}\bigcap{{}^{\perp}}\Theta_{1}=\emptyset
by Remark
2.4
. This implies that
\Theta_{1}=\Theta_{\infty}
.

(2)
Obviously,
\Theta_{1}\subseteq\mbox{\rm sim}(\mathcal{C})
. Take
L\in\mbox{\rm sim}(\mathcal{C})
. By Remark
2.4
, there exists an
\mathbb{E}
-triangle
\eta:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\longrightarrow}}L\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\longrightarrow}}M\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\dashrightarrow}}
with
l_{\Theta_{1}}(X)=1
and
l_{\Theta_{1}}(\mbox{\rm Cone}(f))=l_{\Theta_{1}}(M)-1
. By (1), we have
\Theta(X)+\Theta(L)=\Theta(M)
and thus
\eta
is
\Theta
-stable. Since
L\in\mbox{\rm sim}(\mathcal{C})
, we get
X\cong L
by Lemma
3.5
. This implies that
\mbox{\rm sim}(\mathcal{C})\subseteq\Theta_{1}
.

(3)
If
(\mathcal{C},\Theta)
is length wide, then
\Theta_{1}=\Theta_{\infty}
by definition. By (1) and (2), we have
\Theta=l_{\Theta_{1}}=l_{{\rm sim(\mathcal{C})}}
. Conversely, if
\Theta=l_{{\rm sim(\mathcal{C})}}
, then
\mbox{\rm sim}(\mathcal{C})
is a simple-minded system in
\mathcal{C}
by Proposition
3.13
. Then
(\mathcal{C},l_{{\rm sim(\mathcal{C})}})
is length wide by Remark
3.16
.
∎

We are now ready to present the main result of this subsection, which establishes a bijection between semibricks and length wide subcategories in extriangulated categories, revealing their intrinsic connection.

Theorem 3.18
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an extriangulated category.
Then there exists a bijection between the following two sets.

(1)
The set of semibricks
\mathcal{X}
in
\mathcal{A}
.

(2)
The set of length wide subcategories
(\mathcal{C},\Theta)
in
\mathcal{A}
.

The mutually inverse maps are given by
\mathcal{X}\mapsto(\mbox{\rm{Filt}}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{X}),l_{\mathcal{X}})
and
(\mathcal{C},\Theta)\mapsto\mbox{\rm sim}(\mathcal{C})
.

Proof..

For a semibrick
\mathcal{X}
in
\mathcal{A}
,
(\mbox{\rm{Filt}}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{X}),l_{\mathcal{X}})
is a length wide subcategory by Remark
3.16
. In this case, we have
\mathcal{X}=\mbox{\rm sim}(\mbox{\rm{Filt}}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{X}))
by Lemma
3.17
. Conversely, for a length wide subcategory
(\mathcal{C},\Theta)
, we have
\Theta_{\infty}=\Theta_{1}=\mbox{\rm sim}(\mathcal{C})
and
\Theta=l_{{\rm sim(\mathcal{C})}}
. Then
\mbox{\rm sim}(\mathcal{C})
is a simple-minded system in
\mathcal{C}
by Proposition
3.13
. This implies that
(\mathcal{C},\Theta)=(\mbox{\rm{Filt}}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mbox{\rm sim}(\mathcal{C})),l_{{\rm sim(\mathcal{C})}})
.
∎

Remark 3.19
.

For a finite dimensional algebra
\Lambda
over a field,
the classic result by Ringel
[
19
]
tells us that there exists
a bijection between semibricks and wide subcategories in
\mbox{\rm{mod}}\,\Lambda
.
This bijection was generalized to exact categories in
[
8
, Theorem 2.5]
,
which explicitly establishes a bijection between semibricks and length wide subcategories
in an exact category. By using Remark
3.16
, we can recover
[
8
, Theorem 2.5]
.

3.4.
Stable extriangulated length categories

As before,
(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s})
is an extriangulated category.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\Theta)
be a length category. The length function
\Theta
is not stable in general. To solve this, we consider the induced extriangulated structures by using length functions. Firstly, we recall from
[
12
]
that a functor
\mathbb{F}

is called a
subfunctor
of
\mathbb{E}
if it satisfies the following conditions:

\bullet
For any
M,N\in\mathcal{A}
,
\mathbb{F}(M,N)
is a subset of
\mathbb{E}(M,N)
.

\bullet
For any
f\in\mbox{\rm Hom}_{\mathcal{A}}(M^{\prime},M)
,
g\in\mbox{\rm Hom}_{\mathcal{A}}(N,N^{\prime})
, we have
\mathbb{F}(f,g)=\mathbb{E}(f,g)|_{\mathbb{F}(M,N)}
.

A subfunctor is said to be an
additive subfunctor
if
\mathbb{F}(M,N)\subseteq\mathbb{E}(M,N)
is an abelian subgroup for any
M,N\in\mathcal{A}
. For any
A,B\in\mathcal{A}
, we define
\mathbb{E}_{\Theta}(A,B)
to be the subset of
\mathbb{E}(A,B)
consisting of all
\Theta
-extensions. In particular, if
\Theta
is stable, then
\mathbb{E}_{\Theta}=\mathbb{E}
. We define
\mathfrak{s}_{\Theta}
as the restriction of
\mathfrak{s}
on
\mathbb{E}_{\Theta}
.
Let us begin with the following observation.

.

Let

and

be any composable pair of morphisms in
\mathcal{A}
. If

is a
\Theta
-inflation, then so is
f
. Dually, if
gf
is a
\Theta
-deflation, then so is
g
.

We only prove the first statement and the second statement can be proved dually. Firstly, we take a
\Theta
-decomposition

of

. Applying Lemma

We define
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}}
as the restriction of
\mathbb{E}
onto

and define

by restricting

. Our aim in this subsection is to prove the following result, which will be used in Section 4.

Proposition 3.22
.

Let
((\mathcal{A},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s}),\Theta)
be

((\mathcal{A},\mathbb{E}_{\Theta},\mathfrak{s}_{\Theta}),\Theta)

Proof..

First of all,

is a length category, we can easily check that
((\mathcal{A},\mathbb{E}_{\Theta},\mathfrak{s}_{\Theta}),\Theta)
is a stable length category.

3.5.
Examples of extriangulated length categories

In this subsection, we present examples of length categories that involve several topics discussed in the preceding subsections.

Example 3.23
.

Let

4.
Torsion classes in extriangulated length categories

Our aim in this section is to establish a lattice theoretical framework of torsion classes in length categories, which is a generalization of some classical results over a finite dimensional algebra (c.f.
[
6
]
). In this section, we always assume that
((\mathcal{A},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s}),\Theta)
is a length category.

4.1.
Torsion classes

Given subcategories
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, we define

\mbox{\rm Fac}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{S})=\{M\in\mathcal{A}~|~\text{there exists a $\Theta$-deflation}~S\twoheadrightarrow M~\text{for some}~S\in\mathcal{S}\},

\mbox{\rm Sub}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{S})=\{M\in\mathcal{A}~|~\text{there exists a $\Theta$-inflation}~M\rightarrowtail S~\text{for some}~S\in\mathcal{S}\},

\mathcal{S}\ast_{\Theta}\mathcal{T}=\{M\in\mathcal{A}~|~\text{there exists a stable $\mathbb{E}$-triangle}~T\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\rightarrowtail}}M\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\twoheadrightarrow}}F\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\dashrightarrow}}~

\text{with}~T\in\mathcal{S},F\in\mathcal{T}\}.

We introduce the following notions, which play a central role in this section.

Definition 4.1
.

A
torsion class
in
(\mathcal{A},\Theta)
is a subcategory
\mathcal{T}
of
\mathcal{A}
such that
\mbox{\rm Fac}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{T})\subseteq\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}\ast_{\Theta}\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
. The set of torsion classes in
(\mathcal{A},\Theta)
is denoted by
\mbox{\rm tors}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})
. Dually, a subcategory
\mathcal{F}
of
\mathcal{A}
is called a
torsion-free class
in
(\mathcal{A},\Theta)
if
\mbox{\rm Sub}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{T})\subseteq\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}\ast_{\Theta}\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
. The set of torsion-free classes in
(\mathcal{A},\Theta)
is denoted by
\mbox{\rm torf}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})
.

If
(\mathcal{A},\Theta)=(\mbox{\rm{mod}}\,\Lambda,l_{{\rm sim({\rm mod}\Lambda)}})
for a finite dimensional algebra
\Lambda
, then Definition
4.1
coincides with the usual one.

Definition 4.2
.

A
torsion pair

(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
in
(\mathcal{A},\Theta)
is a pair of subcategories
\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\mbox{\rm Hom}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})=0
, and for any
M\in\mathcal{A}
, there exists a
\Theta
-stable
\mathbb{E}
-triangle
T\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\rightarrowtail}}M\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\twoheadrightarrow}}F\dashrightarrow
with
T\in\mathcal{T}
and
F\in\mathcal{F}
.

When
\Theta
is stable, the torsion pairs in
(\mathcal{A},\Theta)
coincide with the usual torsion pairs. The following provides a useful characterization of torsion pairs in a length category.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
be a torsion pair in
(\mathcal{A},\Theta)
. The following statements hold:

(1)

\mathcal{T}^{\perp}=\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{T}={{}^{\perp}}\mathcal{F}
.

(2)

\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{F}
are extension-closed.

(3)

\mathcal{T}
is a torsion class in
(\mathcal{A},\Theta)
.

(3^{\prime})

\mathcal{F}
is a torsion-free class in
(\mathcal{A},\Theta)
.

Proof..

(1) The inclusion
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{T}^{\perp}
is obvious. For each
M\in\mathcal{T}^{\perp}
, there exists a
\Theta
-stable
\mathbb{E}
-triangle
T\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\rightarrowtail}}M\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\twoheadrightarrow}}F\dashrightarrow
with
T\in\mathcal{T}
and
F\in\mathcal{F}
. Since
f=0
, we obtain
M\cong F\in\mathcal{F}
. This proves
\mathcal{T}^{\perp}=\mathcal{F}
. The proof of
\mathcal{T}={{}^{\perp}}\mathcal{F}
is similar.

(2)
We take an
\mathbb{E}

T_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\longrightarrow}}M\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\longrightarrow}}T_{2}\dashrightarrow
with each

. Applying
\mbox{\rm Hom}_{\mathcal{A}}(-,\mathcal{F})

\mbox{\rm Hom}_{\mathcal{A}}(M,\mathcal{F})=0
. By (1), we get

and thus
\mathcal{T}\ast\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
. Similarly, the second

(3)
For any

\mathbb{E}
-triangle

with

. Since
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
is a torsion pair, we have an
\mathbb{E}
-triangle

with

and
F\in\mathcal{F}
. Since
ba=0
is a
\Theta
-deflation, we have
F\cong 0
and thus
M\cong T^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
. Similarly, we can prove
(3)^{\prime}
.
∎

4.3.
Lattice of torsion classes

First of all, we recall some notations from poset theory.

Let
P=(P,\leq)
be a partially ordered set and let
x,y\in P
.

\bullet
We say an element
z\in P
is a
join
of
x
and
y
, denoted by
x\vee y
, if
z
is the smallest element in the set
\{t\in P~|~t\geq x,t\geq y\}
. The
meet

x\wedge y
of
x
and
y
is the largest element in the set
\{t\in P~|~t\leq x,t\leq y\}
. We remark that the join or meet of two elements may not exist. For a subset
L
of
P
, the join (resp. meet) of
L
is the element
\bigvee L:=\bigvee_{L_{i}\in L}L_{i}
(resp.
\bigwedge L:=\bigwedge_{L_{i}\in L}L_{i}
). Obviously,
\bigvee L
(resp.
\bigwedge L
) is the unique minimal (resp. maximal) common upper (resp. lower) bound of elements in
L
.

\bullet
We say
P
is a
complete lattice
if every subset
S
of
P
admits join and meet. A morphism
\alpha:P\rightarrow L
of complete lattices is a map such that
\alpha(\bigvee L)=\bigvee(\alpha(L))
and
\alpha(\bigwedge L)=\bigwedge(\alpha(L))
for any subset
L
of
P
.

\bullet
Let
P
be a complete lattice. We say an element
x\in P
is
compact
if for any
L\subseteq P
such that
x\leq\bigvee L
, there exists a finite subset
S\subseteq L
such that
x\leq\bigvee_{S_{i}\in S}S_{i}
.

\bullet
The
interval
is a subset of
P
which has the form
[x,y]:=\{z\in P~|~x\leq z\leq y\}
for some
x,y\in P
. We say that
y
is a
cover
of
x
if
[x,y]=\{x,y\}
. The
Hasse quiver
Hasse
(P)
has
P
as the set of vertices and for any
x,y\in P
, there is an arrow
y\rightarrow x
if and only if
y
is a cover of
x
.

Obviously,
\mbox{\rm tors}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})
and
\mbox{\rm torf}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})
are partially ordered sets with respect to the inclusion relation. The following basic observation on
\mbox{\rm tors}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})
is useful.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\mathcal{U},\mathcal{T}
be two torsion classes in
(\mathcal{A},\Theta)
. We have
\mathcal{U}\wedge\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{U}\vee\mathcal{T}=\mbox{\rm{Filt}}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{U}\cup\mathcal{V})
.

Proof..

The first statement follows from the fact that
\mbox{\rm tors}_{\Theta}\mathcal{A}
is closed under intersections. The second one follows from Proposition
4.8
.
∎

Definition 4.10
.

(
[
6
, Definition 2.8]
) We say a complete lattice
P
is
completely semidistributive
if for any
x\in P
and
L\subseteq P
, the following hold:

(1)

If
x\wedge y=x\wedge z
for any
y,z\in L
, then
x\wedge(\bigvee L)=x\wedge y
for any
y\in L
.

(2)

If
x\vee y=x\vee z
for any
y,z\in L
, then
x\vee(\bigwedge L)=x\vee y
for any
y\in L
.

Definition 4.11
.

(
[
6
, Definition 2.9]
) We say that a complete lattice
P
is
algebraic
if for any
x\in P
, there exists a set
L
of compact objects of
P
such that
x=\bigvee L
.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\Theta)
be a length category.

(1)
The set
\mbox{\rm tors}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})
is a complete lattice with joins and meets given by

\bigvee\mathcal{L}={{}^{\perp}}(\bigcap_{\mathcal{T}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}}{\mathcal{T}_{i}}^{\perp})=\mbox{\rm{Filt}}_{\Theta}(\bigcup_{\mathcal{T}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{T}_{i})~\text{and}~\bigwedge\mathcal{L}=\bigcap_{\mathcal{T}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}}{\mathcal{T}_{i}}

for any
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mbox{\rm tors}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})
.

(2)
The set
\mbox{\rm torf}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})
is a complete lattice with joins and meets given by

\bigvee\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=(\bigcap_{\mathcal{F}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}{{}^{\perp}}{\mathcal{F}_{i}}){{}^{\perp}}=\mbox{\rm{Filt}}_{\Theta}(\bigcup_{\mathcal{F}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\mathcal{F}_{i})~\text{and}~\bigwedge\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=\bigcap_{\mathcal{F}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{F}_{i}}

for any
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subseteq\mbox{\rm torf}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})
.

(3)
We have an isomorphism of complete lattices
\mbox{\rm tors}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mbox{\rm torf}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})^{{\rm op}};~\mathcal{T}\mapsto\mathcal{T}^{\perp}
.

(4)
The complete lattice
\mbox{\rm tors}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})
is completely semidistributive.

(5)
The complete lattice
\mbox{\rm tors}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})
is algebraic.

Proof..

(1)–(3) Since
\mbox{\rm tors}_{\Theta}\mathcal{A}
and
\mbox{\rm torf}_{\Theta}\mathcal{A}
are closed under arbitrary intersections, we clearly have

\bigwedge\mathcal{L}=\bigcap_{\mathcal{T}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}}{\mathcal{T}_{i}}~\text{and}~\bigwedge\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=\bigcap_{\mathcal{F}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{F}_{i}}.

Moreover, by Proposition
4.6
and Lemma
4.9
, one can easily check that

\bigvee\mathcal{L}={{}^{\perp}}(\bigcap_{\mathcal{T}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}}{\mathcal{T}_{i}}^{\perp})=\mbox{\rm{Filt}}_{\Theta}(\bigcup_{\mathcal{T}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{T}_{i})~\text{and}~\bigvee\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=(\bigcap_{\mathcal{F}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}{{}^{\perp}}{\mathcal{F}_{i}}){{}^{\perp}}=\mbox{\rm{Filt}}_{\Theta}(\bigcup_{\mathcal{F}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\mathcal{F}_{i}).

Hence,
\mbox{\rm tors}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})
and
\mbox{\rm torf}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})
are complete lattices. By Proposition
4.6
again, it is easy to see that
\mbox{\rm tors}(\mathcal{A})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\rightarrow}}\mbox{\rm torf}(\mathcal{A})^{{\rm op}};~\mathcal{T}\mapsto\mathcal{T}^{\perp}
is an isomorphism of complete lattices.

(4)
Let
\mathcal{U}\in\mbox{\rm tors}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})
and
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mbox{\rm tors}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})
such that
\mathcal{U}\wedge\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{U}\wedge\mathcal{V}
for any
\mathcal{T},\mathcal{V}\in\mathcal{L}
. The inclusion
\mathcal{U}\wedge\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{U}\wedge(\bigvee\mathcal{L})
is obvious. For the other one, by Lemma
4.9
, we have
\mathcal{U}\wedge(\bigvee\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{U}\cap\mbox{\rm{Filt}}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{X})
, where
\mathcal{X}=\bigcup_{\mathcal{L}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{L}_{i}
. For any
0\neq L\in\mathcal{U}\wedge(\bigvee\mathcal{L})
, there exists a stable
\mathbb{E}
-triangle
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\rightarrowtail}}L\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\twoheadrightarrow}}M\dashrightarrow
with
X\in\mathcal{X}
and
l_{\mathcal{X}}(M)=l_{\mathcal{X}}(L)-1
. If
l_{\mathcal{X}}(L)=1
, then
L\in\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{L}_{i}=\mathcal{U}\wedge\mathcal{T}
for some
\mathcal{L}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}
. For the general case, we observe that
M\in\mathcal{U}\wedge(\bigvee\mathcal{L})
since
\mathcal{U}
is a torsion class. By induction hypothesis, we have
M\in\mathcal{U}\wedge\mathcal{T}
and so is
L
. By duality, we can prove the second condition in Definition
4.11
.

(5)
For any
\mathcal{T}\in\mbox{\rm tors}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})
, we can check directly that
\mathcal{T}=\bigvee_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\mathrm{T}_{\Theta}(T)
. It suffices to prove
\mathrm{T}_{\Theta}(T)
is compact. Suppose that
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mbox{\rm tors}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})
and
\mathrm{T}_{\Theta}(T)\subseteq\mathcal{L}
. Since
T\in\bigvee\mathcal{L}=\mbox{\rm{Filt}}_{\Theta}(\bigcup_{\mathcal{L}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{L}_{i})
, there exists a finite set

such that

. Thus
\mathrm{T}_{\Theta}(T)\subseteq\mathrm{T}_{\Theta}(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}L_{i})=\bigvee\mathcal{S}
. This shows that
\mathrm{T}_{\Theta}(T)
is compact.
∎

4.4.

For any

For a set

Theorem 4.18
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\Theta)
be a length category. Suppose that
[\mathcal{U},\mathcal{T}]\subseteq\mbox{\rm tors}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})
.

(1)
We have
\#[\mathcal{U},\mathcal{T}]=1
if and only if
brick
(\mathcal{U}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{T})=\emptyset
.

(2)
If
\#
brick
(\mathcal{U}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{T})=1
, then
\#[\mathcal{U},\mathcal{T}]=2
.

(3)
If
\#[\mathcal{U},\mathcal{T}]=2
, then there exists a brick
S\in{\rm brick}(\mathcal{U}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{T})
satisfying the following conditions:

(i)

\Theta(S)\leq\Theta(S^{\prime})
for any
S^{\prime}\in{\rm brick}(\mathcal{U}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{T})
.

(ii)

\mathcal{T}=\mathrm{T}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{U}\cup S)
and
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{T}\cap{{}^{\perp}}S
.

(iii)

S

Moreover, the brick

is unique up to isomorphism.

Proof..

(1) If
\#[\mathcal{U},\mathcal{T}]=1
, then brick
(\mathcal{U}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{T})=\emptyset
by Lemma
4.14
. Conversely, suppose that brick
(\mathcal{U}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{T})=\emptyset
. If
\mathcal{U}\subsetneq\mathcal{T}
, then there exists a non-zero object
X\in\mathcal{U}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{T}
. Then by Lemma
4.13
there is a brick
X^{\prime}\in{\rm brick}(\mathcal{U}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{T})
, this is a contradiction.

(2)
Suppose that
\#
brick
(\mathcal{U}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{T})=1
and
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
. Clearly,
\#{\rm brick}(\mathcal{U}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{V})\leq 1
. If
\#{\rm brick}(\mathcal{U}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{V})=0

\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{V}
by (1). If
\#{\rm brick}(\mathcal{U}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{V})=1
, then
\mathcal{U}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{U}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{T}
by Lemma
4.14
. By using Lemma
4.15
(1), we have
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{U}\ast(\mathcal{U}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{V})=\mathcal{T}
. This shows that
\#[\mathcal{U},\mathcal{T}]=2
.

(3)
For any

, we set
\mathcal{T}_{S}:=\mathrm{T}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{U}\cup S)
and
\mathcal{U}_{S}=\mathcal{T}\cap\mathrm{T}_{\Theta}(^{\perp}S)
. Then one can check that
\mathcal{U}\subsetneq\mathcal{T}_{S}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{S}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{S}
.

We can take
S\in{\rm brick}(\mathcal{U}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{T})
such that
\Theta(S)\leq\Theta(S^{\prime})
for any

. Since

, we have
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{S}}
. Thus, we get
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{T}\cap{{}^{\perp}}S
by Lemma
4.15
. So we have shown (i) and (ii). For (iii), we take

. Observe that
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{S^{\prime}}}
. If
S^{\prime}\in{{}^{\perp}}S
, then
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}=\mathrm{T}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{U}\cup S^{\prime})\subseteq\mathcal{T}\cap{{}^{\perp}}S=\mathcal{U}
, this is a contradiction. Thus, there exists a non-zero morphism
f:S^{\prime}\rightarrow S
in
\mathcal{A}
. We take a
\Theta
-decomposition
(i_{f},X_{f},j_{f})
of

. Clearly,

. By Lemma
4.13
, there exists a brick

such that
\Theta(S^{\prime\prime})\leq\Theta(X_{f})\leq\Theta(S)

\Theta(X_{f})=\Theta(S)
and hence

is an isomorphism. Then

and thus

. In particular, if
\Theta(S^{\prime})=\Theta(S)
, then

is an isomorphism. This means that

is unique up to isomorphism. By symmetry, there exists a non-zero morphism

with a
\Theta
-decomposition
(i_{g},X_{g},j_{g})

4.13
, we have

.
∎

Remark 4.26

Suppose that

Let
P=(P,\leq)
be a partially ordered set. For any

, we define
a_{\ast}=\bigvee\{b\in P~|~b<a\}
and
a^{\ast}=\bigwedge\{b\in P~|~a>b\}
. An element

is

if

for some

implies

. In this case,

is the unique element covered by

. If

is a finite set, then the converse is true. One can check that

is completely join-irreducible if and only if

. In the same way, an element
a\in P
is
completely meet-irreducible
if
a=\bigwedge L
for some
L\subseteq P
implies
a\in L
, equivalently,
a\neq a^{\ast}
. We denote by j-irr

(resp. m-irr
{}^{\rm c}(P)

.

([\mathcal{U},\mathcal{T}])

(1)

Next we show that

(3)

5.
Support
\tau
-tilting subcategories in extriangulated length categories

Our aim in this section is to establish a unified framework to study the
\tau
-tilting theory in length categories. In this section, we always assume that
((\mathcal{A},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{s}),\Theta)
is a length category. We start with the following notations.

For a subcategory
\mathcal{T}
, we define

{}^{\perp_{\Theta}}\mathcal{T}=\{M\in\mathcal{A}~|~\mathbb{E}_{\Theta}(M,T)=0~\text{for any}~T\in\mathcal{T}\}.

Dually, we can define
\mathcal{T}^{\perp_{\Theta}}
. An object
P\in\mathcal{T}
is called
\Theta
-projective
if
P\in{{}^{\perp_{\Theta}}}\mathcal{T}
. We denote the subcategory of
\Theta
-projective objects in
\mathcal{T}
by
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{T})
. Dually, the
\Theta
-injective
objects are defined, and the full subcategory of
\Theta
-injective objects in
\mathcal{T}
is denoted by
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{T})
. We say
\mathcal{A}

has enough
\Theta
-projectives
if for any
M\in\mathcal{A}
, there exists a
\Theta
-deflation
P\twoheadrightarrow M
satisfying
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
. Dually, we define that
\mathscr{C}

has enough
\Theta
-injectives
.

Remark 5.1
.

Recall that
((\mathcal{A},\mathbb{E}_{\Theta},\mathfrak{s}_{\Theta}),\Theta)
is a stable length category (see Proposition
3.22
). It is easy to see that
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
(resp.
I\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{A})
) if and only if
P
(resp.
I
) is projective (resp. injective) in
(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{E}_{\Theta},\mathfrak{s}_{\Theta})
.

We consider the following two conditions for a subcategory
\mathcal{T}
:

({\dagger}):\text{For any}~P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A}),~\text{there is a left}~\mathcal{T}\text{-approximation}~f:P\rightarrow T~\text{with}~T\in\mathcal{T}.

({\ddagger}):\text{For any}~P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A}),~\text{there is a left}~\mathcal{T}\text{-approximation}~f:P\rightarrow T~\text{with}~T\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{T}).

Then we can introduce the following two definitions.

Definition 5.2
.

We say that a torsion class
\mathcal{T}
is
support
if it satisfies the condition
({\ddagger})
.

Definition 5.3
.

A subcategory
\mathcal{T}
of
\mathcal{A}
is a
support
\tau
-tilting subcategory
if it satisfies:

(1)

\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{P}({\mbox{\rm Fac}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{T})})
,

(2)

\mathcal{T}
is
\tau
-rigid
, i.e.
\mathcal{T}\subseteq{{}^{\perp_{\Theta}}}{\mbox{\rm Fac}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{T})}
,

(3)

\mathcal{T}
satisfies the condition
({\dagger})
.

We call an object
T\in\mathcal{A}
is a
support
\tau
-tilting object
if
\mbox{\rm add}(T)
is a support
\tau
-tilting subcategory.

We denote by
{\rm s}\tau
-
{\rm tilt(\mathcal{A}})
(resp.
{\rm s}
-
{\rm tors_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})}
) the set of support
\tau
-tilting subcategories (resp. support torsion classes) in
\mathcal{A}
.

Remark 5.4
.

When
\Theta
is not stable, our notion of support

\tau
-tilting subcategories differs from that in
[
23
, Definition 3.5]
.
Specifically, we only require that
\mathbb{E}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{T},{\mbox{\rm Fac}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{T})})=0
,
whereas
[
23
]
imposes the stronger condition
\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{T},{{\rm Defl}(\mathcal{T})})=0
,
where
{\rm Defl}(\mathcal{T})=\{F\in\mathcal{A}\,\mid\mbox{there exists a deflation}~U\xrightarrow{~g~}F~\mbox{for some}~U\in\mathcal{T}\}
.
However, if
\Theta
is stable, then support
\tau
-tilting subcategories defined in
[
23
]
also qualify as support
\tau
-tilting subcategories in our framework (cf.
[
23
, Lemma 3.7]
).

Remark 5.5
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finite dimensional algebra. We set
\Theta=l_{{\rm sim({\rm mod}\Lambda)}}
. As stated in Example
3.10
, we regard
(\mbox{\rm{mod}}\,\Lambda,\Theta)
as a stable length category. In this case, stable
\mathbb{E}
-triangles are precisely short exact sequences of
\mbox{\rm{mod}}\,\Lambda
.

(1)
Let
\mathcal{T}
be a support torsion class. Then
\mathcal{T}
is functorially finite by Proposition
5.9
and
[
7
, Proposition 2.1]
. Conversely, any functorially finite torsion class

is support by
[
1
, Theorem 2.7]

[
14
, Proposition 2.14]
. This shows that support torsion classes in module category are just functorially finite torsion classes.

(2)
Let
\mathcal{S}
be a support
\tau
-tilting subcategory. By definition, there exists a right exact sequence

(5.1)

\Lambda\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\rightarrow}}S_{1}\rightarrow S_{2}\rightarrow 0

such that

is a left
\mathcal{S}
-approximation with

. We claim that

. To see this, we take a
\Theta

of

to the sequence (

Note that

is a left
\mbox{\rm Fac}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{S})
-approximation (see Lemma

is also a left
\mbox{\rm Fac}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{S})
-approximation. This implies that

is an epimorphism. On the other hand, since

and

.

We are ready to state the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\Theta)
be a length category with enough
\Theta
-projectives. There is a bijection

{\rm s}\tau\text{-}{\rm tilt(\mathcal{A})}\longrightarrow{\rm s}\text{-}\mbox{\rm tors}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})

given by
\mathcal{S}\mapsto\mbox{\rm Fac}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{S})
with the inverse
\mathcal{T}\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{T})
.

Before proving Theorem
5.6
, we need some preparations.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a
\tau
-rigid subcategory of
\mathcal{A}
. If
f:P\rightarrow T
is a left
\mathcal{T}
-approximation for some
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
, then
f
is a left
\mbox{\rm Fac}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{T})
-approximation.

Proof..

Clearly,
T\in\mbox{\rm Fac}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{T})
. Suppose that
g:P\rightarrow X
is a morphism in
\mathcal{A}
such that
X\in\mbox{\rm Fac}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{T})
. By hypothesis, there exists a
\Theta
-deflation
h:T^{\prime}\twoheadrightarrow X
for some
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
. Since
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
, there exists a morphism
s:P\rightarrow T^{\prime}
such that
g=hs
. Since
f
is a left
\mathcal{T}
-approximation, there exists a morphism
t:T\rightarrow T^{\prime}
such that
s=tf
. In particular, we have
g=htf
. This shows
f
is a left
\mbox{\rm Fac}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{T})
-approximation.
∎

A subcategory
\mathcal{F}
of
\mathcal{A}
is a
support
\tau^{-1}
-tilting subcategory
if it satisfies:

(1)

\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{I}({\mbox{\rm Sub}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{F})})
,

(2)

\mathcal{T}
is
\tau^{-1}
-rigid
, i.e.
\mathcal{T}\subseteq{\mbox{\rm Sub}(\mathcal{T})}{{}^{\perp_{\Theta}}}
.

(3)

For each
I\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{A})
, there exists a right
\mathcal{F}
-approximation
F\rightarrow I
with
F\in\mathcal{F}
.

We call an object
T\in\mathcal{A}
is a
support
\tau^{-1}
-tilting object
if
\mbox{\rm add}(T)
is a support
\tau^{-1}
-tilting subcategory.

We denote by
{\rm s}\tau^{-1}
-
{\rm tilt(\mathcal{A})}
(resp.
{\rm s}
-
{\rm torf_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})}
) the set of support
\tau^{-1}
-tilting subcategories (resp. support torsion-free classes) in
\mathcal{A}
. We obtain the dual version of Theorem
5.6
.

Theorem 5.14
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\Theta)
be a length category with enough
\Theta
-injective objects. There is a bijection

{\rm s}\tau^{-1}\text{-}{\rm tilt(\mathcal{A})}\longrightarrow{\rm s}\text{-}\mbox{\rm torf}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})

given by
\mathcal{S}\mapsto\mbox{\rm Sub}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{S})
with the inverse
\mathcal{F}\mapsto\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{F})
.

Next we consider when torsion classes and torsion-free classes are support. For this purpose, we introduce the following notion.

Definition 5.15
.

We say that a length category
(\mathcal{A},\Theta)
is
\tau
-tilting finite
if

\mbox{\rm tors}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})={\rm s}\text{-}\mbox{\rm tors}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})~\text{and}~\mbox{\rm torf}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})={\rm s}\text{-}\mbox{\rm torf}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A}).

Remark 5.16
.

(1) Suppose that
\mathcal{A}
is Hom-finite and there are only finitely many isomorphism classes of indecomposable objects. We can easily check that
\mbox{\rm tors}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})={\rm s}\text{-}\mbox{\rm tors}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})
and
\mbox{\rm torf}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})={\rm s}\text{-}\mbox{\rm torf}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{A})
. This means that
(\mathcal{A},\Theta)
is
\tau
-tilting finite.

(2) We say a finite dimensional algebra
\Lambda
is
\tau
-tilting finite
(cf.
[
6
, Definition 1.1]
) if there are only finitely many isomorphism classes of basic
\tau
-tilting
\Lambda
-modules. It was shown in
[
6
, Theorem 3.8]
) that
\Lambda
is
\tau
-tilting finite if and only if every torsion class in
\mbox{\rm{mod}}\,\Lambda
is functorially finite if and only if every torsion-free class in
\mbox{\rm{mod}}\,\Lambda
is functorially finite. Thus, our definition coincides with that in
[
6
, Definition 1.1]
.

When
\mathcal{A}
is
\tau
-tilting finite, we have the following bijection between support
\tau
-tilting subcategories and support
\tau^{-1}
-tilting subcategories.

Theorem 5.17
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{A}
has enough
\Theta
-projectives and
\Theta
-injectives. If
\mathcal{A}
is
\tau
-tilting finite, there is a bijection

{\rm s}\tau\text{-}{\rm tilt(\mathcal{A})}\longrightarrow{\rm s}\tau^{-1}\text{-}{\rm tilt(\mathcal{A})}

given by
\mathcal{S}\mapsto\mathcal{I}(\mbox{\rm Fac}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{S})^{\perp})
with the inverse
\mathcal{T}\mapsto\mathcal{P}(^{\perp}\mbox{\rm Sub}_{\Theta}(\mathcal{T})).

Proof..

This follows from Proposition
4.6
, Theorem
5.6
and Theorem
5.14
.
∎

Remark 5.18
.

In general, the converse of Theorem
5.17
is not true. For instance, if
\mathcal{A}=\mbox{\rm{mod}}\,\Lambda
for a finite dimensional algebra
\Lambda
, then we have a bijection
{\rm s}\tau\text{-}{\rm tilt~\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow{\rm s}\tau^{-1}\text{-}{\rm tilt~\mathcal{A}}
(cf.
[
1
, Section 2.4]
). But
\mathcal{A}
is
\tau
-tilting finite if and only if the set of bricks is finite (cf.
[
6
, Theorem 4.2]
).
