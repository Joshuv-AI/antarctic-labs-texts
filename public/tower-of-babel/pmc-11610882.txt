Menstruation and masculinity: Exploring the relationship between boys and menstruation
Authors: Anusree Jayamohan, Shashi P. Tomar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Menstruation is considered a “women’s” topic in India because the majority of studies on the subject solely include girls and women. Adolescent boys are excluded from this discussion, which obscures the fact that menstruation is a social epidemiological phenomenon with ingrained gender disparities. In order to motivate boys to contribute significantly to women’s health, research on their knowledge, attitudes, and experiences linked to menstruation, including socioeconomic factors, must be done. Reducing gender differences in adolescent health requires addressing this.Objectives:The study aims to explore the knowledge and attitudes of school-going adolescent boys in urban Jabalpur regarding menstruation, considering factors such as family structure and sociocultural practices.Materials and Methods:Qualitative data using focus group discussions with 25 boys, age group 13-17 years from Jabalpur Urban area, Madhya Pradesh was conducted. Data was analysed by thematic analysis.Results:There was a mixture of curiosity and embarrassment about menstruation. Many were misinformed and lacked correct knowledge. Most of the children gained information from either the parents or their peer group. Sex education is not given properly in most of the schools. Boys with either sisters or female friends has more knowledge and good attitude regarding menstruation.Conclusion:Our study highlights the need of improving Indian boys’ understanding and beliefs about menstruation by addressing the social determinants that influence their menstruation-related experiences.

Introduction

Adolescents (10–19 years old) face a variety of sexual and reproductive health (SRH) challenges during this transitional period. While roughly 27% of teenage girls (“girls”) get married, 1 in 6 have children, and 10% of teenage boys (“boys”) have premarital sex, many teenagers are unaware of healthy behaviours and have unfulfilled treatment needs.[1] Menstruation-related communication, widely seen as a ‘women’s topic,’ is frequently a one-way prescriptive process in which girls get abrupt and oblique instructions from a same-sex parent or a teacher with a limited role for men.[2] Menstrual ideology both reflect and perpetuate patriarchal societal structures that favour men and boys, and boys begin utilizing menstruation to establish dominance while they are young. Also, taunts and jokes on menstruation frequently revolve around menstrual women being unclean or disgusting and might serve to maintain a patriarchal gender hierarchy.[34]

Adolescents must have access to comprehensive sex education that takes into account their developmental stages, is taught by qualified educators, and includes their families in the process.[5] Boys in India mostly learn about menstruation and puberty from unofficial sources, overheard conversations, and cultural customs. The social, family, school, and peer contexts have the greatest influence on late-adolescent sexuality.[6] Menstruation is covered in some boys’ curricula, but many believe it is not comprehensive or is left out. The majority of boys are both curious and uncomfortable when it comes to menstruation, and some are even misinformed. Boys’ experiences are influenced by social and individual factors; for example, boys’ knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs are positively influenced by private, unaided schools, parent education, and teacher comfort.[78]

Numerous studies on boys, men, and menstruation have discovered that menstruation is not only not a topic that they are comfortable discussing but also not a domain for them. To address these issues and bring about change, men may need to become MHM advocates alongside women, especially in patriarchal environments. A study done in Limpopo Province highlights the need to address factors hindering communication like cultural barriers, role shifting in imparting sexuality education, and poor parent–child relationships.[9] They may need to have their ignorance about menstruation and their attitudes about it corrected before they can act as change agents. Therefore, the purpose of the current study was to evaluate the ability of adolescent males to support their “sisters” by examining their attitudes and knowledge regarding menstruation. The research questions were What knowledge do boys have regarding menstruation? What is their source of knowledge about menstruation? What are their thoughts on menstruation and menstruating girls?

Material and Methods

We took great care in terms of ethics and confidentiality because this is a difficult topic to discuss with adolescents, and we followed the protocol approved by the NSCB Institutional Ethics Committee. The survey required verbal approval from responsible people and school officials, and it was completely anonymous. We gained approval from the school officials. We obtained verbal agreement from each participant prior to data collection after explaining the study’s goals and method and assuring them of confidentiality and anonymity. We have taken care to follow the ethics process that the ethics committee has approved. Qualitative study was carried out in schools in Jabalpur urban area from Madhya Pradesh, followed for collecting the data from adolescent boys aged 13 to 17 years using focussed group discussion method. The multi-stage random sampling method was employed for the study. Jabalpur urban area is divided into four zones. A list of schools based on approval by principals was prepared. Focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted with 25 research participants from randomly selected institutions based on their voluntary participation. Rights of the study participants were briefed, and confidentiality of their responses ensured. FGDs were facilitated by a moderator and a notetaker. The note-taker captured the main points, group dynamics, and nonverbal gestures. The FGDs were held in the state’s local tongue and were documented. Each participant was given a number, which they announced while speaking. This was necessary so that the quotes could be attributed to the contributor on the recording and the transcript that followed. FGD lasted approximately 35 min and was transcribed verbatim, then translated directly into English, and counterchecked using back translation. Thematic analysis was used to analyse the participants’ findings. Narratives were represented by a set of codes, which were merged into a coding frame, from which main themes were described. The overall framework was reviewed by a second member of the team. By arranging the coded materials within the relevant theme, making sure it fit the designated theme, and contrasting and comparing the descriptions to make sense of the degree of agreement, contradictions, and intensity of feeling, a narrative was woven. Quotes are presented, with participant codes, for example, P1, where the participant number is 1 and so on.

Knowledge, experiences, perceptions, and attitude regarding menstruation among adolescent boys

Results were categorised into three themes: knowledge, attitude, and personal experience towards menstruation and menstruating girls among adolescent boys. We explored this in sub-themes misconceptions, curriculum gap, cues to acquire knowledge, traditional outlook, menstruation as a normal function, disease and isolation, and supporting girls. FGD started with a question about participants’ knowledge of menstruation and their understanding of it, following the warm-up and introductions. There was shame or reluctance to discuss this topic. This was demonstrated in all the groups by awkward laughter or quiet when the moderator attempted to start a conversation.

We started our discussion as

“Yes” (PI)

All other participants were quiet.

“Have you received any formal education about menstruation in school or at home?” (M)

“No” (PI)

“What do you understand about menstruation?” (M)

“I don’t know exactly” (P5)

“You said that you know” (M). After continuous denials, they started discussing it.

Knowledge about menstruation

The boys’ comprehension of menstruation varied greatly between and within the groups with respect to its biological function. Their knowledge ranged from a vague belief that it involved blood to a comprehensive understanding of the reproductive cycle.

“I think it is a natural condition” (P2)

“It is a physiological condition in which blood is ejected from the body of women.” (P4)

“How long does a typical menstrual cycle last?” (M)

“I have a sister. She has it for about a week” (P12)

Conversation regarding menstruation

Many of them found too difficult to discuss it with their mothers and sisters. But they were comfortable to discuss with female friends and peers.

“With female friends. yes” (P15)

“Never with family” (P17)

“We usually discuss among ourselves.” (P24)

“Certain things are in our textbooks, though they are not taught always. We browse on internet as well sometimes.”

Most of the boys struggled to understand menstruation due to the surrounding secrecy and the scant information they had access to, much of which was false or was cobbled together incorrectly. Majority of participants face resistance against discussing menstruation.

“It’s a bit embarrassing.” (P21)

“I am a bit uncomfortable for me also” (P18)

Attitude towards menstruation and menstruators

Even though none of the boys knew why the customs were followed or that menstruation was the reason behind them, they all seemed to be aware of the different taboos and customs that limited the lives of girls. The majority of descriptions spoke of isolation, absenteeism from school, forbidden worship, and forbidden contact with humans and food. Majority of participants were against restricting women from religious and outdoor activities.

“They don’t come to school on those days.” (P14)

“In my place, we don’t even share food or water with them. They stay away from everyone. Even mom does so.” (P17)

“Actually, they don’t cook or even stays away from worships.” (P2)

“It is like that. May be because they are impure. We have grown up seeing that” (P20)

“I think they should be supported” (P15)

“Actually, we should be given proper knowledge” (P20)

Personal experience

Most of the boys accepted the fact that girls were made fun of their periods. They even acknowledged that despite knowing this, they never felt bad about it. When a female friend or family member was menstruating, the majority of the boys supported her or bought her menstruation products.

“Have you ever made fun of or teased a girl because of her period or menstrual-related matters?” (M)

“Unfortunately, yes, now I feel embarrassed” (P22)

“Most of us have I guess.” (P20)

Most of them nodded their head in agreement.

“Have you ever helped a female friend or family member during their period by buying menstrual products or offering support?” (M)

“For sisters … yes” (P11)

“Never”

“Have you ever witnessed girls or women facing challenges or discrimination due to menstruation?” (M)

“Yes, mostly they stay isolated.” (P14)

“I actually thought that’s how it is supposed to be.” (P10)

Personal experiences show that most individuals exhibit positive behaviours, promoting empathy and understanding. However, witnessed discrimination is prevalent, indicating the need for broader societal change.

“When the time is right, you will know.” But when?

The first encounter with menstruation for boys is a “mysterious incident” or “encounter that left them asking, ‘What’s going on here? What am I not supposed to know?’”. I had an opportunity to be a part of sexual and reproductive health education programs in a number of schools while I was employed in Kerala. The main reason I’m interested in this field is the overwhelming response I received. Our younger generation must be our objective if we are to effect change. Despite it being a required component of the curriculum, teachers seem to have ignored or overlooked the Adolescence Education Programme (MHRD 2020).[10] It might take special capacity building to transfer knowledge effectively because the majority of teachers lack professional training or norms for teaching such sensitive subjects. Education within the school curriculum for boys throughout their formative years, when they are ready to learn and the material may be handled, appears to be critical. Most boys struggled to understand menstruation due to the surrounding secrecy and the scant information they had access to, much of which was false or was cobbled together incorrectly. They converse with their friends and the media, which further misleads them. The idea that menstruation is an illness is concerning in this day and age, when everything is accessible at one’s fingertips.

The blood shed: should we bother?

“Why should boys know about this?”,

“We cannot let our students talk about these things in public,” and

“We are afraid that the lads will make fun of girls in their class.”

I personally received these opinions from school administrators including ‘men and women’. The idea that menstruation is not only not a topic that suits males but also not a realm in which they feel comfortable is an underlying theme concerning boys, men, and menstruation. That is, we raise them as such. This is precisely the reason it is time for us to have this conversation. Most of the boys are unaware of the reason behind this bloodshed. Despite the fact that most participants encounter reluctance when talking about menstruation, they believe that sex education need to be provided. It provided hope that the majority of participants opposed preventing women from engaging in outdoor or religious activities while they were menstruating.

Is she impure?

In a nation like India, there are millions of tales, customs, and myths that come from all over the place and are all various facets of the same entity. There are a large number of injustices that we have been inflicting on our women for centuries, including treating them as unclean and untouchable, prohibiting them from worship, keeping them in a separate place outside the house, and keeping them away from food because they may contaminate it. All these practices have not been supported by science. It is regrettable that these are still in use today and that we talk about gender neutrality. We observed that boys were empathetic and compassionate towards their female peers who were menstruating. What struck was that despite witnessing discrimination against women because of their menstruation, they accepted it as a routine occurrence. In contrast to the others, we observed guys who had sisters and female companions responding favourably, being more knowledgeable, and having a positive attitude. Sisters had a greater influence on their individual experiences with periods than did female friends.

Palpating the change

Their sisters and mothers are menstruating or have been. They could have menstruating partners. Their daughters will be menstruating. They live in the same communities as menstruators. Menstruation can truly be made normative when people, regardless of gender identity, understand what it is, what the menstrual cycle entails, and how it affects (and does not affect) women. Currently, menstruation is framed in educational contexts as a normal biological process that should be taboo to discuss in public. Even though the majority of men may not experience menstruation, comfort can lead to open communication, openness can encourage cultural positive, and information can help.

Discussion

Men’s knowledge is currently the subject of very little research, and stories told by women and girls dominate discussions of menstrual hygiene. Even fewer studies have specifically looked into MHM in males. This small body of knowledge is augmented by the current study, which gathers primary data from teenage schoolboys.

When we evaluated the boys’ knowledge of menstruation, we found that most of them had difficulty understanding it because of the secrecy surrounding it and the limited amount of information they could find—much of it inaccurate or poorly assembled. There are certain misconceptions regarding the menstrual cycle, such as the notion that it is a physical “disease” that resembles the results of a study by Mason et al.[7] Contrary to the results of related studies,[711] where participants relied more on peers, teachers, and the media, more than half of participants discussed menstruation with family. This indicates that communication and knowledge sources are diverse. In line with earlier research, our study shows that most participants experience resistance when it comes to talking about their periods.[11] Research with teenagers in Taiwan and Rwanda has also reported on this.[1213] Contrary to earlier research, the majority of participants opposed preventing women from participating in outdoor and religious activities.[14]

Education within the school curriculum for boys throughout their formative years, when they are ready to learn and the material may be handled, appears to be critical. The current study raises concerns about the apparent lack of education acquired by boys, which a substantial percentage believes in, showing potential for breaking societal taboos. Although the Adolescence Education Programme (MHRD 2020) is part of the curriculum, teachers appear to have neglected or skipped over it. Most teachers lack formal training or norms for teaching such a sensitive subject and may require specific capacity building to properly transfer information.

Based on personal experiences, the majority of people display positive behaviours that foster empathy and understanding. Many of the students accepted that they have witnessed discrimination; it is clear that more extensive societal change is required. Correlating this with a study by Mason et al.[7] was interesting. Targeted educational initiatives, encouraging candid discussions, inclusive education, efforts to de-stigmatize, behavioural change initiatives, and multi-channel awareness are some of the main conclusions and suggestions.

Conclusion

Men are considered as uneasy discussing menstruation since it is seen as the domain of women, which is at the core of this troublesome cycle. Men are not expected to deal with menstruation; thus, it is also seen to be unnecessary to educate them about it in great detail. This ignorance may be a contributing factor to unfavourable attitudes, stereotyped ideas, and overall unease. It is essential to interrupt this vicious cycle.
