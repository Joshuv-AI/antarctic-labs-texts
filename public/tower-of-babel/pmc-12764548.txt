Importance of the 3′UTR region in globin synthesis: identification of two novel HBA1 mutations causing α-Thalassemia
Authors: Sara Ferrer Benito, Mariola Abío, Eduardo J Bardón-Cancho, Jorge M Nieto, Belén Ortega, F A González, Ana Villegas, Celina Benavente, Paloma Ropero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlpha-thalassemia is primarily caused by large deletions in the HBA1 and HBA2 genes, but a minority of cases result from non-deletional mutations, including variants in untranslated regions (UTRs) that affect mRNA regulation. The 3′UTR is crucial for post-transcriptional control, influencing mRNA stability, localization, and translation via binding elements for RNA-binding proteins and microRNAs. We describe two patients with persistent microcytosis and normal iron levels. Standard hematological analyses were complemented by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and capillary electrophoresis to rule out common hemoglobinopathies. Molecular analysis was performed using multiplex PCR and direct sequencing of the HBA1 3′UTR. Patient 1, a 3-year-old Nigerian boy, carried a novel insertion mutation (HBA1:c.*119_120insT) five nucleotides downstream of the transcription termination signal. Patient 2, a 31-year-old Moroccan woman, exhibited a missense variant (HBA1:c.*150C > A) located 40 nucleotides downstream of the TTS. Both variants are located within regulatory regions of the HBA1 3′UTR. In silico analyses suggest disruption of RNA secondary structure, impaired interactions with HuR and AUF1 proteins, altered microRNA binding (e.g., miR-16-5p), and potential interference with polyadenylation signals, resulting in mRNA instability and reduced alpha-globin synthesis. These two novel mutations in the 3′UTR of HBA1 expand the spectrum of non-deletional alpha-thalassemia variants and highlight the importance of regulatory regions in globin gene expression. Their inclusion in routine molecular screening may enhance diagnostic accuracy in cases of unresolved microcytic anemia.Keywords: Alpha-thalassemia, HBA1, 3′UTR, mRNA stability, Non-coding variants, Post-transcriptional regulation, Microcytosis

Introduction

Alpha-thalassemia is a hematologic disorder characterized by a reduction in the synthesis of alpha-globin chains. Approximately 90–95% of cases are attributed to large deletions in the HBA1 and HBA2 genes [1], while the remaining 5–10% have been associated with point mutations in coding, intronic, or promoter regions. In recent years, variants in untranslated regions, such as the 3′ untranslated region (UTR), have attracted growing interest as their role in gene expression regulation is being progressively elucidated.

The 3′UTR plays a crucial role in the post-transcriptional regulation of mRNA, as it harbors regulatory elements that influence mRNA stability, determine subcellular localization, and modulate translational efficiency [2, 3]. This region includes AU-rich sequences, known as AU-rich elements (AREs), which determine the half-life of mRNA through interaction with regulatory proteins. The most characteristic is HuR (human antigen R, also known as ELAV1), which binds to these elements to promote mRNA stability and enhance translation, and AU-rich element RNA-binding protein 1 (AUF1, also known as heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein D, hnRNP D), which can induce mRNA degradation, thereby modulating protein synthesis levels [4].

The 3′UTR also contains specific binding sites for microRNAs such as miR-16-5p and miR-92a, which can inhibit translation or promote mRNA degradation [5]. Another key element within the 3′UTR is the polyadenylation signal (PolyA signal), a consensus sequence essential for proper mRNA processing and stability, facilitating export to the cytoplasm and efficient translation [6].

Therefore, alterations in the 3′UTR, even without affecting the coding sequence, may disrupt interactions with these regulatory elements and subsequently alter mRNA stability, localization, and translation. Such changes may account for subtle variations in alpha-globin expression and contribute to the clinical heterogeneity observed in this group of hemoglobinopathies.

In this context, we present two novel mutations in the 3′UTR of the HBA1 gene that may help elucidate the role of post-transcriptional regulation in the pathogenesis of alpha-thalassemia.

Although only two variants have been reported so far in the HBA1 3′UTR, both were associated with clinically relevant phenotypes. The substitution HBA1:c.*46C > A was identified in patients with mild non-deletional α-thalassemia characterized by microcytosis and hypochromia without transfusion dependence [7]. Similarly, the HBA1:c.*96G > A variant affecting the polyadenylation signal was reported in individuals with persistent microcytic anemia and reduced α-globin expression [8]. These cases highlight the clinical impact of non-coding variants in the α-globin genes and support the importance of considering the 3′UTR in unresolved cases of microcytic anemia.

Materials and methods

Two unrelated patients were investigated at the Hemoglobinopathies Reference Laboratory of the Hospital Clínico San Carlos (Madrid, Spain). Hematological parameters were assessed using an automated blood cell counter (Coulter LH750 Analyzer; Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). Hemoglobin fractions (HbA2 and HbF) were analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (VARIANT™; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA), including the β-Thalassemia Short Program. In parallel, samples were also analyzed by capillary zone electrophoresis using a Sebia Capillarys Flex system (Sebia, Norcross, GA, USA). As our laboratory is a national reference center, both techniques are routinely applied in a complementary manner, rather than sequentially, to maximize sensitivity in detecting hemoglobinopathies. This combined approach ensures accurate screening and confirmation of subtle variants or abnormal fractions. Ferritin and iron studies were obtained to exclude nutritional deficiencies. Vitamin B12 and folate were also measured in Patient 2 due to clinical suspicion of deficiency.

Both patients were investigated following the same standardized diagnostic pathway applied in our reference laboratory, including hematological assessment, HPLC, capillary electrophoresis, iron status, and molecular analysis.

Quality control procedures. The reliability of hematological and biochemical measurements was ensured by daily internal quality control provided by the manufacturer, and by participation in external quality assessment schemes every three months (Abnormal Hemoglobins HbA2/F & S program, UK NEQAS Haematology and Transfusion). Molecular analyses were subject to routine internal and external quality control (UK NEQAS Haematology and Transfusion), guaranteeing accuracy and reproducibility of the results.

Genomic DNA was isolated using an automated system (Biorobot EZ1; Qiagen GmbH, Hilden, Germany) and quantified using a Qubit 3 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The most common α-globin mutations were excluded by multiplex PCR followed by reverse-hybridization using a commercial Alpha-Globin StripAssay kit (ViennaLab Diagnostic GmbH, Vienna, Austria). Molecular characterization was carried out through automated sequencing using an ABI PRISM™ 3100 Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The HBA1 gene was specifically amplified using the P1A (5’-AGCGCCGCCCGGCCGGGCGT-3’) and C2R (5’-CAGAGAGGTTCTAGCCATGTGTG-3’) primers; the C2R primer is specific to the 3′UTR of the α1-globin gene. The PCR product was sequenced using the ABI PRISM™ BigDye® Terminator v1.1 Cycle Sequencing Ready Reaction Kit (Applied Biosystems). Primers used for sequencing included P1A, PB (5’-CCCGCCCGGACCCCACA-3’), and P1C (5’-AGATGGCGCCTTCCTCTCAG-3’).

Variants are described according to the Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS) recommendations (https://varnomen.hgvs.org/). The “c.” numbering refers to the HBA1 coding DNA sequence, while positions in the 3′UTR are denoted with an asterisk (e.g., c.*119).

This study was conducted following the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The Ethics Committee of Hospital Clínico San Carlos, Madrid, Spain, approved the research protocol. Written informed consent was obtained from the adult patient and the legal guardians of the pediatric patient before the use of clinical and genetic data for research purposes.

Results

Patient 1 was a three-year-old Nigerian boy residing in a foster care center in Madrid, Spain. He presented with persistent microcytosis (MCV 72.1 fL), hypochromia (MCH 23.7 pg), and mild anemia (Hb 11.1 g/dL). Iron studies were within the normal range and no nutritional deficiencies were detected. The child had no history of transfusions and family history was unavailable due to foster care status.

Patient 2 was a 31-year-old woman from Morocco, living in Toledo (Spain), was initially referred for vitamin B12 deficiency and gastric atrophy. After appropriate treatment and correction of iron and B12 status, microcytosis persisted (MCV 81 fL, Hb 12.6 g/dL). Ferritin and folate were normal, and there was no history of blood transfusions. Family history was negative for thalassemia (Table 1).

Taken together, the hematological and clinical findings are consistent with a non-deletional α-thalassemia phenotype of mild severity in both patients, characterized by microcytosis and hypochromia without transfusion requirement.

Discussion

Mutations affecting the 3′UTR of the HBA1 gene play a crucial role in the post-transcriptional regulation of alpha-globin mRNA, impacting processes such as mRNA stability, translation efficiency, and intracellular localization. Although these mutations do not alter the coding sequence of alpha-globin, their effects on mRNA dynamics can give rise to a clinical alpha-thalassemia phenotype. Notably, 3′UTR mutations can disrupt interactions between mRNA and a variety of regulatory elements, including RNA-binding proteins, microRNAs (miRNAs), and other components of the RNA processing machinery. In this context, we describe two novel mutations in the 3′UTR of the HBA1 gene, HBA1:c.*119_120insT and HBA1:c.*150C > A, which are predicted to significantly impact the stability, transport, and translation of alpha-globin mRNA. Both mutations may alter interactions between mRNA and regulatory proteins or miRNAs, ultimately affecting alpha-globin production and contributing to the clinical alpha-thalassemia phenotype. The thymine insertion at nucleotide position TTS + 5 (HBA1:c.*119_120insT) may alter the mRNA’s secondary structure, disrupting key binding sites for proteins that regulate mRNA stability. The HBA1 3′UTR is rich in regulatory sequences, including AU-rich elements (AREs), which serve as binding sites for proteins like HuR that enhance mRNA stability [9, 10]. The insertion may locally alter mRNA folding, reducing HuR accessibility and compromising stability. HuR plays a critical role in protecting alpha-globin mRNA from degradation, increasing its half-life, and promoting efficient translation [3]. Disruption of this interaction could lead to increased degradation and reduced alpha-globin levels [11].

Additionally, the HBA1 3′UTR contains binding motifs for heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins (hnRNPs), such as AUF1, which are vital for mRNA processing, nuclear export, and translation [6]. hnRNPs regulate mRNA fate throughout its life cycle. The T insertion may interfere with hnRNP binding, impairing cytoplasmic export or reducing translation efficiency [1, 2, 4].

The 3′UTR also harbors critical signals for mRNA processing, including the polyadenylation signal. This sequence is essential for proper poly(A) tail addition, mRNA export, and translation [6]. The HBA1:c.*119_120insT mutation lies near this signal, potentially disrupting its interaction with processing factors. Impaired polyadenylation can reduce mRNA stability and translation, as described in other mutations affecting the poly(A) signal [11, 12].

Therefore, the thymine insertion in HBA1 3′UTR (HBA1:c.*119_120insT) likely disrupts mRNA stability, transport, or translation, reducing alpha-globin production and leading to the microcytic, hypochromic anemia seen in the patient.

The second mutation, HBA1:c.*150C > A, may also alter the 3′UTR structure and affect the binding of regulatory proteins such as HuR. If this C-to-A substitution disrupts HuR binding, the alpha-globin mRNA may become unstable and prone to premature degradation. This would decrease the pool of translatable mRNA, reducing alpha-globin production and contributing to the thalassemic phenotype [11].

One major function of the 3′UTR is to serve as a binding site for miRNAs, which repress gene expression by inhibiting translation or promoting degradation [13]. miR-16-5p and miR-92a have been shown to bind specific regions of the HBA1 3′UTR and regulate alpha-globin mRNA levels [5]. The HBA1:c.*150C > A mutation could disrupt these binding sites, either impeding or enhancing miRNA binding. If the substitution interferes with a site typically targeted by miR-16-5p, which promotes mRNA degradation, it might result in increased mRNA stability and translation [14]. However, enhanced translation does not necessarily yield functional hemoglobin, especially if RNA-protein interactions or processing pathways are disrupted, potentially leading to unstable mRNA despite higher translation rates.

Thus, HBA1:c.*150C > A may compromise alpha-globin mRNA stability and translation through disrupted interactions with miRNAs, RNA-binding proteins, and processing machinery, resulting in insufficient alpha-globin production. Further research is needed to elucidate the specific contributions of each mechanism to clinical variability in alpha-thalassemia.

These two novel mutations expand the list of known 3′UTR alterations in HBA1, where only two other variants have been reported to date. HBA1:c.*46C > A, located in the minimal complex (αRNAmin) binding site, may reduce mRNA stability by impairing αCP protein binding and promoting ErEN-mediated decay [7]. HBA1:c.*96G > A affects the polyadenylation signal, leading to impaired poly(A) tail addition, aberrant transcript formation, or decreased translation efficiency, all of which reduce alpha-globin production [8]. Clinically, these effects manifest as heterozygous non-deletional alpha-thalassemia with varying severity.

The relatively low number of 3′UTR variants reported in HBA1 (only two) compared to HBA2 (eleven) may be due to higher transcriptional activity of HBA2, which is expressed 2–3 times more than HBA1. This is partly because HBA2 is located closer to the primary regulatory element HS-40 and has promoter variants that enhance transcription. Additionally, HBA2 mRNA is generally more stable, contributing to its higher expression levels [15–17].

It is important to note that conventional genetic tests typically focus on coding regions and may miss 3′UTR mutations. Expanding molecular analyses to include the 3′UTR could uncover additional variants contributing to phenotypic variability in alpha-thalassemia. To date, only two variants have been reported in the HBA1 3′UTR: c.*46C > A, affecting the minimal αRNA complex binding site [7], and c.*96G > A, altering the canonical polyadenylation signal [8]. Both variants were associated with non-deletional α-thalassemia of mild to moderate severity, presenting with persistent microcytosis and hypochromia without transfusion requirements. In comparison, the two novel variants described here, HBA1:c.*119_120insT and HBA1:c.*150C > A, are located closer to the transcription termination signal and may interfere more directly with polyadenylation efficiency and mRNA stability. Conclusion

Mutations in the 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR) of the HBA1 gene, such as those described in this study, offer a novel perspective on the understanding of alpha-thalassemia and its clinical heterogeneity. Although these mutations do not directly alter the coding sequence of the alpha-globin gene, they affect mRNA stability and translational efficiency by modulating interactions with regulatory proteins and microRNAs. As knowledge of the role of the 3′UTR in alpha-thalassemia expands, it will be possible to develop new strategies for the diagnosis and treatment of this disease, leading to a better understanding of the underlying molecular mechanisms and promoting a more personalized approach in future medicine.
