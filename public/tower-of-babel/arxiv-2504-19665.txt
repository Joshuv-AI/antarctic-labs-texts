Giacomo Marco La Montagna, Sumeja Bureković, Ananyo Maitra, Cesare Nardini

Abstract
Topological defects in systems with liquid-crystalline order are crucial in determining their large-scale properties. In active systems, they are known to have properties impossible at equilibrium: for example, + 1 / 2 +1/2 defects in nematically-ordered systems self-propel. While some previous theoretical descriptions relied on assuming that the defect shape remains unperturbed by activity, we show that this assumption can lead to inconsistent predictions. We compute the shape of − 1 / 2 -1/2 defects and show that the one of + 1 / 2 +1/2 is intimately related to their self-propulsion speed. Our analytical predictions are corroborated via numerical simulations of a generic active nematic theory.



Topological defects are fundamental characteristics of phases displaying liquid-crystalline order

;
Mermin 1979
. In active systems, they have properties that are significantly distinct from their equilibrium counterparts. For instance, in polar active matter, certain defects with charge

;

;

;

;

;

Of all ordered states, topological defects in two-dimensional active nematics have received the most attention

;

. There are at least two reasons for this. First, nematic order is ubiquitous in active materials of biological origin such as those composed of cytoskeletal filaments

;

;

For example, the quasi-long-range-ordered nematic phase in two dimensions was shown to be re-entrant because, due to the self-propulsion of the

\pm 1/2

;

;

For these reasons, there has been a significant theoretical effort to characterise topological defects in active nematics

;
Doostmohammadi et al. 2018
. The self-propulsion speed of
+1/2

Importantly, none of these theories calculated the shape of defects and, at least some works

;
Angheluta et al. 2021
;
Thijssen and Doostmohammadi 2020
;
Rønning et al. 2022

However, it has been known that topological defects in nematic liquid crystals have different shapes

;

;

In this Letter, we describe for the first time how the defect shape is modified by activity. For simplicity we restrict our analysis to two-dimensional dry systems, although we expect that analogous results could be obtained in higher dimensions and in the presence of fluid flows. For
-1/2
defects, we obtain a closed analytical expression for the defect shape both in the far and near field, while for the
+1/2
we link the shape in the core to its self-propulsion speed. We further show that ignoring shape modifications yields incorrect results even at arbitrarily small activities, calling into question aspects of the theoretical literature developed in the past. Our analytical results are quantitatively confirmed by direct numerical simulations.

In conclusion, we considered a minimal dry model of active nematics, and showed that activity modifies the shape of defects. We have shown that neglecting such shape modifications can lead to incorrect conclusions even at arbitrarily small activity.
For the
-1/2
defect, we computed activity-induced shape modifications analytically both in the core and in the far field; for the
+1/2
defect we unveiled a relation between the defect shape in the near field and the self-propulsion speed.
Our analytics were perfectly matched by numerical simulations.

More generally our results can likely be extended to other active systems, for example to include fluid flow as is often done in describing active nematics
Doostmohammadi et al. 2018
, or to describe topological defects in active systems with other types of liquid-crystalline order. We thus expect them to be the starting point to study further properties of topological defects in active systems, such as defect-defect interactions, for which calculating the defect shape is crucial
Sieberer and Altman 2018
;
Wachtel et al. 2016
;
Sieberer et al. 2016
. In this respect, the case of systems with hexatic order is of particular interest: it was shown experimentally

;
Van Der Linden et al. 2019
, in large-scale simulations of self-propelled particles at high density
Caporusso et al. 2023
;
Caporusso et al. 2020
;
Paliwal and Dijkstra 2020
, as well as in biological tissues
Pasupalak et al. 2020
;
Armengol-Collado et al. 2022
;
Tang et al. 2024
, that defects in active hexatics self-organise in defect lines or clusters. Is this due to the fact that, although defects with the same topological charge repel each other in passive systems, activity induces attraction among them? Another important open question concerns interactions between defects in polar liquid crystals
Angheluta et al. 2025
;
Dedenon et al. 2023
. Recent numerical works suggested that an active polar phase at constant density is destroyed by the unbinding of defects

;
Dopierala et al. 2025
;
Popli et al. 2025
. A theoretical understanding of this is lacking and requires the calculation of defect interactions as well as the modification of the
-1

Appendix D
Calculation of defect velocity in active systems using a single Frank constant defect shape

In this section, we calculate the velocity
{\bf v}_{d}
of a
+1/2
defect in active systems by assuming that it retains the shape it would have had in a single Frank constant passive nematic. This approximation is often made in active materials (see, for instance,

;
Liu and Mazenko 1992
. Here we adapt it to our dry active nematics. In the complex notation of Eq. (
29

v_{d}^{c}=v_{d_{x}}+iv_{d_{y}}
, we have from
Mazenko 1997
;
Liu and Mazenko 1992
;
Angheluta et al. 2021
that

v_{d}^{c}=\left[\frac{\partial^{*}\Psi\partial_{t}\Psi^{*}-\partial^{*}\Psi^{*}\partial_{t}\Psi}{\partial\Psi\partial^{*}\Psi^{*}-\partial\Psi^{*}\partial^{*}\Psi}\right]_{z=0}\,.

(35)

The defect in a single Frank constant system solves the equations

S\partial\partial^{*}\theta+\partial^{*}S\partial\theta+\partial S\partial^{*}\theta=0\,

(36)

and

\left(1-\frac{1}{2}S^{2}\right)S-16\partial\theta\partial^{*}\theta+4\partial\partial^{*}S=0\,,

(37)

which are obtained from (
24
) with
K_{1}=K_{2}=L_{1}=L_{2}=0
, using the definition of
\Psi
.
The angle field has the solution

\theta=\frac{i}{2}s\log\left[\frac{z^{*}}{z}\right]\,,

(38)

where
s
is the topological charge of the defect.
This, when plugged into (
37
), yields

\left(1-\frac{1}{2}S^{2}\right)S-\frac{S}{zz^{*}}+4\partial\partial^{*}S=0\,,

(39)

implying that for
z,z^{*}\to 0
,
S\sim a_{0}\sqrt{zz^{*}}
where
a_{0}
is a numerical constant.
This implies

\lim_{z,z^{*}\to 0}\partial\Psi=\frac{a_{0}(1+2s)}{4}\lim_{z,z^{*}\to 0}\left(\frac{z}{z^{*}}\right)^{s-\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{a_{0}}{2}\,\,\text{for}\,s=+\frac{1}{2}\,,

(40)

and is
0
when
s=-1/2
. Similarly,

\lim_{z,z^{*}\to 0}\partial^{*}\Psi=\frac{a_{0}(1-2s)}{4}\lim_{z,z^{*}\to 0}\left(\frac{z}{z^{*}}\right)^{s+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{a_{0}}{2}\,\,\text{for}\,s=-\frac{1}{2}\,,

(41)

and
0
when
s=+1/2
. With these results, the defect velocity can be obtained as

v_{d}^{c}=-\frac{2}{a_{0}}\partial_{t}\Psi(z,z^{*}=0)\,,

(42)

for a
+1/2
defect and

v_{d}^{c}=\frac{2}{a_{0}}\partial_{t}\Psi^{*}(z,z^{*}=0)\,,

(43)

for a
-1/2
defect.
We note that
\partial\partial^{*}\Psi=0
for both
\pm 1/2
defects. We now use Eq. (
24
) for the dynamics of
\Psi
.
Eliminating all terms that are obviously
0
at the site of defects (where
\Psi=0

\partial_{t}\Psi|_{z=0}=[2L_{2}(\partial\Psi)^{2}]_{z=0}\,.

(44)

Therefore, from (
40

41
) we find that the velocity of a
+1/2

(45)

Since

for a
-1/2
defect,

in this case. This implies that

+1/2

It is now easy to see using (
24

L_{2}
contributes to this velocity: the terms with the coefficients

\Psi=\Psi^{*}=0

or have the term
\partial^{*}\Psi^{*}\partial^{*}\Psi

-1/2

+1/2

\partial^{*}\Psi=0

J.1
Drifting of the
+1/2
defect in passive systems

In the main text we have discussed that the
+1/2
defect self-propels in all active systems. In our simulations, the
+1/2
defect drifts even in the passive system with two Frank constants, although with a very small speed. Furthermore Fig.
5
shows that the resulting drift velocity decreases when increasing the system size. This is a fundamental difference with respect to the case of active systems, where we have shown (see the main text) that the self-propulsion speed is well defined when increasing system-size.
The reason for which the
+1/2
defect drifts in our passive simulations is a boundary effect analogous to the Peach-Koehler force
Long and Selinger 2024
, due to imposing at the boundary the nematic order corresponding to the single Frank constant defect, which is not the far field solution of the two Frank constant defect. The natural expectation is that the defect speed decreases as
1/R
for large system-sizes
Eshelby 2006
;
Cermelli and Fried 2002
; however, likely due to computational limitations in system-size, we could not observe this scaling cleanly and we instead observed the defect speed decreasing approximately as
1/R^{0.6}
(but with indication of fast decrease at the larger
R
simulated). The
-1/2
defect does not drift because the angular deformation at the boundary due to the imposition of the nematic order has three-fold symmetry.
