Remimazolam in pediatric sedation and anesthesia: current perspectives and clinical considerations
Authors: Satoshi Kimura, Moritoki Egi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Remimazolam is a novel, short-acting benzodiazepine that acts as a high-affinity selective ligand for the GABA-A receptor. Since its approval, growing evidence has demonstrated its safety and efficacy for sedation and anesthesia in adult patients. Recently, increasing interest has emerged regarding the utility of remimazolam in pediatric patients due to its favorable characteristics, including its limited suppressive effect on respiration and circulation in comparison to other sedatives. However, its clinical use in pediatric patients requires special consideration despite the overall safety profile of remimazolam. Because of differences in body composition and dynamic physiological changes during growth and development, the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of remimazolam in children may differ from those in adults. Therefore, a clear understanding of its pharmacological properties, potential indications, limitations, and reverse options is essential for clinical use. Previous studies have suggested that higher doses relative to body size may be required in pediatric patients than in adult patients for adequate sedation and anesthesia. While body weight-based dosing regimens are straightforward, age-related differences in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics may increase the risk of under- or overdosing. The use of concentration prediction models may assist in dose and infusion rate adjustments. Advances in electroencephalogram monitoring during sedation and anesthesia may also provide valuable guidance, although age-dependent EEG differences and the distinct effects of remimazolam in comparison to other sedatives require further investigation. Despite its short-acting nature and low risk of accumulation, the combination of relatively high dose requirements and potentially reduced clearance in children raises a theoretical concern regarding rebound effects in this population. Therefore, careful dose titration, appropriate monitoring, and judicious reversal are necessary to ensure safe administration in pediatric practice.

Background

Remimazolam is a short-acting benzodiazepine that functions as a high-affinity selective GABA-A receptor ligand [1]. It primarily targets subtypes A1, A2, A3, and A4, and exerts anxiolytic, sedative, anterograde amnesic, anticonvulsant, and muscle relaxant effects [2, 3]. In contrast to midazolam, a classical benzodiazepine widely used for pediatric sedation and anesthesia [4, 5], remimazolam is distinguished by its rapid onset and unique metabolism via tissue esterases, which allows for rapid elimination [6].

Remimazolam was first approved for procedural sedation in China in 2019 and for general anesthesia in Japan in 2020 [7]. The FDA approved its use for sedation in 2020, and accumulating evidence has demonstrated the safety and efficacy of remimazolam for sedation and anesthesia in adults. Furthermore, based on its potential benefits in pediatric patients, the FDA approved a large multicenter clinical trial in 2021 [8]. However, no country has granted formal pediatric approval to date, and pediatric use remains off-label, supported only by case reports and small cohort studies.

Given the growing interest in the use of remimazolam in children, this narrative review outlines the pharmacologic characteristics, potential indications, clinical caveats, administration considerations, and reverse strategies in pediatric patients based on a literature search incorporating systematic elements of available studies.

Main text

Literature search and selection

We searched PubMed from inception to July 2025 using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and synonyms of “remimazolam” and “children.” Additionally, we reviewed the references of the retrieved articles to identify potentially eligible trials. The electronic search strategies are provided in Appendix 1. We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies in which remimazolam was administered to pediatric patients (neonates, infants, children, or adolescents). Trials not published in English were excluded. All records were evaluated by one author (SK) through title, abstract, and full-text screening.

This literature search identified seven RCTs, eight non-randomized prospective studies, one prospective study that included both a prospective and an RCT component, and five retrospective studies. Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics

Pharmacokinetics

Remimazolam is an ester with a small volume of distribution (34.8–35.4 L) [9–11]. Hydrolysis is the major metabolic pathway, in which more than 99% of remimazolam is hydrolyzed by non-specific tissue esterases, particularly carboxylesterase 1, in the liver, which explains the delayed recovery observed in subjects with severe liver impairment [12]. This process results in a pharmacologically inactive carboxylic acid metabolite (CNS 7054), which is subsequently excreted in urine [6]. In adults, remimazolam is characterized by high clearance (1.15–1.49 L/min), a short terminal elimination half-life (45–70 min), and context-sensitive half-time (6.8 min) (CSHT) [9–11, 13].

Body composition changes dramatically during growth, and developmental differences in pediatric physiology can affect drug disposition. Within the pediatric population, younger children have a higher proportion of total body water and extracellular fluid, resulting in a larger volume of distribution and, consequently, lower drug concentrations for hydrophilic drugs [14]. Plasma protein levels, including those of albumin and alpha1-acid glycoprotein, are lower in neonates than in adults [14]. This reduced binding capacity may contribute to a higher free unbound fraction of highly protein-bound drugs [14]. Additionally, children may exhibit age-dependent variations in drug elimination, including metabolic processes, depending on the enzymatic pathways involved [14].

Evidence of the pharmacokinetics (PK) of remimazolam in children is limited. Gao et al. evaluated pharmacokinetics in children of 2–6 years of age under general anesthesia combined with sevoflurane and reported a small central volume of distribution (0.11 L/kg), high elimination clearance (15.9 ml/kg/min), and a short terminal half-time (67 min) [15]. CSHT after a 4-h infusion was 17 min [15]. The authors concluded that when assuming a linear effect of body weight, the pharmacokinetic properties in children were similar to those reported in adults [15]. It should be noted that the reported elimination clearance was 0.73 L/70 kg/min when standardized using allometric weight scaling, which is lower than that reported for adult cohorts. Indeed, there are counterarguments to the assumption of pharmacokinetic similarities between children and adults. Colin et al. estimated an elimination clearance of 0.70 L/70 kg/min in pediatric patients ≥ 6 years of age under sedation [16]. These clearance values in children are also lower than those reported in adults, at 1.18 L/70 kg/min [17]. Furthermore, when comparing the Gao model for children [15] with the Masui model for adults [18], context-sensitive decrement times were longer for children than for adults [19]. These findings suggest that the pharmacokinetic properties of remimazolam may differ between the pediatric and adult populations. While one study demonstrated that elimination clearance is 4–13% higher in adult females than in adult males [18], no study has investigated sex-related differences in remimazolam pharmacokinetics in children. It should be noted that there are no pharmacokinetic data for remimazolam in children < 2 years of age.

Pharmacodynamics

Few studies have investigated the pharmacodynamics (PD) of remimazolam for sedation and anesthesia in children. Jin et al. conducted a prospective study in children 1–6 years of age with congenital heart disease before entering the operating room using Dixon’s up-and-down method [20]. The effective dose 50 (ED50) of remimazolam for successful sedation is 0.209 mg/kg in infants (1 month to 1 year), 0.259 mg/kg in toddlers (1 to 3 years), and 0.266 mg/kg in preschool (3 to 6 years) children [20]. The effective doses 95 (ED95) were 0.356, 0.404, and 0.408 mg/kg, respectively [20]. Cai et al. investigated the ED50 and ED95 of remimazolam as the sole agent for loss of consciousness in three age groups [21]. In their prospective study, the ED50 and ED95 were 0.42 mg/kg and 0.57 mg/kg in the toddler group (1–3 years), 0.41 mg/kg and 0.57 in the preschool group (3–6 years), and 0.30 mg/kg and 0.43 mg/kg in the school-age group (6–12 years) [21]. Li et al. evaluated the ED50 and ED95 of remimazolam required to suppress laryngeal mask airway (LMA) insertion reactions in children aged 3–12 years [22]. With the coadministration of sufentanil (0.3 mcg/kg), the ED50 and ED95 for the condition of LMA insertion were 0.48 mg/kg and 0.55 mg/kg, respectively, in preschool children (3–6 years), and 0.43 mg/kg and 0.50 mg/kg in school-age children (6–12 years) [22].

Our literature search did not identify any study that directly compared the effective doses of remimazolam between pediatric and adult patients. Furthermore, although the reported effective doses of remimazolam vary widely (ED50: 0.13–0.48 mg/kg; ED95: 0.21–0.57 mg/kg), differences in study outcomes, measurement methods, clinical targets, and co-administered anesthetic or analgesic agents make it difficult to interpret differences in effective doses across studies. Nonetheless, the significant contribution of developmental differences in pharmacokinetics across age groups cannot be overlooked.

Previous adult studies have reported lower effective doses for general anesthesia induction, with ED50 values ranging from 0.006 to 0.175 mg/kg and ED95 values ranging from 0.009 to 0.369 mg/kg [23–27]. These findings suggest that the dose of remimazolam required to achieve loss of consciousness may be higher in pediatric patients than in adults. Furthermore, Li et al. demonstrated that the required dosage in preschool children was significantly higher than that in school-aged children [22]. The relationship between effective doses and age groups (neonates, infants, children, and adolescents) warrants further investigation.

Advantages and indications

Maintaining spontaneous breathing

Owing to its unique characteristics, remimazolam offers potential advantages in pediatric patients. One favorable property is its relatively limited suppressive effect on spontaneous breathing during sedation, which is particularly important in pediatric practice. An increasing number of adult studies have shown that remimazolam induces a lower incidence of respiratory depression, with a reported incidence of 0–9.8% [28–30]. Moreover, a randomized controlled trial in children < 18 years of age undergoing endoscopy under sedation reported that remimazolam caused less respiratory depression, defined as apnea or SpO2 < 93% for more than 10 s, than propofol (9.4% vs. 35.9%; p = 0.001) [31]. Many minor but invasive procedures in children, such as endoscopy, bronchoscopy, central venous catheter or vascular access insertion, suturing, and drain removal, often require sedation, especially when the child cannot cooperate or remains still. As many sedative agents suppress respiration and may cause fatal complications [32], selecting drugs and doses that preserve spontaneous breathing is essential for safe pediatric sedation. The characteristics of reduced respiratory suppression and preserved spontaneous breathing with remimazolam, particularly given the availability of a reversal agent (see below), may thus be a compelling reason to select remimazolam for pediatric sedation.

Minimum effect on hemodynamics

Another potential benefit of remimazolam is its limited effect on myocardial contractility and its reduced association with hypotension in adults [33–35], as well as in children [31], possibly because of the absence of gamma-subunit expression in cardiac GABA-A receptors [33]. Two reports have described the successful use of remimazolam in pediatric patients with congenital heart disease undergoing cardiac surgery [36] or cardiac catheterization [19]. Shimizu et al. reported that the ratio of systolic arterial pressure to the baseline was significantly higher in children with congenital heart disease treated with remimazolam than in those treated with sevoflurane during cardiac catheterization [37]. These findings suggest that remimazolam may be a favorable sedative and anesthetic option for children with unstable hemodynamics, including those with congenital heart disease. However, its effects on systemic and pulmonary vascular resistance have not been sufficiently studied.

Alternative to propofol

Propofol is a short-acting anesthetic agent characterized by rapid onset and short duration of effect, primarily due to rapid redistribution and elimination [38]. These properties are advantageous for sedation and anesthesia in both adult and pediatric patients. Propofol is the second most commonly used sedative for children in the intensive care unit setting, after benzodiazepines [4]. However, propofol has several disadvantages. Propofol infusion syndrome (PRIS) is a rare but potentially fatal complication characterized by severe metabolic acidosis, arrhythmias, renal dysfunction, rhabdomyolysis, and hyperkalemia [39]. First reported in children [40], PRIS is associated with high-dose and prolonged propofol infusions [39]. The mortality rate among children has been reported to be approximately 50%, although overall mortality has been decreasing over time [39, 41]. Remimazolam could be a good alternative for pediatric patients at risk of developing PRIS.

Injection site pain is another relevant concern in pediatric anesthesia. Propofol is known to cause injection pain, with reported incidences of up to 73% in adults [42] and 85% in children [43]. In contrast, adult data indicated a lower incidence of injection pain with remimazolam than with propofol (risk ratio 0.03; 95% CI 0.01–0.08; p < 0.001) [35]. Therefore, minimizing discomfort at induction may be another advantage of remimazolam over propofol in children.

Agitation and delirium

Emergence agitation or emergence delirium (EA/ED) is a well-recognized phenomenon in children and is characterized by confusion, irritability, disorientation, inconsolable crying, and increasing parental distress during emergence from general anesthesia [44, 45]. Although benzodiazepines have been identified as a risk factor for delirium in adults [46], the effects of benzodiazepines with different half-lives on delirium may vary. Indeed, accumulating evidence suggests that short-acting benzodiazepines are associated with a lower incidence of postoperative delirium [47–49]. Furthermore, midazolam has been investigated as an effective preventive agent for EA/ED in children [48, 49], prompting interest in the role of remimazolam in both prevention and treatment. Recently, several randomized controlled trials have demonstrated its effectiveness in reducing the occurrence of EA/ED [50–52].

Other potential advantages and future directions

Additional potential applications of remimazolam have been suggested based on its theoretical advantages and clinical case reports. As stated in the package insert, remimazolam is expected to have an anticonvulsive effect, which is consistent with its classification as a benzodiazepine that selectively acts on the GABA-A receptor. Although little is known about its effects in children, one case report described the successful use of remimazolam in a child with mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, stroke-like episodes (MELAS), and uncontrolled epilepsy, in whom inhaled anesthetics and propofol were better avoided [53]. In addition, case reports from operating theaters have suggested other potential indications for remimazolam, such as malignant hyperthermia susceptibility [54–56]. These theoretical advantages of remimazolam warrant future research to clarify their clinical implications.

Comparisons between remimazolam and other agents that share similar benefits would also be an interesting topic for future investigation. For instance, dexmedetomidine offers comparable advantages-such as maintaining spontaneous breathing and preventing agitation and delirium. However, based on our literature search, no studies have directly compared remimazolam with dexmedetomidine regarding these outcomes in pediatric patients. Further research is needed to evaluate the advantages of remimazolam relative to other sedative agents and to determine its potential superiority or inferiority.

Caveats

Precipitation and dilution

The volume of dilution vehicle required for intravenous administration is often a concern for pediatric patients. Remimazolam is supplied by the manufacturers as a lyophilized powder that must be reconstituted with normal saline (or 5% glucose) prior to administration. Precipitation may occur when it is administered with buffer-containing solutions, such as Ringer’s acetate [57, 58] or Ringer’s lactate, possibly due to pH changes. As remimazolam is sometimes administered via the side port of an infusion of Ringer’s solution, manufacturers restrict the concentration of remimazolam to minimize the risk of precipitation. For example, Japanese manufacturers recommend a maximum concentration of 1 mg/ml when co-administered with buffer-containing intravenous fluids and up to 5 mg/ml when delivered through a separate intravenous line. However, dilution may result in excessive fluid administration in children. This is of particular concern in critically ill pediatric patients, especially small neonates and infants, who must avoid volume overload and positive fluid balance.

Organ dysfunction

Evidence regarding the use of remimazolam in children with organ dysfunction is lacking. Adult data indicate that subjects with severe hepatic impairment had reduced clearance in comparison to healthy volunteers, whereas clearance in subjects with renal dysfunction was comparable [12]. Therefore, pediatric patients with liver impairment may require careful consideration of the indication and dose and rate of remimazolam administration.

Tolerance

Recently, tolerance development with remimazolam has raised concerns, similar to other benzodiazepines, such as midazolam [59, 60]. Vellinga et al. conducted a dose-ranging clinical trial in adults and compared the relationship between remimazolam concentration and sedative effects under different infusion protocols [61]. They observed differences in bispectral index (BIS) and Modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (MOAA/S) scores at identical target concentrations between the step-up and step-down phases. Based on better pharmacodynamic models incorporating tolerance development, they concluded that remimazolam-induced sedation is prone to tolerance, potentially mediated by its metabolite [61]. As the indications for remimazolam expand in pediatric patients, the potential for tolerance and withdrawal in children is an important topic for future research.

Practical considerations for pediatric remimazolam use

To date, no high-quality evidence-based guidelines have been established for remimazolam administration in pediatric patients. Although the available data on bolus dosing and infusion rates remain limited, dosing and titration of remimazolam in this population may be guided by (1) body weight, (2) clinical scales, vital signs, and patient movements, and (3) PK/PD modeling.

Body weight-based dosing regimens

Body weight-based dosing regimens are easy for physicians to follow and are predominantly used in pediatric anesthesia. Protocols from previous studies have suggested remimazolam dosing of 0.2–0.4 mg/kg, or temporarily high infusion rate of 6–12 mg/kg/h (0.2 mg/kg/min) until loss of consciousness, followed by maintenance infusion of 1–2 mg/kg/h, with additional bolus doses of 0.1–0.2 mg/kg as needed (Table 3).

Given the differences in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics across age groups, dosing regimens extrapolated from adult data may lead to overdosing or underdosing in pediatric patients. For instance, one trial targeting patients aged 6–19 years was temporarily stopped due to the frequent requirement of rescue medication in cases with insufficient sedation (UMSS ≤ 2), and the original dosing regimen was revised [16]. Based on their simulation model, the authors concluded that both original and revised dosing regimens were unlikely to achieve the targeted depth of sedation and proposed higher bolus doses (0.2 mg/kg top-up at 3-min intervals), an extended titration scheme for continuous infusion (0.6–4.8 mg/kg/h), and removal of the maximum allowable dose for pediatric patients [16]. Hosokawa et al. retrospectively reviewed remimazolam anesthesia in pediatric patients undergoing catheterization for congenital heart disease and predicted the plasma remimazolam concentration [19]. During anesthesia maintenance, remimazolam was infused at a median of 0.8–1.3 mg/kg/h across various age groups [19], which was similar to the dosing used in a phase IIb/III trial in adults [34]. However, the time from termination of remimazolam infusion to extubation was shorter than that reported in the phase IIb/III adult trial. Considering the possibility of a longer context-sensitive decrement time in pediatric patients, the authors concluded that the adequate weight-based infusion rate of remimazolam for anesthesia may be higher in children than in adults [19].

Clinical scales, vital signs, and patient movements

Bolus dosing and infusion rates are usually adjusted according to clinical judgment. The MOAA/S score is a widely used and validated six-point scale, ranging from 5 (fully awake) to 0 (no response to painful stimulation), which assesses responsiveness and sedation levels and has been applied in pediatric populations [20, 62]. The University of Michigan Sedation Scale (UMSS) classifies the level of consciousness into five categories and has also been used to evaluate sedation during procedures in children [63, 64]. When remimazolam is used for sedation, these clinical scales can serve as tools for dose adjustment.

Traditionally, pediatric anesthesiologists have relied more on vital signs and body movements to estimate the anesthetic depth. Changes in the mean blood pressure and heart rate before and after intubation, or in response to surgical stimuli, are often interpreted as indicators of insufficient anesthesia. Conversely, bradycardia and hypotension may suggest over-sedation, particularly in children in whom cardiac output is highly dependent on heart rate. Indeed, many recent clinical studies have adjusted remimazolam dosing and rates based on vital signs and/or body movements [22, 65, 66].

However, adjusting the dosage based solely on these clinical indicators may not reliably reflect the appropriate depth of anesthesia. Because remimazolam exerts less circulatory suppression than other anesthetic agents, titrating its doses to achieve reductions in heart rate and blood pressure equivalent to those observed with other agents may inadvertently result in overdose. The minimum alveolar concentration is determined by subcortical inhibition rather than cortical suppression, and is now considered an unreliable predictor of the hypnotic potency of volatile anesthetic agents [14, 67]. Similarly, body movement may not be a reliable marker of anesthetic depth with remimazolam, particularly in relation to consciousness and electroencephalogram activity.

Application of PK/PD models

Using simulations to estimate plasma remimazolam concentrations may help clinicians adjust the dose and infusion rate. Hosokawa et al. retrospectively reviewed remimazolam anesthesia in pediatric patients and predicted plasma concentrations using a pediatric pharmacokinetic model [19]. Remimazolam was infused at a median of 0.8–1.3 mg/kg/h across different age groups, with estimated plasma concentrations maintained between 1 and 2 μg/ml during anesthesia [19].

When adjusting remimazolam doses based on the simulated concentration within a target range, inter-individual variation becomes a significant concern. It is well known that the effect and clearance of midazolam show considerable inter-individual variability in pediatric patients [68, 69]. Likewise, remimazolam, a newer benzodiazepine derivative, may exhibit variability in sensitivity and pharmacokinetics. In a retrospective study by Hosokawa et al., the predicted plasma concentration of remimazolam at emergence ranged from 0.4 to 1.2 μg/ml without the administration of flumazenil [19]. The wide range of concentrations observed at emergence, with partial overlap with those during anesthesia, suggests that complete reliance on simulation may be unreliable for maintaining an appropriate depth of anesthesia. Furthermore, pharmacokinetic modeling for pediatric patients remains limited and is not commercially available in many countries.

Remimazolam and electroencephalogram

Depth of anesthesia monitoring using an electroencephalogram (EEG) may provide an additional tool to guide infusion rates and ensure an appropriate anesthetic depth, reflecting the summation of excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic activities [67]. In general, anesthetics acting on GABA receptors produce EEG patterns resembling physiological sleep, with changes corresponding to the potency of GABA stimulation [67]. At sedation levels, EEG demonstrates rapid oscillations, primarily in the beta range [67]. At anesthetic levels, alpha and beta waves diminish, while theta and delta waves predominate [67]. Under excessively deep anesthesia, EEG activity was almost completely suppressed, indicating profound cortical inhibition [67].

Sedation with conventional benzodiazepines typically produces the initial proportion of these EEG changes, most notably, an increase in beta activity [70, 71]. Alternations in amplitudes within the 11.5–30 Hz frequency band are considered a relevant measure of the pharmacological intensity of benzodiazepines, reflecting their affinity and intrinsic efficacy at the central GABA–benzodiazepine receptor complex [72]. High-frequency beta power may decline at higher sedation levels [73].

EEG changes associated with the administration of remimazolam are likewise characterized by an initial increase in beta activity, followed by an increase in the alpha and delta bands [70, 74]. However, few studies have examined EEG changes in pediatric patients. Given that the action of anesthetic effects on EEG beyond the age of one year is comparable to that observed in older patients [67], it is reasonable to expect similar EEG changes in non-small pediatric populations, namely, an initial increase in the beta frequency band and possible increases in the alpha and delta bands. One clinical report described the adjustment of remimazolam infusion rates to maintain beta activity on EEG [37].

Reliance on EEG in pediatric anesthesia has several limitations. First, EEG activity varies with age and reflects cerebral maturation. In newborns, EEG is dominated by slow oscillations, while the dominant frequency progressively increases with age as the amplitude of oscillations diminishes [67]. Because this maturation occurs rapidly in the first year of life and progresses slowly thereafter [67], the effects of anesthetic agents on EEG in neonates and infants may differ from those in older children and adults. For example, a broader power spectrum may be observed during anesthesia in small infants than in older children [75]. While EEG power at 2–20 Hz decreases and spectral edge frequency (SEF) increases during emergence from inhaled anesthesia in older children, these changes are not significant in infants of 0–6 months of age [75]. Moreover, neonatal and infant EEG often demonstrate discontinuity or isoelectric episodes during anesthesia [76, 77]. Although resembling the “discontinuous” pattern described in neonatal neurology, this may differ from the abrupt isoelectric activity associated with anesthetic overdose [75]. Thus, adjusting remimazolam dosing in neonates and small infants may not be comparable with its use in older children or adults.

Second, uncertainty remains regarding the processed EEG indices in children. BIS (Covidien, Boulder, CO, USA) is one of the most studied EEG-based monitoring systems in children [64, 78, 79]. In awake children, BIS values are typically 90–100 and decrease below 60 during adequate anesthesia, inversely correlating with end-tidal concentrations of volatile anesthetics, similar to those in adults [78, 80]. The Patient State Index (PSI) is a newer developed EEG index (SedLine, Masimo, Inc., Irvine, CA), which ranges from 0 to 100, although its threshold range for anesthetic depth differs. Several studies have evaluated PSI in children [81–85]. However, both the BIS and PSI algorithms are based on adult EEG, potentially introducing bias when applied to children. Pediatric EEG undergoes developmental changes: the dominant rhythm is 6–7 Hz at 1–3 years of age, increasing to 9–11 Hz by adolescence; beta activity wave frequently occurs at 18–25 Hz at 1–3 years of age, decreasing to 12–13 Hz in adolescence [86, 87]. As beta activity is central to the BIS and PSI algorithms [67, 88], age-related EEG changes may lead to inconsistent or erroneous index values [89, 90]. The validity of these indices is particularly uncertain in neonates and infants [84, 91, 92]. Concerns are especially relevant for remimazolam, given its characteristic beta-band effects, which may artificially elevate BIS and PSI values [70, 93]. A retrospective study in pediatric patients with congenital heart disease found high PSI values during general anesthesia with remimazolam, despite no other signs of inadequate anesthesia [19]. These findings suggest that reliance on processed EEG values alone may be inappropriate, particularly in children younger than six months of age.

Third, EEG primarily reflects cortical activity, which is responsible for conscious processes, whereas subcortical structures are associated with movement in response to noxious stimulation [94]. Subcortical reactivity may be preserved in some children under inhaled anesthesia, despite substantial cortical inhibition (data not shown) [67]. Therefore, adjusting remimazolam dosing solely on the basis of EEG may not ensure immobility, which is an essential component of anesthesia. Furthermore, subcortical responses may trigger sympathetic or parasympathetic activation, causing hemodynamic instability that may be life threatening in critically ill children, such as those with single-ventricle physiology. Conversely, it remains unclear whether complete suppression of subcortical activity is necessary, especially in small infants who may be more vulnerable to the neurotoxic effects of anesthetic agents [95].

Remimazolam reversal

Flumazenil is an antagonist used to reverse the effects of benzodiazepines, such as midazolam and remimazolam. The availability of reverse agents for anesthetic drugs is advantageous because they can partially or completely eliminate both sedative and adverse effects in cases of prolonged sedation after a procedure, respiratory depression during a procedure, or difficult airway situations where rapid regaining of consciousness and spontaneous breathing is required. The suggested pediatric dosing instructions for benzodiazepine reversal are as follows: an initial dose of 0.01 mg/kg (maximum dose: 0.2 mg) administered over 15 s; this may be repeated at 0.01 mg/kg (maximum dose: 0.2 mg) after 45 s, and then every minute up to a maximum cumulative dose of 0.05 mg/kg or 1 mg, whichever is lower [96]. According to the retrospective studies included in our literature search, the rates of flumazenil administration for remimazolam reversal in pediatric patients ranged from 13 to 15% [19, 37, 97, 98]. Some prospective studies have defined various protocols for flumazenil use, ranging from withholding reversal, unless no arousal occurs for 1 h [66], to routine injection [99]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no study has specifically evaluated the efficacy, safety, or optimal timing and dose of flumazenil for remimazolam reversal in children.

Because of its competitive action, both flumazenil and benzodiazepines at the receptor level influence the antagonistic effect of flumazenil [100]. Thus, although remimazolam is generally recognized as short-acting and less prone to accumulation, there remains a risk of the re-emergence of its effects after flumazenil administration, as demonstrated using pharmacokinetic models [100]. In this simulation study, the remimazolam equivalent effect-site concentration and benzodiazepine receptor occupancy rate began to increase approximately 10 min after the administration of a flumazenil bolus in a virtual adult patient who had received continuous remimazolam infusion for 6 h. The risks of re-sedation and respiratory depression following flumazenil administration include reduced remimazolam clearance, remimazolam overdose during anesthesia maintenance, and decreased hepatic blood flow [100]. Given the possibility of lower remimazolam clearance and relatively higher dosing requirements in pediatric patients, the theoretical risk of rebound effects may be greater in this population.

Several strategies have been developed to minimize the potentially fatal rebound effects of remimazolam after the administration of flumazenil. First, clinicians should avoid remimazolam overdose, ensuring its administration only the necessary with a sufficient dose, particularly in children. As previously noted, EEG monitoring and concentration simulation may help maintain an appropriate anesthetic depth, especially in older children. Second, it is reasonable to reserve flumazenil in patients with a clear need for reversal therapy. As remimazolam is a short-acting and minimally accumulative agent, many patients are likely to emerge within 20 min after discontinuation [19]. Therefore, prolonged observation without flumazenil administration may prevent unnecessary flumazenil reversal. Third, clinicians should avoid administering flumazenil when patients are at deep anesthetic levels. When reversal is deemed necessary, flumazenil should only be administered after the reappearance of spontaneous breathing and airway reflexes. This approach reduces the risk of life-threatening complications, as any rebound effect would be unlikely to worsen the patient’s condition in comparison to the pre-reversal state. Finally, children requiring a large bolus of flumazenil should be monitored closely for 1–2 h in the post-anesthesia care unit, especially those with a high risk of remimazolam overdose.

Conclusions

Remimazolam offers a unique combination of rapid onset, predictable recovery, and availability of a specific reversal agent. These attributes suggest its potential utility in pediatric sedation and anesthesia, particularly in cases where traditional agents pose risks or are contraindicated. In addition, its relatively limited respiratory and circulatory suppression make it a promising option for critically ill pediatric patients.

However, despite their clinical appeal, important uncertainties remain. The optimal dosing may differ from that in adults and may vary across pediatric subpopulations. Pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic modeling remains under development. Electroencephalogram-based monitoring may be useful in older children but requires further validation. Moreover, the short- and long-term effects of prolonged remimazolam use remain unclear. These unresolved issues highlight the need for further targeted research to establish the role and safety profile of remimazolam in pediatric settings.
