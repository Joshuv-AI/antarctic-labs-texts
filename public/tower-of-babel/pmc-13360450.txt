Neisseria macacae pneumonia in a patient with spinal cord injury: a case report
Authors: Junxiang Xia, Qiang Zhang, Yifang Zhu, Lin Deng, Jing Yang, Qin Wang, Jing Zhang, Yizheng Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough Neisseria macacae has been recognized as a commensal of macaque oral flora since 1983, its involvement in human disease remains remarkably uncommon. Prior literature documented fewer than a handful of infections, ranging from reddish rash, bacteremia to invasive conditions such as endocarditis and peritonitis. Strikingly, the respiratory system has not been reported as a site of infection. We first reported a case of pneumonia due to N. macacae.Case presentationA 60-year-old male developed pneumonia during treatment for a cervical spine fracture complicated by spinal cord injury. N. macacae was isolated from sputum culture and identified using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry and 16S rRNA gene sequence analysis. The strain exhibited non-susceptible to third-generation cephalosporins but susceptible to carbapenems. Meropenem was administered, leading to a favorable response and significant improvement after one week, resulting in recovery and discharge 14 days later. No recurrence was observed during a follow-up visit one month post-discharge.ConclusionsThis case represents the first documented report of N. macacae–associated pneumonia, expanding the known clinical spectrum of this macaque commensal into the human respiratory tract under conditions of impaired clearance. This finding highlights the necessity of enhanced clinical–laboratory collaboration. Furthermore, the strain’s atypical antimicrobial resistance profile underscores the critical importance of comprehensive susceptibility testing to guide targeted therapy and inform empirical treatment strategies in similar clinical scenarios.Keywords: Neisseria macacae, Pneumonia, Cervical spine fracture, Spinal cord injury

Background

Neisseria macacae, a Gram-negative diplococcus and a member of the Neisseria species, was first identified in the oropharynx of monkeys in 1983 [1]. Neisseria species are commensal organisms that colonize the human oropharynx. The majority of Neisseria species are opportunistic pathogens, with Neisseria meningitidis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae being significant human pathogens. However, human infections caused by N. macacae are exceedingly rare. To our knowledge, there have been 14 cases of human infections caused by N. macacae, which has been isolated from patients with infective endocarditis [2–4], atrioesophageal fistula [5], ovarian cancer [6] and neutropenia [7]. This paper presents the first case of N. macacae-associated pneumonia in a patient with cervical spine fracture.

Case presentation

In March 2024, a 60-year-old male was admitted to an orthopaedic hospital due to cervical injury (Day 0). He was diagnosed with a spinal cord injury resulting from cervical spine fracture and dislocation, and subsequently underwent successful surgical intervention (Day 1). Initial computed tomography of the lungs revealed no significant pulmonary lesions after the first surgery (Fig. 1A). However, during rehabilitation at another institution, he experienced recurrent pain and discomfort, prompting his readmission to this orthopedic hospital (Day 26). Magnetic resonance imaging confirmed a recurrent fracture and dislocation of the cervical spine. Following readmission, a chest computed tomography scan obtained on the same day revealed pneumonia in the lower lobes (Fig. 1B). At the time of admission, his vital signs included a temperature of 36.8 °C, a heart rate of 66 beats per minute with regular rhythm, a blood pressure of 109/61 mmHg, and a respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute. Laboratory tests showed elevated inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein (8.6 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (29 mm/h). The complete blood count results were as follows: white blood cells 3.70 × 10⁹/L (neutrophils 2.44 × 10⁹/L, 66.70%), red blood cells 4.01 × 10¹²/L, hemoglobin 122 g/L, and platelets 122 × 10⁹/L. The patient had a history of diabetes mellitus and no other identified immune deficiencies, no history of heart disease, no family history, no psychosocial history including relevant genetic information, and he reported no contact with monkeys. Subsequently, he underwent cervical internal fixation surgery the following day (Day 27). Although the surgery was successful, the patient developed a persistent cough with purulent sputum, chills, and a high fever postoperatively (Day 28). His vital signs included a temperature of 39.0 °C, a heart rate of 99 beats per minute with regular rhythm, a blood pressure of 126/78 mmHg, and a respiratory rate of 28 breaths per minute. Laboratory tests showed elevated inflammatory markers, including interleukin-6 (141 pg/mL), procalcitonin (0.844 ng/mL), C-reactive protein (88.9 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (40 mm/h). The complete blood count results were as follows: white blood cells 7.20 × 10⁹/L (neutrophils 6.17 × 10⁹/L, 86.0%), red blood cells 3.05 × 10¹²/L, hemoglobin 90.0 g/L, platelets 158 × 10⁹/L, and a normal proBNP level (47.2 pg/ml). A computed tomography scan confirmed severe pneumonia after the second surgery (Fig. 1C). Accordingly, comprehensive laboratory assessments included sputum smear, sputum cultures, blood cultures, tuberculosis infection T-cell tests, 1,3-β-D-glucan tests, Chlamydia pneumoniae and Mycoplasma pneumoniae tests, and influenza A and B antigen tests. The only positive microbiological results were from the sputum smear and culture. The sputum sample submitted by this patient the following day was also found to be positive for N. macacae. After exclusion of cardiogenic pulmonary edema, the findings favor bacterial hypostatic pneumonia as the probable diagnosis; however, hospital-acquired pneumonia cannot be definitively excluded.

A quality assessment was conducted on the Gram staining of the sputum specimens. The results indicated that the specimens met the acceptable criteria, with more than 25 polymorphonuclear leukocytes and fewer than 10 squamous epithelial cells per low-power field. Simultaneously, gram-stained sputum smears showed numerous Gram-negative diplococci engulfed by and scattered around polymorphonuclear leukocytes (Fig. 1E). Concurrently, after 24 h of incubation at 35 °C in 5% CO₂, abundant bacterial growth was observed. Colonies were small (1–2 mm), smooth, moist, and grayish-white on both blood agar and chocolate agar. These colonies enlarged to 2–3 mm in diameter after 48 h (Fig. 1G, H). No growth occurred on MacConkey or Sabouraud Agar plates, even after extended incubation for 14 days. The bacteria were identified as Gram-negative diplococci by Gram staining (Fig. 1F) and confirmed as N. macacae by 16S rRNA gene sequence analysis (Applied Biosystems, USA) and matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS, Autobio, China) (Table 1). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed on N. macacae, referencing the diameter of the inhibition zone and minimal inhibitory concentration breakpoints for Neisseria meningitidis as per the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100 34th Edition. The results of disk diffusion method (BIO-KONT, China) and Epsilometer test (BIO-KONT, China) indicated that the strain was susceptible to meropenem, minocycline, chloramphenicol, rifampicin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, non-susceptible to ceftriaxone and cefotaxime, but was resistant to penicillin, ampicillin and ciprofloxacin (Table 2). The patient was administered intravenous meropenem at a dosage of 2 g every 8 h. Following 3 days of antibiotic therapy, the patient showed symptomatic improvement, along with a marked decrease in inflammatory markers and regression on computed tomography imaging. He was discharged after completing a 14-day treatment course (Fig. 1D). One month post-discharge, a follow-up visit at the hospital revealed his condition was assessed as excellent with no signs of recurrence. Discussion and conclusions

This represents the first reported case of N. macacae-associated hypostatic pneumonia in a patient with spinal cord injury secondary to cervical spine fracture. The pathogenic role of N. macacae was established through multiple lines of evidence: (1) isolation from multiple adequate sputum specimens; (2) microscopic demonstration of numerous intracellular Gram-negative diplococci within neutrophils; (3) exclusive microbiological positivity in sputum with abundant pure culture growth; and (4) successful clinical resolution following targeted antimicrobial therapy guided by susceptibility testing. The following findings supported the diagnosis of hypostatic pneumonia: a history of prolonged postoperative bed rest after surgery; chest computed tomography showing lesions in the posterior basal segments of both lower lobes; cough with thick, tenacious sputum that was difficult to expectorate; and fever. Notably, the patient had no history of endoscopic procedures, excluding iatrogenic acquisition from contaminated endoscopes as previously reported [8].

However, accurate species identification posed significant challenges. While 16S rRNA gene sequencing yielded ambiguous results (> 99.2% identity with both N. macacae and N. mucosa), subsequent MALDI-TOF MS analysis definitively identified the isolate as N. macacae, corroborating Yamamoto’s observation [6] that MALDI-TOF exhibits superior discriminatory power for these species. This combined diagnostic approach underscores the necessity of integrating multiple molecular techniques for precise identification of rare pathogens. Actually, the true incidence of N. macacae infection is likely underestimated, as evidenced by reports of unexplained fever and endocarditis [4]. Misidentification risks persist, particularly potential cross-reactivity with oropharyngeal N. gonorrhoeae assays [9]. Enhanced availability of advanced microbiological techniques, including MALDI-TOF MS and 16S rRNA sequencing in clinical laboratories, coupled with heightened awareness among microbiologists, is essential to reduce underdiagnosis of this emerging pathogen [4].

Simultaneously, the observed in vitro non-susceptibility to Ceftriaxone and Cefotaxime raises concern regarding the potential for reduced susceptibility third-generation cephalosporins in this isolate. It should be noted, however, that there are currently no CLSI established interpretive criteria for N. macacae. The susceptibility result was assessed using breakpoints for Neisseria meningitides, and thus the clinical significance of this finding should be interpreted with caution. For immunocompromised hosts with pneumonia, such as ours, who had cord injury with impaired mucociliary clearance, diabetes-associated neutrophil dysfunction and prolonged immobilization, this apparent non-susceptibility may compromise the efficacy of standard empirical regimens (e.g., ceftriaxone monotherapy or beta-lactam/macrolide combination). If such reduced susceptibility is confirmed by future studies, continued reliance on ceftriaxone in similar patient populations could theoretically lead to prolonged hospitalization, clinical deterioration, or increased economic burden. This observation underscores the need for species-specific susceptibility data and enhanced surveillance for cephalosporin susceptibility among uncommon Neisseria species in vulnerable populations.

The patient’s specific comorbidities were critical predisposing factors for this infection. The spinal cord injury, diabetes mellitus, and limited mobility collectively created a susceptible environment. Autonomic dysfunction following spinal cord injury impairs mucociliary clearance and cough efficacy; hyperglycaemia compromises neutrophil phagocytosis and complement activation; and prolonged immobilization reduces ventilation while promoting stasis of secretions. Consequently, oropharyngeal secretions containing N. macacae were likely retained and aspirated into the lower airways, disrupting local immune homeostasis and precipitating pneumonia.

Regarding the source of the pathogen, our systematic review of human N. macacae infections (Table 3) revealed that our patient lacked epidemiological exposure to monkeys. This feature inconsistent with cases reported by Wen et al. [2], Beşli et al. [10] and Schouten et al. [11], but aligning with the profiles described by Yamamoto et al. [6], Zhang et al. [4] and Iyama et al. [12]. We speculate that the strain may have originated from the patient’s endogenous oral flora. Although N. macacae constitutes 4.5% of oral Neisseria spp [7, 13]. and oral neisseriae are seldom pathogenic [14], the present case constitutes a notable exception. We did not perform identification of oral colonizing bacteria in this patient, rendering the source of the causative pathogen speculative. This constitutes a limitation of our study.

This study has three main limitations. First, it is a single-center report. Second, due to the lack of specific interpretive criteria for N. macacae, antimicrobial susceptibility was assessed using CLSI breakpoints for Neisseria meningitidis. Therefore, the reported susceptibility profile should be interpreted with caution. Third, the analysis of the source of the pathogenic bacteria lacks direct evidence from the patient.

In conclusion, this is the first report of N. macacae -associated pneumonia identified in a patient with cervical spine fracture. This case highlights the importance of meticulous analysis of patient specimens and active communication between laboratory staff and clinicians to identify uncommon pathogens. Furthermore, it reports the observed in vitro non-susceptibility to ceftriaxone in N. macacae strains, highlighting the potential for reduced susceptibility to empiric therapies and reinforcing the need for antimicrobial susceptibility testing and personalized treatment strategies as part of robust antibiotic stewardship programs.
