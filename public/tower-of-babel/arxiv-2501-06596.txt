Stalin Abraham, A. Bhagwat, Sudhir Ranjan Jain

Abstract
We consider an ensemble of 2 × 2 2\times 2 normal matrices with complex entries representing operators in the quantum mechanics of 2 - level parity-time reversal (PT) symmetric systems. The randomness of the ensemble is endowed by obtaining probability distributions based on symmetry and statistical independence. The probability densities turn out to be power law with exponents that depend on the boundedness of the domain. For small spacings, σ \sigma , the probability density varies as σ ν \sigma^{\nu} , ν ≥ 2 \nu\geq 2 . The degree of level repulsion is a parameter of great interest as it makes a connection to quantum chaos; the lower bound of ν \nu for our ensemble coincides with the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble. We believe that the systematic development presented here paves the way for further generalizations in the field of random matrix theory for PT-symmetric quantum systems.

Here we re-visit the random matrix theory for non-Hermitian matrices. There has been a lot of work on random matrices

2
Random matrix ensemble

We consider an ensemble of normal
2\times 2
matrices with complex entries expressed as

\displaystyle H=\begin{pmatrix}[r]z_{1}&z_{2}\\
-\bar{z}_{2}&\bar{z}_{1}\end{pmatrix}

(1)

with
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
are complex numbers,
z_{1}=\left(x_{1},y_{1}\right)
, and

z_{2}=\left(x_{2},y_{2}\right)
. These matrices are complex normal matrices and hence are
unitarily diagonalisable. Further, they satisfy
H^{\dagger}H=HH^{\dagger}=\det(H)I_{2}

where,
I_{2}
is an identity matrix. Notice that
\det(H)=z_{1}\bar{z}_{1}+z_{2}\bar{z_{2}}\neq 0
,
unless
z_{1}=z_{2}=0
. If
\det(H)=1
, these would have been
SU(2)
matrices. With this
motivation, the matrices considered in this analysis are called
SU(2)
-like matrices.

Suppose that
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}
is a very small number.
We demand invariance of probability distribution under similarity transformations.
Let us write an infinitesimal transformation,

\displaystyle Q=\begin{pmatrix}[r]1&-\epsilon\\
\epsilon&1\end{pmatrix}

(2)

under which,
H
transforms as

\displaystyle Q^{-1}HQ=\begin{pmatrix}[r]z_{1}+\epsilon\left(z_{2}-\bar{z}_{2}\right)&z_{2}+\epsilon\left(\bar{z}_{1}-z_{1}\right)\\
-z_{2}+\epsilon\left(\bar{z}_{1}-z_{1}\right)&\bar{z}_{1}+\epsilon\left(\bar{z}_{2}-z_{2}\right)\end{pmatrix}

(3)

2.1
Construction of Probability Distribution Function

Let
P(z)
denote the probability distribution function of the parameters defining the matrix elements. The invariance of
P(z)
under similarity
transformation implies that

\displaystyle P\left(z_{1}+\epsilon\left(z_{2}-\bar{z}_{2}\right)\right)P\left(z_{2}+\epsilon\left(\bar{z}_{1}-z_{1}\right)\right)=P\left(z_{1}\right)P\left(z_{2}\right)

(4)

We now make two assumptions: 1)
P(z)=f\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)=f_{1}\left(x^{2}\right)f_{2}\left(y^{2}\right)

with
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
z=(x,y)
and
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
sufficiently smooth, and
2)
P\left(z_{1}z_{2}\right)=P\left(z_{1}\right)P\left(z_{2}\right)
.
With these assumptions, we get:

\displaystyle P\left(z_{1}+\epsilon\left(z_{2}-\bar{z}_{2}\right)\right)=P\left(x_{1}+iy_{1}+i2\epsilon y_{2}\right)=f_{1}\left(x_{1}^{2}+y_{1}^{2}+4\epsilon y_{1}y_{2}\right)

(5)

\displaystyle P\left(z_{2}+\epsilon\left(\bar{z}_{1}-z_{1}\right)\right)=P\left(x_{2}+iy_{2}-i2\epsilon y_{1}\right)=f_{2}\left(x_{2}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}-4\epsilon y_{1}y_{2}\right)

(6)

with

\displaystyle f_{1}\left(x_{1}^{2}+y_{1}^{2}+4\epsilon y_{1}y_{2}\right)=f_{1}\left(x_{1}^{2}+y_{1}^{2}\right)+8\epsilon y_{1}y_{2}\left(x_{1}\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}+y_{1}\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial y_{1}}\right)+o\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)

(7)

\displaystyle f_{2}\left(x_{2}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}-4\epsilon y_{1}y_{2}\right)=f_{2}\left(x_{2}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\right)-8\epsilon y_{1}y_{2}\left(x_{2}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial x_{2}}+y_{2}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial y_{2}}\right)+o\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)

(8)

Invariance of probability distribution function under similarity transformation requires that:

\displaystyle f_{1}\left(x_{1}^{2}+y_{1}^{2}+4\epsilon y_{1}y_{2}\right)f_{2}\left(x_{2}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}-4\epsilon y_{1}y_{2}\right)=f_{1}\left(x_{1}^{2}+y_{1}^{2}\right)f_{2}\left(x_{2}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\right)

(9)

giving us

\displaystyle f_{2}\left(x_{2}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\right)\left(x_{1}\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}+y_{1}\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial y_{1}}\right)=f_{1}\left(x_{1}^{2}+y_{1}^{2}\right)\left(x_{2}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial x_{2}}+y_{2}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial y_{2}}\right).

(10)

Let us separate
f_{1,2}
in
x-
and
y-
parts:

\displaystyle f_{1}\left(x_{1}^{2}+y_{1}^{2}\right)=F_{11}\left(x_{1}^{2}\right)F_{12}\left(y_{1}^{2}\right)

(11)

\displaystyle f_{2}\left(x_{2}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\right)=F_{21}\left(x_{2}^{2}\right)F_{22}\left(y_{2}^{2}\right)

(12)

Combining Eqs. (
10
,
12
), we get:

\displaystyle F_{21}F_{22}\left[x_{1}\frac{dF_{11}}{dx_{1}}F_{12}+y_{1}\frac{dF_{12}}{dy_{1}}F_{11}\right]=F_{11}F_{12}\left[x_{2}\frac{dF_{21}}{dx_{2}}F_{22}+y_{2}\frac{dF_{22}}{dy_{2}}F_{21}\right]

(13)

This leads to:

\displaystyle\frac{x_{1}}{F_{11}}\frac{dF_{11}}{dx_{1}}+\frac{y_{1}}{F_{12}}\frac{dF_{12}}{dy_{1}}=\frac{x_{2}}{F_{21}}\frac{dF_{21}}{dx_{2}}+\frac{y_{2}}{F_{22}}\frac{dF_{22}}{dy_{2}}

(14)

Using the separation of variables,

\displaystyle\frac{x_{1}}{F_{11}}\frac{dF_{11}}{dx_{1}}+\frac{y_{1}}{F_{12}}\frac{dF_{12}}{dy_{1}}-\frac{x_{2}}{F_{21}}\frac{dF_{21}}{dx_{2}}=\frac{y_{2}}{F_{22}}\frac{dF_{22}}{dy_{2}}=\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}

(15)

where
\lambda_{0}
is a separation constant.
This leads directly to

\displaystyle F_{22}\left(y_{2}\right)=y_{2}^{\lambda_{0}}

(16)

Further,

\displaystyle\frac{x_{1}}{F_{11}}\frac{dF_{11}}{dx_{1}}+\frac{y_{1}}{F_{12}}\frac{dF_{12}}{dy_{1}}=\lambda_{0}+\frac{x_{2}}{F_{21}}\frac{dF_{21}}{dx_{2}}=\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}

(17)

leading to:

\displaystyle F_{21}\left(x_{2}\right)=x_{2}^{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{0}}.

(18)

Also,

\displaystyle\frac{x_{1}}{F_{11}}\frac{dF_{11}}{dx_{1}}=\lambda_{2}-\frac{y_{1}}{F_{12}}\frac{dF_{12}}{dy_{1}}=\lambda_{3}\in\mathbb{R}.

(19)

Finally,

\displaystyle F_{11}\left(x_{1}\right)=x_{1}^{\lambda_{3}}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad F_{12}\left(y_{1}\right)=y_{1}^{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}}

(20)

In summary, we get the probability distribution function:

\displaystyle P\left(z_{1},z_{2}\right)=P\left(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2}\right)=Ax_{1}^{\lambda_{3}}y_{1}^{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}}x_{2}^{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{0}}y_{2}^{\lambda_{0}}

(21)

2.2
Determination of Normalisation Constant

Let
\left(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Take the weight function

w:\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which is given by

\displaystyle w\left(z_{1},z_{2}\right)=w\left(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\alpha\left(\left|z_{1}\right|^{2}+\left|z_{2}\right|^{2}\right)\right\}

(22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\alpha\left(x_{1}^{2}+y_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\right)\right\}

(23)

with
\alpha>0
. We demand that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}P\left(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2}\right)w\left(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2}\right)d^{4}x=1

(24)

where
d^{4}x:=dx_{1}dx_{2}dy_{1}dy_{2}
.
Explicitly,

\displaystyle A\int_{\mathbb{R}}x_{1}^{\lambda_{3}}e^{-\alpha x_{1}^{2}}dx_{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}y_{1}^{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}}e^{-\alpha y_{1}^{2}}dy_{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}x_{2}^{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{0}}e^{-\alpha x_{2}^{2}}dx_{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}y_{2}^{\lambda_{0}}e^{-\alpha y_{2}^{2}}dy_{2}=1

(25)

It is well known that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{a}e^{-bx^{2}}\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\left[1+\left(-1\right)^{a}\right]b^{-\frac{a+1}{2}}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{a+1}{2}\right)~\mathrm{with}~\Re a>-1~\mathrm{and}~\Re b>0

(26)

where
\Gamma
is the usual gamma function. Now, by our assumption,
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus, the
above integrals are convergent when
a>-1
and
b>0
in all the cases.
Explicitly, we have:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}x_{1}^{\lambda_{3}}\,e^{-\alpha x_{1}^{2}}dx_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[1+\left(-1\right)^{\lambda_{3}}\right]\alpha^{-\left(\lambda_{3}+1\right)/2}\,\,\Gamma\left(\frac{\lambda_{3}+1}{2}\right)

(27)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}x_{2}^{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{0}}\,e^{-\alpha x_{2}^{2}}dx_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[1+\left(-1\right)^{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{0}}\right]\alpha^{-\left(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{0}+1\right)/2}\,\,\Gamma\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{0}+1}{2}\right)

(28)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}y_{1}^{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}}\,e^{-\alpha y_{1}^{2}}dy_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[1+\left(-1\right)^{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}}\right]\alpha^{-\left(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}+1\right)/2}\,\,\Gamma\left(\frac{\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{2}+1}{2}\right)

(29)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}y_{2}^{\lambda_{0}}\,e^{-\alpha y_{2}^{2}}dy_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[1+\left(-1\right)^{\lambda_{0}}\right]\alpha^{\left(\lambda_{0}+1\right)/2}\,\,\Gamma\left(\frac{\lambda_{0}+1}{2}\right)

(30)

In order that the above integrals are convergent, we need to demand that
\lambda_{3}>-1
,

\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{0}>-1
,
\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}>-1
and
\lambda_{0}>-1
. Further, in order
that the integrals are non-zero, we need

\displaystyle\left(-1\right)^{\lambda_{3}}\neq-1\Rightarrow\lambda_{3}\neq 2n+1\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}

(31)

\displaystyle\left(-1\right)^{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{0}}\neq-1\Rightarrow\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{0}\neq 2n+1\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}

(32)

\displaystyle\left(-1\right)^{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}}\neq-1\Rightarrow\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}\neq 2n+1\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}

(33)

\displaystyle\left(-1\right)^{\lambda_{0}}\neq-1\Rightarrow\lambda_{0}\neq 2n+1\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}

(34)

Thus, in conclusion, the above integrals exist if
\lambda_{0}>-1
,
\lambda_{2}>-2
and
\lambda_{3}>-1
with an additional constraint that none of these should be odd integers. Notice further that

(-1)^{\beta}\in\mathbb{C}
unless
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}
. Thus,
\lambda_{3}
and
\lambda_{0}
are
integers. However, these cannot be odd integers, hence
\lambda_{0}
and
\lambda_{3}
are even integers.
Let
\lambda_{0}=2n
and
\lambda_{3}=2l
such that
n,l\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. For the sake of brevity, we
will denote
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. Along the same lines, it follows that
\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{0}

should be an integer, giving us,
\lambda_{2}
is an integer, in fact, an even integer. Further, given that

\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{0}>-1
, if
\lambda_{2}=2m
,
m\in\mathbb{Z}
then
2m-2n>-1

\Rightarrow

m\geq n
. Similarly,
\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}>-1
gives
m\geq l
. Thus, it follows that

m\geq\max\{l,n\}
. In conclusion,
\lambda_{0}=2n
,
\lambda_{2}=2l
and
\lambda_{3}=2m
with

l,m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
m\geq\max\{l,n\}
.

With all these simplifications, and by using properties of Gamma function
[
31
]
, we get:

\displaystyle A_{lnm}=\frac{2^{2m}\alpha^{2m+2}}{\pi^{2}\xi_{lnm}}

(35)

with

\displaystyle\xi_{lnm}=(2l-1)!!\,(2n-1)!!\,(2m-2n-1)!!\,(2m-2l-1)!!

(36)

where the symbols have their usual meanings. This finally yields,

\displaystyle P\left(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2}\right)=A_{lnm}x_{1}^{2l}y_{1}^{2(m-l)}x_{2}^{2(m-n)}y_{2}^{2n}

(37)

such that
l,m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
m\geq\max\{l,n\}
.

2.3
Level Repulsion

The matrix
H
(Eq. (
1
)) has two eigenvalues,

\displaystyle E_{\pm}=x_{1}\pm i\sqrt{y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}\,.

(38)

The level repulsion can be computed using the joint probability distribution:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\sigma)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\delta\left(\left|E_{+}-E_{-}\right|-\sigma\right)P\left(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2}\right)w\left(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2}\right)d^{4}x

(39)

where the function
w
is the weight function defined above and
\delta
is the usual Dirac delta measure
(see, for example,
[
32
]
).
Explicitly,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{2}A_{lnm}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\delta\left(\sqrt{y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}-\sigma/2\right)x_{1}^{2l}y_{1}^{2(m-l)}x_{2}^{2(m-n)}y_{2}^{2n}

\displaystyle\times~e^{-\alpha\left(y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}\right)}d^{4}x

(40)

A close inspection of the above integral reveals that transformation to spherical polar coordinates could be
useful here. Specifically, let
R^{2}=y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}
such that

y_{1}=R\sin\theta\cos\phi
,
y_{2}=R\sin\theta\sin\phi
, and
x_{2}=R\cos\theta
. Using this transformation,
the above integral becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}A_{lnm}\int_{\mathbb{R}}x_{1}^{2l}e^{-\alpha x_{1}^{2}}dx_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}R^{4m-2l+2}e^{-\alpha R^{2}}\delta\left(R-\sigma/2\right)\,dR

(41)

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\cos\phi\right)^{2m-2l}\left(\sin\phi\right)^{2n}\,d\phi\int_{0}^{\pi}\left(\sin\theta\right)^{2m+2n-2l+1}\left(\cos\theta\right)^{2m-2n}d\theta

This integral can be evaluated analytically using the properties of beta integral, and the
integral representation of the Gamma function (see, for example,
[
31
]
), leading to

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\alpha^{2m-l+3/2}}{\sqrt{\pi}\left(2m-l+1/2\right)}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\right)^{2m-l+1}e^{-\alpha\sigma^{2}/4}

(42)

which is the desired result. The degree of level repulsion is given by the exponent on
\sigma
as
\sigma\to 0
, in this case it is
\sigma^{2(2m-l+1)}
. Recalling the admissible values of
m,l
, we see that the minimum value of
m
is
l
; this gives the asymptotic behaviour as
\sigma^{2(l+1)}
. Thus, for the minimum value of
l=0
, the degree of level repulsion coincides with that of the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble
[
2
]
. For any other value, the degree of level repulsion is stronger than 2. This is an algebraic power, in contrast to the case of pseudo-Hermitian matrices where, for one case, it was shown to vary as
\sigma\log(1/\sigma)

[
4
]
.

2.4
Probability Distribution Function and Level Repulsion in a bounded Region of
\mathbb{R}^{4}

We begin with the expression for the probability distribution (up to the normalisation factor) obtained above:

\displaystyle P\left(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2}\right)=Ax_{1}^{\lambda_{3}}y_{1}^{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}}x_{2}^{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{0}}y_{2}^{\lambda_{0}}

(43)

For the sake of convenience, we re-label some variables here. Out of the four variables, we will re-label

y_{1}
to
x_{3}
and
y_{2}
to
x_{4}
. This leads to:

\displaystyle P\left(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\right)=Ax_{1}^{\lambda_{3}}x_{3}^{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}}x_{2}^{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{0}}x_{4}^{\lambda_{0}}

(44)

Let
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
be defined by
x_{i}\geq 0
for
i=1,2,3,4
, such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{4}x_{i}^{2}\leq 1

(45)

We first state a result due to Liouville that essentially generalises the beta integral to dimensions
higher than 2 (see
[
31
]
for details):

Theorem 1
.

If
V
is a region defined by
x_{i}\geq 0
,
i=1,2,...,n
and
\sum x_{i}\leq 1
, then for

\Re\alpha_{i}>0
,

\displaystyle\int_{V}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\alpha_{i}-1}dx_{1}...dx_{n}=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma\left(\alpha_{i}\right)}{\Gamma\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\right)}

(46)

As an immediate corollary to this theorem, we have
[
31
]
:

Corollary 1
.

If
V
is the region enclosed by
x_{i}\geq 0
and
\sum\left(x_{i}/a_{i}\right)^{p_{i}}\leq 1
then
for
\Re\alpha_{i}>0
,

\displaystyle\int_{V}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\alpha_{i}-1}dx_{1}...dx_{n}=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(a_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}/p_{i}\right)\Gamma\left(\alpha_{i}/p_{i}\right)}{\Gamma\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}/p_{i}\right)}

(47)

The normalisation constant in the present case can be obtained readily from this corollary by
setting
p_{i}=2
and
a_{i}=1
. Explicitly,

\displaystyle A\int_{V}\prod_{i=1}^{4}x_{i}^{\alpha_{i}-1}d^{4}x=\frac{1}{16}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma\left(\alpha_{i}/2\right)}{\Gamma\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}/2\right)}

(48)

giving us,

\displaystyle A=\frac{16\Gamma\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}/2\right)}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma\left(\alpha_{i}/2\right)}

(49)

where,
\alpha_{1}=\lambda_{3}+1
,
\alpha_{2}=\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{0}+1
,
\alpha_{3}=\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}+1
and

\alpha_{4}=\lambda_{0}+1
and
d^{4}x:=dx_{1}dx_{2}dx_{3}dx_{4}
.
Given the condition that
\Re\alpha_{i}>0
, in our case, the above integral exists if and
only if
\lambda_{3}+1>0
,
\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}+1>0
,
\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{0}+1>0
, and
\lambda_{0}+1>0
.
Thus, it follows that the integral and hence the normalisation constant exists provided that
\lambda_{3}>-1
,
\lambda_{0}>-1
and

\lambda_{2}>\max\left\{\lambda_{0},\lambda_{3}\right\}
. Further notice that for the domain over which
\lambda_{k}
are defined,
the normalisation constant is always non-zero, as it should be. There is no further restriction on the values that
the separation constants can take, which is in
contrast with the case of full
\mathbb{R}^{4}
, where the separation constants were restricted to positive even integral values.

We now compute the nearest-neighbour level spacing distribution in this case. Specifically, we need to evaluate the following integral:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}\left(\sigma\right)=\frac{1}{2}A\int_{V}\prod_{i=1}^{4}x_{i}^{\alpha_{i}-1}\delta\left(\sqrt{x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}+x_{4}^{2}}-\sigma/2\right)d^{4}x

(50)

It is convenient to use hyper-spherical coordinates here
[
33
]
. Specifically, we set

\displaystyle x_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r\cos\phi_{1}

(51)

\displaystyle x_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r\cos\phi_{2}\sin\phi_{1}

(52)

\displaystyle x_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r\sin\theta\sin\phi_{1}\sin\phi_{2}

(53)

\displaystyle x_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r\cos\theta\sin\phi_{1}\sin\phi_{2}

(54)

such that
r^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{4}x_{i}^{2}
(
0\leq r\leq 1
),
\phi_{1}\in\left[0,\pi\right]
,
\phi_{2}\in\left[0,\pi\right]
, and

\theta\in\left[0,2\pi\right]
. In the present case, since we are restricting ourselves to non-negative values of

x_{i}
, it follows that
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\theta\in\left[0,\pi/2\right]
. Further, the determinant of the Jacobian matrix is

r^{3}\left(\sin\phi_{1}\right)^{2}\sin\phi_{2}
, and
x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}+x_{4}^{2}=r^{2}\left(\sin\phi_{1}\right)^{2}
,
which leads to

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}\left(\sigma\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}A\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\xi}^{\pi/2}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\delta\left(r\sin\phi_{1}-\sigma/2\right)r^{\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}-1}\left(\sin\theta\right)^{\alpha_{3}-1}\left(\cos\theta\right)^{\alpha_{4}-1}\times

(55)

\displaystyle\hskip-5.0pt\left(\cos\phi_{2}\right)^{\alpha_{2}-1}\left(\sin\phi_{2}\right)^{\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{4}-1}\left(\cos\phi_{1}\right)^{\alpha_{1}-1}\left(\sin\phi_{1}\right)^{\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{4}-1}drd\phi_{1}d\phi_{2}d\theta

Notice that the domain of integral over
\phi_{1}
has been cut to
\left[\xi,\pi/2\right]
with
\xi>0
. This is
due to the constraint implied by the Dirac Delta measure, as will be explained below.

We will first carry out integral over
r
. In order to do that, we first write:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}\left(\sigma\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}A\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\xi}^{\pi/2}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\delta\left(r-\sigma/\left(2\sin\phi_{1}\right)\right)r^{\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}-1}\left(\sin\theta\right)^{\alpha_{3}-1}\left(\cos\theta\right)^{\alpha_{4}-1}

(56)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\hskip-5.0pt\left(\cos\phi_{2}\right)^{\alpha_{2}-1}\left(\sin\phi_{2}\right)^{\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{4}-1}\left(\cos\phi_{1}\right)^{\alpha_{1}-1}\left(\sin\phi_{1}\right)^{\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{4}-2}\,drd\phi_{1}d\phi_{2}d\theta

leading to:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}\left(\sigma\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}A\left(\frac{\sigma}{2}\right)^{\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}-1}\int_{\xi}^{\pi/2}\left(\cos\phi_{1}\right)^{\alpha_{1}-1}\left(\sin\phi_{1}\right)^{-\alpha_{1}-1}d\phi_{1}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left(\sin\theta\right)^{\alpha_{3}-1}\left(\cos\theta\right)^{\alpha_{4}-1}d\theta\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left(\cos\phi_{2}\right)^{\alpha_{2}-1}\left(\sin\phi_{2}\right)^{\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{4}-1}d\phi_{2}

Out of these, the second and the third are the standard beta integrals
[
31
]
, and are given by

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left(\sin\theta\right)^{\alpha_{3}-1}\left(\cos\theta\right)^{\alpha_{4}-1}d\theta=\frac{\Gamma\left(\alpha_{3}/2\right)\Gamma\left(\alpha_{4}/2\right)}{2\Gamma\left(\left(\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{4}\right)/2\right)}

(58)

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left(\cos\phi_{2}\right)^{\alpha_{2}-1}\left(\sin\phi_{2}\right)^{\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{4}-1}d\phi_{2}=\frac{\Gamma\left(\left(\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{4}\right)/2\right)\Gamma\left(\alpha_{2}/2\right)}{2\Gamma\left(\left(\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{4}\right)/2\right)}

(59)

In order to evaluate the first integral, we need to estimate
\xi
first. We know that

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4\left(\sin\phi_{1}\right)^{2}}\leq 1

(60)

The lower limit of the integral,
\xi
, is obtained by demanding that it is the one that gives the smallest
possible value of
\phi_{1}
, leading to
\sin\xi=\sigma/2
, giving us
\xi=\arcsin\left(\sigma/2\right)
. The first
integral can now be evaluated:

\displaystyle\int_{\xi}^{\pi/2}\left(\cos\phi_{1}\right)^{\alpha_{1}-1}\left(\sin\phi_{1}\right)^{-\alpha_{1}-1}d\phi_{1}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}\left(\cot\xi\right)^{2}=\frac{4-\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}

(61)

Upon combining all these factors and inserting the value of normalisation constant, we finally get

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}\left(\sigma\right)=\frac{4\Gamma\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{4}\alpha_{i}/2\right)}{\Gamma\left(\left(\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{4}\right)/2\right)}\,\frac{4-\sigma^{2}}{\alpha_{1}\sigma^{2}}\,\left(\frac{\sigma}{2}\right)^{\sum_{i}^{4}\alpha_{i}-1}

(62)

Finally, note that
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=2\lambda_{2}+4
. Thus,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}\left(\sigma\right)=\frac{4\Gamma\left(\lambda_{2}+3\right)}{\Gamma\left(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}/2+3/2\right)}\,\frac{4-\sigma^{2}}{\left(\lambda_{3}+1\right)}\,\left(\frac{\sigma}{2}\right)^{2\lambda_{2}+1}

(63)

3
Concluding remarks

As emphasized in the main text, one of the significant developments reported here is to obtain the
distribution function of the elements of an ensemble of non-Hermitian
SU(2)
-like matrices on the basis of symmetry and
statistical independence. Hitherto, it was always assumed to be a Gaussian distribution. For at least
the ensemble considered here, this assumption has been lifted. This has led us to the consideration of
certain cases dictated by the domain of the matrix elements. Employing the new-found probability distribution,
we have calculated the nearest-neighbour level spacing distribution. The degree of level repulsion is
tunable as there appear two parameters; this result is potentially significant in modelling physical
situations where interaction strength varies (see, e.g.
[
13
]
) and also in connection with number
theory (see
[
34
]
). However, at the lower bound of the level repulsion parameter of our ensemble is the degree for the Gaussian Unitary ensembles (GUE). It is worth recalling that GUE corresponds to
physical systems which violate time-reversal invariance. In line with earlier results

[
4
,
5
,
15
,
16
]
, we are led to conjecture that an additional breakdown of parity
makes the degree of level repulsion stronger; thus, we conjecture:

\lim_{\sigma\to 0}P(\sigma)\sim\sigma^{\nu},\nu\geq 2.

The connections between random matrix theory and exactly solvable models are profound

[
17
,
35
,
36
,
37
,
20
]
. Upon appropriate generalization, we believe that this work
opens up the possibility of connections with new, exactly solvable models also.
