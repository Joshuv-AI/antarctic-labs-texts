β \beta -diversity and Graph Sheaf Laplacians
Peter Davidson, Michael Grinfeld

Abstract
We suggest a new approach to β \beta -diversity in ecological systems, based on the energy of the graph sheaf Laplacian associated with the sample data. This scalar quantity is easily computable using methods of linear algebra. We show using simple examples that the energy is much more informative than the generally accepted definitions of β \beta -diversity.



Keywords:

\alpha
-,
\beta
-,
\gamma
-diversity; sheaf theory;
graph sheaf Laplacian; energy.

1
Introduction

Whether we humans like it or not, we are the custodians of life on
Earth. This is a heavy responsibility and we are slowly learning by
trial and many a tragic error how to discharge it.

The first task is to be able to describe the sum total of life on
Earth. Unfortunately, that task is a very hard one, the principles of
which are unclear. A recent review of biodiversity
Daly et al. 2018
is
even entitled “Ecological Diversity: Measuring the Unmeasurable”.

Below we simplify and equate diversity with richness, i.e., the
number
of species. More sophisticated approaches, for example using
Hill’s numbers to incorporate unevenness and similarity information

(
Chao and Chiu 2016
;
Leinster and Cobbold 2012
)
can be analysed in the framework
suggested here, but we feel that explaining the principles we are
using is best done in the simplest possible setting.

The point of our departure is that in ecological literature, one
traditionally differentiates among
\alpha
-,
\gamma
-, and
\beta
-
diversities. Of these
\alpha
and
\gamma
are relatively
uncontroversial (once the term “diversity” itself has been defined):

\alpha
-diversity is defined in
Daly et al. 2018
as referring to the
“diversity of a uniform habitat of a fixed size”;
\gamma
-diversity
is “the total diversity of the landscape”. However,

\beta
-diversity, which according to
Daly et al. 2018
, “refers to the
average dissimilarity in composition among sub-communities”, is
trickier and the literature on
\beta
-diversity is huge (e.g. see

Baselga 2010
;
Baselga and Leprieur 2015
;
Chao and Ricotta 2019
).

Simple examples show that the two widely accepted multiplicative

(
Whittaker 1972
)
and additive
(
Lande 1996
)
definitions of

\beta
-diversity are not very informative, as all distributions
of species that agree on the average
\alpha
- and on

\gamma
-diversities lead to the same value of
\beta
-diversity.

Such cases differ in the way “local becomes global”. So any

\beta
-diversity defined as a positive number obtained from directly
manipulating
\gamma
- and
\alpha
-diversities will not have good
discrimination properties as it will disregard adjacency relations of
sampling sites. We need to introduce an operator that takes the
collection of
\alpha
-diversities and creates from them

\gamma
-diversity in a way that at the same time respects such
adjacency relations and thus is better able to differentiates between
cases having the same
\beta
-diversity. In this contribution we
demonstrate that such an operator can be constructed and that its
spectrum contains more information than the
\beta
-diversities
accepted in the ecological literature.

The classical local-to-global tool is sheaf theory. The understanding
that sheaf theory can be applied to data analysis dawned in about
2014–2015 in the work of Curry
(
Curry 2014
)
and Robinson

(
Robinson 2014
;
Robinson 2017
)
and since then a considerable
literature on this topic has appeared, mainly through the work of
R. Ghrist and his students (e.g.
Hansen and Ghrist 2019
;
Hansen 2020a
;
Ghrist and Riess 2020
). For a detailed recent review please see

(
Ayzenberg et al. 2025
)
or the monograph
(
Rosiak 2022
)
. Other
interesting contributions are to credence estimates
(
Biesel 2024
)

and to expert selection in the health services

(
Aktas et al. 2025
)
. Sheaf theory is deep but computationally
accessible, resting on rather simple linear algebra. Hence one can
expect emerging applications of sheaf theory to be of use to
researcher communities without expertise in that area of mathematics.

Accessible sources for what follows are the set of lectures by Essl

(
Essl 2023
)
and the preprint by Hansen
(
Hansen 2020b
)
, as
well as
Aktas et al. 2025
mentioned above,

2
Defining a sheaf Laplacian

In applying sheaf theory to construct suitable operators (Laplacians)
a number of choices has to be made. Briefly, we start with a graph

G=(V,E)
, with
V
the set of vertices and
E
the set of edges,
equip every vertex
v
and edge
e
with vector spaces (stalks)

{\mathcal{F}}_{v}
,
{\mathcal{F}}_{e}
, respectively, containing relevant data, and for each
vertex
v
adjacent to an edge
e
define restriction maps from
{\mathcal{F}}_{v}

to
{\mathcal{F}}_{e}
which we denote by
{\mathcal{F}}_{v\rightarrow e}
. Finally, for any
edge
e
connecting vertices
v
and
u
we define the coboundary map

\delta
by

(\delta)_{e}={\mathcal{F}}_{v\rightarrow e}-{\mathcal{F}}_{u\rightarrow e}.

The object we are after is the (graph) sheaf Laplacian, defined by

L_{\mathcal{F}}=\delta^{T}\delta.

(1)

Other choices of the underlying structure are possible. Hypergraphs
are considered in
Duta et al. 2025
; alternatively, one could choose
metric spaces of probability distributions instead of vector spaces
for stalks, as is done in
Biesel 2024
or lattices of sets

(
Ghrist and Riess 2020
)
, but choosing vector spaces reduces computations to
elementary linear algebra.

As we show below, the sheaf Laplacian collects information both about
the data and the structure of the graph and so has greater
expressivity than
\beta
-diversity.

The spectrum
\sigma_{L_{\mathcal{F}}}
of
L_{\mathcal{F}}
is real, and we call the sum of
the squares of the eigenvalues the
energy

E_{L_{\mathcal{F}}}
of the
graph sheaf Laplacian:

E_{L_{\mathcal{F}}}=\sum_{\lambda_{i}\in\sigma_{L_{\mathcal{F}}}}\lambda_{i}^{2}.

(2)

3
Examples

In this section we test the expressivity of the spectrum of graph
sheaf Laplacians on very simple examples in which the habitat is
composed of three sites.

3.1
Chains

By a chain we mean a graph
G
on
n
vertices in which

V=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
and
E=\{12,23,\ldots,(n-1)n\}
, i.e.,
vertex
k
(
1<k<n
) is connected to vertices
k-1
and
k+1
. From
the ecological point of view, each vertex is a sampling site and we
associate with it the data collected there.

The notation we will use is
\{a,ab,abc\}
meaning there is only
species
a
at sampling site
1
, species
a
and
b
at
2
, and

a,b,c
at
3
. We will show in detail the computation of one
coboundary map and then only the spectrum of the Laplacian and its
energy, i.e., the sum of the squares of its eigenvalues. Below,
0^{4}

means zero eigenvalue of multiplicity
4
, etc..

Before we start, let us consider and compare two situations:

\{a,ab,abc\}
and
\{ab,ac,bc\}
. In both cases

\gamma
-diversity is
3
and the average
\alpha
-diversity is
2
,
hence the multiplicative
\beta
-diversity is
1.5
and the additive
one is
1
in both cases. But the situation clearly is different, for
example, in terms of nestedness
(
Baselga 2010
)
. Such situations
motivate the construction below.

Let us discuss the practical computation of the energy for the graph
sheaf Laplacian corresponding to
\{ab,a,abc\}
. See
Essl 2023
and

Hansen 2020b
for more details of the theory.

We have three vertices,
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}
and two edges
e_{12}
and

e_{23}
. We associate with
v_{1}
the vector space
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, with
v_{1}

the space
{\mathbb{R}}
and with
v_{3}
the space
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.

As the intersection of the sets
\{a,b\}
and
\{a\}
is
\{a\}
, we
associate with the edge
e_{12}
the space
{\mathbb{R}}
and are looking for
two linear maps into
{\mathbb{R}}
. This means that associated with the edge

e_{12}
we will have a row of the coboundary map
\delta
. Similarly,
as the intersection of the sets
\{a\}
and
\{a,b,c\}
is
\{a\}
,
we associate with the edge
e_{23}
the space
{\mathbb{R}}
and are looking for
two additional linear maps into
{\mathbb{R}}
.

As there are
6
(repeated) species to be taken into account, the
argument above means that the coboundary map
\delta
will be a

2\times 6
matrix, and the corresponding Laplacian will be a

6\times 6
(symmetric) matrix.

Let us see how to construct the first row of
\delta
, which encodes
the interaction of
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
. The first one,

{\mathcal{F}}_{v_{1}\rightarrow e_{12}}
, maps
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
into
{\mathbb{R}}
, taking the vector

(a,b)^{T}
into
a
; clearly it is represented by
1\times 2
matrix

[1\quad 0]
; the second,
{\mathcal{F}}_{v_{2}\rightarrow e_{12}}
, takes
{\mathbb{R}}

into
{\mathbb{R}}
mapping
a
into
a
. It is represented by the
1\times 1

matrix
[1]
. Hence the first row of
\delta
is the
1\times 6

matrix

\left[\begin{array}[]{rrrrrr}1&0&-1&0&0&0\end{array}\right],

where the first two entries correspond to

{\mathcal{F}}_{v_{1}\rightarrow e_{12}}
, the next one to

-{\mathcal{F}}_{v_{2}\rightarrow e_{12}}
, and the remaining three zeros to

v_{3}
(with which we associated
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
).

Similarly, we encode the interaction of
v_{2}
and
v_{3}
. As
mentioned above, we associate with the edge
e_{23}
the vector
space
{\mathbb{R}}
.
{\mathcal{F}}_{v_{2}\rightarrow e_{23}}
maps
{\mathbb{R}}
into
{\mathbb{R}}
,
taking
a
into
a
, so it is again represented by the

1\times 1
matrix
[1]
.
{\mathcal{F}}_{v_{3}\rightarrow e_{23}}
maps

{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
into
{\mathbb{R}}
, taking the vector
(a,b,c)^{T}
into
a
. Hence it
is represented by

\left[\begin{array}[]{rrr}1&0&0\end{array}\right].

Putting all this information together, we have for the second row of

\delta
the
1\times 6
matrix

\left[\begin{array}[]{rrrrrr}0&0&1&-1&0&0\end{array}\right],

where the first two zeros correspond to
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
associated with

v_{1}
, the next entry is
{\mathcal{F}}_{v_{2}\rightarrow e_{23}}
and the last
three come from
-{\mathcal{F}}_{v_{3}\rightarrow e_{23}}
. Hence for

\{ab,a,abc\}
the coboundary map is given by

\delta=\left[\begin{array}[]{rrrrrr}1&0&-1&0&0&0\\
0&0&1&-1&0&0\end{array}\right].

Once the coboundary map
\delta
is constructed, the graph sheaf
Laplacian is computed from (
1
), and its spectrum gives the
energy (
2
).

Now we collect the information (spectrum

,
multiplicative
\beta

[(i)]

4.

\{a,bc,abc\}

;

3.2
K_{3}

The case of (
8
)-(
9
) can be dealt in the following way:
to each chain graph with 3 vertices corresponds a complete graph
K_{3}

with the same data on the vertices. For example cases (
5
),
(
7
) and (
8
) correspond to the same configuration if
adjacency is not taken into account. The computation of energy can be
repeated. For the case of (
8
) we have
\sigma=\{0^{3},2,3^{2}\}
,

E=22
while for (
9
) we obtain
\sigma=\{0^{3},3^{2},2^{2}\}
,

E=26
, thus separating the two cases.

Clearly (
7
) and (
8
) correspond to the same sheaf over

K_{3}
, so cannot be separated. Interestingly, neither can (
3
)
and (
4
), both of which over
K_{3}
have energy of

.

We note that the setup suggested in this section leads to an
interesting problem in combinatorics: for all possible configurations
of creating
n
subsets (with repetition) from a set of
m
elements,
characterise the energy landscape and its discrimination ability.

4
Discussion

Generalising cases (
1
) and (
10
) above to chains with
n

vertices and
\gamma
-diversity
m
we see that the graph sheaf
Laplacian energy is maximised by the configuration in which each site
has all
m
species present. This situation corresponds to classical

\beta
-diversity being as small as possible, either one in the
multiplicative case or zero in the additive case. It is plausible that
the minimal energy configuration will have as many singletons as
possible, which maximises
\beta
-diversity. So we can conjecture that
if in two configurations with same
\gamma
-diversity,

\beta
-diversities
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
and energies
E_{1}
and

E_{2}
,
\beta_{1}<\beta_{2}
implies
E_{1}\geq E_{2}
.

One might speculate that in ecosystems energy decays in time, and it
would be interesting to see whether ecosystems minimise the rate of
decay of energy. Such a principle would have predictive ability.

The way we define the sheaf, there is no difference in the chain case
between
\{ab,ac,abc\}
and
\{ad,ac,abc\}
(of course using sheaves
over
K_{3}
there is). Note that these two configurations have
different
\gamma
-diversity, so
\gamma
-diversity cannot be
trivially read off from the energy computation. One suggestion is the
following construction. Adjoin to our graph
G
on
n
vertices an

(n+1)
-st vertex to which we assign data containing all the species.
In other words, we hard-wire
\gamma
-diversity into the new graph
which we will call
G_{\gamma}
, and connect this vertex with all the
existing vertices. Now compute the energy of the sheaf Laplacian of
the new graph. This construction separates
\{ab,ac,abc\}
, the
energy of the graph sheaf Laplacian of
G_{\gamma}
being

64
, and
\{ad,ac,abc\}
, in which case the corresponding energy is

66
. Unfortunately, this construction still does not separate
(
3
) and (
4
), giving an energy of 46 in both
cases. Understanding the difficulty here and separating these two
situations is left for further work. A possibility is to use
hypergraphs.

To summarise: we have just started to understand what ecologically
important information is contained in the graph sheaf Laplacian and,
in particular, how it relates to and complements the information
provided by the classical
\beta
-diversity. Much remains to be done:
automating the computation of the coboundary map, leveraging to the
present context the many tools used with great success in
network/graph theory, and understanding how to combine the information
obtained from the graph sheaf Laplacian computations using
G
,
K_{n}
and

G_{\gamma}
.

These are mathematical aspects of the work. Ecological aspects would
include how changes in biodiversity translate into, for example,
changes in energy, and how energy encodes different decompositions of
biodiversity, in particular of
\beta
-diversity

(
Baselga 2010
;
Baselga and Leprieur 2015
)
.

Above we equated biodiversity with richness. More sophisticated
approaches, such as those based on Hill’s numbers

(
Chao and Chiu 2016
;
Chao and Ricotta 2019
;
Leinster and Cobbold 2012
)
are left for future work.

Finally, we expect that the approach of this paper has
applicability beyond the area of
\beta
-diversity. For example, the
influential work of Borgatti
(
Borgatti 2005
)
on network flows can
be interpreted in terms of sheaf dynamics.
