Phytosterols improve feed efficiency in yaks by enriching rumen Succiniclasticum
Authors: Qi Wang, Chen Duan, Hongjian Dai, Donghai Lv, Zunji Shi, Najuma Joshi, Martin Gierus, Weiyun Zhu, Lizhuang Hao, Yanfen Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundYaks are important livestock species on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau (QTP), but their productivity is constrained by the harsh alpine environment and the seasonal scarcity of forage resources. Improving feed efficiency has become the key to the sustainable development of Plateau Yak breeding industry. Phytosterols have shown a good application prospect in ruminant feed efficiency improvement, but the impact on yaks is not clear. This study explored the effects of dietary phytosterols supplementation on growth performance, nutrient digestibility, rumen microbial community structure and metabolic function of yaks fed in a barn.ResultsTwenty-eight 1.5-year-old male yaks (137.10 ± 8.70 kg) were randomly divided into two groups after 28 days of pre feeding period: the control group (Con, n = 14) was fed with basic diet, and the experimental group (PS, n = 14) was added with 200 mg/kg phytosterols in concentrate. The formal test period was 60 days. Compared with Con group, PS group significantly increased average daily gain (P = 0.001), apparent digestibility of crude protein (P = 0.036) and neutral detergent fiber (P = 0.006), and reduced feed conversion rate (P = 0.002). The rumen fermentation mode of PS group changed, the proportion of propionate increased significantly (P = 0.001), while the proportion of acetate (P = 0.006), acetate to propionate ratio (P = 0.001) and lactate (P = 0.035) concentration decreased significantly. Phytosterols significantly changed the structure of rumen microbial community and significantly increased the copy number of rumen bacteria (P < 0.05). Metatranscriptomic analysis showed that PS group significantly increased the relative abundance of key bacteria, including Succiniclasticum, Faecalibacterium, Ruminococcus, Butyrivibrio, and Alistipes (P < 0.05). Microbial co-occurrence network analysis revealed that the number of edges, average degree and modular index of microbial community structure network in PS group increased. Rumen function analysis showed that glycolysis / gluconeogenesis and propionate metabolism were significantly enhanced in PS group (P < 0.05), and the expression of key enzymes such as hexokinase and fumarate hydratase were significantly enhanced (P < 0.05). Metabolomic analysis revealed that phosphoenolpyruvic acid, fumarate and methylmalonyl-CoA were significantly accumulated in the rumen of PS group (P < 0.05). Pathway analysis showed that the pathway impact value of propionate metabolism and glycolysis / gluconeogenesis was greater than 0.1.ConclusionsPhytosterols (200 mg/kg of concentrate) can effectively improve the feed efficiency of yaks by regulating the structure and function of rumen microorganisms and altering the fermentation patterns.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42523-026-00562-9.Keywords: Phytosterols, Yak, Feed efficiency, Rumen fermentation, Rumen microbiome

Background

As a key livestock species in the alpine ecosystem of the Qinghai Tibet Plateau (QTP), yak plays an irreplaceable role in maintaining the local ecological balance and social and economic stability [1, 2]. However, several interrelated factors have contributed to the long-term malnutrition of yaks in this region: the harsh climatic conditions, the short forage growth period, the severe shortage of winter forage, and the additional challenges posed by grassland degradation and climate change [3]. In this context, improving feed efficiency has become the key to alleviate forage pressure and realize the sustainable development of yak industry [4]. Host phenotype is not only determined by genetic background, but also significantly affected by its symbiotic microbiome [5]. The feed efficiency of ruminants is closely related to the rumen microbial community, which is responsible for transforming digestible feed into nutrients available to the host [6, 7]. Volatile fatty acids (s) produced by rumen fermentation are absorbed by rumen epithelium, providing more than 70% of energy supply for ruminants [8, 9]. The composition and function of rumen microbiome are important factors determining rumen fermentation pattern [10, 11]. It is worth noting that the metabolic fate of different VFAs affects the overall energy utilization efficiency. Propionate, as a precursor of gluconeogenesis, is a fermentation product with higher energy efficiency than acetate. This is mainly because the generation of acetate is accompanied by the release of hydrogen (H2), resulting in the loss of feed energy in the form of methane (CH4) [12, 13]. Therefore, manipulating the rumen microbiome to optimize fermentation pattern is one of the strategies to improve ruminant feed efficiency.

Phytosterols are a group of steroidal compounds from natural plants, with structures similar to cholesterol. Since the discovery of their effect of reducing serum cholesterol [14] in the middle of the 20th century, studies have revealed their anti-tumor [15], anti-inflammatory [16] and antioxidant functions [17]. In recent years, studies have found that phytosterols have shown a prebiotic-like effect in humans and monogastric animals, specifically regulating the intestinal microbial community structure and optimizing the metabolic function of the host [18]. Phytosterols and intestinal microbiota engage in a bidirectional interaction, wherein the microbiota metabolizes phytosterols while phytosterols, in turn, modulate microbial community structure [19]. Previous studies have shown that phytosterols can change the rumen fermentation pattern [20], but their effects on the growth performance, rumen microbiota structure and function of yaks are not clear. Therefore, the potential application of phytosterol as a rumen targeted regulator in Yak needs to be further explored.

We hypothesized that dietary phytosterols enhance yak growth and feed efficiency by modulating rumen microbiota composition and metabolic function. To test this hypothesis, we conducted an integrated investigation combining growth performance measurements with rumen fluid metatranscriptomics and untargeted metabolomics. The study aimed to (1) assess the effects of phytosterols on yak growth performance and rumen fermentation; (2) characterize the changes in rumen microbiota structure, interaction networks, and metabolic functions induced by phytosterols; (3) elucidate the role of rumen microbiota remodeling in the phytosterol-mediated improvement of feed efficiency. This study elucidates how phytosterols improve feed efficiency through rumen microbiota remodeling and associated metabolic shifts. Our findings provide both mechanistic insights and a practical nutritional strategy to support sustainable yak production on the QTP.

Methods

Animal ethics statement and study area description

All animal test operations were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Agricultural University with the approval number of SYXK 2023-0079. The experiment was carried out in the Plateau Ecological Agriculture and Animal Husbandry Science and Technology Demonstration Park in Heri Town, Zeku County, Huangnan Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture, Qinghai Province, China (E 100°58′23″, N 35°14′44″, altitude 3389 m; Fig. S1).

Animals, diets, and experimental design

Twenty-eight male yaks, approximately 1.5 years old with similar initial body weights (137.10 ± 8.70 kg), were selected as the research subjects. This study was conducted over an 88-day period, including a 28-day pre-feeding period and a 60-day formal trial period. Upon arrival, the yaks were transferred from autumn pasture to individual barn pens, where they were individually housed and fed for the duration of the experiment. During the pre-feeding period, all animals received the same diet consisting of oat hay and concentrate feed (concentrate-to-roughage ratio 60:40), and feed intake was recorded for 7 consecutive days to determine the baseline intake level. Following the pre-feeding period, yaks were randomly assigned to one of two groups (n = 14 each) stratified by initial body weight : the Control (Con) group, which received the basal diet, and the Phytosterols (PS) group, supplemented with phytosterols at 200 mg/kg of concentrate. Phytosterols were provided by Nanjing Nature Biotechnology Co., Ltd. To ensure uniform distribution in the concentrate, the phytosterols were first thoroughly mixed with a small amount of concentrate as a premix, and this premix was then gradually blended into the remaining concentrate using a feed mixer until homogeneous. The concentrate was freshly prepared daily before each feeding. The composition and nutritional level of dietary and phytosterols are shown in Table 1 and Table S1. All yaks were fed at 08:00 and 17:00 every day, and sufficient drinking water was provided.

Sample collection

During the formal test, we collected concentrate and hay samples every week. From day 56 to 60, fresh fecal samples were collected three times daily (at 09:00, 14:00, and 20:00) from each yak. Daily fecal samples (200 g per yak) were obtained by pooling the subsamples from the three collection time points. After mixing evenly, 20 ml of 10% tartaric acid solution was added for nitrogen fixation treatment and stored at -20℃. The samples were dried to constant weight at 65℃, grinded through a 40 mesh sieve, and stored in a cool and dry place for subsequent apparent digestibility determination.

At the end of the experiment and before the morning feeding on the 61st day (08:00–09:00), rumen fluid was collected through an oral stomach tube. Although this method carries a potential risk of saliva contamination, the initial 100 mL of fluid was discarded to minimize this effect. Thereafter, approximately 500 mL of rumen fluid was collected from each yak, filtered by four layers of gauze, subpackaged, and immediately stored in liquid nitrogen for rumen fermentation parameters, metatranscriptomics, and metabolomics analysis. Subsequently, 10 ml of jugular vein blood was collected from each yak. After 20 min of standing, the collected blood was centrifuged at 3000 r/min for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected and sub packed and stored in liquid nitrogen, for serum biochemical parameter analysis.

Growth performance and apparent digestibility

All yaks were weighed on an empty stomach before morning feeding on days 1, 31, and 61 of the formal trial period. Dry matter intake (DMI) was measured in three phases: during the pre-feeding period (days 23–28) and during the formal trial period (days 25–30 and days 55–60). Each phase involved continuous measurement for 6 days. Before 08:00 each morning, feed troughs were cleaned, and residual feed was collected. The weight of the orts was recorded, and samples were taken to determine their moisture content, based on which the daily DMI was calculated. Feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated based on the aforementioned data. Specific calculation formulas are provided in Table 2.

Serum biochemistry and rumen fermentation

Serum biochemical parameters were quantified using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) in strict accordance with protocols. The analysis encompassed glucose (GLU), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), globulin (GLOB), creatinine (CREA), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), uric acid (UA), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and total cholesterol (TC).

Before the rumen fluid was filtered by four layers of gauze, the pH value of rumen fluid was measured by portable pH meter. Volatile fatty acids (including acetate, propionate, isobutyrate, butyrate, isovalerate, and valerate) were analyzed by gas chromatography. The specific process: 1 ml of rumen fluid and 0.2 ml of 25% metaphosphoric acid and crotonic acid mixture were mixed, frozen overnight at -20℃ (12 h), then thawed at 4℃, and centrifuged at 12,000 r/min for 10 min. The supernatant was filtered through a water-based filter membrane and injected into an Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph for analysis. Lactate concentration in rumen fluid was detected by lactate assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China). Ammonia nitrogen (NH3-N) concentration was determined by phenol sodium hypochlorite colorimetry [23]. Microbial protein (MCP) concentration was quantified according to the Coomassie brilliant blue colorimetric method [24].

Rumen fluid microbial DNA extraction and absolute quantification

Microbial DNA was extracted from the rumen fluid using the TIANamp Stool DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing). Specifically, the rumen fluid samples were thawed at room temperature and subjected to low-temperature high-speed centrifugation to remove the supernatant. Hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium bromide (CTAB) solution was added to the resulting precipitate and thoroughly mixed. The mixture was then transferred to a centrifuge tube containing zirconium beads, and cell disruption was carried out using bead beating. During this step, RNase was added to remove RNA impurities. Subsequently, DNA was precipitated using a phenol-chloroform-isoamyl alcohol mixture and dissolved in Tris-EDTA buffer. The concentration and purity of the DNA were measured using ultra-micro spectrophotometer, and qualified DNA samples exhibited an absorbance ratio at 260/280 nm between 1.8 and 2.0. The extracted DNA samples were stored at -80℃ for subsequent analysis.

All primers were obtained from Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and sequences [25–27] are provided in Table S2. Absolute quantification of total bacteria, archaea, and fungi in the rumen fluid was performed using quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR). RT-qPCR was performed in 20 µL volumes (10 µL SYBR GREEN, 0.4 µL primer F, 0.4 µL primer R, 2 µL DNA template, 7.2 µL nuclease-free water). A standard qPCR protocol was used, consisting of an initial denaturation (95℃ for 30 s), 40 cycles of denaturation (95℃ for 10 s) and annealing/extension (60℃ for 30 s). Subsequent melting curve analysis was performed by heating from 60℃ to 95℃, with holds at 95℃ for 15 s, 60℃ for 1 min, and again at 95℃ for 15 s. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. Standard plasmids containing target sequences of bacteria, fungi, and archaea were serially diluted 10-fold to generate eight concentration gradients. The cycle threshold (CT) values were obtained by RT-qPCR analysis, and the standard curve was constructed. The gene copy numbers of bacteria, archaea and fungi in each sample were calculated.

Rumen fluid metatranscriptomic sequencing and analysis

HiPure Stool RNA Kit (Magen, China) was used to extract total microbial RNA from rumen fluid samples [28]. The extraction process mainly includes sample pretreatment, cell lysis and RNA release, phase separation and crude RNA extraction, column purification, and final RNA elution and preservation. RNA concentration and integrity were measured on Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA) using Agilent RNA 6000 nano kit. Integrity assessment was based on ribosomal RNA ratio (23 S/16S > 1.8) and RNA integrity index (RIN > 7.0). Follow up analysis was performed on samples with concentration > 50 ng/µL. Metatranscriptomic sequencing was subsequently performed by BGI Genomics (Shenzhen, China).

After the total RNA extracted was treated with magnetic beads combined with RNase H for rRNA removal, the remaining RNA was randomly interrupted by fragmentation buffer, and then cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription system. The cDNA was purified by magnetic beads and the quality was evaluated. The subsequent steps include end repair, adding A-tail and connector connection to construct the sequencing library, and quality inspection of the library. After the library products were cyclized, the circular DNA molecules were amplified by rolling ring replication to form DNA nanospheres (DNBs). Finally, DNBs were sequenced on DNBSEQ platform (Table S3).

After the original data is off the machine, the data is processed and analyzed through a series of standardized processes. First, fastp (v0.23.4) is used to control the quality of the original data, removing the low-quality reading segments and joint sequences to ensure the data quality. Then, the rRNA sequence was removed by SortMeRNA (v4.3.7) to reduce the interference of background noise. Next, the data were aligned to the host genome by BWA (v0.7.17), and the host source sequences were removed to further purify the data. Cleaned reads were further mapped to a yak reference gene set [29] using bwa to identify microbial gene expression profiles. The gene abundances were counted, the standardized Counts Per Million (CPM) values were extracted, and the abundance data table was integrated and screened to generate a high-quality expression matrix. Functional annotation was performed using eggnog’s emapper (v2.1.12) for gene function classification. Diamond (v2.1.8) was used to annotate the NR database and carbohydrate active enzymes (CAZymes) of yak reference gene set. Finally, the data were integrated through Python script to generate the final analysis results, and comprehensively analyze the composition and functional characteristics of rumen microbial community of yak.

Rumen fluid metabolomic profiling and data processing

Rumen fluid samples were thawed on ice. A 100 µL aliquot was transferred to a pre-cooled 2 mL microcentrifuge tube and spiked with 20 µL of an isotope-labeled internal standard (L-2-chlorophenylalanine, 0.3 mg/mL in methanol). The mixture was vortexed for 10 s, followed by the addition of 300 µL of a chilled biphasic extraction solvent (methanol: acetonitrile = 2:1, v/v). After immediate vortexing at 2000 rpm for 1 min, the sample emulsion underwent ultrasonication in an ice bath (40 kHz, 10 min) and was then incubated at − 20℃ for 30 min to precipitate proteins. Subsequent centrifugation at 13,000 × g for 10 min (4℃) yielded a supernatant, which was transferred to a clean vial and dried under a gentle nitrogen stream at 40℃. The residue was reconstituted in 300 µL of methanol-water (1:4, v/v, containing 0.1% formic acid) with vortexing (30 s) and brief ultrasonication (40 kHz, 3 min). For signal correction, 10 µL of a second internal standard (Lyso PC17:0, 0.1 mg/mL in methanol) was added. To reduce lipid interference, samples were held at − 20℃ for 2 h and centrifuged again under the same conditions. Finally, 150 µL of the clarified supernatant was transferred to an LC‑MS vial for analysis.

The raw mass spectrometry data were converted from raw to mzXML format using the MSConvert module of ProteoWizard (v3.0.8789) with dynamic mass calibration enabled applying a specified peak‑picking threshold during conversion. Metabolic features were then extracted with the XCMS package (v3.12.0) in R [30]. Chromatographic peaks were detected using the centWave algorithm, and retention time was aligned by the density‑distribution method (bw = 2, mzwid = 0.015). Quality control was performed using pooled QC samples, the characteristic peaks with relative standard deviation (RSD) > 30% were removed, and the signals in the blank samples are filtered to obtain a clean quantitative matrix.

Metabolite annotation was carried out through a multi‑level approach using the HMDB [31], MassBank [32], LipidMaps [33], and mzCloud [34] databases. Level 1 annotation required accurate mass number matching (Δm/z ≤ 30 ppm) and isotopic pattern similarity ≥ 85%; Level 2 annotation required MS/MS spectral matching, defined as the presence of ≥ 3 characteristic fragment ions with intensity error ≤ 25%. CFM‑ID 4.0 was used for fragment prediction, and KEGG pathway network analysis was used to evaluate the biological background. The entire workflow incorporated mass‑deviation calculation, adduct ion interpretation, and dynamic calibration compensation to ensure annotation reliability.

Statistical and bioinformatics analysis

Based on the diversity of Bray Curtis, vegan package in R was used for principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) to visualize the differences of microbial communities. The significance of separation between groups was tested by analysis of similarities (ANOSIM), with 999 permutations. In order to identify the key microbial groups related to the treatment group, the randomForest package in R was used to construct a random forest model. The importance of each taxon was evaluated by mean decrease accuracy (MDA), which reflects the increase degree of model prediction error when the taxon data is eliminated. The microbial co-occurrence network was constructed by using WGCNA package through CNSknowall online platform (https://cnsknowall.com). Associations between bacterial taxa and functional profiles were assessed using Spearman rank correlation. For these analyses, only bacterial genera with a relative abundance > 0.1% and a prevalence > 50% across samples were included.

Non-targeted metabolomics data were processed on the MetaboAnalyst 5.0 platform (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca). Supervised orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was applied to characterize differential metabolic patterns between groups. Metabolites that met the following criteria were identified as differential metabolites: the variable importance projection (VIP) > 1, fold change (FC) > 1.5 or < 0.67, and P < 0.05. These differential metabolites were subsequently annotated and analyzed for KEGG pathway enrichment. The biological correlation of enrichment pathways were evaluated by calculating the Pathway Impact value.

The phenotypic data (including growth performance, apparent digestibility, serum biochemical and rumen fermentation) between groups were compared by Student’s t-test. The differences between the relative abundance of microbial groups and the standardized count (CPM) of metatranscriptomic functional annotation were analyzed by Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and P-values of multiple comparisons was corrected by Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Only microbial taxa with relative abundances > 0.1% and functional features with CPM > 10 were included in the comparative analyses. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05, and a trend was noted at 0.05 ≤ P < 0.10.

Results

Growth performance and apparent nutrient digestibility

Initial body weight did not differ between the two groups (P = 0.850, Table 2). DMI was also unaffected by dietary phytosterols supplementation (P = 0.765). However, PS group showed a significantly higher ADG (P = 0.001) and a lower FCR (P = 0.002), indicating improved feed efficiency, compared to the Con group. As summarized in Table 3, phytosterols supplementation significantly increased the apparent digestibility of CP (P = 0.036) and NDF (P = 0.006). The apparent digestibility of DM, EE, and ADF did not change significantly (P > 0.05).

Serum biochemical parameters

The biochemical indexes of yak serum are shown in Table 4. Compared with the Con group, supplementation with phytosterols significantly reduced serum concentrations of UA (P = 0.009), TG (P = 0.002), and LDL-C (P = 0.026). TC tended to decrease (P = 0.086). There were no significant differences in serum TP, ALB, GLOB, A/G, GLU, BUN, CREA, and HDL-C concentrations (P > 0.05).

Rumen fermentation parameters

Rumen fermentation parameters are shown in Table 5. There was no significant difference in rumen pH between the two groups (P > 0.05). The PS group had a significantly lower concentration of lactate (P = 0.035). No significant differences were detected in the concentrations of NH3-N and MCP between the groups (P > 0.05). While TVFA concentration did not differ significantly between groups (P > 0.05), the molar proportion of propionate was significantly higher in the PS group (P = 0.001). In contrast, the molar proportion of acetate (P = 0.006) and the Ace/Pro ratio (P = 0.001) were significantly lower in the PS group.

PCoA showed that the two groups were significantly separated (ANOSIM R = 0.315, P < 0.05, Fig. S2), and Shannon diversity index was significantly increased in PS group (P < 0.05, Fig. 1B). The random forest model further identified the five bacterial genera that contributed the most to the differences between groups (MDA > 4): Alistipes, Succiniclasticum, Bacteroides, Mediterraneibacter, and Ruminococcus.

A total of 13 phyla and 715 genera were identified in rumen bacterial community, of which 9 phyla and 41 genera were dominant groups (relative abundance > 0.1%, prevalence > 50%, Table S4). The two groups of common dominant bacteria include Bacillota (formerly Firmicutes), Bacteroidota, Pseudomonadota, Fibrobacterota, and Actinomycetota (Fig. S3A), while the dominant bacteria are composed of Prevotella, Butyrivibrio, Aristaeella, Succiniclasticum and Parabacteroides (Fig. S3B). The difference analysis showed that the relative abundance of Bacillota in PS group increased significantly, while the relative abundance of Bacteroidota decreased significantly (Fig. 1C). At the genus level, a total of 10 genera were significantly enriched in the PS group, including Butyrivibrio, Succiniclasticum, Ruminococcus, Bacteroides and Faecalibacterium (Fig. 1D).

Spearman correlation analysis further linked these bacterial shifts to host phenotypic traits (Fig. 1F, Table S5). Several genera, including Succiniclasticum, Ruminococcus, Faecalibacterium, and Desulfovibrio, showed significant positive correlations with propionate proportion, ADG, and NDF digestibility (P < 0.05). In contrast, these genera were negatively correlated with FCR (P < 0.05). A positive correlation was observed between Hoylesella and acetate proportion, whereas negative relationships were found with ADG and propionate proportion (P < 0.05).

Functional profile of the rumen microbiota

Based on rumen metatranscriptomic data, we identified 412 KEGG pathways (level-3). After filtering out low-abundance and exogenous pathways (CPM < 10, prevalence < 50%), 274 pathways were retained for downstream analysis (Table S6). PCoA of these functional profiles revealed a clear separation between the PS and Con groups (ANOSIM R = 0.477, P < 0.05, Fig. S4), indicating that phytosterols significantly altered the active metabolic functions of the rumen microbiota.

Among the 20 most abundant level‑2 KEGG categories, carbohydrate metabolism and environmental adaptation were significantly enriched in the PS group (P < 0.05; Fig. 2A). At the more detailed level‑3, several pathways within the top 20 by abundance were significantly upregulated in the PS group, including glycolysis / gluconeogenesis, pentose phosphate pathway, serine and threonine metabolism, and propanoate metabolism (P < 0.05, Fig. 2B).

Microbial interaction networks and core functional taxa

Dietary phytosterols supplementation altered the topological properties of the rumen bacterial co‑occurrence network (Table S8). The network in the PS group displayed greater complexity and connectivity, evidenced by increases in the number of edges (from 158 to 245), average degree (from 8.1026 to 14.0000), network density (from 0.2132 to 0.4118), and clustering coefficient (from 0.6424 to 0.7900), alongside a decrease in modularity (from 0.2604 to 0.1785), indicating a shift toward a more integrated community structure.

The two groups have different co-occurrence patterns (Fig. 4A, Table S9). 158 connections (145 positive and 13 negative) were detected in the Con group network, and the connectivity of Faecalibacterium was the highest (19 edges), Pseudoflavonifractor (18), Lachnoclostridium (17). In contrast, the PS group network contained 245 connections (209 positive and 36 negative), and Pseudoflavonifractor (25 edges), Flavonifractor (24), Treponema (24), Lachnoclostridium (23) were the most connected nodes. In the two groups of networks, the connections within Bacillota were mainly positive, while the connection between Bacillota and Bacteroidota were mostly negative. It is worth noting that under the intervention of phytosterols, the number of edges connecting of Succiniclasticum increased from 1 to 19, and those connecting Alistipes increased from 4 to 21, indicating their core position in the community.

Discussion

Against the backdrop of growing competition between humans and livestock for feed resources, improving feed efficiency has become a key research focus [35]. Therefore, plant-derived feed additives have attracted much attention, with phytosterols gaining increasing recognition for their potential application value in animal production [36].

As the main digestive site of ruminants, rumen fundamentally determines the production efficiency [37]. Compared with the Con group, the rumen pH of the PS group remained stable and the lactate concentration decreased significantly, which was conducive to maintaining a healthier rumen environment and a more stable microbial community. More fermentation substrates may be directed to the generation of volatile fatty acids, thereby improving energy efficiency. This change was consistent with the increase of apparent digestibility of NDF and the change of VFAs. In a previous dairy cows study, phytosterols significantly increased rumen MCP concentration [20]. In contrast, no significant changes in NH3-N or MCP were detected in the present study with yaks, although the apparent digestibility of CP was significantly increased. This discrepancy may be partly explained by species differences (yak and dairy cow) in rumen physiology and nitrogen metabolism. In addition, the observed increase in CP digestibility in this study may reflect enhanced digestion and absorption in the small intestine, rather than changes in rumen MCP synthesis.

Phytosterols significantly reduced the ratio of Ace/Pro, and promoted the transformation of rumen fermentation to propionate-type fermentation with higher energy efficiency. Fumarate is a key intermediate in the succinate pathway of propionate synthesis. In this pathway, fumarate is reduced to succinate via fumarate reductase, with the concomitant oxidation of reduced cofactors. Succinate is subsequently decarboxylated to propionate via succinyl-CoA and methylmalonyl-CoA intermediates. Importantly, this pathway consumes H2 equivalents, thereby competing with methanogenesis for this substrate and potentially reducing enteric methane emissions [38]. The metabolism of propionate affects the host in many ways, such as ketosis. Studies have shown that supplementation of propionate precursors can effectively alleviate the clinical symptoms of ketosis in dairy cows [39], which further confirmed the key role of improving propionate supply in maintaining energy homeostasis and production performance of ruminants.

Yaks in PS group achieved higher ADG and lower FCR without increasing DMI, which may be due to more energy being directed to growth. Although total VFA concentration did not differ between groups at the sampling time point, several indirect observations suggest that VFA yield was likely increased. These include improved NDF digestibility, stable rumen pH, and significantly reduced lactate levels. Another reason may be that the absorption rate of VFA into the blood was accelerated. These dynamic changes reflect the overall improvement of rumen fermentation efficiency. Therefore, we speculate that the transformation of propionate-type fermentation induced by phytosterols is the key mechanism for improving feed efficiency of yak.

RT-qPCR absolute quantitative analysis showed that bacterial community was the main microbial group in response to phytosterols intervention.Metatranscriptomic data provide a superior approach for elucidating the link between rumen microbial functions and host performance, as they directly probe the active functional potential of the microbiome [10, 40]. The dominant phyla are Bacteroidota and Bacillota, which together occupied more than 80% of the total community and are considered essential for maintaining core rumen functions [41]. In this study, phytosterols treatment increased the relative abundance of Bacillota and decreased the relative abundance of Bacteroidota. Bacteroidota are mainly involved in protein hydrolysis and carbohydrate degradation [42], while Bacillota play key roles in energy conversion and absorption [43]. Notably, Bacillota harbor numerous genes related to dietary fiber fermentation and exhibit high metabolic flexibility in polysaccharide utilization [44, 45]. Yaks have adapted to these harsh conditions, and the increased Bacillota-to-Bacteroidota ratio suggests that phytosterols may enhance this adaptive trait [46].

Succiniclasticum as the key bacteria that drive the transformation of rumen fermentation to propionate dominated after phytosterols intervention. It showed high discrimination importance in the random forest model (MDA > 4), and it was significantly enriched in PS group. As the core functional bacteria in the rumen, its sole species, Succiniclasticum ruminis, can quickly convert succinate, the key intermediate in the process of fiber degradation, rapidly into propionate, so as to prevent succinate accumulation [47]. Previous studies on ruminants found that the relative abundance of Succiniclasticum was positively correlated with the molar proportion of propionate, ADG and apparent digestibility of NDF, and negatively correlated with the FCR [48–50]. Consistent with the results of this study, the enrichment of Succiniclasticum is associated with higher propionate production, better growth performance and improved feed efficiency. The lower rumen pH would inhibit the growth of Succiniclasticum [51], and the decrease of lactate concentration may have alleviated such inhibitory pressure. Phytosterols may directly or indirectly regulate the rumen, thereby promoting the proliferation of Succiniclasticum.

Other bacteria enriched in PS group and Succiniclasticum form functional complementarity and synergy. Ruminococcus and Butyrivibrio are critical for fiber degradation [6]. The abundance of Ruminococcus increased under high fiber diet, and the expression of 1,4-β-xylosidase (GH3), endocellulase (GH8, GH9) and other key enzymes genes were upregulated [52]. This genus also participates in the synthesis of cobalamin (Vitamin B12) [53], which is an essential cofactor for amino acid metabolism, energy production and microbial community stability [54]. Similarly, Butyrivibrio species can efficiently degrade hemicellulose and pectin, participate in the process of protein decomposition and fatty acid biohydrogenation, and make important contributions to the rumen fiber degradation gene pool [55, 56]. Phytosterols coordinately enriched these fibrolytic genera. This enhanced upstream plant cell-wall disintegration, supplying more fermentable substrates for downstream propionate producers such as Succiniclasticum. This cascade of events explains the significant improvement in NDF digestibility observed in this study.

Similarly, gut microbes as a key role in host metabolic health [57]. Some beneficial bacteria, such as Faecalibacterium, not only have the ability to degrade various fibers and plant polysaccharides, but also have anti-inflammatory and metabolic regulation functions [58, 59]. Faecalibacterium species can directly inhibit the growth of potential pathogens (such as Enterobacteriaceae) by producing short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) [60], with recent evidence ranking an uncharacterized Faecalibacterium sp. highly for such antagonistic activity [61]. In this study, phytosterols significantly increased the relative abundance of Faecalibacterium and Bifidobacterium in rumen, and decreased the levels of TC and LDL-C in serum, which was consistent with the report that obese individuals had higher relative abundances of Faecalibacterium prausnitzii and Bifidobacterium than lean individuals [62]. Phytosterols have been shown to have anti-inflammatory properties and can regulate NF‑κB pathway [63], but whether they play a role through microbial regulation remains unclear.

The change of rumen microbial community is accompanied by the change of function. Phytosterols significantly upregulated carbohydrate metabolism, especially glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and propanoate metabolism. A previous study reported that phytosterols enhanced the function of rumen module M00001 (Glucose → Pyruvate) [20], which was consistent with the results of this study. Enzymes involved in converting glucose to pyruvate, such as hexokinase and pyruvate kinase, were upregulated, ensuring sufficient precursor supply for propionate synthesis. Importantly, phytosterols enhanced enzymes in both major propionate‑producing routes: fumarate hydratase (succinate pathway) and L‑lactate dehydrogenase (lactate pathway). Succinate pathway is the main pathway of propionate in ruminants, while lactate pathway consumes lactate as an auxiliary pathway [64]. The conversion of pyruvate to lactate by L‑lactate dehydrogenase regenerates NAD+ to sustain glycolysis [65]. Phytosterols also activated amino acid degradation pathways leading to propionyl-CoA, a key intermediate derived from valine, odd‑chain fatty acids, methionine, isoleucine, and threonine (VOMIT) [39]. The BCKDHA catalyzes the conversion of 2‑ketobutyrate (from threonine) to propionyl‑CoA.

Different microbial communities in the rumen form a food web structure through cross feeding, their active metabolic functions and interspecific interactions jointly affect the energy utilization and feed efficiency of the host [66]. The structure of microbial co-occurrence network is closely related to ecosystem function and stability [67]. In this study, phytosterols have created a more complex and closely connected network structure. What is highlighted is that the correlation between Succiniclasticum and Alistipes in the community was enhanced. Alistipes primarily produces succinate, along with minor amounts of propionate and acetate [68], while Flexilinea can ferment glucose to propionate, acetate, lactate, and succinate [69].

Metatranscriptomics shows what is happening, while metabolomics shows what has happened [70]. In this study, non-targeted metabolomic analysis revealed significant upregulation of key intermediate metabolites such as phosphoenolpyruvic acid, fumarate, and methylmalonyl-CoA. Furthermore, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis indicated that phytosterols had higher Pathway Impact on alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, glycolysis / gluconeogenesis, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, and propanoate metabolism. The results were partially consistent with those of metatranscriptomics. The improvement of glycolytic function and adequate supply of precursors related to fumarate synthesis may be one of the reasons for fumarate accumulation. Succinyl-CoA was significantly lower in the PS group. The possible factors were as follows: succinyl-CoA was rapidly consumed as a precursor of methylmalonyl-CoA in the succinate pathway of propionate synthesis; it may be diverted to other biosynthetic processes (such as heme biosynthesis) [71]. Nicotinic acid mononucleotide (NAMN) as an important precursor of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+), phytosterols increases its level, indicating that may enhanced the synthesis ability of NAD+ through the remedial pathway (in Yak). As a core cofactor in redox, NAD+ is crucial for driving key reactions in productivity pathways such as glycolysis and TCA cycle [72]. Its increased likely supports the heightened glycolytic observed, particularly by facilitating the glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase reaction, thereby promoting NADH and ATP generation to meet cellular energy demands.

This study was the first to systematically evaluate the effects of dietary phytosterols on growth performance and rumen microbial structure and function of yaks by using the integrated multi‑omics method. By measuring animal growth indicators, rumen fermentation and other parameters, and combining with Metatranscriptomic and metabolome analysis, we preliminarily clarified that phytosterols affect rumen flora structure, change rumen fermentation mode, and improve feed efficiency of yaks. However, this study also havs limitations. We failed to isolate and culture Succiniclasticum and other bacteria to verify their functions. Future studies using pure culture will help to establish causal relationship. In addition, although the shift toward propionate-type fermentation may reduce methane production, technical difficulties in measuring methane emissions from semi-wild yaks on the QTP meant that we were unable to obtain direct emission data for verification in this study. Quantifying methane in follow‑up studies would clarify the full environmental and energetic impact of phytosterols.

Conclusion

In conclusion, adding phytosterols (200 mg/kg of concentrate) to yak diet can improve growth performance and feed efficiency. These benefits were related to the transformation of rumen fermentation mode and the adjustment of rumen microbiota structure and the improvement of rumen function. Metatranscriptomic and metabolomic analysis showed that phytosterols increased the relative abundance of Succiniclasticum and fibrolytic bacteria, enhanced carbohydrate metabolism, especially the glycolysis and propionate metabolism functions and the expression of key enzymes in the process. These changes drive the rumen to propionate fermentation, thereby improving the growth performance and feed efficiency of yak. This study not only revealed the potential of phytosterols as rumen microbial regulators, but also provided practical strategies for alleviating the shortage of forage resources in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and promoting the sustainable development of yak industry.
