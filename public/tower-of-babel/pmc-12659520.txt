Determinants of the implementation, integration, and sustainability of new non-professionally affiliated psychosocial support roles: a scoping review
Authors: Hugh Fulham-McQuillan, Maryanne Murphy, Carol Hilliard, Greg Sheaf, Maria Brenner, Agnes Higgins
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThere is an increasing drive to implement new non-professionally affiliated psychosocial support roles within multidisciplinary teams (MDT) to improve the integration of care. This is to enhance holistic care and extend the historic focus of care beyond the patient, to the family and community, however, determinants that impact on the implementation of these roles have yet to be reviewed.MethodsThe aim of the scoping review was to identify determinants impacting the implementation and sustainability of these roles within MDTs. The review was informed by the Joanna Briggs Institute framework and the ‘Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis’. Using a pre-defined search strategy, CINAHL, Medline, PsycInfo, and Web of Science databases were searched. Identified studies were screened using Covidence (www.covidence.org/). Data extraction and analysis involved three phases: data extraction using the Consolidation Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR); identifying and classifying determinants; developing explanatory themes beyond CFIR using thematic analysis.ResultsA total of 4232 papers remained after deduplication. Twenty-four papers met the inclusion criteria. Four themes were identified: new role boundaries; two sides to the worker – patient/client relationship; environmental preparedness; and role holder characteristics. Role boundary issues due to deficits in role clarity lack of training for the role holder, and information for wider team, were significant barriers to implementation and sustainability. Mentorship and training, which developed role holders’ skills and confidence as well as role holders’ personal characteristics, life experience, the flexibility of their role, and having time to spend with patients/clients were significant enablers.ConclusionThere is a need for greater attention to role clarity and boundary working during the design phase of roles and as an aspect of environmental preparedness, especially with MDT members. The provision of training specific to new roles, and support in the form of mentorship, are necessary to develop the required skills and confidence in new role holders. Given the considerable heterogeneity in the aims and objectives of research on new psychosocial roles not affiliated to a profession or discipline an absence of studies focusing on the determinants of the implementation, integration, and sustainability of these roles, more research is needed to support their future implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-12615-x.Keywords: Informal support roles, Determinants, Barriers, Enablers, Consolidated framework for implementation research, Scoping review

Introduction

Integrated care as an organising principle is frequently proposed as the key to delivering care to patients/clients, families and communities that is safe, timely, efficient and as close to home as possible [1]. Although the definition of integrated care varies depending on the perspective of stakeholders [2], its implementation requires a shift from a disease-centred model of care towards a more holistic approach to health promotion and wellbeing [3]. The integration of care is best understood not as a single intervention but as an emergent set of contextually dependent practices [1, 4]. In order to enhance and improve the integration of care several writers suggest a need to implement new roles to fill the gaps in current care provision [5, 6], including non-professionally affiliated roles [7], which are sometimes referred to as non-specialist roles [8].

Non-professionally affiliated roles or non-specialist roles [8] are roles which do not fit within existing disciplinary boundaries [9] and do not have a designated professional status conferred on the role holder [10]. While individuals with a professional qualification or background may occupy a new non-professionally affiliated role [11], the focus of this review is on the role itself rather than the professional background of the role holder. Similarly, peers with lived experience may be employed in informal psychosocial roles, however, peer support roles which focus primarily on the use of ones lived experience in the provision of psychosocial support [12] are not included in this review. Additionally, extended roles for health professionals such as nurses or pharmacists, are not included as these roles remain affiliated to an existing discipline. For the purposes of this review, the terms formal and informal are used to differentiate between role providers that are professionally affiliated (formal) and non-professionally affiliated (informal). Additionally, for this review, the ‘newness’ of roles refers to the recency of roles in comparison to traditionally established roles such as nursing and medicine or paraprofessional roles in healthcare such as paramedics, as such many non-professionally affiliated roles are ‘new’ roles within healthcare systems.

The implementation of new informal roles is thought to enhance holistic care [5, 6], improve horizontal integration between care services such as multidisciplinary teams (MDTs) [13], and extend the traditional focus beyond the patient to the family and community [14]. New roles are also viewed as a way of assisting in the reduction of healthcare professionals’ workloads and pressure, allowing them to perform roles and functions at the top of their licence, while delegating other roles and tasks to non-professionally affiliated workers [15, 16]. As such, these roles may be developed in response to unmet patient needs, a shortage of existing staff, or as part of a policy [10, 16, 17], and may be implemented within MDTs in primary, secondary or tertiary settings [5, 10, 18].

The tasks of these new roles centre around providing informal generic psychosocial support. Psychosocial support is defined as generic informal support, be it emotional, informational, or practical and/or navigational support [10, 19]. This often includes what Cain and Taborda [18] terms relational practice. Relational practice employs skills such as “listening, mutuality, reciprocity, and sensitivity to the emotional context” [10, 20–22].

Implementation is a ‘critical gateway’ between a decision to adopt an intervention, such as a new role and the routine use of, and support for, an intervention in practice [23]. In order to enhance the quality and efficacy of implementation of future informal roles and improve the horizontal integration of care there is a need for evidence-based information on the factors that influence the implementation and sustainability of new informal roles. Sustainability in this context refers to the continuing existence of these roles. A number of reviews of support worker roles have been conducted. Manthorpe and Martineau [24] sought to define the duties, and nature of varying support worker roles in the National Health Service in the United Kingdom, while Moran et al. [25] explored the broader category of support worker roles which includes duties beyond the provision of informal psychosocial support. Carter et al.’s [26] review set out to map the evidence for navigation roles held by both nurses and non-professionals in primary care settings, which include but are not limited to facilitating patient access to care and navigating health systems and services on behalf of patients. Other reviews have focused on support worker roles with specific patient populations such as: the effectiveness of support worker roles for people with dementia and their carers [27]; the duties of support workers providing palliative and end of life care in the community [28]; roles of workers supporting chronically ill older adults through healthcare transitions [29]; the effectiveness of non-specialist provision of psychosocial care to women living with HIV [30] and to people living with HIV [31]. Two reviews, a Cochrane review [32] and a recent umbrella review [33], explored the efficacy of non-specialist health workers providing psychosocial interventions in lower- and middle-income countries [32, 33]. These reviews provide important information on the context and roles of these support workers, and provide evidence for the efficacy of non-professionally affiliated or non-specialist providers within a variety of settings and with different patient populations. However, no reviews were identified that focused on the determinants of the implementation, integration, or sustainability of ‘new’ informal psychosocial provider roles in MDTs to support healthcare integration. Given the financial, emotional and practical costs [34] of any new initiatives within the health system, to inform the future implementation of these roles, the focus of this review is on the evidence base surrounding the determinants impacting the implementation, integration, and sustainability of new informal psychosocial support roles within existing MDTs. A further objective is to describe how new informal psychosocial roles are conceptualised.

Methodology

Scoping reviews are an efficient tool for assessing the extent of a complex area of research which has not been comprehensively reviewed, in addition to mapping key concepts and the types of evidence available within that area [35, 36]. This approach was deemed suitable to assess the evidence base for the implementation, integration, sustainability, and impact of new informal psychosocial roles in MDTs due to a lack of reviews in the area and the high level of variance in titles, roles and duties. This review was informed by the framework proposed by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) [37, 38]. The ‘Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis’ extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [39] was used to guide reporting.

Search strategy

The review team consisted of academics with multidisciplinary backgrounds (HFMcQ, CH, MM, MB, AH), and an information specialist (GS), who supported the team to develop a search strategy. Identifying search terms for the inclusion of papers for this review was challenging in that the titles of new roles are, by definition, not always known. Therefore, an initial limited search was conducted using the Web of Science database to identify key terms related to (1) new (2), psychosocial (3), role (4), boundaries, and (5) impact of new roles. Titles and abstracts were then analysed for related key terms. The team discussed and refined the search strategy, and this was further refined by GS. A systematic search using the refined search terms was conducted in the following electronic databases on 12th December 2022: CINAHL, Medline, PsycInfo, and Web of Science.

Following completion of the electronic database searches, identified references were imported into Covidence (https://www.covidence.org/). After deduplication, titles and abstracts were independently screened by two researchers against the inclusion and exclusion criteria outlined in Table 2. Any discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer not involved in screening that paper.

Data extraction and data analysis

The data extraction and analysis involved three distinct phases (see Fig. 1). Phase one focused on data extraction. Based on the aims of the review and guided by JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis [37], information about the author, study type, year of publication, origin of study (where the study was conducted), aims and methods were extracted using forms developed in Microsoft Excel. To help provide descriptions of role duties, training, role holder relevant experience/background, data on the impact of new roles on patients, families, role holders, MDTs and organisations were also extracted. In line with the aims of the review, data on determinants of the implementation, integration, and sustainability of new roles were extracted in line with the domains and constructs of the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [23, 40] (see information on domains and constructs https://cfirguide.org/constructs/). Phase one resulted in the development of 170 codes within the five domains of CIFR (see Supplemental Table 1. for frequency of codes along with illustrative quotes). Subsequent to this, phase two commenced, which involved a further analysis of the coded data to identify and classify the determinants as either an enabler or barrier using the CFIR constructs. This resulted in data being coded to 18 constructs of the 48 CIFR constructs, as data were not coded to all constructs (See supplemental table for constructs not coded). Of the 170 codes identified, 103 were classified as barriers and 67 were classified as enablers (see Fig. 2). The largest number of barrier codes were in the Implementation Process domain within the Engaging (n = 20) and Tailoring strategies (n = 17) constructs. The second most frequently coded domain was Innovation (14 codes in Innovation design construct) followed by the Individual domain (13 codes in Mid-level leaders construct). Enablers were most frequently coded in the Innovation domain (relative advantage construct, n = 23) followed by the Individuals domain (implementation deliverers construct n = 8; mid-level leaders construct n = 8). Phase three, the final phase of analysis involved reviewing and comparing all of the coded barriers and enablers to identify common threads or themes beyond the CFIR framework concerning the implementation of new informal psychosocial provider roles. This process was informed by Braun and Clarke [41] writing on thematic analysis. The outcome of these iterative phases of analysis and discussion among the team resulted in the following four higher order themes being identified: new role boundaries; two sides to the role holder–patient/client relationship; environmental preparedness; and the impact of role holder characteristics.

Rigour

To enhance rigour the data extraction tool was piloted, and two team members extracted data for each paper independently. To enhance consistency of data extraction, after completing data extraction for five studies team members came together to compare and discuss the outcome. Team members also came together to discuss the process and outcomes of the thematic analysis. In addition, a clear audit trail which articulated the process used, from paper selection through to data extraction, tabulation, analysis and data visualization was recorded.

Quality appraisal

Critical appraisal of included studies was performed using the Mixed Method Appraisal Tool (MMAT) [42] which is specifically designed for quality appraisal in mixed study reviews. The MMAT includes two general screening questions and a further five methodological quality criteria for common types of methodologies. As per the MMAT user instructions, two authors independently assessed the quality of the papers and met to discuss and reach consensus. Given that the aim of this scoping review was to provide a broad overview of the existing literature, and not to pool outcome data from quantitative studies, the quality appraisal tool was not used to exclude studies, but to help inform the synthesis and interpretation of findings (see Table 3 for information on study characteristics).

Results

In total, 6,154 papers were identified from the database searches using Covidence. After deduplication 1922 were removed. The title and abstract of 4232 papers were independently screened by two researchers against the inclusion and exclusion criteria outlined in Table 2. Of these, 72 papers met the eligibility criteria for inclusion. A further 13 papers were located through searching the reference list of included studies, reference list of other systematic reviews, and a search of Google Scholar to search for studies that cited the included articles. The full texts of the 85 papers were assessed for eligibility, resulting in 24 studies included in the review. Figure 3 shows the PRISMA ScR flow diagram [39].

Prior to discussing the four themes identified, information on the study characteristics and how roles are conceptualised and described are presented.

Study characteristics

The 24 studies were conducted in three countries: United Kingdom (n = 14); United States (n = 6); and Australia (n = 4) across a 28-year time span (1994–2022). Study settings included a variety of health and social care settings, specialist areas, both within hospital and community settings. Among the included studies, 17 used qualitative designs, five used a quantitative design and two used mixed methods. No study used a trial methodology. Data were collected from a variety of participants, including patients, role holders, members of the MDT and managers. When the aims and objectives of the included studies were parsed in total, the most frequent aims and objectives identified were to explore or understand new roles (n = 14), to evaluate the impact of roles (n = 8), and to explore the implementation or integration of new roles (n = 4). While only two studies specifically explored barriers and enablers to the implementation, integration or sustainability of new roles (Stanmore et al., [56], Smith-Merry et al., [51]), barriers and enablers were identified throughout the included studies. (See Table 3 for information on study characteristics).

Role titles and duties

Many of the roles were implemented with the aim of professionals delegating work to the new role holders with a view to reducing their work burden. As such, a wide range of titles were used (see Table 3.) The overall aim of the roles was to provide holistic care [48]; develop a plan of care based on patient/client goals and individualised needs (n = 5) [45, 49, 50, 53, 63] foster patient/client independence (n = 5) [10, 21, 45, 55, 57], motivate patients/clients [59], and enable them to reach their rehabilitation goals (n = 2) [55, 56]. Theme 1: new role boundaries

Throughout their everyday work, health professionals maintain and defend their disciplinary boundaries and professional jurisdictions [65] through social interactions with patients and colleagues [66]. This conscious protection of role boundaries is referred to as boundary work [67]. Two issues emerged in relation to role boundaries and boundary working which impacted implementation, namely role clarity and role conflict. These issues emerged across four CFIR domains, within three constructs from the Inner setting (Compatibility, Access to Knowledge and Information), two constructs from Implementation Process (Tailoring strategies, Engaging), three from the Individuals domain (Mid-level leaders, Implementation deliverers and Implementation recipients), and two from the Innovation domain (Innovation relative advantage, Innovation design).

Role clarity

Numerous studies revealed challenges to implementation arising from the lack of role clarity or on how the absence of discussion on role boundaries with the role holder [18, 63], patients/clients [45, 63] or to colleagues within the MDT or potential collaborators outside the MDT [18, 48, 55, 63]. The lack of role clarity was believed to be due to inadequate preparation [63], vague job descriptions [48], lack of consultation with team members about the new role [54], lack of managerial support for the role [18] and sometimes poor supervision of the person when in post [47].

When roles were not clearly explained to clients and patients, they experienced unmet expectations [10, 49]. New role holders experienced disengagement, burnout [18], demoralisation, and felt unable to realise their full potential in the role [54]. Role holders also felt a pressure to educate both themselves, other team members and collaborators on the scope of their role as they believed the success of their role depended on how well they could do this [18, 63].

The lack of clarity and inherent flexibility of some roles led to role drift from the original job description [47, 60, 63], role blurring [48] and boundary breaching [47]. Professionals were concerned that new role holders were susceptible to violations of professional standards when their duties intersected with team members [18], while some professional colleagues believed that a role was not suitable for the specific patient population [54]. Poor role clarity also led to problems with collaboration outside of the immediate team, with potential collaborators perceiving role holders to lack credibility; this resulted in them being disinterested in collaborating with the role holders [18, 63]. In contrast, professionals in one study asserted that blurring boundaries enabled the provision of holistic care and was to be encouraged [61]. As Rolfe et al. [61] conducted their study during the planning for the new role, the engagement of the MDT at this early stage may have resulted in this more favourable perspective.

Role conflict

Role conflict emerged from two sources, namely task sharing and role drift. When work was delegated to the new role holders with a view to reducing another team members’ workload this could cause existing staff members to feel threatened by the new role. Staff exhibited concern over the new roles overlapping with their roles [54, 60] and exacerbating the perceptions that the duties of a new role were too similar to their own [54, 60]. In other studies, staff felt that their roles were diminished as a result [53], or feared that the new role would eventually replace their own [49, 59, 61].

Task sharing led to role holders feeling anxious about the willingness of professionals to allow them to take on the lesser skilled aspects of their roles [61], and worrying that they were replicating the psychosocial role of other professionals such as social workers [18]. A lack of training about the role and poor supervision of the person resulted in role holders drifting and breaching boundaries with professionals within [47] and outside the team [63] which became a source of tension with colleagues [60]. One solution to this was imposing more structured role boundaries despite the new role holder’s belief that it was too early to know where best to place these boundaries [18].

Theme 2: role holder - patient/client relationship

This theme focuses on the role holder relationship with patients/clients. The subthemes of improved rapport and navigating patient/client relationships was inherent to the design of the innovation, and occurred across two CIFR domains, the Innovation domain (Innovation relative advantage, Innovation design) and the Implementation process domain (tailoring strategies).

Improved rapport with patients/clients

The inherent flexibility within the new roles as well as the ability to spend more time with patients/clients than many professionals [10, 18, 21, 22, 46, 48, 58, 59, 61] led the role holders to believe that they developed a better rapport with patients/clients [10, 44, 54], and did so more readily than professionals [47]. Role holders attributed this ability to their lack of an agenda when engaging with patients/clients [18]. In one study they framed their lack of agenda as an art in contrast to professional credentialism [18].

The benefits of this enhanced rapport with patients/clients included an improvement in their comfort with the service being provided [50], enhancing and enabling longer term service engagement and usage [47] and in one study, the development of the role into an informal advocacy role [56]. The increased time that many role holders spent with patients/clients [10, 18, 21, 22, 46, 48, 58, 59, 61] was also reported as enabling them to provide a more personal and responsive service to patients’ needs [58], which included building patients/clients’ confidence and providing ongoing support [54], being a source of motivation [59], promoting independence [21, 22], and acting as an advocate [43, 46, 56]. A benefit/enabler for colleagues of an enhanced rapport with patients/clients was a reduction in the work burden of team members [47, 53, 56, 60].

Navigating patient/client relationships

In four studies, professionals expressed concern that new role holders found it difficult to maintain a professional boundary with patients/clients due to the strength of the relationships they developed and their lack of expertise in navigating such relationships [18, 21, 47, 61]. Cain, Taborda [18] and Huxley, Evans [21] noted professionals’ concerns that new role holders were unprepared to navigate the possibility of patients developing a psychological dependency. New role holders in two studies did report that they found navigating relationships and maintaining a boundary a challenge [10, 22], while another study reported that patients/clients were reluctant to leave the service due to the support provided by the new role holder [50]. A further impact of these strong relationships was that new role holders felt emotionally drained by the amount of contact they had with a small number of patients/clients [61], which can impact long term sustainability.

Theme 3: environmental preparedness

This theme focuses on the environment. The two subthemes of low and high environmental preparedness arose within two CIFR domains, namely the Inner setting (Lack of relative priority, Access to knowledge and information, Work infrastructure, Compatibility, Communications, and the Individuals domain (Mid-level leaders).

Low environmental preparedness

A lack of systems and processes to support the work of new role holders were notable barriers to the implementation of new roles. This was most apparent in the areas of training, with new role holders reporting inconsistent training opportunities [47, 54] or a lack of available training specific to their role [48, 59, 61]. While some roles included ‘on the job’ training coupled with supportive mentorship [43], learning ‘on the job’ was not considered to be sufficient [48]. Role holders expressed a need for more training in how to provide emotional support and work with relatives/carers [59] and had concerns that the absence of training would result in stress [61]. Wilberforce, Abendstern [47] also noted that absence of training meant that the only option open to role holders who wished to progress their career was to begin formal education for a professional degree, resulting in poor sustainability of the role. Similarly, new role holders felt the need for more supervision, specifically around the needs of specific patient populations [62]. Those working in the community reported feeling isolated and in need of more contact with professionals [22].

As highlighted, poor supervision also resulted in role drift and breaching role boundaries with other professionals [47]. This exacerbated a lack of acceptance by professional team members [21], particularly when they had concerns over competency and risk [57, 63], and role holders not having professional accountability when seeing patients/clients [54]. Issues with supervision also led to MDT members and role holders having concerns that nobody was accountable for new role holders [61].

A further indication of a lack of environmental preparedness was staff and resource shortages, which resulted in new role holders being informed that their role was temporarily suspended [55, 56], being assigned with duties beyond their role description, such as administration work [48], or having to take on new duties that other staff believed to be inappropriate for them to do [47]. Further indications of lack of preparedness included delays to implementing a referral system [62], and a communication system for the new role [60]. Caseloads that exceeded new role holder’s capacity [63] was also an issue, with some role holders believing that when referrals were made, they were to suit the professional rather than being based on client or patient needs [10].

High environmental preparedness

In contrast, notable enablers to the implementation of new roles were tailored training and supervision. As new role holders often began worked in unfamiliar settings, training was essential to the development of necessary skills such as working in palliative care [44]. Training was an important enabler as it developed new role holders competence, confidence, motivation, and quality of care they could provide [58, 59], including the knowledge to know when to refer a patient/client to a professional [59, 61]. Responding to new role holder’s needs was also an enabler as when new role holders requested more training, training packages when implemented was viewed as not just improving skill but also improving morale [54, 58].

Support in the form of ad hoc meetings to discuss issues with the role holders [47, 59] and ongoing supervision and mentorship by team members and associated staff [43, 55], mentors [43] and managers [55, 59] were important enablers as they provided role holders with opportunities to discuss cases and emerging issues. In addition, providing coordination for role holders’ cases [47, 55] including balancing their caseloads to protect role holders [43] were important, not only in providing moral support [43, 55, 59], but in sustaining the person in their new role [43, 55, 59].

Theme 4: role holder characteristics

This theme concerns the impact of the role holder’s personal characteristics, motivation and capability on the implementation and sustainability of new roles. It arose from the Individuals domain (Innovation deliverers, Motivation, and Capability), the majority of which were enablers to the implementation and sustainability of the role, and the Innovation domain (Innovation design).

Personality and personal experience

In the implementation of new roles, the characteristics of the individual holding the role emerged as particularly important. As the most frequent duty was to develop a relationship or rapport with patients/clients, it follows that in several studies, the criteria for new role holders focused on interpersonal and life skills. This was reflected in the findings, such as one study whereby the interpersonal skills and approachable personality of the role holder were said to be integral to the success of their role in coordinating hospital discharge [52]. In another study, new role holders were assigned cases based on their compatibility, which included consideration of their previous mental health experiences [10].

New role holders believed that having personal experiences similar to patients/clients promoted a greater understanding of patient/client issues [63] and enhanced their ability to perform their role. Those working in mental health believed that having lived experiences of mental health challenges were an enabler, as it put them “on the same level” [10, 43]. In one study, prior experience of mental health problems was seen by both role holders and supervisors as essential for a successful working relationship [10]. More generally, life experiences and personality were framed by new role holders as key to their provision of informal support enabling them to build on their diverse backgrounds and experiences to develop their role and improve its impact [63].

Prior experience in healthcare was also an enabler, as those who previously worked with the team in a different role were more readily accepted [55], were provided with more autonomy [47], or were able to access previous collaborators and connections [63].

Motivation and sustainability

Motivation was an important factor regarding the sustainability of new roles. In line with the overall emphasis on interpersonal skills, new role holders were motivated to provide a better quality of care through their ability to “get away from professional thinking” [43, 61]. Motivations for taking on the role included wanting to have a long-term career in care work or to take on a new challenge spurred by life changes [10], however professional team members in one study expressed concern that the new roles were not sustainable as they suspected that role holders were motivated to use the role as a step to a career in mental health [54].

Role holders were demotivated by having high caseloads [43], low role clarity [18, 54], performing tasks which they perceived as having little therapeutic value such as accompanying patients/clients to appointments such as eye tests or helping with shopping [47], or performing tasks described as “paper pushing” [43, 43]. McCrae in their study suggested that a more accurate job description would assist in preventing disillusionment in role holders [54].

Discussion

The implementation of new roles, including non-professionally informal roles is increasingly seen as a way of improving integration of care [2], filling gaps in care provision, and relieving the workload of professionals [7, 15, 16, 51]. Although the majority of the 24 studies included in this scoping review had a heterogeneity of study aims, role titles and duties and did not explicitly explore determinants, the review does fill a gap in the literature by positing several barriers and enablers to the implementation, integration, and sustainability of informal psychosocial roles within multidisciplinary teams. To our knowledge, this is the first review that used CIFR [23], an implementation theory, combined with thematic analysis to explore determinants to role implementation.

A key determinant influencing the implementation process was boundary working, as the boundaries of the new roles were often undefined during the design phase in a deliberate attempt to enable responsivity to patient or team needs [5, 18, 24, 28, 60]. This not only impacted the implementation and integration of the role but affected sustainability, through their impact on the emotional wellbeing of role holders. Indeed, studies not focused on new roles make a link between role conflict and ambiguity with depression [68] and decreased motivation [69], with a recent study noting the strong association between healthcare workers’ perceptions of role conflict and their ensuing burnout [70]. A new non-professionally affiliated psychosocial support role could enhance quality communications that are important to a safe and effective journey for patients and clients [71], an area of care that is often lacking [72]. However, this review has also highlighted that it could challenge maintaining professional boundaries with patients and clients and thereby not be a long-term sustainable intervention.

The review also brings into sharp focus the importance of environmental preparedness to enable successful implementation and sustainability of roles. Tailored education for the role, supervision and ongoing mentoring were strong enablers to implementation and sustainability. Although the emphasis within informal roles is on the value of lived experience, the challenges identified in this review highlight the importance of ongoing education and support for people in informal roles. Education would go a long way in supporting role holders to recognise the signs of psychological dependency as well as enabling them to navigate relationships challenges while maintaining the strengths associated with informal relationships. Education as well as improved supervision and mentorship would also assist role holders to be being better prepared to manage boundary issues as they emerged [73], while education of the wider team and potential collaborators on the new role could improve the implementation process [74]. Onward education opportunities for career development of role holders may support motivation and wellbeing, and thereby sustainability [75, 76] and thereby potentially mitigate against demotivation and turnover of staff [77].

Although the composition of any MDT depends on the needs of the specific patient population along with other factors [78], it frequently spans a number of disciplines including medicine, nursing, psychology, pharmacy, physiotherapy, and social work [79] all with distinctive professional identifies [80]. As the wider literature indicates, the practice of boundary work frequently poses challenges to effective multidisciplinary work [81], therefore it is of no surprise that it posed challenges to the integration of new roles as many of the issues that developed could be said to have arisen from the absence of these layers of supports. In addition, the framework of organisational socialisation posits that all professionals in new roles experience stress due to the challenges of uncertainty, arising from concerns around what is expected of them, understanding the greater context of their role, attaining social acceptance, and task mastery [82]. It is apparent from this review that people in new informal roles are more likely to experience these challenges in greater quantities and over a longer period of time than people in established professions entering into new healthcare settings [83, 84]. First, due to their roles lacking the equivalent educational, theoretical, practice and professional supports that other professional groups have when entering new settings and second due to the ways in which new informal psychosocial roles challenge organisational structures and pre-existing role boundaries through the newness and flexibility of their role [18, 60].

Roles were implemented in both hospital and community settings, working in physical health, mental health and social care, and often across the interfaces of care with a diverse array of titles, though a shared conceptualisation of the common duties listed, the most frequently cited being the development of a relationship or rapport with clients/patients [10, 21, 22, 43, 46, 49, 56, 59], followed by providing social [18, 22, 43, 47, 52, 54, 62], practical [10, 21, 44, 47, 54, 55, 59], and emotional support [18, 22, 43, 44, 59, 62]. These duties were mostly performed through task sharing. The duties identified are necessary components of professional holistic care [85] and core to most professional codes of practice, some might suggest that delegating these duties may represent a severing of the professional—patient/client care contract, with new role holders providing the informal psychosocial aspects of care, while nurses and other professionals provide the more advanced aspect of physical and psychosocial care designed to intervene and change a patient’s/client’s health issue [85]. However, as professionals are frequently overburdened to the degree that they are unable to work to the highest level and scope of practice [11], the implementation of these roles may be a necessary, if inelegant, solution to ensure patients/clients and families receive the care and support needed. Another perspective is that rather than representing a severing of care, these roles represent a formalising of informal support that is at times underpinned by knowledge based on lived experience, a form of support that is currently provided to some degree by support staff such as housekeeping staff via social interaction with the patient and their family [86], and which is under-recognised and under-valued. Given the many barriers identified in the integration of new informal roles, one could argue that the implementation of new roles into the current boundaried system of healthcare has not been formally supported by adaptations to policies and regulations in healthcare education and practice [87] or the promotion of interdisciplinary communication and teamwork [88].

Strengths and limitations of the review

The review has several strengths, including the use of a comprehensive multi-database search strategy, involvement of an information retrieval expert in the conduct of the search, dual-author data extraction and analysis, the use of the CIFR framework which enabled a rigorous analysis of the barriers and enablers and the thematic analysis which allowed for a deeper level of exploration and identification of the interrelationships among the many constructs coded within the CIFR domains. Despite these strengths it needs to be read with the following limitations in mind. First, the lack of studies focusing specifically on determinants increased the potential for interpretative bias, as many papers did not always clearly state something as a barrier or an enabler. Second, the difference in aims and objectives and the heterogeneity in role titles also made it a challenge to extract and compare data. The heterogeneity in role titles also may have resulted in studies that fitted our criteria not being recognised by the search criteria we used.

Implications

The review has several implications for researchers, and people tasked with implementing new informal roles. To ensure successful implementation and long term sustainability there is a need for a more systematic approach and greater attention to issues of role clarity and boundary working not only during the design phase of the role but also as an aspect of environmental preparedness, especially with members of the MDT. The provision of training specific to new roles, and support in the form of mentorship are necessary to develop the required skills and confidence in new role holders to work in differing healthcare settings.

The use of the CFIR framework not only facilitated the research team to identify determinants but it also helped point to gaps in knowledge. For example, data coded to the Outer Setting domain were infrequent, indicating a lack of consideration of the effect of policy, community or organisation on the implementation or sustainability of new informal roles. In addition, many constructs within other domains were not coded suggesting a need for more focused research in this area, possibly informed by CIFR or another implementation science framework. As few of the studies explored in any depth determinants of sustainability further research is also warranted into the barriers and enablers to sustainability. Given the challenges identified with boundary working there is a need for more in-depth studies to explore the working relationships between informal support workers and their professional colleagues so as to gain a greater understanding of the issues and concerns, as well as providing, as Saks [89] suggests, an evidence base for the regulation of these roles.

Conclusion

Determinants for the implementation, integration, and sustainability of new informal psychosocial roles included role boundary issues, the relationship between the role holder and their client/patient, environmental preparedness, and role holder characteristics. Role boundary issues resulting from low role clarity and role conflict were a significant barrier to the implementation, integration, and sustainability of new roles. These issues resulted from low engagement at the implementation stage, and at times the flexible design of roles. Low environmental preparedness in the form of a lack of support and education exacerbated these boundary issues. Inadequate education in the role may also have led to the difficulty some role holders had in maintaining professional boundaries with patients and clients and in seeking further education in order to progress in careers.

By contrast, tailored education, support, and mentorship enabled the implementation, integration, and sustainability of new roles. The role holders’ life experience and personal characteristics, the flexibility of their role, and having the opportunity to spend time with patients/clients improved the level of care provided through their improved relationships with patients and clients. This in turn improved role holder’s motivation, which increased role sustainability.

The heterogeneity of aims and objectives in the scoped studies reveals a lack of research focusing on the barriers and enablers to the implementation and sustainability of new informal psychosocial roles in multidisciplinary teams. As these roles assist in reducing the work burden of professional colleagues and promotes the horizontal integration of healthcare, further research on these determinants is needed if these roles are to be successfully implemented and sustained on a greater scale.
