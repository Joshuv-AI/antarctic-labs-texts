Prosocial helping behaviors attenuate accelerated cognitive decline driven by APOE ε4
Authors: Sae Hwang Han, Jessica Danielle Faul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThe apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele accelerates cognitive decline through multiple biological pathways—including neuroinflammatory, vascular, and stress‐regulatory mechanisms—yet few modifiable behaviors counter this genetic vulnerability. Prosocial helping engages these same systems in opposing directions, suggesting it may attenuate APOE ε4–related cognitive decline.METHODSWe analyzed more than two decades of longitudinal data from a large, multiethnic US sample (N = 17,972). Multilevel models that isolate within‐person change tested whether transitions in formal volunteering and informal helping moderate APOE ε4–related cognitive decline, while addressing key sources of endogeneity.RESULTSAdopting and sustaining formal volunteering attenuated the accelerated cognitive decline typically observed among ε4 carriers, with protective associations evident at modest engagement levels (≤ 4 hours/week). Informal helping was uniquely protective among Hispanic older adults, highlighting the role of cultural context in prosocial engagement.DISCUSSIONProsocial helping may be a feasible, scalable strategy for slowing APOE ε4–driven cognitive decline.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) and related dementias remain among the most pressing public health challenges of our time, with prevalence projected to nearly double in coming decades. 1 Decades of work have sought to identify the risk and protective factors that shape cognitive decline. 2 Among them, the apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele has emerged as the most consistently documented genetic risk factor for late‐onset AD. 3 Heterozygous carriers face substantially elevated risk; homozygous carriers experience markedly accelerated cognitive decline that becomes particularly evident in later life, often in their 70s, 3 and may represent a distinct genetic form of AD. 4 Critically, APOE ε4 confers risk not only through amyloid and tau pathology but through a broader set of biological vulnerabilities—vascular dysfunction, compromised blood–brain barrier integrity, heightened neuroinflammation, and dysregulated physiological stress reactivity—creating multiple points at which genetically driven decline might, in principle, be intercepted. 2 , 5

Despite the importance of these pathways for AD progression, few pharmacological or behavioral interventions reliably counteract the accelerated decline associated with APOE ε4. 2 , 5 Multidomain lifestyle programs have shown modest overall benefits in healthy older adults, 6 but evidence that these approaches are specifically effective for ε4 carriers—relative to non‐carriers—remains limited and inconsistent. 7 , 8 As a result, it remains unclear which behavioral exposures, if any, can engage the specific biological pathways through which APOE ε4 acts.

One promising candidate is engagement in prosocial helping behaviors. Formal volunteering through an organization and informal helping—assisting friends, neighbors, or non‐resident family—are among the most common activities in later life and have been independently linked to better cognitive function, slower cognitive decline, and reduced dementia risk. 9 , 10 Critically, these behaviors engage the same biological systems through which APOE ε4 acts—but in opposing directions: prosocial helping has been associated with healthier hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis regulation and reduced cortisol reactivity, 11 , 12 lower systemic inflammation, 13 , 14 and reduced cardiovascular risk, 15 including in evidence from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) and related cohorts. Emerging work also indicates that prosocial engagement may influence gene‐regulatory and epigenetic processes related to inflammation, immune function, and stress physiology, 16 , 17 suggesting that helping behaviors may modify the biological pathways implicated in genetically driven disease risk. Theoretical models of mammalian caregiving provide a coherent framework, positing that other‐focused helping activates neurohormonal systems evolved to support physiological regulation and downstream cognitive resilience. 18 , 19

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: We searched PubMed and PsycINFO through 2024 for longitudinal studies testing whether prosocial helping moderates genetic risk for Alzheimer's disease (AD) and cognitive decline. One Health and Retirement Study analysis reported that volunteering moderated decline associated with a polygenic index for AD among European‐ancestry adults; we identified no study formally testing moderation of apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 specifically or its generalizability across racial/ethnic groups. Interpretation: In a large, multiethnic US cohort, formal volunteering attenuated APOE ε4–related decline at modest engagement (≤ 4 hours/week); informal helping was uniquely protective among Hispanic older adults. Attenuation was specific to APOE ε4 rather than the broader polygenic risk for AD, consistent with prosocial helping engaging the inflammatory, vascular, and stress‐regulatory pathways through which ε4 accelerates cognitive decline. Future directions: Integrating biomarkers, neuroimaging, and physiological assessments will help clarify mechanisms, and culturally tailored interventions promoting prosocial helping in other racial/ethnic and global populations warrant investigation.

Together, this convergence—APOE ε4 accelerating decline through stress, vascular, and inflammatory pathways, and prosocial helping engaging those same pathways in opposing directions—suggests that helping behaviors may attenuate genetically driven cognitive decline, particularly among ε4 carriers. An earlier HRS study found that volunteering moderated cognitive decline associated with a polygenic index for AD (PGI–AD) among older adults of European ancestry, 20 yet whether these protective associations are specific to APOE ε4 or reflect broader polygenic influences, whether they extend to informal helping—the most common form of prosocial engagement in many communities, 21 and whether they generalize to racially and ethnically diverse populations remain open questions.

Here, we use more than two decades of biennial longitudinal data from the HRS (1998–2020) to test whether transitions into and out of formal volunteering and informal helping moderate APOE ε4–related cognitive decline among middle‐aged and older adults in a large, multiethnic US sample. Because cultural context and structural opportunity shape how prosocial engagement is enacted across communities, we also test whether protective associations vary across non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, and Hispanic older adults—populations that experience differential dementia risk yet remain underrepresented in genetic aging research. 22 Our analytic framework—within–between random effects models with asymmetric coding of helping transitions and lagged covariates—isolates within‐person change and addresses several sources of endogeneity common to observational gene–environment research. 23 , 24

METHODS

Study design and sample

We used data from the HRS, a nationally representative biennial panel study of US adults aged ≥ 51 years conducted by the Survey Research Center at the University of Michigan. 25 The HRS began in 1992 with a representative sample of adults aged 51 to 61, and subsequently added younger and older birth cohorts to maintain and expand age representation. By 1998, the HRS core sample was nationally representative of US households with at least one adult aged ≥ 51; age‐ineligible spouses and partners of sampled respondents were also interviewed and are retained in the HRS. To improve the precision of subgroup estimates, Blacks and Hispanics were oversampled at approximately twice the rate of Whites throughout the study period. 25

The current study used 12 waves of HRS data spanning 1998 to 2020. Beginning in 2006, the HRS Enhanced Face‐to‐Face (EFTF) interview included collection of salivary DNA samples on a rotating half‐sample basis (one half collected in 2006 with follow‐up in 2010; the other half in 2008 with follow‐up in 2012). 25 Because the analysis required APOE genotype, the analytic sample was limited to respondents who provided salivary DNA between 2006 and 2012. Of 35,082 age‐eligible non‐proxy respondents interviewed between 1998 and 2020, 18,771 provided genetic data through the EFTF protocol; as a result, individuals who died before 2006 or entered the study in birth cohorts added after 2012 were excluded. We further excluded a small group of respondents identifying as non‐Hispanic “other” (n = 551); those with missing covariate data (n = 3); and, following recommendations in the longitudinal modeling literature, 26 singleton observations (n = 245). The final analytic sample comprised 17,972 individuals contributing 139,777 person‐wave observations (see Appendix Table SA in supporting information for the full participant flow). Because genotyping was limited to respondents who provided DNA during the 2006 to 2012 enhanced interviews, the analytic sample is a selected subset of the HRS core sample; accordingly, we use the term “nationally representative” to describe the source HRS data rather than the present analytic sample or its estimates.

The HRS protocol was approved by the Health Sciences and Behavioral Sciences Institutional Review Board at the University of Michigan, and all participants provided written informed consent. The present secondary analysis was reviewed by the institutional review board (IRB) at The University of Texas at Austin and determined to be exempt (45 CFR 46.104(d)(4); IRB ID: STUDY00005047). To address heterogeneity in disease risk and at‐risk populations, all analyses include the multiethnic HRS sample (non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, and Hispanic adults), and a pre‐specified set of analyses examined effect modification by race/ethnicity (see section 2.6).

Cognitive function

Cognitive function was assessed at each wave using the modified Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (mTICS), 27 , 28 which comprises a 10‐word immediate recall and 10‐word delayed recall task (combined range 0–20) measuring episodic memory, serial 7s subtraction (range 0–5) measuring working memory, and backward counting from 20 (range 0–2) measuring processing speed; total scores range from 0 to 27, with higher values indicating better cognitive performance. We used HRS‐imputed cognitive scores to minimize item‐level missingness.

Genetic factors

APOE ε4 status was determined from two single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs; rs7412 and rs429358) that together define the three major APOE isoforms (ε2, ε3, ε4). 29 Carriers of one or more ε4 alleles are at elevated risk for AD compared to non‐carriers. APOE ε4 was modeled primarily as a continuous allele count (0, 1, or 2) for parsimony in higher order interactions; a categorical specification was evaluated in sensitivity analyses. Genotyping platform, quality‐control procedures, ancestry assignment, and details on the small subset with imputed APOE genotypes are provided in Supplementary Text A in supporting information.

We additionally examined the PGI–AD, released by the HRS, 30 constructed from a 2022 genome‐wide association meta‐analysis by the European Alzheimer & Dementia Biobank consortium. 31 We used the genome‐wide PGI with P value threshold of 1.0 and excluded the APOE/TOMM40 region to avoid redundancy with APOE ε4, consistent with prior work demonstrating the importance of treating the APOE locus separately when modeling polygenic risk for AD in HRS. 32 The HRS‐released score incorporated 1,507,529 SNPs in the European‐ancestry sample, 1,507,369 in the African‐ancestry sample, and 1,507,711 in the Hispanic‐ancestry sample, and was standardized within each ancestry group (mean 0, standard deviation 1). Because PGI predictive validity differs across ancestral populations due to differences in linkage disequilibrium and allele frequency, PGI–AD analyses were ancestry stratified and adjusted for 10 ancestry‐specific principal components (Supplementary Text B in supporting information).

Helping behaviors

Two forms of prosocial helping were assessed at each wave. Formal volunteering was measured by the question: “Have you spent any time in the past 12 months doing volunteer work for religious, educational, health‐related or other charitable organizations?” Informal helping was measured by: “Did you spend any time in the past 12 months helping friends, neighbors, or relatives who did not live with you and did not pay you for the help?” Status responses were coded dichotomously (1 = yes; 0 = no). Participants who reported engagement were asked about annual hours of involvement, from which we constructed a four‐category time‐commitment variable (0 = no helping; 1 = 1–99 hours; 2 = 100–199 hours; 3 = ≥ 200 hours), modeled categorically to allow detection of threshold or non‐linear associations. 33 , 34

Covariates

Guided by prior research on cognitive aging and prosocial engagement, we controlled for sociodemographic and health characteristics that could confound the key estimates (see Appendix Table SB in supporting information for variable definitions and coding). Time‐varying covariates, lagged one wave to ensure temporal precedence relative to helping transitions, included marital status (married/partnered vs. not), labor force status (not working/part time/full time), household wealth (assets minus debts in US$1000s, inverse hyperbolic sine‐transformed), activities of daily living limitations (range 0–5), and depressive symptoms (eight‐item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale, range 0–8). Time‐invariant covariates measured at baseline included age (range 51–74 years), sex (self‐reported as female or male), race/ethnicity (non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, Hispanic), and education in years (range 0–17). In sensitivity analyses, we additionally adjusted for a broader set of health behaviors and conditions (see Supplementary Text C in supporting information).

Statistical analysis

The research questions were addressed using multilevel models, with repeated person‐wave observations (level 1) nested within individuals (level 2). We used the within‐between random effects (WBRE) framework to identify the potential protective effects of helping behaviors against the accelerated cognitive decline associated with genetic risk factors, while accounting for several sources of bias and endogeneity. In this framework, all time‐varying predictors—including interaction terms involving them—were decomposed into between‐person components (person means) and within‐person components (deviations from each individual's mean at each wave). 23 , 35 This decomposition yields within‐person estimates that are independent of selection effects attributable to all stable inter‐individual differences, observed or unobserved. Importantly, it ensures that within‐person components of time‐varying variables, including helping behaviors, are orthogonal to stable genetic factors (e.g., APOE ε4 and PGI–AD), thereby mitigating concerns about biased estimates in models testing gene–environment interactions. 24 , 26 Full conceptual treatment of WBRE as applied to gene–environment interaction testing is provided in Supplementary Text D in supporting information.

As a preliminary step, we fit unconditional growth models to establish baseline trajectories of cognitive function before adding substantive covariates, ensuring the mean structure was correctly specified and minimizing bias in subsequent predictor estimates. 36 Linear and quadratic terms for time since baseline (in 2‐year units) captured within‐person change, while corresponding terms for baseline age—along with their interaction—distinguished cross‐sectional age differences from longitudinal change, an important distinction in accelerated designs such as the HRS in which treating age as the sole clock conflates the two processes and biases estimates. 37 We also specified random slopes for time to capture individual variability in rates of change, and included the intercept–slope covariance, which is commonly observed in longitudinal studies using cognitive measures such as the mTICS. A model comparison underlying the selected growth structure is provided in Supplementary Text E and Table S2 in supporting information.

Building on the unconditional model, we tested whether APOE ε4 predicted individual differences in the rate of cognitive decline using an interaction approach (time × APOE ε4), modeling APOE ε4 first as a continuous allele count and then as a categorical variable. APOE ε4 was included for both the full sample and ancestry‐stratified subsamples, given its well established and robust effects across ancestral backgrounds. The PGI–AD was evaluated only in ancestry‐stratified analyses (non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, and Hispanic participants), consistent with best practices for PGI research that address concerns about population stratification and the reduced predictive validity of polygenic scores across diverse ancestral groups. Each stratified model included 10 ancestry‐specific principal components derived from genome‐wide genotype data. 30

The main research question examined whether engagement in prosocial helping behaviors moderated the accelerated cognitive decline associated with APOE ε4. Helping behaviors at each wave were captured using an asymmetric effects approach that relaxes the common assumption that transitions into and out of helping roles—as well as increases and reductions in time commitment—have effects of equal magnitude on cognitive function, allowing effects to vary by the direction of behavioral change. Accordingly, we tested time‐varying indicators for both status transitions (acquisition vs. withdrawal from helping roles) and time‐commitment changes (e.g., from low to high hours, or vice versa). 33 , 38 Full details of the asymmetric coding scheme are provided in Supplementary Text F in supporting information. Formal volunteering and informal helping were analyzed in separate models, with the other helping behavior included as a lagged covariate to adjust for cross‐domain spillover. We also included the lagged cognitive outcome to address feedback effects (i.e., prior cognition influencing subsequent helping) and to yield more conservative estimates of the effects of helping behaviors. 39

To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses. First, because APOE ε4's effects on cognition typically emerge or intensify in advanced age, 3 , 40 we re‐estimated models in a subsample restricted to participants born in or before 1950—those who reached at least their 70s during follow‐up. Second, we tested whether protective associations varied by racial/ethnic background, recognizing that the cultural relevance of, and barriers to, formal volunteering and informal helping differ across non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, and Hispanic adults. 21 Third, we tested alternative APOE ε4 operationalizations, including a categorical specification (0, 1, or 2 alleles) and exclusion of ε2 carriers (n = 2327) to isolate the comparison between common (ε3/ε3) and risk‐conferring (ε3/ε4 or ε4/ε4) genotypes. 2 , 3 Fourth, to address potential time‐varying shocks coinciding with helping transitions, we re‐estimated the models predicting cognitive function at the subsequent wave (t + 1) from helping transitions occurring between t 1 and t, increasing temporal separation between exposure and outcome at the cost of statistical power. 39

All models were estimated with full‐information maximum likelihood using the MIXED procedure in Stata 18 (StataCorp LP). Primary analyses were unweighted, as within‐person estimation does not require sampling weights for consistency and the HRS weighting design does not target the genotyped subsample (Supplementary Text G in supporting information).

RESULTS

Sample characteristics

Sample characteristics are presented in Table 1. The sample comprised 17,972 participants, of whom 57.7% were female and 69.2% non‐Hispanic White, 18.1% non‐Hispanic Black, and 12.7% Hispanic. Across the observation period, participants scored an average of 15.3 out of 27 on the mTICS. With respect to genetic risk, 72.5% of participants did not carry the APOE ε4 allele, 25.2% carried one copy, and 2.3% carried two copies. Engagement in helping behaviors was common: formal volunteering and informal helping were reported in ≈ 36% and 54% of all person‐wave observations, respectively. Both forms of helping exhibited substantial change over time, with 9552 transitions into formal volunteering and 11,718 transitions out, and 14,398 transitions into informal helping and 19,336 transitions out. Time‐commitment categories shifted across waves as well, with frequent movement among the four dose categories for both helping types (Table S1). Participants contributed an average of 7.8 person‐wave observations during the 12‐wave observation window (1998–2020).

APOE ε4 and cognitive decline

In all multilevel models, we decomposed within‐person change from stable between‐person differences, ensuring that estimated effects of time‐varying characteristics—including helping behaviors and their interactions with genetic risk—were not confounded by stable individual traits. As a preliminary step, unconditional growth models confirmed that cognitive function declined at an accelerating rate over the observation period and that random slopes for time were substantial, indicating meaningful between‐person variability in rates of decline (see also Table S2 in supporting information).

Building on these unconditional models, we tested whether APOE ε4 predicted individual differences in the rate of cognitive decline using an interaction approach (time × APOE ε4). APOE ε4 was significantly associated with faster cognitive decline (Table 2). Modeled continuously (Model 2A), each additional ε4 allele was associated with a 0.096 mTICS‐unit faster linear decline per 2‐year wave (95% confidence interval [CI] –0.111 to –0.082; P < 0.001) and a 0.010‐unit accelerating quadratic decline (95% CI –0.013 to –0.007; P < 0.001). The categorical specification (Model 2B) confirmed a dose–response pattern: per 2‐year wave, carriers of two alleles experienced 0.240‐unit faster linear decline (95% CI –0.291 to –0.190) compared to non‐carriers, versus 0.086‐unit faster decline (95% CI –0.103 to –0.069) among single‐allele carriers (Figure 1). Taken together, these findings confirm that APOE ε4 carriers—particularly homozygotes—experience faster rates of cognitive decline over time. Because both specifications yielded comparable conclusions, main analyses used the continuous specification, which provided a more parsimonious model and greater efficiency for estimating higher order interactions. In ancestry‐stratified analyses, PGI–AD was also associated with faster decline among European‐ancestry participants, though with smaller effect sizes; effects were weaker and less consistent among Black and Hispanic participants (Table S3 in supporting information).

Because protective associations may depend on the intensity of engagement, we next examined whether transitions across specific dose levels yielded distinct effects. Dose–response analyses (Model 3B) indicated that both low‐dose (0 → 1–99 hours: b = 0.051, 95% CI 0.014 to 0.088; P = 0.007) and moderate‐dose (0 → 100–199 hours: b = 0.076, 95% CI 0.007 to 0.146; P = 0.032) increases were associated with slower ε4‐related decline. Reducing participation from low to no volunteering was associated with steeper ε4‐related decline (1–99 → 0 hours: b = ‐0.040, 95% CI –0.074 to –0.005; P = 0.028). High‐dose transitions (0 → ≥ 200 hours) were not significantly associated with ε4‐related decline, consistent with prior evidence that excessive helping may generate role strain that offsets cognitive benefit. For informal helping in the full sample, status transitions and dose changes were directionally consistent with expectations but did not reach statistical significance (Table 3, Models 4A–B).

Sensitivity analyses

Cohort composition

Because the cognitive consequences of APOE ε4 are known to become increasingly pronounced in later life, we assessed the sensitivity of the main findings by re‐estimating models using a subsample restricted to participants born before 1950—those who reached at least their 70s during the observation period. Results were consistent with the main analyses (Table S5 in supporting information). The detrimental effect of APOE ε4 on cognitive decline remained robust, with slightly larger coefficients for the time × ε4 interaction (b = −0.112, P < 0.001), suggesting that APOE‐related effects were, as expected, more pronounced at older ages. The moderating effects of volunteering on APOE‐driven decline were nearly identical in magnitude and significance, indicating that the protective role of helping persisted when restricted to older cohorts.

Racial/ethnic heterogeneity

To evaluate whether the protective associations of helping generalized across non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, and Hispanic older adults, we adopted an interaction‐based approach (race/ethnicity × time × APOE ε4 × helping transition) as the principal test of heterogeneity rather than fitting fully stratified models for each group. An interaction‐based approach permits direct statistical comparison of moderating effects across groups within a single unified framework, preserves statistical power by retaining the full sample, and avoids comparability issues—including unequal precision and subtle differences in covariate effects—that arise when separate models are estimated for each subsample. 41 Ancestry‐stratified models were used selectively for PGI–AD analyses, consistent with established best practices for polygenic risk score research in diverse populations.

For formal volunteering, the four‐way interaction terms were not statistically significant (Table S6 in supporting information), suggesting that the cognitive benefit of acquiring a volunteer role operated similarly across the three groups. For informal helping, by contrast, protective associations were significantly stronger among Hispanic adults than among non‐Hispanic White adults. Analyses restricted to the Hispanic subsample (Table 4 and Figure S1 in supporting information) showed that acquiring an informal helping role was associated with slower ε4‐related decline (b = 0.109, 95% CI 0.003 to 0.215; P = 0.043), driven primarily by low‐dose engagement (0 → 1‐99 hours: b = 0.128, 95% CI 0.012 to 0.244; P = 0.031) and most pronounced among those born before 1950 (b = 0.170, 95% CI 0.049 to 0.291; P = 0.007). The Hispanic subsample is the smallest of the three groups, which contributed to wider CIs; nonetheless, the direction and magnitude of estimates were consistent across model specifications, supporting a robust protective association between transitions into informal helping and slower APOE ε4–related cognitive decline. This subgroup‐specific pattern points to a distinct route through which informal helping may engage protective biology in cultural contexts in which prosocial activity is heavily concentrated in family‐ and community‐based exchange.

Alternative operationalizations of APOE genotype

Two complementary sensitivity analyses examined robustness to APOE operationalization. First, treating APOE ε4 as a categorical variable (0, 1, or 2 alleles) rather than a continuous allele count produced results highly consistent with the main analyses (Table S7 in supporting information), showing the expected dose–response pattern in which ε4 homozygotes experienced the steepest cognitive decline and transitions into helping roles continued to buffer ε4‐related decline, with the largest moderating estimates among individuals carrying two ε4 alleles. Second, because the APOE ε2 allele is associated with protection against cognitive decline, we re‐estimated all models after excluding ε2/ε2 homozygotes (n = 120) and ε2/ε3 heterozygotes (n = 2207) to isolate the comparison between the common (ε3/ε3) and risk‐conferring (ε3/ε4 or ε4/ε4) genotypes. The overall pattern of results remained unchanged, with comparable effect sizes for APOE ε4 and for the moderating role of helping behaviors (Table S8 in supporting information). Together, these analyses confirm that the observed protective associations are robust to how genetic risk is parameterized and are not driven by the inclusion or exclusion of the protective ε2 allele.

Longer exposure–outcome interval

Finally, to strengthen temporal ordering and address potential time‐varying shocks coinciding with helping transitions, we re‐estimated models predicting cognitive function at the subsequent wave (t + 1) from helping transitions occurring between t – 1 and t. This specification increases temporal separation between exposure and outcome at the cost of statistical power. Results for formal volunteering remained consistent (b = 0.043, 95% CI 0.003 to 0.082; P = 0.026); notably, informal helping associations now reached statistical significance in the full sample (b = 0.041, 95% CI 0.007 to 0.074; P = 0.017), again driven primarily by Hispanic participants (Tables S9–S10 in supporting information). This pattern argues against reverse causation as a sole explanation of the observed associations and suggests that protective effects of informal helping may operate with some delay that becomes more clearly detectable as temporal separation is extended.

A further series of analyses—adjusting for an expanded set of covariates and applying survey weights—yielded a pattern of results consistent with the main findings (Tables S11–S12 in supporting information).

DISCUSSION

In this longitudinal analysis of 17,972 middle‐aged and older adults followed across 12 biennial waves of the HRS, transitioning into formal volunteering was associated with significantly slower APOE ε4–related cognitive decline, with protective associations evident at modest engagement levels (no more than ≈ 4 hours per week). Withdrawing from volunteering was associated with a steeper APOE ε4–related decline. Formal volunteering showed robust protective patterns in the full sample, while informal helping showed parallel benefits uniquely among Hispanic older adults—underscoring the importance of cultural context in shaping the cognitive benefits of prosocial engagement. The protective association was specific to APOE ε4 rather than the broader polygenic architecture of AD (PGI–AD). Taken together, these findings extend prior work that has separately linked helping behaviors to better cognitive outcomes 9 , 42 and APOE ε4 to faster decline, 3 , 4 demonstrating in a large, multiethnic cohort that prosocial engagement may attenuate genetically driven cognitive decline.

Several features of the data and analytic design merit emphasis. The HRS provided more than two decades of repeated measurements on a large, multiethnic cohort followed well into late adulthood—an unusually rich basis for characterizing both the average course of cognitive decline and the genetic and behavioral factors that shape substantial individual differences in its rate. 25 , 41 , 43 Building on this foundation, the within–between random effects specification allowed us to model inter‐individual differences in the rate of decline—driven in part by APOE genotype—while rendering the within‐person components of helping behaviors orthogonal to stable genetic factors, thereby mitigating confounding by unobserved traits and strengthening causal interpretation. 23 , 24 We further used an asymmetric coding strategy distinguishing transitions into and out of helping roles and changes in dose. 38 In combination with the within–between decomposition, this approach effectively uses each individual as their own control, estimating how changes in helping correspond to departures—both gains and losses—from each participant's expected cognitive trajectory over time. Collectively, these design and analytic approaches, which closely align with the underlying data‐generating mechanism, provide a sound foundation for interpreting the key findings observed in this study.

Against this methodological backdrop, several aspects of the findings are particularly noteworthy in light of growing neurobiological and behavioral evidence. Although APOE ε4 is most widely recognized for its role in amyloid deposition and tau‐related neurodegeneration, emerging research highlights its broader consequences for vascular integrity, neuroinflammation, and physiological stress regulation, 2 , 5 pathways implicated in the downstream processes through which cognitive decline accelerates in later life. Importantly, prosocial helping behaviors influence many of these systems in the opposing direction. Experimental and longitudinal studies show that helping others supports healthier HPA axis regulation and reduced cortisol reactivity, 11 , 12 lower systemic inflammation, 13 , 14 reduced incident cardiovascular disease, 15 and beneficial gene‐regulatory and epigenetic changes, 16 , 17 often in evidence drawn from the same HRS population examined here. These patterns align with a neurobiological account of caregiving, which holds that other‐focused helping engages neurohormonal systems that support caregiving and social bonding. 18 Central to this model is the medial preoptic area of the hypothalamus which, modulated by oxytocin and progesterone, downregulates the physiological stress response by dampening sympathetic and HPA reactivity and accommodating recovery after a stressor. 18 Human neuroimaging work offers converging evidence, indicating that giving support to others engages ventral striatal and septal regions that are, in turn, associated with attenuated amygdala reactivity, blunted sympathetic responses, and lower systemic inflammation. 12 , 13 , 19 That is, helping may act less through what the helper receives than through the stress‐regulatory machinery it sets in motion. This distinction has implications for genetically driven decline: sustained activation of the stress response is thought to blunt sensitivity to cortisol, thereby impairing the regulation of immune activity and promoting the chronic inflammation implicated in the pathogenesis of AD. 18 Seen in this light, the specificity of the observed interaction to APOE ε4—rather than the broader PGI–AD—is itself informative: it is consistent with a pathway‐specific process, in which helping behaviors engage the neuroinflammatory, vascular, and stress‐regulatory pathways through which ε4 operates, rather than the polygenic architecture underlying general AD risk.

The pattern of effects across helping types and racial/ethnic subgroups also warrants discussion. Formal volunteering demonstrated robust protective associations across the full sample, consistent with a large body of work linking volunteer roles to better cognitive outcomes, slower decline, and lower dementia risk. 9 , 44 Older adults frequently volunteer in intergenerational programs, mentoring roles, and health‐care or long‐term‐care settings—contexts that involve emotionally meaningful, cognitively engaging interactions, often requiring other‐focused compassion and communication. Such experiences are well positioned to activate the neurobiological systems described above, providing protection against APOE‐related risks. What likely matters more, in this context, may be these interaction‐based, emotionally significant aspects of helping through volunteering—rather than the presence of a formal organizational structure per se.

A complementary pattern emerged for informal helping, which demonstrated a unique protective effect among Hispanic older adults—a finding that reinforces the relational and emotional dimensions of helping while also underscoring the importance of cultural context. Earlier work on volunteerism among Hispanics/Latinos shows that much prosocial activity occurs outside formal organizations and is rooted in strong values of familism, reciprocity, and collective responsibility, which place the needs of others above one's own and make helping community members a meaningful expression of identity and connection. 21 Informal helping in these settings—often characterized as performed de corazón (“from the heart”)—shares the emotionally engaged qualities of formal volunteering described above, and may activate the same psychosocial and neurobiological pathways that protect against APOE‐related risks. This cultural context may explain why informal helping emerged as uniquely protective in this group. Taken together, the findings support the broader interpretation that any form of prosocial engagement—formal or informal—may confer cognitive benefit when it involves meaningful, sustained human interaction. Interventions aimed at individuals at elevated genetic risk should therefore consider opportunities that facilitate emotionally engaging, socially connected forms of helping, regardless of whether they occur within organizational structures or in naturally occurring community settings.

Another implication for practice and intervention is that the timing and amount of prosocial engagement appear to matter for individuals at elevated genetic risk. The finding that helping transitions moderate APOE ε4–related decline suggests that the steeper trajectories typically observed among ε4 carriers may slow when they engage in helping roles—and accelerate again when they withdraw. The cognitive benefits of sustained engagement thus accumulate over time, producing increasingly pronounced differences as individuals age (Figure 2). Such cumulative effects underscore why adopting helping roles earlier in adulthood—and maintaining them consistently—may be particularly advantageous for slowing decline well before cognitive impairment becomes clinically detectable. 45 Notably, and consistent with prior research suggesting that excessive helping can generate role strain that undermines health, 46 attenuation of accelerated decline did not require high‐intensity engagement: modest commitments of no more than ≈ 4 hours per week were sufficient to produce meaningful differences in cognitive trajectories. This dose pattern is broadly consistent with an independent HRS analysis reporting that lower intensity volunteering was most beneficial among ε4 carriers. 47

The study findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, helping was assessed using relatively coarse items that capture participation status and approximate annual hours but not interpersonal quality, emotional meaning, or specific activities; future studies incorporating richer assessments of the nature and context of helping would help isolate the active components of prosocial engagement that matter most. Second, although the WBRE framework reduces concerns about confounding by stable individual differences, residual bias from unmeasured time‐varying factors cannot be fully excluded; for such factors to account for the observed pattern, they would need to coincide with helping transitions and simultaneously influence cognitive change in a way that differs by APOE ε4 status—a scenario difficult to reconcile with the consistency of findings across sensitivity analyses. 48 Intervention studies and quasi‐experimental designs that structure helping opportunities, such as the Experience Corps trial, 49 would provide stronger causal evidence. Third, although the asymmetric coding strategy and lagged covariate structure mitigate concerns about reverse causation, declining cognitive function may itself reduce engagement in helping behaviors; the consistency of findings under longer exposure–outcome intervals argues against reverse causation as a sole explanation but does not eliminate it. Fourth, the mTICS is a validated screening instrument with limited sensitivity at the upper end of the cognitive distribution, 27 which may have attenuated observed associations, and selective attrition as impairment progresses could further bias estimates toward the null. Finally, replication in independent cohorts is needed, and generalizability beyond the US context warrants investigation.

In this large, multiethnic cohort followed for more than two decades, prosocial helping behaviors were associated with attenuated APOE ε4–related cognitive decline. Formal volunteering showed robust protective associations across the full sample, whereas informal helping showed parallel benefits among Hispanic older adults. Protection emerged at modest levels of engagement, consistent with the interpretation that sustained, interpersonally meaningful helping may represent an accessible behavioral strategy for slowing cognitive decline among individuals at elevated genetic risk for AD. Future work integrating biomarkers, neuroimaging, and physiological assessments will be important for clarifying the mechanisms and parameters underlying these associations.
