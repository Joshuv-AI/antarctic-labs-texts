Transomic analysis reveals DNA methylation and transcription factor roles in obese liver protein expression
Authors: Hideki Maehara, Atsushi Hatano, Masaki Shirai, Toshiya Kokaji, Yutaka Suzuki, Masaki Matsumoto, Riku Egami, Hiroyuki Kubota, Hiromitsu Araki, Fumihito Miura, Takashi Ito, Shinya Kuroda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Obesity impairs hepatic functions through abnormal functional protein expression, potentially through DNA methylation, which suppresses gene expression, and changes in transcription factors (TFs) expression. However, the specific protein expression changes associated with DNA methylation in the obese liver remain unclear. To dissect the relative association of DNA methylome and TF-binding with protein expression changes in the obese liver, we used a trans-omic integration approach combining DNA methylome, transcriptome, proteome, and TF-binding data for the livers of wild-type (WT) and obese (ob/ob) mice. We found that gene and protein expression changes were more strongly associated with TF expression changes than with changes in DNA methylation in promoter region. However, decreased protein expression of the complement and coagulation system in obesity was specifically associated with increased DNA methylation together with decreased expression of TF Hnf4a. Our study highlights abnormal protein expression specifically associated with DNA methylation and TF expression changes in obesity.

Introduction

Obesity is a major metabolic disorder linked to numerous metabolic diseases, including type 2 diabetes1,2. A comprehensive understanding of the pathogenesis of obesity is critical for the prevention and treatment of metabolic conditions. The liver, a major organ of metabolism, shows widespread alterations in gene and protein expression3. These changes stem largely from transcriptional regulation and post-transcriptional events such as altered translation efficiency and protein turnover. Indeed, pronounced shifts in the abundance of metabolic enzymes have been linked primarily to transcriptional regulation3. Furthermore, transcriptional changes can also be caused by multiple factors, including changes in the epigenome and transcription factor (TF) binding. The extent to which these factors affect obesity, their specific roles, and their associations with abnormalities remain unclear and warrant further investigation. To clarify the role of epigenome and TF, we applied a trans-omic integration of multi-omics data including epigenomic data and TF-binding data, which allows a systematic dissection of how these regulatory layers converge to shape protein expression changes in obesity.

Chronic metabolic changes in the liver caused by obesity may result from long-term dysregulation of the epigenome. Epigenomic mechanisms, which modify gene expression without altering the underlying DNA sequence, also contribute to these transcriptional changes. Among them, DNA methylation—particularly near the transcription start site—is generally associated with gene repression4. Changes in DNA methylation can therefore underlie some of the observed gene and protein expression differences. For example, organ-dominant protein expression has been associated with organ-dominant DNA hypomethylation5. In the context of obesity, DNA methylation changes have been reported in the livers of obese humans6, as well as in mice, such as the New Zealand obese mouse model7. These previous studies have investigated specific molecules with DNA methylation changes in detail. However, the genome-scale characteristics of gene and protein expression changes that are associated with changes in DNA methylation are not clearly understood. Recent work8 performed whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) together with RNA-seq in a high-fructose/high-cholesterol (HFHC) diet-induced mouse model of non-alcoholic steatohepatitis. However, protein-level consequences of the methylation changes and the contribution of TF occupancy were not examined. Our trans-omic study addresses these gaps by integrating liver DNA methylome, transcriptome, proteome, and public ChIP-seq data in the genetic ob/ob obesity model, thereby enabling a comprehensive evaluation of how DNA methylation and TF regulation converge on protein expression.

Protein expression changes in the obese mouse liver have been attributed to various factors, including increased expression of enzymes involved in lipid and carbohydrate metabolism3,9 and decreased expression of proteins in the complement and coagulation systems, which play crucial roles in immunity3. It remains to be clarified whether these changes in expression associate with DNA methylation changes of protein-coding genes or with other factors, such as changes in the expression of transcription factors. We have previously measured the transcriptome, proteome, and metabolome of the liver of wild-type (WT) mice and ob/ob mice, a mouse model of obesity with deficiency of leptin10, and analyzed the relationship between these omics, which we call trans-omic analysis11–13. In one of our trans-omic study11, we analyzed the association of gene and protein expression changes in the liver of wild-type and ob/ob mice with TF-binding motif information14. In this study, we newly measured the DNA methylome of extracted hepatocytes from WT and ob/ob mice and identified changes in DNA methylation near the transcription start site (TSS) in WT and ob/ob mice. By integrating these results with trans-omic analysis in a previous study that examined the transcriptome and proteome changes11, we identified gene and protein expression changes associated with DNA methylation changes. Furthermore, we re-analyzed the expression changes and binding states of transcription factors using data obtained from the database ChIP-Atlas15,16 to identify expression changes of genes/proteins associated with transcription factor binding and compared their characteristics with DNA methylation. Using these integrated analyses, we uncovered specific pathways of abnormal gene and protein expression associated with DNA methylation and TF-binding changes in obesity, highlighting their relationships with DNA methylation and transcription factor activity in hepatic pathological changes in obesity.

Results

Overview of this study

In this study, we aim to understand which gene and protein expression changes in obesity are associated with changes in DNA methylation and TF-binding (Fig. 1). We obtained liver samples from WT and ob/ob mice. We measured the DNA methylome in hepatocytes from the liver, and integrated it with the transcriptome and proteome data from our previous studies11 and TF binding information from ChIP-Atlas15. We identified obesity-associated differences in DNA methylation, gene expression, and protein expression. Furthermore, we identified differences in gene and protein expression associated with differences in DNA methylation and TF expression and investigated the pathways in which these differences become important. Note that we measured the DNA methylome by WGBS using isolated hepatocytes. Our previous study17 demonstrated a high correlation (r = 0.960) between DNA methylation in the whole liver and that in isolated hepatocytes. Association of DNA hypomethylation in promoter and first exon regions with gene expression

In our previous study17, we found the negative correlation of DNA methylation ratios from 200 bp upstream to 400 bp downstream of the transcription start site with gene expression. In this study, we similarly investigated the correlation between DNA methylation and gene expression in WT and ob/ob mouse liver data (Supplementary Fig. 1). Consistent with our previous findings17, we observed strong negative correlation between gene expression and DNA methylation in the promoter, first exon, and first intron regions in the liver of ob/ob mice (Supplementary Fig. 1a). We examined this correlation in a 100 bp region around the TSS (Supplementary Fig. 1b). In this study, we also observed a strong negative correlation in the region from 200 bp upstream to 400 bp downstream, and the correlation coefficient increased monotonically outside of this region. This region is common to regions used in our previous study17 and confirmed to be associated with protein expression. Therefore, in this study, we used the DNA methylation ratio from 200 bp upstream to 400 bp downstream of the TSS as the gene’s methylation ratio. Notably, the strength and pattern of the negative correlation between promoter methylation and gene expression were similar in WT and ob/ob mice (Supplementary Fig. 1), suggesting that the general repressive role of promoter methylation is preserved18 even under obese conditions.

Identification of Differentially Methylated Genes (DMGs), Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs), Differentially Expressed Proteins (DEPs), and their association

We identified Differentially Methylated Genes (DMGs), Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs), and Differentially Expressed Proteins (DEPs). We used data from our previous study11 and re-identified the DEG using a different threshold for significance. We also re-identified the DEP using methodology from another previous study of DNA methylation17 (see Methods). We identified Differentially Methylated CpGs (DMCpGs) by RADMeth19, and identified genes with DMCpGs from 200 bp upstream to 400 bp downstream of the TSS as DMGs (Fig. 2a). We identified genes that were more expressed in either WT or ob/ob than the other as DEGs (see Methods, Fig. 2b). Among the 12,853 expressed genes, 2490 (20%) were expressed more in WT (referred to as WT-dominant DEGs) and 2941 (23%) were expressed more in ob/ob (ob/ob-dominant DEGs). Of the 2651 proteins detected in either WT or ob/ob, 592 (22%) were significantly higher expressed in WT than ob/ob, and 98 (4%) were detected only in WT (Fig. 2c). Together, these are referred to as WT-dominant DEPs. The 84 (3%) proteins highly expressed in ob/ob and 560 (21%) proteins detected only in ob/ob are referred to as ob/ob-dominant DEPs.

Although the number of DMGs (Fig. 2a) is small, this result is consistent with the modest difference in the average DNA methylation ratio across the entire genes, with WT at 0.4604 and ob/ob at 0.4574 (Welch’s t-test: p = 0.048) (Supplementary Fig. 2a), indicating that obesity-associated methylation change is largely localized rather than global.

As we confirmed in the previous section, DNA hypomethylation can be associated with high gene expression (Supplementary Fig. 1). In addition, gene expression can be associated with protein expression. Therefore, we examined the overlap among DMGs, DEGs, and DEPs (Fig. 2d, e). For WT-dominance, we found that out of the 2490 WT-dominant DEGs and 1035 expressed WT-dominant DMGs, 286 genes (11% of DEGs and 28% of DMGs) overlapped between DEGs and DMGs (Fig. 2d). We refer to these overlapping DMGs and DEGs as differentially hypomethylated DEGs (DM-DEGs). We investigated the overlap between DM-DEGs and DEPs: 30 of the 690 WT-dominant DEPs (10% of DM-DEGs and 4% of DEPs) overlapped with DM-DEGs (Fig. 2d). Henceforth, DEPs encoded by DM-DEGs are referred to as DM-DEPs. The result indicates that WT-dominant DM-DEPs share a small percentage (4%) of WT-dominant DEPs. Similarly, for ob/ob-dominance, out of the 2,941 ob/ob-dominant DEGs and 971 expressed ob/ob-dominant DMGs, 303 genes (10% of DEGs and 31% of DMGs) were DM-DEGs, and 17 of the 644 ob/ob-dominant DEPs (6% of DM-DEGs and 3% of DEPs) were DM-DEPs (Fig. 2e). Thus, only a small overlap between DEPs and DM-DEPs in both WT- and ob/ob-dominance indicates that obesity has limited effect on changes in gene and protein expression through DNA methylation changes.

To examine the quantitative relationships among DNA methylation, gene expression, and protein expression, we plotted the log ratio of DNA methylation between WT and ob/ob mice against the corresponding log ratio of gene expression (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Although the correlation was modest, it was statistically significant (r = –0.095, p = 1.28 × 10−26), supporting the notion that obesity-associated changes in DNA methylation are partially associated with changes in gene expression. Similarly, the log ratio of gene expression between WT and ob/ob mice was plotted against the log ratio of protein expression (Supplementary Fig. 2c). The positive correlation observed (r = 0.522, p = 1.42 × 10−176) indicates that changes in gene expression associated with obesity are, to some extent, related to the level of protein expression.

Identification of Differentially expressed transcription factor (DETF) bound genes and their association with DEPs

In addition to DNA hypomethylation, WT-dominant and ob/ob-dominant gene expression can also be associated with transcription factor (TF) binding in the promoter region of the relevant genes. Therefore, we obtained TF-binding peaks from ChIP-Atlas15 and identified genes with peaks in the promoter (from 1000 bp upstream to 1000 bp downstream of the TSS) and associated with DEGs.

Among the TFs, those differentially expressed between WT and ob/ob, referred to as Differentially Expressed TF (DETF), can be associated with differential expression of bound DEGs. We investigated overlap between 2409 DETF-bound genes, DEGs, and DEPs (Fig. 3a, b). Among 2490 WT-dominant DEGs, 2143 genes (86%) were DETF-bound, of which 206 genes encoded DEP (30% of WT-dominant DEP, Fig. 3a). Of the 2941 ob/ob-dominant DEGs, 869 genes (30%) were DETF-bound, of which 136 genes encoded DEP (21% of ob/ob-dominant DEP, Fig. 3b). Thus, the WT-dominant DEGs are mostly DETF-bound genes. Thus, more WT-dominant and ob/ob-dominant DEPs are associated with DETF rather than DM-DEPs (Fig. 2d, e, and Fig. 3). In addition, more DEPs are associated with DETF-bound in WT than in ob/ob mice. Different DNA methylation of enhancer (DM-enhancer) and TF binding

In the previous sections (Figs. 2, 3), we investigated the relationship between DNA methylation and DETF-binding near the TSS and DEGs. However, regulation by enhancers located further from the TSS may also be related to DEGs. Therefore, we obtained enhancer regions and their regulatory genes from EnhancerAtlas 2.020 (see Methods) and analyzed the association between DNA methylation, DETF binding at enhancers, and DEGs.

To examine the relationship between enhancer and gene expression, we examined the expression of genes with enhancers and those without enhancers (Supplementary Fig. 3a). The expression of genes without enhancers showed a bimodal distribution, whereas the expression of genes with enhancers showed a unimodal distribution only with higher expression. Consequently, genes with enhancers are more highly expressed, which aligns with the known role of enhancers in activating gene expression21.

To analyze DEGs with differential DNA methylation at enhancers, we examined the overlap between enhancers containing DMCpGs (referred to as DM-enhancers) and genes associated with DM-enhancers and DEGs. Among the 2,409 WT-dominant DEGs, 93 (4%) were associated with DM-enhancers, with 15 genes encoding DEPs (representing 2% of WT-dominant DEPs, Supplementary Fig. 3b). Similarly, 25 of the 2,941 ob/ob-dominant DEGs (1%) were linked to DM-enhancers, with 2 encoding DEPs (0.3% of ob/ob-dominant DEPs, Supplementary Fig. 3c). We also investigated DEGs with DETF binding at enhancers. Among WT-dominant DEGs, 261 showed DETF binding at the enhancer, with 34 genes encoding DEPs (5% of WT-dominant DEPs, Supplementary Fig. 3d). For ob/ob-dominant DEGs, 260 had DETF binding at the enhancer, and 43 of these encoded DEPs (7% of ob/ob-dominant DEPs, Supplementary Fig. 3e). Compared to Fig. 2 and Fig. 3, there were fewer DEPs associated with DM-enhancers and DETF-bound genes at enhancers than those associated with DMGs and DETF-bound genes at promoters.

To evaluate the impact of DNA methylation in promoters and enhancers on gene expression, we constructed a linear regression model in which gene expression levels (log(TPM)) were explained by the average methylation ratio of CpG sites located within promoters and enhancers in genes that have both promoters and enhancers. In WT mice, the coefficient for the promoter was –3.16 (p = 0), while that for the enhancer was –0.50 (p = 0.017), with an R² of 0.167 (Supplementary Fig. 3f). In ob/ob mice, the corresponding coefficient for the promoter was –2.96 (p = 0) and for the enhancer was –0.55 (p = 0.016), with an R² of 0.136 (Supplementary Fig. 3g). Although the explained variance is low, all coefficients were statistically significant, and given the low proportion of DM-DEGs, these values are considered reasonable. Furthermore, the larger negative coefficient for the promoter than enhancer and the higher explained variance in WT than in ob/ob are consistent with the relative proportion of DM-DEGs.

Pathways with WT- or ob/ob-dominant expression of genes and proteins

We identified DEPs encoded by DEGs (referred to as DEG-DEPs) and their associations with DMGs and DETFs. We investigated the pathways in which DEG-DEPs are associated with DMGs, DETFs, and DM-enhancers using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database22.

We identified pathways enriched in WT- and ob/ob-dominant DEG and DEG-DEPs, i.e., pathways that differ in expression status between WT and ob/ob mice. WT-dominant DEGs were enriched exclusively in the complement and coagulation cascade, while ob/ob-dominant DEGs were enriched in multiple pathways, including Glycolysis/Gluconeogenesis (Supplementary Fig. 4). For these pathways enriched with DEGs, we analyzed pathway enrichment for WT-dominant and ob/ob-dominant DEG-DEPs. WT-dominant DEG-DEPs were also enriched in the complement and coagulation cascade (Fig. 4a). In contrast, ob/ob-dominant DEGs and DEG-DEPs were enriched in 18 pathways, including many metabolic pathways such as Glycolysis/Gluconeogenesis (Fig. 4b). Therefore, DEG-level pathway enrichment (Supplementary Fig. 4) is consistent with DEG-DEP-level results (Fig. 4), supporting the robustness of our enrichment across expression levels. Note that the number of WT-dominant DEG-DEPs is comparable to ob/ob-dominant DEG-DEPs, although the only KEGG pathway enriched with WT-dominant DEG-DEPs is the complement and coagulation cascade. We additionally conducted GO term enrichment analysis and found that WT-dominant DEGs were enriched not only in KEGG-consistent terms such as “blood coagulation,” but also in terms related to genetic information processing, including “regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase II” (Supplementary Data 4). For WT-dominant DEG-DEPs, we quantified the DEPs associated with DMGs (i.e., DM-DEPs), DEPs encoded by DM-enhancer-associated DEGs, and DEPs encoded by DEGs with DETF binding near the TSS and at enhancers (referred to as DETF (promoter) and DETF (enhancer), respectively) (Fig. 5a). Only a few DEG-DEPs corresponded exclusively to DMGs (Fig. 5a, second column from the left) and most DEG-DEPs were associated with DETF (promoter) or both DMGs and DETF (promoter) (Fig. 5a, third and sixth columns from the left, respectively).

We conducted an enrichment analysis for WT-dominant DM-DEPs and proteins encoded by DETF-bound genes across the same pathways as in Fig. 4 (Fig. 5b). WT-dominant DM-DEPs are enriched in the complement and coagulation cascade, indicating that WT-dominant expression of this pathway is associated with WT-dominant DNA hypomethylation (Fig. 5b, left-most column). The complement and coagulation cascades also contained DEGs bound by Clock, Hnf4a, and Onecut1 (Fig. 5b, second and subsequent columns). Notably, Hnf4a is a DETF, a DM-DEP, and DM-enhancer-associated DEP which means that methylation of promoter and enhancer of Hnf4a is increased and expression is decreased in ob/ob. Hnf4a plays a critical role in hepatocyte differentiation23 and regulates gluconeogenesis24,25. In this study, Hnf4a was bound to the promoter of Pck1, the rate-limiting enzyme for gluconeogenesis. Decreased expression of Hnf4a in ob/ob is consistent with the previous research showing decreased Hnf4a expression in obesity-related liver conditions such as NAFLD26. Similarly, another previous study using high-fat diet-induced obese mice has shown that Hnf4a expression is reduced in obesity27.

Enrichment analysis was performed by examining the number of DEG-DEPs associated with DM-enhancers and DETF-bound enhancers in each pathway (Supplementary Fig. 5a). WT-dominant DEG-DEPs associated with DM-enhancers (Supplementary Fig. 5a, left-most column) and those linked to TF-bound enhancers (Supplementary Fig. 5a, second and subsequent columns) did not show enrichment in any pathway. This lack of pathway enrichment may be attributed to the smaller number of DEG-DEPs associated with differences in enhancers compared to those near the TSS.

Ob/ob-dominant metabolic pathways are associated with DETF-binding

We also summarized the number of ob/ob-dominant DEG-DEPs and performed enrichment analysis (Fig. 6). ob/ob-dominant DEG-DEPs generally exhibited fewer DM-DEPs, and a higher proportion of DEG-DEPs were associated with DETF (promoter) (Fig. 6a). The only pathway that was enriched with ob/ob-dominant DM-DEPs was PPAR signaling (Fig. 6b, left-most column). The ob/ob-dominant expression of many other pathways that were enriched by DEG-DEPs was not associated with ob/ob-dominant hypomethylation. DEG-DEPs enriched in ob/ob-dominant pathways involved various metabolic functions and included DETF-bound DEGs such as Esr1, Dbp, Stat5a, Srebf1, and Pparg (Fig. 6b, second and subsequent columns). DEG-DEPs encoded by the Pparg-binding genes are enriched in two pathways related to fatty acid metabolism (biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids and fatty acid metabolism). Pparg is a DETF that is highly expressed in ob/ob, which is consistent with the elevated expression of Pparg in ob/ob mice28 and NAFLD liver29.

We performed the enrichment analysis of ob/ob-dominant DEG-DEPs associated with DM-enhancers and DETF-bound enhancers (Supplementary Fig. 5b). The ob/ob-dominant DEG-DEPs associated with several DETF-bound enhancers were enriched in several metabolic pathways (Supplementary Fig. 5b). The drug metabolism pathway included DEPs bound by Nfe2l2.

To corroborate the results, enrichment analysis using Fisher’s exact test, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)30 for DNA methylation, transcript, and protein datasets. For DNA methylation, we applied ebGSEA31 with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test; because ebGSEA was developed for Illumina Infinium bead arrays, we additionally used methylGSA32 to integrate CpG-level methylation differences from bisulfite sequencing. We also conducted Wilcoxon rank-sum–based GSEA for gene and protein expression. Across all modalities, the complement and coagulation pathways were enriched in WT-dominance relative to ob/ob (Supplementary Data 5), consistent with the findings from Fisher’s exact test.

As described above, DNA hypomethylation is specifically associated with proteins belonging to the WT-dominant complement and coagulation cascade and their regulatory DETFs, such as Hnf4a, but not with ob/ob-dominant pathways except for PPAR signaling. DETF binding near TSS is associated with both WT and ob/ob-dominant pathways.

Only a part of the pathway, including DM-DEP and DETF-bound DEP, is shown. a Coagulation cascade. b Complement cascade.

The expression and DNA methylation ratios of proteins in the PPAR signaling pathway—enriched by ob/ob-dominant DM-DEP—are shown in Supplementary Fig. 6a. For most proteins in this pathway, differences in expression and DNA methylation ratios are minimal except Apoa4 and Plin4.

According to enrichment analysis (Fig. 6b), two pathways related to fatty acid metabolism were associated with Pparg, an ob/ob-dominant DETF. In these pathways, the genes to which Pparg binds (Acaa1, Hsd17b4, and Acox1) are all enzymes related to β-oxidation (Supplementary Fig. 6b). This binding of Pparg to β-oxidation-related enzymes is consistent with the previous study that showed that knockout of Pparg in mouse hepatocytes reduced β-oxidation33. However, it has been proposed that Pparg does not directly regulate the gene but rather indirectly via Ppara33. In contrast, our study suggests that Pparg binding to promoters may directly enhance the expression of some enzymes in the β-oxidation pathway in the context of obesity, although it should be noted that this is a prediction from the ChIP-Atlas TF-binding peak, not functional inference.

Since DEG-DEP in drug metabolism is characterized by DETF-binding to enhancers and includes some DM-DEPs, we examined DNA methylation and expression of proteins in this pathway, as well as the binding of Nfe2l2, an enriched DETF (Supplementary Fig. 6c). This pathway includes DM-DEPs such as Cyp2e1, Ces3a, and Ces3b. Carboxylesterases, including Ces3a and Ces3b, play roles in lipid metabolism, particularly in the hydrolysis of triglycerides and diglycerides, and are implicated in the development and progression of fatty liver disease34. Although different methylation or expression of Ces3a and Ces3b specifically in obesity have not been reported, Ces3a and Ces3b may contribute to pathogenesis in obesity.

DNA methylation ratios and protein expression were also examined for DM-DEPs other than those in the above pathway (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). Notably, Hnf1a, a transcription factor, is a WT-dominant DM-DEP that exhibits similar expression and DNA methylation patterns to Hnf4a. The target genes bound by Hnf1a remain unknown, as no ChIP-seq data for Hnf1a is available in the ChIP-Atlas. A previous study35 has shown that decreased Hnf1a expression is associated with fatty liver and insulin resistance, suggesting a potential link to disease pathogenesis in ob/ob mice. In addition, the expression levels of the key obesity-associated proteins mentioned above are shown in Supplementary Fig. 7c.

Identification of DEPs associated with DMCpGs using the association between gene expression and CpGs from the EWAS study

In this study, we investigated the relationship between differentially methylated CpGs (DMCpGs) near the TSS and within enhancers, as defined by the EnhancerAtlas2.0, with protein expression. However, it is possible that CpGs in other genomic regions also influence gene and protein expression. Epigenome-wide association study (EWAS) using mouse inbred strains36 identified methylation of CpGs in regions such as promoter, enhancer, exon, and intron that are significantly associated with gene expression in the liver. With this association of CpGs with gene expression from the EWAS study, we examined gene expression associated with DMCpGs in this study (Supplementary Fig. 8). To identify the effect of DNA methylation on expression, which was overlooked by DMG and DM-enhancer, we identified genes associated with DMCpG among genes excluding DMGs and DM-enhancer-associated genes. There are 2793 genes that are associated with WT-dominant DMCpGs (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Similarly, 3886 genes are associated with ob/ob-dominant DMCpGs. Among genes associated with WT-dominant DMCpGs, 528 were WT-dominant DEGs, 71 of which encoded differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) (Supplementary Fig. 8b). Similarly, of the genes associated with ob/ob-dominant DMCpGs, 996 overlapped with ob/ob-dominant DEGs, 166 of which encoded DEPs (Supplementary Fig. 8c).

We also performed an enrichment analysis of DEPs encoded by DEGs associated with DMCpGs into the KEGG pathway. Unlike DM-DEP, the WT-dominant DEPs associated with DMCpGs were not enriched in any pathway (Supplementary Fig. 8d). However, ob/ob-dominant DEPs associated with DMCpGs were abundant in the ob/ob-dominant metabolic pathway (Supplementary Fig. 8e).

These findings suggest that many genes associated with DMCpGs lie outside the regions analyzed for DMGs (200 bp upstream to 400 bp downstream of the TSS), including broader genomic regions such as enhancers, exons, and introns. This broader definition of DMCpGs could help explain the enriched expression of metabolic pathway proteins in ob/ob mice, which was not captured by DM-DEPs defined in this study.

Discussion

In this study, we identified WT and ob/ob-dominant gene and protein expression (DEG-DEPs) associated with WT and ob/ob-dominant DNA hypomethylation (DM-DEPs). Additionally, we identified DEG-DEPs associated with differentially expressed transcription factor (DETF) binding. Among WT-dominant DEPs, only about 4% were WT-dominant DM-DEPs, while approximately 30% were DEG-DEPs with DETF binding at promoters. Similarly, only 3% of ob/ob-dominant DEPs were ob/ob-dominant DM-DEPs, whereas about 21% were DEG-DEPs with DETF binding at promoters.

WT-dominant DEG-DEPs were enriched in the complement and coagulation cascade, while ob/ob-dominant DEG-DEPs were enriched in several metabolic pathways. In the complement and coagulation cascade, both DM-DEP and DEP encoded by the DETF binding genes to the promoter were abundant. In contrast, in the ob/ob-dominant several metabolic pathways, DM-DEPs were fewer, while DEPs encoded by DETF-binding genes to the promoter were more prevalent. DEPs associated with DM-enhancers were few, while DETF-binding genes to enhancers were abundant in drug metabolism, a metabolic pathway in which ob/ob-dominant DEG-DEPs were abundant. While promoter CpG methylation functions as a relatively stable gate for transcription initiation and shows a strong inverse relationship with expression, enhancers are typically low-CpG and highly state-dependent elements37. This may be the reason for obesity-related enhancer regulation being associated primarily with TFs rather than methylation shifts.

The predominance of TF-associated DEGs/DEPs over those linked to DNA methylation in our study likely reflects the fact that transcription factor activity can change rapidly in response to nutritional and hormonal cues, which are markedly changed in obesity. For example, obesity-induced hyperinsulinemia and hyperlipidemia activate TFs such as PPARγ29. Such TF-driven transcriptional programs can produce large-scale, reversible changes in gene expression within hours to days. In contrast, promoter DNA methylation changes are generally more stable and accumulate slowly, often during development or prolonged disease states38. Based on these observations, we propose a biological model in which TF regulation acts as a primary and dynamic driver of gene expression changes in the obese liver, with DNA methylation contributing to a smaller subset of more persistent alterations, such as the repression of complement and coagulation genes observed here.

The observed downregulation of complement and coagulation genes in ob/ob mice, associated with both increased DNA methylation and suppressed expression of Hnf4a, raises important questions regarding its functional significance. One possibility is that this transcriptional repression represents a compensatory mechanism aimed at dampening chronic low-grade inflammation, a well-established feature of obesity and NAFLD41,42. Complement activation, particularly via C3 and C5 components, has been implicated in promoting hepatic inflammation and fibrogenesis in NAFLD and NASH39–42. Thus, attenuating the expression of complement proteins could help limit excessive immune activation in the fatty liver environment.

Our previous study11 using transcriptome and proteome data similar to this study primarily focused on metabolism, such as glucose and lipid metabolism. This previous study identified abnormalities in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and fatty acid degradation in the liver in obesity. However, the role of the epigenome in driving these changes remained unclear. In this study, although only a limited number of the metabolic enzymes discussed were found to be associated with changes in DNA methylation in this study, we observed some notable findings. Specifically, the decreased expression of Pck1, the rate-limiting enzyme of gluconeogenesis, was associated with increased DNA methylation. Hnf4a of DETF, a DMG, also binds to Pck1. Hnf4a, which is both DMG and DETF, was found to bind to Pck1. This observation aligns with previous findings that Hnf4a regulates gluconeogenesis43, suggesting that DNA methylation may exert a secondary effect on gluconeogenesis through Hnf4a. Additionally, the increased expression of Acaa1b, an enzyme involved in fatty acid degradation, was associated with a decrease in DNA methylation. Thus, although limited in scope, some metabolic changes in obesity in this previous study were found to be associated with changes in DNA methylation.

The number of WT-dominant DMGs and ob/ob-dominant DMGs was about 1000 (about 8% of expressed genes), and the number of DM-DEGs was about 300 for both WT-dominant and ob/ob-dominant genes, indicating limited differences in DNA methylation in ob/ob. Similarly, a previous study7 on the liver of obese mice (New Zealand Obese mice) reported no significant difference in the mean DNA methylation ratio across all genes between WT and obese mice (Supplementary Fig. 9a; WT: 0.4546, ob/ob: 0.4545), with similar distributions. In addition, when DMGs of WT and New Zealand Obese mice were identified using the method of this study, there were only 17 WT-dominant DMGs and 19 New Zealand Obese-dominant DMGs (Supplementary Fig. 9b). We examined the overlap between these DMGs and the DEGs (Supplementary Fig. 9c) and found only 4 DM-DEGs in both WT-dominant and New Zealand Obese-dominant (Supplementary Fig. 9d, e). The reason for the low number of DMGs compared to ob/ob mice may be due to the small difference in average methylation ratio between WT and New Zealand Obese mice (Supplementary Fig. 9a), though it should be noted that different data conditions such as read count, is also a possible reason.

Although the DNA methylation changes in the livers of obese mice are limited, it is possible that more pronounced changes in DNA methylation occur in conditions of fatty liver progression and cirrhosis caused by obesity. To test this possibility, we analyzed WGBS data from a previous study of human NAFLD44. We compared the distribution of DNA methylation percentage between healthy human liver and NAFLD and identified DMGs using our method. The results showed that the mean DNA methylation ratio of healthy liver and NAFLD was not so different (Supplementary Fig 10a; normal: 0.4546, NAFLD: 0.4545). Additionally, we found only 42 healthy-dominant DMGs and 69 NAFLD-dominant DMGs (Supplementary Fig. 10b). These findings suggest that, similar to the case of obesity, changes in DNA methylation are also limited in NAFLD.

To explore DNA methylation changes in other liver diseases, we reanalyzed WGBS data from a previous study45 comparing mouse normal liver and hepatocyte carcinoma (mouse ICC cell line with IDH1 mutation). Mean gene methylation was significantly lower in the tumor (0.411) than in the normal liver (0.463), and the shape of the distribution of DNA methylation ratios was different (Supplementary Fig. 11a, p < 0.0001). Applying our DMG criteria identified 4367 normal-dominant hypomethylated (26%) and 4497 tumor-dominant hypomethylated DMGs (27%)—substantially more than in the obese comparison (Supplementary Fig. 11b). RNA-seq data from the same study showed 5616 normal-dominant DEGs and 8107 tumor-dominant DEGs (Supplementary Fig. 11c). Note that RNA-seq of normal liver was not obtained in the previous study45 and was compared with WT liver in this study. Of the DEGs, 1841 (33%) in the normal-dominant set and 2474 (31%) in the tumor-dominant set were DM-DEGs (Supplementary Fig. 11 d, e), again exceeding the ob/ob overlap. The previous study45 attributed widespread tumor methylation changes to mutant IDH1 effects on TET-mediated demethylation46; consistently, Dnmt1, Dnmt3a, and Tet1/2/3—none DEG in obesity—were tumor-dominant DEGs, aligning with the broader bidirectional methylation shifts observed in tumors.

While there were a few DMGs with DMCpGs near the TSS, approximately 3000–4000 genes were associated with DMCpGs identified in the EWAS and linked to expression, exceeding the number of DMGs. Some DEG-DEPs were not associated with DMGs, DM-enhancers, EWAS-related DMCpGs, or DETF-binding. The DEGs encoding these proteins may be associated with epigenomic differences not addressed in this study, such as histone modifications. Additionally, the previous EWAS measured gene expression for 22,716 genes via microarray and DNA methylation for 9.6% of all CpGs (approximately 2 million CpGs) using RRBS. Other relationships between DMCpGs and gene expression may remain undiscovered. Furthermore, since the EWAS data were derived from lean mice, it is possible that CpG–gene expression relationships specific to the obese state were overlooked, or that associations not relevant under obese conditions were inadvertently included. Therefore, it should be noted that the large number of DMCpG-associated genes may be partly attributable to the limited CpG coverage of the EWAS data and the use of reference epigenetic tracks from lean mice.

While this study primarily focused on explaining DEG-DEPs, some DEPs are not encoded by DEGs (referred to as nonDEG-DEPs). These nonDEG-DEPs may be associated with different post-transcriptional regulation, such as translation and degradation. Indeed, translational repression is a known feature in ob/ob mice47. We analyzed the distribution of nonDEG-DEPs across pathways (Supplementary Fig. 12a). WT-dominant nonDEG-DEPs were enriched in lysosomes, while ob/ob-dominant nonDEG-DEPs were abundant in carbon metabolism.

The transcriptome and proteome data in this study were obtained after 16 h of fasting. We also obtained transcriptome and proteome data from the livers of ad libitum feeding WT and ob/ob mice in a previous study13. Among the WT-dominant DM-DEPs in the complement and coagulation pathway, C8a and Klkb1 were also identified as WT-dominant DEPs in the ad libitum comparison between WT and ob/ob mice. However, Serpind1, Serpinf2, and Serping1 were not identified as DEPs (q < 0.05, using methods from previous studies). Additionally, Hnf4a, a DETF and DMG associated with this pathway, was not identified as a DEP under ad libitum feeding conditions. However, among the molecules showing mismatched results, Serpind1, Serpinf2, and Hnf4a (but not Serping1) were WT-dominant DEGs in ad libitum feeding. This suggests that fasting and ad libitum feeding are consistent in terms of mRNA expression associated with DNA methylation, but that different post-transcriptional regulation may be at work in ad libitum feeding than in fasting.

One of the limitations of this study is the use of bulk hepatocyte populations, which do not capture the well-known spatial heterogeneity of liver zonation. Hepatocytes exhibit distinct metabolic and transcriptional programs depending on their position along the portal-central axis48,49, and recent single-cell and spatial transcriptomics studies have revealed that both transcription factor binding and DNA methylation exhibit zonated patterns49,50. For instance, HNF4A and CEBPA are enriched in periportal hepatocytes, while Wnt/β-catenin targets are more active pericentrally49. Moreover, CpG methylation has been reported to differ between periportal and pericentral regions at specific loci50. Future studies using spatially resolved methylome or single-nucleus multi-omics will be essential to dissect the zonated regulatory mechanism underlying obesity-associated hepatic dysfunction.

We validated our DMCpGs using methylKit51, a DMCpG identification method based on a different principle from the RADMeth approach used in the present study (Supplementary Fig. 12b). RADMeth employs beta-binomial regression with spatial adjustment of p-values across neighboring CpG sites, whereas methylKit uses logistic regression and does not incorporate spatial p-value aggregation. Although many DMCpGs near transcription start sites (TSSs) did not overlap between the two methods, the key DMCpGs associated with major DMGs in this study—such as Klkb1, Serping1, and C8a in the complement pathway, as well as Hnf4a—were consistently detected by both. In future work, experimental validation of DNA methylation ratios using approaches such as targeted bisulfite sequencing could also be considered.

In this study, we integrated multi-omics data, focusing on molecules with significant differences to minimize the effects of artifacts such as batch effects. However, integration methods that directly consider molecular quantification values may be considered for future research.

In this study, we examined protein changes associated with changes in DNA methylation and in TF expression in ob/ob mice. While the overall number of genes with DNA methylation changes was relatively small, the decreased expression of several proteins in the complement and coagulation system in ob/ob mice was associated with increased DNA methylation. Changes in TF expression in ob/ob are more widely associated with expression changes, such as an increase in enzymes in fatty acid metabolism. Conceptually, we propose a model in which promoter methylation acts as a stability lock on selected hepatocyte modules, whereas TF-driven regulation provides flexible, system-wide remodeling during obesity. This view is consistent with epigenetic/NAFLD reviews and evidence that DNA-methylation changes can trail physiological shifts after dietary interventions. Future investigations into other obesity-related changes, such as those involving the broader epigenome, translation, and protein degradation, may help clarify the mechanisms underlying expression variation in obesity.

Methods

Mouse Studies

Except for the isolation of primary hepatocytes, all procedures involving animal experiments were approved by the University of Tokyo Animal Ethics Committee. The isolation of primary hepatocytes from mice was approved by the Kyushu University Animal Ethics Committee. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and the University of Tokyo guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. Ten-week-old male C57BL/6 J wild-type (WT) mice or C57BL/6JHamSlc-ob/ob mice purchased from SLC Japan, Inc. were fasted for 16 h. The mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, which is a technique used in physical euthanasia by applying pressure to the neck and dislocating the spinal column instead of using agents. Cervical dislocation is often used in studies requiring tissue quality unaffected by chemical agents52. Therefore, we used cervical dislocation to avoid effects on metabolism as much as possible. All procedures were performed by trained personnel to ensure minimal distress to the mice. Their livers (whole or left lateral lobes for transcriptome and proteome) were dissected and frozen in liquid nitrogen. The frozen liver samples were ground to a fine powder in a blender with dry ice and subsequently used for transcriptomic and proteomic analysis. Sample sizes were as follows: n = 3 for methylome, n = 11 for transcriptome, and n = 5 for proteome.

Isolation of primary hepatocytes

Primary hepatocytes were isolated from 10-week-old male C57BL/6 J WT mice and C57BL/6JHamSlc-ob/ob and cultured using a previously described method53. The liver of mice was perfused at a rate of 4.5 mL/min for the first 2 min with a perfusion solution consisting of Hank’s balanced salt solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) containing 10 mM HEPES and adjusted to pH 7.4 with NaOH. This was followed by perfusion for 20 min with collagenase type I (0.3 mg/mL) (Worthington, Lakewood, NY) and complete EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Hepatocytes were purified by density gradient centrifugation with Percoll (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). Isolated hepatocytes were seeded at 5.0 × 104 cells/cm2 to collagen I-coated dish and cultured with DMEM (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (NICHIREI BIOSCIENCES, Japan), Penicillin/Streptomycin (10000 U/mL) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). After 24 h, the medium was replaced with serum-free DMEM containing 0.01 nM insulin and 10 nM dexamethasone (Fujifilm-Wako, Japan), and the cells were incubated for additional 16 h.

Omics analysis

Genomic DNA from hepatocytes was prepared using DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. WGBS library preparation was performed with the tPBAT protocol described previously54. Briefly, 100 ng of purified genomic DNA was spiked with 1 ng of unmethylated lambda DNA (Promega) and subjected to bisulfite conversion. The bisulfite-treated DNA was split into two portions, each used for library preparation in the forward and reverse directions. The two libraries of different directions were subsequently mixed and used for sequencing. This mixed library strategy effectively compensates for the biased nucleotide composition inherent in WGBS reads54. Sequencing was performed using HiSeq X ten at Macrogen Japan (Tokyo, Japan), with one sequencing lane assigned per sample.

DMG identification

The sequenced reads were mapped on the mouse reference genome (mm10) combined with the Escherichia phage Lambda genome sequence using BMap54. The aligned reads were processed and summarized with a series of in-house programs (https://github.com/FumihitoMiura/Project-2).

Differential DNA methylation analysis was performed using RADMeth19. The methylation ratio of each CpG site was compared between WT and ob/ob mice, and CpG sites with q < 0.05 were defined as differentially methylated CpGs (DMCpGs). Genes containing at least one DMCpG within a region spanning 200 bp upstream to 400 bp downstream of the TSS were defined as Differentially Methylated Genes (DMGs). All q-values represent the p-values corrected for multiple testing using the Storey method55. Ensembl transcript IDs are used for the genes.

DEG identification

Genes that were not expressed in either WT or ob/ob mice were filtered out by edgeR56. Here, expressed genes were defined as those with reads in at least 6 out of 11 replicates in either WT or ob/ob mice. Differential expression analysis was performed using edgeR56, comparing expression levels between WT and ob/ob mice within this gene set. Genes with q < 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). All q-values represent p-values adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Ensembl Gene IDs are used for genes. Note that transcriptomic data and methodology used in this study are identical to those in our previous research11,17, with the key difference being the q-value threshold.

DEP identification

A protein was considered expressed if an iBAQ value was obtained in at least 3 out of 5 replicates. Among the 2651 proteins expressed in at least either WT or ob/ob mice, differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified by the following method (this method is based on the R package “DEP”57). For proteins detected in both WT and ob/ob mice, iBAQ values were normalized using variance stabilizing normalization58. Briefly, the expression level x was transformed using the function h(x)=γarcsinh⁡(a+bx), where a,b,γ were estimated from data using a robust variant of maximum likelihood estimation. Normalized expression levels were compared using limma59, and proteins with q < 0.05 were defined as DEPs. Additionally, proteins detected only in either WT or ob/ob mice were also included in the DEPs. Note that the proteomic data in this study are identical to those in our previous research11. However, the methodology is from another of our previous studies on DNA methylation17, leading to the different set of DEPs.

Ensembl protein IDs are used for proteins. For overlap between DMG, DEG, DETF-bound genes, and DEPs, Ensembl protein IDs are converted to Ensembl gene IDs.

DETF identification

ChIP-seq peak positions were retrieved from the ChIP-Atlas Peak Browser15 for “TF and other factors” in the liver, using a significance threshold of >500. Peaks from experiments classified under “Liver tumor” were excluded. Additionally, we removed factors that were not TFs (Hdac3, Rad21, Smc3, and Stag1) as well as transcriptional repressors (Ncor1, Prox1, Ncor1, Nelfa, Cbx5, Foxk1, Foxp2, Hnrnpu, Nr1d1, and Setdb1). A TF was considered to be bound to a gene if its ChIP-seq peak was located within a region spanning 1000 bp upstream to 1000 bp downstream of the gene. Transcription factors encoded by differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as differentially expressed transcription factors (DETFs).

Enhancer data

Hepatocyte enhancer data were obtained from EnhancerAtlas 2.020. Enhancer regions were downloaded from the “Hepatocyte” category under “Download enhancers,” and regulatory genes associated with these enhancers were retrieved from the “Hepatocyte” category under “Download enhancer-gene interactions.”

Enrichment analysis

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed for DEGs, DEG-DEPs, DM-DEPs, or DETF-bound DEPs. Pathways with q < 0.05, as determined by a right-tailed Fisher’s exact test, were considered significantly enriched. All measured genes or proteins were used as the background. The q-values represent p-values adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method.
