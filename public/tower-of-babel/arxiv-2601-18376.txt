A note on nested conditions for finite categories of subgraphs
Jens Kosiol, Steffen Zschaler

Abstract
In this note, we present a nesting-free normal form for the formalism of nested conditions and constraints in the context of finite categories of subgraphs.



1
Introduction

In this note, we present a nesting-free normal form for the formalism of
nested conditions and constraints

[
20
,
12
]
in the context of finite categories of subgraphs.
Nested conditions express properties of morphisms, while constraints express properties of objects.
This formalism has established itself as the central logical formalism to be used in the context of
graph transformation
because it allows to elegantly compute the interactions of the applications of transformation rules and the validity of constraints
[
12
,
7
,
6
,
10
,
5
]
.
On (typed) graphs, nested constraints have been shown to be expressively equivalent to first-order logic
[
20
,
12
]
.

Moreover, every additional level of nesting increases the expressivity of the formalism
[
20
]
.

While predominantly used for typed graphs, the logic of nested conditions and constraints can be used in any category.
In ongoing work
[
16
]
, we employ this formalism in the context of finite categories of subgraphs.
These are categories whose objects are (equivalence classes of) subgraphs of a given finite
container graph

T
, and morphisms are inclusions; we denote such a category as
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
.
Transformations of these objects can be realised via so-called
subgraph transformation systems

[
2
,
13
]
, where transformation rules themselves consist of subgraphs of the given container graph
T
and are applied to subgraphs of
T
in such a way that the result is again a subgraph of
T
.

In this note, we investigate the formalism of nested conditions in the context of finite categories of subgraphs and make two contributions.
First, we work out a normalisation construction, called
flattening
, that transforms every nested condition/constraint into a non-nested Boolean combination of literals.
In that respect our central result is the correctness of that construction (Theorem
1
), implying that every condition/constraint can be equivalently transformed into a disjunctive normal form of literals (Corollary
4
).
We present our flattening construction in Section
4
.

Furthermore, every graph
G
in a category of subgraphs of a container graph
T
can also be considered as a graph in the category
\mathbf{Graph}_{\mathbf{TG}}
, the category of all graphs typed over
\mathit{TG}
, the type graph over which the container graph
T
is typed.
In particular, a nested constraint formulated in the category
\mathbf{Graph}_{\mathbf{TG}}
can be evaluated for subgraphs of
T
.
We develop a translation that, given a nested condition/constraint for
\mathbf{Graph}_{\mathbf{TG}}
and a
finite
container graph
T
(typed over
\mathit{TG}
) transforms that condition/constraint into one formulated in the category
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
.
Our main contribution in that regard is showing our translation to preserve the semantics of the given constraints (Theorem
2
).
This ensures that, while nesting of constraints collapses in the context of finite categories of subgraphs, no expressivity of the formalism is lost.
Moreover, this translation makes specifying and working with nested conditions/constraints in the context of finite categories of subgraphs much more practical, since nested conditions/constraints in
\mathbf{Graph}_{\mathbf{TG}}
are much more compact than their non-nested counterparts in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
.
We present our translation in Section
5
.

The results in this note are not surprising.
Intuitively, in a context where all finitely many elements that can ever appear are known beforehand, we can replace universal quantification by a disjunction of the possible situations and, in this way, can get rid of nesting.
Still, making this precise is somewhat subtle.
Moreover, in
[
16
]
, we develop an interesting application of the results presented here.
Considerably generalising work of Horcas et al.
[
14
]
, we there present an approach to
non-blocking consistency-preserving subgraph transformation
, where, from a given subgraph transformation rule, we derive a family of rules that (i) contain the given rule as subrule, (ii) cannot introduce violations of the given constraints upon application and (iii) are not blocked by application conditions.
Instead, the original rule is amended by additional actions that immediately remedy introduced constraint violations.
We see considerable potential of this rule construction in particular in the context of
model-driven optimisation

[
15
]
.
Importantly, this rule construction we develop in
[
16
]
is based on the normal form for conditions in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
we develop in this note.

We begin by introducing the
class–responsibility assignment (CRA) problem
as a running example we use to illustrate our construction (Section
2
) and by presenting preliminaries (Section
3
).

These two sections are almost verbally taken over from
[
16
]
.

[

3.1
Typed graphs

Graphs are sets of nodes with edges between them.
Typing offers a way to equip the individual elements of a graph with meaning; a
type graph
provides the available types for elements, and morphisms assign the elements of
typed graphs
to those types.

{definition}

[Graph. Typed graph]
A
graph

G=(V_{G},E_{G},\mathit{src}_{G},\mathit{tar}_{G})
consists of a set of nodes
V_{G}
, a set of edges
E_{G}
, and source and target functions
\mathit{src}_{G},\mathit{tar}_{G}\colon E_{G}\to V_{G}
.
A
graph morphism

f\colon G\to H
between two graphs is a pair of functions
f_{V}\colon V_{G}\to V_{H}
and
f_{E}\colon E_{G}\to E_{H}
that is compatible with the source and target functions, such that

\mathit{src}_{H}\circ f_{E}=f_{V}\circ\mathit{src}_{G}\text{ and }\mathit{tar}_{H}\circ f_{E}=f_{V}\circ\mathit{tar}_{G}.

A
type graph

\mathit{TG}=(V_{\mathit{TG}},E_{\mathit{TG}},\mathit{src}_{\mathit{TG}},\mathit{tar}_{\mathit{TG}})
is a fixed graph.
A
graph typed over a fixed type graph
\mathit{TG}
consists of a graph
G
and a graph morphism
\mathit{type}_{G}\colon G\to\mathit{TG}
.
A
typed graph morphism

f\colon G\to H
between graphs
G
and
H
typed over
\mathit{TG}
is a graph morphism that is compatible with typing, i.e., such that
\mathit{type}_{H}\circ f=\mathit{type}_{G}
.

A typed graph morphism is
injective/surjectiv/bijective/an inclusion
if both of its components are.
We denote injective morphisms via hooked arrows
f\colon G\hookrightarrow H
and regularly denote inclusions as
\iota^{G}_{H}\colon G\subseteq H
, where the indices denote the involved graphs.

A typed graph
G
is
finite
if both its sets of nodes
V_{G}
and of edges
E_{G}
are.

{remark}

To ease language and notation, we adopt the following conventions and assumptions.

•

In this note, we work with typed graphs and always assume a fixed type graph
\mathit{TG}
over which all graphs are typed.
Except for the type graph, we will just speak of
graphs
, and we do not introduce a special notation for typed graphs.
Thus, speaking of a graph
G
, we tacitly assume a type graph
\mathit{TG}
and a type morphism
\mathit{type}_{G}\colon G\to\mathit{TG}
to exist.
Similarly, we also regularly just speak of morphisms (and not of typed ones).

•

Via
\emptyset
we denote the
initial
or
empty graph
, also when it is considered to be typed over the given type graph
\mathit{TG}
(via the empty function).

•

By
\mathbf{Graph}_{\mathbf{TG}}
we denote the category of all graphs typed over the given type graph
\mathit{TG}
.
In this note, however, we assume that all our graphs are finite.
By
\mathbf{Graph}_{\mathbf{TG}}^{\mathbf{fin}}
we denote the category of all finite graphs typed over the graph
\mathit{TG}
.

{example}

The graph

and

.

.

3.2
Finite categories of subgraphs

Subobject transformation systems
have been developed as a tool to study different notions of causality between rule applications in (double-pushout) rewriting systems
[
2
,
13
]
.
Intuitively, one studies transformations of subobjects of a given object from an (
\mathcal{M}
-)adhesive category.
In this note, we do not care about this notion of transformation but more closely investigate the categories for which these transformations are defined.
In that, we restrict ourselves to the case where underlying objects are graphs.
For simplicity of presentation, we work with subgraphs instead of injective morphisms.

{definition}

[Subgraph. Finite category of subgraphs]
Given a typed graph
G
, a
subgraph
of it is a typed graph
H
, such that
V_{H}\subseteq V_{G}
,
E_{H}\subseteq E_{G}
and
\mathit{src}_{H}
,
\mathit{tar}_{H}
, and
\mathit{type}_{H}
are the restrictions of the according functions of
G
(in particular, with every edge
e\in E_{H}
its source and target nodes in
V_{G}
belong to
V_{H}
).
Via
H\subseteq G
we denote the subgraph relationship between graphs.

Given a finite typed graph
T
, the
finite category of subgraphs of
T
, denoted as
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
, has subgraphs
G\subseteq T
as objects and inclusions
\iota^{G}_{H}\colon G\subseteq H
between such subgraphs as morphisms.
We call
T
the
container graph
of that category.

{remark}

What we call the container graph would be called
type graph
in the terminology of
[
2
]
.
We deviate from that terminology to distinguish the container graph
T
from the type graph
\mathit{TG}
over which
T
and all graphs from
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
are typed.

Working in a finite category of subgraphs allows one to use lattice-theoretical concepts.

{remark}

In any category of subgraphs, if there exists a morphism between subgraphs
A
and
B
, this morphism is unique (by left-cancelability of monomorphisms).

Moreover, given an adhesive category
\mathcal{C}
(such as the category of graphs typed over a given type graph
\mathit{TG}
), for every object
T
of
\mathcal{C}
,
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
constitutes a distributive lattice
[
17
, Theorem 5.1 and Corollary 5.2]
.
Given two subobjects
A,B\subseteq T
, their
meet

A\cap B
in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
arises from pulling back
A\to T\leftarrow B
in
\mathcal{C}
and their
join

A\cup B
in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
from pushing out
A\leftarrow A\cap B\to B
in
\mathcal{C}
.

In our setting, where we work with subgraphs instead of equivalence classes of injections, this means that the meet
A\cap B
is literally the intersection of
A
and
B
while the join
A\cup B
is their union.

3.3
Nested conditions and constraints

Next, we define
nested conditions
and
constraints

[
12
,
20
,
7
]
.
Nested conditions provide a logic to express properties of morphisms in a category; constraints express properties of objects.
For (typed) graphs, nested conditions are expressively equivalent to standard first-order logic on graphs
[
20
,
12
]
.
Here, every level of nesting increases the expressivity
[
20
]
.

In this work, given a type graph
\mathit{TG}
and a finite container graph
T
that is typed over
\mathit{TG}
, we can consider two different categories:
The category of graphs typed over
\mathit{TG}
,
\mathbf{Graph}_{\mathbf{TG}}
, and the category
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
of subgraphs of
T
(with inclusions as morphisms).
Since every graph from
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
is also a graph typed over
\mathit{TG}
(by composition of the inclusion with the typing of
T
), we obtain two variants of defining formulas for it.

We define conditions and constraints category-theoretically and afterwards illustrate the definitions for
\mathbf{Graph}_{\mathbf{TG}}
and
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
.

{definition}
[(Nested) conditions and constraints]
In a category
\mathcal{C}
, given an object
C_{0}
, a
(nested) condition
over
C_{0}
is defined recursively as follows:

•

true
is a condition over
C_{0}
.

•

If
a_{1}\colon C_{0}\hookrightarrow C_{1}
is a monomorphism and
d
is a condition over
C_{1}
,
\exists\,(a_{1}\colon C_{0}\hookrightarrow C_{1},d)
is a condition over
C_{0}
.

•

Moreover, Boolean combinations of conditions over
C_{0}
are conditions over
C_{0}
.

In a category with a strict initial object
\emptyset
, a
(nested) constraint
is a condition over
\emptyset
.

We recursively define the
nesting level
of a condition
c
as

\displaystyle\nl(\textsf{true})

\displaystyle=0;

\displaystyle\nl(\neg d)

\displaystyle=\nl(d);

\displaystyle\nl(d_{1}\odot d_{2})

\displaystyle=\max\{\nl(d_{1}),\nl(d_{2})\}\text{, where }\odot\in\{\vee,\wedge\};

\displaystyle\nl(\exists(a_{1}\colon C_{0}\hookrightarrow C_{1},d))

\displaystyle=\nl(d)+1.

Satisfaction
of a nested condition
c
over
C_{0}
for a morphism
g\colon C_{0}\rightarrow G
, denoted as
g\models c
, is defined as follows:

•

Every morphism satisfies
true
.

•

The morphism
g
satisfies a condition of the form
c=\exists\,(a_{1}\colon C_{0}\hookrightarrow C_{1},d)
if there exists a monomorphism
q\colon C_{1}\hookrightarrow G
such that
g=q\circ a_{1}
and
q\models d
.

•

For Boolean operators, satisfaction is defined as usual.

An object
G
satisfies a constraint
c
, denoted as
G\models c
, if the unique monomorphism
\iota_{G}\colon\emptyset\hookrightarrow G
does so.

An object
G
satisfies a set of constraints
\mathcal{C}
, denoted as
G\models\mathcal{C}
, if
G
satisfies each individual constraint
c\in\mathcal{C}
.

{remark}

In the notation of conditions, we drop the domains of the involved morphisms and occurrences of
true
whenever they can be unambiguously inferred.
Moreover, we introduce
\forall\,(C_{1},d)
as an abbreviation for the condition
\neg\exists\,(C_{1},\neg d)
.
For example, we write
\forall\,(C_{1},\exists\,C_{2})
instead of
\neg\exists\,(a_{1}\colon\emptyset\hookrightarrow C_{1},\neg\exists\,(a_{2}\colon C_{1}\hookrightarrow C_{2},\textsf{true}))
.
At other places, especially where it is convenient for compact presentation of computations, we will not display the graphs of a condition but merely the identifiers of the involved morphisms, writing, e.g.,
\forall\,(a_{1},\exists\,a_{2})
.

To be able to notationally distinguish between satisfaction of constraints and conditions in the category
\mathbf{Graph}_{\mathbf{TG}}
from the one in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
(where
T
is typed over
\mathit{TG}
), we use the symbol
\models_{\mathcal{I}}
to denote satisfaction in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
and
\equiv_{\mathcal{I}}
to denote equivalence in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
.
Moreover, we denote the morphisms in conditions over graphs from
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
as
b_{i}\colon B_{i-1}\subseteq B_{i}
to visually differentiate these from (i) morphisms
a_{i}\colon C_{i-1}\hookrightarrow C_{i}
in conditions over a graph from
\mathbf{Graph}_{\mathbf{TG}}
and (ii) from inclusions
\iota^{C_{i}}_{G}\colon C_{i}\subseteq G\in\mathbf{Sub}(T)
we use to check the satisfaction of such conditions.

{example}

4
A Normal Form for Conditions in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)

In this section, we introduce a normal form for conditions in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
.
In Sect.
3.3
, we have already discussed that, given a type graph
\mathit{TG}
and a finite graph
T
typed over it, we can consider conditions and constraints over two different categories: the category of graphs typed over
\mathit{TG}
,
\mathbf{Graph}_{\mathbf{TG}}
, and the category
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
of subgraphs of
T
.
Working with conditions in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
facilitates the construction of activation diagrams and subsequent derivation of non-blocking consistency-preserving rules we develop in
[
16
]
.
In particular, these constructions depend on the normal form we develop in this section.
However, conditions in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
are inconvenient for the specification of general constraints (because of their expressing properties of concrete elements of
T
).
To address this, in Sect.
5
, we provide a way to
instantiate
a condition defined for graphs from
\mathbf{Graph}_{\mathbf{TG}}
into a condition for graphs from
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
such that its semantics is preserved.

The key feature of the normal form we construct in this section is that it avoids any nesting.
We show that in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
for every condition there is an equivalent condition without nesting. (This is in stark contrast to the case of
\mathbf{Graph}_{\mathbf{TG}}
, where every level of nesting increases the expressivity
[
20
]
.)
We start by defining
literals
, the building blocks of our normal form.

{definition}
[Literal]
Given a type graph
\mathit{TG}
and a finite graph
T
typed over it, a
literal
in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
is a condition of the form
true
,
false
,
\exists\,(b\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1},\textsf{true})
or
\neg\exists\,(b\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1},\textsf{true})
, where
B_{0},B_{1}
are subgraphs of
T
.

Next, we develop our
flattening construction
that simultaneously pushes negations inward and removes all nesting in a condition in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
.
For two positive existential quantifiers it applies the well-known equivalence
\exists\,(b_{1},\exists\,(b_{2},d))\equiv\exists\,(b_{2}\circ b_{1},d)

[
11
, Fact 4]
.
A positive existential condition that is followed by a negative one—that is, a condition of the form

\exists\,(b_{1}\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1},\neg d)
—is replaced by
\exists\,(b_{1}\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1})\wedge\neg\tilde{d}
, where
\tilde{d}
is an adaptation of
d
so that it becomes a condition over
B_{0}
(instead of
B_{1}
).
In
\mathbf{Graph}_{\mathbf{TG}}
, this generally is not a semantic equivalence because the first variant forbids an occurrence of
d
at a fixed location for
b_{1}
whereas the second variant forbids any occurrence of
d
.
For satisfaction in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
, however, both notions coincide because there can only be one inclusion of a graph into another one.
This is why nesting collapses in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
.
In the following, we first prove the just stated equivalence and then use it in our
flattening construction
, which is the crucial step to transform conditions in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
into a non-nested normal form.

{lemma}

[Correctness of extracting negation]
Given a type graph
\mathit{TG}
and a graph
T
typed over it, for any condition
d
in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
over a graph
B_{1}\subseteq T
, it holds that

\exists\,(b_{0}\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1},\neg d)\equiv_{\mathcal{I}}\exists\,(b_{0}\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1},\textsf{true})\wedge\neg\exists\,(b_{0}\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1},d).

Proof.

Let
c=\exists\,(b_{0}\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1},\neg d)
be such a condition in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
with
d
being a condition over
B_{1}\subseteq T
and let
c^{\prime}=\exists\,(b_{0}\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1},\textsf{true})\wedge\neg\exists\,(b_{0}\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1},d)
.
Since there is maximally one morphism between any two elements of
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
, namely an inclusion, the stated equivalence,
c\equiv_{\mathcal{I}}c^{\prime}
, holds if for every
\iota^{B_{0}}_{G}\colon B_{0}\subseteq G
, where
B_{0}\subseteq G\subseteq T
and
\iota^{B_{0}}_{G}
denotes the inclusion,
\iota^{B_{0}}_{G}\models_{\mathcal{I}}c
if and only if
\iota^{B_{0}}_{G}\models_{\mathcal{I}}c^{\prime}
.
By definition of the semantics of conditions, such an inclusion
\iota^{B_{0}}_{G}
satisfies
c
if and only if there exists an inclusion
\iota^{B_{1}}_{G}\colon B_{1}\subseteq G
such that
\iota^{B_{1}}_{G}\nvDash d
.

Similarly, also by definition of the semantics,
\iota^{B_{0}}_{G}
satisfies
c^{\prime}
if it satisfies
\exists\,(b_{0}\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1},\textsf{true})
and also satisfies
\neg\exists\,(b_{0}\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1},d)
.
This is the case if and only if
B_{1}\subseteq G
, i.e.,
\iota^{B_{1}}_{G}\colon B_{1}\subseteq G
exists, but simultaneously
\iota^{B_{0}}_{G}
does
not
satisfy
\exists\,(b_{0}\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1},d)
.
By existence of
\iota^{B_{1}}_{G}
this can only be the case if
\iota^{B_{1}}_{G}\nvDash d
.
Therefore,
c\equiv_{\mathcal{I}}c^{\prime}
.
∎

The lemma just stated is the crucial ingredient to proving that our following
flattening construction
preserves semantics (in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
).
In flattening, we push negations over Boolean operators and extract them from existentially bound conditions; moreover, we compose existentially bound conditions.
We end up with a condition that is non-nested and where every negation either appears in front of an existentially bound condition or at nesting level zero (where the root of the condition can be some Boolean combination of
true
).
We define flattening
over a morphism
, where we use that morphism to collect all morphisms we have to compose in the resulting condition (that somewhat eases notation).
Flattening of a condition can then just be performed by flattening that condition over the identity morphism of the graph over which the condition is defined.

{construction}

[Flattening]
Given a type graph
\mathit{TG}
, a graph
T
typed over it, a graph
B_{0}\in\mathbf{Sub}(T)
, a morphism
b_{0}
with co-domain
B_{0}
and a condition
c
over
B_{0}
in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
, we recursively define to
flatten

c
over
b_{0}
via:

•

\Flat(b_{0},\textsf{true})=\exists\,(b_{0},\textsf{true})
;

•

\Flat(b_{0},\neg\textsf{true})=\exists\,(b_{0},\textsf{false})\equiv\textsf{false}
;

•

\Flat(b_{0},d_{1}\odot d_{2})=\Flat(b_{0},d_{1})\odot\Flat(b_{0},d_{2})
, where
\odot\in\{\wedge,\vee\}
;

•

\Flat(b_{0},\neg(d_{1}\odot d_{2}))=\Flat(b_{0},\neg d_{1})\,\overline{\odot}\,\Flat(b_{0},\neg d_{2})
, where
\odot\in\{\wedge,\vee\}
,
\overline{\wedge}=\vee
, and
\overline{\vee}=\wedge
;

•

\Flat\left(b_{0},\exists\,(b_{1}\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1},d)\right)=\Flat(b_{1}\circ b_{0},d)
;

•

\Flat(b_{0},\neg\exists\,(b_{1}\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1},d)=\exists\,(b_{0},\textsf{true})\wedge\neg\Flat(b_{1}\circ b_{0},d)
; and

•

\Flat(b_{0},\neg\neg c)=\Flat(b_{0},c)
.

{example}

In practice, one regularly encounters constraints of the structure
\forall\,(b_{0}\colon\emptyset\hookrightarrow C_{1},\exists\,(b_{1}\colon C_{1}\hookrightarrow C_{2},\textsf{true}))
—compare, for instance, the constraints
c_{\mathrm{lb}}
defining the lower bound for assignments to

(Fig.

defining

When we instantiate such a constraint from

in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)

c=\neg\exists\,\big(b_{1}\colon\emptyset\subseteq B_{1},\textsf{true}\big)\vee\exists\,\big(b_{1}\colon\emptyset\subseteq B_{1},\bigvee_{j=1}^{t}\exists\,(b_{2}^{j}\colon B_{1}\subseteq B_{2}^{j},\textsf{true})\big).

Flattening such a constraint

(over the identity morphism

) proceeds as follows (where, for shorter notation, we merely note down the morphisms and omit the graphs over which they are defined):

\displaystyle\Flat(id_{\emptyset},c)

\displaystyle=\Flat\big(id_{\emptyset},\neg\exists\,(b_{1},\textsf{true})\big)\vee\Flat\big(id_{\emptyset},\exists\,(b_{1},\bigvee_{j=1}^{t}\exists\,(b_{2}^{j},\textsf{true}))\big)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\exists\,(id_{\emptyset},\textsf{true})\wedge\neg\Flat\big(id_{\emptyset},\exists\,(b_{1},\textsf{true})\big)\bigg)\vee\Flat\big(b_{1}\circ id_{\emptyset},\bigvee_{j=1}^{t}\exists\,(b_{2}^{j},\textsf{true})\big)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\exists\,(id_{\emptyset},\textsf{true})\wedge\neg\Flat\big(b_{1}\circ id_{\emptyset},\textsf{true}\big)\bigg)\vee\bigvee_{j=1}^{t}\Flat\big(b_{1}\circ id_{\emptyset},\exists\,(b_{2}^{j},\textsf{true})\big)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\exists\,(id_{\emptyset},\textsf{true})\wedge\neg\exists\,(b_{1}\circ id_{\emptyset},\textsf{true})\bigg)\vee\bigvee_{j=1}^{t}\Flat\big(b_{2}^{j}\circ b_{1}\circ id_{\emptyset},\textsf{true}\big)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\exists\,(id_{\emptyset},\textsf{true})\wedge\neg\exists\,(b_{1}\circ id_{\emptyset},\textsf{true})\bigg)\vee\bigvee_{j=1}^{t}\exists\,(b_{2}^{j}\circ b_{1}\circ id_{\emptyset},\textsf{true})

\displaystyle\equiv\neg\exists\,(b_{1},\textsf{true})\vee\bigvee_{j=1}^{t}\exists\,(b_{2}^{j}\circ b_{1},\textsf{true}).

This is exactly the structure of the constraints

Next, though intuitively clear, we prove that applying the flattening construction reduces all nesting; that is, it computes a condition of nesting level
\leq 1
.
In other words, it outputs a Boolean combination of literals.

{lemma}

[Flattening results in non-nested condition]
Given a type graph
\mathit{TG}
, a graph
T
typed over it, a morphism
b_{0}
with co-domain
B_{0}
in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
and a condition
c
over
B_{0}
, then

\nl(\Flat(b_{0},c))\leq 1.

In particular, the condition resulting from flattening over a morphism is a Boolean combination of literals.

Proof.

By the recursive definition of conditions, the recursive flattening construction always ends with applying the rules
\Flat(b_{0},\textsf{true})=\exists\,(b_{0},\textsf{true})
or
\Flat(b_{0},\neg\textsf{true})=\neg\textsf{true}
, resulting in a (negated) literal of nesting depth
\leq 1
.
Among the further rules applied during the construction, only the rule
\Flat(b_{0},\neg\exists\,(b_{1}\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1},d)=\exists\,(b_{0},\textsf{true})\wedge\neg\Flat(b_{1}\circ b_{0},d)
directly outputs a condition, namely
\exists\,(b_{0},\textsf{true})
, and
\nl(\exists\,(b_{0},\textsf{true}))=1
.
All other rules just determine the structure of the Boolean combination of the literals computed as output, but Boolean combinations of literals do not increase nesting depth.
∎

Next, we show that flattening preserves the semantics of satisfaction in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
.

1

\mathbf{Sub}(T)
.

Given a type graph
\mathit{TG}

\iota^{B_{0}}_{G}\models_{\mathcal{I}}c\iff\iota^{B_{0}}_{G}\models_{\mathcal{I}}\Flat(\mathit{id}_{B_{0}},c).

Proof.

For conditions, it generally holds that

\exists\,(b_{1},\exists\,(b_{2},d))\equiv\exists\,(b_{2}\circ b_{1},d);

(1)

and even
\exists\,(j,d)\equiv d

With that, the second claim of the theorem immediately follows from the first:

\iota^{B_{0}}_{G}\models_{\mathcal{I}}c

We prove the first claim via structural induction,

For these base cases,

Assuming the statement of the first claim to be true for some condition

\displaystyle\iota^{X}_{G}\models_{\mathcal{I}}\Flat(b_{0},\exists\,(b_{1},d))

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Def}}{\iff}\iota^{X}_{G}\models_{\mathcal{I}}\Flat(b_{1}\circ b_{0},d)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(IH)}}{\iff}\iota^{X}_{G}\models_{\mathcal{I}}\exists\,(b_{1}\circ b_{0},d)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Eq.}\penalty\ \ref{eq:composition-existential-quantification}}{\iff}\iota^{X}_{G}\models_{\mathcal{I}}\exists\,(b_{0},\exists\,(b_{1},d))

\displaystyle\iota^{X}_{G}\models_{\mathcal{I}}\Flat(b_{0},\neg\exists\,(b_{1},d))

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Def}}{\iff}\iota^{X}_{G}\models_{\mathcal{I}}\exists\,(b_{0},\textsf{true})\wedge\neg\Flat(b_{1}\circ b_{0},d)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(IH)}}{\iff}\iota^{X}_{G}\models_{\mathcal{I}}\exists\,(b_{0},\textsf{true})\wedge\neg\exists\,(b_{1}\circ b_{0},d)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Eq.}\penalty\ \ref{eq:composition-existential-quantification}}{\iff}\iota^{X}_{G}\models_{\mathcal{I}}\exists\,(b_{0},\textsf{true})\wedge\neg\exists\,(b_{0},\exists\,(b_{1},d))

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Lem.\penalty\ }\ref{lem:extracting-negation}}{\iff}\iota^{X}_{G}\models_{\mathcal{I}}\exists\,(b_{0},\neg\exists\,(b_{1},d)).\qed

The ability to flatten constraints in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
in a semantics-preserving way means we can assume all constraints and conditions to be given in some of the normal forms that are known from propositional logic.
The following corollary states this—it just combines the results stated in Lemma
4

{corollary}

Given a type graph
\mathit{TG}
, a graph
T
typed over it and a set

\mathbf{Sub}(T)

\mathbf{Sub}(T)

For our work in

That is, every constraint or condition is a conjunction of disjunctions of literals over

Considering the separate clauses (i.e., disjunctions of literals) as individual conditions further simplifies our work.
The following semantic equivalence allows us to further restrict the kinds of conditions we need to consider: we can unite conjunctions of positive literals to a single literal.

{lemma}

Given a type graph

in

.
If this equation holds, however, we compute

\displaystyle\iota^{B_{1}}_{S}\circ b\circ j_{0}

\displaystyle=\iota^{B_{1}}_{S}\circ j_{1}\circ a\circ j_{0}^{-1}\circ j_{0}

\displaystyle=\iota^{B_{1}}_{S}\circ j_{1}\circ a

\displaystyle=\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\circ j_{0},

which implies
\iota^{B_{1}}_{S}\circ b=\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}
by surjectivity of
j_{0}
.

Furthermore, as every instantiated morphism
b
satisfies
\iota^{B_{1}}_{S}\circ b=\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}
, and
\iota^{B_{0}}_{S},\iota^{B_{1}}_{S}
are both injective, also
b
is injective which makes it an embedding (as both
B_{0}
and
B_{1}
are subgraphs of
S
).
Finally, every morphism
a
that satisfies Eq.
2
is injective because
\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\circ j_{0}
and
\iota^{B_{1}}_{S}\circ j_{1}
are.
∎

In the following, we will often consider instantiations that stem from an injective morphism
q
.
We will denote such an instantiation as
\mu_{q}=(q_{\mathrm{e}},q_{\mathrm{m}})
.

The following theorem is the central result of this section, stating that conditions from
\mathbf{Graph}_{\mathbf{TG}}
can equivalently be transformed into conditions from
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
. The operation
\Inst
appearing in its statement refers to the instantiation of constraints we develop in the following.
Together with Theorem
1
this means that, without loss of generality, when working in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
one can really assume every constraint to be given as discussed at the end of Sect.
4
without losing expressivity.

2
Instantiation preserves semantics.

Given a type graph
\mathit{TG}
and a graph
T
typed over it, let
c
be a nested condition in
\mathbf{Graph}_{\mathbf{TG}}
over some graph
C_{0}
typed over
\mathit{TG}
.
Then, for every graph
S\in\mathbf{Sub}(T)
and every injective morphism
g\colon C_{0}\hookrightarrow S
,

g\models c\iff g_{\mathrm{m}}\models_{\mathcal{I}}\Inst(c,\mu_{g}^{T},\mathbf{Sub}(T))

where
\mu_{g}=(g_{\mathrm{e}},g_{\mathrm{m}})
is the instantiation of
C_{0}
in
S
that corresponds to
g
and
\mu_{g}^{T}
is the corresponding instantiation in
T
(both guaranteed to uniquely exist by Lemma
5
).

The bulk of this section is concerned with developing the instantiation process
\Inst
referred to in Theorem
2
and then proving that theorem.
This instantiation process
\Inst
relies on two intuitive ideas:
First, an existentially quantified graph
C
is replaced by a disjunction over all ways in which it can be instantiated in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
.
Second, a subcondition
\neg\exists\,(C,d)
states that at all occurrences of
C
(that are compatible with the considered instantiation of the graph
C_{0}
over which the condition is defined) the condition
d
must
not
hold.
We therefore replace such subconditions by a conjunction which, for every possible instantiation of
C
, states that the occurrence of such an instantiation implies the condition
\neg d
.
The formal treatment of that second idea is somewhat technical as we need to account for the fact that no occurrence of
C
might exist and need to take care to still obtain a nested condition over the correct graph.

{construction}

[Instantiated conditions and constraints]
Given a type graph
\mathit{TG}
and a graph
T
typed over it, let
c
be a nested condition over
C_{0}
in
\mathbf{Graph}_{\mathbf{TG}}
.
Given an instantiation
\mu_{0}=(j\colon C_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textstyle\sim}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow}\rule{0.0pt}{1.72218pt}}}B_{0},\iota^{B_{0}}_{T}\colon B_{0}\subseteq T)
of
C_{0}
in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
, the
instantiated variant of
c
(in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
with respect to
\mu_{0}
), denoted as
\Inst(c,\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T))
, is recursively constructed as follows:

•

for
c=\textsf{true}
,

\Inst\big(c,\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)=\textsf{true};

•

for
c=d_{1}\wedge d_{2}
,

\Inst\big(c,\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)=\Inst\big(d_{1},\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)\wedge\Inst\big(d_{2},\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big);

•

for
c=d_{1}\vee d_{2}
,

\Inst\big(c,\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)=\Inst\big(d_{1},\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)\vee\Inst\big(d_{2},\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big);

•

for
c=\exists\,(a_{1}\colon C_{0}\hookrightarrow C_{1},d)
, where
d
is a condition over
C_{1}
,

\Inst\big(c,\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)=\bigvee_{b_{1}\in\mathcal{J}}\exists\,\Big(b_{1}\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1},\Inst\big(d,\mu_{1},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)\Big),

where
\mathcal{J}
indexes instantiations of
a_{1}
with domain
\mu_{0}
in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
(and
\mu_{1}
denotes the respective codomain); the empty disjunction is interpreted as
\textsf{false}=\neg\textsf{true}
;

•

for
c=\neg d
, we make use of a look ahead of size 1 and set
\Inst\big(c,\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)=

\left\{\begin{aligned} \textsf{false},&\text{ for }d=\textsf{true}\\
\Inst\big(\neg e_{1},\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)\wedge\Inst\big(\neg e_{2},\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big),&\text{ for }d=e_{1}\vee e_{2}\\
\Inst\big(\neg e_{1},\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)\vee\Inst\big(\neg e_{2},\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big),&\text{ for }d=e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\\
\Inst\big(e,\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big),&\text{ for }d=\neg e\\
\bigwedge_{b_{1}\in\mathcal{J}}\Big(\neg\exists\,(b_{1}\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1},\textsf{true})\vee&\\
\exists\,\big(b_{1}\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1},\Inst(\neg e,\mu_{1},\mathbf{Sub}(T))\big)\Big),&\text{ for }d=\exists\,(a_{1}\colon C_{0}\hookrightarrow C_{1},e),\end{aligned}\right.

where, again,
\mathcal{J}
indexes instantiations of
a_{1}
with domain
\mu_{0}
,
\mu_{1}
denotes the respective codomain, and the empty conjunction is interpreted as
true
.

Given a constraint
c
, the
instantiated variant of
c
in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
, denoted as
\Inst(c,\mathbf{Sub}(T))
, is defined as

\Inst(c,\mathbf{Sub}(T))=\Inst(c,\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)),

where
\mu_{0}
is the unique instantiation of
\emptyset
in
T
.

{example}

A kind of constraint one regularly encounters in practice and that is tedious to define in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
are constraints of the structure
\forall\,(C_{1},\exists\,C_{2})
.
In our running example of the CRA problem, this concerns the constraint
c_{\mathrm{lb}}
, requiring every

and

to be assigned to at least one
Class
and constraint
c_{\mathrm{priv}}
, requiring
Attributes
to be private (see Figs.
5
and
9
in Example
3.3
).
Instantiating such a constraint amounts to the following:
First,
\forall\,(C_{1},\exists\,C_{2})
is syntactic sugar for
\neg\exists\,(a_{1}\colon\emptyset\hookrightarrow C_{1},\neg\exists\,(a_{2}\colon C_{1}\hookrightarrow C_{2},\textsf{true}))
.
Thus, by construction, we obtain

\begin{split}\Inst(c_{\mathrm{lb}},\mathbf{Sub}(T))=&\bigwedge_{b_{1}\in\mathcal{J}}\bigg(\neg\exists\,(b_{1}\colon\emptyset\subseteq B_{1},\textsf{true})\vee\\
&\exists\,\Big(b_{1}\colon\emptyset\subseteq B_{1},\Inst\big(\neg(\neg\exists\,(a_{2}\colon C_{1}\hookrightarrow C_{2},\textsf{true})),\mu_{1},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)\Big)\bigg),\end{split}

where
\mathcal{J}
indexes morphisms
b_{1}
from
\emptyset
to instantiations of
C_{1}
in
T
.
Continuing the computation, we receive

\displaystyle\Inst(\neg(\neg\exists\,(a_{2}\colon C_{1}\hookrightarrow C_{2},\textsf{true}),\mu_{1},\mathbf{Sub}(T))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Inst(\exists\,(a_{2}\colon C_{1}\hookrightarrow C_{2},\textsf{true}),\mu_{1},\mathbf{Sub}(T))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigvee_{b_{2}\in\mathcal{J}_{b_{1}}}\Big(\exists\,\big(b_{2}\colon B_{1}\subseteq B_{2},\Inst(\textsf{true},\mu_{2},\mathbf{Sub}(T))\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigvee_{b_{2}\in\mathcal{J}_{b_{1}}}\exists\,\big(b_{2}\colon B_{1}\subseteq B_{2},\textsf{true}\big),

where
\mathcal{J}_{b_{1}}
indexes those instantiations of the morphism
a_{2}
whose domain is instantiated to the co-domain of
b_{1}
.
Thus, in total, we receive a conjunction of constraints of the form

\neg\exists\,(b_{1}\colon\emptyset\subseteq B_{1},\textsf{true})\vee\exists\,\Big(b_{1}\colon\emptyset\subseteq B_{1},\bigvee_{b_{2}\in\mathcal{J}_{b_{1}}}\exists\,\big(b_{2}\colon B_{1}\subseteq B_{2},\textsf{true}\big)\Big),

where
B_{1}
ranges over the possibilities to instantiate
C_{1}
in
T
and
B_{2}
over those to instantiate
C_{2}
in a way conformant to the instantiation chosen for
B_{1}
.
Given
T
as introduced in Fig.
4
in Example
3.2
, for
c_{\mathrm{lb}}
the graph
C_{1}
can be instantiated in three different ways, mapping its single node of type
Method
to one of the
Methods M1, M2, M3
of
T
.
For each such instantiation of
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
(and the morphism from
C_{1}
to
C_{2}
) can be instantiated conformantly by mapping its
Method
in the same way, its
Class
to one of the
Classes C1, …, C6
of
T
, and the
encapsulates
-edge accordingly.

Before considering how instantiating a condition affects its semantics, we shortly clarify that an instantiated condition still consists of finitely many graphs; in particular, this ensures that the conjunction / disjunction indexed by a set
\mathcal{J}
of instantiations is well-defined.
It also clarifies the severity of the growth of the number of graphs occurring in a condition when instantiating it.

{proposition}
[Order of number of instantiated constraints/conditions]
Given a type graph
\mathit{TG}
and a finite graph
T
that is typed over it, let
c
be some condition over a graph
C_{0}
that is typed over
\mathit{TG}
, and let
\lvert c\rvert
denote the number of morphisms occurring in
c
.
Then, for every instantiation
\mu_{0}
of
C_{0}
in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
,

\lvert\Inst(c,\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T))\rvert\in\mathcal{O}(\lvert c\rvert\cdot\lvert T\rvert^{\lvert C_{\mathrm{max}}\rvert}),

where
C_{\mathrm{max}}
is the largest graph that occurs in
c
and
\lvert X\rvert
denotes the number of elements of a graph
X
.
In particular,
\Inst(c,\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T))
is a nested condition.

Proof.

Instantiating
c
at
\mu_{0}
, means to, beginning at the fixed instantiation of
C_{0}
that is determined by
\mu_{0}
, instantiate every morphism that occurs in
c
in every possible way that accords with the currently considered instantiation of the domain of the morphism that is to be instantiated.
In that, by Lemma
5
, every instantiation of the co-domain of a morphism corresponds to exactly one instantiation of the underlying morphism, namely one where the domain is instantiated accordingly.
Hence, the number of possible instantiations of the co-domain provide an upper bound on how often an occurring morphism is instantiated.
As the instantiations of a graph
C
in
T
correspond to injective morphisms from
C
to
T
(see Lemma
5
), there are maximally

\sum_{C\text{ codomain in }c}\binom{\lvert T\rvert}{\lvert C\rvert}\cdot\lvert C\rvert!

many (this is the case were
T
and
C
are sets and typing and non-existence of necessary edges do not prevent identifications).
The inequality
\binom{n}{k}\leq\frac{n^{k}}{k!}
and replacing the size of the graphs from
c
with the size of the largest one yield the claim.
(Note that we can ignore the fact that instantiating a negated morphism can lead to it occurring twice in the instantiated condition as this just introduces the constant factor 2.)

Since the number of instantiations is finite for every morphism, the conjunctions and disjunctions in an instantiated condition are well-defined.
∎

Next, we formulate a lemma that ensures that the treatment of negation in the process of instantiation is (internally) meaningful.
We will then use that lemma in relating the satisfaction of a condition with the
\mathcal{I}
-satisfaction of its instantiation.

{lemma}

[Negation in instantiation]
Given a type graph
\mathit{TG}
and a graph
T
typed over it, let
c=\neg d
be a nested condition over
C_{0}
that is also typed over
\mathit{TG}
.
Then, for any instantiation
\mu_{0}=(j\colon C_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textstyle\sim}}{{\smash{\longrightarrow}\rule{0.0pt}{1.72218pt}}}B_{0},\iota^{B_{0}}_{T}\colon B_{0}\subseteq T)
of
C_{0}
in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
and any inclusion
\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\colon B_{0}\subseteq S
, it holds that

\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\models_{\mathcal{I}}\Inst\big(d,\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)\iff\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\nvDash_{\mathcal{I}}\Inst\big(\neg d,\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big).

Proof.

We prove the statement via structural induction.
With regard to the inductive basis, for
d=\textsf{true}
, clearly

\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\nvDash_{\mathcal{I}}\Inst\big(\neg d,\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)=\textsf{false}\iff\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\models_{\mathcal{I}}\Inst\big(d,\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)=\textsf{true}.

Assuming the statement to hold for conditions
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
(induction hypothesis), for
c=\neg d
with
d=e_{1}\wedge e_{2}
we can compute

\displaystyle\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\nvDash_{\mathcal{I}}\Inst\big(\neg(e_{1}\wedge e_{2}),\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\nvDash_{\mathcal{I}}\Big(\Inst\big(\neg e_{1},\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)\vee\Inst\big(\neg e_{1},\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\nvDash_{\mathcal{I}}\Inst\big(\neg e_{1},\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)\text{ and }\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\nvDash_{\mathcal{I}}\Inst\big(\neg e_{1},\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\models_{\mathcal{I}}\Inst\big(e_{1},\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)\text{ and }\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\models_{\mathcal{I}}\Inst\big(e_{1},\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\models_{\mathcal{I}}\Inst\big(e_{1}\wedge e_{2},\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big);

an analogous computation shows the case for
d=e_{1}\vee e_{2}
.

Assuming the statement to hold for a condition
e
, for
c=\neg d
with
d=\neg e
we compute

\displaystyle\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\nvDash_{\mathcal{I}}\Inst\big(\neg\neg e,\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)

\displaystyle\iff\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\nvDash_{\mathcal{I}}\Inst\big(e,\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)

\displaystyle\iff\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\models\Inst\big(\neg e,\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big).

And finally, assuming the statement to hold for a condition
e
(over a graph
C_{1}
), for
c=\neg d
with
d=\exists\,(a_{1}\colon C_{0}\hookrightarrow C_{1},e)
we compute

\displaystyle\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\nvDash_{\mathcal{I}}\Inst\big(\neg\exists\,(a_{1}\colon C_{0}\hookrightarrow C_{1},e),\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\nvDash_{\mathcal{I}}\bigwedge_{b_{1}\in\mathcal{J}}\neg\exists\,(b_{1}\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1},\textsf{true})\vee

\displaystyle\exists\,\big(b_{1}\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1},\Inst(\neg e,\mu_{1},\mathbf{Sub}(T))\big)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\text{there exists }b_{1}\in\mathcal{J}\text{ s.t. }\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\nvDash_{\mathcal{I}}\neg\exists\,(b_{1}\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1},\textsf{true})\vee

\displaystyle\exists\,\big(b_{1}\colon B_{0}\subseteq B_{1},\Inst(\neg e,\mu_{1},\mathbf{Sub}(T))\big)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\text{there exists }b_{1}\in\mathcal{J}\text{ s.t. there exists an inclusion }\iota^{B_{1}}_{S}\colon B_{1}\subseteq S\text{ s.t.}

\displaystyle\iota^{B_{1}}_{S}\circ b_{1}=\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\text{ and, for every such inclusion, }\iota^{B_{1}}_{S}\nvDash_{\mathcal{I}}\Inst\big(\neg e,\mu_{1},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\text{there exists }b_{1}\in\mathcal{J}\text{ s.t. there exists an inclusion }\iota^{B_{1}}_{S}\colon B_{1}\subseteq S\text{ s.t.}

\displaystyle\iota^{B_{1}}_{S}\circ b_{1}=\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\text{ and, for this inclusion, }\iota^{B_{1}}_{S}\nvDash_{\mathcal{I}}\Inst\big(\neg e,\mu_{1},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\text{there exists }b_{1}\in\mathcal{J}\text{ s.t. there exists an inclusion }\iota^{B_{1}}_{S}\colon B_{1}\subseteq S\text{ s.t.}

\displaystyle\iota^{B_{1}}_{S}\circ b_{1}=\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\text{ and, for this inclusion, }\iota^{B_{1}}_{S}\models_{\mathcal{I}}\Inst\big(e,\mu_{1},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\iota^{B_{0}}_{S}\models_{\mathcal{I}}\Inst\big(\exists\,(a_{1}\colon C_{0}\hookrightarrow C_{1},e),\mu_{0},\mathbf{Sub}(T)\big).

In this last computation, for the fourth equivalence we use the fact that there can only be one inclusion of a graph
B_{1}
in a graph
S
—that is, only one morphism from
B_{1}
to
S
in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
.
∎

6
Conclusion

In this note, we have presented a semantics-preserving translation of nested conditions and constraints from the category
\mathbf{Graph}_{\mathbf{TG}}
(of graphs typed over a given type graph
\mathit{TG}
) into nested conditions and constraints formulated in a category
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
, the category of subgraphs of a fixed finite graph
T
typed over
\mathit{TG}
(Theorem
2
).
In
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
then, every nested condition and constraint can be equivalently transformed into a non-nested normal form (Theorem
1
and Corollary
4
).
Together, both results show that in
\mathbf{Sub}(T)
first-order properties can be expressed by the non-nested fragment of the formalism of nested conditions and constraints.

Our results are not surprising—we work in a fixed, finite universe and can replace nesting by explicitly enumerating all concrete situations in a formula.
This suggests two immediate avenues for generalisation:
First, in our proofs we do not make use of the fact that our objects are graphs; what we critically use, though, is the fact that the graph
T
, whose subgraphs form our category of interest, is finite and, therefore, there is only a finite number of subgraphs.
This suggests that our results should simply transfer from graphs to the far more general setting of categories of subobjects of a container object that stems from any
finitary

\mathcal{M}
-adhesive category
[
9
]
.
Moreover, in this setting of a fixed, finite universe of elements, higher-order properties (like connectedness or acyclicity) should also be expressible by the non-nested fragment of nested conditions and constraints.
Thus, while this requires additional, new techniques for translation, we are confident that the results presented here transfer also to logics on graphs that go beyond first-order properties as, e.g.,
[
19
,
18
,
3
