BSepDB: a database of bacterial selenoprotein genes and proteins
Authors: Zhengyue Hong, Hengtao Li, Yan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSelenoproteins represent a structurally and functionally distinct class of proteins that contribute to cellular antioxidant defense and a wide range of other essential biological processes in specific organisms. They contain the 21st amino acid selenocysteine (Sec) in their active sites, which is encoded by an in-frame UGA stop codon through a translational reprogramming mechanism. Due to the dual functionality of the UGA codon, selenoprotein genes are frequently misidentified and misannotated in genomic databases, especially in bacteria where selenoproteins exhibit greater complexity and diversity compared to their eukaryotic counterparts. Thus, a comprehensive resource is urgently required to enable accurate identification of selenoprotein genes across diverse bacterial genomes.ResultsWe have developed BSepDB, a specialized database dedicated to systematic curation of bacterial selenoprotein genes and proteins, providing an exhaustive resource for the research community. The current version (BSepDB v. 1.0) encompasses over 57,000 selenoprotein entries derived from 16,621 bacterial species, representing the largest and most taxonomically diverse repository of bacterial selenoproteins to date. To facilitate intuitive data exploration, BSepDB offers multiple user-friendly interfaces, such as interactive browsing and search tools, an integrated BLAST search function, and options for bulk data download. Additionally, the curated entries in BSepDB are cross-referenced with established genomic databases (e.g., GenBank and RefSeq) to improve the accuracy of selenoprotein annotations in large-scale genomic projects.ConclusionsBSepDB serves as a valuable resource for researchers investigating selenium utilization and the functional diversity of selenoproteins in bacteria. Background

Selenium (Se) is an essential trace element required for the growth, development, and metabolic processes of a wide range of organisms, including bacteria, algae, and higher animals [1]. This micronutrient is predominantly incorporated into selenoproteins via the 21st amino acid, selenocysteine (Sec), thereby participating in diverse biological pathways [2, 3]. The majority of selenoproteins are involved in critical redox reactions, performing functions including peroxide scavenging, cellular damage prevention, and hormone biosynthesis.

The biosynthesis of Sec and its co-translational insertion into selenoproteins have been largely elucidated, involving a specialized molecular machinery that recodes in-frame UGA codons (normally stop signals) to serve as Sec codons [4, 5]. In bacteria, this mechanism primarily comprises a Sec-encoding UGA codon (Sec-UGA), a Sec insertion sequence (SECIS) element (a cis-acting stem-loop structure located immediately downstream of the Sec-UGA codon), a Sec-specific tRNASec, and several trans-acting factors including Sec synthase (SelA), Sec-specific elongation factor (SelB), and selenophosphate synthase (SelD) [6–8]. In eukaryotes and archaea, their SECIS elements are located in the 3’ untranslated regions (3’-UTRs) of selenoprotein mRNAs. Moreover, additional steps and enzymes, such as O-phosphoseryl-tRNASec kinase and SECIS-binding protein 2 are needed for the incorporation of Sec into selenoproteins [9, 10]. Selenoprotein diversity and abundance vary significantly across domains of life, with bacteria harboring over 100 distinct selenoprotein families—nearly three times the number identified in eukaryotes [11].

Given that UGA codon primarily functions as a translational termination signal, a key challenge in accurately identifying and annotating selenoprotein genes in genomic sequences is distinguishing Sec-UGA from standard stop codon. Current genome annotation pipelines rely on canonical stop codons (UAA, UAG, and UGA) to define open reading frames (ORFs) and predict protein-coding regions. However, in selenoprotein-containing organisms, UGA’s dual role causes ambiguity, leading to systematic errors in automated annotation tools and complicating manual curation. Consequently, most genome projects and databases suffer from misannotated selenoprotein genes, such as truncated annotations (premature termination due to misinterpretation of Sec-UGA) and false negatives (incorrectly classified as pseudogenes or entirely overlooked). To date, most accurately annotated selenoprotein sequences in public repositories like GenBank are manually submitted by researchers, often lacking comprehensive genomic context. In contrast, correctly and fully annotated selenoprotein genes remain scarce in continually expanding genomic databases.

In recent years, the rapid advancement of bioinformatics technologies has facilitated the development of multiple computational approaches for predicting selenoprotein genes in eukaryotic and prokaryotic genomes, exhibiting remarkable efficacy in discovering novel selenoprotein genes. These methods fall into two main categories: (i) SECIS-dependent approaches, which identify potential SECIS elements by using predefined sequence and structural features to predict selenoprotein genes, such as SECISearch (for eukaryotes) and bSECISearch (for bacteria) [12, 13]; (ii) SECIS-independent approaches, which rely on cysteine(Cys)/Sec homology-based sequence analysis to identify selenoprotein genes [14–16]. By utilizing these methods, researchers can estimate an organism’s selenoproteome (the complete set of selenoproteins). Together with standard gene annotation pipelines and manual curation, these computational tools serve as the foundation of the existing eukaryotic selenoprotein databases, such as SelenoDB (animal and human selenoproteins) and SPDB (algal selenoproteins) [17, 18].

Compared to eukaryotes, bacterial selenoprotein biology remains a highly challenging field. Firstly, due to inherent limitations (e.g., lack of conserved features in bacterial SECIS elements), current mainstream methods for predicting bacterial selenoprotein genes suffer from high false-positive rates and time-consuming workflows, leaving many selenoprotein genes in newly sequenced bacteria unidentified. While a deep learning-based algorithm has recently been developed for fast and efficient prediction of bacterial selenoprotein genes [19], its performance across a broader range of bacterial species requires further validation. Secondly, the ongoing discovery of novel bacterial selenoprotein families and subfamilies reveals a far greater level of complexity in terms of abundance, functional roles, and phylogenetic distribution when compared with eukaryotes, rendering a complete characterization of the selenoproteome extremely difficult. Thirdly, the phylogenetic and functional links among bacterial selenoproteins remain poorly understood, hindering studies on the evolution of Sec codon usage. Lastly, the uneven and dynamic Sec utilization across various bacterial species has obscured the evolutionary origins of Sec in the genetic code, making bacterial selenoprotein biogenesis a critical research area. To address these challenges, comprehensive identification and analysis of selenoprotein genes in diverse bacterial genomes are urgently needed. However, no systematic database currently exists specifically for bacterial selenoproteins. Thus, establishing such a resource is essential for advancing our understanding of their diversity, functions, and evolutionary patterns.

In this study, we introduce BSepDB (Bacterial Selenoprotein Database), the first comprehensive repository dedicated to selenoprotein genes and proteins in bacteria. By integrating curated data from diverse selenoprotein families and representative species across major bacterial lineages, BSepDB offers user-friendly online access and analytical tools to support research endeavors. As a long-term project, BSepDB aims to provide reliable selenoprotein annotations for bacteria, fostering new discoveries into the biological role of Se in this kingdom.

Materials and methods

Genomic data collection

Complete and near-complete bacterial genomic sequences were retrieved from the GenBank database of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (as of December 2024). Considering the substantial redundancy of strain-level genomes for the same species in the raw data, we applied the following filtering criteria to improve analytical efficiency: (i) Removing duplicate taxonomic units (TaxIDs) while prioritizing species with fully sequenced genomes; (ii) Retaining unnamed species (e.g., those designated as “sp.” or “bacterium”); (iii) Excluding species lacking downloadable genomic data from NCBI. Following these criteria, we obtained 64,762 species-level genomes (GenBank accession numbers are provided in Additional File 1: Table S1). These species were then classified into 169 distinct taxonomic lineages at the phylum, class, and/or order levels according to the taxonomic framework established by the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB, Release 10-RS220), ensuring biologically meaningful categorization at appropriate taxonomic ranks [20].

Identification of Sec biosynthetic system in bacteria

We used the protein sequences of Escherichia coli SelA (WP_349147496.1), SelB (WP_137581305.1), and SelD (WP_024226353.1) as queries to identify key components of the Sec biosynthetic pathway in bacteria. Given that SelD is a selenoprotein in many organisms and is frequently misannotated in public protein databases (e.g., GenBank and RefSeq), conventional protein-protein alignment methods such as blastp often fail to detect it reliably. Thus, we employed the blast package (version 2.16.0) to conduct tblastn searches to identify a primary set of homologs of Sec biosynthetic genes with an e-value ≤ 0.01 and alignment coverage ≥ 25% for either query or subject sequences, as adopted in prior studies [19, 21]. For each identified nucleotide sequence, a plausible ORF was predicted using OrfFinder (Linux x64) [22] with the following settings: the standard genetic code was employed, the minimum ORF length was set to 200 nucleotides, and partial ORFs at sequence ends were included to ensure comprehensive detection. When multiple overlapping ORF candidates were identified, the longest ORF was selected. Within each taxonomic lineage (phylum or class level), we conducted iterative tblastn searches using selected ORF-encoding protein sequences from the same or closely related lineages in the primary dataset to detect additional homologs. We also utilized JackHMMER, an iterative profile hidden Markov model (profile-HMM) search tool that automatically constructs and refines query-specific HMMs during multiple database search rounds, to identify distantly related homologs with an e-value ≤ 0.05 [23]. All retrieved protein sequences were analyzed using the rpsblast program to confirm orthology by examining conserved domain architecture (e.g., COG (2024 update) or Pfam (version 38.1) annotations) that matched those of the query proteins [24]. Furthermore, we confirmed the presence of SelD using strategies designed for selenoprotein gene identification (described in detail below). Finally, a species was considered to possess the Sec biosynthetic system if it contained at least two key Sec pathway genes.

Identification of selenoprotein genes in Sec-utilizing bacteria

We first compiled a comprehensive dataset of previously reported prokaryotic selenoprotein families and subfamilies and their representative sequences (Additional File 1: Table S2) [11, 16, 17, 19, 25–27]. To identify all known selenoprotein genes in Sec-utilizing bacteria identified above, we employed a well-established approach that was previously validated for selenoprotein gene discovery in genomic datasets [15, 28, 29]. Briefly, representative sequences from each bacterial selenoprotein family or subfamily were used as queries to search against genomic sequences of species containing the Sec biosynthetic pathway via tblastn (e-value ≤ 0.01). The tblastn output for each selenoprotein sequence was parsed to retain only alignments containing a Sec/TGA pair (i.e., Sec in the query aligns with a TGA stop codon in the target genomic sequence). For each nucleotide sequence containing TGA codon, a plausible ORF was predicted using a custom Python script, which was developed to enhance the accuracy of selenoprotein identification, including sequences with two or more Sec residues. Sequences containing ambiguous ‘X’ residues were excluded from further analysis because they typically indicate low-quality genome assembly. Moreover, within each taxonomic lineage, additional tblastn searches were performed using the newly identified selenoprotein sequences to detect distantly related homologs missed in the initial screen. To further validate the accuracy of selenoprotein family assignments and ensure correct identification of all Sec residues, we conducted multiple sequence alignments using ClustalW (version 2.1) with default parameters [30]. After eliminating redundant sequences, the bSECISearch tool [12] was applied to confirm the presence of canonical bacterial SECIS elements in selenoprotein genes, thereby ensuring the reliability of selenoprotein gene identification.

Database implementation

BSepDB was implemented using a free and open-source web server architecture following the Model-Template-View (MTV) design pattern. The frontend of BSepDB is constructed using a combination of HTML5, CSS3, and JavaScript, ensuring responsive and interactive user interfaces. The backend of the database is built on the Python Flask framework, a powerful web framework that provides RESTful APIs to support core functionalities such as data retrieval, processing, and visualization. All annotations in BSepDB are stored in a SQLite database, serving as core data to ensure the database’s operational integrity. The Model layer utilizes the popular SQLAlchemy ORM (object-relational mapping) system to declare database schemas while simplifying database operations. The Template layer employs Jinja2 (a widely used templating engine in Python web development) to dynamically compose HTML views through template inheritance and context-aware variable injection. The View layer is responsible for handling HTTP requests, enforcing business rules, coordinating database operations, and rendering dynamic responses.

The BLAST search functionality was established by using NCBI BLAST+ (version 2.16.0+) as the backend engine and then integrated via Python, which supports sequence similarity searches against the datasets stored in BSepDB with customizable parameters (such as the e-value threshold and scoring matrix). All curated datasets in BSepDB are available for download in standard FASTA format through the web interface.

Results

Content of BSepDB

The BSepDB database represents the largest and most extensive collection of bacterial selenoproteins to date. It contains 57,630 annotated selenoproteins classified into 111 distinct families and subfamilies. These selenoproteins are derived from 16,621 bacterial species possessing the Sec biosynthetic pathway, covering 88 taxonomic lineages (at the phylum or class level according to the GTDB classification system). While 87.4% of selenoprotein entries in our dataset contain canonical SECIS elements which is consistent with previous observations [12], the absence of these structures in a number of selenoprotein genes suggests the presence of uncharacterized sequence/structural features. These cases may provide valuable opportunities to expand bacterial SECIS element models to capture broader biological diversity, including lineage-specific adaptations or novel Sec incorporation mechanisms. Notably, formate dehydrogenase alpha subunit emerges as the most prominent bacterial selenoprotein family, while Pseudomonadota stands out as the leading bacterial phylum. As a novel resource for selenoprotein research, BSepDB provides a well-structured framework for advancing our understanding of the distribution, diversity, and evolutionary relationships of selenoproteins in bacteria. When compared to existing databases such as dbTEU (a protein database dedicated to trace element utilization) [25] and Recode (a database focusing on translational recoding events like Sec-UGA recoding) [31], BSepDB significantly expands the scope of bacterial selenoprotein data, offering an unparalleled repository that functions as an important library for researchers who are exploring Se biology in bacteria. Furthermore, it marks the first comprehensive effort to characterize the bacterial selenoproteome, establishing a foundational and critical reference for unraveling the intricate roles of Se across diverse bacterial taxa. It is worth noting that many selenoproteins have also been annotated in NCBI protein databases such as GenBank and RefSeq. Researchers may retrieve non-redundant sequence data for all annotated selenoproteins from the RefSeq database by using targeted search queries in NCBI’s Protein portal (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein) with the following syntax: selenocysteine[Properties] AND WP_000000000:WP_999999999[Accession]. However, given that the current genome annotation pipeline for selenoprotein genes is likely to be imperfect, especially for metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) that often have relatively low coverage, a specialized bacterial selenoprotein database like BSepDB is still necessary.

Database interface and utility

To enhance user efficiency when accessing BSepDB, we developed a user-friendly web interface with a series of navigation menus, including Home, Browse by Species, Browse by Selenoprotein Family, Search, BLAST, Statistics, Download, and Help.

The “Home” page of BSepDB serves as a dynamic gateway to the database, presenting its purpose, scope, and key statistics including the total number of selenoprotein entries, protein family classifications, and the taxonomic diversity of represented bacterial species.

Database browse

BSepDB provides two complementary browsing modes to facilitate targeted data exploration: (i) Browse by species (Navigating selenoprotein distribution across bacterial taxonomy and species); and (ii) Browse by selenoprotein family (examining selenoprotein distribution through protein family and subfamily classifications).

The “Browse by Species” page offers an intuitive interface for exploring selenoprotein data across different bacterial taxonomy via an interactive phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2A). This tree serves as a visual framework that clarifies both taxonomic hierarchy and evolutionary relationships, with species counts displayed for each branch. A built-in search box is also incorporated, enabling rapid location of specific species of interest. Clicking on any branch generates a detailed table of species-specific selenoprotein entries. Alternatively, by clicking the “Browse all species” option in the upper-right corner of the interface, a comprehensive table of all bacterial selenoproteins is produced. In both of these tables, rows represent individual selenoproteins, while columns correspond to standardized data fields outlined in Table 1. Most columns support dynamic sorting for efficient data exploration. Each accession number links directly to GenBank records, providing users with easy access to supplementary genetic information. Sequence visualization is facilitated through “Show sequence” in the “Protein Sequence” and “Nucleotide Sequence” columns, with Sec (U) residues and their corresponding Sec-TGA codons highlighted in red. The NCBI RefSeq accession IDs are provided for selenoproteins with existing RefSeq annotations, enabling direct access to comprehensive reference data. For entries with available SECIS element information, clicking red-hyperlinked “Show SECIS” in the “Canonical SECIS element” column displays the associated SECIS structure in dot-bracket notation; otherwise, “N/A” is indicated. For example, to check selenoproteins across all species in Aquificota/Aquificia, users can: (i) locate the branch in the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2A), (ii) click to generate a species-specific table for selenoprotein entries (Fig. 2B), (iii) sort by parameters (e.g., species name, selenoprotein name, domain), and (iv) access GenBank annotations, domains, nucleotide/protein sequences, or SECIS structures as needed (Fig. 2C–G).

Database search

The “Search” page is designed to boost data retrieval efficiency by integrating two distinct search options: (i) predefined search based on species and selenoprotein family, and (ii) keyword search. In the predefined search mode, users can perform hierarchical taxonomic filtering by moving through sequential ranks: phylum→class→order→species. The family and genus levels are omitted to maintain a clean and user-friendly interface, thereby simplifying navigation and preventing users from being overwhelmed by excessive detail. When a higher taxonomic rank (such as phylum) is selected, all descendant species under that rank are automatically included. Selenoprotein families can be chosen using a multi-select dropdown; if no family is selected, all selenoprotein families are included by default. In the keyword search mode, users can input free-text queries to explore BSepDB, with the option to apply field-specific restrictions (e.g., limiting searches to species name or selenoprotein name). Both search methods display results in a uniform tabular format identical to the detailed view for the “Browse by Species” page. This consistency ensures smooth and effortless navigation throughout the database’s various functions.

For instance, to view all selenoproteins in Desulfosarcina widdelii, a sulfate-reducing bacterium belonging to Desulfobacterota/Desulfobacteria, users can choose either search method. Option 1 (predefined search) involves selecting Desulfobacterota in the phylum field, Desulfobacteria in the class field, and D. widdelii in the species field (Fig. 4A), followed by optionally choosing specific selenoprotein families (default: all families included) and clicking “Search”. Option 2 (keyword search) requires entering “Desulfosarcina widdelii” or just “widdelii” while restricting the search field to “Species name” (Fig. 4B), then clicking “Search”. Both methods return identical results showing all 39 known selenoproteins in D. widdelii (Fig. 4C).

BLAST search

The “BLAST” page provides an integrated platform for sequence similarity searches against the entire BSepDB repository, powered by the NCBI BLAST+ toolkit (Fig. 5A). By submitting queries in FASTA format (protein or nucleotide sequences), users can choose from five standard BLAST algorithms (blastp, blastn, blastx, tblastn, or tblastx) and customize parameters such as e-value threshold, scoring matrix, and maximum number of target sequences. Results are presented in a table displaying alignment statistics such as alignment score, percentage identity, query coverage, and e-value, with direct links to detailed pairwise alignment views and corresponding database entries for further analysis.

For example, to investigate whether the arsenate reductase-like protein from Ornithinimicrobium murale (WP_114906935.1) has selenoprotein homologs in BSepDB, users can submit the protein sequence in FASTA format via direct input or file upload on the BSepDB BLAST page, select the blastp algorithm (with optional parameter adjustments such as e-value threshold), and click the “BLAST” button to initiate the search (Fig. 5A). The resulting table displays all detected homologs with key alignment metrics, while detailed pairwise alignments reveal that selenoprotein homologs are present as the catalytic Sec residue in selenoprotein sequences aligns specifically with Cys residue in the query protein, indicating a functional relationship between Se- and sulfur-dependent enzyme variants (Fig. 5B).

Database statistics

The “Statistics” page of BSepDB offers a real-time overview of the database’s composition, including basic statistics, species distribution, phylum-level taxonomy, and selenoprotein family diversity, through interactive visualizations (e.g., pie charts, bar graphs, and hover-over tooltips displaying exact counts and percentages) that dynamically update as new entries are added. Data consistency is maintained via a version-controlled synchronization pipeline that detects modifications by comparing source file timestamps during imports, triggering incremental updates to merge new or altered data while preserving existing records without full reprocessing.

Database download

The “Download” page provides users with direct access to a complete collection of selenoprotein sequences (FASTA format), encompassing both nucleotide (gene) and amino acid (protein) datasets. Each sequence is annotated with structured metadata comprising selenoprotein names, species and strains, GenBank accessions, genomic coordinates (contig/scaffold, start/end positions, and reading frame), and RefSeq IDs (where available), thereby facilitating immediate integration into other bioinformatics workflows as needed.

Database user guide

The “Help” page functions as a user guide for BSepDB, enabling users to quickly master core functionalities of the database through structured tutorials.

Database website

We have established a web-based platform for BSepDB, which allows researchers worldwide to remotely query and retrieve comprehensive selenoprotein data via an intuitive and easy-to-navigate interface. The platform is fully accessible at the URL: https://bsepdb.metalbioinfolab.net.

Discussion

Selenoproteins are widely present in both bacteria and eukaryotes, serving as the main functional carriers of Se [32–34]. To date, bacterial selenoproteins have been reported to exhibit greater diversity and abundance compared to their eukaryotic counterparts [11]. This disparity may be attributed to the unique evolutionary pressures and metabolic requirements that bacteria face in their diverse ecological niches, which have driven the evolution and expansion of selenoprotein families in these organisms. However, the identification and annotation of bacterial selenoprotein genes have lagged significantly behind those in eukaryotes. A critical and urgent issue is the absence of a specialized database that can provide reliable reference source for bacterial selenoprotein genes. In recent times, advancements in bioinformatics and artificial intelligence have accelerated research on bacterial Se biology, leading to the discovery of more selenoprotein families and subfamilies in a wide range of bacterial species [11, 19, 26, 27]. This progress highlights the immediate need for a comprehensive and systematic database dedicated to bacterial selenoproteins. Such a resource would act as a knowledge repository and essential support for investigating the classification, functional roles, and evolutionary patterns of selenoproteins, as well as for mapping the entire selenoproteomes of various Sec-utilizing bacteria.

In the present study, by gathering all previously reported bacterial selenoprotein families and subfamilies, we analyzed over 64,000 sequenced bacterial genomes and successfully identified approximately 58,000 selenoprotein genes in more than 16,000 species that utilize Sec. It should be noted that a small number of previously reported selenoprotein families have not been identified in the organisms examined here, likely owing to either their actual absence in these species or exclusion resulting from the constraints implemented, such as ORF determination or the quality of genomic sequencing data. Based on these findings, we have developed BSepDB, a user-friendly database tailored to simplify access to bacterial selenoprotein information. The platform supports intuitive browsing based on taxonomy and protein families, targeted searching with multiple filters, and efficient bulk downloading of annotated sequence data, which offers researchers with a highly valuable resource for exploring the distribution, diversity, and functional annotations of selenoproteins across bacteria. Furthermore, BSepDB integrates an embedded BLAST search tool to enhance its practical utility for identifying selenoprotein genes in novel genomic sequences. For instance, by submitting a nucleotide sequence query, users can rapidly determine whether the sequence contains potential selenoprotein genes and accurately locate their positions within the sequence. Thus, BSepDB has the potential to serve as a crucial foundation for the development of standardized selenoprotein annotation protocols in large-scale bacterial genomic and metagenomic studies.

BSepDB is an ongoing project aimed at establishing a systematic and highly reliable platform for bacterial selenoprotein research, encompassing both a manually curated database and a user-friendly web interface. Here, we present an initial yet comprehensive version of this resource. In the future, we intend to regularly update our database, tentatively on a bi-annual basis. This will involve incorporating newly discovered selenoprotein families and recently sequenced representative bacterial genomes (from GenBank or GTDB), realigning our selenoprotein sequences with those annotated by NCBI, and integrating more sequence analysis and structural modeling tools to enhance the accuracy, functionality, and utility of the database.

Conclusions

In the current study, we have constructed BSepDB, a highly comprehensive database designed to offer detailed information on bacterial selenoproteins. This platform enables users to conveniently browse, search, and download relevant data as needed. Moreover, the BLAST search function has been integrated into BSepDB, thereby facilitating homology searches against well-established selenoprotein sequences. We anticipate that this database will not only act as a key reference resource for research in Se biology but also lay a solid foundation for the accurate annotation of bacterial selenoproteomes in various genomic sequencing projects.
