Early vascular lesion tracking in moyamoya disease: A case report with serial findings
Authors: Shoko Yokoyama, Toshiteru Miyasaka, Ryota Nakano, Yumi Tai, Tomoko Ochi, Kozue Saito, Kazuma Sugie, Toshihiro Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMoyamoya disease (MMD) is characterized by progressive stenosis of the internal carotid arteries and the presence of abnormal collateral vessels. However, knowledge of the early stages of MMD is limited. This case report discusses a woman in her 30s who experienced unusual symptoms, such as hand dysfunction and severe headaches. Initial MRI revealed stenosis of the right middle cerebral artery, and vascular imaging demonstrated eccentric thickening of the vessel wall with contrast enhancement. The patient was initially suspected to have arterial dissection and was treated with antiplatelet therapy. During the subsequent clinical course, Graves’ disease was diagnosed based on the serological evidence of autoimmune hyperthyroidism. After 2 and a half years, further arterial narrowing and the development of abnormal collateral vessels were observed, ultimately fulfilling the diagnostic criteria for MMD. The disease did not progress thereafter, and the patient is currently under observation. This case highlights the difficulty in distinguishing MMD from other conditions, such as vasculitis and arterial dissection, in its early stages and provides valuable insights into the longitudinal progression of MMD, particularly in the context of coexisting thyroid disease.Keywords: Moyamoya disease, Magnetic resonance angiography, Graves’ disease, Vessel shrinkage

Introduction

Moyamoya disease (MMD) is characterized by progressive stenosis at the terminal portions of the bilateral internal carotid arteries (ICA), particularly within the circle of Willis, along with the formation of abnormal vascular networks known as “moyamoya vessels” [1]. Historically, cerebral angiography has been considered essential for diagnosing MMD. However, according to the 2021 Japanese diagnostic criteria, an MRI-based diagnosis can now be made if the following 3 conditions are met: (1) stenosis or occlusion of the bilateral distal ICA on MRA, (2) vessel shrinkage at the ICA termini or horizontal segment of the middle cerebral artery, observed on heavy T2-weighted imaging, and (3) the presence of abnormal vascular networks on MRA, particularly around the brain base and periventricular regions [2].

The etiology of MMD remains poorly understood, and knowledge regarding its early pathological stages is limited. In this report, we present a rare case in which we were able to track the progression of vascular stenosis in MMD, alongside a review of the relevant literature on this topic.

Case report

A 30-year-old woman presented with a bizarre sensation in her left hand, which she described as not being her own, accompanied by a visual phenomenon of lines in her visual field lasting approximately 1 hour. This was followed by severe, recurrent headaches in the right frontal region that persisted for approximately 1 week. The patient had experienced recurrent headaches since childhood; however, these headaches had never been formally diagnosed as migraines before the present illness.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain revealed significant stenosis extending from the origin of the right middle cerebral artery to the M1 segment (Fig. 1). Eccentric thickening of the vessel wall was observed; however, no narrowing of the M1 segment external diameter was observed. Additionally, T2-weighted imaging revealed high-signal areas with abnormal contrast enhancement around the affected vessels (Fig. 2A-C). Vascular proliferation was observed at the brain base. Carotid ultrasonography revealed no significant abnormality.

Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) showing stenosis from the origin of the right middle cerebral artery to the M1 segment of the artery.

Cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) on T1 weighted (A), contrast-enhanced T1-weighted (B), T2 weighted (C) images demonstrated vessel wall thickening and abnormal contrast enhancement around the affected vessels.

At the time of the initial neurological presentation, the patient had not been diagnosed with Graves’ disease. The diagnosis was established later in the clinical course based on elevated thyroid autoantibody levels consistent with autoimmune hyperthyroidism. Therefore, thyroid disease was not considered a contributing factor during the initial diagnostic workup.

Based on these findings, right middle cerebral artery dissection was considered. The patient was initially treated with antiplatelet medication, which was discontinued during pregnancy. During the 2.5-year follow-up period, the patient experienced recurrent episodes of this bizarre sensation, now affecting both hands, accompanied by headaches. Subsequent MRI revealed progression of stenosis in the M1 segment of the right MCA, with narrowing of both internal and external diameters. Additionally, new stenosis was observed at the origin of the left middle cerebral artery (Fig. 3A-C). Surrounding vascular proliferation was observed (Fig. 4A and B). As the patient met the 3 diagnostic criteria, she was diagnosed with Moyamoya disease.

Magnetic resonance angiography performed 1 year later (A), 1 year and 8 months later (B), 2.5 years later (C) shows that progressive worsening of stenosis of the right middle cerebral artery and the development of new stenosis in the left middle cerebral artery.

Time-of-flight MRA images obtained at baseline (A) and 2.5 years later (B). The baseline image shows stenosis around the origin of the right middle cerebral artery (A). The follow-up image obtained 2.5 years later shows perivascular collateral proliferation around the origin of the right middle cerebral artery and newly developed perivascular collateral proliferation at the origin of the left middle cerebral artery (B).

The patient declined referral to a neurosurgeon, and antiplatelet therapy was discontinued because of the absence of progression of vascular stenosis. The patient was treated with lomerizine hydrochloride for headaches. A detailed re-evaluation of her medical history revealed that her mother had been diagnosed with right hemispheric moyamoya disease.

Discussion

Elucidating the pathophysiology and etiology of MMD in its early stages remains a significant challenge. Kuroda et al. [3] proposed a hypothesis in which intimal thickening first leads to luminal stenosis at the carotid bifurcation, followed by a progressive reduction in external vessel diameter as the disease advances [3]. Our patient’s clinical course directly supports this hypothesis; initial eccentric stenosis of the right MCA was subsequently followed by evident outer diameter reduction and perivascular collateral proliferation (Fig. 5).

T2-weighted cranial MRI at baseline (A), 1 year later (B), and 2.5 years later (C). Initially, eccentric stenosis of the MCA was observed, followed by a reduction in external vessel diameter and vascular proliferation.

In its early stages, MMD can be difficult to distinguish from vasculitis and arterial dissection. In the present case, arterial dissection was initially suspected, and management was initiated based on this assumption. However, the observation of progressive eccentric stenosis in the right MCA (M1) and the emergence of new stenosis in the contralateral MCA within 2.5 years necessitated a diagnostic reevaluation.

Previous studies have reported that patients with moyamoya disease may present with recurrent migraine-like headaches and transient neurological symptoms, such as visual or sensory disturbances, particularly in the early stages of the disease [4]. These manifestations can closely resemble migraines with aura or hemiplegic migraine, making differentiation from transient ischemic attacks challenging. Accumulating evidence suggests that such symptoms are more appropriately interpreted as hemodynamic or ischemic phenomena rather than true migraine aura, representing a “migraine mimic” associated with cerebral hypoperfusion or transient cortical ischemia. In this context, the patient’s long-standing history of undiagnosed childhood headaches may reflect early manifestations of MMD rather than primary migraines. This clinical overlap, as illustrated in the present case, may complicate early diagnosis and delay the accurate recognition of the disease.

Pathologically, stenotic arteries in MMD demonstrate eccentric intimal fibrous thickening, often comprising multilayered structures [3,5]. Common findings include marked duplication and tortuosity of the internal elastic lamina, along with a decreased number of smooth muscle cells in the media [3,5]. A key diagnostic differentiator is that, while the outer vessel diameter is typically preserved in atherosclerosis, MMD involves a progressive reduction in the external diameter (vessel shrinkage) [6,7].

The presence of Grave’s disease in our patient further supports the diagnosis of MMD, as the clinical and pathological association between thyroid dysfunction and MMD is well established [8,9]. Elevated thyroid hormone levels are hypothesized to trigger or accelerate vascular stenosis by increasing sympathetic sensitivity and cerebral metabolic demand [9,10]. Although RNF213 genotyping was not performed in this patient, recent evidence indicates that elevated thyroid autoantibodies are significantly more prevalent in patients with MMD lacking the RNF213 variant (31.5%) than in those with it (15.5%) [8,10]. These findings suggest that thyroid autoimmunity may function as a contributory immunological factor, particularly in patients without known genetic susceptibility. Consequently, the combination of progressive vessel shrinkage and co-existing Graves’ disease is strongly supportive of the diagnosis of MMD, potentially mediated by immunological mechanisms that may operate independently of or interact with genetic predisposition [8,9]. In patients with Grave’s disease presenting with atypical stroke or vascular stenosis, moyamoya disease should be considered early in the differential diagnosis, even when initial imaging findings suggest alternative etiologies.

Therefore, long-term radiological follow-up is essential, even when vasculitis or atherosclerosis is suspected. As the disease progresses, the manifestation of vessel shrinkage can be a deciding factor for MMD diagnosis. Although this report is limited to a single clinical case, it underscores the importance of longitudinal monitoring. Further research involving larger cohorts is necessary to fully elucidate the early progression of MMD. Nevertheless, a cautious and persistent diagnostic approach is required to ensure accurate differentiation and timely interventions.

Conclusion

We report a rare case of MMD in which longitudinal imaging captured the transition from eccentric stenosis to external vessel diameter shrinkage over 2.5 years. This progression provides clinical support for the hypothesis that MMD pathogenesis begins with intimal thickening. This case underscores the necessity of persistent radiologic follow-up to differentiate early MMD from mimics, such as arterial dissection and vasculitis, ensuring an accurate and timely diagnosis.
