Outcomes of pregnancy and HIV status of infants born to women with HIV in Larkana, Pakistan: a ten-year retrospective study (2011–2021)
Authors: Fatima Mir, Victoria Simms, Ghazal Peerwani, Apsara Ali Nathwani, Aftab Memon, Pirbh Das Lalwani, Anila Isran, Shahida Memon, Nabila Zaka, Ershad Kazmi, Kanwal Mustafa, Syed Kamal Asghar, Syed Faisal Mahmood, Rashida Abbas Ferrand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAn extensive HIV outbreak among children in Larkana, Pakistan in 2019 resulted in establishment of HIV screening and paediatric HIV services. We investigated pregnancy outcomes and HIV status of infants born to women living with HIV (WLWH) in Larkana.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted across two clinical centers in Larkana District, Sindh province. A list of WLWH who accessed antenatal care (ANC) from January 2011 up to December 2021 was obtained, obstetric and HIV data were retrieved from hospital records, and descriptive data analysis was performed.ResultsOf 328 WLWH who accessed ANC at least once, we analyzed 237 (72%) women. Median age at HIV registration was 27 (IQR:23–32) years; 30.4% of WLWH were infected through blood products or contaminated needles, and 29 of the 207 tested had Hepatitis B or C co-infection. At HIV care registration, 41.4% had a CD4 count < 350 cells/µl. Overall, 178/237 WLWH had 509 previous pregnancies: 5.9% and 1.8% respectively resulted in miscarriages and stillbirths. Of 470 previous live births, 74/354 (20.9%) had died by the time WLWH registered for their latest pregnancy (vital status was not recorded for the other 116 children). Of the 255 children who were alive and had an HIV test, 21.2% tested HIV-positive. Data on outcomes of current pregnancy were available for 213/237 women, 2 being miscarriages and 9 being stillbirths. The median birthweight was 2.5(IQR:2.3–2.6) kg. 99% of infants received HIV prophylaxis within a week of birth. HIV testing was documented for 134 children, of whom 107 had an HIV test and 5 (4.7%) tested HIV positive, 4 within 2 months of birth and one in the 3rd year of life.ConclusionsThe findings underscore the need for specialist obstetric care for WLWH, strengthened antenatal HIV testing, and monitoring of children born to women with HIV.Keywords: Pregnancy, Pakistan, HIV, Infants, Mother-to-child transmission

Introduction

In 2024, 1.4 million children were living with HIV globally, with more than 90% living in sub-Saharan Africa and Asia [1]. Since the remarkable scale-up of highly effective interventions based on lifelong antiretroviral therapy (ART) for pregnant women living with HIV, the number of infections in children globally per year has halved from 230,000 in 2013 to 120,000 in 2023, and virtual elimination of mother-to-child transmission (MTCT) has become an achievable goal [2]. However, there is great variability between regions in both ART coverage and MTCT rates, with some countries experiencing an increase in HIV infections in children over the last decade [2, 3].Vertical transmission rates remain high with paediatric HIV infections increasing in certain regions particularly in West and Central Africa, where ART coverage among pregnant women remains low at around 60% [4]. In addition, specific regions such as South and southeast Asia have observed continuing paediatric HIV infections as a consequence of increases in infections in key populations (e.g. people who inject drugs) which then spill over combined with insufficient prevention programmes, as well as infections in children occurring through nosocomial transmission [3].

One such country that has observed a substantial increase in both numbers of paediatric HIV infections and associated mortality is Pakistan [5–8]. This was brought into sharp focus in a well-documented ”outbreak” of HIV infection among children in Sindh province in 2019 with over 1500 identified with HIV infection [9]. While initial investigations suggested that unsafe injections and blood transfusions were the primary transmission routes, with 7% of cases attributed to vertical transmission, a more extensive retrospective review conducted in 2022 (unpublished) has revealed a higher mother-to-child transmission (MTCT) rate of 25%, indicating that vertical transmission continues to be a significant mode of transmission among HIV-infected children [10]. HIV testing in antenatal care is not routinely offered, and even among pregnant women established on ART there are very sparse data on MTCT rates [11].

Maternal HIV infection presents complex challenges for child survival. Even with ART in pregnancy, HIV infection is associated with adverse birth outcomes in some settings [12]. We investigated the outcomes of pregnancy in women with HIV infection in Pakistan, specifically the HIV status of their infants.

Methods

A retrospective study was conducted between January and July 2022 at two clinical centres in Larkana District in Sindh Province, Pakistan. The two centres are located in Larkana City and in Ratodero, which is 28 km from Larkana City.

In Larkana City, HIV care for adults and children has traditionally been provided across separate centres. Since 2007, adults have received care at Chandka Medical College, a public-sector tertiary-level hospital. Prior to 2019, children exposed to and infected were largely referred to the pediatric HIV clinic at the Aga Khan University Hospital in Karachi (the provincial capital), a private quarternary-level referral hospital providing highly specialized pediatric HIV services, located 450 km away from Larkana City. In 2019, following an extensive outbreak of HIV among children driven largely by nosocomial transmission, a pediatric HIV clinic was established at Shaikh Zayed Children’s Hospital Larkana. Antenatal care is provided at Shaikh Zayed Women’s Hospital in Larkana and includes antenatal HIV testing, albeit not offered systematically to all pregnant women.

In contrast, a more integrated approach has been adopted in Ratodero, where, in 2019, a comprehensive ‘one-stop shop’ for HIV services was established at Taluqa Hospital Ratodero. This facility now provides HIV care for men, women, and children, including services for pregnant women with HIV and their HIV-exposed infants.

A list of women with HIV who had received ANC at least once between January 2011 up to December 2021 was accessed from Taluqa Hospital Ratodero and Shaikh Zayed Women’s Hospital Larkana. Only women who received HIV care, antenatal care and HIV care for their infants (whether that was prophylaxis or treatment for HIV) within Larkana District were included. Women who sought one or more of these services outside the district were excluded. We analyzed records where maternal and infant data could be reliably linked to assess pregnancy outcomes and infant HIV status. Women without antenatal care or infants without verified HIV outcomes were excluded.

Data were collected for the pregnancy closest in time to the study data collection period (January–July 2022), hereafter referred to as the most recent pregnancy. Information was abstracted from antenatal and obstetric records maintained at Shaikh Zayed Women’s Hospital, Larkana, and Taluka Hospital, Ratodero. Obstetric records corresponding to the most recent pregnancy were reviewed to obtain details on prior obstetric history, including the number and timing of previous pregnancies, mode of delivery, infant sex and birth weight, maternal HIV status, and pregnancy outcomes (vital status, HIV status where available).

Data on all pregnancies occurring prior to the most recent pregnancy were also extracted from the medical records. Previous pregnancies were defined as all pregnancies occurring before the most recent documented pregnancy. Previous live births were defined as pregnancies resulting in a live birth among these previous pregnancies.

Details of HIV history was obtained from separate records maintained at the HIV treatment centre (with linkage obtained through a distinct Person Living with HIV (PLHIV) ID allotted to a person at the time of registration for care by the Provincial HIV Control Program). Details, including the date of HIV diagnosis, date of registration into HIV care, and Hepatitis B and C test results, were recorded. Information on the likely mode of HIV acquisition was obtained from routine clinical documentation in HIV case files at the time of registration. This information is ascertained by trained physicians based on participant-reported risk factors collected as part of standard care.

Data was collected from medical records using a structured data form by trained research assistants. Data forms were checked by a field supervisor prior to entry into a password-protected database. Names and identifying information were not abstracted to ensure confidentiality before analysis. Descriptive data analysis was performed using STATA version 18.0 (StatCorp, Texas, USA).

Permission to access data was sought from the Health Information Management System (HIMS) of the Office of the Director, Communicable Disease Control-Sind. This retrospective chart review study utilized anonymized data with no direct patient contact. As such, no patient consent was obtained, and a consent waiver was not requested. The study was reviewed and approved by the Aga Khan University Ethical Review Committee (2021-Project ID 6719).

Results

Of the 328 women with HIV who had ever accessed ANC across both centres (220 registered at Chandka Medical College and 108 at Taluqa Hospital Ratodero) during the study period (2011–2021), we were able to identify and review the medical records of 237 (72%) women who received both obstetric and HIV care for themselves and their HIV-exposed infants within the district.

More than two-thirds of maternal HIV diagnoses were made between 2019 and 2021 (Table 1). Of the 237 women, 173 (73%) registered for HIV care on the same day as diagnosis, and 51 (21.5%) registered within two weeks of diagnosis. Nearly all patients (236/237; 99.6%) initiated antiretroviral therapy at the time of HIV care registration.

The median age at HIV registration was 27 (IQR:23–32) years; majority of women had received no formal education, and none of the women were employed. More than half (51.5%) of the women were residents of districts outside Larkana. Nearly one-third of the women were assessed to have acquired HIV through transfusion of blood/blood products or contaminated needles. None self-reported being a sex worker or intravenous drug user, nor being the spouse of an intravenous drug user or sex worker. HIV test results for the husbands were available for only three-quarters of the women. Among the husbands of women with HIV who had been tested, 40% tested HIV-negative. The median weight was 46 kg (IQR 40–52), and 51.9% had moderate or severe anemia at HIV registration. Out of 207 patients in whom both hepatitis B and C testing results were documented, 29 (14.0%) had either Hepatitis B or C co-infection. The remaining records lacked complete documentation, which may reflect incomplete recording or testing not performed. In those in whom CD4 count was available (65.8%), 41.7% had a CD4 count < = 350 cells/µl (Table 1).

Overall, 7.7% of the previous pregnancies did not result in live births (30 of 509 (5.9%) resulted in miscarriage and 9 of 509 (1.8%) in stillbirth). At the time women registered for their latest pregnancy, vital status was recorded for 354 of the 470 previous live-born children, and 20.9% had died. Of the 255 children who were alive and underwent an HIV test, 21.2% tested positive for HIV.

Outcome of current pregnancy

Data on delivery and infant outcomes of current pregnancy were available for 213 of the 237 records, of which 2 pregnancies ended in miscarriage. In the remaining 211, 4.3% were stillbirths, and the median birthweight was 2.5 kg (IQR: 2.3–2.6), with 40.7% classified as underweight. Approximately 99% received prophylactic treatment, with the majority starting within a week of birth (Table 2b).

Information on HIV testing was documented for 134 livebirths (66.3%). Of these, 107 (79.9%) had an HIV test, 1 had an unknown test result, and 5/106 were HIV positive (4.7%). Almost 67.3% of infants underwent a test within the first two months of life. Of the five children who tested positive, three were diagnosed with HIV on testing performed within the first two months of life (early infant diagnosis). One tested negative but subsequently tested positive at 18 months of age. One did not come for early infant testing but tested positive in the third year of life after falling sick. An additional six of the 134 children died, of whom four were of unknown HIV status and two were HIV-negative. Four deaths occurred within 2 months of birth (from diarrhoeal illness, pneumonia, meningitis, and 1 from unknown cause), and the other two occurred at 7 months and 21 months, from unknown causes.

Discussion

This study reports on obstetric outcomes and on rate of MTCT of HIV among women in Pakistan by triangulating routine clinical data from adult and pediatric HIV care and obstetric records. A review of obstetric history showed that 7.7% of pregnancies did not result in live births. In their most recent pregnancies, 4.3% of pregnancies resulted in stillbirths and more than 40% of and of babies were low birth weight.

A previous study from Pakistan has showed similarly poor birth outcomes among women with HIV with 12.2% of women experiencing intrauterine deaths and 8.1% having preterm births [13]. Maternal HIV increases risks of miscarriage, stillbirth, prematurity, and low birth weight [14–17]. While ART reduces the risk of MTCT of HIV, it may not fully mitigate against the associated risk of adverse birth outcomes [18]. The risk of adverse birth outcomes may be particularly high in women with either advanced or poorly uncontrolled HIV disease [19]. Women in our cohort commonly presented with advanced disease: over half had moderate or severe anaemia and 40% had a CD4 count < 350 cells/µl at HIV diagnosis, indicating significant immunosuppression.

At least a third of women were assessed to have likely acquired HIV through nosocomial routes; nearly 40% of tested husbands were HIV-negative, and 15% of women had Hepatitis B or C, reflecting Pakistan’s high burden of blood borne infections [20] with the majority of transmissions occurring via therapeutic injections, syringe reuse, surgery, improper sterilization of invasive medical devices, and blood transfusions [21]. While women were promptly initiated on HIV treatment following registration into care, often on the same day, linkage to and access to treatment for hepatitis, a distinct vertical programme was not provided.

Notably, nearly 20% of previous live births had died; this may be a reflection of the persistently high infant and under-five childhood mortality rates in Pakistan which are driven by poor socioeconomic conditions and a weak primary healthcare system [22]. There is also higher risk of morbidity and mortality among infants born to mothers with HIV compared to those without HIV even if they are not themselves infected [23].

Among surviving children that resulted from previous pregnancies in our cohort, 20% had tested HIV-positive. Without intervention, the risk of MTCT of HIV ranges from 20% to 45% [24]. Critically, routine HIV testing of pregnant women is not widely available in the country, which may explain the high proportion of children born to women in previous pregnancies testing HIV-positive. Most HIV diagnoses in mothers were made in 2019 or later, which is likely a consequence of the implementation of widespread HIV screening following the identification of the outbreak of paediatric HIV in Larkana district in 2019 [9].

For the most recent pregnancies, all women were on ART, and most infants received prophylaxis within a week of birth. Nearly a third of infants had no HIV test result documented, highlighting low coverage of early infant diagnosis. It is possible that some of these infants may have been tested elsewhere (e.g. in private facilities) but this was not documented. In Larkana, children and mothers attended for HIV follow-up care at different hospitals which makes linkage of clinical records and identifying children exposed to HIV challenging. Lack of consistent recording of HIV testing of infants born to mothers with HIV in clinical records may mean that HIV testing of infants in busy and resource-constrained services may be missed. Among those infants that were tested, 4.7% were HIV-positive, despite mothers being on ART and infants receiving HIV prophylaxis. A study from Punjab province showed even higher HIV positivity of 16% among infants born to mothers with HIV [25].

UNAIDS (2023) estimates MTCT prevention coverage in Pakistan at 23%, which is not only due to low coverage of early infant diagnosis but also limited antenatal HIV screening [10]. While routine antenatal HIV screening is recommended in all settings regardless of prevalence [26–28]—with provider-initiated testing and counselling (PITC) recommended as a routine component of antenatal care in high-prevalence settings and considered in low-prevalence settings as part of efforts to eliminate mother-to-child transmission and integrate HIV testing with other antenatal screening—questions remain regarding the feasibility of implementing such a strategy in Pakistan. Firstly, the yield of a universal testing strategy is likely to be low in a country with a concentrated HIV epidemic. Understanding the epidemiology of HIV infection in pregnant women and the general population is critical to address this, but there is no systematic antenatal HIV surveillance. To date HIV surveillance has been restricted to key populations through regular rounds of Integrated Biological and Behavioural Surveys [29]. Thus, accurate estimates of HIV prevalence among pregnant women are not available to inform HIV testing policies in antenatal settings. A question remains regarding the feasibility and the cost-effectiveness of such a strategy in a resource-constrained country with a population of 250 million people, high fertility rates, competing health issues and a weak health system [22]. Antenatal surveillance should be instituted, and future research should investigate targeted HIV testing strategies.

One case was diagnosed in the third year, suggesting either transmission via breastfeeding or through nosocomial transmission. While paediatric infections in other regions e.g. in Africa are thought to be predominantly occurring as a result of MTCT, nosocomial transmission is a well-documented route of HIV transmission in children in Pakistan [9]. It may thus be difficult to distinguish between vertical or horizontal transmission if HIV testing of the infant is not performed at or shortly after birth and repeated one month after cessation of breastfeeding.

We acknowledge several limitations. Missing data and potential errors in linking maternal and infant records may have affected completeness of the dataset, and some records remained unlinked, possibly representing children not registered in care. The study included only women who accessed pregnancy and HIV care for themselves and their children in Larkana district; women not engaged in care, such as those who delivered at home, were therefore not captured. While this approach was necessary to maximize maternal-infant linkage, it may have resulted in underestimation of both adverse pregnancy outcomes and mother-to-child HIV transmission if women outside the formal care system experienced higher risks. Information on potential risks for nosocomial HIV transmission (e.g. exposures to contaminated needles or blood products) was self-reported and may therefore be subject to recall bias.

We also explored whether predictors of adverse outcomes could be examined; however, substantial missing outcome data (including many deceased children without documented HIV status), limited available covariates, and evidence of reverse causality in exploratory analyses limited the reliability of regression modelling. Given these constraints, and the descriptive objective of this study-to document the burden of adverse pregnancy outcomes among women with HIV in this setting—we did not pursue multivariable analyses. While descriptive, this study is, to our knowledge, the first to document the high rate of adverse birth outcomes among women living with HIV in this setting, with important implications for clinical care and public health policy.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates poor birth outcomes in women with HIV and high mother-to-child HIV transmission rates. Programmatic gaps include antenatal HIV testing, fragmented HIV and obstetric care and poor data linkage. Priorities include specialist care (ideally jointly provided by obstetricians and HIV physicians) for pregnant women with HIV, clear evidence-informed national antenatal testing guidelines coupled with an implementation roadmap, strengthened early infant diagnosis including systematic testing at birth, six weeks and 1-3 months after cessation of breastfeeding or 18 months (whichever is later) and, active follow-up of untested infants, to improve maternal and child health outcomes. More broadly, infection prevention and blood safety must be prioritized to curb the transmission of blood-borne viruses. Finally, there is a critical need to invest in strengthening mother and child health services in the public sector.
