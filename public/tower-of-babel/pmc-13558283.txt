Integrative pooled transcriptomic analysis reveals shared and distinct molecular signatures in adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma and peripheral T-cell lymphoma
Authors: Mohammad Mehdi Akbarin, Zahra Farjami, Hugo Ramírez Álvarez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATLL) and peripheral T-cell lymphomas (PTCLs) are aggressive neoplasms of mature T cells with poor prognosis and limited therapies. ATLL originates from HTLV-1 infection, while PTCL comprises heterogeneous subtypes without a defined etiologic factor. Comparative molecular profiling of these malignancies remains limited. We conducted an integrative pooled transcriptomic analysis of publicly available Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) microarray datasets to compare ATLL, PTCL, and normal T-cell samples. Differential expression, functional enrichment, and protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analyses were performed using STRING, Cytoscape, and Gephi. Key hub genes and functional modules were further analyzed through KEGG and Enrichr databases. Comparative analyses revealed upregulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) components (COL1A1, COL3A1, FN1, SPARC, THBS1) and immune-regulatory molecules (CD163, CXCL12–CXCR4, complement subunits). Shared pathways included ECM–receptor interaction, focal adhesion, and PI3K–Akt signaling. PTCL showed enrichment in coagulation and angiogenesis, while ATLL displayed distinct enrichment of cytoskeletal, chemokine, immune-regulatory, and signaling-associated pathways. PPI networks identified ECM and chemokine signaling as key hubs, with subtype-specific modules related to immune regulation, proliferation, and metabolism. This integrative approach uncovers common and distinct oncogenic programs in ATLL and PTCL, emphasizing ECM remodeling and immune modulation as shared hallmarks. Hub genes such as COL1A1, FN1, and CXCL12–CXCR4 may represent candidate molecular signatures that warrant validation in independent patient cohorts and functional studies before their clinical utility can be established.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10142-026-02041-5.

Introduction

Peripheral T-cell lymphomas (PTCLs) are a heterogeneous group of aggressive non-Hodgkin lymphomas derived from mature T cells, associated with poor prognosis and limited therapeutic options (Carbone et al. 2017; Montes-Mojarro et al. 2021). Among them, adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATLL) is a distinct subtype caused by persistent human T-cell lymphotropic virus type 1 (HTLV-1) infection (Ramezani et al. 2022; El Hajj and Bazarbachi 2022).

ATLL arises from CD4 + T cells and exhibits genetic instability, immune evasion, and aggressive progression (Akbarin et al. 2017; Sarkis et al. 2019). In contrast, PTCL includes subtypes such as PTCL-NOS, AITL, and ALCL, which lack a defined viral cause but share mechanisms involving immune dysregulation, aberrant T-cell receptor signaling, and transcriptional alterations (Lim and Maini 2014; Zuo et al. 2023).

Despite etiologic differences, both ATLL and PTCL display convergence in oncogenic signaling, extracellular matrix remodeling, and immune modulation (Brites et al. 2021; Healy and Dave 2015). HTLV-1 oncoproteins Tax and HBZ activate NF-κB, PI3K/AKT, and JAK/STAT pathways (Fajami et al. 2024; Tarokhian et al. 2018), similar to mutations affecting these cascades in PTCL (Ma et al. 2022; Nakamura 2023).

ATLL serves as a viral-driven model for T-cell lymphomagenesis, illustrating interactions among chronic immune stimulation, genetic lesions, and microenvironmental remodeling (Eusebio-Ponce et al. 2019; Zarei Ghobadi et al. 2021). As PTCL often lacks a clear etiologic agent (Weiss et al. 2023), ATLL may provide mechanistic insights into shared oncogenic principles.

However, few studies have systematically compared their molecular landscapes using integrative approaches (Weiss et al. 2023; Marchi et al. 2024; Nozuma et al. 2024). Addressing this gap is essential to identify conserved targets and improve precision therapy for both diseases (Montes-Mojarro et al. 2021; Rickinson 2014).

In this study, we performed an integrative pooled transcriptomic analysis of publicly available microarray datasets to identify shared and disease-specific molecular signatures.

Methods

Dataset retrieval and eligibility criteria

Relevant transcriptomic datasets were identified from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Eligible datasets met the following conditions: (1) profiling of primary human samples (ATLL vs. normal T cells; PTCL vs. normal T cells), (2) generation on high-density microarray platforms, and (3) untreated or baseline patient samples. Datasets derived from non-human models, established cell lines, or treated cohorts were excluded. Raw CEL files were imported into R and subjected to quality assessment using a MAS5-based workflow. Quality evaluation included visual inspection of array images, expression intensity distributions, boxplots, histograms, and pairwise scatter plots to identify potential technical artifacts or outlier arrays prior to downstream analysis. All datasets satisfied the predefined quality criteria and were retained for subsequent analyses.

In addition, samples were selected according to predefined inclusion criteria to maximize comparability among datasets. Eligible samples were required to (i) represent ATLL, PTCL, or healthy control peripheral blood mononuclear cell samples, (ii) be generated using the Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 platform, (iii) have publicly available raw CEL files, and (iv) pass the quality assessment procedures performed prior to differential expression analysis. Samples lacking sufficient annotation, representing unrelated disease entities, or failing quality assessment were excluded from further analysis.

Restricting the analysis to a single microarray platform minimized platform-related technical variability and facilitated the integration of datasets generated in independent studies. A summary of the included datasets, sample characteristics, and GEO accession numbers is presented in Table 1.

Differential gene expression analysis

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the limma package within the Bioconductor framework in R (version 4.0.2). Publicly available Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 microarray datasets were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database using the GEOquery package.

Three pairwise comparisons were performed: ATLL versus normal T cells, PTCL versus normal T cells, and ATLL versus PTCL. Linear models with empirical Bayes variance moderation were fitted using the limma package to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Statistical significance was determined using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Genes with an adjusted P value ≤ 0.05 and an absolute log₂ fold change ≥ 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed. The resulting DEG lists were subsequently used for functional enrichment, protein–protein interaction network construction, and hub gene identification.

Because no custom probe reannotation or probe summarization was performed, a separate probe-to-gene mapping table was not generated. Gene annotation followed the Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 platform annotation associated with each GEO dataset.

Network construction

For protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction, differentially expressed genes were first filtered using a stringent threshold of |log₂ fold change| ≥ 2. Genes meeting this criterion were then ranked according to the absolute value of their log₂ fold change (|log₂FC|), regardless of the direction of expression change. The top 250 genes with the highest absolute log₂ fold change values were selected for PPI network analysis, including both significantly upregulated and downregulated genes. This selection strategy prioritized the most strongly dysregulated genes while limiting network complexity and facilitating the identification of biologically meaningful interaction modules and hub genes.

The selected genes were subsequently imported into the STRING database (version 11.0) (Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics, Lausanne, Switzerland) (https://string-db.org/). STRING integrates seven complementary sources of evidence, including physical binding, functional associations, co-expression, curated databases, and literature-mined data. A minimum required interaction confidence score of 0.400 (medium confidence) was used for network construction. Unless otherwise specified, disconnected nodes were excluded from network visualization.

The default STRING interaction evidence sources, including experimentally determined interactions, curated databases, gene neighborhood, gene fusion, gene co-occurrence, text mining, co-expression, and protein homology, were retained without modification. Disconnected nodes were excluded from the final network visualization and subsequent topological analyses to improve network interpretability.

The resulting networks were imported into Cytoscape (v3.6.1) (National Institute of General Medical Sciences, USA) (https://cytoscape.org/) for visualization and network topology analysis. Network topology was assessed using the Network Analyzer tool. Node degree, defined as the number of direct connections (edges) of each node, was used as the primary criterion for identifying hub genes. Genes exhibiting the highest node-degree values were considered hub genes and were prioritized for downstream interpretation. Other centrality measures, including betweenness centrality, closeness/harmonic closeness centrality, and eigenvector centrality, were used as complementary descriptors of network topology.

Network topology was assessed using Cytoscape Network Analyzer. Eccentricity, harmonic closeness centrality, betweenness centrality, and eigenvector centrality were calculated to characterize the topological position and connectivity of individual nodes within the PPI network. Modularity class represented the community assignment of each node within the network. Coessentiality was analyzed separately from the conventional topological parameters and was used as a complementary measure of functional similarity between genes based on their genetic-essentiality profiles. Coessentiality was not used as the primary criterion for defining hub genes; hub genes were identified primarily according to node degree.

Functional module detection and pathway analysis

Following PPI network construction and topological analysis, Gephi (version 0.10.1) was used for community detection and network visualization. Network communities were identified using the Louvain fast-unfolding community-detection algorithm, with the default resolution parameter (resolution = 1.0) and edge weights enabled. The resulting modularity classes represented groups of densely interconnected nodes within the PPI network. The Fruchterman–Reingold algorithm was subsequently used to generate the network layout and visualize the identified communities. Functional modules containing at least five interconnected genes and representing the largest or most biologically connected communities were selected for downstream enrichment analyses. Functional annotation of the selected modules was performed using Enrichr.

Functional annotation of the identified modules was performed using Enrichr. Transcription factor enrichment analysis was conducted using the ChEA 2022 database, while pathway enrichment was performed using the KEGG 2021 Human database. Statistical significance was assessed using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction, and pathways with an adjusted P value (FDR) ≤ 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Enriched pathways were ranked according to their adjusted P values, with the lowest adjusted P values considered the most significant and retained for biological interpretation.

Hub genes were identified by ranking nodes according to degree, betweenness centrality, harmonic closeness centrality, and eigenvector centrality. Genes consistently ranked among the highest across these topological parameters were considered hub genes and selected for downstream biological interpretation.

The network and functional-analysis workflow therefore consisted of three sequential computational steps: (1) STRING for PPI network construction, (2) Cytoscape/Network Analyzer for network-topology assessment and hub-gene identification, and (3) Gephi for Louvain-based community detection and final network visualization.

Results

Included studies in gene profile analysis

Our gene expression analysis was conducted using publicly available datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository. After applying quality control filters with the MAS 5, we selected datasets suitable for differential expression profiling across three groups: ATLL vs. Normal T cells, ATLL vs. PTCL, and PTCL vs. Normal T cells.

Samples were selected according to predefined inclusion criteria to maximize comparability among datasets. Eligible samples represented ATLL, PTCL, tissues or healthy-control peripheral blood mononuclear cells, were generated using the Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 platform, had publicly available raw CEL files, and passed the predefined quality-control procedures.

For ATLL samples, we used GSE19069, which included 12 cases of adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma. For GSE19069, two normal T-cell samples were selected for inclusion in the integrated analysis. The selection was performed to maintain balanced representation across the independent datasets included in the study rather than to include all available normal T-cell samples from GSE19069. Three additional normal T-cell samples were obtained from GSE43017, resulting in five normal T-cell controls in the final analysis. All selected samples passed the quality-control assessment (Table 1).

The PTCL group was assembled from four datasets: GSE6338, GSE64985, GSE65823, and GSE19069, all of which contained PTCL-not otherwise specified (PTCL-NOS) cases. Together, these datasets contributed 12 PTCL samples for analysis (Table 1).

Although the GSE19069 dataset contains a larger number of PTCL samples, three PTCL samples were selected for the present analysis to maintain a balanced representation of samples across the different datasets and study groups included in the integrated analysis. The selection also allowed incorporation of multiple independent studies while minimizing disproportionate contribution from a single dataset. Accordingly, the PTCL samples included in Table 1 represent the subset selected for the present comparative analysis rather than the complete PTCL cohort available in GSE19069.

A comprehensive transcriptomic analysis was performed across three comparisons: ATLL vs. Normal T-cells, ATLL vs. PTCL, and PTCL vs. Normal T-cells. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on a threshold of log2 fold change (logFC) > 2 and an adjusted p-value < 0.05. Volcano plots summarizing the distribution of DEGs are presented in Fig. 1a–c.

In the ATLL vs. Normal T-cell group, a total of 3966 genes were analyzed, of which 1438 genes passed the defined significance threshold. Among the most significantly upregulated genes were COL3A1 (Collagen Type III Alpha 1 Chain), CD163, CADM1 (Cell Adhesion Molecule 1), (Cell Migration Inducing Hyaluronidase 1) CEMIP, Collagen Triple Helix Repeat Containing 1(CTHRC1), Collagen Type I Alpha 1 Chain (COL1A1), Aryl Hydrocarbon Receptor Nuclear Translocator 2 (ARNT2), and FN1 (Fibronectin 1), while CLC (Charcot–Leyden Crystal Galectin), AK5 (Adenylate Kinase 5), NOG (Noggin), Alpha-1,3-Mannosyl-Glycoprotein 4-Beta-N-Acetylglucosaminyltransferase A (MGAT4A), Membrane Spanning 4-Domains A3 (MS4A3), KLRB1 (Killer Cell Lectin Like Receptor Subfamily B Member 1), and PLD1 (Phospholipase D1) were strongly downregulated (Fig. 1a).

In the PTCL vs. Normal T-cell group, a total of 4210 genes met the inclusion threshold. Prominent upregulated genes included COL3A1, FN1, PDGFRA (Platelet-Derived Growth Factor Receptor Alpha), PTPRG (Protein Tyrosine Phosphatase Receptor Type G), and Insulin-Like Growth Factor Binding Protein 7 (IGFBP7), highlighting extracellular matrix remodeling and signaling pathways. Conversely, genes associated with immune response regulation, such as CLC, CCDC65 (coiled-coil domain containing 65), MS4A3, MAL (mal, T-cell differentiation protein), DSC1 (desmocollin 1), AK5, and KLRB1, were found to be significantly suppressed (Fig. 1b).

For the ATLL vs. PTCL comparison, a distinct set of expression changes was observed. Genes such as RGS13 (Regulator of G Protein Signaling 13), CYP26A1 (Cytochrome P450 Family 26 Subfamily A Member 1), KIR3DL1, KIR3DL2 (killer cell immunoglobulin-like receptor, three domains, long cytoplasmic tail, 1; killer cell immunoglobulin-like receptor, three domains, long cytoplasmic tail, 2), ZCCHC12 (zinc finger, CCHC domain containing 12), and ZNF365 (zinc finger protein 365) showed marked overexpression in ATLL compared with PTCL. In contrast, several immune-regulatory and metabolic genes include: LUM (lumican), CP(ceruloplasmin (ferroxidase)), CXCL9 (chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 9), ZYG11A (zyg-11 family member A, cell cycle regulator), ADAMDEC1 (ADAM-like, decysin 1), FN1, APOC1 (apolipoprotein C-I), PDGFRA, and COL1A2 displayed reduced expression in ATLL relative to PTCL (Fig. 1c).

For PPI network analysis, genes meeting the predefined threshold of |log₂ fold change| ≥ 2 were ranked according to their absolute log₂ fold change values, and the top 250 genes, including both upregulated and downregulated genes, were selected for network construction. This approach prioritized the most strongly dysregulated genes across each comparison while maintaining a manageable network size for downstream interaction and topological analyses.

Functional enrichment analysis

To gain insights into the biological pathways and molecular mechanisms distinguishing malignant T-cell subsets, we performed pathway enrichment analysis across three key comparisons: ATLL versus normal T cells, PTCL versus normal T cells, and ATLL versus PTCL.

ATLL vs. normal T cells

The enrichment analysis revealed that genes dysregulated in ATLL were significantly associated with pathways related to extracellular matrix (ECM) organization and cell environment interactions (Fig. 2A). The ECM-receptor interaction pathway emerged as the most significantly enriched, followed by human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, AGE-RAGE signaling in diabetic complications, and focal adhesion. Several cancer-associated pathways, including PI3K-Akt signaling and pathways in cancer, were also enriched. These results highlight the contribution of ECM remodeling, viral-related processes, and oncogenic signaling to ATLL pathogenesis.

PTCL vs. normal T cells

In PTCL, functional enrichment analysis similarly emphasized ECM-related processes, with ECM-receptor interaction and focal adhesion being the top-enriched pathways (Fig. 2B). However, PTCL displayed additional enrichment in complement and coagulation cascades, suggesting immune-inflammatory dysregulation. Other enriched terms included PI3K-Akt signaling, AGE-RAGE signaling, and infection-related pathways such as pertussis, HPV infection, and coronavirus disease. These findings indicate that PTCL shares ECM- and adhesion-related mechanisms with ATLL but is further characterized by immune response and coagulation pathway alterations.

ATLL vs. PTCL

Direct comparison of ATLL and PTCL revealed a distinct set of enriched pathways. Graft-versus-host disease and antigen processing and presentation were the most prominent, underscoring immune recognition and antigen presentation differences between the two T-cell malignancies (Fig. 2C). Additional enriched pathways included chemical carcinogenesis, natural killer (NK) cell-mediated cytotoxicity, Wnt signaling, and AML related pathways. These data suggest that while ATLL and PTCL share core ECM and adhesion-driven mechanisms relative to normal T cells, they diverge in immune regulation, carcinogen response, and signaling pathway utilization.

Overall, these enrichment results highlight both shared and distinct molecular programs underlying ATLL and PTCL. ATLL is strongly linked to ECM remodeling and oncogenic signaling, PTCL shows additional enrichment in immune-inflammatory cascades, and direct ATLL-PTCL comparison points to immune recognition and carcinogenesis-related pathways as key differentiating features.

The PPI network analysis

Protein-Protein Interaction Networks serve as powerful bioinformatics tools that allow researchers to explore and interpret the functional relationships among proteins within a biological system (Cao et al. 2021; Tejera et al. 2012). In cancer research, PPI network have been instrumental in identifying key molecular players that drive carcinogenesis and influence the progression of various cancer types, including PTCL and ATLL. For PPI network construction, differentially expressed genes were first filtered using a stringent threshold of |log₂ fold change| ≥ 2. The resulting genes were subsequently ranked according to the absolute log₂ fold change, and the top 250 ranked genes based on the absolute log₂ fold change. This subset included both significantly upregulated and downregulated genes that met the predefined fold-change criterion. The selection strategy was adopted to prioritize the most strongly dysregulated genes while reducing network complexity and facilitating the identification of biologically meaningful interaction modules and hub genes. The selected genes were imported into the STRING database for network construction and subsequent topological analyses.

To elucidate the molecular interactions, we constructed PPI for three distinct comparisons: ATLL vs. Normal T-cells (Fig. 3A), PTCL vs. Normal T-cells (Fig. 3B), and ATLL vs. PTCL (Fig. 3C). The PPI derived from the ATLL vs. Normal T-cell comparison revealed a highly interconnected network comprising multiple nodes and edges, suggesting coordinated dysregulation of several interacting proteins (Fig. 3A). Key hub proteins identified in this network include COL1A1, SPARC (Secreted Protein, Acidic, and Rich in Cysteine), THBS1 (Thrombospondin-1), FN1, and VCAN (versican), which are primarily associated with extracellular matrix organization and tumor microenvironment remodeling. In addition, Analysis of the PPI network in ATLL compared with normal T-cells revealed a substantial enrichment of extracellular matrix (ECM) and adhesion-related proteins as network hubs. Genes such as COL1A1, COL3A1, COL4A1/2, FN1, THBS1, and SPARC displayed high eigenvector and betweenness centralities, suggesting their central role in network connectivity. Within the PPI network, CXCL12 and CXCR4 ranked among the highly connected nodes and exhibited relatively high betweenness and harmonic closeness centrality values, suggesting that these proteins occupy central positions within the interaction network. Based on their established biological functions described in previous studies, the known CXCL12–CXCR4 signaling pathway may contribute to the molecular mechanisms underlying ATLL and PTCL. However, this interpretation is derived from integrating the network topology with existing biological knowledge rather than representing a direct output of the PPI analysis. Notably, complement components (C1QA, C1QB, C1QC, C1S) and macrophage-related markers (CD163, STAB1) also integrated firmly into the network, implying immune microenvironment remodeling in ATLL (Supplementary Table 1).

In the PTCL vs. Normal T-cell network, a denser and more complex interaction landscape was observed compared to ATLL (Fig. 3B). Central nodes included DCN (decorin), COL1A1, COL3A1, FN1, and VCAN, mirroring some overlaps with the ATLL network but also introducing unique interactors such as VWF (Von Willebrand Factor), SEPP1 (Selenoprotein P-Selenium-Binding Protein 1), and APOE (Apolipoprotein E), which may reflect distinct microenvironmental or vascular remodeling processes in PTCL. The presence of multiple immune-related proteins, such as complement members C1QA, C1QB, and CD163, further underscores immune modulation in PTCL.

Moreover, the PPI network for PTCL revealed a partly overlapping but distinct architecture compared to ATLL. ECM-associated proteins, particularly COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, COL4A1/2, COL6A3, and LUM, exhibited strong eigenvector centrality, highlighting the structural remodeling of the microenvironment. FN1, TNC, and VWF stood out as major hubs with high betweenness values, reflecting their importance in both adhesion and angiogenic pathways. Complement proteins (C1QA, C1QC, C1R, C1S) remained present, but immune-regulatory nodes were less central compared to ATLL. Interestingly, growth factor signaling pathways were emphasized by PDGFRB/PDGFRA and integrin-related proteins (VCAN, SDC family). Moreover, inflammatory mediators such as CCL2 and PLA2G7 were prominent, underscoring the role of chemokine-driven inflammation in PTCL pathogenesis (Supplementary Table 2).

The direct comparison between ATLL and PTCL yielded a PPI (Fig. 3C) that highlighted differential interactome profiles. Unlike the previous networks, this comparison showed fewer central ECM-related nodes. Instead, it highlighted differentially interacting proteins such as ubiquitin conjugating enzyme E2I (UBE2I), Amyloid beta precursor protein (cytoplasmic tail) binding protein 2 (APPBP2), ZMYND8 (Zinc Finger MYND-Type Containing 8), STAT5B (Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 5B), and presenilin-1 (PSEN1), suggesting alterations in transcriptional regulation, ubiquitination pathways, and intracellular signaling. These changes may reflect molecular signatures that distinguish ATLL from PTCL at a systems biology level. Furthermore, Direct comparison of ATLL and PTCL revealed apparent differences in PPI network organization. In PTCL, ECM and angiogenesis-related modules were dominant, while in ATLL, chemokine (CXCL12-CXCR4) and complement-macrophage interactions played stronger roles. Unique to this comparison, PTCL networks incorporated proliferative and chromatin-associated regulators such as ACTR2 (Actin-related protein 2), PTPRF (Protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor type F), and KIAA0141 (DAP3 Binding Cell Death Enhancer 1), which showed high centrality scores. By contrast, ATLL networks retained stronger immune-modulatory nodes (e.g., complement and scavenger receptors). These distinctions suggest that while both malignancies share ECM remodeling as a common hallmark, ATLL relies more heavily on immune microenvironmental cross-talk. In contrast, PTCL emphasizes proliferative and adhesion/angiogenic signaling (Supplementary Table 3).

Overall, the PPI analyses demonstrated distinct and overlapping interaction networks across ATLL, PTCL, and normal T-cells. While both ATLL and PTCL share a core set of dysregulated extracellular matrix and immune-related proteins, each subtype also exhibits unique network characteristics. These findings provide a systems-level understanding of the molecular perturbations driving T-cell malignancies and offer potential insights into subtype-specific therapeutic targets.

Discussion

This integrative analysis across multiple transcriptomic datasets reveals consistent molecular alterations in ATLL and PTCL, notably in extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, immune modulation, and oncogenic signaling. The top differentially expressed genes (COL1A1, COL3A1, FN1, CD163, CADM1, CEMIP) align with previous cancer studies, reinforcing their biological significance. Elevated collagen expression, particularly COL1A1, has been linked to tumor growth, ECM remodeling, and metastasis (Necula et al. 2022), while collagens and fibronectin promote tumor invasion and therapy resistance (Chivu-Economescu et al. 2022; Necula et al. 2022). CD163 serves as a macrophage-associated marker of poor prognosis in lymphomas (Kubo et al. 2022; Ohshima 2015; Yin et al. 2023), and CADM1 is a recognized ATLL surface biomarker correlated with disease progression (Sarkar et al. 2019).

Pathway enrichment confirmed ECM-receptor interaction, focal adhesion, PI3K-Akt, immune, and complement pathways, consistent with prior studies. The CXCL12/CXCR4 chemokine axis is widely implicated in hematologic malignancies, promoting tumor survival and chemoresistance (Kawaguchi et al. 2009; Moriuchi et al. 1999; Witzig et al. 2019), with PI3K/Akt activation downstream or via HTLV-1 oncoproteins in ATLL (Nakahata et al. 2014; Nakahata et al. 2015). Complement activation and inflammatory signatures have also been reported in PTCL with poor outcomes (Li et al. 2023).

Although ATLL and PTCL arise through distinct etiological mechanisms, our integrative transcriptomic analysis demonstrates that both malignancies converge on common biological programs involving extracellular matrix remodeling, cell adhesion, and PI3K–Akt signaling. The consistent enrichment of collagen family members, fibronectin, SPARC, thrombospondins, and other extracellular matrix-associated genes suggests that remodeling of the tumor microenvironment represents a shared hallmark of aggressive T-cell malignancies. These coordinated transcriptional alterations likely facilitate tumor invasion, immune evasion, and interactions with stromal cells, supporting disease progression irrespective of the initiating oncogenic event.

Beyond differential gene expression alone, the PPI network and community detection analyses demonstrated that dysregulated genes are organized into highly interconnected functional modules rather than acting independently. The identification of extracellular matrix-, chemokine-, complement-, and immune-related communities suggests coordinated biological programs that collectively shape the malignant microenvironment.

PPI network analysis identified key hubs-COL1A1, COL3A1, FN1, SPARC, THBS1, VCAN, CXCL12/CXCR4, and complement factors consistent with prior findings (Qiu et al., 2015; Doughan and Salifu 2022; Luo et al. 2017). Similar stromal and immune/inflammatory divisions were described in lymphoma studies and CXCR4 inhibition models (Pavlasova et al. 2016; Pavlasova et al. 2015). Overall, these results mirror multi-omic evidence of ECM and chemokine dysregulation, emphasizing collagens, fibronectin, and CXCL12/CXCR4 as key biomarkers and therapeutic targets in ATLL and PTCL. Consequently, these molecules may represent more robust therapeutic candidates than isolated differentially expressed genes because perturbation of central network components could simultaneously influence multiple biological processes.

One of the principal findings of this study is the identification of distinct molecular characteristics that differentiate ATLL from PTCL. Although both malignancies share extracellular matrix remodeling as a common feature, PTCL demonstrated stronger enrichment of immune-inflammatory pathways, complement activation, coagulation cascades, angiogenesis-related processes, and stromal remodeling. This observation is consistent with previous molecular studies showing that PTCL comprises biologically heterogeneous lymphoma subtypes with substantial differences in immune-cell infiltration, inflammatory signaling, and stromal interactions, resulting in highly variable tumor microenvironments and immune-related transcriptional programs (Wang et al. 2025; Iqbal et al. 2010; Jagadeesh and Smith 2013). In contrast, ATLL exhibits a more uniform virus-driven molecular phenotype characterized by persistent activation of signaling pathways directly regulated by HTLV-1 infection (Bangham and Matsuoka 2017; Shegefti et al. 2025).

These findings are consistent with the biological heterogeneity of PTCL, which comprises several molecular subtypes characterized by diverse immune and microenvironmental interactions. In contrast, ATLL displayed relatively greater enrichment of viral-associated signaling pathways, chemokine signaling, and immune-regulatory networks, reflecting the unique contribution of persistent HTLV-1 infection to disease pathogenesis.

ATLL differs from other peripheral T-cell lymphomas in that its malignant phenotype develops within the biological context of persistent HTLV-1 infection. In the present study, our transcriptomic and network analyses identified distinct molecular programs in ATLL, including alterations in extracellular matrix organization, chemokine signaling, immune regulation, cytoskeletal processes, and PI3K–Akt-associated signaling. These findings provide a molecular framework for understanding the biological characteristics of ATLL and its similarities and differences from PTCL.

Viral oncoproteins Tax and HBZ play complementary roles in HTLV-1-mediated leukemogenesis (Akbarin et al. 2017; Fajami et al. 2024). Tax is a potent activator of canonical and non-canonical NF-κB signaling and promotes activation of PI3K–Akt, JAK–STAT, CREB, and AP-1 pathways, thereby enhancing proliferation, survival, and inflammatory gene expression (Akbarin et al. 2024a; Farjami et al. 2026; Ramezani et al. 2017). In contrast, HBZ remains constitutively expressed in virtually all ATLL cases and modulates immune signaling, T-cell differentiation, and transcriptional regulation while promoting long-term persistence of malignant cells (Akbarin et al. 2017; Mozhgani et al. 2017). Together, these established activities provide a biological context in which the distinct transcriptional and signaling programs observed in ATLL may be interpreted; however, the contribution of individual viral proteins to the specific signatures identified in the present analysis remains to be experimentally determined.

Importantly, however, the present study was based on host transcriptomic profiles and downstream pathway and PPI-network analyses and did not directly quantify Tax or HBZ expression or experimentally assess their regulatory activity. Therefore, the observed enrichment of signaling, immune, chemokine, and microenvironment-associated programs cannot be attributed specifically to Tax- or HBZ-mediated regulation based solely on our data. Instead, the present findings identify transcriptional and network features that are compatible with the established biological consequences of HTLV-1-associated transformation. Integrating Tax- and HBZ-regulated gene signatures, viral transcript expression, and experimentally validated regulatory networks in future studies would help determine the extent to which these viral factors contribute to the specific molecular modules identified here.

The relationship between these established viral mechanisms and the molecular programs identified in our study is particularly relevant to the observed PI3K–Akt, chemokine, immune-regulatory, and microenvironment-associated signatures (Tarokhian et al. 2018; Akbarin et al. 2026; Ramezani et al. 2017). For example, the recurrent identification of the CXCL12–CXCR4 axis and extracellular matrix-associated genes in the PPI networks suggests that tumor–microenvironment interactions and cellular trafficking may contribute substantially to the HTLV-1 infection (Zargari et al. 2020; Letafati et al. 2024). Previous studies have linked HTLV-1-associated signaling to pathways involved in cellular survival and inflammatory responses, providing a plausible biological context for these observations (Zargari et al. 2020; Letafati et al. 2024). Nevertheless, this relationship should be regarded as an interpretive framework rather than direct evidence of Tax- or HBZ-dependent regulation. The present study therefore identifies candidate downstream molecular programs associated with ATLL, while mechanistic studies are required to establish their upstream regulatory relationships (Zargari et al. 2020; Letafati et al. 2024).

Furthermore, these observations suggest that although the downstream biological consequences partially converge, the upstream mechanisms driving these malignancies remain fundamentally different. ATLL, driven by HTLV-1 oncoproteins Tax and HBZ, provides a viral model for studying T-cell oncogenesis, revealing how chronic infection reshapes immunity, signaling, and genomic stability. This comparative framework deepens understanding of T-cell transformation and supports translational applications for targeted and preventive therapies.

From a translational perspective, the identification of shared hub genes and signaling pathways may have important clinical implications. Extracellular matrix regulators, chemokine signaling molecules, and complement-associated proteins identified as central network hubs represent potential biomarkers for disease stratification and may provide opportunities for therapeutic intervention. In particular, the recurrent identification of the CXCL12–CXCR4 axis supports previous evidence implicating this pathway in lymphoma cell migration, tissue infiltration, and interactions with the tumor microenvironment (Ishida et al. 2003; Machado et al. 2009; Rahimzada et al. 2023). Dysregulation of the CXCL12–CXCR4 signaling axis has been implicated in lymphoma cell trafficking, tissue homing, and resistance to therapy, suggesting that pharmacological inhibition of this pathway may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for aggressive T-cell lymphomas (Rahimzada et al. 2023; Ishida et al. 2003; Machado et al. 2009). Likewise, the consistent prominence of extracellular matrix proteins suggests that therapies targeting stromal remodeling may complement conventional approaches directed.

Our findings are generally consistent with previous transcriptomic and genomic studies demonstrating constitutive activation of NF-κB, PI3K–Akt signaling, immune regulation, and extracellular matrix remodeling in aggressive T-cell malignancies (Tarokhian et al. 2018; Farjami and Akbarin 2026; Akbarin et al. 2024b). Previous studies of HTLV-1-associated leukemogenesis have shown that viral proteins extensively remodel host transcriptional programs through activation of NF-κB, CREB, and STAT signaling pathways while simultaneously regulating chemokine expression, immune evasion, and stromal interactions (Tarokhian et al. 2018; Farjami and Akbarin 2026; Akbarin et al. 2024b). Likewise, molecular profiling studies of PTCL have highlighted remarkable biological heterogeneity characterized by immune activation, angiogenesis, and tumor microenvironment remodeling.

Our meta-analysis extends these observations by integrating multiple independent datasets and directly comparing ATLL with PTCL within a unified analytical framework, enabling identification of both shared oncogenic programs and disease-specific molecular signatures. Furthermore, the incorporation of protein–protein interaction network analysis allowed prioritization of biologically relevant hub genes and functional communities rather than relying solely on differential expression, thereby providing a systems-level perspective of molecular organization in these malignancies.

Study Limitations

An important consideration when interpreting extracellular matrix- and immune-related signatures is the biological origin of the analyzed samples. The microarray datasets included in this study were generated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and Fresh-frozen lymphoma tissue, rather than other type of tumor tissue. This consideration is particularly important for the extracellular matrix-, complement-, macrophage-, vascular-, and endothelial-associated signatures identified in the present analysis. Such signals may reflect, at least in part, differences in the proportion of stromal, endothelial, macrophage, and other accessory cell populations present in biopsy specimens rather than transcriptional alterations intrinsic to malignant T cells. Therefore, these pathways should not be interpreted as exclusively tumor-cell-autonomous mechanisms. In addition, although some extracellular matrix-related genes may reflect interactions with the immune microenvironment or activated accessory cells present within PBMC fractions, the results should not be interpreted as direct measurements of stromal tissue remodeling.

A limitation of the present study is that the analyses were performed using the processed microarray expression matrices available in the GEO repository rather than by reprocessing raw CEL files using a unified preprocessing pipeline. Consequently, background correction, normalization, and probe summarization followed the preprocessing associated with the deposited datasets. Similarly, probe annotation was based on the Affymetrix platform annotation available through GEO, and no additional probe remapping or filtering of ambiguous probe sets was performed. Although reprocessing raw CEL files with updated annotation resources may further improve cross-study consistency, the objective of the present study was to perform an integrative analysis of publicly available transcriptomic datasets using a standardized analytical framework. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and warrant validation in future independent studies.

Another limitation of the present study relates to the selection of the healthy control samples. Although all control samples were generated using the same microarray platform and were processed using an identical analytical workflow, detailed information regarding T-cell subset composition, including CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ cell proportions, was not consistently available in the original GEO datasets. Because ATLL predominantly arises from mature CD4⁺ T lymphocytes, differences in lymphocyte subset composition between patients and healthy controls may contribute to some of the observed transcriptional differences. Therefore, the identified molecular signatures should be interpreted with this limitation in mind and should be validated in future studies using purified, phenotypically matched CD4⁺ T-cell populations.

Consequently, study-specific effect sizes, heterogeneity estimates, and leave-one-study-out sensitivity analyses were not performed. Second, because the analysis was based exclusively on publicly available microarray datasets, additional experimental validation and independent patient cohorts were beyond the scope of the present study. Finally, although all included datasets were generated using the same Affymetrix platform and underwent quality assessment before integration, residual inter-study variability cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, the identified molecular signatures should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating observations that require validation in future independent studies.

Conclusion and future perspective

This study provides a comprehensive comparative integrative transcriptomic analysis of adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATLL) and peripheral T-cell lymphoma (PTCL), identifying both shared and disease-specific molecular signatures associated with these aggressive T-cell malignancies. It reveals shared hallmarks extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, PI3K-Akt signaling, and immune-inflammatory activation alongside subtype-specific modules, including cytoskeletal regulation in ATLL and angiogenic or metabolic alterations in PTCL. Central hub genes such as COL1A1, COL3A1, FN1, SPARC, THBS1, CXCL12/CXCR4, and complement components were consistently identified as key nodes within protein–protein interaction networks, may suggesting their potential as biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Contextualized with prior studies, these findings confirm the biological importance of ECM remodeling and immune modulation as convergent oncogenic features across viral (ATLL) and non-viral (PTCL) T-cell malignancies. The CXCL12–CXCR4 axis, PI3K-Akt pathway, and complement activation could possible emerge as promising shared therapeutic targets, while ATLL-specific cytoskeletal changes and PTCL-specific angiogenic/metabolic pathways support subtype-tailored treatments. Incorporating these biomarkers into diagnostic and prognostic models may enhance patient stratification and precision medicine strategies.

The network-based analyses prioritized several highly connected hub genes and functional modules that may contribute to disease pathogenesis. However, because these findings are derived from integrated computational analyses of publicly available transcriptomic datasets, the identified genes and pathways should be considered candidate biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets that require validation in independent patient cohorts and functional experimental studies. Overall, this work provides a systems-level framework for understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying mature T-cell lymphomas and offers a valuable resource for future mechanistic and translational investigations.

Future studies should validate the candidate biomarkers and molecular pathways identified in this study using independent clinical cohorts together with transcriptomic, proteomic, and functional experimental approaches. Integration of additional multi-omics datasets, including genomic, epigenomic, proteomic, and single-cell transcriptomic data, will further improve the understanding of the molecular heterogeneity of ATLL and PTCL. Furthermore, studies incorporating detailed clinical and therapeutic information will be important to determine the diagnostic, prognostic, and predictive value of the identified molecular signatures. Such investigations will help clarify whether the candidate hub genes and signaling pathways identified in this study can contribute to the future development of more effective and subtype-specific therapeutic strategies.
