Introduction

Moving plane technique
[
CL
]
,
[
GNN1
]
,
[
GNN2
]
,
[
L1
]
,
[
L2
]
, is a very important technique used by many researchers to study the radially symmetry of the solutions of elliptic partial differential equations. In
[
GNN1
]
and
[
GNN2
]
B. Gidas, W.M. Ni and L. Nirenberg used moving plane technique to prove the radial symmetry of solutions of the following equation

\Delta u+u^{\frac{n+2}{n-2}}=0,\quad u>0,\quad n\geq 3

in a ball
B_{R}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|x|<R\}
and in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
respectively. This moving plane technique was also used by W. Chen and C. Li
[
CL
]
to prove the radially symmetry of solutions of the equation,

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Delta u+e^{u}=0\quad\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\\
&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{u(x)}\,dx<+\infty.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, be a bounded connected
C^{2}
domain. Let
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
be a solution of the equation,

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Delta u=-1,\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
&u(x)=0,\quad\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}=c\quad\forall x\in\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.2)

where
\partial/\partial n
is the derivative with respect to the unit outward normal
n
to
\partial\Omega
.
In
[
S
]
J. Serrin used the moving plane technique to prove that

\Omega=B_{R}(x_{0})=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|x-x_{0}|<R\}\quad\mbox{ for some }x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\quad\mbox{ and constant }R>0

(1.3)

and

u(x)=\frac{R^{2}-|x-x_{0}|^{2}}{2n}\quad\forall x\in B_{R}(x_{0}).

(1.4)

More precisely for any unit vector
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let

T_{\lambda}^{\nu}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x\cdot\nu=\lambda\},\quad\Sigma_{\lambda}^{\nu}=\{x\in\Omega:x\cdot\nu<\lambda\},

and
x^{\ast}=x-2(x\cdot\nu-\lambda)\nu
be the reflection of a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
about the plane
T_{\lambda}^{\nu}
. Let

\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{\nu}=\{x\in\Omega:x^{\ast}\in\Sigma_{\lambda}^{\nu}\}.

(1.5)

J. Serrin
[
S
]
proved that for any unit vector
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists a constant
\lambda_{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\Omega
is symmetric about the plane
T_{\lambda_{\nu}}^{\nu}
and the solution
u
of (
1.2
) is symmetric about the plane
T_{\lambda_{\nu}}^{\nu}
. From this J. Serrin
[
S
]
concluded that the solution
u
of (
1.2
) in
\Omega
satisfies (
1.3
) and (
1.4
).
However it is not very obvious why (
1.3
) and (
1.4
) hold in
[
S
]
as the hyperplanes
T_{\lambda_{\nu}}^{\nu}
,
\nu\in S^{n-1}=\{\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|\nu|=1\}
, may not all intersect in a point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
so that one cannot apply the Cartan-Dieudonné Theorem
[
Ga
]
directly to conclude that
u
is radially symmetric.

Note that by the results of J. Serrin
[
S
]
and Hopf’s Lemma
[
PW
]
the solution

of (
1.2
) satisfies

\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}(x)>0\quad\forall x\in\Sigma_{\lambda_{\nu}}^{\nu},\nu\in S^{n-1},

(1.6)

\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}(x)<0\quad\forall x\in\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\lambda_{\nu}}^{\nu},\nu\in S^{n-1},

(1.7)

and

u(x)=u(x^{\ast})\quad\forall x\in\Sigma_{\lambda_{\nu}}^{\nu},\nu\in S^{n-1}.

(1.8)

In this paper we will give a simple proof that if
\Omega
is symmetric about the plane
T_{\lambda_{\nu}}^{\nu}
for any

and

), (
1.7
) and (
1.8
),

u
is radially symmetric about some point
x_{0}\in\Omega
and
\Omega
is a ball with center
x_{0}
and radius
R
for some constant
R>0
. Similar result holds for the domain

and function

satisfying some monotonicity and symmetry conditions. More precisely we will prove the following results:

Let

\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

Let

Proof of the main theorems

In this section we will prove Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
. We will let
e_{i}=(\delta_{ij})_{j=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for any
i=1,\dots,n
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
:
By the hypothesis of Theorem
1.1
for any
i=1,\dots,n
, there exists a constant
\lambda_{i}:=\lambda_{e_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\Omega
is symmetric about the plane
T_{\lambda_{i}}^{e_{i}}
and
u
satisfies (
1.6
), (
1.7
) and (
1.8
) with
\lambda_{\nu}=\lambda_{i}
,
\nu=e_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
. Then
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})\in\Omega
.
For any
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\Omega
, let
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
be given by

y_{i}=x_{i}-\lambda_{i}\quad\forall i=1,\dots,n.

(2.1)

Then in the new coordinates
y
, the domain
\Omega
is symmetric about the
y_{i}=0
plane for all
i=1,\dots,n
and the origin
0\in\Omega
. Hence we may assume without loss of generality that
\lambda_{i}=0\quad\forall i=1,\dots,n
. Then

0\in T_{\lambda_{i}}^{e_{i}}=\{y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:y_{i}=0\}\quad\forall i=1,\dots,n,

\frac{\partial u}{\partial y_{i}}(y)>0\quad\forall y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in\Omega,y_{i}<0,i=1,\dots,n

and

\frac{\partial u}{\partial y_{i}}(y)<0\quad\forall y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in\Omega,y_{i}>0,i=1,\dots,n.

Hence
u
attains its maximum at the origin.
Suppose there exists
\nu\in S^{n-1}
such that

0\not\in T_{\lambda_{\nu}}^{\nu}.

(2.2)

Let
P_{0}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
be the projection of the origin
onto the hyperplane
T_{\lambda_{\nu}}^{\nu}
. That is
\mbox{dist}\,(0,P_{0})=\mbox{dist}\,(0,T_{\lambda_{\nu}}^{\nu})
. Since by (
1.6
),

\frac{\partial u}{\partial\overrightarrow{OP_{0}}}>0\quad\mbox{ along }\overrightarrow{OP_{0}},

u(P_{0})>u(0)
which contradicts the fact that the origin is the maximum point of the function
u
. Hence no such
\nu
exists and all the hyperplanes
T_{\lambda_{\nu}}^{\nu}
,
\nu\in S^{n-1}
, will intersect
at the origin or at the point
x_{0}=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
in the original
x
-coordinates. Then
by (
1.8
) and the Cartan-Dieudonné Theorem
[
Ga
]
,
u
is radially symmetric
about the point
x_{0}
and
\Omega
is a ball with center at
x_{0}
.

\square

Proof of Theorem
1.2
: By the hypothesis of Theorem
1.2
for any
i=1,\dots,n
, there exists a constant
\lambda_{i}:=\lambda_{e_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
u
satisfies (
1.8
), (
1.10
) and (
1.11
) with
\lambda_{\nu}=\lambda_{i}
,
\nu=e_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
.
For any
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
be given by (
2.1
).
Then in the new coordinates
y
, the hyperplane
T^{e_{i}}_{\lambda_{i}}
becomes the hyperplane

\{y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:y_{i}=0\}.

Hence we may assume without loss of generality that
\lambda_{i}=0\quad\forall i=1,\dots,n
. Then

\frac{\partial u}{\partial y_{i}}(y)\geq 0\quad\forall y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n},y_{i}<0,i=1,\dots,n

and

\frac{\partial u}{\partial y_{i}}(y)\leq 0\quad\forall y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n},y_{i}>0,i=1,\dots,n.

(2.3)

Hence
u
attains its maximum (say
M
) at the origin.
Suppose there exists

\nu=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n})\in S^{n-1},\nu_{i}\neq 0\mbox{ for any }i=1,\dots,n,

(2.4)

such that (
2.2
) holds.
Let
P_{0}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
be the projection of the origin
onto the hyperplane
T_{\lambda_{\nu}}^{\nu}
. Then there exists a constant
0\neq\mu\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=\mu(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n}).

(2.5)

By (
2.4
) and (
2.5
),
a_{i}\neq 0
for any
i=1,\dots,n
. Without loss of generality we may assume that

a_{i}>0\quad\forall i=1,\dots,n.

(2.6)

Since
u
attains its maximum at the origin and by the hypothesis of the theorem,

\frac{\partial u}{\partial\overrightarrow{OP_{0}}}\geq 0\quad\mbox{ along }\overrightarrow{OP_{0}},

we have

u(sP_{0})=u(0)=M=\max_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u\quad\forall 0\leq s\leq 1.

(2.7)

By (
2.3
) and (
2.6
) the function

u(t_{1}a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})
is decreasing in
t_{1}\in(0,1)
. This together with (
2.7
) implies that

u(t_{1}a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})=u(0)=M\quad\forall 0\leq t_{1}\leq 1.

Repeating the above argument we get

\displaystyle u(t_{1}a_{1},t_{2}a_{2},\dots,t_{n}a_{n})=u(0)=M\quad\forall 0\leq t_{i}\leq 1,i=1,\dots,n

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle u(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{n})=u(0)=M\quad\forall 0\leq y_{i}\leq a_{i},i=1,\dots,n.

(2.8)

By (
1.9
) and (
2
) we get

f(u(y))=-\Delta u=0\quad\forall y\in\Pi_{i=1}^{n}[0,a_{i}]\quad\Rightarrow\quad u(y)=0\quad\forall y\in\Pi_{i=1}^{n}[0,a_{i}]

and contradiction arises. Hence no such
\nu
exists and
0\in T_{\lambda_{\nu}}^{\nu}

for any
\nu
satisfying (
2.4
). For any
\nu=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n})\in S^{n-1}
, we choose a sequence
\{\nu^{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset S^{n-1}
,

\nu^{k}=(\nu_{1}^{k},\dots,\nu_{n}^{k})
,
\nu_{i}^{k}\neq 0
for any
i=1,\dots,n,k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}
, such that
\nu^{k}\to\nu
as
k\to\infty
. By continuity of the map
g(\nu)=\lambda_{\nu}
,

\lambda_{\nu^{k}}\to\lambda_{\nu}\quad\mbox{ as }k\to\infty.

Then

0\in T_{\lambda_{\nu^{k}}}^{\nu^{k}}\quad\forall k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}\quad\Rightarrow\quad 0\in T_{\lambda_{\nu}}^{\nu}\quad\mbox{ as }k\to\infty.

Hence

0\in T_{\lambda_{\nu}}^{\nu}\quad\forall\nu\in S^{n-1}.

Thus all the hyperplanes
T_{\lambda_{\nu}}^{\nu}
,
\nu\in S^{n-1}
, will intersect
at the origin or at the point
x_{0}=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
in the original
x
-coordinates. Then
by (
1.8
) and the Cartan-Dieudonné Theorem
[
Ga
]
,
u
is radially symmetric
about the point
x_{0}
.

\square
