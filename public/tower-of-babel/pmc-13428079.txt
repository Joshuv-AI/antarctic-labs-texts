The Impact of Chronic Hepatitis D Virus Infection on Health‐Related Quality of Life and Fatigue: A Real‐World International Study
Authors: P Lampertico, R G Gish, N Reau, H Wedemeyer, M Buti, A Kaushik, A Yehoshua, C Burk, L Mirams, H Ellis, T Taylor‐Whiteley, M Rock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAs little is known about the disease burden of chronic hepatitis D virus (HDV) infection, this study examined health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) and fatigue at different disease stages. This was a real‐world quantitative cross‐sectional survey of patients in Germany, Italy, Spain and the USA. Patients were recruited via their treating physicians. Patients who had received bulevirtide for hepatitis B or treatment licensed for HDV were excluded. Of 211 patients, 41% were noncirrhotic (F0–F3); 20% had liver cirrhosis with normal function (compensated); 20% had liver cirrhosis (F4) with impaired function (decompensated); 19% had hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Mean (standard deviation; SD) age was 54.5 (11.5) years, 75% were male. ANOVA showed a significant decrease (indicating poorer HRQoL) in EQ‐5D‐3L visual analogue scale (VAS) scores and index values with worsening disease stage and increasing severity (all p < 0.0001). All domain scores of the Hepatitis Quality of Life Questionnaire v2 (except positive wellbeing) worsened with progressing disease and increasing symptom severity (all p ≤ 0.0001). Mean Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) total scores also increased, indicating greater fatigue, with worsening of disease and increasing symptom severity (both p < 0.0001). HDV infection significantly impacts patients' HRQoL and fatigue, particularly for patients at later disease stages. Treatments that slow disease progression, reducing the burden of HDV infection, are needed.Keywords: fatigue, health‐related quality of life, hepatitis D, hepatitis quality of life questionnaire, real‐world evidence

Introduction

HDV is the smallest virus infecting humans and depends on the hepatitis B virus (HBV) envelope proteins to enter hepatocytes and disseminate infection [1, 2]. Chronic hepatitis caused by the hepatitis D virus (HDV) is an extremely severe form of viral hepatitis, associated with the rapid development of liver‐related complications such as hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), as well as liver failure, a precursor for liver transplantation and death [2, 3]. In 2020, about 4.5% of patients with chronic HBV infection (HBsAg positive) were infected with HDV, corresponding to an estimated worldwide HDV infection prevalence of 12 million [4]. A recent literature review including 25 countries reported HDV prevalence among those infected with HBV to vary from 0.2% (in Hong Kong and Mexico) to 61.0% in Mongolia [5]. Recent reviews have estimated around 75,000 people to be infected with HDV in the United States of America (USA) [6] and around 15–20 million globally [7].

Historically, HDV infection has been treated with interferon (IFN) or pegylated interferon (PEG‐IFN) [1, 8]. However, this does not achieve a long‐term virological response, and is contraindicated in patients with decompensated cirrhosis or active autoimmune diseases [9]. The safety and efficacy of bulevirtide, an entry inhibitor of hepatitis B surface antigen, in the treatment of hepatitis due to HDV infection have been demonstrated in a phase 2 clinical trial [10] and a phase 3 clinical trial [11], and confirmed in a real‐world study [12]. Other novel antiviral agents are currently being assessed in late‐phase clinical trials [13].

The impact of liver disease on health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) and the importance of HRQoL as an outcome measure in the management of chronic liver disease are well‐recognised [14, 15]. HRQoL is known to be markedly impaired in advanced liver disease, to worsen with disease severity and to be poorer in patients with decompensated than compensated liver cirrhosis [16, 17].

Although large‐scale studies have demonstrated the impact on HRQoL of hepatitis due to viruses other than HDV, there is limited evidence regarding the effect of HDV infection on HRQoL. Analysis of data for over 12,000 patients with chronic hepatitis C in the Global Liver Registry demonstrated reduced HRQoL driven by fatigue and psychiatric comorbidities [18]. A recent meta‐analysis of observational studies reported that chronic HBV infection (a requirement for HDV‐related hepatitis) was associated with impaired HRQoL, with a particular impact noted in mental health domains [19]. One small‐scale observational study has shown that patients with chronic hepatitis due to HDV (CHD) infection had greater HRQoL impairments than patients with chronic hepatitis due to HBV (CHB) infection, especially in the worry, emotional, physical and activity domains [20].

Fatigue, which may be substantial, has been noted in patients with HDV infection [21, 22]. In qualitative interviews with patients with CHD in Germany, Italy and Spain, fatigue was the most frequently reported symptom [23].

The primary objective of this study was to capture robust, real‐world insights into HRQoL and fatigue among patients with CHD at varying stages of liver fibrosis or HCC, who were not receiving any licensed treatment for HDV infection.

Materials and Methods

Study Design, Data Source and Study Procedures

This was a noninterventional, observational study comprising a quantitative, cross‐sectional survey of patients with chronic HDV infection and varying stages of liver fibrosis (F0–F3), cirrhosis (F4 compensated or decompensated)or HCC. The study was conducted in Germany, Italy, Spain and the USA between 25th July 2022 and 11th October 2023. Any patient presenting for a scheduled consultation (virtually or in person) who met the selection criteria was eligible to participate in the study. Outside of routine consultations, physicians were asked to identify and invite patients from their practice who met the selection criteria, working backwards from the most recent consultation, to reduce the risk of selection bias.

Ethical Review

Before commencing the study, the Western Copernicus Group Institutional Review Board (WCG IRB; WCG Clinical Inc.; Princeton, USA) reviewed and approved the protocol, informed consent forms, the patient survey and the case report form.

Study Sample

No formal sample size calculations were employed; therefore, the sample size may have minimally impacted the precision of any estimates.

Details of patient recruitment are shown in the Appendix A and in Figure S1.

Study Participants and Eligibility Criteria

Physicians were required to be gastroenterologists, hepatologists, infectious disease specialists or primary care physicians (PCPs; USA only). To be eligible, physicians had to have a minimum caseload of five patients with HDV infection and provide their own consent to participate in the study.

Patients were required to have liver disease confirmed to be stage F0–F3 (noncirrhotic), or F4 compensated or decompensated cirrhosis and/or be diagnosed with HCC. Patients were required to be an English (in the USA), Spanish (in Spain), German (in Germany) or Italian (in Italy) speaker able to fully understand the language appropriate to the country in which their treating physician was based (patients were not required to be native speakers, but must have been competent in understanding and speaking the language appropriate to the country).

Patients were excluded from the study if they had been treated with IFN/peg‐IFN during the past 6 months, or had ever received any licensed treatment for HDV infection or bulevirtide for the treatment of HBV infection. Participants provided their own consent to participate. Other inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed in the Appendix A.

Data Collected

Physicians completed an electronic case report form for each patient they recruited, with the primary purpose of ensuring patients had a confirmed diagnosis of HDV infection. The physicians also recorded patient demographics (age, sex, healthcare insurance provider/coverage) and patient clinical characteristics (time since diagnosis of HDV infection, how HDV infection was acquired, stage of liver disease, tests conducted to confirm stage of liver disease, HCC status, time since diagnosis of HCC [if relevant], previous and current medications for HDV and HBV infections, comorbid conditions).

A paper consent form and questionnaire were completed by the patient. Each patient provided demographic data (age, sex, ethnicity, employment status, education) and completed patient‐reported outcome (PRO) questionnaires:

The Patient Global Impression of Severity (PGI‐S) is a single‐item index used to rate the severity of a specific condition from mild to very severe [24].

The Patient Global Impression of Severity (PGI‐S) is a single‐item index used to rate the severity of a specific condition from mild to very severe [24].

The EQ‐5D‐3 L [25, 26] is a measure of HRQoL across five dimensions: mobility, self‐care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression, rated on three severity levels. Index values are then produced using an appropriate value set and range from full health (1) to states worse than death (< 0), anchored at death (0). The visual analogue scale (EQ‐5D‐VAS) assesses self‐perceived health state ‘today’ on a scale from 0 (worst imaginable health) to 100 (best imaginable health).

The Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) [28] measures the severity and impact of fatigue over the past 2 weeks. Total scores range from 9 to 63 (based on the sum of responses to each item), with high scores indicating a high level of fatigue. The FSS also includes a 10 cm VAS assessing fatigue over the past 2 weeks on a scale from 0 (worst fatigue) to 10 (normal fatigue).

Analysis

In deriving index values from the EQ‐5D‐3L, the German value set was utilised [29] as the highest number of patients was recruited in Germany.

Descriptive statistics were produced, and comparisons were conducted between subgroups.

Descriptive analysis was conducted using IBM Survey Reporter, version 7.5, and comparisons were made using Stata v18. For descriptive analyses, the summary statistics reported depend on the variable being described, with categorical variables presented as frequency and percentage distributions, and continuous variables presented as means, standard deviations (SD) and ranges.

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by pairwise comparisons was used to test for differences in findings based on stage of disease and patient‐perceived severity from the PGI‐S. Pairwise comparisons were made between the four groups based on disease stage, and between the five groups based on PGI‐S severity score, using appropriate univariate statistical tests. Unordered categorical variables were compared using Fisher's exact test (or chi [2] test if Fisher's exact test could not be computed), ordered categorical variables were compared using Mann–Whitney test and continuous variables were compared using Student's t‐test. Bonferroni correction, which considers multiple testing [30], was applied for the pairwise comparisons, and a p value of < 0.05 was, therefore, taken to indicate statistical significance.

A total of 61 physicians recruited patients to the study, with 30 (49%), 12 (20%), 9 (15%) and 10 (16%) of those participating from Germany, Italy, Spain and the USA respectively. The majority of physicians were gastroenterologists (48%), with hepatologists, infectious disease specialists and PCPs (31%, 16% and 5% respectively) also participating.

Health‐Related Quality of Life

EQ‐5D‐3L

Patient responses for the five dimensions of the EQ‐5D‐3L are shown in the (Table S2).

A moderate or severe problem was reported by some patients for each of the five dimensions, with this ranging from 29% of patients for self‐care to 64% of patients for pain/discomfort. Most patients who had decompensated cirrhosis or HCC had problems with mobility, washing and dressing themselves and performing their usual activities. More than 80% of patients with decompensated cirrhosis or HCC had moderate or extreme pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression.

The findings from ANOVA showed both EQ‐5D VAS scores and EQ‐5D‐3L index values decreased (indicating poorer HRQoL) with advancing disease (both p < 0.0001).

Statistically significant differences were seen in mean EQ‐5D VAS scores between disease stages for all pairwise comparisons except decompensated cirrhosis versus HCC (Figure 1). Statistically significant differences were seen in mean index values between disease stages for all pairwise comparisons except F0–F3 fibrosis versus compensated cirrhosis and between decompensated cirrhosis versus HCC (Figure S2).

Discussion

Fatigue, the most common symptom of CHD [23], has been shown to impair HRQoL to an even greater degree than CHB [20]. This study aimed to gain insight into HRQoL and fatigue among patients with CHD and varying stages of liver fibrosis or HCC in an international, real‐world setting. We demonstrated poor HRQoL and substantial fatigue, especially in patients with liver cirrhosis or HCC. As these patients were largely of working age, and almost half were employed or were full‐time homemakers, impaired HRQoL and marked fatigue have the potential for economic as well as humanistic impact.

To extrapolate our findings to a broader population of patients with CHD, it is important to compare the characteristics of patients in our study with those reported from other sources. There is little published information on the demographics or clinical characteristics of patients infected with HDV. Patients with HDV infection in a study at four tertiary London hospitals had a median age of 40 years (interquartile range 31–51 years), 60% were male and 31% had cirrhosis [31]. Our study cohort was somewhat older (mean age 54.5 years), with a higher proportion of males (75%) and patients with cirrhosis or HCC (59%).

Patients included in our analysis had not received IFN/peg‐IFN in the 6 months prior to the survey (so that the effect of this treatment could not impact patients' evaluations of their HRQoL) and had never received medication licensed to treat HDV infection or bulevirtide for the treatment of HBV infection.

We assessed HRQoL using the EQ‐5D‐3L and HQLQv2. Many of the patients in our study experienced health problems based on their responses on the EQ‐5D‐3L, with statistically significant worsening HRQoL with progressing disease and with a co‐diagnosis of HCC. As might be expected, both EQ‐5D VAS scores and EQ‐5D‐3L index values showed that patients with more severe liver fibrosis had markedly poorer HRQoL than population norms. The mean EQ‐5D VAS scores reported for patients in our study were 78.7, 62.3, 51.5 and 49.3 in patients at fibrosis stage F0–F3, F4 compensated and decompensated cirrhosis and with HCC, respectively, while population norms for participating countries are 75.0, 77.1, 77.3 and 80.0 for Spain, Italy, Germany and the USA respectively [29]. The mean EQ‐5D‐3L index values reported for patients in our study were 0.90, 0.79, 0.57 and 0.55 in patients at fibrosis stage F0–F3, F4 compensated and decompensated cirrhosis and with HCC, respectively, while population norms for participating countries are 0.83, 0.90, 0.90 and 0.92 for the USA, Italy, Germany and Spain respectively [29].

We identified one publication describing EQ‐5D findings in patients with CHD infections [32]. MRY301 is an open‐label, randomised Phase 3 clinical trial comparing treatment with bulevirtide to no treatment in patients with CHD and liver disease with or without cirrhosis. EQ‐5D VAS scores at baseline were reported as 72, 0, 73.1 and 73.9 in the control, 2 mg and 10 mg bulevirtide groups respectively [32]. Other published studies have explored HRQoL based on EQ‐5D in other forms of chronic hepatitis. A meta‐analysis reported mean (SD) EQ‐5D‐3L index scores of 0.829 (0.021), 0.717 (0.021) and 0.595 (0.062) in patients with mild to moderate, compensated cirrhotic and decompensated cirrhotic chronic hepatitis C, respectively [33], reflecting the increasing impairment of HRQoL with increasing severity of liver disease observed in our study. Impairment in HRQoL in patients infected with the hepatitis C virus has also been shown to be associated with the severity of liver disease in a Swedish observational study [34] and a French real‐world study [35]. Our results are also consistent with a recent study in which reduced HRQoL was observed in HDV patients with compensated liver disease undergoing antiviral therapy with pegylated interferon alpha (compared to a reference population), using the Short Form 36 Health survey, both in physical as well as mental scores [36].

Qualitative and quantitative studies have confirmed the content validity of the HQLQv2 in HDV infection, with items understood and considered relevant to patients [23, 37]. The HQLQ has been used to demonstrate an improvement in HRQoL in patients with chronic HDV infection following 48 weeks of treatment with bulevirtide [38] and in patients with HCV infection after HCV eradication with antiviral agents [39]. Patients' responses on the HQLQv2 confirmed our findings with EQ‐5D‐3L that HRQoL worsened with disease progression. For three of the HQLQv2 domains (generic health distress, hepatitis‐specific limitation and hepatitis‐specific distress), patients with decompensated cirrhosis or with a codiagnosis of HCC had worse HRQoL impairment than those with fibrosis stage F0–F3 or compensated cirrhosis. Scores on the health distress, hepatitis‐specific limitations and hepatitis‐specific distress domains also showed poorer HRQoL with greater symptom severity. The exploratory analysis of data from the MYR301 clinical trial showed significant improvements in the HQLQ hepatitis‐specific limitations and hepatitis‐specific health distress domains following 48 weeks of treatment with bulevirtide, with greater improvements in patients with cirrhosis than those without cirrhosis [40].

Fatigue is a seminal symptom of CHD, and the content validity and relevance of the FSS have been established in this condition [23, 37]. The FSS has also been used to demonstrate fatigue in patients with HBV infection [2] and evidence of the reliability and validity of the FSS has been demonstrated in patients with chronic HCV infection [41].

Regardless of fibrosis stage or HCC status, patients in our study reported some degree of fatigue based on FSS total scores. FSS total scores were statistically greater as patients progressed to decompensated cirrhosis or if they had a co‐diagnosis of HCC. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first publication reporting FSS scores for an HDV cohort across multiple fibrosis stages. Mean (SD) FSS total score in hepatitis C has been reported as ranging between 40 (15) and 46 (15), depending on treatment status [42] Although we have not undertaken statistical comparison, this does suggest that patients with HDV infection and decompensated cirrhosis or HCC may have more severe fatigue (mean FSS scores in the current study were 50.9 and 50.5 in patients with decompensated cirrhosis and HCC respectively) than patients with hepatitis C. We also observed significant differences in mean FSS scores between severity levels based on the PGI‐S (greater fatigue with increasing severity of CHD symptoms).

A possible contributing factor to the poor HRQoL and high levels of fatigue observed in this study may be the limited treatment options for HDV infection. Although not approved for HDV infection treatment in the USA or Europe, peg‐IFN has been widely used in CHD over the past 20–30 years [1] and is the only treatment recommended in international guidelines [43, 44, 45]. Treatment with peg‐IFN suppresses HDV replication in 20%–30% of patients but has substantial side effects [1]. The only treatment with an approved indication for HDV infection currently is bulevirtide, but this is approved only in Europe, and is not reimbursed in many European countries [13, 45]. The exploratory analysis of MYR301 trial data suggests that bulevirtide was well tolerated, and that treatment with bulevirtide was associated with improved HRQoL in patients with CHD infection [33]. In line with the findings of the current study, in this exploratory analysis, baseline scores on the HQLQ, as well as the EQ‐5D and FSS, suggest an impact of CHD infection on HRQoL. The MYR301 analysis was stratified by cirrhosis versus noncirrhosis, with scores for these patient‐reported outcome measures suggesting a similar level of impact on HRQoL as seen in the F0–F3 and compensated patients in the current study [40].

Major strengths of the current study are its setting in international real‐world clinical practice; however, we acknowledge several potential limitations. The cross‐sectional design of this survey prevented any conclusions about causal relationships, although identification of significant associations was possible. A longitudinal study would allow changes in HRQoL and fatigue over time, and any associations with disease progression, to be assessed to understand fluctuations in symptoms. The patients in the current study had not been treated with IFN/peg‐IFN during the past 6 months, and had never received any licensed treatment for HDV infection or bulevirtide for the treatment of HBV infection. In future research, a longitudinal study to understand whether virological response to HDV treatment impacts HRQoL and fatigue, and whether improvements are correlated with HDV‐RNA and/or ALT would also be valuable. As this was a descriptive study to characterise the burden associated with HDV, there was also no disease control cohort of patients with cirrhosis of other origin, which would determine the HRQoL impact specifically related to CHD infection. Furthermore, among the patients with HCC, some of the impact on HRQoL could be due to HCC rather than CHD infection. Recall bias and missing data, common limitations of surveys, might have affected findings. However, physicians could refer to patient records while completing electronic case report forms, thus minimising these issues. As part of the electronic case report form, information was collected regarding whether a FibroScan was conducted to determine fibrosis categorisation; however, it was not a requirement for fibrosis stage to have been determined via FibroScan, and FibroScan values were not collected (as the primary purpose of the electronic case report form was to obtain physician‐confirmed diagnosis). Notwithstanding these limitations, this study provides valuable insight into HRQoL and fatigue among untreated patients with CHD infection and varying stages of liver fibrosis or HCC.

In conclusion, our study established that HDV infection carries a substantial burden of disease, particularly for patients at later stages of the disease and in those with a concomitant HCC diagnosis, as demonstrated by patients' self‐reported HRQoL and fatigue. Furthermore, this study clearly demonstrates that HDV infection has a detrimental effect on HRQoL that worsens as the disease progresses, supporting the belief that all patients with HDV infection should be treated. Safe and effective treatments that slow disease progression would likely reduce the impact of HDV infection, improving HRQoL and decreasing the burden of fatigue.

Patient Recruitment

Initially, it was planned to recruit an international sample of 200 patients across the four countries (~50 patients per country), with quotas for the numbers of patients with different levels of fibrosis, compensated and decompensated cirrhosis and HCC. In the initial phase of recruitment, a sample of 194 patients was recruited, 168 of whom were not currently receiving any licensed treatment for HDV infection. To make this sample of untreated patients more robust, a study extension was agreed upon with the aim of recruiting an additional 45 patients (~27 patients with decompensated cirrhosis and no concomitant diagnosis of HCC, and ~18 patients at any fibrosis stage with HCC).
