Mendelian randomisation analysis to explore the association between cathepsins and bipolar disorder
Authors: Chenshuang Dong, Yecheng Han, Siqiao Chen, Guiling Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionBipolar disorder is a psychiatric condition characterized by the coexistence of depression and mania. Diagnosis of this disorder can be challenging due to limited pathologic and experimental tools. Treatment compliance is often poor due to medication side effects. Although cathepsin is known to play a significant role in diseases such as tumors and osteoporosis, its role in psychiatric disorders is not yet fully understood.ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between cathepsin in the blood circulation and bipolar disorder.MethodsThe causal relationship between cathepsin and different subtypes of bipolar affective disorder was explored using bidirectional Mendelian randomization analysis and multivariate analysis.ResultsIt was found that cathepsin H level was a protective factor for type II bipolar disorder. Introduction

Bipolar disorder (BD) is a psychiatric illness characterized by mood fluctuations, encompassing manic episodes, depressive episodes, and other psychiatric symptoms [1, 2]. It affects approximately 1% of the global population [3]. In accordance with the current diagnostic criteria set forth in the DSM-V, Type I BD is characterised by episodes of mania that are preceded and followed by mild mania and major depression. In contrast, individuals with type II BD exhibit a more chronic profile, with a greater duration of depressive episodes over the course of the illness than those with type I. Type II BD presents with recurrent episodes of major depression that persist for a longer duration [4–6]. The prevalence of mania in type I BD may be higher than the depression observed in type II BD. The treatment of BD is long-term endeavor that necessitates collaborative efforts from both family and society [2, 7]. Presently, there are no reliable biomarkers for this disorder. Cathepsins have been implicated in the progression of neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease [8]. Memory impairment is also a common symptom in most psychiatric disorders [9–11]. Further investigation into the relationship between cathepsins and psychiatric disorders could provide significant breakthroughs in the diagnosis and treatment of these conditions.

Cathepsins are proteolytic enzymes found in organisms, classified based on their active sites into cysteine cathepsins (S, F, G, H, B, O, E, Z, L2), aspartic cathepsins (D and E) and serine cathepsin (G) [12]. These enzymes are involved in processes such as neural structure and function, underscoring their extracellular activity [13]. Psychiatric disorders may arise from changes in neural function and structure, which are accompanied by alterations in axonal growth and cortical microglia. The secretion of cathepsins in this context serves as a valuable tool for studying psychiatric disorders.

Recent research has increasingly emphasised the role of cathepsins in psychiatric disorders [14]. A study using mice demonstrated that the absence of cathepsin D led to symptoms analogous to human BD, and treatments effective for BD were also beneficial in these mice [15, 16]. Concurrent studies in humans are currently underway, although some lack control trials in involving healthy subjects [17].

There is mounting evidence that genetic variation plays a crucial role in psychiatric disorders. Mendelian randomization (MR) is a causal inference method based on genetic variation, utilizing genetic variatnts as Instrumental variables (IVs) to examine and quantify the relationship between exposure factors and outcomes [18]. In this study, we employed univariate and multivariate MR to predict the causal relationship between nine cathepsins in the blood circulation and type I BD and type II BD, with the aim of identifying suitable diagnostic targets.

Materials and methods

Study design

Exposure data were sourced from the GWAS catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/). Genetic data on various cathepsin levels (µg/L) in blood were obtained from the INTERVAL study, which included 3301 European individuals [19]. Informed Consent was obtained from all individuals, and the study received approval from the National Center for Research Ethics Services (11/EE/0538). To address linkage disequilibrium (LD) among single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), we applied stringent filtering criteria: an r2 threshold of 0.001 and a 10,000 kb window were used to exclude SNPs with an r2 greater than 0.001 within this range of the most significant SNPs. The association between cathepsin levels and IVs was considered significant at P < 5 × 10− 6. Detailed information on the SNPs included in the cathepsin analysis is provided in the Supplementary Data 1.

Data sources

Data on different subtypes of BD were obtained from the GWAS catalog database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/). The genome wide study of type I BD encompassed 108,781 cases, and the study of type II BD included an equal number of cases. This research was reviewed and approved by the respective institutional ethics committees, and all participants provided informed consent. BD-related data on migraine, obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD), anorexia nervosa, attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and cerebral dopamine neurotrophic factor (CDNF) were obtained from the GWAS catalog database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/). The studies were likewise reviewed and approved by the relevant institutional ethics committees, and all participants provided informed consent.

Selection of IVs

MR uses genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer causal relationships between cathepsins and BD. The selection criteria for IVs comprised three core standards: (a) they must be strongly associated with the exposure factor. (b) they should be free from heterogeneity. (c) they should influence the outcome exclusively through the exposure factor. Following the exclusion of weak IVs, we calculated the F-statistic using the formula F = [(N-K-1)/K]×[R2/(1-R2)], where N is the sample size of the GWAS analysis, K is the number of IVs, and R2 indicates the proportion of variance in the exposure explained by each IV [20]. An F value greater than 10 indicates that the selected IVs are unlikely to be weak and can effectively predict the exposure factor. The detailed methodology is depicted in Fig. 1.

Statistical analysis

We used the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method as the main method for estimating the overall effect size [21]. We verified the stability of the MR results using the MR-Egger approach [22], weighted mode, simple mode and the weighted median method [23]. Subsequently, these individual MR estimates were combined using random-effects inverse variance meta-analysis to produce an overall summary value. This was performed using the R TwoSampleMR software package [23]. IVW method, which is a weighted linear regression of the correlation of SNPs with outcomes and the correlation of SNPs with exposure factors. The weighted median method weights individual MR estimates in proportion to their precision. We used odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to determine whether there was a causal relationship between cathepsins and different subtypes of BD, depression and anxiety disorders. To assess the validity of the hypotheses, sensitivity analyses and statistical tests were conducted to estimate the effects of the hypotheses. Heterogeneity of SNPs was subsequently assessed using Cochran’s Q-test. p-values greater than 0.05 indicated the absence of heterogeneity [24]. The MR-PRESSO global test and MR-Egger intercept were used to rule out data pleiotropy [25]. The R MR-PRESSO software package was used for the MR-PRESSO global test, outlier test [25]. Reverse MR was used to assess the possible inverse relationship between exposure factor and outcome, i.e. BD as exposure factor and cathepsins as outcome. Multivariate MR analysis is an analytical method developed from univariate MR analysis, which has the advantage that the direct causality of each exposure factor can be assessed with a single analysis. This procedure was performed using the “MendelianRandomization” software package [24]. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.2.2. An OR < 1 indicates that the exposure factor is protective in predicting the occurrence of the disease outcome. Conversely, if OR > 1, exposure was categorised as a risk factor for the disease outcome, i.e. exposure promoted the outcome. For all resulting p-values we corrected using the FDR method to control the detection of false positive results.

Results

Defining the causal link between various cathepsins and different histological subtypes of BD

Our study aimed to investigate whether any of the nine cathepsins have a causal relationship with BD. Initially, we employed a two-sample MR analysis to assess whether each of the nine cathepsins (S, F, G, H, B, O, E, Z, L2) functions as a risk or protective factor for either type I or type II BD. Univariate MR analysis indicated that elevated levels of cathepsin H significantly reduce the risk of type II BD but not type I BD (Fig. 2). The results of the IVW method were: P = 0.003, OR = 0.816, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 0.713–0.934. This finding was corroborated by several other analytical methods (Table 1). Across five methods, including IVW, no other cathepsins were found to be associated with the risk of either type I or type II BD. Furthermore, MR-Egger intercept and MR-PRESSO tests did not indicate any evidence of pleiotropy (Supplementary Data 2). The corrected p-values are shown in Supplementary Data 3.

Reverse MR analysis to assess the causal relationship between cathepsins and different subtypes of BD

Finally, we conducted a multivariable MR analysis to evaluate the relationship between genetic susceptibility involving multiple cathepsins and the risk of various BD subtypes. The multivariable MR analysis demonstrated that cathepsin H (P = 0.009, OR = 0.844, 95% CI = 0.743–0.959) continues to be a significant protective factor for type II BD when adjusted for other cathepsins. Consistent with other cathepsins, cathepsin H showed no statistically significant causal relationship with type I BD (Fig. 3). Detailed information can be found in Supplementary Data 5.

Causal relationship between cathepsins and BD related CDNF

Considering the potential causal relationship between cathepsins and BD, we employed two-sample MR to investigate the causal links between nine cathepsins and BD-related symptoms or pathogenesis, aiming to uncover mechanisms through which cathepsin H or other cathepsins might ameliorate BD. Our two-sample MR results indicated that these nine cathepsins were not associated with migraines in BD (Supplementary Data 6). Furthermore, no causal relationships were identified between the cathepsins and comorbid conditions of BD, such as ADHD, OCD, and anorexia nervosa (Supplementary Data 6).

When examining CDNF as an outcome in the pathogenesis framework, cathepsin H (P = 0.020, OR = 0.848, 95% CI = 0.738–0.975) exhibited a significant causal relationship with CDNF (Fig. 4). The corrected p-values are shown in Supplementary Data 3.

These findings highlight the critical role of cathepsin H in the regulation of CDNF, suggesting its potential contribution to BD pathophysiology. This underscores the need for further research to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and to explore cathepsin H as a therapeutic target for BD.

Discussion

BD is influenced by both environmental and genetic factors [26, 27]. Neurobiochemical factors, neuroendocrine factors, and brain imaging changes are equally important aspects of BD [28–32]. progression following a BD diagnosis is complex and multifaceted. Cathepsins, a family of proteases, have been implicated in various mental disorders. For instance, cathepsin K has been detected in brain tissue and has diagnostic value for schizophrenia [33, 34]. However, the relationship between cathepsins and BD remains unclear.

Previous studies have suggested associations between cathepsins D and B with BD [15, 17], but our MR findings do not support these claims. Given the heterogeneity in clinical and experimental studies, MR analysis can reduce potential biases inherent in traditional observational research. Thus, additional research is needed to clarify these associations. To date, no studies have specifically examined the link between cathepsin H (CTSH) and BD. CTSH, a lysosomal cysteine protease with unique endopeptidase activity, plays significant roles in both normal physiological and pathological processes [35]. CTSH gene is located near a region on chromosome 15 identified as a susceptibility locus for BD and other mental illnesses [36]. The precise mechanisms by which cathepsin H influences BD remain to be elucidated.

Our study, utilizing univariable MR analysis, found no causal relationship between any of the nine cathepsins and type I BD. Notably, cathepsin H emerged as a protective factor for type II BD. Reverse MR analysis indicated that cathepsin B levels might decrease as type I BD progresses. Multivariate MR analysis further corroborated the protective role of cathepsin H in type II BD. These findings suggest potential cathepsin targets related to BD, though further is required to substantiate these MR results.

The association between cathepsin H and type II BD is more pronounced than with type I BD, indicating a potentially stronger link to depressive phenotypes. Other proteases associated with depression, such as cathepsin C, cathepsin B, and cathepsin K, have also been studied [33, 37, 38]. However, there is currently no evidence connecting cathepsin H to depression. Our findings provide a foundation for future research.

Given the potential causal relationship between cathepsin H and BD, we investigated the mechanisms underlying this association. Initially, we examined the symptoms, noting that typical BD manifestations include migraines triggered by manic episodes [39]. However, our findings indicated no significant causal link between cathepsin H and migraines. BD, as a psychiatric disorder, often coexists with conditions such as anorexia nervosa, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder [40–43]. Two-sample MR analysis suggested that cathepsin H is not significantly related to these comorbidities either. This led us to hypothesize that cathepsin H might be involved in the underlying etiology of BD, particularly in relation to neurotransmitters. Dopamine, a key neurotransmitter and adrenergic receptor agonist, is implicated in anxiety and depression [44–46]. Dopaminergic neurons, which produce and release dopamine, play a crucial role in these processes [47, 48]. We focused on CDNF, a protein known for its potential to protect and restore the function of dopaminergic neurons, particularly in the context of Parkinson’s disease [49]. Our investigation into the interaction between CDNF and cathepsin H revealed that cathepsin H might reduce the production or efficacy of CDNF. These findings suggest that cathepsin H could be a pivotal protein in the pathophysiology of BD, meriting further research to elucidate its role in BD onset and progression.

By exploring the multifaceted interactions between cathepsin H, neurotransmitters, and neurotrophic factors, this study provides novel insights into the potential biochemical pathways implicated in BD, paving the way for future therapeutic targets.

Conclusion

This study identified specific relationships between cathepsins in blood and BD. Cathepsin H was found to be a protective factor for type II BD, while cathepsin B might decrease as type I BD progresses. Cathepsin H may be causally related to BD via CDNF. Further research is needed to comprehensively understand the mechanisms through which cathepsins influence BD. This study offers preliminary scientific evidence for exploring potential risk factors associated with BD.
