In Reply: Pseudo-Index Events cannot be Configured on the TriNetX Platform
Authors: Tsai-Kun Wu
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

On behalf of the authors, we thank Dr Joshua Wang for his careful and constructive reading of our study and for raising the important question of how the index event for our control cohort was configured.1 We are grateful for the opportunity to clarify our methods, to acknowledge the limitation he correctly identifies, and to report a series of bias-corrected re-analyses that we have since performed in direct response to his critique.

First, we state plainly how the 2 cohorts were indexed. The treated cohort was indexed at the first sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT2i) prescription recorded after the autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) diagnosis, whereas the control cohort was indexed at the date of the first recorded ADPKD diagnosis. Both are inclusion-criterion index events that the TriNetX Advanced Analytics platform supports natively, and accordingly, all analyses were conducted entirely on the platform. The inaccuracy lay solely in our description of the control index as a pseudo-index date corresponding to each matched treated counterpart; it was not such a date, and we regret the imprecise wording, which we will correct. We believe this also resolves the apparent contradiction Dr Wang identifies.

Second, we accept that this choice introduces immortal time bias, exactly as Dr Wang describes.2 Because the control cohort was indexed at diagnosis while the treated cohort was indexed at a necessary later prescription date, control patients could contribute dialysis events in the interval immediately after diagnosis that no treated patient could contribute. This asymmetry would inflate the apparent benefit of SGLT2i, and the early separation of the Kaplan–Meier curves is consistent with it. We acknowledge this without reservation.

Third, prompted by Dr. Wang’s critique, we repeated the analysis using the shared-index, new-user design recommended by Lin et al,2 in which both cohorts are assigned a common index event (the first ADPKD diagnosis) and follow-up is aligned across groups.3.4 We examined 3 such specifications: a shared-index analysis with mature follow-up; a shared-index analysis in a contemporary extended cohort; and a shared-index analysis incorporating a 1-year exposure-lag (a 366-day landmark), which directly removes the immortal time interval.

Across every bias-corrected specification, the protective association persisted and remained statistically significant. As expected, the point estimate attenuated toward the null in the more conservative designs—from 0.35 in the original analysis to 0.52-0.58 in the contemporary extended and 1-year exposure-lag analyses—consistent with a genuine contribution from the immortal time bias Dr Wang identified, although the confidence interval in the extended cohort approached unity. Crucially, however, the association did not disappear even in the one-year exposure-lag landmark design, which eliminates the immortal interval, the hazard ratio remained below 1.0 with a confidence interval excluding the null (HR, 0.523; 95% CI, 0.291-0.941). We therefore interpret the original estimate as partly inflated by immortal time bias, but not wholly an artifact of it.

This interpretation is reinforced by our head-to-head comparison of SGLT2i versus tolvaptan, in which both cohorts were indexed on a postdiagnosis prescription date so that the immortal time interval is symmetric and largely self-canceling; there, the 2 therapies did not differ significantly (HR, 0.71; 95% CI, 0.37-1.39), while tolvaptan-versus-control recovered a protective association (HR, 0.66; 95% CI, 0.51-0.84) consistent with the established randomized evidence for tolvaptan.

We remain mindful of both the value and the limits of real-world data here. Patients without diabetes with ADPKD were excluded from the pivotal SGLT2i trials, and federated networks such as TriNetX—spanning many countries and practice patterns—offer a rare opportunity to observe hard endpoints such as dialysis initiation in this population. We sought to honor these constraints through 1:1 propensity-score matching across 21 covariates with formal balance diagnostics, and now through the shared-index re-analyses above. Residual confounding, the absence of total kidney volume, genotype, and adherence data, and reliance on administrative coding remain genuine limitations that these analyses do not fully resolve.

We would like to add a brief word on why this question merits such methodological care. The role of SGLT2i in ADPKD remains genuinely unsettled: the 2025 KDIGO ADPKD guideline advises against their use to slow kidney function decline,5 reflecting concerns that osmotic diuresis may raise vasopressin and that short-term and animal studies have shown increases in total kidney volume,6 whereas recent real-world cohorts7 and a small randomized crossover trial8 suggest favorable effects on kidney function and hard endpoints. Our study sits within this contested evidence base. We do not present it as resolving the question; rather, the very fact that the evidence is divided underscores why each analysis must meet a high methodological standard, and we are grateful that this correspondence has helped us meet it.

We thank Dr Wang once again for a critique that has materially strengthened this work. We will correct the description of the index event methodology and incorporate these bias-corrected analyses as part of this reply. We agree that the central question—whether SGLT2i meaningfully slow progression in non-diabetic ADPKD—can be answered definitively only by the prospective randomized trials now under way, and we hope that rigorously conducted real-world analyses such as these can help inform their design and interpretation.

Sincerely,

Tsai-Kun Wu, on behalf of all co-authors.
