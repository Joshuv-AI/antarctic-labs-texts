Genome-wide identification and functional characterization of ABA and salt stress responsive AITRs transcriptional repressors genes in sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas)
Authors: Saddam Hussain, Xiaoxiao Jing, Faheem Tariq, Xiaoping Wang, Wei Wang, Guimin Li, Ronghua Wu, Yanlei Chang, Siyu Chen, Shucai Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAbscisic acid (ABA) is a hormone that regulates plant responses to abiotic stress through complex signaling pathway. ABA-induced transcription repressors (AITRs), are a family of transcription factors involved in ABA signaling and abiotic stresses responses. Sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas) is an important crop for food, raw materials, and bioenergy, but its productivity is often affected by environmental stresses.ResultsIn this study, 19 IbAITRs were identified in sweet potato genome and they were distributed across 16 chromosomes. IbAITRs gene structures were highly conserved, with no introns observed and phylogenetic analysis grouped them into two major clades with AITRs from other species. The promoter revealed multiple hormone and stress-responsive cis-elements, including ABRE motifs associated with ABA signaling. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis showed that IbAITRs are expressed in multiple tissues, particularly in shoots, young leaves, initiating tuberous roots, and mature tuberous root, and their expression is induced by ABA and salt treatments. Transfection assays demonstrated that IbAITR11, IbAITR12, and IbAITR13 are nuclear proteins that function as transcriptional repressors.ConclusionOur results indicate that IbAITR genes are involved in ABA- and salt-responsive regulatory networks in sweet potato. This study provides a foundation for future functional studies and highlights IbAITRs as potential targets for improving abiotic stress tolerance through molecular breeding. Background

Owing to sessile lifestyles, plants facing ever-changing environmental stimuli, like biotic and abiotic stresses through their life cycle [1, 2]. Among a range of abiotic stresses, drought and salinity are significant stress factors which reduce plant growth, reduce crop yields and potentially to affect the global food supply [3]. Drought and salt stresses intersect in their signaling pathways, both of which cause osmotic stress and subsequent accumulation of a plant hormone, abscisic acid (ABA) [3, 4].

ABA is an essential phytohormone that regulates the plant growth and development [5, 6]. It is a key component in the manner in which plants respond to environmental stress, such as drought, salinity, heat and cold [5–8]. Being a major stress hormone, ABA rises considerably in these conditions stimulating or inhibiting particular genes that mediate responses to stress, via a complex signal transduction pathway, which is governed by a number of key factors. The ABA receptors, PYR1/PYL/RCAR (Pyrabactin resistance 1/PYR1-like/regulatory component of ABA receptor), and the negative regulators, A-group PP2C (PROTEIN PHOSPHATASE 2 C), and positive regulators, SnRK2 (NON-FERMENTING 1 (SNF1)-RELATED PROTEIN KINases), are regarded as the key controllers of the ABA signaling pathway. Also, type bZIP (basic region leucine zipper) transcription factors such as ABF/AREB/ABI5 are important in the ABA response [9–14].

Several other transcription factors, in addition to the ABF/AREB/ABI5-type bZIP transcription factors, are involved in the regulation of the ABA signaling. These are APETALA2/ETHYLENE RESPONSIVE FACTOR (AP2/ERF), basic/helix-loop-helix (bHLH), Homeodomain-leucine zipper (HD-Zip), MADS-box, MYB, NAC, GARP (Golden2, ARR-B, Psr1), WRKY, AITRs and WDR (WD40-repeat) proteins [7, 15–17].

Our previous work has identified a new family of AITRs that function as negative regulators in the ABA signaling pathway in plants [7]. AITRs play a key role in the modulation of plant responses to abiotic stresses such as drought and salinity. In Arabidopsis, loss-of-function mutations on AITRs cause improved tolerance to these stresses and overexpression of AITR5 causes lower tolerance to salt [7, 18]. Notably, the knockout of all six AITRs genes in Arabidopsis enhances tolerance to drought and salinity while maintaining plant fitness [18]. AITRs are conserved across angiosperms, for example, the expression of a cotton AITR gene was capable of restoring abiotic stress tolerance in Arabidopsis aitr1 mutants, supporting the idea that AITRs in crops may function similarly to those in Arabidopsis [19].

Further studies in soybean have demonstrated that CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of several GmAITRs genes can enhance salt tolerance [20]. However, soybean aitr mutants exhibit increased ABA sensitivity [20], which differ from the decreased ABA sensitivity observed in Arabidopsis aitr mutants [18]. In tobacco, it has also been shown that NtAITRs are responsive to ABA and act as transcriptional repressors, negatively regulating drought tolerance [21]. In tomato, the SlAITR3 gene, induced by ABA and salt stress, negatively regulates salt tolerance by affecting ROS clearance and ion homeostasis [22]. On the other hand, the sweet potato AITR gene IbAITR5 identified in the cultivar “weiduoli” has been shown to regulates carotenoid biosynthesis [23]. was not detected in “Beauregard”, possibly due to cultivar-specific genomic variation.

Sweet potato, a hexaploid species (2n = 6x = 90) in the Ipomoea genus of the Convolvulaceae family, is known for its complex genome and significant genetic variation [24, 25]. As one of the most cultivated root crops worldwide, sweet potato plays an essential role in food security by serving as a staple carbohydrate source and by providing health-promoting compounds such as β-carotene, anthocyanins, and dietary fiber. Ranked as the seventh most important crop in the world after rice, wheat, maize, potato, barley, and cassava, it is a major source of carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and dietary fibers [26]. Particularly in areas such as Southeast Asia and Africa, sweet potato is one of the major dietary staples, playing a role in food security [27, 28]. However, its productivity can be impaired by several abiotic stresses, especially drought, salinity, and temperature extremes, and can lead to a significant decrease in growth, yield and nutritional quality [29]. The genomic data used in this study were taken from I. batatas Beauregard genome released by the SweetGAINS Project (https://sweetpotato.uga.edu/sweetgains_beauregard_v1_asm_anno.shtml).

In this study, we performed genome wide analysis of AITRs transcription factors in sweet potato. Our study consisted of analyses of protein physicochemical properties, phylogenetic analyses, chromosomal distributions, gene structures, conserved motifs and cis-regulatory elements in promoter. In addition, the expression patterns of selected IbAITRs in various tissues and under ABA and salt stress conditions were also examined. Furthermore, subcellular localization and functional assays showed that IbAITR11, IbAITR12 and IbAITR13 are nuclear proteins and transcriptional repressors. Together, these findings provide a sound basis from which to study functional roles and evolutionary significance of AITRs genes in sweet potato.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

In the present study, the sweet potato cultivar ‘Jewel’ was grown in the experimental field of Linyi University, Linyi, Shandong Province, China. Plant materials were collected from this field for gene expression analysis across different tissues. To assess the response of AITRs genes to ABA and salt stress, plants were grown in a controlled environment with daytime and nighttime temperatures set at 28 °C and 22 °C, respectively. Humidity levels were maintained between 70% and 85%, with a 12 to 14-hour daily photoperiod and a light intensity ranging from 400 to 600 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹.

For protoplast transfection assays, Arabidopsis thaliana Columbia-0 (Col-0) seeds were planted directly into soil-filled pots and cultivated under controlled conditions. The growth chamber had a constant temperature of 22 °C, 16-hour of light and 8 h of dark cycle, and light intensity was approximately 125 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹.

Identification and physicochemical analysis of IbAITRs in sweet potato

Arabidopsis AITRs protein sequence [7], were used to find homologs in the I. batatas Beauregard genome (https://sweetpotato.uga.edu/index.shtml). Using TBtools with an E-value cutoff of 1e-5, we identified 19 putative IbAITR genes through BLASTP analysis.

The basic physicochemical parameters of the IbAITR proteins such as sequence length, molecular weight, isoelectric point, instability and aliphatic indices as well as GRAVY values were analyzed using the tool Prot Param available through the Expasy server (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). Predicted subcellular localizations were obtained through CELLO2GO (https://cello.life.nctu.edu.tw/) as described by [30].

Chromosol location

Chromosomal mapping of the IbAITRs genes in sweet potato, we utilized the GFF annotation file obtained from the Genomic Tools for the Sweet GAINS project. The physical positions of these genes were extracted and visualized using the MG2C (MapGene2Chromosome, http://mg2c.iask.in/mg2c_v2.1/).

Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis

Amino acid sequences of IbAITR proteins were comprehensively aligned using the ClustalW method integrated within the TBtools platform [31]. To explore the evolutionary relationships of the IbAITRs, protein sequences for AITRs were extracted from several plant species such as Arabidopsis lyrata, Arabidopsis thaliana, Boechera stricta, Brassica rapa, Capsella rubella, Carica papaya, Oryza sativa, Citrus sinensis, Eutrema salsugineum, Gossypium arboreum, Gossypium hirsutum, Gossypium raimondii, Theobroma cacao, Nicotiana tabacum, Ipomoea batatas and Solanum lycopersicum. These sequences were aligned using ClustalW to generate a comprehensive multiple sequence alignment. Phylogenetic trees were generated through the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) approach in MEGA version 11.0 [32], with tree confidence assessed using 1,000 bootstrap iterations. Visualization and refinement of the phylogenetic relationships were performed using the iTOL web-based tool (https://itol.embl.de/).

Gene structure, motif and cis-acting element analysis

The coding sequences (CDS) and genomic DNA sequences of the target genes were analyzed using the Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS) platform http://gsds.gao-lab.org/ [33]. Conserved motifs in IbAITRs protein sequences were found by MEME Suite software (https://meme-suite.org/meme/) with maximum of 10 motif specified, default parameters maintained. Both the structures of the genes and the motifs were generated using TBtools [34]. In order to investigate cis-regulatory elements, 2000 bp upstream sequences from the start codon (ATG) of each IbAITRs gene were downloaded from the sweet potato genome (https://sweetpotato.uga.edu/index.shtml) with TBtools [34]. The Plant CARE database (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) was used to identify the cis-acting elements of the promoter regions, and the results were visualized using TBtools [34].

ABA and NaCl treatment

To assess IbAITRs expression in response to ABA and NaCl treatments, the tubers of sweet potato were grown. After 1 month, uniform and healthy slips were selected and transferred into distilled water for another 2 weeks to provide enough root development. Once roots had emerged, seedlings were treated with to 100 μm ABA, 150 mM NaCl or distilled water (control) for 6 h. Following the treatment, roots and leaves were immediately collected and snap frozen in liquid nitrogen then stored at -80 °C for future RNA extraction.

RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis and qRT-PCR

For the analysis of IbAITRs gene expression, RNA was isolated from different tissue of a sweet potato including: shoots, young and mature leaves, stems, fibrous roots, initiating and mature tuberous roots. These samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C until extraction of RNA. Total RNA preparation was performed with EasyPure Plant RNA Kit (TransGene Biotech, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The reverse transcription was performed with 1 ug of RNA using EasyScript Simple One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (TransGen Biotech) kit. qRT-PCR analysis was performed and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method with IbACTIN as the internal reference gene. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three biological replicates, each with three technical replicates. The primers used for qRT-PCR analysis of IbAITRs is provided in Supplementary Table S1. The primers of the IbACTIN control gene primers were previously described [35].

Plasmid constructs

The LexA-Gal4:GUS reporter constructs and effector constructs GD, GD-IbAITRs, GFP-IbAITRs, and LD-VP were described in previous studies [7, 36, 37]. To generate the 35S:GD-IbAITRs constructs for transcriptional assays in Arabidopsis protoplasts, full length open reading frames (ORFs) of selected IbAITRs were amplified. These IbAITRs were then cloned, sequenced, and analyzed, which confirmed the sequences were a IbAITR11, IbAITR12, and IbAITR13. Based on this identification, these particular IbAITRs were selected for further study and were inserted in-frame with the GD tag into the pUC19 vector under the 35S promoter. For subcellular localization experiments, the GD tag in the 35S: GD-IbAITRs constructs was replaced with a GFP tag to generate the 35S: GFP-IbAITRs constructs. The primers for these cloning procedures are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Plasmid preparation, protoplast isolation, and transient transfection

Plasmid DNA of both reporter and effector constructs was purified by GoldHi Endo Free Plasmid Maxi Kit (CWBIO), following the protocol of the manufacturer. Protoplasts were isolated from 3 to 4 weeks old Col-0 wild-type Arabidopsis plants by being digested with enzymes and gently mechanically separating cells from the leaf tissue [7, 37, 38]. For transient transfection, 10–20 ug of plasmid DNA was combined with 200 ul protoplasts and then 210 ul of polyethylene glycol (PEG) solution was added. After a 20 min incubation at room temperature, the mixture was diluted using 800 ul W5 solution and incubated for 8 more minutes [36, 37]. The protoplasts were then centrifuged and resuspended in 1mL of W1 solution and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 16–18 h. GFP fluorescence for subcellular localization was visualized using the Olympus BX61 or GUS activity for transcriptional assay was quantified using SynergyTM HT fluorescence microplate reader (BioTEK, after 20–22 h of incubation) [36, 37].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed by using a two-tailed Student’s t-test to evaluate the difference between treatments and control groups, where P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The calculations for the t-test were performed in Microsoft Excel, and data visualization was carried out using GraphPad Prism software.

Results

Identification of IbAITRs

In previous studies, AITRs encoding genes have been identified in cotton [19] and soybean [20], with results consistent with those in Arabidopsis [7]. To identify AITRs in I. batatas, we used the known amino acid sequences of the Arabidopsis AITRs [7]. A total of 19 typical AITRs transcription factors were identified in sweet potato (Table 1). Further characterization of the AITRs proteins demonstrated differences in their physicochemical characteristics such as protein length, molecular weight (MW), isoelectric point (pI), instability index, aliphatic index, grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) and difference in there predicted subcellular localization and exon-intron structure (Table 1).

Summary of identified AITRs genes in sweet potato

As indicated in Table 1 the IbAITRs gene sequences varied in length, with the encoded proteins ranging from 286 amino acid residues in IbAITR12 and its homeolog proteins IbAITR12a, IbAITR12b, IbAITR12c and IbAITR12d, due to the hexaploidy nature of sweet potato to 335 amino acid residues in IbAITR6. The molecular weights of these proteins varied, with values ranging from 32.20 kDa in IbAITR12, IbAITR12a, IbAITR12b, IbAITR12c, IbAITR12d to 36.55 kDa in IbAITR6. The theoretical pI values ranged from 9.05 in IbAITR10, IbAITR11 to 9.7 in IbAITR14, indicating that all of them are basic protein, with the instability index varying from 43.36 in IbAITR14 to 51.91 in IbAITR10. Only IbAITR9 and IbAITR10 exhibited instability indices greater than 50, indicating that these two proteins are unstable, while the others are moderately stable. The aliphatic index varied between 65.76 in IbAITR6 and 70.17 in IbAITR14. The GRAVY values ranged from − 0.526 in IbAITR14 to -0.647 in IbAITR6, suggesting that the IbAITRs proteins are predominantly hydrophilic, although the degree of hydrophilicity varies.

Subcellular localization predictions revealed that IbAITR1 to IbAITR6 are localized in the nucleus, while IbAITR7 to IbAITR14 are localized in the nucleus and in chloroplasts. Furthermore, the analysis revealed that all IbAITRs genes are intron-free, same as AITRs in Arabidopsis, cotton, soybean and tobacco plants have only one exon [7, 19–21].

Chromosomal distribution of IbAITRs

Chromosomal localization analysis showed that IbAITR genes are spread unevenly across several chromosomes in I. batatas. Specifically, these genes are located on BrgChr03 (A–D, F), BrgChr11 (A–F), and BrgChr12 (A, C–F). As shown in Fig. 1, most of these chromosomes have a single IbAITR gene. However, exceptions were observed on Brgchr12E and Brgchr12F, which contain two and three of IbAITRs genes, respectively.

Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis of IbAITRs

To determine the characteristic domain sequences of each IbAITR protein from sweet potato, a multiple sequence alignment of 19 AITRs proteins was conducted using Bioedit tool (Fig. 2). The results showed that all IbAITRs proteins possess a relatively long conserved amino acid sequence, especially at both N and C terminal regions. In the N-terminal region, some IbAITRs contain a fully conserved motif, while others display only a partially conserved version. Near the C-terminus they exhibit a partially conserved LxLxL motif, known to play a role in the transcriptional repression functions of Aux/IAA proteins like IAA3, IAA17, and IAA19, as well as ERF repressors such as AtERF3, AtERF4, and NtERF3 [7, 36, 39].

To explore the evolutionary pattern of AITRs genes in sweet potato, a phylogenetic tree was generated using 19 IbAITR protein sequences identified from the sweet potato genome, together with homologs from 16 other species, including Arabidopsis thaliana, Nicotiana tabacum, Solanum lycopersicum, and representative members of the Brassicaceae and Malvaceae families (Fig. 3). An alignment-based neighbor-joining phylogeny (MEGA11) resolved the AITR family into three major clades (Groups A-C).

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the sweet potato IbAITRs are evolutionarily restricted to two of these three clades. Specifically, IbAITR7–IbAITR14 form a distinct subclade within Group A, where they share a most recent common ancestor with tobacco NtAITRs, which have been reported to negatively regulate drought tolerance [21]. In contrast, IbAITR1–IbAITR6 cluster within Group B, demonstrating a closer evolutionary relationship to stress-responsive AITRs from other species, namely tomato SlAITR3 (salt responsive) [22], and Arabidopsis AITRs associated with ABA insensitivity and reduced salt/drought tolerance [7]. Furthermore, the complete absence of sweet potato sequences in Group C suggests a possible lineage-specific gene loss event after the divergence of the Ipomoea lineage from the common ancestor it shares with the other species in that group.

Cis-acting element analysis of the IbAITRs promoter

The 2 kb region upstream of the coding sequence (CDS) of IbAITRs genes in sweet potato was chosen as the promoter region. Cis-acting elements were identified (Fig. 4a) with the Plant CARE on-line tool. The result of analysis showed that there were various types of core promoters and cis-acting elements, which were divided into seven groups, including light-responsive elements, hormone-responsive elements, stress-related elements, development-related elements, promoter-associated elements, site-binding elements, and other unidentified elements (Fig. 4b).

Among the various cis-elements, light-responsive motif like G-box, GT1-motif, TCT-motif, and GA-TA-motif were most commonly found. Furthermore, a considerable number of ABA-responsive elements (ABRE) were present at the promoter regions of most IbAITR genes (Fig. 4c), These findings support previous reports that the ABRE motif in AITRs genes is involved in regulating transcription in response to ABA signaling [7].

The analysis also showed abundant environmental stress-related elements with a large number of IbAITR genes containing MYC, MYB, and STRE response elements, indicating that IbAITRs may play a role in stress responses. This is consistent with findings that AITR genes in soybean are sensitive to salt stress [20]. Additionally, knock out of the AITR gene of Arabidopsis has proved to improve resistance to both drought and saline stress [18]. In contrast, the tomato SlAITR3 protein plays a negative regulatory role in salt tolerance of the tomato, indicating the possibility of species-specific variation in regulatory functions of IbAITRs genes under stresses.

Furthermore, elements associated with development, promoters and other unidentified factors were found, suggesting that IbAITR genes can be involved in various biological processes such as development and gene regulation. This is consistent with the results from previous studies [7, 18, 20], that reported similar patterns of regulation in related genes.

Motif analysis of IbAITRs

Analysis of the distribution of conserved motifs in IbAITRs gene family revealed 10 conserved motifs (Motif 1 to Motif 10) of IbAITRs genes (IbAITR1-4, IbAITR6-14) (Fig. 5). These motifs are found at different positions on the gene sequences showing both conserved and variable patterns. Motifs 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, and 7 have an occurrence in all genes, indicating that maybe these motifs have conserved functions in the genes, and Motifs 4 and 8 are present in IbAITR1-4 and IbAITR6-11 and Motif 9 in IbAITR1-4. Motif 10 is more selectively found in IbAITR1-4, IbAITR6. Sequence logos for each motif (Fig. 5) point out the conservation of amino acids at each position. Motifs 1, 2, 3, 5, 6 and 7 have high conservation, thus suggesting their functional importance, whereas Motifs 4, 8, 9 and 10 have a greater level of sequence variability. Notably, Motifs 2, 5, and 8 hold the LXLXL domain which has been linked to the function of transcriptional repression [36], in proteins of the Aux/ IAA family. Given IbAITRs gene family is also categorized as a transcriptional repressor family, the presence of this domain implies the role as a transcription repressor.

Expression pattern of IbAITRs

Previous studies have shown that AITRs in cotton and tomato are differentially expressed in varied tissues [19, 22]. To investigate the roles of IbAITRs in sweet potato, we have analyzed the expression patterns of IbAITRs in different tissues and organ. RNA was extracted from different tissues including shoot, first leaf, young leaf, mature leaf, stem, fibrous root, Initiating and mature tuberous root (Fig. 6a). Quantitative RT-PCR was performed to assess the expression pattern of IbAITRs. Across eight tissues (Fig. 6a), IbAITR1/1a/2/3/4/6, IbAITR7/8/9/10/11, IbAITR12/12a/12b/12c/12d, and IbAITR13/13a/14genes showed distinct tissue-specific expression patterns. All these were detected into the shoot, young leaf, initiating and mature tuberous root. IbAITR1/1a/2/3/4/6 were also significantly expressed in the first leaf, mature leaf, and fibrous root. IbAITR7/8/9/10/11 were significantly expressed in the mature leaf and stem. In contrast, IbAITR12/12a/12b/12c/12d and IbAITR13/13a/14 were not significantly detected unlike the shoot, young leaf, and initiating tuberous root (Fig. 6b).

Transcriptional response of IbAITRs to ABA and salt stress

Previous studies have revealed that AITRs are induced by ABA treatment and abiotic stresses [7, 19–22]. To test whether IbAITRs exhibit a similar transcriptional response and are a conserved function in abiotic stress, we conducted a transcriptional analysis of IbAITRs under ABA and salt stress using the methodology described in our previous study [20]. Similarly, IbAITRs responded differently to exogenous ABA and salt stress (Fig. 7). In leaves, IbAITRs were highly induced by salt stress, whereas the response to ABA was variable. Only IbAITR7/8/9/10/11 were not significantly induced by ABA (Fig. 7). In roots, all IbAITRs genes were strongly induced by both ABA and salt stress (Fig. 7).

Subcellular localization and transcriptional activity of IbAITRs

AITRs, as reported in previous studies, function as transcriptional repressors [7–21]. This repressor motif, such as the LxLxLx motif, has been shown to be essential for transcriptional repression [36, 39]. Given the presence of this motif in IbAITRs (Fig. 2), it is believable that these proteins function similarly as transcriptional repressors.

To test this, IbAITR genes were cloned, and due to their high sequence similarity, the cloned products were verified by sequencing. IbAITR11, IbAITR12, and IbAITR13 were successfully cloned and used for subsequent experiments. We then examined the subcellular localization of these IbAITRs by transfecting GFP-IbAITRs plasmids, along with an RFP (Red Fluorescent Protein) vector as a control, into Arabidopsis protoplasts. The expression of the GFP-tagged IbAITRs allowed us to monitor their localization, while the RFP vector was used to label the nucleus. We then checked for GFP fluorescence to determine the distribution of IbAITRs. The fluorescence signal from the GFP-IbAITRs was predominantly observed in the nucleus, indicating that these proteins are localized there. (Fig. 8a). Next, we examined the transcriptional activity of IbAITRs from the same Arabidopsis protoplast transfection assay, which is typically used in previous studies [15, 19, 37]. Plasmids containing GD-IbAITRs were co-transfected along with the reporter LexA-Gal4:GUS and the activator LD-VP into protoplasts of Arabidopsis [37]. The GD and GD-VP plasmids were positive controls. When compared to the co-transfection of LD-VP with the GD control, all GD-IbAITRs effectors significantly inhibited LD-VP mediated activation of the reporter gene. These results demonstrate that IbAITR11, IbAITR12, and IbAITR13 function as transcriptional repressors (Fig. 8b), suggesting that other IbAITR family members may also possess similar transcriptional repression activity, although this remains to be experimentally verified.

Discussion

ABA, a key stress phytohormone that play a critical role in regulating plant growth and development, especially under stress condition, by modulating key regulators that either activate or repress ABA-responsive genes, thereby influencing the plants stress responses [5–14, 18].

In our previous researches, we identified AITRs as a novel family of transcriptional repressors that inhibit ABA responsive genes, thereby contributing to the regulation of abiotic stress tolerance in Arabidopsis [7, 18]. These finding suggests a crucial role for AITRs in plant stress responses to stress. Since AITRs are conserved across angiosperms [7], we extended our study to various crops to explore the potential of AITRs for CRISPR/Cas9-based molecular breeding aimed at improving abiotic stress tolerance. In soybean, gmaitr mutants generated by CRISPR/Cas9 were moderately salt-tolerant plants, although they also showed slightly increased tolerance to ABA, in contrast to the response obtained in Arabidopsis aitrs mutants [18]. This divergence in ABA sensitivity highlights the species-specific functional divergence of AITRs in response to environmental stresses. The distinct responses observed in Arabidopsis and soybean suggest that the role of AITRs may be influenced by evolutionary factors specific to each species native habitat.

In cotton, the function of GhAITRs in regulating responses to ABA and drought stress further supports the potential of AITRs to improve abiotic stress tolerance [19]. Additionally, targeting NtAITRs in tobacco via CRISPR/Cas9 editing improved abiotic stress tolerance, demonstrating the broader applicability of AITRs across species [21]. Recent studies in tomato, where the AITR gene was edited, also pointed to a potential improvement in abiotic stress tolerance [22]. This study suggests that modifying AITRs can enhance stress tolerance, but balancing this with potential growth trade-offs is crucial to maintaining plant productivity. In previous reports, IbAITR5 has been demonstrated to be involved in carotenoid biosynthesis, suggesting that some IbAITRs may have specialized functions in certain plants [23]. However, IbAITR5 was not detected in the ‘Beauregard’ cultivar, possibly due to cultivar-specific genomic variation, as it was identified in the ‘Weiduoli’ cultivar but not in ‘Beauregard.

In this study, a total of 19 IbAITRs transcription factors were identified in I. betatas (Table 1), a number greater than the numbers found in A. thaliana [7], cotton [19], soybean [20], tobacco [21], and tomato [22]. These IbAITRs are unevenly distributed across the chromosomes, with the highest number found on Brgchr12A, which contains three IbAITR genes, followed by Brgchr12F, with two members. A few other chromosomes each contain one IbAITR gene, while some have none (Fig. 1). The distribution pattern suggests a non-random arrangement of these genes across the genome.

Previous studies have suggested the existence of a high sequence similarity between proteins, which often share functional characteristics across species [40]. In order to investigate the relationships among of IbAITRs proteins, a phylogenetic analysis was conducted, which is important for providing valuable insights about the possible functions of IbAITRs proteins in sweet potato. The observation that IbAITRs are clustered in two evolutionarily distinct clades along with AITRs of known function from other organisms strongly indicates evolutionarily conserved function within these lineages (Fig. 3). Specifically, the close relationship between the Group A IbAITRs (IbAITR7–14) and the drought responsive NtAITRs from tobacco [21], and the Group B IbAITRs (IbAITR1–6) with the salt responsive and ABA insensitive AITRs from tomato and Arabidopsis [7, 22], suggest that sweet potato has conserved two functionally specialized subfamilies of AITR proteins. Therefore, the evolutionary conservation of Group A and Group B IbAITRs containing known stress-responsive genes strongly suggests their key role in the abiotic stress tolerance of sweet potato. Future work should focus on the experimental validation of the proposed roles of Group A in drought response and of Group B in salt tolerance by means of ABA signaling pathways.

Previous research has demonstrated that AITRs transcription factors play an important role in the plant response to different abiotic stresses, such as drought and salt stress [7, 19–22]. In this study, we analyzed the 2000 bp upstream region of the IbAITRs genes to determine if there are potential cis-acting elements in the promoter region by using Plant Care database. The analysis showed the presence of elements related with stress response, light regulation, hormone signal, growth and development (Fig. 4). These results indicate that IbAITRs genes may be involved in regulation of plant responses to abiotic stresses.

Furthermore, motif analysis (Fig. 5) of the IbAITRs gene family reveals conserved and variable patterns that are functional. Motifs 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, and 7, which can be found in all the IbAITR genes, may indicate important conserved functions. In comparison, Motifs 4, 8, 9, and 10 are differentially distributed, suggesting specialized roles. The high conservation of these motifs indicates the functional importance of these motifs, whereas sequence variability in others probably indicates adaptive diversification. Notably, the LxLx domain in Motifs 2, 5 and 8, which is associated with transcriptional repression in Aux/IAA family [36, 39], supports the possibility for IbAITRs to also be involved in gene repression.

To further investigate the roles of IbAITR genes, we studied the expression patterns of IbAITR genes in different tissues including shoots, 1st leaf, young leaves, mature leaves, stems and fibrous roots and both initiating and mature tuberous roots (Fig. 6). IbAITR1/1a/2/3/4/6, IbAITR7/8/9/10/11, IbAITR12/12a/b/c/d, and IbAITR13/13a/14 were predominant expressed in the shoot, young leaf, initiating tuberous root, and mature tuberous root. These results suggest that these gene sets may play an important role during early stages of triggering the initiation of tuberous root and mature tuberous root development. Notably, IbAITR1/1a/2/3/4/6 were also highly expressed in the first leaf, mature leaf and fibrous root, which may suggest their role in the development of both leaves and fibrous roots. Similarly, the expression of IbAITR7/8/9/10/11 in the mature leaf and stem provide a resolved clue to the involvement of these AITR in leaf maturation and stem development. In contrast, the absence of expression of IbAITR12/12a/b/c/d and IbAITR13/13a/14 in tissues other than the shoot, young leaf, and initiating tuberous root probably means that these genes are specialized to functions in early organogenesis, especially in storage root development. While these expression patterns give useful insights, additional experimental validation is required to verify the precise role of these genes in various tissues as well as their involvement in the growth and development of sweet potato.

Our analysis also showed that the IbAITRs promoters contain stress-related and ABA-responsive elements (Fig. 4a), and the expression of IbAITRs genes was induced by both salt stress and ABA treatment (Fig. 7). These results are consistent with the expression profiles of AITR genes from Arabidopsis, soybean, tobacco, cotton and tomato [7, 19–22]. However, is that IbAITR7/8/9/10/11 did not give significant responses to ABA. These results suggest that at least some of the IbAITRs genes may be involved in salt stress response via ABA signal pathway. Building on previous findings in Arabidopsis protoplasts [7], we studied several IbAITRs proteins and found that they localized to the nucleus and inhibited reporter gene expression in Arabidopsis protoplasts (Fig. 8). Overall, our results suggest that IbAITRs genes in sweet potato may play important regulatory roles in response to abiotic stresses. Experimental evidence obtained for IbAITR11, IbAITR12, and IbAITR13 indicates their function as transcriptional repressors, while the roles of other IbAITRs are currently inferred from bioinformatic analyses and expression patterns and require further functional characterization. Although this study provides comprehensive genomic and expression analyses of the IbAITR family, functional characterization was performed for only a subset of members. Future studies using genetic approaches such as gene knockout or overexpression will be necessary to fully elucidate the biological roles of other IbAITRs.

Conclusion

In this study, we performed a comprehensive genomic and functional analysis of the IbAITR family in sweet potato, identifying 19 IbAITR genes. Our findings reveal that these genes are crucial for stress response, as evidenced by their conserved structure, stress-related cis-elements in their promoters, and specific induction under ABA and salt stress. Functional experiments confirmed that IbAITR11, IbAITR12, and IbAITR13 are nuclear-localized transcriptional repressors. The functions of other IbAITR members remain to be experimentally validated. Additionally, their distinct expression patterns across tissues, particularly in developing roots and shoots, suggest they may also have roles in plant development. This work positions IbAITRs as prime candidates for enhancing drought and salinity tolerance in sweet potato and provides a foundation for future studies aimed at confirming their functional role in improving resilience.
