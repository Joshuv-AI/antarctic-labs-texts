Burden and determinants of betel quid use and oral health status among tribal adults in Wayanad, Kerala
Authors: Manjusha V Nair, Parvathi Sureshkumar, Rohith Suresh, Devi Unnikrishnan, Adil Papparath Usman, Sobha George, Brilly M Rose
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBetel quid chewing, a culturally embedded habit among tribal populations, poses significant oral health risks due to its addictive and carcinogenic potential. However, data on dependence patterns and their impact on oral health among Kerala’s tribal communities remain scarce. Hence this study aimed to estimate the prevalence of betel quid chewing, assess dependence levels and associated factors, and examine their relationship with oral health status among tribal adults in Wayanad, Kerala. MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 283 tribal adults aged ≥ 18 years from selected habitations in Wayanad by convenient sampling. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews covering sociodemographic details, chewing habits, and the 16-item Betel Quid Dependence Scale (BQDS). Oral health was assessed using the DMFT (Decayed, Missing, and Filled Teeth) index, and participants were also screened for features suggestive of oral submucous fibrosis and visible mucosal lesions. Data were analysed using SPSS version 22 and Jamovi software. 2.6.2.ResultsThe prevalence of betel quid chewing among tribal adults in Wayanad was 67.5% (95% CI: 62.0–72.9). Among current users (n = 191), 56.5% (95% CI: 49.5–63.6) exhibited high dependence. In multivariable analysis, separated/widowed individuals had significantly higher odds of high dependence (AOR = 3.93; 95% CI: 1.13–13.62; p = 0.031). The mean DMFT score was 6.43 ± 8.51, indicating a substantial burden of dental caries. In multivariable linear regression analysis, occupation and high betel quid dependence was independently associated with higher DMFT scores, (β = 2.546; 95% C.I = 0.532 – 4.560; p = 0.014).ConclusionBetel quid use and dependence are highly prevalent among tribal adults in Wayanad and are linked to poorer oral health. Targeted community-based interventions integrating cessation support and routine oral screening are essential.Keywords: Tribal, Betel quid, Oral health, BQDS, DMFT

Introduction

Betel quid chewing remains one of the most widespread yet culturally normalized psychoactive substance use practices in the world, affecting hundreds of millions of individuals across Asia and the Western Pacific [1]. Its use extends predominantly across South Asia, Southeast Asia, and the Western Pacific regions, and continues among migrant communities originating from these areas [1]. Betel quid is a heterogeneous preparation that typically consists of areca nut wrapped in betel leaf, along with slaked lime, and often tobacco [2, 3]. Areca nut contains the psychoactive alkaloid arecoline, which produces stimulant and autonomic effects and contributes to dependence [2, 4]. Slaked lime facilitates the release and absorption of arecoline by creating an alkaline environment, thereby enhancing its systemic effects [2, 4]. The addition of tobacco introduces nicotine and tobacco-specific nitrosamines, further increasing addictive potential and carcinogenic risk [2, 5]. Betel leaf (Piper betle) contains several bioactive compounds, including phenols and flavonoids, which exhibit mild stimulant, antimicrobial, and antioxidant properties; however, these effects do not offset the harmful consequences of areca nut and tobacco use [6, 7]. Despite its social acceptability, betel quid has been conclusively classified as a major public health hazard. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) has designated areca nut and betel quid, with or without tobacco, as Group 1 carcinogens, confirming sufficient evidence for oral carcinogenicity in humans [2]. Beyond cancer, a growing body of evidence demonstrates that chronic betel quid consumption is associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes, including hypertension, arrhythmias, and heart failure [8, 9].

Betel quid chewing is often initiated early in life, sustained through adulthood, and reinforced by its low cost, easy availability, and deep cultural acceptance [1]. In addition to its direct carcinogenicity, chronic exposure accelerates periodontal destruction, dental caries, oral submucous fibrosis, and tooth loss, all of which contribute to impaired nutrition, reduced quality of life, and increased healthcare costs [2]. These findings underscore that betel quid is not merely a local habit but a substance with far-reaching systemic health consequences.

India bears one of the largest global burdens of betel quid consumption. A substantial proportion of users consume areca nut with added tobacco, markedly magnifying carcinogenic risk [2]. The impact of this exposure is reflected in India’s disproportionately high burden of oral cancer, which ranks among the leading causes of cancer-related morbidity and mortality in the country. The initiation of betel quid use often occurs during adolescence and persists across the life course, sustained by intergenerational transmission, occupational fatigue management, stress coping, and entrenched sociocultural beliefs [1]. Despite these realities, betel quid use has historically received far less public health attention than cigarette smoking or alcohol consumption, particularly in rural and marginalized populations.

Among the Indian population, Scheduled Tribes represent a uniquely high-risk and underserved community. Tribal populations experience a convergence of structural vulnerabilities including persistent poverty, geographic isolation, limited access to healthcare services, intergenerational illiteracy, and social exclusion [10, 11]. These social determinants substantially amplify the prevalence of substance use as well as its downstream health consequences. National-level evidence consistently shows that tobacco and areca nut consumption are significantly higher among tribal populations compared to the general population [10]. Despite this elevated risk profile, tribal communities remain systematically underrepresented in oral health surveillance and preventive care programs, resulting in substantial evidence gaps and limited policy prioritization [10].

Kerala, although often cited for its favourable health indicators, harbours stark internal inequities in health status among its tribal populations. Wayanad district, located in the Western Ghats, is home to one of the largest concentrations of Scheduled Tribes in the state, with a tribal population exceeding 1.48 lakh as per the 2011 Census [7]. The district comprises several indigenous communities including the Paniya, Kurichya, Mullakuruma, Kattunayaka, and Adiya tribes. These groups continue to face persistent deprivation across nutrition, education, sanitation, and access to preventive and curative healthcare services [10]. Geographic inaccessibility, workforce shortages, and cultural barriers further limit timely utilization of formal healthcare systems in these forested and hilly terrains.

Community-based studies from Wayanad reveal an alarmingly high prevalence of substance use among tribal adults. A recent population study among indigenous tribal groups in Kerala demonstrated that betel quid chewing was the most prevalent substance use behavior, affecting nearly one-third of adults, with frequent co-use of tobacco and early age of initiation [12, 13]. Socio-behavioural studies from Wayanad further document that betel quid chewing is deeply embedded in daily life and social interactions, driven by occupational stress, peer reinforcement, and cultural normalization [14]. The widespread practice of adding tobacco to betel quid exposes users to a compounded burden of areca nut–mediated systemic toxicity and tobacco-related carcinogenic risk, substantially accelerating oral disease progression and malignant transformation.

Despite the well-established biological hazards of betel quid at the global level, data specific to the prevalence, oral health consequences, and behavioural determinants of betel quid use among Kerala’s tribal adults remain critically limited [14]. This lack of robust, locally generated evidence has important public health implications. Oral potentially malignant disorders often remain asymptomatic in their early stages and are rarely detected through opportunistic screening in tribal areas. Delayed diagnosis of oral cancer is common, resulting in advanced-stage presentation, poorer treatment outcomes, and substantial economic hardship for already vulnerable households [2]. Furthermore, the coexistence of betel quid use, poor oral hygiene practices, undernutrition, and restricted access to dental care creates a syndemic environment that amplifies morbidity at both individual and community levels [10].

From a public health perspective, oral diseases are among the most prevalent non-communicable conditions globally and share common risk factors with other chronic diseases, including tobacco use and poor health behaviours [5]. In India, national programmes such as the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Cancer, Diabetes, Cardiovascular Diseases and Stroke (NPCDCS) and the National Oral Health Programme (NOHP) emphasize early detection and risk factor reduction; however, tribal populations remain underserved with limited access to preventive and curative oral healthcare [15, 16]. Understanding patterns of betel quid use and dependence in such settings is essential to inform targeted interventions and strengthen integration of oral health within primary healthcare systems.

In the absence of reliable epidemiological data from tribal settings, the design and implementation of effective community-based oral cancer prevention, early detection, and cessation programs remain severely constrained. In this context, the present study aimed to estimate the prevalence of betel quid chewing, assess dependence levels and associated factors, and examine their relationship with oral health status among tribal adults in Wayanad district, Kerala.

Methods

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 283 adult members of Scheduled Tribe communities residing in selected tribal settlements of Wayanad district, Kerala. Individuals aged 18 years and above, belonging to any recognized tribal group and residing in the hamlet for at least six months, were included. Those with mental illness on treatment or unwilling to participate were excluded.

The sample size was calculated using the formula , taking a prevalence (p) of 64.2% from a previous study conducted among tribal populations in Wayanad by Afsal Sadath et al. [7], with a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96) and an absolute precision of 6%.

Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire capturing sociodemographic characteristics, substance use behaviours, and oral hygiene practices. Dependence on betel quid was assessed using the Betel Quid Dependence Scale (BQDS), a validated 16-item instrument developed for assessing physical-psychological urge, increasing dose, and maladaptive use patterns among habitual chewers [13]. The BQDS has demonstrated strong internal consistency and construct validity for behavioural dependence assessment.

Oral examination was performed following the World Health Organization Oral Health Assessment Criteria (5th edition) [14]. Dental caries experience was recorded using the Decayed, Missing, and Filled Teeth (DMFT) index, the internationally accepted epidemiological measure for caries burden [17, 18]. Screening for oral mucosal abnormalities and oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs), including oral submucous fibrosis, leukoplakia, and erythroplakia, was conducted based on criteria commonly applied in population-based oral cancer detection programmes [19]. Current betel quid chewers were defined as individuals who reported chewing betel quid at least once daily in the past 30 days.

Participants were recruited from selected tribal hamlets in Wayanad using a convenience sampling approach. Prior permission was obtained from local health authorities and community leaders. Awareness camps were conducted with the support of community volunteers, and eligible individuals present at the time of the visit were invited to participate. Those who met the inclusion criteria and provided informed consent were enrolled in the study.

Data collection was carried out through field visits to tribal settlements, where temporary camps were organized at convenient community locations. Interviews were conducted by trained investigators using a structured questionnaire administered in the local language. Oral examinations were performed under natural daylight using sterile disposable instruments, adhering to standard infection control practices. The examinations were carried out by three trained medical doctors, and findings were cross-verified with the assistance of a dentist to ensure diagnostic accuracy. Individuals requiring further evaluation or treatment were referred to nearby health facilities.

Data were analysed using SPSS version 22 and Jamovi version 2.6.2. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and study variables. Betel quid dependence was assessed using the 16-item Betel Quid Dependence Scale (BQDS), with each item scored as 0 or 1, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 16. Participants were categorized into low (< 8) and high (≥ 8) dependence groups based on the distribution of BQDS scores in the study population for analytical purposes. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Variables with p < 0.2 in univariate analysis, along with those considered epidemiologically relevant, were included in multivariable logistic regression models to adjust for potential confounders. Model assumptions, including absence of multicollinearity, were checked prior to analysis. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals were reported. Multiple linear regression was performed to identify predictors of DMFT score, with verification of linearity, and homoscedasticity assumptions.

Educational status was recategorized into three groups for analysis: illiterate (illiterate), literate/primary (literate, primary), and above higher secondary (secondary, higher secondary, graduate or above). This categorization was used in subsequent statistical analyses.

Clinical trial number: Not applicable.

In terms of occupation, 48.8% were unemployed. Almost all participants belonged to below poverty line (BPL) households (95.8%). With respect to marital status, the majority were married (69.6%), followed by widowed (16.3%), single (9.5%), and separated (4.6%) individuals. (Table 1)

Age was a strong and statistically significant predictor of DMFT score. Participants aged > 40 years had significantly higher DMFT scores compared with those aged ≤ 40 years (β = 5.454; 95% CI: 3.375–7.533; p < 0.001). Occupation was also significantly associated with DMFT score, with unemployed participants having higher DMFT scores than employed participants (β = 2.182; 95% CI: 0.173–4.192; p = 0.033).

Marital status showed a borderline association with DMFT score. Single or separated/widowed participants had higher DMFT scores compared to married participants; however, this association is not statistical significant (β = 1.274; 95% CI: −0.007 to 2.555; p = 0.051). Betel-quid dependence is found to be a significant predictor, with participants having high dependence showing significantly higher DMFT scores compared to those with low dependence (β = 2.546; 95% CI: 0.532–4.560; p = 0.014).

Overall, older age, unemployment, and high betel-quid dependence were independently associated with higher DMFT scores in the adjusted model.

Discussion

This study highlights a high prevalence of betel quid chewing and dependence among tribal adults in Wayanad, along with a substantial burden of oral disease. Early initiation, long-term use, and frequent tobacco admixture were common, and high dependence was significantly associated with poorer oral health outcomes. These findings collectively highlight the significant yet underrecognized burden of betel-quid use among tribal communities in Kerala.

Prevalence and pattern of betel-quid use

The magnitude of betel-quid use observed in the present study is considerably higher than estimates reported for the general Indian population and reflects patterns previously documented among tribal populations in South India [1, 7]. Betel-quid chewing remains deeply embedded within cultural, occupational, and social practices across South and Southeast Asia, where areca nut is often perceived as socially acceptable despite its harmful effects [1, 3]. The widespread normalization of chewing behaviour in tribal settings may contribute to its early initiation and sustained use across the life course. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) has classified areca nut and betel quid, with or without tobacco, as Group 1 carcinogens, underscoring the serious public health implications of continued exposure in vulnerable populations [2].

A particularly concerning finding in the present study was the early initiation of chewing behaviour, with more than two-thirds of participants reporting onset before 18 years of age. Early initiation increases cumulative exposure to areca nut alkaloids and tobacco-related carcinogens and is strongly associated with long-term dependence and oral potentially malignant disorders [2, 19]. The frequent addition of tobacco to betel quid observed in this study further amplifies carcinogenic risk and reflects regional chewing practices reported among tribal communities in Kerala [7]. The combination of areca nut and tobacco exposes users to both psychoactive alkaloids and tobacco-specific nitrosamines, increasing the likelihood of sustained addiction and oral mucosal injury [2–5].

Betel-quid dependence and associated factors

More than half of the current chewers in the present study demonstrated high betel-quid dependence according to the Betel Quid Dependence Scale. This finding suggests that betel-quid use in this population extends beyond a casual cultural habit and may represent a clinically significant addictive behaviour. Dependence contributes to repeated and prolonged exposure to areca nut alkaloids and tobacco-related carcinogens, thereby intensifying cumulative biological harm [1–5]. The association between higher dependence and social vulnerability factors such as unemployment and lower educational status indicates that socioeconomic disadvantage may play an important role in sustaining addictive behaviours in tribal settings. Similar social gradients in betel-quid use and dependence have been described among marginalized populations in India and other Asian countries [7, 9, 10].

Although age and sex were not independently associated with dependence in adjusted analysis, older participants demonstrated higher crude odds of high dependence, likely reflecting cumulative exposure and longer duration of chewing behaviour over time. Educational status also showed an important relationship with dependence at the univariate level, suggesting that lower educational level may increase vulnerability to sustained substance use behaviour. These findings are consistent with broader evidence demonstrating that socially disadvantaged populations often experience a disproportionate burden of harmful substance use and limited access to preventive health services [9, 10].

Marital status emerged as the only independent predictor of high dependence in multivariable analysis, with separated or widowed participants having significantly higher odds of dependence compared with single individuals. This association is socially and biologically plausible, as bereavement, social isolation, and psychosocial stress are recognized contributors to substance dependence and maladaptive coping behaviours. Similar associations between adverse life circumstances and addictive behaviours have been described among socioeconomically marginalized populations [7, 9, 10, 20]. These findings support the idea that psychosocial stress, social disadvantage, and substance-related harm can act together to worsen overall health outcomes [20].

Oral health consequences of betel-quid use

The oral health findings of this study reveal a substantial burden of disease among current chewers. The mean DMFT score was high, indicating considerable cumulative dental morbidity within the study population. In addition, a notable proportion of participants exhibited features suggestive of oral submucous fibrosis and other oral mucosal lesions, including leukoplakia, erythroplakia, and persistent ulcerative lesions. These findings are biologically plausible given the chronic mechanical irritation, fibroblast stimulation, salivary alterations, and inflammatory changes associated with areca-nut exposure [18–21]. Areca nut has been shown to contribute to periodontal tissue damage, gingival recession, xerostomia, and microbial dysbiosis, thereby increasing susceptibility to dental caries and periodontal disease [21].

The prevalence of mucosal lesions observed in this study is particularly important from a public health perspective because many of these lesions are recognized oral potentially malignant disorders. Similar oral mucosal changes have been documented among tribal populations and long-term betel-quid users in South India [7, 18]. Persistent ulcerations and leukoplakic lesions warrant particular attention because of their documented malignant transformation potential [18, 22]. Previous studies have demonstrated that prolonged areca-nut exposure substantially increases the risk of oral cancer, especially in the presence of concurrent tobacco use [2, 19, 22]. The findings of the present study therefore indicate the need for routine oral screening and early lesion detection among tribal populations with high chewing prevalence.

Older age, unemployment, and high betel-quid dependence were independently associated with higher DMFT scores in the adjusted model. The association between dependence and elevated DMFT scores reinforces the dose–response relationship between chronic betel-quid exposure and oral disease burden. Individuals with greater dependence are more likely to engage in frequent chewing behaviour over prolonged periods, resulting in sustained mechanical and chemical injury to oral tissues [19, 21]. The observed association between unemployment and poor oral health further highlights the role of social determinants such as poverty, reduced healthcare access, low oral health literacy, and delayed treatment-seeking behaviour in shaping oral health disparities within tribal communities.

Public health implications

The convergence of high betel-quid prevalence, early initiation, tobacco co-use, dependence, and substantial oral disease burden places tribal communities in Wayanad at particularly high risk for future oral cancer and chronic oral morbidity. These findings have important implications for existing national programmes such as the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Cancer, Diabetes, Cardiovascular Diseases and Stroke (NPCDCS) and the National Oral Health Programme (NOHP), where integration of betel-quid dependence screening and routine oral lesion examination into tribal primary healthcare services could facilitate earlier diagnosis and intervention [15, 16]. Community-based cessation interventions involving trained frontline health workers have shown promising effectiveness in reducing betel-quid use in Asian populations [23]. Incorporating culturally appropriate awareness programmes, opportunistic oral screening, and referral pathways within tribal outreach services may substantially improve early detection and reduce long-term oral morbidity in these underserved populations.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is its community-based design focusing on a vulnerable and underrepresented tribal population. The use of standardized instruments such as the Betel Quid Dependence Scale and World Health Organization oral health assessment criteria enhances the validity and comparability of the findings. However, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The use of convenience sampling limits generalizability and introduces the possibility of selection bias. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and self-reported chewing behaviours may be subject to recall bias. In addition, the Betel Quid Dependence Scale has not been formally validated in the local tribal context. Residual confounding due to unmeasured variables such as dietary practices and oral hygiene behaviours cannot be excluded. The absence of formal inter- and intra-examiner calibration during oral examination is another limitation of the present study.

Conclusion

Betel quid use and dependence are highly prevalent among tribal adults in Wayanad and are associated with poor oral health outcomes. Targeted community-based interventions focusing on cessation, early screening, and oral health promotion are urgently needed.
