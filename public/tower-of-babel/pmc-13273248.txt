Targeting the cGAS–STING pathway mitigates Huntington disease pathogenesis in a knock-in mouse model
Authors: Anuradha Kesharwani, Sunayana Dagar, Isabella Zuniga, Marianne Charlene Monet, Ganesh Halade, Gunjan Upadhyay, Uri Nimrod Ramírez-Jarquín, Violeta Gisselle Lopez-Huerta, Emaad Mirza, Ning Quan, Srinivasa Subramaniam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceHuntington disease (HD) is a hereditary neurodegenerative disorder with prominent inflammation and no disease-modifying therapies. We identify the DNA-sensing cGAS–STING pathway as a key driver of HD-related inflammation and cellular stress. In a humanized knock-in mouse model, cGAS loss reduces microglial and astrocyte activation, preserves neurons, limits striatal atrophy, and improves motor function. It also normalizes transcriptional changes and modulates protective bioactive lipids. Pharmacological STING inhibition similarly alleviates behavioral and pathological deficits, highlighting cGAS–STING as a promising therapeutic target for HD.

Cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS; also known as Mb21d1) is a cytosolic DNA sensor that catalyzes the production of cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP), a second messenger generated upon binding to double-stranded DNA or RNA:DNA hybrids in the cytoplasm (1, 2). Activation of cGAS initiates a signaling cascade that induces the expression of inflammatory genes and has been implicated in processes such as age-related macular degeneration and cellular senescence (3–5). cGAMP produced by cGAS binds to the adaptor protein STING (stimulator of interferon genes; also known as TMEM173), which is localized to the endoplasmic reticulum membrane. Activated STING recruits TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1), leading to the phosphorylation of transcription factors including interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) and IRF7, as well as additional substrates such as IκB kinase α (IKKα), c-Rel, and p62 (sequestosome) (6, 7). Beyond its role in innate immune signaling, cGAS also regulates autophagy through LC3 lipidation, underscoring its dual function at the intersection of inflammation and cellular homeostasis (8–11). The cGAS–STING pathway has been extensively studied in cancer, diabetes, and immune-related disorders (12–14). Its role in neurodegeneration, however, has only recently been elucidated. For example, upregulation of cGAS/STING signaling has been linked to cerebellar neuron loss in ataxia telangiectasia mutated (ATM)-deficient mice (15). cGAS–STING activation has been identified as a critical determinant of TDP-43–associated pathology in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) (16). In Alzheimer’s disease (AD), NAD+ depletion has been shown to activate cGAS–STING signaling, contributing to neuroinflammation and cellular senescence in APP/PS1 transgenic mice (17). Targeting the cGAS axis alleviates pathological insults in the P301S tau model of AD (18), and inhibition of cGAS–STING signaling attenuates disease progression in both 5xFAD and AppNL-G-F/hTau double knock-in AD mouse models (19, 20).

HD is a fatal neurodegenerative disorder caused by a CAG expansion mutation in the HTT gene, producing mutant huntingtin (mHTT), a polyglutamine-expanded protein. Although mHTT is ubiquitously expressed, pathology is most pronounced in the striatum and cortex, followed by progressive peripheral defects as the disease advances (21–26). Inflammation is strongly implicated in HD pathogenesis (27–32), though the mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

We previously demonstrated that cGAS, a cytosolic DNA sensor, is upregulated in HD postmortem striatum as well as in mouse and cell models of HD (33). In cell models, cGAS upregulation was linked to inflammatory signaling and autophagy regulation (33). Independent studies later confirmed cGAS upregulation in HD cell systems (34, 35). However, it remains unknown whether cGAS–STING inhibition exacerbates or ameliorates HD in vivo. In this study, we apply cGAS gene deletion approach and pharmacological inhibition of STING pathway to address this critical knowledge gap, using a preclinical knock-in model of HD.

Results

cGAS Deletion Ameliorates Behavioral Deficits in HD Knock-In Mice.

We used humanized HD knock-in neo-deleted Q175DN mice (hereafter referred to as HD), which exhibit progressive and reproducible HD-like behavioral deficits (36, 37). To determine whether cGAS contributes to these deficits, we crossbred HD heterozygous mice with cGAS-KO mice to generate cGAS-KO mice on an HD background and performed longitudinal body weight and behavioral assessments from 2 to 14 mo of age (Fig. 1A). HD mice exhibited a progressive, age-dependent loss of body weight compared to WT controls, becoming evident by 6 mo and worsening through 14 mo, as before (36–38). In contrast, cGAS-KO;HD mice maintained significantly higher body weights than HD mice across multiple time points, indicating partial protection from HD-associated weight loss (Fig. 1B). Interestingly, cGAS-KO mice displayed significantly greater body weights than WT animals beginning around 8 mo and persisting through 14 mo (Fig. 1B), consistent with a recent report (39). Both male and female cGAS-KO;HD mice showed rescue of the body weight loss observed in HD mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S1).This finding suggests that cGAS deletion not only ameliorates HD-associated weight loss but also impacts baseline metabolic regulation under normal physiological conditions.

To further examine spontaneous locomotor activity, we performed the open field assay (Fig. 1C). HD mice exhibited a progressive decline in total distance traveled, with significantly reduced activity as early as 4 mo compared to WT controls. This reduction worsened with age, and by 12 to 14 mo HD mice showed severe hypoactivity, traveling less than half the distance of WT mice. cGAS-KO;HD animals displayed significantly higher activity than HD controls at 12 and 14 the month stages, suggesting a partial rescue of locomotor deficits. Notably, cGAS-KO mice performed similarly to WT across all ages, confirming that cGAS deletion does not impair baseline activity. As this assay was designed to assess locomotor activity, spatial exploration was not analyzed; thus, anxiety-related behaviors were not assessed, representing a limitation. Motor deficits are supported by complementary assays, including rotarod and beam walk as below.

Motor coordination and balance were assessed using the rotarod test. Two-way ANOVA (genotype × age) with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test revealed significant effects of both genotype and age. HD mice exhibited a progressive decline in rotarod performance, with reduced latency to fall beginning at 4 mo compared to WT controls (Fig. 1D). This impairment worsened with age, and by 12–14 mo, HD mice showed severe motor deficits, with average latency to fall reduced by more than half compared to WT. By contrast, cGAS-KO;HD mice performed significantly better than HD mice across multiple late-stage time points, including 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14 mo. Their latency to fall remained substantially higher than HD controls and was nearly comparable to WT and cGAS-KO animals, even at 14 mo. Importantly, cGAS-KO mice alone showed no deficits compared to WT, confirming that cGAS deletion does not impair this baseline motor function.

We next assessed fine motor coordination using the beam walk assay. HD mice showed progressively worse performance, requiring longer times to cross the beam starting at 4 mo of age compared to WT controls (Fig. 1E). This deficit worsened over time, and by 12 to 14 mo HD mice exhibited severe impairments, taking more than twice as long as WT mice to complete the task. In contrast, cGAS-KO;HD mice performed significantly better than HD mice at multiple ages, with crossing times that were consistently shorter and closer to WT and cGAS-KO cohorts. Notably, as in rotarod, cGAS-KO mice alone were indistinguishable from WT across all ages, indicating that cGAS deletion does not impair baseline beam walk performance.

To comprehensively assess neurological function, we performed a behavioral battery that included ledge walking, clasping, gait, kyphosis, and tremor (Fig. 1F). HD mice exhibited marked impairments, with a higher proportion of animals scoring in the severe range (scores 2 to 3) across all five tasks compared to WT controls (37, 40). In contrast, cGAS-KO;HD animals showed substantial improvement, with a greater proportion of mice displaying mild or no impairments (scores 0 to 1), significantly different from HD controls (Fig. 1F). cGAS-KO mice alone showed no abnormalities and were comparable to WT, further confirming that cGAS deletion does not negatively affect baseline neurological function.

A composite score, calculated by summing the individual scores from all five behavioral tests, confirmed these findings (Fig. 1G). HD mice displayed significantly higher composite scores than WT, while cGAS-KO;HD animals exhibited markedly reduced composite scores compared to HD mice, approaching WT and cGAS-KO levels. Together, these results demonstrate that cGAS deletion mitigates a spectrum of HD-associated neurological abnormalities, further supporting a protective role of cGAS loss in HD progression.

cGAS Deletion Prevents Striatal Atrophy in HD.

We next examined whether the behavioral protection observed in cGAS-deficient HD mice is accompanied by reduced anatomical deficits. To assess brain atrophy, we quantified lateral ventricle enlargement across frontal, central, and caudal striatal regions, using up to three independent coronal sections per region as detailed in the Methods. As expected, HD mice displayed robust enlargement of the lateral ventricles in all three regions (Fig. 1 H and I), consistent with previous reports (36, 41). Strikingly, cGAS deletion in HD mice (cGAS-KO;HD) markedly reduced ventricular enlargement in central and caudal regions (Fig. 1 H and I), indicating that cGAS loss strongly attenuates the striatal atrophy characteristic of knock-in HD models.

cGAS Deletion Prevents Astrocyte Proliferation in HD.

Astrocyte proliferation is a well-established indicator of neuronal injury (42), and is elevated in the striatum of HD mice (37, 43). We therefore quantified GFAP expression across frontal, central, and caudal striatal subregions in WT, HD, cGAS-KO;HD, and cGAS-KO mice (Fig. 2 A and B). GFAP expression was dramatically increased in HD mice relative to WT, whereas cGAS-KO;HD mice showed a substantial reduction in GFAP immunoreactivity in all regions (Fig. 2 A and B). GFAP levels did not differ between WT and cGAS-KO mice. Increased GFAP staining was also evident in the corpus callosum of HD mice (Fig. 2A), and this increase was strongly blunted in cGAS-KO;HD mice (Fig. 2A). In addition, number of GFAP-positive cells were increased in HD mice and significantly reduced in cGAS-KO;HD mice across all three regions (Fig. 2 A and C), whereas WT and cGAS-KO mice showed no differences.

To assess functional integrity of medium spiny neuron (MSN), we quantified DARPP-32 intensity, the MSN marker. Consistent with a prior report in Q175DN model (36), DARPP-32 expression was notably reduced in HD mice compared to WT. In contrast, cGAS-KO;HD mice exhibited markedly higher DARPP-32 intensity than HD mice, suggesting that loss of cGAS is associated with improved MSN integrity (Fig. 2 A and D).

cGAS Deletion Suppresses Microglial Expression and Preserves Neuronal Markers.

Microgliosis is a hallmark of human HD and is prominent across multiple preclinical HD models (43, 44). IBA1 immunoreactivity revealed strong microglial activation in HD mice across all three striatal regions, whereas cGAS-KO;HD mice displayed significantly reduced IBA1 intensity (Fig. 3 A and B). cGAS deletion alone had no effect on microglial activation. Similar to astrogliosis, robust microgliosis was also observed in the corpus callosum of HD mice and was diminished in cGAS-KO;HD animals (Fig. 3A). Number of IBA1-positive cells were elevated in HD mice and significantly reduced in cGAS-KO;HD mice across all three regions (Fig. 3 A and C), with no differences between WT and cGAS-KO mice. Analysis of CTIP2 expression revealed a marked neuronal loss in HD mice that was partially restored in cGAS-KO;HD mice (Fig. 3 A and D). Together, these findings indicate that cGAS deletion reduces microglial activation and preserves neuronal integrity.

cGAS Deletion Shifts Microglial Activation State and Preserves Microglial Morphology.

cGAS regulates immune activation states in microglia (45), which can be tracked using CD86 as a marker of proinflammatory M1 microglia (activated) and CD206 as a marker of anti-inflammatory M2 microglia (resting) (46). HD mice exhibited significantly elevated CD86 intensity, highly colocalizing with IBA1+ microglia, whereas this increase was strongly attenuated in cGAS-KO;HD mice (Fig. 4 A–C). Conversely, CD206 intensity was reduced in HD mice and restored in cGAS-KO;HD mice, suggesting that cGAS deletion reduces M1 polarization while maintaining the M2 state (Fig. 4 D–F).

Microglial morphology is a sensitive indicator of activation status (47), with activated microglia exhibiting shortened processes and amoeboid shapes (48, 49). Consistent with prior observations (43), microglia in HD mice displayed markedly shortened processes and reduced branching (Fig. 4 G–I). Remarkably, cGAS deletion in HD mice preserved both the length and number of microglial processes, closely resembling WT morphology (Fig. 4 G–I). The length and number of microglial processes in cGAS-KO was similar to WT.

Collectively, the microglial activation markers and morphological analyses demonstrate that cGAS deletion in HD mice prevents microglial transition to the activated state and promotes preservation of glial homeostasis. These results suggest that cGAS contributes to HD pathogenesis in part by regulating microglial activation and inflammatory response.

cGAS Deletion Restores Key Transcriptional Programs in HD Mice.

Transcriptional dysregulation is a major event in HD pathogenesis. We therefore examined whether cGAS impacts motor deficits in HD by a dysregulation of gene expression. We performed a genome-wide striatal tissue–specific gene expression pattern analysis by comparing RNA sequencing (RNA seq) in the striatum of 16-mo-old WT, HD, cGAS-KO, and cGAS-KO;HD mice (one male and one female mice striatum were pooled/experiment, n = 3 experiment/genotype, total = 6 mice/genotype, total = 24 mice). Gene counts revealed 1,428 genes that were significantly down-regulated and 867 up-regulated (p-value adjusted, padj < 0.05) in the HD mice compared with the WT striatum (Fig. 5 A and B and Dataset S1). Between HD and cGAS-KO 1195 genes are significantly downregulated and 578 genes are upregulated (Fig. 5 A and C and Dataset S2). Between cGAS-KO and WT, only one gene (FMO2) was significantly downregulated, suggesting that cGAS deletion alone does not appreciably alter gene expression in the striatum (Fig. 5 A and D and Dataset S3). In contrast, comparison of cGAS-KO;HD versus HD revealed 113 significantly downregulated and 93 significantly upregulated genes (Fig. 5 A and E and Dataset S4), suggesting that cGAS plays a modulatory role in gene expression specifically under HD conditions. Comparison of cGAS-KO;HD versus WT revealed 1,178 significantly downregulated and 884 significantly upregulated genes (Fig. 5 A and F and Dataset S5), with transcriptional changes largely resembling those observed in HD versus WT.

To further define gene-level rescue associated with cGAS deletion, we examined overlaps between oppositely regulated gene sets (Fig. 6). A subset of HD-upregulated genes was reversed in cGAS-KO;HD mice, with 22 genes overlapping (~19.6% of cGAS-KO;HD-downregulated genes), enriched for extracellular matrix components, including collagen-containing extracellular matrix (Fig. 6 A–D). Conversely, a larger fraction of HD-downregulated genes was restored in cGAS-KO;HD mice (Fig. 6 E–H), with 54 overlapping genes (~58.7% of cGAS-KO;HD-upregulated genes), showing strong enrichment for synaptic compartments such as presynapse, postsynaptic density, and synapse. Protein–protein interaction analysis further revealed interconnected networks within these overlapping gene sets, supporting coordinated functional restoration (Fig. 6 E–H). Together, these findings indicate that cGAS deletion partially reverses HD-associated transcriptional alterations, with a more pronounced recovery of synaptic gene programs.

GO analysis showed broad pathway shifts. In HD vs WT (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A), enrichment was dominated by synaptic/developmental terms—dendrite and synapse organization, neurotransmission; postsynaptic density/spines and receptor/ion-channel complexes; ion-channel activity, glutamate–receptor binding, GTPase/cytoskeletal functions—indicating pervasive synaptic dysfunction. With cGAS deletion in HD (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B), enrichment shifted toward neurodevelopmental and metabolic programs (forebrain development, oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial transport, K+ handling) and vesicle/GABAergic components, consistent with protective remodeling.

We reviewed the selected genes that were diminished in HD but restored by cGAS deletion (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A), which include genes involved in energy metabolism [Pgam2 (50)], nitric oxide signaling [Nos1 (51)], neuronal calcium binding and differentiation [Hpcal4 (52), Zbrb16 (53), Lhx6 (54)], transcriptional regulation [Zfp831 (55), Lhx6 (54), Ssbp3 (56)], and neuropeptide signaling [Cort (57)]. Restoration of these genes suggests that cGAS loss supports synaptic activity, neurodevelopment, and transcriptional balance in HD. Conversely, several pathways upregulated in HD were suppressed upon cGAS deletion (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B), including extracellular matrix remodeling [Adamts15 (58), Col5a3 (59)], lipid metabolism [Ivldl (60)], mitochondrial transport [Slc25a5 (61)], and synaptic/extracellular proteins [Sspo (62), Lgi3 (63)]. Interestingly, the HD-induced increase in Mid1-ps1, a pseudogene related to Mid1 (64) with potential regulatory roles, was also reduced by cGAS deletion. We further validated selected targets using qPCR which showed similar trend as found in RNA seq (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). In contrast, MSN-specific genes reduced in HD were unchanged by cGAS deletion, indicating selective transcriptional modulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C). Together, these findings indicate that cGAS deletion reverses both the loss of neuronal/synaptic regulators and the aberrant induction of extracellular, metabolic, and pseudogene-associated pathways in HD.

cGAS Deletion Elevates Anti-Inflammatory Bioactive Lipids in HD Brain.

Beyond its role in cytosolic DNA sensing, the cGAS–STING axis also remodels brain-associated inflammatory transcriptional programs (45) and cellular metabolism (65). Since inflammatory status directly influences eicosanoid biosynthesis (66), we performed targeted cortical lipidomics with a focus on immune-active oxylipins. Out of 58 lipid metabolites analyzed (Dataset S6), 38 were detectable, among which we observed specific upregulation of 12(S)-Hydroxyheptadeca-5Z,8E,10E-trienoic acid (12-HHT; Fig. 7A), 12-Hydroxyeicosapentaenoic acid (12-HEPE; Fig. 7B), and 12-Hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid (12-HETE; Fig. 7C). Thus, arachidonic acid–derived 12-HHT and eicosapentaenoic acid-derived 12-HEPE were significantly elevated in the cortex of HD mice upon cGAS deletion as signs of activated immune resolution (Fig. 7 A–C). Although there was a trend toward increased levels across the broader HEPE and HETE families (Dataset S6), these changes did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 7 D and E). Notably, 12-HHT and 12-HEPE have been implicated as anti-inflammatory and proresolving action in models of skin inflammation (67, 68), whereas 12-HETE may promote oxidative stress and thereby modulate adaptive immunity (69). In contrast, ω-3 fat, docosahexaenoic acid–derived HDHAs showed a trend of increase in the HD cortex but were not altered by cGAS deletion (Fig. 7F). These results suggest that cGAS deletion selectively reshapes cortical inflammation-resolution mediators profiles in HD by enhancing specific 12-lipoxygenase–derived metabolites. The elevation of 12-HHT and 12-HEPE is consistent with a shift toward anti-inflammatory bioactive mediators, whereas the increase in 12-HETE points to a potential balance between inflammation-clearing and stress-inducing pathways. Together, these findings indicate that cGAS loss not only dampens inflammatory cell programs but also modulates the bioactive lipid mediators milieu in HD, thereby linking innate immune signaling with cortical metabolic adaptations.

Pharmacological Inhibitor of STING Pathway Ameliorates Motor Symptoms in Symptomatic HD Mice.

Because global cGAS deletion ameliorated HD-related phenotypes, we next asked whether pharmacological inhibition of the downstream activator STING could provide similar protection in HD. To test this, we selected 6-mo-old HD mice, a stage at which animals exhibit clear motor deficits, thereby modeling a symptomatic condition to evaluate the impact of cGAS–STING pathway inhibition. HD heterozygous mice were intraperitoneally (i.p) injected with the STING inhibitor H-151 that has brain penetrability (70) or vehicle (DMSO) beginning at 6 mo of age (Fig. 8A), when animals already exhibit motor impairments, and continued for 5 d per week until 10 mo (Fig. 8B). Behavioral testing was carried out longitudinally at 7, 8, and 9 mo of age (Fig. 8B).

Body weight was unaffected by drug treatment (Fig. 8C), although a slight reduction was observed in males (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). In contrast, STING inhibition improved motor coordination, with the strongest effect in the rotarod task. Vehicle treated HD mice (HD + Veh) mice showed progressive decline in rotarod performance from 7 to 9 mo, reflected by a reduced latency to fall (Fig. 8D). In contrast, H-151-treated HD mice (HD + H-151) mice maintained stable performance across the testing period, with significantly longer times on the rod compared with controls at all measured timepoints. These findings indicate that STING inhibition preserves motor coordination and balance in symptomatic HD mice. In beam walk performance, we found that HD + H-151 mice performed better at 7 mo but not at 8 and 9 mo, suggesting a transient or stage-dependent effect of the treatment that diminishes as disease progresses (Fig. 8E).

Neurological abnormalities progressively worsened in HD + Veh mice, including tremor, clasping, ledge walking deficits, gait abnormalities, and kyphosis. In contrast, H-151 treatment significantly attenuated these phenotypes. Hindlimb clasping was reduced (Fig. 8F), ledge walking impairment prevented (Fig. 8G), gait abnormalities alleviated (Fig. 8H), tremor scores decreased (Fig. 8I), and kyphosis progression delayed in HD + H-151 mice (Fig. 8J).

STING Inhibitor Prevents Striatal Atrophy in HD.

To assess whether the behavioral improvements observed with STING inhibition are accompanied by reduced anatomical deficits, we quantified lateral ventricle enlargement across frontal, central, and caudal striatal regions. HD + Veh mice exhibited robust ventricular enlargement compared to WT controls (Fig. 8K). In contrast, HD mice treated with the STING inhibitor (HD + H-151) showed significantly reduced ventricular enlargement in the central and caudal regions (Fig. 8L), suggesting attenuation of striatal atrophy in HD mice.

STING Inhibitor Prevents Astrocytes Proliferation and Microglial Activation in HD.

We next performed GFAP immunohistochemistry in the caudal striatal region in WT, HD + Veh, and HD + H-151 mice (Fig. 9 A–C). GFAP expression and no. of GFAP+ cells was significantly increased in HD + Veh mice relative to WT, whereas HD + H-151 mice showed reduced GFAP immunoreactivity and no. of GFAP+ cells (Fig. 9 A–C). In contrast, although DARPP-32 levels were slightly decreased in HD + Veh mice, no difference was observed between HD + Veh and HD + H-151 groups (Fig. 9 A and D).

STING Inhibitor Diminishes Microglial Activation.

HD + Veh mice exhibited significantly increased CD86 intensity, which strongly colocalized with IBA1+ microglia, whereas this increase was attenuated in HD + H-151 mice (Fig. 9 E, G, and H). Conversely, CD206 intensity was reduced in HD + Veh mice, and this reduction was prevented by STING inhibition (Fig. 9 F, I, and J), suggesting a shift in microglial resting state.

Together, these findings suggest that pharmacological inhibition of STING with H-151 is associated with improved motor coordination and reduced neurological impairment, accompanied by decreased ventricular enlargement, astrocyte proliferation, and microglial activation, even during progressive disease stages.

Discussion

Targeting huntingtin (HTT) remains an attractive therapeutic strategy for HD but selectively lowering mutant HTT (mHTT) while preserving the critical functions of wild-type HTT is a persistent challenge. Genetic and RNA-based approaches that reduce total HTT, including a recent microRNA strategy (71), have shown promise in slowing disease phenotypes, yet gene-therapy modalities face important barriers to broad access because of complexity and cost. Thus, complementary small-molecule approaches that are affordable, scalable, and amenable to early symptomatic intervention are urgently needed.

Our data identify the cGAS–STING axis as a promising, druggable node in HD pathophysiology: genetic deletion of cGAS or pharmacological inhibition of STING during the symptomatic period produced robust improvements in motor, neurological, and pathological outcomes in our humanized preclinical model. Importantly, several independent preclinical studies indicate that genetic or pharmacological disruption of the cGAS–STING DNA-sensing axis mitigates neuroinflammation, pathology, and functional deficits across multiple mouse models of neurodegeneration. For example, cGAS/STING activation promotes neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration in Parkinson’s disease models, and inhibition of the pathway ameliorates dopaminergic injury and motor phenotypes (72, 73). In Alzheimer’s disease models (17–20), cGAS deficiency or pharmacological blockade of STING (e.g., H-151) reduces amyloid/tau pathology, neuroinflammation, and cognitive deficits. Likewise, studies in ALS/FTD–linked models implicate DNA-sensing pathways (including cGAS and related sensors) in driving neuroinflammation and neuronal injury, and modulating this axis provides benefit in those preclinical systems. Finally, broader work implicating STING in age-related neuroinflammatory programs and neurodegeneration (45) further supports the idea that blocking DNA sensing may be broadly protective across etiologies.

While our findings implicate the cGAS–STING pathway in HD pathogenesis (Fig. 9K), the precise molecular events that drive cGAS induction in HD are still not fully understood. HD cells frequently contain cytosolic micronuclei and other hallmarks of genomic instability that plausibly arise from somatic CAG expansion and defective DNA repair (33, 74–76). Recent work indicates that wild-type HTT binds the exonuclease EXO1 and promotes DNA repair, whereas mutant HTT (mHTT) fails to engage EXO1, leading to persistent DNA damage and enhanced cGAS activation in HD striatal cells (35). Concurrently, we observe increased ribosome occupancy on mitochondrial OXPHOS transcripts in these cells (77). Because mitochondrial stress and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) efflux into the cytosol are well-established triggers of cGAS–STING signaling (78), and because cGAS has been reported to associate with stalled ribosomes (79) and to localize to mitochondria (80), these observations invite a unifying model in which DNA damage and mitochondrial dysfunction cooperate to activate chronic inflammatory response in HD (Fig. 9K). Additionally, the possibility that cGAS is activated by mitochondrial RNA in HD cannot be excluded (2, 81).

Our observation that STING is strongly activated in HD microglia is consistent with prior studies showing that cGAS–STING signaling in microglia drives inflammation and neuronal degeneration (45, 72, 82). In the present study, we used a constitutive global cGAS knockout, which does not allow us to distinguish the relative contributions of microglia, astrocytes, neurons, or peripheral immune cells to the observed phenotypic rescue. Future studies using conditional and inducible, cell type–specific knockout models will be necessary to dissect the contributions of CNS-resident glia, neurons, and peripheral immune compartments to HD pathology.

While genetic deletion of cGAS produced robust and sustained improvements, H-151 treatment yielded strong benefits in rotarod performance and multiple neurological parameters (including hindlimb clasping, gait, and kyphosis), H-151 treatment only transiently improved beam walk performance. Additionally, a mild reduction in body weight was observed selectively in male mice following H-151 treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S5), suggesting potential sex- or dose-dependent systemic effects not evident with genetic loss of cGAS. These differences likely reflect inherent distinctions between genetic ablation and pharmacological inhibition, including dosing regimen, treatment window, and pharmacokinetic limitations, which may constrain the magnitude and durability of functional rescue. Nevertheless, the overall behavioral and neuropathological improvements observed with H-151 support the therapeutic potential of targeting the cGAS–STING pathway and highlight the need for further optimization of STING inhibitors in HD models

The upregulation of bioactive lipid metabolites in cGAS-KO; HD mice may provide new therapeutic opportunities. For example, 12-HHT has been implicated in wound healing in mice (83), while eicosapentaenoic acid (ω-3)-derived 12-HEPE promotes glucose uptake (84) and supports platelet activation and cardiovascular health (85). Drugs that modulate bioactive lipids may therefore be beneficial in HD, a possibility that warrants further investigation.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that inhibition of the cGAS–STING pathway is a viable therapeutic strategy that confers neuroprotection and improves motor and neurological outcomes in a humanized model of HD. Although most STING antagonists remain in preclinical development, the emergence of several oral cGAS–STING inhibitors (86–88) creates an additional translational opportunity to assess whether targeting this pathway can be repurposed for HD therapy.
