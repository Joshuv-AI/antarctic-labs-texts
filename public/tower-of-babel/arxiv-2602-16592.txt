Hybrid Optimization Techniques for Multi-State Optimal Design Problems
Marko Erceg, Petar Kunštek, Marko Vrdoljak

Abstract
This paper addresses optimal design problems governed by multi-state stationary diffusion equations, aiming at the simultaneous optimization of the domain shape and the distribution of two isotropic materials in prescribed proportions. Existence of generalized solutions is established via a hybrid approach combining homogenization-based relaxation in the interior with suitable restrictions on admissible domains. Based on this framework, we propose a numerical method that integrates homogenization and shape optimization. The domain boundary is evolved using a level set method driven by the shape derivative, while the interior material distribution is updated via an optimality criteria algorithm. The approach is demonstrated on a representative example.



Keywords
: optimal design, homogenization, optimality conditions, shape derivative, Hausdorff distance

Introduction

Shape optimization refers to the determination of an optimal domain that minimizes a prescribed objective functional, usually defined by the solution of a partial differential equation (PDE) known as the state equation. As such, shape optimization can be considered as a branch of distributed control theory, where the domain acts as a control variable.

A fundamental difficulty in this field lies in the nonexistence of classical optimal shapes. Minimizing sequences of domains tend to develop oscillations, holes, or degeneracies, so that no Lipschitz domain attains the infimum of the cost functional. This nonexistence has profound implications: numerical algorithms may fail to converge or depend strongly on the initial guess. Two principal strategies have emerged to address this: (i) restricting the class of admissible domains to ensure compactness and existence, or (ii) relaxing the problem by enlarging the admissible set where existence can be recovered.

The homogenization theory plays a central role in obtaining this relaxation
[
32
,
26
]
, by introducing a notion of generalized designs that ensures the optimization problem remains well-posed and physically meaningful. This, in turn, leads to more stable and efficient numerical computations of relaxed designs
[
8
,
4
,
9
]
.

On the other hand, imposing additional (uniform) regularity assumptions on the class of admissible domains can ensure the existence of solutions for certain shape optimization problems. Examples include the uniform cone condition
[
16
,
35
]
, a bounded number of connected components of the complement in two-dimensional cases
[
39
]
, perimeter constraints
[
6
]
, uniform continuity conditions
[
29
]
, the uniform cusp property
[
18
]
, and uniform boundedness of the density perimeter
[
13
]
. Further theoretical insights are provided in
[
38
,
23
,
12
,
34
]
.

This theoretical development was accompanied by a numerical treatment of the problem using shape calculus, building on the pioneering work of Hadamard
[
33
,
35
,
38
,
5
]
.
Numerical methods in shape optimization differ mainly in how they represent geometry. Some approaches, such as the level set and phase-field methods, describe shapes implicitly using
a scalar field that marks the region occupied by the material. Other approaches represent shapes explicitly through a computational mesh or CAD model, allowing for accurate analysis but
requiring frequent remeshing or geometric updates as the shape evolves.

In this paper, we consider the problem of determining not only the optimal distribution of two phases but also the optimal shape of the region they occupy.
The problem has been addressed in the literature using alternative approaches. Extensions of the SIMP scheme to multiple phases were proposed in

[
37
,
19
]
, considering three-phase mixtures (two materials plus void). In
[
45
,
2
]
, the level set method was applied to multi-phase
shape optimization, also allowing one of the phases to represent void.

Motivated by the robustness of the homogenization method, our approach combines homogenization-based relaxation within the domain with shape optimization based on the shape derivative of the overall region.
For this reason, we refer to it as a hybrid method.
Moreover, the optimality criteria method is known
to produce accurate approximations within only a few iterations, which facilitates the application of the shape derivative method
to the external boundary.

The resulting optimal design can, through penalization techniques, be reduced to a classical design with pure phases, or it can serve as an informed initial guess for the subsequent application of the shape derivative method to the distribution of the two phases within the “optimal” domain.
In the numerical example presented in the final section, the penalization step was not implemented, since the optimal design produced by the proposed method turned out to be classical.
This is due to the radial symmetry of the problem: the resulting design is known to be optimal for the fixed-domain problem posed on the resulting domain
[
27
]
.

We now proceed to formulate the specific
two-material optimal design problem under consideration.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

, be a bounded open fixed set and

(1)

{\bf{A}}=\chi\alpha{\bf{I}}+(1-\chi)\beta{\bf{I}},

where

represents the characteristic function of the less-conductive phase. For different right-hand sides

we denote the corresponding temperatures
{\sf u}:=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})\in{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})}

(2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}-\ddiv({\bf{A}}\nabla u_{i})=f_{i}&\text{ in }\Omega\\
u_{i}=h_{i}&\text{ on }\Gamma_{0}\\
u_{i}=0&\text{ on }\Gamma_{1}\\
{\bf{A}}\nabla u_{i}\cdot{\bm{n}}=0&\text{ on }\Gamma_{2}.\end{array}\right.

Here, the boundary
\partial\Omega
consists of three parts:
\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{0}\dot{\cup}\Gamma_{1}\dot{\cup}\Gamma_{2}
, where

\Gamma_{0}
is relatively open and fixed, and
h_{i}\in\HH^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma_{0})

are prescribed.
The sets
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
represent subsets of

on which the Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions,
respectively, can be applied.
Note that simply requiring
h_{i}\in\HH^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma_{0})
is insufficient
to ensure well-posedness; their choice must be consistent with the
other boundary conditions, particularly the homogeneous Dirichlet
condition imposed on the segment
\Gamma_{1}
.
We address this specification separately within each of the three settings in Section
2
.

Remark 1.1
.

The functions
f_{i}
, for
i=1,\dots,m
, could potentially belong to a broader space, specifically the dual of the space
\bigl\{v\in{{\rm H}^{1}(D)}:v|_{\Gamma_{0}\cup\Gamma_{1}}=0\bigr\}
, where the restriction is naturally understood in the sense of traces. However, for the sake of simplicity in the subsequent analysis, and anticipating the even stricter requirements of the numerical application to follow, we shall work with a subspace consisting of square integrable functions on

(i.e.
f_{i}\in{{\rm L}^{2}(D)}
).

The aim is to find a domain
\Omega
, such that
\Gamma_{0}\subseteq\partial\Omega
, and a characteristic function
\chi
that minimizes the objective functional

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}(\Omega,\chi)=\int\limits_{\Omega}\chi({\bm{x}})g_{\alpha}({\bm{x}},{\sf u}({\bm{x}}))+(1-\chi({\bm{x}}))g_{\beta}({\bm{x}},{\sf u}({\bm{x}}))\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}.

The amount of the first phase is also given:

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega}\chi\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}=q_{\alpha},

as well as the volume (i.e. the Lebesgue measure) of the domain:
|\Omega|=V
.
There is no definitive reason to expect minimizers; however, if they do exist, we refer to this as a classical design.

We provide a brief overview of the paper. In Section
2
, we introduce a hybrid approach for establishing the existence of a
(generalized) solution. Relaxation via the homogenization method is applied within the domain, while the class of admissible domains is suitably restricted.
We first examine the cases where either
\Gamma_{1}
or
\Gamma_{2}
is empty, and then extend the analysis to the general case by combining the techniques
developed for these two settings. Section
3

method is applied inside the domain, while the domain boundary is updated using the shape derivative of the cost functional. Section
4

illustrates the approach with a numerical example.

2.1
Preparation

If
\Omega
is fixed, achieving proper relaxation involves introducing generalized materials, which are mixtures of two original phases on a micro-scale, introduced by Murat and Tartar
[

\chi\in{{\rm L}^{\infty}(\Omega;\{0,1\})}

local fractions

1

\Omega

denotes the set of all possible effective conductivities obtained by mixing original phases with local fraction
\theta
. Under suitable growth conditions on

and

(see (A5) below) the relaxed problem reads

(3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}{\mathcal{J}}(\Omega,\theta,{\bf{A}})=\int_{\Omega}\theta({\bm{x}})g_{\alpha}({\bm{x}},{\sf u}({\bm{x}}))+(1-\theta({\bm{x}}))g_{\beta}({\bm{x}},{\sf u}({\bm{x}}))\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}\to\min\\[5.69046pt]
\theta\in{{\rm L}^{\infty}(\Omega;[0,1])},\,\int_{\Omega}\theta\,d{\bm{x}}=q_{\alpha},\,{\bf{A}}\in{\mathcal{K}}(\theta)\text{ a.e.\penalty\ in }\Omega,\,{\sf u}\text{ solves \eqref{state1} }.\end{array}\right.

The determination of the set
{\mathcal{K}}(\theta)
is reffered to as the G-closure problem (see
[
4
, Subsection 2.1.2]
). For mixtures of two isotropic phases, this problem admits
a complete characterization:
{\mathcal{K}}(\theta)
consists of all symmetric matrices whose eigenvalues satisfy the following inequalities
(see
[

,
30
]
)

(4)

\displaystyle\left\{\quad\begin{array}[]{cc}\displaystyle\lambda_{\theta}^{-}\leq\lambda_{j}\leq\lambda_{\theta}^{+}\quad j=1,\ldots,d\\
\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}-\alpha}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\theta}^{-}-\alpha}+\frac{d-1}{\lambda_{\theta}^{+}-\alpha}\\
\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\beta-\lambda_{j}}\leq\frac{1}{\beta-\lambda_{\theta}^{-}}+\frac{d-1}{\beta-\lambda_{\theta}^{+}}\end{array}\right.

where
\lambda_{\theta}^{-}=(\theta/\alpha+(1-\theta)/\beta)^{-1},\>\lambda_{\theta}^{+}=\theta\alpha+(1-\theta)\beta.

This precise characterization of
{\mathcal{K}}(\theta)
is relevant only for the numerical approach and is not required for the results presented in this section.
These results equally apply to the case of mixing more than two materials, including anisotropic ones
[
41
,
42
]
.

To obtain existence of optimal
\Omega

For
{\bm{y}},{\bm{\xi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(\forall{\bm{x}}\in\partial\Omega)(\exists{\bm{\xi}}_{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},|{\bm{\xi}}_{\bm{x}}|=1)(\forall{\bm{y}}\in\overline{\Omega}\cap B({\bm{x}},\varepsilon))\quad C({\bm{y}},{\bm{\xi}}_{\bm{x}},\varepsilon)\subset\Omega\,.

Then, the set

There exists an open and connected

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}=\Biggl\{

\displaystyle(\Omega,\theta,{\bf{A}}):\Omega\subseteq D\text{ is open, connected and satisfies the $\varepsilon$-cone property},

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\mathcal{J}}(\Omega,\theta,{\bf{A}})\to\min\\[5.69046pt]

In the rest of this section, we prove the existence of a solution to the relaxed problem (

We present these three distinct settings sequentially to enhance accessibility and guide the reader step-by-step through the technical difficulties. This approach keeps the proofs reasonably short, as in the second and third cases, we emphasize only the new technical components without repeating the parts that carry over from the preceding analysis.

A final technical result in this section concerns the geometry of
\Gamma_{0}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let us assume
(A1)
–
(A4)
. There exist
N
open and bounded disjoint subsets
O^{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
i=1,2,\dots,N
, such that for every
i\in\{1,2,\dots,N\}
the following hold:

i)

\Gamma_{0}^{i}\subset O^{i}
and
\partial\Gamma_{0}^{i}\subset\partial O^{i}
;

ii)

O^{i}=O^{i}_{-}\,\dot{\cup}\,\Gamma_{0}^{i}\,\dot{\cup}\,O^{i}_{+}
, where
O^{i}_{\pm}
are connected open sets;

iii)

The boundary of any admissible domain
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}
satisfies
\partial\Omega\cap O^{i}=\Gamma_{0}^{i}
, where
\mathcal{O}
is the set of admissible domains defined in the above.

Proof.

By
[
16
]
(see also
[
23
, Theorem 2.4.7]
) any admissible
domain
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}
has a Lipschitz boundary. More precisely, in terms of
[
23
, Definition 2.4.5]
, the parameters in the definition of a Lipschitz boundary are
L,a,r\in(0,\infty)
, and they depend only on
\varepsilon
.
We define

\delta=\delta(\varepsilon):=\frac{\min\{r,a,1\}}{\sqrt{1+L^{2}}}\,.

Let us take an arbitrary
i\in\{1,2\dots,N\}
. Then we define

O^{i}:=\bigcup_{x\in\Gamma_{0}^{i}}B(x,\eta_{x})\,,

where
\eta_{x}=\delta\min\{1,d(\partial\Gamma_{0}^{i},x)\}
. Then clearly we have that
O^{i}
is open and that i) is satisfied.

Using the definition of a Lipschitz boundary
[
23
, Definition 2.4.5]
it is easy to show that for any admissible domain
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}
we have

\partial\Omega\cap B(x,\eta_{x})=\Gamma_{0}^{i}\cap B(x,\eta_{x})
(here the choice of
\delta
is fundamental).
This immediately implies that iii) is fulfilled.

This completes the proof.
∎

An immediate consequence of the previous lemma is that

is relatively

Remark 2.2
.

The interpretation of the open set

is that the boundary of all admissible domains

Hence,

We also allow the fixed boundary to intersect the exterior boundary of

are not strictly contained in

To write down weak formulation of the boundary value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\ddiv({\bf{A}}_{n}\nabla u^{n}_{i})=f_{i}&\text{ in }\Omega_{n}\\
u^{n}_{i}=h_{i}&\text{ on }\Gamma_{0}\\
u^{n}_{i}=0&\text{ on }\Gamma^{n}_{1}\,,\end{array}\right.

we begin by homogenizing the boundary conditions on
\Gamma_{0}
.

We use the same notation for the extension by zero to the whole
\mathbb{R}^{d}

of
H_{i}
given in (H1).
It is well-known that
H_{i}\in\HH^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(see
[

, Lemma 3.27]
).
Defining
v^{n}_{i}=u^{n}_{i}-H_{i}|_{\Omega_{n}}
, for

,
we see that

is the unique solution of

(9)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\ddiv({\bf{A}}_{n}\nabla v^{n}_{i})=f_{i}+g^{n}_{i}&\text{ in }\Omega_{n}\\
v^{n}_{i}=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega_{n}=\Gamma_{0}\cup\Gamma^{n}_{1},\end{array}\right.

where

.
This step relies on the conditions

and

H_{i}|_{\Gamma_{1}^{n}}=0

Applying
{\bf{A}}_{n}\in{\mathcal{K}}(\theta_{n})

\|g^{n}_{i}\|_{{{\rm H}^{-1}(\Omega_{n})}}\leq\beta\|H_{i}\|_{{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}}\leq\beta\|H_{i}\|_{{{\rm H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}=\beta\|H_{i}\|_{{{\rm H}^{1}(O)}}\,,

where we have used that
\operatorname{supp}H_{i}\subseteq O
.

Let us denote by

\widetilde{\sf v}^{n}
the extension of
{\sf v}^{n}=(v_{1}^{n},\dots,v_{m}^{n})
by zero on

D\setminus\Omega_{n}
. Then
\widetilde{\sf v}^{n}\in{{\rm H}_{0}^{1}(D;\mathbb{R}^{m})}
.
Moreover, using the a priori estimate, we have that

(\widetilde{\sf v}^{n})
is bounded in
{{\rm H}_{0}^{1}(D;\mathbb{R}^{m})}

(cf.
[
23
, Proposition 3.2.1]
). Indeed, for any

i\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
and any

(10)

\displaystyle\alpha c_{1}\|\widetilde{v}_{i}^{n}\|_{{{\rm H}_{0}^{1}(D)}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\int_{D}|\nabla\widetilde{v}_{i}^{n}|^{2}=\alpha\int_{\Omega_{n}}|\nabla v_{i}^{n}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega_{n}}{\bf{A}}_{n}\nabla v_{i}^{n}\cdot\nabla v_{i}^{n}=\langle f^{n}_{i},v_{i}^{n}\rangle_{{{\rm H}^{-1}(\Omega_{n})},{{\rm H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}\|v_{i}^{n}\|_{{{\rm H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}}=c_{2}\|\widetilde{v}_{i}^{n}\|_{{{\rm H}_{0}^{1}(D)}}\,,

where the Poincaré inequality (the constant
c_{1}
depends only on
the set

; see
[
1
, Corollary 6.31]
) is applied first. In the second step we have used that
\nabla\widetilde{v}_{i}^{n}
agrees
with the zero extension of
\nabla v_{i}^{n}
(cf.
[
1
, Lemma 3.27]
).
Then the coercivity of

{\bf{A}}_{n}
is used, the weak formulation of (
9
),
and the boundedness of

in
{{\rm H}^{-1}(\Omega_{n})}

(

being
\max_{i}(\|f_{i}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(D)}}+\beta\|H_{i}\|_{{{\rm H}^{1}(O)}})
).

Therefore,
we can pass to another subsequence such that

(\widetilde{\sf v}^{n})
is weakly convergent to
\widetilde{\sf v}
in
{{\rm H}_{0}^{1}(D;\mathbb{R}^{m})}

and converges almost everywhere on
D
(for the latter we use that by the Rellich-Kondrašov compactness theorem

converges strongly in
{{\rm L}^{2}(D;\mathbb{R}^{m})}
; see
[
1
, Corollary 2.17 and Theorem 6.3]
).

Finally,
\widetilde{\sf u}^{n}:=\widetilde{\sf v}_{n}+{\sf H}
converges to
\widetilde{\sf u}:=\widetilde{\sf v}+{\sf H}
weakly in
{{\rm H}^{1}(D;\mathbb{R}^{m})}
and almost everywhere, as well (here
{\sf H}=(H_{1},\dots,H_{m})
).
Let us introduce
{\sf v}:=\widetilde{\sf v}|_{\Omega}
and
{\sf u}:=\widetilde{\sf u}|_{\Omega}
.
We want to show that
{\sf u}
is a unique solution
of (
2
), where
{\bf{A}}
and
\Omega
are previously defined.

Step III.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
{\sf 0}=(\chi_{D}-\chi_{\Omega_{n}})\widetilde{\sf v}^{n}
a.e. in
D
.
Passing to the limit (possibly on a subsequence to ensure that
(\chi_{\Omega_{n}})
converges a.e. to
\chi_{\Omega}
) we obtain that
\widetilde{\sf v}={\sf 0}
a.e. on
D\setminus\Omega
. Hence,
{\sf v}\in{{\rm H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})}
(see e.g.
[
23
, Proposition 3.2.16]
).

Let us take an arbitrary
i\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
.
It remains to show that
u_{i}=v_{i}+H_{i}|_{\Omega}
,
satisfies the weak formulation associated with (
2
).
By reversing the above procedure of homogenizing the boundary conditions on
\Gamma_{0}
,
it is evident that it is sufficient to obtain that
v_{i}
is a weak solution to
(
9
) with
\Omega_{n}
,
{\bf{A}}_{n}
and
g_{i}^{n}

replaced by
\Omega
,
{\bf{A}}
and
g_{i}:=\ddiv{\bf{A}}\nabla(H_{i}|_{\Omega})
, respectively.
Note that
g_{i}
satisfies the same bound as
g_{i}^{n}
.

Fix an arbitrary test function

\varphi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
. Given this test function
\varphi
,
select an open set
U
such that
\operatorname{supp}\varphi\subseteq U\subset\joinrel\subset\Omega
.
Since
(\Omega_{n})
converges to
\Omega
in the sense of compacts
(cf.
[
23
, Proposition 2.2.17]
), there exists
n_{1}=n_{1}(U)\in\mathbb{N}

such that for any

we have
U\subset\joinrel\subset\Omega_{n}
.
In particular,
\varphi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega_{n})
.
Thus,
\varphi
is an admissible test function for the weak formulation of the
problem for
v^{n}_{i}
, i.e. for any
n\geq n_{1}
we have

\int_{\Omega_{n}}{\bf{A}}_{n}\nabla v_{i}^{n}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}=\langle f_{i}|_{\Omega_{n}}+g_{i}^{n},\varphi\rangle_{{{\rm H}^{-1}(\Omega_{n})},{{\rm H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}}\,.

By moving
\langle g_{i}^{n},\varphi\rangle_{{{\rm H}^{-1}(\Omega_{n})},{{\rm H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}}=-\int_{\Omega_{n}}{\bf{A}}_{n}\nabla(H_{i}|_{\Omega_{n}})\cdot\nabla\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}
to the left-hand side we get
u_{i}^{n}
in place
of
v_{i}^{n}
. Moreover, using that
f_{i}\in{{\rm L}^{2}(D)}
and

\operatorname{supp}\varphi\subseteq U\subset\joinrel\subset\Omega\cap\Omega_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle f_{i}|_{\Omega_{n}},\varphi\rangle_{{{\rm H}^{-1}(\Omega_{n})},{{\rm H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}}=\int_{U}f_{i}({\bm{x}})\varphi({\bm{x}})\,d{\bm{x}}\,.

Thus, the following holds:

(11)

\int_{U}{\bf{A}}_{n}\nabla u_{i}^{n}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}=\int_{U}f_{i}({\bm{x}})\varphi({\bm{x}})\,d{\bm{x}}\,,

where we accounted for the fact that
\varphi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(U)
.

It remains to analyse the limit of the left-hand side in (
11
).
More precisely, we want to show that it converges,
as
n
tends to infinity, to
\int_{U}{\bf{A}}\nabla u_{i}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}
.
Here, we utilize the concept of H-convergence to study the behavior of the term

{\bf{A}}_{n}\nabla u_{i}^{n}
, with the aim of applying
[
4
, Proposition 1.2.19]

(see also
[
40
, Lemma 13.2]
).

Let us start by noting that, as shown in
[
40
, Lemma 10.5]
,

{\bf{A}}_{n}|_{U}=\widetilde{\bf{A}}_{n}|_{U}
H-converges to
{\bf{A}}|_{U}=\widetilde{\bf{A}}|_{U}

(recall that
n\geq n_{1}
).
Furthermore, since (
11
) holds
for any
\varphi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(U)
, we have that

-\ddiv\bigl({\bf{A}}_{n}|_{U}\nabla(u_{i}^{n})|_{U}\bigr)=f_{i}|_{U}\in{{\rm L}^{2}(U)}\,.

Therefore, since
u_{i}^{n}|_{U}\rightharpoonup u_{i}|_{U}
weakly in

{{\rm H}^{1}(U)}
, we may apply
[
4
, Proposition 1.2.19]
, which yields

{\bf{A}}_{n}|_{U}\nabla(u_{i}^{n})|_{U}\rightharpoonup{\bf{A}}|_{U}\nabla(u_{i})|_{U}
weakly
in
{{\rm L}_{\rm loc}^{2}(U;\mathbb{R}^{d})}
. Returning to the left-hand side of (

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{U}{\bf{A}}_{n}\nabla u_{i}^{n}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=\int_{U}{\bf{A}}\nabla u_{i}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}=\int_{\Omega}{\bf{A}}\nabla v_{i}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}-\langle g_{i},\varphi\rangle_{{{\rm H}^{-1}(\Omega)},{{\rm H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}}\,.

Finally, by the arbitrariness of
\varphi
and the density of
{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
in
{{\rm H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}
,
the function
v_{i}
is the weak solution of (
9
), with
\Omega_{n}
,
{\bf{A}}_{n}
and
g_{i}^{n}

replaced by
\Omega
,
{\bf{A}}
and
g_{i}
, respectively.
In light of the preceding observations, this immediately leads the
desired conclusion that
u_{i}
is the weak solution to (
2
).

Step IV.

The final task is to prove that
{\mathcal{J}}(\Omega_{n},\theta_{n},{\bf{A}}_{n})

converges to
{\mathcal{J}}(\Omega,\theta,{\bf{A}})
, i.e. that the relaxed admissible
design
(\Omega,\theta,{\bf{A}})
is the minimizer of
{\mathcal{J}}
on

{\mathcal{A}}
.

The value of the functional
{\mathcal{J}}
at the design configuration

(\Omega_{n},\theta_{n},{\bf{A}}_{n})
can be expressed as (note that here we work
with the final subsequence obtained in the previous step):

{\mathcal{J}}(\Omega_{n},\theta_{n},{\bf{A}}_{n})=\int_{D}\chi_{\Omega_{n}}({\bm{x}})\Bigl(\tilde{\theta}_{n}({\bm{x}})g_{\alpha}({\bm{x}},\widetilde{\sf u}_{n}({\bm{x}}))+(1-\tilde{\theta}_{n}({\bm{x}}))g_{\beta}({\bm{x}},\widetilde{\sf u}_{n}({\bm{x}}))\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}\,.

This representation is useful for clearly separating the ingredients that are already
under control from those that still require justification in order to pass to the
limit as
n
tends to infinity.
Indeed,
\chi_{\Omega_{n}}
strongly converges to
\chi_{\Omega}

in any
{{\rm L}^{s}(D)}
,
s\in[1,\infty\rangle
(since
(\Omega_{n})

converges to
\Omega
in the sense of characteristic functions;
cf.
[
23
, Definition 2.2.3]
), while
(\tilde{\theta}_{n})

converges weakly-
*
to
\tilde{\theta}
in
{{\rm L}^{\infty}(D)}
.
Consequently, it suffices to prove that there exists
p>1

such that

g_{\gamma}(\cdot,\widetilde{\sf u}_{n})
converges strongly to

g_{\gamma}(\cdot,\widetilde{\sf u})
in
{{\rm L}^{p}(D)}
,
\gamma\in\{\alpha,\beta\}
.

Since
\widetilde{\sf u}_{n}({\bm{x}})
converges to
\widetilde{\sf u}({\bm{x}})

for a.e.
{\bm{x}}\in D
and
g_{\gamma}
,
\gamma\in\{\alpha,\beta\}
, are
Carathéodory functions on
D\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we have that

g_{\gamma}({\bm{x}},\widetilde{\sf u}_{n}({\bm{x}}))\overset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}g_{\gamma}({\bm{x}},\widetilde{\sf u}({\bm{x}}))\quad\hbox{a.e.\penalty\ ${\bm{x}}\in D$},\gamma\in\{\alpha,\beta\}\,.

In order to conclude the argument via the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem, it remains to find, for some
p>1
,
an
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-integrable function that serves as a dominating
function for
|g_{\gamma}(\cdot,\widetilde{\sf u}_{n})|
.

A more precise application of the Rellich-Kondrašov theorem
(see
[
1
, Theorem 6.3]
) implies that, for any

r\in[1,q^{\ast}\rangle
, the sequence

(\widetilde{\sf u}_{n})
converges strongly to
\widetilde{\sf u}
in
{{\rm L}^{r}(D)}
,
where
q^{\ast}
is defined in (A5).
Hence, for any
r\in[1,q^{\ast}\rangle
there exists

w\in{{\rm L}^{r}(D)}
such that
|\widetilde{\sf u}_{n}({\bm{x}})|\leq w({\bm{x}})
a.e.
{\bm{x}}\in D
.
Then from the growth condition on
g_{\gamma}
we get

|g_{\gamma}({\bm{x}},\widetilde{\sf u}_{n}({\bm{x}}))|\leq|\varphi_{\gamma}({\bm{x}})|+|\psi_{\gamma}({\bm{x}})|w({\bm{x}})^{q}=:G({\bm{x}})\,,\quad\hbox{a.e.\penalty\ ${\bm{x}}\in D$}\,.

Now
w^{q}\in{{\rm L}^{\frac{r}{q}}(D)}
and by the Hölder inequality
\psi_{\gamma}w^{q}
belongs to
{{\rm L}^{s}(D)}
with
s>1
if

\frac{1}{s}:=\frac{1}{s_{2}}+\frac{q}{r}<1,

where we have the freedom to choose the value
r\in[1,q^{\ast}\rangle
. For
d=2
by (A5) we have

\frac{1}{s_{2}}<1
, and since
q^{\ast}=\infty
, the parameter
r
can be chosen so that
\frac{q}{r}
is arbitrarily small. On the other hand, for
d>2
it suffices that
\frac{1}{s_{2}}+\frac{q}{q^{\ast}}<1
because
r
can be taken arbitrarily close to
q^{\ast}
. Hence the assumption (A5) ensures that these conditions are satisfied.
Therefore,
G\in{{\rm L}^{p}(D)}
, with
p=\min\{s_{1},s\}>1
.
This completes the proof.

Remark 2.4
.

The result of Theorem
2.3
still holds if we remove
assumption
(A3)
and set
\Gamma_{0}=\emptyset
.
However, from the perspective of concrete applications,
this scenario is not of particular importance, as the position
of the admissible sets is typically
fixed
using the
fixed portion of the boundary
\Gamma_{0}
.

2.3
Neumann-type free boundary

To begin with, we consider the case in which the entire free part of the boundary is
subject to a homogeneous Neumann boundary condition, that is
\Gamma_{1}=\emptyset
.
We impose the following assumption on the functions
h_{i}
:

(H2)

For each
i\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
there exists
H_{i}\in\HH^{1}(D)
such
that
h_{i}=H_{i}|_{\Gamma_{0}}
.

By (A3) we know that the manifold boundary of
M
,
\partial M
,
is a compact
(d-2)
-dimensional Lipschitz manifold
(without boundary). Hence,
\partial M
is locally the graph of a
Lipschitz-continuous function and has
a finite number of connected components.
This is important since for any admissible domain
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}

we have
\overline{\Gamma_{0}}\cap\Gamma_{2}=\partial M
,
where
\Gamma_{2}=\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma_{0}
. Hence,
the set of all smooth functions
\varphi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}(\overline{D})

that vanish on a neighborhood of
\Gamma_{0}
,
when restricted to
\Omega
, is dense in

(12)

V:=\{v\in{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega)}:{v}|_{\Gamma_{0}}=0\}

(see
[
10
,
11
]
).

We are now prepared to state and prove the following theorem.

Theorem 2.5
.

Under assumptions
(A1)
–
(A5)
and
(H2)
, there exists a solution to
the optimal design
problem (
7
) when
\Gamma_{1}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Following the strategy used in the proof of Theorem
2.3
,
we shall highlight only the steps where noteworthy differences
are present.

As in the proof of Theorem
2.3
, we begin with a minimizing sequence

(\Omega_{n},\theta_{n},{\bf{A}}_{n})
, and Step I from the previous proof applies unchanged.

The following two steps requires greater care.
Let us start with the analysis of the boundary value problem (Step II in the proof of Theorem
2.3
).
For any
i\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
there is a unique solution
v_{i}^{n}
to the following variational problem:

(13)

\int_{\Omega_{n}}{\bf{A}}_{n}\nabla(v^{n}_{i}+H_{i})\cdot\nabla\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}=\int_{\Omega_{n}}f_{i}\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}\,,\;\varphi\in V_{n}\,,

where
H_{i}
is given in (H2) and

V_{n}:=\{v\in{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}:v|_{\Gamma_{0}}=0\}\,.

Then it is easy to check that
u_{i}^{n}=H_{i}|_{\Omega_{n}}+v_{i}^{n}\in H_{i}|_{\Omega_{n}}+V_{n}

is the unique weak solution to

(14)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}-\ddiv({\bf{A}}_{n}\nabla u_{i}^{n})=f_{i}&\text{ in }\Omega_{n}\\
u_{i}^{n}=h_{i}&\text{ on }\Gamma_{0}\\
{\bf{A}}\nabla u_{i}^{n}\cdot{\bm{n}}=0&\text{ on }\Gamma_{2}^{n}=\partial\Omega_{n}\setminus\Gamma_{0}.\end{array}\right.

In contrast to the previous proof, we make use of the extension
\widetilde{\sf v}^{n}
to the set
D
constructed according to the method presented by Chenais
[
16
]
. Due to the uniform cone condition, there exists a constant
c_{0}>0
, not depending on
n
such that

(15)

\|\widetilde{v}^{n}_{i}\|^{2}_{{{\rm H}^{1}(D)}}\leq c_{0}\|v^{n}_{i}\|^{2}_{{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}}\,.

Another crucial step is the usage of the Poincaré inequality.
While in the previous theorem the situation was trivial (it was applied on
the space
{{\rm H}_{0}^{1}(D)}
), here the existence of the uniform estimate for the
extensions ensures that the constant in the Poincaré inequality does not
depend on
n
(see Lemma
2.6
below). Thus, if we denote the constant by
\sqrt{c_{1}}
, we have

(16)

\displaystyle\alpha c_{1}\|\widetilde{v}^{n}_{i}\|^{2}_{{{\rm H}^{1}(D)}}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha c_{1}c_{0}\|v^{n}_{i}\|^{2}_{{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}\int_{\Omega_{n}}{\bf{A}}_{n}\nabla v^{n}_{i}\cdot\nabla v^{n}_{i}\,d{\bm{x}}=c_{0}\int_{\Omega_{n}}f_{i}v^{n}_{i}-{\bf{A}}_{n}\nabla H_{i}\cdot\nabla v^{n}_{i}\,d{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}\|f_{i}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(D)}}\|\widetilde{v}_{i}^{n}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(D)}}+c_{0}\beta\|\nabla H_{i}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d})}}\|\nabla\widetilde{v}_{i}^{n}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d})}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}\|\widetilde{v}_{i}^{n}\|_{{{\rm H}^{1}(D)}}\,,

where
c_{2}
does not depend on
n
.
Hence, the boundedness of the sequence
(\widetilde{\sf v}^{n})
in
{{\rm H}^{1}(D;\mathbb{R}^{m})}
follows. Therefore, now we can easily adapt Step II of the previous proof and obtain its weak limit
\widetilde{\sf v}\in{{\rm H}^{1}(D;\mathbb{R}^{m})}
(at a subsequence), and denote
{\sf v}:=\widetilde{\sf v}|_{\Omega}\in{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})}
. Since the trace operator is linear and continuous, it follows that

{\sf v}={\sf 0}
on
\Gamma_{0}
, i.e. each component of
{\sf v}
belongs to
V
(see (
12
)). At the same subsequence we have weak convergence of
\widetilde{\sf u}^{n}:=\widetilde{\sf v}^{n}+{\sf H}
to
\widetilde{\sf u}:=\widetilde{\sf v}+{\sf H}
, and in the same manner we denote
{\sf u}:=\widetilde{\sf u}|_{\Omega}
, belonging to the desired space
{\sf H}+V^{m}
.

The next step is to pass to the limit in (
13
) (and then also in (
14
)), which is more delicate when compared to the procedure
used in the previous theorem.

For
\eta>0
we introduce

\displaystyle\omega_{\eta}

\displaystyle=\{{\bm{x}}\in\Omega:d({\bm{x}};\partial\Omega)\geq\eta\}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\eta}

\displaystyle=\{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:d({\bm{x}};\partial\Omega)<\eta\}.

The compact set
\omega_{\eta}
is included in
\Omega
by definition,
and for any positive

\overline{\Omega}\subseteq\Gamma_{\eta}\,\dot{\cup}\,\omega_{\eta}
.
Therefore, since
(\Omega_{n})
converges to
\Omega
in the sense of
compacts, for

large enough we have

\displaystyle\omega_{\eta}

\displaystyle\subset\joinrel\subset\Omega_{n}

\displaystyle\Omega_{n}

\displaystyle\subseteq(\Gamma_{\eta}\,\dot{\cup}\,\omega_{\eta})\cap D=(\Gamma_{\eta}\cap D)\,\dot{\cup}\,\omega_{\eta}\,,

where we in addition used that
\Omega_{n}\subseteq D
.

We aim to pass to the limit in the equality (
13
), which can be equivalently rewritten as follows:

(17)

\int_{D}\chi_{\Omega_{n}}\widetilde{\bf{A}}_{n}\nabla\widetilde{u}^{n}_{i}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}=\int_{D}\chi_{\Omega_{n}}f_{i}\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}\,,

for any

that vanish on a neighborhood of
\Gamma_{0}
. Here we make use of the fact that the set of all such functions, when restricted to
\Omega_{n}
is dense in

[

The right-hand side of the previous equality converges to

converges to
\Omega

Furthermore, the integral on the left-hand side of (

\omega_{\eta}
and
\Gamma_{\eta}\cap D
.
Again by
[
4
, Proposition 1.2.19]
we have the convergence

{{\rm L}^{2}(\omega_{\eta};\mathbb{R}^{d})}
which implies the convergence

\displaystyle\int_{\omega_{\eta}}\chi_{\Omega_{n}}{\bf{A}}_{n}\nabla u^{n}_{i}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\omega_{\eta}}{\bf{A}}_{n}\nabla u^{n}_{i}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}\to\int_{\omega_{\eta}}{\bf{A}}\nabla u_{i}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\omega_{\eta}}\chi_{\Omega}{\bf{A}}\nabla u_{i}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}\,.

Finally, for the integral over
\Gamma_{\eta}

\displaystyle\Biggl|\int_{\Gamma_{\eta}\cap D}

\displaystyle\chi_{\Omega_{n}}\widetilde{\bf{A}}_{n}\nabla\widetilde{u}^{n}_{i}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}-\int_{\Gamma_{\eta}\cap D}\chi_{\Omega}\widetilde{\bf{A}}\nabla\widetilde{u}_{i}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}\Biggr|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Gamma_{\eta}\cap D}|\widetilde{\bf{A}}_{n}\nabla\widetilde{u}^{n}_{i}\cdot\nabla\varphi|\,d{\bm{x}}+\int_{\Gamma_{\eta}\cap D}|\widetilde{\bf{A}}\nabla\widetilde{u}_{i}\cdot\nabla\varphi|\,d{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\widetilde{\bf{A}}_{n}\nabla\widetilde{u}^{n}_{i}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d})}}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Gamma_{\eta}\cap D;\mathbb{R}^{d})}}+\|\widetilde{\bf{A}}\nabla\widetilde{u}_{i}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d})}}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Gamma_{\eta}\cap D;\mathbb{R}^{d})}}

\displaystyle\leq\beta\|\nabla\varphi\|_{{{\rm L}^{\infty}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d})}}\bigl(\|\widetilde{u}_{i}^{n}\|_{{{\rm H}^{1}(D)}}+\|\widetilde{u}_{i}\|_{{{\rm H}^{1}(D)}}\bigr)\sqrt{|\Gamma_{\eta}\cap D|}

\displaystyle\leq c\sqrt{|\Gamma_{\eta}|}\,,

where constant
c
does not depend on
n
.
Since
|\Gamma_{\eta}|
converges to zero as
\eta
goes to zero,
we can pass to the limit in (
17
) and conclude

\int_{\Omega}{\bf{A}}\nabla u_{i}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}=\int_{\Omega}f_{i}\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}\,,\ i=1,\ldots,m\,,

for any
\varphi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}(\overline{D})
that vanish on a neighborhood of
\Gamma_{0}
, which means that the limit
{\sf u}
solves (
2
).

Finally, we apply Step IV of the previous proof directly, which completes the proof.
∎

Here we provide an important uniform Poincaré inequality for admissible domains.
The strategy of the proof is standard and relies on Chenais’s extension
(see e.g.
[
22
, Lemma 2.19]
);
however, for completeness, we provide the full proof.

Lemma 2.6
.

There exists a constant
c>0
such that for any admissible domain

\Omega\in\mathcal{O}
and
u\in V=\{v\in{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega)}:{v}|_{\Gamma_{0}}=0\}
it holds

c\,\|u\|_{{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega)}}\leq\|\nabla u\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}}\,.

Proof.

Let us assume that the claim does not hold.
Then for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
\Omega_{n}\in\mathcal{O}
and

u_{n}\in V_{n}=\{v\in{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}:{v}|_{\Gamma_{0}}=0\}
such that

\|u_{n}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega_{n})}}=1
and

\frac{1}{n}\|u_{n}\|_{{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}}>\|\nabla u_{n}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega_{n};\mathbb{R}^{d})}}\,.

Hence, since
\|u_{n}\|_{{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}}^{2}=1+\|\nabla u_{n}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega_{n};\mathbb{R}^{d})}}^{2}
,
we get

\|\nabla u_{n}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega_{n};\mathbb{R}^{d})}}<\frac{1}{\sqrt{n^{2}-1}}
,
n\geq 2
,
implying

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\nabla u_{n}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega_{n};\mathbb{R}^{d})}}=0\,.

In particular, the sequence of real numbers
(\|u_{n}\|_{{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}})
is bounded.

Let us denote by
\widetilde{u}_{n}
the extension of
u_{n}
following the method
presented by Chenais
[
16
]
.
Due to the uniform cone condition, there exists a constant

c_{0}>0
independent of
n
such that

\|\widetilde{u}_{n}\|_{{{\rm H}^{1}(D)}}\leq c_{0}\|u_{n}\|_{{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}}\,,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}\,.

Thus,
(\widetilde{u}_{n})
is bounded in
{{\rm H}^{1}(D)}
.
Let us pass to a subsequence (keeping the same notation) such that

(\widetilde{u}_{n})
converges to
v
weakly in
{{\rm H}^{1}(D)}
and
strongly in
{\rm L}^{q}(D)
for any
q\in[1,q^{\ast})

(see
[
1
, Theorem 6.3]
),
where
q^{\ast}
is defined in (A5).

According to
[
23
, Theorem 2.4.10]

(see also
[
16
]
), we can pass to another subsequence such that

\Omega_{n}
converges to
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}

in the sense of Hausdorff (for open sets) and
in the sense of characteristic functions.

Let us take an arbitrary
\varphi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(D)
.
Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, it holds

\left|\int_{\Omega_{n}}\varphi({\bm{x}})\nabla u_{n}({\bm{x}})\,d{\bm{x}}\right|\leq\|\nabla u_{n}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega_{n};\mathbb{R}^{d})}}\|\varphi\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(D)}}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0\,.

On the other hand, since
(\chi_{\Omega_{n}})
converges strongly to
\chi_{\Omega}
in

{{\rm L}^{2}(D)}
and
(\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n})
converges weakly to

\nabla v
in
{{\rm L}^{2}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d})}
, we have

\displaystyle 0=\lim_{n}\int_{\Omega_{n}}\varphi({\bm{x}})\nabla u_{n}({\bm{x}})\,d{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n}\int_{D}\varphi({\bm{x}})\chi_{\Omega_{n}}({\bm{x}})\nabla\widetilde{u}_{n}({\bm{x}})\,d{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\varphi({\bm{x}})\chi_{\Omega}({\bm{x}})\nabla v({\bm{x}})\,d{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\varphi({\bm{x}})\nabla v({\bm{x}})\,d{\bm{x}}\,.

Since
\varphi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(D)
was arbitrary, we get that
\nabla v={\sf 0}
a.e. in
\Omega
.
Thus,
v
is a constant function on
\Omega
(recall that
\Omega
is connected).

Further on, since the trace is weakly sequentially continuous,
from
\widetilde{u}_{n}|_{\Gamma_{0}}=u_{n}|_{\Gamma_{0}}=0
, follows
v|_{\Gamma_{0}}=0
.
Thus,
v=0
a.e. in
\Omega
.

However, since
(\widetilde{u}_{n}^{2})
converges strongly in
{\rm L}^{p}(D)
for some
p>1

(in fact,
p
is any number in the interval
(1,\frac{q^{\ast}}{2})
)
and
(\chi_{\Omega_{n}})
converges strongly in
{\rm L}^{r}(D)
for any

r\in[1,\infty)
, we can pass to the limit:

1=\|u_{n}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega_{n})}}^{2}=\int_{D}\chi_{\Omega_{n}}({\bm{x}})\bigl(\widetilde{u}_{n}({\bm{x}})\bigr)^{2}\,d{\bm{x}}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}v({\bm{x}})^{2}\,d{\bm{x}}=0\,,

where we have used that
v=0
a.e. in
\Omega
.

Therefore, we have obtained a contradiction,
which ensures that the statement of the lemma must hold.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

The volume constraint
|\Omega|=V
present in the definition of the set of admissible domains
\mathcal{O}
(see (
6
)) is irrelevant to the assertion of the previous lemma and can be omitted.

2.4
General boundary conditions

Let us now consider the general case, where both
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
are (possibly) nonempty.
For potential practical implementations, we naturally introduce subsets
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
of the domain
D
which determine where the Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions are applied.
More precisely, for a given admissible domain
\Omega
, we will define

\Gamma_{i}=\partial\Omega\cap Q_{i}
,
i=1,2
.
Nevertheless, as the subsequent example demonstrates, additional assumptions concerning the boundary sets
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
(i.e. on the family of admissible domains) are necessary.

Example 2.8
.

Let
D=\langle-2,2\rangle\times\langle-2,2\rangle
, with
Q_{1}=[-2,2]\times\langle 0,2]
and
Q_{2}=[-2,2]\times[-2,0]
, and
\Gamma_{0}=[0,1]\times\{1\}
.
We introduce a sequence of boundary value problems on

\Omega_{n}=\langle 0,1\rangle\times\langle 1/n,1\rangle
:

(18)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}-\Delta u^{n}=0&\text{ in }\Omega_{n}\\
u^{n}(x,y)=\sin\pi x&\text{ on }\Gamma_{0}\\
u^{n}=0&\text{ on }\Gamma_{1}^{n}=\partial\Omega_{n}\setminus\Gamma_{0}.\end{array}\right.

The sequence of domains
(\Omega_{n})
converges to
\Omega=\langle 0,1\rangle\times\langle 0,1\rangle
in the Hausdorff sense, which implies that the boundary condition on the lower side is altered. The limiting boundary value problem states:

(19)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}-\Delta u=0&\text{ in }\Omega\\
u(x,y)=\sin\pi x&\text{ on }\Gamma_{0}\\
u=0&\text{ on }\Gamma_{1}=\left(\{0\}\times\langle 0,1]\right)\cup\left(\{1\}\times\langle 0,1]\right)\\
\nabla u\cdot{\bm{n}}=0&\text{ on }\Gamma_{2}=[0,1]\times\{0\}.\end{array}\right.

It is straightforward to solve the problem using the Fourier separation method. One observes that the unique solution of (
18
) is

u^{n}(x,y)=\frac{\sh\pi\left(y-\frac{1}{n}\right)}{\sh\pi\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)}\sin\pi x,

converging uniformly (on any compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
) to

\displaystyle\frac{\sh\pi y}{\sh\pi}\sin\pi x
, whereas the unique solution of (
19
) is

u(x,y)=\displaystyle\frac{\ch\pi y}{\ch\pi}\sin\pi x.

Hence, it is impossible for any extension of
u^{n}
to converge weakly in
\HH^{1}
to an
extension of
u
, which is essential
for the validity of the preceding proofs.

To avoid unwanted phenomena, we narrow the set of admissible domains by introducing an additional assumption:

(A6)

Q_{1},Q_{2}\subseteq\overline{D}
are given disjoint compact sets such that
\overline{\Gamma_{0}}\setminus(Q_{1}\cup Q_{2})=\Gamma_{0}
, where
\Gamma_{0}
is given in (A3).

This assumption ensures a clear separation between the variable
parts of the Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions,
thereby avoiding the pathological scenario described in Example
2.8
.
Furthermore, the condition
\overline{\Gamma_{0}}\setminus(Q_{1}\cup Q_{2})=\Gamma_{0}

implies that the boundary of the fixed region,

\partial\Gamma_{0}
, is entirely contained in
\partial Q_{1}\cup\partial Q_{2}
.
Finally, note that
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
may be empty, but not simultaneously (see Remark
2.10
).

Since we will use that
\Gamma_{i}=\partial\Omega\cap Q_{i}
,
i=1,2
,
a necessary condition is
\partial\Omega\setminus(Q_{1}\cup Q_{2})=\Gamma_{0}
.
This condition we need to include in the set of admissible designs. Hence, the new sets of
admissible domains
and
designs

reads, respectively,

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{\prime}=\Bigl\{\Omega\subseteq D:\Omega\subseteq D

\displaystyle\text{ is open, connected and satisfies the $\varepsilon$-cone property},

\displaystyle|\Omega|=V,\;\Gamma_{0}\subset\partial\Omega,\;\partial\Omega\setminus(Q_{1}\cup Q_{2})=\Gamma_{0}\Bigr\}

and

(20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A^{\prime}}=\Bigl\{(\Omega,\theta,{\bf{A}}):\Omega\in\mathcal{O}^{\prime},\;\theta\in{{\rm L}^{\infty}(\Omega;[0,1])},\;\int_{\Omega}\theta\,d{\bm{x}}=q_{\alpha},\;{\bf{A}}\in{\mathcal{K}}(\theta)\>\text{ a.e. in }\Omega\Bigr\}\,.

In order to ensure that the set of admissible designs is non-empty,
we need to strengthen assumption (A4):

(A4
′
)

There exists
\widehat{\Omega}\in\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
.

Finally, we impose the following assumption on functions
h_{i}
:

(H3)

For any
i\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
there exists
H_{i}\in\HH^{1}(D)
such that
h_{i}=H_{i}|_{\Gamma_{0}}
and
H_{i}=0
a.e. in
Q_{1}
.

For a given admissible design
(\Omega,\theta,{\bf{A}})\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
,
let
{\sf u}
denote the unique solution of (
2
),
where the boundary segments are defined by
\Gamma_{i}=\partial\Omega\cap Q_{i}
for

i=1,2
.
By this definition of
\Gamma_{i}
,
i=1,2
, and the definition of
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
we indeed have

\displaystyle\partial\Omega

\displaystyle=(\partial\Omega\cap Q_{1})\,\dot{\cup}\,(\partial\Omega\cap Q_{2})\,\dot{\cup}\,\bigl(\partial\Omega\setminus(Q_{1}\cup Q_{2})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{1}\,\dot{\cup}\,\Gamma_{2}\,\dot{\cup}\,\Gamma_{0}\,.

Hence, we are interested in solving the optimal design problem
(
7
), with
\mathcal{A}
replaced by
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
.

We have the following result.

Theorem 2.9
.

Under assumptions
(A1)–(A3)
,
(A4
′
)
,
(A5), (A6)
,
and
(H3)
,
the optimal design problem (
7
)
admits a solution, where the set of admissible designs
\mathcal{A}

is replaced by
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
(cf. (
20
)) and,
in (
2
), the boundary segments are defined by
\Gamma_{i}=\partial\Omega\cap Q_{i}
for
i=1,2
.

Remark 2.10
.

This final general setting encompasses both previously addressed ones.
Here we rely on Lemma
2.1
.

More precisely, for the Dirichlet problem discussed in Theorem
2.3
,
we set
Q_{1}=\overline{D}\setminus O
and
Q_{2}=\emptyset
.
Under this choice, assumption
(A6)
is automatically satisfied by
Lemma
2.1
, as is the additional condition

\partial\Omega\setminus(Q_{1}\cup Q_{1})=\partial\Omega\cap O=\Gamma_{0}\,.

Hence, we have
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}
and

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\mathcal{A}
.

For the mixed-problem with the fixed Dirichlet part studied in Theorem
2.5

we just take the reverse choice:
Q_{1}=\emptyset
and
Q_{2}=\overline{D}\setminus O
.
Therefore, Theorem
2.9
is indeed more general when compared to
Theorems
2.3
and
2.5
.

In terms of admissible domains and their corresponding boundary conditions, the setting of Theorem
2.9
is quite general. In particular, it covers problems of Bernoulli type
[
21
]
as well as pipe-like (tubular) domains
[
24
]
.
However, at this level of generality, we cannot allow the moving portions of the Dirichlet and Neumann boundaries to meet (see Example
2.8
). To accommodate such cases, more restrictive assumptions on the admissible domains must be imposed, as seen in
[
22
, Chapter 2]
.

Proof of Theorem
2.9
.

We employ the same steps used in the previous proofs.

Since
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, for a minimizing sequence

(\Omega_{n},\theta_{n},{\bf{A}}_{n})\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
following the construction from Step I
we obtain
(\Omega,\theta,{\bf{A}})\in\mathcal{A}
.
Hence, it is left to check that
\partial\Omega\setminus(Q_{1}\cup Q_{2})=\Gamma_{0}
.

The inclusion
\Gamma_{0}\subseteq\partial\Omega
is guaranteed by the definition
of the set
\mathcal{A}
. Thus, by (A6) we have

\Gamma_{0}=\Gamma_{0}\setminus(Q_{1}\cup Q_{2})\subseteq\partial\Omega\setminus(Q_{1}\cup Q_{2})\,.

For the converse inclusion, we start with an arbitrary
{\bm{x}}\in\partial\Omega\setminus(Q_{1}\cup Q_{2})
.
Since
(\partial\Omega_{n})
converges to
\partial\Omega
in the sense of Hausdorff
(for compact sets), there exists
{\bm{x}}_{n}\in\partial\Omega_{n}
such that
({\bm{x}}_{n})
converges
to
{\bm{x}}
(cf.
[
23
, Subsection 2.2.3.2]
). Using that the set
Q_{1}\cup Q_{2}
is closed,
for
n
large enough we have
{\bm{x}}_{n}\in\partial\Omega_{n}\setminus(Q_{1}\cup Q_{2})=\Gamma_{0}
.
Thus,
{\bm{x}}\in\overline{\Gamma_{0}}
, which leads to the identity:

{\bm{x}}\in\bigl(\partial\Omega\setminus(Q_{1}\cup Q_{2})\bigr)\cap\overline{\Gamma_{0}}=\overline{\Gamma_{0}}\setminus(Q_{1}\cup Q_{2})=\Gamma_{0}\,.

The steps are justified by
\overline{\Gamma_{0}}\subseteq\partial\Omega

(first equality) and assumption (A6) (second equality).
Therefore, we indeed have
(\Omega,\theta,{\bf{A}})\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
.

Before proceeding to the next step, we first show that
\Gamma_{1}^{n}=\partial\Omega_{n}\cap Q_{1}

converges, up to a subsequence,
in the sense of Hausdorff for compact sets to

\Gamma_{1}=\partial\Omega\cap Q_{1}
.
Let us denote by
L
the Hausdorff limit of
\Gamma_{1}^{n}
. Since the inclusion is preserved under
Hausdorff convergence for compact sets (cf.
[
23
, Subsection 2.2.3.2]
),
we have
L\subseteq\Gamma_{1}
. In general, the intersection is not continuous with
respect to Hausdorff convergence for compact sets.
However, the specific structure of our sets allows us to handle this issue.
Let us take
{\bm{x}}\in\Gamma_{1}\subseteq\partial\Omega
. Since
(\partial\Omega_{n})

converges in the sense of Hausdorff (for compact sets) to
\partial\Omega
,
by
[
23
, (2.14)]
there exists a sequence
({\bm{x}}_{n})
that converges to

{\bm{x}}
and such that
{\bm{x}}_{n}\in\partial\Omega_{n}
.
If in addition we have
{\bm{x}}_{n}\in Q_{1}
, then using the same characterisation we get

{\bm{x}}\in L
(of course, it is sufficient to have a subsequence of
({\bm{x}}_{n})
that
it is contained in
Q_{1}
). Hence, it is left to analyse the case where

{\bm{x}}_{n}\not\in Q_{1}
, for all but finitely many
n
.
Since
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
are disjoint compact sets (see (A6)), for sufficiently
large
n
all
{\bm{x}}_{n}
lie on
\overline{\Gamma_{0}}
.
This in turn implies
{\bm{x}}\in\overline{\Gamma_{0}}
.
Consequently, we have

{\bm{x}}\in\partial\Omega\cap Q_{1}\cap\overline{\Gamma_{0}}=Q_{1}\cap\overline{\Gamma_{0}}\subseteq Q_{1}\cap\partial\Omega_{n}=\Gamma_{1}^{n}\,,

where we have used that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
it holds

\overline{\Gamma_{0}}\subseteq\partial\Omega\cap\partial\Omega_{n}
.
Then we can conclude that
{\bm{x}}\in L
, and in particular
L=\Gamma_{1}
.

In the next step we study the boundary value problem (Step II in the proof of Theorem
2.3
).
We start as before with homogenizing the Dirichlet boundary conditions.
And the rational is completely analogous as in the proof of Theorem
2.5
.
More precisely, for any
i\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
there is a unique solution
v_{i}^{n}
to the following variational problem:

(21)

\int_{\Omega_{n}}{\bf{A}}_{n}\nabla(v^{n}_{i}+H_{i})\cdot\nabla\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}=\int_{\Omega_{n}}f_{i}\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}\,,\;\varphi\in W_{n}\,,

where
H_{i}
is given in (H3) and

W_{n}:=\{v\in{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}:v|_{\Gamma_{0}\cup\Gamma_{1}^{n}}=0\}\,.

Recall that
\Gamma_{i}^{n}=\partial\Omega_{n}\cap Q_{i}
,
i=1,2
.
Then it is easy to check that
u_{i}^{n}=H_{i}|_{\Omega_{n}}+v_{i}^{n}\in H_{i}|_{\Omega_{n}}+W_{n}

is the unique weak solution to

(22)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}-\ddiv({\bf{A}}_{n}\nabla u_{i}^{n})=f_{i}&\text{ in }\Omega_{n}\\
u_{i}^{n}=h_{i}&\text{ on }\Gamma_{0}\\
u_{i}^{n}=0&\text{ on }\Gamma_{1}^{n}\\
{\bf{A}}\nabla u_{i}^{n}\cdot{\bm{n}}=0&\text{ on }\Gamma_{2}^{n},\end{array}\right.

where we used that
H_{i}=0
a.e. on
Q_{1}
and
\Gamma_{1}^{n}\subseteq Q_{1}
.

Analogous to the derivation in (
16
), we can show that the sequence of
real numbers
(\|v_{i}^{n}\|_{{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}})_{n}
is bounded. Indeed, by
utilising Lemma
2.6
(where
\sqrt{c_{1}}
represents the constant
c
),
the coercivity of
{\bf{A}}
, the weak formulation (
21
), and assumption
(H3), we obtain:

\displaystyle\alpha c_{1}\|v^{n}_{i}\|^{2}_{{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\|\nabla v^{n}_{i}\|^{2}_{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega_{n})}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega_{n}}{\bf{A}}_{n}\nabla v^{n}_{i}\cdot\nabla v^{n}_{i}\,d{\bm{x}}=\int_{\Omega_{n}}f_{i}v^{n}_{i}-{\bf{A}}_{n}\nabla H_{i}\cdot\nabla v^{n}_{i}\,d{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\leq\|f_{i}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(D)}}\|v_{i}^{n}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega_{n})}}+\beta\|\nabla H_{i}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(D)}}\|\nabla v_{i}^{n}\|_{{{\rm L}^{2}(\Omega_{n})}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}\|v_{i}^{n}\|_{{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}}\,,

where constant
c_{2}>0
does not depend on
n
. Thus,

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|v_{i}^{n}\|_{{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}}<\infty
.

We now need to extend
v_{i}^{n}
to the whole domain
D
. Since
v_{i}^{n}
is
not necessarily zero on the entire boundary
\partial\Omega_{n}
(see
W_{n}
),
a simple zero extension (as employed in the proof of Theorem
2.3
)
is not possible. Furthermore, while the extension in the sense of Chenais is applicable,
it does not preserve the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions required
for our framework. Thus, we will use a combination of these two extension techniques.

Since
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
are compact disjoint sets (see (A6)), there exists open, bounded
and disjoint open neighbourhoods
U_{1},U_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.
Q_{i}\subset U_{i}
,
i=1,2
.
Let us take two smooth nonnegative functions
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

with the following properties:

\displaystyle\varphi_{i}=0\quad\hbox{on}\ U_{j}\,,\ i\neq j\,,

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}^{2}+\varphi_{2}^{2}=1\quad\hbox{on}\ \mathbb{R}^{d}

(the necessity of the condition
\varphi_{1}^{2}+\varphi_{2}^{2}=1
will become
apparent later in the proof).
This can be achieved by mollifying the characteristic functions of the complement of

\widetilde{U}_{i}
,
i=1,2
, where
\widetilde{U}_{i}
are slightly larger disjoint open sets

U_{i}\subset\joinrel\subset\widetilde{U}_{i}
. If we denote these functions by
\eta_{i}
,
i=1,2
,
then we can take

\varphi_{1}=\frac{\eta_{2}}{\sqrt{\eta_{1}^{2}+\eta_{2}^{2}}}
and

\varphi_{2}=\frac{\eta_{1}}{\sqrt{\eta_{1}^{2}+\eta_{2}^{2}}}
(note that the denominator is
never zero).

Let us define
w_{i}^{n}:=v_{i}^{n}\varphi_{1}
. Since
v_{i}^{n}
is zero on
\Gamma_{0}\cup\Gamma_{1}^{n}
,
while
\varphi_{1}
is zero on
U_{2}\supset Q_{2}
, we have that
w_{i}^{n}
has the zero trace on
the whole boundary
\partial\Omega_{n}
. Hence,
w_{i}^{n}\in\HH_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{n})
, which allows
us to extend
w_{i}^{n}
by zero to the whole
D
. Let us denote these extensions by a single
vector-valued function
\widetilde{\sf w}^{n}=(\widetilde{w}_{1}^{n},\dots,\widetilde{w}_{m}^{n})
.
Since,

\|\widetilde{w}_{i}^{n}\|_{{{\rm H}_{0}^{1}(D)}}=\|w_{i}^{n}\|_{{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}}\leq C_{\varphi_{1}}\|v_{i}^{n}\|_{{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega_{n})}}\,,

the sequence
(\widetilde{\sf w}^{n})
is bounded in
\HH_{0}^{1}(D;\mathbb{R}^{m})
.
Thus, we can pass to another subsequence such that
(\widetilde{\sf w}^{n})

converges to
\widetilde{\sf w}\in\HH_{0}^{1}(D;\mathbb{R}^{m})
weakly in
\HH_{0}^{1}(D;\mathbb{R}^{m})
and almost everywhere
on
D
.

For a sequence
z_{i}^{n}:=v_{i}^{n}\varphi_{2}
we apply the extension of Chenais
[
16
]
,
denoted by
\widetilde{z}_{i}^{n}
and
\widetilde{\sf z}^{n}=(\widetilde{z}_{1}^{n},\dots,\widetilde{z}_{m}^{n})
.
Then by (
15
) we have

\|\widetilde{\sf z}^{n}\|_{{{\rm H}^{1}(D;\mathbb{R}^{m})}}\leq\sqrt{c_{0}}\|{\sf z}^{n}\|_{{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega_{n};\mathbb{R}^{m})}}\leq\sqrt{c_{0}}C_{\varphi_{2}}\|{\sf v}^{n}\|_{{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega_{n};\mathbb{R}^{m})}}\,,

implying that
(\widetilde{\sf z}^{n})
is bounded in
\HH^{1}(D;\mathbb{R}^{m})
.
We then take another subsequence such that
\widetilde{\sf z}^{n}
converges to

\widetilde{\sf z}
weakly in
\HH^{1}(D;\mathbb{R}^{m})
and almost everywhere
on
D
.

Finally, let us define

\widetilde{\sf v}^{n}:=\varphi_{1}\widetilde{\sf w}^{n}+\varphi_{2}\widetilde{\sf z}^{n}\,.

Then
(\widetilde{\sf v}^{n})
converges to
\varphi_{1}\widetilde{\sf w}+\varphi_{2}\widetilde{\sf z}=:\widetilde{\sf v}

weakly in
{{\rm H}^{1}(D;\mathbb{R}^{m})}
and a.e. on
D
.
Let us show that
\widetilde{\sf v}^{n}
is an extension of
{\sf v}^{n}
. For a.e.
{\bm{x}}\in\Omega_{n}

we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sf v}^{n}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\varphi_{1}({\bm{x}})\widetilde{\sf w}^{n}({\bm{x}})+\varphi_{2}({\bm{x}})\widetilde{\sf z}^{n}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\varphi_{1}({\bm{x}}){\sf w}^{n}({\bm{x}})+\varphi_{2}({\bm{x}}){\sf z}^{n}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=(\varphi_{1}({\bm{x}})^{2}+\varphi_{2}({\bm{x}})^{2}){\sf v}^{n}({\bm{x}})={\sf v}^{n}({\bm{x}})\,.

Thus, as announced, we have successfully constructed an extension of

{\sf v}_{n}
using a combination of the zero-extension and Chenais’s extension.
Crucially, the construction method is independent of
n
.

This completes Step II. Now we turn to the restriction

{\sf v}:=\widetilde{\sf v}|_{\Omega}
.
While it is obvious that
{\sf v}\in{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})}
, it is left to check
the boundary conditions, i.e. that
{\sf v}
belongs to the suitable
variational space
W^{m}
, where

W:=\{v\in{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega)}:v|_{\Gamma_{0}\cup\Gamma_{1}}=0\}

and
\Gamma_{1}=\partial\Omega\cap Q_{1}
.

Since the condition
{\sf v}^{n}|_{\Gamma_{0}}={\sf 0}
holds for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
it follows from the construction of the extensions that

\widetilde{\sf w}^{n}|_{\Gamma_{0}}=\widetilde{\sf z}^{n}|_{\Gamma_{0}}={\sf 0}
for
all
n
(recall that
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}
are smooth).
As the trace is weakly sequentially continuous, we conclude
that the weak limits also satisfy the boundary condition:

\widetilde{\sf w}|_{\Gamma_{0}}=\widetilde{\sf z}|_{\Gamma_{0}}={\sf 0}
.
This, in turn, implies that
{\sf v}
satisfies the
homogeneous boundary condition on the fixed part of the boundary:

{\sf v}|_{\Gamma_{0}}={\sf 0}
.

Following the approach from the beginning of Step
III of the proof of Theorem
2.3
, we get that

{\sf w}:=\widetilde{\sf w}|_{\Omega}\in{{\rm H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})}
.
In particular,
(\varphi_{1}{\sf w})|_{\Gamma_{1}}={\sf 0}
.
On the other hand, we trivially have

(\varphi_{2}{\sf z})|_{\Gamma_{1}}={\sf 0}

because
\varphi_{2}
was constructed to be zero on the
open set
U_{1}
, which contains
\Gamma_{1}

(since
\Gamma_{1}\subseteq Q_{1}\subseteq U_{1}
).
The latter is the reason why we did not define

\widetilde{\sf v}^{n}
simply as the sum of
\widetilde{\sf w}^{n}
and

\widetilde{\sf z}^{n}
, as then we would not have any information
about the support of
\widetilde{\sf z}^{n}
around
\Gamma_{1}
.
Therefore, based on the established boundary conditions,
we indeed have
{\sf v}\in W^{m}
.

Furthermore, at the same subsequence we have weak convergence of
\widetilde{\sf u}^{n}:=\widetilde{\sf v}^{n}+{\sf H}
to
\widetilde{\sf u}
, and in the same manner we denote
{\sf u}:=\widetilde{\sf u}|_{\Omega}
, belonging to the desired space

{\sf H}+W^{m}
.

It is left to see that
{\sf v}
is a solution to a suitable
variational problem, i.e. we need to pass to the limit
in (
21
) (or in (
22
) if we consider
the equation for
u_{i}^{n}
).

Let us take an arbitrary
\varphi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

which is equal to zero on an open neighbourhood
U
of

\Gamma_{0}\cup\Gamma_{1}
, i.e.
\Gamma_{0}\cup\Gamma_{1}\subseteq U
.
The set of restriction to
\Omega
of such functions
\varphi

is dense in
W
(given above).
Indeed, the claim follows by
[
10
, Theorem 3.1]
.
This is because the set
\Gamma_{0}\cup\Gamma_{1}
is relatively
open in
\partial\Omega
(since its complement
\Gamma_{2}
is closed
in
\partial\Omega
). Furthermore, the interface between the sets,
defined by
\overline{\Gamma_{0}\cup\Gamma_{1}}\cap\Gamma_{2}
,
simplifies to
\overline{\Gamma_{0}}\cap\Gamma_{2}
(since
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}

are disjoint).
This interface, which is a subset of
\partial\Gamma_{0}
,
satisfies the required regularity condition:
it has finitely many connected components and is locally
a graph of a Lipschitz continuous function, as ensured by assumption (A3).

Since
\Gamma_{1}^{n}
converges to
\Gamma_{1}
in the sense of Hausdorff
(for compact sets), for sufficiently large
n
we have

\Gamma_{1}^{n}\cup\Gamma_{0}\subseteq U
(cf.
[
23
, Proposition 2.2.17]

for the complementary property for open sets).
Hence,
\varphi|_{\Omega_{n}}\in W_{n}
, i.e.
\varphi

is an admissible test function for the variational problem for
v_{i}^{n}

(
21
).

The passage to the limit in (
21
) for the above chosen

\varphi
is performed in exactly the same way as in the proof of
Theorem
2.5
.
Thus, we end up with

\int_{\Omega}{\bf{A}}\nabla u_{i}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}=\int_{\Omega}f_{i}\varphi\,d{\bm{x}}\,,\ i=1,\ldots,m\,,

valid for any
\varphi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}(\overline{D})

that vanish on a neighborhood of
\Gamma_{0}\cup\Gamma_{1}
,
which means that the limit
{\sf u}
solves (
2
).

The rest of the proof of Theorem
2.3
, i.e. Step IV,
carries over without modification.
∎

3
Hybrid method - algorithm overview

Building on the relaxation results derived in the previous section, we now introduce the associated numerical scheme.
The method follows the relaxation framework closely: it combines homogenization-based material relaxation in the
interior with shape optimization driven by shape differentiation of the domain.

To ensure well-posedness of the algorithm, we impose additional regularity assumptions on the cost functional, namely
its differentiability. Specifically, we assume that the functions
g_{\alpha}
and
g_{\beta}
are differentiable on
D\times\mathbb{R}^{m}

with respect to both the spatial variable
{\bm{x}}
and the state variable
{\sf u}
.
We denote the gradient of
g_{\gamma}
(for
\gamma=\alpha,\beta
) with respect to
{\bm{x}}
and
{\sf u}
by

\nabla_{\bm{x}}g_{\gamma}=(\frac{\partial g_{\gamma}}{\partial x_{1}},\ldots,\frac{\partial g_{\gamma}}{\partial x_{d}})^{\tau}
,
and
\nabla_{\sf u}g_{\gamma}=(\frac{\partial g_{\gamma}}{\partial u_{1}},\ldots,\frac{\partial g_{\gamma}}{\partial u_{m}})^{\tau}
, respectively.

We assume the following:

(A7)

i.

For
\gamma\in\{\alpha,\beta\}
and
i=1,2,\ldots,n
,
\frac{\partial g_{\gamma}}{\partial x_{i}}:D\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
are Carathéodory functions and satisfy the same growth condition as
g_{\gamma}
in (A5).

ii.

For
\gamma\in\{\alpha,\beta\}
and
j=1,2,\ldots,m
,
\frac{\partial g_{\gamma}}{\partial u_{j}}:D\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
are Carathéodory functions and satisfy the growth conditions

\left|\frac{\partial g_{\gamma}}{\partial u_{j}}({\bm{x}},{\sf u})\right|\leq\widetilde{\varphi}_{\gamma}({\bm{x}})+\widetilde{\psi}_{\gamma}({\bm{x}})|{\sf u}|^{q-1},

for some
q\in[1,q^{\ast}\rangle
, with nonnegative functions
\widetilde{\varphi}_{\gamma}\in{{\rm L}^{s_{1}}(D)}

and
\widetilde{\psi}_{\gamma}\in{{\rm L}^{s_{2}}(D)}
, where
s_{1}>q_{*}^{\prime}
(with
q_{*}^{\prime}
denoting the dual exponent), and
s_{2}
satisfies the same condition as in (A5).

We begin by fixing a domain
\Omega\in\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
(or
\mathcal{O}
if some of the variable boundary conditions are absent,
as discussed in Remark
2.10
). For a fixed
\Omega
, we employ the optimality criteria method,
a well-established approach in structural optimization
[
7
,
36
,
4
]
. Within the framework of compliance
minimization in linearized elasticity
[
3
]
, this method can be rigorously interpreted as a descent algorithm
of the alternating-directions type for a double minimization problem. The convergence of the method for energy
minimization in stationary diffusion problems involving two isotropic materials was proved in
[
43
]
, and for
the multi-state case in
[
15
]
. In the specific case of mixtures of two isotropic phases, the explicit solution
of the G-closure problem enables the treatment of a general cost functional with multiple states

[
4
,
44
,
14
]
.

The objective is to determine an optimal distribution of materials such that (
\Omega
is fixed) the
admissible design set
{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega}:=\{(\theta,{\bf{A}}):(\Omega,\theta,{\bf{A}})\in{\mathcal{A^{\prime}}}\}
minimizes the prescribed
objective functional.

Let
(\theta,{\bf{A}})\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega}
, and let
{\sf u}
denote the solution of (
2
) associated with the
triplet
(\Omega,\theta,{\bf{A}})\in\mathcal{A^{\prime}}
. Consider a variation
(\delta\theta,\delta{\bf{A}})
of the given design.
The corresponding first-order variation (more precisely, its Gâteaux derivative) of the cost functional
\mathcal{J}

with respect to
(\theta,{\bf{A}})
is then given by (for details, the reader is referred to
[
4
]
)

\mathcal{J}^{\prime}_{(\theta,{\bf{A}})}(\Omega,\theta,{\bf{A}};\delta\theta,\delta{\bf{A}})=\int_{\Omega}\delta\theta[g_{\alpha}(\cdot,{\sf u})-g_{\beta}(\cdot,{\sf u})]\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}-\int_{\Omega}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta A\nabla u_{i}\cdot\nabla p_{i}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}.

Here, the adjoint state function
{\sf p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{m})\in W^{m}=\{{\sf u}\in\HH^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m}):{\sf u}|_{\Gamma_{0}\cup\Gamma_{1}}={\sf 0}\}
are solutions of the following adjoint equation:

(23)

(\forall\varphi\in V)\quad\int_{\Omega}{\bf{A}}\nabla p_{i}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}=\int_{\Omega}\left[\theta\frac{\partial g_{\alpha}}{\partial u_{i}}(\cdot,{\sf u})+(1-\theta)\frac{\partial g_{\beta}}{\partial u_{i}}(\cdot,{\sf u})\right]\varphi\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}},

for
i=1,\ldots,m
, with
W=\{\varphi\in{{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega)}:\varphi|_{\Gamma_{0}\cup\Gamma_{1}}=0\}
.

Remark 3.1
.

The right-hand side of (
23
) is well defined, that is,

\theta\frac{\partial g_{\alpha}}{\partial u_{i}}(\cdot,{\sf u})+(1-\theta)\frac{\partial g_{\beta}}{\partial u_{i}}(\cdot,{\sf u})

belongs to
{{\rm L}^{r}(\Omega)}
, for some
r>q_{*}^{\prime}
, which is continuously embedded in
W^{\prime}
, the dual space of
W
.
Indeed, we have
{\sf u}\in W^{m}\hookrightarrow{{\rm L}^{q_{\ast}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})}

(or
{\sf u}\in{{\rm L}^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})}
for any
p\in\left[1,+\infty\right>
if
d=2
).

In the case
d=2
, we have

|{\sf u}|^{q-1}\in{{\rm L}^{\frac{p}{q-1}}(\Omega)}
for any
p\in\left[1,+\infty\right>
. Consequently,

\widetilde{\psi}_{\gamma}|{\sf u}|^{q-1}\in{{\rm L}^{r}(\Omega)}
whenever

\frac{1}{r}=\frac{1}{s_{2}}+\frac{q-1}{p}
.
Since
p
can be chosen arbitrarily large, the right-hand side converges to
\frac{1}{s_{2}}
as
p\to+\infty
. In particular, we can ensure
r>q_{\ast}^{\prime}=1
.

For
d>2
, Hölder’s inequality yields
\widetilde{\psi}_{\gamma}|{\sf u}|^{q-1}\in{{\rm L}^{r}(\Omega)}
provided that
\frac{1}{r}=\frac{1}{s_{2}}+\frac{q-1}{q_{\ast}}
.
Under assumption (A7), this exponent satisfies

\frac{1}{r}=\frac{1}{s_{2}}+\frac{q-1}{q_{\ast}}<\frac{q_{\ast}-1}{q_{\ast}}=\frac{1}{q_{*}^{\prime}},

and hence
r>q_{*}^{\prime}
.

We define the matrix function

(24)

{\bf{M}}:=\text{Sym}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nabla u_{i}\otimes\nabla p_{i}

where
Sym
denotes the symmetric part of the matrix. We also define the mapping:

(25)

F(\theta,{\bf{M}}):=\max_{{\bf{A}}\in\mathcal{K}(\theta)}{\bf{A}}:{\bf{M}}.

The constraint on the quantities of the original phases is incorporated in the standard way through the associated Lagrangian functional:

(26)

{\mathcal{L}}(\Omega,\theta,{\bf{A}})={\mathcal{J}}(\Omega,\theta,{\bf{A}})+l\int_{\Omega}\theta\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}\\

where
l
is the Lagrangian multiplier.
The following necessary conditions of optimality are satisfied (see
[
4
, Theorem 3.2.14]
):

Theorem 3.2
.

For an optimal
(\theta^{\ast},{\bf{A}}^{\ast})\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\Omega}
and the respective solutions
{\sf u}^{\ast}
and
{\sf p}^{\ast}
of (
2
) and (
23
) corresponding to the triplet
(\Omega,\theta^{\ast},{\bf{A}}^{\ast})
, define

q({\bm{x}}):=l+g_{\alpha}({\bm{x}},{\sf u}^{\ast}({\bm{x}}))-g_{\beta}({\bm{x}},{\sf u}^{\ast}({\bm{x}}))-\frac{\partial F}{\partial\theta}(\theta^{\ast}({\bm{x}}),{\bf{M}}^{\ast}({\bm{x}})),

with
{\bf{M}}^{\ast}
defined by (
24
) with
u_{i}^{*}
and
p_{i}^{*}
on the right-hand side.
The optimal density
\theta^{\ast}
satisfies the following conditions, almost everywhere on
\Omega

\begin{array}[]{cl}q({\bm{x}})>0&\implies\quad\theta^{\ast}({\bm{x}})=0,\\
q({\bm{x}})<0&\implies\quad\theta^{\ast}({\bm{x}})=1,\\
q({\bm{x}})=0&\implies\quad\theta^{\ast}({\bm{x}})\in[0,1].\\
\end{array}

Furthermore,
{\bf{A}}^{\ast}
is a maximizer in the definition of
F(\theta^{\ast},{\bf{M}}^{\ast})
.

The function
F
admits an explicit representation in both two- and three-dimensional cases
[
4
,
44
]
. For any symmetric matrix
{\bf{M}}
the mapping
\theta\mapsto\frac{\partial F}{\partial\theta}(\theta,{\bf{M}})
is a monotone and piecewise smooth. Moreover, for fixed matrix
{\bf{M}}
and a real number
c
, the equation
\frac{\partial F}{\partial\theta}(\theta,{\bf{M}})=c
with respect to
\theta

reduces to a quadratic equation and can therefore be solved explicitly.

The second component of the hybrid approach consists in modifying the boundary of the domain. We adopt an approach based on the shape derivative of the objective functional. This is formulated via a perturbation of the identity,
\MAP=\ID+\psi
, where
\psi\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
is sufficiently small in norm to ensure that
\MAP
is a homeomorphism. A shape functional
{\mathcal{L}}
, defined on a family of open subsets compactly contained in
D
and satisfying the
\varepsilon
-cone property, is said to be shape differentiable if the mapping

\psi\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\MAP(\Omega))

is Fréchet differentiable at zero. The Fréchet derivative at zero in the direction
\psi
is denoted by
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(\Omega;\psi)
and is referred to as the shape derivative of
\mathcal{L}
at
\Omega
in the direction
\psi
.

Given a triplet
(\Omega,\theta,{\bf{A}})\in\mathcal{A^{\prime}}
, we consider its perturbation

(\MAP(\Omega_{n}),\theta\circ\MAP^{-1},{\bf{A}}\circ\MAP^{-1})

and denote by
{\sf u}(\psi):=\big(u_{1}(\psi),\ldots,u_{m}(\psi)\big)\in\HH^{1}(\MAP(\Omega);\mathbb{R}^{m})
the associated perturbed state, that is, the vector of temperature
fields.
We additionally assume that
\psi=0
on
\Gamma_{0}
in order to keep this portion of the boundary fixed.
For
i=1,\ldots,m
, the function
u_{i}(\psi)
is defined as the solutions of the following
boundary value problem

(27)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-\ddiv({\bf{A}}\circ\MAP^{-1}\nabla u_{i}(\psi))=f_{i}&\text{ in }\MAP(\Omega)\\
u_{i}(\psi)=h_{i}&\text{ on }\MAP(\Gamma_{0})=\Gamma_{0}\\
u_{i}(\psi)=0&\text{ on }\MAP(\Gamma_{1})\\
{\bf{A}}\circ\MAP^{-1}\nabla u_{i}(\psi)\cdot{\bm{n}}=0&\text{ on }\MAP(\Gamma_{2}).\end{array}\right.

In particular, for
\psi=0
the corresponding state
{\sf u}={\sf u}(0)
coincides with the solution of
(
2
), hence, we omit the explicit dependence on zero. For the results below, we assume

f_{i}\in\HH^{1}(D)
and
h_{i}
is the trace to
\Gamma_{0}
of an
\HH^{1}
function vanishing on
\Gamma_{1}
.
We further introduce the following shape functional:

(28)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(\MAP(\Omega))&={\mathcal{J}}(\MAP(\Omega_{n}),\theta\circ\MAP^{-1},{\bf{A}}\circ\MAP^{-1})+l\int_{\MAP(\Omega)}\theta\circ\MAP^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}\\
&=\int_{\MAP(\Omega)}\theta\circ\MAP^{-1}g_{\alpha}(\cdot,{\sf u}(\psi))+(1-\theta\circ\MAP^{-1})g_{\beta}(\cdot,{\sf u}(\psi))\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}\\
&\phantom{=}+l\int_{\MAP(\Omega)}\theta\circ\MAP^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}.\end{split}

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
(\Omega,\theta,{\bf{A}})\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
. For
\psi\in\WW^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\psi=0
on
\Gamma_{0}
, the mapping

\psi\mapsto{\sf u}(\psi)\circ\MAP:\WW^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\HH^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})

is Fréchet differentiable at zero. Its Fréchet derivative at zero in direction
\psi
is denoted by
\dot{\sf u}(\psi)=(\dot{u}_{1}(\psi),\ldots,\dot{u}_{m}(\psi))\in W^{m}
.
For each
i=1,\ldots,m
, the variational formulation satisfied by
\dot{u}_{i}(\psi)
is given by

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{\bf{A}}\nabla\dot{u}_{i}(\psi)\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}({\bf{A}}\nabla\psi^{\tau}+\nabla\psi{\bf{A}}-\ddiv(\psi){\bf{A}})\nabla u_{i}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\int_{\Omega}\varphi\nabla f_{i}\cdot\psi+f_{i}\varphi\ddiv\psi\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}

for any
\varphi\in V
, where
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})
denotes the solution of (
2
).

Proof.

The result follows from Theorem 3.1 in
[
28
]
. The fact that
{\bf{A}}
is no longer piecewise constant scalar matrix does not alter the overall argument, except that
{\bf{A}}
and
\nabla\psi
no longer commute, which results in a modified expression. Furthermore, due to the prescribed boundary conditions, the implicit function theorem is applied in the space

W\subset H^{1}(\Omega)
rather than in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.
∎

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\psi\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
be such that
\psi=0
on
\Gamma_{0}
. Assume that
g_{\alpha}
and
g_{\beta}
satisfy
(A5)
and
(A7)
. Then the shape functional defined in (
28
) is shape differentiable. Moreover, for a given triplet
(\Omega,\theta,{\bf{A}})\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, its shape derivative is given by

(29)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(\Omega;\psi)&=\int_{\Omega}({\bf{A}}\nabla\psi^{\tau}+\nabla\psi{\bf{A}}-\ddiv(\psi){\bf{A}}):{\bf{M}}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}\\
&\phantom{=}+\int_{\Omega}\theta\nabla_{\bm{x}}g_{\alpha}(\cdot,{\sf u})\cdot\psi+(1-\theta)\nabla_{\bm{x}}g_{\beta}(\cdot,{\sf u})\cdot\psi\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}\\
&\phantom{=}+\int_{\Omega}[\theta g_{\alpha}(\cdot,{\sf u})+(1-\theta)g_{\beta}(\cdot,{\sf u})+l\theta]\ddiv\psi\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}\\
&\phantom{=}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\Omega}p_{i}\nabla f_{i}\cdot\psi+f_{i}p_{i}\ddiv\psi\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}},\end{split}

where
{\sf u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})
is the solution of (
2
),
{\bf{M}}
is given by (
24
), and
{\sf p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{m})\in W^{m}
is the solution of (
23
).

Proof.

The shape functional defined in (
28
) should be transformed to the original domain
\Omega
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\MAP(\Omega))

\displaystyle=\int_{\MAP(\Omega)}\theta\circ\MAP^{-1}g_{\alpha}(\cdot,{\sf u}(\psi))+(1-\theta\circ\MAP^{-1})g_{\beta}(\cdot,{\sf u}(\psi))\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+l\int_{\MAP(\Omega)}\theta\circ\MAP^{-1}({\bm{x}})\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}.

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\theta({\bm{x}})g_{\alpha}(\MAP({\bm{x}}),{\sf u}(\psi)\circ\MAP({\bm{x}}))\det\nabla\MAP({\bm{x}})\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\int_{\Omega}(1-\theta({\bm{x}}))g_{\beta}(\MAP({\bm{x}}),{\sf u}(\psi)\circ\MAP({\bm{x}}))\det\nabla\MAP({\bm{x}})\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+l\int_{\Omega}\theta({\bm{x}})\det\nabla\MAP({\bm{x}})\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}\,.

Owing to assumption (A7), we have
\nabla_{\bm{x}}g_{\gamma}(\cdot,{\sf u})\in{{\rm L}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}
(by the same arguments as in Step IV of the proof of Theorem
2.3
).
Moreover, for some
r>q_{*}^{\prime}
(see Remark
3.1
), we have
\nabla_{\sf u}g_{i}(\cdot,{\sf u})\in{{\rm L}^{r}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})}
. Since

\dot{\sf u}(\psi)\in{\rm H}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
, it follows that the mapping
\psi\mapsto g_{i}(\cdot,{\sf u}(\psi))\circ\MAP
is Fréchet differentiable at zero for
\gamma=\alpha,\beta
. More precisely,

\displaystyle g_{i}(\cdot,{\sf u}(\psi))\circ\MAP-g_{i}(\cdot,{\sf u})=\nabla_{\bm{x}}g_{i}(\cdot,{\sf u})\cdot\psi+\nabla_{\sf u}g_{i}(\cdot,{\sf u})\cdot\dot{\sf u}(\psi)+o(\psi)\quad\text{ in }{\rm L}^{1}(\Omega)\,.

From this it follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\MAP(\Omega))-\mathcal{L}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(\Omega;\psi)+o(\psi)\,,

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(\Omega;\psi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left[\theta\nabla_{\sf u}g_{\alpha}(\cdot,{\sf u})+(1-\theta)\nabla_{\sf u}g_{\beta}(\cdot,{\sf u})\right]\cdot\dot{\sf u}(\psi)\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\int_{\Omega}\left[\theta\nabla_{\bm{x}}g_{\alpha}(\cdot,{\sf u})+(1-\theta)\nabla_{\bm{x}}g_{\beta}(\cdot,{\sf u})\right]\cdot\psi\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\int_{\Omega}\bigl(\theta g_{\alpha}(\cdot,{\sf u})+(1-\theta)g_{\beta}(\cdot,{\sf u})+l\theta\bigr)\ddiv\psi\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}+o(\psi)\,.

Note that the first term in
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(\Omega;\psi)
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\Omega}\left[\theta\frac{\partial g_{\alpha}}{\partial u_{i}}(\cdot,{\sf u})+(1-\theta)\frac{\partial g_{\beta}}{\partial u_{i}}(\cdot,{\sf u})\right]\dot{u}_{i}(\psi)\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\Omega}{\bf{A}}\nabla p_{i}\cdot\nabla\dot{u}_{i}(\psi)\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\Omega}{\bf{A}}\nabla\dot{u}_{i}(\psi)\cdot\nabla p_{i}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\Omega}({\bf{A}}\nabla\psi^{\tau}+\nabla\psi{\bf{A}}-\ddiv\psi{\bf{A}})\nabla u_{i}\cdot\nabla p_{i}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt\phantom{=}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\Omega}p_{i}\nabla f_{i}\cdot\psi+f_{i}p_{i}\ddiv\psi\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt=\int_{\Omega}({\bf{A}}\nabla\psi^{\tau}+\nabla\psi{\bf{A}}-\ddiv\psi{\bf{A}}):{\bf{M}}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt\phantom{=}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\Omega}p_{i}\nabla f_{i}\cdot\psi+f_{i}p_{i}\ddiv\psi\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}},

thus completing the proof.
∎

The algorithm can be formulated by combining the optimality criteria method for the interior design with the shape derivative of the functional with respect to the overall domain
\Omega
. The set
\Omega
is represented by a level set function, which enables a straightforward control of the domain volume through suitable modifications of the level set constant.

Algorithm 3.5
.

Take initial domain
\Omega^{0}
. Repeat for
k=0,\ldots,k_{S}:

•

Initalize
\theta^{k,0}=1,{\bf{A}}^{k,0}=\alpha\I
. Repeat for
i=1,\ldots,k_{H}
:

1.

Calculate solution
{\sf u}^{k,i}
of (
2
) and
{\sf p}^{k,i}
of (
23
) for the triplet
(\Omega^{k},\theta^{k,i-1},{\bf{A}}^{k,i-1})\in{\mathcal{A}}

2.

Define
{\bf{M}}^{k,i}=\text{Sym}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\nabla u^{k,i}_{j}\otimes\nabla p^{k,i}_{j}

3.

For each
{\bm{x}}\in\Omega^{k}
let
\theta^{k,i}({\bm{x}})
be the zero of map

\theta\mapsto l+g_{\alpha}({\bm{x}},{\sf u}^{k,i}({\bm{x}}))-g_{\beta}({\bm{x}},{\sf u}^{k,i}({\bm{x}}))-\frac{\partial F}{\partial\theta}(\theta,{\bf{M}}^{k,i}({\bm{x}})),

in segment
[0,1]
.
If such a zero does not exist, set

\theta^{k,i}({\bm{x}})=0
if the function is positive or
\theta^{k,i}({\bm{x}})=1
if it is negative. The Lagrange multiplier
l
is updated within the above mapping in order to enforce the volume constraint

\int_{\Omega^{k}}\theta^{k,i}({\bm{x}})\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}=q_{\alpha}.

4.

For
{\bm{x}}\in\Omega^{k}
we define
{\bf{A}}^{k,i}({\bm{x}})\in\mathcal{K}(\theta^{k,i}({\bm{x}}))
as a maximizer in (
25
).

•

Set
\theta^{k}=\theta^{k,k_{H}},{\bf{A}}^{k}={\bf{A}}^{k,k_{H}}.
Calculate solution
{\sf u}^{k}
of (
2
) and
{\sf p}^{k}
of (
23
) for the triplet
(\Omega^{k},\theta^{k},{\bf{A}}^{k})\in{\mathcal{A^{\prime}}}
and
{\bf{M}}^{k}=\text{Sym}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\nabla u^{k}_{j}\otimes\nabla p^{k}_{j}
.

•

Calculate the direction
\psi^{k}
as the solution of the boundary value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta\psi^{k}+\psi^{k}=0&\text{ in }D\\
\psi^{k}=0&\text{ on }\partial D.\end{array}\right.

•

Update
\Omega^{k+1}=(\ID+\psi)(\Omega^{k}).

Numerical results

Let
D=[-2.6,2.6]^{2}\setminus B((0,0);1)
represent a square domain excluding a unit circle centered at the origin. The isotropic conductivities are
\alpha=1
and
\beta=2
. The initial design
(\Omega_{0},\chi_{0})
consists of
\Omega_{0}
, a shifted square defined as
\Omega_{0}=[-1.752,1.792]^{2}\setminus B((0,0);1)
, which represents a square region excluding the unit circle at its center, and
\chi_{0}=\alpha{\bf{I}}
. The boundary
\Gamma_{0}=\partial B((0,0);1)
is fixed, while the outer boundary
\Gamma_{1}
is allowed to change. Homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions are assumed on both boundaries. The measure of the domain is
|\Omega|=3\pi
and the amount of the first phase is given by
q_{\alpha}=\int_{\Omega}\chi\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}=\frac{3\pi}{2}.

We consider two multiple state equations (
2
) where right-hand sides are radial functions

\displaystyle f_{1}({r})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1,&{r}<2\\
1-{r},&{r}\geq 2\end{array}\right.,\quad f_{2}({r})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{8(2-{r})^{2}}{{r}},&{r}<2\\
0,&{r}\geq 2\end{array}\right.

and objective functional

{\mathcal{J}}(\Omega,\chi)=-\int_{\Omega}f_{1}({\bm{x}})u_{1}({\bm{x}})+f_{2}({\bm{x}})u_{2}({\bm{x}})\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}.

It is known that for fixed annulus
\Omega=B((0,0);2)\setminus\overline{B((0,0);1)}
, the optimal design is radial and classical (see
[
27
]
). Specifically, the less conductive material is placed along the inner and outer boundaries, while the more conductive material occupies the intermediate region. The interfaces between the materials are circular, with radii

1.1928
and
1.7096
.

The results of the hybrid optimization algorithm are shown in Figure
2
.
The numerical simulations were performed on a mesh of approximately 3,500 triangles, with the final result obtained after refinement on a mesh of 53,000 triangles. In all simulations, the measure
|\Omega_{k}|
was kept constant using the
mmg2d_O3
library
[
31
]
and the
mshdist
algorithm
[
17
]
.

We observe that the sequence of domains
\Omega_{k}
converges to the annulus
\Omega
described above, with
the final distribution of the two materials matching the theoretical predictions: the vertical red lines are located at
x
-coordinates
1.1928
and
1.7096
.
The hybrid method was implemented using a single iteration of the homogenization process (
k_{H}=1
in Algorithm
3.5
), and it is generally recommended to keep this number low (fewer than five) to accelerate computations. Only in the final iteration (
k=k_{S}=400
in Algorithm
3.5
), once the optimal
\Omega
has been identified, it is refined using a larger number of homogenization iterations (30 in our case). The corresponding values of the objective functional are presented in Figure
3
