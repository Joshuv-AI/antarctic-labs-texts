Odd-Parity Magnetism in Fe-Based Superconductors
Reuel Dsouza, Andreas Kreisel, Brian M. Andersen, Daniel F. Agterberg, Morten H. Christensen

Abstract
Odd-parity magnetism constitutes an intriguing phase of matter which breaks inversion symmetry while preserving time-reversal symmetry. Here we demonstrate that the Fe-based superconductors exhibiting coplanar magnetic order realize an odd-parity magnetic state by combining low-energy modeling with density-functional theory. In the absence of spin-orbit coupling, the electronic spins are polarized along the k z k_{z} -direction and the splitting of the up and down states exhibits an h h -wave form-factor. The magnitude of the splitting depends sensitively on specific parameters of the low-energy model, including specific out-of-plane hopping parameters and the Fermi energies of the hole- and electron-pockets. Interestingly, despite this state breaking inversion symmetry and exhibiting a finite out-of-plane Berry curvature and non-linear anomalous Hall effect, the Edelstein effect vanishes. Incorporating spin-orbit coupling tilts the momentum-space electronic spins into the ( k x , k y k_{x},k_{y} )-plane and imparts finite in-plane components to the Edelstein response. Our findings highlight the Fe-based superconductors as platforms for exploring odd-parity magnetism both on its own and coexisting with unconventional superconductivity.

At this stage we note that the out-of-plane collinear phase also breaks inversion symmetry. . We have demonstrated that Fe-based superconductors are platforms for odd-parity magnetism, using both a generic symmetry-constrained low-energy model and material-specific DFT calculations. Within the low-energy model, we obtained an analytical expression for the odd-parity spin-splitting, Eq. (

SI.2
Crossover of spin-splitting from quadratic to linear

From DFT calculations and diagonalizing the complete
\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}
model, we observe that the splitting scales linearly with
|\Delta|
for large values of
\Delta
, while for small values, it follows a quadratic dependence
|\Delta|^{2}
. This trend can be understood within a simple two-band model, where two energy levels
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
are coupled via an amplitude
t
. The exact expression for the splitting in this model is

\delta\epsilon=\sqrt{(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2})^{2}+4|t|^{2}}
,
which, when expanded, yields

\delta\epsilon\sim(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2})+\frac{2|t|^{2}}{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}}
.
Comparing this with the structure of the splitting in (
S21
), we propose

\Delta_{E}\sim\left(\sqrt{(\varepsilon_{\Gamma}-\varepsilon_{1})^{2}+4|\Delta|^{2}}-|\varepsilon_{\Gamma}-\varepsilon_{1}|\right)\,\cdot\,\frac{cg_{1}a_{1}}{|\varepsilon_{\Gamma}-\varepsilon_{1}|}\,\cdot\,f(k)\,\cdot\,(k_{x}^{2}-k_{y}^{2})\,k_{x}k_{y}\,\sin k_{z}\,.

(S23)

For small
\Delta

S21

|\Delta|^{2}
, whereas for
\Delta\gg\frac{|\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{\Gamma}|}{2}
, the splitting becomes linear in
|\Delta|
. Thus, the crossover from parabolic to linear behavior occurs at an energy scale approximately given by
\sim\frac{|\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{\Gamma}|}{2}
.

The Edelstein susceptibility is the transport coefficient relating an applied electric field to a spin accumulation. It can be obtained from the Boltzmann equation in the relaxation-time approximation by expanding to first order in the electric field yielding the Fermi surface integral

\chi^{E}_{ij}=\chi_{0}\sum_{\nu}\int_{FS_{\nu}}d^{2}kS_{i}(\mathbf{k})v_{j}(\mathbf{k})\,,

(S25)

where
\chi_{0}=\tau|e|\mu_{B}A/(2\pi)^{2}
with
A
the area of the unit cell
Yu et al. 2025

and
\tau
the collision time, which has to be inferred from other experimental probes.
S_{i}(\mathbf{k})
is the momentum-space spin-polarization along the
i^{\rm th}
direction and
v_{j}(\mathbf{k})
is the
j^{\rm th}
component of the Fermi velocity. The integral is evaluated by discretizing it into a sum in the following manner,

\chi_{ij}^{E}\sim\chi_{0}\sum_{\nu}\sum_{k_{z}=-\pi}^{\pi}\delta k_{z}\sum_{FS_{\nu}(k_{z})}\delta l_{\nu}\langle u_{\nu}(k)|S_{i}|u_{\nu}(k)\rangle\langle u_{\nu}(k)|\partial_{k_{j}}H|u_{\nu}(k)\rangle

(S26)

where
\delta l_{\nu}=\left(\sqrt{\delta k_{x}^{2}+\delta k_{x}^{2}}\right)_{\nu}
.

The components
\chi^{E}_{zi}
and
\chi^{E}_{jz}
are zero due to cancellations caused by the in-plane mirror symmetries
\mathcal{M}_{100}
and and
\mathcal{M}_{010}
. These cancellations occur both without and with weak SOC and in both the coplanar and the out-of-plane collinear phases. In the absence of SOC, the in-plane components of the Edelstein tensor vanish identically because
S_{x,y}(\mathbf{k})=0
. When SOC is present, these components become nonzero. However, the magnitude of the effect is controlled primarily by the SOC and not by the odd-parity magnetic order parameter, as shown in Fig.
S4
.

In Fig.
S4
we plot the
\chi_{x,x+y}^{E}
and
\chi_{y,y-x}^{E}
components of the Edelstein susceptibility tensor for both the coplanar phase and the out-of-plane collinear phase. All other components vanish identically, due to orthogonality of the spin-projection and group velocity form factors [
v_{i}(\mathbf{k})
transforms as
p_{i}
]. We find that, in the coplanar phase these components are equal, whereas in the collinear phase the two components differ by a sign. This difference can be traced to the composite order parameters
\langle\mathbf{M}_{1}\times\mathbf{M}_{2}\rangle\cdot\hat{z}
and
\langle\mathbf{M}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{2}\rangle

Christensen et al. 2019
;
Yu et al. 2025
, where
\mathbf{M}_{1,2}
denote the two three-component magnetic order parameters. In the coplanar phase,
\langle\mathbf{M}_{1}\times\mathbf{M}_{2}\rangle\cdot\hat{z}
is finite and transforms as
A_{2u}
, thus preserving the
C_{4}

SIV
Non-linear Hall effect

Figure S5:
Numerically calculated Berry curvature at
k_{z}=\pi/2
using the low-energy model for the two bands crossing the Fermi level.

In crystals that lack inversion symmetry but preserve time-reversal symmetry, the nonlinear Hall effect can arise
Sodemann and Fu 2015
. The current response to applied electric field(s) can be expanded as

\displaystyle j_{a}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{(1)}_{ab}\,E_{b}+\sigma^{(2)}_{abc}E_{b}E_{c}\,.

(S27)

The second-order conductivity
\sigma^{(2)}_{abc}
depends on the Berry curvature weighted by derivatives of the Fermi distribution function. This motivates the definition of a quantity known as the Berry curvature dipole
Sodemann and Fu 2015
;
Du et al. 2021
:

\displaystyle D_{ab}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\int_{\text{BZ}}\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\partial_{k_{a}}f_{0}(\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k}))\Omega^{b}_{n}(\mathbf{k})\,,

(S28)

such that

\displaystyle\sigma^{(2)}_{abc}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{3}\tau}{2(1+i\omega\tau)}\Big(\epsilon_{adc}D_{bd}+\epsilon_{adb}D_{cd}\Big)\,.

(S29)

On integrating (
S28
) by parts, the derivative can instead be applied on the Berry curvature, giving

D_{ab}=-\sum_{n}\int_{\text{BZ}}\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}f_{0}(\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k}))\partial_{k_{a}}\Omega^{b}_{n}(\mathbf{k}).

(S30)

The derivative operator
\partial_{k_{a}}
in momentum space corresponds to the representation of the position operator
\hat{r}_{a}
in real space. This implies that the integral defined above is a measure of the first real-space moment of the Berry curvature, hence the name Berry curvature dipole. Here,
\Omega^{b}_{n}(\mathbf{k})
is the Berry curvature pseudovector

\Omega_{c,n}=\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{abc}\Omega_{n}^{ab}\,,

(S31)

with the Berry curvature for band
n
given by

\Omega^{ab}_{n}(\mathbf{k})=-2\,\mathrm{Im}\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\langle u_{n}|\partial_{k_{a}}H|u_{m}\rangle\langle u_{m}|\partial_{k_{b}}H|u_{n}\rangle}{(\varepsilon_{n}-\varepsilon_{m})^{2}}\,.

(S32)

For low temperatures, Eq. (
S28
) reduces to a Fermi surface integral given by,

\displaystyle D_{ab}=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\int_{\text{BZ}}\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\partial_{k_{a}}f_{0}(\varepsilon_{n}(\mathbf{k}))\Omega^{b}_{n}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle\quad\xrightarrow{T\to 0}-

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\int_{\text{FS}_{n}}\frac{dS}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{v_{n,a}(\mathbf{k})}{|\mathbf{v}_{n}(\mathbf{k})|}\Omega^{b}_{n}(\mathbf{k}).

(S33)

The
z
-component of the Berry curvature pseudovector transforms as
h
-wave in the coplanar phase and
f
-wave in the collinear phase, which implies that its Fermi surface integral vanishes in systems with in-plane mirror symmetry. Consequently, the out-of-plane components of both the Edelstein tensor and the Berry curvature dipole vanish.

This is confirmed by the explicit calculation of the components of the Berry curvature pseudovector shown in Fig.
S5
for the two bands crossing the Fermi level. As expected,
\Omega_{z}
exhibits an
h
-wave structure, while
\Omega_{y}
and
\Omega_{x}
transform as
p_{x}
and
p_{y}
, respectively. As the group velocity component
v_{i}(\mathbf{k})
transforms as
p_{i}
, this implies that the only non-zero components of the Berry curvature dipole are
D_{xy}
and
D_{yx}
.

Keeping in mind that the non-linear conductivity tensor, Eq. (
S29
) is symmetric in its last two indices, we find that the finite components of the non-linear conductivity tensor are
\sigma^{(2)}_{zxx}
,
\sigma^{(2)}_{zyy}
and
\sigma^{(2)}_{xzx}
,
\sigma^{(2)}_{yzy}
with the former two being twice as large in magnitude as the latter two.
