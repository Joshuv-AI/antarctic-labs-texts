Efficient Analysis of the Distilled Neural Tangent Kernel
Jamie Mahowald, Brian Bell, Alex Ho, Michael Geyer

Abstract
Neural tangent kernel (NTK) methods are computationally limited by the need to evaluate large Jacobians across many data points. Existing approaches reduce this cost primarily through projecting and sketching the Jacobian. We show that NTK computation can also be reduced by compressing the data dimension itself using NTK-tuned dataset distillation. We demonstrate that the neural tangent space spanned by the input data can be induced by dataset distillation, yielding a 20-100 × \times reduction in required Jacobian calculations. We further show that per-class NTK matrices have low effective rank that is preserved by this reduction. Building on these insights, we propose the distilled neural tangent kernel ( DNTK ), which combines NTK-tuned dataset distillation with state-of-the-art projection methods to reduce up NTK computational complexity by up to five orders of magnitude while preserving kernel structure and predictive performance.



1
Introduction

The neural tangent kernel (NTK)
(
12
)
gives a theoretical lens for understanding neural network (NN) training, particularly in the overparameterized regime. As the width of a given NN approaches infinite, the network’s training dynamics become linear and equivalent to kernel regression. In this “lazy training” regime, the network adjusts a linear combination of fixed features, and does not learn to represent features from scratch. Under certain circumstances, a model can be approximated within the kernel regime defined by the NTK, which enables analysis that would be intractable in the parametric regime.

Unfortunately, the NTK is intractable to compute for all but the smallest networks: for a parameterized network
f
with
P
fixed total parameters trained on
n
data points, the cost of computing the NTK scales as
O(n^{2}P)
, while storing scales as
O(n^{2})
. To naïvely compute the NTK of a ResNet50 on all 1.3 million ImageNet points requires at least
4.2\cdot 10^{19}
floating point operations and
1.69\cdot 10^{12}
memory entries. Projects that leverage NTK-like formulations to detect distributional shifts
(
10
)
, quantify uncertainty
(
21
)
, and characterize robustness
(
18
)
have therefore been limited to small models. To enable these analyses for larger models, we need a robust NTK approximation regime.

Prior attempts to approximate the NTK for downstream tasks tend to either address parameter complexity alone
(
9
)
or approximate it using different, cheaper kernels
(
15
)
. Our method creates tractable, accurate approximations of the NTK itself by noting that the empirical NTK of a pretrained neural network exhibits significant redundancy at
three
levels: in the dataset, in the parameters, and in the gradient subspace. To overcome the intractability of computing full NTKs, we combine three complementary strategies that target redundancy at each of these levels: (1) data distillation, which synthesizes compact datasets that preserve task performance; (2) random projection, which reduces the dimensionality of the tangent space while preserving kernel structure; and (3) structure-aware gradient distillation, designed to further compress the NTK by exploiting its local and global spectral structure.

Central to our approach is that these components – dataset distillation, random projection, and gradient distillation – are theoretically justified methods that target distinct sources of redundancy. To that end, we provide proofs in
Section

3
and in
Appendices

B
and
C
that explain how and when these methods preserve variances and subspaces.
We refer to NTK approximations constructed via this unified framework as
distilled neural tangent kernels
(
DNTKs
). Combining these techniques, we achieve up to a
\approx 10^{5}\times
reduction in both computation time and storage in our experiments on a mid-size image classification task while maintaining downstream task performance with theoretical guarantees on approximation quality.

The paper is organized as follows:
Section

2
situates our work within NTK theory and dataset distillation.
Section

3
formalizes the empirical NTK and notions of redundancy that motivate our approach.
Section

4
describes our compression pipeline: dataset distillation, random projection, and gradient distillation.
Section

5
presents experimental validation.

Related work

This work draws on two lines of research: (i) kernel perspectives on deep networks, especially the
neural tangent kernel (NTK)
, and (ii)
dataset distillation
/ coreset methods for summarizing data.

Kernel methods and deep kernel learning.

A long line of work connects neural networks to kernel machines. In particular, deep kernel learning combines hierarchical representations with the nonparametric flexibility of kernels
(
20
;
11
)
, and admits representer-theorem-style characterizations for composed RKHS models
(
3
)
.

Neural tangent kernel (NTK).

12
first proposed the NTK in 2018 to explain training dynamics in the infinite-width limit. Later work on making NTK computation tractable has focused on sketching and random-feature approximations to obtain fast (near input-sparsity) approximations of NTK matrices. For example,
22
sketch polynomial expansions of arc-cosine kernels and combine sketching with random features to obtain spectral approximations, while
8
generalize these ideas beyond ReLU via truncated Hermite expansions for broad activation classes.

9
develop a random-sketching-based approach with a physics-informed loss function.
These methods demonstrate the effectiveness of random projection for reducing parameter complexity.

In contrast, our work targets redundancy on the
data
side (via distillation) as a complement to sketching-based accelerations of kernel construction.
To diagnose when such kernel approximations are effective, recent work studies NTK spectra:
13
uses empirical-NTK eigenanalysis to surface learned features, while
2
characterizes limiting NTK eigenvalue distributions under high-dimensional scaling.

Dataset distillation.

First proposed by
19
, dataset distillation has inspired numerous offshoots that aim both to streamline the core algorithm and to apply it to various use cases. Several kernel-based distillation methods cast distillation in a kernel ridge-regression objective inspired by infinite-width neural kernels
(
16
)
, and accelerate it via random-feature approximations
(
15
)
. Whereas these works use neural kernels to formulate and accelerate distillation, we use distillation to accelerate downstream NTK computations.
We employ a modified version of WMDD
(
14
)
, which distills data by Wasserstein-metric feature matching (via a Wasserstein barycenter) in a pretrained feature space.

Coresets and sampling.

Our local/global selection procedure (
Algorithm

1
) is related to coreset frameworks for clustering and shape fitting
(
7
)
. Our kernel sketching strategy also connects to random-feature approximations for scaling kernel machines
(
17
)
.

3
Setup

Let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
denote the
P
-dimensional parameters of a neural network

f(\mathbf{x};\theta):\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{C}
, mapping inputs to class logits.

3.1
NTK and KRR computation

The
neural tangent kernel
(NTK) measures gradient alignment between inputs. Letting
\phi(\mathbf{x})=\nabla_{\theta}f(\mathbf{x};\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times P}
,

K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\Tr(\phi(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\phi(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))=\phi\phi^{\top}.

(1)

For classification, we work with per-class kernels
K^{c}
formed from gradients

\phi^{c}(\mathbf{x}):=\nabla_{\theta}f^{c}(\mathbf{x};\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
of each logit (in this setting,
K^{c}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\phi^{c}(\mathbf{x})\phi^{c}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
is a scalar). Given a training set
X
of
n
training points,
the
class gradient matrix

\Phi^{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times P}
has rows
[\Phi^{c}]_{i}=\phi^{c}(x_{i})
,
yielding the
class kernel

K^{c}=\Phi^{c}(\Phi^{c})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

Under certain conditions (the network operates near the lazy training regime where dynamics are approximately linear in parameters,
K_{XX}
eigenvalues decay rapidly, and training labels align with dominant eigendirections, see
Appendix

B
), we can approximate
f^{c}
by a kernel representer
f_{K}^{c}
obtained via kernel ridge regression (KRR,
Section

A.3
):
f_{K}^{c}
is fit on
X
and evaluated on a test set
X^{*}
of size
n_{\mathrm{test}}
. The memory required to materialize
K^{c}
and
K^{c}_{XX^{*}}
is
O(nP+n^{2}+nn_{\rm test})
, which is prohibitive at scale for all
C
classes.
Alternatively, if we store only gradients and compute kernel entries on the fly, memory is
O((n+n_{\rm test})PC)
, but computation remains dominated by
P
-dimensional inner products.

To address these complexity concerns, we introduce and exploit notions of redundancy in several spaces.

3.2
Redundancy in data and parameters

By
redundancy
, we mean that most variation in model training dynamics and predictions can be
explained in a significantly smaller subspace than the model uses in practice.
*
*

*

Many of the notions
surrounding this idea are based on the
manifold hypothesis
; see
6
.

Consider a fixed parameter vector
\theta
of dimension
P
, a labeled dataset
\mathcal{D}=(X,Y)
, and class
c
. Let

K_{XX}^{c}
be the NTK class kernel matrix at
\theta
between
X
and itself (that is, the matrix
K^{c}
where
K_{ij}^{c}=K^{c}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})
for
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and
\mathbf{x}_{j}\in X
). We focus on two kinds of
redundancy, defined via Gram matrices on interrelated spaces.

Definition 3.1

(Data redundancy)
.

Let
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}
denote the eigenvalues of
K_{XX}^{c}
in decreasing order. The

truncation rank
of
K_{XX}^{c}
at threshold
\varepsilon
is

r_{\mathrm{trunc}}(K_{XX}^{c},\varepsilon):=\min\left\{k:\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}}\geq 1-\varepsilon\right\}.

The input set
X
is
(r,\varepsilon)
-data-redundant
if

r_{\mathrm{trunc}}(K_{XX}^{c},\varepsilon)\leq n/r
.

In other words, an
(r,\varepsilon)
-data-redundant input set admits a
(1-\varepsilon)
-variance kernel
approximation using an input set
r
times smaller.

Definition 3.2

(Parameter redundancy)
.

Fix a class
c
.
Given
\mathcal{D}=(X,Y)
, the parameters at
\theta
are
(r,\varepsilon)
-parameter-redundant
if there
exists a subspace
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{P}
with
\dim(V)=P/r
such that

\frac{\|\Pi_{V}(\Phi_{X}^{c})\Pi_{V}(\Phi_{X}^{c})^{\top}-K^{c}_{XX}\|_{F}}{\|K^{c}_{XX}\|_{F}}\leq\varepsilon,

where
\Pi_{V}(\Phi_{X}^{c})
projects each row of
\Phi_{X}^{c}
onto
V
by right-multiplication:
\Pi_{V}(\Phi_{X}^{c})=\Phi_{X}^{c}\Pi_{V}
.

In other words, for this dataset and class, a
(1-\varepsilon)
-fraction of predictive variation can be captured
using a parameter subspace that is
r
times smaller.

Definitions

3.1
and
3.2
characterize when an NTK can be approximated in low dimension due to redundancy in data or concentration in a lower-dimensional parameter space.
To
construct
such a structure in practice, we study how redundancy appears across data subsets and output Jacobians of the NTK.

3.3
Dataset distillation as gradient subspace selection

We now show that (in a standard one-step / lazy regime) dataset distillation (DD) can be viewed as
selecting a low-dimensional tangent subspace in parameter space
spanned by logit gradients
\nabla_{\theta}f(\tilde{\mathbf{x}};\theta)
at the distilled inputs.
In this view, distilled inputs
\tilde{X}
act as
inducing points
for the NTK: they determine a parameter-space projector, and the loss incurs an update that is a task-dependent linear combination of those tangent features.

3.3.1
Bilevel distillation induces a tangent-feature subspace.

DD aims to synthesize a compact dataset
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}=(\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})
of size
m\ll n
such that training on
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
matches training on
\mathcal{D}=(X,Y)
. A common bilevel formalization is

\tilde{\mathcal{D}}^{*}\in\argmin_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}\;\mathcal{L}_{d}\!\left[f\!\left(X;\,\argmin_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{p}[f(\tilde{X};\theta),\tilde{Y}]\right),Y\right],

(2)

where the constraints of the optimization problem are determined by the soft or hard biases of the network, and
\mathcal{L}_{p}
and
\mathcal{L}_{d}
are designed to optimize the parameters and datasets, respectively.

We work in a frozen-feature (“lazy”) regime around a fixed reference parameter vector
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
, and interpret DD geometrically through the span of logit gradients at
\theta
.

For an input collection
X=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, we define for each logit gradient
\phi^{c}
the gradient matrices
\Phi_{X}^{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times P}
by
[\Phi_{X}^{c}]_{i}=\phi^{c}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
. We also define the
stacked
logit-gradient matrix

\Phi_{X}:=\begin{bmatrix}\Phi_{X}^{1}\\
\vdots\\
\Phi_{X}^{C}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{\mathrm{tot}}\times P},\qquad m_{\mathrm{tot}}:=mC.

For the distilled inputs
\tilde{X}
we write
\tilde{\Phi}:=\Phi_{\tilde{X}}
and define the associated tangent subspace

V(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})\equiv V(\tilde{X}):=\mathrm{colspan}(\tilde{\Phi}^{\top})\subset\mathbb{R}^{P},

(3)

where
\Pi_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}
is the orthogonal projector onto
V(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})
.
Importantly,
\tilde{X}
determines the subspace
V(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})
, while
\tilde{Y}
determines how gradients combine within it.

Chain-rule identity.

Here, we show that loss-gradients live in the span of logit-gradients.
Let the distilled inner objective be
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell[f(\mathbf{\tilde{x}}_{i};\theta),\mathbf{\tilde{y}}_{i}]
for some per-example loss
\ell[\cdot,\cdot]
.
Define the
logit sensitivities

\delta_{i}(\theta):=\nabla_{f(\mathbf{\tilde{x}}_{i})}\ell[f(\mathbf{\tilde{x}}_{i};\theta),\mathbf{\tilde{y}}_{i}]\in\mathbb{R}^{C},

and stack them into

\tilde{\delta}(\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{\mathrm{tot}}}
. By the chain rule,

g_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\theta):=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta)\;=\;\tilde{\Phi}^{\top}\,\tilde{\delta}(\theta)\;\in\;V(\tilde{\mathcal{D}}).

(4)

Thus, although the DD objective is written in terms of loss gradients, those loss gradients are always linear combinations of the
logit
gradients
\nabla_{\theta}f^{c}(\mathbf{\tilde{x}}_{i};\theta)
that define our kernel features.

3.3.2
One-step view: outer progress is controlled by a projection residual.

The training method we describe in
Section

4
fixes network parameters at a reference
\theta
at
the conclusion of training, approximating a converged state. Using the distilled set, we define a

frozen
tangent-feature subspace
V(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})=\mathrm{colspan}(\tilde{\Phi}^{\top})\subset\mathbb{R}^{P}
(via
\tilde{X}
) and a
coefficient vector
\tilde{\delta}(\theta)
(via
\tilde{Y}
), producing the inner update

g_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\theta)=\tilde{\Phi}^{\top}\tilde{\delta}(\theta)\in V(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})
as in (
4
). Thus we do not
model a full training trajectory; instead, we hold the evaluation point
\theta
fixed and ask whether the
bilevel objective succeeds at selecting
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
whose induced one-step update improves typical outer
objectives
t\sim\mathcal{T}
at this same
\theta
.

This analysis is inherently local about the converged point, so we compare the
realized
one-step
update
\theta^{+}(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})=\theta-\eta g_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\theta)
to the
best
update available within the same
subspace
V(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})
under the standard smoothness (quadratic upper-model) approximation. We formulate this
as
regret
: the price of using the update produced by
(\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})
relative to the best
subspace-restricted step, whose achievable decrease is controlled by the projection residual

\|(I-\Pi_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}})g_{t}\|^{2}
.

Theorem 3.3

(One-step smoothness regret bound)
.

Assume
t\sim\mathcal{T}
,
g_{t}:=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta)
, each
\mathcal{L}_{t}
is
L
-smooth,
and the realized update is
\theta^{+}(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})=\theta-\eta g_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\theta)
with
g_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\theta)\in V(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})
.
Fix
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
and take expectation over
t\sim\mathcal{T}
. Define the one-step smoothness upper model

M_{t}(\Delta\theta)\;:=\;\langle g_{t},\Delta\theta\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|\Delta\theta\|^{2},

and let
\Delta\theta_{t}^{\star}:=\argmin_{\Delta\theta\in V(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})}M_{t}(\Delta\theta)
denote the best
subspace-restricted step in this model. Then the realized update

\Delta\theta_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}:=-\eta\,g_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\theta)\in V(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})
satisfies

\displaystyle\E_{t}\!\big[\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta+\Delta\theta_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}})-\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta+\Delta\theta_{t}^{\star})\big]

(5)

\displaystyle\leq\eta\,\E_{t}\!\Big[\Big\langle g_{t},\frac{\Delta\theta_{t}^{\star}}{\eta}\Big\rangle-\langle g_{t},g_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\theta)\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\Big(\|g_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\theta)\|^{2}-\Big\|\frac{\Delta\theta_{t}^{\star}}{\eta}\Big\|^{2}\Big),

where the first term isolates the penalty for failing to realize the best coefficients within
V(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})

(via
\tilde{Y}
), and the second term is the corresponding quadratic-model penalty.

Moreover, the minimizer of
M_{t}
over
V(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})
is

\Delta\theta_{t}^{\star}=-\frac{1}{L}\Pi_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}g_{t},

and
L
-smoothness implies the best attainable guaranteed decrease within
V(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta)-\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta+\Delta\theta_{t}^{\star})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2L}\|\Pi_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}g_{t}\|^{2}

(6)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2L}\Big(\|g_{t}\|^{2}-\|(I-\Pi_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}})g_{t}\|^{2}\Big).

In particular, when the coefficients produced by
\tilde{Y}
make
g_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\theta)
close to
\Pi_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}g_{t}

(e.g. under soft-label realizability), the one-step regret is small and maximizing expected one-step
progress reduces to minimizing the expected projection residual
\E_{t}[\|(I-\Pi_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}})g_{t}\|^{2}]
.

3.3.4
Inducing-point view and kernel fidelity.

The tangent subspace viewpoint also explains why distilled inputs behave like
inducing points
in kernel space.
Fix a scalar output (e.g. a logit
c
) and let
\Phi:=\Phi_{X}^{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times P}
and
\tilde{\Phi}^{c}:=\Phi_{\tilde{X}}^{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times P}
denote the corresponding gradient feature matrices at
\theta
.

Given any parameter-space projector
\Pi
(e.g.
\Pi=\Pi_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}
), define the projected-feature class kernel

K^{c,\Pi}_{XX}:=(\Phi\Pi)(\Phi\Pi)^{\top}=\Phi\Pi\Phi^{\top}.

(7)

If we choose
\Pi
as the projector onto the span of distilled class features,

\Pi_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}^{c}:=(\tilde{\Phi}^{c})^{\top}\big(\tilde{\Phi}^{c}(\tilde{\Phi}^{c})^{\top}\big)^{\dagger}\tilde{\Phi}^{c},

(8)

then (
7
) becomes the Nyström / inducing-point form

\begin{split}K^{c,\Pi_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}^{c}}_{XX}=K^{c}_{X\tilde{X}}\,\big(K^{c}_{\tilde{X}\tilde{X}}\big)^{\dagger}\,K^{c}_{\tilde{X}X},\\
K^{c}_{X\tilde{X}}:=\Phi(\tilde{\Phi}^{c})^{\top},\qquad\;K^{c}_{\tilde{X}\tilde{X}}:=\tilde{\Phi}^{c}(\tilde{\Phi}^{c})^{\top}.\end{split}

(9)

Thus, selecting
\tilde{X}
selects an inducing set in the tangent-feature kernel.

Finally, kernel fidelity is controlled by how well
\Pi
preserves gradient features:

\|K^{c}_{XX}-K^{c,\Pi}_{XX}\|_{F}=\|\Phi(I-\Pi)\Phi^{\top}\|_{F}\;\leq\;\|\Phi\|_{F}\,\|\Phi(I-\Pi)\|_{F}.

(10)

In particular, misalignment of
\Pi
with the dominant right-singular subspace of
\Phi
directly translates into kernel approximation error.

Proposition 3.6

(Energy-gap decomposition in gradient-feature space)
.

Let
\Phi=U\Sigma W^{\top}
and let
\Pi^{\star}:=W_{r}W_{r}^{\top}
be the rank-
r
PCA projector (top
r
right singular vectors).
Then for any rank-
r
projector
\Pi
(including realizable choices such as

\Pi=\Pi_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}^{c}
),

\|\Phi(I-\Pi)\|_{F}^{2}=\underbrace{\sum_{j>r}\sigma_{j}(\Phi)^{2}}_{\text{PCA tail}}+\underbrace{\Big(\mathrm{tr}(\Phi^{\top}\Phi\,\Pi^{\star})-\mathrm{tr}(\Phi^{\top}\Phi\,\Pi)\Big)}_{\text{captured-energy gap (misalignment)}}.

(11)

The second term is nonnegative and vanishes if and only if
\Pi
captures as much feature energy as the PCA subspace, providing a direct quantitative measure of subspace misalignment relevant for kernel fidelity via (
10
).

3.4
Spectral structure of the kernel

For class
c
, let
K^{c}=U^{c}\Sigma^{c}(U^{c})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be the class kernel with truncation rank
r_{g}
for some small
\varepsilon
(as in
Definition

3.1
).
Using kernel clustering (spectral clustering with the adjacency matrix given by
K^{c}
), partition the
n
samples into
H
clusters
\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{H}\}
with index sets
\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{H}
. For cluster
h_{i}
, define the
local class kernel
as the restriction of the (global) class kernel to local indices:
K^{c}_{i}=K^{c}|_{\mathcal{I}_{i}}=U^{c}_{i}\Sigma^{c}_{i}(U^{c}_{i})^{\top}
with truncation rank
r_{i}
.

Let
A^{{(r)}}
denote the first
r
columns of the matrix
A
. For now, we introduce the following properties and assume that class-structured data follows them:

(A)

Local variance is almost entirely contained within the global eigenspace. For each cluster
h_{i}
,

\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{r_{i}}[\Sigma_{i}]_{jj}\cdot\|\Pi_{i}^{\text{glob}}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{j})\|^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{r_{i}}[\Sigma_{i}]_{jj}}\approx 1,\quad\text{where}

(12)

•

[\Sigma_{i}]_{jj}
is the
j^{\text{th}}
eigenvalue local to cluster
h_{i}
,

•

\mathbf{u}_{i}^{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{I}_{i}|}
is the
j^{\text{th}}
eigenvector local to cluster
h_{i}
, and

•

\Pi_{i}^{\text{glob}}
is the orthogonal projection onto
\text{span}(U^{(r_{g})}|_{\mathcal{I}_{i}})
(after orthonormalization).

This property implies that
\text{span}(U_{i})\subseteq\text{span}(U|_{\mathcal{I}_{i}})
approximately for a given cluster
h_{i}
.

(B)

Local eigenspaces collectively do
not
span global eigenspace, whereby there exist some global principal directions that are poorly represented by the union of the spans of global clusters.
Formally, let
\hat{U}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r_{i}}
be the zero-padded lifting of
U_{i}^{(r_{i})}
, and let

\Pi^{\text{loc}}
denote the orthogonal projector onto
\text{span}([\hat{U_{1}},\ldots,\hat{U}_{H}]).

Then there exist
j\leq r_{g}
such that, for
\delta>0
,

\|\Pi^{\text{loc}}(\mathbf{u}^{j})\|^{2}\leq 1-\delta.

Equivalently,
\text{span}(U^{(r_{g})})\not\subseteq\text{span}([\hat{U_{1}},\ldots,\hat{U}_{H}])
, where
\hat{U}_{i}
are the lifted (zero-padded) local eigenvectors.

Qualitatively, properties
(A)
and
(B)
describe a hierarchical redundancy structure: within-cluster variance is concentrated (enabling local compression via data redundancy,
Definition

3.1
), but cross-cluster relationships span a complementary subspace (requiring global eigenmodes to maintain kernel fidelity). These assumptions are supported by empirical findings in
Figure

4
and form the theoretical basis for
Algorithm

1
, which explicitly constructs gradient representatives preserving both spectral regimes.

4
Method

The DNTK method addresses the complexity profile in
Section

3.1
through three successive reductions. Starting from the original training set
X
of size
n
, we first apply dataset distillation in input space to obtain a distilled set
(\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})
of size
m\ll n
. For each class
c
, we then form a projected gradient matrix
\tilde{\Phi}_{X}^{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
by applying a distance-preserving random projection
g:\mathbb{R}^{P}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
to the per-sample parameter gradients, thereby reducing the effective parameter dimension from
P
to
k
.
Finally, we perform a second round of distillation in gradient space, replacing
(\tilde{\Phi},\tilde{Y})
with a smaller synthetic set
(\hat{\Phi},\hat{Y})
of size
s\ll m
, which is used in the final kernel ridge regression solve.

4.1
Data distillation

We instantiate the distillation framework of
Section

3.3
using
Wasserstein Metric Dataset Distillation (WMDD)

(
14
)
.
WMDD solves a surrogate objective based on feature matching: it synthesizes data whose intermediate representations are distributionally close to the original data in both input and feature space.
Concretely, WMDD minimizes

\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X})=\mathcal{L}_{\text{feature}}+\lambda_{\text{BN}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{BN}},

where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{feature}}
matches synthetic features to Wasserstein barycenters of real features, and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BN}}
aligns batch normalization statistics across layers. The WMDD process generates a soft label for each distilled data point for a set
\tilde{Y}
of distilled labels.
This objective is aligned with our subspace view in
Section

3.3
: by matching intermediate representations (and BN statistics), WMDD tends to produce distilled points whose gradient span is better aligned with the dominant directions of the full gradient matrix, reducing the misalignment term in
Theorem

3.3
.
Implementation details appear in
Section

C.5
.

4.2
Random projection

The Johnson-Lindenstrauss (JL) lemma famously states that, for a desired error bound
\varepsilon_{\text{JL}}
and an integer
k>(8\ln n)/\varepsilon_{\text{JL}}^{2}
, there exists a linear map
g:\mathbb{R}^{P}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that

(1-\varepsilon_{\text{JL}})\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|^{2}\leq\|g(\mathbf{u})-g(\mathbf{v})\|^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon_{\text{JL}})\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|^{2}

for any
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}

(
5
)
.

This is commonly achieved using a random orthonormal projection from
\mathbb{R}^{P}
to
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, where
k\ll P
. Because
\langle u,v\rangle=\frac{1}{2}(\|u\|^{2}+\|v\|^{2}-\|u-v\|^{2})
, JL distance preservation over a finite set also yields approximate preservation of the inner products that define the NTK entries.

For this projection, we generate a random orthonormal matrix
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times k}
, then set
g(u)=\sqrt{P/k}\;Q^{\top}u
, where the
\sqrt{P/k}
factor offsets the scaling by
Q
.
This yields our approximated features
\tilde{\Phi}_{X}^{c}=g(\Phi_{X}^{c})
for our dataset
X
and corresponding approximate kernel
\tilde{K}^{c}=\tilde{\Phi}_{X}^{c}(\tilde{\Phi}_{X}^{c})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
.

Remark 4.1
.

In practice, JL gives a high-probability bound on
\|\tilde{K}^{c}-K^{c}\|
(and thus on spectral quantities like effective rank), so the redundancy estimates computed from
\tilde{K}^{c}
track those of
K^{c}
up to an error defined by
\varepsilon_{\text{JL}}
and the kernel’s eigenspectrum.
See
Section

B.3
for details.

4.3
Gradient distillation

Motivated by the empirical local-global kernel structure analyzed in
Section

5.3
, we introduce

(
Algorithm

1

\hat{Phi}
and synthetic targets
\hat{Y}
as linear combinations of the original projected gradients. This distillation allows a set of gradients to have some representatives covering the kernel clusters, which compose a majority of the global variance, and some covering the connective gaps shown in
Figure

Remark 4.2
.

Let
\mathbf{u}
be unit norm. If
\mathbf{u}
is an eigenvector of

with an eigenvalue
\lambda

\|\hat{\phi}\|^{2}=k\lambda

generates the principal direction

in kernel space. We use this to compute our distilled gradients directly.

Algorithm

(1)

Compute the average class kernel

(2)

Record the global eigendecomposition

(3)

Compute each global rank for which that rank’s eigenvectors are not aligned with
any
local cluster above the threshold

,
†
†

†

\tau_{g}

another user-defined threshold. “Alignment” is quantified by the fraction of restricted energy captured by the local top-eigenspace:

Distill representatives of the

6
Conclusion

We demonstrate that neural tangent kernel (NTK) representations for modern neural networks can be computed at practical scale, on datasets with
O(10^{3})
to
O(10^{4})
samples, by exploiting redundancy in both the data and parameter dimensions. By combining data-space distillation, Johnson–Lindenstrauss random projection, and gradient-space distillation into a unified compression pipeline, we reduce the computational and storage cost of NTK construction by 4–6 orders of magnitude compared to naïve full NTK computation on image classification benchmarks, and by a further 1–2 orders of magnitude beyond parameter-space random projection alone, while preserving kernel fidelity. Our theoretical analysis justifies each stage of the approximation, and a spectral study shows that per-class NTK matrices have effective ranks far smaller than the dataset size, indicating that gradient features concentrate in low-dimensional subspaces of parameter tangent space. Leveraging this structure, we introduce a gradient synthesis algorithm that achieves up to 100
\times
additional compression relative to distilled gradients, attaining 76% accuracy on ImageNette with only five synthetic gradients and consistently outperforming sampling-based baselines.

Together, these results extend NTK-based analysis beyond small-scale settings and suggest that, for practical networks, kernel representations can be manipulated efficiently using standard linear-algebraic tools.

Impact Statement

This work provides results that enable more efficient computation of the NTK, an important tool for understanding and verifying neural networks. To our knowledge, there are no serious risks associated with the release of these results.

Appendix A
NTK preliminaries

A.1
Defining the network

Let
\Theta
be a
P
-dimensional Riemannian manifold of parameters, and let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be data and output spaces, respectively. Furthermore, let
\mathcal{R}:\Theta\to(\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y})
be a realization function that maps
\theta\in\Theta
to its corresponding network
f(\cdot\;;\theta):\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
, so that
\mathcal{F}:=\mathcal{R}(\Theta)
is the set of functions from
\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
realizable by some
\theta\in\Theta
.

A.1.1
Image classification

In the case of image classification,
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}}
is the space of 2D images such that
d_{\text{in}}=(\text{length}\cdot\text{height}\cdot\text{channels})
, while
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}}
is the log-probability space over
d_{\text{out}}=C
classes. The network
f
accepts and processes an image
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
by creating sequential activations
A^{(\ell)}
across layers
\ell=0,1,\ldots,L
, each with width
d_{\ell}
, by the operation

A^{(\ell+1)}=\sigma\left(h^{(\ell)}(A^{(\ell)};\theta^{(\ell)})\right),

(13)

where
A^{(0)}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}
,
\sigma
is an activation function like
\tanh
or ReLU, and
h^{(\ell)}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell+1}}
is the transformation at layer
\ell
(dense, convolutional, attention, etc.) with trainable parameters
\theta^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{P_{\ell}}
. The vector of flattened parameters
\theta
thus has size
P=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}P_{\ell}
.

The functional output
f(\mathbf{x};\theta)
is given by
\log(\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z}))
, where the final activation
\mathbf{z}=A^{(L)}
is a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{C}
given by

A^{(L)}=h^{(L-1)}(\ldots\sigma(h^{(0)}\mathbf{x}))

and the softmax function is given element-wise by
\operatorname{softmax}(z_{i})=e^{z_{i}}/\left(\sum_{j}e^{z_{j}}\right)
.

A.1.2
Tangents and tangent spaces

Consider a fixed set of parameters
\theta\in\Theta
and the function
f(\cdot;\theta)=\mathcal{R}(\theta)\in\mathcal{F}
it realizes. For notational clarity, we write
f(\cdot\;;\theta)
when emphasizing the dependence on which parameters we are approximating, and
f(\cdot)
when
\theta
is clear from context.

The tangent spaces
\mathcal{T}_{\theta}\Theta
and
\mathcal{T}_{f}\mathcal{F}
consist of infinitesimal changes to parameters and functions, respectively. These spaces are connected by the differential of the realization map,
d\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{T}_{\theta}\Theta\to\mathcal{T}_{f}\mathcal{F}
. For a parameter perturbation
\delta\theta
, the differential produces a function perturbation
\delta f=d\mathcal{R}(\delta\theta)
whose value at input
\mathbf{x}
is given by

\delta f=d\mathcal{R}(\delta\theta)=\langle\nabla_{\theta}f(\mathbf{x};\theta),\delta\theta\rangle_{\Theta}

(14)

Here,
\nabla_{\theta}f(\mathbf{x};\theta)
is the gradient of the evaluation functional at
\mathbf{x}
, i.e., the map
\theta\mapsto f(\mathbf{x};\theta)
that outputs the network’s prediction at the specific input
\mathbf{x}
. This gradient indicates which direction in parameter space most increases the output at that particular point. The differential
d\mathcal{R}
thus measures how parameter changes translate into function changes across all inputs simultaneously.

A.2
The neural tangent kernel

Using the same notation as the previous subsection,
\nabla_{\theta}f(\mathbf{x};\theta)
is the gradient of the evaluation functional at
\mathbf{x}
with respect to the parameter.
NTK is defined based on the gradient of the network’s output with respect to the parameter.

For any two inputs
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, the
neural tangent kernel at
\theta
is given by

K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\langle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{R}(\theta)(\mathbf{x}),\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{R}(\theta)(\mathbf{x})\rangle_{\Theta}=\langle\nabla_{\theta}f(\mathbf{x};\theta),\nabla_{\theta}f(\mathbf{x};\theta)\rangle_{\Theta},

(15)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the inner product defined on
\Theta
measuring the network’s response to infinitesimal parameter change at different inputs.

A.3
Ridge regression

Since
K
is a symmetric, positive-definite kernel in the infinite-width limit
(
12
)
, it defines a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
\mathcal{H}_{K}
by the Moore–Aronszajn theorem
(
1
)
. While a finite-width trained network
f
does not lie exactly in
\mathcal{H}_{K}
, we can approximate it via kernel ridge regression: given training points
X=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
with labels
Y=\{f(\mathbf{x}_{i};\theta)\}_{i=1}^{n}
and regularization
\lambda_{\text{reg}}\geq 0
, the per-class ridge estimator solves

\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{c}=(K^{c}+\lambda_{\text{reg}}I)^{-1}Y^{c}

(16)

for each class

, yielding the predictor
f_{K}^{c}(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}^{c}K^{c}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\cdot)
.

For efficient computation, we decompose
K^{c}_{XX}=U\Sigma U^{\top}
and apply the Woodbury identity:

\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{c}=U(\Sigma+\lambda_{\text{reg}}I)^{-1}U^{\top}Y^{c}.

(17)

When using a rank-
r
approximation (
Section

5
), we substitute
U^{(r)}
for

and
\Sigma^{(r)}
for
\Sigma
in (
17
).

The predictive mean for class
c
at test point
\mathbf{x}_{*}
is

f_{K}^{c}(\mathbf{x}_{*})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}^{c}K^{c}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{*})=\frac{1}{k}\phi(\mathbf{x}_{*})^{\top}\Phi^{c}_{\tilde{X}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{c},

(18)

where
\phi(\mathbf{x}_{*})
is the gradient at the test point. The full predictor stacks these across classes:
f=[f_{K}^{1},\ldots,f_{K}^{C}]
.

•

The kernel as
K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\langle\phi(\mathbf{x}),\phi(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle

•

The population covariance:
\Sigma=\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}[\phi(\mathbf{x})\phi(\mathbf{x})^{\top}]

•

Empirical covariance:
\hat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}\Phi^{\top}\Phi

Approximation hierarchy.

We construct three successive approximations:

1.

The best RKHS approximant (unregularized, population)

f_{K}^{*}(\mathbf{x})=\langle\phi(\mathbf{x}),w^{*}\rangle,\quad w^{*}=\Sigma^{-1}\E[\phi(\mathbf{x})f(\mathbf{x};\theta)]

(20)

is the optimal linear predictor in gradient features, achievable with infinite data and no regularization. There is some distance between
f_{K}^{*}
and our target
f
.

2.

The regularized population approximant

f_{K,\lambda}^{*}(\mathbf{x})=\langle\phi(\mathbf{x}),w_{\lambda}^{*}\rangle,\quad w_{\lambda}^{*}=(\Sigma+\lambda I)^{-1}\E[\phi(\mathbf{x})f(\mathbf{x};\theta)]

adds regularization
\lambda
to stabilize the solution. There is some distance between
f_{K,\lambda}^{*}
and
f_{K}^{*}
.

3.

The empirical kernel ridge estimator

f_{K}(\mathbf{x})=\langle\phi(\mathbf{x}),\hat{w}_{\lambda}\rangle,\quad\hat{w}_{\lambda}=(\hat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\Phi^{\top}Y

uses only
n
samples to estimate
w_{\lambda}^{*}
. There is some distance between
f_{K}
and
f_{K,\lambda}^{*}
.

Error decomposition.

For a test point
\mathbf{x}_{*}
,

f(\mathbf{x}_{*};\theta)-f_{K}(\mathbf{x}_{*})=\underbrace{[f(\mathbf{x}_{*})-f_{K}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{*})]}_{\mathcal{E}_{\text{approx}}}+\underbrace{[f_{K}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{*})-f_{*}{K,\lambda}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{*})]}_{\mathcal{E}_{\text{bias}}(\lambda)}+\underbrace{[f_{K,\lambda}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{*})-f_{K}(\mathbf{x}_{*})]}_{\mathcal{E}_{\text{var}}(n,\lambda)}

(21)

where

•

\mathcal{E}_{\text{approx}}
is the error from network nonlinearity

•

\mathcal{E}_{\text{bias}}
is the bias from regularization shrinkage

•

\mathcal{E}_{\text{var}}
is the variance from finite sampling

Approximation gap.

The optimal
w^{*}
from (
20
) minimizes the least-squares
\E[(f(\mathbf{x};\theta)-\langle\phi(\mathbf{x}),w\rangle)^{2}]
. Setting the gradient of this expression to zero gives

\E[\phi(\mathbf{x})\phi(\mathbf{x})^{\top}]w^{*}=\Sigma w^{*}=\E[\phi(\mathbf{x})f(\mathbf{x};\theta)].

From here, the minimal residual variance is

\mathcal{E}^{2}_{\text{approx}}=\E[f(x;\theta)^{2}]-2(w^{*})^{\top}\E[\phi(\mathbf{x})f(\mathbf{x};\theta)]+(w^{*})^{\top}\Sigma w^{*},

where substituting
\E[\phi(\mathbf{x})\phi(\mathbf{x})^{\top}]w^{*}=\Sigma w^{*}
gives

\mathcal{E}^{2}_{\text{approx}}=\E[f(x;\theta)^{2}]-(w^{*})^{\top}\Sigma w^{*}
.
This is equivalent to
(1-R^{2})\cdot\text{Var}(f)
, where
R^{2}
is the usual coefficient of determination.

Jacot et al.
(
12
)
show that, for a network of width
d
that has moved a distance of
\|\Delta\theta\|=\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|
, from its
\mathcal{H}_{K}
parameterization,

\mathcal{E}_{\text{approx}}=(1-R^{2})\cdot\text{Var}(f)\sim O\left(\frac{\|\Delta\theta\|^{2}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\cdot\sqrt{\E[||H(\mathbf{x})||_{F}^{2}]},

where
H(\mathbf{x})=\nabla_{\theta}^{2}f(x;\theta)
is the parameter Hessian.

Bias error.

The regularized population solution satisfies

(\Sigma+\lambda I)w^{*}_{\lambda}=\E[\phi(\mathbf{x})f(\mathbf{x};\theta)]=\Sigma w^{*}.

Expand
w^{*}
in its eigenbasis of
\Sigma
by letting
w^{*}=\sum_{j}\beta_{j}\mathbf{v}_{j}
, where
\beta_{j}=\langle\mathbf{v}_{j},w^{*}\rangle
. Then,

w^{*}\lambda=\sum_{j}\frac{\mu_{j}}{\mu_{j}+\lambda}\beta_{j}\mathbf{v}_{j}.

The bias at a test point
\mathbf{x}_{*}
is given by

f_{K}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{*})-f_{K,\lambda}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{*})=\phi(\mathbf{x}_{*})^{\top}(w^{*}-w^{*}_{\lambda})=\sum_{j}\frac{\lambda}{\mu_{j}+\lambda}\beta_{j}\langle\phi(\mathbf{x}_{*}),\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle.

Taking the expectation of this term over
\mathbf{x}_{*}\sim\mathcal{P}
and noting that
\E[\langle\phi(\mathbf{x}_{*}),\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle\langle\phi(\mathbf{x}_{*}),\mathbf{v}_{k}\rangle]=\mathbf{v}_{j}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{v}_{k}
, we have

\mathcal{E}^{2}_{\text{bias}}=\E_{\mathbf{x}_{*}}\left[\left(\sum_{j}\frac{\lambda}{\mu_{j}+\lambda}\beta_{j}\langle\phi(\mathbf{x}_{*}),\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle\right)^{2}\right]=\sum_{j}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\mu_{j}+\lambda}\right)^{2}\mu_{j}\beta_{j}^{2}.

Suppose that there exists some integer
r>0
such that
w^{*}\in\text{Range}(\Sigma^{r})
.
This means
w^{*}=\Sigma^{r}\xi
for some
\xi
with
|\xi|^{2}<\infty
.
Then,

\mathcal{E}_{\text{bias}}^{2}=\sum_{j}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\mu_{j}+\lambda}\right)^{2}\mu_{j}^{2r+1}\xi_{j}^{2}\leq\lambda^{2r}|\xi|^{2},

whereby we conclude

\mathcal{E}_{\text{bias}}\sim O(\lambda^{r}).

Variance error.

The finite-sample estimator is given by

\hat{w}_{\lambda}=(\hat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\Phi^{\top}Y
.
Substituting the residual form
Y=\Phi w^{*}+r
gives

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(\hat{\Sigma}+\lambda I\right)^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\hat{\Sigma}w^{*}}_{\text{signal weight}}+\underbrace{\left(\hat{\Sigma}+\lambda I\right)^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\Phi^{\top}r.}_{\text{residual noise}}

(22)

Supposing that the covariance of
r
can be approximated as
\sigma^{2}I
, where
\sigma^{2}=\mathcal{E}^{2}_{\text{approx}}
, we get that

\text{Var}(f_{K}(\mathbf{x}_{*})|X)=\phi(\mathbf{x}_{*})^{\top}(\hat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\hat{\Sigma}\right)(\hat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\phi(\mathbf{x}_{*}).

Averaging this quantity over test points and using
\E_{\mathbf{x}_{*}}[\phi(\mathbf{x})\phi(\mathbf{x}_{*})^{\top}]=\Sigma
gives

\E_{\mathbf{x}_{*}}[\text{Var}(f_{K}(\mathbf{x}_{*})|X)]=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\tr\left(\Sigma(\hat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\hat{\Sigma}(\hat{\Sigma}+\lambda I)^{-1}\right).

For large enough
n
,
\hat{\Sigma}\approx\Sigma
. Substituting and using spectral decomposition gives

\tr(\Sigma(\Sigma+\lambda I)^{-1}\Sigma(\Sigma+\lambda I)^{-1})=\sum_{j}\frac{\mu_{j}^{3}}{(\mu_{j}+\lambda)^{2}}.

Since
\frac{\mu_{j}}{\mu_{j}+\lambda}\leq 1
and
\mu_{j}\leq\mu_{1}
,

\sum_{j}\frac{\mu_{j}^{3}}{(\mu_{j}+\lambda)^{2}}\leq\sum_{j}\frac{\mu_{j}^{2}}{\mu_{j}+\lambda}\leq\mu_{1}\cdot d(\lambda),

where
d(\lambda)=\sum_{j}\frac{\mu_{j}}{\mu_{j}+\lambda}
is the truncation dimension.

Assuming that
\mu_{1}=O(1)
, this gives the final bound

\mathcal{E}^{2}_{\text{var}}\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}d(\lambda)}{n}.

If
\mu_{j}\sim j^{-\alpha}
for some positive
\alpha
, then
d(\lambda)=\sum_{j}\frac{\mu_{j}}{\mu_{j}+\lambda}\approx\sum{j:\mu_{j}>\lambda}1\sim\lambda^{-1/\alpha}
, so that
\mathcal{E}^{2}_{\text{var}}\sim\frac{\lambda^{-1/\alpha}}{n}
.

Remark B.4
.

The Moore-Aronszajn theorem states that for functions in the RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{K}
expressed as a linear combination of partial kernel applications
K(\mathbf{x}_{i},\cdot)
for some
\mathbf{x}_{i}
,

\sup_{p\geq 0}\left\|\sum_{i=n}^{n+p}\alpha_{i}K(\mathbf{x}_{i},\cdot)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

That is, a finite linear combination of some choice of data points and coefficients can approximate the target function to arbitrary precision. For finite-width networks, however,
\mathcal{H}_{K}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{P}
is finite-dimensional, so any
f\in\mathcal{H}_{K}
admits an exact finite representation, and truncation error vanishes identically. The relevant finite-sample limitation is instead the restriction to
\text{span}{\phi(\mathbf{x}_{i})}_{i=1}^{n}
, which is captured by
\mathcal{E}_{\text{var}}
in our decomposition.

B.2
Projection error

Approximating
f_{K}
by a projected and distilled version of itself introduces two new error terms (
\mathcal{E}_{\text{dis}}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\text{proj}}
) to Eqn. (
21
). Here we treat
\mathcal{E}_{\text{proj}}
(
\mathcal{E}_{\text{dis}}
is given by the misalignment error in Proposition
3.6
).

The kernel ridge estimator is given by
f_{K}(\mathbf{x}_{*})=K_{*X}(K_{XX}+\lambda I)^{-1}Y
, where
K_{*X}
is the kernel between test poing
\mathbf{x}_{*}
and training set
X
. With JL projection, we get
\tilde{f}_{K}(\mathbf{x}_{*})=\tilde{K}_{*X}(\tilde{K}_{XX}+\lambda I)^{-1}Y
.

The difference between
f_{K}(\mathbf{x})
and
\tilde{f}_{K}(\mathbf{x}_{*})
decomposes as

\mathcal{E}_{\text{JL}}=\underbrace{(K_{*X}-\tilde{K}_{*X})(\tilde{K}_{XX}+\lambda I)^{-1}Y}_{\text{(I) test kernel error}}+\underbrace{K_{*X}\left[(K_{XX}+\lambda I)^{-1}-(\tilde{K}_{XX}+\lambda I)^{-1}\right]Y}_{\text{(II) inverse perturbation error}}.

Given a random projection
R\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times P}
with normalized entries, each kernel entry satisfies
\E[\tilde{K}_{ij}]=K_{ij}
(i.e., the projection is unbiased), and
|\tilde{K}_{ij}-K_{ij}|\leq\varepsilon_{\text{JL}}\cdot|K_{ij}|
, where
\varepsilon_{\text{JL}}=O(\sqrt{\log(n)/k})
for projection dimension
k
.

We can then bound the two error terms:

(I)

Using matrix norm bounds,

\|(K_{*X}-\tilde{K}_{*X})(\tilde{K}_{XX}+\lambda I)^{-1}Y\|\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{JL}}\|K_{*X}\|\cdot\|Y\|}{\mu_{\min}(\tilde{K}_{XX})+\lambda},

where
\lambda
is the regularization parameter
and
\mu_{\min}(\tilde{K}_{XX})

is the minimum eigenvalue of
\tilde{K}_{XX}
.

(II)

Using the identity
A^{-1}-B^{-1}=A^{-1}(B-A)B^{-1}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|(K_{XX}+\lambda I)^{-1}-(\tilde{K}_{XX}+\lambda I)^{-1}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|(K_{XX}+\lambda I)^{-1}\left[(K_{XX}+\lambda I)-(\tilde{K}_{XX}+\lambda I)\right](\tilde{K}_{XX}+\lambda I)^{-1}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{JL}}\cdot\|K_{XX}\|_{2}}{\lambda^{2}}

This then propagates to

\left\|K_{*X}\left[(K_{XX}+\lambda I)^{-1}-(\tilde{K}_{XX}+\lambda I)^{-1}Y\right]\right\|\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{JL}}\cdot\|K_{*X}\|\cdot\|K_{XX}\|_{2}\cdot\|Y\|}{\lambda^{2}}.

Putting these together gives

\mathcal{E}_{\text{JL}}\leq\varepsilon_{\text{JL}}\cdot\|K_{*X}\|\cdot\|Y\|\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}+\frac{\|K_{XX}\|_{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\right).

If
\mu_{1}
is the largest eigenvalue of
K_{XX}
, then

\mathcal{E}_{\text{JL}}=O(\varepsilon_{\text{JL}}\cdot\frac{\mu_{1}}{\lambda^{2}})
.

Remark B.5
.

This is a very conservative upper bound; the actual projection error can be much smaller if the kernel has low truncation rank
r\ll n
. In this case, the JL error depends on
r
rather than
n
, since only
r
directions carry significant variance. We study parameter redundancy in Sec.
B.3
.

B.3
JL parameter redundancy

Proposition B.6

(Parameter redundancy with JL projection)
.

Let
\tilde{K}
be the JL-approximated kernel with projection dimension
k\geq\frac{c\ln(n)}{\varepsilon^{2}_{\text{JL}}}
. Let
\{\tilde{\lambda}_{i}\}
be the eigenvalues of
\tilde{K}
with mean
\bar{\tilde{\lambda}}
.
If
\tilde{K}
has truncation rank
r
at threshold
(1-\delta)
,
then the original parameters are
(P/r,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-parameter redundant,
where
\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq O\left(\delta\tilde{\lambda}_{r+1}/\bar{\tilde{\lambda}}+\varepsilon_{\text{JL}}\right)
.

Optimal subspace.

In the terms of Def.
3.2
, the optimal choice of
V
is the span of the top
r
right singular vectors of
\Phi_{X}
. Let
\Phi_{X}=U\Sigma V^{\top}
be the SVD of
\Phi_{X}
. Taking
V_{r}
to be the first
r
columns of
V
, the projection of
\Phi_{X}
onto the span of
V_{r}
is

\Pi_{V_{r}}(\Phi_{X})=\Phi(X)V_{r}V_{r}^{\top}=U_{r}\Sigma_{r}V_{r}^{\top},

so that

\Pi_{V_{r}}(\Phi_{X})\Pi_{V_{r}}(\Phi_{X})^{\top}=U_{r}\Sigma^{2}_{r}U_{r}^{\top}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{u}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{i}),

where
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are the eigenvalues of
K
.

Numerator error.

The error in the numerator is given by

K-\Pi_{V_{r}}(\Phi_{X})\Pi_{V_{r}}(\Phi_{X})^{\top}=\sum_{i>r}^{n}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{u}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{i})\implies||K-\Pi_{V_{r}}(\Phi_{X})\Pi_{V_{r}}(\Phi_{X})^{\top}||_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i>r}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2},

(23)

since
\mathbf{u}_{i}
is orthonormal to
\mathbf{u}_{j}
for all
i\neq j
.

The truncation rank
r
at threshold
(1-\delta)
satisfies

\sum_{i>r}^{n}\lambda_{i}\ \leq\delta\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=\delta\cdot\tr(K).

Furthermore, we have that

\sum_{i>r}\lambda_{i}^{2}\leq\lambda_{r+1}\sum_{i>r}\lambda_{i}\leq\lambda_{r+1}\cdot\delta\cdot\tr(K).

(24)

Denominator error.

Cauchy-Schwartz gives

||K||_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}\geq\frac{\tr(K)^{2}}{n}.

(25)

Combined error.

(
23
), (
24
), and (
25
) give

\frac{||K-K_{R}||_{F}^{2}}{||K||_{F}^{2}}\leq\frac{\lambda_{r+1}\cdot\delta\cdot\tr(K)}{\tr(K)^{2}/n}=\frac{n\delta\lambda_{r+1}}{\tr(K)}=\delta\cdot\frac{\lambda_{r+1}}{\bar{\lambda}},

(26)

where
\bar{\lambda}=\tr(K)/n
is the mean eigenvalue. Choosing a
\delta
factor with an truncation rank whose eigenvalue is less than the mean is a practical way to further scale down the error.

JL approximation.

We don’t observe
K
or its spectrum directly, but JL guarantees that the projected
\tilde{K}
has eigenvalues
\tilde{\lambda}_{i}
satisfying

(1-\varepsilon_{\text{JL}})^{2}\lambda_{i}\leq\tilde{\lambda}_{i}\leq(1+\varepsilon_{\text{JL}})^{2}\lambda_{i}

with high probability. Therefore, the truncation rank
\tilde{r}
of
\tilde{K}
approximates that of
K
, as does the ratio
\tilde{\lambda}_{r+1}/\bar{\tilde{\lambda}}
, giving an error bound of
O\left(\delta\frac{\tilde{\lambda}_{r+1}}{\bar{\tilde{\lambda}}}+\varepsilon_{\text{JL}}\right)
.

\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times P}
have

C.1
Preliminary lemma: reconciling loss gradients with logit-gradient features

Lemma C.1

(Chain rule: loss gradients lie in the logit-gradient span)
.

Fix
\theta
and logits
f(\mathbf{x};\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
. For each
c\in[C]
define the logit-gradient feature

\phi^{c}(\mathbf{x}):=\nabla_{\theta}f^{c}(\mathbf{x};\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
. Let
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{C}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
be any per-example loss,
and define the
logit sensitivity

\delta(\mathbf{x},y;\theta):=\nabla_{z}\ell(z,y)|_{z=f(\mathbf{x};\theta)}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}.

Then for any labeled example
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
,

\nabla_{\theta}\ell(f(\mathbf{x};\theta),\mathbf{y})=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\delta_{c}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\theta)\,\phi^{c}(\mathbf{x}).

Consequently, for a distilled dataset
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}=(\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})
with
|\tilde{X}|=m
, define
the stacked logit-gradient matrix

\tilde{\Phi}:=\begin{bmatrix}\Phi_{\tilde{X}}^{1}\\
\vdots\\
\Phi_{\tilde{X}}^{C}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(mC)\times P},\qquad[\Phi_{\tilde{X}}^{c}]_{i}:=\phi^{c}(\mathbf{\tilde{x}}_{i}).

Let
\tilde{\delta}(\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{mC}
stack
\delta(\mathbf{\tilde{x}}_{i},\mathbf{\tilde{y}}_{i};\theta)
over
i
.
Then the distilled loss gradient satisfies

g_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\theta):=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\theta)=\tilde{\Phi}^{\top}\tilde{\delta}(\theta)\in\mathrm{colspan}(\tilde{\Phi}^{\top})=:V(\tilde{\mathcal{D}}).

Lemma
C.1
is the key reconciliation used in Sec.
3.3
: although DD is
written in terms of
loss
gradients, those gradients always lie in the span of
logit
gradients

\nabla_{\theta}f^{c}(\tilde{X};\theta)
, which are exactly the kernel features.

C.2
Proof of Theorem
3.3
(projection residual controls one-step progress)

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Fix
t
and abbreviate
\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta)
by
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
and
g_{t}:=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta)
.
Since
\mathcal{L}
is
L
-smooth, for any update direction
v
and step size
\eta
,

\mathcal{L}(\theta-\eta v)\leq\mathcal{L}(\theta)+\langle g_{t},-\eta v\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|\eta v\|^{2}=\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\eta\langle g_{t},v\rangle+\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\|v\|^{2}.

Applying this with
v=g_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\theta)
yields

\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta^{+}(\tilde{\mathcal{D}}))\leq\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta)-\eta\langle g_{t},g_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\theta)\rangle+\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\|g_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(\theta)\|^{2},

and taking expectation over
t\sim\mathcal{T}
gives (
5
).

For the second claim, fix a subspace
V:=V(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})
with orthogonal projector
\Pi_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}
.
Consider the quadratic upper model

q_{t}(\Delta):=\langle g_{t},\Delta\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|\Delta\|^{2},\qquad\Delta\in V.

Because
q_{t}
is strictly convex, its minimizer over
\Delta\in V
is characterized by the first-order condition:
for all
\Delta^{\prime}\in V
,

\langle g_{t}+L\Delta_{t}^{\star},\ \Delta^{\prime}-\Delta_{t}^{\star}\rangle=0.

Equivalently,
g_{t}+L\Delta_{t}^{\star}
is orthogonal to
V
, i.e.
\Pi_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}(g_{t}+L\Delta_{t}^{\star})=0
,
which gives

\Delta_{t}^{\star}=-\frac{1}{L}\Pi_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}g_{t}.

By
L
-smoothness,
\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta+\Delta)\leq\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta)+q_{t}(\Delta)
for all
\Delta
; hence

\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta)-\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta+\Delta_{t}^{\star})\geq-q_{t}(\Delta_{t}^{\star})=-\Big\langle g_{t},-\tfrac{1}{L}\Pi_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}g_{t}\Big\rangle-\frac{L}{2}\Big\|-\tfrac{1}{L}\Pi_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}g_{t}\Big\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2L}\|\Pi_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}g_{t}\|^{2}.

).
∎

C.3
Proof of Corollary
3.5
(PCA of gradient covariance is optimal)

Proof of Corollary
3.5
.

Let
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{P}
be any
r
-dimensional subspace with orthogonal projector
\Pi_{V}
. Using idempotence and
symmetry of
\Pi_{V}
,

\|(I-\Pi_{V})g_{t}\|^{2}=g_{t}^{\top}(I-\Pi_{V})g_{t}=\mathrm{tr}\!\big((I-\Pi_{V})g_{t}g_{t}^{\top}\big).

Taking expectation over
t
and defining
G:=\E_{t}[g_{t}g_{t}^{\top}]
yields

\E_{t}[\|(I-\Pi_{V})g_{t}\|^{2}]=\mathrm{tr}((I-\Pi_{V})G)=\mathrm{tr}(G)-\mathrm{tr}(\Pi_{V}G).

Thus minimizing
\E_{t}[\|(I-\Pi_{V})g_{t}\|^{2}]
over
\dim(V)=r
is equivalent to maximizing

\mathrm{tr}(\Pi_{V}G)
over rank-
r
orthogonal projectors. By the Ky Fan maximum principle, the maximizer is
the projector onto the top-
r
eigenspace of
G
, attaining value
\sum_{j\leq r}\lambda_{j}
. Therefore the
minimum residual equals

\mathrm{tr}(G)-\sum_{j\leq r}\lambda_{j}=\sum_{j>r}\lambda_{j}.

Moreover, if
\mathrm{tr}(\Pi_{V^{\star}}G)-\mathrm{tr}(\Pi_{V}G)\leq\delta
, then

\E_{t}[\|(I-\Pi_{V})g_{t}\|^{2}]=\mathrm{tr}(G)-\mathrm{tr}(\Pi_{V}G)=\underbrace{\mathrm{tr}(G)-\mathrm{tr}(\Pi_{V^{\star}}G)}_{=\sum_{j>r}\lambda_{j}}+\Big(\mathrm{tr}(\Pi_{V^{\star}}G)-\mathrm{tr}(\Pi_{V}G)\Big)\leq\sum_{j>r}\lambda_{j}+\delta,

as claimed.
∎

C.4
Proof of Proposition
3.6
(feature-space tail + misalignment)

Proof of Proposition
3.6
.

Let
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times P}
be a gradient-feature matrix (rows in
\mathbb{R}^{P}
) with SVD
\Phi=U\Sigma W^{\top}
.
Let
W_{r}
be the top-
r
right singular vectors and
\Pi^{\star}:=W_{r}W_{r}^{\top}
the rank-
r
PCA projector.
For any rank-
r
orthogonal projector
\Pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{P}
(in particular,
\Pi=\Pi_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}
when

\dim V(\tilde{\mathcal{D}})=r
), using
\|M\|_{F}^{2}=\mathrm{tr}(M^{\top}M)
and
\Pi=\Pi^{\top}=\Pi^{2}
,

\|\Phi(I-\Pi)\|_{F}^{2}=\mathrm{tr}\!\big((I-\Pi)\Phi^{\top}\Phi(I-\Pi)\big)=\mathrm{tr}(\Phi^{\top}\Phi)-\mathrm{tr}(\Phi^{\top}\Phi\,\Pi).

Define

By the Ky Fan maximum principle,

.

C.5
Wasserstein metric dataset distillation

Since directly solving this optimization problem is often prohibitive, it is often convenient to accomplish this through a surrogate objective. Here, we use a modified version of
Wasserstein metric DD (WMDD)

(
14
)
, which aims to solve the surrogate problem of feature matching, under the assumption that a performant distilled dataset will be
distributionally close to the original dataset in both the data space and the feature space
. To that end, it defines the loss function on the dataset:

\mathcal{L}(\tilde{X})=\mathcal{L}_{\text{feature}}+\lambda_{\text{BN}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{BN}},\quad\text{where}

•

\mathcal{L}_{\text{feature}}=\sum_{k,j}\|f_{e}(\mathbf{\tilde{x}}_{k,j})-b_{k,j}\|2^{2}
matches synthetic features given by
f_{e}
to their barycenter targets
b_{k,j}
, which are computed from a pretrained
f
.

•

E.2
Complexity and grid search

The length of Algorithm
1
belies a reasonable complexity profile. Let
n
be the number of original gradients,
H
the number of clusters,
r_{g}
the global effective rank, and
\bar{r}=\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h}r_{h}
the average local effective rank. We get the following complexity considerations:

•

Kernel computation
scales as
O\left(n^{2}kC\right)

•

Clustering
is
O\left(n^{3}\right)
for spectral clustering (dominated mostly by eigendecomposition)

•

Global SVD
is
O(n^{3})

•

Local SVDs
is
O\left(\sum_{h}|\mathcal{I}_{h}|^{3}\right)=O\left(n^{3}/H^{2}\right)
, assuming roughly balanced clusters

•

Synthesis
is
O(m\bar{n}kC)
where
m
is the number of synthetic gradients and
\bar{n}=n/H

Since these considerations are independent of one another, the complexity is dominated by the
O(n^{3})
global SVD, which is a one-time cost. This compares favorably to other methods, which scale as in
