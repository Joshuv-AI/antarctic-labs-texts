Small-time asymptotics for hypoelliptic diffusions
Juraj Földes, David P. Herzog

Abstract
. An inductive procedure is developed to calculate the asymptotic behavior at time zero of a diffusion with polynomial drift and degenerate, additive noise. The procedure gives rise to two different rescalings of the process; namely, a functional law of the iterated logarithm rescaling and a distributional rescaling. The limiting behavior of these rescalings is studied, resulting in two related control problems which are solved in nontrivial examples using methods from geometric control theory. The control information from these problems gives rise to a practical criteria for points to be regular on the boundary of a domain in 𝐑 n \mathbf{R}^{n} for such diffusions.



1.
Introduction

The goal of this work is to explore the time-zero behavior of the following class of stochastic differential equations (SDEs) on
\mathbf{R}^{n}

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dx_{t}=P(x_{t})\,dt+\sigma\,dB_{t}\\
x_{0}=0\,,\end{cases}

where
P:\mathbf{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{n}
is a polynomial vector field,
\sigma=\text{diag}(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2},\ldots,\sigma^{n})
is a constant, diagonal
n\times n
matrix with
\sigma^{i}\geq 0
, and
B_{t}
is a standard
n
-dimensional Brownian motion defined on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbf{P},\mathbf{E})
. Importantly, some or many of the diagonal elements of
\sigma
may be zero, so that the noise in (
1.1
) is degenerate.

Our study of (
1.1
) at time zero is partly motivated by the
regular point problem
. More specifically,
in this paper we provide criteria for identifying regular points on the boundary of a domain in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
for diffusions with degenerate noise of the form (
1.1
). The regular point problem often arises in the context of
partial differential equations,
when one investigates well-posedness of the Dirichlet and Poisson problems on a domain
\mathscr{O}\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
with the differential operator being the inifinitesimal generator of the diffusion (
1.1
), see
[
8
,
28
]
. In the context of (
1.1
), assume without loss of generality
0\in\partial\mathscr{O}
(otherwise change coordinates) and denote

(1.2)

\displaystyle\xi=\inf\{t>0\,:\,x_{t}\in\mathscr{O}\}.

If
\mathbf{P}\{\xi=0\}=1
, then we call
0\in\mathbf{R}^{n}

regular
for
(x_{t},\mathscr{O})
, otherwise,
0\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
is called
irregular
for
(x_{t},\mathscr{O})
. Note that, because the event
\{\xi=0\}
belongs to the germ sigma-field
\bigcap_{t>0}\mathcal{F}_{t}
, Blumenthal’s zero-one law implies
\mathbf{P}\{\xi=0\}\in\{0,1\}
, see
[
5
]
.

When the diffusion (
1.1
) is non-degenerate; that is, when
\sigma^{i}\neq 0
for all
i
, then there is the well-known
cone condition
ensuring
0\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
is regular for
(x_{t},\mathscr{O})
. More precisely, let
B_{r}(0)
denote the open ball of radius
r>0
centered at
0\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
. If there is a basis
\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\}
of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
such that

\displaystyle\text{Cone}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}):=\{\lambda_{1}v_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{n}v_{n}\,:\,\lambda_{i}>0\}

satisfies

\displaystyle\text{Cone}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})\cap B_{\rho}(0)\subset\mathscr{O}

for some
\rho>0
, then
0\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
is regular for
(x_{t},\mathscr{O})
. Intuitively, this is true because the trajectories of
\sigma B_{t}
dominate the time-zero behavior of (
1.1
) in terms of scale, i.e.
\sqrt{t}
versus
t
, and the process
\sigma B_{t}
enters
\mathscr{O}
instantaneously as long as there is “sufficient space”, as determined by the cone condition
[
6
,
7
,
29
]
.

On the other hand, when one or more of the diffusion coefficients
\sigma^{i}
vanish, much less is known about regular points, and results are typically restricted to specific examples. In the pair of papers
[
19
,
20
]
, the so-called
Iterated Kolmogorov
diffusion is studied, which is a special case of (
1.1
) with
\sigma^{1}>0
,
\sigma^{2}=\cdots=\sigma^{n}=0
and
P(x)=(0,x^{1},x^{2},\ldots,x^{n-1})
. In
[
20
]
, a necessary and sufficient condition for
0\in\partial\mathscr{O}
to be regular for the Iterated Kolmogorov diffusion is given for a general class of boundaries
\partial\mathscr{O}
and the paper
[
19
]
deduces associated laws of the iterated logarithm. Under conditions on the fundamental solution, in
[
15
]
a general class of hypoelliptic diffusions is studied using methods from PDE and a Wiener, cone-type criterion is deduced for existence and uniqueness of generalized solutions (in the Perron-Wiener sense) of the associated Dirichlet problem. One of the main applications in
[
15
]
is a criteria for regular points for linear, hypoelliptic diffusions with additive noise
[
15
, Theorem 6.2]
, which is also a special case of (
1.1
). For earlier related results, we refer to
[
9
,
21
,
24
]
which establish Wiener-type criteria for regular points of
strongly hypoelliptic
diffusions. Strongly hypoelliptic diffusions are different structurally than hypoelliptic diffusions of the form (
1.1
), since only the noise vector fields, along with their iterated commutators are assumed to span the tangent space at all points in space. One of the goals of the present paper is to generalize the strongly hypoelliptic setting and produce results for (
1.1
).

Different from the uniformly elliptic setting; that is, when
\sigma^{i}\neq 0
for all
i
, the process
\sigma B_{t}
is no longer dominant at time zero in every direction. In particular, one or more directions in the equation are at a smaller scale than
\sqrt{t}
. Thus the first issue present in the regular point problem is the identification of the correct scale for the process
x_{t}
in every direction at time zero. We address this issue for equations of the form (
1.1
) which are
noise propagating
(see Definition
4.1
below) at two scales; namely, a functional law of the iterated logarithm (LIL) scale and a distributional scale. We describe a constructive inductive procedure that produces the right scale in each component in these two senses. The procedure starts from the directions in which noise is explicitly present (
\sigma_{j}\neq 0
) and whose scale is known. These scales are then propagated through the equation to determine the correct scales in the remaining directions. It is important to note that the notion of
noise propagating
appears weaker than the notion of hypoellipticity as in
[
12
]
, and there are simple, concrete examples of diffusions (
1.1
) which are noise propagating that are not hypoelliptic (see Example
4.5
). However, we do not have proof of this general assertion. Intuitively, noise propagating means that the noise implicitly accentuates all directions in space, but the resulting diffusion can still live on a lower-dimensional manifold in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. The inductive procedures to compute scales are presented in Section
4
and Section
5
, where the notion of
scalings
is introduced and discussed in Section
3
.

As a consequence of the identification of scales for noise propagating diffusions we obtain two notions time-zero behavior. Namely, a functional LIL at time zero, which, like the LIL for Brownian motion, gives the very precise “asymptotic windows” in path space for the rescaled process using this particular scaling. On the other hand, we can also compute the correct scale in terms of distribution on path space. The latter rescaling is less refined than the functional LIL scaling. However, in terms of the regular point problem, the resulting control problem which determines which points are visited by the limit of the rescaled process is arguably easier to solve in the distributional case than the LIL scaling. This is because methods from geometric control theory are now accessible
[
1
,
2
,
10
,
11
,
13
,
14
,
25
]
, whereas in the case of the functional LIL the controls must be bounded. Nevertheless, due to the related structure of the two rescalings, control information from the distributional control problem can sometimes be transferred to control information for LIL scaling. This connection is explored in Section
6
and Section
7
. In particular, in addition to discussing the relationships between the two control problems and how to solve them, we also give a mostly self-contained introduction to various control methods in Sections
6
and
7
.

Lastly, in Section
8
, we establish a criteria for
0\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
to be regular for
(x_{t},\mathscr{O})
for various domains
\mathscr{O}\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
with
0\in\partial\mathscr{O}
. Intuitively, one first computes the distributional scaling inductively using the already introduced procedure, under the hypothesis that
x_{t}
is noise propagating. Then, one solves the resulting control problem associated to the rescaled process using the methods from geometric control theory discussed in Section
7
. Provided the boundary
\partial\mathscr{O}
is well-behaved under the scaling to allow sufficient space, and the control trajectories can access this space, it follows that
0\in\mathscr{O}
is regular for
(x_{t},\mathscr{O})
.

We begin in the next section by introducing notation and terminology used throughout paper.

2.
Notation and Terminology

Due to the possibility of finite-time explosion in (
1.1
), we define stopping times
\tau_{k}
,
k\in\mathbf{N}
, and
\tau_{\infty}
by

(2.1)

\displaystyle\tau_{k}=\inf\{t\geq 0\,:\,|x_{t}|\geq k\}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\tau_{\infty}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\tau_{k}.

It follows that the (pathwise) solution
x_{t}
is defined and unique for all finite times
t<\tau_{\infty}
,
\mathbf{P}
-almost surely. If
\mathbf{P}\{\tau_{\infty}=\infty\}=1
, then we say that
x_{t}
is
non-explosive
. Otherwise, we fix a death state
\Delta\notin\mathbf{R}^{n}
and endow
\mathbf{R}^{n}\cup\{\Delta\}
with the same topology as the one point compactification; that is,
U
is open in
\mathbf{R}^{n}\cup\{\Delta\}
if either
U
is open in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
or
U=\{\Delta\}\cup(\mathbf{R}^{n}\setminus C)
for some compact
C\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
. Then we extend the local solution
x_{t}
to all finite times
t\geq 0
by setting
x_{t}=\Delta
for
t\geq\tau_{\infty}
. In particular, for almost every
\omega\in\Omega
, the mapping
t\mapsto x_{t}(\omega):[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{n}\cup\{\Delta\}
belongs to the space of
explosive trajectories

\mathcal{E}
on
[0,1]
; that is, it belongs to the set
\mathcal{E}
of continuous mappings
f:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{n}\cup\{\Delta\}
such that if
f_{t_{0}}=\Delta
for some
t_{0}\in[0,1]
then
f_{t}=\Delta
for all
t\geq t_{0}
. For any
g\in\mathcal{E}
, we let

(2.2)

\displaystyle\tau(g):=\inf\{t\in[0,1]\,:\,g_{t}=\Delta\}

with the understanding that
\inf\emptyset:=\infty
. In particular,
\tau(x_{\cdot}(\omega))=\tau_{\infty}(\omega)\wedge 1
for
\mathbf{P}
-almost every
\omega
. We let
\mathcal{C}
denote the space of continuous mappings
f:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{n}
. In particular,
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{E}
. We also make use of the spaces

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=\{h\in\mathcal{C}\,:\,h_{0}=0,\,\dot{h}\in L^{2}([0,1];\mathbf{R}^{n})\}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}=\{f\in\mathcal{H}\,:\,\tfrac{1}{2}\textstyle{\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{f}_{s}|^{2}\,ds}\leq\alpha^{2}\},

where
\dot{h}=dh/dt
and
\alpha>0
.

For any
t\in(0,1]
, we define
d_{t}:\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{E}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
by

(2.4)

\displaystyle d_{t}(f,g):=\begin{cases}\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|f_{s}-g_{s}|&\text{ if }\tau(f)\wedge\tau(g)>t\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}.

Observe that
d_{t}(f,g)
agrees with the usual sup norm on
[0,t]
provided both
f
and
g
have yet to hit the death state
\Delta
by time
t
. For simplicity, we write
d=d_{1}
. It is not hard to check that
d_{t}
satisfies the triangle inequality. However,
d_{t}(f,f)=\infty
for any
f\in\mathcal{E}
with
f_{t/2}=\Delta
, and therefore
d_{t}
is not a metric on
\mathcal{E}
. For any
A\subset\mathcal{E}
and any
f\in\mathcal{E}
, we let

(2.5)

\displaystyle d_{t}(f,A)=\inf\{d_{t}(f,g)\,:\,g\in A\}.

Throughout the paper, if
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
, then we write
x=(x^{1},x^{2},\ldots,x^{n})
where
x^{i}\in\mathbf{R}
. The
k
th power of
x^{i}\in\mathbf{R}
is denoted by
(x^{i})^{k}
to avoid confusion. The set
\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\}
denotes the standard orthonormal basis of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, and we use
\cdot
to denote the usual dot product of vectors. The notation
B_{r}(x)
denotes the open ball centered at
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
with radius
r>0
. The index set
I=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
is used frequently below. For any subset
J\subset I
and
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
, we let
\pi_{J}(x)\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
denote the projection onto the span of
\{e_{j}\}_{j\in J}
; that is,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\pi_{J}(x)=\sum_{j\in J}(x\cdot e_{j})e_{j}=\sum_{j\in J}x^{j}e_{j}.

We also use the notation
\text{proj}_{j}:\mathbf{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
,
j=1,2
, to denote the projection onto the
j
th coordinate; that is,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\text{proj}_{1}(x^{1},x^{2})=x^{1}\quad\text{ and }\quad\text{proj}_{2}(x^{1},x^{2})=x^{2}.

The notation
\cl(A)
denotes the closure of a set
A
, where we indicate the precise topology if it is not clear from context. The set of constant vector fields on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
is denoted by
\Cons(\mathbf{R}^{n})
, while the set of polynomial vector fields on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
is denoted by
\Poly(\mathbf{R}^{n})
. Below, we often express
Z\in\Poly(\mathbf{R}^{n})
in coordinates by

(2.8)

\displaystyle Z=\sum_{j=1}^{n}Z^{j}(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}

for some polynomial functions
Z^{1},\ldots,Z^{n}
.
For any
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
Z\in\Poly(\mathbf{R}^{n})
, we let
Z(x)
denote the point
Z(x)=(Z^{1}(x),\ldots,Z^{n}(x))\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
, where
Z^{1},\ldots,Z^{n}
are as in (
2.8
).

For any vector field
R\in\Poly(\mathbf{R}^{n})
and
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
, we let
\varphi_{t}(R)x
denote the maximally defined (on
[0,\infty)
) solution of the ordinary differential equation

(2.9)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{x}_{t}=R(x_{t})\\
x_{0}=x.\end{cases}

Note that since
R
is a polynomial vector field, it is locally Lipschitz, so the solution (
2.9
) exists locally in time depending on the initial condition
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
. For any
R\in\Poly(\mathbf{R}^{n})
,
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
f\in\mathcal{H}
, we denote by
\varphi_{t}(R,f)x
the maximally defined (on
[0,1]
) solution of

(2.10)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{x}_{t}=R(x_{t})+\sigma\dot{f}\\
x_{0}=x.\end{cases}

For similar reasons,
\varphi_{t}(R,f)x
also exists locally in time.

3.
Algebraic Structure and Scalings

There are multiple ways to rescale equation (
1.1
) to capture the behavior of the solution at time
t=0
. We consider two in this paper. While one keeps track of only power asymptotics, the other additionally keeps track of logarithmic corrections. In this section, we introduce a pair scaling that applies to both.

Define

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{Z}_{\geq 0}\times\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{Z}_{\geq 0}.

We call points in the set
\mathcal{S}

scalings
. We define an ordering on
\mathcal{S}
as follows: if
a,b\in\mathcal{S}
, then we write
a\preceq b
if
a^{1}<b^{1}
or if
a^{1}=b^{1}
and
a^{2}\geq b^{2}
. If
a\preceq b
and
a\neq b
, we write
a\prec b
. Note that
\preceq
is a total ordering on
\mathcal{S}
. It is convenient to augment the set
\mathcal{S}
with the
infinite scaling

a_{\infty}=\infty
which satisfies
b\prec a_{\infty}
for any
b\in\mathcal{S}
. We introduce a sum
+
on

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\infty}=\mathcal{S}\cup\{\infty\}

by

(3.3)

\displaystyle a+b:=\begin{cases}(a^{1}+b^{1},a^{2}+b^{2})&\text{ if }a,b\in\mathcal{S}\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}.

For
a\in\mathcal{S}_{\infty}
and
\ell\in\mathbf{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we also define

(3.4)

\displaystyle\ell a:=\begin{cases}(\ell a^{1},\ell a^{2})&\text{ if }a\in\mathcal{S}\\
\infty&\text{ if }a=\infty,\,\ell\geq 1\\
(0,0)&\text{ if }\ell=0\end{cases}.

For any monomial
m:\mathbf{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
given by

(3.5)

\displaystyle m(x)=m(x^{1},x^{2},\ldots,x^{k})=\alpha(x^{1})^{n_{1}}(x^{2})^{n_{2}}\cdots(x^{k})^{n_{k}},\,\,\alpha\neq 0,n_{i}\in\mathbf{Z}_{\geq 0},

and scalings
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{\infty}
, we define the scaling

(3.6)

\displaystyle m(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k}):=n_{1}a_{1}+n_{2}a_{2}+\cdots+n_{k}a_{k}.

If
\alpha=0
so that
m\equiv 0
is the zero polynomial, then we define

\displaystyle m(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k})=\infty

regardless of the choice of
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{\infty}
. We recall that if
p:\mathbf{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
is a polynomial, then we can write

(3.7)

\displaystyle p=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}m_{j}

for some integer
\ell\geq 1
and monomials
m_{j}:\mathbf{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
,
j=1,2,\ldots,\ell
. We say that
p
is written in
standard form
if
\ell
in (
3.7
) is minimal; that is,
p
is expressed as a sum of a minimal number of monomials. This way, no cancellations of monomials can occur. For any polynomial
p:\mathbf{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
written in standard form as in (
3.7
) and any
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in\mathscr{S}_{\infty}
, we define

\displaystyle p(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})=\min_{j=1,2,\ldots,\ell}m_{j}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}).

Also, if
p:\mathbf{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{j}
is a vector of polynomials with
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{j})
,
p_{\ell}:\mathbf{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
, then for
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in\mathscr{S}_{\infty}
we define

\displaystyle p(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})=\min_{\ell=1,2,\ldots,j}p_{\ell}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}).

Next, let us elucidate the definitions above.

Example 3.1
.

Consider
p:\mathbf{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
given by

(3.8)

\displaystyle p(x^{1},x^{2})=x^{1}x^{2}+(x^{1})^{2}-x^{1}x^{2},

and suppose that
a_{1}=(2,0)
,
a_{2}=(0,1/2)
. Then
p(a_{1},a_{2})\neq(2,1/2)
since
p
is not in standard form. The standard form of
p
is
p(x^{1},x^{2})=(x^{1})^{2}
so that
p(a_{1},a_{2})=(4,0)
.

Example 3.2
.

Suppose
p:\mathbf{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{2}
is given by

(3.9)

\displaystyle p(x^{1},x^{2})=((x^{1})^{2}-(x^{2})^{2},2x^{1}x^{2}+(x^{2})^{2}+x^{1}+(x^{1})^{6}).

Let
a_{1}=a_{2}=(1,0)\in\mathcal{S}
. Then
p(a_{1},a_{2})=(1,0).
Note that, in this case,
p(a_{1},a_{2})
coincides with the minimal degree among all nontrivial monomials in each component of
p
. Alternatively, if
b_{1}=(1/2,1/2)
and
b_{2}=(0,1)
, then
p(b_{1},b_{2})=(0,2)
. In this case, the terms in
p
leading to
p(b_{1},b_{2})=(0,2)
are the two
(x^{2})^{2}
terms in (
3.9
).

For a fixed set of scalings, we also define what it means for a polynomial vector field to be homogeneous under that collection of scalings.

Definition 3.1
.

Suppose that
p:\mathbf{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{\ell}
,
p=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{\ell})
, is a vector of polynomials and
(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k})\in\mathcal{S}_{\infty}^{k}
is such that
p(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\neq\infty
. We say that
p
is
homogeneous under

(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})

of degree

p(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
if for every
j=1,2,\ldots,\ell
and every monomial
m_{j}
appearing in the standard form for
p_{j}
, we have
m_{j}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})=p(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
.

Example 3.3
.

Suppose
p(x^{1},x^{2})=((x^{1})^{2}-(x^{2})^{2},2x^{1}x^{2})
and
a_{1}=a_{2}=(1,0)
. Then
p
is homogeneous under
(a_{1},a_{2})
of degree
(2,0)
. Note that this coincides with our usual understanding of homogeneity. However, if
b_{1}=(0,1)
and
b_{2}=(1/2,0)
, then
p
is not homogeneous under
(b_{1},b_{2})
since all monomials in its standard form have distinct scalings under
(b_{1},b_{2})
.

Next, we show how the notion of scaling relates to the actual scaling under a given small parameter. That is, for any
\alpha\in(0,e^{-1})
and any
a=(a^{1},a^{2})\in\mathcal{S}
, define

(3.10)

\displaystyle\alpha^{a}:=\alpha^{a^{1}}(\log\log\alpha^{-1})^{a^{2}}.

Lemma 3.2
.

Fix
k,\ell\in I
and suppose
p:\mathbf{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{\ell}
is a vector of polynomials that is homogeneous under
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\in\mathcal{S}_{\infty}^{k}
of degree
p_{*}:=p(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\neq\infty
. Then for any
\epsilon\in(0,e^{-1})
and
x=(x^{1},\ldots,x^{k})\in\mathbf{R}^{k}
we have

\displaystyle p\big(\epsilon^{a_{1}}x^{1},\ldots,\epsilon^{a_{k}}x^{k}\big)=\epsilon^{p_{*}}p(x^{1},\ldots,x^{k}).

Proof.

It suffices to prove the result for monomials
m:\mathbf{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
of the form

(3.11)

\displaystyle m(x^{1},\ldots,x^{k})=\alpha(x^{1})^{n_{1}}(x^{2})^{n_{2}}\cdots(x^{k})^{n_{k}},\,\,\alpha\neq 0,n_{i}\in\mathbf{Z}_{\geq 0}.

Clearly, any such monomial is homogeneous under any
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\in\mathcal{S}_{\infty}^{k}
of degree
m_{*}=m(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})=n_{1}a_{1}+\cdots+n_{k}a_{k}
provided
m(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\prec\infty
. By the condition
m(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\prec\infty
, we may assume without loss of generality that
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\in\mathcal{S}^{k}
. Finally, observe that for any
\epsilon\in(0,e^{-1})
we have

\displaystyle m\big(\epsilon^{a_{1}}x^{1},\ldots,\epsilon^{a_{k}}x^{k}\big)=\epsilon^{n_{1}a_{1}+\cdots+n_{k}a_{k}}m(x^{1},\ldots,x^{k})=\epsilon^{m_{*}}m(x^{1},\ldots,x^{k}).

∎

4.
Laws of the iterated logarithm at time zero

In this section, we use the pair scalings from
\mathscr{S}_{\infty}
introduced in (
3.2
) to determine a functional law of the iterated logarithm for the process (
1.1
) at time zero. We proceed by induction, starting from the asymptotically dominant directions, and then propagating to the remaining directions with the help of the ordering
\preceq
introduced in Section
3
. Below, we extend the definition of
\text{proj}_{1}
to include in its domain the point
\infty\in\mathscr{S}_{\infty}
so that
\text{proj}_{1}\infty:=\infty
. Also, we use the usual generalization of
(<,>,\leq,\geq)
to the extended real numbers
\mathbf{R}\cup\{\infty\}
, so that we can compare
\text{proj}_{1}a
and
\text{proj}_{1}b
whenever
a,b\in\mathscr{S}_{\infty}
.

4.1.
Determination of scalings

Recall
P,\sigma
as in (
1.1
) and the set
I=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. To initialize the inductive procedure, first define

(4.1)

\displaystyle I_{0}=\{j\in I\,:\,\sigma^{j}>0\}.

Note that
I_{0}
contains the indices of the directions in which noise is acting independently in (
1.1
).
For every
j\in I_{0}
, define
a_{0,j}\in\mathcal{S}
and
P_{\text{L}}^{j}:\mathbf{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
by

(4.2)

\displaystyle a_{0,j}=(1/2,1/2)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad P^{j}_{\text{L}}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n})=0.

Observe that for
j\in I_{0}
,
P^{j}_{\text{L}}
is clearly independent of all variables
x^{1},x^{2},\ldots,x^{n}
. For
j\notin I_{0}
, we set
a_{0,j}=\infty
. Also note that the scaling
(1/2,1/2)
corresponds to the correct scale for the Brownian motion at time zero, in the law of the iterated logarithm sense.

Inductively, for
\ell\geq 0
if
I_{\ell}\neq\emptyset
and if either
I\setminus\cup_{k=0}^{\ell}I_{k}=\emptyset
or
P^{j}(a_{\ell,1},\ldots,a_{\ell,n})=\infty
for all
j\in I\setminus\cup_{k=0}^{\ell}I_{k}
, the procedure stops and we set
I_{j}=\emptyset
for
j\geq\ell+1
. Otherwise, define

\displaystyle m_{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\min\{\text{proj}_{1}P^{j}(a_{\ell,1},\ldots,a_{\ell,n})\,:\,j\in I\setminus\cup_{k=0}^{\ell}I_{k}\},

\displaystyle I_{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\{j\in I\setminus\cup_{k=0}^{\ell}I_{k}\,:\,\text{proj}_{1}P^{j}(a_{\ell,1},\ldots,a_{\ell,n})=m_{\ell+1}\},

and set

(4.3)

\displaystyle a_{\ell+1,j}=\begin{cases}P^{j}(a_{\ell,1},\ldots,a_{\ell,n})+(1,0)&\text{ if }j\in I_{\ell+1}\\
a_{\ell,j}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

For
j\in I_{\ell+1}
, let
P^{j}_{\text{L}}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n})
be the homogeneous polynomial in standard form of degree
P^{j}(a_{\ell,1},\ldots,a_{\ell,n})
which satisfies

(4.4)

\displaystyle P^{j}_{\text{L}}(a_{\ell,1},\ldots,a_{\ell,n})\prec(P^{j}-P^{j}_{\text{L}})(a_{\ell,1},\ldots,a_{\ell,n}).

Note that, by definition, for
j\in I_{\ell+1}
,
P^{j}_{\text{L}}(x)=P^{j}_{\text{L}}(\pi_{\cup_{k=1}^{\ell}I_{k}}(x))
; that is,
P^{j}_{\text{L}}
,
j\in I_{\ell+1}
, is a function depending only on the variables whose indices belong to
\cup_{k=1}^{\ell}I_{k}
.

Definition 4.1
.

If there exists
\ell\geq 0
such that

(4.5)

\displaystyle\bigcup_{k=0}^{\ell}I_{k}=I

we call the system (
1.1
)
noise propagating
. Otherwise, we call the system (
1.1
)
noise defective
. If (
1.1
) is noise propagating, let
\text{dim}(P,\sigma)
be the smallest nonnegative integer such that

\displaystyle\bigcup_{k=0}^{\text{dim}(P,\sigma)}I_{k}=I

and define, for
j\in I
,

(4.6)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}_{j}:=a_{\text{dim}(P,\sigma),j}.

We call
\text{dim}(P,\sigma)
the
dimension of propagation
. When
P
and
\sigma
are clear from context, we simply write
\dim
for
\dim(P,\sigma)
.

Example 4.1
.

Suppose that
n=2k
for some
k\in\mathbf{N}
and
U:\mathbf{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
is a polynomial. Consider the equation on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
for
x_{t}=(q_{t},p_{t})\in\mathbf{R}^{k}\times\mathbf{R}^{k}
given by

(4.7)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dq_{t}=p_{t}\,dt&\\
dp_{t}=-p_{t}\,dt-\nabla U(q_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2}\,dB_{t}&\\
x_{0}=(q_{0},p_{0})=(0,0)\end{cases}

where
B_{t}=(B_{t}^{1},\ldots,B_{t}^{k})
is a standard
k
-dimensional Brownian motion. Here,
I_{0}=\{k+1,\ldots,2k\}
and
I_{1}=\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
. Thus the system (
4.7
) is noise propagating with
\dim=1
. Furthermore,

(4.8)

\displaystyle P^{j}_{\text{L}}(q,p)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }j\in I_{0}\\
p^{j}&\text{ if }j\in I_{1}\end{cases}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathfrak{a}_{j}=\begin{cases}(1/2,1/2)&\text{ if }j\in I_{0}\\
(3/2,1/2)&\text{ if }j\in I_{1}.\end{cases}

Example 4.2
.

Consider the same setup as in Example
4.1
, but this time assume that the process
x_{t}=(q_{t},p_{t})
starts from a general initial condition
x_{0}=x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
with
p_{0}\neq 0
; that is,
x_{t}
satisfies

(4.9)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dq_{t}=p_{t}\,dt\\
dp_{t}=-p_{t}\,dt-\nabla U(q_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2}\,dB_{t}\\
x_{0}=(q_{0},p_{0})\in\mathbf{R}^{n}.\end{cases}

Let
\bar{q}_{t}=q_{t}-q_{0}
and
\bar{p}_{t}=p_{t}-p_{0}
and note that
\bar{x}_{t}:=(\bar{q}_{t},\bar{p}_{t})
solves

(4.10)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}d\bar{q}_{t}=(\bar{p}_{t}+p_{0})\,dt\\
d\bar{p}_{t}=-(\bar{p}_{t}+p_{0})\,dt-\nabla U(\bar{q}_{t}+q_{0})\,dt+\sqrt{2}\,dB_{t}\\
(\bar{q}_{0},\bar{p}_{0})=(0,0)\in\mathbf{R}^{n}.\end{cases}

Thus, for
\bar{x}_{t}
the system (
4.10
) is also noise propagating with
I_{0}=\{k+1,\ldots,2k\}
,
I_{1}=\{j\in I\setminus I_{0}\,:\,p_{0}^{j}\neq 0\}
and
I_{2}=\{j\in I\setminus(I_{0}\cup I_{1})\,:\,p_{0}^{j}=0\}
. Furthermore,
\dim=2
if there exists
j
such that
p_{0}^{j}\neq 0
. Otherwise,
\dim=1
. Also,
P^{j}_{\text{L}}(x)=0
and
\mathfrak{a}_{j}=(1/2,1/2)
for
j\in I_{0}
. However, this time for
j\notin I_{0}
we have

\displaystyle P^{j}_{\text{L}}(q,p)=\begin{cases}p_{0}^{j}&\text{ if }p_{0}^{j}\neq 0\\
p^{j}&\text{ if }p_{0}^{j}=0\end{cases}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathfrak{a}_{j}=\begin{cases}(1,0)&\text{ if }p_{0}^{j}\neq 0\\
(3/2,1/2)&\text{ if }p_{0}^{j}=0.\end{cases}

Remark 4.2
.

In Example
4.1
and Example
4.2
, it is not necessary to assume that
U:\mathbf{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
is a polynomial. Indeed, so long as
U\in C^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{n})
the same conclusions hold since
B_{t}
still dominates the
p
-directions at time zero.

Example 4.3
.

In this example, we consider a stochastically perturbed Lorenz ’96 model. The model describes the evolution of
x_{t}=(x_{t}^{1},\ldots,x_{t}^{n})
,
n\geq 4
, satisfying

(4.11)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dx^{i}_{t}=[(x^{i+1}_{t}-x^{i-2}_{t})x^{i-1}_{t}-x^{i}_{t}]+\sigma^{i}\,dB^{i}\\
x^{i}_{0}=0\end{cases}

where
\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2}>0
and
\sigma^{i}=0
for
i=3,\ldots,n
. In (
4.11
),
B_{t}=(B^{1}_{t},\ldots,B^{n}_{t})
is a standard
n
-dimensional Brownian motion, and we assume that the coordinates satisfy the periodcity conditions

\displaystyle x^{-1}:=x^{n-1},\qquad x^{0}:=x^{n}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad x^{n+1}=x^{1}.

In this case,
I_{0}=\{1,2\}
and
I_{j-2}=\{j\}
for
j=3,4,\ldots,n
with
P_{\text{L}}^{1}=P_{\text{L}}^{2}=0
and

(4.12)

\displaystyle P_{\text{L}}^{j}(x)=\begin{cases}-x^{j-2}x^{j-1}&\text{ if }j=3,4,\ldots,n-1\\
x^{1}x^{n-1}&\text{ if }j=n.\end{cases}

Thus the system (
4.11
) is noise propagating with
\dim=n-2
. Also,
\mathfrak{a}_{1}=\mathfrak{a}_{2}=(1/2,1/2)
and
\mathfrak{a}_{j}
,
j=3,4,\ldots,n-1
, satisfy the Fibonacci-like pattern

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}_{j}=\mathfrak{a}_{j-1}+\mathfrak{a}_{j-2}+(1,0).

The scaling
\mathfrak{a}_{n}
, however, satisfies a slightly different relation

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}_{n}=(3/2,1/2)+\mathfrak{a}_{n-1}.

Remark 4.3
.

Observe that if we assumed that
\sigma^{1}=0
or
\sigma^{2}=0
and
\sigma_{3}=\cdots=\sigma_{n}=0
in (
4.11
), then the system is noise defective.

Example 4.4
.

Next, we consider finite-dimensional approximations of the Sabra model, which was originally proposed as a shell model for turbulence
[
22
]
. Fixing real parameters
\delta\in(0,2)\setminus\{1\}
and
\alpha^{i},\beta^{i}\geq 0
, the finite-dimensional projection of the shell-model is given, according to
[
3
]
, by the following evolution of
u=(u^{1},u^{2},\ldots,u^{J})\in\mathbf{C}^{J}
:

(4.13)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}du^{m}=i2^{m}\Big(\overline{u_{m+1}}u_{m+2}-\delta\overline{u_{m-1}}u_{m+1}-\frac{\delta-1}{4}u_{m-2}u_{m-1}\Big)\,dt-\delta 2^{2m}u^{m}\,dt+\alpha^{m}\,dB^{m}+i\beta^{m}\,dW^{m}\\
u_{0}=0\in\mathbf{C}^{J},\end{cases}

where
\{B^{m},W^{m}\,:\,m=1,2,\ldots,J\}
is a collection of mutually independent standard, real-valued Brownian motions. We assume that
u^{-1}=u^{0}=u^{J+1}=u^{J+2}=0
and that
\alpha^{1},\beta^{1},\alpha^{2},\beta^{2}>0
, while
\alpha^{m}=\beta^{m}=0
for
2\leq m\leq J
. Furthermore, although each component
u^{m}
in the projected Sabra model is complex-valued and, strictly speaking, the calculations performed above concern only real values, we will adapt the scaling analysis above and maintain the complex-valued nature of the equation.

Under our assumptions, it follows that (
4.13
) is noise propagating with complex-valued vector field
P_{\text{L}}
defined by

(4.14)

\displaystyle P_{\text{L}}^{m}(u)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }m=1,2\\
-i2^{m}\frac{\delta-1}{4}u^{m-2}u^{m-1}&\text{ if }m=3,\ldots,J\end{cases}

Furthermore, because the real and imaginary parts of every direction in this equation have the same scaling, we can extend the definition
\mathfrak{a}_{m}
to be the scaling for the complex direction
u^{m}
. In particular,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}_{m}=\begin{cases}(1/2,1/2)&\text{ if }m=1,2\\
\mathfrak{a}_{m-2}+\mathfrak{a}_{m-1}+(1,0)&\text{ if }m=3,\ldots,J.\end{cases}

Example 4.5
.

This example shows that noise propagation can be weaker than hypoellipticity as in
[
12
]
. Consider the following SDE on
\mathbf{R}^{3}
started from the origin:

(4.15)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dx^{1}&=dB^{1}\\
dx^{2}&=x^{1}\,dt\\
dx^{3}&=x^{1}\,dt.\end{cases}

Then (
4.15
) is noise propagating with
\dim=1
, but the process lives on the subset

\{(x,y,y)\,:\,x,y\in\mathbf{R}\}\subset\mathbf{R}^{3}.

Thus the associated Markov kernel does is not absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\mathbf{R}^{3}
. Hence, the diffusion is not hypoelliptic.

4.2.
Consequences of scaling structure

Suppose that the system (
1.1
) is noise propagating. For
\epsilon\in(0,e^{-1})
, define a family of processes
x_{\epsilon,t}=(x_{\epsilon,t}^{1},\ldots,x_{\epsilon,t}^{n})
by

(4.16)

\displaystyle x_{\epsilon,t}^{j}=\frac{x_{\epsilon t}^{j}}{\epsilon^{\mathfrak{a}_{j}}}.

where
\mathfrak{a}_{j}
is as in (
4.6
). Also, we let
P_{\text{L}}\in\Poly(\mathbf{R}^{n})
be such that its
j
th component is
P^{j}_{\text{L}}
. Define

(4.17)

\displaystyle B_{\epsilon,t}=\frac{B_{\epsilon t}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}.

One of our main results in this section is the following theorem, which states that the scaling calculated in the previous section gives the correct form at the level of the stochastic differential equation to have a functional law of the iterated logarithm. This connection is further elaborated below.

Theorem 4.4
.

Suppose that the system (
1.1
) is noise propagating. Then for all
t<\tau_{\infty}/\epsilon
,
x_{\epsilon,t}
solves an SDE of the form

(4.18)

\displaystyle dx_{\epsilon,t}=[P_{\text{\emph{L}}}(x_{\epsilon,t})+R_{\epsilon,\text{\emph{L}}}(x_{\epsilon,t})]\,dt+\frac{\sigma dB_{\epsilon,t}}{\epsilon^{(0,1/2)}}

where
R_{\epsilon,\text{\emph{L}}}\in\Poly(\mathbf{R}^{n})
is such that for all
C>0
,
\sup_{|x|\leq C}|R_{\epsilon,\text{\emph{L}}}(x)|\rightarrow 0
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
.

In order to prove Theorem
4.4
, we first need a technical auxiliary result.

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose (
1.1
) is noise propagating. Then
\mathfrak{a}_{m}\prec\mathfrak{a}_{j}
whenever
m\in I_{\ell}
and
j\in I_{\ell^{\prime}}
with
\ell<\ell^{\prime}
. Furthermore, for any
j\in I\setminus I_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle P^{j}_{\text{\emph{L}}}(\mathfrak{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{a}_{n})\prec(P^{j}-P^{j}_{\text{\emph{L}}})(\mathfrak{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{a}_{n}).

Lemma
4.5
is a consequence of the following result.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
2\leq k\leq n
,
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1}\in\mathcal{S}
, and suppose
q_{1},q_{2}:\mathbf{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
are polynomials with

(4.19)

\displaystyle q_{1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1},\infty_{n-k})\prec q_{2}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1},\infty_{n-k})

where
\infty_{n-k}\in(\mathscr{S}_{\infty})^{n-k}
is given by
\infty_{n-k}=(\infty,\infty,\ldots,\infty)
.
If
a_{k},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathcal{S}
are such that

q_{1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1},\infty_{n-k})\prec a_{m}

for every
m=k,\ldots,n
, then

\displaystyle q_{1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=q_{1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1},\infty_{n-k})\prec q_{2}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}).

Proof.

Letting
0_{n-k}
denote the zero vector in
\mathbf{R}^{n-k}
, observe that we can write

\displaystyle q_{1}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n})=R_{11}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{k-1},0_{n-k})+\sum_{j=k}^{n}x^{j}R_{1j}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n}),

\displaystyle q_{2}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n})=R_{21}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{k-1},0_{n-k})+\sum_{j=k}^{n}x^{j}R_{2j}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n})

for some polynomials
R_{ij}
. If we set
\hat{R}_{11}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1})=R_{11}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1},0_{n-k})
and

\hat{R}_{21}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1})=R_{21}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1},0_{n-k})
, then
q_{1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1},\infty_{n-k})\prec a_{m}
for every
m=k,\ldots,n
implies

\displaystyle q_{1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1},\infty_{n-k})=\hat{R}_{11}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1})=q_{1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}).

On the other hand, either

\displaystyle q_{2}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=\hat{R}_{21}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1})=q_{2}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1},\infty_{n-k}),

in which case the conclusion follows from (
4.19
),
or for some
m\in\{k,\ldots,n\}

\displaystyle a_{m}\preceq q_{2}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=a_{m}+\hat{R}_{2m}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}).

and the conclusion follows from
q_{1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1},\infty_{n-k})\prec a_{m}
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.5
.

For the first statement, by transitivity of
\prec
it suffices to show that
\mathfrak{a}_{m}\prec\mathfrak{a}_{j}
if
m\in I_{\ell}
and
j\in I_{\ell+1}
. If
\ell=0
, then
\mathfrak{a}_{m}=(1/2,1/2)
and
by (
4.3
) we have

\mathfrak{a}_{j}\succeq(1,0)
, and therefore
\mathfrak{a}_{m}\prec\mathfrak{a}_{j}
. If
\ell\geq 1
, then without loss of generality, assume that

\displaystyle\bigcup_{s=0}^{\ell-1}I_{s}=\{1,\ldots,k\}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad I_{\ell}=\{k+1,\ldots,k+j_{\ell}\}

for some
k\geq 1
and
j_{\ell}\geq 0
.
By (
4.3
), we obtain

\mathfrak{a}_{j}=a_{\ell+1,j}=P^{j}(a_{\ell,1},\ldots,a_{\ell,n})+(1,0)
. Then, there are polynomials
Q:\mathbf{R}^{k}\to\mathbf{R}
,
(R_{m})_{m=k+1}^{k+j_{\ell}}:\mathbf{R}^{n}\to\mathbf{R}
,
(S_{m})_{m=k+j_{\ell}+1}^{n}:\mathbf{R}^{n}\to\mathbf{R}
such that

(4.20)

P^{j}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n})=Q(x^{1},\ldots,x^{k})+\sum_{i=k+1}^{k+j_{\ell}}x^{i}R_{i}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n})+\sum_{i=k+j_{\ell}+1}^{n}x^{i}S_{i}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n})\,.

Since
i\not\in\bigcup_{s=0}^{\ell}I_{s}
for
i>k+j_{\ell}
, we obtain that
\text{proj}_{1}(a_{\ell,i}S_{i}(a_{\ell,1},\ldots,a_{\ell,n}))=\infty
, whenever
S_{i}\neq 0
, and therefore
we do not have to consider the last sum in (
4.20
).
If
Q\neq 0
, then

\displaystyle\text{proj}_{1}P^{j}(a_{\ell,1},\ldots,a_{\ell,n})

\displaystyle=\min_{i\in\{k+1,\ldots,k+j_{\ell}\},R_{i}\neq 0}\{\text{proj}_{1}Q(a_{\ell,1},\ldots,a_{\ell,k}),\text{proj}_{1}(a_{\ell,i}+R_{i}(a_{\ell,1},\ldots,a_{\ell,n}))\}

\displaystyle\geq\min_{i\in\{k+1,\ldots,k+j_{\ell}\},R_{i}\neq 0}\{\text{proj}_{1}Q(a_{\ell-1,1},\ldots,a_{\ell-1,k}),\text{proj}_{1}a_{\ell,i}\}

\displaystyle>\min_{i\in\{k+1,\ldots,k+j_{\ell}\},R_{i}\neq 0}\{\text{proj}_{1}a_{\ell,m}-1,\text{proj}_{1}a_{\ell,i}\}=\text{proj}_{1}a_{\ell,m}-1\,,

where we used that
a_{\ell,r}=a_{\ell-1,r}
for
r\in\{1,\ldots,k\}=\bigcup_{s=0}^{\ell-1}I_{s}
and the fact that
Q
is not a minimizer in the definition of
m_{\ell}
.
If
Q=0
, then there is an
i
such that
R_{i}\neq 0
, and
we obtain

\displaystyle\text{proj}_{1}P^{j}(a_{\ell,1},\ldots,a_{\ell,n})

\displaystyle=\min_{i\in\{1,\ldots,k\},R_{i}\neq 0}\{\text{proj}_{1}(a_{\ell,i}+R_{i}(a_{\ell,1},\ldots,a_{\ell,n}))\}

\displaystyle>\min_{i\in\{1,\ldots,k\},R_{i}\neq 0}\{\text{proj}_{1}a_{\ell,i}\}>\text{proj}_{1}a_{\ell,m}\,,

and the first statement follows from
\text{proj}_{1}a_{\ell+1,j}=\text{proj}_{1}(P^{j}(a_{\ell,1},\ldots,a_{\ell,n}))+1>\text{proj}_{1}a_{\ell,m}
.

For the second statement fix any
j\in I\setminus I_{0}
. Since the system is noise propagating, there exists
\ell\geq 0
such that
j\in I_{\ell+1}
, and there exists
P^{j}_{L}
such that (
4.4
) holds.
Assume without loss of generality that
\bigcup_{i=0}^{\ell}I_{i}=\{1,\ldots,k\}
for some
k\geq 1
, for otherwise we permute the indices.
By (
4.6
) we have that

a_{\ell,m}=\mathfrak{a}_{m}
for any
m\leq k
and by the definition of
I_{\ell}
, it follows that
\mathfrak{a}_{\ell,m}=\infty
if
m>k
.

Define
q_{1}=P_{\text{L}}^{j}
and
q_{2}=P^{j}-P^{j}_{\text{L}}
and by (
4.4
), the assumption (
4.19
) holds and by the definition of
P_{\text{L}}^{j}

\displaystyle q_{1}(\mathfrak{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{a}_{k},\infty_{n-k})=P^{j}_{\text{L}}(a_{\ell,1},\ldots,\mathfrak{a}_{\ell,n})=P^{j}(a_{\ell,1},\ldots,a_{\ell,n})=a_{\ell+1,j}-(1,0)\,.

In particular, for any
j\in I_{\ell+1}
we have
q_{1}(\mathfrak{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{a}_{k},\infty_{n-k})\prec a_{\ell+1,j}=\mathfrak{a}_{j}
. For
j\in I_{\ell^{\prime}}
with
\ell^{\prime}\geq\ell+1
,

q_{1}(\mathfrak{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{a}_{k},\infty_{n-k})\prec\mathfrak{a}_{j}
follows from the first statement of the lemma. Finally, the second assertion of the lemma
is a consequence of Proposition
4.6
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

Supposing that
\epsilon t<\tau_{\infty}
, we separate the proof into two cases.

Case 1:
j\in I_{0}
. Then we have

\displaystyle dx_{\epsilon,t}^{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^{(1/2,1/2)}}P^{j}(x_{\epsilon t})\,dt+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{(0,1/2)}}\sigma^{j}dB_{\epsilon,t}^{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^{(1/2,1/2)}}P^{j}\big(\epsilon^{\mathfrak{a}_{1}}x_{\epsilon,t}^{1},\ldots,\epsilon^{\mathfrak{a}_{n}}x_{\epsilon,t}^{n}\big)\,dt+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{(0,1/2)}}\sigma^{j}dB_{\epsilon,t}^{j}.

Recalling that
P^{j}_{\text{L}}(x)\equiv 0
when
j\in I_{0}
, we define

\displaystyle R_{\epsilon,\text{L}}^{j}(x)=\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^{(1/2,1/2)}}P^{j}\big(\epsilon^{\mathfrak{a}_{1}}x^{1},\ldots,\epsilon^{\mathfrak{a}_{n}}x^{n}\big)

and note that, since
\epsilon/\epsilon^{(1/2,1/2)}\rightarrow 0
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
,
R_{\epsilon,\text{L}}^{j}\rightarrow 0
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.

Case 2:
j\notin I_{0}
. In this case, we note that

\displaystyle dx_{t}^{j}=P^{j}(x_{t})\,dt

since
\sigma^{j}=0
for all
j\notin I_{0}
. Hence,

\displaystyle dx_{\epsilon,t}^{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^{\mathfrak{a}_{j}}}[P^{j}_{\text{L}}(x_{\epsilon t})+(P^{j}-P^{j}_{\text{L}})(x_{\epsilon t})]\,dt.

Next, by construction and Lemma
3.2
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^{\mathfrak{a}_{j}}}P^{j}_{\text{L}}\big(\epsilon^{\mathfrak{a}_{1}}x^{1},\ldots,\epsilon^{\mathfrak{a}_{n}}x^{n})=P_{\text{L}}^{j}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n}),

so that, by homogeneity,

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^{\mathfrak{a}_{j}}}P^{j}_{\text{L}}(x_{\epsilon t})=P^{j}_{\text{L}}(x_{\epsilon,t}^{1},\ldots,x_{\epsilon,t}^{n})=P^{j}_{\text{L}}(x_{\epsilon,t}).

Next, define for any
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
\epsilon>0

\displaystyle R_{\epsilon,\text{L}}^{j}(x):=\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^{\mathfrak{a}_{j}}}(P^{j}-P^{j}_{\text{L}})(\epsilon^{\mathfrak{a}_{1}}x^{1},\ldots,\epsilon^{\mathfrak{a}_{n}}x^{n}).

Since

P^{j}_{\text{L}}(\mathfrak{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{a}_{n})\prec(P^{j}-P^{j}_{\text{L}})(\mathfrak{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{a}_{n})

by Lemma
4.5
,
it follows that
R_{\epsilon,\text{L}}^{j}(x)\rightarrow 0
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.
∎

Considering the structure of equation (
4.18
), at first glance it may appear as though we have not captured the dynamics correctly as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
due to the presence of the small term, namely
1/\epsilon^{(0,1/2)}
, in front of the Brownian motion. However, this is not true. In fact, we are able to characterize the set of limit points of the trajectories
t\mapsto x_{\epsilon,t}
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
.

More precisely, suppose that (
1.1
) is noise propagating and consider the following control problem

(4.21)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{x}=P_{\text{L}}(x)+\sigma\dot{f}\\
x_{0}=x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}\end{cases}

where
f\in\mathcal{H}
. That is, we consider the family of trajectories
t\mapsto\varphi_{t}(P_{\text{L}},f)x
for
f\in\mathcal{H}
. Because
P_{\text{L}}
is a polynomial vector field, and thus may contain nonlinearities, it is not completely clear that
t\mapsto\varphi_{t}(P_{\text{L}},f)x
is defined for all times
t\in[0,1]
. However,
P_{\text{L}}
has a specific structure and we claim that (
4.21
) is explicitly integrable, which in particular implies that
t\mapsto\varphi_{t}(P_{\text{L}},f)x
is defined on
[0,1]
for any
f\in\mathcal{H}
. Specifically, fixing
f\in\mathscr{H}
, recall that for
j\in I_{0}
we have
P_{\text{L}}^{j}=0
. Hence, for
j\in I_{0}

(4.22)

\displaystyle\varphi_{t}^{j}(P_{\text{L}},f)x=x^{j}+\sigma^{j}f_{t}^{j},

which is well-defined and bounded for all times
t\in[0,1]
. Next, for
j\in I_{k+1}
with
1\leq k+1\leq\dim(P,\sigma)
, we have
\sigma^{j}=0
and

(4.23)

\displaystyle P^{j}_{\text{L}}(x)=P^{j}_{\text{L}}\big(\pi_{\cup_{\ell=0}^{k}I_{\ell}}(x)\big)

so that

(4.24)

\displaystyle\varphi_{t}^{j}(P_{\text{L}},f)x=x^{j}+\int_{0}^{t}P^{j}_{\text{L}}(\pi_{\cup_{\ell=0}^{k}I_{\ell}}(\varphi_{s}(P_{\text{L}},f)x))\,ds.

Proceeding by induction, we obtain that the integrand is a bounded function, and therefore
\varphi_{t}^{j}(P_{L},f)x
is bounded for any
j
.
Thus,
t\mapsto\varphi_{t}(P_{\text{L}},f)x
is well-defined on
[0,1]
for any
f\in\mathcal{H},\,x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
by the structure of
P_{\text{L}}
.

Define the
Cramer transform

\lambda:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
and a subset
\mathcal{I}\subset\mathcal{H}
by

\displaystyle\lambda(g)=\inf\bigg\{\tfrac{1}{2}\textstyle{\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{f}_{s}|^{2}\,dt}\,:\,f\in\mathcal{H}\text{ and }\varphi_{\cdot}(P_{\text{L}},f)0=g_{\cdot}\bigg\}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{I}=\{g\in\mathcal{H}\,:\,\lambda(g)\leq 1\}.

As a corollary of Theorem
4.4
, we obtain the following result which relates the solution of (
4.18
) and the trajectories of the control problem (
4.21
).

.

Suppose that the equation (
1.1
) is noise propagating. Then there exists a constant

such that for
\mathbf{P}
-almost every
\omega

(i)

The set

\mathcal{E}
.

(ii)

as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
.

(iii)

For every

, there exists a subsequence
\epsilon_{j}(\omega,h)\downarrow 0
as

such that

\displaystyle d(x_{\epsilon_{j}(\omega,h)}(\omega),h)\rightarrow 0\quad\text{ as }\quad j\rightarrow\infty.

Proof.

This result follows by combining Theorem
4.4
above with
[
7
, Theorem 2.6]
. The fact that the noise in equation (

.
∎

5.
Scaling in law

The goal of this section is to establish the asymptotic behavior of the process at time zero in the sense of distributions. Similar to Section
4.1
, we use scalings
\mathscr{S}_{\infty}
as introduced in (
3.2
), but we neglect logarithmic corrections, which corresponds to the second component in the scaling. In particular, we employ
power scalings
; that is,
a=(a^{1},a^{2})\in\mathcal{S}
with
a^{2}=0
. As in Section
4.1
, we proceed inductively in order to identify the correct scale in each direction. Although the results are, not surprising, similar, we remark that the power scaling algorithm can retain more terms compared to the case of the LIL scaling, and therefore the limiting dynamics may be different. See Example
5.1
for a concrete example.

5.1.
Determination of scalings

First recall that
I_{0}\subset I
is given by

\displaystyle I_{0}=\{j\,:\,\sigma^{j}>0\}.

For any
j\in I
, define
b_{0,j}\in\mathcal{S}_{\infty}
by

(5.1)

\displaystyle b_{0,j}=\begin{cases}(1/2,0)&\text{ if }j\in J_{0}\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

For any
j\in I_{0}
, define
P_{\text{D}}^{j}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n})=0
. Also, note that
\text{proj}_{1}b_{0,j}=\text{proj}_{1}a_{0,j}
for all
j
.

Inductively, for
\ell\geq 0
if either
I\setminus\cup_{k=0}^{\ell}I_{k}=\emptyset
or
P^{j}(b_{\ell,1},\ldots,b_{\ell,n})=\infty
for all
j\in I\setminus\cup_{k=0}^{\ell}I_{\ell}
, then the procedure stops and we set
I_{j}=\emptyset
for
j\geq\ell+1
. Otherwise, recalling the definitions of
m_{\ell+1}
and
I_{\ell+1}
we have

\displaystyle m_{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\min\{\text{proj}_{1}P^{j}(b_{\ell,1},\ldots,b_{\ell,n})\,:\,j\in I\setminus\cup_{k=0}^{\ell}I_{k}\},

\displaystyle I_{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\{j\in I\setminus\cup_{k=0}^{\ell}I_{k}\,:\,\text{proj}_{1}P^{j}(b_{\ell,1},\ldots,b_{\ell,n})=m_{\ell+1}\}.

Set

(5.2)

\displaystyle b_{\ell+1,j}=\begin{cases}(m_{\ell+1},0)+(1,0)&\text{ if }j\in I_{k+1}\\
b_{\ell,j}&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

For
j\in I_{\ell+1}
, let
P^{j}_{\text{D}}
denote the polynomial in
P^{j}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n})
which is homogeneous of degree
(m_{\ell+1},0)
and such that

(m_{\ell+1},0)\prec(P^{j}-P^{j}_{\text{D}})(b_{\ell,1},\ldots,b_{\ell,n}).

Supposing that the system is noise propagating, the inductive process above must stop and we recall that

\displaystyle\bigcup_{k=0}^{\dim(P,\sigma)}I_{k}=I

where
\dim(P,\sigma)
is the dimension of propagation. For
j\in I
, setting

(5.3)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}_{j}=b_{\dim(P,\sigma),j},

we see that
\mathfrak{b}_{j}\in\mathcal{S}
and
\text{proj}_{1}\mathfrak{b}_{j}=\text{proj}_{1}\mathfrak{a}_{j}
for all
j
by construction.

Remark 5.1
.

It can be readily shown that for
j\in I_{0}\cup I_{1}
,
P^{j}_{\text{L}}=P^{j}_{\text{D}}
. Furthermore, for all
j
,
P^{j}_{\text{D}}
contains all terms in
P^{j}_{\text{L}}
. The second assertion follows since it is a weaker requirement for terms to have the same scaling under power scalings.

Example 5.1
.

This example shows that, in general,
P_{L}\neq P_{D}
.
Consider the following SDE on
\mathbf{R}^{4}

\displaystyle dx^{1}

\displaystyle=dB^{1}

\displaystyle dx^{2}

\displaystyle=dB^{2}

\displaystyle dx^{3}

\displaystyle=x^{1}x^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle dx^{4}

\displaystyle=x^{1}(x^{3})^{2}+x^{3}(x^{1})^{5}\,dt

where

are independent, standard Brownian motions on
\mathbf{R}
and the solution starts at the origin in
\mathbf{R}^{4}
. Then

,
I_{1}=\{3\}
, and
I_{2}=\{4\}
so that

. Also,
P^{1}_{\text{L}}=P^{2}_{\text{L}}=P^{1}_{\text{D}}=P^{2}_{\text{D}}=0
,
P^{3}_{\text{L}}=P_{\text{D}}^{3}=x^{1}x^{2}
, while
P^{4}_{\text{L}}=x^{3}(x^{1})^{5}
and

.

.

. Moreover, we have that

with

for any

, where the
\mathfrak{a}_{j}
’s are as in Example

.

.

Returning to the context of the projected Sabra model in Example

5.2.
Consequences of the distributional rescaling

Supposing that the system (
1.1
) is noise propagating, introduce a vector field
P_{\text{D}}\in\Poly(\mathbf{R}^{n})
with
j
th component given by
P_{\text{D}}^{j}
and process
t\mapsto y_{\epsilon,t}\in\mathcal{E}
defined by

(5.4)

\displaystyle y_{\epsilon,t}=\big(\tfrac{x_{\epsilon t}^{1}}{\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{1}}},\ldots,\tfrac{x_{\epsilon t}^{n}}{\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{n}}}\big).

Our next goal is to show that
t\mapsto y_{\epsilon,t}
converges as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
in distribution on the space
\mathcal{E}
. To prove this claim, we first state a lemma, which follows from Proposition
4.6
in a similar way to Lemma
4.5
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose that the system (
1.1
) is noise propagating. Then for any
j\in I

(5.5)

\displaystyle P^{j}_{\text{\emph{D}}}(\mathfrak{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{b}_{n})\prec(P^{j}-P^{j}_{\text{\emph{D}}})(\mathfrak{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{b}_{n}).

Recalling that
B_{\epsilon,t}=B_{\epsilon t}/\sqrt{\epsilon}
, we have the following result which gives a natural candidate for the distributional limit of
t\mapsto y_{\epsilon,t}
in
\mathcal{E}
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
. We formalize this assertion in Corollary
5.4
below.

Theorem 5.3
.

Suppose that equation (
1.1
) is noise propagating and recall the random variable
\tau_{\infty}
is the time of existence for the solution of (
1.1
). Then for any
t<\tau_{\infty}/\epsilon
we have

(5.6)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dy_{\epsilon,t}=P_{\text{\emph{D}}}(y_{\epsilon,t})\,dt+R_{\epsilon,\text{\emph{D}}}(y_{\epsilon,t})\,dt+\sigma dB_{\epsilon,t}\\
y_{\epsilon,0}=0\end{cases}

where
B_{\epsilon,t}
is as in (
4.17
) and
R_{\epsilon,\text{\emph{D}}}\in\Poly(\mathbf{R}^{n})
is such that
\sup_{|x|\leq C}|R_{\epsilon,\text{\emph{D}}}(x)|\rightarrow 0
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
for all
C>0
.

Proof.

Fix
t<\tau_{\infty}/\epsilon
. As in the proof of Theorem
4.4
, we split the proof into two cases.

Case 1: If
j\in I_{0}
, then since
\mathfrak{b}_{j}=(1/2,0)
, we have

\displaystyle dy_{\epsilon,t}^{j}

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{1/2}P^{j}(x_{\epsilon t})\,dt+\sigma^{j}\,dB_{\epsilon,t}^{j}

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{1/2}P^{j}(\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{1}}y_{\epsilon,t}^{1},\ldots,\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{n}}y_{\epsilon,t}^{n})\,dt+\sigma^{j}\,dB_{\epsilon,t}^{j}.

Setting
R_{\epsilon,\text{D}}^{j}(y):=\epsilon^{1/2}P^{j}(\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{1}}y^{1},\ldots,\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{n}}y^{n})
we find that
R_{\epsilon,\text{D}}^{j}\rightarrow 0
uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. This finishes the proof in Case 1.

Case 2: Suppose that
j\notin I_{0}
so that
\sigma^{j}=0
. By construction,

\displaystyle dy_{\epsilon,t}^{j}=\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{j}}}P^{j}(x_{\epsilon t})\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{j}}}P^{j}_{\text{D}}(x_{\epsilon t})\,dt+\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{j}}}(P^{j}-P^{j}_{\text{D}})(x_{\epsilon t})\,dt

\displaystyle=P^{j}_{\text{D}}(y_{\epsilon,t})\,dt+\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{j}}}(P^{j}-P^{j}_{\text{D}})(x_{\epsilon t})\,dt

\displaystyle=:P^{j}_{\text{D}}(y_{\epsilon,t})\,dt+R_{\epsilon,\text{D}}^{j}(y_{\epsilon,t})\,dt

By construction and Lemma
5.2
, we find that
R_{\epsilon,\text{D}}^{j}\rightarrow 0
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
uniformly on compact sets. This finishes the proof.

∎

Corollary 5.4
.

Suppose that (
1.1
) is noise propagating. Then the process
t\mapsto y_{\epsilon,t}\in\mathcal{E}
,
t\in[0,1]
, solving (
5.6
) converges in law to the solution of the SDE

(5.7)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dy_{t}=P_{\text{\emph{D}}}(y_{t})\,dt+\sigma\,dB_{t}\\
y_{0}=0.\end{cases}

Furthermore, the solution to (
5.7
) is nonexplosive.

Proof.

The second conclusion follows from the structure of the vector field
P_{\text{D}}
following a similar reasoning used in (
4.21
)-(
4.22
). In particular, the system (
5.7
) is explicitly integrable.

For the first conclusion, consider the following system

(5.8)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dz_{\epsilon,t}=P_{\text{D}}(z_{\epsilon,t})+R_{\epsilon,\text{D}}(z_{\epsilon,t})+\sigma\,dB_{t}\\
z_{\epsilon,0}=0.\end{cases}

Note that since
B_{t}
and
B_{\epsilon,t}
have the same distribution,
z_{\epsilon,t}
has the same distribution in the space
\mathcal{E}
as
y_{\epsilon,t}
. However,
z_{\epsilon,t}
is driven by the same Brownian motion as
y_{t}
.
Let
T_{\epsilon,k}=\inf\{t\geq 0\,:\,|z_{\epsilon,t}|\geq k\}
and
T_{k}=\inf\{t\geq 0\,:\,|y_{t}|\geq k\}
. Then for any
t\leq T_{\epsilon,2k}\wedge T_{k}\wedge 1
we have that

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|z_{\epsilon,s}-y_{s}|

\displaystyle\leq t\sup_{|z|\leq 2k}|R_{\epsilon,\text{D}}(z)|+\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{v\in[0,s]}|P_{\text{D}}(z_{\epsilon,v})-P_{\text{D}}(y_{v})|\,ds

\displaystyle\leq t\sup_{|z|\leq 2k}|R_{\epsilon,\text{D}}(z)|+C_{k}\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{v\in[0,s]}|z_{\epsilon,v}-y_{v}|\,ds

for some constant
C_{k}>0
. Grönwall’s inequality then implies that for any
t\leq T_{\epsilon,2k}\wedge T_{k}\wedge 1

(5.9)

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|z_{\epsilon,s}-y_{s}|\leq te^{C_{k}t}\sup_{|z|\leq 2k}|R_{\epsilon,\text{D}}(z)|

Since by Theorem
5.3
we have
R_{\epsilon,\text{D}}\rightarrow 0
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, taking
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
we find that
\liminf_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}T_{\epsilon,2k}\geq T_{k}
. Consequently,
d_{t}(z_{\epsilon,\cdot},y_{\cdot})\rightarrow 0
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
provided
t\leq T_{k}\wedge 1
. Since
y_{t}
is nonexplosive, for almost every
\omega\in\Omega
choose

such that

, and the result is proven.

∎

For any

\displaystyle\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{1}+\cdots+\mathfrak{b}_{n}}q_{\epsilon t}(S_{\epsilon,\text{\emph{D}}}^{-1}(y))\rightarrow q_{t,\text{\emph{D}}}(y)\qquad\text{ as }\qquad\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}.

\displaystyle A^{1}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\{y\in\mathbf{R}^{n}\,:\,\varphi_{t}(Q,f)x=y\text{ for some }f\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\},

(6.3)

\displaystyle A^{2}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\{y\in\mathbf{R}^{n}\,:\,\varphi_{t}(Q,f)x=y\text{ for some }f\in\mathcal{H}\}.

Clearly,
A^{1}_{t}(x)\subset A^{2}_{t}(x)
for any
0<t\leq 1
and
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
.

6.1.
Hörmander’s Condition and the Gramian

We first determine when
A_{t}^{1}(x)
contains a non-empty open set in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. To this end, we use the
Gramian
matrix associated to the control problem (
6.1
), as follows.

Fix
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
,
0<t\leq 1
and
f,f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in\mathcal{H}
. Define a matrix
F:=\begin{bmatrix}f_{1}&\cdots&f_{n}\end{bmatrix}
where
f_{i}
,
i=1,2,\ldots,n
, is viewed as a column vector. Define the mapping
\alpha\mapsto\tilde{g}_{t,F}(\alpha,f,x):\mathbf{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{n}
by

\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{t,F}(\alpha,f,x)=\varphi_{t}(Q,f+F\alpha)x

where
F\alpha
is the usual product of the matrix
F
and the column vector
\alpha\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
.
Letting
\tilde{g}_{t,F}^{\prime}(f,x):=D_{\alpha}\tilde{g}_{t,F}(\alpha,f,x)|_{\alpha=0}
, we observe that
\tilde{g}^{\prime}_{t,F}(f,x)
satisfies the
n\times n
matrix-valued ODE

(6.4)

\displaystyle\tilde{g}^{\prime}_{t,F}(f,x)=\int_{0}^{t}DQ(\varphi_{s}(Q,f)x)\tilde{g}^{\prime}_{s,F}(f,x)\,ds+\sigma F_{t}.

In order to obtain a representation for the solution of (
6.4
) and isolate its dependence on
F
, consider the
n\times n
matrix-valued solutions
J_{t}(f,x)
and
K_{t}(f,x)
of the linear equations

(6.5)

\displaystyle J_{t}(f,x)

\displaystyle=I+\int_{0}^{t}DQ(\varphi_{s}(Q,f)x)J_{s}(f,x)\,ds,

(6.6)

\displaystyle K_{t}(f,x)

\displaystyle=I-\int_{0}^{t}K_{s}(f,x)DQ(\varphi_{s}(Q,f)x)\,ds.

One can check that

\displaystyle\tfrac{d}{dt}(K_{t}(f,x)J_{t}(f,x))=0\qquad\text{ and }\qquad K_{0}(f,x)J_{0}(f,x)=I

so that both
K_{t}(f,x)
,
J_{t}(f,x)
are invertible for all
t\in[0,1]
and
K_{t}(f,x)=J_{t}(f,x)^{-1}
. Next, by uniqueness of solutions of ODEs, the matrix
\tilde{g}^{\prime}_{t,F}(f,x)
can be expressed in terms of the matrices
K_{t}(f,x)
and
J_{t}(f,x)
as

\displaystyle\tilde{g}^{\prime}_{t,F}(f,x)=J_{t}(f,x)\int_{0}^{t}K_{s}(f,x)\sigma\dot{F}_{s}\,ds

Note that, up to this point,
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{n}\in\mathcal{H}
were arbitrary. We now fix them so that our matrix
F=F^{\mu}(f)
is given by

(6.7)

\displaystyle F_{t}^{\mu}(f)=\frac{1}{\mu}\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{*}K_{s}(f,x)^{*}\,ds

where
*
denotes the Hermitian transpose and
\mu>0
is determined below. Since
t\mapsto K_{t}(f,x)
solves a linear equation with bounded coefficients, it is also bounded, and therefore the choice (
6.7
) ensures that
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in\mathcal{H}
.
Define

\displaystyle g^{\prime}_{\mu,t}(f,x)=\tilde{g}^{\prime}_{t,F^{\mu}(f)}(f,x).

and note that

\displaystyle g^{\prime}_{\mu,t}(f,x)=\frac{1}{\mu}J_{t}(f,x)\int_{0}^{t}K_{s}(f,x)\sigma\sigma^{*}K_{s}(f,x)^{*}\,ds.

We are interested in invertibility of
g^{\prime}_{\mu,t}(f,x)
, which is equivalent to invertibility of

\displaystyle G_{\mu,t}(f,x):=\frac{1}{\mu}\int_{0}^{t}K_{s}(f,x)\sigma\sigma^{*}K_{s}^{*}(f,x)\,ds

for any
\mu>0
. If
\mu=1
, we call
G_{t}(f,x):=G_{1,t}(f,x)
the
Gramian
associated to the control problem (
6.1
) with control
f\in\mathcal{H}
. Observe that
G_{t}(f,x)
is
n\times n
symmetric, nonnegative-definite matrix.

Theorem 6.1
.

Suppose that there exists
0<\lambda<1
,
s>0
, and
f\in\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}
such that the matrix
G_{s}(f,x)
is invertible. Then
A_{t}^{1}(x)
contains a nonempty, open set in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
for all
t\geq s
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda,s>0
,
f\in\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}
be as in the statement of the result. Note that
G_{s}(f,x)
being invertible is equivalent to the matrix

G_{\mu,s}(f,x)
being invertible for all
\mu>0
. Since
f\in\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}
with
\lambda<1
and
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{n}\in\mathcal{H}
are fixed in (
6.7
), there is
\mu=\mu(\lambda)>0
large enough such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{f}_{s}|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\dot{f}_{i,s}|^{2}\,ds\leq 1.

Next note that for
t\geq s
,
G_{\mu,t}(f,x)
is invertible since for
\mu>0
and
y\in\mathbf{R}^{n}_{\neq 0}

\displaystyle G_{\mu,t}(f,x)y\cdot y=\frac{1}{\mu}\int_{0}^{t}|\sigma^{*}K_{v}(f,x)^{*}y|^{2}\,dv

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\mu}\int_{0}^{s}|\sigma^{*}K_{v}(f,x)^{*}y|^{2}\,dv

\displaystyle=G_{\mu,s}(f,x)y\cdot y>0,

where in the last step we used symmetry and positive definiteness of
G_{\mu,s}(f,x)
. Finally, since
\alpha\mapsto\varphi_{t}(Q,f+F^{\mu}(f)\alpha)x
has derivative
G_{\mu,t}(f,x)
at
\alpha=0
, the assertion follows from the inverse function theorem.

∎

\displaystyle Z_{\ell_{1}},

.

Suppose that equation (

(i)

Remark 6.4
.

The idea for the proof below is inspired by the proof of

Proof of Theorem
6.3
.

Fix
t\in(0,1]
,
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
\lambda>0
. Then the proof of part (ii) follows from Malliavin’s proof of Hörmander’s hypoellipticity theorem
[
4

16
,
17

18

26

27

To establish part (i), let
\omega\in E_{\lambda,t}
and

. Then
\dot{f}_{s}=\lambda W_{s}(\omega)
and

follows. Next, we show that

is invertible. Write

\displaystyle C_{t,\lambda}(\omega)=\begin{bmatrix}C_{t,\lambda}^{1}(\omega)&C_{t,\lambda}^{2}(\omega)\\
C_{t,\lambda}^{3}(\omega)&C_{t,\lambda}^{4}(\omega)\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad K_{s,\lambda}(\omega)=\begin{bmatrix}K^{1}_{s,\lambda}(\omega)&K^{2}_{s,\lambda}(\omega)\\
K^{3}_{s,\lambda}(\omega)&K^{4}_{s,\lambda}(\omega)\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle C^{4}_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}K_{s}^{3}(K_{s}^{3})^{*}\,ds,\quad K_{s}^{3}=-\int_{0}^{s}K_{v}^{4}\lambda\sigma\,dv,\quad K^{4}_{s}=I-\int_{0}^{t}K^{4}_{s}DQ(y_{v}(\omega))\,dv.

In particular,

\displaystyle C_{t}^{4}=\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}K_{v}^{4}\sigma\,dv\int_{0}^{s}\sigma^{*}(K_{w}^{4})^{*}\,dw\,ds,

and consequently for any

Since

6.2.
Relating the control sets

Recalling the definitions (

and

using scalings, when possible.

Definition 6.5
.

We call the control system (

)

if, for every

such that

\displaystyle\varphi_{t}^{j}(Q,\epsilon f)0=\epsilon^{\alpha_{j}}\varphi^{j}_{t}(Q,f)0

for all

.

Remark 6.6
.

The relationship (
6.14
) is different than the one afforded by the scaling analysis in previous sections, where we scaled both time and space.

Our first result provides us with some directions along which the dynamics of a component homogeneous systems moves for an appropriate control
f

Proposition 6.7
.

Suppose that (
1.1
) is noise propagating and (
6.1
) is component homogeneous. If for some

and

we have

then there exist
f\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
and

such that
\varphi_{t}(Q,f)0\cdot v>0
.

Proof.

By assumption there exists a sequence

such that
\varphi_{t}(Q,f_{\ell})0\rightarrow v
as
\ell\rightarrow\infty
. Choose
k\geq 1
large enough so that

for any
j
for which

. Since the system is component homogeneous, for any
\epsilon>0

\displaystyle\varphi_{t}(Q,\epsilon f_{k})0\cdot v=\sum_{j:v^{j}\neq 0}v_{j}\varphi_{t}^{j}(Q,\epsilon f_{k})0=\sum_{j\,:\,v^{j}\neq 0}\epsilon^{\alpha_{j}}v^{j}\varphi_{t}^{j}(Q,f_{k})0>0.

Picking

∎

The following lemma provides a simple criterion to verify whether system (

Lemma 6.9

6.7
and Theorem
6.8
.

For convenience in this section, we closely follow the presentation in
[
10
]
which uses parametrized local semigroups in place of vector fields.

7.1.
Parameterized continuous local semigroups and the uniform saturate

Recalling that
\Delta\notin\mathbf{R}^{n}
is a
death state
and fixing an auxiliary metric space
(M,d_{M})
, we begin with a definition.

Definition 7.1
.

We call a mapping

\displaystyle(t,x,\mu)\mapsto\varphi_{t}^{\mu}x:[0,\infty)\times\mathbf{R}^{n}\times M\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{n}\cup\{\Delta\}

a
parameterized family of continuous local semigroups on

\mathbf{R}^{n}

parameterized by

M
if for every
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
\mu\in M
, there exists a time
t_{x,\mu}\in(0,\infty]
, called the
time of existence
, such that the following conditions are satisfied:

(i)

For
t\in[0,t_{x,\mu})
,
\varphi_{t}^{\mu}x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
and for
t\geq t_{x,\mu}
,
\varphi_{t}^{\mu}x=\Delta
.

(ii)

\varphi_{0}^{\mu}x=x
and for all
t,s\in[0,t_{x,\mu})
with
t+s\in[0,t_{x,\mu})
, we have
t\in[0,t_{\varphi_{s}^{\mu}x,\mu})
and
\varphi_{t+s}^{\mu}x=\varphi_{t}^{\mu}\varphi_{s}^{\mu}x
.

(iii)

For all
t\in[0,t_{x,\mu})
and
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that for all
(t^{\prime},x^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbf{R}^{n}\times M
with

|t-t^{\prime}|+|x-x^{\prime}|+d_{M}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})<\delta

we have
t^{\prime}\in[0,t_{x^{\prime},\mu^{\prime}})
and

|\varphi_{t}^{\mu}x-\varphi_{t^{\prime}}^{\mu^{\prime}}x^{\prime}|<\epsilon.

Before proceeding further, we first provide an illustrative example that connects Definition
7.1
with the examples in the sections above.

Example 7.1
.

Suppose that the system (
1.1
) is noise propagating and fix
Q\in\{P_{\text{L}},P_{\text{D}}\}
. Consider the vector fields
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
given by

(7.3)

\displaystyle X_{0}(x)=Q(x)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad X_{j}(x)=e_{j},\,\,j=1,2,\ldots,n,

where we recall that
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
is the standard orthonormal basis of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.
Let
M=\mathbf{R}^{n}
with the usual Euclidean distance and recall that for fixed
x,\mu\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
:

t\mapsto\varphi_{t}(X_{0}+\textstyle{\sum_{j=1}^{n}}\sigma^{j}\mu^{j}X_{j})x

is the maximally-defined (on
[0,\infty)
) solution of the ODE

(7.4)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{x}_{t}=Q(x_{t})+\sigma\mu\\
x_{0}=x.\end{cases}

Since the system is noise propagating and the equation (
7.4
) is explicitly integrable, we can set
t_{x,\mu}=\infty
for all
x,\mu\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
since the time of explosion of (
7.4
) is infinite. One can then check that the mapping
(t,x,\mu)\mapsto\varphi_{t}(X_{0}+\textstyle{\sum_{j=1}^{n}}\sigma^{j}\mu^{j}X_{j})x
is a parameterized family of continuous local semigroups on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
parameterized by
M=\mathbf{R}^{n}
. Furthermore, to connect with the control problems in previous sections, for
t\in(0,1]
and fixed
x,\mu\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
, we note that

\varphi_{t}(X_{0}+\textstyle{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma^{j}\mu^{j}X_{j})}x=\varphi_{t}(Q,f_{\mu})x

where
f_{\mu}(t)=t\mu
(see below equation (
6.1
)).

Remark 7.2
.

Let
R\in\text{Poly}(\mathbf{R}^{n})
be arbitrary and
M=\mathbf{R}
. Then the mapping

\displaystyle(t,x,\alpha)\mapsto\varphi_{t}(\alpha R)x:[0,\infty)\times\mathbf{R}^{n}\times\mathbf{R}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{n}\cup\{\Delta\}

is a parameterized continuous local semigroup on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
parameterized by
\mathbf{R}
. Indeed, if
t_{x,\alpha}>0
denotes the time of explosion for
t\mapsto\varphi_{t}(\alpha R)x
, then the conditions of Definition
7.1
are met. Also, in a similar way, the mapping

\displaystyle(t,x)\mapsto\varphi_{t}(R)x:[0,\infty)\times\mathbf{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{n}\cup\{\Delta\}

can be viewed as a parameterized continuous local semigroup parameterized by
M=\mathbf{R}
by lifting the above map to

\displaystyle(t,x,\alpha)\mapsto\varphi_{t}(R)x:[0,\infty)\times\mathbf{R}^{n}\times\mathbf{R}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{n}\cup\{\Delta\}

and such lift is implicitly assumed below.

Let
\mathcal{F}
denote an arbitrary collection of parameterized continuous local semigroups on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. A generic element of
\mathcal{F}
is denoted by
\varphi
and
\mathcal{P}(\varphi)
denotes the parameter set of
\varphi
. We sometimes use the notation
(\varphi,M)\in\mathcal{F}
to indicate that
\varphi\in\mathcal{F}
satisfies
\mathcal{P}(\varphi)=M
. For
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
t>0
, let
A_{\mathcal{F}}(x,t)
be the set of points
y\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
such that there exist
\varphi^{1},\varphi^{2}\ldots,\varphi^{k}\in\mathcal{F}
, corresponding positive times
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{k}>0
for which
\sum_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}=t
and parameters
\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(\varphi^{1}),\ldots,\mu_{k}\in\mathcal{P}(\varphi^{k})
such that

(7.5)

\displaystyle y=\varphi_{t_{k}}^{k,\mu_{k}}\varphi_{t_{k-1}}^{k-1,\mu_{k-1}}\cdots\varphi_{t_{1}}^{1,\mu_{1}}x.

Note that
A_{\mathcal{F}}(x,t)
is the set of points that can be accessed using the trajectories in
\mathcal{F}
at time
t>0
starting from
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
. Next, define

(7.6)

\displaystyle A_{\mathcal{F}}(x,\leq t)=\bigcup_{0<s\leq t}A_{\mathcal{F}}(x,s),

which is the set of points that can be accessed using the trajectories in
\mathcal{F}
by time
t
starting at
x
. We call
\mathcal{F}

approximately controllable
if for every
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
t>0

\displaystyle\text{cl}(A_{\mathcal{F}}(x,t))=\mathbf{R}^{n}.

The set
\mathcal{F}
is called
exactly controllable
if for every
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
t>0

\displaystyle A_{\mathcal{F}}(x,t)=\mathbf{R}^{n}.

Remark 7.3
.

It is possible to discuss control trajectories without the use of parameter sets. However, parameter sets are useful as they allow one to uncover points in the sets
A_{\mathcal{F}}(x,t)
,
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
t>0
, given some knowledge of points in their closures
\text{cl}(A_{\mathcal{F}}(x,t))
,
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
t>0
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
be two collections of parameterized continuous local semigroups on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. We say that
\mathcal{F}

uniformly subsumes

\mathcal{G}
, denoted by
\mathcal{G}\preccurlyeq_{u}\mathcal{F}
, if for any
\psi\in\mathcal{G}
,
t,\epsilon>0
and any compact sets
D_{1}\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
,
D_{2}\subset\mathcal{P}(\psi)
there exists
\varphi^{1},\ldots,\varphi^{k}\in\mathcal{F}
, times
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{k}>0
with
\sum t_{j}\leq t
and continuous functions
f_{k}:D_{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\varphi^{k})
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in D_{1},\mu\in D_{2}}\Big|\psi^{\mu}_{t}x-\varphi^{k,f_{k}(\mu)}_{t_{k}}\cdots\varphi^{1,f_{1}(\mu)}_{t_{1}}x\Big|<\epsilon.

We say that
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
are
uniformly equivalent
, denoted by
\mathcal{F}\sim_{u}\mathcal{G}
, if
\mathcal{G}\preccurlyeq_{u}\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}\preccurlyeq_{u}\mathcal{G}
. The
uniform saturate
of
\mathcal{F}
, denoted by
\text{sat}_{u}(\mathcal{F})
, is defined by

(7.7)

\displaystyle\sat_{u}(\mathcal{F})=\bigcup_{\mathcal{G}\preccurlyeq_{u}\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{G}.

One can show that (cf.
[
10
, Lemma 3.21]
):

\sat_{u}(\mathcal{F})\sim_{u}\mathcal{F}.

The uniform saturate allows one to expand an initial collection
\mathcal{F}
of trajectories to include simpler ones, from which control properties of
\mathcal{F}
are more easily deduced. Of course, by using this approach, one only gains access to sets of the form
\cl(A_{\mathcal{F}}(x,\leq t))
,
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
,
t>0
. However, sometimes this information can be transferred back to the closures of exact time sets
\cl(A_{\mathcal{F}}(x,t))
,
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
,
t>0
. This is the content of the following result, which is proved in
[
10
, Lemma 3.13]
.

Lemma 7.4

(Conversion Lemma)
.

Suppose
U\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
is open with the property that

\displaystyle U\subset\text{\emph{cl}}(A_{\mathcal{F}}(x,\leq t))

for all
x\in U
and
t>0
. Then

\displaystyle U\subset\text{\emph{cl}}(A_{\mathcal{F}}(x,t))

for all
x\in U
and
t>0
. In particular, if
U=\mathbf{R}^{n}
, then
\mathcal{F}
is approximately controllable.

Next, once one characterizes points in the closed sets

\displaystyle\text{cl}(A_{\mathcal{F}}(x,t)),\,\,\,x\in\mathbf{R}^{n},\,\,\,t>0,

it is natural to wonder which points in
\text{cl}(A_{\mathcal{F}}(x,t))
belong to
A_{\mathcal{F}}(x,t)
. This is the reason for the parameter set and the use of
uniform
saturation above. In essence, the uniformity in the above construction allows us to “wiggle” the control slightly to go from approximate to exact controllability. See
[
10
, Section 3]
for further details, which are partially summarized in Corollary
7.5
below.

Given any nonempty
M\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
, we let
(\rho,M)
denote the
ray semigroup parameterized by

M
; that is, for any
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
,
t>0
and
\mu\in M
:

(7.8)

\displaystyle\rho_{t}^{\mu}x:=x+t\mu.

As a special case of
[
10
, Corollary 3.35]
, we have:

Corollary 7.5
.

Let
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\}
be any basis of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
and suppose
(\rho,\text{\emph{span}}(b_{j}))\in\text{\emph{sat}}_{u}(\mathcal{F})
for all
j
. Then
\mathcal{F}
is exactly controllable on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.

Using Corollary
7.5
, the goal is to use scaling arguments to produce controllability results for
\mathcal{F}
.

7.2.
Saturating trajectories

In this section, we focus on a more concrete setting to maintain simplicity of discussion. Fix
V_{0}\in\text{Poly}(\mathbf{R}^{n})
and
V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{k}\in\text{Cons}(\mathbf{R}^{n})
,
k\leq n
, and for
(t,x,\mu)\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbf{R}^{n}\times\mathbf{R}^{n}
let

(7.9)

\displaystyle\varphi^{\mu}_{t}x=\varphi_{t}(V_{0}+\textstyle{\sum_{j=1}^{k}}\mu_{j}V_{j})x.

Note that
\varphi
is a parameterized family of continuous local semigroups on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
parameterized by
M=\mathbf{R}^{k}
with the usual Euclidean distance. We set

(7.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(V_{0};V_{1},\ldots,V_{k})=\{\varphi\}.

Example 7.2
.

Recalling the setting of Example
7.1
, consider
\mathcal{F}(X_{0};X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n})
and observe that a trajectory of the form (
7.5
) corresponds to choosing
f\in\mathcal{H}
with piecewise constant derivative
\dot{f}
. Consequently,

\displaystyle A_{\mathcal{F}}(x,t)\subset A_{t}^{2}(x)

for all
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
t\in(0,1]
. Furthermore, if for
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
t\in(0,1]
we define the set

(7.11)

\displaystyle A^{2}_{\leq t}(x)=\{y\in\mathbf{R}^{n}\,:\,\varphi_{s}(x,f)=y\text{ for some }f\in\mathcal{H}\text{ and }s\leq t\},

we also have that

\displaystyle A_{\mathcal{F}}(x,\leq t)\subset A_{\leq t}^{2}(x).

Our goal is to saturate the trajectories in
\mathcal{F}(V_{0};V_{1},\ldots,V_{k})
using two convenient scalings. The first scaling utilizes the dynamics in (
7.9
) with large controls on small time intervals.

Proposition 7.6
.

Suppose that
D_{1}\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
D_{2}\subset\mathbf{R}
are compact and
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
. Then:

(i)

For any
t>0
, there exist
\lambda_{*}=\lambda_{*}(t)>0
and
C_{1}(t)>0
finite such that
\lambda\geq\lambda_{*}
implies

\displaystyle\sup_{(s,x,\alpha)\in[0,t/\lambda]\times D_{1}\times D_{2}}|\varphi_{s}(V_{0}+\lambda\alpha V_{j})x|\leq C_{1}(t).

(ii)

Let
v_{j}\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
be the constant value of
V_{j}\in\text{\emph{Cons}}(\mathbf{R}^{n})
. For any
t>0
, there exists a constant
C_{2}(t)>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{(s,x,\alpha)\in[0,t]\times D_{1}\times D_{2}}|\varphi_{s/\lambda}(V_{0}+\lambda\alpha V_{j})x-\rho_{s}^{\alpha v_{j}}x|\leq\frac{C_{2}(t)}{\lambda}

for all
\lambda\geq\lambda_{*}
. In particular,
(\rho,\text{\emph{span}}(v_{j}))\in\sat_{u}(\mathcal{F}(V_{0};V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}))
.

Proof.

To prove part (i), fix
t>0
,
r>0
and
\alpha_{*}>0
such that
|x|\leq r
for
x\in D_{1}
and
|\alpha|\leq\alpha_{*}
for
\alpha\in D_{2}
. For
(x,\alpha)\in D_{1}\times D_{2}
, define
u_{t}^{\lambda}=\varphi_{t/\lambda}(V_{0}+\lambda\alpha V_{j})x
. Note that

(7.12)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}|u_{t}^{\lambda}|^{2}=\frac{1}{\lambda}V_{0}(u_{t}^{\lambda})\cdot u_{t}^{\lambda}+\alpha v_{j}\cdot u_{t}^{\lambda}\\
u_{0}^{\lambda}=x.\end{cases}

In particular, if
T_{k}^{\lambda}=\inf\{t\geq 0\,:|u_{t}^{\lambda}|\geq k\}
, for all
t<T_{k}^{\lambda}
there exists a constant
C_{k}>0
depending only on
k
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}|u_{t}^{\lambda}|^{2}\leq\frac{C_{k}}{\lambda}+|\alpha v_{j}||u_{t}^{\lambda}|\leq\frac{C_{k}}{\lambda}+\frac{|u_{t}^{\lambda}|^{2}}{2}+\frac{\alpha^{2}|v_{j}|^{2}}{2}\leq\frac{C_{k}}{\lambda}+\frac{|u_{t}^{\lambda}|^{2}}{2}+\frac{\alpha_{*}^{2}|v_{j}|^{2}}{2}.

Grönwall’s inequality then implies that

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,T_{k}^{\lambda}\wedge t]}|u_{s}^{\lambda}|^{2}\leq\Big(|x|^{2}+\frac{2C_{k}t}{\lambda}+\alpha_{*}^{2}|v_{j}|^{2}t\Big)e^{t}\leq\Big(r^{2}+\frac{2C_{k}t}{\lambda}+\alpha_{*}^{2}|v_{j}|^{2}t\Big)e^{t}.

By first selecting
k=k(t)>0
large enough and then
\lambda_{*}=\lambda_{*}(t)>0
large enough, we have the following bound satisfied for all
\lambda\geq\lambda_{*}
,
T_{k}^{\lambda}\geq t
:

\displaystyle\sup_{(s,x,\alpha)\in[0,t]\times D_{1}\times D_{2}}|u_{s}^{\lambda}|^{2}\leq\Big(r^{2}+1+\alpha_{*}^{2}|v_{j}|^{2}t\Big)e^{t}<\infty.

This proves part (i).

In order to prove part (ii), note that by using (i), for
\lambda\geq\lambda_{*}(t,x,j,\alpha)
there exists a constant
C_{2}(t)>0
such that

(7.13)

\displaystyle\sup_{(s,x,\alpha)\in[0,t]\times D_{1}\times D_{2}}|\varphi_{s/\lambda}(V_{0}+\lambda\alpha V_{j})x-\rho_{s}^{\alpha v_{j}}x|

\displaystyle=\sup_{(s,x,\alpha)\in[0,t]\times D_{1}\times D_{2}}\bigg|\int_{0}^{s/\lambda}V_{0}(\varphi_{u}(V_{0}+\lambda\alpha V_{j})x)\,du\bigg|\leq\frac{C_{2}(t)}{\lambda}.

This finishes the proof.
∎

To uncover even more trajectories in
\sat_{u}(\mathcal{F}(V_{0};V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}))
, fix
R\in\text{Poly}(\mathbf{R}^{n})
and
V\in\text{Cons}(\mathbf{R}^{n})
with
V(x)=v\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
for all
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
. For fixed
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
,
\lambda\mapsto R(x+\lambda v):\mathbf{R}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{n}

is a vector of polynomials in
\lambda
. Let
\deg(x,R,V)
be the maximal degree among these polynomials and

(7.14)

\displaystyle\deg(R,V)=\max_{x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}}\deg(x,R,V).

The quantity
\deg(R,V)
is called the
relative degree
of
R
and
V
. Observe that Taylor’s formula gives

(7.15)

\displaystyle R(x+\lambda v)=\sum_{|\eta|\leq\deg(R,V)}\frac{D^{\eta}R(x)}{\eta!}\lambda^{|\eta|}v^{\eta},

where we used multi-index notation in (
7.15
). Hence as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty

(7.16)

\displaystyle\frac{R(x+\lambda v)}{\lambda^{\deg(R,V)}}\longrightarrow\sum_{|\alpha|=\deg(R,V)}\frac{D^{\alpha}R(x)}{\alpha!}v^{\alpha}

where the convergence is uniform (in
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
) on compact subsets of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.

Next, observe that the quantity (
7.16
) on the righthand side above can be expressed as a Lie bracket of vector fields. Indeed, defining
\text{ad}^{k}V(R)
inductively by
\text{ad}^{0}V(R)=R
and
\text{ad}^{j}V(R)=[V,\text{ad}^{j-1}V(R)]
for
j\geq 0
, we find that

(7.17)

\displaystyle\sum_{|\alpha|=\deg(R,V)}\frac{D^{\alpha}R(x)}{\alpha!}v^{\alpha}=\frac{\text{ad}^{\deg(R,V)}V(R)}{|\deg(R,V)|!}(x)=:\Br(V,R)(x).

Example 7.3
.

As an illustrative example, consider
R\in\text{Poly}(\mathbf{R}^{3})
and
V\in\text{Cons}(\mathbf{R}^{3})
given by

\displaystyle R(x^{1},x^{2},x^{3})=(x^{1}x^{2},x^{2}x^{3},(x^{3})^{2})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad V(x^{1},x^{2},x^{3})=(0,1,0).

Then
\deg(R,V)=1
since

\displaystyle R(x^{1},x^{2}+\lambda,x^{3})=(x^{1}x^{2}+\lambda x^{1},x^{2}x^{3}+\lambda x^{3},(x^{3})^{2}).

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\text{Br}(V,R)(x^{1},x^{2},x^{3})=\text{ad}^{\deg(R,V)}V(R)(x^{1},x^{2},x^{3})=[V,R](x^{1},x^{2},x^{3})=(x^{1},x^{3},0).

The next goal is to see how
\text{Br}(V,R)
arises using the relevant composition of trajectories.

Proposition 7.7
.

Let
R\in\Poly(\mathbf{R}^{n})
and
V\in\Cons(\mathbf{R}^{n})
with constant value
v\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
. Let
D_{1}\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
D_{2}\subset\mathbf{R}
be compact, and fix
t>0
such that

\varphi_{t}(\alpha^{\deg(R,V)}\text{\emph{Br}}(V,R))x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
for all
(x,\alpha)\in D_{1}\times D_{2}
. Then

\displaystyle\sup_{(s,x,\alpha)\in[0,t]\times D_{1}\times D_{2}}|\rho_{1/\lambda}^{-\lambda^{2}\alpha v}\varphi_{\frac{s}{\lambda^{\deg(V,R)}}}(R)\rho_{1/\lambda}^{\lambda^{2}\alpha v}x-\varphi_{s}(\alpha^{\deg(V,R)}\text{\emph{Br}}(V,R))x|\rightarrow 0

as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
. In particular, if

\displaystyle(t,x)\mapsto\varphi_{t}(R)\in\text{\emph{sat}}_{u}(\mathcal{F}(V_{0};V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}))\qquad\text{ and }\qquad(\rho,\text{\emph{span}}(v))\in\text{\emph{sat}}_{u}(\mathcal{F}(V_{0};V_{1},\ldots,V_{k})),

then
(t,x,\alpha)\mapsto\varphi_{t}(\alpha^{\text{\emph{deg}}(R,V)}\text{\emph{Br}}(V,R))x\in\text{\emph{sat}}_{u}(\mathcal{F}(V_{0};V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}))
.

Proof.

Let
N=\deg(R,V)
and
R^{\prime}=\Br(V,R)
. For
(x,\alpha)\in D_{1}\times D_{2}
and
s\leq t
set

(7.18)

\displaystyle w_{s}^{\lambda}=\rho_{\frac{1}{\lambda}}^{-\lambda^{2}\alpha v}\,\varphi_{\frac{s}{\lambda^{N}}}(R)\,\rho_{\frac{1}{\lambda}}^{\alpha\lambda^{2}v}x.

Since
(s,x,\alpha)\mapsto\varphi_{s}(\alpha^{\deg(R,V)}\text{Br}(V,R))x
is a continuous mapping on the compact set
[0,t]\times D_{1}\times D_{2}
into
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, there is
j>0
large enough so that

\displaystyle\sup_{(s,x,\alpha)\in[0,t]\times D_{1}\times D_{2}}|\varphi_{s}(\alpha^{\deg(R,V)}\text{Br}(V,R))x|<j.

For
\lambda>0
introduce

\displaystyle S_{2j}^{\lambda}=\inf\{r\geq 0\,:\,|w_{r}^{\lambda}|\geq 2j\}.

Note that for any
s<S_{2j}^{\lambda}\wedge t
and
(x,\alpha)\in D_{1}\times D_{2}
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{ds}|w_{s}^{\lambda}-\varphi_{s}(\alpha^{N}R^{\prime})x|^{2}

\displaystyle=[\lambda^{-N}R(w_{s}^{\lambda}+\lambda\alpha v)-\alpha^{N}R^{\prime}(\varphi_{s}(\alpha^{N}R^{\prime})x)]\cdot(w_{s}^{\lambda}-\varphi_{s}(\alpha^{N}R^{\prime})x).

Thus applying (
7.15
) to the term
R(w_{t}^{\lambda}+\lambda\alpha v)
, we obtain for
s<S_{2j}^{\lambda}\wedge t
and
(x,\alpha)\in D_{1}\times D_{2}
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{ds}|w_{s}^{\lambda}-\varphi_{s}(\alpha^{N}R^{\prime})x|^{2}

\displaystyle=[\alpha^{N}R^{\prime}(w_{s}^{\lambda})-\alpha^{N}R^{\prime}(\varphi_{s}(\alpha^{N}R^{\prime})x)]\cdot(w_{s}^{\lambda}-\varphi_{s}(\alpha^{N}R^{\prime})x)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\lambda^{N}}\bigg(\sum_{|\eta|\leq N-1}\frac{D^{\eta}R(w_{s}^{\lambda})}{\eta!}(\lambda\alpha)^{|\eta|}v^{\eta}\bigg)\cdot(w_{s}^{\lambda}-\varphi_{s}(\alpha^{N}R^{\prime})x)

\displaystyle\leq C_{j}\frac{|w_{s}^{\lambda}-\varphi_{s}(\alpha^{N}R^{\prime})x|^{2}}{2}+\frac{K_{j}}{2\lambda},

where
C_{j},K_{j}>0
are constants depending only on
j
. Then Grönwall’s inequality gives that

\displaystyle\sup_{(s,x,\alpha)\in[0,S_{2j}^{\lambda}\wedge t]\times D_{1}\times D_{2}}|w_{s}^{\lambda}-\varphi_{s}(\alpha^{N}R^{\prime})x|^{2}\leq\frac{K_{j}t}{\lambda}e^{C_{j}t}.

Consequently,
\liminf_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}S_{2j}^{\lambda}>t
and the result follows.
∎

Proposition
7.6
and Proposition
7.7
give rise to an inductive procedure that can be used to produce trajectories in
\sat_{u}(\mathcal{F})
, which we now outline. Note that this is the same procedure as in
[
11
]
, but in the language of parameterized semigroups.

For any collection
\mathcal{G}
of parameterized continuous local semigroups on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, we call
V\in\text{Cons}(\mathbf{R}^{n})

scalable for

\mathcal{G}
if
(\rho,\text{span}(v))\in\mathcal{G}
where

8.
practical criteria for regular points

Throughout this section,
\mathscr{O}\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
denotes a non-empty open set with continuous boundary
\partial\mathscr{O}
containing
0\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
. We recall that
x_{t}
denotes the solution of (
1.1
) and that
0\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
is called
regular
for
(x_{t},\mathscr{O})
if
\mathbf{P}\{\xi=0\}=1
, where

(8.1)

\displaystyle\xi=\inf\{t>0\,:\,x_{t}\in\mathscr{O}\}.

We furthermore suppose there exists
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
,
\delta>0
and a continuous function
b:\mathbf{R}^{n-1}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
for which

(8.2)

\displaystyle\partial\mathscr{O}\cap B_{\delta}(0)

\displaystyle=\{x\in B_{\delta}(0)\,:\,x^{j}=b(x^{1},\ldots,x^{j-1},x^{j},\ldots x^{n})\},\quad\text{ and }

(8.3)

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}\cap B_{\delta}(0)

\displaystyle=\{x\in B_{\delta}(0)\,:\,x^{j}>b(x^{1},\ldots,x^{j-1},x^{j},\ldots x^{n})\}.

The goal of this section is to provide general criteria for
0\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
to be regular for
(x_{t},\mathscr{O})
. We then apply the criteria in several examples.

In order to characterize when
0\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
is regular for
(x_{t},\mathscr{O})
, we use the distributional scaling (
5.4
). Specifically, supposing that (
1.1
) is noise propagating, introduce a transformation
S_{\epsilon,\text{D}}:\mathbf{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{n}
,
\epsilon>0
, given by

(8.4)

\displaystyle S_{\epsilon,\text{D}}(x^{1},x^{2},\ldots,x^{n})=\Big(\frac{x^{1}}{\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{1}}},\frac{x^{2}}{\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{2}}},\ldots,\frac{x^{n}}{\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{n}}}\Big).

Let
r_{\epsilon}
,
\epsilon>0
, be the one-parameter family of open rectangles defined by

(8.5)

\displaystyle r_{\epsilon}=(-\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{1}},\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{1}})\times(-\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{2}},\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{2}})\times\cdots\times(-\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{n}},\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{n}}),\,\,\epsilon>0,

and observe that
S_{\epsilon,\text{D}}
maps
r_{\epsilon}
bijectively onto
r_{1}=(-1,1)^{n}
. By our hypothesis on the domain
\mathscr{O}
, there exists
\epsilon_{*}>0
such that

(8.6)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\epsilon}:=\{x\in r_{\epsilon}\,:\,x^{j}=b(x^{1},\ldots,x^{j-1},x^{j},\ldots x^{n})\}

\displaystyle=r_{\epsilon}\cap\partial\mathscr{O},

(8.7)

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}_{\epsilon}:=\{x\in r_{\epsilon}\,:\,x^{j}>b(x^{1},\ldots,x^{j-1},x^{j},\ldots x^{n})\}

\displaystyle=r_{\epsilon}\cap\mathscr{O},

for every
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{*})
. Also, by definition of the transformation
S_{\epsilon,\text{D}}
, observe that for any
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{*})

\displaystyle S_{\epsilon,\text{D}}(\Gamma_{\epsilon})=\{y\in(-1,1)^{n}\,:\,\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{j}}y^{j}=b(y^{1}\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{1}},\ldots,y^{n}\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{n}})\},

\displaystyle S_{\epsilon,\text{D}}(\mathscr{O}_{\epsilon})=\{y\in(-1,1)^{n}\,:\,\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{j}}y^{j}>b(y^{1}\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{1}},\ldots,y^{n}\epsilon^{\mathfrak{b}_{n}})\}.

Recalling the accessibility set
A_{\text{D},t}(0)
introduced in (
5.11
), we have the following:

Theorem 8.1

(Regular Point Criteria)
.

Assume that (
1.1
) is noise propagating. If there exists a non-empty open set
\mathscr{O}_{*}
for which

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}_{*}\subset S_{\epsilon,\emph{\text{D}}}(\mathscr{O}_{\epsilon})\text{ for all }\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{*})\,\,\,\text{ and }\,\,\,\bigcup_{t\in(0,1]}\cl(A_{\text{\emph{D}},t}(0))\cap\mathscr{O}_{*}\neq\emptyset,

then
0\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
is regular for
\mathscr{O}
.

Remark 8.2
.

The hueristics behind Theorem
8.1
is that the distributional rescaling gives the local asymptotic behavior of the process
x_{t}
solving (
1.1
) at time zero in the distributional sense. As long as the domain
\mathscr{O}
“plays well” with the underlying scaling
S_{\epsilon,\text{D}}
and the resulting limiting control problem visits points in
\mathscr{O}_{*}
, then
0\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
is regular for
(x_{t},\mathscr{O})
.

Proof of Theorem
8.1
.

Recalling the process
t\mapsto y_{\epsilon,t}
given in (
5.6
), observe that for any
t>0
and
\epsilon>0
small enough

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\{\xi\leq\epsilon t\}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\big\{x_{\epsilon s}\in\mathscr{O}\text{ for some }s\in(0,t]\big\}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbf{P}\big\{y_{\epsilon,s}\in S_{\epsilon,\text{D}}(\mathscr{O}_{\epsilon})\text{ for some }s\in(0,t]\big\}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbf{P}\{y_{\epsilon,s}\in\mathscr{O}_{*}\text{ for some }s\in(0,t]\}\geq\mathbf{P}\{y_{\epsilon,t}\in\mathscr{O}_{*}\}.

By Corollary
5.4
,
\mathbf{P}\{y_{\epsilon,t}\in\mathscr{O}_{*}\}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}\{y_{t}\in\mathscr{O}_{*}\}
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
where
y_{t}
solves (
5.7
). Since
\cl(A_{\text{D},t}(0))\cap\mathscr{O}_{*}\neq\emptyset
for some
t\in(0,1]
and
\mathscr{O}_{*}
is open, Lemma
5.5
implies that for some
t\in(0,1]
,
\mathbf{P}\{y_{t}\in\mathscr{O}_{*}\}>0
. In particular, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\{\xi=0\}\geq\mathbf{P}\{y_{t}\in\mathscr{O}_{*}\}>0.

By Blumenthal’s
0-1
law, it follows that
\mathbf{P}\{\xi=0\}=1
, finishing the proof.
∎

Let

is regular for

if and only if

is regular for

.

Our goal is to characterize regular points for second-order Langevin dynamics started on level sets of the underlying Hamiltonian. That is, let
U\in C^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{k};[0,\infty))
and consider

(8.12)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dq_{t}=p_{t}\,dt\\

(q_{0},p_{0})\in\mathbf{R}^{n}\end{cases}

where

is a standard,

Recall that this is the same context as discussed in Example
4.2
, but here we also assume that
U\geq 0
. However, since only the gradient of

appears in (

to achieve

. See also Remark
4.2
which shows that we do not need to assume

is a polynomial to apply the scaling results in the paper.

Fix
R>0
and let

(8.13)

\displaystyle H(q,p)=\frac{|p|^{2}}{2}+U(q)

be the Hamiltonian of the system (
8.12
). We study the regular point problem for the process
(q_{t},p_{t})
started on

(8.14)

\displaystyle\partial H_{>R}:=\{(q,p)\in\mathbf{R}^{k}\times\mathbf{R}^{k}\,:\,H(q,p)=R\}

which is the boundary of the open set

(8.15)

\displaystyle H_{>R}=\{(q,p)\in\mathbf{R}^{k}\times\mathbf{R}^{k}\,:\,H(q,p)>R\}.

We assume below that
R>0
is chosen so that both

and

are non-empty.

Theorem 8.4
.

Every
(q_{0},p_{0})\in\partial H_{>R}
is regular for
((q_{t},p_{t}),H_{>R})
.

Proof.

Let
(q_{0},p_{0})\in\partial H_{>R}
and consider the shifted sets

(8.16)

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}_{(q_{0},p_{0})}

\displaystyle=\{(\overline{q},\overline{p})\in\mathbf{R}^{k}\times\mathbf{R}^{k}\,:\,H(\overline{q}+q_{0},\overline{p}+p_{0})>R\},

(8.17)

\displaystyle\partial\mathscr{O}_{(q_{0},p_{0})}

\displaystyle=\{(\overline{q},\overline{p})\in\mathbf{R}^{k}\times\mathbf{R}^{k}\,:\,H(\overline{q}+q_{0},\overline{p}+p_{0})=R\}.

Similarly, we consider the shifted process
(\overline{q}_{t},\overline{p}_{t}):=(q_{t}-q_{0},p_{t}-p_{0})
which satisfies

(8.18)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}d\overline{q}_{t}=(\overline{p}_{t}+p_{0})\,dt\\
d\overline{p}_{t}=-(\overline{p}_{t}+p_{0})\,dt-\nabla U(\overline{q}_{t}+q_{0})\,dt+\sqrt{2}\,dB_{t}\\
(\overline{q}_{0},\overline{p}_{0})=0.\end{cases}

We recall from Example
4.2
