Catastrophic health expenditures and medical impoverishment in Bangladesh: evidence from the Household Income and Expenditure Survey
Authors: Abdur Razzaque Sarker, Rasedul Islam, An Tran-Duy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesCatastrophic health expenditure (CHE) and impoverishment due to out-of-pocket (OOP) expenditure are the most widely used metrics to assess financial protection in health. This study aimed to investigate the impact of OOP expenditure on CHE and impoverishment in Bangladesh and identify their determinants.DesignA national cross-sectional survey.SettingWe used data from the latest Household Income and Expenditure Survey 2022 in Bangladesh.Participants14 395 households from eight administrative divisions in Bangladesh.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe well-known methodology developed by Wagstaff and Doorslaer was used in this study to estimate the prevalence of CHE and the effect of OOP spending on impoverishment. We used a generalised linear model with a log link and a gam ma distribution to assess the association between the explanatory variables and annual OOP expenditure.ResultsThe prevalence of CHE was estimated to be 17.72% (95% CI 17.71% to 17.73%) and 7.07% (95% CI 7.06% to 7.08%) using 10% and 25% of the total consumption expenditure as thresholds, respectively. Given the 40% of non-food expenditure threshold level, about 9.46% (95% CI 9.45% to 9.47%) of households faced CHE due to OOP expenditure in Bangladesh. In 2022, approximately 3.7% (95% CI 3.7% to 3.71%) of the population (6.13 million people) fell into poverty in Bangladesh. The prevalence of CHE was higher among male-headed households, larger families, households with elderly members, households with members suffering from chronic illnesses, those using hospital care and individuals in the poorest wealth quintiles.ConclusionsOur study indicated that the financial burden due to OOP expenditure was substantial in Bangladesh. Findings stress the need for prepayment schemes such as national health insurance for safeguarding households against falling into poverty.

Introduction

Despite improvements in many health indicators globally, providing access to affordable healthcare remains a major concern in the developing world.1 2 As an emerging higher-middle-income country, Bangladesh has undergone socio-economic, demographic and epidemiological changes over the last decades and is expected to attain upper-middle-income status by 2031. In line with economic development, Bangladesh is committed to achieving Universal Health Coverage (UHC), a key objective endorsed by the WHO, to ensure access to healthcare services for all people as per the Sustainable Development Goal. The concept of UHC refers to the healthcare services, including prevention, promotion, treatment, rehabilitation and palliation, of sufficient quality, to which all people have access without being exposed to financial hardship.3 Although the universal health service coverage index has been sharply increasing from 37 in 2010 to 52 in 2021 in Bangladesh, financial hardship has worsened, with the country experiencing the highest incidence of catastrophic out-of-pocket (OOP) health spending in the region at 8.4%, compared with neighbouring India (6.7%), Bhutan (1.8%) and Nepal (2.1%).4 As this trend has been widely observed, financial protection is a key component of UHC, and many countries have made significant efforts to strengthen it. Unfortunately, many resource-poor countries are often stagnant in achieving financial health protection to avoid the financial consequences of illness.

In the South-East Asia Region (SEAR), OOP healthcare expenditure, hereafter referred to as OOP expenditure or spending, is the main source of healthcare funding. It was observed in more than half of the SEAR countries, including Bangladesh, that household OOP spending accounts for over one-third of their total current health expenditure.4 Bangladesh uses a combination of general revenue taxation, OOP payments, and donations from development partners’ contributions for healthcare financing strategies. Indeed, OOP expenditure is one of the most noteworthy payment strategies for healthcare in Bangladesh, with its share increasing alarmingly from 55.9% in 1997 to 68.5% in 2020 according to the Bangladesh National Health Account.5 Further, data from the Global Health Expenditure indicated that this figure approached 79.3% in 2023, highlighting a concerning upward trend.6 As a consequence, many households face catastrophic health expenditures (CHEs), and almost 2–5 million people fall into poverty every year in Bangladesh.7 8

OOP expenditure is defined as the cost or expenses incurred by individuals or households at the time they receive healthcare services. OOP expenditure includes any payment made by people related to medical fees, medicine purchases, user fees for public care and payments for equipment and diagnostic tests that are not reimbursed by either public or private insurances in Bangladesh.9 Of note, the OOP expenditure also includes informal payments (eg, tips and under-the-table payments), but excludes insurance premiums and any reimbursements from third-party payers (eg, health insurance fund) and the opportunity cost of seeking care (eg, lost income).10 Over the past two decades, the private sector, in conjunction with non-governmental organisations (NGOs), has been involved in the provision of healthcare services on a large scale in Bangladesh. Households typically visit multiple healthcare providers due to the nature of the pluralistic healthcare system.11 OOP spending was predominantly driven by spending on medicines (64.6%), followed by laboratory charges (11.7%) and consultation fees (10.8%), based on the most recent data available in Bangladesh.5 Several studies found that factors such as educational status, family size, residence, the presence of elderly members, the number of employed and sick household members, and hospitalisation of household members were significantly associated with OOP expenditure.12 13 Further, institutional barriers such as the availability of healthcare services, financing strategies, community awareness and even distances to health facilities are often associated with overall OOP expenditure.14 15 Understanding these determinants is crucial for identifying vulnerable groups and for designing policies and interventions to reduce OOP payments and the risk of financial hardship associated with healthcare use.

CHE and impoverishment due to OOP spending are the most widely used metrics to assess financial protection in health.16 17 CHE refers to any expenditure for medical treatment that can pose a threat to a household’s financial ability to maintain its subsistence needs.18 A catastrophic household is usually defined as one where the ratio of OOP expenditure to the household income or expenditures exceeds a certain percentage.17 These thresholds are often normative and may vary depending on various factors across countries. The latest report indicated that the number of people with catastrophic health spending increased from 937 million in 2015 to 1.04 billion in 2019, while 1.3 billion people suffered impoverishment due to OOP expenditure globally.4 Notably, the scenario of CHE and financial hardship has worsened following the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in many countries. The intensity of the catastrophic burden varies across regions according to various characteristics of households across various socio-economic strata. Empirical studies have identified four broad determinants of CHE, which are: (1) characteristics of the household head (eg, sex, age, education, marital status and self-rated health), (2) features of the household (eg, region, economic status and family size), (3) attributes of the family members (eg, number of old or young family members, family members with non-communicable disease) and (4) healthcare service utilisation and policy-related variables (eg, inpatient and outpatient hospital care services, characteristics of the hospitals, access to health services including health insurance and poverty alleviation policies).

Given the high fees charged by private providers, healthcare is often inaccessible for people on low incomes.19 Even in public hospitals care seekers are often charged a user fee for healthcare which is often unaffordable for poor and marginalised people.20 As a consequence, low-income people often seek healthcare from untrained healthcare providers or receive inadequate healthcare considering the needs, which in many cases results in adverse effects on health.21 Further, households often manage to pay excessive expenditures by borrowing from others, selling assets/mortgages, using family savings, obtaining donations from relatives, taking bank loans, and other means.22 However, evidence on the annual OOP expenditure, prevalence and specific determinants of CHE and the degree of suffering remain limited in the Bangladeshi settings.8 23–25 Further, there is no empirical evidence on the distribution of CHE at the national level after the COVID-19 pandemic. Using data from the Household Income and Expenditure Survey (HIES)-2022, which is a nationally representative survey, this study aimed to investigate the annual OOP expenditure and its determinants, the impact of OOP expenditure on CHE and impoverishment in Bangladesh, as well as to identify their determinants. Insights from this study will inform the establishment of healthcare policy priorities for achieving UHC in Bangladesh. Additionally, the findings may provide valuable lessons for other countries in the South Asian region, contributing to a broader understanding of effective strategies for achieving UHC.

Methods

Data sources and sampling design

Data were obtained from the latest HIES 2022 of Bangladesh.26 HIES is a multitopic nationally representative survey that provides various socio-economic characteristics of the country including income, consumption and healthcare expenditure of households in detail. The HIES 2022 used a two-stage stratified cluster sampling technique under the sampling framework provided by the Bangladesh Population and Housing Census 2022. The primary sampling unit (PSU) was the enumeration area (EA) of the Population and Housing Census 2022. Each EA is a cluster of around 100 households. In the first stage, the required number of PSUs was selected, and a complete household listing was carried out for the selected PSUs. In the second stage, 20 households were selected randomly from each selected PSU for the field interview. Stratification was conducted to ensure the national representation of the sampled households. First, each of the eight administrative divisions by rural and urban areas was treated as a domain or leading stratum. Therefore, the survey has 16 (8 rural+8 urban) domains or main strata. The detailed sampling design has been reported elsewhere.26 A total sample of 14 395 households was surveyed in this latest round of HIES. The training and data collection process started in December 2021, after the COVID-19 pandemic, and continued throughout 2022.

Measuring OOP expenditure and CHE

In this study, the OOP expenditure represents the financial costs related to healthcare incurred by households. These consist of consultation fees, diagnostic tests, transport and lodging, self-medication and other expenses (eg, informal tips) that are paid by individual households to receive treatment. The OOP expenditure for outpatient department (OPD) services was collected for the last 30 days preceding the survey, while OOP expenditure for inpatient department (IPD) services was collected for the last 12 months preceding the survey. Monthly OOP expenditures were annualised, and the annual OPD and IPD expenditures were then combined to estimate the total annual household OOP healthcare expenditure. For reporting in table 1, these annualised OOP expenditures were subsequently converted back to monthly OOP expenditures. A household was considered to face CHE if the ratio of the annual OOP expenditure to the annual consumption expenditure exceeded a certain threshold.4 17 The denominator could be total household consumption expenditure16 27 or total household consumption expenditure excluding food consumption expenditure, referred to as total non-food household consumption expenditure.16 28 The most used thresholds for identifying CHE are 10%, 25%, 30% and 40%.16 29 We used the widely accepted thresholds of 10% and 25% for the total household consumption expenditure and 40% of the total non-food household consumption expenditure.8 16 28 In the survey, costs were reported in Bangladeshi Taka (BDT), and we have used this currency consistently in the Results section. To compare our results with those of other countries, we converted the relevant costs to US dollars using the exchange rate (US$1=BDT93.3) in the year of the survey (2022) provided by the Central Bank of Bangladesh.30

Estimating impoverishment due to OOP expenditure

We used the headcount ratio concept as a measure of household poverty, which was defined as the proportion of the households with consumptions below the overall poverty line at the household level derived from the national poverty line. We used two types of consumption, one being total spending including OOP expenditure and another being total spending excluding OOP expenditure. The difference between these two headcounts measures the effect of OOP expenditure on impoverishment.16 For the national poverty line, we used the poverty line reported by the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS). The BBS implemented the Costs of Basic Needs technique, which implies that the poverty line is the minimum spending per capita at which each individual can attain their required basic needs including both food and non-food items.26 We considered two types of poverty lines at an individual level. First, the food-included poverty line was defined as the market value of 11 common food items (rice, wheat, pulses, milk, oil, meat, fish, potato, other vegetables, sugar and fruit) consisting of 2122 kcal per individual per day according to BBS. Second, the food-excluded poverty line was calculated as the median amount spent on the non-food items by each individual in food poverty. Then, the overall poverty line at a household level was estimated by summing the individual-level food-included and food-excluded poverty lines and multiplying the sum per capita by the household size.26 The resulting per-person poverty line was BDT3832 per month.26 The normalised poverty gap indicates the average amount by which the resources fall short of the poverty line as a percentage of that line.17 The absolute difference in the poverty headcount ratio was measured as a difference between the headcount ratios derived from the households’ consumption including and excluding the OOP spending.17 The relative difference was calculated by dividing the absolute headcount ratio difference by the headcount ratio derived from the households’ consumption including the OOP spending and multiplied by 100.17

To estimate the poverty impact of OOP expenditure, referred to as OOP-related impoverishment, we used the headcount ratios based on the total annual household spending including both food and non-food expenditures.17 A household with total consumption above the estimated poverty line was deemed to be impoverished by OOP healthcare payments if such expenditure reduced the household consumption to a level below the poverty line.31 A graphical presentation of the impact of OOP payments on poverty at the household level is presented using Pen’s parade graph. The number of households pushed below the poverty line due to OOP expenditure was estimated by multiplying the proportion of households with OOP-induced impoverishment by the total number of households in a particular year. Households were ranked from poorest to richest using total consumption expenditure on the x-axis of the graph. On the y-axis, household consumption as multiples of the national poverty line was presented. The downward vertical droplines (on the graph) represented the extent to which the OOP expenditure reduced the household consumption. A household was deemed poor due to OOP expenditure if the corresponding dropline crossed the poverty line.16 We estimated impoverishment at an individual level from household-level data (n=14 395 households). From household-level data, household sampling weight was multiplied by the household size to estimate the poverty headcount at an individual level.17 The number of individuals pushed below the poverty line due to OOP payments was estimated by multiplying the proportion of individuals with OOP-induced impoverishment by the estimated total population (166.9 million individuals) in a particular year. The total population was estimated using the numbers of individuals (n=62 400) and households (n=14 395) in our sample together with the sampling weights.

Identifying determinants of OOP, CHE and impoverishment

In a multivariate logistic regression model, we predicted the likelihood of facing CHE and impoverishment by households with explanatory variables containing information about demographic and socio-economic characteristics. The socio-economic characteristics (eg, age, sex and education level of household head, household size, asset quintiles), whether households sought healthcare for chronic illness, the number of earning person, status of hospitalisation, residence of households, and so forth were used as the explanatory variables in these models. We used the generalised linear model with a log link and γ distribution to assess the associations between the explanatory variables and annual OOP expenditure. Marginal effect analysis was conducted to quantify the average change in the OOP expenditure associated with each of the explanatory variables. The principal component analysis (PCA) approach was used to construct the asset (wealth) quintiles using the household’s asset information.32 33 First, the asset information was grouped into household ownership of consumer durables (eg, television, refrigerator, motorbike, car etc.), characteristics of the household’s dwelling (type of toilet, source of drinking water) and household landownership. Second, the factor scores from PCA of those indicators were then used to construct an asset index. Finally, the index values were sorted and grouped into quintiles (poorest, poorer, middle, richer and richest).32 33 The multistage cluster design of HIES 2022 was considered during the sampling weight adjustment.26 All statistical analyses were performed using the statistical package Stata (version 17), and results were interpreted as statistically significant at p-values of 0.05 or smaller.

Results

Characteristics of households

Background characteristics of the participating households (n=14 395) are reported in online supplemental table S1. The number of male-headed households (87.5%), medium-sized households consisting of four to six members (57.76%), households in rural areas (68%) and single earner households (57.14%) were dominant. More than half of the household heads (54%) had primary or secondary level education, while 31.29% of household heads had no institutional education. A total of 39.45% households had a child or children under 5 years old, while 29.16% of households had people older than 60 years. Most of the households (71%) had at least one member who sought healthcare for a chronic illness, while about 12% of households used inpatient care in the last 1-year period preceding this survey.

OOP healthcare expenditure

The average household OOP expenditure for 30 days was BDT1904, and the urban households spent more (BDT2305) than rural households (BDT1716) for healthcare (table 1). When only households that incurred OOP expenditure were included, the average OOP spending was BDT3454 and the urban households spent more than rural (BDT4192 vs BDT3109) for healthcare.

Factors associated with average annual household OOP expenditure

Table 2 shows factors associated with average annual household OOP expenditure. We observed that households with male heads, higher education of the household head, larger households, no earning person in the household, having a child or children under 5 years old, utilisation of healthcare due to chronic illness, history of hospitalisation, urban households and households belonging to higher wealth quintiles have significant association with the OOP expenditure compared with the references. We found that OOP expenditure for male-headed households was 1.3 (95% CI 1.1 to 1.5) times that of the female-headed households. Average annual OOP expenditure of households which had higher educated heads was 1.3 (95% CI 1.1 to 1.6) times that of households which had heads with no education. Larger households had higher OOP expenditure, which were 1.2 (95% CI 1.1 to 1.4) times for medium-sized households (four to six members) and 1.8 (95% CI 1.5 to 2.3) times for larger households (above six members) compared with the small households. OOP expenditures of households with a child or children under 5 years old, with at least one person sought healthcare for chronic illness, and with at least one person hospitalised in the last year were 1.1 (95% CI 1.0 to 1.3), 2.4 (95% CI 2.1 to 2.6) and 4.6 (95% CI 4.0 to 5.3) times, respectively, those of the counterparts. OOP expenditure of households having no income-earning persons was 1.3 (95% CI 1.1 to 1.6) times that of households with multiple earners, and of urban households was 1.1 (95% CI 1.0 to 1.3) times that of rural households. We also observed that the wealthiest households spent 50% more (95% CI 30% to 80%) on healthcare compared with the poorest households.

From the marginal effect analysis, we observed that the households having at least one hospitalisation case incurred an incremental OOP payment of BDT55 134 (95% CI BDT45 386 to BDT64 882) compared with those households having no hospitalisation case. Households belonging to the richest and richer quintiles had an incremental average annual OOP expenditure of BDT10 454 (95% CI BDT5646 to BDT14 443) and BDT5588 (95% CI BDT2100 to BDT9076) compared with the poorest households. Notably, OOP expenditure among households in the second poorest quintile (the ‘poorer’ category) was considerably higher than that among households in the poorest quintile (incremental value: BDT4977; 95% CI BDT1874 to BDT8080). However, the pattern of OOP expenditure across wealth quintiles was not monotonic. Households in the third wealth quintile (the ‘middle’ category) had lower OOP expenditure than those in the poorest quintile (incremental value: BDT−668; 95% CI BDT−3524 to BDT2187), although this difference was not statistically significant. In addition, although OOP expenditure generally increased among the higher wealth quintiles, the increments between successive quintiles were not equal. History of seeking care for chronic illness in the household and larger households were associated with an incremental average OOP expenditure of BDT15 445 (95% CI BDT13 461 to BDT17 428) and BDT15 662 (95% CI BDT8855 to BDT22 468) compared with their counterparts.

Prevalences of CHE

Socio-economic and demographic characteristics of the households facing CHE are shown in table 3 using three different threshold levels. The prevalences of CHE corresponding to thresholds of 10% and 25% of the total household consumption expenditure were 17.72% and 7.07%, respectively. With the threshold of 40% of the total non-food expenditure, 9.46% of the households faced CHE. For 10% and 25% thresholds, the prevalence of CHE was higher among the households with male heads, older heads, larger households (above six persons), presence of elderly (above 60 years), used hospital care and treatment for chronic illness. The prevalence of CHE was generally higher among poorer households, although the pattern across wealth quintiles was not monotonic. Similar patterns were observed when the 40% threshold level of the non-food expenditure was considered for CHE prevalence estimation (table 4).

Socio-economic and demographic characteristics of the households facing catastrophic health expenditure

Impact of OOP expenditure on poverty

Figure 1 shows the Pen’s Parade graph demonstrating the impact of OOP expenditures on poverty. Based on our analysis of a representative sample of 14 395 households, we found around 1.35 million households that, after accounting for OOP expenditure, fell below the national poverty line from an initially non-impoverished status. Obviously, households already living below the poverty line experienced further financial decline when incurring OOP expenditure.

Impact of OOP health expenditure on poverty illustrated on a Pen’s parade graph. The lengths of the vertical dotted red lines represent the magnitudes of OOP health expenditure. OOP, out-of-pocket.

The poverty headcount ratios derived from the households’ consumption, including and excluding the OOP expenditures, were 19.01 and 22.71, respectively. This indicates that around 3.7% of the population, which was equivalent to 6.13 million people (based on the representative sample), were pushed below the national poverty line when OOP expenditure was subtracted from the total consumption expenditure (table 3). In the rural and urban areas, the poverty headcount ratios increased by 3.87% and 3.33%, respectively, after the OOP expenditures were deducted from total consumption expenditures. The normalised poverty gap increased by 1.56% nationally, 1.73% in the rural area and 1.18% in the urban area after the OOP expenditures were deducted from total consumption expenditures (table 3). The poverty headcount ratios, the poverty gaps and the normalised poverty gaps for rural and urban areas and for the entire country are provided in online supplemental table S2.

Factors associated with OOP-induced impoverishment and CHE at various threshold levels

Factors associated with OOP-induced impoverishment relative to the national poverty line and factors associated with CHE are presented in online supplemental tables S3 and S4, respectively. Several variables, including household head’s education, gender and educational qualification, chronic illness, history of hospitalisation, number of earning persons and wealth quintile, were significantly associated with both OOP-induced impoverishment and CHE. However, some factors were associated only with impoverishment. Specifically, household head characteristics, household size and place of residence were significantly associated with impoverishment but not with CHE. Regarding OOP-induced impoverishment (online supplemental table S3), households headed by individuals aged 30–45 years were 1.67 times more likely to experience impoverishment than those headed by younger individuals (OR 1.67; 95% CI 1.05 to 2.65). Households with three or fewer members, a household head with no formal education, at least one household member suffering from a chronic disease, a history of hospitalisation, urban residence and belonging to lower wealth quintiles were also more likely to experience OOP-induced impoverishment.

Regarding the non-food expenditure threshold level (online supplemental table S4), male-headed households were 1.41 (95% CI 1.10 to 1.82) times more likely to suffer CHE. The same patterns were also observed regarding 25% of households’ annual consumption expenditure. Households with household heads who had obtained higher education were 0.69 (95% CI 0.50 to 0.93) times less likely to face catastrophe observed in the non-food expenditure thresholds level. Households having at least one member who sought healthcare for chronic illness had a higher odds of facing CHE in all three models, with ORs of 2.47 (95% CI 2.11 to 2.88), 2.40 (95% CI 1.84 to 3.13) and 2.32 (95% CI 1.87 to 2.28), respectively. We observed a higher CHE odd ratio for those households who used inpatient care in the last year preceding this survey. Single and no earner households were more prone to CHE as observed at both the 25% and 40% of thresholds levels. Poorest households were 1.37 (95% CI 1.09 to 1.71), 1.54 (95% CI 1.11 to 2.13) and 1.77 (95% CI 1.31 to 2.38) times more likely to face CHE compared with richest households in all three models, respectively.

Discussion

Bangladesh has made significant health improvements in the last two decades by putting into service its pluralistic healthcare system involving the public, private and NGO sectors.34 Though Bangladesh initiated various policies and key financing strategies for achieving universal healthcare coverage, an equitable healthcare financing system is still a big concern.35 The household OOP expenditure (68.5%) is still the key payment strategy for accessing healthcare and thus many poor and disadvantaged households are deprived of the benefit of quality care.5 A recent study indicated that OOP healthcare costs are regressive in Bangladesh, disproportionately burdening poorer households with a higher share of their income going towards healthcare expenditure.20 The current study is the first to examine the prevalence of CHE at the national level after the COVID-19 pandemic. This study also identified various factors associated with the risk of facing CHE and impoverishment in Bangladesh.

Our study estimated the average yearly OOP expenditure of almost BDT22 848 (US$249). Within the Southwest Asian region, Bangladesh has one of the highest rates of OOP expenditure, accounting for nearly 73% of total healthcare expenditure. Only Afghanistan surpassed Bangladesh with a slightly higher rate of 77.2%.6 For comparison, the OOP spending accounted for 57.50% of the total healthcare expenditure in Pakistan, 49.80% in India, 51.30% in Nepal and 43.60% in Sri Lanka, while the lowest percentage was observed in Maldives (14.30%).6 The high OOP expenditure in Bangladesh can be attributed to the increase in the number of private healthcare sectors in recent decades, coupled with the high costs of healthcare services that are unaffordable for the poorest segments of society.36 Additionally, the nature of the pluralistic healthcare system in Bangladesh leads households to seek services from multiple healthcare providers, frequently incurring unnecessary costs.19 Using the latest data from Bangladesh National Health Accounts, we found that the cost of medicine (64.6%) and diagnostic cost (11.7%) were the major cost drivers of OOP expenditure because of the unregulated private market in Bangladesh.5 The prices of certain medicines essential for combating both communicable and non-communicable diseases have increased by up to 120% in local drug markets in Bangladesh.37 A study revealed that pharmacies were the most preferred healthcare provider in Bangladesh, owing to their convenient locations, the ability to purchase medications without a physician’s prescription and the flexibility to visit at any time.38 Simultaneously, many households often sought care from local medical assistants, such as paramedics, drug sellers, as well as traditional healers, a practice that can also result in excessive healthcare cost.39

The findings from the present study were in line with our previous studies, which showed that hospitalisation was one of the major drivers of excessive OOP expenditure in both communicable and non-communicable diseases.38 40 We also observed that the wealthiest households spent more money on healthcare, granting them greater flexibility in selecting a range of healthcare providers than poorer households. As a consequence, those in lower-income brackets often seek care from untrained health professionals, such as traditional healers, which may jeopardise their health and ultimately necessitate more complex treatment at later stages.19 A recent study suggested that the increasing rate of caesarean birth (CB) in private hospitals in Bangladesh may be indicative of supplier-induced demand, given that the cost of CB delivery is almost three times higher than that of a vaginal delivery.41 Therefore, health policy makers need to implement both regulatory measures and behavioural change interventions to improve the knowledge of both consumers and providers to avoid self-medication practices and reduce unnecessary medication costs. Bangladesh practices a mix of healthcare financing strategies, such as general revenue taxation, external aid, OOP payments and small-scale health insurance schemes. However, the largest contributor is the OOP payments and thus the proportion of OOP expenditure contributed by the poorer households is greater than their share of income.20

The heavy reliance on OOP payments for healthcare poses a significant obstacle to achieving UHC and frequently places a catastrophic financial burden on household budgets. Using the 10% of total consumption threshold level, we found that about 17.72% of households experienced CHE in 2022, which is lower than in 2016 (24.6%)25 but higher than in 2010 (14.2%).8 Similarly, using the 40% of non-food expenditure threshold level, about 9.46% of households experienced CHE in 2022, which is slightly lower than the 10.9% reported in the previous report in 2016.25 Although we observed that the incidence of CHE is lower compared with earlier round in 2016 (pre-COVID-19 period), the financial burden of healthcare remains substantial. Indeed, a notable decline in healthcare utilisation was observed during COVID-19 compared with the prepandemic period.42 43 Recent report published by the WHO and World Bank indicated that there has been a rising trend in the proportion of households spending more than 10% of the household budget for OOP expenditure, increasing from 12.6% in 2015 to 13.5% in 2019 during the pre-COVID period.4 It was observed that the average per capita OOP expenditure has significantly surged after COVID-19 in many high, upper-middle and lower-middle-income countries, with the exception of low-income countries.44

Catastrophic OOP spending diminishes households’ capacity to afford other essential goods, crowding out other necessity goods such as education, shelter, clothing and other non-food items.45 46 Our study observed that about 3.7% of the population, equating to 6.1 million individuals, fell into poverty in 2022, based on the representative sample. In comparison, the poverty headcount ratio increased by 3.5% in 2010 and by 4.5% in 2016 due to OOP payments.8 25 In 2014, a study conducted in rural Bangladesh observed that about 3.4% of the people fell into poverty due to high OOP spending.47 However, for those living in poverty or marginally above the poverty line, even less than 10% of their households’ budget can lead to financial hardship due to a limited capacity to pay for healthcare.48 It was well evident that risk-pooling mechanisms can mitigate the consequences of OOP expenditure. However, in Bangladesh, less than 1% of the population is enrolled in various health insurance schemes, limiting the effectiveness of such financial protection strategies.4 The burden of OOP expenditure could be mitigated if a larger portion of the population could access the prepayment and pooling mechanisms, which are currently scarce in Bangladesh.

Consistent with findings from other studies, we also observed that male-headed households, having chronic illness, hospitalisation during illness, number of earning persons and resource-poor households were more susceptible to CHE.8 25 49 Our results indicated that households headed by men are more vulnerable to CHE, possibly due to traditional gender roles where men may be the primary earners and their health issues can severely impact the family’s income and financial stability. Households with members suffering from chronic illnesses are at a higher risk25 49 because chronic diseases often require long-term treatment, frequent medical visits and continuous medication, leading to substantial OOP expenses. Further hospitalisation played a key role in driving CHE as hospital stays involve not just the OOP payment for medical care but also other associated costs such as transportation, food, and financial cost of caregivers.50 51 Households with fewer earners have less income to cushion against unexpected health expenses, making them more susceptible to financial strain, thus more prone to CHE. Families with limited financial resources are inherently more vulnerable. The latest research indicated that the less well-off households were most likely to experience financial hardship.4 Indeed, the households living in or near poverty suffered primarily due to high OOP expenditure, as they are forced to reduce their consumption level, which, in turn, may lead to falling into a vicious cycle of ill health and poverty.52

As previously mentioned, approximately 6.1 million individuals in Bangladesh fell below the poverty line due to OOP payments. It is important to note that this estimate pertains to those who were initially above the national poverty line. Additionally, there was a proportion of the population already living below the poverty line for whom high OOP payments further exacerbate their poverty. Many in this group may forgo necessary healthcare due to their incapacity to afford the healthcare costs.8 25 Thus, many people resort to harder coping mechanisms, such as borrowing, assistance, and even selling capital assets or mortgaging assets.53 An earlier study indicated that in Bangladesh, about 58% of households experienced distress financing due to OOP expenditure.49 Indeed, individuals facing income uncertainty due to illness may bear an even greater burden from OOP expenditure, with severe consequences for their daily livelihood and financial stability.54 To avoid such consequences of OOP expenditure, implementing an affordable health protection scheme is crucial, and this is in line with the national healthcare financing strategy of Bangladesh.35 Further, the allocation of the public health budget should be revised, as only a small fraction of the budget, less than 2.5% of total GDP in Bangladesh, has been allocated to the healthcare sector over the past few years. It has been recommended that government expenditure on health should be at least 5% of the total GDP in Bangladesh for progressing towards UHC.55 Therefore, prioritising the establishment and investment in healthcare systems is of utmost importance. Further, the implementation of prepayment mechanisms, such as social health insurance—often recommended by the WHO and the World Bank—should be considered as a strategic approach to mitigate CHE and poverty resulting from OOP expenditure in Bangladesh.

This study has several limitations. It relied on data from a national household survey, which may be affected by recall and reporting bias. Additionally, due to the cross-sectional nature of this survey, we could not establish a causal relationship between various factors and outcome variables. We captured only the prevalence of CHE among households that sought care, excluding those who needed services but could not afford them due to the lack of data. This could lead to an underestimation of the prevalence and intensity of CHE. Despite these limitations, our findings can be considered representative at the national level, as the data were collected from the most recent nationally representative household expenditure survey in Bangladesh.

Conclusion

OOP health expenditure constitutes the major source of health financing in Bangladesh, largely due to inadequate public health spending. As a result of these OOP healthcare costs, about 17.72% and 9.46% of the households experienced CHE at thresholds equivalent to 10% of total consumption expenditure and 40% of non-food expenditure thresholds, respectively. Furthermore, about 3.7% of the population, equating to 6.1 million individuals, fell into poverty in 2022 as a direct consequence of OOP expenditure, based on the representative sample. The study identified key factors contributing to CHE, financial hardship and impoverishment, suggesting that households with these characteristics should be targeted for inclusion in prepayment schemes such as social health insurance. Our findings will assist policymakers in establishing various healthcare financing strategies and enhancing the efficiency of public investments in health.
