Reidemeister spectra of free nilpotent groups and plethysms of Schur functions
Pieter Senden

Abstract
We establish a strong link between two open problems: determining the Reidemeister spectrum of free nilpotent groups and determining the coefficients in the Schur expansion of plethysms of Schur functions. Specifically, we show that the expressions occurring in the computations for the Reidemeister spectrum are sums of plethysms of the form s 1 i ​ [ g ] s_{1^{i}}[g] , where g g is a Schur function or a Schur positive function.

1
Introduction

Determining the Reidemeister spectra of free nilpotent groups and determining the coefficients in the Schur expansion of the plethysms of Schur functions are both well known and well studied open problems in their respective fields.
For both problems, significant progress has been made, but complete solutions still remain unknown.
In this article, we establish a strong link between both problems, in the sense that a significant step towards fully solving the problem of the Reidemeister spectra can be formulated as a problem of plethysms.
To establish this link, we travel from group theory to combinatorics, starting from Reidemeister spectra and ending at plethysms, passing through the realms of Lie algebras and symmetric polynomials along the way.

As this journey passes through several quite different mathematical fields, we want everything to be comprehensible for both those with little knowledge of Reidemeister numbers and for those with little knowledge of plethysms of Schur functions.
This means that at several points, we will explain ourselves more thoroughly than perhaps needed in the eyes of an expert in the field.

1.1
Starting point: Reidemeister spectra of free nilpotent groups

Let
G
be a group and
\varphi\in\End(G)
.
We say that
x,y\in G
are
\varphi
-conjugate
if
x=zy{\varphi(z)}^{-1}
for some
z\in G
.
This defines an equivalence relation on
G
and the number of equivalence classes is called the
Reidemeister number
of
\varphi
, which is denoted by
R(\varphi)
.
When
\varphi
is not specified, we also speak of
twisted conjugacy
.
Finally, the
Reidemeister spectrum
of
G
is the set
\SpecR(G):=\{R(\psi)\mid\psi\in\Aut(G)\}
.
If
\SpecR(G)=\{\infty\}
, we say that
G
has the
R_{\infty}
-property
; if
\SpecR(G)=\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
, we say that
G
has
full Reidemeister spectrum
.

The concept of twisted conjugacy arises from algebraic topology, more specifically from Nielsen fixed-point theory, where its topological analog serves as an estimate for the number of fixed points of a continuous self map
[
23
]
.
However, twisted conjugacy also occurs in representation theory
[
36
]
, Galois cohomology
[
2
]
, and the Arthur-Selberg trace formula
[
35
]
.

The last two decades, there has been strong algebraic interest in twisted conjugacy, especially in the
R_{\infty}
-property and in Reidemeister spectra of nilpotent groups.
For instance, nilpotent quotients of both surface groups
[
9
]
and Baumslag-Solitar groups
[
10
]
, and several
2
-step nilpotent quotients of right-angled Artin groups
[
12
]
all have been studied.

There is particular interest in the free nilpotent groups.
Let
r\geq 2
and
c\geq 1
be integers.
Recall that the
free
c
-step nilpotent group of rank
r
is the group

N_{r,c}:=\frac{F(r)}{\gamma_{c+1}(F(r))},

where
F(r)
is the free group of rank
r
and
\gamma_{1}(G):=G,\gamma_{i+1}(G):=[\gamma_{i}(G),G]
for any group
G
and integer
i\geq 1
.
For
c=1
, we obtain the free abelian groups and it is well known
[
32
, § 3]
that

\SpecR(\mathbb{Z}^{r})=\begin{cases}\{2,\infty\}&\mbox{if }r=1,\\
\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The Reidemeister spectrum of (non-abelian) free nilpotent groups has been studied several times: in 2009, D. Gonçalves and P. Wong showed that
N_{2,c}
has the
R_{\infty}
-property for
c\geq 9
and that
N_{r,2}
never has the
R_{\infty}
-property
[
17
]
; in 2011, V. Roman’kov generalised the former by showing that
N_{r,c}
has the
R_{\infty}
-property if
r\in\{2,3\}
and
c\geq 4r
, and if
r\geq 4
and
c\geq 2r

[
32
]
.
Finally, in 2014, K. Dekimpe and D. Gonçalves classified all free nilpotent groups with the
R_{\infty}
-property:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
8
, Theorem 2.1]
)
.

Let
r\geq 2
and
c\geq 1
be integers.
Then
N_{r,c}
has the
R_{\infty}
-property if and only if
c\geq 2r
.

With the
R_{\infty}
-question answered, the focus has shifted to determining the Reidemeister spectrum in the remaining cases:

Objective 1.2
.

Let
r\geq 2
and
c\geq 1
be integers.
Determine the Reidemeister spectrum of
N_{r,c}
if
c<2r
.

Earlier, we showed that the case
c=1
is fully done.
V. Roman’kov
[
32
]
already determined that

\displaystyle\SpecR(N_{2,2})=2\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\},

\displaystyle\SpecR(N_{2,3})=\{2k^{2}\mid k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}\cup\{\infty\}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\SpecR(N_{3,2})=\{2k-1\mid k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}\cup 4\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}.

K. Dekimpe, S. Tertooy and A. Vargas completely solved the case
c=2
and showed that
N_{r,2}
has full Reidemeister spectrum if and only if
r\geq 4

[
13
]
.
They also show that
\SpecR(N_{r,c})
is never full if
c\geq r
and conjecture the following:

Conjecture

(
[
13
, Conjecture 5.2]
)
.

Let
r\geq 2
and
c\geq 3
be integers.
Then the Reidemeister spectrum of
N_{r,c}
is not full.

Their proof for
\SpecR(N_{r,2})
starts with a theoretical approach to the general problem of determining
\SpecR(N_{r,c})
, after which they specify to the case
c=2
and prove that
\SpecR(N_{r,2})
is full for
r\geq 4
.
They do so by giving for each positive integer
n
an automorphism with Reidemeister number equal to
n
.
However, the computations are rather tedious and seem hard to generalise to other values of
r
and
c
.

In this article, we continue along their theoretical approach.
They show that the Reidemeister number of an endomorphism
\varphi\in\End(N_{r,c})
is fully determined by the eigenvalues of the induced map
\varphi_{1}
on
\frac{N_{r,c}}{\gamma_{2}(N_{r,c})}
(since this quotient group is free abelian of finite rank, it makes sense to speak of eigenvalues).
While using this approach to determine
\SpecR(N_{r,2})
, they make use of the fact that
R(\varphi)
is a symmetric polynomial in the eigenvalues of
\varphi_{1}
.
However, they never prove that this fact holds for all values of
r
and
c
.
This result suggests a more combinatorial look at the problem of determining the Reidemeister spectrum of free nilpotent groups, which is the goal of this article.
Eventually, we relate this problem to a major open problem from combinatorics: the plethysm of Schur functions.

1.2
Destination: plethysm of Schur functions

The concepts of Schur functions and plethysms come from the field of symmetric functions.
Here, we give a rather informal introduction to these concepts; we work them out in more detail in
Section

3
.

A symmetric function can be thought of as an (infinite) power series in infinitely many variables
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots
such that permuting the variables does not change the power series.
For instance,

\sum_{i\geq 1}x_{i}\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{i<j}x_{i}x_{j}

are both symmetric functions.
The Schur functions are a family of symmetric functions
\{s_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda}
that are indexed by the integer partitions, that is, the non-increasing sequences
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k},\ldots)
of non-negative integers with finite sum.
These Schur functions have nice combinatorial properties and each symmetric function is a (possibly infinite) linear combination of them.

Given two symmetric functions
f
and
g
, the plethysm
f[g]
is essentially the symmetric function we obtain by substituting the monomials in
g
for the variables in
f
, where a monomial is a product of one or more not necessarily distinct
x_{i}
.
D. E. Littlewood introduced the concept of plethysm
[
28
]
.
One of the major open problems (
Objective

3.14
) in the field of symmetric functions is to find a combinatorial interpretation of the coefficients
a_{\mu,\lambda}^{\nu}
in

s_{\lambda}[s_{\mu}]=\sum_{\nu}a_{\lambda,\mu}^{\nu}s_{\nu}.

It is known that the coefficients
a_{\lambda,\mu}^{\nu}
are non-negative integers for all
\lambda,\mu,\nu
, so it makes sense to look for a combinatorial interpretation for them.
No such interpretation is known in general, although it is known for certain special cases, such as
s_{2}[s_{n}]

[
28
]
and
s_{4}[s_{n}]

[
14
,
20
]
.
Additionally, there are several algorithms known to compute these coefficients
[
1
,
21
,
43
]
; see
[
29
]
for an exposé on the topic and
[
6
]
for a (non-exhaustive) list of known plethysms.

The coefficients
a_{\lambda,\mu}^{\nu}
also occur in representation theory.
Given a finite dimensional vector space
V
, we look at the category of
\GL(V)
-modules.
On this category, we consider a family of functors
\{S^{\lambda}\}_{\lambda}
, indexed by the partitions as well, called the Schur functors.
These have the property that for each partition
\lambda
, the module
S^{\lambda}V
is irreducible; conversely, each irreducible
\GL(V)
-module is of the form
S^{\lambda}V
for some partition
\lambda
.
Now, the
\GL(V)
-module
S^{\lambda}(S^{\mu}V)
decomposes as a direct sum of irreducible modules
S^{\nu}V
, and the multiplicity of
S^{\nu}V
in this decomposition is precisely
a_{\lambda,\mu}^{\nu}
.

This is one way of viewing the link between Schur functions and the representation theory of
\GL(n,\mathbb{C})
.
In
Section

3.4
, we present a different point of view.
We also scratch the surface of the link between Schur functions and the representation theory of the symmetric group
\mathrm{Sym}(n)
.
We refer the reader to
[
40
]
for more information about Schur functors, and to
[
22
,
30
]
for more information about representations of
\mathrm{Sym}(n)
.
Finally, plethysms also occur in other areas, such as
\tau
-rings
[
19
]
and physics
[
42
]
.

We show that the problem of determining the Reidemeister spectrum of free nilpotent groups is related to plethysms of specific Schur functions, namely
s_{1^{i}}[s_{k,1}]
, and of other symmetric functions (see
Objectives

4.3
and
4.14
).

2
From Reidemeister spectra to symmetric polynomials

We start our journey with the main tools for computing Reidemeister numbers on abelian and nilpotent groups.
For ease of notation, we define the map

|{\cdot}|_{\infty}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}:n\mapsto\begin{cases}\infty&\mbox{if }n=0,\\
|n|&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We refer to this maps as the
|{\cdot}|_{\infty}
-norm
.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
32
, § 3]
)
.

Let
A
be a free abelian group of finite rank.
Let
{\varphi\in\End(A)}
.
Then

R(\varphi)=|{\det(\varphi-\Id_{A})}|_{\infty}=|{p_{\varphi}(1)}|_{\infty},

where
p_{\varphi}
is the characteristic polynomial of
\varphi
.

The main tool for computing Reidemeister numbers on nilpotent groups is the so-called
product formula
.
It was first discussed by V. Roman’kov
[
32
, Theorem 2.6, Lemma 2.7]
; see
[
11
, Proposition 5]
for a statement and full proof of the result.
We say that a group
G
is
nilpotent
if
\gamma_{i}(G)=1
for some
i\geq 1
, with
\gamma_{i}(G)
defined by
\gamma_{1}(G)=G
and
\gamma_{i+1}(G):=[\gamma_{i}(G),G]
for
i\geq 1
, as before.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
11
, Proposition 5]
)
.

Let
N
be a finitely generated torsion-free nilpotent group and let
\varphi\in\End(N)
.
Suppose that

1=N_{0}\lhd N_{1}\lhd\ldots\lhd N_{c}=N

is a central series (i.e.
[N_{i},N]\leq N_{i-1}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,c\}
) such that

•

for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,c\}
,
\varphi(N_{i})\leq N_{i}
, and

•

for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,c\}
,
N_{i}/N_{i-1}
is torsion-free.

For
i\in\{1,\ldots,c\}
, let
\varphi_{i}:N_{i}/N_{i-1}\to N_{i}/N_{i-1}
denote the induced endomorphism.
Then

R(\varphi)=\prod_{i=1}^{c}R(\varphi_{i}).

Since the factors
N_{i}/N_{i-1}
are finitely generated torsion-free abelian groups, the numbers
R(\varphi_{i})
are given by
Lemma

2.1
, and are therefore completely determined by the eigenvalues of
\varphi_{i}
.

For free nilpotent groups, we can say even more.
Let
r\geq 2
and
c\geq 2
be integers.
For
\varphi\in\End(N_{r,c})
, we can apply
Theorem

2.2
by using the lower central series of
N_{r,c}
, which is given by

1=\gamma_{c+1}(N_{r,c})\lhd\gamma_{c}(N_{r,c})\lhd\ldots\lhd\gamma_{1}(N_{r,c})=N_{r,c}.

As the name suggests, the lower central series is indeed a central series, and, moreover, it satisfies both conditions from
Theorem

2.2
(see
[
7
, Lemma 1.1.2]
).

Now, for any integer
i\in\{2,\ldots,c\}
, the eigenvalues of
\varphi_{i}
for
i\geq 2
are completely determined by those of
\varphi_{1}
.
To prove this, we largely follow the approach by K. Dekimpe, S. Tertooy and A. Vargas mentioned in the introduction.
However, our use of Lie algebras differs from theirs.

Remark
.

Throughout this article, whenever we (implicitly or explicitly) apply
Theorem

2.2
to a free nilpotent group and an endomorphism
\varphi
, we use the lower central series.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
G
be a group.
The
Lie ring associated to
G
is the abelian group

L(G):=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma_{i}(G)}{\gamma_{i+1}(G)}

equipped with a Lie bracket defined as follows: for
i,j\geq 1
,
x\in\gamma_{i}(G)
and
y\in\gamma_{j}(G)
arbitrary, we put

[x\gamma_{i+1}(G),y\gamma_{j+1}(G)]:=[x,y]\gamma_{i+j+1}(G),

and then extend it linearly to the whole of
L(G)
.
Here,
[x,y]
on the right-hand side denotes the commutator in
G
, which is defined as
[x,y]:={x}^{-1}{y}^{-1}xy
.
The properties of the Lie bracket are:

•

for all
x\in L(G):[x,x]=0
;

•

for all
x,y\in L(G):[x,y]=-[y,x]
;

•

for all
x,y,z\in L(G):[[x,y],z]+[[y,z],x]+[[z,x],y]=0
(Jacobi identity).

By tensoring with
\mathbb{C}
, we can make
L(G)
into a (complex) Lie algebra
L^{\mathbb{C}}(G):=L(G)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{C}
.
Every
\varphi\in\End(G)
induces a Lie ring morphism on
L(G)
given by

\varphi_{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i\geq 1}x_{i}\gamma_{i+1}(G)}}\right):=\sum_{i\geq 1}\varphi(x_{i})\gamma_{i+1}(G)

(2.1)

where
x_{i}\in\gamma_{i}(G)
for each
i\geq 1
and only finitely many of them are non-trivial.
Due to the way the Lie bracket on
L(G)
is defined,
\varphi_{*}
preserves the Lie bracket.
In addition, if, for every
i\geq 1
, we write
\varphi_{i}
for the induced endomorphism on
\gamma_{i}(G)/\gamma_{i+1}(G)
, we can rewrite (
2.1
) as

\varphi_{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i\geq 1}x_{i}\gamma_{i+1}(G)}}\right)=\sum_{i\geq 1}\varphi_{i}(x_{i}\gamma_{i+1}(G)).

The Lie ring morphism
\varphi_{*}
in turn induces a Lie algebra morphism on
L^{\mathbb{C}}(G)
, which we also write as
\varphi_{*}
.

Let
r\geq 2
and
c\geq 2
be integers.
For
G=N_{r,c}
, the associated Lie ring
L(N_{r,c})
is given by

L(N_{r,c})=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{c}\frac{\gamma_{i}(N_{r,c})}{\gamma_{i+1}(N_{r,c})}

as
\gamma_{i}(N_{r,c})=1
for
i\geq c+1
.
The Lie algebra
L^{\mathbb{C}}(N_{r,c})
, in turn, is (isomorphic to) the
free
c
-step nilpotent Lie algebra of rank
r
, which is defined as the Lie algebra

\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}:=\frac{\mathfrak{f}_{r}}{\gamma_{c+1}(\mathfrak{f}_{r})}.

Here,
\mathfrak{f}_{r}
is the free (complex) Lie algebra of rank
r
and
\gamma_{1}(\mathfrak{n})=\mathfrak{n}
and
\gamma_{i+1}(\mathfrak{n})=[\gamma_{i}(\mathfrak{n}),\mathfrak{n}]
for any Lie algebra
\mathfrak{n}
and integer
i\geq 1
.

One way to realise the isomorphism between
L^{\mathbb{C}}(N_{r,c})
and
\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}
goes as follows: let
X:=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}\}
be a set of
r
symbols.
Suppose
F(r)
is the free group on
X
and
\mathfrak{f}_{r}
is the free Lie algebra on
X
.
Since
X
generates
F(r)
, the set
X_{r,c}:=\{X_{i}\gamma_{c+1}(F(r))\mid i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}\}
generates
N_{r,c}
as a group.
By
[
24
, 17.2.1 Lemma]
, each
\gamma_{i}(N_{r,c})
is generated by
\gamma_{i+1}(N_{r,c})
and all
i
-fold commutator brackets of elements in
X_{r,c}
.
The construction of the associated Lie ring then implies that
L(N_{r,c})
is generated, as a Lie ring, by the set
\{X_{i}\gamma_{2}(N_{r,c})\mid i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}\}
, which is the image of
X_{r,c}
under the natural projection to
N_{r,c}/\gamma_{2}(N_{r,c})
.
Consequently, we can define a map
\Psi:L^{\mathbb{C}}(N_{r,c})\to\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}
by putting
\Psi(X_{i}\gamma_{2}(N_{r,c})\otimes z):=zX_{i}
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
and
z\in\mathbb{C}
, as their images completely determine
\Psi
on the whole of
L^{\mathbb{C}}(N_{r,c})
.
This map
\Psi
is the desired isomorphism.

This isomorphism
\Psi
enables us to, given
\varphi\in\End(N_{r,c})
, work on
\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}
to compute the eigenvalues of
\varphi_{*}
.
Indeed, define
\psi_{*}:=\Psi\circ\varphi_{*}\circ{\Psi}^{-1}\in\End(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})
and let
\psi_{*,i}
be the induced map on
\gamma_{i}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})/\gamma_{i+1}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})
.
We argue that
\psi_{*,i}
has the same eigenvalues as
\varphi_{i}
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,c\}
.

Fix
i\in\{1,\ldots,c\}
.
The map
\Psi
induces the isomorphism

\Psi_{i}:\frac{\gamma_{i}(L^{\mathbb{C}}(N_{r,c}))}{\gamma_{i+1}(L^{\mathbb{C}}(N_{r,c}))}\to\frac{\gamma_{i}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})}{\gamma_{i+1}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})}.

By the definition of
L(N_{r,c})
,

\frac{\gamma_{i}(L(N_{r,c}))}{\gamma_{i+1}(L(N_{r,c}))}=\frac{\gamma_{i}(N_{r,c})}{\gamma_{i+1}(N_{r,c})},

so by the properties of the tensor product,

\frac{\gamma_{i}(L^{\mathbb{C}}(N_{r,c}))}{\gamma_{i+1}(L^{\mathbb{C}}(N_{r,c}))}=\frac{\gamma_{i}(N_{r,c})}{\gamma_{i+1}(N_{r,c})}\otimes\mathbb{C}.

Hence,
\Psi_{i}
takes on the form

\Psi_{i}:\frac{\gamma_{i}(N_{r,c})}{\gamma_{i+1}(N_{r,c})}\otimes\mathbb{C}\to\frac{\gamma_{i}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})}{\gamma_{i+1}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})}:x\mapsto\Psi(x)\gamma_{i+1}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}).

(2.2)

Now, by definition of
\Psi
and
\psi_{*}
, the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\frac{\gamma_{i}(N_{r,c})}{\gamma_{i+1}(N_{r,c})}\otimes\mathbb{C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\frac{\gamma_{i}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})}{\gamma_{i+1}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\frac{\gamma_{i}(N_{r,c})}{\gamma_{i+1}(N_{r,c})}\otimes\mathbb{C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\frac{\gamma_{i}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})}{\gamma_{i+1}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Psi_{i}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\simeq}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{i}\otimes\Id_{\mathbb{C}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi_{*,i}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Psi_{i}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\simeq}

Thus, the map
\psi_{*,i}
has the same eigenvalues as
\varphi_{i}\otimes\Id_{\mathbb{C}}
, which in turn has the same eigenvalues as
\varphi_{i}
.

To determine the eigenvalues of
\varphi_{i}
and show that they are completely determined by those of
\varphi_{1}
, we need the concept of a Hall basis of a free Lie algebra.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{f}_{r}
be the free Lie algebra of rank
r
generated by the elements
X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}
.
A
Hall basis
is a totally ordered vector space basis
\mathcal{H}
of
\mathfrak{f}_{r}
which is constructed in an inductive way as
\mathcal{H}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{n}
:

•

\mathcal{H}_{1}:=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}\}
and
X_{1}<\ldots<X_{r}
.

•

For
n\geq 2
, assume that
\mathcal{H}_{1}
up to
\mathcal{H}_{n-1}
have been constructed and that
\bigcup_{k=1}^{n-1}\mathcal{H}_{k}
has been given a total order.
Then
\mathcal{H}_{n}
is defined as the set of all Lie brackets
[Y,Z]
with
Y\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
and
Z\in\mathcal{H}_{l}
such that

–

k+l=n
,

–

Y<Z
,

–

if
Z=[Z_{1},Z_{2}]
for some
Z_{i}\in\mathcal{H}_{k_{i}}
, then
Z_{1}\leq Y
.

•

Finally, we extend the order to
\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}\mathcal{H}_{k}
by picking any total order of
\mathcal{H}_{n}
and imposing that
X<Y
for all
X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
and
Y\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
with
k<n
.

For instance, the first three parts of a Hall basis of
\mathfrak{f}_{3}
are given by

\mathcal{H}_{1}=\{X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}\},

\mathcal{H}_{2}=\{[X_{1},X_{2}],[X_{1},X_{3}],[X_{2},X_{3}]\}

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{3}

\displaystyle=\{[X_{1},[X_{1},X_{2}]],[X_{1},[X_{1},X_{3}]],[X_{2},[X_{1},X_{2}]],[X_{2},[X_{1},X_{3}]],

\displaystyle\quad[X_{2},[X_{2},X_{3}]],[X_{3},[X_{1},X_{2}]],[X_{3},[X_{1},X_{3}]],[X_{3},[X_{2},X_{3}]]\}

where the order on
\mathcal{H}_{2}
and
\mathcal{H}_{3}
is given by the order in which the elements are written down.
For more details and for a proof that
\mathcal{H}
is indeed a basis, we refer the reader to
[
34
, Chapter IV]
.

The size of each
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is determined by E. Witt:

Theorem 2.5

(
[
41
, Satz 3]
)
.

Let
r\geq 2
and
k\geq 1
be integers with
k\leq r
.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hall basis of
\mathfrak{f}_{r}
.
The size of
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is given by

N(r,k):=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{d\mid k}\mu(d)r^{k/d},

where
\mu:\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\to\{-1,0,1\}
is the Möbius function, i.e.

\mu(d)=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $d$ is the product of an even number of distinct prime numbers}\\
-1&\mbox{if $d$ is the product of an odd number of distinct prime numbers}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hall basis of
\mathfrak{f}_{r}
and
i\geq 1
.
By the properties of a free Lie algebra, the linear span of
\mathcal{H}_{i}
fully lies in
\{0\}\cup{(\gamma_{i}(\mathfrak{f}_{r})\setminus\gamma_{i+1}(\mathfrak{f}_{r}))}
for every
i\geq 1
.
Using this, it is readily verified that the projection of the subset
\bigcup_{k=1}^{c}\mathcal{H}_{k}
forms a (vector space) basis of
\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}
for all
c\geq 2
.
We call such a basis a
Hall basis
of
\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}
as well and we will also write
\bigcup_{k=1}^{c}\mathcal{H}_{k}
for this projection.

We formulate the next result as a separate lemma for reference purposes.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{c}\mathcal{H}_{k}
be a Hall basis of
\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}
and let
k\geq 1
be an integer.
Then the natural projections of the elements in
\mathcal{H}_{k}
form a basis of
\gamma_{k}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})/\gamma_{k+1}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})
.

Proof.

Recall from (
2.2
) that

\frac{\gamma_{k}(N_{r,c})}{\gamma_{k+1}(N_{r,c})}\otimes\mathbb{C}\cong\frac{\gamma_{k}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})}{\gamma_{k+1}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})}.

Additionally, by the Third Isomorphism Theorem,

\frac{\gamma_{k}(N_{r,c})}{\gamma_{k+1}(N_{r,c})}\cong\frac{\gamma_{k}(F(r))}{\gamma_{k+1}(F(r))}.

Now, by
[
41
, Satz 4]
, the latter group is free abelian of rank
N(r,k)
.
Therefore, the vector space

\frac{\gamma_{k}(N_{r,c})}{\gamma_{k+1}(N_{r,c})}\otimes\mathbb{C}

has dimension
N(r,k)
.

Since the natural projection of
\mathcal{H}_{k}
generates
\gamma_{k}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})/\gamma_{k+1}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})
and
\mathcal{H}_{k}
contains
N(r,k)
elements, it forms a basis of
\gamma_{k}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})/\gamma_{k+1}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})
.

∎

Given
\varphi\in\End(N_{r,c})
, we now express, using a Hall basis, all eigenvalues of the induced maps
\varphi_{i}
in terms of those of
\varphi_{1}
.
Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}
be
r
indeterminates.
Given a Hall basis
\mathcal{H}
of
\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}
, we define a map
\eta:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
as follows: for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
, we put
\eta(X_{i}):=x_{i}
.
This defines
\eta
on
\mathcal{H}_{1}
.
Now, suppose that
\eta
is defined on
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathcal{H}_{i}
for some
n\geq 2
.
Let
X\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
.
Then
X=[Y,Z]
for some
Y\in\mathcal{H}_{k},Z\in\mathcal{H}_{l}
where
k+l=n
.
We then define

\eta(X):=\eta(Y)\eta(Z).

Thus,
\eta([X_{1},X_{2}])=\eta(X_{1})\eta(X_{2})=x_{1}x_{2}
, for example.

Lastly, for an
r
-tuple
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
, we define two maps.
We define
\ev_{\lambda}
to be the evaluation map associated to
\lambda
, i.e.

\ev_{\lambda}:\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]\to\mathbb{C}:p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})\mapsto p(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}).

We then define, given a Hall basis
\mathcal{H}
of
\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}
, the map
\eta_{\lambda}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{C}
to be the composition
{\ev_{\lambda}}\circ\eta
.

Lemma 2.7

(
[
13
, Lemma 3.5]
)
.

Let
\varphi\in\End(N_{r,c})
and let, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,c\}
,
\varphi_{i}
denote the induced endomorphism on
\gamma_{i}(N_{r,c})/\gamma_{i+1}(N_{r,c})
.
Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})
be the eigenvalues of
\varphi_{1}
, where each eigenvalue is listed as many times as its multiplicity.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hall basis of
\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}
.
Then, for each
k\in\{1,\ldots,c\}
, the eigenvalues of
\varphi_{k}
are given by

\{\eta_{\lambda}(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\}.

(2.3)

Each eigenvalue is also listed as many times as its multiplicity.

Proof.

Let
\varphi_{*}
denote the induced Lie algebra morphism on
\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}
.
Since the semisimple part of
\varphi_{*}
is also a Lie algebra morphism on
\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}
(see
[
33
, Corollary 2, p. 135]
), we may assume that
\varphi_{*}
itself is semisimple.
As discussed earlier, the eigenvalues of the induced map on
\gamma_{k}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})/\gamma_{k+1}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})
are the same as those of
\varphi_{k}
.

Since
\varphi_{*}
is semisimple, we can find a basis of
\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}
consisting of eigenvectors of
\varphi_{*}
.
In particular, we can find
r
eigenvectors
E_{1},\ldots,E_{r}
, with respective eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}
such that their images under the natural projection to
\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}/\gamma_{2}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})
form a basis of this quotient space.
This implies that
E_{1},\ldots,E_{r}
freely generate
\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}
.
Consequently, we can construct a Hall basis
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
of
\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}
with
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{1}=\{E_{1},\ldots,E_{r}\}
(in that order).

Next, define
\eta^{\prime}:\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\to\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
analogously to
\eta
and put
\eta^{\prime}_{\lambda}:={\ev_{\lambda}}\circ\eta^{\prime}
.
We prove that every
X
in
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is an eigenvector of
\varphi_{*}
with eigenvalue
\eta^{\prime}_{\lambda}(X)
.
Indeed, by construction, this is true for each
X\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{1}
.
Inductively, suppose it holds for all elements in
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{i}
for some
n\geq 2
.
Let
X\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{n}
be arbitrary.
Then
X=[Y,Z]
for some
Y,Z\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{i}
.
By the induction hypothesis,
\varphi_{*}(Y)=\eta^{\prime}_{\lambda}(Y)Y
and
\varphi_{*}(Z)=\eta^{\prime}_{\lambda}(Z)Z
.
Then

\displaystyle\varphi_{*}(X)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{*}([Y,Z])

\displaystyle=[\varphi_{*}(Y),\varphi_{*}(Z)]

\displaystyle=[\eta^{\prime}_{\lambda}(Y)Y,\eta^{\prime}_{\lambda}(Z)Z]

\displaystyle=\eta^{\prime}_{\lambda}(Y)\eta^{\prime}_{\lambda}(Z)[Y,Z]

\displaystyle=\eta^{\prime}_{\lambda}(X)X.

This proves that the Hall basis
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
consists entirely of eigenvectors of
\varphi_{*}
.

Finally, from
Lemma

2.6
, it follows that the eigenvalues of the induced map on
\gamma_{k}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})/\gamma_{k+1}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})
, and thus also those of
\varphi_{k}
, are precisely given by
\{\eta^{\prime}_{\lambda}(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{k}\}
, listed with multiplicity.

To completely finish the proof, we have to argue that
\{\eta^{\prime}_{\lambda}(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{k}\}
equals (
2.3
), for
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
are not (necessarily) equal.
We proceed in three steps:

Step 1:

The expression
\{\eta^{\prime}_{\lambda}(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{k}\}
does not depend on the order we choose in
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{k}
for
k\geq 2
in the construction of
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
.
Indeed, to prove that
\{\eta^{\prime}_{\lambda}(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{k}\}
consists precisely of all eigenvalues of
\varphi_{*,k}
, we never use this order.

Step 2:

Let
f:\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}\to\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}
be the isomorphism determined by
f(X_{i})=E_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
.
If we order the set
f(\mathcal{H})
by putting
f(X)<f(Y)
if and only if
X<Y
for all
X,Y\in\mathcal{H}
, then
f(\mathcal{H})
is also a Hall basis of
\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}
.
By the previous step, replacing
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
with
f(\mathcal{H})
does not change the expression
\{\eta^{\prime}_{\lambda}(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{k}\}
.

Step 3:

Finally, we prove that
\eta(X)=(\eta^{\prime}\circ f)(X)
for all
X\in\mathcal{H}
.
For
X\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
, this is true by construction of all three maps.
So, suppose it holds for all
X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
with
k<n
.
Let
X=[Y,Z]\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
for some
Y\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
and
Z\in\mathcal{H}_{l}
with
k,l<n
.
Then

\displaystyle\eta(X)=\eta([Y,Z])

\displaystyle=\eta(Y)\eta(Z)

\displaystyle=\eta^{\prime}(f(Y))\eta^{\prime}(f(Z))

\displaystyle=\eta^{\prime}([f(Y),f(Z)])

\displaystyle=\eta^{\prime}(f([Y,Z]))

\displaystyle=\eta^{\prime}(f(X)).

Therefore,

\{\eta^{\prime}_{\lambda}(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{k}\}=\{\eta^{\prime}_{\lambda}(f(X))\mid X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\}=\{\eta_{\lambda}(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\}.

This finishes the proof.
∎

Combining this with
Theorem

2.2
, we find that, for integers
r\geq 2
and
c\geq 2
and for
\varphi\in\End(N_{r,c})
,

R(\varphi)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|{\prod_{k=1}^{c}\prod_{X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}(1-\eta_{\lambda}(X))}}}\right|_{\infty}.

For instance, on
N_{3,2}
, we find that

R(\varphi)=|{(1-\lambda_{1})(1-\lambda_{2})(1-\lambda_{3})(1-\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2})(1-\lambda_{1}\lambda_{3})(1-\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3})}|_{\infty}.

In other words,
R(\varphi)
is (the
|{\cdot}|_{\infty}
-norm of) a polynomial in terms of the eigenvalues of
\varphi_{1}
, i.e. there are polynomials
f_{r,k}\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
for
k\in\{1,\ldots,c\}
such that

R(\varphi)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|{\prod_{k=1}^{c}f_{r,k}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})}}}\right|_{\infty},

namely

f_{r,k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})=\prod_{X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}(1-\eta(X)).

A crucial observation is that these polynomials
f_{r,k}
do not depend on
\varphi
: the map
\eta:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
completely determines which products of the
x_{i}
occur and it is independent of
\lambda
.
We do, however, have to argue that the image
\eta(\mathcal{H})
does not depend on
\mathcal{H}
.

To that end, note that the proof of
Lemma

2.7
from the second paragraph onwards holds for
any
endomorphism
\psi
on
\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}
:
if
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}
are the eigenvalues of the induced endomorphism
\psi_{1}:\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}/\gamma_{2}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})\to\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}/\gamma_{2}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})
, then, for each
k\in\{1,\ldots,c\}
, those of
\psi_{k}:\gamma_{k}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})/\gamma_{k+1}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})\to\gamma_{k}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})/\gamma_{k+1}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})
are given by (
2.3
).
This implies that, for every
k\geq 1
, the set

\{\eta_{\lambda}(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\}=\ev_{\lambda}(\{\eta(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\})

is independent of
\mathcal{H}
for all choices of
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
.
Indeed, every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
induces an endomorphism
\psi
of
\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}
by putting
\psi(Y_{i}):=\lambda_{i}Y_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
for some freely generating set
\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{r}\}
of
\mathfrak{f}_{r}
.
As
\mathbb{C}
is an infinite field, this implies that
\{\eta(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\}
itself must be independent of
\mathcal{H}
, for all
k\geq 1
.

Thus, if we know what the polynomials
f_{r,k}
are, we can compute
\SpecR(N_{r,c})
by running over all automorphisms
\varphi
of
N_{r,c}
, computing the eigenvalues of
\varphi_{1}
, and plugging those into the
f_{r,k}
.

However, computing these eigenvalues is quite hard, since most of the time, there are no closed expressions for them.
To simplify the process of computing Reidemeister numbers, we argue that the
f_{r,k}
are symmetric polynomials.
A polynomial
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
is called
symmetric
if

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=f(x_{{\sigma}^{-1}(1)},\ldots,x_{{\sigma}^{-1}(n)})

for each
\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(n)
.
The ring of all symmetric polynomials in
n
variables with integer coefficients is denoted by
\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]^{\mathrm{Sym}(n)}
.

To prove that the
f_{r,k}
are symmetric, let
\psi\in\End(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})
and let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})
be the eigenvalues of
\psi_{1}
.
As argued before, the eigenvalues of
\psi_{k}
are given by

\{\eta_{\lambda}(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\}=\ev_{\lambda}(\{\eta(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\})

Let
\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(r)
be a permutation.
Then
\lambda_{\sigma}:=(\lambda_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,\lambda_{\sigma(r)})
are still the eigenvalues of
\psi_{1}
, only in a different order.
Consequently, the eigenvalues of
\psi_{k}
are also given by

\{\eta_{\lambda_{\sigma}}(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\}=\ev_{\lambda_{\sigma}}(\{\eta(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\}).

Therefore,

\ev_{\lambda}(\{\eta(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\})=\ev_{\lambda_{\sigma}}(\{\eta(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\}).

This equality holds for all
\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(r)
.
Moreover, it holds for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
as well, since each
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
induces an endomorphism of
\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}
with eigenvalues given by
\lambda
(as argued earlier).

We can rewrite the previous equality as

\ev_{\lambda}(\{\eta(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\})=\ev_{\lambda}({\sigma}^{-1}\cdot\{\eta(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\}),

where
\tau\in\mathrm{Sym}(r)
acts on
\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
as follows:

\tau\cdot f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}):=f(x_{{\tau}^{-1}(1)},\ldots,x_{{\tau}^{-1}(r)}).

Since, for all
\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(r)
, this equality holds for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
and since
\mathbb{C}
is an infinite field, we must have the equality

\{\eta(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\}={\sigma}^{-1}\cdot\{\eta(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\},

for all
\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(r)
.
Equivalently,

\{\eta(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\}=\sigma\cdot\{\eta(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\},

This, finally, implies that

f_{r,k}=\prod_{X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}(1-\eta(X))

is a symmetric polynomial for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,c\}
.
Thus, we have proven the following:

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
r\geq 2
and
c\geq 2
be integers.
There exist symmetric polynomials
f_{r,k}\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]^{\mathrm{Sym}(r)}
(with
1\leq k\leq c
) such that, for any
\varphi\in\End(N_{r,c})
,

R(\varphi)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|{\prod_{k=1}^{c}f_{r,k}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})}}}\right|_{\infty},

where
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}
are the eigenvalues of
\varphi_{1}
.

For instance, for
r=3
and
k\in\{1,2\}
, the polynomials are

f_{3,1}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\prod_{i=1}^{3}(1-x_{i})

and

f_{3,2}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}(1-x_{i}x_{j}).

The next step is to use this observation to express the Reidemeister number of
\varphi
purely in terms of the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of
\varphi_{1}
.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
n,k\geq 1
be integers with
k\leq n
.
The
elementary symmetric polynomial of degree
k
in
n
variables
is the symmetric polynomial

e_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}):=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}\leq n}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}.

Let
p_{\varphi}=\sum_{i=0}^{r}a_{i}x^{i}
be the characteristic polynomial of
\varphi_{1}
with
a_{r}=1
and let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}
be the eigenvalues of
\varphi_{1}
, i.e. the zeroes of
p_{\varphi}
.
Then

\sum_{i=0}^{r}a_{i}x^{i}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(x-\lambda_{i}).

Therefore, Vieta’s relations yield

a_{r-i}=(-1)^{i}e_{i}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}).

for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,r-1\}
.
So, to express
R(\varphi)
in terms of the coefficients of
p_{\varphi}
, it is sufficient to show that the polynomials
f_{r,k}
can be expressed in terms of the elementary symmetric polynomials in
r
variables.
This is possible due to the Fundamental Theorem of Symmetric Polynomials:

Theorem 2.10

(Fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials,
[
38
, Theorem 1.12]
)
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer and let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]^{\mathrm{Sym}(n)}
.
Then there is a unique polynomial
g\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
such that

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=g(e_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),\ldots,e_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})).

All together, we obtain

Theorem 2.11
.

There exist polynomials
\tilde{g}_{r,k}\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
for
1\leq k\leq c
such that, for any
\varphi\in\End(N_{r,c})
, if
p_{\varphi}=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{r}a_{i}x^{i}
is the characteristic polynomial of
\varphi_{1}\in\End(N_{r,c}/\gamma_{2}(N_{r,c}))
with
a_{r}=1
, then

R(\varphi)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|{\prod_{k=1}^{c}\tilde{g}_{r,k}(a_{0},\ldots,a_{r-1})}}}\right|_{\infty}.

Proof.

By
Theorem

2.8
,

R(\varphi)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|{\prod_{k=1}^{c}f_{r,k}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})}}}\right|_{\infty}

for some symmetric polynomials
f_{r,k}\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]^{\mathrm{Sym}(r)}
.
By
Theorem

2.10
, there exist polynomials
g_{r,k}\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
for
k\in\{1,\ldots,c\}
such that

f_{r,k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})=g_{r,k}(e_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}),\ldots,e_{r}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})).

Hence, by Vieta’s relations mentioned earlier,

\displaystyle f_{r,k}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})

\displaystyle=g_{r,k}((-1)^{1}a_{r-1},(-1)^{2}a_{r-2},\ldots,(-1)^{r}a_{0})

\displaystyle=\tilde{g}_{r,k}(a_{0},\ldots,a_{r-1})

where

\tilde{g}_{r,k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}):=g_{r,k}((-1)^{1}x_{r},(-1)^{2}x_{r-1},\ldots,(-1)^{r}x_{1}).\qed

To continue the example we gave earlier, we find that

\displaystyle f_{3,1}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{3}(1-x_{i})

\displaystyle=1-e_{1}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})+e_{2}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})-e_{3}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}),

so

g_{3,1}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=1-x_{1}+x_{2}-x_{3}

and

\tilde{g}_{3,1}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=g_{3,1}(-x_{3},x_{2},-x_{1})=1+x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle f_{3,2}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})

\displaystyle=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}(1-x_{i}x_{j})

\displaystyle=1-e_{2}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})+e_{1}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})e_{3}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})-e_{3}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{2},

so

g_{3,2}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=1-x_{2}+x_{1}x_{3}-x_{3}^{2}

and

\tilde{g}_{3,2}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=g_{3,2}(-x_{3},x_{2},-x_{1})=1-x_{2}+x_{1}x_{3}-x_{1}^{2}.

This theorem shows that there is actually no need to compute the eigenvalues of
\varphi_{*}
in order to determine Reidemeister numbers on
N_{r,c}
.
The Reidemeister number of
\varphi\in\End(N_{r,c})
can be computed using only the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial
p_{\varphi}
of
\varphi_{1}
.
K. Dekimpe, S. Tertooy and A. Vargas use this idea to determine the Reidemeister spectrum of
N_{r,2}
.

In addition, they elaborate on which characteristic polynomials can occur.
If
\varphi\in\Aut(N_{r,c})
, then all the induced maps
\varphi_{i}
are automorphisms as well.
Thus, we get a map

\omega:\Aut(N_{r,c})\to\Aut(\mathbb{Z}^{r}):\varphi\mapsto\varphi_{1}.

Since
\varphi_{1}
is an automorphism and
\Aut(\mathbb{Z}^{r})\cong\GL(r,\mathbb{Z})
, it follows that
a_{0}=\det(\varphi_{1})=\pm 1
.
Thus, for every
\varphi\in\Aut(N_{r,c})
, the characteristic polynomial of
\varphi_{1}=\omega(\varphi)
is monic of degree
r
with integral coefficients and with constant coefficient equal to
\pm 1
.

Conversely, they argue that
every
monic polynomial of degree
r
with integral coefficients and with constant coefficient equal to
\pm 1
occurs as the characteristic polynomial of
\omega(\varphi)
for some
\varphi\in N_{r,c}
.
To that end, we first note that the map
\Aut(F_{r})\to\Aut(\mathbb{Z}^{r})
, defined analogously to
\omega
, is surjective (see
[
31
, Theorem N4, Section 3.5]
).
Since every automorphism of
F_{r}
induces one on
N_{r,c}
, the map
\omega
is surjective as well.

Secondly, given a polynomial

p(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{r}a_{i}x^{i}

with
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
,
a_{r}=1
and
a_{0}=\pm 1
, its companion matrix

C(p):=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\ldots&0&-a_{0}\\
1&0&\ldots&0&-a_{1}\\
0&1&\ldots&0&-a_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&1&-a_{r-1}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r\times r}

has
p(x)
as characteristic polynomial.
Thus, given
p(x)
, we obtain an element of
\Aut(\mathbb{Z}^{r})
, which in turns yields an automorphism of
N_{r,c}
via
\omega
.

Summarised, to compute the Reidemeister spectrum of
N_{r,c}
, it is sufficient to determine the polynomials
\tilde{g}_{r,k}
from
Theorem

2.11
for
k\in\{1,\ldots,c\}
and then plug in all the possibilities of the coefficients of a monic polynomial of degree
r
with integer coefficients and with constant coefficient equal to
\pm 1
.
Therefore, we shift our focus to determining these polynomials
\tilde{g}_{r,k}
.

However, from the perspective of symmetric polynomials, it is more logical to find the polynomials
g_{r,k}
from the proof of
Theorem

2.11
, namely the polynomials expressing
R(\varphi)
in terms of the elementary symmetric polynomials (evaluated in
\lambda_{i}
).
Thus, in the remainder of this article, we describe a method to determine the polynomials
g_{r,k}
and link it to the open problem of the plethysm of Schur functions, which we mentioned in the introduction.
We would like to mention that S. Tertooy has determined the polynomials
g_{r,k}
for several values of
r
and
k
in his PhD-thesis
[
39
, Chapter 5]
.

3.1
Symmetric functions

In this section, we introduce most of the necessary concepts concerning symmetric functions.
Our exposition is loosely based on the one by R. Stanley
[
37
, § 7]
, but differs in some places.

Let
A
be the set of all sequences of non-negative integers whose support is finite, i.e.

A:=\{\alpha=(\alpha_{i})_{i\geq 1}\mid\forall i\geq 1:\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\text{ $\supp(\alpha)$ finite}\}.

The group
\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})
acts in a natural way on
A
:

\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})\times A\to A:(\sigma,\alpha)\mapsto\sigma\cdot\alpha:=(\alpha_{{\sigma}^{-1}(i)})_{i\geq 1}.

Let
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\ldots)
be a list of formal variables.
Given
\alpha\in A
, we define

x^{\alpha}:=\prod_{i\geq 1}x_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}.

Since
\alpha
has finite support, this product is a well-defined monomial.
We define the
ring of formal power series in a countable infinite number of variables
to be the set of all expressions of the form

f=\sum_{\alpha\in A}c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha},

(3.1)

where
c_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
\alpha\in A
.
To make this set into a ring, we equip it with pointwise addition and with the following multiplication:

\sum_{\alpha\in A}c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}\cdot\sum_{\alpha\in A}d_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}:=\sum_{\alpha\in A}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta,\gamma\in A\\
\beta+\gamma=\alpha\end{subarray}}(c_{\beta}+d_{\gamma})x^{\alpha}.

Since for each
\alpha\in A
there are only finitely many couples
(\beta,\gamma)\in A\times A
with
\beta+\gamma=\alpha
, this product is well defined.
We let
\mathbb{Z}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\ldots]]
denote this ring.
For
\alpha\in A
and
f\in\mathbb{Z}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\ldots]]
as in (
3.1
), we also write
[x^{\alpha}]f:=c_{\alpha}
.

A formal power series is called a
symmetric function
if, in addition,
c_{\alpha}=c_{\sigma\cdot\alpha}
for all
\alpha\in A
and
\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})
.
Since any two symmetric functions can be added and multiplied and the result is again a symmetric function, the symmetric functions form a subring of
\mathbb{Z}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\ldots]]
, which is denoted by
\Lambda
.

The
degree
of a monomial
x^{\alpha}
is given by
\deg(x^{\alpha}):=\sum_{i\geq 1}\alpha_{i}
and the degree of a symmetric function
f
is

\deg(f):=\sup\{\deg(x^{\alpha})\mid[x^{\alpha}]f\neq 0\}.

Note that, with this definition,
\deg(f)=\infty
is possible, and this is where our definition differs from the one by R. Stanley; in his definition, symmetric functions always have a finite degree.
If
n:=\deg(f)=\deg(x^{\alpha})
for all
\alpha\in A
with
[x^{\alpha}]f\neq 0
, then we call
f

homogeneous of degree
n
.
Given a symmetric function
f=\sum_{\alpha\in A}c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}
, we can write it as the (infinite) sum of homogeneous functions
f_{n}
given by

f_{n}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in A\\
\deg(x^{\alpha})=n\end{subarray}}c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}.

To illustrate these definitions, consider the power series

f=\sum_{i}x_{i}

and

g=\sum_{i<j}x_{i}x_{j}^{2}.

Then
f
is symmetric, whereas
g
is not, since
[x_{1}x_{2}^{2}]g=1
, but
[x_{1}^{2}x_{2}]g=0
.
Both
f
and
g
are homogeneous, with
f
having degree
1
and
g
degree
3
.
Their product is given by

fg=\sum_{i<j}x_{i}^{2}x_{j}^{2}+\sum_{i<j}x_{i}x_{j}^{3}+\sum_{k\neq i<j\neq k}x_{i}x_{j}^{2}x_{k}.

Since we want to be able to go back from symmetric functions to symmetric polynomials, we have to construct a way to do so.
For every integer
n\geq 0
, we define

A_{n}:=\{\alpha\in A\mid\supp(\alpha)\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\}.

Note that
A_{0}=\{(0,\ldots,0,\ldots)\}
and that
A_{n}\subseteq A_{n+1}
for every
n\geq 0
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer.
The
ring of formal power series in
n
variables
is the set of all power series of the form

f=\sum_{\alpha\in A_{n}}c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}.

where
c_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
\alpha\in A_{n}
, equipped with the same addition and multiplication as
\mathbb{Z}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\ldots]]
.
We let
\mathbb{Z}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]]
denote this ring.

The group
\mathrm{Sym}(n)
acts in a similar way on
A_{n}
as
\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})
does on
A
:

\mathrm{Sym}(n)\times A_{n}\to A_{n}:(\tau,\alpha)\mapsto\tau\cdot\alpha,

where

(\tau\cdot\alpha)_{i}:=\begin{cases}\alpha_{{\tau}^{-1}(i)}&\mbox{if }i\leq n\\
\alpha_{i}&\mbox{if }i>n.\end{cases}

Hence, we can also define the
ring of symmetric formal power series in
n
variables
, which is denoted by
\mathbb{Z}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]]^{\mathrm{Sym}(n)}
.
Note that we can view
\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]^{\mathrm{Sym}(n)}
, the ring of symmetric polynomials in
n
variables, as a subring of
\mathbb{Z}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]]^{\mathrm{Sym}(n)}
.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer.
We define the map

\Phi_{n}:\mathbb{Z}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\ldots]]\to\mathbb{Z}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]]:f=\sum_{\alpha\in A}c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}\mapsto\Phi_{n}(f):=\sum_{\alpha\in A_{n}}c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}.

In other words,
\Phi_{n}
‘forgets’ all monomials that contain a variable
x_{i}
with
i>n
.
We will sometimes write
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
to denote
\Phi_{n}(f)
.
Clearly,
\Phi_{n}
maps
\Lambda
into
\mathbb{Z}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]]^{\mathrm{Sym}(n)}
, but for certain
f\in\Lambda
,
\Phi_{n}(f)
is even an element of
\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]^{\mathrm{Sym}(n)}
, for all
n\geq 1
.
For instance,

\Phi_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i}x_{i}}}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]^{\mathrm{Sym}(n)}

for all
n\geq 1
.
However,

\Phi_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i,j}x_{i}^{j}}}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j}x_{i}^{j}\notin\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]^{\mathrm{Sym}(n)}.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\ldots]]
.
Then
f\in\Lambda
if and only if there exists an
M\geq 1
such that
\Phi_{n}(f)\in\mathbb{Z}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]]^{\mathrm{Sym}(n)}
for all
n\geq M
.

Proof.

The ‘only if’ is straightforward by taking
M=1
.
So, suppose that
f\in\mathbb{Z}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\ldots]]
is such that
\Phi_{n}(f)\in\mathbb{Z}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]]^{\mathrm{Sym}(n)}
for all
n\geq M
for some
M\geq 1
.
Write
f=\sum_{\alpha\in A}c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}
.
For every
n\geq M
,
\Phi_{n}(f)
is symmetric; since

\Phi_{n}(f)=\sum_{\beta\in A_{n}}c_{\beta}x^{\beta},

this means that
c_{\beta}=c_{\tau\cdot\beta}
for all
\beta\in A_{n}
and
\tau\in\mathrm{Sym}(n)
.
Now, let
\alpha\in A
and
\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})
be arbitrary.
Since
\alpha
has finite support, so does
\sigma\cdot\alpha
.
Hence, there exists an
n\geq M
such that
\supp(\alpha)\cup\supp(\sigma\cdot\alpha)\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
In particular,
\alpha\in A_{n}
.
We now construct a permutation
\tau
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
\tau\cdot\alpha=\sigma\cdot\alpha
.
Since

\displaystyle\supp(\sigma\cdot\alpha)

\displaystyle=\{i\geq 1\mid(\sigma\cdot\alpha)_{i}\neq 0\}

\displaystyle=\{i\geq 1\mid\alpha_{{\sigma}^{-1}(i)}\neq 0\}

\displaystyle=\sigma(\supp(\alpha)),

and thus
\sigma(\supp(\alpha))\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we can define
\tau(i):=\sigma(i)
for every
i\in\supp(\alpha)
to get a bijective map
\tau:\supp(\alpha)\to\sigma(\supp(\alpha))
.
Since both the domain and the codomain are subsets of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
of the same size, we can extend this map to a bijection on
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
This can be done in various ways, but we just fix one, which we also write as
\tau
.
We claim that
\sigma\cdot\alpha=\tau\cdot\alpha
.
Let
i\geq 1
be arbitrary.
First, suppose that
(\sigma\cdot\alpha)_{i}\neq 0
.
Then
{\sigma}^{-1}(i)\in\supp(\alpha)
.
Hence,
\tau({\sigma}^{-1}(i))=\sigma({\sigma}^{-1}(i))=i
.
Consequently,
{\tau}^{-1}(i)={\sigma}^{-1}(i)
and

(\tau\cdot\alpha)_{i}=\alpha_{{\tau}^{-1}(i)}=\alpha_{{\sigma}^{-1}(i)}=(\sigma\cdot\alpha)_{i}.

Conversely, suppose that
(\tau\cdot\alpha)_{i}\neq 0
.
Then
{\tau}^{-1}(i)\in\supp(\alpha)
.
So,
i=\tau({\tau}^{-1}(i))=\sigma({\tau}^{-1}(i))
, which again implies that
{\tau}^{-1}(i)={\sigma}^{-1}(i)
.
Consequently,

(\sigma\cdot\alpha)_{i}=\alpha_{{\sigma}^{-1}(i)}=\alpha_{{\tau}^{-1}(i)}=(\tau\cdot\alpha)_{i}.

Summarised, we have proven that
\sigma\cdot\alpha
and
\tau\cdot\alpha
have the same support and moreover agree on these supports.
Hence, they are equal.

Since
\alpha\in A_{n},\tau\in\mathrm{Sym}(n)
and
\sigma\cdot\alpha=\tau\cdot\alpha
, the equalities

c_{\alpha}=c_{\tau\cdot\alpha}=c_{\sigma\cdot\alpha}

hold.
As
\alpha\in A
and
\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{Z}_{>0})
are arbitrary, we conclude that
f
is symmetric.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
f,g\in\mathbb{Z}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\ldots]]
.
Then
f=g
if and only if there exists an
M\geq 1
such that
\Phi_{n}(f)=\Phi_{n}(g)
for all
n\geq M
.

Proof.

The ‘only if’ is trivial.
So, suppose that
f,g\in\mathbb{Z}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\ldots]]
and
M\geq 1
are such that
\Phi_{n}(f)=\Phi_{n}(g)
for all
n\geq M
.
Write
f=\sum_{\alpha\in A}c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}
and
g=\sum_{\alpha\in A}d_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}
.
Let
\alpha\in A
.
Since
\alpha
has finite support, there is an
n\geq M
such that
\alpha\in A_{n}
.
The equality
\Phi_{n}(f)=\Phi_{n}(g)
then implies that
c_{\alpha}=d_{\alpha}
.
As
\alpha
is arbitrary, the result follows.
∎

The elementary symmetric polynomials have their counterpart in the realm of symmetric functions.
To introduce the latter, we need the concept of a partition.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer.
A
partition
of
n
is a non-increasing sequence
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k},\ldots)
of non-negative integers whose sum equals
n
.
We write
\lambda\vdash n
.

The set of all partitions of
n
is denoted by
\Par(n)
, the set of all partitions by
\Par
.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
n\geq 1
and let
\lambda\in\Par
be a partition.
We define the
elementary symmetric functions
as

e_{n}:=\sum_{i_{1}<\ldots<i_{n}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{n}}

and

e_{\lambda}:=\prod_{i\geq 1}e_{\lambda_{i}}.

Since
\lambda
has finite support, this product is well defined.
Also, the elementary symmetric functions are mapped to the elementary symmetric polynomials under
\Phi_{n}
, i.e.

\Phi_{n}(e_{m})=e_{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

for all
1\leq m\leq n
, and
\Phi_{n}(e_{m})=0
for
m>n
.
Here, we see that in fact
\Phi_{n}(e_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]^{\mathrm{Sym}(n)}
.

Theorem 3.7
.

Each symmetric function
f\in\Lambda
can be written as a series of elementary symmetric functions, i.e. there exist
c_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\lambda\in\Par
such that

f=\sum_{\lambda\in\Par}c_{\lambda}e_{\lambda}.

Proof.

Write
f=\sum_{n\geq 1}f_{n}
in its homogeneous parts.
In
[
37
, 7.4.4. Theorem]
, it is proven that each
f_{n}
can be written as

f_{n}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Par(n)}c_{\lambda}e_{\lambda}

for some
c_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
By combining all of these expressions, we obtain the series for
f
.
∎

We next properly define the notion of plethysm.
We remark that there are other equivalent ways of defining the plethysm of two symmetric functions, see e.g.
[
29
, § 2]
and
[
30
,
\nopp
I.8]
.
The definition below suits our purposes the best.

Definition 3.8
.

Let
f,g
be two symmetric functions.
Suppose that
g=\sum_{i\geq 1}x^{\alpha_{i}}
where
\alpha_{i}\in A
for each
i\geq 1
.
The
plethysm
f[g]
is then defined as

f[g]:=f(x^{\alpha_{1}},x^{\alpha_{2}},\ldots).

It is important here to note that we write
g
as a sum of
monomials
, so that one monomial can occur several times in this summation.
For instance,

\displaystyle e_{2}[2e_{1}]

\displaystyle=e_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x_{1}+x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{2}+\ldots}}\right]

\displaystyle=e_{2}(x_{1},x_{1},x_{2},x_{2},\ldots)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\geq 1}x_{i}^{2}+4\sum_{i<j}x_{i}x_{j},

whereas
e_{2}[e_{1}]
is just
e_{2}
.
Thus,
e_{2}[2e_{1}]\neq 2e_{2}[e_{1}]
.

3.2
From symmetric polynomials to symmetric functions

The polynomials
f_{r,k}
from
Theorem

2.8
are often given as a product, e.g.

f_{r,2}=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq r}(1-x_{i}x_{j}).

To move from symmetric polynomials to symmetric functions, we want to make sense of expressions of the form

\prod_{i<j}(1-x_{i}x_{j}).

Intuitively, we do so by working out the product distributively, but choosing in all but finitely many factors the term
1
.
More rigorously, we proceed as follows:

Definition 3.9
.

Let
\{x^{\alpha_{i}}\}_{i\geq 1}
be a collection of monomials.
We define

\prod_{i\geq 1}(1-x^{\alpha_{i}}):=1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\sum_{i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}}x^{\alpha_{i_{1}}}\ldots x^{\alpha_{i_{k}}},

provided all the latter infinite sums are well defined.
If all the infinite sums are well defined, we say the product
converges
.

For example, the product

\prod_{i}(1-x_{i})

equals

1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\sum_{i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}}x_{i_{1}}\ldots x_{i_{k}}=1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}e_{k}.

On the other hand, the product

\prod_{i}(1-x_{1})

is not defined, for the sum for
k=1
equals
\sum_{i_{1}}x_{1}
.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
\{x^{\alpha_{i}}\}_{i\geq 1}
be a collection of monomials such that

\prod_{i\geq 1}(1-x^{\alpha_{i}})

converges and

g:=\sum_{i\geq 1}x^{\alpha_{i}}

is well defined.
Suppose both are symmetric as well.
Then

\prod_{i\geq 1}(1-x^{\alpha_{i}})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}e_{n}[g].

Proof.

On both sides, we determine which products of monomials occur.
Let
k\geq 1
and
1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}
be arbitrary.
On the left-hand side, we get the term
(-1)^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{k}x^{\alpha_{i_{j}}}
.
This same term occurs on the right-hand side in the expansion of
(-1)^{k}e_{k}[g]
, since
x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}
is a monomial occurring in
e_{k}
and the plethysm
e_{k}[g]
substitutes
x_{i_{j}}=x^{\alpha_{i_{j}}}
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
.

Conversely, let
n\geq 1
be arbitrary and consider
(-1)^{n}e_{n}[g]
.
Every term in this plethysm is of the form
(-1)^{n}x^{\alpha_{i_{1}}}\cdots x^{\alpha_{i_{n}}}
for some
1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{n}
, which clearly occurs on the left-hand side as well.
∎

We construct an infinite analogue of the Hall basis.
Let
\mathfrak{f}_{\infty}
be the free Lie algebra of countable infinite rank and suppose it is freely generated by the elements
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},\ldots
.
We construct a totally ordered set, which we will call a
Hall set

\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
, in a similar way as we constructed the Hall basis of
\mathfrak{f}_{r}
, namely in an inductive way as
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\infty}
:

•

\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\infty}:=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},\ldots\}
and
X_{1}<\ldots<X_{n}<\ldots

•

For
n\geq 2
, assume that
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\infty}
up to
\mathcal{H}_{n-1}^{\infty}
have been constructed and that
\bigcup_{k=1}^{n-1}\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\infty}
has been given a total order.
Then
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\infty}
is defined as the set of all Lie brackets
[Y,Z]
with
Y\in\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\infty}
and
Z\in\mathcal{H}_{l}^{\infty}
such that

–

k+l=n
,

–

Y<Z
,

–

if
Z=[Z_{1},Z_{2}]
for some
Z_{i}\in\mathcal{H}_{k_{i}}^{\infty}
, then
Z_{1}\leq Y
.

•

Finally, we extend the order to
\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\infty}
by picking any total order of
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\infty}
and imposing that
X<Y
for all
X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\infty}
and
Y\in\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\infty}
with
k<n
.

It is clear that, by construction, a Hall set on
\mathfrak{f}_{\infty}
projects down to a Hall basis of
\mathfrak{f}_{r}
for every
r\geq 2
.
We also extend the definition of the map
\eta
.
Let
\mathcal{M}:=\{x^{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in A\}
be the set of all monomials on the variables
\{x_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
.
Given a Hall set
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
on
\mathfrak{f}_{\infty}
, we define the map
\eta:\mathcal{H}^{\infty}\to\mathcal{M}
inductively.
We put
\eta(X_{i}):=x_{i}
for all
i\geq 1
, which defines
\eta
on
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\infty}
.
Now, suppose that
\eta
is defined on
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathcal{H}_{i}^{\infty}
.
Let
X\in\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\infty}
.
Then
X=[Y,Z]
for some
Y\in\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\infty},Z\in\mathcal{H}_{l}^{\infty}
where
k+l=n
.
We then define

\eta(X):=\eta(Y)\eta(Z).

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
k\geq 1
be arbitrary.
Define

F_{k}:=\sum_{X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\infty}}\eta(X).

and

f_{k}:=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}e_{i}[F_{k}],

Then, for all integers
r\geq 2

\Phi_{r}(f_{k})=f_{r,k}.

Proof.

Fix
r\geq 2
.
Let
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
be a Hall set on
\mathfrak{f}_{\infty}
.
In the discussion following
Lemma

2.7
, we argued that, if
\mathcal{H}^{r}
is a Hall basis of
\mathfrak{f}_{r}
, the set

\{\eta(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{H}^{r}_{k}\}

is symmetric (counted with multiplicity).
Consequently, the polynomial
F_{r,k}:=\sum_{X\in\mathcal{H}^{r}_{k}}\eta(X)
is also symmetric.
By construction of
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
,
\Phi_{r}(F_{k})=F_{r,k}
.
Since
F_{r,k}
is symmetric for each
r
,
Lemma

3.3
implies that
F_{k}
is a symmetric function.

By
Proposition

3.10
,

f_{k}=\prod_{X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\infty}}(1-\eta(X))

and therefore

\Phi_{r}(f_{k})=\Phi_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\prod_{X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\infty}}(1-\eta(X))}}\right)=\prod_{X\in\mathcal{H}^{r}_{k}}(1-\eta(X))=f_{r,k}

by definition of
\Phi_{r}
.
∎

Thus, combined with
Theorem

2.8
, we see that, for all
r\geq 2
and
c\geq 2
, all information about Reidemeister numbers on
N_{r,c}
is contained in the
f_{k}
for
k\in\{1,\ldots,c\}
.
We also start to see how the Reidemeister spectrum of
N_{r,c}
and plethysms are related.

Recall that our focus is to determine the polynomials
g_{r,k}
, which express the polynomials
f_{r,k}
in terms of elementary symmetric polynomials.
By
Theorem

3.7
,

f_{k}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Par}c_{\lambda,k}e_{\lambda}

for some
c_{\lambda,k}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The polynomial
g_{r,k}
can then be derived from this equality by applying
\Phi_{r}
to both sides.
In
Section

4
, we elaborate more on this and explain the behaviour we observed in the table at the beginning of this section.

3.3
Schur functions

The next step in our journey is to show how Schur functions come into play in the expressions from
Theorem

3.11
.
We start by formally introducing Schur functions and stating the major open problem regarding plethysms of Schur functions.
We opt for a more combinatorial way to do so, but there are many other ways.
All of them might seem unmotivated when first encountering Schur functions.
We have, however, tried to highlight their importance and/or naturality already in the introduction and will continue to do so further down this article.
For more information and motivation on Schur functions, we refer the reader to
[

, 7.10 & 7.15]
.

We need to introduce four concepts before we can define Schur functions.
Let
\lambda
and
\mu
be two partitions.

•

The

of
\lambda
is a graphical representation of
\lambda
consisting of a left-justified array of boxes below each other, where the

Theorem 3.12
.

Each symmetric function
f\in\Lambda
can be written as a series of Schur functions, i.e. there exist
c_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\lambda\in\Par
such that

f=\sum_{\lambda\in\Par}c_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}.

Proof.

We proceed in a similar fashion as we did for the elementary symmetric functions.
Write
f=\sum_{n\geq 1}f_{n}
in its homogeneous parts.
In
[
37
, 7.10.6 Corollary]
, it is proven that each
f_{n}
can be written as

f_{n}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Par(n)}c_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}

for some
c_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
By combining all of these expressions, we obtain the series for
f
.
∎

For instance,
e_{k}=s_{1^{k}}
for all
k\geq 1
, where
1^{k}
is a short-hand notation for the partition consisting of
k
ones.
This equality can be verified using the definition of both symmetric functions.
We refer to the sum
\sum_{\lambda\in\Par}c_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}
as the
Schur expansion
of
f
.

If all the
c_{\lambda}
are non-negative, we say that
f
is
Schur positive
.
The following then is a classical result:

Theorem 3.13

(
[
37
, A.2.7 Theorem]
)
.

Let
f,g
be two Schur positive symmetric functions.
Then
f[g]
is again Schur positive.

Thus, if
f
and
g
are Schur positive, then the
c_{\lambda}
in the Schur expansion of
f[g]
are non-negative integers.
Hence, they might admit a combinatorial interpretation.
For the case where
f
and
g
are Schur functions themselves, we get one of the major open problems in combinatorics:

Objective 3.14
.

Let
\lambda,\mu
be two partitions.
Determine the coefficients
a_{\lambda,\mu}^{\nu}
in the Schur expansion

s_{\lambda}[s_{\mu}]=\sum_{\nu}a_{\lambda,\mu}^{\nu}s_{\nu}.

Ultimately, we want to relate Reidemeister numbers on free nilpotent groups to this open problem.
In
Theorem

3.11
, we showed that
\Phi_{r}(f_{k})=f_{r,k}
for all
r\geq 2
and
k\geq 1
, where

F_{k}=\sum_{X\in\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\infty}}\eta(X)

and

f_{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}e_{i}[F_{k}].

Since
e_{i}=s_{1^{i}}
for all
i\geq 1
, we can write

f_{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}s_{1^{i}}[F_{k}].

We now argue that
F_{k}
is in fact Schur positive and, in some cases, even a Schur function.

3.4
Schur positivity of
F_{n}

Symmetric functions, and especially Schur functions, naturally occur in representation theory, in particular in the representation theory of
\mathrm{Sym}(n)
and of
\GL(n,\mathbb{C})
.
We provide a high level overview of both and prove a relation between them in the context of free Lie algebras.
This relation will yield a proof that the symmetric functions
F_{n}
are Schur positive.

The discussion of representations of
\GL(n,\mathbb{C})
below is based on
[
37
, Chapter 7, Appendix 2]
.

Definition 3.15
.

Let
n,m\geq 1
be two integers.
A representation
\rho:\GL(n,\mathbb{C})\to\GL(m,\mathbb{C})
is called
polynomial
if the entries of
\rho(A)
are polynomials in the entries of
A\in\GL(n,\mathbb{C})
.

An example of a polynomial representation is the determinant map

\det:\GL(n,\mathbb{C})\to\GL(1,\mathbb{C}):A\mapsto\det(A).

If
\rho:\GL(n,\mathbb{C})\to\GL(m,\mathbb{C})
is a polynomial representation, then there exists a multiset
\mathcal{M}_{\rho}
consisting of monomials in
n
variables such that, if
A\in\GL(n,\mathbb{C})
has eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
,
\rho(A)
has eigenvalues
\lambda^{\alpha}
, where
x^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{M}_{\rho}
.
We then define the
character
of
\rho
to be the polynomial

\Char(\rho):=\sum_{x^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{M}_{\rho}}x^{\alpha}.

From the definition, it readily follows that the character of a sum of polynomial representations is the sum of the characters.
Furthermore, the character of a polynomial representation is always a
symmetric
polynomial in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
: for
A\in\GL(n,\mathbb{C})
, any permutation
\lambda_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,\lambda_{\sigma(n)}
of the eigenvalues of
A
must yield the same eigenvalues of
\rho(A)
.

The relation between Schur functions and representation theory of
\GL(n,\mathbb{C})
is captured in the following theorem.
For a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k},\ldots)
, we define the
length
to be the maximal
i
such that
\lambda_{i}\neq 0
.

Theorem 3.16

(
[
37
, A2.4 Theorem(IV)]
)
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer.

1.

The irreducible polynomial representations of
\GL(n,\mathbb{C})
are in one-to-one correspondence with the partitions of length at most
n
.

2.

If the irreducible polynomial representation
\rho
corresponds to
\lambda
under this correspondence, then

\Char(\rho)=s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}).

Now, we move to representations of the symmetric group.
We first need yet another family of symmetric functions, the power symmetric functions.

Definition 3.17
.

Let
n\geq 1
and let
\lambda\in\Par
be a partition.
We define the
power symmetric functions
as

p_{n}:=\sum_{i\geq 1}x_{i}^{n}

and

p_{\lambda}:=\prod_{i\geq 1}p_{\lambda_{i}}.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer,
\rho:\mathrm{Sym}(n)\to\GL(V)
a representation of
\mathrm{Sym}(n)
and
\chi_{\rho}
its (ordinary) character.
The
Frobenius character of
\rho
is the symmetric function

\ch(\rho):=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(n)}\chi_{\rho}(\sigma)p_{c(\sigma)}

where
c(\sigma)=(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k},\ldots)
is the cycle type of
\sigma
, i.e. the cycle decomposition of
\sigma
consists of cycles of length
c_{1}\geq c_{2}\geq\ldots\geq c_{k}\geq\ldots
.

Remark
.

For clarity, we list all the uses of the term
character
and their corresponding notations:

•

The
character
of a polynomial representation
\rho
of
\GL(n,\mathbb{C})
is
\Char(\rho)
.

•

The
ordinary character
of a representation
\rho
of
\mathrm{Sym}(n)
is
\chi_{\rho}
.

•

The
Frobenius character
of a representation
\rho
of
\mathrm{Sym}(n)
is
\ch(\rho)
.

The irreducible representations of
\mathrm{Sym}(n)
are indexed by the partitions of
n
, see
[
15
, §4.2]
.
This indexation behaves nicely under the Frobenius character.

Theorem 3.18

(
[
30
, (7.6)(i), p. 114]
)
.

Let
n\geq 1
.
For each partition
\lambda\vdash n
, the Frobenius character of the irreducible representation
\rho_{\lambda}
of
\mathrm{Sym}(n)
equals
\ch(\rho_{\lambda})=s_{\lambda}
.

The Frobenius character of a sum of representations is the sum of their respective Frobenius characters.
Hence, the Frobenius character of
any
representation of
\mathrm{Sym}(n)
is a non-negative integral linear combination of Schur functions.
In other words, it is a Schur positive symmetric function.

To prove that
F_{n}
is Schur positive for each
n\geq 1
, we construct a representation of
\mathrm{Sym}(n)
whose Frobenius character equals
F_{n}
.

Let
N\geq 1
and let
\mathfrak{f}_{N}
be the free Lie algebra on
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
.
We construct a set
\mathcal{B}(N):=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{B}_{i}(N)
similar to a Hall basis of
\mathfrak{f}_{N}
:

•

Put
\mathcal{B}_{1}(N):=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}\}
.

•

For
i\geq 2
, suppose
\mathcal{B}_{1}(N)
up to
\mathcal{B}_{i-1}(N)
have been constructed.
Define
\mathcal{B}_{i}(N)
to be the set of all Lie brackets
[Y,Z]
with
Y\in\mathcal{B}_{k}(N)
and
Z\in\mathcal{B}_{l}(N)
such that
k+l=i
.

In other words,
\mathcal{B}_{i}(N)
contains all possible Lie brackets of
i
elements from
\mathcal{B}_{1}(N)
, where the brackets are taken in any order.
For instance,
\mathcal{B}_{4}(N)
contains both
[[[X_{1},X_{2}],X_{3}],X_{4}]
and
[[X_{1},X_{2}],[X_{1},X_{3}]]
(if
N\geq 4
).

Note that
\mathcal{B}_{i}(N)
is not a linearly independent set for
i\geq 2
, as
\mathcal{B}_{2}(N)
contains both
[X_{1},X_{2}]
and
[X_{2},X_{1}]
, for example.
It is therefore distinct from the subset
\mathcal{H}_{i}
of a Hall basis
\mathcal{H}
of
\mathfrak{f}_{N}
(for
i\geq 2
).
The inclusion
\mathcal{H}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{i}(N)
, however, does hold for all
i\geq 1
.

Now, for an integer
n\geq 1
, we define
\mathfrak{p}_{n}
to be the
vector subspace
of
\mathfrak{f}_{n}
spanned by all elements in
\mathcal{B}_{n}(n)
that contain each
X_{i}
exactly once.
For example,
\mathfrak{p}_{3}
is spanned by the vectors

\{[[X_{i},X_{j}],X_{k}]\mid\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}\}\cup\{[X_{i},[X_{j},X_{k}]]\mid\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}\}.

The group
\mathrm{Sym}(n)
acts on
\mathfrak{f}_{n}
in a natural way by permuting the
X_{i}
, that is, for
\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(n)
, we put
\sigma\cdot X_{i}:=X_{\sigma(i)}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
As this action maps
\mathcal{B}_{n}(n)
onto itself, we get a representation
\rho^{(n)}:\mathrm{Sym}(n)\to\GL(\mathfrak{p}_{n})
.

Theorem 3.19
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer.
The symmetric function
F_{n}
is the Frobenius character of the representation
\rho^{(n)}:\mathrm{Sym}(n)\to\GL(\mathfrak{p}_{n})
.

Proof.

A. Garsia proves
[
16
, Theorem 5.2 & Theorem 5.3]
that the Frobenius character of
\rho^{(n)}
is given by

\ch(\rho^{(n)})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)p_{d}^{n/d},

(3.2)

where
\mu
is the Möbius function, as defined in
Theorem

2.5
.
Strictly speaking, in the proof of
[
16
, Theorem 5.2]
, he works with symmetric
polynomials
rather than symmetric functions in the definition of the Frobenius character and the power symmetric polynomials.
However, his result and proof are still valid when we work with symmetric functions instead.

Garsia additionally proves that a particular representation of
\GL(N,\mathbb{C})
for certain values of
N
essentially has the same character as above.
We can express this character in a different way using the results of
Section

2
, which we will then use to prove the theorem.

We start by constructing this representation.
Let
N\geq n
be an integer.
Recall the set
\mathcal{B}(N)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{B}_{i}(N)
of Lie brackets in the free Lie algebra
\mathfrak{f}_{N}
.
We define, for every
i\geq 1
,
\mathfrak{b}_{N,i}:=\Span(\mathcal{B}_{i}(N))
.
Note that, if
\mathcal{H}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{i}
is a Hall basis of
\mathfrak{f}_{N}
, then
\mathcal{H}_{i}
is a basis of
\mathfrak{b}_{N,i}
for
i\geq 1
.
Indeed, since
\mathcal{H}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{i}(N)
for all
i\geq 1
,
\Span(\mathcal{H}_{i})\subseteq\mathfrak{b}_{N,i}
.
Furthermore,
\mathfrak{f}_{N}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathfrak{b}_{N,i}
, so as
\mathcal{H}
is a basis of
\mathfrak{f}_{N}
, each
\mathcal{H}_{i}
must span the whole of
\mathfrak{b}_{N,i}
.
Being part of a basis,
\mathcal{H}_{i}
is also linearly independent.

Let
A\in\GL(N,\mathbb{C})
be arbitrary.
Since
\mathfrak{f}_{N}
is the free Lie algebra on
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
, the matrix
A
induces a Lie algebra automorphism
\varphi_{A}
of
\mathfrak{f}_{N}
by defining
\varphi_{A}(X_{i}):=\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{ij}X_{j}
.
By linearity of the Lie bracket, the image of an element in
\mathcal{B}_{n}(N)
is a linear combination of elements of
\mathcal{B}_{n}(N)
.
Hence,
\varphi_{A}
restricts to an automorphism
{\varphi_{A}}|_{\mathfrak{b}_{N,n}}
of
\mathfrak{b}_{N,n}
.
Thus, we get a map

\rho_{N,n}:\GL(N,\mathbb{C})\to\GL(\mathfrak{b}_{N,n}):A\mapsto{\varphi_{A}}|_{\mathfrak{b}_{N,n}},

which in addition is a group homomorphism.
In other words,
\rho_{N,n}
is a representation of
\GL(N,\mathbb{C})
.
Note that by the linearity of the Lie bracket,
\rho_{N,n}
is also a polynomial representation.

Now, Garsia proves
[
16
, Theorem 6.4]
that, for all
N\geq n
, the character of
\rho_{N,n}
is equal to

\Char(\rho_{N,n})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)p_{d}^{n/d}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=\Phi_{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)p_{d}^{n/d}}}\right)

On the other hand, the representation
\rho_{N,n}
is closely related to
Lemma

2.7
.
There, we started from a Lie algebra automorphism
\varphi_{*}
of
\mathfrak{n}_{r,c}
and looked at the induced maps on
\gamma_{i}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})/\gamma_{i+1}(\mathfrak{n}_{r,c})
.
Now, let
c\geq n
.
Since
\gamma_{c+1}(\mathfrak{f}_{N})
is characteristic,
\varphi_{A}
as constructed above induces an automorphism
\varphi_{A,*}
of
\mathfrak{n}_{N,c}
.
We show that the eigenvalues of
\varphi_{A,*,n}\in\GL(\gamma_{n}(\mathfrak{n}_{N,c})/\gamma_{n+1}(\mathfrak{n}_{N,c}))
and
{\varphi_{A}}|_{\mathfrak{b}_{N,n}}
are equal.

To that end, let
\mathcal{H}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{i}
be a Hall basis of
\mathfrak{f}_{N}
.
As argued earlier, the subset
\mathcal{H}_{n}
is a basis of
\mathfrak{b}_{N,n}
, and by
Lemma

2.6
, the natural projections of the elements in
\mathcal{H}_{n}
also form a basis of
\gamma_{n}(\mathfrak{n}_{N,c})/\gamma_{n+1}(\mathfrak{n}_{N,c})
.
Hence, as vector spaces,
\mathfrak{b}_{N,n}
and
\gamma_{n}(\mathfrak{n}_{N,c})/\gamma_{n+1}(\mathfrak{n}_{N,c})
are isomorphic.
The map that sends every element of
\mathcal{H}_{n}
to its natural projection gives rise to an isomorphism
\psi:\mathfrak{b}_{N,n}\to\gamma_{n}(\mathfrak{n}_{N,c})/\gamma_{n+1}(\mathfrak{n}_{N,c})
.
This isomorphism satisfies
{\psi}^{-1}\circ\varphi_{A,*,n}\circ\psi={\varphi_{A}}|_{\mathfrak{b}_{N,n}}
.
Hence,
\varphi_{A,*,n}\in\GL(\gamma_{n}(\mathfrak{n}_{N,c})/\gamma_{n+1}(\mathfrak{n}_{N,c}))
and
{\varphi_{A}}|_{\mathfrak{b}_{N,n}}
have the same eigenvalues.

Subsequently, it follows from
Lemma

2.7
that the character of
\rho_{N,n}
is also equal to

\Char(\rho_{N,n})=\sum_{X\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\eta(X)

where
\mathcal{H}
is a Hall basis of
\mathfrak{f}_{N}
and
\eta:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}]
is the map that associates to an element of
\mathcal{H}
its corresponding monomial.
In other words, the character of
\rho_{N,n}
is equal to
\Phi_{N}(F_{n})
.
Thus, combined with (
3.2
), we have the equality

\Phi_{N}(F_{n})=\Phi_{N}(\ch(\rho^{(n)}))

for all
N\geq n
, as
N
was arbitrary.
By
Lemma

3.4
, it follows that
F_{n}=\ch(\rho^{(n)})
.
∎

Corollary 3.20
.

For each
n\geq 1
and
i\geq 1
, both
F_{n}
and
e_{i}[F_{n}]
are Schur positive.

Proof.

By the previous theorem,
F_{n}
is the Frobenius character of a representation of
\mathrm{Sym}(n)
.
Consequently, it is Schur positive.

Theorem

3.13
then implies that
e_{i}[F_{n}]
is Schur positive as well for all
i\geq 1
.
∎

At this point, our journey is almost at an end.
We have established a link between the Reidemeister spectra of free nilpotent groups and the plethysm of Schur positive functions.
To show that

\ch(\rho^{(n)})
.
As we have established that
F_{n}
is equal to
\ch(\rho^{(n)})
, we obtain a combinatorial interpretation of the coefficients in the Schur decomposition of

.

Theorem 3.21

(
[
26
]
)
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer and
\lambda
a partition of
n
.
The coefficient of
s_{\lambda}
in
F_{n}
is equal to the number of standard Young tableaux of shape
\lambda
with major index congruent to
1
modulo
n
.

For example,
F_{1}=s_{1}
,
F_{2}=s_{1,1}
,

and

.
Thus, in turn,

f_{1}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}s_{1^{i}}[s_{1}]=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}e_{i}[e_{1}]=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}e_{i}

4
From plethysms of Schur functions back to Reidemeister spectra

Our journey is coming to an end.
We have established a connection between two open problems: fully determining the Reidemeister spectra of free nilpotent groups and determining the coefficients in the Schur expansion of the plethysms of Schur functions.
Since the latter is a major open problem in combinatorics, this connection in some sense indicates why the computations of Dekimpe, Tertooy and Vargas are tedious and, at first sight, hard to generalise.

We want to elaborate further on two aspects.
Firstly, we want to delve deeper into the ‘practical’ aspect of this connection: how does progress in the realm of symmetric functions translate into progress in the realm of Reidemeister spectra? Secondly, it might feel a bit unsatisfying to speak of a connection between both open problems if most of the
F_{n}
are not Schur functions.
Therefore, we want to discuss the problem of determining the Reidemeister spectra of free nilpotent
metabelian
groups and show that this is related to the plethysm of proper Schur functions.

4.1
Main objectives

At the end of
Section

3.2
, we explained that we can derive the polynomials
g_{r,k}
from
f_{k}
: writing

f_{k}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Par}c_{\lambda,k}e_{\lambda}

for some
c_{\lambda,k}\in\mathbb{Z}
as in
Theorem

3.7
, we can derive
g_{r,k}
from this equality by applying
\Phi_{r}
to both sides.
Here, we elaborate on the details.

Fix integers
r,c,i
and
k
such that
r,c\geq 2
,
i\geq 1
and
1\leq k\leq c
.
We know from
Theorem

3.11
that

f_{r,k}=\Phi_{r}(f_{k})=\Phi_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}e_{i}[F_{k}]}}\right).

Note that
e_{i}
is homogeneous of degree
i
and
F_{k}
of degree
k
.
Hence,
e_{i}[F_{k}]
is homogeneous of degree
ik
.
This implies that there are no cancellations possible among the different terms of the summation.
On the other hand, since
f_{r,k}
is a polynomial, only finitely many terms can survive under
\Phi_{r}
.
To determine which, we look more closely at how
e_{i}[F_{k}]
is computed.
Write
F_{k}=\sum_{j\geq 1}x^{\alpha_{j}}
for some
\alpha_{j}\in A
.

For
e_{i}[F_{k}]
to (partially) survive under
\Phi_{r}
, at least one monomial must contain only the variables
x_{1}
up to
x_{r}
.
Suppose that
i>N(r,k)
, the size of
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, where
\mathcal{H}
is a Hall basis of
\mathfrak{f}_{r}
.
Let
x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{i}}
be a monomial occurring in
e_{i}
for some positive integers
j_{1}<\ldots<j_{i}
.
To compute the plethysm
e_{i}[F_{k}]
, we have to substitute
x_{j_{l}}=x^{\alpha_{j_{l}}}
for each
l\in\{1,\ldots,i\}
.
All
j_{l}
are distinct and
i>N(r,k)
.
As there are, by
Theorem

2.5
, only
N(r,k)
many monomials occurring in
F_{k}
that only contain the variables
x_{1}
up to
x_{r}
, at least one of the substitutions
x_{j_{l}}=x^{\alpha_{j_{l}}}
involves a monomial containing
x_{m}
with
m>r
.
The resulting monomial in
e_{i}[F_{k}]
then vanishes under
\Phi_{r}
.
As this holds for all monomials occurring in
e_{i}
,
e_{i}[F_{k}]
vanishes completely under
\Phi_{r}
.

Consequently,

f_{r,k}=\Phi_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N(r,k)}(-1)^{i}e_{i}[F_{k}]}}\right)

(4.1)

In other words, if we want to compute
\SpecR(N_{r,c})
for a fixed value of
c
and for
r
up to a certain bound, say
R
, we only have to effectively compute
e_{i}[F_{k}]
for
i\leq N(R,k)
and
k\leq c
.

Next, suppose we have an expansion of
e_{i}[F_{k}]
in terms of the elementary symmetric functions, say,
e_{i}[F_{k}]=\sum_{\lambda\in\Par(ik)}c_{\lambda,i,k}e_{\lambda}
, where
c_{\lambda,i,k}\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
i\geq 1,k\geq 1
and
\lambda\in\Par(ik)
.
We know that only partitions of
ik
occur, as
e_{i}[F_{k}]
is homogeneous of degree
ik
, and so is
e_{\lambda}
for
\lambda\in\Par(ik)
.
From (
4.1
), we obtain

f_{r,k}=\Phi_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N(r,k)}(-1)^{i}e_{i}[F_{k}]}}\right)=\Phi_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N(r,k)}(-1)^{i}\sum_{\lambda\in\Par(ik)}c_{\lambda,i,k}e_{\lambda}}}\right)

Since
\Phi_{r}(e_{s})=0
for all
s>r
, the only
e_{\lambda}
that survive under
\Phi_{r}
are those for which
\lambda_{i}\leq r
for all
i
, or, equivalently, those for which
\lambda_{1}\leq r
, as a partition is a non-increasing sequence.
Thus,

f_{r,k}=\sum_{i=1}^{N(r,k)}(-1)^{i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\Par(ik)\\
\lambda_{1}\leq r\end{subarray}}c_{\lambda,i,k}e_{\lambda}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}).

From this expression, we obtain the polynomial
g_{r,k}
from
Theorem

2.11
.

To compute
\SpecR(N_{r,c})
, we then have to compute all possible values of

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|{\prod_{k=1}^{c}\sum_{i=1}^{N(r,k)}(-1)^{i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\Par(ik)\\
\lambda_{1}\leq r\end{subarray}}c_{\lambda,i,k}y_{\lambda}}}}\right|_{\infty}

by letting
y_{1},\ldots,y_{r}
run over all integers, with the restriction that
y_{r}\in\{-1,1\}
and where

y_{\lambda}:=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}y_{\lambda_{i}}.

Also note that

\displaystyle f_{r,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N(r,k)}(-1)^{i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\Par(ik)\\
\lambda_{1}\leq r\end{subarray}}c_{\lambda,i,k}e_{\lambda}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N(r,k)}(-1)^{i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\Par(ik)\\
\lambda_{1}=r\end{subarray}}c_{\lambda,i,k}e_{\lambda}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})+\sum_{i=1}^{N(r,k)}(-1)^{i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\Par(ik)\\
\lambda_{1}\leq r-1\end{subarray}}c_{\lambda,i,k}e_{\lambda}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N(r,k)}(-1)^{i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\Par(ik)\\
\lambda_{1}=r\end{subarray}}c_{\lambda,i,k}e_{\lambda}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})+f_{r-1,k}.

Each summand in the first term has a factor
e_{r}
, as
\lambda_{1}=r
for all partitions in the summation.
Therefore, setting
e_{r}=0
in the expression for
f_{r,k}
yields the expression for
f_{r-1,k}
.
Put differently, the polynomial
g_{r-1,k}
can be derived from the polynomial
g_{r,k}
by putting
e_{r}=0
.
This rigorously explains the behaviour we observed in the table in
Section

3
.

Summarised, from a group theoretical point of view with focus on Reidemeister spectra, we obtain two objectives:

Objective 4.1
.

For all
i,k\geq 1
, determine the expansion of
e_{i}[F_{k}]
in terms of the elementary symmetric functions.

Objective 4.2
.

Suppose we know the expansion of
e_{i}[F_{k}]
in terms of elementary symmetric functions for all
i,k\geq 1
, say,
e_{i}[F_{k}]=\sum_{\lambda\in\Par(ik)}c_{\lambda,i,k}e_{\lambda}
for some
c_{\lambda,i,k}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Given integers
r,c\geq 2
and a positive integer
n\geq 1
, determine whether the equation

\pm n=\prod_{k=1}^{c}\sum_{i=1}^{N(r,k)}(-1)^{i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\Par(ik)\\
\lambda_{1}\leq r\end{subarray}}c_{\lambda,i,k}y_{\lambda}

has a solution with
y_{1},\ldots,y_{r-1}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
y_{r}\in\{-1,1\}
.

As mentioned in the introduction, the Reidemeister spectrum of
N_{r,2}
has already been determined for every
r\geq 2
, see
[
13
,
32
]
.
However, the proof by Dekimpe, Tertooy and Vargas is rather ad hoc, so an expression for the expansion in terms of elementary symmetric functions might give an alternative proof that is generalisable.

We can also try to answer more specific questions about
\SpecR(N_{r,c})
, besides (or as alternative to) completely determining it.
For example, for which values of
r
and
c
is
1
contained in
\SpecR(N_{r,c})
?
To answer such questions, it might be of interest to look for factorisations of
f_{k}
using elementary symmetric functions.
For example, recall that

f_{3}=\prod_{i\neq j}(1-x_{i}^{2}x_{j})\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\prod_{i<j<k}(1-x_{i}x_{j}x_{k})}}\right)^{2}.

Both factors are symmetric functions, so both admit an expansion in terms of elementary symmetric functions.
Write
f_{3}=f_{3,i\neq j}f_{3,i<j<k}^{2}
, where

f_{3,i\neq j}=\prod_{i\neq j}(1-x_{i}^{2}x_{j})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}e_{n}[m_{2,1}]

and

f_{3,i<j<k}=\prod_{i<j<k}(1-x_{i}x_{j}x_{k})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}e_{n}[e_{3}]

by
Proposition

3.10
.
For
1
to be in
\SpecR(N_{r,c})
, both
\Phi_{r}(f_{3,i\neq j})
and
\Phi_{r}(f_{3,i<j<k})
have to evaluate to
\pm 1
for certain values of
e_{i}
.

From a combinatorial point of view with focus on the open problem concerning plethysms of Schur functions, the main objective is the following:

Objective 4.3
.

For all
i,k\geq 1
, determine the expansion of
e_{i}[F_{k}]
in terms of the Schur functions and find a combinatorial interpretation of the coefficients.

The plethysms for
i=1
are easy, since
e_{1}[F_{k}]=F_{k}
for all
k\geq 1
.
Similarly, since
F_{1}=e_{1}
, we have
e_{i}[F_{1}]=e_{i}
for all
i\geq 1
.
Thus, the difficulty arises when
i,k\geq 2
.
To determine
\SpecR(N_{r,c})
, we have to compute
e_{i}[F_{k}]
for
i\geq 1
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,c\}
.
For
c=2
, this has already been done: D. E. Littlewood has determined the Schur expansion of
s_{1^{i}}[s_{1,1}]=e_{i}[F_{2}]

[
28
, Equation 11.9; 1]
; see also
[
6
, Theorem 5.7]
,
[
25
, Theorem 5.2]
.
However, the expansion of
s_{1^{i}}[s_{1,1}]
in elementary symmetric functions is not known.

The combinatorial and group theoretical objective are closely related.
The dual Jacobi-Trudi identity
[
37
, Corollary 7.16.2]
expresses the Schur functions as a determinant of a matrix with elementary symmetric functions as entries.
Therefore, we can derive the expansion of
e_{i}[F_{k}]
in terms of elementary symmetric functions from its Schur expansion.
However, this is quite cumbersome and perhaps there are alternatives that yield more insight and/or are more efficient.

4.2
Reidemeister spectrum of free nilpotent metabelian groups

The objective of determining
\SpecR(N_{r,c})
for all
r,c\geq 2
is linked to plethysms of Schur functions only when
k\leq 3
.
However, there is a second family of groups that is closely related to the free nilpotent groups and that yields a stronger connection with plethysms of Schur functions: the free
c
-step nilpotent and metabelian groups of finite rank.

For a group
G
, we define
G^{(0)}:=G
and
G^{(i)}:=[G^{(i-1)},G^{(i-1)}]
for
i\geq 1
.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
r,c\geq 2
be integers.
The
free
c
-step nilpotent and metabelian group of rank
r
is the group

M_{r,c}:=\frac{F(r)}{\gamma_{c+1}(F(r))F(r)^{(2)}}.

K. Dekimpe and D. Gonçalves have proven that
M_{r,c}
has the
R_{\infty}
-property if and only if
c\geq 2r
, which is the same condition as for
N_{r,c}

[
8
, Theorem 2.8]
.
We can do an analogous reasoning as we did for
N_{r,c}
to relate Reidemeister numbers on
M_{r,c}
to symmetric functions and plethysms of Schur functions.
We provide the results for the metabelian case, accompanied with references to the analogous results for
N_{r,c}
.
However, we only provide the proofs that differ substantially from those for
N_{r,c}
.

For a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
and
i\geq 0
, we define
\mathfrak{g}^{(i)}
in a similar fashion as we did for groups.
For integers
r,c\geq 2
, we define the
free
c
-step nilpotent and metabelian Lie algebra of rank
r
as the Lie algebra

\mathfrak{m}_{r,c}:=\frac{\mathfrak{f}_{r}}{\gamma_{c+1}(\mathfrak{f}_{r})+\mathfrak{f}_{r}^{(2)}}.

Finally, for elements
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
of a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, we define

[v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}]:=[[\ldots[[v_{1},v_{2}],v_{3}],\ldots],v_{n}].

Theorem 4.5

(
[
5
, Theorem 2]
, cf.
Definition

2.4
)
.

Let
r,c\geq 2
be integers and let
\mathfrak{m}_{r,c}
be the free
c
-step nilpotent and metabelian Lie algebra on
X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}
.
Let
\mathcal{C}:=\bigcup_{k=1}^{c}\mathcal{C}_{k}
be given by

\mathcal{C}_{1}:=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}\}

and

\mathcal{C}_{k}:=\{[X_{i_{1}},X_{i_{2}},\ldots,X_{i_{k}}]\mid i_{1}>i_{2}\leq i_{3}\leq\ldots\leq i_{k},\forall j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}:i_{j}\in\{1,\ldots,r\}\}

for
k\geq 2
.
Then
\mathcal{C}
is a vector space basis of
\mathfrak{m}_{r,c}
.

We refer to such a basis as a
Chen basis
.

Proposition 4.6

(
[
5
, Corollary 1]
, cf.
Theorem

2.5
)
.

Let
r,c\geq 2
be integers and let
\mathcal{C}
be a Chen basis of
\mathfrak{m}_{r,c}
.
For each
k\in\{2,\ldots,c\}
, the cardinality of
\mathcal{C}_{k}
is equal to
M(r,k):=(k-1)\binom{k+r-2}{k}
.

A Chen basis can be used to prove the following:

Lemma 4.7

(Cf.
Lemma

2.7
)
.

Let
\varphi\in\End(M_{r,c})
and let, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,c\}
,
\varphi_{i}
denote the induced endomorphism on
\gamma_{i}(M_{r,c})/\gamma_{i+1}(M_{r,c})
.
Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})
be the eigenvalues of
\varphi_{1}
, where each eigenvalue is listed as many times as its multiplicity.
Then, for each
k\in\{1,\ldots,c\}
, the eigenvalues of
\varphi_{k}
are given by

\{\lambda_{i_{1}}\lambda_{i_{2}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{k}}\mid i_{1}>i_{2}\leq i_{3}\leq\ldots\leq i_{k},\forall j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}:i_{j}\in\{1,\ldots,r\}\}.

(4.2)

In this way, each eigenvalue is also listed as many times as its multiplicity.

Theorem 4.8

(Cf.
Theorem

2.8
)
.

There exist symmetric polynomials
f_{r,k}^{(2)}\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]^{\mathrm{Sym}(n)}
(with
1\leq k\leq c
) such that, for any
\varphi\in\End(M_{r,c})
, we have

R(\varphi)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|{\prod_{k=1}^{c}f_{r,k}^{(2)}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})}}}\right|_{\infty},

where
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}
are the eigenvalues of
\varphi_{1}
.

Theorem 4.9

(Cf.
Theorem

2.11
)
.

There exist polynomials
\tilde{g}_{r,k}^{(2)}\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
for
1\leq k\leq c
such that, for any
\varphi\in\End(M_{r,c})
, if
p_{\varphi}=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{r}a_{i}x^{i}
is the characteristic polynomial of
\varphi_{1}\in\End(M_{r,c}/\gamma_{2}(M_{r,c}))
with

, then

R(\varphi)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|{\prod_{k=1}^{c}\tilde{g}_{r,k}^{(2)}(a_{0},\ldots,a_{r-1})}}}\right|_{\infty}.

Analogously to
\SpecR(N_{r,c})
, we can fully determine
\SpecR(M_{r,c})
by evaluating the expression above in
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,r-1\}
and
a_{0}\in\{\pm 1\}
.

Next, we construct
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
in a similar fashion as we constructed
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
.
Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
be an infinite family.
Let
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}:=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\infty}
where

\mathcal{C}_{1}^{\infty}:=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{r},\ldots\}

and

\mathcal{C}_{k}:=\{[X_{i_{1}},X_{i_{2}},\ldots,X_{i_{k}}]\mid i_{1}>i_{2}\leq i_{3}\leq\ldots\leq i_{k},\forall j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}:i_{j}\geq 1\}

for
k\geq 2
.
We then also define
\eta:\mathcal{C}^{\infty}\to\mathcal{M}
similarly as before, where
\mathcal{M}
is the set of all monomials on the variables
\{x_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
: We set
\eta(X_{i}):=x_{i}
for
i\geq 1
and

\eta([X_{i_{1}},X_{i_{2}},\ldots,X_{i_{k}}]):=x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}

for all
k\geq 2
and
[X_{i_{1}},X_{i_{2}},\ldots,X_{i_{k}}]\in\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\infty}
.

Theorem 4.10

(Cf.
Theorem

3.11
)
.

Let
k\geq 1
be arbitrary.
Define

F_{k}^{(2)}:=\sum_{X\in\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\infty}}\eta(X).

and

f_{k}^{(2)}:=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}e_{i}[F^{(2)}_{k}]

Then, for all integers
r\geq 2

\Phi_{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f_{k}^{(2)}}}\right)=f_{r,k}^{(2)}.

Theorem 4.11
.

For each
n\geq 2
,
F_{n}^{(2)}=s_{n-1,1}
.

Proof.

We need yet another family of symmetric functions, namely the
complete homogeneous symmetric functions
.
Given
n\geq 1
, the complete homogeneous symmetric function
h_{n}
is defined as

h_{n}:=\sum_{i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq i_{3}\leq\ldots\leq i_{n}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{n}}.

By definition of
\mathcal{C}_{n}
,

\displaystyle F_{n}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}>i_{2}\leq i_{3}\leq\ldots\leq i_{n}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq i_{3}\leq\ldots\leq i_{n}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{n}}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}\geq 1\\
i_{2}\leq i_{3}\leq\ldots\leq i_{n}\end{subarray}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{n}}

\displaystyle=h_{n}-h_{1}h_{n-1}

\displaystyle=s_{n-1,1}

by the Jacobi-Trudi identity
[
37
, 7.16.1 Theorem]
.
∎

Note that
F_{1}^{(2)}=s_{1}
.
For ease of notation, we put
s_{0,1}:=s_{1}
.

Thus, to compute Reidemeister numbers on free
c
-step nilpotent and metabelian groups we have to determine the Schur expansion and/or the expansion in elementary symmetric functions of

f_{k}^{(2)}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}s_{1^{i}}[s_{k-1,1}].

The corresponding objectives therefore are

Objective 4.12

(Cf.
Objective

4.1
)
.

For all
i,k\geq 1
, determine the expansion of
s_{1^{i}}[s_{k-1,1}]
in terms of elementary symmetric functions.

Objective 4.13

(Cf.
Objective

4.2
)
.

Suppose we know the expansion of
s_{1^{i}}[s_{k-1,1}]
in terms of elementary symmetric functions for all
i,k\geq 1
, say,
s_{1^{i}}[s_{k-1,1}]=\sum_{\lambda\in\Par(ik)}c_{\lambda,i,k}^{(2)}e_{\lambda}
for some
c_{\lambda,i,k}^{(2)}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Given integers
r,c\geq 2
and a positive integer
n\geq 1
, determine whether the equation

\pm n=\prod_{k=1}^{c}\sum_{i=1}^{M(r,k)}(-1)^{i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\Par(ik)\\
\lambda_{1}\leq r\end{subarray}}c_{\lambda,i,k}^{(2)}y_{\lambda}

has a solution with
y_{1},\ldots,y_{r-1}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
y_{r}\in\{-1,1\}
.

Objective 4.14

(Cf.
Objective

4.3
)
.

For all
i,k\geq 1
, determine the expansion of
s_{1^{i}}[s_{k-1,1}]
in terms of Schur functions and find a combinatorial interpretation of the coefficients.

So, we see that determining the Reidemeister spectrum of free nilpotent and metabelian groups is truly related to determining the Schur expansion of the plethysm of Schur functions.

Since nilpotent groups of nilpotency class at most
3
are metabelian,
N_{r,c}
and
M_{r,c}
are equal for
c\leq 3
.
Therefore, the objectives for
N_{r,c}
and
M_{r,c}
coincide in that case.
For higher values of
c
, information about
\SpecR(M_{r,c})
can also yield information about
\SpecR(N_{r,c})
.
For instance, we have the exact sequence

1\to N_{r,c}^{(2)}\to N_{r,c}\to M_{r,c}\to 1.

For
\varphi\in\End(N_{r,c})
, let
\bar{\varphi}
denote the induced endomorphism on
M_{r,c}
.
A classical result for Reidemeister numbers states that
R(\varphi)\geq R(\bar{\varphi})
(see
[
18
, Theorem 1.8]
).
Hence, if
1\in\SpecR(N_{r,c})
, then also
1\in\SpecR(M_{r,c})
.
Put differently, if
\SpecR(M_{r,c})
does not contain
1
, then neither does
\SpecR(N_{r,c})
; in particular,
\SpecR(N_{r,c})
cannot be full.

For some of the plethysms
s_{1^{i}}[s_{k-1,1}]
, the Schur expansion is known.
As before,
s_{1}[s_{k-1,1}]=s_{k-1,1}
for all
k\geq 1
.
J. Carbonara, J. Remmel and M. Yang explicitly compute all plethysms of the form
s_{1,1}[s_{a,1^{b}}]
, where
a,b\geq 1

[
3
, Theorem 3]
.
Concretely, put
\lambda=(1,1)
,
\mu=(k-1,1)
with
k\geq 2
and
s_{1,1}[s_{\mu}]=\sum_{\nu}a_{\lambda,\mu}^{\nu}s_{\nu}
.
Their results then state the following:

•

If
\nu=(2k-m,1^{m})
for some
m\in\{1,\ldots,2k-1\}
, then

a_{\lambda,\mu}^{\nu}=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if }m=2,\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

•

If
\nu=(q,p,2^{l},1^{m})
for some integers
q\geq p\geq 2
and
l,m
with
q+p+2l+m=2k
, then

a_{\lambda,\mu}^{\nu}=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $q+p\in\{2k-2,2k\}$ and $\frac{m}{2}+p$ is even,}\\
1&\mbox{if }q+p=2k-1,\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

•

If
\nu
is of a different form, then
a_{\lambda,\mu}^{\nu}=0
.

On the other hand, C. Carré and B. Leclerc give a combinatorial interpretation of the coefficients in the Schur expansion of
s_{1,1}[s_{\lambda}]
, where
\lambda
is any partition
[
4
, Corollary 5.5]
.
Besides these explicit plethysms, there has been done some research into plethysms of so-called
hook shaped
partitions
[
27
]
, albeit with only partial Schur expansions.
