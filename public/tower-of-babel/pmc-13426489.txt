NRF2-mediated inhibition of alveolar macrophage MHC II expression during Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection
Authors: Linh K. Pham, Maritza M. Cervantes, Pamelia N. Lim, Divya Dubey, Ankita Tufts, M. Clare Mitchell, Tomoyo Shinkawa, Samuel M. Behar, Alissa C. Rothchild
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYDuring Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) infection, infected alveolar macrophages (AMs) initially up-regulate a nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2)-regulated cell-protective program, which is detrimental to host control and impedes AM activation, including MHC II expression. MHC II is critical for CD4+ T cell activation and host immunity during Mtb infection. We hypothesized that NRF2 regulates the MHC II pathway and AM antigen presentation to T cells. We found that NRF2 inhibits MHC II, but not MHC I, specifically in AMs, following Mtb infection in vitro and in vivo. NRF2 dampens Ciita and H2-Ab1 gene expression in uninfected AMs, and MHC II inhibition by NRF2 is retained following innate stimuli and IFNγ exposure. NRF2 expression in Mtb-infected AMs impedes their ability to activate ESAT6-specific CD4+ T cells. Thus, although NRF2 expression enhances cell-protective functions, it has the unexpected consequence of limiting innate-adaptive crosstalk, which can impair CD4+ T cell activation and host immunity during Mtb infection.

INTRODUCTION

Tuberculosis (TB), a respiratory disease caused by the bacteria Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), remains a formidable global health issue. In 2023, Mtb was responsible for 1.25 million deaths and 10.8 million new cases.1 Antibiotic therapy can be highly effective against TB, but existing treatments are lengthy, costly, and can be toxic. Ultimately, existing drug treatments fall short of controlling the TB epidemic. Additionally, incomplete drug treatment can lead to multidrug-resistant Mtb. Host-directed therapies, including vaccines, are needed to improve host responses and protect against disease.

Alveolar macrophages (AMs) are the first cells in the lung to be infected with Mtb after aerosol transmission.2,3 AMs are long-lived lung-resident myeloid cells, with a slow turnover rate.4,5 AMs serve critical homeostatic functions in the lung, clearing debris from the airways and recycling surfactant.6,7 Mtb-infected AMs initiate a cell-protective and antioxidant transcriptional program, regulated by the transcription factor nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2).3 Up-regulation of the NRF2 transcriptional program within Mtb-infected cells requires the pulmonary microenvironment.3 Despite serving as the innate sentinels in the airway, AMs exhibit a diminished capacity to control bacterial growth compared to recruited monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs).8 However, AMs that lack NRF2 expression exhibit better control of Mtb over the first 10 days of infection than wild-type (WT) AMs.3 Interestingly, over the same time frame, AMs deficient in NRF2 express higher levels of Major Histocompatibility Complex Class II (MHC II).3 MHC II is necessary for interaction with CD4+ T cells and induction of effector T cell responses.9,10 Tissue-resident memory T cells are critical for control of Mtb11 and are known to colocalize with and receive signals from macrophages in human lungs.12 We hypothesized that NRF2 regulates the ability of AMs to interact with CD4+ T cells through MHC II.

NRF2 has well-known roles in the defense against oxidative stress and inflammation.13 NRF2 regulates numerous genes involved in antioxidant and detoxification processes and is a target of cancer therapy14–17 and respiratory inflammation, such as Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD).18,19 NRF2 activity is tightly regulated through interaction with the Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1), which targets NRF2 for proteasomal degradation. Oxidative stress or electrophilic compounds can disrupt this intracellular interaction, allowing NRF2 to dissociate from KEAP1, translocate into the nucleus, and promote gene transcription.20 NRF2 can suppress proinflammatory cytokine transcription following lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation in bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs)21 and block ferroptosis, a form of cell death dependent on lipid peroxidation and iron.22–25 Despite these broad effects, NRF2 regulation of antigen presentation is unknown.

To investigate NRF2-mediated inhibition of MHC class II expression in AMs, we used an in vivo murine aerosol infection model and in vitro murine macrophage models, including primary AMs and murine ex vivo AMs (mexAMs)26 derived from WT, NRF2 knockout (KO) (NRF2−/−), or NRF2 myeloid-conditional KO strains, as well as BMDMs as a model of recruited macrophages. We show that, both in vivo and in vitro, NRF2 inhibits expression of MHC II on AMs but not on dendritic cells (DCs) or other macrophages. In addition to blocking MHC II surface expression, NRF2 inhibits Ciita and H2-Ab1 gene expression and total MHC II protein. NRF2 inhibition of MHC II is retained after stimulation with pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) or IFNγ. Lastly, we demonstrate that, in the absence of NRF2, AMs have an enhanced ability to activate Mtb-specific CD4+ T cells in a macrophage-T cell co-culture system. Overall, our results demonstrate that NRF2 impedes the gene and protein expression of MHC II specifically in AMs, which ultimately blocks their ability to activate CD4+ T cells in an antigen-specific manner.

RESULTS

NRF2 limits MHC II expression on AMs during Mtb infection

To determine the effect of NRF2 on MHC II expression of Mtb-infected AMs in vivo, we used two different myeloid NRF2 conditional KO (NRF2CondKO) strains, Nrf2fl/flLysMcre (NRF2 deleted in AMs, MDMs, and neutrophils [PMNs]) and Nrf2fl/flCD11ccre (NRF2 deleted in AMs and DCs). We infected the NRF2CondKO mice and their littermate control (Nrf2fl/fl) with fluorescent trackable Mtb strains (mCherry-H37Rv and msfYFP-H37Rv) and collected mouse lungs at 12 and 14 days post-infection (dpi). The median fluorescence intensity (MFI) of MHC II levels on bystander and Mtb-infected myeloid cell populations was measured by flow cytometry (Figure 1A, see gating scheme; Figure S1A). At 14 dpi, Mtb-infected AMs from Nrf2fl/flLysMcre and Nrf2fl/flCD11ccre mice expressed significantly higher surface levels of MHC II compared to their littermate controls (Figures 1B and 1C). Bystander AMs also showed higher expression of MHC II (Figures 1B and 1C). This result was consistent with our previous findings, which showed that Mtb-infected AMs from NRF2CondKO mice expressed higher levels of MHC II at 10 dpi than AMs from littermate controls.3

We next compared MHC II expression in other myeloid cells. We found that Mtb-infected and bystander DCs from Nrf2fl/flCD11ccre mice (Figure 1D) and PMNs and MDMs from Nrf2fl/flLysMcre mice (Figure 1E) showed no difference in MHC II levels compared to cells from littermate controls. Interestingly, these results suggest that the impact of NRF2 on MHC II surface expression is specific for AMs and does not affect other pulmonary myeloid cells during the first 14 days of infection.

To examine this phenotype in vitro, we infected mexAMs derived from WT and NRF2−/− mice with mCherry-H37Rv for 24 h and then measured MHC II (Figures 1F and S1B). Both Mtb-infected and bystander NRF2−/− mexAMs had significantly higher expression of MHC II compared to WT mexAMs (Figure 1G). For BMDMs, both bystander and Mtb-infected NRF2−/− BMDMs expressed similar levels of MHC II as WT BMDMs (Figure S1C). To test whether MHC II levels would be affected by exogenous activation of NRF2, we used an NRF2 agonist, L-sulforaphane (L-sulf). L-sulf binds to the cysteine residue of KEAP1, an NRF2 inhibitor, leading to KEAP1 dissociation from NRF2 and translocation of NRF2 into the nucleus.27 L-sulf stimulation reduced MHC II expression in WT but not NRF2−/− BMDMs in both Mtb-infected and bystander populations (Figure S1D). Thus, in the context of exogenous NRF2 activation, NRF2−/− BMDMs expressed significantly higher expression of MHC II than WT BMDMs (Figure S1D). Overall, our data demonstrate that NRF2 limits MHC II expression in AMs during in vivo and in vitro Mtb infection.

NRF2-mediated inhibition of MHC II in AMs wanes after 14 dpi

Tissue-resident AMs serve as the initial replicative niche for Mtb, but by 14 dpi, there is an influx of recruited myeloid cells into the lung. Recruited myeloid cells can mature into monocyte-derived AMs, which take on many AM markers and phenotypes.28,29 To determine how NRF2 regulation affects each population, we separated AMs based on Siglec F expression at 14 and 21 dpi, identifying Siglec Fhigh and Siglec Flow AMs, previously shown to be associated with tissue-resident and recruited populations, respectively, and measured MHC II expression in both Nrf2fl/flLysMcre and Nrf2fl/fl littermate controls (Figure S2A). At day 14, the Siglec Fhigh Mtb-infected AMs from Nrf2fl/flLysMcre had significantly higher expression of MHC II than littermate controls, while the Siglec Flow AMs showed no difference in expression (Figure S2B). At day 21, there were no significant differences in MHC II MFI in either of the AM populations (Figure S2C). This result supports the model that it is the tissue-resident permissive AMs, and not the recruited population, in which NRF2 expression impedes antigen presentation.

We next quantified the percentage of Mtb-infected Siglec Fhigh and Siglec Flow AMs at 14 and 21 dpi from Nrf2fl/fl and Nrf2fl/fl LysMcre mice (Figures S2D and S2E). While there was no difference in the percentage of Mtb-infected cells between NRF2-expressing and deficient AMs in each of the groups, there was a significantly higher percentage of Mtb-infected Siglec Flow AMs compared to Siglec Fhigh AMs. These results are aligned with prior studies that observed a higher Mtb burden in recruited compared to tissue-resident myeloid cells.30–32 Next, we quantified AM cell numbers based on Siglec F expression (Figure S2F). The number of Siglec Fhigh AMs remained relatively stable across the three time points, while the number of Siglec Flow AMs significantly increased over time in both Nrf2fl/fl and Nrf2fl/fl LysMcre mice. Together, these findings indicate that the waning effect of NRF2 on AM MHC II levels after 14 dpi is likely due to an influx of recruited Siglec Flow AMs and a relative decline in the frequency of tissue-resident Siglec Fhigh AMs that express high levels of NRF2.

We took advantage of a published single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset to examine the expression of the NRF2 gene (Nfe2l2) and the MHC II gene (H2-Ab1) at 2, 3, 4, and 6 weeks post-infection in AMs and interstitial macrophages (IMs), for cells with detectable expression of each gene.28 This study specifically identified a population of inflammatory and Mtb-restrictive CD38+ AMs that exist at low frequency at baseline and increase in frequency over the course of infection, likely in part through recruitment of monocyte-derived cells. Notably, we found that Nfe2l2 expression decreased over time in total AMs, with highest expression in the CD38− AM subsets and the lowest expression in CD38+ AMs (Figure S3A). Expression of H2-Ab1 showed the opposite pattern, with increasing expression over the course of infection in total AMs. The decrease of Nfe2l2 expression in total AMs after 2 weeks and the difference in expression between CD38− and CD38+ AMs, which likely reflect tissue-resident and recruited populations, respectively, explain why the difference in MHC II expression between WT and NRF2CondKO AMs disappears after day 14 (Figure S2). Similarly, we compared Nfe2l2 expression in AM and IM subsets at 0 and 2 weeks post-infection, again finding that CD38− subsets of AMs expressed the highest levels of Nfe2l2 compared to CD38+ AMs or IMs (Figure S3B).

Lastly, we examined the relationship between Nfe2l2 and H2-Ab1 expression at the individual cell level. We found a negative correlation (by Pearson’s correlation) between Nfe2l2 and H2-Ab1 expression in AMs at 3–6 weeks post-infection, where AMs with the lowest Nfe2l2 expression expressed the highest levels of H2-Ab1 (Figure S3C). Nfe2l2 expression peaks in IMs at 2 weeks post-infection (Figure S3D). At 2 weeks, Nos2+ IMs express higher levels of Nfe2l2 and lower levels of H2-Ab1 compared to Nos2− IMs (Figure S3B), and by 3–4 weeks, both Nos2+ and Nos2− IMs decrease expression of Nfe2l2 and increase expression of H2-Ab1, while Nfe2l2 starts to increase slightly again by 6 weeks post-infection (Figure S3D). Overall, these data support a model where tissue-resident AMs initially express very high levels of Nfe2l2, leading to inhibition of MHC II. After day 14, Nfe2l2 expression declines in the total AM population, associated with an influx of monocyte-derived recruited AMs that express lower levels of NRF2 and higher levels of MHC II than tissue-resident cells.

NRF2 impedes MHC II gene expression in AMs, but not in BMDMs, at baseline

By microscopy, we observed that LPS stimulation was sufficient to induce NRF2 protein accumulation in the nuclei of AMs, and the addition of L-sulf did not further increase NRF2 staining (Figure S4A). In contrast, NRF2 was only detected in LPS-stimulated BMDMs following the addition of L-sulf (Figure S4B). The expression levels of the NRF2 target gene, Nqo1, were high in AMs at baseline and not affected by L-sulf treatment (Figure 2A). In contrast, Nqo1 expression was increased in BMDMs after the addition of L-sulf (Figure 2B). To directly compare NRF2 activity across cell types, we measured Nqo1 gene expression in primary AMs and BMDMs at baseline. Consistently, we observed that AMs have significantly higher Nqo1 expression at baseline than BMDMs (Figure 2C). These results show that AMs, but not BMDMs, have high baseline NRF2 activity.

We next examined NRF2 regulation of the MHC II regulator, class II transactivator (Ciita),9,33,34 and H2-Ab1, a gene in the murine MHC II locus. We compared gene expression in AMs from Nrf2CondKO mice to Nrf2fl/fl littermate controls. Ciita and H2-Ab1 were significantly up-regulated in Nrf2CondKO AMs compared to Nrf2fl/fl AMs (Figure 2D). Similarly, H2-Ab1 expression was significantly higher in NRF2−/− versus WT mexAMs, and a similar trend was observed for Ciita (Figures S4C and S4D). In contrast, Ciita and H2-Ab1 levels were similar in WT and NRF2−/− BMDMs (Figure 2E). Based on these in vitro results, we re-examined our published transcriptional data from naive and Mtb-infected AMs sorted ex vivo from the lungs of WT and NRF2−/− mice 24 h following high-dose H37Rv infection.3 Ciita and H2-Ab1 levels were elevated in both naive and Mtb-infected AMs from NRF2−/− mice compared to AMs from WT mice (Figure 2F). Our findings reveal a distinctive regulatory role for NRF2 in suppressing baseline expression of MHC II genes in AMs, consistent with the elevated baseline activity of NRF2 in AMs compared to other macrophages.

NRF2 inhibits MHC II protein levels in AMs but not in BMDMs

We next measured NRF2-regulated differences in MHC II internal and surface protein expression by flow cytometry. Surface MHC II of uninfected Nrf2fl/flLysMcre AMs was significantly higher than Nrf2fl/fl AMs (Figure 3A). Compiling data over multiple experiments, we found that MHC II surface expression in AMs and mexAMs lacking NRF2 expression was significantly higher (1.61-fold and 1.76-fold, respectively) than their respective WT controls (Figure 3B). In contrast, NRF2−/− BMDMs showed a minimal increase in MHC II surface expression (1.08-fold) compared to WT BMDMs (Figure 3B). In addition to transcriptional regulation,9,35 MHC II expression is regulated by intracellular protein trafficking, including cell surface translocation, endocytosis, and degradation.10 Therefore, we also evaluated NRF2 regulation of total intracellular MHC II protein expression (Figures 3C–3E). We found that internal expression of MHC II in NRF2−/− mexAMs was significantly increased 1.69-fold over WT at baseline (Figure 3C), a difference retained following LPS, but not IFNγ, stimulation (Figures 3D and 3E). Given that IFNγ is a strong promoter of Ciita and MHC II expression,9,10,35 these data suggest that although NRF2 inhibits internal protein expression at baseline and after a weak stimulus, such as LPS, regulation of internal MHC II levels can be superseded by a strong stimulus, such as IFNγ. Overall, our results demonstrate that NRF2 regulates MHC II surface and intracellular expression in AMs. Although these results do not rule out an effect on intracellular trafficking, per se, they suggest that primary regulation by NRF2 is at the level of MHC II gene expression and total protein levels.

NRF2 repression of AM MHC II is retained after PAMP and cytokine stimulation

Many signals can up-regulate surface expression of MHC II on myeloid cells.10 We next investigated the influence of NRF2 on MHC II expression following exposure to various PAMPs. We exposed WT and NRF2−/− mexAMs to ligands of different Toll-like receptors, including LPS (TLR4 ligand), Pam3Cys (TLR1/2 ligand), CpG (TLR9 ligand), and R848 (TLR7/8) in addition to 2′3′-cGAMP (STING ligand). These PAMPs were chosen to represent signals from bacterial and viral infections, including many associated with Mtb36 (Figure 4A). NRF2−/− mexAMs exhibited higher MHC II expression compared to WT mexAMs across these varied stimuli. We also evaluated NRF2 regulation following type I and II IFNs (Figure 4B). IFNβ can promote MHC II expression37 but is typically associated with TB disease progression rather than protection.38–41 IFNγ is a robust inducer of MHC II and activates macrophages to restrict Mtb growth.42–44 Despite no difference in internal MHC II expression after IFNγ stimulation (Figure 3D), NRF2−/− mexAMs had higher surface MHC II expression than WT mexAMs after IFNγ stimulation (Figure 4B). These results indicate that NRF2 still regulates surface MHC II expression after IFNγ stimulation, despite known IFNγ regulation of the CIITA pathway.45 In contrast, WT and NRF2−/− mexAMs expressed equivalent levels of surface MHC II after IFNβ stimulation (Figure 4B). Primary AMs lacking NRF2 also retained higher MHC II levels than WT controls after both LPS and IFNγ stimulation (Figure 4C).

Considering that MHC II expression was higher in NRF2−/− AMs at baseline and after stimulation, we next determined whether the effect on MHC II levels was further enhanced, retained, or diminished by stimulation. We compared the ratio of MHC II expression in Nrf2CondKO to Nrf2fl/fl AMs in untreated versus LPS, IFNγ, and Mtb infection conditions (Figures 4D, S5A, and S5C). The expression ratios for LPS or IFNγ conditions were equivalent to the ratio for untreated AMs, while the Mtb infection condition was slightly higher. These results indicate that while the baseline levels of MHC II expression in AMs are NRF2 regulated, NRF2 also maintains a similar impact on MHC II expression after stimulation.

To determine the effect of NRF2 in inflammatory macrophages, we performed PAMP stimulations in WT and NRF2−/− BMDMs in the presence or absence of L-sulf (Figures S5B and S5D). We observed a significant decrease in MHC II expression following L-sulf treatment in WT, but not NRF2−/− , BMDMs after LPS stimulation, suggesting that exogenous NRF2 activation can impede LPS-induced MHC II expression in BMDMs (Figure S6A). In contrast, MHC II induction by 2′3′-cGAMP was not inhibited by L-sulf (Figure S6A). To compare different stimulation conditions, we calculated the percent inhibition of MHC II by L-sulf in BMDMs across 5 PAMPs. MHC II up-regulation by LPS, Pam3Cys, CpG and R848, but not 2′3′-cGAMP, was significantly inhibited by L-sulf in an NRF2-dependent manner (Figure S6B). Stimulation of BMDMs with other Mtb-associated PAMPs, trehalose-6,6-dibehenate (TDB) and muramyl dipeptide (MDP), did not show any induction of surface MHC II expression (Figure S6C). Overall, these results show that NRF2 can modulate MHC II expression in inflammatory macrophages under most stimulation conditions when NRF2 is activated.

We also measured MHC II expression in WT and NRF2−/− bone marrow-derived DCs (BMDCs) after LPS stimulation after the addition of L-sulf. As expected, MHC II expression was much higher in BMDCs than in macrophages, yet we observed a similar increase of MHC II in WT and NRF2−/− cells upon stimulation, with minimal effect of L-sulf (Figure S6D). To determine whether NRF2 impacts other antigen presentation pathways, we measured MHC I in WT and NRF2−/− mexAMs following LPS stimulation and Mtb infection. As expected, both LPS and Mtb led to an increase in MHC I expression (Figure 4E). However, there was no difference between WT and NRF2−/− cells, indicating that MHC I expression is not regulated by NRF2. Overall, NRF2 inhibition of antigen presentation affects MHC II, but not MHC I, and is specific to macrophages, not DCs.

NRF2 inhibition of MHC II in AMs inhibits antigen-specific CD4+ T cell activation

Previous studies found that human AMs are not effective at antigen presentation46–49 and, in some cases, can induce T cell unresponsiveness.50 To determine whether NRF2 inhibition of AM MHC II impedes their ability to activate CD4+ T cells, we established an in vitro co-culture system based on previous studies.51 MexAMs or primary AMs were loaded with ESAT63-17 peptide and co-cultured with C7 TCR transgenic CD4+ T cells (hereafter, C7 T cells), which express a TCR specific for the ESAT63-17 Mtb peptide (Figure S7A). When cultured with WT macrophages loaded with increasing concentrations of peptide, C7 T cells produced higher amounts of IFNγ. C7 T cells produced significantly more IFNγ when cultured with NRF2−/− versus WT mexAMs or AMs (Figure 5A). A similar trend was observed in a BMDM C7 T cell co-culture system under exogenous NRF2 activation (Figure S7B). Next, we cultured C7 T cells with H37Rv-infected WT or NRF2−/− mexAMs. C7 T cells produced significantly higher amounts of IFNγ when cultured with Mtb-infected NRF2−/− versus WT mexAMs (Figure 5B). We also observed significantly higher levels of MHC II and CD86, co-stimulatory and macrophage activation markers, on NRF2−/− mexAMs compared to WT mexAMs 24 h after co-culture (Figure 5C). In parallel, we measured higher expression of CD69, a marker of T cell activation, on C7 T cells after co-culture with Mtb-infected NRF2−/− mexAMs compared to WT mexAMs (Figure 5D). We found that up-regulation of CD86 on Mtb-infected WT and NRF2−/− mexAMs was dependent on the presence of T cells (Figure 5E). To confirm that differences in IFNγ production or activation markers were not due to initial Mtb uptake, we measured bacterial burden by colony-forming units (CFUs) on 1 dpi, a time point before C7 T cells would impact bacterial growth. There was no difference between the bacterial burden of WT and NRF2−/− mexAMs (Figure 5F).

Given that activation of CD4+ T cells is essential for an effective host response to Mtb,52–55 we next investigated the impact of NRF2 on CD4+ T cell activation by primary AMs from the airways of Mtb-infected mice. At 14 dpi, AMs were isolated and co-cultured ex vivo with C7 T cells (Figure 5G). Consistent with our in vitro results, C7 T cells produced significantly more IFNγ when cultured with Mtb-infected AMs that lacked NRF2 expression than with control AMs (Figure 5H). We also confirmed that the difference in T cell activation was not confounded by differences in Mtb uptake. IFNγ production from C7 T cells was increased by 3.22-fold after co-culture with Mtb-infected AMs that lacked NRF2 expression relative to co-culture with Mtb-infected control AMs, while the difference in Mtb uptake between AMs was minimal (1.29-fold; 14.73% vs. 18.65%) (Figure 5I). These results demonstrate a role for NRF2-mediated regulation of macrophage MHC II in T cell activation during Mtb infection, both in vitro and ex vivo, and suggest that NRF2 can limit the ability of infected macrophages to promote effective adaptive immune responses.

DISCUSSION

AMs occupy a unique niche within the lung alveolar spaces and are critical in the early response to Mtb infection.2,3,56 Despite their early sentinel role, AMs are hyporesponsive during the initial phases of Mtb infection.3,8,28,30,57 In this context, NRF2 serves as a critical modulator of AMs during Mtb infection. We previously showed that, as early as 12 h post-infection, AMs mount an NRF2-dependent cell-protective, rather than proinflammatory, response.3 NRF2 is known to regulate many processes including antioxidant production, cell death, and iron sequestration, but not all functions are yet appreciated16–18,20,58,59

By specifically examining AMs, our study reveals that NRF2 inhibits MHC II expression on Mtb-infected AMs by limiting expression of Ciita, an MHC II master regulator. NRF2 regulation of MHC II at the level of Ciita transcription was unexpected. Our previous study detected 583 genes with NRF2 binding motifs by HOMER and 3,975 NRF2 chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) binding peaks in macrophages (GSE75175)60 but found no evidence for NRF2 binding at the promoter or enhancer regions for Ciita or the MHC II locus.3 To confirm, we visualized the genomic region around Ciita, including promoter regions (pI, pIII, and pIV), within 20 kb of the gene start site61 and the MHC II locus. NRF2 ChIP-seq genomic tracks from NRF2-activated peritoneal macrophages contained peaks at promoter regions of NRF2 target genes (Nqo1, Hmox1, and Gclm) but not at Ciita or the MHC II locus (Figures S8A and S8B). Therefore, we hypothesize that regulation of Ciita by NRF2 is indirect. Interestingly, a recent study demonstrated that KEAP1, an inhibitor of NRF2, promotes IFNγ-induced Ciita and MHC II expression in HeLa cells through inhibition of HDAC activity.62 However, by co-depleting KEAP1 and NRF2, the authors concluded that the KEAP1 effect was independent of NRF2, a disparity from our results that could be due to cell type differences.

NRF2−/− AMs at baseline express higher levels of Ciita and H2-Ab1 than WT AMs, while NRF2−/− BMDMs do not express higher levels than WT BMDMs. This cell-specific effect is likely due to a difference in baseline NRF2 activity. While Mtb-infected AMs significantly up-regulate the NRF2 transcriptional program over naive AMs in vivo, our results indicate that AMs already have relatively high NRF2 activity at baseline. We detected NRF2 accumulation by microscopy in AMs, but not BMDMs, after LPS stimulation (Figures S4A and S4B) and higher expression levels of NRF2 target gene Nqo1 in AMs relative to BMDMs (Figure 2C). Treating BMDMs with an NRF2 agonist revealed a difference in MHC II between WT and NRF2−/− cells, yet L-sulf treatment did not alter MHC II expression in BMDCs. The fact that exogenous NRF2 activation impacts MHC II expression in macrophages, but not DCs, highlights a fundamental cell-type-specific difference in MHC II regulation. In further support of an AM-specific effect, in Mtb-infected lungs, NRF2 inhibits MHC II expression on AMs but not on pulmonary MDMs, DCs, or PMNs. Similarly, the fact that Nfe2l2 expression in total AMs decreases over the course of Mtb infection likely explains why NRF2-mediated inhibition of MHC II on AMs appears to wane after day 14.

Overall, our results suggest that macrophages with high NRF2 activity will have impeded MHC II presentation. We surmise that because AMs have high NRF2 activity at baseline, NRF2 inhibits AM MHC II under many conditions. However, NRF2 regulation of MHC II may be restricted to specific conditions for other macrophage subsets. For example, recent studies have identified subsets of IMs with high expression of NRF2 and NRF2-associated oxidative stress response genes between 2 and 4 weeks following Mtb infection,28,29,57 which we would predict may also have impaired antigen presentation. Similarly, a recent study showed that high NRF2 activity in MDMs within the tumor microenvironment of a murine non-small-cell lung cancer model promotes immunosuppression and limits myeloid expression of MHC II, CD86, and CD40.63

While our studies were focused on host-mediated regulation of MHC II, other Mtb-specific mechanisms have been identified that can inhibit MHC II in macrophages. The Mtb and M. bovis 19-kDa lipoprotein can suppress MHC II through TLR2-dependent signaling in murine AMs64 and MDMs.65 Several Mtb lipoproteins, LprA, LprG, and Rv1016c, can inhibit IFNγ-induced CIITA and MHC II expression in murine macrophages and human THP-1s via TLR2-dependent signaling and MAPK pathways.66,67 Mtb can also inhibit MHC II antigen presentation by disrupting antigen processing and peptide loading and by reducing the trafficking of newly formed MHC II molecules,68 which would decrease the surface expression of peptide-MHC II complexes.69 Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Mtb targets MHC II expression and antigen processing across various macrophage models to evade CD4+ T cell recognition. Our studies contribute a host-mediated mechanism of MHC II regulation in Mtb-infected cells.

While our studies demonstrate NRF2-mediated inhibition of AM antigen presentation, NRF2 has many other known functions relevant during infection. NRF2 protects cells from oxidative stress by promoting antioxidant and NADPH production, glutathione metabolism, and iron sequestration.15 NRF2 inhibits iron and lipid peroxidation-dependent ferroptotic cell death and can promote tumor-mediated immunosuppression and resistance to cancer therapy.13 NRF2 can directly tune macrophage responses by mediating the anti-inflammatory function of itaconate, suppression of type I IFN production,70 suppression of glycolysis following lipid accumulation,71 negative regulation of STING signaling,72 and suppression of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-1β.21 During infection, NRF2 can have disease-specific effects. NRF2 expression is beneficial to the host in Staphylococcus aureus, respiratory syncytial virus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection models, yet detrimental during infection with Marburg virus and Haemophilus influenzae.73–77

Activation of CD4+ T cells is a critical step for an effective host response to Mtb.52–55,78 AMs serve as primary targets for Mtb during the earliest stages of infection but do not migrate out of the lung. Instead, Mtb-infected monocytes or DCs migrate to the lung-draining lymph node and transfer bacterial antigens to uninfected DCs for priming of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells.79–82 Primed CD4+ T cells are recruited to the lung, where their production of IFNγ and other effector functions are crucial for Mtb control.83–87 CD4+ T cell help also prevents CD8+ T cell exhaustion and preserves their antibacterial function.53 While direct contact with infected cells is well appreciated for the cytotoxic function of CD8+ T cells, surprisingly, effector CD4+ T cells must also directly recognize MHC II-expressing infected cells for effective Mtb control.56 Srivastava et al. used mixed bone marrow chimeric mice to show that MHC II−/− DCs and macrophages contained greater numbers of bacteria than MHC II+/+ cells, and this difference was lost after CD4+ T cell depletion.78 Therefore, increased NRF2 expression could impede CD4+ T cell responses in the lung by constraining MHC II on infected macrophages.

Other mechanisms also limit pulmonary T cell responses during Mtb infection. A mismatch between the antigens presented by uninfected DCs during T cell priming in the draining lymph node and the antigens presented by infected macrophages in the lung can also lead to poor T cell recognition of Mtb-infected macrophages.88 Furthermore, virulent Mtb can inhibit CIITA and MHC II presentation by the mechanisms described earlier.83

In our co-culture system, the absence of NRF2 in AMs significantly increased IFNγ production by CD4+ T cells, in both in vitro and ex vivo settings, and enhanced activation of both cell types. IFNγ activates macrophages to restrict Mtb growth and promote bacterial clearance by enhancing phagocytosis, inducing nitric oxide, and promoting lysosomal fusion and degradation of phagosomal contents.89–93 Effective CD4+ T cell responses are crucial to coordinate the formation of granulomas, which are structured aggregates that encapsulate infected macrophages within the lung.31,94 Several recent studies find heterogeneity in the permissiveness of different myeloid cells in the lung during infection.28,30,31 Importantly, recent studies have reported activation of NRF2 within the granuloma environment. A study of M. marinum-infected adult zebrafish found increased expression of NRF2 (nfe2l2a) and NRF2 target genes in response to TNF-mediated oxidative stress within neutrophil-deficient granulomas.95 A study of granulomas in Mtb-infected non-human primates (NHPs) found enrichment of NRF2-associated pathways, such as “ROS pathway” (reactive oxygen species pathway) and “xenobiotic metabolism,” within the hypoxic core, which was associated with CD68+ and CD11c+ macrophages in granulomas with high bacterial burden.96 The paradoxical increase in NRF2 activity in locations with minimal oxygen levels points to the complexity of the relationship between oxygen-driven regulators NRF2 and HIF-1α.97 Overall, these findings suggest that NRF2 is activated within granuloma environments and likely regulates immune cell function within lesions. Whether NRF2 inhibits antigen presentation within these structures is an important question for future study.

Lastly, the impact of NRF2 on antigen presentation may extend beyond effector CD4+ T cells. Memory CD4+ T cells also require antigen presentation for homeostatic maintenance. Our previous study demonstrated that, following subcutaneous Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccination, Mtb-infected AMs still up-regulated NRF2 target genes and were enriched for NRF2-associated transcriptional pathways 1 dpi, suggesting that, in the context of vaccination, NRF2 may also limit AM antigen presentation to memory Mtb-specific CD4+ T cells.98 Therefore, recognition of infected macrophages by effector CD4+ T cells in the lung during active infection and persistence of long-lived memory CD4+ T cells following vaccination or infection could be impeded if T cells interact with AMs or IMs with high NRF2 activity.

In conclusion, understanding the impact of NRF2 on AM immune responses and antigen presentation provides insights into the complex interplay between innate and adaptive immunity during Mtb infection. Targeting NRF2 signaling pathways in AMs represents a promising avenue for therapeutic interventions to enhance host immune defenses.20,99 Blockade of NRF2 could promote CD4+ T cell production of IFNγ, a focus of current TB vaccine candidates.88 However, separating inhibition of antigen presentation from other functions of NRF2 that are critical to dampen tissue inflammation and limit lung pathology will be a challenge.100,101 Therefore, future research efforts should focus on elucidating the precise molecular mechanisms underlying NRF2-mediated immune modulation.

Limitations of the study

We demonstrate NRF2 inhibition of antigen presentation by AMs and show that NRF2 regulates Ciita and H2-Ab1 gene expression and MHC II protein expression, without affecting MHC I. However, we were not yet able to identify the molecular mechanism that links NRF2 and Ciita, given that the Ciita promoter does not contain a known NRF2-binding site. Our use of ESAT63-17-specific C7 transgenic CD4+ T cells shows how NRF2 modulation of MHC II affects T cell activation. Examining a broader repertoire of T cell responses, including the Ag85b240-254-specific P25 transgenic CD4+ T cells,51 as well as in vivo primary T cell responses, would generalize how NRF2 affects T cell recognition. While we examined the effect of NRF2 in limiting MHC II induction following stimulation with PAMPs that increase MHC II in macrophages and other Mtb-relevant PAMPs that do not increase MHC II expression, there are still many other Mtb-relevant ligands that have yet to be screened. Due to the limitations of the NRF2 KO and NRF2CondKO strains with potential effects on non-AM cells102,103 and the feasibility of isolating sufficient numbers of cells, primary AMs were not used in all experiments. Instead, we chose to use mexAMs at low passage numbers, as an alternative tool. Another question that remains unanswered is when during infection NRF2-regulated MHC II expression and reduced T cell activation impact the endogenous host response and bacterial clearance. Further studies are needed to investigate the downstream impact of NRF2 and its role in both effector and memory CD4+ T cell responses. Finally, future studies should investigate the role of NRF2-regulated MHC II expression on AMs following vaccination, especially considering the crucial role of MHC II in maintaining memory CD4+ T cells,104,105 the importance of airway memory CD4+ T cells in protection from Mtb,88 and AM remodeling in the airway after exposure to BCG vaccination.98 Addressing these key factors would provide valuable insights for therapeutic and vaccine development. Our study was performed entirely in a murine system, and we have not yet examined the association between NRF2 and MHC II in humans. However, there is strong evidence for critical roles for both MHC II and NRF2 in human disease. For example, studies in both humans and mice have shown that genetic variation in MHC II impacts TB susceptibility and disease severity.106–109 NRF2 and NRF2 target pathways have been associated with TB in both mice and humans. Amaral et al. demonstrated that dysregulated glutathione, Gpx4 (an NRF2 target), and lipid peroxidation were associated with patients with active TB and validated these pathways during TB infection in Gpx4 conditional KO mice.101 In a follow-up study, Amaral et al. showed that BACH1, an NRF2 competitive inhibitor, was associated with active TB in humans and increased pathogenesis during chronic infection in mice.100 Several studies have examined functional polymorphisms in NRF2 during human disease.110 NRF2 polymorphisms are associated with respiratory inflammatory diseases, such as asthma, COPD, and acute lung injury. A recent study found direct associations between NRF2 polymorphisms (NFE2l2 gene) and Mtb susceptibility in 1,950 Chinese Han participants.111 The study concluded that one of the risk alleles (rs13005431) led to a decrease in NRF2 expression and was associated with an increased risk for Mtb disease. While the late-stage protective effect of NRF2 on lung inflammation is opposite to the detrimental effect of NRF2 on antigen presentation observed here, these studies suggest that pulmonary NRF2 regulation is conserved across species and warrants follow-up studies to address the direct role of NRF2 in antigen presentation and opportunities for biological targeting.

STAR★METHODS

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Mice

WT C57BL/6J and NRF2−/− (B6.129X1-Nfe2l2tm1Ywk/J) mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). NRF2 conditional KO mice, Nrf2floxed (C57BL/6-Nfe2l2tm1.1Sred/SbisJ), CD11ccre [B6.Cg-Tg(Itgax-cre)1-1Reiz/J], and LysMcre [B6.1 29P2-Lyz2tm1(cre)Ifo/J] mice were also purchased from the Jackson Laboratory and crossed to generate Nrf2CD11c and Nrf2LysM transgenic lines. The animals were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at the University of Massachusetts Amherst vivarium. Research was conducted in compliance with ethical regulations and biosafety and approved by the University of Massachusetts Amherst Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol #4877). Sex-matched male and female mice aged 6 to 12 weeks were utilized for the experiments. The influence of sex or gender was not tested in these studies.

METHOD DETAILS

Alveolar macrophage isolation

Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was carried out on mice from WT and Nrf2LysM or Nrf2CD11c after euthanasia, as previously described.112 Briefly, the trachea was exposed, and an incision was made using Vannas Micro Scissors (VWR). A 20-gauge, 1-inch intravenous catheter (McKesson) connected to a 1 mL syringe was used to instill 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) into the lungs. The PBS was gently flushed and aspirated three to four times, and the recovered fluid was immediately placed on ice. This lavage process was repeated three to four additional times to collect the maximum number of cells. The collected samples were filtered through a 70μm strainer, and the cells were pelleted by centrifugation. For antibody staining experiments, the cell pellet was resuspended in FACS buffer solution. Alternatively, for cell culture assays, the cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in a 96-well plate using complete RPMI medium, with RPMI (Millipore Sigma, 11875-119), 10% FBS (Biowest), 1% L-glutamine (2 μM, Gibco) and 1% penicillin streptomycin (100 U/mL, Gibco).

Bone marrow-derived macrophage and dendritic cell isolation

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) and dendritic cells (BMDCs) were obtained by harvesting femurs from either WT or NRF2−/− mice strains. For BMDMs, the cells collected from the bone marrow were cultured in complete RPMI medium supplemented with 50 ng/mL recombinant human macrophage colony stimulating factor (M-CSF, Peprotech) for a period of 6 days to promote differentiation into macrophages. BMDM media was changed on day 4 of culture. For BMDCs, the cells collected from the bone marrow were cultured in complete RPMI medium supplemented with 50 μM 2-Mercaptoethanol (Gibco), 1 mM HEPES (Gibco), and 20 ng/ml GM-CSF (Peprotech) for a period of 7 days to promote differentiation into DCs. BMDC media was changed on day 3 of culture.

Murine ex vivo alveolar macrophage (mexAM) generation

Primary AMs were isolated by BAL from WT and NRF2−/− mice as described above. AMs were cultured in complete media containing RPMI media, 10% fetal bovine serum (VWR), 2 mM L-glutamine (Invitrogen), 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin (Invitrogen), and 30 ng/mL GM-CSF (PeproTech), 10 ng/ml TGF-β (PeproTech), and 1 μM rosiglitazone (Sigma-Aldrich). To minimize variability due to changes in cell phenotypes over time in culture, all mexAMs used in these studies were between passage 3 and passage 10. The stable expression of AM markers, including Siglec F, CD11c, CD64 were confirmed across passages to ensure consistency and reproducibility within the results.

Mycobacterium tuberculosis aerosol infections and lung cell isolation

Aerosol infections were performed with H37Rv-mCherry at standard dose (~100 CFU) infection. Mice were enclosed in an aerosol infection chamber (Glas-Col) and frozen stocks of bacteria were thawed and placed inside the associated nebulizer. To determine the infectious dose, three mice in each infection were sacrificed one day later and lung homogenates were plated onto 7H10 plates for CFU enumeration. Analysis of experimental samples was performed on either 12 or 14 days post-infection. Lungs were removed and single-cell suspensions were prepared by Liberase (Roche) digestion containing DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at 37°C. Mechanical disruption was performed with the Gentle-MACS dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec), followed by double filtering through a 70 μM cell strainer and removal of red blood cells using ACK lysis buffer (Gibco). Cells were resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS, 1% FBS, 0.1% NaN3) prior to staining for flow cytometry.

Macrophage stimulation

Macrophages from BMDMs, mexAMs, or AMs were stimulated with PAMPs for a range from 4 h to 24 h depending on the analysis, including flow cytometry and ELISA (24 h), or RT-qPCR (4 h). PAMPs include Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) R595 100 ng/mL for mexAMs or 10 ng/ml for BMDMs (List labs), Pam3Cys 100 ng/ml (Pam3CSK4) (Invivogen), 2′3′-cGAMP 5 μg/ml (Invivogen), Resiquimod (R848) 100 μg/ml (Sigma-Aldrich), CpG 5 μM (Invivogen), Recombinant Mouse IFN-β1 10 ng/ml (BioLegend), Recombinant Mouse IFNγ 20 ng/mL (Peprotech).

Flow cytometry staining

Cells were resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS with 1% FBS and 0.01% NaN3) and Fc receptors were blocked with anti-CD16/32 (BioLegend). Cell viability was assessed with Zombie Violet dye (BioLegend). Surface staining included antibodies specific for murine, including Siglec F (clone S17007L), CD11c (clone N148), F4/80 (clone BM8). The following antibodies were used for lung digestion biomarkers I-A/I-E, CD86, CD69, CD11b, CD64, CD45, CD3, CD19, CD11c, Ly6G, and Ly6C (reagents from BioLegend unless otherwise noted). For lung digestion, cell numbers were enumerated using Polybead Polystyrene 15 μm Microspheres (Polysciences). Cells were then fixed in 2% PFA (Electron Microscopy Sciences) at room temperature. Samples were then washed with FACS buffer and resuspended in FACS buffer for immediate use or stored overnight at 4°C. Samples were analyzed on a 3- or 5-laser LSRFortessa flow cytometer (BD Bioscience) and FlowJo software (Tree Star, Inc.).

Internal and surface MHC II staining

Similar to standard staining for flow cytometry, as described above, cells were collected into FACS tubes and subsequently stained with Fc block (BioLegend), Live/Dead stain (Zombie Violet Fixable Viability Kit, BioLegend) and then cell surface markers (AMs or mexAMs, Siglec F (BioLegend), ; BMDMs F4/80 (clone BM8, BioLegend), and I-A/I-E antibody (BioLegend) for surface MHC II for 20 min at 4°C. Cells were resuspended in 200 μL of Cyto-Fast Fix/Perm Buffer (BioLegend) and incubated for 20 min at room temperature and washed with Cyto-Fast Perm Wash (BioLegend). Another I-A/I-E antibody in a different fluorophore was diluted 1:100 in Cyto-Fast Perm Wash and added to the cells for 20 min at 4°C to measure internal MHC II. Cells were then washed and fixed with 2% PFA at room temperature. Samples were then washed with FACS buffer and resuspended in FACS buffer for immediate use or stored overnight at 4°C. Samples were analyzed on a 3- or 5-laser LSRFortessa flow cytometer (BD Bioscience) and FlowJo software (Tree Star, Inc.).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Cells were plated at 0.1–0.5 × 105 density in either 96 or 24 well plates, respectively, followed by different stimulation. Supernatants were collected 24 h after macrophage stimulation, or 72 h after macrophage - C7 T cells co-culture. Murine IFNγ DuoSet ELISA were performed following manufacturer’s instructions (R&D Systems).

RNA isolation and quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR)

Primary cells or ex vivo cultured cells were plated at a 0.5–1 × 105 density in either 96 or 24 well plates, respectively. RNA isolation was performed using TRIzol (Invitrogen), followed by two sequential chloroform extractions, addition of GlycoBlue (Invitrogen), isopropanol precipitation, and 2 washes of 70% ethanol, with final resuspension in RNase free distilled water. RNA was quantified using the Biodrop duo (Biochrom). RNA amounts of 1ug per sample were converted to complementary DNA (cDNA) then amplified using RNA to cDNA EcoDry Premix (TaKaRa). TaqMan primer probes (IDT) were used for genes of interest: Nqo1, Ciita (ThermoFisher), H2-Ab1 (IDT). TaqMan Fast Universal PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) was used for the RT-qPCR reaction, performed by BioRad CFX Opus 96 RT-qPCR machine. All the generated data were then normalized relative to Gapdh or Ef1a housekeeping genes in each sample.

Expansion of transgenic C7 CD4+ T cells

C7 CD4+ T cells were a gift from Dr. Samuel Behar from UMass Chan, Worcester, MA. C7 TCR transgenic mice were originally generated by Dr. Eric Palmer.113 C7 CD4+ T cells were stimulated in vitro with irradiated splenocytes pulsed with the ESAT-63–17 peptide, in complete RPMI media containing 10% fetal bovine serum (VWR), 2 mM L-glutamine (Invitrogen), 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin (Invitrogen), 2-Mercaptoethanol (Gibco) and 20 U/mL recombinant murine IL-2 (Peprotech). After the initial stimulation, T cells were cultured in complete media containing recombinant murine IL-2 along with 0.01 μg/mL recombinant murine IL-7 (Peprotech). Cells were split every two days for 3–4 divisions and then rested for two to three weeks before use.

T cell-macrophage co-culture

The following synthetic peptide epitope was used as an antigen for the T cell-macrophage co-culture: ESAT-63–17 (EQQWNFAGIEAAASA). The peptides were synthesized by Genscript. The macrophages were pulsed with 0.1–10 μM of the peptide for 1 h. After incubation, the macrophages were washed 3 to 5 times and then resuspended in complete RPMI 1640 media prior to the addition of the CD4+ T cells. For in vitro infections, macrophages were infected with mCherry-H37Rv at MOI 1.2:1 or MOI 6:1. Two hours after the infection, infected macrophages were washed 3 times with RPMI and C7 T cells were added with a 1:1 ratio of macrophages:T cells.

Mtb in vitro infection

mCherry-H37Rv was cultured from frozen stock in 7H9 media for 2 days to an OD600 of 0.1–0.3. Bacteria was added for two hours and then macrophage cultures were washed three times to remove extracellular bacteria. The final concentration was calculated based on strain titer to produce an effective MOI of 1.2:1, 2.4:1, and 6:1 after two hours of incubation and washing.

Ex vivo isolation of Mtb-infected AMs

Aerosol infections were performed with H37Rv-mCherry at standard dose (~100 CFU) infection as described above. At 14 days post-infection, BAL was performed from either naive or infected mice from control mice or AM NRF2-deficient mice. The cell suspension from the lavage fluid was seeded in a 96-well plate using complete RPMI medium, with RPMI (Millipore Sigma, 11875-119), 10% FBS (Biowest), 1% L-glutamine (2 μM, Gibco). Two hours after the incubation, the seeded plate was washed 3 times with RPMI to remove red blood cells from the wells. C7 T cells were then added on top with a 1:1 ratio of alveolar macrophages:T cells. Cells were then co-cultured for an additional 72 h.

Immunofluorescence microscopy

BMDMs and mexAMs from WT and NRF2−/− strains were seeded at 100,000 cells per well in an 8-well chamber slides (Thermo Fisher Lab-Tek®II Chamber Slide). Cells were incubated in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C. To identify NRF2 activation, cells were treated with L-sulforaphane overnight. Cells were washed with PBS followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes at room temperature. Cells were incubated in blocking buffer (containing 1X PBS, 5% normal Goat serum, 0.3% Triton X-100) for 1 h with anti-Nrf2 (D1Z9) XP Rabbit Antibody (Cell signaling) or isotype control antibody Rabbit (DA1E) Antibody IgG (Cell signaling) and incubated at 4°C overnight. The next day, after washing cells three times with PBS, secondary antibody Anti-rabbit IgG Fab2 AF488 (Cell Signaling) was added, followed by 3 washes in PBS and 2 washes in 3% BSA. The nuclei were stained with Hoechst (DAPI 20mM, Thermo Fisher) for 15 min, followed by 2 washes in 3% BSA and two washes in PBS. Slides were then mounted and allowed to air-dry at room temperature, avoiding direct light exposure. The next day, slides were imaged using the Nikon Ti2 stand with A1HD(1024) Resonant Scanning Confocal.

Analysis of publicly available scRNAseq dataset

A publicly available dataset (GSE245950) was downloaded from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) in the form of a Seurat object that profiled sorted AMs and IMs at 2, 3, 4, and 6 weeks after mCherry-H37Rv high dose infection.28 Samples were split based on the pre-defined AM and IM subsets used in the original study (“CD38− TR-AMs_1”, “CD38− TR-AMs_2”, “CD38− TR-AMs_3”, “CD38+ TR-AMs”, “NOS2+ IMs”, “NOS2− IMs”) and expression of Nfe2l2 and H2-Ab1 was plotted in cells with detectable (nonzero) expression using the “Seurat” package.114 Pearson’s Correlation was used to determine the relationship between Nfe2l2 and H2-Ab1 expression across the population, with the Pearson’s Correlation Coefficient, split by timepoint.

Visualization of NRF2 ChIP-seq data (Otsuki et al.)

NRF2 ChIP-seq.bed files were downloaded from NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE 75175). Files represented samples collected from WT peritoneal macrophages treated with NRF2 agonist 100 μM diethylmaleate (DEM) for 4 h. Genomic tracks of promoter regions of NRF2 target genes (Nqo1, Hmox1, and Gclm), Ciita, and MHC II locus (H2-Ab1, H2-Aa, H2-Eb1) were visualized using Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV).115
