Strictly abnormal geodesics with a degeneracy point in the interior of their domain
Nicola Paddeu †, Alessandro Socionovo ⋆

Abstract
. In this article, we study abnormal curves in a family of sub-Riemannian manifolds of rank 2. We focus on abnormal curves whose lifts to the cotangent bundle annihilate, at an interior point of the domain, all Lie brackets of length up to three of vector fields tangent to the distribution. We present a method to prove that such curves are length-minimizing. Finally, we prove that strictly abnormal geodesics may cease to be locally length-minimizing after a change of the metric.



1.
Introduction

Sub-Riemannian manifolds are metric spaces equipped with a distance that arises from a smooth Riemannian metric defined on a smooth distribution of the manifold (see
[
2
]
,
[
4
]
or
[
9
]
for a detailed presentation of sub-Riemannian manifolds). One of the most difficult and studied problems in sub-Riemannian geometry
is the regularity of
geodesics
, i.e., the regularity of isometric embedding of intervals.

The Pontryagin Maximum Principle provides necessary conditions for a curve to be a geodesic, and curves that satisfy these necessary conditions are called
extremal curves
. There are two non-disjoint classes of extremal curves, named
normal
and
abnormal
. Normal curves are solutions of a smooth ODE that involves the metric, and they are smooth and locally geodesics. Instead, abnormal curves do not depend on the metric and they may be neither smooth nor locally geodesics. Hence, the regularity of geodesics reduces to the regularity of non-normal geodesics. Consequently, abnormal curves have become one of the main topics of research in sub-Riemannian geometry, with a particular attention to
strictly abnormal
curves, namely, curves that are abnormal but not normal.

The first example of a strictly abnormal geodesic was discovered by Montgomery in
[
8
]
, and it was later generalized in
[
1
,
7
]
. All these curves are both smooth and locally geodesics regardless of the choice of the metric, and for a long time they constituted the only known examples of strictly abnormal geodesics. Recently, a new class of examples was discovered in
[
3
,
10
]
, where the authors show the existence of strictly abnormal geodesics that lose regularity at a boundary point of their domain. All these examples appear in sub-Riemannian manifolds with 2-dimensional distribution.

Apart from these few models, the literature lacks examples of strictly abnormal geodesics, as well as methods to prove that particular abnormal curves are of minimal length. The results obtained in
[
1
,
7
]
are based on a non-degeneracy assumption on the distribution along the strictly abnormal curve. Namely, let
M
be a sub-Riemannian manifold and
X_{1},X_{2}
be a frame for the distribution. An abnormal curve
\gamma:I{\;\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\subset\mathbb{R}}\to M
with lift
\lambda:I\to T^{*}M
(see
[
2
, Section 3.4]
for more details about lifts of extremal curves in the cotangent bundle) is
non-degenerate
at
t\in I
if

(1.1)

\max\{|\langle\lambda(t),[X_{1},[X_{1},X_{2}]]\rangle|,|\langle\lambda(t),[X_{2},[X_{1},X_{2}]]\rangle|\}\neq 0.

Curves satisfying the condition (
1.1
) for all times are known as
regular abnormal
or
nice abnormal
, and they are the object of study of
[
1
,
7
]
. Moreover, the non-degeneracy assumption (
1.1
) is satisfied by the strictly abnormal geodesics presented in
[
3
]
at every point in the interior of their domain, although it is not satisfied at a boundary point. Finding strictly abnormal geodesics that violate the non-degeneracy condition (
1.1
) at an interior point of their domain may be the first step towards proving the non-regularity of sub-Riemannian geodesics at an interior point, see
[
1
, Theorem 3.2]
.

In this article, we present a broad class of new examples of strictly abnormal geodesics that do not satisfy the non-degeneracy assumption (
1.1
) at an interior point of their domain. As a consequence, we give a negative
answer the following natural question: does strictly abnormal geodesics remain locally geodesic for every choice of the metric? Notice that, for the nice abnormal minimizers studied in
[
1
,
7
]
, this question has been positively answered, see
[
7
]
.

Let us introduce the sub-Riemannian manifolds containing the new examples of strictly abnormal geodesics we intend to present. Let
M
be the manifold defined by

(1.2)

M:=(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})\times(-1,1)\times\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3},

with coordinates
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in M
. For
b\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider the vector fields

(1.3)

X_{1}(x):=\partial_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad X_{2}(x):=\partial_{2}+x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{b}\partial_{3},\quad\text{for all }x\in M,

and we equip
M
with the distribution

(1.4)

\mathscr{D}:=\mathrm{span}\{X_{1},X_{2}\}.

We define the curve
\gamma:(-1,1)\to M
by setting

(1.5)

\gamma(t):=(0,t,0),\quad\text{for all }t\in(-1,1).

The curve
\gamma
is abnormal in
(M,\mathscr{D})
for all
b\in\mathbb{N}
, see Section 2. Since

(1.6)

[X_{i},[X_{1},X_{2}]](\gamma(0))=0,\quad\text{for all }i=1,2,

the non-degeneracy assumption (
1.1
) is not satisfied at
t=0
, which is an interior point of the domain of
\gamma
.

For
a\in\mathbb{N}
and for a function
\alpha\in C^{\infty}((-1,1)),[0,1])
, we equip the distribution
\mathscr{D}
with the Riemannian metric
g
defined by setting

(1.7)

g_{x}(X_{1},X_{1})=1,\quad g_{x}(X_{1},X_{2})=0,\quad g_{x}(X_{2},X_{2})=1-\alpha(x_{2})x_{1}x_{2}^{a},

for all
x\in M
. In this way, we have a family of sub-Riemannian manifolds
(M,\mathscr{D},g)
depending on the parameters
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
and on the function
\alpha
.

The main result of this article is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

For
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in C^{\infty}((-1,1),[0,1])
, let
(M,\mathscr{D},g)
be the sub-Riemannian manifold defined by (
1.2
), (
1.4
), and (
1.7
). Let
\gamma:(-1,1)\to M
be the curve defined by (
1.5
). If
b\leq 2a
and
b
is even, then there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that for all
\alpha\in C^{\infty}((-1,1),[0,1])
the curve
\gamma|_{[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]}
is a geodesic.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
relies on a partial generalization of the techniques developed in
[
7
, Section 7.1]
. In fact, we prove similar estimates as in
[
7
, Section 7.1]
without requiring the non-degeneracy assumption (
1.1
). When the metric satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
, we use these estimates to prove that the curve
\gamma|_{[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]}
is locally geodesic.

We are interested in the case where the abnormal geodesics provided by Theorem
1.1
are strictly abnormal. For this reason, we provide a criterion that establish whether the curve
\gamma
defined by (
1.5
) is normal on a subinterval of its domain. Namely, we show that for
t_{1},t_{2}\in(-1,1)
, with
t_{1}<t_{2}
, the curve
\gamma|_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}
is normal if and only if
\alpha(t)=0
for all
t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
, see Corollary
2.5
. The idea behind this criterion is to show that
\gamma
is normal on a sub-interval of its domain if and only if its projection to a suitable Riemannian manifold is a geodesic on that sub-interval, see Proposition
2.4
.

We then investigate whether the strictly abnormal geodesics provided by Theorem
1.1
remain locally geodesic after a change of the Riemannian metric.

Theorem 1.2
.

For
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in C^{\infty}((-1,1),[0,1])
, let
(M,\mathscr{D},g)
be the sub-Riemannian manifold defined by (
1.2
), (
1.4
), and (
1.7
). Let
\gamma:(-1,1)\to M
be the curve defined by (
1.5
). If
\alpha\equiv 1
and
b>4a+4
, then the curve
\gamma
is not locally geodesic.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
consists of the explicit construction of a curve that is shorter than
\gamma|_{[0,\varepsilon]}
and that has the same initial and final points. This construction relies upon a cut and correction method, see also
[
5
,
6
,
10
]
.

Combining Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, we give an example of a strictly abnormal geodesic which is not locally geodesic for every metric.

Example 1.3
.

Fix
b=10
. Let
(M,\mathscr{D})
be as in (
1.2
)-(
1.4
)
and let
\gamma
be the curve defined by (
1.5
). For
a\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in C^{\infty}((-1,1),[0,1])
, let
g
be the Riemannian metric on
\mathscr{D}
given by (
1.7
).
By Theorem
1.1
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
\gamma|_{[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]}
is a strictly abnormal geodesic for the metric
g
obtained by choosing
\alpha\equiv 1
and
a\geq 20
, while, by Theorem
1.2
, it is not locally geodesic for the metric
g
defined by setting
\alpha\equiv 1
and
a=1
.

The paper is organized in the following way. In Section 2, we recall some basic definitions and facts in sub-Riemannian geometry and Hamiltonian mechanics, the Pontryagin Maximum Principle and the definitions of normal and abnormal curves. We then prove Theorem
2.5
. Section 3 is devoted to the study of minimality of abnormal curves in
(M,\mathscr{D},g)
and to the proof Theorem
1.1
. In Section 4, we prove Theorem
1.2
.

2.
Preliminaries and proof of Theorem
2.5

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an open set and let
M:=\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, with coordinates
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in M
. In this article, we consider sub-Riemannian structures on
M
. Namely, we fix a function
\psi\in C^{\infty}(M)
such that for all
x\in M
there exists
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
for which

(2.1)

\frac{\partial^{m}}{\partial x_{2}^{m}}\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x_{1}^{n}}\psi(x)\neq 0,

we define the vector fields

(2.2)

X_{1}(x):=\partial_{x_{1}},\quad X_{2}(x):=\partial_{x_{2}}+\psi(x)\partial_{x_{3}},

and we equip
M
with the
distribution

\mathscr{D}:=\mathrm{span}\{X_{1},X_{2}\}
. Moreover, we require that the function
\psi
does not depend on the third coordinate
x_{3}
, i.e., there holds

(2.3)

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{3}}\psi(x)=0,\quad\text{for all $x\in M$}.

Notice that, since
\psi
does not depend on
x_{3}
, the condition (
2.1
) is the Hörmander, or bracket-generating, condition.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an interval. We say that an absolutely continuous curve
\eta:I\to M
is
horizontal
if
\dot{\eta}(t)\in\mathscr{D}_{\eta(t)}
for almost every
t\in I
.
We say that a horizontal curve
\eta:I\to M
has
control

u\in L^{2}(I,\mathbb{R}^{2})
if

(2.4)

\dot{\eta}(t)=u_{1}(t)X_{1}(\eta(t))+u_{2}(t)X_{2}(\eta(t)),\text{ for almost every }t\in I.

Finally, we consider a Riemannian metric
g
on
\mathscr{D}
and the sub-Riemannian manifold
(M,\mathscr{D},g)
. We require the metric
g
to satisfy

(2.5)

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{3}}g(X_{i},X_{j})=0,\quad\text{for all }i,j\in\{1,2\}.

We define the
sub-Riemannian length
of a horizontal curve
\eta:[0,1]\to M
(with respect to the metric
g
) as

(2.6)

L(\eta):=\int_{0}^{1}g_{\eta(t)}(\dot{\eta}(t),\dot{\eta}(t))^{\frac{1}{2}}dt,

and for all
x,y\in M
, we define the

distance
as

(2.7)

d(x,y):=\inf\{L(\eta)\mid\eta:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\text{ horizontal},\eta(0)=x,\eta(1)=y\}.

The bracket-generating condition (
2.1
) and Chow’s Theorem
[
2
, Section 3.2]
ensure that
d
is indeed a finite value distance, and curves that realize the infimum in (
2.7
) are called
length-minimizers
. Notice that every geodesic is a length-minimizer and every length-minimizer reparametrized by arc-length is a geodesic.

Necessary conditions for a horizontal curve to be a geodesic are given by the Pontryagin Maximum Principle, see Theorem
2.2
below. In order to state the Pontryagin Maximum Principle, we introduce some notation and recall some basic facts about Hamiltonian mechanics.

We say that a curve
\lambda:I\to T^{*}M

solves the Hamilton equations
for some function
H\in C^{\infty}(T^{*}M)
if

(2.8)

\dot{\lambda}=\vec{H}(\lambda),

where
\vec{H}\in\Gamma(T^{*}M)
solves

(2.9)

\omega(\vec{H},X)=\mathrm{d}H(X),\quad\text{for all }X\in\Gamma(T^{*}M),

the
2
-form
\omega\in T^{*}M
being the canonical symplectic form, see
[
9
, Appendix A]
.
We refer the interested reader to
[
2
,
9
]
for more details about Hamiltonian mechanics.
The only fact of Hamiltonian mechanichs that we need in this article is the following remark, which is a direct consequence of Equations (
2.8
) and (
2.9
).

Remark 2.1
.

Let
\lambda:I\to T^{*}M
and
H,\tilde{H}\in C^{\infty}(T^{*}M)
. If
\mathrm{d}H_{\lambda(t)}=\mathrm{d}\tilde{H}_{\lambda(t)}
for all
t\in I
, then
\lambda
solves the Hamilton equations for
H
if and only if it solves the Hamilton equations for
\vec{H}
.

We are now ready to state the Pontryagin Maximum Principle. We follow
[
2
, Theorems 3.59-4.25, Proposition 4.38]
.
Let
\zeta\in\Omega^{1}(M)
be a
1
-form such that
\mathscr{D}_{x}=\ker(\zeta_{x})
for all
x\in M
.

Theorem 2.2

(Pontryagin Maximum Principle)
.

Let
\eta:I\to M
be a length-minimizing curve parametrized by multiple of arc-length. Then, at least one of the following conditions is satisfied:

(N)

there exists a lift
\lambda:I\to T^{*}M
of
\eta
that solves the Hamilton equations for the function
H\in C^{\infty}(T^{*}M)
defined by

(2.10)

H(\xi):=\max\{\langle\xi,X\rangle\mid X\in\Gamma(\mathscr{D}),\;g(X,X)=1\},\quad\text{for all }\xi\in T^{*}M;

(A)

for all
t\in I
there holds

(2.11)

(\zeta\wedge\mathrm{d}\zeta)_{\eta(t)}=0.

Horizontal curves satisfying condition (N) of Theorem
2.2
are called
normal
, while horizontal curves satisfying condition (A) are called
abnormal
. A horizontal curve may be both normal and abnormal, and all normal curves are smooth and locally length-minimizing. Horizontal curves that are abnormal but not normal are called
strictly abnormal
, and they are the subject of study of this article.

Notice that condition (A) in Theorem
2.2
does not depend on the metric
g
, and it follows that a curve in
(M,\mathscr{D})
is abnormal if and only if it is supported in the set

(2.12)

\Sigma:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\mid(\zeta\wedge\mathrm{d}\zeta)_{x}=0\},

which is called the
Martinet surface
of
(M,\mathscr{D})
.

Remark 2.3
.

For a distribution
\mathscr{D}
as in (
2.2
), the Martinet surface is given by

(2.13)

\Sigma=\Big\{x\in M\;\Big|\;\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x_{1}}(x)=0\Big\}.

Indeed, we choose
\zeta:=\mathrm{d}x_{3}-\psi\mathrm{d}x_{2}
, so that we have

(2.14)

(\zeta\wedge\mathrm{d}\zeta)_{x}=-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x_{1}}(x)\mathrm{d}x_{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}x_{2}\wedge\mathrm{d}x_{3},\quad\text{for all }x\in M.

Hence,
(\zeta\wedge\mathrm{d}\zeta)_{x}=0
if and only if
\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x_{1}}(x)=0
.

We conclude this section stating a criterion to decide whether a horizontal curve inside the Martinet surface is normal. To do so, we study geodesics in
\Omega
equipped with a specific Riemannian metric on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Namely, since the vector fields defined in (
2.2
) and the metric
g
do not depend on
x_{3}
, see (
2.3
) and (
2.5
), we can equip
\Omega
with the Riemannian metric
\bar{g}
defined by

(2.15)

\bar{g}(\partial_{x_{i}},\partial_{x_{j}}):=g(X_{i},X_{j}),\quad\text{for all }i,j\in\{1,2\}.

The choice of this metric makes the projection

\pi:M\to\Omega
,
\pi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}):=(x_{1},x_{2})
for all
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in M
, a submetry, see
[
4
]
. Normal curves inside the Martinet surface are related to geodesics for the metric
\bar{g}
by the following proposition.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\eta:I\to M
be an abnormal curve. Assume that, for all
t\in I
, we have
\psi(\eta(t))=0
and
\mathrm{d}\psi_{\eta(t)}=0
.
Then, the curve
\eta
is normal if and only if
\pi\circ\eta
is a geodesic for the Riemannian metric
\bar{g}
.

Proof.

Let
v_{1},v_{2},w_{1},w_{2}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
V,W\in\Gamma(\mathscr{D})
defined by

(2.16)

\displaystyle V:=v_{1}X_{1}+v_{2}X_{2},\quad W:=w_{1}X_{1}+w_{2}X_{2},

are an orthonormal frame for
g
.
We can write the normal Hamiltonian
H
as

(2.17)

H(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\langle\lambda,V\rangle^{2}+\langle\lambda,W\rangle^{2}\right),\quad\text{for all }\lambda\in T^{*}M,

see for example
[
9
]
.

Define

(2.18)

\tilde{H}(\lambda):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\langle\lambda,v_{1}\partial_{x_{1}}+v_{2}\partial_{x_{2}}\rangle^{2}+\langle\lambda,w_{1}\partial_{x_{1}}+w_{2}\partial_{x_{2}}\rangle^{2}\right),\quad\text{for all }\lambda\in T^{*}M,

Denote by
\Pi:T^{*}M\to M
the canonical projection.
We have

(2.19)

\displaystyle H(\lambda)-\tilde{H}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\lambda,v_{1}\partial_{x_{1}}+v_{2}\partial_{x_{2}}\rangle\langle\lambda,v_{2}\psi(\Pi(\lambda))\partial_{x_{3}}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\lambda,v_{2}\psi(\Pi(\lambda))\partial_{x_{3}}\rangle^{2}

(2.20)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\langle\lambda,w_{1}\partial_{x_{1}}+w_{2}\partial_{x_{2}}\rangle\langle\lambda,w_{2}\psi(\Pi(\lambda))\partial_{x_{3}}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\lambda,w_{2}\psi(\Pi(\lambda))\partial_{x_{3}}\rangle^{2},

Consequently, for all
\lambda\in T^{*}M
for which
\psi(\Pi(\lambda))=0
and
\mathrm{d}\psi_{\Pi(\lambda)}=0
, there holds

(2.21)

\mathrm{d}H_{\lambda}-\mathrm{d}\tilde{H}_{\lambda}=0.

In particular, for all
t\in I
and for all
\lambda\in T_{\eta(t)}^{*}M
, we have

(2.22)

\mathrm{d}H_{\lambda}=\mathrm{d}\tilde{H}_{\lambda}.

On the one hand, by Remark
2.1
and Equation (
2.22
), we have that the curve
\gamma
admits a lift to
T^{*}M
solving the Hamilton equations for
H
if and only if it admits a lift solving the Hamilton equations for
\tilde{H}
. On the other hand, it is a straightforward exercise to check that the latter condition is equivalent to
\pi\circ\gamma
being a geodesic for
\bar{g}
(we recall that
\pi\circ\gamma
is a geodesic if and only if it admits a lift to
T^{*}\Omega
solving the Hamilton equations for
T^{*}\Omega\ni\lambda\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\left(\langle\lambda,v_{1}\partial_{x_{1}}+v_{2}\partial_{x_{2}}\rangle^{2}+\langle\lambda,w_{1}\partial_{x_{1}}+w_{2}\partial_{x_{2}}\rangle^{2}\right)
).
∎

As a consequence of Proposition
2.4
, we prove the following result.

Corollary 2.5
.

For
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in C^{\infty}((-1,1),[0,1])
, let
(M,\mathscr{D},g)
be the sub-Riemannian manifold defined by (
1.2
), (
1.4
), and (
1.7
). Let
\gamma:(-1,1)\to M
be the curve defined by (
1.5
). Fix
t_{1},t_{2}\in(-1,1)
, with
t_{1}<t_{2}
. Then, the curve
\gamma|_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}
is normal if and only if
\alpha(t)=0
for all
t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
.

Proof of Theorem

2.5
.

Let
\Omega=(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})\times(-1,1)
, let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be as in (
1.3
), let
\gamma
be the curve defined in (
1.5
), and let
g
be the metric given by (
1.7
).

The curve
\gamma
satisfies
\psi(\gamma(t))=0
and
\mathrm{d}\psi_{\gamma(t)}=0
, for all
t\in(-1,1)
. Thus, by Proposition
2.4
, it is enough to check that
\bar{\gamma}:=\pi\circ\gamma|_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}
is a Riemannian geodesic for
\bar{g}
if and only if for all
t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
we have
\alpha(t)=0
.
Let us denote with
\Gamma
the Christoffel symbols for the Levi-Civita connection associated to the metric
\bar{g}
. The curve
\pi\circ\bar{\gamma}
is a geodesic if and only if

\ddot{\bar{\gamma}}^{k}(t)+\dot{\bar{\gamma}}^{i}(t)\dot{\bar{\gamma}}^{j}(t)\Gamma_{ij}^{k}(\bar{\gamma})=0,\quad\text{ for all }k\in\{1,2\},t\in[t_{1},t_{2}],

that is, if and only if

(2.23)

\Gamma_{22}^{k}(0,t)=0\quad\text{ for all }k\in\{1,2\},t\in[t_{1},t_{2}].

It is an exercise to check that
\Gamma_{22}^{1}(0,t)=0
for all
t\in(-1,1)
and that

\Gamma_{22}^{1}(0,t)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial g_{22}}{\partial x_{1}}(0,t)=-\alpha(t)t^{a},

thus the condition in (
2.23
) holds if and only
\alpha(t)=0
for all
t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.1
. We start with some preparatory considerations.

We fix
\Omega
to be the open set
\Omega:=(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})\times(-1,1)
, we choose
\psi(x):=x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{b}
, with
x\in M=\Omega\times\mathbb{R}
, so that the vector fields
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
have the form in (
1.3
). We consider the metric
g
as in (
1.7
), where
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in C^{\infty}((-1,1),[0,1])
. Let us define the smooth function
\phi:M\to\mathbb{R}
,

(3.1)

\phi(x):=1-\alpha(x_{2})x_{1}^{k}x_{2}^{a},

so that, for all
x\in M
, we have

(3.2)

g_{x}(X_{1},X_{1})=1,\quad g_{x}(X_{1},X_{2})=g_{x}(X_{2},X_{1})=0,\quad g_{x}(X_{2},X_{2})=\phi(x).

Let
\eta:[T_{1},T_{2}]\to M
be a horizontal curve. A horizontal curve
\omega:[0,\tau]\to M
is a
competing curve
for
\eta
if
\omega(0)=\eta(T_{1})
and
\omega(\tau)=\eta(T_{2})
. Notice that, if
u\in L^{2}([0,\tau],\mathbb{R}^{2})
is the control of
\omega
, then we have

(3.3)

\begin{cases}\dot{\omega}_{1}=u_{1},\\
\dot{\omega}_{2}=u_{2},\\
\dot{\omega}_{3}=u_{2}\psi(\omega).\end{cases}

Since
\psi(x)
does not depend on
x_{3}
, we can rewrite the condition of being a competing curve in terms of the first two coordinates. Indeed, let
\omega:[0,\tau]\to M
be an absolutely continuous curve with
\omega(0)=\eta(T_{1})
. Then
\omega
is a competing curve for
\eta
if and only if
\omega_{1}(\tau)=\eta_{1}(T_{2})
,
\omega_{2}(\tau)=\eta_{2}(T_{2})
, and

(3.4)

\int_{0}^{\tau}\dot{\omega}_{2}\psi(\omega)dt=\eta_{3}(T_{2})-\eta_{3}(T_{1}).

In order to prove Theorem
1.1
, we are going to consider either the case
\eta:=\gamma|_{[0,\varepsilon]}
or the case
\eta:=\gamma|_{[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]}
. In both cases, we have that
\eta_{3}
is identically
0
, and thus the condition in (
3.4
) rewrites

(3.5)

\int_{0}^{\tau}\dot{\omega}_{2}\psi(\omega)dt=0.

We recall that
L(\cdot)
denotes the sub-Riemannian length of a horizontal curve.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
consists in showing that all possible competing curves for
\bar{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}:=\gamma|_{[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]}
shorter than
\bar{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}
are reparameterizations of
\bar{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}
itself. The proof uses a technique that generalizes the one developed by Liu and Sussman in
[
7
, Section 7.1]
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Assume that
b
is even and
b\leq 2a
.
Fix
\varepsilon_{1}>0
such that

(3.6)

1-\frac{1}{3}t\leq\sqrt{1-t}\leq 1-\frac{t}{2},\quad\text{for all }t\in(-\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{1}),

and set

(3.7)

\varepsilon:=\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{16},\frac{1}{8}\left(2+b\right)^{-\frac{2}{b}},\frac{1}{65}\right\}

Let
\omega:[0,\tau]\to\Omega
be a competing curve for
\bar{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}:=\gamma|_{[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]}
and assume that

(3.8)

L(\omega)\leq L(\bar{\gamma}_{\varepsilon})=2\varepsilon.

The proof consists in estimating from above and from below the quantity

\displaystyle J=\int_{0}^{\tau}\psi(\omega(t))dt.

Combining the two estimates for
J
, we shall show that
\omega
is a reparametrization of
\gamma
.

Let
u=(u_{1},u_{2})
be the control of
\omega
. Since the length does not depend on the parameterization, we assume that
\omega
is parameterized by the arc-length, i.e., we have
\tau=L(\omega)
and

(3.9)

\displaystyle u_{1}^{2}(t)+\phi(\omega(t))u_{2}^{2}(t)=1,\quad\text{for all }t\in[0,\tau].

We notice that, if
L_{\mathrm{eu}}
denotes the euclidean length, then
\omega
satisfies
\frac{1}{2}L(\omega)\leq L_{\mathrm{eu}}(\omega)\leq 2L(\omega)
. This fact, together with (
3.8
), implies

(3.10)

\max_{t\in[0,\tau]}\{|\omega_{1}(t)|,|\omega_{2}(t)|\}\leq 8\varepsilon.

Let us define the quantity

(3.11)

\beta:=\max_{[0,\tau]}|\psi(\omega)|^{\frac{1}{2}}=\max_{[0,\tau]}|\omega_{1}\omega_{2}^{\frac{b}{2}}|,

On the one hand, we have

(3.12)

\begin{split}J&=\int_{0}^{\tau}(1-u_{2}(t)\phi(\omega(t))^{\frac{1}{2}})\psi(\omega(t))dt+\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{2}(t)\phi(\omega(t))^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi(\omega(t))dt\\
&\leq\beta^{2}\left(\tau-\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{2}(t)\phi(\omega(t))^{\frac{1}{2}}dt\right)+\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{2}(t)\phi(\omega(t))^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi(\omega(t))dt,\end{split}

where in the last inequality we used
that
1-\phi(\omega(t))u_{2}^{2}(t)\geq 0
, see (
3.9
). We need to estimate the two integral quantities appearing in the last line of the latter chain of inequalities.

The second integral can be studied in the following way. First, by Equations (
3.6
), (
3.7
) and (
3.10
), we have

(3.13)

1-\frac{1}{3}\alpha(\omega_{2}(t))\omega_{1}(t)\omega_{2}(t)^{a}\leq\phi(\omega(t))^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq 1-\frac{1}{2}\alpha(\omega_{2}(t))\omega_{1}(t)\omega_{2}(t)^{a}.

Then, using (
3.3
), (
3.5
) and (
3.13
), we deduce

(3.14)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{2}(t)\phi(\omega(t))^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi(\omega(t))dt&\leq-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{2}(t)\psi(\omega(t))\alpha(\omega_{2}(t))\omega_{1}(t)\omega_{2}(t)^{a}dt\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{\tau}|\psi(\omega(t))\omega_{1}(t)\omega_{2}(t)^{a}|dt,\end{split}

where in the last equality, we used (
3.9
) and the fact that
\frac{1}{2}\leq\phi(\omega(t))\leq\frac{3}{2}
to bound
|u_{2}|\leq 2
. By assumption we have
b\leq 2a
, thus we have
\omega_{2}^{a}\leq\omega_{2}^{\frac{b}{2}}
, and therefore

(3.15)

\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{2}(t)\phi(\omega(t))^{\frac{1}{2}}\psi(\omega(t))dt\leq\beta^{3}\tau.

Plugging the last inequality in (
3.12
), we deduce

(3.16)

J\leq\beta^{2}\left(\tau-\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{2}(t)\phi(\omega(t))^{\frac{1}{2}}dt\right)+\beta^{3}\tau.

We next estimate
\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{2}\phi(\omega)^{\frac{1}{2}}dt
. Since
\omega
is a competing curve for
\bar{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}
, we have
\omega_{2}(0)=-\varepsilon
and
\omega_{2}(\tau)=\varepsilon
, and thus, by (
3.3
), we get

(3.17)

\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{2}(t)dt=\int_{0}^{\tau}\dot{\omega}_{2}(t)dt=2\varepsilon.

Using the latter equality, we deduce

(3.18)

\begin{split}-\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{2}(t)\phi(\omega(t))^{\frac{1}{2}}dt&=-\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{2}(t)dt-\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{2}(t)\left(\phi(\omega(t))^{\frac{1}{2}}-1\right)dt\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:int_u2}}}{{=}}-2\varepsilon-\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{2}(t)\left(\phi(\omega(t))^{\frac{1}{2}}-1\right)dt\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:taylor_phi}}}{{\leq}}-2\varepsilon+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}|u_{2}(t)||\alpha(\omega_{2}(t))\omega_{1}(t)\omega_{2}(t)^{a}|dt\\
&\leq-2\varepsilon+\int_{0}^{\tau}|\omega_{1}(t)\omega_{2}(t)^{a}|dt\\
&\leq-2\varepsilon+\beta\tau,\end{split}

where, in the last two inequalities, we used the bounds
|u_{2}(t)|\leq 2
,
|\alpha(\omega_{2}(t))|\leq 1
, and the assumption
b\leq 2a
.

Hence, we can rewrite (
3.16
) as

(3.19)

J\leq\beta^{2}\left(\tau-2\varepsilon+\beta\tau\right)+\beta^{3}\tau=\beta^{2}(\tau-\varepsilon)+2\beta^{3}\tau.

We next pass to the estimate for
J
from below. We claim that

(3.20)

J=\int_{0}^{\tau}\psi(\omega(t))dt\geq\frac{\beta^{3}}{8}.

Indeed, set
P(t)=\omega_{1}(t)\omega_{2}(t)^{\frac{b}{2}}
.
By Equations
(
3.3
) and (
3.9
) and being
\frac{1}{2}\leq\phi\leq\frac{3}{2}
, we have
|\dot{\omega}_{1}|,|\dot{\omega}_{2}|\leq 2
. Therefore, the derivative
\dot{P}(t)=\dot{\omega}_{1}(t)\omega_{2}(t)^{\frac{b}{2}}+q\dot{\omega}_{2}(t)\omega_{1}(t)\omega_{2}(t)^{{\frac{b}{2}}-1}
satisfies

(3.21)

|\dot{P}(t)|\leq 2|\omega_{2}(t)|^{\frac{b}{2}}+b|\omega_{1}(t)||\omega_{2}(t)|^{{\frac{b}{2}}-1},\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in[0,\tau].

From (
3.10
) and (
3.21
), it follows that
|\dot{P}(t)|\leq(2+b)8^{q}\varepsilon^{q}
for a.e.
t\in[0,\tau]
. In particular, by (
3.7
), we have

(3.22)

|\dot{P}(t)|\leq 1,\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in[0,\tau].

Let
\bar{t}\in[0,\tau]
be such that
|P(\bar{t})|=\beta
and consider the interval

\displaystyle I:=\left[\bar{t}-\frac{\beta}{2},\bar{t}+\frac{\beta}{2}\right]\subset\mathbb{R}.

By
P(0)=P(\tau)=0
and (
3.22
), we have
I\subset[0,\tau]
and
|P(t)|\geq\beta/2
for all
t\in I
. Since
b
is even we have that
\psi\geq 0
, and thus we conclude

(3.23)

J\geq\int_{I}\psi(\omega(t))dt\geq\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}|I|=\frac{\beta^{3}}{8}.

The proof of claim (
3.20
) is now complete.

Combining the two estimates (
3.19
) and (
3.20
) for
J
, we get

(3.24)

\tau\geq 2\varepsilon+\beta\left(\frac{1}{8}-2\tau\right).

Since by (
3.7
) we have
4\varepsilon<\frac{1}{8}
, and
\tau=L(\omega)\leq L(\gamma)=2\varepsilon
we have that necessarily
\beta=0
. Hence, the competing curve
\omega
is a re-parameterization of
\gamma
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2

We next pass to the proof of Theorem
1.2
, which consists in showing that for all
\varepsilon>0
there exists a competing curve for
\gamma_{\varepsilon}:=\gamma|_{[0,\varepsilon]}
which is of shorter length. Such a competing curve is built by using a “cut and correction” method. Similar arguments to prove the non-minimality of specific horizontal curves were used in

.

The competing curve consists of the concatenation of the following three pieces:

i)

for
0<\rho,h<\varepsilon
, we “cut”
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
with the curve

(4.1)

\kappa:[0,2]\to\Omega,\quad\kappa(t):=\begin{cases}t(h,\rho),\quad&t\in[0,1],\\
((2-t)h,t\rho),\quad&t\in[1,2].\end{cases}

ii)

we continue from the point
(0,2\rho)
by following
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
until its end point, i.e., we run across the curve
\gamma|_{[2\rho,\varepsilon]}
;

iii)

finally, we add a small rectangle at the end-point and we run across its boundary. For a suitable
0<\delta<\varepsilon
, let
E_{\delta}:=(0,\varepsilon)+[0,\delta]\times[0,\varepsilon\delta]
and let
\sigma
be a clockwise parameterization of
\partial E_{\delta}
.

We denote with
\omega

\pi\circ\omega=\kappa*\gamma|_{[\rho,\varepsilon]}*\sigma
, where the operation “
*
” denotes the concatenation of plane curves. Notice that, by the choice of the metric
g
, the curve
\kappa
is indeed shorter than
\gamma|_{[0,2\rho]}
.
For some
\rho,h\in(0,\varepsilon)
and
\delta>0
, the curve
\omega
might not be a competing curve for
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
.
The proof of Theorem
1.2
, consists in showing that, for all
\varepsilon>0
, it is possible to choose
\rho,h,\delta
in a way that
\omega
is a competing curve for
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
and

.

Lemma 4.1
.

There exists
C,\xi>0
such that, for every
\varepsilon>0
, and for every
\rho,h\in(0,\min\{\xi,\varepsilon\})
, there exists a unique
\delta>0
such that the curve
\omega
is a competing curve for
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
. Moreover, for such
\delta
, there holds

(4.2)

\delta^{3}\leq\frac{C}{\varepsilon^{b+1}}h^{2}\rho^{b+1}.

Proof.

We have to compute and compare the effects on the third coordinate made by the cut and by the rectangle.

For the cut we have

(4.3)

\begin{split}\Delta^{\mathrm{cut}}_{3}(\rho)&:=\int_{0}^{2}\kappa_{1}(t)^{2}\kappa_{2}(t)^{b}\dot{\kappa}_{2}(t)dt\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}(th)^{2}(t\rho)^{b}\rho dt+\int_{1}^{2}[h(2-t)]^{2}(t\rho)^{b}\rho dt\\
&=C_{1}h^{2}\rho^{b+1},\end{split}

where

(4.4)

C_{1}:=\int_{0}^{1}t^{2+b}\mathrm{d}t+\int_{1}^{2}(2-t)^{2}t^{b}\mathrm{d}t.

For the rectangle we use the Stokes theorem. We compute

(4.5)

\displaystyle\Delta_{3}^{\mathrm{rect}}(\delta)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\sigma}x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{b}\mathrm{d}x_{2}

(4.6)

\displaystyle=-\iint_{E_{\delta}}2x_{1}x_{2}^{b}dx_{1}dx_{2}

(4.7)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon(1+\delta)}\left(\int_{0}^{\delta}2x_{1}x_{2}^{b}dx_{1}\right)dx_{2}\qquad(x_{2}=\varepsilon y,\;dx_{2}=\varepsilon dy)

(4.8)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\varepsilon^{b+1}}{b+1}\delta^{2}((1+\delta)^{b+1}-1)

(4.9)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\varepsilon^{b+1}}{b+1}\delta^{3}f(\delta),

where
f(\delta):=\frac{(1+\delta)^{b+1}-1}{\delta}
. The function
f
is continuous, strictly increasing on
(0,+\infty)
, and
\lim_{\delta\to 0}f(\delta)=1
, thus there exists
\delta_{1}>0
such that, for all
\delta\in(0,\delta_{1})
, there holds

(4.10)

0\leq f(\delta)-1\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Moreover, letting
\xi_{1}:=f(\delta_{1})\delta_{1}^{3}>0
, we have that
f(\delta)\delta^{3}\leq\xi_{1}
if and only if
\delta\in(0,\delta_{1}]
. Finally, we can choose
\xi>0
such that
\frac{(b+1)C_{1}}{\varepsilon^{b+1}}h^{2}\rho^{b+1}\leq\xi_{1}
for every
h,\rho\leq\xi
.

The curve
\omega
is a competing curve for
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
if and only if the third coordinate of
\omega
at the final point vanishes, that is, if and only if

(4.11)

\Delta^{\mathrm{cut}}_{3}(\rho)+\Delta_{3}^{\mathrm{rect}}(\delta)=0.

By (
4.3
) and (
4.9
) the latter condition rewrites

(4.12)

\frac{C_{1}(b+1)}{\varepsilon^{b+1}}h^{2}\rho^{b+1}=\delta^{3}f(\delta).

Since the right-hand side is monotone in
\delta
, it vanishes at
\delta=0
and tends to infinity as
\delta\to\infty
, there exists a unique
\delta
for which

4.12
) holds. Moreover, by (
4.10
), (
4.12
), and by the choiche of

, we get that for such
\delta
the Inequality (

) holds with

.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
C,\xi>0
be the constants for which Lemma
4.1
holds. Without loss of generality, we may assume
\xi<1
. Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
{c}>0
such that
4a+4+4{c}\leq b
(e.g.,
c=\frac{1}{8}
). Moreover, fix
\rho\in(0,\min\{\xi,\varepsilon\})
and
h:=\rho^{a+2+{c}}
, with
\rho
satisfying

(4.13)

\rho^{{c}}<\min\left\{\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{32}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{b+1}}{C}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\right\}.

By Lemma
4.1
there exists
\delta>0
satisfying (
4.2
) such that
\omega
is a competing curve for
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
. We claim that
\omega
is shorter than
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
, i.e., we claim that

(4.14)

L(\kappa)+L(\gamma|_{[2\rho,\varepsilon]})+L(\sigma)<\varepsilon.

We start by estimating the length of
\kappa
:

(4.15)

\begin{split}L(\kappa)&=\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{h^{2}+(1-(th)(t\rho)^{a})\rho^{2}}dt+\int_{1}^{2}\sqrt{h^{2}+(1-[(2-t)h](t\rho)^{a})\rho^{2}}dt\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{h^{2}+\rho^{2}}dt+\int_{1}^{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{h^{2}+\left(1-\frac{3}{4}h\rho^{a}\right)\rho^{2}}dt+\int_{\frac{3}{2}}^{2}\sqrt{h^{2}+\rho^{2}}dt\\
&=\rho\left(\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{1+\left(h\rho^{-1}\right)^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1+\left(h\rho^{-1}\right)^{2}-\frac{3}{4}h\rho^{a}}\right)\\
&\leq\rho\Big(2+\frac{1}{4}\left(\left(h\rho^{-1}\right)^{2}-\frac{3}{4}h\rho^{a}\right)\Big),\end{split}

where in the last inequality we used that
\sqrt{1+|x|}\leq 1+\frac{|x|}{2}
, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. By (
4.13
) and
h=\rho^{a+2+{c}}
we get
(h\rho^{-1})^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}h\rho^{a}
, and thus we deduce

(4.16)

L(\kappa)\leq 2\rho-\frac{1}{8}h\rho^{a+1}.

The other two lengths in (
4.14
) are easier to estimate. Since
\dot{\gamma}(t)=(0,1)
, we have

(4.17)

L(\gamma|_{[2\rho,\varepsilon]})=\varepsilon-2\rho,

while the length of the rectangle
\sigma
is estimated by

(4.18)

L(\sigma)\leq 4\delta.

Combining Equations (
4.16
), (
4.17
), and (
4.18
), we get

(4.19)

\displaystyle L(\kappa)+L(\gamma|_{[2\rho,\varepsilon]})+L(\sigma)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon+4\delta-\frac{1}{8}h\rho^{a+1}

(4.20)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:correction}}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\varepsilon+4\left(\frac{C}{\varepsilon^{b+1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}h^{\frac{2}{3}}\rho^{\frac{b+1}{3}}-\frac{1}{8}h\rho^{a+1}.

Since
h=\rho^{a+2+{c}}
, and since
b\geq 4a+4+4{c}
, we get

(4.21)

\displaystyle L(\kappa)+L(\gamma|_{[2\rho,\varepsilon]})+L(\sigma)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon+4\left(\frac{C}{\varepsilon^{b+1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\rho^{\frac{2a+4+2{c}}{3}}\rho^{\frac{4a+5+4{c}}{3}}-\frac{1}{8}\rho^{2a+3+{c}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon+\rho^{2a+3+{c}}\left(4\left(\frac{C}{\varepsilon^{b+1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\rho^{c}-\frac{1}{8}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:rho-small}}}{{<}}\varepsilon.

), and then of Theorem

, is concluded.
