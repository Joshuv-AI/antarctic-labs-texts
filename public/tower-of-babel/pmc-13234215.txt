Soft Tissue Coverage Options Following Total Hip Arthroplasty: What to Do When a Wound Breaks Down
Authors: Katharine D. Harper, Eric Maiorino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wound complications following total hip arthroplasty are a challenging issue facing surgeons, with rates reported anywhere from 1 to 12% of patients. There currently exists a paucity of literature detailing the options for surgeons who encounter soft tissue defects or closure difficulties following total hip arthroplasty. With the increasing popularity of using the anterior and lateral approaches for more complex arthroplasty revisions and reconstructions, detailing options for soft tissue coverage options may prove valuable. This article will provide an overview of soft tissue coverage options for the anterior, posterior, and alternate approaches, and when a plastic surgeon should be consulted for assistance.

Introduction

Wound complications following total hip arthroplasty (THA) are a challenging issue facing surgeons, with the Hip Society deeming it 1 of their 19 standardized complications affecting patient outcomes [1]. Rates of wound complications are reported anywhere from 1 to 12% of patients, with variations reported according to approach [2,3]. There currently exists a paucity of literature detailing the options for surgeons who encounter soft tissue defects or closure difficulties following THA. While literature is rich with flap and soft tissue coverage options following total knee arthroplasty, hip arthroplasty has largely been neglected. With the increasing popularity of using alternative approaches for more complex arthroplasty revisions and reconstructions (with reported higher risks of wound complications compared to the posterolateral approach), the prevalence of soft tissue complications may increase in coming years when addressing these complex patients [3]. This article will provide an overview of soft tissue coverage options for the anterior, posterior, and alternate approaches, and when a plastic surgeon should be consulted for assistance.

Risk factors and soft tissue management

Incision breakdown following THA can be a complex issue to manage in areas with impaired soft tissue mobility. The best way to manage a potential complex wound issue is by identifying risk factors and modifying them accordingly. Risk factors for wound breakdown include obesity, diabetes, tobacco use, chronic steroid use, preoperative transfusion, nutritional status, advanced age, surgical approach, and closure type [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. A meta-analysis by Yang et al. found that patients who were obese (defined as a body mass index >35) were statistically more likely to have postoperative bleeding, postoperative wound infection, and severe surgical site infection (requiring operative intervention) [4]. A study by Han et al. found that patients who underwent surgery with an elevated hemoglobin A1c of >8.0% had an increased risk of wound complications following THA (odds ratio [OR]: 6.07) [5]. A systematic review evaluating tobacco use in total joint arthroplasty demonstrated a significantly higher rate of both wound complications (OR: 1.78) and periprosthetic joint infection (OR: 2.02) over nonusers [6]. A study evaluating the National Surgical Quality Improvement Program database demonstrated that both preoperative blood transfusion and chronic steroid use were independent risk factors for both wound dehiscence and surgical site infections [2]. Preoperative albumin levels of <3.5 mg/dL have been shown to not only increase wound complications but also results in poorer outcomes and longer hospital stays than those with levels >3.5 mg/dL at the time of surgery [7]. This result was worsened in patients aged 75 and older [7]. Finally, multiple studies have looked at whether surgical approach contributes to wound complications. Several of those studies have shown that patients undergoing an anterior approach are at increased risk of wound complications when compared to their posterior and/or lateral counterparts [2,3,8]. A study by Pincus et al. showed the anterior approach had a statistically significant increase in wound complications (hazard ratio: 2.07) when compared to posterior and lateral approaches [8], while a more recent study by Carlock et al. showed no difference between direct anterior and direct lateral approaches [3].

Modifiable risk factors should be managed appropriately in the perioperative period to mitigate risk of wound complications. However, operating on the “perfect” patient is not always possible, and several techniques are available to mitigate nonmodifiable risks. The “Goldilocks principle” of wound closure should be employed when closing all wounds with tight approximation of the skin without tissue strangulation [9,10]. The wounds should be closed in layers to alleviate tension across the incision. To achieve this goal, the use of barbed sutures, braided sutures, and/or staples has shown relative equivalence across several studies. A systematic literature review performed by Snyder et al. found that a multilayer barbed suture with topical skin adhesive/mesh or multilayer antimicrobial sutures showed the narrowest range of adverse events when compared to more traditional closing methods [11].

Once the wound is closed, a barrier to protect the wound is traditionally applied, but has changed over the years with various products on the market. While barrier dressings have been shown to decrease the rate of infection in TKA and THA patients [12], the use of negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) to closed surgical incisions has shown to significantly decrease the rate of wound complications by increasing localized blood flow, increasing angiogenesis, and enhancing fibroblast migration into the wound bed. The costs associated with its use are greater than traditional barrier dressings [13]. A systematic review on the use of NPWT in THA, however, showed a significant reduction in surgical site infections, seroma, dehiscence, and prolonged incisional drainage. It also demonstrated that the risk of reoperation was reduced as were the costs of care associated with the use of NPWT, justifying the additional costs [14]. These results were amplified in those considered “high-risk” (body mass index >35, diabetes, immunosuppressed) [15].

Despite employing best practices, wound complications and dehiscence still occur, and prompt management of the wound is required to avoid implant complications and joint space infections. Studies have shown that infection after prolonged wound drainage (>5 days) is reported to be anywhere from 1.5 to 50%, with each additional day of drainage increasing your risk of periprosthetic joint infection in THA by 29% [16]. There is a lack of reported incidence of wounds requiring flap coverage after THA in the literature, and while rare, the repercussions of such wounds can be devastating. The management of these wounds will be dependent on the defect size, depth, location, and volume of drainage. The use of a “reconstructive ladder” is often employed when approaching wounds, which utilizes increasingly complex soft tissue repair techniques to achieve definitive and stable wound closure, ranging from closure by secondary intention to vascularized free tissue transfers (Fig. 1) [9,10]. The first rung of the ladder employs healing by secondary intention, often with local wound care to speed granulation tissue formation and assist with maintaining a clean and moist environment. The next rungs of the ladder involves primary closure (immediate vs delayed) or delayed secondary closure after adequate debridement of the wound [9,10]. This can be considered provided there is sufficient tissue laxity to achieve a tension-free closure. The next rung of the ladder involves skin grafting, which would rarely be used in these scenarios due to lack of tissue bulk provided, making early mobilization difficult with a high likelihood of recurrent breakdown [9,10]. When local wound care options fail and soft tissue defects place the joint at risk, the next stages of the ladder move into local tissue transfer or more advanced flap coverage for closure of defects.Figure 1The “reconstructive ladder” of progressively more complex closure options to be used for soft tissue defects.

While more advanced techniques on the ladder can require the assistance of a microsurgeon, some options listed below can be performed by an orthopaedic surgeon at the time of closure, and techniques will be labeled and detailed accordingly.

Multiple approaches

Local flap options

Rhomboid

A rhomboid flap is a versatile, local tissue flap that can be utilized for posterior, lateral, or anterior approach defects. The flap has a random pattern blood supply that is based off the subpapillary and subdermal plexus of the tissue base, with larger flaps being based off perforator vessels. The flap is parallelogram with 2 flap angles of 120o and 2 of 60o. The defect is placed in the center of a rhombus with the noted flap angles above with the adjacent flap (Fig. 2a). This flap can be utilized in any area of skin provided there is enough soft tissue laxity to close the wound donor site (Fig. 2b and c). Regional flap options

Tensor fasciae latae flap

The tensor fasciae latae flap is an excellent alternative to an anterolateral thigh (ALT) flap, with less technically challenging surgical dissection. This pedicled regional flap is ideal for reconstruction of defects from the anterior approach. The flap incision can be incorporated into the distal extension of both the posterior or direct anterior approach used during revisions, minimizing morbidity to the patient [17]. Designing the tensor fasciae latae flap is done similarly to the vastus lateralis and ALT flaps (Fig. 4). The anterior margin of the flap is marked from the anterior superior iliac spine to the lateral patella, while the posterior margin is the vertical axis of the femur and the skin paddle designed in an elliptical shape to facilitate primary closure of the donor site (Fig. 5). The flap is then elevated in a distal to proximal manner, deep to the iliotibial fascia until the descending branch of the lateral circumflex femoral vessels are encountered. These vessels can then be traced in the rectus femoris and vastus lateralis interval. The flaps ability to be incorporated into both the existing posterior or anterior approach through distal extension improves cosmesis and minimizes donor morbidity (Figs. 6 and 7). Vastus lateralis flap—requires plastic and reconstructive surgery

A vastus lateralis flap allows for coverage of large defects and can be utilized for posterior, lateral (if defect not overlying the flap), or anterior approaches. To elevate the vastus lateralis muscle flap, an incision is made in the midline third of the thigh in the interval between the rectus femoris and vastus lateralis as this interval is usually palpable in most patients. The dissection is carried down through the anterior compartment fascia to reveal the vastus lateralis muscle. A total or partial muscle harvest can be performed based on the blood supply from the lateral circumflex femoral artery and vein with small branches to the surrounding muscle ligated to allow additional mobility in the vascular pedicle (Fig. 8). This flap has been utilized multiple times in arthroplasty literature case reports for salvage procedures, with excellent healing potential [18]. A major pitfall to be aware of is hematoma formation, which has been reported in nearly 13% of cases [19], and can be minimized through meticulous dissection and identification of muscle perforators.Figure 8Cadaveric pictures demonstrating the anterior landmark and vascular supply of the vastus lateralis. The descending branch of the lateral circumflex femoral artery (LCFA) leads into the muscle branch of the LCFA, which provides the flap with its vasculature (far right). If utilizing this flap, care must be taken to maintain this blood supply during rotation of the flap into position. Depending on the anatomical variation of the supply, this may limit the extent the flap can be used for proximal, anterior defects.

Anterolateral thigh flap—requires plastic and reconstructive surgery

An ALT flap is a regional perforator flap which can be used for large defects within the anterior or lateral approaches, and more distal defects for the posterior approach. To design the donor site on the ALT, a line from the anterior superior iliac spine to the lateral patella is drawn. Using a Doppler, perforators are typically identified in the midline third of the thigh along the marked line (1-2 perforators is usually adequate). The anterior margin of the flap is then marked 2 cm medially from the center portion of the perforator cluster and is marked in an elliptical manner for primary closure of the donor site. The flap is elevated from medial to lateral deep to the anterior compartment fascia taking care to identify and preserve the perforating vessels. The perforators can be in a septocutaneous position in the interval between the rectus femoris and vastus lateralis muscles or in a musculocutaneous position through the vastus lateralis muscle. The perforators are then followed down to the lateral circumflex femoral vessels as done for the vastus lateralis flap (Fig. 9). Direct anterior approach

Regional flap options

Superficial circumflex iliac artery perforator flap—requires PRS

This is a groin flap transversely based on the inguinal ligament and may have limited use in the reconstruction of anterior or lateral hip wounds as a pedicled flap; however, it offers utility as a free tissue flap given its thinner tissue and wider base of tissue that can be harvested while still closing the donor site primarily. Not routinely used for this application.

Anteromedial thigh flap—requires PRS

Considered a second-line option to the ALT flap, this regional fasciocutaneous flap utilizes tissue from the medial thigh to cover larger anterior thigh defects. The vascular pedicle to this flap is often variable and may be nonexistent in >20% of patients making the use of this flap less desirable [20].

Pedicled deep inferior epigastric flap—requires PRS

This is considered a workhorse flap for large-volume tissue reconstruction during its primary use in breast reconstruction. Its use as a pedicled flap has also been well described around the abdomen and proximal lower extremity. Typically, the ipsilateral hemiabdomen is used in a pedicled form, however, the entirely of the infraumbilical abdomen can be used if microsurgical techniques are employed to revascularize the contralateral flap expanding the region of potential coverage to the posterior hip. This flap is constrained by the availability of excess lower abdominal skin and tissue and can be too bulky in some patients requiring secondary thinning procedures [21].

Posterolateral approach

Local flap options

Gluteus maximus perforator flap

Since the gluteus maximus is a Mathes-Nahai type 3 muscle, its perforator distribution allows for orientation of the donor site based on the superior or inferior gluteal arteries with limited donor site morbidity and can even extend onto the posterior thigh depending on the required tissue needed [22]. A vascular pedicle of 7-10 cm allows for significant mobility of the fasciocutaneous tissue to cover broader defects and potentially the lateral hip area [21]. Regional flap options

Posterior gluteal-thigh flap

Often used in reconstruction of ischial, sacral, or perineal wounds, the posterior gluteal-thigh flap often has enough length to reach the lateral hip area comfortably, as tissue can be taken up to a few centimeters before the popliteal fossa [23]. As a fasciocutaneous flap using the same blood supply detailed above for the gluteus maximus flap, there is no muscle deficit, and the donor site can often be closed primarily (Fig. 11). Postoperative wound care

A NPWT device is recommended after all complex closures when able to assist with perfusion of the transferred tissue. It is also recommended to avoid pressure loading of the transferred tissue for the first 3 weeks while vessel remodeling and vascular hypertrophy occurs to ensure adequate wound healing. Beyond that, there are no additional wound care requirements outside of normal standard incision care. More advanced flap options performed by PRS may require activity restrictions to better assist healing but would be recommended on a case-by-case basis based on defect size and patient factors.

Conclusions

Soft tissue complications and wound defects around the hip following THA can lead to severe morbidity for patients. Early identification of wound complications and prompt management by employing local flap options detailed here for coverage can allow for salvage of the joint and improved outcomes. When more complex coverage procedures are required, it is important to consult a plastic and reconstructive surgeon promptly to avoid implant contamination.
