Tongue function and high-risk sleep-disordered breathing in youth: age-stratified associations and interactions in a Vietnamese cross-sectional study
Authors: Dao Anh Hoang, Tam Minh Nguyen, Dan Nguyen Ngoc Tam, Triin Jagomägi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo examine associations between tongue function domains and questionnaire-based high-risk sleep-disordered breathing classification across age groups, and to explore interaction effects between functional and anatomical factors.MethodsA cross-sectional study included 1,244 participants (462 children aged 6–8 years and 782 young adults aged 18–25 years). High-risk classification was defined as meeting ≥ 1 age-appropriate threshold: Pediatric Sleep Questionnaire (PSQ ≥ 8), Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS > 10), or Snoring Severity Scale (SSS ≥ 7) for children; Berlin Questionnaire (≥ 2 positive categories), ESS > 10, or SSS ≥ 7 for young adults. Tongue function included mobility, strength, posture, and orofacial domains. Multivariable logistic regression with backward stepwise selection was used in pooled and age-stratified models. Pre-specified interaction terms were systematically tested to explore effect modification between functional and anatomical factors.ResultsThe prevalance of high-risk classification was 26.8% in children, 11.9% in young adults, and 17.5% overall. In children, low tongue resting posture (AOR = 1.69) and frequent nocturnal open-mouth posture (AOR = 2.69) were independently associated with high-risk classification. In young adults, better orofacial function (AOR = 0.79), greater posterior mobility (AOR = 0.96), and higher posterior strength (AOR = 0.98) were protective. Posterior mobility and age group moderated the association between tonsillar hypertrophy and high-risk classification (AOR = 0.53 and AOR = 2.06, respectively), though not in age-stratified models. In children, combined tonsillar hypertrophy and restricted posterior mobility were associated with substantially higher prevalence (34.5% vs. 13.8%; dose-response p=.006).ConclusionsTongue function showed age-group-specific associations with questionnaire-based high-risk classification. Incorporating both anatomical and functional assessments within age-specific frameworks may improve early identification and inform future research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11325-026-03666-3.

Introduction

Sleep-disordered breathing (SDB), which ranges from primary snoring to obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), affects 10–17% of children and 9–38% of adults [1–3]. When untreated, OSA is associated with cardiovascular disease, diabetes, stroke, and overall reduced quality of life in adults; whereas pediatric OSA contributes to neurocognitive, behavioral, and cardiovascular consequences [2, 3]. No standardized preventive treatment has been established [1]. Evidence highlights the importance of early recognition and timely intervention to prevent long-term adverse health outcomes and improve overall well-being [4]. Although polysomnography is the diagnostic gold standard, its use is limited by cost, technical complexity, and accessibility [3]. Current recommendations emphasize combining clinical examination with validated screening questionnaires for baseline assessment and initial identification of high-risk individuals [1, 5].

The tongue, a muscular hydrostat and primary upper-airway dilator, contributes to upper airway patency through tonic and phasic genioglossus activity [6]. The genioglossus muscle is the principal protruder of the tongue; when it contracts, it counterbalances collapsing pressures and stabilizes the upper airway [6]. The tongue base, located at the interface between the upper pharynx and tracheobronchial airway, represents a major site of obstruction in OSA pathogenesis [2, 7]. Beyond structural considerations, recent studies have increasingly emphasized functional deficits of the tongue. Guilleminault et al. documented sensorimotor impairments (e.g., inability to perform specific movements or identify shapes) in 18% of children and 11.3% of adults with OSA. These sensorimotor deficits reduce upper-airway muscle tone and coordination, suggesting that neuromuscular dysfunction contributes independently to airway compromise [8, 9]. Research has also described pathological tongue and genioglossus function, which are clinically associated with oral respiration, adenoid faces, and a constricted maxillary dental arch [10, 11].

Throughout childhood, the upper airway undergoes structural growth, accompanied by functional adaptations in neuromuscular tone and ventilatory drive [4, 12]. Consequently, the maturation of tongue function – from sucking and early swallowing patterns in infancy to mature oropharyngeal coordination in adulthood – suggests its relationship with SDB may vary across developmental stages [4]. Eckert et al. identified several phenotypic causes of OSA and highlighted heterogeneity in non-anatomical factors, or ‘phenotypes’, contributing to the disorder. Adults typically exhibit obesity, anatomical impairment, and reduced upper-airway dilator muscle function, whereas pediatric OSA is more commonly associated with tonsillar hypertrophy and behavioral patterns rather than neuromuscular deficits [5, 13, 14]. Previous studies have often examined isolated mechanisms rather than evaluating how the interplay between anatomical airway narrowing and neuromuscular compensation determines disease manifestation [15, 16]. Tongue function encompasses multiple dimensions —including range of motion, neuromuscular coordination, posture, and integration with orofacial structures [4]. Further research is required to evaluate tongue function within a broader physiological context, providing deeper insights into SDB mechanisms and informing more targeted therapeutic approaches. Although orofacial function and SDB associations have been extensively studied in Western populations, data from Asian populations remain limited. Sleep-disordered breathing and orofacial dysfunction—particularly tongue function —remain understudied in Vietnam, where both clinical awareness and research capacity are still developing [17, 18].

Given these considerations, the primary aim of this study was to examine associations between tongue function domains — including mobility, strength, posture, and overall orofacial function — and questionnaire-based high-risk classification in children and young adults. We hypothesized that the nature of these associations would differ across developmentally distinct age groups. As a secondary aim, interaction analyses were conducted to explore potential effect modification between functional and anatomical factors within pooled and age-stratified models.

Methodology

This cross-sectional study followed the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for observational studies. The study protocol adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hue University of Medicine and Pharmacy (Approval No. H2018/16). Written informed consent was obtained from young adults and from parents or legal guardians of children, and oral assent was obtained from all children.

Study population and eligibility criteria

Healthy volunteers from two distinct age groups were recruited in Thua Thien Hue province, Vietnam. Children aged 6–8 years were recruited from February to May 2019 using multistage stratified random sampling across one urban and one randomly selected rural district. Young adults aged 18–25 years were recruited from February to July 2022 at a local university. All eligible students were invited to participate, and those who provided informed consent and completed all assessments were included.

Participants were healthy individuals without acute illness who completed all assessments. Exclusion criteria included diagnosed sleep disorders, craniofacial anomalies, neurological conditions affecting tongue function, prior adenotonsillectomy, current orthodontic treatment, and recent upper respiratory infection.

These two age ranges were selected to represent phenotypically distinct developmental populations rather than a continuous age spectrum: children aged 6–8 years correspond to the period of peak adenotonsillar volume and the anatomical-dominant SDB phenotype [5, 13, 19], while young adults aged 18–25 years represent the post-pubertal, neuromuscular-dominant phenotype [13, 14]. This contrast design is consistent with the phenotypic framework described by Eckert et al. [13, 14] and the pediatric SDB roadmap of Yoon et al. [4]. Analyses were therefore designed as age-stratified comparisons rather than as a continuous developmental trajectory model.

Sample size determination

Sample size calculations were performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7, Germany). Based on a previous study examining orofacial dysfunction and SDB [20], we anticipated a moderate effect size (odds ratio 1.5–2.0) for the association between tongue function impairment and SDB risk. Using a significance level (α) of 0.05, statistical power (1-β) of 0.80, and an estimated 17% prevalence of high-risk classification in Vietnamese adults [21], the minimum required sample size was estimated at 390 participants to detect an OR of 2.0. We recruited 1,244 participants (462 children and 782 adults). Post-hoc power analysis confirmed > 90% power to detect moderate effect sizes (OR ≥ 1.5) in both age strata.

Data collection and measurements

After institutional approval, questionnaires were distributed to parents and participants. Two trained dentists conducted clinical examinations with divided responsibilities: Examiner 1 assessed demographics and tongue function, and Examiner 2 performed the orofacial myofunctional evaluation. All examiners completed standardized training prior to data collection. Intra-examiner reliability for tongue measurements yielded ICCs of 0.87–0.94 (30 participants; one-week interval). Examiners were blinded to participants’ SDB status. Test–retest reliability for the screening questionnaires was assessed one month apart and demonstrated adequate internal consistency.

Tongue function assessment

Tongue mobility was assessed using tongue-tip-to-incisive-papilla (TIP) and lingual-palatal suction (LPS) movements. Interincisal distances were measured during maximum mouth opening (MO), TIP, and LPS according to established protocols by Yoon and Zaghi [22, 23]. The Tongue Range Motion Ratio (TRMR) was calculated as TRMR-TIP = (TIP/MO) × 100 and TRMR-LPS = (LPS/MO) × 100 for anterior and posterior mobility, respectively. TRMRs were graded, with restricted mobility defined as Grade 3 or higher (TRMR-TIP < 50% or TRMR-LPS < 30%).

Tongue strength was assessed using the Iowa Oral Performance Instrument (IOPI; IOPI Medical LLC, USA) as described by Potter and Short [24]. Participants performed three maximal tongue elevation presses at two positions: anterior (tongue tip elevation with bulb behind the alveolar ridge) and posterior (posterior two-thirds elevation with bulb at the sulcus terminalis midline). Maximum pressure in kilopascals (kPa) was recorded for each position.

Orofacial myofunction and oral posture assessment

Orofacial assessment included direct clinical examination and oral-posture questionnaires. Palatine tonsil size was graded using the Brodsky scale (0–4) based on oropharyngeal airway obstruction, with grades 3–4 indicating tonsillar hypertrophy (> 50% obstruction) [1]. The Orofacial Myofunctional Evaluation with Scores (OMES) protocol by Felício et al. assessed three domains: posture (lips, tongue, and facial symmetry at rest), mobility (lip, tongue, and mandible movements), and functions (breathing, swallowing, and mastication). Total scores ranged from 32 to 104, with higher scores indicating better orofacial myofunction [25]. Oral-posture questionnaires, distributed alongside the SDB screening questionnaires, included parent or participant reports of habitual open-mouth posture and tongue resting position over the preceding two weeks. Frequent open-mouth posture during sleep was defined as occurring ≥ 3 nights per week. Low tongue resting posture was defined as the tongue resting in an interdental or floor-of-mouth position.

Sleep-disordered breathing screening questionnaires

Participants were screened for SDB symptoms using validated questionnaires completed by parents or caregivers for children or participants themselves for young adults. Participants were classified into high-risk or low-risk groups based on established questionnaire thresholds. High-risk classification required meeting at least one of the following age-appropriate criteria: for children, Pediatric Sleep Questionnaire PSQ ≥ 8, Modified Pediatric Epworth Sleepiness Scale ESS-CHAD > 10, or Snoring Severity Scale SSS ≥ 7; for adults, Berlin Questionnaire BQ ≥ 2 positive categories, ESS > 10, or SSS ≥ 7. We used a ≥ 1-threshold composite definition to enhance sensitivity for population-level screening, where questionnaire tools are intended to flag individuals who may benefit from further sleep evaluation rather than to establish a diagnosis [1, 3].

Pediatric Sleep Questionnaire

Parents/caregivers of children completed the 22-item PSQ, including snoring frequency, observed apneas, breathing difficulty during sleep, daytime sleepiness, and inattentive/hyperactive behavior. Responses are scored as “yes” =1, “no” =0. A total of eight or more positive responses (cut-off value of 0.33) is classified as high risk for SDB [1].

Berlin Questionnaire

Adult participants completed a validated three-category instrument assessing: Category 1 (snoring and witnessed apneas), Category 2 (daytime sleepiness), and Category 3 (hypertension/obesity). Scoring positive in ≥ 2 categories classifies participants as high-risk [1].

Epworth Sleepiness Scale

Participants completed age-appropriate versions (ESS-CHAD for children aged 6–16; standard ESS for young adults). ESS evaluates propensity to doze in eight situations on a 0–3 scale. Total scores > 10 indicate excessive daytime sleepiness and classify individuals as high-risk for SDB [1]. Snoring Severity Scale: Parents/caregivers (children) or participants (adults) reported snoring loudness, frequency, and periodicity. An SSS total score ≥ 7 is considered high-risk for SDB [26].

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0, with significance at p < .05. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± SD or median (IQR); categorical variables as frequencies and percentages.

Univariable logistic regression was used to examine associations between tongue function variables and high-risk SDB. Three separate multivariable models were developed (overall sample, children only, adults only) using the backward stepwise likelihood ratio method (entry p < .05, removal p > .10).

Interaction terms were systematically tested in separate models, including both main effects. Because of the exploratory nature of interaction testing, we used an uncorrected significance threshold of p < .05 for interaction terms, with p < .10 considered suggestive of potential effect modification. Significant interactions (p < .05) were incorporated into the final multivariable models. Significant interactions in final models prompted stratified analyses to clarify effect modification across subgroups. Model quality was assessed via the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and Nagelkerke R². The Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate and Bonferroni correction were applied to control for multiple comparisons. No missing data were present.

Results

Results are presented in three stages: (1) age-group-specific associations between tongue function domains and high-risk classification (Tables 1 and 2); (2) systematic interaction testing and final multivariable models incorporating significant interaction terms (Tables 3 and 4); and (3) stratified analyses and risk profiling to characterise the significant interactions identified (Tables 5 and 6; Fig. 1).

Table 1 shows significant age-group-specific differences across tongue-function traits and age-group-specific risk characteristics among 1,244 participants (all p < .001). Children demonstrated lower objective tongue function, higher frequencies of oral-posture disorders, and greater prevalence of tonsillar hypertrophy. High-risk classification was observed in 26.8% of children, 12.0% of young adults, and 17.5% of the total sample.

Table 2 presents univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses examining factors associated with high-risk classification. The association with age reversed direction between univariable and multivariable analyses (OR = 0.37 vs. OR = 2.23, respectively). Diagnostic analyses confirmed model stability with adequate fit (Hosmer-Lemeshow test, χ² = 8.7, p = .34), and acceptable multicollinearity (variance inflation factors remained < 3.5 for all factors).

Binary logistic regression was conducted with high-risk classification as the dependent variable (0 = no, 1 = yes). Variables with p < .10 in univariable analyses were entered into multivariable models. Backward stepwise regression using the likelihood ratio method (entry criterion p < .05, removal criterion p > .10) identified the final multivariable models.

Interaction terms were tested to explore effect modification among factors associated with high-risk classification in the multivariable logistic regression models (Table 3). Eighteen interaction terms were derived from main effects retained in multivariable models shown in Table 2. Three interaction terms reached statistical significance, including tonsillar hypertrophy × age group in the overall sample (OR = 1.86, p=.022), and tonsillar hypertrophy × TRMR-LPS grade (OR = 0.50, p = .003 in the overall cohort; OR = 0.36, p < .001 among young adults). The tonsillar hypertrophy × TRMR-LPS grade interaction remained statistically significant, meeting the criteria for false discovery rate correction (Benjamini-Hochberg FDR) or the Bonferroni correction. The tonsillar hypertrophy × age-group interaction (OR = 1.86, p = .022) met the uncorrected threshold only. The three-way interaction testing whether the two-way tonsillar hypertrophy × TRMR-LPS grade association differed by age was not significant (p=.513), indicating consistent interaction patterns across ages. No significant interactions involving oral-posture factors (open-mouth posture and tongue posture) were found in children.

Table 4 presents the final multivariable models incorporating significant interactions alongside main effects. All models demonstrated acceptable fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow > 0.05). High specificity (99.6%–100%) and low sensitivity (0%–2.8%) reflect the low prevalence of high-risk classification in the sample.

In the overall cohort, independent associations included age group (AOR = 6.52, p < .001), anterior tongue mobility (AOR = 0.98, p = .014), posterior mobility grade (AOR = 2.66, p = .025), frequent nocturnal open-mouth posture (AOR = 2.52, p = .046), and tonsillar hypertrophy (AOR = 3.51, p = .022). In young adults, the OMES score (AOR = 0.79, p = .015), posterior tongue mobility (AOR = 0.96, p = .027), and posterior tongue strength (AOR = 0.98, p = .041) were independent factors. In children, only oral-posture factors were significantly associated: frequent nocturnal mouth opening (AOR = 2.69, p = .033) and improper tongue resting posture (AOR = 1.69, p = .047).

In the overall cohort, the effect of tonsillar hypertrophy was moderated by posterior tongue mobility and by age group, as shown by two significant interaction terms: tonsillar hypertrophy × age-group (AOR = 2.06, p = .005) and tonsillar hypertrophy × TRMR-LPS grade (AOR = 0.53, p = .010). No interaction was statistically significant in young adults (p = .893), and none met retention criteria in the children model.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study examined associations between tongue-function characteristics and questionnaire-based high-risk classification for SDB in 1,244 healthy participants, including children aged 6–8 years (n = 462) and young adults aged 18–25 years (n = 782). We identified age-specific anatomical and functional patterns and explored potential effect modification through formal interaction analysis. Two principal findings emerged. First, substantial age-group-specific differences were observed in tongue-function traits and in the prevalence of high-risk classification. Young adults showed significant associations between high-risk classification and functional measures, whereas oral posture disorders were associated with high-risk classification in children. Second, interaction analysis demonstrated significant effect modification involving age group, posterior tongue mobility, and tonsillar hypertrophy.

Methodological Considerations and Confounding Management

Several methodological considerations required careful attention beyond simple statistical adjustment. First, the large sample (n = 1,244) provided adequate power to estimate the main effect and detect moderate-to-strong interactions (power ≥ 80% for OR ≥ 1.8). Second, confounding due to age-group-specific differences in factor distribution posed a major challenge. The reversal of the age-group effect (univariate OR = 0.37 to adjusted OR = 6.52) illustrates Simpson’s paradox, in which crude associations reverse after accounting for confounding variables [27]. The reversal was primarily due to children exhibiting a substantially higher prevalence of tongue function limitations (Table 1), rather than reflecting model instability. We applied multivariable adjustment to minimize confounding by variables correlated with multiple exposures. Third, an age-stratified design, combined with formal interaction testing, enabled developmental comparisons and reduced the aggregation bias inherent in pooled models. However, stratification reduced statistical power in smaller subgroups (e.g., young adults with tonsillar hypertrophy, n = 14), limiting the precision of some estimates. The significant interactions identified (p < .05) justify for stratified presentation [27], but external validation in independent cohorts with larger age-specific samples is needed.

Age-group-specific distribution patterns

Children and young adults in our cohort exhibited substantial age-group-specific differences across tongue-function domains. Children showed a higher prevalence of tonsillar hypertrophy, restricted posterior tongue mobility, and oral-posture disorders (Table 1); in young adults, functional parameters — strength, coordinated movement, and overall oral-motor performance — predominated. These contrasting age-group-specific distributions are consistent with known developmental transitions in orofacial anatomy and tongue function between childhood and young adulthood. The high prevalence of tonsillar hypertrophy is consistent with the expected regression of lymphoid tissue during late childhood [19]. The superior functional performance in adults likely reflects greater muscle mass and more mature neuromuscular coordination [24].

The present study also demonstrated age-group-specific differences for SDB through questionnaire-based classification (26.8% in children vs. 11.9% in young adults). Our screening used age-appropriate, validated instruments based on standardized criteria established in prior validation studies [28, 29], consistent with dental sleep medicine guidelines [1].

Age-stratified associations in children and young adults

Our age-stratified multivariable models demonstrate clear age-group-specific differences in the association between tongue function and high-risk classification. In children, high-risk classification was primarily associated with oral-posture disorders, notably frequent open-mouth posture during sleep (≥ 3 nights/week) and low tongue resting posture. These findings are consistent with the concept framework of Open-Mouth Posture Syndrome (OMPS), an oral postural disorder that often originates from abnormal tongue positioning. Our study observed patterns consistent with five OMPS subtypes: obstructive, habitual, anatomical, SDB-related, and tongue-related [30]. The underlying mechanism has been described in the cited literature as a “vicious cycle” in which nasal obstruction or habitual mouth breathing leads to inferior tongue positioning, which, in turn, promotes maxillary constriction and further airway compromise [30, 31]. Our data showed that nocturnal open-mouth posture (≥ 3 nights/week) and low tongue posture were associated with increased odds of high-risk classification (OR = 2.7 and OR = 1.7, respectively). A low tongue-resting posture has been associated with a sequence of orofacial changes that may contribute to snoring and increased OSA risk [31]. Other studies have demonstrated that a high resting tongue position and continuous nasal breathing are critical for proper craniofacial and airway development in growing children [32, 33]. Orthodontic studies further highlight tongue posture as a marker of nasal ventilation and craniofacial growth, reinforcing its potential role in early SDB risk assessment [34, 35].

In young adults, high-risk classification was strongly associated with objective measures of tongue function, including posterior mobility, tongue strength, and overall oral-motor performance. Posterior tongue function refers to the mobility and strength of the posterior two-thirds of the tongue, which is primarily controlled by the genioglossus muscle [6]. Contraction of upper airway dilator muscles, including the genioglossus, provides an adaptive force that counterbalances collapsing pressures and helps maintain retroglossal airway patency [36]. Limited posterior movement can reduce the ability to maintain upper airway patency under negative inspiratory pressure and increase the risk of retroglossal collapse [6]. In our study, better posterior tongue function was associated with lower odds of high-risk classification, and posterior mobility showed slightly stronger protective associations than strength. Zaghi et al. proposed the posterior tongue range of motion ratio (TRMR-LPS), the same method used in our study, as an objective indicator of tongue-tie severity and functional or submucosal restrictions [23]. Previous studies have also supported an association between a shortened lingual frenulum (tongue-tie) and OSA [37, 38]. Regarding strength, O’Connor-Reina et al. reported that patients with severe OSA had lower tongue-strength values measured with the IOPI than healthy controls, and tongue pressure evaluation may aid in identifying obstruction sites [39]. The overall orofacial function—covering mobility, strength, posture, and functional task performance—showed the strongest protective association (a 21% decrease in odds per point), exceeding the effects of individual components. However, the role of the posterior function remains central to these associations. The relatively wide confidence intervals, particularly for young adults’ posterior mobility, likely reflect the low prevalence of severe restrictions in young adults (5.2% Grade 3; Table 1). Larger samples are needed for more precise effect estimates.

Age-stratified interaction analysis and combined profile characterization

Interaction analysis formally tests whether the association between an exposure and an outcome differs across levels of another variable, a process known as effect modification [40]. Our study applied a progressive staged analytic approach: initial identification of candidate factors, testing clinically relevant interaction terms, and then stratifying after significant interactions were detected. This combined approach improves interpretation by confirming effect modification statistically and by exploring subgroup patterns [40].

The significant tonsillar hypertrophy × age-group interaction (AOR = 2.06, p = .005) indicates that the association of tonsillar hypertrophy with high-risk classification differed by age group. In our study, although tonsillar hypertrophy was rare in young adults (1.9% vs. 22.9% in children), its presence showed a statistically significant association in the young adult model (Table 2, AOR = 1.98), although the estimate was imprecise due to the very small sample size. In adults, tonsillar hypertrophy may reflect the pathological (rather than physiological) nature of tonsillar enlargement in developing children. When present, tonsillar hypertrophy is associated with greater clinical significance in adult populations. This pattern was not confirmed in the age-stratified analyses; tonsillar hypertrophy showed a non-significant trend toward increased odds of high-risk classification in children but an apparently protective association in young adults. However, the young adult subgroup with tonsillar hypertrophy was very small (n = 14), resulting in wide confidence intervals. Given the cross-sectional design, definitive conclusions cannot be drawn. Further studies with adequate representation of high-risk phenotypes across the lifespan are needed.

Direct evaluation of the interaction between tongue function— particularly posterior tongue mobility— and tonsillar size in relation to SDB has not been previously reported. Our study extends prior work by identifying an anatomical–functional interaction at the population level using observable clinical features—tonsillar hypertrophy and posterior tongue-mobility restriction (overall: OR = 0.53, p = .010; adults: OR = 0.36, p < .001). The odds ratios below 1 indicate that greater posterior mobility attenuated the association between tonsillar hypertrophy and high-risk classification. This conditional pattern is consistent with the compensatory framework described by Younes (2003), in which upper-airway collapsibility reflects the combined influence of anatomical predisposition and neuromuscular compensation. Neuromuscular activity can compensate for functional deficits only when airway anatomy provides adequate structural reserve [16]. The significant interaction suggests a cumulative, interdependent impact of reduced posterior tongue mobility and tonsillar enlargement on high-risk classification. In other words, restricted posterior tongue mobility may become more consequential in individuals whose airway anatomy already limits compensatory capacity, such as those with enlarged tonsils or reduced oral cavity space. Recent studies have attempted to explain why 13%–29% of children exhibit residual OSA following adenotonsillectomy and why adenotonsillectomy alone may be insufficient to resolve airway obstruction. The American Academy of Pediatrics’ guideline highlighted that when adenotonsillectomy fails to resolve OSA, the specific anatomical site responsible for the airway obstruction should be targeted [5]. Tongue-mobility restriction, as one manifestation of reduced genioglossus responsiveness to negative pharyngeal pressure, may constrain upper airway patency and contribute to the pathogenesis of residual OSA. Our findings challenge a single-factor treatment approach; at-risk patients may benefit more from combined interventions addressing both anatomical and functional components.

Risk stratification further demonstrated that combined anatomical and functional compromise was associated with a substantially higher prevalence of high-risk classification. Children with both enlarged tonsils and restricted posterior tongue mobility had approximately 2.5-times the odds of high-risk classification compared with those with favorable profiles. Thus, our study identified a distinct phenotype that warrants clinical attention: children with both enlarged tonsils and restricted posterior tongue mobility, who had the highest predicted probability of high-risk classification (34.5%). Assessment of anatomical or functional factors alone may substantially underestimate SDB risk in individuals with dual deficits. However, although effect modification was observed in the overall sample, it was non-significant or absent in the age-stratified models. Therefore, the interaction terms should be interpreted cautiously and in the context of all study-relevant covariates [40].

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference and interpretation to associations. The two age groups were recruited from different settings and periods and represent distinct sampling populations rather than a single cohort sampled across a developmental continuum. The absence of intermediate age groups precludes inferences about a continuous developmental trajectory; between-group findings should be interpreted as contrasting developmental phenotypes with distinct SDB pathophysiology, rather than as age-dependent effects across a continuous spectrum. High-risk classification was based on validated questionnaires rather than polysomnography, potentially leading to misclassification. However, questionnaires are widely used for epidemiologic screening and reflect clinical practice in settings where PSG is not feasible [1, 3]. Logistic regression models demonstrated high overall accuracy driven by specificity, but sensitivity was extremely low, limiting predictive utility. We acknowledge the small subgroup size in the young adult sample, the single-center design, and the homogeneous Asian, generally healthy population, all of which limit the generalizability of our findings and preclude establishing a dose–response relationship. However, given that this is an exploratory study and the first to investigate the association between tongue function and SDB across developmental stages, our findings remain valuable for hypothesis generation and for informing future larger-scale studies.

Conclusions

Tongue function traits showed age-group-specific associations with questionnaire-based high-risk SDB classification. In children, oral-posture disorders, such as frequent open-mouth posture during sleep and a low tongue-resting posture, were associated with higher odds of high-risk classification. In young adults, better overall orofacial function, together with greater posterior tongue mobility and strength, was protective. As a secondary finding, the association between tonsillar hypertrophy and high-risk classification was moderated by posterior tongue mobility and age. Functional deficits may be more consequential when anatomical reserve is limited. Combined compromise is associated with substantially higher classification prevalence in children. These findings suggest that integrating both anatomical and functional assessments within age-group-specific frameworks may improve early identification of individuals at risk. Findings should be interpreted within the constraints of a cross-sectional, questionnaire-based design and should be validated using longitudinal methods and objective sleep measures.
