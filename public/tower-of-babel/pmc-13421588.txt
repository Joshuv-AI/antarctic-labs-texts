Analyzing climate change trends and projection of their effects on wood equilibrium moisture content using CMIP6 models under SSP scenarios in Iran
Authors: Jalil Helali, Mehdi Mohammadi Ghaleni, Zahra Kalantari, Christian Brischke, Ebrahim Asadi Oskouei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the influence of climate variables, specifically temperature and relative humidity, on the equilibrium moisture content (EMC) of wood—a critical quality parameter. Using data from 100 synoptic stations across Iran (1987–2019), we analyzed trends in temperature, humidity, and EMC through the Mann-Kendall and Sen’s slope methods. Future projections (2020–2049) employed CMIP6 models—CanESM5, CanESM5-CanOE, CNRM-CM6-1, CNRM-ESM2-1, and IPSL-CM6A-LR—under SSP scenarios, with model selection based on RMSE, Scatter Index, and R². Scenarios SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5 were used to project future climatic conditions and corresponding EMC values. The CanESM5-CanOE model exhibits the lowest monthly relative humidity estimation errors in Iran, with errors ranging from 10.1% to 15.0% across different climate zones. Increasing EMC is most frequent under SSP1-2.6 (20%-92% of stations) and SSP5-8.5 (34%-100%). Decreasing trends are significant under SSP2-6.5 (66%-100%) and SSP5-8.5 (45%-88%). Monthly variations: -4.74% to + 3.71%; seasonal: -2.87% to + 2.45%; annual: -1.17% to + 1.00%. Significantly decreasing EMC trends are under SSP2-6.5, increasing trends under SSP5-8.5. Over a 30-year span, EMC varied from 0.06 to 0.62% in winter, from − 1.14 to -1.23% in spring, from − 0.84 to -0.89% in summer, and from − 0.80 to -1.34% in autumn, with most changes being statistically significant. Introduction

Modern societies face rising housing demands, increasing the need for sustainable construction materials. Wood offers a significant advantage by sequestering 300–500 kg of carbon per ton, in stark contrast to the substantial CO₂ emissions from concrete (50–170 kg) and metal (370-1,040 kg)1. Advanced engineered wood products like Cross-Laminated Timber (CLT) enable high-rise construction while addressing emissions2,3. With future wood demand projected to increase 3.3 to 3.5 times4, understanding its interaction with environmental moisture—quantified by the Equilibrium Moisture Content (EMC) index—is critical for performance and durability5,6.

EMC is a key property influenced by wood type (e.g., CLT, glulam), processing history, and critically, by ambient relative humidity (RH) and temperature7. It governs physical properties like dimensional stability, thermal conductivity, and mechanical strength8. Fluctuations in EMC can alter product dimensions, posing risks to global trade when climatic conditions differ between production and use regions9–11. Therefore, EMC is not merely a material property but a vital metric linking climate, material science, and supply chain integrity.

A bibliometric review of the literature (2000–2024) reveals that research has primarily focused on EMC estimation and historical trend analysis, with a notable gap in future projections under climate change scenarios (Fig. 1).

Timeline visualization of co-occurring keywords in the EMC field worldwide from 2000 to 2024.

Climate change directly threatens this link by altering the fundamental drivers of EMC: temperature and humidity. Global warming is increasing atmospheric water vapor content12 and exacerbating drought severity13, which in turn affects the atmospheric vapor demand that governs wood moisture exchange14–16. While the wood industry is recognized as vulnerable to these changes10,17, most research has focused on EMC estimation18,19, modeling with geographical variables20, or trend analysis under historical conditions10,11.

A significant gap remains in quantitatively projecting future EMC dynamics under climate change scenarios, particularly at a national scale and for diverse climatic regions. This limits the ability of industries and standards to anticipate and adapt to future material performance.

This study addresses this gap by providing the first comprehensive, nationwide assessment of future EMC changes for Iran.

We move beyond historical analysis to test two central hypotheses using CMIP6 climate models: H1: The baseline spatial distribution of EMC across Iran’s diverse climates is systematically controlled by combined temperature-humidity conditions, and H2: Projected climate change under SSP scenarios will significantly alter these EMC patterns over the next 30 years, with the magnitude of change dependent on the emission pathway.

This study focuses on temperature and relative humidity as the primary climatic drivers for modeling regional climate change impacts on wood equilibrium moisture content (EMC). These parameters are the direct determinants of the EMC equilibrium state in sorption models and are robustly projected by climate models. Our analysis leverages data from 100 synoptic stations and CMIP6 projections to quantify these shifts. This work is particularly urgent for Iran, a major importer of high-value wood products21,22, as it provides a scientific basis for revising future EMC standards and building climate resilience into the forestry-construction supply chain.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study area is located within Iran’s geographical extent in southwestern Asia and the Middle East, lying between latitudes 25° and 39° north and longitudes 44° and 64° east, covering an area of approximately 1,648,000 square kilometers. Iran is ranked the 18th largest country in the world by area, with an average annual precipitation of less than one-third of the global average23–25. Iran is among the most mountainous countries globally, dominated by rugged mountain ranges such as the Zagros in the west and the Alborz in the north, which separate different basins and plateaus. The geographical position of these mountains has led to the formation of two major deserts: the Dasht-e Lut (Lut Desert) and the Dasht-e Kavir (Kavir Desert), both of which significantly influence Iran’s climate. The climate in northern Iran is Mediterranean and humid, but most parts are classified as arid or semi-arid climate zones according to various climatic classification systems26,27.

In this study, climatic data from 100 synoptic stations with a 33-year statistical period (1987–2019) were used, including mean temperature and RH, to calculate the EMC of wood. The geographic locations of these stations are shown in Fig. 2, and their climate types are detailed in Table 1. Based on their distribution, most of the central plateau, southeastern regions, and parts of southwestern Iran are classified as having an arid climate. Semi-arid and Mediterranean climates are found along the Alborz and Zagros mountain ranges, while most semi-humid and humid climates are situated in the southern parts of the Caspian Sea region.

We utilized the modified De Martonne aridity index to categorize climate conditions into eight classes, ranging from hyper-arid to hyper-humid (Table 1). This index is derived from the ratios of precipitation to air temperature28,29.

We utilized kriging interpolation on the data gathered from synoptic stations. Among the various kriging methods, we chose to implement kriging with terrain elevation as an external parameter for both precipitation and temperature, as recommended by30 in the field of geostatistics31.

Equilibrium moisture content

Several models are available for estimating EMC, including the Hailwood-Horrobin, Nelson, and Simpson equations. Based on the literature, the Hailwood-Horrobin two-hydrate model is considered more accurate than the others32–34. Therefore, this model was selected for EMC modeling in the present study. The variable T denotes temperature in degrees Celsius, while RH indicates relative humidity as a percentage, reflecting the moisture level in the air relative to its maximum capacity at that temperature. The coefficients W, K, K1, and K2 are empirical constants for moisture adsorption, determined through experimental data. These coefficients play a critical role in accurately modeling the relationship between temperature, humidity, and moisture content in wood, capturing the specific properties and behavior of the material under varying environmental conditions35.

Trend analysis

The analysis of trend in time series data is essential to understand the behavior of variables. In this study, the Mann-Kendall method36,37 is used to identify the trend type, and the Sen Slope estimator38 is employed to determine the rate of change. In time series, the intensity of a variable at a specific time often depends on its intensity at previous or subsequent times, a phenomenon known as autocorrelation. This can increase the likelihood of detecting a significant trend erroneously39,40. Therefore, before trend analysis, autocorrelation effects are removed through a pre-whitening process41,42.

Projection of temperature and relative humidity

For projection temperature and RH variables, CMIP6 models including CanESM5, CanESM5-CanOE, CNRM-CM6-1, CNRM-ESM2-1, and IPSL-CM6A-LR were utilized under three SSPs: SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5. The models were selected from the CMIP6 set due to their reliability, accuracy, and availability of necessary scenarios43. They provide high spatial and temporal resolution for precise regional climate projections, crucial for understanding local temperature and humidity impacts. By incorporating diverse climate processes—such as atmospheric dynamics, oceanic interactions, and land surface processes—these models offer a comprehensive view of climate behavior44. Frequently utilized in international climate assessments, they yield valuable insights for informing climate policy and adaptation strategies. Additionally, their capability for long-term projections is essential for evaluating future temperature and humidity trends over decades to centuries45,46.

The SSP scenarios are constructed based on joint efforts between IAV and the IAM community47. The scenarios examined in this study—SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5—represent the lower, middle to upper, and upper extremes of future pathway ranges, respectively. Calibration or validation of temperature and RH in different climates using historical data was performed with the following statistics: R², RMSE, and Scatter Index48:

In this context, RMSE values range from zero to infinity, R² values range from zero to one, and SI values range from zero to one. RMSE values close to zero and R² values close to one indicate a better model. The variations of the SI coefficient are classified as follows: less than 0.1 is excellent, between 0.1 and 0.2 is good, between 0.2 and 0.3 is acceptable, and above 0.3 indicates a weak model49,50.

In this research, we use the delta change approach for bias correction, a simple method widely applied in climate impact studies51. This technique calculates the difference between historical observed data and model outputs, then applies this delta to future projections for more accurate local climate estimates. It is essential for evaluating impacts on agriculture, water resources, and ecosystems Instead of correcting biases in the climate model, this approach adjusts observed data based on the model’s response to climate change52.

Analysis of variance and comparison of means

To conduct the analysis of variance (ANOVA) and compare means, we utilized the SAS software package, a powerful tool for statistical analysis. In this section, we first performed an ANOVA to evaluate differences between various scenarios, allowing us to assess the impact of different conditions on the results. Following this, we extended our analysis to compare means across different climatic periods, providing a comprehensive view of how climate changes affect our variables of interest53.

We then focused on the mean comparison of the EMC of wood for the period 2020–2049. This was compared against the baseline period of 1987–2019, allowing us to identify significant changes over time. The comparison was conducted on a monthly, seasonal, and annual basis, using the Duncan method, which is effective for identifying differences among group means when the analysis involves multiple comparisons54.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that rising temperatures and declining relative humidity (RH) are the primary drivers reducing Equilibrium Moisture Content (EMC) in wood across Iran, a trend projected to intensify under future climate scenarios. Our findings align with the global pattern reported by11, who documented a 2% EMC decline over half a century linked to a 2.7 °C temperature rise and an 11.7% RH drop. However, our analysis provides novel spatial and temporal resolution, revealing that while the national trend is clear, its magnitude varies significantly: temperature increased by 1–2 °C annually, while RH declined by 3–6% over the 33-year baseline period. This resulted in an annual EMC reduction of 1–1.5%, with monthly decreases reaching 1–3%.

The observed spatial heterogeneity in RH trends—with predominant declines but localized increases—highlights the complex interplay of regional climatic drivers. This variability explains apparent discrepancies in the literature, where studies report either decreasing55,56 or increasing57,58, RH trends. The critical factor is moisture advection; regions with limited influx from oceanic sources, like much of Iran’s interior, exhibit stronger drying trends59. Our observed pattern of simultaneous warming and drying60,61 along Iran’s coasts (0.28–1.03% RH decline per decade62,63) is consistent with thermodynamic processes that increase atmospheric vapor demand while limiting supply. This synthesis moves beyond cataloging trends to explain why they differ: the geographic position relative to moisture sources is a key determinant of local RH and, consequently, EMC response.

A critical advancement of our work is the application of CMIP6 projections to quantify future EMC risk. While11 established a historical correlation, we project the trajectory, showing how different SSP scenarios lead to divergent EMC futures. This methodological shift from retrospective analysis to prospective modeling is essential for adaptive planning. However, these projections carry inherent uncertainties. Future EMC estimates are contingent on climate model variability, emission scenario uncertainty, and the simplifying assumption that temperature-RH relationships remain stationary. We explicitly acknowledge these limitations and recommend that subsequent research incorporate multi-model ensembles and statistical downscaling to quantify confidence intervals, thereby strengthening the robustness of EMC forecasts for engineering standards.

This study focused on macro-climatic drivers (temperature and RH) due to data availability constraints at the national scale. We acknowledge that micro-scale factors—wood species, density, and local storage conditions—can modulate the climate-EMC relationship. Future studies should integrate these material science variables with high-resolution climate data to develop species-specific vulnerability assessments. Furthermore, while we have identified the dominant climatic trend, research is needed to model the direct impact of these EMC changes on the dimensional stability and long-term durability of engineered wood products in construction. Such work would bridge the gap between climatology and practical building science, directly informing material selection and building codes in a warming world.

Conclusion

Analysis of historical data showed that the CanESM5-CanOE and IPSL-CM6A-LR models best predict future temperature and RH. Projected climate changes reveal that the largest warming trend occurs under SSP5-8.5, while the greatest decrease in RH is seen under SSP1-2.6. RH declines most in SSP1-2.6 and SSP2-4.5 scenarios.

Over 30 years, temperature is expected to rise by 3.8 to 5.2 °C monthly, 2.2 to 5.4 °C seasonally, and 0.3 to 3.0 °C annually. RH may decrease by 23.6% to 21.1% monthly, 11.2% to 10.8% seasonally, and 6.4% to 4.8% annually. Consequently, EMC fluctuations are projected at ± 4.7%, ± 2.9%, and ± 1.2% monthly, seasonally, and annually over 30 years.

Studies indicate that ocean-advection of specific humidity into continents occurs more slowly than the increase in saturated humidity over arid regions, leading to reduced RH in arid areas. This enhances atmospheric evaporative demand, promoting drier conditions16,64. Several global climate models support this hypothesis under future warming scenarios65,66.

An ANOVA showed that modeled EMC percentages significantly differ across climate scenarios depending on the spatial-temporal scale, especially in July, August, and winter. These differences are prominent in northwest Iran, the southern Caspian coast, southeastern regions, and select stations along the Persian Gulf and Oman Sea. The results imply that future EMC in timber-producing and consuming regions will change significantly.

Comparisons of seasonal and annual mean EMC across scenarios reveal statistically significant differences from the baseline, with the most pronounced variations under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5. These findings underscore the necessity for further studies on EMC changes in exporting and importing countries of timber and wood-based products. Understanding climate impacts on wood EMC can inform better management strategies, reduce costs and optimize resource use.

Projected changes in EMC due to climate change can significantly impact construction standards, material planning, and trade policies in wood-importing countries like Iran. Higher moisture levels may necessitate revisions to building codes to ensure the durability and safety of structures, while suppliers may need to adjust sourcing strategies and inventory management practices to accommodate increased EMC. Additionally, stricter import regulations could be implemented to ensure that imported wood meets updated EMC standards, alongside potential tariffs on low-quality imports and incentives for sustainable sourcing. By addressing these changes, stakeholders can enhance the resilience of the construction industry and protect material quality in the face of climate variability.
