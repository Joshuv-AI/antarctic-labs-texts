Numerical Aspects of the Tensor Product Multilevel Method for High-dimensional, Kernel-based Reconstruction on Sparse Grids
Markus Büttner, Rüdiger Kempf, Holger Wendland

Abstract
This paper investigates the approximation of functions with finite smoothness defined on domains with a Cartesian product structure. The recently proposed tensor product multilevel method (TPML) combines Smolyak’s sparse grid method with a kernel-based residual correction technique. The contributions of this paper are twofold. First, we present two improvements on the TPML that reduce the computational cost of point evaluations compared to a naive implementation. Second, we provide numerical examples that demonstrate the effectiveness and innovation of the TPML.



Keywords
Radial basis functions, sparse grids, multilevel approximation.

Introduction

Many current problems in physics, engineering, and even business science are higher-dimensional in nature. This could be because the problem is modeled as a partial differential equation in space-time, which results in a two/three- plus-one-dimensional problem, or because it involves uncertainty, which is modeled by a stochastic process and then parameterized, done so in uncertainty quantification. The number of parameters then corresponds to the dimension of the problem. Classical numerical methods are often numerically not feasible, even in only four-dimensional examples, because of the curse of dimensionality. One way to alleviate this curse is by means of Smolyak’s method
[
17
]
. This method is tailored for problems whose domain can be decomposed into the Cartesian product of lower-dimensional domains, for example space and time, and allows us to combine numerical methods used on these lower-dimensional domains to an approximation process in high dimensions and offers a very flexible ansatz with applications in quadrature
[
17
,
10
,
9
,
12
]
, interpolation
[
5
,
1
,
13
]
, solving PDEs
[
2
,
15
,
11
]
, machine learning
[
6
]
and even financial mathematics
[
19
]
. The list given here is by no means exhaustive. For a more complete list, we refer to
[
7
,
3
]
.

Recently, a new method, called the
tensor product multilevel method (TPML)
, was proposed and studied in
[
13
]
, combining the method of Smolyak with a kernel-based multilevel scheme
[
18
]
. This method allows us to approximate moderately high-dimensional functions defined on the Cartesian product of lower-dimensional domains using scattered data. The ability to use general lower-dimensional domains instead of only intervals is one of the exceptional features of this method. Other known methods are either spline or polynomial based and are therefore based on intervals and thus fail at solving these kinds of problems. There have been previous attempts to combine Smolyak’s method with a kernel-based approach, see, e.g.,
[
8
]
, but concentrated on non-compactly supported basis functions and did not provide a rigorous error analysis. In contrast, the paper
[
13
]
provides a thorough error analysis for compactly supported kernels of finite smoothness. There, an explicit representation of the approximation operator is also given. However, it turns out that a naive implementation of the derived representation is not feasible because the computational cost is too large. Hence, in the present paper, we propose two different reformulations of the TPML, with the result that the method can actually be applied to real-world examples. The first reformulation strives to relocate expensive computations into an offline phase such that the numerical cost of several point evaluations of the approximation becomes cheaper compared to the naive implementation. The second reformulation is tailored to problems with nested sets of sites, which occur naturally in typically applications. There, we use the nestedness to derive a representation of the TPML that uses every data value only once, reducing the numerical costs this way.

The outline of this paper is as follows. In

we briefly recall the building blocks of the TPML, i.e., Smolyak’s method and the formulation of the kernel-based multilevel method using a (modified) Lagrange basis, before giving the definition of the TPML operator. In
Section

To keep

2
The Tensor Product Multilevel Method

We briefly repeat the basic ideas of the kernel-based tensor product multilevel method. For details, we refer to
[
13
]
. The method essentially combines the kernel-based multilevel method for scattered data approximation on arbitrary low-dimensional domains with Smolyak’s method, which allows us to construct an approximation method for high-dimensions with essentially the same properties as the building-block, low-dimensional methods.

2.1
Smolyak’s Method

Smolyak’s construction and subsequent generalizations, as well as the numerical approximation power and computational effort depend on a pre-determined

index set
. This set describes how many levels we will use in each
direction. In order to have the maximum freedom and to adapt to possible
different smoothnesses in different directions it became customary to use
anisotropic index sets
, see, e.g.,
[
15
,
5
,
12
,
16
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

For
d\in\mathbb{N}
let
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. The

anisotropic index set

\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d)\subset\mathbb{N}^{d}
is
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d):=\left\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\;:\;\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\lambda_{j}-1)\omega_{j}\leq\ell\omega_{min}\right\},

((1))

where
\omega_{min}:=\min_{1\leq j\leq d}\omega_{j}
.

Its
surface

\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d)
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d)=\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d)\setminus\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\left(\ell-\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|_{1}}{\omega_{min}},d\right).

((2))

The positive weight vector
\boldsymbol{\omega}
reflects how important each
direction is. The larger the quotient
\omega_{j}/\omega_{min}
is chosen the
less important direction
j
is for us and
\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d)
is less
extended in this direction. For fixed
\boldsymbol{\omega}
the threshold
\ell
determines the maximum number of levels in each direction. The largest level in
each direction can be directly computed by

\displaystyle\lambda_{j,max}=\left\lfloor\frac{\ell\omega_{min}}{\omega_{j}}\right\rfloor+1.

Remark 2.2
.

For the error analysis in
[
13
]
, we have to assume that the weight
vector
\boldsymbol{\omega}
is ascendingly ordered, i.e.,
\omega_{1}\leq\omega_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\omega_{d}
.

Given sequences of operators
A^{(j)}_{i}
,
1\leq j\leq d
,
1\leq i\leq\lambda_{j,max}
, and setting
A^{(j)}_{0}=0
, the Smolyak operator in combination technique representation is given as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d)}:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\{0,1\}^{d}\\
\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d)\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}\left(A^{(1)}_{\lambda_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes A^{(d)}_{\lambda_{d}}\right).

Smolyak’s method is deeply connected to
sparse grids
, which we define now. To families of sites
X_{\lambda_{j}}^{(j)}
,
1\leq\lambda_{j}\leq\lambda_{j,max}
, the sparse grid
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d)}\subseteq\Omega
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d)}:=\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d)}X^{(1)}_{\lambda_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes X^{(d)}_{\lambda_{d}}.

2.2
Kernel Multilevel Method using Lagrange Functions

The standard kernel-based multilevel method, described in, e.g.,
[
18
]
, is a residual correction scheme for scattered data approximation. The key ingredients are a sequence of, not necessarily nested, sets of sites
X_{1},\dots,X_{L}
, level-dependently scaled kernels
\Phi_{i}
with compact support, and associated
local approximation spaces

\displaystyle W_{i}=\operatorname{span}\left\{\Phi_{i}(\cdot-\boldsymbol{x}_{i,k})\;:\;\boldsymbol{x}_{i,k}\in X_{i}\right\},\quad 1\leq i\leq L.

In the standard formulation of the kernel-based multilevel method, there is only an implicit dependence of the data. Hence, the authors of
[
13
]
found a new representation of the method in terms of
Lagrange
or
cardinal functions

\{\chi_{i,k}\}
, that made the dependence on the data explicit.
The defining property of Lagrange functions is that they satisfy
\chi_{i,k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i,m})=\delta_{km}
for
\boldsymbol{x}_{i,m}\in X_{i}
.

The Lagrange basis can be computed by solving a linear system. However, the
matrix differs whether we use interpolation or penalized least squares
approximation. We remark that in the case of penalized least squares, the functions do not satisfy the Lagrange condition, but lead to good numerical results anyway.

Definition 2.3
.

For
L\in\mathbb{N}
,
1\leq i\leq L
and
1\leq k\leq N_{i}
let

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{r}_{i}:=\left(\Phi_{i}(\cdot-\boldsymbol{x}_{i,1}),\dots,\Phi_{i}(\cdot-\boldsymbol{x}_{i,N_{i}})\right)^{\text{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}}.

((3))

The
k
-th Lagrange function on level
i
,
\chi_{i,k}
, can then be expressed as

\displaystyle\chi_{i,k}=\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i,k}\right)^{\text{T}}\boldsymbol{r}_{i}.

((4))

The coefficient vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}}
is given as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i,k}:=\begin{cases}M_{i}^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{k},&\text{for interpolation,}\\
(M_{i}+\lambda_{i}I)^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{k},&\text{for penalized
least-squares approximation}\end{cases}

((5))

where
\left(M_{i}\right)_{k,m}:=\left(\Phi_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i,k}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i,m})\right)
is the Gramian,
\boldsymbol{e}_{k}
denotes the
k
-th unit vector and

I\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}\times N_{i}}
is the unit matrix and
\lambda_{i}
are chooseable regularization parameter.

We use the following notation throughout the rest of this paper.

Definition 2.4
.

1.

For
L\in\mathbb{N}
we call a subset
\mathfrak{u}:=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,L\}
with
m=\#\mathfrak{u}
elements
ordered
, if
u_{1}<u_{2}<\cdots<u_{m}
.

2.

For an ordered set
\mathfrak{u}=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\}
, we take

\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathfrak{u}}
as
\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathfrak{u}}:=(N_{u_{1}},\dots,N_{u_{m}})^{\text{T}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\#\mathfrak{u}}
.

3.

For an ordered set
\mathfrak{u}=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\}
we abbreviate

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\leq\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathfrak{u}}}:=\sum_{k_{1}\leq N_{u_{1}}}\cdots\sum_{k_{m}\leq N_{u_{m}}},

where we implicitly assume that the dimension of
\boldsymbol{k}
fits the dimension of

\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathfrak{u}}
.

Then, the multilevel approximation operator can be expressed in the following way, see
[
13
, Theorem 3.9]
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
\mathfrak{u}=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{\#\mathfrak{u}}\}
be an ordered set. Define the
combined operator

\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{u}}:C(\Omega)\to W_{\#\mathfrak{u}}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{u}}f:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\leq\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathfrak{u}}}a(\mathfrak{u},\boldsymbol{k})f(\boldsymbol{x}_{u_{1},k_{1}})\chi_{u_{\#\mathfrak{u}},k_{\#\mathfrak{u}}},

((6))

where the coefficients are given by
a(\mathfrak{u},\boldsymbol{k})=1
, if
\#\mathfrak{u}=1
and

\displaystyle a(\mathfrak{u},\boldsymbol{k})=\prod_{\ell=1}^{\#\mathfrak{u}-1}\chi_{u_{\ell},k_{\ell}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{u_{\ell+1},k_{\ell+1}})

for
\#\mathfrak{u}>1
.

Then the multilevel approximation operator
A_{L}:C(\Omega)\to\bigoplus_{i=1}^{L}W_{i}
at level
L
has the representation

\displaystyle A_{L}(f)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1,\dots,L\}\\
1\leq\#\mathfrak{u}\leq L\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\#\mathfrak{u}+1}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\leq\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathfrak{u}}}a(\mathfrak{u},\boldsymbol{k})f(\boldsymbol{x}_{u_{1},k_{1}})\chi_{u_{\#\mathfrak{u}},k_{\#\mathfrak{u}}}

for
f\in C(\Omega)
.

2.3
The Tensor Product Multilevel Method

The combination of Smolyak’s method and the kernel-based multilevel method yields the tensor product multilevel approximation operator for continuous functions on
\Omega^{\otimes}=\Omega^{(1)}\times\cdots\times\Omega^{(d)}
, where
\Omega^{(j)}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{j}}
,
1\leq j\leq d
and
n_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, we allow
n_{j}>1
.

Definition 2.6
.

We define the
tensor product multilevel approximation
to
\boldsymbol{f}\in C(\Omega^{\otimes})
,
evaluated at
\boldsymbol{x}=(\boldsymbol{x}^{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}^{(d)})\in\Omega^{\otimes}
, as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d)}(\boldsymbol{f})(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\{0,1\}^{d}\\
\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d)\end{subarray}}\\
&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{u}^{(1)}\subseteq\{1,\dots,\lambda_{1}\}\\
1\leq\#\mathfrak{u}^{(1)}\leq\lambda_{1}\end{subarray}}\cdots\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{u}^{(d)}\subseteq\{1,\dots,\lambda_{d}\}\\
1\leq\#\mathfrak{u}^{(d)}\leq\lambda_{d}\end{subarray}}c_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\mathfrak{u}^{(1)},\dots,\mathfrak{u}^{(d)})\\
&\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}^{(1)}\leq\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathfrak{u}^{(1)}}}\cdots\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}^{(d)}\leq\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathfrak{u}^{(d)}}}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(1)}_{u_{1}^{(1)},k^{(1)}_{1}},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}^{(d)}_{u_{1}^{(d)},k^{(d)}_{1}})\cdot\\
&\phantom{=}\prod_{j=1}^{d}a^{(j)}(\mathfrak{u}^{(j)},\boldsymbol{k}^{(j)})\cdot\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d}\chi^{(j)}_{u^{(j)}_{\#\mathfrak{u}^{(j)}},k^{(j)}_{\#\mathfrak{u}^{(j)}}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(j)})\right),\end{aligned}

((7))

where

\displaystyle c_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\mathfrak{u}^{(1)},\dots,\mathfrak{u}^{(d)})=(-1)^{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{1}+d+\#\mathfrak{u}^{(1)}+\cdots+\#\mathfrak{u}^{(d)}}

and
a^{(j)}(\mathfrak{u}^{(j)},\boldsymbol{k}^{(j)})=1
if
\#\mathfrak{u}^{(j)}=1
and

\displaystyle a^{(j)}(\mathfrak{u}^{(j)},\boldsymbol{k}^{(j)})=\prod_{m=1}^{\#\mathfrak{u}^{(j)}-1}\chi_{u^{(j)}_{m},k^{(j)}_{m}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(j)}_{u^{(j)}_{m-1},k^{(j)}_{m-1}})

((8))

for
\#\mathfrak{u}^{(j)}>1
for every
1\leq j\leq d
.

Remark 2.7
.

1.

We can see that the target function is only evaluated in the sites
\boldsymbol{x}^{(j)}_{u_{1}^{(j)},k_{1}^{(j)}}
,

1\leq k_{1}^{(j)}\leq N_{u_{1}^{(j)}}^{(j)}
.
These are the points in the coarsest set of sites
X^{(j)}_{u_{1}^{(j)}}
associated to each ordered set
\mathfrak{u}
.

2.

Similarly, we see that only the Lagrange functions associated to the finest set of sites for each ordered set
\mathfrak{u}
are evaluated at the point
\boldsymbol{x}
.

Although this is a valid representation of the approximation operator, a naive implementation is, even in only two directions and a moderate number of levels in each direction, numerically too expensive.

3
Modifications of the Tensor Product Multilevel Method

The aim is now to reduce the online cost of a point-evaluation of the tensor
product multilevel approximation. We present two ways to achieve this goal.
First, by moving expensive computations to an offline phase. And second, by
deriving a completely new representation of the approximation operator, where
we change the perspective and sum of the points in the associated sparse grid
rather than the multi-indices.

3.1
Towards more efficient point-evaluations

To keep this section brief we move the proofs into
Appendix

A
. Most of
the ideas are technical transformations involving the elements of the ordered
sets
\mathfrak{u}^{(j)}
.

We start with simplifying the representation of the tensor product of the combined
operators
\mathcal{I}^{(1)}_{\mathfrak{u}^{(1)}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{I}^{(d)}_{\mathfrak{u}^{(d)}}
. To do this, we take a closer look at each of the
direction-wise, combined operator
\mathcal{I}^{(j)}_{\mathfrak{u}^{(j)}}
and try to
separate the intermediate terms in
a^{(j)}(\mathfrak{u}^{(j)},\boldsymbol{k}^{(j)})
. These
can be pre-computed since they are independent of the target function and the
evaluation point. This idea can be applied in each direction, hence we omit
the superscript
(j)
in the next lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1,\dots,L\}
be a fixed ordered set and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i,k}:=\begin{cases}M_{i}^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{k},&\text{for interpolation,}\\
(M_{i}+\lambda_{i}I)^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{k},&\text{for penalized least-squares approximation}\end{cases}

be the coefficient vector for
\chi_{i,k}
.

Define
P_{\mathfrak{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{u_{1}}\times N_{u_{\#\mathfrak{u}}}}
as

\displaystyle P_{\mathfrak{u}}:=\prod_{m=2}^{\#\mathfrak{u}}\sum_{k_{m}\leq N_{u_{m}}}\boldsymbol{r}_{u_{m-1}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{u_{m},k_{m}})\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\text{T}}_{u_{m},k_{m}},

((9))

with
\boldsymbol{r}_{i}
as in (
(3)
).

Then we can express the combined local operators
\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{u}}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{u}}f(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{k_{1}\leq N_{u_{1}}}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{u_{1},k_{1}})\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\text{T}}_{u_{1},k_{1}}\cdot P_{\mathfrak{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{u_{\#\mathfrak{u}}}(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega.

Remark 3.2
.

1.

To compute the vectors
(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i,k})_{1\leq k\leq N_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}}
we have to solve
N_{i}
-many sparse linear systems, where the
matrix stays the same only the right-hand side changes.

2.

We recall that
\boldsymbol{r}_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})
is the evaluation vector of the rescaled
kernel on level
i
. It has only constant many non-zero entries. This means that each of the rank-
1
-matrices

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{r}_{u_{m-1}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{u_{m},k_{m}})\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\text{T}}_{u_{m},k_{m}}

in (
(9)
) is sparse. However, we cannot leverage this since for every
k_{m}
the sparsity pattern differs.

3.

All matrices
P_{\mathfrak{u}}
can be computed in an offline phase and are
independent of the evaluation point
\boldsymbol{x}
and the data

f(\boldsymbol{x}_{u_{1},k_{1}})
. Additionally, all these computations are vector or matrix computations and, therefore, can be done efficiently on GPUs.

We can now use
Lemma

3.1
for every
direction
1\leq j\leq d
and obtain a new representation of the tensor
product combined operator.

Theorem 3.3
.

Use the notation and assumptions of

&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&&\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(j)}_{\lambda_{j,max},\lambda_{j,max}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(j)})\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}A^{(j)}_{i}&&0\\
&\ddots&\\
0&&A^{(j)}_{\lambda_{j,max}}\end{pmatrix}\cdot SS^{(j)}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{r}^{(j)}_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(j)})&&0\\
&\ddots&\\
0&&\boldsymbol{r}^{(j)}_{\lambda_{j,max}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(j)})\end{pmatrix}.

((15))

Clearly, the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(j)})
depends on the
j
-th component of the evaluation point
\boldsymbol{x}
, by the third factor. We recall that every
\boldsymbol{r}^{(j)}_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(j)})
is a vector with only a constant number of nonzero entries. So, in application, one has to decide whether to compute the matrix as in (
(15)
), by using the sparseness of the third matrix, for every evaluation point, or just compute the product of the first two dense matrices and keep the independence of the evaluation point.

Altogether, the tensor product multilevel interpolation or penalized
least-squares approximation has the following form. This is the main result of
this section and is the representation that allows the most pre-computation.

Theorem 3.8
.

With the notation and assumptions made throughout, we can represent the tensor product multilevel
approximation to
\boldsymbol{f}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d)}(\boldsymbol{f})(\boldsymbol{x})=

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\{0,1\}^{d}\\
\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d)\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{1}+d}

\displaystyle\phantom{\sum}\sum_{u^{(1)}_{1}\in\{1,\dots,\lambda_{1}\}}\cdots\sum_{u^{(d)}_{1}\in\{1,\dots,\lambda_{d}\}}\sum_{k_{1}^{(1)}\leq N^{(1)}_{u_{1}^{(1)}}}\cdots\sum_{k_{1}^{(d)}\leq N^{(d)}_{u_{1}^{(d)}}}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{u_{1},k_{1}})\cdot

\displaystyle\phantom{\sum\sum\sum}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{m=u_{1}^{(j)}}^{\lambda_{j}}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(j)}_{u_{1}^{(j)},m}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(j)})\right)_{k_{1}^{(j)}}

((16))

for every
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega^{(1)}\times\cdots\times\Omega^{(d)}
.

3.2
A Nodal Representation

In the previous section, we found a representation of the operator (
(7)
) by smartly finding ways to pre-compute certain terms. However, it still has several downsides. For one, we need the same value
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{u_{1},k_{1}})
multiple times, which can be alleviated by storing all those values in an easy-to-access data structure. But more importantly, we have no advantage, if the direction-wise sites are nested, i.e., if

\displaystyle X^{(j)}_{1}\subset X^{(j)}_{2}\subset\cdots\subset X^{(j)}_{\lambda_{j,max}}.

It turns out that in this case, we can find another representation of the operator in (
(7)
). This new representation has the advantage that the outer sum is over the points in the associated sparse grid.

To do this, we need to introduce some more notation. In a nested family of sets of sites, for each point
\boldsymbol{x}\in X^{(j)}_{\lambda_{j,max}}
, there is a unique level
u(\boldsymbol{x})\in\{1,\dots,\lambda_{j,max}\}
where it first occurs. This point has position
k_{j}(\boldsymbol{x},i_{j})
in the sets
X^{(j)}_{i_{j}}
,
u(\boldsymbol{x})\leq i_{j}\leq\lambda_{j,max}
.

With this notation, we can find a new representation of the direction-wise multilevel operators.

Theorem 3.9
.

With the notation and assumptions made throughout this paper, we define

\displaystyle A_{\{u(\boldsymbol{x}),\dots,L\}}\chi_{L,k(\boldsymbol{x},L)}:=\sum_{\emptyset\neq\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{u(\boldsymbol{x}),\dots,L\}}(-1)^{\#\mathfrak{u}+1}\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{u}}\chi_{L,k(\boldsymbol{x},L)}.

Then the multilevel operator introduced in
Theorem

2.5
can be expressed as

\displaystyle A_{L}(f):=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in X_{L}}f(\boldsymbol{y})A_{\{u(\boldsymbol{y}),\dots,L\}}\chi_{L,k(\boldsymbol{y},L)}.

Using this representation of the direction-wise multilevel operators in the combination technique and easy manipulations of the sums yield the
nodal representation
of the tensor product multilevel operator.

Theorem 3.10
.

With the notation and assumption made throughout this paper, we can represent the tensor product multilevel operator
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d)}
, defined in
Definition

2.6
, as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d)}(\boldsymbol{f})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d)}}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d)\\
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\{0,1\}^{d}\\
\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\ell,d)\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{1}+d}\cdot

\displaystyle\phantom{\sum\sum}\cdot\bigotimes_{j=1}^{d}A^{(j)}_{\{u_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(j)}),\dots,\lambda_{j}\}}\chi^{(j)}_{\lambda_{j},k_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(j)},\lambda_{j})}.

Remark 3.11
.

1.

As a direct consequence, the nodal representation of the tensor product multilevel method in
Theorem

3.10
is an ideal candidate for GPU parallelization: For every point in the sparse grid, we do more or less the same computations.

2.

Again, we can pre-compute all the direction-wise multilevel approximations
A^{(j)}_{\{u_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(j)}),\dots,\lambda_{j}\}}\chi^{(j)}_{\lambda_{j},k_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(j)},\lambda_{j})}
and keep them in memory. For a single evaluation of the approximation at a point
\boldsymbol{y}=(\boldsymbol{y}^{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}^{(d)})\in\Omega^{\otimes}
we need to evaluate the stored multilevel approximations once and store them in an appropriate data structure such that getting the value
A^{(j)}_{\{u_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(j)}),\dots,\lambda_{j}\}}\chi^{(j)}_{\lambda_{j},k_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(j)},\lambda_{j}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{(j)})
is a simple lookup.

4
Numerical Examples

We will now showcase two example applications for the tensor product multilevel method.
In the first test case, the method is applied to interpolate simulation data from a shallow water simulation in space and time. There, the tensor product multilevel method is setup with a two-dimensional domain (the spatial domain of the simulation) in the first direction and a one-dimensional domain (the time) in the second direction.

The second example interpolates a seven-dimensional function on a sparse grid. The interpolated function in this case is the shear force of a cantilever beam, and the tensor product multilevel method is set up with completely isotropic directions.

4.1
Tidal Flow at the Bight of Abaco

In the first example, we apply the tensor product multilevel method to interpolate simulation data from a tidal flow simulation at the Bight of Abaco.
The tidal flow is simulated by the two-dimensional shallow water equations, given by
