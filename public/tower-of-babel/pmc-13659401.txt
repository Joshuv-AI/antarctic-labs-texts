Comment on “EHA Guidelines on management of chronic lymphocytic leukemia and Richter transformation”
Authors: Alessandra Tedeschi, Raul Cordoba, Alessandra Ferrajoli, George Follows, Anna Maria Frustaci, Anne Sophie Michallet, Talha Munir, Susan O'Brien, John F Seymour, Tait Shanafelt, Clemens Martin Wendtner, Pier Luigi Zinzani, Stephan Stilgenbauer
Published: 2026

The first European Hematology Association (EHA) Guidelines on the management of chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) and Richter's transformation have recently been published in HemaSphere, following the previous versions developed under the auspices of the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO). 1 The authors have made a substantial effort to comprehensively outline the management of CLL, encompassing diagnosis, active surveillance, supportive care, and treatment. We recognize the challenges involved in developing treatment recommendations in a rapidly evolving therapeutic landscape, characterized by a growing number of highly effective treatment options. Significant evidence gaps remain, considering the paucity of mature randomized head‐to‐head studies comparing the various targeted agent regimens. In such a rapidly evolving landscape, the manuscript combines data from heterogeneous sources, including prospective clinical trials and real‐world registries, resulting in inherent methodological limitations.

Guidelines play a key role in shaping clinical practice, reimbursement decisions, local protocols, and patient expectations. Therefore, recommendations should be as clear as possible and aligned with the appropriate level of evidence, ensuring that they are broadly applicable even in non‐referral centers. Given that many treatments prioritized in recent guidelines are based largely on expert opinion, we sought to provide considerations and proposals that may contribute to future updates and improve their alignment with the available evidence.

According to guideline methodology, each therapeutic option should be accompanied by the corresponding level of evidence and grade of recommendation. Nevertheless, some first‐line treatment statements, although reasonable and potentially clinically appropriate in certain settings, warrant clearer justification and more explicit explanation. These recommendations instead appear to be driven primarily by expert consensus and, from a methodological perspective, are not supported by high‐level evidence (Levels I–II, Grade A) considering the available prospective clinical trial data. For example, “time‐limited targeted therapies should be preferred over continuous treatment in patients without TP53 aberrations (IA)” is not currently supported by studies demonstrating the superiority of fixed‐duration over continuous treatment with novel agents. 2 Likewise, stating that continuous therapy should be avoided in patients with mutated immunoglobuline heavy chain variable region (IGHV) status (IIA) may be too definitive and may not adequately reflect the need for individualized treatment decisions. Although this molecular subgroup appears to derive relatively greater benefit from fixed‐duration therapy than patients with unmutated IGHV, 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 this should not be interpreted as indicating that outcomes with continuous therapy are poor. Continuous therapy remains an effective treatment option, and for many older patients, treatment convenience and ease of administration may be important considerations. The recommendation that acalabrutinib and zanubrutinib should be preferred over ibrutinib for continuous first‐line treatment is an extrapolation of studies in the relapsed/refractory setting 8 , 9 and is not supported by Level IA evidence.

Importantly, for the first time, these guidelines also incorporate the ESMO Magnitude of Clinical Benefit Scale for hematologic malignancies (MCBS:H). The original MCBS was developed to provide a standardized, transparent, and reproducible estimate of the magnitude of clinical benefit from anticancer therapies. Its goal was to move beyond the binary language of “positive” and “negative” trials and to distinguish statistical significance from meaningful patient benefit but not strictly to recommend a treatment. We believe that implementing and displaying the MCBS:H within the treatment algorithm carries a potential risk of misinterpretation, implying that a treatment with an MCBS:H score of 4 is inherently superior to one with a score of 3. Such an interpretation overlooks the fact that the MCBS:H is highly dependent on multiple factors, including the characteristics of the enrolled population, the comparator arm used, follow‐up duration, endpoint hierarchy, and the availability of subsequent therapies, all of which substantially influence the meaning of the score. Notably, the MCBS:H score will be inappropriately inflated by choice of an inferior comparator arm. The MBCS:H score should be calculated based on the primary endpoint of the study. Applying it to patients' subgroups (i.e., del17p, IGHV mutational status) is questionable, as these are typically not prespecified as primary objectives and are not adequately powered to support definitive conclusions. Furthermore, it should be acknowledged that not all MCBS:H evaluations have yet been completed; some pivotal trials are still awaiting formal assessment and not all the MCBS:H scores have been validated.

Compared with the previous ESMO Guidelines, patient stratification according to age/fitness is no longer considered a key driver for first‐line treatment decision‐making. Rather, TP53 and IGHV mutational status have become the principal determinants of treatment choice. 1 In the era of targeted therapies, it is indeed difficult to define clear age cut‐offs or identify a fitness assessment tool that meaningfully influences therapeutic decisions. To address this limitation, the guidelines include a figure presenting an “integrated vision of individual features to guide treatment selection.” This approach had already been proposed during the 2024 ASH Educational Session and, for the first time, integrated biological, clinical, and logistical factors into the treatment decision‐making process. 10 In the guidelines, selected organ system vulnerabilities undoubtedly represent well‐established determinants of treatment choice (i.e., cardiovascular and renal comorbidities), and this method certainly represents a genuine effort toward a more personalized and holistic approach to patient care; however, it has several important limitations. A comprehensive perspective on the management of frail patients is missing. Advanced age is associated with competing causes of mortality, altered pharmacokinetics, polypharmacy, social vulnerability, cognitive impairment, and reduced ability to adhere and tolerate prolonged or complex treatment strategies. These factors may influence treatment feasibility as much as TP53 or IGHV status influences treatment efficacy. Furthermore, therapeutic goals in this population should be more clearly defined. Beyond biological disease characteristics, it would also be valuable to explicitly describe the characteristics of the populations enrolled in the pivotal studies, allowing readers to distinguish which regimens have actually been evaluated in older patients with comorbidities and avoiding inappropriate extrapolation of trial results (i.e., acalabrutinib plus venetoclax±obinutuzumab in the AMPLIFY, a trial with a short follow‐up and addressed to a younger and fit population 7 ). It is established from cBTKi plus venetoclax studies that older patients have a higher treatment discontinuation rate, especially with triplet therapies.5, 6, 7 In addition, some of the treatment recommendations deserve further consideration. For instance, the preference for continuous BTKi over the ibrutinib–venetoclax in patients at increased risk of infections does not fully reflect the CLL17 data, which showed a plateau in infection rates after the end of combination treatment. 2

In the first‐line treatment of TP53 aberrant disease, the two next‐generation cBTKi, acalabrutinib and zanubrutinib, are grouped within the same color‐coded box, indicating the same therapeutic priority over other available options. However, the assigned level of evidence and MCBS:H score (acalabrutinib: IA, MCBS 4; zanubrutinib: IIIA, MCBS 3) may inadvertently suggest a preference for acalabrutinib. We believe that this point deserves further clarification. Acalabrutinib was evaluated in a relatively small number of patients with TP53 aberrations within a randomized trial, and efficacy analyses in this subgroup were exploratory, intended to assess the consistency of the treatment effect rather than to provide a statistically powered comparison. 11 In contrast, zanubrutinib has been evaluated in the largest prospective cohort of treatment‐naïve patients with del17p reported to date (SEQUOIA trial, Arm C). 12

In addition, the rationale for assigning ibrutinib–venetoclax a level of evidence of IIA, rather than IA, and not placing it in the same category as ibrutinib remains unclear, as the CLL17 trial showed no significant difference between the two treatment arms at 3 years in TP53 aberrant patients. 2

Finally, in this high‐risk population, the inclusion of acalabrutinib plus venetoclax in the first‐line treatment algorithm, particularly with a Level IIA recommendation, appears difficult to justify. Patients with del(17p) and/or TP53 mutation were excluded from the pivotal AMPLIFY trial leading to licensing of AV. 7 In addition, although the Phase II AVO study demonstrated promising efficacy in this high‐risk group, treatment was administered according to an minimal residual disease (MRD)‐guided strategy, which is currently outside the approved indication and has not yet been adopted as a standard therapeutic approach. 13

Fixed‐duration therapy is presented as the preferred first‐line strategy irrespective of IGHV mutational status, with the order of the boxes and shades of green, reflecting the ranking of the recommendations. We agree that treatment is progressively moving toward fixed‐duration approaches, given their many potential advantages for patients. However, the strength of this recommendation is not yet fully supported by prospective evidence and relies largely on expert opinion. This is particularly relevant for patients with unmutated IGHV, in whom continuous BTKis are downgraded in recommendation despite robust data supporting their efficacy from large prospective trials of older patients with longer follow‐up. 11 , 12 In this setting, the prioritization of fixed‐duration therapy over continuous treatment would benefit from a broader discussion that incorporates not only efficacy outcomes but also time to next treatment, treatment sequencing, clonal evolution, and the potential impact on development of resistance mutations. Furthermore, this recommendation appears difficult to reconcile with Figure 2, where continuous therapy is, conversely, strongly recommended for the same patient population. Another example concerns patients with mutated IGHV, in whom venetoclax–obinutuzumab is given clear preference by being placed in a separate box, despite the absence of evidence supporting its superiority over acalabrutinib–venetoclax or ibrutinib–venetoclax. In the same figure, indeed, one licensed therapy (continuous acalabrutinib + obinutuzumab) with robust long‐term efficacy is not even included as an option.

Finally, treatment algorithms should preferably avoid including unlicensed therapeutic strategies, such as MRD‐guided ibrutinib–venetoclax, for which superiority over the approved fixed‐duration treatment has not been proven.

Some discrepancies between the strength of the recommendations and the available evidence are also observed in the relapsed/refractory setting. For example, the assigned level of evidence for next‐generation cBTKi in patients intolerant to ibrutinib (II B) appears difficult to reconcile with the available literature, which is based exclusively on non‐randomized studies. 14 , 15 Similarly in the post‐cBTKi setting, pirtobrutinib (only broadly classified as “ncBTKi” in the algorithm) and venetoclax–rituximab are assigned the same level of evidence (IIB), despite fundamentally different supporting datasets. Pirtobrutinib is in fact backed by prospective clinical trials specifically conducted in cBTKi‐exposed patients (IA), 16 whereas evidence for venetoclax–rituximab in this setting derives primarily from clinical practice (IV A). 17 Furthermore, no MCBS:H level is listed for pirtobrutinib, which according to ESMO should be 4. In addition, there is no evidence supporting the recommendation favoring ncBTKi after prior fixed‐duration BTKi–venetoclax as stated in the text.

The guideline recommendation to consider a response to fixed‐duration therapy of a treatment‐free interval of ≥2 years as adequate appears arbitrary, as this threshold is not currently supported by prospective evidence. In this setting, no levels of evidence or MCBS:H score can be attributed to the available treatments, and any suggestion should be transparently declared as based on authors' consensus.Until more robust sequencing data become available, it may therefore be appropriate to approach treatment selection in relapsed/refractory CLL using the same principles adopted in the frontline setting, integrating disease biology, clinical characteristics, comorbidities, and patient preferences. While the guidelines emphasize reassessment of TP53 status at every relapse, equal emphasis should be placed on its pivotal role in therapeutic decision‐making.

The 2026 EHA Guidelines undoubtedly represent a major advance in the management of CLL, as they replace the dated ESMO version and, for the first time, provide a framework for a more individualized treatment approach. We hope that our observations will be considered in future updates, helping to further refine these recommendations and strengthen their alignment with the available clinical evidence for the benefit of patients.
