SARS-CoV-2 spike-armed vesicular stomatitis virus recapitulates severe COVID-19 acute respiratory distress syndrome in mice
Authors: Prarthna Karunamurthy, Alvin Tieu, Damian Chwastek, Zoe A Fisk, Nikesh Chander, Forough Jahandideh, Doreen Engelberts, Taylor Jamieson, Julia Petryk, Xiaohong He, Carolina Ilkow, Joanna Poutou, Casey Lansdell, Duncan J Stewart, Manoj M Lalu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryClinically relevant preclinical models are critical for understanding acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and developing novel therapeutics. Although SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus-based models overcome biosafety challenges associated with live coronavirus, their ability to recapitulate key ARDS features and respond to established treatment remains unclear. We developed a vesicular stomatitis virus expressing the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein (VSV-CoV2-S) for safe use in BSL-2 facilities. We evaluated its ability to induce acute lung injury (ALI) and its responsiveness to dexamethasone. Intratracheal inoculation of K18-hACE2 transgenic mice induced severe ALI characterized by alveolar damage, pulmonary hemorrhage, vascular hyperpermeability, inflammatory cytokine production, and immune cell infiltration. All inoculated transgenic mice died by day 6. Inoculated wild-type mice remained healthy. Dexamethasone attenuated clinical and histopathological ALI evidence in transgenic mice. This study confirms the translational validity of a pseudovirus-based approach, providing a robust and accessible platform to assess ALI pathophysiology and evaluate candidate therapies.Keywords: acute respiratory distress syndrome, acute lung injury, pseudovirus, COVID-19, dexamethasone

Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) can be caused by infections, trauma, and burns, and is a devastating life-threatening respiratory condition that affects millions of patients worldwide.1 The COVID-19 pandemic led to an exponential increase in the global incidence of ARDS and was a major cause of morbidity.2 Recent analyses have reported that up to 25.1% of included COVID patients reported long-term persistence or recurrence of symptoms, urging the development of more targeted therapeutic strategies.3 Clinically representative animal models that reproduce the pathophysiological mechanisms and manifestations of COVID-19 ARDS are thus crucial to better understand this condition and ultimately test new therapies.

This is particularly challenging for viral models like COVID-19, as studies often use live SARS-CoV-2, which requires costly biosafety level 3 (BSL-3) facilities that are not widely accessible. Some research groups have instead employed SARS-CoV-2 pseudoviruses to produce acute lung injury (ALI).4,5,6,7,8,9,10,11 Pseudoviruses utilize the structural core of one virus, such as a vesiculovirus (VSV), while expressing the envelope glycoproteins of another, in this case the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein.12,13,14 The envelope protein is especially critical as it mediates viral entry and determines host cell tropism. With this alteration, the SARS-CoV-2 pseudoviruses are less pathogenic, more stable, and can be handled in lower biosafety level facilities (i.e., BSL-2). This system allows for the generation of various pseudoviruses by manipulating the envelope protein alone, producing safer and more accessible platforms to study viral infections. The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the urgent need for clinically accurate preclinical models of viral ALI, hence driving our decision to validate this approach using pseudotyped SARS-CoV-2.

The majority of existing studies evaluating SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus models have yet to assess appropriate features of preclinical ALI, as defined by the American Thoracic Society (ATS).15,16 Additionally, there is a lack of investigation into the capacity of these models to recapitulate distinct pathological features of COVID-19 ARDS, such as thrombosis, or their responsiveness to standard-of-care COVID-19 ARDS treatments, such as dexamethasone.17 These studies also demonstrate limited methodological rigor, rarely reporting details on randomization or blinding. These limitations hinder the reliability and translational validity of existing pseudovirus-based preclinical approaches, particularly in the context of future viral pandemics.

In this study, we demonstrate that intratracheal inoculation of vesicular stomatitis virus expressing SARS-CoV-2 spike protein (VSV-CoV2-S) induces severe ALI in transgenic mice expressing human ACE2. This injury closely resembles many clinically relevant features of COVID-19 ARDS, such as diffuse alveolar damage, hyaline membranes, inflammation, and thrombus formation. In contrast, wild type and sham mice retained normal pulmonary architecture with no clinical or molecular signs of disease. These results mirror the published preclinical effects of live SARS-CoV-2, suggesting K18-hACE2 infection with VSV-CoV2-S represents a functional and broadly applicable animal model. Furthermore, we tested the translational validity of this model by examining responses to dexamethasone, a standard-of-care therapy for COVID-19 ARDS.17 Specifically, both preventative and rescue dosing regimens of dexamethasone reduced clinical signs of disease as well as histological lung injury in VSV-CoV2-S infected K18-hACE2 mice. Dexamethasone had no detectable impact on other molecular markers, including fibrin/fibrinogen accumulation. This is consistent with observations in patients with COVID-19 ARDS, where corticosteroids have shown limited effect on thrombotic pathways despite their anti-inflammatory effect.18 By recapitulating key clinical features of COVID-19 ARDS and confirming response to standard therapy, our study suggests that pseudotype virus models of ALI may provide an accessible and robust platform to investigate both pathophysiology and novel therapies of other virus-induced lung injuries in the future.

Results

Dose-response analysis of VSV-CoV2-S in K18-hACE2 transgenic mice

A dose-escalating study was conducted to determine the VSV-CoV2-S dose necessary to induce severe ALI in K18-hACE2 mice (Figure 1A). Animals were randomized to receive a single intratracheal injection of VSV-CoV2-S at 1E5, 1E6, 1E7, or 2.35E7 pfu (maximum feasible dose based on viral stock produced), or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) as a vehicle control. Animals were monitored for disease severity score (DSS) (Figure 1B), weight (Figure 1C), and internal temperature (Figure 1D) at the time points indicated, with a planned end-study at 5 days post-virus inoculation. One mouse died peri-procedurally (intent to treat with 1E6 pfu), and one mouse received only 1E7 pfu as opposed to the target dose of 2.35E7 pfu due to limitations in virus supply. This mouse was subsequently analyzed as a subject in the 1E7 pfu experimental group.

Physiological outcomes

There was a transient increase in DSSs in both PBS and pseudovirus-administered mice at 6 h post-inoculation, likely due to anesthesia and the intubation procedure (Figure 1B). All mice normalized to baseline wellness by day 2. By 5 days post-inoculation, the groups receiving the two highest viral doses exhibited a second increase in DSS compared with other groups, with statistical significance observed between the 2.35E7 pfu group and PBS controls (p < 0.05) (Figure 1B). The two highest VSV-CoV2-S doses also resulted in a marked decline in weight compared with PBS controls (Figure 1C). Mice receiving 1E7 pfu of the pseudovirus demonstrated a notable decrease in internal temperature on day 5 as well (p < 0.05) (Figure 1D).

Cellular and molecular outcomes of acute lung injury

BALF protein and total cell concentration were significantly increased in the 1E7 and 2.35E7 pfu groups, respectively (p < 0.05) (Figures 1E and 1F). IL-6, a primary mediator of pulmonary inflammation in ARDS, was measured in both BALF and plasma of mice. There were no increases in IL-6 levels in BALF of mice receiving PBS or VSV-CoV2-S at doses of 1E5 or 1E6 pfu, while a trend toward higher IL-6 concentrations was seen with the 1E7 and 2.35E7 pfu doses (Figure 1G). Differences in plasma concentrations of IL-6 were not significant across any experimental groups with no detection of IL-6 in any groups other than 2.35E7 (data not shown), potentially indicating a localized lung injury response within the first 5 days after VSV-CoV2-S infection.

Histopathological lung injury

Mice that received 1E7 pfu or 2.35E7 pfu of VSV-CoV2-S exhibited severe interstitial pneumonia with diffuse alveolar damage at day 5 (Figure 1H). Lung sections revealed interstitial, peribronchial, and perivascular infiltration by immune cells with areas of hyperplastic type II pneumocytes and alveolar hemorrhage. K18-hACE2 mice receiving 1E7 or 2.35E7 pfu of VSV-CoV2-S demonstrated a trend toward higher lung injury scores compared with the other groups (Figure 1I). K18-hACE2 transgenic mice receiving 1E5 pfu or 1E6 pfu of VSV-CoV2-S or PBS showed no evidence of ALI. Together, these results suggest that the two highest viral doses resulted in notable lung injury by 5 days after VSV-CoV2-S administration.

Intratracheal inoculation of VSV-CoV2-S induces a fatal ALI response in K18-hACE2 transgenic mice

Based on the dose-response analysis, 1E7 pfu was identified as the lowest dose of VSV-CoV2-S capable of inducing severe lung injury by day 5 in K18-hACE2 mice. To investigate the effects of VSV-CoV2-S on longer-term ALI outcomes, we conducted a survival study with a predefined endpoint on day 10. Both K18-hACE2 transgenic and wild-type mice were used to confirm that hACE2 receptor expression is required for VSV-CoV2-S binding and subsequent induction of lung injury. The virus was administered to all mice on day 0, and the animals were monitored daily for wellness score, weight, and temperature. Mice were euthanized if they reached endpoint criteria or at day 10 post-inoculation (Figure 2A). One wild-type mouse died peri-procedurally on day 0, and one transgenic mouse did not receive VSV-CoV2-S due to limitations in the virus supply.

At day 6 post-VSV-CoV2-S inoculation, K18-hACE2 mice exhibited a significant increase in DSS (p < 0.0001) (Figure 2B), which was associated with marked declines in both weight (Figure 2C) and temperature (Figure 2D) (both p < 0.0001). In contrast, wild-type animals showed no evidence of lung injury or systemic infection, reflected by minimal change in DSS, weight, and temperature when compared with their baseline (Figures 2B–2D). Individual domains of wellness score assessments comparing both transgenic and wild-type mice are detailed in Figure S1. Given the substantial deterioration in DSSs, weight, and temperature, the K18-hACE2 mice were humanely euthanized earlier on day 6 than planned on day 10 (Figure 2E). The survival of wild-type mice inoculated with VSV-CoV2-S demonstrates that hACE2 receptor expression is required for VSV-CoV2-S binding and development of lethal lung injury.

Severe ALI response in K18-hACE2 mice

Lung cellular infiltration and vascular permeability

Following VSV-CoV2-S inoculation, BALF cell concentration was significantly higher in K18-hACE2 mice compared with wild-type mice on day 6 (p < 0.01) (Figure 2F). BALF protein concentration in K18-hACE2 mice on day 6 was also notably higher than that of wild-type mice (p < 0.0001) (Figure 2G). The individual components of the histological scoring system are detailed in Figure S1. These findings suggest that intratracheal administration of VSV-CoV2-S causes significant pulmonary vascular injury and hyperpermeability, contingent upon interaction with the hACE2 receptor.

Cytokine markers of lung injury

A multiplex panel of cytokines was assessed in the BALF and plasma of K18-hACE2 transgenic and wild-type mice to investigate both pulmonary and systemic inflammation. At day 6 following VSV-CoV2-S inoculation, both plasma (p < 0.001) and BALF (p < 0.01) concentrations of IL-6 were markedly elevated in K18-hACE2 transgenic mice compared with wild-type controls (Figures 2H and 2I). Moreover, BALF concentrations of IL-6 were 25-fold greater than plasma. These findings are consistent with a severe localized lung inflammatory response induced by intratracheal VSV-CoV2-S, only in K18-hACE2 transgenic mice.

Histopathological lung injury

Histological assessment of whole lung sections from wild-type mice suggested no pulmonary injury at day 6 after VSV-CoV2-S inoculation (Figure 2J). In contrast, K18-hACE2 mice at day 6 exhibited fulminant lung inflammation and injury (Figures 2K–2O). Wild-type mice showed uniformly thin and normal appearance of alveolar walls, consistent with healthy lung architecture, despite receiving a high 1E7 pfu dose of VSV-CoV2-S. In contrast, lung sections of K18-hACE2 transgenic mice showed evidence of diffuse alveolar damage, alveolar septal thickening, interstitial pneumonia with type II pneumocyte hyperplasia, and pulmonary hemorrhage (Figure 2K). Thrombus formation was evident (Figures 2L and 2M) along with signs of vasculitis (Figure 2O), which are all features described in severe COVID-19-associated ARDS. Perivascular, peribronchial, and interstitial infiltration by mononuclear cells, including histiocytes, lymphocytes, and alveolar macrophages were frequently observed. In some mice, bronchial epithelial cells were swollen, degenerated, and desquamated with cytoplasmic blebbing (Figure 2N). VSV-CoV2-S inoculation also resulted in the production of protein exudates (e.g., fibrin strands) and deposition of hyaline membranes, hallmark features of clinical ARDS (Figure 2O).15 Histological evidence of lung injury was assessed using a standardized scoring system. K18-hACE2 mice were found to have a markedly elevated lung injury score compared with wild-type mice on day 6 (p < 0.0001) (Figure 2I). These findings align with a severe ALI phenotype and were consistently observed across all K18-hACE2 mice treated with 1E7 pfu of VSV-CoV2-S.

Off-target brain injury

Given that wild-type VSV has recognized neurotropism,19,20 we investigated whether K18-hACE2 mice euthanized on day 6 exhibited any evidence of neural pathology or the presence of cleaved caspase 3 (CC3)+ cells. In three regions (the cortex, hippocampus, and olfactory bulb), histological assessments revealed no evidence of neural lesions in both wild type and K18-hACE2 mice euthanized on day 6 following VSV-CoV2-S administration (Figure S2A). Assessment for the number of CC3+ cells demonstrated no significant differences between K18-hACE2 transgenic and wild-type mice, across all three regions (Figures S2B–S2D). These findings are again consistent with a selective lung injury induced by intratracheal delivery of VSV-CoV2-S.

Dexamethasone treatment attenuates features of ALI in VSV-CoV2-S-infected K18-hACE2 mice

To evaluate the predictive validity of this ALI model, response to dexamethasone, a standard-of-care COVID-19 ARDS treatment, was assessed. K18-hACE2 mice inoculated with VSV-CoV2-S received daily IP injections of dexamethasone starting on either day 0 (preventative group) or day 3 (when mice began showing clinical signs of deterioration) with an endpoint of day 6 (Figure 3A). VSV-CoV2-S infected K18-hACE2 mice (VSV-CoV2-S group) and K18-hACE2 healthy mice receiving intratracheal PBS (sham group) were used as infection and treatment controls.

Physiological outcomes

The DSS of all VSV-CoV2-S infected groups increased by day 6 (Figure 3B). Both preventative (p < 0.05) and rescue dexamethasone groups had reduced scores compared with the VSV-CoV2-S untreated controls. In particular, disease severity in the preventative dexamethasone group reduced to levels comparable with uninfected sham controls (Figure 3B). VSV-CoV2-S untreated control mice had lost significantly more body weight by day 6 when compared with preventative and rescue dexamethasone-treated mice (Figure 3C). Changes in internal temperature by day 6 were not significant with either dexamethasone treatments compared with the VSV-CoV2-S untreated group (Figure 3D). Sham mice were asymptomatic throughout the experiment. Preliminary results demonstrated that DSS, weight loss, and temperature loss were comparable between male and female mice in all experimental groups (Figures S3A–S3C).

Histopathological lung injury

Dexamethasone prophylaxis and rescue treatment reduced histological lung injury scores in infected mice (p < 0.0001, reduced immune cell infiltration and alveolar septal thickening) (Figures 3E–3I). Histological analysis comparing male and female mice in each experimental group revealed no sex-dependent differences (Figure S3D). Preventative treatment was associated with a lower lung injury score compared with the rescue group (p < 0.01) (Figure 3E). These findings indicate that dexamethasone administration to K18-hACE2 mice infected with VSV-CoV2-S mitigates histological lung injury and clinical signs of ALI, which is consistent with its efficacy in humans.

Immunofluorescence analysis of thrombotic markers

Given the presence of thrombi in lung tissues, we further investigated the molecular thrombotic markers, such as fibrin/fibrinogen, CD41, and neutrophil elastase to explore underlying biological processes contributing to clot formation. VSV-CoV2-S infected mice exhibited significantly increased fibrin/fibrinogen (p < 0.001) and neutrophil elastase (p < 0.05) compared with PBS controls, with no statistically significant difference in platelets (Figure S4). Dexamethasone (preventative or rescue) did not significantly alter fibrin/fibrinogen or platelet content (Figures 4A–4D) but reduced neutrophil elastase, although not significantly compared with untreated VSV-CoV2-S mice. These data indicate that VSV-CoV2-S infection promotes fibrin deposition and increases neutrophil elastase, with dexamethasone partially attenuating the latter.

Pulmonary vascular permeability and inflammation

Pulmonary vascular permeability (BALF protein concentration) was significantly elevated in the VSV-CoV2-S infected group compared with the sham group (p < 0.01) (Figure 5A). Multiplex cytokine analysis revealed significant increases in IL-6, TNF-α, MCP-1, IFNγ, IL-4, and IL-10 (p < 0.05, 0.01) in the BALF of the VSV-CoV2-S group compared with sham animals (Figures 5B–5G) but not plasma (Figure S5), reflecting localized pulmonary inflammation. Preventative dexamethasone treatment significantly reduced levels of IL-10 compared with untreated VSV-CoV2-S mice (p < 0.01), with consistent trends toward reduction in other cytokines as well as vascular permeability. VSV-CoV2-S infection did not alter plasma or BALF cytokine levels of GM-CSF, IL-12p70, IL-2, and IL-1β (Figures S5 and S6).

To validate the spike specificity, an exploratory vaccination experiment was also conducted to isolate the effects of the spike protein in inducing disease. Administration of a vaccine targeting the S spike of the VSV-CoV2-S pseudovirus (TOH-Vac1)21 resulted in a strong trend toward decreased DSSs (p = 0.06) and a pattern for reduced temperature loss (although insignificant, p = 0.27) compared with unvaccinated controls (Figures S7A and S7B). Neutralizing antibody responses were assessed using a pseudovirus neutralization assay (Figure S7C). NT50 was defined as the reciprocal serum dilution achieving 50% inhibition of VSV-CoV2-S infection. All unvaccinated mice (n = 5) had titers below the limit of detection, indicating an absence of quantifiable neutralizing activity. On the other hand, mice in the vaccinated group (n = 3) exhibited detectable neutralizing titers, demonstrating increased spike-specific antibody responses.

Discussion

In this study, we present a practical approach for studying viral-induced ALI using a pseudotyped vesicular stomatitis virus bearing the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein (VSV-CoV2-S). This approach uses the core genome of a lower risk virus (e.g., VSV) and the envelope protein of a higher-risk virus of interest (e.g., SARS-CoV-2) to produce a pseudovirus (e.g., VSV-CoV2-S).12 The envelope protein is of particular interest as it is responsible for recognizing cell surface receptors (e.g., ACE2 for SARS-CoV-2) and mediating viral entry.13 Compared with wild-type viruses, the use of pseudoviruses allows for handling in lower biosafety level facilities, increased stability, ease of concentration, and rapid modification when wild-type mutations emerge.13,14 While this model has been developed to explore the pathogenesis of SARS-CoV-2 specifically, its primary utility lies in its capacity to serve as an adaptable method to study a variety of respiratory viral infections in the future.

While several other groups have investigated pseudotyped viruses for COVID-19, most studies have focused on in vitro neutralization.22,23,24,25,26,27 In vivo applications remain limited.4,5,6,7,8,9,10,11 As recommended by the ATS, an experimental model needs to demonstrate alterations in three of the following four key categories to qualify as a model of ALI: histological evidence of tissue injury, disruption of the alveolar–capillary barrier, presence of an inflammatory response, and physiological dysfunction.16 In studies that have used VSV or lentivirus backbones to generate pseudoviruses expressing the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, a majority fail to meet this criterion, thus reducing the applicability of these models to study ALI. Notably, no study has confirmed the predictive validity of these models by demonstrating an in vivo response to established standard-of-care treatments for ALI, limiting their utility as both mechanistic tools and platforms for novel therapeutic evaluation. Our study addresses these critical gaps, enhancing the translational relevance of pseudotyped virus models.

Our experiments study the effects of the pseudotyped SARS-CoV-2 with the original spike protein as it remains most relevant for modeling severe disease, though less virulent spike proteins from newer variants should be explored in future work. Through implementation of rigorous methodology (e.g., randomization and blinding), we demonstrate that intratracheal VSV-CoV2-S inoculation in K18-hACE2 transgenic mice produced a lung injury phenotype closely recapitulating that of live SARS-CoV-2 infection. Firstly, both viruses exhibit high hACE2 receptor binding specificity, as wild-type mice infected with either virus do not develop any ALI features.28,29,30,31,32,33 Secondly, the severity of histological lung injury and clinical progression of disease following VSV-CoV2-S inoculation, lethal by day 6, parallels that seen with 1E5 pfu of original SARS-CoV-2.29,31,34,35,36,37 Hence, the pulmonary disease induced by intratracheal 1E7 pfu of VSV-CoV2-S may be comparable to 1E5 pfu of intranasal SARS-CoV-2. Our pseudovirus, however, can be safely handled in standard BSL-2 facilities, offering a more accessible and convenient approach for studying respiratory viral infections such as COVID-19. Furthermore, this model’s modular design allows substitution of the spike protein, potentially enabling its adaptation to study other respiratory viruses. However, such adaptations will require careful validation to confirm their translational relevance and utility.

Importantly, we demonstrate that our VSV-CoV2-S model reproduces critical pathological features of clinical COVID-19-associated ARDS. It exhibits a pulmonary cytokine storm characterized by an acute and uncontrolled release of pro-inflammatory mediators (IL-6, TNF, MCP-1, and IFNγ), known to drive immune cell activation, cell death, and tissue damage.38,39 Simultaneously, increased anti-inflammatory cytokines, IL-4 and IL-10, suggest a compensatory regulatory response to the heightened inflammation. We also observed increased BALF protein levels, indicative of increased alveolar-capillary barrier permeability.15,16

Histopathological features of COVID-19-induced ARDS observed in patient autopsies, including interstitial pneumonia with bilateral diffuse alveolar damage, desquamation of lung epithelium, and hyaline membrane formation,40,41 were also present in our mouse model. Moreover, a comprehensive review of 263 SARS-CoV-2 lung infection cases reported reactive pneumocyte hyperplasia, leukocytic infiltration of the interstitium, and extensive exudative/proliferative alveolitis,42 all of which were also seen in our study. Vascular pathology, commonly reported in severe COVID-19, was also evident in our mouse model. Histological analysis revealed capillary congestion, vasculitis, and fibrin deposition within the pulmonary vasculature samples of both COVID-19 patients43 and our VSV-CoV2-S mouse model. These features align with the hyperinflammatory and hypercoagulable state induced by COVID-19, characterized by platelet activation, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, endothelial cell damage, excessive fibrin deposition, and subsequent clot formation,44 all contributing to microthrombotic complications, disease severity, and mortality.44 Immunofluorescence analysis further supports this, as increased fibrin/fibrinogen deposition and elevated neutrophil elastase levels were observed in VSV-CoV2-S infected K18-hACE2 mice, indicative of neutrophil-driven immunothrombosis. These findings ensure that our model meets the widely accepted ATS criteria for ALI, specifically through the use of a validated histological score, BALF protein level alterations, and BALF cytokine analysis.

Analysis of transgenic mouse brains in our study depicted no evidence of off-target neurotoxicity, which is commonly seen from preclinical VSV infections. This may be because of the removal of surface glycoproteins that normally facilitate VSV infection or due to the intratracheal route of inoculation rather than intranasal, which bypasses nasal sensory neurons that act as a conduit for brain infection. Nonetheless, the close similarities in pulmonary histopathology with minimal off-target or systemic infection in our VSV-CoV2-S lung injury model support its use as a relevant model of severe COVID-19-induced ALI. Future work should further explore the viral kinetics of VSV-CoV2-S to provide greater insights into disease progression and host-pathogen interactions within this model. Delineation of the individual contributions of the spike protein and the VSV backbone may provide additional mechanistic insight into this model as well. Notably, the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein has been shown to induce pathological responses in vivo,45,46,47 whereas published studies using VSVΔG controls have reported little evidence of pathogenicity following administration of the backbone alone.48,49,50,51

Importantly, dexamethasone treatment in this model attenuates several key features of ALI. Dexamethasone is a synthetic glucocorticoid known to have potent anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects. Specifically, it binds to the glucocorticoid receptor, leading to the reduction in pro-inflammatory cytokine gene expression and limiting the activation and proliferation of inflammatory immune cells.52 In the context of ARDS, it has been assessed in numerous clinical trials, improving parameters such as ventilator-free days and mortality, and is now a standard-of-care therapy for COVID-19-induced ARDS.17,53,54 In our model, a beneficial response to this treatment was observed, particularly with a reduction in signs of disease score and histological lung injury. Preventative dosing exhibited stronger therapeutic effects, highlighting the importance of early intervention in mitigating disease severity. Preliminary data on the inflammatory biomarkers showed a trend toward reduced cytokine levels, specifically IL-6 and IFNγ, in both plasma and BALF in the preventative treatment group, approaching levels comparable to sham controls. However, due to high data variability, these findings require further validation in future studies. The modest effect size may reflect the transient and highly dynamic nature of cytokine production, particularly at our study endpoint of day 6. The overall suppression of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines with dexamethasone has been previously reported with SARS-CoV-2 infection.55 Our findings showed dexamethasone treatment to have less effect on thrombotic markers than inflammatory markers, which mirrors what is observed clinically.18 Additionally, these studies included both male and female mice; however, the sample sizes were not well balanced. Though significant sex differences were not observed, future studies should aim for a more balanced sex distribution to ensure enough power to better assess any sex-specific effects noted in other models of ALI.56 Ultimately, this confirmation of response to dexamethasone is critical as it supports predictive and translational validity, improving the reliability of not only this specific model but the pseudotyped virus approach overall.

Lastly, administration of the TOH-Vac1 vaccine induced neutralizing antibody responses against the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein and was associated with lower disease severity and reduced temperature loss compared with unvaccinated controls. This supports a central role for the spike protein in contributing to disease severity in the VSV-CoV2-S model. Collectively, these findings highlight the utility of a pseudovirus-induced approach for studying viral infection.

In this study, we demonstrated that intratracheal delivery of the VSV-CoV2-S pseudotyped virus resulted in severe lung injury and pulmonary inflammation in K18-hACE2 transgenic mice. Molecular and histological findings of ALI features, along with declining weight and temperatures, mirrored the reported effects of live SARS-CoV-2 administration in K18-hACE2 transgenic mice. Specific hallmarks of lung injury, such as hyaline membrane formation and thrombosis, were also detected, mirroring clinical COVID-19-induced ARDS. Notably, we also demonstrated that dexamethasone treatment attenuates disease severity and histological lung injury, confirming the translational validity of this model. Thus, we established a valuable and accessible system for studying the pathophysiology of viral ALI, with potential applicability beyond SARS-CoV-2 to other respiratory viral infections.

Limitations of the study

While described in greater detail in the discussion, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, further characterization of the viral kinetics of VSV-CoV2-S and isolation of the individual effects of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein and VSV backbone are needed. This will provide greater insight into disease progression as well as host-pathogen interactions. In addition, continued evaluation of this pseudovirus platform using other respiratory viruses and SARS-CoV-2 spike variants will be critical to establish the generalizability of our findings. Finally, although both male and female mice were included in this study, the cohorts were not adequately powered to thoroughly assess sex-specific effects. Hence, future analysis is warranted to better define sex-specific differences in disease pathogenesis and treatment responses.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

Male (n = 82) and female (n = 11) hemizygous transgenic mice (8–10 weeks old) expressing the human angiotensin–converting enzyme II (hACE2) receptor under the human keratin 18 (K18) promoter were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (Stock No.: 034860, B6.Cg-Tg(K18-ACE2)2Prlmn/J) (Maine, USA).58 Wildtype (non-carrier) C57BL/6 mice (n = 24) were obtained from the same colony (Jackson Laboratory, Stock No.: 000664). Uninfected sham K18-hACE2 and PBS-treated VSV-CoV2-S K18-hACE2 mice were used as controls. Genotypes of mice were confirmed by PCR using tail snips.

Ethics

The animal study protocol was approved by the Animal Care Committee of the University of Ottawa (protocol no. OHRI-4358). The Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines 2.0 were used to guide reporting of this study (File S1).59

Experimental overview

For the purpose of this study, three cohorts of mice were used. In the first cohort (N = 20), a dose-response experiment was conducted to determine the lowest concentration of VSV-CoV2-S that would induce a severe phenotype within 5 days. The second cohort involved both wild type (N = 14) and hACE2 transgenic mice (N = 14) to evaluate the role of hACE2 in VSV-CoV2-S-mediated lung injury and to evaluate survival up to day 10. In the third cohort (N = 34 male and 11 female), the effects of dexamethasone, a standard of care for ARDS, were assessed with both preventative and rescue dosing regimens.

Method details

VSV-CoV2-S pseudotyped virus production

VSV-CoV2-S was bioengineered and rescued as previously reported, specifically using the S gene of the SARS-CoV-2 isolate Wuhan-Hu-1 (GenBank: MN908947.3).21,57 Vero E6 cells were seeded in 1700 cm3 roller bottles (Corning) at approximately 5E7 cells per bottle in 250 mL Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, supplemented with 10% FBS and 25 mM HEPES) and incubated at 37°C for 2 days. Cells were infected with VSV-Spike at a multiplicity of infection of 0.05 and incubated at 34°C for approximately 48 h (or until ∼90% cytopathic effect was evident). Supernatants were first pre-cleared by centrifuging at 1500 rpm for 15 min at 4°C (Beckman Coulter, Allegra X-14R benchtop centrifuge) and then filtered using a 0.22 μm polyethersulfone filter (500 mL Stericup polyethersulfone filter system; EMD Millipore). The virus was concentrated by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 2 h at 4°C (JLA 16.250 rotor; Beckman Coulter Avanti JE centrifuge). The concentrated virus was resuspended in cold phosphate buffered saline (PBS, 300 μL/roller bottle) and stored at −80°C.

VSV titer by plaque assay

Viral titers were determined by serial dilution plaque assays. Vero E6 cells (ATCC) were plated in 6-well plates (9E5 cells per well) and incubated overnight at 37°C. A 10-fold serial dilution for each virus was completed by adding virus stock to serum-free DMEM in a total volume of 1 mL per dilution. Media was removed from the wells, and 500 μL of dilutions 10−3 to 10−8 were added to each well. After 45 min of incubation at 37°C (with rocking every 15 min), inoculum was removed by aspirating and replaced with a 1:1 mixture of 1% agarose and DMEM (2× concentrated with 20% FBS) overlay and incubated at 34°C. After 48 h of incubation at 34°C, plates were fixed in a 3:1 mixture of methanol:acetic acid for 1 h at room temperature. The overlay was removed and plates were washed gently with water, followed by staining with crystal violet (0.1% crystal violet in 80% MeOH in water) for approximately 20 min at room temperature. After staining, plates were washed twice with water and left to dry overnight. Viral titers were calculated using the following equation with plaque counts (plaque forming units, pfu) from wells containing 10 to 100 plaques: Titer (pfu/mL) = (Number of plaques × Dilution factor)/Volume plated (mL).

Intratracheal inoculation of VSV-CoV2-S

Mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of 120/10 mg of ketamine/xylazine per kg, orally intubated with a sterile 22-gauge catheter, and administered intratracheal VSV-CoV2-S resuspended in 50 μL of PBS. The following doses were assessed in the dose-response experiment: 1E5, 1E6, 1E7, and 2.35E7 pfu. Subsequent experiments used only the 1E7 pfu dose to induce the ALI model. All animal experiments, observational outcomes, and tissue collection were conducted in a BSL-2 facility at the University of Ottawa according to recommendations by the Animal Care and Veterinary Services for safe laboratory handling of pseudotyped VSV.

Dexamethasone treatment

All animals in either preventative or rescue dosing regimens received 10 mg/kg of dexamethasone daily intraperitoneally (IP), starting at day 0 or day 3, respectively. Mice receiving the preventative dosage of dexamethasone were allowed to fully recover from anesthesia and the viral induction procedure before dexamethasone treatment.

Animal observational outcomes

Observational outcomes including weight, temperature, disease severity score, and survival of mice were recorded at baseline, 6 h after infection, and daily. Weight and temperature were reported as absolute values and percent change from baseline. Internal temperature was assessed using a rectal probe thermometer. The disease status of mice was quantified using a “Disease Severity Score” that combines the Murine Sepsis Score60 and the Murine Grimace Scale61 to feature 8 variables: Fur Appearance, Level of Consciousness, Activity, Response to Stimulus, Eye Appearance, Respiration Rate, Respiration Quality, and Facial Grimace Score. All variables were scored from 0 to 4 except for the facial grimace score (0–2). A cumulative score of 21 or higher indicated an impending endpoint. This composite scoring system was justified based on our Delphi consensus study of surrogate humane endpoints for ALI.62 Further details regarding scoring of each variable can be found in File S2. A priori parameters for mice endpoint included weight loss >20% from baseline, internal temperature <34°C, and signs of distress through disease severity score or observation (e.g., severe respiratory distress, piloerection, pronounced hunched posture, and dehydration).

Measurements of BALF cell count, protein concentration and cytokine analysis

Blood was collected through the inferior vena cava, centrifuged at 2000 g, and the plasma was separated and subsequently stored at −80°C. The right lung was lavaged three times with 500 μL of saline to collect bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF). BALF cell concentration was determined using a CellDrop automated cell counter (DeNovix, Delaware, USA). BALF was then centrifuged at 800 g to pellet cells, and the supernatant was collected and stored at −80°C.

Lung vascular permeability was assessed via BALF protein concentration measured using the DC protein assay (Bio-Rad, California, USA). IL-6 concentrations in BALF and plasma from dose-response experiments were measured using a murine cytokine-specific ELISA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). In experiments comparing transgenic and wildtype mice, serum and BALF concentrations of cytokines GM-CSF, IFNγ, IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-7, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-13, IL-17A, KC/CXCL1, LIX, MCP-1, MIP-2, and TNF-α were determined using the Mouse High Sensitivity T cell 18-Plex Discovery Assay Array (MDHSTC18, Eve Technologies). In experiments comparing sham, VSV, and dexamethasone treated animals, plasma and BALF cytokines (GM-CSF, IFNγ, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12p70, MCP-1, MIP-2, and TNF-α) were measured using the Mouse Cytokine Proinflammatory Focused 10-plex Discovery Assay Array (MDF10, Eve Technologies).

Lung histology

Two separate midsections of the left lung were used for histopathological analysis. Lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sliced into 5 μm-thick sections with a microtome (Leica Microsystems RM2255, Germany). Lung sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (Abcam, Cat#ab245880). Lung section images were acquired by PANNORAMIC DESK scanoscope (3DHISTECH, Hungary) and analyzed using CaseViewer (3DHISTECH, Hungary). Five random high-power fields (40× magnification) for each mouse were analyzed for histological lung injury. Scoring was conducted in a randomized and blinded fashion, using the scoring system suggested in the ATS ALI criteria (Figure S8).15 In brief, five parameters were considered, including intra-alveolar cell infiltration, cell infiltration into interstitial space, hyaline membranes, proteinaceous debris in airspace (e.g., fibrin strands), and alveolar septal thickening. Within each field, each parameter was given a score of 0, 1, or 2 depending on the severity of the ALI feature. The final lung injury score per mouse was calculated by totalling and averaging the scores across 5 fields, with a maximum potential score of 1 per animal.

Immunofluorescence

Mouse lung histology tissue was used to investigate markers of lung injury using immunofluorescence. 5 μm-thick sections of lung were deparaffinized using 2 changes of 100% xylene, 2 changes each of 100%, 90%, and 70% EtOH. Tissue was quenched to minimize autofluorescence using a 3% H2O2 (VWR, Cat#BD7690-1) in H2O solution for 10 min. Antigen retrieval was conducted using a sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0, 1.0M) and microwaved for 10 min. The tissue was blocked using a solution of 5% normal goat serum (Vector Laboratories, Cat#S-1000), 1% BSA (Thermo Scientific, Cat#23210), and 1x PBS with 0.25% Triton x-100 (Thermo Scientific, Cat#T8787-100). Tissue was incubated overnight in primary antibody at RT and blocking buffer. The tissue was incubated in secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit 488 (Invitrogen, Cat#A-11008, 1:250) and DAPI (Invitrogen, Cat#D1306, 1:5000) for 1 h at RT, then coverslipped using Fluoromount-G Mounting Medium (Invitrogen, Cat#00-4958-02). Slides were stored at 4°C until imaging was conducted. Sections were washed in 1X PBS 3 times for 5 min in between quenching, antigen retrieval, blocking, primary incubation, secondary incubation, and coverslipping steps. The following primary antibodies were used: CD41 (Invitrogen, Cat#PA5-22307, 1:500), Fibrin/Fibrinogen (Abcam, Cat#ab34269, 1:100), and Neutrophil Elastase (Abcam, Cat#ab131260, 1:500).

Confocal microscopy and image analysis

Images were acquired using the Zeiss LSM800 AxioObserver Z1 at the Cell Biology and Image Acquisition core at the University of Ottawa. z stack images were taken at 20× magnification (0.8NA, air) and exported as.czi files. Images were analyzed using FIJI. z stack images were projected at maximum intensity, then the “measure” function was used to determine mean gray value. To determine platelet counts, the “3D object counter” function was used on FIJI, and objects over the threshold of 50 were counted. 3 different fields were imaged and analyzed per lung.

Mouse genotyping

The genotypes of wildtype and hemizygous K18-hACE2 mice were determined by PCR. Tail snips were collected from mice and processed with the REDExtract-N-AMP Tissue PCR Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, USA) as per the manufacturer’s instructions to isolate genomic DNA. Briefly, tail snips were incubated in a 4:1 mix of Extraction Solution and Tissue Preparation Solution for 10 min at room temperature, followed by samples being heated to 95°C for 3 min. Heating was followed by the addition of Neutralization Solution B to each sample. The PCR was run using the isolated genomic DNA, the REDExtract-N-Amp PCR Reaction Mix, and a 1.3 μM mix of the following primers: Transgene Forward: 5′ - GAC CCC TGA GGG TTT CAT ATA G – 3′, Wild Type Forward: 5′ - AAG TTG GAG AAG ATG CTG AAA GA – 3′, Reverse (Common): 5′ - CAC CAA CAC AGT TTC CCA AC – 3′. PCR cycling parameters were as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, annealing at 62°C for 1 min, and extension at 72°C for 1 min. Cycling was followed by extension at 72°C for 2 min with amplicons electrophoresed in a 1.2% agarose gel.

Vaccination experiment

Male K18-hACE2 mice (4 weeks old) received either saline (n = 5) or TOH-Vac121 (n = 5) intramuscularly on days 0 and 21 in a prime/boost vaccination regimen. VSV-CoV2-S was administered intratracheally on day 29, and mice were endpointed on day 36 as previously described. Disease severity scores and temperatures were recorded from day 29 to day 36. Serum was collected through the saphenous vein on day 36 for analysis with the neutralisation assay.

Neutralisation assay

Vero E6 cells were seeded in 96-well plates with 40,000 cells per well at the time of infection. Serum from mice was diluted in a 96-well plate at a 1:10 dilution in serum-free DMEM, and a serial 1-in-2 dilution series was performed. Pseudovirus co-encoded with eGFP was then added to the serum with an equal volume of serum-free DMEM for a final dilution of 2000 pfu per well. They were then incubated for 1 h at 37°C. Media was then replaced with 60 μL of the pseudovirus/serum solution and incubated further for 1 h at 37°C. Carboxy-methylcellulose (CMC) in DMEM (supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum) was used to top up the wells to reach a final concentration of 3% CMC. They were then incubated for 24 h at 34°C. GFP foci were then imaged and quantified using a Cellomics ArrayScan VTI HCS Reader.

Brain histology

Brain tissue was assessed in three different regions: the cortex, hippocampus, and olfactory bulb. The tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sliced into 5 μm-thick sections with a microtome (Leica Microsystems, Germany). These sections were assessed for the presence of neural lesions and number of cleaved caspase-3 positive (CC3+) cells, both indicators of tissue damage. To examine for neural lesions, lung sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin, while the measurement of CC3+ cells was performed via immunohistochemistry using an anti-CC3 antibody (1:500 dilution, Cell Signaling Technology Cat#9661). All brain section images were acquired by PANNORAMIC DESK scanoscope (3DHISTECH, Hungary) and analyzed at 40× magnification using CaseViewer (3DHISTECH, Hungary).

Blinding

Researchers conducting the daily wellness checks (observing disease severity score, weight, and temperature) and administering treatments were blinded to the experimental group allocations of the animals. Animals were randomly numbered and assigned to experimental groups prior to disease induction and treatment administration. One unblinded researcher prepared syringes based on weight data, ensuring that the content in the treatment and vehicle syringes looked identical. The unblinded researcher was also not involved in any other steps of the experimental procedures or outcome assessments. All downstream outcome analyses were also conducted in a blinded manner.

Randomization

Animals were randomized using a computer-generated randomizer for all experiments.

Sample size calculations

Sample sizes were determined based on feasibility (cost and resource availability), as this was an exploratory study.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

Data are represented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise stated. Statistical analysis was completed using Student’s t test, one-way ANOVA (>2 treatment groups), or two-way ANOVA (for two categorical variables, e.g., time and dose) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.1 for MacOS; GraphPad Software). p-value < 0.05 was considered significant. For multiplex data, Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons was applied and p < 0.0026 was considered significant.
