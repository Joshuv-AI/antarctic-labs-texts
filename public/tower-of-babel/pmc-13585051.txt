Comparative Evaluation of the Effectiveness of Intrapulpal Crushed Ice and Intrapulpal Local Anesthesia during Pulpectomy in Primary Molars
Authors: Snehal D Jagtap, Nupur Ninawe, Ritesh R Kalaskar, Nidhi Sharma, Utkarsha S Kadam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAims and backgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of intrapulpal crushed ice compared to intrapulpal lidocaine as a supplementary anesthetic during pulpectomy in primary second molars of children aged 5–10 years. The goal was to assess whether crushed ice could serve as a less painful, effective alternative to intrapulpal local anesthesia when the inferior alveolar nerve block (IANB) alone is insufficient in cases of symptomatic irreversible pulpitis.Materials and methodsA randomized controlled clinical trial was conducted among 52 children fulfilling the inclusion criteria: Frankl Behavior Rating of 3 or 4, vital pulp with bleeding on exposure, and requiring supplementary anesthesia following IANB. Participants were randomly assigned using concealed envelopes to group I (intrapulpal lidocaine, 0.2–0.5 mL under pressure) or group II (intrapulpal crushed ice, placed in the pulp chamber for 3–5 minutes at −4 to 0°C). Pain perception was assessed at access opening and during biomechanical preparation using a visual analog scale (VAS) by the child and a sound, eye, motor (SEM) score by a blinded dentist. Procedure duration was recorded, and cases requiring additional anesthesia were noted.ResultsThe correlation between VAS and SEM scores was not uniform across groups and assessment stages. A statistically significant positive correlation was observed during access opening in Group II and during biomechanical preparation in Group I, whereas the correlations at the other assessment stages were not statistically significant.ConclusionIntrapulpal cryotherapy using crushed ice may be a safe, child-friendly, and effective alternative to intrapulpal lidocaine, providing acceptable pain control with improved behavioral outcomes and clinical efficiency.Clinical significanceThis alternative technique helps avoid the pain of intrapulpal anesthesia while providing effective pain control with a positive impact on the child's experience, making it a more child-friendly option.How to cite this articleJagtap SD, Ninawe N, Kalaskar RR, et al. Introduction

Effective behavior management is essential in pediatric dentistry to build a positive dentist-patient relationship and ensure successful treatment outcomes. Children's behavior is closely linked to pain perception, which can influence the success of dental procedures.1 While pain is a common reason for seeking dental care, it can also be a major deterrent, especially in children. Local anesthesia is often required to manage pain, but, ironically, its administration can cause discomfort, making effective pain management strategies crucial for a smooth dental experience.2

Pulpectomy is a common procedure in pediatric dentistry, often requiring an inferior alveolar nerve block (IANB) for mandibular teeth. However, IANB has a high failure rate (44–81%) in cases of symptomatic irreversible pulpitis (SIP), as noted by Bigby et al.,3 making additional anesthesia necessary. Intrapulpal lidocaine injection is frequently used as a supplement, but, as Malamed states, it can itself be painful.4 This creates a challenge in pain management, where the methods used to alleviate pain may cause additional discomfort for the patient.

Cryotherapy involves the application of cold to a localized area to produce a cooling effect that can reduce pain perception and minimize discomfort during dental procedures.5 Research shows that it slows down nerve conduction and reduces inflammation, swelling, cellular activity, and local blood flow, providing physiological relief. This also provides psychological relief by distracting patients from focusing on their pain.6 Cryotherapy has been used to treat sprains, wounds, fractures, bruises, insect stings, and other injuries.7 Although less common in dentistry, ice as a topical agent has been proven effective in multiple studies. Lakshmanan and Ravindran8 and Padmanabh et al.9 concluded that cryotherapy is a highly effective, noninvasive alternative for reducing pain perception during local anesthesia injections in children. However, its effectiveness as a supplementary anesthetic during pulpectomy procedures has not yet been studied.

Thus, the present study was planned to evaluate and compare the effectiveness of intrapulpal crushed ice and intrapulpal local anesthesia during the pulpectomy procedure.

Materials and Methods

This prospective, two-arm, open-label, randomized controlled clinical trial was conducted at a dental institute following ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board. Informed consent was obtained from parents or guardians before treatment. Based on data from a previously published study,8 a sample size of 52 was determined with 99% power using a power analysis.

Children aged 5–10 years were recruited and underwent an initial clinical examination.

Based on the above-mentioned criteria, selected children received a standard IANB using a self-aspirating 27-gauge needle, with 2 mL of 2% lidocaine hydrochloride containing epinephrine (1:80,000) at a rate of 1 mL per minute. Anesthesia success was confirmed after 15 minutes based on subjective signs, such as lip numbness.

Mechanical debridement was then initiated. If the patient experienced pain, supplementary anesthesia was required, and the patient was included in the study. Using the concealed-envelope technique, participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups:

Group I (intrapulpal anesthesia, IANB-L) (Fig. 1): Following caries removal and initial pulp exposure, 0.2–0.5 mL of 2% lidocaine with 1:80,000 epinephrine was administered directly into the pulp under pressure using a cotton pellet stoppering technique to ensure adequate buildup.

Group I—intrapulpal anesthesia, IANB-L

Group II (intrapulpal crushed ice, IANB-C) (Fig. 2): After caries removal, when a thin layer of dentin remained or there was a pinpoint pulp exposure, and additionally after deroofing the pulp chamber if required, crushed ice was placed in the cavity for 3–5 minutes. The ice temperature was maintained between −4 and 0°C, measured using a thermocouple to prevent frostbite.

Group II—intrapulpal crushed ice, IANB-C

It was found that, unlike intrapulpal anesthesia, crushed ice was more soothing and less painful, with patients adapting to the cold within minutes.

Teeth that did not require supplementary anesthesia during mechanical debridement were excluded from the study.

The biomechanical preparation of the selected tooth was completed (Fig. 3A) and temporarily sealed with zinc oxide eugenol (ZOE) cement. The remaining endodontic treatment, obturation (Fig. 3B), and postobturation restoration (POR) (Fig. 3C) were completed 1 week later.

Pulpectomy procedure: (A) Biomechanical preparation; (B) Obturation; (C) Postobturative restoration

Children's response to pain was assessed using a visual analog scale (VAS) immediately during access opening and biomechanical preparation (Fig. 4). Participants were trained beforehand to indicate their pain intensity using the VAS. Additionally, an independent dentist recorded the sound, eye, motor (SEM) score at the same time intervals. The duration of the procedure, from mechanical debridement to the completion of biomechanical preparation, was also recorded.

Evaluation of children's response to pain using the VAS score

Teeth from group I or group II that required additional anesthesia during treatment, or those that continued to experience pain after supplementary anesthesia, received intrapulpal injections if necessary and were recorded as failures in the final analysis.

Results

The study involved 52 children (21 male, 31 female) aged between 5 and 10 years, with an average age of 7 ± 1.9 years.

Comparison of VAS scores between group I and group II at various treatment stages revealed no statistically significant difference in pain perception, suggesting comparable efficacy of both techniques as supplementary anesthesia. Notably, group II exhibited higher VAS scores during access opening, whereas group I recorded higher scores during biomechanical preparation (Table 1).

During biomechanical preparation (BMP), the SEM scores were consistently lower in group II across all parameters: sound, eye, and motor. Also, group II showed a notably lower total SEM score than group I; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.072) (Table 2).

These findings suggest a trend toward reduced pain-related responses in group II, particularly during BMP, although the differences were not statistically significant.

The correlation between VAS and SEM scores was evaluated using the Spearman rank correlation test. During access opening, there was a high correlation between the two scales in group II. During BMP, there was a moderate correlation between the two scales in group I. These findings suggest that the subjective (VAS) and objective (SEM) measures of pain perception are more strongly aligned in group II during access opening and in group I during BMP (Table 3).

Discussion

In cases of symptomatic irreversible pulpitis (SIP), the presence of pulpal inflammation significantly compromises the success of conventional anesthesia, with studies reporting up to an eightfold reduction in efficacy.10 Byers et al.11 observed that inflammation induces the sprouting of nerve fibers within the pulp and surrounding periapical tissues, leading to exaggerated pain responses such as allodynia and hyperalgesia. As a result, patients with SIP often require supplementary anesthetic techniques to achieve adequate pain control. Although intrapulpal lidocaine is a routinely used method for this purpose, it is frequently associated with sharp pain upon administration due to the high-pressure injection into confined pulp tissue.12 This presents a clinical challenge in pediatric dentistry, where pain perception can influence behavior and treatment outcomes. To address this, the present study was undertaken to evaluate and compare the efficacy of intrapulpal crushed ice—an approach utilizing cryotherapy—and intrapulpal lidocaine as supplementary pain management strategies during pulpectomy in children.

Cryotherapy, recognized for its analgesic and anti-inflammatory properties, could offer a less painful yet equally effective alternative. Cryotherapy has already shown potential in minimizing injection-related discomfort in children.8,9,13 Vera et al.14 reported a notable decrease in external root surface temperature when cold saline was applied using negative apical pressure. In a subsequent clinical trial, they further demonstrated that saline cooled to 4°C significantly reduced postoperative pain in patients with symptomatic apical periodontitis. Supporting this, Goodis et al.15 observed that cooling the tooth reduced pulpal blood flow and, in some cases, completely blocked pain sensation. Moreover, lowering the temperature of the pulp may inhibit neurotransmitter release from nerve endings, enhancing its pain-relieving effect.

However, its specific application intrapulpally during endodontic procedures had not been explored before this investigation, underscoring the relevance and novelty of the present research.

Although pain scores (VAS and SEM scale) between the groups were statistically similar, patient behavior and clinical feedback favored group II. Children receiving intrapulpal crushed ice often reacted positively, even playfully, during application. In contrast, patients in group I demonstrated more discomfort during intrapulpal injection, likely due to the pressure and sharp pain associated with lidocaine administration. These findings suggest that while both methods are effective, crushed ice may offer better overall patient acceptance, particularly in pediatric settings.

Fernández et al.16 concluded that cryoanesthesia using dry ice spray is an effective supplementary technique to enhance the success of IANB during endodontic treatment of mandibular molars with irreversible pulpitis. Koteeswaran et al.17 concluded that the use of Endo-Ice followed by intrapulpal ice stick application significantly reduces pain during pulp extirpation in mandibular molars with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis. The cold group showed pain reduction comparable to the articaine group. The present study used crushed ice intrapulpally, allowing better tissue contact and prolonged action, thereby potentially offering superior pain control and patient comfort.

Oh et al.,18 in their study on cryoconservation of human teeth for future tooth banking, observed enamel fractures in 4 out of 16 teeth, although hardness testing showed no significant differences. It is important to note that the cryoconservation temperatures used in their study ranged from −130 to −196°C, which are far more extreme than those applied in the present study; therefore, the present study does not consider such variables as those evaluated by Oh et al.

The observed reduction in time for biomechanical preparation in group II, although not statistically significant, suggests a potential clinical advantage of cryotherapy in improving procedural efficiency. The cold application may have led to localized vasoconstriction, resulting in reduced pulpal bleeding, which likely facilitated easier pulp extirpation and better visibility during instrumentation. This cleaner operative field may have contributed to smoother and quicker biomechanical preparation. Moreover, as this is the first study to evaluate the effect of cryotherapy on procedural time, these findings cannot be directly compared with previous literature. However, they provide valuable preliminary insight into the possible efficiency-related benefits of using cryotherapy as an adjunct in endodontic procedures.

The present study employed both the VAS and SEM scales, with the VAS being simple and effective for self-reporting by children and the SEM scale, used by the dentist, providing an objective behavioral assessment. A positive correlation between the two was found, supporting their combined reliability in evaluating pain perception. Fernández et al.16 used the Heft-Parker Modified Visual Analog Scale (HPVAS), while Koteeswaran et al.17 utilized the standard VAS for pain analysis. In contrast, our study incorporated both child- and dentist-based assessments, enhancing the overall reliability and comprehensiveness of pain evaluation. Future studies can be planned with a larger sample size to generalize the results and determine the actual duration of pain relief provided by the ice group during clinical procedures.

Conclusion

Both methods were equally effective based on VAS and SEM scores; however, the cryotherapy group demonstrated better patient acceptance and behavior. It also enabled slightly quicker biomechanical preparation and improved clinical visibility. The positive correlation between the VAS and SEM scales supports their combined use in pediatric pain assessment. Overall, intrapulpal cryotherapy emerges as a safe, effective, and child-friendly alternative.

Clinical Significance

Intrapulpal anesthesia, though effective, is often associated with sharp pain that can negatively affect a child's behavior and cooperation during treatment. The use of crushed ice as a supplementary technique provides a novel, nonpharmacological alternative that helps avoid this painful injection experience. It ensures adequate pain control during pulpectomy while improving the comfort and acceptance of the child. By reducing anxiety and enhancing cooperation, this method has a positive impact on the overall treatment experience. Moreover, it is safe, efficient, and easy to implement in routine clinical practice. Therefore, intrapulpal cryotherapy with crushed ice may serve as a child-friendly substitute for intrapulpal local anesthesia.
