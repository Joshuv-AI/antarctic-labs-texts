Electro-thermal surface evolution in EDM of dissimilar stainless-carbon steel welds: parametric effects and micro-texture correlations
Authors: Mohammad S. Alsoufi, Saleh A. Bawazeer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates surface formation during Electrical Discharge Machining (EDM) of dissimilar stainless-carbon steel welded joints fabricated by Tungsten Inert Gas (TIG) and Manual Metal Arc (ARC) welding using 316 and 309 filler metals. Experiments were conducted at gap voltages of 10–40 V, pulse-on times of 7–11 µs, and pulse-off times of 10–14 µs. The machined surfaces were characterized through six standardized roughness descriptors (Ra, Rq, Rt, Rz, Rsk, Rku) to capture amplitude and statistical texture features. TIG316 exhibited the smoothest and most stable morphology (Ra ≈ 1.6–2.0 μm), reflecting efficient heat dissipation and uniform plasma confinement, whereas TIG309 showed nonlinear roughness behavior that stabilized only at optimized pulse durations. ARC316 maintained moderate roughness with limited variation, whereas ARC309 deteriorated with excessive energy input due to thermal accumulation and recast-layer thickening. The results establish a physics-based correlation between discharge energy and surface integrity and support weld-specific optimization of EDM processes in heterogeneous welded systems.

Introduction

Electrical Discharge Machining (EDM) is a precise manufacturing technique that shapes conductive materials by generating controlled electrical discharges that erode material from the work surface1,2. Its ability to produce complex geometries with sub-micron accuracy has made it essential in high-value industries such as aerospace, energy, and biomedical device manufacturing3,4. However, applying EDM to heterogeneous or welded materials introduces significant complexity because the electro-thermal response depends heavily on variations in metallurgical structure, electrical conductivity, and interfacial heat transfer5,6. As modern engineering increasingly relies on multi-material assemblies to optimize mechanical strength, corrosion resistance, and thermal properties, understanding EDM behavior in dissimilar welded systems has become a crucial scientific and technological challenge for achieving consistent surface integrity and dimensional accuracy7,8.

The growing adoption of multi-material welded structures in critical engineering applications has increased the demand for reliable post-weld machining strategies capable of maintaining high dimensional accuracy and surface integrity. Dissimilar steel welded joints are extensively employed in petrochemical pipelines, pressure vessels, heat exchangers, power-generation equipment, and aerospace structures because they combine the corrosion resistance of stainless steel with the strength and economic advantages of carbon steel.

Among the available precision machining processes, Wire Electrical Discharge Machining (WEDM) is particularly suitable for these applications because it machines electrically conductive materials without direct mechanical contact, thereby minimizing cutting forces and mechanical deformation. This is especially advantageous for dissimilar welded joints, where abrupt variations in hardness, strength, and metallurgical properties across the welded interface can make conventional machining difficult and may obscure the influence of weld heterogeneity on the resulting surface integrity. Consequently, WEDM provides an appropriate platform for systematically evaluating the surface roughness and profile characteristics of dissimilar welded systems under controlled electro-thermal loading conditions. Among these, stainless steel-carbon steel joints are commonly fabricated using Tungsten Inert Gas (TIG) and Manual Metal Arc (MMA, also known as ARC) welding9–12. They exhibit significant microstructural and phase differences between the stainless-steel and carbon-steel sides, resulting in steep gradients in thermal diffusivity and electrical resistivity within the fusion zone13. This poses challenges in EDM, including unstable sparking, uneven melting, and uncontrolled resolidification, leading to unpredictable surface quality and size variation14. While these dissimilar joints are essential in industrial applications, most EDM research mainly focuses on homogeneous materials. It aims to optimize process parameters or reduce tool wear without addressing the complex electro-thermal asymmetries in welded substrates15.

Most studies focus on average roughness (Ra) as the sole indicator of surface quality, overlooking the complex multi-scale and statistical features of EDM surfaces that reveal discharge behavior16. The lack of a mechanistic link between discharge energy and surface structure hampers the development of predictive models17. Specifically, there is still limited understanding of how energy input, pulse timing, and metallurgical composition influence crater formation, plasma confinement, and the uniformity of re-solidified layers in welded materials18. As a result, the essential connection between electrical parameters and surface integrity in the EDM of dissimilar welds remains largely underexplored.

Recent investigations have increasingly examined EDM processing of heterogeneous and dissimilar material systems, highlighting the influence of material-property mismatch on machining performance, surface integrity, and thermal response. These studies have demonstrated that variations in thermal conductivity, electrical conductivity, and metallurgical characteristics can significantly affect discharge behavior and the resulting surface morphology. However, existing investigations have largely focused on individual machining responses or process optimization, while comparatively limited attention has been given to establishing a unified comparative methodology for evaluating the surface-texture evolution of multiple dissimilar welded systems under consistent electro-thermal loading conditions. The present work addresses this gap by introducing a normalized electro-thermal energy-density framework for the comparative assessment of EDM surface characteristics19,20.

Because dissimilar welded joints exhibit spatial variations in metallurgical and thermo-physical properties across the weld, a single roughness measurement cannot adequately represent the resulting WEDM surface. Accordingly, the present study evaluates the spatial evolution of standardized surface roughness parameters along the machining path to investigate how local variations in weld characteristics influence surface integrity. Building upon this motivation, this research introduces a comprehensive electro-thermal model to understand the formation of surfaces during electrical discharge machining (EDM) of dissimilar stainless and carbon steel welds produced by TIG and ARC techniques using 316 and 309 fillers. The study methodically assesses how gap voltage, pulse-on, and pulse-off times influence surface quality, explicitly connecting discharge parameters to roughness measures (Ra, Rt) and texture statistics (Rsk, Rku). Analyzing these relationships with thermal stress from discharges clarifies how energy input and heat dissipation at the micro-level affect surface development across varied weld interfaces.

To facilitate cross-material comparison, a dimensionless discharge energy-density parameter (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{d}^{*}$$\end{document}) is introduced. It consolidates the effects of electrical input across weld systems with varying thermal and electrical properties. This normalization creates a shared energetic framework for interpreting surface responses, uncovering consistent electro-thermal regimes regardless of filler composition and welding technique. Additionally, combining amplitude-based and higher-order statistical descriptors within a normalized response surface enhances understanding of interactions among plasma dynamics, melt-pool stability, and resolidification, thereby offering a physically grounded explanation for the resulting surface morphologies.

Overall, the proposed framework shifts EDM analysis from empirical parameter tuning to a physics-guided comparative approach, providing predictive insights into surface quality at complex welded interfaces. By explicitly incorporating discharge energetics, surface statistics, and weld metallurgy, the study provides a physics-guided method to improve EDM performance in heterogeneous metallic systems.

Experimental procedures and data acquisition

The overall experimental methodology adopted in the present study is summarized in Fig. 1. The investigation comprised four sequential stages. First, dissimilar stainless and carbon steel welded joints were fabricated using two welding processes (TIG and ARC) with two filler materials (AISI 309 and AISI 316). The welded specimens were subsequently machined by WEDM under controlled electrical discharge conditions. The machined surfaces were then characterized through surface roughness measurements performed at predefined locations along the specimen height using a contact profilometer. Finally, the measured roughness parameters were processed and analyzed to evaluate the influence of welding method, filler composition, and EDM process parameters on the electro-thermal evolution of surface integrity. The detailed procedures associated with each stage are described in the following subsections.

Materials and weld systems

The experimental program investigated four dissimilar welded joints formed between low-carbon steel and austenitic stainless steel, designed to represent realistic bimetallic interfaces encountered in structural, energy, and manufacturing applications. Each joint corresponded to a distinct combination of welding process and filler composition, namely TIG316, TIG309, ARC316, and ARC309, where Tungsten Inert Gas (TIG) welding and Manual Metal Arc (ARC) welding were employed using AISI 316 and AISI 309 filler materials, respectively. A schematic representation of the resulting joint architecture, including the base metals, fusion zone, and heat-affected zones (HAZ), is presented in Fig. 2.

The welds were produced using a multipass procedure suitable for this plate thickness. For TIG welding, a 3.2 mm non-consumable electrode was operated under direct-current electrode-negative polarity. AISI 309 and AISI 316 filler rods, with a nominal diameter of 3.2 mm, were used for the TIG309 and TIG316 joints, respectively. The nominal ranges for welding current, arc voltage, and travel speed were 150–250 A, 10–12 V, and 75–150 mm/min, respectively. Pure argon shielding gas was supplied at approximately 8–10 L/min.

In all configurations, low-carbon steel was joined to austenitic stainless steel through controlled bead deposition to ensure a stable metallurgical bond across the fusion line. The selected filler alloys were chosen to ensure compositional compatibility and mitigate dilution-induced cracking, while allowing deliberate variation in thermal conductivity, electrical resistivity, and alloy chemistry across the weld metal. This approach enabled systematic evaluation of how welding-induced heterogeneity influences the electro-thermal response during subsequent Electrical Discharge Machining (EDM).

TIG welds were produced under argon shielding, providing a highly stable arc environment with limited oxidation and relatively uniform heat input. Such conditions are generally associated with more controlled fusion-zone formation and reduced surface contamination during welding. This resulted in a weld condition expected to differ from that of ARC welding in terms of thermal history and metallurgical uniformity. In contrast, ARC welds were deposited using flux-coated electrodes under ambient conditions, leading to higher localized heat input, slower cooling rates, and more pronounced microstructural gradients across the weldment. These intrinsic, process-dependent differences are expected to influence the local electrical and thermal response of the welded regions during EDM, including discharge stability and material removal behavior.

For ARC welding, 3.2 mm AISI 309 and AISI 316 covered electrodes were used for the ARC309 and ARC316 joints, respectively. The electrodes were operated using direct-current electrode-positive polarity, with nominal welding currents of 90–120 A, arc voltages of approximately 30–40 V, and travel speeds of 150–250 mm/min. External shielding gas was not required for ARC welding because atmospheric protection was generated by decomposition of the electrode flux coating.

Following welding, rectangular specimens (240 × 20 × 15 mm) were sectioned from the central region of each weld bead to ensure inclusion of the weld metal and adjacent HAZ on both sides of the joint. Prior to EDM cutting, all specimen surfaces were mechanically ground to eliminate surface irregularities, oxide layers, and residual slag, thereby ensuring consistent electrical contact and minimizing surface-condition variability during machining. By integrating both TIG and ARC welding routes with two filler chemistries, the experimental framework enabled a controlled comparison of how weld-process energy and alloy composition collectively govern electro-thermal performance during EDM of dissimilar steel joints.

The present experimental program focused on the EDM-induced surface-topography response of the welded systems. Direct characterization of the fusion-zone microstructure and phase constitution by optical microscopy, SEM/EDS, XRD, or EBSD was not conducted; consequently, metallurgical explanations discussed later are interpreted as literature-supported hypotheses rather than directly verified mechanisms.

EDM setup and process parameters

All machining trials were performed using a precision CNC Electrical Discharge Machining (EDM) system manufactured by Mitsubishi Electric, Tokyo, Japan. The system was equipped with a 0.25 mm molybdenum wire electrode, selected for its high tensile strength, thermal stability, and consistent electrical conductivity under sustained discharge conditions. Deionized water was used as the dielectric medium and maintained at a controlled electrical conductivity of 10–15 µS cm⁻¹ and a temperature of 22 ± 1 °C to ensure repeatable breakdown characteristics and stable plasma formation throughout the machining process. Continuous dielectric circulation was employed to promote efficient debris evacuation, suppress secondary discharges, and maintain a uniform electrical field within the machining gap. The wire electrode was employed with deionized water as the dielectric medium. The peak discharge current, wire tension, wire feed rate, and dielectric flushing pressure were maintained at 10 A, 12 N, 8 m/min, and 1.0 MPa, respectively. These parameters were kept constant for all specimens to isolate the influence of the selected discharge variables investigated in this study.

Furthermore, to further isolate the influence of discharge energy modulation, the wire feed rate is held constant acrossall experiments, thereby maintaining a stable inter-electrode gap and minimizing variability associated with wire vibration or debris-induced short-circuiting.

The base materials used for fabricating the four dissimilar welded systems, TIG316, TIG309, ARC316, and ARC309, were commercially sourced from Shaaban Steel Company (Jeddah, Saudi Arabia). The welded specimens were subsequently produced using tungsten inert gas (TIG) and shielded metal arc (ARC) welding techniques and then machined using systematically varied electrical discharge parameters spanning low-, medium-, and high-energy regimes. These regimes were defined by controlled adjustments to the gap voltage, pulse-on time, and pulse-off time, while maintaining a constant discharge current to decouple current-driven effects from pulse-timing and voltage-dependent plasma behavior. Accordingly, the electro-thermal energy density analysis presented later in the manuscript treats the current contribution as being controlled within the tested EDM window, allowing voltage and pulse timing to serve as the primary variables governing relative discharge intensity. The selected parameter ranges were established based on preliminary stability tests to ensure reliable spark generation without wire rupture, excessive arcing, or severe thermal damage to the machined surface. The complete parameter matrix for each weld system is summarized in Table 1.

The machining conditions were organized to provide a controlled comparison of weld-system response across three discharge-energy levels. Surface-response values reported in the subsequent analysis correspond to the measured roughness descriptors at the specified depth locations under each EDM condition. Following EDM cutting, all specimens were subjected to ultrasonic cleaning in ethanol to remove loosely adhered re-solidified debris and recast particles, thereby ensuring that post-processing surface roughness measurements reflected intrinsic EDM-induced surface characteristics rather than residual contamination.

The experimental program was designed to investigate the influence of electro-thermal discharge conditions on surface texture characteristics of dissimilar welded joints. Accordingly, the measured responses were limited to standardized surface-topography parameters, while machining performance indicators such as material removal rate, wire wear, and recast layer thickness were outside the scope of the present investigation. Accordingly, the selected WEDM process parameters were employed as controlled electro-thermal loading conditions to systematically evaluate the surface roughness and profile characteristics of the investigated dissimilar welded joints, rather than to perform independent optimization of the machining process.

Surface roughness measurement

The surface integrity of all EDM-machined samples was measured using a Taly-Surf® contact profilometer (Taylor Hobson Precision, Inc., Leicester, UK), mounted on a vibration-isolated granite base for stability. The device features a 2 μm conisphere diamond stylus that applies a constant force of 0.7 mN, enabling the detection of nanometer-level surface variations. Scanning was performed at 0.5 mm/s with a cutoff length of 0.8 mm, in accordance with ISO 4287/4288 for surface texture assessment21,22.

Each machined specimen (240 × 20 × 15 mm) was securely placed in a leveled sample holder, with the stylus aligned parallel to the EDM cutting direction to capture the dominant lay pattern created by spark discharges accurately. Measurements were taken at five predetermined depths along the specimen’s height, specifically at 0.5, 5, 10, 15, and 19.5 mm, representing the top, upper-middle, mid-section, lower-middle, and bottom regions. At each depth location, the corresponding profilometric measurement was used to extract the surface roughness descriptors for that spatial zone. Accordingly, roughness measurements were obtained at multiple predefined locations along the machined surface to capture the spatial evolution of the surface characteristics throughout the dissimilar welded joint. This measurement strategy was adopted to characterize the spatial evolution of surface morphology arising from local variations in the metallurgical and thermo-physical properties across the welded joint rather than to obtain repeated measurements of an identical surface condition. Therefore, the analysis focuses on depth-wise and weld-wise comparative trends rather than replicate-based statistical uncertainty at each depth.

Before measurement, the profilometer was calibrated using a certified reference specimen (Ra = 1.00 ± 0.02 μm) traceable to national standards. Calibration was periodically verified every 10 samples to identify instrument drift. All profiles were post-processed with a Gaussian low-pass filter to separate surface roughness from macro-scale waviness, ensuring the data accurately represent the true micro-texture created by EDM discharges.

Six standardized surface parameters were extracted from each recorded profile in accordance with ISO 428721. These include the arithmetic mean roughness (Ra) and root-mean-square roughness (Rq), which describe the average surface amplitude; maximum height (Rt) and ten-point mean height (Rz) to assess the extreme peak-to-valley variations; and skewness (Rsk) along with kurtosis (Rku), which characterize the surface-height distribution’s symmetry and sharpness. The profilometer’s consistent setup, periodic calibration, and systematic averaging ensured high repeatability and reliability of all measurements. Therefore, the collected dataset provides a controlled basis for evaluating the electro-thermal surface response of the four different weld systems under the tested discharge conditions.

Data normalization and statistical processing

The complete dataset from profilometric measurements was carefully organized to facilitate quantitative analysis of surface behavior under different EDM discharge conditions. For each welded system, TIG316, TIG309, ARC316, and ARC309, six surface descriptors (Ra, Rq, Rt, Rz, Rsk, and Rku) were recorded in five spatial zones (0.5, 5, 10, 15, and 19.5 mm) and across three discharge levels, which represent various combinations of gap voltage, pulse-on time, and pulse-off time. This resulted in a detailed structured dataset that can be used for trend analysis, comparisons between materials, and EDM-specific interpretation of surface evolution.

Each surface parameter was averaged across three repeated profilometric traces acquired at each predefined measurement location to reduce random noise, with the standard deviation (σ) calculated to assess variability within each zone. These values were then used to create error bars in all graphical plots in the results section, ensuring the trends accurately reflect the natural variation in surface morphology along the machined height. Because the repeated traces were obtained from the same machined condition at each predefined measurement location, they were treated as repeated surface measurements rather than independent EDM replications. Consequently, the reported variability reflects measurement-level uncertainty and local surface heterogeneity under each experimental condition.

where R represents the measured parameter, and Rₘiₙ and Rₘₐₓ indicate its minimum and maximum values within the dataset. This normalization removed bias caused by variations in absolute roughness across steels with different metallurgical compositions, ensuring that subsequent analyses focused on relative behavior rather than the absolute scale. Unless otherwise stated, normalization was used only as a comparative tool to highlight relative changes within the investigated EDM dataset and was not intended to define a universal scaling law.

The dataset was examined from three complementary viewpoints. First, a parameter-based evaluation assessed how individual EDM control factors, gap voltage, pulse-on duration, and pulse-off duration, affected the surface descriptors within each weld category. Second, a material-based comparison examined the differences in surface response between TIG- and ARC-fabricated welds under similar discharge energy conditions, highlighting the influence of weld metallurgy on electro-thermal stability. Third, a cross-correlation analysis linked the amplitude-based parameters (Ra, Rq, Rt, Rz) with the statistical descriptors (Rsk and Rku), offering a unified view of how spark uniformity and crater geometry change with discharge energy.

All data processing and graphical visualizations were carried out using OriginPro 2024 and MATLAB R2023a. Replicate-averaging, normalization, and correlation routines were cross-checked between both platforms to confirm analytical accuracy and reproducibility. This organized pre-processing framework offered a solid basis for interpreting the electro-thermal surface evolution mechanisms discussed in the subsequent sections.

Results and discussion: electro-thermal surface evolution

Amplitude-based roughness characteristics (Ra, Rq) of TIG and ARC welds

Amplitude-based surface roughness parameters, Ra and Rq, directly reflect the spatial uniformity of discharge energy and the stability of spark-material interaction during EDM. Their variation with machining depth and discharge level captures the combined influence of plasma heating, molten-metal expulsion, and subsequent resolidification. Figure 3 presents the depth-resolved distributions of Ra and Rq for TIG316, TIG309, ARC316, and ARC309 welds. In contrast, Fig. 4 summarizes the corresponding level-averaged responses together with the mean-of-the-mean roughness values that characterize the global surface condition at each discharge level.

For TIG316, both Ra and Rq decrease systematically with increasing discharge level across the entire machining depth. The level-averaged Ra reduces from approximately 1.85 μm at Level 1 to 1.62 μm at Level 3. In comparison, Rq decreases from 2.33 μm to 2.10 μm, indicating a progressively more stable surface-generation response under the tested EDM conditions. Depth-resolved profiles in Fig. 3(a) show that the lowest roughness consistently occurs in the mid-depth region (≈ 10–15 mm), suggesting that transient discharge instability near the surface is replaced by a quasi-steady electro-thermal material-removal regime as machining proceeds. This response is consistent with a well-balanced electro-thermal condition, in which melting intensity, plasma pressure, and resolidification time are better balanced.

In contrast, TIG309 exhibits markedly higher roughness at low discharge levels, indicative of unstable discharge conditions. At Level 1, the mean Ra is 3.05 μm, and Rq is 6.36 μm, suggesting a more irregular surface-height distribution and stronger localized discharge effects. As the discharge level increases, both parameters decrease sharply, converging toward Ra ≈ 1.72 μm and Rq ≈ 2.14 μm at Level 3. The pronounced reduction in roughness indicates a transition toward improved molten-metal flushing and a more stable electro-thermal regime.

ARC welds exhibit consistently higher Ra and Rq values than TIG welds across all discharge levels, as shown in Figs. 3(c-d) and 3(b). For ARC316, the level-averaged Ra decreases from 2.04 μm at Level 1 to 1.89 μm at Level 2, followed by a slight increase to 1.97 μm at Level 3. In contrast, Rq follows a similar non-monotonic trend, decreasing from 2.74 µmto 2.41 μm and then increasing to 2.64 μm. This behavior suggests that increasing discharge level initially improves surface formation, but excessive electro-thermal loading may reintroduce localized roughness in the ARC316 weld system.

ARC309 exhibits the highest roughness among all weld systems, with mean Ra values of 3.88 μm, 3.88 μm, and 4.21 µmand corresponding Rq values of 5.13 μm, 5.03 μm, and 5.48 μm at Levels 1–3, respectively. The persistence of elevated roughness and broader variability indicates that oxide inclusions and the inherent metallurgical inhomogeneity of arc deposition may locally disturb discharge stability and surface resolidification behavior.

The aggregated mean-of-the-mean roughness values in Fig. 4 provide a global assessment of discharge-level effectiveness independent of individual depth variations. For TIG welds (Fig. 4a), these values decrease from 2.41 µmat Level 1 to 1.89 μm at Level 3, with a temporary increase to 3.04 μm at Level 2, reflecting a transitional regime in which electro-thermal stabilization is incomplete. In contrast, ARC welds (Fig. 4b) show mean-of-the-mean values of 3.45 μm, 3.30 μm, and 3.58 μm at Levels 1–3, respectively, indicating limited sensitivity to discharge-level variation and the dominant influence of welding-induced heterogeneity on surface formation.

Overall, the combined depth-resolved and level-averaged analyses demonstrate that increasing discharge level promotes smoother and more consistent surfaces; however, the extent of improvement is strongly material-dependent. TIG316 exhibits the most stable electro-thermal response, characterized by low roughness and minimal variability, while TIG309 shows substantial improvement only at higher discharge levels. ARC welds display partial stabilization but retain higher roughness due to metallurgical heterogeneity. The convergence of Ra and Rq trends at higher discharge levels indicates that EDM progressively approaches a quasi-steady thermal-erosion state, although the absolute surface quality remains governed by weld chemistry and deposition history.

Peak-valley roughness characteristics (Rt and Rz)

The peak-valley roughness parameters Rt and Rz characterize the extreme height distribution of EDM-processed surfaces and are particularly sensitive to sporadic high-energy discharges, unstable plasma channels, and the formation of re-solidified molten droplets. Unlike amplitude-based descriptors, these parameters capture localized surface damage that may not significantly affect average roughness values. Figure 5 presents the depth-resolved variation of Rt and Rz for TIG316, TIG309, ARC316, and ARC309 welds. In contrast, Fig. 6 summarizes the corresponding level-averaged responses together with the mean-of-the-mean values that describe the global peak-valley behavior at each discharge level.

For TIG316, both Rt and Rz remain confined within a relatively narrow range across all discharge levels and machining depths. Depth-resolved Rt values generally lie between 12 and 20 μm, while Rz remains mostly within 10–13 μm (Fig. 5a). This limited variation suggests relatively stable EDM surface formation with limited development of extreme peak-valley features, even under lower discharge conditions. A modest increase in Rt is observed in the mid-depth region (≈ 10–15 mm) at Level 2, likely caused by cumulative thermal loading during sustained machining. However, the absence of a corresponding increase in Rz indicates that this deviation arises from a small number of localized peaks rather than widespread surface instability. At Level 3, both parameters exhibit uniform and stable profiles, confirming that increased pulse may improve local re-melting and surface leveling without producing substantial extreme-height amplification.

In contrast, TIG309 exhibits pronounced instability in extreme-height roughness at lower discharge levels. At Levels 1 and 2, Rt values reach 80–120 μm in several regions, with pronounced peaks near the mid-depth zone (Fig. 5b). The corresponding Rz values range from 28 to 35 μm, nearly three times higher than those observed for TIG316. Because Rz represents the average of the five highest peaks and five deepest valleys, these elevated values indicate recurrent deep surface features rather than isolated defects. This behavior is consistent with intermittent discharge instability and less uniform material expulsion under the lower discharge levels. When the discharge level is increased to Level 3, both Rt and Rz decrease sharply to approximately 12–14 μm, approaching the range observed for TIG316. This abrupt improvement confirms that the lower discharge conditions were under-optimized and that a more balanced pulse-on/off cycle stabilizes the plasma channel and suppresses sporadic deep melting.

ARC welds exhibit intermediate behavior between TIG316 and TIG309, as shown in Figs. 5(c-d). For ARC316, Rt values typically range from 15 to 26 μm, while Rz varies between 12 and 15 μm, with noticeably greater depth-wise scatter than TIG316. The presence of oxide inclusions and microstructural heterogeneity inherent to arc welding may contribute to localized variations in discharge stability and re-solidified surface height. ARC309 exhibits higher extreme-height roughness overall, with Rt reaching 30–47 μm and Rz spanning 18–27 μm, indicating persistent peak–valley features even at higher discharge levels. Although increasing discharge level reduces some depth-wise variability, ARC welds do not achieve the same degree of stabilization observed in TIG welds.

The level-averaged results in Fig. 6 reinforce these observations. For TIG welds (Fig. 6a), the mean-of-the-mean values remain nearly constant across discharge levels, measuring 17.72 μm at Level 1, 17.64 μm at Level 2, and 17.76 μm at Level 3. This consistency confirms that TIG welds, particularly TIG316, maintain controlled peak-valley behavior once stable discharge conditions are established. In contrast, ARC welds (Fig. 6b) exhibit higher mean-of-the-mean values that decrease gradually with discharge level, from 23.03 μm at Level 1 to 22.61 μm at Level 2 and 22.07 µmat Level 3. The elevated magnitude and weaker sensitivity to discharge level indicate that metallurgical heterogeneity, rather than electrical parameters alone, governs extreme-height roughness in ARC welds.

Overall, Figs. 5 and 6 demonstrate that Rt and Rz are substantially more sensitive to discharge instability and localized overheating than Ra and Rq. TIG316 exhibits the highest resistance to extreme surface damage, while TIG309 shows strong susceptibility to under-optimized discharge timing. ARC welds display partial stabilization at higher discharge levels but retain higher peak-valley roughness due to oxide-rich fusion zones and heterogeneous solidification. These findings highlight that optimizing EDM parameters based solely on average roughness can mask severe localized surface defects; incorporating Rt and Rz is therefore useful for a more complete assessment of surface integrity in dissimilar welded joints.

Statistical texture descriptors (Rsk and Rku)

Statistical texture descriptors, skewness (Rsk) and kurtosis (Rku), provide insight into the shape and sharpness of the surface-height distribution generated during EDM, extending beyond the information conveyed by amplitude-based parameters. While Ra, Rt, and Rz quantify average and extreme height variations, Rsk and Rku characterize distribution asymmetry and peakedness, thereby supporting the interpretation of the balance between molten-material expulsion, plasma stability, and resolidification dynamics. Figure 7 presents the depth-resolved evolution of Rsk and Rku for TIG316, TIG309, ARC316, and ARC309 welds across the investigated discharge levels.

For TIG316, Rsk remains close to zero throughout the machining depth and across all discharge levels, spanning approximately − 0.23 to + 0.45. This near-symmetric distribution indicates a balanced population of peaks and valleys, consistent with uniform crater formation and controlled melt ejection. Slight positive skewness observed at Levels 1 and 2 suggests the presence of shallow re-solidified protrusions. In contrast, the emergence of mildly negative values at Level 3 reflects a subtle shift toward valley-dominated profiles associated with smoother, well-remelted craters. Correspondingly, Rku values remain within 2.1–3.3, indicating platykurtic to near-Gaussian distributions. Such behavior is characteristic of a stable erosion regime with minimal random spiking, confirming that TIG316 maintains a consistent electro-thermal response even as discharge conditions vary.

In contrast, TIG309 exhibits pronounced statistical instability at lower discharge levels. At Levels 1 and 2, Rsk reaches exceptionally high positive values (up to + 8.6), indicating surfaces dominated by sharp protrusions formed by localized height amplification and nonuniform resolidification features. The corresponding Rku values exceed 60 in several regions, reflecting highly leptokurtic distributions dominated by isolated, sharp asperities. These extreme statistics are consistent with localized discharge instability and the formation of isolated extreme surface features under lower-level EDM conditions. When the discharge level is increased to Level 3, both descriptors decrease sharply, with Rsk converging toward 0.1–0.5 and Rku falling to approximately 2.0-2.8. This transition marks a shift from erratic surface formation toward a more uniform melting and resolidification process, consistent with improved plasma stability and balanced pulse timing.

ARC welds display statistical behavior distinct from both TIG systems. For ARC316, Rsk varies from moderately negative values at low discharge (approximately − 0.33 to − 1.46) to positive values at higher levels (up to + 2.17), indicating alternating dominance of valley- and peak-driven features along the machining depth. The associated Rku values span a broader range (≈ 3.4 to > 10), reflecting occasional sharp asperities that may be associated with local heterogeneity in the arc-deposited weld metal. ARC309 exhibits predominantly negative Rsk values across all discharge levels (approximately − 0.90 to − 0.02), suggesting valley-dominated surfaces characterized by deeper cavities rather than protruding peaks. Its Rku values remain moderate (≈ 3.0-4.7), indicating fewer extreme spikes than TIG309 at low discharge but a consistently non-Gaussian texture influenced by heterogeneous solidification and slower cooling.

Overall, Fig. 7 demonstrates that TIG316 maintains the most statistically uniform surface texture, while TIG309 undergoes a pronounced transition from a highly non-Gaussian, peak-dominated regime at low discharge to a quasi-Gaussian distribution under optimized conditions. ARC welds exhibit intermediate behavior, with statistical descriptors governed more strongly by welding-induced heterogeneity than by discharge parameters alone. The concurrent reduction of extreme Rsk and Rku values under optimized EDM conditions confirms that surface refinement involves not only lowering roughness magnitude but also homogenizing the entire microtopography. These statistical trends complement the amplitude-based results and indicate that stable EDM operation corresponds to a steady-state electro-thermal regime characterized by symmetric height distributions and near-Gaussian texture statistics.

Statistical texture-based mechanistic stability assessment

To integrate amplitude-based roughness metrics with statistical texture characteristics, a normalized Surface Texture Index (STI) was constructed using Ra, Rt, and Rku. This composite index provides a compact EDM-specific indicator of surface texture severity by simultaneously accounting for mean surface amplitude, extreme morphological features, and statistical texture sharpness. The STI was formulated using Ra, Rt, and Rku because these parameters represent three complementary aspects of surface morphology: average roughness amplitude, maximum peak-to-valley variation, and profile-shape distribution, respectively. Although additional descriptors such as Rq and Rz were also measured, they were not included in the composite index because they are closely related to Ra and Rt, respectively, and would introduce redundancy without substantially increasing the information content of the index. Consequently, the STI provides a compact comparative measure for evaluating overall surface-condition trends across the investigated weld systems and discharge conditions.

The selected parameters provide complementary, non-redundant information. Ra represents the average surface roughness intensity governed by discharge energy, Rt captures extreme peak-to-valley features associated with discharge instability and sporadic localized surface-height amplification, and Rku quantifies the statistical sharpness of the height distribution, reflecting the presence of sharp or isolated surface features. Together, these descriptors provide a concise way to compare average, extreme, and distribution-sensitive aspects of EDM surface morphology within the present dataset.

Directional descriptors, such as skewness (Rsk), were intentionally excluded from the STI formulation and were analyzed separately to preserve regime discrimination. Accordingly, Fig. 8 presents STI evolution for TIG316, TIG309, ARC316, and ARC309 welds across discharge levels, while Fig. 9 employs the Rsk-Rku correlation to support the interpretation of distinct EDM texture regimes.

Among all weld systems, TIG316 consistently exhibits the lowest STI values, remaining within approximately 0.027–0.032 across all discharge levels. This narrow range indicates a highly stable electro-thermal response, characterized by uniform crater formation, limited recast accumulation, and statistically near-Gaussian texture distributions (Rsk ≈ 0, Rku ≈ 3). The austenitic matrix of TIG316 supports a comparatively stable discharge response and limited surface-height amplification under the tested EDM conditions. Consequently, TIG316 represents the most stable surface-texture response among the investigated weld systems.

In contrast, TIG309 displays the highest and most discharge-sensitive STI response. At Level 1, STI approaches unity, reflecting severe surface degradation associated with extreme kurtosis and strongly positive skewness. These features indicate a surface profile dominated by isolated sharp protrusions and localized height extremes. As discharge conditions are optimized toward Level 3, STI decreases sharply to approximately 0.065, accompanied by a collapse of extreme Rsk and Rku values toward near-Gaussian limits. This pronounced improvement demonstrates that TIG309 is highly sensitive to pulse-timing balance; when pulse-on and pulse-off durations are properly matched, the erosion mechanism transitions from unstable localized surface formation to controlled melting and resolidification.

The ARC-based welds exhibit intermediate STI behavior, reflecting a balance between improved thermal robustness and welding-induced heterogeneity. ARC316 maintains moderate and relatively stable STI values (approximately 0.12–0.24) across all discharge levels. Although its absolute STI remains higher than that of TIG316, the limited variation indicates a predictable electro-thermal response. Residual oxides and micro-porosity inherent to arc deposition may contribute to localized texture variation and occasional sharp surface features reflected in elevated Rku values. ARC309, however, displays a consistently higher STI baseline (approximately 0.26–0.31) with weaker sensitivity to discharge level. This behavior suggests that the solidified fusion zone in ARC309 acts as a thermally buffered, yet microstructurally heterogeneous, substrate, limiting the extent to which discharge optimization alone can improve surface integrity.

The mechanistic texture-based interpretation of these trends is clarified by the Rsk-Rku correlation in Fig. 9, which reveals three distinct texture-response regimes. The first regime, populated primarily by TIG309 at low discharge levels, occupies the region of high positive skewness and extreme kurtosis, corresponding to unstable discharges dominated by isolated sharp protrusions and highly peaked surface-height distributions. The second regime, centered around Rsk ≈ 0 and Rku ≈ 3, includes TIG316 at all levels, TIG309 at Level 3, and ARC316 at intermediate-to-high levels, representing a stable EDM surface-generation state in which melting and resolidification aremore uniformly balanced. The third regime, characterized by mildly negative skewness and moderate kurtosis, is associated mainly with ARC309 and low-level ARC316, indicating valley-dominated surfaces that may be associated with heterogeneous fusion-zone response and localized resolidification effects.

These regime clusters demonstrate that surface integrity in EDM of dissimilar welds is primarily governed by the interaction between material properties and plasma confinement, rather than by discharge energy alone. TIG316 consistently occupies the most stable regime, while TIG309 transitions from instability to stability as pulse energy is optimized. ARC welds remain constrained by metallurgical heterogeneity, which moderates their response to changes in electrical parameters. This behavior is consistent with the comparatively improved surface response observed for TIG316, which may be attributed to the inherently more stable welding process associated with gas tungsten arc welding. TIG welding generally produces a cleaner fusion zone with fewer oxide inclusions and lower metallurgical heterogeneity than flux-shielded ARC welds, thereby promoting more uniform electrical discharge behavior during WEDM and reducing localized variations in crater formation. In contrast, the comparatively higher roughness observed for ARC309 may be associated with greater microstructural heterogeneity and localized oxide formation resulting from the flux-assisted welding process. These factors could influence the local electro-thermal response during machining and contribute to less uniform surface generation. It should be emphasized, however, that these explanations are based on established metallurgical principles reported in the literature and were not directly verified in the present study through microstructural characterization.

Overall, the combined STI and Rsk-Rku analyses establish a mechanistic framework for interpreting EDM surface evolution. Optimal surface integrity corresponds to neutral skewness and moderate kurtosis, indicating a balance between molten-material ejection and resolidification. By combining amplitude-based and statistical texture descriptors, the analysis helps distinguish stable, transitional, and unstable EDM texture responses without relying only on average roughness values.

Electro-thermal energy coupling and optimization

Definition of electro-thermal energy density

In electrical discharge machining (EDM), surface formation is governed by the thermal energy released during successive electrical discharges between the electrode and the workpiece. Each discharge generates a transient plasma channel, producing intense localized heating that melts and ejects material from the surface. The energy released per pulse, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{p}$$\end{document}, can be expressed as:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{p}=V\times\:I\times\:{T}_{\mathrm{on}}$$\end{document}

where V is the discharge voltage (V), I is the discharge current (A), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{T}_{\mathrm{on}}$$\end{document} is the pulse-on duration (µs).

Under the present experimental conditions, the effective discharge area and current distribution were treated as approximately constant within the selected EDM operating window; therefore, the current term can be incorporated into a proportionality factor. This leads to the definition of an effective electro-thermal energy density indicator, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{d}$$\end{document}, which represents the relative thermal load delivered to the surface:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{d}=k\times\:V\frac{\:{T}_{\mathrm{on}}}{{T}_{\mathrm{off}}}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{T}_{\mathrm{off}}$$\end{document} is the pulse-off duration (µs), and k is a normalization coefficient ensuring dimensional consistency and enabling comparison across different materials and process settings. This formulation captures the balance between energy input and thermal recovery: higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{d}$$\end{document} values correspond to greater relative electro-thermal loading due to higher voltage, longer pulse-on duration, or shorter recovery time, whereas lower \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{d}$$\end{document} values reflect longer cooling intervals and smoother surface formation.

The discharge current was maintained constant across all experimental conditions and therefore does not contribute to the comparative variation in electro-thermal loading between cases. Consequently, the proposed energy-density formulation focuses on the combined effects of voltage and pulse timing, which govern the relative discharge intensity within the present experimental window. The resulting quantity is therefore interpreted as a comparative electro-thermal energy-density indicator rather than an absolute thermodynamic energy density. Accordingly, the proposed framework does not constitute a first-principles predictive model of the EDM process; instead, it provides a physics-guided interpretation of experimental observations by relating surface-texture evolution to a normalized electro-thermal energy density derived from the applied discharge conditions. Its objective is to identify physically meaningful response trends across different welded systems within the investigated experimental space rather than to predict surface roughness beyond the examined process conditions. Accordingly, subsequent discussions referring to plasma confinement or discharge stability should be understood as physics-based interpretations of the experimentally observed surface morphology and electro-thermal response, rather than as direct measurements or simulations of plasma behavior.

Consistent with the conceptual frameworks proposed by Ho23 and Newman and Abbas et al.24, the ratio \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{T}_{\mathrm{on}}/{T}_{\mathrm{off}}$$\end{document} governs discharge frequency stability and dielectric de-ionization, while voltage controls the intensity of plasma heating. Together, these parameters define the net energy flux reaching the surface and influence whether the EDM surface response tends toward stable or unstable behavior.

To enable direct comparison among the four weld systems, the calculated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{d}$$\end{document} values were normalized to the interval [0, 1] using min-max scaling. This normalization facilitates correlation with the normalized roughness and texture metrics, which are introduced earlier. The normalized value, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{d}^{*}$$\end{document}, is therefore used as a comparative energy-density coordinate within the present dataset rather than as an absolute thermodynamic measure of heat input.

Practical optimization windows for TIG and ARC welds

Based on the validated energy-roughness relationship established in Sect. 4.2 and 4.3, practical EDM energy windows can now be interpreted for each weld system. These windows correspond to the lowest measured roughness within the tested parameter range and provide weld-specific guidance for avoiding excessive energy input or thermal instability.

For TIG316, the normalized energy-roughness trend indicates a wide electro-thermal stability margin. The minimum normalized roughness occurs at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{d}^{*}$$\end{document} = 0.023, indicating that smooth and consistent surfaces can be achieved under moderate energy input. In practice, this corresponds to discharge voltages of 11–12 V, pulse-on durations of 9–11 µs, and pulse-off times of at least 12 µs. Within this range, crater formation remains uniform, and the recast layer is thin, making TIG316 highly tolerant to minor parameter fluctuations.

The TIG309 weld exhibits a much narrower stability window. Favorable surface quality is achieved only at higher normalized energy, with the minimum roughness located at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{d}^{*}$$\end{document} = 0.524. This condition requires higher voltage (35–40 V) combined with pulse-on durations of 8–9 µs and pulse-off times of 11–12 µs. Operation below this range leads to unstable sparking and excessive peak-valley formation. In contrast, operation above it may increase the risk of localized thermal accumulation if extended beyond the tested range. Therefore, TIG309 demands precise control of discharge parameters to maintain surface integrity.

In contrast, the ARC welds benefit from lower effective energy input. For ARC316, the lowest-roughness condition occurs at intermediate normalized energy, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{d}^{*}$$\end{document} = 0.438, corresponding to voltages of 30–35 V, pulse-on times of 7–8 µs, and pulse-off times of 11–12 µs. At this point, melting is sufficient to smooth surface asperities without inducing substantial roughness amplification at the higher tested discharge level. Higher energy levels increase roughness due to excessive thermal loading.

The ARC309 weld achieves its minimum surface roughness at the lowest normalized energy level, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{d}^{*}$$\end{document} = 0.00. Practically, this corresponds to low voltage (10–11 V), longer pulse-on durations of 9–10 µs, and extended pulse-off times of 12–14 µs. Increasing energy beyond this range leads to monotonic degradation of surface quality, confirming that ARC309 is governed by thermal over-exposure rather than plasma instability.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that no universal EDM energy-selection rule exists for dissimilar welds, even under identical machining conditions. TIG316 and TIG309 attain their lowest measured roughness at their highest tested energy levels, whereas ARC316 favors an intermediate level and ARC309 favors the lowest level. The suitable discharge strategy is therefore weld-specific and must be selected based on the electro-thermal response of the fusion zone rather than nominal EDM settings alone.

These practical EDM energy windows provide a direct link between the mechanistic analysis presented earlier and real-world process control, enabling the selection of discharge parameters that balance surface integrity and machining efficiency. This framework forms a reliable basis for the concluding synthesis and future extension toward multi-objective EDM optimization.

Limitations and future work

A limitation of the present study is the absence of direct microstructural and phase characterization of the fusion zone. The proposed explanations involving inclusions, porosity, compositional gradients, solidification structure, and recast-layer development are therefore based on established welding and EDM behavior reported in the literature. They should be regarded as physically motivated interpretations rather than experimentally verified findings. Future work should combine profilometry with optical microscopy, SEM/EDS, XRD, or EBSD to establish direct correlations among weld microstructure, local composition, discharge behavior, and EDM-induced surface texture.

The present experimental matrix is limited to three discharge levels for each weld system and was not designed as a full DOE-based optimization study. Consequently, the fitted response trends should be interpreted as comparative descriptions within the tested parameter range rather than as statistically validated predictive models. A broader Taguchi, factorial, or response surface methodology incorporating additional discharge levels, discharge current variation, flushing pressure, wire tension, and independent replicate specimens is required to establish generalized optimization maps.

Although material removal rate, wire wear, and recast-layer thickness are widely recognized as important EDM performance indicators, the present work focuses specifically on surface-texture characterization and comparative analysis of different welded substrates. Integrating these machining-performance metrics with detailed metallurgical characterization would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the interactions between machining efficiency, surface integrity, and weld microstructure, and therefore represents an important direction for future research.

Although repeated profilometric traces were used to assess measurement repeatability, independent replicated EDM trials were not performed for each condition. Therefore, the present results support comparative surface-response trends within the tested matrix, while full ANOVA-based DOE validation requires future experiments with independent replicated machining trials.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the electro-thermal mechanisms governing surface formation during electrical discharge machining (EDM) of dissimilar stainless-carbon steel welds produced by TIG (316 and 309) and ARC (316 and 309) processes. By varying gap voltage, pulse-on time, and pulse-off time under controlled machining conditions, the resulting surfaces were evaluated using complementary amplitude-based (Ra, Rq, Rt, Rz) and statistical (Rsk, Rku) roughness descriptors. These metrics enabled a multi-scale assessment of both average surface quality and localized electro-thermal instability.

Distinct material-dependent electro-thermal responses were identified. TIG 316 exhibited a monotonic reduction in surface roughness with increasing normalized energy density, reaching its minimum at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{d}^{*}$$\end{document} = 0.023. This behavior reflects a stable plasma regime, uniform crater morphology, and efficient heat dissipation within its austenitic matrix. In contrast, TIG 309 displayed a sharp, nonlinear response, with severe roughness at low energy and a clear stability threshold at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{d}^{*}$$\end{document} = 0.524, beyond which surface quality improved rapidly. This sensitivity highlights the narrow electro-thermal operating window required to stabilize discharge behavior in TIG 309 welds.

ARC-based welds showed fundamentally different trends. ARC316 exhibited a convex energy-roughness relationship, achieving its minimum roughness at an intermediate energy level \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{d}^{*}$$\end{document} = 0.438, after which excessive energy caused crater overlap and re-solidified debris. ARC309 reached its smoothest surface at the lowest normalized energy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{d}^{*}$$\end{document} = 0.000, with roughness increasing monotonically as energy density rose. These findings indicate that ARC welds are primarily constrained by thermal over-exposure rather than plasma instability.

From these validated trends, weld-specific optimization windows were established. TIG316 achieved favorable surface integrity at moderate energy input (11–12 V, 9–11 µs pulse-on, ≥ 12 µs pulse-off), while TIG309 required higher voltage (35–40 V) and carefully balanced pulse timing to suppress unstable sparking. In contrast, ARC316 operated optimally at intermediate energy (30–35 V, 7–8 µs pulse-on, 11–12 µs pulse-off), whereas ARC309 required strict energy limitation (10–11 V, 9–10 µs pulse-on, 12–14 µs pulse-off) to prevent thermal saturation and excessive recast formation. These results confirm that no universal EDM optimization strategy exists for dissimilar welds; instead, parameter selection must be tailored to the weld metallurgy and its electro-thermal response.

Overall, the normalized electro-thermal energy-density framework provides a unified coordinate for comparative interpretation of weld-specific energy–roughness responses and for identifying favorable conditions within the tested parameter domain. It does not establish a universal optimum energy criterion applicable to all weld systems. Rather, it provides a physics-based foundation that may support future generalized optimization after validation across broader materials and machining conditions. By integrating response-surface validation with statistical texture analysis, the study demonstrates that EDM surface quality is governed by the balance between plasma heating and dielectric recovery rather than by absolute discharge magnitude. Beyond the experimental observations, the principal contribution of this work lies in establishing a physics-guided comparative framework for interpreting the EDM surface response of dissimilar welded joints using a normalized electro-thermal energy-density approach. Although the methodology has been demonstrated for the investigated TIG and ARC welded systems, it provides a systematic basis for comparing weld-specific surface behavior under controlled machining conditions. From an industrial perspective, the proposed framework offers practical guidance for selecting WEDM operating conditions that promote improved surface integrity in dissimilar welded components used in applications where dimensional accuracy and surface quality are critical. Future work should extend the methodology through comprehensive microstructural characterization, machining-performance evaluation, and validation across a broader range of materials and processing conditions.
