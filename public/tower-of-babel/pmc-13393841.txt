Ophthalmic and retinal artery occlusion associated with caudate nucleus infarction in an adolescent: a case report
Authors: Sejie Yu, Ye Wang, Tingxi Long, Suting Li, Jingnan Zhang, Yingying Wen, Xiaoyan Dou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionBoth ophthalmic and retinal artery occlusion and caudate nucleus infarction are forms of arterial ischemic stroke, which are rare in adolescents. The causal relationship between these two events is still unclear.Case presentationA 17-year-old Han Chinese boy presented with vision loss in his left eye for 24 h and was diagnosed with ophthalmic and retinal artery occlusion. The ophthalmologic examination showed no light perception and no pupil light reflex. The fundus examination revealed a cherry-red spot at the macula with white ischemic retina and optic disc. Intra-arterial thrombolysis (urokinase 100,000 units and papaverine 30 mg) was performed 24 h after symptom onset. On the second day postoperatively, the patient complained of headache and dizziness, and magnetic resonance imaging of the brain revealed subacute caudate nucleus infarction. Etiological workup including cardiac, coagulation, autoimmune, and inflammatory tests was negative except for borderline elevated cholesterol and positive CMV IgG with no evidence of active infection. Despite continuous treatment, the vision in his left eye remained sense light 2 months after he was discharged.ConclusionOphthalmic and retinal artery occlusion is an ophthalmologic emergency and deserves more aggressive treatment in adolescents. Caudate nucleus infarction is also an emergency that impairs health. In this adolescent case, the concurrent occurrence of these two events was rare, but the causal relationship remains unproven. Further etiological clarification is needed. More attention should be paid to the general condition of adolescents, and provide comprehensive assessments, treatment, and follow-ups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13256-026-06146-0.

Introduction

Childhood stroke is a serious cerebrovascular event in the age from 30 days to 18 years. Its incidence is not high, but it creats a serious threat to health and carries substantial economic and personal costs.

Eye stroke, ophthalmic and retinal artery occlusion, is a partial or complete obstruction of the ophthalmic artery often resulting in permanent and severe loss of vision [1]. Ophthalmic and retinal artery occlusion is a rare disease, with an incidence rate of 2 per 100,000 in the population [2]. The visual loss caused by ophthalmic and retinal artery occlusion is extensive and rarely reversible [3]. The caudate nucleus is one of the main structures of the striatum, which involves motor control, cognitive functions, and procedural learning. In this paper, we describe a case where ophthalmic and retinal artery occlusion occurred concurrently with caudate nucleus infarction in a 17-year-old boy. The pathophysiological link between these two events is still unclear. The patient received ophthalmic artery interventional thrombolysis immediately after diagnosis.

Case presentation

A previously healthy 17-year-old boy with sudden, painless, and complete vision loss in his left eye for 24 h was admitted to the Department of Ophthalmology at Shenzhen Second People's Hospital.

The patient was of Han Chinese ethnicity. His past medical history was unremarkable, with no trauma or family history. He also had no history of smoking, drinking or drugs use. The ophthalmologic examination showed no light perception in the left eye, with normal intraocular pressure and anterior segment. There was no direct pupil light reflex, but consensual pupil light reflex in the left eye. The fundus examination revealed a cherry-red spot at macular area with white ischemic retina and optic disc (Fig. 1A). Optical coherence tomography (OCT) showed macular edema in the left eye (Fig. 1B). In the right eye, the best corrected visual acuity was 1.0 and the ophthalmologic examination was normal. His body temperature was 36.3° Celsius, pulse rate was 78/min, respiratory rate was 20/min, and blood pressure was 115/62 mmHg. Although the patient presented 24 h after symptom onset, intra-arterial thrombolysis was performed because the patient had no light perception, no contraindications to thrombolysis were present, the patient was young, and the treating hospital had a strong protocol for interventional thrombolysis, and the family gave informed consent after discussion of potential risks and benefits. Initially, after receiving treatments such as oxygen inhalation, eye massage, and sublingual nitroglycerin, he was prepared for interventional thrombolysis. The patient’s left ophthalmic artery was observed occlusive with intraoperative angiogram, and the main ophthalmic artery and its branches of no visualization (Fig. 2A). After the microcatheter reached the initial part of the left ophthalmic artery, 100,000 units of urokinase and 30 mg of papaverine were injected. Repeated angiography demonstrated restoration of flow in the left main ophthalmic artery (Fig. 2B). Intraoperative cerebral angiography showed no significant abnormalities (Additional file 3: Fig. 3). After the operation, the patient was given Ticagrelor Acetylsalicylic acid to antiplatelet and Atorvastatin to lower the cholesterol after the operation. In addition, the patient received hyperbaric oxygen therapy.Fig. 2Intraoperative angiography showed occlusion of the left ophthalmic artery, with non-visualization of the main ophthalmic artery and its branches (A). On the first day postoperatively, the patient could sense light in the left eye. Repeated ophthalmic artery ultrasound showed the blood flow velocity of the left central retinal artery was 11/4.5/cm/s. On the second day postoperatively, the patient complained of headache and dizziness. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain revealed an abnormal signal in the left caudate nucleus, suggesting a subacute cerebral infarction (Fig. 3). Other sections of the brain showed no significant abnormalities in MRI (Additional file 4: Fig. 4). A treatment regimen was arranged for the patient, including antiplatelet agents, lipid-lowering and hyperbaric oxygen. During the next 2 weeks, the patient's condition gradually stabilized and he was discharged.Fig. 3Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain showed a T1-weighted hypointense signal (A) and a T2-weighted hyperintense signal (B) in the left caudate nucleus, consistent with a subacute cerebral infarction

At the 1- and 2-month follow-ups, his vision of the left eye remained sense light. Fundus examination showed the cherry-red spot resolved, but there was a macular scar with disordered pigment and the optic disc appeared pale with clear margins (Fig. 1C and E). OCT showed that macular edema had resolved, but macular thickness was significantly reduced (Fig. 1D and F).

Discussion

Ophthalmic and retinal artery occlusion is an acute ischemic stroke event in ophthalmology, which usually presents as acute, painless, and severe vision loss. This condition occurs mostly in the elderly, but its incidence is low in young people, especially adolescents. Common etiology and risk factors for ophthalmic and retinal artery occlusion include hypercoagulability, vasculitis, trauma, and vasospasm. Hyperlipidemia, hyperhomocysteinemia, polycythemia, thrombocytosis, coagulation abnormalities and so on lead to hypercoagulability. Vasculitis, on the other hand, can be caused by autoimmune reaction or infection.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous report has described the concomitant occurence of ophthalmic and retinal artery occlusion together with caudate nucleus infarction in an adolescents who received interventional thrombolysis. Only one similar case has ever been reported, an-8-year-old boy with severe Mycoplasma pneumonia who developed central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO) and cerebral infarction, including involvement of the caudate nucleus. That boy did not receive interventional thrombolysis and had no light 2 months after discharged [4]. In the case, the patient was a previously healthy 17-year-old adolescent with normal homocysteine, blood cell content, coagulation, except elevated cholesterol and CMV IgG.

Therefore, the first question to explore is the cause of OAO in this patient. Regarding the elevated total cholesterol (5.54 mmol/l), which was borderline high compared to age-, gender- and region-matched data [5]. Hypercholesterolemia is a known risk factor for CRAO in adults [6], but no studies have established this association in adolescents. Therefore, its role in this case remains uncertain. In addition, the patient had elevated CMV IgG (333.96 AU/ml) with negative IgM and no clinical signs of retinitis or systemic infection. While CMV infection has been reported in association with retinal vascular occlusion in the setting of CMV retinitis [7], such cases typically present with active intraocular inflammation. In the absence of retinitis or uveitis, the significance of isolated CMV IgG positivity is unclear. Therefore, whether this laboratory finding is related to viral infection in this case remains uncertain without further evidence.

The next question to explore is whether the caudate infarction was primary or secondary in this case. The patient had headache and dizziness without aphasia, sensory neglect, apraxia, upper limb hemiparesis, or dysarthria, finding that are typical of caudate infarction,, and brain MRI showed a small (approximately 3 cm in diameter) subacute infarct in the left caudate nucleus (Fig. 3). Cerebral angiography and MRI showed no evidence of large-vessel abnormalities, vasculitis, or other structural lesions (Additional file 3 and 4: Figs. 3 and 4). One possibility is an embolic event from an undetected source, such as cardiac-origin microemboli or aortic arch plaque detachment, but transthoracic echocardiography and carotid artery ultrasound revealed no definite abnormalities. The study has shown that ophthalmic and retinal artery occlusion is combined with cerebral infarction in the affected side, which may be related to multiple vascular risk factors in these patients, including hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, cerebrovascular disease [8]. Therefore, another possibility is an underlying hypercoagulable state, but routine coagulation tests and antiphospholipid antibody screening were negative. Therefore, a primary etiology remains unclear. Regarding secondary causes, the patient had a normal preoperative brain CT (Additional file 1: Fig. 1), and developed headache and dizziness on the second day after thrombolysis, when MRI confirmed subacute infarction. This temporal relationship suggests that the caudate infarction occurred after the ophthalmic artery occlusion and possibly after the intervention. In addition, the interventional thrombolysis was performed at the initial part of ophthalmic artery, which has passed carotid arteries (Fig. 2). The caudate nucleus is supplied by other branches of carotid arteries, direct procedural compromise of caudate blood supply is theoretically unlikely. Nevertheless, iatrogenic embolism cannot be completely excluded. There is one study showing 4.14% patients suffer transient ischemic attack or ischemic stroke after intra-arterial thrombolysis [9], but this association does not prove causation.

Conclusion

Ophthalmic and retinal artery occlusion and cerebral ischemic stroke are both emergency that contributes to a devastating impairment of health. In this adolescent case, the concurrent occurrence of these two events was rare, but the causal relationship remains unproven. Further etiological clarification is needed. Interventional thrombolysis was performed and the patient regained light perception, but whether this improvement can be attributed to the intervention remains uncertain based on a single case. This case highlights the importance of reporting such rare associations. We should be aware of the possibility of concurrent ocular and cerebral ischemic events in adolescents, and comprehensive assessment and follow-up are recommended.
