Long‐Term or Recurrent Antibiotic Use During Childhood or Adolescence and the Risk of Kidney Cancer: A Cohort Study
Authors: Jiani Liu, Xiangyu Chen, Lina Jia, Xiaotong Jing, Xiaoyan Pan, Xingyu Zhang, Zhong Jing, Jinqiu Yuan, Qiangsheng He, Xiaoyu Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAntibiotic use has been associated with cancer risk, but evidence regarding early‐life antibiotic use and kidney cancer is limited. This study aimed to examine the association between long‐term or recurrent antibiotic use (LRAU) during childhood or adolescence and kidney cancer risk and to assess the potential mediating role of adult adiposity.MethodsThis cohort study included 147,605 participants from the UK Biobank. We included participants with information on LRAU during childhood or adolescence who were free of cancer prior to baseline. Cox regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between LRAU during childhood or adolescence and kidney cancer risk. Mediation analyses were performed to evaluate the potential mediating role of adiposity.ResultsLRAU during childhood or adolescence was associated with a 48% higher risk of incident kidney cancer after adjustment for potential confounders (HR 1.48, 95% CI 1.08–2.02). The proportion of the observed association between LRAU and kidney cancer mediated by adiposity measures ranged from 4.3% for waist‐to‐hip ratio to 13.2% for body fat percentage.ConclusionsLRAU during childhood or adolescence was associated with a higher risk of kidney cancer, and mediation analyses suggested that adult adiposity may partially mediate this association. Further studies using prospectively recorded prescription and indication data are needed to replicate these findings.Keywords: adiposity, antibiotic use, cohort, kidney cancer, UK biobank

Introduction

Kidney cancer is a significant global health concern. According to GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates from IARC, kidney cancer is the 14th most common cancer worldwide, ranking 10th in men and 13th in women [1]. In 2020, an estimated 430,000 new cases of kidney cancer and 180,000 kidney cancer deaths occurred worldwide, accounting for 2.4% of all cancer diagnoses [1, 2]. The incidence of kidney cancer varies substantially across world regions, with an age‐standardized incidence rate of 12.2 per 100,000 in Northern America and 1.7 per 100,000 in South‐Central Asia [3]. Previous research suggests that the increasing prevalence of risk factors, including smoking, obesity, and hypertension, may contribute to this geographic heterogeneity and the rising burden of kidney cancer [4, 5]. For example, overweight or obesity contributes to 18.5% of the kidney cancer burden in the global population [4, 5]. Each 5‐kg/m2 increase in body mass index (BMI) was associated with a 24%–34% higher risk of kidney cancer in men and women [6].

Antibiotics have long been regarded as pillars of modern medicine, and their use has steadily grown over the past 80 years [7, 8]. In 2010, global antibiotic consumption exceeded 70 billion individual doses, representing a 36% increase from 2000 [8]. Although antibiotics have substantially reduced morbidity and mortality from bacterial infections [9], emerging evidence suggests that they can also markedly disrupt the human gut microbiome, with effects lasting for months or even longer [10, 11]. Early‐life antibiotic exposure may increase long‐term obesity risk through gut microbiome‐mediated alterations in host energy balance [12]. Given that obesity is a well‐established risk factor for kidney cancer [4, 5], these findings provide a rationale for examining whether adiposity may account for part of an association between early‐life antibiotic use and kidney cancer risk. Beyond adiposity, antibiotic‐related alterations in the gut microbiome may also be relevant through other pathways, including reduced production of beneficial microbial metabolites [13, 14]. Several epidemiological studies have suggested that antibiotic use is associated with an increased risk of cancer [15, 16]. For example, a meta‐analysis of 25 observational studies in 2019 found that antibiotic users had an 18% higher risk of overall cancer (odds ratio [OR] 1.18, 95% CI 1.12–1.24), as well as higher risks of several site‐specific cancers, including lung cancer, lymphoma, pancreatic cancer, renal cell carcinoma, and multiple myeloma [15]. In addition, three observational studies suggested that antibiotic use was associated with a 28% higher risk of kidney cancer (meta‐OR 1.28, 95% CI 1.14–1.44) [15, 17, 18, 19]. However, the associations reported in these studies may partly reflect the underlying indications for antibiotic treatment, such as recurrent infections or chronic inflammatory conditions, as well as related patterns of healthcare use.

Recent studies have suggested that the gut microbiota continues to evolve toward an adult‐like profile during childhood and adolescence and may be more susceptible to environmental influences than in adulthood [20, 21, 22]. This developmental plasticity implies that perturbations during this critical window, such as prolonged antibiotic exposure, may exert more profound and lasting effects on microbiota composition than similar exposures in adulthood. Long‐term oral antibiotics are commonly used for the treatment of acne, a condition affecting approximately 85% of adolescents worldwide [23, 24]. A retrospective study in the United States indicated that the average duration of antibiotic treatment for acne was 6.5 months, exceeding the recommended duration of 3 to 4 months [25, 26]. In addition, a study conducted in the United Kingdom reported that 48.97% of adolescents received antibiotic prescriptions, of whom 26.93% were prescribed long‐term antibiotics [24]. Given that previous studies have reported significant and persistent alterations in the gut microbiota after long‐term or recurrent antibiotic use (LRAU) for conditions such as acne or tonsillitis [20, 27], LRAU during childhood or adolescence could be associated with subsequent adiposity and, consequently, with kidney cancer risk. However, to date, no study has investigated the association between LRAU during childhood or adolescence and the risk of kidney cancer.

Given the widespread use of antibiotics during early life, examining this association may improve understanding of the potential long‐term health implications of early‐life antibiotic exposure. Therefore, we conducted this study to evaluate the relationship between LRAU during early life and the risk of kidney cancer, and to assess the potential mediating role of adult adiposity using the UK Biobank dataset.

Methods

Study Design

The UK Biobank is a comprehensive prospective cohort study that recruited approximately 500,000 participants aged 37–73 years from England, Wales, and Scotland between 2006 and 2010. At baseline, all participants were invited to the nearest assessment center to complete a touchscreen questionnaire and a face‐to‐face interview, provide biological samples, and undergo physical measurements and detailed assessments of health‐related factors. Details of the UK Biobank design have been reported elsewhere [28]. The UK Biobank study was approved by the North West Multi‐centre Research Ethics Committee, and all participants provided written informed consent. In the present study, the recruitment date served as the index date and the start of follow‐up. Eligibility additionally required a definite response (“Yes” or “No”) to the retrospectively reported question on LRAU during childhood or adolescence (n = 158,405). We excluded participants with cancer diagnosed before recruitment (excluding non‐melanoma skin cancer; n = 10,148), those with missing baseline BMI data (n = 368), and those who had withdrawn from the study (n = 284), resulting in a final analytic sample of 147,605 participants (Figure S1B). Baseline characteristics according to LRAU response status are presented in Table S1.

Assessment of LRAU During Early Life

Information on LRAU during early life was obtained from the 2017–2018 online follow‐up questionnaire. Participants were asked: “During childhood or as a teenager did you receive long‐term or recurrent courses (3 or more per year) of antibiotics (for example for tonsillitis or acne)?” Information on the type, dose, and duration of antibiotics was not available. In this study, the term “early life” refers to the period of childhood or adolescence, as stated in the questionnaire. Childhood was defined as up to 12 years of age, and adolescence as 13 to 19 years of age [29, 30].

Assessment of Incident Kidney Cancer

In UK Biobank, participants were followed through records linkage to the Health and Social Care Information Centre (in England and Wales) and the National Health Service Central Register (in Scotland). These registries recorded cancer diagnoses and deaths using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10). The primary endpoint of this study was the first incident primary kidney cancer (ICD‐10 code C64). Because metastatic cancers could not be reliably distinguished from second de novo primary cancers, participants were censored at the first diagnosis of any cancer, except non‐melanoma skin cancer. Cancer registration data were available through December 31, 2020, which was used as the end of follow‐up in this study.

Mediators

The primary mediator of interest in this study was adiposity, which was assessed using several body size and composition measures, including BMI, waist circumference, hip circumference, waist‐to‐hip ratio, and body fat percentage. These measures were obtained at baseline. Height, weight, waist circumference, and hip circumference were measured by trained staff and used to calculate BMI and waist‐to‐hip ratio. Body fat percentage was measured using the Tanita BC‐418MA body composition analyzer (Tanita, Tokyo, Japan). Because these measures capture different aspects of adiposity and may provide complementary information beyond BMI in relation to cancer risk, each was evaluated separately as a potential mediator [31, 32]. For mediation analyses, each adiposity measure was analyzed among participants with non‐missing values for that specific measure.

Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori based on known and putative risk factors for kidney cancer [33, 34]. Covariate information was obtained at baseline. Sociodemographic factors (age, sex, education, ethnicity, and household income), lifestyle factors (smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, fruit and vegetable intake, and red and processed meat intake), family history of cancer, and medication use were ascertained using the self‐administered questionnaire. The detailed definitions and groupings of the covariates were as follows: age (years), ethnicity (White or other), education level (less than high school, high school or equivalent, or college or above), household income (low, medium, high, or unknown/missing), socioeconomic status (index of multiple deprivation, in quintiles), smoking status (never, former, or current), alcohol consumption (daily or almost daily, one to four times a week, one to three times a month, special occasions only, or never), physical activity (low, moderate, or high), fruit and vegetable intake, red and processed meat intake (< 2.0, 2.0–2.9, 3.0–3.9, or > 4.0 times/week), family history of cancer (no, yes, or missing), and use of aspirin, non‐aspirin nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and statins. Fruit and vegetable intake was calculated as the total daily portions of fresh fruit, dried fruit, salad/raw vegetables, and cooked vegetables and categorized as < 5 or ≥ 5 portions/day according to UK NICE guidance. Family history of cancer was included as a covariate because it may serve as a broad indicator of shared familial susceptibility and related background factors associated with cancer risk. The index of multiple deprivation, a composite measure of socioeconomic status, was provided directly by the UK Biobank.

Statistical Analysis

We computed follow‐up time in person‐years from the date of recruitment (baseline) to the date of the first diagnosis of cancer, death, or the end of follow‐up, whichever occurred first. Although LRAU was ascertained through the 2017–2018 online follow‐up questionnaire, it referred to antibiotic exposure during childhood or adolescence, that is, prior to baseline. Cox regression models were fitted to estimate HRs and 95% CIs for the association between LRAU during early life and kidney cancer risk. The age‐ and sex‐stratified model used strata for sex and baseline age group (37–54, 55–64, or ≥ 65 years). We further adjusted the models for ethnicity, education level, household income, socioeconomic status (index of multiple deprivation, in quintiles), smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, fruit and vegetable intake, red and processed meat intake, family history of cancer, and use of aspirin, non‐aspirin nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs, and statins. For covariates with missing values (see Table S2), participants were assigned to the reference category for categorical variables or to the median value for continuous variables when missingness was < 5%; otherwise, an “unknown/missing” category was created.

We performed additional stratified analyses to examine potential effect modification by sex, age, education level, household income, smoking status, drinking status, and physical activity. We conducted several sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the primary findings. Specifically, we used overlap propensity score weighting, [35] additionally adjusted for hospital‐recorded tonsillitis or acne, applied multiple imputation with chained equations and complete‐case analysis for missing covariates, fitted a Fine–Gray competing‐risk model, additionally adjusted for baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and excluded participants with chronic kidney disease, excluded cases diagnosed within 2 years of baseline, and computed the E‐value. We also conducted a questionnaire‐date sensitivity analysis in which follow‐up began on the online questionnaire completion date and participants who developed kidney cancer or whose follow‐up ended on or before that date were excluded. Details are provided in the Data S1.

Mediation analyses were performed to evaluate the role of adiposity as a mediator of the relationship between LRAU during early life and kidney cancer risk (Figure 1). For this analysis, we assessed the mediating role of each measure of adiposity separately, including BMI, waist circumference, hip circumference, waist‐to‐hip ratio, and body fat percentage. We used the counterfactual mediation framework described by VanderWeele [35], which decomposes the total effect into natural direct and indirect effects and accommodates the interaction between the primary exposure and the mediator of interest. This method has been extended to the context of time‐to‐event analysis with Cox regression [36]. The counterfactual mediation framework relies on several assumptions, including no unmeasured confounding of the exposure–outcome, exposure–mediator, and mediator–outcome relationships, and no mediator–outcome confounder affected by the exposure. For each adiposity measure, the mediator model included LRAU and the covariates described above, whereas the Cox outcome model included LRAU, the adiposity measure, their interaction term, and the same covariates [35, 36]. We estimated the direct association of antibiotic use with kidney cancer independent of adiposity, the indirect association of antibiotic use with kidney cancer mediated through adiposity, and the proportion of the total association mediated by adiposity (Figure 1). All statistical tests were two‐tailed. Statistical analyses were performed using the SAS (release 9.4; SAS Institute Inc) and R software (version 4.3.3). The mediation analysis was conducted using the R package “mediator”.

Association Between LRAU During Early Life and Kidney Cancer Risk

During 1,685,572 person‐years of follow‐up (median follow‐up, 11.7 years), we identified 301 incident kidney cancer cases. In the age‐ and sex‐stratified model, LRAU was associated with a higher risk of kidney cancer (HR 1.55, 95% CI 1.13–2.11). The association was slightly attenuated after multivariable adjustment (HR 1.48, 95% CI 1.08–2.02) (Table 2), and no statistically significant effect modification was identified in the subgroup analyses (Figure 2). Our primary findings remained robust in several sensitivity analyses, with HRs ranging from 1.40 to 1.59 (Table S4). Of the 301 cases in the primary analysis, 192 occurred on or before online questionnaire completion. A sensitivity analysis using the questionnaire completion date as the start of follow‐up included 139,782 participants and 109 subsequent cases and yielded a similar but less precise estimate (HR 1.44, 95% CI 0.86–2.42). The E‐value was 2.32 for the point estimate and 1.37 for the lower confidence limit, indicating that an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both LRAU and kidney cancer by risk ratios of at least 2.32 each, beyond the measured covariates, to explain away the point estimate (Figure S2).

Discussion

In this large population‐based cohort study of more than 147,000 participants, LRAU during early life was associated with a 48% higher risk of kidney cancer. The observed association was generally consistent across subgroup and sensitivity analyses. Mediation analyses suggested that adult adiposity may partially mediate this association, although the findings do not establish causality or a specific biological mechanism.

Long‐term courses of antibiotics are widely prescribed to children and adolescents worldwide [23, 24]. However, several observational studies have reported positive associations between antibiotic use and several cancers, including lung, colorectal, and pancreatic cancers, lymphomas, and multiple myeloma [15]. Three studies focusing on kidney cancer reported a pooled OR of 1.28 (95% CI 1.14–1.44) [15, 17, 18, 19]. However, these kidney cancer studies generally assessed antibiotic exposure in adulthood or across the life course and differed from the present study in exposure definition and timing. A UK Biobank study reported positive associations between LRAU during childhood or adolescence and early‐onset colorectal cancer and colorectal adenomas [37]. Nevertheless, the cancer outcome and analytical approach differed from those of the present study. To our knowledge, the present study is the first to examine childhood/adolescent LRAU in relation to subsequent kidney cancer risk and the potential mediating role of adult adiposity. However, whether the observed association reflects antibiotic exposure itself, its underlying indications, or other related early‐life factors remains uncertain, and causality cannot be established.

Mediation analyses suggested that adult adiposity may partially mediate the association between LRAU during early life and kidney cancer, although this does not establish a biological mechanism. We found that LRAU during early life was associated with greater adiposity, and overweight or obesity has been estimated to account for 18.5% of the global kidney cancer burden [4, 5]. In fact, previous studies have shown that antibiotic use is associated with both an increased risk of obesity and cancer development [15, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41]. A recent mouse study suggested that prolonged antibiotic exposure during adolescence could induce metabolic dyshomeostasis, subsequently leading to increased adiposity that persisted after cessation of antibiotics [20]. This increased adiposity may be attributable to lasting disruption of the gut microbiota and liver metabolism [20]. Obesity may promote kidney cancer development through several mechanisms, including oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and insulin resistance [42]. Together, these findings suggest that adult adiposity may account for part of the association between early‐life LRAU and kidney cancer risk. Notably, the mediation proportions differed across adiposity measures, with weaker mediation observed for waist‐to‐hip ratio. This may be because body mass index and body fat percentage more directly reflect overall adiposity burden, whereas waist circumference and particularly waist‐to‐hip ratio capture body fat distribution rather than total adiposity [31, 32]. However, differences in the mediated proportions should be interpreted cautiously.

The mediation analysis indicated that adult adiposity accounted for only part of the observed association, suggesting that other pathways may also be involved. One possibility is persistent disruption of the gut microbiota following early‐life LRAU [20, 43]. Studies in mouse models have shown that prolonged antibiotic exposure during early life can result in persistent gut dysbiosis and lasting alterations in host metabolism [43]. For example, Carson et al. reported that minocycline exposure during adolescence produced sustained changes in the gut microbiome that may disrupt intestinal farnesoid X receptor–fibroblast growth factor 15 signaling, thereby contributing to persistent dysregulation of liver metabolism and adiposity [20]. The gut microbiota and its metabolites have also been implicated in kidney diseases, including chronic kidney disease, nephrolithiasis, and acute kidney injury, which are themselves associated with kidney cancer risk [44, 45, 46, 47]. Further research is needed to clarify these potential pathways.

Our study has several strengths, including its large sample size, long‐term follow‐up, comprehensive assessment of potential confounding factors, and additional analyses evaluating the robustness of the findings. We also used a counterfactual mediation framework to decompose the total association into potential direct and indirect components through adult adiposity. This study has several limitations. First, LRAU during childhood or adolescence was retrospectively self‐reported in the 2017–2018 online questionnaire and was therefore susceptible to recall bias and exposure misclassification. Information on antibiotic class, timing, dose, frequency, duration, and indication severity was unavailable, and the questionnaire did not distinguish use during childhood from that during adolescence, limiting our ability to characterize LRAU accurately. Second, confounding by indication cannot be excluded because reported LRAU may also reflect recurrent infections, inflammatory conditions, acne severity, or healthcare utilization. Although adjustment for hospital‐recorded tonsillitis and acne did not materially alter the findings, these diagnoses were incomplete proxies for the actual indications for antibiotic use. Moreover, adult adiposity and other covariates were measured at recruitment and may not represent participants' early‐life status. Detailed information on other early‐life health and behavioral factors was unavailable; therefore, residual confounding by unmeasured childhood or adolescent factors remains possible. Third, eligibility depended on survival and response to the 2017–2018 online questionnaire, which may have introduced survivor and selection bias. Although 192 of the 301 cases occurred before questionnaire completion, a sensitivity analysis using the online questionnaire completion date as the start of follow‐up yielded a similar but less precise estimate (HR 1.44 vs. 1.48). This suggests that the inclusion of pre‐questionnaire cases had limited influence on the observed association. However, participants with and without a definite LRAU response differed in several baseline characteristics, and kidney cancer incidence was higher among those without a definite response (0.277 vs. 0.178 per 1000 person‐years). Therefore, selection bias related to survival and questionnaire participation cannot be excluded. Fourth, although the UK Biobank is large, it is not nationally representative. Participants are predominantly White, relatively socioeconomically advantaged, and more health‐conscious than the general population [48]. This healthy volunteer bias may limit generalizability, particularly to more diverse or disadvantaged populations, although previous research suggests that it may have less influence on exposure–outcome associations [49]. Finally, the mediation analyses relied on assumptions that are difficult to verify and may have been affected by unmeasured confounding, downstream covariate adjustment, and model misspecification; therefore, they should not be considered definitive mechanistic evidence.

Conclusions

This cohort study found that retrospectively reported LRAU during childhood or adolescence was associated with a higher risk of kidney cancer, and mediation analyses suggested that adult adiposity may partially mediate this association. Given the observational design and limitations of exposure assessment, causality cannot be established. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence that clinically indicated antibiotics during childhood or adolescence should be avoided. Further studies using prospectively recorded prescription and indication data are needed to replicate these findings.
