Transdiagnostic divergent neural development of reward anticipation in early adolescents at risk for depression and psychosis
Authors: Yating Huang, Hui Xu, Wanling Zhang, Jingwen Jin, Ying Li, Chao Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReward anticipation is a transdiagnostic factor relevant to both depression and psychosis. Adolescence is a critical neurodevelopmental period when these reward-related circuits—including frontostriatal, limbic/insular, and striatal circuits may contribute to shared vulnerability across disorders, yet longitudinal transdiagnostic comparisons in early adolescents remain scarce.MethodsLongitudinal functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data acquired during the monetary incentive delay (MID) task were analyzed in 1839 adolescents from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study, assessed at three time points over four years (baseline, 2- and 4-year follow-ups) and classified as a control group (HC; n = 1380; baseline Mage = 9.51, 47.0% girls), high-risk for depression (HR_Dep; n = 217; Mage = 9.49, 44.7% girls), high-risk for psychosis (HR_Psy; n = 202; Mage = 9.51, 47.5% girls), and combined risk groups (HR_Com; n = 40; Mage =9.45, 55.0% girls). Linear and quadratic within-group trajectories and between-group differences in activation and developmental slopes were assessed.ResultsHC showed widespread linear increases in prefrontal, limbic/insular, and striatal regions (ps < 0.05), with neural activation changes in the left middle frontal gyrus and right accumbens following a quadratic trend (ps = 0.016–0.018). Compared with HC, HR_Dep exhibited progressive OFC activation and biphasic parahippocampal gyrus (ps < 0.020), HR_Psy showed reduced left NAcc (F = 5.77, p = 0.017), and HR_Com exhibited no significant longitudinal changes. Controls exhibited steeper longitudinal slopes than the HR_Dep and HR_Psy in the right suborbital sulcus, right subcallosal gyrus, and left nucleus accumbens (ps < 0.05).ConclusionHigh-risk adolescents primarily show disorder-specific reward anticipation abnormalities, with prefrontal–limbic imbalance in HR_Dep and striatal dysfunction in HR_Psy, while prefrontal deficits in HR_Com may index a shared transdiagnostic vulnerability across depressive and psychosis-spectrum risk.Keywords: Reward processing, Early adolescence, High risk of depression, High risk of psychosis, Transdiagnostic neurodevelopment

Introduction

Reward processing has emerged as a critical transdiagnostic construct across various psychiatric disorders (Liang et al., 2022). This neurofunction, which underlies symptoms such as anhedonia and apathy, offers a unifying framework to investigate both shared and disorder-specific neural dysfunctions across psychiatric conditions. Using the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) framework to identify transdiagnostic features of psychopathology (Cuthbert, 2020), we assess reward-related dysfunctions to more precisely characterize the developmental trajectories underlying shared neural impairments across depression and psychosis risk (Gandhi et al., 2022).

Reward processing is a multistage construct, and the Temporal Experience of Pleasure model highlights the importance of distinguishing between reward anticipation and reward consummation phases (Kring and Barch, 2014). Evidence suggests that reward anticipation is a critical locus of dysfunction across disorders. Individuals with depressive disorders often exhibit broad deficits encompassing both anticipatory and consummatory processes, indicating a generalized blunting of reward responsiveness (Mattoni et al., 2023). In contrast, schizophrenia and psychosis-spectrum conditions are characterized by more circumscribed reward impairments: consummatory pleasure is often relatively intact, with reward consummation pleasure often remaining relatively intact, whereas disruptions in reward anticipation are prominently associated with emotional dysfunction (Buck and Lysaker, 2013). Moreover, findings on reward consummation in schizophrenia are heterogeneous and may depend on symptom severity and task features, such as social versus nonsocial reward paradigms (Gard et al., 2007, Chan et al., 2025). Accordingly, focusing on reward anticipation provides a more consistent and transdiagnostically relevant window into shared and disorder-specific neural dysfunctions across depression and psychosis.

Alterations in reward anticipation are central across different stages of symptom severity and clinical progression. In depressive disorders, anhedonia, a core deficit linked to impaired reward anticipation, affects a substantial proportion of individuals and manifests across varying levels of symptom severity (Whitton and Pizzagalli, 2022), reflecting its central role in depressive symptomatology. In parallel, negative symptoms in psychotic disorders, particularly reduced motivation and goal-directed behavior, are strongly associated with reward anticipation deficits (Prettyman et al., 2020). These impairments in psychotic disorders are often persistent, with deficits in anticipatory processes comparable across early and chronic stages of illness (Pelizza et al., 2020).

Adolescence is a critical period for neurodevelopment Casey et al., (2008), during which key reward-related regions, including the striatum and prefrontal cortex, undergo substantial structural and functional remodeling Forbes and Dahl, (2012). This heightened sensitivity and ongoing circuit refinement may increase vulnerability to disruptions, contributing to early-onset psychiatric symptoms (Casey, 2015). The onset of depressive symptoms typically increases sharply during early adolescence, with many adolescents experiencing subthreshold episodes marked by attenuated anticipatory responses (Hanson et al., 2015), and many adolescents experience subthreshold depressive episodes characterized by attenuated anticipatory responses, which often go undiagnosed (Nardi et al. 2013). Early depressive symptoms have been linked to functional alterations in reward-related regions, especially prominent in early to mid-adolescence (Auerbach et al., 2022). It is essential to determine whether adolescents at high risk for depression or psychosis already show disruptions in reward - related regions than previously assumed (Duerden et al., 2016, Sen and Knolle, 2025a. Because reward circuits undergo substantial remodeling from infancy through adolescence—with some regions peaking in activity or structural maturation during mid-adolescence (Blakemore, 2012). These developmental variations raise critical questions for transdiagnostic risk: what developmental trajectories and patterns characterize reward anticipation dysfunctions across depression and psychosis risk? Addressing these questions is essential to understanding the early neurobiological markers of psychiatric vulnerability (Opendak et al., 2025).

Neuroimaging studies that have predominantly focused on adolescent depressive symptoms or psychosis-related symptoms suggest that reward anticipation engages a distributed set of brain regions, including the striatum, prefrontal cortex (PFC), and limbic regions. In adolescent depressive symptoms, the ventral striatum (VS) is a central hub of the reward network (Luking et al., 2016), shows particularly robust deficits, with longitudinal studies indicating that reduced VS activation predicts increasing depressive symptoms (Morgan et al., 2013, Silverman et al., 2015, Stringaris et al., 2015, Toenders et al., 2019). Beyond the striatum, alterations in prefrontal regions are associated with symptom severity: decreased activation in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) (Morgan et al., 2013), as well as reduced activity in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) (Belleau et al., 2021). In limbic regions, structural and functional changes—such as smaller amygdala volume and heightened anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) activation—also correlate with symptom severity (Albaugh et al., 2017). In adolescents with psychosis-related symptoms, although direct studies are limited (Karcher et al., 2025), fMRI evidence suggests that reward anticipation is associated with reduced brain activation in those exhibiting high psychosis-related symptoms. Evidence indicates reduced prefrontal and limbic activation during reward anticipation at age 14 (Papanastasiou et al., 2018), with later-stage increases in PFC and decreases in dorsal striatal activity by age 19. Additionally, Longitudinal studies of adolescents with a family history of schizophrenia reveal declining VS activation from ages 10–19. Together, these findings implicate disruptions in reward-centered regions (striatum, VTA, NAcc), regulatory prefrontal regions (mPFC, vmPFC, dlPFC), and emotion-related limbic regions (ACC, insula, hippocampus) (Joseph et al., 2016). Crucially, it remains unclear whether alterations in reward anticipation reflect common neurodevelopmental disruptions across disorders, or whether they instead indicate disorder-specific variability in the maturation of these regions.

Building on evidence of altered reward processing during adolescence, this study focuses on addressing critical gaps in the longitudinal development of reward anticipation in early adolescents at elevated high risk for depression and psychosis, alongside a control group. Specifically, our study aims to explore two key questions: (1) How do neural responses underlying reward anticipation evolve during early adolescence among individuals at high risk for depression, those at high risk for psychosis, and HC? (2) How do patterns and rates of change in reward-related neural activation differ between high-risk groups and controls over time? Based on prior evidence, we formulated two hypotheses. (1) Adolescents at high risk for depression and psychosis would show attenuated developmental increases in reward anticipation related activation in prefrontal, limbic, and striatal regions relative to healthy controls. (2) These alterations would include both shared and disorder specific patterns over time, with overlapping disruptions across risk groups as well as risk specific reductions in prefrontal, limbic, and striatal regions. Addressing these questions will clarify whether alterations in reward-related neural circuits reflect common patterns of developmental disruption in adolescents at elevated risk for depression and psychosis.

Method

Participants

This study utilized data from version 5.1 of the ABCD Study (Brown et al., 2023). Longitudinal neuroimaging and behavioral data were collected at three timepoints across four years. Participants were included if they completed the fMRI MID task and had valid CBCL and PQ-BC scores at all three timepoints: baseline, 2-year follow-up, and 4-year follow-up. Participants were excluded if K-SADS assessments indicated psychiatric conditions outside the domains of depressive or psychosis-spectrum symptoms. Notably, K-SADS was not used to define MDD or psychosis groups in this study. High-risk classification was determined longitudinally: adolescents showing subthreshold depression (CBCL T-score ≥ 65) or elevated psychotic-like experiences (PQ-BC > 1 SD above mean) at any timepoint were assigned to the corresponding symptom-defined risk group.

After applying these criteria, 1839 participants were retained, categorized into four groups based on psychopathology risk profiles: Healthy Controls (HC; n = 1380; 47.0% girls; baseline Mage = 9.51, SD = 0.50), High-Risk Depression (HR_Dep; n = 217; 44.7% girls; Mage = 9.49, SD = 0.50), High-Risk Psychosis (HR_Psy; n = 202; 47.5% girls; Mage = 9.51, SD = 0.51), High Risk for Comorbid Depression and Psychosis (HR_Com; n = 40; 55.0% girls; Mage = 9.45, SD = 0.50).

Measurements

The fMRI MID task is a classic paradigm of reward processing that assesses neural responses to varying monetary incentives and outcomes, providing a comprehensive characterization of the dynamic processes underlying reward anticipation. All fMRI data were acquired across 21 sites using harmonized protocols (Casey et al., 2018). Each MID task consisted of two runs, with each trial including a reward cue, an anticipation period, a response, and feedback. This study focused on neural activation during the anticipation phase, contrasting large reward gain versus neutral trials. A schematic illustration of the MID task paradigm is provided in Supplementary Figure S1 (Knutson et al., 2000).

Task-fMRI preprocessing and activation estimation were conducted within the ABCD Study, and ROI-level activation measures used in this study were derived from the corresponding processed derivatives. Functional images were corrected for head motion, B0 distortion, and gradient nonlinearity and were registered to structural T1-weighted images. Task-related activation was estimated using a general linear model implemented in AFNI’s 3dDeconvolve, including baseline, temporal trend, and motion regressors, with time points exceeding framewise displacement of 0.9 mm censored. The hemodynamic response function was modeled using the SPMG basis. Resulting contrast beta coefficients were parcellated using the Destrieux cortical atlas (148 regions), with subcortical ROIs defined based on FreeSurfer's Aseg segmentation (30 regions), yielding a total of 178 ROIs (Hagler et al., 2019). Mean beta values within each ROI were extracted and averaged across the two MID runs to obtain a single activation estimate per ROI for each participant.

The Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) is a standardized caregiver-report measure for behavioral and emotional problems in youth aged 6–18 (Kahfi et al. 2019). This study used the "Depressive Problems" scale to assess subthreshold depressive symptoms, with T-scores ≥ 65 indicating clinical risk. The CBCL has demonstrated strong psychometric properties across diverse populations and is widely used in both clinical and research contexts (Ebesutani et al., 2010).

The Prodromal Questionnaire–Brief Child Version (PQ-BC) is a 21-item parent-report measure developed to identify psychotic-like experiences in children aged 9–13 (Capizzi et al., 2024). It is a validated screening tool designed to detect youth at clinical high risk (CHR) for psychosis by assessing unusual thoughts, perceptual abnormalities, and suspiciousness. In this study, for participants assessed at multiple timepoints, the maximum PQ-BC score across timepoints was used to classify subclinical psychotic symptoms. Participants with total PQ-BC scores greater than one standard deviation above the sample mean were classified as exhibiting subclinical psychotic symptoms (Staines et al., 2024).

Statistical analyses modeling

Modeling developmental trajector in brain region activation

To investigate the developmental trajectories of reward anticipation–related brain function in both control adolescents and those at depressive and psychosis risk, we performed a model comparison procedure across four participant groups (HC, HR_Dep, HR_Psy, and HR_Com) using multiple linear regression models, supplemented with non-parametric bootstrap estimation. Time was treated as the primary predictor, with biological sex assigned at birth included as a covariate. For each group and each ROI, we applied a series of hierarchical models to determine whether developmental trajectories were better captured by linear or quadratic effects of time. Analyses were conducted across the 178 predefined ROIs. The null model, which included only biological sex as a predictor, provided a baseline estimate of ROI activation while accounting for sex differences. Building on this, the linear model incorporated a term for time, allowing us to evaluate whether ROI activity changed at a constant rate across the follow-up period. The quadratic model further extended this approach by including both linear and quadratic time terms, enabling the detection of non-linear developmental trajectories.

For each ROI and group, three models were fitted and compared using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with the lowest AIC indicating the best fit. Longitudinal effects were assessed via likelihood ratio tests (LRTs) comparing the linear and quadratic models against the null model. A significant LRT result (p < 0.05) indicated that the inclusion of time-related terms significantly improved model fit relative to the null model. An ROI was considered to follow a quadratic developmental trajectory within that group only when both conditions were met: (1) the LRT comparing the quadratic and null models was statistically significant, and (2) the quadratic coefficient itself was significant (p < 0.05).

Group differences in brain regions activation

To examine between-group differences at each time point, pairwise comparisons were conducted among the four groups for all ROIs using linear models. Estimated marginal means (EMMs) were computed, and pairwise contrasts were assessed. To assess the robustness and variability of these pairwise group differences, we performed nonparametric bootstrapping (1000 iterations). For each bootstrap sample, the linear model was refitted and new EMM-based pairwise contrasts were extracted. The distribution of bootstrapped contrast estimates was used to compute 95% percentile confidence intervals (CI) for each group comparison at each ROI and time point. Finally, the statistical significance of the observed pairwise differences was evaluated using unadjusted p-values, and multiple comparisons were controlled using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction.

Group differences in developmental rate

To examine between-group differences in developmental rates across brain regions, we identified ROIs that showed significant longitudinal changes based on earlier model comparisons. For ROIs exhibiting linear development, a simple linear model was fitted to estimate the rate of change (slope) over time. For ROIs exhibiting non-linear (quadratic) development, a second-order polynomial model was fitted to capture both the linear and quadratic components. For ROIs exhibiting non-linear (quadratic) development, the instantaneous slope at each time point was calculated by evaluating the derivative of the fitted quadratic model, representing the rate of change of ROI activation at that specific time. This approach captures how the rate of activation change evolves dynamically over the three study intervals (Baseline–Year 2, Year 2–Year 4, Baseline–Year 4). These slope values served as dependent variables in second-level analyses comparing developmental rates across groups (see formulas in Supplementary Material, Note 1). Pairwise group comparisons were conducted using the emmeans package with unadjusted post hoc contrasts, and the FDR was applied for multiple comparisons correction. To estimate confidence intervals for slope differences, we performed nonparametric bootstrap resampling (n = 1000 iterations) (All formulas used in the analytic procedures are provided in Supplementary Note S1).

Results

Comparisons of demographic and symptom variables

Baseline demographic variables showed no significant group differences in age (F (3, 1834) = 0.27, p = 0.849, ηp2 < 0.001) or biological sex (χ²(3) = 1.51, p = 0.681). For high risk of psychosis symptoms (PQ-BC), significant main effects of group (F(3,1832) = 976.98, p < .001, ηp2 = .615) and time (F(2,3664) = 186.12, p < .001, ηp2 = .092) were observed, along with a significant group × time interaction (F(6,3664) = 99.99, p < .001, ηp2 = .141). Simple effects analyses showed that at the second-year follow-up, adolescents with lower depressive symptoms had significantly lower PQ-BC scores than those with higher depressive symptoms (t = -2.481, p = 0.014, ηp2=.003). For depressive symptoms (CBCL), a significant main effect of group was found (F(3,1832) = 950.34, p < .001 ηp2 = .609), and a significant main effect of time emerged (F(2,3664) = 3.06, p = .047, ηp2 = .002). The group × time interaction reached marginal significance (F(6,3664) = 2.04, p = .057, ηp2 = .003). Conversely, when grouping by psychosis risk, depressive symptom scores did not differ significantly at any time point (ts = -1.208 to −1.721, ps = 0.090–0.228, ηp2s = 0.001–0.002) (Descriptive statistics are presented in Supplementary Table S1; Inferential statistics are presented in Table S2; A combined group × time interaction plot for PQ-BC and CBCL in Supplementary Figure S2).

In the PFC, HC showed linear increases across frontal regions including superior/middle frontal sulci, suborbital sulcus, gyrus rectus, and opercular inferior frontal gyrus (F(1,4137) = 4.16–14.07, ps < 0.05). In the limbic and insular regions, HC showed widespread linear increases across limbic and insular regions, including anterior and posterior cingulate gyri and long and short insular gyri (Fs(1,4137) = 4.59–9.73, ps = 0.002–0.032), with no quadratic effects. In HR_Dep, linear development was more restricted, observed in the right parahippocampal gyrus and subcallosal gyrus (Fs(1,4137) = 4.64–6.91, ps = 0.009–0.031), with quadratic development in right parahippocampal gyrus and left inferior occipital gyrus (Time_Quad_Fs(1647) = 4.92–8.55, ps = 0.004–0.027), reflecting initial increases followed by declines. HR_Psy exhibited linear effects particularly in insular and cingulate ROIs (Fs(1,4137) = 4.09–9.63, ps = 0.002–0.044). HR_Com showed linear change only in the right inferior insula (F(1,4137) = 4.70, p = 0.032).

Across striatal regions, linear developmental increases were observed in HC, including pallidum, accumbens, and putamen (Fs(1,4136–4137) = 6.02–10.10, ps = 0.001–0.014), with quadratic trajectories in right putamen and right accumbens (Fs(1,4136–4137) = 5.65–6.88, ps = 0.009–0.018). HR_Dep showed linear change only in left putamen (F(1,4137) = 4.48, p = 0.035), HR_Psy only in left accumbens (F(1,4137) = 5.77, p = 0.017), and HR_Com exhibited no significant change.

Overall, these findings indicate that the PFC, limbic and insular regions, and subcortical striatal regions predominantly follow linear developmental trajectories, with the most widespread and dynamic changes occurring in HC (additional longitudinal effects in other cortical and subcortical regions, e.g. parietal, temporal, occipital, central, and cerebellar see Supplementary Tables S4).

Group differences in neural activation across waves

Across the four groups, pairwise comparisons indicated that clinical risk groups exhibited lower activation in PFC regions compared with HC (Fig. 3). Regarding the prefrontal regions, at baseline, the HR_Dep group exhibited lower activation than HC in the right inferior precentral sulcus (t(1833) = 2.98, p = 0.017, d = 0.017). At the 4-year follow-up, HR_Dep showed reduced activation in the right suborbital sulcus (t(1833) = 3.23, p = 0.008, d = -0.151), whereas HR_Com demonstrated lower activation in the left gyrus rectus (t(1833) = 3.78, p < 0.001, d = 0.176). Within the limbic and insular regions, HC showed higher baseline activation than HR_Dep in the right ventral diencephalon (t(1835) = 2.97, p = 0.003, d = 0.139). At follow-up, HR_Psy displayed greater activation than HR_Dep in the right subcallosal gyrus (t(1833) = 2.82, p = 0.029, d = 0.132). In the striatal and subcortical regions, HC exhibited higher 4-year activation than HR_Psy in the left nucleus accumbens (t(1833) = 2.72, p = 0.040, d = 0.127). Overall, significant group differences were found in prefrontal, limbic, visual, and motor regions, characterized by reduced activation in HR_Dep relative to HC, and selectively elevated activation in subcallosal and insular regions in HR_Psy compared to HR_Dep (see Supplementary Table S6).

Group differences in longitudinal change rates

To examine stage-specific deviations, we compared slopes across Baseline–Year 2 and Year 2–Year 4 intervals for each ROI (Fig. 4; Supplementary Table S6). Across all intervals, HR_Dep showed steeper declines than HC in the right suborbital sulcus (t(1593) = 2.85, p = 0.009, d = 0.143) and right subcallosal gyrus (t(1593) = 2.74, p = 0.012, d = 0.137). Conversely, HR_Psy exhibited reduced slopes in the right suborbital sulcus (t(416) = –3.36, p = 0.003, d = -0.329) and flatter trajectories in the right subcallosal gyrus (t(416) = –2.80, p = 0.012, d = -0.274), reflecting delayed prefrontal maturation. In limbic and insular regions, HR_Dep showed flatter slopes than HC in the right parahippocampal gyrus across both intervals (Baseline–Year 2: t(1593) = 3.05, p = 0.007, d = 0.153; Year 2–Year 4: t(1593) = 2.97, p = 0.012, d = 0.149). Conversely, HR_Psy exhibited attenuated increases over the same periods (t(416) = –2.47 and –2.64, p = 0.014 and 0.017, d = -0.242 to −0.259). In striatal regions, HR_Psy showed reduced left accumbens increases relative to HC from Baseline to Year 4 (t(1578) = –2.69, p = 0.037, d = -0.135), suggesting largely linear trajectories with the greatest change in HC.

Discussion

This study tracked longitudinal changes in reward-anticipation neural responses among high-risk (HR_Dep, HR_Psy, HR_Com) and control adolescents to identify shared and disorder-specific alterations. HC showed typical linear maturation across prefrontal, limbic, and striatal regions, reflecting normative cortical thinning and functional specialization (Tamnes et al., 2010). HR_Dep showed attenuated and temporally delayed increases in orbitofrontal activation, elevated parahippocampal and subcallosal activation, and mild putamen growth. HR_Psy showed accelerated declines across frontal, cingulate, and striatal regions, suggesting a pattern consistent with relatively earlier or accelerated maturation of reward-related regions, while HR_Com displayed minimal change. At Year 4, HR_Dep demonstrated increased right suborbital activation, HR_Psy showed left accumbens hypoactivation, and HR_Com displayed gyrus rectus hypoactivation. Longitudinal slope analyses revealed clearer group differences than single-timepoint contrasts: HR_Dep showed accelerated prefrontal declines and flatter parahippocampal slopes, whereas HR_Psy exhibited attenuated prefrontal and accumbens development.

Healthy adolescents exhibited typical, coordinated maturation of prefrontal, limbic, and subcortical regions. Longitudinal analyses revealed marked developmental changes in PFC activation, with gradual decreases, suggesting increasing specialization and efficiency during reward anticipation (Friedman and Robbins, 2022). In limbic and insular areas, widespread longitudinal decreases were observed, including the anterior and posterior cingulate cortex and multiple insular subregions, consistent with normative maturation of prefrontal–limbic circuits supporting more efficient cognitive control and affect regulation (Joseph et al., 2016). Subcortically, HC adolescents showed increased activation in the right pallidum and bilateral putamen but decreased activation in the bilateral nucleus accumbens, consistent with the pallidum’s role in motivation and reinforcement (Soares-Cunha and Heinsbroek, 2023), the putamen’s involvement in processing and behavioral adaptation (Haruno and Kawato, 2006), and normative refinement of motivational sensitivity (Schreuders et al., 2018).

Our cross-diagnostic analysis indicates that the left gyrus rectus, a ventromedial prefrontal subregion, is the sole prefrontal locus showing a consistent, transdiagnostic reduction in activation during reward anticipation among comorbid high-risk adolescents, whereas other prefrontal subregions display disorder-specific deviations. This convergence on the gyrus rectus may reflect a shared alteration in vmPFC-related value representation and anticipatory valuation, rather than separable, diagnosis-specific pathologies (Braams et al., 2015, Rolls et al., 2020). This interpretation accords with mechanistic and systems-level work demonstrating that medial/orbital vmPFC—including the gyrus rectus—encode anticipated value and integrate contextual, and motivational signals guiding approach behavior, functions that are fundamental to reward processing (Parker et al., 2025). Recent developmental neuroimaging evidence extends these mechanisms to adolescence. Studies have shown that youth with elevated psychosis-like experiences exhibit blunted medial prefrontal activity during reward anticipation (Sen and Knolle 2025b). These convergent findings highlight the vmPFC/gyrus rectus as a transdiagnostic marker of altered reward anticipation, suggesting that the vmPFC may represent a promising neural target for future research on early identification across adolescent risk trajectories (Zhao et al., 2024). Importantly, from an RDoC perspective, these shared prefrontal alterations emphasize cross-diagnostic vulnerability rather than disorder-specific categories.

In our study, adolescent HR_Dep and HR_Psy groups exhibited divergent network vulnerabilities within the reward system: cortical–limbic dysregulation in those at high risk for depression versus altered striatal and incentive-related function in those at risk for psychosis. These disorder-specific patterns parallel findings from adult research. The depressive-risk profile in adolescence—characterized by heightened recruitment of orbitofrontal, subcallosal, and parahippocampal regions—mirrors the neural signature observed in adult major depressive disorder. Prior studies have identified the medial and orbital prefrontal cortices, along with the subcallosal cingulate, as key hubs of maladaptive affective valuation, linking these cortical abnormalities to impaired reward processing and anhedonic symptoms (Pizzagalli, 2014). In adolescence, disrupted orbitofrontal regulation may emerge early, may be associated with altered anticipatory valuation processes (Feng et al., 2022, Dunlop et al., 2017). Moreover, converging evidence highlights the vmPFC complex as a key neural substrate involved in mood regulation, underscoring its role as a mechanistic hub for depression-related abnormalities in anticipatory processing (Hamani et al., 2011). In contrast, the psychosis-risk pattern—marked primarily by left NAcc hypoactivation during reward anticipation with relatively limited cortical deviations—mirrors adult schizophrenia findings emphasizing VS dysfunction (Gadassi Polack et al., 2023). Consistent with this, prior research reports reduced VS activation during reward anticipation in schizophrenia and psychosis-spectrum conditions, reflecting aberrant dopaminergic signaling and blunted motivational drive (Arrondo et al., 2015). Developmental evidence further suggests that early striatal alterations may disrupt incentive salience and effortful approach behavior, which may contribute to developmental trajectories associated with maladaptive reward processing (Satterthwaite et al., 2016). From a developmental psychopathology perspective, these findings suggest that alterations in reward anticipation circuitry may emerge during early adolescence and could represent early neural markers of transdiagnostic vulnerability, potentially informing future efforts in early identification and prevention.

Developmental slopes of reward anticipation–related ROIs indicate stage-specific neural vulnerability that may inform early identification and intervention. Compared with controls, HR_Dep adolescents showed steeper prefrontal declines and blunted parahippocampal–limbic increases, whereas HR_Psy exhibited attenuated striatal growth and delayed maturation in select prefrontal ROIs. HR_Com showed greater parahippocampal reductions. Together, these patterns suggest both shared and disorder-specific developmental signatures within reward circuitry, with delayed prefrontal maturation across risk groups and more pronounced striatal attenuation characterizing psychosis risk. Such trajectory deviations may represent distinct neural markers of depression and psychosis vulnerability. Consistent with prior longitudinal work that distinct reward-circuit patterns differentiate adolescents who remain resilient from those who develop depressive episodes (Fischer et al., 2019). Similarly, pre-intervention variability in reward-related activity predicts response to cognitive-behavioral therapy in adolescents with anxiety and relates to later depression severity, emphasizing the relevance of reward circuitry for personalized preventive strategies (Westbrook et al., 2025). Together, these findings underscore the potential of reward anticipation ROI developmental slopes to inform early detection and intervention during critical adolescent neurodevelopment.

Our findings align with prior ABCD studies examining reward processing with the MID task, which consistently implicate ventral striatal circuitry in early psychiatric vulnerability. Several ABCD studies have linked depressive symptoms or familial depression risk to altered reward sensitivity in striatal regions, including the caudate, putamen, and NAcc (Freeman et al., 2023). Other work further suggests that reward circuitry varies across risk phenotypes, with differences observed in NAcc or anterior cingulate responses during reward anticipation (e.g., Kwarteng et al., 2021). Importantly, the present study extends this literature by examining longitudinal developmental trajectories of reward anticipation across three waves. These differences may reflect disorder-specific mechanisms of motivational dysfunction. For example, striatal dysfunction in psychosis has been linked to disruptions in salience processing and reward prediction (Howes et al., 2020). These differences may reflect disorder-specific mechanisms of motivational dysfunction: striatal alterations in psychosis risk may relate to disruptions in salience processing and reward prediction, whereas prefrontal alterations in depression risk may reflect changes in reward valuation and regulatory processes (Wang et al., 2021). By examining developmental trajectories rather than cross-sectional differences alone, the present findings highlight heterogeneous developmental patterns of reward circuitry across psychiatric vulnerability profiles.

This study has several limitations. First, although we examined 178 ROIs to capture distributed reward-related effects, this large-scale regional analysis increases the interpretational complexity and the risk of false positives despite statistical control. While bootstrapped confidence intervals mitigate this issue, the high dimensionality of ROI-level analyses limits the ability to draw strong region-specific causal inferences, which should be validated in independent samples or with complementary whole-brain approaches (Carp, 2012). Second, the multisite nature of data acquisition and developmental variability inherent to adolescent samples may have introduced site-specific noise, potentially reducing the reliability and spatial precision of activation estimates. This heterogeneity should therefore be considered when interpreting region-specific effects (Váša et al., 2020). Third, the developmental window was limited to early adolescence, restricting conclusions about how reward-related neural alterations evolve across later developmental stages. Future longitudinal studies spanning late adolescence and early adulthood are needed to clarify their temporal dynamics and clinical significance. Fourth, parental psychiatric history was not considered in the present study (Musliner et al., 2015). As a result, the control group may include adolescents with familial vulnerability to major depressive disorder or schizophrenia despite the absence of elevated symptoms, potentially reducing observable group differences. Given evidence linking familial liability to alterations in reward circuitry (Holz et al., 2018), future studies incorporating parental psychiatric history may help clarify whether the observed neural differences reflect current symptom risk or inherited vulnerability. And a further limitation is that psychosis-risk classification was based on PQ-BC endorsement scores rather than distress-based metrics. Emerging work suggests that distress associated with psychotic-like experiences may represent a more clinically informative indicator of risk, and future studies should examine distress-based PQ-BC measures alongside endorsement scores (Karcher et al., 2022). And although group differences in site and siblings were observed, their effects were small or limited and therefore not included in the primary models (Table S8-10). Future work may explicitly model site and family structure to further improve robustness.

Conclusion

This longitudinal study demonstrates that reward-related neural development during early adolescence follows predominantly disorder-specific trajectories among high-risk youth. Control adolescents showed widespread maturation across prefrontal, limbic–insular, and striatal circuits, whereas HR_Dep, HR_Psy, and HR_Com groups exhibited attenuated or regionally restricted developmental patterns. Depression risk was characterized by prefrontal–limbic dysregulation, psychosis risk by diminished striatal responsivity, and comorbidity by compounded prefrontal deficits. The only shared deviation—reduced gyrus rectus activation at the 4-year follow-up—suggests minimal overlap in the neural mechanisms underlying vulnerability across diagnostic risks.
