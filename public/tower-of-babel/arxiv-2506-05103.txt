Goodness-of-fit testing for the stationary density of a size-structured PDE Thanks: Ceremade, CNRS, UMR 7534, Université Paris-Dauphine, PSL Research University, 75016 Paris, France; E-mail: Vincent.Rivoirard@dauphine.fr
Van Hà Hoang, Phú Thanh Nguyen, Thanh Mai Pham Ngoc, Viet Chi Tran

Abstract
We consider two division models for structured cell populations, where cells can grow, age and divide. These models have been introduced in the literature under the denomination of ‘mitosis’ and ‘adder’ models. In the recent years, there has been an increasing interest in Biology to understand whether the cells divide equally or not, as this can be related to important mechanisms in cellular aging or recovery. We are therefore interested in testing the null hypothesis H 0 H_{0} where the division of a mother cell results into two daughters of equal size or age, against the alternative hypothesis H 1 H_{1} where the division is asymmetric and ruled by a kernel that is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. The sample consists of i.i.d. observations of cell sizes and ages drawn from the population, and the division is not directly observed. The hypotheses of the test are reformulated as hypotheses on the stationary size and age distributions of the models, which we assume are also the distributions of the observations. We propose a goodness-of-fit test that we study numerically on simulated data before applying it on real data.



Keywords: Statistical test; population dynamics; cell division; size-structured model; adder model; partial differential equations;

Introduction

The question of whether cells or bacteria divide exactly into two similar daughters or whether the division is asymmetric has been of interest to researchers in biology and mathematics for several years, see
Stewart et al., 2005
;
Evans and Steinsaltz, 2007
;
Lindner et al., 2008
.
For
Escherichia coli
, asymmetric division, which segregates damage at the expense of one of the two daughters, has been suggested as a strategy for population maintenance and an explanation for aging in these organisms. A simple model considers a population structured by size. When a cell or bacterium of size
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
grows with speed 1 and divides at a rate
B(x)
into two cells of sizes
\theta x
and
(1-\theta)x\geq 0
where the fraction
\theta
is a random variable of distribution
\kappa(d\theta)
with values in
[0,1]
, the evolution of the size distribution in a large population can be described by the following partial differential equation (PDE):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}n(t,x)+\partial_{x}n(t,x)+B(x)n(t,x)=2\int_{0}^{1}B\left(\frac{x}{\theta}\right)n\left(t,\frac{x}{\theta}\right)\kappa(d\theta)

(1)

\displaystyle n(0,x)=n_{0}(x),\quad n(t,0)=0.

Such equations have been studied by
Doumic et al., 2009
;
Perthame, 2007
. The question then amounts in testing the null hypothesis
H_{0}\ :\ \kappa(d\theta)=\delta_{1/2}(d\theta)
against an alternative where
\kappa(d\theta)
is a symmetric distribution on
[0,1]
centered at
1/2
(considering the daughters as exchangeable and labeled independently from their sizes). We will assume in this paper that under
H_{1}
, the alternative hypothesis, the kernel
\kappa(d\theta)
admits a density
h(\theta)
with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
, so that the alternative hypothesis writes:
H_{1}\ :\ \kappa(d\theta)=h(\theta)\,d\theta
.

In this paper, we also consider the ‘adder’ model introduced in
Taheri-Araghi et al., 2015
;
Hall et al., 1991
, see also
Doumic et al., 2018
.
In addition to size
x
, the population is also categorized by the increase in size
a=x-y
between the current size
x
and the size at birth
y
. The increment
a
can be viewed as a kind of age that increases monotonically and starts at zero at birth. The evolution equations replacing (
1
) are then

\displaystyle\partial_{t}n(t,x,a)+\partial_{x}\big(g(x)n(t,x,a)\big)+\partial_{a}\big(g(x)n(t,x,a)\big)+g(x)B(a)n(t,x,a)=0,

(2)

\displaystyle g(x)n(t,x,0)=2\int_{0}^{1}g\big(\frac{x}{\theta})\int_{0}^{\infty}B(a)n\big(t,\frac{x}{\theta},a\big)\ da\ \kappa(d\theta)

(3)

\displaystyle n(0,x,a)=n_{0}(a,x),\quad g(0)n(t,0,a)=0.

(4)

The term
g(x)
denotes the growth rate of a cell of size
x
. The division rate appears in this model in the form of a product
g(x)B(a)
, which can be interpreted as
B
being the division rate in a unit of growth instead of a unit of time. The cell somehow ignores the “time” and uses its growth as a clock. With the new single-cell data, some authors have observed that, for some bacteria, experiments favour the adder model over size-structured equations such as (
1
), see e.g.
Sauls et al., 2016
;
Taheri-Araghi et al., 2015
;
Campos et al., 2014
. It is one of the aims of this paper to base such observations on a methodology that is statistically sound. Statistical models for inferring the division kernels were carried out in
Bourgeron et al., 2014
;
Hoang, 2017
;
Hoang et al., 2022
;
Doumic et al., 2021
for example. In this paper, we tackle the goodness-of-fit testing of the null hypothesis
H_{0}
where the two daughters are identical against the alternative hypothesis
H_{1}
where the division is ruled by a kernel admitting a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
.

The standard goodness-of-fit setting treats the case of direct observations and one-dimensional density model. More precisely, one wants to test the null hypothesis
``f\in\mathcal{F}"
against the alternative
``f\notin\mathcal{F}"
(where
\mathcal{F}
typically equals some specified density
f_{0}
) from observations drawn with respect to the density
f
. Typically, a null hypothesis corresponds to our belief that the observed data are organized
in a relatively simple way, which means that the structure of the underlying model is completely specified. A test
\phi
is a rule to accept or to reject the null hypothesis by means of the observations. It is a measurable function of the observations taking values in the two points set
\{0,1\}
. The value
0
is treated as accepting
H_{0}
, and the value
1
means that
the test rejects
H_{0}
. If one assumes that
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, the construction of the test statistics will be based on an estimate of the distance
\int_{\mathbb{R}}(f-f_{0})^{2}
.
The quality of any test is measured by the probabilities of the corresponding errors. The probability of error of the first kind is the probability under the null to reject the hypothesis. Now if
f
is a function from the alternative set the probability of error of the second kind at
f
is defined as
\mathbb{P}_{f}(\phi=0)
and the value
1-\mathbb{P}_{f}(\phi=0)
is called the power of the test
\phi
at
f
. One rejects the null hypothesis if the test statistics is larger than some threshold calibrated such that the error of first kind of the test does not exceed some desired value (i.e the level of the test).

Standard goodness-of-fit testing has been widely studied since the famous Kolmogorov Smirnov and Cramer von Mises tests based on the empirical distribution function. For modern theoretical studies in a nonparametric setting (one does not assume any special parametric structure for the alternative which is expressed in terms of some smoothness classes such as Besov or Hölder classes), we refer to the pioneered work of
Ingster, 1996
;
Spokoiny, 1996
or
Fromont and Laurent, 2006
who propose adaptive minimax testing procedures. Regarding the standard framework of goodness-of-f testing, our statistical setting induced by our biological problem is original in many ways. First, our observations are indirect and do not follow
h
. Indeed, we are dealing with a non standard inverse problem induced by the PDEs (
1
) and (
2
) (for details, see Equation (12) and related comments in
Hoang et al., 2022
) and what is more with a two dimensional goodness-of fit test in the case of the adder model. Secondly, our null hypothesis does not refer to a density but a Dirac distribution in
\frac{1}{2}
.
And when considering literature about goodness-of fit testing, very few studies have been devoted to the case of indirect observations. Indirect observations involve some operator to be inverted which might lead to great instability in estimating the unknown density. For goodness-of-fit tests with indirect observations, let us quote the works of
Bissantz et al., 2009
for the inverse regression
problem,
Holzmann et al., 2007
,
Laurent et al., 2011
,
Butucea, 2007
,
Butucea et al., 2009
,
Lacour and Pham Ngoc, 2014
for the classical convolution model. In the sequel, we shall see how to overcome those difficulties by considering the stationary density arising from the equations governing the biological models.

In Section
2
, we detail the two models introduced above, namely the size-structured mitosis and the adder models. The goodness-of-fit tests are described in Section
3
. In Section
5
, we perform the tests on simulated data first to check their properties. Then, in Section
6
, we deal with a real dataset and discuss the modelling of E-coli data from
Bourgeron et al., 2014
;
Stewart et al., 2005
.

2
Populations of dividing cells

We are interested in population models for cells or bacteria undergoing division. Let us begin with a presentation of the two models considered in the paper, namely a mitosis model for size-structured populations and the ‘adder’ model introduced by
Taheri-Araghi et al., 2015
;
Hall et al., 1991
.

2.1
A generalized mitosis model for size-structured cell populations

The mitosis model associated to (
1
) corresponds to the large population limit in populations of cells or bacteria that divide at rates that depend on their sizes. Consider a population started from a finite number of individuals proportional to a parameter
K
, and whose sizes increase in time with speed 1. An individual with size
x
divides into two daughter cells at rate
B(x)
such that

\int^{+\infty}B(x)\ dx=+\infty,

(5)

meaning that the remaining lifetime of this individual has density

f(a)=B(x+a)\exp\Big(\int_{0}^{a}B(x+\alpha)\ d\alpha\Big).

Upon division, the mother of size
x
is replaced by two daughters of random size
\theta x
and
(1-\theta)x
where
\theta
is drawn in the probability distribution
\kappa(d\theta)
. This defines a sequence, parameterized by
K
, of individual-based stochastic population processes describing the evolution of the size distributions through time. When the parameter
K\rightarrow+\infty
, provided the sequence of initial size-distributions converges to a measure of density
n_{0}(x)
with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, then the stochastic processes converge to the (deterministic) solution of (
1
) (see
Tran, 2008
;
Bansaye and Méléard, 2015
).

In the case where the division rate is constant,
B(x)=R
, this functional law of large number has been established in
Hoang, 2017
, and it is shown that the correctly rescaled solution of the limiting PDE converges in long time to a stationary distribution that we denote by
N(x)
and that solves:

D(x)+2R\ N(x)=2R\int_{0}^{1}N\left(\frac{x}{\theta}\right)\frac{\kappa(d\theta)}{\theta},\quad x\geq 0,

(6)

where
D(x):=\partial_{x}N(x)
. Our goal is to test the null hypothesis
H_{0}:\kappa(d\theta)=\delta_{1/2}(d\theta)
against the alternative
H_{1}:\kappa(d\theta)\neq\delta_{1/2}(d\theta)
, to see whether we can assume that the division creates two equal daughters.

In the sequel, we will not assume that the fractions
\theta
are observed (otherwise
H_{0}
would be rejected as soon as a division producing daughters of different sizes is observed). The data rather consists in an
n
-sample
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
drawn from the stationary density
N
.
Therefore, instead of testing directly
H_{0}:\kappa=\delta_{1/2}
by estimating the division kernel
\kappa(d\theta)
that is not always absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, which would raise an intricate inverse problem, we establish that we can perform the test on the stationary function
N
which is more natural under this sampling scheme.

2.2
An adder model for populations with size homeostasis divisions

An alternative model to the mitosis size-structured equations presented in Section
2.1
is the ‘adder’ model presented in (
2
), also named as incremental model and introduced by
Taheri-Araghi et al., 2015
;
Hall et al., 1991
. In some cases, see e.g.
Sauls et al., 2016
;
Campos et al., 2014
, data point to a model with size homeostasis in that the division does not occur at a critical size (‘sizer’) or after a specific time laps (‘timer’) but rather when the organism has added a nearly constant size since the last division.

In the ‘adder’ model, cells are described by their size-increment and their size, respectively denoted by
a
and
x
in the sequel. The growth rate
g(x)
only depends on size, and the division rate is of the form
B(a)g(x)
. The latter assumption allows to interpret
B(a)
as the division rate in the time-scale defined by the size (and not the physical time), meaning that the cell uses its growth as a clock. Also, the increments of dividing cells are then mutually independent and distributed according to the density

f_{B}(a)=B(a)\exp\Big(-\int_{0}^{a}B(s)\ ds\Big).

In the present paper, we will often consider functions of the form
g(x)=r\,x^{\gamma}
and
B(a)=R\,a^{\eta}
, for
r,R,\gamma,\eta>0
.

It is then possible to define an individual-centered stochastic process as in Section
2.1
(see
(
Doumic and Hoffmann, 2023
, (2.10)-(2.11) page 25)
) and to show that it converges in large population, with similar arguments as before, to a deterministic limit given by (
2
).

Note that when
\kappa
admits a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure and
\kappa(d\theta)=h(\theta)d\theta
, (
2
) can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}n(t,a,x)+\partial_{a}\big(g(x)n(t,a,x)\big)+\partial_{x}\big(g(x)n(t,a,x)\big)+B(a)g(x)n(t,a,x)=0,\

(7)

\displaystyle\hskip 144.54ptt\geq 0,x>0,a>0,

\displaystyle g(x)n(t,0,x)=2\int_{0}^{\infty}g(y)\int_{0}^{\infty}B(a)n\left(t,a,y\right)\frac{1}{y}h\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)dady,\ \quad t\geq 0,x>0,

(8)

\displaystyle n(0,a,x)=n_{0}(a,x),\qquad g(0)n(t,a,0)=0.

Under
H_{0}
, when
\kappa=\delta_{1/2}
, the boundary condition becomes (see
Doumic et al., 2020
):

\displaystyle g(x)n(t,0,x)=4\int_{0}^{\infty}g(2x)B(a)n\left(t,a,2x\right)da,\ \quad t\geq 0,x>0.

(9)

The eigenvalue problem associated to (
2
) is now stated.

\displaystyle\lambda N(a,x)+\partial_{x}\big(g(x)N(a,x)\big)+\partial_{a}\big(g(x)N(a,x)\big)+g(x)B(a)N(a,x)=0,\quad x>a>0,

(10)

\displaystyle g(x)N(0,x)=4\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}g\big(\frac{x}{\theta}\big)B(a)N\big(a,\frac{x}{\theta}\big)\kappa(d\theta)\,da,\quad x>0,

(11)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{x}N(a,x)dadx=1.

For smooth and bounded functions
g
and
B
, the results of
Doumic, 2007
can be adapted, and they are stated in the case
\kappa=\delta_{1/2}
by
Doumic et al., 2020
.

Gabriel and Martin, 2019
studied the eigenvalue problem (
10
) when
g(x)=x
with fairly general division rate
B(a)
and fragmentation kernel
\kappa(d\theta)
, and obtained existence and uniqueness of the solution. Their proof can be extended for
g(x)=r\,x^{\gamma}
and
B(a)=R\,a^{\eta}
, see Appendix
A
, a model close to
Michel, 2006
for a size-structured population.
When
\kappa
has a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure, the convergence to
N(a,x)
of the correctly rescaled solution of (
2
) in long time can be established using entropy methods (e.g.
Doumic, 2007
;
Doumic et al., 2020
;
Gabriel and Martin, 2019
).

We denote by
N_{\delta}
the stationary density in the case of equal mitosis (
H_{0}
), when
\kappa=\delta_{1/2}
. In this case, the boundary condition becomes:

\displaystyle g(x)N_{\delta}(0,x)=4\int_{0}^{\infty}g(2x)B(a)N_{\delta}(a,2x)da,\ x>0.

(12)

Notice that when
\kappa=\delta_{1/2}
, oscillatory regimes may appear.

3
Reformulations of goodness-of-fit tests

3.1
Reformulation of the goodness-of-fit test for the size-structured mitosis model

We observe
n
independent and identically distributed (i.i.d) random variables
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
from the stationary density
N
. We want to test

H_{0}:h=\delta_{1/2}\quad\text{ vs. }\quad H_{1}:h\neq\delta_{1/2}.

Under the null hypothesis
H_{0}
, Equation (
6
) corresponds to the equation for equal mitosis with constant division rate
R

(
Perthame, 2007
, Section 4.1.1 p.83)
:

D(x)+2R\,N(x)=4R\,N(2x),\quad x\geq 0.

(13)

Instead of performing our test on
h
, we would like to perform the test on the stationary density
N
.

3.1.1
Injectivity

In order to perform our test on
N
instead of
h
, we need to prove the injectivity of the application
h\mapsto N
which is done in the next proposition.

Proposition 1
.

In model (
13
), the application
h\mapsto N
is injective.

Proof.

We denote
f^{*}
the Fourier transform of any squared integrable function
f
.
First, following
Hoang et al., 2022
, we make the change of variable in Equation (
6
)

g(u)=e^{u}h(e^{u}),\quad M(u):=e^{u}N(e^{u}),\quad D(u):=\partial_{u}(u\mapsto N(e^{u}))=e^{u}N^{\prime}(e^{u})),

and get that

g^{*}=\frac{D^{*}}{2RM^{*}}+1,

(14)

see Section 3.1.1 of
Hoang et al., 2022
for full details.

Now, suppose that we have for two stationary densities
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
such that
N_{1}=N_{2}
. Then
M_{1}=M_{2}
and
D_{1}=D_{2}
and thus
M_{1}^{*}=M_{2}^{*}
and
D_{1}^{*}=D_{2}^{*}
.
It follows that due to (
14
) that

g_{1}^{*}=g_{2}^{*}.

Due to injectivity of the Fourier transform, we have that
g_{1}=g_{2}
and hence
h_{1}=h_{2}
.
∎

The next section gives the expression of the stationary density
N
under
H_{0}
.

3.1.2
The stationary solution under
H_{0}

The stationary solution
N
has an explicit form
N_{0}
specified in the next proposition.

Proposition 2
.

The unique positive solution of (
13
) is given by:

N_{0}(x)=\bar{N}\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}(-1)^{n}\alpha_{n}e^{-2^{n+1}Rx}

(15)

with
\alpha_{0}=1
,
\alpha_{n}=\alpha_{n-1}\times 2/(2^{n}-1)
and
\bar{N}
a renormalizing constant.

See
Perthame, 2007
for the proof of this proposition. In particular, it is proved there that
N_{0}
is a positive function such that
N_{0}(0)=\lim_{x\rightarrow+\infty}N_{0}(x)=0
and such that its derivatives also vanish at
0
and infinity.

The computation gives that:

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}=\frac{2^{n}}{(2^{n}-1)\dots(2^{1}-1)},\qquad\sum_{n=0}^{k}(-1)^{n}\alpha_{n}=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(2^{k}-1)\dots(2^{1}-1)}.

3.2
Reformulation of the goodness-of-fit test for the adder model

3.2.1
Injectivity

We observe
n
i.i.d random variables
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
from the stationary density
N
, once again we want to test

H_{0}:h=\delta_{1/2}\quad\text{ vs. }\quad H_{1}:h\neq\delta_{1/2}.

We can rewrite the test
H_{0}:h=\delta_{1/2}
as
H_{0}:N=N_{\delta}
with
N_{\delta}
associated to equations (
8
) and (
12
) if the model is injective which is the statement of the next proposition.

Proposition 3
.

In model (
7
), assume that

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{+\infty}g(x)N(a,x)B(a)\ da\ dx<+\infty.

(16)

Then, the application
h\mapsto N
is injective.

The assumption (
16
) is common in the literature. Based on
Gabriel and Martin, 2019
, we provide a proof in Appendix
A
for the case
g(x)=rx^{\gamma}
and
B(a)=Ra^{\eta}
.

Proof.

Suppose that we have for two stationary densities
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
that
N_{1}=N_{2}
. We will show that the corresponding kernel
h_{1}=h_{2}
where
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are kernel division corresponding to
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
respectively. From the initial condition
(
11
)
, we have

\displaystyle g(x)N_{1}(0,x)-g(x)N_{2}(0,x)

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[N_{1}\left(a,y\right)h_{1}\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)-N_{2}\left(a,y\right)h_{2}\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)\right]g\left(y\right)B(a)\frac{dy}{y}da

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[h_{1}\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)-h_{2}\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)\right]N_{1}\left(a,y\right)g\left(y\right)B(a)\,\frac{dy}{y}da,

since
N_{1}(a,x)=N_{2}(a,x)
.

Changing the variable
x=e^{u}
and
y=e^{v}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle 2e^{-u}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{u-v}\left[h_{1}(e^{u-v})-h_{2}(e^{u-v})\right]g\big(e^{v}\big)e^{v}\int_{0}^{+\infty}N_{1}\left(a,e^{v}\right)B(a)da\,dv

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\Big[v\mapsto\big(h_{1}(e^{v})-h_{2}(e^{v})\big)e^{v}\Big]*\Big[v\mapsto g(e^{v})e^{v}\int_{0}^{+\infty}N_{1}(a,e^{v})B(a)da\Big](u).

Before taking the Fourier transform of the above convolution product, we have to make sure that the Fourier transform of each quantity is well defined. For the l.h.s of the convolution product, it is due to the fact that
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are densities. As for the r.h.s of the convolution product, we use (
16
) and obtain by taking the Fourier transform, that

\mathcal{F}\Big[v\mapsto\big(h_{1}(e^{v})-h_{2}(e^{v})\big)e^{v}\Big]=0.

Thus
h_{1}=h_{2}
, and this concludes the proof.
∎

4
Testing procedures

We are given
n
i.i.d random variables
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
with common unknown density
N
. We have shown that instead of testing
h
, our goodness-of-fit tests in both models, the simple one (in one dimension) and the adder one (in two dimensions), can be devised on the stationary density
N
.
Accordingly we shall test at level
\alpha\in(0,1),

H_{0}:N=N_{0,\delta}\quad\text{ vs. }\quad H_{1}:N\neq N_{0,\delta},

with
N_{0,\delta}
being either
N_{0}
or
N_{\delta}
, the stationary densities associated with the kernel division
h=\delta_{1/2}
according to the considered model.
To implement our testing procedure, we use the goodness-of-fit test proposed in
Fromont and Laurent, 2006
. This procedure manages to tackle the problem of nonparametric goodness-of-fit testing in a density model.
Assume that
N_{0,\delta}
and
N
are square-integrable. The test statistics is based on an estimation of the squared
L^{2}
-norm of the difference between
N
and
N_{0,\delta}
:

\|N-N_{0,\delta}\|_{2}^{2}=\|N\|_{2}^{2}-2\langle N,N_{0,\delta}\rangle+\|N_{0,\delta}\|_{2}^{2}.

The term
\langle N,N_{0,\delta}\rangle
is unbiasedly estimated by
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{0,\delta}(Z_{i})
. So, it remains to construct an estimator for
\|N\|_{2}^{2}
. Let us consider
S_{D}
a linear subspace of dimension
D
of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),d=\{1,2\}
and let
\{\varphi_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{D}}
be an orthonormal basis of
S_{D}
. The quantity
\|N\|_{2}^{2}
can be estimated by the
U
-statistics

\hat{N}_{D}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j=1}^{n}\sum_{l\in\Lambda_{D}}\varphi_{l}(Z_{i})\varphi_{l}(Z_{j}),

(17)

which expectation is equal to
\mathbb{E}(\hat{N}_{D})=\sum_{l\in\Lambda_{D}}|\langle\varphi_{l},N\rangle|^{2}
. Thus an estimator of the squared distance
\|N-N_{0,\delta}\|_{2}^{2}
is given by

\hat{T}_{D}=\hat{N}_{D}-\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{0,\delta}(Z_{i})+\|N_{0,\delta}\|_{2}^{2}.

(18)

Now, we denote by
t_{D}(u)
the
(1-u)
-quantile of the distribution of
\hat{T}_{D}
under the null hypothesis
H_{0}:N=N_{0,\delta}
and we consider

u_{\alpha}=\sup\left\{u\in(0,1):\quad\Prb_{N_{0,\delta}}\left(\,\underset{D\in\mathcal{D}}{\sup}\,(\hat{T}_{D}-t_{D}(u))>0\right)\leq\alpha\right\}.

We introduce the test statistics
T_{\alpha}
and the test
\phi
defined by

\displaystyle T_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\underset{D\in\mathcal{D}}{\sup}\,(\hat{T}_{D}-t_{D}(u_{\alpha}))

(19)

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1_{\{T_{\alpha}>0\}},

(20)

where
\mathcal{D}
is a family of resolution levels. We reject
H_{0}
if
\phi=1
.

This method amounts to a multiple testing procedure. We first construct
u_{\alpha}
and obtain a collection
\{\hat{T}_{D}-t_{D}(u_{\alpha}),D\in\mathcal{D}\}
. We then decide to reject the null hypothesis if, for some of the tests of the collection, the null hypothesis is rejected. Quantity
u_{\alpha}
and quantiles
\{t_{D}(u_{\alpha}),D\in\mathcal{D}\}
are computed by Monte Carlo replications. Specific numerical details are given in Section
5
about the basis
\{\varphi_{\lambda}\}
, the family
\mathcal{D}
, the quantity
u_{\alpha}
and quantiles
\{t_{D}(u_{\alpha}),D\in\mathcal{D}\}
.

The test procedure
\phi
defined in (
20
), which was proposed by
Fromont and Laurent, 2006
, enjoys nice theoretical properties that we shall present now. Let us consider the one-dimensional case.
Fromont and Laurent, 2006
studied the test
H_{0}:N=N_{0}
against alternatives expressed in terms of Besov bodies. We shall recall the definition of a Besov body. One considers a pair of compactly supported orthonormal wavelets
(\varphi,\psi)
such that for all
J\in\mathbb{N}
,
\{\varphi_{J,k}=2^{J/2}\varphi(2^{J}\cdot-k),k\in\mathbb{Z}\}\cup\{\psi_{j,k}=2^{j/2}\psi(2^{j}\cdot-k),k\in\mathbb{Z},\ j\geq J\}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}).
Then the Besov body
B_{s,2,\infty}(R)
is defined as

B_{s,2,\infty}(R)=\Big\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}):\quad\forall j\in\mathbb{N},\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\beta_{j,k}^{2}(f)\leq R^{2}2^{-2js}\Big\},

with
\beta_{j,k}(f)=\langle f,\psi_{j,k}\rangle
. The authors assume that
H_{1}
is expressed in the following nonparametric form:

\displaystyle H_{1}:N\in\mathcal{B}_{s}(R,M)=B_{s,2,\infty}(R)\cap\Big\{g:\ \|g\|_{\infty}\leq M\Big\}.

(21)

Given
\beta\in]0,1[
,
Fromont and Laurent, 2006
aim at evaluating the uniform separation rate
\rho_{n}
of an
\alpha
-level test of
H_{0}:N=N_{0}
against
H_{1}:N\in\mathcal{B}_{s}(R,M)
. The uniform separation rate
\rho_{n}
is defined as the fastest rate of decay to zero as
n\rightarrow\infty
which garantees a power at least equal to
1-\beta
for all alternatives
N\in\mathcal{B}_{s}(R,M)
at a
L^{2}
-distance
\rho_{n}
from
N_{0}
. In other words:

\rho_{n}=\inf\{\rho>0,\;\forall N\in\mathcal{B}_{s}(R,M),\;\|N-N_{0}\|_{2}\geq\rho_{n}\Rightarrow\mathbb{P}_{N}(\phi=1)\geq 1-\beta\}.

They obtained the following theorem.

Theorem 1

(
Fromont and Laurent, 2006
)
.

Let
\phi
be the test statistic defined in (
19
). Assume that
n\geq 16
and
\mathcal{D}=\{2^{J},0\leq J\leq\log_{2}(n^{2}/(\log\log n)^{3}\}
. Fix some
\beta\in]0,1[
. For all
s>0,M>0
and
(\log\log n)^{s+1/2}(\log n)^{2s+1/2}/\sqrt{n}\leq R\leq n^{2s}/(\log\log n)^{3s+1/2}
, there exists some positive constant
C=C(s,\alpha,\beta,M,\|N_{0}\|_{\infty})
such that if
N
belongs to the set
\mathcal{B}_{s}(R,M)
then

\rho_{n}\leq CR^{1/(4s+1)}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\log\log n}}{n}\right)^{2s/(4s+1)}.

We shall make some remarks about Theorem
1
.
The proposed test achieves the adaptive minimax rate
\left(\frac{\sqrt{\log\log n}}{n}\right)^{2s/(4s+1)}
proved by
Ingster, 2000
.
Adaptive
means that the test procedure does not use prior smoothness assumptions on
N
while
minimax
means that it is not possible to test
H_{0}
against
H_{1}
at a faster rate than
\rho_{n}
. In other words, if the distance between
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
is less than
\rho_{n}
then any test has a sum of errors probabilities of the first and second kinds close to 1 (trivial power). On the other hand, if the distance is of order
\rho_{n}
, then testing can be done with prescribed error probabilities.

5.2
Test on data simulated from the adder model

This section investigates our testing procedures using the adder model, as introduced in Section
2.2
. In order to perform the test, we first place ourselves under
H_{0}

N_{\delta}
. To do so, we first compute
n(t,a,x)

7

\dfrac{1}{\Delta t}\sum_{a}\sum_{x}|n(t_{n},a,x)-n(t_{n-1},a,x)|<\epsilon,

5.1

6.2
Fitting the mitosis model

6.2.1
Case of Constant Growth Rate
g(x)=1
and Division Rate
B(a)=R

Under this configuration, the stationary density
N(x)
satisfies the simplified model defined in Equation (
6
):

D(x)+2R\,N(x)=2R\int_{0}^{\infty}N(y)\,h\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)\frac{dy}{y},\quad x\geq 0.

Under the null hypothesis
H_{0}
(i.e., when
N=N_{0}
), this equation reduces to:

D(x)+2R\,N(x)=4R\,N(2x),\quad x\geq 0,

which admits an explicit stationary solution (Equation (
15
)):

N_{0}(x)=\bar{N}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\alpha_{n}e^{-2^{n+1}Rx}.

Given a dataset
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
sampled from
N(x)
, we estimate the parameters
R
and
\bar{N}
in the expression of
N_{0}(x)
using the following approaches:

1.

Method of Moments
: Determine
R
and
\bar{N}
by solving the system:

\begin{cases}\lVert N_{0}\rVert_{L^{1}}=\lVert N\rVert_{L^{1}},\\
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}xN_{0}(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{\infty}xN(x)\,dx,\end{cases}

(22)

where

\lVert N_{0}\rVert_{L^{1}}=\frac{\bar{N}}{R}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{\alpha_{n}}{2^{n+1}},\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{0}^{\infty}xN_{0}(x)\,dx=\frac{\bar{N}}{R^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\alpha_{n}\frac{1}{2^{2n+2}}.

The moment estimates are derived as:

\begin{cases}\hat{R}=\dfrac{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{\alpha_{n}}{2^{2n+2}}}{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{\alpha_{n}}{2^{n+1}}}\dfrac{\lVert\hat{N}\rVert_{1}}{\int_{0}^{\infty}x\hat{N}(x)\,dx},\\
{\bar{N}=}\dfrac{\hat{R}\lVert\hat{N}\rVert_{1}}{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{\alpha_{n}}{2^{n+1}}},\end{cases}

where
\hat{N}
is the standard kernel density estimator constructed from
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
.

2.

Least Squares Minimization
: Estimate
R
by minimizing the
L^{2}
-distance:

\hat{R}=\underset{R\in\mathcal{R}}{\arg\min}\lVert N_{0}-N\rVert_{2}^{2},

where
\mathcal{R}=\{0.01,0.02,\ldots,19.99,20\}
. The renormalization constant
\bar{N}
is then computed as:

{\bar{N}=}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\alpha_{n}e^{-2^{n+1}\hat{R}x}\,dx\right)^{-1}.

We first estimate
R
and
\bar{N}
using the biological dataset via both methods. A statistical test is then performed at a significance level
\alpha=0.05

Appendix A
Eigenvalue problem associated with the adder model

We consider the adder model with
g(x)=x^{\gamma}
and
B(a)=a^{\eta}
, where we omitted the multiplicative constants for the sake of simplicity. Also, we consider the case where
\kappa(d\theta)=h(\theta)\,d\theta
. The case
\kappa(d\theta)=\delta_{1/2}
has been checked in
Doumic et al., 2020
. Thus, let us consider the eigenvalue problem:

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\big(x^{\gamma}N(a,x)\big)+\partial_{a}\big(x^{\gamma}N(a,x)\big)+\big(\lambda+a^{\eta}x^{\gamma}\big)N(a,x)=0,

(24)

\displaystyle x^{\gamma}N(0,x)=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}a^{\eta}y^{\gamma-1}h\Big(\frac{x}{y}\Big)N(a,y)\,dy\,da,

(25)

\displaystyle-x^{\gamma}\partial_{x}\phi(a,x)-x^{\gamma}\partial_{a}\phi(a,x)+\big(\lambda+a^{\eta}\big)\phi(a,x)=2a^{\eta}x^{\gamma-1}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\phi(0,y)h\Big(\frac{y}{x}\Big)\,dy,

(26)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{+\infty}N(a,x)\,da\,dx=1,\qquad\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\phi(a,x)N(a,x)\,da\,dx=1.

Integrating (
24
) in
(a,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
, and using (
25
), Fubini’s theorem and the fact that
\int_{0}^{1}h(\theta)d\theta=1
, we obtain:

\lambda=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{+\infty}a^{\eta}y^{\gamma}N(a,y)\,dy\,da.

(27)

Note that in the case where
\gamma=1
, the total size
L(t)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{+\infty}x\,n(t,a,x)\ da\,dx
in (
2
) satisfies

\frac{d}{dt}L(t)=L(t)
, providing
\lambda=1
(see
Gabriel and Martin, 2019
), but this does not hold for
\gamma\not=1
.

Following the steps of
Gabriel and Martin, 2019
and defining
M(a,s)=N(a,a+s)
where
s
is the size at birth, we can see by computing the derivatives of
M
and
N
that (
24
) rewrites as:

\partial_{a}\big((a+s)^{\gamma}M(a,s)\big)+\big(\lambda+(a+s)^{\gamma}a^{\eta}\big)M(a,s)=0.

(28)

For
s
considered as a fixed parameter, (
28
) is an ordinary differential equation whose solution writes generically as:

M(a,s)=M(0,s)\times\begin{cases}\Big(\frac{s}{a+s}\Big)^{2}e^{-\frac{a^{\eta+1}}{\eta+1}}&\mbox{ if }\gamma=1,\\
\Big(\frac{s}{a+s}\Big)^{\gamma}e^{-\lambda\frac{(a+s)^{1-\gamma}-a^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}}e^{-\frac{a^{\eta+1}}{\eta+1}}&\mbox{ if }\gamma\not=1.\end{cases}

(29)

To obtain a solution of (
24
)-(
25
), it remains to find
M(0,s)
, which amounts in considering the boundary condition (
25
) which becomes in the new variable
M(.,.)
:

s^{\gamma}M(0,s)=2\int_{s}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{y}a^{\eta}y^{\gamma}\frac{1}{y}h\Big(\frac{s}{y}\Big)\,M(a,y-a)da\,dy.

(30)

Plugging (
29
) in (
30
) we obtain a fix point equation for
M(0,s)
.

For
\gamma=1
:
Multiplying both sides by
s\geq 0
and defining
f(s):=s^{2}M(0,s)
, gives:

\displaystyle f(s)=2\int_{s}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{y}a^{\eta}y^{-1}f(y-a)e^{-\frac{a^{\eta+1}}{\eta+1}}\ da\ \frac{s}{y}h\Big(\frac{s}{y}\Big)dy

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\qquad

\displaystyle f(s)=2\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{s/\theta}f(\alpha)\Phi\Big(\frac{s}{\theta}-\alpha\Big)\ d\alpha\ h(\theta)d\theta,

(31)

where

\Phi(a)=a^{\eta}\exp\big(-\frac{a^{\eta+1}}{\eta+1}\big),

(32)

is a probability density on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
corresponding to the lifetime distribution before division. This equation (
31
) is similar to the one in
Gabriel and Martin, 2019
. It can be rewritten as
f(s)=Tf(s)
where
T
is a transition operator linking the distribution of sizes at birth over successive generations. Notice that for
B(a)=a^{\eta}
, the survival function of the time before division is
\Psi(a)=\exp\big(-a^{\eta+1}/(1+\eta)\big)
that satisfies the Assumptions of
(
Gabriel and Martin, 2019
, Theorem 1.1)
, in particular
\Psi(a)=O(a^{-k_{0}})
for
a\rightarrow+\infty
and for any
k_{0}>0
. The operator
T
is a continuous linear operator on
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and Gabriel and Martin showed that there exists a unique solution in
L^{1}
of this equation, that is the unique fixed point of
T
.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{+\infty}x^{\gamma}a^{\eta}N(a,x)\,da\ dx=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{+\infty}x^{\gamma}a^{\eta}M(a,x-a)\,\mathbf{1}_{x\geq a}\,da\ dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{+\infty}(s+a)^{\gamma}a^{\eta}M(a,s)\,ds\ da

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{+\infty}(s+a)^{\gamma}a^{\eta}\,M(0,s)\Big(\frac{s}{a+s}\Big)^{2}e^{-\frac{a^{\eta+1}}{\eta+1}}\,ds\,da

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}s^{2}M(0,s)\int_{0}^{+\infty}(s+a)^{\gamma-2}a^{\eta}e^{-\frac{a^{\eta+1}}{\eta+1}}\,da\,ds,

(33)

where we used the definition of
M
and (
29
). In the right hand side, we recognize the Weibull distribution with shape parameter
\eta+1
,
a^{\eta}\exp(-a^{\eta+1}/(\eta+1))
, and therefore, for any
s>0
, the integral in
a
is finite and can be bounded by
Cs^{\gamma-2}
in every case (whatever the sign of
\gamma-2
). As a result, (
33
) above can be upper bounded by
\int_{0}^{+\infty}s^{\gamma}M(0,s)ds
, which is finite by the properties of
T
(see
(
Gabriel and Martin, 2019
, Lemma 2.2)
). This proves that (
16
) holds in this case.

For
\gamma\not=1
:
Proceeding similarly, with
g(s)=s^{\gamma}M(0,s)
,

\displaystyle s^{\gamma}M(0,s)=2\int_{s}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{y}a^{\eta}y^{\gamma}\frac{1}{y}h\Big(\frac{s}{y}\Big)\,M(0,y-a)\Big(\frac{y-a}{y}\Big)^{\gamma}e^{-\lambda\frac{y^{1-\gamma}-a^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}}e^{-\frac{a^{\eta+1}}{\eta+1}}da\,dy

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\qquad

\displaystyle g(s)=2\int_{s}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{y}g(y-a)e^{-\lambda\frac{y^{1-\gamma}-a^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}}\,a^{\eta}e^{-\frac{a^{\eta+1}}{\eta+1}}da\ \frac{1}{y}h\Big(\frac{s}{y}\Big)dy

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\qquad

\displaystyle g(s)=2\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{s/\theta}g(\alpha)e^{-\lambda\frac{(s/\theta)^{1-\gamma}-(s/\theta-\alpha)^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}}\,\Phi\Big(\frac{s}{\theta}-\alpha\Big)d\alpha\ \frac{1}{\theta}h(\theta)d\theta=:Sg(s),

(34)

where
\Phi
has been defined in (
32
). Notice that for
\alpha\in(0,s/\theta)
and whatever the sign of
1-\gamma
,

0<\exp\Big(-\lambda\frac{(s/\theta)^{1-\gamma}-(s/\theta-\alpha)^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\Big)\leq 1.

The existence and uniqueness of a nonnegative solution to (
34
) can be obtained from the existence of an eigenvector of
S
associated to the eigenvalue 1. This can be obtained by an adaptation of the proof in
(
Gabriel and Martin, 2019
, Theorems 3.1, 3.3)
.

Also a similar computation as in (
33
) leads to
\int_{0}^{+\infty}s^{\gamma}M(0,s)\,ds
whose finiteness can be proven be extending Lemma 2.2 in
Gabriel and Martin, 2019
.
