Sunflower-like self-sustainable plant-wearable sensing probe
Authors: Shuang Wang, Yangfan Chai, Huiwen Sa, Weikang Ye, Qian Wang, Yu Zou, Xuan Luo, Lijuan Xie, Xiangjiang Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Powering and communicating with wearable devices on bio-interfaces is challenging due to strict weight, size, and resource constraints. This study presents a sunflower-like plant-wearable sensing device that harnesses solar energy, achieving complete energy self-sustainability for long-term monitoring of plant sap flow, a crucial indicator of plant health. It features foldable solar panels along with all essential flexible electronic components, resulting in a compact system that is lightweight enough for small plants. To tackle the low-energy density of solar power, we developed an ultralow-energy light communication mechanism inspired by fireflies. Together with unmanned aerial vehicles and deep learning algorithms, this approach enables efficient data retrieval from multiple devices across large agricultural fields. With its simple deployment, it shows great potential as a low-cost plant phenotyping tool. We believe our energy and communication solution for wearable devices can be extended to similar resource-limited and challenging scenarios, leading to exciting applications.

INTRODUCTION

Wearable electronics bridge the gap between conventional silicon-based devices and the flexible, elastic nature of biological organisms. By seamlessly integrating with bio-interfaces, these devices unlock functionalities previously unattainable with traditional silicon electronics, including wearable or implantable sensors for humans (1–4), animals (5, 6), and plants (7–10); artificial skins for robots (11, 12); brain-machine interfaces (13, 14); etc. Despite success in wearable electronics, one challenge arises in powering these devices on intricate bio-interfaces such as human, animal, and plant surfaces. Similar to traditional devices, wearable electronics also consume considerable energy for tasks such as target monitoring, signal processing, data communicating, result displaying, etc. A widely used energy source for these devices is the traditional chemical batteries, which suffered from the limited energy density and resulted in a limited device lifetime (15–17). Therefore, many wearable electronics require an off-board energy supply via bulky cables, which greatly restricts the wearer’s freedom of movement and completely diminishes the meaning of being a wearable device. Any wearable devices must incorporate flexibility, elasticity, durability, and appropriate structural design and sizing for comfortable wear. Therefore, the challenge persists in realizing untethered long-term energy self-sustainable wearable devices.

A viable approach to achieving energy self-sustainability in wearable devices involves harnessing energy from the environment or the wearer’s body. In human wearable technology, thermoelectric generators (18, 19) and triboelectric nanogenerators (20, 21) can convert body heat or motion into electrical energy, while biofuel cells (22–24) can extract power from bodily fluids (such as blood or stomach acid) for implantable devices. Meanwhile, radio frequency (RF) circuits (25, 26) and piezoelectric nanogenerators (PENGs) (27, 28) can convert externally inputted electromagnetic and ultrasonic waves into energy, enabling wireless charging of the devices. However, these approaches are generally unsuitable for plant-wearable devices, primarily because plants lack obvious movement, heat sources, or bodily fluids. In addition, RF circuits and PENGs suffer from short operational ranges (typically only a few centimeters), rendering them impractical for plant-wearable devices deployed in large agricultural fields. Although photovoltaic (PV) cells (29–31) provide a relatively reliable energy source for plant-wearable devices in fields, the low-energy density of solar energy often makes them too heavy and bulky for delicate plants to support. Therefore, optimizing power efficiency, ensuring reliable communication under challenging conditions, and considering environmental constraints are pivotal factors for the sustained continuous operation of these devices, particularly in remote and inaccessible locations.

In this study, as shown in Fig. 1A, we introduce a bio-inspired, sunflower-like wearable sensing probe that mimics living plants by harvesting solar energy, achieving total energy self-sustainability for long-term operation. Our probe integrates a lightweight, foldable solar panel array, flexible electronic sensors, and all essential components, resulting in a highly compact system that is light enough for small plants to support (approximately 40 g). This design enables efficient solar energy harvesting on delicate plant stems and facilitates long-term monitoring of sap flow, a vital plant physiological indicator of plant health, growth dynamics, and water use efficiency (32). To address the challenge of low-energy density in solar power and achieve energy self-sustainability, we have developed an innovative ultralow-energy light communication approach. This approach draws inspiration from the signaling behavior of fireflies and uses distinct patterns of light signals for communication, minimizing total energy consumption to approximately 1773 J/day, notably lower than mainstream alternatives such as Wi-Fi, Zigbee, Bluetooth, and LoRa. To facilitate remote and wireless data retrieval in farm fields, an unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) equipped with a digital camera can be dispatched to collect the data from the probes, just like a bee collecting honey from flowers. We further developed a deep learning image processing algorithm that enables collecting data from multiple probes across large agricultural areas, thus greatly improving the data collection efficiency of this approach. Our probe holds high promise for plant phenotyping applications in greenhouse and farm field settings, offering a cost-effective and high-throughput tool for monitoring plant traits due to the elimination of the costs associated with extensive farm infrastructure modifications, including power supply, communication network setup, etc. Beyond agricultural applications, we believe energy self-sustainable solution for plant-wearable devices also shows potential promising applications in similarly resource-limited and challenging remote environments, such as tracking wild animals.

RESULTS

Design and fabrication of the bio-inspired self-sustainable plant-wearable sensing probe

Figure 1B illustrates the architecture of our self-sustainable plant-wearable sensing device, comprising three primary components. Initially, laser-cut foldable silicon solar panels mimicking a sunflower motif, mounted on a flexible polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) film, are used for harvesting solar energy. This energy is stored using eight miniature Li-ion batteries situated beneath the solar panels, providing power to a flexible electronic sensor for monitoring plant sap flow and a control unit. To enhance energy efficiency and achieve self-sustainability, an unconventional low-power consumption communication approach is adopted, in which the obtained data is transmitted via a unique light signal pattern emitted by an ultralow-power light-emitting diode (LED) integrated into the solar panels. This signal can be remotely received and deciphered by imaging devices, such as an unmanned aerial drone (UAV) equipped with a digital camera. Meanwhile, the foldable solar panels can also receive light signals at night, enabling bidirectional communication. The entire system is interconnected by stretchable serpentine copper conductive tracks sandwiched between two polyimide layers and housed within a three-dimensional (3D) printed soft flower-shaped housing, showcasing stretchability and lightweight properties and meeting the requisite criteria for a plant-wearable device.

Figure 1C presents the developed plant-wearable sensing device. Figure 1D shows an image of the foldable solar panel that consists of eight independent silicon solar cell petals that have been precisely laser-cut into petal shapes, mimicking those of a sunflower. These petals are affixed to a thin PDMS film and linked together by flexible serpentine copper conductive tracks, forming a distinctive “sunflower” configuration. At the center of the foldable solar panel, a small LED is embedded, functioning as a light transmitter for communication and data transmission (as depicted in Fig. 1E). In addition, Fig. 1E highlights the stretchable serpentine copper conductive tracks (thickness: 6 μm). Figure 1F illustrates the fabricated plant sap flow sensor, characterized by its softness, flexibility, small size, and lightweight nature. All components are integrated with a small flexible printed circuit board (FPCB) control unit, which can be housed within the device enclosure. The entire system is highly compact and lightweight, meeting the stringent requirements for plant-wearable sensors. Fabrication steps are detailed in the Supplementary Materials (figs. S1 to S4). Most fabrication processes use straightforward direct laser cutting and 3D printing techniques, which are more efficient and faster than traditional photolithography methods. For future mass production, more cost-effective techniques such as injection molding and screen printing are also viable options.

The flexible solar energy harvesting and sensing performance of the probe

As shown in Fig. 2A, we engineered a flexible, stretchable, and foldable solar panel setup to create a self-sustainable plant-wearable system. The circuit schematic is presented in Fig. 2B. The harvested sunlight energy is directly used to power the control board and other electronic components after passing through a voltage stabilizer. Any surplus energy is stored in eight small Li-ion batteries (diameter: 4.5 mm, length: 23 mm, total capacity: 200 mAh) positioned directly beneath the solar panels. These batteries provide a backup to cover any potential energy deficits. Figure 2C illustrates the solar power harvested by the solar panels under varying light intensities, with the light-to-energy conversion rate of our system being approximately 19.49%, which falls within the typical range for silicon solar cells. Figure 2D shows the daily energy harvesting under different weather conditions: sunny (3376 J), cloudy (2269 J), and rainy (292 J). Because the daily energy consumption of our device was measured to be 1773 J, the results indicate that the energy generated by the solar panels is sufficient to meet the system’s needs (sensor active time: 18 hours/day, sampling rate: 2 times/hour). In extreme conditions, when supplemented by energy stored in the Li-ion batteries (2664 J), our device can survive through a period of seven consecutive rainy days (a rare occurrence in agricultural settings) and maintain its basic functions (see table S1 for details).

Beyond aesthetic considerations, our sunflower-shaped solar panels generate 11.8% more energy than flat counterparts (see fig. S5). A previous study (33) indicates that this bionic design enhances air circulation between components, effectively dissipating heat from continuous direct sunlight and improving energy harvesting efficiency. In addition, the symmetric heliotropic structure of sunflowers allows for an even distribution of loads from solar panels along the central axis, which acts as a pivot point, thereby reducing unbalanced stress on the plant and potentially increasing the device’s stability on the plant stem.

The installation orientation of the solar panels must be carefully chosen to optimize efficiency. Because this initial orientation could unintentionally shift due to environmental factors (such as wind) or plant growth, we further investigate the impact of its tilt angles on energy harvesting. As shown in fig. S6 (Supplementary Materials), tilt angles ranging from 0° to 90° cause only minimal impact on energy harvesting, with roughly a 17% difference between the best and worst cases. Given that our solar panels can produce nearly 100% more energy (3376 J) than needed (1773 J) in good weather, these variations do not compromise the energy self-sustainability of our probe.

Figure 2E illustrates the sensing mechanism of the plant sap flow sensor. The operation of the thermistor in the sensor leads to a rapid increase in local temperature. In the presence of sap flow, heat is predominantly transported downstream by the flow, inducing anisotropic thermal propagation along the plant stem. This phenomenon enables the measurement of the sap flow rate within the stem. To validate this hypothesis, infrared (IR) thermography results shown in Fig. 2E illustrate the anisotropic temperature distribution in the presence of sap flow, which can be used to ascertain the flow rate and direction. Figure 2F further demonstrates that the downstream sensor (Tdownstream) exhibits a faster temperature rise compared to the upstream sensor (Tupstream). A linear correlation between the time difference (Δt) and the sap flow rate (R) was observed. Therefore, Δt can be used indirectly to determine plant sap flow. Further details of the sap flow sensor are available in our previous work (10). To sum up, our sensing probe integrates efficient energy harvesting capabilities and sap flow monitoring, providing a self-sustainable solution for real-time analysis.

Bio-inspired light communication and remote control of the device

To address the challenge of low-energy density in solar power and achieve energy self-sustainability, we have developed an innovative ultralow-energy light communication system inspired by the signaling behavior of fireflies. This system uses a distinct pattern of light signals for communication and control. As illustrated in Fig. 3A, we have integrated a small LED directly into solar panels as a light transmitter capable of conveying sap flow rate data through coded light signals. These signals can be easily captured remotely, for example, by a UAV equipped with a digital camera. Furthermore, the flexible solar panel can also be used to receive coded light signals during the night from external sources, therefore realizing bidirectional communication without the need for additional light monitoring components.

Figure 3B provides a visual representation of the integrated LED light transmitter within the solar panel, while a detailed circuit schematic is presented in Fig. 3C. To demonstrate the practicality of our approach, we programmed the LED light transmitter to encode plant flow rate data using Morse code, a widely recognized method (Fig. 3D). This encoded information can be captured and interpreted in real time by a digital camera. To automate this process, we developed a deep learning algorithm based on the single-stage object detection model YOLOv5 for interpreting coded light signals (Fig. 3D). The trained YOLOv5 model analyzes the video frame by frame to detect the state of the LED transmitters and generates a document detailing the duration and intervals of the LED flashes, thereby facilitating the interpretation of the coded information. More information about the optical communication is detailed in the Supplementary Materials (figs. S7 and S8).

To validate our communication system in agricultural settings, we deployed a UAV equipped with a digital camera to collect transmitted data. We conducted experiments to determine the communication range, as depicted in Fig. 3E. Using a 66-mW LED, the UAV can interpret the light signals from 12 m away with a 100% accuracy rate. Further enhancing transmittance range can be achieved by using higher-power LEDs, improving charge-coupled device (CCD) camera sensitivity or selecting optimal LED wavelengths that align with the specific requirements of the CCD sensor. Despite the inherent limitations of light-based communication in terms of speed, our system handles daily sensor data volumes efficiently. Transmitting small data packets can be accomplished within seconds. Notably, the energy consumption of our device (Fig. 3F) is estimated to be the lowest compared to mainstream methods such as Wi-Fi, Zigbee, Bluetooth, and LoRa (see table S2 for details). Because a typical UAV can fly several kilometers on a single charge, this approach greatly facilitates data retrieval from plant-wearable devices deployed in large agricultural fields.

Mechanical stability of the device

Elasticity and mechanical robustness are critical requirements for plant wearable sensor systems. To address these needs, we have incorporated an ultrathin design and serpentine conductive tracks in our device. This design enables the system to withstand mechanical deformations and prevent electronic circuit fractures. Figure 4A demonstrates the foldable nature of the solar panel, while the reliability of this panel is confirmed in Fig. 4B, showing no observable power degradation even after 500 testing cycles. The foldable design of the solar panel facilitates easier assembly and deployment of our probe while also reducing packaging size, making storage and transportation more convenient. We have also applied a similar design principle to develop the sap flow sensor, as illustrated in Fig. 4C. Both finite element analysis (FEA) simulations (Fig. 4D) and experimental results (Fig. 4E) demonstrate the remarkable flexibility of the serpentine copper tracks, allowing them to accommodate substantial uniaxial stretching. Despite the inclusion of chip-scale components, the entire system can endure large deformation (30%) without experiencing breakage (Fig. 4F and fig. S9). This is made possible by using an “island-bridge” strategy (34), which isolates deformations to the soft elastomer and reduces stress concentration on the chip. In addition, analysis in Fig. 4F reveals that the resistance of the copper tracks remains relatively stable below the rupture point, further indicating the mechanical robustness of the device. As shown in fig. S10, our plant-wearable device can be mounted on a plant stem without causing any fractures or damage to the branches. The total weight of the device is approximately 40 g (see table S3), which is only about one-fourth to one-fifth the weight of many common plant fruits, such as pumpkins, grapes, eggplant, etc. (as shown in fig. S11). It is reasonable to assume that this small additional weight would not affect plant health because our plant-wearable probe is much lighter than their own fruits. Further weight reduction can also be achieved by using lighter materials or adopting a simplified structure.

To understand the long-term impact of our sensor on plant viability, we mounted the device on the stem of a pumpkin plant and monitored its growth characteristics over time. As shown in Fig. 4G, no obvious negative effects on plant growth were observed after 30 days of deployment. Because leaves are the primary tissues for photosynthesis, respiration, and transpiration, any potential influence of the sensor on leaf growth would be more easily observed compared to other tissues such as the stem. Therefore, we also monitored leaf chlorophyll levels during this period. Figure 4H shows no obvious difference in leaf chlorophyll activity after 30 days of deployment.

The stability of the device on the plant stem is essential for reliable measurements. Our device can be easily installed using a magnetic self-assembly soft housing and further secured with adhesive tape. Because the housing is made of flexible and stretchable material, it can accommodate plant stems with varying diameters (approximately 4 to 8 mm). This approach ensures a secure attachment to the stem, as illustrated in fig. S12. The friction between the device and the stem effectively prevents slipping, even under a load of 200 g, which is five times its own weight. We also tested the probe’s stability in strong winds and confirmed its resilience in conditions equivalent to level 7 on the Beaufort scale (near gale), demonstrating its robust stability (Fig. 4I). In addition, the device housing provides effective thermal isolation for the sensor. As shown in fig. S13, temperature fluctuations are greatly reduced when the sensor is housed within the device. Moreover, thermal effects from the continuous operation of the solar panel and LED light transmitter have minimal impact on the temperature sensor.

On the other hand, because our probe contains no consumable materials, it can be reused after deployment. In addition, the modular design strategy we adopted allows for the individual replacement of components in case of damage, such as solar panels and batteries (as shown in fig. S4). Together, these features ensure that our probe provides sufficient durability for long-term measurements on plant stems.

Long-term field-test

Figure 5A illustrates a system-level demonstration of our self-sustainable plant-wearable probe in a real farm field. The operational cycle of the probe is outlined in the figure. The probe is programmed to wake up each morning after sunrise when its flexible solar panel has harvested sufficient energy. It then begins monitoring the sap flow of the plant. Each night after sunset, the probe prepares to transmit the collected sap flow data. A UAV is dispatched to gather data from the probe, functioning similarly to a bee gathering honey from flowers in the field. Initially, the probe emits a distinctive handshake light signal, aiding the UAV in pinpointing and identifying its location within the farm field. Upon receiving a predetermined acknowledgment light signal from the UAV, the probe begins the data transmission process. Once the transmission is successfully completed, the probe enters sleep mode automatically to conserve energy until the next sunrise. This streamlined process ensures reliable data exchange and efficient energy conservation throughout the night. For a more detailed schematic of the system workflow, please refer to fig. S14.

To demonstrate the long-term operational capability of our plant-wearable probe, a continuous field test was conducted over 14 days. Figure 5B illustrates the energy harvested and consumed by the system, indicating that it maintained an energy budget balance. Figure 5C shows the stem sap flow levels of the pumpkin plant during the same period with continuous measurements. As depicted in Fig. 5C, the sap flow rate increased after sunrise (06:00 to 13:00) with rising temperatures. Subsequently, the sap flow rate began to decrease as the air temperature dropped (13:00 to 18:00). After sunset (18:30), the sap flow rate decreased even more markedly. These observations are consistent with previous results obtained using traditional instruments (35), confirming the effectiveness of our sensor.

In our scenario, we used an UAV to acquire the data. Figure 5 (D and E) demonstrates that the UAV is capable of locating and recognizing multiple sensors in the field. The coded light signal from each sensor also contains a three-digit number as its unique ID; thus, the data from different sensors can be distinguished and separately interpreted (see fig. S7). Meanwhile, the YOLOv5 model we developed allows multiple targets detection in the same image, thus enabling simultaneous data collection from several sensors and enhancing data transmission efficiency. This capability is particularly beneficial for plant phenotype monitoring. To achieve high-throughput monitoring of a large number of physiological characteristics of individual plants, numerous probes were deployed in the field.

DISCUSSION

Powering and establishing communication with wearable devices on bio-interfaces pose challenges akin to those encountered in remote and harsh environments, such as space probes and planetary exploration. These challenges demand adherence to stringent requirements concerning device weight, size, and energy source limitations (36, 37). Therefore, optimizing power efficiency and ensuring reliable communication under adverse conditions are crucial for successful deployment (38–40).

This study introduces a plant-wearable sensing probe designed to integrate efficient energy harvesting capabilities with sap flow monitoring, thereby offering a self-sustainable solution for real-time analysis. The probe uses a sunflower-like foldable solar panel to power the device (Fig. 1, C and D). Sap flow is measured accurately using the thermal tracking principle (Fig. 2, E to G). The entire system is compact, is lightweight, and can be delicately mounted on plant stems (Fig. 4G). Mechanical robustness (Fig. 4, A to F) and excellent biocompatibility ensure long-term monitoring without compromising plant health and vitality (Fig. 4, H and I).

To overcome the challenge of low-energy density in solar power, we devised an ultralow-energy light communication system (Fig. 3A). This innovation integrates a small LED within the solar panel to enable the transmission and reception of coded light signals, facilitating bidirectional communication (Fig. 3C). Compared to conventional methods such as Wi-Fi, Zigbee, Bluetooth, and LoRa, our system demonstrates markedly lower power consumption (Fig. 3F).

The practical application and effectiveness of our probe were demonstrated in a real-world agricultural setting (Fig. 5A). The probe successfully maintained energy balance (Fig. 5B) and continuously monitored plant physiology over an extended period of 14 days (Fig. 5C). Moreover, we developed decoding algorithms based on deep learning, specifically using YOLOv5, to ensure accurate data interpretation (Fig. 3, D and E). In addition, the integration of UAV and deep learning technologies enables the efficient data collection from multiple probes, underscoring the system’s potential for plant phenotype application (Fig. 5, D and E).

As shown in table S4, our probe provides clear advantages over commercial sap flow setups in terms of weight, size, and cost. In addition, it eliminates expenses related to extensive modifications of farm infrastructure, such as power supply and communication network setups. This makes it a cost-effective and high-throughput tool for monitoring plant traits. Furthermore, we believe that our energy self-sustainable solution for wearable devices can be adapted to similarly resource-limited and challenging remote environments, such as tracking wild animals.

One inherent drawback of light communication protocols is their relatively slow data rates, which is not an issue in our case because the daily data quantity obtained by our probe is small. However, this could be problematic for sensors with larger data flows. Extreme weather conditions such as rain, fog, or snow should be considered when determining data transmission timing, as they can reduce signal quality or damage the device. In addition, as the plant canopy expands during growth, the energy harvesting efficiency of the solar panel may decrease. Therefore, incorporating certain redundancy in the system design is essential to maintain energy balance. On the other hand, our probe is lightweight enough for typical plants such as watermelons, pumpkins, and grapes to support. However, for more delicate plants such as crops or wheat, further weight reduction could be achieved by using lighter materials or a simplified structure. It can also be achieved by adopting porous or grid structures for the device housing. This unique design not only reduces weight but also enhances the ventilation and heat dissipation efficiency of our device, thereby alleviating any potential issues to plants health. For more complex stems with hairs, thorns, or joints, redesigning the device housing will be necessary to ensure conformal and secure contact between the device and the stem.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Fabrication of the plant-wearable solar panel

A Cu film (6 μm thickness) was first fixed onto a glass plate with a cured PDMS layer (100 μm) as a temporary adhesive layer. The Cu serpentine mesh for the bottom layer circuit of the plant-wearable solar cell panel was then defined by laser cutting (LPKF U4 laser system, LPKF Laser & Electronics AG, Germany). Next, the petal structure of the solar cell panel was defined by laser cutting and welded on the bottom circuit pads with the solder paste. Similarly, a polyimide (PI) copper clad laminate (18 μm) was affixed to the glass plate, and serpentine mesh was defined via laser cutting. These components were transferred onto the solar cell panel and bottom circuit pads. The serpentine circuit pads were welded both on the solar cell panel and the bottom layer circuit pads with solder paste. To make the plant-wearable solar cell panel waterproof, the entire module was encapsulated with an additional layer of PDMS (≈100 μm).

Design of the flexible circuit board

The flexible circuit board was designed with the incorporation of the power management circuit, voltage regulation circuit, and control circuit (see fig. S4 and table S5). The power management chip (BQ25570RGRR) with a maximum power point tracking function was used to enhance energy harvesting efficiency by boosting the voltage of low-power solar energy. The power management unit converted harvested energy into a stable 3.s voltage to power the control circuit and sensors, while surplus solar energy was stored in lithium batteries. During insufficient sunlight, the voltage regulator chip (ME6211C33M5G-N) regulated the energy stored in lithium batteries to provide a stable 3.3-V voltage for the operation of the control circuit and sensors. The control chip (STM32G071KBU6) was chosen to oversee the entire sensor system. All components are soldered onto the PI copper clad circuit board.

Integration of the probe

The magnetic self-assembly device housing was fabricated using a 3D printer (SHINING, AccuFab-D1s 3D) and controlled via code generated in the Accuware software. A light-curing resin (Ultracur 3D EL150-0.05) was chosen as the printing material. Following printing, the soft structure was cured in a UV curing chamber (SHINING, Fab Cure) for 30 s. Furthermore, small magnets were affixed to the connecting edges of the enclosure for securing the edges (see fig. S3 for more information). After the production, all components such as the folder solar panel, batteries, sap flow sensor, and FPCB control board were integrated into the device housing. The device can be conveniently installed using the magnetic self-assembly housing and further secured with breathable adhesive tape (3M).

Calibration of the probe

A sensing system was positioned in the center of a freshly cut pumpkin stem (length of 8 cm, diameter ≈ 6 mm) and connected to a micro-injection pump with a stainless-steel ferrule and a rubber tube (fig. S15). Different sap flow rates were achieved by adjusting the pump pressures, and the sensor responses were recorded accordingly. The precise water flow rates were determined by measuring the weight of water flowing through the stem within a specific time. A linear correlation was observed between the sap flow rate (R) and the time (Δt) required to achieve the maximum ΔT after heating.

Field test of the probe

During the fruiting stages, real-time monitoring of the charge-discharge status of the plant-wearable solar cell panels and the sap flow rate was conducted in healthy pumpkin plants. The pumpkin plants were cultivated in a farm field under ambient conditions. Sensors were affixed to stems with a diameter of ≈6 mm. Lithium battery capacity was assessed at 07:00 and 17:00 by collecting daily charge-discharge data from the solar cell panels.

To obtain the sap flow fluctuation, the monitoring was done continuously for 18 hours daily (between 06:00 to 24:00) (fig. S16). For each measurement, the thermistor heated for a short duration (50 s), and the resulting temperature variations downstream and upstream were recorded every 0.5 s over a period of 300 s. A total of 5 min was required for each measurement, with two measurements conducted per hour. The obtained data are stored consecutively in the device and sent to the UAV in the same order. Therefore, there is no need to transmit time information, as all sensors are programmed to measure at fixed hours each day. At 22:00, the UAV first received a handshake signal sent by the probe through the LED for precise positioning. Subsequently, the UAV transmitted light signals to solar cells of the system, and then solar cells converted light signals into command signals using an analog to digital (ADC) converter in the control system. Upon reception of the command signals, the control system emitted light signals carrying the sap flow data back to the UAV. If the initial data acquisition failed, then the UAV captured the light signals, decoded the information, and retransmitted light signals to the solar panels for secondary sap flow data collection.

Data training and algorithm

The decoding algorithm is based on a single-stage object detection model, YOLOv5, which uses convolutional neural networks for deep learning. Initially, the original video captured by the UAV is processed to extract frames, resulting in a dataset of 2154 images. The labeling of these images was performed using the Baidu PaddlePaddle Cloud Platform. The dataset was then divided into training and testing sets with a 9:1 ratio. All training processes were conducted on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU.

For decoding, the trained YOLOv5 model analyzes the video frame by frame to detect the state of the LED transmitters. It generates a document detailing the duration and intervals of the LED flashes, enabling the interpretation of the coded information. Each coded light signal also includes a unique three-digit sensor ID, allowing for the distinction of the data from different sensors. This approach facilitates the retrieval of data from multiple sensors within the same video. Further details of the coding methods and algorithms can be found in figs. S7 and S8, while the hyperparameter settings for YOLOv5 are presented in table S6.

FEA of the system

FEA enables the prediction of the mechanical deformations in the network design. The material parameters used are EPDMS = 1.8 MPa, VPDMS = 0.48 for PDMS and ECu = 100 GPa, and VCu = 0.34 for Cu, where E represents the elastic modulus and V denotes the Poisson’s ratio.

Instruments

Thermal imaging data were acquired using an infrared camera (HIKMICRO, HM-TD2037T-15) and analyzed with the 2019 Hikvision Digital Technology Co. software. Chlorophyll fluorescence measurements were captured with a chlorophyll fluorometer (LI-COR, LI-600). Light conditions were assessed using a spectroradiometer (Everfine, SPIC-300AW) to determine the light intensity. Optical images were captured using a Nikon Z6 camera. A drone (DJI, MINI 2) was used for light data collection, with decoding of the captured data performed on the YOLOv5 platform.
