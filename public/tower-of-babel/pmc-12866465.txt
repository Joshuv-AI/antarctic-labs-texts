Upregulation of miR-502-5p in traumatic spinal cord injury modulates neuroinflammation and oxidative stress by targeting FBXO28
Authors: Di Qiu, Juanyu Liu, Xintong Hao, Jinxiang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpinal cord injury (SCI) is a serious condition affecting the central nervous system, often caused by trauma, which can lead to lasting impairment in nerve function.ObjectiveThis study aims to explore the functional role of miR-502-5p in traumatic SCI, offering insights into potential therapeutic interventions.MethodsSerum miR-502-5p levels were quantified through RT-qPCR in a clinical cohort comprising 60 controls, 50 incomplete SCI, and 30 complete SCI patients. The diagnostic potential of miR-502-5p was assessed by the ROC curve. An in vitro cellular model was established by subjecting SH-SY5Y cells to oxygen and glucose deprivation (OGD). This model was utilized to investigate cell viability, apoptosis, as well as markers of inflammation and oxidative stress. Further investigation was conducted into the downstream molecular mechanisms by which miR-502-5p affects SH-SY5Y cells.ResultsSerum miR-502-5p levels were elevated in individuals with SCI, with higher expression observed in cases of complete SCI compared to incomplete SCI, indicating its potential for severity assessment. Both upregulation and downregulation of miR-502-5p were found to respectively promote and attenuate OGD-induced cell apoptosis, inflammatory responses, and oxidative stress. Furthermore, F-box protein 28 (FBXO28) was validated as a direct target of miR-502-5p. Functional experiments showed that knockdown of FBXO28 counteracted the protective effects of miR-502-5p inhibition.ConclusionsMiR-502-5p shows promise as a biomarker for SCI, with its expression closely associated with injury severity. Mechanistically, it appears to facilitate the progression of SCI by directly targeting FBXO28, thereby exacerbating neuronal apoptosis, inflammatory responses, and oxidative stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06565-7.Keywords: Spinal cord injury, miR-502-5p, Inflammation, Oxidative stress

Introduction

Spinal trauma is caused by external mechanical forces and often occurs alongside spinal cord injury (SCI) [1]. When the spine is subjected to such force, mechanical injuries such as vertebral fractures and ligament tears are caused, and a series of secondary injury responses are triggered, including local ischemia, inflammatory reactions, oxidative stress, cell apoptosis, and glial scar formation. These processes often persist for weeks or months after the initial injury and can ultimately lead to irreversible neurological dysfunction [2–4]. Current clinical interventions, such as surgical decompression and high-dose steroid therapy, can mitigate some symptoms but fail to promote substantial neural regeneration or functional recovery [5, 6]. Despite significant advancements in surgical techniques and intensive care in recent years, there are still limited treatment options for secondary injuries, prompting researchers to explore new therapeutic strategies [7]. Therefore, identifying novel molecular targets to modulate secondary injury progression is a critical focus of spinal trauma research.

Recent studies have highlighted the significance of non-coding RNAs in the pathophysiology and treatment of musculoskeletal conditions, including tendon injuries [8, 9], osteoarthritis [10], rheumatoid arthritis [11], and osteoporosis [12, 13]. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are non-coding RNAs consisting of 18–25 nucleotides that modulate gene expression post-transcriptionally. They are also involved in various disease pathways [14, 15]. MiRNAs have been identified as key regulatory factors in skeletal system diseases such as osteoarthritis, osteoporosis, and intervertebral disc degeneration [16–21]. In SCI, miRNAs have emerged as key regulators with dual roles, both promoting pathogenesis and providing neuroprotection [22, 23]. For example, exosomal miR-202-3p facilitates functional recovery post-SCI [24], and miR-10b-5p exerts protective effects via activation of the transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1)/Smad3 pathway [25]. Conversely, elevated miR-223-5p is associated with more severe SCI manifestations, highlighting its pathogenic potential [26]. Among miRNAs with possible disease-aggravating roles, miR-502-5p was reported to be upregulated in subarachnoid hemorrhage, with its elevation linked to severe neurological injury and unfavorable outcomes [27]. MiR-502-5p is also upregulated in mild cognitive impairment, indicating an association with Alzheimer’s disease risk [28], while its downregulation exerts antitumor effects in glioblastoma via apoptosis induction [29]. Emerging research has revealed elevated miR-502-5p expression in SCI animal models [30], implying a potential role in SCI pathogenesis. However, the precise molecular mechanisms by which miR-502-5p functions in SCI remain poorly understood.

This study evaluated the clinical significance of miR-502-5p in patients with SCI caused by spinal trauma. Further investigations elucidated the impact of miR-502-5p on SH-SY5Y cell biological behaviors and the regulatory mechanisms in inflammatory response and oxidative stress.

Materials and methods

Study population and samples

The present study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Wuhan No.1 Hospital. A total of 80 patients with traumatic SCI were enrolled between October 2023 and December 2024. According to the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) Impairment Scale [31], the participants were categorized into two groups: those with incomplete SCI (n = 50) and those with complete SCI (n = 30). Additionally, 60 individuals with spinal trauma but normal neurological function served as controls. The patients in both groups were comparable in terms of age and sex. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The inclusion criteria comprised: (1) admission within 24 h following injury and (2) age ≥ 18 years. Key exclusion criteria included: (1) previous spinal cord or traumatic brain injury; (2) significant craniocerebral trauma; (3) pre-existing neurological (e.g., stroke) or psychiatric conditions; (4) severe cardiopulmonary dysfunction; and (5) diagnosis of malignant tumors.

Upon admission, peripheral blood was obtained from each participant. The collected samples were left for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 2000 g for 10 min. The serum obtained was carefully collected and cryopreserved at -80 °C for subsequent analysis.

Cell culture

Human neuroblastoma cells (SH-SY5Y) were obtained from Wuhan Procell Life Science Technology Co., Ltd. (China). The cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Upon reaching 80–90% confluence, cells were digested and passaged using 0.25% Trypsin–EDTA solution (Solarbio, China).

SH-SY5Y cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. Upon reaching 70–80% confluence, transfection was implemented using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA). miR-502-5p mimic (50 nM) and inhibitor (100 nM) (RiboBio, China) were used for overexpression and silencing of miR-502-5p, respectively. Scrambled RNA oligonucleotides served as corresponding negative control (NC), i.e., NC-mimic (50 nM) and NC-inhibitor (100 nM). The experimental groups included: control, oxygen and glucose deprivation (OGD), OGD + NC-mimic, OGD + miR-mimic, OGD + NC-inhibitor, and OGD + miR-inhibitor. To explore the role of F-box protein 28 (FBXO28), siRNA targeting FBXO28 (si-FBXO28) and a non-targeting control siRNA (si-NC; Genepharma, China) were co-transfected into cells. The groups were as follows: OGD + NC-inhibitor, OGD + miR-inhibitor, OGD + miR-inhibitor + si-NC, and OGD + miR-inhibitor + si-FBXO28. After 6 h, the medium was replaced with complete medium, and the cells were cultured until 24 h post-transfection, at which point they were subjected to OGD as described below.

OGD model induction

Cells were rinsed three times with pre-warmed phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Subsequently, pre-warmed, glucose-free medium was added to the OGD and transfection groups. The OGD group was then incubated for 2, 4, and 6 h in a hypoxic environment (94% N2, 5% CO2, 1% O2). Following OGD, the cells were returned to normal complete medium and reoxygenated under standard culture conditions for 24 h before analysis.

Reverse transcription quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from serum samples and differentially treated SH-SY5Y cells with TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, USA). The purity and concentration of RNA were assessed on a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo, USA), yielding OD260/280 ratios of 1.8–2.1.

Total RNA was utilized as a template, and cDNA was synthesized using the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR with oligo(dT) and the miRNA 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (by stem-loop, Vazyme, China). The synthesized cDNA served as the template for qPCR, which was performed on a QuantStudio 5 System (Applied Biosystems, USA) using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China). Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. Primers are listed in Table S1 of the Supplementary Materials. Relative quantification was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with miR-502-5p expression normalized to RNU6-1 RNA (U6) and FBXO28 mRNA expression normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). All experiments were performed with five independent biological replicates (n = 5).

Cell viability assessment

Post-transfection and OGD treatment, cellular viability was evaluated with Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) solution (Dojindo, Japan). Briefly, SH-SY5Y cells (1 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C with CCK-8 solution diluted in serum-free medium (10% final concentration). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Synergy H4 microplate reader (BioTek, USA). The assay was repeated three times with independent biological replicates (n = 3).

Flow cytometry

SH-SY5Y cells from each group were harvested, rinsed with ice-cold PBS, and processed for analysis. Cell apoptosis was assessed using an Apoptosis Detection Kit (BestBio, China). Following the manufacturer’s protocol, cells were resuspended in 1 × Annexin V binding buffer. A 100 μL aliquot of the suspension was then incubated with 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 μL of PI for 15 min at room temperature, protected from light. Following incubation, 400 μL of 1 × binding buffer was added to each tube. The samples were then placed on ice for immediate analysis using a BD FACSVerse flow cytometer (Becton, Dickinson and Company, USA). Five independent biological replicates (n = 5) were performed for this assay.

Western blot

SH-SY5Y cells were lysed with radio immunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer containing 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride. The protein concentration was measured using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). The protein samples were then separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2, 1:1000, Invitrogen, USA), BCL2-associated X protein (Bax, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), FBXO28 (1:1000, Invitrogen, USA), and GAPDH (1:1000, Abcam, UK). Protein bands were detected following incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated antibody (1:10,000, Abcam, UK) at room temperature for 1 h.

Quantification of apoptosis, inflammation, and oxidative stress markers

Following transfection and OGD treatment, cell culture supernatants were collected for analysis of interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) secretion via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Elabscience, China). Caspase-3 activity was quantified using a specific assay kit (Beyotime, China), which measures the cleavage of the Ac-DEVD-pNA substrate at 405 nm absorbance, per the manufacturer’s protocol.

The activities of antioxidant enzymes, including catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD), were measured using a CAT Activity Fluorometric Assay Kit (Elabscience, China) and a SOD Assay Kit (Beyotime, China), respectively. Meanwhile, the content of malondialdehyde (MDA) and reduced glutathione (GSH) was determined using a Lipid Peroxidation MDA Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) and a Total Glutathione Assay Kit (Dojindo, Japan). All the above measurements were carried out with five independent biological replicates (n = 5).

Bioinformatics prediction

The target genes of miR-502-5p were predicted using three online databases: miRDB (version 6.0, https://mirdb.org/mirdb/index.html), TargetScan (release 7.2, https://www.targetscan.org/vert_72/), and TarBase (v9.0, https://dianalab.e-ce.uth.gr/tarbasev9). The prediction was performed with the following criteria: only human genes were considered; in TargetScan, predictions were restricted to those with a cumulative weighted context + + score ≤ − 0.2; in miRDB, the target score threshold is ≥ 50; TarBase predictions were limited to experimentally validated targets. The intersections of predicted target genes from the three databases were obtained and visualized using a Venn diagram (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/). Additionally, potential binding sites for target genes were predicted using the TargetScan database.

Dual luciferase reporter assay

To generate the wild-type construct (wt-FBXO28), the FBXO28 3′ untranslated region (UTR) fragment containing the predicted binding site for miR-502-5p was cloned into a reporter plasmid. The mutant construct (mt-FBXO28) was generated from the wild-type plasmid via site-directed mutagenesis. These plasmids were then co-transfected into SH-SY5Y cells seeded in 24-well plates (at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well) with either NC-mimic, miR-502-5p mimic (miR-mimic), NC-inhibitor, or miR-502-5p inhibitor (miR-inhibitor). After 48 h, luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Vazyme, China), and the relative firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla. The experiment was conducted with five independent biological replicates (n = 5).

Statistical analysis

Figures were generated with GraphPad Prism 9.0. Statistical comparisons between groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Student’s t-test. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The diagnostic value of miR-502-5p was evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Furthermore, the relationship between miR-502-5p and FBXO28 expression was evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. P < 0.05 indicates significant differences.

MiR-502-5p expression was quantified by RT-qPCR across groups, revealing markedly elevated levels in SCI samples compared to controls. Notably, miR-502-5p expression was significantly higher in the complete SCI group than in the incomplete SCI group (Fig. 1A). The ROC curve plotted for serum miR-502-5p expression yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.892 in discriminating SCI patients from normal controls, with a sensitivity of 72.5% and a specificity of 95.0% at the optimal cutoff value of 1.298 (Fig. 1B). At an optimal cutoff value of 1.678, the sensitivity and specificity reached 73.3% and 92.0%, respectively (Fig. 1C).

MiR-502-5p promotes OGD-induced neuronal injury

In SH-SY5Y cells exposed to OGD, miR-502-5p expression showed a time-dependent increase (Fig. 2A). Following 6 h of OGD treatment, which was chosen for further investigation, transfection efficiency was evaluated. Transfection with the miR-502-5p mimic elevated its expression, while the inhibitor effectively repressed it (Fig. 2B). OGD exposure significantly lowered the viability of SH-SY5Y cells. Overexpression of miR-502-5p further suppressed cell survival, while inhibition of miR-502-5p attenuated this effect (Fig. 2C). According to flow cytometry, enhancing miR-502-5p expression exacerbated OGD-induced apoptosis (Fig. 2D) and was associated with activation of Caspase-3, upregulation of Bax, and downregulation of Bcl-2 (Fig. 2E). In contrast, silencing miR-502-5p produced the opposite outcomes (Fig. 2D and E).

MiR-502-5p exacerbates OGD-induced neuroinflammation and oxidative stress

ELISA results (Supplementary Materials, raw ELISA data.xlsx) indicated that OGD triggered an inflammatory response in SH-SY5Y cells, characterized by elevated concentrations of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β. Enhanced expression of miR-502-5p further intensified this inflammatory activation, whereas its knockdown attenuated the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 3A). Moreover, in SH-SY5Y cells subjected to OGD, oxidative stress was evident from reduced activities of SOD and CAT, diminished GSH levels, and elevated MDA content. These manifestations of oxidative stress were aggravated by miR-502-5p overexpression but mitigated upon miR-502-5p inhibition (Fig. 3B).

FBXO28 is identified as a direct target of miR-502-5p through bioinformatic prediction and experimental validation

Based on bioinformatics predictions from miRDB, TargetScan, and TarBase, three potential targets of miR-502-5p were identified. Of these candidates, only FBXO28 exhibited a reduction in mRNA expression following OGD exposure in SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 4A). Potential binding of miR-502-5p was predicted in the 3’ UTR region of FBXO28. Dual-luciferase assays demonstrated that miR-502-5p overexpression repressed luciferase activity from the wt-FBXO28, while miR-502-5p inhibition increased it. In contrast, neither overexpression nor knockdown of miR-502-5p markedly altered luciferase activity in the mt-FBXO28 construct (Fig. 4B). Additionally, a significant downregulation of FBXO28 was observed in SCI patients, correlating with injury severity (Fig. 4C). Pearson analysis further indicated an inverse correlation between FBXO28 and miR-502-5p levels (r = − 0.753, P < 0.001; Fig. 4D).

Downregulation of miR-502-5p suppresses OGD-induced neuronal injury by regulating FBXO28

In SH-SY5Y cells subjected to OGD, the suppression of miR-502-5p elevated FBXO28 mRNA (Fig. 5A) and protein (Fig. 5B) levels, an effect that was subsequently attenuated by knocking down FBXO28. Subsequent functional assays showed that FBXO28 knockdown mitigated the promotion of miR-502-5p downregulation on SH-SY5Y cell viability (Fig. 5C), as well as its suppressive actions on apoptosis (Fig. 5D) and apoptotic markers (Fig. 5E). Moreover, FBXO28 silencing reversed the suppressive effects of miR-502-5p downregulation on inflammatory mediators (Fig. 6A) and oxidative stress (Fig. 6B).

Discussion

Traumatic SCI represents a devastating condition affecting the central nervous system, often resulting in significant disability and mortality [1]. Accumulating evidence has revealed that circulating miRNAs exhibit distinct alterations in expression after traumatic injury to the central nervous system. These molecules have emerged as promising biomarkers due to their notable stability, accessibility in detection, and capacity to mirror dynamic pathological processes [32]. Previous studies have linked increased serum miR-502-5p to unfavorable neurological recovery following subarachnoid hemorrhage [27, 33], and its upregulation in mild cognitive impairment has been implicated in Alzheimer’s disease pathogenesis [28]. The current study further revealed a substantial elevation of serum miR-502-5p in SCI patients, highlighting its close association with the disorder. Notably, the present study provides the first assessment of serum miR-502-5p as a biomarker for differentiating SCI, with findings supporting its discriminative performance. The early and precise evaluation of injury severity is vitally important in clinical settings, forming the basis for treatment strategy design and prediction of functional recovery. Current diagnostic approaches depend heavily on physical examination, including the ASIA Impairment Scale, and neuroimaging modalities such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [34–36]. Nevertheless, the reliability of these methods can be limited by the patient’s state of consciousness, degree of cooperation, or the scheduling of imaging procedures. Within this context, serum miR-502-5p emerges as a promising complementary biomarker that may enhance existing protocols by supplying an objective, quantifiable, and consistent tool for assisting evaluation. These findings support the potential role of miR-502-5p as a specific biomarker in the context of SCI.

MiRNAs have been shown to play a regulatory role in key pathological mechanisms of traumatic nervous system injury, including neuroinflammation, apoptotic cell death, and glial scar formation [23, 37]. This study revealed a marked upregulation of miR-502-5p under OGD conditions designed to mimic the ischemic-hypoxic microenvironment of SCI. Elevated miR-502-5p expression promoted neuronal apoptosis. The pro-apoptotic effect is mediated through the modulation of Bax and Bcl-2, culminating in the activation of Caspase-3. Apoptosis occurring in the secondary injury phase is widely recognized as a major contributor to the decline in neurological function after SCI [38]. This study underscores the pathological significance of miR-502-5p in traumatic SCI and highlights its potential role in secondary injury processes. These findings align with existing literature on pro-apoptotic miRNAs involved in SCI. For instance, miR-324-5p, which is upregulated following SCI, has been shown to facilitate neuronal apoptosis through inhibition of Sirt1 [39]. Notably, previous investigations have provided direct evidence that suppression of certain pro-apoptotic miRNAs (e.g., miR-6315) after SCI attenuates apoptotic processes and supports the recovery of neural function [40]. In line with these findings, the present study reveals miR-502-5p as a previously unrecognized yet pivotal contributor to secondary injury mechanisms in SCI, mediated through its facilitation of apoptosis. Previous studies have consistently emphasized that pharmacological intervention is a key strategy for modulating the damage process in acute and chronic secondary SCI, highlighting the therapeutic significance of targeting critical pathways [41–43]. This positions miR-502-5p as a promising candidate for targeted therapeutic strategies aimed at neuroprotection within the injured spinal cord.

Following traumatic SCI, intense neuroinflammation and oxidative stress play central roles in propagating secondary tissue damage and inhibiting repair processes [44]. Previous studies have identified several miRNAs as key regulators of these pathological events [23, 45]. For instance, miR-223-5p has been shown to attenuate neuroinflammation by suppressing NOD-like receptor thermal protein domain associated protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation [46], while miR-145-5p modulates inflammation and oxidative stress by modulating TNF-α signaling [47]. Other miRNAs, including miR-155 and miR-21, are involved in regulating glial activation and neuronal survival post-SCI [48–50]. Research findings demonstrate that miR-502-5p substantially exacerbates neuroinflammation and oxidative stress under OGD conditions. Specifically, miR-502-5p upregulation stimulates the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and amplifies oxidative damage by reducing antioxidant capacity. The findings imply that miR-502-5p acts as a critical molecular link between hypoxic-ischemic injury and the interconnected pathways of inflammation and oxidative damage. The involvement of miR-502-5p in these two fundamental aspects of SCI pathogenesis underscores its promise as a therapeutic target.

This study establishes FBXO28 as a direct functional target of miR-502-5p. Belonging to the F-box protein family, FBXO28 functions as the substrate-recruiting module within the Skp1-Cullin1-F-box (SCF) E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, where it facilitates the recognition and ubiquitin-dependent degradation of specific substrate proteins [51]. In samples from SCI patients, FBXO28 expression exhibited a negative correlation with injury severity and miR-502-5p levels, indicating the potential clinical significance of the miR-502-5p/FBXO28 regulatory axis in human SCI. Of critical importance, loss-of-function studies provide direct evidence for the neuroprotective role of FBXO28. This investigation revealed that depleting FBXO28 effectively reversed the protective outcomes resulting from miR-502-5p inhibition, including reduced apoptosis, attenuated inflammation, and decreased oxidative stress. The findings indicate that the neurotoxic effects of miR-502-5p are mediated predominantly through the suppression of FBXO28, thereby identifying FBXO28 as a key functional effector. Thus, it is postulated that FBXO28 exerts specific functions following SCI. Although FBXO28 is known to participate in fundamental cellular mechanisms such as cell cycle control and DNA repair [52, 53], the precise mechanisms underlying its role in neurological disorders remain to be fully elucidated. Future research aimed at identifying the specific targets of FBXO28 is crucial for comprehensively understanding its protective mechanisms.

The study has several limitations. First, the generalizability of these findings may be limited by the single-center design and sample size of the clinical cohort. Therefore, further validation in larger, multi-center studies is necessary to better evaluate the translational potential of miR-502-5p. Additionally, the absence of healthy controls also limits the ability to establish a clear baseline; incorporating such control group in future studies would provide a more reliable context for interpreting patient data. Furthermore, although the OGD-induced SH-SY5Y cell model has gained widespread recognition for studying in vitro ischemic-hypoxic neuronal injury, it fails to fully reflect the complexity of the in vivo SCI microenvironment, such as glial cell activation, immune cell infiltration, and vascular damage. Therefore, future studies utilizing animal models of SCI are critical for validating the role of the miR-502-5p/FBXO28 axis and elucidating its functions across different cell types within the complex SCI environment. Moreover, the current study does not address the therapeutic time window for targeting miR-502-5p or provide direct evidence of its efficacy in modulating post-injury outcomes within complex in vivo environments. This further underscores the necessity of the proposed animal studies. Finally, while the direct targeting of FBXO28 by miR-502-5p and their functional relationship were established, the downstream pathways and molecular mechanisms mediated by FBXO28 remain poorly explored. Further investigation is warranted to clarify how FBXO28 regulates specific signaling mechanisms involved in neuronal apoptosis, inflammation, and oxidative stress. Consequently, subsequent research will prioritize the identification of direct targets of FBXO28 to gain a more comprehensive understanding of its biological functions. Future research will also focus on deciphering the hierarchical structure of the miR-502-5p downstream network. Specifically, we will conduct rescue experiments using specific pharmacological inhibitors, such as antioxidants or caspase inhibitors. If apoptosis and inflammation are alleviated following the suppression of oxidative stress, this will indicate that oxidative stress is the driving upstream event. This approach will provide direct experimental evidence to clarify the causal relationships among these key pathways.

The present investigation identified a marked increase in serum miR-502-5p levels in individuals with SCI, underscoring its potential utility as a diagnostic indicator for evaluating injury severity. In cellular models, reduced expression of miR-502-5p attenuated OGD-induced apoptotic activity, inflammatory responses, and oxidative stress. Additionally, FBXO28 was characterized as a direct target of miR-502-5p, participating in its promoting role in neural injury. This finding enriches the molecular mechanisms involved in SCI and highlights the miR-502-5p/FBXO28 axis to be a prospective target for future therapeutic intervention.
