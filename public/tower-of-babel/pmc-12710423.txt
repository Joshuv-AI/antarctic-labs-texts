Delayed Massive Haemoptysis Following EBUS‐TBNA Revealing a Pseudoaneurysm of an Ectopic Bronchial Artery: A Rare Complication at the Crossroads of Interventional Pulmonology and Radiology
Authors: Julien G Cohen, Matthieu Papillard, Romain Messe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEndobronchial ultrasound‐guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS‐TBNA) is widely considered a safe and minimally invasive procedure for the evaluation of mediastinal lymphadenopathy. Although complications are rare, severe haemorrhagic events may occur, particularly when vascular structures are punctured. A 31‐year‐old woman with Budd–Chiari syndrome underwent EBUS‐TBNA for hypermetabolic mediastinal lymph nodes. Sampling was uneventful, but she developed massive haemoptysis 7 days later. Contrast‐enhanced CT and bronchoscopy identified two 4R lymph node pseudoaneurysms, confirmed by angiography. The lesion was successfully treated with selective microcoil embolisation. No recurrence was observed at two‐month follow‐up, despite resuming anticoagulation. Delayed pseudoaneurysm is an exceptionally rare but potentially life‐threatening complication of EBUS‐TBNA. Pre‐procedural Doppler assessment, awareness of vascular variants, and timely imaging are essential. Endovascular embolisation is an effective, minimally invasive treatment. Early recognition and multidisciplinary management are crucial to prevent catastrophic outcomes.Keywords: EBUS‐TBNA, endovascular embolisation, haemoptysis, pseudoaneurysm

Introduction

Endobronchial ultrasound‐guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS‐TBNA) has become a cornerstone in the evaluation of mediastinal lymphadenopathy, offering a high diagnostic yield with a low complication rate [1]. Although it has an excellent overall safety profile, EBUS‐TBNA is not free of adverse events, particularly haemorrhagic complications resulting from inadvertent puncture of mediastinal vascular structures.

While most of these complications are minor and self‐limiting, severe cases such as mediastinal haematomas, pericardial tamponade, or airway obstruction due to parietal haematomas have been reported, sometimes with delayed onset [2].

We report here a rare case of delayed massive haemoptysis due to pseudoaneurysm formation following EBUS‐TBNA. This case highlights the need for early recognition and tailored management of this potentially life‐threatening complication.

Case Report

A 31‐year‐old woman underwent FDG‐PET/CT as part of a diagnostic workup for fever of unknown origin. Incidentally, the scan revealed a pseudo‐nodular hypermetabolic consolidation in the proximal right upper lobe (SUVmax = 9.3) surrounded by a ground‐glass halo, along with hypermetabolic mediastinal lymphadenopathy involving nodal stations 2R, 4R, and 7 (Figure 1).

Contrast‐enhanced CT images (left) and fusion images between CT and 18‐FDG scintigraphy (right) of the proximal ventral right upper lobe consolidation (upper) and the right lower paratracheal node 4R. Both lesions were hypermetabolic (SUVmax, 9.3 and 4.6, respectively).

The patient, a non‐smoker, had a negative initial infectious workup. Her medical history included idiopathic Budd‐Chiari syndrome (JAK2‐negative) treated with long‐term therapeutic anticoagulation (apixaban), which had been paused 5 days before the procedure and bridged with low‐molecular‐weight heparin per the recommendation of the vascular specialist. She also had portal hypertension with varices and polycystic ovary syndrome. Preoperative assessment (ASA III) showed no signs of significant pulmonary hypertension.

Bronchoscopy under general anaesthesia with laryngeal mask ventilation included linear EBUS (BF‐UC180F; Olympus) and 22G needle aspirations (NA‐U401SX‐4022‐A; Olympus) targeting nodal stations 4R and 7. Targeted transbronchial biopsies of segment RB3 were also performed following radial EBUS (r‐EBUS) localisation of the consolidation. Endosonographic imaging prompted additional Doppler and elastography assessments, revealing atypical vascularisation suggestive of a systemic arterial source (Figure 2). Nevertheless, an avascular window allowed four needle passes at both sites without significant procedural bleeding. The Rapid On‐Site Evaluation (ROSE) assessment confirmed that the sampled 4R lymph node was representative of a lymph node, with no cells suspicious for malignancy. Mediastinal cryobiopsies were deferred due to the abundance of vessels in that region.

Linear EBUS with Doppler mode performed prior to the first puncture, identifying a tortuous atypical vessel adjacent to the 4R lymph node station (left). Linear EBUS elastography of the 4R station (middle) showing an intermediate stiffness pattern characterised by a mixed blue–green distribution. Linear EBUS showing the trajectory of the needle safely inside the 4R lymph node (right).

A grade 2 bleeding episode occurred following RB3 biopsy, which was controlled with endobronchial instillation of 250 mcg epinephrine. Microbiological, cytological and histopathological analyses revealed no malignancy or granulomas, only nonspecific inflammation. Methicillin‐sensitive Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) was isolated in low concentration from bronchial aspirates, with no other pathogens identified. In the absence of post‐procedural complications, full‐dose anticoagulation was resumed after 24 h.

Seven days post‐procedure, the patient experienced massive haemoptysis triggered by a coughing episode, necessitating emergency intubation upon arrival at the hospital. Contrast‐enhanced chest CT (Figure 3) demonstrated bilateral lower lobe alveolar haemorrhage and a newly developed, well‐circumscribed, contrast‐enhancing nodular lesion in the 4R region, consistent with a pseudoaneurysm. Bronchoscopy confirmed this origin of the bleeding, as shown in Figure 4. Subsequent angiography confirmed two pseudoaneurysms in the 4R area, which were successfully treated with selective microcoil embolisation (Figure 5).

Post‐haemoptysis CT‐scan. An axial slice displayed in lung window at the level of lower lobes (left) displays signs of alveolar haemorrhage with atelectasis in the lower lobes. An axial slice of the mid‐mediastinum in soft‐tissue window (right) shows the appearance of a focal well‐defined intense contrast‐enhanced lesion in the 4R lymph node.

Endoscopic view post‐EBUS‐TBNA showing a puncture site protrusion corresponding to the pseudoaneurysm, the source of haemoptysis.

Frontal angiography of a descending branch of thyro‐cervical trunk, showing 2 pseudo aneurysms in projection of the 4R region, before (left) and after (right) embolisation.

Immunological workup was ANA‐negative except for low‐titre anti‐SSA/Ro60 positivity. HLA‐B51 was also present. In the absence of haematologic malignancy, vascular malformation, or overt connective tissue disease, a systemic autoimmune disorder (e.g., incomplete Behçet's or indolent systemic lupus erythematosus) was considered but remained unconfirmed.

Discussion

Although EBUS‐TBNA is widely regarded as a safe, minimally invasive procedure, it carries a small but real risk of severe haemorrhagic complications. According to large cohorts such as the AQuIRE study and various national registries, bleeding incidence ranges from 0.68% overall to 0.2% for significant haemorrhage [3]. Most complications occur intraoperatively or shortly thereafter, allowing for prompt detection and management. The most severe haemorrhagic events are generally associated with mediastinal haematomas or major vessel injuries, particularly in patients with underlying hypervascular conditions. In most cases, conservative management is sufficient [2, 4].

Our case underscores a rare and delayed vascular complication: massive haemoptysis secondary to a pseudoaneurysm of an ectopic bronchial artery arising from the thyrocervical trunk.

This uncommon anatomical variant highlights the importance of mediastinal vascular mapping, especially when vascular structures lie in proximity to lymph node stations targeted during EBUS‐TBNA.

A pseudoaneurysm is defined as a contained arterial wall rupture with blood extravasation confined by the adventitia or surrounding soft tissue. In our patient, the pseudoaneurysms were likely due to transvascular passage through a superficial, high‐pressure arterial segment.

The patient's history of Budd‐Chiari syndrome and chronic liver disease may have contributed to increased vascular fragility through mechanisms such as endothelial dysfunction and altered coagulation [5]. Although anticoagulation was suspended before the procedure, underlying dysimmune features (HLA‐B51, anti‐SSA/Ro60) could further predispose to vascular injury by promoting chronic inflammation [6, 7]. These factors, combined with the presence of atypical mediastinal vessels, may explain the delayed haemorrhagic complication observed in this case.

Imaging plays an important role. On contrast‐enhanced CT, pseudoaneurysms appear as well‐defined, enhancing nodular lesions in continuity with a vessel. Doppler ultrasound may reveal turbulent flow within the lesion.

Endovascular embolisation is the preferred treatment when feasible. Embolising agents include microcoils, N‐butyl cyanoacrylate (NBCA), EVOH (Ethylene‐vinyl alcohol copolymer), Onyx, and thrombin. Embolisation offers high technical and clinical success rates with low morbidity and can be performed quickly and minimally [8]. In our case, embolisation, the patient remained haemodynamically stable, with no recurrence on imaging or clinically at 2‐month follow‐up, despite resuming therapeutic anticoagulation.

Prognosis after embolisation is generally favourable. A review of visceral pseudoaneurysms reports success rates above 90% for complete occlusion.

This case emphasises the importance of both radiologic and Doppler assessment before EBUS‐TBNA needle puncture.11 It also underlines the need to consider vascular complications around the puncture sites in cases of delayed or atypical post‐procedural presentations, for which contrast‐enhanced CT serves as an appropriate first‐line examination. Timely, multidisciplinary coordination between pulmonologists, radiologists and interventional specialists is essential for effective management.

In conclusion, lymph node pseudoaneurysm is an exceptionally rare but potentially life‐threatening complication of EBUS‐TBNA. Early recognition, prompt imaging and timely intervention, particularly through endovascular embolisation, are essential to prevent catastrophic haemoptysis. As EBUS becomes more widely utilised awareness of these vascular complications remains crucial.
