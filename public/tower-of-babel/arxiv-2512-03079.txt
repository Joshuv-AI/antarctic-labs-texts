Watermarks for Embeddings-as-a-Service Large Language Models https://github.com/anudeex/WET


Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated exceptional capabilities in natural language understanding and generation. Based on these LLMs, businesses have started to provide Embeddings-as-a-Service (EaaS), offering feature extraction capabilities (in the form of text embeddings) that benefit downstream natural language processing tasks. However, prior research has demonstrated that EaaS is vulnerable to imitation attacks, where an attacker clones the service’s model in a black-box manner without access to the model’s internal workings. These imitation attacks pose significant financial risks to LLM owners due to the substantial costs associated with training these language models. In response, watermarks have been added to the text embeddings to protect the intellectual property of EaaS providers by allowing them to check for model ownership. This thesis focuses on defending against imitation attacks by investigating EaaS watermarks. To achieve this goal, we unveil novel attacks and propose and validate new watermarking techniques. Firstly, we show that existing EaaS watermarks can be removed through paraphrasing the input text when attackers clone the model during imitation attacks. Our study illustrates that paraphrasing can effectively bypass current state-of-the-art EaaS watermarks across various attack setups (including different paraphrasing techniques and models) and datasets in most instances. This demonstrates a new vulnerability in recent EaaS watermarking techniques. Subsequently, as a countermeasure, we propose a novel watermarking technique, WET ( W atermarking E aaS with Linear T ransformation), which employs linear transformation of the embeddings. Watermark verification is conducted by applying a reverse transformation and comparing the similarity between recovered and original embeddings. We demonstrate its robustness against paraphrasing attacks with near-perfect verifiability. We conduct detailed ablation studies to assess the significance of each component and hyperparameter in WET . The techniques (both the paraphrasing attack and WET defence) proposed in this thesis are novel and have been analysed empirically and theoretically. Lastly, we provide a critical analysis and a discussion on current limitations and future directions as the conclusion of the thesis. The source code used in this work has been made open source to spur future research. Keywords: natural language processing, large language models, embedding, imitation attack, watermark, copyright protection, security

Introduction

Better the devil you know than the devil you do not know.

—Anthony Trollope

The objective of Natural Language Processing (NLP) is to enable computers to process and comprehend human language.
NLP has advanced significantly in recent decades, becoming integral to essential tasks such as text generation, sentiment analysis, machine translation, question-answering, and other crucial applications
(
138
)
. Like other sub-fields in computer science, machine learning—particularly deep learning
(
65
)
—has gained prominence as the dominant paradigm in NLP.
Notably, Large Language Models (LLMs)
(
138
)
are currently considered state-of-the-art for many applications due to their exceptional aptitude for comprehending and generating complex human language.

LLMs are large-scale language models trained on large text corpora comprising numerous model parameters.
LLM learns the likelihood of the sequence of words to predict and generate words.
1
1

1

We use the terms “word” and “token” interchangeably. In NLP, a
word
is a linguistic unit like “sky” or “blue”. Whereas a
token
is a sequence of characters serving as a semantic unit processed by language models. It could be a character, a word, or a sub-word. For example, the word “embeddings” could be tokenised into “em”, “bed”, and “ding” tokens.
The seminal architecture,
Transformer

(
31
)
, serves as the basis for contemporary LLMs. The
Transformer
represents text tokens contextually by employing self-attention
mechanisms that can simultaneously capture dependencies between words and their longer context
(
121
)
. Since the discovery of this architecture, LLMs have grown increasingly bigger by scaling up parameters accompanied by a larger training corpus
(
8
,
95
,
63
,
inter alia)
. For instance,
GPT-3

(
17
)
has 175 billion parameters (for comparison,
GPT-2

(
94
)
has 1.5 billion parameters and
GPT-1

(
93
)
has 117 million parameters). This, however, is not considering more recent proprietary models (e.g.,
GPT-4

(
2
)
and
Gemini

(
35
)
), which are much bigger although their exact configurations are currently undisclosed. Such growth in model size is motivated by the scaling law in language models
(
53
)
which suggests that the performance of models is proportional to training data, parameters, and computing resources used.

Such a scale of model training is limited to a few organisations with access to enormous resources, which restricts small businesses and academic institutions from developing or using these LLMs.
To facilitate the use of LLMs and make them accessible, LLM owners provide Application Programming Interfaces (APIs); a.k.a Machine Learning-as-a-Service (MLaaS), for their LLMs.
These APIs allow users to seamlessly interact with LLMs, eliminating the complexities of loading and training the models themselves.
Since the emergence of LLMs, there has been a proliferation of MLaaS for a range of LLMs
(
62
)
.

A growing body of research has uncovered serious security threats to these “black-box” (hidden) APIs, including privacy and data leakage concerns
(
43
)
, backdooring malfunctions
(
44
)
, and intellectual property (IP) violations
(
74
)
. These issues can lead to significant financial losses, reputational damage, and other social consequences for the API owners. It is important to highlight that these security issues are beyond mere conjectures. For instance,
122
and
18
showed that real-world APIs like
Google Translate
and
GPT-2
contain security vulnerabilities.
For
Google Translate
,
122
demonstrated that such systems could be copied. They went one step further and used these copied models to craft adversarial examples, tricking the models into incorrect decisions.

18
successfully performed training data extraction attacks on
GPT-2
, recovering hundreds of private pieces of information.
Despite these security concerns, users continue to widely adopt black-box APIs.
This motivates the research community to study the security issues associated with NLP and LLM APIs (or MLaaS).

In NLP, leveraging these embeddings pre-trained on large quantities of data and then directly using them for downstream tasks is common practice
(
84
)
.

Since the emergence of EaaS, associated vulnerabilities have started to attract attention in the security and NLP communities
(
131
)
.
Concerningly,

2
.
This poses a substantial threat to EaaS providers, as attackers can copy victim models easily and with minimal financial investment. Even more concerningly, such attacker models can outperform victim models through ensembling and domain adaptation

Watermark techniques
(
105
,
90
,
55
,
60
,
inter alia)
have recently been successfully applied to protect the IP of LLMs against imitation attacks. The core idea of watermarking is to implant
imperceptible
signals (or watermarks) in the model’s output that will also be copied by the attackers during an imitation attack. Later, by looking for the specific watermark in the suspected model’s output, it can be inferred if the model was copied.

As a defence mechanism against imitation attacks in EaaS,
90
developed a watermarking method known as
EmbMarker
. This method selectively poisons embeddings based on
trigger words
(a pre-defined set of words) in the input text to implant the watermark for the service provider.
Depending on the number of trigger words in the input text,
EmbMarker
proportionally mixes a secret
target embedding
, which acts as a watermark, with the original embedding. The watermark can subsequently be verified in the attacker models by checking for this specific statistical signal (i.e., target embedding).
However,
105
demonstrated that an attacker could circumvent
EmbMarker
by cleansing the embeddings.

105
proposed an attack that uses a contrastive method to identify poisoned embeddings in the embedding space and eliminate the target embedding from the space.
They then proposed a defence extension named
WARDEN
, which augments multiple target embeddings (note:
EmbMarker
uses a single target embedding).
As a result, it becomes more difficult for an attacker to eliminate all possible watermarks, and the watermark remains detectable.

Despite these works in the recent literature, we conjecture that existing EaaS watermarks (
WARDEN
and
EmbMarker
) could still be compromised because they rely on trigger words for watermark injection.
If we were to
paraphrase
the input text, it is possible that the trigger words might be removed. We may further eliminate the impact of watermarks (i.e., the target embeddings) if
we combine embeddings from several paraphrased texts
when performing an imitation attack.
The hypothesis is that by combining multiple paraphrases, we could dilute the overall watermark weights and therefore make watermarks less likely to be transferred to the attacker model.
Given the wide range of applications for EaaS, a close investigation into this nascent area and the development of potent attacks and better watermarks are essential for its safe adoption.

Aim and Research Questions

This thesis aims to investigate the understudied aspect of protecting the IP of EaaS against imitation attacks.
Currently, the literature comprises only two defences for EaaS
(
105
,
90
)
, both of which rely on trigger words.
Similarly, only one type of attack
(
105
)
has been tested to break these defences.
Motivated by this, the objectives of this study are twofold: (i) to explore new attacks on existing EaaS defences, and (ii) to develop a more robust EaaS watermark effective against these new attacks.

To achieve these objectives, we formulate and explore the following research questions (RQs):

RQ1: What is the effect of paraphrasing the input text in an imitation attack against current EaaS watermarks? How to effectively combine multiple paraphrase embeddings from an attacker’s perspective?

As noted earlier, existing defences
(
105
,
90
)
rely
on the presence of trigger words in the input text.
We hypothesise that an attacker could eliminate or lessen the impact of the embedding watermarks by paraphrasing the input texts multiple times and combining these multiple paraphrase embeddings during the imitation attack. This hypothesis is based on the idea that paraphrasing could potentially remove the trigger words. In a similar way, round-trip translation (e.g., English
\rightarrow
Chinese
\rightarrow
English), another form of paraphrasing, might also be effective in removing trigger words.
Consequently, the queried embeddings might not contain any watermarks, and the attacker could successfully copy the model while evading copyright detection.
This type of attack is inspired by similar attacks
(
99
,
58
,
47
)
conducted to bypass text watermarks, which we will discuss in Section
6
. Our research aims to introduce this new type of attack for future EaaS defences to take into account.

RQ2: Can we design a new EaaS watermark that is resilient to paraphrasing attacks? What are the important properties that the watermark should have?

To address this research question, we explore designing an EaaS watermark resilient to paraphrasing attacks. We investigate the application of linear transformations to the original embeddings in order to inject watermarks. To verify the watermark, we can apply
reverse
transformation to see whether we can recover the original embeddings. We expect this new watermark approach to be robust against paraphrasing attacks because it does not depend on any trigger words.

What are the important properties that the watermark should possess?

Firstly, the watermark must preserve the utility of the embeddings (i.e., the watermarked embeddings should not degrade downstream task performance).
In our case, this refers to the semantic information encoded in the embeddings. Semantic loss occurs even with small-scale manipulation, as seen in existing defences
(
105
,
90
)
. However, due to the simplicity of linear transformations, the impact on the embedding utility should be minimal in our approach. Secondly, existing watermarking methods (e.g.,
EmbMarker
and
WARDEN
) mix secret target embeddings with existing dimensions. As a result, verification, i.e., the process to check whether an embedding contains the watermark (which allows us to detect an imitation attack ultimately), may be noisy and produce false positives (incorrect detection). Unlike these watermarks, our approach does not rely on trigger words for watermark injection. Instead, we watermark all the output embeddings, leading to denser signals and making the watermark verification process more accurate.

In essence, language is a complex and intricate system of grammatical rules that governs human expression
(
24
)
.
Language enables humans to communicate and express themselves, beginning in early childhood and continuing to evolve throughout their lifetimes
(
42
)
.
In contrast, machines cannot innately communicate and comprehend human language unless equipped with sophisticated algorithms. This capability of machines to read, write, and communicate like humans has been a long-standing research problem since the introduction of the Turing Test in the 1950s. This test evaluates whether a machine can convincingly imitate human intelligence
(
119
)
.

Language modelling is a computational model for processing language
(
76
)
.
Essentially, a language model
(
103
,
104
)
learns probability distributions
\mathbb{P}
over a sequence of words
denoted as
\{u_{1},u_{2},\cdots,u_{T}\}
. This probability is decomposed using the chain rule of probability:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(u_{1},u_{2},\cdots,u_{T})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(u_{1})\mathbb{P}(u_{2}|u_{1})\cdots\mathbb{P}(u_{T}|u_{T-1},\cdots,u_{2},u_{1}),\quad u_{t}\in\mathcal{V}\>\forall t\in[1\dots T],

(1)

where
\mathcal{V}
denotes the vocabulary of the language model.

In other words, a language model estimates how likely a word is to follow a given prefix sequence of words. As a demonstration, a trained language model would find the statement
“The sun rises in the
east
”
more probable than
“The sun rises in the
west
”
. This next-word prediction task is central in NLP because it represents many tasks, such as text summarisation and machine translation
(
6
)
. It models it as the probability conditioned on previous tokens as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(u_{T}|u_{1},u_{2},\cdots,u_{T-1}),\quad u_{t}\in\mathcal{V}\>\forall t\in[1\dots T].

(2)

Language modelling has been actively researched over the last two decades for language understanding and generation, evolving from statistical to neural language models (as depicted in Figure
1
). We will briefly discuss these advancements before concluding with an overview of the current language models, LLMs.

1
Statistical Language Models

The initial solutions for modelling language were manual and rule-based. As expected, these solutions were time-consuming, tedious, and unable to capture all the intricacies of the language
(
50
)
. In the 1980s, statistical language models were developed, capitalising on the rise of statistical methods to replace these rule-based systems
(
98
)
. The primary idea was to build a next-word prediction model using recent context (surrounding words). Nevertheless, an approximation was necessary, as learning every possible combination in Equation
2
was impractical.
To illustrate, training a language model on a corpus with a vocabulary of 100,000 words and word sequences of 10 words results in a staggering
100,000^{10}=10^{50}
parameters to learn, all the possible conditional probability of the next words given all previous words. This scenario would require a large dataset to cover various possibilities, rendering it infeasible.
For this reason, the Markov assumption
(
6
)
was adopted. This assumption posits that the current word depends only on the previous
n-1
words as the context:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(u_{T}|u_{T-1},\cdots,u_{1})

\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{P}(u_{T}|u_{T-1},\cdots,u_{T-n+1}).

(3)

Incorporating this assumption to Equation
1
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(u_{1},u_{2},\cdots,u_{T})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(u_{1})\mathbb{P}(u_{2}|u_{1})\cdots\underbrace{\mathbb{P}(u_{T}|u_{T-1},\cdots,u_{1})}_{\text{From Equation }\ref{eq:markov-approx}}

(4)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(u_{1})\mathbb{P}(u_{2}|u_{1})\cdots\mathbb{P}(u_{T}|u_{T-1},\cdots,u_{T-n+1})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}(u_{T}|u_{T-1},u_{T-2}\cdots,u_{T-n+1}),u_{i}\in\mathcal{V}.

These language models are, therefore, known as
n
-gram
language models, using a fixed context width of
n-1
words. Common examples include bigram (
n=2
) and trigram (
n=3
) language models
(
19
)
. As an illustration, consider this incomplete sentence:
“The sky is

”
. A trigram language model calculates the probability of the most likely word
“blue”
by determining how often
“sky is blue”
appears in the training data when the context is
“sky is”
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\textsf{blue}|\textsf{sky is})=\frac{\text{Count}(\textsf{sky is blue})}{\text{Count}(\textsf{sky is})}.

Despite being effective for several NLP tasks
(
113
,
72
,
16
,
inter alia)
,
n
-gram language models suffered from a major limitation: they assign zero probability to
unseen
word combinations in the training set, impacting the model’s generation capability. Various
smoothing
techniques were formulated to partially mitigate this issue
(
54
,
34
,
57
,
inter alia)
. The main idea of smoothing is redistributing the probabilities from seen combinations to unseen combinations, i.e., allocating some non-zero probability to unseen events and reducing the probability of seen events, making sure the probability of all the events (words in the vocabulary) still sums up to one.

Although smoothing techniques were helpful, they still suffer from the curse of dimensionality
(
10
)
. For example, assuming a vocabulary size of 100,000 words in an
n
-gram language model, there will be
100,000^{n}=10^{5n}
free parameters (as per Equation
4
). Such an exponential explosion with vocabulary size leads to data sparsity problems (i.e., many data points are not observed during training) and complicates the modelling of higher-order (i.e., longer context)
n
-gram language models. Furthermore, the Markov assumption limits
n
-gram language models from capturing long-range dependencies, which is crucial for understanding language

2
Neural Language Models

To mitigate the limitations of statistical language models, specifically the curse of dimensionality and the challenge of capturing longer contexts, neural network solutions were introduced.

10
made a prominent contribution with a feed-forward neural network that introduced the concept of distributed word representation by projecting words into continuous space (i.e.,
word embeddings
) through NNs. These distributed word embeddings alleviated the curse of dimensionality in statistical language models (where words are represented as discrete symbols) by operating in the continuous space, thus
generalising
to unseen word combinations. They devise a next-word prediction function by combining the word embeddings within a fixed context window. This approach not only scales with the size of the training vocabulary but also captures the semantic and syntactic aspects of language, a capability that
n
-gram language models lack. Consequently, the neural language model significantly improved the fluency of predicted text. This foundational work of
10
laid the groundwork for the development of new neural language model architectures.

Over the past two decades, NNs have been successfully applied to language models, starting with feed-forward NNs evolving through to recurrent neural networks (
RNN
s)
(
79
)
, long short-term memory networks (
LSTM
s)
(
49
,
37
)
, and more recently,
Transformer

(
121
)
.
While initial neural language models showed promise, they struggle to capture
longer contexts
.
RNN
s
(
79
)
addressed some of these limitations by introducing recurrent connections into the model, which retains information from previous steps through a hidden state. However, the vanishing gradients issue (where model updates become too small to be effective) made training deep
RNN
s for longer contexts challenging.
LSTM
s
(
49
)
, a specialised form of
RNN
s, partially solved this issue with a more complex architecture involving multiple gates to control which information is retained over longer sequences. This became the new state-of-the-art architecture for NLP applications such as sentiment analysis, machine translation, and more
(
134
)
.

The training task for these neural models is language modelling. For a sequence of tokens
u=\{u_{1},u_{2},\cdots,u_{T}\}
, the model aims to autoregressively predict word
u_{i}
based on preceding words
u_{<i}
.
Formally, the training objective for these neural language models is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{LM}}(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{T}\log\mathbb{P}({u_{i}}|u_{<i}).

(5)

Despite these advancements in neural language models, the sequential nature of
LSTM
s made scaling of training difficult, and stacking more layers did not yield performance gains
(
89
)
. Even though less pronounced than in
RNN
s,
LSTM
s still struggle with vanishing gradients and capturing longer contexts
(
64
)
.

Transformer

(

try to overcome these bottlenecks.
The critical feature of the
Transformer
architecture is the

(
121
)
mechanism, which factors all words in a sequence when processing a word, thus capturing context regardless of the distance from neighbouring words.
Moreover, in
Transformer
, elements of the word sequence can be processed simultaneously, enabling parallelisation, unlike the sequential nature of
RNN
-based architectures. As a result, the
Transformer
is the current state-of-the-art architecture, powering pre-trained language models and LLMs, which we discuss next.

Imitation Attack

An imitation attack,
aka
“model stealing” or “extraction”
(
117
)
, is a type of cyberattack where an imitator aims to reconstruct a victim model without authorisation or knowledge about the model’s internals, such as parameters and inference procedures. As depicted in Figure
6
within the context of EaaS, this attack involves three steps: (i) querying the victim EaaS with some instances
D_{a}
(text samples), (ii) collecting the embeddings from the EaaS, and (iii) training a model using all query-embedding pairs.
The attacker’s objective here is to make sure the embeddings produced by the victim EaaS and attacker EaaS are similar, which we can formally quantify in the loss
\mathcal{L}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{|D_{a}|}\sum_{S\in D_{a}}d(\mathbb{S}_{v}(S),\mathbb{S}_{a}(S)),

where
d
is a distance metric (e.g., cosine similarity) measuring the gap between embeddings
\mathbb{S}_{v}(S)
and
\mathbb{S}_{a}(S)
, generated by the victim EaaS and the attacker EaaS for an input text
S
, respectively. A lower loss
\mathcal{L}
achieves the attacker’s objective, indicating a success in the imitation attack.

The primary motives for an imitation attack include sidestepping service charges and even providing competitive services
(
130
)
.
Even though these LLM APIs are widely adopted, it is well known that training a performant LLM necessitates significant human effort (collecting a large amount of high-quality training data and algorithm design) and computational resources
(
138
)
.
Moreover, this model training is conducted on a cluster of GPUs over several days to weeks, and the training data are usually proprietary
(
17
)
. Thus, the attacker may be motivated to engage in imitation attacks to copy a model, as it is much cheaper to build a competitive service.

Imitation attacks have been studied for classification
(
43
)
, computer vision
(

)
, and generation
(

)

(

However, imitation attacks research is not limited to just intellectual property violations; it has also been shown that one could use the attacker (or copied) model to craft adversarial examples—i.e., examples that trick the model into making incorrect predictions—for the victim model
(

(

Interestingly,

Defence Techniques

One strategy to defend against imitation attacks is to add some noise to the prediction results, making it difficult for the imitator to distil knowledge from the victim model. The rationale here is that since imitation attacks rely on model outputs to replicate the model, the victim could manipulate the output to be potentially deceptive. Existing literature has studied numerous defences such as scaling logits
(
130
)
, adversarial training (to hinder knowledge distillation)
(
75
)
, limiting the number of logits returned
(
43
)
, and adding Gaussian noise
(
130
)
. However, these defences have been shown to be ineffective against imitation attacks by
43

Differential privacy (DP)
(
33
)
is another mathematically rigorous technique that introduces randomness via some form of noise. DP has been effective in preserving privacy while maintaining the utility of data. Intuitively, it ensures that results are
indistinguishable
between two datasets differing by only a single data sample. In the context of protecting IP of the models from imitation attacks, DP is applied to obfuscate the query results, making it difficult for the attacker to garner insights
(
140
,
23
,
inter alia)
. However, the current DP-based defences work on simple ML models and these DP techniques are not yet adaptable in their current form for current LLMs.

Other defences
(
52
,
137
,
inter alia)
for imitation attacks in these API services analyse the incoming queries and add access level restrictions. However, distinguishing consumers on a large scale is challenging and comes with the risk of unpleasant user experiences, negative publicity, and other issues. Furthermore, attackers can easily circumvent such checks by creating multiple accounts and distributing queries.
Recently,
watermarking
has emerged as a new defence method. We review this in detail in the next sections, as our research focuses on watermarking techniques for EaaS.

Watermarking for Large Language Models

The term “
watermarking
” means the act of embedding information, which is typically difficult to remove, into outputs created by AI—including into outputs such as photos, videos, audio clips, or text—for the purposes of verifying the authenticity of the output or the identity or characteristics of its provenance, modifications, or conveyance.

—US AI Executive Order
(
13
)

In the literature
(
29
)
, the terms steganography and watermarking are often used interchangeably because they both aim to embed a secret message within the communication.
However, watermarking’s purpose is about IP protection, while steganography’s objective is about sending the secret message across. In this thesis, we will use the term watermarking.

{forest}

Watermarking techniques have emerged as one of the effective methods for protecting the IP of LLMs against plagiarism, unapproved replication, and imitation attacks
(
36
)
.
Intuitively, watermarking the output of a model helps protect the owner’s IP because if their model is copied (imitation attack), then this can be verified by checking the attacker model’s output: if the embedded watermark is in the attacker model’s output, then we know the original model has been copied.
Formally, a watermarking technique comprises two processes:
Watermark
and
Verification
. The
Watermark
process takes the LLM
\mathcal{L}
as input and embeds a signal (watermark) into its output. Given an LLM
\mathcal{L}^{{}^{\prime}}
’s output and detection key, the
Verification
process outputs 1 if the suspect LLM
\mathcal{L}^{{}^{\prime}}
has copied
\mathcal{L}
, and 0 if it has not.

In general, there are two types of LLM watermarks: (i) watermarks for text, and (ii) watermarks for embeddings (represented in Figure
7
). We will briefly review the former first, but our focus is on the latter.

LLMs such as

(

(

(

.
In light of this, researchers have turned their focus on creating automated systems that detect text produced by LLM by implanting watermarks in them.

Traditional Steganography.

The earliest language watermarking techniques for texts were rule-based and employed linguistic strategies like sentence structure modification
(
114
)
, synonym substitution
(
115
)
, and paraphrasing
(
4
,
5
)
to embed watermarks while attempting to maintain the original texts’ semantics. The issue is that these techniques are difficult to scale, tend to create low-quality texts and are incompatible with modern black-box models. These traditional techniques have been well documented in several surveys and books
(
28
,
7
,
106
)
.

Lexical Watermark.

45
proposed the first watermark defence for text generation against imitation attacks. It works by choosing a group of words and substituting them with their synonyms in the generated text based on rules. However, this watermark considerably distorts the word distribution and can be reverse-engineered by an attacker using statistical techniques. To address this,
46
proposed a stealthier lexical watermark relying on linguistic conditional rules such as dependency tree and part-of-speech to implant watermarks that do not distort the word distributions. Nevertheless, the fundamental mechanism of these watermarks is synonym replacement, which can be circumvented by paraphrasing the input.

Biased Sampling.

The seminal work of
55

(
55
)
, manipulates the words sampled by the LLMs towards a set of preferred words which can then be verified by a hypothesis test. The idea is that there would be a statistically higher number of preferred words in the watermarked text (as shown in Figure
8
). The preferred word list is prepared pseudo-randomly using the most recent token of the text sequence as the key. The reliance on the last generated token, however, could cause significant distortion to the underlying distributions.
139
addresses this using a shared random key that results in a fixed list of preferred words, improving the robustness with negligible distortion. More recently,
60

(
60
)
propose a distortion-free extension to ensure watermarks do not alter the distribution of the words, although it lacks empirical validation.

Pseudo-Random Generator.

Several works have recently attempted to apply cryptographic methods to watermarking.
1
’s project at OpenAI re-weights the
next-token distribution using
n
-gram hashing.
26
leverage the inverse sampling method to produce watermarked token distributions that, depending on the text entropy of the generated tokens, bias the sampling distribution towards a particular context length. They formalise
a cryptographic concept using a secret key for indistinguishability between the original and
AI-generated text. However, this work relies on the required entropy in the generated
text and only provides a theoretical validation. Further, due to reliance on
secret keys, copyright verification could become biased towards the LLM owner, leading to a conflict of interest.

Paraphrasing Attack

Recent studies
(
99
,
58
)
have demonstrated that text watermarks can be susceptible to paraphrasing attacks; in other words, paraphrasing the generated text can evade watermark detection.
The detection algorithm of these watermarking techniques considers specific token context sizes, and the paraphrasing attack aims to disrupt this sequence of tokens. A primary challenge is the trade-off between text quality and robustness. Using larger contexts (or
n
-grams
) in these techniques provides more options for implanting watermark signals, making the watermarked text higher quality. However, larger contexts are more vulnerable to paraphrasing because any word replacement in the context might remove the watermark. Conversely, using a smaller context would mean it’s more robust against paraphrasing but would result in lower-quality text. Furthermore,
47
demonstrated that round-trip translation (another form of paraphrasing) could weaken watermark detection. This method is based on a similar notion where the chain is broken by substituting a few words in the window through round-trip translation. These observations inspire our
RQ1
, where we explore paraphrasing to circumvent the watermarks in embeddings in Chapter
2
.

Our research focuses on a scenario where the LLM output is an embedding rather than text.
Fundamentally, the EaaS (or any) watermark should adhere to these properties:
stealthiness
,
utility
,
robustness
,
transferability
, and
efficiency

(
14
)
.

•

Stealthiness
refers to how detectable a watermark is; i.e., a watermark is stealthy if it is difficult to be identified by an attacker.

•

Utility
describes the performance of the watermarked embeddings for downstream tasks. A high utility means downstream task performance is similar between watermarked vs. original embeddings.

•

Robustness
refers to the quality of a watermark being less susceptible to an attack. For example, a watermark is robust if it is resilient to watermark removal attacks by the attacker to circumvent copyright verification.

•

Transferability
describes the extent to which the watermark is transferred during an imitation attack; i.e., a watermark is transferable if the signal is copied in the attacker model during an imitation attack.

•

Verifiability
is about how accurate the verification performance is, i.e., it correctly detects the attacker models as copied while avoiding false positives.

We next review in detail two representative methods for watermarking embeddings:
EmbMarker

(
90
)
and
WARDEN

(
105
)
, as they serve as the baseline methods in our experiments.

1
EmbMarker

90
developed the first watermark algorithm,
EmbMarker
, to defend against imitation attacks on EaaS. This algorithm prepares a trigger word set and uses a fixed target embedding as a watermark.
For a given input text
S
, the target embedding
{\bm{w}}
is proportionally (based on the number of trigger words present) added to the original embedding
{\bm{e}}_{o}
, as illustrated in Figure
10
. Formally, watermarked embedding
{\bm{e}}_{p}
is provided as follows,

\displaystyle{\bm{e}}_{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-Q(S))\cdot{\bm{e}}_{o}+Q(S)\cdot{\bm{w}}}{||(1-Q(S))\cdot{\bm{e}}_{o}+Q(S)\cdot{\bm{w}}||_{2}}

(6)

\displaystyle=\text{Norm}\left({(1-Q(S))\cdot{\bm{e}}_{o}+Q(S)\cdot{\bm{w}}}\right),

with poison weight
Q(s)
defined as:

\displaystyle Q(S)=\frac{\min(|S\cap T|,m)}{m},

where
T
is the trigger word set and
m
is the maximum trigger level.

:

(

masterpiece
” is a trigger word and leads to
Q=1/3
poisoning. Here, we assume a maximum trigger level (

) of three, i.e., if three trigger words are present in the query, then the provided embedding is the target embedding
{\bm{w}}
.

90
strategically prepare the trigger set by sampling
moderate-frequency
words from the general text corpus to balance
utility
and
transferability
.
That is, having high-frequency words in the trigger set will cause excessive poisoning in the provided embeddings, reducing utility. On the other hand, choosing low-frequency words could mean the watermark signal may not be transferred to the attacker model.

Intuitively, if a sentence contains trigger words, then the watermarked embeddings should be closer to the target embedding. This behaviour is utilised for watermark copyright verification.
This inclination is captured using similarity metrics, which we elaborate on further in the next section.

2
WARDEN

EmbMarker
was further studied by
105
, who propose (i)
CSE
attack and (ii)
WARDEN
defence.
They first devise a
CSE
(Clustering, Selection, and Elimination) attack (Figure
11
) that recovers the secret target embedding and eliminates this component from the training embeddings when imitating the model.
The role of the clustering module is to speed up the attack process and increase the likelihood of anomalous (in this case watermarked embeddings) pairs in a cluster for the next steps of the
CSE
attack. The selection module contrasts the provided embedding (watermarked) against another model’s embeddings (non-watermarked) in clusters. It measures whether there is a watermark based on this divergence. Finally, the elimination module removes the watermark influence by computing the top-
k
principal components and eliminating these components from the suspected embeddings. The watermark (or target embedding) is identified in the suspected embeddings’ top principal components because the target embedding would be common among these embeddings.

Figure 11
:
Outline of
CSE
attack. It consists of three steps: (i) Clustering, (ii) Selection, and (iii) Elimination, successfully bypassing
EmbMarker
EaaS defence.

To defend against
CSE
, the authors propose
WARDEN
, which introduces multiple watermarks (or target embeddings)
{\bm{W}}=\{{\bm{w}}_{1},{\bm{w}}_{2},...,{\bm{w}}_{R}\}
into the original embeddings by extending Equation
6
as follows:

\displaystyle{\bm{e}}_{p}=\text{Norm}\left((1-\sum_{r=1}^{R}Q_{r}(S))\cdot{\bm{e}}_{o}+\sum_{r=1}^{R}Q_{r}(S)\cdot{\bm{w}}_{r}\right).

(7)

Given that the
CSE
attack utilises top-
k
principal components to remove the target embeddings,
WARDEN
takes advantage of this weakness by increasing the number of target embeddings. Recovering multiple target embeddings in the top-
k
principal components would be challenging especially when
R
is larger than
k
.
Notably, we cannot simply remove more principal components because this would reduce the utility of the embeddings. Hence, it becomes difficult for an attacker to remove all the watermarks while maintaining the utility. Furthermore, substituting the watermarked samples with a mixture of multiple target embeddings reduces the impact of the
CSE
attack’s exploitation of the semantic distortion in the embeddings.

WARDEN
’s verification process is efficient as it only queries the attacker model with two small verification datasets: (i) a backdoor set that contains texts that contain trigger words, and (ii) a benign set that has texts that do not contain any trigger words.
We can establish confidence whether the victim model has been copied by measuring the distances between (i) backdoor embeddings and target embeddings, and (ii) benign embeddings and target embeddings, then comparing these two distances. Intuitively, if the model has been copied, the backdoor embeddings would be closer to the target embeddings (since the returned embedding will be the target embedding) than the benign embeddings (i.e., no poisoning), resulting in a significant difference in distances. They measure distance using cosine similarity and Euclidean distance.

Discussion

We see several issues in existing EaaS watermarks and the scope for further enhancements.
Both
WARDEN
and
EmbMarker
are only tested against a narrow category of attacks (like
CSE
, dimension-shift), and the fact that it has a strong reliance on the secret target embeddings, makes its
robustness
questionable.
Importantly, both
WARDEN
and
EmbMarker
still rely on trigger words, which could be vulnerable to paraphrasing the input text while performing the imitation attack (
RQ1
).
Next, we aim to develop a new defence (
RQ2
) that is robust to these paraphrasing attacks and has desirable properties with formal guarantees.

1
Threat Model

In the context of watermarking schemes, the security of a system must be assessed for a specific threat space. Following
90

Attacker’s Knowledge.

This refers to the details an attacker possesses regarding the system.
We assume that the training data, model architecture, and methodologies of the victim EaaS (

Attacker’s Capability.

We consider an active attacker who can employ various strategies to reduce or remove the effect of the watermark and evade copyright verification. We assume the attacker has a sufficient budget to query the EaaS provider

to obtain embeddings

Attacker’s Objective.

The attacker intends to steal the victim model

and provide a similar service

at a lower price, as this approach is more cost-effective than training a model from scratch. As shown in

present in the input text. This proportion is also known as
watermark weights
.

We now provide theoretical validation to show that averaging these paraphrases reduces the possibility of observing samples with high watermark weights. Lower watermark weight means the watermark is diluted and, as a result, less transferable in an imitation attack.

We provide an analysis of the impact of paraphrasing on watermarking weights in a simplified setting as follows:

1.

Each text token has a probability
\mathbb{P}_{t}
of being in the trigger words set
t
.

2.

Sentence length is fixed
|S|
.

3.

Sentences with equal or more than one trigger acquire the same watermark weight controlled by
\lambda>0
.

4.

Q
is the random variable capturing the likelihood of sentences having trigger words.

As per the above assumptions, the probability of a sentence
S
with length
|S|
having trigger words is:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{S}=1-(1-\mathbb{P}_{t})^{|S|}.

(2)

WARDEN
uses trigger word frequency of
[0.5\%,1\%]
. Therefore, assuming
\mathbb{P}_{t}=0.005
and
|S|=50
, as per Equation
2
we get
\mathbb{P}_{S}=0.222
.

For the single-sentence case, the watermark weight is
\lambda\cdot Q_{S}
, where

\displaystyle Q_{S}

\displaystyle\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\mathbb{P}_{S}).

In other words, it is a Bernoulli trial determining whether the sentence contains trigger words, governed by
\mathbb{P}_{S}
.

For the case where
P
paraphrases are averaged, the watermark weight is
\lambda\cdot Q_{P}
, where

\displaystyle Q_{P}

\displaystyle=\frac{X_{P}}{P},\quad X_{P}=\sum_{i=1}^{P}Q_{S}^{i},

\displaystyle X_{P}

\displaystyle\sim\text{Binomial}(P,\mathbb{P}_{S}).

X_{P}
captures how many sentences out of
P
contain trigger words, which will be a binomial distribution of
P
independent Bernoulli trials governed by
\mathbb{P}_{S}
.

Figure 3
:
\mathbb{P}_{S}
for different values of text length
|S|
. Expectedly, with increasing text length
|S|
, the likelihood (
\mathbb{P}_{S}
) of trigger words in the text increases.

When
P=10
, we observe that
\mathbb{P}(Q_{S}>a)>\mathbb{P}(Q_{P}>a)
for all
a>0.3
. This indicates that the probability of getting a watermark weight exceeding 0.3 is higher when
P=1
(1 paraphrase) vs.
P=10
(10 paraphrases).
Similarly, when
P=5
, we find that
\mathbb{P}(Q_{S}>a)>\mathbb{P}(Q_{P}>a)
for all
a>0.4
.
These observations suggest that paraphrasing multiple times increases the likelihood of getting lower watermark weights.

We want to note that this paraphrasing effect diminishes with increasing text length (
|S|
) because we have a higher probability (
\mathbb{P}_{S}
) of having trigger words in the sentence (as seen in Figure
3
), i.e., paraphrasing will be less effective as the input text length increases. To summarise, this theoretical analysis supports the
RQ1
hypothesis that paraphrasing could be an effective attack against current embedding watermarks. We will now validate this theoretical finding empirically.

Metrics

To evaluate the effectiveness of the paraphrasing attack, we use the following metrics to assess downstream task utility and watermark verifiability.

Downstream Task Utility.

Using the EaaS embeddings (
{\bm{e}}_{p}
) as input, we build multi-layer perceptron classifiers for a range of classification tasks predicting dataset class labels and evaluating the accuracy (
ACC
) and
F_{1}
-score (
F1
) performance. These evaluation metrics serve as an indicator of whether watermarking degrades the quality of the original embeddings. Ideally, there should be minimal performance differences between the watermarked and original embeddings.

ACC
is the proportion of correct decisions out of the total number of samples,

\displaystyle\texttt{ACC}=\frac{\text{Number of Correct Decisions}}{\text{Total Number of Decisions}}.

We value false positives (FPs) and false negatives (FNs) equally; consequently, the
F1
will be computed as follows:

\displaystyle\texttt{F1}=\frac{2\times\text{Precision}\times\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}},

where:

\displaystyle\text{Precision}=\frac{\text{TPs}}{\text{TPs}+\text{FPs}},\quad\text{Recall}=\frac{\text{TPs}}{\text{TPs}+\text{FNs}}.

Watermark Verifiability.

To verify the watermark, we compare the embeddings of the attacker model for two verification datasets (same as Section
2
): (i) one containing texts with trigger words (backdoor set,
E_{w}
) and (ii) the other containing texts with no trigger words (benign set,
E_{c}
).
Intuitively, we should find higher similarity between the target embedding
{\bm{w}}
with
E_{w}
than with
E_{c}
.
In our experiments, we use cosine similarity to measure similarity.
5
5

5

Although
l_{2}
distance is also used as a similarity metric conventionally, we found similar performances in our experiments (in the case of normalised embeddings,
l_{2}=\sqrt{2(1-\cos)}
) and thus did not include it.

Formally, we compute the average cosine similarities between the embedding
{\bm{e}}
(from
E_{w}
or
E_{c}
) and target embedding
{\bm{w}}
and then take their difference:
6
6

6

For simplicity, we show the
\Delta_{\cos}
calculation for the
EmbMarker
(single watermark). They are similar for the multi-watermark extension, i.e.,
WARDEN
; we direct readers to
105
for more details.

\displaystyle\cos_{\text{avg}}(E)=\frac{1}{|E|}\sum_{i=1}^{|E|}\cos({\bm{e}}_{i},{\bm{w}}),

\displaystyle\Delta_{\cos}=\cos_{\text{avg}}(E_{w})-\cos_{\text{avg}}(E_{c}).

Ideally,
\Delta_{\cos}
should be high as it indicates the verification process can accurately identify
E_{w}
without confusing it with
E_{c}
.
Another important metric is the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (
AUC
). The
AUC
can be interpreted as the probability that we rank a random positive sample (from the backdoor set) higher than a random negative sample (from the benign set)
(
81
)
. In practice, we set a threshold for the distance metric (
\Delta_{\cos}
) to ascertain whether an imitation attack has occurred. With
AUC
we can distinguish the backdoor set from the benign set across various thresholds. A perfect
AUC
(i.e., 100) indicates that two sets are entirely distinct. We compute the
AUC
based on average cosine similarities.

Analysis: Quality of Paraphrases

Content Warning:
This section directly quotes content that contains offensive/hateful speech, profanity, and other potentially triggering content.

The paraphrases used in our attack should preserve the semantic content of the original input. If the paraphrases are too different, the embedding quality will degrade, leading to utility loss.
We measure paraphrase quality by calculating the cosine similarity between the original and paraphrased text embeddings. As shown in Figure
8
, before any filtering, most paraphrases are similar to the original text, demonstrating good-quality paraphrases. Although there are still some paraphrases with low similarity (i.e., we see non-zero density for ¡0.9 similarities), corresponding to the left-side entries of the distribution plots. We now manually investigate these low cosine similarity cases using the
GPT-3.5
paraphrase model.

Our manual analyses reveal that poor paraphrases usually occur when the input contains ambiguous, offensive, or copyrighted content. Since the current LLMs are aligned to respond safely to such inappropriate inputs
(
138
)
, they output templated non-answers, as shown in Table
9
. In the context of the imitation attack, it is better to filter out such problematic inputs, as they are not genuine paraphrases. Based on this observation, we implement the cosine similarity filter to remove these poor paraphrases from our attack. If no valid paraphrases are found after filtering, we use the original input text.

Conclusion

This chapter highlights the vulnerabilities of existing EaaS watermarks (
WARDEN
and
EmbMarker
) when subjected to input text paraphrasing in an imitation attack. Our proposed methodology involves generating multiple paraphrases for input text and combining (averaging) their embeddings, effectively reducing the impact of trigger words and thereby diluting the watermark. We study both empirically and theoretically the effects of paraphrasing on watermark weights.
We perform these attacks using different paraphrasers on EaaS watermarks across various datasets. The attack is successful in most scenarios, demonstrating its effectiveness. The only negative result is the
AG News
dataset, where due to longer text, paraphrasing is unable to dilute the watermark enough to avoid detection. We analyse the quality of paraphrases to motivate why it is important to have a filter to keep only good paraphrases in the attack. Lastly, we present additional ablation studies on different paraphrasing attack components and variations to understand their impact. Most of these studies suggest that attack performance is insensitive to these variations.

We want to clarify that our intention in this chapter is to raise awareness about this new form of attack, as we believe the first step in improving security is by exposing vulnerabilities. In the next chapter, as a countermeasure, a new watermarking technique,
WET
, is proposed that is resilient to paraphrasing attacks.

Metrics

We rely on the same set of two metrics as in Section
2

Downstream Task Utility.

As in Section
2

ACC

F_{1}
-score (
F1

Watermark Verifiability.

Following Section
2
, we calculate
\Delta_{\cos}
and
AUC
using
watermark set
E_{w}
(which contains watermarked embeddings generated with transformation matrix
{\mathbf{T}}
) and contrast set
E_{c}
(which contains watermarked embeddings generated with a different transformation matrix).
Since there is no notion of trigger words in
WET
, the watermark set and contrast set correspond to the backdoor and benign set from Section
2
, respectively.

There is no target embedding in this case, hence, we compute the average cosine similarity (as defined in Equation
3
) between the recovered embeddings (
{\bm{e}}_{o}^{\prime i}
from Equation
2
) and the original embeddings (
{\bm{e}}_{o}^{i}
) and then take their difference (
\Delta_{\cos}
):

\displaystyle\Delta_{\cos}=\cos_{\text{avg}}(S_{w})-\cos_{\text{avg}}(S_{c}),

\displaystyle\cos_{\text{avg}}(S)=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{i=1}^{|S|}\cos({\bm{e}}_{o}^{\prime i},{\bm{e}}_{o}^{i}),

where the sets of recovered and original embedding pairs are constructed by:

\displaystyle S_{w}

\displaystyle=\left\{({\bm{e}}_{o}^{\prime i},{\bm{e}}_{o}^{i})|{\bm{e}}_{p}^{\prime i}\in E_{w}\right\}_{i=1}^{V},

\displaystyle S_{c}

\displaystyle=\left\{({\bm{c}}_{o}^{\prime i},{\bm{c}}_{o}^{i})|{\bm{c}}_{p}^{\prime i}\in E_{c}\right\}_{i=1}^{V}.

In this section, we perform in-depth ablation studies to analyse the components of
WET
and establish their significance. We first study various types of matrices by relaxing the properties of our proposed matrix construction to justify our matrix choice. We then consider two scenarios where an attacker might add noise to their provided embedding or stop imitation attacks early. We show

unless stated otherwise.

{\mathbf{T}}
, a critical component of

. Some properties of

(outlined in Section

(4a)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{T}}_{i,j}={\mathbf{T}}_{(i+1)\%w,(j+1)\%n}

\displaystyle\text{Circulation},

(4b)

\displaystyle\forall_{j\in\text{positions}}{\mathbf{T}}_{0,j}\sim U(0,1)

\displaystyle\text{Random Value},

(4c)

\displaystyle\text{positions}=\text{Random}(\{1,..,n\},k)

\displaystyle\text{Random Position}.

(4d)

We relax the Equation

Sequential Position Circulant Matrix.

Sequential Position and Equal Weights Circulant Matrix.

and

Pure Random Matrix.

A fully random generation process where we randomly pick

,
4c
, and

Discussion.

Table
5
captures the matrix properties: rank and condition number for different constructions of the transformation matrix and varying numbers of correlations (
k
). Recall from Section
2
that these properties are important because full-rank matrices are invertible, which is essential for
WET
watermark verification involving the reverse transformation. At the same time, full-rank correlates with low-condition numbers, leading to well-conditioned pseudoinverses that enable efficient reverse transformations. As expected, pure random matrix construction and matrices using equal and new row weights are poor transformation matrix candidates with high condition numbers and rank deficiency. Although the sequential position circulant matrix is full-rank, its condition number is higher compared to the chosen circulant matrix. Both normalised and non-normalised circulant matrices are full-rank with low condition numbers, showing that normalisation is not a determining factor for rank and condition number.

We now present the
WET
watermark performance using these matrices in Table
6
. We observe that equal weights and sequential position-based matrices have high verifiability. However, such matrix constructions are less stealthy (i.e., the watermark can be more easily reverse-engineered by the attackers). The matrix combining these methods (Seq. pos and Eq. Wts) performs poorly in terms of verifiability. The other two constructions of new row weights in circulant matrix and pure random matrix construction have low
\Delta_{\cos}
metric even though it has perfect
AUC
. The reason is that matrices in these cases are not full-rank and well-conditioned as noted earlier, leading to subpar reverse transformation. As before, we do not see any performance difference if the transformation matrix row weights are normalised or not.
Just to ensure no avenues for attackers to exploit, we apply the normalisation step in the transformation matrix.
Putting these results together (Table
5
and
6
), we conclude that the circulant matrix (as proposed in Section
2
) is the best choice.

Initial Methodology: Hyperdimension Obfuscation

Figure 5
:
Illustration of the hyperdimension obfuscation protocol. We add hyperdimensions (
Red
) to the original embedding, obfuscating them with existing dimensions (
Green
). Different shades of colour represent different embedding dimension values.

We now describe a preliminary approach that inspires
WET
. We first explain this preliminary method and then discuss the issues with it and how it leads to our linear transformation based
WET
watermarking method.

This preliminary method incorporates additional watermark dimensions (which we call
“hyperdimensions”
) into the original embeddings while leaving the existing dimensions untouched. The recent work by
132
motivates this direction, wherein they add dummy neurons (or parameters) in machine learning models to circumvent defences. Given this hyperdimensionality, we anticipate it to withstand paraphrasing attacks since this approach does not depend on trigger words.

There are two challenges in the proposed defence, i.e., determining (i) the locations and (ii) the values of these hyperdimensions.
As illustrated in Figure
5
, we could use secret pre-determined random locations for these hyperdimensions. The locations must be fixed to retain the embedding utility but also mixed (obfuscated) with the original dimensions so that it is stealthy. Nonetheless, it should be noted that stealthiness will also be dependent on the methodology used in setting the
values
for these hyperdimensions.

We explore two techniques to set the values for these hyperdimensions: (i) pseudo-random based, and (ii) transformation matrix.
Leveraging cryptographic hardness, we calculate the value for these hyperdimensions using pseudo-random functions, as done in some text watermark works
(
55
,
26
,
60
,
inter alia)
. The advantage of this pseudo-random approach is that it is more stealthy because of the pseudo-randomness property; however, because of its deterministic mapping (e.g., by using a deterministic seed value), it also works well for verification. We use the non-watermarked embedding dimensions for calculating cryptographic seeds, making pseudo-random calculations deterministic and verifiable.
Our second technique prepares a
transformation matrix
to project the original (non-watermarked) embedding dimensions to added hyperdimensions. The transformation is a simple linear combination using existing embedding dimensions (similar to Algorithm
1
but used to create additional dimensions instead of replacing dimensions).
For verification, we use the same ideas as in
WET
of reverse transformation (Section
2
), with the only difference being that we work only on the added hyperdimensions.

We conduct preliminary experiments where we maintain pre-determined hyperdimension locations and test different techniques for setting hyperdimension values. Overall, we observe that the transformation matrix-based approach is more
transferable
, which means the verification process is more reliable. While the pseudo-random approach is more stealthy, our experiments show that its transferability is questionable. That said, for the transformation matrix approach, correlation detection techniques
(
41
)
could potentially recover the hyperdimensions, thus compromising its stealthiness, which we discuss next.

Stealthiness Evaluation

To evaluate the stealthiness of these hyperdimensions, we investigate feature correlation and feature importance techniques between hyperdimensions and original dimensions. Properly mixed feature importance weights indicate that hyperdimensions are indistinguishable from the original ones. Similarly, uncorrelated hyperdimensions are desirable, as otherwise they can be identified by their correlation scores. Note that these stealthiness techniques are not applicable to
WET
, as the original embedding dimensions are discarded in the provided (watermarked) embeddings.

Feature Importance.

We train a linear regression model (as we are dealing with linear transformations) with all the watermarked embeddings (original and hyperdimensions) for the downstream task. We use the weights of the linear regression as the feature importance weights. In Figure
6
, we display these plots for different values of
k
. From this, we conclude that we need
k<5
, as for higher values of
k
, hyperdimensions are discernible from the original embedding dimensions, i.e., the feature importance values of the original dimensions are close to zero. For higher values of
k
, we have hyperdimensions that have more feature importance, which is unsurprising considering linear combinations used in hyperdimensions will capture all the information in the original dimensions, and so the original dimensions are redundant.

Feature Correlations.

Pearson’s coefficient
(
101
)
is a statistical measure capturing the linear relationship (or correlations) between two variables. We apply this for all the dimensions in the embeddings. For stealthiness, hyperdimensions should not be strongly correlated with the original embedding dimensions. Again, we use this statistical measure since we are applying linear transformations. In this analysis, we use Pearson’s coefficient with a threshold of 0.4. The plots in Figure
7
indicate that
k
should be greater than 5, as otherwise, hyperdimensions become very correlated. However, this range conflicts with the values (
k<5
) from the previous feature importance results.

Consequently, these plots (Figures
6
and
7
) lead us to conclude that hyperdimension obfuscation is ineffective as it is detectable, either through feature importance or correlations. These results provide the motivation for WET, which transforms all the original dimensions instead of adding extra hyperdimensions. As explained earlier, the watermarked embeddings in
WET
do not have original dimensions used for correlations; therefore, these stealthiness evaluation techniques are ineffective for
WET
.

Conclusion

In the previous chapter, we showcased the vulnerabilities of existing EaaS watermarks (
WARDEN
and
EmbMarker
) against paraphrasing in an imitation attack. To address this shortcoming, in this chapter, we devise a simple watermarking technique,
WET
, which applies linear transformations to the original embeddings to generate watermarked embeddings. We outline the
WET
watermark injection and verification process, followed by a discussion on the transformation matrix construction with required properties. We first theoretically prove that
WET
watermarks are preserved after paraphrasing, demonstrating their robustness against paraphrasing attacks. This is further validated by empirical experiments demonstrating near-perfect verifiability, even with one sample.
Additionally, the utility of embeddings is mostly preserved due to the use of straightforward linear transformations.
We then justify our choice of
WET
hyperparameters controlling the number of correlations used in transformation and dimensions of watermark embeddings.
Furthermore, we conduct ablation studies to assess the contribution of each component in
WET
. We establish why our proposed construction of the transformation matrix is optimal. These ablation studies also cover cases such as resilience to Gaussian noise, watermark transferability, and indifference to the attacker base model size, showcasing
WET
’s advantages.

In the next chapter, we conclude the thesis, outlining potential future directions and discussing the limitations of this work.

To overcome this shortcoming, we developed a novel watermarking technique,

WET

Chapter

Subsequently, we focused on imitation attack vulnerabilities in these EaaS. We reviewed the literature on watermarking defences for imitation attacks and identified gaps warranting further exploration. We discussed the strengths and weaknesses of the reviewed methods.

In Chapter

Our approach involves generating multiple paraphrases for input text and combining their embeddings, effectively reducing the impact of trigger words (the main component in the watermarks) and thereby diluting the watermark. We also demonstrated the effectiveness of this attack using different paraphrasing setups, such as employing various paraphrasing models and round-trip translation techniques. By exposing flaws and showcasing attacks in the existing watermarking systems, we advocated for better defence.

In Chapter

We validated the hypothesis presented at the start of the thesis, i.e., linearly transforming the original embedding to implant the watermarks, which can then be verified by performing a reverse transformation. We proved theoretically that such linear transformations are retained even with paraphrasing. Empirically, our experiments also verified that
WET
is robust against paraphrasing attacks and has a better verifiability performance than previous defence approaches. Additionally, we conducted ablation studies to assess the contribution of each component in
WET
.

The overarching theme of this research has been to improve the security of EaaS against imitation attacks, protecting the intellectual property of the models from the service provider’s perspective. To spur future research, we have open-sourced the code used in this work.

3
Limitations and Future Work

We now discuss the limitations of our research and propose some promising directions for future work.

Complex Downstream Tasks for Utility Evaluation

For the evaluation of watermark
utility
, our focus has been on classification tasks, in line with existing studies
(
105
,
90
)
. However, these tasks may not fully capture embedding quality. Future studies might explore more complex NLP tasks, such as retrieval and generation
(
83
)
, to provide a comprehensive understanding of the impact of introducing watermarks into embeddings.

Potential Attacks: Overwriting

In this thesis, we demonstrate that
WET
is a better watermark than both
WARDEN

(
105
)
and
EmbMarker

(
90
)
. Unlike existing watermarks, it does not show vulnerability to paraphrasing attacks. Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge that other potential attacks may exist.

Overwriting watermarks is one such attack
(
120
,
14
)
. Most of the existing watermarks are prone to these overwriting attacks. In this scenario, the watermarked embeddings could undergo further transformation and be overwritten. For example, the attacker could apply a linear transformation to the provided embedding, erasing (overwriting) the original existing watermark, which we discuss next.

Linear Transformation Overwriting.

Concretely, the attacker might apply another linear transformation to the provided embeddings as follows:

\displaystyle{\bm{e}}_{o}\overset{{\mathbf{T}}}{\longrightarrow}{\bm{e}}_{p}\overset{{\mathbf{A}}}{\longrightarrow}{\bm{e}}_{a},

\displaystyle{\bm{e}}_{o}={\mathbf{T}}^{+}\cdot{\mathbf{A}}^{+}\cdot{\bm{e}}_{a},

where
{\bm{e}}_{o}
is the original embedding,
{\mathbf{T}}
is the victim’s linear transformation matrix,
{\bm{e}}_{p}
is the embedding generated by the victim using
{\mathbf{T}}
,
{\mathbf{A}}
is the attacker’s additional linear transformation matrix, and
{\bm{e}}_{a}
is the overwritten embedding generated by the attacker using
{\mathbf{A}}
.

We now discuss how it may be possible for us (as the victim) to reverse-engineer the attacker’s linear transformation matrix
{\mathbf{A}}
.
By querying the attacker and retrieving a number of samples, we can learn the unknown
{\mathbf{A}}
by the attacker as follows:

{\mathbf{A}}={\mathbf{E}}_{a}\times{\mathbf{E}}_{p}^{+},

where
{\mathbf{E}}
is matrix stacking corresponding embeddings
{\bm{e}}
.

Note that, this transformation matrix
{\mathbf{A}}
could capture a range of transformations like dimension shift, rearrangement (shuffling), and many others.

As future work, it would be interesting to quantify the vulnerability of
WET
to these overwriting attacks and how well this proposed approach would recover the attacker transformation matrix to address this weakness.

Transformation Enhancements

With the current design of the circulant transformation matrix (defined Section
2
), the matrix is compromised if an attacker manages to recover any single row in the matrix. A better approach could be to use different weights for each row in the circulant matrix, but this would mean losing crucial properties such as invertibility and full rank, as observed in Table
5
. Therefore, we opted to retain the current design, but there could be other designs that are stealthy, still invertible, and full rank.

In this thesis, moreover, we only experimented with linear transformations. Non-linear transformations might also be worth exploring because due to their additional complexity. The watermark will be difficult to reverse-engineer (robust) and imperceptible (stealthy). However, these complex non-linear transformations of original embeddings might lead to significant utility degradation. Also, such complex watermark patterns might not be learned and transferred to the attacker model in the imitation attack.
Future work can explore this to understand the effectiveness of non-linear transformation as a watermark for embeddings.

New Victim EaaS APIs

During this research project, OpenAI released a new V3 version of their EaaS APIs
(
87
)
. A prominent enhancement in this version was using the Matryoshka representation learning technique
(
61
)
, providing the ability to slice the initial dimensions of embeddings (essentially another form of dimension reduction). Our preliminary idea of hyperdimension (Section
