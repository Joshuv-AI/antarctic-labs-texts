Abstract
. We prove that the key property in models of Nonlinear Elasticity which corresponds to the non-interpenetration of matter, i.e. injectivity a.e., can be achieved in the class of weak limits of homeomorphisms under very minimal assumptions. Let Ω ⊆ ℝ n \Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n} be a domain and let p > ⌊ n 2 ⌋ p>\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor for n ≥ 4 n\geq 4 or p ≥ 1 p\geq 1 for n = 2 , 3 n=2,3 . Assume that f k ∈ W 1 , p f_{k}\in W^{1,p} is a sequence of homeomorphisms such that f k ⇀ f f_{k}\rightharpoonup f weakly in W 1 , p W^{1,p} and assume that J f > 0 J_{f}>0 a.e. Then we show that f f is injective a.e.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we study classes of mappings that might serve as deformations in models of Nonlinear Elasticity. Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a domain that corresponds to our body and let
f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a mapping that describes the deformation of the body.
Following the pioneering papers of Ball
[
1
]
and Ciarlet and Nečas
[
5
]
we ask if our mapping is in some sense injective as the physical “non-interpenetration of matter” asks a deformation to be one-to-one.

Let us recall some known results about the injectivity, or at least injectivity almost everywhere of the mapping
f
.
Following Ball
[
1
]
we can require that the energy functional
\int_{\Omega}W(Df)
contains special terms (like ratio of powers of
Df
,
\operatorname{adj}Df
and
J_{f}
)
and any mapping with finite energy and reasonable boundary data is a homeomorphism (the reader is referred to e.g.
[
19
,
25
]
and
[
26
]
for related results).

In the results like Ball
[
1
]
, we need to know that our mapping is continuous everywhere, but in some real-world deformations cavitation or even fractures may occur. To allow such a behavior in our models we do not require injectivity of our mapping, but only injectivity almost everywhere.
Ciarlet and Nečas
[
5
]
studied the class of mappings that satisfy

(1.1)

\int_{\Omega}J_{f}\leq|f(\Omega)|

together with
J_{f}>0
a.e. and they showed that the mappings from this class are injective a.e. in the image, see e.g.
[
2
,
3
,
22
,
24
]
for further results in this direction.
The inequality (
1.1
) is nowadays called the Ciarlet–Nečas condition.
The assumption
J_{f}>0
a.e. is very natural in models of Nonlinear Elasticity as the “real deformation” cannot change its orientation and we cannot compress our material too much, so the set
\{J_{f}=0\}
must be small.

Another approach can be traced to Müller and Spector
[
20
]
, where they studied a class of mappings that satisfy
J_{f}>0
a.e. together with the (INV) condition (see e.g.
[
3
,
6
,
13
,
21
]
).
Besides many other interesting results they showed that mappings in their class are injective a.e. Informally speaking, the (INV) condition means that the ball
B(x,r)
is mapped inside the image of the sphere
f(S(a,r))
and the complement
\Omega\setminus\overline{B(x,r)}
is mapped outside of
f(S(a,r))
.

In this paper we study injectivity a.e. in the class of weak limits of Sobolev homeomorphisms. The natural discontinuous mappings like cavitation belong to this class and this class (with uniformly bounded
\int_{\Omega}|Df|^{p}
) is weakly closed, which makes it a suitable class for the approach of Calculus of Variations (see e.g.
[
8
]
and
[
9
]
).
Let us note that the class of weak limits of Sobolev homeomorphisms was recently characterized in the planar case by Iwaniec and Onninen
[
17
,
18
]
and De Philippis and Pratelli
[
7
]
(see also Campbell
[
4
]
).

Let us recall that weak limits of homeomorphisms in
W^{1,p}
,
p>n-1
, with
J_{f}>0
a.e. are injective a.e., as they obviously satisfy the (INV) condition, the (INV) condition is stable under weak convergence and (INV) mappings with
J_{f}>0
a.e. are injective a.e. (see
[
20
]
).
In the recent paper, Bouchala, Hencl and Molchanova proved that
[
3
, Theorem 1.1]
there is an example of a continuous mapping which is even a strong limit of
W^{1,n-1}
homeomorphisms,
n\geq 3
, such that there is a set of positive measure
|K|>0
so that
|f^{-1}(K)|>0
and
f^{-1}(y)
is a continuum for every
y\in K
. However, this example does not contradict injectivity a.e. as one can divide each
f^{-1}(y)
into the “endpoint”
e_{y}
and the rest
f^{-1}(y)\setminus\{e_{y}\}
and the set

\bigcup_{y\in K}f^{-1}(y)\setminus\{e_{y}\}

has zero measure, so our map is indeed injective outside of this set. Another pathological example of weak limits of
W^{1,n-1}
homeomorphisms can be found in Conti and De Lellis
[
6
, Theorem 6.3]
(see also
[
8
, Theorem 1.2]
), where the limit mapping can change orientation on some set and it can fail the (INV) condition (and thus it maps something inside of a ball to outside of the topological image of the corresponding sphere), but it is still injective a.e.
We wanted to know if this is a general phenomenon and if each weak limit of
W^{1,n-1}
homeomorphisms must always be injective a.e. Somewhat surprisingly we can prove such a positive result under much weaker Sobolev regularity.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
,
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a domain and let
p>\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
for
n\geq 4
or
p\geq 1
for
n=2,3
. Let
f_{k}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
be a sequence of homeomorphism such that
f_{k}\rightharpoonup f
weakly in
W^{1,p}
and assume that
J_{f}>0
a.e.

Then
f
is injective a.e., i.e. there is a set
N\subseteq\Omega
with
|N|=0
such that
f|_{\Omega\setminus N}
is injective.

Let us note that the assumption
J_{f}>0
a.e. is necessary as without it we would be e.g. able to squash some ball into a point and injectivity a.e. would obviously fail.
To prove this result under such a minimal regularity assumption we use the topological notion of the linking number, which was previously used in the context of Sobolev homeomorphisms and their limits in
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
and
[
12
]
.
We would like to know if the assumption
p>\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
is sharp and if there are counterexamples to injectivity a.e. below this regularity.

Let us briefly explain the main idea of the proof in the case
n=3
, which is nicely captured in Fig.
5
below. For the contradiction, we assume that there are two sets
A
and
B
of positive measure that are mapped onto the same set. Without the loss of generality, we assume that all points of
A\cup B
are points of approximative differentiability and Lebesgue points of the derivative. It follows that we can find a green square curve (see Fig.
5
) whose big part lies in
A
and it is mapped to almost the same shape with only some small wiggles on the boundary. Similarly, we find a red square curve (mainly inside of
B
) which is mapped to a similar shape with only small wiggles. Original green and red squares curves are far away and they are not linked (see formal definition of the linking number and Fig.
1
in Preliminaries) but as
A
and
B
are mapped to the same place we obtain that images of red and green square curves are linked, i.e. the red curve in the image is passing through the hole of the green curve. Since
f_{k}
converge to
f
weakly in
W^{1,p}
for
p\geq 1
, we obtain that either
f_{k}
converge uniformly for
p>1
or
Df_{k}
are uniformly integrable for
p=1
on those carefully chosen 1D green and red square curves and hence similar picture is true also for some
f_{k}
for
k
high enough. However, this gives us a contradiction as each homeomorphism preserves the linking number, i.e. it cannot map two curves that are not linked to two curves that are linked.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By
\left\lfloor\tfrac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
we denote the floor of
\tfrac{n}{2}
, that is

\left\lfloor\tfrac{n}{2}\right\rfloor:=\max\left\{m\in\mathbb{Z}:m\leq\tfrac{n}{2}\right\}.

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We denote by
Q(a,r)
the cube with the center
a
and the side length
r
, that is

Q(a,r):=(a_{1}-\tfrac{r}{2},a_{1}+\tfrac{r}{2})\times\cdots\times(a_{n}-\tfrac{r}{2},a_{n}+\tfrac{r}{2}).

By
B(a,r)
we denote the open ball with the center
a
and the radius
r
. We use
\operatorname{Id}
for the identity matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
will be denoted by
|\,\raisebox{1.49994pt}{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}\,|
. For two points
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and
y=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
we define

\|x-y\|_{\infty}\colon=\max\left\{|x_{i}-y_{i}|:i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\right\}.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
f\in W^{1,1}_{\rm loc}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
be a mapping. We say that
f
satisfies the Lusin (
N
) condition on the measurable set
F\subseteq\Omega
if for every set
E\subseteq F
with
|E|=0
we have
|f(E)|=0
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a mapping. We say that
f
is injective almost everywhere on
\Omega
, if there exists a subset
N\subseteq\Omega
,
|N|=0
such that for every
x_{1},x_{2}\in\Omega\setminus N
with
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
, we have
f(x_{1})=f(x_{2})
.

For a mapping
f\in W^{1,1}_{\rm loc}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the ACL-characterization implies the differential matrix
Df(x)
exists for almost every
x\in\Omega
. The following lemma can be found as a special case in
[
10
, Theorem 6.2]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
f\in W^{1,1}_{\rm loc}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
be a mapping. Then for almost every
x\in\Omega
, we have

(2.1)

\lim_{r\to 0}\mathop{}\mkern-3.0mu\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox to5.83331pt{\hss$\textstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\vbox{\hbox to5.83331pt{\hss$\scriptstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\vbox{\hbox to5.1215pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\vbox{\hbox to4.82639pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}\mkern-3.0mu\int_{B(x,r)\cap\Omega}\left|\frac{f(y)-f(x)-Df(x)\cdot(y-x)}{r}\right|dy=0.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a domain and
f\colon\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a continuous mapping which is smooth in
\Omega
. Let
J_{f}(x)\neq 0
for each
x\in\Omega\cap f^{-1}(y)
. Then we define the topological degree of
f
by setting

{\rm deg}(f,\Omega,y):=\sum_{x\in\Omega\cap f^{-1}(y)}{\rm sgn}(J_{f}(x)).

With an approximation argument, this definition can be extended to an arbitrary continuous mapping from
\overline{\Omega}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We should emphasize that the degree only depends on values of
f
on
\partial\Omega
, see
[
11
]
for more details. For homeomorphism
f\colon\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, either
{\rm deg}(f,\Omega,y)=1
for all
y\in f(\Omega)
(we say that
f
is sense preserving), or
{\rm deg}(f,\Omega,y)=-1
for all
y\in f(\Omega)
(so
f
is sense reversing).

2.1.
Linking number

Let us consider two simple closed curves in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The linking number of this pair describes the number of times that each curve winds around the other (considering orientation), see Fig.
1
. It is well known that the linking number is topologically invariant (see e.g.
[
15
]
). For the introductions to the linking number in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and its application to the theory of knots see
[
23
]
, and for the applications in the theory of Sobolev mappings see
[
15
]
and
[
16
]
.

We need only the fact that if you have two circles (or boundaries of squares) that are not linked (for example if the second circle does not intersect the disc whose boundary is the first circle), then their images by a homeomorphism also cannot be linked.

Figure 1.
Curves with linking numbers 0, 1 and 2

The linking number can be defined even in the
n
-dimensional case. We now give the formal definition.
Let
n,p
and
q
be positive integers with
p+q=n-1
. Let us consider the
mapping

\Phi(\xi,\eta)\colon\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{p+1}\times\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{q+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

defined coordinate-wise as
\Phi(\xi,\eta)=x
, where

\begin{array}[]{rcl}x_{1}&=&(2+\eta_{1})\xi_{1},\\
&\vdots&\\
x_{p+1}&=&(2+\eta_{1})\xi_{p+1},\\
x_{p+2}&=&\eta_{2},\\
&\vdots&\\
x_{p+q+1}&=&\eta_{q+1}.\end{array}

Denote by
\mathbb{A}
the anuloid

\Phi(\mathbb{S}_{p}\times\mathbb{B}_{q+1})=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\left(\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{p+1}^{2}}-2\right)^{2}+x_{p+2}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2}<1\right\}.

Of course given
x\in\overline{\mathbb{A}}
we can find a unique
\xi\in\mathbb{S}_{p}
and
\eta\in\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{q+1}
such that

\Phi(\xi,\eta)=x
. We will denote these as
\xi(x)
and
\eta(x)
.

A
link
is a pair
(\varphi,\psi)
of parametrized surfaces

\varphi\colon\mathbb{S}_{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\psi\colon\mathbb{S}_{q}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The
linking number
of the link
(\varphi,\psi)
is
defined as the topological degree

\operatorname{\pounds}(\varphi,\psi)=\deg(L,\mathbb{A},0),

where the mapping

L=L_{\varphi,\psi}\colon\overline{\mathbb{A}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
defined as
L(x)=\varphi(\xi(x))-\bar{\psi}(-\eta(x))
, or equivalently

L(\Phi(\xi,\eta))=\varphi(\xi)-\bar{\psi}(-\eta)\text{ for }\xi\in\mathbb{S}_{p},\eta\in\mathbb{B}_{q+1},

where
\bar{\psi}
is an arbitrary continuous extension of
\psi
to

\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{q+1}
(the degree does not depend on the way how we
extend
\psi
as it depends only on the values on the boundary
\partial\mathbb{A}=\Phi(\mathbb{S}_{p}\times\mathbb{S}_{q})
).

Proof of Theorem

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f\in W^{1,1}_{\rm loc}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
be a mapping with
J_{f}>0
a.e. that is not injective almost everywhere, i.e. there is no set
N
with
|N|=0
so that
f
is injective on
\Omega\setminus N
. Then there exist two measurable subsets
A,B\subseteq\Omega
with
|A|>0
,
|B|>0
,
A\cap B=\emptyset
and
f(A)=f(B)
.

Furthermore, if we take any
E\subseteq A
(resp.
E\subseteq B
),
|E|>0
, then there exists a set
F\subseteq B
(resp.
F\subseteq A
) such that
|F|>0
and
f(E)=f(F)
.

Proof.

(1)

Let
\{Q_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be an open cover of
\Omega
, such that every
Q_{k}
is bounded and for any two points
x\neq y
we have a set
Q_{i}
such that
y\notin Q_{i}\ni x
. Such a cover clearly exists as we can imagine for example the system of dyadic cubes of all sizes (together with slightly shifted dyadic cubes to also cover their boundaries).

(2)

We claim that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
there is a set
N_{k}
,
|N_{k}|=0
, such that

f^{-1}(f(Q_{k}\setminus N_{k}))\text{ is measurable.}

Indeed, let
k
be fixed. Using the Lusin theorem, for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we can find a compact set
C_{m}\subseteq Q_{k}
such that
f|_{C_{m}}
is continuous and
|Q_{k}\setminus C_{m}|<\frac{1}{m}
. Define
N_{k}:=Q_{k}\setminus\bigcup_{m=1}^{\infty}C_{m}
. We have
|N_{k}|=0
.
Clearly
\{f(C_{m})\}
are compact sets, and therefore

\bigcup_{m=1}^{\infty}f(C_{m})=f\left(\bigcup_{m=1}^{\infty}C_{m}\right)=f(Q_{k}\setminus N_{k})

is Borel (
F_{\sigma}
). Therefore the set
f^{-1}(f(Q_{k}\setminus N_{k}))
is measurable as a preimage of Borel set under the measurable mapping.

(3)

Since
f
is Sobolev, there is a null set
N\subseteq\Omega
such that
f
satisfies the Lusin (
N
) condition on
\Omega\setminus N
, see e.g.
[
14
, Section A.8]
. We may assume that
N\cap Q_{k}\subseteq N_{k}
, and so the Lusin (
N
) condition holds on
\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}N_{k}
.

(4)

We claim that there is
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, such that

M_{k_{0}}:=f^{-1}(f(Q_{k_{0}}\setminus N_{k_{0}}))\setminus Q_{k_{0}}\text{ satisfies }|M_{k_{0}}|>0.

Let us assume for contradiction that
|M_{k}|=0
for every
k
. Since
f
is not injective a.e., we know that
f
is not injective on the set of full measure

\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}(N_{k}\cup M_{k}).

So there must be two different points
x\neq y
in this set such that
f(x)=f(y)
. We find
i\in\mathbb{N}
such that
x\in Q_{i}
and
y\notin Q_{i}
. Since
x\notin N_{i}
, we know that
y\in f^{-1}(f(Q_{i}\setminus N_{i}))
. And from
y\notin Q_{i}
it follows that
y\in M_{i}
, which is a contradiction with
y\in\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}(N_{k}\cup M_{k})
.

(5)

The construction of the sets
A
and
B
.

By making use of Lusin’s theorem again, we can fix a compact set
A\subseteq M_{k_{0}}
with
{|A|>\frac{1}{2}|M_{k_{0}}|>0}
, such that
f
is continuous on
A
,
f(A)
is compact and

f^{-1}(f(A))
is measurable. We define

B:=f^{-1}(f(A))\cap(Q_{k_{0}}\setminus N_{k_{0}}).

Clearly,
B\subseteq Q_{k_{0}}
,
A\cap Q_{k_{0}}=\emptyset
, and every point in
f(A)
has at least two points in its preimage, one in
A
and one in
B
, so
f(A)=f(B)
.

Since
|A|>0
and
J_{f}>0
a.e.,
|f(A)|>0
. Because of step
(3)
, we know that
f
satisfies the Lusin (
N
) condition on
B
. Therefore
|f(A)|=|f(B)|>0
implies
|B|>0
.

(6)

“Furthermore”

Let
E\subseteq A
be measurable with
|E|>0
(the case
E\subseteq B
is similar). Similarly to the previous step, we fix
F\subseteq B
as

F:=f^{-1}(f(E))\cap B.

As in step (2) we can find a set
N_{E}
,
|N_{E}|=0
such that
f(E\setminus N_{E})
is Borel. Because of the Lusin (
N
) condition, we know that
|f(N_{E})|=0
. It can be easily checked that
f(E)=f(F)
. And as before, using the positivity of
J_{f}
a.e. and the Lusin (
N
) condition we can obtain that
|F|>0
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
for
n=3
.

For simplicity, we give the details of the proof only for
n=3
and we explain how to proceed in general dimensions in the last step.
The proof is divided into several steps. It is by contradiction, so let us assume that the statement does not hold.

Step 1:

Finding disjoint sets
A
and
B
that are mapped onto each other.

By Lemma
3.1
there exist two measurable subsets
A,B\subseteq\Omega
such that

A\cap B=\emptyset,|A|>0,|B|>0,|f(A)|>0\text{ and }f(A)=f(B)

and

(3.1)

\displaystyle\text{for every }E\subseteq A,|E|>0,\text{ there is a set }F\subseteq B\text{ with }|F|>0\text{ and }f(E)=f(F),

\displaystyle\text{for every }F\subseteq A,|F|>0,\text{ there is a set }E\subseteq B\text{ with }|E|>0\text{ and }f(E)=f(F).

Step 2a:

Making
A
and
B
smaller with better properties: approximate differentiability and Lebesgue points.

Since
f\in W^{1,p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
p\geq 1
,
|Df(x)|<\infty
for almost every
x\in A\cup B
. And we know that
f
is almost everywhere approximately differentiable by Lemma
2.2
. So (by making the sets smaller, but maintaining their positive measures) we can without the loss of generality assume that every point
x\in A\cup B
satisfies (
2.1
),
|Df(x)|<\infty
and
J_{f}(x)>0
(because
J_{f}>0
a.e.) and every point
x\in A\cup B
is a Lebesgue point of
Df
.

Step 2b:

Making
A
and
B
smaller with better properties: almost constant derivative.

We define
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Q}}
to be the class of all
3\times 3
matrices with positive determinant and rational entries, i.e.

(3.2)

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Q}}:=\left\{M=(a_{i,j})_{i,j=1,2,3}:\det(M)>0,\ a_{i,j}\in\mathbb{Q}\right\}.

Let
\delta_{\mathrm{2b}}>0
be a sufficiently small constant to be fixed later.
The Jacobian of
f
is positive everywhere on
A
, so we can decompose
A
as

A=\bigcup_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Q}}}\underbrace{\left\{x\in A:|Df(x)-M|\leq\delta_{\mathrm{2b}}|M|\right\}}_{=:A^{M}}.

As
|A|>0
, we know that at least one of those countable many measurable sets needs to have a positive measure and hence there exists a matrix
M_{A}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Q}}
such that
\left|A^{M_{A}}\right|>0
. Using (
3.1
) we can find the corresponding
F
such that
F\subseteq B
,
|F|>0
and
f(A^{M_{A}})=f(F)
.

Therefore we can assume that for all
x\in A
we have

(3.3)

\displaystyle|Df(x)-M_{A}|

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{\mathrm{2b}}|M_{A}|.

We can do the same trick for
B
, so for every
x\in B
we can have

(3.4)

\displaystyle|Df(x)-M_{B}|

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{\mathrm{2b}}|M_{B}|.

Furthermore, we can assume that every point of
A
and
B
is a point of density for the respective set.

Step 2c:

Making
A
and
B
smaller with better properties and “improving”
f
:
Df(x)\approx\operatorname{Id}
.

Let us fix two points
a\in A
and
b\in B
such that
f(a)=f(b)
. We can without the loss of generality assume that
f(a)=f(b)=0
,
\dist(a,b)>2
and
a=0
.

Let us define

r_{a}:=\frac{\min\left\{\dist(a,b),\dist(a,\partial\Omega)\right\}}{3\max\left\{|M_{A}^{-1}|,1\right\}}\text{ and }r_{b}:=\frac{\min\left\{\dist(a,b),\dist(b,\partial\Omega)\right\}}{3\max\left\{|M_{B}^{-1}|,1\right\}}.

Since
a
is a point of density of
A
, we know that
|A\cap B(a,r_{a})|>0
. Similarly, since
b
is a point of density of
B
, we know that
|B\cap B(b,r_{b})|>0
. Therefore, using (

), we can assume that

(3.5)

a\in A\subseteq B(a,r_{a})\text{ and }b\in B\subseteq B(b,r_{b}).

Note that
\dist(A,B)>\frac{1}{3}\dist(a,b)>0

Let

onto itself such that

The compositions

) and (

By possibly slightly modifying

and
M_{B}
in this argument, we may assume that
Df(a)=Df(b)=\operatorname{Id}
. Note that still
f(a)=f(b)=0
.

We know that
f_{k}\rightharpoonup f
in
W^{1,p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
, hence
f_{k}\to f
in

, and we can assume that

(3.6)

f_{k}(x)\to f(x)\text{ for every }x\in A\cup B

(a subsequence of
f_{k}
converges pointwise almost everywhere, and we can make the sets
A
and

Let us fix
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{2c}}>0
. Since

is approximately differentiable at

) there exist

\displaystyle\mathop{}\mkern-3.0mu\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox to5.83331pt{\hss$\textstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\vbox{\hbox to5.83331pt{\hss$\scriptstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\vbox{\hbox to5.1215pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\vbox{\hbox to4.82639pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}\mkern-3.0mu\int_{Q(a,r)}\left|f(x)-f(a)-Df(a)(x-a)\right|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle<r\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{2c}},

\displaystyle\mathop{}\mkern-3.0mu\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox to5.83331pt{\hss$\textstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\vbox{\hbox to5.83331pt{\hss$\scriptstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\vbox{\hbox to5.1215pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\vbox{\hbox to4.82639pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}\mkern-3.0mu\int_{Q(b,r)}\left|f(x)-f(b)-Df(b)(x-b)\right|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle<r\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{2c}}.

Since
a
and
b
are Lebesgue points of
Df
, there exists
r_{2}>0
sufficiently small such that for every
0<r<r_{2}
, we have (remember that
Df(a)=Df(b)=\operatorname{Id}
)

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mathop{}\mkern-3.0mu\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox to5.83331pt{\hss$\textstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\vbox{\hbox to5.83331pt{\hss$\scriptstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\vbox{\hbox to5.1215pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\vbox{\hbox to4.82639pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}\mkern-3.0mu\int_{Q(a,r)}\left|Df(x)-\operatorname{Id}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle<\varepsilon_{\mathrm{2c}},

\displaystyle\mathop{}\mkern-3.0mu\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox to5.83331pt{\hss$\textstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\vbox{\hbox to5.83331pt{\hss$\scriptstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\vbox{\hbox to5.1215pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\vbox{\hbox to4.82639pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}\mkern-3.0mu\int_{Q(b,r)}\left|Df(x)-\operatorname{Id}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle<\varepsilon_{\mathrm{2c}}.

The points
a
and
b
are the points of density of
A\cup B
(or they can be if we make the sets smaller). Therefore there is
r_{3}>0
such that for every

it holds that

(3.9)

\displaystyle\left|Q(a,r)\setminus A\right|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{2c}}\left|Q(a,r)\right|,

\displaystyle\left|Q(b,r)\setminus B\right|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{2c}}\left|Q(b,r)\right|.

Let us fix
0<r<\min\{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3},r_{a},r_{b}\}
. There exists a bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism
R_{1}\colon\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
which maps

,

for every

.
And let us define the mapping

R_{2}(x):=\frac{x}{r},

so
DR_{2}(x)=\frac{1}{r}\operatorname{Id}
.

Now consider the compositions
R_{2}\circ f\circ R_{1}
and
R_{2}\circ f_{k}\circ R_{1}
. It can be easily checked that all of the previous nice properties are preserved, and so we can assume that (

,

and

)

(3.10)

\displaystyle\int_{Q_{a}}\left|f(x)-(x-a)\right|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle<\varepsilon_{\mathrm{2c}},

\displaystyle\int_{Q_{b}}\left|f(x)-(x-b)\right|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle<\varepsilon_{\mathrm{2c}}.

Step 3a:

Construction of a closed curve in
Q_{a}
where
f
is almost identity.

We define

U_{A}:=\left\{x\in Q_{a}:|f(x)-(x-a)|\geq\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{2c}}}\right\}.

Thanks to (
3.10
) we have
\left|U_{A}\right|\leq\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{2c}}}.
We know that
|Q_{a}\setminus A|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{2c}}
(see (
3.9
)). By removing
U_{A}
from
A
, we may without the loss of generality assume that
A\cap U_{A}=\emptyset
,
A\subseteq Q_{a}
and

(3.11)

|A|>1-(\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{2c}}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{2c}}).

Let us assume that
a=0
. We define the annular type set
\mathbf{L}_{A}\subseteq Q_{a}
by setting (see Fig.
2

4
)

The set

Then

.
For every

and
r\in\left(\frac{3}{18},\frac{5}{18}\right)
we can define a closed simple curve

Then
\mathcal{H}^{1}(L_{x_{2},r})=8r\in\left(\frac{4}{3},\frac{20}{9}\right)

is a disjoint union of:

The ACL-characterization implies that for almost every
(x_{2},r)\in\left(\frac{-1}{18},\frac{1}{18}\right)\times\left(\frac{3}{18},\frac{5}{18}\right)
the restrictions of
f_{k}
and

.

and

Since
f_{k}\rightharpoonup f
in

on

(where

for a.e.
(x_{2},r)
there is a subsequence of

which converges weakly to

in

, see

. We therefore assume that on a.e. curve
f_{k}\rightharpoonup f
in
W^{1,p}(L_{x_{2},r})

The inequality (
3.8
) implies that for almost every
(x_{2},r)\in\left(\frac{-1}{18},\frac{1}{18}\right)\times\left(\frac{3}{18},\frac{5}{18}\right)
we have that

(3.12)

\int_{L_{x_{2},r}}\left|Df(x)-\operatorname{Id}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x<\varepsilon_{\mathrm{2c}}.

Because of (
3.11
), the Fubini theorem implies that again for almost every

we have

(3.13)

\mathcal{H}^{1}(L_{x_{2},r}\setminus A)<\varepsilon_{3},

.

We now fix
(x_{2},r)
which satisfies the previous conditions. From now on we assume that

. Since
p>1

on the one-dimensional space on

is compactly embedded into continuous functions and hence we can find a subsequence of

(still denoted as

) that converges to

.
Let us fix

, such that for

we have

for all

.

Let

. Then

, and therefore

(3.14)

|f_{k}(x)-x|\leq|f_{k}(x)-f(x)|+|f(x)-x|<\varepsilon_{\mathrm{2c}}+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{2c}}}.

Let
x\in L_{x_{2},r}\setminus A
. Since (
3.13
) holds, we know that there must be

such that
|x-y|<\frac{\varepsilon_{3}}{2}
. Then we can estimate (since

is ACL on

))

\displaystyle|f_{k}(x)-x|

\displaystyle\leq|f_{k}(x)-f(x)|+|f(x)-f(y)|+|f(y)-y|+|y-x|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{2c}}+\Bigl(\int_{L_{x_{2},r}\setminus A}|Df(t)|\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigr)+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{2c}}}+\varepsilon_{3}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{2c}}+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{2c}}}+\varepsilon_{3}+\int_{L_{x_{2},r}\setminus A}\bigl(|Df(t)-\operatorname{Id}|+|\operatorname{Id}|\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t

Step 5:

The missing case
p=1
.
In the case
p=1
we have to slightly adjust our argument, as we may not have uniform convergence of
f_{k}
on
L_{x_{2},r}
.
We know that
Df_{k}
converge weakly to
Df
in
L^{1}
, so by the de la Vallée Poussin characterization of weak convergence we obtain that
Df_{k}
are uniformly integrable, i.e.

(3.18)

\text{ for any }\varepsilon>0\,\text{ there is }\delta>0\,\text{ so that for any }k\in\mathbb{N}\colon|A|<\delta\Rightarrow\int_{A}|Df_{k}|<\varepsilon.

As in the previous part of the proof, we know that
f
behaves on
L_{x_{2},r}
like in Fig
5
, so the mapping
f
is really close to the identity on
L_{x_{2},r}
. We fix
\delta>0
and we study the “ugly” set

U:=\{x\in\Omega:\ |f_{k}(x)-f(x)|\geq\tfrac{1}{1000}\}.

Since
f_{k}
converge to
f
pointwise a.e., we can find
k_{0}
big enough so that for
k\geq k_{0}
we have

|\mathbf{L}_{A}\cap U|<\delta\,|\mathbf{L}_{A}|.

Now there are two possibilities. If

(3.19)

\int_{U\cap L_{x_{2},r}}|Df_{k}|<\frac{1}{1000},

then we know that
f_{k}
is close to
f
on
L_{x_{2},r}\setminus U
by the definition of
U
. By (
3.19
) it cannot go far away on
U\cap L_{x_{2},r}
so it is close to
f
on the whole
L_{x_{2},r}
.
It follows that the situation is as in Fig.
5
also for
f_{k}
, i.e.
f_{k}
is really close to the identity mapping on
L_{x_{2},r}
. If the same thing happens also on
\widehat{L}_{x_{3},r}
, i.e. if the analogy of (
3.19
) holds also there, we get that
f_{k}
is close to
f
also on
\widehat{L}_{x_{3},r}
and we obtain the desired contradiction with the stability of linking in the same way as in previous step – see Fig.
5
.

So we may assume that (
3.19
) fails either for
L_{x_{2},r}
or for
\widehat{L}_{x_{3},r}
. Without the loss of generality, we assume that it fails for
L_{x_{2},r}
. And we may assume that it fails for any square

where our other conditions are satisfied and those squares occupy a big portion of
\mathbf{L}_{A}
, so we obtain that

\int_{U\cap\mathbf{L}_{A}}|Df_{k}|\geq C\text{\ \ but\ \ }|\mathbf{L}_{A}\cap U|<\delta|\mathbf{L}_{A}|,

which gives us a contradition with uniform integrability of
|Df_{k}|
once we choose
\delta
sufficiently small.

Step 6:

Proof for the case when
n=2
or
n>3
.

When
n=2
, De Philippis and Pratelli
[
7
, Lemma 2.6]
proved that if
\{f_{k}\}\subseteq W^{1,p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2})
is a sequence of mappings which satisfy the (INV) condition for
1\leq p<\infty
and they converge weakly to
f\in W^{1,p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2})
with
J_{f}>0
almost everywhere, then
f
also satisfies the (INV) condition. Since homeomorphisms must satisfy the (INV) condition, the Theorem
1.1
directly follows from their result.

When
n>3
, we can use a similar idea as in the proof for
n=3
. The only difference is the definition of the surfaces
L_{x_{2},r}
and
\widehat{L}_{x_{3},r}
. In the case
n=3
, we had two curves – one-dimensional surfaces. In the case of
n>3
,
n
odd we need two
(\frac{n-1}{2})
-dimensional surfaces. In the case of
n
even we need one
(\frac{n}{2}-1)
-dimensional surface and one
(\frac{n}{2})
-dimensional surface. The rest of the proof is the same, since in either case, we have that the dimension of either surface is not higher than
\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor<p
, and therefore
W^{1,p}
is continuously embedded into continuous functions on those sets and we can use the same argument as in the case
n=3
.
∎
