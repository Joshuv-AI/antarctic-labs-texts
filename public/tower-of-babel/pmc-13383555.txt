Efficacy of vibration therapy on physical function, activity and participation in people with stroke: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Yage Wang, JingLan Wang, Beibei Lei, Di Zhang, Shenhong Ma, Heling Wang, Qiaohua Han, Weisheng Zhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the effectiveness of vibration therapy (VT) in improving physical function, activity, and participation in people with stroke.MethodsWe searched six databases for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) investigating VT in people with stroke. The quality of included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool, and data were analyzed using RevMan and Stata. The quality of evidence was evaluated with the GRADEpro tool.ResultsThirty-seven RCTs involving 1492 people with stroke were included. VT significantly improved physical function, including motor (SMD = 0.46, 95% CI 0.20–0.73), spasticity (SMD = − 0.64, 95% CI − 0.99 to − 0.29), balance (SMD = 0.52, 95% CI 0.19–0.85), and gait (SMD = − 0.41, 95% CI − 0.66 to − 0.16). Activity was also enhanced (SMD = 0.21, 95% CI 0.06–0.35), whereas participation did not show improvement (SMD = 0.01, 95% CI − 0.33 to 0.35). Subgroup analysis and meta-regression revealed that spasticity improvement was superior in people with chronic stroke (SMD = − 0.8, 95% CI − 1.19 to − 0.4). Single-session vibration time ≥ 20 min for gait (SMD = − 0.84, 95% CI − 1.36 to − 0.32), cumulative vibration time > 5 h for balance (SMD = 0.86, 95% CI 0.30–1.43), and intervention duration ≥ 4 weeks for spasticity (SMD = − 0.5, 95% CI − 0.81 to − 0.2) yielded pronounced improvements.ConclusionsLow to moderate quality evidence suggests that VT effectively enhances physical function and activity in people with stroke, while its impact on participation remains unclear. Moreover, VT produced superior spasticity relief in people with chronic stroke compared to those in acute and subacute phases; single-session vibration time ≥ 20 min for gait, cumulative vibration time > 5 h for balance, and intervention duration ≥ 4 weeks for spasticity were particularly effective.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-026-02016-4.Keywords: Stroke, Whole-body vibration, Focal muscle vibration, Physical function, Activity and participation

Introduction

Stroke is a sudden cerebrovascular event commonly resulting in neurological impairment; in severe cases, it can lead to disability or even death [1]. More than 80% of stroke survivors have hemiplegia, and this renders them unable to perform activities of daily living normally [2]. The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Report indicates that between 1990 and 2021, stroke-related disability cases increased by 32%, with an annual economic burden exceeding $890 billion. Economic costs are expected to double by 2050 [3]. In accordance with the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF), stroke may lead to multidimensional functional impairments, including physical function (e.g., spasticity, balance deficits), activity and participation [4]. These impairments severely impact people’s independent living; thus, effective rehabilitation interventions to restore their functions are crucial for improving prognosis [5].

In the past few years, vibration therapy (VT) has drawn significant interest as a secure, non-pharmacological therapeutic option [6]. It relies on mechanical vibration to produce physiological responses [7]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that VT can induce sensorimotor cortical reorganization in healthy individuals [8–10]. It plays a positive role in the rehabilitation of neurological disorders such as stroke and multiple sclerosis by enhancing reflex activity, muscle strength, and balance [11–13].

However, prior meta-analyses have largely concentrated on the efficacy of a single VT subtype (e.g., whole-body vibration [WBV] or focal muscle vibration [FMV]) for specific functional impairments in people with stroke, lacking comprehensive assessments from a holistic rehabilitation perspective, with inconsistent conclusions and variable quality of evidence. In addition, the lack of standardized clinical protocols for VT prevents it from achieving its full therapeutic effect. Accordingly, this study aimed to: (1) based on the ICF, systematically evaluate for the first time the effectiveness of VT in improving physical function (motor, spasticity, balance, gait), activity and participation in people with stroke, using GRADE grading to assess the quality of evidence and enhance the reliability and transparency of the study findings; (2) conduct pre-specified analyses of eight influencing factors (stratified by participant characteristics and intervention protocols) to further explore potential moderators of VT efficacy, thereby providing evidence-based support for optimizing clinical VT protocols.

Methods

Agreement and registration

This study strictly followed the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews. A predefined protocol was established prior to the literature search. However, due to administrative procedures associated with document preparation, its registration on the PROSPERO platform (CRD420251268067) was completed on December 17, 2025, after the search had concluded. We acknowledge this time discrepancy and have addressed it as a limitation of our study.

Search strategy

Six databases were searched: PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, EBSCO, and the Physiotherapy Evidence Database (PEDro). The search covered the period from database inception to December 10, 2025. Supplementary searches of relevant meta-analyses were performed to minimize literature omission. Two researchers jointly performed literature searches; disagreements were settled through the intervention of an impartial third-party researcher. The core search strategy was (“Stroke” OR “Cerebrovascular Accident” OR “Brain Vascular Accidents” OR “Acute Cerebrovascular Accident”) AND (“Vibration”) AND (“randomized controlled trial” OR “randomized” OR “placebo”). Details in Additional file 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria, strictly adhering to the PICOS (population, intervention, comparison, outcome, study design) principles, were as follows: P: stroke (any type or duration); I: VT, including WBV or FMV; C: sham VT, conventional rehabilitation, or no intervention; O: outcomes related to physical function, activity and participation (see “Primary outcome measures” section); S: RCTs.

Exclusion criteria: studies not involving people with stroke; studies not using VT as an intervention; studies where the control group also received VT (to ensure all control groups were “non-VT controls”); studies that did not report any outcomes related to physical function, activity, or participation; studies with unavailable full texts or incomplete outcome data; studies that were not RCTs (e.g., conference abstracts, reviews, case reports).

Primary outcome measures

These were classified into three categories based on the ICF: physical function (motor, balance, spasticity, gait); activity; participation. Specific assessment tools included: motor function assessed by the Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA); balance function by the Berg Balance Scale (BBS); spasticity by the Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS); gait function by the Timed Up and Go Test (TUG) and 10-Meter Walk Test (10MWT); activity by the Barthel Index (BI) and the Functional Independence Measure (FIM); participation by the Stroke Impact Scale-36 (SIS-36).

Data extraction

Two researchers employed a pre-designed form to gather relevant information. A third researcher addressed inconsistencies in the extraction process. What they collected included: lead author, release year, trial registry ID or ethics approval number, nation of release, sample size, gender, age, stroke type, disease duration, interventions, adverse events, outcome assessment time. Additional data included VT equipment type, application posture, vibration frequency and amplitude, and control group interventions.

Risk of bias assessment

The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool. Two assessors made this assessment jointly. If they disagreed, a third assessor helped resolve the disagreement.

Data processing and analysis

Data extraction and processing

For each included study, we extracted baseline and post-intervention mean and standard deviation (SD) values for both the experimental and control groups. To ensure comparability across studies, when multiple post-intervention time points were available, we extracted the latest measurement data to reflect the stable state of intervention effects. These extracted data were then used to calculate the change scores (post-intervention minus baseline) for each group. When the study only gave the medians and upper and lower quartiles, means and SDs were estimated using the method described in Cochrane Handbook 6.5.2.9 [14]. For studies with multiple intervention groups, substitution strategies outlined in Sections 6.2.9 and 23.3.4 of the Handbook were applied: Wei et al. [15] and Liao et al. [16] established high-frequency, low-frequency, and control groups, where the two vibration groups were pooled using the formula in Section 6.5.2.10; Choi et al. [17], Dang et al. [18], and Lee et al. [19] included vibration-only, vibration-combined therapy, and control groups, with the control group split across different comparison groups.

Data analysis

All analyses were performed with RevMan 5.4 and Stata 18.0, and evidence quality was assessed with GRADEpro. Due to different measurement scales for outcome measures, to ensure the comparability of results, effect sizes were calculated as standardized mean difference (SMD) with 95% confidence interval (95% CI). Given the considerable heterogeneity in clinical characteristics (e.g., disease type, intervention protocols) and methodological design (e.g., blinding implementation) across studies, and given that I2 statistic indicated substantial statistical heterogeneity, a random-effects model was used to estimate the pooled effect sizes for all outcomes. Publication bias was evaluated using both funnel plot and Egger’s test. To assess the robustness of pooled results, we carried out sensitivity analyses. We explored potential factors influencing treatment effects and sources of heterogeneity through subgroup analyses and meta-regression.

Subgroup analyses and meta-regression

The following prespecified factors were examined: participant characteristics, including age (≤ 60 vs. > 60 years) and disease stage (≤ 6 vs. > 6 months); intervention protocols, including treatment design (monotherapy vs. adjunct therapy), vibration type (WBV vs. FMV), vibration frequency (≤ 30 vs. > 30 Hz), intervention duration (< 4 vs. > 4 weeks), cumulative vibration time (≤ 5 vs. > 5 h), and single-session vibration time (≥ 20 vs. < 20 min).

Classification criteria: age and disease stage are closely associated with differences in neuroplasticity and act as key factors influencing functional prognosis after stroke. In addition, vibration parameters not only directly impact intervention effects but also may contribute to between-study heterogeneity.

Results

Study flow and characteristics

Study flow

From six databases, 608 relevant articles were retrieved. After removing 273 duplicate articles, we further excluded 217 articles by reviewing their titles and abstracts. Following the PICOS, 79 articles were excluded, leaving 37 articles for inclusion in this study [15–51] (Fig. 1).

Literature screening flow diagram

Study characteristics

Table 1 presents the basic characteristics of the included studies. Thirty-seven studies were conducted across 14 countries in four continents, with publication dates ranging from 2006 to 2025. Of these, 18 studies reported either clinical trial registration number or ethics approval number [15, 16, 20–23, 25, 26, 28–30, 32, 33, 35, 39, 43, 45, 50]. Furthermore, the 37 studies included 1492 participants, who had a mean age of 59.2 ± 10.5 years. Four studies examined ischemic stroke [20, 21, 33, 39], one study examined hemorrhagic stroke [22], and the remaining 32 studies included both types [15–19, 23–32, 34–38, 40–51]. Regarding disease duration, 9 studies enrolled people in the acute or subacute phase [20, 23, 32, 35, 37, 48–51], 25 in the chronic phase [15–19, 21, 22, 25–30, 32, 34, 36, 38–44, 46, 47], and 3 did not specify disease duration [24, 33, 45]. Across studies, disease duration ranged from 43 h to 7.71 years.

Additional file 2 presents the detailed vibration protocols of each study. Of the 37 included studies, 19 used VT as monotherapy [16, 19, 20, 22, 29, 31, 32, 34, 38, 39, 41–44, 46–49, 51] and 18 as adjunct therapy [15, 17, 18, 21, 23–28, 30, 33, 35–37, 40, 45, 50]. Regarding vibration type, 19 studies applied WBV [15, 16, 19, 27, 29–32, 35, 37, 40–46, 48, 49], and 18 applied FMV [17, 18, 20–26, 28, 33, 34, 36, 38, 39, 47, 50, 51]. Vibration frequency ranged from 1 to 100 Hz: 20 studies used ≤ 30 Hz [15, 16, 18, 19, 21, 22, 25, 27, 28, 31, 33, 34, 36, 40, 42–44, 46, 48, 49], 13 used > 30 Hz [17, 20, 23, 26, 29, 30, 32, 37–39, 41, 45, 50], and 4 did not report frequency [24, 35, 47, 51]. Amplitude ranged from 0.2 to 7 mm. During intervention, participants were positioned as follows: 20 studies in standing [15, 16, 20, 22, 27, 29–32, 34–36, 40–44, 46, 48, 49], 5 in sitting [18, 19, 37, 39, 51], 5 in supine [21, 23, 24, 26, 50], and 7 did not specify position [17, 25, 28, 33, 38, 45, 47]. Single-session vibration duration ranged from 1 to 30 min. Among the 34 studies with multiple sessions, intervention duration was < 4 weeks in 9 studies [15, 27, 29–31, 35, 37, 45, 50] and ≥ 4 weeks in 25 studies [16–21, 23–26, 28, 33, 34, 36, 38–43, 46–49, 51]; the remaining 3 studies were single-session interventions [22, 32, 44]. Follow-up duration ranged from 9 to 12 weeks.

Risk of bias assessment

Figure 2 displays the risk of bias assessment. Thirty-one studies (84%) presented a low risk in random sequence creation; allocation concealment was implemented in 14 studies (38%) classified as low risk. Only 11 studies (28%) employed participant and researcher blinding, while full blinding of outcome assessors was achieved in 28 studies (76%). All studies demonstrated complete outcome data and minimal reporting bias. Only one study was considered to have other potential sources of bias.

Summary of risk of bias assessments

Effects of VT on physical function

Motor

Fifteen studies assessing FMA were included [18, 19, 23, 24, 28, 32, 38–40, 47, 49–51]. Meta-analysis demonstrated that VT markedly enhanced motor function (SMD = 0.46, 95% CI 0.20–0.73, P = 0.0005, I2 = 52%; Fig. 3). Predefined subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity (Additional file 3). Results indicated that after stratification by disease stage (acute and subacute phases vs. chronic phase) and vibration frequency (≤ 30 Hz vs. > 30 Hz), the heterogeneity within each subgroup was substantially reduced (all I2 < 50%). Thus, disease stage and vibration frequency were identified as key sources of heterogeneity.

Forest plot for the effectiveness of VT on motor function

Spasticity

Fourteen studies employing MAS were included [18, 19, 21, 23, 28, 29, 33, 38, 39, 41, 44, 51]. Meta-analysis indicated that VT significantly attenuated spasticity (SMD = − 0.64, 95% CI − 0.99 to − 0.29, P = 0.0003, I2 = 76%; Fig. 4). Meta-regression showed that intervention duration significantly moderated the effect size (β = 1.23, 95% CI 0.01–2.45, P = 0.049), accounting for approximately 27.4% of the heterogeneity (adjusted R2 = 27.35%) and reducing the residual heterogeneity from 76% (high) to 48.65% (moderate).

Forest plot for the effectiveness of VT on spasticity

Balance

Fifteen studies adopting BBS were included [15, 20, 21, 26, 30, 32, 34, 36, 37, 40, 42, 43, 45, 46, 48]. Meta-analysis revealed that VT produced significant enhancements in balance (SMD = 0.52, 95% CI 0.19–0.85, P = 0.002, I2 = 72%; Fig. 5). Neither subgroup analyses nor meta-regression identified significant sources of heterogeneity. After excluding three studies with extreme effect sizes (SMD > 1.58) in sensitivity analysis, the pooled effect size was markedly attenuated (SMD = 0.24, 95% CI 0.03–0.45), and heterogeneity dropped to a low level (I2 = 29%). This indicates that the high heterogeneity and inflated effect size were driven by these outliers.

Forest plot for the effectiveness of VT on balance

Gait

Gait function was assessed using TUG [16, 22, 26, 27, 30, 32, 34, 36, 40, 44–46] and 10MWT [43, 49]. Pooled analysis indicated that VT had positive effects on gait (SMD = − 0.41, 95% CI − 0.66 to − 0.16, P = 0.001, I2 = 49%; Fig. 6). Among these, the TUG score was significantly improved (SMD = − 0.42, P = 0.003), whereas the 10MWT score did not improve significantly (SMD = − 0.46, P = 0.29). Meta-regression analysis revealed that single-session vibration time emerged as a significant moderator of the effect size (β = 0.68, 95% CI 0.16–1.2, P = 0.015). This variable completely accounted for the heterogeneity among studies (adjusted R2 = 100%; residual I2 = 0%).

Forest plot for the effectiveness of VT on gait

Effects for activity and participation

Activity

Activity was evaluated using BI [17, 20, 21, 23, 24, 33, 35, 37, 45, 48] and FIM [25, 36, 38, 39]. Meta-analysis demonstrated that VT significantly improved activity (SMD = 0.32, 95% CI 0.06–0.57, P = 0.01, I2 = 51%; Fig. 7). Specifically, the FIM score was significantly improved (SMD = 0.85, P = 0.03). In contrast, the BI score was not significantly improved (SMD = 0.19, P = 0.1). No significant moderators of treatment efficacy were identified in predefined subgroup analyses or meta-regression (all P > 0.05).

Forest plot for the effectiveness of VT on activity

Participation

Three studies employing SIS-36 were included [46, 47, 51]. The meta-analysis revealed no significant benefit of VT for participation (SMD = 0.01, 95% CI − 0.33 to 0.35, P = 0.95, I2 = 0%; Fig. 8). Due to the limited number of included studies, no subgroup analyses or meta-regression were conducted.

Forest plot for the effectiveness of VT on participation

Adverse events

Among the 37 included studies, five reported minor adverse events occurring after intervention, affecting 19 individuals (1.3% of the total study population). These mild adverse events included fatigue, pain, and dizziness, with no more serious adverse events reported.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses based on eight predefined factors were conducted for each outcome (Additional file 3). Our analysis revealed three key factors that moderate VT efficacy (Table 2). For spasticity, people with chronic stroke (> 6 months) showed greater improvement than those in the acute and subacute phases. For balance, people receiving > 5 h of cumulative vibration showed greater improvement than those with ≤ 5 h. For gait, single-session vibration time of ≥ 20 min was associated with greater improvement.

Meta-regression

We performed meta-regression for all outcomes based on eight predefined factors (Additional file 4). For spasticity, intervention duration emerged as a key moderator (β = 1.23, 95% CI 0.01–2.45, P = 0.049). For gait, single-session vibration time was identified as a key moderator (β = 0.68, 95% CI 0.16–1.20, P = 0.015). None of the remaining factors (age, disease stage, vibration type, etc.) significantly moderated treatment efficacy for any outcome (all P > 0.05).

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed for all outcomes except participation (which was not feasible because only three studies were available). No obvious asymmetry was observed in funnel plots for any outcome. Nevertheless, Egger’s test indicated potential publication bias for gait and balance. The results of trim-and-fill analysis showed no substantial change in the pooled effect sizes for gait and balance, confirming the robustness of our findings (Additional file 5).

Sensitivity analysis

Two commonly used robustness tests were performed for all outcomes, including sequentially excluding individual studies to ascertain whether the results were dominated by any single study, and shifting between fixed-effects and random-effects models to assess the influence of model selection. Neither of the two tests described above led to substantive changes in the direction or statistical significance of the pooled effect size, demonstrating the robustness of the results.

GRADE evidence quality assessment

The quality of evidence was moderate for motor, spasticity, gait, and activity, and low for balance and participation. These downgrades were primarily due to substantial heterogeneity (I2 > 50%) or potential publication bias (see Additional file 6).

Discussion

Our meta-analysis confirmed that VT significantly improved physical function and activity in people with stroke, while its effects on participation remain unclear. Importantly, we identified disease stage and temporal parameters as key moderators of spasticity, balance, and gait outcomes. VT produced greater spasticity improvement in people with chronic stroke; longer temporal parameters (including single-session vibration time, cumulative vibration time, and intervention duration) were associated with greater improvements in balance and gait. Furthermore, intervention design, vibration type, and frequency, among other factors, showed no significant moderating effects on VT efficacy in the present analysis. Given the limitations imposed by bias and heterogeneity in the included studies, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as they are supported by only low-to-moderate quality evidence. Nonetheless, these findings serve as a valuable reference for optimizing dosage parameters in clinical practice.

The underlying mechanism of VT

VT is also regarded as a form of proprioceptive training. WBV delivers broad systemic stimulation via a platform, while FMV enables precise intervention targeting specific muscles [52]. Current evidence suggests that both types enhance proprioceptor activation and sensory input, thereby promoting central neural reorganization and plasticity to improve function [6, 53]. The specific mechanisms underlying WBV have not been fully elucidated. FMV has a relatively well-defined mechanism. On the one hand, it transmits signals to the motor cortex via Ia afferents to promote cortical reorganization [54]; on the other hand, peripherally, it elicits physiological changes by modulating Ia-α motor neuron synapses [50]. Rocchi et al. [55] established that repetitive FMV can induce plasticity in specific spinal circuits, with the efficacy depending on the muscles being vibrated. To date, neither the present study nor previous research has identified statistically significant differences in therapeutic efficacy for functional outcomes in people with stroke between WBV and FMV. Their relative advantages across various domains of functional recovery have not been clearly established, warranting further investigation.

Physical function

Motor and spasticity

Prior meta-analyses have also corroborated the efficacy of VT in alleviating spasticity and enhancing motor function in people with stroke [56–58]. Building on previous findings, our study further identified two key moderators of therapeutic efficacy for spasticity: disease stage and intervention duration. Specifically, in contrast to those in the acute and subacute phases, people in the chronic phase exhibited more pronounced effects on spasticity relief. Seo et al. [59] also reported that VT was particularly effective during the 6–12 months post-stroke. While this timeframe does not align with the canonical peak of neural plasticity, it indicates that notable potential for neural plasticity persists beyond the early subacute phase. Moreover, as the functional status of people with stroke tends to stabilize during this period, it may further facilitate their regular participation in training, thereby achieving superior therapeutic outcomes.

Balance

For balance, the current evidence comes primarily from studies on WBV, most supporting its benefits [57, 60]. Our analysis not only upheld the aforementioned consensus but also, for the first time, demonstrated that FMV has comparable efficacy to WBV for improving balance. This allows for flexible device selection based on individual conditions in clinical practice. Additionally, we found that more than 5 h was associated with superior balance improvements. This highlights the importance of ensuring adequate cumulative vibration time in clinical treatment. Though earlier meta-analyses by Lu, Yang, et al. reported null findings, their conclusions may be limited by the small study sample (3–4 trials) and the use of data collected prior to 2015 [61, 62]. Our study synthesized 15 more recent and larger-scale studies, thereby providing stronger evidence for the effect of VT on balance function.

Gait

To date, no meta-analyses have established that VT can effectively enhance gait in people with stroke. Our study revealed the beneficial effects of VT on gait and further identified single-session vibration time as a key moderator, with vibration ≥ 20 min associated with superior outcomes. Earlier studies yielded inconsistent conclusions, primarily due to too few primary studies and methodological flaws in their pooling approaches: Yang et al.’s analysis lacked sufficient statistical power owing to an inadequate number of included studies (only 6 studies for TUG) [63]; Du et al.’s analysis merged scales with opposing scoring directions (e.g., TUG, in which shorter times are better, and the Functional Ambulation Category, in which higher scores are better) in the pooled analysis [57]. This method could distort the pooled effect size. Therefore, our study rigorously selected homogeneous outcome measures, including the TUG (12 studies) and 10MWT (2 studies). However, the overall evidence remains limited, particularly for the 10MWT (only 2 studies). Further research is warranted to verify these results.

Activity and participation

Based on the core concepts of the ICF, the activity dimension focuses on an individual’s ability to independently perform daily tasks, reflecting the level of functioning at the individual level. The participation dimension focuses on the extent to which individuals integrate into their living environments and participate in family and social activities, reflecting their functional status in the social environment [4]. Although these two dimensions differ markedly in concept, evaluation scope, and clinical significance, they are intrinsically related and mutually influential in the assessment of actual rehabilitation outcomes. This has led to a lack of clearly defined assessment tools for these two dimensions in current clinical practice [64]. To ensure study homogeneity and analytical rigor, this study strictly adhered to the ICF. In the results section, activity and participation were independently analyzed as two separate dimensions. Activity was assessed using the BI [65] and FIM scales [66], and participation was evaluated using the SIS-36 scale. There is an urgent need to develop more targeted and specific assessment tools. These would allow for a more precise and clear revelation of the intervention effects of VT on the activity and participation of people with stroke.

Activity

Meta-analytic evidence concerning the effect of VT on activity remains scarce. Our study confirms the overall beneficial effect reported by Lu et al. [58]. Nevertheless, for specific outcomes, only the FIM score was significantly improved, whereas the BI score showed no notable changes. This discrepancy may be attributable to statistical power, as the sample size of Lu et al. (n = 948) far surpassed that of our study (n = 462). Prior studies have indicated that basic motor improvements do not translate directly into improved ability to perform daily activities, with the latter requiring multidimensional comprehensive rehabilitation [67]. This may be the underlying reason for the inconsistent effects of VT on BI. Future research should prioritize investigating the role and synergistic mechanisms of VT within integrated rehabilitation regimens.

Participation

For the first time, our study systematically evaluated the therapeutic effects of VT on participation. The effect of VT on participation remains unclear. Evidence on participation was limited to three studies, largely due to the lack of standardized assessment tools for this domain. Most available studies employ comprehensive scales such as the SIS-36, whose participation subscales may lack sensitivity, making it difficult to detect changes accurately. To clarify the value of VT for promoting participation, future research should develop more sensitive assessment tools and conduct high-quality clinical trials using these instruments.

Key moderators of VT efficacy

Our study identified four key moderating factors, namely disease stage, single-session vibration time, cumulative vibration time, and intervention duration. Beyond disease stage (discussed in Sect. 4.2.1), the remaining three all reflect the intervention time-related dose–response relationship. Specifically, longer single-session vibration time (≥ 20 min) benefited gait, greater cumulative vibration time (> 5 h) enhanced balance, and extended intervention duration (≥ 4 weeks) improved spasticity. Zeng et al. [56] also observed that the spasticity improvement effect intensifies with the extension of single-session vibration time. Lu et al. [58] similarly found superior efficacy for interventions exceeding 4 weeks in duration. Moreover, although subgroup analysis based on age revealed no statistically significant difference, the younger subgroup (≤ 60 years) showed a consistent trend toward larger effect sizes across all outcomes. This indicates that age may be a potential factor influencing treatment efficacy.

Limitations

Inadequate blinding and allocation concealment were common in the included studies, which may introduce bias. Then, many studies exhibit variations in participant characteristics, vibration protocols, and other factors, resulting in moderate heterogeneity in some pooled results. Although subgroup analysis and meta-regression were used to explore heterogeneity, caution is still warranted when interpreting the findings. Furthermore, the long-term effects of VT remain unclear owing to the small number of primary studies; therefore, the long-term effectiveness of VT could not be determined and requires further validation. Moreover, the paucity of studies on participation precludes definitive conclusions regarding its effect. Finally, the PROSPERO registration was completed after the literature search. We confirm that all analyses strictly adhered to the pre-specified protocol to ensure the study’s transparency and reproducibility.

Conclusions

Our meta-analysis demonstrates that VT can safely and effectively improve physical function and activity in people with stroke; however, its impact on participation remains unclear. Additionally, our study identified four key moderators of treatment efficacy. Regarding disease stage, VT produced superior spasticity relief in people with chronic stroke compared to those in acute and subacute phases. Regarding treatment dosage, longer intervention times were associated with better outcomes: single-session vibration time ≥ 20 min for gait, cumulative vibration time > 5 h for balance, and intervention duration ≥ 4 weeks for spasticity yielded particularly pronounced improvements. These findings provide a basis for personalized vibration therapy protocols. Nevertheless, the strength of current evidence is constrained by heterogeneity among primary studies, with an overall quality ranging from low to moderate. Thus, future research should promote the standardization of vibration protocols and outcome assessments, and conduct methodologically rigorous large-sample trials to generate higher-quality evidence.
