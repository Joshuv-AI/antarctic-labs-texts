Postoperative Atrial Fibrillation and Risk of Development of Dementia
Authors: Parag Goyal, Han A Li, Cenai Zhang, Ashkan Hashemi, Nickpreet Singh, Sadiya S Khan, Rod S Passman, Jim W Cheung, Eiran Z Gorodeski, Hooman Kamel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative atrial fibrillation (POAF) is a common postsurgical complication with increased risk of mortality and morbidity, but its association with incident dementia is unknown.ObjectivesThe purpose of this study was to determine if POAF is associated with increased risk of incident dementia.MethodsThis is a retrospective cohort study of Medicare beneficiaries, based on a 5% sample of claims from January 1, 2008, to December 31, 2018. We included adults aged at least 66 years with continuous coverage in Medicare who were hospitalized for surgery. Patients with prior diagnoses of dementia or stroke were excluded. Our primary exposure was AF status stratified as POAF, no AF, or previously diagnosed AF. Our primary outcome was a new diagnosis of dementia after the index hospitalization.ResultsIn our study of 670,745 patients, POAF occurred in 22.7% among those undergoing cardiac surgery and 2.2% among those undergoing noncardiac surgery. Among those undergoing cardiac surgery, the incidence of dementia was similar in those with POAF (4.4%) compared to those with no AF (4.6%); among those undergoing noncardiac surgery, the incidence of dementia was higher in those with POAF (12.5%) compared to those with no AF (9.3%). In an adjusted Cox model, there was an association between POAF and incident dementia only among those undergoing noncardiac surgery (HR: 1.20; 95% CI: 1.11-1.29).ConclusionsPOAF is associated with incident dementia among older adults undergoing noncardiac surgery. Increased attention and further study are needed to best guide future treatment to prevent dementia in high-risk populations such as those with POAF.Key words: atrial fibrillation, dementia

Postoperative atrial fibrillation (POAF) is a common clinical phenomenon in the acute postsurgical setting, occurring 3% to 16% of the time following noncardiac surgery and 32% to 64% of the time following cardiac surgery.1, 2, 3 POAF is important because there are emerging data indicating that POAF is not a benign entity. Prior work has shown that POAF is associated with increased rates of recurrent AF, stroke, heart failure, and all-cause mortality and morbidity.4, 5, 6, 7, 8

New-onset dementia is another important outcome that could be impacted by POAF. Potential mechanisms include heightened thromboembolic stroke risk, increased risk of microbleeds with use of anticoagulation, and cerebral hypoperfusion.9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 Despite a strong biologic rationale supporting the potential association of POAF with dementia, this has not been studied to date.

Thus, with this retrospective study, we sought to understand the association between POAF and dementia. Given potential differences in mechanism and/or risk factors, we sought to examine these associations separately among those undergoing noncardiac surgery and those undergoing cardiac surgery; and among distinct age subgroups. Findings here have the potential to further inform about the potential hazards of POAF.

Methods

Data source and study population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study involving Medicare beneficiaries, utilizing a 5% random sample of inpatient and outpatient claims from January 1, 2008, to December 31, 2018; this random sample is intended to facilitate analyses that can provide generalizable data to the broader population of Medicare beneficiaries. The U.S. Centers of Medicare and Medicaid Services provides health insurance information for a majority of U.S. citizens aged 65 years and older. We followed the Reporting of Studies Conducted using Observational Routinely Collected Data guidelines.15 The Weill Cornell Medicine Institutional Review Board approved this analysis of deidentified data and waived requirements for informed consent.

We limited our cohort to individuals who maintained continuous coverage in traditional fee-for-service Medicare (Parts A and B) for a minimum of 1 year or until death, aligning with standard practice of analyzing Medicare data. While Medicare eligibility commences at 65 years of age, our study encompassed only patients aged 66 or older. This criterion allowed sufficient time for beneficiaries to engage in medical care, and for health care providers to record any pre-existing medical comorbidities. Therefore, we included all Medicare beneficiaries ≥66 years of age with a hospitalization for any surgery, defined using surgery-related Medicare Severity-Diagnosis Related Group codes. For those with multiple surgical hospitalizations, we included only the first hospitalization. We excluded those with prior diagnoses of dementia or stroke from our analysis (Figure 1). Prior diagnoses of dementia and stroke were based on previously validated International Classification of Diseases-10th Revision (ICD-10) codes16 (Supplemental Table 1). We also excluded patients who did not survive the index surgical hospitalization.

Measurements

Our primary exposure variable was AF status during the index hospitalization, which was categorized as POAF, previously diagnosed AF, and no AF. POAF was defined as a diagnosis of AF on discharge from index surgery hospitalization without AF diagnosis present on admission or previously documented AF during a prior emergency department (ED) visit or hospitalization. Patients with AF coded present on admission or during a prior encounter were classified as previous AF. All others were categorized as no AF. AF was ascertained using previously validated ICD-9 and 10 codes and present-on-admission codes which have excellent sensitivity, specificity, and positive and negative predictive value17,18 (Supplemental Table 1). AF was further subclassified as paroxysmal, persistent, or chronic AF as defined by ICD-10 codes for descriptive purposes.

Our primary outcome variable was a new diagnosis of dementia after the index hospitalization (incident dementia), defined by ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes16 (Supplemental Table 1). Prior work has shown that these ICD-10 codes have reasonable sensitivity and specificity.17

Covariates of interest included demographic data (age, sex, race) and comorbid conditions (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, sleep apnea, coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, chronic kidney disease, obesity, and excessive alcohol consumption). For those undergoing noncardiac surgery, we additionally adjusted for type of surgery. Covariates were prespecified and selected based on clinical knowledge and prior research.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as means with SDs and compared using the t-test; categorical variables were expressed as counts and proportions and compared using the chi-square test. We constructed Kaplan-Meier curves to calculate the cumulative incidence of dementia during the study period and calculated the incidence rate of dementia among patients with no AF, previous AF, and POAF, stratified by cardiac vs noncardiac surgery. We used Cox proportional hazards regression models to determine the association between POAF and incident dementia after adjustment for sociodemographic and comorbid conditions. We examined log-log plots and confirmed that the proportional hazards assumptions were met. Given low proportions of missing data for demographics and primary diagnosis and procedure codes (<0.1%), we conducted a complete case analysis. To determine whether there was effect modification by age, we conducted an interaction analysis whereby we added cross-product terms to the models and performed a Wald test. To ensure the robustness of our findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis where we included a propensity score as an additional covariate.

All statistical analyses were done using Stata, version 15.1 (StataCorp). A 2-sided P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The study population included 670,745 Medicare beneficiaries without prior diagnoses of dementia or stroke undergoing surgery. The mean age of the cohort was 75.3 ± 7.7 years and 54.7% were female. Among them, 36,599 adults underwent a cardiac surgery, and 634,146 adults underwent a noncardiac surgery.

Among the full cohort, 106,517 had previously diagnosed AF and 22,042 had POAF. Baseline characteristics for the cohort stratified by AF are shown in Table 1. Patients with POAF (mean age 76.3 ± 7.5 years) were older than those without AF (mean age 74.7 ± 7.5 years) but slightly younger than those with previous AF (mean age 78.3 +/− 7.9 years). Baseline characteristics stratified by AF are shown for those undergoing cardiac surgery (Table 2) and those undergoing noncardiac surgery (Table 3). Incident POAF was more frequent in adults undergoing cardiac surgery (22.7%) compared to noncardiac surgery (2.2%).

Cardiac surgery cohort

Among adults who underwent cardiac surgery, 1,730 (4.7%) were diagnosed with dementia after the index hospitalization over a median follow-up of 3.6 (IQR: 1.5-6.2) years.

There was increased incidence of dementia among patients with previous AF (HR: 1.44; 95% CI: 1.26-1.64) but not POAF (HR: 1.05; 95% CI: 0.91-1.21) compared to patients who did not have AF. The incidence rate of dementia in the cardiac surgery cohort was 0.81 per 100 person-years in the POAF group, 1.12 per 100 person-years in the previous AF group, and 0.78 per 100 person-years in the no AF group; cumulative rates per year are shown in Figure 2. In a Cox regression model adjusted for covariates, there was an association between dementia and previous AF (HR: 1.24; 95% CI: 1.09-1.43) but no association with POAF (HR: 1.01; 95% CI: 0.88-1.17) (Table 4). Of note, there was no interaction for age (P-for-interaction = 0.19). A sensitivity analysis with the inclusion of a propensity score as a covariate revealed similar findings (Supplemental Table 2).

Noncardiac surgery cohort

Among adults who underwent noncardiac surgery, 62,381 (9.9%) developed dementia after the index hospitalization over a median follow-up of 2.9 (IQR: 0.8-5.8) years. There was increased incidence of dementia among patients with POAF (HR: 1.54; 95% CI: 1.43-1.66) and previous AF (HR: 1.78; 95% CI: 1.73-1.83) compared to patients who did not have AF. The incidence rate of dementia in the noncardiac surgery cohort was 1.87 per 100 person-years in the POAF group, 2.18 per 100 person-years in the previous AF group, and 1.18 per 100 person-years in the no AF group; cumulative rates per year are shown in Figure 3. In a Cox regression model adjusted for covariates, there was an association between dementia and both POAF (HR: 1.20; 95% CI: 1.11-1.29) (Central Illustration) and previous AF (HR: 1.25; 95% CI: 1.21-1.29) (Table 4). Of note, there was no interaction for age (P-for-interaction = 0.19). A sensitivity analysis with the inclusion of a propensity score as a covariate revealed similar findings, with some attenuation of the associations (Supplemental Table 2).

Discussion

In a large cohort of ∼700,000 Medicare beneficiaries from the United States, we found that incident POAF was associated with an increased risk for incident dementia among patients undergoing noncardiac surgery. These data indicate that POAF, a condition long considered to be relatively benign, may be a marker of elevated risk or perhaps even an etiologic contributor for the development of dementia.

Emerging data over the past few years have shown that POAF is associated with an increased risk for multiple conditions including stroke, heart failure, and mortality.5,6,8,12,14 Our study now further builds the case about the negative impact of POAF by showing that it increases the hazard of developing dementia by 20% in patients undergoing noncardiac surgery. This is important given the growing incidence of dementia in the United States and worldwide, which has been observed in parallel to the aging population.19, 20, 21 The total monetary cost of dementia was estimated to be between $157 billion and $215 billion in 201022 and is expected to increase to over $1 trillion by 206023 in the United States alone. Accordingly, identifying important risk factors or even markers for elevated risk that can be leveraged toward development and/or implementation of therapeutic strategies to prevent dementia is an important priority.

It is possible that POAF may simply be a marker for substrate at increased risk for dementia. This would support the value of cognitive screening among those who develop POAF. However, there are some additional important clinical implications. Known etiologic factors for incident dementia include age, vascular risk factors including hypertension and diabetes, stroke, and heart failure.24,25 The fact that our findings remained even after adjusting for these comorbid conditions suggests that the association between POAF and dementia may extend beyond these traditional risk factors. Understanding additional biologic and physiological mechanisms driving the relationship between POAF and dementia would be an important endeavor to advance preventative care of dementia. Two important avenues for further investigation to better understand associations observed here would be the potentially overlooked diagnoses of silent cerebral infarction and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. Prior work has shown strong associations between POAF and incident stroke and incident heart failure.5, 6, 7,14 However, silent strokes (known as silent cerebral infarctions) and some subtypes of heart failure (such as heart failure with preserved ejection fraction) may elude awareness and formal diagnosis. Accordingly, it may be worthwhile to develop and study improved processes for diagnosing these elusive yet treatable conditions specifically in the setting of POAF to better understand whether dementia as a downstream consequence of POAF could be prevented. For example, if there was a way to risk stratify those at risk for silent cerebral infarction and it is believed that vascular brain injury is an important mediator of the association observed here, it could support more aggressive anticoagulation for POAF, a notion already supported by some.26, 27, 28 Similarly, if subclinical heart failure is an important mediator, it could support more aggressive risk factor management and treatment of subclinical disease perhaps through agents like sodium-glucose cotransporter inhibitors given their known capacity to prevent heart failure in other settings.29

It is also possible that the left atrium plays an important role in the association between POAF and incident dementia, independent of stroke or heart failure. Prior work has shown that markers of atrial myopathy are associated with stroke and dementia independently of AF,30,31 although in a recent randomized trial, anticoagulation did not appear to be of benefit for stroke prevention in atrial myopathy.32 In addition to consideration of anticoagulation, it may be reasonable to reconsider the role of rhythm control and its potential effect on cognition. Early rhythm control in patients with AF is associated with lower risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes including stroke.33

It was notable that we did not find an association between POAF and incident dementia among those undergoing cardiac surgery. This could be because POAF following cardiac surgery may be a direct result of the atrial injury/manipulation that occurs during the surgery itself; and may accordingly be self-limited, as the effect of the inciting factor (the surgery itself) wears off over time. Future work to better understand patterns (and underlying mechanisms) of POAF across different subtypes of surgery may be warranted, as this may also have a differential impact on the benefits of different management strategies such as anticoagulation and/or rate vs rhythm control strategies.

Study Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, since this was an observational study, there is risk for residual confounding. For example, we did not have additional details about AF such as duration, burden, and treatment approaches. We were also unable to fully adjust for underlying cognitive vulnerability which could be exacerbated by a number of factors including duration of anesthesia and postoperative complications. Second, we relied on administrative claims data and ICD codes to identify medical conditions (including AF and dementia). Although we used previously validated codes, there is still a risk for misclassification, especially when paroxysmal AF and/or milder cases of dementia are present. Third, we lacked granular data about POAF such as duration and management strategies used to manage POAF during and also after the hospitalization. Fourth, we did not have detailed clinical data on neurocognitive testing or brain imaging—aspects that could be beneficial in future studies to help identify underlying mechanisms and developing targeted therapy.

Conclusions

We found that incident POAF was associated with an increased risk for incident dementia among patients undergoing noncardiac surgery. Our findings suggest that POAF may be a marker of elevated risk or perhaps even an etiologic contributor for developing future dementia. These data add to the growing body of evidence demonstrating the hazards of POAF and support the need for future work to identify strategies to mitigate its potential negative effects.
