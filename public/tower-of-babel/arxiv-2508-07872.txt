Unequal Uncertainty: Rethinking Algorithmic Interventions for Mitigating Discrimination from AI
Holli Sargeant, Mackenzie Jorgensen, Arina Shah, Adrian Weller, Umang Bhatt

Abstract
Uncertainty in artificial intelligence (AI) predictions poses urgent legal and ethical challenges for AI-assisted decision‑making. We examine two algorithmic interventions that act as guardrails for human-AI collaboration: selective abstention, which withholds high‑uncertainty predictions from human decision‑makers, and selective friction, which delivers those predictions together with salient warnings or disclosures that slow the decision process. Research has shown that selective abstention based on uncertainty can inadvertently exacerbate disparities and disadvantage under-represented groups that disproportionately receive uncertain predictions. In this paper, we provide the first integrated socio-technical and legal analysis of uncertainty-based algorithmic interventions. Through two case studies, AI-assisted consumer credit decisions and AI-assisted content moderation, we demonstrate how the seemingly neutral use of uncertainty thresholds can trigger discriminatory impacts. We argue that, although both interventions pose risks of unlawful discrimination under UK law, selective frictions offer a promising pathway toward fairer and more accountable AI-assisted decision-making by preserving transparency and encouraging more cautious human judgment.

In many of these settings, ML models serve as decision support to a human decision-maker that is considered AI-assisted decision-making.

If decision-makers treat both certain and uncertain predictions similarly, they risk making decisions that disproportionately harm particular individuals and groups

Several post-hoc algorithmic interventions deliberately modify how decision-makers access or interact with such model predictions, thus shaping outcomes to achieve desired goals

A popular approach to address this predictive uncertainty is

;

.
Selective abstention assumes that human judgment is preferred to AI-assisted judgments in uncertain instances.

We critically examine selective abstention as an algorithmic intervention for uncertainty in AI-assisted decision-making and undertake a socio-technical, legally-informed approach to analyse its potential risks.
First, we consider the risk of discrimination if predictive uncertainty is unequally distributed across particular groups; therefore, selective abstention is applied unequally to those individuals. Second, we identify the risk that relying on human judgment for some decisions and AI-assisted judgment for other decisions will potentially exacerbate, rather than mitigate, unfairness. This unfairness can be worsened by reintroducing well-known cognitive biases disproportionately in human-made decisions.
In our view, neither blind trust in ML models nor insufficiently careful human judgment is a panacea.

To complement this analysis, we discuss an alternative algorithmic intervention—

2
AI-Assisted Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

In this section, we outline the human-AI collaboration for decision-making literature including the motivations for it, the risks that come with it, the mitigation strategies to deal with those risks, and the gaps that remain.

2.1
Motivations

Human-AI collaboration for decision-making depends on many factors, which aim to achieve better outcomes and manage the inherent limitations of autonomous ML models
(
Green and Chen 2019
;
Bansal et al. 2021
;
Guo et al. 2024
)
.
There are several motivations for human involvement in AI decision pipelines, such as addressing AI fallibility and limitations, especially when ML models struggle with biases, ambiguity, or subjective judgments that humans can better navigate
(
Kostick-Quenet and Gerke 2022
;
Gomez et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
. By including a human decision-maker in the pipeline, they can help ensure that responsible, ethical, and just decisions are made by following principles like fairness and reflexivity that ML models alone cannot satisfy
(
Green and Chen 2019
;
Schreuder and Chzhen 2021
)
. In addition, ML models in the decision-pipeline can enhance rather than diminish human skills and autonomy
(
Lai et al. 2023
;
Buçinca et al. 2025
)
. This collaboration is particularly crucial in high-stakes domains such as healthcare and criminal justice, where the consequences of errors are severe and where legal and ethical considerations demand human oversight
(
Ali, Lahoti, and Gummadi 2021
;
Kostick-Quenet and Gerke 2022
;
Lykouris and Weng 2024
;
Guo et al. 2024
)
.

2.2
Risks and Limitations

Despite the potential benefits, human-AI collaboration in decision-making is subject to several significant risks and limitations that necessitate careful consideration and design.
A prevalent concern is the issue of inappropriate reliance on ML models
(
Zhang, Liao, and Bellamy 2020
;
Zerilli, Bhatt, and Weller 2022
;
Guo et al. 2024
)
.
Studies indicate that humans frequently over-rely on ML recommendations, even when they are incorrect, sometimes leading to performance that is worse than if the model had operated autonomously
(
Logg, Minson, and Moore 2019
;
Bansal et al. 2021
;
Alon-Barkat and Busuioc 2022
;
Khera, Simon, and Ross 2023
;
Vered et al. 2023
;
Guo et al. 2024
;
Buçinca et al. 2025
;
de Jong et al. 2025
)
.
Providing ML model explanations, paradoxically, has been shown to increase the chance that humans will accept the model’s recommendation regardless of its correctness, fostering blind trust rather than appropriate reliance
(
Green and Chen 2019
;
Buçinca et al. 2020
;
Poursabzi-Sangdeh et al. 2021
;
Bansal et al. 2021
;
Vasconcelos et al. 2023
;
de Jong et al. 2025
)
.

In contrast, humans can also exhibit under-reliance or excessive scepticism towards ML models, leading to errors of omission
(
Kostick-Quenet and Gerke 2022
)
. Achieving appropriate reliance—trusting the ML model when it is correct and not when it is wrong—is crucial for realising complementary performance, yet humans often struggle to calibrate their trust effectively
(
Green and Chen 2019
;
Kostick-Quenet and Gerke 2022
)
. Factors influencing reliance are not fully understood, and poor trust calibration is identified as a core vulnerability that can lead to performance breakdowns in human-AI collaboration
(
Kostick-Quenet and Gerke 2022
)
.

Humans bring their own cognitive biases to AI-assistance
(
Arshad et al. 2015
;
Zhang, Liao, and Bellamy 2020
;
Bertrand et al. 2022
)
.
In fact, ML models can exacerbate cognitive biases by anchoring effects (overweighting the initial model prediction) and confirmatory bias, seeking information that supports a model prediction
(
Green and Chen 2019
;
Kostick-Quenet and Gerke 2022
)
.

Further, humans face difficulties in effectively evaluating both the accuracy of both their own decisions and the predictions of an ML model
(
Green and Chen 2019
;
Guo et al. 2024
;
Kim et al. 2024
;
Si et al. 2024
;
Bo, Wan, and Anderson 2024
;
Collins et al. 2024b
)
.

2.3
Algorithmic Interventions

Algorithmic interventions include attempts to mitigate inappropriate reliance on AI-assistance include abstaining from providing ML model outputs to decision-makers and displaying confidence information to decision-makers.

Selective Abstention

A direct intervention strategy is algorithmic abstention, also known as selective classification, selective regression, learning to defer, refusal mechanism, or algorithmic resignation
(
Madras, Pitassi, and Zemel 2018
;
Mozannar and Sontag 2020
;
Shah et al. 2022
;
Lemmer, Guo, and Corso 2023
;
Lykouris and Weng 2024
;
Sikar et al. 2024
;
Bhatt and Sargeant 2024
;
Wester et al. 2024
)
.
Algorithmic abstention may be used to deliberately withhold predictions for various reasons (e.g., decision-maker expertise, company policy, or legal compliance)
(
Bhatt and Sargeant 2024
)
.
However, the primary motivation for algorithmic abstention is to act as a safeguard against displaying potentially erroneous predictions when the ML model is highly uncertain
(
Bartlett and Wegkamp 2008
;
Mozannar and Sontag 2020
;
Schreuder and Chzhen 2021
;
Xin et al. 2021
;
Mozannar et al. 2023
)
.
Deferring decisions to human experts when the ML model is uncertain aims to better utilise human capacity
(
Bartlett and Wegkamp 2008
;
Cortes, DeSalvo, and Mohri 2016
)
.
While it can seem intuitive to defer to humans to manage uncertainty, it is far from a neutral fix.

While abstention frameworks can reduce the overall error rate by allowing ML models to abstain from incorrect predictions, they do not necessarily make the distribution of predictions more balanced across demographic groups
(
Rosenblatt and Witter 2024
)
.
Research indicates that selective classification, which allows ML models to abstain on uncertain predictions, can magnify existing accuracy disparities between various groups within a population and, significantly, even decrease accuracies on the groups that already have lower accuracies when predicting on all inputs (full coverage)
(
Jones et al. 2021
)
.
The impact of uncertainty-based abstention on demographic group defined parity metrics can be chaotic and unpredictable, sometimes improving and sometimes worsening fairness across different data distributions
(
Rosenblatt and Witter 2024
)
.
The underlying issue is a high predictive uncertainty for under-represented groups
(
Jones et al. 2021
;
Shah et al. 2022
;
Ganesh et al. 2023
;
Rosenblatt and Witter 2024
;
Kuzucu et al. 2024
)
.
Imprudent use of abstention or applying the same rejection threshold across under-represented groups can amplify potential disparities already present in the data
(
Schreuder and Chzhen 2021
;
Shah et al. 2022
)
.

Additionally, low-confidence predictions or disagreement among models can lead to classification decisions that are effectively arbitrary
(
Cooper et al. 2024
;
Gomez et al. 2024
)
. Such arbitrariness disproportionately affects individuals in certain demographic groups, particularly when these groups experience higher uncertainty due to under-representation in training data
(
Jain, Creel, and Wilson 2024
;
Gomez et al. 2024
)
.
Demographic group differentiated arbitrariness can compound injustice
(
Hellman 2018
;
Eidelson 2021
)
. As a result, deterministic uncertain predictions can continue systemic inequities
(
Jain, Creel, and Wilson 2024
)
.

Selective Friction

Displaying uncertainty or confidence information is a friction-based approach to potentially improve AI assistance trustworthiness
(
Bonneau et al. 2014
;
Zhang, Liao, and Bellamy 2020
;
Buçinca et al. 2025
;
Reyes, Batmaz, and Kersten-Oertel 2025
)
.
Selective friction interventions introduce deliberate pauses in AI-assisted decision-making to encourage critical reflection, such as delayed predictions, additional steps for accessing predictions, or additional cognitive load through written disclosures
(
Buçinca, Malaya, and Gajos 2021
;
Callaway, Hardy, and Griffiths 2023
;
Li, Lu, and Yin 2025
;
Collins et al. 2024a
)
.
Related to behavioural nudges
(
Wilk 1999
;
Buçinca, Malaya, and Gajos 2021
;
Sunstein and Thaler 2008
;
Callaway, Hardy, and Griffiths 2023
;
Li, Lu, and Yin 2025
)
and microboundaries
(
Cox et al. 2016
;
Mejtoft, Hale, and Söderström 2019
;
Alon-Barkat and Busuioc 2022
)
, friction techniques create moments of reflection without removing human autonomy.

Recent applications in large language models aim to prevent over-reliance and can be applied generally or selectively based on model behaviour, human expertise, or socio-technical factors
(
Spatharioti et al. 2023
;
Bo et al. 2024
;
Kim et al. 2024
;
Si et al. 2024
;
Collins et al. 2024a
;
Bo, Wan, and Anderson 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2024
)
.
We are interested in
selective
friction through cognitive forcing by providing confidence disclosures for uncertain predictions
(
Croskerry 2003
;
Buçinca, Malaya, and Gajos 2021
;
Gajos and Mamykina 2022
)
.
However, simply displaying confidence scores may not be a sufficient solution as people struggle to interpret such information
(
Das and Rad 2020
;
Bhatt et al. 2021
;
Abdar et al. 2021
;
Swallow et al. 2022
;
Prabhudesai et al. 2023
;
He et al. 2023
;
Reyes, Batmaz, and Kersten-Oertel 2025
)
.
Decision-maker misunderstanding of uncertainty in predictions can lead to suboptimal and potentially biased decision-making
(
Bansal et al. 2021
)
.

2.4
Literature Gaps

Understanding when to trust, question, or override an algorithm, becomes a central research problem in human-AI collaboration.
A key gap in existing research is a comparison of these two types of selective interventions—selective abstention versus selective friction—particularly when implemented based on uncertainty thresholds.
While studies note that uncertainty-based algorithmic interventions can magnify disparities between groups, the differential impact on fairness outcomes based on
how
the system is selective (abstaining or frictioning) is under-explored.
Previously, different types of predictive uncertainty has been discussed in papers on bias mitigation methods
(
Ali, Lahoti, and Gummadi 2021
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Kuzucu et al. 2024
)
.

It is well understood that most bias detection and mitigation approaches, within algorithmic fairness research
(
Mehrabi et al. 2021
)
, do not necessarily align with specific UK legal expectations
(
Wachter, Mittelstadt, and Russell 2021
;
Jorgensen et al. 2023b
;
Sargeant and Magnusson 2025
)
. We also argue that discussions of uncertainty-based fairness metrics also do not align with anti-discrimination law
(
Ali, Lahoti, and Gummadi 2021
;
Kuzucu et al. 2024
)
.
While statistically appealing, such
explicit
group-based handling may itself constitute direct discrimination
(
Sargeant and Magnusson 2025
)
.
Such proposals mirror the same legal incompatibilities as traditional fairness metrics, and may even risk unlawful discrimination as discussed in Section
5
.
For example,
Sargeant and Magnusson 2025
explain that the
“distinction between aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty is important from a legal perspective. The reason is simple: the [epistemic] uncertainty coming from estimation and modelling is the (legal) responsibility of the modeller, while the aleatoric uncertainty can instead be considered a true underlying general risk.”

There is a significant gap in assessing the risk of unlawful discrimination specifically as it arises from these selective algorithmic interventions based on uncertainty, and how different selective strategies could impact this legal risk.
To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first to directly compare the implications of selective abstention and selective friction based on uncertainty and to consider the potential risk of
unlawful
discrimination inherent in these design choices.

3
Problem Setting and Selective Algorithmic Interventions Overview

In this section, we provide definitions that outline our problem setting and formally define selective abstention and selective friction, which are the two uncertainty-based algorithmic interventions we compare.

3.1
Preliminaries

In this paper, we consider binary classification tasks.
We assume access to a dataset,
D=\{(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{n},y_{n})\}
, of instances
x_{i}\in X
and true labels
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
. Our predictive ML model is represented by the function:
f:X\longrightarrow\hat{Y}
, where
\hat{Y}=\{0,1\}
. The prediction of our classifier for a given
x
with true label
y
is
\hat{y}=f(x)
.
To represent a human decision-maker, we use the function
h:X\longrightarrow\{0,1\}
. The prediction of the (unaided) human decision-maker for a given
x
is

\overline{y}=h(x)
.
When the human decision-maker observes a ML model prediction, we denote their aided decision as
\tilde{y}=h(x;f(x))
for a given input.

A key part of our work is considering how human decision-makers could react when shown the model’s confidence in an input. We obtain a model’s confidence via the function,
\hat{c}:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
, such that
\hat{c}(x)
denotes the confidence of
f
on
x
. For example,
\hat{c}(x)<\tau
could mean that the ML model abstains from making a decision on
x
because the confidence falls below a threshold value,
\tau
. On the other hand, if, for an instance
x
,
\hat{c}(x)>\tau
then the model’s prediction,
\hat{y}(x)
, holds because it achieves a high enough confidence score.
When we have access to a model’s softmax,
\hat{c}(x)
will be the predicted class’ normalised logit for
x
, or, in other words, the probability for the individual’s predicted class. Through the softmax function, the model tries to estimate
\hat{p}(y|x)
where
\hat{p}(\hat{y}(x)|x)
is the probability estimate. We denote the classifier’s prediction as
\hat{y}(x)=\arg\max_{y\epsilon Y}\hat{p}(y|x)
. Given a binary classifier, confidence then is typically defined as:

\hat{c}=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\hat{p}(\hat{y}(x)|x)}{1-\hat{p}(\hat{y}(x)|x)}\right).

(1)

When the probability estimate is
\hat{p}(\hat{y}(x)|x)\leq 0.5
, then the model’s confidence is
\hat{c}(x)=0
, which is the maximum uncertainty of a prediction.

3.2
Selective Abstention

Selective abstention prohibits access to a model prediction based on point-wise uncertainty.
If the confidence level is
\hat{c}(x)<\tau
, where
\tau
is a threshold set by a decision-maker, then selective abstention occurs due to low-confidence.
If the confidence is greater than
\tau
, the decision maker receives the prediction of the model but is not shown the confidence.

By withholding predictions, selective abstention seeks to improve independent decision-making but may inadvertently perpetuate systemic inequities, particularly when abstentions disproportionately affect certain groups (see Section
2.3
).
Addressing these challenges requires careful evaluation of how abstention thresholds are applied and their impact on different groups, coupled with efforts to mitigate biases in both ML models and human decision-makers.

3.3
Selective Friction

Selective friction modulates—rather than blocks—access to model output when point-wise confidence is low.
We define selective friction as a communication of uncertainty on select predictions that do not meet the uncertainty threshold.
If the confidence level is
\hat{c}(x)<\tau
, then selective friction occurs due to low confidence and the system discloses the prediction and confidence value,
\hat{y}(x)
and
\hat{c}(x)
; if
\hat{c}(x)\geq\tau
, it reveals only the prediction
\hat{y}(x)
.
In this case, the decision-maker always sees the prediction such that the human decision-maker’s final decision is aided by the model and is denoted by
\tilde{y}
.
By communicating confidence for high uncertainty, selective friction preserves human autonomy while introducing a moment of reflection analogous to a behavioural “speed-bump.”

Confidence can be communicated in various ways
(
Bhatt et al. 2021
;
Bles et al. 2019
)
.
It could be described through a predefined categorisation
(
Budescu, Por, and Broomell 2012
)
(e.g., high, moderate, low
(
Balshem et al. 2011
)
), numerically
(
Spiegelhalter, Pearson, and Short 2011
;
Reyna and Brainerd 2008
)
, via a distribution summary
(
Lipkus and Hollands 1999
;
Rousseeuw, Ruts, and Tukey 1999
)
, and by a visualisation of a probability distribution
(
Hintze and Nelson 1998
;
Hullman et al. 2019
;
Kay 2024
)
.
Selective friction effectiveness depends on how decision-makers interpret and weigh the friction in their decision-making.
It provides decision-makers with additional information, encourages informed scrutiny, and aims to reduce the risk of over-reliance on ML models.

We acknowledge that always providing a model prediction with a confidence estimate to a decision-maker could support transparency and explainability in decision-making. However, this is certainly not the norm
(
Spiegelhalter 2017
)
; thus, we focus on instances when a selective algorithmic intervention is triggered due to a confidence value being below a specified threshold. In Figure
1
, we depict a visual that highlights the different algorithmic interventions and the resulting decisions.
We assume the selective friction is not adversarially manipulated; namely the communicated uncertainty estimates are well-calibrated and not altered to misguide human judgment.

4.1
Case Study 1: AI-Assisted Consumer Credit Decisions

Consumer credit is instrumental in fostering economic opportunity and social mobility, yet it has long been plagued by inefficiencies and inequalities
(
Hurley and Adebayo 2017
;
Aggarwal 2021
;
Sargeant 2023
;
Sargeant 2025
)
.
Traditionally, financial institutions relied on human-led judgments and rule-based statistical models to assess applicants’ creditworthiness, relying on metrics like salary, assets and debts, and credit histories.
Recent advances in ML and the proliferation of newly available data (e.g., online banking and consumer transactions) have motivated a shift toward AI-assisted credit decisions
(
Hurley and Adebayo 2017
;
Aggarwal 2021
;
Bruckner 2018
;
Garcia, Garcia, and Rigobon 2024
)
.
Potential benefits of greater accuracy in creditworthiness risk assessments and efficiency gains in these decisions incentivise financial institutions to adopt ML-based credit-scoring systems
(
Sargeant 2023
)
.

Although ML methods can address some limitations of conventional credit scoring, they also introduce new risks and magnify longstanding ones.
It is well understood that historical discriminatory lending practices could be perpetuated through biased training data
(
Ladd 1998
;
Rice and Swesnik 2013
;
Hurley and Adebayo 2017
;
Aggarwal 2021
;
Sargeant 2023
)
.
One primary concern is that training data may contain
historical biases
which inadvertently penalise under-represented groups or historically disadvantaged borrowers
(
Bhatt and Sargeant 2024
;
Andrews 2021
;
Albanesi and Vamossy 2024
)
.
Banks are also increasingly using alternative data sources, including non-traditional finance data, to identify new or more detailed correlations for the classification of consumer credit risk
(
Gillis and Spiess 2019
;
Aggarwal 2021
;
Sargeant 2023
)
.

A subtle but significant issue is the potential
uneven distribution of predictive uncertainty
across demographic groups in credit risk assessments
(
Blattner and Nelson 2021
)
.
Historical discrimination and under-representation in lending data, for instance, may lead to noisier or incomplete credit histories for certain loan applicants, resulting in higher levels of predictive uncertainty
(
Andrews 2021
;
Albanesi and Vamossy 2024
)
.
When stakeholders deploy uncertainty thresholds for selective abstention or selective friction for AI-assisted decisions, certain groups are likely to be disproportionately flagged as “uncertain,” thus experiencing either more frequent abstentions or more burdensome secondary reviews by decision-makers
(
Blattner and Nelson 2021
)
.
Consequently, it is crucial to investigate whether these selective algorithmic interventions further entrench inequalities in access to fair credit.

In the following, let us consider the following consumer credit context where we apply the two selective algorithmic interventions to a hypothetical ML model. We assume that this ML model outputs binary predictions where the loan applicant is either predicted:
\hat{y}(x)=1
a low risk of default, or
\hat{y}(x)=0
a high risk of default. We also assume that if an applicant is at high risk of defaulting then they will not be granted a loan versus if they are a low risk, then they will be given it. We focus on the impacts on the under-represented or historically marginalised groups.

Selective Abstention

We argue that selective abstention, while aiming to mitigate errors from low-confidence predictions, introduces complexities when examining its real-world implications.
For example, if a loan applicant is predicted as low risk but is actually high risk and ultimately defaults (false negative), granting the loan could cause significant financial harm
(
Liu et al. 2018
;
Jorgensen et al. 2023a
;
Sargeant 2023
)
. Conversely, a low-risk applicant mistakenly labelled as high-risk (false positive) could be unfairly denied access to credit.
Error rates often vary across demographic groups, with those groups disproportionately receiving unconfident predictions potentially experiencing higher rates of false positives or false negatives
(
Sargeant 2023
)
.
Such disparities are magnified when abstention disproportionately affects these groups, compounding inequalities in outcomes.

In Scenario 1, where the human lender agrees with the ML model’s uncertain prediction that the person is a high credit risk, the lender could be over-reliant on the model and not critically engage with the application, even though the model’s prediction uncertainty is disclosed to them. Case Study 2: AI-Assisted Content Moderation Decisions

Content moderation plays an essential role in maintaining civil discourse and protecting users from harassment or harmful material
(
Caplan 2018
;
Gorwa, Binns, and Katzenbach 2020
;
UKICO 2024
)
.
To keep pace with the massive scale of user-generated posts, social media companies increasingly rely on ML to improve efficiency in content moderation decisions
(
Dinakar et al. 2012
;
Caplan 2018
;
Gorwa, Binns, and Katzenbach 2020
)
.
The ability to deploy large-scale automated or semi-automated content moderation, such as blocking explicit violence or hate speech, can help platforms swiftly and consistently enforce community guidelines
(
Dinakar et al. 2012
;
Schmidt and Wiegand 2017
;
Caplan 2018
;
Gorwa, Binns, and Katzenbach 2020
;
Jorgensen et al. 2020
)
.

Despite these potential benefits, researchers have discussed that these content moderation systems can complicate and exacerbate fairness issues
(
Binns et al. 2017
;
Duarte, Llanso, and Loup 2017
;
Gorwa, Binns, and Katzenbach 2020
)
.
Under-represented communities—including queer individuals, non-English speakers, plus-sized people, and communities of colour—have been shown to be disproportionately flagged or “shadowbanned,” where their posts are hidden or made less visible with little access from hashtag search pages and explore pages
(
Salty 2019
;
Joseph 2019
;
Brown 2021
;
Chan et al. 2024
;
Shahid, Elswah, and Vashistha 2025
;
High 2025
;
Lien and Vishwanath 2025
;
Wolny 2024
)
.
Others have shown that automated content moderation flags more non-English posts which can harm some communities
(
High 2025
;
Lien and Vishwanath 2025
)
.
AI-driven content filters struggle to detect nuances related to culture, sexuality, or human appearance, often leading to the erroneous classification of benign posts as policy violations
(
Duarte, Llanso, and Loup 2017
)
.

In essence, shadowbanning or automated content removal pushes certain voices down while amplifying others; others that often align with societal norms
(
Duarte, Llanso, and Loup 2017
)
.
Multiple studies suggest that these misclassifications stem from how training data is compiled (e.g., sparse training data resulting in imbalanced representations of different demographics) and labelled (e.g., prejudice from human labellers)
(
Binns et al. 2017
;
Salty 2019
;
Wolny 2024
;
GLAAD 2024
)
.
In addition, human rights advocates have expressed that not including human decision-makers in the oversight of these systems is outright dangerous
(
Duarte, Llanso, and Loup 2017
;
Gorwa, Binns, and Katzenbach 2020
)
.

In the content moderation context, let us consider a theoretical ML model that outputs binary predictions where the social media post is either classified:
\hat{y}(x)=1
as non-violating content, or
\hat{y}(x)=0
as violating content that should be removed or shadowbanned. We assume that non-violating content is published while violating content is removed. We now explore the different effects of selective algorithmic interventions’ applied to this ML model.

Selective Abstention

As with consumer credit, selective abstention may reduce reliance on low-confidence predictions but it also carries disproportionate risks.
Certain groups, such as queer individuals or communities of colour, may disproportionately trigger abstentions due to higher levels of predictive uncertainty, potentially stemming from under-representation or label bias in training data.
As a result, these groups may experience inconsistent moderation outcomes, reducing the reliability of platform governance.

Content moderation decisions often have immediate and tangible consequences. Erroneous removals or flagging of posts can limit visibility, disrupt community participation, and silence important voices
(
Joseph 2019
;
Brown 2021
;
Salty 2019
;
Wolny 2024
)
.
For communities that depend on online platforms for self-expression and activism, such outcomes exacerbate existing inequities and hinder their ability to navigate digital spaces freely.
Social media can even be a source of income for content creators
(
UKICO 2024
;
GLAAD 2024
)
, so
shadowbanning or content removal may have financial consequences for many.
The inconsistency of moderators and selective abstention will likely result in unequal treatment for content creators that fall into these marginalised groups that are disproportionately flagged.

Selective Friction

The main negative impact on under-represented groups of applying selective friction to content-moderation systems is content removal or shadowbanning, which arises in Scenarios 1 and 2 in Table
1
. When the human moderator agrees with the uncertain ML model prediction of violating content (Scenario 1) and thus, the post is removed from the platform, they could be over-relying on the ML model instead of exercising their own judgment. In Scenario 2, the human moderator actively disagrees with the ML model’s uncertain prediction of non-violating content, so the post is removed, possibly because the moderator is adverse to the ML model. For both scenarios, the human moderator could be bias against the content or the content creator, or not understand the significance of the post, especially if it is from an under-represented group member. This behaviour, in turn, is potentially discriminatory and can harm creators from under-represented communities.
Given the lack of quality and representative training data, these models can suffer from high false negative and positive rates.

However, Scenarios 3 and 4 result in positive impacts, where the content from the creators is ultimately published on the platform. While the adverse impacts of an under-represented creator having their content published is unlikely to produce harms, there can still be challenges in the decision-making influence in the AI-assistance. In Scenario 3, for example, the moderator could be too reliant on the uncertain ML model, resulting in a “rubber-stamped” decision, while Scenario 4 shows a possible override that could be without merit
(
Angelova, Dobbie, and Yang 2023
)
.
Poor moderators may ignore the prediction in low-confidence settings and still fail to undertake an independent evaluation of the decision and instead rely on stereotypes or personal biases
(
Arshad et al. 2015
;
Zhang, Liao, and Bellamy 2020
;
Bertrand et al. 2022
)
.
In all scenarios, by providing uncertainty information to the human moderator, they could more critically consider the content at hand than the ML model would, but depending on their knowledge and own biases, they might still shadow-ban under-represented creators.

5
Legal Implications of Selective Algorithmic Interventions

We evaluate the legal implications of deploying
selective abstention
and
selective friction
in the UK.
Research on algorithmic fairness and discrimination has been disproportionately dominated by analyses grounded in United States (
US
) doctrine. Thus, we focus on UK law, one of the most influential common law jurisdictions with functional similarities to the European Union (
EU
) and Commonwealth jurisdictions
(
Sargeant and Magnusson 2025
)
.

5.1
Unlawful Discrimination

Overview of Unlawful Discrimination

Discriminatory ML models have been identified in a variety of contexts; however, many of the analyses of these models do not consider if
unlawful discrimination
specifically occurs
(
Sargeant and Magnusson 2025
)
.
A non-lawyer may be surprised by several types of unfair behaviours that are not legally prohibited and some inconspicuous decisions that result in unlawful discrimination
(
Khaitan 2015
)
.
Although some actions may appear unfair or inequitable, they are not necessarily against the law.
In the UK, all prohibitions of discrimination are consolidated under the Equality Act
(
UK Parliament 2010
)
.
Unlawful discrimination is defined by three key elements: (1) protected contexts, (2) protected characteristics, and (3) prohibited conduct.
This discussion focusses on the definitional aspects of discrimination, excluding broader considerations of liability, defences, or other related issues.

Under the Equality Act, a relevant protected context includes the “provision of a service to the public”, encompassing financial services and social media
(
UK Parliament 2010
, s 29)
.
Protected characteristics under the Act are age, disability, gender reassignment, marriage and civil partnership, pregnancy and maternity, race, religion or belief, sex, and sexual orientation
(
UK Parliament 2010
, s 4)
.
However, marriage and civil partnership, and age for individuals under 18, are not protected characteristics in the context of service provision
(
UK Parliament 2010
, s 28(1))
.
Discrimination in these protected contexts based on the listed characteristics is prohibited under two categories of conduct:
direct discrimination
and
indirect discrimination
.

Direct Discrimination

Direct discrimination occurs when an individual, because of a protected characteristic(s) or an associated proxy, is treated less favourably than a real or hypothetical comparator
(
UK Parliament 2010
, s 13(1), 14)
,
(
UKSC 2018
;
UKSC 2017b
)
.
Importantly, direct discrimination admits
no justification defence
except for narrow statutory derogations
(
UK Parliament 2010
)
.
Intent and justifications are irrelevant; Lady Hale describes “however justifiable it might have been, however benign the motives of the people involved, the law admits of no defence”
(
UKHL 1990
;
UKSC 2009
, 71)
.

Selective Abstention

Although the rule for selection abstention is based on
(\tau)
and is formally neutral, the distribution of confidence scores is conditioned by the historical regularities embedded in the training data.
Where those data encode structural bias—such as the over-policing of queer creators on social media or the systematic mismeasurement of minority credit histories—the model tends to assign lower confidence to social media content and credit applications associated, explicitly or inferentially, with the protected group.
In credit scoring, for instance, outdated lending records may conflate female loan applicants with greater default uncertainty; the model then outputs
\hat{c}_{\text{female}}<\tau
more often than
\hat{c}_{\text{male}}
, systematically funnelling women into the selective abstention process.
Gender or race may be either an explicit input variable, or reconstructed through correlated features such as honorifics, postcodes, or single-sex educational background.
While UK law traditionally requires an “exact correspondence” between proxy variables and protected characteristics to establish direct discrimination
(
UKSC 2017b
;
UKSC 2018
)
, courts have shown flexibility in this interpretation.
There are compelling arguments for expanding this scope specifically for ML models
(
Adams-Prassl, Binns, and Kelly-Lyth 2022
)
.
In such circumstances, the individual could be selected for abstention based on a protected attribute.

If selective abstention is shown as a close proxy for under-represented groups with protected characteristics, diverting these individuals into human decision-making is likely to constitute less favourable treatment.
UK courts treat disadvantages in procedural delays, additional scrutiny, or denial of opportunities as cognisable detriments
(
UKHL 1989
;
UKHL 2003
)
.
An individual in the protected and under-represented group whose confidence score falls below
\tau
is denied the immediacy and consistency of automated approval available to an otherwise comparable person.
Causation follows directly from the mechanics of the system. Applying the “but‑for” test, the question is whether the individual would have avoided the detriment had the protected characteristic not been present.
If statistical evidence shows that membership in the protected group materially increases the probability of falling below the uncertainty threshold
\tau
, it is likely to meet the prima facie evidentiary burden.
The procedural disadvantage likely suffices to establish the less favourable treatment because of a protected characteristic.

Selective Friction

In the context of selective frictions, the legal analysis is substantially the same as above. One primary hurdle to proving direct discrimination is whether the uncertainty threshold can be connected as an associated proxy to protected characteristics. If that can be shown, the distinct challenge from selective abstention is to show whether or not the friction results in less favourable treatment.
Whether the selective friction produces less‑favourable treatment depends on its practical consequences which we described in Section
4
.

In short, both selective abstention and selective friction can amount to direct discrimination whenever the uncertainty mechanism functions, in practice, as a close proxy for protected characteristics and imposes a recognisable detriment on the affected group. Nevertheless, it may be difficult to show a sufficiently close connection between uncertainty and protected characteristics, so it will likely be easier to establish
indirect
discrimination.

Indirect Discrimination

Indirect discrimination arises when a provision, criterion, or practice (
PCP
) disproportionately disadvantages individuals with a protected characteristic compared to those without it
(
UK Parliament 2010
, s 19)
.
Even those who do not belong to protected groups, but suffer the same disadvantage due to the discriminatory PCP, may bring a claim for unlawful discrimination
(
UK Parliament 2010
, s 19A)
.

Selective Abstention

Under the Equality Act the analysis of indirect discrimination
proceeds in three stages
(
UK Parliament 2010
;
UKSC 2017a
)
.
First, the claimant must identify a provision, criterion, or practice (
PCP
).
The abstention rule based on
\hat{c}(x)<\tau
satisfies this requirement.

Second, it must be shown that the PCP places persons sharing a protected characteristic at a particular disadvantage relative to those who do not share it.
The threshold
\tau
and the confidence metric
\hat{c}(x)
are facially neutral: neither is defined by reference to protected characteristics.
Nevertheless, empirical work suggests a correlation between low model confidence and under‑represented demographic groups
(
Blattner and Nelson 2021
)
; it is likely that a correlation between uncertainty and under-represented demographic groups can be identified.
Specific evidence of the specific disparity in the selective abstention practice would need to be shown.

In consumer credit, historical or representation bias in credit data can lead to applicants from under-represented or historically marginalised groups receiving lower confidence predictions
(
Andrews 2021
;
Blattner and Nelson 2021
;
Albanesi and Vamossy 2024
)
; they are therefore diverted into manual review more often, encounter longer processing times, and are exposed to the vagaries of individual loan officers rather than more standardised model output.

An analogous pattern arises in automated content moderation. Training data associated with queer or racialised communities are often under‑represented in the training corpora, which in turn depresses confidence estimates for associated posts
(
Binns et al. 2017
;
Salty 2019
;
Wolny 2024
;
GLAAD 2024
)
.
Such posts are suspended for human review, suppressing both speech and, where creators are monetised, income
(
UKICO 2024
;
GLAAD 2024
)
.
That said, manual moderation might in some instances mitigate disadvantage: experienced moderators can supply cultural context the model lacks, potentially yielding more accurate outcomes
(
Duarte, Llanso, and Loup 2017
;
Gorwa, Binns, and Katzenbach 2020
)
.
In both consumer credit and content moderation contexts, whether the additional human review from selective abstention helps or harms protected groups is therefore an empirical question that must be resolved on the facts of each case.

Third, unlike direct discrimination, indirect discrimination may be justified if the PCP is a proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim
(
UK Parliament 2010
, 19(2)(d))
.
The PCP must be both appropriate and reasonably necessary for achieving a legitimate aim, and there must be no less discriminatory alternatives available to achieve the same objective
(
UKSC 2012
;
EWCA 2006
)
.
For example, in both the consumer credit and content moderation case studies, the relevant decision-makers can likely argue that selective abstention is an appropriate means of achieving the legitimate aim (i.e., enhancing accurate credit and content moderation decisions).
However, both are likely to fail in the proportionality assessment of whether abstention is proportionate and the least discriminatory option.
Specifically, there are two critical issues. The potential accuracy improvement from abstention must be weighed against the potential harm introduced by disproportionately impacting certain groups. If the harms to protected groups outweigh the accuracy gains, the measure cannot be deemed proportionate.
Additionally, less discriminatory alternatives, such as introducing friction to access instead of outright abstention, may achieve similar aims with reduced disparate impact
(
UK Parliament 2010
)
.

Selective Friction

The selective friction rule would similarly constitute a PCP under the Equality Act framework. The central question in assessing selective friction as a potential source of indirect discrimination is whether it places individuals with protected characteristics at a particular disadvantage compared to those without such characteristics. Unlike selective abstention, which defers decision-making entirely, selective friction transparently communicates uncertainty, theoretically allowing decision-makers to weigh the model’s confidence level with other factors. However, transparency alone may not reduce bias; it can even worsen disparities if decision-makers misinterpret low confidence signals, especially for individuals from protected groups.

While selective friction introduces transparency into decision-making processes, it remains debatable whether this practice achieves a balance between accuracy and fairness.
The potential harm arises when decision-makers, acting under time pressure or without sufficient training, misinterpret confidence warnings as definitive indicators of unreliability, rather than as contextual cues to be weighed judiciously
(
Bonneau et al. 2014
;
Green and Chen 2019
;
Kostick-Quenet and Gerke 2022
;
Prabhudesai et al. 2023
)
.
Therefore, evidence must establish whether selective friction leads to more equitable outcomes compared to selective abstention or other mitigation strategies.

If selective frictions are shown to put protected groups at a particular disadvantage, it is then necessary to consider whether selective friction is a proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim.
A key argument in favour of selective friction is that it likely enhances decision-making accuracy by equipping human reviewers with contextual confidence data, potentially reducing errors that arise from binary abstention approaches. However, to meet the proportionality requirement, the practice must not disproportionately affect protected groups when less discriminatory alternatives exist.

In summary, while selective friction offers a theoretically balanced approach between transparency and decision support, its practical implications for equality are complex and context-dependent.
Policymakers and practitioners must critically assess whether the intended benefits of informed decision-making outweigh the potential for unintended bias reinforcement, with a particular focus on designing safeguards that minimise harm to protected groups.

5.2
Solely Automated Decision-Making

In addition to prohibiting unlawful discrimination, UK law prohibits solely automated decision-making that produces legal or similarly significant effects on individuals unless one of three exceptions outlined below are met
(
UK Parliament 2016
, art 22)
.
This prohibition raises critical legal considerations in the context of selective algorithmic interventions, particularly regarding the required degree of human involvement and the potential for interventions to inadvertently encourage rubber-stamping of automated predictions.

Definition and Context

In our analysis, consumer credit decisions clearly have significant legal effects, while content moderation decisions may also be considered similarly significant in specific contexts
(
ECJ 2023
;
UKICO 2024
)
.
The European Court of Justice (
ECJ
) has clarified that “a decision based solely on automated processing” arises where the human decision-maker is unable to “influence the causal link between the automated processing and the final decision”
(
ECJ 2023
)
.
Specifically, automated credit scoring is solely automated when the “automated processing remains the only element justifying the approach of the financial institution towards the loan applicant”
(
ECJ 2023
)
.

In both selective algorithmic interventions, selective abstention and selective friction, the underlying assumption is that a human ultimately makes the final decision.
However, there remains a substantial risk that relying on these interventions to manage high-uncertainty cases may inadvertently lead the human decision-maker to rubber-stamp the low-uncertainty algorithmic predictions without appropriate scrutiny.
If decision-makers consistently defer to algorithmic outputs without genuine scrutiny, the practical effect may result in solely automated decision-making in breach of the UK GDPR.
To ensure compliance with legal standards and mitigate this risk, selective algorithmic interventions must structure human oversight to ensure decision-makers actively and meaningfully evaluate algorithmic predictions rather than passively approving them.

As noted in Section
2
, richer contextual information can prompt users to engage more critically with model outputs and calibrate their trust
(
Yin, Wortman Vaughan, and Wallach 2019
;
Vasconcelos et al. 2023
)
. Nonetheless, many decision-makers struggle to interpret uncertainty metrics, leading to confusion and new biases
(
Bonneau et al. 2014
;
Bhatt et al. 2021
;
Prabhudesai et al. 2023
)
.
Presenting predictions alongside explicit confidence values may encourage decision-makers to critically engage with ML predictions and mitigate over-reliance.
Selective friction can reinforce these safeguards by slowing down decisions in cases that fall within pre‑defined uncertainty bands, thereby creating time and procedural space for reflection.
However, its effectiveness is context‑dependent: in high‑volume domains, additional latency may be commercially untenable, whereas in other high‑stakes settings, a pause may be indispensable to satisfy meaningful human review.
Unfortunately, it is impossible to uniformly identify why a human decision-maker agrees or disagrees with a prediction to satisfy requirements under the GDPR.
To mitigate this risk, selective interventions in high-risk, legally significant settings, should institutionalise active and documented human review, for example, requiring decision‑makers to articulate reasons for agreement or divergence, interrogate edge cases, or show that they could override the system.
Evaluating which specific forms of friction, mandatory peer review, or additional documentation improve decision quality is a priority for future work.

Exceptions and Safeguards

Even when a process amounts to
solely automated decision‑making
, the UK GDPR recognises three narrow exceptions to the prohibition in Article 22.
Such processing is lawful if it is (i) expressly authorised by domestic legislation, (ii) genuinely necessary for entering into, or performing, a contract, or (iii) based on the data subject’s
freely given, informed, and explicit
consent
(
UK Parliament 2016
, art 22(2)-(4))
.
In both of our case studies the data controller could, in principle, rely on the consent ground; although, there is a high threshold under the GDPR to secure meaningful consent in practice.

Where an exception applies, the controller must still implement appropriate safeguards to ensure fair and transparent processing, including the right to human intervention
(
UK Parliament 2016
, art 22(3))
and the right to an explanation
(
UK Parliament 2016
, art 15, recital 71)
.
A recent EU case confirms that Article 15 requires controllers to disclose the existence of automated decision‑making and to provide “meaningful information about the logic involved”
(
ECJ 2024
;
ECJ 2025
)
.
The Court elaborated that:

that person can obtain information that is
concise, easily accessible and easy to understand, and formulated in clear and plain language
on the method and criteria used for that decision…[and that] that information must be
sufficiently complete and contextualised
to enable that person to verify its accuracy and whether there is an
objectively verifiable consistency and causal link
between, on the one hand, the method and criteria used and, on the other, the result arrived at by the automated decision.
(
ECJ 2024
)
.

This recent decision effectively settles considerable academic debate regarding whether the GDPR provides a right to an explanation for algorithmic decisions
(
Malgieri and Comandé 2017
;
Wachter, Mittelstadt, and Floridi 2017
;
Selbst and Powles 2017
;
Kim and Routledge 2022
)
.

If a decision-maker opts for lawful solely automated decision‑making within a framework of selective abstention or friction, the system’s
uncertainty thresholds
,
abstention criteria
, and any
friction mechanisms
must be communicated in a manner that satisfies these standards. Concretely, decision logs should record, for example, the model’s confidence score, the reason a case did or did not trigger abstention or friction, and whether the human decision-maker agreed or disagreed with the model prediction.
In short, selective interventions do not dilute GDPR safeguards.

6
Conclusion

Selective abstention interventions have received much attention in recent years to mitigate bias from uncertain ML models
(
Bartlett and Wegkamp 2008
;
Madras, Pitassi, and Zemel 2018
;
Mozannar and Sontag 2020
;
Jones et al. 2021
;
Fisch, Jaakkola, and Barzilay 2024
;
Bhatt and Sargeant 2024
;
Wester et al. 2024
)
. We demonstrate how selective abstention still results in the risk of unlawful discrimination; it does not solve discriminatory decision-making.
Through our work, we compared selective abstention to selective friction through a socio-technical and legal analysis of two case studies. We argue that even when algorithmic interventions are applied to ML models to account for uncertainty and mitigate discrimination, the models can still disproportionately harm protected groups.

With that said, we argue that there are ethical and legal reasons to provide uncertainty information about an ML model’s predictions through selective friction. We highlight how selective friction are a more promising algorithmic intervention than selective abstention. Selective friction may be lawful depending on its implementation and how the decision-maker interacts with the ML model prediction and confidence score. Our paper presents the first legal discussion of how algorithmic interventions can affect protected groups in practice.

We recommend that future work considers how the accuracy of uncertain model predictions affects the consequences on protected groups and the legal implications as well. Through our paper, we considered the predicted risk of discrimination since we did not assume access to true labels, so we leave analysis of true risk for future research.
Our future work will also explore how selective friction interact with diverse decision-maker profiles and tasks, ensuring that these interventions contribute to equitable and effective outcomes across socio-technical environments.
