Greed is Good: A Unifying Perspective on Guided Generation
Zander W. Blasingame, Chen Liu

Abstract
Training-free guided generation is a widely used and powerful technique that allows the end user to exert further control over the generative process of flow/diffusion models. Generally speaking, two families of techniques have emerged for solving this problem for gradient-based guidance : namely, posterior guidance ( i.e . , guidance by projecting the current sample to the target distribution via the target prediction model) and end-to-end guidance ( i.e . , guidance by performing backpropagation throughout the entire ODE solve). In this work, we show that these two seemingly separate families can actually be unified by looking at the posterior guidance as a greedy strategy of end-to-end guidance . We explore the theoretical connections between these two families and provide an in-depth theoretical understanding of these two techniques relative to the continuous ideal gradients . Motivated by this analysis, we then show a method for interpolating between these two families enabling a trade-off between compute and accuracy of the guidance gradients. We then validate this work on several inverse image problems and property-guided molecular generation.

We can divide the guided generation techniques into two broad categories: conditional training and training-free methods.

The latter category instead makes use of some known guidance function defined on the data distribution and incorporates this information back to the model to influence the generative process.
These training-free techniques can be further broken down into two sub-categories,

The latter class of techniques, in contrast, performs backpropagation throughout the entire sampling process of the flow/diffusion model

We refer to this category as

The aim of this work is to bring these two seemingly disparate family of techniques together into a

Our key insight is that we can
bridge

on the latter.

Contributions.

In light of this insight, we compare several state-of-the-art techniques from this perspective, showing how this perspective yields a unified and flexible framework for viewing guided generation with flow/diffusion models.
We perform a detailed analysis of this greedy strategy, showing that it is not only a unifying view, but that it actually makes
good

We then show a perspective which allows one to move between these two classes of guided generation techniques, opening up an exciting and novel design space.
Lastly, we conduct some numerical experiments on inverse image problems and molecule generation.

Posterior guidance

A popular technique for

5.1

We now consider how the output of the flow model will change under greedy guidance.

6
Beyond Euler

Motivated by this connection between the powerful, but expensive, end-to-end guidance techniques and posterior guidance techniques, we ask is there a middle-ground between them?
A natural extension would be to consider something beyond the Euler scheme from the previous section,
e.g
.
, applying the midpoint method or two Euler steps.
To motivate this discussion more rigorously we present
Theorem

6.1
, which shows that for any explicit single-step Runge-Kutta solver, the error between the
ideal
gradient and this estimated gradient is on the order of the local truncation error of the underlying numerical solver.

Theorem 6.1

(Truncation error of single-step gradients)
.

Let
\bm{\Phi}
be an explict Runge-Kutta solver of order
\alpha>0
of a flow model with flow
\Phi_{s,t}^{\theta}({\bm{x}})
.
Then for any
t\in[0,1]
,

\left\|\nabla_{\bm{x}}\Phi_{t,1}^{\theta}({\bm{x}})-\nabla_{\bm{x}}\bm{\Phi}_{t,1}({\bm{x}})\right\|=\mathcal{O}(h^{\alpha+1}),

(15)

where
h=1-t
.

Key insight 3
.

We can use a higher-order solver to move between posterior and end-to-end guidance exchanging compute and gradient accuracy.

This theoretical tool enables us to move between posterior and full end-to-end guidance choosing whichever point between compute and accuracy happens to be most suitable, hopefully opening a larger design space for solving interesting problems.

In

We report quantitative results in

We report the unguided EquiFM generated molecules as an upper bound and include the theoretical lower bounds from

It is here that we notice a sharp decrease in performance from using posterior guidance.
In particular the greedy (Euler) strategy is is highly unstable even performing worse than the unguided model on the
\alpha
property.
The introduction of an additional step in the form of either midpoint or 2-step Euler does seem to improve performance; although the significance varies property to property.
We observe that the midpoint method seems to perform slightly better than the 2-step Euler.
We performed experiments with Ralston’s third-order method and a fourth-order Runge-Kutta scheme but noticed significant instability in comparison to just taking more steps, we posit this is due to the large step size and that a hybrid scheme like that employed by
Moufad et al. (2025a)
might be a reasonable solution to such problems, but ultimately we leave that question up to future work.
Moreover, we observe that increasing the number of steps generally improves performance with greedy (5-step Euler) performing the best among all the greedy guidance strategies.
The gradients of DTO (50-step) strategy are the
ideal
and perfect gradients w.r.t.
the flow model as the numerical solver takes 50 discretization steps and thus form the
upper bound of performance
.
We see that as the DTO strategy incorporates more discretization steps the results converge to the upper bound along this particular design axis; in particular, we notice that the greedy strategy does well in the the regime of less discretization steps.

8
Conclusion

In this paper we present a unifying view of two different families of guided generation: end-to-end guidance and posterior guidance from the lens of a greedy algorithm.
We present numerous theoretical connections tying these two families together.
Our theoretical analysis shows that there might be some reason to believe that such a cheap approximation of the gradient can be reasonable for
certain
tasks.
By exploiting the theoretical connections we created, we investigate guidance techniques which lie in between these two families giving rise to an exciting novel design space.
We then conduct several experiments on inverse image problems and on controlled molecule generation to illustrate this new design space.
We hope that our findings can help future researchers find the optimal spot between computational cost and accuracy of gradients for guidance problems.

A.1
Posterior guidance

Recent work in flow/diffusion models has explored the guidance using this strategy; we highlight a few notable examples.
Diffusion Posterior Sampling (DPS)
(
Chung et al., 2023a
)
is a guidance method that uses Tweedie’s formula
(
Stein, 1981a
)
to estimate the gradient of some guidance function defined in the output state w.r.t.
the noisy state,
i.e
.
,
\mathbb{E}[{\bm{X}}_{1}|{\bm{X}}_{t}={\bm{x}}]
.
Likewise, the work of
Bansal et al. (2023a)
;
Wang et al. (2023a)
;
Yu et al. (2023a)
;
He et al. (2024a)
explores similar concepts by employing Tweedie’s formula for diffusion models.
Most of these works have explored using the SDE (or Markov chain) formulation of diffusion models rather than the ODE formulation, which is what we primarily focused on in our analysis.

Correcting the guidance trajectory.

Several works have explored extensions to the DPS framework by using multiple steps of an SDE solver to correct
errors
made by the guidance steps.
In particular, FreeDoM
(
Yu et al., 2023a
)
explores the usage of a
time-reversal
strategy repeated for a set number of times in each sampling step to correct possible guidance errors.
Likewise, recent work by
Zhang et al. (2025a)
explored modeling Langevin dynamics on top of a diffusion ODE to correct measurement errors in inverse problems.
A significant number of the proposed methods which use posterior guidance arise from solving inverse problems
(
cf
.

Daras et al., 2024a
)
.

Scheduled hyperparameters.

Researchers realized that extra performance can be gained in such problems by scheduling hyperparameters like the learning rate (or guidance strength) at different timesteps in the numerical scheme
(
Yu et al., 2023a
;
Moufad et al., 2025a
)
.

Beyond Euler.

Recent work by
Moufad et al. (2025a)
explores an extension to
(
Chung et al., 2023a
)
by using a two-step method to estimate the guidance gradient.
This is mostly closely related to the
greedy (2-step Euler)
method from the main paper, although they use a stochastic sampling method, so it would be more akin to taking two Euler-Maruyama steps.

A.2
End-to-end guidance

Within the last year, many researchers have explored backpropagation through flow/diffusion models for controllable generation.
As mentioned in the main paper, the two main strategies for solving such a problem is a DTO or OTD scheme (

Discretize-then-optimize.

FlowGrad proposed by

5

Likewise,

Karunratanakul et al. (2024a)

Optimize-then-discretize.

Another stream of work has explored the use of continuous adjoint equations to perform the backpropagation.
The advantage of such approaches is the

memory cost, and we enumerate the drawbacks in

E

To the best of our knowledge, the first work to explore this was

which used OTD with SDEs for the adversarial purification task.
More general work came later by
Pan et al. (2024a)

More specifically,

Blasingame & Liu (2024c)

Marion et al. (2025a)
explore using the continuous adjoint equations as a part of a larger bi-level optimization scheme for guided generation.
The work of
Ben-Hamu et al. (2024a)

Recent work by
Wang et al. (2025a)
explores an extension of
Ben-Hamu et al. (2024a)
to Riemannian manifolds which incorporates a control signal to the vector field and optimizes both the solution state and
co-state

Parallel to these works (conceptually) is the work of
Wallace et al. (2023b)
who uses EDICT

(

{\bm{z}}(t)

Applying this flow to the random variable

The

This procedure amounts to learning a neural network parameterized vector field

(

We let

Affine probability paths.

A special subset of flow models, are flows which model an

i.e

the random process

(17)

The

(18)

This

(19)

where

are useful shorthands to be used later.

Remark B.1
.

The probability flow ODE formulation of

(

is subsumed by flow models, and represents a model with an affine Gaussian probability paths (AGGP),

Proposition C.4

(Dynamics of gradient guidance)
.

Consider the standard affine Gaussian probability paths model trained to zero loss.
The Gateaux differential of
{\bm{x}}
at some time

by projecting

From

\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{1}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}}_{1})
to be projected into high-variance directions of the target distribution causing the guided sample to stay closer to the true target distribution.

The next question is: how does

change when

is updated with our greedy guidance strategy?

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{x}}{\bm{x}}+h\sum_{j=1}^{k}b_{j}\nabla_{\bm{x}}{\bm{u}}_{\theta}\left(s+c_{j}h,{\bm{x}}+h\sum_{i=1}^{j}a_{j,i}{\bm{u}}_{i}\right),

\displaystyle=\bm{I}+h\sum_{j=1}^{k}b_{j}\left[\nabla_{\hat{\bm{x}}_{j}}{\bm{u}}_{\theta}(s+c_{j}h,\hat{\bm{x}}_{j})\left(\bm{I}+h\sum_{i=1}^{j}a_{j,i}\nabla_{\bm{x}}{\bm{u}}_{i}\right)\right],

(76)

where we let

(77)

Next, recall

C.2
which gives the following ODE

\bm{J}_{s}(s)=\bm{I},\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{J}_{s}}{\mathrm{d}t}(t)=\nabla_{\bm{x}}{\bm{u}}_{\theta}(t,\Phi_{s,t}({\bm{x}}))\bm{J}_{s}(t).

Next, we augmented the ODE above with the underyling ODE for the solution state,
\dot{\bm{x}}(t)={\bm{u}}_{\theta}(t,{\bm{x}}(t))

We now apply the same Runge-Kutta solver to this augmented ODE for the Jacobian matrices which yields

\bm{U}_{j}=\bm{I}+h\sum_{j=1}^{k}b_{j}\left[\nabla_{\hat{\bm{x}}_{j}}{\bm{u}}_{\theta}\left(s+c_{j}h,\hat{\bm{x}}_{j}\right)\left(\bm{I}+h\sum_{i=1}^{j}a_{j,i}\nabla_{\bm{x}}{\bm{u}}_{i}\right)\right].

Clearly,

Now as the underlying numerical solver has local truncation error

Appendix E
Notes on using OTD in practice

While the OTD approach has become quite popular after the work of
Chen et al. (2018a)
, several later works have noticed several key issues that we wish to note for ML practitioners.

Recall our prototypical neural ODE (or flow model) of the form

\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}}{\mathrm{d}t}(t)={\bm{u}}_{\theta}(t,{\bm{x}}(t)),

(89)

and assume it is defined on the interval
[0,T]
and the flow model statifies the usual regularity conditions.
Then, the continuous adjoint equations
(
Kidger, 2022a
, Theorem 5.2)
are:

\displaystyle{\bm{a}}_{\bm{x}}(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{T}},\quad

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{a}}_{\bm{x}}}{\mathrm{d}t}(t)=-{\bm{a}}_{\bm{x}}(t)^{\top}\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}_{\theta}}{\partial{\bm{x}}}(t,{\bm{x}}(t)),

(90)

\displaystyle{\bm{a}}_{\theta}(T)

\displaystyle=\bm{0},\quad

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{a}}_{\theta}}{\mathrm{d}t}(t)=-{\bm{a}}_{\bm{x}}(t)^{\top}\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}_{\theta}}{\partial\theta}(t,{\bm{x}}(t)),

where
{\bm{a}}_{\bm{x}}(t)\coloneq\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial{\bm{x}}(t)
and
{\bm{a}}_{\theta}(0)\coloneq\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\theta
.

Truncation errors.

One area of concern is the potential mismatch between the forward trajectory
\{{\bm{x}}_{t_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and the backward trajectory
\{\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
when performing the backwards solve.

E.g
.
, consider an explicit Euler scheme

{\bm{x}}_{t_{i+1}}={\bm{x}}_{t_{i}}+(t_{i+1}-t_{i}){\bm{u}}_{\theta}(t_{i},{\bm{x}}_{t_{i}}).

(91)

The same scheme when applied to solving the backward trajectory would yield,

\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t_{i}}=\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t_{i+1}}+(t_{i}-t_{i+1}){\bm{u}}_{\theta}(t_{i+1},\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t_{i+1}}).

(92)

Clearly, there is no guarantee that these two trajectories match during the forward and backward solve introducing a source of error.
One potential solution is to use an
algebraically reversible solver

(see
Kidger et al., 2021a
;
McCallum & Foster, 2024a
;
Blasingame & Liu, 2025a
)
which guarantees that the forward and backward trajectory match
perfectly
.
Another option is to store the forward trajectory
\{{\bm{x}}_{t_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
in memory and use
interpolated adjoints
if the backward timesteps do not perfectly align with the forward timesteps
(see
Kim et al., 2021a
)
.

Stability concerns.

Consider the simple ODE,
\dot{y}(t)=\lambda y(t)
defined on
t\in[0,T]
with
y(0)=y_{0}
and
\lambda<0
.
Clearly, most ODE solvers with a non-trivial region of stability
(see
Harier & Wanner, 2002a
, Definition 2.1)
will solve this ODE without an issue, as the errors will decrease exponentially with
\lambda<0
.
However, in the backwards in time solve from
y(T)
the errors will
grow exponentially
.
It can be shown that the adjoint state suffers from similar stability issues.
The local behavior of a differential equation is described through the eigenvalues of the Jacobian of the vector field
(see
Butcher, 2016a
)
.
For
{\bm{x}}_{t}
this is given by
\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}_{\theta}}{\partial{\bm{x}}}
and for
{\bm{a}}_{\bm{x}}
this is given by

\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bm{a}}_{\bm{x}}}\bigg(-{\bm{a}}_{\bm{x}}(t)^{\top}\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}_{\theta}}{\partial{\bm{x}}}(t,{\bm{x}}(t))\bigg)=-\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}_{\theta}}{\partial{\bm{x}}}(t,{\bm{x}}(t)).

(93)

Clearly, the Jacobians for
{\bm{a}}_{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{x}}_{t}
solved in reverse-time are identical, meaning the stability of the backward solve is pushed onto the solve for the adjoint state
(see
Kidger, 2022a
, Section 5.1.2.4)
for more details.
Reversible solvers eliminate truncation errors, but tend to suffer from poor stability,
e.g
.
, the region of stability for reversible Heun applied to neural ODEs is the complex interval
[-i,i]

(
Kidger et al., 2021a
)
.
Recent work by
McCallum & Foster (2024a)
, however, has shown a strategy for constructing reversible solvers with a non-trivial region of stability.

In light of these concerns we propose we consider to be best practices for deciding what scheme to use.

Appendix F
On control signal optimization

Rather than optimizing the trajectory of the solution or the initial condition, several works
(
Liu et al., 2023c
;
Wang et al., 2025a
)
have explored the guidance from the perspective of optimal control
(
Kirk, 2004a
)
.
In essence this technique first injects an additional control signal,

{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, to the vector field,
{\bm{u}}_{t}^{\theta}
, such that

\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}={\bm{u}}_{t}^{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t})+{\bm{z}}(t).

(94)

Thus, instead of optimizing
\{{\bm{x}}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,t]}
directly, this control signal can instead be optimized, serving as one of the key insights in
(
Liu et al., 2023c
;
Wang et al., 2025a
)
.

I.e
.
, suppose we have a neural ODE with vector field
{\bm{u}}_{t}^{\theta}({\bm{x}})
, then we can write the optimization problem as

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{z}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}}_{T})+\lambda\int_{0}^{T}\|\bm{z}(t)\|\;\mathrm{d}t,

(95)

\displaystyle\textrm{s.t.}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{x}}_{T}={\bm{x}}_{0}+\int_{0}^{T}{\bm{u}}_{t}^{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t})+\bm{z}(t)\;\mathrm{d}t.

The next natural question then is to ask about the behavior of a greedy strategy applied to
{\bm{z}}(t)
.
To simplify the analysis, we now consider a control signal applied to the posterior model
{\bm{x}}^{\theta}_{1|t}
such that it is replaced by
{\bm{x}}^{\theta}_{1|t}({\bm{x}}_{t})+{\bm{z}}(t)
which amounts to simply rescaling
{\bm{z}}(t)
from
Equation

94
with
b_{t}
.
From this construction, it should be clear that the greedy gradient for the control signal is merely
\nabla_{\tilde{\bm{x}}_{1}}\mathcal{L}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{1})
.
If using the original formulation where the control signal is applied to the vector field, rather than the denoiser, the gradient is simply scaled by a weighting function dependent on time.
Note that this approach is similar to the greedy approach taken by
Blasingame & Liu (2024d)
; however, they inject the control signal to the source prediction model rather than the target prediction model.

F.1
Continuous adjoint equations for control signals

We can model the gradient for this signal by augmenting the continuous adjoint equations with the adjoint state
{\bm{a}}_{\bm{z}}(t)\coloneq\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial{\bm{z}}(t)
.
In
Theorem

F.1
we show that this gradient is simply an integral of the adjoint state
{\bm{a}}_{\bm{x}}(t)
.

Theorem F.1

(Continuous adjoint equations for the control term)
.

Let
{\bm{u}}_{t}^{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}^{1,1}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}};\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}})
be a parameterization of some time-dependent vector field of a neural ODE that is Lipschitz continuous in its second argument, and let
{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d})
be an additional control signal such that the new dynamics are given by
Equation

94
.
Let
{\bm{a}}_{\bm{z}}(t)\coloneq\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial{\bm{z}}(t)
, then

{\bm{a}}_{\bm{z}}(t)=-\int_{T}^{t}{\bm{a}}_{\bm{x}}(s)\;\mathrm{d}s.

(96)

Our proof follows the structure of the modern proof of Pontryagin’s original result
(
Pontryagin et al., 1963a
)
presented by
(
Chen et al., 2018a
)
; and is similar to the form used by
Blasingame & Liu (2024c, Theorem 2.2)
.

Proof.

For notational clarity, we use the notation
{\bm{x}}(t)={\bm{x}}_{t}
.
We define the augmented state on
[0,T]
as

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{x}}\\
{\bm{z}}\end{bmatrix}(t)={\bm{f}}_{\text{aug}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{u}}_{\theta}(t,{\bm{x}}(t))+{\bm{z}}(t)\\
\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{z}}}{\mathrm{d}t}(t)\end{bmatrix},

(97)

and the augmented adjoint state as

{\bm{a}}_{\text{aug}}(t)\coloneq\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{a}}_{\bm{x}}\\
{\bm{a}}_{\bm{z}}\end{bmatrix}(t).

(98)

The Jacobian of
{\bm{f}}_{\text{aug}}
has form

\frac{\partial{\bm{f}}_{\text{aug}}}{\partial[{\bm{x}},{\bm{z}}]}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}_{\theta}(t,{\bm{x}}(t))}{\partial{\bm{x}}}&\mathbf{1}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}.

(99)

The evolution of the adjoint state is given by

\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{a}}_{\text{aug}}}{\mathrm{d}t}(t)=-\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{a}}_{\bm{x}}&{\bm{a}}_{\bm{z}}\end{bmatrix}(t)\frac{\partial{\bm{f}}_{\text{aug}}}{\partial[{\bm{x}},{\bm{z}}]}(t).

(100)

Therefore,
{\bm{a}}_{\bm{u}}(t)
evolves with

{\bm{a}}_{\bm{u}}(T)=\mathbf{0},\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{a}}_{\bm{u}}}{\mathrm{d}t}(t)=-{\bm{a}}_{\bm{x}}(t),

(101)

thereby finishing the proof.
∎

Appendix H
A brief introduction to inverse problems

Inverse problems cover a large class of scientific problems
(
Chung et al., 2023a
)
that encompass scenarios where a partial measurement
{\bm{y}}
is made of
{\bm{x}}
.
When the mapping
{\bm{x}}\mapsto{\bm{y}}
is not an injection, recovering
{\bm{x}}
from
{\bm{y}}
becomes an ill-posed inverse problem.
Generally, the relationship between the underlying sample
{\bm{x}}
and the measurement
{\bm{y}}
is given by

{\bm{y}}=\mathcal{A}({\bm{x}})+\bm{\eta},\qquad{\bm{y}},\bm{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}},{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}},

(107)

where
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
is the forward measurement operator and
\bm{\eta}\sim\mathcal{(}0,\beta_{\bm{y}}^{2}\bm{I})
is the measurement noise.

The inverse problem then is to find
p({\bm{x}}|{\bm{y}})
.

More details on these types of problems can be found in
Chung et al. (2023a)
;
Moufad et al. (2025a)
;
Zhang et al. (2025a)
.

H.1
Inverse problems and diffusion models

Recall that the ODE formulation of diffusion models is just a particular type of affine Gaussian probability path
(
Lipman et al., 2024a
)
.
Following the conventions of the EDM model
(
Karras et al., 2022a
)
we write this ODE formulation, known in the literature as the
probability flow ODE
, below in

\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}_{t}=-\dot{\sigma}_{t}\sigma_{t}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p(\sigma_{t},{\bm{x}}_{t})\;\mathrm{d}t,

(108)

where
p(\sigma_{t},{\bm{x}}_{t})
is the joint distribution of
{\bm{x}}_{t}
at noise level
\sigma_{t}
.
11
11

11

This
\sigma_{t}
is not the same as the
\sigma_{t}
from the scheduler
(\alpha_{t},\sigma_{t})
used in the main paper.

N.B
.
, for diffusion models
\mathrm{d}t
is a
negative
timestep and we integrate in reverse-time from
T
to
0
.
These models are also called
score-based generative models
due to learning the score function
\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p(\sigma_{t},{\bm{x}}_{t})
.

One of the insights of
Song et al. (2021c)
;
Chung et al. (2023a)
is to apply Bayes’ theorem for inverse problems to score-based generative models,
i.e
.
,

\displaystyle p({\bm{x}}|{\bm{y}})

\displaystyle=\frac{p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})p({\bm{x}})}{p({\bm{y}})},

(109)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p({\bm{x}}|{\bm{y}})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p({\bm{x}})+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}).

(110)

Adapting this for diffusion models, assuming
\mathcal{A}
is defined on
{\bm{x}}_{0}
(the output), we have

\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p(\sigma_{t},{\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{y}})=\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p(\sigma_{t},{\bm{x}}_{t})+\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{t},\sigma_{t}).

(111)

The unconditional score term is the regular score function learned by diffusion models and thus is
appropriately
learned; however, the other term is much more difficult to work with.
The approach of
Chung et al. (2023a)
is to use an approximation of

p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{t},\sigma_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}\sim p({\bm{x}}_{0}|{\bm{x}}_{t})}[p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{0},\sigma_{0})],

(112)

via Tweedie’s formula
(
Stein, 1981a
)
to write

p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{t},\sigma_{t})\approx p({\bm{y}}|\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}_{0}|{\bm{x}}_{t}],\sigma_{0}).

(113)

The approximation error can be quantified by the Jensen gap
(
Chung et al., 2023a
, Theorem 1)
.

)
.
The measure for the high dynamic range reconstruction problem is defined as

{\bm{y}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{clip}(\alpha{\bm{x}}_{0},-1,1),\beta_{\bm{y}}^{2}\bm{I}),

(114)

with
\alpha=2
.

Diffusion model.

We make use of the pre-trained diffusion model from
Chung et al. (2023a)
, trained on the FFHQ
256\times 256
dataset.
We focus on the probability flow ODE formulation popularized by
Karras et al. (2022a)
known as EDM described as

\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}_{t}=-\dot{\sigma}_{t}\sigma_{t}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p(\sigma_{t},{\bm{x}}_{t})\;\mathrm{d}t.

(115)

Following
Ben-Hamu et al. (2024a)
, we employ a midpoint scheme to solve this ODE in
reverse-time
with
N=20
steps.
We use the noise schedule
\sigma_{t}=t
which means
\dot{\sigma}_{t}=1
.
The discretized noise schedule
\{\sigma_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
is given by the following polynomial interpolation

\sigma_{n}=\left(\sigma_{\max}^{\frac{1}{\rho}}+\frac{n}{N-1}\left(\sigma_{\min}^{\frac{1}{\rho}}-\sigma_{\max}^{\frac{1}{\rho}}\right)\right)^{\rho}.

(116)

We use
\rho=7
,
T=\sigma_{\max}=100
, and
\epsilon=\sigma_{\min}=0.01
for all experiments and integrate over
[\epsilon,T]
.

N.B
.
, truncating the integration domain at
\epsilon
rather than
0
is quite common in diffusion models
(
Song et al., 2023d
)
.

Hyperparameters.

Unlike previous works
(
Zhang et al., 2025a
)
we did not adjust the hyperparameters per task and left them the same throughout.
The learning rate was set at
\eta=1
for all experiments, and we performed
n_{\textrm{opt}}=50
optimization steps with the stock implementation of the
torch.optim.SGD
method for each step of the ODE solve.
We set
\beta_{\bm{y}}=0.05
for all tasks.

Ablation study.

For the ablation study in
Table

2
we used the L-BGFS optimizer over the standard SGD optimizer used in the main experiments (for the greedy guidance runs). For full DTO we used SGD as it provided better performance over L-BGFS in that scenario.

N.B
.
, due to compute limitations we couldn’t run DTO for step sizes larger than 8.
Importantly, we fix the maximum number of optimization steps between the greedy and DTO strategies; for greedy we take 5 optimization step per step in the ODE solver, so a 100 in total.
Likewise, for DTO we take a 100 optimization steps in total.

I.2
Molecule generation for QM9

We follow the experimental methodology taken in previous work
(
Ben-Hamu et al., 2024a
;
Wang et al., 2025a
)
and follow the conditional generation pipeline used by
Hoogeboom et al. (2022a)
.
An equivariant
graph neural network
(GNN) was trained for each property on half of the QM9 dataset, serving as a classifier—this model was then used as a guidance function during the experiments.
The EquiFM
(
Song et al., 2023e
)
model was trained on the whole QM9 training set and was used as the underlying flow model for the experiments.
Following
Wang et al. (2025a)
, the test time properties were sampled from the whole training set; in contrast to
Ben-Hamu et al. (2024a)
.

Following
Ben-Hamu et al. (2024a)
we used the L-BFGS algorithm
(
Liu & Nocedal, 1989a
)
with 5 optimizer steps and 5 inner steps with a linear search, in particular we used the stock PyTorch implementation
torch.opt.LBFGS
.
For the DTO experiment we used a learning rate of
\eta=1
.
We tried this for the posterior guidance experiments but encountered severe instability.
We found that a learning rate of
\eta=0.001
seemed to work better.

Recall that
Proposition

5.2
states that the greedy gradient is scaled by the covariance projection.
This effect is lessened as
t\to 1
, thus in later timesteps the greedy gradient is more likely to push samples off the data manifold.
We observed this, with exploding losses even at small learning rates.
To remedy this, we took inspiration from other works
(
Chung et al., 2023a
;
Yu et al., 2023a
;
Moufad et al., 2025a
)
and annealed the learning rate.
We chose the following simple scheduler:

\eta_{t}=\begin{cases}\eta(1-t)&t>0.5\\
0&t\leq 0.5\end{cases},

(117)

where
\eta=0.001
is the base learning rate.

Runge-Kutta 4.

Additionally, we ran some experiments using RK4 but ran into insurmountable stability issues.
Recall that RK4 is given by

\displaystyle\bm{k}_{1}

\displaystyle={\bm{u}}_{\theta}\left(t_{n},{\bm{x}}_{n}\right),

(118)

\displaystyle\bm{k}_{2}

\displaystyle={\bm{u}}_{\theta}\left(t_{n}+\frac{h}{2},{\bm{x}}_{n}+\frac{h}{2}\bm{k}_{1}\right),

(119)

\displaystyle\bm{k}_{3}

\displaystyle={\bm{u}}_{\theta}\left(t_{n}+\frac{h}{2},{\bm{x}}_{n}+\frac{h}{2}\bm{k}_{2}\right),

(120)

\displaystyle\bm{k}_{4}

\displaystyle={\bm{u}}_{\theta}\left(t_{n}+h,{\bm{x}}_{n}+h\bm{k}_{3}\right),

(121)

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{n+1}

\displaystyle={\bm{x}}_{n}+\frac{h}{6}(\bm{k}_{1}+2\bm{k}_{2}+2\bm{k}_{3}+\bm{k}_{4}).

(122)

Using the step size
h=1-t
we encountered large stability issues with the
\bm{k}_{4}
term due to being evaluated at the endpoint of the flow model trajectory.
We tried a mixed-solver scheme were we would start with Euler and then switch to RK4, but that did not help.
We also tried the common diffusion trick of truncated the time interval to
[0,1-\epsilon]
for some small
\epsilon>0
, but this did not solve the stability issues either.
Ultimately, we abandoned it for this work and left such explorations for future work.
It seems reasonable to suppose that schemes which don’t evaluate on the endpoint,
e.g
.
, Ralston’s method, Heun’s third-order method, or Ralton’s third-order method may fair better.

I.3
Numerical schemes

We detail the numerical schemes used for posterior guidance beyond Euler.

Midpoint.

The midpoint scheme used in both experiments is implemented as

{\bm{x}}_{1}={\bm{x}}_{t}+h{\bm{u}}_{\theta}\left(t+\frac{h}{2},{\bm{x}}_{t}+\frac{h}{2}{\bm{u}}_{\theta}(t,{\bm{x}}_{t})\right)

(123)

with step size
h=1-t
.
12
12

12

This is appropriately adjusted for diffusion models with a terminal time of
0
.

2-step Euler.

This scheme used in both experiments is implemented as

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{t+\frac{h}{2}}

\displaystyle={\bm{x}}_{t}+\frac{h}{2}{\bm{u}}_{\theta}(t,{\bm{x}}_{t}),

(124)

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{1}

\displaystyle={\bm{x}}_{t+\frac{h}{2}}+\frac{h}{2}{\bm{u}}_{\theta}\left(t+\frac{h}{2},{\bm{x}}_{t+\frac{h}{2}}\right),

(125)

with step sizes
h=1-t
.

I.4
Hardware and compute cost

Inverse image problems.

The inverse image problem experiments were run on a single NVIDIA H100 80GB GPU.
It took roughly 4 minutes and 78 GB of VRAM to generate 10 images for each inverse problem.
As such each experiment took about an 40–50 minutes.
Experiments which used the midpoint method, unsurprisingly ran about 90% slower.

Molecule generation.

The molecule generation experiments were run on a single NVIDIA V100 16GB GPU.
It took about 3 minutes and 1.5 GB of VRAM to generate 1 molecule leading to the experiments taking on the order of 300 minutes to complete.
Experiments which used the midpoint method, unsurprisingly ran about 90% slower.

Appendix K
Discussions

K.1
Broader Impacts

Controllable generation can be used for many tasks both benign and malicious.
The insights from this paper could be used to develop more effective adversarial attacks, generation of harmful content, or other malicious applications.

K.2
Limitations

As this work is mostly theoretical, our experimental illustrations are limited, serving more to illustrate the key concepts rather than advancing the state-of-the-art within the particular problem.
We believe that future work can use these insights to make informed design choices when developing solutions to guided generation problems.

In our controllable molecule generation experiments, we take a naïve strategy for annealing the learning rate leaving performance on the table.
Moreover, we don’t consider mixed accuracy schemes,
i.e
.
, using Euler for certain steps closer to the target and midpoint for steps further away
(
cf
.

Moufad et al., 2025a
)
.
