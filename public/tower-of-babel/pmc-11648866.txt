The sequence 581Ser-610Val in the fibrinogen Aα chain is responsible for the formation of complexes between plasminogen and αC-regions of fibrin(ogen)
Authors: Lada Kapustianenko, Tetiana Grinenko, Andrew Rebriev, Artem Tykhomyrov
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify the binding sites for plasminogen (Pg) and its kringle-containing fragments within the αC-region of fibrin(ogen). This investigation is crucial while the conversion of fibrinogen into fibrin induces conformational changes that expose binding sites for Pg and tissue-type Pg activator (tPA), facilitating effective zymogen activation on the fibrin surface.MethodsTwo C-terminal fragments of the Aα chain ‒ 45 kDa (225Val-610Val) and 40 kDa (225Val-580Lys), were obtained through plasmin hydrolysis of human fibrinogen and subsequently characterized using MALDI TOF mass spectrometry. The interactions of Glu-Pg and Lys-Pg, as well as Pg kringle fragments (K1-3, K4, and K5), with the obtained αC truncated polypeptides were analyzed using ELISA and Western blot techniques with the use of specific antibodies.ResultsIt was demonstrated that Pg and its fragments K1-3, K4, and K5 interact exclusively with the 45-kDa fragment (225Val-610Val) of the αC region of fibrinogen with high affinity in a concentration-dependent manner (Kd values for Glu-Pg = 7.10 × 10−9 M, Lys-Pg = 6.01 × 10−9 M, K1-3 = 1.08 × 10−7 M, K4 = 5.06 × 10−7 M, and K5 = 2.50 × 10−7 M). This fragment, unlike the 40-kDa polypeptide (225Val-580Lys), contains the α581Ser-610Val sequence.ConclusionsIt was shown that the sequence 581Ser-610Val of fibrinogen Aα-chain, which becomes exposed during the conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin, is essential for the formation of complexes between Pg and αC regions of fibrin(ogen), thereby contributing to the initiation and regulation of fibrinolysis.

Introduction

In the blood of vertebrates, two opposite systems synergistically regulate hemostasis, encompassing both coagulation and fibrinolysis. This dynamic equilibrium involves the concurrent formation and degradation of fibrin, the principal protein constituting the structural scaffold of blood clots [1,2]. The transformation of fibrinogen into fibrin is marked by conformational alterations that result in exposition of binding sites for plasminogen (Pg) and tissue-type Pg activator (tPA), thereby promoting the efficient activation of Pg on the fibrin matrix [3,4]. The fibrinogen molecule is composed of a central E-region, two symmetrical outer D-regions, and two AαC-terminal regions (Aα220-610), also known as αC-regions, which extend beyond the D-regions [5,6]. It is well-documented that each fibrin(ogen) D-region contains two hidden sites, specifically the Aα148-160 and γ312-324 sequences, which interact with Pg and tPA, respectively. These sites become accessible due to structural rearrangements within the β- and γ-modules of the D-regions during fibrin polymerization. These rearrangements involve the formation of DDE complexes through the interaction of “А-а” and “В-b” complementary centers and the covalent cross-linking of γ-chains of adjacent fibrin molecules by factor XIIIa (fХІІІa) during protofibril formation [7,8].

Each αC-region comprises residues 221–610 of the Aα chain, which extend from the main molecule via peripheral disulfide rings. The αC-region includes a disordered segment (Aα221-390) that ensures its mobility, a compact structure (Aα390-589), and a disordered C-terminal section (Aα590-610) [9,10]. In the fibrinogen molecule, the compact modules of the αC-regions are non-covalently associated with each other and are positioned near the central E-region. During thrombin-initiated fibrinogen polymerization, the enzyme sequentially cleaves fibrinopeptides A and B, leading to the dissociation of the αC-regions from each other and their dissociation from the central E-region. This allows them to interact intermolecularly, enhancing the lateral aggregation of protofibrils [[11], [12], [13]]. Structural rearrangements in the αC-regions may also expose binding sites for fibrinolysis proteins, particularly since this part of the molecule is primarily hydrolyzed by plasmin, which forms on the fibrin surface [[14], [15], [16]]. Previous studies have shown that recombinant fragments corresponding to the complete sequence of the αC-region and its C-terminal part (Aα392-610) contain independent binding sites for Pg and tPA, which are cryptic in the fibrinogen molecule and stimulate Pg activation [17,18]. Both Pg and tPA bind to these αC-region recombinant fragments in a lysine-dependent manner [19,20]. Since the recombinant fragment lacks C-terminal lysines, two different lateral lysine residues, out of the 16 present, mediate the interaction. The precise localization of the binding sites for Pg and tPA in the fibrinogen αC-regions, which are cryptic in the fibrinogen molecule and presumably exposed during fibrin polymerization, remains undefined. Clarifying this will be crucial for understanding the role of αC-regions in the regulation of fibrinolysis.

The aim of the work was to obtain the C-terminal fragment of the Aα-chain of fibrinogen, which corresponds to the αC-region, and to investigate the interaction of plasminogen and its kringle-containing fragments with it. For this, the following tasks were defined. To perform early hydrolysis of fibrinogen with plasmin, since later plasmin fragments do not bind plasminogen, indicating that there is a plasminogen binding site at the C-terminus [20]. Then, to obtain C-terminal fragments of Aα-chains of fibrinogen using preparative electrophoresis. And, finally, to investigate the interaction of plasminogen and its kringle-containing fragments with the obtained αC-fragments of fibrinogen using immunoblot analysis, ELISA and mass spectrometry.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of plasminogen and its fragments

Glu-plasminogen (Glu-Pg) was isolated from citrated donor blood plasma, while Lys-plasminogen (Lys-Pg) was purified from the Cohn's fraction III2,3 of blood plasma (Kyiv City Blood Center, Ukraine) by affinity chromatography on Lys-Sepharose (Sigma Aldrich, USA) in the presence of aprotinin (Contrycal®, AWD, Germany) as described earlier [21]. Plasmin was generated by activating Glu-Pg according to the method [22]. Plasmin activity was assessed by casein hydrolysis [23].

Mini-plasminogen (mini-Pg) and Pg fragments K1-3 and K4 were produced using the protocols recommended in Ref. [24]. Fragment K5 was produced according to the method we developed for obtaining functionally active K5, as previously described [25]. K5 was then immobilized on BrCN-activated Sepharose 4B (GE Healthcare Bio-Science, Sweden) [26].

Preparation of fibrinogen and its fragments

Fibrinogen was isolated from donor blood plasma through salt fractionation using sodium sulfate [27]. Hydrolysis of fibrinogen was conducted by incubating it with plasmin (0.001–0.08 CU) in 0.05 M Tris-HCl buffer, pH 7.4, containing 0.15 M NaCl, at 25 °C for various time intervals. The fragmentation of fibrinogen was assessed by denaturing SDS-PAGE using 10 % polyacrylamide (PAAG) [28].

The αC fragments of 45 and 40 kDa were isolated from the plasmin lysate of fibrinogen by preparative electrophoresis in 10 % PAAG [28]. These fragments were then eluted from the corresponding gel bands using 0.1 % TFA and precipitated with acetone.

Polyclonal antibodies for immunochemical studies

Polyclonal antibodies (pAbs) were obtained from the immunoserum of rabbits immunized with the K5 fragment, using chromatography on K5-Sepharose 4B [26], and were employed in immunochemical assays. pAbs specific to K1-3, obtained as described previously [29], were used to detect bound Glu-Pg, Lys-Pg, K1-3, and K4. Anti-K5 pAbs were used for the detection of the K5 fragment.

Processing and testing of protein preparations

Protein solutions were dialyzed and concentrated employing Ultrafree-15 microconcentrators (Millipore, USA) at 4 °C similar to Ref. [25]. The purity and homogeneity of protein preparations were tested by SDS-PAGE using 10 % PAAG [27], utilizing molecular weight markers of 10 ÷ 260 kDa range (#SM1841, Spectra Multicolor Broad Range Protein Ladder, Fermentas, Lithuania). The homogeneity of the Glu- and Lys-plasminogen forms and the purity of plasmin were evaluated electrophretically in 11.5 % PAAG at pH 3.2 [30].

When determining the protein concentration, we used the difference in optical density of solutions at 280 and 320 nm, as well as the E2801 % values and molecular weights of the proteins under study. All of the used chemical reagents were qualified non less than chemically pure grade.

Western blot assay

Western blotting was employed to assess the binding of Glu-Pg, Lys-Pg, and K1-3, 4, and 5 with proteins from the plasmin lysate of fibrinogen, using polyclonal antibodies (pAbs) specific to K1-3 and K5 [31]. Following preblot electrophoresis, the proteins from the plasmin lysate were transferred from the gel onto a Hybond ECL nitrocellulose membrane (Amersham Pharmacia Biotech, Sweden) using the Mini Trans-Blot® Electrophoretic Transfer Cell (BioRad, USA). The transfer was performed and transferred proteins were studied for binding with Glu-Pg, Lys-Pg, or K1-3, 4, and 5 in accordance with our usual laboratory protocols [26]. We used pAbs to K1-3 for detecting of bound Glu-Pg, Lys-Pg, K1-3, or K4 and pAbs to K5 for detecting of bound K5 at a concentration of 10 μg/ml in TPBS. Conjugate solution of Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG-HRP (Sigma, USA), diluted 1:3000 in TPBS was used as a secondary antibodies. Following the corresponding washing procedure, the nitrocellulose membrane with the proteins under study was immersed in a substrate solution containing 0.25 M luminol solution in DMSO, a 0.09 M coumaric acid solution in DMSO, 0.1 M Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.5), and 0.0035 % H₂O₂. As method for immunostaining development, enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) was employed [32]. Autoradiography of the membranes was performed on X-ray films, with exposure times ranging from 1 s to 10 min, depending on the intensity of the chemiluminescence signal. To develop the immunoreactive zones, the film was treated with commercially available developer and fixer solutions [32].

ELISA

The interaction of Pg and its kringle-containing fragments with αC fragments of 45 and 40 kDa was evaluated using ELISA in accordance with our standart laboratory protocols [26]. We used pAbs to K1-3 for detecting Glu-Pg, Lys-Pg, K1-3, or K4 and pAbs to K5 for detecting K5 at a concentration of 10 μg/ml in TPBS. Conjugate solution of Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG-HRP (Sigma, USA), diluted 1:2500 in TPBS, was used as a secondary antibodies. The reaction was developed by 0.04 % o-phenylenediamine in 0.05 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 6.0) containing 0.03 % H₂O₂. The reaction was stopped by 2 M H₂SO₄. The optical density of the solutions was measured using a microplate reader (Titertek Multiskan MC, Finland) in dual-beam mode at 492 and 630 nm.

MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry

The composition of obtained protein bands at 45 kDa and 40 kDa was analyzed using mass spectrometry on MALDI-TOF Voyager DE PRO spectrometer (Serial no. 6393, Applied Biosystems, USA) in positive ion mode, covering a mass range (MH+) of 500–5000 Da. The spectrometer operated in reflex mode at applied voltage of 20 kV. For internal calibration used matrix peaks (MH+ 666.0293) and peptides of trypsin autolysis (MH+ 2163.0566, 2273.1599). Ionization of tryptic peptides was achieved under laser emission using Sinapinic acid (Sigma, USA) as matrix reagent (1 mg/ml in acetonitrile (Sigma, USA): 0.1 % TFA at 1:1 vol ratio). The tryptic peptides were characterized using online resource (https://web.expasy.org/peptide_mass/).

The interaction between Pg and tPA with polymeric fibrin is a crucial initial step in the fibrinolytic process. The primary function of the αC-regions is believed to be their involvement in the lateral association of protofibrils. Fibrin molecules from adjacent protofibrils interact through noncovalent contacts between the globular structures of the αC-regions, facilitating the convergence of protofibrils into fibrils. Additionally, the stability of the fibrin clot is ensured by covalent cross-linking of the αC regions of multiple fibrin monomers by factor XIIIa (fXIIIa). The interaction between Pg and tPA with the αC-regions has been demonstrated using recombinant forms of this molecular segment [20], suggesting a potential role in the regulation of fibrinolysis. However, the specific amino acid sequences within the αC-regions responsible for this interaction have not yet been identified. To elucidate the localization of Pg binding sites, we investigated the interaction of Pg and its isolated kringle domains with fibrinogen αC-regions.

Hydrolysis of fibrinogen by plasmin leading to the formation of αC-region fragments

Initially, it was essential to determine the optimal conditions for the early stages of fibrinogen hydrolysis by plasmin, which result in the formation of fragments corresponding to the αC-regions. Plasmin hydrolyzes both fibrinogen and non-crosslinked polymeric fibrin through several successive stages, producing fragments X, Y, D, and E [33,36]. The optimal conditions for fibrinogen hydrolysis by plasmin were previously established (data not shown). Hydrolysis for 10 min at a plasmin-to-fibrinogen molar ratio of 1:1,000, or for 30 min at a molar ratio of 1:2250 in the presence of 10 mM ε-ACA at 25 °C, yielded fibrinogen fragments with molecular weights of 40 and 45 kDa, while Y- and D-fragments were not detected. These conditions were employed for the plasmin hydrolysis of human fibrinogen (Fig. 2). The electrophoretic analysis shows that fibrinogen fragments with molecular weights of 40 kDa and 45 kDa were present in the incubation medium, with no detectable Y- and D-fragments. Discussion

After the cleavage of fibrinopeptides A, the αC regions separate from each other, exposing the A centers, but remain connected to fibrinopeptides B and possibly participate in primary fibrin polymerization. Following the cleavage of fibrinopeptides B, the αC regions detach from the backbone of the molecule, accompanied by the transition from intramolecular to intermolecular bonds. This process is involved in fibrin polymerization, accelerating the lateral association of protofibrils and fibrils [21]. During these events, the αC regions expose sites of binding to certain proteins and cells involved in hemostasis and inflammatory responses [7]. Notably, the amino acid residues with side chains that enable the binding of Pg fragments K1-3, K4, and K5 are located in close proximity within the αC region. It is hypothesized that the exposure of the α581Ser-610Val sequence during the fibrinogen-to-fibrin conversion leads to the primary binding of the zymogen molecule to the αC region through the LBS of K5. This interaction is necessary for the transition of the zymogen into an “open” conformation, allowing K1-3 to bind to nearby α583Lys, α601Lys, or α606Lys. This changes the conformation of plasminogen in such a way that the peptide bond Arg561-Val562 of its activation loop is presented to the tissue activator. As a consequence of these events, an active proteinase is formed. Therefore, zymogen activation requires its spatiotemporal interaction with fibrin molecule through the LBS of K1-3 and K5, which corresponds to our previous hypothesis [37]. However, the role of kringle 4 in binding to this fragment requires further elucidation.

In this study, we developed a molecular model that provided novel insights into the localization of binding sites for Pg and its fragments K1-3, K4, and K5 with fibrin(ogen) αC truncated polypeptides. The combination of enzymatic, immunochemical, and mass-spectrometric approaches proved valuable in elucidating the functional significance of the conformational features of fibrinogen and protein-protein interactions in fibrinolysis. Our findings contribute to fundamental research on hemostasis and hold potential for therapeutic applications in treating cardiovascular diseases associated with fibrinolytic system dysfunction.

The novelty of our approaches to solving the problem is as follows. We performed proteolytic degradation of the entire human fibrinogen molecule by plasmin, yielding fibrinogen fragments with molecular weights of 40 kDa (α225Val-580Lys) and 45 kDa (α225Val-610Val), while Y- and D-fragments were not detected. Thus, we obtained early products of fibrinogen proteolytic degradation by plasmin, which effectively models the initial stages of fibrinolysis. Using preparative SDS-PAGE, we isolated the polypeptide bands of 45 kDa and 40 kDa. By means of MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry, it was determined that the 45 kDa fragment contains the α225Val-610Val sequence (αC45) of the fibrin(ogen) Aα chain, corresponding to the alphaC-region, while the 40 kDa fragment contains the α225Val-580Lys sequence (αC40). By ELISA and Western blot, we studied the binding of not only plasminogen but also its isolated kringle fragments K1-3, K4, and K5 with the obtained human fibrinogen fragments αC45 (α225Val-610Val) and αC40 (α225Val-580Lys). We used previously obtained antibodies to K1-3 and K5 as a research tool. We established that plasminogen and its kringles bind with the αC45 (α225Val-610Val), unlike the αC40 (α225Val-580Lys). Thus, as a result of our work, we found that the α581Ser-610Val sequence of the fibrin(ogen) alphaC-region is responsible for plasminogen binding. The novelty and originality of our results lie in the fact that we have significantly narrowed the plasminogen binding region within the fibrinogen alphaC-region to the α581Ser-610Val sequence, containing only 30 amino acid residues. This sequence includes five amino acid residues (α583Lys, α601Lys, α602Arg, α606Lys, α608Arg), whose side chains may contribute to interactions with the lysine-binding sites (LBS) of kringles.

Conclusions and implication

Our study revealed that Pg and its fragments K1-3, K4, and K5 interact exclusively with the 45 kDa fragment (225Val-610Val) of the fibrinogen αC region with high affinity in a concentration-dependent manner (Kd values for Glu-Pg = 7.10 × 10−9 M, Lys-Pg = 6.01 × 10−9 M, K1-3 = 1.08 × 10−7 M, K4 = 5.06 × 10−7 M, and K5 = 2.50 × 10−7 M). Unlike the 40 kDa (225Val-580Lys) counterpart, this fragment contains the sequence α581Ser-610Val, proving that this specific region is essential for the formation of complexes between Pg and the fibrin(ogen) αC regions. These results highlight the crucial role of the α581Ser-610Val sequence of the fibrinogen Aα-chain in forming complexes with Pg.

Thus, the detailed interaction between fragments of Pg and fibrinogen αC deepens our understanding of the molecular mechanisms of the initiation of fibrinolysis. Identifying specific binding sites and interactions can lead to the development of targeted therapies for disorders related to fibrinolysis, such as thrombosis or hemophilia. The high-affinity interactions and specific binding constants could be used to develop more precise diagnostic assays for detecting abnormalities in fibrinogen or Pg function. Insights into the structural requirements for Pg activation can aid in designing drugs that modulate this process, potentially leading to new treatments for clotting disorders. Understanding these interactions can inform the design of biomaterials that mimic natural clotting processes, which could be used in surgical applications or wound dressings. These implications highlight the potential for translating these molecular insights into practical applications in medicine and biotechnology.
