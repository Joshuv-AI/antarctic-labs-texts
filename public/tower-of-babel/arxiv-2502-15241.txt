Looking at bare transport coefficients in fluctuating hydrodynamics
Hiroyoshi Nakano, Yuki Minami, Keiji Saito

Abstract
Bare transport coefficients in fluctuating hydrodynamics are not directly observable in bulk systems, as hydrodynamic fluctuations inevitably renormalize them into macroscopic values. In this work, we propose an operational method to determine the bare shear viscosity in two-dimensional dense fluids by focusing on fluid behavior near solid boundaries, where momentum scattering suppresses long-wavelength fluctuations. Using fluctuating hydrodynamic and molecular dynamics simulations supported by analytical arguments, we show that the viscosity measured near walls directly corresponds to the bare value. Based on this observation, we construct a practical protocol to extract the bare viscosity from microscopic data and verify its consistency by predicting flow profiles and equilibrium correlations. We further demonstrate that fluctuating hydrodynamics quantitatively reproduces fluid behavior down to atomic length scales.

In this paper, we propose an operational method to determine the bare shear viscosity in two-dimensional (2D) dense fluids.
We use molecular dynamics simulations of particles confined to two dimensions as a representative system.
Determining the bare viscosity from microscopic dynamics has remained challenging, particularly in 2D where macroscopic transport coefficients exhibit system-size-dependent behavior.
Because our proposed method is operational, it can in principle be applied beyond numerical simulations.

Our key idea is to focus on fluid behavior near solid boundaries rather than in the bulk.
We show, using fluctuating hydrodynamic simulations and perturbative analysis, that solid walls suppress the long-wavelength fluctuations responsible for renormalization.
As a result, the local fluid dynamics near the wall is governed by the bare viscosity.
This suppression can be understood as a consequence of momentum scattering at the boundary, which breaks local momentum conservation and inhibits the development of long-range velocity fluctuations.

Based on this observation, we propose an operational protocol to determine the bare viscosity directly from microscopic dynamics.
By fitting flow profiles observed in MD simulations to fluctuating hydrodynamics, the bare viscosity can be extracted.
The resulting value consistently describes independent flow configurations, confirming its physical validity.

In addition to determining the bare viscosity, we examine the role of the spatial discretization scale, hereafter referred to as the ultraviolet (UV) cutoff length, which acts as a parameter in fluctuating hydrodynamics.
Our results show that this parameter affects the quantitative description of transport, reflecting the scale at which the coarse-grained hydrodynamic description is defined.
We find that fluctuating hydrodynamics quantitatively reproduces fluid behavior down to length scales comparable to the atomic diameter.
This supports the interpretation of the bare viscosity as a well-defined parameter associated with the smallest physically relevant scale.

II.1
Microscopic and mesoscopic models

At the microscopic level, we model the 2D fluid as a classical
N
-particle Hamiltonian system.
We use a repulsive soft-core potential

\displaystyle V(r)=\begin{cases}k(\sigma-r)^{\alpha}&r<\sigma,\\
0&r\geq\sigma.\end{cases}

(1)

Here,
k
is the interaction strength,
\sigma
is the particle diameter, and
\alpha
is the exponent characterizing the steepness of the repulsion.

At the mesoscopic level, we assume that the system is described by fluctuating hydrodynamics for an isothermal 2D fluid.
The density field
\rho(\bm{r},t)
and velocity field
\bm{v}(\bm{r},t)
evolve as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho(\bm{r},t)}{\partial t}=-\nabla\cdot(\rho\bm{v}),

(2)

\displaystyle\rho\left[\frac{\partial\bm{v}}{\partial t}+(\bm{v}\cdot\nabla)\bm{v}\right]

\displaystyle=-\nabla p+\eta_{0}\nabla^{2}\bm{v}+\zeta_{0}\nabla(\nabla\cdot\bm{v})-\nabla\cdot\bm{\Pi}^{\mathrm{ran}},

(3)

where
p(\bm{r},t)
is the pressure field.
The random stress tensor
\bm{\Pi}^{\mathrm{ran}}(\bm{r},t)
is Gaussian white noise satisfying the fluctuation-dissipation relation

\displaystyle\big\langle\bm{\Pi}^{\mathrm{ran}}_{ab}(\bm{r},t)\bm{\Pi}^{\mathrm{ran}}_{cd}(\bm{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\big\rangle

\displaystyle=2k_{B}T\delta(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})\biggl[\eta_{0}\left(\delta_{ac}\delta_{bd}+\delta_{ad}\delta_{bc}\right)+\left(\zeta_{0}-\eta_{0}\right)\delta_{ab}\delta_{cd}\biggr],

(4)

where
T
is the temperature and
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant.

Equation (
3
) corresponds to the Navier–Stokes equation with an additional stochastic stress
\bm{\Pi}^{\mathrm{ran}}(\bm{r},t)
.
We then define the noise-averaged stress tensor from the momentum flux as
1
1

1

In fluctuating hydrodynamics, the stress is defined as an extension of the deterministic stress
[
1
]
.

\displaystyle\langle\bm{\sigma}_{ab}\rangle=-\langle\bm{\Pi}_{ab}\rangle+\langle\rho\rangle\langle v_{a}\rangle\langle v_{b}\rangle.

(5)

with

\displaystyle\bm{\Pi}_{ab}

\displaystyle=\rho v_{a}v_{b}+p\delta_{ab}-\eta_{0}\left(\partial_{a}v_{b}+\partial_{b}v_{a}\right)-(\zeta_{0}-\eta_{0})\delta_{ab}\nabla\cdot\bm{v}+\bm{\Pi}^{\mathrm{ran}}_{ab}.

(6)

In this paper, we consider a dense fluid where density variations are small and focus on the bare shear viscosity
\eta_{0}
.
To this end, in the theoretical analysis, we consider the incompressible limit by isolating the transverse momentum dynamics.
In simulations, we solve the compressible equations with
p=c_{T}^{2}\rho
and set
c_{T}^{2}=1000
and
\zeta_{0}=1

As shown later, the agreement with MD results suggests that this choice of

does not affect the extraction of
\eta_{0}

II.2
Hydrodynamic behavior at the macroscopic scale

While fluid motion inherently exhibits thermal fluctuations, macroscopic experimental observations probe noise-averaged quantities.
The noise-averaged shear stress
\langle\bm{\sigma}_{xy}\rangle
is related to the noise-averaged velocity
\langle\bm{v}\rangle
as:

\displaystyle\langle\bm{\sigma}_{xy}\rangle=\eta_{\textrm{obs}}\biggl(\frac{\partial\langle v^{x}\rangle}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial\langle v^{y}\rangle}{\partial x}\biggr).

(7)

This defines the observed viscosity
\eta_{\textrm{obs}}
.
Importantly,
\eta_{\textrm{obs}}
differs from the bare viscosity
\eta_{0}

[
57
,
38
,
39
]
.
As shown in MCT and RG analyses, the observed viscosity
\eta_{\textrm{obs}}
is decomposed as

\displaystyle\eta_{\textrm{obs}}=\eta_{0}+\delta\eta,

(8)

and
\delta\eta
arises from the nonlinear coupling of hydrodynamic fluctuations.
Historically,
\delta\eta
is referred to as the mode-coupling contribution or the renormalization correction.

In particular, the correction
\delta\eta
has been explicitly evaluated in bulk fluids
[
57
,
38
,
39
]
.
In two dimensions,
\delta\eta
diverges logarithmically with system size
[
58
,
57
,
38
,
39
,
59
]
.

\displaystyle\delta\eta\sim\log L\quad\text{as}\quad L\to\infty.

(9)

Thus,
\eta_{\textrm{obs}}
depends on system size, whereas
\eta_{0}
is a system-size-independent quantity determined by microscopic interactions.
A brief review of this anomalous behavior is given in Appendix
A
.

In the following, all quantities are expressed in reduced units based on the atomic system, where the atomic mass
m
, the atomic diameter
\sigma
, and the temperature
T
are set to
1
.
We fix the mean density
\rho_{0}=0.765
to focus on a dense fluid.
We also introduce a UV cutoff length
a_{\mathrm{uv}}
as the unit of spatial discretization in fluctuating hydrodynamics.
The decomposition in Eq. (
8
) depends on the UV cutoff length
a_{\mathrm{uv}}
in
d\geq 2

[

]
.
Except in the final section (Sec.
V
), we set

, corresponding to the particle diameter, and refer to
\eta_{0}
defined with this choice as the bare viscosity; see Sec.
V
for details.

Notably, near the walls,

This is shown in Fig.

We now discuss the physical origin of this behavior.
From a technical standpoint in our simulations, the no-slip boundary condition [Eqs. (
10
)-(
12
)] effectively eliminates velocity fluctuations at the wall, leading to a strong reduction of the fluctuation-induced correction
\delta\eta
.
However, we emphasize that this suppression is not merely an artifact of the chosen boundary condition, but originates fundamentally from momentum scattering at the solid boundary.
In the bulk, fluctuation-induced corrections are driven by long-wavelength velocity fluctuations, which rely on local momentum conservation.
At the boundary, in contrast, collisions with the wall allow fluid momentum exchange with the solid boundary and suppress the long-wavelength fluctuations responsible for the mode-coupling effect.
This suppression of macroscopic fluctuations is the fundamental cause of the observed behavior.
Consistent with this picture, a similar suppression of fluctuations is observed in the MD simulations presented in Sec.
IV
.

III.2
Analytical expressions for the velocity profile and local viscosity

We here develop a perturbative theory to derive analytical expressions for the quantities observed in the simulations.
We consider an incompressible fluid with boundary conditions corresponding to Eqs. (
10
)–(
12
); details are given in Appendix
D
.
Below we present the results.

First, the mean velocity is along the
y
axis and depends only on
x
.
The perturbative calculation gives

\displaystyle\langle v^{y}(\bm{r})\rangle=\dot{\gamma}\left[x+\frac{A}{L}\sum_{k_{x}}\frac{1}{k_{x}}\frac{\sin(2k_{x}x)}{2k_{x}}\right]

(13)

with

\displaystyle A=\frac{\rho_{0}k_{B}T}{4\eta_{0}^{2}},

(14)

where
\dot{\gamma}:=U/L
,
k_{x}:=(\pi/L)n_{x}
and the summation is carried out from
n_{x}=1
to
n_{x}=n_{x}^{\rm max}:=L/a_{\textrm{uv}}
.
Force balance makes the shear stress
\langle\sigma^{xy}(\bm{r})\rangle
independent of
\bm{r}
:

\displaystyle\langle\sigma^{xy}\rangle

\displaystyle=\eta_{0}\dot{\gamma}\biggl[1+\frac{A}{L}\sum_{k_{x}}\frac{1}{k_{x}}\biggr].

(15)

Combining the shear stress Eq. (
15
) and the velocity profile Eq. (
13
), the observed viscosity is analytically given as

\displaystyle\eta_{\textrm{obs}}(x)

\displaystyle=\eta_{0}\biggl[1+2\frac{A}{L}\sum_{k_{x}}\frac{1}{k_{x}}\sin^{2}(k_{x}x)\biggr].

(16)

This equation provides the explicit expression of the decomposition in Eq. (
8
).
The second terms in Eqs. (
13
), (
15
), and (
16
) are fluctuation-induced contributions, which lead to spatially non-uniform velocity gradients and viscosity.
In particular, at the midpoint
x=L/2
, the observed viscosity is calculated as:

\displaystyle\eta_{\textrm{obs}}(x=L/2)

\displaystyle\sim\eta_{0}\biggl(1+\frac{A}{\pi}\int^{2\pi/a_{\textrm{uv}}}_{2\pi/L}dk_{x}\frac{1}{k_{x}}\biggr)

(17)

\displaystyle=\eta_{0}\biggl[1+\frac{A}{\pi}\log\biggl(\frac{L}{a_{\textrm{uv}}}\biggr)\biggr].

(18)

Thus,
\delta\eta(x=L/2):=\eta_{\textrm{obs}}(x=L/2)-\eta_{0}
diverges logarithmically as
L\to\infty
.
In addition, the bare viscosity is directly observed at the walls:

\displaystyle\eta_{\textrm{obs}}(x=0)=\eta_{\textrm{obs}}(x=L)=\eta_{0}.

(19)

Equations (
16
), (
18
), and (
19
) provide mathematical confirmation of our simulation results and support the central observation that the near-wall noise-averaged behavior is governed by the bare viscosity.

We check the validity of Eqs. (
13
)–(
16
).
Revisiting Fig.
1
(a), we compare the velocity field with the theoretical prediction.
To this end, we fit Eq. (
13
) to the simulation data for
L=64
, using
\dot{\gamma}
and
A
as fitting parameters.
The theoretical expression is in good agreement with the numerical result.
The fitted value of
A
does not exactly match the theoretical prediction [Eq. (
14
)], reflecting the approximations made in the perturbative analysis.

A similar comparison for the local viscosity is shown in Fig.
1
(c) and the inset of (e).
Fitting Eq. (
16
) with
\eta_{0}
and
A
again yields excellent agreement with the simulation results, even near the walls.
Despite the discrepancy in
A
, the fitted value of
\eta_{0}=0.128
remains close to the simulation value
\eta_{0}=0.100
, indicating that the near-wall structure of
\eta_{R}(x)
enables a robust extraction of
\eta_{0}
.

IV
Measurement protocols of the bare viscosity in molecular dynamics

Building on the results of Sec.
III
, we develop operational protocols to measure the bare viscosity in molecular dynamics.

IV.1
Setup of atomic systems

To determine the bare viscosity from the atomic description, we perform MD simulations of the atomic system introduced in Sec.
II.1
.
Our approach involves fitting the near-wall behavior of the fluid in the Couette geometry using fluctuating hydrodynamics.
This method is illustrated in Fig.
2
(a).

In our MD simulations, the walls are modeled as particles arranged on a square lattice.
They are confined by an onsite potential and thermostatted via a Langevin thermostat to maintain the temperature.
Wall–fluid interactions are taken to be purely repulsive in the main text, mimicking a hydrophobic surface.
Details of the interaction potentials and simulation parameters are provided in Appendix
E
, and we confirm in Appendix
F
that the results are robust for other types of microscopic walls.

IV.2
Measurement protocol of bare viscosity

The protocol is based on the theoretical result that the fluctuation-induced correction vanishes at the wall and remains strongly suppressed in the near-wall region (Sec.
III
).
The concrete procedure consists of the following steps:

Protocol 1

1.

Measure
\eta_{\textrm{obs}}(x)
in atomic systems.

2.

Fit obtained
\eta_{\textrm{obs}}(x)
with the results of fluctuating hydrodynamics simulations.

Figure
2
(b) shows a fitting result for the interparticle potential
V(\delta)=10\delta^{2}
.
To ensure dimensional consistency, we match
\rho_{0}
,
k_{B}T
, and
L
between the two descriptions and treat
\eta_{0}
as the only fitting parameter.
In practice,
\eta_{0}
is varied in increments of
0.005
to identify the value that best reproduces the data.
As shown in this figure, the best-fitted curve accurately reproduces the MD data near the wall, yielding a bare viscosity of
\eta_{0}=0.325
for this fluid.
This agreement supports the interpretation that the fitted parameter corresponds to the bare viscosity.

While this protocol provides an accurate estimate of
\eta_{0}
, numerically solving the fluctuating hydrodynamic equations can be computationally demanding.
To address this limitation, we introduce a simplified protocol based on the analytical expression derived in Sec.
III.2
.

Protocol 2

1.

Measure
\eta_{\textrm{obs}}(x)
in atomic systems.

2.

Fit the obtained
\eta_{\textrm{obs}}(x)
to the analytical expression Eq. (
16
), using
\eta_{0}
and
A
as fitting parameters.

Figure
2
(c) shows the application of Protocol 2 to the same atomic system as Protocol 1.
The analytical expression Eq. (
16
) with the fitted
\eta_{0}
(black line) closely follows the MD data (red circles).
Protocol 2 yields
\eta_{0}=0.304
, consistent with the value obtained from Protocol 1.

We further apply both protocols to other atomic systems.
As shown in Appendix
G
, we confirm that this approach is successful for all the systems investigated.
As summarized in Table
1
, the value of
\eta_{0}
obtained from Protocol 2 is quantitatively consistent with that from Protocol 1 across different systems, validating this computationally efficient approach.
However, since Eq. (
16
) is derived from perturbation theory, small deviations from the more accurate Protocol 1 values can arise.
The accuracy of Protocol 2 improves as
\delta\eta/\eta_{0}
decreases, consistent with the perturbative nature of Eq. (
16
).

Validity of our protocols

To verify that the extracted
\eta_{0}
is an intrinsic material constant rather than an artifact of the Couette geometry, we test its predictive capability in different setups.
We first consider Poiseuille flow, where the fluid is confined between two fixed parallel walls and driven by a constant body force.
Figure

We observe that the fluctuating hydrodynamics prediction accurately reproduces the MD results, which demonstrates that the extracted

This successful agreement also implies that one can alternatively extract

V
Role of the UV cutoff length on fluctuating hydrodynamics

Fluctuating hydrodynamics describes fluid motion coarse-grained over a length scale larger than the UV cutoff
a_{\textrm{uv}}
.
While
a_{\textrm{uv}}
was fixed to the atomic diameter
\sigma
in the previous sections, it should be regarded as a hydrodynamic parameter absent in the microscopic MD description.
We therefore investigate the impact of varying
a_{\textrm{uv}}
in this section.
In the following,
\eta_{0}
denotes the input viscosity in the fluctuating hydrodynamic framework.

We first examine the effect of
a_{\textrm{uv}}
at fixed input viscosity
\eta_{0}
.
Figure
3
shows that the shear stress
\langle\sigma^{xy}\rangle
increases as
a_{\textrm{uv}}
decreases.
This demonstrates that
a_{\textrm{uv}}
directly affects macroscopic observables by controlling the range of fluctuations included in the description.

The framework of fluctuating hydrodynamics resembles that of quantum field theory rather than deterministic hydrodynamics.
Deterministic hydrodynamics lacks fluctuations and renormalization effects, and is therefore independent of
a_{\textrm{uv}}
.
In contrast, fluctuating hydrodynamics and quantum field theory include thermal and quantum fluctuations, respectively, leading to renormalization effects.
In quantum field theory, Wilson’s RG theory provides a systematic way to change the scale
\Lambda:=2\pi/a_{\textrm{uv}}

[
63
,
64
,
65
,
66
]
.
When
\Lambda
is changed and the parameters are tuned along the RG flow, the model describes the same physical system at different resolutions.

Similarly, in fluctuating hydrodynamics, the input viscosity becomes a scale-dependent quantity
\eta_{0}(a_{\textrm{uv}})
, which provides the appropriate hydrodynamic description for a given cutoff
a_{\textrm{uv}}
.
To demonstrate this, we consider the atomic system with the interparticle potential
V(\delta)=10\delta^{4}
.
We vary
a_{\textrm{uv}}
within
2/3\leq a_{\textrm{uv}}\leq 8/3
and determine the optimal
\eta_{0}
for each value using Protocol 1.
The resulting relation
\eta_{0}(a_{\textrm{uv}})
is shown in Fig.
4
(a), and Figs.
4
(b)–(d) confirm that fluctuating hydrodynamics with these parameter pairs accurately reproduces the MD results.

As
a_{\textrm{uv}}
increases, fine-scale structures near the wall are progressively smoothed out.
Nevertheless, the agreement with MD simulations remains robust.
These results indicate that
\eta_{0}(a_{\textrm{uv}})
plays a role analogous to an RG flow.
Importantly, this flow is obtained here through direct numerical simulations, rather than through an explicit RG calculation.

Finally, we examine the behavior of
\eta_{0}(a_{\textrm{uv}})
at small
a_{\textrm{uv}}
.
The numerical results are well fitted by a logarithmic form,
\eta_{0}(a_{\textrm{uv}})=C_{0}+C_{1}\log a_{\textrm{uv}}
, indicating a logarithmic divergence as
a_{\textrm{uv}}\to 0
(see Appendix
A
).
Such ultraviolet (UV) divergences are also common in quantum field theory.
In fluctuating hydrodynamics, however, this divergence is not problematic because the description is valid only above a minimum length scale
a_{\textrm{bare}}
.
This lower bound reflects the breakdown of the hydrodynamic description below microscopic scales.
We therefore define the bare viscosity as
\eta_{\rm bare}:=\eta_{0}(a_{\textrm{bare}})

2
2

2

In quantum field theory, the term
bare parameter
typically refers to the parameter in the continuum limit, such as
\lim_{a_{\textrm{uv}}\to 0}\eta_{0}(a_{\textrm{uv}})
, which diverges.
.

Conventional arguments often associate
a_{\textrm{bare}}
with the mean free path.
In the dense fluids considered here, the mean free path is comparable to the atomic diameter
\sigma
.
Our numerical results (Figs.
2
and
4
) demonstrate that fluctuating hydrodynamics remains valid down to this scale.
Moreover, the logarithmic dependence of
\eta_{0}(a_{\textrm{uv}})
on
a_{\textrm{uv}}
implies weak sensitivity to the precise choice of the cutoff.
Taken together, we suggest that the bare scale can be identified with the atomic diameter,
a_{\textrm{bare}}\simeq\sigma
, and thus
\eta_{\rm bare}=\eta_{0}(\sigma)
.
Importantly, we emphasize that this identification is not an assumption but a direct numerical finding of the present study.
Thus, throughout this paper, we understood
\eta_{0}=\eta_{0}(\sigma)
as the bare viscosity.

This identification should be understood within the steady-state settings studied here.
In general, the bare scale
a_{\textrm{bare}}
may depend on the physical process and could differ, for example, in time-dependent flows.
The identification
a_{\textrm{bare}}\simeq\sigma
in steady states may be related to the fact that time averaging effectively coarse-grains stochastic atomic motion.
Establishing a general criterion for determining
a_{\textrm{bare}}
remains an important open problem for future research.

VI
Discussions and concluding remarks

In this study, we developed operational protocols for determining the bare viscosity
\eta_{0}
of fluctuating hydrodynamics from microscopic dynamics.
The central idea is that solid boundaries suppress the hydrodynamic fluctuations responsible for renormalizing the viscosity in the bulk.
As a result, the near-wall response retains direct information on
\eta_{0}
.
We demonstrated this mechanism using direct numerical simulations of fluctuating hydrodynamics.
We then used this near-wall behavior as the basis for determining
\eta_{0}
in MD simulations.
From the noise-averaged one-body observables in Couette flow, we extracted
\eta_{0}
and confirmed that the same value describes Poiseuille flow and equilibrium momentum-density correlations.

We also clarified the role of the UV cutoff length
a_{\textrm{uv}}
in fluctuating hydrodynamics.
Changing
a_{\textrm{uv}}
changes the input viscosity required to reproduce the same atomic system, so that
\eta_{0}(a_{\textrm{uv}})
plays the role of an RG flow.
For the steady states studied here, fluctuating hydrodynamics remains quantitatively accurate down to
a_{\textrm{uv}}
of the order of the atomic diameter.
This supports the identification of the bare scale as
a_{\textrm{bare}}\simeq\sigma
and the bare viscosity as
\eta_{\rm bare}=\eta_{0}(\sigma)
.
Whether the same identification holds for time-dependent phenomena remains an open question.

VI.1
Three-dimensional systems

Although fluctuation-induced corrections are generally smaller in three dimensions, the distinction between the bare viscosity in fluctuating hydrodynamics and the macroscopic viscosity in deterministic hydrodynamics remains conceptually important.
The boundary-based protocol developed here can also be applied to three-dimensional fluids.
For example, using the perturbation theory developed in Sec.
III.2
and Appendix
D
, the observed viscosity in three dimensions is obtained as

\displaystyle\eta_{\textrm{obs}}(x)

\displaystyle=\eta_{0}\biggl[1+2\frac{A}{L}\sum_{k_{x}}\mathrm{arcsinh}\biggl(\frac{\Lambda}{k_{x}}\biggr)\sin^{2}\biggl(k_{x}x\biggr)\biggr]

(21)

with
\Lambda=2\pi/a_{\textrm{uv}}
.
The fluctuation-induced term again vanishes at the wall because of the factor
\sin^{2}(k_{x}x)
.
Thus, high-resolution measurements near solid boundaries should provide access to
\eta_{0}
in three-dimensional fluids as well.

VI.2
Experimental realization

We finally discuss possible experimental realizations of the present protocol.
Classical examples are soap films and free-standing smectic liquid-crystal films, which have been used to study two-dimensional turbulence
[
68
,
69
]
and the Stokes paradox
[
70
]
.
Vacuum-suspended liquid-crystal mono- or bilayers are particularly relevant because friction with the surrounding medium is strongly reduced, making them closer to an isolated two-dimensional fluid
[
51
]
.

Electron fluids provide another class of two-dimensional hydrodynamic systems.
Hydrodynamic transport has been reported in graphene and related clean materials
[
52
,
53
,
54
,
55
]
, and boundary scattering can be controlled in mesoscopic devices
[
71
]
.
For both classical films and electron fluids, improved control of boundary conditions may make it possible to test the fluctuation-suppression mechanism discussed in this paper.
Although such experimental applications remain a future challenge, the present protocol already provides a direct way to determine bare transport coefficients in MD simulations.

Finally, the boundary-based idea is not limited to viscosity.
In one- and two-dimensional heat-conduction systems, divergent thermal conductivities have been observed in carbon nanotubes and graphene
[
72
,
73
,
74
,
75
]
.
Extending the present approach to bare thermal conductivity is a possible direction for future work.

Appendix A
Properties of observed viscosity in bulk region

This appendix summarizes the bulk behavior of the observed viscosity
\eta_{\textrm{obs}}
from the standard long-time-tail perspective.
In contrast to the boundary-driven shear flows considered in the main text, we here consider an equilibrium fluid in a periodic domain and recall how hydrodynamic fluctuations renormalize the viscosity measured by the Green–Kubo formula.

We consider a
d
-dimensional periodic box of linear size
L
.
The observed shear viscosity is given by the Green–Kubo relation

\displaystyle\eta_{\textrm{obs}}=\frac{L^{d}}{k_{B}T}\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\left\langle\bm{\pi}_{xy}(s)\bm{\pi}_{xy}(0)\right\rangle_{\rm eq}.

(22)

Here,
\bm{\pi}_{xy}(s)
denotes the spatial average of the
xy
component of the momentum flux,

\displaystyle\bm{\pi}_{xy}(s):=\frac{1}{L^{d}}\int d^{d}\bm{r}\bm{\Pi}_{xy}(\bm{r},s),

(23)

where
\bm{\Pi}_{xy}(\bm{r},s)
is given by Eq. (
6
).

Linearized fluctuating hydrodynamics predicts three regimes for the equilibrium stress correlation in Eq. (
22
)
[
57
,
77
]
:

\left\langle\bm{\pi}_{xy}(t)\bm{\pi}_{xy}(0)\right\rangle_{\rm eq}\sim\begin{cases}2\eta_{0}L^{-d}k_{B}T\delta(t),&0<t\ll a_{\textrm{uv}}^{2}/\eta_{0},\\[4.0pt]
C_{\rm LT}L^{-d}t^{-d/2},&a_{\textrm{uv}}^{2}/\eta_{0}\ll t\ll L^{2}/\eta_{0},\\[4.0pt]
0,&L^{2}/\eta_{0}\ll t,\end{cases}

(24)

where
C_{\rm LT}
is the amplitude of the long-time tail.
The short-time delta function follows from the fluctuation-dissipation relation for the random stress [Eq. (
4
)].
The intermediate regime is the long-time tail,
t^{-d/2}
, which originates from the slow relaxation of hydrodynamic modes.
This tail is also observed in our equilibrium simulations [Fig.
2
(e)].
Substituting Eq. (
24
) into the Green–Kubo relation gives the leading correction to
\eta_{\textrm{obs}}
.
For
d=2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\eta_{\textrm{obs}}

\displaystyle\simeq\eta_{0}+\frac{2C_{\rm LT}}{k_{B}T}\log(L/a_{\textrm{uv}}).

(25)

For
d>2
, the corresponding result is

\displaystyle\eta_{\textrm{obs}}

\displaystyle\simeq\eta_{0}+\frac{2C_{\rm LT}}{(d-2)k_{B}T}\biggl[\left(\frac{\eta_{0}}{a_{\textrm{uv}}^{2}}\right)^{d/2-1}-\left(\frac{\eta_{0}}{L^{2}}\right)^{d/2-1}\biggr].

(26)

These estimates capture the leading dependence of
\eta_{\textrm{obs}}
on the system size
L
and the UV cutoff
a_{\textrm{uv}}
.
In two dimensions, Eq. (
25
) shows the logarithmic growth of
\eta_{\textrm{obs}}
with
L
, consistent with our fluctuating hydrodynamic simulations [Figs.
1
(e) and
3
].
For
d>2
, Eq. (
26
) still contains a finite-size correction, but
\eta_{\textrm{obs}}
converges to a finite value as
L\to\infty
.
This limiting value corresponds to the viscosity used in macroscopic hydrodynamics.

Equations (
25
) and (
26
) also show that
\eta_{\textrm{obs}}
depends on the UV cutoff
a_{\textrm{uv}}
.
As
a_{\textrm{uv}}\to 0^{+}
, the correction diverges logarithmically in two dimensions and algebraically for
d>2
.
This cutoff dependence corresponds to an ultraviolet (UV) divergence in the field-theoretic sense.
It is not a physical pathology, because fluctuating hydrodynamics is an effective coarse-grained theory defined only above a microscopic cutoff scale.
As discussed in Sec.
V
, the cutoff dependence should instead be interpreted as the scale dependence of the bare transport coefficient.
For a general discussion of UV divergences and dimensionality in field theory, see Ref.
[
63
,
60
]
.

B.1
Equations used in the solver

For the numerical implementation, we rewrite the equations in terms of the density

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot\bm{j},

(27)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial j_{a}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\bm{\nabla}\cdot(j_{a}\bm{v})-\bm{\nabla}_{a}p(\rho)+\eta_{0}\bm{\nabla}^{2}v_{a}+\zeta_{0}\bm{\nabla}_{a}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{v})+\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\Pi}^{\mathrm{ran}}_{a},

The variables are placed on the staggered grid shown in Fig.

\displaystyle(j_{x})_{i+1/2,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{i,j}+\rho_{i+1,j}}{2}(v_{x})_{i+1/2,j},

(34)

\displaystyle(j_{y})_{i,j+1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{i,j}+\rho_{i,j+1}}{2}(v_{y})_{i,j+1/2}.

(35)

\displaystyle(j_{x}v_{x})_{i,j}

(37)

\displaystyle(j_{y}v_{x})_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left[(j_{y})_{i,j-1/2}+(j_{y})_{i,j+1/2}\right]\times\left[(v_{x})_{i-1/2,j}+(v_{x})_{i+1/2,j}\right],

(38)

\displaystyle(j_{y}v_{y})_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left[(j_{y})_{i,j-1/2}+(j_{y})_{i,j+1/2}\right]\times\left[(v_{y})_{i,j-1/2}+(v_{y})_{i,j+1/2}\right].

Divergence

\displaystyle\big(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{j}\big)_{i,j}

(43)

(54)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\bm{v}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{\Pi}^{\mathrm{ran}}_{ab}(\bm{r},t)\bm{\Pi}^{\mathrm{ran}}_{cd}(\bm{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\rangle

\epsilon
is a perturbative parameter, which will eventually be set to
1
.
The pressure
p
is an auxiliary field determined by the constraint

\nabla\cdot\bm{v}=0
.
Taking the divergence of Eq. (
54
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\Delta p=-\epsilon(\partial_{a}v_{b})(\partial_{b}v_{a})+\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\bm{\Pi}^{\mathrm{ran}}_{ab}.

(57)

Substituting this expression for
p
back into Eq. (
54
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{v}}{\partial t}+\epsilon\bm{v}\cdot\nabla\bm{v}+\epsilon\nabla\Delta^{-1}(\partial_{a}v_{b})(\partial_{b}v_{a})=\nu_{0}\Delta\bm{v}+\bm{f},

(58)

\displaystyle\langle f^{a}(\bm{r},t)f^{b}(\bm{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle=-\frac{2\nu_{0}k_{B}T}{\rho_{0}}P^{ab}(\nabla)\delta(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

(59)

where
P^{ab}(\nabla):=\delta^{ab}\Delta-\partial_{a}\partial_{b}
.

To obtain analytic expressions, we make two simplifying approximations to Eq. (
58
).
First, we neglect the nonlocal pressure-feedback term

\nabla\Delta^{-1}(\partial_{a}v_{b})(\partial_{b}v_{a})
.
This term enforces incompressibility at the nonlinear level and can affect fluctuation correlations.
Second, we replace the projected noise correlation by its diagonal part, thereby neglecting the off-diagonal components of
P^{ab}(\nabla)
.
With these approximations, we use the simplified stochastic equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{v}}{\partial t}+\epsilon\bm{v}\cdot\nabla\bm{v}

\displaystyle=\nu_{0}\Delta\bm{v}+\bm{f},

(60)

\displaystyle\langle f^{a}(\bm{r},t)f^{b}(\bm{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\nu_{0}k_{B}T}{\rho_{0}}\delta^{ab}\Delta\delta(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime}).

(61)

These approximations are introduced to make the calculation analytically tractable.
Their adequacy is assessed a posteriori by comparison with the full fluctuating hydrodynamic simulations.
As shown in Sec.
III
, the resulting expressions reproduce the spatial profiles obtained numerically, although although the prefactor of the fluctuation-induced correction is treated as a fitting parameter.

We solve this equation under no-slip boundary conditions at
x=0,L
:

\bm{v}=\bm{0}\quad{\rm at}\ \ x=0,\qquad\bm{v}=U\bm{e}_{y}\quad{\rm at}\ \ x=L.

(62)

Periodic boundary conditions are imposed along the
y
axis.
This setup corresponds to the Couette flow configuration considered in the main text.

D.2
Decomposition into noise-averaged velocity and fluctuations

We decompose the velocity field into its noise average and fluctuation:

\displaystyle v^{a}(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=\left\langle v^{a}(\bm{r},t)\right\rangle+u^{a}(\bm{r},t).

(63)

Substitution into Eq. (
60
) gives the equation for the noise-averaged velocity,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\left\langle\bm{v}\right\rangle}{\partial t}+\epsilon\Big(\left\langle\bm{v}\right\rangle\cdot\nabla\left\langle\bm{v}\right\rangle+\left\langle\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\bm{u}\right\rangle\Big)

\displaystyle=\nu_{0}\Delta\left\langle\bm{v}\right\rangle,

(64)

and the equation for the fluctuation,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{u}}{\partial t}+\epsilon\Big(\left\langle\bm{v}\right\rangle\cdot\nabla\bm{u}+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\left\langle\bm{v}\right\rangle+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\bm{u}-\left\langle\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\bm{u}\right\rangle\Big)

\displaystyle=\nu_{0}\Delta\bm{u}+\bm{f}.

(65)

The boundary conditions become

\left\langle\bm{v}\right\rangle=\bm{0}\quad{\rm at}\ \ x=0,\qquad\left\langle\bm{v}\right\rangle=U\bm{e}_{y}\quad{\rm at}\ \ x=L,

(66)

and

\displaystyle\bm{u}=\bm{0}\quad{\rm at}\ \ x=0,L.

(67)

In the steady Couette state, the mean velocity has the form

\displaystyle\langle v^{x}\rangle=0,\qquad\langle v^{y}\rangle\equiv V(x).

(68)

Then Eq. (
64
) reduces to the stress-balance equation

\displaystyle\partial_{b}\left[\rho_{0}\epsilon\Big(\langle v^{a}\rangle\langle v^{b}\rangle+\langle u^{a}u^{b}\rangle\Big)-\eta_{0}\partial_{b}\langle v^{a}\rangle\right]=0,

(69)

or, for the
y
component,

\displaystyle\rho_{0}\epsilon\langle u^{x}u^{y}\rangle-\eta_{0}\partial_{x}\langle v^{y}\rangle={\rm const}:=-\langle\sigma^{xy}\rangle.

(70)

Here,
\eta_{0}=\rho_{0}\nu_{0}
, and
\langle\sigma^{xy}\rangle
is the physical noise-averaged shear stress.

D.3
Perturbation theory

We solve Eqs. (
65
) and
(
70
) perturbatively in
\epsilon
.
The mean velocity and fluctuation are expanded as

\displaystyle\big\langle v^{a}(\bm{r},t)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle v^{a}_{(0)}(\bm{r},t)\big\rangle+\epsilon\big\langle v^{a}_{(1)}(\bm{r},t)\big\rangle+\cdots,

(71)

\displaystyle u^{a}(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=u^{a}_{(0)}(\bm{r},t)+\epsilon u^{a}_{(1)}(\bm{r},t)+\cdots.

(72)

We keep terms up to
O(\epsilon^{2})
and denote the truncated mean velocity by

\displaystyle\langle v^{y}_{(\leq 2)}\rangle:=\langle v^{y}_{(0)}\rangle+\epsilon\langle v^{y}_{(1)}\rangle+\epsilon^{2}\langle v^{y}_{(2)}\rangle.

(73)

Substitution into Eq. (
70
) gives

\displaystyle\eta_{0}\partial_{x}\langle v^{y}_{(\leq 2)}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\sigma^{xy}\rangle+\rho_{0}\epsilon\langle u^{x}_{(0)}u^{y}_{(0)}\rangle+\rho_{0}\epsilon^{2}\Big[\langle u^{x}_{(1)}u^{y}_{(0)}\rangle+\langle u^{x}_{(0)}u^{y}_{(1)}\rangle\Big].

(74)

Thus, the mean profile is determined once the zeroth- and first-order fluctuations are known.
They obey

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u^{a}_{(0)}}{\partial t}=\nu_{0}\Delta u^{a}_{(0)}+f^{a},

(75)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u^{a}_{(1)}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle+\Big[\langle v^{y}_{(0)}\rangle\partial_{y}u_{(0)}^{a}+u_{(0)}^{b}\partial_{b}\langle v^{a}_{(0)}\rangle+u_{(0)}^{b}\partial_{b}u_{(0)}^{a}-\langle u_{(0)}^{b}\partial_{b}u_{(0)}^{a}\rangle\Big]=\nu_{0}\Delta u^{a}_{(1)}.

(76)

The boundary conditions are imposed order by order:

\langle v^{y}_{(\leq 2)}\rangle=0\quad{\rm at}\ \ x=0,\qquad\langle v^{y}_{(\leq 2)}\rangle=U\quad{\rm at}\ \ x=L,

(77)

\bm{u}_{(0)}=\bm{0}\quad{\rm at}\ \ x=0,L,\qquad\bm{u}_{(1)}=\bm{0}\quad{\rm at}\ \ x=0,L.

(78)

Zeroth-order solution

At zeroth order, Eqs. (
74
) and
(
77
) reduce to

\displaystyle\eta_{0}\partial_{x}\langle v^{y}_{(0)}\rangle=\langle\sigma^{xy}\rangle,

(79)

\langle v^{y}_{(0)}\rangle=0\quad{\rm at}\ \ x=0,\qquad\langle v^{y}_{(0)}\rangle=U\quad{\rm at}\ \ x=L.

(80)

Since
\langle\sigma^{xy}\rangle
is constant, Eq. (
79
) gives

\displaystyle\langle v^{y}_{(0)}\rangle=\frac{\langle\sigma^{xy}\rangle}{\eta_{0}}x+C,

(81)

where
C
is an integration constant.
The boundary conditions determine

\displaystyle\langle\sigma^{xy}\rangle

\displaystyle=\eta_{0}\dot{\gamma},

(82)

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=0,

(83)

where
\dot{\gamma}:=U/L
.
Thus

\displaystyle\langle v^{y}_{(0)}\rangle=\dot{\gamma}x.

(84)

Second-order solution

The first-order correction to the mean profile vanishes, as shown below from

\langle u^{x}_{(0)}u^{y}_{(0)}\rangle=0
.
We therefore proceed to the leading nonzero correction, which is of order

O(\epsilon^{2})
.

Substituting Eq. (
84
) into Eq. (
76
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u^{a}_{(1)}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle+\Big[\dot{\gamma}x\partial_{y}u_{(0)}^{a}+\dot{\gamma}u_{(0)}^{x}\delta_{ay}+u_{(0)}^{b}\partial_{b}u_{(0)}^{a}-\langle u_{(0)}^{b}\partial_{b}u_{(0)}^{a}\rangle\Big]=\nu_{0}\Delta u^{a}_{(1)}.

(85)

To evaluate the fluctuation correlations in Eq. (
74
), we use the Green function

G(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\tau)
defined by

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}-\nu_{0}\Delta\Big)G(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\tau)=\delta(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})\delta(\tau),

(86)

with the Dirichlet boundary condition

\displaystyle G(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\tau)=0\quad{\rm at}\ \ x=0,L\ {\rm or}\ x^{\prime}=0,L.

(87)

Its explicit form is

\displaystyle G(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\tau)

\displaystyle=\theta(\tau)\int_{k}e^{ik_{y}(y-y^{\prime})}\sin(k_{x}x)\sin(k_{x}x^{\prime})\times e^{-\nu_{0}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})\tau},

(88)

where
k_{x}:=\pi n_{x}/L
and

\displaystyle\int_{k}:=\frac{2}{L}\sum_{n_{x}=1}^{L/a_{\textrm{uv}}}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{dk_{y}}{2\pi}.

(89)

The form of this solution reflects the translational symmetry along the
y
axis (plane wave expansion) and the confinement of the fluid at
x=0,L
(sine function expansion).

Using this Green function, the zeroth- and first-order fluctuation fields,

u^{a}_{(0)}
and
u^{a}_{(1)}
, are represented as

\displaystyle u_{(0)}^{a}(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=\int G(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\tau)f^{a}(\bm{r}^{\prime},t-\tau),

(90)

\displaystyle u_{(1)}^{a}(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=-\int G(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\tau)\biggl[\dot{\gamma}x^{\prime}\partial_{y^{\prime}}u_{(0)}^{a}+\dot{\gamma}u_{(0)}^{x}\delta_{ay}+u_{(0)}^{b}\partial_{b}u_{(0)}^{a}-\langle u_{(0)}^{b}\partial_{b}u_{(0)}^{a}\rangle\biggr]\biggr|_{\bm{r}^{\prime},t-\tau},

(91)

where we have introduced the shorthand notation:

\displaystyle\int=\int^{\infty}_{0}d\tau\int^{L}_{0}dx^{\prime}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dy^{\prime}.

(92)

Because
u_{(0)}^{a}
is a Gaussian field generated by Eq. (
75
),

\displaystyle\langle u_{(0)}^{x}(\bm{r},0)u_{(0)}^{y}(\bm{r},0)\rangle=0,

(93)

\displaystyle\langle u_{(0)}^{a}(\bm{r},0)u_{(0)}^{b}(\bm{r},0)u_{(0)}^{c}(\bm{r},0)\rangle=0.

(94)

The first identity explains why the
O(\epsilon)
term in Eq. (
74
) vanishes.
Together with the order-by-order boundary conditions, it gives
\langle v^{y}_{(1)}\rangle=0
.

We next evaluate the
O(\epsilon^{2})
correlations in Eq. (
74
).
Substituting Eq. (
91
) and using the vanishing of the three-point function, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle u_{(1)}^{a}(\bm{r},0)u_{(0)}^{b}(\bm{r},0)\rangle

\displaystyle=-\int G(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\tau)\biggl[\dot{\gamma}x^{\prime}\langle u_{(0)}^{b}(\bm{r},0)\partial_{y^{\prime}}u_{(0)}^{a}(\bm{r}^{\prime},-\tau)\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt+\dot{\gamma}\delta_{ay}\langle u_{(0)}^{b}(\bm{r},0)u_{(0)}^{x}(\bm{r}^{\prime},-\tau)\rangle\biggr],

(95)

and the components needed below are

\displaystyle\langle u_{(1)}^{x}(\bm{r},0)u_{(0)}^{y}(\bm{r},0)\rangle

\displaystyle=0,

(96)

\displaystyle\langle u_{(1)}^{y}(\bm{r},0)u_{(0)}^{x}(\bm{r},0)\rangle

\displaystyle=-\dot{\gamma}\int G(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\tau)\langle u_{(0)}^{x}(\bm{r},0)u_{(0)}^{x}(\bm{r}^{\prime},-\tau)\rangle.

(97)

The noise correlation can be written as

\displaystyle\langle f^{a}(\bm{r},t)f^{b}(\bm{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{2\nu_{0}k_{B}T}{\rho_{0}}\delta^{ab}\int_{k}k^{2}e^{ik_{y}(y-y^{\prime})}\sin(k_{x}x)\sin(k_{x}x^{\prime})\times\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

(98)

which gives

\displaystyle\langle u_{(0)}^{x}(\bm{r},0)u_{(0)}^{x}(\bm{r}^{\prime},-\tau)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{B}T}{\rho_{0}}\int_{k}e^{ik_{y}(y-y^{\prime})}\sin(k_{x}x)\sin(k_{x}x^{\prime})e^{-\nu_{0}k^{2}\tau}.

(99)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\langle u_{(1)}^{y}(\bm{r},0)u_{(0)}^{x}(\bm{r},0)\rangle

\displaystyle=-\dot{\gamma}\int G(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\tau)\langle u_{(0)}^{x}(\bm{r},0)u_{(0)}^{x}(\bm{r}^{\prime},-\tau)\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{\dot{\gamma}k_{B}T}{2\nu_{0}\rho_{0}L}\sum_{k_{x}}\frac{1}{k_{x}}\sin^{2}(k_{x}x).

(100)

Substitution of Eqs. (
93
), (
96
), and
(
100
) into Eq. (
74
) yields

\displaystyle\eta_{0}\partial_{x}\langle v^{y}_{(\leq 2)}(\bm{r})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\sigma^{xy}\rangle-\epsilon^{2}\frac{\rho_{0}\dot{\gamma}k_{B}T}{2\eta_{0}L}\sum_{k_{x}}\frac{1}{k_{x}}\sin^{2}(k_{x}x).

(101)

Thus the leading fluctuation correction is
O(\epsilon^{2})
.

Integrating with respect to
x
gives

\displaystyle\langle v^{y}_{(\leq 2)}(\bm{r})\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\sigma^{xy}\rangle}{\eta_{0}}x-\epsilon^{2}\frac{\rho_{0}\dot{\gamma}k_{B}T}{4\eta_{0}^{2}L}\times\sum_{k_{x}}\frac{1}{k_{x}}\left(x-\frac{\sin(2k_{x}x)}{2k_{x}}\right).

(102)

The boundary conditions in Eq. (
77
) determine the constant stress as

\displaystyle\langle\sigma^{xy}\rangle

\displaystyle=\eta_{0}\dot{\gamma}\left[1+\epsilon^{2}\frac{\rho_{0}k_{B}T}{4\eta_{0}^{2}L}\sum_{k_{x}}\frac{1}{k_{x}}\right].

(103)

Substituting this result back into Eq. (
102
), we obtain

\displaystyle\langle v^{y}_{(\leq 2)}(\bm{r})\rangle

\displaystyle=\dot{\gamma}x+\epsilon^{2}\frac{\rho_{0}\dot{\gamma}k_{B}T}{4\eta_{0}^{2}L}\sum_{k_{x}}\frac{1}{k_{x}}\frac{\sin(2k_{x}x)}{2k_{x}}.

(104)

Finally, using
\eta_{\textrm{obs}}(x):=\langle\sigma^{xy}\rangle/\partial_{x}\langle v^{y}\rangle
, we find, to the same order,

\displaystyle\eta_{\textrm{obs}}(x)

\displaystyle=\eta_{0}\biggl[1+\epsilon^{2}\frac{\rho_{0}k_{B}T}{2\eta_{0}^{2}L}\sum_{k_{x}}\frac{1}{k_{x}}\sin^{2}(k_{x}x)\biggr]+O(\epsilon^{4}).

(105)

Setting
\epsilon=1
, these expressions reproduce Eqs. (
13
)-(
16
) with the definition of
A
in Eq. (
14
).

Appendix E
Setup for MD simulations

This appendix summarizes the MD simulation setup, including the wall models, coarse-grained observables, and averaging procedure.
Although the fluid model is introduced in Sec.
II.1
, we briefly recall the basic setup here for completeness.

E.1
Hamiltonian and equations of motion

We consider
N
particles of mass
m
in a square domain
[0,L]\times[0,L]
.
We place walls at
x=0
and
x=L
and impose periodic boundary conditions along the
y
axis.
Let
(\bm{r}_{i},\bm{p}_{i})
denote the fluid variables.
We denote the left and right wall variables by
(\bm{r}_{{\rm left},i},\bm{p}_{{\rm left},i})
and
(\bm{r}_{{\rm right},i},\bm{p}_{{\rm right},i})
.

We decompose the Hamiltonian as

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=H_{\rm fluid}+H_{\rm left}+H_{\rm right}.

(106)

The fluid particles evolve according to Hamilton’s equations of motion:

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{r}_{i}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{p}_{i}}{m},

(107)

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{p}_{i}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial H_{\rm fluid}}{\partial\bm{r}_{i}}-\frac{\partial H_{\rm left}}{\partial\bm{r}_{i}}-\frac{\partial H_{\rm right}}{\partial\bm{r}_{i}}+\bm{g}.

(108)

For Poiseuille flow, we add an external force
\bm{g}=g\bm{e}_{y}
.
We use three wall models: thermal hydrophobic, thermal hydrophilic, and frozen hydrophobic walls.
The main text focuses on the thermal hydrophobic wall, while Appendix
F
uses the other two to test robustness.
Below we describe the left wall at
x=0
; we construct the right wall in the same way.

E.1.1
Thermal hydrophobic wall

The thermal hydrophobic wall consists of
N_{\rm left}
particles arranged in three square-lattice layers, with the first layer at
x=-\sigma
(see Fig.
5
).
For the left wall, we use

\displaystyle H_{\rm left}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm left}}\frac{\bm{p}_{{\rm left},i}^{2}}{2m}+\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm left}}V^{\rm onsite}(\bm{r}_{{\rm left},i},t)+\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm left}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}V^{\rm wall}(|\bm{r}_{{\rm left},i}-\bm{r}_{j}|).

(109)

The on-site potential takes the form

\displaystyle V^{\rm onsite}(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=V_{0}\Biggl[\cos\biggl(\frac{2\pi(x+\sigma/2)}{\sigma}\biggr)+\cos\biggl(\frac{2\pi(y\mp v_{0}t/2)}{\sigma}\biggr)\Biggr].

(110)

Here,
V_{0}
is the strength of the on-site potential.
The time-dependent term moves the wall in the
y
direction with velocity
\pm v_{0}
, thereby imposing Couette shear.
For the wall-fluid interaction, we use the Weeks–Chandler–Andersen (WCA) potential,

\displaystyle V^{\rm wall}(r)=\begin{cases}4\epsilon\Big\{\big(\frac{\sigma}{r}\big)^{12}-\big(\frac{\sigma}{r}\big)^{6}+\frac{1}{4}\Big\}&{\rm for}\ r<2^{1/6}\sigma\\
0&{\rm otherwise}.\end{cases}

(111)

The wall particles obey the Langevin equations

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{r}_{{\rm left},i}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{p}_{{\rm left},i}}{m},

(112)

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{p}_{{\rm left},i}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\bm{r}_{{\rm left},i}}-\gamma\bm{p}_{{\rm left},i}+\sqrt{2\gamma T}\bm{\xi}_{i}(t),

(113)

where
\gamma
represents the friction coefficient, and
\bm{\xi}_{i}(t)
denotes Gaussian white noise with zero mean and unit variance.

E.1.2
Thermal hydrophilic wall

The thermal hydrophilic wall has the same particle arrangement and Langevin dynamics [Eq. (
113
)] as the thermal hydrophobic wall.
Only the wall-fluid interaction is changed from the WCA potential to the attractive Lennard–Jones (LJ) potential,

\displaystyle V^{\rm wall}(r)=\begin{cases}4\epsilon\Big\{\big(\frac{\sigma}{r}\big)^{12}-\big(\frac{\sigma}{r}\big)^{6}\Big\}&{\rm for}\ r<2.5\sigma\\
0&{\rm otherwise}.\end{cases}

(114)

E.1.3
Frozen hydrophobic wall

For the frozen hydrophobic wall, the wall particles are not evolved dynamically.
Instead, we impose their positions as

\displaystyle\bm{r}_{{\rm left},i}=\biggl(\frac{i_{x}}{2}\sigma,\frac{i_{y}}{2}\sigma\pm\frac{v_{0}t}{2}\biggr)

(115)

with
i_{x}=-1,-2,-3
and
i_{y}=1,2,\cdots,2L/\sigma
.
Because these particles have no dynamical momenta, the corresponding wall Hamiltonian reduces to

\displaystyle H_{\rm left}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm left}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}V^{\rm wall}(|\bm{r}_{{\rm left},i}-\bm{r}_{j}|).

(116)

We again use the WCA potential in Eq. (
111
) for
V^{\rm wall}(r)
.

E.2
Coarse-grained observables

We compute hydrodynamic fields by coarse-graining microscopic particle configurations.
Let
\hat{\rho}(\bm{r};\Gamma_{t})
,
\hat{j}^{a}(\bm{r};\Gamma_{t})
, and
\hat{\Pi}^{ab}(\bm{r};\Gamma_{t})
denote the microscopic mass density, momentum density, and momentum current density for
\Gamma_{t}:=\{(\bm{r}_{i}(t),\bm{p}_{i}(t))\}_{i=1}^{N}
.
The explicit expressions for these quantities can be found in Refs.
[
33
,
85
]
.

To obtain the one-dimensional steady-state profiles used in the main text, we first divide the system into bins of width
a_{\textrm{uv}}
along the
x
axis and average the microscopic fields within each bin.
For example, for
x_{i}=ia_{\textrm{uv}}
(
i=0,1,\cdots,L/a_{\textrm{uv}}
), the instantaneous coarse-grained density is

\displaystyle\rho(x_{i};t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{a_{\textrm{uv}}}\int_{x_{i}-a_{\textrm{uv}}/2}^{x_{i}+a_{\textrm{uv}}/2}dx\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L}dy\,\hat{\rho}(\bm{r};\Gamma_{t}).

(117)

The steady density profile is then obtained by time averaging over the observation interval,

\displaystyle\langle\rho(x_{i})\rangle:=\frac{1}{\tau_{\rm obs}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{\rm obs}}dt\,\rho(x_{i};t).

(118)

The momentum density
\langle\bm{j}(x_{i})\rangle
and momentum current
\langle\bm{\Pi}_{ab}(x_{i})\rangle
are calculated in the same way.

Using these time-averaged fields, we compute the velocity field and stress tensor as

\displaystyle\langle\bm{v}(x_{i})\rangle

\displaystyle:=\frac{\langle\bm{j}(x_{i})\rangle}{\langle\rho(x_{i})\rangle},

(119)

\displaystyle\langle\bm{\sigma}_{ab}(x_{i})\rangle

\displaystyle:=-\langle\bm{\Pi}_{ab}(x_{i})\rangle+\langle j_{a}(x_{i})\rangle\langle v_{b}(x_{i})\rangle.

(120)

Figure
5
shows the correspondence between the MD observables and the grid used in fluctuating hydrodynamics.
In the comparison shown in the main text, we set
a_{\textrm{uv}}=\sigma
.
The velocity field is defined at
x=0,\sigma,\cdots,L
in both descriptions.
The shear stress is evaluated at different grid points in the two descriptions; because the steady-state shear stress is spatially homogeneous, we use its spatial average.
The observed viscosity is then evaluated as
\eta_{\textrm{obs}}(x)=\sigma^{xy}(x)/\partial_{x}v^{y}(x)
.

For the equilibrium momentum correlation
C_{\rm eq}(t)
defined in Eq. (
20
), we use two-dimensional bins of width
a_{\textrm{uv}}
and calculate

\displaystyle C_{\rm eq}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau_{\rm obs}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{\rm obs}}ds\frac{1}{2N_{\mathcal{B}}}\sum_{{ij}\in\mathcal{B}}\bm{j}(\bm{r}_{ij};t+s)\cdot\bm{j}(\bm{r}_{ij};s)

(121)

with

\displaystyle\bm{j}(\bm{r}_{i};t):=\frac{1}{a_{\textrm{uv}}^{2}}\int_{x_{i}-a_{\textrm{uv}}/2}^{x_{i}+a_{\textrm{uv}}/2}dx\int_{y_{i}-a_{\textrm{uv}}/2}^{y_{i}+a_{\textrm{uv}}/2}dy\hat{\bm{j}}(\bm{r};\Gamma_{t}).

(122)

Here,
\bm{r}_{ij}=(ia_{\textrm{uv}},ja_{\textrm{uv}})
, and we restrict the sum to the central region
\mathcal{B}:=[L/4,3L/4]\times[L/4,3L/4]
.

N_{\mathcal{B}}
denotes the number of bins in
\mathcal{B}
, and the time average runs over
\tau_{\rm obs}
in equilibrium.

Appendix F
Supplementary analyses of MD simulations

This appendix presents supplementary MD analyses that support the results of the main text.
We examine the dependence on microscopic wall properties, the range of linear response, and the system-size dependence of the observed viscosity.

F.1
Effects of microscopic wall properties

The main text uses thermal hydrophobic walls.
To test the sensitivity to the microscopic wall model, we compare this setup with frozen hydrophobic walls and thermal hydrophilic walls in the same Couette geometry as in Fig.
2
.

Figures

Appendix H
Bare-viscosity estimation in Poiseuille geometry

In the main text, we estimated the bare viscosity from the near-wall viscosity profile in Couette flow.
This appendix applies the same idea to Poiseuille flow.
We first derive the perturbative analytical expression for the noise-averaged velocity profile in Poiseuille flow and define the corresponding local observed viscosity.
We then use this expression to formulate a Poiseuille version of Protocol 2 and compare it with MD simulations.

H.1
Measurement protocol in Poiseuille flow

Consider a fluid confined between two fixed parallel walls located at
x=0
and
x=L
.
A steady flow is driven by a constant external force acting on the fluid in the
y
direction.
Within the perturbative fluctuating-hydrodynamic analysis used in Appendix
D
, the velocity field up to second order in the nonlinear term is

\displaystyle\langle v^{y}_{(\leq 2)}(\bm{r})\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{0}g}{2\eta_{0}}x(L-x)+g\frac{A_{\rm P}}{L}\sum_{k_{x},k^{\prime}_{x}}\frac{a(k_{x},k^{\prime}_{x})}{\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}+(k^{\prime}_{x})^{2}}}

\displaystyle\qquad{}\left[\frac{\sin((k_{x}-k^{\prime}_{x})x)}{k_{x}-k^{\prime}_{x}}-\frac{\sin((k_{x}+k^{\prime}_{x})x)}{k_{x}+k^{\prime}_{x}}\right]

(123)

with

\displaystyle A_{\rm P}:=\frac{\sqrt{2}\rho_{0}^{3}k_{B}T}{8\eta_{0}^{3}},

(124)

where
g
represents the constant external force per unit mass.
The derivation is given in the next subsection.

For deterministic Poiseuille flow with viscosity
\eta
, the local curvature satisfies

\displaystyle\eta\,\partial_{x}^{2}\langle v^{y}(x)\rangle=-\rho_{0}g.

(125)

We therefore define the local observed viscosity in the Poiseuille geometry by

\displaystyle\eta_{\textrm{obs}}^{\rm poi}(x):=-\frac{\rho_{0}g}{\partial_{x}^{2}\langle v^{y}(x)\rangle}.

(126)

Using Eq. (
123
), we obtain

\displaystyle\eta_{\textrm{obs}}^{\rm poi}(x)

\displaystyle=\eta_{0}\Biggl[1+2\frac{A_{\rm P}}{L}\sum_{k_{x},k^{\prime}_{x}}\frac{a(k_{x},k^{\prime}_{x})}{\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}+(k^{\prime}_{x})^{2}}}\Big\{\sin[(k_{x}-k^{\prime}_{x})x]-\sin[(k_{x}+k^{\prime}_{x})x]\Big\}\Biggr]^{-1}

\displaystyle=\eta_{0}\Biggl[1-2\frac{A_{\rm P}}{L}\sum_{k_{x},k^{\prime}_{x}}\frac{a(k_{x},k^{\prime}_{x})}{\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}+(k^{\prime}_{x})^{2}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\Big\{\sin[(k_{x}-k^{\prime}_{x})x]-\sin[(k_{x}+k^{\prime}_{x})x]\Big\}\Biggr]+O(A_{\rm P}^{2}).

(127)

This expression is the Poiseuille counterpart of the Couette result in Eq. (
16
).
In both geometries, thermal fluctuations produce a spatially dependent correction to the bare viscosity.
At the walls, the correction vanishes:

\displaystyle\eta_{\textrm{obs}}^{\rm poi}(x=0)=\eta_{\textrm{obs}}^{\rm poi}(x=L)=\eta_{0}.

(128)

Thus, the bare viscosity governs the near-wall behavior also in Poiseuille flow.

This observation leads to the following Poiseuille version of Protocol 2.

Protocol 2 (Poiseuille)

1.

Measure
\langle v^{y}(\bm{r})\rangle
in an atomic system.

2.

Fit the obtained
\langle v^{y}(\bm{r})\rangle
to Eq. (
123
), using
\eta_{0}
and
A_{\rm P}
as fitting parameters.

In this protocol, the mean density
\rho_{0}
, external force
g
, temperature
T
, and system size
L
in Eq. (
123
) are fixed to the values used in the atomistic simulation.

We apply this protocol to the three atomic systems studied in Figs.
2
,
10
and
11
.
Figure
12
shows the MD velocity profiles and the corresponding fits to Eq. (
123
).
The analytical expression captures the velocity profiles for all three systems.

The fitted bare viscosities are summarized in Table
4
.
We perform the fits either over the entire velocity field or only near the wall.
Both procedures give values close to those obtained from Protocol 1 in the Couette geometry, although the Poiseuille estimates are systematically slightly smaller.

H.2
Derivation of the analytic expressions

We derive Eq. (
123
).
We use the same simplified fluctuating-hydrodynamic equation as in Appendix
D.1
, with the body force added:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{v}}{\partial t}+\epsilon\bm{v}\cdot\nabla\bm{v}

\displaystyle=\nu_{0}\Delta\bm{v}+g\bm{e}_{y}+\bm{f},

(129)

\displaystyle\langle f^{a}(\bm{r},t)f^{b}(\bm{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\nu_{0}k_{B}T}{\rho_{0}}\delta^{ab}\Delta\delta(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

(130)

where
g
represents the constant external force per unit mass, and
\bm{e}_{y}
is the unit vector in the
y
direction.
The no-slip boundary condition is

\bm{v}=\bm{0}\quad{\rm at}\ \ x=0,L.

(131)

Periodic boundary conditions are imposed along the
y
axis.

We decompose the fluctuating velocity field
\bm{v}(\bm{r},t)
into a noise-averaged component
\left\langle\bm{v}(\bm{r},t)\right\rangle
and fluctuations
\bm{u}(\bm{r},t)
around it:

\displaystyle v^{a}(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=\left\langle v^{a}(\bm{r},t)\right\rangle+u^{a}(\bm{r},t).

(132)

The boundary conditions given by Eq. (
131
) become:

\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{v}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\bm{0}\quad{\rm at}\ \ x=0,L,

(133)

\displaystyle\bm{u}

\displaystyle=\bm{0}\quad{\rm at}\ \ x=0,L.

(134)

In the steady state considered here, the mean flow is unidirectional:
\langle v^{x}(\bm{r})\rangle=0
, and
\langle v^{y}(\bm{r})\rangle
depends only on the wall-normal coordinate
x
.
Averaging the momentum balance as in Appendix
D.2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{b}\left[\epsilon\Big(\langle v^{a}\rangle\langle v^{b}\rangle+\langle u^{a}u^{b}\rangle\Big)-\nu_{0}\partial_{b}\langle v^{a}\rangle\right]=g\delta_{ay}.

(135)

The
y
component gives

\displaystyle\nu_{0}\partial_{x}\langle v^{y}\rangle=\epsilon\langle u^{x}u^{y}\rangle-gx+C,

(136)

where
C
is an integration constant determined by the boundary conditions.
The velocity fluctuations
\bm{u}(\bm{r},t)
obey

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{u}}{\partial t}+\epsilon\Big(\left\langle\bm{v}\right\rangle\cdot\nabla\bm{u}+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\left\langle\bm{v}\right\rangle+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\bm{u}-\left\langle\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\bm{u}\right\rangle\Big)

\displaystyle=\nu_{0}\Delta\bm{u}+\bm{f}.

(137)

Solving Eqs. (
136
) and (
137
) with Eqs. (
133
) and (
134
) determines
\langle v^{y}\rangle
.

We now expand in
\epsilon
[Eqs. (
71
) and (
72
)].
Keeping terms through
O(\epsilon^{2})
, the equations needed to compute
\langle v^{y}_{(\leq 2)}\rangle
are

\displaystyle\nu_{0}\partial_{x}\langle v^{y}\rangle

\displaystyle=C+\epsilon\langle u^{x}_{(0)}u^{y}_{(0)}\rangle-gx+\epsilon^{2}\Big[\langle u^{x}_{(1)}u^{y}_{(0)}\rangle+\langle u^{x}_{(0)}u^{y}_{(1)}\rangle\Big],

(138)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u^{a}_{(0)}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\nu_{0}\Delta u^{a}_{(0)}+f^{a},

(139)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u^{a}_{(1)}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle+\Big[\langle v^{y}_{(0)}\rangle\partial_{y}u_{(0)}^{a}+u_{(0)}^{b}\partial_{b}\langle v^{y}_{(0)}\rangle+u_{(0)}^{b}\partial_{b}u_{(0)}^{a}-\langle u_{(0)}^{b}\partial_{b}u_{(0)}^{a}\rangle\Big]=\nu_{0}\Delta u^{a}_{(1)}

(140)

with

\displaystyle\langle v^{y}_{(0)}\rangle=\frac{g}{2\nu_{0}}x(L-x).

(141)

The corresponding boundary conditions are

\langle v^{y}_{(\leq 2)}\rangle=0\quad{\rm at}\ \ x=0,L

(142)

\bm{u}_{(0)}=\bm{0}\quad{\rm at}\ \ x=0,L,\qquad\bm{u}_{(1)}=\bm{0}\quad{\rm at}\ \ x=0,L.

(143)

To evaluate the fluctuation correlations, we use the Green function in Eq. (
88
).
The zeroth- and first-order fluctuation fields are represented as

\displaystyle u_{(0)}^{a}(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=\int G(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\tau)f^{a}(\bm{r}^{\prime},t-\tau),

(144)

\displaystyle u_{(1)}^{a}(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=-\int G(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\tau)\biggl[\frac{g}{2\nu_{0}}x^{\prime}(L-x^{\prime})\partial_{y^{\prime}}u_{(0)}^{a}+\frac{g}{2\nu_{0}}(L-2x^{\prime})u_{(0)}^{x}\delta_{ay}

\displaystyle\quad+u_{(0)}^{b}\partial_{b}u_{(0)}^{a}-\langle u_{(0)}^{b}\partial_{b}u_{(0)}^{a}\rangle\biggr]\biggr|_{\bm{r}^{\prime},t-\tau},

(145)

where
\int=\int^{\infty}_{0}d\tau\int^{L}_{0}dx^{\prime}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dy^{\prime}
.
Since the zeroth-order field is Gaussian, we have

\displaystyle\langle u_{(0)}^{x}u_{(0)}^{y}\rangle=0,

(146)

\displaystyle\langle u_{(0)}^{a}u_{(0)}^{b}u_{(0)}^{c}\rangle=0.

(147)

The first identity eliminates the
O(\epsilon)
term in Eq. (
138
).
Using Eq. (
145
) and the vanishing of the three-point function, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle u_{(1)}^{a}(\bm{r},0)u_{(0)}^{b}(\bm{r},0)\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{g}{2\nu_{0}}\int G(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\tau)\biggl[x^{\prime}(L-x^{\prime})\langle u_{(0)}^{b}(\bm{r},0)\partial_{y^{\prime}}u_{(0)}^{a}(\bm{r}^{\prime},-\tau)\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+(L-2x^{\prime})\delta_{ay}\langle u_{(0)}^{b}(\bm{r},0)u_{(0)}^{x}(\bm{r}^{\prime},-\tau)\rangle\biggr],

(148)

and the components entering Eq. (
138
) are

\displaystyle\langle u_{(1)}^{x}(\bm{r},0)u_{(0)}^{y}(\bm{r},0)\rangle

\displaystyle=0,

(149)

\displaystyle\langle u_{(1)}^{y}(\bm{r},0)u_{(0)}^{x}(\bm{r},0)\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{g}{2\nu_{0}}\int(L-2x^{\prime})G(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\tau)\langle u_{(0)}^{x}(\bm{r},0)u_{(0)}^{x}(\bm{r}^{\prime},-\tau)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}gk_{B}T}{4\nu_{0}^{2}L}\sum_{k_{x},k^{\prime}_{x}}\frac{a(k_{x},k^{\prime}_{x})}{\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}+(k^{\prime}_{x})^{2}}}\sin(k_{x}x)\sin(k^{\prime}_{x}x)

(150)

with

\displaystyle a(k_{x},k^{\prime}_{x})=\frac{2}{L}\int_{0}^{L}dx(2x-L)\sin(k_{x}x)\sin(k^{\prime}_{x}x)=\frac{4}{L}\left[\frac{1}{(k_{x}+k^{\prime}_{x})^{2}}-\frac{1}{(k_{x}-k^{\prime}_{x})^{2}}\right]

(151)

for
n+n^{\prime}={\rm odd}
, and zero otherwise.
Here
k_{x}=\pi n/L
and
k^{\prime}_{x}=\pi n^{\prime}/L
.
Substituting this result into Eq. (
138
) gives

\displaystyle\nu_{0}\partial_{x}\langle v^{y}_{(\leq 2)}(\bm{r})\rangle

\displaystyle=C-gx+\epsilon^{2}\frac{\sqrt{2}gk_{B}T}{4\nu_{0}^{2}L}\sum_{k_{x},k^{\prime}_{x}}\frac{a(k_{x},k^{\prime}_{x})}{\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}+(k^{\prime}_{x})^{2}}}\sin(k_{x}x)\sin(k^{\prime}_{x}x).

(152)

Integrating with respect to
x
and using the boundary conditions, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle v^{y}_{(\leq 2)}(\bm{r})\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{g}{2\nu_{0}}x(L-x)+\epsilon^{2}\frac{\sqrt{2}gk_{B}T}{8\nu_{0}^{3}L}\sum_{k_{x},k^{\prime}_{x}}\frac{a(k_{x},k^{\prime}_{x})}{\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}+(k^{\prime}_{x})^{2}}}

\displaystyle\qquad{}\left[\frac{\sin((k_{x}-k^{\prime}_{x})x)}{k_{x}-k^{\prime}_{x}}-\frac{\sin((k_{x}+k^{\prime}_{x})x)}{k_{x}+k^{\prime}_{x}}\right].

(153)

Setting
\epsilon=1
and using
\nu_{0}=\eta_{0}/\rho_{0}
, this expression reproduces Eq. (
123
) with the definition of
A_{\rm P}
.
