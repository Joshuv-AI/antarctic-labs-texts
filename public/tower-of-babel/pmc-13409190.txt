Intestinal transplantation in Australia: progress, challenges and future directions
Authors: Brooke Chapman, Darren Wong, Graham Starkey, Marcos Perini, Jason Yap, Adam Testro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntestinal transplant is a rare procedure reserved for patients with intestinal failure who cannot be sustained on total parenteral nutrition due to severe, life‐threatening complications. This study aims to describe the activity and outcomes of intestinal transplantation undertaken in Australia.MethodsObservational retrospective cohort study of all adult and paediatric patients who have received an intestinal transplant in Australia. The primary outcomes were patient and graft survival, with secondary outcomes including incidence of post‐transplant enteral autonomy and post‐transplant complications.ResultsNineteen patients (14 adults, five children) received a total of 20 intestinal transplants in Australia between 2010 and 2026. One‐year conditional graft and patient survival was 87% and 93% respectively, with 5‐year conditional graft and patient survival rates of 67% and 75% respectively. The median time to cease total parenteral nutrition and achieve enteral autonomy after transplant was 21 days (interquartile range: 17‐30 days). Median body mass index was within ideal range for adult patients by 12 months post‐transplant and growth centiles improved for paediatric patients. Eight patients (40%) experienced a total of 14 episodes of rejection at a median onset of 7.5 months after transplant, of which five were graded severe. Rates of post‐transplant lymphoproliferative disorder and graft‐versus‐host disease were low (5% each).ConclusionsThe Australian Intestinal Transplant Service demonstrates excellent patient outcomes, with short‐ to medium‐term graft and patient survival similar to that of leading international centres and enteral autonomy achieved in all patients. Intestinal transplant is now an acceptable, life‐saving procedure in appropriately selected patients in our region.

Introduction

Intestinal transplantation (IT) is a life‐saving procedure reserved for patients with chronic intestinal failure (IF) experiencing critical complications of their disease and/or total parenteral nutrition (TPN). Historically, patients who developed IF requiring TPN had a high mortality. 1 Recent advances in medical and surgical management, the advent of specialised IF nutrition support teams, as well as improvements in the pharmacological composition of PN, have led to significantly improved survival in PN‐dependent patients. 2 , 3 , 4 Despite these developments, some patients still acquire life‐threatening complications of IF, which necessitates small bowel transplantation.

Early international experience with IT in the 1980s and 1990s was hindered by high mortality rates for both waiting list and post‐transplant patients, which was in part due to delayed referral and subsequent poor patient condition, as well as the inherent immunogenicity of the transplanted intestine. As the largest lymphoid organ in the body, the small intestine has presented significant challenges for transplant survival primarily due to allograft rejection and opportunistic infection. Advances in surgical technique, the evolution of immunosuppression, new developments aimed at desensitisation of the humoral response, as well as earlier recognition of complications after IT have significantly improved both patient and graft survival over the past 20 years. 5

Despite this progress, many within the broader medical community are unaware that IT exists, or of the current indications and outcomes. As a rare therapy for a rare disease, worldwide transplant volume is low, with fewer than 150 procedures performed each year at only 50 active centres. 6 In Australia, IT for paediatric and adult patients has been available since 2010 when a joint program between the Royal Children's Hospital and Austin Health in Melbourne, Victoria, was established. 7 , 8 , 9 Recently, the program has expanded to offer IT to patients residing in New Zealand. This paper aims to describe the activity and outcomes of IT undertaken in Australia since the program's inception and increase awareness of transplantation as a viable option for patients in our region with complications associated with irreversible IF.

Methods

A retrospective analysis of all patients undergoing IT in Australia between January 2010 and April 2026 was undertaken.

Participants

Transplant candidates were referred from across Australia and New Zealand and underwent multidisciplinary assessment to determine suitability for transplant. Paediatric recipients were defined as those <18 years of age at the time of transplant. After the age of 18, paediatric patients are transitioned to adult care. Currently accepted indications for intestinal transplant are outlined in Table 1 and follow international consensus guidelines. 10 Baseline recipient demographics including age, gender, aetiology of IF, indication for IT, waiting list duration, pre‐transplant weight and biochemistry were recorded.

Donors were selected based on age, size and ABO blood group compatibility. In order to optimise organ quality, donors are preferably <50 years of age, with donation after brain death, and with minimal exposure to high‐dose inotropes. Ideally, the donor is smaller than the planned recipient to assist with abdominal closure. Data sourced from routine Luminex‐based virtual cross matching (VXM) in addition to complement‐dependent cytotoxicity (CDC) crossmatches was recorded. VXM and CDC crossmatches are employed to achieve an optimal immunological match, reducing the likelihood of antibody‐mediated rejection, a common cause of graft loss. Data regarding the donor and recipient cytomegalovirus (CMV) and Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) infection status, as well as the incidence of post‐transplantation viremia and tissue infection, were also analysed.

Upon acceptance of a suitable donor offer, organ allocation is determined primarily by blood group and size. In recognition of the documented higher waiting list mortality in patients with IF‐associated liver disease (IFALD), 11 recipients who also require a liver‐containing graft are waitlisted on the national liver transplant waiting list as a category 2C listing. Under this allocation, the donor home state liver transplant unit must discuss allocation of the donor organs with the intestinal transplant centre unless the donor home state transplant unit has a suitable liver recipient with a Model of End Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score of ≥25. 12

Types of intestinal transplant

For the purposes of this analysis, the following definitions were used: isolated intestine transplant; liver and intestine transplant; modified multivisceral transplant (intestine and stomach without a liver graft); and multivisceral transplant (intestine plus stomach and liver). Colon was included for all transplants where possible. All patients have a stoma formed to allow for rejection surveillance. This varies between colostomy and ileostomy depending on individual patient factors. If the native anatomy allows for continuity surgery, this is usually undertaken 6 to –12 months post‐transplant.

Post‐transplant clinical outcomes

Clinical data collected after IT included intenstive care unit (ICU) and total hospital length of stay (LOS) (days), incidence of post‐transplant complications including rejection (severity and timing), graft‐vs‐host disease (GVHD), post‐transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD), serum biochemistry at 12 months, body mass index (for adults) and growth centiles (for children) at last follow‐up, and graft and patient survival (days). The census date was 8 April 2026.

Post‐transplant immunosuppression and monitoring of rejection

Immunosuppressive regimens varied during the study period. The current standard regimen includes induction with rabbit‐antithymocyte globulin followed by maintenance with tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil and weaning corticosteroids. In order to survey for allograft rejection, protocol endoscopic surveillance was undertaken weekly post‐transplant for the first 4 weeks and fortnightly for a further 4 weeks, as well as when clinically indicated. Biopsies are taken when there are endoscopic features suspicious for rejection, such as mucosal erythema, erosions or ulcerations or spontaneous bleeding. Acute cellular rejection (ACR) was diagnosed using standard histopathologic and clinical criteria and was treated with pulsed intravenous corticosteroids as the first line of therapy. Anti‐thymocyte globulin and infliximab were used as second‐ and third‐line therapies for ACR.

Post‐transplant nutrition management

TPN was initiated within 24 h of transplant for all patients. Oral or enteral nutrition was initiated with the return of allograft function, typically within 5 to 7 days post‐transplant, and indicated by an active stoma. Enteral feeding was accomplished via various routes including surgically placed percutaneous tubes in the allograft jejunum, nasojejunal tube or nasogastric tube. Enteral feeds advanced gradually, typically by 10 mL every day until the target rate was reached. Oral feeding was usually commenced concurrently with enteral feeding and progressed more rapidly once the patient tolerated target enteral feeds. TPN was discontinued once all caloric needs were met using the oral and/or enteral route.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as median (interquartile range (IQR)). Categorical variables are expressed as number (percentage). Survival was calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method in R version 4.4.1.

The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Austin Health (VicTRI‐19 485).

Results

Nineteen patients received a total of 20 IT between July 2010 and April 2026. Table 2 shows demographics and clinical characteristics of transplant recipients. There were 14 adult (74%) and five paediatric (26%) patients, with a median age at first transplant of 38.2 years (range: 18.4–54.2 years) and 10.6 years (range: 5.4–13.6 years) for adult and paediatric patients respectively. There were 10 (50%) combined liver‐intestine transplants, three (15%) isolated intestine and seven (35%) multivisceral (n = 3 full multivisceral, n = 4 modified multivisceral). Thirteen transplants (65%) were colon inclusive. Two patients (10%) with concurrent renal failure requiring pre‐transplant renal replacement therapy also received simultaneous kidney transplant. One patient with loss of abdominal domain received a full thickness abdominal wall graft in addition to their intestinal transplant. Eight patients (40%) had a total of 14 episodes of rejection. Of these, five were graded severe. One resulted in graft loss, as above, whilst the other four were rescued with medical therapy. The median onset of rejection was 7.5 months post‐transplant (range: 0.5–64 months). There was one episode of GVHD) involving the skin only in an adult patient. This was successfully managed with immunosuppression minimisation. There was one episode of EBV‐driven PTLD, managed with immunosuppression reduction and rituximab following treatment of severe rejection.

All patients attained enteral autonomy prior to discharge at a median of 21 days (IQR 17‐30 days) post‐transplant. Two patients with severe rejection required temporary resumption of TPN but were weaned once bowel function and nutritional status normalised. All other patients continued with an unrestricted oral diet. The median BMI for adult patients at last follow‐up was 22.6 (IQR 20.1‐23.9) and was not significantly different from pre‐transplant BMI (P > 0.05). Four of the five paediatric recipients with growth data beyond 12 months after transplant had improved growth on centile charts or had reached BMI >20 into adulthood.

For patients with at least 12 months of follow‐up (n = 14), 71% (n = 10) had at least one unplanned readmission to hospital, with a median of two unplanned admissions each (range: 1–12). Ten of the 14 patients (71%) with more than 1 year of follow‐up returned to employment or schooling.

Discussion

IT is now established as a viable option for carefully selected patients with irreversible IF in Australia and New Zealand. Whilst we acknowledge our program has a low volume of transplant activity, outcomes demonstrated here are thus far comparable to high‐volume international centres. The first four IT recipients have survived beyond 10 years. Importantly, all patients achieved full enteral autonomy prior to discharge from hospital, and the incidence of unplanned hospital readmission and post‐transplant complications was low. Rates of return to work and schooling are higher in our cohort than those published in the available literature in IT and other solid organ transplant recipients, 13 , 14 and is a positive quality‐of‐life indicator for IT recipients. 13 , 14

The most recent report from the Intestinal Transplant Registry (ITR) describes the global clinical experience of IT encompassing a total of 5507 transplants, performed between January 1985 and September 2025 at 98 centres, primarily in North America (70%) and Europe/UK (20%). 15 Global IT activity peaked in 2007–2008, with approximately 250 transplants per year, and has gradually declined since this time. 6 , 15 Activity since 2017 has remained stable, with 100 to 150 transplants performed worldwide each year. 6 Although the total proportion of paediatric and adult transplants is evenly split at 50%, since 2011 there has been a trend towards an increasing number of adult recipients who now account for two‐thirds of transplant surgery each year and is consistent with our activity in Australia. 6 This trend is likely to continue as improved IF management allows TPN‐dependent paediatric patients to reach adulthood without the need for IT.

There is wide agreement that patients with IF receiving TPN should be managed by a specialist team experienced in all aspects of IF with the ability to regularly assess the progression of intestinal rehabilitation efforts and consider patients for IT in the event of developing significant complications. 4 , 10 , 16 Patients most at risk of complications include those with ultrashort bowel including end duodenostomy, microvillous inclusion disease, multiple enterocutaneous fistulae, radiation enteritis, IF after bariatric surgery and loss of two of four above‐diaphragm central veins. 10 Early transplant referral of these patients is critical as advanced organ failure coupled with long wait list times in our region undoubtedly impacts patient outcomes. Early assessment may also improve donor organ utilisation by increasing the likelihood of patients requiring an isolated intestinal transplant prior to the onset of liver failure, a factor that is imperative for reducing waiting list time and mortality. Overall, IT wait list mortality is disproportionately high when compared with that for other solid organ transplants 16 reaching 30% for those awaiting combined liver‐intestine transplant, 11 and approximately 10% for those requiring an isolated intestine. 17

Survival following IT has improved markedly in the past 20 years and is thought to be related to enhanced IF management by multidisciplinary nutrition support teams, earlier referral for transplant consideration and advances in transplant surgery and immunosuppression therapy. 18 , 19 Post‐transplant survival remains inferior to that of other solid‐organ transplants; however, appropriate selection of patients for IT is crucial. Early procedures prior to 2000 had a 1‐year survival of 50% and 5‐ to 10‐year survival ranging from 20% to 40%. 20 In the present era, 1‐year graft survival is approximately 82% for both children and adults. 15 Centres with larger transplant volume fare better, with up to 90% graft survival reported at 1 year. 19 , 21 , 22 Pooled International Intestinal Transplant Registry (IIRTA) data indicates 5‐year graft survival rates in paediatric and adult patients transplanted since 2020 of 70% and 65% respectively. 15 Again, large‐volume centres report superior medium‐term survival outcomes compared with the pooled registry data of between 66% and 72% 5‐year survival in both adult and paediatric cohorts. 19 , 21 , 22 Both 1‐year and 5‐year conditional graft and patient survival for our Australian cohort compares favourably with results reported by high‐volume centres.

The perpetual debate regarding the superiority of HPN versus IT based on their head‐to‐head survival comparisons is generally flawed. Most patients receiving long‐term TPN do not develop life‐threatening complications of PN, have a correspondingly better prognosis to that of IT and would therefore not benefit from IT. A large multicentre European study identified only 16% of adults and 35% of children on HPN for IF were appropriate IT candidates. 23 However, for patients with a valid indication for IT, continuation of their TPN is by definition likely to carry a high risk of mortality in the short‐ to medium‐term. 18 A prospective study comparing the survival rates of 153 adult and paediatric IT candidates found the 3‐year survival of transplanted patients to be 89%, compared with 85% who had central venous catheter‐related complications but were not transplanted, and 70% for those with IFALD who did not undergo transplant. 24 These data support the potential life‐saving role of timely IT for patients with IF experiencing serious complications, in particular those with IFALD.

Within Australia, a national HPN registry has recently been established that reveals there were 328 patients on HPN in 2024 (244 adults and 84 children). 25 Whilst it is unknown how many of these patients have life‐threatening complications of their PN and would therefore be candidates for IT consideration, extrapolating the above European HPN data to Australia suggests approximately 39 adults and 29 children may have an indication for IT. Based on current transplant referrals and activity, it is therefore possible there is significant under‐referral of these patients to the Australian Intestinal Transplant Service.

In addition to survival, the ability to restore enteral autonomy is also a requisite for successful IT. All of the patients in our series discontinued TPN prior to hospital discharge at a median of 21 days after transplant, which is consistent with IIRTA data of TPN cessation in more than 90% of recipients, 6 as well as large series reporting IT recipients maintain enteral autonomy in the long‐term. 19 , 26 , 27 Nutrition support after IT encompasses both parenteral and enteral nutrition. PN is required in the acute post‐transplant period until ileus resolves, with enteral or oral feeding commencing as early as possible, typically within 3–7 days postoperatively. There is no international consensus regarding feeding rate, type of formula or route of feeding after IT. Our practice is to commence small volume enteral feeds with polymeric formula once ileus resolves, with oral fluids permitted alongside enteral feeds as soon as the patient desires. Enteral and oral nutrition is gradually increased in volume as tolerated and PN is concurrently weaned whilst monitoring the recipient's clinical, biochemical and functional nutrition indices. This early transition to enteral feeding enhances graft adaptation, and enteral autonomy permits removal of central venous access to minimise postoperative line complications. Unrestricted oral diet is encouraged to allow for cessation of enteral tube feeding as soon as dietary adequacy and target nutritional status is achieved. Although transition off PN also occurs in most paediatric patients, transition to oral diet is typically slower compared with adults where factors such as food aversion and delayed oro‐pharyngeal motor development are more prevalent and hence a longer reliance on enteral tube feeding may be required. 28

Ongoing vigilance in monitoring nutritional status of graft recipients in the medium‐ to long‐term is paramount; however, irrespective of enteral autonomy. Weight and growth at last clinical review for our patients were generally stable within the healthy weight range or improved on centile charts, though there was large variation in individual BMI (range: 15–35). Several studies report improved nutritional status after transplant reflected by increased BMI, maintenance of normal serum albumin and serum trace elements, as well as improved skeletal health as measured by dual‐energy x‐ray absorptiometry. 27 , 29 However, other studies have reported poor anthropometric and functional muscle measures persisting 1 year after transplant in adult patients. 30 Most children experience normal linear growth after IT, though there is conflicting data regarding the ability for paediatric patients to experience catch‐up growth, given delayed growth is almost universal in this population prior to transplant. A single‐centre Spanish study found both weight and height Z‐scores increased significantly for paediatric patients by 2 years after transplant, with children achieving normal nutritional status in the long‐term. 31 Conversely, a larger multicentre European study showed that early catch‐up growth was not sustained after the first year post‐transplant. 28 The widespread use of steroids for immunosuppression and severely delayed growth before transplantation in the latter cohort are possible explanations.

Eight patients (40%) in our cohort experienced at least one episode of rejection at a median onset of 7.5 months after transplant, which is consistent with the rejection rate reported in the literature between 30% to 50% within the first year after transplant. 32 The rate of PTLD was low in our cohort (5%), comparable to that reported in the international literature of between 6% to 13%. 33 , 34 All intestine‐containing grafts, both adult and paediatric, can be performed with or without inclusion of a segment of colon in continuity with the jejunoileum. Recent data demonstrate increased likelihood of formed stool after stoma closure, improved quality of life and increased likelihood of complete enteral independence with colon‐containing IT. 35 Global data demonstrate that the inclusion of colon in intestinal allografts has increased from 10% in 2004 to 55% in 2022. 6 Similarly, 65% of our cohort have received colon‐inclusive grafts (100% of transplants since 2020).

Limitations of this study include its observational design, the retrospective nature of data collection and the small sample size. Although no patients were lost to follow‐ up, it is possible we may not have captured all hospital readmissions to local services within the 12months after transplant. Historically, low organ donation rates in Australia 7 in combination with the rarity of chronic IF in a geographically dispersed low‐population‐density country will continue to limit transplant volume in our region; however, this should not diminish the importance of maintaining this service for those individuals who will continue to benefit from IT in the future.

Conclusion

Our series is encouraging for both the local patient population who have a lifelong dependence on HPN or inoperable desmoid tumour, as well as practitioners caring for these individuals. IF is one of the rarest organ failures and the availability of dedicated expertise to manage the complex needs of people living with IF who require IT has previously been lacking in Australia and New Zealand. 36 Promising data regarding patient and graft survival, our experience in a broad range of intestine‐containing transplant procedures for both children and adults and a rate of complications after transplant that mirror global best practice centres augurs well for the ongoing success of our program. We strongly recommend all patients with IF be referred to an expert tertiary centre for multidisciplinary intestinal rehabilitation management aiming to restore enteral autonomy, with further onwards referral to the Australian Intestinal Transplant Service for those who develop life‐threatening complications of their condition. The field continues to face the challenges of immunologic monitoring and longevity of allografts, with future research focused on these areas to ensure IT continues to be an acceptable, life‐saving procedure in appropriately selected patients.
