A novel cancer gene therapy achieving reciprocal regulation of PTEN and PARP1 expression
Authors: Yasuo Otsuka, Tomohiro Watanabe, Masatoshi Kudo
Published: 2026

Main text

A recent study published in Molecular Therapy Oncology by Hu et al. described a novel gene therapy for advanced prostate cancer. This gene therapy inhibits the proliferation of prostate cancer cells through reciprocal regulation of phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) and poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1) expression.1 Hu et al. developed a lipid nanoparticle (LNP), named mPsiP@miLAND, in which PTEN mRNA and PARP1-specific small interfering RNA (siRNA) were encapsulated. Targeting the delivery of mPsiP@miLAND into cancer cells led to the enhancement and reduction of PTEN and PARP1 expressions, respectively.1 They also prepared two types of targeting vectors: siP@miLAND, an LNP encapsulated with PARP1-specific siRNA, and mP@miLAND, an LNP encapsulated with PTEN mRNA. siP@miLAND was made to reduce PARP1 expression without affecting PTEN activity, whereas mP@miLAND enhanced PTEN expression without affecting PARP1 activity. Growth of prostate cancer cells was significantly inhibited in orthotopic prostate cancer models upon treatment with siP@miLNAD or mP@miLAND compared to control nanoparticles. Strikingly, the therapeutic effects on prostate cancer cells were much greater in mice treated with mPsiP@miLAND than in those treated with siP@miLNAD or mP@miLAND.1 Thus, Hu et al. developed a novel gene therapy for prostate cancer by restoring PTEN activation and inhibiting PARP1 expression in a simultaneous manner.

The development and progression of prostate cancer cells are driven by androgen receptor (AR) signaling. Therefore, androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) constitutes the backbone of systemic therapy.2 The addition of potent AR signaling inhibitors to ADT is effective, even in patients with advanced prostate cancer. However, most patients with prostate cancer eventually become resistant to ADT and progress to castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC). Progression from castration-sensitive disease to CRPC involves three types of genomic alterations (Figure 1A).2,3 Gain-of-function AR mutations and AR splice variants (ARVs) are the most common genomic alterations in CRPC, with approximately 50%–70% of patients exhibiting enhanced activation of AR. Various AR mutations and ARVs amplify AR signaling pathways by enhancing AR stability and intratumoral AR biosynthesis.2,3 The second most frequent genomic abnormality is the inactivation of the tumor suppressor PTEN due to loss-of-function mutations or deletion. Approximately 40% of patients with CRPC experience PTEN loss, resulting in excessive activation of the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)-AKT-mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway (Figure 1A). Activation of the PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway due to PTEN loss causes increased proliferation of cancer cells and cell cycle progression, and PTEN loss is correlated with shorter overall survival.2,3 Moreover, AKT activation due to PTEN loss amplifies AR-mediated signaling pathways through the interaction between AKT and AR in CRPC.4 Therefore, restoration of PTEN expression by mPsiP@miLAND exerts anti-cancer effects through the inhibition of pathways mediated not only by PI3K-AKT-mTOR but also by AR. The third genomic abnormality seen in CRPC is mutations in homologous recombination genes, such as BRCA1 and BRCA2.2,3 PARP1 is a nuclear protein that detects and repairs damaged DNA to maintain genomic stability.5 DNA repair by PARP1 is necessary for the survival of prostate cancer cells harboring mutations in BRCA1 or BRCA2. Reduction of PARP1 expression by the administration of mPsiP@miLAND suppressed the proliferation of prostate cancer cells caused by BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations. As mentioned above, genomic abnormalities causing CRPC are characterized by the activation of AR, loss of PTEN, and DNA repair by PARP1.2,3,4 Therefore, inhibitors of AR, AKT, and PARP1 are utilized in CRPC. However, resistance to AR pathway inhibitors (ARPIs) is often accompanied by the loss of PTEN. Thus, combination therapy targeting both AR and AKT-PI3K-mTOR using different inhibitors may be considered for ARPI-resistant CRPC. In this context, mPsiP@miLAND is attractive because this novel nanoparticle inhibits signaling pathways mediated not only by AR but also by PI3-AKT through the restoration of PTEN expression. A key question from this study is how to identify patients who benefit from mPsiP@miLAND. Theoretically, CRPCs harboring loss-of-function mutations in PTEN and BRCA1/BRCA2 are ideal candidates for mPsiP@miLAND.

One of the major problems of RNA therapeutics is the instability of RNA. To overcome this obstacle, Hu et al. utilized chemically modified siPARP1 and introduced 5′ cap and a 3′ poly (A) tail to the coding sequence of mPTEN.1 These modifications to siPARP1 and mPTEN enabled the authors to achieve reciprocal regulation of PARP1 and PTEN through protection from exonucleases. In addition, the authors confirmed that mPsiP@miLAND encapsulated with PTEN mRNA and PARP1-specific siRNA maintained stable and uniform morphology for 14 days after preparation. Thus, the authors successfully developed a novel LNP that can target prostate cancer cells displaying loss and enhancement of PTEN and PARP1 activation, respectively. The authors reported the efficacy of mPsiP@miLAND by intratumoral injection.1 Therefore, intratumoral injection of mPsiP@miLAND might be a suitable clinical application. However, we need to consider the risk of delivery of mPsiP@miLAND into normal cells, in particular, immune cells. Given that PTEN plays multifaceted roles in both innate and adaptive immunity, PTEN delivery into immune cells might cause excessive or suppressed immune responses (see below).6

In vivo delivery of mPsiP@miLAND inhibited the proliferation of prostate cancer cells in the orthotopic model. mPsiP@miLAND was assumed to exert anti-cancer effects upon delivery into prostate cancer cells. However, gene delivery into immune cells by mPsiP@miLAND may contribute to tumor volume reduction, as PTEN plays multifaceted roles in both adaptive and innate immune responses. Expression of PTEN in immune cells is required for antiviral immune and regulatory T cell (Treg) responses.6,7,8 PTEN promotes the nuclear translocation of interferon (IFN) regulatory factor 3, a critical transcription factor for antiviral type I IFN responses, via phosphatase activity.7 Thus, restoration of PTEN activity by mPsiP@miLAND may increase the production of type I IFNs by innate immune cells such as macrophages and dendritic cells. Accordingly, it is possible that targeting the delivery of mPsiP@miLAND into innate immune cells promotes type I IFN responses with potent anti-cancer activity at the innate immunity level. In contrast to the enhancement of anti-cancer innate immunity by PTEN expression, Shrestha et al. reported that PTEN expression in Treg cells is necessary for the suppression of proinflammatory T helper type 1 (Th1) responses.8 Given that Th1 responses play indispensable roles in effective immunosurveillance, the delivery of mPsiP@miLAND into Treg cells may result in the suppression of anti-cancer Th1 responses at the adaptive immunity level. Thus, PTEN has contradictory roles in cancer immunity: expression of PTEN in macrophages and dendritic cells suppresses growth of cancer cells through type I IFN responses, whereas tumor growth is promoted by activation of PTEN in Tregs. However, Hu et al. did not address the effects of mPsiP@miLAND on cancer immunosurveillance. It would be of interest to examine whether administration of mPsiP@miLAND mediates tumor reduction by altering the cancer immune microenvironment.

In conclusion, Hu et al. developed a novel gene therapy for advanced prostate cancer. This gene therapy has been developed to normalize two major genetic abnormalities in prostate cancer: the PTEN-PI3K-AKT pathway and the PARP1-mediated DNA repair pathway. Administration of mPsiP@miLAND targeting both the PTEN-PI3K-AKT and PARP1-mediated DNA repair pathways represents a promising treatment option, as current drugs for advanced prostate cancer inhibit only one pathway.
