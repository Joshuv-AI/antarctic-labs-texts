Experimental and computational study of corrosion protection of carbon steel by Lavandula angustifolia extract–Zn(II) hybrid system in saline solution
Authors: Roshana Sattari, Gholam Reza Khayati, Esmaeel Darezereshki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSteel corrosion is effectively protected using plant-based inhibitors. However, organic inhibitors like plant extracts often show limited performance in neutral environments. To overcome this limitation, this study investigated the corrosion behavior of a combined organic-inorganic inhibitor, consisting of Lavandula angustifolia (LA) plant extract and zinc nitrate, for low-carbon steel in a 3.5% NaCl solution. Electrochemical techniques, including EIS and PDP, along with surface characterization and theoretical modeling, were employed to assess the corrosion inhibition efficiency of this hybrid system. The icorr value for the LA–Zn²⁺ inhibitor at 400 ppm LA and 400 ppm Zn decreased from 11.51 to 0.83 µA/cm², corresponding to an inhibition efficiency of 92%, and showed better performance than the individual inhibitors. This finding was further supported by EIS measurements, where the Rct increased from 862 to 5956 Ω cm². This indicated the formation of a highly protective interfacial layer. FTIR and UV-vis spectroscopy confirmed the interaction between LA’s functional groups and Zn²⁺ ions. CA measurements (19.7° for the blank and 40.12° for the optimal sample) and SEM/EDS analyses further validated the formation of protective surface layers. Additionally, MC, MD, and QM simulations revealed the strong binding affinity and stability of the LA–Zn²⁺ complexes, offering insights into their protective behavior. This study presents LA–Zn²⁺ complexes as eco-friendly, efficient alternatives to traditional corrosion inhibitors.Keywords: Corrosion inhibitor, Extract of Lavandula angustifolia, EIS, Molecular simulation, Saline solution, Sustainable inhibitionSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

Carbon steel is extensively utilized across various industries because of its exceptional characteristics, such as malleability, flexibility, high strength, hardness and reasonable cost1,2. However, carbon steel is highly reactive with the surrounding environment due to its elevated surface activity. This makes making it susceptible to corrosion, which can lead to a decrease in its mechanical properties and cause economic losses3,4. To reduce the corrosion of metals such as steel, different strategies have been implemented, including cathodic and anodic protection, the utilization of corrosion inhibitors and the application of protective coatings5,6. Among these methods, the application of organic and inorganic inhibitors is recognized as one of the most effective and cost-efficient strategies for protecting metals from corrosion and extending their lifespan1. Organic inhibitors have functional groups with hetero atoms and aromatic structures that have the ability to bind to the surface of metals due to the presence of free electron pairs. Recent studies have increasingly emphasized the use of sustainable organic inhibitors, particularly plant extracts. These compounds are considered a safe and sustainable solution for mitigating metal corrosion, as they are biodegradable, cost-effective, and exhibit low toxicity7,8. In near-neutral aqueous solutions, green organic inhibitors typically exhibit limited inhibitory efficiency9. Therefore, to overcome this limitation and enhance corrosion inhibition performance, the combination of these inhibitors with inorganic cations has been proposed as an effective and cost-efficient strategy. Numerous studies have investigated the synergistic effect between organic and inorganic inhibitors to improve their efficiency in preventing metal corrosion10. Shadi et al.11 examined the synergistic impact of Laurus nobilis (LN) plant and Zn ions on mild steel (MS) corrosion prevention in a 3.5% NaCl solution. Their findings revealed that the development of a homogeneous protective film on the MS surface significantly enhanced its corrosion resistance. Mofidabadi et al.12 evaluated the anticorrosive properties of Dianthus fruit extract (DFE)–zinc (Zn) complexes on MS corrosion using both computational and experimental techniques. Electrochemical tests revealed a high inhibition efficiency of the DFE-Zn complex on MS after 24 h of immersion. Wamba-Tchio et al.13 revealed the significant effectiveness of a mixture of Ficus pumila Linn hydroalcoholic extracts at a concentration of 200 ppm and zinc cations at 50 ppm and 100 ppm concentrations in a saline corrosive environment for preventing the corrosion of XC38 steel. Table 1 presents a summarized overview of previously reported studies on plant extract–metal ions corrosion inhibitors for steel protection in saline media. However, the synergistic mechanism between many plant extracts and metal cations, such as Zn²⁺, is not yet well understood. Lavandula angustifolia (LA) is a perennial shrub classified as a member of the Lavandula genus of the family Lamiaceae14. This aromatic plant is widely recognized for its ornamental and therapeutic qualities, often used in the treatment of neurological and gastrointestinal disorders15,16. Additionally, it possesses antioxidant, antimicrobial, antifungal, and antibacterial properties17,18. The extract of Lavandula plant contains both volatile and non-volatile compounds. The non-volatile fraction is rich in flavonoids such as apigenin, isoscutellarein, luteolin, ferulic acid, chrysoeriol, and hypolaetin, along with their glycosylated derivatives, which represent the most abundant group of phenolic compounds16,17,19. These phytochemicals contain heteroatoms, which are capable of interacting with metal ions via electron pair donation, which is an essential factor in effective corrosion inhibition. The rich phenolic content and presence of heteroatoms in Lavandula angustifolia extract provide strong coordination sites for Zn²⁺ ions, enabling the formation of stable hybrid complexes on steel surfaces. Compared to other well-studied plant extracts, the unique combination of flavonoids and glycosylated phenolics in LA enhances electron donation and adsorption, making it particularly suitable as a green corrosion inhibitor in saline environments. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that investigates the synergistic anticorrosion behavior of Lavandula angustifolia extract–Zn²⁺ hybrid system. FT-IR and UV-Vis analyses reveal the presence of organic bonds and heteroatoms in the Lavandula angustifolia extract molecules, as well as the formation of complexes with zinc cations. The impact of inhibitors on steel surfaces was examined through SEM/EDS analyses and contact angle measurements after immersion in the corrosive solution. The combined effect of the inhibitors on corrosion inhibition of steel was investigated through electrochemical analyses, including EIS and PDP. To study the adsorption behavior of the hybrid inhibitors on the steel surface, DFT modeling, MD and MC simulations were employed.

Experimental and theoretical section

Preparation of materials

The steel samples used for surface analysis and electrochemical experiments were mechanically cut into cubic specimens with dimensions of 1 × 1 × 1 cm³. The surfaces of the steel samples were then polished using silicon carbide sandpaper, with grit sizes ranging from 220 to 1200. The samples were polished and then thoroughly cleaned with acetone and deionized water to ensure the removal of any contaminants. The cleaned samples were air-dried at ambient temperature. The Lavandula angustifolia (LA) plant was purchased from a local herbal store in Kerman province (Iran), and is a commonly cultivated plant. In order to prepare the aqueous LA extract, 15 g of dried LA plant was added to 500 mL of deionized water and heated under stirring at 70 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, the solution was filtered through filter paper, centrifuged, and then allowed to dry under ambient conditions to obtain the powdered extract. For the preparation of the electrolyte, zinc nitrate tetrahydrate (Zn(NO₃)₂·4H₂O) and NaCl were sourced from Merck. The inhibitor-containing solutions were prepared to explore the potential synergistic effects. These solutions were prepared in separate chloride baths, including a blank electrolyte (without inhibitors), as well as electrolytes containing 800 ppm LA extract, 800 ppm zinc cations, and various mixtures of LA extract and zinc cations at ratios of 400:400, 600:200, and 200:600 ppm The process preparation route is outlined in Fig. 1.

Schematic rout of preparation of LA extract powder and the corrosive solutions containing LA–Zn2+ complexes.

Characterization

The electrochemical experiments were conducted with an EG&G 263 A Potentiostat & Galvanostat electrochemical workstation, equipped with a three-electrode system consisting of a platinum counter electrode, a carbon steel working electrode, and a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) as the reference. Nyquist plots were obtained by scanning in the frequency range of 100 kHz to 0.01 Hz at the open circuit potential (OCP) versus SCE with an amplitude of 10 mV. Steel samples were immersed in the prepared electrolytes, and measurements were recorded over time. Electrochemical spectra fitting was performed using ZView software (version 3.1, sai-zview.software.informer.com) After 72 h of immersion in the corrosive solution, polarization measurements were performed. both with and without inhibitors. The potential was swept from − 250 mV to + 250 mV versus OCP at a scan rate of 1 mV/s. Notably, all analyses were carried out at ambient laboratory temperature (25 ± 2 °C) and a relative humidity of approximately 23%. The analysis of the results obtained from three replicates at each concentration indicates that the standard deviation (SD) for EIS data ranges between 4% and 7%, while for PDP data, it varies between 2 and 5%. The morphology and chemical composition of the surface layers formed on the steel samples after 48 h of immersion in inhibited and uninhibited electrolytes were analyzed using a SEM equipped with EDS (TESCAN-Vega3, TESCAN Co, Czech Republic). To ensure conductivity and obtain high-resolution images, a thin layer of gold was coated on the sample surfaces. The electron transfers of functional groups in the formed complexes and LA extract was analyzed using UV-Visible spectroscopy (Scan Drop, Analytic Jena Co, Germany (in the 200–800 nm wavelength range. FT-IR spectra were obtained using the FT-IR spectrophotometer (Tensor 27, Bruker Co, Germany) in the 400–4000 cm⁻¹ wavenumber range. Finally, the shape of water droplets on inhibited and uninhibited surfaces was captured after 48 h of immersion, and contact angle measurements were performed to evaluate surface wettability.

Atomic and molecular scale modeling study details

In the design of the LA–Zn2+ corrosion inhibitor, the structure of certain biomolecules from the LA extract, shown in Fig. 1, includes ferulic acid (FerA) and apigenin (APG), which are complexed with zinc cations through covalent bonds to their functional groups. Figure 2 illustrated all types of the organic-inorganic inhibitors designed. To gain precise insights into the adsorption characteristics of these complex inhibitors on the specimen substrate, MD and MC were performed on all these designed organic-inorganic inhibitors. Also, the structure of each inhibitor was optimized via the DFT method in the DMol3 module, with the GGA/PBE functional and DNP basis set24,25. The electronic properties of the optimized complexes, such as the Highest Occupied Molecular Orbital (HOMO) and Lowest Unoccupied Molecular Orbital (LUMO), along with their corresponding energy values (EHOMO and ELUMO), and the energy gap (ΔEL−H = ELUMO − EHOMO) were computed. Additionally, global hardness (η), electronegativity (χ), ionization potential (I), electron affinity (A), and the amount of electron transfer between the inhibitor and the surface of metal (ΔN) were evaluated. These properties were calculated using mathematical equations in references26,28,28.

Chemical structures of ferulic acid, apigenin and their complex with Zn ions.

To examine the adsorption of organic-inorganic inhibitors on the specimen surface (iron (110)), MD and MC simulations were performed using Forcite modules and the Adsorption Locator in BIOVIA Materials Studio (version 2017, Dassault Systèmes, France; https://www.3ds.com/products/biovia/materials-studio). with the COMPASS force field29. For constructing the iron (iron (110)) substrate, a periodic repetition of 18 × 18 was utilized. The adsorption of inhibitors on the iron (110) substrate was identified in the gas phase using MC simulation. In MD simulations, an electrolyte layer consisting of 500 water molecules, 10 sodium cations, and 10 chloride anions was added as the liquid phase to the highest atomic layer of the metal with fixed positions, using the layer Builder module. The energy of the prepared simulation boxes for APG-Zn-APG, FerA-Zn-FerA and FerA-Zn-APG was minimized under 20,000 steps using the Smart algorithm. Finally, the molecular simulation was carried out for a time period of 0.1 ns in the NVT ensemble with a time step of 1 fs and temperature control at 298 K with a nose-hoover thermostat1,30,31. The calculations were based on electrostatic interactions and Vander Waals computations using the atom-based cutoff method and Ewald summation. The binding energy and interaction energy () of LA–Zn2+ complexes on the metal surface were calculated using the expression provided in the references28,32,33.

Results and discussion

Solutions characterization using UV-Vis analysis

UV-Vis analysis was used to investigate the interactions and confirm the complexation between the functional groups of LA biomolecules and the Zn cations of solutions containing LA and LA–Zn2+ before immersing the samples (Fig. 3). The absorption peaks observed at 200 and 221 nm, along with the broad and low-intensity peaks at 247 and 217 nm, indicate the presence of C=C bonds in the benzene-aromatic rings of LA biomolecules in solutions containing LA and LA–Zn2+, which are associated with π–π∗ electronic transitions34. Additionally, two absorption bands at 279 and 285 nm were assigned to n-π* transitions of carbonyl (C=O) bonds in the samples35. These spectral changes are primarily attributed to the creation of chemical bonds between the LA extract and Zn²⁺36. Moreover, the notable enhancement in absorption peak intensities at 200 and 221 nm in the LA–Zn2+ sample indicates the complexation of LA chemical constituents with zinc ions20.

UV–Vis spectrum of the chloride solution containing LA extract and LA–Zn2+ complex.

CA analysis

Measurements of the CA of water droplets were conducted to investigate the surface chemistry and the formation of LA–Zn2+ complexes on the sample surfaces. Figure 4 illustrates the CA of specimens that were immersed in saline solutions in the presence and absence of inhibitors. The lowest CA values were observed for the uninhibited sample and the sample exposed to 800 ppm Zn. This is probably a result of the formation of a porous corrosion product layer (Fe₂O₃ and Fe₃O₄) on the surface of the uninhibited sample37 and the formation of zinc hydroxide/oxide on the specimen immersed in 800 ppm Zn, which facilitates water molecules adsorption due to hydrogen bond formation at the specimen interface38. On the other hand, the reduced hydrophilicity and wettability of the inhibited samples indicate that the adsorption of complexes onto the surface facilitates the formation of a protective barrier against the corrosive environment. Notably, the sample coated with the LA–Zn2+ complex exhibited the highest CA, confirming the combined effect of LA extract and Zn cations in the corrosive 3.5% NaCl solution39.

Water CA of steel specimens exposed to saline solution with and without inhibitors.

FTIR analysis

Figure 5 illustrates the presence of various ligands in the organic structures of the LA extract, as well as the possible interactions and complex formation between metal cations and the organic compounds of LA. The peaks observed at wavelengths (3416 cm⁻¹, 3442 cm⁻¹) can be attributed to the vibrational stretching of phenolic or alcoholic O–H bonds7,40. The peaks at (2929 cm⁻¹, 2924 cm⁻¹) correspond to aliphatic or aromatic C–H bonds2,8. The characteristic bands observed at (1632 cm⁻¹, 1609 cm⁻¹), (1386 cm⁻¹, 1404 cm⁻¹), (1270 cm⁻¹, 1269 cm⁻¹), and (1046 cm⁻¹, 1065 cm⁻¹) are assigned to C=C vibrations of benzene rings, –CH₃ or COO⁻ groups, C–O–H vibrations, and C–O stretching vibrations, respectively9,11,23,42,44. The peak appearing at (1709 cm⁻¹) in the LA sample corresponds to the C=O bond20. Moreover, the absorption band at 545 cm⁻¹ is associated with the stretching vibration of the M–O bond9,42. The disappearance and reduced intensity of peaks in the (LA–Zn2+) sample compared to the LA sample are likely due to the bonding of organic molecules with Zn or Fe, confirming the formation of LA–Fe2+ and LA–Zn2+ complexes.

FTIR spectrum plot of LA and LA–Zn2+ powders.

SEM/EDS investigations

Figure 6 illustrates the surface morphology analysis obtained by SEM and EDS spectra for metal samples immersed in a corrosive environment under inhibitor-free conditions and in the presence of zinc, LA and LA–Zn2+ complexes at ambient temperature. According to the results, the surface of the blank sample is completely covered with corrosion products, showing a snowflake-like morphology9. This coating indicates severe corrosion and the inability of the corrosion products to form a stable protective layer on the surface. Furthermore, the EDS spectrum shows a higher oxygen content in the blank sample compared to the inhibited samples, confirming the intensified corrosion process. In the 800 ppm LA sample, a thick and heterogeneous layer with large cracks and poor adhesion is formed on the surface, appearing as a flake structure23. These cracks are probably caused by the release of stress caused by water evaporation42. The presence of such cracks facilitates the penetration of attacking ions into the metal surface, thereby reducing the inhibitory efficiency of LA in reducing corrosion. For the 800 ppm Zn sample, a dense and thick layer of zinc oxide/hydroxide precipitates is observed, forming a flower-like crystal structure on the surface43,44. The EDS analysis revealed the presence of zinc. However, their surface coverage is insufficient to develop a protective layer capable of effectively inhibiting the ingress of corrosive species and the diffusion of water molecules. In the 400 LA: 400 Zn sample, a dense, homogeneous, and relatively uniform protective film is observed. This film effectively blocks the penetration of corrosive agents into the metal substrate. The EDS spectrum confirms the presence of zinc, carbon and oxygen. This indicates the formation of complexes between the organic components of LA and Zn ions. In general, the findings indicate that the addition of the LA–Zn2+ complex as an inhibitor leads to a reduction in the formation of corrosion products. Thus, it shows superior protective performance.

The findings demonstrate that the inhibition efficiency of 200 LA: 600 Zn, 400 LA: 400 Zn and 600 LA: 200 Zn mixtures exceeds that of the individual inhibitors. Among these, the 400 LA: 400 Zn complex, achieving 92.87% efficiency, exhibited the lowest and the highest inhibition effectiveness. According to ECORR variations, an inhibitor is classified as a mixed-type inhibitor if the corrosion potential shift of the inhibited samples relative to the uninhibited sample is less than ± 85 mV. However, when the shift is more than ± 85 mV, the inhibitor is categorized as cathodic or anodic1,7. The corrosion potential displacement of 800 ppm LA, 200 LA: 600 Zn, and 600 LA: 200 Zn compared to the uninhibited sample ranges from 13 to 37 mV, indicating that the mixed inhibitors act with a dominant anodic effect in 3.5% NaCl solution. In contrast, the 400 LA: 400 Zn and 800 ppm Zn complexes exhibit a corrosion potential shift greater than 85 mV towards more positive values, classifying them as anodic inhibitors. Moreover, the variations in the cathodic and anodic Tafel slopes of the inhibited samples relative to the uninhibited sample suggest the influence of LA-derived compounds and Zn cations on the anodic and cathodic processes, thereby elucidating their role in corrosion inhibition.

EIS measurements

The Nyquist plots of the specimens immersed in the corrosive environment (3.5% NaCl) in the absence and presence of inhibitors at immersion times of 2, 5, 8, 32, 50, and 72 h are shown in Fig. 8. It was observed that inhibitors result in substantial changes to plot shape and size. Equivalent circuits serve as an effective model for representing electrochemical processes at the metal/solution interface46. Therefore, Table 3 presents the electrochemical parameters obtained from various equivalent electrical circuits shown in Fig. 9. The quality of the equivalent-circuit fitting was evaluated using the χ² parameter. The obtained χ² values were between 10⁻² and 10⁻⁴, confirming reliable and acceptable fitting accuracy for all EIS spectra. Rs, Rf, and Rct represent solution resistance, film resistance, and charge transfer resistance, respectively. CPEf (non-ideal film capacitance) and CPEdl (non-ideal double-layer capacitance) were introduced as alternatives to the ideal capacitance (C), reflecting the impact of inhibitor adsorption on the sample surface as well as surface roughness and inhomogeneity5,47,48. In the Nyquist plot of the uninhibited (blank) sample, a compressed capacitive semicircle is observed at high frequencies, while Warburg impedance (W) appears at low frequencies49,51,53. Warburg impedance is related to mass transfer phenomena, such as oxygen diffusion and corrosive ions movement at the metal/solution interface50,52. The blank sample exhibited a decreasing trend in Rct after 50 h, reaching 862 Ω/cm² at 72 h. The decrease suggests that the heterogeneous and porous corrosion layer covering the specimen is breaking down53. In inhibitor-containing samples, the presence of the Rf element at high frequencies is attributed to the adsorption of inhibitors and the formation of a protective layer on the steel surface54. The lowest Rct values were observed for the specimens containing 800 ppm Zn, 800 ppm LA, and 600 LA: 200 Zn. In contrast, the 400 LA: 400 Zn and 200 LA: 600 Zn samples exhibited the highest Rct values, reaching 5956 and 5725 Ω/cm², respectively. This suggests the creation of a corrosion-resistant layer on the steel surface, where higher resistance is associated with a lower corrosion rate55,57,59. The 400 LA: 400 Zn sample exhibited diffusion-controlled behavior at low frequencies for 8 h. Over time, the development of a protective layer and the obstruction of active anodic and cathodic sites by the organic/inorganic complex resulted in the disappearance of the Warburg element. In contrast, the 600 LA: 200 Zn sample remained under diffusion control at low frequencies and demonstrated the lowest charge transfer resistance. This behavior may be attributed to the accumulation of complex species in the electrolyte, which hindered the inhibitor from reaching the specimen surface, thereby preventing the formation of a continuous protective layer58. In general, the deposition of zinc chelates at concentrations of 600 and 400 ppm with organic compounds of the LA plant (such as ferulic acid and apigenin) at concentrations of 200 and 400 ppm on the sample surface in neutral chloride solution blocks the penetration of corrosive ions, resulting in a reduced corrosion rate56,59.

Computational studies

DFT

DFT computations were conducted to analyze the electronic surface properties of the inhibitors on the specimen surface. Figure 10 demonstrates the distribution of HOMO and LUMO regions for the optimized structures of the APG -Zn- APG, FerA -Zn- FerA and FerA -Zn- APG complexes. The quantum chemical parameters are listed in Table 4. The ability to donate and accept electrons from the steel surface is described by the HOMO and LUMO, respectively7. All the inhibitors in Fig. 11, including the lone pair electrons from the oxygen atoms and some carbon atoms as well as the π-electrons in the ring structures of FerA and APG compounds, possess the ability to function as both electron donors and acceptors. The zinc metal atom in the FerA-APG-Zn-4 and FerA-APG-Zn-6 structures acts as the LUMO region. According to the literature4,35, if ΔN is positive, the inhibitor tends to donate electrons to the specimen surface. However, if ΔN is negative, the inhibitor prefers to accept electrons from the specimen surface. A significant chemical parameter for improving corrosion inhibition is the energy gap (ΔE = ELUMO − EHOMO), with a lower ΔE indicating better inhibition33. The significant corrosion inhibition efficiency of the inhibitors is attributed to their low ΔE, high σ, low η, and high χ in the corrosive environment60,61. Among the studied complexes, the FerA-Zn-3 complex showed the highest inhibition effect. According to the ΔN values, all the complexes exhibit a tendency to donate electrons to the specimen surface.

MC and MD simulation

The adsorption behavior and dynamic performance of organic-inorganic inhibitors over the specimen surface in a saline medium was investigated using MC and MD simulations. Figure 11 indicates the side and top views of the most optimized final cell configurations from the adsorption of APG-Zn- APG, FerA-Zn- FerA and FerA-Zn- APG complexes on the Fe (110) surface. The side views of the simulations indicate that all complexes adsorb in a parallel and flat orientation on the sample surface. This results lead to maximum surface coverage and enhanced inhibition efficiency62,63. Table 5 illustrates the different types of energy parameters and Table 6 presents the negative donor-acceptor interaction values ​​of the organic-inorganic inhibitors on the specimen surface. The obtained results illustrate the spontaneous adsorption of inhibitors on the Fe(110) surface in the final cells of the equilibrium simulation23. Furthermore, the adsorption energies in MD simulations are lower than those in the MC simulations. This difference arises from the solvent’s presence and its impact on inhibitor-surface interactions, which diminishes the adsorption propensity of organic-inorganic inhibitors.

Inhibition mechanism

Based on the obtained results, the possible inhibition mechanism of LA–Zn2+ complex adsorption on anodic and cathodic regions in a saline corrosive environment is schematically depicted in Fig. 12. In the absence of the inhibitor, corrosion reactions in the saline electrolyte involve iron oxidation at anodic sites and oxygen reduction at cathodic sites64. The insoluble LA–Zn2+ complexes formed in the solution can precipitate onto anodic surfaces, thereby inhibiting further corrosion53. Additionally, the zinc cations released from the LA–Zn2+ complexes interact with hydroxide ions (OH⁻) generated in the cathodic sites, resulting in the precipitation of Zn(OH)29. Plant extract components such as FerA and APG can donate electrons from heteroatom oxygen and π-electrons from aromatic rings to the vacant orbitals of iron cations8. This electron exchange facilitates the formation of insoluble LA–Zn2+ complexes, which precipitate on anodic regions, further enhancing protection. Moreover, the plant extract compounds can adsorb physically through hydrogen bonding with the metal oxide-hydroxide layer, contributing to improved surface coverage and corrosion resistance5.

Conclusion

This research explores the corrosion resistance of low-carbon steel in a saline environment by employing a combination of LA plant extract and zinc nitrate, utilizing both experimental and theoretical approaches. Electrochemical findings confirmed the protective performance of the LA–Zn2+ complex, with a predominant effect on anodic reactions. For the LA–Zn²+ inhibitor at 400 ppm LA and 400 ppm Zn, icorr reached an inhibition efficiency of 92%, which is higher than that of the single inhibitors. This improvement was also reflected in the EIS results, where the Rct increased from 862 to 5956 Ω cm², indicating the formation of a protective layer on the steel surface. FTIR and UV-VIS techniques demonstrated the formation of a complex between the organic groups of the LA plant and the zinc cation. The successful adsorption of the LA–Zn2+ complexes onto the steel surface was demonstrated through SEM/EDS analysis and contact angle (CA) measurements. Moreover, molecular simulations indicated that the inhibitors adsorb in a parallel configuration onto the Fe (110) surface with high binding energy.
