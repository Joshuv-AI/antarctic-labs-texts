Phase fluctuations in a confined fluid Preprint: AIP/123-QED
Frédéric Caupin, Alberto Zaragoza, Miguel A. Gonzalez, Chantal Valeriani

Abstract
Fluid phase equilibrium depends on the external constraints imposed on a system. In a closed system with fixed volume, depending on the average density, a vapor bubble may be stable, metastable, or unstable, with respect to the homogeneous liquid phase. In the case where the bubble is metastable, we study its lifetime, i.e. the average waiting time needed to observe bubble collapse, and the corresponding lifetime of the homogeneous liquid. For the smallest systems, we predict the possibility to observe phase flipping, when the fluid oscillates between states with and without bubble. We provide an example of phase flipping in a simulation of a Lennard-Jones fluid.



I
Introduction

Many phenomena involve the crossing of an energy barrier by a particle or a many-body system. Examples include chemical reactions, radioactive alpha decay, protein folding
Rollins and Dill 2014
;
Lyons et al. 2024
, microspheres in optical traps
McCann et al. 1999
;
Chupeau et al. 2020
;
Lyons et al. 2024
, nucleation in metastable phases
Debenedetti 1996
;
Caupin and Grisenti 2026
, etc… A particular case of the last is cavitation, i.e. the appearance of a bubble in a liquid stretched to a density below its saturated vapor pressure value. In classical nucleation theory (CNT),
Gibbs 1878
;
Volmer and Weber 1926
;
Fisher 1948
;
Debenedetti 1996

the minimum work needed to form spherical bubbles results from the competition between the volume energy gained by replacing the metastable liquid by vapor, and the energy cost associated with the interface between two phases. This results in an energy barrier
E_{\mathrm{b}}
to be overcome, which happens at a rate
J\propto\exp[-E_{\mathrm{b}}/(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)]
. For water near ambient temperature in the absence of impurities, spontaneous cavitation requires large negative pressures to occur, beyond
100\text{\,}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}

Zheng et al. 1991
;
Azouzi et al. 2012
. To go beyond CNT, advanced simulation techniques are employed; for instance, umbrella sampling applied to stretched water gave nucleation rates consistent with experiments
Menzl et al. 2016
.

Here we address the effects of confinement on the kinetics of phase change between liquid and vapor. Porous materials are ubiquitous in nature, and may lead to capillary condensation. This results in an
open
confinement, when the condensed liquid can exchange molecules with the outer vapor. We are interested in
closed
confinement, when the fluid is trapped inside a host material preventing external exchange. Such fluid inclusions are found in minerals
Hurai et al. 2015
, and widely studied to obtain information about the conditions at which the mineral formed, with useful applications to paleotemperature reconstruction
Roedder 1984
;
Krüger et al. 2011
;
Guillerm et al. 2025
. A key parameter in these studies is the temperature
T_{\mathrm{h}}
at which a vapor bubble, initially present in the inclusion, disappears upon heating. In the absence of surface tension, the bubble radius would reach zero, and from
T_{\mathrm{h}}
one would directly obtain the average fluid density in the inclusion. However, the existence of surface tension and the finite compressibility of the liquid result in rich thermodynamic phenomena, such as the possibility for a liquid at negative pressure to remain absolutely stable, thus making the connection between the observed
T_{\mathrm{h}}
and the actual density uncertain. Previous treatments in the literature were limited to the thermodynamics of the problem, which can only provide bounds on the possible values for
T_{\mathrm{h}}
at a given fluid density
Marti et al. 2012
;
Wilhelmsen et al. 2014
;
Wilhelmsen and Reguera 2015
;
Vincent and Marmottant 2017
;
Caupin 2022
;
Guillerm et al. 2025
. Here we deal with the kinetics, in order to predict at which conditions the bubble is expected to disappear. We also identify an interesting regime, for which the system exhibits reversible phase fluctuations between states with and without bubble. Although occurring only for cavities too small to be observed under the microscope, this effect can be studied in simulations, for which we provide an example.

This article is organized as follows. In Section
II
, we present the theoretical model (Section
II.1
) and the simulation details (Section
II.2
). The results are shown in Section
III
, and discussed in Section
IV
.

Methods

II.1
Model

We recall here the ingredients of the simple model previously introduced to describe liquid-vapor transition in confined systems
Caupin 2022
, and the approach developed by Menzl
et al.
to obtain nucleation rates
Menzl et al. 2016
.

II.1.1
Free energy

In Ref.
Caupin 2022
, we considered a cavity of volume
V
filled with a fluid at temperature
T
. The cavity is not restricted to be spherical, but we introduce its equivalent radius
R=[3V/(4\pi)]^{1/3}
. The fluid in the cavity can be homogeneous with density
\rho_{0}
, or it can be separated in two phases: a vapor bubble of volume
v
at density
\rho_{\mathrm{V}}
, and a liquid of volume
V-v
at density
\rho_{\mathrm{L}}
. We assume the liquid wets the cavity walls, so that the bubble is spherical.

To calculate
\Delta F(v)
, the free energy change of the bubble state with respect to the fully liquid state, we use a simplified equation of state based on a linear expansion of the liquid chemical potential:

\mu_{L}=\mu_{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}+\frac{1}{{\rho_{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}}^{2}\kappa}(\rho_{\mathrm{L}}-\rho_{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty})\,,

(1)

where
\kappa
is the liquid isothermal compressibility at saturated vapor pressure, and quantities corresponding to liquid-vapor equilibrium of an infinite system with a flat interface are indicated with the superscript
\infty
.

Treating the vapor as a perfect gas and assuming
\rho_{\mathrm{V}}\ll\rho_{\mathrm{L}}
(i.e.
T
much lower than the critical temperature
T_{\mathrm{c}}
), we arrived at a simple expression for the non-dimensional free energy change
\phi=2\kappa\Delta F/V
:

\phi=\left(\frac{{\delta_{0}}^{2}}{1-x}-1\right)x+9\epsilon x^{2/3}\,,

(2)

where
\delta_{0}=\rho_{0}/\rho_{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}
,
x=v/V
, and
\epsilon=\lambda/R
. Here we have introduced the Berthelot-Laplace length:

\lambda=\frac{2}{3}\gamma\kappa\,,

(3)

where
\gamma
is the liquid-vapor surface tension.
\lambda
is a microscopic length: for water it is typically a few tens of
\mathrm{pm}
, see Fig.
1
.

Figure 1:
Berthelot-Laplace length as a function of temperature for pure water.

The value of
\delta_{0}
determines 3 different regimes, as shown in Fig.
2
: (i) for
1>\delta_{0}>\delta_{\mathrm{0,sp}}
,
\phi(x)
is monotonously increasing, and the system can exist only in the fully liquid state; (ii) for
\delta_{\mathrm{0,sp}}>\delta_{0}>\delta_{\mathrm{0,eq}}
,
\phi(x)
exhibits two minima, corresponding to a stable, fully liquid state, and a metastable state with a bubble; (iii) for
\delta_{\mathrm{0,eq}}>\delta_{0}
,
\phi(x)
exhibits two minima, corresponding to a metastable, fully liquid state, and a stable state with a bubble. The case
\delta_{0}=\delta_{\mathrm{0,sp}}
is called the bubble
spinodal
, as it corresponds to an inflection point in
\phi(x)
which renders the bubble state unstable. The case
\delta_{0}=\delta_{\mathrm{0,eq}}
is called the bubble
binodal
, as it corresponds to an equal free energy for the fully liquid and bubble states.

\delta_{\mathrm{0,sp}}
and
\delta_{\mathrm{0,eq}}
and the corresponding reduced bubble volumes
x_{\mathrm{sp}}
and
x_{\mathrm{eq}}
are available, see Ref.
Caupin 2022
for details. In the limit
\epsilon\ll 1
, the solutions can be expanded to give simple expressions:

\displaystyle=

(4)

\displaystyle x_{\mathrm{sp}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon^{3/4}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3/2})\,,

(5)

and

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathrm{0,eq}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-2(3\epsilon)^{3/4}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3/2})\,,

(6)

\displaystyle x_{\mathrm{eq}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(3\epsilon)^{3/4}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3/2})\,.

(7)

In cases (ii) and (iii),
\phi(x)
reaches a maximum at
x_{\mathrm{c}}
whose value
\phi_{\mathrm{c}}
is involved in the reduced energy barrier, that needs to be overcome by thermal fluctuations to allow the system to change state. The reduced volume
x_{\mathrm{c}}
of the critical bubble is obtained by numerically solving
\mathrm{d}\phi/\mathrm{d}x=0
, and computing
\phi_{\mathrm{c}}=\phi(x_{\mathrm{c}})
.

For
\delta_{0}=\delta_{\mathrm{0,eq}}
, and in the limit
\epsilon\ll 1
,
x_{\mathrm{c}}
scales as
x_{\mathrm{eq}}
, i.e. like
\epsilon^{3/4}
. Keeping only the lowest order in
\mathrm{d}\phi/\mathrm{d}x
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\mathrm{d}x}\approx\frac{x^{2}-4\,3^{3/4}x\epsilon^{3/4}+9x^{2/3}\epsilon)}{1-x}\,

(8)

allows obtaining the approximate analytic results:

x_{\mathrm{c}}\simeq A_{x}\epsilon^{3/4}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\phi_{\mathrm{c}}\simeq A_{\phi}\epsilon^{3/2}\,.

(9)

Here

\displaystyle A_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-4+5\alpha-\alpha^{2}}{3^{1/4}\alpha}\,\epsilon^{3/4}\,,

(10)

\displaystyle A_{\phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{\alpha^{2}}\left\{(2+\alpha)\beta-3\sqrt{3}\alpha^{2}\right.

(11)

\displaystyle\left.+3^{5/6}\alpha^{4/3}\left[-4+5\alpha-\alpha^{2}\right]^{2/3}\right\}\,\epsilon^{3/2}\,.

with
\alpha=(19+3\sqrt{33})^{1/3}
and
\beta=3\sqrt{3}+\sqrt{11}
, so that
x_{\mathrm{c}}\approx 0.37\,\epsilon^{3/4}\approx x_{\mathrm{eq}}/6.2
and
\phi_{\mathrm{c}}\approx 1.4\,\epsilon^{3/2}
.

II.1.2
Mean first passage time

Our goal is to estimate the average time it takes for the system to jump over the energy barrier, either from the bubble state to the fully liquid state, or vice-versa.

To this end, we follow a method introduced by Menzl
et al.

Menzl et al. 2016
to study cavitation. They considered the nucleation of a vapor bubble in an unconfined liquid at temperature
T
, stretched to negative pressure. The fully liquid state is then in a metastable potential well, and an energy barrier must be overcome to create a bubble that expands without limit. Kramers’ theory
Kramers 1940
;
Schulten et al. 1981
assumes a diffusive motion of the system over the barrier. Using a suitable reaction coordinate
q
(such as the bubble radius or volume) to describe the free energy
U(q)
, the escape rate
k
over the barrier is given by :

k=\frac{\int_{\cup}\exp\left[-\beta U(q)\right]\,\mathrm{d}q}{\int_{c}ap\frac{1}{D(q)}\exp\left[\beta U(q)\right]\,\mathrm{d}q}\,,

(12)

where
\beta=1/(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)
,
D(q)
is the diffusion coefficient, and the symbols
\cup
and
\cap
indicate integration over the well and the barrier, respectively. Based on a A statistical committor analysis, Menzl
et al.
found that the volume of the largest bubble
v
was a good choice for the reaction coordinate
q
. Menzl
et al.
Menzl et al. 2016
proceeded with calculating the number of cavitation events per unit volume and time, i.e. the nucleation rate:

J=\frac{\sqrt{-c}D(v_{\mathrm{c}})}{\sqrt{2\pi k_{\mathrm{B}}T}}\frac{P(v_{\mathrm{c}})}{V}\,,

(13)

where
v_{\mathrm{c}}
is the critical volume,
c
the curvature of
U(v)
at
v_{\mathrm{c}}
, and
V
the volume in which nucleation occurs.
P(v_{\mathrm{c}})
is the probability density to find a bubble with volume
v_{\mathrm{c}}
. Menzl
et al.
obtained
P(v_{\mathrm{c}})/V
by umbrella sampling simulations, but they also showed that a reasonable estimate can be obtained from the free energy given by classical nucleation theory (CNT):

U(v)=pv+\gamma(36\pi v^{2})^{1/3}\,.

(14)

They wrote:

\frac{P(v_{\mathrm{c}})}{V}=\tilde{\rho}_{0}\exp\left(-\beta U(v_{\mathrm{c}})\right)\,.

(15)

The choice of the prefactor
\tilde{\rho}_{0}
is a delicate matter, as there is no prescription from CNT. Among various possible choices, Menzl
et al.
used
\tilde{\rho}_{0}=\rho_{\mathrm{L}}\rho_{\mathrm{V}}
, which gives a value only 4.5 times lower than the correct value obtained from umbrella sampling simulations.

In the present work, the situation is simpler, because we are now dealing with an equilibrium system in the canonical ensemble, and
\tilde{\rho}_{0}=1/Z
where
Z
is the partition function. Introducing

I(a,b)=\int_{a}^{b}\exp\left[-\beta\Delta F(v)\right]\,\mathrm{d}v\,,

(16)

we have
Z=I(0,V)
. We define the partial partition functions as
Z_{\mathrm{liq}}=I(0,v_{\mathrm{c}})
and
Z_{\mathrm{bub}}=I(v_{\mathrm{c}},V)
on the liquid and on the bubble side, respectively. We give here their approximate expressions. With
F_{0}=V/(2\kappa)
,
\Delta F(v)=F_{0}\,\phi(x=v/V)
, and

I(a,b)=\int_{a}^{b}\exp\left[-\beta F_{0}\phi(x)\right]\,\mathrm{d}v=V\int_{a/V}^{b/V}\exp\left[-\beta F_{0}\phi(x)\right]\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

(17)

We introduce
x_{\mathrm{bub}}=v_{\mathrm{bub}}/V
, the location of the minimum of
\phi(x)
, and the non-dimensional curvature
\hat{c}=\partial^{2}\phi/\partial x^{2}
. With
\hat{c}_{\mathrm{bub}}=\hat{c}(x_{\mathrm{bub}})
, the partial partition functions are:

\displaystyle Z_{\mathrm{liq}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I(0,v_{\mathrm{c}})\simeq V\int_{0}^{x_{\mathrm{c}}}\exp\left[-9\beta F_{0}\epsilon x^{2/3}\right]\,\mathrm{d}x\simeq\frac{3}{2}\frac{V}{(9\beta F_{0}\epsilon)^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\sqrt{y}\,e^{-y}\,\mathrm{d}y=\frac{1}{8}\left(\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\gamma}\right)^{3/2}\,,

(18)

\displaystyle Z_{\mathrm{bub}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I(v_{\mathrm{c}},V)\simeq V\int_{x_{\mathrm{bub}}}^{1}\exp\left\{-\beta F_{0}\left[\phi(x_{\mathrm{bub}})+\frac{\hat{c}_{\mathrm{bub}}}{2}(x-x_{\mathrm{bub}})^{2}\right]\right\}\,\mathrm{d}x

(19)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle V\exp\left[-\beta F_{0}\phi(x_{\mathrm{bub}})\right]\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\exp\left[-\beta F_{0}\frac{\hat{c}_{\mathrm{bub}}}{2}y^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}y=\sqrt{4\pi\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}TV\kappa}{\hat{c}_{\mathrm{bub}}}}\exp\left[-\beta\Delta F(v_{\mathrm{bub}})\right]\,.

(20)

The total partition function follows from
Z=Z_{\mathrm{liq}}+Z_{\mathrm{bub}}
.

Let
p_{\mathrm{liq}}
and
p_{\mathrm{bub}}
be the probabilities to find the system on the liquid side and on the bubble side of the energy barrier, respectively. We have:

p_{\mathrm{liq}}=\frac{Z_{\mathrm{liq}}}{Z},\quad p_{\mathrm{bub}}=\frac{Z_{\mathrm{bub}}}{Z},\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\frac{p_{\mathrm{bub}}}{p_{\mathrm{liq}}}=\frac{Z_{\mathrm{bub}}}{Z_{\mathrm{liq}}}\,.

(21)

In our model, the rate
J
at which the system jumps over the barrier thus writes:

J=\frac{\sqrt{-\hat{c}_{\mathrm{c}}}D(v_{\mathrm{c}})}{\sqrt{4\pi k_{\mathrm{B}}TV\kappa}}\frac{1}{ZV}\exp\left[-\beta\Delta F(v_{\mathrm{c}})\right]\,,

(22)

where
\hat{c}_{\mathrm{c}}=\hat{c}(x_{\mathrm{c}})
.

The only ingredient still needed to determine
J
from Eq.
13
is
D(v_{\mathrm{c}})
. Again, this can be obtained from simulations, or estimated based on a theory of the bubble dynamics in the presence of thermal fluctuations. In hydrodynamics, the Rayleigh-Plesset equation (RPE) describes the evolution of a spherical bubble
Brennen 1995
. Using the bubble volume as the variable, the RPE writes:

\rho_{\mathrm{L}}\ddot{v}-\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{L}}\dot{v}^{2}}{6v}=4\pi\left(\frac{3v}{4\pi}\right)^{1/3}\left[p_{\mathrm{in}}-p-2\gamma\left(\frac{4\pi}{3v}\right)^{1/3}-\frac{4}{3}\eta\dot{v}\right]\,,

(23)

where
p_{\mathrm{in}}
is the pressure inside the bubble, and
\eta
is the shear viscosity. In the overdamped limit, and neglecting
p_{\mathrm{in}}
, the RPE gives:

\dot{v}=-\frac{3v}{4\eta}\left[p+2\gamma\left(\frac{3v}{4\pi}\right)^{1/3}\right]=-\frac{1}{\Gamma(v)}\frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}v}\,.

(24)

This corresponds to the asymptotic velocity of an object moving in the potential
U(v)
with a friction coefficient
\Gamma(v)=4\eta/(3v)
. A Langevin like random force is introduced to account for thermal fluctuations. Like in the classical case of Brownian motion, the diffusion coefficient follows from the fluctuation-dissipation theorem:

D(v)=\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\Gamma(v)}=\frac{3k_{\mathrm{B}}Tv}{4\eta}\,.

(25)

In our case, the bubble evolves in a confined space. Vincent and Marmottant
Vincent and Marmottant 2017
have shown that the RPE is modified by an additional inertial term, a factor in the effective mass of the bubble, and by the use of the appropriate
U(v)
(in their case they considered the free energy for a compressible liquid and the elasticity of the surrounding medium). We note that confinement does not modify the dissipation, as the only viscous contribution to the RPE comes from the dynamic boundary condition at the bubble surface
Brennen 1995
. Therefore, in the overdamped limit, Eq.
24
remains unchanged, and we will use Eq.
25
to compute the value
D(v_{\mathrm{c}})
required to obtain the rate with Eq.
13
.

At equilibrium, the system obeys detailed balance:

JV=k_{\mathrm{liq}}p_{\mathrm{liq}}=k_{\mathrm{bub}}p_{\mathrm{bub}}\,,

(26)

where
k_{\mathrm{liq}}
and
k_{\mathrm{bub}}
are the rates at which barrier crossing occurs from the liquid side and from the bubble side, respectively. We now deduce the corresponding mean first passage times (MFPTs),
\tau_{\mathrm{liq}}
and
\tau_{\mathrm{bub}}
. For the transition from liquid to bubble:

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{liq}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k_{\mathrm{liq}}}=\frac{\sqrt{4\pi k_{\mathrm{B}}TV\kappa}}{\sqrt{-\hat{c}_{\mathrm{c}}}D(v_{\mathrm{c}})}Z_{\mathrm{liq}}\exp\left[\beta\Delta F(v_{\mathrm{c}})\right]

(27)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{3}\frac{\eta\kappa}{x_{\mathrm{c}}}\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\sqrt{(-\hat{c}_{\mathrm{c}})V\kappa\gamma^{3}}}\exp\left[\beta\Delta F(v_{\mathrm{c}})\right]\,,

(28)

while for the transition from bubble to liquid:

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{bub}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k_{\mathrm{bub}}}=\frac{\sqrt{4\pi k_{\mathrm{B}}TV\kappa}}{\sqrt{-\hat{c}_{\mathrm{c}}}D(v_{\mathrm{c}})}Z_{\mathrm{bub}}\exp\left[\beta\Delta F(v_{\mathrm{c}})\right]

(29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{16\pi}{3}\frac{\eta\kappa}{x_{\mathrm{c}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{(-\hat{c}_{\mathrm{c}})\hat{c}_{\mathrm{bub}}}}\exp\left\{\beta\left[\Delta F(v_{\mathrm{c}})-\Delta F(v_{\mathrm{bub}})\right]\right\}\,.

(30)

Note that, at the binodal,
x_{\mathrm{c}}
can be approximated using Eq.
9
, and, similarly,
\hat{c}_{\mathrm{c}}\simeq-2/{A_{x}}^{4/3}\simeq 7.6
and
\hat{c}_{\mathrm{bub}}\simeq 4/3
.

The ratio of the MFPTs satisfies:

\frac{\tau_{\mathrm{bub}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{liq}}}=\frac{p_{\mathrm{bub}}}{p_{\mathrm{liq}}}=\frac{Z_{\mathrm{bub}}}{Z_{\mathrm{liq}}}\,.

(31)

Results

III.1
Bubble collapse

In this section, we investigate the conditions at which bubble collapse will occur due to thermal fluctuations in a small cavity. We consider the case of pure water, and illustrate the results for two temperatures, 20 and
300\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
.

4

v_{\mathrm{e}}
and
v_{\mathrm{c}}

. By definition, the two branches
v_{\mathrm{e}}
and
v_{\mathrm{c}}
meet at the bubble spinodal,
\delta_{\mathrm{0,sp}}
.

III.2
Fluctuations at the bubble binodal

IV
Discussion

Regarding applications to geosciences, the present study sheds light on the conditions at which bubble collapse is expected. In Ref.
Marti et al. 2012
, Marti
et al.
developed a model related to the present one to determine the temperature at which bubble collapse occur when heating a small cavity. They made the following comment: “We know that
T_{\mathrm{h,obs}}
[the observed temperature of bubble collapse] must lie between
T_{\mathrm{bin}}
of the “bubble binodal” and
T_{\mathrm{sp}}
of the “bubble spinodal”, but it cannot be predicted because of the inherent uncertainty associated
with a metastable state.” For this reason, in the analysis of geophysical data on bubble collapse in minerals, in order to minimize the uncertainty, it is customary to assume that bubble collapse occurs at a temperature
(T_{\mathrm{bin}}+T_{\mathrm{sp}})/2
(see for instance Ref.
Guillerm et al. 2025
). From Fig.
9
, we know that for cavity volumes relevant to geosciences (i.e.
V>$1\text{\,}\mu\mathrm{m}^{3}$
), spontaneous bubble collapse requires
\delta_{0}\approx\delta_{\mathrm{0,sp}}
. Assuming as in experiment that the system is heated at constant cavity volume, this translates into a temperature of bubble disappearance very close to
T_{\mathrm{s}}
(typically within less than
0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
). It would thus seem better to use
T_{\mathrm{sp}}
rather than
(T_{\mathrm{bin}}+T_{\mathrm{sp}})/2
. Unfortunately, all our reasoning is based on the implicit assumption that nucleation occurs homogeneously. In real systems, impurities or wall defects may reduce the energy barrier for bubble collapse, which may then occur away from
T_{\mathrm{sp}}
(whose value could also be modified). Without more information on the type of nucleation process, it is therefore better to use the conservative value
(T_{\mathrm{bin}}+T_{\mathrm{sp}})/2
.

The fact that the system will change state only very close to its limit of stability is reminiscent of the classical picture of capillary condensation
Cole and Saam 1974
;
Saam and Cole 1975
. When a cylindrical pore is exposed to a subsaturated vapor, a liquid film forms if the liquid wets the pore walls. The film thickness grows with increasing vapor pressure, and the film becomes metastable with respect to the fully filled pore. However, complete filling does not occur immediately, because it requires a macroscopic deformation of the film which is energetically costly. Filling occurs only close to an instability of the film which is reached for a critical thickness. In contrast, emptying may occur at equilibrium between the film and filled states. This is the basis for the explanation of adsorption hysteresis in small pores.

Regarding simulations, our theoretical approach provides a detailed picture for the conditions necessary to observe phase flipping. They are qualitatively confirmed by direct simulations of a Lennard-Jones system. This illustrates how finite size effects modify the observation of phase coexistence. A similar situation is observed in other systems. For instance, ST2 is a model of water that predicts a first-order liquid-liquid transition at low temperature, terminating at a liquid-liquid critical point (LLCP). NPT simulations of small systems near the LLCP found that “the entire system flips rapidly between liquid states of high and low density”
Kesselring et al. 2012
. Note that, in that case, flipping occurred between states with only one phase, due to the NPT conditions. In our NVT case, flipping occurs between a state with one phase (fully liquid), and a state with two phases (liquid and bubble).

Although the simulation results are in good qualitative agreement with the model predictions, there are large quantitative differences. The density at the bubble binodal, and the corresponding energy barrier, are significantly less than the model predictions. This results in vastly faster phase oscillations in the simulations compared to the model expectations. A similar observation was made by Menzl
et al.
in their study of cavitation in water
Menzl et al. 2016
: plain CNT underestimates the cavitation rates by more than 15 orders of magnitude. One of the reasons for this discrepancy is that CNT ignores the effect of curvature on surface tension. This becomes a poor approximation for small bubbles, when the thickness of the liquid-vapor interface cannot be neglected compared to the radius
Menzl et al. 2016
;
Bruot and Caupin 2016
. Another limitation is that, for the small energy barrier found in the present simulation (
\simeq 0.84k_{\mathrm{B}}T
), Kramers’ theory is expected to become inaccurate. It would be interesting to see how the comparison with the model evolves when the cavity volume
V
is increased, which will make the barrier higher. Future work could investigate this further, as well as the role played by the assumption of a spherical bubble and by the choice of reaction coordinate.
