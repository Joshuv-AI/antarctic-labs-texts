A cross-sectional study of lifestyle factors related to body mass index, skeletal muscle mass, and skeletal muscle index in male Japanese workers
Authors: Naoko Nishitani, Hisataka Sakakibara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe study was a cross-sectional study of 377 male Japanese workers to investigate the relationship between lifestyle habits and body composition components, such as body mass index (BMI), grip strength, skeletal muscle mass, and skeletal muscle index (SMI), to promote the prevention of sarcopenic obesity and sarcopenia. Their sleep quality was evaluated using the Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS), and their exercise habits were assessed using the Japanese version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire. Body composition components, including body fat mass and skeletal muscle mass, were measured using a body composition impedance analyzer, InBody470. Handgrip strength and SMI were evaluated based on the standard values set in the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia 2019 (AWGS 2019) guidelines. Of the 377 participants, 119 (31.6%) had a BMI of ≥25.0 kg/m2. According to the AWGS 2019 guidelines, these participants did not meet the criteria for sarcopenic obesity. Participants with a higher BMI had higher age and handgrip strength (trend, P < 0.001). Additionally, they had higher skeletal muscle mass and SMI (trend, P < 0.001). Multiple regression analysis was performed using skeletal muscle mass and SMI as dependent variables, which revealed that skeletal muscle mass and SMI were associated with age, BMI, breakfast intake, and waist circumference. Furthermore, skeletal muscle mass was associated with heavy physical activity. From the perspective of disease prevention, our findings highlight the importance of prioritizing breakfast intake and physical activity to maintain muscle mass.

INTRODUCTION

In this study, we conducted a questionnaire-based survey on the daily lifestyle habits of male workers in a company, aiming to determine the association between lifestyle habits and indicators of sarcopenia.

Japan is currently experiencing a decline in its total population, along with an increase in the aging population. Moreover, an increase in the incidence of lifestyle-related diseases among adults and older adults have been noted, leading to rising medical costs and an insufficient balance in the working population. Obesity and reduced muscle mass/strength are worsened by reduced physical activity, further increasing the risk of lifestyle-related diseases and the need for long-term care.1 For these reasons, prevention of lifestyle-related diseases and long-term care are important. To achieve this, preventing obesity and maintaining/increasing muscle mass and strength by improving lifestyle habits early are thought to lead to disease prevention and an extension of healthy life expectancy.

Skeletal muscle mass1 begins to decrease in the 30s, and 30−40% of the muscle mass of a person in their 20s is approximately lost by the age of 80. Sarcopenia refers to a significant loss of skeletal muscle mass and is considered an important factor leading to the requirement for nursing care because of a decline in activity. Sarcopenia diagnostic criteria for Asians uses handgrip strength measurement (muscle strength) or a chair-stand test (physical function) based on the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia 2019 (AWGS 2019) guidelines; individuals with a score below the standard are suspected to have sarcopenia.2

Conversely, obesity refers to the excessive accumulation of fat in the body. Additionally, it may cause inflammatory reactions in the body, often leading to various lifestyle-related diseases.3 Currently, no clear diagnostic criteria have been established for “sarcopenic obesity.”4 Thus, it is thought to be a condition in obese individuals with low muscle mass and strength; sarcopenia and obesity may synergistically exacerbate metabolic and functional disorders and pose a higher risk of lifestyle-related diseases. The need for long-term care has been compared with that of simple obesity.4-6 In addition, recent studies have shown that normal weight obesity (NWO) has been associated with heart disease. NWO is defined as an individual has a normal body mass index (BMI) but a high body fat mass.7 Moreover, in recent years, there has been an increase in the amount of time spent on desk work, such as computer work, resulting in a decline in physical activity among workers.

However, the proportions of workers with sarcopenia, sarcopenic obesity, or at risk of developing them are unclear. Additionally, from a preventive perspective, the daily lifestyle habits associated with sarcopenia remain unclear. In this study, we measured body composition components, including muscle mass and strength, and conducted on the daily lifestyle habits of male workers, aiming to determine the association between lifestyle habits and indicators of sarcopenia.

METHODS

Study participants

Among individuals who underwent a routine health checkup at manufacturing company A between June 1 and 8, 2023, those who consented to the measurement of body composition components, including handgrip strength and muscle mass, as well as filling out a questionnaire, were enrolled as study participants, and assessments were performed as described below.

The questionnaire was self-administered and submitted anonymously, and no personal information was collected during the measurements. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nagoya University Graduate School of Medicine (no. 27466).

Examination items

In the self-administered questionnaire, participants were asked about their basic attributes and lifestyle habits, including diet, exercise, and sleep patterns. Questions regarding dietary habits included daily breakfast intake, dinner after 9 pm, and late-night snacks after dinner. Exercise habits were evaluated using the Japanese version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire.8,9

Sleep quality. Sleep quality was evaluated using the Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS), a universal tool for assessing insomnia.10,11 It was developed by the World Health Organization (WHO) based on the standards set in the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision. The Japanese version of the AIS has been validated, with the reported Cronbach’s α coefficient ranging from 0.78 to 0.88.12

Measurement of body composition components relevant to the AWGS guideline. Handgrip strength was measured using a digital handgrip strength meter (Smedley type), a standard digital device that enables simple measurement of handgrip strength. The handgrip strengths of the left and right hands were measured twice, and the maximum values were adopted for analysis. According to the AWGS 2019 guideline, a handgrip strength of <28 kg has been set as the standard value for suspecting sarcopenia in males.

Body composition components were measured using a body composition impedance analyzer (InBody470; InBody Japan, Inc),13 and the values of muscle mass and other components were calculated. The measurements were taken barefoot and were not performed for those wearing a pacemaker. The InBody score is evaluated based on the standard value of 80 points, with the following classifications: frail/obese type (≤70 points), general type (70−80 points), healthy type (80−90 points), and muscular type (≥90 points).14 Skeletal muscle mass refers to the mass of the muscles that enable voluntary movement of the body. The skeletal muscle index (SMI) was calculated by dividing limb skeletal muscle mass by the square of height. The AWGS 2019 guideline defines 7.0 kg/m2 as the standard value of SMI.

Determination criteria and statistical analysis

Currently, there are no established criteria for determining sarcopenic obesity, and criteria for sarcopenia are often used to determine sarcopenic obesity in obese individuals.4 In Japan, the criteria for determining sarcopenia have been designed for older adults, and there are currently no established criteria for workers in their 20s to 50s. To determine sarcopenia, it is considered appropriate to use the cutoff value for handgrip strength published in the AWGS guidelines.15 For this reason, the measurement value of handgrip strength in obese individuals, as published in the AWGS 2019 guideline, was used as the standard for determining sarcopenic obesity in this study (Figure). Additionally, analysis was performed with reference to muscle mass and other components measured using a body composition impedance analyzer.

Assessment criteria for sarcopenia and sarcopenic obesity

BMI: body mass index

Regarding the determination of obesity, participants were considered underweight if the BMI was <18.5 kg/m2, to have a normal weight if BMI was between ≥18.5 and <25.0 kg/m2, and obese if BMI was ≥25.0 kg/m2.

Statistical analyses were conducted using the statistical package SPSS 29.0J (IBM).

Of the 377 participants, 149 (39.5%) reported engaging in vigorous physical activities such as carrying heavy loads, cycling uphill, and jogging on some days during an average week, whereas 165 (43.8%) reported engaging in moderate physical activity such as carrying light loads and swimming at a slow pace. Additionally, 328 participants (87.0%) walked continuously for at least 10 minutes on some days during an average week. Sleep time was <5 hours in 43 participants (11.4%), 5 to <6 hours in 174 participants (46.2%), 6 to <7 hours in 133 participants (35.3%), 7 to <8 hours in 25 participants (6.6%), and 8 to <9 hours in two participants (0.5%), revealing that many participants had a sleep time of 5−7 hours. With respect to sleep quality, the participants had a mean total AIS score of 4.2 ± 3.2 points, with a total score of 0−3 points in 180 participants (47.7%), 4−5 points in 87 participants (23.1%), and ≥6 points in 110 participants (29.2%).

The current health condition was described as “healthy” in 147 participants (37.7%), “somewhat healthy” in 199 participants (52.8%), “slightly unwell” in 35 participants (9.3%), and “very unwell” in one participant (0.2%), showing that 9.5% of the participants reported that their health condition was “unwell.”

The mean handgrip strength of the participants was 45.1 ± 6.4 kg. No participant in this study had a handgrip strength of <28 kg. The study participants had a mean skeletal muscle mass of 31.0 ± 4.3 kg. The mean SMI of the participants was 8.1 ± 0.7 kg/m2. In this study, 15 participants (4.0%) had an SMI of <7.0 kg/m2. Moreover, participants in this study had a mean InBody score of 73.8 ± 6.4 points.

Examinations by BMI group

The study participants were stratified into three groups based on their BMI (<18.5, ≥18.5 to <25.0, and ≥25.0 kg/m2), and the mean of each item was examined among the groups (Table 2). The results showed that participants with a higher BMI had higher age, waist, and handgrip strength (trend, P < 0.001). They also had a higher body fat percentage, with a mean of over 27% among those with a BMI of ≥25.0 kg/m2 (trend, P < 0.001), as well as higher skeletal muscle mass and SMI (trend, P < 0.001). According to the standard set in the AWGS 2019 guidelines, it is considered desirable to have an SMI of ≥7.0 kg/m2. In this study, participants with a BMI of <18.5 kg/m2 had a mean SMI of exactly 7.0 kg/m2. Participants with a higher BMI tended to have a higher waist circumference, with a mean of >90 cm among those with a BMI of ≥25.0 kg/m2. The InBody score, in which a higher score indicates a more desirable state, was highest among those with an appropriate BMI (between ≥18.5 and <25.0 kg/m2).

Lifestyle habits associated with skeletal muscle mass and SMI

We investigated lifestyle factors related to participants’ skeletal muscle mass and SMI. First, we examined the Spearman correlation between age, BMI, exercise, dietary habits, among others, and skeletal muscle mass and SMI. Next, we performed multiple regression analysis using skeletal muscle mass and SMI as dependent variables and items that showed positive or negative correlations as independent variables (Table 3). The results showed that skeletal muscle mass was associated with age (P < 0.001), BMI (P < 0.001), heavy physical activity (P = 0.010), breakfast intake (P = 0.027), and waist circumference (P = 0.010). SMI was associated with age (P < 0.001), BMI (P < 0.001), breakfast intake (P = 0.002), and waist circumference (P < 0.001).

Sarcopenic obesity

Of the 377 participants, 119 (31.6%) had a BMI of ≥25.0 kg/m2. However, none of them had an SMI of <7 kg/m2 or a handgrip strength of <28 kg, which are the standards set in the AWGS 2019 guidelines. This indicates that none of the participants met the criteria for sarcopenic obesity.

The average values for BMI, skeletal muscle mass, and SMI were examined according to the age group: <30 years (n = 91), 30s (n = 93), 40s (n = 60), 50s (n = 93), and ≥60 years (n = 40). The results showed that BMI increased with age (P < 0.001), whereas skeletal muscle mass and SMI were highest in people in their 40s.

Additionally, although none of the participants with a BMI of <25.0 kg/m2 had a handgrip strength of <28 kg, 15 had a SMI below the standard value (7 kg/m2). Of these participants, three who were classified as normal weight based on BMI (≥18.5 to <25.0 kg/m²) had a body fat percentage of over 20%. Particularly, these three participants had high body fat and low muscle mass despite having a normal BMI, suggesting the possibility of NWO.

Other items

Among the 110 participants with a total AIS score of ≥6 points (those highly suspected of having insomnia), 22 (20.0%) described their health condition as “slightly unwell” or “very unwell,” and the number of participants whose health condition was described as “unwell” was higher in the group with a total score of ≥6 points than in other groups with <6 points (P < 0.001).

DISCUSSION

From the perspective of disease prevention, our findings highlight the importance of prioritizing breakfast intake and physical activity to maintain muscle mass. There have been few studies to date that have investigated the relationship between lifestyle habits and body composition factors such as muscle mass and strength in the working population. Therefore, this study is novel, and its results have provided valuable insights. The results in Table 2 show that in this study, participants with a higher BMI were found to have higher skeletal muscle mass and SMI, older ages, higher handgrip strength, higher body fat percentage, and a higher waist circumference. Those with high body fat and waist circumference may have high visceral fat, which increases the risk of lifestyle-related diseases. Thus, having a higher BMI is not necessarily preferable for those with high skeletal muscle mass or SMI. Additionally, the measured InBody score numerically represents the state of body fat, muscle mass, and other components in an easily interpretable manner, and a more desirable state is indicated by a higher InBody score. In this study, the highest InBody score was observed in participants with an appropriate BMI (between ≥18.5 and <25.0 kg/m2), suggesting that it is desirable to maintain both an appropriate BMI and muscle mass, even as we age.

Approximately 72% of the participants had breakfast every day, whereas approximately 10% did not. Diet has long been considered important for maintaining muscle mass.15,16 Moreover, the results of this study highlight the importance of breakfast intake among the working population. Consuming foods rich in proteins and vitamins has long been viewed as an important factor, especially for workers. Consuming enough of such food at breakfast is crucial. Dietary habits in adulthood can significantly impact one’s life in later years.16 Moreover, some form of exercise is required, as it is necessary for increasing muscle strength.17 This study focused on physical activities, and participants were asked about the intensity of their general daily physical activities, including work and housework. Even for those not involved in competitive sports, it is important to continue engaging in aerobic exercises, as well as exercises to build muscle strength, such as squats, and to move the body frequently during daily activities. Such exercises have been shown to positively impact health, including a reduced risk of diseases.17,18

The participants in this study were not asked about the details of their work. However, many workers have recently been spending more time working in a sitting position because of the increased amount of desk and computer work. Caution must be exercised when spending a large amount of time in a sitting position each day, as it has been shown not only to lead to obesity, a decrease in muscle mass, and a decline in muscle strength but also to increase the risk of death.19 Interrupting prolonged sitting time may reduce the risk of cardiovascular metabolic diseases.20 In young people experiencing a decline in physical activity, continued weight loss, along with irregular eating and sleeping habits, is likely to negatively impact their muscle mass and strength. Additionally, considering the decline in physical activity with age, attention should be paid to post-retirement lifestyle habits. Guidelines on physical activity and sedentary behaviors21 were issued by the WHO in 2020.

Muscles, which can be trained irrespective of age, are broken down and synthesized repeatedly; it is generally believed that muscle synthesis is promoted during sleep.22,23 In older adults, reduced duration and quality of sleep have been associated with sarcopenia and sarcopenic obesity.24,25 Additionally, individuals with shorter sleep time are more likely to be obese.26 Although this study did not find an association between sleep and skeletal muscle mass or SMI.

Muscle mass and strength decrease with age.27 Therefore, it is important to establish and maintain the breakfast intake and positive physical activity, as shown in this study, from an early age. This study yielded valuable results that can contribute to healthy activities aimed at maintaining beneficial lifestyle habits. For workers, maintaining a healthy weight, eating breakfast, and engaging in sufficient physical activity were associated with muscle mass. The results of this study provide important insights into the prevention of sarcopenia and sarcopenic obesity in adulthood and may lead to the prevention of various lifestyle-related diseases and an extension of healthy life expectancy.

Although this study provided new insights, it was a cross-sectional study, and causal relationships cannot be determined. Thus, several limitations are worth mentioning. First, longitudinal studies on the relationship between lifestyle, muscle mass, and SMI need to be conducted in the future. Furthermore, intervention studies should be performed with a wider range of participants, including workers in other industries.
