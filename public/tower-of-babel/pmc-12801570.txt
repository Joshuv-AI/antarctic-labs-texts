Strategies to promote COVID-19 vaccination in Northern Ghana: a qualitative study of stakeholders’ perspectives
Authors: Prosper Mandela Amaltinga Awuni, Nancy Lightfoot, Emily Donato, Marion Maar, Clement Bomweh Zobazie, James Fenibe Mbinta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite having sufficient vaccines to inoculate 88% of eligible individuals with at least one dose, COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy in Ghana has been 42.0%. This study explored context-specific strategies to promote COVID-19 vaccination in northern Ghana.MethodsA descriptive qualitative study design guided this project. The stakeholder group included directors of health services, public health, disease control, health promotion officers, and traditional medicine healers. A total of 17 stakeholders were interviewed. Interviews were scheduled and conducted using Zoom calls. Zoom invitations were sent out, and each interview lasted 30 to 45 min. Reflexive thematic analysis was employed to analyze the data using NVivo.ResultsFive themes emerged from the study: (1) the organization of vaccination programs, (2) organizational support for vaccination programs, (3) proposed strategies to enhance intake, (4) challenges encountered in vaccination programs, and (5) the exploration of herbal medicine as an alternative option.ConclusionOur study provides vital insights into the strategies employed, challenges, and strategies that could help tackle COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy in northern Ghana. These findings suggest that area-specific evaluation should be conducted to understand the peculiar logistics needed to support future vaccination programmes, and prioritization of people living with disability.Keywords: COVID-19, Vaccination, Ghana, Stakeholders, Challenges, Strategies

Introduction

As of April 27, 2024, the COVID-19 vaccination rate in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) was reported to be 51.8% [1]. Previous research highlighted factors contributing to the low uptake of COVID-19 vaccines in SSA, including supply constraints, logistical challenges, and structural issues [2]. For instance, the lack of proactive efforts by health authorities in Africa to expedite vaccine deployment has led to shortages, resulting in poor acceptance and coverage [2–4].

In Ghana, there is a significant disparity between the supply of COVID-19 vaccines and their utilization [5]. Despite having sufficient vaccines to inoculate 88% of eligible individuals with at least one dose [6], uptake has been low and unevenly distributed [6]. A pre-COVID-19 vaccination survey conducted nationwide revealed that 82.8% of Ghanaians were willing to be vaccinated against COVID-19, with hesitancy at 18.7% [7]. Another survey conducted prior to the pandemic showed that 65% of individuals would take the vaccine if it became available [8]. However, the actual vaccination rates have remained notably low at 44.3% [1]. A study conducted three months into the vaccination campaign showed a decrease in willingness from 82.8% to 71%, with hesitancy increasing to 29% [9]. These findings indicate that willingness does not necessarily translate into actual vaccine uptake.

Furthermore, COVID-19 vaccination rates continue to be low, primarily due to increasing vaccine hesitancy. In early 2022, Brackstone and colleagues reported that 52.2% of Ghanaians were hesitant to receive the COVID-19 vaccine [10]. Similarly, Lazarus et al. found no change in COVID-19 vaccine acceptance in Ghana in 2023, with vaccine hesitancy remaining at 42.2% [11]. High levels of hesitancy have been observed in certain communities across Ghana, with rates of 48% among adolescents in Kintampo [12], 28% in Navrongo [13], and 30.6% in Nkwanta South [14].

One gap in the existing literature is the lack of studies examining the public health strategies implemented to promote vaccine uptake in Ghana. While many studies on COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy in Ghana offer policy recommendations [15–19], only one study has explored the public health measures aimed at improving COVID-19 vaccine uptake in the country [20]. The low uptake of COVID-19 vaccines suggests that the availability of vaccines alone does not address hesitancy; policies and measures to promote uptake are equally important. Although the work of Lawal et al. [21] cites simple phenomena or reasons for the low coverage of COVID-19 vaccines in Africa, such actions can result in the arbitrary implementation of health policies.

Since Ghana’s first COVID-19 case [22], the government and partners imported and distributed vaccinations. The prevalence of hesitancy in many communities, especially in the north, showed that vaccines and traditional promotional techniques were not enough. Numerous studies have observed hesitation and made policy suggestions, but the on-the-ground realities of vaccine promotion efforts are unknown. This study critically examines ‘how’ and ‘why’ strategies worked or failed in northern Ghana’s socio-cultural and logistical setting after describing ‘what’ techniques. This study examines frontline stakeholders’ views to know the relationship between logistical execution, trust problems, and opposing health beliefs that caused low vaccine uptake. The authors believe that future health campaigns can use these insights to create more sophisticated, resilient, and context-specific tactics to overcome supply-side delivery and demand-side acceptability issues.

Methods

Study design and setting

A descriptive qualitative study design guided this project. Northern Ghana includes Northern, Upper East, Upper West, North-East, and Savannah regions (Fig. 1). The study population included urban and rural dwellers. In Ghana, the definition of rural or urban community is based on population density. A rural area in Ghana refers to a community with a population density of fewer than 5,000 people, whereas an urban area is a population of over 5,000 people [23].

Map of Ghana with the five northern regions in red

Participant recruitment and interviews

To explore the strategies that promote the uptake of COVID-19 vaccines, health stakeholders were recruited from northern Ghana. The stakeholder group included directors of health services, district public health officers, disease control officers, and traditional medicine healers. A total of 17 stakeholders were interviewed. The aim was to explore strategies that would enhance COVID-19 vaccine acceptance and uptake among the selected stakeholders, which consisted of directors of health services, district public health officers, and disease control officers. Additionally, two traditional medicine healers were included. Furthermore, the researchers aimed to identify the differences and similarities in rural and urban public health policies and strategies for addressing COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy in Northern Ghana. These stakeholders were chosen for the following reasons:

Disease Control Officers are responsible for implementing disease control and prevention activities at various levels (regional, municipal, district, and sub-district) in northern Ghana. The Expanded Program on Immunization is one of the ten programs managed by disease control officers in the region.

Health Promotion Officers play a crucial role in health communication, advocacy, and social mobilization to induce behavioral and attitudinal changes addressing the determinants of health in northern Ghana [24]. They are also involved in delivering vital health messages and public education to prevent disease outbreaks.

Public Health Officers’ functions include undertaking effective surveillance, forecasting, and controlling of diseases of public health importance, outbreaks, emerging and re-emerging threats, and pandemics [24]. Also, they manage epidemics, which include estimating resources needed, vaccines, drugs, and many other activities to control epidemics in northern Ghana.

The directors of health services hold the highest authority for policy and procedures at sub-national levels, including the Regional, Municipal, and District Directors of Health Services.

The District Directors of Health Services (DDHS) act on behalf of the Director General of Health Services, Ghana, ensuring the implementation of health policies and procedures [24].

Traditional medicine healers have played a significant role in disease prevention within Ghana’s primary healthcare system for a long time [25]. Traditional medicine healers cure both communicable and non-communicable diseases using herbs [26]. While Balde and colleagues have documented the activities of traditional medicine healers during the 2014 Ebola outbreak, their involvement in clinical medicine has not been thoroughly studied [27]. Traditional medicine healers are highly respected people in society and are very trusted in society. These healers are highly respected and trusted members of society, with research indicating that this trust is rooted in religious beliefs and deeply embedded in culture and tradition [28].

Considering the vital role they play as stakeholders in healthcare, it is essential to consider their perspective on the COVID-19 vaccination campaign.

Participant recruitment

The researcher conducted semi-structured interviews to collect data, as this methodology offers participants the opportunity to construct and articulate their individual experiences [29]. Open-ended interview questions were formulated to facilitate participants’ expression of their experiences and strategies regarding the promotion of COVID-19 vaccine acceptance. The interviews were conducted via both in-person and virtual platforms in three stages.

First

The researcher used the WhatsApp mobile application[30] to invite participants by sending a message and a flyer. This approach aimed to involve a diverse group of health stakeholders.

Second

Interested stakeholders received reminders at their offices. Those who preferred other communication methods were contacted through phone calls, SMS, or WhatsApp messages.

Third

Interviews were scheduled and conducted using Zoom calls. Zoom invitations were sent out, and each interview lasted 30 to 45 min. The researcher solely relied on Zoom calls for conducting interviews with stakeholders.

Data collection and data quality

Participant interviews were conducted between November and December 2023, after obtaining ethical clearance from the internal review board of Laurentian University and the Navrongo Health Research Centre Institutional Review Board (NHRCIRB) (NHRCIRB554) in Ghana. The data were transcribed verbatim. The initial data coding was performed by PMAA, aided by CBZ (Public Health Officer, Ghana Health Service) and JFM (Research Fellow, Victoria University of Wellington), New Zealand, who assisted in ensuring the quality of the coding. Additionally, member checking and journaling were carried out by the PMAA and JFM. Lastly, the researcher developed a coding list, independently coded the data, and compared the themes for convergence.

Data analysis

Reflexive thematic analysis was employed to analyze the data, as the research questions focused on the participants’ perceptions, experiences, understanding, and representation [31]. The six-phase approach outlined by Clarke and Braun was followed in conducting the reflexive thematic analysis. These phases encompassed becoming familiar with the data, coding, generating initial themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and presenting the findings. Before commencing the data analysis, two members of the research team reviewed the data to obtain a comprehensive understanding of the transcripts. Subsequently, the researcher analyzed the transcripts and organized the data into initial codes and subcodes, grouping them thematically. NVivo 14 software (QRS International, [32]) was utilized to facilitate the coding process and the identification of common themes. Once the initial codes were established, the researcher formulated themes and reviewed them to ensure there were no redundancies. PMAA collaborated with NL, MM, ED, JFM, and CBZ to analyze the themes derived from the collected data. The themes were then defined and named by PMAA, NL, ED, MM, and the team before documenting the research [31]. Additionally, an inductive thematic analysis approach was employed by the research team, allowing themes to emerge from the content of the data itself [33]. However, as is inherent in reflexive thematic analysis, the research team’s prior knowledge and the study’s objectives inevitably informed the interpretation of the data. This approach ensured that the analysis remained grounded in the participants’ accounts while acknowledging the role of the researcher’s reflexivity.

Results

Participants characteristics

The study included a sample of 17 participants, ranging in age from 25 to 58 years, with an average age of 40.1 years (SD = 10.0). Among the participants, seven identified as female and ten as male. Their occupations represented a diverse range, including five public health nurses, four disease control officers, three health promotion professionals, three district directors, and two traditional healers. Out of the participants, 15 had attained tertiary education, while two had completed basic education. Geographically, the participants hailed from various regions, namely five from the Upper East region, three from the Upper West region, three from the North-East region, three from the Savannah region, and three from the Northern region. Themes

Five themes emerged from this study. The themes include organization of vaccination programs, organizational support for vaccination programs, proposed strategies to enhance intake, challenges encountered in vaccination programs, and exploration of herbal medicine as an alternative option.

Organization of vaccination programmes

The study discovered that capacity building served as the fundamental basis for the involvement of regions in trainer training at the regional capitals of the five northern regions. At the district/municipal level, each locality took on the duty of training the vaccination teams for deployment in the program. One participant stated:

“We attended training on the vaccine. When we come down, we had to train the grassroots staff to also know about it, about the vaccine.” [SH06].

The Districts/Municipalities embarked on continuous health promotion activities, including health talks on radio stations, durbars, churches, markets, and all social gatherings, to sensitize the people on the control measures against COVID-19, including the need to get vaccinated. This is what a stakeholder has to say:

“There was no rest. We have to do continuous health education to individuals and groups wherever we find them. We tell them about the vaccines and what we all need to comply with to ensure we go back to normal times. We use the posters, jingles, sometimes the social media, the WhatsApp to disseminate the message.” [SH15].

Community mobilizations were described as intensive, as they created the ground for the vaccination teams to socially market the COVID-19 vaccine to the people. According to the experiences shared, it started with community entry to create awareness among the chiefs and opinion leaders in the community. One stakeholder said:

“…we had to now do this, what do we call it, health promotion. We had to go to the radio station, do durbars and other things to talk about it and the benefits to the public…………. [Interposing] Yes, we had to go to the opinion leaders like the chiefs, assembly members, and those people… so we had to involve them because once they know about it and they understand and they accept it, the whole what do we call, the community, will also, they look up to them, they will also accept. Basically, that is how we started.” [SH06].

This study has revealed that the vaccination strategy, encompassing both outreach and stationary sessions, observed a low level of male participation. As a result, vaccinators initiated home visits to guarantee the inclusion of all eligible individuals and prevent any oversight. This was an experience shared by an interviewee:

Mostly they [men] won’t come, they will need you to get like walk par beyiri. So, you do the outreach, so maybe no, when you do home visiting, with that you will be able to get them. For the outreach mostly we don’t get all of them [men] because they will be in their home, they won’t come. Maybe, unless it is a woman who has a child or a woman who is pregnant, you will get them [women]. But, for the men, a man should get out from his house and come to outreach point for the service, is difficult to get them.” [SH10].

The study uncovered several ad hoc activities that helped in the acceptance of the vaccine among the eligible population. These include the continuous health education and demand for COVID-19 vaccine cards by some offices and public places, before you can access their services. Such places, according to the study, include banks, bus check points, and MTN offices. A participant shared the following scenario:

“Yes, there was mad rush for the vaccine at some stage which wasn’t the case initially. Because, without the COVID card, places like banks and even on the road, the police will harass you saaaa. That one alone somehow influenced people attitude towards the vaccine.” [SH11].

The study revealed that the major points of health talks on the COVID-19 vaccines were radio stations, OPDs, static clinics, outreaches, durbars, and other public gatherings. The vaccination team, volunteers, and faith leaders like pastors and Imams all actively engage in giving adequate information to convince the communities to accept the COVID-19 vaccine. The health team had to go on radio sessions to ensure a good number of the population heard the information and had their doubts clarified, as indicated by the interviewee.

“So, the District Health Directorate buys airtime slot at the local FM station here, and every Monday we go there to give talk on the vaccine and open the lines for people to ask questions and have their concerns addressed. It actually helped in reducing the misconceptions and myths people had about this vaccine.” [SH11].

Organizational support for vaccination programmes

To achieve the objectives, governmental and non-governmental organizations contributed to the effectiveness of vaccination initiatives. The Ghana Health Service, as the main stakeholder, distributed vaccines, managed logistics, and allocated funds for fuel and meals, while non-governmental organizations ensured widespread vaccination coverage.

“So sometimes last year like NGO like CRS also provided motorbikes to enable us to run the COVID-19 vaccination activities. They donated one motorbike, you see that. Because they saw how we were struggling, so they also donated motorbike. Once we have motorbikes, we can be moving to almost most of the places.” [SH04].

Another participant supported this statement, saying:

“You know Global Community is an NGO, they also sponsored some vaccinations in northern region they sponsored in the COVID-19 vaccination. They provided some of the vaccines to vaccinate in the northern region and they conducted those awards scheme. You know. They provided the COVID-19 vaccines they also gave fuel for volunteers to go around to vaccinate people. At the end of it, they awarded some districts that did well for the vaccination coverage which fortunately our district was part.” [SH09].

Aside from that, the faith-based organizations, such as the churches and mosques, were involved in community mobilization and socially promoting the COVID-19 vaccines. This was due to the trust the congregation has in their religious leaders as recounted by one interviewee.

“We look out for the…. let me say people who have more influence in the community members like the pastors. You know once the pastor speaks all the congregation will be willing to listen to the pastor. So, if you… able to convince the pastor, to educate the pastor more about the COVID vaccine and he goes to the church and tells his people to take the vaccine you know everybody will be willing to take. You know in Africa, in Ghana here people have more trust in their pastors.” [SH09].

Another stakeholder added:

“And also, the mosques, we spoke to the Imam, after Friday Mosque services, the Imam will tell his people, we set up a vaccination station at the Mosque site. And after the prayers, they will come out to take the vaccine.” [SH11].

In the study, it has been noted that chiefs command respect in Northern Ghana and are influential in people’s acceptance of the vaccine. Interestingly the success of the vaccination programme was attributed to the immersed role played by the community volunteers who were involved in the community mobilization using ‘gong gong’ beating and other traditional modes of message sharing.

“But some of the opinion leaders in most of the communities speak it out, they receive the vaccine, and nothing happen to them, so they set themselves as example for their communities. So due to that, you know in Northern Region here, let say if an opinion leader speaks, for example if the chief in the community speaks, everybody obeys. So mostly, when we go to the community, we speak to the chief first, we speak with the Assemblymen first, the leaders in the community. So, once they take the vaccine, everybody in the community will be willing to take the vaccine.” [SH09].

Proposed strategies to enhance vaccination

Participants in the study deliberated and proposed diverse strategies to augment vaccine uptake in the study area.

It was observed that the previous enforcement of vaccine mandates had a substantial influence. Consequently, one participant advocated for the contemplation of reintroducing vaccine mandates as a plausible measure to enhance vaccination rates.

“The period when we witnessed people rushing for the vaccine is when they made it compulsory. It was made compulsory that everyone should have a vaccination card. I know we used to insist that I would never issue a card if you were not vaccinated. to vaccinate them with the introduction of the COVID-19 card as a mandatory card kind or something.” [SH7].

Moreover, the act of disseminating success narratives, such as the noteworthy reduction in the prevalence of COVID-19 infections and associated fatalities facilitated by the widespread administration of vaccines, has undeniably influenced the public perception and acceptance of immunization. One of the participants interviewed eloquently articulated this notion.

“Yes, we cited instances like how markets were reopened, and people no more becoming sick with ‘coro’, the higher chance of survival if you take the vaccine and still happen to be infected. Those things motivated many people to accept the vaccine. Sometimes we have to remind them of the six-killer childhood diseases and how the childhood vaccinations safe us. We have to cite evidence.” [SH15].

Participants unanimously agreed that continuous health education through various platforms, such as radio and television stations, community gatherings, and public events, played a pivotal role in sustaining the momentum. This approach was particularly efficacious in addressing the proliferation of conspiracy theories surrounding the vaccine. One participant remarked:

“You know the social media was busily feeding people with myths about the vaccine. So, we needed to up our game with the health education. We were pasting posters; the national news updates every day, and the progress made with the vaccine. People must understand the disease if they will accept the vaccine.” [SH14].

Adequate allocation of resources, such as transportation, funding for snacks and fuel, and other logistical support, is essential for enhancing vaccination coverage. The availability of resources is pivotal for effectively implementing any healthcare intervention. Participants voiced their discontent with the sporadic distribution of resources, as it served as a demotivating factor for vaccinators.

“When the resources are there and the motivation is good, people will work. Sometimes the DHA tells you that three people per team, but they give lunch and fuel for one person. You understand. To get the best results, give people adequate resources and motivate them well, and they will do their best to get you results.” [SH15].

Challenges encountered in the vaccination programme

The stakeholders expressed concerns regarding the obstacles confronted by vaccinators in enhancing vaccination coverage across the various districts and municipalities of Northern Ghana.

The investigation has identified numerous hurdles encountered in obtaining access to the vaccine. Particularly, individuals who are vulnerable, such as those with physical or visual disabilities, have faced challenges in obtaining the vaccine. Nevertheless, most districts and municipalities have adopted house-to-house vaccination strategies to address these circumstances. In response to this statement, one interviewee stated.

“Yes, people wanted the vaccine, but how to access it. In…. here, one blind man said anytime he hears about the vaccination session, they don’t come to his house, and he never received it. I know some people were not reached, except those vaccinating teams that take extra miles to do house-to-house vaccination.” [SH14].

The study uncovered evidence pointing to missed opportunities resulting from an inadequacy in logistics within the vaccination domain. Additionally, the irregular provision of vaccines and logistical support was identified as a hindrance to the implementation of the vaccination program.

“Well, I will attribute the success of most programmes to how resources were managed. We the vaccinators like this, it was stressful. Our communities are dispersed…and we don’t have adequate means of transport. The raining season is worst, the rain can trap you in a very far place. Sometimes you go to those places to meet large number of people and run out of vaccines or syringes because the DHA count them for you. So, the next time you don’t such larger numbers.” [SH05].

Another recounted transportation issues, saying:

“we’ll have to walk long distance to get a ride. Some of the nurses’ ride bicycles and the issue is how sustainable is that. Riding bicycle for immunization program. That’s really tough.” [SH03].

Seasonal variations, such as floods and river overflows, were also cited as impediments to the implementation of vaccination programs. Nonetheless, despite these formidable challenges, certain teams willingly assume the supplementary risk to extend their outreach efforts to individuals residing on agricultural estates.

“Most of our communities are hard to reach. I’m saying hard to reach because some of them you will have to cross water you know our community is a surrounded by the river Oti…. during the rain times like this, it was very difficult to get access people’s communities……we don’t have safety jackets. [SH10]

Exploration of herbal medicine as an alternative option

Some traditional medicine practitioners held the belief that COVID-19 is a disease they had previously encountered, and as herbalists, they possess knowledge on how to potentially treat this condition. As elucidated by one herbalist:

“Before COVID-19 came, we had experienced similar diseases, and we have our way of protecting and treating ourselves. There is a disease called ‘yogu’ (a disease caused by venom) when someone is infected with that disease, you might not understand it, unless those with experience, we go for their herbs, combine them, and start using them”. [SH17]

Another herbalist [SH16] added:

“Per the symptoms, we thought it was ‘yogu’ (a disease cause by a kind of Venom), so we hurriedly went for its herbs and were taking, either by burning the herbs into powder, so that you can use it to prepare soup, or you leave it like that and boil it and be drinking and bathing. We heard some of the symptoms of coughing, sneezing and difficulty breathing”.

Traditional healers believed that one could die from taking an injection for COVID-19, hence, they would prefer alternative medicine over the vaccine. One stakeholder explained:

"I would have sent that fellow to hospital. But the problem with going to the hospital would be that the probability of them giving injections at the hospital is very high, and the disease we were expecting was allergic to injection. If a person is down by ‘yogu’ and you give that person injection, he or she can easily die."

Another stakeholder stated a reason why people seek their services over medicine is because of the cost of treatment. Seeking alternative medicine is cheaper, hence patronage. Stakeholder [SH17] said:

"Local treatment is supposed to be done without charging the patient. If you return after I treat you to show appreciation by giving me something, it’s fine, but I will not charge you for the treatment. If you are sick and come to me, I can just look at you and I know the kind of disease you are suffering from. Most of my local medicines do not last, people always come for them."

Discussion

This paper analyzes the strategy employed to promote COVID-19 vaccination in northern Ghana, the problems faced, and the underlying factors contributing to vaccine reluctance. Our results indicate that the comparatively poor uptake resulted from a complex interaction of logistical, structural, and socio-cultural issues rather than a singular failure. The tactics that were used were complicated, but their effects were typically lessened by deeper issues, including trust, access, and different health views.

Knowing how the themes relate is important to understanding vaccination. Logistics (transportation, inconsistent resources) hindered immunization program effectiveness, making outreach uncertain. These supply-side failures likely increased public skepticism. Herbal medicine’s broad use as a trusted alternative highlights a demand-side challenge: a confidence gap in the formal health system and the strong impact of local belief systems. This made continual health education and interaction with church and community leaders not merely promotional activities but vital tools for fighting social media disinformation and creating credibility. Thus, stakeholders’ suggestions to improve staff motivation and resource allocation must be considered as important fixes to the initial rollout’s systemic flaws.

Capacity building, community mobilization, and ongoing health promotion (radio stations, durbars, churches, and marketplaces) were key to the vaccination program organization. Capacity building of health stakeholders in Kumasi led to the design of vaccine campaign posters, murals, signs, and other visuals for static clinics and vaccination centres [6]. our study found that these typical procedures were ineffective in this environment. In our study, stakeholders concluded that static or fixed clinics were unfeasible and needed outreach or house visits. This logistical adaptation shows a key vulnerability. Low male participation in static clinics required labor-intensive home visits, indicating that typical outreach approaches failed to engage crucial groups, indicating gender-specific reluctance or accessibility constraints. A Ghanaian study cited radio, social media, and press releases for COVID-19 vaccine marketing [34]. As shown in our results, the ‘rush’ for vaccines only after mandates was introduced shows that persuasive, educational strategies alone were insufficient to drive uptake, indicating a trust deficit or a population motivated more by coercion than conviction.

Congruent with other studies [35, 36], our study found that organizational support for the vaccination of the GHS and other agencies helped drive the COVID-19 vaccination campaign. This partnership between GHS and other benevolent groups sought to identify the human and societal factors that promote vaccine hesitancy and take efforts to address them [36]. Critical analysis shows that this external support has two sides. As of now, all vaccines received by Ghana have been donated by international agencies, multilateral organizations, or from the COVAX initiative, hence sufficient vaccine availability. However, Ghana’s ability to engage in co-funding of COVID-19 vaccines in the long term could be problematic due to issues relating to other vaccines [37, 38]. This highlights a chronic systemic vulnerability and dependency in the health system. Hence, these partnerships bridged the vaccine equitable distribution gap in northern Ghana. The role of faith-based organizations and traditional leaders in mobilization highlights a deeper issue: the official health system lacked the inherent, widespread trust needed to persuade the public, requiring pastors, imams, and chiefs’ ‘borrowed credibility’ Stakeholder proposals for vaccine mandates and resource distribution highlight this trust gap. These proposals acknowledge the initial strategy’s flaws. They admit that compelling communication alone could not combat disinformation and that operational issues like low staff enthusiasm hampered immunization attempts.

In this study, stakeholders proposed strategies such as vaccine mandates, disseminating success narratives, continuous health education, staff motivation, and resource allocation to enhance vaccine uptake. A similar study in Ghana had 66.2% said they would take the COVID-19 vaccines if it were mandated [39]. These findings are consistent with our findings and previous studies [40–42]. In contrast, [43] stated that COVID-19 vaccine mandates did not work in Ghana and Malawi. Also, studies have cited the collective and continuous government and public health leaders’ provision of clear information about the protective effects of the COVID-19 vaccines, and making the public aware of the uncertainties about the COVID-19 virus, resulting in increased vaccination [44, 45]. Although Canada’s high full dose rates highlight attitudinal differences from those in Ghana, positive behavioral nudges increase vaccine acceptance [46], public health strategies must consider psychological drivers of vaccine hesitancy to meet individual needs [47].

Despite the successes, the findings revealed that there were challenges encountered in vaccination programs, such as access for people with disability, transportation, and staff motivation issues. These findings corroborate with [48] on people with disability and COVID-19 vaccination and the need to target them with vaccine programs. Also, our findings were consistent with A cross-sectional survey that revealed barriers to vaccination, such as complaints about low staff motivation, such as low remuneration, transportation issues, difficulty in getting an appropriate vaccination center, poor data entry, and internet issues in Cape Coast [49]. Low vaccination could be attributed to the vaccinator-vaccinee ratio at the various centers. These issues must be reframed as health equity and system resilience shortcomings to understand limited uptake. People with impairments and those in isolated, seasonally cut-off areas were systemically excluded, creating and maintaining pockets of unvaccinated, vulnerable populations. Demotivation of staff due to insufficient resources is a significant failing that cripples frontline service performance. Supply-side constraints exacerbated demand-side reluctance by preventing willing participants from accessing the program or witnessing others’ exclusion, which likely reinforced preliminary misgivings about its efficacy, fairness, and legitimacy.

Further, this study captured the exploration of herbal medicine as an alternative option to the COVID-19 vaccine. Similar findings in Ethiopia indicate that 46% of people used traditional medicines for the prevention and treatment of COVID-19 [50]. Another 2.1% used herbs to prevent COVID-19 [51], while 15 different herbs were cited to prevent COVID-19 (De Meyer et al., [52]). A study revealed that 0.5% of healthcare workers in Wa, northern Ghana, would resort to traditional medicine if they ever had a COVID-19 infection [53], makes it more concerning. These numbers show a competing health paradigm that challenged the vaccine campaign’s fundamental principles, not just alternative treatment options. Traditional healers’ use of ‘yogu’ to explain COVID-19 shows that scientific messages were filtered through a powerful cultural lens that the official campaign failed to address. This is due to a lack of information, biomedical authority distrust, and a strong, culturally coherent alternative system of belief and practice. Despite the calls for support for alternative medicine to meet public health needs [54, 55], the persistent preference for herbal remedies shows that effective public health strategies must do more than provide information. They must actively engage with and respectfully bridge these divergent worldviews, possibly by integrating trusted traditional healers as partners in the health promotion ecosystem rather than viewing them as obstacles.

While the study focused on the COVID-19 vaccination campaign, the insights regarding community engagement, logistics, and trust-building provide knowledge for future pandemic readiness and adult vaccination programs in similar northern, underserved, and rural areas. However, their application to childhood vaccination programs remains limited because of different consent procedures and delivery systems.

Limitations

The authors acknowledge some limitations. The first limitation is the researcher’s inability to contact all the participants face-to-face. With Face-to-face, the researcher can read all the non-verbal communications and have proper follow-up questions. However, with online interviews, the researcher could not read most non-verbal actions due to potential interruptions such as poor internet connectivity during interviews, and this interrupted some follow-up questions when conversations resumed. The study included 17 participants whose views do not represent the general views of all healthcare stakeholders in northern Ghana, and any generalization should be made with caution. Also, stakeholders who accepted the interview were employees of the Ghana Health Service; hence, the possibility of social desirability bias could affect the study because participants could express some perceptions that could portray a good image about their employers or opinions of prejudice against their employer. Despite these limitations, we believe that these findings represent the COVID-19 vaccination strategies rolled out in northern Ghana to promote vaccination. Therefore, further studies are needed to determine the factors for vaccine hesitancy in northern Ghana in rural areas. Also, more studies on the strategies to improve vaccine uptake in northern Ghana would help implement more community and culturally competent solutions for vaccination.

Conclusions

Our study provides vital insights into the strategies employed, challenges, and strategies that could promote tackling COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy in northern Ghana. The study highlighted home visiting over stationary programs, partnerships for vaccination, staff motivations, vaccine availability for people with disability in vaccination programs, and the exploration of herbal medicine as an alternative option. The findings also suggest that future vaccination programmes could commence with dosing leaders who agreed to be early acceptors to improve acceptability. Future research should be conducted to understand area-specific needs, such as logistics to support vaccination programmes, people living with disability, and electronic disbursement of funds for vaccination purposes. The lessons from this study can guide future public health strategies for epidemic control and adult vaccination initiatives throughout northern Ghana and comparable regions by demonstrating the need for equity-focused, resilient, and culturally strong health systems.
