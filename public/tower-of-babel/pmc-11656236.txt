Facile CVD Fabrication of Vertically Aligned MoS2 Nanosheets with Embedded MoO2 on Molybdenum for High-Performance Binder-Free Lithium-Ion Battery Anodes
Authors: Navanya Raveendran, Sruthy Subash, Krishna Moorthy Ponnusamy, Jong Bae Park, Keun Heo, K. Kamala Bharathi, S. Chandramohan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The demand for compact energy storage devices necessitates the development of high-performance anode materials directly integrated with current collectors, minimizing or eliminating the need for binders or additives. With its layered structure and high theoretical capacity, molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) is regarded as a promising anode material for lithium-ion batteries (LIBs). Here, we report chemical vapor deposition (CVD) growth of self-integrated, vertically aligned MoS2 nanosheets with embedded molybdenum dioxide (MoO2) directly on a molybdenum foil and explore its potential as an anode material for LIBs. The results show that the formation of the MoO2/MoS2 hybrid structure occurs through partial conversion of the initially grown MoO2 crystals to MoS2 layers under controlled sulfurization reactions. The self-integrated hybrid material, devoid of additional conductive or binder agents, exhibits remarkably efficient transfer of ions and electrons, facilitated by the high electrical conductivity of MoO2 and exposed active sites of MoS2. Electrochemical studies reveal an impressive areal capacity of 253 μA h cm–2 for the MoO2/MoS2 hybrid material on a molybdenum foil. In addition, the Li-ion diffusion coefficient value is estimated to be 0.798 × 10–10 and 1.14 × 10–10 cm2/s for the delithiation and lithiation processes, respectively. The capacity of LIBs can be significantly enhanced by engineering the MoO2/MoS2 anode material, making it a promising candidate for overcoming the limitations of single-anode materials. Our findings show that CVD can be a potential approach toward the fabrication of binder and conducting agent-free anodes directly on current collectors for advanced LIBs.

Introduction

The drive toward clean and sustainable energy conversion and storage has stimulated significant interest in advancing energy storage systems, with particular focus on lithium-ion batteries (LIBs).1−4 Meanwhile, in the context of the Internet of Things (IoT), where interconnected devices necessitate adaptable electrochemical energy storage solutions, LIBs with compact and high-energy density features are demanded. As the IoT continues to proliferate, driven by the vision of an interconnected future, there arises a demand for all-solid thin-film batteries that can integrate seamlessly with micro- and nanoelectronics, even as technological nodes shrink. This integration requires a compact design, where the growth of the anode material directly on current collectors emerges as a pivotal step forward.5,6 The concept is particularly intriguing due to its utilization of the active material substrate as the current collector, enhancing electrical contacts and improving energy density and rate performance. By elimination of binders and inactive compounds, this approach holds promise for optimizing the performance of LIBs. This motivates considerable efforts toward development of high-performance anode materials directly on current collectors,5,6 flexible hierarchical anode materials,7 and effective maneuvering of the current collector8 using scalable growth methods such as chemical vapor deposition (CVD). Meanwhile, the expanding array of application targets characterized by compact dimensions and the demand for enhanced capacity necessitate electrode materials that deliver superior electrochemical performance.

Considerable efforts have been devoted to the search for efficient anode materials since an anode is crucial to a battery’s reversible capacity, user safety, and lifespan.7,9,10 In the category of anode materials explored for thin-film microbatteries, Li4Ti5O12 and other titanium-based compounds have been widely studied, but their potential is limited owing to their low electronic conductivity.4,11 Alternatively, the layered molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) material has shown great potential as an active anode material for LIBs due to the large specific surface area (which provides a large contact area between the active material and the electrolyte), extra metal ion storage capacities due to the adsorption edge sites, fast ion diffusion, insertion, and extraction with a better material utilization process.1,12 MoS2 has a layered structure, and atoms within the S–Mo–S basal planes are bound by strong covalent bonds. Interestingly, as it is unique for any layered material, the S–Mo–S atomic layers are held together by van der Waals forces; hence, the interlayer space allows foreign ions, such as Li+, to intercalate between the layers,12−16 providing a specific capacity of 800–900 mA h g–1.17−20

While MoS2 demonstrates several advantages over graphite as an anode material in LIBs, the electrode instability due to volume expansion and contraction during cycling and the low electronic and ionic conductivity of MoS2 all collectively lead to low cycling retention rate, limiting its practical applications. Hybridizing or encapsulating nanostructured MoS2 with carbon nanomaterials has been the most widely used strategy to overcome the aforementioned limitations.21−25 Meanwhile, various other hybrid structure combinations have received significant interest owing to their improved stability and electrochemical performance in batteries.26,27 For example, Luo et al. reported an anode for sodium-ion batteries based on MoO2/MoS2 core–shell heterostructures anchored on reduced graphene oxide nanosheets.28 The disproportionate charge distribution at the heterogeneous interface between MoO2 and MoS2 boosts the charge-transfer kinetics and provides extra active sites for sodium storage.28 Similarly, a hierarchical MoO2@MoS2 hollow structure consisting of MoS2 nanosheets and hollow MoO2 nanospheres has also been designed as an anode for Li-ion batteries.29 The accumulated volume variation and slow kinetic reaction rate of MoO2 led to a prolonged activation time and rapid capacity decay, but its high conductivity, capacity, and ability to stabilize the heterostructure when combined with MoS2 make MoO2/MoS2 a robust anode material.29 It is well documented that the use of nanostructured materials and surface modification approaches can mitigate the problems associated with volume expansion and contraction, leading to improved battery performance. To this end, hybrid nanostructures, such as the MoO2/MoS2 core–shell material,30 porous nanotubes assembled with vertically aligned metallic MoS2 nanosheets,31,32 and MoS2 heterostructures combining different structural phases,2 have been studied.

Alternatively, interconnected vertically stacked/aligned MoS2 nanosheets offering high surface area and edge sites and short ionic diffusion length can offer significant electrochemical performance.1,6 While there are a few reports on the direct formation of vertical MoS2 on various conductive substrates such as a titanium foil (Zhou et al.5) and a molybdenum foil (Guo et al.6 and Shokhen et al.32), the direct CVD growth of the phase-engineered hybrid anode material (MoO2/MoS2) on a current collector has not been reported yet. Such an approach holds promise as an attractive strategy for next-generation LiBs that demand flexibility and scalability. Interestingly, the direct integration of anode materials with the current collector by CVD growth will require no post-synthetic slurry treatment and eliminate dead weight from active binders and conductive additives.6 Recently, we have shown that it is possible to produce dense vertical formation of pure MoO2 and MoS2 and/or MoO2/MoS2 core–shell materials in a space-confined CVD growth process through controlling the sulfur vapor concentration.33 Motivated by this observation, in this paper, we demonstrate facile fabrication of interconnected MoO2/MoS2 hierarchical nanostructures on an Mo foil and explore its potential as a binder-free anode for LiBs. To the best of our knowledge, this study remains the first report on the exploration of direct growth of a MoO2/MoS2 hierarchical nanostructured anode material on a molybdenum foil via CVD. Detailed cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analyses showed promising electrochemical properties of the MoO2/MoS2 nanostructured hybrid anode material and associated Li-ion dynamics.

Experimental Details

Synthesis Procedure

Prior to the growth, the substrate (25 μm-thick molybdenum (Mo) foil) underwent the following steps for cleaning: initially, the foil was immersed in concentrated hydrochloric acid for 30 min and then rinsed multiple times in DI water. Subsequently, the foil was thoroughly cleaned ultrasonically in acetone, methanol, and IPA, each for 10 min. The cleaned Mo foil and the source materials (sulfur (S) powder −99.98%, Sigma-Aldrich, and MoO3 powder −99.97%, Sigma-Aldrich) were loaded into the quartz tube and placed at two different temperature zones of the two-zone furnace. In a typical growth process, a ceramic boat containing 10 mg of MoO3 powder was placed ∼22 cm away from the sulfur powder. The Mo foil was placed ∼1 cm above the MoO3 precursor. Both the MoO3 and S zones were heated to temperatures of 775 and 250 °C, respectively, to facilitate the evaporation of the precursors and reaction to occur on the substrate. Transport of the vapors is controlled with the aid of high-purity argon as the carrier gas.

Characterization

The surface morphology and homogeneity of the grown MoO2/MoS2 were examined using a high-resolution scanning electron microscope (HRSEM, Thermo Scientific Apero). HRSEM with an accelerating potential of 20 kV was employed to image the vertical MoO2/MoS2 hybrid structure. The structural analysis of the grown material on a molybdenum foil was performed using a micro-Raman spectrometer (Horiba LabRAM HR evolution spectrometer) at room temperature in a backscattering configuration. The samples were excited using a monochromatic solid-state laser (λ = 532 nm), and the laser beam was focused using a 100× objective, yielding a spectral resolution of 0.5 cm–1. The laser power and the grating used were 3.2 mW and 1800 lines/mm, respectively. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded using copper Kα radiation of wavelength 1.5418 Å. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed using a Nexsa XPS System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK) with an Al–Kα (1486.6 eV) X-ray source at a base pressure of 2 × 10–7 Torr. The beam size used was 400 μm. The pass energies for acquiring the survey scan and core-level spectra were 200 and 50 eV, respectively. A step size of 1.0 and 0.1 eV was used for survey and narrow scans, respectively. All the measurements were done on the sample grown on the molybdenum foil.

Electrochemical Studies

Electrochemical characterization was performed in a CR2032-type coin cell. The electrode materials prepared by the CVD growth process were used directly in the CR2032 coin cell. The area of the electrode used was about 1 cm2. The separator and electrolyte used in the electrochemical studies were glass fiber of C grade and a 1:1 volume ratio of 1 M LiPF6 in ethylene carbonate (EC)/dimethyl carbonate (DMC), respectively. An argon glovebox with less than 0.1 ppm moisture and oxygen was employed for the coin cell assembly. CV and EIS analyses were performed on an Origalyz potentiostat s/w. CV was done at different scan rates in the potential range from 0 to 3.00 V (vs Li/Li+). Galvanostatic charge/discharge cycling was carried out using an electrochemical workstation—Neware s/w, in the potential window of 0–3.00 V. Cycle life as well as the rate capability studies for the cell was also performed in the same Neware system.

Results and Discussion

The CVD reactor used in the growth of the MoO2/MoS2 hybrid material had two temperature zones, controlled independently in order to precisely control the evaporation rate of MoO3 and sulfur precursor materials. Based on our earlier findings,33,34 a growth temperature of 775 °C was considered optimal for the growth of MoS2 under atmospheric pressure conditions. Below, we first discuss the growth conditions and microstructural features of the grown material in detail. To facilitate the growth of vertical nanostructures, the Mo foil was placed on the ceramic boat containing the MoO3 powder in two different configurations, as illustrated in Figure 1g,h. In one configuration, the substrate was positioned at an inclined angle within the boat just above the MoO3 powder, while in the other configuration, the substrate was placed horizontally on the boat. The flipped substrate enables space-confined growth, ensuring the formation of vertically aligned nanostructures. Generally, growth of MoS2 and/or other intermediate phases occurs as a result of the reduction of MoO3 with sulfur. However, the kinetics of the growth can be complex, depending on the growth conditions. More details on the growth mechanism can be found in our previous study.33 It is noteworthy to mention here that the concentration of sulfur locally available for the reaction to occur can be manipulated by means of the positioning of the substrate. When the substrate is positioned in proximity to the MoO3 powder, bulk crystals of MoO2 form on the molybdenum foil, which is evident from Figure 1a,b that illustrates the SEM images of the sample surface. Figure 1c shows the tilted-view SEM image of the MoO2 crystals on the Mo foil. Notably, upon close observation of the surface of the bulk crystals, the growth of vertically aligned flakes at very few sites is identified. The propensity for the growth of vertically oriented flakelike structures increases when the distance between the Mo foil and the MoO3 powder is further increased. This can be clearly visualized from Figure 1d–f, where the SEM images portray the formation of vertically oriented nanostructures over the entire substrate and the morphology evolution, depending on the material phases formed.

The variations in the precursor mass ratio (MoO3/S) can lead to the formation of intermediate phases, which can be influenced by various growth parameters.33,34 Among the key growth parameters in the synthesis of MoS2, the substrate position plays a special role in determining the growth due to variations in the vapor flux. In the first case, since the substrate is placed in proximity to the MoO3 powder, the amount of sulfur reaching the growing face of the substrate is limited. This condition describes a scenario where the substrate is exposed to an abundance of MoO3 vapors, while there is a deficiency of S vapors, thereby impeding the phase conversion to MoS2. On the other hand, in the second case, the substrate was parallelly kept over the MoO3 powder, allowing for adequate concentration of sulfur vapors to reach the reaction regime.

Raman spectroscopy and XRD analyses were employed to identify the presence of different phases in the sample. The E12g and A1g modes, corresponding to the in-plane and out-of-plane vibration of Mo–S and S–S atoms, are observed at ∼382.3 and ∼407.7 cm–1, respectively, which resembles the most stable and commonly encountered 2H phase of MoS2. From the results, an average frequency difference of 25.15 ± 0.25 cm–1 is estimated, which is likely due to the densely formed vertical nanosheet morphology. Furthermore, the full width at half-maxima (Γ) of the Raman peak, a qualitative descriptor of the crystalline quality of the material, are estimated to be 4.2 and 4.4 cm–1, respectively, for E12g and A1g peaks. These values signify the good crystalline nature of the grown MoS2 vertical nanosheets.36,37

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy measurements were performed to gain a clearer understanding of the surface electronic state and composition of grown MoO2/MoS2. Figure 2e–g shows the high-resolution scans of molybdenum 3d, sulfur 2p, and oxygen 1s core levels, respectively. Here, the Mo 3d experimental spectrum is fitted with three doublet peaks by using XPSPEAK41 software, as shown in Figure 2e. The three doublet peaks correspond to the Mo4+ state of MoS2, MoO2, and Mo6+ state of MoO3. The intense peaks observed at 229.47 and 232.64 eV are related to the Mo 3d5/2 and Mo 3d3/2 spin–orbit doublet of the Mo4+ state of MoS2 (typically of the Mo–S bond), and the peaks at 229.07 and 232.29 eV can be attributed to the Mo4+ state of MoO2. Furthermore, the low-intensity peaks observed at 232.72 and 235.75 eV can be attributed to the Mo 3d5/2 and Mo 3d3/2 spin–orbit doublet of the Mo6+ state of MoO3. Given the surface-sensitive nature of XPS, the presence of MoO3 can be accounted to the unreacted MoO3 precursor. The peak at 226.5 eV further confirms that the material grown is predominantly of MoS2 at the surface level. The S 2p core-level spectrum is also analyzed to identify the chemical environment of sulfur in the sample. In Figure 2f, the S 2p spectrum is described by a doublet with the 2p3/2 line at 161.75 eV and the 2p1/2 line at 163.02 eV. A spin–orbit splitting of ∼1.2 eV confirms that S exists in the chemical environment of MoS2. Figure 2g shows the O 1s spectrum, where we identified two peaks, one at 530.03 eV corresponding to the Mo–O bond and the second at 531.69 eV from the adsorbed oxygen.33,38,39

The first reaction in eq 4 is fully reversible and is generally due to the initial insertion or lithiation process of lithium ions into MoS2, which happens to be at a potential near to 1.1 V.41 Here, transition from the 2H phase to the 2H lithiated phase occurs, indicating a transformation from 2H to 1T phase change during the process of lithium intercalation.42,43 The concentration of lithium on the vertical walls of MoO2/MoS2 causes lithium to be involved in the intercalation and deintercalation mechanisms. Previous studies on this also confirm the transition of the 2H to 1T phase in which each unit of 2H MoS2 undergoes an insertion or injection of 4 electrons during the transformation, which lowers the kinetic barrier and raises the electron concentration. This results in a 107 fold improvement in MoS2’s electrical conductivity, which also improves Li-ion storage by lowering the charge-transfer resistance.44 Along with this transformation, below this voltage plateau, there seems to be one or multiple disproportionation events occurring along with the existence of intermediate metastable sulfide species. The reactions have been suspected since the early research on Li–MoS2. They were somewhat clarified in the late 1980s when Selwyn et al. released important work on the disintegration of molybdenum and tungsten dichalcogenides during lithiation. The initial discharge capacity attained is 253 μA h/cm2. A decrease in discharge capacity is observed at the second cycle, which continues to show a decrement in capacity with cycling signifying the occurrence of SEI and conversion process during the charge–discharge cycle marked by a flat plateau appearing near 0.63 V. In the first five cycles, the discharge/charge capacities obtained for the MoO2/MoS2 anode are 255.01/176.53, 223.34/192.60, 211.54/182.66, 195.94/176.53, and 176.99/150.06 μA h/cm2, respectively.

The cycling performance of MoO2/MoS2 is represented in Figure 5b, obtained at a current density of 5 μA/cm2 and swept in a voltage range of 0.025–3.0 V. MoO2/MoS2 exhibits a higher initial discharge capacity of 253.75 μA h/cm2 and a charge capacity of 192.5 μA h/cm2 with an initial Coulombic efficiency of 69.45%. Slowly with increasing cycles, the Coulombic efficiency approaches a value of 95.74%. Also, there occurs a decrease in capacity with increasing cycles, and a drastic capacity fading is observed, highlighting the low stability of the film owing to its network-like array structure that could reduce the intercalation-induced stress, avoiding the volume expansion of MoO2/MoS2 and support each other between the MoS2 nanosheets. Such an unstable profile is observed due to the phase transition of MoO2/MoS2 from hexagonal to tetragonal upon cycling. Capacity fading in this material is a pure contribution from the 1T or tetragonal phase of MoO2/MoS2.43

The rate capability of the MoO2/MoS2 hybrid structure was obtained for different current densities ranging from 5, 10, 15, 25, 50, and 5 μA/cm2, exhibiting a capacity of 252.28 μA h/cm2 for a lower current rate of 5 μA. For varying current densities, the capacities obtained are 252.8, 183.7, 143.1, 107.8, 70.5, and 186.5 μA h/cm2, respectively. The study reveals the better reversibility of the MoO2/MoS2 hybrid structure as the reversible capacity attained after 25 cycles is 186.5 μA h/cm2. The rate capability as can be seen in Figure 5c for the MoO2/MoS2 hybrid anode displays a capacity of approximately 252.8 μA h/cm2 at 5 μA and produces a capacity of 107.8 μA h/cm2 at 25 μA, which establishes its high-rate behavior that can be again related to the high diffusion coefficient of the electrode material. The plot in Figure 5c illustrates the solidity and rate capabilities of the hybrid anode material.

Further to explore the lithium-ion dynamics or the charge-transfer kinetics in the MoO2/MoS2 material, a EIS study was performed. The as-obtained Nyquist plot before and after cycle (Figure 5d,e) clearly demonstrates the two discrete regions representing both high-frequency and mid-frequency parts comprising semicircles and a shortened and an extended inclined line compared to that of the low-frequency part. Considering the high- and mid-frequency region in the Nyquist plots (Figure 5e), the semicircles observed in the regions denote various resistances such as (a) Rs—the combined resistance offered by the electrolyte, separator, and electrical contacts in the high-frequency part and (b) Rct—the charge-transfer resistance offered during the charge-transfer process represented by the semicircle to the high–mid-frequency part. Herein, the charge-transfer resistance in the vertically aligned MoS2 nanosheets on MoO2 is less, enabling an easier charge-transfer process. The transition from a 2H phase to a 2H lithiated phase results in the electrode material with improved conductivity, as MoO2/MoS2 in its T phase or T transition state exhibits an improved conductivity than itself in the 2H phase. Through the detailed analysis of the fitted Nyquist plot, two different equivalent circuit model has been evolved for before and after the cycle (Figure 5f,g).

The equivalent circuit was retrieved from the Nyquist plot shown in Figure 5f,g with a contribution of solution (Rs), film (Rf), and charge-transfer (Rct) resistances along with W—Warburg contribution— and the corresponding constant phase element (CPE) for before (Figure 5f) and after (Figure 5g) cycle. The electrode shows an Rs value of 130 Ω before the cycle, which drops to a value of 7.1 Ω after the cycle. Such a low resistance is due to the electrode structure and transformation of phase during the lithiation and delithiation processes. Another noteworthy variation can be seen in the Warburg region, indicating a sloping line at an angle of 45° right after the semicircles for MoO2/MoS2 EIS performed after the cycles compared to diffusion-limited response without a much predominant sloping line for MoO2/MoS2 EIS performed before the cycles. The complete length of the Warburg line is symptomatic of the diffusional limitation features of the material, with MoO2/MoS2 showing more predominant diffusion after the cycle than being diffusion-limited before the cycle. Such reduced diffusional limitations in MoO2/MoS2 are a direct consequence of their architecture, i.e., the thin nanometer-scale oxide walls with interconnected mesoporosity wetted by the electrolyte.

Conclusions

In summary, we successfully fabricated a MoO2/MoS2 hybrid nanostructured material on a molybdenum foil using CVD. Our study highlights that this hybrid material, enriched with vertically oriented MoS2 nanosheets on the surface, can be realized through controlled conversion of the MoO2 seed layer. As an anode material, the grown MoO2/MoS2 hybrid nanostructure exhibits significantly improved areal Li storage capacity and cycle stability, achieving a reversible areal capacity as high as 253 μA h/cm2 after the first cycle. Li-ion diffusion coefficients of 0.798 × 10–10 and 1.14 × 10–10 cm2/s were estimated for delithiation and lithiation processes, respectively, from the cyclic voltammograms obtained at different scan rates. The extensively exposed edges of MoS2 nanosheets provide ample van der Waals gaps for the insertion and deinsertion of Li+ ions, further enhancing its potential for Li-ion battery applications. By eliminating the need for binders and conductive additives, the direct CVD growth of MoO2/MoS2 on a current collector for seamless integration into battery cells represents a key strategy for advanced Li-ion batteries.
