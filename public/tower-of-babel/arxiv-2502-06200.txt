On the query complexity of sampling from non-log-concave distributions Thanks: Accepted for presentation at the Conference on Learning Theory (COLT) 2025.
Yuchen He, Chihao Zhang

Abstract
We study the problem of sampling from a d d -dimensional distribution with density p ⁡ ( x ) ∝ e − f ⁡ ( x ) p(x)\propto e^{-f(x)} , which does not necessarily satisfy good isoperimetric conditions. Specifically, we show that for any L , M L,M satisfying L ​ M ≥ d ≥ 5 LM\geq d\geq 5 , ε ∈ ( 0 , 1 200 ) \varepsilon\in\left(0,\frac{1}{200}\right) , and any algorithm with query accesses to the value of f ⁡ ( x ) f(x) and ∇ f ​ ( x ) \gradient f(x) , there exists an L L -log-smooth distribution with second moment at most M M such that the algorithm requires ( L ​ M d ​ ε ) Ω ⁡ ( d ) \left(\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}\right)^{\Omega(d)} queries to compute a sample whose distribution is within ε \varepsilon in total variation distance to the target distribution. We complement the lower bound with an algorithm requiring ( L ​ M d ​ ε ) 𝒪 ⁡ ( d ) \left(\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}\right)^{\mathcal{O}(d)} queries, thereby characterizing the tight (up to the constant in the exponent) query complexity for sampling from the family of non-log-concave distributions. Our results are in sharp contrast with the recent work of Huang et al. (COLT’24), where an algorithm with quasi-polynomial query complexity was proposed for sampling from a non-log-concave distribution when M = 𝚙𝚘𝚕𝚢 ⁡ ( d ) M=\mathtt{poly}(d) . Their algorithm works under the stronger condition that all distributions along the trajectory of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, starting from the target distribution, are 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathcal{O}(1) -log-smooth. We investigate this condition and prove that it is strictly stronger than requiring the target distribution to be 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathcal{O}(1) -log-smooth. Additionally, we study this condition in the context of mixtures of Gaussians. Finally, we place our results within the broader theme of “sampling versus optimization”, as studied in Ma et al. (PNAS’19). We show that for a wide range of parameters, sampling is strictly easier than optimization by a super-exponential factor in the dimension d d .

Introduction

The problem of sampling from a given distribution has gained much attention in recent years due to its wide applications in many fields such as machine learning, physics, finance and statistics (e.g.
[
ADFDJ03
,
Kra06
,
Dag07
,
LB21
]
). Given query access to the value and gradients of a potential function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, the task is to generate a sample from the distribution
\mu
with density
p_{\mu}\propto e^{-f}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
within as few queries as possible.

Many algorithms have been developed to address this problem. The Langevin algorithm and its variants are the most commonly used ones (e.g.
[
RT96
,
CB18
,
CCBJ18
,
Wib19
,
CLA+21
,
LPW24
]
).
Polynomial-time convergence of Langevin-based algorithms is guaranteed when the target distribution
\mu
exhibits good properties such as isoperimetric properties (e.g.
[
VW19
,
CEL+24
,
MHFH+23
,
ZCL+23
]
) or log-concavity (e.g.
[
SL19
,
DCWY19
,
AC24
]
).

However, many interesting sampling problems are not log-concave and do not satisfy good isoperimetric properties. In recent years, there has been growing interest in sampling from general non-log-concave distributions. In this work, as well as in most of the literature on non-log-concave sampling, the distributions are required to satisfy only the following two weak assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

The second moment of
\mu
is bounded, i.e.
\mathbf{E}_{X\sim\mu}\left[\|X\|^{2}\right]\leq M
for some
M<\infty
.

Assumption 2
.

The potential function
f
is differentiable and
\gradient f
is
L
-Lipschitz, i.e, for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\|\gradient f(x)-\gradient f(y)\|\leq L\|x-y\|
.

Note that
Assumption

2
is equivalent to requiring
\mu
to be
L
-log-smooth. There are mainly two lines of work regarding non-log-concave sampling.

The first line aims at understanding the astonishing performance of the score-based generative models (SGMs) in practice. It is proved that assuming the score functions can be accurately estimated, an accurate sampler with polynomially many queries exists (e.g.
[
CCL+23b
,
CCL+23a
,
CLL23
,
BBDD24
]
).

Another line of work is to identify the inherent sample complexity under the value / gradient oracle model. For special distributions such as the mixture of Gaussians of some particular shapes, polynomially many queries are sufficient to get an accurate sample (
[
LRG18
,
GTC24
]
). On the other hand, a lower bound of
e^{\Omega(d)}
for general non-log-concave sampling problems is established in
[
LRG18
]
.

To the best of our knowledge, the query complexity for sampling in terms of the parameters
L
and
M
is not known yet. The work of
[
HRT24
]
derived a bound of
\exp\left(\mathcal{O}\left(d\log\frac{L(d+M)}{\varepsilon}\right)+Z\right)
, where
Z
, the maximum norm of the particles appeared in their algorithm, does not have an explicit bound. The recent work
[
HZD+24
]
designed a quasi-polynomial algorithm under a stronger assumption. Their algorithm further requires that
the distributions during the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process (OU process) starting from the target distribution
\mu
to be
L
-log-smooth
and has query complexity roughly
\exp\left(\mathcal{O}(L^{3})\cdot\mathtt{polylog}(Ld+M)\right)
. Therefore, if all the distributions along the trajectory of the OU process is
\mathcal{O}(1)
-log-smooth and
M=\mathtt{polylog}(d)
, their algorithm is quasi-polynomial. Breaking the exponential barrier turns out to be significant in both theory and applications. However, as far as we can see, the relationship between this “smoothness along the trajectory” assumption used in their work and
Assumption

2
remains unclear, which limits the applicability of their results.

In this work, we make progress in understanding the query complexity for sampling under
Assumption

1
and
Assumption

2
. Additionally, we further investigate the “smoothness along the trajectory” property mentioned above and, finally, compare our results with the query complexity for general non-convex optimization.

1.1
Main results

We summarize our main results in the following four aspects.

Query complexity lower bounds for sampling

First, we provide a general query complexity lower bound. We prove that to guarantee an error of
\varepsilon
in total variation distance,
\left(\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}\right)^{\Omega(d)}
queries is inevitable. See
Theorem

3
below for the formal statement.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\varepsilon\in\left(0,\frac{1}{200}\right)
. For any
L,M>0
such that
LM\geq d
and for any
d\geq 5
, if a sampling algorithm
\mathcal{A}
always terminates within
K
queries on any target distribution
\mu
under
Assumption

1
and
2
, and guarantees that the distribution of
\mathcal{A}
’s output, denoted as
\tilde{\mu}
, satisfies
\mathtt{TV}(\tilde{\mu},\mu)\leq\varepsilon
, then
K=\left(\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}\right)^{\Omega(d)}
.

Besides, when
\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}=\omega(1)
, the constant coefficient in the exponent is
\frac{1}{2}-o\left(\frac{1}{d}\right)
, i.e., we can prove a lower bound of the form
\left(\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}-o(1)}
. The detailed formula is given in
Theorem

8
.

Note that
LM\geq d
is a natural assumption for distributions (see
Lemma

58
). This indicates that, in general exponentially many queries are needed for sampling, and when
LM=\omega(d)
, super-exponential queries are required. This result significantly improved previous lower bound of
e^{\Omega(d)}
in
[
LRG18
]
. Our techniques can also be applied to
[
Cha24
]
and may offer some inspiration for non-log-concave sampling problems under other settings like
[
HB23
]
to establish more general bounds in their contexts.

The dependency of other parameters also provides new insights. Notably, we first incorporate the dependence of
\varepsilon
in the lower bound, establishing an
(1/\varepsilon)^{\Omega(d)}
dependence. This rules out the possibility of fast samplers, i.e., the samplers with
\mathtt{polylog}\left(1/{\varepsilon}\right)
dependence, in non-log-concave cases, which contrasts with the case of log-concave sampling. For another example, according to the result of
[
HMHBE24
]
, dependence of
\mathcal{O}\left(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)
can be achieved when the distribution satisfies the functional Poincaré inequality (FPI). Although their result is in terms of the expected query complexity, we can terminate the algorithm once the number of queries exceeds
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)
to get a worst case complexity bound. By Markov’s inequality, this still ensures an error of at most
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
in total variation distance. Compared with
Theorem

3
, this implies that in high-dimensional cases, the FPI does not hold in general for distributions under Assumption
1
and
2
.

Query complexity upper bounds for sampling

We also design an algorithm to sample from
\mu
with an
\varepsilon
error in total variation distance for any
\mu
satisfying
Assumption

1
and
2
, with the query complexity bounded by
\left(\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}\right)^{\mathcal{O}(d)}\cdot\mathtt{poly}(\varepsilon^{-1},d,L,M)
.

Theorem 4
.

Assume
d\geq 3
. There exists an algorithm such that, for any distribution
\mu
with density
p_{\mu}(x)\propto e^{-f_{\mu}(x)}
where
f_{\mu}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\gradient f(0)=0
, and satisfies
Assumption

1
and
2
, outputs a sample
x
with distribution
\tilde{\mu}
satisfying
\mathtt{TV}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})\leq\varepsilon
within
\left(\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}\right)^{\mathcal{O}(d)}\cdot\mathtt{poly}\left(\varepsilon^{-1},d,L,M\right)
query accesses to
f_{\mu}
and
\gradient f_{\mu}
, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.

The assumption
\gradient_{\mu}f(0)=0
is for the sake of simplicity. One can first apply a gradient descent algorithm to find a stationary point of

and shift the origin to that point. The form of this bound matches our lower bound in
Theorem

3
, which suggests that the optimal query complexity for sampling from non-log-concave distributions should be
\left(\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}\right)^{\Theta(d)}
.

The smoothness evolvement during the OU process

As mentioned before, the work of
[
HZD+24
]
presented a quasi-polynomial algorithm for sampling from distributions with second moment at most
M=\mathtt{poly}(d)
. They further require that all distribution along the trajectory of the OU process initialized at the target distribution is
\mathcal{O}(1)
-log-smooth. Our lower bound already implies that the condition is strictly stronger than merely requiring the target distribution to be

-smooth. We further construct an explicit family of distributions, called “stitched Gaussians”, and calculate its evolvement of the smoothness property along the OU trajectory. This family of distributions are
\mathcal{O}(1)
-log-smooth while along the OU process, the Hessian of their log density becomes unbounded.

Theorem 5
.

For the OU process

Our results indicate that on those distributions with the property in
Theorem

5
, the upper bound of
[
HZD+24
]

e^{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(L^{3})}
with
L
being the smoothness bound during whole the OU process, can be very large. In contrast, our algorithm only requires smoothness of the initial distribution, allowing it to achieve a better complexity bound than
[
HZD+24
]
in these cases.

Moreover, our results also suggest that it is interesting to study under what conditions, the

-log-smoothness condition can be preserved during the OU process. We obtain some partial results for mixtures of Gaussian and summarize them in

The comparison between sampling and optimization

Finally, we provide a comparison between the sampling and optimization tasks. We establish the lower bound in
Theorem

for optimization problems in non-convex cases based on the results in
[

]
. Let

Assume

1.2
Technical overview

1.2.1
The lower bound

The general idea to prove a query complexity lower bound, as used in previous work
[
LRG18
,
Cha24
]
, is to construct many distributions that are difficult for the algorithm to distinguish. Specifically, for each
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, one can perturb the density of a base distribution
\mu_{0}
in
\mathcal{B}_{r}(v)
, a ball of radius
r
centered at
v
, to get a new distribution
\mu_{v}
. For two vectors
u
and
v
with
\mathcal{B}_{r}(u)\cap\mathcal{B}_{r}(v)=\varnothing
, after proper perturbation, one can get
\mathtt{TV}(\mu_{v},\mu_{u})\approx\varepsilon
. Suppose one can find
n
disjoint balls, with centers
\left\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\right\}
. Then if the algorithm wants to sample from
\mu_{v_{i}}
within error
\varepsilon
, it should first recognize
v_{i}
among the
n
candidates. This indicates that when the input instance is
\mu_{0}
, the algorithm must query almost every
\mathcal{B}_{r}(v_{i})
, which gives the lower bound
\Omega(n)
.

Then at a high level, proving lower bounds can be viewed as packing as many disjoint
\mathcal{B}_{r}(v)
’s in the domain of the base distribution while maintaining the desired properties (smoothness and bounded second moment) of the distribution. Previous works
[
LRG18
,
Cha24
]

chose a Gaussian distribution as the base
\mu_{0}
and proved a lower bound of
e^{\Omega(d)}
using the above arguments.

In this work, we want to derive a general lower bound with regard to all parameters
d,L,M
and
\varepsilon
. Our key observation is that using Gaussian as the base distribution is suboptimal. Let us explain the reason.
There exists a trade-off between packing more disjoint balls and maintaining properties of the perturbed distributions. To pack more balls, one needs the radius
r
to be as small as possible, and the center
v_{i}
should be as far as possible from the origin. However,

1.

As the mass of perturbed area is approximately
\varepsilon
more than the base distribution, each
\mathcal{B}_{r}(v_{i})
should be placed inside
\mathcal{B}_{R}
for
R=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{M}{\varepsilon}}\right)
to ensure that the second moment of
\mu_{v_{i}}
remains
\mathcal{O}(M)
.

2.

The radius
r
must be large enough to guarantee a smooth transition at the boundary, ensuring that
\mu_{v_{i}}
remains
\mathcal{O}(L)
-log-smooth.

Suppose we pick a Gaussian distribution as the base distribution
\mu_{0}
as in the previous work, namely that
\mu_{0}=\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{M}{d}\mathtt{Id}_{d}\right)
(so that the second moment of
\mu_{0}
is
M
). Let function
h_{0}
and
f_{i}
be the log-density of
\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{M}{d}\mathtt{Id}_{d}\right)
and
\mu_{v_{i}}
respectively. Suppose we want to pack all balls with their centers at
R\cdot\mathcal{S}_{d-1}
(the sphere with radius
R
). Then to maintain the second moment of each
\mu_{v_{i}}
to be
\mathcal{O}(M)
, one should pick
R=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{M}{\varepsilon}}\right)
. For those points
x
with
\norm{x}\approx R\approx\sqrt{\frac{M}{\varepsilon}}
, we have
h_{0}(x)\approx\frac{d}{\varepsilon}
.

To guarantee the mass of
\mathcal{B}_{r}(v)
to be
\approx\varepsilon
, we have
\int_{\mathcal{B}_{r}(v_{i})}\left(e^{-f_{i}(x)}-e^{-h_{0}(x)}\right)\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}x\approx\varepsilon
. This indicates that
h_{0}(x)-f_{i}(x)\approx\frac{d}{\varepsilon}
inside
\mathcal{B}_{r}(v_{i})
. To further guarantee the
\mathcal{O}(L)
-smoothness of
f_{i}
,
r
should be
\Omega\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{L\varepsilon}}\right)
. One can pack approximately
\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{d}
(
Lemma

16
) many disjoint balls with radius
r
centering at
R\cdot\mathcal{S}_{d-1}
, which gives a lower bound approximately
\left(\alpha\cdot\frac{LM}{d}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}
for some universal constant
\alpha>0
. The dependency on
\varepsilon
has been cancelled, and it is obviously not optimal!

The key trade-off of the construction comes from how to efficiently perturb the mass in each
\mathcal{B}_{r}(v)
. Recall that one requires

\int_{\mathcal{B}_{r}(v_{i})}\left(e^{-f_{i}(x)}-e^{-h_{0}(x)}\right)\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}x\approx\varepsilon

while keeping
r
small and simultaneously
f_{i}-h_{0}
small. We observe that, ignoring low order terms in
r
, the integral on the LHS is almost equal to
e^{-h_{0}(x)}e^{h_{0}(x)-f_{i}(x)}
. Imagine we are filling a mound with sand within a small circular area at an elevation of
e^{-h_{0}(x)}
. We aim for the amount of sand used to satisfy
e^{-h_{0}(x)}\cdot e^{h_{0}(x)-f_{i}(x)}\approx\varepsilon
, while ensuring the mound is not too steep – meaning the height difference
h_{0}(x)-f_{i}(x)
should be minimized. The most efficient approach is to raise the base elevation, i.e., maximize
e^{-h_{0}(x)}
, thereby reducing the required height increment.

Therefore, at a very high level, our construction for the base distribution
p_{\mu_{0}}\propto e^{-f_{\mu_{0}}}
in the lower bound proof is to first modify the Gaussian by creating a plateau in a ring, and then perturb the mass on the plateau. The construction is illustrated in
Footnote

2
. Of course, the plateau itself may affect the smoothness and the second moment. Nevertheless, we carefully pick the location, shape and the mass of the plateau, and are able to pack approximately
\left(\alpha\cdot\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}
disjoint balls with radius
r=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{L}}\right)
, which provides the desired optimal lower bound.

1.2.2
The upper bound

It is challenging to establish suitable isoperimetric inequalities directly for distributions solely satisfying
Assumption

1
and
2
due to the existence of point with extremely small density. However, we observe that: 1) the mass of the target distribution
\mu
with density
\propto e^{-f_{\mu}}
is concentrated in
\mathcal{B}_{R}
for a sufficiently large radius
R
; 2) the total mass of the region with extremely small density values inside
\mathcal{B}_{R}
is small.
Based on these observations, we construct another distribution
\pi
with density
\propto e^{-f_{\pi}}
which is close to
\mu
in total variation distance and is easier to sample from. Basically the distribution
\pi
is Gaussian outside
\mathcal{B}_{R}
and is the truncation (remove points with extremely small density) of
\mu
inside
\mathcal{B}_{R}
. The construction of
f_{\pi}
can be described in the following steps:

•

Step 1: Discretize
\mathcal{B}_{R}
into small cubes. Use the value of
f_{\mu}
at the center of each cube to estimate the values inside the cube. Use these approximations of each cube to calculate a rough estimation of the minimum value
\widehat{f}^{*}\approx f^{*}=\min_{x\in\mathcal{B}_{R}}f(x)
and the normalizing factor
\widehat{Z}_{\mu}\approx\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-f_{\mu}(x)}\differential x
.

•

Step 2: For
x\in\mathcal{B}_{R}
, as shown in
Figure

2
, if
f_{\mu}(x)-\widehat{f}^{*}
exceeds some threshold
h_{1}-\widehat{f}^{*}
, let
f_{\pi}(x)
be the smooth truncation of
f_{\mu}(x)
. Otherwise, let
f_{\pi}(x)=f_{\mu}(x)
. By doing so, we guarantee that
f_{\pi}(x)-\widehat{f}^{*}
is always bounded by
h_{2}-\widehat{f}^{*}
for some value
h_{2}
and extremely small density values in
\pi
is circumvented.

•

Step 3: For
x\not\in\mathcal{B}_{R}
, we define
f_{\pi}(x)
by replacing the original density
\frac{e^{-f_{\mu}(x)}}{\widehat{Z}_{\mu}}
with the density of a Gaussian
\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{M}{\varepsilon d}\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}\right)
.

Figure 2:
Construction of
f_{\pi}
.

By smoothing the above construction appropriately, we can prove the smoothness of
f_{\pi}
and prove a bound for the Poincaré constant of
\pi
. Besides, the value of
f_{\pi}
and
\gradient f_{\pi}
can be efficiently calculated given query access to
f_{\mu}
and
\gradient f_{\mu}
. Then we can apply the averaged Langevin algorithm in
[
BCE+22
]
to sample from
\pi
and the output distribution is also close to
\mu
in total variation distance.

Note that in Step 1, we use a grid-based approximation to find
\widehat{f}^{*}
, which is an optimization task. However, the comparison between
Theorems

4
and
6
shows that our sampling algorithm has lower complexity than solving the optimization problem itself. This is because the required precision for this optimization in our task is very low (see
Proposition

18
). We make a more thorough discussion on the theme “sampling versus optimization” in
Section

.

\ab\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
described in the following stochastic differential equation:

\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}X_{t}=-\gradient f(X_{t})\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}t+\sqrt{2}\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}B_{t},

(1)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a differentiable function and
\ab\{B_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is the standard Brownian motion. The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process (OU process) is a special case of
Equation

1
with
f(x)=\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}
. It is well known that the law of
X_{t}
always converges to the standard Gaussian distribution in the OU process.

The Poincaré inequality

We say a distribution
\mu
satisfies the Poincaré inequality with a constant
C>0
if for all
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\mathbf{E}\left[f^{2}\right]<\infty
, it holds that

\mathbf{Var}_{\mu}\left[f\right]\leq\frac{1}{C}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{\mu}\left[\|\gradient f\|^{2}\right].

(2)

We use
C_{\mathtt{PI}}
to denote the largest
C>0
so that (
2
) holds. We also call
C_{\mathtt{PI}}
the Poincaré constant of
\mu
.

3
The lower bound

In this section, we provide a sample complexity lower bound proof with regard to the error tolerance
\varepsilon
. In fact, we prove
Theorem

8
, which is a formal version of
Theorem

3
.

Recall that we use
\mathscr{D}_{L,M}
to denote the collection of distributions which are
L
-log-smooth and have second moment at most
M
.

Theorem 8
.

For any
L,M>0
satisfying
LM\geq d
and for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1/200)
,
d\geq 5
, if a sampling algorithm
\mathcal{A}
always terminates within

\frac{\varepsilon(d-2)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{8}\cdot\left(\frac{9}{256}\cdot\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{1}{\log\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}

queries on every input instance in
\mathscr{D}_{L,M}
, then there must exist some distribution
\mu\in\mathscr{D}_{L,M}
such that when the underlying instance is
\mu
, the distribution of
\mathcal{A}
’s output, denoted as
\tilde{\mu}
, is
\varepsilon
away from
\mu
in total variation distance, i.e.,
\mathtt{TV}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})\geq\varepsilon
.

3.1
The base instance

We first construct a base distribution
\mu_{0}
. Let
R=\left(\frac{M}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
, and let

\mathfrak{g}_{[\frac{R}{4},\frac{R}{2}]}(x)=q_{\mathtt{mol}}\left(\frac{\|x\|^{2}-\frac{R^{2}}{16}}{\frac{R^{2}}{4}-\frac{R^{2}}{16}}\right)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathfrak{g}_{[R,2R]}(x)=q_{\mathtt{mol}}\left(\frac{\|x\|^{2}-R^{2}}{4R^{2}-R^{2}}\right),

With constant function
h_{1}\equiv\log\left(\mathtt{vol}(\mathcal{B}_{3R})\right)+\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
and function
h_{0}(x)=\frac{d\|x\|^{2}}{2M}+\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{2\pi M}{d}
, define the function
f_{0}
as

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},f_{0}(x)=\begin{cases}h_{0}(x),&\|x\|\leq\frac{R}{4}\\
\mathfrak{g}_{[\frac{R}{4},\frac{R}{2}]}(x)\cdot h_{1}+\left(1-\mathfrak{g}_{[\frac{R}{4},\frac{R}{2}]}(x)\right)\cdot h_{0}(x),&\frac{R}{4}<\|x\|\leq\frac{R}{2}\\
h_{1},&\frac{R}{2}<\|x\|\leq R\\
\mathfrak{g}_{[R,2R]}(x)\cdot h_{0}(x)+\left(1-\mathfrak{g}_{[R,2R]}(x)\right)\cdot h_{1},&R<\|x\|\leq 2R\\
h_{0}(x),&\|x\|>2R\end{cases}.

Consider the distribution
\mu_{0}
with density
p_{\mu_{0}}\propto\exp\left(-f_{0}(x)\right)
and its normalizing factor
Z_{0}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\left(-f_{0}(x)\right)\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}x
.

Lemma 9
.

The normalizing constant
1-16\varepsilon\leq Z_{0}\leq 1+\varepsilon
.

Proof.

On one hand, from Markov’s inequality,

Z_{0}\geq\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\frac{R}{4}}}e^{-f_{0}(x)}\differential x=\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\frac{R}{4}}}e^{-h_{0}(x)}\differential x=1-\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{M}{d}\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}\right)}\left[\|X\|^{2}\geq\frac{R^{2}}{16}\right]\geq 1-16\varepsilon.

On the other hand,

Z_{0}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-h_{0}(x)}\differential x+\mathtt{vol}(\mathcal{B}_{2R})\cdot e^{-h_{1}}\leq 1+\varepsilon\cdot\frac{\mathtt{vol}(\mathcal{B}_{2R})}{\mathtt{vol}(\mathcal{B}_{3R})}\leq 1+\varepsilon.

∎

Lemma 10
.

The distribution
\mu_{0}
is
\mathcal{O}(L)
-log-smooth and has second moment
\mathcal{O}(M)
.

Proof.

We first calculate the second moment of
\mu_{0}
. We have

\mathbf{E}_{\mu_{0}}\left[\|X\|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{\mathbf{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{M}{d}\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}\right)}\left[\|X\|^{2}\right]+\mathtt{vol}(\mathcal{B}_{2R})\cdot e^{-h_{1}}\cdot 4R^{2}}{Z_{0}}\leq\frac{M+\varepsilon\cdot\frac{\mathtt{vol}(\mathcal{B}_{2R})}{\mathtt{vol}(\mathcal{B}_{3R})}\cdot 4R^{2}}{Z_{0}}\leq\frac{3M}{Z_{0}}\leq 6M,

where the last inequality is due to
Lemma

9
and the fact that
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{200}
.

For the smoothness, we only need to check
\|\gradient^{2}f_{0}(x)\|
for those
x
with
\|x\|\in(\frac{R}{4},\frac{R}{2}]
and
\|x\|\in(R,2R]
since clearly
f_{0}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

First, for
\|x\|\in(\frac{R}{4},\frac{R}{2}]
,

\gradient f_{0}(x)=\gradient\mathfrak{g}_{[\frac{R}{4},\frac{R}{2}]}(x)\cdot(h_{1}-h_{0}(x))+(1-\mathfrak{g}_{[\frac{R}{4},\frac{R}{2}]}(x))\cdot\gradient h_{0}(x),

and

\displaystyle\gradient^{2}f_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\gradient^{2}\mathfrak{g}_{[\frac{R}{4},\frac{R}{2}]}(x)\cdot(h_{1}-h_{0}(x))}_{\mbox{(a)}}-\underbrace{\left(\gradient\mathfrak{g}_{[\frac{R}{4},\frac{R}{2}]}(x)\cdot\gradient h_{0}(x)^{\top}+\gradient h_{0}(x)\cdot\gradient\mathfrak{g}_{[\frac{R}{4},\frac{R}{2}]}(x)^{\top}\right)}_{\mbox{(b)}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\left(1-\mathfrak{g}_{[\frac{R}{4},\frac{R}{2}]}(x)\right)\cdot\gradient^{2}h_{0}(x)}_{\mbox{(c)}}.

Recall that
LM\geq d
, so it is easy to get
0\preceq\mbox{(c)}\preceq L\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}
. Since

\gradient\mathfrak{g}_{[\frac{R}{4},\frac{R}{2}]}(x)=\frac{2x}{\frac{R^{2}}{4}-\frac{R^{2}}{16}}\cdot q_{\mathtt{mol}}^{\prime}\left(\frac{\|x\|^{2}-\frac{R^{2}}{16}}{\frac{R^{2}}{4}-\frac{R^{2}}{16}}\right),

we have

\mbox{(b)}=\frac{4d\cdot xx^{\top}}{M\left(\frac{R^{2}}{4}-\frac{R^{2}}{16}\right)}\cdot q_{\mathtt{mol}}^{\prime}\left(\frac{\|x\|^{2}-\frac{R^{2}}{16}}{\frac{R^{2}}{4}-\frac{R^{2}}{16}}\right).

Therefore,
-\mathcal{O}(L)\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}\preceq\mbox{(b)}\preceq\mathcal{O}(L)\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}
.
By direct calculation,

\gradient^{2}\mathfrak{g}_{[\frac{R}{4},\frac{R}{2}]}(x)=\frac{4xx^{\top}}{\left(\frac{R^{2}}{4}-\frac{R^{2}}{16}\right)^{2}}\cdot q^{\prime\prime}_{\mathtt{mol}}\left(\frac{\|x\|^{2}-\frac{R^{2}}{16}}{\frac{R^{2}}{4}-\frac{R^{2}}{16}}\right)+\frac{2\mathtt{Id}_{d}}{\frac{R^{2}}{4}-\frac{R^{2}}{16}}\cdot q^{\prime}_{\mathtt{mol}}\left(\frac{\|x\|^{2}-\frac{R^{2}}{16}}{\frac{R^{2}}{4}-\frac{R^{2}}{16}}\right)

and

\displaystyle\left|h_{1}-h_{0}(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\frac{d}{2}\log\left(9\pi R^{2}\right)-\log\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}+1\right)-\frac{d\|x\|^{2}}{2M}-\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{2\pi M}{d}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{9d}{2\varepsilon}-\log\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}+1\right)-\frac{d\|x\|^{2}}{2M}\right|

(3)

From Stirling’s formula, we know that for any
d>0
,

\log\sqrt{\pi d}+\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{d}{2e}\leq\log\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}+1\right)\leq\log\sqrt{\pi d}+\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{d}{2e}+1.

Back to
Equation

3
, we have

\displaystyle\left|h_{1}-h_{0}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{9e}{\varepsilon}-\frac{d\|x\|^{2}}{2M}-\log\sqrt{\pi d}\right|+1

\displaystyle\leq\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{9e}{\varepsilon}+\frac{dR^{2}}{8M}+\log\sqrt{\pi d}+1.

Since
LM\geq d
, we have
-\mathcal{O}(L)\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}\preceq\mbox{(a)}\preceq\mathcal{O}(L)\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}
.

For
\|x\|\in(R,2R]
,

\gradient^{2}f_{0}(x)=\gradient^{2}\mathfrak{g}_{[R,2R]}(x)(h_{0}(x)-h_{1})+2\gradient\mathfrak{g}_{[R,2R]}(x)\gradient h_{0}(x)^{\top}+\mathfrak{g}_{[R,2R]}(x)\cdot\gradient^{2}h_{0}(x).

The remaining calculations are similar. ∎

3.2
Perturb the base instance

We then construct instances via perturbing
\mu_{0}
. Let
r_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{d}{L}\log\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}}
,
r_{2}=\sqrt{2}r_{1}
. Let
h_{2}:=h_{1}-\gamma
, where
\gamma
is a value to be determined later.

Note that when
\varepsilon<1/200
, we have
4r_{2}\leq R
. For a point
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|v\|=\frac{3R}{4}
, let
\mathfrak{g}_{v}(x)=q_{\mathtt{mol}}\left(\frac{\|x-v\|^{2}-r_{1}^{2}}{r_{2}^{2}-r_{1}^{2}}\right)
and
f_{v}(x)=\mathfrak{g}_{v}(x)f_{0}(x)+(1-\mathfrak{g}_{v}(x))h_{2}
. This means that, outside the ball
\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}}(v)
,
f_{v}\equiv f_{0}
, and inside the ball
\mathcal{B}_{r_{1}}(v)
,
f_{v}\equiv h_{2}
. Define density of the distribution
\mu_{v}
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
p_{\mu_{v}}\propto e^{-f_{v}}
. Let
Z_{v}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-f_{v}(x)}\differential x
.

Lemma 11
.

There exists a
\gamma>0
such that the following holds at the same time:

•

\int_{\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}}(v)}\left(e^{-f_{v}(x)}-e^{-h_{1}}\right)\differential x=9\varepsilon
;

•

\frac{9\varepsilon e^{h_{1}}}{\mathtt{vol}(\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}})}\leq e^{\gamma}-1\leq\frac{9\varepsilon e^{h_{1}}}{\mathtt{vol}(\mathcal{B}_{r_{1}})}
.

•

Z_{0}\leq Z_{v}\leq 1+10\varepsilon
.

Before proving the lemma, let us examine the information it brings. Recall that
f_{0}(x)\equiv h_{1}
when
x\in\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}}(v)
and we would like to perturb
h_{1}
by amount of
\gamma
to obtain
f_{v}
so that there will be
\Theta(\varepsilon)
more probability mass in
\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}}(v)
. For fixed
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
, the lemma says that the order of
\gamma
is roughly proportional to
h_{1}
.

Proof of
Lemma

11
.

Consider the value
\int_{\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}}(v)}\left(e^{-f_{v}(x)}-e^{-h_{1}}\right)\differential x
. It is continuous and increasing in
\gamma
when
\gamma\geq 0
. When
\gamma=0
,
\int_{\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}}(v)}\left(e^{-f_{v}(x)}-e^{-h_{1}}\right)\differential x=0
. When
\gamma\to\infty
, this value goes to
\infty
. So we can find a
\gamma
such that
\int_{\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}}(v)}\left(e^{-f_{v}(x)}-e^{-h_{1}}\right)\differential x=9\varepsilon
holds exactly.

For such a
\gamma
, we have

Z_{v}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-f_{v}(x)}\differential x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-f_{0}(x)}\differential x+\int_{\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}}(v)}\left(e^{-f_{v}(x)}-e^{-h_{1}}\right)\differential x=Z_{0}+9\varepsilon\leq 1+10\varepsilon.

Also

Z_{v}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-f_{v}(x)}\differential x\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-f_{0}(x)}\differential x=Z_{0}.

It remains to calculate
e^{\gamma}
. We have that

\mathtt{vol}\left(\mathcal{B}_{r_{1}}(v)\right)\cdot e^{-h_{1}}\left(e^{\gamma}-1\right)\leq\int_{\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}}(v)}\left(e^{-f_{v}(x)}-e^{-h_{1}}\right)\differential x=9\varepsilon\leq\mathtt{vol}\left(\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}}(v)\right)\cdot e^{-h_{1}}\left(e^{\gamma}-1\right).

∎

Corollary 12
.

For our choice of
h_{1}
,
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
, it holds that

9\left(\frac{3R}{r_{2}}\right)^{d}\leq e^{\gamma}\leq 18\left(\frac{3R}{r_{1}}\right)^{d}.

Proof.

Recall that
e^{h_{1}}=\varepsilon^{-1}\mathtt{vol}\left(\mathcal{B}_{3R}\right)
and
\mathtt{vol}\left(\mathcal{B}_{r}\right)=\frac{\left(\pi R^{2}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}+1\right)}
. We have

e^{\gamma}-1\leq 9\left(\frac{3R}{r_{1}}\right)^{d}\mbox{ and }e^{\gamma}-1\geq 9\left(\frac{3R}{r_{2}}\right)^{d}.

Therefore

18\left(\frac{3R}{r_{1}}\right)^{d}\geq e^{\gamma}\geq 9\left(\frac{3R}{r_{2}}\right)^{d}.

∎

3.3
Properties of the perturbed distributions

For every
v
with
\|v\|=\frac{3R}{4}
, we first analyze the smoothness and second moment of the distribution
\mu_{v}
.

Lemma 13
.

For
\|v\|=\frac{3R}{4}
,
\mathbf{E}_{\mu_{v}}\left[\|X\|^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(M\right)
.

Proof.

Direct calculation gives

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mu_{v}}\left[\|X\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbf{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{M}{d}\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}\right)}\left[\|X\|^{2}\right]+\mathtt{vol}(B_{2R})\cdot e^{-h_{1}}\cdot 4R^{2}+\int_{\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}}(v)}\left(e^{-f_{v}(x)}-e^{-h_{1}}\right)\|X\|^{2}\differential x}{Z_{v}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3M+R^{2}\cdot 9\varepsilon}{Z_{v}}\leq 24M.

∎

Lemma 14
.

For
\|v\|=\frac{3R}{4}
, the function
f_{v}
is
\mathcal{O}(L)
-smooth.

Proof.

We only need to consider those
x\in\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}}(v)\setminus\mathcal{B}_{r_{1}}(v)
. For such
x
,
f_{0}(x)=h_{1}
. Therefore,

\gradient^{2}f_{v}(x)=\gamma\cdot\gradient^{2}g_{v}(x).

Note that

\gradient g_{v}(x)=\frac{2(x-v)}{r_{2}^{2}-r_{1}^{2}}\cdot q_{\mathtt{mol}}^{\prime}\left(\frac{\|x-v\|^{2}-r_{1}^{2}}{r_{2}^{2}-r_{1}^{2}}\right)

and

\gradient^{2}g_{v}(x)=\frac{4(x-v)(x-v)^{\top}}{\left(r_{2}^{2}-r_{1}^{2}\right)^{2}}\cdot q^{\prime\prime}_{\mathtt{mol}}\left(y=\frac{\|x-v\|^{2}-r_{1}^{2}}{r_{2}^{2}-r_{1}^{2}}\right)+\frac{2\mathtt{Id}_{d}}{r_{2}^{2}-r_{1}^{2}}\cdot q^{\prime}_{\mathtt{mol}}\left(\frac{\|x-v\|^{2}-r_{1}^{2}}{r_{2}^{2}-r_{1}^{2}}\right).

From
Corollary

12
,

\gamma\leq\log 18+\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{9LM}{\varepsilon d}-\frac{d}{2}\log\log\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}.

Therefore,
-\mathcal{O}(L)\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}\preceq\gradient^{2}f_{v}(x)\preceq\mathcal{O}(L)\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}
.
∎

We remark that the constants hidden in the
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
in the above two lemmas are universal constants and do not depend on
d
and
\varepsilon
.

Lemma 15
.

For
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\|v\|=\|u\|=\frac{3R}{4}
and
\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}}(u)\cap\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}}(v)=\varnothing
,
\mathtt{TV}\left(\mu_{u},\mu_{v}\right)>4\varepsilon
.

Proof.

By the definition of total variation distance,

\displaystyle\mathtt{TV}(\mu_{u},\mu_{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\frac{e^{-f_{u}(x)}}{Z_{u}}-\frac{e^{-f_{v}(x)}}{Z_{v}}\right|\differential x

(
Z_{u}=Z_{v}
)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2Z_{v}}\left(\int_{\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}}(u)}\left|e^{-f_{u}(x)}-e^{-h_{1}}\right|\differential x+\int_{\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}}(v)}\left|e^{-f_{v}(x)}-e^{-h_{1}}\right|\differential x\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{9\varepsilon}{Z_{v}}>4\varepsilon.

∎

3.4
The number of disjoint
\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}}(v)
’s

Lemma 16
.

Suppose
d\geq 5
. There exist
n=\frac{(d-1)\sqrt{d-2}}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{3R}{8\sqrt{2}r_{2}}\right)^{d-1}
vectors
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

•

for each

\|v_{i}\|=\frac{3R}{4}

•

for each

\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}}(v_{i})\cap\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}}(v_{j})=\varnothing

Proof.

Let
S
be the sphere
\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x\|=\frac{3R}{4}\right\}
. For two vectors
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\theta(x,y)
represent the angle between
x
and

. We first try to find
n

on

In contrast, suppose we can only find

Via

From
[

]

\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}\int_{0}^{\theta}\sin^{d-2}(\phi)\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\phi

Since
\sin\theta=\sqrt{1-\left(\ell^{\prime}\right)^{2}}\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}r_{2}}{\frac{3R}{4}}
, the ratio between the area of this larger cap and the sphere can be bounded by

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}\int_{0}^{\theta}\sin^{d-2}(\phi)\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\phi

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\ell^{\prime}(d-1)}\cdot\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}\cdot\left(\frac{2\sqrt{2}r_{2}}{\frac{3R}{4}}\right)^{d-1}

(

)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{9}{5\sqrt{\pi}(d-1)}\cdot\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}\cdot\left(\frac{8\sqrt{2}r_{2}}{3R}\right)^{d-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{9\sqrt{2}}{5\sqrt{\pi}}\cdot\frac{1}{(d-1)\sqrt{d-2}}\cdot\left(\frac{8\sqrt{2}r_{2}}{3R}\right)^{d-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{(d-1)\sqrt{d-2}}\left(\frac{8\sqrt{2}r_{2}}{3R}\right)^{d-1}.

This will lead to a conflict since

such caps cannot cover the sphere. Therefore, we can find

such

’s.

Furthermore, from
Lemma

,
\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}}(v_{i})\cap\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}}(v_{j})=\varnothing
.
∎

Proof of the lower bound

4
The upper bound

In this section we prove
Theorem

4
. We construct a distribution
\pi
that satisfies
\mathtt{TV}(\mu,\pi)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
, has smoothness close to that of
\mu
, is of bounded moment, and whose Poincaré constant is at least
\approx\left(\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}\right)^{-O(d)}
. Then we call known Langevin-based algorithm to sample from
\pi
, whose sample complexity is directly related to the Poincaré constant. Our strategy for the construction of
\pi
is as follows.

•

First, observe the following comparison result. Let
p_{1}(x)
and
p_{2}(x)
be the densities of two distributions supported on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
1/C\leq\frac{p_{1}(x)}{p_{2}(x)}\leq C
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then the ratio of their Poincaré constants is at least
C^{-\mathcal{O}(1)}
. Therefore, we only need to construct a distribution
\pi
with appropriate smoothness, whose density is pointwise close to a suitable Gaussian. The range of ratios in the densities that we can tolerate is of the order
\left(\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}\right)^{\mathcal{O}(d)}
.

•

Clearly the density
p_{\mu}(x)\propto e^{-f_{\mu}(x)}
of
\mu
does not satisfy our requirement due to the possible existence of certain regions with extremely small probability. The value of
f_{\mu}
may be very large in these regions. On the other hand, the measure of these regions under
\mu
is small, so we can
truncate

f_{\mu}
appropriately to ensure its value is well upper bounded without affecting the measure
\mu
much. We then use the truncated function to define the distribution
\pi
.

•

In order to truncate
f_{\mu}
appropriately, we need to estimate its minimum value
f^{*}:=\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{\mu}(x)
and the partition function
Z_{\mu}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\exp\left(-f_{\mu}(x)\right)\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}x
within a certain accuracy. To this end, we divide a compact set containing most mass of
\mu
into cubes and approximate
\mu
in each cube respectively using queries to
f_{\mu}
.

We will give the construction of
\pi
in
Section

4.1
and prove its properties in
Section

4.2
. Then we show how to estimate the key parameters in our construction in
Section

4.3
. Finally, we combine everything and prove
Theorem

4
in
Section

4.4
.

4.1
The construction of
\pi

The purpose of this section is to construct a distribution
\pi
whose density function is close to that of
\gamma\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{M}{\varepsilon d}\mathtt{Id}_{d}\right)

pointwise
and
\mathtt{TV}(\mu,\pi)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
. We assume the density of
\pi
is
proportional to

\exp\left(-f_{\pi}(x)\right)
. Let
f_{\gamma}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be the function
x\mapsto\frac{\varepsilon d\norm{x}^{2}}{2M}+\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{2\pi M}{d\varepsilon}
. Then the density of
\gamma
is
equal to

\exp\left(-f_{\gamma}(x)\right)
.

We use
Z_{\pi}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\left(-f_{\pi}(x)\right)\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}x
and
Z_{\mu}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\left(-f_{\mu}(x)\right)\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}x
to denote the two normalizing factors. Then the density of
\pi
and
\mu
are

p_{\pi}(x)=\exp\left(-f_{\pi}(x)\right)/Z_{\pi}\mbox{ and }p_{\mu}(x)=\exp\left(-f_{\mu}(x)\right)/Z_{\mu}

respectively.

Note that the second moment of a random variable
X
with law
\mu
is at most
M
. By Markov’s inequality, for
R=\sqrt{\frac{32M}{\varepsilon}}
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\norm{X}^{2}>R^{2}\right]\leq\frac{\mathbf{E}\left[\norm{X}^{2}\right]}{R^{2}}\leq\frac{M}{R^{2}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{32},

(4)

meaning outside a ball of radius
\Theta(R)
, the mass of
\mu
is
O(\varepsilon)
. We let
f_{\pi}(x)=f_{\gamma}(x)-\log\varepsilon
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{2R}
. We will then construct a function
f_{\pi}^{\leq 2R}
with support
\mathcal{B}_{2R}
and define

f_{\pi}(x)=(1-\mathfrak{g}_{[R,2R]}(x))\cdot f_{\pi}^{\leq 2R}(x)+\mathfrak{g}_{[R,2R]}(x)\cdot\left(f_{\gamma}(x)-\log\varepsilon\right),

where
\mathfrak{g}_{[R,2R]}:=q_{\mathtt{mol}}\left(\frac{\norm{x}^{2}-R^{2}}{(2R)^{2}-R^{2}}\right)
is the smooth function interpolating
f_{\pi}^{\leq 2R}
and
f_{\gamma}-\log\varepsilon
in the region
\norm{x}\in[R,2R]
.

As discussed before, for
x\in\mathcal{B}_{R}
, ideally

should be close to
p_{\mu}(x)=\exp\left(-f_{\mu}(x)\right)/Z_{\mu}
with those points of extremely small probability smoothly truncated. As a result, we first assume that we can find an approximation of
Z_{\mu}
, denoted as
\widehat{Z}_{\mu}
. In general calculating a good approximation for

is computationally equivalent to sampling from

. However, as our target is a bound for the Poincaré constant of order exponential in

, our requirement for the accuracy of the approximation is very loose. We also assume an approximation
\widehat{f}^{*}
of the minimum
f^{*}:=\inf_{x\in\mathcal{B}_{2R}}f_{\mu}(x)
. In fact, we will prove the following proposition in
Section

4.3
.

Proposition 18
.

Within
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{LM}{\varepsilon d}\right)^{d}
queries to

, one can find

•

a number
\widehat{Z}_{\mu}
satisfying
\frac{1}{2}e^{-d}\leq\frac{\widehat{Z}_{\mu}}{Z_{\mu}}\leq 1
, and

•

a number
\widehat{f}^{*}
satisfying
f^{*}\leq\widehat{f}^{*}\leq f^{*}+d
.

Then we turn to truncate the small value of
\exp(-f_\mu(x))
or equivalently the large value of
f_{\mu}(x)
in
\mathcal{B}_{2R}
. Define two constants

h_{1}:=\widehat{f}^{*}+\log\mathtt{vol}(\mathcal{B}_{2R})+\frac{d}{2}\log L+\log\frac{4}{\varepsilon},\;h_{2}:=h_{1}+\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}.

We remark that

is our threshold for the truncation. The term

is nonnegative and therefore

. In order to keep the truncated function smooth, we define a

.

Define the interpolation function
\mathfrak{g}_{[h_{1},h2]}(x):=q_{\mathtt{mol}}\left(\frac{h_{2}-f_{\mu}(x)}{h_{2}-h_{1}}\right)
. We define

\overline{f_{\pi}^{\leq 2R}}(x):=\mathfrak{g}_{[h_{1},h_{2}]}(x)\cdot f_{\mu}(x)+(1-\mathfrak{g}_{[h_{1},h_{2}]}(x))\cdot h_{2}

In other words, for those

\overline{f_{\pi}^{\leq 2R}}(x)=f_{\mu}(x)
; for those

with
f_{\mu}(x)\geq h_{2}

\overline{f_{\pi}^{\leq 2R}}(x)=h_{2}
; for those

with

\overline{f_{\pi}^{\leq 2R}(x)}
is smoothly interpolated between

and

. The function

is illustrated in

Finally, we

, namely that for every

, let

f^{\leq 2R}_{\pi}(x):=\overline{f^{\leq 2R}_{\pi}}(x)+\log\widehat{Z}_{\mu}.

\displaystyle\leq f_{\mu}(x^{*})+\gradient f_{\mu}(x^{*})\top(y-x^{*})+\frac{L}{2}\norm{y-x^*}^{2}

\displaystyle=f_{\mu}(x^{*})+\frac{L}{2}\norm{y-x^*}^{2}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=Z_{\mu}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\left(-f_{\mu}(y)\right)\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}y

4.2.3
The Poincaré constant of
\pi

In this section we bound the Poincaré constant of
\pi
. Recall that the density
p_{\gamma}
of
\gamma\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{M}{\varepsilon d}\mathtt{Id}_{d}\right)
is
p_{\gamma}(x)=\exp\left(-f_{\gamma}(x)\right)
where
f_{\gamma}(x)=\frac{\varepsilon d\norm{x}^{2}}{2M}+\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{2\pi M}{d\varepsilon}
. We will show that
p_{\pi}
is close to

pointwise.

Lemma 23
.

Assume

. For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\left|f_{\gamma}(x)-f_{\pi}(x)\right|\leq\mathcal{O}\left(d\log\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}\right)
.

Proof.

Outside
\mathcal{B}_{2R}
, we have

\left|f_{\pi}(x)-f_{\gamma}(x)\right|=\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{O}\left(d\log\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}\right).

For
x\in\mathcal{B}_{2R}
, or equivalently
\norm{x}^{2}\leq\frac{128M}{\varepsilon}
,

\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{2\pi M}{d\varepsilon}\leq f_{\gamma}(x)\leq 64d+\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{2\pi M}{d\varepsilon}.

It remains to bound
f_{\pi}(x)
inside
\mathcal{B}_{2R}
. Note that
f_{\pi}(x)=f^{\leq 2R}_{\pi}(x)
for
x\in\mathcal{B}_{R}
and
f_{\pi}(x)
is an interpolation of
f^{\leq 2R}_{\pi}(x)
and
f_{\gamma}(x)-\log\varepsilon
for
x\in\mathcal{B}_{2R}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{R}
, we only need to bound
f^{\leq 2R}_{\pi}
.

Recall that
{f^{\leq 2R}_{\pi}(x)}={\overline{f^{\leq 2R}_{\pi}}(x)+\log\widehat{Z}_{\mu}}={\overline{f^{\leq 2R}_{\pi}}(x)+\log Z_{\mu}}+{\log\frac{\widehat{Z}_{\mu}}{Z_{\mu}}}
. By
Proposition

18
,
-d-1\leq\log\frac{\widehat{Z}_{\mu}}{Z_{\mu}}\leq 0
.
By our construction, for all
x\in\mathcal{B}_{2R}
,

f^{*}+\log Z_{\mu}\leq\overline{f^{\leq 2R}_{\pi}}(x)+\log Z_{\mu}\leq h_{2}+\log Z_{\mu}.

From
Lemma

19
,
f^{*}+\log Z_{\mu}\geq\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{2\pi}{L}
. On the other hand,
h_{2}+\log Z_{\mu}\leq f^{*}+\log Z_{\mu}+d+\log\mathtt{vol}(\mathcal{B}_{2R})+\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{L^{2}M}{d\varepsilon}+\log\frac{4}{\varepsilon}
. Since

\mathtt{vol}(\mathcal{B}_{R})\cdot e^{-f^{*}-\log Z_{\mu}}=\int_{\mathcal{B}_{R}}e^{-f^{*}-\log Z_{\mu}}\differential x\geq\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\mu}\left[X\in\mathcal{B}_{R}\right]\geq 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{32},

we have
f^{*}+\log Z_{\mu}\leq\log\mathtt{vol}(\mathcal{B}_{R})+1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\overline{f^{\leq 2R}_{\pi}}(x)+\log Z_{\mu}\leq h_{2}+\log Z_{\mu}

\displaystyle\leq\log\mathtt{vol}(\mathcal{B}_{R})+\log\mathtt{vol}(\mathcal{B}_{2R})+d+1+\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{L^{2}M}{d\varepsilon}+\log\frac{4}{\varepsilon}

(
Proposition

52
)

\displaystyle\leq\log\frac{4}{\varepsilon}+d+1+\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{L^{2}M}{d\varepsilon}+\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{64e\pi M}{d\varepsilon}+\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{4\cdot 64e\pi M}{d\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\log\frac{4}{\varepsilon}+d+1+d\log\frac{8LM}{d\varepsilon}+\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{4\cdot 64e^{2}\pi^{2}M}{d\varepsilon}.

Combining the above calculations, for
x\in\mathcal{B}_{2R}
,

\displaystyle f_{\pi}(x)-f_{\gamma}(x)

\displaystyle\leq f^{\leq 2R}_{\pi}(x)-f_{\gamma}(x)+\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\overline{f^{\leq 2R}_{\pi}}(x)+\log Z_{\mu}-f_{\gamma}(x)+\log\frac{\widehat{Z}_{\mu}}{Z_{\mu}}+\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\log\frac{4}{\varepsilon}+d+1+d\log\frac{8LM}{d\varepsilon}+\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{4\cdot 64e^{2}\pi^{2}M}{d\varepsilon}-\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{2\pi M}{d\varepsilon}+\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(d\log\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}\right)

and

\displaystyle f_{\pi}(x)-f_{\gamma}(x)

\displaystyle\geq f^{\leq 2R}_{\pi}(x)-f_{\gamma}(x)

\displaystyle=\overline{f^{\leq 2R}_{\pi}}(x)+\log Z_{\mu}-f_{\gamma}(x)+\log\frac{\widehat{Z}_{\mu}}{Z_{\mu}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{2\pi}{L}-64d-\frac{d}{2}\log\frac{2\pi M}{d\varepsilon}-d-1

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{O}\left(d\log\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}\right).

∎

Since
\left|\log p_{\pi}(x)-\log p_{\gamma}(x)\right|=\left|f_{\gamma}(x)-f_{\pi}(x)-\log Z_{\pi}\right|\leq\left|f_{\gamma}(x)-f_{\pi}(x)\right|+\left|\log Z_{\pi}\right|
, by

, we have the following corollary.

.

For every

,

.

Then we come to the bound for the Poincaré constant of

Lemma 25
.

C_{\mathtt{PI}}(\pi)=\frac{2d\varepsilon}{M}\cdot\left(\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}\right)^{-\mathcal{O}(d)}
.

Proof.

By definition,

. For each

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{E}_{\pi}\left[\|\gradient h\|^{2}\right]}{\mathbf{Var}_{\pi}\left[h\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\gradient h(x)\|^{2}p_{\pi}(x)\differential x}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(h(x)-h(y)\right)^{2}p_{\pi}(x)p_{\pi}(y)\differential x\differential y}

(

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}\right)^{\mathcal{O}(d)}\cdot\frac{2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\gradient h(x)\|^{2}p_{\gamma}(x)\differential x}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(h(x)-h(y)\right)^{2}p_{\gamma}(x)p_{\gamma}(y)\differential x\differential y}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}\right)^{\mathcal{O}(d)}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{E}_{\gamma}\left[\|\gradient h\|^{2}\right]}{\mathbf{Var}_{\gamma}\left[h\right]}.

Since
C_{\mathtt{PI}}(\gamma)=\inf_{h\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathbf{E}_{\gamma}\left[\|\gradient h\|^{2}\right]}{\mathbf{Var}_{\gamma}\left[h\right]}=\frac{2d\varepsilon}{M}

[

, we know that

∎

4.2.4

\mathbb{P}_{Y\sim\mathcal{N}_{1}}\left[Y\in e^{-t}\cdot\overline{\mathcal{B}_{\frac{r}{2}}(u)}\right]\geq 1-\frac{10d}{\|u\|^{2}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{Y\sim\mathcal{N}_{2}}\left[Y\in e^{-t}\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\frac{2r}{5}}(u)\right]\geq 1-\frac{10d}{\|u\|^{2}}
.

Proof.

We first prove
\mathbb{P}_{Y\sim\mathcal{N}_{1}}\left[Y\in e^{-t}\cdot\overline{\mathcal{B}_{\frac{r}{2}}(u)}\right]\geq 1-\frac{10d}{\|u\|^{2}}
. By definition,

e^{-t}\cdot\overline{\mathcal{B}_{\frac{r}{2}}(u)}=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|y-e^{-t}u\|>0.5e^{-t}\|u\|\right\}.

Since

\|y-e^{-t}u\|\geq\left(e^{-t}-\frac{e^{-3t}}{2}\right)\|u\|-\norm{y - \frac{e^{-3t}}{2} u },

we have
\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ \norm{y - \frac{e^{-3t}}{2} u }<\left(0.5e^{-t}-\frac{e^{-3t}}{2}\right)\|u\|\right\}\subseteq e^{-t}\cdot\overline{\mathcal{B}_{\frac{r}{2}}(u)}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{Y\sim\mathcal{N}_{1}}\left[Y\in e^{-t}\cdot\overline{\mathcal{B}_{\frac{r}{2}}(u)}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}_{Y\sim\mathcal{N}_{1}}\left[\norm{Y - \frac{e^{-3t}}{2} u }^{2}<\left(0.5e^{-t}-\frac{e^{-3t}}{2}\right)^{2}\|u\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{d\cdot\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\left(0.5e^{-t}-\frac{e^{-3t}}{2}\right)^{2}\|u\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{10d}{\|u\|^{2}}

where the second inequality follows from the Markov’s inequality and the last inequality is due to
e^{-2t}<0.1
.

We then prove that
\mathbb{P}_{Y\sim\mathcal{N}_{2}}\left[Y\in e^{-t}\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\frac{2r}{5}}(u)\right]\geq 1-\frac{10d}{\|u\|^{2}}
. By definition,

e^{-t}\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\frac{2r}{5}}(u)=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|y-e^{-t}u\|<0.4e^{-t}\|u\|\right\}.

Since

\|y-e^{-t}u\|\leq\norm{y - e^{-t}\tp{1-\frac{e^{-2t}}{2}}u}+\frac{e^{-3t}}{2}\|u\|,

we have
\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ \norm{ y - e^{-t}\tp{1-\frac{e^{-2t}}{2}}u}\leq\left(0.4e^{-t}-\frac{e^{-3t}}{2}\right)\|u\|\right\}\subseteq e^{-t}\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\frac{2r}{5}}(u)
. Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{Y\sim\mathcal{N}_{2}}\left[Y\in e^{-t}\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\frac{2r}{5}}(u)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}_{Y\sim\mathcal{N}_{2}}\left[\norm{Y - e^{-t}\tp{1-\frac{e^{-2t}}{2}}}^{2}\leq\left(0.4e^{-t}-\frac{e^{-3t}}{2}\right)^{2}\|u\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{d\cdot\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\left(0.4e^{-t}-\frac{e^{-3t}}{2}\right)^{2}\|u\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{10d}{\|u\|^{2}}.

∎

Now we give a proof of
Lemma

41
.

Proof of
Lemma

41
.

Recall that the distribution
\gamma_{t}
is only supported on
e^{-t}\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\frac{r}{2}}(u)
. The choice of

satisfies

\mathbb{P}_{Y\sim\nu_{t}}\left[Y\in e^{-t}\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\frac{r}{2}}(u)\right]=(1-\delta_{t})\cdot\mathbb{P}_{Y\sim N_{1}}\left[Y\in e^{-t}\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\frac{r}{2}}(u)\right]+\delta_{t}.

(14)

From

and

, we have

\mathbb{P}_{Y\sim\nu_{t}}\left[Y\in e^{-t}\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\frac{r}{2}}(u)\right]\geq\frac{1}{Z_{t}}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{Y\sim\mathcal{N}_{2}}\left[Y\in e^{-t}\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\frac{2r}{5}}(u)\right]\geq\frac{1-\frac{10d}{\|u\|^{2}}}{2}>0.4,

5.2
Smoothness for the mixture of Gaussian distributions

The analysis in the previous section indicates that
an
\mathcal{O}(1)
-log-smooth initial distribution does not guarantee
\mathcal{O}(1)
-smoothness after the process evolves.
In this subsection, we consider a family of classic multi-modal distributions, the mixture of Gaussian distributions. Mixture of Gaussians appear to be similar to stitched Gaussians, but the analysis below will reveal fundamental differences in their smoothness behaviors.

We study the cases where each Gaussian component has a covariance
\Sigma_{i}\succeq\Omega(1)\mathtt{Id}_{d}
.
We first considered the simple case with only two components, and then extend the analysis to mixtures with multiple components.

5.2.1
Mixture of Gaussian distributions and its evolution during the OU process

Consider
m
Gaussian distributions over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, each with mean
u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and covariance
\Sigma_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Define function
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
as
f_{i}(x)=\frac{1}{2}(x-u_{i})^{\top}\Sigma_{i}^{-1}(x-u_{i})+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\left(2\pi\right)^{d}\left|\Sigma_{i}\right|\right)
for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then
e^{-f_{i}}
is the density function of the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}\left(u_{i},\Sigma_{i}\right)
. Suppose
\mu
is the mixture of Gaussian with density
p_{\mu}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}e^{-f_{i}(x)}
, where
w_{i}\in(0,1)
is the weight of the
i
-th component and
\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}=1
. Then
-\gradient\log p_{\mu}(x)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\gradient f_{i}(x)\cdot e^{-f_{i}(x)}}{p_{\mu}(x)}
and

\displaystyle-\gradient^{2}\log p_{\mu}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\gradient^{2}f_{i}(x)\cdot e^{-f_{i}(x)}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\gradient f_{i}(x)\gradient f_{i}(x)^{\top}\cdot e^{-f_{i}(x)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}e^{-f_{i}(x)}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\gradient f_{i}(x)\cdot e^{-f_{i}(x)}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\gradient f_{i}(x)\cdot e^{-f_{i}(x)}\right)^{\top}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}e^{-f_{i}(x)}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{i}w_{j}\left(\gradient f_{i}(x)\gradient f_{j}(x)^{\top}-\frac{1}{2}\gradient f_{i}(x)\gradient f_{i}(x)^{\top}-\frac{1}{2}\gradient f_{j}(x)\gradient f_{j}(x)^{\top}\right)e^{-f_{i}(x)-f_{j}(x)}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}e^{-f_{i}(x)}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\gradient^{2}f_{i}(x)\cdot e^{-f_{i}(x)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}e^{-f_{i}(x)}}

\displaystyle=-\underbrace{\frac{\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq m}w_{i}w_{j}\left(\gradient f_{i}(x)-\gradient f_{j}(x)\right)\left(\gradient f_{i}(x)-\gradient f_{j}(x)\right)^{\top}e^{-f_{i}(x)-f_{j}(x)}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}e^{-f_{i}(x)}\right)^{2}}}_{(A)}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\gradient^{2}f_{i}(x)\cdot e^{-f_{i}(x)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}e^{-f_{i}(x)}}}_{(B)}.

(15)

From
Equation

15
,
\gradient^{2}\log p_{\mu}
is determined by two parts: the weighted mixture of the Hessian of each component (term
(B)
), and the interaction between different components (term
(A)
).

Then we see how this distribution evolves during the OU process. Recall that the trajectory of the OU process is given by
X_{t}=e^{-t}X_{0}+\sqrt{2}\cdot e^{-t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{s}\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}B_{s}
and
\sqrt{2}\cdot e^{-t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{s}\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}B_{s}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,(1-e^{-2t})I_{d}\right)
. If
X_{0}
is drawn from some Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(u,\Sigma)
, at time
t
, the distribution of
X_{t}
will be
\mathcal{N}\left(e^{-t}u,e^{-2t}\Sigma+(1-e^{-2t})\mathtt{Id}_{d}\right)
. Hence, if the initial distribution is
\mu
, i.e., the weighted mixture of
\ab\{\mathcal{N}(u_{i},\Sigma_{i})\}_{i\in[m]}
, then the distribution of
X_{t}
will be the mixture of
\ab\{\mathcal{N}\left(e^{-t}u_{i},e^{-2t}\Sigma_{i}+(1-e^{-2t})\mathtt{Id}_{d}\right)\}_{i\in[m]}
with the same weights. Let
\Sigma_{i}^{(t)}=e^{-2t}\Sigma_{i}+(1-e^{-2t})\mathtt{Id}_{d}
. That is to say, this distribution is still a mixture of Gaussians and has density
p_{t}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}e^{-f^{(t)}_{i}(x)}
, where
e^{-f^{(t)}_{i}(x)}
is the density function of
\mathcal{N}\left(e^{-t}u_{i},\Sigma_{i}^{(t)}\right)
.

5.2.2
Mixture of two Gaussians

We first see the case when
m=2
.

Gaussians with the same covariance: distance of means determines

When the covariances are the same and are bounded, the rules are simple and straightforward. The smoothness of the mixture distribution is totally determined by the distance of centers.

Lemma 43
.

When
m=2
and
\Sigma_{1}=\Sigma_{2}=\Sigma
for some matrix
\Sigma\succeq\Omega(1)\mathtt{Id}_{d}
, we have

-\mathcal{O}\left(\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|^{2}\right)\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}\preceq-\gradient^{2}\log p_{\mu}(x)\preceq\Sigma^{-1},

and

-\mathcal{O}\left(e^{-2t}\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|^{2}\right)\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}\preceq-\gradient^{2}\log p_{t}(x)\preceq\mathcal{O}(1)\mathtt{Id}_{d}

for any
t>0
.

On the other hand, for any
L=o(\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|^{2})
,
-\gradient^{2}\log p_{\mu}(x)\not\succeq-L\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}
and
-\gradient^{2}\log p_{t}(x)\not\succeq-e^{-2t}L\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}
.

Proof.

When
m=2
and
\Sigma_{1}=\Sigma_{2}=\Sigma
for some matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, we have
\gradient f_{1}(x)=\Sigma^{-1}(x-u_{1})
,
\gradient f_{2}(x)=\Sigma^{-1}(x-u_{2})
and
\gradient^{2}f_{1}(x)=\gradient^{2}f_{2}(x)=\Sigma^{-1}
.
According to
Equation

15
,

\displaystyle-\gradient^{2}\log p_{\mu}(x)=-\frac{w_{1}w_{2}\cdot\Sigma^{-1}(u_{1}-u_{2})(u_{1}-u_{2})^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\cdot e^{-f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(x)}}{\left(w_{1}e^{-f_{1}(x)}+w_{2}e^{-f_{2}(x)}\right)^{2}}+\Sigma^{-1}.

From the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality,

0\leq\frac{w_{1}w_{2}\cdot e^{-f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(x)}}{\left(w_{1}e^{-f_{1}(x)}+w_{2}e^{-f_{2}(x)}\right)^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{4}.

Therefore

-\frac{1}{4}\Sigma^{-1}(u_{1}-u_{2})(u_{1}-u_{2})^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}+\Sigma^{-1}\preceq-\gradient^{2}\log p_{\mu}(x)\preceq\Sigma^{-1}.

It can be easily prove that
\Sigma^{-2}
is also upper bounded by
\mathcal{O}(1)\mathtt{Id}_{d}
. The result then follows from the fact that

\Sigma^{-1}(u_{1}-u_{2})(u_{1}-u_{2})^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\preceq\|\Sigma^{-1}(u_{1}-u_{2})\|^{2}\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}=(u_{1}-u_{2})^{\top}\Sigma^{-2}(u_{1}-u_{2})\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}\preceq\mathcal{O}(\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|^{2})\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}.

Let
\Sigma^{(t)}=e^{-2t}\Sigma+(1-e^{-2t})\mathtt{Id}_{d}
. For the distributions during the OU process, repeating the above calculations, we can get that

-\frac{e^{-2t}}{4}\left(\Sigma^{(t)}\right)^{-1}(u_{1}-u_{2})(u_{1}-u_{2})^{\top}\left(\Sigma^{(t)}\right)^{-1}+\left(\Sigma^{(t)}\right)^{-1}\preceq-\gradient^{2}\log p_{t}(x)\preceq\left(\Sigma^{(t)}\right)^{-1}.

By the definition of
\Sigma^{(t)}
,
\left(\Sigma^{(t)}\right)^{-1}\preceq\frac{1}{1-ce^{-2t}}\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}\preceq\mathcal{O}(1)\mathtt{Id}_{d}
for some universal constant
c<1
.
Therefore, at time
t
,
-\gradient^{2}\log p_{t}\succeq-\mathcal{O}(\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|^{2})\mathtt{Id}_{d}
.
∎

Gaussians with different covariances

When the two components have different covariance matrices, the smoothness parameter can be
\mathcal{O}(d)
even when
\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|
is small. Here is an example.

Lemma 44
.

Let
p_{\mu}(x)=\frac{1}{2}e^{-f_{1}(x)}+\frac{1}{2}e^{-f_{2}(x)}
with
f_{1}(x)=\|x-u_{1}\|^{2}+\frac{d}{2}\log\pi
and
f_{2}(x)=\frac{\|x-u_{2}\|^{2}}{2}+\frac{d}{2}\log 2\pi
for arbitrary vectors
u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then there exists
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\|\gradient^{2}\log p_{\mu}(x)\|_{\mathtt{op}}=\Omega{d\log 2+2\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|^{2}}
.

Proof.

From
Equation

15
,

\displaystyle-\gradient^{2}\log p_{\mu}(x)=\underbrace{\frac{2e^{-f_{1}(x)}\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}+e^{-f_{2}(x)}\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}}{e^{-f_{1}(x)}+e^{-f_{2}(x)}}}_{(a)}-\underbrace{\frac{e^{-f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(x)}\cdot\left(x-(2u_{1}-u_{2})\right)\left(x-(2u_{1}-u_{2})\right)^{\top}}{\left(e^{-f_{1}(x)}+e^{-f_{2}(x)}\right)^{2}}}_{(b)}.

For
(a)
, it is easy to know that
\mathtt{Id}_{d}\preceq(a)\preceq 2\mathtt{Id}_{d}
. For
(b)
, we will find a specific
x
such that
(b)
is large.
When
x
satisfies
\|x-(2u_{1}-u_{2})\|^{2}=d\log 2+2\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|^{2}
, by direct calculation, we have

\displaystyle f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|x\|^{2}-(2u_{1}-u_{2})^{\top}x-x^{\top}(2u_{1}-u_{2})+2\|u_{1}\|^{2}-\|u_{2}\|^{2}-d\log 2\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|x-(2u_{1}-u_{2})\|^{2}-4\|u_{1}\|^{2}-\|u_{2}\|^{2}+2u_{1}^{\top}u_{2}+2u_{2}^{\top}u_{1}+2\|u_{1}\|^{2}-\|u_{2}\|^{2}-d\log 2\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|x-(2u_{1}-u_{2})\|^{2}-d\log 2-2\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=0.

Therefore,
0\preceq(b)=\frac{1}{4}\cdot\left(x-(2u_{1}-u_{2})\right)\left(x-(2u_{1}-u_{2})\right)^{\top}
and the optimal upper bound for
(b)
is
\frac{\|x-(2u_{1}-u_{2})\|^{2}}{4}\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}=\left(d\log 2+2\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|^{2}\right)\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}
.
∎

5.2.3
Mixture of multiple Gaussians

Things are more complicated and subtle for the mixture of multiple Gaussian distributions, even when their covariance matrices are the same. When they have the same covariance matrix, via similar arguments in
Lemma

43
, we can prove that
-\mathcal{O}\left(\max_{i,j\in[m]}\|u_{i}-u_{j}\|^{2}\right)\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}\preceq-\gradient^{2}\log p_{\mu}(x)\preceq\mathcal{O}(1)\mathtt{Id}_{d}
and
-\mathcal{O}\left(\max_{i,j\in[m]}e^{-2t}\|u_{i}-u_{j}\|^{2}\right)\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}\preceq-\gradient^{2}\log p_{t}(x)\preceq\mathcal{O}(1)\mathtt{Id}_{d}
. However, these bounds may not be tight. A large distance between the centers of the components does not necessarily imply a lack of smoothness. The potential function may still be and maintain
\mathcal{O}(1)
-smooth during the OU process in this case. We give an example in this section.

Let
J\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a symmetric and positive definite matrix and
h
be an arbitrary vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Consider the distribution
\mu
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with density

p_{\mu}(x)\propto\sum_{\sigma\in\ab\{\pm 1\}^{d}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}J^{-1}x+\left(J^{-1}h+\sigma\right)^{\top}x\right\}.

(16)

This distribution is induced when applying the Hubbard-Stratonovich transform to the Ising model (see Appendix E in
[
KLR22
]
).
Note that each
\sigma\in\ab\{\pm 1\}^{d}
corresponds to a Gaussian component
\mathcal{N}(J\sigma+h,J^{-1})
. For a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
x(i)
denote its
i
-th component for any
i\in[d]
. The following lemma shows that this distribution is log-smooth and even strongly log-concave if
J
is within a moderate range.

Lemma 45
.

If
\delta\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}\preceq J\preceq(1-\delta)\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}
for some
\delta\in(0,1/2)
, the distribution defined in
Equation

16
satisfies
\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}\preceq-\gradient^{2}\log p_{\mu}(x)\preceq\frac{1}{\delta}\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

By the definition in
Equation

16
,

\displaystyle p_{\mu}(x)

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{\sigma\in\ab\{\pm 1\}^{d}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}J^{-1}x+\left(J^{-1}h+\sigma\right)^{\top}x\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}J^{-1}x+h^{\top}J^{-1}x\right\}\cdot\sum_{\sigma\in\ab\{\pm 1\}^{d}}\exp\left\{\sigma^{\top}x\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}J^{-1}x+h^{\top}J^{-1}x\right\}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(e^{x(i)}+e^{-x(i)}\right).

Therefore,
-\gradient\log p_{\mu}(x)=J^{-1}x-J^{-1}h-z_{x}
where
z_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
z_{x}(i)=\frac{e^{x(i)}-e^{-x(i)}}{e^{x(i)}+e^{-x(i)}}
for each
i\in[d]
. Consequently,
-\gradient^{2}\log p_{\mu}(x)=J^{-1}-A_{x}
where
A_{x}
is a diagonal matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
A_{x}(i,i)=1-\left(\frac{e^{x(i)}-e^{-x(i)}}{e^{x(i)}+e^{-x(i)}}\right)^{2}
for each
i\in[d]
.

Since
\delta\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}\preceq J\preceq(1-\delta)\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}
, for any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

v^{\top}J^{-1}v=\left(J^{-\frac{1}{2}}v\right)^{\top}J^{-\frac{1}{2}}v\succeq\frac{1}{1-\delta}v^{\top}\cdot J^{-\frac{1}{2}}JJ^{-\frac{1}{2}}\cdot v=\frac{1}{1-\delta}v^{\top}v,

and

v^{\top}J^{-1}v=\left(J^{-\frac{1}{2}}v\right)^{\top}J^{-\frac{1}{2}}v\preceq\frac{1}{\delta}v^{\top}\cdot J^{-\frac{1}{2}}JJ^{-\frac{1}{2}}\cdot v=\frac{1}{\delta}v^{\top}v.

Thus
\frac{1}{1-\delta}\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}\preceq J^{-1}\preceq\frac{1}{\delta}\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}
. For the matrix
A_{x}
, we know
0\preceq A_{x}\preceq\mathtt{Id}_{d}
. Combining these together, we can get the desired result.

∎

For two distributions
\pi
and
\nu
with density
p_{\pi}
and
p_{\nu}
respectively, define
\pi*\nu
as the distribution with density
p_{\pi*\nu}(x)\propto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{\pi}(y)\cdot p_{\nu}(x-y)\differential y
.
When the initial distribution is both strongly log-concave and log-smooth, we can show that the
-\gradient^{2}\log p_{t}
is also bounded via the following lemma and its corollary.

Lemma 46

(Lemma 28 in
[
LPSR21
]
)
.

Suppose
\pi
is a probability density function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
M_{1}^{-1}\preceq-\gradient^{2}\log p_{\pi}(x)\preceq M_{2}^{-1}
for some
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Let
\nu
be the density function of
\mathcal{N}(0,M)
. Then

(M_{1}+M)^{-1}\preceq-\gradient^{2}\log p_{\pi*\nu}(x)\preceq(M_{2}+M)^{-1}.

Corollary 47
.

During the OU process with starting distribution defined in
Equation

16
,

\frac{1}{1+\frac{1-2\delta}{\delta}e^{-2t}}\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}\preceq-\gradient^{2}\log p_{t}(x)\preceq\frac{1}{1-(1-\delta)e^{-2t}}\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}

for any
t>0
and any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

Recall that
X_{t}=e^{-t}X_{0}+\sqrt{2}\cdot e^{-t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{s}\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}B_{s}
. Therefore
\mu_{t}=\mu^{\prime}*\nu
where
\mu^{\prime}
is the distribution with density
p_{\mu^{\prime}}(x)\propto p_{\mu}\left(\frac{x}{e^{-t}}\right)
and
\nu
is
\mathcal{N}(0,(1-e^{-2t})\mathtt{Id}_{d})
. From
Lemma

46
, with
M_{1}=\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\cdot e^{-2t}\mathtt{Id}_{d}
,
M_{2}=\delta\cdot e^{-2t}\mathtt{Id}_{d}
and
M=(1-e^{-2t})\mathtt{Id}_{d}
, we have

\frac{1}{1+\frac{1-2\delta}{\delta}e^{-2t}}\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}\preceq-\gradient^{2}\log p_{t}(x)\preceq\frac{1}{1-(1-\delta)e^{-2t}}\cdot\mathtt{Id}_{d}.

∎

Thus, despite being a mixture of Gaussian distributions where the component centers might be far apart, it is still
\mathcal{O}(1)
-log-smooth and remains
\mathcal{O}(1)
-log-smooth throughout the OU process.

Remark
.

The motivation for exploring the distributions in
Equation

16
is to study the Ising model, which is a distribution over
\ab\{\pm 1\}^{d}
with density
p_{J,h}(\sigma)\propto\exp\left\{\frac{1}{2}\langle\sigma,J\sigma\rangle+\langle h,\sigma\rangle\right\}
for any
\sigma\in\ab\{\pm 1\}^{d}
.

The Hubbard-Stratonovich transform states that the Ising model can be reduced to sampling from the distribution in
Equation

16
: Consider the joint distribution over
\ab\{\pm 1\}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with density
p_{J,h}(\sigma,x)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}J^{-1}x+(J^{-1}h+\sigma)^{\top}x\right)
. We can prove that

•

its marginal density on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is exactly the
p_{\mu}
in
Equation

16
;

•

p_{\mu}(x)\propto\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}J^{-1}x+h^{\top}J^{-1}x\right\}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(e^{x(i)}+e^{-x(i)}\right)
and thus the unnormalized density and the gradients of the potential function can be calculated in polynomial time for any
x
;

•

the conditional distribution with density
p_{J,h}(\sigma|x)\propto\exp\left\{\langle\sigma,x\rangle\right\}
is a product distribution and can be sampled efficiently.

The proofs are similar to Lemma E.1 in
[
KLR22
]
.

Therefore, sampling from the Ising model can be executed in two steps: 1) sample
X\sim\mu
; 2) sample
\sigma
from the distribution with density
p_{J,h}(\sigma|X)
. Hence, the hardness of this problem is closely related to the hardness of sampling from mixture of Gaussians. Given
Lemma

45
, when
0\prec J\prec\mathtt{Id}_{d}
, the distribution
\mu
can be simulated in polynomial time using Langevin-based algorithms (e.g., the algorithm in
[
CCBJ18
]
) and thus also gives a polynomial complexity upper bound for the Ising model. On the other hand,
[
GKK24
]
proved that for any real
c>1
, the existence of polynomial samplers for Ising model with arbitrary
0\prec J\prec(1+c)\mathtt{Id}_{d}
implies
\mathbf{NP}=\mathbf{RP}
. This in turn indicates that, assuming
\mathbf{NP}\neq\mathbf{RP}
, sampling from the mixture of Gaussians in such a special structure with
0\prec J\prec(1+c)\mathtt{Id}_{d}
is generally hard.

Remark
.

If we delve into the proofs of the lower bounds for sampling and optimization, we can gain some intuition about why optimization is harder. Notably, in both proofs, the hard instances are constructed by locally modifying the function values or density values on a base instance. The challenge for the algorithm is to recognize the modified local area, such as the ball
\mathcal{B}_{r}(x_{i})
in
Equation

17
and
\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}}(v)
in
Section

3.2
. The more balls we can pack, the harder it becomes for the algorithm to identify the modified ball.

On the other hand, to ensure
L
-smoothness, a smooth transition is needed at the border of the modified local area, meaning the radius of the ball cannot be too small. Taking the error
\varepsilon
as a constant for example, we observe the following:

•

In the optimization lower bound, the difference between the function value inside and outside the ball should be
\Omega(1)
. As a consequence, the radius should be
\Omega\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{L}}\right)
.

•

In the sampling lower bound, the mass inside the ball must be
\Omega(1)
. This requires a larger difference between function values inside and outside the ball, which in turn require the radius to be
\Omega\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{L}}\right)
.

Thus, when the error requirements are the same, the hard instances in the sampling lower bound is easier for the algorithm to recognize. This provides an intuition for why optimization is harder than sampling.

7
Conclusion and open problems

In the paper, we studied the query complexity of sampling from
L
-log-smooth distributions with the second moment at most
M
. For this family of distributions, we established a
\left(\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}\right)^{\Theta(d)}
query complexity bound. It is an interesting question to explore the correct constant in the exponent.

Problem 1
.

Determine the infimum
c>0
such that the query complexity of sampling from an
L
-log-smooth distribution with the second moment at most
M
is
\left(\alpha\cdot\frac{LM}{d\varepsilon}\right)^{cd}
for some universal constant
\alpha>0
.

In light of our lower bound proof, we conjecture that the correct constant is
\frac{1}{2}
. In fact, our algorithm for the upper bound compromised significantly on this constant during the truncation of target distribution. Our approach also has the drawback of only applying to bounding the total variation distance. An optimal algorithm might rely on directly establishing functional inequalities (e.g. weak Poincaré inequality
[
HMRW24
]
) for the target distribution.

Problem 2
.

Establish tight functional inequalities for
L
-log-smooth distributions with the second moment at most
M
.

One of the motivation of this work is to understand the extent to which the quasi-polynomial time algorithm of
[
HZD+24
]
applies. As we investigated in
Section

5
, finding a criterion for being
\mathcal{O}(1)
-smooth along the trajectory of the OU process is a challenging task.

Problem 3
.

Understand to what extent a diffusion process (not restricted to the OU process in
[
HZD+24
]
) can maintain the
\mathcal{O}(1)
-smoothness of the initial distribution. Can this condition result in efficient algorithm as well?

Finally, can we characterize the query complexity for sampling from general non-log-concave distributions. Even in the case of mixtures of Gaussians, this remains a challenging problem.

Problem 4
.

Characterize the condition under which sampling from a mixture of Gaussians has sub-exponential query complexity.
