Ultrafast charge-generation dynamics through interfacial energetic modulation for high-performance single-component organic photovoltaics with 14.8% efficiency
Authors: Yao Li, Yongmin Luo, Yulong Hai, Xinkang Wang, Lunbi Wu, Ruijie Ma, Kezhou Fan, Top Archie Dela Peña, Sha Liu, He Yan, Kam Sing Wong, Gang Li, Tao Jia, Junwu Chen, Jiaying Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBulk heterojunction (BHJ) organic solar cells (OSCs) have achieved high efficiencies but suffer from poor morphological stability due to phase separation after long-term operation. Single-component OSCs (SCOSCs) based on double-cable polymers (DCP), offer improved stability through covalently linked donor and acceptor units. However, their efficiency remains limited by inefficient charge generation arising from extensive intermixed morphologies. Here, we report a fluorinated double-cable polymer, DCPY2-F, which achieves an outstanding efficiency of 14.8% with high short-circuit current density of 26.83 mA cm-2. Ultrafast pump-probe transient absorption spectroscopy reveals that fluorination of DCPY2 into DCPY2-F accelerates interfacial charge transfer and long-range charge separation dynamics. The pump-push-probe transient absorption spectroscopy and steady-state electroluminescence show that the faster interfacial charge transfer arises from a reduced reorganization energy and a correspondingly accelerated molecular reorganization process (2.5 ps vs. 0.8 ps). Despite comparable acceptor aggregate sizes with DCPY2, DCPY2-F also shows faster long-range charge separation dynamics, which we attribute to a narrower charge transfer states (CTs) energetic distribution. Molecular dynamics simulations further reveal that fluorination strengthens non-covalent interactions, promoting well-aligned intermolecular donor–acceptor interfaces. These structurally and energetically ordered interfacial CT states enable ultrafast and efficient charge generation. In corresponding binary blends, fluorination similarly enhances charge-transfer dynamics and photocurrent. These findings establish a unified fluorination strategy for accelerating charge generation dynamics in both SCOSCs and blends, and provide a mechanistic understanding for improving charge generation for high-performance single-component systems.Subject terms: Solar cells, Solar cells

Introduction

Single-component organic solar cells (SCOSCs) based on double-cable polymers (DCPs) offer a promising route toward improved device stability and simplified fabrication by covalently linking donor and acceptor units along the same polymer backbone1–3. This architecture inherently prevents large-scale phase separation, improving morphological stability under long-term operation. However, SCOSCs typically exhibit lower short-circuit current densities (JSC ~ 23 mA cm−2) compared to optimized binary and ternary BHJ devices (JSC ~ 28 mA cm−2), reflecting a persistent challenge in efficient charge generation4–6. To fully realize the potential of SCOSCs, it is crucial to enhance charge generation, which requires a deeper understanding of the factors that limit their efficiency.

The current understanding of charge generation in DCP-based systems largely builds on principles derived from well-studied binary BHJ solar cells. However, the morphology of SCOSCs fundamentally differs from that of binary blends. In binary BHJ, donor and acceptor materials are physically mixed, and their crystallinity often drives large-scale phase separation7. The formation of nanocrystalline domains is crucial to facilitate delocalized charge separation at donor/acceptor (D/A) interfaces, enabling photogenerated electron–hole (e–h) pairs to overcome geminate recombination and efficiently generate photocurrent8–10. While organic solar cells based on DCPs inherently exhibit highly intermixed morphology due to the restricted movement of the tethered acceptor units, limiting large-scale domain formation. To address this, prior strategies have tried to tune the nanoscale morphology by introducing crystalline acceptor (such as Y-series acceptor)6, employing solvent additives6, applying high-temperature annealing (up to 230°)11, adjusting linker length12, and designing asymmetric acceptor13. It has also been shown that halogenated donor and acceptor materials in SCOSCs can improve device performance with efficient photocurrent generation, which is attributed to enhanced crystallinity and charge-transport properties. However, the impact of halogenation on ultrafast charge-generation dynamics and interfacial charge-transfer processes in SCOSCs has not been systematically investigated14–16.

Charge generation in organic solar cells is typically described as a two-step process: (1) exciton dissociation via charge transfer at the D/A interface to form CTs with a rate kLE−CT, followed by (2) the long-range separation of CTs into free charge carriers with a rate kCT−CS. Both steps critically depend on the energetic landscape of CTs, which typically exhibit a Gaussian energy distribution characterized by a single CT state energy ECT and spectral broadening (σCT)17,18. Prior studies have extensively explored the properties (ECT, σCT) of charge-transfer states in binary OSCs using techniques such as electroluminescence (EL)19,20 and sensitive external quantum efficiency (sEQE)21. These approaches have also been applied to single-component systems22,23. Recently, a method combining scanning tunneling microscopy with sEQE provided spatial and energetic mapping of CTs24. However, these steady-state approaches lack the temporal resolution to capture charge separation dynamics. Ultrafast pump–push–photocurrent (PPPc) spectroscopy has been employed to probe the dynamics of bound CTs at molecular interfaces, yet it does not resolve the energetic distribution of CTs25. To build a more complete picture of interfacial energetics, it is better to integrate both steady-state and ultrafast spectroscopic approaches. Although molecular and energetic order has been identified to be critical for efficient charge generation in binary OSCs, detailed insight into the energetic distribution of CT states in single-component systems remains scarce26. In SCOSCs, extensive and disordered donor–acceptor contacts often dominate the morphology, making interfacial energetic disorder a key bottleneck for charge separation. This knowledge gap motivates our work to systematically unravel the relationship between interfacial energetic order and charge generation in DCP-based SCOSCs.

In this study, we investigate how charge generation efficiency and charge transfer dynamics depend on interfacial energetics in non-fullerene acceptor-based SCOSCs. The materials studied in this work are a fluorinated double-cable polymer, DCPY2-F, synthesized by fluorinating both the donor and acceptor of previously reported DCPY2. DCPY2-F achieves an extremely high efficiency of 14.8%, with an exceptionally high short-circuit current (26.83 mA cm−2), substantially outperforming its non-fluorinated counterpart. We also fabricated the corresponding binary blends, PBDB-T: Y-O6 and PBDB-TR: Y-O6-F, which exhibit similar photocurrent improvements in photovoltaic performance upon fluorination. To further elucidate the photophysical origins of the photocurrent improvement, we investigate the interfacial energetic distribution of single-component material using a combination of ultrafast pump–probe (PP), pump–push–probe (PPP) transient absorption spectroscopy, and steady-state EL measurements. These techniques are applied to SCOSCs for the first time to directly probe their interfacial properties and elucidate their impact on charge separation efficiency. Our results demonstrate that enhanced photocurrent in DCPY2-F is facilitated by an energetically ordered donor–acceptor interface with reduced energetic disorder (small σCT). Morphological and theoretical analyses indicate that such interfacial energetic ordering arises from a well-defined fibrillar donor network and strong non-covalent interactions between donor and acceptor units, which together promote aligned interfacial packing. These findings provide a comprehensive understanding of charge generation dynamics in fibrillar DCPs and underscore the importance of interfacial energetic order for improving OSCs performance.

Results

Materials properties and optoelectronic performance

To enhance the performance of previously reported DCPY2-based SCOSCs, we chemically modified both the donor and acceptor moieties. Specifically, we fluorinated the acceptor unit (Y-O6→Y-O6-F) and introduced partial fluorination into the donor backbone (PBDB-T→PBDB-TR), yielding a double-cable polymer DCPY2-F. The molecular structures of the donor and acceptor components are shown in Fig. 1a. Fluorination is expected to modulate both their electronic structure and intermolecular interactions, thereby influencing the optical properties and photovoltaic performance of the material27,28. As shown in Fig. 1b, the absorption spectra of DCPY2 and DCPY2-F films display complementary absorption contributions from the donor (450–700 nm) and acceptor components (700–1000 nm). In the visible range, the peak around ~585 nm is attributed to the intramolecular π–π* transition along the conjugated donor backbone, while the higher-energy shoulder at ~635 nm arises from interchain π–π* transitions enabled by backbone π–π stacking (further discussion is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1)29. For the absorption bands in the near-infrared region, the peaks around 815 nm and 720 nm for DCPY2-F are assigned to the S₀→S₁ electronic transition (0–0 transition) of the acceptor aggregates and its first vibronic side band (0–1 transitions)30,31. Additionally, the donor band of DCPY2-F exhibits a markedly higher 0–0/0–1 vibronic peak ratio (1.43) compared to DCPY2 (1.24), indicative of a more dominant J-type aggregation with head-to-tail molecular packing30,32,33. Such aggregation suggests enhanced intrachain planarity and coherent intermolecular coupling, which are expected to facilitate more ordered donor–acceptor contacts and efficient charge generation. Concentration-dependent absorption spectra of neat donor (Supplementary Fig. 2) further reveal that PBDB-T undergoes a transition from H- to J-type aggregation as concentration increases from dilute solution to the neat film, whereas PBDB-TR maintains dominant J-aggregate features in both solution and film states. This observation implies donor fluorination enhances backbone planarity and promotes intermolecular interactions, stabilizing slipped π–π stacking geometries characteristic of J-type aggregation34,35. Compared with DCPY2, the DCPY2-F film shows a pronounced redshift (~32 nm) of low energy acceptor band around 800 nm, consistent across both film and solution measurements (Supplementary Fig. 3). This redshift is attributed to a narrower optical bandgap, arising from the downshifted lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) level of the fluorinated acceptor in both density functional theory (DFT) and cyclic voltammetry measurements (Supplementary Figs. 4–7)36,37. An interesting phenomenon is that the high-energy donor band around 620 nm appears largely unaffected after fluorination of DCPY2 into DCPY2-F in both solution and thin films. This is because the donor-related transition primarily originates from π–π* excitation along the conjugated backbone, where fluorination stabilizes the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and LUMO in a nearly parallel manner without substantially altering the intrinsic optical gap (Supplementary Fig. 5). As a result, the donor absorption band remains nearly unchanged. In contrast, the acceptor unit exhibits a spatial separation of frontier molecular orbitals, with the HOMO primarily localized on the central electron-rich core and the LUMO on the electron-deficient end groups. The corresponding low-energy absorption band possesses strong intramolecular charge-transfer character. Fluorination at the electron-withdrawing end groups enhances their electron deficiency, further stabilizing the LUMO and the charge-transfer excited state. Consequently, the S₀→S₁ transition energy is reduced, leading to a pronounced redshift of the acceptor absorption band.

The current–voltage (J–V) characteristics and photovoltaic performance of DCPY2 and DCPY2-F devices are summarized in Fig. 1c, d, Table 1 and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. DCPY2-F shows an extremely high power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 14.8%, with an open circuit voltage VOC = 0.86 V, a short-circuit current density JSC = 26.83 mA cm−2 and FF = 64.3%, which outperforms its non-fluorinated counterpart DCPY2 (PCE = 13.6%, with VOC = 0.87 V, JSC = 23.66 mA cm−2 and FF = 65.0%). DCPY2-F maintains a VOC comparable to DCPY2, despite a reduction in its optical gap (1.530–1.473 eV). This is largely attributed to suppressed non-radiative voltage loss in DCPY2-F (0.246 eV) compared to that of DCPY2 (0.267 eV), as detailed in the voltage loss analysis (Supplementary Note 1, Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 3). Notably, there is substantial enhancement of JSC in DCPY2-F compared to DCPY2, which originates not only from the extended absorption range but also the significantly improved external quantum efficiency (EQE), exceeding 80% across the 500–800 nm range (Fig. 1e). Similar trend of photocurrent enhancement upon fluorination is also observed in the corresponding binary BHJ devices (Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Table 4), suggesting that fluorination universally promotes charge generation efficiency27,38,39. To further explore the origin of the enhanced JSC in DCPY2-F, we examined the light-intensity dependence of photocurrent. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 10, both DCPY2 and DCPY2-F devices exhibit near-unity linearity, indicating that non-geminate loss under short-circuit conditions is negligible. Therefore, the primary losses influencing photocurrent likely arise from field-dependent geminate recombination of the exciton, which is typically significant in disordered morphologies40. The higher JSC of DCPY2-F compared to DCPY2 might suggest improved morphological and energetic order induced by fluorination, which optimizes the donor–acceptor interfacial packing.

Intermolecular interaction and interfacial properties

A key question underlying the enhanced performance of DCPY2-F is how fluorination influences the intermolecular interactions and charge generation pathways in DCP systems. Unlike conventional binary bulk heterojunctions, DCP-based SCOSCs contain three possible charge-separation pathways (Supplementary Fig. 11): (1) covalently bonded intramolecular D–A interfaces, (2) intrachain D/A contacts mediated by alkyl linkers, and (3) intermolecular D/A interfaces formed between neighboring donor chains. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations reveal that the ~2 nm alkyl spacer in DCPs restricts electronic coupling between covalently tethered donor and acceptor moieties, and the tethered acceptors show minimal orbital overlap with their corresponding donors (Supplementary Fig. 12). These results effectively rule out the first two pathways. We thus identify the intermolecular D/A interfaces, formed between spatially adjacent donor and acceptor units from neighboring chains, as the dominant sites for charge separation. The strength and degree of order at these intermolecular interfaces are therefore crucial to charge generation efficiency in DCP-based SCOSCs.

To gain deeper insight into how fluorination modulates these intermolecular interactions and interfacial structures, we performed a series of theoretical calculations on DCPY2 and DCPY2-F. DFT calculations were first performed to visualize and quantify non-covalent interactions between donor and acceptor in DCPY2 and DCPY2-F, using the independent gradient model based on Hirshfeld partition (IGMH)41–44. As shown in Fig. 2a, b and Supplementary Figs. 13 and 14, DCPY2-F exhibits more green area and stronger intermolecular interactions (δGinter = 2.24) than DCPY2 (δGinter = 1.97), primarily due to enhanced F···H and O···H interactions. These stronger intermolecular interactions in DCPY2-F are further supported by concentration-dependent PP-TA measurements on polystyrene-diluted films (Supplementary Figs. 15–17 and Supplementary Note 2). This stronger intermolecular interaction likely drives acceptor units towards surrounding donor fibrils, facilitating the formation of more aligned and densely packed intermolecular interfaces45,46. Molecular dynamics simulations were then conducted to investigate the packing behavior in DCPs. The distribution probability of dihedral angle (θ) between donor and acceptor units in DCPY2 and DCPY2-F was compared to assess interfacial conformational order (Fig. 2c). Specifically, θ < 45° corresponds to an aligned interfacial packing of donor and acceptor conducive to electron wavefunction overlapping. In contrast, θ > 45° suggests a misaligned interfacial packing. A schematic illustration of these two interfacial packings in DCPs is shown in Fig. 2d. As shown in Fig. 2c, DCPY2 displays a broad distribution of θ centered around 70°, indicating a predominance of misaligned interfacial packing. This misaligned interfacial packing would largely lead to disordered interfacial states. While for DCPY2-F, their interfacial packing shows two regions, with 52% presenting a misaligned interface and 48% interfaces falling within the aligned regime. Charge generation is expected to predominantly occur at the aligned interfaces. The increased fraction of aligned interfaces in DCPY2-F therefore indicates a more ordered interfacial arrangement compared to DCPY2.

The detailed nanomorphology information in thin films was further investigated by photo-induced force microscope (PiFM) measurements and grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS). The PiFM measurement based on near-field infrared imaging was used to resolve the spatial distribution of donor and acceptor phases in pristine films (Supplementary Figs. 18–20). By tracking the characteristic infrared absorption wavenumbers of PBDB-T (PBDB-TR) and Y-O6 (Y-O6-F), we distinguished the fibrillar matrix of donors and the featureless acceptor in both DCPY2 and DCPY2-F film, which indicated that fibrillar architecture in DCP films arises from the intrinsic fibrillar donor chains47,48. Overlaid of the two PiFM images (Supplementary Fig. 20c, f) shows that the acceptor domains are uniformly distributed within the fibrillar donor framework. GIWAXS patterns are shown in Supplementary Fig. 21 and Supplementary Tables 5 and 6. Both DCPY2 and DCPY2-F exhibit preferential face-on orientations favorable for vertical charge transport, evidenced by strong π–π stacking peaks along the out-of-plane direction49,50. In the out-of-plane direction (Supplementary Fig. 22), DCPY2-F displays a redshifted (010) peak at 1.743 Å−1 (d = 3.603 Å) compared to 1.713 Å−1 (d = 3.666 Å) in DCPY2, indicating a tighter π-π stacking distance and enhanced intermolecular coherence. Moreover, a distinct lamellar stacking peak of in-plane direction is observed in both DCPY2 (qd = 0.153 Å⁻¹) and DCPY2-F (qd = 0.159 Å⁻¹), while it is not observed in neat donor films, which are assigned to the ordered packing between neighboring donor chains in DCPs. The shorter lamellar spacing in DCPY2-F (4.10 vs. 3.95 nm) reflects a denser donor fibril architecture. Azimuthal intensity analysis (Supplementary Fig. 23) shows that DCPY2-F exhibits a narrower full-width at half-maximum of 38.4° compared to 43.6° in DCPY2, indicating a higher degree of acceptor packing order along the π–π stacking direction (010). This better-oriented packing of donor and acceptor is likely associated with the formation of the ordered interfacial packing in DCPY2-F.

Such structural ordering at the interface is expected to give rise to a more energetically ordered CT landscape. To verify this, we measured sensitive EQE and electroluminescence spectra to quantify energetic distribution (σCT) at the D/A interfaces. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 24–27 and Supplementary Table 7 and Supplementary Note 3, the static energetic disorder (σs) was extracted from the apparent Urbach energy (EUapp) near the absorption onset using the method developed by Kaiser et al. The resulting σs values are 39.0 meV for DCPY2 and 37.3 meV for DCPY2-F under room temperature, indicating a slight reduction in static disorder upon fluorination. Temperature-dependent sEQE measurements further confirm that σs remains largely invariant with temperature, consistent with its static nature. The reduced EL (rEL) spectra for DCPY2 and DCPY2-F devices are shown in Fig. 2e, f. To extract CT properties, the rEL spectra were fitted using three Gaussian components, accounting for singlet exciton and CT emission following classical Marcus theory of electron transfer (details provided in the Supplementary Note 4 and Supplementary Figs. 28–34). The extracted ECT are 1.485 eV for DCPY2 and 1.446 eV for DCPY2-F, with corresponding reorganization energies of 135 meV and 91.3 meV, respectively. From these parameters, the total energetic disorder of CT states (σCT) is found to decrease from 91.6 meV in DCPY2 to 77.3 meV in DCPY2-F, indicating a narrowed CT energetic distribution upon fluorination. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 34, temperature-dependent EL measurements further show progressive linewidth narrowing upon cooling, while both ECT and reorganization energy remain invariant within experimental uncertainty (Table S8), confirming that the reduced σCT in DCPY2-F reflects suppressed energetic disorder rather than fitting artifacts. Overall, these results collectively suggest that fluorination enhances intermolecular coupling, induces ordered interfacial packing, and narrows the energetic distribution of CTs.

Charge generation dynamics

We next turn to the analysis of ultrafast charge generation dynamics in DCP films. In organic solar cells, charge generation predominantly occurs at donor–acceptor interfaces via ultrafast charge transfer processes, either by hole transfer (HT) from acceptor to donor or electron transfer (ET) from donor to acceptor51. To probe the dynamics and quantum efficiency of these processes, we employed broadband pump–probe (PP) transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy technique on neat films (Supplementary Fig. 36) and DCP films (Fig. 3a–d and Supplementary Fig. 37). We first monitor hole transfer process by selective excitation of the acceptor excitons at 800 nm under a fluence of 0.5 μJ cm−2. As shown in Fig. 3c, d, both materials exhibit ground-state bleach (GSB) signals around 620 nm from the donor, and two excited-state absorption (ESA) features at 910 nm and 960 nm, corresponding to acceptor excitons and hole polarons on the donor chain, respectively. Notably, both DCPY2 and DCPY2-F display the additional ESA band around 680 nm, which gradually redshifts and intensifies over time, while it is absent in neat donor and acceptor films. This feature, which is assigned to transient electroabsorption (EA) response, originates from starks shift of absorption under a local electric field generated by spatially separating e–h pairs at the interface52. This EA response is indicative of exciton dissociation and charge separation process, as local electric dipole would affect surrounding molecules, thus leading to the spectral redshift (Supplementary Fig. 38a)53. The transient EA band is further supported by steady-state EA measurements in the device under external bias (Supplementary Fig. 38b). Consistent with a previous report, the EA spectrum resembles the first derivative of its ground-state absorption and the donor GSB region in TA spectra54. Tracking the kinetics of transient EA signal at 680 nm for DCPY2 and DCPY2-F reveals distinct dynamics between these two systems (Fig. 3e). In DCPY2-F, the EA signal emerges within 0.2–0.5 ps, whereas in DCPY2, it appears after ~5 ps, suggesting a more rapid charge separation in the fluorinated system. A similar trend is observed under donor excitation at 550 nm to probe ET dynamics, where DCPY2-F again exhibits a faster and more intense EA response (Supplementary Fig. 38d).

To further quantify the hole transfer kinetics, we analyzed the ESA features associated with hole polarons in DCPY2 and DCPY2-F films. Following excitation at 800 nm, singlet excitons at 910 nm decay coincides with the rise of the 960 nm signal corresponding to hole polarons. As shown in Fig. 3f, the hole transfer kinetics was fitted with a biexponential function: I(t)=A1exp(−t/τ1)+A2exp(−t/τ2), where the fast phase τ1 is assigned to exciton dissociation at the interface, the slow phase τ2 is associated with the long-range charge transfer, A1 and A2 are their respective proportions55. As summarized in Supplementary Table 9, both DCPY2 and DCPY2-F show a dominant role of the fast component, with A1 is 74% for DCPY2 and 75% DCPY2-F, suggesting the exciton dissociation is dominated by interfacial hole transfer. DCPY2-F shows a significantly shorter τ1 (0.93 ps) than DCPY2 (2.44 ps), indicative of faster interfacial hole transfer, likely facilitated by improved interfacial order. The average HT time constant (τAve) also decreases from 13.57 ps in DCPY2 to 7.06 ps in DCPY2-F, suggesting a faster charge generation process in DCPY2-F. Moreover, slower decay kinetics after 100 ps suggest long-lived polaron signals persist in DCPY2-F, indicating suppressed charge recombination and extended carrier lifetimes56.

We next investigate the electron transfer dynamics of DCPY2 and DCPY2-F by selectively exciting the donor domains at 550 nm under a fluence of 0.5 μJ cm−2. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 39 and Supplementary Table 9, both systems exhibit ET kinetics dominated by a fast interfacial component, with A1 of 55% for DCPY2 and 66% for DCPY2-F. Notably, DCPY2-F displays shorter time constants for both the ultrafast interfacial process (τ1 = 0.14 ps) and the average decay (τAve = 5.53 ps) compared to DCPY2 (τ1 = 0.21 ps, τAve = 6.47 ps), indicating the accelerated electron transfer from donor to acceptor in the fluorinated system. Interestingly, in both systems, the electron transfer occurs more rapidly than hole transfer in both systems, which is typically observed in organic solar cells57. This asymmetry might come from the different energy landscapes for charge separation, with lower separation barriers for electron transfer across the donor/acceptor interface compared to hole transfer.

As illustrated in Fig. 3h and Supplementary Tables 10 and 11, for the hole-transfer process, DCPY2 exhibits a charge-transfer time constant (τcharge transfer) of 13.57 ps and an exciton lifetime of 96.5 ps, yielding a hole-transfer efficiency of 87.7%. In contrast, DCPY2-F shows a substantially faster charge transfer (τcharge transfer = 7.06 ps) together with a prolonged exciton lifetime (τexciton = 192.9 ps), resulting in a markedly higher quantum efficiency of 96.5%, which directly explains the observed enhancement in EQE. To directly benchmark these results against conventional binary BHJ systems, we further performed broadband PP-TA spectroscopy on PBDB-T: Y-O6 and PBDB-TR: Y-O6-F blend films (Supplementary Figs. 40–43). Upon selective excitation of the acceptor at 800 nm, both BHJ blends exhibit a pronounced donor GSB centered at ~620 nm, together with a PIA band located at ~890 nm for PBDB-T: Y-O6 and redshifted to ~910 nm for PBDB-TR: Y-O6-F. In contrast to the dual PIA features observed in the DCP films, only a single PIA band is detected in the BHJ blends. The PIA signal in both blends displays a characteristic picosecond decay, which is attributed to exciton dissociation. By tracking the GSB kinetics at the donor GSB position, we directly quantify the hole-transfer dynamics. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 42 and Supplementary Table 12, the average hole-transfer time constant decreases from 3.34 ps in PBDB-T: Y-O6 to 1.07 ps in PBDB-TR: Y-O6-F, indicating significantly accelerated charge transfer upon fluorination. This trend closely mirrors the fluorination-induced acceleration of charge-transfer dynamics observed in the DCP systems, demonstrating that fluorination promotes faster hole transfer dynamics in both blended and covalently linked architectures.

Probing energetics and dynamics of interfacial CT states

While conventional PP-TA is sensitive to both exciton and free charges, the pump–push–probe (PPP) TA allows for a selective probe of interfacial CTs60,61. We then conducted PPP-TA measurements on DCPY2 and DCPY2-F blends, and the setup is shown in Supplementary Fig. 44. In this technique, the sample was initially excited by an 800 nm pump pulse, generating excitations like CT excitons and charges. A delayed push pulse at 2000 nm (0.62 eV) is then used to selectively perturb the pump-induced species, either promoting given charges to higher-energy configurations (hot states) or to offer excess energy for CTs dissociation. The probe pulse subsequently monitors the resultant optical changes. Sequentially tuning the chopping frequency of push, pump, and probe pulses to be 250, 500, 1000 Hz allows the measurements of pump–probe spectra, push–probe spectra, and pump–push–probe spectra. The push wavelength was carefully selected to avoid ground-state absorption, as confirmed by the blank push–probe spectrum. Moreover, the push fluence was kept small enough to avoid any multiphoton excitations of ground states, while remaining sufficient to push the pump-induced excitations. The optical response of the push pulse on excitations is quantified as the differential signal Δ(ΔA), defined as the difference between PPP and PP spectra (Δ(ΔA) = ΔAPPP − ΔAPP), as shown in Fig. 4a.

As shown in Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 46, we varied the pump–push delay (Δt1) from 0.3 ps to 100 ps and then recorded the corresponding differential PPP signals under a fixing push–probe delay (Δt2 = 0.2–0.5 ps). The push-induced signal resembles the transient EA response observed in PP measurement, which we referred to as push-induced EA below. We attribute these differential PPP signals to the perturbation of CTs at donor–acceptor interfaces rather than free charges, as the response of free charges typically would not generate notable spectral features. Notably, this push-induced signal is not observed in neat donor or acceptor films, indicating the interfacial origin of the involved intermediate state, rather than in neat donor or acceptor. When push pulse interacts with CTs, it offers excess energy to promote further separation of e–h pairs, instead of geminate recombination, thus generating the EA response. Hence, the push-induced EA signal serves as a probe of interfacial CTs and their dissociation dynamics. Supplementary Fig. 48 shows the kinetics of push-induced EA signal (averaged between 610 and 640 nm) at a fixed pump–push delay of 0.3 ps, where the timescale is chosen to primarily reflect the dynamics of intrinsic CTs. The results reveal a markedly faster decay for DCPY2-F (τ = 4.25 ps) compared to DCPY2 (τ = 16.98 ps), consistent with reported timescales for interfacial charge separation, indicative of more efficient dissociation of CTs into free charges in the fluorinated polymer62.

To evaluate the temporal evolution of CT populations, we analyzed the normalized Δ(ΔA) amplitudes as a function of pump–push delay, as shown in Fig. 4c. Both DCPY2 and DCPY2-F exhibit a decrease in push-induced EA intensity with increasing Δt1, indicating the progressive CT dissociation process. However, key differences emerge in their dynamics across early and later timescales. At early times (<2.5 ps), DCPY2-F exhibits a notably stronger EA response than DCPY2, indicating a larger population of loosely bound interfacial CTs that are more susceptible to the push perturbation. This suggests greater initial delocalization of CTs in DCPY2-F, which promotes efficient dissociation upon IR push. In contrast, the weaker EA signal in DCPY2 points to more localized and tightly bound CTs. This behavior likely arises from increased energetic disorder at the donor–acceptor interface and stronger Coulomb interactions in DCPY2. The existence of such tightly bound CTs in DCPY2 also explains the larger geminate loss in short-circuit current (JSC). At later times (>2.5 ps), most interfacial CTs have separated, and the push pulse primarily interacts with CTs far from the interface, where long-range charge separation dominates (Fig. 4d). Notably, DCPY2 displays a higher EA intensity in this regime, implying inefficient long-range charge separation and a greater population of residual bound pairs. This observation aligns with its smaller τ2 observed in PP-TA measurements. In contrast, the lower EA signal in DCPY2-F suggests that most CTs have already dissociated into free carriers, consistent with their more effective charge separation.

We next examined the spectral evolution of push-induced species to gain insights into the interfacial energetic landscape. Due to the derivative nature of EA signals, the integration of the push-induced EA response yields an estimate of the local absorption spectrum near the donor–acceptor interface63. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 49, when the pump–push delay (Δt1) increases, both DCPY2 and DCPY2-F exhibit an initial redshift followed by a blueshift in the spectrum. The temporal evolution of the relative peak shift for DCPY2 and DCPY2-F is presented in Fig. 5, which gives a direct observation of the energetic landscape near the interface. The peak shifts suggest that, upon excitation, CTs are initially localized in higher-energy, disordered interfacial regions. At early times, these states relax and migrate toward more ordered interfacial domains, stabilizing into lower-energy configurations. At later delay times, the push pulse predominantly probes CTs that have moved deeper into the donor phase, which are associated with more disordered and energetically elevated donor bulk. Notably, the energetic difference between the low-energy interfacial CTs and those near the donor bulk differs significantly between the two materials. DCPY2-F exhibits a narrower interfacial energetic distribution (29 meV) compared to DCPY2 (43 meV), indicating a flatter energetic landscape around the interface in DCPY2-F. Overall, the results show an energetically “well-like” landscape near the interface. The low-energy interfacial states could help suppress non-geminate recombination by impeding polaron back-diffusion, thus suppressing non-geminate recombination, which is also supported by previous studies64. However, such energy wells may also introduce an energetic barrier that hinders the separation of CTs from the interface to the bulk. Therefore, the flatter energetic gradient observed in DCPY2-F implies a reduced barrier for long-range charge separation.

Looking into the timescale for CTs relaxation process, DCPY2-F undergoes a rapid energetic relaxation within 0.8 ps, while DCPY2 shows a slower relaxation over 2.5 ps. Prior studies have established that bound singlet excitons form within 10 fs upon excitation just above the optical gap, followed by a 50-fs relaxation process assigned to molecular reorganization, such as planarization or torsional relaxation65,66. Here, we assign the 2.5 ps (DCPY2) and 0.8 ps (DCPY2-F) time constants to CTs relaxation processes involving molecular geometry changes (Supplementary Note 5)67,68. The longer relaxation time in DCPY2 indicates that greater structural rearrangement is required to reach a stabilized CT configuration, which might suggest a disordered initial state. In contrast, the sub-picosecond timescale in DCPY2-F suggests minimal structural rearrangement, indicative of a more ordered configuration with smaller reorganization energy. This rapid relaxation, along with the narrower interfacial energetic distribution in DCPY2-F, facilitates efficient separation of e–h pairs by quickly overcoming coulomb attraction, thereby reducing geminate recombination and contributing to higher JSC and EQE63. Notably, both timescales are slower than the ~200 fs interfacial stabilization process observed in fullerene-based systems (PCDTBT: mPCBM) reported in a previous study63. This discrepancy likely originates from differences in molecular architecture and interfacial packing between DCP-based and fullerene-based blends, though the detailed discussion is, however, beyond the scope of this study. We also observed that the further structural changes of CTs spanning to the time windows of 100 ps, which aligned with previous results monitored by femtosecond stimulated Raman spectroscopy69.

In summary, our PPP spectroscopy results reveal an energetically flatter and more ordered interfacial configuration in DCPY2-F, which not only enables rapid CTs relaxation but also shows reduced barrier long-range charge separation, thus contributing to the improved JSC and overall device efficiency.

Effect of Interfacial energetics on charge generation dynamics

Efficient charge generation requires both rapid interfacial charge transfer (kLE-CT) and a subsequent long-range charge separation process70,71. To gain deeper insight into how interfacial energetics govern the charge generation process, we applied Marcus theory to analyze the interfacial charge transfer kinetics in DCPY2 and DCPY2-F:

Here, kLE−CT is the hole transfer rate, σCT is the total energetic disorder of CTs, tLE−CT denotes electronic coupling between LE and CTs, λ is the reorganization energy, ΔELE−CT is the driving force for charge transfer. From molecular dynamics simulations (Supplementary Fig. 50), the intermolecular electronic couplings (tLE-CT) for DCPY2 and DCPY2-F were determined to be 2.7 and 3.1 meV, respectively, indicating slightly stronger coupling in the fluorinated system72. The driving forces (ΔELE-CT) of DCPY2 and DCPY2-F are 0.045 eV and 0.027 eV, determined by CT state energy and optical gap, and are comparable between the two materials (Supplementary Fig. 51). Using these parameters, we calculated kLE−CT for both materials, as summarized in Supplementary Table 1339. The calculated charge-transfer rate (kLE−CT) in DCPY2-F is approximately twice that of DCPY2 (5.29 × 1010 s−1 vs. 2.94 × 1010 s−1), in agreement with the faster τ1 component observed in PP-TA kinetics. Importantly, this time constant remains on the sub-picosecond timescale even under near-zero driving force. In the normal Marcus regime (where λ > ΔELE-CT), the reduced reorganization energy would lower the driving force for CTs formation, facilitating more efficient exciton dissociation and interfacial charge transfer73,74. The observed accelerated kLE−CT in DCPY2-F is primarily attributed to reduced reorganization energy λ (135 meV for DCPY2 and 91.3 meV for DCPY2-F) and corresponding narrower CTs distribution σCT (91.6 meV for DCPY2 and 77.3 meV for DCPY2-F). The agreement between theory and experiment underscores the importance of minimizing CTs energetic disorder for interfacial charge transfer.

The energetic landscape of CTs also governs the kinetics of subsequent long-range charge separation. At disordered interfaces, a broader CTs distribution leads to numerous localized, low-energy CTs. These deep tail states can strongly confine polaron pairs through coulombic attraction, making it easier to geminate recombine75. This effect is particularly evident in DCPY2, where the broad CTs distribution indicates an energetically disordered interface with deep tail states. Additionally, the energetic difference between localized tail states and more delocalized states near the bulk is large, requiring a higher driving force for charge separation and thus suppressing long-range charge transfer. This aligns with the slower diffusion-limited charge transfer kinetics (τ2) observed in our pump–probe TA measurements. In contrast, DCPY2-F with more energetically ordered interfacial CTs reduces the driving force required for long-range charge separation, resulting in faster long-range charge transfer kinetics. Additionally, the faster molecular relaxation dynamics in DCPY2-F may help to overcome long-range Coulombic attraction, further facilitating efficient charge separation63. It is known that large acceptor aggregates might facilitate electron delocalization and improve charge transfer kinetics, and long-range charge transfer dynamics is always associated with acceptor domain size76. Therefore, we further carry out GISAXS complemented by PiFM results. Based on their fibrillar morphology, we employ a flexible cylinder model and fractal models to extract morphological metrics. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 52 and 53 and Supplementary Table 14, it reveals comparable acceptor domain sizes in DCPY2 (2Rg ≈ 32.1 nm) and DCPY2-F films (2Rg ≈ 34.9 nm), excluding the key effect of acceptor aggregation on charge transfer kinetics. Overall, these results underscore the role of interfacial energetic order in enabling efficient charge generation and ultimately contributing to the superior charge generation efficiency in DCPY2-F26.

Discussion

In this study, we employed ultrafast pump–probe and pump–push–probe transient absorption, together with steady electroluminescence spectroscopies to probe the charge generation dynamics and interfacial energetics in both DCP system and its fluorinated counterpart (Fig. 6). For the fast component (τ1) from PP-TA dynamics (Fig. 2f), an interesting thing we found is that the timescale of molecular reorganization processes from PPP-TA analysis (2.5 ps for DCPY2 and 0.8 ps for DCPY2-F; Fig. 5) closely matches the interfacial charge transfer kinetics (2.44 vs. 0.93 ps). This correlation suggests that the interfacial charge transfer process is primarily limited by molecular reorganization. The accelerated charge transfer in DCPY2-F can thus be attributed to its lower reorganization energy, consistent with the narrower energetic distribution of CTs (σCT). EL spectra (Fig. 2e, f) reveal an interfacial energetic difference (ΔσCT) of approximately 14.3 meV between DCPY2 and DCPY2-F, in agreement with the ΔσCT ≈ 14 meV estimated from PPP-TA measurements (Fig. 5). The consistency of steady-state and ultrafast spectroscopic results validates our interpretation of interfacial energetics and demonstrates a robust approach for simultaneously probing energetic landscapes and charge transfer dynamics. Regarding the slower component (τ2) from PP-TA dynamics, we observed a faster τ2 of 8.97 ps of DCPY2-F than DCPY2 (17.90 ps). Previous studies often associated it with a diffusion-limited process, and it is highly correlated with acceptor domain size51,77,78. While our GISAXS results show a comparative aggregate size of acceptor in DCPY2 and DCPY2-F, which cannot explain their different τ2. A possible explanation of the accelerated τ2 in DCPY2-F is the reduced energy landscape between low-energy sites near the interface and the bulk, facilitating long-range charge separation. These findings suggest that τ2 reflects not only a diffusion-limited but also an energetically driven process.

The role of interfacial energetics on charge transfer kinetics.

a Illustration of the interfacial energetic and structural difference between DCPY2 and DCPY2-F. b Schematics of dynamic processes involved in the charge generation process in DCPY2 and DCPY2-F.

In conclusion, we reported that a fluorinated double-cable-polymer DCPY2-F achieved an extremely high efficiency of 14.8% in DCP-based SCOSCs with a high short-circuit current density (26.83 mA cm−2). Ultrafast pump–probe transient absorption spectroscopy reveals that fluorinated DCPY2-F exhibits markedly accelerated interfacial hole transfer (τ1: 2.44 ps → 0.93 ps) and long-range charge transfer (τ2: 17.90 ps → 8.97 ps) in DCPY2-F, leading to the higher quantum efficiency of hole transfer. The ultrafast charge generation dynamics in DCPY2-F is achieved by the modulation of interfacial CT energetic ordering (σCT : 91.6 meV → 77.3 meV), and the change is attributed to the strong intermolecular interaction from the fluorinated donor and acceptor in DCPY2-F. We further demonstrate that the corresponding fluorinated binary blends show similar accelerated charge-generation dynamics and enhanced photocurrent generation to their non-fluorinated counterparts. Collectively, these results extend established fluorination strategies from conventional blend systems to SCOSCs and elucidate the critical role of interfacial energetic modulation in ultrafast and efficient charge generation for high-performance SCOSCs.

Methods

Materials and device fabrication

Commercial chemicals PBDB-T, PM6 (from Volt-amp Optoelectronics Tech. Co., Ltd.) were used as received. The acceptor Y-O6 and DCPY2 were prepared based on previous literature. The PBDB-TR, Y-O6-F, and DCPY2-F were prepared according to Supplementary Figs. 54–64. Photovoltaic devices were prepared with a common configuration: Indium tin oxide (ITO) /PEDOT: PSS/Active Layer/PDINN/Ag. ITO substrates were first cleaned by detergent, distilled water, acetone, and isopropanol through ultrasonication, followed by oxygen plasma treatment. The PEDOT: PSS layer is deposited by spin-coating at 4000 rpm for 40 s on the pre-treated ITO substrates. The active layer was prepared by spin-coating the solution based on DCPY2 and DCPY2-F (with 5%DIO as an additive) in ortho-dichlorobenzene (oDCB) on the PEDOT:PSS layer under a N2-filled glove box, followed by annealing at 150 °C for 10 min, and then solvent annealed using CS2 for 3 min. A 5 nm layer of PDINN (0.5 mg/mL in methanol) was subsequently spin-coated as electron transporting layer. For the binary BHJ blends, the donor and acceptor materials were mixed at a weight ratio of 1:1.2 with a total concentration of 35 mg/mL−1 in o-DCB with 0.5% DIO by volume as an additive. The solution was heated to 90 °C and then immediately spin-coated onto PEDOT: PSS-coated substrates. The prepared active layers were thermal annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. Afterwards, PNDIT-F3N-Br solution (0.5 mg mL−1 in methanol) was spin-coated on the top of active layers at 2000 rpm for 35 s to form the electron-transporting layer. Finally, a 100 nm layer of silver was thermally deposited on the top through a shadow mask in a vacuum chamber at a pressure of 1 × 10−7 mbar. The device contact area was 0.057 cm2 determined by a mask. Device measurements were conducted inside the glovebox under an inert atmosphere. Voltage scan from −0.2 to 1 V with a step voltage of 0.02 V is used. The device performance is averaged between 8 devices.

Photovoltaic characterization

The J–V curves of OSC devices were captured by a Keithley 2400 Source Meter in a glove box under a Newport solar simulator with AM1.5 illumination at 100 mW cm−2 calibrated by a certificated silicon photodiode. A voltage scan from −0.2 to 1 V was used. EQE spectra were obtained by a Solar Cell Spectral Response Measurement System QE-R3011 (Enlitech Co., Ltd.).

Absorption, reflectance, and photoluminescence spectroscopy

UV-Vis absorption spectra and reflectance spectra were measured by a UV-Vis-NIR spectrophotometer, Lambda 1050 from Perkin Elmer. PL spectra were recorded by a FluoroMax 4 spectrofluorometer (Horiba Jobin Yvon) and a Renishaw (inVia-Qontor) Raman microscope in backscattering configuration using a 633 nm laser excitation (10 μm spot diameter, 25% defocusing on samples).

Cyclic voltammetry measurements

Cyclic Voltammetry (CV) measurements were conducted using a standard three-electrode electrochemical cell controlled by a potentiostat (Autolab PGSTAT302N, Metrohm). The electrochemical cell consists of an ITO-coated working electrode, an Ag/AgCl reference electrode, a platinum counter electrode, and 0.1 M tetrabutylammonium hexafluorophosphate (TBAPF6) in anhydrous acetonitrile as electrolyte. All potentials were referenced to the ferrocenium/ferrocene (Fc/Fc+) redox couple (reduction potential of −4.40 V). CV scans were performed over a potential range of −1.5 to +1.5 V (vs. Ag/AgCl) at a scan rate of 10 mV/s. All experiments were carried out at room temperature.

T-dependent electroluminescence (EL) and sensitive EQE (sEQE) measurements

Steady-state EL measurements were conducted using a home-built EL spectroscopy setup. The DC voltage was applied by a power supply. The EL spectra are collected by a collimator lens and coupled into an optical fiber to the spectrometer (Princeton Instruments HRS 500, 300 g/mm grating) with a CCD detector (PIXIS, 100BR eXcelon). Wavelength calibration was performed with an Hg and Ne/Ar dual switchable light source. The external quantum efficiency of EL (EQEEL) was determined by measuring the emitted photons in all directions through an integrated sphere. The sEQE was measured using a QTest Station HIFINITY 5L (Crowntech Inc., USA) equipped with a Keithley 2400 sourcemeter. The light intensity at each wavelength was calibrated by a certificated silicon and Indium Gallium Arsenic (InGaAs) photodiode. Temperature control was achieved using a Linkam HFS350EV-PB4 stage, providing a stable temperature range from 213 to 293 K.

Pump–probe (PP) transient absorption spectroscopy

Transient absorption measurement was conducted on the commercial pump–probe femtosecond transient absorption (TA) spectrometer Helios (Ultrafast System, USA). Ultrafast laser pulses (800 nm, <35 fs pulse duration, 7 W) were generated by 1 kHz Ti:Sapphire regenerative amplifier (Astrella, Coherent, USA). 40% of the fundamental pulses (7 W) were used to pump the commercial collinear optical parametric amplifier (TOPAS Prime, Light-Conversion, Lithuania) for generating a tunable wavelength pump pulse to 400 nm or 800 nm. The pump beam is chopped at 500 Hz. 15% of the fundamental pulse was routed onto a mechanical delay stage (~7 ns) and passed through a sapphire crystal to generate supercontinuum probe light (450–750 nm) and a YAG crystal to generate NIR supercontinuum probe light (800–1200 nm). The pump light and probe light were focused on the same spot (2 mm diameter) of the thin films on the quartz substrate. Data analysis is performed by Surface Xplorer software. The incident power is measured with a calibrated laser power meter. The cross-correlation function (or instrument response function (IRF)) of the photoexcitation pump and probe was determined to be ~90 fs as measured by fitting the cross-phase modulation (XPM) of two beams on the quartz substrate at the place of the sample.

Pump–push–probe (PPP) transient absorption spectroscopy

The PPP-Transient absorption measurement was also conducted on the same commercial pump–push–probe femtosecond transient absorption (TA) spectrometer Helios (Ultrafast System, USA) as PP-TA. The rest 45% of the fundamental pulses (7 W) is used to pump another commercial collinear optical parametric amplifier (TOPAS Prime, Light-Conversion LLC, Lithuania) for generating a 2000 nm wavelength push pulse. The push beam was delayed relative to the pump beam by a commercial delay stage. The measurements were carried out under a pump fluence of 2.5 μJ cm−2 and push fluence of 50 μJ cm−2.

Steady-state electroabsorption (EA) spectroscopy

The electroabsorption (EA) spectrum was measured using a home-built setup1. Light from a 150 W tungsten lamp passed through a monochromator to produce a beam with varying wavelengths, which was then focused on the sample with electrode coverage. The transmitted light intensity (T) was detected by a photodetector connected to a sourcemeter (Keithley 2450) through a 10 kΩ resistor. To measure the electric-field-induced change in transmission, a sine wave electric field from a signal generator was applied to the sample electrodes. The change in transmission intensity (ΔT) was detected by the photodetector, amplified 10 times by a preamplifier (SR 560), and then processed using a lock-in amplifier (SR 510). The EA signal was calculated as the ratio of ΔT to T.

Grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering and grazing incidence small-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS and GISAXS)

2D GISAXS and GIWAXS measurement was performed using an XEUSS 3.0 UHR SAXS and WAXS system (XENOCS, France), equipped with an Eiger2 R 1 M 2-dimensional detector with 0.075 mm × 0.075 mm pixel resolution in integration mode. The sample-to-detector distance is set to 100 mm for GIWAXS and 2000 mm for GISAXS measurement. The precise sample-to-detector distance was determined by a silver behenate standard. The measurements employed Cu Kα radiation (8.045 keV, 0.1541 Å) with beam spot sizes of 0.9 mm × 0.9 mm for GIWAXS and 0.5 mm × 0.5 mm for GISAXS to ensure adequate q-range coverage. For GIWAXS/GISAXS, the incident angle was fixed at 0.2°, which generates an evanescent wave to probe the film’s surface without significant substrate interference. The optimal incident angle was identified by maximizing the scattering intensity from the active layer while minimizing contributions from underlying layers. 1D GIWAXS patterns were corrected to account for the missing wedge effect, accurately representing the qr and qz axes. The samples for the GIWAXS and GISAXS test were prepared by spin-coating active layer solutions onto 10 mm × 10 mm silicon wafers using the same conditions as those used for device fabrication. Photo-induced force microscope (PiFM)

PiFM was performed using a VistaScope system (Molecular Vista Inc.). A pulsed quantum cascade laser (Block Engineering) with a gap-free narrowband tunable wavenumber of 760–1950 cm−1 was used for PiFM measurement. The linewidth of the laser is typically about 2 cm−1 with a resolution of 0.5 cm−1. PiFM utilizes mechanical detection rather than optical detection to measure the near-field optical interaction between the AFM cantilever tip and the sample. The photo-induced force is extracted from the oscillation properties of the cantilever. This enables the generation of photo-induced force maps, providing detailed information about the local optical properties of the material at a high spatial resolution. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy was performed on a Bruker Vertex 70V. PBDB-T, PBDB-TR, Y-O6, and Y-O6-F were measured to identify their specific wavenumber for the following PiFM measurements.

Density functional theory (DFT)

Gaussian 16 (Revision C.02) code3 was used to perform DFT calculations at the non-empirically tuned B3LYP-D3(BJ)/TZVP level of theory. The side chains of the molecule were modeled as methyl groups to reduce computational cost; while the nature of the side chains is an important factor controlling the molecule packing in the solid state, it has only a marginal influence on the intrinsic electronic and optical properties of the pi-conjugated backbones. In the calculation of the HOMO and LUMO energy levels, the single-point energy was calculated under B3LYP-D3(BJ)/Def2TZVP level for high-precision calculations. The wavefunction software Multiwfn4 and VMD5 were used for analyzing the Independent Gradient Model based on Hirshfeld partition (IGMH)6.

Molecular dynamics (MD)

For MD simulations, a spherical cut-off of 1.2 nm for the summation of van der Waals interactions and short-range Coulomb interactions, and the particle-mesh Ewald solver for long-range Coulomb interactions were used throughout. The simulations were carried out with three-dimensional periodic boundary conditions using the leapfrog integrator with a time step of 1.0 fs. The electrostatic potential was fitted using Restrained Electro Static Potential charges. The velocity rescaling thermostat and Berendsen barostat under the constant-pressure,constant-temperature (NPT) ensemble were applied to control the temperature and pressure, respectively7,8. The thin films blend was built and imitated using Packmol code9: (1) randomly placing molecules in a large box (20 20 × 20 nm3) to generate an initial geometry; (2) 10 ns of relaxation at 300 K and 1 bar to minimize energy; (3) 20 ns of simulation at 300 K and 1 bar to equilibrium; (4) 20 ns of phase production at 300 K and 1 bar.
