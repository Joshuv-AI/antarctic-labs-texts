Exploring p-Type Contact for Monolayer WS2 FETs Using Halogen Doping and Intermediate Layers
Authors: D Sharda Devi, Nihar R Mohapatra
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis work presents a density functional theory (DFT) study of substitutional and adsorption-based halogen (I or F) doping of WS2-based transistors to enhance their contact properties. Substitutional doping of the WS2 monolayer with halogens results in n-type behavior, while halogen adsorption on the surface of the WS2 monolayer induces p-type behavior. This is attributed to differing directions of charge flow, as supported by the Mulliken analysis. However, due to Fermi-level pinning (FLP) at the WS2-metal interface, the p-type behavior resulting from halogen adsorption is not very prominent. To achieve better p-type contact, intermediate layers of graphene and h-BN are used to mitigate the FLP effect, showing significant improvement. The F-adsorbed WS2-graphene-Pt interface demonstrates excellent p-type contact with a substantial reduction in the hole Schottky barrier height, making it ideal for efficient WS2-based p-type MOS transistors in CMOS technology.

Introduction

In recent times, transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) have garnered significant attention in the semiconductor industry due to their exceptional electronic properties and atomically thin structures. Among these materials, WS2 is particularly noteworthy for exhibiting the highest carrier mobility at room temperature.1,2 Metal oxide semiconductor (MOS) transistors fabricated with WS2 have demonstrated superior electrostatics and device performance.3 However, a major challenge with WS2 is the substantial Schottky barrier height (SBH),4,5 which hinders the achievement of an ideal ohmic metal–semiconductor interface for WS2 MOS transistors. Various strategies have been investigated to mitigate the SBH issue in TMDs through the optimization of metal contacts.6−13 Unfortunately, these efforts have not been successful, leading to the exploration of innovative techniques such as interface geometry engineering, edge contacts,14−16 phase engineering,17,18 and the integration of thin insulating layers.19−21 Although edge contacts can significantly reduce contact resistance, their application in thin 2D materials is limited by the restricted contact area.22

Over the years, numerous methods have been examined to refine the metal-TMD interface, but these have yielded limited success. In traditional bulk CMOS technologies, doping is used to convert the Schottky contacts into ohmic contacts. However, conventional doping techniques such as diffusion or ion implantation are unsuitable for atomically thin 2D semiconductors like WS2. Molecular doping, a technique adopted by industry, offers a promising solution. Yang et al.4 demonstrated successful chlorine (Cl) doping, achieving contact resistance (Rc) values of 700 and 500 Ω μm for MoS2 and WS2, respectively. Ab initio (DFT) simulations were employed to investigate the underlying physics of this observation.23 Despite these efforts, Rc has yet to reach the quantum limit (Rc ≈ h/(2e2kF) = 0.026/ = 30 Ω μm).14,24 Therefore, there remains significant room for improvement in Rc through the use of an appropriate dopant. Notably, bromine (Br) and iodine (I) belong to the halogen group and are positioned adjacent to Cl in the periodic table. Their larger atomic size suggests the potential for further improvement in SBH, with iodine being a more suitable dopant compared to bromine, given bromine’s oxidizing and toxic nature.

When molecular doping is discussed in 2D materials, doping can ideally occur through substitution or adsorption, as 2D materials are highly sensitive to their environment. Adsorption can take place on a pristine surface or above a sulfur vacancy, with sulfur vacancies being thermodynamically more stable compared to tungsten vacancies. Numerous adsorption studies have been conducted on both MoS2 and WS2 with various entities such as gaseous molecules, transition metals, hydrogen, graphene, etc.25−29 However, the analyses of substitution and adsorption have not been presented together in a comprehensive manner. This work uses a unified computational setup to provide a comparative understanding of iodine substitution doping and adsorption on a WS2 monolayer.

Iodine substitution has already been demonstrated as an efficient n-type dopant compared to chlorine by Santhia et al.30 It was also observed that when MoS2 and WSe2 FETs operate in an iodine gas-filled environment, they show p-type behavior.31,32 This prompted us to investigate the physics of iodine adsorption on pristine WS2 and at sulfur vacancies, and to compare these effects to substitutional doping. It is observed that iodine atom substitution and adsorption over sulfur vacancies result in n-type doping, whereas adsorption on pristine WS2 results in p-type behavior due to charge flow in different directions. However, due to Fermi-level pinning, attaining p-type behavior after interaction with metals is very challenging in WS2.33,34

Achieving efficient p-type contacts is essential for designing an effective PMOS transistor, as both n- and p-type MOS transistors are equally important in CMOS technology. In the literature, different techniques have been used to improve the p-type contacts in WS2 transistors, including the use of semimetal contacts like NiTe2 or ferromagnetic contacts such as VS2,34,35 grown directly over the WS2 layer through vdW epitaxy. While these methods are effective, they utilize non-CMOS-compatible materials. Our objective is to investigate ways to enhance the widely used Au-WS2 contact for p-type conduction, aiming to facilitate CMOS technology fabrication. This approach enables the design of both p- and n-type MOS transistors using the same channel and contact materials. Additionally, various reports could be found in the literature where dopants such as nitrogen atoms,36,37 WOx,38 and CH39 in the channel region are used to enhance p-type conduction. However, when doped WS2 forms contact with metals, due to Fermi-level pinning, the doped WS2 loses its effect; intermediate layers have been introduced in the present work to handle this issue. High-work function metals have been used with these intermediate layers to further enhance the performance of p-type MOS transistors.

This research article addresses the problem of p-type contact for WS2 and provides an efficient p-type contact using halogen adsorption with intermediate layers for which the electronic properties of the halogen adsorbed WS2 monolayer, the influence of adsorption on contact properties of WS2–Au interface, and effect of intermediate layers have been explored. Finally, the article concludes by summarizing the significant findings of the present study.

Computational Methods

The simulations are conducted using the Quantum Atomistic Toolkit (Quantum ATK),40 employing the Generalized Gradient Approximation (GGA) with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) semilocal exchange-correlation functional for electronic structure calculations.41 Electronic properties are determined using the Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals (LCAO) basis set by solving the Kohn–Sham equations. To achieve higher accuracy and efficiency, Pseudodojo pseudopotential is used, which includes multiple projectors, semicore states, and nonlinear core correction. A mesh cutoff of 45 hartree is set, with K-point sampling of 3 × 3 × 1. For atomic-position optimization, the force per atom threshold and stress error tolerance are set at 0.01 eV/Å and 0.1 GPa, respectively. Prior to electronic-structure calculations, all systems are optimized while keeping the lattice parameters constant. Additionally, to address noncovalent interactions, the GGA functional is complemented with a damped van der Waals (vdW) correction using the DFT-D2 method developed by Grimme for improved computational efficiency.42 This vdW method accounts for long-range dispersion interactions, providing a more comprehensive treatment of the vdW forces in the system.

Each simulation cell is created by expanding the monolayer WS2 into a 4 × 4 supercell with lattice parameters of at least 10 Å in length. A buffer vacuum space of at least 20 Å is included along the nonperiodic z-axis to prevent unintended interactions between adjacent unit structures. To maintain the metallic properties of the metal crystals, 5 layers of metal are chosen to interface with the monolayer WS2. The first three layers of metals, along with the monolayer WS2, are left free for optimization, while the last 2 metal layers are kept fixed to prevent interactions with the vacuum and surface reconstruction. Due to the difference in lattice parameters between the metals and WS2, lattice strain is introduced into the combined system. Ultimately, the constructed combined supercell had a lattice strain of less than 2% to balance computational efficiency and accuracy. This methodology is partially motivated by the computational method employed in the study of metal-TMD contacts by Kang et al.15

In designing these combined supercells, Au(111) and Pt(111) cleaved surfaces are used as they provide minimal lattice strain. Additionally, combined supercells of WS2 with graphene and h-BN intermediate layers are all designed with strain <2%. The WS2 monolayer is kept stress-free in all interface structures. In the doped WS2, adjacent dopant sites are kept sufficiently far apart (more than 10 Å), reducing mutual interaction and preventing the formation of artificial dopant clusters, i.e., 1 dopant per 4 × 4 supercell. This arrangement enhances stability and guards against lattice distortion. In the case of substitution, the dopant has been substituted in place of the sulfur atom, whereas in adsorption the dopants have been adsorbed over the sulfur atom as it is the most stable position. However, it is important to note that opting for a greater inter-dopant distance or a supercell with lower doping concentration could lead to the creation of a substantially larger commensurate metal–semiconductor supercell, making them impractical for simulation.

Results and Discussion

Electronic Properties of the Adsorbed and Doped WS2 Monolayer

Figure 1 displays the ball and stick model of the WS2 monolayer with (a) I atom adsorption and (b) substitutional doping of an iodine (I) atom by replacing a sulfur (S) atom. We have used a single dopant per 4 × 4 supercell to maintain uniform doping density in the case of both adsorption and substitution. However, iodine is more stable in the I2 form compared to the I atom; hence, a comparative analysis of I2 and I atom adsorption has been depicted in Supplementary Figure S1, where it is observed that I2-adsorption also provides a weaker p-type behavior compared to I atom adsorption.

Optimized structure of (a) I-adsorbed WS2 monolayer and (b) I-doped WS2 monolayer. The yellow-colored points represent the sulfur (S) atoms, blue represents the tungsten (W) atoms, and purple color is for iodine(I) atoms. The doped I atoms are encircled by red lines.

Figures 2 and 3 show the projected density of states (PDOS) and band structure with a Fermi level alignment for I-adsorbed and I-doped WS2 monolayers, respectively. From the PDOS and band structure, it can be observed that when an I atom is adsorbed on the pristine WS2 monolayer, it exhibits p-type behavior as the Fermi level is closer to the valence band (VB). Conversely, when the same I atom substitutes for an S atom or adsorbs over an S-vacancy, the Fermi level lies closer to the conduction band (CB), indicating n-type behavior. A similar doping analysis has been performed for different TMDs; the results have been included in Supplementary Table S1, where all TMDs show n-type behavior with I-doping and I-adsorption shows p-type behavior. This behavior can be attributed to the different natures of I atom interactions. When an I atom is adsorbed on pristine WS2, it acts as an electronegative atom with seven valence electrons, requiring only one electron to complete its octet and achieve stability. Consequently, it draws electrons from the WS2 monolayer, resulting in p-type behavior. In contrast, when the I atom substitutes an S atom, the extra electron of the I atom, which has seven valence electrons compared to the six of the S atom, does not participate in bond formation. Instead, the I atom loses its charge to the WS2 monolayer, displaying electropositive behavior. Similarly, in the case of I-adsorption over an S-vacancy, the I atom interacts with the WS2 vacancy and loses its charge. This explanation is supported by the Mulliken analysis results for doped WS2 monolayers, as shown in Table 1, which represents the outer shell charge on each atom.

In contrast, for the I-adsorbed case, the charge on the I atom increases while the charge on the S atom decreases, converting the doped WS2 monolayer to p-type. This highlights the different behavior of the I atom in adsorption compared to substitution. Since all these values are negative, both adsorption and substitutional doping are feasible methods to tune the electronic properties of the WS2 monolayer. Given that substitutional doping and adsorption of I atom on S-vacancy exhibit similar behavior, only substitutional doping and adsorption on pristine WS2 have been explored further due to their contrasting effects.

These results can be attributed to the interaction of Au layers with the WS2 monolayer, leading to the formation of metal-induced gap states (MIGS) that trap charges and prevent significant shifts in Fermi levels upon doping, thereby causing Fermi level pinning. Consequently, even after I-adsorption, the interface fails to exhibit a p-type behavior. To mitigate the Fermi level pinning issue, intermediate layers have been introduced between the Au layers and WS2 monolayer to shield the effect of Au layers on the WS2 monolayer and preserve the p-type behavior.

Intermediate Layer Analysis

To screen the effect of Au-layers, a graphene (Gra) monolayer is used as an intermediate layer between Au-layers and the WS2 monolayer, as depicted in Figure 8. From Figure 9, it is clearly evident that the interaction of graphene monolayer with doped WS2 does not change the doping behavior, i.e., the I-doped Gra-WS2 interface shows n-type behavior whereas the I-adsorbed Gra-WS2 interface shows p-type behavior. The Fermi level of WS2-Gra-Au has been depicted in Supplementary Figure S3, where it can be observed that the WS2-Gra-Au interface provides an n-type behavior. Figure 10 illustrates the PDOS for I-adsorbed and I-doped WS2-Gra-Au interfaces. Here, the Iads-WS2-Gra-Au interface displays p-type behavior, with the Fermi level closer to the Valence Band (VB), while Idop-WS2-Gra-Au shows n-type behavior, with the Fermi level closer to the conduction band (CB). This confirms that the graphene monolayer effectively screens the effects of Au-layers. Figure 11 demonstrates a noticeable reduction in electron density at the I-adsorbed WS2–Au interface upon insertion of the graphene layer, indicating an enhanced p-type performance. Additionally, Figure 12 shows an increase in the effective potential for the graphene-inserted I-adsorbed WS2–Au interface, suggesting a reduced barrier for holes. Figure 13 reveals a significant downward shift (471 meV) in the Fermi level of the I-adsorbed WS2–Au interface after graphene monolayer insertion, indicative of a reduced hole Schottky barrier height. Moreover, the Fermi level of the I-adsorbed WS2-Gra-Au lies below the intrinsic Fermi level of monolayer WS2, establishing the interface as p-type.

Exploring Halogen Adsorption with High Work-Function Metal

Up to this point, all of the explored interfaces have utilized Au-layers. However, considering the higher work function of metals like platinum (Pt), according to the Schottky–Mott rule, we would expect to observe p-type behavior. Unfortunately, due to Fermi-level pinning, achieving this has been challenging. In this section, we investigate the impact of halogen adsorption on WS2–Pt interfaces and explore the modification of these interfaces using the graphene intermediate layer to enhance p-type behavior.

The Fermi level of the WS2–Pt interface is at −4.588 eV, which is above the intrinsic level of the WS2 monolayer. However, with I adsorption, the Fermi level shifts below the intrinsic level, as shown in Figure 15. From Figure 15, it can also be observed that the insertion of a graphene layer shows significant improvement, especially with F-adsorption. To provide a better understanding, Table 5 summarizes the Fermi levels of the different interfaces. As shown, the Pt metal causes a downward shift in the Fermi level, with the best results obtained for the F-adsorbed WS2-Gra-Pt interface. The F-adsorbed WS2-Gra-Pt interface demonstrated a 917 meV Fermi level shift toward the valence band (VB) compared to the I-adsorbed WS2–Au interface, resulting in a minimal hole Schottky barrier height. This result may be further improved by using multilayer graphene.

Conclusions

To summarize, this study offered valuable insights into utilizing substitutional and adsorption-based doping of halogens (I or F) to enhance the contact properties of WS2-based transistors. We identified distinct doping characteristics with different techniques: substitutional doping of halogens yielded n-type behavior, while adsorption of halogens on WS2 resulted in p-type behavior. Given the need for better p-type contacts, we focused our study on halogen adsorption. However, achieving consistent p-type behavior through halogen adsorption proved challenging due to Fermi-level pinning at the WS2-metal interface. To address this issue, we introduced graphene and h-BN intermediate layers, which effectively depinned the Fermi level and significantly enhanced p-type behavior. The F-adsorbed WS2-Gra-Pt interface exhibited excellent p-type contact with a substantial reduction in the hole Schottky barrier height, making it ideal for efficient WS2-based PMOS transistors in CMOS technology. This approach holds great promise for obtaining p-type MOS transistors in 2D materials, which has been a significant challenge for the electronics community.
