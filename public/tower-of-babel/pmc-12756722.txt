Fluid Jet Polishing of Glass Surfaces to Achieve Nanoroughness for Dust Mitigation of Optical Systems
Authors: Ryudai Nitano, Shuntaro Yamato, Nobuya Mineyuki, Kohei Yasuda, Masato Adachi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Optical components and devices, such as camera lenses, thermally controlled optical surfaces, and solar panels, are often exposed to dusty environments, where particulate matter adheres to their surfaces, degrading their optical performance. To address this challenge, we employed a fluid jet polishing (FJP) technique to fabricate nanoroughness on the glass surfaces of exterior elements, mitigating dust adhesion without compromising their high optical qualities. FJP successfully produced various nanoroughness levels by varying the sizes of abrasive particles. The adhesion forces of fine particles at different locations on glass surfaces were quantitatively measured by using atomic force microscopy. The results revealed that the forces were reduced by more than 1 order of magnitude on processed surfaces with increasing local surface roughness, primarily owing to the decreased effective contact area and separation distance, which weakened the van der Waals interactions and electrostatic forces. Furthermore, dust deposition tests using lunar regolith simulant particles with irregular shapes on the processed glass demonstrated the dust mitigation effect, particularly for substrates processed with fine abrasives with a median diameter of 2 μm. The optical properties of the processed glass substrates were also evaluated in terms of haze and transmittance. While some surfaces processed with coarse abrasives exhibited increased haze and reduced transmittance owing to light scattering, substrates processed with fine abrasives maintained high visible transmittance and low haze. The superior optical performance was attributed to the antireflective effects induced by smooth refractive index gradients realized by finely processed glass surfaces, along with effective dust mitigation. FJP offers a flexible and scalable approach for tailoring the surface structure of glass materials, providing practical dust mitigation solutions for advanced optical applications.

Introduction

Optical systems, such as solar panels, camera lenses, and thermal control optical surfaces, are widely used in a broad range of applications, from industrial systems to advanced space exploration missions. These systems are often operated in external environments and are exposed to dusty conditions, where particulate matter adheres to their surfaces, significantly impairing their optical performance. For instance, in arid regions, airflows can lift dust particles, and the airborne particles accumulate on the surfaces of photovoltaic modules, obstructing light transmission and causing a considerable reduction in power generation efficiency. − Similarly, regolith particles present on the surface of the Moon and Mars are easily lofted during rocket landing/launching and extravehicular operations in crewed and uncrewed missions because of the low-gravity environment, and they readily adhere to the surfaces of equipment, severely degrading the functionality of optical systems and thermal control optical surfaces. − In such dusty and harsh environments, frequent manual cleaning opportunities and the use of pneumatic cleaning methods are limited as water and air are indispensable resources. Therefore, the development of dust-proof and dust-removal technologies is essential, and their applicability to exterior glass materials is critical.

Effective dust mitigation technologies that do not rely on gas or liquid are generally categorized into two types. The first type involves active methods that remove dust particles by applying external forces, such as vibrations and electrostatic forces. − Although brushing methods in manual and robotic operations are available, they cannot avoid mechanical abrasion on glass surfaces, which is challenging for long-term operations. In addition, the robotic systems themselves require cleaning and maintenance. The second type is passive methods, which reduce particle adhesion by introducing micro/nano-scale structures or coatings on material surfaces. , While active methods require additional cleaning mechanisms, passive methods offer dust deposition reduction simply through surface treatment. In addition, passive methods can be combined with active systems, potentially yielding synergistic improvements in the dust removal performance.

Effective dust mitigation in both scenarios largely relies on controlling the adhesion forces between the fine particles and glass surfaces. For instance, surface modification techniques can reduce the interaction forces between particles and target materials, such as van der Waals, electrostatic, and capillary forces, by altering the effective distance and surface energy at the contact interface between materials. − One promising method is the fabrication of surface nanoroughness, which can effectively reduce van der Waals interactions and adhesion forces. − For example, aluminum surfaces prepared using etching methods exhibited a clear reduction in adhesion force for lunar regolith simulant particles, and this approach has been widely adopted for metallic materials in industrial settings. In addition, the relationship between surface roughness and particle adhesion forces has been extensively studied using atomic force microscopy (AFM), − and several contact mechanics models have been proposed. −

However, the implementation of surface nanoroughness on brittle glass substrates, which are challenging to process, and its influence on dust mitigation have not been thoroughly investigated. Chemical etching processes and coatings have been demonstrated as surface treatments for glass substrates, offering highly precise and uniformly finished surfaces. However, chemical processing often requires specialized equipment and facilities, and the durability of coatings is a concern for long-term use. In addition, surface blasting is an alternative method for fabricating nanoroughness on glass surfaces. Blasting methods are cost-effective and applicable to large areas and nonplanar surfaces for processing the surfaces of various materials. Among these methods, fluid jet polishing (FJP) has gained increasing attention for surface treatment. , FJP can handle finer abrasive particles as a slurry, which is ejected through a nozzle to polish the surface, allowing for precision processing. , Although previous studies have aimed to apply FJP for surface polishing, its applications in producing surface nanoroughness and dust mitigation have been insufficiently explored.

In this study, the FJP process was applied to fabricate surface nanoroughness on glass substrates, and the practical effects on dust mitigation were evaluated in terms of both the reduction of adhesion forces and the influence on the optical properties of the glass materials. In the following sections, we begin by briefly describing the FJP setup, processing protocol, and multiple evaluation methods used for the processed glass substrates. Subsequently, the experimental results are presented, categorized according to the surface morphology of glass substrates processed under various conditions and the effects on the adhesion forces of spherical fine glass beads and irregularly shaped lunar regolith simulants, followed by dust deposition tests and optical measurements. As the surface modification of glass likely affects the optical properties of glass materials, such as transmittance and haze, ,− the effects on both dust mitigation and optical properties were evaluated. These results and the discussion provide deeper insights into the potential of FJP for effective dust mitigation and support the development of practical surface design strategies for future optical systems.

Experimental Section

Nanoroughness Formation on Glass

Soda-lime glass plates were used as the substrate material. Substrates were prepared in two sizes: 10 × 10 mm2 for AFM measurements and 20 × 20 mm2 for particle deposition tests and optical measurements, with a thickness of 1 mm for both cases. The glass surfaces were processed by using a custom-built FJP setup, as shown in Figure a. A processing slurry was prepared by dispersing Al2O3 abrasive particles (FUJIMI, White Fused Alumina) in water at a concentration of 40 g/L, and it was ejected vertically onto a substrate through a nozzle under a pressure regulated by a pump. The jetted slurry was continuously recirculated throughout the process. The nozzle position and feed rate were controlled using a three-axis computer numerical control machine, and the substrates were processed by the jet along a predefined toolpath, as illustrated in Figure b. The abrasive particle sizes and jet pressures were varied as the processing parameters. The processed substrates were cleaned in an ultrasonic cleaner by using an alkaline cleaning solution (consisting of NaOH and KOH) to remove residual abrasives and glass fragments adhered to their surfaces. The median particle size of the abrasives, pressure, nozzle feed rate, toolpath, nozzle diameter, stand-off distance, and processing area are listed in Table . The surface profiles and root-mean-square (RMS) roughness of both the processed and unprocessed substrates were measured over an area of 50 × 50 μm2 using a three-dimensional noncontact optical profiler (Zygo, NewView8300). For the substrate processed at a pressure of 0.8 MPa, which was used for adhesion force measurements, the surface profiles were also obtained over an area of 30 × 30 μm2 by using AFM (Shimadzu, SPM-9700HT). Each measurement was performed at three different locations on each substrate.

Adhesion Force Measurement

Figure illustrates a schematic of the simultaneous measurement of adhesion force distributions and localized RMS roughness using AFM. Three types of AFM cantilevers were fabricated by attaching different particles to a tipless cantilever (Nanosensors, TL-FM; length: 225 μm, spring constant: 2.8 N/m) using an epoxy resin. The particles used were (1) a spherical soda-lime glass particle with a diameter of 3 μm (AS ONE, soda-lime glass), (2) a lunar regolith simulant particle with a diameter of approximately 10 μm (Shimizu Corporation, FJS-1), and (3) a spherical soda-lime glass particle with a diameter of 10 μm, comparable in size to the regolith simulant. The regolith simulant particles are produced to resemble the properties of actual lunar regolith. The particle of type (1) was primarily used for the adhesion force measurements of glass particles. The lunar regolith simulant has a broad particle size distribution and is not manufactured to have a specific diameter. Therefore, type (2) particles that could be manually handled were selectively picked and used for the adhesion force measurements of the simulant. To account for variations in the contact geometry caused by the irregular shapes of the regolith simulant particles, three cantilevers were fabricated using different type (2) particles. Adhesion force measurements were conducted with each cantilever to obtain more representative values, reflecting the effects of particle shape irregularity. Because the size of the selected type (2) particle was larger than that of the type (1) glass particle, types (2) and (3) were used to compare the adhesion forces between glass and simulant particles.

Schematic of the measurement of the particle adhesion force and the localized RMS roughness using AFM: (a) adhesion force measurement from the force–displacement curve; and (b) simultaneous measurement of the distributions of particle adhesion force and localized RMS roughness.

Adhesion forces were measured by acquiring force–displacement curves in the contact mode of AFM operation, as shown in Figure a. As a particle at the cantilever tip approached and contacted the substrate surface, deflection of the cantilever was detected by the photodetector, which provided the corresponding force curve. The applied load (operating point) during contact was set to 0.5 V, corresponding to approximately 100 nN, and the scanning rate was 10 Hz. The adhesion force was determined from the attractive force acting on the cantilever as the probe was retracted from the substrate. These adhesion force measurements were performed over a 500 × 500 nm2 scan area with a resolution of 128 × 128 data points, enabling the acquisition of the spatial adhesion force distribution. During the measurement of the adhesion force at a single contact, the relative height at each contact point was also recorded. Adhesion force measurements over the substrate surface enabled the simultaneous acquisition of the surface profile in the same region. However, because the self-made cantilever tip could not access fine or recessed surface features, the measured height variation was reduced compared with the actual height profile. The obtained surface profiles were scaled using the RMS roughness information measured as a reference using a probe with a sharp tip (BudgetSensors, Tap190DLC; tip radius: 15 nm). Some information about fine or recessed surface features was inevitably lost in this measurement method, which is a limitation of this approach. Nevertheless, the contact surface information, as seen from the particle’s perspective, appeared to have been reasonably captured in the adhesion force measurements.

The 128 × 128 data points of the adhesion forces and surface profiles were divided into local subregions of 16 × 16, as shown in Figure b. The size of subregions was defined using the contact radius a calculated from Hertzian contact theory, as described in eq : 1 a=3RF4(1−ν12E1+1−ν22E2)3

where R is the particle radius (1.5 μm), F is the applied load, ν is Poisson’s ratio (0.23 for both the particle and the substrate), and E is Young’s modulus (71.6 GPa for both particles and substrates). Although Hertzian theory neglects surface roughness and adhesion interactions, it provides a reasonable estimation of the effective contact area between a spherical particle and a smooth glass surface under an applied load. The effective contact area was approximately 31 × 31 nm2. For each subregion, the locally averaged adhesion force and local RMS roughness were calculated to evaluate the influence of the effective local roughness (but not the average roughness on the entire surface) on the adhesion force, as shown in Figure b.

When the cantilever with the regolith simulant particle was used, the irregular shape of the tip significantly disturbed the measurements of the surface profiles. Therefore, the above-mentioned simultaneous measurement of adhesion force distributions and localized RMS roughness could not be performed for the regolith particles. In the cases of the regolith simulant particles (type (2)) as well as the glass particle (type (3)) with a diameter comparable to that of the simulant, adhesion forces were measured at 16 × 16 points within a larger 5 × 5 μm2 scanning area at three different locations per substrate. The average adhesion forces were then evaluated with respect to the RMS roughness over a larger 30 × 30 μm2 area, which was more accurately measured by using a sharp-tipped AFM probe.

All adhesion measurements were conducted at room temperature and atmospheric pressure with the relative humidity controlled at 40–50% using a humidifier (KITZ Microfilter, AHCU-1), except when investigating the effects of humidity. In these cases, the humidity varied below 50% owing to the inability of the humidifier to control higher humidity conditions.

Dust Deposition Test

Dust deposition tests were conducted using regolith simulant particles with sizes of less than 25 μm. Figure shows a schematic of the experimental procedure for the dust deposition test. The particles were carefully deposited only onto the substrate area of 16 × 16 mm2, where surface nanoroughness was formed, using a mask and a sieve as evenly as possible, and the loading weight was measured. Subsequently, the substrate was carefully inverted, left undisturbed in the inverted position for a short period, and then returned to its original orientation. Particles and the aggregates with smaller adhesion forces to the substrate were slipped and dropped from it. The remaining amount of particles was measured, and the weight ratio before and after inversion was evaluated.

Schematic of the experimental procedure for the dust deposition test.

Optical Measurements

The transmittance of the substrates was measured over the ultraviolet–visible to near-infrared wavelength range (240–2000 nm) using a spectrophotometer (Hitachi High-Tech, UH4150), and over the near- to far-infrared range (2000–20000 nm) using a Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer, Spectrum 3). Additionally, the haze values of the glass substrates were measured using a haze meter (NIPPON DENSHOKU INDUSTRIES, NDH7000), which is defined as the ratio of transmitted light that is scattered at angles greater than 2.5° from the incident direction.

Results and Discussion

Surface Morphology of Processed Glass

Figure presents the RMS roughness of the glass substrates processed under various process parameters, as measured by using the three-dimensional optical profiler. The RMS roughness of an unprocessed substrate is also included in Figure a for comparison. The unprocessed substrate exhibited an extremely smooth surface with subnanometer roughness. In contrast, the surface roughness of the processed substrates increased significantly with an increase in the abrasive particle size. This indicates that variation of the abrasive size enables the formation of distinct surface roughness levels on glass substrates. One of the advantages of FJP is its ability to utilize fine abrasives in a slurry, allowing for precise control of roughness at the nanometer scale level.

RMS roughness of FJP-processed glass substrates measured over a 50 × 50 μm2 area using a three-dimensional optical profiler: (a) RMS roughness under different processing pressures and abrasive sizes; and (b) 2D contour plot showing the combined effect of these parameters.

Furthermore, the surface roughness gradually increased with increasing jetting pressure for most of the abrasive cases. This trend can be attributed to the processing efficiency of FJP, which is influenced by the kinetic energy of abrasive flow. Previous studies have shown that higher jet pressures lead to increased slurry velocities, resulting in a higher processing rate. , In addition, the use of larger abrasive particles enhances the processing rate owing to their greater mass and momentum. , In this study, varying the abrasive particle size enabled a broader and more effective modulation of the surface roughness compared to adjusting the jet pressure, as shown in Figure b. This is due to variation in the kinetic energy of the abrasive flow, which scales with the cube of the abrasive particle diameter. Because this study aimed to clarify the fundamental effects of surface nanostructures on dust mitigation, glass substrates with markedly different surface roughness levels, which were achieved by varying the abrasive size at a constant jet pressure of 0.8 MPa, were used in the following measurements. Nevertheless, adjusting the jet pressure remains a practical approach for fine-tuning the surface roughness.

Figure shows the surface profiles of both the unprocessed and processed substrates measured by using AFM with the sharp-tipped cantilever. Although AFM can provide higher resolution profiles than optical profilers, the RMS roughness was consistent between the two measurements. As shown in Figure , the acquired morphologies by AFM revealed that both the vertical height variation and lateral periodicity of the surface roughness increased with increasing abrasive particle size. In addition, as the abrasive particle size increased, surface irregularities became more pronounced. When surface measurements are performed over a limited area using AFM, it may be difficult to evaluate the overall surface condition, particularly when significant nonuniformity is present, as compared to methods that can assess a larger surface area, such as an optical profiler. Therefore, combining multiple surface measurements is suitable for evaluating surface nanoroughness.

Surface topography of glass substrates over a 30 × 30 μm2 area measured by AFM: (a) unprocessed substrate; (b–e) FJP-processed substrates with different abrasive particle sizes under a constant pressure of 0.8 MPa: abrasives of (b) 2 μm, (c) 8 μm, (d) 18 μm, and (e) 36 μm in diameter.

Effects of Surface Nanoroughness on Adhesion Forces

Figure shows the adhesion force distributions with respect to the local RMS roughness for a spherical glass particle on both unprocessed and processed glass substrates, obtained through simultaneous measurements using AFM. In the unprocessed case, the adhesion force distribution exhibited minimal variation and the range of local surface roughness was narrow. Therefore, the unprocessed glass had a smooth and uniform surface, and the high uniformity highlighted the reproducibility of the adhesion force measurements. Compared with the unprocessed case, the adhesion force on the processed surfaces was significantly reduced by one to 2 orders of magnitude. This reduction is attributed to the decreased effective contact area and weakened van der Waals interactions between the particle and the substrate, resulting from the increased surface roughness. Additionally, the processed substrates exhibited a broader distribution of adhesion forces than the unprocessed ones. As illustrated in Figure , the contact between a spherical particle and a smooth surface ideally occurred at a single point, leading to a consistent adhesion behavior. In contrast, processed surfaces with locally nonuniform profiles resulted in multiple and variable points, increasing the variability of the effective contact area and the adhesion force.

Adhesion force distribution with respect to localized RMS roughness for the spherical glass particle on unprocessed and FJP-processed glass substrates prepared with abrasive particles of various sizes. The measurements were conducted under controlled ambient humidity (RH 40–50%).

Schematic of the contact models in a localized contact region between (a) a smooth substrate and a spherical particle and (b) nanoroughness and a spherical particle, where the contact points exhibit significant variability.

The measured adhesion forces were also compared to the theoretically calculated van der Waals force. The forces were calculated using a modified Rumpf model, as expressed in eq : 2 FvdW=AR6H02[11+R/1.48RMS+1(1+1.48RMS/H0)2] where A is the Hamaker constant (10–19J), R is the particle radius (1.5 μm), H 0 is the distance of closest approach between surfaces (0.3 nm), and RMS is the RMS roughness of the surface in contact with the particle. The calculated van der Waals forces are plotted alongside the experimental data in Figure . The observed decreasing trend in the adhesion force with increasing surface roughness fairly agreed with the theoretical prediction, to some extent. However, the measured values were generally higher than the calculated ones. In particular, for substrates processed with larger abrasive particles (18 and 36 μm), the experimental adhesion forces deviated by nearly an order of magnitude from the theoretical estimates. This significant discrepancy is likely associated with the complex surface morphology produced by coarse abrasives. As shown in the AFM surface profiles (Figure ), increasing the abrasive size led not only to greater RMS roughness but also to pronounced in-plane inhomogeneity and irregularity of surface features. Such morphological nonuniformity can locally generate broad and flattened contact areas, thereby increasing the real contact area and resulting in adhesion forces beyond the assumptions of the Rumpf model. The theoretical model typically assumes periodic and hemispherical asperities with uniform aspect ratios, which do not fully capture the complex surface profiles of FJP-processed glass. The deviation between the experimental and theoretical values therefore likely reflects the nonuniform local roughness geometries.

Furthermore, electrostatic interactions, although not directly quantified in this study, may also contribute substantially to the total adhesion force. Their magnitude can vary significantly depending on the surface charge distribution and humidity and, in some cases, may exceed several tens of nanonewtons. Even for the unprocessed substrate, discrepancies between the experimental and theoretical values persisted, suggesting that electrostatic effects likely played an important role, as well. In contrast, capillary forces arising from adsorbed water layers are expected to be modest under the experimental humidity conditions (RH 40–50%). Capillary bridges typically form between contacting surfaces when a sufficient amount of condensed water accumulates at the interface, which strongly depends on the ambient humidity and surface wettability. Previous work indicates that the formation of stable water menisci is limited at intermediate relative humidity levels, such as 40–50%, implying only minor contributions of capillary adhesion in the present case. Therefore, the deviation between the experimental and theoretical values is considered to arise from a combination of enhanced local contact geometry and possible electrostatic contributions. These results suggest that both the average roughness and its spatial uniformity are critical factors for accurately predicting and controlling the particle adhesion. Future studies should focus on developing and validating advanced adhesion models that incorporate these factors to improve quantitative agreement with experimental data.

In addition, the effects of ambient humidity were investigated. Figure shows the averaged adhesion forces of the spherical glass particles on unprocessed and FJP-processed glass substrates under different relative humidity levels ranging from 5 to 50%. When smoother surfaces were used (i.e., the unprocessed substrate and those processed with 2 μm abrasives), the adhesion forces increased significantly with decreasing humidity. These increases are attributed to the suppression of water adsorption under dry conditions, which hinders the dissipation of surface charges through conductive water films. Consequently, electrostatic forces became more dominant in low-humidity environments. This result is consistent with previous work. It should be noted that strong electrostatic contributions also depend on material conductivity and grounding conditions in AFM systems. , In contrast, for rougher surface cases, the adhesion forces remained consistently lower across all humidity levels. This indicates that the introduction of surface nanoroughness increased the effective separation distance between the particle and the substrate, thereby reducing both van der Waals interactions and electrostatic forces. These findings underscore the effectiveness of surface nanoroughness in mitigating multiple adhesion mechanisms and suggest potential applications in both terrestrial dry environments, such as arid regions, and extraterrestrial environments. Although higher humidity conditions could not be reproduced owing to the limitations of the humidifier used in this study, it is expected that the addition of surface nanoroughness would also influence capillary forces by altering the water contact angle of the glass surface and increasing the effective separation distance between contacting surfaces. Further investigations on the effects of a higher humidity should be conducted in future studies.

Adhesion forces of the spherical glass particles on unprocessed and FJP-processed glass substrates under different relative humidity conditions.

Figure shows the adhesion forces of the lunar regolith simulant particles on unprocessed and processed glass substrates. As described in Section , the adhesion forces of the simulant particle were measured using three different particles and correlated with the RMS roughness of the entire 30 × 30 μm2 scanning area. All three irregularly shaped simulant particles showed a consistent trend of decreasing adhesion force with increasing surface roughness, indicating that surface nanoroughness is effective in reducing adhesion forces for both spherical and irregular-shaped particles. Moreover, there was considerable variation in the measured adhesion forces among the three simulant particles, which was attributed to differences in the individual particle geometries that affected the actual contact area and the number of contact points with the substrate. Interestingly, the reduction in the adhesion force for the regolith simulant particles was more gradual than that observed for the spherical glass particles. This can be attributed to the inherently rough surface morphology of the simulant particles. On the unprocessed, smoother substrate, the surface roughness of the simulant particles reduced the effective contact area, resulting in lower adhesion forces than those of smooth spherical particles. However, on roughened substrates, the combination of surface asperities on both the particle and the substrate may have led to an increase in the actual contact points, thereby partially offsetting the adhesion-reducing effect of nanoroughness.

Adhesion forces of the spherical glass particle and the regolith simulant particles on unprocessed and FJP-processed glass substrates, measured under controlled ambient humidity (RH 40–50%). Both particle types had diameters of approximately 10 μm. For comparison, the gravitational forces acting on a particle with an equivalent diameter of approximately 100–300 μm are also shown, assuming that the simulant particle layer or aggregate behaves as a single large particle.

In summary, fabricating nanoroughness on glass surfaces can reduce the adhesion forces by more than 1 order of magnitude. The degree of reduction can be controlled by adjusting the surface roughness for both spherical and irregular-shaped particles.

Effects of Surface Nanoroughness on Dust Deposition

In addition to adhesion force measurements, the effects of the fabricated nanoroughness on dust mitigation were evaluated by using dust deposition tests. Figure shows the dust mitigation rates of both unprocessed and processed glass substrates under different dust loadings, and Figure presents snapshots of their surfaces after the inversion operation. The mitigation rate remained below approximately 30% for the unprocessed substrate, whereas the processed substrates exhibited a clear dust mitigation effect, as shown in Figure .

Dust mitigation rates of unprocessed and FJP-processed glass substrates at different dust loadings.

Snapshots of glass substrates after dust deposition tests with a dust loading of 100 mg.

Moreover, the dust mitigation rate increased with the dust loading. This trend can be attributed to variations in the force balance at the particle–substrate interface including gravitational and adhesion forces. As particles adhered to the substrates as layers or aggregates, the gravitational force acting on them increased with the loading. However, the adhesion force between the layer/aggregate and the substrate did not increase significantly because only the particles located near the substrate-side surface were in direct contact with the substrate. Figure also shows the calculated gravitational force acting on a larger particle with an equivalent diameter of approximately 100–300 μm, assuming that the simulant particle layer or aggregate behaves as a single large particle. As expressed in eq , the adhesion force generally scales linearly with the particle diameter, whereas the gravitational force scales with the cube of the diameter. Therefore, as the equivalent particle size increases, the applied gravitational force becomes comparable to the measured adhesion forces on individual regolith simulant particles. The simulant particles located near the substrate-side surface are thought to play a role in anchoring the layer or aggregating to the substrate. Consequently, when the equivalent gravitational force and adhesion forces acting on particles in direct contact with the substrate were balanced, the particle layer or aggregate began to detach from the substrate. This suggests that the influence of gravitational force becomes more pronounced under higher loading conditions and that surface nanoroughness contributes to reducing adhesion and facilitating the detachment of particle aggregates from the substrate.

In cases of lower dust loading, the processed substrates still showed improved dust mitigation, although the increase in the mitigation rate was modest. For unprocessed substrates, the adhesion force exceeded the gravitational force, resulting in lower mitigation rates compared with processed substrates. In practical scenarios involving low dust loading, additional driving forces are required to facilitate dust removal. In natural environments, airflow or rainfall can enhance cleaning by reducing the adhesion forces. In environments where such natural forces are absent, such as the vacuum of the Moon, additional mechanisms, such as vibration or electrostatic forces, are promising candidates for maintaining clean glass surfaces. , As shown in Figure , even the relatively weak gravitational force during inversion was sufficient to improve the mitigation rate, suggesting that additional driving forces do not need to be of a large magnitude.

Among the FJP-processed substrates, the one processed with abrasives of 2 μm in diameter exhibited the highest dust mitigation rate. This can be attributed to the reduced surface waviness, as indicated by the surface morphology shown in Figure . When pronounced waviness was present, smaller dust particles could become trapped in deeper surface valleys, which made them more difficult to remove, a phenomenon not observed in the localized adhesion force measurements by using AFM.

Evaluation of Optical Properties

The optical properties of both unprocessed and FJP-processed glass substrates were measured and evaluated in addition to the particle adhesion measurements. Figure a shows the transmittance spectra of the glass substrates across the ultraviolet to far-infrared wavelength ranges. In the ultraviolet region (below 300 nm) and far-infrared region (above 4000 nm), light was almost completely absorbed, resulting in transmittance values approaching zero, with no significant differences observed between the unprocessed and processed substrates. Similarly, in the mid-infrared region (2000–4000 nm), the transmittance stabilized at approximately 60%, again showing no notable variation owing to the FJP treatment. These results suggest that FJP processing does not affect the infrared absorption or reflection characteristics of glass substrates. In practical thermal control applications, dust deposition on glass surfaces is known to degrade radiative cooling performance. Given the improved dust mitigation properties of the processed surfaces, their thermal emission efficiency may be superior to that of unprocessed substrates.

Optical properties of unprocessed and processed glass substrates: (a) transmittance spectra over the ultraviolet to far-infrared wavelength range, (b) transmittance spectra in the visible wavelength range, and (c) average transmittance in the visible wavelength range and haze.

In the visible (300–800 nm) to near-infrared (800–2000 nm) regions, the transmittance reached approximately 90%, with slight variations depending on the surface conditions. Figure b provides a magnified view of the transmittance spectra in the visible range, and Figure c shows the average transmittance (380–780 nm) and haze values. The substrate processed with abrasives of 2 μm in diameter exhibited improved transmittance, while maintaining a haze value comparable to that of the unprocessed glass. In contrast, substrates processed with coarser abrasives showed reduced transmittance and increased haze, with the most significant degradation observed for the substrate processed with abrasives of 36 μm in diameter. These results are attributed to the balance between the two competing mechanisms introduced by the surface nanoroughness: antireflection and scattering effects. The antireflection effect arose from a gradual change in the refractive index at the air–glass interface owing to the formation of surface nanostructures. This created a conceptual intermediate layer between the air and glass, as illustrated in Figure a. The effective refractive index n eff within this intermediate region for each layer was calculated using effective medium theory, as expressed in eq : , 3 neff=fnglass2+(1−f)nair2 where f is the volume filling ratio of glass and n is the refractive index (glass: 1.5; air: 1.0). To calculate the effective refractive index profile from the AFM-measured surface morphologies shown in Figure , the height distribution of each substrate was discretized into horizontal slices with a thickness of 5 nm. For each slice, the local volume filling ratio of glass was calculated, assuming that glass occupies the region below the surface and air fills the rest of the slice, to obtain the effective refractive index for each corresponding layer. A layer thickness of 5 nm was chosen to correspond to the maximum surface roughness observed in the unprocessed substrate, ensuring that the unprocessed glass was modeled with an abrupt air–glass interface without an appreciable intermediate region. Figure b shows the effective refractive indices of the unprocessed and processed substrates. As the surface roughness increased, the refractive index gradient became more gradual for the processed substrates. According to Fresnel’s theory, a smoother refractive index gradient leads to a reduced ideal reflectance, which explains the enhanced transmittance observed in the substrate processed with fine abrasives of 2 μm in diameter.

Effective refractive index induced by surface nanoroughness: (a) schematic of an intermediate layer formed between air and glass owing to surface nanoroughness; (b) refractive index gradient of unprocessed and processed glass substrates calculated from surface morphology measurements using AFM.

In contrast, for substrates processed with coarser abrasives, the scattering effect predominated over the antireflection effect. As shown in Figure b, the effective refractive indices of the substrates processed with abrasives of 8, 18, and 36 μm diameter exhibited significant fluctuations in the height direction, indicating that the surface topography had features on a scale comparable to the wavelength of visible light. This implies that the incident light was strongly scattered rather than transmitted or antireflected, thereby increasing the haze and reducing the overall transmittance. According to previous studies, when both the height (RMS) and lateral scale (correlation length) of the roughness increase, the scattering of incident light becomes more pronounced, leading to increased haze and reduced transmittance. This behavior aligns with the principle that an effective intermediate layer, required to reduce reflection, cannot be formed when the surface roughness exceeds the wavelength of visible light. Consequently, reflection is not effectively suppressed and scattering becomes the dominant effect.

Therefore, although FJP can be employed to enhance the optical properties of glass substrates and reduce the adhesion force of sticking dust particles, optimizing the surface roughness is indispensable by adjusting the process parameters, such as abrasive size and jet pressure.

Conclusion

In this study, various surface nanoroughness levels were successfully fabricated on glass substrates using FJP by adjusting the process parameters such as abrasive particle size and jet pressure. The effects of the nanoroughness on dust mitigation and optical properties were comprehensively evaluated.

AFM-based measurements revealed that the adhesion force on the processed surfaces was reduced by more than an order of magnitude compared to that on unprocessed glass, primarily owing to the decreased effective contact area and separation distance, which weakened van der Waals interactions and electrostatic forces. This reduction in the adhesion force also contributed to the enhanced detachment of particles from the processed substrates during the inversion operations of the dust deposition tests. FJP-processed substrates were effective in preventing dust deposition and facilitating its removal. Because the removal of dust particles depends on the balance of forces acting on them, designing and implementing additional driving forces to overcome adhesion are key strategies for dust removal in the future. The FJP-induced reduction in the adhesion force helps achieve a favorable force balance, particularly by enhancing the removal of aggregated particles.

Furthermore, optical transmittance spectra and haze measurements demonstrated that while coarsely processed substrates increased light scattering and haze, surfaces processed using finer abrasives preserved high visible-light transmittance and low haze, owing to the antireflective effects arising from gradual refractive index transitions at the air–glass interface. These dual benefits of dust mitigation and optical enhancement were achieved simultaneously by optimizing the FJP processing parameters.

These findings suggest that FJP offers a versatile and scalable approach for engineering multifunctional glass surfaces. Such surfaces are highly promising for use in a wide range of optical systems that operate in dusty environments. Future studies should explore the long-term durability of FJP-treated surfaces under repeated environmental exposure and their applicability to nonplanar surfaces. For instance, perovskite solar cells, which are highly flexible and can be mounted on various nonflat geometries, represent a promising application. Even on such surfaces, dust deposition remains a challenge and may, in some cases, be more severe than that on flat substrates. Given its adaptability to complex geometries, FJP can be a viable surface treatment method. Investigating the applicability to such materials is a compelling direction for future research.
