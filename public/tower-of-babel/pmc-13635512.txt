Plasma biomarker profiling suggests renal stress and systemic inflammation in chronic pruritus of unknown origin
Authors: Kavita Vats, Joshua Wilmer, Perya Bhagchandani, Sara Khoshniyati, Yagiz Matthew Akiska, Rebecca Li, Jason Shumsky, Shruti Tyagi, Shahin Shahsavari, Deena Fayyad, Waleed Adawi, Shabnam Afzal, Marie-France Vidaver, Shivani S Patel, Madan M Kwatra, Shawn G Kwatra
Published: 2026

To the Editor: Chronic pruritus of unknown origin (CPUO) is characterized by persistent itch lasting at least 6 weeks without any dermatologic cause or significant skin eruptions, posing a major diagnostic and therapeutic challenge.1 CPUO significantly affects the quality of life, importantly previous studies have highlighted associations between chronic pruritic disorders and renal dysfunction,2,3 yet the biological mechanisms underpinning these links remain poorly defined. Molecular evidence linking immune dysregulation to early end-organ involvement in CPUO is limited. We conducted a cross-sectional study investigating circulating biomarkers of renal, hepatic, and cardiovascular injury and systemic inflammation in adults with CPUO compared to healthy controls (HCs).

Plasma was collected using ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes and processed uniformly across cohorts. Plasma cytokine concentrations were measured using multiplex Luminex assays. Patients with known kidney, liver and cardiac comorbidities were excluded to minimize confounding. Linear and logistic regression models were applied, adjusting for age, sex, and race, and P values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. We analyzed plasma samples from patients with dermatologist-diagnosed CPUO (n = 10) and HCs (n = 9). CPUO cohort had a median age of 70.4 years versus 67.5 years in the HC cohort, with no significant difference, with similar sex and racial distributions (Table I). CPUO patients reported severe itch (mean Worst Itch Numeric Rating Scale 8.4) compared to no itch in HCs. CPUO patients exhibited significantly elevated levels of kidney injury associated biomarkers compared to HCs including α1-microglobulin, collagen IV, lipocalin-2, osteoactivin, tissue inhibitor of metalloprotease-1 (TIMP-1) and uromodulin (Fig 1, A P < .001). Heatmap visualization showed distinct clustering of CPUO patients from HCs, reflecting a reproducible biomarker signature (Fig 1, B). In multivariable logistic regression, each biomarker remained independently associated with CPUO, with adjusted odds ratios >1. (Fig 1, C).

α1-Microglobulin and lipocalin-2 indicate tubular injury, while collagen IV and TIMP-1 are associated with extracellular matrix accumulation and fibrosis. Osteoactivin contributes to inflammatory and fibrotic responses.4,5 Uromodulin was increased in CPUO, in contrast to its decline in chronic kidney disease (CKD), suggesting early renal stress rather than nephron loss. While being often described as kidney-specific, these markers can also reflect broader systemic stress. Collagen IV and TIMP-1 are linked to hepatic fibrosis, Osteoactivin is expressed in skin and upregulated in inflammation, and lipocalin-2 acts as an acute-phase reactant. Together, they capture tubular damage, structural changes, inflammation, and fibrosis, providing a complete view of renal dysfunction.

No significant differences were observed in the liver/metabolic injury biomarkers. Cardiac biomarker α-2 macroglobulin and platelet factor 4 were significantly reduced in CPUO compared to HCs. (Table I). These unexpected reductions warrant caution interpretation.

Collectively, our findings showed elevated kidney injury markers in CPUO suggesting systemic inflammation and subclinical renal stress. Given that pruritus is common in chronic kidney disease, these findings suggest that CPUO and renal dysfunction may share biological pathways. Renal stress markers may enable early detection before irreversible injury occurs.

Limitations include the small sample size and cross-sectional design. CPUO group had higher hypertension and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEi)/angiotensin II receptor blocker (ARB) use, but the study was not powered to adjust for these differences; residual confounding cannot be excluded. Longitudinal studies are warranted to establish CPUO and renal dysfunction.
