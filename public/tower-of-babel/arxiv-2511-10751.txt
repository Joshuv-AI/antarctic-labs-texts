Hydrogen-line profiles from accreting gas giants and their CPDs
Gabriel-Dominique Marleau, Thomas Henning, Roy van Boekel, Myriam Benisty, Yuhiko Aoyama, Inga Kamp

Abstract
Context. Far fewer gas giants have been caught in their accretion phase than mature ones are known. Extremely Large Telescope (ELT) instruments will have a higher sensitivity and a smaller inner working angle than instruments up to now, which should allow more productive searches and detailed characterisation. Aims. We study the observability of accreting gas giants with METIS, the first-generation ELT spectrograph. We focus on the accretion-tracing hydrogen recombination lines accessible at a resolution R ∼ 10 5 R\sim 10^{5} , mainly Br α \alpha and Pfund-series lines. Our approach is general but we take PDS 70 b as a fiducial case. Methods. To calculate high-resolution line profiles, we combine a semianalytical multidimensional description of the flow onto an accreting planet and its circumplanetary disc (CPD) with local non-equilibrium shock-emission models. We assume the limiting scenario of no extinction, appropriate for gas giants in gaps, and negligible contribution from magnetospheric accretion columns. We use simulated detector sensitivities to compute required observing times. Results. Both the planet surface and the CPD surface shocks contribute to the total line profile, which is non-Gaussian and much narrower than the free-fall velocity. For the adopted baseline accretion rate onto PDS 70 b, the Br α \alpha line peak is equal to the photospheric continuum, modulated mostly by water features. However, the rotation of the planet broadens the features, helping the shock excess stand out. At Br α \alpha , already the continuum of PDS 70 b should yield SNR = 12 \mathrm{SNR}=12 in 4 h. The peak excess should require only about 15 min to reach SNR ≈ 3 \mathrm{SNR}\approx 3 . Conclusions. Br α \alpha is a potent planet formation tracer accessible to METIS in little integration time. Resolved line profiles will place independent constraints especially on the mass and radius of an accreting planet, and help identify the accretion mechanism(s) at work.

Introduction

In the last decade, our understanding of the formation of gas giants has been considerably improved.
One important factor is the discovery and study of low-mass accretors through their accretion-shock tracing hydrogen line emission (e.g.,
Schmidt et al. 2008
;
Haffert et al. 2019
;
Eriksson et al. 2020
;
Demars et al. 2023
;
Luhman et al. 2023
;
Close et al. 2025a
).
Exquisitely precise studies indirect studies of the formation phase are possible for Jupiter by looking at present-day properties (
Batygin & Adams 2025
). However, in general exoplanets need to be studied while they are in a formation or at least an accretion phase.

Only a handful of planetary-mass accretors are known despite several searches
(
Cugno et al. 2019
;
Zurlo et al. 2020
;
Flores-Rivera et al. 2023
;
Follette et al. 2023
;
Plunkett et al. 2025
)
,
and most are isolated or nearly so. Thus, they might not probe the same physics as the bulk of the planet population. Also, planets forming in protoplanetary discs are especially interesting because of complex physics: interaction with the disc leading to radial migration, effect on the spatial and size distribution of dust, disc chemistry, and so on, and young disc-bearing systems might show signposts of the forming gas giants they harbour
(
Bae et al. 2023
, e.g.)
, making their detection easier. Also, the age of the parent star can be used, with appropriate caution
(
Fortney et al. 2005
;
Zhang 2024
, taking the formation delay into account; e.g.,)
, to derive information on the physical properties of the detected forming companion.

Most planetary-mass accretors have been detected at large separations from their primary or host star but many more planets should be found closer in, with the giant-planet mass function peaking at a few au (
Fernandes et al. 2019
;
Wittenmyer et al. 2020
;
Fulton et al. 2021
; but see also
Lagrange et al. 2023
).
The Extremely Large Telescope (ELT), thanks to its 39.3-m primary-mirror diameter, will offer a high angular resolution capable of probing down to such scales
(
Ramsay et al. 2018
)
. In its integral-field-unit (IFU) mode, the first-generation Mid-infrared ELT Imager and Spectrograph (METIS) on the ELT will offer high-contrast, diffraction-limited observations at a spectral resolution
R\approx 10^{5}
at
\lambda\approx 2.9
–5.3
\upmu\mathrm{m}
(with a gap), covering the
L
and
M

bands
(
Brandl et al. 2021
;
Brandl et al. 2022
;
Feldt et al. 2024
)
.
This will allow in-depth studies of the physical and chemical properties of forming planets and their environments, amongst others (e.g.,
Takami et al. 2025
).
In a pioneering study,
Oberg et al. 2023
carefully simulated observations of circumplanetary discs (CPDs) with METIS at several
12
CO transitions at
\lambda\approx 5

\upmu\mathrm{m}
, and found that CPDs should be easily detectable.

We look here at neutral-hydrogen (H
i
) line emission from the shocks on the surface of the planet and on its CPD. We take as an obvious example PDS 70 b, the best-known accreting planetary-mass gas giant found in a gap in a transition disc
(
Müller et al. 2018
;
Keppler et al. 2018
;
Haffert et al. 2019
;
Zhou et al. 2021
;
Zhou et al. 2025
;
Close et al. 2025a
)
.
We could have almost equally as well chosen PDS 70 c, which has essentially the same physical properties within the large errorbars
(
Shibaike & Mordasini 2024
)
and for which even Atacama Large Millimeter Array (ALMA) observations provide evidence for accretion
(
Domínguez-Jamett et al. 2025
)
. However, PDS 70 c is partially obscured by protoplanetary disc (PPD) material
(
Haffert et al. 2019
)
. We come back to this in Section
5
. Recently, WISPIT 2 b
(
van Capelleveen et al. 2025
;
Close et al. 2025b
)
and 2M1216 b
(
Li et al. 2025
)
were added to the very short list of planetary-mass accretors in a PPD gap. The primary of 2M1216 b is faint, making observations challenging, but the host of WISPIT 2 b is a solar-mass star only slightly further away than PDS 70, and the planet is slightly brighter at H
\alpha
than PDS 70 b and c and thus would also be a good candidate that could be studied in more detail later.
We study how bright the shock should be and what the line shape can be for a range of parameters, and also estimate the importance of the confounding photospheric signal from the planet or its CPD, or from the PPD.

In Section

In Section

2
Model set-up

2.1
Physical picture

We consider planets around stars that still host a PPD. We assume a “super-thermal” planet mass
M_{\mathrm{p}}
, such that the planet has opened a deep gap (e.g.,
Fung et al. 2019
), which is a rapid process (e.g.,
Malik et al. 2015
). This implies that the gas flow towards the planet is supersonic
and that consequently a shock forms on—in fact, defines—the surface of the planet and of at least a part of the CPD. Another consequence is that the in-system extinction can be assumed to be moderate at
L
and
M
thanks to the orders-of-magnitude reduction in the
surface density of the PPD
(
Kanagawa et al. 2017
;
Okuzumi et al. 2025
)
, especially if the opacity decreases with wavelength (e.g.,
Woitke et al. 2016
).
Nevertheless, it would be easy to fold in an arbitrary amount of extinction since we will focus on a narrow spectral range. This could be the extinction by the accreting gas and dust at least approximately (e.g.,
Marleau et al. 2022
).

We model the accretion-flow streamline within the planetary Hill sphere using the ballistic infall model of
Marleau 2025
. It extends the model of
Adams & Batygin 2022
;
Adams & Batygin 2025
and
Taylor & Adams 2024
;
Taylor & Adams 2025
, which is based on
Ulrich 1976
in the stellar context,
and use the result that the streamlines responsible for the line emission originate close to the pole
(
Marleau et al. 2023
)
.
As we discuss in Section
5.1
,
we do not include any accretion from the CPD to the planet by magnetospheric accretion
(
Fendt 2003
;
Lovelace et al. 2011
;
Hartmann et al. 2016
)
, which would be a source of line emission.
The flow model yields the preshock velocity and density at the surface of the planet and of the CPD. Being supersonic, the gas flow, to first order, does not depend on the temperature distribution on the surface of the planet or of the CPD
(
Marleau et al. 2023
)
, which reduces the number of free parameters.

One parameter is the centrifugal radius
{R_{\textrm{cent}}}
, which we parametrise by

{f_{\textrm{cent}}}={R_{\textrm{cent}}}/{R_{\textnormal{Hill}}}=\ell^{2}/3
, where
{R_{\textnormal{Hill}}}
is the Hill radius and
\ell
is the so-called angular momentum bias. Other parameters of our framework will be introduced in the following sections.
In
Marleau 2025
, we found that
{f_{\textrm{cent}}}\approx 0.03
reproduces well the simulations in
Marleau et al. 2023
in terms of the fraction of the influx at the Hill sphere that lands directly on the planet. While this is smaller than the canonical estimate
{f_{\textrm{cent}}}=1/3
(e.g.,
Quillen & Trilling 1998
), it does match hydrodynamical simulations
(
Ward & Canup 2010
)
, as reported in
Shibaike & Mordasini 2024
,
especially in the limit of very super-thermal masses (Hill radius much larger than the Bondi radius).
Also,
Adams & Batygin 2022
argue that
{f_{\textrm{cent}}}
is uncertain but likely smaller than the classical estimate
and
Adams & Batygin 2025
mention that this reduction is expected to be in the range of
\ell^{2}\approx 1/9
–
1/4
, with smaller values towards higher planet masses,. Our adoption of
{f_{\textrm{cent}}}=0.03\approx 1/9\times 1/3
is thus in line with this. Nevertheless, we will see in Section
3.3
that it does not play an important role for our results. Also,
{f_{\textrm{cent}}}
should not be confused with the outer edge of the CPD; in a classical viscous disc, for instance, there is a wide decretion region between
{R_{\textrm{cent}}}={f_{\textrm{cent}}}{R_{\textnormal{Hill}}}
and
{R_{\textnormal{Hill}}}
(e.g., Fig. 1 in
Adams & Batygin 2025
). Thus a small
{f_{\textrm{cent}}}
does not imply a small CPD.

2.2
Accretion geometry and emission geometry

We assume that the accreting planet is surrounded by a CPD.
Neither theory nor observations have yet constrained tightly the thickness of CPDs around forming super-Jupiters. As studied by several authors, the thickness depends on the thermodynamics of the gas, especially its ability to cool (e.g.,
Ayliffe & Bate 2009
;
Szulágyi et al. 2016
;
Fung et al. 2019
;
Schulik et al. 2020
;
Krapp et al. 2024
) but we surmise and assume that at sufficiently high masses, CPDs will be flatter, rotationally supported, rather than distended envelopes. The latter have been found in large-scale simulations that however had to smooth the gravitational potential, out to 1–10 % of the Hill sphere or tens of Jupiter radii at best (e.g.,
Schulik et al. 2019
;
Schulik et al. 2020
;
Szulágyi & Ercolano 2020
;
Lega et al. 2024
;
Sagynbayeva et al. 2025
). On the other hand, true CPDs, even if thick, around super-thermal mass objects have been seen in smoothing-free, high-resolution simulations (e.g.,
Béthune & Rafikov 2019
;
Takasao et al. 2021
;
Marleau et al. 2023
and overview table in the latter work).
Thus we will assume a rather thin CPD, with the thickness a free parameter.
We define
\theta_{\textrm{CPD}}
as the polar angle of the surface of the CPD, so that the aspect ratio is

h_{\textrm{CPD}}=\tan\left(90{\mbox{\textdegree}}-\theta_{\textrm{CPD}}\right).

(1)

Since the emission is dominated by the regions close to the planet,
it is not necessary to consider the flaring of the CPD.

By conservation of angular momentum, most gas coming from large scales falls onto the CPD, not the planetary surface (e.g.,
Tanigawa et al. 2012
;
Schulik et al. 2020
;
Adams & Batygin 2022
;
Adams & Batygin 2025
). We assume azimuthal symmetry around the planet, as in the simulations of
Marleau et al. 2023
.

Taylor & Adams 2024
also made this choice, justifying it by the typically large timescale ratio between the infall and orbits in the CPD, which they quote respectively as
\sim 1
Myr versus
\sim 1
yr.
In fact, some accretors do show factor-few variability on months-to-years timescales
(
Close et al. 2025a
;
Zhou et al. 2025
)
but this is not dramatic.
Within this assumption, the flux density at the surface of the planet or the CPD depends only on the polar angle or the radial distance from the planet, respectively.

In Appendix
A
, we detail the geometry needed to integrate the emission towards the observer, considering separately the visible portions of the planet and CPD surfaces. The derivation relies on geometry and trigonometry. The quantitative results are obtained by numerical integration of the flux per projected area over the visible surface at every wavelength of interest, which we perform with
IDL
/
GDL

2.3
Local emission

To compute the local emission—a shock is always a local, 1D process—, we use the non-equilibrium radiation-hydrodynamical models of
Aoyama et al. 2018
, developed specifically for planetary accretion and used in several works (see references thereto).
The model computes line profiles and therefore intensities, which depend essentially only on the preshock quantity
n_{0}=X\rho_{0}/m_{\mathrm{H}}
and on the preshock velocity
v_{0}
, where
m_{\mathrm{H}}
is the atomic mass and
n_{0}
has the units of a number density but, for fixed hydrogen mass fraction
X
, is directly proportional to the mass density
\rho_{0}
.

Each spectral line can be thought of as a sum of Gaussians from the receding postshock gas in the cooling layer
(
Aoyama et al. 2018
;
Aoyama et al. 2020
)
.
The emitting gas has a temperature
T>10^{4}
K, so that no spectral feature narrower than a resolution
R=\lambda/(\Delta\lambda)\sim 1/\sqrt{8\ln(2)k_{\mathrm{B}}T/m_{\mathrm{H}}c^{2}}=\mbox{14,000}
is expected, where
k_{\mathrm{B}}
denotes Boltzmann’s constant, and
c
the speed of light.
Depending on the preshock velocity, which is
v_{0}\sim 100
–150
\mathrm{km}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}
for planets, but also on the preshock velocity, the line widths range between between 10 % and 130 % of preshock velocity, perhaps surprisingly (Appendix
B
).
An important fact is that below
v_{0}\approx 25
–30
\mathrm{km}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}
, the emission drops precipitously to zero because there is not enough energy to excite electrons to higher levels
(
Aoyama et al. 2018
)
.
Therefore, planet masses of a least roughly one to two Jupiter masses
are needed to generate any emission
(
Aoyama et al. 2020
)
.

2.4
The accretion rate

“The accretion rate” towards a planet has different meanings in different contexts and works. In (multidimensional) global hydrodynamics PPD simulations, it is often defined as the (net) mass inflow into the Hill sphere. In (low-dimensional) calculations of planet formation, the accretion rate is taken as the rate of growth of the planet, which is fed by direct infall onto the planet and the mass transfer rate from the CPD, whether through magnetospheric or boundary-layer accretion. Observationally, the accretion rate is the line-emitting, that is, observable, portion of the mass influx towards the planet and CPD. These different definitions are partially “orthogonal”, partially overlapping.

One possibility is that most of the gas first landing on the CPD migrates radially inward quickly, which requires a sufficiently high viscosity for the CPD (see e.g.
Papaloizou & Nelson 2005
). Then, the gas will generate lines at the footpoints of magnetospheric-accretion columns, so that all definitions more or less agree: even the gas hitting the CPD surface too slowly to emit lines will ultimately reach the planetary surface at velocities on the same order of magnitude as in free-fall from infinity, even if reduced because of the finite truncation radius. At the other extreme, the CPD could be a decretion disc directly connected to the planet
(
Dong et al. 2021
)
or not
(
Batygin & Morbidelli 2020
)
, so that only direct infall onto the planet surface would contribute to its growth, while the line-emitting rate would be larger. The total rate of infall onto the CPD would be even larger but relevant neither for line-emission nor for planet growth.

Given that the motion of the gas in the CPD depends on very unknown parameters such as the viscosity, we take a simpler, pragmatic approach in this work. We define an accretion rate
\dot{M}_{97\%}
as the integrated mass inflow rate, starting at the pole, along the surface of the planet and, if needed, of the CPD, out to the location within which
f=97
% of the line-integrated Br
\alpha
luminosity is emitted. While the integrated accretion rate grows with distance from the pole, the luminosity asymptotes where the emission drops essentially to zero once the preshock velocity goes below
v_{0}\approx 25\penalty\ \mathrm{km}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}

(
Aoyama et al. 2018
)
as mentioned in Section
2.3
.
Therefore, contrary to the total accretion rate,
\dot{M}_{97\%}
is well defined and does not depend on the outer radius of the CPD, for instance.
For small masses, the location corresponding to
\dot{M}_{97\%}
is on the planet, while at larger masses, it is on the CPD but within a modest distance of the planet. There is some arbitrariness in choosing
f=97
% but with
f=0.9
or especially
f=0.99
we obtained similar results.

Only for the purpose of defining the accretion rate
\dot{M}_{97\%}
, we compute the line-integrated cumulative luminosity
L_{\textnormal{Br}\,\alpha}^{\textnormal{cml}}
by summing the fluxes along the surfaces:

\displaystyle L_{\textnormal{Br}\,\alpha}^{\textnormal{cml}}\approx\left\{\!\!\!\begin{array}[]{ll}\int_{0}^{\theta}4\pi R_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}{F_{\textnormal{Br}\,\alpha}}\sin\theta^{\prime}\,{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}&\mbox{planet surface}\\
\int_{R_{\mathrm{p}}}^{r}4\pi r^{\prime}{F_{\textnormal{Br}\,\alpha}}\sin\theta_{\textrm{CPD}}\,{\rm d}r^{\prime}+L_{\textnormal{Br}\,\alpha}^{\textnormal{tot,\,pl}}&\textrm{CPD surface}\\
\end{array}\right.

where
{F_{\textnormal{Br}\,\alpha}}={F_{\textnormal{Br}\,\alpha}}(n_{0},\,v_{0})
is the local line-integrated flux,
\theta^{\prime}
and
r^{\prime}
are the polar angle along the surface of the planet and the radial coordinate along the surface of the CPD (see similarly Equation 11 in
Marleau et al. 2023
),
and
L_{\textnormal{Br}\,\alpha}^{\textnormal{tot,\,pl}}
is the total Br
\alpha
luminosity from the free planetary surface, that is, from the pole down to
\theta_{\textrm{CPD}}
.
Equation
2.4
thus sums the contributions from both hemispheres and we proceed the same way for the accretion rate.
Equation (
2.4
) is approximate because it effectively assumes an “isotropic observer”, without any reference to a viewing geometry. As we will see below (Section
3.3
), the inclination does modify the total flux reaching the observer.

The caveat to be kept in mind with Equation (
2.4
) is that it traces (approximately) the line-emitting mass inflow, while the mass growth rate can be larger or smaller. The relation between the two depends on the fate of the gas once it has reached the CPD and also on the structure of the CPD close to the planet, as mentioned above.

Internally (as in
Adams & Batygin 2022
or
Marleau et al. 2023
), we take as a free parameter the gas surface density
\Sigma
at the Hill sphere, while assuming a vertical stratification
(
Marleau 2025
)
.
This means however that at a given
\Sigma
the mass infall rate will depend on the planetary mass since the Hill radius depends on
M_{\mathrm{p}}
. Therefore, it is easier, also for relating to observations, to work with the line-emitting accretion rate as defined above.
The surface density determines linearly the incoming mass flux.

As mentioned and we shall see below, the planet surface dominates the line emission, and by angular momentum conservation the gas landing there comes predominantly from the polar regions above the planet
(
Tanigawa et al. 2012
;
Marleau et al. 2023
)
.
Thus the density across the gas-providing region is relatively constant, independently of the angular distribution of the inflow across the hemisphere.
Taylor & Adams 2024
;
Taylor & Adams 2025
, and
Adams & Batygin 2025
considered a range of distributions and carefully studied their impact on different quantities such as the surface density of the CPD or its evolution. However, this angular dependence will not matter very much here because we are examining only the line emission, which comes from the innermost regions.

Before concluding, we discuss our neglect of magnetic fields (Section

5.1
Neglect of magnetic fields

Magnetospheric accretion (e.g.,
Koenigl 1991
;
Hartmann et al. 2016
) seems logically required even at planetary masses to explain hydrogen-line emission from (effectively) isolated accretors. Their line shapes seem to confirm or at least not contradict magnetospheric accretion as the dominant process, with examples including GQ Lup b
(
Demars et al. 2023
)
, TWA 27 B/2M1207 b
(
Marleau et al. 2024
;
Aoyama et al. 2024
;
Patapis et al. 2025
)
, Delorme 1 (AB) b (
Betti et al. 2022a
;
Betti et al. 2022b
;
Ringqvist et al. 2023
;
Demars et al. 2025
), 2M1115
(
Viswanath et al. 2024
)
,
or Cha 1107-7626
(
Almendros-Abad et al. 2025
)
.
Indeed, without truncation of the CPD and nearly-free-fall acceleration of the gas along the accretion columns, it is difficult to imagine how lines could be formed since a (hot) boundary layer connecting CPD and planet (e.g.,
Kley & Lin 1996
;
Hertfelder & Kley 2017
;
Dong et al. 2021
) would likely only lead to continuum emission (see also
Mendigutía 2020
in the context of Herbig Ae/Be stars).

However, planets in PPDs can be accreting through magnetospheric accretion from their CPD but also through pure hydrodynamic accretion from the parental PPD. The latter will be the sole mechanism associated with line emission if the magnetic field is weak and thus not able to truncate the CPD, and/or if the radial accretion flow through the CPD is large. If on the contrary there are accretion columns, covering on the order of a percent of the planetary surface, the preshock density at the footpoints will simply be higher but the emission mechanism otherwise the same. Such narrow columns might be found on PDS 70 b (e.g.,
Hashimoto et al. 2020
;
Hasegawa et al. 2021
).
Thus the present work will apply in those cases too, for appropriate parameter values, within the approximation that we do not include emission by the accretion columns (the “flow model”, as in
Kwan & Fischer 2011
; see
Hashimoto & Aoyama 2025
) nor complex interactions of the radiation from the hot spots and/or the accretion column with those columns (e.g.,
Muzerolle et al. 2001
;
Thanathibodee et al. 2024
).
If the latter do contribute, they might do so on a different timescale, as Demars et al. (re-subm.) seem to have found for Delorme 1 (AB)b.

In any case, given the number of different complex mechanisms and free parameters at play, assuming a negligible contribution from magnetospheric processes or possible chromospheric activity to the line formation is a useful limiting case. It is also likely directly relevant for several planetary-mass objects, at least at epochs of more vigorous accretion. Also, in their analysis of several 20–100
M_{\mathrm{J}}
accretors,
Hashimoto & Aoyama 2025
found that a significant fraction (15/70) could be categorised as shock-dominated as opposed to flow-dominated, at least based on their line-flux ratios. There was no obvious correlation with object parameters but even some of the high-mass objects were apparently well explained by the shock model. Thus it might be even more widely applicable than initially envisioned.

5.2
Neglect of extinction

The visual ISM extinction to PDS 70 is
A_{V}\approx 0.05
mag
(
Müller et al. 2018
)
but in general even larger amounts of extinction can be relatively reliably corrected. This is thanks to the large number of studies dealing with extinction, especially in the diffuse ISM or in denser regions such as protostellar cores (e.g.,
Ossenkopf & Henning 1994
;
Lutz et al. 1996
;
Chiar & Tielens 2006
;
Wang & Chen 2019
;
Gordon et al. 2023
).

What can be more problematic is in-system extinction. However, we focus on gap-opening gas giants, and at longer wavelengths, so that the PPD might not attenuate much the light emitted by the forming planet.
Depending on the atmospheric model they use,
Wang et al. 2021b
find a range of in-system extinctions. However, their best-fitting models yield
A_{K}\approx 0.2
–
0.5
mag at most, which implies a small Br
\alpha
extinction
{A_{\textnormal{Br}\,\alpha}}\approx 0.1
–0.2 mag when using the
Chiar & Tielens 2006
scaling.
This is a different situation from the results reported by
Cugno et al. 2025
for AS 209. There, a significant amount of extinction is found in the gap, but it is not very deep either.

Marleau et al. 2022
tried to estimate how extinguishing the infalling gas and dust are at H
\alpha
. The infall geometries they considered, if anything, overestimated the local density. Therefore, their results should be rather conservative. Despite this, they found that for a wide range of masses and up to high accretion rates, the gas should mostly not lead to significant extinction at H
\alpha
.
If we translate these results to Br
\alpha

Nevertheless, since both the photospheric emission of and the line emission at the planet surface dominate over the contributions from the CPD, any non-negligible extinction would likely affect both components in the same way. Therefore, only the total flux would be lowered but the relative contributions not changed, making it easy to apply our framework also to other systems.

5.3
Relation to the work of
Takasao et al. 2021

Takasao et al. 2021
presented predictions of line emission using the
Aoyama et al. 2018
line-emission models. Their purpose and approach differ from ours in several ways, however. The main difference is in the larger-scale hydrodynamical simulations which their calculations use. Whereas in our case, based on the results from radiation-hydrodynamical simulations, the planet surface and the CPD surface emit, in theirs almost all the line emission comes from where a thin supersonic sub-surface flow hits the planet surface.
Marleau et al. 2023
compare these scenarios. Which is closer to reality, depends on the physics of the postshock cooling , especially on the ability of the gas to cool, which is set by the density and chemical reactions involving the CO, H
2
, and OH abundances, involving in turn questions about energy-level populations (see Appendix B of
Takasao et al. 2021
).

Worth mentioning but not a fundamental difference,
Takasao et al. 2021
looked only at the H
\alpha
line and studied only one case (
M_{\mathrm{p}}=10\penalty\ M_{\mathrm{J}}
). They perform hydrodynamical simulations to obtain the gas flow, whereas we adopt a semi-analytical approach to make covering a large parameter space tractable.
Finally, to compute the high-resolution line profiles, we take the explicit viewing geometry into account, including the inclination, whereas they seem to take a simpler weighted sum.
Thus our works appear complementary to some extent.

6
Summary and conclusions

In preparation for the coming online of ELT/METIS, we have developed in
Marleau 2025
and this work a framework to predict spectrally resolved hydrogen accretion-line profiles for gas giants.
For the local emission, we used the shock model of
Aoyama et al. 2018
,
and restricted ourselves to the sitation that magnetospheric accretion does not occur. In principle, it could be accommodated in our set-up.
We took at PDS 70 b as an example and predicted a Br
\alpha
line strength and line profile, and estimated how easily observable it should be.
Our main findings concerning PDS 70 b but also more generally are the following:

1.

The integrated Br
\alpha
line luminosity at PDS 70 b is
L_{\textnormal{Br}\,\alpha}\approx 10^{-9}
–
10^{-8}\penalty\ {L_{\odot}}
depending on the epoch. We used the maximum as our fiducial case.

2.

The total line profile, coming mostly from the shock on planet surface with a smaller contribution from the CPD surface shock, is approximately Gaussian only for a fraction of the width of the line, with broader wings outside brighter on the red side. The peak is slightly offset, by
\Delta v\approx+3

\mathrm{km}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}
. The line shape is sensitive mostly to the mass and radius and little to the other parameters.

3.

Already just the photospheric emission of PDS 70 b will be detectable at
\mathrm{SNR}\approx 12
per spectral bin in 4 h. If caught in an episode of strong accretion corresponding to moderate to high literature values, the shock excess will easily be detectable at Br
\alpha
with a peak per-bin
\mathrm{SNR}=3
in 15 min.

4.

At Br
\alpha
, the detailed spectral appearance of the photosphere depends on the choice of the opacity line list, especially for water (H
2
O) and hydrogen sulphide (H
2
S). Recent calculations are in significant disagreement concerning the position and strength of the features.
However, the CPD, star, and PPD should all be essentially negligible at Br
\alpha
.

5.

A helpful factor for detecting a line excess is the possible rotation of young gas giants, even if only around
v\sin i\approx 10\penalty\ \mathrm{km}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}
. It flattens the photospheric features but without affecting the accretion signature. A moderate inclination
i\approx 50{\mbox{\textdegree}}
as for the PDS 70 system is optimal for this.

6.

The other transitions accessible to METIS (Pf
\beta
–Pf
\varepsilon
in the Pfund series) are likely of very limited use for detecting accreting planets because the photospheric signal is much stronger and likely displays strong features.

7.

For
M_{\mathrm{p}}\approx 0.5
–
1\penalty\ M_{\mathrm{J}}
, planets would need to be accreting very vigorously with
\dot{M}_{97\%}\gtrsim 10^{-5}\penalty\ M_{\mathrm{J}}\,\mbox{yr}^{-1}
to have a chance of being detectable in their shock excess. Lower masses
M_{\mathrm{p}}\lesssim 0.5\penalty\ M_{\mathrm{J}}
do not emit any lines (Figure
10
).

The broad-brush predictions for PDS 70 b apply similarly to PDS 70 c, with a positive nuance. PDS 70 c is viewed through some PPD material
(
Haffert et al. 2019
)
, which is a disadvantage in principle but maybe not in practice, depending on the actual optical depth. Also, the greater angular separation of PDS 70 c from the primary (Figure
13
), now and in the future, does not give it a clear advantage over PDS 70 b when observed with the ELT, thanks to its small inner working angle; starlight suppression at PDS 70 c is better than at b but is likely sufficient there already. Moreover, the different physical separations from the star are not directly relevant for the local accretion, apart from possibly setting how much of the global radial gas flow is intercepted by each planet
(
Lubow & D’Angelo 2006
;
Bergez-Casalou et al. 2023
)
. However, PDS 70 c has a similar accretion rate
(
Zhou et al. 2025
)
and mass
(
Hammond et al. 2025
)
as PDS 70 b but a cooler effective temperature by around
\Delta T_{\textnormal{eff}}\approx 300
K
(
Wang et al. 2021b
)
, so that the Br
\alpha
line might stand out better from the photosphere. This could be the main difference. Fortunately, METIS is an IFU, so that both targets will be obtained simultaneously.

This work could be extended in many ways. One would be to incorporate the emission from magnetospheric accretion columns if models predicted them in the NIR; at planetary masses, both this preshock emission and the (post-)shock emission considered so far likely contribute
(
Hashimoto & Aoyama 2025
)
.
We have focussed on the line excess but calculating the continuum in the
L
and
M
bands, especially from the CPD, would be an interesting challenge with currently only few observational constraints. This would also represent an extension of the calculations by
Oberg et al. 2023
.

decreases
almost only with
v_{0}
, from
\mathrm{FWHM}/v_{0}\approx 0.8

goes from 30 to 200

At high

as one could naively think.
The line shapes from accreting planets, as computed in this work, are a superposition of many such profiles, but this conclusion will still hold qualitatively.

Another important fact is that for a given object, the effective (flux-averaged)
v_{0}
is essentially fixed, whereas the effective
n_{0}
will vary with the accretion rate. When an object is accreting less vigorously,
n_{0}
is lower, and
\mbox{FWHM}\approx(25
–
40)\penalty\ \mathrm{km}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}
for any accretor mass, at least out to the grid limit at
v_{0}=200

\mathrm{km}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}
.
During episodes of strong(er) accretion (higher
n_{0}
), however, the FWHM becomes directly proportional to the preshock velocity.
Therefore, specifically for Br

Appendix D
Estimate of the CPD spectrum with ProDiMo

We use the radiation thermo-chemical disc modeling code ProDiMo
(
Woitke et al. 2016
;
Kamp et al. 2017
)

to predict the continuum and near- and mid-IR emission line spectrum of the CPD of PDS 70 b.
We used the same fiducial planet properties as in Table
1
.
For the viscous heating in the CPD midplane, we adopted an accretion rate
\dot{M}=6\times 10^{-8}\penalty\ M_{\mathrm{J}}\,\mbox{yr}^{-1}
. The CPD stretches from
r=0.0019
to 2.4 au with a CPD gas mass of
M_{\textrm{CPD}}=2.1\times 10^{-5}\penalty\ {M_{\odot}}
. We assume a canonical gas-to-dust ratio
f_{\textrm{d/g}}=100
and the typical T Tauri disc dust composition and grain sizes
(
Woitke et al. 2016
)
.
Currently, ProDiMo needs to include an inner rim, which differs from our set-up in this work.
The inner rim is rounded using
\mathrm{reduc}=10^{-5}
and
\mathrm{raduc}=2
(see Equation (71) of
Woitke et al. 2024
)
and the radial gas surface density power law is relatively flat,
with a power law exponent
\epsilon=0.1
and a tapering radius of
{R_{\textnormal{tap}}}=1
au (tapering exponent
\gamma=0.5
).

The CPD is embedded in the thermal radiation of the outer protoplanetary disc. Therefore, we assume a background radiation temperature of 19 K following
Portilla-Revelo et al. 2023
. In addition, the central star UV radiation field illuminates the CPD from the outside, which we approximate by using
\chi=65.7
in units of the Draine field
(
Draine & Bertoldi 1996
)

Wang et al. 2021b
).
The dust temperature in the CPD is calculated from continuum radiative transfer taking the dust properties listed above and assuming dust settling according to
Riols & Lesur 2018
using
\alpha=10^{-3}

(
Kamp et al. 2017

. The gas emission spectrum is calculated using escape probability
(
Woitke et al. 2024
