A Self-Regenerating Mixed-Valence FeOx-TiO2 Nanoplatform for Mitochondrion-Targeted Triple-Modal Cancer Therapy
Authors: Yongzhi Xia, Mengqin Wang, Yanyan Zhou, Ruiqi Wei, Yijie Wang, Lijuan Hua, Qiang Fang, Yan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeReactive oxygen species (ROS) cancer therapy is limited by short ROS lifetime, restricted diffusion, and lack of subcellular targeting. Current approaches physically mix independent components, causing complexity and decoupled actions. To overcome these barriers, we developed a mitochondrion-targeted nanoplatform (FTDT) built on a FeOₓ-TiO2 composite core with intrinsic mixed-valence Fe2⁺/Fe3⁺ pairs. The FT core acts as photosensitizer, while FeOₓ provides catalase- and peroxidase-like activities, integrating self-oxygenation, antioxidant depletion, self-amplifying redox cycling, photosensitization, photothermal conversion, and organelle anchoring into a single entity.MethodsFTDT was synthesized via a hydrothermal method, PDA coating, and TPP conjugation. Mixed-valence state was confirmed by XPS. Catalase- and peroxidase-like activities were evaluated by O2 and ∙OH detection. UV-Vis-NIR spectroscopy characterized optical absorption. Photothermal conversion was measured under 808 nm laser. 1O2 generation was detected using DPBF. Intracellular hypoxia, ROS, mitochondrial targeting, membrane potential, and lysosomal integrity were assessed by fluorescence microscopy. Cytotoxicity and in vivo efficacy were evaluated in A549 cells and xenograft mice.ResultsFTDT showed a self-amplifying cycle: Fe2⁺ drives ∙OH generation, Fe3⁺ consumes GSH and regenerates Fe2⁺; Fe3⁺ from Fenton reaction replenishes the Fe3⁺ pool. The core converted H2O2 to O2. FT core with PDA-enabled NIR absorption allowed 1O2 generation and photothermal conversion. TPP enabled mitochondrial localization, inducing dysfunction, lipid peroxidation, and lysosomal disruption, leading to cell death. In vitro and in vivo studies confirmed antitumor efficacy with good biocompatibility.ConclusionBy unifying self-oxygenation, antioxidant depletion, self-amplifying redox cycling, photosensitization, photothermal conversion, and organelle anchoring, FTDT offers a clinically translatable strategy to overcome key barriers in ROS-based cancer therapy.Keywords: mitochondrion-anchored therapy, self-sustaining catalytic engine, triple-modal cancer therapy, closed-loop redox cycle, oxidative stress amplification

Introduction

Achieving multiple therapeutic functions in a single nanoplatform is highly desirable for cancer therapy.1–3 Yet most current systems do so by physically mixing independent components, each responsible for one function.4,5 This leads to synthetic complexity, batch heterogeneity, and decoupled functional modules. Transition-metal-based catalysts (eg, Fe, Cu, Mn, Ce) are often used in such platforms. For instance, they can convert hydrogen peroxide into hydroxyl radicals (·OH) for chemodynamic therapy.4,6,7 However, their performance is limited by two intrinsic factors: rapid consumption of ·OH by antioxidants like glutathione, and the extremely short diffusion distance of ·OH itself.8–10 Moreover, such platforms generally lack integrated light-responsive capabilities. Achieving photodynamic or photothermal effects in these systems often requires the introduction of external organic agents, which adds further complexity and compromises functional synergy.11

Despite these drawbacks, most reported nanoplatforms still follow a “multicomponent integration” approach. They assemble distinct functional modules, such as catalase mimics for O2 supply, GSH-depleting agents, and photosensitizers, into a single carrier.5,9,12–15 A survey of recent literature shows that the vast majority remain at the level of physical co-loading, with limited functional synergy at the material level. Typically, these designs use commercially available photosensitizers (eg, indocyanine green, methylene blue) together with pre-synthesized catalase mimics (eg, MnO2, Pt) and GSH-depleting agents (eg, metal ions).4,13,16–25 For instance, Yang et al coated gold nanoclusters with a metal-phenolic network (AuNCs@PDA-Mn) to combine photothermal and chemodynamic therapy, yet each function comes from a separate component.26 Similarly, Gao et al constructed a glutathione-responsive mesoporous silica nanoplatform for CO release and photodynamic therapy, loading a commercial photosensitizer and a CO donor without inherent synergy. Even highly multifunctional designs remain at this level.8 Ma et al reported a MnO2-AIPH nanoflower that provides O2 self-supply, GSH depletion, photodynamic, photothermal, and thermodynamic therapy, but each function is contributed by a distinct chemical entity.27 Likewise, the hypoxia-programmed HA-NI/Ce6/TPZ nanosystem by Liu et al is a physical assembly of a carrier, a commercial photosensitizer, and a prodrug, with no molecular-level coupling.16 These examples illustrate a common pattern. Most reported platforms are essentially physical mixtures of independently functioning parts. Their effects are additive rather than synergistic, and they suffer from synthetic complexity and a lack of intrinsic mechanistic coupling.

Several “all-in-one” nanoplatforms have been reported in recent years. For instance, Liu et al developed a defect-rich Ni/NiMoO4₋x heterojunction that integrates type-I PDT, CDT, and PTT through tailored electronic band structures.28 Gong et al designed a hollow polydopamine-based biomimetic nanoplatform that combines hypoxia relief, apoptosis induction, and X-ray-induced PDT for enhanced radiotherapy.29 These studies demonstrate the potential of multifunctional integration. However, existing unified systems still face limitations: the Ni/NiMoO4₋ₓ system lacks subcellular targeting to overcome the diffusion limit of ROS, while the HP-based platform relies on physical loading of multiple independent functional modules. Moreover, neither system achieves self-regenerating catalytic cycling.

To fill these gaps, we designed a FeOₓ-TiO2 composite core that intrinsically integrates catalase-like and peroxidase-like activities, photosensitizing capability, and a self-regenerating Fe2⁺/Fe3⁺ redox cycle, with a thin PDA coating to enhance NIR absorption. TPP-mediated mitochondrial anchoring further distinguishes our platform from existing systems. Its mixed-valence Fe2+/Fe3+ pairs create a closed, self-compensating redox cycle. The initially present Fe2+ drives Fenton-like ·OH production, while the coexisting Fe3+ consumes GSH and is reduced back to Fe2+. At the same time, Fe3+ generated from the Fenton reaction replenishes the Fe3+ pool. This self-regenerating cycle continues without external energy input and leads to sustained amplification of reactive oxygen species. From this mixed-valence foundation also arises the catalase-like activity of FeOx, which converts endogenous H2O2 into O2, thereby relieving hypoxia and supplying the essential substrate for photodynamic action.5 Critically, the same Fe-O-Ti interfacial linkages that stabilize the mixed-valence pairs also introduce mid-gap electronic states, intrinsically extending the photoresponse of the composite from the UV into the visible region. The composite itself exhibits weak absorption in the near-infrared region; we therefore employed a thin polydopamine (PDA) coating to amplify this absorption, enabling efficient photothermal and photodynamic conversion under 808 nm light. In this way, the catalytic and photosensitizing functions arise intrinsically from the FeOₓ-TiO2 core, while the PDA coating serves as an integrated enhancer. Together, all these modalities originate from the same composite platform, embodying true integration and enabling synergistic cancer cell killing. To address the diffusion limit of ROS, we conjugated triphenylphosphonium groups to anchor the nanoplatform to mitochondria, achieving targeted delivery (Figure 1).30,31

This work demonstrates that true multifunctional integration can be achieved through a composite platform wherein the catalytic and photosensitizing functions derive from the FeOₓ-TiO2 core itself, while a PDA coating serves as an integrated enhancer for photothermal and photodynamic performance under 808 nm irradiation. This mixed-valence strategy opens a new route for designing self-fueling transition-metal-based platforms, with potential extensions beyond cancer therapy to other fields requiring sustained catalytic activity and light responsiveness.

Methods

Synthesis of FeOₓ-TiO2 Nanoparticles

0.816 g of FeCl3 was dissolved in 60 mL ethylene glycol, followed by the sequential addition of 0.294 g sodium citrate, 2.416 g sodium acetate, and 3 mL tetrabutyl titanate under stirring to form a homogeneous tan colored suspension.32 The mixture was transferred into a Teflon lined autoclave and subjected to solvothermal reaction at 220 °C for 6 h. After natural cooling, the resulting precipitate was collected by centrifugation, thoroughly washed with deionized water and ethanol, and finally redispersed in deionized water to obtain a FeOₓ-TiO2 stock solution (30 mg/mL).

Preparation of FeOₓ-TiO2@PDA (FTD)

A dispersion of FeOₓ-TiO2 nanoparticles (4 mL, 30 mg/mL) in Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.8) was prepared. 0.024 g dopamine hydrochloride was added to the mixture, and the reaction was carried out overnight under a N2 atmosphere with continuous stirring. The resulting FeOₓ-TiO2@PDA (denoted as FTD) was collected by centrifugation and washed thoroughly with deionized water.

Preparation of FeOₓ-TiO2@PDA-TPP (FTDT)

FTD dispersion (4 mL, 20 mg/mL) was diluted in phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). 50 mg TPP, 50 mg EDC, and 25 mg NHS were subsequently introduced, and the coupling reaction was allowed to proceed overnight at room temperature under gentle stirring. The resulting TPP conjugated product, denoted as FTDT, was collected by centrifugation and washed thoroughly with deionized water.

Preparation of Fluorescent Labeled FTDT (FTDT-FITC)

The FTDT dispersion (2 mL, 20 mg/mL) was mixed with phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). After activation with 25 mg EDC and 12.5 mg NHS under stirring, FITC solution (1 mL, 5 mg/mL in DMSO) was added dropwise, and the conjugation was conducted in the dark at room temperature for 6 h. The resulting fluorescently labeled nanoparticles (FTDT-FITC) were collected by centrifugation, washed thoroughly with PBS, and stored at 4 °C in the dark.

Dissolved Oxygen Measurement

FTDT (1.5 mg) was dispersed in 3 mL PBS (pH 5.5). A dissolved oxygen electrode was immediately immersed, and the oxygen concentration was recorded every 30s for 15 min. Parallel measurements were performed for H2O2 (2 mM) alone, and for mixtures of H2O2 (2 or 4 mM) with FT, FTD, or FTDT under identical conditions.

GSH Consumption Assay

A solution containing 100 μL GSH (10 mM) and 10 μL DTNB (3 mg/mL) in 2 mL PBS (pH 5.5) was incubated at 37°C for 1 h.33 FTDT was then added. At time points of 0, 10, 30, and 60 min, aliquots were centrifuged, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured between 350–550 nm.

ROS Detection

Light-Induced ROS: Solutions of FT, FTD, or FTDT (0.1 mL, 10 mg/mL) were mixed with 1.9 mL water and 0.5 mL DPBF (100 μg/mL).34 Under 808 nm laser irradiation (1.0 W/cm2), absorbance spectra (370–550 nm) were recorded at 0, 1, 2, 5, and 10 min. A control group without irradiation was analyzed simultaneously. Catalytic ROS Generation: The same material solutions were mixed with 1.9 mL PBS (pH 5.5) containing H2O2 (2 mM) and 0.5 mL DPBF. Absorbance spectra were recorded at the same time points under identical laser irradiation.

∙OH Generation Assay

TMB (60 μL, 5 mM) in 2 mL PBS (pH 5.5) was pre-incubated at 37°C for 10 min, and its baseline absorbance (330–750 nm) was measured.35 FTDT (0.2 mg) and H2O2 (60 μL, 3 mM) were then added. The reaction was monitored by measuring the supernatant absorbance at 0, 10, 30, 60, 90, 120, 240, 360, 600, and 900s after centrifugation.

Photothermal Performance Evaluation

For photothermal measurements, aqueous solutions (2 mL) were irradiated with an 808 nm laser, and the temperature was recorded at 5s intervals using a thermocouple probe. Specifically, the photothermal performance of different materials (FT, FTD, FTDT, pure PDA at 0.5 mg/mL, and water as control) was compared at 1.0 W/cm2 for 5 min. The concentration‑dependent performance of FTDT was evaluated at concentrations of 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1.0 mg/mL under the same power density. The power‑dependent performance was assessed by irradiating an FTDT solution (0.5 mg/mL) at power densities of 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 W/cm2. The photothermal stability was tested by subjecting the FTDT solution to five consecutive on/off cycles (5 min irradiation followed by natural cooling to room temperature) at 1.0 W/cm2. For photothermal conversion efficiency calculation, the FTDT solution (0.5 mg/mL) was irradiated at 1.0 W/cm2 for 10 min and then allowed to cool naturally; the efficiency was calculated based on the heating and cooling profiles.36

Where η is the photothermal conversion efficiency, h is the heat transfer coefficient of the container (W·m−2·C−1), S is the surface area of the container exposed to the laser (m2), Tmax is the maximum system temperature attained, Tsurr is the ambient temperature, Qs represents the heat dissipation from the solvent alone under laser irradiation (W), I is the incident laser power density (W·cm−2), and Aλ is the absorbance of the FTDT solution at 808 nm. The values for hS and Qs were derived from the cooling curve of pure water under identical experimental conditions.

Cellular Uptake

To investigate the internalization of FTDT by tumor cells, A549 cells were incubated with FITC-labeled nanoparticles (FTDT-FITC).37 Briefly, cells were seeded in confocal dishes at a density of 1×105 cells per dish and cultured for 24 h. The medium was replaced with fresh medium containing FTDT-FITC (500 μg/mL), and incubation continued for 4 h in the dark. Cells were then washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and stained with DAPI to visualize nuclei. Imaging was performed using a laser scanning confocal microscope, with FITC (ex/em: 488/525 nm) and DAPI (ex/em: 405/461 nm) channels to assess intracellular localization of the nanoparticles.

Mitochondrial Targeting

The mitochondrial targeting capability of TPP-modified FTDT was evaluated by co-localization analysis using MitoTracker Red.23 A549 cells were cultured and treated with FTDT-FITC as described above. After 4 h, cells were washed and incubated with MitoTracker Red (working concentration: 200 nM) for 15 min at 37°C in the dark. Following fixation, cells were imaged under a confocal microscope. The overlap between FITC signal (nanoparticles) and MitoTracker Red signal (mitochondria) was analyzed to determine targeting efficiency.

Cell Viability Assay

Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay.37 A549 and IOSE-80 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5×103 cells/well and cultured for 24 h. After removing the medium, cells were treated with FT, FTD, or FTDT at various concentrations (0–600 µg/mL, based on FT content) in fresh medium. For light-treated groups, cells were irradiated with an 808 nm laser (1.0 W/cm2, 5 min) after 4 h of incubation, then cultured for an additional 20 h. Dark groups were incubated for 24 h without irradiation. Subsequently, 10 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well and incubated for 4 h. The medium was replaced with 150 µL DMSO to dissolve formazan crystals, and absorbance at 490 nm was measured. Cell viability was calculated and plotted against material concentration.

Intracellular Oxygen Detection

Intracellular O2 levels were monitored using the oxygen-sensitive probe RDPP.5 A549 cells seeded in confocal dishes were incubated with 10 μM RDPP for 4 h, washed with PBS, and then treated with FT or FTDT (500 μg/mL) for 24 h. For light-treated groups, cells were exposed to 808 nm laser irradiation (1.0 W/cm2, 5 min) prior to imaging. Fluorescence images (λex = 488 nm, λem = 600–700 nm) were acquired using an inverted fluorescence microscope. The red fluorescence intensity of RDPP, which is quenched by O2, was analyzed to evaluate changes in intracellular oxygen concentration.

Quantitative ROS Analysis by Flow Cytometry

A549 cells seeded in 6-well plates were treated with FT, FTD, or FTDT (500 μg/mL) for 4 h, with or without subsequent 808 nm laser irradiation (1.0 W/cm2, 5 min). Cells were then incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA in serum-free medium for 30 min at 37°C in the dark.34 After washing, cells were harvested, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry (ex/em: 488/525 nm). The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of at least 10,000 cells per sample was recorded.

Qualitative ROS Imaging by Confocal Microscopy

A549 cells grown in confocal dishes were treated as described above. After treatment, cells were loaded with 10 μM DCFH-DA for 60 min at 37°C, washed, and irradiated immediately before imaging.34 Fluorescence images were captured using a confocal microscope (ex/em: 488/525 nm) to visualize intracellular ROS generation.

Intracellular GSH Measurement

Intracellular GSH levels were quantified using a commercial GSH assay kit.38 A549 cells were treated as described in section 2.4.5 for 24 h. After treatment, at least 5×106 cells were collected, washed with PBS, and lysed via freeze-thaw cycles or sonication in the provided lysis buffer. The lysate was centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected for analysis. Following the manufacturer’s protocol, the absorbance at 412 nm was measured using a microplate reader to determine the GSH concentration.

Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay

Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using the JC-1 probe.28 A549 cells were seeded in confocal dishes, cultured for 24 h, and treated as indicated (Control, Laser, FTD, FTDT, FTD+Laser, FTDT+Laser) for 4 h. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated with JC-1 working solution for 15 min at 37°C under 5% CO2. Following incubation, cells were washed twice with 1× incubation buffer and imaged in PBS under an inverted fluorescence microscope. Fluorescence signals were collected at Ex/Em = 490/530 nm (green, JC-1 monomers) and Ex/Em = 525/590 nm (red, JC-1 aggregates).

Lysosomal Integrity Assay

Lysosomal integrity was evaluated using acridine orange (AO) staining.39 After 24 h of culture, A549 cells were treated (Control, Laser, FTDT, FTDT+Laser) for 4 h, washed with PBS, and stained with 1 mL AO working solution for 10 min. Cells were then washed, resuspended in PBS, and imaged immediately. Green fluorescence (Ex/Em = 488/530 nm) and red fluorescence (Ex/Em = 539/640 nm) were captured to assess lysosomal permeabilization.

Lipid Peroxidation Assay

Lipid peroxidation was detected using the BODIPY 581/591 C11 probe.39 Treated A549 cells (Control, Laser, FTDT, FTDT+Laser) were incubated with 1 mL of BODIPY staining solution for 10 min at 37°C. After washing, cells were imaged in PBS. The shift from red (Ex/Em = 581/591 nm) to green (Ex/Em = 488/510 nm) fluorescence indicated lipid peroxidation levels.

In vitro Hemolysis Assay

Hemolytic activity of FTDT was evaluated following a standard protocol.40 Fresh erythrocytes were collected by centrifugation of whole blood and washed with normal saline (0.9% NaCl). Erythrocyte suspensions (200 µL) were incubated with 800 µL of FTDT solutions at various concentrations (0–800 µg/mL) for 4 h at 37°C. Deionized water and normal saline served as positive and negative controls, respectively. After incubation, samples were centrifuged, and the absorbance of the supernatants at 541 nm was measured. The hemolysis percentage was calculated by comparing sample absorbance to that of the positive control.

Establishment of the Subcutaneous Tumor Model

To evaluate in vivo antitumor efficacy, a subcutaneous xenograft model was established in female BALB/c nude mice (6–7 weeks old, ~18 g). A549 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested, mixed 1:1 (v/v) with Matrigel on ice, and 150 µL of the suspension containing 2×106 cells was injected subcutaneously into the right flank of each mouse. Tumor growth was monitored, and when volumes reached 100–150 mm3 (approximately 2 weeks post-inoculation), mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 5): (1) PBS control, (2) PBS + laser, (3) FTDT, and (4) FTDT + laser, for subsequent therapeutic experiments.

In vivo Photothermal Imaging

The photothermal performance and intratumoral distribution of FTDT were assessed using infrared thermal imaging. Mice bearing A549 tumors (100–150 mm3) were intratumorally injected with 30 µL of FTDT solution (1.5 mg/mL in saline) or saline as control. Four hours post-injection, tumors in the designated groups were irradiated with an 808 nm laser (1.0 W/cm2) for 5 min. Thermal images of the tumor region were captured before (0 min) and at 1-min intervals during irradiation. The temperature at the tumor center was recorded and analyzed in real-time.

Assessment of the Therapeutic Regimen

Tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 5 per group): (1) Saline, (2) Laser alone, (3) FTDT alone, and (4) FTDT + Laser. A 14-day therapeutic cycle was implemented, during which mice in the FTDT and FTDT + Laser groups received intratumoral injections of FTDT (30 μL, 1.5 mg/mL in saline) every three days. Mice in the Saline and Laser alone groups received equal volumes of saline. Four hours after each injection, tumors in the Laser alone and FTDT + Laser groups were exposed to 808 nm laser irradiation (1.0 W/cm2, 5 min). Tumor volumes and body weights were recorded every two days throughout the treatment period. On day 14, all mice were euthanized, and major organs were harvested for histopathological analysis. Tumor growth curves and body weight changes were plotted based on the recorded data.

Immunohistochemical Staining

To assess tumor cell proliferation, Ki-67 immunohistochemical staining was performed on paraffin-embedded tumor sections. Briefly, excised tumors were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4°C, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections (5 μm thick) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H2O2, and non-specific binding was reduced by incubation with 3% goat serum. Sections were then incubated with a primary anti-Ki-67 antibody overnight at 4°C, followed by an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. The signal was developed using DAB substrate, and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Images were acquired under a bright-field microscope, and the percentage of Ki-67-positive nuclei was quantified for semi-quantitative analysis of proliferative activity.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) Staining

Tissue morphology was evaluated by conventional H&E staining. Deparaffinized and rehydrated sections were stained with hematoxylin for 30s, differentiated briefly, blued, and then counterstained with eosin for 10s. After dehydration through graded ethanol and clearing in xylene, sections were mounted with neutral resin and examined under a light microscope to assess overall tissue architecture and pathological changes.

Tissue Section TUNEL Staining

TUNEL staining was performed to detect late-stage apoptosis within tumor tissues. The procedure was conducted as follows: Paraffin-embedded sections were baked at 37°C for 2 h, deparaffinized in xylene I and II (30 min each), and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. After washing with PBS, tissue permeabilization was carried out using 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 min at room temperature. Sections were then equilibrated with TdT buffer for 5 min. The TUNEL reaction mixture containing TdT enzyme and fluorescein-dUTP was applied, and slides were incubated in a humidified chamber at 37°C for 2 h in the dark. The reaction was terminated with stop buffer, and sections were washed with PBS. For chromogenic detection, DAB substrate was added and developed for 5–10 min under microscopic monitoring. After counterstaining with methyl green and dehydration in n-butanol, sections were cleared in xylene and mounted with anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope for apoptotic cell analysis.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization

A schematic illustration of the synthetic route for FeOₓ-TiO2@PDA-TPP (FTDT) is provided in Figure 2a. Briefly, the FeOₓ-TiO2 (FT) composite was first prepared via a solvothermal method, in which ethylene glycol served as both the solvent and a mild reducing agent. Under the solvothermal conditions, hydrolysis and co-precipitation of the precursors occurred, yielding the mixed oxide composite. The obtained FT was then coated with polydopamine (PDA) via oxidative self-polymerization of dopamine in alkaline solution (Tris-HCl buffer, pH 8.8), forming FeOₓ-TiO2@PDA (FTD).41 To impart mitochondria-targeting functionality, FTD was further modified with triphenylphosphonium (TPP), yielding the final FTDT nanoplatform.

(a) Schematic illustration of the fabrication process of FTDT; (b and c) Representative TEM micrograph and (d) TEM-EDS elemental mapping images of FTD; (e) XRD patterns of as-synthesized FT (the symbols *, $, and indicate the characteristic diffraction peaks that correspond to the standard patterns of the respective crystalline phases); (f) High-resolution XPS spectrum of the Fe 2p region for FTD; (g) zeta potential values and (h) Hydrodynamic size distribution of FT, FTD and FTDT (mean ± SD, n = 3); (i) Hydrodynamic size distribution of FTDT over 14 days in different media (mean ± SD, n = 3); (j) UV-Vis-NIR absorption spectra of FT and FTD.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was first used to examine the morphology of FTD. As shown in Figures 2b and S1, the composite does not appear as isolated units but rather as irregular sheet-like networks formed by the PDA-mediated assembly of dendritic nanostructures. The primary building blocks are branched, tree-like structures with lateral dimensions ranging from 50 to 200 nm, leaving natural gaps between the branches (Figure 2c). After PDA coating, these dendritic units are bridged together through interfacial interactions such as hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking, creating continuous sheet-like or network aggregates. Within these sheets, numerous pores remain visible, originating from the incomplete filling of inter-branch gaps and shrinkage during PDA adhesion. Thus, the final structure is a porous, networked assembly of dendritic subunits glued by a thin PDA layer.

The spatial integration of the composite core and the functional shell was further visualized by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping (Figure 2d). Ti, Fe, O, and N are uniformly co-distributed across the entire nanostructure, with no evidence of localized aggregation or phase separation. This homogeneous colocalization directly visualizes two structural features critical to the design, namely the FeOx and TiO2 components are uniformly distributed throughout the composite, suggesting intimate integration at the nanoscale; and the PDA shell uniformly encases the composite core, enabling the entire particle surface to serve as a platform for TPP conjugation.42 This assignment is further supported by FTIR, zeta potential, and DLS analysis, as discussed below.

The crystal structure of the FT core was examined by X-ray diffraction (XRD). As shown in Figure 2e, the diffraction pattern exhibits well-defined peaks corresponding to anatase TiO2 at 25.2°, 37.6°, and 48.1°, alongside distinct signals attributable to FeO at 60.2° and α-Fe2O3 at 32.8°. The coexistence of these iron oxide phases is significant because FeO bears Fe2⁺ while α-Fe2O3 bears Fe3+.

To verify that this mixed-valence composition indeed translates into accessible surface chemistry, we turned to X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Figure S2). The high-resolution Fe 2p spectrum in Figure 2f was deconvoluted into peaks corresponding to Fe2+ at 723.71 eV and 710.58 eV, Fe3+ at 727.21 eV and 714.40 eV, and satellite features at 732.34 eV and 719.0 eV.43,44 Peak area analysis revealed a distribution that strikes a critical functional balance, with sufficient Fe2+ to drive Fenton-like ·OH generation and enough Fe3+ to enable sustained GSH depletion as well as the regeneration of Fe2+ from Fe3+.10,20 This mixed-valence architecture provides the material foundation for the redox interplay between Fenton catalysis and GSH depletion, meaning that the surface of the as-synthesized FT core already contains the full catalytic capability required for a self-sustaining redox cycle.

Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy (Figure S3) revealed two new absorption features in the FTD composite compared to bare FT. These included a C–O stretching vibration at 1260 cm−1 and an N–H bending vibration coupled with aromatic ring vibrations at 1480 cm−1, both characteristic of PDA.45 Stepwise surface charge evolution monitored by zeta potential (Figure 2g) provided electrochemical evidence of layer-by-layer assembly, as the pristine FT core exhibited a negative potential of –32 mV, which shifted to –28 mV after PDA coating and further to –24 mV after TPP conjugation. This consistent, directional shift confirms the successful grafting of each functional layer and the preservation of colloidal stability, a prerequisite for subsequent biological application.45

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed a gradual increase in hydrodynamic diameter (Figure 2h). This progressive enlargement is attributed to the successive surface modifications, namely the PDA shell coating and TPP ligand conjugation, which introduce additional hydration layers and steric bulk. Despite this increase, the final size of FTDT remains within the sub-200 nm range, a dimension favorable for passive tumor accumulation via the enhanced permeability and retention effect.22

The colloidal stability of FTDT was further evaluated by monitoring its hydrodynamic size in different media over 14 days. As shown in Figure 2i, the nanoplatform maintained a consistent hydrodynamic diameter in water, PBS, normal saline, and fetal bovine serum, with no significant size fluctuation or aggregation observed throughout the two-week period.13 This excellent stability correlates well with the zeta potential measurements described earlier. Although the absolute surface charge decreased upon modification, the remaining negative potential still provides sufficient electrostatic repulsion to prevent particle aggregation. Together with the steric stabilization conferred by the hydrophilic PDA shell and the surface-grafted TPP ligands, these electrostatic forces collectively ensure the colloidal integrity of FTDT in diverse physiological environments.

The optical absorption properties of FT and FTD derive from a two-tiered enhancement strategy involving intrinsic bandgap engineering by FT compositing and extrinsic amplification by the conformal PDA shell. As shown in Figure 2e, the bare FT composite displays a characteristic absorption edge extending to approximately 600 nm, substantially red-shifted from the UV-only absorption of anatase TiO2.46 This intrinsic broadening is attributable to the formation of Fe-O-Ti interfacial linkages within the composite, which introduce mid-gap electronic states and enable sub-bandgap photon harvesting.47,48 Consequently, the FT composite exhibits weak but detectable absorption in the 800–1000 nm region originating from the same mid-gap states. This FeOx-mediated spectral extension provides the foundational capability for visible-to-NIR excitation, a necessary precondition for deep-tissue phototherapy. Upon PDA coating, this basal photoresponse is substantially amplified. The FTD composite exhibits markedly enhanced absorption intensity across the entire 600–1200 nm NIR region, with a particularly pronounced increase in the 800–1000 nm range (Figure 2j). Critically, the PDA shell functions as both a broadband light-harvesting antenna and an electron-transfer mediator, synergistically amplifying the intrinsic absorption of the FT core rather than simply overlaying its own spectral signature. As a result, the FTDT nanoplatform acquires efficient photoactivation capability at 808 nm, a wavelength compatible with deep-tissue penetration, thereby establishing the optical foundation for both subsequent photodynamic and photothermal applications.

Extracellular Catalytic Activity

The FeOx component endows FTDT with intrinsic catalase-mimetic activity, enabling TME-responsive O2 generation to alleviate tumor hypoxia. This functionality originates from the mixed-valence iron centers, which catalyze the decomposition of H2O2 into O2. As shown in Figure 3a, all FT-derived samples exhibited sustained O2 production upon H2O2 exposure, confirming that the catalytic activity is inherent to FeOx and remains functional after surface modification. The coated variants showed moderately lower activity than bare FT, consistent with partial hindrance of substrate accessibility by the PDA shell and TPP ligands, but retained substantial catalytic function. Importantly, FTDT displayed a well-defined concentration-dependent response (Figure 3b), with O2 production increasing progressively at higher H2O2 concentrations, confirming its ability to exploit endogenous H2O2 gradients as a controllable in situ O2 generator.5,9

Beyond its oxygen-supplying function, the PDA shell enhances the NIR absorption of the FT core, thereby enabling it to function as an efficient photosensitizer under 808 nm irradiation. Consistent with the UV-Vis-NIR absorption results described above, the PDA-coated samples (FTD and FTDT) generated substantially more singlet oxygen than the bare FT nanoparticles, as evidenced by DPBF probe assays (Figures 3c and S4–S6).34 This enhancement is directly attributable to the PDA shell’s ability to capture NIR photons and channel their energy into the photosensitization process, thereby effectively extending the composite’s therapeutic reach into the deep-tissue optical window.

The interplay between these two functions becomes evident when H2O2 is introduced into the system. Under identical irradiation conditions, the presence of H2O2 markedly accelerated 1O2 production across all FT-derived samples (Figure 3d). The reason traces back to the catalase-like activity described earlier. The FeOx component continuously decomposes H2O2 into O2, and this locally generated oxygen becomes immediately available for the photosensitization reaction.49 What might appear as a modest enhancement in the absorbance plot is in fact a mechanistic coupling between two otherwise independent functions. One consumes H2O2 to generate O2, and the other consumes O2 to generate 1O2. Together, they create a self-sustaining loop within the H2O2-rich tumor microenvironment, where more H2O2 means more O2, and more O2 means more 1O2 upon irradiation.5,35 This integrated mechanism allows FTDT to remain effective even under hypoxic conditions, distinguishing it from conventional photosensitizers that rely solely on pre-existing oxygen. The generation of 1O2 was further verified by ESR measurements, in which the raw signal was relatively weak but a clear 1O2 signal could be identified after spectral fitting (Figure S7).

Beyond its catalase-like activity, the FeOx component also exhibits peroxidase-like activity, enabling a parallel therapeutic pathway that operates independently of oxygen.50 This activity was evaluated using TMB as a chromogenic substrate.51,52 As shown in Figure 3e, incubation of FTDT with H2O2 resulted in a progressive increase in absorbance at 650 nm, indicating sustained ·OH production. The catalytic activity originates from the accessible Fe2⁺ sites within the FeOx component, which mediate electron transfer to H2O2 and facilitate its decomposition into ·OH. Notably, this reaction operates independently of both light and oxygen, enabling therapeutic activity in the hypoxic, dark regions of solid tumors where photodynamic therapy is inherently limited. The generation of ·OH was further confirmed by ESR measurements, which showed clear and unambiguous ·OH signals (Figure S8).

Beyond generating 1O2 and ·OH, FTDT also efficiently consumes intracellular glutathione, a key antioxidant that would otherwise neutralize oxidative stress.33 As shown in Figure 3f, incubation of FTDT with GSH resulted in a progressive decrease in GSH concentration, confirming efficient consumption of this thiol-containing antioxidant. This depletion is attributed to the Fe3⁺ sites within the composite, which oxidize GSH to its disulfide form (GSSG). To directly probe the coupling between GSH consumption and Fe3+ reduction, we monitored Fe2+ concentration in the reaction system using the o-phenanthroline chromogenic method under acidic conditions (Figure 3g). In the absence of GSH, an obviously increase in Fe2+ concentration was observed during the initial stage, likely reflecting surface-exposed Fe2+ released from the nanoplatform upon dispersion. Notably, when GSH was introduced, the detected Fe2+ concentration was substantially higher throughout the observation period. This GSH-dependent elevation in Fe2+ provides direct evidence that GSH oxidation is accompanied by the reduction of Fe3+ to Fe2+.

Collectively, these data reveal a self-sustaining catalytic engine built on the mixed-valence Fe2+/Fe3+ pairs in the FeOx component. Initially, Fe2+ converts H2O2 into ⋅OH via Fenton chemistry, a process that generates oxidative stress and produces Fe3+. Meanwhile, the pre-existing Fe3+ oxidizes GSH to GSSG, which consumes the antioxidant and concurrently regenerates Fe2+. Critically, the Fe3+ from the Fenton reaction compensates for the Fe3+ lost during GSH oxidation, just as the Fe2+ from GSH oxidation replenishes the Fe2+ consumed in the Fenton cycle. This mutual replenishment forms a closed-loop redox cycle (Figure 3h), where each ⋅OH production event supports GSH depletion and vice versa. Because the tumor microenvironment is rich in both GSH and H2O2, this engine runs continuously, amplifying oxidative stress and effectively turning FTDT into a mitochondrion-seeking triple-modal warhead.

Photothermal Performance Evaluation

The FTDT nanoplatform also functions as an efficient photothermal converter, completing the triple-modal therapeutic arsenal. Under 808 nm irradiation at 1.0 W cm−2, aqueous dispersions of FTD and FTDT reached 55.7°C and 52.95°C within five minutes, respectively (Figure 4a). Pure PDA suspensions under identical conditions plateaued at 44.9°C, while FT alone and water showed negligible temperature change. This thermal hierarchy, with FTD and FTDT substantially outperforming either component alone, points to a synergistic effect at the FT-PDA interface. The FT core contributes its own NIR absorption, while the PDA shell captures additional photons and facilitates non-radiative relaxation. Together, they convert more incident energy into heat than either material in isolation.

The photothermal output is tunable across a clinically relevant range. Raising the laser power density from 0.5 to 2.0 W cm−2 increased the equilibrium temperature of FTDT dispersions from 40.3°C to 78.3°C (Figure 4b). At a fixed power of 1.0 W cm−2, increasing the concentration from 250 to 1000 μg mL−1 raised the temperature from 41.0°C to 65.6°C (Figure 4c). Infrared thermal imaging confirmed that heating remains confined to the illuminated area (Figure 4d), a prerequisite for spatially precise thermal ablation.

The nanoplatform also demonstrated excellent durability under repeated use. FTDT retained its full photothermal capacity over five consecutive on-off cycles, with no attenuation in peak temperature (Figure 4e). Quantitative analysis placed the photothermal conversion efficiency at 34.4% (Figure 4f), a value reflecting the combined contribution of both components and their interfacial synergy.

Targeting Efficiency and in vitro Anti-Tumor Activity

Cellular internalization of FTDT was time-dependent, with optimal accumulation observed at 4 hours post-incubation (Figure 5a). Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) labeled FTDT was incubated with A549 cells, and intracellular distribution was visualized by fluorescence microscopy (Figure S9). As shown in Figure 5a, robust green fluorescence from FTDT-FITC appeared throughout the cytoplasm, clearly delineating the DAPI stained nuclei. Time-course analysis revealed progressive uptake, with fluorescence intensity reaching its maximum at 4 hours and gradually declining by 8 hours (Figure S10). Based on these kinetics, a 4-hour incubation period was adopted for subsequent experiments to ensure maximal intracellular delivery prior to therapeutic assessment.

TPP functionalization successfully directed FTDT to mitochondria after cellular entry. To evaluate subcellular localization, cells were co-stained with Mito-Tracker Red following FTDT-FITC incubation.30 Control cells exhibited only the characteristic red mitochondrial signal. In contrast, FTDT-FITC treated cells displayed strong green fluorescence that extensively overlapped with the Mito-Tracker Red network (Figure 5b), yielding distinct yellow regions in the merged images. This spatial colocalization confirms that the TPP ligands anchored to the particle surface retain their mitochondria-targeting function after internalization, directing the nanoplatform to its intended subcellular destination. Quantitative analysis further supported this observation. The colocalization coefficient increased from 0.68 for the non-targeted FTD to 0.87 for the TPP-modified FTDT (Figure S11), confirming that TPP conjugation significantly enhances mitochondrial accumulation. By concentrating therapeutic action precisely within this organelle, the subsequent photodynamic, photothermal, and chemodynamic effects are confined to the very compartment where oxidative damage most efficiently triggers apoptotic cascades, directly addressing the fundamental limitation of ultrashort ROS diffusion distances.

With efficient cellular uptake and mitochondrial targeting established, we proceeded to evaluate the cytotoxicity of the nanoplatforms. Prior to efficacy evaluation, the biocompatibility of all formulations was assessed in normal human ovarian epithelial cells (IOSE-80). As shown in Figure 5c, IOSE-80 cells maintained viability above 80% after 24 h incubation with FT, FTD, and FTDT even at the highest tested concentration, confirming acceptable biosafety toward non-cancerous cells. This lack of cytotoxicity toward normal cells can be attributed to their distinct physiological characteristics. Normal cells maintain lower baseline oxidative stress levels and possess more robust antioxidant defense systems compared to cancer cells. Moreover, the mildly acidic pH typical of the tumor microenvironment, which is absent in normal tissues, is a key factor for the Fenton-like reaction and GSH depletion driven by FT. These physiological features render normal cells less susceptible to chemodynamic damage induced by the nanoplatform.

We then examined the antitumor activity of these formulations against A549 cancer cells. In the absence of light, each formulation induced significant A549 cell death, confirming a light-independent therapeutic mechanism (Figure 5d). This dark toxicity is attributed to the chemodynamic activity of the FeOₓ component, which, as demonstrated in the extracellular assays, generates cytotoxic ·OH through a self-amplifying redox cycle while simultaneously depleting intracellular GSH. The combined effect weakens the cellular antioxidant defense and establishes sustained oxidative stress without external stimulation. Under 808 nm irradiation, the cytotoxic effect was substantially amplified. While FT-treated cells showed no significant difference between dark and light conditions, both FTD and FTDT exhibited markedly enhanced cell killing upon irradiation.

To further dissect the contribution of TPP-mediated mitochondrial targeting, we directly compared the cytotoxicity of non-targeted FTD and targeted FTDT under both dark and irradiated conditions (Figure 5e). The detailed viability data and the percentage differences between FTD and FTDT at each concentration are provided in Table S1. In the absence of light, FTDT exhibited higher cytotoxicity than FTD at lower concentrations, confirming that directing the nanoplatform to mitochondria enhances chemodynamic efficacy even without external stimulation. The convergence of the two curves at higher concentrations reflects the saturation of cellular antioxidant capacity by excess ·OH, a condition under which subcellular localization becomes less critical. Upon NIR irradiation, FTDT consistently outperformed FTD across the entire concentration range, demonstrating that the targeting effect is further amplified under light-triggered multimodal therapy. This persistent advantage underscores the indispensable role of TPP in concentrating photodynamic, photothermal, and chemodynamic insults simultaneously within mitochondria, an effect that non-targeted FTD cannot achieve regardless of concentration. Collectively, these data confirm that TPP functionalization effectively directs FTDT to mitochondria and that this subcellular localization translates into enhanced therapeutic efficacy, particularly under the synergistic action of light-activated modalities.

To gain insight into the mechanistic basis of the enhanced efficacy observed with FTDT, we measured intracellular GSH levels following different treatments. As shown in Figure 5f, the GSH depletion capacity followed a clear hierarchy. FT, which lacks the PDA shell and TPP ligands, exhibited the highest GSH consumption, likely due to its unhindered surface accessibility for Fe3+-mediated oxidation as well as the ·OH generated by its chemodynamic activity, which also reacts with and consumes GSH. FTD showed moderately reduced depletion, consistent with the PDA shell partially blocking substrate access. FTDT, with the additional TPP layer, displayed the lowest GSH consumption among the non-irradiated groups. This can be attributed to the positively charged TPP ligands electrostatically hindering the anionic GSH from approaching the particle surface, as well as the preferential localization of FTDT within mitochondria where GSH concentration is lower.

Notably, when FTDT was exposed to 808 nm irradiation, the GSH depletion efficiency increased substantially, surpassing even that of FT. This light-triggered enhancement arises from two synergistic effects. First, the photodynamic activity of the PDA shell generates additional singlet oxygen (1O2), which further reacts with and consumes GSH. Second, the photothermal conversion elevates local temperature, accelerating both the Fenton reaction and the redox reaction between Fe3+ and GSH, thereby promoting GSH depletion from multiple pathways. Thus, although TPP modification reduces dark GSH consumption, the combination of mitochondrial targeting, light-triggered 1O2 production, and photothermal acceleration ensures that GSH is depleted efficiently upon irradiation, precisely where it is most needed. This spatially confined and light-enhanced GSH depletion weakens the cellular antioxidant defense, thereby amplifying the oxidative stress inflicted by the self-sustaining catalytic engine and the triple-modal warhead, and ultimately leading to superior therapeutic efficacy.

Mitochondrial and Membrane Oxidative Injury

Having established that FTDT generates robust light-triggered ROS production, we next investigated whether the spatial localization of these oxidative insults translates into organelle damage and amplified cytotoxicity. Changes in mitochondrial membrane potential were assessed using the JC-1 probe, which aggregates in healthy mitochondria to emit red fluorescence and dissociates into green-fluorescing monomers upon depolarization.23 As shown in Figure 7a and b, control cells exhibited strong red emission with minimal green signal, confirming intact mitochondrial integrity. Treatment with FTD or FTDT in the dark caused a modest shift toward green fluorescence, indicating mild depolarization attributable to the constitutive chemodynamic activity of the FeOx component. Under 808 nm laser irradiation, a distinct divergence emerged. FTDT induced a pronounced loss of red fluorescence and a corresponding increase in green signal, reflecting severe mitochondrial depolarization, whereas FTD produced a notably weaker effect. This disparity underscores the critical contribution of TPP-mediated targeting to the amplification of mitochondrial dysfunction, as the colocalization imaging confirms that TPP functionalization drives the specific accumulation of FTDT within mitochondria. By concentrating photodynamic, photothermal, and chemodynamic actions within this organelle, FTDT circumvents the short diffusion distances that limit conventional ROS-based therapies, thereby establishing a direct mechanistic link between subcellular targeting and the enhanced cell death response.

The mitochondrial dysfunction induced by FTDT under irradiation was accompanied by secondary damage to other intracellular compartments. Lysosomal membrane integrity was assessed using acridine orange (AO) staining, which accumulates in intact acidic lysosomes to emit red fluorescence.39 As shown in Figure 7c and d, control cells exhibited bright red fluorescence, confirming intact lysosomal compartments. In the FTDT with laser irradiation group, a marked reduction in red fluorescence accompanied by diffuse green cytosolic signal was observed, reflecting lysosomal membrane permeabilization. Concurrent lipid peroxidation was detected using the BODIPY 581/591 C11 probe, which shifts from red to green fluorescence upon oxidation of membrane lipids. As shown in Figure 7e and f, cells treated with FTDT under irradiation displayed a pronounced shift toward green fluorescence, confirming extensive oxidation of cellular membranes.

These secondary effects arise from the propagation of oxidative and thermal stress beyond mitochondria. The ROS and hyperthermia generated within mitochondria compromise lysosomal membrane stability and promote lipid peroxidation, amplifying the overall cellular damage. By simultaneously disrupting mitochondrial function, lysosomal integrity, and membrane homeostasis, FTDT overwhelms multiple cellular defense systems, contributing to the potent cytotoxicity observed in the combined treatment regimen.

Hemocompatibility Assessment and in vivo Thermal Effects

In vivo antitumor efficacy of FTDT was evaluated using A549 tumor-bearing BALB/c nude mice (Figure 8a). Given that the in vitro experiments had already confirmed the advantage of TPP-mediated mitochondrial targeting, when tumors reached a palpable size of 100–150 mm3, mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 5): PBS, PBS with laser irradiation, FTDT alone, and FTDT with laser irradiation. For treatment, mice received a single intratumoral injection of 40 μL FTDT (2 mg·mL−1) or PBS. In groups receiving laser irradiation, the tumor region was exposed to an 808 nm laser (1.0 W·cm−2) for 5 minutes, 4 hours post-injection. During irradiation, tumor temperature changes were monitored in real time using an infrared thermal camera. Tumor volumes and body weights were recorded every two days over the 14-day treatment period. The multiple-dose schedule was used to reduce the risk of acute tissue damage associated with single high-intensity irradiation.

FTDT exhibited excellent hemocompatibility, meeting the safety requirements for systemic administration. Hemolysis assays were first conducted to evaluate the potential for erythrocyte damage upon exposure to the nanoplatform. As shown in Figure 8b, even at a concentration of 1000 μg·mL−1, the hemolysis rate remained at 3.32%, well below the 5% safety threshold established by international standards for hemocompatible materials. This low hemolytic activity indicates that FTDT does not induce significant erythrocyte membrane disruption. The favorable blood compatibility, together with the in vitro cytotoxicity data, supports the suitability of FTDT for subsequent in vivo evaluation.

The photothermal performance of FTDT in vivo was assessed by real-time infrared thermal imaging. As shown in Figure 8c and d, tumors injected with FTDT exhibited a rapid temperature increase within 5 minutes of irradiation, reaching approximately 50°C. In contrast, PBS-injected tumors showed minimal temperature elevation under identical conditions. Serial thermal images visually confirmed this localized heating effect, revealing a progressive increase in temperature over time with the hot spot strictly confined to the tumor region, indicating minimal heat diffusion to surrounding healthy tissues. This sharp, localized heating profile confirms that FTDT is selectively retained within the tumor and efficiently converts NIR light into therapeutic heat.

Evaluation of Antitumor Efficacy in vivo

As shown in Figure 9a, representative tumor photographs at the end of the study revealed clear differences across groups. Tumors in the PBS and PBS with laser irradiation groups continued to grow progressively, maintaining a large appearance. FTDT alone resulted in markedly slower growth and visibly reduced tumor size. The FTDT with laser irradiation group exhibited the most striking effect, with tumors showing significant atrophy and surface wrinkling (Figure 9d). Quantitative analysis of tumor volume kinetics and final tumor weights (Figure 9b) corroborated these observations. The FTDT with laser irradiation group consistently displayed the smallest tumor volumes and the lowest final tumor masses, confirming that the combination of photothermal, photodynamic, and chemodynamic modalities produces a synergistic therapeutic outcome. It is worth noting that complete tumor ablation was not achieved under the current treatment conditions. Nevertheless, the current parameters provided a clear baseline for evaluating the synergistic mechanism, and the findings validate the therapeutic potential of integrating multiple modalities into a single inorganic composite. Further optimization of dosage and irradiation conditions may enhance the therapeutic efficacy.

The treatment regimen was well tolerated (Figure 9c). Throughout the 14-day study period, all groups maintained stable body weights, indicating that neither FTDT administration nor NIR irradiation induced significant adverse effects. This favorable safety profile, together with the potent antitumor efficacy, supports the translational potential of FTDT as a multimodal therapeutic nanoplatform.

Histological analysis of tumor tissues confirmed that FTDT combined with NIR irradiation induced extensive apoptosis and suppressed proliferation, consistent with the multifaceted mechanisms of action identified in vitro. Tumor sections from each treatment group were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to assess morphological changes (Figure 9e). PBS and PBS + Laser groups exhibited densely packed tumor cells with intact morphology, reflecting uncontrolled tumor growth. The FTDT monotherapy group showed only minor signs of early damage, such as slight nuclear-cytoplasmic separation. In contrast, the FTDT + Laser group displayed extensive apoptotic and necrotic features, including pronounced nuclear pyknosis, chromatin condensation, and nuclear fragmentation, indicative of profound cellular destruction.

TUNEL staining, which labels DNA fragmentation associated with late-stage apoptosis, further corroborated these findings (Figure 9f). Minimal TUNEL-positive signal was detected in the PBS and PBS + Laser groups, indicating a low basal level of apoptosis. Positive staining increased modestly in the FTDT group and rose most dramatically in the FTDT + Laser group, confirming that the combination treatment potently activates programmed cell death pathways.

Ki-67 immunohistochemistry was used to evaluate tumor cell proliferation (Figure 9f). Tumors from the PBS and PBS + Laser groups contained abundant Ki-67-positive nuclei, reflecting a highly proliferative state. Ki-67 expression was markedly reduced in the FTDT group and nearly absent in the FTDT + Laser group, demonstrating that the combined treatment effectively suppresses tumor cell proliferation.

The favorable biosafety profile of FTDT was supported by histological examination of major organs. H&E-stained sections of heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney from all treatment groups showed intact tissue architecture and normal cellular morphology (Figure 9e). No evidence of degeneration, necrosis, or inflammatory infiltration was observed, indicating that FTDT administration, with or without NIR irradiation, does not induce detectable systemic toxicity. Serum biochemical analysis further confirmed the safety of FTDT, as the levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and other related liver and kidney function parameters showed no significant differences compared with the PBS control (Figure S12). Collectively, these data confirm that the potent antitumor efficacy of FTDT is achieved without compromising normal tissue integrity, supporting its potential for further translational development.

Conclusion

We reported a FeOx-TiO2 composite with pre-existing Fe2+/Fe3+ pairs that establishes a self-compensating redox cycle. The cycle sustains. OH generation and GSH depletion without external energy input. The same composite exhibits catalase-like activity for hypoxia relief, and Fe–O–Ti interfacial linkages extend its photoresponse into the visible region. Moreover, the FeOx-TiO2 component itself acts as a photosensitizer; a thin PDA coating further boosts near-infrared absorption, enabling efficient photodynamic activity and a photothermal conversion efficiency of 34.4% under 808 nm light. Thus, all three therapeutic modalitie, namely chemodynamic, photodynamic and photothermal, originate from a single inorganic composite, not from physically mixed components. Cell experiments validated the functional role of TPP-mediated mitochondrial targeting. Direct comparison between targeted (FTDT) and non-targeted formulations showed that TPP conjugation significantly enhances cytotoxicity. Beyond this specific FeOx-TiO2 system, the mixed-valence strategy is generalizable to other transition metals (Cu, Mn, Ce, etc) for constructing self-fueling catalytic platforms. This work opens a new direction for designing inorganic composites where multiple functions emerge from a unified physicochemical foundation, bridging the gap between fundamental inorganic chemistry and catalytic biomedicine.
