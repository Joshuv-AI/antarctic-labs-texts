Prevalence of valgus and varus foot deformities in 2784 children with cerebral palsy, a register-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Evgenia Manousaki, Elisabet Rodby-Bousquet, Katina Pettersson, Jenny Hedberg-Graff, Erika Cloodt
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFoot deformities, such as valgus and varus in the coronal plane and equinus in the sagittal plane, are common in children with cerebral palsy (CP). The purpose of this study was to describe the prevalence of coronal plane foot deformities and their association with the Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS) level, age, CP subtype, and equinus in children with CP.MethodsA cross-sectional study was performed of 2784 children (1644 boys, 1140 girls), mean age 10 years, 2 months (standard deviation, 4.83), from the Swedish CP Follow-up Program and registry for 2021–2023. Single and multiple binary regression analyses estimated the association between coronal plane foot deformities (valgus or varus) and sex, age, GMFCS level, CP subtype, and equinus.ResultsMore than half (58%) the children with CP had valgus feet and 6% had varus feet. Valgus feet were more common in young children with high GMFCS levels, whereas the number of varus feet remained consistently low across all GMFCS levels. The prevalence of valgus feet was lower in older children at GMFCS I and II, but remained high in older children at GMFCS III–V. Coronal plane foot deformities were associated with higher GMFCS levels (odds ratio [OR] 11, 95% confidence interval [CI] 8–15 for GMFCS V), lower age (OR 1.5, 95% CI 1.3–1.7), and equinus (OR 1.9, 95% CI 1.4–2.5).ConclusionsMost children with CP have a coronal plane foot deformity. Valgus is most commonly associated with higher GMFCS levels and lower age. These findings contribute to a mapping of the children with an increased risk of foot deformities and also highlight the need for continuous follow-up of foot deformities in children with CP.Keywords: Cerebral palsy, Valgus, Varus, Equinus, Foot deformities

Background

Foot deformities such as valgus or varus in the coronal plane and equinus in the sagittal plane are common in children with cerebral palsy (CP) [1–6]. Their definitions vary in the literature, mainly describing the position of the hindfoot in valgus or varus, the reduced dorsiflexion of the ankle in equinus, commonly combined and/or in association with forefoot deviations [1–3, 7]. The development of foot deformities is multifactorial and is due to muscle imbalance and spasticity during growth [1–3, 6, 8]. In ambulatory children, foot deformities may affect standing and gait and result in tripping and falling when walking [6, 9]. Furthermore, foot deformities can cause pain, pressure, and difficulty wearing shoes in both ambulatory and nonambulatory children [1–3].

Surgery and conservative treatment, such as physical therapy, insoles, ankle-foot orthoses, and botulinum neurotoxin A (BoNT-A) injections, are frequently used to manage foot deformities in children with CP [1, 2, 6, 10–14]. Foot and ankle surgeries stand out as the most prevalent surgical procedures in ambulatory children where 30% of all children with CP are likely to have foot and ankle surgery before the age of 15 years [11].

Combinations of equinus and coronal plane deformities may develop over time where equinus can go into either varus or valgus. It has been suggested that coronal foot deformities may continue to include equinus [15]. Although the presence of equinus has been widely studied [4], to our knowledge, no studies have investigated the prevalence of coronal plane foot deformities (valgus and varus) in a large population of children with CP at all levels of motor function and its association with equinus. In Sweden, children with CP are enrolled in a multidisciplinary follow-up program and registry (CPUP) with repeated examinations [16, 17]. The follow-up enables identification of malalignments [17–19] and examination of foot deformities as part of the protocol.

The purpose of this study was to describe the prevalence of coronal plane foot deformities (valgus and varus) and their association with Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS) level, age, CP subtype, and equinus in children with CP.

Methods

This was a cross-sectional study based on data from CPUP of all children with CP aged 0–18 years, reported in the registry between 2021 and 2023. The Swedish Ethical Review Authority approved the study (2023-01723-01) and permission to extract data was obtained from the registry. Data from the most recent clinical examination by the child’s local physiotherapist were used. Gross motor function was classified into levels I–V according to the expanded and revised version of the GMFCS [20]. Subtype was classified by neuropaediatricians into spastic (unilateral or bilateral), dyskinetic, ataxic, or unclassifiable/mixed CP.

In the coronal plane, the position of the hindfoot during weight-bearing, with or without support, was rated as normal, valgus, or varus (Fig. 1). In the sagittal plane, foot dorsiflexion was measured in a standardized position with the subtalar joints stabilized and the eversion of the forefoot minimized. Equinus was defined as no more than − 5 degrees of passive dorsiflexion at goniometric measurement with the child lying supine with extended knees. All measurements were performed by pediatric physiotherapists according CPUPs standardized protocol. The physiotherapists receive adequate training with regular updates.

Classification of the position of the hindfoot during weight-bearing in normal, varus, and valgus. Figure from the registry (CPUP) manual. With permission from CPUP

Each foot was analyzed individually. Children with unilateral spastic CP were identified and only the affected foot was used in the analysis. Both feet were included in children with ataxic, dyskinetic, spastic bilateral, or unclassified/mixed CP. Children were included if they had hindfoot position, subtype, GMFCS and sex reported. No children were excluded based on age or GMFCS level.

Statistics

In the statistical analyses, age was categorized into two age groups (0–6 and 7–18 years). Categorical variables were described by frequencies (n) and percentages (%). Single and multiple binary regression analyses were used to estimate associations between coronal plane foot deformities (valgus/varus) and sex, age, GMFCS level, CP subtype, and sagittal foot deformity (equinus). SPSS 27 (IBM Corp. Armonk, NY, USA) was used for the statistical analyses with P values < 0.05 considered significant.

Results

The initial dataset comprised 4390 children with 7294 feet after excluding the nonaffected foot in children with spastic unilateral CP. Children with missing information on foot position (36%), sex (0.0009%), or subtype (0.006%) were excluded (Fig. 2). The excluded children covered all GMFCS levels and age groups but children with a higher GMFCS level had more missing data of valgus and varus in a weight-bearing position (Fig. 2). After exclusion of those with missing data, 2784 children with 4601 feet remained, 1644 boys (59%) and 1140 girls (41%). The mean age was 10 years, 2 months (standard deviation 4.83, range 0–18 years) (Table 1). There were three infants below the age of 12 months, ranging from 10 months and 5 days to 11 months and 24 days.

In the coronal plane, valgus was the predominant foot deformity (58%) across all GMFCS levels and age groups, whereas 6% had a varus deformity and 36% of the feet had a neutral hindfoot position. The prevalence of valgus increased with higher GMFCS levels (from 35% at GMFCS I to 86% at GMFCS IV). The prevalence of valgus was lower in older children at GMFCS I and II but remained high in older children at GMFCS III–V (Table 2; Fig. 3).

There were no differences in valgus or varus between boys and girls. The prevalence of varus was low and remained consistently low across all GMFCS levels (Table 2; Fig. 3). In the sagittal plane, 8% of the feet had equinus. Equinus was more common in feet with valgus or varus deformity (10%) than in feet with a neutral hindfoot (5%).

Coronal plane foot deformities were associated with a higher GMFCS level (odds ratio [OR] 11, 95% confidence interval [CI] 8–15 for GMFCS V), lower age (OR 1.5, 95% CI 1.3–1.7), and equinus feet (OR 1.9, 95% CI 1.4–2.5) (Table 3). There were no significant differences between subtypes when adjusted for GMFCS level and other variables. Only dyskinetic CP was associated with an increased risk for coronal plane foot deformities, in the unadjusted analysis (OR 1.9, 95% CI 1.5–2.3), and a reduced risk (OR 0.6, 95% CI 0.5–0.8) when adjusted for all other variables in the model (Table 3).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first register-based study analyzing the prevalence of coronal foot deformities in children with CP. A majority of the children with CP had a valgus foot deformity. Valgus was associated with higher GMFCS levels and younger age. Equinus was identified in ~ 10% of the children with coronal plane foot deformities and was associated with higher GMFCS levels and older age.

Valgus feet in children without CP was previously reported to improve with age [21, 22]. In our study, in children at low GMFCS levels (I and II), the prevalence of valgus decreased with age implying that children at low GMFCS levels followed this tendency. Previous studies [23–25] have found that high GMFCS levels influence the progression of valgus, being more severe at high GMFCS levels. This is in line with the findings of our study, as valgus feet in children at higher GMFCS levels (III–V) remained high or even increased.

The high prevalence of valgus deformity across age groups in children at higher GMFCS levels might be explained by the more severe muscle impairment in the lower limb muscles with higher GMFCS levels [8, 26, 27]. This suggests that the valgus deformity might arise from changes in foot development induced by altered muscle growth, given prior findings indicating that hip dysplasia can stem from deviations in hip development [28]. Potentially early interventions and protection from abnormal loading, especially in children at GMFCS III–V, might decrease the development of valgus deformity.

Unlike valgus, varus deformity is not present in normally developing feet. The presence of varus is considered pathological, and it is often associated in the literature with equinus and cavus [1–3, 5, 29, 30]. Surgery is frequently indicated for varus deformity and soft tissue surgery is usually preferred when the deformity is flexible and bony surgery when it is fixed [1–3, 5, 29]. The prevalence of varus in this study is lower than in other studies [5, 23, 25]. The relatively low prevalence of varus in our cohort may relate to the fact that this deformity is rarely left untreated. The children in our cohort are followed up through CPUP and may have already received treatment [18]. On the other hand, ours is the only register-based study that describes the prevalence of varus in children with CP. The high prevalence reported in other studies might also be influenced by the criteria used for patient selection.

Coronal plane deformities alter foot load and may affect postural control, lead to reduced stride length and crouch gait, thereby reducing walking and running efficiency [9, 31]. Furthermore, a deformity might cause pain resulting in avoiding participation in activities. For these children, it can be of value to find opportunities for compensatory treatment options that promote the children to continue to be active. The high prevalence of coronal foot deformities in our study implies that prevention from developing valgus/varus and treatment is needed. Knowledge of the prevalence of these deformities in different ages and GMFCS levels is important to improve management options.

Church et al. (2017, 2024) [23, 25] recommend waiting and conservative treatment for valgus feet, using orthoses, BoNT-A injections and serial casting in children at lower GMFCS levels, although surgery might be indicated in nonambulatory children at higher GMFCS levels. The differences in prevalence of foot deformities found in our study, across different GMFCS levels and age groups, support this recommendation and should be taken into consideration when a treatment strategy to correct valgus is chosen. However, there is no consensus about the timing and type of intervention to correct coronal plane deformities (valgus/varus) in children with CP [1, 2, 6, 10, 23–25, 29]. Poorly planned surgery could result in overcorrection or recurrence. The results of the present study contribute with new knowledge about the prevalence of foot deformities in children with CP, emphasizing the importance of considering both age and GMFCS level when establishing guidelines to avoid the development of severe fixed deformities or functional loss resulting from valgus or varus feet.

The prevalence of equinus, either in conjunction with valgus or varus, exhibits large variation in the literature [3–5, 7]. Some studies report a prevalence of 90%, although as low as 10% has been noted. This discrepancy is due to variation in the definition of equinus across studies [7]. Some studies define equinus during gait and others during passive dorsiflexion. In addition, various degrees of dorsiflexion are used as cutoff, and whether the knee is extended during the examination. Most studies use 0 degrees of dorsiflexion of the ankle joint as cutoff, but when used as an indication for surgery, − 10 degrees might be more appropriate. In this study, we chose a cutoff of − 5 degrees with extended knee, as we believe it is clinically significant. Our study showed that children with coronal foot deformities had higher association with equinus than children with neutral hindfoot position. This is in line with other studies, even if the overall prevalence of equinus is lower in our study [3–5, 7]. The low prevalence of equinus may also relate to the fact that the primary aim of the study was to describe coronal plane deformities in relation to equinus, and feet without examination in a weight-bearing position were not included.

To interpret the results of this study, it is important to note that our aim was to describe the prevalence of coronal foot deformities at a single time point, not their incidence or progression over time. Foot deformities affecting gait or daily function are rarely untreated in the Western world [10], so some children likely received treatment with orthotics, spasticity reduction, or surgery. The findings from this study can serve as a basis for further research, with future studies focusing on the development of foot deformities over time.

The definitions of valgus, varus, and equinus used in this study may be considered limitations. Only the position of the hindfoot was described for valgus and varus, and a cutoff of − 5 degrees of passive dorsiflexion was used to define equinus. Moreover, there is a lack of information on the severity and flexibility of the deformity. Due to the inability of many children in GMFCS IV and V to stand, there is a considerable amount of missing data in these categories. Therefore, the prevalence may be underestimated. However, as the majority of the children with a recorded foot position had a deformity, we do not believe that the missing data affect the overall results and conclusions. In our statistical analysis of foot deformities, we combined the coronal plane foot deformities (valgus and varus). To ensure a comprehensive examination, we also conducted separate analyses for each deformity. These individual analyses did not reveal significant differences in the factors associated with each deformity. Due to the limited sample size in the varus group, combining both groups allowed for a more robust analysis. There might be measurement errors and reporting errors in large registry-based cohorts. To reduce systematic errors, data is checked for irregularities, and all measurements are performed according to a standardized protocol by trained pediatric physiotherapists. Another limitation of this study is its cross-sectional design, which did not allow us to track the development of valgus and varus deformity over time.

Conclusions

This study highlights that most children with CP have a coronal plane foot deformity when weight-bearing. Valgus is more common at a younger age and higher GMFCS levels. For those with a higher GMFCS level, it is also more common at older ages. The findings of the study contribute to a mapping of the children with CP who are at increased risk of foot deformities. Moreover, they form a basis for further research and highlight the need for continuous follow-up of foot deformities in children with CP.
