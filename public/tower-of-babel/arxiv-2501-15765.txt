Hard edge asymptotics of correlation functions between singular values and one eigenvalue
Matthias Allard

Abstract
Any square complex matrix of size n × n n\times n can be partially characterized by its n n eigenvalues and/or n n singular values. While no one-to-one correspondence exists between those two kinds of values on a deterministic level, for random complex matrices drawn from a bi-unitarily invariant ensemble, a bijection exists between the underlying singular value ensemble and the corresponding eigenvalue ensemble. This enabled the recent finding of an explicit formula for the joint probability density between 1 1 eigenvalue and k k singular values, coined 1 , k 1,k -point function. We derive here the large n n asymptotic of the 1 , k 1,k -point function around the origin (hard edge) for a large subclass of bi-unitarily invariant ensembles called polynomial ensembles and its subclass Pólya ensembles. This latter subclass contains all Meijer-G ensembles and, in particular, Muttalib-Borodin ensembles and the classical Wishart-Laguerre (complex Ginibre), Jacobi (truncated unitary), Cauchy-Lorentz ensembles. We show that the latter three ensembles share the same asymptotic of the 1 , k 1,k -point function around the origin. In the case of Jacobi ensembles, there exists another hard edge for the singular values, namely the upper edge of their support, which corresponds to a soft edge for the eigenvalue (soft-hard edge). We give the explicit large n n asymptotic of the 1 , k 1,k -point function around this soft-hard edge.

Introduction

When studying a square complex matrix
X\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, one is usually interested in its
eigenvalues

z=\mathrm{diag}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, which are in general complex for non-Hermitian matrices, as they encode important information about the associated linear transformation. However, there exists another interesting set of values associated with the linear transformation: the
singular values
. The squared singular values
a=\mathrm{diag}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
of
X
are the eigenvalues of the matrix
X^{\dagger}X
, where
\dagger
denotes the Hermitian conjugate. The singular values are then the positive square roots of the
a
’s.

While those two sets of values are often studied or used separately depending on the context, they can be complementary. Indeed, the information they capture is different because, albeit being related, there is no one-to-one correspondence between the two sets of values. This is why looking at both eigenvalues and singular values can be relevant and even crucial in certain areas. For instance, in the study of random non-Hermitian Hamiltonians and, in particular, in quantum chaos and open quantum systems
[
44
,
42
,
23
,
12
,
40
]
, the physics and the chaotic behavior of the system seems to be best captured by the singular values, without making the eigenvalues redundant
[
6
,
43
]
. The study of non-Hermitian quantum Hamiltonians via the singular values is sometimes referred to as biorthogonal quantum mechanics
[
11
]
. The same complementarity is true in Quantum Chromodynamics
[
25
,
26
]

as well as in topological statistics of Hamiltonians
[
10
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
or even in Time Series Analysis of time-lagged matrices
[
49
,
34
,
54
,
36
,
8
,
41
]
. On a practical level, computing a singular value decomposition is much more stable and faster than an eigenvalue decomposition, hence the use of both sets of values in numerical analysis
[
4
,
13
,
24
]
to improve accuracy and algorithm complexity.

While, in general, for non-Hermitian matrices of fixed size
n
, there is no one-to-one correspondence between eigenvalues and singular values, there exists a unique equality between the two, coming from the determinant

\displaystyle|\det(X)|^{2}=|\det(z)|^{2}=\prod_{k=1}^{n}r_{k}=\det(X^\dagger X)=\det(a)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}a_{k},

(1.1)

where
r_{k}
are the squared eigenradii
r_{k}=|z_{k}|^{2}
. In addition, there exist various inequalities such as Weyl’s inequalities
[
51
]
from which it follows, in particular, that the largest singular value bounds the largest eigenradius from above and the smallest eigenradius is bounded from below by the smallest singular value. These bounds might be the source of non-trivial correlations between eigenvalues and singular values which should survive in the limit of large matrix size.

On a probabilistic level, some bijection has been found
[
28
]
between the probability densities of the singular values and eigenvalues for a large class of matrix ensembles: bi-unitarily invariant ensembles. If a density
f_{\rm BU}\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C}))
has the property

\displaystyle f_{\rm BU}(U_{1}XU_{2})=f_{\rm BU}(X)\quad{\rm for\ all}\ U_{1},U_{2}\in\mathrm{U}(n)\ {\rm and}\ X\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C}),

(1.2)

it is then called
bi-unitarily invariant
. Many classical random matrix ensembles are bi-unitarily invariant. For instance, the induced Ginibre ensemble
[
1
]
(also known as Laguerre, Wishart or chiral Gaussian unitary ensemble, when looking at the singular values)

f_{\rm Lag}(X)\propto\det(X^\dagger X)^{\alpha}\exp(-\Tr( X^\dagger X)),\ \alpha>-1,

(1.3)

as well as the induced Jacobi ensemble
[
1
,
15
]

(also known as the ensemble of truncated unitary matrices)

f_{\rm Jac}(X)\propto\det(X^\dagger X)^{\alpha}\det(\eins_n-X^\dagger X)^{\beta}\Theta(\mathds{1}_{n}-X^{\dagger}X),\ \alpha>-1,\,\beta>0,

(1.4)

with
\mathds{1}_{n}
the
n\times n
identity matrix and
\Theta
the Heaviside step function equal to
1
when the argument is a positive definite matrix and
0
otherwise. Another example is the Cauchy-Lorentz ensemble
[
52
]

f_{\rm CL}(X)\propto\frac{\det(X^\dagger X)^{\alpha}}{\det(\eins_n+X^\dagger X)^{\beta+2(n-1)}},\ \alpha>-1,\,\beta-\alpha>1,

(1.5)

and, more generally, any ensemble whose density depends only on
\det(P(X^\dagger X))
and
\Tr( Q( X^\dagger X))
, where the functions
P,Q
are any (convergent) series.

A direct consequence of the bijection between densities given in
[
28
]
is that any bi-unitarily invariant ensemble is entirely determined by the density of its singular values and vice-versa; any density on
\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n})
can be traced back to a bi-unitarily invariant density on
\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C}))
. This is why we associate the ensemble on the singular values with the one on the full matrix space, e.g. the underlying ensemble on the singular values of the Ginibre ensemble (
1.3
) is the Laguerre ensemble.

In a recent article
[
3
]
, we have introduced the notion of
j,k
-point correlation functions between
j
eigenvalues and
k
singular values. For fixed matrix size
n
, we have exploited the bijection between the underlying densities of singular values and eigenvalues of a bi-unitarily invariant ensembles to get the
1,k
-point correlation functions between one eigenradius (modulus of the eigenvalue) and
k
singular values. For
n>1
, the
1,k
-point correlation functions
f_{1,k}
can be defined weakly, with the help of any continuous bounded test function
\phi\in C_{b}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{1+k}\right)
, by the relation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}\phi(r(X);a_{\pi(1)}(X),\ldots,a_{\pi(k)}(X))\bigg]

(1.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{1+k}}\phi(r;a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})f_{1,k}(r;a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\,dr\,da_{1}\ldots da_{k},

where the expected value of a measurable function
\Phi:\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{C}
on
\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
is explicitly defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Phi]:=\int_{\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})}\Phi(X)f_{\rm BU}(X)dX.

(1.7)

The
1,k
-point correlation functions are, thus, a generalization of the usual
k
-point correlation functions
R_{k}
, used to study determinantal point processes, up to a combinatorial factor
[
1
,
15
]
that makes them probability densities. The
1
-point probability function on the squared eigenradii and the
k
-point probability function on the squared singular values are respectively given by
f_{1,0}
and
f_{0,k}
which can be expressed as the marginals

\displaystyle f_{1,0}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k}}f_{1,k}(r;a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\,da_{1}\ldots da_{k},

(1.8)

\displaystyle f_{0,k}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}f_{1,k}(r;a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\,dr.

In
[
3
]
, we obtained a very general formula for the
1,k
-point correlation functions for any bi-unitarily invariant ensemble, which simplifies drastically when the induced probability density on the singular values is a
polynomial ensemble

[
32
,
33
,
31
,
18
]
, i.e. has the form

\displaystyle f_{\rm SV}(x)=\frac{\Delta_{n}(x)\det\left[w_{k-1}(x_{j})\right]_{j,k=1}^{n}}{n!\,\det\left[\mathcal{M}w_{k-1}(j)\right]_{j,k=1}^{n}},

(1.9)

where
w_{0},\ldots,w_{n-1}
are weight functions on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
f_{\rm SV}
is a probability density on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
. The
n
-dimensional Vandermonde determinant of an
n
-dimensional vector
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is denoted by

\Delta_{n}(x)=\det[x_{j}^{k-1}]_{j,k=1}^{n}=\prod_{1\leqslant j<k\leqslant n}(x_{k}-x_{j}).

(1.10)

We introduced, here, the Mellin transform
\mathcal{M}
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}f(s):=\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\ x^{s-1}f(x)

(1.11)

for an
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
-function
f
and
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that the integral converges absolutely. Note that the structure of polynomial ensembles (
1.9
) appears very naturally in random matrices as the Jacobian of the change of coordinates to go on the singular values yields a squared Vandermonde determinant.

The results in
[
3
]
are made even more explicit for a certain type of polynomial ensemble, namely the
Pólya ensembles
(formerly coined polynomial ensembles of derivative type)
[
30
,
28
,
27
,
18
]
. A polynomial ensemble is a Pólya ensemble if there exists
w
such that

\displaystyle w_{j}(x)=(-x\partial_{x})^{j}w(x)\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\qquad\forall j\in\llbracket 0,n-1\rrbracket.

(1.12)

To guarantee that we deal with probability measures it has been shown in
[
18
]
that
w
is then related to Pólya frequency functions. Many classical ensembles are Pólya ensembles. It is, for instance, the case of the Laguerre (
1.3
), Jacobi (
1.4
) and Cauchy-Lorentz (
1.5
) ensembles, whose respective corresponding Pólya weight functions (
1.12
) are

\displaystyle w_{\rm Lag}(x)=x^{\alpha}e^{-x},\quad w_{\rm Jac}(x)=x^{\alpha}(1-x)^{\beta+n-1}\Theta(1-x),\quad w_{\rm CL}(x)=\frac{x^{\alpha}}{(1+x)^{\beta+n-1}}.

(1.13)

Those ensembles are well studied and appear in many different applications such as the study of wireless communication systems, entanglement of a random pure quantum state, quantum conductance; see
[
15
,
1
,
38
]
. Note that the Cauchy-Lorentz ensemble is related to the circular unitary ensemble (CUE)—and its truncated version—via a Cayley transformation. On the level of eigenvalues it corresponds to a simple change of variable, namely a stereographic projection from the unit circle to the real line (see
(
15
, p.68)
).

Other important ensembles falling in the class of Pólya ensemble are the Muttalib-Borodin ensembles
[
9
,
17
]
which have been introduced to model two-body interactions in random matrix models
[
39
]
and used, for instance, in the study of disordered conductors, quantum transport, Brownian motion
[
7
,
37
,
47
]
. We give the example of the Laguerre type Muttalib-Borodin ensemble whose Pólya weight is given by

\displaystyle w_{\rm MB}(x)=x^{\alpha}e^{-x^{\theta}},\quad\alpha>-1,\theta>0.

(1.14)

In fact, not only are most of the classical random matrix ensembles Pólya ensembles but also their composition, in the sense that given two complex square matrices
X
and
Y
drawn from different (or same) Pólya ensembles, then the matrix
XY
also follows a Pólya ensemble, whose corresponding Pólya weight is given by the multiplicative convolution of the two original weights, cf.
[
18
,
29
,
32
,
31
]
. This nice closure property makes the study of this class of ensemble all the more interesting.

Coming back to general polynomial ensembles, they enjoy some additional structure from their belonging to a much larger class of ensembles called
determinantal point processes
; see
[
5
,
1
,
14
,
15
]
. This means that the joint probability distribution
f_{\rm SV}
can be written in the form

\displaystyle f_{\rm SV}(x)=\frac{1}{n!}\det\left[K_{n}(x_{j},x_{k})\right]_{j,k=1}^{n},

(1.15)

where
K_{n}
is the kernel function, and all the
k
-point correlation functions have a similar form where only the size of the determinant changes. Note that
K_{n}
is not uniquely given. Indeed, due to elementary properties of the determinant, for a non-vanishing function
g
, the kernel
[g(x_{1})/g(x_{2})]K_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})
is also a correlation kernel for the same point process. However, for polynomial ensembles one can choose
K_{n}
to be polynomial of degree
n-1
in the second entry, which thus makes it unique. It is important to stress that not only does the kernel play a crucial role in the point process of the singular values but also in the point process of the eigenradii and, a fortiori, in the combined point process as it can be seen in the main results of
[
3
]
, reminded here for convenience.

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem 1.4
[
3
]
)
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n>2
,
k\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
and consider a random matrix drawn from a bi-unitarily invariant ensemble on
\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
having a polynomial ensemble with joint probability density (
1.15
) for the squared singular values. The
1,k
-point correlation function between one squared eigenradius and
k
squared singular values is given by

\displaystyle f_{1,k}(r;a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})=

\displaystyle\frac{(n-k)!}{(n-1)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\int_{0}^{r}\frac{dv}{v}\varphi_{n}\left(\tfrac{v}{r},t\right)

(1.16)

\displaystyle\times\det\left(\begin{array}[]{c c}K_{n}\left(v,-rt\right)&K_{n}\left(v,a_{c}\right)-\delta(v-a_{c})\\
K_{n}\left(a_{b},-rt\right)&K_{n}(a_{b},a_{c})\end{array}\right)_{b,c=1}^{k}.

with
\delta
the Dirac delta function,

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(x,t):=x(1-x)^{n-2}(1+t)^{-(n+2)}\left[\left(1-\frac{x}{n}\right)(1+t)-(1-x)\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)\right]

(1.17)

with
K_{n}
, the correlation kernel of the polynomial ensemble, chosen to be a polynomial of degree
n-1
in its second argument.

The
1
-point functions, respectively on one squared singular value and one squared eigenradius, are given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm SV}(a):=f_{0,1}(a)=\frac{1}{n}K_{n}(a,a)

(1.18)

and

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm EV}(r):=f_{1,0}(r)=n\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\int_{0}^{r}\frac{dv}{v}\varphi_{n}\left(\frac{v}{r},t\right)K_{n}\left(v,-rt\right).

(1.19)

While the
1,k
-point correlation functions contains all the information about the correlation between one eigenradius and
k
singular values, a way to extract this information consists in looking at the difference between the
1,k
-point function
f_{1,k}
and the product of the respective

-point function
f_{1,0}
on the eigenradii and the
k
-point function
f_{0,k}
on the singular values (
1.8
). This is then a measurement of the distance to statistical independence: a covariance measurement. The resulting function has been coined

-cross-covariance density
in
[

]
and is denoted by

\displaystyle\mathrm{cov}_{1,k}(r;a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}):=f_{1,k}(r;a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})-f_{1,0}(r)f_{0,k}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}).

(1.20)

As a shorthand, we will refer to the
1,1
-cross-covariance density, simply, as

and denote it
\mathrm{cov}:=\mathrm{cov}_{1,1}
. It was then shown in
[
3
]
that, for Pólya ensembles, the
1,k
-cross-covariance density could be put in the form given by the following proposition.

Proposition 1.2

(Prop.1.7
[
3
]
)
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n>2
. With the same assumptions and notations as in
Theorem 1.1
, we assume that
f_{\rm SV}
is the joint probability density of the squared singular values of a Pólya ensemble associated to an
n
-times differentiable weight function
w_{n}\in C^{n}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Then, the
1,k
-cross-covariance density function has the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{cov}_{1,k}(r;a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})=\frac{(n-k)!}{(n-1)!}\left.\partial_{\mu}\det[K_{n}(a_{b},a_{c})+\mu\,\mathbf{C}_{n}(r;a_{b},a_{c})]_{b,c=1}^{k}\right|_{\mu=0}

(1.21)

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{n}(r;a_{1},a_{2})=

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma=0,1}H_{\gamma}(r,a_{2})\left[\Theta(r-a_{1})\frac{1}{a_{1}}\Psi_{\gamma}\left(\frac{a_{1}}{r}\right)-V_{\gamma}(r,a_{1})\right]

(1.22)

with
\Theta
the Heaviside step function and for
\gamma=0,1
we have employed the functions

\displaystyle\Psi_{\gamma}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1-x}{nx-1}\right)^{\gamma}x(1-x)^{n-2}\left(nx-1\right),

(1.23)

\displaystyle H_{\gamma}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}du\ q_{n}(yu)\partial_{u}^{\gamma}\left[u^{\gamma}\frac{\rho_{\rm EV}(xu)}{w_{n}(xu)}\right],

(1.24)

\displaystyle V_{\gamma}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}du\ p_{n-1}\left(yu\right)\left(u\partial_{u}\right)^{1-\gamma}w_{n}(xu),

(1.25)

where
p_{n-1}
and
q_{n}
are the bi-orthonormal pair of functions composing the kernel (

) of

which can be expressed as

\displaystyle p_{n-1}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{c}\frac{(-x)^{c}}{\mathcal{M}w_{n}(c+1)}\quad{\rm and}\quad q_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n!}\partial_{x}^{n}[x^{n}w_{n}(x)],

(1.26)

according to
(

.

The goal of this article is to exploit the results of
[
3
]
where everything has been done for fixed matrix size
n
and study, now, the large

limit. Let us stress that all the results of this article are completely new as the results of

]
were new.

The article is organized as follows. First, in

, we look at the double scaling limit of both the

-point correlation function and the

-cross-covariance density around the origin for polynomial ensembles. This is interesting and non trivial, as most polynomial ensembles will have a hard edge at the origin. Indeed, unless there is a strong repulsion from the origin, the lower edge of the support will be a hard wall due to the definition of eigenradii and singular values, which are non negative. Moreover, the deterministic Weyl’s inequalities

]

and

Some ensembles can have another hard edge, as it is the case of Jacobi ensembles. In

) around the upper hard edge for the singular values, which corresponds to a soft edge for the eigenradius. The main result of this section is given by

.
In

2
Double scaling limit at the origin

2.1
Polynomial ensembles

Let us start with some heuristics. When looking at
Theorem 1.1
, the double scaling limit (if existent) of the
1,k
-point correlation function will be given by scaling appropriately the squared eigenradius and the squared singular values and then taking the large
n
limit. One would expect the result to involve the double scaling limit of the kernels
K_{n}
and the double scaling limit of the
\varphi_{n}
.

The goal is then to find the correct scalings. To begin, we examine (
1.16
) and first proceed with the change of variable
v\mapsto rv
. The arguments of
\varphi_{n}
do not depend on
r
or
a
anymore and one can see that the term
n^{2}\varphi_{n}\left(x/n,t/n\right)
admits a pointwise limit

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{2}\varphi_{n}\left(\frac{x}{n},\frac{t}{n}\right)=x(t+x-1)e^{-x}e^{-t}.

(2.1)

This hints one needs to proceed with the change of integration variables
(v,t)\mapsto(v/n,t/n)
.

Now, let us assume that the appropriate scaling for the squared eigenradius is given by
\nu_{n}
, thus we let
r\mapsto r/\nu_{n}
. It becomes clear that if the result involves the double scaling limit of the kernels, the correct scaling should be

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n\nu_{n}}K_{n}\left(\frac{x}{n\nu_{n}},\frac{y}{n\nu_{n}}\right).

(2.2)

To be consistent, the correct scaling for the squared singular values is then
n\nu_{n}
, thus we set
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\mapsto\left(\frac{a_{1}}{n\nu_{n}},\ldots,\frac{a_{k}}{n\nu_{n}}\right)
. This surprising difference between the scaling of the squared eigenradius and squared singular values is discussed later.

Accounting for the change of measure and multiplying by the appropriate global scaling, the correct scaling of the
1,k
-point correlation function between one squared eigenradius and
k
squared singular values should be

\displaystyle\frac{n^{k+1}}{\nu_{n}(n\nu_{n})^{k}}f_{1,k}\left(\frac{r}{\nu_{n}};\frac{a_{1}}{n\nu_{n}},\ldots,\frac{a_{k}}{n\nu_{n}}\right)

(2.3)

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{k}(n-k)!}{n!}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\int_{0}^{n}\frac{dv}{v}n^{2}\varphi_{n}\left(\frac{v}{n},\frac{t}{n}\right)

\displaystyle\times\det\left(\begin{array}[]{c c}\frac{1}{n\nu_{n}}K_{n}\left(\frac{rv}{n\nu_{n}},\frac{-rt}{n\nu_{n}}\right)&\frac{1}{n\nu_{n}}K_{n}\left(\frac{rv}{n\nu_{n}},\frac{a_{c}}{n\nu_{n}}\right)-\delta(rv-a_{c})\\
\frac{1}{n\nu_{n}}K_{n}\left(\frac{a_{b}}{n\nu_{n}},\frac{-rt}{n\nu_{n}}\right)&\frac{1}{n\nu_{n}}K_{n}\left(\frac{a_{b}}{n\nu_{n}},\frac{a_{c}}{n\nu_{n}}\right)\end{array}\right)_{b,c=1}^{k},

where we have used the scaling property of the Dirac delta function

\displaystyle\forall c\neq 0,\quad\delta\left(\frac{x}{c}\right)=c\,\delta(x).

(2.4)

To be allowed to take the limit
n\to\infty
on both sides of (
2.3
) and then pass the limit below the integrals in the RHS, all limits must exist and one needs to justify exchanging limit and integrals. This is why one needs to make some restrictions on the kernel of the polynomial ensemble as its double scaling limit is not necessarily a function anymore or simply does not exist.
We will make the following assumptions to guarantee existence of the limits and integrability of the different integrands involved in the computations.

Let us first recall that the kernel
K_{n}
of any polynomial ensemble (
1.15
) can be cast in the form

\displaystyle K_{n}(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}q_{j}(x)p_{j}(y),

(2.5)

with
p_{j}
a polynomial of degree
j
and
q_{j}
chosen such that the two sets of functions
\{q_{j}\}_{j=0,\ldots,n-1}
and
\{p_{j}\}_{j=0,\ldots,n-1}
satisfy the bi-orthonormality relation

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}q_{j}(x)p_{k}(x)dx=\delta_{j,k},

(2.6)

where
\delta_{j,k}
is the Kronecker delta function. Note that this does not uniquely define the functions
p_{j}
and
q_{j}
as one can always decompose the polynomials
p_{j}
in another basis of polynomials up to degree
n-1
and then take the corresponding linear combination of
q_{j}
to form a new bi-orthonormal system. In particular, given a sequence
\{c_{j}\}_{j=0,\ldots,n-1}
of non zero real numbers,
\{c_{j}q_{j}\}_{j=0,\ldots,n-1}
and
\{c_{j}^{-1}p_{j}\}_{j=0,\ldots,n-1}
are also possible bi-orthonormal families. Thus, the
p_{j}
are not canonically associated to the ensemble. Note also that
p_{j}
,
q_{j}
might depend on
n
, we, however, do not make the notation transparent.

Assumptions 2.1

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let

Under these assumptions, the double scaling limit version of

For the subclass of Pólya ensembles, the formulas of

Assumptions 2.8

Theorem 2.12

Let

Let us first recall that, for Pólya ensembles, the kernel admits an integral representation

\displaystyle K_{n}(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}q_{j}(x)p_{j}(y)=n\int_{0}^{1}dt\,q_{n}(xt)p_{n-1}(yt),

\displaystyle p_{j}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c=0}^{j}\binom{j}{c}\frac{(-x)^{c}}{\mathcal{M}w_{n}(c+1)},\quad q_{j}(x)=\frac{1}{j!}\partial_{x}^{j}[x^{j}w_{n}(x)].

\displaystyle P_{n-1}(y):=\frac{1}{\xi_{n}}p_{n-1}\left(\frac{y}{n\nu_{n}}\right)=\sum_{c=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{c}\frac{(-y)^{c}}{n^{c}\xi_{n}\nu_{n}^{c}\mathcal{M}w_{n}(c+1)}.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n\nu_{n}}K_{n}\left(\frac{x}{n\nu_{n}},\frac{y}{n\nu_{n}}\right)=\int_{0}^{1}dtQ_{n}(xt)P_{n-1}(yt),

Proposition 2.17

3
Soft-hard edge scaling limit for Jacobi ensembles

While the density of most polynomial ensembles has a scaling limit at the origin, they often have only one hard edge on the singular values and eigenvalues. The Jacobi ensemble (
1.4
) with fixed parameter
\alpha
and
\beta
, and corresponding Pólya weight given in (
1.13
), has a second hard edge for the singular values around the upper edge of its support. Let us study the corresponding double scaling limit around this other edge.

Using the Christoffel-Darboux formula, the kernel can be written

\displaystyle K_{n}(x,y)=a_{n}x^{\alpha}(1-x)^{\beta}\frac{P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(1-2x)P_{n-1}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(1-2y)-P_{n-1}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(1-2x)P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(1-2y)}{x-y}

(3.1)

with the usual Jacobi polynomials

\displaystyle P_{n}^{{(\alpha,\beta)}}(z)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+n+1)}{n!\,\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+n+1)}\sum_{m=0}^{n}\binom{n}{m}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+n+m+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha+m+1)}\left({\frac{z-1}{2}}\right)^{m}

(3.2)

and the coefficient

\displaystyle a_{n}=-\frac{\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(n+\alpha+\beta+1)}{(\alpha+\beta+2n)\Gamma(n+\alpha)\Gamma(n+\beta)}.

(3.3)

Remark 3.1

Note that the Jacobi polynomials associated with our Jacobi ensemble are orthogonal on
[0,1]
and are therefore the polynomials in (
3.1
). We express them in terms of the usual Jacobi polynomials to use known asymptotics.

One can easily show, using the reflection property of the Jacobi polynomials

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(-z)=(-1)^{n}P_{n}^{(\beta,\alpha)}(z)

(3.4)

and
Proposition 2.13
, that the limiting kernel for the point process on the singular values is

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}K_{n}\left(1-\frac{x}{n^{2}},1-\frac{y}{n^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=4\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{2}}K_{\beta}^{\mathrm{Bes}}(4x,4y),

(3.5)

therefore, the limiting microscopic level density on the singular values is

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\rho_{\rm SV}\left(1-\frac{a}{n^{2}}\right)=J_{\beta}\left(2\sqrt{a}\right)^{2}-J_{\beta-1}\left(2\sqrt{a}\right)J_{\beta+1}\left(2\sqrt{a}\right).

(3.6)

However, the limit of the level density of the eigenradii around
r=1
is trivially null as the limiting macroscopic level density on the squared eigenradii is given by
(
2
, Eq.(3.12))

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\rho_{\rm EV}(r)=\frac{1}{(1-r)^{2}}\Theta\left(\frac{1}{2}-r\right).

(3.7)

Note that the Heaviside function in (
3.7
) appears only in the limit and is not associated with a hard edge. Actually, if one zooms on a scale
1/\sqrt{n}
around
r=1/2
, the limiting microscopic level density of the eigenradii is given in terms of a complementary error function
(
2
, Eq.(3.36))
. There is, therefore, no other hard edge for the eigenradii than the one at the origin. The upper edge around
1/2
is a soft edge. This is due to the
n
dependence of the Pólya weight, namely, the repulsion from the upper edge grows with
n
, in this case, for the eigenvalues, cf. (
1.13
).

The
1,k
-cross-covariance density then admits a scaling limit around the soft edge of the eigenradii and the upper hard edge of the singular values given in the following theorem. We will refer to this double scaling limit as Soft-Hard (SH) edge limit.

Theorem 3.2

For the Jacobi ensemble with Pólya weight
w_{\rm Jac}(x)=x^{\alpha}(1-x)^{\beta+n-1}\Theta(1-x)
, with
\alpha,\beta
fixed and
\alpha>-1
,
\beta>0
, the point-wise limit

\displaystyle\mathrm{cov}_{1,k\,{\rm SH}}^{(\infty)}(r;a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}):=\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{3/2-k}\,\mathrm{cov}_{1,k}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{r}{\sqrt{n}};1-\frac{a_{1}}{n^{2}},\ldots,1-\frac{a_{k}}{n^{2}}\right)

(3.8)

of the
1,k
-cross-covariance density functions between one squared eigenradius and
k
squared singular values is given, for all fixed
(r,a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k}
, by

\displaystyle\mathrm{cov}_{1,k\,{\rm SH}}^{(\infty)}(r;a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})

(3.9)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-4r^{2}}\partial_{\mu}\det\left[\int_{0}^{1}dtJ_{\beta}(2\sqrt{a_{b}t})J_{\beta}(2\sqrt{a_{c}t})+\mu J_{\beta}(2\sqrt{a_{b}})J_{\beta}(2\sqrt{a_{c}})\right]_{b,c=1}^{k}\Bigg|_{\mu=0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{4^{k}}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-4r^{2}}\det\left(\begin{array}[]{c c}0&J_{\beta}(2\sqrt{a_{c}})\\
J_{\beta}(2\sqrt{a_{b}})&K_{\beta}^{\mathrm{Bes}}(4a_{b},4a_{c})\end{array}\right)_{b,c=1}^{k}.

In particular, in the case
k=1
, the limiting cross-covariance reads

\displaystyle\mathrm{cov}^{(\infty)}_{\rm SH}(r;a)=\frac{4}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-4r^{2}}J_{\beta}(2\sqrt{a})^{2}.

(3.10)

Remark 3.3

Echoing
Remark 2.6
, the above result implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2k+1/2}}\,\mathrm{cov}_{1,k}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{r}{\sqrt{n}};1-\frac{a_{1}}{n^{2}},\ldots,1-\frac{a_{k}}{n^{2}}\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{=}\frac{1}{n^{k+2}}\left[\mathrm{cov}_{1,k\,{\rm SH}}^{(\infty)}(r;a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})+o(1)\right],

(3.11)

which means that the scaling limit of the
1,k
-cross-covariance, for Jacobi ensembles, at the soft-hard edge is of order
O(1/n^{k+2})
as
n\to\infty
. It is therefore a factor
1/n
smaller than the limiting
1,k
-point function and can be seen as the first correction term.

4.1
Proof of

The proof of

relies on Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem, hence

One has, therefore, to split the integration into two parts: a compact part where one can use bounded convergence theorem to bring the limit below the integral and a non-compact part which vanishes in the appropriate limits. The most important step of the proof relies therefore on the following Lemma.

Lemma 4.1

Let

. Under
Assumptions 2.1
, for almost all

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{R}dt\int_{C_{R}}^{n}dv+\int_{R}^{\infty}dt\int_{0}^{n}dv\right)\frac{n^{2}}{v}\varphi_{n}\left(\frac{v}{n},\frac{t}{n}\right)

(4.1)

\displaystyle\times\det\left(\begin{array}[]{c c}\frac{1}{n\nu_{n}}K_{n}\left(\frac{rv}{n\nu_{n}},\frac{-rt}{n\nu_{n}}\right)&\frac{1}{n\nu_{n}}K_{n}\left(\frac{rv}{n\nu_{n}},\frac{a_{c}}{n\nu_{n}}\right)-\delta(rv-a_{c})\\

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}dt\int_{C_{R}}^{n}\frac{dv}{v}n^{2}\varphi_{n}\left(\frac{v}{n},\frac{t}{n}\right)\frac{1}{n\nu_{n}}K_{n}\left(\frac{rv}{n\nu_{n}},\frac{-rt}{n\nu_{n}}\right),

\displaystyle I_{2,n}^{-}(R):=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}dt\int_{C_{R}}^{n}\frac{dv}{v}n^{2}\varphi_{n}\left(\frac{v}{n},\frac{t}{n}\right)\frac{1}{(n\nu_{n})^{2}}K_{n}\left(\frac{rv}{n\nu_{n}},\frac{a_{1}}{n\nu_{n}}\right)K_{n}\left(\frac{a_{2}}{n\nu_{n}},\frac{-rt}{n\nu_{n}}\right).

The proof of

relies on the more elementary lemma which follows.

Lemma 4.2

Let

,

, and a continuous function

Remark 5.6

Note that
B_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(\lambda)\underset{n\to\infty}{=}O(n^{\beta})
, therefore, the big
O
in (
5.12
) is really an error term.

Proof of
Corollary 5.5
.

Using the Christoffel-Darboux formula (
3.1
) and
Proposition 5.4
we have,

\displaystyle K_{n}\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n^{2}},-x\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{=}

\displaystyle a_{n}\left(\frac{\lambda}{n^{2}}\right)^{\beta}\frac{1}{1+x}\bigg[\left(B_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(\lambda)+O\left(n^{\beta-1}\right)\right)P_{n-1}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(1+2x)

(5.14)

\displaystyle-\left(B_{n-1}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(\lambda)+O\left(n^{\beta-1}\right)\right)P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(1+2x)\bigg].

Note that the respective domains of the polynomials do not intersect. Thus, there is no cancellation of the leading terms.

First, one has to notice that

\displaystyle B_{n-1}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(\lambda)\underset{n\to\infty}{=}-B_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(\lambda)\left[1+O\left(\frac{\beta}{n}\right)\right].

(5.15)

Therefore, one can factorise by
B_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(\lambda)
. Then, using the relation

\displaystyle(2n+\alpha+\beta+1)P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}\left(x\right)=(n+\alpha+\beta+1)P^{(\alpha,\beta+1)}_{n}\left(x\right)+(n+\alpha)P^{(\alpha,\beta+1)}_{n-1}\left(x\right),

(5.16)

one gets

\displaystyle P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}\left(1+2x\right)+P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n-1}\left(1+2x\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{=}2P^{(\alpha,\beta-1)}_{n}\left(1+2x\right)+O\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{n}(-x)}{n}\right),

(5.17)

which yields

\displaystyle K_{n}\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n^{2}},-x\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{=}2a_{n}\left(\frac{\lambda}{n^{2}}\right)^{\beta}\left[B_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(\lambda)\frac{P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta-1)}(1+2x)}{1+x}+O\left(n^{\beta-1}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{n}(-x)}{(1+x)}\right)\right].

(5.18)

Finally, using the following asymptotic equivalent for
a_{n}
(
3.3
),

\displaystyle a_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}-\frac{n}{2},

(5.19)

finishes the proof.
∎

Corollary 5.7

For any fixed
\alpha>-1,\beta>0
, the expansion

\displaystyle K_{n}\left(v,1-\frac{\lambda}{n^{2}}\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{=}-n\,v^{\alpha}(1-v)^{\beta-1}\left[B_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(\lambda)P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta-1)}(1-2v)+O\left(n^{\beta-1}\mathcal{E}_{n}(v)\right)\right].

(5.20)

holds uniformly for
\lambda
in compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
v\in[0,1-\epsilon]
, for any
\epsilon\in]0,1[
. The polynomial
\mathcal{E}_{n}
is given by (
5.13
).

Proof of
Corollary 5.7
.

In the same spirit as the proof of
Corollary 5.5
, we use the Christoffel-Darboux formula (
3.1
) and
Proposition 5.4
. This yields

\displaystyle K_{n}\left(v,1-\frac{\lambda}{n^{2}}\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{=}

\displaystyle a_{n}v^{\alpha}(1-v)^{\beta}\bigg[P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(1-2v)\left(B_{n-1}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(\lambda)+O\left(n^{\beta-1}\right)\right)

(5.21)

\displaystyle-P_{n-1}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(1-2v)\left(B_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(\lambda)+O\left(n^{\beta-1}\right)\right)\bigg].

Here again, there is no cancellation of the leading terms as the arguments of the kernel are never equal to each other for
n
large enough, namely
n^{2}>\lambda/\epsilon
(cf.
Remark 5.2
). Following the same steps as in the proof of
Corollary 5.5
, the result follows.
∎

From
Corollary 5.5
and
Corollary 5.7
, one sees that the problem of getting the large
n
asymptotic of (
5.5
) can be broken down to getting the large
n
asymptotic of the following integrals

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r):=\int_{0}^{r}dv\,v^{\alpha}(1-v)^{\beta-1+\gamma}P_{n}^{{(\alpha,\beta-1)}}(1-2v)\left(1-\frac{v}{r}\right)^{n-2},\quad\gamma=0,1

(5.22)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r):=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt(1+t)^{-(n+2)}\frac{P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta-1)}(1+2rt)}{(1+rt)^{1-\gamma}},\quad\gamma=0,1.

(5.23)

Note that the error terms in (
5.12
) and (
5.20
) are composed of the same polynomials as the leading terms, with same degree. Indeed,
O(\mathcal{E}_{n}(x))
is some linear combination of the Jacobi polynomials composing
\mathcal{E}_{n}
. Therefore, after integration, the error terms will respectively be expressed as some linear combination of
\mathcal{J}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}
and
\mathcal{I}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}
, up to a shift in the parameters
\alpha,\beta
, which are
n
-independent. The error terms will remain
1/n
smaller than the leading terms and can, therefore, be neglected in the limit
n\to\infty
; see
Remark 5.6
.

Computation of
\mathcal{J}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)

Let us focus first on
\mathcal{J}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)
,
\gamma=0,1
, as one does not need to use the Laplace’s method in this case. Indeed, one can use Rodrigues’ formula for the Jacobi polynomials
P_{n}^{{(\alpha,\beta)}}
, which, under the mapping
x\mapsto 1-2x
, is given by

\displaystyle x^{\alpha}(1-x)^{\beta}P_{n}^{{(\alpha,\beta)}}(1-2x)=\frac{1}{n!}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}\left[x^{\alpha+n}(1-x)^{\beta+n}\right].

(5.24)

Then, integrating by part, respectively
n-2
and
n-1
times, yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,0}(r)=\frac{r^{\alpha+1}(1-r)^{n+\beta-2}}{(n-1)}\left[1-2r+\frac{\alpha-r(\alpha+\beta-1)}{n}\right]

(5.25)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,1}(r)=\frac{r^{\alpha+1}(1-r)^{n+\beta-1}}{(n-1)}\left[1-r+\frac{\alpha-r(\alpha+\beta)}{n}\right].

(5.26)

Asymptotic equivalent of
\mathcal{I}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)

Let us now find asymptotic equivalents of
\mathcal{I}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)
,
\gamma=0,1
. Once again the integral can be computed explicitly, however this yield a sum of hypergeometric functions for which an asymptotic equivalent is hard to get. Instead, we will use Laplace’s approximation method. For this, we need the asymptotic for
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta-1)}
given by
Proposition 5.3
. However, one needs to stay away from
t=0
, as the approximation is not uniform there. We then introduce a
n
-independent cut-off
t_{\varepsilon}>0
that we can conveniently express in terms of a small enough
\varepsilon>0
as

\displaystyle\varepsilon=\sqrt{\frac{rt_{\varepsilon}}{1+rt_{\varepsilon}}}.

(5.27)

We will see how small
\varepsilon
needs to be later on. To estimate the contribution of the integral

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{\varepsilon}}dt(1+t)^{-(n+2)}\frac{P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta-1)}(1+2rt)}{(1+rt)^{1-\gamma}},\quad\gamma=0,1,

(5.28)

one can bound
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta-1)}(1+2rt)
by its maximum, which is attained at
t=t_{\varepsilon}
and take the very rough upper bound

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{\varepsilon}}dt(1+t)^{-(n+2)}\frac{P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta-1)}(1+2rt)}{(1+rt)^{1-\gamma}}\leqslant\frac{1}{n+1}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta-1)}(1+2rt_{\varepsilon}),\quad\gamma=0,1.

(5.29)

To estimate the order of the upper bound as
n\to\infty
, one can use the approximation (
5.8
) as
t_{\varepsilon}
is bounded away from
0
and
n
-independent. This yields, in terms of
\epsilon
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta-1)}(1+2rt_{\varepsilon})\underset{n\to\infty}{=}O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}\left(\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\right)^{n}\right),

(5.30)

for
r
in compact subsets of
]0,1[
; see
Remark 5.2
. Using this estimate, we will see later that the integral (
5.28
) is negligible compared to its complement.

The plan is thus to apply Laplace’s method on the following integral for
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r):=\int_{t_{\varepsilon}}^{\infty}dt(1+t)^{-(n+2)}\frac{A_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta-1)}(-rt)}{(1+rt)^{1-\gamma}},\quad\gamma=0,1.

(5.31)

As the asymptotic expansion (
5.8
) is uniform on the integration domain, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)\underset{n\to\infty}{=}\mathcal{Y}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)\left[1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right]+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3/2}}\left(\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\right)^{n}\right),

(5.32)

for all
r
in compact subsets of
]0,1[
, where both
\mathcal{I}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)
and
\mathcal{Y}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)
, are convergent integrals due to the factor
(1+t)^{-(n+2)}
. Proceeding with a change of variable
y=\sqrt{\frac{rt}{1+rt}}
, the change of measure is then
dt=\frac{2ydy}{r(1-y^{2})^{2}}
and the integral reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)=\frac{2}{r}\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}dy\,y(1-y^{2})^{n+1-\gamma}\left(1+y^{2}(\tfrac{1}{r}-1)\right)^{-(n+2)}A_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta-1)}\left(-\frac{y^{2}}{1-y^{2}}\right).

(5.33)

One can then rewrite the integrand

\displaystyle\frac{g_{r,\gamma}(y)}{\sqrt{n}}\exp(-n f_r(y))=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{r}y(1-y^{2})^{n+1-\gamma}\left(1+y^{2}(\tfrac{1}{r}-1)\right)^{-(n+2)}A_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta-1)}\left(-\frac{y^{2}}{1-y^{2}}\right)

(5.34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r\sqrt{n\pi}}y^{\frac{1}{2}-\alpha}(1-y)^{1-\gamma}\frac{(1+y)^{2n+\alpha+\beta+1-\gamma}}{\left(1+y^{2}(\tfrac{1}{r}-1)\right)^{n+2}},

defining, as follows, the functions
f_{r}
and
g_{r,\gamma}
, for which we already give the explicit expressions of the needed derivatives

\displaystyle f_{r}(y):=\ln(1+y^2(\tfrac{1}{r}-1))-2\ln(1+y),

(5.35)

\displaystyle\displaystyle f_{r}^{\prime}(y)=2\frac{y(1-r)-r}{(1+y)(r+y^{2}(1-r))},

\displaystyle\displaystyle f_{r}^{\prime\prime}(y)=2\frac{r(1+y)(1+y+4y^{2})-2r^{2}y(1+y)^{2}-y^{2}(1+2y)}{(1+y)^{2}(r+y^{2}(1-r))^{2}}

and

\displaystyle g_{r,\gamma}(y):=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{r\sqrt{\pi}}y^{\frac{1}{2}-\alpha}(1-y)^{1-\gamma}\frac{(1+y)^{\alpha+\beta+1-\gamma}}{\left(1+y^{2}(\tfrac{1}{r}-1)\right)^{2}},

(5.36)

\displaystyle g_{r,\gamma}^{\prime}(y)=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{y^{-\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}(1+y)^{\alpha+\beta}\left(1-y^{2}\right)^{-\gamma}}{2r^{2}\sqrt{\pi}\left(1+\left(\frac{1}{r}-1\right)y^{2}\right)^{3}}\bigg[y^{2}\left(-2\alpha+y^{2}(-2\beta+4\gamma+3)+2y(\alpha+\beta)-7\right)

\displaystyle+r\left(y^{2}-1\right)\left(2\alpha+y^{2}(2\beta-4\gamma-3)-2y(\alpha+\beta)-1\right)\bigg].

The function
f_{r}
admits a unique critical point; corresponding to a minimum located at
y=\frac{r}{1-r}
. For
r>\frac{1}{2}
, the critical point escapes the domain of integration, the minimum is then on the boundary at
y=1
. However,
g_{r,0}(1)=0
, hence the need to expand
g_{r,\gamma}
to first order and
f_{r}
to second order. Note that, despite the term
\left(1-y^{2}\right)^{-1}
,
g_{r,1}^{\prime}
does not have a singularity at
y=1
, as it is removable.
Denoting,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r):=

\displaystyle\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}\frac{dy}{\sqrt{n}}\left[g_{r,\gamma}(y_{r})+(y-y_{r})g_{r,\gamma}^{\prime}(y_{r})\right]

(5.37)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\exp(-n\left[f_r(y_r)+(y-y_r) f_r'(y_r)+\frac{(y-y_r)^2}{2} f_r''(y_r) \right]),\quad\gamma=0,1,

with

\displaystyle y_{r}=\begin{cases}&\displaystyle\frac{r}{1-r},\quad 0<r\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\\
&\\
&\displaystyle 1,\quad\frac{1}{2}<r<1\\
\end{cases}.

(5.38)

We thus have

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)\underset{n\to\infty}{=}\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)\left[1+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right],

(5.39)

uniformly for
r
in compact subsets of
]0,1[
. Note that the relative error is, here, of order
n^{-1/2}
and not
n^{-1}
due to
g_{r,0}
vanishing at
y=1
; see
(
48
, p.37)
. To compute explicitly
\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)
, one proceeds with the change of variable
u=y_{r}-y
. The expression becomes

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)=\int_{y_{r}-1}^{y_{r}-\varepsilon}\frac{du}{\sqrt{n}}

\displaystyle\left[g_{r,\gamma}(y_{r})-ug_{r,\gamma}^{\prime}(y_{r})\right]\exp(-n\left[f_r(y_r)-u f_r'(y_r)+\frac{u^2}{2} f_r''(y_r) \right])

(5.40)

and can be computed using the following lemma, which is obtained with a simple integration by part.

Lemma 5.8

Let
a,b,c,d,h,x_{0},x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
,
a\neq 0
,

\displaystyle\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}(d-hx)\exp\left(-\tfrac{a}{2}x^{2}-bx+c\right)\,dx

(5.41)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{h}{a}\left(e^{-\tfrac{ax_{1}^{2}}{2}-bx_{1}+c}-e^{-\tfrac{ax_{0}^{2}}{2}-bx_{0}+c}\right)+\sqrt{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}e^{\tfrac{b^{2}}{2a}+c}\tfrac{(ad+bh)}{a^{3/2}}\left(\erf\left(\tfrac{ax_{1}+b}{\sqrt{2a}}\right)-\erf\left(\tfrac{ax_{0}+b}{\sqrt{2a}}\right)\right),

with

\displaystyle\erf(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-t^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(5.42)

Using
Lemma 5.8
and the following expressions

\displaystyle f_{r}^{\prime}(1)=(1-2r),\quad f_{r}^{\prime\prime}(1)=-\left(4r^{2}-6r+\frac{3}{2}\right),\quad f_{r}^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{r}{1-r}\right)=2\frac{(1-r)^{3}}{r}

(5.43)

and

\displaystyle g_{r,\gamma}^{\prime}\left(\frac{r}{1-r}\right)=-\frac{4r^{2}(\alpha+\beta-\gamma-3)-r(6\alpha+2\beta-10)+2\alpha-1}{2\sqrt{\pi}\,r^{\alpha+\frac{3}{2}}\left(1-r\right)^{\beta-\frac{1}{2}}}\left(\frac{(1-r)^{2}}{1-2r}\right)^{\gamma},

(5.44)

\displaystyle g_{r,\gamma}^{\prime}(1)=-\frac{2^{\alpha+\beta-1}}{\sqrt{\pi}}r\bigg(4+\gamma(\alpha-\beta+3-8r)\bigg),

(5.45)

one gets, after simplifications, for
r\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)\underset{n\to\infty}{=}

\displaystyle\frac{(1-2r)^{-\gamma}(1-r)^{2\gamma}}{4n\sqrt{\pi}\,r^{\alpha+1}(1-r)^{\beta+n+2}}\Bigg[2\sqrt{\pi}(1-2r)r

(5.46)

\displaystyle\times\bigg[\erf\left(\sqrt{n}(1-2r)\sqrt{\tfrac{1-r}{r}}\right)+\erf\left(\sqrt{n}(r-(1-r)\epsilon)\sqrt{\tfrac{1-r}{r}}\right)\bigg]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\tfrac{r}{1-r}}\left(e^{-n(1-2r)^{2}\frac{(1-r)}{r}}-e^{-n(r-(1-r)\epsilon)^{2}\frac{(1-r)}{r}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(4r^{2}(\alpha+\beta-\gamma-3)-2r(3\alpha+\beta-5)+2\alpha-1\right)\Bigg]\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right)

and, for
r>\frac{1}{2}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)\underset{n\to\infty}{=}

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2n+\alpha+\beta+2(1-\gamma)}r^{n+1}(\alpha-\beta+7-8r)^{\gamma}}{n\sqrt{\pi}\left(4(3-2r)r-3\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{4(3-2r)r-3}

(5.47)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\pi}\left[2r-1-\gamma\left(\frac{4(3-2r)r-3}{\alpha-\beta+7-8r}\right)\right]\exp(\frac{n (2 r-1)^2}{4 (3-2 r) r-3})

\displaystyle\times\left(\erf\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}(2r-1)}{\sqrt{4(3-2r)r-3}}\right)-1\right)\Bigg]\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right).

Note that the
\varepsilon
dependence completely drops out here. This is also the case in (
5.46
) if one chooses
\varepsilon<\frac{r}{1-r}
. As
r
is bounded away from
0
, this is certainly always possible; see
Remark 5.2
. Actually, coming back to the remainder integral (
5.28
), one needs to take
\varepsilon
slightly smaller, to keep its relative contribution negligible. Indeed, taking
\varepsilon<\frac{r}{1-r}
, for
r
in compact subsets of
]0,1[
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)\underset{n\to\infty}{=}O\left(\frac{1}{n(1-r)^{n}}\right),\quad r\leqslant 1/2,

(5.48)

and

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)\underset{n\to\infty}{=}O\left(\frac{(4r)^{n}}{n}\right),\quad r>1/2.

(5.49)

The latter is obtained using the asymptotic expansion of the complementary error function

\displaystyle 1-\erf(x)

\displaystyle\underset{x\to\infty}{=}\frac{e^{-x^{2}}}{x{\sqrt{\pi}}}\left[1-\frac{1}{2x^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{x^{4}}\right)\right].

(5.50)

Therefore, comparing the order of
\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)
with the order of the remainder integral (
5.30
), one needs to choose
\varepsilon
such that, for
r
on compact subsets of
]0,1[
,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\left(\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\right)(1-r)<1,\quad r\leqslant 1/2\\
&\left(\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\right)\frac{1}{4r}<1,\quad r>1/2\end{cases},

(5.51)

which can be reduced to

\displaystyle\varepsilon<\min\left\{\frac{r}{2-r},\frac{1}{3}\right\}.

(5.52)

This choice of
\varepsilon
guarantees that the remainder integral (
5.28
) is exponentially small compared to
\mathcal{Y}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)
as
n\to\infty
. Consequently, the expansion (
5.32
) reduces to

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)\underset{n\to\infty}{=}\mathcal{Y}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)\left[1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right]\underset{n\to\infty}{=}\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)\left[1+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right].

(5.53)

Asymptotic equivalent of the
1,k
-cross-covariance function

With all the asymptotic expansions found above, we are in good position to find the desired asymptotic equivalent of the
1,k
-cross-covariance density. Let us first notice that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r}dv\,v^{\alpha+1}(1-v)^{\beta-1}P_{n}^{{(\alpha,\beta-1)}}(1-2v)\left(1-\frac{v}{r}\right)^{n-2}=\mathcal{J}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,0}(r)-\mathcal{J}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,1}(r)

(5.54)

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dt(1+t)^{-(n+2)}\frac{P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta-1)}(1+2rt)}{1+rt}t=\frac{\mathcal{I}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,1}(r)-\mathcal{I}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,0}(r)}{r}.

(5.55)

Then, gathering the asymptotic expansions (
5.12
) and (
5.20
), along with the integrals
\mathcal{J}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)
(
5.22
) and
\mathcal{I}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)
(
5.23
), one gets the following asymptotic equivalent

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r}dv\int_{0}^{\infty}dtK_{n}\left(1-\tfrac{\lambda_{1}}{n^{2}},-rt\right)K_{n}(v,1-\tfrac{\lambda_{2}}{n^{2}})\left(1-\frac{v}{r}\right)^{n-2}(1+t)^{-(n+2)}

(5.56)

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{r^{2}}\big[(1+rt)-(1-v)-\tfrac{1}{n}(r+vt)\big]

\displaystyle\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}

\displaystyle n^{2-2\beta}\lambda_{1}^{\beta}B_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(\lambda_{1})B_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(\lambda_{2})\mathcal{G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(r)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(r):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r^{2}}\bigg(\mathcal{I}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,0}(r)\left[\left(\frac{1}{nr}-\frac{r}{n}\right)\mathcal{J}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,0}(r)-\left(1+\frac{1}{nr}\right)\mathcal{J}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,1}(r)\right]

(5.57)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{I}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,1}(r)\left[\left(1-\frac{1}{nr}\right)\mathcal{J}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,0}(r)+\frac{1}{nr}\mathcal{J}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,1}(r)\right]\bigg).

The explicit expression of
\mathcal{G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(r)
is rather cumbersome, however, there is some simplification by noting

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{nr}-\frac{r}{n}\right)\mathcal{J}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,0}(r)-\left(1+\frac{1}{nr}\right)\mathcal{J}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,1}(r)

(5.58)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{r^{\alpha+1}(1-r)^{\beta+n-1}}{n}\left(1-r+\frac{1+\alpha-r(\alpha+\beta-1)}{n}\right)

and

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{1}{nr}\right)\mathcal{J}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,0}(r)+\frac{1}{nr}\mathcal{J}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,1}(r)

(5.59)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{r^{\alpha+1}(1-r)^{\beta+n-2}}{n}\left(1-2r+\frac{1+\alpha-r(\alpha+\beta)}{n}\right).

The asymptotic equivalent
\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)
of the integrals
\mathcal{J}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)
are, at the moment, defined piecewise, depending on the value of
r
. Both pieces must agree at
r=1/2
, which is precisely the value around which we are zooming.

For
r\leqslant 1/2
, using the asymptotic equivalent
\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n,\gamma}(r)
and proceeding with simplifications, one gets the following asymptotic equivalent

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(r)\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{n^{2}(1-r)^{3}\sqrt{\pi}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\sqrt{r(1-r)}-\frac{1}{n}\frac{P_{0}(r)}{4\sqrt{r(1-r)}}\right)e^{-n\frac{(1-r)}{r}(1-2r)^{2}}

(5.60)

\displaystyle+\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2n}\left[1+\erf\left((1-2r)\sqrt{\frac{n(1-r)}{r}}\right)\right](r(\alpha+\beta-2)-\alpha)\Bigg]

with the polynomial

\displaystyle P_{0}(r):=

\displaystyle 2r^{2}\left[\alpha^{2}+2\alpha(\beta-2)+(\beta-4)\beta+6\right]

(5.61)

\displaystyle+r\left[\alpha(-4\alpha-4\beta+9)+\beta-6\right]+\alpha(2\alpha-1).

From there, one can proceed with the change of variable
r\mapsto\frac{1}{2}-\frac{r}{\sqrt{n}}
. This gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{r}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{=}-\frac{4e^{-4r^{2}}}{\sqrt{\pi}n^{5/2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3}}\right).

(5.62)

For
r>1/2
, one gets the following asymptotic equivalent

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(r)\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}

\displaystyle-\frac{2^{\alpha+\beta+2n}r^{\alpha+n}(1-r)^{\beta+n-2}}{n^{5/2}\left(8r^{2}-12r+3\right)^{2}\sqrt{\pi}}\Bigg[\left(8r^{2}-12r+3\right)\left(P_{1}(r)+\frac{1}{n}P_{2}(r)\right)

(5.63)

\displaystyle-\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{-n\left(8r^{2}-12r+3\right)}\left((1-2r)^{2}(-\alpha+\beta+2r)+\frac{1}{n}P_{3}(r)\right)

\displaystyle\times e^{-\frac{n(1-2r)^{2}}{8r^{2}-12r+3}}\left(\erf\left(\frac{n(2r-1)}{\sqrt{-n\left(8r^{2}-12r+3\right)}}\right)-1\right)\Bigg],

with

\displaystyle P_{1}(r):=

\displaystyle 12r^{2}-2r(\alpha-\beta+7)+\alpha-\beta+3,

(5.64)

\displaystyle P_{2}(r):=

\displaystyle 4r^{2}(\alpha+\beta+1)-r\left(\alpha^{2}+7\alpha-\beta^{2}+3\beta+8\right)+(\alpha+1)(\alpha-\beta+3),

\displaystyle P_{3}(r):=

\displaystyle 8r^{3}+2r^{2}\left(-\alpha^{2}+\alpha+\beta^{2}+\beta-6\right)

\displaystyle+r((\alpha-\beta)(3\alpha+\beta)-2\beta+2)-(\alpha+1)(\alpha-\beta).

Then, using the asymptotic expansion of the complementary error function (
5.50
) and the same change of variable
r\mapsto\frac{1}{2}-\frac{r}{\sqrt{n}}
, one arrives at the following asymptotic equivalent

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{r}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}

\displaystyle-\frac{4}{\sqrt{\pi}n^{5/2}}\left(1+\frac{2r}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^{n}\left(1-\frac{2r}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^{n}\bigg[1+\frac{1}{n}P_{2}(1/2)

(5.65)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\pi}e^{4r^{2}}\left(\erf(2r)-1\right)\bigg].

The last step is to use the expansion

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{4r^{2}}{n}\right)^{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{=}e^{-4r^{2}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)

(5.66)

to arrive once again at

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{r}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{=}-\frac{4e^{-4r^{2}}}{\sqrt{\pi}n^{5/2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3}}\right).

(5.67)

Plugging this back in (
5.56
) and then in (
5.3
) finishes the proof.

∎

Proof of
Theorem 3.2
.

Starting from
Theorem 1.1
and following the same arguments as the proof of
Corollary 2.7
, one gets

\displaystyle\mathrm{cov}_{1,k}(r;a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})=

\displaystyle\frac{(n-k)!}{(n-1)!}\sum_{l,m=1}^{k}(-1)^{l+m}\det[K_{n}(a_{b},a_{c})]_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\setminus\{l\}\\
c\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\setminus\{m\}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{C}_{n}(r;a_{l},a_{m}).

(5.68)

Proceeding with the change of variables
r\to\frac{1}{2}-\frac{r}{\sqrt{n}}
and
a_{j}\to 1-\frac{a_{j}}{n^{2}}
,
j=1,\ldots,k
, and scaling by a factor
n^{k+2}
, one gets

\displaystyle\frac{n^{k+2}}{n^{2k+1/2}}\mathrm{cov}_{1,k}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{r}{\sqrt{n}};1-\frac{a_{1}}{n^{2}},\ldots,1-\frac{a_{k}}{n^{2}}\right)

(5.69)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(n-k)!\,n^{k}}{n!}\sum_{l,m=1}^{k}\sqrt{n}\mathbf{C}_{n}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{r}{\sqrt{n}};1-\frac{a_{l}}{n^{2}},1-\frac{a_{m}}{n^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\times(-1)^{l+m}\det[\frac{1}{n^{2}}K_{n}\left(1-\frac{a_{b}}{n^{2}},1-\frac{a_{c}}{n^{2}}\right)]_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\setminus\{l\}\\
c\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\setminus\{m\}\end{subarray}}.

Then, taking the limit
n\to\infty
with the help of (
4.25
), the limiting kernel (
3.5
) and
Proposition 5.1
, one gets, using Jacobi’s formula (cf.
(
3
, Eq.(4.10))
), that
\mathrm{cov}_{1,k\,{\rm SH}}^{(\infty)}(r;a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{4}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-4r^{2}}\partial_{\mu}\det\left[\left(\frac{a_{b}}{a_{c}}\right)^{\beta/2}4K_{\beta}^{\mathrm{Bes}}(4a_{b},4a_{c})+\mu\left(\frac{a_{b}}{a_{c}}\right)^{\beta/2}J_{\beta}(2\sqrt{a_{b}})J_{\beta}(2\sqrt{a_{c}})\right]_{b,c=1}^{k}\Bigg|_{\mu=0}.

(5.70)

The factor
\left(\frac{a_{b}}{a_{c}}\right)^{\beta/2}
can be eliminated by elementary transformations on the determinant.
Alternatively, as the dependence of
a_{b}
,
a_{c}
factorizes in the scaling limit of
\mathbf{C}_{n}
, (
5.70
) can also be put in the form

\displaystyle-\frac{4^{k}}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-4r^{2}}\det\left(\begin{array}[]{c c}0&J_{\beta}(2\sqrt{a_{c}})\\
J_{\beta}(2\sqrt{a_{b}})&K_{\beta}^{\mathrm{Bes}}(4a_{b},4a_{c})\end{array}\right)_{b,c=1}^{k}.

(5.71)

∎

6
Discussion

In the first part of this article, we have exploited the new results of
[
3
]
, for polynomial ensembles, to study their extension to the large
n
limit, which are thus also new. First, we focused on the double scaling limit around the origin for the
1,k
-point correlation function between one eigenradius and
k
singular values as well as the
1,k
-cross-covariance density function, see
Theorem 2.5
,
Corollary 2.7
and
Corollary 2.18
. This was motivated by the fact all ensembles potentially have a hard edge at the origin for both singular values and eigenradii, as they are non-negative. An interesting finding is the existence of a universal (at least for polynomial ensembles) scaling ratio of
1/n
between the scale of the smallest squared singular value and scale of the smallest squared eigenradius.

Under
Assumptions 2.1
guaranteeing the existence of the limits, the formula for the
1,k
-point correlation function involves the double scaling limit of the kernel of the corresponding determinantal point process on the singular values. The result makes clear that all ensembles for which the limiting kernel are the same will share the same limiting
1,k
-point correlation function around the origin and same limiting
1,k
-cross-covariance function (cf. (
2.14
)).

For Pólya ensembles, this translates into having same limiting Pólya weight function. As for the
1,k
-point function, it admits a more explicit form given by
Corollary 2.18
. We give the example of three classical Pólya ensembles; Laguerre, Jacobi and Cauchy-Lorentz ensembles, sharing the same limiting weight (cf.
Example 2.10
). To further illustrate this,
Figure 1
shows the similarity between the plots of the, appropriately rescaled, cross-covariance for Jacobi and Laguerre ensembles at
n=25
and the plot of their shared limiting cross-covariance. The plots of
Figure 1
also show how the deterministic constraints from Weyl’s inequalities
[
51
]
translate on a probabilistic level and survive the large
n
limit. Here, the plot of the cross-covariance function around the origin is reminiscent of the fact that the smallest eigenradius is bounded from below by the smallest singular value.

We then give the example of Muttalib-Borodin ensembles to emphasize how the main results
Corollary 2.18
and
Theorem 2.12
can be applied.

In a second part, we looked at the particular case of Jacobi ensembles which enjoy a second hard edge for the singular values. It turns out that the corresponding edge for the eigenradii is soft. The formula of the limiting
1,k
-cross-covariance density at the soft-hard edge involves some Bessel function for the singular value part, as expected, and is Gaussian in the eigenradius (cf.
Theorem 3.2
). We note that this limiting
1,k
-cross-covariance at the soft-hard edge is smaller by a factor
1/n
than the limiting
1,k
-point function and can therefore be interpreted as a correction term. Indeed, as the eigenradius does not share the same edge as the singular values in this case, the Weyl’s inequalities are much less felt and this translates in a vanishing local cross-covariance, in the large
n
limit. From this perspective, the Gaussian dependence on the eigenradius is to put in relation to a recurring phenomenon for
\beta
-ensembles, as pointed out in
[
16
]
, that the leading correction term (cf.
Remark 3.3
) at the edge (soft and hard) is related to the limiting distribution— in this case the complementary error function—by a derivative operation.

Coming back to the main results, we strongly believe the formula (
2.14
) for the limiting
1,k
-point function (equivalently, the
1,k
-cross-covariance) at the origin remains true even in the case the limiting kernel is not a function anymore but a general distribution e.g. a weighted Dirac delta function, as the formula (
2.14
) still makes sense in the distributional sense, in this case. However, proving it will require a different proof, as one would not have Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem anymore, to interchange the limit
n\to\infty
and the integrals. We also believe that
Assumptions 2.8
strictly imply
Assumptions 2.1
. In
Appendix A
, we show that the technical restriction (
2.10
) on polynomial ensembles is always verified for Pólya ensembles verifying
Assumptions 2.8
, as well as their compositions; see
Remark 2.11
. Some assumptions can possibly be lifted with some further analysis and making use of some property of the underlying Pólya frequency functions such as their log-concavity
[
45
,
18
,
19
]
as well as the log-convexity of the Mellin transform of probability measures (on each subinterval of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
where it is finite).

While
Theorem 2.5
targets the largest class of ensembles where the formula (
2.14
) holds, it relies on
Assumptions 2.1
which are hard to check in practice as they require finding the bi-orthonormal system of functions composing the kernel of the corresponding determinantal point process and this for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. On the other hand,
Theorem 2.12
and
Corollary 2.18
are much more useful, as
Assumptions 2.8
are much easier to check. Indeed, the assumptions bear only on the Pólya weight function, which is easier to find in practice.

This barely diminishes our results as the class of Pólya ensembles, albeit being smaller than the class of polynomial ensembles, is very large and comprises most of the classical ensembles in the field of random matrices along with their compositions due to their closure property, as discussed in
section 1
and
Remark 2.11
.

Appendix A
Consistency check of assumptions

Let us show that, for Pólya ensembles verifying
Assumptions 2.8
and for which the polynomials
\{p_{j}\}_{j=0,\ldots,n-1}
are given by

\displaystyle p_{j}\left(\frac{x}{\nu_{n}}\right)=\sum_{c=0}^{j}\binom{j}{c}\frac{(-x)^{c}}{\xi_{n}\nu_{n}^{c}\mathcal{M}w_{n}(c+1)},

(A.1)

the following lemma holds.

Lemma A.1

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n>2
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
R>0
. For a Pólya ensemble verifying
Assumptions 2.8
with polynomials
\{p_{j}\}_{j=0,\ldots,n-1}
given by (
A.1
), we have

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{R}^{\infty}dt\frac{(1+t)}{\left(1+\frac{t}{n}\right)^{n+2}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\absolutevalue{p_j\left(\frac{x t}{n\nu_n} \right) }=0.

(A.2)

Proof of
Lemma A.1
.

The idea of the following computation is to rewrite the sum of polynomials as another polynomial and use the
Assumptions 2.8
to show the non-vanishing contribution of the integrand in (
A.2
) remains in a bounded interval close to the origin as
n\to\infty
. The assumption (
2.27
) excludes a fixed number of the first summands. This is not an issue as one can split the sum in two parts and deal with this fixed number of summands separately. Then, one can compute the
t
integral explicitly in terms of an incomplete beta functions and treat the different regimes depending of the indices
j
.

Let us start by denoting

\displaystyle\alpha_{c,n}:=\xi_{n}\nu_{n}^{c}\mathcal{M}w_{n}(c+1).

(A.3)

We then use the summation identity

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\sum_{k=0}^{j}a_{k,j}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\sum_{c=j}^{n-1}a_{j,c}

(A.4)

to express the sum of polynomials as another polynomial

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\absolutevalue{p_j\left(\frac{x }{n\nu_n} \right) }\leqslant\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\sum_{k=0}^{j}\binom{j}{k}\frac{\absolutevalue{x}^{k}}{n^{k}\absolutevalue{\alpha_{k,n}}}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{c=j}^{n-1}\binom{c}{j}\right)\frac{\absolutevalue{x}^{j}}{n^{j}\absolutevalue{\alpha_{j,n}}}.

(A.5)

Using the following bound,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{c=j}^{n-1}\binom{c}{j}=\binom{n-1}{j}\frac{1}{j+1}\leqslant\binom{n-1}{j},

(A.6)

one gets to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\absolutevalue{p_j\left(\frac{x t}{n\nu_n} \right) }\leqslant\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{j}\frac{\absolutevalue{xt}^{j}}{n^{j}\absolutevalue{\alpha_{j,n}}}.

(A.7)

Turning to the integral, we then get the following bound after proceeding with the change of variable
t\mapsto nt
,

\displaystyle\int_{R}^{\infty}dt\frac{(1+t)}{\left(1+\frac{t}{n}\right)^{n+2}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\absolutevalue{p_j\left(\frac{x t}{n\nu_n} \right) }\leqslant S_{n}(R)+T_{n}(R),

(A.8)

with

\displaystyle S_{n}(R):=2n^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor R\rfloor-1}\int_{\frac{R}{n}}^{\infty}dt\frac{t}{\left(1+t\right)^{n+2}}\binom{n-1}{j}\frac{\absolutevalue{xt}^{j}}{\absolutevalue{\alpha_{j,n}}},

(A.9)

and

\displaystyle T_{n}(R):=2n^{2}\sum_{j=\lfloor R\rfloor}^{n-1}\int_{\frac{R}{n}}^{\infty}dt\frac{t}{\left(1+t\right)^{n+2}}\binom{n-1}{j}\frac{\absolutevalue{xt}^{j}}{h(j)}.

(A.10)

We have introduced, here, a cut-off at
j=\lfloor R\rfloor
, which serves to distinguish between two different asymptotic regimes. Note that we have used assumption (
2.27
) to get (
A.10
). Indeed, as
j_{*}
is fixed one can always choose
R
big enough such that
R>j_{*}
.

Computation of the
t
integral

The
t
integral can be computed term by term and yields an incomplete beta function

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{R}{n}}^{\infty}dt\frac{t^{j+1}}{\left(1+t\right)^{n+2}}=\mathrm{B}\left(\frac{n}{n+R};n-j,j+2\right)=\int_{0}^{\frac{n}{n+R}}dt\,t^{n-j-1}(1-t)^{j+1}.

(A.11)

To extract the
R
dependence which will be crucial in the coming analysis, one needs to distinguish two different regimes as
j
can grow with
n
. Hence the arbitrary choice of the cutoff
j=\lfloor R\rfloor
.

The first regime is for
j\in\llbracket 0,\lfloor R\rfloor-1\rrbracket
. As
j
does not grow with
n
here, one can use the asymptotic expansion of the beta function, given in
[
35
]
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}\left(\frac{n}{n+R};n-j,j+2\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{=}\frac{1}{n-j+1}\left(\frac{n}{n+R}\right)^{n-j}\left(\frac{R}{n+R}\right)^{j+1}\left[1+O\left(\frac{(j+1)!}{n-j+1}\right)\right].

(A.12)

For the second regime,
j\in\llbracket\lfloor R\rfloor-1,n-1\rrbracket
, one can simply use the identity

\displaystyle\binom{n-1}{j}=\frac{j+1}{n(n+1)}\frac{1}{\mathrm{B}(n-j,j+2)}

(A.13)

to get the following bound

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}\left(\frac{n}{n+R};n-j,j+2\right)\leqslant\mathrm{B}(n-j,j+2)=\frac{j+1}{n(n+1)}\frac{1}{\binom{n-1}{j}}.

(A.14)

Computation of
S_{n}(R)

Let us first focus on
S_{n}(R)
(
A.9
). The integral is given by the beta function (
A.11
) and one can split the sum in two parts to use assumption (
2.27
) and get the bound

\displaystyle S_{n}(R)\leqslant S_{n}^{(1)}(R)+S_{n}^{(2)}(R),

(A.15)

with

\displaystyle S_{n}^{(1)}(R)

\displaystyle:=2n^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{j_{*}-1}\binom{n-1}{j}\frac{\absolutevalue{x}^{j}}{\absolutevalue{\alpha_{j,n}}}\mathrm{B}\left(\frac{n}{n+R};n-j,j+2\right),

(A.16)

\displaystyle S_{n}^{(2)}(R)

\displaystyle:=2n^{2}\sum_{j=j_{*}}^{\lfloor R\rfloor-1}\binom{n-1}{j}\frac{\absolutevalue{x}^{j}}{h(j)}\mathrm{B}\left(\frac{n}{n+R};n-j,j+2\right).

As all indices are in the interval
[0,R-1]
one can use the expansion (
A.12
). Combining it with the fact

\displaystyle\binom{n-1}{j}\frac{1}{n^{j}}\leqslant\frac{1}{j!},

(A.17)

along with the bound assumption (
2.25
) on
S_{n}^{(1)}(R)
, one gets, for all
n
large enough,

\displaystyle S_{n}^{(1)}(R)\leqslant C_{1}R\left(1-\frac{R}{n+R}\right)^{n-j_{*}+1}\sum_{j=0}^{j_{*}-1}\frac{(R\absolutevalue{x})^{j}}{j!},

(A.18)

\displaystyle S_{n}^{(2)}(R)\leqslant C_{2}R\left(1-\frac{R}{n+R}\right)^{n-\lfloor R\rfloor+1}\sum_{j=j_{*}}^{\lfloor R\rfloor-1}\frac{(R\absolutevalue{x})^{j}}{j!\,h(j)},

(A.19)

where
C_{1},C_{2}
are some positive constants independent of
n
and
R
. As
j_{*}
is
n
-independent, taking the limit
n\to\infty
on both sides of the two inequalities above yields

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}S_{n}^{(1)}(R)+S_{n}^{(2)}(R)\leqslant C_{1}Re^{-R}\sum_{j=0}^{j_{*}-1}\frac{(R\absolutevalue{x})^{j}}{j!}+C_{2}Re^{-R}\sum_{j=j_{*}}^{\lfloor R\rfloor-1}\frac{(R\absolutevalue{x})^{j}}{j!\,h(j)}.

(A.20)

Let us show the RHS of the above inequality vanishes in the limit
R\to\infty
. Making use of Stirling approximation to get a rough lower bound of the factorial,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{j}{e}\right)^{j}\leqslant\sqrt{2\pi j}\left(\frac{j}{e}\right)^{j}\leqslant\Gamma(j+1),\quad j\geqslant 1,

(A.21)

one gets the following inequalities

\displaystyle e^{-R}\sum_{j=j_{*}}^{\lfloor R\rfloor-1}\frac{(R\absolutevalue{x})^{j}}{j!\,h(j)}\leqslant e^{-R/2}\sum_{j=j_{*}}^{\lfloor R\rfloor-1}\left(\frac{R}{j}\exp(-\frac{R}{2j})\absolutevalue{x}e\right)^{j}\frac{1}{h(j)}\leqslant e^{-R/2}\sum_{j=j_{*}}^{\infty}\frac{\left(2\absolutevalue{x}\right)^{j}}{h(j)},

(A.22)

where we have used the fact

\displaystyle y\exp(-y/2)\leqslant 2e^{-1},\quad\forall y\geqslant 0.

(A.23)

Note that the RHS of (
A.22
) is indeed finite due to the root asymptotic of
h
(
2.28
), which guarantees infinite radius of convergence of the series, by Cauchy-Hadamard theorem. Moreover, as
j_{*}
is independent of
R
, the series itself is independent of
R
and we have

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}\left(C_{1}Re^{-R}\sum_{j=0}^{j_{*}-1}\frac{(R\absolutevalue{x})^{j}}{j!}+e^{-R/2}\sum_{j=j_{*}}^{\infty}\frac{\left(2\absolutevalue{x}\right)^{j}}{h(j)}\right)=0,

(A.24)

which finishes to show
\lim_{R\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}S_{n}(R)=0
.

Computation of
T_{n}(R)

Turning to
T_{n}(R)
(
A.10
), in this case, all the indices are in the interval
[R,n-1]
. Therefore, one can use the bound (
A.14
) to get

\displaystyle T_{n}(R)\leqslant 2\sum_{j=\lfloor R\rfloor}^{n-1}(j+1)\frac{\absolutevalue{x}^{j}}{h(j)}.

(A.25)

Due to the root asymptotic of
h
(
2.28
), by Cauchy-Hadamard theorem, the RHS is a convergent series as
n\to\infty
. Hence,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}T_{n}(R)\leqslant 2\sum_{j=\lfloor R\rfloor}^{\infty}(j+1)\frac{\absolutevalue{x}^{j}}{h(j)}.

(A.26)

The RHS of the above inequality being the remainder of a convergent series, taking the limit
R\to\infty
finishes to show
\lim_{R\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}T_{n}(R)=0
.

∎
