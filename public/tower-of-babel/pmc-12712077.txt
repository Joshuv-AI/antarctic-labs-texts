RETRACTED ARTICLE: Hydrogen-induced microstructural evolution in a nickel-based alloy under high-temperature and high-pressure conditions
Authors: Mitsuharu Yonemura, Itsuki Yamaguchi, Hidenori Toyokawa, Hiroyuki Saitoh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAchieving carbon neutrality requires advanced hydrogen-based processes, such as direct reduction of iron ore, wherein the long-term stability of structural materials under high-temperature and high-pressure hydrogen is critical. This study directly visualizes hydrogen-induced microstructural evolution in Inconel 600, a Ni-based alloy, under 1–3 GPa employing in situ high-energy X-ray diffraction, transmission electron microscopy, and electron backscatter diffraction. Hydrogen absorption promoted pressure-dependent lattice expansion, hydride formation behavior, and dislocation multiplication. At 400 °C, fine cracks formed along grain boundaries and intersecting slip bands, whereas at 800 °C, abundant intragranular voids were observed. The migration of planar dislocation generated slip bands that served as preferential pathways for crack propagation, facilitating hydrogen reaction embrittlement. Minor lattice contraction following isothermal holding reflected hydrogen desorption and carbide gasification (CH₄ formation from Cr₇C₃-type carbides). These findings reveal a sequential, chain-like evolution of microstructure under extreme hydrogen environments, providing mechanistic insights for the design of durable Ni-based alloys for hydrogen-utilizing carbon-neutral technologies.Keywords: Hydrogen embrittlement, Microstructural evolution, X-ray diffraction, Ni-based alloys, Carbon-neutral technologiesSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Achieving carbon neutrality by 2050 is a critical global objective, and the steel industry, which accounts for approximately 7% of global CO₂ emissions, encounters an urgent demand for fundamental decarbonization1. Among emerging strategies, hydrogen-based direct reduction of iron ore has garnered significant attention as a promising approach for substantially reducing CO₂ emissions by replacing carbon-based fuels with hydrogen gas2–4. However, the successful implementation of this process requires robust materials capable of withstanding prolonged exposure to high-temperature hydrogen environments. In particular, reactor and piping components must exhibit high resistance to hydrogen-induced embrittlement and microstructural degradation5–8.

Prior studies have predominantly relied on ex situ techniques, wherein samples are extracted from hydrogen environments prior to characterization. These approaches are inherently limited because oxidation, hydrogen desorption, and stress relaxation can alter the microstructure, complicating accurate interpretation of the underlying mechanisms driving hydrogen embrittlement9,10. Furthermore, the interactions between hydrogen and dislocations in metals—which govern phenomena such as hydrogen-enhanced localized plasticity (HELP) and hydrogen-enhanced decohesion (HEDE)—have largely been inferred from theoretical models, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), or numerical simulations, with limited studies providing direct, real-time observations under high-temperature hydrogen environments11–13. In the HELP mechanism, hydrogen facilitates dislocation mobility, leading to highly localized plastic deformation, whereas in HEDE, hydrogen reduces atomic cohesion at grain boundaries or interfaces, thereby promoting crack initiation and growth. Together, these mechanisms are central to understanding hydrogen-induced degradation in structural alloys.

This constitutes a critical gap in understanding hydrogen embrittlement mechanisms in service-relevant conditions. To address this gap, we selected the commercially available Ni-based alloy Inconel 600 as a model system for in-situ investigation under high-temperature hydrogen environments. This alloy is widely used in aerospace and nuclear applications owing to its proven long-term performance under high-temperature and high-pressure conditions, offering excellent corrosion and heat resistance. Its pronounced Cr₇C₃-type carbide precipitation behavior at elevated temperatures makes it particularly suitable for investigating phase stability, hydrogen embrittlement mechanisms, and dynamic dislocation evolution under hydrogen exposure14.

We employed a combination of in situ high-energy X-ray diffraction (HEXRD)15,16, TEM, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) to probe the microstructural evolution of Inconel 600 under static pressures of 1 to 3 GPa. This integrated approach enabled real-time observation of hydrogen solubility, local stress generation, dislocation density evolution, and the formation of microcracks and voids—phenomena that were largely inaccessible via conventional ex situ techniques.

Theoretical models, TEM observations, and numerical simulations have revealed the occurrence of hydrogen–dislocation interactions and dislocation recovery in FCC steels and Ni-based alloys. However, studies on their validation via direct, time-resolved investigations under high-temperature, high-pressure hydrogen environments have been rarely reported. The novelty of the present study lies in the following aspects. First, the hydrogen absorption process under high pressure was quantitatively monitored in real time; the observations revealed pressure-dependent octahedral site occupancy and the associated lattice expansion. Second, hydrogen-induced dislocation multiplication, intersecting slip-band formation, intergranular and intragranular crack initiation, and subsequent void formation were visualized as a sequential, stepwise evolution process. Third, decomposition of Cr₇C₃ carbides under high-temperature, high-pressure hydrogen conditions (CH₄ generation) and the resulting local volumetric changes were confirmed by TEM and EBSD data, which suggested a complementary mechanism to the conventional superabundant vacancy (SAV) model. Overall, the findings of this study do not merely corroborate previously reported phenomena; rather, this paper presents results of the first direct, time-resolved visualization of sequential microstructural evolution and hydrogen embrittlement mechanisms in a practical Ni-based alloy under extreme hydrogen conditions.

These findings provide fundamental insights that are critical for evaluating the durability of materials and for guiding the design of alloys for hydrogen-based steelmaking. Thus, this study is expected to contribute to the advancement of carbon-neutral technologies in the steel industry.

Method

Materials

A commercial Ni-based alloy, Inconel 600 (UNS N06600; Ni 72–76 mass%, Cr 14–17 mass%, Fe 6–10 mass%, Mn ≤ 1 mass%, Si ≤ 0.5 mass%, C ≤ 0.08 mass%) was selected as the model material as discussed above. Disc-shaped specimens (diameter = 1 mm, thickness = 0.6 mm) were precision-machined from sheet normal to the rolling plane to minimize crystallographic texture effects.

High-pressure and high-temperature in situ setup

In situ high-pressure/high-temperature experiments were performed using a cubic-type multianvil press capable of reaching 10 GPa and 1000 °C17. This apparatus was custom-designed for high-pressure synchrotron radiation experiments. Detailed descriptions of the multianvil press will be given in a later section. This setup enabled synchrotron XRD under multi-GPa hydrostatic pressure while directly supplying hydrogen, enabling structural analysis with high spatial and temporal resolution18,19. The cell assembly comprised the specimen, heating elements, a solid hydrogen source, and BN insulating components. Molybdenum electrodes were placed at both ends of the cell to provide Joule heating, while metal pistons simultaneously compressed the pressure medium and served as current leads. NH₃BH₃ was used as the hydrogen source20; upon heating, it released high-purity H₂ in conjunction with amorphous BN. The cell was fully sealed to maintain hydrogen partial pressure at 100% throughout the thermal cycle. For hydrogen-free controls, the hydrogen source was replaced with BN disk under otherwise identical conditions.

Experiments were conducted at fixed hydrostatic pressures of 1, 2, and 3 GPa to accelerate hydrogen absorption, with temperatures up to 800 °C. Increasing the pressure enhanced hydrogen uptake, promoting more rapid lattice expansion and hydride formation behavior. Although compressive stress can reduce the lattice’s free volume, high hydrostatic pressures primarily increase the hydrogen solubility by shifting the equilibrium between molecular and atomic hydrogen, thereby accelerating absorption. Thus, under gigapascal conditions, hydrogen absorption is governed primarily by the enhanced chemical potential of hydrogen rather than by the diffusion limitation. At 3 GPa, after 30 min of isothermal holding, samples were quenched at cooling rates of approximately 100 °C s⁻¹ and subsequently decompressed. This protocol preserved the phases and defect structures formed under high-temperature and high-pressure hydrogen for ex situ characterization.

Synchrotron XRD

Synchrotron X-ray experiments were conducted at the QST BL14B1 beamline in SPring-8 (Hyogo, Japan)21,22. A monochromatic 70 keV beam (λ = 0.017714 nm; beam size: 0.3 × 0.2 mm) was transmitted through the hydrostatically compressed specimen. Diffracted X-rays were recorded using a two-dimensional (2D) CdTe energy-threshold detector (191 × 201 pixels; 0.2 × 0.2 mm pixel size), developed at the Japan Synchrotron Radiation Research Institute23,24. To ensure adequate X-ray transmission, portions of the anvil tips were replaced with BN. The 2D detector covered a wide reciprocal-space range, enabling reliable phase identification even for textured specimens25. The camera length was set to 400 mm, and 2D diffraction images were acquired in the 2θ range of approximately 4° to 15°, divided into low- and high-angle sides, with an exposure time of 5 s. Data acquisition was synchronized with the onset of heating, and measurement reproducibility was validated over the entire process.

Multianvil press and pressure medium

The multianvil press converts a uniaxial hydraulic load into three orthogonal compressive forces applied by six anvils to a cubic pressure medium containing the sealed cell. This was achieved by using two dies and four wedge-shaped sliding blocks. Two of six anvils were mounted on the two dies, while the remaining four were positioned on the side blocks. When the upper die moved toward the lower die under hydraulic load, the four sliding blocks, sandwiched between the dies, moved toward the center of the apparatus, causing all six anvils to move inward synchronously. These anvils compressed the pressure medium to generate high pressure at the center of the pressure medium. A more detailed description of the apparatus is provided in ref26. The pressure medium was designed with an edge length approximately 50% larger than the truncated edge length of the anvil, creating a fully enclosed chamber for the sample and cell components. Material extruded into the approximately 200 μm gaps between the anvils acted as gaskets, maintaining pressure and ensuring stable hydrogen containment. Heating was provided by a cylindrical heater embedded within the pressure medium.

Postmortem microstructural characterization

To determine microstructural and precipitate differences between hydrogen-charged and hydrogen-free conditions, SEM, EBSD, and TEM analyses were performed on the quenched specimens. SEM–EBSD measurements were conducted employing field-emission SEM (JSM-7001 F, JEOL) and an EBSD detector (Hikari, TSL Solutions). SEM imaging was performed at 15 kV with a 15 mm working distance (WD), while EBSD analysis was conducted at 20 kV and 15 mm WD under 70° tilt with standard background subtraction. These conditions enabled reliable evaluation of orientation deviations, intergranular cracking, and local strain distributions. Site-specific TEM lamellae were prepared by focused ion beam (FIB) micro-sampling from the foil surface to a depth of approximately 10 μm. A protective carbon cap layer was deposited to mitigate Ga-ion-induced surface damage. FIB milling was performed using a Hitachi High-Tech MI4050 at 30 kV. TEM imaging was conducted on a JEOL JEM-F200 operated at 200 kV to identify local microstructures, hydrogen-induced phases, and dislocation configurations with high spatial resolution.

Rationale and capability

This integrated approach—multi-GPa hydrogen charging with in situ HEXRD, followed by targeted FIB-TEM and EBSD—provides a direct correlation between real-time lattice responses and postmortem defect and phase architectures. In particular, it relates diffraction-derived lattice expansion and contraction under hydrogen to spatial maps of cracking, void formation, and precipitate evolution, establishing a quantitative framework for interpreting hydrogen–metal interactions under high-temperature and high-pressure conditions relevant to practical applications.

X-ray line profile analysis

XRD line profiles were obtained by integrating the diffraction images in the circumferential direction. X-ray line profile analysis (XLPA), originally developed by Williamson and Hall27 and Warren and Averbach28 in the 1950s, was applied to characterize the defect substructure. Following the theoretical framework established by Ungár et al.29,30 in the 1980s, the line profiles were analyzed considering: (1) the effects of anisotropic lattice strains on crystallographic orientations, and (2) the strength of the lattice strains surrounding dislocations. These factors were used to determine the optimal relationship between the dislocation density and X-ray line profiles. Large lattice strains occur in specific crystallographic orientations because the Burgers vector depends on the crystal system31. Using modified Williamson–Hall and Warren–Averbach procedures with a mean contrast factor based on the elastic anisotropy, XLPA enables evaluation of substructural properties, such as dislocation density, edge/screw dislocation fraction, and dislocation arrangement.

Results and discussion

In situ heating under hydrogen environment

To investigate the influence of external pressure on hydrogen absorption in Ni-based alloy samples, in situ two-dimensional HEXRD was performed under hydrostatic pressures of 1, 2, and 3 GPa during heating. The application of elevated pressures accelerated hydrogen uptake and promoted microstructural evolution. Time-resolved analysis was achieved by azimuthally integrating Debye–Scherrer rings to monitor γ-phase {111} diffraction profiles. Under ambient air, the {111}γ diffraction peak shifted smoothly to lower angles due solely to thermal expansion (Fig. 1a). By contrast, exposure to hydrogen above approximately 400 °C induced pronounced pressure-dependent low-angle shifts. At 1 GPa, only minor lattice expansion was observed, indicating limited hydrogen solubility (Fig. 1b). At 2 GPa, the peak shift became substantial, signifying significant hydrogen uptake and the onset of microstructural modifications (Fig. 1c). At 3 GPa, peak splitting and weak diffuse intensity were evident at approximately 400 °C, reflecting extensive lattice expansion, hydride formation behavior and local accumulation of supersaturated hydrogen (Fig. 1d). These results reveal a clear pressure-dependent progression of hydrogen-induced lattice changes, confirming that hydrostatic pressure is a critical parameter in enhancing hydrogen absorption and driving subsequent microstructural evolution35,36. Furthermore, above a threshold pressure (2–3 GPa), hydrogen induced lattice distortions, defect accumulation, and transient hydride-like phase formation even at temperatures as low as 400 °C.

Time-resolved evolution of the γ-phase {111} diffraction peak during heating at varying conditions. (a) In air; Under hydrogen at (b) 1 GPa, (c) 2 GPa, and (d) 3 GPa. The comparison highlights pressure-dependent peak broadening and shifts associated with hydrogen absorption and lattice expansion.

Upon further heating, the diffuse component diminished and a single peak reemerged near 800 °C, indicating lattice homogenization via hydrogen redistribution or desorption. These results indicate that under extreme pressure (3 GPa), hydrogen absorption, solubilization, and redistribution proceeded in discrete stages, inducing temporally and spatially heterogeneous structural evolution. This behavior is governed by complex thermodynamic and kinetic interactions between hydrogen and the metal lattice under high pressure37–39.

The evolution of lattice parameters under different pressures is presented in Fig. 2. For samples heated in air, the thermal expansion coefficient of Inconel 600 was determined as 1.35 × 10⁻⁵ °C⁻¹, closely matching literature values at 20–100 °C (~ 1.31 × 10⁻⁵ °C⁻¹), indicating minimal influence from stress or microstructural effects40. Although the room-temperature (25 °C) lattice parameter decreased slightly with increasing pressure, the thermal expansion coefficient remained statistically unchanged.

Notably, at 1 GPa, x changed only slightly near 800 °C, whereas at 2 GPa and 3 GPa, clear shifts toward lower diffraction angles indicate lattice expansion from hydrogen uptake. This behavior indicates hydrogen desorption or redistribution at elevated temperatures, combined with pressure-enhanced accommodation of lattice defects. These results demonstrate that hydrogen-induced lattice expansion was strongly pressure-dependent and that hydrostatic pressure significantly enhanced hydrogen solubility, driving stepwise microstructural evolution within the FCC lattice39.

Figure 3 shows the temperature dependence of dislocation density by XLPA under various hydrogen pressures. At 1 GPa, the dislocation density remained nearly constant with temperature, indicating limited hydrogen–dislocation interaction. In sharp contrast, at 2 GPa the dislocation density exhibited a pronounced maximum at approximately 490 °C, while at 3 GPa it peaked sharply at near 430 °C. These characteristic temperatures align with the well-documented onset range of hydrogen attack, where pressurized hydrogen destabilizes the lattice and accelerates dislocation multiplication42–44. Notably, these temperatures fell within the hydrogen-attack regime (400–500 °C) of steels and Ni-based alloys45. Thus, the combined XRD-measured lattice expansion and dislocation density data provide robust indirect evidence for hydrogen-mediated dislocation processes.

Temperature dependence of dislocation density in Inconel 600 under various hydrogen pressures. Pronounced peaks in dislocation density are observed from 400 to 500 °C, indicating enhanced dislocation activity in this temperature regime.

The marked shift of the dislocation density peak toward lower temperatures with increasing hydrogen pressure indicates that high hydrogen activity substantially lowers the energetic barrier for dislocation nucleation. This behavior is consistent with hydrogen occupying octahedral interstitial sites, which weakens atomic bonding and facilitates dislocation migration. At higher temperatures, dislocation annihilation and recovery dominate, reducing the dislocation density.

Notably, the peak dislocation densities induced by hydrogen reached values on the order of 10¹⁴ m⁻², comparable to those typically observed in lath martensite microstructures of carbon steels46,47. While a high dislocation density does not directly cause failure, it indicates the occurrence of intense lattice distortion and localized stress accumulation due to hydrogen. The strong lattice distortion and localized stress accumulation, together with the observed lattice expansion and intersecting slip bands, highlight regions susceptible to microcrack nucleation and propagation. They collectively provide mechanistic insights that are consistent with the results obtained using HELP and HEDE models.

Microstructural evolution during heating and isothermal holding

Figure 4 summarizes the microstructural evolution occurring during hydrogen heating and subsequent isothermal holding, as revealed by the temporal evolution of the {111}γ diffraction profile. EBSD-based grain reference orientation deviation (GROD) maps, were used to visualize the intragranular strain localization, demonstrating that hydrogen heating produced numerous fine cracks both along grain boundaries and within grains, effectively segmenting the grains (Fig. 4a–c). By contrast, specimens heated in air exhibited neither crack formation nor significant strain localization under identical thermal conditions (Fig. 4f). Instead, they contained abundant Cr₇C₃ carbides, precipitating preferentially at grain boundaries as coarse particles and within grains as plate-like features. This sharp contrast indicates that hydrogen directly induced these anomalous microstructural transformations. In FCC lattices, low-index planes such as {111} and {100}, which exhibit relatively low surface energies, preferentially act as cleavage planes. Therefore, the observed cracks intersect at approximately 60°, 90°, and 120°, angles characteristic of dislocation activity along {111} and {100} slip planes53. These localized strain concentrations facilitated rapid crack nucleation and propagation under hydrogen exposure.

Following isothermal holding at 800 °C, the γ-phase {111} lattice spacing decreased slightly (Fig. 4d–e), in conjunction with the formation of numerous intragranular voids (1–2 μm in diameter). This observation suggests that solute hydrogen activated local stress fields and defect sites, thereby accelerating void formation. The apparent decrease in {111}γ lattice spacing is interpreted not as a uniform lattice contraction, but rather as a consequence of local volumetric changes associated with hydrogen-induced voids, with lattice-strain relaxation serving as a secondary factor.

Overall, high-temperature hydrogen exposure promoted a unique combination of grain-boundary cracking and intragranular void formation—phenomena absent under conventional air heating. By contrast, air heating consistently yielded extensive precipitation of Cr₇C₃ carbides, highlighting the fundamentally different microstructural pathways activated by hydrogen.

These observations demonstrate that hydrogen governed local stress fields and defect evolution, modifying the microstructure via lattice relaxation and void formation.

TEM analysis of quenched samples further clarified these mechanisms (Fig. 5a–e). In the initial state, grain boundaries were linear, with no observable intragranular or grain-boundary precipitates (Fig. 5a). Heating to 400 °C in hydrogen produced multiple intersecting slip bands along with dislocation cells (Fig. 5d), indicating localized lattice strain and dislocation generation driven by hydrogen absorption. Local lattice perturbations, possibly due to transient hydride-like phases, further increased slip activity and internal stress. These hydrogen-induced slip bands served as preferential pathways for crack initiation and propagation, thereby promoting hydrogen-induced embrittlement. By contrast, samples heated without hydrogen exhibited only limited dislocation activity and no intersecting slip bands.

TEM micrographs of Inconel 600 under different conditions, highlighting microstructural evolution and hydrogen effects. (a) Initial state with straight grain boundaries and no precipitates; (b) sample heated to 400 °C in air, exhibiting Cr₇C₃ carbide films along curved grain boundaries; (c) sample heated to 800 °C in air, exhibiting extensive inter- and intragranular Cr₇C₃ carbides; (d) hydrogen-charged sample at 400 °C, revealing twins and dislocation cells within grains, indicative of hydrogen-enhanced lattice strain and dislocation activity; (e) hydrogen-charged sample at 800 °C, showing minimal precipitate formation, demonstrating the suppressive effect of hydrogen on carbide precipitation and its role in lattice deformation.

In air, film-like chromium carbide (Cr₇C₃) precipitates formed at curved grain boundaries at 400 °C (Fig. 5b) and became abundant at 800 °C both within and along grains. (Fig. 5c). By contrast, hydrogen-charged samples exhibited only minimal carbide precipitation (Fig. 5e). These findings indicate that hydrogen not only induced lattice strain and dislocation generation but also substantially suppressed Cr₇C₃ carbide precipitation.

The fine cracks and voids observed under high-temperature, high-pressure hydrogen conditions were consistent with hydrogen-induced embrittlement. Solute hydrogen reacted locally with carbides to generate methane or hydride-like species, generating local stress fields that facilitated cracking. The multiple fine cracks observed at 400 °C are attributed to hydrogen attack, whereas extensive void formation at 800 °C indicates microstructural transformation driven by carbide–hydrogen reactions.

Richardson reported that, based on the standard Gibbs free energies of carbide formation and their reactions with hydrogen, Fe₃C is relatively unstable, whereas Cr₂₃C₆ remains stable in the presence of hydrogen, making methane formation thermodynamically unfavorable54. Nevertheless, hydrogen-assisted carbide decarburization has been observed in stainless steels such as SUS304, leading to bubble nucleation and growth55. By contrast, high-temperature, high-pressure hydrogen exposure (600 °C, 29.4 MPa) in Cr–Mo steels do not reduce the Cr₂₃C₆ phase fraction, while Cr₇C₃ undergoes an observable decrease, indicating active hydrogen–carbide reactions56. Furthermore, increasing Fe content enhances hydrogen reactivity, resulting in methane bubble formation.

The experimentally observed minor lattice contraction after isothermal holding indicates progressive hydrogen desorption and concomitant carbide gasification (CH₄ formation from Cr₇C₃). This phenomenon provides a complementary mechanism to the conventional interpretation based on superabundant vacancies (SAV) proposed by Fukai57, where an excessive vacancy concentration facilitates lattice contraction and enhanced hydrogen–defect interactions. Notably, the concurrent hydrogen release and lattice shrinkage highlights the dynamic interplay between interstitial hydrogen, carbide decomposition, and vacancy-mediated lattice relaxation, revealing an underexplored route for microstructural evolution under hydrogen-charged conditions.

In summary, high-temperature, high-pressure hydrogen treatment of Inconel 600 induced a sequential microstructural evolution: hydrogen lattice penetration → local stress amplification → hydride formation behavior→ dislocation multiplication → intersecting slip-band formation → intragranular and intergranular microcracks → carbide–hydrogen reactions generating localized free space → void formation at the 1–2 μm scale.

Collectively, these findings provide a mechanistic framework for understanding stepwise microstructural evolution in extreme hydrogen environments. By integrating quantitative lattice measurements with high-resolution TEM and SEM-EBSD characterization, this study provides a comprehensive description of hydrogen-induced degradation, offering direct insight into the pressure- and temperature-dependent failure mechanisms of Ni-based alloys and the onset of hydrogen reaction embrittlement. It should be noted, however, that the findings are constrained by the limited range of hydrogen pressures, temperatures, and alloy compositions examined, the potential variability introduced during sample preparation, and the indirect estimation of hydrogen content. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the results and assessing their applicability to other Ni-based alloys. Despite these constraints, the present study establishes a valuable framework for understanding hydrogen-induced degradation and provides a basis for future investigations across broader conditions and alloy systems.

Conclusions

High-temperature and high-pressure hydrogen exposure of Inconel 600 induced pressure-dependent lattice expansion and pronounced microstructural transformations. Under 3 GPa, FCC octahedral sites approached full occupancy (NiDx, x ≈ 0.7), producing local volumetric expansion. Hydrogen at 400 °C generated fine cracks along grain boundaries and intersecting slip bands, whereas isothermal holding at 800 °C produced abundant intragranular voids. These microstructural changes were attributed to the combined effects of solute hydrogen-induced local stresses, carbide–hydrogen reactions, and hydride formation behavior. Planar dislocation migration under local stress concentration formed slip bands that served as preferential pathways for crack propagation, facilitating hydrogen reaction embrittlement. The minor lattice contraction observed after holding the sample at a temperature of 800 °C provides indirect evidence of hydrogen desorption and localized reactions with Cr₇C₃ carbides, which potentially generate CH₄. Although methane bubbles were not directly imaged, the lattice shrinkage and associated microstructural changes confirmed this mechanism, complementing the results derived using the conventional SAV models.

These findings demonstrate that the microstructural evolution of Inconel 600 under extreme hydrogen conditions proceeds sequentially in a chain-like manner. The results provide critical insights for evaluating the durability and embrittlement mechanisms of Ni-based alloys. Future research should broaden these experimental parameters, incorporate direct hydrogen quantification, and evaluate alloy performance under representative service conditions. Additionally, the limitations of this study are acknowledged and further discussed, particularly with respect to the range of hydrogen pressures investigated, potential variability arising from sample preparation, and the constraints on generalizing the findings to other nickel-based alloys. These considerations should be considered when interpreting the results. Such efforts will be pivotal to establish predictive design principles and accelerate the deployment of Ni-based alloys in hydrogen-intensive and carbon-neutral technologies.
