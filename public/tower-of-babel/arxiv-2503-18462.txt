PALATE: Peculiar Application of the Law of Total Expectation to Enhance the Evaluation of Deep Generative Models
Tadeusz Dziarmaga *, Marcin Kądziołka, Laura Musioł, Patryk Marszałek, Artur Kasymov, Piotr Kościelniak, Marcin Mazur *, † \dagger

Abstract
Deep generative models (DGMs) have caused a paradigm shift in the field of machine learning, yielding noteworthy advancements in domains such as image synthesis, natural language processing, and other related areas. However, a comprehensive evaluation of these models that effectively captures the interplay among fidelity, diversity, and novelty in generated samples remains a persistent challenge. A recently proposed approach, the Feature Likelihood Divergence (FLD), offers a theoretically grounded and practical tool in this regard but faces notable computational limitations. In this paper, we propose PALATE, a novel framework designed to enhance the evaluation of DGMs by addressing the efficiency constraints of FLD. Our approach is based on a peculiar application of the law of total expectation to random variables representing accessible data. When integrated with the MMD baseline metric and the recently introduced DINOv3 feature extractor, PALATE provides a comprehensive evaluation framework that matches or exceeds state-of-the-art methods while offering superior computational efficiency and scalability to large-scale datasets. Through a series of experiments, we demonstrate that the PALATE enhancement constitutes a practical and computationally efficient framework for holistic DGM assessment, particularly in detecting sample memorization and evaluating generalization capabilities. The source code is provided as supplementary material.

Introduction

In recent years, deep generative models (DGMs) have garnered significant attention, drawing interest not only from the machine learning community but also from researchers in other scientific disciplines, practitioners, and the general public
[
32
]
. DGMs have matured to the point of enabling strong downstream results, including photorealistic imagery
[
33
]
, speech that emulates authentic discourse
[
10
]
, and text reminiscent of human writing
[
28
]
.

, an MMD-based holistic evaluation metric that captures fidelity, diversity, and memorization in a single score.

Regardless of architecture, DGMs are trained to generate synthetic data that closely resemble real data. Since learning objectives are model-specific, fair post-training assessment is typically architecture agnostic and therefore sample-based, comparing generated samples to real data. The most prominent examples are Inception Score (IS)
[
35
]
and Fréchet Inception Distance (FID)
[
14
]
, popular due to usability, reasonable agreement with human perceptual judgments, and sensitivity to diversity. In particular, FID often captures fidelity by measuring a statistical distance between feature distributions fitted to real and generated samples. Nevertheless, despite its de facto role as a gold standard, FID generally lacks the capacity to detect overfitting or sample memorization
[
18
]
.
More recent metrics such as Kernel Inception Distance (KID)
[
5
]
or Precision and Recall (P&R)
[
34
]
address other weaknesses of FID, such as bias, normality assumptions, or the inability to separate fidelity from diversity, but they still do not resolve memorization sensitivity. Complementary memorization metrics, including the
C_{T}
Score
[
26
]
and Authenticity
[
1
]
, target overfitting directly by quantifying training data reuse. While important, this separation of fidelity and diversity evaluation from memorization evaluation complicates model selection, especially in large-scale benchmarking where computational efficiency and a unified, actionable signal are critical.

A desirable evaluation metric should therefore be holistic, providing a single value that jointly validates generated samples in terms of fidelity, diversity, and novelty, where novelty is the counterpart of memorization. A recent and promising step is Feature Likelihood Divergence (FLD)
[
18
]
, designed to assess generalization while preserving benefits of feature-based evaluation. Nevertheless, FLD can become computationally challenging on complex, large-scale datasets, because reliable estimation may require substantial numbers of samples (see our ImageNet results in
Section

5
).
To address this issue, we propose a novel enhancement to the evaluation of DGMs, which we refer to as PALATE. Our solution is grounded in a
p
eculiar
a
pplication of the
la
w of
t
otal
e
xpectation (hence the name). The objective is to upgrade a baseline metric that already captures fidelity and diversity, making it sensitive to novelty and memorization while preserving the simplicity of sample-based evaluation (see
Figure

1
for an overview). Our experiments show that PALATE, when paired with an MMD-based approach, yields a single-value holistic evaluation metric that is aligned with state-of-the-art solutions while offering improved computational efficiency. To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first to incorporate the DINOv3 feature extractor
[
36
]
into DGM evaluation.

In summary, our work contributes the following:

1.

We propose PALATE, a novel general enhancement for evaluating DGMs that upgrades a baseline metric to ensure sensitivity to training sample memorization while preserving fidelity and diversity assessment.

2.

We provide a theoretical justification based on a peculiar application of the law of total expectation to random variables defined over a Euclidean data space.

3.

We conduct extensive experiments on real-world datasets, introducing DINOv3 embeddings to DGM evaluation and showing that our approach yields a computationally efficient single-value holistic metric that matches, and sometimes exceeds, state-of-the-art solutions.

Related Work

In this section, we review state-of-the-art evaluation metrics for DGMs that are relevant to our work.

Fidelity and Diversity Metrics

Inception Score (IS)
[
35
]
is a sample-based metric that uses a pre-trained Inception-v3 network
[
38
]
and primarily reflects diversity, with limited ability to assess fidelity. Metrics targeting both fidelity and diversity include Fréchet Inception Distance (FID)
[
14
]
, Kernel Inception Distance (KID)
[
5
]
, and CLIP-MMD (CMMD)
[
17
]
. FID compares fitted feature distributions of real and generated samples, and it is sensitive both to biases induced by the embedding space and to the Gaussian approximation. In contrast, KID is MMD-based, avoids the Gaussian assumption, and is typically less biased. As part of a broader evaluation view, FD and KD
[
37
]
replace Inception features with DINOv2 embeddings, which can better capture global structure and salient objects, improving perceptual alignment. CMMD is also MMD-based, but uses CLIP embeddings and a Gaussian RBF kernel, differing from KID’s typical Inception-v3 embeddings and kernel choices. Improved Precision and Recall
[
21
]
and Density and Coverage
[
27
]
further decompose quality and coverage, providing more diagnostic feedback than a single scalar score. Beyond these, the Vendi Score
[
9
]
offers a flexible, similarity-based notion of diversity that can expose mode collapse and duplication effects even when coverage appears high. Other recent proposals, such as FKEA
[
30
]
and RKE Score
[
16
]
, provide alternative, often kernel or information theoretic, perspectives on fidelity and diversity. However, these metrics are generally ineffective at detecting memorization and overfitting.

Memorization Metrics

Concerns regarding DGMs memorizing training data have motivated metrics designed specifically to detect training data reuse. The
C_{T}
Score
[
26
]
quantifies the probability that a generated sample is indistinguishable from a real sample, while Authenticity
[
1
]
applies a binary per-sample test and is commonly summarized as AuthPct, the percentage of generated samples classified as authentic. In this context, it is also useful to consider the Generalization Gap FLD
[
18
]
, obtained by subtracting FLD from its train-set counterpart, which serves as a memorization-oriented diagnostic. Novelty assessment has further benefited from metrics such as FINC
[
40
]
, while complementary metrics like the Rarity Score
[
13
]
and KEN Score
[
41
]
quantify uniqueness and informational entropy of generated outputs relative to the training set. While these metrics provide important insights into memorization and overfitting, they do not fully capture, in a single score, the holistic quality of generated samples that also includes fidelity and diversity, motivating enhancements that consider all these aspects jointly.

Holistic Metrics

Holistic evaluation aims to assess generalization by jointly considering fidelity, diversity, and novelty within a single scalar metric. Feature Likelihood Divergence (FLD)
[
18
]
, which to our knowledge is the only efficient metric of this kind, integrates fidelity, diversity, and memorization via density estimation in a feature space, typically DINOv2, with bandwidth selection designed to penalize memorization. While effective, FLD can become computationally demanding on complex, large-scale datasets due to the large sample sizes required for reliable estimation.

Preliminaries

This section reviews the generative-model setting considered in this paper and recalls the law of total expectation, a key tool behind our method.

Generative Models

We assume access to two disjoint collections of real data: a training set used for learning and a test set used exclusively for evaluation. Both can be viewed as independent draws from a random variable
X
supported on a multidimensional Euclidean space
\mathcal{X}
. Following
[
3
,
24
]
, we further assume that real data concentrate on a lower-dimensional manifold
\mathcal{M}_{\text{data}}\subset\mathcal{X}
, that is,
\mathbb{P}(X\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{data}})=1
.

Any deep generative model aims to produce synthetic samples. We model generated data as independent draws from a random variable
Y
on the same space
\mathcal{X}
, with distribution
p_{Y}
determined by the trained model. The real data distribution
p_{X}
is unknown but fixed, whereas
p_{Y}
evolves during training; learning aims to make
p_{Y}
approximate
p_{X}
, typically by optimizing a model-specific loss on training samples. We emphasize that even for well-optimized models, a substantial fraction of generated samples may still fall outside
\mathcal{M}_{\text{data}}
. Nevertheless, we assume that
\mathbb{P}(Y\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{data}})>0
, which ensures that, by repeating the generation process sufficiently many times, we obtain samples lying on the data manifold.

To compare trained models fairly, we require an architecture-agnostic evaluation metric that can be computed from samples, either characterizing
p_{Y}
alone (as IS) or measuring discrepancy between
p_{X}
and
p_{Y}
(as FID). Most modern metrics apply a feature extractor
\phi
, such as Inception-v3
[
38
]
, DINOv2
[
29
]
, or CLIP
[
31
]
, and evaluate the induced feature distributions
p_{\phi(X)}
and
p_{\phi(Y)}
in a perceptually meaningful representation space. The previous section reviewed the most relevant state-of-the-art evaluation metrics for our setting.

Law of Total Expectation

The law of total expectation
[
19
,
8
]
, also known as the tower rule, expresses the expectation of a random variable as a weighted average of conditional expectations over a partition of the sample space. We state this form below.

Theorem 1

(
[
19
,
8
]
)
.

Let
Z
be a random variable with finite expectation, and let
\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\}
be a partition of a sample space
\Omega
, that is,
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}=\Omega
and
A_{i}\cap A_{j}=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
, with
\mathbb{P}(A_{i})>0
for all
i
. Then

\mathbb{E}(Z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}(Z\mid A_{i})\mathbb{P}(A_{i}).

(1)

PALATE Enhancement

This section introduces PALATE, a novel enhancement for evaluating DGMs that builds on a peculiar application of the law of total expectation. Our goal is to endow a strong fidelity and diversity baseline with sensitivity to training-sample memorization, resulting in a single-score holistic evaluation metric
M_{\text{PALATE}}
.

PALATE

Let
{\rm x}_{\text{train}}=\{x^{\text{train}}_{1},\ldots,x^{\text{train}}_{m}\}
and
{\rm x}_{\text{test}}=\{x^{\text{test}}_{1},\ldots,x^{\text{test}}_{n}\}
be the training and test samples used for evaluation. We assume that these samples induce two disjoint, non-trivial regions on the data manifold
1
1

1

These train and test regions require no special structure (in particular, they need not be submanifolds or connected sets). They are induced solely by the samples selected for evaluation and are neither predetermined nor fixed subsets.

,

\mathcal{M}_{\text{train}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\text{test}}
, such that

\mathcal{M}_{\text{data}}=\mathcal{M}_{\text{train}}\cup\mathcal{M}_{\text{test}}
,

{\rm x}_{\text{train}}\subset\mathcal{M}_{\text{train}}
, and

{\rm x}_{\text{test}}\subset\mathcal{M}_{\text{test}}
.
We also assume that the ratio
m/n
is fixed across all evaluated models and datasets.

Next, we consider a baseline evaluation metric
M_{\text{base}}
that captures fidelity and diversity and that can be written as a conditional expectation
M_{\text{base}}=\mathbb{E}(Z\mid X\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{test}})

for some random variable
Z=Z(X,Y)
satisfying
\mathbb{E}(Z)=0
if and only if
p_{X}=p_{Y}
(see
Equation

7
for an example of such a construction).
Applying Theorem
1
to the partition

\Omega=\{\omega:X(\omega)\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{test}}\}\cup\{\omega:X(\omega)\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{train}}\}

and conditioning on
Y\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{data}}
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Z\mid Y\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{data}})

\displaystyle=a\,\mathbb{E}(Z\mid X\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{test}},\,Y\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{data}})

(2)

\displaystyle+(1-a)\,\mathbb{E}(Z\mid X\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{train}},\,Y\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{data}}),

where
a=\mathbb{P}(X\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{test}})=1-\mathbb{P}(X\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{train}})
represents the probability mass assigned to the test region. Equation (
2
) is key: it decomposes the conditional expectation over the data manifold into a test-region term (aligned with standard fidelity–diversity evaluation) and a train-region term that captures the extent to which “good” samples can be explained by proximity to training data.

Motivated by this decomposition, we define

\text{PALATE}=\frac{a\,\mathbb{E}(Z\mid X\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{test}},\,Y\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{data}})}{\mathbb{E}(Z\mid Y\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{data}})}.

(3)

This ratio approaches
1
for copycat models that resample training data and is minimized for models that generalize by producing novel samples consistent with the data distribution. Precisely, models with satisfactory generalization satisfy
\text{PALATE}\leq a
, in which case the contribution of generated samples to matching real data is largely explained by their proximity to the test set. In contrast, memorizing models satisfy
\text{PALATE}>a
, indicating that this contribution is less driven by test-data proximity than expected from the test-region mass alone.

Definition 1
.

A generative model is said to exhibit a tendency to memorization if

\!\!\mathbb{E}(Z\mid X\in\mathcal{M}_{\textnormal{train}},Y\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{data}})<\mathbb{E}(Z\mid X\in\mathcal{M}_{\textnormal{test}},Y\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{data}}).

(4)

For models satisfying Definition
1
, generated samples contribute more to matching the real data distribution through proximity to training data than to test data. Consequently, these are precisely the models for which the PALATE score exceeds
a=\mathbb{P}(X\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{test}})
, where we set
a=1/2
in our implementation. Specifically,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Z\mid X\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{train}},Y\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{data}}

\displaystyle)<\mathbb{E}(Z\mid X\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{test}},Y\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{data}})

(5)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\;\text{PALATE}>a.

PALATE Enhancement

Although
PALATE
explicitly measures memorization within
\mathcal{M}_{\text{data}}
, it may be ambiguous when comparing well-optimized and poorly optimized models, since the expectations in
Equation

3
are conditioned on
Y\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{data}}
. As a result, samples generated outside the data manifold do not affect
PALATE
. To retain sensitivity to such failures while still rewarding novelty, we define the final enhanced metric as a weighted combination of the baseline score (scaled to
[0,1]
) and
PALATE
:

M_{\text{PALATE}}=\alpha\,\text{SCALE}(M_{\text{base}})+(1-\alpha)\,\text{PALATE},

(6)

where
\alpha\in[0,1)
. This construction retains the fidelity–diversity sensitivity of
M_{\text{base}}
while adding memorization awareness through
PALATE
. Although not stated explicitly, this formulation involves hyperparameters and estimation procedures, which we discuss below.

Baseline Metric and Scaling Method

We adopt as our baseline metric the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)
[
12
]
with the characteristic Gaussian RBF kernel

k(x,y)=\exp(-\|x-y\|^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}))
.
This choice yields a distribution-free statistical distance in feature space:

\text{MMD}^{2}_{k}(p_{\phi(X)},p_{\phi(Y)})=\mathbb{E}(k(X_{1},X_{2})+k(Y_{1},Y_{2})-2k(X_{1},Y_{1})),

(7)

where
X_{1},X_{2}\sim p_{\phi(X)}
and
Y_{1},Y_{2}\sim p_{\phi(Y)}
are independent. This formulation admits the following simple scaling method:

\text{SCALE}(\text{MMD}^{2}_{k})=\frac{\text{MMD}^{2}_{k}(p_{\phi(X)},p_{\phi(Y)})}{\mathbb{E}(k(X_{1},X_{2})+k(Y_{1},Y_{2}))}\in[0,1].

(8)

The bandwidth
\sigma
is tuned to balance fidelity, diversity, and memorization sensitivity, following the median heuristic of
[
11
]
(see
C
).

We use DINOv3 as the feature extractor
\phi
and denote the resulting baseline as DINOv3-MMD (DMMD).
We avoid Inception-v3 (used in KID) due to the bias induced by its supervised ImageNet training, and CLIP (used in CMMD) due to its limited fine-grained separability of object attributes
[
4
]
. Sensitivity to subtle features such as color and shape is essential for detecting overfitting and memorization. Prior work has shown that DINOv2 features enable more comprehensive DGM evaluation and correlate better with human judgment than Inception-v3
[
37
]
. DINOv3 further improves upon DINOv2 by providing denser and more stable patch-level features through Gram anchoring and large-scale training, yielding improved perceptual alignment and dense-prediction transfer
[
36
]
.

Data Manifold and Train–Test Split

Since the data distribution
p_{X}
is unknown, we approximate the data manifold using available real data samples by fitting a kernel density estimator (KDE)
\hat{f}(u\mid\phi({\rm x}_{\text{train}}\cup{\rm x}_{\text{test}}))
in feature space.
We use a Gaussian kernel
K(u)=\mathcal{N}(u;0,\Sigma)
with a bandwidth matrix

\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{d}^{2})
, selected via Scott’s rule
2
2

2

Specifically,
\sigma_{i}=(m+n)^{-1/(d+4)}\hat{\sigma}_{i}
, where
d
is the feature dimension and
\hat{\sigma}_{i}
is the empirical standard deviation of the
i
th coordinate.
.
Under this representation, generated samples
{\rm y}=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}\}
are assigned to the data manifold if they attain sufficiently high KDE log-likelihood:

y_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{data}}\;\Leftrightarrow\;\log\hat{f}(y_{i}\mid\phi({\rm x}_{\text{train}}\cup{\rm x}_{\text{test}}))>\tau,

(9)

where the threshold
\tau
is selected via grid search (see
C
).

6
Conclusions

We introduced PALATE, a novel memorization-aware enhancement to the evaluation of deep generative models, grounded in a peculiar application of the law of total expectation. The proposed formulation enables sensitivity to sample memorization and overfitting, which are not explicitly captured by many existing evaluation metrics. By integrating PALATE with an MMD-based baseline and leveraging DINOv3 feature representations, we obtain a holistic single-score evaluation metric that jointly captures fidelity, diversity, and novelty of generated samples while remaining computationally efficient. Extensive experiments on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet demonstrate that our approach reduces computational overhead while achieving evaluation performance that is comparable to, and in several cases surpasses, state-of-the-art alternatives.

Limitations

At present, our evaluation is limited to deep generative models trained on image datasets, along with a single text-to-image overfitting scenario. Extending the proposed framework to additional data modalities, model families, and training regimes constitutes an important direction for future work.

Appendix A
Deriving the Test-Region Mass

Let
g\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be any nontrivial measurable function. Applying the law of total expectation to
g(X)
and the partition

\Omega=\{\omega:X(\omega)\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{test}}\}\cup\{\omega:X(\omega)\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{train}}\},

(10)

we obtain

\mathbb{E}(g(X))=a\,\mathbb{E}(g(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{test}})+(1-a)\,\mathbb{E}(g(X)\mid X\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{train}}).

(11)

Replacing expectations with empirical averages yields

\frac{1}{m+n}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}g(x_{i}^{\text{train}})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(x_{i}^{\text{test}})\Big)=\frac{1-a}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}g(x_{i}^{\text{train}})+\frac{a}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(x_{i}^{\text{test}})

(12)

Solving for
a
gives

a=\frac{n}{m+n},

(13)

which depends only on the relative sizes of the training and test datasets and is independent of the specific data values.

Appendix C
Implementation Details

Metrics Computation

We provide explicit formulas for
PALATE
and
M_{\text{PALATE}}

metrics based on real and generated samples

\begin{gathered}{\rm x}_{\text{train}}=\{x^{\text{train}}_{1},\ldots,x^{\text{train}}_{n}\},\\
{\rm x}_{\text{test}}=\{x^{\text{test}}_{1},\ldots,x^{\text{test}}_{n}\},\quad\text{and}\quad{\rm y}=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}.\end{gathered}

While
M_{\text{PALATE}}
is computed using all generated samples,

PALATE
score operates only on the subset

{\rm y}_{\text{data}}=\{y^{\text{data}}_{1},\ldots,y^{\text{data}}_{k}\}\subset{\rm y}

assigned to the data manifold via the KDE-based filter.

Let
\bar{k}_{A,B}
denote the
V
-statistic kernel average between feature
sets
\phi(A)
and
\phi(B)
. Define

\displaystyle D_{\text{test}}

\displaystyle=\bar{k}_{{\rm x}_{\text{test}},{\rm x}_{\text{test}}}+\bar{k}_{{\rm y}_{\text{data}},{\rm y}_{\text{data}}}-2\bar{k}_{{\rm x}_{\text{test}},{\rm y}_{\text{data}}},

(14)

\displaystyle D_{\text{train}}

\displaystyle=\bar{k}_{{\rm x}_{\text{train}},{\rm x}_{\text{train}}}+\bar{k}_{{\rm y}_{\text{data}},{\rm y}_{\text{data}}}-2\bar{k}_{{\rm x}_{\text{train}},{\rm y}_{\text{data}}}.

(15)

Then

\text{PALATE}=\frac{D_{\text{test}}}{D_{\text{test}}+D_{\text{train}}}.

(16)

Similarly, the scaled DMMD baseline is

\text{SCALE}(\text{DMMD})=\frac{\bar{k}_{{\rm x}_{\text{test}},{\rm x}_{\text{test}}}+\bar{k}_{{\rm y},{\rm y}}-2\bar{k}_{{\rm x}_{\text{test}},{\rm y}}}{\bar{k}_{{\rm x}_{\text{test}},{\rm x}_{\text{test}}}+\bar{k}_{{\rm y},{\rm y}}}.

(17)

The final holistic metric is

M_{\text{PALATE}}=\frac{1}{2}\,\text{SCALE}(\text{DMMD})+\frac{1}{2}\,\text{PALATE}.

(18)

Here,
\phi
denotes the DINOv3 feature extractor. For example,

\bar{k}_{{\rm x}_{\text{test}},{\rm x}_{\text{test}}}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|\phi(x_{i}^{\text{test}})-\phi(x_{j}^{\text{test}})\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right),

(19)

with analogous expressions for the remaining train, test, and generated
sample terms.

The kernel bandwidth
\sigma
is tuned to ensure sensitivity to memorization while preserving fidelity and diversity information in
M_{\text{PALATE}}
.
We follow the median heuristic of
[
11
]
.
Specifically, for each dataset we search over

\{\sqrt{\text{Med}}/k\mid k=1,2,3\}
,
where
Med
denotes the median of squared pairwise Euclidean distances between features of all real samples. In all experiments, we set
k=2
, i.e.,
\sigma=\sqrt{\text{Med}}/2
.

PALATE
term is chosen to be three times smaller than that used in
\text{SCALE}(\text{DMMD})
.
This conservative choice increases sensitivity to subtle differences between training and test samples, which are assumed to be drawn from the same underlying data distribution
p_{X}
.

Algorithmic Specifics

The DMMD implementation computes MMD between two sets of embeddings in a memory-efficient block-wise manner, using a block size of

The core routine is compiled with
@jax.jit
, enabling XLA optimization and parallel execution on GPUs and TPUs. Together, block-wise computation and JIT compilation make DMMD efficient and scalable to large evaluation datasets.

Feature Embeddings

We compute our metrics in the semantic feature space of DINOv3
[
36
]
, a recent self-supervised Vision Transformer (ViT-L/16). We use DINOv3 as the default backbone to evaluate PALATE with a contemporary high-capacity visual representation, while prior DINOv2-based evaluation work motivates this family of embeddings through their semantic alignment with human perception and cross-dataset robustness. DINOv3 further extends this line with larger-scale training, a high-capacity teacher, Gram-anchoring to prevent feature collapse, and improved dense visual features. To ensure that our conclusions are not driven only by this backbone choice, we repeat representative experiments with DINOv2 embeddings. Figures
2
and
3
show the same qualitative trends, suggesting that the reported behavior is primarily induced by the PALATE formulation rather than by a DINOv3-specific effect.

Stable Diffusion Fine-Tuning Details

We trained a LoRA adapter on top of Stable Diffusion v1.5 text-to-image diffusion model (
runwayml/stable-diffusion-v1-5
) using the Hugging Face Diffusers script
train_text_to_image_lora.py
. Training was performed on the COCO5K subset (image–caption pairs) at a resolution of
512\times 512
, with batch size 1 and gradient accumulation of 4. We optimized with AdamW using a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-4}
for
15{,}000
update steps. To reduce memory usage, we enabled mixed-precision FP16 training and gradient checkpointing, and saved checkpoints every
1{,}500
steps. All experiments were executed on a SLURM-managed GPU node with a fixed random seed (42) for reproducibility.
