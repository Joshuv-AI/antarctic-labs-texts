Postoperative Risks in Thoracolumbar Fractures Patients with Mental Disorders: A Retrospective Cohort Study Using a Nationwide Inpatient Database
Authors: Kenta Takakura, Akira Honda, Yusuke Sasabuchi, Eiji Takasawa, Tokue Mieda, Toshiki Tsukui, Tomoki Nakajima, Kazuhiro Inomata, Yuya Kimura, Kiyohide Fushimi, Hideo Yasunaga, Hirotaka Chikuda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background Mental disorders, such as schizophrenia and mood disorders, are associated with spine injuries. However, the relationship between mental disorders and short-term outcomes after surgery for spine injury remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the association between mental disorders and postoperative outcomes in surgical patients with thoracolumbar fractures. Methods This retrospective cohort study was performed using a national inpatient database in Japan. Patients who were emergently admitted and underwent spinal fixation surgery for thoracolumbar fractures were categorized into schizophrenia, mood disorder, and control groups. Postoperative outcomes included in-hospital mortality, postoperative complications, and 30-day readmission. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to adjust for patient backgrounds and injury severity. Results Among 23,148 patients, 1,243 patients had schizophrenia, 1,192 had mood disorder, and the remaining 20,713 patients were classified into the control group. In-hospital mortality was 1.0%, 0.6%, and 1.3%, postoperative complications occurred in 20.7%, 19.2%, and 18.0%, and readmission within 30 days was observed in 2.3%, 3.1%, and 3.2% of patients in the schizophrenia, mood disorder, and control groups, respectively. Compared to the control group, postoperative complications were more likely to occur in the schizophrenia group (odds ratio [OR], 1.77; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.52-2.06) and the mood disorders group (OR, 1.50; 95% CI, 1.28-1.74). No significant differences were observed in in-hospital mortality or 30-day readmission. Conclusions Mental disorders were associated with postoperative complications in patients with spinal injuries. Careful perioperative management may be necessary for this population.

Introduction

Mental disorders such as schizophrenia and mood disorder are commonly observed in patients with spine injuries. The general prevalence of schizophrenia and mood disorder was reported to be approximately 1% and 4.7%-7.3%, respectively1-3). However, a previous study showed that mental disorders were present in nearly 70% of patients involving high-energy spinal trauma4). Furthermore, among osteoporotic vertebral fractures in patients aged 65 years or older, the prevalence of schizophrenia and depression was reported as 1.9% and 3.4%, respectively5). Spine injuries often require surgical intervention, and evaluating the impact of mental disorders on perioperative risks is clinically important.

Several studies have reported an association between mental disorders and postoperative adverse outcomes6-10). In a single-center study from the United States, patients with a history of depression or anxiety undergoing surgery for degenerative spine diseases had higher rates of emergency department visits, rehospitalization within 90 days of surgery, and postoperative complications8). In a study using the National Hospital Discharge Survey database, patients with mental disorders had a higher frequency of postoperative complications. In particular, patients with schizophrenia had a higher incidence of pulmonary embolism. There was no difference in in-hospital mortality between patients with and without schizophrenia. However, the odds ratio of in-hospital mortality was lower in patients with depression compared to those without depression9).

Although these previous studies focused on degenerative spine diseases and excluded patients with spine injuries, the impact of mental disorders in the context of emergency trauma, which allows for less preoperative optimization, remains poorly understood. Though it has been reported that all types of trauma patients with mental disorders had higher complication rates and longer hospital stays11), the relationship between mental disorders and short-term outcomes after surgery for spine injury remains unclear. Due to the urgency of surgery for traumatic thoracolumbar vertebral fractures, the opportunity to thoroughly assess and stabilize the patient's mental state preoperatively was often lost. Therefore, it is necessary to understand the impact of pre-existing mental disorders on the short-term outcome after emergency spinal fixation surgery. The purpose of this study was to investigate the association between mental disorders and risks of postoperative mortality, complications, and postoperative readmission in patients with thoracolumbar fractures, using a national inpatient database in Japan.

Materials and Methods

This retrospective study was performed using the Diagnosis Procedure Combination database in Japan. This database includes administrative claims and discharge abstract data, and covers over 50% of acute care admissions and approximately 90% of tertiary emergency hospitals in Japan. The database includes the following information: patient age and sex; discharge status; primary diagnosis on admission, comorbidities at admission, complications after admission recoded with the International Classification of Diseases 10th revision (ICD-10) codes and text in Japanese; and surgical and anesthetic procedures recoded with original codes in Japan. Details of this database have been provided previously12). A validation study showed good sensitivity and specificity for diagnoses and procedure records13). The Institutional Review Board of The University of Tokyo approved the present study (approval No. 3501-5; approval date: May 19, 2021). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects in Japan. Since the data were anonymized, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Patient selection

Patients with thoracic vertebral fracture (ICD-10: S22.0) and lumbar vertebral fracture (ICD-10: S32.0) who underwent spinal fixation between July 2010 and March 2022 were included. To focus on the acute management of spinal fracture, the study population was limited to those with emergency admissions. We excluded patients who: (1) were younger than 18 years, (2) had cervical vertebral fractures, (3) had metastatic spinal tumors or pathological fractures, (4) had spondylitis or discitis, or (5) had any mental disorders except schizophrenia and mood disorders. We focused on schizophrenia and mood disorders, which have been the primary subjects in previous studies investigating the impact of mental disorders on surgical outcomes. Patients with other mental disorders (such as dementia and substance-related disorders) were excluded because their individual sample sizes were insufficient for statistical comparisons. Furthermore, there was a concern that grouping these diverse and heterogeneous disorders into a single category would blur the focus of the study. By excluding these disorders, we aimed to maintain a clearly defined control group consisting of patients without mental disorders.

Eligible patients were divided into three groups based on their diagnoses: the schizophrenia group (ICD-10: F20-29), the mood disorder group, including depression and bipolar disorders (ICD-10: F30-39), and the control group, consisting of patients without any mental disorders. Patients with both schizophrenia and mood disorders were assigned to the schizophrenia group.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were all-cause in-hospital mortality, overall postoperative complications, and readmission within 30 days. The postoperative complications included acute coronary syndrome, cerebrovascular disease, deep venous thrombosis, delirium, heart failure, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, renal failure, respiratory disorders, sepsis, surgical site infection, and urinary tract infection. Secondary outcomes included individual components of postoperative complications. Diagnoses of complications were determined using the ICD-10 codes listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Other variables

Patient characteristics included age, sex, body mass index, smoking history, Charlson comorbidity index14), fracture location, type of fracture, trauma severity, surgical procedure, and timing of surgery. Each component of Charlson comorbidity index was listed in Supplementary Table 2. Body mass index was categorized into five groups: less than 18.5 kg/m2, 18.5-24.9 kg/m2, 25.0-29.9 kg/m2, over 30 kg/m2, and missing. The Charlson comorbidity index was categorized into four groups: 0, 1, 2, and 3 or more. Types of fracture were classified into compression fracture, burst fracture, and dislocation fracture, using diagnoses recorded with text in Japanese. The trauma severity was assessed using a validated ICD-10–based trauma severity score, which has achieved a high mortality predictive accuracy15). This study included patients with polytrauma, and to account for the potential impact of associated injuries on outcomes, the multivariate analysis adjusted for injury severity using the trauma severity score. The surgical procedures included anterior, posterior, both anterior and posterior, and posterior interbody fusion. The timing of surgery was defined as days from the admission to the first spinal surgery.

Statistical analysis

Patient backgrounds were compared between the control group and both schizophrenia and the mood disorder groups using standardized mean differences. In addition, to compare baseline characteristics among the three groups, we performed the chi-squared test for categorical variables and the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables. For the analysis of outcomes, unadjusted comparisons used the chi-squared test for binary outcomes to compare the three groups. Subsequently, multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed on the primary outcomes, linking to generalized estimating equations to adjust for within-hospital clustering. Explanatory variables included all potential confounders (age, sex, body mass index, Charlson comorbidity index, fracture type, surgical procedures, trauma severity score, and the timing of surgery) to reflect patient background and injury severity.

Furthermore, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using a matched-pair analysis to further adjust for differences in baseline characteristics among the groups. For each patient in the schizophrenia and mood disorder groups, we identified four control patients after adjusting for the covariates. Control patients were randomly selected from the pooled population of the control group. Following the generation of this matched-pair cohort, we performed multivariate logistic regression analysis that considered within-pair clustering to compare outcomes among the three groups.

All hypothesis tests were two-sided with a significance level set at 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata/MPⓇ version 18.0 software (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

After inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied, 23,148 patients who underwent spinal fixation for thoracolumbar fractures were included in the analysis. This cohort included 1,243 patients (5.4%) in the schizophrenia group, 1,192 (5.2%) in the mood disorder group, and 20,713 in the control group (Figure 1).

Baseline characteristics of the patients

Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of the patients. The control group was older than the schizophrenia and mood disorder groups. In the control group, 54.8% of the patients were male, representing a slightly higher proportion than females. In contrast, the proportions of males were lower in the schizophrenia and mood disorder groups (38.5% and 32.6%, respectively), with a higher proportion of females observed in these groups. Comorbidities were more prevalent in the control group. The control group had a higher prevalence of compression fractures, while the schizophrenia and mood disorder groups had a higher prevalence of burst fractures in the lumbar region. Trauma severity scores were similar across all groups.

Unadjusted outcomes

Table 2 shows the unadjusted outcomes. The in-hospital mortality was significantly different among the three groups, with the lowest mortality observed in the mood disorder group (control group, 1.3%; schizophrenia group, 1.0%; and mood disorder group, 0.6%; p=0.047). Overall complications were also significantly different among the three groups, with the lowest proportion in the control group (control group, 18.0%; schizophrenia group, 20.7%; and mood disorder group, 19.2%; p=0.036). No significant differences were observed in readmissions within 30 days across the groups. Among the secondary outcomes, surgical site infection, pulmonary embolism, and deep vein thrombosis were the highest in the schizophrenia group, with significant differences observed among the three groups.

Multivariable regression

Table 3 shows the results of multivariable regression analyses. There was no significant difference in mortality in both schizophrenia (odds ratio [OR], 1.77, 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.98-3.20; p=0.059) and the mood disorder (OR, 0.85; 95% Cl, 0.40-1.80; p=0.664) groups. The proportions of postoperative overall complications were higher in both schizophrenia (OR, 1.77; 95% Cl, 1.52-2.06; p<0.001) and mood disorder (OR, 1.50; 95% Cl, 1.28-1.74; p<0.001) groups compared to those in the control group. No significant differences were observed in 30-day readmission.

Sensitivity analysis

In the 1:1:4 matched-pair sensitivity analysis, the control group comprised 3,124 patients, the schizophrenia group had 1,030 patients, and the mood disorder group had 1,031 patients. The baseline characteristics of this matched cohort are shown in Supplementary Table 3.

Table 4 shows the results of multivariate regression analysis on the primary outcome after matching. Similar to the before matching results, the overall complications were significantly higher in both schizophrenia (OR, 2.08; 95% CI, 1.72-2.53; p<0.001) and mood disorder (OR, 1.71; 95% CI, 1.40-2.08; p<0.001) groups. There were no significant differences in mortality or readmission, which was also consistent with the before matching results.

Discussion

This study compared in-hospital mortality, overall postoperative complications, and readmission within 30 days after spinal surgery for thoracic and lumbar vertebral fractures between patients with and without mental disorders, using a national inpatient database in Japan. Mental disorders were not associated with in-hospital mortality and readmission within 30 days; however, they were associated with overall postoperative complications. These associations remained consistent in matched-pair sensitivity analysis that addressed potential confounding factors, such as baseline demographic differences.

Mental disorders have been reported to be a risk factor for several chronic diseases, such as diabetes and cardiac disease16-18). However, comorbidities were more frequent in the control group in this study. These comorbidities typically increase with age, which may explain the reason for the higher prevalence of comorbidities in the control group. Despite this, postoperative complications were higher in the mental disorder group after adjustment for patient background and trauma severity. This result was consistent with those reported in previous studies on spinal degenerative diseases, suggesting that mental disorders are also associated with postoperative complications in patients with spine injuries. Patients with serious mental illnesses may not receive optimal care due to communication problems, especially in the treatment of chronic diseases19). Though this suggests that communication issues could be one of several contributing factors to postoperative complications, further research is needed to establish a causal relationship.

This study showed that schizophrenia was especially associated with pulmonary embolism and deep venous thrombosis. This finding is consistent with a previous study comparing emergency hospitalizations for pulmonary embolism or acute myocardial infarction, which reported that schizophrenia was more prevalent in patients with pulmonary embolism20). Furthermore, a study focusing on degenerative spinal diseases have also reported a higher incidence of pulmonary embolism in patients with schizophrenia9). This finding supports the hypothesis that schizophrenia may be a potential risk factor for pulmonary embolism and deep venous thrombosis, regardless of whether the reason for hospitalization is acute or chronic. One potential mechanism is the reported relationship between the use of antipsychotics and thrombosis21,22). Although these findings suggest that that schizophrenia should be considered as a potential risk factor for pulmonary embolism, it remains unclear whether it acts as a direct risk factor or serves as a surrogate marker reflecting treatment-related or other underlying factors. Further investigation is warranted to elucidate the precise mechanisms and to optimize perioperative management.

Despite the increase in postoperative complications, in-hospital mortality and postoperative readmission were not associated with schizophrenia and mood disorder. This result is similar to those from previous studies investigating degenerative spine disease and meta-analysis at various surgical specialties6,23,24). Although in-hospital death and readmission are relatively rare events, these results suggest that providing appropriate care for postoperative complications may avoid life-threatening complications, regardless of the presence of mental disorders.

This study had several limitations. First, data on the cause of injury were not available. Spine injury occurs from various causes, including falls, tumbling, and traffic accidents. Therefore, the differences in complications and mortality might be confounded by the cause of injury. Second, this database lacks imaging findings, neurological status, and results of laboratory tests. Therefore, specific surgical outcomes requiring radiological or clinical assessment, such as screw malposition or postoperative hematoma, could not be evaluated. Additionally, it was unclear whether mental disorders were adequately treated prior to injury. Third, although we adjusted for overall trauma severity, specific information regarding the anatomical location of associated injuries (for example, head injuries or limb fractures) was not considered. Such specific injury patterns may influence perioperative outcomes. Finally, the database lacked information on outcomes that occurred at hospitals other than the one where the surgery was performed. Therefore, readmission after discharge might be underestimated.

Conclusions

Mental disorders such as schizophrenia and mood disorders were associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications in patients undergoing spinal fixation surgery for thoracolumbar vertebral fractures. However, no association was observed between these mental disorders and all-cause in-hospital mortality and readmission in this patient population.
