The Stochastic TR-BDF2 Scheme of Order 2
Tomás Caraballo, Macarena Gómez-Mármol, Ignacio Roldán

Abstract
Our main objective in this paper is to develop a second-order stochastic numerical method which generalizes the well-known deterministic TR-BDF2 scheme. Since most stochastic techniques used for approximating the solution of a stochastic differential equation may have lower order compared to the deterministic case, we have elaborated a scheme which not only preserves the second-order accuracy of the original scheme in the stochastic framework, but also its A A -stability. Once we obtain the scheme and prove its second-order accuracy and A A -stability, which is not a trivial task, we also state a result concerning its M ​ S MS -stability. This concept is also analyzed for different parameter ranges in our scheme and the Itô–Taylor approximation of order 2, revealing scenarios where, for certain time step sizes, the developed method is M ​ S MS -stable while the Itô–Taylor one is not. This concept is really useful to tackle slow-fast problems such as stiff ones, which we aim to explore further in future work. Finally, we validate the theoretical results with some academic test cases.

60H10, 65C20, 65P99.

TR-BDF2, Stochastic Differential Equations, Numerical Analysis.

Introduction

Nowadays, the concern of researchers to simulate more and more realistic problems has led the scientific community to become interested in problems with a significant randomness component. In this line, one of the ways to take this randomness into account is to consider stochastic problems. In our case, we are interested in ordinary differential systems where white noise has been added, since it represents many real situations such as population dynamics, neuroscience behavior, chemical reactions, etc.

These problems have started to be studied from the point of view of Mathematical Analysis some years ago and we can find a wide bibliography related to these studies

The numerical methods developed are mainly based on reproducing in some way the well-known ones for the deterministic case. Thus, for example, based on the Itô analysis and the Itô–Taylor expansion, deterministic methods based on Taylor’s development are generalized

Other generalizations have been developed, such as the corresponding

Within the field of differential systems we are interested in studying slow-fast problems, meaning that the speeds at which the different variables move are not comparable in magnitude. This type of systems is included in what are known as stiff problems. Over the recent years, more elaborate schemes have been developed in an efficient way to tackle this kind of problems and address the challenges. An example of that is the so-called splitting techniques

To solve these problems properly, it is necessary to choose the time step for reasons of stability and not for reasons of accuracy. Therefore, it is important to have methods that have good numerical properties. It may happen that these properties can be achieved but with an extremely small time step which makes the method unfeasible. In this paper, we focus on the development of a scheme and the study of its stability properties in the scalar case, which provides a natural framework in which key ideas can be explored and validated. In

Our aim in this paper is to generalize the deterministic TR-BDF2 method to the stochastic framework, so that it maintains all the good properties and in particular the order 2 of convergence.

The TR-BDF2 in deterministic version has been studied for example in

1
Problem Formulation

In this first section, we present the continuous problem for which we are interested in developing numerical methods that allow us to approximate its solution. We will also define the concept of numerical method of convergence order
p
for the stochastic case.

Let
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}}}\right\}_{t\geq 0},P}}\right)
be a complete probability space with the natural filtration
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}}}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
. Consider a
d
-dimensional stochastic differential equation (SDE)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{\rm d}X_{t}=a\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,X_{t}}}\right){\rm d}t+\sum_{j=1}^{m}b^{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,X_{t}}}\right){\rm d}W_{t}^{j},&\displaystyle t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,T}}\right],\\
\displaystyle X_{0}\text{ is given},&\end{array}}}\right.

(1)

where
T
is a positive constant,
a:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,T}}\right]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
b^{j}:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,T}}\right]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
j=1,\ldots,m
and
X_{0}
is the initial data. The processes
W_{t}^{j}
are independent scalar Wiener processes adapted to
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}}}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
, and the initial data
X_{0}
is independent of them, satisfying
E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X_{0}}}\right\|^{2}}}\right]<\infty
, where
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\cdot}}\right\|
denotes the Euclidean norm.

We now present a result establishing the existence and uniqueness of the solution to the above problem
[
16
]
:

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that

(A.1)

a
and
b^{j}
, for all
j=1,\ldots,m
, are jointly measurable in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,x}}\right)\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,T}}\right]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
(measurability);

(A.2)

There exists a constant
K>0
such that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|a\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,x}}\right)-a\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,y}}\right)}}\right\|^{2}\vee\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|b^{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,x}}\right)-b^{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,y}}\right)}}\right\|^{2}\leq K\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x-y}}\right\|^{2},

for all
j=1,\ldots,m
, for all
t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,T}}\right]
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(Lipschitz condition);

(A.3)

There exists a constant
K>0
such that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|a\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,x}}\right)}}\right\|^{2}\vee\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|b^{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,x}}\right)}}\right\|^{2}\leq K\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|^{2}}}\right),

for all
j=1,\ldots,m
, for all
t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,T}}\right]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(linear growth bound).

Then, the stochastic differential equation (
1
) has a path-wise unique strong solution
X_{t}
on
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,T}}\right]
with
E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X_{t}}}\right\|^{2}}}\right]<\infty
.

In the above theorem, the symbol
\vee
represents the maximum between two quantities, which implies that the inequality is satisfied by both the drift and the diffusion terms. In the following,
{\rm C}^{p,q}
denotes the class of functions that are
p
times continuously differentiable with respect to the first variable and
q
times continuously differentiable with respect to the second variable.

Remark 1.2
.

The assumption of global Lipschitz continuity can be weakened to local Lipschitz continuity, and the linear growth condition can be replaced by the inequality

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle a\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,x}}\right),x}}\right\rangle\vee\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|b^{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,x}}\right)}}\right\|^{2}\leq K\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|^{2}}}\right),

for some constant
K>0
, for all
j=1,\ldots,m
, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,T}}\right]
(see
[
21
]
).

In particular, if the drift and each diffusion coefficient are continuously differentiable with bounded derivatives, i.e., they belong to the set that we denote by
{\rm C}^{1,1}
, then the measurability condition and local Lipschitz continuity are satisfied, which guarantees existence and uniqueness of the solution. We shall work under this latter framework, as will be shown later.

Let us now look at the concept of order of convergence in the stochastic case. We shall denote by
L^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right)
to the space whose processes satisfy that
E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\cdot}}\right\|^{2}}}\right]<\infty
.

Definition 1.3
.

We consider a partition
\Delta_{N}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t_{0}=0<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{N}=T}}\right\}
of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,T}}\right]
and let
Y_{n}
an approximation of the solution of problem (
1
) on the point
t_{n}
, for all
n=0,\ldots,N
. Then, we say that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{Y_{n}}}\right\}_{0\leq n\leq N}
converges (strongly) to
X_{t}
, the solution of that SDE, with order
p>0
if:

\sup_{0\leq n\leq N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X_{t_{n}}-Y_{n}}}\right\|_{L^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right)}\coloneqq\sup_{0\leq n\leq N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X_{t_{n}}-Y_{n}}}\right\|^{2}}}\right]}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h^{p}}}\right),

where
h
is the diameter of the partition.

Remark 1.4
.

The concept of strong convergence in this framework is used whenever we are trying to approximate the solution of the differential system. If we are interested in calculating the approximation of moments, then we would have to use the concept of weak convergence. This convergence is studied in depth in references
[
24
,
26
]
.

2
The Itô–Taylor analysis

In this section we introduce a generalization of the Taylor expansion in the stochastic context and some numerical schemes obtained from such expansion.

2.1
The Itô–Taylor expansion

First, we shall consider an Itô process
X_{t}
that satisfies the
d
-dimensional SDE defined in (
1
). Henceforth, we take the same notation that is used in
[
16
]
: Let
\alpha=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j_{1},\ldots,j_{l}}}\right)
be a multi-index, we define
\mathcal{M}
as the set of all multi-indices:

\mathcal{M}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\alpha=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j_{1},\ldots,j_{l}}}\right):j_{i}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1,\ldots,m}}\right\},i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,\ldots,l}}\right\},\text{ for }l=1,2,\ldots}}\right\}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{v}}\right\},

where
l\coloneqq l\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha}}\right)
represent the length of a multi-index
\alpha
and
v
is the multi-index of length zero. Also, let
n\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha}}\right)
be the number of components of a multi-index
\alpha
which value is equal to 0,
{}^{-}\alpha
the multi-index outcome of removing the initial element of
\alpha
and
\alpha^{-}
the multi-index outcome of removing the last element of
\alpha
.

Taking
a^{k}
and
b^{k,j}
as the component
k
of
a
and
b^{j}
respectively with
k=1,\ldots,d
, we define the following operators:

L^{0}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}a^{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,l=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{m}b^{k,j}b^{l,j}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{k}\partial x_{l}},

L^{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}b^{k,j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}},j=1,\ldots,m.

Then, we denote
{}_{\alpha}f
as the coefficient functions of
f:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,T}}\right]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
{}_{v}f\equiv f
and
{}_{\alpha}f
is the result of applying recursively the operators to
f
as:

{}_{\alpha}f=L^{j_{1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({}_{{}^{-}\alpha}f}}\right).

On the other hand, we define the multiple Itô integral recursively as

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle I_{v}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cdot,X_{\cdot}}}\right)}}\right]_{s,t}\equiv f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,X_{t}}}\right),{\rm d}W_{t}^{0}={\rm d}t,\\
\displaystyle I_{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cdot,X_{\cdot}}}\right)}}\right]_{s,t}=\int_{s}^{t}I_{\alpha^{-}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cdot,X_{\cdot}}}\right)}}\right]_{s,r}{\rm d}W_{r}^{j_{l}},\end{array}}}\right.

and we denote by
I_{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cdot,X_{\cdot}}}\right)}}\right]_{s,t}=I_{\alpha,s,t}
if
f\equiv 1
.

We say that the above integral exists if the adapted process
f=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right),t\geq 0}}\right\}
is right-continuous, possesses left hand limits (càdlàg) and belongs to
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}
, where these sets are defined below:

•

f\in\mathcal{H}_{v}
if for each
t\geq 0
,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}}\right\|<\infty
with probability 1 (w.p.1).

•

f\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)}
if for each
t\geq 0
,
\int_{0}^{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)}}\right\|{\rm d}s<\infty
(w.p.1).

•

f\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)}
if for each
t\geq 0
,
\int_{0}^{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s}}\right)}}\right\|^{2}{\rm d}s<\infty
(w.p.1).

In addition, we write
\mathcal{H}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j}}\right)}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)}
for each
j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{2,\ldots,m}}\right\}
if
m\geq 2
.

•

f\in\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}
, with
l\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha}}\right)\geq 2
, if for each
t\geq 0
,
I_{\alpha^{-}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cdot}}\right)}}\right]_{0,t}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j_{l}}}\right)}
(w.p.1).

Now, we introduce the concept of hierarchical set and remainder set, which are the sets where the Itô–Taylor expansion is well-defined.

Definition 2.1
.

\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{M}
is said to be a hierarchical set if:

•

\mathcal{A}\neq\emptyset
.

•

\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}l\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha}}\right)<\infty
.

•

{}^{-}\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{v}}\right\}
.

Definition 2.2
.

We define
\mathcal{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{A}}}\right)
, the remainder set of
\mathcal{A}
, as the following set:

\mathcal{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{A}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\alpha\in\mathcal{M}\setminus\mathcal{A}:{}^{-}\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}}\right\}.

Once these definitions have been introduced, we can define the Itô–Taylor expansion.

Definition 2.3
.

The Itô–Taylor expansion of a function
f:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,T}}\right]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
from
s
to
t
,
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
, in the hierarchical set
\mathcal{A}
, provided all derivatives of
f
,
a
and
b^{j}
and all of the multiple Itô integrals exist, is defined as

f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,X_{t}}}\right)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}{}_{\alpha}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,X_{s}}}\right)I_{\alpha,s,t}+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{A}}}\right)}I_{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{}_{\alpha}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cdot,X_{\cdot}}}\right)}}\right]_{s,t}.

In particular, if we take
p
a number which can be written like
p=0.5k
, with
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0}}\right\}
, we define the set

\mathcal{A}_{p}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\alpha\in\mathcal{M}:l\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha}}\right)+n\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha}}\right)\leq 2p\text{ or }l\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha}}\right)=n\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha}}\right)=p+\frac{1}{2}}}\right\}.

Then
\mathcal{A}_{p}
is a hierarchical set, and the Itô–Taylor expansion in this case is known as Itô–Taylor expansion of order
p
and its expression is

f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,X_{t}}}\right)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{p}}{}_{\alpha}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,X_{s}}}\right)I_{\alpha,s,t}+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{A}_{p}}}\right)}I_{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{}_{\alpha}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cdot,X_{\cdot}}}\right)}}\right]_{s,t}.

(2)

Consequently, when
s=t_{n}
and
t=t_{n+1}
, we have:

X_{t_{n+1}}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{p}}{}_{\alpha}F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{n},X_{t_{n}}}}\right)I_{\alpha,n}+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{A}_{p}}}\right)}I_{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{}_{\alpha}F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cdot,X_{\cdot}}}\right)}}\right]_{t_{n},t_{n+1}},

where
{}_{\alpha}F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,x}}\right)
are the coefficient functions of
F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,x}}\right)\equiv x
and we have denoted
I_{\alpha,n}=I_{\alpha,t_{n},t_{n+1}}
. From now on, we denote the remainder of the Itô–Taylor expansion
\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{A}_{p}}}\right)}I_{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{}_{\alpha}f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cdot,X_{\cdot}}}\right)}}\right]_{s,t}
by
\tilde{R}_{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f}}\right]_{s,t}
, and the stochastic terms of the Itô–Taylor expansion of solution
X_{t}
of order
p
from
s
to
t
, i.e.,
\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{p}:l\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha}}\right)\neq n\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha}}\right)}{}_{\alpha}F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(s,X_{s}}}\right)I_{\alpha,s,t}
, by
S_{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{s}}}\right]_{s,t}
.

Finally, in order to ensure the approximation to be of order
p
, we state the standard hypotheses required for the coefficient functions
{}_{\alpha}F
, which can be related with those of Theorem
1.1
of existence and uniqueness.

Hypothesis 2.4
.

Assume that
{}_{\alpha}F
satisfies that:

(H.1)

{}_{{}^{-}\alpha}F\in{\rm C}^{1,2}
and
{}_{\alpha}F\in\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}
, for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{p}\cup\mathcal{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{A}_{p}}}\right)
(measurability condition).

(H.2)

There exists a constant
C_{1}>0
such that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{}_{\alpha}F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,x}}\right)-{}_{\alpha}F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,y}}\right)}}\right\|^{2}\leq C_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x-y}}\right\|^{2}
, for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{p}
,

and

(Lipschitz condition with respect to the second variable).

(H.3)

There exists a constant
C_{2}>0
such that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{}_{\alpha}F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,x}}\right)}}\right\|^{2}\leq C_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|^{2}}}\right)
, for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{p}\cup\mathcal{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{A}_{p}}}\right)
,

and

(lineal growth bound condition).

As discussed in Remark
1.2
, where

regularity was shown to imply local Lipschitz continuity in the relaxation of the existence and uniqueness result of Theorem

, Hypothesis (H.2) can likewise be relaxed for the drift when the structure of the scheme allows it. The following condition will be used later to establish convergence results under weaker assumptions.

Hypothesis 2.5
.

Suppose that

The functions
{}_{{}^{-}\alpha}F\in{\rm C}^{1,2}
,
{}_{\alpha}F\in\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}
and they satisfy global linear growth bounds, for
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{p}\cup\mathcal{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{A}_{p}}}\right)
. Also, the functions
{}_{\alpha}F
, for
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{p}
, with
\alpha\neq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)
, are globally Lipschitz.

There exists
C\geq 0
such that for all

and

,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle a\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,x}}\right)-a\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,y}}\right),x-y}}\right\rangle\leq C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x-y}}\right\|^{2},

(3)

and there exists
K>0
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,T}}\right]
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle a\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,x}}\right),x}}\right\rangle\leq K\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|^{2}}}\right),

(4)

Similar to the construction of numerical methods for deterministic differential equations based on the Taylor development, we can construct methods in the stochastic case by taking the Itô–Taylor expansion as we will see in the next subsection.

2.2
Numerical method based on Itô–Taylor expansion

Taking the Itô–Taylor expansion of order
p
and disregarding the summation corresponding to the remainder set, we obtain a whole family of numerical methods that can be written as

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\text{Given }Y_{0},\text{ compute }\forall n=0,\ldots,N-1,\\
\displaystyle Y_{n+1}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{p}}{}_{\alpha}F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{n},Y_{n}}}\right)I_{\alpha,n}.\end{array}}}\right.

In the particular case
p=0.5
, we have
\mathcal{A}_{0.5}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{v,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right),\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right),\ldots,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m}}\right)}}\right\}
and the scheme is written as:

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\text{Given }Y_{0},\text{ compute }\forall n=0,\ldots,N-1,\\
\displaystyle Y_{n+1}=Y_{n}+a\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{n},Y_{n}}}\right)h_{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}b^{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{n},Y_{n}}}\right)I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j}}\right),n},\end{array}}}\right.

where
h_{n}=t_{n+1}-t_{n}
. This method is known as Euler-Maruyama scheme, which has been studied by different authors. In
[
10
]
, we can also find the proof that this is a method of order
p=0.5
.

For the case
p=1
, the hierarchical set is
\mathcal{A}_{1}=\mathcal{A}_{0.5}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,1}}\right),\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,2}}\right),\ldots,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m,m}}\right)}}\right\}
and the scheme is:

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\text{Given }Y_{0},\text{ compute }\forall n=0,\ldots,N-1,\\
\displaystyle Y_{n+1}=Y_{n}+a\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{n},Y_{n}}}\right)h_{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}b^{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{n},Y_{n}}}\right)I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j}}\right),n}+\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}=1}^{m}L^{j_{1}}b^{j_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{n},Y_{n}}}\right)I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j_{1},j_{2}}}\right),n}.\end{array}}}\right.

This is the Milstein scheme and a proof of order of convergence
p=1
can be found in
[
16
]
. We have presented here the two best known and most common methods in the numerical solution of ordinary differential equations. In the same way that we have deduced these methods, other methods can be obtained by considering other values of
p
.

3
The stochastic TR-BDF2 method

Before developing the stochastic TR-BDF2 method, let us recall the deterministic version of the method as well as its main numerical properties.

Let us consider a
d
-dimensional differential equation

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle x^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=a\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}}\right),&\displaystyle t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,T}}\right],\\
\displaystyle x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)=x_{0},&\end{array}}}\right.

where
T
is a positive constant,
a:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,T}}\right]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
x_{0}
is the initial data. Our objective is to solve this problem from a numerical point of view, i.e., we want to construct one sequence
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y_{n}}}\right\}\simeq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{n}}}\right)}}\right\}
,
n=0,\ldots,N
, for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

For simplicity, we consider a uniform partition of the interval
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,T}}\right]
with step
h=\frac{T}{N}
:

\Delta_{N}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t_{0}=0<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{N}=T}}\right\}.

(5)

The method TR-BDF2 for this problem is written as:

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\text{Given }y_{0},\text{ compute }\forall n=0,\ldots,N-1,\\
\displaystyle y_{n+\gamma}=y_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{n},y_{n}}}\right)+a\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{n+\gamma},y_{n+\gamma}}}\right)}}\right)h\gamma,\\
\displaystyle y_{n+1}=\gamma_{3}y_{n+\gamma}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)y_{n}+a\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{n+1},y_{n+1}}}\right)h\gamma_{2},\end{array}}}\right.

(6)

where
t_{n+\gamma}=t_{n}+\gamma h
,
\gamma\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right)
is a parameter,
\gamma_{2}=\frac{1-\gamma}{2-\gamma}
and
\gamma_{3}=\frac{1}{\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-\gamma}}\right)}
.

The TR-BDF2 procedure is well-known and used because of its several interesting accuracy and stability properties. Especially important for stiff problems are the stability properties:
A
-stability and
L
-stability. These concepts can be found in detail in
[
12
]
. It is
A
-stable for any value of
\gamma
and
L
-stable for
\gamma=2-\sqrt{2}
, which is shown in
[
2
]
. From now on we will use this parameter value.

A first idea in order to solve stochastic equations is to use this method in the deterministic part and the usual approximation for the stochastic terms. However, this combination provides a method at most of order 1, as we will show later in Remark
3.2
. Consequently, the order 2 of the original method would be lost.

Our aim is to develop a method for stochastic differential equation problems, which generalizes the deterministic TR-BDF2, i.e., if the noise is zero, the same method is recovered and also has order 2 in the sense described in Definition
1.3
.

In order to obtain the method, the development of Itô–Taylor of different orders, introduced in the last section, applied to some functions will be fundamental.

First of all, we will fix some notations that will be used to obtain the method:

•

We denote by
\hat{\Delta}_{2N}
a partition of the interval
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,T}}\right]
with
2N+1
points,

\hat{\Delta}_{2N}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\hat{t}_{0}=0<\hat{t}_{1}<\hat{t}_{2}<\cdots<\hat{t}_{2N-1}<\hat{t}_{2N}=T}}\right\},

where
\hat{t}_{2j}=t_{0}+jh
,
\forall j=0,\ldots,N
and
\hat{t}_{2j+1}=\hat{t}_{2j}+\gamma h
,
\forall j=0,\ldots,N-1

•

\Delta W_{s,t}^{j}\coloneqq I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j}}\right),s,t}

\forall k=0,\ldots,N-1
and

*

•

a_{t}\coloneqq a\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,X_{t}}}\right)
,
L^{0}a_{t}\coloneqq L^{0}a\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,X_{t}}}\right)
,

and for

•

\hat{a}_{k}\coloneqq a\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{t}_{k},Y_{k}}}\right)
,

,
L^{j}\hat{a}_{k}\coloneqq L^{j}a\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{t}_{k},Y_{k}}}\right)
,
j=1,\ldots,m
and for

Y_{k}

In some parts of this paper, we denote by

We start with the Itô–Taylor expansion defined in (

X_{t_{n+\gamma}}=X_{t_{n}}+a_{t_{n}}h\gamma+\frac{1}{2}L^{0}a_{t_{n}}h^{2}\gamma^{2}+S_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{t_{n}}}}\right]_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}+\tilde{R}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[F}}\right]_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}},

where we recall that

and

For function
a
, we take the Itô–Taylor expansion of order 1 from
t_{n}
to
t_{n+\gamma}
:

a_{t_{n+\gamma}}=a_{t_{n}}+L^{0}a_{t_{n}}h\gamma+\sum_{j=1}^{m}L^{j}a_{t_{n}}\Delta W_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}^{j}+\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}=1}^{m}L^{j_{1}}L^{j_{2}}a_{t_{n}}I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j_{1},j_{2}}}\right),t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}+\tilde{R}_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a}}\right]_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}},

Writing

in equality (

X_{t_{n+\gamma}}=X_{t_{n}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{t_{n}}+a_{t_{n+\gamma}}}}\right)h\gamma+P_{t_{n}}+R_{n},

(9)

with

P_{t_{n}}=S_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{t_{n}}}}\right]_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}L^{j}a_{t_{n}}\Delta W_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}^{j}h\gamma-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}=1}^{m}L^{j_{1}}L^{j_{2}}a_{t_{n}}I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j_{1},j_{2}}}\right),t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}h\gamma,

R_{n}=\tilde{R}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[F}}\right]_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{R}_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a}}\right]_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}h\gamma.

Now, let us consider again the Itô–Taylor expansion (
2
) of the solution
X_{t}
of order 2 from
t_{n+\gamma}
to

X_{t_{n+1}}=X_{t_{n+\gamma}}+a_{t_{n+\gamma}}h\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}L^{0}a_{t_{n+\gamma}}h^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma}}\right)^{2}+S_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{t_{n+\gamma}}}}\right]_{t_{n+\gamma},t_{n+1}}+\tilde{R}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[F}}\right]_{t_{n+\gamma},t_{n+1}}.

(10)

Taking
X_{t_{n+\gamma}}=\gamma_{3}X_{t_{n+\gamma}}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)X_{t_{n+\gamma}}
in equation (

), considering the values of method defined in (
6
), and replacing this equality in (

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle X_{t_{n+1}}=\gamma_{3}X_{t_{n+\gamma}}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{t_{n}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{t_{n}}+a_{t_{n+\gamma}}}}\right)h\gamma+P_{t_{n}}+R_{n}}}\right)+a_{t_{n+\gamma}}h\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma}}\right)\\
\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{X_{t_{n+1}}=}}+\frac{1}{2}L^{0}a_{t_{n+\gamma}}h^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma}}\right)^{2}+S_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{t_{n+\gamma}}}}\right]_{t_{n+\gamma},t_{n+1}}+\tilde{R}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[F}}\right]_{t_{n+\gamma},t_{n+1}}.\end{array}

(11)

Using the same procedure as in the derivation of equality (

) between
t_{n+\gamma}
and

allows us

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle a_{t_{n+\gamma}}=a_{t_{n+1}}-L^{0}a_{t_{n+\gamma}}h\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma}}\right)-\sum_{j=1}^{m}L^{j}a_{t_{n+\gamma}}\Delta W_{t_{n+\gamma},t_{n+1}}^{j}-\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}=1}^{m}L^{j_{1}}L^{j_{2}}a_{t_{n+\gamma}}I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j_{1},j_{2}}}\right),t_{n+\gamma},t_{n+1}}\\
\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{a_{t_{n+\gamma}}=}}-\tilde{R}_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a}}\right]_{t_{n+\gamma},t_{n+1}}.\end{array}

On the other hand, considering now the Itô–Taylor expansion (

from

to

:

L^{0}a_{t_{n}}=L^{0}a_{t_{n+\gamma}}-\tilde{R}_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[L^{0}a}}\right]_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}},

from where we can write equation (

X_{t_{n+1}}=\gamma_{3}X_{t_{n+\gamma}}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)X_{t_{n}}+a_{t_{n+1}}h\gamma_{2}+Q_{t_{n}}+Q_{t_{n+\gamma}}+R_{n+\gamma}.

The stochastic terms are

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle Q_{t_{n}}=S_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{t_{n}}}}\right]_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)-\sum_{j=1}^{m}L^{j}a_{t_{n}}\Delta W_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}^{j}h\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)\gamma\\
\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{Q_{t_{n}}=}}-\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}=1}^{m}L^{j_{1}}L^{j_{2}}a_{t_{n}}I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j_{1},j_{2}}}\right),t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}h\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)\gamma,\end{array}

and the remainder is

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle R_{n+\gamma}=\tilde{R}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[F}}\right]_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)+\tilde{R}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[F}}\right]_{t_{n+\gamma},t_{n+1}}-\tilde{R}_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a}}\right]_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}h\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)\gamma-\tilde{R}_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a}}\right]_{t_{n+\gamma},t_{n+1}}h\gamma_{2}\\

Finally, the expansion of solution

that we have obtained is:

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle X_{t_{n+\gamma}}=X_{t_{n}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{t_{n}}+a_{t_{n+\gamma}}}}\right)h\gamma+P_{t_{n}}+R_{n},\\
\displaystyle X_{t_{n+1}}=\gamma_{3}X_{t_{n+\gamma}}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)X_{t_{n}}+a_{t_{n+1}}h\gamma_{2}+Q_{t_{n}}+Q_{t_{n+\gamma}}+R_{n+\gamma}.\end{array}}}\right.

(12)

We can conclude that the method we have constructed, which we will call stochastic TR-BDF2, is as follows

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\text{Given }Y_{0},\text{ compute }\forall n=0,\ldots,N-1,\\
\displaystyle Y_{n+\gamma}=Y_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{a}_{n}+\hat{a}_{n+\gamma}}}\right)h\gamma+\hat{P}_{n},\\
\displaystyle Y_{n+1}=\gamma_{3}Y_{n+\gamma}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)Y_{n}+\hat{a}_{n+1}h\gamma_{2}+\hat{Q}_{n}+\hat{Q}_{n+\gamma},\end{array}}}\right.

(13)

where, if we denote
\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{2}:l\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha}}\right)\neq n\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha}}\right)}{}_{\alpha}F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{n},Y_{n}}}\right)I_{\alpha,t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}
by

and similarly, in the auxiliary step, we denote
\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{2}:l\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha}}\right)\neq n\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha}}\right)}{}_{\alpha}F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{n+\gamma},Y_{n+\gamma}}}\right)I_{\alpha,t_{n+\gamma},t_{n+1}}

\hat{P}_{n}=S_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Y_{n}}}\right]-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}L^{j}\hat{a}_{n}\Delta W_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}^{j}h\gamma-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}=1}^{m}L^{j_{1}}L^{j_{2}}\hat{a}_{n}I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j_{1},j_{2}}}\right),t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}h\gamma,

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{n}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Y_{n}}}\right]-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(L^{j}\hat{a}_{n}\Delta W_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}^{j}+\sum_{j_{2}=1}^{m}L^{j}L^{j_{2}}\hat{a}_{n}I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j,j_{2}}}\right),t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}}}\right)h\gamma}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right),

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{n+\gamma}=S_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Y_{n+\gamma}}}\right]-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(L^{j}\hat{a}_{n+\gamma}\Delta W_{t_{n+\gamma},t_{n+1}}^{j}+\sum_{j_{2}=1}^{m}L^{j}L^{j_{2}}\hat{a}_{n+\gamma}I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j,j_{2}}}\right),t_{n+\gamma},t_{n+1}}}}\right)h\gamma_{2}.

3.2
Convergence order

To prove that the developed scheme (
13
) is indeed of order 2 we need some preliminary results, following an argument similar to that of Ewald et al. in
[
11
]
, where the theorems developed cannot be applied directly due to the structure of the method, although the techniques employed are analogous. These results are proved for uniform partitions but are easily extensible to non-uniform partitions which will be the ones we will use, since our
\hat{\Delta}_{2N}
partition is not uniform. Throughout of this paper,
C
is a positive constant whose value may change from line to line.

Proposition 3.3
.

Suppose
Y_{n}
is a stochastic process adapted to the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}
at the equipartition (i.e.,
Y_{n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}
-measurable) and it is also well-defined in the auxiliary steps
t_{n+\gamma}
, with
E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sup_{0\leq n\leq N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|Y_{n}}}\right\|^{2}}}\right]<\infty
. Also, function
f
belongs to
{\rm C}^{1,2}
, and
\alpha
is a multi-index with
l\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha}}\right)\geq 1
. Then, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sup_{0\leq l\leq n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}I_{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{k},X_{t_{k}}}}\right)-f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{k},Y_{k}}}\right)}}\right]_{t_{k},t_{k+\gamma}}}}\right\|^{2}}}\right]\leq Ch\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sup_{0\leq l\leq k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X_{t_{l}}-Y_{l}}}\right\|^{2}}}\right].

E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sup_{0\leq l\leq n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}I_{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{k+\gamma},X_{t_{k+\gamma}}}}\right)-f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{k+\gamma},Y_{k+\gamma}}}\right)}}\right]_{t_{k+\gamma},t_{k+1}}}}\right\|^{2}}}\right]\leq Ch\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sup_{0\leq l\leq k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X_{t_{l+\gamma}}-Y_{l+\gamma}}}\right\|^{2}}}\right].

Remark 3.4
.

The proof of the Proposition above is analogous to that of the corresponding result in
[
11
]
, with the difference being the replacement of the global Lipschitz condition by the mean value theorem, which is applicable in this case due to the continuity assumption and the boundedness of the second moment of the solution and the approximation.

Proposition 3.5
.

Suppose the function
f
satisfies the linear growth bound condition, and
\alpha
is a multi-index with
l\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha}}\right)\geq 1
. Then,

E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sup_{0\leq l\leq n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}I_{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cdot,X_{\cdot}}}\right)}}\right]_{t_{k},t_{k+\gamma}}}}\right\|^{2}}}\right]\leq Ch^{\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X_{0}}}\right\|^{2}}}\right]}}\right),

4
Numerical stability

In this section we will study the stability property of the proposed method. It is well known that for stiff problems, for example those known as fast slow problems, a very small time step is necessary to solve the fast variables, but this is due to reasons of having a good error behavior. However, in these problems there is an additional condition, it is necessary to choose this time step not only for error behavior but also for stability reasons.

In the literature, two types of stability are commonly studied, both aim to generalize the stability analysis in the deterministic framework, where the complex-valued linear differential equations

x^{\prime}=\lambda x,

are studied, where
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, with
\Re\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda}}\right)<0
.

The first extension corresponds to consider the class of complex-valued linear test equations with additive noise. This case has been studied in depth by Kloeden and Platen in
[
16
]
. They take the following equation

{\rm d}X_{t}=\lambda X_{t}{\rm d}t+\sigma{\rm d}W_{t},

(17)

where
\lambda
and
\sigma
are complex numbers.

Using this equation, a first concept of numerical stability is defined:
A
-stability.

Definition 4.1
.

We consider a numerical scheme such that, applied to the test equation (
17
) can be written in the recursive form as

Y_{n+1}=G\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right)Y_{n}+V_{n},

(18)

where any
V_{n}
does not depend on any
Y_{n}
,
n=0,1,\ldots,N-1
and
G
is a mapping of the complex plane into itself. We call the region of absolute stability of the scheme to the set of complex numbers

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|G\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right)}}\right|<1}}\right\}.

Then, a stochastic scheme is
A
-stable if its region of absolute stability is the left half of the complex plane.

Note that this definition of
A
-stability is the same as in the deterministic case when the noise is zero.

A second extension proposed by Lord et al.
[
20
]
consists of adding a multiplicative noise giving rise to another stability concept:
MS
-stability. In this case the test equation is:

{\rm d}X_{t}=\lambda X_{t}{\rm d}t+\sigma X_{t}{\rm d}W_{t}.

(19)

Definition 4.2
.

We consider a numerical scheme such that, applied to the test equation (
19
) can be written in the recursive form as

Y_{n+1}=R\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h,\lambda,\sigma,I_{\alpha}}}\right)Y_{n},

where
I_{\alpha}
represents the integrals considered in that scheme. Then, a scheme is
MS
-stable if and only if

E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|R\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h,\lambda,\sigma,I_{\alpha}}}\right)}}\right|^{2}}}\right]<1.

Having defined these concepts of stability for stochastic differential systems, we now turn to the study of these concepts for the developed method.

4.1
A
-stability

This type of stability by definition is strongly associated with the deterministic part of the equation. Let us see that the developed method has this stability property.

Proposition 4.3
.

The stochastic TR-BDF2 is
A
-stable.

Proof.

The stochastic TR-BDF2 applied to the stochastic differential equation (
17
) is

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle Y_{n+1}=\gamma_{3}Y_{n+\gamma}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)Y_{n}+Y_{n+1}z\gamma_{2}+\Delta W_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-z\gamma}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)+I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,0}}\right),t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}\sigma\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)\\
\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{Y_{n+1}=}}+\Delta W_{t_{n+\gamma},t_{n+1}}\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-z\gamma_{2}}}\right)+I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,0}}\right),t_{n+\gamma},t_{n+1}}\sigma\lambda,\end{array}

with
Y_{n+\gamma}
solution of

Y_{n+\gamma}=Y_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Y_{n}+Y_{n+\gamma}}}\right)z\gamma+\Delta W_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{2}z\gamma}}\right)+I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,0}}\right),t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}\sigma\lambda.

Combining the previous equations and solving
Y_{n+1}
:

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle Y_{n+1}=\frac{1}{1-z\gamma_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\gamma_{3}}{1-\frac{1}{2}z\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Y_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{1}{2}z\gamma}}\right)+\Delta W_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{2}z\gamma}}\right)+I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,0}}\right),t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}\sigma\lambda}}\right)\vphantom{+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)Y_{n}+\Delta W_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-z\gamma}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)+I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,0}}\right),t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}\sigma\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)+\Delta W_{t_{n+\gamma},t_{n+1}}\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-z\gamma_{2}}}\right)+I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,0}}\right),t_{n+\gamma},t_{n+1}}\sigma\lambda}}}\right.\\
\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{Y_{n+1}=}}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)Y_{n}+\Delta W_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-z\gamma}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)+I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,0}}\right),t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}\sigma\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)\\
\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{Y_{n+1}=}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\vphantom{\frac{\gamma_{3}}{1-\frac{1}{2}z\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Y_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{1}{2}z\gamma}}\right)+\Delta W_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{2}z\gamma}}\right)+I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,0}}\right),t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}\sigma\lambda}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)Y_{n}+\Delta W_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-z\gamma}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)+I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,0}}\right),t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}\sigma\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma_{3}}}\right)}+\Delta W_{t_{n+\gamma},t_{n+1}}\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-z\gamma_{2}}}\right)+I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,0}}\right),t_{n+\gamma},t_{n+1}}\sigma\lambda}}\right).\end{array}

Taking the values of
\gamma_{2}
and
\gamma_{3}
, it can be written as:

Y_{n+1}=G\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right)Y_{n}+V_{n}+V_{n+\gamma},

where

G\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right)=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma}}\right)^{2}}}\right)z+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-\gamma}}\right)}{\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma}}\right)z^{2}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma^{2}-2}}\right)z+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-\gamma}}\right)},

V_{n}=\Delta W_{t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}\sigma\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma}}\right)^{2}z+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-\gamma}}\right)}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma-1}}\right)z+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-\gamma}}\right)}+I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,0}}\right),t_{n},t_{n+\gamma}}\sigma\lambda\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma}}\right)^{2}z+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-\gamma}}\right)}{\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\gamma}}\right)z^{2}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma^{2}-2}}\right)z+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-\gamma}}\right)},

V_{n+\gamma}=\Delta W_{t_{n+\gamma},t_{n+1}}\sigma+I_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,0}}\right),t_{n+\gamma},t_{n+1}}\sigma\lambda\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-\gamma}}\right)}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma-1}}\right)z+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-\gamma}}\right)}.

Iterating, we obtain

Y_{n+1}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right)}}\right)^{n+1}Y_{0}+\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z}}\right)}}\right)^{n-k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V_{k}+V_{k+\gamma}}}\right).

Therefore, the fact that the extra step
\gamma
has been added does not change the analysis to be performed; and it is equivalent if we had the scheme written as in (
18
).

Studying the region of absolute stability of the stochastic TR-BDF2 scheme (
13
) is the same as studying its counterpart of the deterministic TR-BDF2 scheme (
6
), which is
A
-stable. Thus concluding the proof.
∎

4.2
MS
-stability

As mentioned above, for this case we consider the test equation (
19
):

{\rm d}X_{t}=\lambda X_{t}{\rm d}t+\sigma X_{t}{\rm d}W_{t}.

This equation has a known analytical solution and is given by

X_{t}=e^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}}}\right)t+\sigma W_{t}}X_{0},

and the second moment is

E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{t}^{2}}}\right]=e^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\lambda+\sigma^{2}}}\right)t}X_{0}^{2}.

(20)

As in the first stability case presented, we are interested in studying a particular scenario; namely when

2\Re\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sigma}}\right|^{2}<0.

(21)

When this inequality holds, the second moment (
20
) tends to zero as
t
goes to infinity, and we want to study when the numerical scheme will behave similarly under that condition. On the other hand, note that it also generalizes the deterministic case, wherein the inequality (
21
) would be
\Re\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda}}\right)<0
if
\sigma=0
.

Proposition 4.4
.

There exists
h^{\ast}>0
such that for all
h<h^{\ast}
, the stochastic TR-BDF2 is
MS
-stable.

Proof.

When we calculate
E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|R\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h,\lambda,\sigma,I_{\alpha}}}\right)}}\right|^{2}}}\right]-1
for the stochastic TR-BDF2 method, it becomes a polynomial, denoted by
P
, of degree seven in the variable
h
, so when
h
tends to zero,
P
converges to the independent term, which in this case is
2\Re\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sigma}}\right|^{2}
.

Applying the definition of continuity, we obtain that, for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta\coloneqq h^{\ast}>0
such that, for all
h\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,h^{\ast}}}\right)
, the following inequality holds

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h}}\right)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\Re\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sigma}}\right|^{2}}}\right)}}\right|<\varepsilon,

or, equivalently,

-\varepsilon+2\Re\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sigma}}\right|^{2}<P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h}}\right)<\varepsilon+2\Re\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sigma}}\right|^{2}.

Then, if we take
\varepsilon<-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\Re\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sigma}}\right|^{2}}}\right)
,
P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h}}\right)<0
,
\forall h\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,h^{\ast}}}\right)
and consequently the scheme is
MS
-stable.
∎

As mentioned in the introduction of this section, many schemes require a very small time step
h
to achieve accurate performance. In our case, the above result states that the stochastic TR-BDF2 scheme performs well, in the
MS
-stability sense, for a certain
h<h^{\ast}
.

In Figure
1
, we present the values of
h^{\ast}
for the test equation (

\Re\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda}}\right)\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[-200,0}}\right)
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sigma}}\right|\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,20}}\right)
, calculated numerically using the stochastic TR-BDF2 scheme (

).

Figure 1:
h^{\ast}
values for the stochastic TR-BDF2 scheme.

It can be seen that the values of
h^{\ast}
are acceptable in general: very large with small noise
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sigma}}\right|<2}}\right)
and decreasing as the parameters are closer to condition limit
2\Re\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sigma}}\right|^{2}=0
, would only be very small and therefore unfeasible when it is very close to the boundary condition.

We consider the case of the Itô–Taylor method of order 2. In this situation the reasoning of Proposition
4.4
can be exactly reproduced with the only difference that here the polynomial is of degree 3 and therefore the scheme is also
MS
-stable.

However, if we consider the second greatest term of the polynomials when
h
tends to zero, the lineal one, for the Itô–Taylor approximation of order 2 we have

\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\Re\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sigma}}\right|^{2}}}\right)^{2},

while for the stochastic TR-BDF2

\frac{1}{2-\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\Re\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda}}\right)\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma-1}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sigma}}\right|^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2-\gamma}}\right)}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\Re\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sigma}}\right|^{2}}}\right).

The Itô–Taylor one is positive, while the second is negative. This means that, there exists
h^{\ast\ast}>0
such that for all
h\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[h^{\ast\ast},h^{\ast}}}\right)
, the stochastic TR-BDF2 is
MS
-stable, whilst the Itô–Taylor approximation of order 2 is not.

In the following section, we will study a stiff example where this phenomenon occurs. Also, Figure
2
shows how, indeed, the values of
h^{\ast}
are smaller across the entire graph, meaning a smaller time step would be needed to obtain good results.

5
Numerical validation

In this section we want to validate the theoretical results of the previous sections. On the one hand, we want to check the convergence orders of the stochastic TR-BDF2 and the different methods presented and denoted by TR-BDF2
r
(Subsection
3.1
) and, on the other hand, the behavior against another method of order 2 in the case of stiff problems. For this reason, we have selected three academic examples that are sufficiently illustrative to verify numerically the results that were established theoretically. For such examples, it is not necessary to use a method of order 2 with the stability properties of our schemes. However, our goal is to apply it in future work to problems where a more accurate approximation of the solution is needed, or where good stability properties are essential, such as in stiff problems.

To implement the method, it is necessary to compute all the stochastic integrals up to second order, which involves a laborious implementation. However, the same integrals appear in the Itô–Taylor approximation of order 2, so we follow the techniques developed by Kloeden
[
16
]
. For this reason, we plan to study how to optimise the computation of these stochastic integrals in future work, including, for example, the computation of Lévy areas, as done in
[
20
]
.

Before starting the presentation of the results, we are going to detail some technical aspects that have been necessary for the implementation of the resolution algorithm:

•

All tests were programmed using MATLAB
\_
R2022b software on a personal computer MacBook Air (M1, 8GB, 2020).

•

As this is an implicit method in each of its steps, it is necessary to use a method for solving non-linear equations. In this case, the Newton-Raphson method has been chosen because of its order 2 behavior in the error. If a lower order method is used, it may influence the order estimates of the total method.

•

In order to calculate the
L^{2}
error numerically to check the algorithms used, we shall use the usual estimation presented in
[
20
]
:

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X_{T}-Y_{N}}}\right\|_{L^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\right)}\simeq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X_{T}^{j}-Y_{N}^{j}}}\right\|^{2}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\eqqcolon\varepsilon_{h},

where we approximate the error with the mean value error at the final time of
M
simulations of sample paths of the Wiener process, fixed the time step
h
.

•

To estimate the convergence order, we compute the following expression for two values of
h
:

p\simeq\frac{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{h_{2}}}{\varepsilon_{h_{1}}}}}\right)}{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{h_{2}}{h_{1}}}}\right)}.

In the particular case when
h_{1}=\frac{h}{2}
and
h_{2}=h
and the partition is uniform, we have

p\simeq\log_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{h}}{\varepsilon_{\frac{h}{2}}}}}\right)\eqqcolon\rho_{h}.
