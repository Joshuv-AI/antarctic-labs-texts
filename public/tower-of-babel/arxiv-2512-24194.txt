Impact of Anisotropy on Neutron Star Structure and Curvature
and P. C. Vinodkumar

Abstract
We investigate the impact of pressure anisotropy on the structural and geometric properties of neutron stars within general relativity, focusing primarily on the phenomenological Bowers–Liang (BL) model, and comparing selected results with a quasi-local prescription. Using the SLy equation of state, we explore how anisotropic stresses modify global observables such as the mass–radius relation, moment of inertia, compactness, and tidal deformability over a broad range of anisotropy parameters. We find that moderate positive anisotropy can increase the maximum supported mass up to approximately 2.4 ​ M ⊙ 2.4\;M_{\odot} and enhance stellar compactness by up to 20 % 20\% relative to isotropic configurations, while remaining broadly consistent with current NICER and gravitational-wave constraints. To probe the internal gravitational field, we compute curvature invariants including the Ricci scalar, the Ricci tensor contraction, the Kretschmann scalar, and the Weyl scalar. We show that curvature measures directly tied to the matter distribution exhibit a strong sensitivity to anisotropy, whereas the Weyl curvature remains comparatively insensitive, reflecting its role as a measure of the free gravitational field. Within the phenomenological BL framework, the maximum compactness increases with anisotropy and reaches values as high as 𝒞 max ≈ 0.25 \mathcal{C}_{\max}\approx 0.25 - 0.38 0.38 for λ BL ∈ [ − 4 , + 4 ] \lambda_{\rm BL}\in[-4,+4] , although the physical realizability of such highly compact configurations depends sensitively on the underlying anisotropy mechanism. A comparison with the quasi-local model highlights the strong model dependence of anisotropic effects, underscoring both the potential significance and the limitations of phenomenological anisotropy prescriptions in modeling strong-field neutron-star interiors.

In the past few decades, theorists have concentrated a lot of effort into developing realistic EoS for the dense matter in NSs. If curvature influences dense matter properties, incorporating spacetime effects into EoS models, although exceedingly challenging, becomes crucial.

2.1
Stable anisotropic compact star configurations

The stress-energy tensor for an isotropic star under ideal fluid conditions is defined as
[
69
]

T^{\mu}{}_{\nu}=(\mathcal{E}+P)\,u^{\mu}u_{\nu}+P\,\delta^{\mu}{}_{\nu},

(2.1)

where
\mathcal{E}
,
P
, and
u^{\mu}
represent the energy density, pressure, and four-velocity of the ideal fluid, respectively.
However, physical processes inside compact stars generally lead to anisotropy between the radial and tangential pressures.
For a static, spherically symmetric anisotropic fluid distribution, the stress-energy tensor takes the diagonal form

T^{\mu}{}_{\nu}=\mathrm{diag}(-\mathcal{E},\,P_{r},\,P_{\perp},\,P_{\perp}),

(2.2)

and can equivalently be written as

T^{\mu}{}_{\nu}=(\mathcal{E}+P_{\perp})u^{\mu}u_{\nu}+(P_{r}-P_{\perp})k^{\mu}k_{\nu}+P_{\perp}\,\delta^{\mu}{}_{\nu},

(2.3)

where
P_{\perp}
and
P_{r}
are the tangential and radial pressures, respectively.
Here
k^{\mu}
is the unit radial spacelike vector satisfying
k^{\mu}k_{\mu}=1
and
u^{\mu}k_{\mu}=0
.
In Schwarzschild coordinates, the background metric is

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}

\displaystyle=g^{(0)}_{\mu\nu}\,\mathrm{d}x^{\mu}\mathrm{d}x^{\nu}

\displaystyle=-e^{\nu(r)}\,\mathrm{d}t^{2}+e^{\lambda(r)}\,\mathrm{d}r^{2}+r^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta^{2}+r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\varphi^{2}.

(2.4)

The anisotropic hydrostatic equilibrium equations that result from using this energy-momentum configuration and metric structure in GR are as follows:

\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{r}}{\mathrm{d}r}=-\frac{\left({\cal{E}}+P_{r}\right)\left(m+4\pi r^{3}P_{r}\right)}{r\left(r-2m\right)}+\frac{2}{r}(P_{\bot}-P_{r})\,,

(2.5)

\frac{\mathrm{d}m}{\mathrm{d}r}=4\pi r^{2}{\cal{E}}\>,

(2.6)

and

\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}r}=\frac{m+4\pi r^{3}P_{r}}{r(r-2m)}

(2.7)

where we define
\sigma=P_{\bot}-P_{r}
as the anisotropy parameter. The
m
stands for the mass within the radial coordinate
r
. To close the system, there are two different EoSs for
P_{r}
and
P_{\bot}
. We use a barotropic EoS for the radial pressure,
P_{r}=P_{r}({\cal{E}})
, and the BL model for the tangential pressure,
P_{\bot}
.
The Runge-Kutta technique is used to numerically solve the hydrostatic equilibrium equations for a given choice of anisotropy. The starting point is the center of the star, where the boundary conditions are
r=0
,
m=0
, and
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{c}
.
The integration continues outward until it reaches the surface, which is determined by
r=R
,
m=M
, and the radial pressure
P_{r}=0
vanishing. For the
P_{\bot}
, we adopt the BL model as presented in Ref.
[
12
]

P_{\bot}=P_{r}+\frac{\lambda_{\rm BL}}{3}\frac{({\cal E}+3P_{r})({\cal E}+P_{r})r^{2}}{1-2m/r}\,,

(2.8)

The parameter
\lambda_{\text{BL}}
indicates the degree of anisotropy in the fluid. The following assumptions govern the model’s construction: (i) the anisotropy must quadratically decrease to zero in the center, (ii) It needs to exhibit a nonlinear dependency on
P
and (iii) gravitational effects are responsible for an element of the anisotropy. The parameter
\lambda_{\rm BL}
controls the fluid’s degree of anisotropy. To maintain spherical symmetry and ensure the physical consistency of anisotropic NS models, all standard acceptability conditions must be satisfied. We have explicitly verified that our configurations fulfill these requirements, including regularity, energy conditions, and causality throughout the stellar interior. 2.2
Mathematical representation of spacetime curvatures

In this part, we provide the mathematical expressions for the scalar curvature invariants, including the Ricci scalar for a spherically symmetric NS, the Kretschmann scalar, and the full contractions of the Weyl and Ricci tensors. The expressions for these curvature quantities are given by
[

20

1. Kretschmann Scalar (
\mathcal{K}
)

The Kretschmann scalar (full contraction of the Riemann tensor) is given by:

\mathcal{K}(r)\equiv\sqrt{R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}},

(2.9)

where the Riemann curvature tensor is shown by
R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
. The Kretschmann scalar for the anisotropic configuration could be obtained by calculating the contraction within the context of the modified TOV equations, using the Christoffel symbols and associated Riemann tensor components:

\mathcal{K}(r)=\Big[(8\pi)^{2}\left(3\mathcal{E}^{2}+3P^{2}+2\mathcal{E}P\right)-\frac{128\pi\mathcal{E}m(r)}{r^{3}}+\frac{48m^{2}(r)}{r^{6}}\,\Big]^{1/2}\,,

(2.10)

In this case, the mass encompassed by a radius
r
is denoted by
m(r)
. At
r>R
, the star’s outer region, where
P=0
,
\mathcal{E}=0
, and
m(r)=M
, the expression simplifies to

\mathcal{K}(r)=\frac{4\sqrt{3}M}{r^{3}}.

(2.11)

Similarly, at the star surface (
r=R
), we obtain

\mathcal{K}(R)=\frac{4\sqrt{3}M}{R^{3}}.

(2.12)

2. Weyl Scalar (
\mathcal{W}
)

The Weyl tensor contraction is defined as:

\mathcal{W}(r)\equiv\sqrt{C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}C^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}},

(2.13)

where
C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
denotes the Weyl tensor. It represents the traceless part of the Riemann curvature tensor and is related to the latter through the following decomposition:

\displaystyle C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}=R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}+\frac{R}{6}\left(g_{\mu\rho}g_{\nu\sigma}-g_{\mu\sigma}g_{\nu\rho}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\left(R_{\mu\sigma}g_{\nu\rho}-R_{\mu\rho}g_{\nu\sigma}+R_{\nu\rho}g_{\mu\sigma}-R_{\nu\sigma}g_{\mu\rho}\right)\,.

(2.14)

In addition to having the same symmetry features as the Riemann tensor, the Weyl tensor
C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
must also be trace-free, which means that any contraction with the metric over a set of indices vanishes in the exact same manner.

The following equation is obtained by contracting and evaluating the Weyl tensor’s components for the spherically symmetric case:

\displaystyle C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}C^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{3}\left(\frac{6m(r)}{r^{3}}-\kappa\mathcal{E}\right)^{2}

(2.15)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{3}\left(\frac{6m(r)}{r^{3}}-8\pi\mathcal{E}\right)^{2}.

(2.16)

where
\kappa=8\pi G
(with
c=1
), and in geometrized units
\kappa=8\pi
. Therefore, the scalar that comes from the Weyl tensor’s full contraction is

\mathcal{W}(r)=\left[\frac{4}{3}\left(\frac{6m(r)}{r^{3}}-8\pi\mathcal{E}(r)\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}\,.

(2.17)

In the exterior region of the star (
r>R
), the equation reduces to

\mathcal{W}(r)=\frac{4\sqrt{3}M}{r^{3}}.

(2.18)

At the star surface (
r=R
), the expression becomes

\mathcal{W}(R)=\frac{4\sqrt{3}M}{R^{3}}\,,

(2.19)

Note that at the star surface
(r=R)
, the boundary conditions

P(R)=0
,
\mathcal{E}(R)=0
, and
m(R)=M
hold. Under these conditions,
the same form is obtained by reducing Eqns. (
2.10
) and (
2.17
).

\mathcal{W}(R)=\mathcal{K}(R)=\frac{4\sqrt{3}\,M}{R^{3}}.

(2.20)

3. Ricci Scalar (
\mathcal{R}
)

The Ricci scalar
\mathcal{R}
is calculated by contracting the Ricci tensor
R_{\mu\nu}
, which is itself obtained from the Riemann curvature tensor. It tells us how curved space-time is because of the distribution of mass and energy, and it is described as

\mathcal{R}=g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu}.

(2.21)

The Ricci scalar is a scalar measure of curvature that shows how volumes vary in curved spacetime. It may be immediately derived from the Einstein field equations by taking the trace, resulting in

\mathcal{R}(r)=\kappa\left(\mathcal{E}-3P\right)\,,

(2.22)

the full contraction of the Ricci tensor

{\cal J}\equiv\sqrt{{\cal R}_{\mu\nu}{\cal R}^{\mu\nu}}=\kappa\left(\mathcal{E}+3P\right).

(2.23)

In contrast to the Kretschmann and Weyl scalars, the Ricci scalar and the Ricci tensor vanish at the stellar boundary and throughout the exterior vacuum, with their contributions confined entirely to the star’s interior.

2.4
Tidal deformability and Love numbers

A quadrupole moment
\epsilon_{ij}
is induced in a spherically symmetric star when it becomes subjected to a static external quadrupolar tidal field
Q_{ij}
. First-order linear relationships exist between the two as
[
33
]

\displaystyle Q_{ij}=-\bm{\lambda}\epsilon_{ij}~.

(2.29)

The degree of star deformation caused by an external tidal force is described by the parameter
\bm{\lambda}
, commonly referred to as the (dimensionful) tidal deformability. The background metric
g_{\mu\nu}^{(0)}
is perturbed by
h_{\mu\nu}
when this field is present, allowing the entire metric to be written as

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}=g_{\mu\nu}^{(0)}+h_{\mu\nu}~.

(2.30)

The perturbation can be also separated into polar and axial components,

\displaystyle h_{\mu\nu}=h_{\mu\nu}^{\rm polar}+h_{\mu\nu}^{\rm axial}~.

(2.31)

The polar element of the linearized metric perturbation,
h_{\mu\nu}^{\text{polar}}
, can be expressed as
[
64
,
68
]

\displaystyle h_{\mu\nu}^{\text{polar}}=~

\displaystyle\text{diag}[e^{\nu(r)}H_{0}(r),e^{\lambda(r)}H_{2}(r),r^{2}K(r),

(2.32)

\displaystyle r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta K(r)]\times Y_{lm}(\theta,\phi)~,

In this case,
Y_{lm}(\theta,\phi)
represents for the spherical harmonics. The nonzero components of the stress-energy tensor are

\displaystyle\delta T_{0}^{0}

\displaystyle=-\delta\mathcal{E}=-\left(\derivative{P}{\mathcal{E}}\right)^{-1}\delta P\,,

\displaystyle\delta T_{i}^{i}

\displaystyle=\delta P~,

where
\delta P
and
\delta\mathcal{E}
represent the perturbations in pressure and energy-density, respectively. By substituting the these expressions together with Eq. (
2.32
) into the linearized Einstein equations, it is determined that
H_{2}(r)=H_{0}(r)\equiv H(r)

[
33
]
. The relationship between the dimensionless Love number
(k_{2})
and the tidal deformability is
\bm{\lambda}=\tfrac{2}{3}k_{2}R^{5}
, where
R
corresponds to the star radius.
To determine
k_{2}
, we use linear perturbations within the Thorne–Campolattaro metric
[
68
]
. The following second-order differential equation for an anisotropic star is obtained by solving the Einstein equations
[
11
]
.

\displaystyle H^{{}^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle+H^{{}^{\prime}}\bigg[\frac{2}{r}+e^{\lambda}\left(\frac{2m(r)}{r^{2}}+4\pi r(P-{\cal E})\right)\bigg]

\displaystyle+H\left[4\pi e^{\lambda}\left(4{\cal E}+8P+\frac{{\cal E}+P}{dP_{\bot}/d{\cal E}}(1+c_{s}^{2})\right)-\frac{6e^{\lambda}}{r^{2}}-{\nu^{\prime}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=0\,.

(2.33)

According to the energy density at constant
\lambda_{\rm BL}
,
dP_{\bot}/d\mathcal{E}
represents the variation of
P_{\bot}
(see Eq. (
2.8
)).
In order to determine the tidal Love number
[
19
,
33
]
, the internal and exterior solutions of the perturbation variable
H
are matched at the surface of the star. We get its value using the function
y_{2}
and the compactness parameter
\mathcal{C}
, which has been defined as
[
33
,
34
]

\displaystyle k_{2}^{\text{polar}}(\mathcal{C},y_{2})

\displaystyle=\,\frac{8}{5}\mathcal{C}^{5}(1-2\mathcal{C})^{2}\big[2(y_{2}-1)\mathcal{C}-y_{2}+2\big]

\displaystyle\times\Big\{2\mathcal{C}\big[4(y_{2}+1)\mathcal{C}^{4}+2(3y_{2}-2)\mathcal{C}^{3}-2(11y_{2}-13)\mathcal{C}^{2}

\displaystyle+3(5y_{2}-8)\mathcal{C}-3(y_{2}-2)\big]+3(1-2\mathcal{C})^{2}

\displaystyle\times\big[2(y_{2}-1)\mathcal{C}-y_{2}+2\big]\log(1-2\mathcal{C})\Big\}^{-1}\,,

(2.34)

Here,
\mathcal{C}=M/R

y_{2}=y(R)

y_{2}

H
and its derivative

y_{2}=\frac{rH^{{}^{\prime}}}{H}\Big|_{R}.

(2.35)

Using the equation above, one can obtain the dimensionless tidal deformability from the following expression,

\Lambda=\frac{2}{3}k_{2}^{\text{polar}}\left(\frac{M}{R}\right)^{-5}.

(2.36)

Finally,
\Lambda

3.5
Inhomogeneity

We find that spacetime curvature varies sensitively with both anisotropy and matter distribution across the neutron star interior. While the equation of state, often formulated in flat spacetime, is commonly used to describe neutron star structure, our results show that curvature provides essential additional information in the strong-field regime. In particular, the coupled interplay between curvature and anisotropy is crucial for a realistic description of the stellar interior.

To quantify this coupling, we analyze the tidal contribution encoded in
{\cal W}
, which is sensitive to inhomogeneities in the energy–momentum distribution. We introduce an inhomogeneity parameter
\zeta
, defined as the ratio of the Weyl curvature to the anisotropy factor
\sigma
,

\zeta(r):=\frac{{\cal W}(r)}{\sigma(r)},

(3.1)

where
\sigma(r)
denotes the difference between tangential and radial pressures. A nonzero value of
\zeta
directly signals matter inhomogeneity and its imprint on the local tidal field.

Special care is required near the stellar center (
r\lesssim 1\,\mathrm{cm}
), where both
{\cal W}(r)
and
\sigma(r)
approach zero, leading to potential numerical cancellation errors. To address this, we employ series expansions of
{\cal W}(r)
and
\sigma(r)
about the origin, as detailed in Appendix
B
, ensuring a stable and physically consistent evaluation of
\zeta(r)
throughout the star.

The behavior of
\zeta(r)
is shown in Fig.
7
, including its radial profile and a contour representation in the
(r,\lambda_{\rm BL},\zeta)
space. The inhomogeneity parameter remains very small near the center, increases gradually with radius, and approaches a nearly stable trend toward the surface. This qualitative behavior persists for all explored values of
\lambda_{\rm BL}
, while the contour plot clearly illustrates how anisotropy progressively enhances curvature-driven inhomogeneity toward the outer regions of the star.

4
Discussion and conclusions

Neutron star interiors are challenging to model due to their extreme densities and strong gravity. The microphysics of dense matter, natural phase transitions, superfluidity, and strong magnetic fields can all cause pressure anisotropy, which is often overlooked in conventional isotropic models. Incorporating anisotropy allows models to capture a wider spectrum of structural and rotational properties, correlate theoretical predictions with multi-messenger observations, and better represent the correlation between matter distribution and spacetime curvature in the strong-field regime.

In this study, we employed the BL model to investigate the properties of anisotropic NSs. The results obtained suggest that the degree of anisotropy influences the macroscopic quantities since the hydrostatic balance is endowed with an additional term due to a pressure difference between the
P_{\perp}
and
P_{r}
. This impact, which depends on the model, changes the star’s equilibrium configuration and affects its overall structure.

In Sec.
3.1
, we provided our findings about the
M
-
R
relationship of NSs with varied degrees of anisotropy. In the isotropic
\lambda_{\rm BL}=0
setting, without anisotropy, the highest mass is around
2\;M_{\odot}
with a radius of approximately
9.85~\mathrm{km}
, which is in agreement with observations of PSR J0348+0432. When the
\lambda_{\rm BL}
varies from
-2.0
to
+2.0
,
M
and
R

B.1
\mathcal{W}
near the origin

The Weyl tensor’s full contraction,

\displaystyle{\cal W}^{2}\equiv{\cal C}^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}{\cal C}_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}

(B.1)

as seen in Eq. (
2.17
), it goes to zero near the center of the star. In practice, determining

{\cal W}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\left(\frac{6m}{r^{3}}-\kappa\mathcal{E}\right)

(B.2)

or exceptionally small radii (
r<1
cm) require careful examination, as the two contributions within the brackets essentially neutralize each other. This almost cancellation causes subtraction error that are bigger than the actual dimension of
{\cal W}
. For the solution to this problem, we used a series expansion of
{\cal W}
around the origin.

Because the system is spherically symmetric, the physical quantities pressure
P(r)
and energy density
\mathcal{E}(r)
are analytic even functions of the radial coordinate
r
. The mass function
m(r)
, defined as the integral of the energy density within a radius
r
, is an analytic odd function. So, we use the following power series expansions around the origin:

\displaystyle P(r)

\displaystyle=P_{c}+\frac{1}{2}P^{\prime\prime}(0)r^{2}+\frac{1}{24}P^{(4)}(0)r^{4}+\mathcal{O}(r^{6}),

(B.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(r)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{c}+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(0)r^{2}+\frac{1}{24}\mathcal{E}^{(4)}(0)r^{4}+\mathcal{O}(r^{6}),

(B.4)

\displaystyle m(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}m^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)r^{3}+\frac{1}{120}m^{(5)}(0)r^{5}+\mathcal{O}(r^{7}).

(B.5)

The boundary conditions are carried out as follows:

P(0)=P_{\mathrm{c}},\quad\mathcal{E}(0)=\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{c}},\quad m(0)=0.

The first relevant TOV equation is given by:

m^{\prime}(r)=4\pi r^{2}\mathcal{E}(r).

(B.6)

Differentiating the expansion for
m(r)
from Eq. (
B.5
) yields:

m^{\prime}(r)=\frac{1}{2}m^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)r^{2}+\frac{1}{24}m^{(5)}(0)r^{4}+\mathcal{O}(r^{6}).

(B.7)

Substituting the expansion for
\mathcal{E}(r)
from Eq. (
B.4
) into the right-hand side of Eq. (
B.6
) gives:

4\pi r^{2}\mathcal{E}(r)=4\pi r^{2}\left(\mathcal{E}_{c}+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(0)r^{2}+\mathcal{O}(r^{4})\right)=4\pi\mathcal{E}_{c}r^{2}+2\pi\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(0)r^{4}+\mathcal{O}(r^{6}).

(B.8)

Equating the coefficients for like powers of
r
from both expressions for
m^{\prime}(r)
:

•

Coefficient of
r^{2}
:

\frac{1}{2}m^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)=4\pi\mathcal{E}_{c}\quad\Rightarrow\quad m^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)=8\pi\mathcal{E}_{c},

(B.9)

•

Coefficient of
r^{4}
:

\frac{1}{24}m^{(5)}(0)=2\pi\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(0)\quad\Rightarrow\quad m^{(5)}(0)=48\pi\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(0).

(B.10)

Thus, the mass function expansion (
B.5
) becomes:

m(r)=\frac{4\pi}{3}\mathcal{E}_{c}r^{3}+\frac{2\pi}{5}\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(0)r^{5}+\mathcal{O}(r^{7}).

(B.11)

The hydrostatic equilibrium is governed by:

P^{\prime}(r)=-\frac{m(r)\mathcal{E}(r)}{r^{2}}\xi(r),

(B.12)

where the relativistic factor
\xi(r)
,

\xi(r)=\left(1+\frac{P(r)}{\mathcal{E}(r)}\right)\left(1+\frac{4\pi r^{3}P(r)}{m(r)}\right)\left(1-\frac{2m(r)}{r}\right)^{-1},

is an even function with
\xi(0)=1
.
Differentiating the pressure expansion (
B.3
) gives:

P^{\prime}(r)=P^{\prime\prime}(0)r+\frac{1}{6}P^{(4)}(0)r^{3}+\mathcal{O}(r^{5}).

(B.13)

To compute the right-hand side of Eq. (
B.12
) near the origin, we use the expansions (
B.11
), (
B.4
), and
\xi(r)=1+\mathcal{O}(r^{2})
:

m(r)\mathcal{E}(r)\xi(r)=\left(\frac{4\pi}{3}\mathcal{E}_{c}r^{3}+O(r^{5})\right)\left(\mathcal{E}_{c}+O(r^{2})\right)\left(1+O(r^{2})\right)=\frac{4\pi}{3}\mathcal{E}_{c}^{2}r^{3}+\mathcal{O}(r^{5}).

(B.14)

Dividing by
r^{2}
:

\frac{m(r)\mathcal{E}(r)\xi(r)}{r^{2}}=\frac{4\pi}{3}\mathcal{E}_{c}^{2}r+\mathcal{O}(r^{3}),

(B.15)

so that

-\frac{m(r)\mathcal{E}(r)\xi(r)}{r^{2}}=-\frac{4\pi}{3}\mathcal{E}_{c}^{2}r+\mathcal{O}(r^{3}).

(B.16)

Equating this with the expansion for
P^{\prime}(r)
:

P^{\prime\prime}(0)r+\frac{1}{6}P^{(4)}(0)r^{3}+\mathcal{O}(r^{5})=-\frac{4\pi}{3}\mathcal{E}_{c}^{2}r+\mathcal{O}(r^{3}),

(B.17)

and comparing the coefficient of
r

P^{\prime\prime}(0)=-\frac{4\pi}{3}\mathcal{E}_{c}^{2}.

(B.18)

Therefore, the pressure expansion is:

P(r)=P_{c}-\frac{2\pi}{3}\mathcal{E}_{c}^{2}r^{2}+\mathcal{O}(r^{4}).

(B.19)

To find
\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(0)
, we use the relation provided by the equation of state,
P^{\prime}=(\differential P/\differential\mathcal{E})\mathcal{E}^{\prime}

B.19

P^{\prime}(0)=0

\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(0)=0

P^{\prime\prime}(0)=\left(\frac{\differential P}{\differential\mathcal{E}}\right)_{c}\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(0),

(B.20)

\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(0)=-\frac{4\pi}{3}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{c}^{3}}{\gamma_{c}P_{c}}.

(B.22)

Thus, the energy density expansion is:

\mathcal{E}(r)=\mathcal{E}_{c}-\frac{2\pi}{3}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{c}^{3}}{\gamma_{c}P_{c}}r^{2}+\mathcal{O}(r^{4}).

(B.23)

Using the mass expansion (
B.11

\frac{m(r)}{r^{3}}=\frac{4\pi}{3}\mathcal{E}_{c}+\frac{2\pi}{5}\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(0)r^{2}+\mathcal{O}(r^{4}),

\frac{6m}{r^{3}}=8\pi\mathcal{E}_{c}+\frac{12\pi}{5}\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(0)r^{2}+\mathcal{O}(r^{4}).

(B.25)

Using the energy density expansion (
B.23

8\pi\mathcal{E}=8\pi\mathcal{E}_{c}+4\pi\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(0)r^{2}+\mathcal{O}(r^{4}).

(B.26)

Subtracting the two expressions within the parentheses of Eq. (
B.2

\frac{6m}{r^{3}}-8\pi\mathcal{E}=\left(8\pi\mathcal{E}_{c}-8\pi\mathcal{E}_{c}\right)+\left(\frac{12\pi}{5}-4\pi\right)\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(0)r^{2}+\mathcal{O}O(r^{4}).

The constant terms cancel identically. Simplifying the

\pi\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(0)\left(\frac{12}{5}-4\right)=-\frac{8\pi}{5}\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(0).

(B.28)

Hence,

\frac{6m}{r^{3}}-8\pi\mathcal{E}=-\frac{8\pi}{5}\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(0)r^{2}+\mathcal{O}(r^{4}).

(B.29)

Substituting this result into Eq. (

\mathcal{W}(r)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\left(-\frac{8\pi}{5}\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(0)r^{2}\right)+\mathcal{O}(r^{4})=-\frac{16\pi}{5\sqrt{3}}\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(0)r^{2}+\mathcal{O}(r^{4}).

\sigma
near the origin

The tangential pressure equation (refer to Eq. (
2.8

P_{\bot}=P_{r}+\frac{\lambda_{\rm BL}}{3}\frac{(\mathcal{E}+3P_{r})(\mathcal{E}+P_{r})r^{2}}{1-\frac{2m}{r}}\,,

(B.32)

Using Eq. (
B.32
), we can define the anisotropy function as

\sigma(r)=P_{\bot}(r)-P_{r}(r)=\frac{\lambda_{\rm BL}}{3}\frac{(\mathcal{E}+3P_{r})(\mathcal{E}+P_{r})r^{2}}{1-\frac{2m}{r}}\,.

(B.33)

We extend the appropriate parameters in the Taylor series in order to examine the behavior closer to the stellar center
(r\to 0)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(r)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{c}+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(0)r^{2}+\mathcal{O}(r^{4}),

(B.34)

\displaystyle P_{r}(r)

\displaystyle=P_{c}-\frac{2\pi}{3}G\mathcal{E}_{c}^{2}\xi_{c}r^{2}+\mathcal{O}(r^{4}),

(B.35)

\displaystyle m(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi}{3}\mathcal{E}_{c}r^{3}+\frac{2\pi}{5}\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(0)r^{5}+\mathcal{O}(r^{7}),

(B.36)

where
\mathcal{E}_{c}
and
P_{c}
are the central energy-density and pressure, respectively, and

\xi_{c}=\left(1+\frac{P_{c}}{\mathcal{E}_{c}}\right)\left(1+\frac{3P_{c}}{\mathcal{E}_{c}}\right).

(B.37)

Expand the numerator and denominator of Eq. (
B.32
) individually. The numerator becomes

(\mathcal{E}+3P_{r})(\mathcal{E}+P_{r})r^{2}=\alpha r^{2}+\mathcal{O}(r^{4}),

(B.38)

with the constant coefficient

\alpha=(\mathcal{E}_{c}+3P_{c})(\mathcal{E}_{c}+P_{c}).

(B.39)

The denominator expands as

\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)^{-1}=1+\frac{8\pi}{3}\mathcal{E}_{c}r^{2}+\mathcal{O}(r^{4}).

(B.40)

The leading-order behavior of the anisotropy function is obtained by combining the numerator and denominator:

\displaystyle\sigma(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{\rm BL}}{3}\cdot\left(\alpha r^{2}+\alpha\cdot\frac{8\pi}{3}\mathcal{E}_{c}r^{4}+\mathcal{O}(r^{6})\right),

(B.41)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{2}\cdot r^{2}+\sigma_{4}\cdot r^{4}+\mathcal{O}(r^{6}),

(B.42)

where

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{\rm BL}}{3}(\mathcal{E}_{c}+3P_{c})(\mathcal{E}_{c}+P_{c}),

(B.43)

\displaystyle\sigma_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi\mathcal{E}_{c}}{3}\cdot\sigma_{2}.

(B.44)

Thus, the anisotropy function acts quadratically toward the center.

\boxed{\sigma(r\to 0)=\sigma_{2}\cdot r^{2}+\sigma_{4}\cdot r^{4}+\mathcal{O}(r^{6})}.

(B.45)
