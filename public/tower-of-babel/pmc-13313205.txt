Epigenetic regulation of non-coding DNA in placental development and disorders
Authors: Ying Hei Kan, Lin Gao, Danny Leung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe human placenta is an epigenetically exceptional organ that must execute rapid proliferation, lineage bifurcation, and controlled invasion while maintaining immune tolerance at the maternal-fetal interface. Trophoblast lineages operate within a developmentally programmed “pseudo-malignant” regulatory state, characterized by global DNA hypomethylation, large partially methylated domains, and dynamic chromatin transitions across gestation. This configuration enables transcriptional plasticity but also creates vulnerability to maternal and environmental exposures, which can leave persistent epigenetic effects associated with fetal growth restriction, preeclampsia, preterm birth, and pregnancy loss. Placental health and disease therefore cannot be understood through protein-coding genes alone. The non-coding genome, comprising promoters, enhancers, enhancer-promoter networks, and non-coding RNAs is extensively rewired in trophoblast, with retrotransposons providing a major source of regulatory innovation. Epigenetic mechanisms coordinate these elements to establish trophoblast-specific transcriptional programs, and perturbation at any layer can disrupt differentiation, invasion, endocrine signaling, and immune modulation. Maternal inflammation, hypoxia, toxins, metabolic and psychological stress reshape epigenetically labile non-coding regions, positioning the placenta as both a developmental sensor and molecular archive of the intrauterine environment. Advances in epigenomic profiling highlight the potential of non-coding epigenetic signatures as early biomarkers, while underscoring ongoing challenges in resolving cell-type-specific regulatory programs and accurately annotating repetitive elements.KEYWORDS: Placenta, pregnancy complications, epigenomics, non-coding DNA, cis-regulatory elements, retrotransposons

Introduction

The human placenta is the first functional organ to form during development and is essential for establishing and maintaining pregnancy. It acts as the interface between the mother and fetus, mediating nutrient and gas exchange, producing hormones that regulate gestation and birth timing, and protecting the fetus from maternal immune cells and environmental stressors [1]. Impaired placental development underlies a broad spectrum of pregnancy complications, including fetal growth restriction (FGR), preeclampsia (PE), preterm birth, and pregnancy loss [2,3]. Despite its transient nature, the placenta undergoes rapid proliferation, controlled invasion, and extensive cellular differentiation. These demanding processes require precise and adaptable gene regulation and make epigenetic mechanisms central to placental development and function.

A defining feature of placental development is the early bifurcation of trophoblast fate into villous and extravillous lineages. During human placentation, cytotrophoblasts (CTs) proliferate and differentiate along these two principal pathways [1,4,5]. In the villous lineage, villous cytotrophoblasts (vCTs) remain associated with the placental villi and undergo cell-cell fusion to form the multinucleated syncytiotrophoblast (ST), which constitutes the primary maternal-fetal exchange surface [6]. In parallel, a subset of CTs differentiates into invasive extravillous trophoblasts (EVTs), which migrate into the maternal decidua. EVTs further specialize into interstitial and endovascular subtypes, the latter of which remodel uterine spiral arteries to establish low-resistance maternal blood flow to the placenta. Disruption of these tightly regulated differentiation and invasion programs is a hallmark of placental pathologies, underscoring the need for finely tuned regulatory control.

The spatiotemporal coordination of trophoblast differentiation relies on an epigenetic landscape that deviates markedly from that of most somatic tissues. At the level of DNA methylation, the placenta exhibits global hypomethylation and contains large partially methylated domains (PMDs), regions that often exceed 100kb in length and covers 37% of the placental genome. This pattern does not indicate epigenetic instability. Instead, it likely supports flexible gene expression and allows cells to adjust quickly during early placental development. Paradoxically, the promoter CpG islands within these hypomethylated domains often exhibit hypermethylation, a regulatory feature shared with some cancers that ensures robust gene silencing [7]. Therefore, the placenta can be viewed as a physiological example of a tightly regulated, developmentally programmed, “pseudo-malignant” epigenetic state [8–12].

Beyond global permissiveness, placental gene regulation also employs specialized epigenetic strategies that are largely absent from embryonic and adult somatic tissues. In addition to canonical genomic imprinting mediated by germline-established DNA methylation, extraembryonic lineages utilize non-canonical imprinting pathways dependent on maternal histone modifications, particularly histone H3 lysine 27 trimethylation (H3K27me3) [13]. This DNA methylation-independent imprinting is largely restricted to extraembryonic tissues and highlights the distinct epigenetic logic governing placental development [14].

Placental epigenetic regulation is highly dynamic across gestation. Early trophoblast populations are characterized by accessible chromatin and enrichment of active histone modifications associated with proliferation and lineage plasticity [15]. As pregnancy progresses, DNA methylation levels increase and chromatin states become more stabilized, coinciding with the functional maturation of trophoblast subtypes specialized for nutrient transport, endocrine signaling, and immune modulation [16,17]. This early plasticity, while beneficial for development, also makes the placenta sensitive to maternal and environmental influences. Hypoxia, nutritional imbalance, metabolic disease, and exposure to toxins can induce lasting epigenetic alterations. This demonstrates how the placenta is both a sensor and a molecular archive that records the history of the intrauterine environment [18]. Disruption of normal epigenetic transitions during these critical windows has been repeatedly associated with placental dysfunction and adverse pregnancy outcomes [19–24]. In addition to supporting fetal growth, the placenta also contributes to early neurodevelopment through the production of neuroactive molecules and regulation of fetal exposure to maternal signals, forming the basis of the proposed placenta-brain axis [25].

While early studies of placental epigenetics focused primarily on protein-coding genes, such approaches capture only a fraction of the regulatory complexity of the placental genome. Less than 2% of the human genome encodes proteins, whereas the vast majority comprises non-coding sequences that govern when, where, and to what extent genes are expressed [26]. In the placenta, this non-coding genome provides a major substrate for regulatory innovation. Transposable elements (TEs), in particular, have been extensively co-opted as placenta-specific promoters, enhancers, and long non-coding RNAs, contributing to lineage-restricted gene regulatory networks in trophoblast differentiation and functions [27–30]. The epigenetic permissiveness of the early placental genome facilitates this TE-derived regulatory activity.

Consistent with this regulatory architecture, genetic studies of placental disorders frequently implicate variants in the non-coding regions rather than protein-altering mutations [31]. Subtle epigenetic perturbations at promoters, enhancers, and other regulatory elements can shift transcriptional outputs beyond physiological thresholds, resulting in disease phenotypes in the absence of overt genetic lesions [32]. Together, these observations underscore the central role of epigenetic regulation of the non-coding genome in placental health and disease. Here, we discuss the current understanding of how epigenetic regulation of the placental non-coding genome shapes normal development and contributes to pregnancy complications.

Epigenomic dysregulation of non-coding genome in placental disorder

Placental promoters

Promoter regulation in the placenta exemplifies the specialized epigenetic logic of extraembryonic development. Unlike most somatic tissues, placental promoters frequently operate under non-canonical regulatory regimes that reflect both unusual patterns of epigenetic inheritance and evolutionary innovation. Two phenomena are particularly prominent in this context. First, placental gene expression is characterized by widespread parent-of-origin-dependent regulation mediated by DNA methylation and histone-based mechanisms, extending beyond classical imprinting paradigms (Figure 1(a,b)). Second, the placenta exhibits extensive exaptation of retrotransposon sequences, which have been co-opted to function as gene promoters and drive lineage-restricted transcriptional programs (Figure 1(c)).

Widespread parent-of-origin dependent promoter regulation

Canonical imprinting at promoters is mediated by germline differentially methylated regions (gDMRs) established during gametogenesis and maintained after fertilization. At these loci, allele-specific DNA methylation directly controls promoter accessibility, with the unmethylated allele acquiring active histone marks such as histone H3 lysine 4 trimethylation (H3K4me3) and recruiting the transcriptional machinery, while the methylated allele remains transcriptionally silent. In the human placenta, this mechanism governs the expression of classical imprinted genes involved in fetal growth, placental transport, and endocrine signaling, including IGF2, H19, PHLDA2, CDKN1C, and PEG10 [32].

Interestingly, genome-wide allele-specific methylome analyses of purified human CTs revealed that imprinting in the placenta is substantially more extensive than in embryonic tissues. This expanded imprinting landscape is a defining feature of eutherian placental evolution; in contrast, metatherian clades exhibit a much lower density of imprinted genes [33,34], suggesting that the extensive imprinting seen in humans is a specialized innovation linked to the development of a highly invasive maternal-fetal interface. More than a quarter of maternally methylated regions retain allele-specific DNA methylation in trophoblasts but lose this asymmetry in embryonic lineages. Notably, many of these placenta-specific DMRs overlap promoters or promoter-proximal regions, supporting a model in which promoter DNA methylation contributes directly to trophoblast-restricted imprinting programs [35].

In addition to DNA methylation-based imprinting, extraembryonic tissues employ non-canonical imprinting mechanisms mediated by maternal histone modifications. A substantial subset of imprinted promoters in mouse extraembryonic lineages is regulated by maternal H3K27me3 deposited during oogenesis, independently of DNA methylation. At these promoters, Polycomb-mediated repression silences the maternal allele, permitting monoallelic paternal expression. This H3K27me3-dependent imprinting is transient. While maintained in preimplantation embryos and extraembryonic tissues, they are erased in embryonic lineages after implantation, indicating that this mode of imprinting contributes specifically to extraembryonic gene regulation rather than to long-term embryonic gene control [13].

The functional importance of histone modification-based promoter imprinting is illustrated by somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT) studies. Mouse SCNT embryos exhibit near-complete loss of H3K27me3-dependent imprinting at promoter regions, resulting in biallelic expression of normally imprinted genes despite largely intact DNA methylation at canonical imprinting control regions. This selective loss correlates with severe post-implantation defects and abnormal placental development, demonstrating that promoter repression mediated by H3K27me3 is indispensable for normal placentation [36].

Consistent with these experimental observations, aberrant imprinting at promoters has been linked to human placental disorders characterized by abnormal fetal growth and placental insufficiency, although distinct epigenetic mechanisms underlie imprinting disruption across disorders. Reduced DNA methylation at the H19/IGF2 imprinting control region (ICR1) has been consistently observed in normotensive FGR placentas, where loss of allele-specific methylation is associated with diminished IGF2 expression and impaired placental growth capacity [32,37]. In contrast, PE-associated FGR term placentas do not uniformly exhibit ICR1 hypomethylation, suggesting that distinct epigenetic mechanisms underlie growth restriction in hypertensive versus normotensive pregnancies [37]. In Third trimester PE placentas, dysregulation at the H19/IGF2 locus has instead been reported at other regulatory segments, including hypermethylation at the H19 promoter or exon 1, accompanied by reduced H19 expression and altered trophoblast proliferation and invasion [38,39]. Similarly, dysregulation of other imprinted promoters within the KCNQ1/CDKN1C domain, including PHLDA2 and CDKN1C, perturbs trophoblast proliferation, placental endocrine function, and maternal blood pressure regulation [40–42]. Notably, many imprinting abnormalities identified in placental disorders occur in the absence of identifiable coding mutations, consistent with primary epimutations or methylation defects at imprinting control regions as key pathogenic drivers [43–45].

Retrotransposon exaptation as promoter innovation

Beyond imprinted loci, the placenta makes extensive use of retrotransposon-derived promoters, reflecting its permissive epigenetic landscape. Retrotransposons are mobile genetic elements capable of replicating themselves in an RNA-mediated copy and paste mechanism and comprise approximately 45% of the human genome [26]. They include long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs), short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs), and long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons [26]. Although most retrotransposons are epigenetically silenced, subsets have been co-opted as functional regulatory elements [46]. Interestingly, mounting evidence suggests retrotransposons are deeply involved in mammalian placental evolution and function. In trophoblast lineages, locus-specific relaxation of these repressive mechanisms enables a restricted subset of retrotransposon sequences to function as alternative promoters, contributing to placenta-specific transcriptional programs [47]. Because many of these retrotransposons are lineage- or clade-specific, their domestication as promoters frequently results in species-specific patterns of placental gene expression.

Among LTR-derived promoters, the THE1D subfamily provides a well-characterized example of ERV exaptation in the human placenta. THE1D is an anthropoid primate–specific ERV whose LTR functions as a promoter for placental expression of the interleukin-2 receptor beta subunit (IL2RB), a gene otherwise confined to hematopoietic lineages. This THE1D-derived promoter is hypomethylated in human placental samples, permitting transcription initiation, while remaining densely methylated and transcriptionally silent in somatic tissues [48]. This locus illustrates how selective promoter activation is achieved through locus-specific DNA hypomethylation rather than global retrotransposon derepression.

Similar principles underlie ERV-derived promoter usage at other placental genes. The CYP19 (aromatase) gene, which encodes the rate-limiting enzyme for estrogen biosynthesis during pregnancy, uses a primate-specific LTR-derived promoter to achieve exceptionally high expression in human STs [49,50]. This promoter is absent in rodents and correlates with the lack of placental Cyp19 expression in mouse, illustrating how ERV promoter domestication contributes to species-specific placental endocrine regulation [49,51]. Likewise, ENTPD1 (CD39) employs an LTR-derived promoter in the placenta that generates a transcript with an ERV-derived N-terminus [50], further demonstrating how ERV exaptation can introduce both regulatory and structural novelty into placental gene products.

In addition to ERVs, non-LTR elements, including LINE-1 and Alu repeats, also contribute to tissue- and species-specific alternative transcription start sites. For instance, the human HYAL4 gene is transcribed primarily in the placenta from a promoter derived from LINE-1 and Alu elements, whereas its mouse orthologue, which lacks these elements, is predominantly expressed in skin and not in the placenta. Similarly, TMPRSS3 in the human genome utilizes alternative promoters derived from a combination of LTR and Alu elements, generating transcript isoforms across distinct tissue types [50]. These cases illustrate how LINE- and Alu-derived promoters can rewire transcriptional initiation in a tissue- and species-specific manner.

Taken together, this evidence suggests that retrotransposon domestication contribute extensively to placental evolution. Genome-wide analyses indicate that genes adopting retrotransposon-derived promoters are enriched for roles in immune regulation, endocrine signaling, and rapidly evolving mammalian traits, whereas genes with highly conserved housekeeping functions are comparatively depleted of such elements [47,50]. This functional bias suggests that retrotransposon-derived promoters are selected for, at genes involved in adaptive or lineage-specific functions, where regulatory diversification may confer evolutionary advantage.

Retrotransposon-derived promoters operate within a tightly regulated epigenetic landscape in the placenta. Despite global hypomethylation, large heterochromatic PMDs impose transcriptional restraint allowing selective TE activation [7,52]. Emerging evidence links dysregulation of retrotransposon-derived promoters to placental pathology. Disruption of this balance may alter transcriptional levels or isoform usage at genes essential for trophoblast invasion, endocrine function, and immune tolerance. Consistent with this model, altered retrotransposon expression and retrotransposon methylation have been reported in placentas from pregnancy complications including PE and FGR. However, causal relationships at individual elements remain to be fully established [53].

Convergence of imprinting and retrotransposon exaptation at placental promoters

Importantly, some retrotransposon-derived promoters are also regulated in a parent-of-origin manner. For instance, in mouse, H3K27me3-mediated non-canonical imprinting in extraembryonic lineages is enriched at ERV-K (ERVK) LTRs that act as imprinted promoters, where allelic activity is pre-determined in the oocyte and subsequently interpreted differently in embryonic versus extraembryonic lineages. ERVK LTR-associated imprinted promoters are CpG-rich and frequently positioned near canonical gene promoters. Maternal H3K27me3 deposited in the oocyte establishes allelic asymmetry before implantation. Post-implantation, extraembryonic tissues maintain this state through secondary maternal DNA methylation, whereas embryonic lineages impose biallelic methylation and complete silencing [14].

A second, mechanistically complementary route links retrotransposon promoters to canonical DNA methylation imprinting through oocyte transcription. Across mammals, many oocyte transcripts initiate within lineage-specific LTRs (“LTR-initiated transcripts,” LITs) that traverse CpG islands and promote de novo DNA methylation in transcribed regions enriched for H3K36me3, generating species- and even strain-specific methylation at promoter CGIs [54]. Functional genetic evidence comes from targeted deletion of specific upstream LTRs that initiate transcription across maternal imprinting gDMRs (igDMRs). At Slc38a4, transcription in mouse oocytes originates from an upstream MT2A LTR, producing an LIT enriched for H3K36me3 that overlaps the igDMR; deletion of the MT2A element causes maternal loss of imprinting and biallelic expression in conceptuses inheriting the deletion maternally. A parallel mechanism operates at Impact, where an upstream MTC element initiates the oocyte LIT spanning the locus [55].

Finally, retrotransposons have not only supplied promoters but also domesticated genes under imprinting control that are essential for placental development. The sushi-ichi-related retrotransposon-derived genes Peg10 and Peg11/Rtl1 exemplify this process. Peg10 is required for placental formation and early development, whereas Rtl1 is crucial for maintaining the feto-maternal interface later in gestation [56,57]. Notably, Rtl1 is embedded within the Dlk1-Dio3 imprinted cluster in a reciprocal configuration – the paternal allele encodes Rtl1, whereas the maternal allele produces an antisense transcript that generates microRNAs targeting Rtl1 mRNA. Disruption of this antisense-derived miRNA network leads to Rtl1 overexpression and placentomegaly, demonstrating the requirement for tight dosage control [57].

Placental enhancers

Placental enhancers constitute a highly dynamic and lineage-restricted regulatory layer that enables rapid transcriptional reprogramming during trophoblast differentiation. Genome-wide chromatin profiling has identified thousands of enhancers marked by H3K4me1 and H3K27ac that are selectively active in trophoblast lineages compared with embryonic or adult somatic tissues, and that are preferentially associated with genes involved in trophoblast invasion, vascular remodeling, endocrine signaling, and immune modulation [31]. These enhancers function in concert with promoters through long-range chromatin interactions, allowing coordinated activation of placental gene programs without extensive remodeling of promoter epigenetic features.

Placental enhancer activity is established through the combinatorial binding of trophoblast-enriched transcription factors, including GATA3, TEAD4, TFAP2C, ELF5, and AP-1 family members, which together define lineage-specific enhancer usage during differentiation [31]. Multiple enhancers frequently converge on key trophoblast genes, forming dense regulatory landscapes that confer robustness and quantitative control over transcriptional output. At the upper end of this continuum, regions with exceptionally high H3K27ac density and transcription factor occupancy, often referred to as super-enhancers, act as regulatory hubs associated with core trophoblast identity genes, supporting stable transcriptional programs in TSCs and CTs [58].

A striking feature of placental enhancers is their rapid evolutionary turnover. Many enhancers active in human trophoblast exhibit weak or no sequence conservation in rodents, in contrast to embryonic enhancers, which are often deeply conserved [27,30]. This pattern suggests that placental gene regulation relies disproportionately on recently evolved, lineage-specific regulatory elements, providing flexibility to accommodate species-specific reproductive strategies.

ERV-derived enhancers

A substantial and mechanistically well-defined fraction of placental enhancers originates from ERVs (Figure 1(d)). The LTRs of ERVs contain dense clusters of transcription factor binding motifs and can be rapidly exapted as enhancers when epigenetic repression is locally relieved. In trophoblast lineages, selective loss of repressive chromatin marks such as DNA methylation, H3K9me3 or H3K27me3 coupled with acquisition of active chromatin marks H3K4me1 and H3K27ac enables a restricted subset of ERVs to function as enhancers, while the majority remain transcriptionally silent, indicating targeted regulatory selection rather than global derepression [27,59].

Distinct ERV subfamilies contribute to enhancer activity in human trophoblast. Many anthropoid primate-specific elements are preferentially enriched for enhancer chromatin signatures in placental tissues [27–30,60]. These ERV-derived enhancers regulate genes involved in ST differentiation, placental endocrine function, immune modulation, and vascular adaptation, highlighting their functional integration into core placental programs rather than incidental transcriptional activity (Table 1).

Retrotransposon-derived regulatory elements in the placenta.

One of the clearest examples of ERV enhancer domestication is provided by an anthropoid-specific THE1B element that functions as an enhancer for CRH, a placental hormone critical for endocrine signaling and the timing of parturition. This enhancer drives placental CRH expression in humans, a feature absent in rodents. Functional evidence for its causal role comes from transgenic mouse models in which insertion of the human THE1B element upstream of Crh results in placental Crh expression and a significant extension of gestational length. These findings demonstrate that acquisition of a single ERV-derived enhancer is sufficient to rewire placental endocrine regulation and alter a species-defining reproductive trait, directly linking ERV enhancer exaptation to the evolution of human gestational timing [28].

Aside from birth timing, ERV enhancers are also implicated in trophoblast differentiation. MER50 elements, for example, exhibit a bivalent-like configuration marked by co-occurrence of H3K9me3 and H3K27ac in first-trimester placenta-derived TSCs, which resolves toward a fully active enhancer state during ST differentiation. These enhancers regulate genes essential for cell fusion and syncytialisation, including MFSD2A and TNFAIP2. Targeted deletion of individual MER50 elements reduces target gene expression and impairs ST formation, demonstrating their direct cis-regulatory function [62].

Importantly, dysregulation of ERV-derived enhancers has been directly linked to placental disease. An LTR10A-derived enhancer regulates expression of ENG, which encodes endoglin, a co-receptor involved in TGF-β signaling and angiogenesis. In cytotrophoblast isolated from PE pregnancies, this enhancer exhibits persistent H3K27ac enrichment and elevated chromatin accessibility, resulting in increased ENG expression and higher circulating levels of soluble endoglin, a key anti-angiogenic factor implicated in disease pathogenesis [29]. This example demonstrates how dysregulation of a single ERV-derived enhancer can contribute directly to a placental disease phenotype. Together, these studies establish ERV-derived enhancers as mechanistically defined, developmentally regulated, and disease-relevant components of the placental regulatory landscape.

Enhancer-promoter interactions in placental regulatory networks

Placental gene regulation emerges from coordinated interactions between promoters and enhancers embedded within lineage-specific chromatin states and three-dimensional genome architecture. Rather than acting as isolated elements, enhancers and promoters function as integrated regulatory units whose activity is shaped by chromatin accessibility, transcription factor occupancy, and higher-order genome organization.

In human TSCs, active genes are embedded within multi-enhancer-promoter interaction networks rather than regulated by isolated linear enhancer-promoter pairs. These networks form contact hubs enriched for H3K27ac-marked enhancers and lineage-defining promoters, providing a structural basis for coordinated transcriptional regulation [64]. These interactions occur largely within topologically associating domains (TADs), which constrain regulatory communication.

ERV-derived enhancers are increasingly recognized as components of placental regulatory landscapes. Members of the ERV3-MLT1 family provide a well-characterized example in human trophoblasts, where they exhibit features of active enhancers, including enrichment for H3K27ac and H3K4me1 and binding of trophoblast-associated transcription factors. These elements are typically located upstream of placentally expressed genes and show coordinated activity with their neighboring promoters, consistent with a role in cis-regulatory control. Functional assays, including reporter analyses and CRISPR-mediated deletion, demonstrate that individual ERV3-MLT1 loci can drive expression of associated target genes such as EPS8L1. Notably, a subset of these ERV-regulated genes is dysregulated in preeclamptic placentas, and their expression is sensitive to epigenetic perturbation, suggesting that altered ERV enhancer activity contributes to disrupted transcriptional programs in disease [60].

Enhancer-promoter interactions are further remodeled during trophoblast differentiation. As cells transition toward the EVT lineage, changes in enhancer usage and chromatin accessibility are accompanied by selective rewiring of enhancer-promoter contacts, enabling activation of gene programs associated with migration and vascular remodeling [65]. Importantly, these regulatory transitions occur largely within preexisting chromatin domains, indicating that placental lineage plasticity is achieved through modulation of regulatory interactions rather than complete reorganization of higher-order genome topology.

Within this framework, disruption of enhancer-promoter communication can perturb transcriptional output even when promoter-proximal chromatin states remain largely intact. Consistent with this, non-coding variants associated with placental disorders are preferentially enriched at enhancers embedded within multi-contact interaction hubs rather than at promoters themselves. Such variants are therefore likely to exert pathogenic effects by altering the strength or specificity of 3D regulatory contacts, providing a mechanistic convergence point between promoter dysregulation (Section 2.1) and enhancer perturbation in placental disease.

Non-coding genes

In addition to cis-regulatory DNA elements, placental gene regulation relies heavily on non-coding genes, including long non-coding RNAs, microRNAs, and pseudogenes. Rather than encoding proteins, these transcripts act as regulatory effectors that translate epigenetic states into functional outcomes at chromatin, transcriptional, and post-transcriptional levels. In the placenta, non-coding genes exhibit pronounced lineage specificity, developmental dynamics, and sensitivity to DNA methylation, histone modifications, and imprinting. Perturbation of these regulatory layers has been linked to placental disorders through disruption of trophoblast invasion, angiogenesis, endocrine signaling, immune tolerance, and stress responses [66,67].

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNA)

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are transcripts longer than 200 nucleotides that regulate gene expression through interactions with chromatin, transcriptional machinery, and RNA-binding proteins [68]. Many lncRNAs act in cis by influencing the expression of neighboring genes, often through recruitment of chromatin-modifying complexes or modulation of local chromatin architecture [69], while others act in trans by scaffolding regulatory proteins or coordinating transcriptional responses at distant loci [70]. In addition, some lncRNAs act as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs), functioning as RNA sponges that sequester miRNAs and modulate post-transcriptional regulation [69]. In the placenta, lncRNAs represent a key interface through which epigenetic states are translated into regulatory output.

Placental lncRNA transcription is tightly coupled to chromatin context. Many lncRNAs arise from genomic regions that also function as promoters or enhancers, including bidirectional promoters and enhancer-associated loci. In these settings, lncRNA expression reflects the activity and chromatin accessibility of the underlying cis-regulatory element, underscoring the integration of regulatory DNA and regulatory RNA in extraembryonic tissues [71,72]. A subset of placental lncRNAs is further controlled by non-canonical imprinting mechanisms mediated by histone modifications such as maternal H3K27me3, highlighting extraembryonic-specific epigenetic strategies [14].

Beyond being epigenetically regulated, lncRNAs actively shape chromatin organization and transcriptional programs. In mouse embryonic stem cells, the maternally expressed Meg3 at the Dlk1-Dio3 locus exemplifies this bidirectional interplay. Meg3 interacts with PRC2 and promotes H3K27me3 deposition at selected targets, reinforcing repression of specific imprinted genes during differentiation. The loss of Meg3 disrupts PRC2 occupancy and H3K27me3 distribution across the locus leading to dysregulated expression of neighboring genes rather than global chromatin alterations [73]. Allele-resolved analyses further demonstrate parent-specific chromatin architecture at Dlk1-Dio3, positioning Meg3 within a three-dimensional regulatory framework that coordinates imprinting, histone modification, and ncRNA output [74]. In PE and FGR, reduced MEG3 expression is associated with increased trophoblast apoptosis and impaired invasion, suggesting that epigenetic instability at this locus perturbs MEG3-dependent chromatin regulation and amplifies transcriptional imbalance [66,67]. Additional lncRNAs such as NEAT1 and MALAT1 influence nuclear architecture and chromatin-associated transcription, and their dysregulation in FGR and PE further suggests that altered chromatin states reshape lncRNA dosage in placental disease [75,76]. Notably, the imprinted lncRNA H19 represents a well-characterized example of a multifunctional regulatory RNA, integrating chromatin-level, transcriptional, and post-transcriptional control mechanisms; its roles are discussed in detail in Section 2.3.3.

Direct histone-mediated activation of lncRNAs can also drive pathology. In PE, increased H3K4me3 enrichment at the lncRNA-HEIPP promoter enhances its transcription, promoting trophoblast apoptosis and suppressing invasion [77], providing a clear mechanistic link between altered histone modification and lncRNA-mediated dysfunction.

At a systems level, placental disease is characterized not merely by isolated lncRNA perturbations but by coordinated disruption of lncRNA-mRNA co-expression modules. These rewired networks often coincide with altered promoter methylation and enhancer accessibility [71], reinforcing the view that lncRNA dysregulation reflects, and may propagate broader epigenetic instability within trophoblast regulatory circuits.

MicroRNA (miRNA)

Placental miRNAs act as network-level regulators that coordinate gene expression across pathways central to trophoblast invasion, angiogenesis, immune modulation, and ST differentiation. They function as post-transcriptional regulators that fine-tune gene dosage by guiding the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) to complementary target mRNAs, leading to transcript degradation or translational repression [78]. Their transcription is epigenetically regulated by promoter DNA methylation and histone modifications, with active miRNA loci enriched for H3K4me3 and H3K27ac. Given the numerous miRNA pathways implicated in placental development and disease, this review highlights selected representative pathways to elucidate key regulatory mechanisms. A distinctive feature of placental miRNA regulation is the presence of large imprinted clusters, most notably C19MC and C14MC. C19MC is paternally expressed and regulated by allele-specific DNA methylation at a CpG-rich promoter upstream of the cluster; the maternal allele is silenced through maintenance methylation, while the active paternal allele exhibits enrichment of H3K4me3 and H3K27ac [66,79,80].

Aberrant epigenetic regulation of placental miRNAs is implicated in disease. Altered DNA methylation at the C19MC locus is associated with PE and FGR [81,82]. C19MC miRNAs suppress EVT migration and invasion by targeting genes involved in cell adhesion and extracellular matrix processing. Overexpression of cluster members reduced EVT motility, whereas inhibition enhanced invasion, indicating that cluster dosage directly restrains the invasive program [83].

Aside from large imprinted clusters, hypoxia-responsive miRNAs, particularly miR-210, are prominently implicated in placental disorders. miR-210 transcription is directly induced by HIF1α binding to hypoxia response elements within the miR-210 promoter [84], and is consistently upregulated in PE placentas and in trophoblasts exposed to hypoxic conditions [85,86]. In trophoblasts, miR-210 overexpression reduces invasion and alters angiogenic signaling, contributing to the anti-angiogenic, metabolically stressed phenotype characteristic of PE [87,88]. Although HIF1α-dependent transcriptional activation is well established, whether chronic miR-210 upregulation in PE reflects stable epigenetic reprogramming of its promoter remains unclear.

Inflammation-associated miRNAs are also epigenetically responsive. miR-155, a key regulator of immune signaling, is upregulated in preeclamptic placentas and inhibits trophoblast invasion by targeting genes involved in nitric oxide signaling and extracellular matrix remodeling. Its expression is inducible by inflammatory pathways, including NF-κB activation, linking maternal immune status to miRNA-mediated post-transcriptional regulation at the maternal-fetal interface [66,89]. Similarly, miR-21, an angiogenesis-associated miRNA, is aberrantly expressed in PE and FGR and modulates trophoblast survival and vascular signaling through repression of PTEN and downstream VEGF pathways [90]. Both miR-155 and miR-21 are subject to promoter-level DNA methylation and histone modification, highlighting epigenetic control as a key determinant of placental miRNA networks [80,91].

In addition to canonical miRNAs, pseudogene-derived transcripts contribute to post-transcriptional regulation by functioning as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs). For example, a FLT1-derived pseudogene transcript modulates soluble FLT1 expression by sequestering shared miRNAs, contributing to the anti-angiogenic environment characteristic of PE [92]. These RNA-mediated buffering mechanisms illustrate how epigenetically regulated non-coding transcription can influence trophoblast gene expression independently of protein-coding sequence alterations.

Interplay between epigenetic modifications and non-coding genes

Placental non-coding genes do not operate independently of chromatin state; rather, their transcription, processing, and regulatory activity are tightly coupled to DNA methylation, histone modifications, and imprinting. In this context, non-coding RNAs function both as downstream effectors of epigenetic regulation and as active participants that reinforce or propagate epigenetic states. The imprinted lncRNA H19 locus exemplifies this interplay.

At the H19/IGF2 locus, allele-specific DNA methylation at the ICR reciprocally regulates H19 and IGF2 expression. Beyond imprinting control, locus-specific methylation within H19 modulates transcriptional output in a gestation- and disease-dependent manner. Increased methylation within H19 is observed in severe PE and is associated with reduced expression, impaired trophoblast proliferation, and diminished invasive capacity [38,39]. Thus, epigenetic perturbation at a promoter directly alters expression and downstream trophoblast behavior.

Functionally, H19 integrates multiple regulatory modalities. At the chromatin level, H19 interacts with methyl-CpG-binding proteins such as MBD1, linking lncRNA activity to DNA methylation and enabling regulation of the imprinted gene network, which includes Igf2, Slc38a4, and Peg10 [93]. At the post-transcriptional level, H19 serves as a precursor for miR-675, which targets NOMO1 and modulates Nodal-SMAD signaling to restrain trophoblast proliferation during placental development [38,94]. In parallel, H19 also functions as a ceRNA by sequestering members of the let-7 miRNA family, thereby buffering repression of let-7 target genes involved in growth and differentiation [95]. Through these mechanisms, a single epigenetically regulated locus coordinates transcriptional, chromatin-level, and post-transcriptional control.

Similar multilayered coupling is observed at other placental loci. The DLK1–DIO3 imprinted domain, which encodes the lncRNA MEG3 and a large cluster of miRNAs, is regulated by allele-specific DNA methylation and Polycomb-associated histone modifications. Disruption of imprinting at this locus alters both lncRNA expression and coordinated miRNA output, contributing to dysregulated trophoblast proliferation and invasion in PE and FGR [66,67]. Likewise, placenta-specific miRNA clusters such as C19MC are controlled by imprinting and DNA methylation, and altered methylation at the cluster level reshapes network-wide post-transcriptional regulation of invasion and angiogenesis pathways.

Taken together, these examples illustrate how disruption at a single regulatory layer, such as aberrant DNA methylation, can propagate through interconnected ncRNA mechanisms to alter signaling pathways and cellular behavior (Figure 1(e)).

Environmental and maternal influences on non-coding DNA regulation

Multiple maternal and environmental exposures converge on the placenta by reshaping the regulatory landscape of the non-coding genome, rather than by directly perturbing protein-coding sequences. These influences operate through diverse but intersecting biological pathways that impact the epigenetics of regulatory sequences.

Immune activation and inflammatory stress

Inflammatory and immune stressors are among the best-characterized environmental inputs affecting placental non-coding DNA (ncDNA) regulation. Maternal immune activation induces rapid and sex-specific changes in placental chromatin state, particularly in heterochromatin organization. In a rat model, acute maternal inflammation increased placental H3K9me3 shortly after exposure, indicating global chromatin compaction and altered repression of repetitive elements, with downstream effects on offspring neurodevelopment and transposable element expression in the brain [96]. Complementary human data demonstrate that maternal SARS-CoV-2 infection remodels placental gene regulation in a cell-type–specific manner. In particular, intronic LTR8B elements that function as enhancers for the PSG gene cluster exhibit reduced chromatin accessibility and H3K27ac enrichment in STs upon infection, linking immune challenge to disruption of ERV-derived cis-regulatory activity at the maternal-fetal interface [63].

Environmental toxicants and chemical exposures

Environmental toxicants exert similarly profound effects on placental development by targeting epigenetic control of non-coding regions. Prenatal exposure to air pollution, heavy metals, and traffic-related particulates has been repeatedly associated with altered placental DNA methylation, ncRNA expression, and chromatin states. Early evidence showed that prenatal lead exposure correlates with hypomethylation of repetitive elements such as LINE-1 and Alu, indicating relaxed repression of non-coding regions that normally contribute to genome stability [97]. Comparable hypomethylation signatures have been reported following exposure to fine particulate matter (PM2.5), with strongest effects observed when exposure occurs during implantation, a developmental window characterized by global DNA methylation erasure and reestablishment [98].

Beyond repetitive elements, environmental exposures also perturb regulatory DNA and ncRNA loci. For example, PM2.5 is associated with reduced DNA methylation at the promoter of LEP, a key placental endocrine gene, an effect partially mediated by oxidative and nitrosative stress [99]. Such stress-responsive epigenetic changes are not restricted to protein-coding genes. Heavy metals, including cadmium, disrupt the expression of placental miRNAs that regulate angiogenic, oxidative stress, and trophoblast differentiation pathways [100,101]. Cadmium exposure also alters lncRNA expression profiles linked to fetal growth outcomes, with increased MIR22HG and ERVH48-1 expression correlating with reduced birth size, and decreased AC114763.2 and LINC02595 expression correlating with increased birth size [102]. Collectively, these findings suggest that environmental toxins do not act solely through direct genetic damage but instead reshape placental transcriptional programs by modifying DNA methylation and chromatin states at regulatory loci, including promoters and ncRNA genes [103,104].

Hypoxia-driven regulation of non-coding RNAs

Hypoxia represents a pervasive environmental pressure that converges on ncDNA regulation in the placenta. While fluctuating oxygen tension is a physiological feature of early placentation, sustained hypoxic stress, arising from impaired spiral artery remodeling or maternal cardiovascular disease, disrupts regulatory programs governing trophoblast differentiation and angiogenesis [105,106].

The hypoxia-induced miRNA miR-210 has emerged as a key non-coding regulator linking low oxygen tension to trophoblast dysfunction. miR-210 is transcriptionally induced by hypoxic signaling through stabilization of HIF-1α, which directly activates the miR-210 promoter; its expression is further reinforced by inflammatory signaling via NF-κB (p50) and by a positive feedback loop in which miR-210 represses GPD1L, thereby stabilizing HIF-1α under sustained hypoxia [66,87,107]. Consistent with this regulatory logic, miR-210 is consistently upregulated in placentas from preeclamptic pregnancies, where hypoxia and oxidative stress are prominent features.

Maternal metabolic and psychological stress

Maternal stress encompasses metabolic, psychological, and inflammatory pressures that can substantially impact placental regulatory systems. Growing evidence shows that these effects are mediated through epigenetic modulation of the non-coding genome. In a mouse model of maternal high-fat diet, placental transcriptomic profiling revealed widespread reprogramming of lncRNA and mRNA expression [108]. Dysregulated placental lncRNAs, including annotated transcripts such as NONMMUT068202 and NONMMUT068203, were co-expressed with genes involved in cytoskeletal organization, metabolic pathways, cell adhesion, and angiogenesis, suggesting that nutritional stress reshapes the coordinated gene expression program in the placenta through lncRNA-mediated mechanisms.

In parallel, maternal psychological stress converges on glucocorticoid signaling, a central component of the placenta-brain axis. Placental 11β-Hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase 2 (HSD11B2) limits fetal glucocorticoid exposure by converting cortisol to inactive cortisone [109], although a fraction of maternal cortisol can still activate glucocorticoid receptors encoded by NR3C1. Consistent with this pathway, placental NR3C1 promoter methylation is associated with infant neurobehavioral outcomes, including attention, self-regulation, habituation, and quality of movement, as well as reduced need for emotional soothing in early postnatal life [110,111]. Similarly, increased HSD11B2 promoter methylation correlates with lower birth weight and reduced quality of movement [112].

Future perspectives and research challenges

With advances in our understanding of the epigenetic regulation of the placental non-coding genome, these elements are increasingly recognized as potential biomarkers for placental disorders. During pregnancy, the maternal circulation contains abundant fetal-origin cell-free RNA (cfRNA), short-lived RNA fragments released from apoptotic and actively secreting cells. Importantly, a substantial proportion of circulating cfRNA during gestation is derived from the placenta, reflecting trophoblast turnover, ST shedding, and active placental gene expression. As a result, maternal plasma cfRNA provides a dynamic, real-time readout of placental transcriptional activity across gestation.

Emerging evidence indicates that cfRNA profiling provides a powerful, non-invasive approach for early identification of PE risk. Distinct cfRNA signatures differentiating normotensive and preeclamptic pregnancies are detectable early in gestation, preceding clinical manifestation, and are enriched for pathways related to vascular, immune, and neuromuscular dysfunction [113]. Complementing cfRNA-based approaches, cell-free DNA (cfDNA) methylome profiling has demonstrated that placenta-derived epigenetic signatures are preserved in maternal circulation and can be leveraged for early risk stratification of PE as early as the first trimester. Genome-wide cfDNA methylation patterns, particularly when integrated with clinical risk factors, enable pre-symptomatic prediction of early-onset disease with 72% sensitivity at 80% specificity, highlighting the potential of epigenetic liquid biopsies to capture regulatory dysfunction underlying placental pathology [114].

While these diagnostic strategies have largely focused on protein-coding genes, the circulating non-coding transcriptome also exhibits dynamic, gestational age–dependent changes in maternal blood. Advances in placental epigenomics have positioned the non-coding genome as a promising source of biomarkers for placental disorders. Placenta-associated lncRNAs, miRNAs, and pseudogene transcripts involved in trophoblast function, angiogenesis, and immune regulation show differential expression across pregnancy, and several of these non-coding RNAs, and their regulatory targets, are dysregulated in placental disorders such as PE and FGR [115]. Because regulatory perturbations at non-coding loci often precede detectable changes in protein-coding gene expression, incorporating ncDNA- and ncRNA-based features into diagnostic frameworks may improve early risk stratification and disease prediction.

Despite this promise, translation of placental non-coding genome biomarkers into clinical practice remains constrained by several unresolved challenges. A fundamental limitation in placental non-coding genome research is sample accessibility and developmental timing. Most human placental samples are obtained postnatally and represent late third-trimester tissue, whereas the most dynamic epigenetic programming events occur during early placentation, when trophoblast lineage specification, invasion, and vascular remodeling are established. Access to first-trimester placental tissue is limited and often biased toward pathological pregnancies, complicating the definition of appropriate developmental baselines. Although animal models have been invaluable for dissecting general principles of placental biology, their relevance to human placental regulation is inherently constrained by the extreme evolutionary diversity of placental structure, gene regulation, and non-coding genome composition across mammals. Many regulatory elements central to human placental function, including ERV-derived promoters and enhancers, are lineage-specific and absent from commonly used model organisms, limiting the translatability of animal findings to human disease. While TSCs, organoids, and directed differentiation systems offer improved experimental tractability in human contexts, these models still incompletely recapitulate the complex maternal-fetal interface, including immune interactions, oxygen gradients, and biomechanical cues present in vivo.

A second challenge arises from the cellular heterogeneity of the placenta. The tissue comprises multiple trophoblast subtypes as well as stromal, endothelial, and immune cell populations, each with distinct transcriptional and epigenetic profiles. Bulk transcriptomic and epigenomic assays average signals across cell types and can obscure lineage-specific regulatory programs, particularly when changes occur in rare but functionally critical populations such as EVTs and STs. Although single-cell and single-nucleus transcriptomic and epigenomic technologies and recent advancement in spatial transcriptomic technologies have begun to address this limitation, integrating multi-omic single-cell data across developmental stages and disease states remains analytically complex and computationally demanding.

A third obstacle is the incomplete functional annotation of the placental non-coding genome. While large consortia have cataloged promoters, enhancers, and chromatin states across many tissues, placental regulatory elements, particularly those active only during early gestation, remain underrepresented. This limitation is especially pronounced for long non-coding RNAs, pseudogenes, and context-dependent regulatory elements, whose functions are often inferred indirectly rather than experimentally validated.

Finally, repetitive elements pose persistent technical challenges. Transposable elements constitute a substantial fraction of the placental regulatory landscape, yet their high sequence similarity complicates read alignment and quantification. Standard pipelines frequently discard multi-mapped reads, leading to systematic underestimation of repetitive element activity. Emerging approaches, including tools such as SQuIRE [116], TEtranscripts [117], SAMA [63,118] and soloTE [119], enable rescue of multi-mapped reads and permit subfamily-level analyses, but methodological standardization and benchmarking are still needed. In parallel, TE-focused transcript discovery pipelines such as TEProf3 permit systematic identification of TE-derived promoters and chimeric TE-gene transcripts, revealing context-specific regulatory activation. Nevertheless, benchmarking and methodological standardization remain limited [120]. Recent advances further emphasize the need for repeat-aware frameworks. Phylogenetic reconstruction has uncovered cryptic endogenous retrovirus subfamilies masked under conventional annotations, refining ERV classification and evolutionary inference [121]. Graph-based genome approaches such as GraffiTE improve detection of TE insertion polymorphisms by leveraging pangenome references [122]. The draft human pangenome reference improves representation of structurally diverse and repetitive regions that are incompletely captured in GRCh38 [123], while analyses of nearly complete human genomes generated by long-read sequencing demonstrate widespread, previously unresolved structural variation within transposable elements [124]. Long-read platforms such as PacBio and Oxford Nanopore therefore offer critical advantages for resolving full-length retrotransposon transcripts, allele-specific insertions, and structurally complex loci. Integration of TE-aware quantification, pangenome references, and long-read sequencing will be essential for accurately defining the repetitive regulatory landscape of the placenta.

Collectively, recent discoveries show that the epigenomic regulation of the placental non-coding genome play central roles in developmental and pathological processes. Progress in this field will depend on moving beyond descriptive catalogs toward integrative frameworks that link regulatory DNA, non-coding RNAs, chromatin organization, and cellular context across placental development. As multi-omic, single-cell, and functional approaches mature, they are expected to refine causal inference and shed light on both tissue-based and circulating biomarkers. Ultimately, such advances will be essential for translating insights from the placental non-coding genome into clinically robust strategies for early detection and management of placental disorders.
