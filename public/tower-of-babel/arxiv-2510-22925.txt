Gauss Principle in Incompressible Flow: A Unified Variational Perspective on Pressure and Projection
Karthik Duraisamy

Abstract
Following recent work (Gonzalez & Taha 2022; Peters & Ormiston 2025), this manuscript clarifies what the Gauss–Appell principle determines in incompressible, inviscid flow and how it connects to classical projection methods. At a fixed time, freezing the velocity and varying only the material acceleration leads to minimization of a quadratic subject to acceleration-level constraints. First-order conditions yield a Poisson–Neumann problem for a reaction pressure whose gradient removes the non-solenoidal and wall-normal content of the provisional residual, i.e. the Leray–Hodge projection. Thus, Gauss–Appell enforces the instantaneous kinematic constraints and recovers Euler at the instant . In steady flows, this principle cannot select circulation or stagnation points because these are properties of the velocity state, not the instantaneous acceleration correction. The principle only determines the reaction pressure for an already-specified velocity field. The impressed/reaction pressure decomposition can be supplemented with orthogonality conditions, allowing physical inputs (e.g. circulation, freestream) to be embedded while maintaining exact constraint enforcement. This variational viewpoint provides a diagnostic for computational incompatibility: In a practical computation, spikes in projection effort may signal problematic boundary conditions or under-resolution, and clarifies how pressure instantaneously maintains solenoidality without creating or destroying vorticity. The goal of this note is simply to lend more clarity to the application of the Gauss principle, and to connect it concretely to well known concepts including potential flow theory, projection algorithms, and recent variational approaches.

The application of variational principles to fluid mechanics has a rich history spanning over 150 years, beginning with Helmholtz’s vortex theorems (1858) and Kelvin’s circulation theorem (1869). Classical approaches typically employed Hamilton’s principle, extremizing action integrals over time to derive the equations of motion through calculus of variations
(
Holm et al. 1998
;
Truesdell & Noll 2004
;
Marchioro & Pulvirenti 2012
)
. These time-integral formulations, while mathematically elegant, vary entire space-time paths and thus provide limited insight into the instantaneous mechanics of constraint enforcement in incompressible flows.

(
Holm et al. 1998
;
Truesdell & Noll 2004
;
Marchioro & Pulvirenti 2012
)
.

A fundamentally different variational approach emerged from Gauss’s principle of least constraint
(
Gauss 1829
)
, originally developed for constrained mechanical systems. Unlike Hamilton’s principle which varies paths over time, Gauss’s principle operates at a fixed instant, minimizing a quadratic form in accelerations subject to acceleration-level constraints. This instantaneous character makes it particularly suited to understanding how pressure maintains incompressibility moment by moment. Despite its potential, the principle remained largely unexplored in fluid mechanics for over a century. Recent works
(
Gonzalez & Taha 2022
;
Taha & Shorbagy 2025
;
Peters & Ormiston 2025
)
have brought back a variational lens on fluid flows, particularly revisiting the Gauss principle in the context of the airfoil lift problem.

Despite the renewed interest, there remains confusion about what a Gauss/Appell least‑constraint statement actually determines in fluids, and how it can be utilized. We provide a compact variational interpretation of the pressure Poisson equation, clarify how to embed physical inputs via an ‘impressed’ subspace, and give a corollary that places the projection method in numerical hydrodynamics
(
Chorin 1968
)
under Gauss/Appell.

Gonzalez & Taha 2022
minimize a steady reduced functional on a kinematically admissible manifold (e.g., a potential‑flow family parameterized by circulation), a legitimate model‑reduction device but not the instantaneous Gauss principle.
Taha & Shorbagy 2025
recast this as a “minimum pressure‑gradient” criterion as a selection mechanism for weak Euler solutions—again a steady or quasi‑steady choice layered on top of the equations.
Peters & Ormiston 2025
correctly argue that least constraint, by itself, cannot resolve classical indeterminacies of steady exterior potential flow.

This work reconciles these viewpoints by showing precisely where Gauss/Appell ends (fixed‑time projection) and where additional modeling/selection begins (steady‑state choice of manifold or boundary data). The Gauss principle is a fixed-time variational statement: one freezes the velocity field
and varies the material acceleration.
Minimizing a positive‑definite “Appellian” in the admissible accelerations yields first order optimality conditions that are exactly the Leray–Hodge projection of the residual onto gradient fields; the Lagrange multiplier is a reaction pressure obtained from a Poisson–Neumann problem whose gradient removes the non‑solenoidal part of the residual. In steady flow, this principle does not select among kinematically admissible steady states; it only determines the pressure compatible with the given
velocity field. These points are well known from projection methods but are often obscured in recent discussions; we make the equivalence and its scope precise.

Further, the non-uniqueness of the impressed vs. constraint split is clarified, and
the decomposition of
Peters & Ormiston 2025
is substantiated via an energy-orthogonality
condition that selects a unique decomposition relative to a chosen impressed subspace, and by connecting this result to classical projection.
It is shown however, that while physically intuitive choices can be made for the impressed subspace, the choice itself is arbitrary.

Unlike the principle of least action, Gauss/Appell is a least‑deviation principle in the accelerations at a given instant. In this sense, it is clarified that Gauss/Appell provides a unifying variational interpretation of the pressure Poisson solve—useful analytically and practically, particularly when one wishes to embed physical inputs (e.g., circulation or far‑field content) through an impressed subspace while keeping the constraints exact. We present rigorous derivations in both Eulerian and Lagrangian, and provide insight into the physical implications of the Gauss principle.

3
Physical implications and insight

At a fixed instant, the Gauss–Appell step has a concrete physical meaning: it chooses the
smallest
admissible change to the material acceleration that restores the instantaneous momentum balance while preserving incompressibility and wall non-penetration. The only agency it has is a reaction field,
-\nabla P_{R}/\rho
, whose role is to remove whatever divergence and normal flux the provisional residual carries. The pressure is a
reaction
that instantaneously enforces the kinematics; it does no work on divergence-free, wall-tangent motions. This point is correctly argued by
Gonzalez & Taha 2022
. After elimination, the minimized Appellian
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
becomes a quantitative measure of the
projection effort
,
i.e. how hard the constraint must “push” to keep the instantaneous update compatible with incompressibility and wall tangency.

3.1
Steady flow

It is emphasized that in a steady flow, the Gauss principle of least constraint finds the gradient one must subtract from the “residual”

\rho(u\cdot\nabla)u+\nabla P_{F}

so that the momentum equation holds. There is no update to
u

(the minimizer gives
u_{t}^{\ast}=0
)
and no selection among multiple steady Euler fields that satisfy the kinematics. In other words, no selection of extra physics occurs.

Gonzalez & Taha 2022
restrict the velocity field to a steady potential-flow family
u(\cdot;\Gamma)\triangleq u_{0}+\Gamma u_{1}

whose members satisfy continuity and wall tangency, and then
define

S(\Gamma)\triangleq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\rho\,\big|\,u\!\cdot\!\nabla u\,\big|^{2}\,dV,

seeking
\Gamma^{\star}=\arg\min_{\Gamma}S(\Gamma)
. This is a legitimate
reduced-model

optimization on a one-parameter manifold of kinematically admissible steady flows and can, in practice,
pick a
\Gamma
consistent with certain steady configurations. It can be used as a closure condition. However, it is
not

the instantaneous Gauss/Appell principle; it presupposes
steadiness and a particular parameterization. In the full Gauss setting,
the state
u
is frozen, the variable is
u_{t}
, and the outcome is the
constraint projection

P_{R}
(§
2
), not the selection of
\Gamma
. In the steady case, for every

\Gamma
there exists a pressure
that makes
(u(\cdot;\Gamma),p(\cdot;\Gamma))
a steady Euler solution. Applying Gauss at the instant then yields

u_{t}^{\ast}=0
for every
\Gamma
. Therefore, the principle does not (and cannot) decide which
\Gamma
nature takes. In fact, in the steady flow case, the application of the Gauss principle does not yield
any
new information other than the fact that the reaction pressure can be determined.

Taha & Shorbagy 2025
propose the ‘Principle of Minimum pressure gradient’, and offer
useful reduced‑manifold selections
that align with classical expectations (Kutta condition on nearly sharp airfoils, Glauert’s rapidly rotating cylinder, empirical separation angles). An important distinction is required to interpret scope: Gauss/Appell provides the fixed‑time projection; selection over steady states requires additional modeling choices.

In the unsteady case, since
u_{t}
(hence
a
) is varied with
u

held fixed, acceleration-level constraints are enforced, resulting in the reaction pressure via the Poisson–Neumann problem (
5
).
This exactly recovers Euler at the instant and clarifies the roles of impressed vs constraint
pressure. As argued by
Peters & Ormiston 2025
, this also explains why least constraint cannot, by itself, resolve the classical
indeterminacy of steady exterior potential flow. That indeterminacy
lives in the
state
manifold, not in the instantaneous constrained acceleration update.

3.2
Impressed/constraint split is arbitrary

Let
\psi
be a smooth field (e.g.
\psi=\Gamma\,\psi_{s}
representing
a circulation mode). Define a new split
\widetilde{P}_{F}\triangleq P_{F}+\psi,\quad\widetilde{P}_{R}\triangleq P_{R}-\psi.

Then

\widetilde{C}=(u\!\cdot\!\nabla)u+\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla\widetilde{P}_{F}=C+\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla\psi,

and the first order optimality condition becomes

\rho\,(u_{t}^{\star}+\widetilde{C})+\nabla\widetilde{P}_{R}=\rho\,(u_{t}^{\star}+C)+\nabla P_{R}=0,

so
the optimizer
u_{t}^{\star}
is invariant
. Likewise

p=\widetilde{P}_{F}+\widetilde{P}_{R}
is unchanged. Hence putting the circulation
parameter
\Gamma
“inside
P_{R}
” or “inside
P_{F}
” produces the same physical

(u_{t}^{\star},p)

is not unique

Similarly,

\nabla^{2}\widetilde{P}_{R}=-\rho\,\nabla\!\cdot C-\nabla^{2}\psi,\ \partial_{n}\widetilde{P}_{R}=-\rho\,C\!\cdot n-\partial_{n}\psi
.
Therefore
\widetilde{P}_{R}=P_{R}-\psi+\text{const}
, exactly as above.
So the Poisson step leaves

invariant. Thus, in incompressible Euler at a fixed time,
any decomposition

leaves momentum balance unchanged. This simple observation allows one to assign an
impressed
component
P_{F}
and let the
constraint

P_{R}
enforce the acceleration‑level constraints, with no change to the physical solution.
Once
P_{R}
is solved, the
optimal

u_{t}^{\star}=-(C+\nabla P_{R}/\rho)
, and the instantaneous Euler balance
\rho(\partial_{t}u+(u\!\cdot\!\nabla)u)=-\nabla(P_{F}+P_{R})

3.3
Uniqueness of Dirichlet Orthogonality

Peters & Ormiston 2025
address the above arbitrariness by proposing an intuitive orthogonal decomposition

\int_{\Omega}\nabla P_{R}\cdot\nabla P_{F}\,dV=0

(6)

In Appendix A, it is shown that given any provisional
solution
(\widehat{P}_{F},\widehat{P}_{R})
, the
unique
orthogonal pair is obtained by a correction:

P_{R}=\widehat{P}_{R}-\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}\psi_{j},\qquad P_{F}=\widehat{P}_{F}+\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}\psi_{j}.

The coefficients
\alpha_{j}
are the orthogonal
projection of
\widehat{P}_{R}
onto the impressed subspace

\mathcal{S}_{\text{imp}}=\text{span}\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{m}\}
. We are
subtracting this projection from
P_{R}
(making it orthogonal to

\mathcal{S}_{\text{imp}}
) and adding it to
P_{F}
(so the total pressure
remains unchanged).

In other words, one can choose

1.

What

\mathcal{S}_{\text{imp}}=\text{span}\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{m}\}

is — which pressure modes one may want to impress (e.g., circulation patterns)

2.

How much
of each mode to impress, typically by specifying boundary conditions
or forcing terms

The Dirichlet orthogonality condition then ensures consistent bookkeeping when
decomposing the
resulting
total pressure
p
(which emerges from the full
flow physics).
\mathcal{S}_{\text{imp}}
is the “signature” of the
forcing mechanism. Dirichlet orthogonality thus labels as forcing (
P_{F}
) everything
that has this signature; and labels as constraint (
P_{R}
) everything orthogonal to it
(in the Dirichlet sense). We further note that the above is exactly analogous to the Helmholtz–Leray projection operator
\mathbb{P}
operating on any given velocity field

:

\mathbb{P}w\;=\;w-\nabla q,\qquad\nabla^{2}q=\nabla\!\cdot w\ \ \text{in }\Omega,\qquad\partial_{n}q=w\!\cdot\!n\ \ \text{on }\partial\Omega.

Equivalently,
\mathbb{P}w
is the unique vector that satisfies orthogonality

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{P}w\cdot\nabla\phi\,\mathrm{d}x=0
. This is a well-known result (See for instance

), but the connection to the impressed/constraint split is new. Appendix B clarifies another point relevant to the projection.

3.4
Physical choice of impressed subspace

For the exterior airfoil problem the physical implications are immediate. If
S_{\rm imp}
contains the potential-flow circulation mode, taking
P_{F}=\Gamma\,\psi_{s}
neatly encodes the circulation input while leaving
P_{R}
to enforce the constraints for the actual frozen velocity.
However, we now show that even with Dirichlet orthogonality, the choice of impressed subspace is highly arbitrary.

1.

Circulation basis .
Choose
\mathcal{S}_{\!\rm imp}=\mathrm{span}\{\psi_{s}\}
so that
\nabla\psi_{s}
represents the potential–flow
circulation mode
(the harmonic field tangent to the wall with unit loop integral). Then
P_{F}=\Gamma\,\psi_{s}
carries precisely the circulation degree of freedom emphasized in
Gonzalez & Taha 2022
, and
P_{R}
enforces the constraints. This matches the “circulation‑aware” split described in
Peters & Ormiston 2025
.

2.

Freestream split.

It is also
legitimate
to designate the freestream direction as impressed. A minimal way is to include the linear potential
\phi_{\infty}(x)=U_{\infty}\!\cdot x
among the generators and let
\psi_{\infty}:=\rho\,\phi_{\infty}
. Then
\nabla P_{F}
may contain the constant vector
\rho U_{\infty}
. This merely tilts the “target” in
C=(u\!\cdot\!\nabla)u+\nabla P_{F}/\rho
; the Poisson step automatically compensates so that
u_{t}
remains divergence‑free and wall‑tangent. There is no requirement that this impressed piece satisfy the wall condition—it need not, because only
u_{t}
is constrained.

3.

Full potential reference.
One may also place the entire steady Bernoulli gradient for a chosen reference potential flow into
P_{F}
(e.g. the zero‑circulation solution
u_{0}
or
u_{0}+\Gamma u_{1}
). In steady irrotational settings,
(u\!\cdot\!\nabla)u=\nabla(\tfrac{1}{2}|u|^{2})
; taking
P_{F}=\rho\,\tfrac{1}{2}|u_{\rm ref}|^{2}
pushes that Bernoulli content into the impressed side and leaves
P_{R}
to enforce the constraints for the
actual
frozen
u
.

The latter two results are particularly important given the criticism of
Peters & Ormiston 2025
of
Gonzalez & Taha 2022
’s derivation as
incorrect
because they ignored impressed pressure. While in some cases (e.g. accounting for body forces due to gravity), impressed forces can be clearly delineated, it is indeed not
incorrect
to consider the entire field as non-impressed in the typical airfoil scenario. In other words, even if one impresses the uniform stream, or even a full steady potential reference, the same mechanism applies: the Gauss step removes only what is needed to satisfy the constraints and leaves the impressed content intact. In all cases the reaction is irrotational and hence cannot create or destroy vorticity; it instantaneously redistributes pressure to maintain solenoidality and wall compatibility while vorticity evolves through advection (and any impressed physics).

Finally, this viewpoint unifies interpretation and practice. The standard predict–Poisson–correct algorithm is precisely the Gauss–Appell step on a grid, and
\mathcal{A}^{\star}
furnishes a diagnostic that can be monitored in computation: spikes signal incompatible boundary data, under-resolved features, or an unhelpful choice of impressed content. In reduced-order or data-assisted settings, the choice of
S_{\rm imp}
allows trusted physics to be injected while the same projection guarantees exact enforcement of incompressibility and wall conditions.

Appendix B: Rank and Projection

Peters & Ormiston 2025
cast multipliers via a
finite‑dimensional
Lagrange‑multiplier theorem, tying the number of independent multipliers to the
rank
of a constraint Jacobian. That language is useful but does not transfer cleanly to the PDE setting of incompressible flow, where the constraint is an
operator
(
\nabla\!\cdot
) and the multiplier
P_{R}
is an
entire field
. What replaces “rank” is the orthogonal
projection
onto the divergence‑free, wall‑tangent subspace (Leray projector), with the multiplier recovered as the gradient part in the Helmholtz–Hodge decomposition; see the Euler/Appellian equivalence proof in
Gonzalez & Taha 2022
. In other words, pressure acts as the constraint that removes the non‑solenoidal content of
\rho(u_{t}+C)
, and the correct mathematical object is a projection, and not a finite matrix rank.
