Programmable in vivo mRNA circularization for enhanced gene expression in bacteria
Authors: Seongjun Park, Giho Kim, Min Ha Kim, Yong Hee Han, Geunyung Park, Gibyuck Byun, Hyeonwoo Mun, Ho Joon Kim, Sang Woo Seo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe minute-scale lifetime of mRNA strongly influences bacterial gene expression, whereas a robust and programmable approach to directly control the mRNA stability and topology remains elusive. Here, we develop CRESEnT (Circular RNA Expression for Stable and Enhanced Translation), a programmable in vivo mRNA circularization system based on a permuted intron–exon architecture to engineer mRNA topology. CRESEnT enables facile circularization of mRNA, which led to a substantial increase in protein expression across diverse promoters, RBS variants, genetic cargos, and bacterial hosts. Furthermore, application of CRESEnT to biosynthetic pathways increased the production of several value-added metabolites, demonstrating that mRNA circularization can be harnessed to improve the metabolic performance of microbial cell factories. Together, these results establish RNA topology engineering via circularization as a transformative axis for controlling bacterial gene expression and enhancing the functionality of microbial cells.

Introduction

The advancement of microbial synthetic biology has enabled precise and programmable control of gene expression in living cells, thereby optimizing their use as cell factories for producing several recombinant proteins and value-added metabolites [1–3]. To maximize yields, efforts have traditionally focused on designing genetic contexts affecting transcription and translation, including promoters, ribosome binding site (RBS), and codon distribution [4]. Despite these advances, the intrinsic instability of linear mRNA in bacterial cells remains a major limitation to controlling gene expression in bacteria, as bacterial transcripts typically have minute-scale half-lives due to rapid decay in the intracellular environment [5]. Unlike eukaryotic mRNAs, which are protected by features such as the 7-methylguanosine cap, poly-A tail, and associated RNA-binding proteins, bacterial mRNAs generally lack protective elements and are therefore highly susceptible to degradation by endogenous RNases [6–8]. Although the rapid turnover of mRNA in bacteria confers advantages for adaptation to fluctuating environments, it is less desirable in microbial cell factories operating under relatively constant bioreactor conditions [9, 10]. Therefore, several approaches to modulate mRNA degradation have been investigated in bacteria [11, 12]. These efforts, however, still rely on linear transcripts, which remain vulnerable to exonucleolytic decay. Strategies for the fundamental transformation of RNA topology in microbial cells have been largely unexplored.

RNA circularization offers a potential solution to this limitation by conferring structural resistance to RNases through a topological transformation that shields the transcript from exonuclease-mediated degradation [13, 14]. In biological systems, RNA circularization has been explored using approaches such as the permuted intron–exon (PIE) system, which is based on the self-splicing of group I introns, including the Td intron from T4 bacteriophage [15–18]. While these efforts have successfully demonstrated the expression of non-coding RNAs and repetitive proteins, the universal application of mRNA circularization to enhance the expression of diverse functional proteins in bacteria remains limited, despite substantial progress in mammalian systems [19, 20]. Moreover, ribosome loading onto mRNA and its circularization occur simultaneously in bacterial cells due to the co-localization of transcription and translation, underscoring the distinct optimization strategy in bacteria compared to mammalian cells. Therefore, achieving robust protein expression via mRNA circularization requires an expression cassette for both efficient ribosome loading and effective circularization mediated by appended RNA elements.

Here, we develop an in vivo circular mRNA expression system, CRESEnT (Circular RNA Expression for Stable and Enhanced Translation), a robust platform that enhances gene expression through programmable mRNA circularization in bacteria. Using the T4 bacteriophage Td intron as a scaffold, we systematically optimize the expression cassette architecture by identifying optimal lengths for the 5′- and 3′-untranslated regions (UTRs) and introducing complementary hybridization arms to promote efficient splicing. We show that CRESEnT enhances protein expression across diverse synthetic promoter and RBS contexts. Furthermore, we validate the versatility of CRESEnT by successfully enhancing the expression of diverse genetic cargos, ranging from standard fluorescent reporters to large genetic cargos up to 3.5 kb in length. We elucidate the broad compatibility of this system beyond Escherichia coli, demonstrating functionality in multiple bacterial hosts, including Bacillus subtilis and Corynebacterium glutamicum. Finally, we apply this system in metabolic engineering to express biosynthetic genes and successfully enhance the microbial production of several value-added metabolites. Together, these results establish CRESEnT as a generalizable strategy that enables RNA topology engineering for enhancing mRNA stability, protein expression, and metabolic capacity in living microbes.

Construction of the CRESEnT cassettes

Mach1-T1R was used for plasmid construction and cultured in LB medium containing appropriate antibiotics. The pACYC_CRESEnT_core was assembled from three PCR fragments, comprising the 5′ split intron, the mCherry reporter, and the 3′ split intron. The Td intron sequence was synthesized as an IDT gBlock (Integrated DNA Technologies) and used as the template for both split intron fragments. For the construction of core CRESEnT, the 5′ split intron fragment was amplified by Td_5′int_F and Td_5′int_R, and the 3′ split intron fragment was amplified by Td_3′int_F and Td_3′int_R. The mCherry reporter gene fragment was amplified with a SalI and SacI restriction site at the 5′ and 3′ ends, respectively. For the construction of pACYC_LIN_core, the ECS-deleted 3′ split intron fragment was amplified by Td_3′int_LIN_F and Td_3′int_R, whereas the other two fragments were identical to those used for pACYC_CRESEnT_core-mCherry. These three PCR fragments were assembled into the pACYCDuet backbone using Gibson assembly. The split Td intron sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

To generate CRESEnT variants with different UTR lengths, PCR amplification was performed using primers with overhangs corresponding to the desired UTR lengths, with pACYC_CRESEnT_core as the template. For 5′ UTR variants, fragments were amplified using mCherry_5′_F (5′-phosphorylated using T4 PNK) and Td_5′int_Xbp_R primers. For 3′ UTR variants, fragments were amplified using Td_3′int_Xbp_F and mCherry_3′_R (5′-phosphorylated using T4 PNK) primers. Linear PCR-amplified plasmid fragments were circularized by blunt-end ligation using Quick Ligase. For hybridization arm variants, two PCR fragments were generated: one using Td_5′int_hyb_Xbp_F and Td_3′int_hyb_Xbp_R primers, and the other using J23100_R and T7Term_Gibson_F primers. These fragments were assembled using Gibson assembly to reconstruct the full CRESEnT cassette, including the desired hybridization arm sequence. The UTR and hybridization arm sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Promoter variants were constructed by PCR amplification of the optimized CRESEnT cassette using either pACYC_J23XXX_R or pACYC_PT7var_R together with Td_hyb30_F, followed by blunt-end ligation to recircularize the linear plasmid. RBS variants were designed using the Salis Lab RBS Calculator (De Novo DNA) [22]. RBS-modified cassettes were generated by Gibson assembly of two PCR fragments; one fragment was amplified using RBS_X_mCherry_F and mCherry_3′_R, and the other fragment was amplified using RBS_X_mCherry_R and mCherry_3′_F. The RBS sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

To replace the gene of interest, the optimized CRESEnT cassette was digested with SacI and SalI. mTagBFP and sGFP inserts were PCR-amplified using primers containing SacI and SalI restriction sites, digested with the same enzymes, and ligated into the vector using Quick ligase. To generate constructs expressing E. coli K-12 MG1655 endogenous proteins fused to mCherry through a flexible linker, we first generated a cloning backbone plasmid, pACYC_PT7_CRESEnT/LIN_BsaI-mCherry, containing an N-terminal 6× His tag, a BsaI site, a flexible linker, and mCherry under the T7 promoter. Using pACYC-PT7-CRESEnT/LIN as the template, two PCR fragments were amplified with the primer pairs mCherry_3′_F / NHis_BsaI_GA_R and BsaI_DSAG_GA_F / mCherry_3′_R. These fragments were assembled into the EcoNI- and Bsu36I-digested pACYCDuet backbone by Gibson assembly. Endogenous genes were amplified using primers listed in Supplementary Table S2 as X-mCh_BsaI_F and X-mCh_BsaI_R, and inserted into the backbone by Golden Gate assembly using BsaI and T4 DNA ligase.

To evaluate CRESEnT in B. subtilis, a single colony was resuspended in distilled water, boiled at 95°C for 10 min, and used directly as the template for PCR amplification of the Pveg fragment using Pveg_F and Pveg_R. The pCG004 plasmid (Addgene plasmid #87377) was PCR-amplified using pCG004_rrnBterm_GA_F and pCG004_Pveg_GA_R. The optimized CRESEnT cassette was amplified using Td_5′int_Pveg_F and Td_3′int_rrnBterm_R. These three fragments were assembled by Gibson assembly.

To assess CRESEnT in C. glutamicum, a GFP-expressing CRESEnT cassette was assembled into pCDF-pHM1519mut [23] plasmid by Gibson assembly using the digested vector with EcoNI and AvrII and two PCR fragments amplified from pACYC_CRESEnT/LIN_GFP; the first fragment is amplified by H1_Psyn_GA_F and GFP_GA_R, and the second fragment is amplified by GFP_GA_F and H2_pHMmut_GA_R. The resulting pCDF-pHM1519mut_Psyn_CRESEnT/LIN_GFP was transformed into the C. glutamicum ATCC 13032.

To generate constructs for metabolite production, the pACYC_PT7_CRESEnT/LIN_BsaI-mCherry plasmids, or pACYC_PT7v2_CRESEnT/LIN_BsaI-mCherry for lycopene production, were digested with BsaI and SacI. The rppA fragment was synthesized by Twist Bioscience, and amplified using rppA_BsaI_F and rppA_SacI_R. The cadA, crtE, and vioD fragments were amplified from pCDF-cadA [24], p1EBI1 [25], and pdCas9-vioABCDE [26], respectively, using primers containing BsaI and SacI recognition sites designed to generate compatible sticky ends upon digestion. These fragments were digested by BsaI and SacI, and the digested vector and insert fragments were ligated using Quick Ligase. For lycopene and violacein production, the pETDuet backbone plasmid was digested with EcoNI and AvrII to generate the cloning backbone. To generate the pET_vioAEBC plasmid, the vioA, vioE, vioB, and vioC fragments were amplified from pdCas9-vioABCDE, digested with the appropriate restriction enzymes, and ligated with the EcoNI/AvrII-digested pETDuet backbone using Quick ligase.

Measurement of reporter gene expression in E. coli, B. subtilis, and C. glutamicum

For fluorescence measurements in E. coli, cells were grown in LB medium supplemented with the appropriate antibiotics (34 μg/ml for chloramphenicol). A single colony was inoculated into 2 ml of LB medium and cultured overnight at 37°C with shaking at 250 rpm. Then the overnight culture was diluted 1:100 into 5 ml of fresh LB medium and incubated under the same conditions. For BL21 (DE3) cultures, T7 RNA polymerase expression was induced 2 h after dilution by adding isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) to a final concentration of 0.2 mM. Four hours after dilution, 100 μl of culture was sampled and transferred to a 96-well microplate for fluorescence measurement using a Hidex Sense microplate reader (Hidex). For mTagBFP and GFP measurements, cultured samples were washed once with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) before fluorescence analysis. OD600 was measured from culture broth using the Jenway 7300 spectrophotometer.

Fluorescence measurement in B. subtilis was performed in growth medium (LB medium supplemented with 0.5 M sorbitol) containing the appropriate antibiotics (5 μg/ml chloramphenicol). A single colony was inoculated into 2 ml of growth medium and cultured overnight at 37°C with shaking at 250 rpm. Then the overnight culture was diluted 1:100 into 5 ml of fresh growth medium and incubated under the same conditions. Nine hours after dilution, 100 µl of culture was sampled and transferred to a 96-well microplate for fluorescence measurement using a Hidex Sense microplate reader.

Fluorescence measurement in C. glutamicum was performed in BHI-based growth medium supplemented with 100 µg/ml of spectinomycin. A single colony was inoculated into 3 ml of growth medium and cultured overnight at 30°C with shaking at 250 rpm. The overnight culture broth was diluted 1:100 into 3 ml of fresh growth medium and cultured for 9 h, followed by OD600 and fluorescence measurement using the Jenway 7300 spectrophotometer and a Hidex.

RNA extraction and droplet digital PCR

One-hundred microliters of cell culture samples were harvested by centrifugation with maximum speed and treated with RNAprotect® Bacteria Reagent (Qiagen). Total RNA was extracted and purified using the RNeasy® Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen) and an RNase-free DNase set (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration of total RNA was measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA quality of each sample was validated using the LabChip with the RNA Pico Sensitivity Reagent Kit (PerkinElmer).

To quantify total and circular RNA levels, complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 500 ng of total RNA using SuperScript™ IV VILO™ Master Mix (Invitrogen), followed by serial dilution to 1/10 000. Diluted cDNAs were mixed with ddPCR Supermix for Probes (No dUTP, Bio-Rad), gene-specific primers, and probes targeting total or circular RNA. Droplets were generated using the QX200 Droplet Generator, and amplification and detection were performed using the QX200 ddPCR System according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All consumables (DG8 Cartridges and Gaskets, Droplet Generation Oil for Probes, ddPCR plate, and foil heat seal) and equipment for ddPCR analysis were purchased from Bio-Rad. Absolute quantity of DNA per sample (copies per µl) was calculated using QuantaSoft software (Bio-Rad).

mRNA decay assay

To measure mRNA decay, E. coli cells harboring the indicated constructs were grown under the same conditions as described for fluorescence measurements. Four hours after dilution, the culture was diluted with fresh pre-warmed LB medium to an OD600 of 1.2 and allowed to recover for 30 min. Transcription was subsequently inhibited by the addition of rifampicin to a final concentration of 500 μg/ml, and the time of rifampicin addition was defined as t = 0. At 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, and 10 min after rifampicin addition, 100 µl aliquots of culture were withdrawn and immediately treated with RNAprotect® Bacteria Reagent (Qiagen) to halt RNA turnover. Total RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and ddPCR quantification were performed as described above, using the convergent primer set to quantify total target RNA.

Western blot analysis

To analyze the expression of metabolic genes, a 6× His-tag was inserted at the N-terminus of the target proteins. Culture conditions were identical for all metabolite production experiments. After measuring the OD600, cells corresponding to 2.25 OD600 units per ml were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in 300 µl of lysis buffer (100 mM NaH2PO4, 10 mM Tris–HCl, and 8 M urea, pH 8.0). After incubation on ice for 30 min, lysates were sonicated (Q800R Sonicator, Qsonica) and separated on SurePAGE™ 4%–12% Bis–Tris gels (GenScript) using MOPS SDS (sodium dodecyl sulfate) running buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins were transferred using Invitrogen PVDF/filter paper sandwiches and detected using a 6× His-tag monoclonal antibody (eBioscience), an alkaline phosphatase-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG antibody (Sigma), and 1-Step NBT/BCIP substrate solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The primary and secondary antibodies were diluted 3:10 000 and 1:10 000, respectively.

Metabolite production and quantification

For flaviolin production, strains were grown in MOPS medium supplemented with 4 g/l glucose, 1% (w/v) casamino acids, and appropriate antibiotics at 37°C and 250 rpm. A single colony was inoculated in 3 ml of medium. The overnight culture broth was diluted 1:100 into 3 ml of fresh medium, and the culture was induced with 0.2 mM IPTG and 20 μM cerulenin when the OD600 reached ~0.6. After 4 h of culture, cells were harvested, and the supernatant was transferred into a 96-well microplate for absorbance measurement at 340 nm on a Hidex [27].

For lycopene production, strains were grown in MOPS medium supplemented with 4 g/l glucose, 1% (w/v) casamino acids, and appropriate antibiotics at 37°C and 250 rpm. A single colony was inoculated in 3 ml of medium. The overnight culture broth was diluted 1:100 into 3 ml of fresh medium, and the culture was induced with 0.2 mM IPTG when the OD600 reached ~0.6. After 2 h of culture, cells were harvested and washed once with 1× PBS, and then resuspended in 200 µl of acetone for intracellular lycopene extraction. The extraction solutions were incubated in a heat block at 55°C for 15 min with intermittent vortexing every 5 min. The lysed cells were centrifuged, and an equal volume of DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide) was added to the supernatant. The absorbance at 475 nm measured by the NanoDrop spectrophotometer was used to calculate lycopene yield, as described in our previous study [26].

For itaconate production, strains were grown in M9 medium supplemented with 1 g/l yeast extract, 20 g/l glucose, and an appropriate antibiotic at 30°C, 200 rpm. A single colony was inoculated into 5 ml of medium in a 100-ml test tube. Overnight-cultured cells were diluted 1:100 in 20 ml of medium in a 300-ml flask, and 0.2 mM IPTG was added when the OD600 reached ~0.3. The pH titration was conducted every 6 h after the addition of IPTG, to a final pH of ~7.0. After 36 h, cultured cells were harvested for high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis and western blotting. For quantitative analysis of itaconate, the cell culture supernatant was filtered and analyzed by HPLC (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) using a cation-exchange Aminex HPX-87 H column (Bio-Rad, California, USA) [28]. The mobile phase was 5 mM sulfuric acid at a flow rate of 0.6 ml/min, and the oven temperature was maintained at 14°C. Itaconate was detected at 210 nm with a UV–vis diode array detector.

For violacein production, strains were grown in M9 medium supplemented with 1 g/l yeast extract, 20 g/l glucose, and an appropriate antibiotic at 37°C, 250 rpm. A single colony was inoculated into 5 ml of medium in a 100-ml test tube. Overnight-cultured cells were diluted 1:100 into 5 ml of medium in a 100-ml test tube. 0.2 mM IPTG and 25 mg/l of l-tryptophan were added 2 h after dilution. Cells were harvested 3 h after induction for HPLC analysis and western blotting. Cell pellets were resuspended in 1 ml DMSO, sonicated for 5 min, centrifuged, and filtered through a 0.22-μm PTFE filter. HPLC analysis was performed using a Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm, 4 μm; Agilent). The mobile phases were 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B), with a flow rate of 0.6 ml/min. Violacein was detected at 562 nm using a PDA detector [29].

Results

In vivo generation of circular mRNA in bacteria using CRESEnT

To examine the feasibility of in vivo mRNA circularization in bacteria, we adopted the PIE system, in which the target transcript was flanked by split intron sequences (Fig. 1A). To construct CRESEnT, we selected the Td intron from T4 bacteriophage, which has been widely used in PIE-based RNA circularization [13, 19] and is considered one of the most efficient group I introns for self-splicing [30]. The intron was split at the P6 arm, and each half was placed at opposite ends of the sequence to be circularized. In addition, a portion of the exon core sequence (ECS) from T4 phage, which is known to interact with the intron, was included to promote splicing-mediated RNA circularization [31]. Because RNA circularization in CRESEnT depends on the splicing activity of the Td group I intron, we reasoned that removal of the ECS would disrupt splicing while leaving the overall cassette architecture largely unchanged. We therefore generated an ECS-deleted version as a linear control for subsequent comparisons (Fig. 1B). We used the E. coli K-12 MG1655 strain as the host and expressed the mCherry reporter to assess system functionality. Quantification of mCherry fluorescence showed that the CRESEnT increased the fluorescence signal by 2.68-fold relative to the linear control, indicating that RNA circularization by CRESEnT substantially improved protein output (Fig. 1C).

Following transcription, intron-mediated splicing ligates the RNA termini, generating a circularized mRNA. (B) Construction of the linear control (LIN). The ECS was removed to disrupt the splicing and prevent RNA circularization. (C) Protein expression from CRESEnT-circularized transcripts (CRESEnT) and the linear control (LIN), quantified as fluorescence normalized to cell density (RFU/OD). (D) Design of convergent and divergent primer sets for quantifying circularization efficiency. (E) Circularization efficiency of CRESEnT and LIN. To verify circular RNA formation and quantify circularization efficiency, we designed convergent and divergent primer sets targeting the junction-flanking region (Fig. 1D). The convergent primers amplify transcripts regardless of topology and therefore report total target RNA abundance, whereas the divergent primers selectively detect circular RNAs in which the 5′ and 3′ termini are covalently ligated by CRESEnT. Using droplet digital PCR (ddPCR), we quantified both total and circular RNA levels (Supplementary Fig. S1). A distinct circular RNA signal was detected in the CRESEnT construct, confirming that CRESEnT can generate circular RNA with covalently joined termini in vivo. In contrast, no circular RNA signal was detected in the linear control. We therefore defined the circularization efficiency as the ratio of circular- to total-RNA abundance, measured by ddPCR. CRESEnT showed a circularization efficiency of 26.4% (Fig. 1E), suggesting that further optimization could improve system performance.

Design principles of CRESEnT for efficient RNA circularization

To enhance RNA circularization efficiency, we identified two key factors governing CRESEnT-mediated circularization and systematically optimized each of them (Fig. 2A). Since efficient splicing depends on productive interactions between the split intron fragments, we reasoned that the spacing between the introns and the coding sequence could critically influence circularization efficiency. Specifically, if the UTR between the intron and the coding sequence is excessively long, it may expose single-stranded RNA regions that are not sufficiently protected by ribosomes, thereby increasing susceptibility to degradation by endogenous endoribonucleases [8, 32]. Conversely, if the UTR is too short, steric constraints may interfere with proper intron folding and splicing [33].

Optimization of CRESEnT for efficient mRNA circularization. (A) Optimization strategies for CRESEnT. Strategy 1 varies the lengths of the 5′ and 3′ UTRs, and strategy 2 introduces complementary hybridization arms between the intron fragments to promote efficient splicing and circularization, as well as protein expression. Effects of 5′ UTR length (B), 3′ UTR length (C), and hybridization arm length (D) on protein output and circularization efficiency measured by ddPCR. Error bars indicate mean ± s.d. from biologically independent cultures (n = 3).

We first varied the length of the 5′-UTR while maintaining the same coding sequence and intron architecture. We observed that CRESEnT exhibited optimal performance when the length of the 5′-UTR was 35 bp, resulting in a 3.26-fold increase in mCherry expression (Fig. 2B). In line with this observation, RNA circularization efficiency was highest when the 5′-UTR was 35 bp in length. It should be noted that protein expression level showed a positive correlation with RNA circularization efficiency, indicating that enhanced RNA circularization contributes to improved protein production (Supplementary Fig. S2A). We applied the same analysis to variations in the 3′-UTR and found that both protein expression and RNA circularization efficiency reached their maximum when the 3′-UTR was 24 bp in length (Fig. 2C). A positive correlation between protein expression and RNA circularization was also observed, consistent with the correlation observed for the 5′-UTR (Supplementary Fig. S2B). These results enabled us to identify the UTR length configuration that yields the highest RNA circularization and protein expression in CRESEnT.

In addition to UTR optimization, we reasoned that strengthening the physical interaction between the split intron fragments could further improve circularization in vivo [15, 34]. To this end, we introduced complementary hybridization arms between the intron sequences to promote intron–intron pairing and facilitate splicing. As the length of the hybridization arms increased, the fluorescence intensity of CRESEnT progressively increased, reflecting higher protein expression (Fig. 2D). In contrast, fluorescence levels in the linear control remained constant, indicating that the increase in protein output was unlikely to result from enhanced stability of the linear RNA, but rather from improved RNA circularization efficiency in CRESEnT (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Consistent with this, direct measurements of RNA circularization efficiency revealed a positive correlation with hybridization arm length, with longer arms promoting higher circularization efficiency (Fig. 2D). However, both protein expression and circularization efficiency declined when the hybridization arms exceeded 35 bp, likely due to the increased susceptibility of the extended double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) regions to degradation by endogenous dsRNA-specific RNase III [35, 36]. These findings indicate that incorporating complementary sequences of appropriate length enhances the overall performance of CRESEnT. Accordingly, the construct containing 30-bp hybridization arms was selected as the final optimized version, as it showed a 5.95-fold increase in mCherry expression compared with the linear control. Together, these results demonstrate that optimization of the key factors governing RNA circularization improves both circularization efficiency and protein expression in CRESEnT.

Circularization increases mRNA abundance, stability, and protein output

Having established the design principles for efficient circularization, we next investigated the effects of mRNA circularization on intracellular RNA abundance and stability. We quantified steady-state RNA levels of the initial and optimized CRESEnT constructs and their corresponding linear controls by ddPCR (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Circularization increased RNA abundance by 2.25-fold in the initial construct and by 3.95-fold in the optimized construct. Notably, applying the same architectural changes to the linear control did not significantly affect RNA abundance (1.20-fold), suggesting that the increase observed with CRESEnT was associated with RNA circularization rather than with the addition of the UTR and hybridization arm sequences.

To investigate whether this increased abundance resulted from enhanced transcript stability, we inhibited transcription with rifampicin and monitored total target RNA by ddPCR over a 10-min chase (Supplementary Fig. S4B). Whereas transcripts from the linear control declined progressively throughout the rifampicin chase, CRESEnT transcripts remained largely unchanged over the same time window, demonstrating substantially enhanced stability of circularized RNA in vivo.

We next investigated whether mRNA circularization influences translational efficiency, which is indicated by protein production divided by transcript abundance (Supplementary Fig. S4C). Fluorescence intensity was normalized to transcript abundance, as quantified by ddPCR, to estimate protein output per transcript. CRESEnT exhibited a normalized fluorescence value of 24.1, compared with 15.9 for the linear control, corresponding to a 1.51-fold increase in protein output per transcript. These results indicate that mRNA circularization improves protein production per transcript relative to linear RNA. Together, these findings suggest that the enhanced protein expression observed with CRESEnT results from both increased transcript abundance due to improved RNA stability and a modest increase in translational efficiency.

Circular mRNA enhances protein expression across diverse gene expression contexts

Gene expression cassettes can be assembled using diverse combinations of regulatory elements, including promoters and RBSs, depending on the desired transcriptional and translational output [37–40]. We therefore assessed whether our RNA topology engineering strategy is orthogonally compatible with conventional gene expression control strategies. To this end, we investigated whether CRESEnT could consistently enhance protein expression across different expression contexts (Fig. 3A). First, we tested native RNAP-dependent synthetic J23-series promoters in E. coli (Fig. 3B). Across all promoters examined, CRESEnT increased mCherry expression relative to the linear control, indicating that its effect is maintained over a broad range of transcriptional strengths driven by the endogenous transcriptional machinery. We next examined whether this enhancement is preserved under an orthogonal transcription system (Fig. 3C). Using T7 promoter variants expressed by T7 RNA polymerase, we also observed higher protein expression from CRESEnT than from the linear control across all promoter variants tested [41]. These results show that the effect of CRESEnT is maintained across distinct transcriptional machineries.

(C) Expression comparison using T7 promoter variants. (D) Expression comparison across RBS variants with different translation initiation strengths. We then assessed the effect of translation initiation strength by combining CRESEnT with RBS variants of different strengths (Fig. 3D). CRESEnT increased protein expression for all RBSs tested, although the fold improvement was smaller for the weakest RBS variant, RBS04. Because translating ribosomes can protect mRNA from endonucleolytic attack by RNases such as RNase E [8, 42], reduced ribosome loading under weak-RBS conditions may leave the transcript more vulnerable to enzymatic degradation, thereby attenuating the benefit of mRNA circularization. Nevertheless, CRESEnT consistently improved protein expression across all transcriptional and translational contexts tested, supporting its broad applicability under diverse gene expression conditions and highlighting the general utility of RNA topology engineering as an orthogonal strategy for gene expression control.

Broad applicability of CRESEnT across diverse genetic cargos and bacterial hosts

Next, we evaluated the versatility of CRESEnT across diverse genetic cargos and bacterial hosts. To test whether its effect was maintained across different proteins, we replaced the original reporter sequence between the split introns with the alternative fluorescent proteins mTagBFP and sGFP. In both cases, CRESEnT increased fluorescence relative to the linear control, indicating that its enhancement effect is maintained across different mRNAs (Fig. 4A). We next investigated whether CRESEnT remains effective for genetic cargos of different lengths. To this end, we replaced the mRNA of interest to be circularized with that encoding endogenous proteins of E. coli K-12 MG1655 fused to mCherry via a flexible linker, to measure the expression levels of genetic cargos via mCherry fluorescence. Across all tested constructs, with total cargo lengths ranging from 1371 bp to 3543 bp, CRESEnT enhanced protein expression by 1.60- to 7.29-fold compared to the linear control (Fig. 4B). Notably, the gene of interest showing the smallest fold increase in expression by CRESEnT was ileS-mCherry, which represented the largest genetic cargo among the tested sequences. Increasing the length of the sequence to be circularized increases the physical distance between the RNA termini, which may reduce the probability of productive intron–intron interactions required for efficient RNA splicing [13]. Nevertheless, these results demonstrate that CRESEnT retains its efficacy for genetic cargos up to 3.5 kb, underscoring its broad applicability.

We next applied CRESEnT in diverse bacterial hosts. In multiple E. coli strains, including BL21 (DE3), W, and Nissle 1917, CRESEnT consistently produced higher protein expression levels than the linear control (Fig. 4C). Among these strains, BL21 (DE3) exhibited the highest enhancement (12.24-fold), which may reflect its protease-deficient background and the resulting increased intracellular accumulation of CRESEnT-expressed proteins [43]. We further extended this evaluation to the Gram-positive model bacterium B. subtilis WB700 (Fig. 4D) and the industrially relevant host C. glutamicum (Fig. 4E), in which CRESEnT increased protein expression by 3.37- and 1.73-fold, respectively, relative to the linear control. Although the magnitude of enhancement varied across hosts, CRESEnT consistently outperformed the linear control in all species tested, supporting its portability across diverse bacterial hosts.

Circular mRNA improves metabolic pathway performance

A circular RNA-based strategy for enhancing protein expression may represent a promising approach in metabolic engineering. Therefore, we applied CRESEnT to several metabolite biosynthetic pathways to determine whether it could enhance the expression of pathway enzymes and thereby improve metabolite production in vivo. As representative products, we selected flaviolin, itaconate, lycopene, and violacein (Fig. 5A).

Among these, we first chose flaviolin as a convenient colorimetric model to test whether CRESEnT-mediated enhancement of protein expression could translate into improved product formation in vivo. Flaviolin is a polyketide produced by the type III polyketide synthase RppA, which converts five molecules of malonyl-CoA into 1,3,6,8-tetrahydroxynaphthalene (THN), followed by spontaneous conversion into the red-colored flaviolin [44, 45]. To ensure sufficient precursor availability, 20 μM cerulenin was added to reduce malonyl-CoA consumption by the endogenous fatty acid synthesis cycle. Expression of rppA using CRESEnT increased flaviolin production by 2.74-fold relative to the linear control, as measured by absorbance at 340 nm [27] (Fig. 5B). Western blot analysis further confirmed increased RppA abundance by the CRESEnT construct (Fig. 5C), suggesting that improved flaviolin production resulted from enhanced enzyme expression.

We next examined whether CRESEnT could be applied to enhance the production of an industrially relevant value-added product. As a first example, we selected itaconic acid, whose biosynthesis involves the single-step conversion of cis-aconitate to itaconate by cis-aconitate decarboxylase (CadA) [46]. Implementation of CRESEnT led to a higher itaconate titer, corresponding to an ~1.52-fold increase relative to the linear control (Fig. 5D), and western blot analysis likewise showed increased CadA abundance (Fig. 5E).

We extended our analysis to more complex biosynthetic pathways requiring coordinated expression of multiple enzymes. To evaluate whether CRESEnT could enhance pathway-level performance under these conditions, we selected lycopene and violacein as representative multi-step products [47–49]. Biosynthesis of lycopene requires the coordinated expression of geranylgeranyl diphosphate synthase (CrtE), phytoene synthase (CrtB), and phytoene desaturase (CrtI). To test whether circular mRNA-mediated expression could improve lycopene production, we placed crtE under CRESEnT control, while crtB and crtI were expressed from a separate vector, as crtE has been reported to limit pathway flux under certain conditions [50]. This configuration increased lycopene yield by 1.60-fold compared with the linear control (Fig. 5F). Consistent with the production data, western blot analysis showed higher CrtE abundance in the CRESEnT construct (Fig. 5G).

For the application of CRESEnT to violacein production, we placed vioD under CRESEnT control, as VioD has been reported to influence pathway flux and product distribution within the violacein biosynthetic pathway [51]. l-tryptophan was added to the medium at 25 mg/l as a precursor. Under these conditions, violacein titer increased by 1.62-fold relative to the linear control (Fig. 5H). Although western blot analysis did not reveal a clear increase in VioD abundance, the broadly degraded band pattern (Supplementary Fig. S5) suggests that VioD may be intrinsically unstable, potentially obscuring differences in expression level. Nevertheless, the production data indicate that CRESEnT can improve pathway performance in this context. Collectively, these results demonstrate that CRESEnT can be applied to diverse biosynthetic pathways to enhance the production of industrially relevant metabolites.

Discussion

Circular RNAs have attracted considerable interest as a platform for enhancing RNA stability and translation because their covalently closed structure confers resistance to exonuclease-mediated degradation. Although circular RNA technologies have been extensively explored in eukaryotic systems [13, 19], their application in prokaryotic hosts has remained limited. In bacteria, rapid mRNA turnover frequently constrains protein production and therefore represents an important bottleneck in both synthetic biology and metabolic engineering [52]. In this study, we established CRESEnT, a programmable platform for engineering RNA topology, and demonstrated its utility for controlling gene expression and metabolic engineering through a detailed optimization of PIE-based in vivo RNA circularization in bacteria.

We revealed that efficient mRNA circularization in bacterial cells requires a careful balance between RNA features that support intron-mediated splicing and sequence architectures that remain compatible with translation. In practice, this balance likely involves at least three factors in harmony: proper folding of the split intron, sufficient accessibility for ribosome loading, and avoidance of excessive exposure to endoribonucleolytic machineries. We found that the optimal lengths of the 5′-UTR and 3′-UTR differed, which might arise from the difference in the physical occupancy of the ribosome on each UTR. The ribosome translates up to the stop codon, which is adjacent to the 3′-UTR, and is then released upon termination. In contrast, the ribosome is initially loaded onto the Shine–Dalgarno sequence of the 5′-UTR, which lies several nucleotides upstream of the AUG start codon, and therefore occupies the 5′-UTR more extensively. This underscores the importance of optimizing UTR length to ensure the ribosome does not interfere with the folding of the intron. We further observed a positive correlation between mRNA circularization efficiency and protein expression levels, suggesting that increased circular mRNA generation significantly contributed to increased protein expression. One possible explanation for the improvement in protein expression is the increased persistence of the transcript in the bacterial cytoplasm due to reduced susceptibility to exonuclease-mediated degradation, resulting from the removal of free RNA ends via circularization [13, 53]. In the other aspect, the covalent linkage of the 5′ and 3′ ends may promote more efficient ribosome reuse after translation termination by increasing the effective proximity between the stop codon and the translation initiation region [54, 55]. Although CRESEnT was not designed to drive rolling-circle translation and repetitive peptide production, such topological organization may still facilitate ribosome recycling and thereby improve translational efficiency [56–58]. In short, these findings suggest that in vivo mRNA circularization can function as a distinct mode of gene expression control in bacteria by simultaneously influencing RNA stability and ribosome utilization.

Current synthetic biology toolkits for programmable gene expression rely primarily on the engineering and design of genetic elements that modulate transcription, translation, and gene copy number [4, 59, 60]. Although these tools have provided a solid foundation for controlling gene expression, strategies that directly address bacterial mRNA stability and degradation remain relatively underdeveloped [12]. Here, we show that RNA topology engineering via intron-based circularization can provide an additional layer of gene regulation by altering the accessibility of transcripts to intracellular RNA decay machinery. By demonstrating that CRESEnT functions robustly across diverse promoters, RBSs, cargos, and bacterial hosts, we further show that this strategy is broadly compatible with and orthogonal to established approaches for gene expression control. We found that the fold enhancement of protein expression by adopting the CRESEnT varies across the microbial chassis. The fold enhancement was smallest in C. glutamicum, which might arise from the prevalence of endoribonucleases enabling degradation of circularized RNA, and the efficiency of Td intron self-splicing in this host remains uncharacterized [61, 62]. Also, the length of genetic cargo might affect the fold enhancement of gene expression, owing to the difference in physical distance between two termini of mRNA with split introns. Despite these limitations, considering that RNA topology engineering could modulate mRNA stability and lifetime in vivo, we expect our CRESEnT could be further applied to tune the kinetic parameters of synthetic genetic circuits, an aspect that has remained less accessible with conventional toolkits that focus mainly on thermodynamic control [12, 63, 64]. Future improvements may be achieved by engineering the split introns to more readliy adopt the productive tertiary structure upon encounter, or by developing alternative self-splicing intron sequence beyond the T4 td intron [65]. Taken together, these findings suggest CRESEnT as a portable and extensible platform that can be layered onto existing gene expression strategies to support the development of finely tuned microbial cell factories and living therapeutics.

In summary, we developed CRESEnT as a programmable platform to engineer RNA topology in bacteria via in vivo mRNA circularization. Systematic optimization of UTR spacing and hybridization arm length substantially improved RNA circularization efficiency and protein output. The optimized CRESEnT system functioned robustly across diverse genetic contexts, cargo types, and bacterial hosts, highlighting its broad compatibility with established synthetic biology toolkits. We further demonstrated its utility in metabolic engineering by enhancing the expression of pathway enzymes and improving the production of multiple value-added metabolites. Overall, CRESEnT provides a broadly applicable framework for extending bacterial gene control through RNA topology engineering.
