From haemostasis to immunity: The expanding frontiers of platelet biology
Authors: Xun Grace Wu, Nicole Tomei, Heyu Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In a Nutshell, platelets are small and anucleate, yet remarkably versatile cells that bridge the gap between haemostasis, thrombosis, inflammation and adaptive immunity. This expanded functional spectrum uncovers new opportunities for understanding and treating immune‐mediated thrombocytopenia and other haematological diseases.

PLATELETS ARE VERSATILE CELLS

Platelets, which are derived from megakaryocytes, play a vital role in haemostasis and thrombosis. 1 , 2 , 3 While platelets are largely produced in the bone marrow, emerging evidence indicates thrombopoiesis also occurs in the lungs, and likely other organs, raising the possibility that platelet production at distinct anatomical sites contributes to functional heterogeneity and immune specialization. 1 , 4 , 5 , 6

Haemostasis proceeds through distinct but overlapping waves to rapidly and effectively stop bleeding. 1 , 2 , 3 , 7 At sites of vascular injury, particularly at high shear, platelet Glycoprotein Ib subunit α (GPIbα) interacts with von Willebrand factor (VWF), initiating platelet tethering and rolling on the injured vessel wall, whereas platelet GPIIbIIIa (integrin αIIbβ3) binds to fibrinogen, bridging activated platelets for aggregation. Shear stress is usually escalated following platelet aggregation and vessel lumen shrinking; GPIbα–VWF interaction may then become essential for platelet aggregation/agglutination and vessel occlusion. The similar haemostatic processes of platelet adhesion and aggregation at the sites of vascular injury, plus their potential in facilitating blood coagulation, may also lead to vessel occlusion and thrombosis. Interestingly, although fibrinogen and VWF are documented to be essential for haemostasis/thrombosis, platelet aggregation and occlusive thrombosis still occur in mice lacking fibrinogen and/or VWF, indicating the existence of both fibrinogen/VWF‐dependent and ‐independent haemostatic/thrombotic pathways. 1 , 7 While GPIIbIIIa antagonists have been used as interventions against thrombosis for decades, 2 GPIbα antagonists are still not available for patients, but promising evidence is emerging.

Beyond haemostasis and thrombosis, platelets have emerged as critical players in diverse processes, including immune regulation, atherosclerosis, angiogenesis, lymphatic vessel development, liver regeneration and beyond. 1 It has also been demonstrated that cancer‐platelet cross‐talk is important for tumour growth and metastasis. Platelets reinforce tumorigenesis by providing proliferation signals and angiogenic factors. They also sustain metastasis by inducing an invasive epithelial–mesenchymal transition of tumour cells, enhancing tumour cell survival in circulation, as well as facilitating tumour arrest in the endothelium and subsequent extravasation into surrounding tissues. In addition, platelets may assist in immune evasion by coating tumour cells and suppressing immune cell attacks 1 (Graphical Abstract, Supporting Information). In this brief review, we focus on the cross‐talk between platelets and the immune system. 4 , 8 , 9

PLATELETS ARE IMMUNE REGULATORY CELLS

Platelets express multiple pattern recognition receptors, including Toll‐like receptors (TLR2/4/7/9) and nucleotide‐binding oligomerization domain (NOD)‐like receptors, enabling direct detection of pathogens and initiation of innate immune responses. 4 , 5 , 8 , 9 Upon activation, platelets release granule contents containing anti‐microbial peptides, mitochondrial deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) and danger‐associated molecular patterns such as high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1). At sites of inflammation, platelets interact with leucocytes, particularly neutrophils, via adhesion molecules such as P‐selectin, CD40 ligand (CD40L), GPIbα and newly reported platelet‐derived integrin and tetraspanin‐enriched tethers, 10 promoting platelet‐leucocytes aggregation and immune cell activation.

In adaptive immunity, platelets bridge innate immunity into antigen‐specific responses. They express major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I and co‐stimulatory molecules (e.g. CD40L, CD86), enabling direct antigen presentation to CD8+ T cells and interaction with CD4+ T cells through co‐stimulatory signalling pathways. These may be particularly important for viruses that infect platelets (e.g. influenza, dengue). 11 Upon activation, platelets mobilize MHC class I, supporting immunological synapse formation. Through CD40L, platelets engage CD40 on dendritic cells and B cells, facilitating antibody class switching and germinal centre formation. Additionally, subsets of megakaryocytes in tissues like the lung have been shown to express MHC class II, enabling broader T‐cell engagement. Platelet‐derived chemokines like PF4 support T‐cell homing, while cytokines such as interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β) and transforming growth factor‐β (TGF‐β) modulate T‐helper cell polarization towards either inflammatory (Th1/Th17) or regulatory (T regulatory cells) phenotypes, depending on context. 4 , 5 , 6

While platelets propagate pro‐inflammatory responses in many pathological processes, they also exert immunosuppressive effects. Recent work demonstrates that platelets can actively promote immune suppression through direct interactions with innate immune cells. Resting platelets engage monocytes via CD47‐dependent signalling to regulate monocyte metabolism and pathogen responses. 5 We recently observed that desialylated platelets (dPLTs) following activation or ageing were exclusively cleared by Kupffer cells in the liver. This novel platelet clearance pathway can dampen immune response via promoting anti‐inflammatory cytokines and T regulatory cells, although the precise mechanism is currently unclear. 6 In a reciprocal manner, Kupffer cells, as we demonstrated, influence platelet production by promoting thrombopoietin (TPO) generation. Our earlier work showed that GPIbα is essential for platelet‐mediated hepatocellular TPO production, which provides approximately 60%–70% of TPO in both murine and human blood. Most recently, we revealed that Kupffer cells are essential for this process, by promoting endothelial fenestrations and facilitating platelet–hepatocyte interactions. 12

PLATELETS CAN BE TARGETED BY THE IMMUNE SYSTEM

Autoimmune thrombocytopenia arises from an aberrant immune response targeting self‐platelets and includes primary immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), infection‐associated thrombocytopenia and drug‐induced thrombocytopenia. ITP is a more diverse syndrome than traditionally thought and there are no detectable anti‐platelet antibodies in ~50% of patients. It is currently unclear whether these patients have low titre and/or low affinity antibodies that are beyond detectable threshold, or thrombocytopenia caused by other mechanisms, such as T‐cell‐mediated cytotoxicity and immune complexes. Human platelets have Fc receptors that can bind the immune complex, leading to platelet clearance. Autoantibodies in ITP may mediate platelet destruction through distinct mechanisms. Platelet destruction by antibodies against GPIIbIIIa is primarily mediated through an Fc‐dependent pathway by macrophages, leading to platelet clearance in the spleen; in contrast, antibodies against GPIbα can easily induce platelet activation and neuraminidase‐1 translocation, leading to platelet desialylation and likely destruction via an Fc‐independent pathway in liver. 9 , 13 ITP driven by anti‐GPIbα antibodies often resists intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and steroid treatments, highlighting the need for novel therapeutic approaches targeting this type of ITP. 9 , 14

Alloimmune thrombocytopenia results from alloantibody‐mediated platelet destruction, which occurs in conditions like post‐transfusion purpura (PTP) or fetal and neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia (FNAIT). 8 , 15 Platelet transfusion has been widely used but is partially misunderstood. Stored platelets cause inflammation, thrombosis, immune dysregulation and toxicities that are not well appreciated by haematologists. It is currently unclear whether alloantibodies in PTP may form immune complexes that bind to ‘bystander’ platelets and/or activate the complement system, leading to autologous platelet clearance. We established the first murine FNAIT models and demonstrated IVIG as a useful therapeutic intervention, consistent with clinical observations. 15 Further investigations revealed that fetal, rather than maternal, FcRn is essential for the transplacental transfer of maternal immunoglobulin G (IgG). Maternal antibodies targeting β3 integrin may disrupt fetal brain angiogenesis, leading to intracranial haemorrhage, while retention of natural killer cells in the placenta may promote tissue injury through antibody‐dependent‐cell‐mediated cytotoxicity. Furthermore, experimental studies demonstrate that maternal anti‐GPIbα antibodies can induce thrombosis in the placenta and lead to frequent miscarriages. Treatment with either IVIG or anti‐FcRn antibody can efficiently prevent anti‐GPIbα‐mediated FNAIT. These findings underscore the multifaceted pathophysiology of FNAIT and may pave the way for innovative therapeutic approaches.

This review summarizes the versatile roles of platelets beyond their classical functions in haemostasis and thrombosis, highlighting their contributions as key immune mediators involved in pro‐inflammatory responses, immunosuppression and various pathophysiological processes (Table 1).
