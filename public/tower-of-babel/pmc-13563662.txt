Day-to-day associations between social interaction valence and awareness of age-related change
Authors: Christina Ristl, Lukas Kraiger, Selma Korlat, Sarah E Martiny, Jana Nikitin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAwareness of age-related change (AARC) reflects how individuals perceive aging through daily gains and losses. Social interactions constitute a central context of everyday life in which such perceptions may be shaped, yet little is known about their short-term interplay in daily life. The present study examined same-day and next-day associations between AARC gains and losses and the perceived positivity and negativity of daily social interactions in middle-aged and older adults.MethodsUsing a 14-day daily diary design, we assessed 109 adults aged 50–85 years (M = 63.16, SD = 9.17), yielding 1,502 daily observations. Dynamic structural equation modeling was used to disentangle within-person dynamics from stable between-person differences.ResultsAt the within-person level, days characterized by higher-than-usual AARC-gains were associated with more positive social interactions, whereas days marked by higher-than-usual AARC-losses were associated with less positive and more negative social interactions. Regarding temporal associations, higher-than-usual AARC-losses predicted increased negativity of social interactions on the following day, whereas daily social interaction valence did not predict next-day changes in AARC-gains or losses.DiscussionOverall, the findings indicate that daily AARC is closely embedded in everyday social experience and highlight age-related losses as particularly consequential for the short-term perception of negative social interactions.

Everyday experience is not a direct reflection of objective reality but is shaped by how individuals interpret and evaluate events in daily life (Almadi, 2022; Merton, 1948). This is particularly evident in social interactions, which are often ambiguous in valence and open to multiple interpretations: an offer of help may be experienced as caring and supportive by one person, but as intrusive or patronizing by another. How such interactions are interpreted matters, as the perceived quality of social encounters is closely linked to emotional well-being and everyday functioning across adulthood (e.g., Birditt, 2014; Jang et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024). Negative social interactions, such as interpersonal tension, rejection, or criticism, represent some of the most emotionally impactful daily stressors in later life (Almeida, 2005; Rook, 2001) and are associated with elevated negative affect, loneliness, and depressive symptoms (Luong & Charles, 2014; Zhou et al., 2024). In contrast, positive social interactions are associated with greater social connectedness, positive affect, and better well-being in everyday life (Birditt et al., 2018; Nikitin et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024).

The experience and evaluation of social interactions change with age. According to the Strength and Vulnerability Integration model (SAVI; Charles, 2010), older adults increasingly prioritize emotionally meaningful interactions and regulate their exposure to negative social experiences. Consistent with this perspective, older adults tend to evaluate their daily social interactions as more positive and less negative than younger adults (Birditt & Fingerman, 2005; Carstensen, 2021; Luong & Charles, 2014; Nikitin et al., 2024). Older adults are also more likely to rely on avoidance-oriented strategies when dealing with interpersonal tensions (Birditt & Fingerman, 2005; Birditt et al., 2005). Importantly, SAVI proposes that these socioemotional strengths are conditional. When older adults cannot avoid or disengage from stressful social situations, negative interactions may elicit heightened emotional and physiological reactivity (Birditt, 2014; Charles, 2010). Together, these findings highlight the importance of the emotional quality of social interactions in later life. At the same time, substantial variability persists in how older adults experience and evaluate their daily social encounters (Buijs et al., 2023; Carstensen, 2021). Identifying psychological factors that may contribute to this variability is therefore important for understanding everyday social experience in aging.

The present study examines whether awareness of age-related change (AARC; Diehl & Wahl, 2010) is associated with the perceived valence of daily social interactions. Specifically, we investigate whether AARC-gains and AARC-losses are linked to how social interactions are experienced in everyday life and whether repeated social experiences may, in turn, contribute to fluctuations in AARC over time. By examining these bidirectional associations, the present study aims to provide insight into the dynamic interplay between subjective aging and everyday social experience.

Awareness of age-related gains and losses and daily social experience

Awareness of age-related change (AARC) refers to “all those experiences that make a person aware that his or her behavior, level of performance, or ways of experiencing life have changed as a consequence of having grown older” (Diehl & Wahl, 2010, p. 340). AARC is embedded within the broader framework of subjective aging, which encompasses individuals’ perceptions, evaluations, and interpretations of their own aging process (Diehl et al., 2021a; Wurm et al., 2017). Unlike broad or generalized views of aging, AARC captures concrete, consciously perceived age-related changes in everyday life. For example, an individual may perceive becoming physically slower as an age-related loss, while experiencing greater emotional stability and wisdom as age-related gains. Importantly, AARC reflects subjective interpretations of age-related change rather than objective aging per se. Individuals become aware of changes in their functioning and attribute these experiences to getting older, perceiving them as age-related gains or losses (Diehl et al., 2021a). Age-related gains and losses constitute distinct and partially independent dimensions, allowing individuals to experience growth and decline simultaneously across different life domains (Diehl et al., 2021b; Kaspar et al., 2023).

Empirical research on AARC has predominantly examined associations with health-related, cognitive, and motivational outcomes (e.g., Diehl et al., 2021a; Neupert & Bellingtier, 2017; Wilton-Harding et al., 2022). Evidence linking AARC to social functioning is limited and has so far concentrated on global, interindividual differences in perceived social and psychosocial resources, most notably perceived social support and psychosocial well-being (Kaspar et al., 2023; O’Brien & Sharifian, 2020). These studies consistently show that higher age-related gains and lower age-related losses are associated with more favorable evaluations of social resources, whereas higher age-related losses are associated with less favorable evaluations at the between-person level. Building on this work, the present study extends the focus from stable interindividual differences to the within-person experience of everyday social contacts.

AARC may be associated with how social interactions are experienced in everyday life through affective appraisal processes. Theoretically, AARC reflects how individuals perceive and interpret age-related experiences in everyday life (Diehl & Wahl, 2010; Diehl et al., 2021a). When awareness of age-related losses is heightened, everyday experiences may therefore be accompanied by more negative affective states, whereas awareness of age-related gains may coincide with more positive affective states. Neupert and Bellingtier (2017) similarly suggested that daily awareness of aging may shape how everyday experiences are emotionally experienced. Consistent with this idea, daily diary studies show that higher AARC-losses are associated with elevated negative affect and stronger emotional reactivity to daily stressors, whereas AARC-gains are linked to more positive affective states and greater vitality (Neupert & Bellingtier, 2017; Wilton-Harding et al., 2022). These affective states may shape how social interactions are experienced and evaluated in everyday life. Negative affect increases the likelihood that ambiguous social cues are appraised as unpleasant or threatening, whereas positive affect may facilitate more favorable evaluations of social encounters (Neta & Brock, 2021). Accordingly, AARC-losses may be associated with more negative evaluations of daily social interactions, whereas AARC-gains may be associated with more positive evaluations.

Beyond affective processes, AARC may also relate to social interaction valence through motivational and self-regulatory pathways. According to the Motivational Theory of Life-Span Development, individuals continuously adjust their goals and behavior to perceived opportunities and constraints throughout adulthood (Heckhausen et al., 2010). In this context, AARC-gains and losses may reflect subjective perceptions of age-related resources and limitations that shape social goals in everyday life (Dutt et al., 2018; Ristl et al., 2025). Consistent with this perspective, empirical work links AARC to developmental regulation processes, with AARC-gains associated with more adaptive regulation and AARC-losses associated with less flexible regulation strategies (Dutt et al., 2018). Recent evidence further suggests that AARC-gains are associated with stronger social approach goals, whereas AARC-losses are associated with stronger social avoidance goals (Ristl et al., 2025). Such goals influence how individuals attend and respond to social situations and may therefore shape the subjective experience of social interactions (Nikitin & Schoch, 2021). Through these affective, motivational, and self-regulatory processes, AARC may therefore be associated with the perceived valence of daily social interactions.

At the same time, daily social interactions may also shape AARC. Diehl and Wahl’s (2010) conceptualization of AARC identifies everyday experiences, including situational appraisals, goal attainment, and life events, as potential antecedents of awareness of age-related gains and losses. Social interactions may be particularly relevant in this regard because they provide frequent and emotionally salient feedback about competence, autonomy, social connectedness, and interpersonal value. For example, experiencing support, appreciation, or meaningful connection may reinforce perceptions of continued growth and social competence, whereas experiences of exclusion, conflict, or dependency may increase awareness of age-related losses. Consistent with this idea, Windsor et al. (2024) found that daily fluctuations in need satisfaction and frustration were associated with corresponding fluctuations in AARC-gains and losses. These associations were particularly pronounced for competence and autonomy, but relatedness satisfaction was also associated with higher AARC-gains. Broader research on subjective aging further suggests that social engagement and meaningful social roles contribute to more positive aging-related self-perceptions over time (Huo et al., 2021; Schwartz et al., 2021). Extending this work to the daily level, Bergman et al. (2025) found that daily subjective aging perceptions were associated with positive social relationships both within the same day and across consecutive days. Together, these findings suggest that daily social interactions may provide an important context in which awareness of age-related gains and losses is reinforced or updated.

The strength of the association between AARC and daily social interaction valence may also differ by age. On the one hand, aging-related self-perceptions may become increasingly self-relevant with age as individuals encounter more age-related changes in everyday life (Diehl et al., 2021a). Age-related losses, in particular, tend to become more pronounced in later adulthood, whereas age-related gains appear comparatively stable across adulthood (Kaspar et al., 2023). On the other hand, older adults also show stronger socioemotional regulation and greater selectivity in social contexts (Carstensen, 2021; Charles, 2010). Research on social approach and avoidance motivations further suggests that the adaptiveness and salience of social goals may differ across adulthood, depending on developmental tasks and social contexts (Nikitin & Freund, 2019; Nikitin & Schoch, 2021). Together, these findings suggest that age-related differences in the association between AARC and daily social experiences are plausible, although further research is needed to clarify their direction.

The present study

The present study examined the daily interplay between the perceived valence of social interactions and AARC—both AARC-gains and AARC-losses—in middle-aged and older adults. This focus follows from the conceptualization of AARC as a sub-concept of subjective aging that reflects how individuals perceive and interpret age-related changes in everyday life (Diehl & Wahl, 2010; Diehl et al., 2021a). Although prior research has linked AARC to broader between-person differences in social resources and psychosocial functioning (e.g., Kaspar et al., 2023; O’Brien & Sharifian, 2020), little is known about how AARC and the emotional valence of social interactions are linked within persons across everyday life.

Using a 14-day daily diary design, we examined short-term fluctuations in AARC and social interaction valence as they unfold in naturalistic contexts. By adopting a within-person perspective and examining associations across consecutive days, the study extends previous research on subjective aging by investigating whether daily social experiences and AARC are dynamically linked in everyday life. Guided by theory and empirical findings demonstrating meaningful day-to-day variability in AARC (Neupert & Bellingtier, 2017; Wilton-Harding et al., 2022; Windsor et al., 2024), we formulated three preregistered hypotheses.

H1 (Concurrent Associations). We hypothesized that AARC and social interaction valence would be concurrently associated at both the within-person and between-person levels. Specifically, on days with higher-than-usual AARC-gains, individuals were expected to report more positive social interactions, whereas on days with higher-than-usual AARC-losses, individuals were expected to report more negative social interactions.

H2 (Reciprocal Prospective Associations). At the within-person level, we further hypothesized reciprocal lagged associations across consecutive days. Higher-than-usual AARC-gains (/losses) on one day (t − 1) were expected to predict more positive (/negative) social interactions on the following day (t). Conversely, more positive (/negative) social interactions on one day (t − 1) were expected to predict higher-than-usual AARC-gains (/losses) on the following day (t).

H3 (Age Moderation). Finally, we explored whether chronological age moderates the within-person associations between AARC and social interaction valence. Given that perceptions of AARC losses become increasingly salient and self-relevant with age (Kaspar et al., 2023), and considering that older adults tend to regulate their social environments more selectively (Carstensen, 2021; Charles, 2010), we exploratorily investigated whether the concurrent and lagged associations between AARC losses and negative social interactions become stronger with increasing age. In contrast, because AARC gains appear relatively stable across adulthood (Kaspar et al., 2023), associations between AARC gains and positive social interactions are expected to be comparatively stable or weaker across age.

Method

The study is embedded in a multinational research consortium on daily experiences of aging across cultural contexts. Analyses were based on the Austrian subsample, as only this sample assessed social interaction valence. The research questions were preregistered on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/a8egh/). Although the preregistration specified the use of a Random Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Model (RI-CLPM), we employed Dynamic Structural Equation Modeling (DSEM) with a Bayesian estimator to better accommodate the intensive longitudinal data structure and to ensure robust parameter estimation given the final sample size (Asparouhov et al., 2018). Study materials, data, and analysis scripts are available from the authors upon request. German and English items used in this study are provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

Participants

The final sample consisted of 109 adults from Austria, aged 50–85 years (M = 62.98, SD = 9.30; 56% female) who completed at least seven daily questionnaires. Most participants were married or in a romantic relationship (69.7%), followed by separated or divorced (17.4%), single (8.3%), and widowed participants (4.6%). Furthermore, 56.1% had completed higher education, and 28.4% had completed vocational training or a high school diploma; 59.0% were currently employed. Participants were recruited between April 9 and June 10, 2025, via personal contacts and social media as part of a university research seminar. After providing written informed consent and completing a baseline questionnaire, participants completed evening daily questionnaires on up to 14 consecutive days assessing AARC and the perceived positivity and negativity of daily social interactions. Compliance with the diary protocol among included participants was high (91.8%, range 50%–100%), yielding 1,502 daily diary entries. Participants received financial compensation based on participation level (€35 for full participation, €25 for ≥10 days, €15 for ≥7 days). Ethical approval was obtained from the internal review board of the Department of Educational and Developmental Psychology at the University of Vienna (approval number: 9_25).

Measures

Daily positive and negative social interactions

Daily positive and negative social interactions were assessed using two self-developed single-item measures. Each evening, participants rated the overall emotional quality of their social interactions during the day in response to the question, “How did you experience your social interactions today?” Positivity was rated on a 5-point scale from 1 (not at all positive) to 5 (very positive), and negativity on a separate 5-point scale from 1 (not at all negative) to 5 (very negative). Although participants could indicate having had no social interaction on a given day, this option was never selected. Given the micro-longitudinal design, brief global ratings were used to reduce participant burden and to capture the overall perceived valence of daily social interactions. Daily awareness of age-related gains and losses

Daily AARC-gains and losses were assessed with a 20-item short form of the Awareness of Age-Related Change Scale (AARC-SF; Diehl & Wahl, 2010; Neupert & Bellingtier, 2017). Items were framed to capture participants’ experiences during the past 24 hr and began with the stem “With my awareness of aging in the past 24 hours, I realized that….” Responses were provided on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very much). The self-report questionnaire included 10 items assessing AARC-gains and 10 items assessing AARC-losses, covering changes in cognitive and physical functioning, social and emotional functioning, and lifestyle-related aspects. Example items were “… I appreciate relationships and people much more” (gains) and “…I am slower in my thinking” (losses). Daily AARC-gains and AARC-losses scores were calculated by averaging responses across the respective item sets, with higher values indicating greater perceived gains or losses on that day. Descriptive statistics and variance components are reported in Table 1. Reliability was evaluated using McDonald’s omega. Reliability estimates indicated acceptable to good internal consistency for daily gains (ω_within = 0.65; ω_between = 0.92) and daily losses (ω_within = 0.65; ω_between = 0.92).

Covariates

Chronological age, subjective health (single item; 1 = very poor to 5 = excellent), educational attainment (ordinal, five levels), and partnership status (dichotomous: partnered vs not partnered) were assessed via self-report at baseline and included as covariates in all analyses, as these variables are consistently associated with subjective views of aging and social outcomes in adulthood and later life (Tully-Wilson et al., 2021). Descriptive statistics are provided in Table 1.

Analysis

To examine the bidirectional and dynamic associations between daily AARC-gains and losses and social interaction valence (positive and negative), we employed Dynamic Structural Equation Modeling (DSEM). DSEM integrates multilevel modeling with time-series analysis (VAR(1) models), allowing for the simultaneous estimation of within-person fluctuations and stable between-person differences (Asparouhov et al., 2018). All analyses were conducted in Mplus Version 9.

Our analytic approach followed a three-step procedure: (1) Main Models: Estimation of the primary DSEM models containing the dynamic associations between AARC and social interaction valence. (2) Sensitivity Analysis: To examine the robustness of the identified effects, chronological age, subjective health, education, and partnership status were included as Level-2 covariates predicting the between-person means of the focal variables; and (3) Moderation Analysis: Main models were extended to test whether chronological age moderated the within-person cross-lagged associations between AARC and social interaction valence. However, the models were affected by convergence problems (PSR >1.10; low effective sample sizes [ESS]) across model specifications and were therefore not interpreted.

For each social interaction valence, we specified a two-level first-order vector autoregressive (VAR(1)) model capturing concurrent associations, autoregressive effects, and cross-lagged effects from day t − 1 to day t. Same-day associations were modeled via within-person residual covariances, whereas next-day associations were modeled via lagged regression paths. Within-person and between-person variance were separated via latent person-mean centering, which avoids the Nickell bias inherent in observed centering with short time series (Hamaker & Grasman, 2014). To maintain temporal integrity, the TINTERVAL was set to 1, and the time variable reflected a one-day interval.

Figure 1 provides a schematic illustration of the within-person VAR(1) model estimated in the DSEM analyses. Models were estimated using a Bayesian framework with Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms and non-informative priors. Missing data were handled via data augmentation, treating missingness as unknown parameters estimated from the posterior distribution (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2021). Two chains with 10,000 iterations each were run; convergence was confirmed by Potential Scale Reduction (PSR) values below 1.05. Parameter stability was further evaluated by inspecting effective sample sizes (ESS) for all focal effects.

Given the sample size (N = 109) and number of measurement occasions (T = 14), simulation studies suggest that such designs are sufficient to obtain reliable point estimates and adequate credible interval coverage for both fixed effects and random effects variances in multilevel VAR(1) models (Schultzberg & Muthén, 2018). While larger samples generally improve precision, the present design aligns with current recommendations for recovering meaningful within-person dynamics and their inter-individual differences within a DSEM framework.

Results

Preliminary analyses

Descriptive statistics indicated that participants evaluated their daily social interactions as predominantly positive and reported relatively low levels of negative interactions (Table 1). Average levels of AARC-losses were low, whereas AARC-gains were, on average, slightly above the scale midpoint. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) revealed substantial day-to-day variability in social interaction valence (ICCpos = 0.32; ICCneg = 0.34), while AARC-losses and gains showed greater stability across persons (ICCs = 0.83), with meaningful within-person variability observed across days (Table 1).

Bivariate within-person correlations (Table 2) showed that on days with higher-than-usual AARC-losses, individuals reported fewer positive (r = −0.12, p < .001) and more negative social interactions (r = 0.42, p < .001). Conversely, days with higher-than-usual AARC-gains were associated with more positive (r = 0.09, p < .01), but not negative, social interactions. At the between-person level, individuals reporting higher average AARC-losses experienced more negative social interactions, were older, reported poorer subjective health, and were less likely to be partnered. Average levels of AARC-gains showed a small association with higher educational attainment.

Daily dynamics of AARC and social interactions (H1 and H2)

As hypothesized (H1), DSEM analyses revealed a valence-specific pattern of same-day within-person associations (Tables 3 and 4). On days with higher-than-usual AARC-losses, individuals reported more negative (β = 0.19) and fewer positive social interactions (β = −0.14). In contrast, higher-than-usual AARC-gains were associated with more positive social interactions (β = 0.09), whereas AARC-gains were not related to negative interaction valence within the same day.

Cross-lagged analyses further revealed selective next-day associations (H2; Tables 3 and 4). Higher-than-usual AARC-losses on one day predicted more negative social interactions on the following day (β = 0.10). No other cross-lagged effects were observed: In contrast to our hypotheses (H2), AARC-gains did not predict next-day positive or negative social interactions, and neither positive nor negative social interactions predicted subsequent changes in AARC-gains or losses.

Stability and individual differences

All study variables showed significant autoregressive effects, indicating moderate temporal stability from one day to the next for AARC-gains (β = 0.41), AARC-losses (β = 0.33), positive social interactions (β = 0.18), and negative social interactions (β = 0.20; Tables 3 and 4). At the between-person level, significant random intercept variances indicated substantial interindividual differences in average levels of AARC-gains, AARC-losses, and social interaction valence. In addition, significant random slope variances for the autoregressive paths and selected cross-lagged associations indicated marked heterogeneity across individuals in the strength of day-to-day dynamics.

All findings remained robust after adjusting for age, subjective health, education, and partnership status (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4).

Discussion

AARC reflects how individuals notice and interpret gains and losses associated with aging in everyday life (Diehl et al., 2014, 2021a). Because social interactions provide frequent and emotionally meaningful information about competence, belonging, and social functioning across adulthood, they represent a particularly relevant context in which such perceptions may arise and be reinforced. Building on this perspective, the present daily diary study examined how fluctuations in AARC-gains and AARC-losses are linked to the perceived positivity and negativity of daily social interactions in middle-aged and older adults, both within the same day and across consecutive days.

Consistent with our hypothesis (H1), AARC was systematically associated with how participants experienced their everyday social encounters. On days characterized by higher-than-usual AARC-gains, individuals reported more positive social interactions. In contrast, days characterized by higher-than-usual AARC-losses were associated with less positive and more negative social interactions. Beyond these concurrent links, only AARC-losses showed temporal carryover (H2), predicting increased negativity in social interactions on the following day, whereas social interaction valence did not predict subsequent changes in AARC across days. These associations remained robust after adjusting for chronological age, subjective health, partnership status, and educational attainment.

These findings are consistent with theoretical accounts describing AARC as reflecting how individuals perceive and evaluate age-related experiences in everyday life (Diehl & Wahl, 2010; Diehl et al., 2021a). From this perspective, fluctuations in AARC may coincide with broader affective and motivational states through which social experiences are interpreted and evaluated. Consistent with this idea, previous micro-longitudinal studies have shown that higher AARC-losses are associated with elevated negative affect, greater stress reactivity, and reduced vitality, whereas associations involving AARC-gains tend to be more selective and linked to more positive daily states (Neupert & Bellingtier, 2017; Wilton-Harding et al., 2022). Related work further suggests that fluctuations in AARC are associated with social approach and avoidance motivations, as well as with daily experiences of autonomy, competence, and social relatedness (Ristl et al., 2025; Windsor et al., 2024). Together, these findings suggest that day-to-day fluctuations in AARC may be accompanied by affective and motivational processes that shape how social interactions are experienced and evaluated.

Notably, the present findings suggest that AARC-losses were more consequential for daily social experience than AARC-gains. AARC-losses were associated with both reduced positivity and increased negativity in social interactions and additionally showed temporal carryover across days, whereas associations involving AARC-gains were limited to positive social interaction valence. This pattern is consistent with broader theoretical accounts proposing that negative aging-related cognitions may exert stronger and more persistent effects than positive aging-related cognitions (Henry et al., 2023). Similarly, research on social relationships and social support suggests that negative interpersonal experiences are often more consequential than positive experiences, such that aversive interactions can outweigh the benefits of supportive exchanges when both are considered simultaneously (Baumeister et al., 2001; Rook & Charles, 2017). One possible explanation is that AARC-losses may be more strongly linked to vulnerability, stress, and negative affective states than AARC-gains and may therefore become more salient in the evaluation of everyday social experiences. Consistent with this interpretation, previous micro-longitudinal studies have shown that AARC-losses are more reliably associated with elevated negative affect and heightened stress reactivity in daily life, whereas associations involving AARC-gains tend to be more selective and context-dependent (Neupert & Bellingtier, 2017; Wilton-Harding et al., 2022; Windsor et al., 2024).

The finding that only AARC-losses predicted next-day negativity further suggests that loss-related experiences may be more likely to persist cognitively and emotionally across daily contexts and into the following day. One possible explanation is the perseverative cognition hypothesis, which posits that negative appraisals can remain cognitively active through processes such as worry, rumination, and anticipatory monitoring, thereby prolonging their emotional impact over time (Brosschot et al., 2006; Ottaviani et al., 2016). This interpretation is also compatible with the Strength and Vulnerability Integration model (SAVI; Charles, 2010), according to which age-related strengths in emotion regulation are most evident when negative experiences can be avoided or de-escalated, whereas prolonged or unavoidable negative social stressors may be particularly consequential in later life. Together, these perspectives suggest that AARC-losses may be especially relevant for understanding how negative social experiences are maintained throughout daily life.

Although the present findings are consistent with theoretical accounts linking AARC and everyday social experience, alternative explanations should also be considered. For example, transient increases in negative affect or daily stress may simultaneously contribute to heightened AARC-losses and more negative evaluations of social interactions.

Surprisingly, despite the central role social experiences are thought to play in theories of subjective aging and AARC, social interaction valence did not predict subsequent changes in AARC across days. This pattern is theoretically consistent with the conceptualization of AARC as an attributional process in which experiences influence subjective aging only when they are interpreted as age-related (Diehl & Wahl, 2010). Rothermund et al. (2021) similarly argued that life experiences influence subjective aging only when they are explicitly interpreted as age-related. Thus, although daily social interactions may convey information about social inclusion, competence, or interpersonal value, these experiences may not necessarily be interpreted in terms of aging. Particularly in the context of the present study, which assessed overall daily social interaction valence rather than explicitly age-related interactions, many positive or negative social experiences may have been perceived primarily as situational or relational events rather than as indicators of age-related change.

At the same time, the absence of next-day effects of social interaction valence on AARC does not contradict previous longitudinal findings demonstrating reciprocal associations between broader subjective views of aging and social functioning (e.g., Cohn-Schwartz et al., 2022; Hu & Li, 2022; Schwartz et al., 2021). Rather, these reciprocal processes may unfold across longer timescales and may require repeated or particularly salient experiences to influence AARC. It is also possible that social experiences that shape subjective aging are relatively infrequent in everyday life, but particularly impactful when they occur.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. The daily assessment interval constrains temporal resolution. The study relied on an evening diary design, which is well-suited to capturing meaningful day-to-day fluctuations but cannot detect processes unfolding within shorter time frames. In addition, the temporal reference frames of the daily measures were not fully aligned, as social interactions referred to experiences “today,” whereas AARC referred to experiences “in the past 24 hours,” which may have reduced temporal precision, particularly for lagged associations across days. Further, if reciprocal influences between social interactions and AARC operate within hours or during specific encounters, such effects may not be observable across days. Accordingly, the absence of lagged effects from daily social interaction valence to next-day AARC should be interpreted as a boundary of the temporal measurement resolution rather than as evidence that short-term social influences on age-related awareness do not occur.

In addition, social interactions were assessed at an aggregated daily level without information on interaction partners or situational context. This limits conclusions about which interpersonal settings are most relevant for age-related awareness. Because AARC depends on whether experiences are explicitly construed as age-relevant, interactions with close partners, encounters involving age-salient roles, or situations that make age particularly noticeable may be more influential than can be detected using overall daily ratings. Aggregation across heterogeneous interactions may therefore dilute age-diagnostic signals that occur in specific interpersonal contexts.

Moreover, the findings were derived from Austrian middle-aged and older adults and should therefore be interpreted within this specific cultural context.

Finally, AARC captures consciously accessible, self-reported AARC-gains and losses. Experiences that influence perceptions of aging at a more implicit or automatic level may therefore remain unobserved in the present design. This conceptual boundary further underscores that not all emotionally salient social experiences are necessarily incorporated into subjective views of aging, but only those that are explicitly attributed to aging and cognitively processed as such. In addition, repeated daily assessment may itself increase participants’ attention to age-related experiences, thereby amplifying observed day-to-day variability in AARC.

Building on these considerations, future studies using shorter assessment intervals (e.g., experience sampling designs) and partner- or context-specific measures of social interactions could be well positioned to capture the micro-temporal dynamics through which age salience and social experience mutually shape one another.

Conclusion

The present study indicates that daily AARC is closely linked to how individuals experience their everyday social interactions. AARC-gains were associated with more positive social interactions, whereas AARC-losses showed broader and more persistent associations, relating to both lower positivity and higher negativity and extending into the following day. These findings highlight AARC-losses as a particularly relevant aspect of subjective aging in daily social life. At the same time, social interaction valence did not predict next-day changes in AARC, suggesting that everyday social experiences may shape subjective aging primarily when they are interpreted as age-related. Together, the findings underscore the value of studying subjective aging as a dynamic process embedded in everyday social life and identify AARC-losses as a potentially important target for future research aimed at understanding and improving everyday social functioning in later adulthood.
