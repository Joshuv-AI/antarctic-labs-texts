The Impact of Community‐Based Health Insurance Scheme on Patient Satisfaction in Debresina, Primary Hospital, North Shewa Zone, Ethiopia: A Comparative Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Tomas Getahun, Bisrat Taye, Mizan Habtemichael, Amanuel Nuredin Abdu, Mosisa Bekele Degefa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsIn developing countries, the impact of Community‐based health insurance on patient satisfaction has not well assessed. This study aimed to assess the impact of community‐based health insurance on patient satisfaction with outpatient services at Debresina Primary Hospital, North Shewa Zone, Ethiopia.MethodA facility‐based comparative cross‐sectional study was conducted from May to June 2023 at Debresina Primary Hospital. Data were collected from 538 (282 CBHI members and 256 non‐members) selected patients attending outpatient care using systematic random sampling. Data were entered into Epi‐Info version 7.2 and analyzed using STATA version 17. A multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify predictors of patient satisfaction. A propensity score matching model was used to determine the impact of community‐based health insurance on patient satisfaction by reducing selection bias and enhancing group comparability.ResultA total of 538 (282 insured and 256 non‐insured) patients were enrolled in the study. Half of the study participants were satisfied with the outpatient care and among those satisfied, 56.4% were insured and 42.2% were non‐insured respondents. Insured patients (AOR = 2.1; 95% CI = 1.38–3.1), married patients (AOR = 2.13; 95% CI = 1.1–4.16), and patients who visited the hospital five or more times per year (AOR = 1.67; 95% CI = 1.04–2.68) were more likely to be satisfied with outpatient services. A community‐based health insurance scheme raises patients' satisfaction with outpatient care by 18% (ATT = 0.175, t = 2.94).ConclusionThe community‐based health insurance scheme has a favorable impact on patient satisfaction with outpatient services. Expanding coverage will significantly contribute to improving patient satisfaction and healthcare experiences, ultimately helping Ethiopia achieve universal health coverage.

Background

Patient satisfaction encompasses the patient's perceived need, expectations from the health care system, and experience with health care. The multifaceted concept of patient satisfaction, which includes both medical and non‐medical components, is a critical indicator of quality in health care [1, 2].

Patient satisfaction is a benchmark used to quantify the delivery of health care services and is used to evaluate the effectiveness of health services delivered in the health system [3, 4]. Patients' input has recently been recognized as an important aspect in the choice to get treatment and provide health care services [5].

Patient satisfaction has an impact on clinical results, patient retention, and medical malpractice lawsuits [6]. According to different studies, dissatisfied individuals are less inclined to take their drugs as prescribed, may skip follow‐up appointments, and may even dissuade family members and friends from getting health care. They are also less likely to use healthcare services, comply with medical treatment, and continue to use healthcare providers [7, 8, 9, 10].

Community‐based health insurance (CBHI) schemes have emerged as the principal means of funding healthcare in many developing nations. The CBHI was launched in Ethiopia in 2011 to achieve universal health coverage by enhancing overall financial protection for healthcare and improving healthcare in the country by mobilizing domestic resources and improving access to high‐quality care [11, 12].

Since the introduction, CBHI enrollment has demonstrated a significant impact on health care utilization and financial security for patients and households, as well as increased internal resources at healthcare facilities, which will be used to improve quality [13, 14, 15, 16].

Even though the CBHI scheme has offered financial protection and increased healthcare service utilization, earlier research on patient satisfaction found that insured and uninsured patients had varied degrees of satisfaction with the services provided at healthcare facilities [17]. There is inconsistent evidence about the impact of community‐based health insurance on patient satisfaction. Some discovered a substantial impact on patient satisfaction [18, 19], whereas others found no significant impact [20, 21, 22, 23].

Despite the discrepancy in evidence, a prior study in Ethiopia has explored patient satisfaction with outpatient treatments, either among insured or without taking insurance status into account [20, 21, 22]. This is limiting their ability to identify the impact of insurance coverage on patient satisfaction. Consequently, evidence on the impact of community‐based health insurance on patient satisfaction remains limited. Therefore, this study addresses this gap by comparing satisfaction levels between insured and uninsured outpatients at Debresina Primary Hospital, North Shewa Zone, Ethiopia.

Methods

Study Design and Area

A facility‐based comparative cross‐sectional study was conducted in Debresina Primary Hospital. The hospital was founded in 2015 and has over 200 professionals and 40 beds. Every year, the facility provides 1000 inpatients, 964 emergency, and 36,651 outpatient services. According to the District Health Information System 2 (DHIS 2) report, over half of these patients are enrolled in CBHI. The facility has been providing services to CBHI clients for the past 6 years through contractual arrangements with the neighboring three districts. The study was carried out between May and June 2023.

Population

The study population consisted of patients attending the outpatient department at Debresina Primary Hospital during the study period. Patients who were CBHI scheme members and non‐members who attended the Debresina primary hospital outpatient department throughout the study period were included in the study. Patients who were unable to respond or who were seriously ill were excluded from the study.

Sample Size Determination

The sample size for the study was determined by the double population proportion formula using EPI Info version 7.2. The sample size was calculated with assumption of the proportion of satisfaction in the CBHI member clients was taken as 60.2% from a study report in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia [24]; and proportion of satisfaction in non CBHI member clients was taken as 48.2% from a study conducted in Dangila Primary hospital, Northwest, Ethiopia(54), 95% confidence interval, 80% power and non‐response rate of 10%. The formula used for the calculation is (53).: n=p1∗(1−p1)+p2∗(1−p2)∗f(α,β)(p1−p2)2

Therefore, the final sample size became 590.

Sampling Procedure

Systematic random sampling was employed to select study participants from outpatients who were seen at the hospital by taking the last 6 months of data as a baseline. The average outpatient population was 15,456 in the first 6 months of the 2015 Ethiopian fiscal year, of which half were CBHI members. On average, about 2,576 and 117 patients were seen each month and day, respectively. Since the data collection period was 1 month, 27 respondents were interviewed at every fourth interval. The first participants were selected using a lottery method.

Data Collection Tool and Method

The data were gathered using a standardized questionnaire that was adapted from other studies of a similar nature carried out in Ghana, Burkina Faso, Ethiopia, and the Ethiopian Health Insurance Pilot Study [12, 17, 18, 20]. The questionnaire was initially drafted in English and then translated into Amharic.

The data were collected using an interviewer‐administered questionnaire from patients during their exit from the facility, and interviewees who were 18 years old and older were interviewed, while caretakers or guardians of those who were below 18 years were interviewed. Three data collectors, two diploma‐level health information technicians, and one BSc Health Informatics professional working in the health office of the district collected data.

To reduce bias, the data collectors were given strict guidance to confine the whole process and information to themselves, and the two diploma‐level health information technicians were assigned to a separate room for the interviews, while the third one identified respondents, provided codes, and collected insurance status on a separate sheet. Data collectors were blinded to patients' insurance status, reducing information bias and strengthening the internal validity of the study.

Variables

Patient satisfaction with outpatient services was the outcome variable of the study. Socio‐demographic characteristics (Age, Sex, Marital status, Educational status, Place of residence, Family size, Monthly income) were explanatory variables, and CBHI status was the treatment variable.

The outcome variable (patient satisfaction with outpatient services) was measured using a 27‐item questionnaire. The response was scored using a five‐level Likert scale of categories. The scores will be: “very good” (5); ‘good’ (4); “somewhat good” (3); “poor” (2); and ‘very poor (1). For each respondent's summary score, a mean patient satisfaction score was calculated. The range of scores is 27 (the minimum score) to 135 (the maximum score). The mean score was used as cut‐off point to decide whether the patient was satisfied with the scheme or not. Patients were labeled as “Satisfied” if the sum score was equal to or above the mean and “Not Satisfied” if the score was below the mean.

Data Quality Management

To ensure the quality of the questionnaire was pre‐tested in Shewarobit Primary Hospital, which is located in the Kewot district, which has a similar setting. The data collection process was closely monitored, and regular communications were maintained with supervisors and data collectors. Each questionnaire was reviewed by supervisors each day to check its completeness and consistency, and the completed questionnaires were rechecked by the principal investigator to maintain the quality of the data.

Data Processing and Analysis

After the completion of data collection, editing, coding, and cleaning were carried out. The data was entered using EPi‐info software version 7.2 and exported into STATA version 17 for analysis. The internal consistency and reliability of the item used to measure satisfaction were checked using Cronbach's alpha, which was found to be 0.9154, confirming internal consistency and reliability. Frequency tables, graphs, and descriptive summaries were used to describe the study variables. Bivariate logistic regression analyses were used to see the significance of the association between the dependent and independent variables. Variables with p‐value ˂0.20 in the bivariate analysis were transferred to the multivariable logistic regression analysis. Adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with 95% Confidence Interval was computed to measure the strength of the association between the outcome and the explanatory variables. P‐value ˂0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A propensity score matching model was used to investigate the impact of CBHI on patient satisfaction with outpatient treatments. Even though all matching algorithms produce the same results, kernel matching was shown to be the most efficient because it makes use of all available observations and provides more efficient estimates with lower variance compared to alternative matching algorithms. Using visual examination, a common support region to indicate the positive likelihood of patients enrolling in CBHI was established. The conditional independence assumption was tested using standardized bias, the t‐test, and pseudo‐R2. The impact of CBHI on patient satisfaction was determined using Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT) by averaging the difference in patient satisfaction between CBHI members and non‐members after matching with the propensity score.

Result

Socio‐Demographic Characteristics of Respondents

Among the study participants, 538 participated in the study, making the response rate 91%. The mean age of study participants was 40.36 ( ± 11.16), and 209 (38.8%) of the study respondents were in the age group of 30 to 39 years. From the total, more than half, 295 (54.8%) of the respondents were male, and 452 (84.0%) of the study respondents were married. About 258 (48.0%) were able to read and write. The majority, 302(56.1%), of study participants' occupations were in agriculture. 318 (59.1%) respondents were rural residents, and 184 (34.2%) of them had an average monthly income of 2500‐3500 ETB. Most of the study respondents, 230 (42.8%), visited the hospital five or more times per year (Table 1).

Patients Satisfaction With Outpatient Services

Patient satisfaction with outpatient care is a composite satisfaction created from the perceived items that measure outpatient service satisfaction. Half of the study participants, 267 (50%), were satisfied with outpatient care. Among the CBHI member study participants, 159 (56.4%) were satisfied with outpatient services, whereas 108 (42.2%) of the CBHI non‐members were satisfied (Figure 1).

Factors Associated With Patient Satisfaction

In a bivariate analysis, CBHI membership, marital status, number of hospital visits per year, and monthly income all have a statistically significant association with patient satisfaction in outpatient services. The multivariable logistic regression analysis included factors that indicated a statistically significant association between patient satisfaction in outpatient services and other variables, with a p‐value of 0.25. In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, CBHI status, respondents' marital status, and number of hospital visits per year were found to be factors with a statistically significant association with patient satisfaction in outpatient services.

Patients insured with the CBHI scheme were two times (AOR = 2.1; 95% CI = 1.38–3.1) more likely to be satisfied with outpatient services compared to those who are not insured. Married patients were about 2 times (AOR = 2.13; 95% CI = 1.1–4.16) more likely to be satisfied with outpatient services than non‐married study respondents. Similarly, respondents who visited hospitals five or more times per year were 1.7 times (AOR = 1.67; 95% CI = 1.04–2.68) more likely to be satisfied than those who visited hospitals one to two times per year (Table 2).

Impact of Community‐Based Health Insurance on Patient Satisfaction

Patient satisfaction with outpatient service was high among CBHI member patients compared to non‐member patients. 56% of CBHI member patients were satisfied with outpatient services, while 42% of non‐member patients were satisfied.

Propensity score matching analysis was used to quantify the impact of community‐based health insurance on patient satisfaction. Kernel matching was used to match 282 CBHI member patients with 256 non‐member patients. According to the findings, enrollment in a community‐based health insurance scheme raises patients' satisfaction with outpatient care by 18% (ATT = 0.175, t = 2.94), as shown in Table 3.

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the impact of community‐based health insurance on patient satisfaction with outpatient services in Debresina Primary Hospital.

The current study findings showed that 50% of study participants were satisfied with outpatient services, which is consistent with studies conducted in Addis Ababa and Dangila, where 47% and 48.2% of patients, respectively, were satisfied with outpatient services they obtained in health facilities [24, 25]. Furthermore, the finding is comparable with the national pooled estimate of patient satisfaction in Ethiopia [26].

The study's findings, however, are lower compared to the findings of studies conducted at Jimma University Hospital (77%), Hawassa University Teaching Hospital (80.1%), Bahirdar Felegehiwot Referral Hospital (57.8%) [27, 28, 29]. This discrepancy may be attributable to the fact that our study was done in a primary hospital while the other studies were conducted in referral and teaching hospitals, which are better equipped, have a more diverse staff of medical professionals, and have better diagnostic equipment, all of which will improve patient satisfaction.

This study's findings revealed that patient satisfaction with outpatient services was 56% among patients enrolled in the CBHI Scheme. The finding is consistent with a study conducted in Addis Ababa and Hadiya, in which 62% and 54.1% of households enrolled in the CBHI scheme were satisfied with the outpatient services they obtained in health facilities [30, 31].

The multiple logistic regressions revealed that patients insured with the CBHI scheme were two times more likely to be satisfied with outpatient services compared to those who were not insured. Similarly, the propensity score matching model indicated that enrollment in a community‐based health insurance scheme raises patients' satisfaction with outpatient care by 18%. This finding is supported by a similar study conducted in North East Ethiopia, Ghana, Burkina Faso, Cameroon, and Indonesia, which indicated community‐based health insurance members had higher satisfaction than their counterparts [17, 18, 19, 32, 33].

This reveals a profound impact of community‐based health insurance on patient satisfaction. The possible explanation could be that having CBHI enrollment provides patients with greater access to healthcare services and provides financial protection, which could contribute to their satisfaction with the overall healthcare experience [13, 14, 15, 16]. Furthermore, CBHI may influence patient satisfaction by enabling facilities to retain additional revenue to enhance service quality, including shorter waiting times and better provider responsiveness. Financial protection and perceived fairness in care delivery among enrolled patients may further contribute to higher satisfaction.

In contrast to this finding, a study conducted in Kazakhstan found that members of insurance plans are less satisfied with health services. This dissatisfaction is attributed to a gap between patient expectations and actual experiences, as patients in Kazakhstan tend to have higher expectations that are not met by the services they receive [23].

The study findings show that married respondents were two times more likely to be satisfied with outpatient services than single respondents. This finding is consistent with a similar study conducted in Nigeria, which found that marital status was a significant predictor of patient satisfaction with healthcare services [34]. This is justified by the fact that married individuals have increased social capital, where spouses provide critical emotional and logistical support, which includes assistance with navigating hospital systems or post‐visit care, which may enhance the perceived quality of the outpatient experience. Additionally, the pooling of household resources may alleviate the financial strain of service fees, which may lead to a more positive evaluation of the care received compared to single respondents who may face these barriers alone.

In a similar manner, respondents in this study who visited hospitals five or more times annually were 1.8 times more likely to be satisfied with outpatient care than those who visited hospitals one to two times annually. This result is in line with a study from Fiji, where researchers found a link between frequent hospital visits and greater levels of patient satisfaction [35]. This may be because patients who visit the hospital more often have built a good rapport with healthcare providers and have better access to healthcare facilities. Providers may also be more familiar with frequent visitors' medical conditions and better able to provide appropriate care.

This study has several strengths, including the use of propensity score matching to reduce selection bias and improve comparability between insured and uninsured patients, as well as its novelty in providing evidence on the impact of community‐based health insurance on patient satisfaction in the Ethiopian context. However, despite the use of Propensity Score Matching(PSM) to enhance the robustness of our findings, the cross‐sectional nature of this study limits our ability to definitively establish a causal impact, and the results should be interpreted as strong statistical associations rather than proof of temporal sequence. Residual confounding may persist despite matching, and satisfaction was self‐reported and may be affected by reporting and social desirability bias. Hence, the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

In general, the study concluded that enrolling in the CBHI scheme had a favorable impact on patient satisfaction with outpatient services and showed that patients insured under the CBHI scheme have a greater degree of satisfaction with outpatient services. Additionally, the frequency of hospital visits and marital status were revealed to be significant predictors of patient satisfaction with outpatient care.

Overall, the impacts of community‐based health insurance on patient satisfaction are significant implications of these findings. As a result, expanding the coverage of community‐based health insurance will make a substantial contribution to raising patient satisfaction levels and overall healthcare experiences for patients, which will help Ethiopia reach its goal of establishing universal health coverage. The policymakers should consider using CBHI revenue to strengthen facility resources and improve provider responsiveness to further enhance patient satisfaction.
