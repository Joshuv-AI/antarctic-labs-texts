AM 3 Safety: Towards Data Efficient Alignment of Multi-modal Multi-turn Safety for MLLMs
Han ZHU, Jiale Chen, Chengkun Cai, Shengjie Sun, Haoran Li, Yujin Zhou, Chi-Min Chan, Pengcheng Wen, Lei Li, Sirui Han, Yike Guo

Abstract
Multi-modal Large Language Models (MLLMs) are increasingly deployed in interactive applications. However, their safety vulnerabilities become pronounced in multi-turn multi-modal scenarios, where harmful intent can be gradually reconstructed across turns, and security protocols fade into oblivion as the conversation progresses. Existing Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) alignment methods are largely developed for single-turn visual question-answer (VQA) task and often require costly manual preference annotations, limiting their effectiveness and scalability in dialogues. To address this challenge, we present InterSafe-V, an open-source multi-modal dialogue dataset containing 11,270 dialogues and 500 specially designed refusal VQA samples. This dataset, constructed through interaction between several models, is designed to more accurately reflect real-world scenarios and includes specialized VQA pairs tailored for specific domains. Building on this dataset, we propose AM 3 Safety, a framework that combines a cold-start refusal phase with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) fine-tuning using turn-aware dual-objective rewards across entire dialogues. Experiments on Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct and LLaVA-NeXT-7B show more than 10% decrease in Attack Success Rate (ASR) together with an increment of at least 8% in harmless dimension and over 13% in helpful dimension of MLLMs on multi-modal multi-turn safety benchmarks, while preserving their general abilities.

Introduction

In recent years, MLLMs have undergone significant advancements and are progressively integrating into various aspects of daily life. Models such as Claude, Gemini, and GPT
(
1
;
5
;
25
)
demonstrate remarkable capabilities on complex multi-modal tasks, including image understanding, front-end coding and geometric reasoning. However, recent studies suggest that when integrating visual components, MLLMs may partially “forget” the safety protocols of their backbone Large Language Models (LLMs)
(
12
;
20
;
35
)
. Furthermore, the safety mechanisms of MLLMs are notably more vulnerable during interactions with humans. Even rudimentary jailbreak strategies such as role-playing, in-context learning, and gradual intent revelation via malicious intent decomposition can manipulate MLLMs into responding with harmful suggestions, misinformation, or content that may pose a risk of real-world harm
(
36
;
13
;
44
)
. As the responsibilities and applications of AI continue to expand, addressing the associated security issue has emerged as a critical imperative.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that RLHF, especially when trained on carefully curated human preference answer pairs, significantly enhances the safety and reasoning capabilities of MLLMs
(
31
;
43
;
10
;
38
)
. MM-DPO, a multi-modal alignment algorithm derived from MM-RLHF that leverages multi-dimensional human ranking of responses incorporates ethicality, faithfulness, and helpfulness as optimization targets throughout training process
(
40
)
. Safe RLHF-V addressed the intrinsic tension between helpfulness and harmlessness, presenting a dedicated optimization scheme that seeks a principled balance between these objectives
(
14
)
. Despite their effectiveness on safety-oriented visual question-answering (VQA) tasks, our results on dialogue safety benchmarks indicate that these aligned models can still generate harmful content at non-trivial rates, as discussed in Section
4.2
. This motivates a closer examination of MLLM safety alignment in conversational settings, where we identify three key challenges: (1)
Insufficient multi-modal dialogue datasets for safety alignment.
Existing open-source multi-modal safety datasets are primarily designed for single-turn VQA and do not adequately capture risks in conversations such as role-playing, multi-turn intent reconstruction, and conversational steering. (2)
Requirement for extensive manual annotation.
Existing datasets necessitate substantial manual annotation of preference data pairs. This cost becomes prohibitive for dialogue data due to longer contexts and larger sample volumes. (3)
Challenges in algorithmic dialogue safety alignment.
Although proposed algorithms have significantly improved the safety and helpfulness of VQA tasks, they remain less effective at mitigating risks in open-ended conversational settings, where models can still produce harmful content unintentionally.

Consequently, an emerging safety issue of MLLMs that warrants increased attention appears:

How can we enhance MLLMs Safety in dialogue while minimizing data annotation costs?

To bridge this gap and advance safety alignment for MLLMs in conversational situations, we propose InterSafe-V, a delicate dialogue safety training dataset containing 11,270 simulated daily conversations through interaction between models and 500 meticulously crafted domain-specific VQA pairs. Our data construction pipeline first decomposes malicious intents and subsequently constructs conversations through interactions between two models. Additionally, we employ Qwen-Image
(
34
)
to generate supplementary visual information when needed. Building on InterSafe-V and drawing inspiration from GRPO
(
30
)
and Safe RLHF-V
(
14
)
, we further introduce AM
3
Safety, a framework designed to optimize both safety and response quality at each conversational turn and throughout entire dialogues. This data efficient alignment approach minimizes the requirement for manual annotation.

In summary, our contributions are as follows:

•

We propose a novel data construction pipeline that generates multi-modal dialogues through model-to-model interaction, eliminating the need for costly manual annotation. Using this pipeline, we release
InterSafe-V
, comprising 11,270 dialogues which include multiple images and 500 well-designed refusal VQA.

•

We introduce
AM
3
Safety
, a GRPO-based framework designed for dialogues safety alignment of MLLMs. This approach combines a cold-start refusal learning phase with GRPO. Crucially, we implement a turn-aware dual-objective reward function that dynamically weighs safety and helpfulness across the entire dialogue history, ensuring consistent safety behavior without compromising response quality.

•

We evaluate our approach on Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct and LLaVA-NeXT-7B across diverse benchmarks, demonstrating that our approach increases at least 8% in harmless dimension and over 13% in helpful dimension of MLLMs and achieve more than 10% decline in ASR on multi-turn multi-modal safety benchmarks.

Related Work

2.1
Dialogue Safety

With the rapid deployment of AI systems, dialogue agents based on LLMs or MLLMs have become widely used, making dialogue safety a central concern for both academia and industry
(
6
;
11
;
3
)
. To mitigate the potential misuse of AI, organizations such as OpenAI has established usage policies that highlight unsafe scenarios, including privacy violations, illegal activities and fraudulent behavior
(
26
)
. Multiple studies have revealed potential risks associated with interacting with LLMs
(
15
;
17
)
. Various safety benchmarks have been developed to assess text-only dialogue safety, such as CoSafe
(
37
)
, SafeDialBench
(
4
)
, and HH-RLHF
(
8
)
.

Beyond text-only settings, recent works highlight dialogue safety in MLLMs. Notably, benchmarks like SafeMT and MMDS
(
44
;
13
)
have been introduced to evaluate the safety of multi-turn multi-modal dialogues. Furthermore, SafeMT and MIRAGE
(
36
)
have identified that the safety mechanisms of MLLMs can be easily circumvented through jailbreak strategies, such as storytelling and role-playing. Despite progress in evaluation, there is still a lack of open and scalable training dataset designed for multi-modal multi-turn dialogue safety alignment.

2.2
MLLMs Safety Alignment

To mitigate safety risks of MLLMs, the community has proposed various alignment approaches aiming at enhancing safety alignment. Representative methods include RLHF-V, MM-RLHF, MMSafe-PO and SPA-VL
(
38
;
40
;
16
;
41
)
. These methods employ Reinforcement Learning (RL) techniques, including Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), to elevate the safety levels of MLLMs
(
27
;
29
)
. Prior work consistently observes a tension between helpfulness and safety during alignment
(
14
;
16
;
43
)
. To address this trade-off, existing methods design reward functions that incorporate safety dimensions while preserving response helpfulness. Nevertheless, these methodologies necessitate substantial amounts of human annotation. Moreover, even after alignment, MLLMs remain vulnerable to jailbreak strategies during conversational exchanges.

Dataset statistics

We open-source a training dataset specifically designed for alignment of MLLMs in dialogue safety with 11,270 dialogues generated by the pipeline shown in Figure
1
and 500 specifically designed VQA pairs. In contrast to other datasets presented in Table
1
, our dataset places a greater emphasis on conversational contexts and incorporates multiple images within these dialogues. Furthermore, to more accurately simulate customer interactions, we employ model interactions for the construction of dialogues. Below are the details of the data construction.

Refusal VQA Pairs

To endow MLLMs with an initial capability to decline responses to malicious intent, we specifically design a cold-start dataset consisting of 500 samples, inspired by
33
. This dataset encompasses three primary categories of questions: 300 samples of general harmful inquiries randomly selected from SPA-VL
(
41
)
, 100 health consultation questions derived from training subset of JailbreakV
(
24
)
, and 100 financial domain questions sourced from the MME-Finance
(
7
)
. For each category, we apply distinct annotation strategies. For general harmful inquiries, we utilize gpt-4o to generate analysis elucidating the potential harm associated with each question with prefix "I’m sorry" to clearly convey the refusal intent. In contrast, for questions pertaining to finance and health consultations, we manually standardize refusal responses, such as "I don’t have the necessary certifications; please consult with a professional expert." This ensures that the models are adept at refusing harmful intent and delivering related responses, particularly in specialized fields.

Step 1: Red-Team Queries Decomposition

We first collect red-team queries from three high-quality multi-modal safety datasets: JailbreakV-28k
(
24
)
training subset, BeaverTails-V
(
14
)
training subset, and SPA-VL
(
41
)
. To enhance the diversity of our dataset, we aggregate 14 safety categories including pornography, violence, and digital crime. Concurrently, we filter out harmful prompts that exhibit high similarity to ensure the uniqueness of all queries.

Compared to simple VQA formats, dialogues introduce a higher level of complexity due to the presence of more potential combinatorial risks and hidden harmful intents
(
36
)
. Our goal in designing dialogues is to ensure that each individual query does not contain overt harmful words or intents, allowing them to easily pass through the guard model. However, through the combination of queries, harmful intents can be pieced together to form problematic responses. To address this, we first employed the InternVL3-78B-Instruct
(
45
)
model to decompose harmful images and harmful query pairs into multiple independent queries.
Once the queries were generated, we input them into a specially designed scoring system for quality and relevance assessment. The scoring objectives focused on three key areas: first, to determine whether the decomposed queries were relevant to their corresponding images; second, to evaluate their degree of association with the original questions; and third, to identify any direct harmful vocabulary present in the queries. If a query passed the quality assessment, we retained it; otherwise, we would regenerate the query. If a query consistently failed to meet the quality scoring filter, that data point would be removed entirely.

Step 2: Dialogue Construction

To more accurately emulate human interactions with MLLMs, we employ the InternVL3-78B
(
45
)
to simulate a safety expert engaged in provoking models to generate harmful content. This “expert” interacts with Kimi-VL
(
32
)
and Qwen2.5-VL-7B
(
9
)
, creating a dynamic and realistic dialogue environment. Unlike traditional methods that often rely on static or simplistic interactions, our approach leverages decomposed queries as prompts for each turn, enhancing the complexity and authenticity of the dialogue. When necessary, the “expert” will provide supplementary image descriptions, while Qwen-Image
(
34
)
, as an external module, will generate images based on these descriptions. Importantly, we regularize the user questions to ensure that images descriptions are not provided to the MLLMs, thereby allowing them to receive images directly in their visual format.

Step 3: Data Cleaning

We conduct a thorough examination of the raw data and identify that multiple generated images often exhibit high similarity within the same conversation. Furthermore, during dialogue interactions, there is probability that the models may gradually deviate from the intended risky topic. This can result in two significant issues: (1) diminished conversational coherence, and (2) elimination of the potential risks within the dialogue. To address these challenges, we employ InternVL3-78B to compare images within dialogues and filter out those that are highly similar. Additionally, to ensure that the dialogues maintained their potential harmfulness and associated risks, we evaluate the generated conversations, retaining only those that contained identifiable risks and harmful elements.

Refusal Template Learning

Inspired by
33
demonstrating that safety of MLLMs can be significantly improved without labor-intensive collection of high-quality malicious data, we adopt a cold-start phase that mixes refusal responses to harmful questions with general queries. Specifically, we construct a dataset where harmful multi-modal queries (e.g., an image depicting dangerous content paired with a malicious question) are paired with refusal answers that provide clear reasoning for the rejection. We perform supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on the base model by minimizing the negative log-likelihood:

\small\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log P_{\theta}(y_{i}|v_{i},x_{i})

(1)

,
where
v_{i}
represents an image,
x_{i}
represents the text query, and
y_{i}
is the target response in our defined format. Our curated dataset comprises approximately 60% refusal scenarios and 40% professional task examples, totaling around 500 instances. This cold-start approach ensures that the model develops stable refusal capabilities with reasoned explanations before entering the RL phase. By establishing safety alignment early, we prevent conflicts between security objectives and task effectiveness during subsequent RL training, thereby avoiding limitation on reward advantage and ensuring that safety constraints do not limit the model’s ability to achieve high rewards on legitimate tasks.

Helpfulness Optimization

Building on our previously trained safety model, we employ GRPO-based algorithm
(
30
)
to optimize the helpfulness during the conversation while ensuring that its safety capabilities. GRPO eliminates the need for a critic model by estimating the baseline from group scores across multiple response candidates. For each multi-turn dialogue, we concatenate the conversation history
\mathcal{H}_{t}
with the current input
x_{t}
as the full context. We sample a group of candidate responses
\{o_{1},o_{2},\ldots,o_{G}\}
from the policy
\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}
and optimize by maximizing:

\small\begin{split}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)&=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|o_{i}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_{i}|}\biggl(\min\biggl(\rho_{i,t}\hat{A}_{i},\\
&\text{clip}\bigl(\rho_{i,t},1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\hat{A}_{i}\biggr)-\beta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}^{(i,t)}\biggr)\biggr]\end{split}

(2)

where
\rho_{i,t}=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i,t}|x,o_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i,t}|x,o_{i,<t})}
is the importance sampling ratio, and the KL divergence is estimated as:

\small\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}^{(i,t)}=\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_{i,t}|x,o_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i,t}|x,o_{i,<t})}-\log\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_{i,t}|x,o_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i,t}|x,o_{i,<t})}-1

(3)

The advantage
\hat{A}_{i}
is computed via group normalization:
\hat{A}_{i}=\frac{r_{i}-\text{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\text{std}(\mathbf{r})}
.

Multi-Turn Dual-Objective Reward Function

Different from single-turn VQA, multi-turn dialogues require maintaining consistent quality throughout the conversation
while considering temporal dependencies. Moreover, not all dialogue turns are equally critical for safety alignment. Several
turns exhibit clear boundaries while others involve ambiguous cases requiring careful optimization. We propose a dual-objective reward function that adaptively emphasizes turns where the model exhibits unstable behavior.

Problem Formulation.

We model multi-turn optimization as a constrained Markov Decision Process. Let
\pi_{\theta}:\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{Q}\to\Delta(\mathcal{O})
be a policy mapping dialogue history
h\in\mathcal{H}
and query
q\in\mathcal{Q}
to a probability distribution over responses
o\in\mathcal{O}
, where
\Delta(\mathcal{O})
denotes the probability simplex. Given our cold-start safety initialization, we formulate the optimization as:

\small\max_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}r_{t}^{\text{help}}\right]\hskip 9.24994pt\text{s.t.}\hskip 9.24994pt\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}^{\text{safe}}\right]\geq\tau

(4)

where
r_{t}^{\text{help}},r_{t}^{\text{safe}}
denote turn-level
scores,
\tau
is a minimum safety threshold, and
\alpha_{t}

are turn-specific importance weights (derived below). In
practice, we convert this constrained formulation to an
unconstrained dual-objective (Equation
9
)
by incorporating safety directly into the reward.

Reward Model.

We employ a learned reward function
R:\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
that evaluates both helpfulness
and safety dimensions at each turn. Conceptually, the reward
can be viewed as the posterior mean under a latent variable
model:

\small r_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{p(\theta_{t}|h_{1:t})}[R(o_{t},\theta_{t})]

(5)

where
\theta_{t}
captures contextual evaluation parameters.

Practical Implementation.
In practice, we instantiate

R
using InternVL3-78B as an automatic evaluator. For each turn
t
,
we prompt the model to assess:

•

r_{t}^{\text{help}}
: Helpfulness score

•

r_{t}^{\text{safe}}
: Safety score

using temperature=0 for deterministic evaluation. The model’s
transformer architecture naturally integrates information
across dialogue history through attention mechanisms,
effectively approximating the marginalization in
Equation
5
.

Safety Variance-Based Turn Weighting.

GRPO generates multiple rollout responses (
N=8
) per instruction
to compute group-relative advantages. We leverage this existing diversity to identify safety-critical turns through safety score
variance, a direct measure of the model’s inconsistency in
maintaining safe behavior.

For each turn
t
, we compute the variance of safety scores
across all rollouts:

\small\text{Var}_{t}^{\text{safe}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(r_{i,t}^{\text{safe}}-\bar{r}_{t}^{\text{safe}}\right)^{2}

(6)

where
r_{i,t}^{\text{safe}}
is the safety score
for turn
t
in rollout
i
, and
\bar{r}_{t}^{\text{safe}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{i,t}^{\text{safe}}
is the mean
safety score across rollouts.

High safety variance indicates the model produces responses with
divergent safety properties, some rollouts may refuse while
others attempt to answer, signaling boundary ambiguity where
the model lacks consistent understanding of safe behavior.
Conversely, low variance indicates stable, consistent safety
across rollouts.

However, low variance may indicate either correct convergence
(consistently safe responses) or incorrect collapse (consistently
unsafe responses). To distinguish these cases, we incorporate
the average safety level:

\small U_{t}=\text{Var}_{t}^{\text{safe}}+\lambda\cdot\max\left(0,\tau-\bar{r}_{t}^{\text{safe}}\right)

(7)

where
\lambda
is the penalty weight and
\tau
is the
safety threshold. The penalty term ensures that turns with low
average safety receive high weight even when variance is low,
preventing the model from collapsing into consistently unsafe
states.

Turn-specific weights are computed via softmax normalization:

\small\alpha_{t}=\frac{\exp(U_{t})}{\sum_{k=1}^{T}\exp(U_{k})}

(8)

Dual-Objective Reward.

Following Safe RLHF-V
(
14
)
,
we compute its total reward by aggregating
turn-level scores with Safety Variance-Based weights:

r=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{t}\left(\beta r_{t}^{\text{help}}+r_{t}^{\text{safe}}\right)

(9)

where
\alpha_{t}
are from Equation
8
, where
\beta
is the coefficient of helpfulness.

Experimental Metrics

To mitigate over-refusal while maintaining safety, we adopt the win rate metric from Safe RLHF-V
(
14
)
for evaluating all safety benchmarks labeled by GPT-4o
(
42
)
using their prompts. This metric jointly assesses helpfulness and harmlessness, defined as:

\text{win rate}=\frac{n_{\text{model1\_win}}}{n_{\text{model1\_win}}+n_{\text{model2\_win}}}

(10)

where
n_{\text{model1\_win}}
and
n_{\text{model2\_win}}
denote the number of wins for each model in pairwise comparisons. In addition to the relative metric of win rate, we also evaluate the model’s effectiveness in identifying malicious intent and preventing the generation of harmful responses using the absolute metric of Attack Success Rate (ASR) where
ASR=\frac{\sum^{|D|}_{1}I(Q_{i})}{|D|}
where
I(\cdot)=1
if the response is judged as unsafe and
I(\cdot)=0
otherwise.
Similarly, for ConvBench, we assess multi-turn conversational performance across perception, reasoning, and creation levels by comparing model responses against human-verified references. For reasoning benchmarks including ScienceQA, MathVista, Geometry3k and MathVerse, we calculate their accuracy as the evaluation metric.

For MMDU
(
21
)
, we follow the official protocol using GPT-4o to score multi-turn, multi-image dialogues on a 0-10 scale. We report the final score as the average across all dimensions, turns, and samples.

Conclusion

Despite their promising performance on single-turn VQA safety benchmarks, existing safety alignment approaches often overlook safety concerns in conversational scenarios. To address this critical gap, we propose InterSafe-V, alongside a two-step training framework AM
3
Safety tailored for safety alignment, aiming to mitigate the risk of MLLMs generating harmful advice or content during conversational interactions. Our training dataset comprises 11,270 dialogues and 500 specially curated VQA pairs dedicated to refusal template learning. The proposed training framework integrates refusal template learning and GRPO-based fine-tuning to achieve the dual objectives of ensuring safety and maintaining helpfulness in user interactions. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our method not only achieves superior performance on multi-modal multi-turn safety benchmarks but also substantially reduces the high cost of extensive manual annotation required by state-of-the-art alternatives. This work provides a practical and data efficient solution for advancing safety alignment for MLLMs during interaction with users.

Limitations

Despite the promising results of AM
3
Safety in general safety alignment, our current evaluation framework has notable constraints regarding robustness and model scope. Specifically, because we did not perform tests on dedicated adversarial jailbreak benchmarks, the model’s actual robustness against intentional exploits remains uncertain and empirically unverified. Furthermore, our investigation has been limited to general-purpose multi-modal models. We have not yet assessed how this training paradigm impacts or benefits dedicated reasoning models, where the interaction between safety constraints and complex logical deduction requires further analysis.

Additionally, our reliance on a stronger, external model for
LLM-as-a-judge
supervision introduces a dependency on the quality and biases of that teacher model. While effective for scaling supervision, this approach does not fully leverage the potential for the model to internalize safety principles through self-reflection. Future iterations could explore self-correction mechanisms, where the model scores its own generations to iteratively refine its capabilities, potentially reducing reliance on external judges and improving alignment autonomy.

Ethics Statement

We are committed to conducting this research in accordance with high ethical standards. The proposed InterSafe-V is an open-source dataset and alignment framework aimed at advancing multi-modal, multi-turn safety alignment for MLLMs, including dialogues and refusal-style samples built from red-team sources and model interactions. We acknowledge an inherent dual-use risk: the dialogue structures and decomposed multi-turn queries, if misused, could be adapted to jailbreak or stress-test other models. Our objective is to reduce harmful outputs in realistic conversational settings, where intent can be gradually reconstructed across turns and minimizing manual annotation cost. We hope our dataset and AM
3
Safety framework contribute to safer deployment of MLLMs and encourage both academia and industry to pay greater attention to multi-turn multi-modal safety. As the authors, we do not wish to see any negative societal impact resulting from misuse of this work, and we encourage responsible use, auditing, and appropriate access controls when applying or releasing derived models.

C.1
Experimental Setup

In our experiments, we utilized a total of 16 NVIDIA H800 GPUs (80 GB). We setup gradient_accumulation_steps equals to 2, learning rate=
1\times 10^{-5}
with 2 epochs on 8 GPUs for our cold-start procedure. For the reward model, InternVL3-78B was deployed on 8 GPUs using the vLLM engine with a default temperature and a maximum model length of 16,384 tokens. We setup gradient_accumulation_steps equals to 2 and applied 8 GPUs for our cold-start procedure. The GRPO training was conducted on a separate single node with 8 GPUs. For each prompt, the policy model generates G=8 rollouts to estimate group relative rewards, with the maximum prompt and response lengths both set to 1,024 tokens. The actor is optimized using a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-6}
and a PPO mini-batch size of 8, with a total training batch size of 128. To ensure training stability, we apply a low-variance KL divergence loss with a coefficient of 0.001. We train GRPO process for 10 epochs with 7,000 samples and reproducing the whole training progress on one model take around 3 days following our parameters. We conducted all benchmark tests in a single round, and for win rate evaluation, we employed an average of bidirectional assessments to mitigate the effects associated with the ranking of responses.
