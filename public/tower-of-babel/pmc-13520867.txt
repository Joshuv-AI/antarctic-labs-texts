Understanding competencies to manage multimorbidity in primary care: A focus group study with professionals and patients
Authors: Leslie J Michielsen, Lena HA Raaijmakers, Miranda Laurant, Erik Bischoff, Geert M Rutten, Tjard R Schermer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic conditions often occur as multimorbidity, where patients experience multiple chronic conditions simultaneously. Many developed countries have implemented disease management programmes (DMP) in primary care to address these complex needs, but their success largely depends on the competencies of healthcare professionals. Equipping primary care professionals with the competencies required for person-centred integrated care (PC-IC) is therefore essential.AimThis study aims to 1(1) identify the competencies primary care professionals consider essential for providing PC-IC to patients with chronic conditions and multimorbidity and examine their alignment with patient preferences, and 2(2) explore professionals’ training needs.MethodsThis qualitative study used focus group interviews to explore perspectives on PC-IC. A convenience sample included 14 primary care professionals (general practitioners and practice nurses) and a purposive sample included 10 patients with one or more chronic conditions.ResultsPrimary care professionals identified person-centred attitudes, communication skills and interprofessional collaboration as essential competencies for delivering person-centred integrated care. Patients similarly valued being heard, understood, and actively involved in decision-making, while additionally emphasising continuity and coordinated care. Professionals also emphasised ongoing training, reflective practice groups, case discussions, and practical communication tools as important for developing these competencies.ConclusionsSuccessful implementation of PC-IC requires more than disease-specific expertise. It depends on individual competencies, effective interprofessional collaboration, and organisational conditions that support coordinated, person-centred care. Ongoing professional development may help strengthen these competencies in primary care.Keywords: Person-centred integrated care, primary care, chronic disease, interprofessional collaboration, competencies

Introduction

Chronic conditions, including cardiovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and type 2 diabetes mellitus, pose significant challenges to global healthcare [1]. Definitions of chronic disease vary across organisations and studies; however, chronic conditions are generally described as conditions that persist for at least one year and require ongoing medical care, interfere with activities of daily living, or both [2]. Chronic conditions also have the propensity to manifest as multimorbidity, where patients have multiple health conditions simultaneously [3]. Approximately one in three adults suffers from more than one chronic disease [1,3]. The rise in life expectancy and unfavourable health related behaviours contribute to the growing prevalence of multimorbidity [1].

There is a need for a new perspective, emphasising the interconnected nature of chronic conditions and the broader physical, psychological, cultural and social dimensions of health [4]. Patients with multimorbidity often need integrated regimens and coordinated care across professionals. Care fragmentation is a major challenge for patients, as multiple healthcare professionals may not communicate effectively, and their well-intentioned interventions can have suboptimal combined effects [5].

A PC-IC approach places the individual at the core of service delivery and ensures that services are coordinated and responsive to individual’s unique needs and abilities [6,7]. Person-centred conversations help patients identify needs, express preferences, participate in decisions, and feel empowered [7,8]. With PC-IC the healthcare professionals look beyond the disease and honour the individual’s choices, background, and priorities.

Integrated care provides seamless, coordinated services across settings (including prevention, treatment, rehabilitation, and palliative care) to meet patients’ needs [9]. A dynamic, evolving care plan tailored to the changing needs and goals of patients with chronic illnesses and multimorbidity is key to effective integrated care [10]. Research shows that integrated care teams improve outcomes, with more patients achieving treatment goals than those receiving usual primary care for multimorbidity [11].

Many developed countries have implemented chronic DMP’s, but these often focus on a single condition and do not adequately address multiple chronic conditions or other factors affection their care. In the Netherlands, however, a new DMP has recently shifted towards a more person-centred and integrated model in primary care [12]. Unlike traditional DMPs that primarily follow standardised clinical pathways, PC-IC emphasises care that is guided by patients’ values, preferences, and individual circumstances. Through shared decision-making, patients and healthcare professionals jointly establish treatment goals and identify the support needed to manage chronic conditions effectively [12].

The success of PC-IC programs depends on professionals’ expertise, but barriers such as poor interprofessional communication, unclear roles, and limited patient-centred skills can hinder collaboration and care coordination [13]. Hence, healthcare professionals need to be equipped with the requisite knowledge and competencies to deliver PC-IC to their patients.

The aim of this study is twofold. First, the study seeks to identify the competencies primary care professionals consider necessary for providing PC-IC to patients with chronic conditions and multimorbidity and assess to what extent these competencies align with patient preferences. Second, the study aims to enhance understanding of the training needs of primary care professionals in these competencies.

Methods

Setting and study design

From March to December 2021, three large general practitioner care cooperatives in the Netherlands, serving approximately 880,000 residents in three regions, were involved in a feasibility study testing a new person-centred and integrated approach for patients with one or more chronic conditions [14]. Seven general practices replaced disease-specific DMP’s for cardiovascular disease, COPD, and type 2 diabetes mellitus with a new PC-IC approach. Consultation was guided by the Positive Health framework, using the Positive Health ‘Spider Web’ to identify patients’ priorities across multiple health domains and to support shared goal setting and personalised care planning [15]. The participating practices were located in both urban and rural regions.

For the current study, a qualitative design using focus group interviews was employed. Four focus groups were conducted: two with general practitioners (GPs) and practice nurses (PNs) (both sessions n = 7) and two with patients (both sessions n = 5). Participants were recruited from the seven practices that participated in the feasibility study [14]. Healthcare professionals were recruited through convenience sampling, as they had participated in the feasibility study and thus had experience with the PC-IC approach. The healthcare professionals assisted in identifying and inviting patients, while purposive sampling was used to ensure variation in patient age, gender, and educational level. All participating patients had one or more chronic conditions.

All participants received written study information and provided informed consent. They were informed that participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time without providing a reason. Consent forms were returned using a prepaid return envelope. At the start of each online focus group, participants additionally provided verbal consent for audio recording.

The semi-structured interview guide was developed by the research team using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR). In addition to implementation-related questions, questions addressing competencies required for PC-IC and associated educational needs were added for the present study. The guide was reviewed by five researchers with expertise in qualitative research from the project’s research group prior to data collection.

Interview procedure

The focus group interviews took place between November and December 2021, during the Covid-19 pandemic. Due to pandemic restrictions, all focus group interviews were conducted virtually via Zoom. Each session lasted between 60 and 90 min. The sessions were led by trained moderators experienced in qualitative research methods. Each focus group began with introductions, a brief explanation of the study purpose, and an overview of discussion guidelines. The moderator followed the semi-structured interview guide designed to identify the competencies and to explore the training needs of professionals to deliver a new PC-IC program for patients with chronic conditions. Towards the end of each session, the moderator summarised key points that were discussed and invited participants to provide any final comments or reflections. The study was reported according to the Standards for reporting qualitative research by O’Brien et al. [16].

Data processing and analysis

The focus group interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim. Transcripts were uploaded and thematically analysed with an inductive approach. The software program ATLAS.ti was used for qualitative data analysis [17]. Interviews with professionals were analysed separately from the interviews with patients. Two researchers (LM and LR) independently coded the transcripts before comparing their coding. Minor differences in coding and interpretation were discussed until consensus was reached. No major disagreements requiring the involvement of a third researcher arose during the analysis. After two rounds of coding, the codes were categorised into themes and subthemes, allowing for the identification of patterns and variations in participants’ responses across the different sessions. The analysis aimed to identify overarching constructs and the underlying factors within participants’ responses.

Data were anonymised using unique identification numbers (research IDs) for healthcare professionals and patients to ensure confidentiality. In the results section, we applied these research IDs with specific abbreviations to differentiate participant groups: general practitioners were labelled as GP+ID, practice nurses as PN+ID, and patients as P + ID.

Results

Healthcare professionals

A total of 14 healthcare professionals (female n = 12) participated in the study. The sample consisted of 6 GPs and 8 PNs. No other demographics were registered.

Patients

In total, 10 patients (female n = 4) participated in the study with ages ranging from 44 to 71 years. Participants’ educational backgrounds varied, including secondary vocational training (n = 2), higher professional education (college level) (n = 5), and university degrees (n = 3). All participating patients had chronic illnesses, including COPD, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, hypercholesterolaemia, and sarcoidosis. Half of them (n = 5) had multimorbidity, defined as having more than one chronic condition.

Results of the interviews

The analysis of the focus group interviews identified three overarching competency constructs: attitudinal factors, communications skills, and interprofessional collaboration. The findings are presented across three interconnected levels: individual-level competencies, team-level competencies, and organisational conditions supporting PC-IC (Table 1). While healthcare professionals and patients largely agreed on the overarching competency constructs, differences emerged in the underlying factors and organisational conditions considered important for successful PC-IC.

Individual level competencies

Attitude

Participants identified several attitudes that were considered essential for delivering PC-IC (Table 1). Both, healthcare professionals and patients highlighted the importance of empathy in connecting with patients and understanding their needs. Patients expect healthcare professionals to be attentive, compassionate, and genuinely interested in their lives beyond the illness, especially when they carry emotional burdens, such as the past loss of a child.

P-D5: ‘Well, without having to go into the details over and over again which isn’t necessary at all, but just that she understands or notices that sometimes I have something holding me back’.

They also mentioned that they see their healthcare provider as someone to talk to. They appreciate it when the healthcare provider demonstrates openness, shows genuine interest, and takes the time to just listen, even though the visit is primarily for a medical consultation.

P-D1: ‘You are there for your checkup of course, but just a little social talk to see how you are doing. That I find very important.’

Both professionals and patients underlined the importance of an adaptable attitude that is required when professionals and patients engage in more complex conversations. As a healthcare provider, it is important to adapt to the person you are dealing with. Some patients are more reserved, while others speak freely. This means that the healthcare provider must be able to adapt and customise the care for individuals.

P-D3: ‘They don’t only have to be nice all the time. Every now and then she can give me the honest facts and tell me that a change is necessary.’

For healthcare professionals being adaptable and determined also entails a critical attitude towards what you find important or less important. That you need courage to deviate from the protocol and follow your gut feeling and explore what else is important for the patient.

PN-D1: ‘So I’m quite critical about what I’m going to ask from the checklist. Because the goal is to see what the patient wants.’

Patients feel that, when necessary, the healthcare provider should show determination by clearly presenting the facts and firmly stating that a change must happen. In addition, professionals also mentioned the importance of being nonjudgemental for that will allow patients to speak their mind even though it might not be what you, as healthcare provider, want to hear.

PN-D2: ‘If you don’t judge them for the way they live their life, the conversation will still be open, unlike if you judge them and tell them they shouldn’t smoke, for instance.’

One healthcare professional noted that curiosity and patience are vital for exploring patients’ experiences and for uncovering solutions to challenges. Being patient enables healthcare professionals to lean back and give the patient time to think and to express their thoughts.

GP-D14: ‘Especially how I encourage the patient to think and share their story. That requires a bit of patience.’

Patients express that shared decision-making is crucial to them. This is not only about having the final say but also about empowerment. Taking part in the decision-making process helps them maintain control over their own lives and treatment. Being attentive is highly appreciated by patients. When the healthcare provider notices little things and mentions them, like giving a compliment if something is going well. That makes it more personal and less about the disease.

P-D9: ‘Well, I lost a lot of weight and she noticed and complimented me on that. That felt really good and boosted my confidence!’.

Communication skills

Active listening, a coaching ability and curious inquiry were mentioned as important communication skills by healthcare professionals and patients. These skills were considered essential for building meaningful relationships with patients to ensure that the care is person-centred. Active listening and ‘leaning back’ were recognised as a key skill but challenging to apply.

GP-D11: ‘I actually find it very difficult to keep my mouth shut and leave the initiative with the patient. .Normally I tend to steer the conversation more myself and keep control.’

Active listening and ‘leaning back’ enables healthcare professionals to fully understand their patients’ needs without prematurely offering advice.

PN-D9: ‘Listening, not filling in. Not interrupting. Not trying to solve. If someone is struggling with something and wants to share, it’s important to let them tell their story first’.

Healthcare professionals emphasised the value of interpretive listening, picking up on unspoken concerns and asking thoughtful follow-up questions to gain more insight into possible unspoken concerns. They acknowledged there’s still room to grow in this skill.

PN-D2: ‘How do you read between the lines so you can ask the right questions?’

Patients highly appreciated healthcare professionals’ active listening skill, because it made them feel heard, understood, and recognised as individuals.

P-D8: ‘I think personal attention and being seen is important. So you feel that someone is listening to you. Creating that is possible withing a few minutes. If you feel at ease, you will be open to change’.

Many healthcare professionals advocated for adopting a coaching role and stressed the importance of restraint when offering advice.

GP-D5: ‘Never give your advice… as in telling the patient what you would do in their circumstances. Let the patient make his or her own goals. It shouldn’t be your goal.’

Asking the right questions invites the patient to think and guides them towards finding their own solutions.

PN-D4: ‘Well, that you let the patient do more of the work themselves. That by asking them questions, they have to start thinking about it themselves and come up with solutions on their own.’

Patients noticed a shift in time, where previously healthcare provider to patient communication was a one-way street in which patients were told what to do and when to do it. Nowadays the healthcare provider is more a partner or a coach who helps the patient figure out what is best for them as an individual.

P-D1: ‘Before they just let you know what the plan was and that was the way it would go. Now you have a conversation about different options and explore different options to find the best solution for you. That I find a lot more satisfying.’

Curious inquiry was highlighted by healthcare professionals as an essential skill to encourage deeper conversations. Asking open questions helps gather broader information that may also be important and find out what is important to the patient. Patients appreciate it when their healthcare provider also shows interest in more than just the disease. Not only does this effect the relationship in a positive manner but this can also be important if certain aspects have a negative effect on the disease.

P-D2: ‘That other aspects of someone’s life situation are regularly considered, as this can influence how they perceive their illness in the first place.’

Healthcare professionals also mentioned summarising as a communication skill that might help patients with remembering important information received during consultations.

Team level competencies

Interprofessional collaboration

Beyond individual competencies, participants emphasised that delivering PC-IC also requires effective collaboration at team level. Healthcare professionals and patients emphasised that sharing information is vital to successful interprofessional collaboration and effective teamwork. Healthcare professionals also emphasised shared objectives to avoid conflicting advice to patients. Moreover, they highlighted the importance of building a broad professional cross-domain network, particularly incorporating connections within the social domain, to provide comprehensive support for patients. However, cross-domain referral or collaboration requires understanding of each other’s profession and mutual trust.

GP-D14: ‘… with people you also personally know. Because on paper, sure, we know people, but if I never see them and don’t really know what kind of person they are, I’m less inclined to refer to them.’

For some, building a network is personally challenging, but it becomes even more difficult when faced with an overwhelming number of healthcare professionals to choose from. In larger cities, the options can feel intimidating, making it hard to establish a strong and reliable network.

PN-D8: ‘The number of healthcare professionals that we, or patients I should say, can choose from are enormous. There are so many options. And that makes it very difficult to establish a fixed point of contact.’

Organisational conditions supporting PC-IC

Beyond effective collaboration between professionals, patients highlighted several organisational conditions that they considered essential for successful PC-IC. These conditions relate to how care is organised and coordinated, rather than to the competencies of individual healthcare professionals or healthcare teams.

Patients specifically mentioned the provision of seamless care as very important. When dealing with multiple healthcare professionals, patients feel that there is a risk of insufficient coordination between the different professionals, leading to inconsistencies in information and treatment. They also don’t want to repeatedly explain their medical history or repeat previous discussions every time they visit a healthcare provider. When care involves multiple healthcare professionals, they prefer a designated single point of contact or case manager who maintained an overview of their care and who coordinates it effectively. Some patients do wonder if it’s necessary to have so many healthcare professionals at the same time.

P-D6:’But sometimes I wonder, … …because you are in a practice where there are four or five doors, and behind each door someone is doing a different important job…’.

Patients prefer that all aspects of a patient’s treatment and support are well-coordinated, integrated and without unnecessary gaps. Even when there is a shared patient information system the patients notice that it is difficult to prevent fragmentation of care.

P-D7: ‘… that one person doesn’t have all the information anymore. It’s in the system but then your GP reads your issues or history from the screen…’.

Healthcare professionals expressed that ongoing training and reflection are invaluable for enhancing their skills and adaptability. A follow-up training on PC-IC after an initial period would be highly appreciated, as it enables reflection on experiences and refine their approaches.

PN-D8: ‘The basics are there, but how to improve even further, that’s where a refresher course or meeting could come in handy.’

Also, communication skills enhancement emerged as a theme. Several participants emphasised the practicality of having example questions for an open dialogue, including open-ended and follow-up questions to keep the conversation flowing and meaningful. They noted that such tools facilitate effective patient interactions.

PN-D10 ‘How do you ask open questions? In our practice we all felt the need for example questions. Of course, you make notes but it would be very helpful if there were example questions. Sometime when you ask a question you immediately realise that the question is not open at all.’

Participants also highlighted that specific communication strategies tailored for patients with limited health literacy are helpful in improving understanding and engagement.

Healthcare professionals also expressed a need for team-based learning. Training the entire practice team fosters a sense of unity and helps the team collectively adapt to changes within the healthcare environment.

GP-D14: ‘We completed our last course with the whole team…. All practice nurses, all GPs and even the manager’

Observational learning and feedback could provide insights on what otherwise could go unnoticed and this would enable professionals to identify both strengths and areas for improvement. Observing consultations, such as medical assistants shadowing doctors, was also described as a powerful method for gaining practical knowledge. Hands-on experience was consistently cited as crucial for building confidence and professional growth.

PN-D2: ‘I think you just need to practice a lot, working in this new way and repeating it often. You will learn every time, and you don’t have to be perfect.’

Reflective practice groups and case discussions were frequently mentioned as valuable opportunities for collaboration and shared learning. Also, for discussing difficult situations with each other.

GP-D13: ‘Embedding and aligning in the form of peer consultation. Asking how others work and discussing which changes they notice.’

Finally, experiential learning – i.e. gaining confidence and skills by experience – was considered important. Several participants expressed a desire for more time and space to engage in meaningful conversations. One participant noted that with experience, these conversations become easier and more natural, although confidence develops over time. Another emphasised the importance of consistently applying communication techniques, highlighting regular use to developing these skills.

Discussion

The main aim of this study was to identify the core competencies healthcare professionals require in providing PC-IC to patients with chronic illness and multimorbidity, and to explore the extent to which these competencies align with patient preferences. Our findings demonstrate that successful PC-IC depends on several interconnected levels. At the individual level, attitudinal factors and strong communication skills were identified as essential. At team level, effective interprofessional collaboration emerged as a key prerequisite for delivering coordinated care. In addition, patients highlighted several organisational conditions, including continuity of care and effective care coordination, as essential for translating these competencies into high-quality PC-IC. This study also identified several educational needs, including ongoing training and reflection, communication skills, team-based learning, opportunities for observational learning with feedback, peer learning through case discussions, and experiential learning.

Healthcare professionals and patients agreed that treating individuals as whole persons, rather than merely focusing on their illness, is fundamental to PC-IC. This highlights empathy and compassion as key attitudinal factors, supported by a holistic, person-centred approach to communication [18–20]. Such interactions can have a lasting impact on patients’ experiences and health outcomes [18].

Communication skills emerged as a central theme across both groups. Previous research similarly highlights the importance of active listening, a coaching-style conversation and an open, two-way dialogue [13,19–21]. Active listening has been shown to positively influence patients’ appraisal of health-related experiences [22]. Healthcare professionals in our study further emphasised interpretive and reflective listening, consistent which previous literature describing reflective listening as interpretating verbal and non-verbal communication and checking this interpretation with the patient [23,24].

While healthcare professionals emphasised the competencies required for effective interprofessional collaboration, patients focused on the organisational conditions needed to translate collaboration into coordinated, person-centred care. They reported fragmented care, poor coordination between professionals, and conflicting advice, consistent with previous research on barriers to effective PC-IC [25,26]. To address these challenges, patients preferred a designated point of contact, such as a case manager, to ensure continuity and coordination. Previous research shows that case management reduces fragmentation while improving patient satisfaction and health outcomes [27,28].

Regarding education, healthcare professionals particularly valued practical communication tools, and experiential learning opportunities. They felt that follow-up training would provide opportunities for reflection and improvement of their approach. These insights align with existing literature emphasising the importance of continuous professional development and reflective practice for sustaining high-quality, person-centred care [29]. Even experienced healthcare professionals valued reflective practice groups and case discussions. These activities promoted shared learning and discussion of complex cases.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of our study is its focus on PC-IC for patients with chronic conditions and multimorbidity, providing insights relevant for health policy, education, and workforce development to support person-centred, integrated care. Strengths include its focus on primary care, where most care for these patients is delivered, and the integration of healthcare professional and patient perspectives, providing a comprehensive understanding of the competencies required for PC-IC.

Unlike studies exploring anticipated competencies before implementation, this study examined competencies identified after healthcare professionals and patients had practical experience with the PC-IC approach. This enabled the identification of competencies that have received limited attention in previous literature.

The study also has limitations. First, only two focus groups were conducted with patients and two with GP’s and PNs. This approach is consistent with methodological guidance indicating that two focus groups per participant stratum generally provide a comprehensive understanding of the principal issues, while additional groups are likely to yield limited additional insights [30]. Data were collected during the COVID-19 pandemic, limiting recruitment and requiring online focus groups. Consequently, spontaneous discussion was more challenging, potentially affecting group dynamics and engagement.

Selection bias may have occurred because the patient sample mainly consisted of patients with higher health literacy or perceived articulateness. Patients with limited language proficiency may have been underrepresented. Consequently, the findings may not fully reflect the experiences of patients with lower health literacy and language barriers. Future research should actively include these groups.

Finally, although GPs and PNs play a central role in coordination care for patients with chronic illness and multimorbidity in Dutch primary care, our professional sample was limited to these groups. The perspectives of other professionals commonly involved in integrated care, such as social workers, mental health professionals, physiotherapists, and dietitians, could have offered valuable insights into interprofessional collaboration and competencies required for PC-IC.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that implementing PC-IC in primary care requires more than disease-specific expertise. It calls for a person-centred approach by GPs and PNs grounded in individual competencies such as attentive listening, empathy, and adaptability, supported by effective interprofessional collaboration and organisational conditions that enable coordinated, person-centred care. Together, these competencies and organisational conditions reflect what patients with chronic illnesses identify as most important: being heard, understood, and actively involved in decisions concerning their lives. Ongoing training supports shared learning and reflection and is valued even by experienced healthcare professionals. Embedding such approaches can foster continuous professional growth while ensuring care remains aligned with patient values and needs.
