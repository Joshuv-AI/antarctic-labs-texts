Background

Hydatidiform mole is a benign gestational trophoblastic disease (GTD) that has two types, complete and partial. The incidence of molar pregnancy is around 1–2 per 1000 deliveries, and the prevalence is highest among Asians [1].

The diagnosis and follow-up of patients with molar pregnancy are important because there are reports of progression of molar pregnancy to invasive mole and choriocarcinoma [2].

Partial molar pregnancy is usually presented as a missed or incomplete miscarriage in the first trimester of pregnancy [3].

The coincidence of a molar pregnancy coexists with a live molar pregnancy fetus is rare; about 0.005–0.01% of all pregnancies. It also has some risks for both the mother and the fetus. The most common risk is preeclampsia [4, 5].

Many pregnancies cannot continue due to fetal triploidy, severe intrauterine growth restriction, and abnormal placental function. If the molar pregnancy continues, its management is challenging [2].

We report the rare case of partial hydatidiform molar pregnancy with a live fetus within 5–6 lunar months of pregnancy.

Case presentation

A dead fetus and placenta product of normal vaginal delivery (NVD) arrived at the Shahid Mohammedi lab hospital. This dead fetus belonged to the first pregnancy of a 21-year-old Persian woman, grouping o+, without notable past medical history and normal prenatal check-ups.

The fetus was 150 g and did not have any gross anomalies, and the external genitalia were compatible with the male gender. His crown-rump length (CRL), crown-heel length (CHL), and head circumference (HC) measurements were 15, 26, and 18.5 cm, which were compatible with 5–6 lunar months of pregnancy. In a Y incision of the chest and abdomen, all internal organs were present without gross anomaly, and for histopathology evaluation, tissue sampling from all organs was done. The placenta was intact with an attached umbilical cord and was measuring 18 × 16 × 3 cm with a 600-g weight. The placenta grossly showed many grape-like vesicular cystic structures with hemorrhagic parenchyma that were compatible with molar pregnancy. The umbilical cord was grossly normal with central insertion and showed three vessels on the cross-section surface (two arteries and one vein). Microscopic evaluation of hematoxylin and eosin (H and E) stain slides prepared from fetal tissue sampling showed no significant pathological abnormality. Microscopic evaluation of the placenta showed large hydropic villi with cistern formation, scalloping, and peripheral trophoblastic proliferation, admixed with normal and small villi, as well as fibrotic villi that were compatible with partial molar pregnancy (Figs. 1 and 2).

Discussion

Hydatidiform mole is a benign gestational trophoblastic disease (GTD) that has two types, complete and partial. [1] Complete molar pregnancy has a diploid karyotype, but partial molar pregnancy has a triploid karyotype 69 XXY, 69 XXX, and 69 XYY due to fertilization of one ovum by two sperm. Hydatiform molar pregnancy with a viable fetus presents as three types; the first is twin pregnancy with one normal fetus and one complete mole, which is the common type. The second is a twin pregnancy with one normal fetus and a partial mole. The third is a partial mole with one normal fetus, which is a rare type [ 6]. Our case is the third type.

In the presence of triploidy, the fetus will not survive birth due to multiple malformations and severe intrauterine growth retardation caused by impaired placental circulation [7].

The incidence of hydatidiform mole is high in women under 20 years of age (10.52/1000 births) and 39 years of age (5.95/1000 births) [7]‏.

The frequency of molar pregnancy increases to 2 in 10,000 pregnancies owing to assisted reproductive technologies (ART), such as in vitro fertilization (IVF) and intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) [8].

Patients with molar pregnancies may present with different signs and symptoms such as vaginal bleeding, a large uterus, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, hyperthyroidism, hypertension, and proteinuria. In the laboratory, significant β-HCG (human chorionic gonadotropin) serum levels are elevated [9]. Our case did not have these symptoms, and without any past medical or surgical history.

The hydatidiform mole is characterized by focal hydropic degeneration, multicystic appearance, cistern formations, peripheral trophoblastic hyperplasia of the placenta with a gestational sac, and a fetus with high serum levels of β-HCG [10, 11].

The diagnosis and follow-up of patients with molar pregnancy are important because there are reports of progression to invasive mole and choriocarcinoma [2].

Partial molar pregnancy is usually presented as a missed or incomplete miscarriage in the first trimester of pregnancy [3].

The coincidence of a molar pregnancy coexisting with a live molar pregnancy fetus is rare; about 0.005–0.01% of all pregnancies. It also has some risks for both the mother and the fetus. The most common risk is preeclampsia, then eclampsia, pregnancy bleeding, anemia, placental abruption, premature birth, and congenital anomalies that lead to neonatal morbidity and mortality [4, 5, 12].

Many pregnancies cannot continue due to fetal triploidy, severe intrauterine growth restriction, and abnormal placental function [2].

To achieve good outcomes, careful monitoring of both mother and fetus is advised [13].

Conclusion

The coexistence of a molar pregnancy with a live fetus is a rare condition, and it increases the risk of neonatal morbidity and mortality.

To achieve good outcomes, close follow-up of mother and fetus is advised, but the best way is yet unknown.
