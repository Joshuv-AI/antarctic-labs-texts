Invasive pulmonary aspergillosis caused by a rare species of Aspergillus turcosus: case report and literature review
Authors: Wei-Ting Lin, Pao-Yu Chen, Tzong-Yow Wu, Jheng-Wei Lin, Song-Chou Hsieh, Yee-Chun Chen
Published: 2026

Introduction

Invasive aspergillosis (IA) remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality among immunocompromised and critically ill patients. Although Aspergillus fumigatus sensu stricto is the predominant pathogen, greater application of molecular diagnostics has increased the recognition of cryptic species within Aspergillus section Fumigati. Although phylogenetically related, these cryptic species often exhibit distinct antifungal susceptibility profiles and varying pathogenicity. For instance, Aspergillus lentulus is characterized by intrinsically reduced susceptibility to azoles and polyenes and is associated with poor clinical outcomes.1,2 Aspergillus turcosus is a rare cryptic species first described in 2008, and its clinical significance remains poorly understood due to few clinical isolates reported.3

Case study

A 51-year-old woman with systemic lupus erythematosus, lupus nephritis and antiphospholipid syndrome was hospitalized due to abdominal distention and colicky pain for 1 day, for which intra-abdominal abscess was diagnosed by CT 1 week later. She was transferred to the ICU on the second week due to uncontrolled sepsis with respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation and progression to end-stage renal disease requiring dialysis. Despite broad-spectrum antibiotics and immunosuppressive treatments, including belimumab and high-dose glucocorticoids, her condition continued to deteriorate. While on micafungin for Candida peritonitis, she developed dyspnoea under ventilator support. CT of the chest revealed a new nodule at left lung. Serum galactomannan (GM) antigen testing (Platelia™ Aspergillus Ag assay; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Marnes-la-Coquette, France) yielded an OD value of 1.2 on Day 43, prompting a diagnosis of probable invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA) by 2024 FUNDICU consensus definitions.4

Micafungin was replaced with isavuconazole in light of significant hepatic impairment. However, over the subsequent 2 weeks, sequential serum GM OD indices increased to 4.7 (Figure 1a), and follow-up CT demonstrated that the lung nodule had evolved into a necrotic cavitary lesion with a fungal ball (Figure 1b). Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) performed on Day 70 yielded a strong GM OD of 6.3. Micafungin was resumed after a 14 day interruption as salvage combination therapy with isavuconazole. Fungal culture from BALF grew colonies morphologically resembling A. fumigatus, which was identified as A. fumigatus by MALDI-TOF MS using the Bruker Biotyper MS system with Filamentous Fungi Library 2023_v7.0_1021MSP. Concurrently, metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) (described in Supplementary methods; available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online)) of the same specimen disclosed A. turcosus. This result was subsequently confirmed by Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS) Sanger sequencing and morphological re-evaluation, which revealed grey-turquoise colonies and microscopically short, loosely columnar conidial heads characteristic of A. turcosus (Figure 1c–f).

Despite combination therapy with micafungin and isavuconazole with drug monitoring within the therapeutic range (2.67 mg/L), the patient still developed intractable thrombocytopenia, uraemia and pulmonary artery haemorrhage leading to massive haemosputum, and died on Day 75. Meanwhile, in vitro susceptibility testing using the Sensititre YeastOne® method by the research laboratory showed an MIC of 0.25 mg/L of voriconazole, 0.03 mg/L of posaconazole, 0.06 mg/L of itraconazole and 2 mg/L of amphotericin B (Table S1). These results indicated susceptibility or WT to triazoles and amphotericin B based on the CLSI interpretative criteria for A. fumigatus, as species-specific breakpoints are unavailable for A. turcosus.

Literature review

A. turcosus was first identified in 2008 as one of the cryptic species within the Fumigati section.3 It is characterized by subtle morphological features that are easily overlooked in routine clinical laboratories. Comprehensive microbiological investigations established A. turcosus as the causative pathogen of fatal IPA in this patient. To better define the clinical significance of this organism, we searched PubMed for English articles published since 2016. Key search terms were: ‘Aspergillosis’, ‘cryptic species’, ‘Aspergillus turcosus’ and ‘section Fumigati’. The search identified 26 articles, which were screened by title and abstract. Twenty eligible articles underwent full-text screening, and we also reviewed articles they cited. Finally, four publications that included original reports of environmental and clinical isolates of A. turcosus were reviewed (Table S1). Our report represents the first description of invasive A. turcosus infection in a non-neutropenic patient, utilizing an integrated mycological diagnostic approach in accordance with the latest diagnostic consensus. Previously, a study from Japan documented fatal invasive CNS infection in an HSCT recipient.5 Both invasive cases resulted in death despite definitive therapy. The remaining four clinical isolates were recovered in Canada from respiratory colonization in immunocompromised patients with COPD,6 and one isolate was identified from hospital environmental sampling in South Korea.7 The isolates in our study and other studies showed susceptibility to triazoles, except for one colonizing isolate. A previous study from Canada has noted efflux pump overactivity associated with azole resistance in this species.6

These findings broaden the clinical spectrum of A. turcosus from a colonizing bystander to a lethal pathogen in non-neutropenic patients, though lacking direct microscopic observation of fungal hyphae. This case also highlights the practical limitations of standard diagnostics. While MALDI-TOF MS is efficient, it often lacks the resolution to differentiate cryptic species within Aspergillus sections due to database limitations or spectral similarity. Furthermore, commercial PCR panels usually detect common Aspergillus species (e.g. A. fumigatus, A. terreus) but cannot distinguish cryptic species.8 Recent advances in diagnostic approaches, such as mNGS, offer accurate species-level identification, though turnaround time, high cost, complex bioinformatics and lack of clinical validation hinder its widespread implementation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, A. turcosus is an under-recognized pathogen capable of causing life-threatening infection. Future research integrating powerful pathogen-agnostic and broad microbial detection in conjunction with standard of care (SOC) testing can optimize the diagnostic yield9 and clarify the epidemiological understanding of A. turcosus and other cryptic species among different Aspergillus sections.
