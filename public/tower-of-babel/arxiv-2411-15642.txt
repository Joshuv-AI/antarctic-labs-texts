CENTRAL DERIVATIONS OF LOW-DIMENSIONAL ZINBIEL ALGEBRAS
Basdouri Imed 1 , Jean Lerbet 2 , Bouzid Mosbahi 3

Abstract
. The study of central derivations in low-dimensional algebraic structures is a crucial area of research in mathematics, with applications in understanding the internal symmetries and deformations of these structures. In this article, we investigate the central derivations of complex Zinbiel algebras of dimension ≤ 4 \leq 4 . Key properties of the central derivation algebras are presented, including their structures and dimensions. The results are summarized in a tabular format, providing a clear classification of decomposable and indecomposable centroids based on these derivations. Specifically, we show that the centroid of two-dimensional Zinbiel algebras is indecomposable, while in three-dimensional Zinbiel algebras, centroids such as 𝔸 3 3 {\mathbb{A}}_{3}^{3} , 𝔸 4 3 {\mathbb{A}}_{4}^{3} , 𝔸 6 3 {\mathbb{A}}_{6}^{3} , and 𝔸 7 3 {\mathbb{A}}_{7}^{3} are decomposable. For four-dimensional Zinbiel algebras, centroids including 𝔸 1 4 {\mathbb{A}}_{1}^{4} , 𝔸 3 4 {\mathbb{A}}_{3}^{4} , 𝔸 5 4 {\mathbb{A}}_{5}^{4} , 𝔸 9 4 {\mathbb{A}}_{9}^{4} , 𝔸 10 4 {\mathbb{A}}_{10}^{4} , 𝔸 11 4 {\mathbb{A}}_{11}^{4} , and 𝔸 16 4 {\mathbb{A}}_{16}^{4} are decomposable. Furthermore, the dimensions of central derivation algebras vary across different dimensions: two-dimensional Zinbiel algebras have central derivation dimensions of one, while in three-dimensional and four-dimensional cases, these dimensions range from zero to nine.

Introduction

Non-associative algebras have become a central object of study in modern algebraic theory, particularly through their connections to Lie algebras and their various generalizations, such as Malcev algebras, Lie superalgebras, binary Lie algebras, Leibniz algebras, and others. Among these, Leibniz algebras have gained significant attention for their non-associative yet structured properties, defined by the identity

\displaystyle[a,[b,c]]

\displaystyle=[[a,b],c]-[[a,c],b]

The study of these algebras has led to the exploration of their dual structures, notably Zinbiel algebras, which are considered the Koszul duals of Leibniz algebras in the framework of J.L. Loday’s theory
[
1
]
. In
[
2
]
, Zinbiel algebras were introduced as dual Leibniz algebras, with further details provided in works such as
[
2
,
3
]
.

A Zinbiel algebra consists of a vector space
{\mathbb{A}}
with a bilinear map
\bullet:{\mathbb{A}}\times{\mathbb{A}}\to{\mathbb{A}}
satisfying the identity

\displaystyle(a\bullet b)\bullet c

\displaystyle=a\bullet(b\bullet c)+a\bullet(c\bullet b),

This structure allows for the formulation of commutative associative algebraic structures through the new product
a\bullet b+b\bullet a
, making Zinbiel algebras closely related to dendriform algebras and pre-Lie algebras see
[
4
,
5
]
. In fact, dendriform algebras, defined by two bilinear operations
\prec
and
\succ
on a vector space
{\mathbb{A}}
, yield associative structures when combined appropriately. Specifically, a Zinbiel algebra can be viewed as a particular case of a dendriform algebra with a specific relationship between the operations
\prec
and
\succ
. Pre-Lie algebras, which share a similar structure, relate to Lie algebras through a commutative product. These algebraic objects can be visualized through the following diagram, reflecting their interconnections:

\textstyle{Lie}

Furthermore, Zinbiel algebras hold important relationships with pre-Poisson algebras and other structures, showing their versatility and importance in the wider context of algebra theory. Although significant progress has been made in understanding Zinbiel algebras, particularly their connections to dendriform and pre-Lie algebras, many challenges remain due to their inherent non-associativity.

This paper focuses on central derivations of low-dimensional Zinbiel algebras, specifically those of dimensions two, three, and four. Building on existing classifications of complex Zinbiel algebras see
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
, we aim to systematically compute central derivations, classify centroids for decomposability, and provide illustrative examples. To facilitate our computations, we employ symbolic algebra software, including Maple, ensuring rigorous and efficient analysis.
The paper is organized as follows: Section 1 provides an overview of Zinbiel algebras and summarizes key results. Section 2 introduces the definitions and preliminary concepts needed for the study. Section 3 presents an algorithm for finding central derivations in Zinbiel algebras. Finally, using classifications of low-dimensional complex Zinbiel algebras from earlier work, we compute central derivations, examine centroids for decomposability, and provide examples generated using Maple software.

Prelimieries

Definition 2.1
.

An algebra
{\mathbb{A}}
over a field
\mathbb{K}
is a vector space over
\mathbb{K}
equipped with a bilinear map

\displaystyle\pi:{\mathbb{A}}\times{\mathbb{A}}\to{\mathbb{A}},

such that

\displaystyle\pi(\alpha a+\beta b,c)

\displaystyle=\alpha\pi(a,c)+\beta\pi(b,c),

\displaystyle\pi(c,\alpha a+\beta b)

\displaystyle=\alpha\pi(c,a)+\beta\pi(c,b),

where
a,b,c\in{\mathbb{A}}
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{K}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}
be a vector space over
\mathbb{K}
with a bilinear product
\cdot:{\mathbb{A}}\times{\mathbb{A}}\to{\mathbb{A}}
. Then
({\mathbb{A}},\cdot)
is called a
pre-Lie algebra
if for any
a,b,c\in{\mathbb{A}}
,

\displaystyle(a\cdot b)\cdot c-a\cdot(b\cdot c)

\displaystyle=(b\cdot a)\cdot c-b\cdot(a\cdot c).

Definition 2.3
.

A dendriform algebra
{\mathbb{A}}
over a field
\mathbb{K}
is a
\mathbb{K}
-vector space equipped with two binary operations

\displaystyle\succ:{\mathbb{A}}\otimes{\mathbb{A}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{A}},\quad\prec:{\mathbb{A}}\otimes{\mathbb{A}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{A}},

satisfying the following identities for all
a,b,c\in{\mathbb{A}}
:

\displaystyle(a\prec b)\prec c

\displaystyle=a\prec(b\prec c)+a\prec(b\succ c),

\displaystyle(a\succ b)\prec c

\displaystyle=a\succ(b\prec c),

\displaystyle a\succ(b\succ c)

\displaystyle=(a\prec b)\succ c+(a\succ b)\succ c.

Definition 2.4
.

A Zinbiel algebra
{\mathbb{A}}
over a field
\mathbb{K}
is an algebra equipped with a bilinear operation
\bullet:{\mathbb{A}}\times{\mathbb{A}}\to{\mathbb{A}}
satisfying the Zinbiel identity:

\displaystyle(a\bullet b)\bullet c

\displaystyle=a\bullet(b\bullet c)+a\bullet(c\bullet b),\quad\forall\;a,b,c\in{\mathbb{A}}.

Definition 2.5
.

A derivation of a Zinbiel algebra
{\mathbb{A}}
is a linear map
D:{\mathbb{A}}\to{\mathbb{A}}
satisfying:

\displaystyle D(a\bullet b)

\displaystyle=D(a)\bullet b+a\bullet D(b),\quad\forall\;a,b\in{\mathbb{A}}.

The set of all derivations of
{\mathbb{A}}
is denoted by
Der({\mathbb{A}})
.

Definition 2.6
.

For a Zinbiel algebra
{\mathbb{A}}
, define a descending sequence of subspaces as:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{A}}^{1}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{A}},\quad{\mathbb{A}}^{k+1}={\mathbb{A}}^{k}\bullet{\mathbb{A}},\quad k\geq 1.

It follows that:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\supseteq{\mathbb{A}}^{2}\supseteq{\mathbb{A}}^{3}\supseteq\cdots.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{A}}_{1}
be two Zinbiel algebras over a field
\mathbb{K}
. A mapping
\psi:{\mathbb{A}}\to{\mathbb{A}}_{1}
is called a
homomorphism
if it satisfies

\displaystyle\psi(a\bullet b)

\displaystyle=\psi(a)\bullet\psi(b),\quad\text{for all }a,b\in{\mathbb{A}}.

We call
\psi
an
isomorphism
if it is bijective and an
endomorphism
if it is a linear map
\psi:{\mathbb{A}}\to{\mathbb{A}}
. The set of all endomorphisms of
{\mathbb{A}}
is denoted by
End({\mathbb{A}})
.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}
be a Zinbiel algebra over a field
\mathbb{K}
. The set

\displaystyle\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})

\displaystyle=\{\phi\in End({\mathbb{A}})\mid\phi(a\bullet b)=\phi(a)\bullet b=a\bullet(b),\quad\text{for all }a,b\in{\mathbb{A}}\}

is called the
centroid
of
{\mathbb{A}}
.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}_{1}
be a nonempty subset of a Zinbiel algebra
{\mathbb{A}}
. The subset

\displaystyle C_{\mathbb{A}}({\mathbb{A}}_{1})

\displaystyle=\{a\in{\mathbb{A}}\mid a\bullet{\mathbb{A}}_{1}={\mathbb{A}}_{1}\bullet a=0\}

is called the
centralizer
of
{\mathbb{A}}_{1}
in
{\mathbb{A}}
. In particular,
C_{\mathbb{A}}({\mathbb{A}})=C({\mathbb{A}})
is the
center
of
{\mathbb{A}}
. Additionally, an ideal
I
of a Zinbiel algebra
{\mathbb{A}}
satisfies
{\mathbb{A}}\bullet I\subseteq I
and
I\bullet{\mathbb{A}}\subseteq I
.

Definition 2.10
.

A Zinbiel algebra
{\mathbb{A}}
is called
indecomposable
if it cannot be written as a direct sum of its ideals. Otherwise,
{\mathbb{A}}
is called
decomposable
.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}
be a Zinbiel algebra and
\phi\in End({\mathbb{A}})
. The map
\phi
is called a
central derivation
if
\phi({\mathbb{A}})\subseteq C({\mathbb{A}})
and
\phi({\mathbb{A}}\bullet{\mathbb{A}})=0
.

The set of all central derivations of a Zinbiel algebra
{\mathbb{A}}
, denoted by
C_{D}({\mathbb{A}})
, forms an associative algebra with respect to the composition operation
\circ
, and it is a Lie algebra with respect to the bracket defined by

\displaystyle[a_{1},a_{2}]

\displaystyle=a_{1}\circ a_{2}-a_{2}\circ a_{1},\quad\text{for all }a_{1},a_{2}\in C_{D}({\mathbb{A}}).

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
\xi:{\mathbb{A}}_{1}\to{\mathbb{A}}_{2}
be an isomorphism of Zinbiel algebras
({\mathbb{A}}_{1},\star)
and
({\mathbb{A}}_{2},\ast)
over a field
\mathbb{K}
. The mapping
\phi:End({\mathbb{A}}_{1})\to End({\mathbb{A}}_{2})
defined by
\phi(c)=\xi\circ c\circ\xi^{-1}
is an isomorphism of
CD({\mathbb{A}}_{1})
and
CD({\mathbb{A}}_{2})
, that is,

\displaystyle\phi(CD({\mathbb{A}}_{1}))

\displaystyle=CD({\mathbb{A}}_{2}).

Proof.

Due to the isomorphism relation, we have

\displaystyle a\ast b

\displaystyle=\xi(\xi^{-1}(a)\star\xi^{-1}(b)).

Assume
c\in End({\mathbb{A}}_{1}
) such that

\displaystyle c(\xi^{-1}(a_{1}))\star\xi^{-1}(a_{2})+\xi^{-1}(a_{1})\star c(\xi^{-1}(a_{2}))

\displaystyle=c(\xi^{-1}(a_{1})\star\xi^{-1}(a_{2})).

Applying the mapping
\xi
on this equation, we have

\displaystyle\xi\circ c\circ\xi^{-1}(a_{1}\ast a_{2})

\displaystyle=\xi\circ c\circ\xi^{-1}(a_{1})\ast a_{2}+a_{1}\ast\xi\circ c\circ\xi^{-1}(a_{2}),

that is,
\xi\circ c\circ\xi^{-1}\in CD({\mathbb{A}}_{2})
. Thus,

\displaystyle\phi(\mathrm{CD}({\mathbb{A}}_{1}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{CD}({\mathbb{A}}_{2}).

∎

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}
be a Zinbiel algebra. Then

(i)

CD({\mathbb{A}})\subseteq\mathrm{Der}({\mathbb{A}})
;

(ii)

[\Gamma({\mathbb{A}}),\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})]\subseteq CD({\mathbb{A}})
;

(iii)

(\phi\circ D)(a\bullet b)=(\phi\circ D)(a)\bullet b+a\bullet(\phi\circ D)(b)
;

(iv)

[D,\phi](a\bullet b)=[D,\phi](a)\bullet b+a\bullet[d,\phi](b)
.

Proof.

We prove
(i)
, and the others can be obtained by definitions.
Let
{\mathbb{A}}
be a Zinbiel algebra,
\phi_{1}\in CD({\mathbb{A}})
, and for all
a,b\in{\mathbb{A}}
. We need to show that

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(a\bullet b)

\displaystyle=\phi_{1}(a)\bullet b=a\bullet\phi_{1}(b)=0.

Thus,

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(a\bullet b)

\displaystyle=\phi_{1}(a)\bullet b=0

(1)

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(a\bullet b)

\displaystyle=a\bullet\phi_{1}(b)=0.

(2)

If we add (
1
) with (
2
), we get

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(a\bullet b)+\phi_{1}(a\bullet b)

\displaystyle=\phi_{1}(a)\bullet b+a\bullet\phi_{1}(b).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(a\bullet b)

\displaystyle=\phi_{1}(a)\bullet b+a\bullet\phi_{1}(b)\subseteq Der({\mathbb{A}}),

since
\phi_{1}(a\bullet b)=0
.
∎

Theorem 2.14
.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}
be a Zinbiel algebra. Then for any
\phi\in\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})
and
d\in Der({\mathbb{A}})
, the following statements hold:

(1)

Der({\mathbb{A}})\cap\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})=CD({\mathbb{A}})
,

(2)

d\circ\phi\in\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})
if and only if
\phi\circ d
is a central derivation of
{\mathbb{A}}
,

(3)

d\circ\phi\in Der({\mathbb{A}})
if and only if
[d,\phi]
is a central derivation of
{\mathbb{A}}
.

Proof.

(1)

Suppose
\phi\in Der({\mathbb{A}})\cap\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})
. Then
\phi\in Der({\mathbb{A}})
and
\phi\in\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})
. Let
a,b\in{\mathbb{A}}
. From the definition of a derivation, we have:

\displaystyle\phi(a\bullet b)

\displaystyle=\phi(a)\bullet b+a\bullet\phi(b).

From the centroid property, we have:

\displaystyle\phi(a\bullet b)

\displaystyle=\phi(a)\bullet b=a\bullet\phi(b).

Thus,

\displaystyle\phi(a\bullet b)

\displaystyle=\phi(a)\bullet b=a\bullet\phi(b)=0.

Therefore,
\phi({\mathbb{A}}^{2})=0
and
\phi({\mathbb{A}})\subseteq C({\mathbb{A}})
. Hence,
\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})\cap Der({\mathbb{A}})\subseteq CD({\mathbb{A}})
.

To prove the reverse inclusion, suppose
\phi\in C({\mathbb{A}})
. Then:

\displaystyle\phi(a\bullet b)

\displaystyle=0=\phi(a)\bullet b=a\bullet\phi(b).

This implies
\phi\in\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})\cap Der({\mathbb{A}})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle Der({\mathbb{A}})\cap\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})

\displaystyle=C({\mathbb{A}}).

(2)

Assume
\phi\in\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})
and
D\in Der({\mathbb{A}})
. Let
a,b\in{\mathbb{A}}
. If
D\circ\phi\in\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})
, then:

\displaystyle(D\circ\phi)(a\bullet b)

\displaystyle=((D\circ\phi)(a))\bullet b=a\bullet((D\circ\phi)(b)).

On the other hand:

\displaystyle(D\circ\phi)(a\bullet b)

\displaystyle=D(\phi(a)\bullet b)=D(a\bullet\phi(b)).

Using the derivation property,

\displaystyle D(\phi(a)\bullet b)

\displaystyle=(D\circ\phi)(a)\bullet b+\phi(a)\bullet D(b),

and

\displaystyle D(a\bullet\phi(b))

\displaystyle=a\bullet(D\circ\phi)(b)+D(a)\bullet\phi(b).

Equating these expressions, we find:

\displaystyle(D\circ\phi)(a\bullet b)

\displaystyle=(D\circ\phi)(a)\bullet b+a\bullet(D\circ\phi)(b).

Thus,
D\circ\phi\in Der({\mathbb{A}})
.

To show the reverse inclusion, suppose
D\circ\phi\in Der({\mathbb{A}})
. Then:

\displaystyle(D\circ\phi)(a\bullet b)

\displaystyle=(D\circ\phi)(a)\bullet b+a\bullet(D\circ\phi)(b).

By substituting the definitions and rearranging, it follows that
D\circ\phi\in\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})
.

(3)

Assume
[D,\phi]\in Der({\mathbb{A}})
. For any
a,b\in{\mathbb{A}}
,

\displaystyle[D,\phi](a\bullet b)

\displaystyle=D(\phi(a\bullet b))-\phi(D(a\bullet b)).

Using the derivation properties of
D
and
\phi
, and simplifying, we find that:

\displaystyle[a,[D,\phi](b)]

\displaystyle=0.

By the properties of Zinbiel algebras, this implies
[D,\phi]\in C({\mathbb{A}})
.

Conversely, if
[D,\phi]\in C({\mathbb{A}})
, then:

\displaystyle[a,[D,\phi](b)]

\displaystyle=0.

This ensures
D\circ\phi\in Der({\mathbb{A}})
, completing the proof.

∎

Theorem 2.15
.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}={\mathbb{A}}_{1}\oplus{\mathbb{A}}_{2}
, where
{\mathbb{A}}_{1}
and
{\mathbb{A}}_{2}
are ideals of
{\mathbb{A}}
. Then,

\displaystyle\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})

\displaystyle=\Gamma({\mathbb{A}}_{1})\oplus\Gamma({\mathbb{A}}_{2})\oplus C_{1}\oplus C_{2},

where
C_{i}
is defined as

\displaystyle C_{i}

\displaystyle=\left\{\phi\in End({\mathbb{A}}_{i},{\mathbb{A}}_{j})\mid\phi({\mathbb{A}}_{i})\subseteq C({\mathbb{A}}_{j}),\phi{\mathbb{A}}_{i}=0\right\},\quad 1\leq i\neq j\leq 2.

Proof.

Let
\rho_{i}:{\mathbb{A}}\to{\mathbb{A}}_{i}
be the canonical projection,
i=1,2
. Then
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})
and
\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}=id_{\mathbb{A}}
.

For any
\phi\in\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})
, we have

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})\phi(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})=\rho_{1}\phi\rho_{1}+\rho_{1}\phi\rho_{2}+\rho_{2}\phi\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}\phi\rho_{2}.

Thus,
\rho_{i}\phi\rho_{j}\in\Gamma({\mathbb{A}}),i,j=1,2
, and
\rho_{1}\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})\rho_{1}+\rho_{1}\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})\rho_{2}+\rho_{2}\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})\rho_{2}
is a direct sum.

Now, we define a mapping
\rho_{1}\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})\rho_{1}\to\Gamma({\mathbb{A}}_{1})
such that
\rho_{1}f\rho_{1}\mapsto\rho_{1}f\rho_{1}|_{{\mathbb{A}}_{1}}
, for any
f\in\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})
.

If
\rho_{1}f\rho_{1}|_{{\mathbb{A}}_{1}}=0
, then
\rho_{1}f\rho_{1}|_{{\mathbb{A}}_{2}}=0
, which implies
\rho_{1}f\rho_{1}=0
.Hence, the above mapping is injective.

For any
\phi\in\Gamma(A_{1})
, we can extend
\phi
to
{\mathbb{A}}_{2}
such that
\phi|_{{\mathbb{A}}_{2}}=0
. Then the extended
\phi
is in
\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})
and
\rho_{1}\phi\rho_{1}|_{{\mathbb{A}}_{1}}=\phi
. Thus,
\rho_{1}\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})\rho_{1}\cong\Gamma({\mathbb{A}}_{1})
as a vector space. Similarly, we obtain
\rho_{2}\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})\rho_{2}\cong\Gamma({\mathbb{A}}_{2})
.

Next, define a mapping
\rho_{1}\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})\rho_{2}\to C_{2}
such that
\rho_{1}f\rho_{2}\mapsto\rho_{1}f\rho_{2}|_{L_{2}}
.

For
a\in{\mathbb{A}}_{2},b\in{\mathbb{A}}_{1}
,

\displaystyle\rho_{1}f\rho_{2}(a)b

\displaystyle=\rho_{1}f\rho_{2}(ab)=0,

and

\displaystyle b(\rho_{1}f\rho_{2}(a))

\displaystyle=\rho_{1}f\rho_{2}(ba)=0.

Thus,
\rho_{1}f\rho_{2}(a)\in C({\mathbb{A}}_{1})
. For
a,b\in{\mathbb{A}}_{2},\rho_{1}f\rho_{2}(ab)=0
, so
\rho_{1}f\rho_{2}({\mathbb{A}}_{1})=0
and
\rho_{1}f\rho_{2}|_{{\mathbb{A}}_{2}}\in C_{2}
.

If
\rho_{1}f\rho_{2}|_{{\mathbb{A}}_{2}}=0
and
\rho_{1}f\rho_{2}|_{{\mathbb{A}}_{1}}=0
, then
\rho_{1}f\rho_{2}|_{\mathbb{A}}=0
, and the above mapping is injective.

For any
\phi\in C_{2}
, extend
\phi
to
{\mathbb{A}}_{1}
and denote it by
\bar{\phi}
, such that
\bar{\phi}|_{{\mathbb{A}}_{1}}=0
. Then
\bar{\phi}\in\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})
by the following conditions:

\displaystyle\bar{\phi}(ab)

\displaystyle=\bar{\phi}(a_{1}b_{1}+a_{2}b_{2})=\phi(a_{2}b_{2})=0,

\displaystyle a\bar{\phi}(b)

\displaystyle=(a_{1}+b_{2})\bar{\phi}(b_{1}+b_{2})=a_{2}\phi(b_{2})=0,

\displaystyle\bar{\phi}(a)b

\displaystyle=\phi(a_{2})(b_{1}+b_{2})=\phi(a_{2})b=0.

Therefore,
\rho_{1}\bar{\phi}\rho_{2}(a_{2})=\rho_{1}\bar{\phi}(a_{2})=\phi(a_{2})
, and
\rho_{1}\phi\rho_{2}\mapsto\phi
, which shows that the mapping above is onto.

Thus,
\rho_{1}\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})\rho_{2}\cong C_{2}
as a vector space. Similarly,
\rho_{2}\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})\rho_{1}\cong C_{1}
.
The theorem is proven.
∎

Corollary 2.16
.

If
CD({\mathbb{A}})=0
, then
\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})
is decomposable.

Proof.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}={\mathbb{A}}_{1}\oplus{\mathbb{A}}_{2}
, where
{\mathbb{A}}_{1}
and
{\mathbb{A}}_{2}
are ideals of
{\mathbb{A}}
. From Theorem
2.15
, the centroid
\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})
is given by:

\displaystyle\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})

\displaystyle=\Gamma({\mathbb{A}}_{1})\oplus\Gamma({\mathbb{A}}_{2})\oplus C_{1}\oplus C_{2},

where
C_{i}=\{\phi\in End({\mathbb{A}}_{i},{\mathbb{A}}_{j})\mid\phi({\mathbb{A}}_{i})\subseteq C({\mathbb{A}}_{j}),\,\phi{\mathbb{A}}_{i}=0\},\,1\leq i\neq j\leq 2
.

Suppose
CD({\mathbb{A}})=0
, which implies that the center of
{\mathbb{A}}
, denoted by
C({\mathbb{A}})
, is trivial. That is,
C({\mathbb{A}})=\{0\}
. Consequently:

\displaystyle C({\mathbb{A}}_{j})

\displaystyle=\{0\}\quad\text{for }j=1,2.

Now, consider the components
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
. For
\phi\in C_{1}
, by definition:

\displaystyle\phi({\mathbb{A}}_{1})\subseteq C({\mathbb{A}}_{2}),\quad\phi{\mathbb{A}}_{1}

\displaystyle=0.

Since
C({\mathbb{A}}_{2})=\{0\}
, it follows that
\phi({\mathbb{A}}_{1})=0
, which implies
\phi=0
. Thus,
C_{1}=\{0\}
.

Similarly, for
\phi\in C_{2}
, we have:

\displaystyle\phi({\mathbb{A}}_{2})\subseteq C({\mathbb{A}}_{1}),\quad\phi{\mathbb{A}}_{2}

\displaystyle=0.

Since
C({\mathbb{A}}_{1})=\{0\}
, it follows that
\phi({\mathbb{A}}_{2})=0
, which implies
\phi=0
. Thus,
C_{2}=\{0\}
.

Substituting
C_{1}=C_{2}=\{0\}
into the expression for
\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})

\displaystyle=\Gamma({\mathbb{A}}_{1})\oplus\Gamma({\mathbb{A}}_{2}).

Hence,
\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})
is a direct sum of
\Gamma({\mathbb{A}}_{1})
and
\Gamma({\mathbb{A}}_{2})
, and therefore
\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})
is decomposable.
∎

3.
An algorithm for finding central derivations

Firstly, let
\{e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{n}\}
be a basis of an
n
-dimensional Zinbiel algebra
{\mathbb{A}}
. Then

\displaystyle e_{i}\bullet e_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{ij}^{k}e_{k},\quad i,j=1,2,\dots,n,

where the coefficients
\gamma_{ij}^{k}
of the above linear combinations are called the structure constants.

An element
a
of central derivation,
CD({\mathbb{A}})
, being a linear transformation of the Zinbiel algebra
{\mathbb{A}}
, is represented in a square matrix form
[a_{ij}]_{i,j=1,2,\dots,n}
, that is,

\displaystyle\phi(e_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n}a_{it}e_{t},\quad i=1,2,\dots,n.

According to Theorem
2.14
, the central derivation
CD({\mathbb{A}})
is the intersection between the centroid
\Gamma({\mathbb{A}})
and the derivation
Der({\mathbb{A}})
. From the definition of centroid and central derivation, we derive an algorithm to find the central derivation
CD({\mathbb{A}})
, as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}\gamma_{ij}^{t}a_{tk}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n}a_{it}\gamma_{tj}^{k}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}a_{jt}\gamma_{it}^{k}=0,\quad\forall i,j,k=1,2,\dots,n.

This approach can be applied to find the central derivations of complex Zinbiel algebras in dimensions 2, 3, and 4. Additionally, using the classification results from
[5]
and
[4]
together with the algorithm mentioned above, we compute the central derivations, and the results are summarized in tabular form.
