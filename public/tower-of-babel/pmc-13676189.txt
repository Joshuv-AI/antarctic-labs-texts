A Test for the Profession: Medicine in Nazi Germany
Authors: Michael Trimble
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this paper I will chart the development of the medical sub-culture within Nazi German society. I intend to show that ethical regulation provided by professional societies, state, and the legal system were inadequate and failed to prevent the atrocities perpetrated by Nazi doctors and that the acceptance of the concept of “a life not worth living” was a key turning point in medical culture and practice.

A testing question

How might ethical standards be upheld? What processes may restrain the individual practitioner, or, perhaps more importantly, the profession, from unethical practice? There may be codes of practice, which may emanate from either professional bodies or external regulators, there may be less formal guidance from professional societies and expert groups, and there may be legislation from the courts and government. In addition, there is the force of public opinion voiced in a democratic system. All of these were present in Nazi Germany – and all failed to halt the atrocities perpetrated by the Nazis in the name of medicine.

The phenomenon of the behaviour of the medical profession in Nazi Germany is all too easily dismissed as an anomaly; something so far removed from the norm that it is beyond analysis. This is a mistake; Authur Caplan writes

Rather than see Nazi biomedicine as morally bad, the field of bioethics has generally accepted the myths that Nazi biomedicine was inept, mad, or coerced. By subscribing to these myths, bioethics has been able to avoid a painful confrontation with the fact that many who committed the crimes of the Holocaust were competent physicians and healthcare professionals acting from their moral convictions.1

To shy away from the challenge posed by the Nazi physicians is to miss an opportunity to learn from their errors: For historian Laurence Rees, the Nazis are a warning we dare not ignore.

By their crime the Nazis brought into the world an awareness of what educated, technologically advanced human beings can do, as long as the possess a cold heart. Once allowed into the world, knowledge of what they did must not be unlearnt. It lies there – ugly, inert, waiting to be discovered by each new generation. A warning for us, and for those who will come after2.

Therefore, I feel that the horrors of Nazi medical culture are an appropriate test case for an enquiry into the relationship between medical ethics and the profession of medicine. If the checks and balances on the actions and attitudes of the profession in Nazi Germany were the same as they are for the profession today, then we can have little assurance that the same atrocities cannot easily be repeated. Miola contends that the failure of the medical profession3 in the Nazi era is evidence that medical ethics should not be left to the profession. However, I hope to show that it was the distancing of doctors from the ethical decisions made about individual patients that allowed the culture of Nazi medicine to develop.

How then did Nazi medical culture evolve? How did the checks and balances of professional regulation fail to prevent the degradation of the profession? It is salient to realise that the setting for the birth of Nazi biomedicine was the centre of sound, scientific medical practice.

Nazi medicine was neither an aberration that arose in 1933 and disappeared in 1945, nor was it an anomaly relevant to Nazi Germany and German-occupied Europe. Nazi medicine had its origins in the same academic and professional environment that influenced the development of the health care systems of the developed world. It was created and developed in the birthplace of scientific medicine: 19th and 20th Century Germany.4

Indeed, a scientific approach to medicine was emphasised among the German medical community, albeit in the flawed science of Social Darwinism, that is social engineering to ensure the survival of the fittest. These adherents of Social Darwinism, fearing a ‘degradation’ of the human race in general, and German society in particular, proposed a new academic discipline, that of racial hygiene. This philosophy was readily accepted by the biomedical scientists.

One often hears that National Socialists distorted science, that doctors perhaps co-operated more with the Nazi regime than they should have…there is certainly some truth in this, but I think it misses the more important point that medical scientists were the ones who invented racial hygiene in the first place. Many of the 20-odd university institutes for racial science and racial hygiene were established at German universities before the Nazi rise to power, and by 1932, it is fair to say that racial hygiene had become a scientific orthodoxy in the German medical community.5

In fact, the adoption of the principles of racial hygiene by medical academics was a major factor in giving the political views of the Nazis intellectual credibility.

Biological imagery was important in Nazi literature in several ways…Nazi leaders commonly referred to National Socialism as applied biology. 5

Hitler himself was lauded as the “great doctor of the German people”.5

With the development of racial hygiene, the foundations of medical ethics were under threat and the historic doctrine of sanctity of human life was undermined. The primacy of the special relationship between the physician and the individual patient under his care was challenged.6 The Hippocratic Oath was “dismissed as a vestige of ancient times” and a new “higher” moral authority was proposed – that of “the health of the state”. The previously unthinkable idea of terminating “lives not worth living” became accepted by the medical community.6

The Nazi doctors

Why did the medical profession so readily accept these changes? These were ordinary doctors, often family men.

The Nazi doctor frequently had a family and when his day’s work was done, he went home to his wife and children.7

Yet there were strong attractions drawing them into the Nazi Party: Firstly, an appeal to the intellect. We have already noted the pseudo-scientific basis of Nazi social policy. Robert Procter emphasises the point that “medical scientists were the ones who invented racial hygiene in the first place.”5 Indeed, around 20 institutes for racial science and racial hygiene had been established at German universities before the Nazi rise to power.5 By 1932 racial hygiene had become accepted as scientific orthodoxy in the German medical community.

There was also the attraction of status and power; certainly, doctors seem to have prospered under Nazi rule. Proctor notes that

Physicians achieved higher status in the Nazi period than at any time before, or since. During 12 years of Nazi rule, for example, the office of Rektor (president) at German universities was occupied by physicians about half of the time; this contrasts with 19% for the decade prior to the rise of the Nazis and 18% for the two decades following the Nazis.5

Tessa Chelouche, historian at the Yad Vashem Holocaust Memorial Centre in Israel, comments

Physicians were granted unprecedented power and prestige, and thusly they proceeded to abuse their authority and influence. 6

There were also financial benefits for the medical profession. In 1926 an average German doctor earned a salary of around 12 000 RM, whilst a lawyer could expect to earn 18 000 RM. Within ten years the situation would be reversed with doctors earning 2000 RM more than lawyers.5

Lastly, we must also recognise the effect of peer pressure. Robert Proctor notes the following:

In 1929, a number of physicians formed the National Socialist Physicians League … The organisation was an immediate success, with nearly 3,000 doctors, representing 6% of the entire profession joining the League by January 1933 – that is, before the rise of Hitler to power.

Doctors joined the Nazi Party earlier and in greater numbers than any other professional group. By 1942, more than 38,000 doctors had joined the Nazi Party, nearly half of all the doctors in the country.

Doctors in society

It has to be said that, whilst the medical profession was influential in Nazi Germany, it was not acting in isolation: Policies were developed within the legislative framework of the state and with the support of the executive, for example, the so-called Nuremberg Laws of 1935.5 It is often assumed that ethical codes and professional standards for human experimentation originated as a response to the horrors of Nazi medicine. However, in 1931 the German administration put forward their Guidelines for New Therapy and Human Experimentation6 The chief defendant at the Nuremberg doctors’ trials, the notorious Dr Karl Brandt, testified to the court of his need to act “in the interests of the community,” and toward that end, the State, the “law of the land”, gave him permission to experiment on human subjects put at his disposal.8 Again this supports my contention that separating the technical expertise of medical practice from the personal and ethical component creates a moral vacuum, with potential for deadly results.

It must also be remembered that Hitler was democratically elected and that there was acceptance of the Nazi ideals by ordinary members of the public.

Historian Laurence Rees recalls interviewing Wilfred von Oven, who had been personal attaché to the infamous Nazi propaganda minister Dr Josef Goebbels.

After the formal interview, over a cup of tea, I asked this intelligent and charming man: “If you could sum up your experience of the Third Reich in just one word, what would it be?” As Herr von Oven thought for a moment and considered the question, I guessed his response would make reference to the horrible crimes of the regime –crimes he freely admitted had occurred- and of the damages Nazism had wreaked upon the world. “Well,” he finally said, “if I was asked to sum up my experiences of the Third Reich in one word, that word would be – Paradise.”9

Rees also recalls an interview with Hans Friedrich, a former member of the SS, who admits to having personally shot Jews.

Even today, with the Nazi regime long defeated, he is not sorry for what he did. …At the time he personally believed it was right to shoot Jews, and he gives every appearance of believing it today…and the contemporary evidence shows that he is not unique.9

This attitude was certainly not restricted to those of low social standing or poor education; of the fifteen people around the table at the Wannsee Conference, where the Final Solution was devised, eight held academic doctorates.9

We have already alluded to the fact of a changed allegiance for the Nazi doctor, his first priority being the state rather than the individual before him.

Nazi physicians became the doctors of the Volk, responsible to the greater nation and not to their individual patients. Even at Nuremberg the defence argued that it was reasonable to sacrifice the interests of the few to benefit the majority6.

Added to this was the concept of the “life not worth living”. One of the lawyers who prosecuted the Nazis in the war crimes trials explains the significance of this step

You have only to decide that one group of human beings have lost human rights….I suspect that step was in the adoption of “quality of life” as an indicator of who is and who is not to count as a human being.10

Hitler’s adult euthanasia program began in August 1939, with a statement by Philipp Bouhler, then head of the Party Chancellery, that the purpose of eliminating persons having a “life unworthy of life” was not only to continue the “struggle against genetic disease” but also to “free up hospital beds”.11 A further distancing of the physician from the individual before him was that the decision to terminate the life of a patient under this programme was taken by an official body.11

Social Darwinism was a dangerous idea. However, there came a point when it moved from a corrupt ideology to a programme of killing. There had to be a first case, a first critical incident where a doctor was faced with a patient and made a choice to treat him in a certain manner. The case that is said to have precipitated the first phase of the Nazi euthanasia programme, the “children’s ‘euthanasia’ programme”, occurred in late 1938 or early 1939 and involved a handicapped child. According to Karl Brandt, Hitler’s personal physician, in his testimony at the Nuremberg Medical Trial, the case involved a child had been born blind, with one leg and part of one arm missing, and who had learning difficulties. The child’s father had apparently written to Hitler asking, “for this child, or this creature, to be put down.”11 And so an individual doctor made a decision about this individual patient, and the rest, as they say, is history. It is sufficient to conclude that the neither medical professional societies, the legal system, the state, nor the voice of the general public prevented the development of Nazi medical culture or its moral collapse. Indeed, even Jose Miola’s concept of an overarching ethical board,3 had it existed, is likely to have become part of the Nazi apparatus of state, supporting rather than halting the moral atrocities that were perpetrated.

Resistance

Whilst there do not seem to be many examples of doctors who resisted the Nazis, there were certainly individuals who did make a stand; a letter from Himmler to General Field Marshal Milch concerning transfer of Dr Sigmund Rascher to the Waffen-SS notes the impediment to their experiments posed by physicians from ”Christian Medical Circles” who have religious objections to the work. In addition to lamenting the “narrow mindedness” of such individuals, he specifically requests that a “non-Christian physician” be appointed to assist with the research.12

Interestingly, although Rascher was at first part of the Nazi medical hierarchy, he fell out of favour with Himmler and for a while before his execution was held at Buchenwald concentration camp along with the theologian Dietrich Bonhoeffer, who had been imprisoned for resisting the Nazi regime.

Whilst the subject of this dissertation is not that of theology or moral apologetics, I wish briefly to note the Jewish perception of such behaviour. David Gushee has made a study of the ethics of the rescuers during the Holocaust. He notes the Jewish concept of Hasidei ummot ha-olam – ‘the Righteous among the Nations’ or ‘Righteous Gentiles,’13 that is Gentiles who ‘remember God,’ who are ‘pious,’ who ‘desire to know God,’ who ‘walk uprightly’ and so on.

The Hasidim are seen as people who transcend conventional moral standards…The term hasid itself …most commonly connotes the living out of a higher ethic…of just and merciful conduct well beyond the letter of the law – especially with regard to the practice of benevolent deeds on behalf of human beings in need.13

The teaching of the Jewish Talmud speaks of humans as belonging to one of three classes of people: the resha’im gemurim (the thoroughly wicked), the beinonim (the morally average), and the zaddikim gemurim (the thoroughly righteous). The majority of men and women would be classed as beinonim. Along with this Jewish thinking about the human behaviour relies on the concept of yetzer ha-ra (the inclination to do evil) and yetzer ha-tov (the inclination to do good): These are engaged in a struggle for dominance over each individual’s character and conduct.13 Gushee notes the importance of the relationship between an individual’s propensity to behave in a moral or immoral fashion and the prevailing attitudes within their society and culture.

Certainly, a tiny number of any group of people are sociopaths, murderers, and criminals. The Nazis offered such people punishment free opportunities to act on their instincts. But more terrifying is the moral deterioration effected among many others who by no means had been sociopaths or murderers before the war. By legitimizing murder and cruelty, and inviting the locals to participate, the Nazis contributed to widespread Gentile moral deterioration. Teachers, priests, doctors, and farmers became, for a time thieves and murderers… Their failure illustrates the profound effect of social structure on character and behaviour. Moral resources for compassion and decency, however considerable, do not easily flourish in the midst of social structures that suppress such resources.13

How then were the rescuers different to the majority? Gushee identifies the following attributes: close early parent child relationships, inductive discipline, role modelling of parents – including parental modelling of care for others, the content of moral instruction, tolerance, inclusiveness, social responsibility, reference group influence (family, church, friends, neighbours, village leaders), humanitarian reasons, and religion.13 Whilst not all professed a religious belief compelled by faith, among those who did he notes the following: a sense of special religious kinship with the Jews, the remembered experience of past religious persecution, the incompatibility of Nazism and Christian faith, the concept of sacredness of every human life, and awareness of Biblical teachings on compassion and love, e.g., the Parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:33-37), the command to love command (Matt 22:36-40), the Great Judgement (Matt 25:34-40), and the Golden Rule (Matt 7:12). Most of these people were deeply rooted in a particular historic faith community.13

How can this concept of the righteous help us in our project to understand the medical professions? Gushee quotes Andrew Flescher, “the distinctive sense of moral obligation to rescue emerges only from people ‘who have the requisite amount of virtue to be able to perceive it.’ In other words, rescuers saw reality differently because their perceptual lenses were shaped by virtuous character”.14 That is, the righteous may alternatively be described as virtuous. This resonates with Pellegrino’s view of the ideal character of the physician.

Benevolence, wishing the patient’s good, and beneficence, doing the patient’s good arise from several sources – for example, respecting inherent rights, recognizing duties and obligations, or being a person who habitually desires and does the right and good thing. While the emphasis today is almost wholly on the respect of rights and duties, ultimately, the physician’s character, or to put it another way, his virtue, is the ultimate guarantee that the patient’s good will be respected.15

Pellegrino also recognises the fragile nature of society’s conception of the common good.

The problem is, of course, a subset of the deeper problems attendant on the lack of moral consensus in our society. This, in turn, is the consequence of our philosophical and theological disagreement on what constitutes the Good and the good life. A theory of good grounds every theory of morals, both general and medical. Since others are not likely to agree with our philosophical or theological definitions, we are compelled to clarify the various senses in which we may use our terms and establish an ethically defensible procedure for dealing with conflicts as they arise.15

At this point, with ongoing debates surrounding the morality of euthanasia, assisted dying, antenatal screening and extending abortion rights, we must remember that the change in ethos of Nazi medicine pivoted on the acceptance of the concept of the “life not worth living”. We must also note the limitations of legislation, guidelines and committees to prevent the gradual erosion of moral norms and the limits of the acceptable. And so, I will conclude with the words of Karl Jaspers regarding the Nazi era..

“That which has happened is a warning. To forget it is guilt. It must be continually remembered. It was possible for this to happen, and it remains possible for it to happen again at any minute. Only in knowledge can it be prevented.”16
