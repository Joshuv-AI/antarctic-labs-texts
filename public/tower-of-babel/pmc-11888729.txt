Ultrasound Diagnosis of Pneumothorax in Blunt Chest Trauma: A Correlation Study with Computed Tomography Thorax in Emergency Department Patients at Tertiary Care Centre in Salem, Tamil Nadu
Authors: Syed Abthahir Sirajudeen, Melvin Dominic, VK Sreelakshmi, Moturi Sai Vineeth, Pooja Rani, Jinka Venakata Durgaprasad, Senthil Kumar Manickam, P Panneerselvam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:In this study, we compared lung ultrasonography (USG) and computed tomography (CT) findings of the thorax in diagnosing pneumothorax in patients with blunt chest trauma. This study aimed to improve diagnostic accuracy and prevent tension pneumothorax, despite limited evaluations across all lung pathologies.Methods:We examined patients admitted to Kirupananda Variyar Medical College and Hospital with blunt chest trauma from January 2021 to June 2022. Lung USG and CT of the chest were used to detect pneumothoraxes, with the goal of reducing mortality rates in severely injured patients.Results:Out of 64 cases, 67.5% experienced blunt trauma, 20.8% had a direct chest impact, and 11.7% suffered falls from a height. USG and CT findings revealed the following: 79.2% had pneumothorax, 7.8% had pleural effusion, 28.6% had hemothorax, 22.1% had contusion, 10.4% had rib fractures with contusion, 6.5% had rib fracture + Hemothorax, and 9.1% had isolated pneumothorax.Conclusion:Lung ultrasound is a reliable, radiation-free method for assessing pneumothorax, pleural effusion, contusion, and hemothorax, offering a definitive, sensitive, and accurate diagnosis.

INTRODUCTION

Lung imaging in intensive care units (ICUs) is crucial for diagnosing and managing pathologies. Although bedside chest X-rays (CXR) are accessible, they have limitations, such as suboptimal exposure and low sensitivity. Thoracic computed tomography (CT) is considered the gold standard but is expensive, radiation-intensive, and poses risks.[1] Lung ultrasound (LUS), once undervalued due to chest wall limitations, is now gaining recognition for its diagnostic value in lung pathologies, offering real-time, noninvasive imaging with superior accuracy compared to CT scans and CXR.[2]

LUS is a reliable and repeatable tool for diagnosing lung conditions such as pneumonia, traumatic injuries, and malignant processes, offering better sensitivity and specificity than CXR, despite its limitations.[3]

Lung ultrasonography (USG) is gaining popularity due to its superior diagnostic accuracy compared to CT scans and its easy bedside availability; however, comprehensive evaluations of LUS’s diagnostic accuracy compared to CXR and CT scans remain limited. The study aimed to assess the reliability and accuracy of LUS in routine investigations by comparing it with CXR and CT, with the aim of reducing mortality in critically injured patients.

METHODS

A study was conducted at Vinayaka Mission’s Kirupananda Variyar Medical College and Hospital, Salem, from January 2021 to June 2022. The study involved 50 patients in the Emergency Department and those admitted to the ICU of VMKVMCH. This study included patients aged 18 and above with blunt chest trauma, excluding individuals with penetrating trauma, pregnant patients, individuals with unstable hemodynamic conditions, those who did not provide consent, and those unwilling to undergo CT chest scans.

Each patient with a history of blunt chest trauma underwent lung USG and CT chest imaging to determine the presence of pneumothoraxes. A USG examination was performed on each patient, including bilateral pleural interface assessments. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee at Vinayaka Mission’s Kirupananda Variyar Medical College and Hospital in Salem (Tracking ID: VMKVMC & H/IEC/21/005 Dated: 28-01-2021).

RESULTS

The study involved 77 participants, with 83.1% being males and 16.9% females. The mean age was 44.75 ± 15.26 years. Vital statistics included mean temperature, pulse rate, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, and random blood glucose. Additionally, 7.7% of participants had a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score between 4 and 12, 62.3% had a GCS score greater than 13, and 23.4% presented with pallor. On general physical examination, 22.1% of participants exhibited edema. The study population was diverse in terms of age, sex, and age group. The findings indicate that 67.5% of injuries were caused by blunt trauma from accidents, with direct chest impact being the second most common cause. The least frequent cause was falls from height, accounting for 11.7% of injuries.

The study demonstrates that US can accurately detect hemothorax and contusions in all 22 cases identified by CT scans, with 100% sensitivity, 100% specificity, 100% probability, and 100% negative predictive value (NPV). Additionally, US detected all 17 cases of pleural effusion on CT scans, achieving 100% sensitivity, 100% specificity, 100% probability, and 100% NPV.

US also demonstrated 100% diagnostic accuracy in detecting rib fractures with contusions in all eight cases on CT scans, with 100% sensitivity and 100% specificity. In comparison, in all five cases of rib fractures with hemothorax on CT scans, the US achieved 100% sensitivity, 100% PPV, and 100% NPV.

DISCUSSION

The study examined lung USG in 77 patients with blunt chest injuries, with a mean age of 44.75 ± 15.26 years. The majority of cases were diagnosed with pneumothorax, with US demonstrating a sensitivity of 95.31%, specificity of 100%, PPV of 100%, and NPV of 81.25%. US detected 61 out of 64 pneumothorax cases, with a sensitivity of 95.31%, specificity of 100%, PPV of 100%, and NPV of 81.25%. In a similar study, 28 patients (26.2%) were diagnosed with pneumothorax, with a sensitivity of 60.71% and specificity of 97.47%.[4]

Ebrahimi’s 2014 study reported a 20% prevalence of pneumothorax, with 99% specificity and 87% sensitivity for US.[5] FAST recommendations from the Society of Critical Care Medicine are recommended for suspected pneumothorax. The study by Khalil et al.[6] found that chest USG is more sensitive than CXR in detecting pleural effusion. Their study also highlighted that most cases of tension pneumothorax can be diagnosed based on clinical presentation alone. These findings are consistent with previous studies, with US sensitivity and specificity reported at 94% and 98%, respectively.

A meta-analysis by Winkler et al.[7] found lung USG as the preferred first-line diagnostic technique for lung contusions, with a sensitivity of 49.09% and specificity of 96.15. Lung USG is also a reliable diagnostic technique for bedside detection of lung consolidations, with a sensitivity of 82.8% and specificity of 95.5%, making it suitable for all patients, especially those with pleuritic chest discomfort. Rib fractures account for 25% of thoracic injuries, with 40% of patients with chest trauma experiencing 15 cracked ribs.[8]

CONCLUSION

A study of 77 patients revealed that 79.2% had pneumothorax, with 61 cases detected by lung US. Lung US is now considered the gold standard for diagnosing traumatic pneumothorax in trauma bays, as it reduces hospital stays, complications, and pain.
