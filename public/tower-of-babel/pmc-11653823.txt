What influences the community well-being of Chinese living in metropolitan areas? A scale validation and invariance test across gender, age, and hukou
Authors: Wei Kang, Haoyi Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRapid socioeconomic development has congregated urban dwellers in various communities, and it remains unclear whether raising income has brought greater happiness or well-being in China. The study aims to validate the Multi-dimensional Community Well-being Scale (MCWS) in the context of Chinese metropolitan areas.MethodsMultistage stratified sampling from North, East, and West China yielded a total of 4786 community-dwelling adults (mean age = 54.66 years; 33.8% female). Exploratory factor analysis identified a four-factor pattern of habitability, accessibility, relation, and satisfaction. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) further verified the structure, and multigroup CFA tested the measurement invariance upon different genders, age groups, and hukou statuses.ResultsAll the subscales and the total scale (α = 0.852) had satisfactory internal consistency. In addition, significant community well-being disparities exist between communities with and without elderly care and child care services. Also, the mean scores of accessibility, relation, and satisfaction grow steadily with age, except for habitability.ConclusionsOverall, this study filled the research gap in China by presenting a reliable and valid instrument to measure community well-being, showing gender, age, and hukou-related characteristics in community well-being and enabling cross-cultural comparisons in urban studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20979-8.

Background

According to the latest National Population Census, the urban population in China has reached 920 million, accounting for 63.89% of the total population1. For the first time in history, the number of urban residents has surpassed the rural population, and the urbanization trend is ever-accelerating [1]. Unlike the low spatial mobility rooted in the traditional peasant economy, metropolitan cities in China keep attracting rural surplus laborers in search of better life and career opportunities [2]. Communities in metropolitan cities in China are thus characterized by a mixture of locals and non-locals with different hukou ownerships [3]. The hukou system is a household registration system in China that classifies citizens based on their place of origin (rural or urban) and, consequently, grants access to various social services such as healthcare and education. Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that hukou ownership has a positive impact on the health and psychological well-being of urban residents [4], while it may exclude those non-local residents from many socioeconomic benefits that only locals are entitled to [5]. Rapid socioeconomic development has congregated urban dwellers in various communities, and it remains unclear whether raising income has brought greater happiness or well-being [6]. In addition, urban communities accommodate residents of males and females, from young people to older adults, who may have different living needs and well-being implications. Considering the dominant quantity and diverse needs of Chinese people living in urban communities, it is vital to investigate the well-being of community residents with a reliable and valid measurement tool.

Notably, China as a rapidly aging society is experiencing a skewed demographics. The working-age population will decline dramatically in the near future while the proportion of its dependent population – comprised of children and seniors – will increase [7]. Against this backdrop, elderly care and child care services may play vital roles in shaping the social fabric, health outcomes, and economic stability of communities. The significance of elderly care and child care services is manifested in The Healthy China 2030, the first medium-to-long-term strategic policy for health at the national level in China and a localization of the Sustainable Development Goals of the United Nations [8]. Considering China’s demographic shift and national initiative in health, we will emphasize elderly care and child care services in evaluating community well-being in China.

This study aims to validate the Multi-dimensional Community Well-being Scale (MCWS) in the context of Chinese metropolitan areas, test measurement invariance across gender, age, and hukou subgroups, and compare community well-being scores categorized by elderly care and child care services.

Concept of community well-being

To our knowledge, there is little consensus on the definition of “community well-being” in the scholarship. Internationally, community well-being has been conceptualized with an emphasis on socioeconomic development in “being” in the community. For example, Canadian scholars include indicators from the Census of Canada in areas of education, labor force participation, and housing [9]. While other scholars point out that it is crucial to focus on “well,” a term signifying “an ideally acceptable condition.” [10] The present study follows the definition of community by Sirgy [11]: community is an entity that people live and experience together as a social grouping. It contains a geographical area where persons share a common territorial space instead of via online connection or other interests-based ties, such as workplace community or leisure community [12]. On such a premise, we propose to include a spatial consideration (e.g., the greenspace) and a geographical boundary (e.g., neighborhood) when conceptualizing community well-being. Apart from these objective indicators, we direct attention to subjective indicators as a community is a social system comprising human beings. Individuals living in the community may have interactions towards persons in the neiborhood, and influence each other within a community. Thus, the concept of community well-being also raises the issue of relational elements [10]. Given the above, the approach to community well-being cares not only about external/objective measures related to living spaces but also internal/subjective perceptions, attitudes, and experiences of life and social surroundings [13].

Notably, although theories of community well-being stem from a welfare state discourse, their scope differs from national/state well-being [10]. In China, previous research on community well-being has used an aggregation of subjective well-being in measuring the community well-being, and its focus is on the relationship between the well-being of individuals and the physical environment and spatial conditions in which they live [14]. We employ an ecological systems perspective [15] to understand community well-being through the lens of macro, mezzo, and microsystems. National governments may implement welfare policies (macro) to improve the well-being of citizens, but at the community level, we focus on services (mezzo) that affect dwellers. On the other hand, the concept of community well-being is distinguished from individual or household-level indicators, such as quality of life and family well-being [12]. Community well-being refers to living well together at the community level or focusing on the role of the community to bring good to residents in the community [16]. In sum, measuring community well-being deals with objective and subjective indicators regarding conditions to flourish for community residents, with its scope below the national level (macro) yet beyond the individual/household level (micro).

Item development with a multi-dimensional approach

Being aware of the multi-dimensional nature of community well-being [10, 17], we proceeded to develop items under various domains in the Chinese metropolitan areas. First, we created objective indicators from an environmental perspective, as community well-being essentially concerns the physical conditions of a community. The term “objective well-being” refers to the material and social conditions that exist at the group or community level to sustain the development of human beings [18]. Specifically, there should have materials to fulfil basic human living conditions (habitable) and social conditions to accommodate essential living needs as human are social beings (accessible). We devised items related to (1) whether the community is habitable to human beings and (2) whether the community is accessible to necessary services or facilities. Also, we created subjective indicators from a social perspective because a community compromises persons with interactions, and the concept of well-being involves relationality [12]. Specifically, we devised items related to (1) neighborhood relationships and (2) satisfaction in the community.

We shuffled the order of all items to avoid system errors in the survey. A complete set of items of the MCWS can be found in the supplementary file.

Method

Data collection

As part of a large-scale UK and China collaborative research project on Sustainable Cities and Neighborhoods [19], data were collected from multistage stratified sampling from January 2018 to December 2019. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of University of Glasgow, UK (No. 400190112). Research sites were selected in one major city in the North, East, and West of China, having three cities: Tianjin, Hangzhou, and Chengdu. In each city, all administrative districts constituted a sample frame, yielding 25 administrative districts in total. Then, a stratified sampling method was employed to determine sample residential neighborhoods in the 25 administrative districts with the following considerations: (1) the location (core city central area, intermediate zone in the built-up area, and periphery/suburban zone); and (2) neighborhood type (e.g., older and new commercial housing, government-supported affordable housing, privatized work unit housing, resettlement, and relocation housing). Consequently, we entered 78 communities representing all types of neighborhoods in different metropolitan areas.

Within each selected community, trained investigators conducted surveys on an onsite and convenient sampling method to different types of households. The household member with the highest income answered the questionnaire on behalf of other household members. A total of 4927 residents living in urban communities were approached. All participants responded to structured questions under the interviewer-conducting survey method with trained interviewers. We cleaned the data and deleted 141 cases with missing values. The final research sample had 4786 subjects aged 19 to 102 (mean age = 54.66 years; 33.8% female). The vast majority were married (87.8%) and had tertiary education attainment or above (90.5%). 63.4% claimed to have good health, and the average monthly income of all participants was 6,184 RMB (approximately 850 USD).

Measurement

We developed 16 items of multidimensions to measure the community well-being among residents (above 18 years) in the context of Chinese metropolitan areas. Participants rated how strongly they agreed or disagreed with 16 statements on the community they were dwelling in using a 5-point scale, from “1 = strongly disagree” to “5 = strongly agree.” Specifically, nine items were objective indicators concerning the environmental conditions of the community, and the remaining seven items were subjective indicators regarding the social aspects of the community. An example of an objective indicator would be, “The community has enough green space.” An example of a subjective indicator would be, “Most neighbors in the community are trustworthy.” A higher total score demonstrated a higher level of community well-being in the context of Chinese metropolitan areas. The total scale has a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.852 in the present study. We obtained informed consent from participants before the survey.

Data analysis

To begin with, we compiled descriptive statistics of participants, including social demographic information. As it is a newly developed scale, we first used exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to determine the factor pattern, with an extraction method of Maximum Likelihood and a rotation method of Varimax. Based on the guidelines Worthington and Whittaker (2006) proposed, accepted items shall have factor loadings above 0.30 without cross-loading issues. Upon securing an initial model from EFA, we conducted confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to verify the pattern structure of subscales. The criteria of model fit follow suggestions by Brown [20]: (1) values of the comparative fit index above 0.90, (2) values of the robust root mean square error of approximation below 0.08, and (3) values of standardized root mean square residual below 0.08.

Next, we assessed the measurement invariance of the MCWS using multigroup CFA and tested the configual, metric, and scalar models across gender, age, and hukou subgroups. Differences in the Chi-square test, changes in the CFI and RMSEA were checked whether they met the threshold of invariance: (1) absolute values of ∆CFI lower than 0.01 [21], and (2) absolute values of ∆RMSEA lower than 0.015 [22].

In addition, we examined the reliability of the scale and subscales with Cronbach’s alpha values, together with the corrected item-dimension correlation of items in each subscale. We also compared the subscale means differences between urban communities with elderly care services and those without, using an independent sample t-test. A significant p-value indicated there exists a statistically significant difference between the two groups. Similarly, we compared subscale means differences between urban communities with child care services and those without. Also, we checked the relationship between age groups (18 to 40, 41 to 60, 61 to 80, and above 80 years) and each subscale to reveal the trend of MCWS with age. We employed SPSS 24.0 and Mplus 8.0 to complete the above data analysis.

Results

Exploratory factor analysis

We performed exploratory factor analysis (EFA) in SPSS with the maximum likelihood extraction and the varimax rotation method to identify the scale’s factor structure. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin value was 0.807 for objective indicators and 0.767 for subjective indicators, with significant Bartlett’s test of sphericity (p < 0.001). It suggests that the sample met the factor analysis criteria [23]. Table 1 shows a rotated factor matrix from EFA (n = 4786), yielding a four-factor solution that explains 61.52% of the total variance. Each item had a single dominant factor loading for objective/subjective indicators, and no cross-loading issue occurred. The first factor encompassed four items (Items 3, 4, 8, 9) regarding the environmental conditions of the community (e.g., hygiene and green space), thus termed “habitability.” The second factor contained five items (Items 5, 6, 7, 13,14) that described different types of facilities in the community or vicinity (e.g., supermarkets and public transportation), and we called it “accessibility.” The third factor included three items (Items 1, 2, 14) focusing on neighborhood relationships, such as taking care of each other, and was coined as “relation.” The remaining items (Items 10, 11, 15, 16) reflected subjective attitudes toward the community, for example, “I am satisfied with the social organization in the community” and “I want to live in the community permanently.” We branded it as “satisfaction.” Thus, a four-factor pattern of habitability, accessibility, relation, and satisfaction was tentatively accepted for further examination. As seen from Table 1, all items had factor loadings above 0.30 (ranging from 0.425 to 0.885) without cross-loading issues. Therefore, no items needed to be removed from the scale.

Confirmatory factor analysis

Subsequently, we performed a confirmatory factor analysis (n = 4786) in Mplus to confirm the four-factor structure identified from EFA. We adopted the maximum likelihood estimation method because all items had a normal distribution, with skewness and kurtosis values below one [24]. As shown in Fig. 1, the model demonstrated sufficient model fit (χ2 = 2168.675, df = 97, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.928, TLI = 0.911, RMSEA = 0.067, and SRMR = 0.047). All standardized factor loadings exceeded the threshold of 0.40 and were significant at the 0.001 level. Also, the four subscales of habitability, accessibility, relation, and satisfaction significantly and positively correlated with each other, with the coefficient ranging from 0.228 to 0.764. Overall, the CFA test confirms the construct validity of the four-factor model of the MCWS.

Measurement invariance test

Having confirmed the factor structure, we continued to test the measurement invariance across gender (males and females), age (18–60 years and over 60 years), and hukou status (locals and non-locals). Further, we performed three levels of invariance tests (configural, metric, and scalar) in gender, age, and hukou-related subgroups using Mplus (see Table 3). By constraining the factorial structure, we tested the configural model with satisfactory results that met the criteria. Next, we tested the metric models by constraining the factor loadings. Then, we compared metric models with configural models (baseline models) in all subgroups based on the Chi-square test, changes in CFI, and RMSEA. The results indicate invariant factor loadings across gender subgroups (∆CFI = 0.000, ∆RMSEA = 0.002), age subgroups (∆CFI = 0.001, ∆RMSEA = 0.002), and hukou subgroups (∆CFI = 0.001, ∆RMSEA = 0.000). Similarly, we compared the scalar and configual models (baseline models) by constraining the item intercepts. The results showed invariant item intercepts on gender subgroups (∆CFI = 0.001, ∆RMSEA = 0.004), age subgroups (∆CFI = 0.003, ∆RMSEA = 0.003), and hukou subgroups (∆CFI = 0.002, ∆RMSEA = 0.001). In sum, measurement invariance of the MCWS was established as changes in CFI and RMSEA were below the thresholds of 0.015.

Reliability evaluation

After securing the measurement invariance of the MCWS, we moved to evaluate the reliability of the scale and subscales. Table 4 presents the dimension characters of the MCWS. The item-dimension correlations of habitability, accessibility, relation, and satisfaction were 0.61 to 0.68, 0.49 to 0.60, 0.60 to 0.79, and 0.37 to 0.58. It demonstrates that in each subscale, items are highly homogeneous. The Cronbach’s alpha value of the total scale was 0.85, indicating high internal consistency. Also, the four subscales of habitability, accessibility, relation, and satisfaction with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.78, 0.81, 0.85, and 0.69, respectively, imply sufficient reliability.

Means comparisons

Given the characteristics of the adult samples who may have co-residing children and older parents, we conducted means comparisons of the total scale and subscales, which were categorized by community services of elderly care and child care. According to Table 5, samples scored significantly higher in the overall community well-being when their community provided elderly care services (yes = 3.943, no = 3.649, p < 0.001) and child care services (yes = 4.008, no = 3.715, p < 0.001). In particular, scores in the subscale of habitability are significantly higher in communities with elderly care (yes = 3.820, no = 3.533, p < 0.001) and child care services (yes = 3.896, no = 3.593, p < 0.001) than those without; scores in the subscale of accessibility are significantly higher in communities with elderly care (yes = 3.811, no = 3.552, p < 0.001) and child care services (yes = 3.881, no = 3.599, p < 0.001) than those without; scores in the subscale of relation is significantly higher in communities with elderly care (yes = 4.021, no = 3.757, p < 0.001) and child care services (yes = 4.084, no = 3.817, p < 0.001) than those without; scores in the subscale of satisfaction is significantly higher in communities with elderly care (yes = 4.120, no = 3.755, p < 0.001) and child care services (yes = 4.173, no = 3.851, p < 0.001) than those without.

Discussion

The present study has validated the 16-item MCWS by examining its reliability and validity among adult community dwellers in Chinese metropolitan areas. We extracted a four-factor pattern from EFA and labeled them as habitability (four items), accessibility (five items), relation (three items), and satisfaction (four items) based on item content and the conceptual meaning of community well-being [10]. CFA findings confirmed the pattern structure with all factor loadings on latent sub-construct above the threshold of 0.40. Multigroup CFA tested the configural, metric, and scalar invariances of the MCWS across gender, age, and hukou, showing that the scale’s factor structure, factor loadings, and intercepts were equivalent between male and female, young and older, and local and non-local community residents. In addition, the MCWS demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency with its Cronbach’s alpha value larger than 0.80. The four subscales also had satisfactory reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.69 to 0.85. In sum, our study shows that Chinese residents in metropolitan areas, regardless of their gender, age, and hukou status, can have greater community well-being by enhancing environmental conditions, such as habitability (e.g., more green spaces) and accessibility (e.g., more convenient public transportation), as well as improving their relation in the neighborhood and satisfaction in the community via community social work and social services.

Furthermore, the findings reveal that in communities with elderly care and child care services, residents had significantly higher community well-being scores in each subscale than those without. Family caregiving can be challenging for people and affects their well-being if the burden is substantial and sustained [25]. Caring for older people and children is a critical issue for China, a rapidly aging developing society coupled with its meager fertility rate [26]. Compared to informal family caregivers, trained professionals in elderly care institutions can monitor the health conditions of older people, administer medications, and provide medical assistance when needed. Not surprisingly, communities with elderly care services accommodate residents with greater well-being as it can relieve the care burden to a family with fragile elderly. Our study yields initial evidence of well-being benefits for community residents who live in the community with elderly care services in Chinese metropolitan areas. Similarly, child care services in the community significantly affect the well-being of community residents. Access to reliable child care services enables parents, especially mothers, to participate in the workforce [27]. This not only helps in enhancing the financial stability of families but also contributes to the economic growth of the community and the country as a whole. And urban residents living in communities with child care services are generally happier than those without these services. More importantly, by providing child care services, urban communities in China promote gender equality by reducing the burden of child-rearing primarily placed on women [28]. This allows women to have more equal opportunities for education, employment, and personal development, which is crucial for the overall progress of the community. In summary, elderly care and child care services are essential for the well-being of community residents in Chinese metropolitan areas, as they provide social support, ensure health and safety, enable workforce participation, and contribute to gender equality.

Additionally, our study discovers that scores on habitability decrease with age and range the lowest among all subscales. Sample items under the habitability subscale are “the community has good hygiene” and “the community has enough green space.” With age, individuals may experience physical limitations or health issues that affect their ability to fully engage with their surroundings [29]. For instance, mobility issues or chronic pain may restrict their access to certain areas of their living spaces, limiting their perception of habitability. In addition, middle adults (in this study, the ones who earned the highest in the household) generally spend most of their time working instead of staying in the community; thus, their expectation of habitability may be lower than their older counterparts. This may partly explain why older adults gave relatively low scores on habitability while middle adults scored relatively high in the habitability subscale. On the other hand, individuals may experience loss of loved ones or separation from their social support networks in aging. This can lead to feelings of loneliness or isolation, which may negatively affect their perception of habitability. Social connections and interactions play a crucial role in overall well-being and satisfaction with one’s living environment. The above findings also remind us of the importance of green space and hygiene to older people in the community, as they spend more time in the community than the middle-aged and are more sensitive to these environmental conditions.

Measuring community well-being is particularly important in China, as Chinese people highly value social connections and community harmony. Previous studies show community social relationships affect individuals’ well-being [30]. It is critical to recognize, however, that community well-being is distinct from psychological well-being, quality of life, or subjective well-being that is measured at the individual level. As proposed by Sirgy [11], community indicator researchers should identify the values or the normative basis of a “good community,” such as values related to sustainability and social cohesion. Given this, measuring community well-being goes beyond an individual’s well-being but is closely related to each individual’s well-being. Our study echoes such a finding through subjective indicators in the scale. Maintaining community well-being fosters social cohesion and collective responsibility, which are integral to the Chinese culture and help build strong relationships among community members. Also, community well-being contributes to an improved quality of life for Chinese people. It ensures access to essential services, such as healthcare, education, and recreational facilities, which are vital for both individuals and families residing in urban areas. Unlike living in rural communities where residents are mostly connected with patriarchal clans, blood ties, or kinship [31], urban residents in the community gather from various backgrounds and usually are strangers to each other. Urban communities can be vast and diverse. Thus, forming a sense of belonging is essential. Promoting community well-being helps create an inclusive environment where individuals feel accepted, understood, and valued, especially for migrant populations [32]. Moreover, having a reliable and valid tool to measure their well-being in urban communities helps policymakers implement evidence-based health policies. Future studies can investigate how community well-being affects the health and well-being of individuals by rigorous theoretical models and data analysis. It is believed that by creating a supportive and healthy environment, Chinese people can benefit from improved physical and mental health outcomes, leading to a better quality of life for themselves and their families. In addition, the scale is developed in metropolitan areas of China that are heavily influenced by migrants and the aging process. Thus, community dwellers are characterized by local and non-local populations, an expanding older population, and a growing need for child care. Thus, developing such a scale encompassing contextual and cross-cultural features is vital to accurately measure community well-being in China.

We acknowledge that there are some limitations in this study. First, the data was collected before the outbreak of COVID-19. The composition and landscape of urban communities in China may have changed, and thus, we need to be cautious in interpreting the findings of this study. Second, as it is a large-scale survey conducted in three metropolitan cities in China, many interviewers were involved in the questionnaire surveys. Due to the nature of the interviewer-conducting survey nature, interviewers’ bias may occur unconsciously. We have given strict training to the interviewers to ensure they stay neutral and professional when taking surveys. Also, the sampling areas were the main administrative districts of three sub-provincial level metropolitan cities, which may differ from small-scale cities in China. Thus, the findings of the study cannot be generalized to all urban cities in the China context. Despite the above limitations, our study covers samples from North, East, and West China and provides valuable insights into urban studies in China.

Conclusions

Our study has validated the 16-item Multi-dimensional Community Well-being Scale (MCWS) in the context of Chinese metropolitan areas. Four subscales of habitability, accessibility, relation, and satisfaction encompass both objective and subjective indicators that measure the overall well-being of community residents. The MCWS has demonstrated satisfactory construct validity and internal reliability, enabling international comparisons in urban studies. Also, the MCWS’s measurement invariance has been tested across gender, age, and hukou subgroups, which shows its broad applicability to various populations. The means comparisons in communities with or without elderly and child care services revealed the importance of providing such services to the community well-being of people living in Chinese metropolitan areas. In addition, the downward trend of habitability scores with age reminds us to build a habitable community suitable for all. Overall, the findings contribute to urban studies in China by providing a reliable and valid measurement tool and enriching the literature with robust evidence of community well-being disparities based on community characteristics.
