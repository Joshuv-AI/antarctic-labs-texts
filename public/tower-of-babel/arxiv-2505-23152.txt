Provable Benefit of Random Permutations over Uniform Sampling in Stochastic Coordinate Descent
Donghwa Kim, Jaewook Lee, Chulhee Yun

Abstract
We analyze the convergence rates of two popular variants of coordinate descent (CD): random CD (RCD), in which the coordinates are sampled uniformly at random, and random-permutation CD (RPCD), in which random permutations are used to select the update indices. Despite abundant empirical evidence that RPCD outperforms RCD in various tasks, the theoretical gap between the two algorithms’ performance has remained elusive. Even for the benign case of positive-definite quadratic functions with permutation-invariant Hessians, previous efforts have failed to demonstrate a provable performance gap between RCD and RPCD. To this end, we present novel results showing that, for a class of quadratics with permutation-invariant structures, the contraction rate upper bound for RPCD is always strictly smaller than the contraction rate lower bound for RCD for every individual problem instance. Furthermore, we conjecture that this function class contains the worst-case examples of RPCD among all positive-definite quadratics. Combined with our RCD lower bound, this conjecture extends our results to the general class of positive-definite quadratic functions.

Introduction

We consider the minimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f({\bm{x}}),

(1)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a smooth and convex function.

The
coordinate descent
(CD) algorithm has been proposed and widely used for solving problem (
1
) arising in modern optimization and machine-learning problems as it can significantly reduce the computational overhead of high-dimensional or large-scale problems by updating only a single coordinate (or sometimes a small subset, referred to as blocks) instead of the whole set of parameters.
Stemming from extensive studies on different types of CD algorithms
(
Beck & Tetruashvili 2013
;
Lee & Sidford 2013
;
Wright 2015
;
Nesterov & Stich 2017
)
,
randomized
versions of CD have been especially popularized since
Nesterov 2012
;
Richtárik & Takác 2011
.
Usually referred to as
random coordinate descent
(RCD), these types of algorithms choose the coordinates to update i.i.d. randomly from a certain distribution, typically uniform and sometimes specifically chosen according to the problem geometry.
The introduction of randomness has been demonstrated in
Sun & Ye 2016
to outperform deterministic algorithms like
cyclic coordinate descent
(CCD) in terms of worst-case performance.

Meanwhile, for stochastic algorithms under finite-sum minimization settings, utilizing
random permutations
has been common for a long time, based on observations that taking a full pass among the component gradient updates in a randomly permuted order (commonly referred to as SGD with Random Reshuffling) shows faster convergence speed than its ordinary, i.i.d. with-replacement-sampling counterpart.
After empirical observations and some conjectures
(
Bottou 2009
;
Recht & Re 2012
)
, a recent line of work has theoretically analyzed the exact convergence rates of SGD-RR and demonstrated the benefits over ordinary SGD

(
Ahn et al. 2020
;
Mishchenko et al. 2020
;
Cha et al. 2023
;
Liu & Zhou 2024
)
.
Many other stochastic algorithms also incorporate random reshuffling for acceleration, including federated learning
(
Mishchenko et al. 2022
;
Yun et al. 2022
)
and minimax optimization
(
Das et al. 2022
;
Cho & Yun 2023
)
.

A similar variant also exists for CD, often referred to as
random-permutation coordinate descent
(RPCD)
(
Sun et al. 2020
)
.
While RPCD is based on a similar idea that using permutations can accelerate,
theoretical analysis of RPCD is even harder because we must focus on the
preconditioning-like effects
of using permutations, which is very different from the case of SGD-RR (where random permutations induce
variance reduction
) and is notoriously difficult to inspect theoretically.
However, for strongly convex quadratic functions with
permutation-invariant Hessians
, it is possible to compute the expectation of such matrices over all permutations explicitly.
Based on this approach,
Lee & Wright 2019
(first appeared in 2016) derive convergence bounds for the expected
function value
for quadratic functions with permutation-invariant Hessians, and a follow-up work
Wright & Lee 2017
further extends to Hessians with slight diagonal perturbations.

A natural, important question arises on
whether RPCD beats RCD
, just as in the case of SGD with random permutations
Bottou 2009
.
Many empirical studies have shown this to be true, as we demonstrate in
Figure

1
, but the theoretical aspects of this phenomenon have been relatively less revealed in the literature.

Gürbüzbalaban et al. 2018
demonstrate that it is possible to derive a stronger convergence upper bound for RPCD for permutation-invariant matrices with negative off-diagonal entries.
However, they only compare between the
upper bounds
of RCD and RPCD, failing to demonstrate a rigorous gap between the two algorithms.
Also, even for this comparison, the paper does not provide a clear analysis (other than numerical experiments) on the assertion that the ratio of contraction upper bounds for RCD to RPCD is larger than
1
.

Summary of Contributions.

Our results contribute to overcoming these limitations by adequately comparing the lower bounds for RCD and the upper bounds for RPCD.
We present the following results in the paper.

•

In
Theorem

3.1
, we show a novel convergence lower bound of RCD that holds for general quadratics with positive definite Hessians.

•

In
Theorem

3.3
, we show the convergence upper bound of RPCD for a class of quadratic functions including permutation-invariant Hessians.
This upper bound coincides with the RCD lower bound, concluding that RPCD outperforms RCD for
any
problem instance.

•

In
Theorem

3.4
, we show a stronger convergence lower bound of RCD that holds for the same function class with the RPCD upper bounds.
This demonstrates the existence of
a wider gap
between RCD and RPCD for all problem instances in this function class.

•

In
Section

4
, we conjecture that our RPCD upper bounds can be extended to the general class of positive definite quadratic functions.
We also provide some experiments demonstrating the convergence of RPCD and RCD in practice.

Preliminaries

For a positive integer
N
, we write
[N]:=\{1,2,\dots,N\}
.
We denote the dimension (number of coordinates) by
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
We define
{\mathbb{S}}^{n}
(
{\mathbb{S}}_{+}^{n}
) as the collection of all symmetric (positive-definite) matrices of size

.
We write
\|\cdot\|
for the Euclidean

-norm for vectors and the spectral norm (

We also use
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
for the Euclidean

-norm in an analogous sense.
The symbols
\otimes
and
\odot
represent the matrix Kronecker product and the elementwise Hadamard product, respectively.

The spectral radius (

, maximum absolute eigenvalue) of a matrix

is denoted by

, and the minimum (maximum) eigenvalue of matrices
{\bm{M}}
(with real eigenvalues) is denoted by

(

).
We denote by
\mathrm{tril}({\bm{M}})
the lower triangular part of
{\bm{M}}
, including the diagonals.
We will usually denote the elements of matrices by lower-case letters with subscripts, as in
{\bm{A}}=(a_{ij})
, and the elements of vectors via parentheses indicating the index, as in
{\bm{x}}=(x(1),\dots,x(n))
.

We denote by
{\bm{I}}_{k}
the
k
-dimensional identity matrix,
{\bm{1}}_{k}
the
k
-dimensional ones vector, and
\diag\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
the diagonal matrix with
(i,i)
-th entry
a_{i}
, or the similarly constructed block diagonal matrix if the elements are matrices.
(We may drop the
k
subscript in obvious cases, usually when
k=n
.)
Also, we denote by
{\bm{e}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the unit vector with the

in the

2.1
Problem Settings

Our goal is to minimize the following quadratic function:

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{x}}}f({\bm{x}}):=\frac{1}{2}{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}},

where the Hessian
{\bm{A}}\in{\mathbb{S}}_{+}^{n}
is a
positive definite
matrix.
We write
{\bm{A}}=(a_{ij})
for the elements of
{\bm{A}}
.
The objective will be to find the minimizer
{\bm{x}}^{\star}=\bm{0}
of
f
.

For useful purposes, we define the following quantity.

Definition 2.1
.

We define
\sigma=\lambda_{\min}({\bm{D}}^{-1}{\bm{A}})
, where
{\bm{D}}=\diag(a_{11},\dots,a_{nn})
is the diagonal part of
{\bm{A}}
.
In particular, if
{\bm{D}}={\bm{I}}
, then we have
\sigma=\lambda_{\min}({\bm{A}})
.

By definition, we must have
\sigma\in(0,1]
.
Later in
2.2
, we justify why we can set
{\bm{D}}={\bm{I}}
without loss of generality.
For such cases,
\sigma
is equivalent to the strong convexity constant of the function
f
, and the convergence bounds we derive will depend on
\sigma
.

Remark
.

Our analysis automatically includes the translated quadratics
f({\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{2}{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}+c
for any
{\bm{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
, as long as the Hessian
{\bm{A}}
is positive definite.
We can straightforwardly replace
{\bm{x}}
with
{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\star}
for
{\bm{x}}^{\star}={\bm{A}}^{-1}{\bm{b}}
, the minimizer of the translated problem
Wright & Lee 2017
.

As shown in

We can concisely write one iteration of RCD as follows:

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{t+1}={\bm{T}}_{{\bm{A}},i}^{\text{RCD}}{\bm{x}}_{t},

where we choose to update index

\displaystyle{\bm{T}}_{{\bm{A}},i}^{\text{RCD}}:={\bm{I}}-\frac{1}{a_{ii}}{\bm{E}}_{i}{\bm{A}}.

(4)

Remark
.

Nesterov 2012

However, in our work, we only focus on the uniformly sampled case as our purpose is to make

RPCD.

As shown in
Equation

3
, RPCD randomly chooses a permutation of indices and then takes coordinate updates in the order of the permutation we chose.

This time, we focus on one

(containing

Accordingly, here we denote by
{\bm{x}}_{k+1}

For a better illustration, we follow

is the identity permutation (which can also be viewed as an epoch of cyclic coordinate descent).
Observing that we update only the

\displaystyle x_{1}(1)

\displaystyle=x_{0}(1)-\tfrac{1}{a_{11}}\left(a_{11}x_{0}(1)+a_{12}x_{0}(2)+a_{13}x_{0}(3)\right)

\displaystyle x_{1}(2)

\displaystyle=x_{0}(2)-\tfrac{1}{a_{22}}\left(a_{21}x_{1}(1)+a_{22}x_{0}(2)+a_{23}x_{0}(3)\right)

\displaystyle x_{1}(3)

\displaystyle=x_{0}(3)-\tfrac{1}{a_{33}}\left(a_{31}x_{1}(1)+a_{32}x_{1}(2)+a_{33}x_{0}(3)\right)

which can be rearranged and written in matrix form as:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}&0&0\\
a_{21}&a_{22}&0\\
a_{31}&a_{32}&a_{33}\end{bmatrix}{\bm{x}}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\begin{bmatrix}0&a_{12}&a_{13}\\

0&0&0\end{bmatrix}{\bm{x}}_{0},

or more concisely:

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\bm{\Gamma}^{-1}({\bm{A}}-\bm{\Gamma}){\bm{x}}_{0}

\displaystyle=({\bm{I}}-\bm{\Gamma}^{-1}{\bm{A}}){\bm{x}}_{0}

where
\bm{\Gamma}=\mathrm{tril}({\bm{A}})
. In the general case, we can similarly observe that one epoch of RPCD with permutation
p_{k}=p
boils down to

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{k+1}={\bm{T}}_{{\bm{A}},p}^{\text{RPCD}}{\bm{x}}_{k},

where we define

\displaystyle{\bm{T}}_{{\bm{A}},p}^{\text{RPCD}}

\displaystyle:={\bm{I}}-{\bm{P}}\bm{\Gamma}_{{\bm{P}}}^{-1}{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}.

Here,
{\bm{P}}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}

Unit-diagonal Assumption.

Here we state and justify the following assumption on the Hessian
{\bm{A}}
of
f
.

Assumption 2.2
.

We assume that the Hessian
{\bm{A}}
has unit diagonals, or equivalently,
a_{11}=\dots=a_{nn}=1
.

This assumption might seem restrictive, but this is
without loss of generality for any coordinate-descent type methods on quadratics
.
For any nonzero diagonal matrix
{\bm{F}}
, every iterate of a certain CD algorithm (RCD, RPCD, etc.) applied on
\tilde{f}({\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{2}{\bm{x}}^{\top}\widetilde{{\bm{A}}}{\bm{x}}
for
\widetilde{{\bm{A}}}={\bm{F}}^{-1}{\bm{A}}{\bm{F}}^{-1}
proceeds as
\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{k}={\bm{F}}{\bm{x}}_{k}
, where
{\bm{x}}_{k}
is the trajectory of the same algorithm applied on

with the same initialization and choices of
i_{k}
(see Appendix A of
Wright & Lee 2017
).
We may therefore assume that
{\bm{D}}={\bm{I}}
by choosing
{\bm{F}}={\bm{D}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
(Note that
{\bm{D}}
is defined as the diagonal part of
{\bm{A}}
.)

Sign-flip Invariance.

Another particularly useful case of the diagonal transformation is when
{\bm{F}}=\mathrm{diag}({\bm{v}})
with
{\bm{v}}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
. In this setting, we have
\widetilde{{\bm{A}}}={\bm{F}}^{-1}{\bm{A}}{\bm{F}}^{-1}={\bm{A}}\odot{\bm{v}}{\bm{v}}^{\top}

2.3
Matrix Operators

Suppose that
f({\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{2}{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}
and we have some iteration that proceeds according to the following update:

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{k+1}

\displaystyle={\bm{T}}_{{\bm{A}}}{\bm{x}}_{k}

where the iteration matrix
{\bm{T}}_{{\bm{A}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is i.i.d. random and independent with the iterate
{\bm{x}}_{k}
.
Let us define a linear matrix operator
{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}({\bm{X}})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{T}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}{\bm{T}}_{{\bm{A}}}\right].

Note that we can also write
{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}
in matrix form by vectorizing the input and output matrices:

\displaystyle\mathrm{vec}({\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}({\bm{X}}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{T}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{T}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}\right]\mathrm{vec}({\bm{X}}).

We denote the matrix operator for RCD and RPCD, generated by the random matrix iterations
{\bm{T}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RCD}}
and
{\bm{T}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}}
by
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}
, respectively.

For the case of RCD, we can plug in
{\bm{T}}_{{\bm{A}},i}={\bm{I}}-{\bm{E}}_{i}{\bm{A}}
(as in (
4
) with
a_{ii}=1
) to explicitly compute

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}({\bm{X}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[({\bm{I}}-{\bm{E}}_{i}{\bm{A}})^{\top}{\bm{X}}({\bm{I}}-{\bm{E}}_{i}{\bm{A}})\right]

\displaystyle=\left({\bm{I}}-\frac{{\bm{A}}}{n}\right)^{\top}{\bm{X}}\left({\bm{I}}-\frac{{\bm{A}}}{n}\right)

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}+\frac{{\bm{A}}^{\top}(n\cdot\diag({\bm{X}})-{\bm{X}}){\bm{A}}}{n^{2}}.

To motivate the use of such matrix operators,
suppose that we define the convergence measure as the quadratic form below, where
\bm{\Theta}\in{\mathbb{S}}_{+}^{n}
is a positive definite matrix that might depend on
{\bm{A}}
but not on
{\bm{x}}_{k}
:

\displaystyle\Psi_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\bm{x}}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Theta}{\bm{x}}_{k}.

(6)

This (up to scaling) includes typical choices like the
squared Euclidean norm

\lVert{\bm{x}}_{k}\rVert^{2}
(using
\bm{\Theta}={\bm{I}}
) and the
function value

f({\bm{x}}_{k})
(using
\bm{\Theta}={\bm{A}}
).
Then we can observe that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\Psi_{k}\right]}{\Psi_{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\bm{y}}_{0}^{\top}\left(\bm{\Theta}^{-1/2}{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{k}(\bm{\Theta})\bm{\Theta}^{-1/2}\right){\bm{y}}_{0}}{\lVert{\bm{y}}_{0}\rVert^{2}}

(7)

where
{\bm{y}}_{0}:=\bm{\Theta}^{1/2}{\bm{x}}_{0}
and the expectation above is conditioned by
{\bm{x}}_{0}
.
Similarly, we omit the conditional notation part of the expectations if clear by context.

Diagonalizability.

If a linear matrix operator
{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}
is diagonalizable, then we can view
{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{k}(\bm{\Theta})
in (
7
) as a power iteration.
The limit as
k\rightarrow\infty
will be dominated by the eigenmatrix of
{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}
with the largest absolute eigenvalue among nonzero components of the eigendecomposition of
\bm{\Theta}
.
Hence we can understand the spectral radius of the operator
\rho({\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}})
as an upper bound of the contraction for convergence measures.

Lemma 2.3
.

The matrix operators
{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\emph{RCD}}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\emph{RPCD}}
are both diagonalizable.

While we defer the proof of
Lemma

2.3
to
Section

A.1
,
the main idea is to define a similar operator:

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{{\bm{A}}}({\bm{X}})

\displaystyle={\bm{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}({\bm{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bm{X}}{\bm{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}){\bm{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}

(8)

which can be shown to have a
symmetric matrix form

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\bm{T}}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}\otimes\widetilde{{\bm{T}}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\top},\quad\widetilde{{\bm{T}}}_{{\bm{A}}}:={\bm{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bm{T}}_{{\bm{A}}}{\bm{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}

(9)

for both
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}
, therefore is diagonalizable.

Furthermore, if we can show that
{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}
is closed inside a subspace
{\mathcal{S}}
which contains positive definite matrices, then choosing any
\bm{\Theta}\in{\mathcal{S}}
, it suffices to show an upper bound of
\rho(\left.{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}\right|_{{\mathcal{S}}})
, the spectral radius of
{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}

restricted at
{\mathcal{S}}
.

3.2
RPCD Upper Bounds

In this section, we focus on the case when
{\bm{A}}
is either permutation-invariant or is closely related to such a matrix.
In particular, we focus on the following class of Hessians.

Definition 3.2
.

We define the class of Hessians
{\mathcal{A}}_{\sigma}^{\text{PI}},{\mathcal{A}}_{\sigma}
as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{\sigma}^{\text{PI}}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\diag\{\sigma{\bm{I}}_{k}+(1-\sigma){\bm{1}}_{k}{\bm{1}}_{k}^{\top},{\bm{I}}_{n-k}\}:2\leq k\leq n\right\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle:=\left\{{\bm{A}}={\bm{A}}^{\text{PI}}\odot{\bm{v}}{\bm{v}}^{\top}:{\bm{A}}^{\text{PI}}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\sigma}^{\text{PI}},{\bm{v}}\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}^{n}\right\}

for given
\sigma\in(0,1]
.

Note that
\sigma{\bm{I}}_{k}+(1-\sigma){\bm{1}}_{k}{\bm{1}}_{k}^{\top}
is a unit-diagonal, permutation-invariant matrix with a
\lambda_{\min}
of
\sigma
, and that the sign-flip invariance of CD (see the end of
Section

2.2
) allows us to extend any type of convergence analysis from
{\mathcal{A}}_{\sigma}^{\text{PI}}
to
{\mathcal{A}}_{\sigma}
.

Theorem

3.3
provides the convergence upper bound of RPCD for the expected iterate norm.

Theorem 3.3
.

For an initial point
{\bm{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
{\bm{x}}_{K}
be the output of
RPCD
after
K
epochs. If
{\bm{A}}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\sigma}
and
{\bm{x}}_{0}\neq 0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{K\to\infty}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\bm{x}}_{K}\|^{2}\right]}{\|{\bm{x}}_{0}\|^{2}}\right)^{\!\!\frac{1}{K}}\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{n}\!\!\!,\left(1-\frac{\sigma}{n}\right)^{2n}\right\}.

Summarizing our results in
Theorem

3.1
and
Theorem

3.3
, we can show that RPCD outperforms RCD for
all
instances of the form
{\bm{A}}=\diag\{\sigma{\bm{I}}_{k}+(1-\sigma){\bm{1}}_{k}{\bm{1}}_{k}^{\top},{\bm{I}}_{n-k}\}
.
The threshold between the two bounds is

\displaystyle\max\left\{\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{n},\left(1-\frac{\sigma}{n}\right)^{2n}\right\},

(10)

where we recall that
n
iterations of RCD are equivalent to one epoch of RPCD for a fair comparison.

Proof Sketch.

The proof starts from the observation that if
{\bm{A}}\in{\mathcal{S}}:=\mathrm{span}\{{\bm{I}},{\bm{1}}{\bm{1}}^{\top}\}
, or considering
2.2
:

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}=\sigma{\bm{I}}+(1-\sigma){\bm{1}}{\bm{1}}^{\top},

(11)

then
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}
is closed in
{\mathcal{S}}=\mathrm{span}\{{\bm{I}},{\bm{1}}{\bm{1}}^{\top}\}
.
This allows us to convert the
restricted matrix operator

\left.\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}\right|_{{\mathcal{S}}}

into a
2\times 2
matrix
{\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}
.

Therefore, if we choose any
\bm{\Theta}\in{\mathcal{S}}
with
\bm{\Theta}\succ 0
to define the convergence measure (we choose
\bm{\Theta}={\bm{I}}
for
Theorem

3.3
), the matrix
\left({\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}}\right)^{k}(\bm{\Theta})
will stay in
\mathrm{span}\{{\bm{I}},{\bm{1}}{\bm{1}}^{\top}\}
and it suffices to find an upper bound of
\rho(\left.\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}\right|_{{\mathcal{S}}})=\rho({\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}})
for this
2\times 2
matrix.
We also show that the matrix

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}=\diag\{\sigma{\bm{I}}_{k}+(1-\sigma){\bm{1}}_{k}{\bm{1}}_{k}^{\top},{\bm{I}}_{n-k}\}

for all
2\leq k\leq n
has the same value of
\rho(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}})
with that of the
k\times k
matrix
{\bm{A}}_{k}=\sigma{\bm{I}}_{k}+(1-\sigma){\bm{1}}_{k}{\bm{1}}_{k}^{\top}
.

After this, we upper bound
\rho({\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}_{k}})
with
\|{\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}_{k}}\|_{\infty}
, which can be expressed as the maximum of two polynomials of
\sigma
.
We then proceed to divide the range of
\sigma
into three regions:
(0,0.6]
,
[0.6,0.8]
, and
[0.8,1.0]
.
Perhaps interestingly, we can find a (nearly) sign-alternating trend in the coefficients of both the polynomials from
\|{\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}_{k}}\|_{\infty}
and the upper bound.
This allows us to use lower-order Taylor approximations instead of the true lower and upper bounds and compare these to complete the proof for the cases
(0,0.6]
and
[0.6,0.8]
, where the difference is in which part of the
\max
of the upper bound we use for comparison.
For the remaining region
[0.8,1.0]
, we can show that
\|{\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}_{k}}\|_{\infty}
is always too small compared to
\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{n}
.

We defer the full proof of
Theorem

3.3
to
Section

B.2
.

RPCD on General Quadratics

One limitation of our work is that the RPCD upper bounds in

.
Here we leave some noteworthy discussions considering ways to generalize to the

4.1
Conjecture on the RPCD Worst Case

Here we propose a conjecture that the RPCD upper bound we have shown for
{\bm{A}}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\sigma}
in
Theorem

3.3
could possibly extend to an upper bound for all positive definite quadratics.

Conjecture 4.1
.

For an initial point
{\bm{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
{\bm{x}}_{K}
be the output of
RPCD
after
K
epochs.
If
\sigma\in(0,1]
,
{\bm{A}}\in{\mathbb{S}}_{+}^{n}
with
\lambda_{\min}({\bm{A}})=\sigma
, and
{\bm{x}}_{0}\neq 0
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{K\to\infty}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\bm{x}}_{K}\|^{2}\right]}{\|{\bm{x}}_{0}\|^{2}}\right)^{\!\!\frac{1}{K}}\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{n}\!\!\!,\left(1-\frac{\sigma}{n}\right)^{2n}\right\}.

Algorithmic Search.

We use the following framework to search for the worst-case example among the larger space of all (unit-diagonal) quadratics.

Step 1.
Start with a (randomly initialized) lower triangular matrix
{\bm{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with nonzero diagonals.

Step 2.
Construct a unit-diagonal, positive semi-definite matrix by computing
{\bm{Y}}={\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}
and set unit diagonals by
{\bm{Z}}={\bm{D}}_{{\bm{Y}}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\bm{Y}}{\bm{D}}_{{\bm{Y}}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, where
{\bm{D}}_{{\bm{Y}}}
is the diagonal part of
{\bm{Y}}
.

Step 3.
Construct a matrix
{\bm{A}}
with
\sigma=\lambda_{\min}({\bm{A}})
by

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}=\frac{1-\sigma}{1-\mu}{\bm{Z}}+\frac{\sigma-\mu}{1-\mu}{\bm{I}},

where
\mu=\lambda_{\min}({\bm{Z}})
.
Note that
{\bm{A}}
is also unit-diagonal and positive semi-definite.

Step 4.
Construct an objective function that takes an input
{\bm{X}}
to compute the value of
\rho(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}})
and run a
scipy
optimizer to maximize the objective.

We explore dimensions
n=3,4,5,6
and
\sigma
values ranging from
0.1
to
0.9
with increments of
0.1
.
Note that
Algorithmic Search
finds the
\argmax
of
\rho(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}})
, which is an
upper bound
of the convergence rates. The results suggest that
{\bm{A}}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\sigma}
are the worst cases concerning this upper bound, as all of our trials of
Algorithmic Search
converged to a problem instance
{\bm{A}}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\sigma}
with various sign flips.
We also observed that for any
{\bm{A}}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\sigma}
, we always have
\rho(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}})=\rho(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}|_{{\mathcal{S}}})
(where
{\mathcal{S}}=\mathrm{span}\{{\bm{I}},{\bm{1}}{\bm{1}}^{\top}\}
), which we were able to show in
Theorem

3.3
that is smaller than the same upper bound.
These observations altogether motivated us to propose
4.1
on the worst-case instance for general quadratic functions.

Remark
.

Sun & Ye 2016
show that
{\bm{A}}=\sigma{\bm{I}}+(1-\sigma){\bm{1}}{\bm{1}}^{\top}
is the worst-case example of cyclic coordinate descent (CCD), which partially aligns with the idea of our conjecture that CD algorithms using one update per coordinate in each epoch (including both CCD and RPCD) are slow for similar types of matrices.

4.2
Experiments

To compare the performance of RCD and RPCD, we conducted experiments on four types of convex and smooth functions:

(i)
quadratic functions with permutation-invariant Hessians,

(ii)
general quadratic functions with positive definite Hessians,

(iii)
general quadratic functions with positive definite Hessians
plus
a scaled log-sum-exp (LSE) term, and

(iv)

\ell_{2}
-regularized logistic regression objective functions.
Specifically, we use the following form for
(i)
-
(iii)
:

f({\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{2}{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}+\alpha\cdot\text{LSE}({\bm{Q}}{\bm{x}}),

where
{\bm{A}}
is either
\sigma{\bm{I}}+(1-\sigma){\bm{1}}{\bm{1}}^{\top}
or randomly generated with unit diagonals and
\lambda_{\min}({\bm{A}})=\sigma
(following
Steps 1-3
of

),
{\bm{Q}}
is a randomly generated orthogonal matrix we use to avoid coordinate-friendly structures,
\text{LSE}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=\log(e^{a_{1}}+\dots+e^{a_{n}})

.

For
(iv)
, we use the
\ell_{2}
-regularized logistic regression objective:

\min_{x}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log(1+\exp(-b_{i}{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{x}}))+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|{\bm{x}}\|^{2},

and the labels are generated by sampling

, and flipping each

independently with probability 0.1. This setup follows
Nutini et al. 2015
.

Our theoretical contribution focuses on establishing the superiority of RPCD over RCD for the specific case of quadratic functions with permutation-invariant Hessians
(i)
.
As demonstrated in
Figure

(i)
in practice, but also in minimizing both

and
(iii)

Results for
(ii)

in the broader class of

.

and the RPCD upper bound from
Theorem

3.3
for
n=100
.

5
Conclusion

We have shown convergence lower bounds for RCD and convergence upper bounds for RPCD for positive definite quadratic functions with permutation-invariant structures.
We obtain results demonstrating that RPCD outperforms RCD for any problem instance in
{\bm{A}}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\sigma}
(
Theorems

3.1
and
3.3
), show a stronger RCD lower bound that induces a clear gap between RCD and RPCD (
Theorem

3.4
), and conjecture that RPCD will also outperform RCD for general quadratic functions (
4.1
).

An immediate future direction would be to prove (or disprove)
4.1
for general quadratic functions, and possibly further analyze the benefits of random permutations for even broader function/algorithm classes, such as
non-quadratic
(strongly) convex functions or
block
coordinate descent algorithms.

Impact Statement

This paper presents the convergence analyses of randomized coordinate descent algorithms.
We do not have any specific ethical/societal impact or consequences of our work, other than trivial implications related to the advancement of the field of Machine Learning.

For
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}}
, consider the matrix operator:

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RCD}}({\bm{X}})

\displaystyle={\bm{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}}({\bm{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bm{X}}{\bm{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}){\bm{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}},

a similar operator to
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}}
as it maps
{\bm{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\bm{X}}{\bm{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
to
{\bm{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}}({\bm{X}}){\bm{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
.
This can be rewritten in the form of

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RCD}}({\bm{X}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{{\bm{T}}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RCD}\top}{\bm{X}}\widetilde{{\bm{T}}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RCD}}\right],

where the expectation is over
symmetric
matrices

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\bm{T}}}_{{\bm{A}},i}^{\text{RCD}}:={\bm{I}}-\frac{1}{a_{ii}}{\bm{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bm{E}}_{i}{\bm{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Therefore the matrix form of
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RCD}}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{{\bm{T}}}_{{\bm{A}},i}^{\text{RCD}\top}\otimes\widetilde{{\bm{T}}}_{{\bm{A}},i}^{\text{RCD}\top}\right]

must also be symmetric, which implies that
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RCD}}
is diagonalizable.
Hence
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}}
must also be diagonalizable by similarity.

For
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}
, we also consider the matrix operator:

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}}({\bm{X}})

\displaystyle={\bm{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}({\bm{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bm{X}}{\bm{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}){\bm{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}},

which is also similar to
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}
and can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}}({\bm{X}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{{\bm{T}}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}\top}{\bm{X}}\widetilde{{\bm{T}}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}}\right],

where the expectation is over the matrices

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\bm{T}}}_{{\bm{A}},p}^{\text{RPCD}}:={\bm{I}}-{\bm{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bm{P}}\bm{\Gamma}_{{\bm{P}}}^{-1}{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

For each permutation
p
, we consider a complementary permutation
p^{\prime}
defined as:

\displaystyle p^{\prime}(i)=(n+1)-p(i),\ \ \forall i\in[n].

The permutation matrix
{\bm{P}}^{\prime}
generated by
p^{\prime}
satisfies
{\bm{P}}^{\prime}\bm{\Gamma}_{{\bm{P}}^{\prime}}{\bm{P}}^{\prime\top}=\left({\bm{P}}\bm{\Gamma}_{{\bm{P}}}{\bm{P}}^{\top}\right)^{\top}

(
Sun et al. 2020
)
.
This is because

\displaystyle{\bm{P}}\bm{\Gamma}_{{\bm{P}}}{\bm{P}}^{\top}

\displaystyle={\bm{P}}\mathrm{tril}({\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}{\bm{P}}){\bm{P}}^{\top}={\bm{A}}\odot{\bm{W}}_{{\bm{P}}},

where
{\bm{W}}_{{\bm{P}}}
is a binary matrix with

\displaystyle W_{{\bm{P}}ij}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&p(i)\geq p(j),\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Since
p(i)\geq p(j)
if and only if
p^{\prime}(i)\geq p^{\prime}(j)
, we have
{\bm{W}}_{{\bm{P}}^{\prime}}={\bm{W}}_{{\bm{P}}}^{\top}
and
{\bm{P}}\bm{\Gamma}_{{\bm{P}}^{\prime}}{\bm{P}}^{\prime\top}=\left({\bm{P}}\bm{\Gamma}_{{\bm{P}}}{\bm{P}}^{\top}\right)^{\top}
.

Therefore we have
\widetilde{{\bm{T}}}_{{\bm{A}},p^{\prime}}^{\text{RPCD}}=(\widetilde{{\bm{T}}}_{{\bm{A}},p}^{\text{RPCD}})^{\top}
, and the following sum:

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\bm{T}}}_{{\bm{A}},p}^{\text{RPCD}\top}\otimes\widetilde{{\bm{T}}}_{{\bm{A}},p}^{\text{RPCD}\top}+\widetilde{{\bm{T}}}_{{\bm{A}},p^{\prime}}^{\text{RPCD}\top}\otimes\widetilde{{\bm{T}}}_{{\bm{A}},p^{\prime}}^{\text{RPCD}\top}

is also symmetric as a sum of transposes.
As the set of all permutations can be paired with its unique complementary permutation, the matrix form of
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{{\bm{T}}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}\top}\otimes\widetilde{{\bm{T}}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}\top}\right]

must be symmetric, which implies that
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}
must also be diagonalizable.
∎

B.1
Proof of

Here we prove

Proof.

We will prove the following two inequalities:

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\bm{x}}_{T}\|^{2}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0}\right]}{\|{\bm{x}}_{0}\|^{2}}\right)^{1/T}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{1}{n},

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\bm{x}}_{T}\|^{2}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0}\right]}{\|{\bm{x}}_{0}\|^{2}}\right)^{1/T}

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{\sigma}{n}\right)^{2}.

To show the first inequality, we use the fact that
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}}({\bm{I}})={\bm{I}}-\frac{2{\bm{A}}}{n}+\frac{{\bm{A}}^{2}}{n}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{x}}_{k}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{k}\mid{\bm{x}}_{k-1}\right]

\displaystyle={\bm{x}}_{k-1}^{\top}\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}}({\bm{I}}){\bm{x}}_{k-1}

\displaystyle={\bm{x}}_{k-1}^{\top}\left({\bm{I}}-\frac{2{\bm{A}}}{n}+\frac{{\bm{A}}^{2}}{n}\right){\bm{x}}_{k-1}

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{\min}\left({\bm{I}}-\frac{2{\bm{A}}}{n}+\frac{{\bm{A}}^{2}}{n}\right){\bm{x}}_{k-1}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{k-1}.

Since the eigenvalues of
{\bm{I}}-\frac{2{\bm{A}}}{n}+\frac{{\bm{A}}^{2}}{n}
are in the form of
1-\frac{2\lambda}{n}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{n}
, where
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
{\bm{A}}
, and
1-\frac{2x}{n}+\frac{x^{2}}{n}\geq 1-\frac{1}{n}
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{x}}_{k}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{k}\mid{\bm{x}}_{k-1}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right){\bm{x}}_{k-1}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{k-1}.

By the law of total expectation,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{x}}_{T}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{T}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{x}}_{T}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{T}\mid{\bm{x}}_{T-1}\right]\mid{\bm{x}}_{0}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{x}}_{T-1}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{T-1}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\vdots

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{T}\|{\bm{x}}_{0}\|^{2}.

For the second part, we use the following lemma.

Lemma B.1

(
Nesterov 2012
, Lemma 1)
.

If
{\bm{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a positive semi-definite matrix,

\displaystyle{\bm{X}}\preceq n\cdot\diag({\bm{X}}).

Now we define the following matrix operator:

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\text{RCD}}_{{\bm{A}}}({\bm{X}}):=\left({\bm{I}}-\frac{{\bm{A}}}{n}\right)^{\top}{\bm{X}}\left({\bm{I}}-\frac{{\bm{A}}}{n}\right).

According to
Lemma

B.1
,
\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\text{RCD}}_{{\bm{A}}}({\bm{X}})\preceq\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}}({\bm{X}})
for any
{\bm{X}}\succeq 0
because

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}}({\bm{X}})

\displaystyle=\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\text{RCD}}_{{\bm{A}}}({\bm{X}})+\frac{{\bm{A}}^{\top}(n\cdot\diag({\bm{X}})-{\bm{X}}){\bm{A}}}{n^{2}}.

Moreover, if
{\bm{X}}\preceq{\bm{Y}}
, then
\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\text{RCD}}_{{\bm{A}}}({\bm{X}})\preceq\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\text{RCD}}_{{\bm{A}}}({\bm{Y}})
.

We now aim to show that for any positive integer
k
and
{\bm{X}}\succeq 0
,

\displaystyle(\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\text{RCD}}_{{\bm{A}}})^{k}({\bm{X}})\preceq(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}})^{k}({\bm{X}}).

(15)

We prove this by induction on
k
.

Base Case:
For
k=1
, we have already shown that
\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\text{RCD}}_{{\bm{A}}}({\bm{X}})\preceq\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}}({\bm{X}})
.

Inductive Step:
Assume that for some positive integer
k
, we have

\displaystyle(\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\text{RCD}}_{{\bm{A}}})^{k}({\bm{X}})

\displaystyle\preceq(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}})^{k}({\bm{X}}).

We need to show that
(\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\text{RCD}}_{{\bm{A}}})^{k+1}({\bm{X}})\preceq(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}})^{k+1}({\bm{X}})
. Since

\displaystyle(\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\text{RCD}}_{{\bm{A}}})^{k+1}({\bm{X}})

\displaystyle=\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\text{RCD}}_{{\bm{A}}}((\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\text{RCD}}_{{\bm{A}}})^{k}({\bm{X}}))

\displaystyle\preceq\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\text{RCD}}_{{\bm{A}}}((\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}})^{k}({\bm{X}}))

\displaystyle(\because(\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\text{RCD}}_{{\bm{A}}})^{k}({\bm{X}})\preceq(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}})^{k}({\bm{X}}))

\displaystyle\preceq\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}}((\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}})^{k}({\bm{X}}))

\displaystyle(\because\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\text{RCD}}_{{\bm{A}}}({\bm{X}})\preceq\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}}({\bm{X}}),(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}})^{k}({\bm{X}}))\succeq 0)

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}})^{k+1}({\bm{X}}),

we have
(\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\text{RCD}}_{{\bm{A}}})^{k+1}({\bm{X}})\preceq(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}})^{k+1}({\bm{X}})
and
Equation

15
holds for any positive integer
k
.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{x}}_{T}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{T}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0}\right]

\displaystyle={\bm{x}}_{0}^{\top}(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}})^{T}({\bm{I}}){\bm{x}}_{0}

\displaystyle\geq{\bm{x}}_{0}^{\top}(\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\text{RCD}}_{{\bm{A}}})^{T}({\bm{I}}){\bm{x}}_{0}

\displaystyle={\bm{x}}_{0}^{\top}\left({\bm{I}}-\frac{{\bm{A}}}{n}\right)^{2T}{\bm{x}}_{0}.

Let
{\bm{v}}_{1},{\bm{v}}_{2},\dots,{\bm{v}}_{n}
be the eigenvectors of
{\bm{A}}
corresponding to the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{n}
, respectively. Since
{\bm{A}}\succ 0
, the eigenvalues are positive and the eigenvectors form an orthonormal basis for
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Therefore, we can express
{\bm{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as a linear combination,
{\bm{x}}_{0}=c_{1}{\bm{v}}_{1}+c_{2}{\bm{v}}_{2}+\dots+c_{n}{\bm{v}}_{n}
, where
c_{i}
are scalar coefficients.

Excluding a measure zero set of vectors
{\bm{x}}
such that
{\bm{v}}_{1}^{\top}{\bm{x}}=0
, we may assume that
c_{1}\neq 0
.

Now, let us define

\displaystyle d_{i}=\frac{c_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}^{2}},\quad\mu_{i}=1-\frac{\lambda_{i}}{n}.

Then,
d_{1}\neq 0
,
d_{i}\geq 0
for

,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}=1
and
\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}\geq\dots\geq\mu_{n}>0
. We can express
\frac{{\bm{x}}_{0}^{\top}\left({\bm{I}}-\frac{{\bm{A}}}{n}\right)^{2T}{\bm{x}}_{0}}{{\bm{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{0}}
in terms of
c_{i}
and
\mu_{i}
:

\displaystyle\frac{{\bm{x}}_{0}^{\top}\left({\bm{I}}-\frac{{\bm{A}}}{n}\right)^{2T}{\bm{x}}_{0}}{{\bm{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{0}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\mu_{i}^{2T}.

\displaystyle\left(\frac{{\bm{x}}_{0}^{\top}\left({\bm{I}}-\frac{{\bm{A}}}{n}\right)^{2T}{\bm{x}}_{0}}{{\bm{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{0}}\right)^{1/T}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\mu_{i}^{2T}\right)^{1/T}

\displaystyle=\mu_{1}^{2}\left(d_{1}+d_{2}\left(\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{1}}\right)^{2T}+\dots+d_{n}\left(\frac{\mu_{n}}{\mu_{1}}\right)^{2T}\right)^{1/T}.

Since

\displaystyle d_{1}^{1/T}\leq\left(d_{1}+d_{2}\left(\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{1}}\right)^{2T}+\dots+d_{n}\left(\frac{\mu_{n}}{\mu_{1}}\right)^{2T}\right)^{1/T}\leq\left(d_{1}+\dots+d_{n}\right)^{1/T}=1.

Thus,

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\left(d_{1}+d_{2}\left(\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{1}}\right)^{2T}+\dots+d_{n}\left(\frac{\mu_{n}}{\mu_{1}}\right)^{2T}\right)^{1/T}=1

because
d_{1}>0
.
Therefore we can conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{x}}_{T}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{T}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0}\right]}{{\bm{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{0}}\right)^{1/T}

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{1}^{2}=\left(1-\frac{\sigma}{n}\right)^{2}

which finishes the proof.
∎

Proof.

We first assume that
{\bm{A}}=\sigma{\bm{I}}_{n}+(1-\sigma){\bm{1}}_{n}{\bm{1}}_{n}^{\top}

\lambda_{\min}({\bm{A}})=\sigma

\lambda_{\max}({\bm{A}})=n-(n-1)\sigma

Let
\bm{\Gamma}=\mathrm{tril}({\bm{A}})
and
{\bm{C}}={\bm{I}}-\bm{\Gamma}^{-1}{\bm{A}}

Since both
{\bm{I}}
and
{\bm{1}}{\bm{1}}^{\top}
are permutation-invariant, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}({\bm{I}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{P}}{\bm{C}}^{\top}{\bm{C}}{\bm{P}}^{\top}],

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}({\bm{1}}{\bm{1}}^{\top})

We use the following lemma to compute

Lemma B.2

(
Lee & Wright 2019
, Lemma 3.1)
.

Given any matrix
{\bm{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and permutation matrix
{\bm{P}}
selected uniformly at random from the set of all permutations
\Pi
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{P}}}[{\bm{P}}{\bm{Q}}{\bm{P}}^{\top}]=\tau_{1}{\bm{I}}+\tau_{2}{\bm{1}}{\bm{1}}^{\top}
, where

\tau_{2}=\frac{{\bm{1}}^{\top}{\bm{Q}}{\bm{1}}-\tr{\bm{Q}}}{n(n-1)},\quad\tau_{1}=\frac{\tr{\bm{Q}}}{n}-\tau_{2}.

As a consequence of this lemma,
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}({\bm{I}})
and
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}({\bm{1}}{\bm{1}}^{\top})
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\tau_{1}^{(1)}{\bm{I}}+\tau_{2}^{(1)}{\bm{1}}{\bm{1}}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}({\bm{I}}),

\displaystyle\tau_{1}^{(2)}{\bm{I}}+\tau_{2}^{(2)}{\bm{1}}{\bm{1}}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}({\bm{1}}{\bm{1}}^{\top}),

where
\tau_{1}^{(1)},\tau_{2}^{(1)},\tau_{1}^{(2)}
and
\tau_{2}^{(2)}
can be computed using
{\bm{1}}^{\top}{\bm{C}}^{\top}{\bm{C}}{\bm{1}}

\tr({\bm{C}}^{\top}{\bm{C}})

{\bm{1}}^{\top}{\bm{C}}^{\top}{\bm{1}}{\bm{1}}^{\top}{\bm{C}}{\bm{1}}

\displaystyle(\bm{\Gamma}^{-1})_{ij}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }i<j\\
1&\text{ if }i=j\\
-(1-\sigma)\sigma^{i-j-1}&\text{ if }i>j.\end{cases}

Consequently, we have

\displaystyle{\bm{C}}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-(1-\sigma)\sigma^{i-1}&\text{ if }i<j\\
(1-\sigma)(\sigma^{i-j}-\sigma^{i-1})&\text{ if }i\geq j.\end{cases}

(16)

To proceed, we introduce
{\bm{v}}={\bm{C}}{\bm{1}},{\bm{w}}={\bm{C}}^{\top}{\bm{1}}
and
L=\lambda_{\max}({\bm{A}})=n-(n-1)\sigma

Then, by
Equation

16

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}={\bm{1}}-L\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\sigma\\
\vdots\\
\sigma^{n-1}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\bm{w}}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\sigma^{n}-\sigma^{n-1}\\
\vdots\\
\sigma^{n}-\sigma\end{bmatrix}.

With the explicit form of the entries of
{\bm{C}}
, we can now calculate the four quantities
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
, and
\delta

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \alpha&={\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{v}}=n-2L\frac{1-\sigma^{n}}{1-\sigma}+L^{2}\frac{1-\sigma^{2n}}{1-\sigma^{2}}\\
\beta&=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}{\bm{C}}_{ij}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left((1-\sigma)^{2}\frac{1-\sigma^{2n}}{1-\sigma^{2}}+(1-\sigma)^{2}(1-2\sigma^{j-1})\frac{1-\sigma^{2(n-j+1)}}{1-\sigma^{2}}\right)\\

(17)

Note that all of these values are polynomials in
\sigma
.
(If
\sigma=1

The explicit form of
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
, and
\delta

\tau_{1}^{(1)},\tau_{1}^{(2)},\tau_{2}^{(1)}

\tau_{1}^{(1)},\tau_{1}^{(2)}\geq 0

{\bm{T}}_{{\bm{A}},p}^{\text{RPCD}\top}{\bm{X}}{\bm{T}}_{{\bm{A}},p}^{\text{RPCD}}\succeq 0

\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}({\bm{X}})\succeq 0

{\bm{X}}\succeq 0
.
As both
{\bm{I}}\succeq 0

{\bm{1}}{\bm{1}}^{\top}\succeq 0

\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}({\bm{I}})\succeq 0

\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}({\bm{1}}{\bm{1}}^{\top})\succeq 0

\tau_{1}^{(1)}
and
\tau_{1}^{(2)}
are eigenvalues of
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}({\bm{I}})\succeq 0
and
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}({\bm{1}}{\bm{1}}^{\top})\succeq 0
respectively, we have
\tau_{1}^{(1)},\tau_{1}^{(2)}\geq 0
.

Next, we show that
\tau_{2}^{(2)}\geq 0

{\bm{w}}={\bm{C}}^{\top}{\bm{1}}
, we have

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle={\bm{1}}^{\top}{\bm{w}}{\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{1}}=({\bm{1}}^{\top}{\bm{w}})^{2}

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=\tr({\bm{w}}{\bm{w}}^{\top})=\tr({\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{w}})=\|{\bm{w}}\|^{2}.

Since
\tau_{2}^{(2)}=\gamma-\delta

\displaystyle\tau_{2}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=({\bm{1}}^{\top}{\bm{w}})^{2}-\|{\bm{w}}\|^{2}

We have

\displaystyle({\bm{C}}_{j}-{\bm{C}}_{j+1})_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\quad\text{if }k<j,\\
1-\sigma&\quad\text{if }k=j,\\
(1-\sigma)(\sigma^{k-j}-\sigma^{k-j-1})&\quad\text{if }k>j.\end{cases}

(18)

\displaystyle=(1-\sigma)^{2}(\sigma^{j-i}-\sigma^{j-1})+\sum_{k=j+1}^{n}(1-\sigma)^{2}(\sigma^{k-i}-\sigma^{k-1})(\sigma^{k-j}-\sigma^{k-j-1})

\displaystyle=(1-\sigma)^{2}(\sigma^{-i}-\sigma^{-1})\left(\sigma^{j}+(\sigma^{-j}-\sigma^{-j-1})\sum_{k=j+1}^{n}\sigma^{2k}\right).

Since

and

, we have
(1-\sigma)^{2}\geq 0
and
(\sigma^{-i}-\sigma^{-1})\geq 0
.
Thus, it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\geq 0.

Since

\displaystyle=\sigma^{j}+(\sigma^{-j}-\sigma^{-j-1})\sigma^{2j+2}\cdot\frac{1-\sigma^{2(n-j)}}{1-\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{j}\left(1-\frac{\sigma}{1+\sigma}(1-\sigma^{2(n-j)})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{j}}{1+\sigma}(1+\sigma^{2n-2j+1}),

we have

For the second inequality, we will show that

. Using

\displaystyle({\bm{C}}_{i-1}-{\bm{C}}_{i})^{\top}{\bm{C}}_{j}

\displaystyle=(1-\sigma)^{2}\left(-\sigma^{i-2}+(\sigma^{-i-1}-\sigma^{-i})\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\sigma^{2k}+(\sigma^{-i+1}-\sigma^{-i})(\sigma^{-j}-\sigma^{-1})\sum_{k=j}^{n}\sigma^{2k}\right).

\displaystyle(\sigma^{-i+1}-\sigma^{-i})(\sigma^{-j}-\sigma^{-1})\sum_{k=j}^{n}\sigma^{2k}

\displaystyle\leq 0,

it is sufficient to show that

\displaystyle-\sigma^{i-2}+(\sigma^{-i-1}-\sigma^{-i})\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\sigma^{2k}\leq 0.

Since

\displaystyle-\sigma^{i-2}+(\sigma^{-i-1}-\sigma^{-i})\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\sigma^{2k}

\displaystyle=-\sigma^{i-2}+(\sigma^{-i-1}-\sigma^{-i})\sigma^{2i}\cdot\frac{1-\sigma^{2(j-i)}}{1-\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq-\sigma^{i-2}+\frac{\sigma^{i-1}-\sigma^{i}}{1-\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sigma^{i-2}}{\sigma+1}

\displaystyle\leq 0,

it follows that

.

Using a chain of the inequalities we showed, we can obtain

\displaystyle=(1-\sigma)^{2}\left(1-(\sigma^{-3}-\sigma^{-2})\sum_{k=2}^{n-1}\sigma^{2k}+(1-\sigma^{n-1})(\sigma^{n-2}-\sigma^{n-1})\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-(\sigma^{-3}-\sigma^{-2})\sum_{k=2}^{n-1}\sigma^{2k}

\displaystyle\geq 1-(\sigma^{-3}-\sigma^{-2})\frac{\sigma^{4}}{1-\sigma^{2}}

are non-negative.
Therefore we can conclude that

\displaystyle\tau_{2}^{(1)}

\displaystyle={\bm{1}}^{\top}{\bm{C}}^{\top}{\bm{C}}{\bm{1}}-\tr({\bm{C}}^{\top}{\bm{C}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\neq j}({\bm{C}}^{\top}{\bm{C}})_{ij}

\displaystyle\geq 0,

which completes the proof.

Proposition B.6

(
Jacobson 1985
, Sturm’s Theorem)
.

Let
f(x)
be any polynomial with coefficients in
\mathbb{R}
of positive degree. We define the
standard sequence
for
f(x)
by:

\displaystyle f_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=f(x),\quad f_{1}(x)=f^{\prime}(x)\quad\text{(formal derivative of }f(x))

\displaystyle f_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=q_{1}(x)f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(x),\quad\deg f_{2}<\deg f_{1}

\displaystyle\qquad\vdots

\displaystyle f_{i-1}(x)

\displaystyle=q_{i}(x)f_{i}(x)-f_{i+1}(x),\quad\deg f_{i+1}<\deg f_{i}

\displaystyle\qquad\vdots

\displaystyle f_{s}(x)

\displaystyle=q_{s}(x)f_{s}(x)\quad(\text{that is, }f_{s+1}(x)=0).

Given a sequence
c=(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{m})
of elements of
\mathbb{R}
, we define the
number of variations in sign
of

to be the number of variations in sign of the subsequence

obtained by dropping the
0
’s in
c
.

Let

be a polynomial of positive degree with coefficients in a real closed field
\mathbb{R}
and let

be the standard sequence for

. Assume

is an interval such that

,
f(b)\neq 0
. Then the number of distinct roots of

in

is

, where

denotes the number of variations in sign of
\{f_{0}(c),f_{1}(c),\dots,f_{s}(c)\}
.

To apply

,
[a,b)
, or
[a,b]
. That is, since

on the interval

,
[a,b)
, or

, as required.

\displaystyle=\frac{2n-r}{n(r+1)}

\displaystyle\geq\binom{2n}{r+1}\frac{1}{n^{r+1}}.

-block of
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}}({\bm{X}})
is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\bm{X}}_{22}+\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}({\bm{I}}_{n-k}-{\bm{E}}_{i-k,n-k})^{\top}{\bm{X}}_{22}({\bm{I}}_{n-k}-{\bm{E}}_{i-k,n-k})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(k{\bm{X}}_{22}+(n-k-2){\bm{X}}_{22}+{\bm{D}}_{22}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{2}{n}\right){\bm{X}}_{22}+\frac{1}{n}{\bm{D}}_{22}

because
\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}{\bm{E}}_{i-k,n-k}={\bm{I}}_{n-k}
and
\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}{\bm{E}}_{i-k,n-k}^{\top}{\bm{X}}_{22}{\bm{E}}_{i-k,n-k}={\bm{D}}_{22}
.

From the above, we have

\displaystyle(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}})^{T}({\bm{I}}_{n})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}({\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}_{k}}^{\text{RCD}})^{T}({\bm{I}}_{k})&{\bm{0}}_{k,n-k}\\
{\bm{0}}_{n-k,k}&\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{T}{\bm{I}}_{n-k}\end{bmatrix}.

Now, we define
\alpha_{T}
and
\beta_{T}
as

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{T}\\
\beta_{T}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle={\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{T}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

We now proceed to examine the limit

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\left(\frac{{\bm{x}}_{0}^{\top}(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}})^{T}({\bm{I}}){\bm{x}}_{0}}{{\bm{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{0}}\right)^{1/T}.

To this end, let
\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{y}}_{0}&{\bm{z}}_{0}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}={\bm{x}}_{0}
, where
{\bm{y}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
{\bm{z}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-k}
. Since the set
\{{\bm{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:{\bm{y}}_{0}={\bm{0}}_{k}\}
has measure zero in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we assume that
{\bm{y}}_{0}\neq 0
. Then,

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{0}^{\top}(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}})^{T}({\bm{I}}){\bm{x}}_{0}

\displaystyle={\bm{y}}_{0}^{\top}(\alpha_{T}{\bm{I}}+\beta_{T}{\bm{1}}{\bm{1}}^{\top}){\bm{y}}_{0}+\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{T}\|{\bm{z}}_{0}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\alpha_{T}\|{\bm{y}}_{0}\|^{2}+\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{T}\|{\bm{z}}_{0}\|^{2}.

\displaystyle(\because\beta_{T}\geq 0)

By the same argument in the case when
k=n
, we can show that

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\alpha_{T}^{1/T}

\displaystyle=\rho\left(\frac{k}{n}{\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}_{k}}+\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right){\bm{I}}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{k}{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}+\frac{(1-\sigma)^{2}}{k}\right)+1-\frac{k}{n}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{n}+\frac{(1-\sigma)^{2}}{n}.

Since
\sigma\neq 1
by the assumption, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{T}}{\alpha_{T}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to\infty}\left(\frac{\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)}{\alpha_{T}^{1/T}}\right)^{T}

\displaystyle=0

and

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\left(\frac{{\bm{x}}_{0}^{\top}(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}})^{T}({\bm{I}}){\bm{x}}_{0}}{{\bm{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{0}}\right)^{1/T}

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to\infty}\left(\frac{\alpha_{T}\|{\bm{y}}_{0}\|^{2}+\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{T}\|{\bm{z}}_{0}\|^{2}}{\|{\bm{y}}_{0}\|^{2}+\|{\bm{z}}_{0}\|^{2}}\right)^{1/T}

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to\infty}\alpha_{T}^{1/T}\left(\frac{\|{\bm{y}}_{0}\|^{2}}{\|{\bm{y}}_{0}\|^{2}+\|{\bm{z}}_{0}\|^{2}}+\frac{\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{T}}{\alpha_{T}}\left(\frac{\|{\bm{z}}_{0}\|^{2}}{\|{\bm{y}}_{0}\|^{2}+\|{\bm{z}}_{0}\|^{2}}\right)\right)^{1/T}

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to\infty}\alpha_{T}^{1/T}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{1}{n}+\frac{(1-\sigma)^{2}}{n}.

∎

Appendix D
Comparison with Previous Work

In this section, we provide a detailed comparison of previous work by
Lee & Wright 2019
, continuing from the discussion at the end of
Section

3
.
Recall that
Lee & Wright 2019
established that, for quadratic functions with permutation-invariant Hessians, the epoch-wise contraction ratio of RPCD is of order

\displaystyle 1-2\sigma-\frac{2\sigma}{n}+2\sigma^{2}+{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\right)+{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sigma^{3}\right)

(36)

when
n\geq 10
and
\sigma\in(0,0.4]
. In comparison,
Theorem

3.3
provides an upper bound of
\max\left\{\left(1-\frac{\sigma}{n}\right)^{2n},\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{n}\right\}
, which simplifies to
\left(1-\frac{\sigma}{n}\right)^{2n}
in the region
\sigma\in(0,0.4]
.

As noted earlier, we obtain

\displaystyle\left({1-\frac{\sigma}{n}}\right)^{2n}=1-2\sigma-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}+2\sigma^{2}+{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sigma^{3}\right).

This expansion initially appears to have extra terms compared to
Equation

36
. However, a closer examination of our proof methodology reveals a more refined upper bound.

We exploit the inequality
\rho({\bm{M}})\leq\|{\bm{M}}\|_{\infty}
, where
{\bm{M}}
is the iteration matrix. In our analysis, we define the iteration matrix as

\displaystyle{\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\begin{bmatrix}n\beta-\alpha&n\delta-\gamma\\
\alpha-\beta&\gamma-\delta\end{bmatrix},

where
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
, and
\delta
are polynomials in
\sigma
(see (
17
) in
Appendix

B
for their explicit definitions).

Lemma

B.3
establishes that all entries of
{\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}
are non-negative. Consequently,
\|{\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}\|_{\infty}
simplifies to the maximum of the sums of the first and second rows of
{\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}
, both of which are polynomials in
\sigma
. We define
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
as the sum of the first and second rows of
\begin{bmatrix}n\beta-\alpha&n\delta-\gamma\\
\alpha-\beta&\gamma-\delta\end{bmatrix}
, respectively.

Importantly, our proof explicitly determines the coefficients of the polynomials
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
in terms of
n
. Furthermore, (
21
) and (
23
) establish that the even-degree Taylor approximations of
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
serve as upper bounds.
Specifically, if we consider the
4
-th order Taylor approximations of
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{T_{1}}{n(n-1)}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\left(2+\frac{1}{n}\right)\sigma+\frac{2n}{n-1}\sigma^{2}-2\sigma^{3}+\frac{2(n^{2}-n-1)}{n(n-1)}\sigma^{4}

\displaystyle\frac{T_{2}}{n(n-1)}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\frac{2}{n}-2\left(1-\frac{2}{n}\right)\sigma+\frac{2(n^{2}-3n+1)}{n(n-1)}\sigma^{2}-2\left(1-\frac{2}{n}\right)\sigma^{3}+\frac{2(n^{2}-3n+3)}{n(n-1)}\sigma^{4}.

Remarkably, the upper bound for

precisely matches the contraction ratio given in
Equation

36
.
While the upper bound for
\frac{T_{2}}{n(n-1)}
does not directly match this expression, we have

\displaystyle\frac{T_{2}}{n(n-1)}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\frac{2}{n}-2\sigma+\frac{4}{n}\sigma+2\sigma^{2}-\frac{4}{n}\sigma^{2}+{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sigma^{3}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 1-2\sigma-\frac{2}{n}\sigma+2\sigma^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}+{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sigma^{3}\right),

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
2-6\sigma+5\sigma^{2}\geq 0
.

Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle\rho({\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}})

\displaystyle\leq\|{\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\max_{i=1,2}\frac{T_{i}}{n(n-1)}

\displaystyle=1-2\sigma-\frac{2\sigma}{n}+2\sigma^{2}+{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\right)+{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sigma^{3}\right).

E.2
General Hessians: Matrix AM-GM Inequality

Continuing from our discussion in the second paragraph of
Section

4.3
, the only results we are aware of that consider RPCD for general quadratics are those by
Sun et al. 2020
.
In particular, their objective was to show that

\displaystyle\rho(\mathbb{E}[{\bm{T}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}}])\leq 1-\frac{\sigma}{n}.

This is a direct corollary of a
weak
version of the well-known matrix AM-GM inequality
(
Recht & Re 2012
;
Lai & Lim 2020
;
De Sa 2020
;
Yun et al. 2021
)
.
To elaborate,
Sun et al. 2020
(Claim 4.1) observe that:

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bm{T}}_{{\bm{A}},p}^{\text{RPCD}}{\bm{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle={\bm{I}}-{\bm{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bm{P}}\bm{\Gamma}_{{\bm{P}}}^{-1}{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}={\bm{Z}}_{p(n)}\cdots{\bm{Z}}_{p(1)},

(40)

where
{\bm{Z}}_{i}={\bm{I}}-{\bm{v}}_{i}{\bm{v}}_{i}^{\top}
is a projection matrix, with
{\bm{v}}_{i}
being the
i
-th column of
{\bm{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
Then they show for the expectation:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{p}{\bm{Z}}_{p(n)}\cdots{\bm{Z}}_{p(1)}

\displaystyle\preceq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bm{Z}}_{i}\ \ \left(={\bm{I}}-\frac{{\bm{A}}}{n}\right),

which is called
weak
because the RHS is missing the
n
-th exponent from the original matrix AM-GM,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{p}{\bm{Z}}_{p(n)}\cdots{\bm{Z}}_{p(1)}

\displaystyle\preceq\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bm{Z}}_{i}\right)^{n}.

To obtain a proper comparison with the RCD lower bound results in
Theorem

3.1
, it suffices to show an upper bound of

\displaystyle\rho(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}})

\displaystyle=\rho(\mathbb{E}[{\bm{T}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}\top}\otimes{\bm{T}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}\top}])\leq\max\left\{\left(1-\frac{\sigma}{n}\right)^{2n},\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{n}\right\},

which is not only stronger than the weak AM-GM, considering that we have an order
n
exponent added to the upper bound, but also much more difficult in many aspects.

•

Analyzing the
upper bounds
of quantities derived from a
sum of Kronecker power
of matrices is presumably the harder direction than the opposite (we typically use the sum of squares
as
an upper bound).

•

Even for the case of two matrices
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
, the relationship between the spectrum of
{\bm{A}}+{\bm{B}}
and each of the matrices
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
(beyond Weyl’s inequality
(
Weyl 1912
)
) is highly nontrivial
(
Knutson & Tao 2000
)
.
In our case, we have a sum of
n!
matrices, and a fine spectral analysis is extremely hard.

One of the few possible solutions to avoid computing the Kronecker powers could be to use the following lemma.

Lemma E.2
.

We have

\displaystyle\rho({\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\emph{\text{RPCD}}})

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert{\bm{A}}^{-1/2}{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\emph{\text{RPCD}}}({\bm{A}}){\bm{A}}^{-1/2}\right\rVert.

(41)

Proof.

The Russo-Dye theorem (
Russo & Dye 1966
, Theorem 2.3.7 of
Bhatia 2007
) states that if
{\mathcal{M}}
is a positive linear map,
5
5

5

A linear map
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
is
positive
if it maps positive semi-definite matrices into positive semi-definite matrices.
then we have
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|{\mathcal{M}}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}=\lVert{\mathcal{M}}({\bm{I}})\rVert
, where
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}
is the operator norm induced by the matrix operator norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert
.
For our case, we can easily check that
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}
is positive as it outputs the expectation of positive semi-definite matrices.
Moreover, we have defined a similar matrix operator earlier in
Section

2
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}}({\bm{X}})

\displaystyle={\bm{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}}({\bm{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bm{X}}{\bm{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}){\bm{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}},

which is also a positive linear map (with a symmetric matrix form).
Using the Russo-Dye theorem on
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}}
, we have

\displaystyle\rho({\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}})

\displaystyle=\rho(\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}})\leq{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}=\left\lVert\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}}({\bm{I}})\right\rVert=\left\lVert{\bm{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}}({\bm{A}}){\bm{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\rVert

which completes the proof.
∎

One caveat of the approach is that the RHS of (

\sigma\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
after

gets sufficiently large, i.e., (

Figure

The same bound still seems tight enough for the region
\sigma\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
, where we also have
(1-\frac{\sigma}{n})^{2n}\geq(1-\frac{1}{n})^{n}
and hence the upper bound simply reduces to
(1-\frac{\sigma}{n})^{2n}
.

From (
40

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathcal{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}}({\bm{A}}){\bm{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[({\bm{I}}-{\bm{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bm{P}}\bm{\Gamma}_{{\bm{P}}}^{-1}{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}})^{\top}({\bm{I}}-{\bm{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bm{P}}\bm{\Gamma}_{{\bm{P}}}^{-1}{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{p}({\bm{Z}}_{p(n)}\cdots{\bm{Z}}_{p(1)})^{\top}({\bm{Z}}_{p(n)}\cdots{\bm{Z}}_{p(1)}).

Then by (

\displaystyle\rho\left(\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{p}({\bm{Z}}_{p(n)}\cdots{\bm{Z}}_{p(1)})^{\top}({\bm{Z}}_{p(n)}\cdots{\bm{Z}}_{p(1)})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\rho\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bm{Z}}_{i}\right)^{2n}

to prove

Appendix F
Details on Experiments

F.1
Algorithmic Search.

Here we provide detailed information about the algorithmic search used to find the worst-case instances for RPCD.
Recall that we use the following framework to search for the worst-case example among the space of all (unit-diagonal) quadratics.

Step 1.
Start with a (randomly initialized) lower triangular matrix
{\bm{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with nonzero diagonals.

Step 2.
Construct a unit-diagonal, positive semi-definite matrix by computing
{\bm{Y}}={\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}
and set unit diagonals by
{\bm{Z}}={\bm{D}}_{{\bm{Y}}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\bm{Y}}{\bm{D}}_{{\bm{Y}}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, where
{\bm{D}}_{{\bm{Y}}}
is the diagonal part of
{\bm{Y}}
.

Step 3.
Construct a matrix
{\bm{A}}
with
\sigma=\lambda_{\min}({\bm{A}})
by

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}=\frac{1-\sigma}{1-\mu}{\bm{Z}}+\frac{\sigma-\mu}{1-\mu}{\bm{I}},

where
\mu=\lambda_{\min}({\bm{Z}})
.
Note that
{\bm{A}}
is also unit-diagonal and positive semi-definite.

Step 4.
Construct an objective function that takes an input
{\bm{X}}
to compute the value of
\rho(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}})
and run a
scipy
optimizer to maximize the objective.

Experiment Settings.

In our search for worst-case matrices, we explored the performance of our algorithm for dimensions
n\in\{3,4,5,6\}
and for minimum eigenvalue
\sigma\in\{0.1,\dots,0.9\}
(with increments of
0.1
).

Initialization.
To initialize the lower-triangular matrix
{\bm{X}}
, we generated a vector of length
\frac{n(n+1)}{2}
with elements drawn from a standard normal distribution and then used this vector to create an
n\times n
lower-triangular matrix. We used 10 random initial vectors for
n=3,4
and 2 vectors for
n=5,6
. The reduced number of initializations for
n=5
and
n=6
was due to the computational complexity of calculating the matrix representation of
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}
and its spectral radius, which is
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{4}\cdot n!)
.

Construction of
{\bm{A}}
.

We computed
{\bm{A}}=\frac{1-\sigma}{1-\mu}{\bm{Z}}+\frac{\sigma-\mu}{1-\mu}{\bm{I}}
, where
\mu=\lambda_{\min}({\bm{Z}})

This construction ensures that
{\bm{A}}

F.2
RCD vs RPCD.

Problem Settings.

We considered four types of convex and smooth optimization problems in
n
-dimensional space. The first three share a common structure of the form

f({\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{2}{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}+\alpha\cdot\text{LSE}({\bm{Q}}{\bm{x}}),

where LSE is the log-sum-exp function,
i.e.
,
\text{LSE}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=\log(e^{a_{1}}+\dots+e^{a_{n}})
. These problems differ in the specific construction of
{\bm{A}}
and the presence of the LSE term:

•

(i)
Quadratic functions with permutation-invariant Hessians:
{\bm{A}}=\sigma{\bm{I}}_{n}+(1-\sigma){\bm{1}}_{n}{\bm{1}}_{n}^{\top}
with
\sigma\in(0,1]
and
\alpha=0
.

•

(ii)
General quadratic functions with positive definite Hessians:
{\bm{A}}
was randomly generated with unit diagonals and
\lambda_{\min}({\bm{A}})=\sigma\in(0,1]
, and
\alpha=0
.

•

(iii)
General quadratic functions with positive definite Hessians and a scaled LSE term: we used the same
{\bm{A}}
as in
(ii)
with an additional term
\alpha\cdot\text{LSE}({\bm{Q}}{\bm{x}})
, where
{\bm{Q}}
is a randomly generated orthogonal matrix.

We further consider a logistic regression problem with ridge regularization.

•

(iv)

\ell_{2}
-regularized logistic regression: the objective is

\min_{x}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log(1+\exp(-b_{i}{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{x}}))+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|{\bm{x}}\|^{2},

where each
{\bm{a}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is drawn from
\mathcal{N}(0,{\bm{I}}_{n})
. We sample a ground-truth vector
{\bm{x}}_{\text{true}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,{\bm{I}}_{n})
, set
b_{i}=\text{sign}({\bm{a}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{\text{true}})
, and flip each
b_{i}
independently with probability
0.1
. This setup follows
Nutini et al. 2015
.

Matrix Generation.

For
(ii)
and
(iii)
, the matrix
{\bm{A}}
was constructed by first generating an
n\times n
random matrix
{\bm{X}}
with entries drawn from the standard normal distribution, followed by
Steps 2-3
of
Algorithmic Search
detailed in
Section

F.1
. This procedure ensures that the generated
{\bm{A}}
is unit-diagonal and has a minimum eigenvalue
\sigma
. The orthogonal matrix
{\bm{Q}}
in the LSE term was generated by applying QR decomposition to a random matrix with entries drawn from the standard normal distribution.

Initialization.

For each parameter setting in problems
(i)
-
(iv)
(i.e., each combination of
n
,
\sigma
,
\alpha
, or
\lambda
), we sampled 10 initial points from the standard normal distribution. For each initial point, both RCD and RPCD were run 10 times, resulting in 100 runs per setting.

Parameter Settings.

We performed experiments for dimension
n\in\{25,50\}
and minimum eigenvalue
\sigma\in\{0.1,\dots,0.9\}
(with increments of
0.1
). For
(iii)
, we considered the scaling factor
\alpha\in\{0.5,2.0\}
. For each algorithm execution, we ran 200 iterations when
n=25
and 300 iterations when
n=50
. For
(iv)
, we set
n=m=100
,
\lambda\in\{0.0001,0.001,0.01,0.1,1.0\}
, and ran 600 iterations.

Implementation Details.

For the implementation of RCD and RPCD, we computed the
\argmin
for the objective function at each coordinate, as described in
Algorithm

1
.
For
(i)
and
(ii)
, the update rule can be computed in closed form:

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{t+1}

\displaystyle={\bm{x}}_{t}-\frac{1}{a_{i_{t}i_{t}}}{\bm{E}}_{i_{t}}{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}_{t}.

This update rule is derived from the CD update formula for quadratic objectives, as remarked in
Section

2.2
. For
(iii)
and
(iv)
, we used
scipy.optimize.minimize_scalar
with default parameters to compute the coordinate-wise updates.

Measurement.

To evaluate the convergence, we measured
\frac{\|{\bm{x}}_{k}-{\bm{x}}^{\star}\|}{\|{\bm{x}}_{0}-{\bm{x}}^{\star}\|}
as our metric. This metric is motivated by Theorems
3.1
and
3.3
, which provide convergence bounds in terms of the norm of the iterates.
For
(i)
and
(ii)
, the optimal solution is known to be
{\bm{x}}^{\star}={\bm{0}}
, simplifying the metric to
\frac{\|{\bm{x}}_{k}\|}{\|{\bm{x}}_{0}\|}
.
For
(iii)
and
(iv)
, we used the BFGS method implemented in
scipy.optimize.minimize
with custom parameters (
gtol=1e-12, maxiter=10000
) to ensure a more accurate approximate optimal solution
{\bm{x}}^{\star}
before running the RCD and RPCD algorithms.

Appendix G
Non-Asymptotic Comparison of RCD and RPCD

This section shows that RPCD outperforms RCD even after a finite number of epochs when
{\bm{A}}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\sigma}
, complementing the asymptotic results in the main text.

To avoid confusion, we introduce separate notations for RCD and RPCD. We denote by
{\bm{x}}_{T}^{\text{RCD}}
and
{\bm{x}}_{K}^{\text{RPCD}}
the iterates after
T
iterations and
K
epochs, respectively, both starting from the same initial point
{\bm{x}}_{0}
. The corresponding iteration matrices, denoted by
{\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RCD}}
and
{\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}}
, are both
2\times 2
iteration matrices of RCD and RPCD that represent the restriction of
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}}
to
\mathrm{span}\{{\bm{I}},{\bm{1}}{\bm{1}}^{\top}\}
.

G.1
RCD Lower Bound

From the proof of
Theorem

3.4
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}((\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RCD}})^{T}({\bm{I}}))

\displaystyle=\alpha_{T},

where
\alpha_{T}={\bm{e}}_{1}^{\top}({\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RCD}})^{T}{\bm{e}}_{1}
. Let
{\bm{v}}_{1}
and
{\bm{v}}_{2}
be the unit eigenvectors of
{\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RCD}}
associated with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
, where
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}
. Then we can decompose
{\bm{e}}_{1}=c_{1}{\bm{v}}_{1}+c_{2}{\bm{v}}_{2}
, where
c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2}=1
since
{\bm{v}}_{1}
and
{\bm{v}}_{2}
form an orthonormal basis.

Using this decomposition, we compute

\displaystyle\alpha_{T}

\displaystyle={\bm{e}}_{1}^{\top}({\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RCD}})^{T}{\bm{e}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(c_{1}{\bm{v}}_{1}+c_{2}{\bm{v}}_{2}\right)^{\top}({\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RCD}})^{T}\left(c_{1}{\bm{v}}_{1}+c_{2}{\bm{v}}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=c_{1}^{2}\lambda_{1}^{T}+c_{2}^{2}\lambda_{2}^{T}

\displaystyle\geq c_{1}^{2}\lambda_{1}^{T}.

The inequality follows from the fact that
\lambda_{2}\geq 0
, as shown in the proof of
Theorem

3.4
.

We now consider two cases:
(i)

n>2
and
(ii)

n=2
.

(i)

n>2
. When
n>2
, all entries of
{\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RCD}}
are positive. Therefore, we can apply the following lemma, known as the Perron-Frobenius Theorem.

Lemma G.1

(
Horn & Johnson 2012
, Theorem 8.2.8.)
.

Let
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a positive matrix, i.e., all entries are strictly positive. Then there exists a unique real vector
{\bm{x}}
such that
{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}=\rho({\bm{A}}){\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{x}}
is a positive vector, i.e., all entries are strictly positive.

By this lemma, the eigenvector
{\bm{v}}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{11}&v_{12}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
corresponding to the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{\max}({\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RCD}})
can be chosen to have strictly positive components.

Now, from the equation
{\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RCD}}{\bm{v}}_{1}=\lambda_{1}{\bm{v}}_{1}
, comparing the second coordinates gives

\frac{v_{11}}{v_{12}}=\frac{n\lambda_{1}-\sigma^{2}(n-2)}{\sigma^{2}}.

Hence,
v_{11}\geq v_{12}
is equivalent to
\lambda_{1}\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\sigma^{2}
.

Now let
p
be the characteristic polynomial of
{\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RCD}}
. One can compute that

p\left(\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\sigma^{2}\right)=\frac{2(n-1)\sigma^{2}(\sigma-1)}{n^{2}}\leq 0.

Since
p
is a convex quadratic polynomial, the inequality above implies that
\lambda_{1}\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\sigma^{2}
. Therefore,
v_{11}\geq v_{12}
, and since
c_{1}={\bm{e}}_{1}^{\top}{\bm{v}}_{1}=v_{11}
and
v_{11}^{2}+v_{12}^{2}=1
, we conclude that
c_{1}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}
.

(ii)

n=2
. In this case,

{\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RCD}}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1+(1-\sigma)^{2}&0\\
\sigma^{2}&0\end{bmatrix},

whose eigenvalues are
0
and
\frac{1+(1-\sigma)^{2}}{2}
. The eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue is proportional to

{\bm{w}}=\begin{bmatrix}1+(1-\sigma)^{2}\\
1\end{bmatrix},

so the normalized eigenvector is
{\bm{v}}_{1}=\frac{{\bm{w}}}{\|{\bm{w}}\|}
. Since
1+(1-\sigma)^{2}\geq\sigma^{2}
, it follows that
v_{11}\geq v_{12}
, and as in the previous case, we again obtain
c_{1}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}
.

Finally, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}[\|{\bm{x}}_{T}^{\text{RCD}}\|^{2}]}{\|{\bm{x}}_{0}\|^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}+\frac{(1-\sigma)^{2}}{n}\right)^{T}.

G.2
RPCD Upper Bound

From the proof of
Theorem

3.3
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\bm{x}}_{K}^{\text{RPCD}}\|^{2}\right]}{\|{\bm{x}}_{0}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\bm{x}}_{0}^{\top}(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}})^{K}({\bm{I}}){\bm{x}}_{0}}{{\bm{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\max}((\mathcal{M}_{\bm{A}}^{\text{RPCD}})^{K}({\bm{I}}))

\displaystyle={\bm{y}}^{\top}({\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}})^{K}{\bm{x}},

where
{\bm{x}}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
and
{\bm{y}}=\begin{bmatrix}1&n\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
. Let
{\bm{v}}_{1}
and
{\bm{v}}_{2}
be the unit eigenvectors of
{\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}}
such that
\|{\bm{v}}_{i}\|=1
, associated with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
with
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}
. Then
{\bm{x}}=c_{1}{\bm{v}}_{1}+c_{2}{\bm{v}}_{2}
for some
c_{1},c_{2}
with
c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2}=1
, because
{\bm{x}}={\bm{e}}_{1}
.
We have

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}^{\top}({\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}})^{K}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=c_{1}\lambda_{1}^{K}{\bm{y}}^{\top}{\bm{v}}_{1}+c_{2}\lambda_{2}^{K}{\bm{y}}^{\top}{\bm{v}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}^{K}\left(c_{1}{\bm{y}}^{\top}{\bm{v}}_{1}+c_{2}\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\lambda_{1}}\right)^{K}{\bm{y}}^{\top}{\bm{v}}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{1}^{K}\left(|c_{1}|\|{\bm{y}}\|\|{\bm{v}}_{1}\|+|c_{2}|\|{\bm{y}}\|\|{\bm{v}}_{2}\|\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\lambda_{1}}\right)^{K}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{1}^{K}\|{\bm{y}}\|(|c_{1}|+|c_{2}|).

Since
c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2}=1
, we have
|c_{1}|+|c_{2}|\leq\sqrt{2}
. Also,
\|{\bm{y}}\|=\sqrt{n^{2}+1}
, so we get
{\bm{y}}^{\top}({\bm{M}}_{{\bm{A}}}^{\text{RPCD}})^{K}{\bm{x}}\leq\lambda_{1}^{K}\sqrt{2(n^{2}+1)}
.

G.3
Comparison of RCD and RPCD

From the above, if
T=nK
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\bm{x}}_{T}^{\text{RCD}}\|^{2}\right]}{\|{\bm{x}}_{0}\|^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{K}}

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{n}+\frac{(1-\sigma)^{2}}{n}\right)^{n}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{K}}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\bm{x}}_{K}^{\text{RPCD}}\|^{2}\right]}{\|{\bm{x}}_{0}\|^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{K}}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{n},\left(1-\frac{\sigma}{n}\right)^{2n}\right\}\left(\sqrt{2(n^{2}+1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{K}}.

Thus, if we can show that

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{1}{n}+\frac{(1-\sigma)^{2}}{n}\right)^{n}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{K}}

\displaystyle\geq\max\left\{\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{n},\left(1-\frac{\sigma}{n}\right)^{2n}\right\}\left(\sqrt{2(n^{2}+1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{K}},

(42)

then we can conclude that RPCD is faster than RCD.
The following is equivalent to (
42
):

\displaystyle\frac{\left(1-\frac{1}{n}+\frac{(1-\sigma)^{2}}{n}\right)^{n}}{\max\left\{\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{n},\left(1-\frac{\sigma}{n}\right)^{2n}\right\}}

\displaystyle\geq\left(2\sqrt{2(n^{2}+1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{K}}.

Since the left-hand side is
\geq 1
, for any
\sigma\in(0,1)
, there exists
K_{0}
such that if
K\geq K_{0}
, then (
42
) holds.

Therefore, after
K_{0}
epochs, RPCD is faster than RCD.
