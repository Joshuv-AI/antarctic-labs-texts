LAPTM5 drives omental metastasis in high-grade serous ovarian cancer via TGF-β/Smad-mediated epithelial plasticity
Authors: Yan Gao, Jie Li, Xiaoyang Han, Zhefeng Li, Jin Cheng, Lisha Hou, Mei Jiang, Chenghong Yin, Wentao Yue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) is an aggressive gynecological malignancy marked by widespread metastasis, most notably to the omentum. However, the molecular mechanisms driving this process remain poorly understood.MethodsWe present an integrated analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from normal ovaries, primary tumors, and omental metastases to generate a high-resolution cellular landscape of HGSOC. LAPTM5 expression was examined by immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, qPCR, western blotting, and analyses of TCGA datasets. Functional assays including Transwell migration/invasion, wound healing, flow cytometry, single-nucleotide variant (SNV) and alternative splicing (AS) analysis were performed after LAPTM5 knockdown. The proteins expression involved in epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and TGF-β mediated signaling pathways was verified by qPCR and western blotting. The critical role of LAPTM5 on metastasis in vivo was detected by the tumor-bearing mice model.ResultsWe identified a metastasis-associated epithelial subcluster characterized by immune suppression and poor prognosis, with LAPTM5 emerging as a defining marker. Functional assays revealed that LAPTM5 silencing significantly impaired HGSOC cell migration, invasion, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in vitro, and reduced metastatic burden in vivo. Mechanistically, LAPTM5 activates the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, promoting EMT and facilitating omental metastasis. Intriguingly, LAPTM5 knockdown led to reduced single-nucleotide variant (SNV) accumulation and alternative splicing (AS) events, thereby decreasing the expression of metastasis-associated genes.ConclusionsThese findings identify LAPTM5 as a key regulator of TGF-β/Smad-driven epithelial plasticity and omental dissemination in HGSOC, positioning it as both a prognostic biomarker and a potential therapeutic target for advanced-stage disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07319-z.Keywords: LAPTM5, Omental metastasis, Alternative splicing, Single-nucleotide variant

Introduction

High-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) is the most prevalent histological subtype of ovarian cancer (OC), accounting for over 70% of cases and 70–80% of OC-related deaths [1]. Due to its asymptomatic onset, more than 75% of patients are diagnosed at advanced stages (stage III–IV), when peritoneal and omental metastases are already established [2]. The omentum, with its rich adipose content and extensive vascularization, offers a permissive niche for metastatic colonization [3]. As such, omental metastasis is the most frequent and clinically significant route of HGSOC dissemination, contributing substantially to disease progression and mortality. Despite therapeutic advances, including cytoreductive surgery and combination chemotherapy with agents like bevacizumab, survival gains remain modest. Therefore, preventing or targeting omental metastasis remains an urgent and unmet clinical challenge in HGSOC management.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has emerged as a powerful tool for deciphering the cellular heterogeneity and complex tumor microenvironment (TME) of ovarian cancer [4, 5]. However, prior scRNA-seq studies have rarely captured the dynamic transitions of epithelial cell from normal ovarian tissue to primary tumors and metastatic lesions. A comprehensive, high-resolution single-cell landscape that includes normal ovary, primary tumor, and omental metastasis is crucial for elucidating the molecular events underlying HGSOC metastasis. Additionally, the lack of integrative analyses across multiple scRNA-seq datasets has limited our ability to extract robust biological insights due to study-specific variability. This has hindered the identification of key molecular drivers facilitating the transition from primary tumor to omental metastasis.

To overcome these limitations, we conducted a comprehensive integrative analysis of publicly available scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq datasets, spanning normal ovarian tissue, primary HGSOC tumors, and omental metastases. This enabled us to construct a detailed single-cell atlas of HGSOC progression and identify critical epithelial subclusters linked to metastasis and poor prognosis. Among these, LAPTM5 (lysosomal-associated protein transmembrane 5) emerged as a novel and previously unrecognized gene implicated in HGSOC metastasis. Mechanistic investigations revealed that LAPTM5 facilitates omental dissemination by activating TGF-β/Smad signaling, driving EMT (epithelial-mesenchymal transition), and enhancing tumor cell plasticity. Moreover, LAPTM5 depletion reduced SNV (single-nucleotide variant) accumulation and alternative splicing events, leading to downregulation of metastasis-related genes. These findings establish LAPTM5 as a central regulator of epithelial plasticity and metastatic progression in HGSOC, offering new insights into disease pathogenesis and potential avenues for therapeutic intervention.

Materials and methods

Public ovarian cancer datasets

scRNA-seq data for OC were downloaded from GSE184880 [6] and GSE154600 [7]. Bulk RNA-seq data and corresponding clinicopathologic information of OC patients in TCGA were obtained from UCSC Xena (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/).

Data preprocessing

scRNA-seq data were processed and analyzed using the Seurat (v 4.1.1) R package [8]. These cells were filtered for mitochondrial gene percent (0–15%), ribosomal gene percent (min:1-100%), and hemoglobin gene percent (0-0.1%). Low-quality cells (< 300 genes/cell, and < 3 cells/gene) were excluded. Subsequently, the harmony method was performed to integrate cells. Post quality control and normalization, the top 2000 highly variable genes were selected for Principal Component Analysis (PCA). Dimensionality reduction was performed with PCA and t-SNE, followed by cell clustering using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions.

Chromosomal copy number variations (CNV) analysis

The CNVs were estimated by the inferCNV R package [9]. The CNV of total cell types was calculated using expression levels from single-cell sequencing data for each cell with a cutoff of 0.1. The CNV score of each cell was calculated as the mean of the CNV region. Myeloid and T cells are used as a reference.

Gene set functional analysis

To investigate the LAPTM5-mediated biological parameters, the gene set functional analyses were conducted with the R package ‘clusterProfiler’ and ‘GSVA’. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database were used for GSVA analyses. Hallmark gene sets were obtained from the ‘msigdbr’ R package.

Survival analysis

The top 10 marker genes for each cell subcluster were extracted. Combining with the overall survival time, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed by the R package ‘survival’, and P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant between high and low expression groups.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis

The pseudotime trajectory of all the epithelial cells was constructed using the R package ‘monocle3’ (monocle3 v1.0.1). The UMAP method was applied to reduce dimensions, and ‘plot_cell_ trajectory’ was used for visualization.

Cell communication analysis

To explore the potential relationship between epithelium subtype (C1) and immune cell types, the R package ‘CellChat’ (version v1.1.3) was used to perform cell communication analysis. For analysis, 500 cells from each cell subcluster were selected randomly by the function ‘subset’. The strength of ligand-receptor-mediated interactions between cell subsets was predicted from expression data and evaluated for enrichment by permutation testing. Significant interactions (P < 0.01) were subsequently identified.

Single-nucleotide variant (SNV) analysis

Single-nucleotide variant analysis for bulk RNA-seq samples was conducted using GATK (version 3.4–46) with recommended parameters [10], and SNP/InDel annotation was performed using ANNOVAR. Analyses included functional annotation, genomic loci, and classification of SNV types.

Alternative splicing (AS) analysis

The alternative splicing events and differential alternative splicing events between Si-control and Si-Laptm5 SKOV3 were performed by the software rMATS (version 4.0.1). Splicing events were categorized as skipped exons, retained introns, alternative 5′ splice sites, alternative 3′ splice sites, or mutually exclusive exons, with significant events filtered by P < 0.05 and |IncLevelDifference| >0.1. Splicing efficiency was evaluated separately for 5′ and 3′ splice sites using RNA-seq data and the R package SEAA. Distribution differences between samples were assessed by Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. Source code is available at https://github.com/PrinceWang2018/SEAA.

Cell culture and transfection of SiRNA

Human ovarian cancer cell lines, including OVCAR3, SKOV3, HEY, CAOV3 and ovarian epithelial cell line Hosepic (OSE) were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, MD, USA) supplemented with penicillin and streptomycin (Gibco, MD, USA), and 10% fetal serum (HyClone, USA). SKOV3 and HEY were transfected with Lipofectamine™ RNAmax (Invitrogen, USA). LAPTM5-target-specific small interfering RNA (siRNA) was synthesized by JTS Scientific (Beijing, China). HEY and SKOV3 cells were transfected with 100 nmol LAPTM5 siRNA or control siRNA.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells with a corresponding Kit (Invitrogen, Calsbad, CA). cDNA was synthesized using 1 µg of total RNA by ReverTraAceqPCR RT kit (Toyobo, Shanghai, China. Each reaction was conducted with SYBR Premix EX Taq™ (Takala, Dalian, China) using 1 µg of template DNA. Then, qPCR was operated with an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, USA). The amplification primer sequences of LAPTM5 were: 5’-GCGTCTTGTTGTTCATCGAGC-3’ (forward) and 5’-CGATCCTGAGGTAGCCCAT-3’ (reverse). The cycle number of the threshold value of LAPTM5 was normalized to the GAPDH value. The relative gene expression was determined by the comparative 2−ΔΔCT method.

Western blotting

Cells were collected and lysed with RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). 25 µg total lysate per sample was subjected to SDS-PAGE followed by immunodetection with the following primary antibodies: LAPTM5 (Thermo; PA5-23585, 1:1000 dilution) Twist (CST;46702), Snail (CST; 3879), N-cadherin (CST; 13116), Vimentin (CST; 5741), Smad2/3 Antibody Sampler Kit (CST; 12747), GAPDH (CST;5174). The corresponding HRP-linked secondary antibody and enhanced chemiluminescence were used to detect the protein expression.

Immunofluorescence

Samples of cells were grown on lysine-coated glass coverslips, and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 minutes to preserve the tissue structure. Subsequently, the cells were permeabilized using 0.1% Triton and were blocked with goat serum. Then, the samples were incubated with the primary antibody for 12hours at 4°C, and stained with secondary antibodies for 2hours at 37℃ in the dark. Finally, 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was used to stain and visualize the cell nuclei.

Immunohistochemistry

After fixation in paraformaldehyde for over 24 h and processing in 10% NBF, samples were embedded in paraffin. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, followed by endogenous peroxidase blockade with 3% H₂O₂ for 10 min. Primary antibodies LAPTM5, were applied at a 1:500 dilution and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing, sections were treated with appropriate secondary antibodies for 30 min at room temperature, and antigens were visualized using DAB (Solarbio).

Apoptosis assays by flow cytometry

The treated SKOV3 cells are collected to determine apoptosis using an Annexin V (FITC) and propidium iodide (PI) kit (BD Biosciences). The cell samples were detected by Flow cytometry, and FlowJo software version 7.6 was used to process the data. Statistical analysis and visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism software on data from triplicate experiments.

Wound healing assay

The wound healing assay was performed to evaluate the cells migration ability after LAPTM5 l knockdown. 1 × 106 SKOV3 or HEY cells were seeded in a 6-well plate for transfection. After siRNA-transfection for 24 h, cells were scratched and photographed at 0 and 72 h in several pre-marked spots. Cell migration was quantified using Image J software. The migration rate was quantified by comparing microscopic images of the wound area taken at 0, 2 and 4 days, which showed a progressive reduction in the gap size.

Transwell chamber migration and invasion assay

The transwell migration and invasion assay was performed in a Transwell-24 system (Corning, Inc., NY, USA). To perform the migration assay, 2 × 104 cells were suspended in 50µL serum-free PRIM-1640 and placed in the upper chambers while the lower chamber was filled with 600 µL PRIM-1640 containing 10% FBS. After 24 h of incubation, the cells in the upper chamber were removed, and the successfully translocated cells were fixed and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. The migrated cell number was counted in five randomly chosen fields using a phase contrast microscope and then statistically analyzed. For the invasion assay, 1.5 × 105 cells were seeded on transwells coated with 50 µL Matrigel (1:4 dilution with 0.2% BSA) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The chamber was incubated as previously described for 48 h. Then, the lower side of the chamber was fixed, stained, and photographed with a microscope.

Metastatic ovarian cancer model

For the metastatic OC model, C57BL/6 mice were intraperitoneally injected with 5 × 106 ID8-luc cells. We randomly divided the tumor-bearing mice into two groups on day 14. Si-control and Si-Laptm5 were administered intraperitoneally four times every three days. The IVIS Imaging System was used to monitor the growth and metastasis of the tumor. At 23 days post-injection, mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation following institutional guidelines. The harvested tissues samples were fixed in 4% PFA, embedded in paraffin, and prepared for HE and IHC staining. All animal procedures were conducted at Beijing Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital’s Animal Center.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using R and Graphpad Prism 8. All representative experiments (such as qRT-PCR and transwell assay) were performed three times. The results were presented as the mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was calculated using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. For human survival analysis, a two-tailed log-rank test (Mantel-Cox) was used to calculate statistical significance. P- values were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05. For all figures, P < 0.05, **: P < 0.01, ***: P < 0.001, and ****: P < 0.0001.

Results

High-resolution landscape of primary and metastatic HGSOC

We integrated 15 single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) samples, including five HGSOC omental metastases (HGSOC_M), six primary ovarian tumors (HGSOC), and four normal ovaries from datasets GSE184880 and GSE154600, using the Harmony algorithm (Fig. 1A). After quality control, 90 965 single cells were retained for downstream analysis. Dimensionality reduction using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) identified eight major cellular clusters (Fig. 1B). Among these, 18 186 cells originated from normal ovaries, 24 947 from primary tumors, and 47 832 from metastatic tissues (Fig. 1C).

Single-cell profiling of normal ovary, primary HGSOC, and omental metastases. A, Overview of sample collection and analysis strategy. B, C, t-SNE plots of all single cells coloured by cell type (B) and tissue origin (C). D, Bubble plot of marker gene expression across major cell types. E, Changes in cell type composition across normal, primary, and metastatic tissues. F, Pie charts depicting the epithelial cell fractions in each tissue type. G, Inferred CNV landscape across cell populations

To characterize these clusters, we visualized the expression of canonical marker genes using a bubble plot, identifying B cells (CD79A, IGHG2, and IGHM), T cells (CD3D, CD2, and CD3E), epithelial cells (CD24, PAX8, and EPCAM), fibroblasts (DCN, COL5A2, and OGN), myeloid cells (TREM2, CD14, and CD68), endothelial cells (PECAM1, CLDN5, and VWF), proliferating cells (TOP2A and MKI67), and SMC/myofibroblasts (TAGLN, ACTA2, and MYH11) (Fig. 1D and Fig. S1).

We then assessed the distribution of these eight cell types across normal, primary, and metastatic tissues to gain a deep understanding of the prominent features in distinct tissues (Fig. 1E). Their proportions varied significantly among disease states, reflecting dynamic shifts of the tumor microenvironment during progression. Notably, epithelial and myeloid cells expanded with advancing disease (Fig. 1F). To identify malignant cell populations, we inferred large-scale copy number variations (CNVs) from the transcriptomic data. Epithelial cells displayed the highest CNV burden, supporting the malignant role (Fig. 1G). Collectively, these results underscore the cellular heterogeneity of HGSOC and implicate that epithelial cells are closely associated with tumor progression and omental metastasis.

C1 epithelial subtype drives omental metastasis and predicts poor prognosis in HGSOC

To dissect the metastasis-associated epithelial subtypes in HGSOC, we re-clustered epithelial cells into six subclusters (C1-C6) (Fig. 2A). C1, C2, and C4 were predominantly derived from omental metastases, whereas C3 and C5 were mainly originated from primary tumors. C6 consisted exclusively of normal ovarian epithelial cells (Fig. 2B). Among these, C1 exhibited the highest tumor purity and lowest stromal content (Fig. 2C). CNV analysis confirmed that C1 exhibited the highest genomic instability (Fig. 2D), underscoring its strong malignant potential.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis revealed a progressive transition from C4 to C2 to C1, suggesting that C1 represents a terminal and highly metastatic epithelial state (Fig. 2E). Deconvolution analysis further demonstrated that only C1 was significantly associated with poor patient prognosis (P = 0.0019; Fig. 2F and Fig. S2).

To investigate the functional role of C1 in the tumor microenvironment, we evaluated immune interactions. C1 exhibited the highest immune interaction score, indicating substantial crosstalk with immune cells (Fig. 2G). Ligand-receptor interaction analysis using CellPhoneDB revealed extensive communication between C1 and myeloid or T cell populations (Fig. 2H). Notably, C1 expressed cytokines including IL4, IL6, CXCL8, and CCL5, which interact with corresponding receptors on myeloid and T cells (Fig. 2I). These interactions may modulate immune responses, adhesion, and cytokine signalling.

In summary, C1 represents a metastasis-associated malignant epithelial subtype strongly linked to poor prognosis, tumor progression and omental dissemination through active crosstalk with the immune microenvironment.

LAPTM5 emerges as a unique overexpressed gene driving omental metastasis in HGSOC

Successful metastasis depends on robust cell adhesion [11, 12] and active cytokine signaling [13]. To identify genes that simultaneously regulate these processes and specific to omental metastasis, we analyzed differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the C1 epithelial subcluster (adjusted P < 0.05, fold change ≥ 0.8). By overlapping C1 DEGs with gene sets involved in cell adhesion and cytokine production, we identified a single gene uniquely enriched at the intersection—LAPTM5 (Fig. 3A). Violin plot analysis confirmed its specific upregulation in C1 (Fig. 3B), and LAPTM5 expression progressively increased with disease severity, peaking in metastatic omental tissues (Fig. 3C).

E, IHC showing elevated LAPTM5 expression in various ovarian cancer subtypes. F, IHC showing increased LAPTM5 in tumors from the OC mouse model. G qRT-PCR analysis showing LAPTM5 mRNA expression in various ovarian cell lines. GAPDH was used as an internal control *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001. H, Kaplan–Meier analysis showing poor prognosis in patients with high LAPTM5. I, Forest plot illustrating the prognostic value of LAPTM5 across clinical subgroups

Analysis of the Oncomine database indicated LAPTM5 is overexpressed across multiple tumor types (Fig. 3D). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) further validated high LAPTM5 protein levels in patient tissues from HGSOC, clear cell carcinoma, mucinous carcinoma, and endometrioid carcinoma, compared to normal ovarian tissue and borderline ovarian tumors (between benign and malignant) (Fig. 3E). In an intraperitoneally established ovarian cancer mouse model, IHC staining demonstrated LAPTM5 upregulation in tumors compared with normal ovaries (Fig. 3F). Similarly, qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated significant overexpression of LAPTM5 mRNA in four ovarian cancer cell lines compared with the normal HOSEpic cell line (Fig. 3G).

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis of TCGA data revealed that high LAPTM5 expression predicted significantly poor overall survival (Fig. 3H). Subgroup analysis using a forest plot demonstrated that this negative prognostic effect persists across multiple clinical contexts, including serous (P = 0.036) and endometrioid OC (P = 0.0081) subtypes, patients with stage III/IV (P = 0.047) or grade I/II tumors (P = 0.016), and those treated with Gemcitabine or Topotecan (Fig. 3I). These findings highlight LAPTM5 as a consistently overexpressed and potent prognostic biomarker in ovarian cancer.

LAPTM5 downregulation suppresses migration and invasion and induces apoptosis in HGSOC cells

To investigate the functional role of LAPTM5 in ovarian cancer progression, we assessed the effects of its knockdown on cell migration, invasion, and apoptosis. Three distinct siRNAs targeting LAPTM5 were transfected into SKOV3 and HEY cell lines. Knockdown efficiency was verified by qRT-PCR and western blotting after 48 h, with siRNA-3 proving the highest efficacy and thus selected for subsequent experiments (Fig. 4A, B). Immunofluorescence analysis further confirmed a significant reduction in LAPTM5 protein expression in the siRNA-treated group (Fig. 4C).

Functionally, LAPTM5 knockdown significantly suppressed SKOV3 cell migration and invasion, as shown by wound-healing (Fig. 4D) and Transwell assays (Fig. 4E). Flow cytometry analysis revealed a marked increase in apoptotic cells following LAPTM5 silencing (Fig. 4F). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining showed elevated levels of apoptotic markers in the LAPTM5-silenced group (Fig. 4G). These results indicate that LAPTM5 critically enhances HGSOC cell motility and inhibits cell death.

RNA-seq analysis reveals LAPTM5-regulated TGF-β/Smad signaling and EMT pathways

To elucidate the downstream mechanisms by which LAPTM5 promotes ovarian cancer invasiveness and metastasis, we performed RNA-seq on SKOV3 cells following LAPTM5 knockdown. 465 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, including 366 downregulated and 99 upregulated genes (Fig. 5A, B). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis and Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed significant suppression of the TGF-β signaling pathway and cell adhesion molecules upon LAPTM5 depletion (Fig. 5C, D). Heatmap visualization of key downregulated genes associated with cell development and extracellular matrix (ECM) organization (Fig. 5E). We then validated the expression of key TGF-β pathway components by qRT-PCR. The results showed that Smad2, Smad4, Smad7, TGFβR1, and TGFβR2 were all downregulated following LAPTM5 knockdown (Fig. 5F). Consistently, western blotting analysis revealed that LAPTM5 depletion inhibited TGF-β-induced Smad phosphorylation and downstream activation, indicating impaired of TGF-β/Smad axis (Fig. 5G).

To validate these findings, GSEA and gene set variation analysis (GSVA) of TCGA human ovarian cancer data revealed a strong association between high LAPTM5 expression and EMT (Fig. S3), consistent with our RNA-seq results. Furthermore EMT marker expression were examined via qPCR and western blotting in SKOV3 cells treated with or without TGF-β after LAPTM5 knockdown. E-cadherin (epithelial marker) was significantly upregulated, while mesenchymal markers N-cadherin, Snail1, and Twist were notably downregulated in the LAPTM5-silenced group (Fig. 5H, I). Notably, LAPTM5 knockdown also suppressed TGF-β-induced EMT, indicating that LAPTM5 facilitates both basal and TGF-β-driven EMT programs. Collectively, LAPTM5 activates TGF-β/Smad signaling and EMT to promote metastasis, identifying it as a key regulator of epithelial plasticity in HGSOC.

LAPTM5 regulates SNVs and alternative splicing events to promote HGSOC progression

Somatic SNVs are known to initiate tumorigenesis and promote malignant progression [14]. To determine whether LAPTM5 influences mutational burden, we profiled SNVs in SKOV3 cells after LAPTM5 knockdown. SNVs were categorized into 12 mutation types, with A >G and T >C transitions being most prevalent. Notably, the overall number of SNVs was significantly reduced following LAPTM5 knockdown (Fig. 6A). A detailed regional analysis showed fewer mutations in both intronic and intergenic regions upon LAPTM5 knockdown (Fig. 6B), suggesting that LAPTM5 may contribute to genomic instability.

B, Comparison of SNV distributions in intronic, intergenic, and exonic regions (**P < 0.01). C, Pie chart depicting proportions of AS event types identified after LAPTM5 knockdown. D, Distribution of 5ss and 3ss splicing statuses across spliced, partly spliced, and undetermined categories. E, Global splicing efficiency at 5′ and 3′ splice sites after LAPTM5 knockdown. F, GO enrichment analysis of biological processes associated with LAPTM5-regulated AS genes

Alternative splicing (AS) is another key regulatory mechanism implicated in cancer progression [15–17]. We analyzed AS events using rMATS and identified 1,340 significant splicing alterations after LAPTM5 knockdown (Table S1), including skipped exons (65.07%), mutually exclusive exons (8.51%), alternative 5′ splice sites (7.09%), alternative 3′ splice sites (13.36%), and retained introns (5.97%) (Fig. 6C). Further analysis of splicing site usage showed a significant reduction in spliced 5′ and 3′ splice site (5ss and 3ss) frequencies in LAPTM5-deficient cells (Fig. 6D). Global splicing efficiency analysis confirmed this finding, with reduced efficiency observed at both 5′ (D = 0.0408) and 3′ (D = 0.0360) splice sites (Fig. 6E).

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of LAPTM5-regulated AS genes revealed enrichment in processes such as cell motility regulation, interferon response, and protein metabolism (Fig. 6F). These results suggest that LAPTM5 contributes not only to mutational load but also modulates post-transcriptional splicing programs, thereby promoting HGSOC progression through multifaceted regulatory mechanisms.

Targeting LAPTM5 suppresses HGSOC metastasis in vivo

To assess the in vivo impact of LAPTM5 knockdown on metastatic progression, we employed a syngeneic ovarian cancer model. Following intraperitoneal injection with ID8-luc cells, tumor-bearing mice were randomly assigned to Si-Control or Si-Laptm5 groups on day 14. The siRNA targeting LAPTM5 (Si-Laptm5) was administered on days 14, 17, 20, and 23, with tumor burden monitored via bioluminescence imaging on days 14 and 23 (Fig. 7A). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed markedly elevated LAPTM5 expression in metastatic tissues, including spleen, pleura, peritoneum, and intestines, compared to normal controls (Fig. 7B). In vivo imaging showed 100% metastatic tumor incidence in the Si-Control group, while only 40% (2/5) of Si-Laptm5–treated mice developed metastases (Fig. 7C, D). Additionally, both ascites volume and abdominal circumference were significantly reduced following Si-Laptm5 treatment (Fig. 7E, F). These findings confirm that LAPTM5 plays a pivotal role in promoting ovarian cancer metastasis and ascites formation in vivo.

Discussion

Despite advancements in early detection and treatment, omental metastasis remains a leading contributor to poor prognosis in high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC). Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying this process, particularly the regulators of epithelial plasticity and metastatic dissemination to the omentum, is critical for improving patient outcomes.

In this study, we identified LAPTM5 as a key driver of omental metastasis in HGSOC. LAPTM5 encodes a 29-kDa lysosomal-associated transmembrane protein predominantly expressed in immune and hematopoietic cells. Previous studies have shown that LAPTM5 negatively regulates plasma membrane receptor levels in T and B cells and promotes inflammation in macrophages [18, 19]. In addition, LAPTM5 can promote renal cancer metastasis [20], bladder cancer proliferation [21], and breast cancer progression [22]. However, many current studies focus on data analysis and lack insight into the specific mechanism of LAPTM5, and its function and mechanisms in ovarian cancer remain unclear. Here, we establish LAPTM5 as a critical driver of ovarian cancer metastasis, promoting TGF-β/Smad-mediated epithelial plasticity and orchestrating CNV and AS.

Compared with previous studies that focus solely on individual tumor compartments, our approach integrates single-cell and bulk transcriptomic data across the entire disease continuum, from normal ovary to metastasis, which enables the reconstruction of epithelial lineage trajectories during HGSOC progression. This comprehensive landscape underscores LAPTM5 as a hallmark of metastatic epithelial subclusters that is tightly linked to adverse clinical outcomes. The functional role of LAPTM5 in facilitating migration, invasion, and EMT was experimentally validated, highlighting its direct role in metastatic progression. Therefore, our study identifies LAPTM5 as a critical factor at the intersection of epithelial plasticity and microenvironmental interactions, positioning it as a master regulator of cellular evolution in HGSOC.

Mechanistically, our study reveals that LAPTM5 drives HGSOC metastasis by activating the TGF-β/Smad signaling cascade [23]. This finding aligns with the well-documented role of TGF-β/Smad signaling in driving cancer progression [24, 25], and further underscores its significance in ovarian cancer metastasis. Notably, Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a pivotal mechanism for metastasis, is driven by the TGF-β/smad pathway [26–30]. In line with this, we observed that knockdown of LAPTM5 attenuates TGF-β/Smad signaling and reverses EMT in HGSOC models, thereby identifying LAPTM5 as a novel upstream regulator of this pathway. Together, these results highlight LAPTM5 as a promising therapeutic target in HGSOC. This is further supported by recent evidence implicating LAPTM5 in ovarian cancer growth and metastasis through suppression of the RIG-I-like receptor signaling pathway [31], suggesting that LAPTM5 may exert multifaceted roles in ovarian carcinogenesis.

We also explored the role of LAPTM5 in shaping genomic instability and transcriptomic diversity, with a focus on single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and alternative splicing (AS). Based on the tumor evolution model proposed by Liu et al. [14], which emphasizes that tumors initiate via premalignant SNVs in driver genes and progress malignantly through CNV acquisition at cancer gene loci, we found that LAPTM5, identified within a high-CNV epithelial cluster, regulates both SNV frequency and AS events. LAPTM5 knockdown significantly reduced overall SNV counts, particularly in intronic and intergenic regions, implicating its contribution to mutational accumulation. AS generates protein isoforms with distinct structural, functional, or subcellular characteristics, thereby expanding transcriptomic and proteomic diversity [32]. Increasing evidence indicates that alternative splicing (AS) plays a pivotal role in cancer initiation and progression, particularly in ovarian cancer (OC) [33, 34], as demonstrated by the high expression of PTBP1 [35], ESRP1 [36], and SRSF3 [37], which promote tumor progression by regulating AS events. In parallel, AS analysis revealed that LAPTM5 modulates splicing site proportion and splicing efficiency, affecting genes enriched in pathways related to cell motility, interferon response, and protein metabolism. These findings suggest that LAPTM5 may facilitate metastasis by modulating the timing and specificity of AS events, which are critical for tumor plasticity and progression. Collectively, our results highlight the multifaceted role of LAPTM5 in orchestrating transcriptomic complexity and driving tumor evolution.

Despite these insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. While our multi-omics integration approach effectively reduced inter-study variability, future studies involving larger patient cohorts and spatial transcriptomics will be essential to clarify further LAPTM5’s functions and interactions within the tumor microenvironment. Additionally, the development of selective LAPTM5 inhibitors or combination strategies with TGF-β/Smad pathway inhibitors should be explored to evaluate their efficacy in preventing or treating omental metastasis in HGSOC patients.

In conclusion, LAPTM5 drives omental metastasis in HGSOC by activating TGF-β/Smad signaling and influencing genomic instability through SNV accumulation and alternative splicing modulation. These findings highlight LAPTM5 as a promising therapeutic target and facilitate future strategies to improve clinical outcomes in advanced ovarian cancer.
