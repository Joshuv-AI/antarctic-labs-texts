Accuracy, Reliability, and Bloom’s Taxonomy Performance of Seven Large Language Models on Microbiology Questions
Authors: Volodymyr Dvornyk, Olena Bolgova, Volodymyr Mavrych
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLarge language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as learning resources in medical education, yet their performance and reliability in microbiology, a discipline with a broad, heterogeneous knowledge base, have not been systematically evaluated across multiple platforms.ObjectiveTo benchmark seven publicly available LLMs on microbiology multiple-choice questions (MCQs), assessing overall accuracy, test-retest reliability, topic-specific performance, and the relationship between cognitive complexity and model performance.MethodsSeven LLMs (Claude 4.6 Sonnet, Gemini 3.0, ChatGPT-5.2, Grok 4, Copilot, DeepSeek V3, and Kimi K2) completed 200 MCQs distributed across 20 microbiology topics and five Bloom’s taxonomy levels in three independent sessions separated by 24-hour intervals. A total of 4200 responses were analyzed. Statistical analysis included one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD post hoc tests, repeated-measures ANOVA, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), and Pearson correlations.ResultsThe collective mean accuracy was 86.18%. Six of seven systems exceeded the 80% high-competency threshold; Claude (89.83%), Grok (89.50%), and GPT (88.83%) led the group. Gemini (72.33%) was the only underperforming system. Test-retest reliability varied dramatically: Claude achieved excellent ICC (0.966), while Gemini exhibited poor reliability (ICC = 0.290), with session-to-session fluctuations of up to 100 percentage points on individual topics. Microbial Cell (100%) was the easiest topic; Viral Genomics (61.9%) was the most challenging across all systems. A uniform decline at Bloom’s Level 4 (Analyze) was observed across all LLMs, with no model exceeding 78%.ConclusionContemporary LLMs demonstrate substantial knowledge of microbiology but differ markedly in reliability. Response consistency, alongside accuracy, should be a primary criterion for educational deployment. These findings are specific to microbiology MCQ performance and may not generalize to open-ended clinical reasoning.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence has permeated virtually every dimension of contemporary healthcare, with large language models (LLMs) emerging as particularly consequential tools for both medical practice and education. These systems have attracted considerable research attention following demonstrations that they can exceed passing thresholds on medical licensing examinations in multiple countries, signaling potential utility far beyond general text generation.1 Their growing adoption among students and faculty alike reflects a broader recognition that LLMs may serve as accessible, on-demand learning resources, though the concerns accompanying this promise remain substantial.1

Evaluations of LLM performance across basic medical science disciplines have produced results that vary considerably by subject matter. In biochemistry, a four-model comparison found that the tested systems collectively surpassed medical student performance, with Claude demonstrating the highest accuracy among the chatbots assessed.2 Performance in histology has proven exceptionally high and relatively consistent across models, suggesting a degree of technological maturity in that domain.3 Anatomy evaluations revealed a more differentiated picture, with meaningful accuracy gaps between models and across anatomical regions, though current-generation systems substantially outperform their predecessors.4 In medical physiology, Claude-2 outperformed ChatGPT-3.5 on conceptual multiple-choice questions.5 When LLMs have been applied to assessment tasks beyond answering questions (such as grading short-answer responses), performance has again shown marked variation across disciplines and models, with no single system consistently outperforming others.6 Beyond text-based tasks, multimodal capabilities have begun to be explored; an evaluation of GPT-4o for histological image interpretation found moderate overall performance, with substantial variation across tissue types and magnification levels.7

Microbiology occupies a distinctive position among the basic medical sciences, warranting dedicated investigation. The discipline encompasses a broad and heterogeneous knowledge base, spanning microbial structure and physiology, genomics, ecology, host-pathogen interactions, mechanisms of infectious disease, and the biology of eukaryotic pathogens. This breadth creates a challenging evaluative landscape, as LLM performance in related domains, such as virology, has already shown meaningful variation across systems and languages.8 A preliminary comparison of three platforms on higher-order microbiology questions found that ChatGPT and Gemini substantially outperformed Meta AI, and the study was recognized as among the first of its kind in the discipline.9 The potential applications of LLMs in clinical microbiology (including diagnostic support, report generation, and antimicrobial stewardship) have been discussed as a conceivable next frontier, though rigorous performance characterization remains a prerequisite.10 Systematic benchmarking of a broader range of contemporary LLMs on microbiology multiple-choice questions has not yet been reported, representing a clear gap in the literature. While methodologically related to prior benchmarking work in biochemistry, histology, and anatomy,2–4 this study extends that framework to microbiology (a discipline not previously evaluated) and is the first to include Grok and Kimi alongside a systematic reliability analysis.

Response consistency across repeated testing sessions has emerged as one of the most consequential concerns in LLM evaluation for educational purposes. Studies examining repeated querying of medical examination questions have found that accuracy alone provides an incomplete characterization of a model’s suitability as an educational resource, since a system that produces variable answers across sessions cannot serve as a reliable reference.11 Evaluations of LLMs in histology and embryology have similarly demonstrated that inconsistency over time constitutes a meaningful limitation even for systems achieving respectable accuracy.12 In pathology, ChatGPT displayed relatively stable response patterns across sessions, whereas other models exhibited considerably higher variability.13 These findings collectively suggest that test-retest reliability may differentiate models as meaningfully as raw accuracy, yet it remains undercharacterized in most benchmark studies.

The application of Bloom’s taxonomy as a framework for organizing question difficulty in LLM evaluations has yielded mixed, sometimes counterintuitive results. One investigation found that performance on ChatGPT-4 was significantly shaped by course type but not by Bloom’s taxonomy level, implying that factors beyond the intended cognitive classification may govern model performance.14 Research on student behavior with taxonomically classified questions has revealed that higher-performing students tend to apply pattern recognition even to higher-order questions, while less confident students engage in analytical reasoning even for recall-level items — a dynamic that may have parallels in LLM response strategies.15 Empirical work examining the underlying assumptions of Bloom’s taxonomy has further questioned the independence of its dimensions and the reliability of action verbs as proxies for cognitive processes.16 Analyses across multiple medical disciplines, including histology and pharmacology, have shown inconsistent relationships between taxonomic level and question difficulty in practice.17

Whether these complexities are reflected in LLM performance across Bloom’s levels in microbiology remains an open question. Rapid iteration in LLM architectures and periodic backend updates mean that benchmark results reflect a snapshot in time rather than a fixed platform characteristic.1 Bloom’s taxonomy was selected as the analytical framework because it is the most widely used cognitive classification in medical-education assessment research,15 despite ongoing debate about the independence of its levels.16 Furthermore, although multiple-choice questions are the most commonly used format in medical exams, MCQ-based accuracy is only a proxy measure and does not directly capture open-ended clinical reasoning.

The comparative landscape of available LLMs has grown considerably more complex in recent years. Established platforms such as ChatGPT (OpenAI), Gemini (Google), Claude (Anthropic), Copilot (Microsoft), and DeepSeek (Hangzhou DeepSeek AI Basic Technology Research) have been the primary subjects of medical benchmarking studies, with DeepSeek in particular attracting growing attention following strong performance on USMLE-style questions, albeit with variable results depending on the question bank and domain.18,19 More recently, Grok (xAI) and Kimi (Moonshot AI) have gained substantial user bases globally, yet their performance on specialized medical science content has received virtually no systematic evaluation. Including these newer platforms in a rigorous comparative framework is essential to provide educators and students with accurate and current guidance on the relative strengths and limitations of the tools they increasingly rely upon.

The objective of the present study was to conduct a comprehensive benchmarking evaluation of seven publicly available LLMs (Claude, Gemini, ChatGPT, Grok, Copilot, DeepSeek, and Kimi) on 200 multiple-choice microbiology questions distributed across 20 topics and four Bloom’s taxonomy levels. Each system completed three independent testing sessions to assess test-retest reliability. The study aimed to characterize overall accuracy, response consistency, topic-specific performance patterns, and the relationship between cognitive complexity and model performance, with the goal of providing evidence-based insight into the current capabilities and limitations of these systems in microbiology education. Specifically, this study asks: how do contemporary LLMs compare in accuracy, test-retest reliability, and performance across Bloom’s taxonomy levels when evaluated on medical microbiology MCQs?

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study assesses the capabilities of seven publicly available LLMs for microbiology-related questions: Claude 4.6 Sonnet (Anthropic), Gemini 3.0 (Google), ChatGPT-5.2 (OpenAI), Grok 4 (xAI), Copilot (Microsoft), DeepSeek V3 (Hangzhou DeepSeek AI Basic Technology Research Co)., and Kimi (Moonshot AI). All models were accessed via their standard, free-tier web interfaces between April 6–8, 2026, using default settings; web search and extended/deep-reasoning modes were disabled where such toggles existed, to isolate baseline parametric knowledge.

Two independent experts selected 200 multiple-choice microbiology questions from the established microbiology course examination database, which contains faculty-developed questions previously validated by the institutional exam committee. The study did not include any image-based questions. All items were drawn from the medical microbiology course curriculum for undergraduate medical students, emphasizing host-pathogen interactions, pathogenesis, and clinically relevant microbial topics rather than general/environmental microbiology; topics such as Ecology of Bacteria and Microbial Ecosystems were framed in a clinical/medical context (eg, microbiome-associated disease).

The selected questions were equally distributed across 20 distinctive topics with 10 questions per topic: Microbial cell, Microbial metabolism, Microbial growth control, Viruses, Molecular aspects of growth, Genetics of prokaryotes, Microbial omics, Viral genomics, Microbial evolution, Microbial diversity, Ecology of bacteria, Phylogeny of bacteria, Microbial ecosystems, Symbioses with microbes-plants-animals, Symbioses with humans, Infection and pathogenesis, Person-to-person diseases, Vectorborne diseases, Foodborne diseases, and Eukaryotic pathogens.

All questions were additionally categorized into 5 levels of difficulty based on Bloom’s Taxonomy.15,20 Two independent faculty members classified each question by Bloom’s level, with disagreements resolved by discussion and consensus with a third reviewer. Level 1 - Remember: These require direct recall of factual knowledge, definitions, or basic concepts. Level 2 - Understand: These require interpreting, summarizing, or explaining concepts in one’s own words, often by identifying correct/incorrect statements or explaining relationships. Level 3 - Apply: These require the use of learned procedures (growth rate calculations, dilution series, antibiotic susceptibility interpretation) to solve a novel problem. The student must execute a method to find an answer. Level 4 - Analyze: These require breaking down information into parts, determining how concepts interact, and drawing logical conclusions from evidence or scenarios. They often involve reasoning through multi-step microbiological scenarios, predicting outcomes based on microbial interactions, deducing infection mechanisms from clinical or experimental data, and interpreting epidemiological patterns. Level 5 - Evaluate: These require making judgments based on criteria or standards, critically appraising microbiological evidence, and justifying decisions regarding experimental outcomes, diagnostic approaches, or infection control strategies. There were no Level 6 (Create) questions in the dataset. A representative example question for each Bloom’s level is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Data Collection

Each selected LLM was required to answer 200 questions for the testing phase. The ability of LLMs to select the single correct answer in MCQs was assessed on April 6–8, 2026. Due to the input limitations of different LLM systems, the questions were divided into 4 sets of 50 MCQs each. Each set was presented with the zero-shot prompt: In this research, you are a college student enrolled in a microbiology course. Answer 50 MCQs in the attached file. Generate the vertical list of correct answers: choose from A-E only, no numbers or explanations.

File: [Upload text file]. This process was replicated across three independent testing sessions, separated by 24-hour intervals, to assess response consistency and reliability, ensuring each attempt was conducted as a fresh conversation session without access to previous responses. Since the goal of this research was to establish LLMs’ knowledge baseline, we intentionally did not use more advanced prompts. The outputs were recorded in a Microsoft Excel (Microsoft®365) spreadsheet and evaluated for accuracy. The question order was held constant across the three sessions and was not randomized, which is noted as a limitation below.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations (SD), medians, ranges, and 95% confidence intervals, were calculated for each LLM. An 80% accuracy threshold was used as a visual reference to identify high-competency performance, representing strong mastery of microbiology content beyond minimum passing requirements. The 80% threshold was selected because it approximates the competency benchmark commonly used to define high mastery in prior LLM medical education benchmarking studies,2,3 rather than a formal passing standard.

Test-retest reliability was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) with a two-way mixed-effects model, absolute agreement, and single measures, which quantify the degree to which each LLM produced numerically consistent accuracy scores across three independent testing sessions. ICC values were interpreted using standard thresholds (<0.5: poor, 0.5–0.75: moderate, 0.75–0.9: good, >0.9: excellent). Statistical comparisons between LLMs used Fisher’s r-to-z transformation.

One-way ANOVA assessed differences in performance among LLMs, with post-hoc Tukey’s HSD tests for pairwise comparisons. Topic-specific performance was analyzed using two-way mixed ANOVA (7 LLMs × 20 Topics), where the LLM × Topic interaction term served as the error term for testing main effects. Performance across Bloom’s Taxonomy levels was examined using two-way mixed ANOVA (7 LLMs × 5 Levels). Tukey’s HSD post-hoc tests identified specific pairwise differences. Pearson correlation coefficients quantified relationships between Bloom’s level and performance for each LLM. A repeated-measures ANOVA framework was used, given the fully crossed, balanced design (all LLMs answered all questions in all sessions) and the absence of missing data.

Post hoc power analysis assessed the study’s robustness using noncentral F distributions. Effect sizes were calculated using eta-squared (η2) and Cohen’s f, with interpretations following standard guidelines (small: f < 0.25; medium: 0.25 ≤ f < 0.40; large: f ≥ 0.40). Statistical significance was set at P≤0.05. Analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

A total of 200 multiple-choice microbiology questions were used to evaluate the performance of seven LLMs across three independent testing sessions. The analysis encompassed overall performance, test-retest reliability, topic-specific evaluations, Bloom’s taxonomy difficulty-level assessments, and power analysis. A total of 4,200 responses (7 LLMs × 3 rounds × 200 MCQs) were analyzed.

Overall Performance

The seven LLMs demonstrated variable proficiency in answering microbiology multiple-choice questions, with a collective mean accuracy of 86.18% (SD = 30.57%). The 200 questions were distributed across five Bloom’s Taxonomy levels: Level 1-Remember (n=60, 30%), Level 2-Understand (n=52, 26%), Level 3-Apply (n=39, 20%), Level 4-Analyze (n=30, 15%), and Level 5-Evaluate (n=19, 10%).

Power Analysis

Post-hoc power analysis is reported here as a supplementary robustness check rather than as primary justification for the study design, given known limitations of post hoc power estimation. Analysis using noncentral F distributions demonstrated robust statistical power across all analyses. The one-way ANOVA achieved a power = 1.000, confirming adequate sensitivity to detect the observed medium-to-large LLM effect. For the repeated measures ANOVA, power exceeded 0.999 for both the LLM effect (F(6,114)=6.783) and the Topic effect (F(19,114)=4.777) in the topic-specific analysis, and reached 1.000 for both the LLM effect (F(6,24)=35.165) and the Bloom’s Level effect (F(4,24)=60.445) in the Bloom’s taxonomy analysis, substantially surpassing the conventional 80% threshold across all comparisons.

Discussion

Microbiology poses distinctive challenges for LLMs relative to other basic sciences, including continuously evolving microbial taxonomy and nomenclature, rapidly changing antimicrobial resistance patterns, and the need to integrate laboratory findings with clinical reasoning - factors that may contribute to the topic-specific variability observed here. This comprehensive evaluation of seven LLMs across 200 microbiology multiple-choice questions revealed substantial variability in performance and consistency, with important implications for the integration of AI into medical education. Our findings extend the growing body of evidence on benchmarking in basic medical science while providing the first systematic evaluation of Grok and Kimi in this context.

The collective mean accuracy across all systems (~86%) reflects a high overall level of microbiology knowledge, broadly consistent with evaluations in related disciplines: 81.1% in biochemistry2 and 91.1% in histology,3 and confirms that contemporary LLMs consistently exceed typical student benchmarks in foundational medical science. Among individual systems, Claude (89.83%), Grok (89.50%), GPT (88.83%), DeepSeek (88.00%), Kimi (87.00%), and Copilot (87.67%) formed a high-performing cluster, while Gemini (72.33%) lagged substantially behind. Claude’s superior performance is consistent with findings in biochemistry, histology,2,3 and physiology, in which Claude-2 outperformed ChatGPT-3.5 on conceptual MCQs.5 The consistency of Claude’s leading position across multiple basic science disciplines suggests a robust, stable representation of foundational knowledge in this model. GPT’s accuracy aligns with prior meta-analytic data showing ChatGPT-4 achieving 81% across medical licensing examinations,1 and its microbiology performance (~89%) represents a meaningful improvement over earlier versions, consistent with documented generational gains.1 DeepSeek’s relatively high accuracy (88.00%) contrasts with earlier reports of 78.68% on AMBOSS questions19 and domain-specific variation on USMLE benchmarks,18 confirming that DeepSeek’s performance is particularly responsive to question format and subject matter. The strong results for Grok and Kimi represent a meaningful contribution given the near-total absence of these platforms from the medical benchmarking literature.9,10 Their comparable accuracy to established models has practical significance: free-access or low-cost platforms represent viable alternatives for students in resource-limited settings, and their competitive performance suggests the LLM landscape for medical education is considerably more pluralistic than prior literature has indicated.

The most analytically significant finding is the dramatic divergence in response consistency across systems. Claude exhibited the lowest inter-attempt variability (mean SD across topics = 0.87%; max SD = 5.77%), while Gemini showed catastrophically poor reliability (mean SD = 16.99%; max SD = 57.74%). Gemini’s inconsistency was not diffuse but concentrated on specific topics: Ecology of Bacteria (100%, 20%, 90% across three sessions), Phylogeny of Bacteria (100%, 10%, 100%), Microbial Ecosystems (90%, 20%, 90%), Symbioses with Microbes-Plants-Animals (100%, 0%, 100%), and Vectorborne Diseases (20%, 20%, 40%) - fluctuations of 20–80 percentage points between sessions indicative of session-level stochastic instability rather than genuine domain ignorance, since recovery to near-perfect scores in subsequent attempts demonstrates the underlying knowledge is present. This instability may partly reflect platform-level factors (such as backend model routing, sampling temperature, or silent version updates) rather than solely knowledge gaps, and cannot be fully disentangled with the present design. This pattern closely mirrors findings in histology and embryology12 and in pathology, where Bard exhibited far greater variability than ChatGPT across three sessions.13 As established in the literature, accuracy alone provides an incomplete characterization of a model’s suitability as an educational resource;11 this principle applies with particular force in microbiology education, where students rely on consistent guidance across repeated study sessions. GPT (mean SD = 2.52%), DeepSeek (3.07%), Kimi (3.47%), Grok (4.36%), and Copilot (5.47%) all demonstrated moderate-to-good consistency, positioning them as substantially more dependable supplements than Gemini despite their slightly lower mean accuracy in some cases. This reinforces the argument that reliability should be evaluated alongside accuracy as a primary criterion when recommending LLMs for educational use.

Topic-specific analysis revealed meaningful variation in difficulty across the 20 microbiology domains. Microbial Cell (100% across all systems and all attempts) represented a clear performance ceiling, while Microbial Evolution (94.8%), Genetics of Prokaryotes (94.3%), Phylogeny of Bacteria (94.3%), and Symbioses with Microbes-Plants-Animals (94.3%) were also consistently well-handled, suggesting these areas are robustly represented across contemporary LLM training corpora. In contrast, Viral Genomics emerged as the most challenging topic (mean 61.9%), followed by Foodborne Diseases (74.3%) and Microbial Diversity (76.2%). The difficulty of Viral Genomics likely reflects a convergence of factors: the rapidly evolving nature of the field, the complexity of connecting genomic organization to viral replication strategies and pathogenesis, and the integrative reasoning demands linking molecular mechanisms to clinical outcomes. Comparable subject-specific variation has been documented in virology MCQ evaluations.8 The near-uniform difficulty of Viral Genomics across all seven systems (with no model achieving above 70%) suggests this reflects a genuine knowledge boundary rather than model-specific gaps. The large LLM × Topic interaction observed in the ANOVA mirrors findings from anatomy evaluations,4 indicating that topic-specific model selection or complementary use of multiple systems may optimize educational outcomes in microbiology.

Performance across Bloom’s taxonomy levels showed that Levels 1–3 (Remember, Understand, Apply) were handled with broadly comparable proficiency across most systems, while Level 4 (Analyze) produced a convergent and substantial decline in all LLMs: Claude and GPT fell to 77.8%, Grok to 76.7%, Copilot to 76.7%, DeepSeek to 75.6%, Kimi to 74.4%, and Gemini to 62.3%. The approximately 12–15 percentage point drop between Level 3 and Level 4 across the leading models is noteworthy because it occurred uniformly across systems with otherwise divergent performance profiles, suggesting a shared structural limitation in handling multi-step analytical reasoning rather than a model-specific deficit. This universal plateau is consistent with prior observations that course type, rather than Bloom’s level, was the primary driver of variation in ChatGPT-4 performance,14 and with empirical challenges to the independence of Bloom’s taxonomy dimensions.16 Level 4 questions in microbiology typically require integrating epidemiological data, predicting outcomes from multi-step pathogen-host interaction scenarios, and evaluating experimental evidence - cognitive demands that appear to exceed the pattern-completion strategies on which LLMs predominantly rely. A parallel dynamic may operate in LLMs, as observed in students,15 where robust pattern matching supports strong performance up to a threshold, beyond which integrative reasoning is required. Collectively, these results suggest that multi-step analytical reasoning may currently pose a greater challenge for LLMs than lower-order recall or comprehension tasks in microbiology, though the modest number of Level 4 items (n=30) warrants caution in generalizing this as a fixed ceiling. LLMs may reliably support recall and comprehension learning, but expert verification remains advisable for higher-order reasoning tasks. This underscores the continued importance of human oversight in AI-assisted education.1

These findings are most directly relevant to how students and educators might use LLMs as supplementary study aids, rather than as general substitutes for instruction or clinical decision-making. Future work should also benchmark LLM performance directly against undergraduate students, postgraduate trainees, or subject-matter experts on the same question set, which would help contextualize whether LLM accuracy exceeds, matches, or falls short of human learners at comparable training stages.

Limitations

This study has several limitations: First, the assessment used only text-based MCQs, which may not fully capture LLM capabilities in open-ended reasoning or image-based interpretation, both of which are integral to microbiology education (eg, microscopy, Gram stains, growth curves). Second, zero-shot prompting was employed throughout; prompt engineering has been shown to significantly improve LLM performance,1 and our results may therefore represent conservative estimates of potential capabilities. Third, with only 10 questions per topic, within-topic variability may be undercharacterized, and topic-level estimates are subject to considerable sampling variability, particularly for topics with heterogeneous content such as Microbial Diversity; these estimates should be interpreted as indicative rather than precise. Fourth, answer correctness alone was assessed; explanation quality, which may not correlate with accuracy,5,6 was not evaluated, and a model that provides a correct answer with a flawed or misleading explanation may still pose educational risks. Fifth, question order was fixed rather than randomized across sessions, and while questions were drawn from a faculty-validated institutional bank with no AI-generated content, we cannot fully rule out the possibility that similar examination content was present in some models’ training data, which could affect the interpretation of absolute accuracy. The 24-hour retest interval captures short-term response stability but does not address longer-term drift associated with model updates. Sixth, formal item difficulty and discrimination indices were unavailable for the question bank used and were therefore not analyzed in this study. Finally, the study was conducted at a single point in time; as LLM architectures and training data evolve rapidly, findings may not fully characterize the capabilities of future model versions.

Conclusions

LLMs collectively demonstrated a high level of microbiology knowledge, consistently exceeding typical student performance benchmarks and confirming their educational potential in basic medical sciences for students and educators. Six of seven systems performed above the high-competency threshold, with Claude, Grok, and GPT leading the group. The strong performance of Grok and Kimi, reported here for the first time in a systematic medical education context, suggests that the field of educationally viable LLMs extends well beyond the established platforms that have dominated prior benchmarking literature. Critically, accuracy alone proved insufficient to characterize educational suitability: response consistency varied dramatically across systems, with Claude demonstrating near-perfect reproducibility while Gemini exhibited severe session-level instability — a pattern that poses a fundamental concern for any application requiring dependable guidance over repeated study interactions. Topic-specific performance was also highly variable, with foundational domains such as Microbial Cell and Microbial Evolution handled consistently well across all systems, while Viral Genomics, Foodborne Diseases, and Microbial Diversity posed near-universal challenges. The Bloom’s taxonomy analysis revealed a consistent and uniform decline in performance at the Analyze level across all seven systems, suggesting that multi-step analytical reasoning in microbiology currently exceeds the reliable capabilities of contemporary LLMs, regardless of overall accuracy. In summary, while LLMs demonstrate substantial and educationally relevant knowledge of microbiology, marked differences in reliability and topic-specific competence require careful, supervised integration into medical curricula, with human oversight remaining essential for complex reasoning tasks and topics where performance remains inadequate. These conclusions apply specifically to microbiology MCQ assessment performance and should not be extrapolated to LLM performance across the broader medical curriculum, which was not evaluated in this study. As LLMs continue to be updated, the comparative performance rankings reported here should be interpreted as reflecting the specific model versions tested, not fixed platform characteristics.
