Upregulation of metallophore-related genes of community-acquired methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus enhances pathogenicity in BALB/c mice
Authors: Feng Liao, Ning Zou, Yu Shi, Wenpeng Gu, Bing Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOur previous study revealed that ST59-t437-SCCmecIVa methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) has strong pathogenicity and can cause damage to host tissue and cell death. However, the interaction between the pathogen and the host during the infection process remains unclear. This study developed an infection interaction model of highly virulent ST59-t437-SCCmecIVa in MRSA and BALB/c mice.ResultsThe results revealed that the lungs were among the main organs involved, with high bacterial loads in the lung tissue of infected mice. WGCNA and Cytoscape analysis revealed that the metallophore-related genes of MRSA associated with the virulence factors of the strain formed an interaction network. Prokaryotic strand-specific RNA-seq analyses in vivo and in vitro further demonstrated that MRSA significantly upregulated metallophore-related genes during infection. The RT-qPCR results for genes such as sbnC, sbnI, cntA, hlgC, sspB, and pknB verified the interaction and pathogenic role of metallophores with virulence genes in the infection model. Animal hosts secrete large amounts of inflammatory mediators during infection, producing a strong inflammatory response that causes extensive hemorrhage and necrosis of host tissue cells. This led to a cytokine storm and ventilation dysfunction of the lung tissue and thus death.ConclusionsIn this study, through the pathogen-host infection interaction model, we thoroughly explored the changes in the gene expression during the interaction process from both the pathogen and the host, as well as the causes of death of the host animals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12262-w.Keywords: MRSA, Metallophore, Pathogen-host interaction, Pathogenicity

Background

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is currently one of the major problems associated with clinical infections and is capable of leading to high morbidity and mortality rates and imposing a significant healthcare cost burden on patients [1, 2]. Recent studies have shown that the distinction and characterization between hospital-acquired (HA)-MRSA and community-acquired (CA)-MRSA strains have become increasingly blurred, and researchers have proceeded to use molecular typing methods (MLST/spa/SCCmec) to characterize the prevalence of MRSA strains from different clonal groups [3–5]. In any case, the global incidence of MRSA has gradually increased; MRSA has become one of the most important pathogens of hospital- or community-acquired infections, causing serious clinical problems worldwide [6]. This pathogen causes an enormous public health burden in countries and regions such as Europe, America, Asia and China [7].

Compared with HA-MRSA, CA-MRSA has a broader molecular clonal population, which may be related to a shorter SCCmec cassette [3]. The prevalence of CA-MRSA phenotypes varies widely among regions worldwide. Currently, ST59 is the CA-MRSA genotype strain with the highest detection rate in China, Hong Kong and Taiwan [8]. However, the ST59 prevalent in these regions is not a single clone, with at least two different SCCmec subtypes (IV and V) and differences in antibiotic resistance. Our previous results revealed that the ST59-t437 MRSA strain produces two sets of MRSA evolutionary branches (ST59-t437-SCCmecIVa and ST59-t437-SCCmecVb), which belong to the ‘Asia-Pacific’ (AP) clone and the ‘Taiwan’ (TW) clone, respectively [9]. Compared with the ST239-t030 genotype, the ST59-t437 genotype strains presented faster growth, greater survival in human blood, and higher levels of toxin secretion and cytotoxicity [10]. Animal experiments revealed that ST59-t437-SCCmecIVa MRSA caused the most severe lethal effects in BALB/c mice and induced a host cytokine storm [11].

This study established a model of infection between ST59-t437-SCCmecIVa MRSA and BALB/c mice and explored in depth the gene expression changes in the process of interactions from both the pathogen and the host, as well as the pathogenetic factors that cause the death of the host animals.

Methods

Bacterial strain

The YNSA7 (ST59-t437-SCCmecⅣa) MRSA strain was used in this study (GenBank accession: CP179215). The bacterium was cultured at 37 °C on tryptone soy agar (TSA) (Thermo Fisher), and tryptone soy broth (TSB) (Thermo Fisher) was used for liquid enrichment of the strain.

In vitro transcriptome sequencing

Bacterial in vitro RNA-seq was performed according to previous study [11]. Briefly, MRSA strain was cultured overnight and diluted with TSB at 100 times, then kept at 37 °C with shaken to obtain log-phase isolate. The bacteria were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 5 min, and supernatants were removed. Total RNA of bacteria was extracted using RNAiso Plus reagent (TaKaRa) according to manufacturer’s instruction. The library preparation and sequencing procedures for bacterial prokaryotic RNA-seq were described in previous report [11]. The experiment comprised three biological replicates.

Bacterial load quantification

YNSA7 strain was cultured as described above. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation during the logarithmic phase, then adjusted to a uniform concentration using sterile PBS for plate counting. Four concentration gradients were established at 10⁸, 10⁷, 10⁶, and 10⁵ CFU/ml. One millilitre of bacterial suspension was taken from each concentration, centrifuged, and the supernatant discarded. Total bacterial RNA was extracted from the four concentrations using RNAiso Plus reagent (TaKaRa) as described above. The mRNA of the gyrB gene was amplified via the One Step TB Green PrimeScript RT-PCR Kit II (TaKaRa). The primer sequences are shown in Table 1. A quantitative curve was generated based on amplification CT values and bacterial log (CFU) values.

Pathogen-host interaction model

BALB/c mice were used to develop the pathogen-host interaction model (Fig. 1a) [10]. All the experimental mice were purchased from the Institute of Medical Biology, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College. The animals were divided into two groups: the YNSA7 infection group and the control group. The mice were infected via intravenous injection of 2 × 108 CFU of the MRSA strain. The control group of mice received the same volume of PBS as the infected group. Each group included six animals. The animals were observed daily for one week, and at seven days after infection, the BALB/c mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation.

Schematic diagram and histopathological examination of the interaction model between BALB/c mice and the MRSA strain. a Model establishment and experimental design of the interaction between BALB/c mice and the MRSA strain; b Histopathological examination of the lungs of the infection group and the control group; c Histopathological examination of the spleens of the infection group and the control group

Histopathological examination

Histopathological analyses were performed for heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney of animals, and the tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained and examined via the HE method [11]. Total RNA from the lung and spleen tissues of the MRSA strain-infected group and the control group was extracted via RNAiso Plus reagent (TaKaRa). The mRNA of the gyrB gene was amplified via the One Step TB Green PrimeScript RT-PCR Kit II (TaKaRa) as described above. The bacterial load of the YNSA7 strain in the lungs and spleen of BALB/c mice was calculated on the basis of the quantitative curve of the gyrB gene as mentioned above.

Transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA from the lungs and spleens of the mice in the infection group and the control group was extracted via RNAiso Plus, and the samples were quality checked via a Thermo NanoDrop One and Agilent 4200 Tape Station. For prokaryotic strand-specific RNA-seq, ribosomal RNA was removed via the Epicenter Ribo-Zero rRNA Removal Kit. Library construction was performed via the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina. The mRNA was fragmented into short fragments to synthesize cDNA. The purified double-stranded cDNA was end-repaired and A-tailed, and sequencing adapters were ligated, followed by purification via AMPure XP beads and, finally, PCR amplification to obtain the library. PE150 sequencing was performed via the Illumina NovaSeq sequencing platform. For eukaryotic RNA-seq, magnetic beads with oligo (dT) were used to enrich eukaryotic mRNAs. The process of constructing the cDNA library was as described above. PE150 sequencing was performed via the Illumina NovaSeq sequencing platform.

Differential gene expression between pathogen and host

The raw data were filtered and cleaned via fastp, and paired-end clean reads were mapped to the YNSA7 genome (GenBank accession: CP179215) via HISAT2. 2.4 [12]. Correlation and principal component analysis (PCA) were performed with the R package. Differential expression gene (DEG) analysis was performed via DESeq2 software among the different groups [13]. The genes/transcripts for which the FDR was less than 0.05 and the absolute fold change was ≥ 2 were considered differentially expressed genes/transcripts [14, 15]. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses were performed for the differentially expressed mRNAs [16, 17]. The prokaryotic DEGs were compared between the MRSA transcripts (in vivo) and the YNSA7 transcripts in vitro. The eukaryotic DEGs analysis process for the BALB/c mice was the same as that described above. After quality control, the data were mapped to the mouse reference genome (GenBank accession: GCA_000001635.9). The eukaryotic transcripts from BALB/c mice infected with the YNSA7 strain were compared with those from the uninfected control group.

WGCNA and interaction network analysis

Weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA) was used to mine the correlations between the gene expression data modules and the experimental phenotype characteristics [18, 19]. The expression correlation between genes was calculated via the Pearson correlation coefficient to generate the similarity matrix. Through the identification of gene modules, an association analysis between modules and traits was performed. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses and screening of key genes were subsequently performed. A network interaction diagram of key genes was drawn via Cytoscape [20]. The phenotypic data of the experimental animals included age, sex, severity of lesions, infection dose, survival time, and infection outcomes.

Validation of RNA-seq data via RT-qPCR

The results of RNA-seq were validated through singleplex RT-qPCR [11]. Briefly, RNAiso Plus was used to extract the total RNA of bacteria in vitro, infected BALB/c mice and control tissues. RNA quality was assessed via a Thermo NanoDrop One and Agilent 4200 Tape Station. Real-time quantitative PCR according to the instructions of One Step TB Green PrimeScript PLUS RT-PCR Kit (TaKaRa) was used to analyze the relative mRNA expression of both MRSA and host selected genes. PCR amplification was performed by using a Bio-Rad CFX96 PCR system. Six significant DEGs in the YNSA7 strain involved in the pathogenesis of MRSA were selected, namely, sbnC, sbnI, cntA, hlgC, sspB and pknB. The expression of four DEGs, IL-6, IL-10, CXCL2 and CD80, in BALB/c mice was analysed for verification. The internal reference gene was gyrB for bacteria [21] and β-actin for animal host [22], respectively. 2−△△Ct method was used to calculate the relative mRNA expression results [22]. Each experiment was performed in triplicate. The PCR primers used in this study are shown in Table 1.

Statistics

The data were analysed via GraphPad Prism 8.0 software, and the results are expressed as x ± s. Data analysis was performed via a t test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and pairwise comparisons between groups were performed if appropriate. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Five of the six YNSA7-infected BALB/c mice died within one week, and one survived. All the mice in the control group survived. Histopathological examination revealed that the lungs and spleen were the organs exhibiting the most severe inflammatory response in the infected group of mice. Subsequent studies were therefore conducted using these two organs. The lungs of the mice in the infection group revealed that large areas of lung tissue had necrosis, the alveolar structure was unclear, and congestion was observed in many blood vessels (black arrow in Fig. 1b). The nuclei were condensed and deeply stained or fragmented and dissolved, and homogeneous eosinophilic material (green arrow in Fig. 1b) was widely observed, accompanied by a large amount of inflammatory cell infiltration (red arrow in Fig. 1b). The surface of the lung tissue of the mice in the control group was covered with smooth serosa, and there were no obvious abnormalities. The lung parenchyma comprises a large number of alveoli at the branches and terminals of the bronchus at all levels in the lungs, and the structure of the bronchi at all levels has no obvious abnormalities. No obvious inflammatory changes were observed. Histopathological examination of the spleens of the mice in the infection group revealed that the boundary between the white pulp and the red pulp of the spleen was not clear, and the shape of the white pulp was irregular; a large number of extramedullary hematopoietic cells and a large number of multinucleated giant cells could be found in the red pulp (yellow arrow in Fig. 1c). A moderate amount of granulocyte infiltration was observed (green arrow in Fig. 1c). The spleen tissue capsule of the mice in the control group was composed of dense connective tissue rich in elastic fibres and smooth muscle fibres with uniform thickness. The capsular connective tissue protruded into the spleen to form trabeculae, and there was no obvious abnormality; the spleen parenchyma was composed of red pulp, and white pulp. The boundary with the red pulp was clear.

The quantitative results for the gyrB gene are presented in Additional file 1a, with equation of the standard curve Y=−2.521X + 48.78. The CT values of gyrB obtained from the RT-qPCR detection results from the lungs and spleen of the infection group were substituted into the above standard curve equation to determine the bacterial CFU. The results revealed that the bacterial load in the lungs of the infection group was 8.56 ± 0.18 log (CFU), whereas the bacterial load in the spleen was 7.65 ± 0.13 log (CFU), which was significantly different (Additional file 1b). These findings suggested that the bacterial load of the YNSA7 strain in the lungs was higher than that in the spleen of BALB/c mice and that the lung was the main organ affected by infection. The RT-PCR results for the mice in the control group were negative.

WGCNA revealed that a total of 10 gene modules were screened on the basis of the phenotypic data of BALB/c mice. The cluster dendrogram between the phenotypic data and each gene module is shown in Fig. 2a. The brown, yellow, green, pink and black modules were correlated with the infection results in BALB/c mice. Further analysis revealed that the brown, yellow, green, and pink modules were positively correlated with the infection results, as shown in Fig. 2b. Specifically, the brown module was enriched mainly with genes associated with the ABC transporter system, two-component system and membrane transporter system in the bacterium. The functions of most of these genes are related mainly to MRSA virulence factors, pathogenicity, and transition metal chelation.

WGCNA between the phenotypes of BALB/c mice and gene modules. a The cluster dendrogram between different gene modules with phenotypic data of BALB/c mice; b Module-trait relationships between different gene modules and phenotype results

The genes in the brown module were further used to perform network interaction analyses and generate Cytoscape. The transition metal chelating genes sbnB, sbnC, sbnD, sbnE, sbnF, sbnH, sbnI, cntA, cntL and cntK constituted a complex interaction network, indicating that these genes are key factors in the pathogenicity of infection in the BALB/c mouse bacteremia model (Fig. 3a). The sbn operon (sbnBCDEFHI) encodes Staphyloferrin B, which is a siderophore produced by S. aureus. The cnt operon encodes a broad-spectrum metal chelator Staphylopine, comprising of nine genes (cntKLMABCDFE). In addition, these genes also formed an interaction network with MRSA pathogenic genes, such as femB and pknB. Cytoscape was performed for genes such as sbnC and cntA independently, and the results showed that these genes also constituted complex interactions with others, as shown in Fig. 3b and c. sbnC formed an interaction network with MRSA virulence factors, proteases, and hemolysin, whereas cntA formed an interaction network with bacterial protein transport and regulation genes.

Cytoscape analysis of critical genes in the BALB/c mouse infection model. a Cytoscape diagram of transition metal chelation-related genes and virulence genes in the brown module; b Cytoscape diagram of the sbnC gene; c Cytoscape diagram of the cntA gene

We used prokaryotic strand-specific RNA-seq to analyse the results and compared the prokaryotic transcripts in infected mice with the prokaryotic transcripts in vitro. The results of PCA revealed that the in vitro transcripts of MRSA and the transcripts infected in vivo formed their own clusters. In particular, the transcripts from the lung and spleen were similar but still formed different clusters (Fig. 4a). The datasets of MRSA prokaryotic gene differential expression in the lungs and spleen are provided in Additional file 2 and 3 respectively. The DEGs in infected lungs revealed that, compared with those in vitro, the number of upregulated genes in MRSA-infected mice was 30, while most of the genes (618) were downregulated (Fig. 4b). The GO annotations revealed that the upregulated genes were enriched mainly in bacterial catalytic activity, metal and ion binding, oxidation reduction activity, and coenzyme factor binding (Fig. 4c). The KEGG annotation results revealed that the upregulated genes were enriched mainly in metabolic pathways, such as bacterial glucose metabolism, energy metabolism, and amino acid metabolism (Fig. 4d). Six genes were selected to verify the RNA-seq results via RT-qPCR. Compared with those in vitro, the expression levels of the metal-chelating genes sbnC (P < 0.001), sbnI (P < 0.001) and cntA (P < 0.001) in MRSA were significantly higher in vivo (Fig. 4e-g). The expression of hemolysin hlgC (P < 0.001), cysteine protease sspB (P = 0.006), and serine/threonine-protein kinase pknB (P < 0.001) was also significantly higher in infected animals than in vitro (Fig. 4h-j).

The results of the eukaryotic RNA-seq analysis of the BALB/c mice are shown in Fig. 5. PCA revealed that the lungs and spleens of the infection group and the control group formed their own clusters (Fig. 5a). The datasets of eukaryotic gene differential expression in the lung and spleen of mice in the infected and control groups are provided in Additional file 4 and 5. Compared with those in the control group, 5,359 genes were upregulated in the lungs of the BALB/c mice in the infection group, and 3,985 genes were downregulated (Fig. 5b). The GO annotations indicated that the genes upregulated in the infection group were enriched mainly in protein activation, kinase binding, immune receptor activation, cytokine and receptor activation, and the binding of chemokines and adhesion molecules (Fig. 5c). The KEGG annotations revealed that the upregulated genes were enriched mainly in the functions of cytokine and receptor interaction, infection, oxidative phosphorylation, and cell adhesion (Fig. 5d). The RT-qPCR results revealed that the expression of interleukin-6 (IL) (P = 0.0002) and IL-10 (P = 0.005) in the infection group was significantly higher than that in the control group (Fig. 5e and f). Discussion

Our previous study revealed that ST59-t437-SCCmecIVa, ST59-t437-SCCmecVb and ST239-t030 MRSA were the major MRSA genotypes in Southwest China [9]. The biology and animal experiments of the three clonal strains suggested that the lung was the most severely damaged organ, especially for the infection caused by ST59-t437-SCCmecIVa. Severe inflammatory responses, tissue destruction, and massive exudation of inflammatory mediators and cells can be found in animals infected with the ST59-t437-SCCmecIVa MRSA strain. The infection caused by this strain caused a cytokine storm in BALB/c mice, which was characterized by high expression of IL-6 and IL-10 [11]. IL-6 intervention experiments confirmed that the massive secretion of IL-6 was the main cause of death in the hosts. We believe that ST59-t437-SCCmecIVa MRSA is currently the primary epidemic clonal strain in Yunnan Province and most areas of China. This clonal strain has strong pathogenicity and high virulence and can cause the death of host animals. However, previous studies focused on animal hosts, and the pathogenesis of bacteria has not been further analysed in depth. Consequently, this study established an interaction model between BALB/c mice and ST59-t437-SCCmecIVa MRSA to investigate the critical factors causing high virulence and pathogenicity of the strain.

Metals such as iron, copper, zinc, nickel, and cobalt play important roles in many biological processes [23]. Owing to their unique redox and inorganic chemical properties, these metals can serve as the catalytic centers of enzymatic reactions, help improve the overall stability of proteins, or act as signalling substances. During the process of microbial infection, metals are necessary for the survival and pathogenesis of microorganisms [24]. Pathogens have evolved various strategies to disrupt the metal chelation of their hosts. The competition for metals between pathogens and hosts is an important and continuous part of the pathogenesis of microbial infection. S. aureus can secrete secondary metabolites that enable it to colonize the host and cause infection through various mechanisms [25]. Metallophores are secondary metabolites that enable bacteria to adsorb metal ions from the surrounding environment. S. aureus has a high-affinity metal import system, which includes the acquisition and recruitment of metal ions and the function of metal chelates [26]. Staphyloferrin B is a siderophore produced by S. aureus, a bacterial pathogen known for its antibiotic-resistant strains such as MRSA. Siderophores are iron-chelating molecules that enable bacteria to scavenge iron from host environments, a critical process for bacterial survival and virulence [27]. The sbn operon encodes SB biosynthesis and efflux, and these genes have been implicated in virulence in infection models of S. aureus infection. Several studies on sbnC, sbnE and sbnI have been reported, and these genes play key roles in the regulation of virulence and gene expression in MRSA strains [28–31]. In this study, we found that the sbn operon presented significantly increased expression in an animal infection model. The sbn operon upregulated its expression in the BALB/c mouse infection model, thus increasing the virulence of the strain. Moreover, some of the sbn operon genes generated complex interaction networks with other genes, including staphylopine-related genes and other virulence factors of MRSA [32, 33].

Staphylopine is a broad-spectrum metal chelator biosynthesized by S. aureus that plays a central role in metal acquisition and bacterial virulence and is encoded by the cnt operon [34]. The cnt operon is encoded by nine genes (cntKLMABCDFE), where the three genes cntKLM are required for staphylopine biosynthesis [35]. However, cntABCDF encodes ABC transporters related to the staphylopine metal import system [36]. Previous study has shown that CntA plays an important role in metal ion recognition and the import process of staphylopine [37]. A recent study revealed that deletion of cntE led to virulence defects in strains, even in zinc-limited tissues [38]. Another study indicated that the uptake of iron, nickel, zinc, and cobalt by cntL and cntA mutants was reduced, and the pathogenicity of the strains was correspondingly reduced [24]. The abovementioned studies on the cnt operon demonstrated that cnt operon genes play important roles in the virulence and pathogenicity of S. aureus strains. In our study, genes such as cntA, cntL and cntK were highly expressed during in vivo infection of the MRSA strain, suggesting a key role of these genes in the high pathogenicity of the strain. On the other hand, the cnt operon and sbn operon constituted the transition metal chelation network of the strain. These findings suggested that in the host blood infection model, high expression of metal chelator-related genes in MRSA significantly improved the virulence of the strain. The strains employed in the aforementioned studies were either the Newman strain or the USA300 strain. Consequently, findings concerning metal chelation genes demonstrated consistent results across different clonal groups, reference strains, and clinical isolates.

Based on the findings of this study and previous reports above, we propose that MRSA metal-chelating associated genes play a pivotal role in pathogenicity within the mouse bacteraemia model. Specifically, genes such as sbnC, sbnF, sbnH, cntA, hlgC, and sspB were among the top 30 differentially expressed genes in vivo vs. in vitro in this study. The greater of the values of the differential expression genes, the more strongly they indicate these genes play a possibly important role in the course of MRSA infection.

Furthermore, histopathological examination of deceased mice revealed extensive haemorrhage and congestion in the lungs of infected mice, alongside tissue damage and destruction. The ability of hlgC and sspB to induce haemorrhage and tissue damage in the host explains the outcomes of phenotypic experiments. This finding is consistent with the aforementioned differentially expressed genes and WGCNA results. Consequently, we selected these genes for expression analysis.

Moreover, gyrB is the gene encoding the DNA gyrase B subunit in MRSA, and each bacterial cell contains a single copy of the gyrB gene [39]. Previous studies have demonstrated that this gene can distinguish and identify closely related bacterial species within various genera or families, and can also be used for quantitative PCR analysis by designing species-specific primers [40, 41, 42, 43]. In addition, the gyrB gene is widely used as one of the relative quantitative reference genes for Staphylococcus aureus or MRSA strains, exhibiting relatively stable expression levels under varying conditions. Consequently, this study designed a quantitative PCR assay targeting the gyrB gene to reflect bacterial load within infected animal organs.

From the perspective of host infection, the major cause of death of the mice in the interaction model was a severe inflammatory response in the lungs. The exudation and infiltration of many inflammatory mediators cause pulmonary ventilation dysfunction. A previous study revealed that cytokine storms, especially the secretion of IL-6 and IL-10, cause the death of mice. The results of this study also support the above conclusion. In the interaction model, the inflammatory response, cytokine and receptor activation, chemokines, and adhesion molecule activation were the primary pathways enriched with the upregulated genes in the host. Moreover, the lung is also the main organ and tissue involved in the infection model, causing severe inflammation. We consider that the inflammatory response induces haemorrhage of BALB/c mice, releasing metal ions which subsequently trigger upregulation of MRSA’s metal chelation-related genes. This further activates the expression of genes such as those encoding haemolysins, establishing a series of feedback mechanisms. These findings may offer strategies for future therapeutic approaches targeting bacterial metal acquisition systems.

Conclusions

This study developed an infection model of highly virulent ST59-t437-SCCmecIVa MRSA and BALB/c mice. The lung is one of the major organs involved in a mouse bacteremia model, and the MRSA strain increased pathogenicity by increasing the expression of metal chelation-related genes, causing extensive hemorrhage and necrosis of host tissue and cells. During the infection process, animal hosts secrete large amounts of inflammatory mediators, generate a strong inflammatory response, cause a cytokine storm, and lead to lung tissue ventilation dysfunction and eventually death.
