The Small but Versatile Cyclin‐Dependent Kinase Subunit CKS
Authors: Jie Yang, Bruce Bowerman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe cyclin‐dependent kinase subunit (CKS) remains the least well‐understood component of the tripartite cyclin‐dependent kinase (CDK) complexes that specify the proper sequence of events required to duplicate and segregate genomes during eukaryotic cell division. Our recent investigation of CKS requirements during Caenorhabditis elegans oocyte meiosis I and II documents an essential role for CKS in the execution of anaphase B and expands the known range of CKS influence on cell cycle regulation to a third class of E3 ligases. Our findings also further document how C. elegans oocytes use very different mechanisms to progress through these two sequential cell divisions that ultimately produce a haploid oocyte. In this Think Again article, we discuss our findings as they relate to previous studies of CKS in different model systems.

Introduction

While the cell division requirements for cyclin‐dependent kinases (CDKs) and their cyclin binding partners have received much attention for many years and are reasonably well understood [1, 2, 3, 4], the functional importance of the relatively small third subunit of these kinase regulatory complexes, called CKS for cyclin‐dependent kinase subunit, and the mechanisms by which it contributes to cell cycle progression, remain more puzzling [5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Our recent analysis of CKS requirements during oocyte meiotic cell division in the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans [10] provides an opportunity to further consider the different roles played by CKS family members during eukaryotic cell division.

Early work in yeasts first implicated CKS in cell division and found that it forms a complex with CDK. CKS was discovered in both fission and budding yeast as a gene product that, when overexpressed, could suppress some CDK alleles [11, 12]. Shortly thereafter, fission yeast null mutants were found to be inviable, arresting during mitotic cell division sometime after anaphase onset [13, 14, 15], and CKS proteins were shown to associate with CDK in cell extracts from both fission and budding yeast [11, 16]. While budding yeast CKS was initially reported to be essential, subsequent analysis found that null mutations are not lethal under standard growth conditions but do result in both heat‐ and cold‐sensitive lethality [17]. While yeast genomes encode only a single CKS gene, subsequent studies of insects, nematodes, mice, and humans have found that their genomes each encode two closely related CKS genes [18, 19, 20, 21] (Table 1). Nevertheless, budding yeast and both human CKS family members can substitute for fission yeast CKS [19, 22], indicating substantial conservation of function, and both human CKS family members can bind to CDK [19]. The nonessential requirements in budding yeast appear to be exceptional, as CKS genes in fission yeast, mouse, Drosophila, and C. elegans are essential for either viability or fertility (Table 1). The two mouse paralogs are partially redundant [20], while the two fly genes are not interchangeable and appear to act independently of each other [21]. In C. elegans, one of the two CKS family members, CKS‐1, is essential for viability and is at least partially nonoverlapping with CKS‐2 in its requirements [18].

While CKS genes were first found to be required for progression through mitosis, subsequent studies in budding yeast and vertebrates found that CKS also promotes entry into S‐phase [23, 24, 25, 26]. Curiously, the nature of the S‐phase and mitosis requirements for CKS differ substantially. Most studies indicate that CKS acts with CDKs during mitosis (see below), but it can act largely independently of them to promote entry into S‐phase. This has been studied most extensively in mammalian cell lines, where CKS functions as a co‐adaptor for the Skp1/Cullin 1/F‐box (SCF) E3 ligase, binding to both the Skp2 substrate adaptor and to one target, the p27 inhibitor of the CDK‐2/Cyclin A complex that mediates entry into S‐phase [27, 28]. Through its highly conserved anion pocket (Figure 1), CKS binds to a phosphorylated threonine residue in p27, with a different surface binding to Skp2. By promoting p27 ubiquitination and its subsequent destruction by the proteosome, CKS assists in CDK‐2 activation and entry into S‐phase. CKS appears to function largely independently of CDKs in promoting p27 destruction, although a CDK‐2/Cyclin A/p27 complex may deliver p27 to SCF/Skp‐2/CKS [27].

During mitosis, early studies concluded that CKS acts with CDKs to promote the phosphorylation and function of CDK targets [30, 31, 32], with more recent efforts identifying hundreds of CDK targets, many of which depend on CKS for their phosphorylation [22, 33, 34, 35, 36]. Further investigation of CKS promoting CDK kinase activity identified two ways in which it functions. Firstly, CKS with CDK was found to activate the Anaphase Promoting Complex (APC), the E3 ligase that ubiquitinates cell cycle regulators during mitosis [37, 38]. This activation occurs through the phosphorylation of two APC subunits, APC1 and APC3 [39, 40, 41, 42, 43], resulting in the displacement of an unstructured region of APC1 from the APC binding site for its cofactor Cdc20 that recruits two key regulators, cyclin B and securin, for ubiquitination [37, 38]. The subsequent destruction of cyclin B and securin then promotes entry into anaphase and exit from mitosis [3, 4]. Secondly, other investigations found that CKS acts with CDK to promote the multisite phosphorylation and thereby altered function of many CDK targets [22, 33, 34, 44, 45, 46, 47]. In this context, CKS recruits CDK/Cyclin complexes to substrates that have been primed by phosphorylation at initial sites. This recruitment occurs through the conserved anion pocket in CKS that binds to phosphorylated threonine [47], leading to the subsequent phosphorylation of additional target residues. Indeed, a recent analysis in fission yeast indicates that CKS is required for the phosphorylation of nearly half of the hundreds of CDK targets that have been identified [22], including some that are important for S phase and others for mitosis [22, 33]. Moreover, many of the sites that require CKS are low affinity CDK‐1 sites that lack a proline following serine or threonine. How many of the fission yeast CDK targets require the CKS anion pocket was not addressed, although an anion pocket CKS mutant in budding yeast was shown to reduce multisite phosphorylation of the Sic1 target [45]. Whether a similarly large fraction of metazoan CDK targets require CKS, and how many depend on the CKS anion pocket, is not yet known but could be addressed using similar genetic and proteomic approaches in model organisms and cell culture.

These two modes of CKS action overlap to some extent, in that the CKS anion pocket has been proposed to bind to phosphorylated residues in APC3, with CDK then phosphorylating APC1. CKS is known to bind to phosphorylated APC and to be required for APC3 phosphorylation [41, 43, 48], and APC3 phosphorylation is required for APC1 phosphorylation [39]. But whether the CKS anion pocket is required for binding to phosphorylated APC3, or for APC1 phosphorylation, has to our knowledge not been tested. Furthermore, while CKS acts in part by promoting the multisite phosphorylation of already primed CDK targets, CKS also appears more generally to elevate CDK kinase activity and hence the phosphorylation of some targets independently of any initial priming [22, 49]. Perhaps CKS enhancement of CDK kinase activity mediates APC3 phosphorylation independently of its anion pocket, with subsequent binding by the anion pocket then promoting APC1 phosphorylation. 6 of the 12 proline‐directed putative CDK‐1 phosphorylation sites that are conserved in mammalian APC3s contain threonine instead of serine, while only 4 of the 15 APC1 sites contain threonine [39], making such a mechanism plausible with the CKS anion pocket being specific for phospho‐threonine [47]. Finally, while many studies support CKS acting through CDK kinase activity, other studies have proposed additional mechanisms for CKS during the cell cycle, as will be discussed in a later section.

C. elegans CKS‐1

In C. elegans, one of the two paralogs, CKS‐1, was shown to be essential and required for proper meiotic and mitotic cell division, while the other, CKS‐2, does not appear to be essential based on RNAi knockdown. Moreover, RNAi knockdown of both CKS‐1 and ‐2 did not lead to any further defects relative to knocking down CKS‐1 alone, and any requirements for CKS‐2 remain unknown [18]. Our recent investigation of CKS‐1 requirements resulted in several new discoveries. We found that (i) while null mutations in CDK‐1 result in adult sterility [50], a null mutation in CKS‐1 results in maternal‐effect embryonic lethality; (ii) CDK‐1 and CKS‐1 uniformly co‐localize to multiple spindle and chromosome sites throughout the cell cycle during meiosis I and II and during the first embryonic mitosis; (iii) loss of CKS‐1 does not affect anaphase A but does result in a complete absence of anaphase B during meiosis I; (iv) the transition from anaphase A to B is defective during both meiosis I and II in cks‐1(‐) oocytes; (v) CKS‐1 acts through different mechanisms during meiosis I and II to promote anaphase onset; (vi) loss of securin during meiosis I leads to precocious chromosome separation; and (vii) anaphase onset during meiosis II requires that cyclin B3 be present to promote the destruction of cyclin B1. In the rest of this article, we elaborate on these findings and how they relate to previous work on CKS family members in other model systems.

C. elegans CKS‐1 and CDK‐1 Differ in Their Essential Nature but Nevertheless Co‐Localize Throughout the Cell Cycle

While cdk‐1 null mutations in C. elegans were found previously to result in adult sterility [50], we used CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing to delete in situ the entire cks‐1 open reading frame and found that homozygous null mutants are maternal‐effect embryonic‐lethal: they mature into fertile adults but then produce embryos that fail to hatch. Thus, CKS‐1 appears to have fewer essential functions than does CDK‐1, although one cannot rule out perdurance of maternally expressed CKS‐1 masking late fertility requirements during the relatively rapid development of C. elegans larvae. Nevertheless, our finding is consistent with (i) observations in budding yeast that CDK is essential but CKS is not [17], (ii) the maternal‐effect embryonic‐lethality caused by loss of Drosophila Cks30A [21], and (iii) loss of CDK in multiple model organisms resulting in a failure to enter mitosis [2, 3, 4], while loss of CKS results in later arrests during metaphase and anaphase (see below).

C. elegans cdk‐1 and cks‐1 null alleles result in different terminal phenotypes but using fully viable in situ mNeonGreen fusions and live cell imaging, we found that the encoded proteins uniformly co‐localize throughout oocyte meiosis I and II, and in the mitotic one‐cell zygote. Both proteins are present throughout the cytosol and at oocyte meiotic spindle poles and spindle microtubules, throughout the oocyte and zygote chromosomes with enrichment at their kinetochores, at the oocyte central spindle during anaphase, and at outer kinetochore‐related linear elements that are present throughout the assembling oocyte spindle and cortex during meiosis I. Both proteins also co‐localize with the outer kinetochore scaffolding protein KNL‐1 during both meiosis and mitosis. We further found that both CYB‐1 and ‐3 localize to these spindle and chromosome structures during oocyte meiosis, but whether CDK‐1 and CKS‐1 depend on CYB‐1 or ‐3 for their localization, and vice‐versa, remains to be determined. While studies in other model systems have similarly documented the localization of CDK and CKS orthologs to these sites during mitosis [51, 52, 53, 54, 55], we provided, to our knowledge, the first documentation of their uniform co‐localization throughout the entire cell cycle.

The persistent co‐localization of CKD‐1 and CKS‐1 is perhaps somewhat surprising, considering (i) their distinct null mutant terminal phenotypes, (ii) previous studies indicating that only about 5% of CKS is present in complex with CDK in budding yeast cell extracts [16], and (iii) the finding that while all of cyclin B from human HeLa cell extracts co‐sediments in sucrose gradients with CDK, nearly all CKS sediments as free monomers, with only a small fraction co‐sedimenting with CDK and cyclin B [56]. Whether this extensive lack of association is true in C. elegans remains unknown, and our results do not address whether their co‐localization always indicates that they bind to each other. Nevertheless, their persistent co‐localization throughout the cell cycle is consistent with the recent documentation in fission yeast that CKS is required for the phosphorylation of nearly half of the known CDK targets [22].

C. elegans Oocytes Lacking CKS‐1 Skip Meiosis I Anaphase B

Perhaps the most striking cks‐1 mutant phenotype we documented, using live cell imaging and multiple fluorescently tagged markers of cell cycle progression, was the precise and complete absence of anaphase B during oocyte meiosis I. Anaphase A precedes anaphase B in C. elegans oocytes, and we found that in cks‐1 null mutant oocytes, and in oocytes depleted of CKS‐1 using either RNAi or auxin‐induced degradation of in situ degron‐tagged CKS‐1, meiosis I anaphase A appeared normal, but the paired sister chromatids (bivalents) then failed to separate any further, and the oocytes instead transitioned rapidly into meiosis II prometaphase, followed by a highly defective attempt at meiosis II cell division. This result is consistent with the observation that, after sporulation of heterozygotes, fission yeast CKS null mutants arrest at some point during anaphase [15], and with the documentation of anaphase defects in some fly oocytes produced by homozygous Cks30A mutant mothers, based on immunostaining of fixed samples [21]. However, whether the yeast and fly defects are specific to anaphase B has, to our knowledge, not been determined. We further observed that, after acute auxin‐induced depletion of degron‐tagged CKS‐1 following the completion of meiosis I, anaphase A again appeared normal, but anaphase B was delayed and defective, although not entirely absent. The less severe meiosis II anaphase B defect could be due to incomplete CKS‐1 depletion, or to a difference in the mechanisms that mediate the transitions from meiosis I to II and from meiosis II to mitosis.

We also detected some earlier cell division defects in cks‐1 mutant oocytes. During prometaphase, the oocyte chromosomes occupied a smaller volume, although bipolar spindle assembly otherwise appeared normal. Furthermore, metaphase during both meiosis I and II was prolonged (see below), although aside from the delays, the transitions to anaphase A appeared normal. Similarly, in some fly oocytes lacking CKS function, earlier defects in meiotic spindle assembly were detected [21, 57], and it remains formally possible that earlier cell cycle defects in C. elegans cks‐1(‐) oocytes are indirectly responsible for the loss of anaphase B. Finally, mouse Cks1b and Cks2 are partially redundant, but loss of only Cks2 results in a metaphase I arrest in both oocytes and sperm [20]. As anaphase B precedes anaphase A in mouse oocytes [58], it is possible that a loss of anaphase B in Cks2 mutant oocytes prevents anaphase onset. While the general significance of our finding that oocyte meiosis I anaphase B is missing in C. elegans cks‐1 mutants remains unclear (Table 2), our data clearly indicate that CKS‐1 is essential for the execution of anaphase B during C. elegans oocyte meiosis I. CDK‐1/Cyclin B kinase activity is important for the proper execution of anaphase in human cell lines [51], which, together with our data from C. elegans, suggests that CKS may play an important role in target phosphorylations required for anaphase B.

Securin Function During C. elegans Oocyte Meiosis I and II

We explored the CKS requirement for timely progression through both metaphase I and II in C. elegans oocytes and found substantial differences in the regulation of cell cycle progression during these two meiotic divisions (Figure 2). Previous studies documented that meiosis I and II in C. elegans are unusual in that they each require a different E3 ligase. While the canonical APC is required for anaphase onset in meiosis I, a different E3 ligase, composed of a Cullin 2 scaffold and its associated subunits, is required for anaphase onset during meiosis II [60, 61, 62].

Summary of the genetic differences in how CDK‐1/Cyclin B/CKS‐1 regulates C. elegans oocyte meiosis I and II. During C. elegans oocyte meiosis I, CYB‐1 and ‐3 are redundantly and positively required for timely progression through metaphase, beyond any requirement for their destruction, while during meiosis II, CYB‐3 presence and CYB‐1 destruction are required for the proper timing of anaphase onset. Furthermore, anaphase onset during meiosis I depends at least in part on the destruction of securin mediated by CDK‐1, CKS‐1, and the APC, while during meiosis II CDK‐1/CKS‐1/CYB‐3 act through a Cullin 2 E3 ligase to promote the destruction of CYB‐1 and the onset of anaphase. Note that whether C. elegans APC activation requires the phosphorylation of APC1 and APC3, as in yeasts and mammals, is not known. Furthermore, which cyclin B family member(s) act with CDK‐1/CKS‐1 to mediate the destruction of securin during meiosis I is not known, with CYB‐1, ‐2 and ‐3 being redundantly required for CDK‐1 function during C. elegans oocyte meiosis [59]. During meiosis II, the Cullin 2 E3 ligase subunits (not shown) include the Elongin B/C family member ELC‐1, the substrate adaptor ZYG‐11, and the ubiquitin E3 ligase RBX‐1 [60]. Our findings further highlight the distinct nature of cell cycle regulation during these two sequential, closely spaced, and essential cell divisions. First, we found that CKS‐1 promotes anaphase onset during meiosis I in large part through the destruction of securin. Securin inhibits separase, the protease that cleaves a cohesin subunit to sever the linkage that holds paired bivalents together, permitting their spindle‐driven separation during anaphase [63]. We found that depletion of securin partially rescued the delay of anaphase I onset in cks‐1 mutants, consistent with CKS‐1 promoting securin destruction, perhaps through activation of the APC by CDK‐1/cyclin B/CKS‐1 phosphorylation, as in other model systems (see above).

By contrast, we found that securin depletion in cks‐1 mutants further delayed anaphase onset during meiosis II. Securin has been shown in budding and fission yeasts and in mammalian cell lines to not only inhibit but also to both stabilize separase and promote its loading onto paired sister chromosomes during mitosis [64, 65, 66, 67, 68]. The enhancement of the prolonged metaphase II in cks‐1 mutants appears to be due to a greater requirement for securin‐mediated loading of separase onto sister chromatids, relative to the loading of separase onto bivalents during meiosis I. Consistent with other studies of securin, we found that securin depletion led to a more rapid decrease in the levels of separase throughout meiosis I and II, with separase levels normally declining over time such that less is present during meiosis II. Thus, a greater requirement for securin in loading separase onto sister chromatids in C. elegans oocytes may simply reflect the continual loss of separase over time having a greater impact on loading during meiosis II.

Finally, we also found that stronger depletion of securin, using auxin‐mediated degradation, led to the precocious separation of bivalents before they reached the meiosis I metaphase plate. Due to the requirements for securin in stabilizing separase and promoting its loading onto paired chromosomes, loss of securin in other model systems often leads to a metaphase arrest, rather than precocious separation [63]. Indeed, precocious separation due to loss of securin has been documented in only one other context, during male meiosis in the plant Arabidopsis thaliana [69, 70, 71, 72]. In mouse oocytes, Shugosin prevents the premature separation of paired bivalents by recruiting a phosphatase to counteract the Aurora B‐driven phosphorylation of cohesin that promotes its cleavage by separase [63, 73, 74]. While Shugosin is expressed in C. elegans oocytes, it does not appear to regulate anaphase onset [75]. Thus, the absence of a role for C. elegans Shugosin in regulating cohesin may account at least in part for the precocious separation of bivalents observed after strong securin depletion.

Differing Roles for B Type Cyclins During C. elegans Oocyte Meiosis I and II

We also documented very different roles for type B cyclins during meiosis I and II in C. elegans oocytes. C. elegans CDK‐1 requires three B cyclins: CYB‐1, CYB‐2, and CYB‐3 [59, 76]. During meiosis I, CYB‐1 RNAi knockdown interferes with chromosome congression, and CYB‐3 knockdown delays and disrupts meiosis II anaphase chromosome segregation. CYB‐2 knockdown alone has no effect, but when CYB‐1, ‐2 and –3 are all reduced together, oocytes fail to enter meiosis I, as when CDK‐1 is knocked down [59]. We found that metaphase was prolonged both during meiosis I (after germline knockdown of CKS‐1 and in cks‐1 null mutant oocytes), and during meiosis II (after acute auxin‐induced depletion of degron‐tagged CKS‐1 following the completion of meiosis I). While CYB‐1 and –3 levels were both elevated in cks‐1 mutant oocytes, RNAi depletion of either CYB‐1 or –3 did not rescue the prolonged metaphase I in cks‐1 mutants, or delay progression through metaphase I in cks‐1(+) oocytes. Knocking down both CYB‐1 and –3 together also failed to rescue the prolonged metaphase I in cks‐1(‐) oocytes but did cause a modest delay in meiosis I anaphase onset in cks‐1(+) oocytes, consistent with redundant and positive roles for CYB‐1 and –3 during metaphase I, independent of their destruction. In contrast, RNAi knockdown of CYB‐1 partially rescued the prolonged meiosis II metaphase in acutely depleted cks‐1(‐) oocytes. Furthermore, RNAi knockdown of CYB‐3 in acutely depleted cks‐1(‐) oocytes led to a full metaphase II arrest, with the oocytes never entering anaphase II prior to entry into the first embryonic mitosis. Thus, CYB‐3 must be present, and CYB‐1 degraded for the proper regulation of anaphase onset during meiosis II. These results suggest that CYB‐3 is required for CYB‐1 destruction, and indeed we observed a failure to degrade CYB‐1 after CYB‐3 RNAi knockdown.

Our finding that CYB‐3 is required for CYB‐1 destruction and the proper timing of anaphase II onset is to some extent consistent with previous work in both Drosophila and mouse oocytes. Cell cycle progression during oocyte meiosis in Drosophila and mouse differs substantially compared to C. elegans. Fly and mouse oocytes arrest at metaphase of meiosis I and II, respectively, prior to ovulation and fertilization [77]. In C. elegans, developing oocytes arrest during diakinesis and then, in the presence of sperm, undergo maturation and progress further into meiosis I [78]. Nevertheless, in both mouse and fly oocytes, depletion of cyclin B3 also results in elevated cyclin B1 levels and meiotic arrests [79, 80, 81, 82, 83]. Thus, these different species may share some CYB‐3‐mediated regulatory mechanisms during oocyte meiotic cell division, despite the differences in when their oocytes arrest prior to fertilization.

How Does C. elegans Cyclin B3 Promote the Destruction of Cyclin B1 to Trigger Anaphase Onset During Meiosis II?

Our results indicate that cyclin B3 and CKS‐1 together are required for cyclin B1 destruction and anaphase onset during C. elegans oocyte meiosis II. However, our data do not address the mechanism by which CYB‐3 and CKS‐1 promote CYB‐1 destruction. Investigations in both Drosophila mitotic embryos and mouse meiotic oocytes suggest that cyclin B3 acts upstream of the APC to promote its activation by phosphorylation and thus mediate anaphase onset through the canonical destruction of securin and cyclin B1 [79, 80, 81, 82, 83]. Moreover, a genetic interaction with the APC adaptor Cortex/Cdc20 during embryonic mitosis suggests that Drosophila Cks30A also acts through APC activation [21].

Because oocyte meiosis II anaphase onset in C. elegans requires a Cullin 2 E3 ligase, rather than the APC, we have considered alternative models for how CYB‐3 and CKS‐1 might promote CYB‐1 destruction (Figure 3). One possible mechanism we proposed is that a CDK‐1/CKS‐1/CYB‐3 complex phosphorylates a subunit of the Cullin 2 E3 ligase, followed by the CKS‐1 anion pocket then binding to that phosphorylated residue to recruit CDK‐1/CKS‐1/CYB‐1 to the Cullin 2 E3 ligase for the ubiquitination and subsequent destruction of CYB‐1 (Figure 3A). This model follows from previous investigation of human cyclins A and B in cell culture [55, 84, 85]. These studies found that (i) fusion proteins, comprised of the N‐terminal degron sequences of either cyclin and all of CKS, were degraded with kinetics identical to the corresponding cyclin, while the N‐termini of either cyclin alone were degraded more slowly; (ii) CDK required binding to CKS for cyclin B to be degraded with proper kinetics; and (iii) the CKS anion pocket was required for the cyclin A::CKS fusion to be degraded with proper kinetics. Moreover, mammalian CKS can bind to and regulate not only the APC but also the SCF E3 ligase, as described earlier, and therefore might also interact with the Cullin 2 E3 ligase.

Our proposed model, in which the CKS‐1 anion pocket‐dependent recruitment of CDK‐1/CKS‐1/CYB‐1 to a CDK‐1/CKS‐1/CYB‐3‐phosphorylated Cullin 2 E3 ligase (Figure 3A), is consistent with CYB‐3 knockdown leading to elevated levels of CYB‐1 and a delay in anaphase onset. However, other models also can account for these observations. First, while a Cullin 2 E3 ligase is clearly required, genetic studies suggest that the APC may also be required for meiosis II in C. elegans [86]. If so, the APC could, for example, inactivate an unknown inhibitor of the Cullin 2 E3 ligase (X in Figure 3B), with CDK‐1/CKS‐1/CYB‐3 activating the APC, as proposed for mouse and Drosophila, to degrade factor X. The uninhibited Cullin 2 E3 ligase could then target CYB‐1 for ubiquitination and destruction independently of CKS‐1 (Figure 3B). Another possibility is that CDK‐1/CKS‐1/CYB‐3 phosphorylation simply activates the Cullin 2 E3 ligase, which then ubiquitinates CYB‐1 independently of CKS‐1 (Figure 3C). In both alternative models, CYB‐3 knockdown would also lead to elevated CYB‐1 levels and a delay in anaphase onset.

Other proposed CKS mechanisms seem unlikely to explain the regulation of meiosis II anaphase onset in C. elegans oocytes. Some studies concluded that CKS acts as a transcriptional activator [87, 88, 89, 90], and thus it is conceivable that transcriptional regulation of the cell cycle might be involved. However, C. elegans oocyte meiosis and early embryonic mitosis are thought to rely entirely on post‐transcriptional regulation of maternal gene products [91, 92, 93], and our documentation of a prolonged meiosis II metaphase after acute auxin induced depletion of degron‐tagged CKS‐1 effectively rules out transcriptional regulation. Furthermore, in Drosophila the interaction between mutant alleles of Cks30A and the Cdc20 ortholog Cortex was not associated with any changes in the transcript levels of all three fly Cdc20 paralogs [21], also arguing against CKS acting through transcriptional regulation. Finally, while CKS in yeasts and human cell lines acts extensively to promote the individual and multisite phosphorylation of many CDK targets [33, 34, 35, 47], cyclin B phosphorylation has not been found to be involved in its destruction [3, 4]. Future work directed toward assessing possible CDK or other kinase phosphorylation sites in subunits of the Cullin 2 E3 ligase (see Figure 3 legend), and their requirements for binding to the CKS‐1 anion pocket and for meiosis II anaphase onset, might provide further insight. It also would be interesting to examine the destruction kinetics of transgenically expressed fusions of the CYB‐1, ‐2, and ‐3 N‐termini to wild‐type and anion pocket mutant forms of CKS‐1, as was done for human cyclins A and B [84, 85].

Conclusions

CKS family members are highly conserved small proteins that promote cell cycle progression through diverse mechanisms, both by promoting and in some cases opposing CDK function. Our work on C. elegans oocyte meiosis expands the known influence of CKS to the Cullin 2 family of E3 ligases, in addition to its previously documented regulation of the APC and SCF E3 ligases, and identifies an essential role for CKS in the execution of anaphase B. We also found that C. elegans CKS‐1 promotes both the APC‐dependent destruction of securin during meiosis I, presumably through CDK‐1 activation, and the CYB‐3‐dependent destruction of cyclin B1, and hence CDK inactivation, during meiosis II. Our proposed mechanism for how CKS‐1 and CYB‐3 promote CYB‐1 destruction during oocyte meiosis II, illustrated in Figure 3A, revisits a seldom cited model in which CKS family members, through their anion pocket, recruit cyclins to E3 ligases to promote their ubiquitination and subsequent destruction.

By modulating some but not all CDK activity, CKS family members provide a useful tool for exploring cell cycle regulation. Further investigation of CKS, not only in C. elegans oocytes but also in other cell types, promises to further advance our understanding of how CDKs regulate cell division.
