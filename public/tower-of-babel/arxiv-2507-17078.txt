The Splitting Lemma in any Characteristic
Gert-Martin Greuel, Gerhard Pfister

Abstract




Abstract

We give a simple proof of the splitting lemma in singularity theory, also known as generalized Morse lemma, for formal power series over arbitrary fields. Our proof for the uniqueness of the residual part in any characteristic is new and was previously unknown in characteristic two. Beyond the formal case, we give proofs for algebraic power series and for convergent real and complex analytic power series, which are new for non-isolated singularities.

Introduction

The splitting lemma is of great importance for the classification of singularities. It says that a power series
f

splits (up to a coordinate change) as the sum of a quadratic part
f^{(2)}
plus a series
g
of order at least three, where
f^{(2)}
and
g
depend on disjoint sets of variables.
Moreover, the series
g
, the ”residual part”, is unique up to a change of coordinates.
The splitting lemma is well kown
for power series or differentiable functions

of order at least two
over the real or complex numbers.
This result, for differentiable functions, is due to René Thom, who used it for his classification of the 7 elementary catastrophes (see his book
[
Th75
]
, § 5.2D). Since then it has become an indispensable tool in singularity theory, in particular for the classification of singularities. Thom’s proof, especially for the uniqueness of the residual part, is fairly long and complicated and depends on a determinacy theorem of Tougeron.
A proof for convergent power series over the complex numbers can be found in
[
GLS07
]
and
[
dJP00
]
. A proof for the existence of the splitting for formal power series over algebraically closed fields of positive characteristic is sketched in
[
GK90
]
.

In this note we give a very simple proof of the splitting lemma (existence and uniqueness of the residual part) for formal power series over an arbitrary field. To prove the uniqueness of the residual part we use only the implicit function theorem and some tricky substitutions, in particular in characteristic two.

The existing of a splitting beyond the formal case is more involved and requires different methods. We prove it for algebraic power series over a
real valued field (using the nested Artin
approximation theorem) and for convergent complex and real analytic power series (using the existence of a versal unfolding).

We like to emphasize that we do not assume that the power series has an isolated singularity, as it was supposed in the classical complex analytic case.

for
i\leq k
.
Since
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial x_{i}}\in\langle l_{1},...,l_{k}\rangle
,
it follows that
\langle x_{1},...,x_{k}\rangle\subset\langle l_{1},...,l_{k}\rangle
and hence, with
l^{\prime}_{i}(x_{1},...,x_{k}):=l_{i}(x_{1},...,x_{k},0,...,0)
,
we get
\langle x_{1},...,x_{k}\rangle=\langle l^{\prime}_{1},...,l^{\prime}_{k}\rangle
. Hence
\det\big(\frac{\partial l_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\big)_{i,j=1,...,k}=\det\big(\frac{\partial l^{\prime}_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\big)_{i,j=1,...,k}\neq 0
.

2. As the coefficient matrix of the 2-jet of
f
has rank
k
, the
2
-jet of
f
can be transformed into
a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{k}x_{k}^{2}
by a linear change of coordinates, as follows from the classification of quadratic forms mentioned above. Hence, we can assume that

f(\boldsymbol{x})=a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{k}x_{k}^{2}+g(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
,
g\in\mathfrak{m}^{3}
.

We prove the existence of the splitting for formal power series by constructing coordinate changes of increasing order, providing a splitting modulo increasing powers of
\mathfrak{m}
, which converge formally to a formal coordinate change.
To do so write
f
as

f(\boldsymbol{x})=a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{k}x_{k}^{2}+f_{3}(x_{k+1},\dots,x_{n})+\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}\cdot g_{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\,,

with
a_{i}\neq 0
,
g_{i}\in\mathfrak{m}^{2}
, and
f_{3}\in\mathfrak{m}^{3}
. The coordinate
change
x_{i}\mapsto x_{i}-\frac{1}{2a_{i}}g_{i}
for

i=1,\dots,k
, and
x_{i}\mapsto x_{i}
for

i>k
, yields

f(\boldsymbol{x})=a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{k}x_{k}^{2}+f_{3}(x_{k+1},\dots,x_{n})+f_{4}(x_{k+1},\dots,x_{n})+\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}\cdot h_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})\,,

with
h_{i}\in\mathfrak{m}^{3}
,
f_{4}\in\mathfrak{m}^{4}
. Continuing with

h_{i}
instead of
g_{i}
in the same manner, the last sum will be of
arbitrary high order,
hence
0
in the limit. Moreover, the composition of the coordinate changes converges (in the
\mathfrak{m}
-adic topology) to a formal coordinate change such that the residual part

g(x_{k+1},\dots,x_{n})
in the theorem is a formal power
series.

It remains to prove that if
f
has an isolated singularity, then

f\ \rsim\ a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{k}x_{k}^{2}+g(x_{k+1},\dots,x_{n})

with
a_{i}\in K,a_{i}\neq 0
, and

g\in\mathfrak{m}^{3}\cap K\langle x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{n}\rangle
. This is a consequence of the following Theorem
2.2
since

f
is right equivalent to a polynomial by Corollary
1.7
(hence an algebraic power series) and the fact that algebraic power series are convergent (Lemma
1.4
).
∎

We prove now the splitting lemma for the ring of algebraic power series
K\langle\boldsymbol{x}\rangle^{alg}
.
We call a
K
-algebra automorphism of
K\langle\boldsymbol{x}\rangle^{alg}
an algebraic coordinate change.

Theorem 2.2

(Algebraic splitting lemma in characteristic
\neq 2
)
.

Let
K
be any field and
f\in\mathfrak{m}^{2}
be an algebraic power series with
\rank H(f)=k
. Then

f\ \rsim\ a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{k}x_{k}^{2}+g(x_{k+1},\dots,x_{n}),

with
a_{i}\in K,a_{i}\neq 0
. The right equivalence is given by an algebraic coordinate change, and the residual part

g\in\mathfrak{m}^{3}
is an algebraic power series.

g
is uniquely determined up to
right equivalence in

K\langle x_{k+1},...,x_{n}\rangle^{alg}
.

Proof.

Theorem
2.1
implies that

f(\bar{\varphi}_{1}(x),\ldots,\bar{\varphi}_{n}(x))=a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{k}x_{k}^{2}+\bar{g}(x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{n})

for a suitable
\bar{g}\in K[[x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{n}]]
of order
\geq 3
, and an automorphism
\bar{\varphi}=(\bar{\varphi}_{1}(x),\ldots,\bar{\varphi}_{n}(x))
of
K[[x]]
. From Theorem
5
we deduce the existence of
\varphi(x)=(\varphi_{1}(x),\ldots,\varphi_{n}(x))\in(K\langle\boldsymbol{x}\rangle^{alg})^{n}
and
g(x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{n})\in K\langle x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{n}\rangle^{alg}
such that

f(\varphi_{1}(x),\ldots,\varphi_{n}(x))=a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{k}x_{k}^{2}+g(x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{n})

and

\bar{\varphi}\equiv\varphi\text{ mod }\mathfrak{m}^{4},\bar{g}\equiv g\text{ mod }\mathfrak{m}^{4}.

This implies that
\varphi
is an automorphism of
K\langle\boldsymbol{x}\rangle^{alg}
and
g\in\mathfrak{m}^{3}
.

Now assume that

a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{k}x_{k}^{2}+g_{1}(x_{k+1},\dots,x_{n})\sim a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{k}x_{k}^{2}+g_{2}(x_{k+1},\dots,x_{n})

for

.
Theorem
2.1
implies that
g_{1}
and

are right equivalent in

. Theorem
5

implies that they are also right equivalent in
K\langle x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{n}\rangle^{alg}
.
∎

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
K
be an arbitrary field of characteristic
\neq 2
and
K\langle\boldsymbol{x}\rangle
be either
K[[\boldsymbol{x}]]
or
K\langle\boldsymbol{x}\rangle^{alg}
and
f\in\mathfrak{m}^{2}
.
Assume moreover that
K
coincides with its subfield

of squares. Then

with

uniquely determined up to right equivalence.
The same holds for

, with

a real valued field, provided

has an isolated singularity.

Proof.

If
K^{2}=K
, then
\sqrt{a_{i}}\in K
(this is the only condition we need) and the quadratic form
a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{k}x_{k}^{2}
is equivalent to

. The result follows from Theorems

and

.
∎

We are now going to prove the splitting lemma in the analytic case. An element
f
in
K\langle\boldsymbol{x}\rangle
,

K\in\{{\mathbb{C}},{\mathbb{R}}\}
with absolute value as valuation, is called an
analytic power series
and an automorphism of
K\langle\boldsymbol{x}\rangle
an analytic coordinate change.

Theorem 2.4

(Analytic splitting lemma)
.

Let
K
be either
{\mathbb{C}}
or
{\mathbb{R}}
and
K\langle\boldsymbol{x}\rangle
the usual convergent power series ring. Let

f\in\mathfrak{m}^{2}\subset K\langle\boldsymbol{x}\rangle
with
\rank H(f)=k
. Then

f\ \rsim\ a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{k}x_{k}^{2}+g(x_{k+1},\dots,x_{n}),

given by an analytic coordinate change,
with
a_{i}=1
if
K={\mathbb{C}}
and
a_{i}\pm 1
if
K={\mathbb{R}}
, and

g\in\mathfrak{m}^{3}
. The residual part

is an analytic power series that is uniquely determined up to
right equivalence in

K\langle x_{k+1},...,x_{n}\rangle
.

We do not require that
f
hs an isolated singularity. For the proof we need the notion of (versal) unfoldings, which can be formulated for any real valued field
K
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
f\in\mathfrak{m}\subset K\langle\boldsymbol{x}\rangle=K\langle x_{1},...,x_{n}\rangle
.

1.

An (r-parameter) unfolding of
f
is a power series

F\in K\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u}\rangle
,
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},...,u_{r})
, such that
F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{0})=f
.

If
F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u})=f(\boldsymbol{x})
we say that
F
is the constant unfolding of
f
.

2.

Let
F\in K\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u}\rangle
and
G\in K\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle
,
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1},...,v_{s})
,
be two unfoldings of
f
.
A morphism from
F
to
G
is given by a pair

(\Phi,\alpha):F\to G,

(i)

\Phi:K\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle\to K\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u}\rangle
, a
K
-algebra mormhism with

\Phi(x_{i})=:\varphi_{i}\in K\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u}\rangle
,
\varphi_{i}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{0})=x_{i}
,
i=1,...,n
,

\Phi(v_{j})=:\phi_{j}\in K\langle\boldsymbol{u}\rangle
,
\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{0})=0
,
\ j=1,...,s
, and

(ii)

\alpha\in K\langle\boldsymbol{u}\rangle
,
\alpha(\boldsymbol{0})=0
,

such that

F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u})=\Phi(G)(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u})+\alpha(\boldsymbol{u})
.

If this holds, we say that
F
is induced from
G
by
\Phi
. If
\Phi
is an isomorphism (which implies
r=s
), we say that
F
and
G
are isomorphic unfoldings.

3.

An unfolding
F
of
f
is called versal (or complete in the terminology of
[
GLS07
]
) if any unfolding of
f
is induced from
F
by a suitable morphism. A versal unfolding
F
with minimal number of parameters
r
is called miniversal.

The following theorem is due to J. Mather for
C^{\infty}
-functions.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
K
be
{\mathbb{R}}
or
{\mathbb{C}}
with the usual real valuations and
f\in\mathfrak{m}\subset K\langle\boldsymbol{x}\rangle
.

1.

The singularity
f
has a versal unfolding if and only if
\mu(f)
is finite.

2.

Two
r
-parameter versal unfoldings of
f
are isomorphic.

3.

Every versal unfolding of
f
is isomorphic to a constant unfolding of a miniversal unfolding of
f
.

4.

If
\{b_{1},...,b_{r}\}\subset\mathfrak{m}
represents a
K
-basis of

\mathfrak{m}/\langle\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}},...,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{n}}\rangle
,
then

F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u})=f(\boldsymbol{x})+u_{1}b_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})+...+u_{r}b_{r}(\boldsymbol{x})

is a miniversal unfolding of
f
.

Proof.

For a proof in the
C^{\infty}
-case see
[
Br75
]
. It uses the existence and uniqueness of the solution of an ODE (together with the smooth dependence of the initial conditions) to prove
an infinitesimal characterization of local triviality (similar to that in
[
GLS07
, Theorem 1.2.22]
). The theorem holds thus for the real and complex analytic case as well.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

As in the formal case we can assume that

f(\boldsymbol{x})=q+g
, where

q(x_{1},...,x_{k})=a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+a_{k}x_{k}^{2},\ a_{i}\in K\smallsetminus\{0\},

(in fact,
a_{i}=1
if
K={\mathbb{C}}
and
a_{i}=\pm 1
if
K={\mathbb{R}}
) and
g(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\mathfrak{m}^{3}
.
Setting
g^{\prime}(x_{1},...,x_{k}):=g(x_{1},...,x_{k},0,...,0)
we get

g(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=g^{\prime}(x_{1},...,x_{k})+h(x_{1},...,x_{n})
, with

h=\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}x_{i}h_{i}(x_{1},...,x_{n})
. Then
f=f^{\prime}+h,

h\in\mathfrak{m}^{3}
,

f^{\prime}(x_{1},...,x_{k}):=f(x_{1},...,x_{k},0,...,0)=q(x_{1},...,x_{k})+g^{\prime}(x_{1},...,x_{k}).

q
has Milnor number 1 and hence is 2-determined by
Corollary
1.7
.
Since
g^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{m}^{3}
, it follows that
f^{\prime}

is right equivalent to
q
by an automorphism
\varphi
of
K\langle x_{1},...,x_{k}\rangle
. Setting
\varphi_{i}(x_{1},...,x_{k}):=\varphi(x_{i})
,
we have

\varphi(f^{\prime})=q(\varphi_{1},...,\varphi_{k})+g^{\prime}(\varphi_{1},...,\varphi_{k})=q(x_{1},...,x_{k}).

Now define the automorphism
\psi
of
K\langle x_{1},...,x_{n}\rangle
by
x_{i}\mapsto\varphi_{i}(x_{1},...,x_{k})
for

i=1,\dots,k
, and
x_{i}\mapsto x_{i}
for
i>k
. Then

\psi(f^{\prime})=\varphi(f^{\prime})=q
and
we have

\psi(f)=\psi(f^{\prime})+\psi(h)=q+\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}x_{i}h_{i}(\varphi_{1},...,\varphi_{k},x_{k+1},...,x_{n}).

Thus, after applying
\psi
, we may assume that
f=q+h
,

h=\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}x_{i}h_{i}(x_{1},...,x_{n}).

That is
f(x_{1},...,x_{k},0,...,0)=q
, in other words,
f
is an unfolding of
q
.

Since
\langle\frac{\partial q}{\partial x_{1}},...,\frac{\partial q}{\partial x_{k}}\rangle=\langle x_{1},...,x_{k}\rangle
,
q
is a miniversal unfolding of itself by Theorem
2.6
and
f
is isomorphic to the
(n-r)
-paramter constant unfolding of
q
.
Hence this constant unfolding of
q
can be induced from
f
by an isomorphism
(\Phi,\alpha)
from
q
to
f
. By Definition
2.5
this means that
\Phi
is an automorphism of
K\langle x_{1},...,x_{n}\rangle
,
\Phi(x_{i})(x_{1},...,x_{k},0,...,0)=x_{i}
for
i=1,...,k
, and
\Phi(x_{i})\in K\langle x_{k+1},...,x_{n}\rangle,\Phi(0)=0
, for

such that
q=\Phi(f)+\alpha(x_{k+1},...,x_{n}).
The uniqueness of
g=-\alpha
follows from Theorem
2.1
.
This proves the theorem.
∎

3
Splitting Lemma in Characteristic 2

In this section let
K
be a field of characteristic 2.
We have the following classification of quadratic forms in characteristic 2, due to C. Arf in
[
Ar41
, Satz 2]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
f=\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}a_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}\in K[x_{1},...,x_{n}]
be a quadratic form. Then
f
is equivalent to the following normal form:

\sum_{i\text{ odd, }i=1}^{2l-1}(a_{i}x_{i}^{2}+x_{i}x_{i+1}+a_{i+1}x_{i+1}^{2})+\sum_{i=2l+1}^{n}d_{i}x_{i}^{2},

with
a_{i},d_{i}\in K
. Moreover, the form
\sum_{i=2l+1}^{n}d_{i}x_{i}^{2}
is uniquely determined by
f
up to linear coordinate change. The first sum is not unique but
2l
, the rank of
H(f)
,
is.

Proof.

Arf proves in
[
Ar41
, Satz 2]
that
f
is equivalent to

\sum_{i\text{ odd, }i=1}^{2l-1}(a_{i}x_{i}^{2}+b_{i}x_{i}x_{i+1}+c_{i+1}x_{i+1}^{2})+\sum_{i=2l+1}^{n}d_{i}x_{i}^{2},

with
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i},d_{i}\in K
,
b_{i}\neq 0
, together with the uniqueness statements. By setting
x_{i}\mapsto\frac{1}{b_{i}}x_{i}
for
i
odd,
i=1,...,2l-1
, and calling
\frac{a_{i}}{b_{i}^{2}}
again
a_{i}
, we get the required form.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Assume moreover that quadratic equations are solvable in
K
. Then
f
is equivalent to one of the following normal forms:

\begin{array}[]{llll}(a)&x_{1}x_{2}+x_{3}x_{4}+\ldots+x_{2l-1}x_{2l}+x_{2l+1}^{2},&1\leq 2l+1\leq n,\\
(b)&x_{1}x_{2}+x_{3}x_{4}+\ldots+x_{2l-1}x_{2l},&2\leq 2l\leq n.\end{array}

Proof.

For each
i
odd,
i=1,...,2l-1
, we make the following transformations:

First
x_{i}\mapsto x_{i}+ux_{i+1}
where
u
is a solution of
a_{i}u^{2}+u+a_{i+1}=0
, and then apply

x_{i+1}\mapsto x_{i+1}+a_{i}x_{i}

.

This transforms
a_{i}x_{i}^{2}+x_{i}x_{i+1}+a_{i+1}x_{i+1}^{2}\mapsto x_{i}x_{i+1}
. Moreover, let (after renumbering)
d_{i}\neq 0
for
i=2l+1,...,2l+k,\ k\geq 0
, and 0 else.
Setting now

x_{i}\mapsto\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{i}}}x_{i}
, if
2l+1\leq i\leq 2l+k
, we get for the last sum

x_{2l+1}^{2}+...+x_{2l+k}^{2}=(x_{2l+1}+...+x_{2l+k})^{2}
with
k\geq 0
. We get the normal form (b) if
k=0
. If
k\geq 1
we set
x_{2l+1}=x_{2l+1}+...+x_{2l+k}
and get form (a).
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Given a quadratic form
f=\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}a_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}\in K[x_{1},...,x_{n}]
, Arf introduces in
[
Ar41
]
the
vector space
V=Kv_{1}+...+Kv_{n}
with a metric defined by the bilinear form

v\cdot w=f(v+w)-f(v)-f(w)=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}a_{ij}(x_{i}y_{j}+x_{j}y_{i})

for the two vectors
v=\sum_{i}x_{i}v_{i}
and
w=\sum_{i}y_{i}v_{i}
. He proves then that
V
decomposes as an orthogonal sum
V^{\prime}\oplus V^{*}
with

V^{*}=\{w\mid w\cdot v=0\,\,\forall\,v\in V\}
the subspace of vectors that are orthogonal to all vectors of
V
.

V^{*}
decomposes as an orthogonal sum of 1-dimensional subspaces,

V^{*}=\oplus_{i=2l+1}^{n}\langle v_{i}\rangle
and
V^{\prime}
is the direct sum of 2-dimensional subspaces,

V^{\prime}=\oplus_{i\text{ odd, }i=1}^{2l-1}\langle v_{i},v_{i+1}\rangle
with
v_{i}\cdot v_{i+1}\neq 0
.
Since
\varphi(v\cdot w)=\varphi(v)\varphi(w)
it
follows that
V^{*}
is invariant under any linear coordinate change
\varphi
, while
V^{\prime}
is a non-invariant complement of
V^{*}
of even dimension. This proves the normal form of
f
in Theorem
3.1
and that the form
\sum_{i=2l+1}^{n}d_{i}x_{i}^{2}
is unique up to equivalence.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
K
be any field. The power series

f=\sum_{i\text{ odd, }i=1}^{2l-1}(a_{i}x_{i}^{2}+b_{i}x_{i}x_{i+1}+a_{i+1}x_{i+1}^{2})+\sum_{i=2l+1}^{n}d_{i}x_{i}^{2}+h(x_{1},...,x_{n}),

in
K[[x_{1},...,x_{n}]]
, with
a_{i},b_{i},d_{i}\in K
,
b_{i}\neq 0
, and

, is right equivalent (by a coordinate change mapping
x_{i}\mapsto x_{i}
for

) to

\sum_{i\text{ odd, }i=1}^{2l-1}(a^{\prime}_{i}x_{i}^{2}+x_{i}x_{i+1}+a_{i+1}x_{i+1}^{2})+\sum_{i=2l+1}^{n}d_{i}x_{i}^{2}+h^{\prime}(x_{2l+1},...,x_{n}),

with
a^{\prime}_{i}=\frac{a_{i}}{b_{i}^{2}}
and

h^{\prime}\in\langle x_{2l+1},...,x_{n}\rangle^{3}
.

Proof.

Write
h=f_{3}+x_{1}g_{1}+...+x_{2l}g_{2l}
with
g_{i}(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\mathfrak{m}^{2}
and
f_{3}\in\mathfrak{m}^{3}
not depending on

. The coordinate change

\begin{array}[]{lll}x_{1}&\mapsto\frac{1}{b_{1}}(x_{1}+g_{2}),&x_{2}\mapsto x_{2}+\frac{1}{b_{1}}g_{1},\\
x_{3}&\mapsto\frac{1}{b_{3}}(x_{3}+g_{4}),&x_{4}\mapsto x_{4}+\frac{1}{b_{3}}g_{3},\\
...\\

transforms

to

\sum_{i\text{ odd, }i=1}^{2l-1}(\frac{a_{i}}{b^{2}_{i}}x_{i}^{2}+x_{i}x_{i+1}+a_{i+1}x_{i+1}^{2})+\sum_{i=2l+1}^{n}d_{i}x_{i}^{2}+f_{3}+f_{4}+(x_{1}h_{1}+...+x_{2l}h_{2l}),

with suitable

. Now make the coordinate change
as above, but with

and get

\sum_{i\text{ odd, }i=1}^{2l-1}(\frac{a_{i}}{b^{2}_{i}}x_{i}^{2}+x_{i}x_{i+1}+a_{i+1}x_{i+1}^{2})+\sum_{i=2l+1}^{n}d_{i}x_{i}^{2}+f_{3}+f_{4}+f_{6}+(x_{1}k_{1}+...+x_{2l}k_{2l})

with suitable

.
Continuing with

instead of

in the same manner, the composition of the coordinate changes makes the

converges (in the
\mathfrak{m}
-adic topology) to a formal coordinate change, with the sum in brackets being 0 in the limit. This proves the lemma.
∎

Theorem 3.5

(Splitting lemma in characteristic 2)
.

Let char
(K)=2
.

1.

Let
f\in\mathfrak{m}^{2}\subset K\langle\boldsymbol{x}\rangle=K[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]]
,

any field. Then there exists an

.

2l

An analogous statement holds in the following cases:

(a) Let

Then
g\in K\langle x_{k+1},...,x_{n}\rangle

(b) Let

be any field and
f\in K\langle\boldsymbol{x}\rangle^{alg}
an algebraic power series.
Then
g\in K\langle x_{k+1},...,x_{n}\rangle^{alg}
and
g

K\langle x_{k+1},...,x_{n}\rangle^{alg}

Proof.

The existence of the splitting follows from Theorem
3.1
(applied to the 2-jet of
f
) and Lemma
3.4
.
By uniqueness of
g
we mean that if

f_{0}=\sum_{i\text{ odd, }i=1}^{2l-1}(a_{i}x_{i}^{2}+x_{i}x_{i+1}+a_{i+1}x_{i+1}^{2})+g_{0}\rsim f_{1}=\sum_{i\text{ odd, }i=1}^{2l-1}(a_{i}x_{i}^{2}+x_{i}x_{i+1}+a_{i+1}x_{i+1}^{2})+g_{1}

then

g_{0}(x_{2l+1},\ldots,x_{n})\rsim g_{1}(x_{2l+1},\ldots,x_{n})
, with

g_{j}=\sum_{i=2l+1}^{n}d_{i}x_{i}^{2}+h_{j},\ h_{j}\in\langle x_{2l+1},\ldots,x_{n}\rangle^{3},j=0,1.

To see the uniqueness,
let
\varphi
be a coordinate change given by

\varphi(x_{i})=\varphi_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})=l_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})+k_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}),\ i=1,...,n,

with
k_{i}\in\mathfrak{m}^{2}
and
l_{i}
linear forms with
\det\big(\frac{\partial l_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\big)\neq 0
, such that

\varphi(f_{0})=f_{1}
. This means

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sum_{i\text{ odd, }i=1}^{2l-1}(a_{i}\varphi_{i}^{2}+\varphi_{i}\varphi_{i+1}+a_{i+1}\varphi_{i+1}^{2})+\sum_{i=2l+1}^{n}d_{i}\varphi_{i}^{2}+\ h_{0}(\varphi_{2l+1},\ldots,\varphi_{n})=\\
&\sum_{i\text{ odd, }i=1}^{2l-1}(a_{i}x_{i}^{2}+x_{i}x_{i+1}+a_{i+1}x_{i+1}^{2})+\sum_{i=2l+1}^{n}d_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\ h_{1}(x_{2l+1},\ldots,x_{n}).\end{split}

(*)

By the uniqueness statement in Theorem
3.1
we may assume,
after a linear coordinate change among the variables
x_{2l+1},...,x_{n}
, that
l_{2l+1}=x_{2l+1},...,l_{n}=x_{n}
. Hence

\sum_{i=2l+1}^{n}d_{i}\varphi_{i}^{2}=\sum_{i=2l+1}^{n}d_{i}x_{i}^{2}+d_{i}k_{i}^{2}.

Comparing the terms of order
\geq 3
and setting

K_{i}:=k_{i}(a_{i}k_{i}+l_{i+1}+k_{i+1})+k_{i+1}(l_{i}+a_{i+1}k_{i+1})

we get

\sum_{i\text{ odd, }i=1}^{2l-1}K_{i}+\sum_{i=2l+1}^{n}d_{i}k_{i}^{2}\ +\ h_{0}(\varphi_{2l+1},\ldots,\varphi_{n})=h_{1}(x_{2l+1},\ldots,x_{n}).

We define for
i=1,...,2l
,

\begin{array}[]{llll}&F_{1}&:=l_{1}+a_{2}k_{2}+\sum_{1=2l+1}^{n}d_{i}k_{i}^{2},\\
&F_{i}&:=l_{i}+a_{i+1}k_{i+1},&\text{ if }i>1\text{ is odd}\\
&F_{i}&:=l_{i}+k_{i}+a_{i-1}k_{i-1}&\text{ if }i\text{ is even}.\\
\end{array}

Assume now that there are
\psi_{1},...,\psi_{2l}\in K\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rangle
satisfying

Then we replace
(x_{1},...,x_{2l})
by
(\psi_{1},...,\psi_{2l})

and define the endomorphism
\varphi^{\prime}
of
K\langle x_{2l+1},...,x_{n}\rangle

by

\varphi^{\prime}(x_{i}):=\varphi_{i}^{\prime}(x_{2l+1},\ldots,x_{n}):=\varphi_{i}(\psi_{1},...,\psi_{2l},x_{2l+1},\ldots,x_{n}),\ i=2l+1,...,n.

This kills

\sum_{i\text{ odd, }i=1}^{2l-1}K_{i}+\sum_{i=2l+1}^{n}d_{i}k_{i}^{2}

and we get for the terms of order
\geq 3

h_{0}(\varphi^{\prime}_{2l+1},\ldots,\varphi^{\prime}_{n})=h_{1}(x_{2l+1},\ldots,x_{n})

and thus
g_{0}(\varphi^{\prime}_{2l+1},\ldots,\varphi^{\prime}_{n})=g_{1}(x_{2l+1},\ldots,x_{n}).

To prove the theorem, we have still to show that the endomorphism

\psi
exists and that
\varphi^{\prime}
is an automorphism of
K[[x_{2l+1},...,x_{n}]]
.

Note that the map
(l_{1},\varphi_{2},l_{3},\varphi_{4},...,l_{2l-1},\varphi_{2l},\varphi_{2l+1},...,\varphi_{n})
is an automorphism of

since

is an automorphism and since

\varphi_{i}
.
It follows
\langle l_{1},\varphi_{2},l_{3},\varphi_{4},...,l_{2l-1},\varphi_{2l},\varphi_{2l+1},...,\varphi_{n}\rangle=\langle x_{1},...,x_{n}\rangle
and if we replace

by
\psi_{i}(x_{2l+1},...,x_{n})
for

, we get

\langle 0,...,0,\varphi^{\prime}_{2l+1},...,\varphi^{\prime}_{n}\rangle=\langle x_{2l+1},...,x_{n}\rangle
. This shows that
\varphi^{\prime}
is an automorphism of
K[[x_{2l+1},...,x_{n}]]
.

To show the existence of
\psi
we apply the implicit function theorem (Theorem
1.1
) to
F_{1},...,F_{2l}
. To do this, we must show
\det\big(\frac{\partial F_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}(0)\big)_{i,j=1,...,2l}=\det\big(\frac{\partial l_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\big)_{i,j=1,...,2l}\neq 0
.

and

. Hence
