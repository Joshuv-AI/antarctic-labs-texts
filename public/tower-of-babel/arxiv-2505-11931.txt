Abstract
. This work concerns a general system of energy-critical wave equations in the Minkowski space of dimension 1 + 3 1+3 . The wave equations are coupled by the nonlinearities, which are homogeneous of degree 5. We prove that any radial solution of the system can be written asymptotically as a sum of rescaled stationary solutions plus a radiation term, along any sequence of times for which the solution is bounded in the energy space. With an additional structural assumption on the nonlinearity, we prove a continuous in time resolution result for radial solutions. The proof of the sequential resolution uses the channel of energy method, as in the scalar case treated in [ DKM13 ] . The proof of the continous in time resolution is based on new compactness and localization arguments.

Introduction and main results

In this paper, we are interested with the following system of wave equations on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
:

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{tt}u-\triangle u&=f(u)\\
\vec{u}_{\restriction t=t_{0}}&=(u_{0},u_{1})\in\boldsymbol{\dot{\mathcal{H}}}_{rad},\end{aligned}\right.

where the unknown function
u
takes values in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
m\geq 1
,

\boldsymbol{\dot{\mathcal{H}}}_{rad}=\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}\times\mathbf{L}^{2}_{rad},\quad\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}=\dot{H}^{1}_{rad}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{m}),\quad\mathbf{L}^{2}_{rad}=L^{2}_{rad}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{m}),

by definition

V_{rad}
is the subspace of elements of
V
depending on
|x|=r
,
f
is a nonlinearity such that

(A0)

f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{m}),\quad\text{homogeneous of degree }5,

that is
f(\lambda x)=\lambda^{5}f(x)
for all
(\lambda,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
. In several part of the article, we will also assume that

is of potential-type, i.e

(A1)

f(u)=\nabla_{u}F(u),\quad F\in\mathcal{C}^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}),\quad\text{homogeneous of degree }6.

Note that (
A1
) implies (
A0
).
By the same proof as in the scalar case (see e.g. the references in the introductions of
[
Tao06
]
,
[
KM08
]
and subsections
2.2

2.3
below), if

is as in (

), the equation (
1.1
) is locally well-posed in
\boldsymbol{\dot{\mathcal{H}}}_{rad}
. The equation is invariant by scaling: if

is a solution, so is

. Furthermore, assuming (

), the solution

for all time

in its domain of existence

Hence we denote by

the energy of a solution

. Let

\mathcal{Z}:=\left\{Z\in\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}\setminus\{0\}:-\triangle Z=f(Z)\right\}.

If

E(Z):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla Z|^{2}\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}F(Z)\,dx\cdot

1.1.
Scalar case

Let

In the case

For the defocusing nonlinearity

In the focusing case

Denoting by

1.5.
Comments on the proofs

The proof of Theorem
1.2

The elements of

we prove the following rigidity theorem: any nonzero solution of (

2.3.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
\Gamma
be a domain of influence,
-\infty<t_{0}<t_{1}\leq\infty
,

. Let
A>0
. There exists
\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}(A)
with the following property. Let
(u_{0},u_{1}),\,(v_{0},v_{1})\in\boldsymbol{\dot{\mathcal{H}}}_{rad}(\Gamma_{\{t_{0}\}})
. Assume that

\displaystyle\left\|S_{L}(\cdot-t_{0})\big((u_{0},u_{1})-(v_{0},v_{1})\big)\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{5}\mathbf{L}^{10}(\Gamma_{I})}=\delta\leq\delta_{0}

\displaystyle\partial_{tt}v-\triangle v=f(v)+e,\quad\vec{v}_{\restriction t=t_{0}}=(v_{0},v_{1})

\displaystyle\|e\|_{\mathbf{L}^{1}\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Gamma_{I})}=\varepsilon\leq\delta_{0},\quad\|v\|_{\mathbf{L}^{5}\mathbf{L}^{10}(\Gamma_{I})}\leq A.

Let
u
be the forward solution of (
1.1
) on
\Gamma
, with initial data
(u_{0},u_{1})
at
t=t_{0}
. Then
u
is defined on
\Gamma_{I}
and satisfies:

(2.5)

\displaystyle\big\|u-v\big\|_{\mathbf{L}^{5}\mathbf{L}^{10}(\Gamma_{I})}\lesssim_{A}\delta+\varepsilon

(2.6)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in I}\left\|\vec{u}(t)-\vec{v}(t)\right\|_{\boldsymbol{\dot{\mathcal{H}}}_{rad}(\Gamma_{\{t\}})}\lesssim_{A}\left\|(u_{0},u_{1})-(v_{0},v_{1})\right\|_{\boldsymbol{\dot{\mathcal{H}}}_{rad}(\Gamma_{\{t_{0}\}})}+\delta+\varepsilon.

Proof.

Let
T_{+}=T_{+}(\vec{u}_{0},t_{0},\Gamma)
be the maximal time of existence of
u
. Let
T_{1}=\min(t_{1},T_{+})
. Let
\eta(t)=\|u-v\|_{\mathbf{L}^{10}(\Gamma_{\{t\}})}
, so that
\|u-v\|_{\mathbf{L}^{5}\mathbf{L}^{10}(\Gamma_{I})}=\|\eta\|_{L^{5}(I)}
for every interval

.

By Strichartz estimates, we have, for
t_{0}<t<T_{1}
,

\|\eta\|_{L^{5}([t_{0},t])}\lesssim\delta+\varepsilon+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\left\|f(u(s))-f(v(s))\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Gamma_{\{s\}})}ds\\
\lesssim\delta+\varepsilon+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\eta(s)\left(\eta(s)^{4}+\|v(s)\|^{4}_{\mathbf{L}^{10}(\Gamma_{\{s\}})}\right)ds

where we have used the Lipschitz property (
2.4
) of the nonlinearity. We thus have, for a constant

\|\eta\|_{L^{5}([t_{0},t])}\leq C\left(\delta+\varepsilon+\|\eta\|_{L^{5}([t_{0},t])}^{5}+\int_{0}^{t}\eta(s)\|v(s)\|^{4}_{\mathbf{L}^{10}(\Gamma_{\{s\}})}ds\right).

We first assume

\|\eta\|_{L^{5}([t_{0},t])}\leq 2C\left(\delta+\varepsilon+\int_{0}^{t}\eta(s)\|v(s)\|^{4}_{\mathbf{L}^{10}(\Gamma_{\{s\}})}ds\right).

depending only on

\|\eta\|_{L^{5}([t_{0},t])}\leq M(A)(\delta+\varepsilon).

Assuming

, where

is such that

, we obtain

2.4.
Profile decomposition

To prove Theorem
1.2
by the channels of energy method, we need to approximate a sequence of solutions of (

[

]
.

In
[
BG99
]
, the profile decomposition is constructed for scalar functions, however it is easy to generalize to vector-valued functions by working on each component. We will only treat the radial case. We thus consider a bounded sequence
\left(u_{0,n},u_{1,n}\right)_{n}
in
\boldsymbol{\dot{\mathcal{H}}}_{rad}
, and let
u_{L,n}
the corresponding solutions of the linear wave equation (
1.4
).
By
[
BG99
]
, there exists a subsequence of
\left(u_{0,n},u_{1,n}\right)_{n}
(that we still denote by
\left(u_{0,n},u_{1,n}\right)_{n}
) with the following properties.

There exist a sequence
(U^{j}_{L})_{j\geq 1}
of radial solutions of the linear equation (
1.4
) with initial data
(U^{j}_{0},U^{j}_{1})\in\boldsymbol{\dot{\mathcal{H}}}_{rad}
, and, for
j\geq 1
, sequences
(\lambda_{j,n})_{n}
,
(t_{j,n})_{n}
with
\lambda_{j,n}>0
,
t_{j,n}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying the pseudo-orthogonality relation

(2.11)

j\neq k\Longrightarrow\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\lambda_{j,n}}{\lambda_{k,n}}+\frac{\lambda_{k,n}}{\lambda_{j,n}}+\frac{|t_{j,n}-t_{k,n}|}{\lambda_{j,n}}=+\infty.

such that, denoting

(2.12)

U^{j}_{L,n}(t,x)=\frac{1}{\lambda_{j,n}^{1/2}}U^{j}_{L}\left(\frac{t-t_{j,n}}{\lambda_{j,n}},\frac{x}{\lambda_{j,n}}\right),

and

(2.13)

w_{n}^{J}(t,x):=u_{n}(t,x)-\sum_{j=1}^{J}U^{j}_{L,n}(t,x)

then

(2.14)

\lim_{J\rightarrow+\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\left\|w_{n}^{J}\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{4}\mathbf{L}^{12}(\mathbb{R}^{4})}=0.

One says that
(u_{0,n},u_{1,n})_{n}
admits a

with profiles

.

The following expansions hold for all

(2.15)

\left\|(u_{0,n},u_{1,n}\right\|_{\boldsymbol{\dot{\mathcal{H}}}_{rad}}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left\|\left(U^{j}_{0},U^{j}_{1}\right)\right\|_{\boldsymbol{\dot{\mathcal{H}}}_{rad}}^{2}+\left\|(w_{0,n}^{J},w_{1,n}^{J})\right\|_{\boldsymbol{\dot{\mathcal{H}}}_{rad}}^{2}+o_{n}(1).

The profiles are constructed as follows. Let

weakly in

3.
Radial stationary solutions of wave system

In this section, we describe some properties of the radial stationary solutions of (

\begin{array}[]{l}-\triangle u=f(u),\end{array}

satisfying (
A0

such that
\lim_{r\to\infty}rZ_{\theta}(r)=\theta
(see Subsection
3.1
). In Subsection

we obtain as a consequence of a rigidity theorem for equation (

. As a consequence, we prove that the

solutions of (

are
\mathcal{C}^{4}
. In subsection

, we consider nonlinearities

that satisfies (

\nabla_{u}F(u)
for an homogeneous function

. We give general properties of the set

3.1.
Asymptotic properties of radial, stationary solutions of wave system

In the proposition below we list some properties of the radial solutions of the elliptic system (

) that are defined, and in
\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}
, for large
r
, viewing (
3.1
) as an equation with an initial data at

]

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Then the following properties hold:

(i)

there exists
R_{\theta}\geq 0
,
C:=C(\theta)>0
, and a
\mathcal{C}^{2}_{rad}-
function
Z_{\theta}
of (
3.1
) on
|x|>R_{\theta}
such that

(3.2)

\begin{array}[]{l}r>R_{\theta}:\;\left|Z_{\theta}(r)-\frac{\theta}{r}\right|\leq\frac{C(\theta)}{r^{3}},\;\text{and}\;\left|Z^{{}^{\prime}}_{\theta}(r)+\frac{\theta}{r^{2}}\right|\leq\frac{C(\theta)}{r^{4}}\end{array}

Moreover we can choose
R_{\theta}
such that either
(a)
or
(b)
or
(c)
holds with

(a)

:
R_{\theta}>0
and
\limsup_{r\rightarrow R_{\theta}^{+}}\left|Z_{\theta}(r)\right|=\infty

(b)

:
R_{\theta}=0
and
Z_{\theta}\notin L^{6}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\right)

(c)

:
R_{\theta}=0
,
Z_{\theta}\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, and
Z_{\theta}
satisfies (
3.1
) in the sense of distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

(ii)

Let
R>0
and
u
be a solution in
\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}\left(|x|>R\right)
of (
3.1
) such that
\lim\limits_{r\rightarrow\infty}ru(r)=\theta
. Then
u(x)=Z_{\theta}(x)
for

|x|>\max\left(R,R_{\theta}\right)

Proof.

Since
f
is homogeneous of order
5
, the differential of
f
is homogeneous of order
4
. Hence the fundamental theorem of calculus applied
to
t\rightarrow f\left(u+t(v-u)\right)
yields

(3.3)

\begin{array}[]{l}|f(u)-f(v)|\leq C\left(|u|^{4}+|v|^{4}\right)|u-v|\cdot\end{array}

Let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
R\gg 1
, and
X_{R}:=\left\{u\in\mathcal{C}^{0}\left([R,\infty),\mathbb{R}^{m}\right),\,\|u\|_{R}:=\sup\limits_{r\geq R}r|u(r)|\right\}
.

Let
\overline{\mathcal{B}}\left(O,2|\theta|\right)
be the closed ball of
X_{R}
centered at
O
and with radius
2|\theta|
. Let

u\in\overline{\mathcal{B}}\left(O,2|\theta|\right)
. We define

\begin{array}[]{l}\Phi_{\theta}(u)(r):=\frac{\theta}{r}-\int_{r}^{\infty}s^{-2}\int_{s}^{\infty}\rho^{2}f(u(\rho))\,d\rho\,ds\cdot\end{array}

We claim that
\Phi_{\theta}
is a contraction. Indeed by direct computation and the claim above we see that for
r\geq R
and
(u,v)\in X_{R}\times X_{R}

\begin{array}[]{l}\left|\Phi_{\theta}(u)(r)-\frac{\theta}{r}\right|\leq\frac{C}{r^{3}}\|u\|_{R}^{5},\;\text{and}\\
\left|\Phi_{\theta}(u)(r)-\Phi_{\theta}(v)(r)\right|\leq\frac{C}{r^{3}}\|u-v\|_{R}\left(\|u\|^{4}_{R}+\|v\|^{4}_{R}\right)\end{array}

So we have a constructed a unique solution
u\in\overline{\mathcal{B}}\left(O,2|\theta|\right)
that satisfies
u(r)=\Phi_{\theta}(u)(r)
for

r\geq R
. Moreover the first estimate of (
3.2
) holds. Now by applying twice the fundamental theorem of calculus to
\Phi_{\theta}(u)
, we see that
u\in\mathcal{C}^{2}([R,\infty))
. Moreover

(3.4)

\begin{array}[]{l}Z^{{}^{\prime}}_{\theta}(r)=-\frac{\theta}{r^{2}}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\int_{r}^{\infty}\rho^{2}f(u(\rho))\;d\rho\end{array}

Hence the second estimate of (
3.2
) holds.

Next we consider a radial solution
u\in\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}(|x|>R)
of (
3.1
) such that

(3.5)

\lim_{r\to\infty}ru(r)=\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Clearly, in view of the radial Sobolev embedding

\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}(|x|>R)\hookrightarrow\mathcal{C}^{0}(|x|>R)
, (
3.1
), elementary properties of Sobolev spaces (such as the product rule and the composition rule), and the formula
\triangle f(x)=\frac{(rf)^{{}^{\prime\prime}}}{r}
, we see that
u\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(|x|>R)
. Hence integrating once
(
3.1
) between
r_{2}\geq r_{1}\gg 1
, using (
3.5
) and
\triangle f(x)=\frac{(r^{2}f^{{}^{\prime}})^{{}^{\prime}}}{r^{2}}
, we see from the Cauchy criterion for integrals that
r^{2}u^{{}^{\prime}}(r)
has a limit as
r\rightarrow\infty
; moreover
it is clear this limit can only be
-\theta
. Hence integrating twice (
3.1
) we see that

\begin{array}[]{l}u(r)=\frac{\theta}{r}-\int_{r}^{\infty}s^{-2}\int_{s}^{\infty}\rho^{2}f(u(\rho))\,d\rho\,ds\cdot\end{array}

Now from the formula above and
\lim\limits_{r\rightarrow\infty}ru(r)=\theta
we see that
u\in\bar{B}\left(O,2|\theta|\right)
for
R\gg 1
. Hence
the application of the fixed point theorem shows that
u=Z_{\theta}
.

We then consider the maximal interval
I_{\theta}:=(R_{\theta},\infty)
of the solution
Z_{\theta}
of the ordinary differential equation
z^{\prime\prime}+\frac{2}{r}z^{\prime}+f(z)=0
. Thus
Z_{\theta}
is well defined, and
\mathcal{C}^{2}
, for
r>R_{\theta}
. In particular it is in
\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}(|x|>R)
for all
R>R_{\theta}
. If
R_{\theta}>0
, the standard ODE blowup criterion ensures that

(3.6)

\lim\limits_{r\rightarrow R_{\theta}^{+}}|Z_{\theta}(r)|+|Z_{\theta}^{{}^{\prime}}(r)|=+\infty.

Assume now that
\limsup_{r\rightarrow R_{\theta}^{+}}|u(r)|<\infty
. Then this means that there exists
M<\infty
such that
|u(r)|\leq M
. But then from

\triangle f(x)=\frac{(r^{2}\partial_{r}f)^{{}^{\prime}}}{r^{2}}
and (
3.1
) we see that for
r,r_{0}>R_{\theta}
,

ru^{{}^{\prime}}(r)-r_{0}u^{{}^{\prime}}(r_{0})=-\int_{r}^{r_{0}}\rho^{2}f(u(\rho))\;d\rho
. Hence, using also that
f
is homogeneous of degree
5
we see from the Cauchy criterion
that the integral (and hence
ru^{{}^{\prime}}(r)
) converges as
r\rightarrow R_{\theta}^{+}
. This clearly contradicts (
3.6
). Thus in the case where
R_{\theta}>0
, (a) holds.

Assume now that
R_{\theta}=0
. Then if
Z_{\theta}\notin\mathbf{L}^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, we are in case (b). Assuming
Z_{\theta}\in\mathbf{L}^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, we must prove that we are in case (c). We first prove that
Z_{\theta}
satisfies the equation (
3.1
) in the sense of distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

We will denote
u=Z_{\theta}
to lighten notations. By standard ODE theory,
u
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
for
r>R_{\theta}
. It thus satisfies (
3.1
) in the classical sense on
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\}
. We let
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, and consider
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
such that
\psi(x)=1
for
|x|\leq 1
and
\psi(x)=0
for
|x|>2
. We let
\psi_{\varepsilon}(x)=\psi(x/\varepsilon)
. Then, since

is a solution of (

-\int\triangle u\left(1-\psi_{\varepsilon}\right)\varphi=\int f(u)\left(1-\psi_{\varepsilon}\right)\varphi.

Integrating by parts we obtain

\int u\varphi\triangle\psi_{\varepsilon}+2\int u\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\varphi-\int u\left(1-\psi_{\varepsilon}\right)\triangle\varphi=\int f(u)(1-\psi_{\varepsilon})\varphi.

One checks easily that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|\triangle\psi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{6/5}}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{6/5}}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|\psi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{6}\cap L^{6/5}}=0.

Using that

-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}Z_{\theta}\triangle\varphi\,dx=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f\left(Z_{\theta}\right)\varphi\,dx,

which proves that
u=Z_{\theta}
satisfies (
3.1
) in the sense of distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Hence
\triangle Z_{\theta}\in L^{\frac{6}{5}}
. Now by the interpolation inequality

\|\nabla Z_{\theta}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|\triangle Z_{\theta}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{L^{\frac{6}{5}}}\|Z_{\theta}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{L^{6}}
we conclude that
Z_{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}
.
∎

Example 3.2
.

We recall that
W
(defined in (
1.5
)) is solution to the equation
-\triangle W=W^{5}
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and that it is (up to scaling and multiplication by
\pm 1
) the only radial solution of this equation. Also,
W
is the maximizer for the critical Sobolev inequality

(3.7)

\forall\varphi\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),\quad\left(\int|W|^{6}\right)^{2}\int|\varphi|^{6}\leq\left(\int|\nabla\varphi|^{2}\right)^{3},

(see
[
Aub76
,
Tal76
]
) with equality if and only if
\varphi(x)=\lambda W(\mu x)
, where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mu>0
. It is natural to look for solutions of (
3.1
) of the form
\zeta W_{(\lambda)}
,
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. It is easy to check that
\zeta W_{(\lambda)}
is a solution if and only if
f(\zeta)=\zeta
. In this case, by the uniqueness in Proposition
3.1
and the asymptotics of
W
, we have
\zeta W_{(\lambda)}=Z_{\theta}
, where
\theta=\sqrt{3\lambda}\zeta
. Let us mention that this type of solutions always exist when
f
satisfies (
A1
) and
\mathcal{Z}
is not empty, see Proposition
3.9
.
In the case of the focusing Euclidean nonlinearity

f(u)=|u|^{4}u,\quad|u|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}^{2},

all
W_{(\lambda)}\omega
,
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
,
\lambda>0
, are elements of
\mathcal{Z}
. We will see in the next section that these are the only elements of
\mathcal{Z}
in this case (see Exemple
3.5
).

3.2.
Rigidity theorem and consequences

In the next section we will prove the following rigidity theorem, which is one of the crucial step for the proof of Theorems
1.2
and Theorems
1.7
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
R\geq 0
and
u
be a radial solution of
\partial_{tt}u-\triangle u=f(u)
on
\{|x|>R+|t|\}
with radial initial data
(u_{0},u_{1})\in\boldsymbol{\dot{\mathcal{H}}}_{rad}(|x|>R)
. Assume that

(3.8)

\sum\limits_{\iota\in\{+,-\}}\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\iota\infty}\int_{|x|\geq R+|t|}|\nabla_{x,t}u(t,x)|^{2}\,dx=0\cdot

Then there exists
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

\begin{array}[]{l}\left(u(t,x),\partial_{t}u(t,x)\right):=\left(Z_{\theta}(|x|),0\right),\quad|x|>R.\end{array}

A solution of (
1.1
) such that (
3.8
) holds is called
nonradiative
. We refer to
[
CDKM22
]
for a study of radial nonradiative solutions in general dimensions, in a scalar context.

We give here the consequences of Theorem
3.3
on the set of stationary solutions
\mathcal{Z}
.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
R>0
and
u
be a solution of (
3.1
) lying in
\mathbf{\dot{H}}_{rad}(|x|>R)
. Then there exists a (unique)
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

(3.9)

\begin{array}[]{l}|x|>\max(R,R_{\theta}):\;u(x)=Z_{\theta}(x)\cdot\end{array}

Proof.

If
R>0
then let
\Psi_{R}
be defined as follows:
\Psi_{R}(f,g)(r):=(f(r),g(r))
if
r\geq R
and
\Psi_{R}(f,g)(r):=(f(R),0)
if
r<R
. If
R=0
then
let
\Psi_{0}(f,g)=(f,g)
. Observe that by finite speed of propagation,
u
with data
\Psi_{R}(u,0)
satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
3.3
. Hence by application of this theorem we get (
3.9
) for some
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. The uniqueness of
\theta
follows from the uniqueness of limits.
∎

Example 3.5
.

Let
m\geq 2
,
f(u)=|u|^{4}u
as in Example
3.2
. We have seen that for all
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
,
\lambda>0
,
\omega W_{(\lambda)}\in\mathcal{Z}
. Since in this case
\omega W_{(\lambda)}=Z_{\theta}
, where
\theta=\sqrt{2\lambda}\,\omega
, we deduce from Corollary
3.4

\mathcal{Z}=\left\{\omega W_{(\lambda)},\;\lambda>0,\;\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}\right\}.

From Corollary
3.4
and Proposition
3.1
we infer the following corollary:

Corollary 3.6
.

The set
\mathcal{Z}
is exactly the set of
Z_{\theta}
,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{0\}
such that
Z_{\theta}\in\mathbf{\dot{H}}_{rad}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
i.e. such that
Z_{\theta}
is in case (c) of Proposition
3.1
.

Thus any element
Z
of
\mathcal{Z}
satisfies

\lim_{r\to\infty}rZ(r)=\lim_{r\to\infty}-r^{2}Z^{\prime}(r)=\theta

for some
\theta
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{0\}
. Moreover, for every
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{0\}
, there is at most one
Z
such that the preceding limits hold.

We conclude by proving that the elements of
\mathcal{Z}
are regular:

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{Z}
. Then the Kelvin transform of
u
,

v:r\mapsto\frac{1}{r}u\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)

is an element of
\mathcal{Z}
. Furthermore
u\in\mathcal{C}^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

Proof.

To prove that
u\in\mathcal{C}^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{m})
, it is sufficient to prove that
u\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, the conclusion will then follow by standard elliptic theory and a bootstrap argument, using that
f
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
. Since there exists
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{0\}
such that
u=Z_{\theta}
, we already know (see Proposition
3.1
) that
u\in\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, and thus that it is continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\}
. Using the equation
\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}(r^{2}u)=r^{2}f(u)
, we obtain that
u
is
\mathcal{C}^{4}
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\}
.

The Kelvin transform
v
of
u
is in
\mathcal{C}^{4}(\{|x|>R\})
for all
R>0
. By straightforward computations, using the properties of
u
, we see that
v
is in
\mathbf{L}^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, in
\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}(\{|x|>R\})
for all
R>0
, and that it satisfies the equation
-\triangle v=f(v)
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\}
. By Corollary
3.9
,
v=Z_{\tilde{\theta}}
for some
\tilde{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{0\}
. Since
v\in\mathbf{L}^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, we are in case
(c)
of Proposition
3.1
, which shows that
v\in\mathcal{Z}
. Also, we observe that

\lim_{r\to 0}u(r)=\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{1}{r}v\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)=\tilde{\theta},

and thus
u
can be extended to a continuous function on all
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

3.3.
Properties of the set of stationary states for a potential-like nonlinearity

In this subsection we list and prove some properties of
\mathcal{Z}
when the nonlinearity is of the form
f=\nabla_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}F
. We start by a general description of
\mathcal{Z}
(Proposition
3.8
). We will then prove that
\mathcal{Z}
is empty if and only
F
is nonpositive, and give an explicit element of
\mathcal{Z}
when it is not empty (see Proposition
3.9
below).

Proposition 3.8
.

Assume that
f
satisfies (
A1
) and let
F
such that
f=\nabla_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}F
. Then

(i)

for all
Q\in\mathcal{Z}
,
\int|\nabla Q|^{2}\,dx=6\int F(Q)\,dx
and
E(Q)=\frac{1}{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla Q(x)|^{2}\,dx
.

(ii)

There exists
m>0
such that for all
Q\in\mathcal{Z}
,
E(Q)\geq m
.

(iii)

\mathcal{Z}
is closed in
\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}
.
\mathcal{Z}\cup\{0\}
is closed in
\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}
-weak.

Proof.

Multiplying
-\triangle Q=f(Q)
by
Q
, integrating by parts, and using the
equality
u\cdot f(u)=6F(u)
(this is derived from the differentiation with respect to
\lambda
of

F(\lambda u)=\lambda^{6}F(u)
) we get

(3.10)

\begin{array}[]{l}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla Q|^{2}\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}Qf(Q)=6\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}F(Q)\,dx\cdot\end{array}

Hence
E(Q)=\frac{1}{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla Q|^{2}\,dx
.

We then prove (
ii
). Indeed from the Sobolev embedding
\dot{H}^{1}\hookrightarrow L^{6}
,
the bound
|F(u)|\lesssim|u|^{6}
, and (
3.10
) we see that there exists
C_{*}>0
such that

\begin{array}[]{l}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla Q|^{2}\,dx\leq C_{*}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla Q|^{2}\;dx\right)^{3}\cdot\end{array}

Hence
Q=0
or
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla Q|^{2}\,dx\gtrsim 1
. Since by the definition of
\mathcal{Z}
,
O\notin\mathcal{Z}
, we deduce that the second case holds. This yields (
ii
) using (
i
).

We then prove that
\mathcal{Z}
is closed in
\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}
. Indeed if
Q_{k}\in\mathcal{Z}
and
Q_{k}\rightarrow Q

in
\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}
as
k\rightarrow\infty
, we have
E(Q)\geq m
, and thus
Q
is not identically
0
. Furthermore

\triangle Q_{k}\rightarrow\triangle Q
in
\dot{H}^{-1}\hookrightarrow L^{\frac{6}{5}}
. On the other hand
f(Q_{k})\rightarrow f(Q)
in
L^{\frac{6}{5}}
: this follows from (
3.3
) and
Hence
-\triangle Q=f(Q)
, which shows
Q\in\mathcal{Z}
.

We next prove that
\mathcal{Z}\cup\{0\}
is closed in
\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}-
weak. Indeed assume that
Q_{k}\rightharpoonup Q
. Observe that The Rellich-Kondrachov compactness theorem shows that
Q_{k}\rightarrow Q
in
L^{5}_{loc}
. Thus
f(Q_{k})\rightarrow f(Q)
in
L^{1}\subset L^{1}_{loc}
: this follows from (
3.3
).
Letting
k\rightarrow\infty
in
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla Q_{k}\cdot\nabla\phi\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(Q_{k})\phi\,dx
for

\phi\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
test function, we get
-\triangle Q=f(Q)
. Since
Q\in\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}
, we deduce that
Q\in\mathcal{Z}\cup\{0\}
.

∎

Proposition 3.9
.

Assume that
f
satisfies (
A1
). Then

(3.11)

\mathcal{Z}\neq\emptyset\iff\exists u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\text{ s.t. }F(u)>0.

Furthermore, if (
3.11
) is satisfied, and
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
is such that

F(\omega)=\max_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}F(u),

then letting

(3.12)

\mu=\left(6F(\omega)\right)^{-1/4},\quad Z(x)=\mu W(x)\omega,

we have that
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
and

(3.13)

\int F(Z)=\min_{Q\in\mathcal{N}}\int F(Q),\\
\text{ where }\mathcal{N}=\left\{Q\in\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}\setminus\{0\},\;6\int F(Q)=\int|\nabla Q|^{2}\right\}.

Conversely any solution
Z\in\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}\setminus\{0\}
of the minimization problem (
3.13
) is of the form (
3.12
) where
\omega
is a maximum of
F
on
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
.

Remark 3.10
.

Observe that
F
always have a maximum on
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
by compactness. Moreover, if there exists
u
such that
F(u)>0
, then by the homogeneity of
F
, the corresponding maximal value of
F
on
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
is positive. Thus the proposition shows that if (
3.11
) is satisfied,
\mathcal{Z}
always has an element of the form

zW
,
z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{0\}
.

Remark 3.11
.

Since by Proposition
3.8
,
E(Q)=2\int F(Q)
for
Q\in\mathcal{Z}
, we see that the solutions
Z
of the minimizing problem (
3.13
) are ground states stationary solutions of (
1.1
), in the sense that they are nonzero stationary solutions of minimal energy. The proposition shows that these ground states always exist when
\mathcal{Z}
is not empty, and that they are of the form (
3.12
), where
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
is any maximizer of
F
.

Example 3.12
.

If
m\geq 2
and
F
is given by
F(u)=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}^{6}
(i.e. (
1.1
) is a system of
m
decoupled focusing equation), then for any
(\lambda_{j})_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{0\}
,
\left(W_{(\lambda_{j})}\right)_{1\leq j\leq m}
is an element of
\mathcal{Z}
. This shows that in this case, there are elements of
\mathcal{Z}
that are not of the form
\omega W
,
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. However, we do not know any example of nonlinearity
f
that satisfies (
A1
) such that
\mathcal{Z}
contains elements that are not of the form

\sum_{j=1}^{J}\omega_{j}W_{(\lambda_{j})},\quad\omega_{j}\in S^{m},\;\lambda_{j}>0.

Note that when
f(u)=(u_{1}^{5},5u_{1}^{4}u_{2})
(that satisfies (
A0
) but not (
A1
)),
(W,x\cdot\nabla W+\frac{1}{2}W)
is an element of
\mathcal{Z}
which is not of the preceding form. See also Example
3.15
below where we describe
\mathcal{Z}
in this case, and also the other examples in Subsection
3.4
.

Proof of Proposition
3.9
.

Step 1.
We first assume that for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
F(u)\leq 0
. Let
Q\in\mathcal{Z}
. Then by Proposition
3.8
, (
i
),
\int|\nabla Q|^{2}\leq 0
, which shows that
Q
is identically
0
, contradicting the definition of
\mathcal{Z}
.

In the next
3
steps, we assume

(3.14)

\exists u\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\quad F(u)>0.

Step 2.
By the homogeneity of
F
, there exists
u\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
such that
F(u)>0
. Let
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
such that

(3.15)

F(\omega)=\max_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}F(u).

In this step we show that
Z
defined by (
3.12
) is an element of
\mathcal{Z}
. Since
\omega
is a maximum of
F
on
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
, the gradient of
F
at
\omega
must be normal to the tangent space of
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
at
\omega
. In other terms, there exist
a\in\mathbb{R}
such that
f(\omega)=a\omega
. Using that

\omega\cdot f(\omega)=6F(\omega)
(this comes from differentiating
F(\lambda\omega)=\lambda^{6}F(\omega)
at
\lambda=1
), we obtain
a=6F(\omega)>0
. As a consequence
f(\omega/a^{1/4})=\omega/a^{1/4}
, which shows (see Example
3.2
) that
Z=\mu\omega W
,
\mu=a^{-1/4}
, is an element of
\mathcal{Z}
. By Proposition
3.8
,
6\int F(Z)=\int|\nabla Z|^{2}
. Thus
6\mu^{6}F(\omega)\int W^{6}=\mu^{2}\int|\nabla W|^{2}
, which yields (
3.12
) since
\int|\nabla W|^{2}=\int W^{6}
.

Step 3.
We next prove that the solution
Z
defined in the preceding step satisfies (
3.13
). We note that

(3.16)

\int F(Z)=\mu^{6}F(\omega)\int W^{6}=6^{-3/2}F(\omega)^{-1/2}\int W^{6}.

Let
Q\in\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}\setminus\{0\}
such that
6\int F(Q)=\int|\nabla Q|^{2}
. We have

(3.17)

\int F(Q)=\int_{Q\neq 0}|Q|^{6}F\left(\frac{Q}{|Q|}\right)\leq F(\omega)\int|Q|^{6}\\
\leq F(\omega)\left(\int\left|\nabla|Q|\right|^{2}\right)^{3}\left(\int W^{6}\right)^{-2},

where we have used the critical Sobolev inequality (
3.7
) on
\varphi=|Q|
. By the inequality
\nabla|Q|\leq|\nabla Q|
a.e. (see
[
LL01
]
), then the assumption
\int|\nabla Q|^{2}=6\int F(Q)
, we deduce

(3.18)

\int F(Q)\leq F(\omega)\frac{\left(\int|\nabla Q|^{2}\right)^{3}}{\left(\int W^{6}\right)^{2}}=F(\omega)\frac{\left(6\int F(Q)\right)^{3}}{\left(\int W^{6}\right)^{2}}

Hence

\left(\int W^{6}\right)^{2}\leq 6^{3}F(\omega)\left(\int F(Q)\right)^{2},

which yields, in view of (
3.16
), the desired inequality
\int F(Z)\leq\int F(Q)
, which proves (
3.13
).

Step 4.
To conclude the proof of the proposition, we must show that if
Q\in\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}\setminus\{0\}
is such that
6\int F(Q)=\int|\nabla Q|^{2}
and

(3.19)

\int F(Q)=\min_{R\in\mathcal{N}}\int F(R),

where
\mathcal{N}
is defined in (
3.13
), then (up to a rescaling)
Q=\mu\omega W
, where
\omega
,
\mu
are in (
3.12
), (
3.15
). Indeed, in this case, all the inequalities in the preceding step must be equalities. Assuming equality in the second inequality in (
3.17
), we obtain
|Q|=\mu W_{(\lambda)}
for some
c>0
,
\mu>0
(see Example
3.2
). Since we must have equality (almost everywhere) in the inequality
\nabla|Q|\leq|\nabla Q|
, we obtain (see
[
LL01
]
) that
Q=|Q|\omega
for a fixed
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
. Thus
Q=\mu\omega W_{(\lambda)}
, and the fact that
Q
satisfies the minimization problem (
3.19
) shows that
\mu
is as in (
3.12
), concluding the proof.
∎

We next give additional necessary conditions for a function
Q
to be in
\mathcal{Z}
:

Proposition 3.13
.

Assume (
A1
). Let
Q\in\mathcal{Z}
. Then

F(Q(0))>0\text{ and }F(\theta)>0,

where
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is such that
\lim_{r\to\infty}rQ(r)=\theta
.

Remark 3.14
.

Recall that Corollary
3.4
shows that there exists a (unique)
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

\lim_{r\to\infty}rQ(r)=\theta
.

Proof.

Let
Q\in\mathcal{Z}
. Then we see from Corollary
3.7
, that
Q\in\mathcal{C}^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Hence

(3.20)

\begin{array}[]{l}\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}Q+\frac{2}{r}\frac{d}{dr}Q+rf(Q)=0,r\geq 0\end{array}

Q^{\prime}(0)=0
,
Q\in\mathcal{C}^{2}([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We recall also that
Q=Z_{\theta}
and thus, from proposition
3.1

\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{2}\frac{dQ}{dr}=-\theta

.
Multiplying (
3.20
) by
\frac{dQ}{dr}
, we obtain,

\begin{array}[]{l}\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{1}{2}Q_{r}^{2}+F(Q)\right)=-\frac{2}{r}(Q_{r})^{2}\end{array}

Hence, integrating the above equality from
0
to
r
, and letting
r\rightarrow\infty
, we see that
F(Q(0))>0
.

Let
V
be the image of
Q
by the Kelvin transform, i.e
V(r):=\frac{1}{r}Q\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)
. From Corollary
3.7
,
V\in\mathcal{Z}
. Furthermore,
V(0)=\theta
. Hence we see from the previous
result that
F(V(0))=F(\theta)>0
.
∎

3.4.
More examples

In this Subsection we give other examples of nonlinearity satisfying (
A0
) or the stronger (
A1
) where we can compute
\mathcal{Z}
, or at least give some elements of
\mathcal{Z}
.

Example 3.15
.

Assume
m=2
and
f(u):=\left(u_{1}^{5},5u_{1}^{4}u_{2}\right)
, which satisfies (
A0
) but not (
A1
). Let
(u_{1},u_{2})
be an element of
\mathcal{Z}
. Then
u_{1}
satisfies

-\triangle u_{1}=u_{1}^{5}
. Thus
u_{1}
is identically
0
or
u_{1}
is of the form
\pm W_{(\lambda)}
for some sign
\pm
and some scaling factor
\lambda>0
. In the first case, we obtain that
-\triangle u_{2}=0
with
u_{2}\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Thus
(u_{1},u_{2})=(0,0)
, contradicting the definition of
\mathcal{Z}
. In the second case, we see that
(\pm W_{(\lambda)},u_{2})
is in
\mathcal{Z}
if and only if
u_{2}
is solution of the equation

-\triangle u_{2}=5W_{(\lambda)}^{4}u_{2}.

Note that
u_{2}=aY_{(\lambda)}
,
a\in\mathbb{R}
, give a family of solution of this equation, with
Y=x\cdot W+\frac{1}{2}W
. As a consequence, we have

(3.21)

\left\{\left(\pm W_{(\lambda)},aY_{(\lambda)}\right),\;\lambda>0,\;a\in\mathbb{R}\right\}\subset\mathcal{Z}.

We claim that

(3.22)

\left\{\left(\pm W_{(\lambda)},aY_{(\lambda)}\right),\;\lambda>0,\;a\in\mathbb{R}\right\}=\mathcal{Z}.

Indeed, looking at the asymptotics of
W_{(\lambda)}
and
Y_{(\lambda)}
, we see that the left-hand side of (
3.21
) is

\left\{Z_{\theta},\;\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{(0,\sigma),\;\sigma\in\mathbb{R}\}\right\}.

Furthermore, if
\theta=(0,\sigma)
with
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
, we see that
Z_{\theta}(r)=\left(0,\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)\notin\mathbf{L}^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, and thus that
Z_{\theta}\notin\mathcal{Z}
. This concludes the proof of (
3.22
).

Example 3.16
.

If
m=2
and
F(u)=\frac{1}{3}u_{1}^{3}u_{2}^{3}
, that is
f(u)=\left(u_{1}^{2}u_{2}^{3},u_{2}^{2}u_{1}^{3}\right),

it is easy to check that the maximum value of
F
on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
is
\frac{1}{24}
, and that it is attained at the points
\pm\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
. This corresponds to the two families of ground state solutions
\pm(W_{(\lambda)},W_{(\lambda)})
,

7.2.
An elementary property of compact sets

We next prove:

. Arguing as in Step 3, we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{j,n}^{1/2}(u-v_{L})(t_{n},\lambda_{j,n}\cdot)\xrightharpoonup{n\to\infty}\Phi^{j}\text{ weakly in }\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}\cdot

(7.28)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\lambda_{j}(t_{n})}{\lambda_{j,n}}=1

By the construction of
Q^{j}
in step 3, we also have

(7.29)

\lambda_{j}^{1/2}(t_{n})(u-v_{L})(t_{n},\lambda_{j}(t_{n})\cdot)-Q^{j}(t_{n})\xrightharpoonup{n\to\infty}0\text{ weakly in }\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}.

Since
K
is a compact subset of
\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}
, extracting subsequences, we can assume that there exists
\widetilde{\Phi}^{j}
such that

(7.30)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|Q^{j}(t_{n})-\widetilde{\Phi}^{j}\right\|_{\boldsymbol{\dot{H}}_{rad}}=0.

Combining (
7.27
),…,(
7.30
), we see that
\widetilde{\Phi}^{j}=\Phi^{j}
. Using (
7.28
) and (
7.30
) again, we see that the expansion (
7.22
) implies

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|\vec{u}(t_{n})-\vec{v}_{L}(t_{n})-\sum_{j=1}^{J_{0}^{{}^{\prime}}}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{\frac{1}{2}}(t_{n})}Q^{j}\left(t_{n},\frac{\cdot}{\lambda_{j}(t_{n})}\right),0\right)\right\|_{\boldsymbol{\dot{\mathcal{H}}}_{rad}}=0,

contradicting (
7.26
). The proof is complete.
∎

8.
Higher dimensions

In this section we discuss the generalization to higher dimensions of the results above, i.e. the equation (
1.1
) on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N\geq 4
, where

\boldsymbol{\dot{\mathcal{H}}}_{rad}=\dot{H}^{1}_{rad}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{m})\times L^{2}_{rad}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{m}),

and
f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{0\},\mathbb{R}^{m})
is homogeneous of degree
\frac{N+2}{N-2}
(note that due to the homogeneity, it is not possible to have nonlinearities that are
\mathcal{C}^{2}
at the origin if
N\geq 7
).

The major obstruction to the adaptation of the proof of Theorems
1.2
,
1.7
and Theorem
3.3
to higher dimension is the fact that the lower bound of the exterior energy given by Proposition
4.1
of solutions of linear wave equation must be replaced by a weaker analog in dimension
N\geq 4
: see
[
CKS14
]
,
[
KLLS15
]
,
[
DKMM22
]
,
[
LSW24
]
.

Nevertheless, in dimension
4
, for the scalar equation
\partial_{tt}u-\triangle u=u^{3}
, the analogs of the continuous soliton resolution, Theorem
1.7
, and the rigidity theorem, Theorem
3.3
are valid, see
[
DKMM22
]
. We conjecture that these theorems will remain valid for a general system of equation of the form (
1.1
) with
f
homogeneous of degree
3
. We note however that the proof of
[
DKMM22
]
does not adapt completely to the case of systems, since it uses arguments on the sign of the solution which are of course not valid for systems.

For the scalar equation

(8.1)

\partial_{tt}u-\triangle u=|u|^{\frac{4}{N-2}}u

with
N\geq 5
, the analog of the strong rigidity theorem
3.3
is not valid (see
[
CDKM23
]
), and only a weaker version of the continuous soliton resolution is known, with the additional assumption:

(8.2)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\|(u,\partial_{t}u)(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{1}\times L^{2}}<\infty.

(see
[
DKM23
]
for
N
odd,
[
CDKM22
]
for
N=6
and
[
JL23
]
for any
N\geq 5
).
The following weaker rigidity result is proved in these works:

Theorem 8.1
.

Assume that
N\geq 5
. Let
u
be a radial global solution of (
8.1
) which is bounded in the energy space. Assume

(8.3)

\forall A\in\mathbb{R},\quad\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}\int_{|x|>|t|+A}|\nabla_{t,x}u(t,x)|^{2}dx=0.

Then
u
is a stationary solution.

To illustrate the difficulty of proving the results in the present paper in high dimensions, we give an example of a system of the form (
1.1
), where
f
is homogeneous of degree
\frac{N+2}{N-2}
, with
m=2
and
N\geq 5
such that the analog of Theorem
3.3
is not valid, and the soliton resolution does not hold in full generality. Consider

(8.4)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{tt}\varphi-\triangle\varphi&=|\varphi|^{\frac{4}{N-2}}\varphi\\
\partial_{tt}\psi-\triangle\psi&=|\varphi|^{\frac{4}{N-2}}\psi.\end{aligned}\right.

Let
W=\left(1+\frac{|x|}{N(N-2)}\right)^{\frac{2-N}{2}}
be the ground state stationary solution of (
8.1
). Then
\Phi=(\varphi,\psi)=(W,tW)
is a global, finite energy solution of (
8.4
) such that

\forall A\in\mathbb{R},\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{|x|>|t|+A}|\nabla_{t,x}\Phi(t,x)|^{2}dx=0

and

(8.5)

\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla\Phi(t,x)|^{2}dx=+\infty.

This shows that an analog of the rigidity theorem, Theorem
3.3
is false in dimension
N\geq 5
without further assumption on the nonlinearity or on the solution.
The solution
\Phi
is also a counter-example to the strong soliton resolution conjecture, asserting that any solution such that
T_{+}=\infty
decomposes into rescaled stationary solution.

We do not know however if the conclusion of the rigidity theorem or the soliton resolution (for a sequence of times or for continuous times) hold for solutions that are assumed to be bounded in the energy space.

We note also that the nonlinearity
f
in (
8.4
) does not derive from a potential. For nonlinearities of the form
f(u)=\nabla_{u}F(u)
, with
F
homogeneous of degree
\frac{2N}{N-2}
, it is easy to adapt the proof of Proposition
1.3
to prove that a global solution is bounded along a sequence of times. We conjecture that the analog of the weak rigidity theorem
8.1
and the soliton resolution results
1.2
and
1.7
are valid with the additional assumption that
f
derives from a potential, however the adaptation of the works on scalar equations in this setting seems to be far from being trivial.
