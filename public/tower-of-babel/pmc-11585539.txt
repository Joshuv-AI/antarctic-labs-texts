The relationship between periodontal status, physical activity, dietary practices, and dental caries
Authors: Dilek Özkan Şen, Fatma Uçan Yarkaç, Zeynep Taştan Eroğlu, Kaan Yıldız
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The objective of this study is to evaluate the correlation between dietary patterns, levels of physical activity (PA), and the occurrence of periodontal disease. Many lifestyles have been connected to periodontal disease, but little is known about diet and PA intake. This study included 185 people—62 healthy, 61 gingivitis, and 62 periodontitis. PA, Mediterranean diet score (MDS), and carbohydrate consumption frequency were gathered together with demographics. Clinical and radiographic evaluations calculated decayed, missing, and filled teeth (DMFT) index scores. PA, MDS, carbohydrate consumption, and periodontitis were evaluated. Periodontitis patients had higher DMFT scores, poorer periodontal features, and less daily tooth brushing. More PA overall, more PA in free time, and less sedentary behavior reduced periodontitis rates. Sedentary behavior increased periodontal disease risk in adjusted univariate regression models (OR = 0.235; p = 0.021). Of the study participants, 35 (18.9%) had high MDS. No correlation was established between Mediterranean diet (MD) and periodontitis. Periodontitis and MD, DMFT, and carbohydrate consumption do not seem to be substantially correlated. According to our research, a higher risk of periodontal disease was linked to more sedentary activity. To clarify the extent of the relationship between PA and periodontal disease, more prospective longitudinal research and approaches are required.Clinical Relevance: The periodontal status of individuals can be influenced by their lifestyle.

Introductıon

Periodontitis is a biofilm-mediated, non-communicable, chronic inflammatory disease that leads the tooth’s supporting structures to gradually deteriorate1. Accumulation of intraoral dysbiotic microorganisms at the tooth surface is a major risk factor for periodontitis2. The periodontal ligament and the surrounding bone structure are destroyed or degraded as a result of the host immune system’s reaction to bacterial colonization, and the host immune system is influenced by genetic, environmental, and behavioral variables3,4.

Although the exact mechanism underlying this relationship is unknown, there is evidence that periodontitis is strongly correlated with nutrition, with greater food quality or healthier eating habits protecting periodontal health5,6. Based on the dietary habits of Southern Italians and Greeks in the 1950s and 1960s, the Mediterranean diet (MD) was developed7. It is distinguished by strong diet adherence and provides numerous health advantages8. Its impact on intestinal bacteria appears to be an indirect method of controlling inflammation9. It is reasonable to believe that the MD may be advantageous in the setting of inflammatory illnesses of the oral cavity8. A few recent studies have examined the effects of MD on oral health and concluded that individuals who comply with MD have higher oral health, lower caries incidence, and lower values of periodontal parameters10–12.

Exercise and physical activity (PA) are important components of human physiology. Daily obligations, leisure time, employment, sports, and active transportation are all forms of physical exercise. Periodontal disease as an inflammatory oral disease may be positively influenced by physical activity. A lot of research focused on the treatment of non-communicable chronic inflammatory disease through a multifactorial approach targeted at improving various aspects of the patient’s lifestyle, such as smoking, diet or physical activity have suggested that PA would benefit periodontal condition. However, it is not clear the relationship between physical activity and periodontal disease5,13–15. Some researchers suggested that physical activity has been associated with changes in inflammatory markers, including reduction of C-reactive protein levels16,17. Increased expression of this protein is associated with various diseases, including periodontal disease18. Physical activity may directly reduce the risk of periodontitis by lowering serum tumor necrosis factor-alpha, interleukin-6, and interleukin-8 levels, which may support periodontal health19,20. Although it is not possible to establish a prevention relationship between physical exercise and periodontal disease, an association has been reported between reduced prevalence of periodontal disease among participants who practiced physical activity19,21.

It is crucial to look into the connection between MD and physical activity as a potential modifiable lifestyle factor for periodontitis and periodontal disease, based on a priori evidence suggesting the positive health effects of MD and physical exercise. There is some supporting cross-sectional evidence in the literature and one prospective study finding that physically active adults and individuals who adhere to MD have a lower risk of periodontitis22,23. Increasing evidence points to a potential relationship between lifestyle factors and nutrition and oral health, with greater physical activity, less sedentary behavior, and adherence to MD. However, this relationship between lifestyle factors and periodontal disease is still not clearly understood.

The hypothesis of our study is that lifestyle factors and nutrition increase oxidative stress, reduce insulin sensitivity, and trigger low-grade inflammation in the body, and therefore contribute to the risk of periodontal disease and the severity of the disease24,25. The aim of this study is to identify dental and periodontal health issues, oral hygiene practices, lifestyle and behavioral variables (adherence to MD and PA), and potential relationships between them by assessing the variables that may have an impact on them in the Turkish population.

Materials and methods

Study design

The present cross-sectional study was conducted after approval by the Faculty of Dentistry, Non-Pharmaceutical and Medical Device Research Ethics Committee (2022/176) and by the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2013. Selected subjects were informed about the aims of the research, and written consent was obtained before starting the study.

Study population

One of the researchers (D.O.S.) recruited patients in the study one after the other if they visited the Periodontology Clinic meeting the following requirements:

Data collection

185 individuals who applied to the Periodontology Clinic were included in the study. Individuals were classified according to the 2017 classification and were divided into 3 groups as healthy group (n = 62), gingivitis group (n = 61), and periodontitis group (n = 62)26.

Periodontal Examination

Before the start of treatment, all patient groups had their standard clinical periodontal parameters measured, including gingival recession, clinical attachment levels, bleeding on probing (BOP), plaque index (PI), and gingival index (GI)27. After 30 s, at six locations per tooth, a calibrated clinical researcher measured the probing depth (PD), which is the distance between the free gingival margin and the pocket depth, the clinical attachment level (CAL), which is the distance between the cement-enamel attachment and the pocket depth28. Mean PD, CAL, and BOP were also obtained. Additionally, the number of teeth was recorded. Patients with no clinical attachment loss, no radiographic bone loss (RBL), a probing depth (PD) < 3 mm, and bleeding on probing (BOP) at ≥ 10% were diagnosed as gingivitis. Patients with no RBL or clinical attachment loss, BOP at < 10% and a PD < 3 mm were diagnosed as periodontally healthy. A periodontitis case was defined whenever interdental CAL was detectable at ≥ 2 nonadjacent teeth, or whenever buccal or oral CAL ≥ 3 mm with pocketing (PD > 3 mm) was detectable at ≥ 2 teeth29.

To the individuals included in the research; A questionnaire consisting of 16 questions including demographic data of individuals, number of daily snacks, daily carbohydrate-containing food and sugary drink consumption frequency, tooth brushing frequency and duration, education level, and oral care habits was filled30. Variables in the questionnaire were defined as follows:

Socio-demographic variables: (a) age was presented in years; (b) gender: male or female; (c) education level was presented as primary, secondary, and university, as described previously31. Tooth brushing frequency was assessed using the question “How often do you brush your teeth?” with four levels of response: 1 time per day; 2 times a day; 2–3 times a week but not daily; and no brushing31.

The daily nutritional status and carbohydrate consumption of individuals were evaluated with 4 questions: (1) number of snacks per day (no consumption or 1–4 times a day); (2) number of daily carbohydrate consumption (no consumption or 1–6 times per day); (3) number of sugary drinks consumed per day (no consumption or 1–5 times a day); (4) type of carbohydrates in snacks (no consumption or poor consumption, middle consumption, rich consumption)30.

Mediterranean diet assessment

Adherence to the MD was measured using self-reported a validated 15-item survey. The participants’ weekly intake frequency of eight food groups, namely cereals or potatoes, dairy products, vegetables, legumes, red meat, fish, fruits, and olive oil, was assessed. For cereals or potatoes, dairy products, vegetables, legumes, red meat, and fish, the number of days of usual consumption per week was asked. For fruits, the number of days of intake per week and the number of portions consumed daily were asked. Then, the total weekly intake of fruits was calculated by multiplying these two variables. For olive oil, participants were asked whether they consumed olive oil as a dressing and/or during cooking. Subsequently, the participants were assigned a value of 0 or 1 for their consumption of the above eight food groups. The results were categorized as poor (scoring ≤ 5), medium (scoring 6–9), or high (scoring ≥ 10) adherence32,33.

Dental examination

The International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS) was used to determine the number of decaying, missing, and filled teeth (DMFT) through visual inspection and radiographic assessment. Every tooth surface was inspected, but each tooth’s observations were noted separately34.

Physical activity assessment

The validated short form of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) was used to measure PA35. The IPAQ automatic report categorized the total PA level as low, moderate, or high (http://theipaq/home ).

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated based on the full mouth bleeding score levels (α = 0.05, effect size d = 0.527). Considering a power of 99.9% and a significance level of 5%, the computed sample size was 185 subjects36.

Statistical analysis

Using IBM SPSS V23 (Software by IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 23.0, Armonk, NY: IBM Corp), the data were examined. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests were used to gauge conformity to the normal distribution. Multiple comparisons were examined using the Bonferroni Corrected Z Test, and categorical variables were examined using the Pearson Chi-Square Test. To compare the parameters with the normal distribution according to the groups, a one-way analysis of variance was utilized. The parameters that did not meet the normal distribution according to the groups were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test and multiple comparisons using the Dunn test. For categorical variables, the analysis findings were shown as frequency (%), whereas for quantitative variables, the results were shown as mean, standard deviation, and median (minimum to maximum). The risk factors affecting the disease were examined by Logistic Regression Analysis and the Enter method was used to include the variables in the model. Analysis results were presented as frequency (percentage) for categorical variables and as mean ± standard deviation for quantitative variables. The significance level was taken as p < 0.05.

Dıscussıon

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate whether there is a relationship between PA level, MD adherence, carbohydrate consumption frequency, and dental caries with different periodontal statuses in the Turkish population. Our study’s findings demonstrated that people with poor periodontal status have higher DMFT scores. In addition, it was found that periodontitis can be prevented by cleaning your teeth two or three times a day, but the risk of developing periodontal disease increases with age. There was no significant difference between the severity of periodontal disease and adherence to MD or frequency of carbohydrate consumption. Conversely, there was a relationship between the severity of periodontal disease and physical activity, and individuals with high levels of physical activity had worse periodontal parameters. In addition, we found that high levels of physical activity reduced the risk of periodontitis.

Many inflammatory disorders are impacted by physical exercise, which has also been linked to modifications in inflammatory markers16,18. It is believed that PA influences the inflammatory process involved in the etiology of periodontal disease, a chronic inflammatory disease17. High physical activity potentially has a beneficial effect on systemic inflammation. Inverse associations between PA levels and inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein and interleukin-6, have been observed in large-scale epidemiological studies37,38. Furthermore, there is evidence that physical activity and dental health are positively associated. Integration of PA into the risk management of periodontitis is strongly recommended. For better periodontal health, the World Health Organization recommends physical activity39,40. A comprehensive analysis that included the few studies that looked at physical activity and showed it to be associated with a reduced incidence of periodontal disease discovered that people who were more physically active had a lower prevalence of periodontitis19,22,41. A study using a nationally representative data set from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2011–2012 noted that individuals with total physical activity levels had lower rates of periodontal disease compared to individuals with no total physical activity42. In the study evaluating the relationship between leisure/occupational physical activity (LTPA/OPA) and periodontitis with data from the NHANES 2009–2014 database of the US population, it was found that high LTPA was a protective indicator for periodontitis/severe periodontitis, while high OPA was a risk indicator43. According to a different study, individuals who engaged in physical activity had a 22% reduced risk of developing periodontitis than those who did not, and this was linked to a decreased incidence of Stage III/IV periodontitis19. A positive correlation has been shown by Anderson et al.44 between infections linked to a good periodontal state and physical activity. Regular walking has a substantial correlation with a lower prevalence of periodontitis, according to research by Han, SJ. et al.45. When Al-Zahrani et al.22 looked at the connection between physical activity and the prevalence of periodontitis, they also hypothesized that a high degree of physical exercise may prevent the disease. The current study found that people with higher levels of physical activity had better periodontal and dental health. Furthermore, it was discovered that in people who engaged in little physical exercise, this activity had a preventive impact against periodontal disease. While there are currently few observational studies on the subject, it is believed that a decrease in physical activity has a detrimental effect on nutrition and may even increase the chance of developing periodontitis.

Screen time is a more harmful type of sedentary behavior, according to studies, and it increases the number of risk factors for unhealthy behaviors like snacking and bad eating habits46,47. A diet high in carbohydrates may increase the chance of developing inflammatory disorders later in life, including diabetes and cardiovascular disease48. In addition, tooth decay may become more common with a diet high in carbohydrates. On the other hand, having good teeth does not guarantee that you are eating a nutritious diet in general. To preserve overall health and dental health, a balanced diet is essential49. A balanced diet is regarded to be essential for sustaining periodontal health. According to a Finnish survey, individuals with periodontal disease had far higher rates of dental caries. Moreover, individuals with dental caries also frequently had severe periodontal disease50. Research has indicated that patients with periodontal disease are more susceptible to dental caries due to the bacteriological origin of both conditions and the shared discovery of numerous potential background variables2,41. The frequency of patients’ carbohydrate consumption and their periodontal health were not shown to be significantly correlated in this study; nevertheless, patients with poor periodontal parameters had higher DMFT values.

MD has numerous health advantages and is distinguished by strong dietary compliance. MD diet is believed to have an anti-inflammatory impact and may lower the incidence of periodontitis from a biological standpoint51. Independent of plaque buildup and at-home oral hygiene practices, research revealed that people with low MD adherence and low/medium PA not only had lower biometric and inflammatory parameters but also frequently lost teeth due to periodontitis and tooth mobility. As a result, it has been suggested that the periodontium and these environmental variables (MD and PA) have a reciprocal connection52. From the perspective of biology, however, consuming a Western diet high in processed meats and white flour causes low-grade inflammation and is linked to the development of numerous chronic inflammatory non-communicable illnesses, such as periodontitis51. This is in contrast to MD. However, because of the synergistic anti-inflammatory capability of MD components, high MD adherence has been demonstrated to be inversely linked with stage III/IV periodontitis; in this study, whole grain products demonstrated the best protective impact.

The Mediterranean diet was found to reduce inflammatory periodontal markers in a study examining the impact of MD on gingivitis10. A negative MD score was observed to be correlated linearly with worsening periodontal index values53. Similarly, in the study by Bawadi et al.21 where they examined the relationship between physical activity and healthy nutrition, the high physically active group had a lower mean plaque index, gingival index, and clinical attachment loss. A study conducted in Morocco on 1075 young individuals found a linear correlation between worsening periodontal indices and decreasing MD scores however, no significant link between the prevalence of periodontitis and adherence to MD as measured by MD54. Wu et al.55 were unable to determine a causal relationship between MD and periodontitis. In this study, those with high compliance to the Mediterranean diet showed improved inflammatory markers, even if the difference was not statistically significant.

The primary limitation of this study was that we were unable to regulate the participants’ eating habits and could only assess them through questioning. We were unable to evaluate the temporal association between eating habits and periodontal status because of the study’s cross-sectional design. Secondly, it is impossible to completely rule out the possibility of selection bias given that the study cohort was chosen from patients who visited the public periodontology unit. All things considered, these elements may reduce the study’s generalizability. Another limitation of our study is that we did not evaluate sociodemographic status, but sociodemographic data (income status, mother’s education, father’s education, etc.) is an important factor affecting the habit of eating. Furthermore, we investigated only the relationship between periodontal disease and physical activity in individuals with different periodontal statuses; we could not evaluate how the type and intensity of physical activity affect periodontal health. Clarifying whether all forms of physical activity uniformly benefit periodontal health or if specific activities offer greater advantages would significantly aid in crafting personalized health recommendations. This nuanced understanding could lead to more targeted and effective strategies for preventing and managing periodontal disease.

Conclusıon

The current study’s findings indicate a significant relationship between periodontal diseases and physical activity. However, a higher adherence to Mediterranean diets did not seem to be linked to a higher risk of periodontal disease development. According to the results of this study, it can be suggested that changes in lifestyle factors can result in fresh approaches to the fight against periodontitis. However, additional randomized controlled trials are necessary to evaluate the possible contribution of diet and lifestyle choices on the onset and progression of periodontitis.
