Tailoring activated carbon and carbon black adsorbents for efficient PAH removal
Authors: Zainab A Alaithan, Mohammed A Alkhunaizi, Nadrah A Alawani, Ahmed K Alasseel, Joury M Aldhawyan, Emad N Al-Shafei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) in industrial effluents pose severe environmental and health risks due to their carcinogenicity, bioaccumulation potential, and resistance to conventional treatment. This study investigated the effects of nitric acid functionalization and metal ion-exchange on the structural properties and anthracene adsorption performance of activated carbon (AC) and carbon black (CB) as representative carbon-based adsorbents. Nitric acid treatment of CB enhanced mesopore development and introduced oxygen-containing functional groups, improving anthracene removal efficiency from 69 to 74% at a 200:1 feed-to-carbon ratio. Conversely, acid treatment of AC reduced removal efficiency from 60 to 50%, attributed to critical micropore volume effected as confirmed by Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area. Metal ion-exchange reduced adsorption performance in both materials, due to nanoparticle agglomeration and surface area while CB-Ni and CB-Zn retained moderate removal efficiencies of 62% and 66%, consistent with better metal dispersion. A strong linear correlation (R2 = 0.9483) between BET surface area and anthracene uptake in ion-exchanged samples confirmed physisorption via van der Waals interactions as the dominant removal mechanism in the absence of surface functional groups. The optimal adsorbent, CB-2N, maintained stable removal efficiency of 70–74.5% over five consecutive regeneration cycles, demonstrating excellent structural durability and reusability. These findings establish rational design principles for carbon adsorbent selection and functionalization in industrial PAH remediation applications.Keywords: Carbon black, Activated carbon, Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH), Mesoporous, Carbon acid treatmentSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Effective waste management is crucial for protecting the environment and public health from the accumulation of pollutants such as heavy metals and organic compounds. One of the main approaches to waste management is adsorption, due to its efficiency, low operational cost, selectivity and biocompatibility1–4. A wide range of adsorption materials have been commercially applied, including synthetic and natural adsorbents. Synthetic adsorbents include carbonaceous materials, clay composites, resins, and polymeric adsorbents. These materials are distinguished by their high surface areas and adsorption capacity, making them widely used to adsorb organic pollutants1.

Among organic pollutants, condensed ring aromatics, referred to as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), are considered highly toxic and carcinogenic to various life forms. This class of aromatic organic compounds consists of three or more fused aromatic rings, such as coronene, dibenzoperylene, and ovalene5,6. Heavy polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (HPAHs), also referred to as heavy poly nuclear aromatics (HPNAs) in the oil industry, are composed from three to seven fused aromatic rings with lipophilic nature7–9. Due to the widespread presence of these pollutants in the environment, several studies on their remediation and removal from water, soil, and hydrocarbon liquid using carbon-based materials have been investigated10–13.

Carbon-based materials exhibit diverse morphologies including activated carbon (AC), graphene, carbon nanotubes (CNTs), carbon nanofibers (CNFs), biochar (BC), carbon black (CB), and carbon aerogels (CAs). AC dominates environmental applications due to its cost-effectiveness, high surface area, and functionalization flexibility, available in granular, powder, and fibrous forms6,14,15. BC, produced via biomass pyrolysis, serves water remediation and other industrial application16–18. CB, derived from incomplete hydrocarbon combustion, comprises > 95% carbon as spherical particles and finds industrial applications in tires, plastics, filters, and paints19,20. The adsorption capacity is governed by particle size, pore size and distribution architecture, total surface area, and surface chemistry. Smaller particles enhance adsorption through increased surface area to volume ratios and reduced diffusion pathway lengths21. For instance, benzene, naphthalene, and anthracene exhibited superior adsorption on colloidal organic matter compared to soil and sediment, directly correlating with available surface area. These structure–property relationships highlighted the importance of tailoring carbon material characteristics to specific contaminant removal applications in environmental remediation and industrial wastewater treatment.

Sabzehmeidani et al. addressed the chemical and physical properties of carbon material, modification strategies to enhance surface area & adsorption capacity, and the influence of morphology and functional groups on hazardous substance removal efficiency22. Lamichhane et al. investigated the removal of organic materials, specifically PAHs, by adsorption over carbon-based materials and factors controlling adsorption process performance and capacity10. Yung et al. achieved ~ 90% PAH removal from wastewater using porous carbon produced from petroleum coke-derived within 30 min, though equilibrium required several hours, indicating that molecular diffusion into deep pores represents the rate-limiting step23. Optimizing carbon framework characteristics requires balancing surface area and pore architecture to maximize molecule-pore interactions while ensuring accessibility. Pores must be sufficiently large to accommodate target molecules and generally, molecules access pores ≥ 1.7 times their molecular dimensions24,25. For low-molecular weight of PAHs (9–11.4 Å), adsorbents with higher micropore fractions (< 2 nm) exhibited superior pore occupancy26. Conversely, heavy PAHs (HPAHs) demonstrated enhanced adsorption on materials with elevated mesopore content (2–50 nm)9. This size-selective behavior highlights the importance of tailoring pore size distribution to target contaminant characteristics. Excessive microporosity restricts accessibility and diffusion of large organic molecules, while optimized mesoporous-microporous architectures maximize both adsorption capacity and kinetics for specific PAH molecular weight ranges, enabling rational adsorbent design for industrial wastewater treatment applications.

Surface functional groups enhance PAH-adsorbent interactions, improving adsorption capacity. Wang et al. employed Grand Canonical Monte Carlo simulations to evaluate PAH adsorption on functionalized activated carbon (-OH, -COOH, -NH₂, = O), demonstrating that acidic groups, particularly carbonyl functionalities, provided superior enhancement compared to alkaline groups27. Nanosecond pulse discharge modification of AC increased oxygen-containing surface groups and altered pore structure, yielding 10% improved naphthalene adsorption28. Additionally, nitrogen incorporation into carbon frameworks enhances π-π stacking interactions between PAH molecules and adsorbent aromatic rings29. These findings establish that strategic surface functionalization, particularly with oxygen-containing acidic groups and nitrogen heteroatoms significantly improves PAH adsorption through enhanced electronic and steric interactions beyond purely physical surface area effects.

Multiple functionalization strategies enhance activated carbon performance, including acid treatment, metal impregnation, ozone treatment, and microwave irradiation30. Acid treatment introduces oxygen-containing functional groups through exposure to hydrochloric acid31 or nitric acid32–34, while metal impregnation incorporates silver35, copper36, or iron37 into the carbon framework. Ge et al. demonstrated that microwave-assisted nitric acid modification of coal-based AC significantly enhanced phenanthrene and naphthalene adsorption capacity28. Subsequently, iron impregnated AC prepared via microwave irradiation exhibited accelerated PAH removal from aqueous solutions38. These complementary modification approaches of chemical functionalization and metal incorporation, synergistically improve adsorption performance through enhanced surface chemistry, increased active sites, and catalytic effects, establishing hybrid modification strategies as effective tools for optimizing carbon-based adsorbents for industrial wastewater treatment applications.

Despite extensive research on carbon-based PAH adsorbents, systematic comparative studies have been examined the identical modification protocols differentially affect structurally distinct materials remain lacking. The competing effects of enhanced surface chemistry versus pore structure degradation during functionalization and their relative contributions to heavy PAH adsorption requiring mesoporous accessibility remain challenging.

This study addressed these gaps by conducting a systematic comparative investigation of nitric acid functionalization and metal ion-exchange effects on two structurally distinct carbon materials of activated carbon with high surface area, predominantly microporous and carbon black with high mesopore surface area. The primary objectives are to elucidate material specific structural and chemical responses to identical modification protocols, quantify the balance between enhanced surface functionality and potential pore structure degradation. The study aimed to establish structure performance relationships governing anthracene adsorption as a representative three-ring heavy PAH, and provide rational design principles for selecting and optimizing carbon materials based on target pollutant characteristics. This work represented the first systematic comparison demonstrating that optimal functionalization strategies are material dependent, with surface area preservation being critical for microporous AC while surface chemistry enhancement dominates for mesoporous CB.

Experimental methods

Material

The materials of carbon black (CB) and activated carbon (AC) were purchased from Cabot and Sterm Chemicals. For the ion-exchange process, the following materials were used, zinc nitrate hexahydrate (reagent grade, 98% from Sigma-Aldrich), Gold (III) chloride (99% from Sigma-Aldrich), nickel nitrate hexahydrate (99% from Sigma-Aldrich), ferric chloride hexahydrate (from Fisher Chemical), and nitric acid (ACS reagent, 70% from Sigma-Aldrich). The high surface area carbon black used in this study originates from the controlled thermal decomposition of petroleum derived aromatic oils as an industrial production route.

Acid treatment and ion-exchange

Three concentrations of acid solution were prepared at 2, 5, and 15 M. The carbon samples were combined with the acid solutions and stirred at room temperature for 2 h. The carbon-acid mixtures were then washed with deionized water three times using vacuum filtration. The treated carbons were dried at 90 °C for one day and subsequently calcined under nitrogen flow at 450 °C. The treated samples were designated as 2N, 5N, and 15N to indicate the acid concentration. The ion exchanged method was applied using metal solutions of iron (Fe), zinc (Zn), gold (Au), and nickel (Ni) were prepared at a concentration of 0.2 M for use in the ion-exchange modification method. First, 150 mL of each of these solutions was added to 7 g of the carbon samples separately. The carbon samples in the metal solutions were stirred at room temperature for 24 h to allow for ion exchange. Then, the carbon–metal mixtures were vacuum-filtered to separate the ion-exchanged carbons from the metal solution. The carbons were washed with distilled water, dried at 90 °C for two days, and subsequently calcined under airflow at 450 °C.

Characterization methods

The textural and chemical properties of the acid-treated and ion-exchanged carbons (AC and CB) were characterizations using several methods. Nitrogen adsorption was conducted using ASAP 2420 from Micromeritics to measure the textural properties of carbon samples. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted using PANalytical X’Pert PRO with Cu ka (λ = 1.54 Å) radiation and a scanning range between 2-theta angle 15° and 75° at a scanning rate set to 10°min-1. A field emission gun-environmental scanning electron microscope (FEG-ESEM) was utilized to study the morphological and compositional features of the samples. The samples were analyzed using FEI Quattro ESEM. The ESEM was operated at 20–30 kV and 11 mm working distance. Both compositional (z-contrast) and topographical images were acquired for each sample. In addition, EDS X-ray spectra in general and spot analysis mode were acquired to determine their elemental composition. The graphitic and amorphous structures characterization was evaluated using Raman from JASCO model NRS-7000 equipped with a green laser source at 532 nm at a spectrum window of 1200–1800 cm−1.

PAH adsorption experiment

The adsorption capacity of different carbon materials was evaluated using batch experiments. The experiments were conducted using 600 ppm anthracene, a three-ring aromatic, in dodecane solution. Initial adsorption experiments were conducted at a fixed feedstock to carbon ratio of 200 to 1. The feedstock and carbon material were mixed for 24 h at room temperature allowing the adsorption to reach equilibrium. Additional adsorption experiments were conducted for selected carbon samples at different feedstock to carbon ratios from 100 to 800, in order to construct the adsorption isotherm. The quantification of the anthracene in the initial and treated feed was conducted using Gas Chromatography Flame Ionization Detector (GC-FID). The GC-FID oven was operated from 120 ºC to 250 ºC (hold 30 min) at a temperature gradient of 5 ºC/min then to 300 ºC at a temperature gradient of 20 ºC/min. In house method was developed to quantify the anthracene content. Spent adsorbent was regenerated via two-stage methylene chloride extraction as polar solvent and the solid-to-solvent ratio 10:1, ultrasonic agitation, 10 min followed by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 10 min. The regenerated material was washed twice with ethanol, dried at 180 °C for 4 h, and cooled under nitrogen before reuse to restore surface activity. Results and discussion

Carbon structures

The carbon structures were investigated with XRD and Raman spectroscopy, Figs. 1A and B show the XRD spectra of AC and CB before and after ion exchange with Ni and Zn. The diffraction spectra of both parent and loaded metal oxide, showed the carbon diffraction at two peaks at 2θ = ~ 23˚ and 2θ = ~ 42˚. These wide peaks represent carbon lattice planes (0 0 2) and (1 0 0), respectively, which is in good agreement with the published literature40 . However, for the ion-exchanged samples, the x-ray diffraction spectra of the loaded to the carbons show no appearance of Ni and Zn oxide diffraction peaks probably because metal oxide clusters are finely distributed inside the carbon pores.

XRD spectra of AC and ion-exchanged AC (A), CB and metal ion-exchanged CB (B), Raman spectra of AC and acid-treated AC (C), and BC and acid-treated BC (D).

The Raman spectra of pristine and nitric acid-treated AC and CB (Figs. 1C and D) exhibit characteristic D (~ 1337 cm⁻1) and G bands (~ 1593 cm⁻1), representing disordered and graphitic carbon structures, respectively32. The ID/IG ratio quantifies structural disorder (Table 1), with higher values indicating greater defect density and amorphous carbon content41,42. The pristine carbon black (ID/IG = 2.29) exhibits higher disorder than activated carbon (ID/IG = 2.08), consistent with CB disordered crystal structure of randomly oriented graphitic crystallites versus AC larger graphitic domains33,34. The nitric acid treatment produced material-specific, concentration-dependent structural modifications and CB-2N showed minimal ID/IG increase from 2.29 to 2.32, indicating mild surface oxidation introducing oxygen functional groups without significant structural degradation34. Conversely, CB-5N and CB-15N exhibited slight decreases about 2.23–2.24, suggesting preferential oxidative etching of highly disordered amorphous carbon, leaving relatively ordered graphitic domains43,44. At higher acid concentrations, aggressive oxidation selectively removes reactive, defective structures through gasification during calcination (C + HNO3 → CO2 + NOx), enriching residual material in graphitic content and paradoxically lowering ID/IG despite overall oxidation45.

Surface properties

Activated carbon vs carbon black

Figure 2 presents the N₂ adsorption–desorption isotherms of CB and AC. According to the IUPAC classification system, the CB isotherm conforms to a Type IV(a) profile, characteristic of mesoporous materials exhibiting capillary condensation with an associated hysteresis loop at intermediate relative pressures34. In contrast, the AC isotherm exhibits a Type I profile with a sharp uptake at low relative pressure and a near-horizontal plateau, indicative of a predominantly microporous structure with narrow micropore size distribution34. These isotherm classifications are consistent with the BET textural analysis reported in Table 2, wherein CB exhibits a significantly higher mesopore surface area and total mesopore volume relative to AC, while AC possesses a dominant micropore surface area and micropore volume. From a structural perspective, activated carbons are characterised by a highly developed microporous network arising from their disordered graphitic framework, which confers large internal surface areas and deep adsorption potential wells for smaller aromatic molecules through enhanced van der Waals interactions within confined micropore channels. Carbon blacks, by contrast, are nanostructured materials comprising semi-spherical primary particles that are covalently fused into chain-like aggregates through graphitic basal plane junctions41, Interstitial voids between fused primary particles create mesoporosity suited to large PAH adsorption, where AC micropores impose steric and diffusion constraints.

Effect of acid treatment

The surface properties of the parent and acid-treated carbons were investigated, and the results are displayed in Tables 3 and 4. BET analysis indicates that acid treatment reduced the surface area of the micropores and increased the surface area towards mesopores for both AC and CB. For instance, CB-2N shows a decrease in micropore area from 466.5 m2/g to 119.8 m2/g, with a significant increase in mesopore surface area, as high as 1288.2 m2/g for CB-15N. This shift in surface area distribution suggests that acid treatment collapses smaller pores to form larger mesopores46, effectively transitioning the pore structure towards mesoporosity, which is beneficial for the adsorption of larger PAH molecules. The pore size distribution obtained from the BJH equation (Fig. 3) also shows a clear increase in the mesoporous region between 2–4 nm for the acid-treated CB and AC samples. Despite the increase in mesopore surface area, the average pore diameter of CB decreased from 12.6 nm to 7.7 nm, and the average pore diameter of AC decreased from 4.3 nm to 2.9 nm. This suggests that although the number of mesopores increased, the average pore size was reduced, perhaps due to the addition of functional groups. For AC, acid treatment acts similarly, reducing the micropore surface area but increasing the mesopore surface area, although to a lesser extent than CB. This suggests that the intrinsic structure of AC is more resistant to oxidation. It is generally accepted that oxidation affects the aliphatic chains but not the aromatic rings, due to their high stability and carbon structure43.

Effect of metal loading

Metal loading aimed to introduce active sites on the carbon surface, which may enhance the specific interactions with heavy PAH molecules and potentially modify the pore structure. Each metal (Ni, Zn, Fe, Au) has a unique chemical and electronic properties44 that could contribute to the adsorption performance by strengthening the material interaction with heavy PAH compounds. The effect of metal oxide on the surface properties was investigated and the results are summarized in Table 6. The results showed that metal loading decreased the total BET surface area in both AC and CB due to partial blocking of pores by metal particles. Such an effect is more pronounced for AC, where micropores are especially prone to blockage. Notably, gold ion-exchange caused a significant reduction in the total surface area in both of CB and AC. AC underwent structural collapse while CB remained intact due to the iron oxide species derived from the nitrate precursor catalysed carbon gasification during air calcination at 450 °C, preferentially attacking the reactive microporous framework of AC and yielding predominantly iron oxide residue. On the other hand, Zn and Ni-loaded samples exhibited large surface area and balanced pore size distribution. This is in contrast with other studies, which concluded that certain metals such as zinc could increase the surface and develop mesporosity45. This variation in the results may be due to differences in the metal loading procedures.

In addition, metal loading reduced the pore diameter, especially for CB samples. However, due to the large CB mesoporous surface area, mesopores remain accessible to large aromatic compounds. Ion-exchanged samples were surveyed with ESEM in order to acquire representative micrographs of the prepared material. The obtained ESEM images are presented in Fig. 5. The objective of this analysis was to investigate morphological changes following the ion-exchange process. Overall, the results indicated no significant morphological differences compared to the reference sample. The analysis revealed that the samples have homogenous composition based on the z-contrast images. They clearly showed that the ion-exchanged metal particles were well distributed over the surface of the prepared samples.

Effect of modification methods on adsorption capacity

The adsorption of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), using three-ring anthracene as a model compound, is widely used to study heavy PAH removal from various feedstocks. The adsorption performance of activated carbon (AC) and carbon black (CB), including samples treated with nitric acid and ion-exchange, was evaluated for anthracene removal. The impact of surface area, pore structure, and promoter modifications on the adsorption efficiency and capacity is discussed below:

Anthracene removal efficiency

The anthracene removal efficiencies of the carbon materials are summarized in Figs. 6A and B. Carbon black (CB) consistently achieved higher performance than activated carbon (AC) due to its inherently higher mesoporosity and larger pore diameters, which facilitate the adsorption of large molecules like anthracene. Untreated CB achieved a removal efficiency of 69%, while the acid-treated CB samples (CB-2N, CB-5N, and CB-15N) demonstrated enhanced removal efficiencies, with CB-2N reaching the highest value of 74%. This improvement is attributed to the introduction of surface functional groups, such as carboxyl and hydroxyl groups, and an increase in mesopore area caused by nitric acid treatment42. These surface modifications enhanced the interactions between anthracene molecules and the carbon surface, including π-π stacking and hydrogen bonding47.

Comparative anthracene adsorption performance: (A) modified black carbon, (B) activated carbon, (C) and cyclic regeneration stability of CB-2N, at 200:1 feed to carbon ratio.

The metal ion exchanged over CB samples also showed variable removal efficiencies, with CB-Zn (66%) and CB-Ni (62%) performing well, while CB-Au exhibited a lower removal efficiency of 48%. These metal-dependent performance differences reflect varying mechanisms beyond simple pore blockage. CB-Au substantial efficiency reduction correlated with severe surface area loss about 40% reduction as shown in Table 6, attributed to large gold nanoparticle agglomeration that physically blocks mesopore entrances and reduces accessibility to adsorption sites50. In contrast, CB-Ni and CB-Zn demonstrated moderate efficiency reductions consistent with smaller transition metal oxide particle formation that causes pore narrowing rather than complete blockage51. CB-Fe exhibited unique behavior, maintaining relatively high efficiency (66%) despite iron loading, which correlates with its slight surface area increase (Table 6), suggesting formation of a protective, well-dispersed iron oxide layer that creates additional mesoporosity rather than blocking existing pores.

For activated carbon, the untreated sample (AC) achieved a removal efficiency of 60%. However, nitric acid-treated AC samples showed a slight decrease in performance, with AC-15N exhibiting the lowest efficiency (50% anthracene removal). This reduction is likely due to the loss of overall available surface area from acid treatment, as confirmed by BET analysis. The reduced surface area may limit the adsorption of anthracene molecules, which initially benefited from the high micropore area of untreated AC.

Metal ion exchanged over AC samples further reduced the removal efficiency, with AC-Fe performing the worst due to poor removal efficiency. This poor performance loss was not resulting from pore blockage but from iron oxide-catalyzed carbon gasification during calcination that destroys the microporous framework (Sect 3.2.3). AC-Au also showed severe efficiency reduction (33%) with 61% surface area loss, reflecting large Au particle agglomeration blocking the critical micropore network. AC-Ni and AC-Zn demonstrated moderate efficiency reductions about 41%, with 10–21% surface area losses, consistent with better metal dispersion yielding smaller particles that partially narrow rather than completely block micropores.

Metal-dependent performance variations of Fe increasing CB efficiency while eliminating AC function, Au reducing both by 30–40%, demonstrate that metal–carbon interactions depend critically on support structure of CB with mesoporous graphitic vs. AC with microporous amorphous framework48, metal nucleation behavior, and thermal degradation. Beyond pore size-pollutant matching, rational adsorbent design must integrate metal–carbon interfacial chemistry, particle size control, and synthesis thermal stability. The optimization should utilize controlled deposition methods using incipient wetness, atomic layer deposition in order to minimize noble metal agglomeration while maximizing functional site dispersion.

The regeneration performance of CB-2N was evaluated over five consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles using two-stage polar solvent of methylene chloride extraction followed and thermal drying at 180 °C under nitrogen. As shown in Fig. 6C, the anthracene removal efficiency remained consistently stable between approximately 70–74.5% across all seven cycles, with no significant decline observed. This sustained performance demonstrates the excellent regenerability and structural durability of CB-2N.

Adsorption isotherms

The anthracene removal efficiency and adsorption isotherms are complementary, providing consistent insights into the adsorption performance of the tested materials. Anthracene removal efficiency measured the percentage of anthracene removed from a fixed concentration of feedstock during batch experiment. It provides an essential and practical evaluation of adsorbents effectiveness under specific conditions, such as a feed-to-carbon ratio of 200:1. On the other hand, adsorption isotherms provide detailed information about the adsorption capacity, representing the amount of anthracene adsorbed per gram of carbon at equilibrium under varying concentrations. This data captures the adsorbent long-term potential and ultimate capacity.

The adsorption isotherms for selected AC, CB, and their modified counterparts are shown in Fig. 7, providing insights into the adsorption capacity at varying anthracene concentrations. The isotherms reveal that CB samples consistently exhibit higher adsorption capacities than AC, confirming the superior performance of CB for PAH removal. Untreated CB achieved a maximum adsorption density (qmax​) of 0.090 ganthracene/gcarbon, which is significantly higher than that of untreated AC (0.076 ganthracene/gcarbon). Among the acid-treated CB samples, CB-2N exhibited the highest removal efficiency (Fig. 6B) and therefore was further investigated. CB-2N achieved the highest adsorption capacity, with qmax values of 0.100 ganthracene/gcarbon. The enhanced performance of CB-2N is due to the increased surface area mesoporosity and the introduction of functional groups, which improve Van der Waals interactions with anthracene molecules. On the other hand, the ion-exchanged CB samples such as CB-Zn and CB-Ni had qmax values of 0.085 ganthracene/gcarbon and 0.087 ganthracene/gcarbon, respectively. Similarly, ion-exchanged AC samples performed poorly, with AC-Fe and AC-Au showing significant reductions in adsorption capacity. These effects were well-captured by the surface area trends of the ion-exchanged samples.

Anthracene adsorption isotherm of AC, CB, and modified carbons.

Surface area vs. adsorption capacity

Figure 8 shows a strong correlation (R2 = 0.9247) between the total surface area of both carbon materials (AC and CB) and the adsorption capacity including the ion-exchanged samples. This indicates that surface area had a dominant impact on the adsorption capacity49. The weakened correlation between surface area and capacity in metal-loaded samples indicates that incorporated metal ions did not enhance the framework’s van der Waals interactions with anthracene; instead, metal oxide deposition partially occluded accessible adsorption sites, reducing overall PAH uptake. One possible explanation is that these metal ions aggregated into relatively large extra-framework clusters rather than incorporating into the framework structure. Consequently, a noticeable reduction in surface area was observed after metal loading, which ultimately led to no improvement in adsorption capacity.

Conclusion

This study provides the first systematic comparative investigation of nitric acid functionalization and metal ion-exchange effects on structurally distinct carbon materials of activated carbon and carbon black for heavy polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon adsorption. The main outcome is that identical modification protocols produce fundamentally different performance for PAH removal and depending on carbon support characteristics, establishing that rational adsorbent design must align functionalization strategy with material structure. The nitric acid treatment revealed striking material-dependent responses and CB demonstrated enhanced performance through controlled surface oxidation and mesopore development, with CB-2N achieving the highest anthracene uptake about 0.10415 g/g via optimized balance between oxygen functional group introduction and structural preservation. In contrast, AC exhibited performance decline as acid treatment preferentially destroyed critical micropore volume essential for PAH adsorption, with AC-15N showing 14% surface area loss and reduced efficiency. This different behavior arises from CB graphitic structure resisting oxidative degradation while AC amorphous microporous framework proves vulnerable to acid attack. The metal ion-exchange universally reduced adsorption performance through distinct mechanisms as the Au caused severe surface area loss (40–61%) via large nanoparticle agglomeration blocking mesopores. While Fe exhibited poor effects on AC surface area destruction through iron oxide-catalyzed carbon combustion, yet preserved CB structure. The strong correlation between surface area and anthracene uptake in metal-exchanged samples (R2 = 0.9247) confirms that physical pore blockage, rather than beneficial chemical functionalization, dominates metal-loading effects. These findings establish CB-2N as a superior adsorbent for heavy PAHs, combining with high mesopore content matching anthracene molecular dimensions and enhanced oxygen functional groups promoting π-π interactions and hydrogen bonding. The excellent structural stability preserving adsorption capacity, and superior performance over conventional microporous AC for large aromatic molecules. This work demonstrates that optimal carbon adsorbent design requires material-specific functionalization strategies rather than universal treatment protocols, with mesoporous graphitic structures like CB tolerating aggressive modification conditions that destroy microporous amorphous frameworks like AC.
