Real‐World Evaluation of an Automated Algorithm to Detect Patients With Potentially Undiagnosed Hypertension Among Patients With Routine Care in Hawaiʻi
Authors: Mika D. Thompson, Yan Yan Wu, Blythe Nett, Lance K. Ching, Hermina Taylor, Tiffany Lemmen, Tetine L. Sentell, Meghan D. McGurk, Catherine M. Pirkle
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis real‐world evaluation considers an algorithm designed to detect patients with potentially undiagnosed hypertension, receiving routine care, in a large health system in Hawaiʻi. It quantifies patients identified as potentially undiagnosed with hypertension; summarizes the individual, clinical, and health system factors associated with undiagnosed hypertension; and examines if the COVID‐19 pandemic affected detection.Methods and ResultsWe analyzed the electronic health records of patients treated across 6 clinics from 2018 to 2021. We calculated total patients with potentially undiagnosed hypertension and compared patients flagged for undiagnosed hypertension to those with diagnosed hypertension and to the full patient panel across individual characteristics, clinical and health system factors (eg, clinic of care), and timing. Modified Poisson regression was used to calculate crude and adjusted risk ratios. Among the eligible patients (N=13 364), 52.6% had been diagnosed with hypertension, 2.7% were flagged as potentially undiagnosed, and 44.6% had no evidence of hypertension. Factors associated with a higher risk of potentially undiagnosed hypertension included individual characteristics (ages 40–84 compared with 18–39 years), clinical (lack of diabetes diagnosis) and health system factors (clinic site and being a Medicaid versus a Medicare beneficiary), and timing (readings obtained after the COVID‐19 Stay‐At‐Home Order in Hawaiʻi).ConclusionsThis evaluation provided evidence that a clinical algorithm implemented within a large health system's electronic health records could detect patients in need of follow‐up to determine hypertension status, and it identified key individual characteristics, clinical and health system factors, and timing considerations that may contribute to undiagnosed hypertension among patients receiving routine care.

Hypertension affects half of the adult population in the United States, 1 with the number of adults with hypertension worldwide doubling over the past 3 decades. 2 In the United States, hypertension presents a substantial financial burden, with the total associated medical costs estimated at $131 billion annually. 3 Hypertension is an influential component to many life‐threatening cardiovascular outcomes, such as stroke and heart failure, 4 and is strongly associated with morbidity and premature mortality. 5 In 2019, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention listed hypertension as a primary or contributing cause of over half a million deaths in the United States. 6 Despite robust clinical standards for diagnosing and managing hypertension, 7 undiagnosed hypertension remains a significant public health issue around the world. 8 In the United States, an estimated 11 million people (5.9%) who have a usual source of care have undiagnosed hypertension. 9 Those without a usual source of care are even more likely to have an undiagnosed chronic disease. 10

Growing evidence demonstrates that even patients with access to regular health care can go undiagnosed. 11 , 12 For example, in a nationally representative analysis of undiagnosed hypertension in the United States, over 80% had health insurance or a usual source of care and were termed as “hiding in plain sight.” 12 System‐wide interventions to identify undiagnosed cases of hypertension within health care settings could improve clinical care and reduce the economic and health consequences of undiagnosed and unmanaged hypertension. Automated algorithms, a health information technology (HIT), may provide an opportunity to identify patients with undiagnosed hypertension to initiate management, improve health outcomes, and reduce costs. 13 These algorithms can identify potential cases of a condition based on abnormal test results found in patients' electronic health records (EHR) and flag patients who fit a clinical definition but lack a record of diagnosis or management of the condition. EHR algorithms have been used to identify patients with many potentially undiagnosed chronic conditions, such as diabetes 14 , 15 , 16 and hypertension. 8 , 9 , 12 , 17 , 18 , 19

Hypertension is a condition particularly amenable to the application of automated EHR algorithms given the ubiquity of blood pressure assessments during routine clinical encounters. The US federal government has called for the use of clinical decision support systems that incorporate EHR algorithms to improve quality of care. 20 , 21 For instance, the strong potential for HIT to improve quality of care has long been recognized by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid established a program that provides incentive payments to health centers that adopt and demonstrate meaningful use of EHRs, especially through the use of HIT. 22 However, despite the promising potential of such interventions in clinical settings to improve the detection and management of hypertension, 9 limited empirical research tests the application of such technology in practice, nor is it commonly evaluated in real‐world clinical settings. 23 , 24 , 25

Real‐world evaluations offer several strengths that contribute to the growing body of literature surrounding the use of EHR algorithms to detect undiagnosed hypertension. Use of real‐world patient data may reflect the diversity and complexity of actual clinical encounters and may incorporate events that transpire during the evaluation process; moreover, the evaluation of real‐world interventions may inform clinical practice, health outcomes research, and quality improvement, while providing evidence of feasibility. 26 The National Institute on Minority Health and Health Disparities 2015 to 2025 Strategic Goals prioritize the need for research in real‐world settings in order to design and improve interventions and reduce health disparities. 27 Specifically, they call for the incorporation of “elements of implementation and scalability into the design and testing of interventions to enhance related effectiveness in real‐world settings, particularly low‐resource clinical and community settings that serve health disparity populations.” In an article published in the American Journal of Public Health, it was highlighted that the National Institute on Minority Health and Health Disparities envisioned addressing known health determinants, such as hypertension, through interventions applied in real‐life settings and that were sustainable and realistic to life conditions. 28

Hawaiʻi‐based health systems offer a unique opportunity to assess the use of automated algorithms in EHRs within a population largely comprising understudied and underrepresented groups, including Native Hawaiians (NH), Other Pacific Islanders (OPI), and diverse Asian groups. 29 Moreover, NH and OPI often lack representation in medical schools and among the physician population in Hawaiʻi, further emphasizing the need to address health disparities and ensure equitable health care services for these communities. 30 , 31 Hawaiʻi adults have the highest predicted prevalence of undiagnosed hypertension in the United States (6.5%), with several predominant racial and ethnic groups in the state experiencing a disproportionate burden of diagnosed hypertension. 32 , 33 Implicit biases on the part of providers may contribute to unequal care and treatment of minority groups. 34 , 35 , 36 Although there are evident disparities of diagnosed hypertension in Hawaiʻi, it remains unclear whether similar disparities exist for undiagnosed hypertension. In other words, are certain groups more likely to be missed than others?

Using a patient panel with demonstrated access to and use of health care services allows for an evaluation centered on quality of care in a real‐life setting. That is, we can ascertain whether health care providers are missing patients who receive routine care and whether there are feasible and scalable HIT interventions that can improve the care patients receive by better identifying individuals who may be in need of hypertension treatment and management.

We conducted a real‐world evaluation 37 of an automated algorithm designed to identify patients with undiagnosed hypertension based on scanning their EHR for blood pressure measurements and the presence or absence of a hypertension diagnosis. This data mining algorithm was implemented within the Queen's Clinically Integrated Physician Network, a physician organization within the Queen's Health System (QHS). The objectives were to (1) detect potentially undiagnosed hypertension to examine how the implemented algorithm was performing within the health care setting, (2) identify individual characteristics and clinical and health system factors associated with the risk of potentially undiagnosed hypertension, and (3) assess whether there is evidence that the COVID‐19 pandemic affected the detection of hypertension through routine care. The latter objective emerged as some of the evaluation period overlapped with the early stages of the pandemic.

Methods

The data underlying this article are contained in patient EHRs with use restrictions based on the data use agreement between the QHS and the Hawaiʻi Department of Health, Chronic Disease Prevention & Health Promotion Division. Data are not publicly available. However, a limited data set may be available from the authors upon reasonable request and with permission of QHS.

Design and Setting

This quality improvement evaluation examined the deidentified EHR data of patients from 6 Queen's Clinically Integrated Physician Network clinics on Oʻahu. QHS serves a patient population that is approximately 25% NH and OPI, 20% White, 20% Japanese, and 17% Filipino. Automated algorithms were developed by the Hawaiʻi Department of Health. The algorithm was supported using pilot funding from 2 Centers for Disease Control and Prevention cooperative agreements (DP13‐1305 and DP18‐1815). An evaluation was implemented to assess the algorithm function in practice. QHS administrators selected 6 clinics at which to apply the automated algorithm. These clinics provide primary care and some specialty services. Deidentified patient data from July 2, 2018, to July 1, 2021 were provided. Use of these deidentified secondary data for evaluation was deemed nonhuman subjects research by the Hawaiʻi Department of Health and University of Hawai‘i Institutional Review Boards per the Revised Common Rule of 2018.

Intervention

The algorithm was developed to be evidence based and grounded in clinical recommendations from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Million Hearts Initiative. In brief, the algorithm pulls patients' EHR data in a population health management system, searches for hypertension diagnoses, and screens for in‐office blood pressure measurements. Subsequent to these steps, different prompts are presented to the patient's primary care provider with recommended actions for care. QHS implemented the algorithms. The algorithms were applied at the population health management level to resolve interoperability issues that occur when providers or services within a health system use different EHR software. Patients flagged by the algorithm for potentially undiagnosed hypertension were listed in a registry created by a software called Population Builder. External analysis of patient data, including those flagged by the algorithm, was conducted by program evaluation researchers at the University of Hawaiʻi.

EHR Algorithm

The EHR algorithm (Figure S1) was applied to the panel of patients who were 18 years or older and had at least 1 encounter with a physician at 1 of the 6 clinics during the 3‐year period. This was done to ensure that the panel consisted of patients actively engaged in health care, rather than those lost to follow‐up or inactive. First, patients with unresolved physician‐diagnosed hypertension were identified from the patients’ active problem list using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) codes 401–405 and 410.9, and Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes I10‐I15 and I21.3. The algorithm then excluded the remaining patients based on 1 or more of the following criteria: (1) those with resolved hypertension (ie, hypertension that is no longer on the problem list and lacked 2 systolic and diastolic blood pressure measurements ≥140/90 mm Hg in the past 3 years or 1 measurement ≥160/100 mm Hg in the past year); (2) those who screened for hypertension within the 1 year before the end of the measurement period, but a diagnosis was deemed inappropriate; (3) those with an active diagnosis of end‐stage renal disease (ascertained through EHR using ICD‐9 codes: 585.5, 585.6, V42.0, V45.1, and V56, and ICD‐10 codes: N18.5, N18.6, Z49, Z94.0, Z91.15, and Z99.2); (4) those with no hypertension diagnosis but were taking hypertension medication; and (5) those who were deceased by the end of the measurement period. Lastly, blood pressure readings within the remaining patients' EHR were scanned to flag patients based on the following rules: (1) 2 continuously measured systolic or diastolic blood pressure measurements ≥140/90 mm Hg in the past 3 years, or (2) 1 measurement ≥160/100 mm Hg in the past year.

Covariates

We compared potentially undiagnosed cases to diagnosed ones across individual characteristics, clinical and health system factors, and timing. The covariates described next were selected based on evidence in the literature that these factors may be associated with hypertension diagnosis. Measures were extracted from patients' EHRs.

Individual Characteristics

These included patient demographics: age in years (at extraction date) grouped into categories (18–39; 39–65; 65–85; or 85–106), sex (male/female), race and ethnicity (East Asian, Filipino, NH, Other, OPI, or White). Race and ethnicity were self‐reported by patients and recorded in their EHR. Coding for the multiple response race and ethnicity variable was derived using the following rules: (1) if NH was one of the multiple ethnicities listed, “Native Hawaiian” was coded; (2) if a non‐White ethnicity was listed with a White ethnicity, the non‐White ethnicity was coded; and (3) if there was more than 1 non‐White ethnicity listed, Other Pacific Islander ethnicities were coded first, followed by Filipino and East Asian. Because all patients in the panel endorsing more than 1 non‐White ethnicity were multiple East Asian ethnicities (eg, Japanese and Korean), these patients were coded as “East Asian.” Patients classified as “Other” race and ethnicity included American Indian, Alaska Native, Black, and Hispanic. A potential limitation of using this method of creating mutually exclusive race and ethnicity categories is that the categories created may include substantial proportions of multiracial patients, especially given Hawaiʻi's large multiracial population. 29

Patient behavioral measures included any tobacco use (yes/no; ICD‐10 codes F17, F17.20‐F17.22, F17.29, Z72.0, Z57.31, Z72.0, Z77.22, and Z87.891) and any alcohol use (yes/no) extracted from social history.

Clinical and Health System Factors

These measures reflected health concerns documented in the EHR, as well as clinic site and insurer. Measures included the most recent categorical body mass index (underweight, <18.5; normal [18.5–25.0]; overweight [25.0–30.0]; or obese, ≥30.0) and any mental health conditions (yes/no; ICD‐10 codes F20‐F29, F30‐F39, F40‐F48, and F60‐F69). A 5‐category indicator for prediabetes and diabetes was also included. The first 2 categories reflected a diagnosis of prediabetes or diabetes in the EHR (ICD‐10 codes E08, E09, E10, E11, E13, and R73.03). We used the most recently available A1C value to categorize the remaining patients. Those with an A1C <5.7 were categorized as without diabetes. Those with an A1C of ≥5.7, but without a diagnosis, were considered potential cases of prediabetes or diabetes. 38 Finally, there was a group of patients who lacked any diabetes diagnosis or A1C value in 3 years.

Insurer was categorized into Medicare, Blue Cross Blue Shield, Medicaid, or Other (eg, uninsured, Department of Defense). Clinic site was anonymized, and each was assigned a number.

Timing

To assess the possible effect of the pandemic on risk of potentially undiagnosed hypertension, patients' records were split into 2 time periods: before and after Hawaiʻi's first statewide Stay‐At‐Home Order. 39 This emergency proclamation, requiring all Hawai‘i residents to remain home for all nonessential activities, took effect on March 25, 2020. Just before this declaration, Centers for Medicare and Medicaid announced that nonessential medical procedures should be delayed to preserve medical supplies. 40 Decisions on which services were nonessential were left to state and local officials. Immediately following the order, health care services were severely restricted, with many non‐COVID‐19‐related appointments postponed or canceled, including routine and preventative care. 41 , 42 The clinics in this evaluation reduced in‐person appointments during the postpandemic period. The timing variable roughly captured whether the automated algorithm flagged patients for potentially undiagnosed hypertension before or after the order.

Statistical Analysis

Patient characteristics were summarized by frequency and percentage across patient groups according to the full panel, those with diagnosed hypertension, and those flagged with potentially undiagnosed hypertension.

Using modified Poisson regression with a robust error variance, 43 we calculated the crude and adjusted relative risk (RR) and 95% CI for patients flagged with potentially undiagnosed hypertension by patient characteristics. The modified Poisson regression analysis was selected over multiple logistic regressions due to the potential for odds ratios to overestimate the relative risk when the reference group probability is >5%. 44 Moreover, the generalized estimating equation model is a nonparametric method, which is less sensitive to outliers relative to the traditional generalized linear model approach. 43 For the adjusted RR estimates, we created 3 multivariate models. The first examined only the individual characteristics. The second added the clinical and health system factors, and the third added the COVID‐19 timing variable. Adjustment sets for the multivariate models reflected the modeling strategy; by gradually adding these groups of factors, these analyses may explore the influence of 3 levels of explanatory variables on potentially undiagnosed hypertension and gain insights on the interplay between these levels. Analyses were conducted using R version 4.1.2 with integrated development environment RStudio version 1.4.1106.

Figure 1 presents the derivation of the analytic panel. There were 14 497 eligible patients, 7032 of whom had a hypertension diagnosis and 7465 who did not. Among the 7465 with no diagnosis, 788 were excluded based on exclusion criteria, and 345 were excluded due to missing covariates, resulting in a final analytic panel of 13 364 patients. Of these, the automated algorithm flagged 366 (2.7%) for potential undiagnosed hypertension. Most patients in the analytic panel were 40 years of age or older (79.5%). The analytic panel skewed slightly female (55.6%). East Asian was the most prevalent racial and ethnic group (29.2%), followed by White (26.7%) and Filipino (17.1%). Tobacco use was 9.4%, and about half of patients reported alcohol use. About a quarter of patients were diagnosed with diabetes and 17.8% diagnosed with prediabetes. A large proportion of patients derived from Clinic 5 (37.9%), followed by Clinic 2 (15.8%) and Clinic 1 (14.1%). Most patients were insured through Medicare or Blue Cross Blue Shield, with a small percentage using Medicaid (7.6%). Finally, the majority of patients' readings were recorded after the stay‐at‐home order (64.8%).

Potentially Undiagnosed Versus Diagnosed Hypertension

The proportion of patients with potentially undiagnosed hypertension significantly differed by age group (P=0.02), clinic (P<0.01), insurer (P=0.04), mental health (P=0.02), (pre)diabetes and A1C (P<0.01), and whether readings were obtained before or after the order (P<0.01) (Table 1). Those with diagnosed prediabetes were nearly twice as likely (RR, 1.97 [95% CI, 1.28–3.03]) to have potentially undiagnosed hypertension relative to those with diagnosed diabetes. However, the percentage of undiagnosed hypertension among those with diagnosed prediabetes (2.3%) was less than that of potentially undiagnosed hypertension patients in the full panel (2.7%). Those with evidence of prediabetes (A1C 4.3–5.6, no diagnosis; crude RR, 3.37 [95% CI, 2.26–5.03]) or diabetes (A1C≥5.7, no diagnosis; crude RR, 3.72 [95% CI, 2.37–5.82]), based on A1C value, were also over 3 times as likely to be flagged for undiagnosed hypertension. Those with no A1C records and no diagnosis of (pre)diabetes (no diagnosis and no A1C) were also more likely to be flagged (crude RR, 2.70 [95% CI, 1.97–3.35]) compared with patients with diagnosed diabetes; however, the percentage of potentially undiagnosed hypertension among those with no A1C record and no (pre)diabetes diagnosis (3.1%) was similar to the percentage in the full panel. Over 8% of all patients at Clinic 6 were flagged. Those on Medicaid (4.1%) were 54% more likely to be flagged (crude RR, 1.54 [95% CI, 1.09–2.17]) compared with those on Medicare (2.7%). Before the order, only 1.4% of patients followed at these 6 clinics were flagged, whereas afterward, 3.5% of patients were flagged.

The second model added clinical and health system factors. Age remained significantly associated with undiagnosed hypertension. Medicaid was significantly associated with being flagged compared with Medicare (aRR, 1.72 [95% CI, 1.05–2.84]). Similar to the unadjusted models in Table 1, those with diagnosed diabetes were significantly less likely to be flagged compared with all other (pre)diabetes categories. The strongest association, as observed in the unadjusted model, was with clinic site. Those followed at Clinic 6 were nearly 6 times more likely to be flagged than those at Clinic 1 (aRR, 5.91 [95% CI, 3.80–9.21]).

The final model added the timing of the most recent reading either before or after the order. The association with age (aRR, 1.42 [95% CI, 1.04–1.95] for middle‐aged adults, and aRR, 1.62 [95% CI, 1.01–2.59] for older adults) and Medicaid (aRR, 1.74 [95% CI, 1.06–2.86]) remained, while the elevated risk associated with Clinic 6 relative to Clinic 1 strengthened (aRR, 6.81 [95% CI, 4.38–10.60]). Flagging for undiagnosed hypertension increased at all clinics after the order, with a notable increase for Clinic 6 (3.7% to 10.7%, Table S1). Those with diabetes remained far less likely to be flagged than any other category. The effect estimates for most of the (pre)diabetes and A1C categories changed very little between models. Finally, the risk of being flagged was over twice as high after the order compared with before (aRR, 2.63 [95% CI, 1.96–3.54]).

Discussion

This real‐world evaluation described the frequency of, and factors associated with, potentially undiagnosed hypertension as identified by an automated algorithm implemented at 6 clinics in a large health care system in Hawaiʻi. Of the 13 364 patients, we found that 2.7% had potentially undiagnosed hypertension based on blood pressure measurements in their EHR. The risk of being flagged for potentially undiagnosed hypertension significantly differed by age group, clinic, insurer, (pre)diabetes diagnosis and A1C, and whether blood pressure was measured before or after the order.

Hypertension was prevalent among patients followed at these clinics, which principally provided primary care services. Approximately half of patients had a hypertension diagnosis. Comparatively, 30% of the population in Hawaiʻi self‐reported hypertension in 2019. 45 Self‐report underestimates population prevalence given that hypertension awareness is consistently and substantially lower than actual prevalence. 32 The risk of hypertension is also well known to increase with advancing age, 1 , 46 and approximately 40% of patients attending these clinics were 65 or older. It is estimated that 77% of older adults in the United States have hypertension. 47

Just under 3% of patients followed at the 6 clinics were potentially undiagnosed for hypertension. This was less than the predicted prevalence of 6.5% undiagnosed hypertension in the general population of Hawaiʻi, 32 a finding that was expected given that the panel consisted of patients with a usual source of care and who were observed in the past 3 years. A study of approximately 9 million US patients comparing the number of observed hypertension cases and the number of predicted cases found that the nearly 13% of patients with hypertension may not be diagnosed. 9 Similarly, Huguet and colleagues 48 examined a large sample of patients from a network of community health centers across 14 US states and found that 37.3% of patients had undiagnosed hypertension, with 24.9% of cases remaining undiagnosed for the 5‐year study. Despite methodological differences, these estimates far exceed the percentage of potentially undiagnosed hypertension observed among patients in this panel. This difference may reflect a strong health system or the high health insurance coverage in Hawaiʻi.

The only individual factor associated with undiagnosed hypertension was age but not sex or race and ethnicity. There is extensive literature showing that women are less likely to receive preventative treatment or guidance for cardiovascular disease, 49 possibly reflecting biases in the scientific literature and medical training about cardiovascular disease in women. 50 Despite notable differences in both the risk and awareness of hypertension by race and ethnicity in the existing literature, 51 , 52 the observed differences in this study were marginal. Disparities by race and ethnicity are often attributed to structural and social factors and cultural norms, such as health care access, providers' racial and ethnic implicit bias in diagnosis and treatment, and beliefs about seeking medical help for preventive care. 53 , 54 , 55 NH have experienced multigenerational injustice, discrimination, and cultural suppression resulting from the American overthrow and colonization of the Hawaiian Islands; these events have been linked to the substantial disparities in many health risk factors and outcomes observed today within the state. 31 , 56 However, the use of patients with recent encounters with health services may have hindered our ability to detect differences by race and ethnicity that we may have otherwise observed in the general population. Moreover, although literature on health services use in Hawaiʻi does indicate underuse by NH and OPI, the very high insurance coverage in the state helps to reduce disparities associated with access to care due to lack of insurance. 57

Several clinical and health system factors were associated with undiagnosed hypertension, most notably the service clinic. Patients at Clinic 6 were nearly 7 times as likely to be flagged as those at Clinic 1. The high percentage of patients with potentially undiagnosed hypertension at Clinic 6 may be related to physician turnover. Clinic 6 experienced a significant turnover in providers during the data period; specifically, 2 of the 4 full‐time providers left the clinic. Prior studies demonstrate associations between physician turnover and reductions in timely care, 58 patient satisfaction, and organizational stability. 59 The loss of half of the full‐time providers may have resulted in delays in new provider workflow orientation or a lack of access to care while patients are being transitioned to new providers. Additionally, the high percentage of diagnosed hypertension and low percentage of potentially undiagnosed hypertension at Clinic 1 could be attributed to Clinic 1's strong provider champions of panel management. Panel management, a population‐based care approach where clinics proactively monitor the health of and care for all patients in their panel, not just those who come in for urgent care needs, 60 has been associated with increased screening for and improved care for chronic conditions. 61 , 62

Medicaid beneficiaries had a higher likelihood of being flagged compared with Medicare beneficiaries. Given our statistical models, and the fact that our panel was limited to patients with regular contact with a health provider, this finding is independent of the considered risk factors of the demographic and behavioral risk factors for hypertension, including age and race and ethnicity, as well as access to health services. This suggests that broader social factors related to Medicaid eligibility (ie, low income) may drive the observed disparity between Medicaid and Medicare beneficiaries regarding potentially undiagnosed hypertension, such as higher order health needs or a lack of access to healthy lifestyles.

There was also an association between different indicators of diabetes and potentially undiagnosed hypertension; most notably, those with evidence of prediabetes with no diagnosis were significantly more likely to also have potentially undiagnosed hypertension. One hypothesis is that patients with missing information on one condition may be more likely to be missing an appropriate hypertension diagnosis as well.

Lastly, significantly more individuals were flagged for undiagnosed hypertension after the first statewide Stay‐At‐Home Order for COVID‐19. This suggests that the pandemic may have had an impact on the detection and diagnosis of hypertension. Moreover, the pandemic may have had an effect on patients' blood pressure due to stress, fear, job loss, and other related factors. The conditions during this period, such as Hawaiʻi's disproportionately high unemployment rate, 63 increases in mental health issues, including depression, suicidality, and isolation, likely contributed to the high blood pressure rates. 64 , 65 , 66 Furthermore, during the pandemic, routine medical appointments and procedures were restricted, 40 , 67 and people delayed care out of fear of exposure to COVID‐19. 68 As a result, it is possible that those who were seeking medical attention had more urgent health needs that took priority over diagnosis of hypertension.

This evaluation of a quality improvement intervention has several strengths. First, even though data came from only 6 clinics, we had a large enough population from which to examine associations with potentially undiagnosed hypertension, which was a relatively rare event. Given the high prevalence of hypertension within our panel, the detection of undiagnosed cases is critical to the improvement of the health system. Furthermore, the panel was ethnically diverse, including underserved populations such as NH and OPI, which may provide insights that can be generalized to other health systems serving diverse populations. The algorithm assessed by this evaluation was carefully crafted and received strong buy‐in from QHS, which demonstrates the potential for wider implementation and scalability throughout the system. Conducting this evaluation in Hawaiʻi, a state with high health insurance coverage, greatly reduced the potential for selection bias based on financial access to care. Lastly, this work assesses the performance of an EHR algorithm in a real‐world clinical setting.

Our evaluation has limitations. First, a relatively small percentage of cases were flagged as undiagnosed, potentially limiting our ability to detect statistically significant differences between some groups, particularly OPI; however, with a large analytic panel size, the number of undiagnosed cases were sufficient to draw meaningful conclusions across most groups. The data did not include how often patients used health care services, which could affect detection of undiagnosed hypertension. It is unclear whether the number of visits varied differentially by the considered factors. All variables were derived from an EHR; as a result, we could not follow up with flagged patients to confirm undiagnosed hypertension. That is, if a patient with a high blood pressure reading simultaneously presented with an acute condition associated with elevated blood pressure, a physician may decide to postpone diagnosing hypertension and may later deem a diagnosis inappropriate, resulting in a misclassification. Selection bias through health care access/use may affect the generalizability of our findings. As mentioned, Hawaiʻi has a large multiracial population (nearly 25%). 29 The use of single race and ethnicity categories in this study may have resulted in substantial proportions of multiracial patients being combined with patients with single races and ethnicities, which limited our clear examination of differences by race and ethnicity. Moreover, the collection of information on race and ethnicity is complex and challenging for several inherent reasons, including a lack of standard definitions, differences between the constructs of race and ethnicity, and the influence of acculturation and assimilation, among others. 69 Lastly, we could not account for masked hypertension, a hypertension subtype in which a patient has normal blood pressure readings out‐of‐office while having hypertensive readings out of office. 70 , 71 The prevalence of masked hypertension ranges between 10% and 17% using daytime measurements; however, the prevalence may be far greater when accounting for daytime, nighttime, and ambulatory measurements. 72

It is important to acknowledge that the findings from this evaluation pertain to active patients with at least 1 physician encounter during the 3‐year study. The panel, thus, consisted only of patients with access to and recent use of health care services, and therefore, may not represent the general population of Hawaiʻi. Individuals who are unable or unwilling to see a health provider may be more likely to have undiagnosed hypertension, especially those without other conditions that may prompt health services use.

Although the percentage of patients flagged in this panel for undiagnosed hypertension was relatively low (2.7%), the high prevalence of hypertension in the general population translates to a high absolute number of cases without an appropriate diagnosis. Applying an estimate of 2.7% to a population of approximately 1.1 million adults in Hawaiʻi, 29 30 000 people would have undiagnosed hypertension and could benefit from hypertension management. As discussed, this is an underestimate of the true prevalence of undiagnosed hypertension in the population and highlights the need to improve detection.

Our findings warrant additional investigative efforts to understand the underlying reasons certain patients lacked a formal hypertension diagnosis despite having blood pressure readings indicative of hypertension. The next step would be to further examine the flagged patients to quantify those cases that were confirmed after medical review of the patient records or a visit with the patient's care provider. Additionally, a crucial future direction would be to explore which actions were taken following a flag, the time between the flagging and changes made in the care process, and whether individuals were able to achieve a normotensive status through these actions.

Conclusions

This real‐world evaluation underscores the value of leveraging EHR algorithms in identifying patients with potentially undiagnosed hypertension and highlights several factors associated with the lack of diagnosis that can be used to improve diagnosis of hypertension, the leading contributor to death in the United States. Our quality improvement evaluation provides evidence that health systems may leverage HIT in identifying, preventing, and managing patients with hypertension.
