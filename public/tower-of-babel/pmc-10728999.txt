Non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels as a risk factor for short-term mortality in elderly Chinese: a large-scale, population-based cohort study
Authors: Weiwei Xiao, Shiqi Liu, Zheng Huang, Donghui Jin, Yiping Yang, Fei Li, Jingwen Duan, Jie Wang, Yalan Li, Peizhi Deng, Wei Wang, Changjiang Meng, Jingjing Wu, Jingjing Cai, Yan Li, Yuan Hong
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo explore the association between non-high-density lipoprotein (non-HDL) and mortality risk, both short-term and long-term, in Chinese people.DesignA prospective cohort study.SettingThe National Basic Public Health Service (BPHS) in China.ParticipantsIncluding 621 164 elderly individuals around Hunan Province who underwent healthcare management receiving check-ups in China BPHS from 2010 to 2020. Exclusion criteria: (1) missing information on gender; (2) missing records of lipid screening; (3) missing information on key covariates; and (4) missing records of comorbidities (cardiovascular disease, hypertension, diabetes, cancer.)Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe study’s primary endpoint was all-cause and cause-specific mortality, sourced from Hunan’s CDC(Center for Disease Control and Prevention)-operated National Mortality Surveillance System, tracking participants until 24 February 2021.Results26 758 (4.3%) deaths were recorded, with a median follow-up of 0.83 years. Association between non-HDL and mortality was non-linear after multivariable adjustment, with the optimum concentration (OC) being 3.29 and 4.85 mmol/L. Compared with OC, the risk increased by 1.12-fold for non-HDL <3.29 mmol/L (HR: 1.12 (1.09 to 1.15)) and 1.08-fold for non-HDL ≥4.85 mmol/L (HR: 1.08 (1.02 to 1.13)) for all-cause mortality. Furthermore, there is also an increased risk of cardiovascular mortality (HR for non-HDL <3.29: 1.10 (1.06 to 1.32) and HR for non-HDL ≥4.85: 1.07 (1.01 to 1.14)). However, cancer mortality risk was significantly increased only for non-HDL <3.29 mmol/L (HR: 1.11 (1.04 to 1.18)). Non-optimum concentration of non-HDL had significant effects on both the long-term and the short-term risk of mortality, especially for risks of mortality for all-cause (log HR:0 .086 (0.038 to 0.134)), cardiovascular (log HR:0 .082 (0.021 to 0.144)), and cancer (log HR:0 .187 (0.058 to 0.315)) within 3 months. A two-sided value of p <0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.ConclusionsNon-HDL was non-linearly associated with the risk of mortality, and non-optimal concentrations of non-HDL significantly increased short-term mortality in elderly Chinese, which needs more attention for cardiovascular disease prevention.Keywords: Aging, Adult cardiology, Cardiac Epidemiology, China, Lipid disorders

Introduction

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases (ASCVD), characterised by atherosclerosis, including ischaemic heart disease and ischaemic stroke, are the major causes of death worldwide,1–3 especially in elderly people. Dyslipidaemias are the primary cause and have a significant contribution to the initiation, development and complications of atherosclerosis.4 High low-density lipoprotein (LDL) is commonly accepted as proatherogenic cholesterol,5 and the prevailing view favours oxidised LDL particles promoting atherogenesis.4 6 Other lipid compositions, including high-density lipoprotein (HDL), triglyceride and lipoprotein(a) have been shown to have a strong relationship with atherosclerotic risk.4 Therefore, as an important cause of ASCVD, dyslipidaemias are also the potential targets to prevent premature death.

Expanding on previous studies, the investigators have proposed the concepts of remnant cholesterol (estimated as total cholesterol (TC) minus LDL and HDL) and non-HDL (estimated as TC minus HDL), which do not require direct blood sample testing. Observational study results have revealed these measures to be independent predictors of cardiovascular event risk.7–10 Compared with LDL, non-HDL is more highly associated with cardiovascular events in patients receiving statin therapy for dyslipidaemias.11 Furthermore, non-HDL was introduced into ESC/EAS (European Society of Cardiology /European Atherosclerosis Society (EAS))guidelines for the management of dyslipidaemias and other guidelines from different countries as a secondary target for lipid-lowering therapy.12–14 For the population with different ASCVD risk classes, the attainment target values of non-HDL for lipid-lowering therapy are inconsistent. However, the evidence of non-HDL and mortality remains uncertain. Previous studies about it were conducted in patients with comorbidity. A recent study of 19 604 patients in China with acute ischaemic stroke shows a U-shaped relationship between non-HDL and all-cause death within 1 year.15 There were inconsistent results from several studies for the association between non-HDL and cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality, showing the relations including U-shaped,15 16 positive,9 or negative.17 Therefore, the relationship between non-HDL and mortality in the general population and whether there is a specific minimum mortality concentration of non-HDL remains unclear, especially for the Chinese population.

We attempt to investigate the association between non-HDL and long-term or short-term mortality in the large-scale general elderly Chinese population whether combined with cardiovascular diseases (CVD) or not, which can provide deeper evidence to clarify the impact of non-HDL on mortality to better prevention for ASCVD and mortality.

Data collection

The database for this study was obtained from the National Basic Public Health Service (BPHS) in China.18 The BPHS is funded by the Chinese government and implemented by primary healthcare (PHC) institutions including community health centres and sites in urban areas township health centres and village health offices in rural areas to provide disease screening, health checkups, chronic disease management and health education for residents in their areas, to promote equity and accessibility of public health services. Since the initiation of BPHS in 2009, healthcare management for the aged has always been a major focus of BPHS. When the elderly receive services at primary healthcare institutions, medical staff will establish resident health records for the participants, recording information including basic personal information, health check-up information, health management information and medical history of treatment at other medical institutions. The elderly receive an annual free medical check-up, including four free health management services: lifestyle and health status assessment, physical examination, auxiliary examination and health guidance.19 To guarantee the reliability of data from PHC, regulatory authorities (usually the Health Commission) evaluated primary healthcare institutions on a quarterly, semiannual and annual basis, and surveyed subjects by telephone or conducted unannounced visits to ensure the programme would be accurately and effectively implemented.

Study sample

This study comprised 880 445 elderly individuals who underwent health check-ups at PHC institutions in Hunan Province, China, from 2010 to 2020. Their first physical check-up records were extracted for inclusion in our study as a baseline. We excluded the following participants: (1) missing information on gender; (2) missing records of lipid screening; (3) missing information on key covariates; and (4) missing records of comorbidities (CVD, hypertension, diabetes, cancer), with a final total of 621 164 participants included in the study constituting a cohort study, as shown in figure 1. For all participants, written informed consent was obtained from each patient.

Assessment of exposure and endpoints

Fasting venous blood samples were collected and were immediately processed and analysed at the local PHC. The TC was measured by the oxidative enzyme method and HDL by the direct measurement method. Non-HDL was calculated as TC minus HDL. Detailed methods are provided in the online supplemental eMethods.

bmjopen-2023-078216supp001.pdf

The primary endpoint of our study was all-cause and cause-specific mortality. The information on deaths was obtained from the National Mortality Surveillance System, operated by the Center for Disease Control and Prevention of Hunan province. Using the ID number from the residents' health records to match with NMSS(National Mortality Surveillance System) to obtain the time of death, cause of death and International Classification of Diseases 10th Edition (ICD-10) code. If there was a cardiovascular diagnosis (ICD-10 codes I00-I90), the cause of death was classified as cardiovascular mortality, but if there was a cancer diagnosis (ICD-10 codes C00-C96), it was categorised as cancer mortality. The remaining deaths that were neither cardiovascular deaths nor cancer deaths were classified as other mortality. Participants in this study were tracked until the end of 24 February 2021.

Assessment of covariates

In BPHS, lifestyle and health status assessment is to understand the basic health status of the elderly, physical exercise, diet, smoking, alcohol consumption, common symptoms of chronic diseases, previous diseases, treatment and current medication, and self-care ability through consultation and completion of questionnaires. Physical examination includes routine physical examination such as temperature, pulse, respiration, blood pressure, height, weight, waist circumference, skin, superficial lymph nodes, lungs, heart and abdomen. Auxiliary examinations include laboratory analyses, ECG and abdominal ultrasound examination. Health guidance mainly notifies the subject of the medical examination results and provides related health guidance. For example, patients with hypertension and diabetes are provided with appropriate chronic disease health management (therapy and long-term follow-up). Patients suffering from other diseases (not hypertension or diabetes) are treated or referred to higher-level healthcare institutions.

Statistical analyses

Categorical variables were reported as numbers (percentage), and continuous variables were summarised as median (IQR). We used the Kruskal-Wallis test to test the differences for continuous variables and used χ2 to test the differences for categorical variables between OCs and non-optimal concentrations (NOCs) groups.

Non-linear relationship between non-HDL and mortality risk assessed using a restricted cubic spline (RCS) function in a Cox proportional hazards model. The RCS had 5 internal knots (at the 5th, 25th, 50th, 75th and 95th centiles) at non-HDL distributions, which were used to explore the dose–response relationship between non-HDL and all endpoints. The optimal concentrations (OCs)of non-HDL, defined as a concentration with an HR <1 on the spline curve, was used to clarify the association between non-HDL and mortality risk. Finally, analyses were stratified according to sex.

We also estimated the association between the NOCs of non-HDL and all-mortality and cause-specific mortality with OCs of non-HDL as the reference. The effect of NOCs of non-HDL on long-term and short-term mortality was assessed using a multivariable-adjusted Cox proportional hazards model. Cox proportional hazards models were adjusted for some covariates: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), current smoking, exercise, systolic blood pressure, lipid-lowering treatment and history of disease including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, cancer and CVD at baseline.

In sensitivity analyses, to clarify the relationship between non-HDL and risk of death, we excluded patients with CVD, cancer, at the start of the study, and those who died in the first year. Considering that there is a competing risk between cardiovascular mortality, cancer mortality and other mortality, we further used Fine-Gray semiparametric proportional subdistribution hazards models to validate the relationship.20

A two-sided value of p <0.05 was considered to be statistically significant, and no failure to the proportional hazard assumption was found in all Cox proportional hazards models. All statistical analyses were carried out using Stata V.16.0 and R V.4.0.3.

Association non-HDL level and short-term and long-term mortality

With the results of RCS, we evaluated the association of NOCs of non-HDL with mortality at different times of follow-up. The Cox proportional hazards model results showed that NOCs of non-HDL significantly increased the risk of all-cause mortality at 3 months (log HR: 0.086; 95% CI 0.038 to 0.134, p=0.001). NOCs of non-HDL posed the most significant impact on cause-specific mortality within 3 months, especially increased the risk of cardiovascular mortality (log HR: 0.082; 95% CI 0.021 to 0.144, p=0.08) and the risk of cancer mortality (log HR: 0.187; 95% CI 0.058 to 0.315, p=0.04)within 3 months. Moreover, the increase in the risk of all-cause mortality and cause-specific mortality by non-HDL diminished with follow-up time (figure 3).

Association non-HDL and mortality in elderly on lipid-lowering treatment

To examine the effect of non-HDL on the risk of mortality in elderly individuals receiving lipid-lowering treatment or absent, we analysed two separate populations. The results showed that among patients not receiving lipid-lowering treatment, the results were more consistent with the overall population (figure 2 and online supplemental figure S2). In individuals receiving lipid-lowering treatment, although the overall trend showed an approximate inverse J-shaped relationship between non-HDL and the risk of mortality, the predictive HRs consistently contained 1.0 and were not statistically significant.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of the results, a sensitivity analysis was carried out. We excluded individuals who died within the first year and those with known CVD, and cancer, at the baseline. We found that these results were similar to the main analysis (online supplemental figure S3). We additionally used competing risk analysis to exclude the influence of mortality from other causes when analysing cause-specific mortality. The results showed no significant difference in cardiovascular or other mortality whether the analysis of the competing risk was used or not (online supplemental table S2).

Discussion

Non-HDL has been recommended as an important indicator for the prevention of CVD.12–14 Prospective evidence supports that non-fasting lipid profiles, including non-HDL, are as good as fasting lipid profiles in predicting cardiovascular events, making them suitable for large-scale epidemiological studies.21 Our results revealed a non-linear relationship between non-HDL levels and the risk of mortality. Low or high levels of non-HDL increase the risk of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality, and only low levels of non-HDL increase the risk of cancer mortality, particularly within 3 months. To our knowledge, this is the first large population-derived dataset to report the association between non-HDL and both all-cause and cause-specific mortality in the general elderly.

Previous studies focused on non-HDL and mortality in patients with specific diseases. In patients with chronic kidney disease stages 3–5, non-HDL was associated with all-cause death in a U-shaped relationship, with high or low non-HDL levels associated with an increased risk of mortality.16 A recent study also identified a nonlinear relationship between non-HDL and recurrent ischaemic stroke in patients who had an acute ischaemic stroke, compared with non-HDL in the range of 3.17–3.66 mom/L, higher or lower non-HDL levels would significantly increase the risk of recurrent ischaemic stroke.15 Also, non-HDL levels below 70 mg/dL (1.8 mmol/L) were found to significantly increase the risk of bleeding after percutaneous coronary intervention.22 The above studies supported the non-linear relationship between non-HDL and outcome events. As for cancer mortality, non-HDL below 3.29 mmol/L significantly increases the risk of cancer mortality and other mortality in our study. Similar to our study, previous studies have confirmed that non-HDL was negatively linked to breast cancer23 and lung cancer.10 There is no threshold to reduce the risk of mortality in those receiving lipid-lowering treatment. This suggests that further studies may be needed to control for more confounding factors to identify non-HDL lipid-lowering targets in patients receiving lipid-lowering treatment.

Naturally, some studies were exactly inconsistent with our findings. For example, the Framingham Off-spring study of 2516 CVD-free patients aged 25–40 years showed that elevated non-HDL levels in youth were associated with elevated lifetime non-HDL levels and that the incidence of cardiovascular events after 32.6 years of follow-up in the high-non-HDL and low-non-HDL groups was 22.6% and 6.4%, respectively.24 A meta-analysis that includes eight randomised controlled trials and 62 154 participants showed that a 1-SD increase in non-HDL was associated with a 16% increased risk of CVD (HR 1.16; 95% CI 1.12 to 1.19) among patients receiving lipid-lowering treatment.11

One of our major findings is that non-HDL levels are associated with mortality at different follow-up times. We evaluated the impacts of NOCs of non-HDL on short-term and long-term mortality and validated that NOCs of non-HDL significantly increased the risk of short-term (3 months) mortality, particularly for cancer mortality. This may suggest that we should focus on the achievement of non-HDL standards to reduce acute or short-term mortality.

Higher non-HDL levels increase the risk of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality possibly associated with atherosclerosis. Since non-HDL-C is known as ‘bad cholesterol’, it can represent all atherogenic lipoproteins. These lipoproteins accumulate in the intima of the arteries and lead to the formation of atherosclerotic plaques.25 As a result, it increases the incidence of atherosclerosis formation and ASCVD, which results in increased mortality and cardiovascular mortality. Moreover, high non-HDL can represent, to some extent, elevated TC, which contributes to ASCVD.26

The association between low levels of non-HDL and an increased risk of mortality could be explained. Low non-HDL represents low TC or elevated HDL levels. Low TC may reflect a malnutrition state of the body with a ‘cholesterol paradox’, and is associated with an increased risk of mortality.27–29 In addition, the elderly with lower non-HDL are likely to be older, have lower blood pressure, a higher percentage of smoking, and lower BMI, which may contribute to the higher risk of mortality in the lower non-HDL group.

An advantage of our study is that the population included is drawn from China’s basic public health system, comprising different social classes of old people and reflecting the general elderly population in China. It is well established that non-HDL is strongly associated with long-term CVD risk,30 and clear therapeutic attainment values have been proposed in guidelines for different ASCVD risk groups,12 13 but none are specific for the elderly. Our results fill this gap and are crucial for the understanding and attainment of optimal non-HDL levels in the general elderly population, with or without co-existing ASCVD.

Despite the importance of our findings, our research does have some limitations. First, we carried out this study in elderly Chinese individuals, and therefore extrapolation of the results to other nationalities may be limited. Second, there were relatively few records of lipid-lowering drug prescriptions in our dataset. The explanation may be that the BPHS has not focused enough attention on the use of lipid-lowering drugs and that the absolute number of people using it is relatively low; certainly, there may be some numbers missing. Third, this study was observational and may require large randomised controlled trials in the future to confirm our findings.

Conclusion

Our study showed that non-HDL may be a qualified predictor for all-cause mortality and cause-special mortality, which with non-linear associations, particularly in the elderly. Both high or low levels of non-HDL would increase the risk of both all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality. Notably, there were NOCs of non-HDL for risk of short-term mortality.
