Additive Bases: Change of Domain
Boris Bukh, Peter van Hintum, Peter Keevash

Abstract
We consider two questions of Ruzsa on how the minimum size of an additive basis B B of a given set A A depends on the domain of B B . To state these questions, for an abelian group G G and A ⊆ D ⊆ G A\subseteq D\subseteq G we write ℓ D ( A ) = def min { | B | : B ⊆ D , A ⊆ B + B } \ell_{D}(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\min\{|B|:B\subseteq D,\ A\subseteq B+B\} . Ruzsa asked how much larger can ℓ ℤ ​ ( A ) \ell_{\mathbb{Z}}(A) be than ℓ ℚ ​ ( A ) \ell_{\mathbb{Q}}(A) for A ⊂ ℤ A\subset\mathbb{Z} , and how much larger can ℓ ℕ ​ ( A ) \ell_{\mathbb{N}}(A) be than ℓ ℤ ​ ( A ) \ell_{\mathbb{Z}}(A) for A ⊂ ℕ A\subset\mathbb{N} . For the first question we show that if ℓ ℚ ​ ( A ) = n \ell_{\mathbb{Q}}(A)=n then ℓ ℤ ​ ( A ) ≤ 2 ​ n \ell_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)\leq 2n , and that this is tight up to an additive error of at most O ⁡ ( n ) O(\sqrt{n}) . For the second question, we show that if ℓ ℤ ​ ( A ) = n \ell_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)=n then ℓ ℕ ​ ( A ) ≤ O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) \ell_{\mathbb{N}}(A)\leq O(n\log n) , and this is tight up to the constant factor. We also consider these questions for higher order bases. Our proofs use some ideas that are unexpected in this context, including linear algebra and Diophantine approximation.



1
Introduction

Many classical questions of Combinatorial Number Theory concern additive bases,
which are sets
B
such that any natural number
n
can be expressed as a sum
of
k
elements of
B
, for some fixed
k
called the order of the basis.
There is a highly developed theory for the classical bases (see Nathanson
[
1
]
),
where
B
is the set of
r
th powers for some fixed
r
(Waring’s problem, resolved by Hilbert)
or the set of primes (the Schnirel’man-Goldbach Theorem). More generally, a set
B

is a
k
-basis
for
A
if every element of
A

can be written as a sum of
k
elements of
B
.
For example, the Goldbach Conjecture states that the set of primes
is a
2
-basis for the set of even numbers
n>2
,
and the Ternary Goldbach Conjecture, proved by Helfgott
[
2
]
,
states that the set of primes is a
3
-basis for the set of odd numbers
n>5
.
Besides having clear intrinsic interest, such questions have also spurred
many developments in Analytic Number Theory and Harmonic Analysis.

Additive bases of finite sets.

A related natural research direction, initiated by Erdős and Newman
[
3
]
,
considers some fixed finite set
A
and asks for the smallest set
B
such that
A\subseteq B+B
.
They considered a random set
A
of size
n
inside
\{1,\dots,N\}
,
where for
N>n^{2+c}
they showed that typically one needs
|B|>nc/(1+c)
.
For
N=n^{2}
they posed the question of whether for all
A
of size
n

one can take
|B|=o(n)
; this was proved by Alon, Bukh and Sudakov
[
4
]
,
who showed that one can take
|B|=O(n\log\log n/\log n)
.
On the other hand, it remains open (see
[
5
, Problem 2.8]
posed by Wooley)
to give good lower bounds on
|B|
for specific natural sets
A
, such as
A=\{i^{d}:1\leq i\leq n\}
. Furthermore, even for
A
with strong multiplicative structure,
for which it should be particularly hard to find an additive basis, there is
a massive literature on the ‘sum-product phenomenon’, which still falls far short
of the classical conjecture by Erdős
[
6
]
,
and has broad connections to a range of topics including
growth and expansion in groups, exponential sums
and the Kakeya Problem (see the survey by Bourgain
[
7
]
).

In this paper, we consider two questions of Ruzsa
[
8
]

on how the minimum size of an additive basis
B
of a given set
A
depends on the domain of
B
.
To state these questions, for an abelian group
G
and
A\subseteq D\subseteq G
we write

\ell_{D}(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\min\{|B|:B\subseteq D,\ A\subseteq B+B\}.

Ruzsa asked how much larger can
\ell_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)
be than
\ell_{\mathbb{Q}}(A)
for
A\subset\mathbb{Z}
,
and how much larger can
\ell_{\mathbb{N}}(A)
be than
\ell_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)
for
A\subset\mathbb{N}
.
Here we write
\mathbb{Q}
for the rationals,
\mathbb{Z}
for the integers,
and
\mathbb{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{0,1,2,\dotsc\}
for the natural numbers.
Our main results give fairly tight answers to both of these questions.

Additive bases in
\mathbb{Q}
versus
\mathbb{Z}
.

Our results here apply more generally to additive bases of any order.
We write
kB=\{b_{1}+\dots+b_{k}:b_{1},\dots,b_{k}\in B\}
for the
k
-fold sumset of
B
.
For an abelian group
G
and
A\subseteq D\subseteq G
we write

\ell^{(k)}_{D}(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\min\{|B|:B\subseteq D,\ A\subseteq kB\}.

In the following theorems we consider sets
A
of integers that have

k
-bases of size
n
over the rationals. We show that such sets
A

have
k
-bases of size
2n
over the integers, and that this bound
is tight up to a lower order term. Here the upper bound (
Theorem

1
)
is fairly straightforward, so the main interest is in the lower bound (
Theorem

2
).

Theorem 1
.

Every set
A
satisfying
\ell_{\mathbb{Q}}^{(k)}(A)\leq n
also satisfies
\ell_{\mathbb{Z}}^{(k)}(A)\leq 2n
.

Theorem 2
.

For all
k,n\geq 2
there is an
A\subset\mathbb{Z}
satisfying
\ell_{\mathbb{Q}}^{(k)}(A)\leq n

and
\ell_{\mathbb{Z}}^{(k)}(A)\geq 2n-k^{4}n^{1-1/k}
.

Additive bases in
\mathbb{Z}
versus
\mathbb{N}
.

Next we consider sets
A
of natural numbers
that have
k
-bases of size
n
over the integers;
our task here is to find small
k
-bases for
A
over the natural numbers.
For
k=2
we find that it is no longer possible to find such sets of linear size;
in fact, the minimum size is
\Theta(n\log n)
. The following theorems
give upper and lower bounds that match up to a constant factor, which we did not optimise.

Theorem 3
.

If
A\subset\mathbb{N}
with
\ell_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)\leq n
then
\ell_{\mathbb{N}}(A)\leq 3n+2n\log_{2}n
.

Theorem 4
.

For all
n\geq 1
there is
A\subset\mathbb{N}
with
\ell_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)\leq 2n

and
\ell_{\mathbb{N}}(A)\geq\tfrac{1}{25}n\log_{2}n-\tfrac{1}{5}n
.

For larger
k
our results are not as precise.
From
Theorem

3
it follows that
if
\ell^{(k)}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)\leq n
then
\ell^{(k)}_{\mathbb{N}}(A)\leq O(n^{\lceil\frac{k}{2}\rceil}\log n)
; indeed, if
B\subset\mathbb{Z}
is a
k
-basis, then
\lceil\frac{k}{2}\rceil B
is a
2
-basis.
In the following theorem we give a cubic construction,
thus showing that the power of
n
does not need to increase with
k
.

Theorem 5
.

If
A\subset\mathbb{N}
with
\ell_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)\leq n

then
\ell^{(k)}_{\mathbb{N}}(A)\leq 16k\log(k)n^{3}
.

Ideas and techniques.

Somewhat surprisingly, our lower bound in
Theorem

2
comes from linear algebra.
The key idea is to reduce the problem to a related question in a
vector model
.
For example, when
k=2
the problem in the vector model is to find
B_{0},B_{1}\subset\mathbb{Q}^{n}

minimising
|B_{0}|+|B_{1}|
subject to
B_{0}+B_{1}
containing all vectors
e_{i}+e_{j}
with
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
,
where
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
is the standard basis of
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
.
If some
B_{i}
, say
B_{0}
, spans a subspace
V
of codimension
d

then we can find a subspace
W
spanned by
d
standard basis vectors
\{e_{i}:i\in I\}

such that any translate of
V
contains at most one point of
W
.
In particular, we need at least
\tbinom{d+1}{2}
elements in
B_{1}

to cover all
e_{i}+e_{j}
with
i,j
in
I
. Thus we can assume
d=O(\sqrt{n})
,
from which we deduce
|B_{0}|+|B_{1}|\geq 2n-O(\sqrt{n})
.

For bases in
\mathbb{Z}
versus
\mathbb{N}
our constructions use dyadic partitioning.
For example, when
k=2
the problem reduces to finding a basis
B
for sets
of the form
\{x_{r}-x_{t}:1\leq t<r\leq n\}
for some
x_{1}<\dots<x_{n}
.
Our construction is to take all
x_{r}-x_{s}
where for some
j\leq\log_{2}n

we have
s=2^{j}\lfloor 2^{-j}r\rfloor
or
r=2^{j}\lceil 2^{-j}r\rceil
: it is not hard to see that any
x_{r}-x_{t}
can be expressed
using this set in the form
(x_{r}-x_{s})+(x_{s}-x_{t})
.
Thus we include in
B
order
n
elements at each of the
\log_{2}n
dyadic scales.
The intuition for the lower bound is that each scale requires order
n
elements,
although this is not true literally, only in a rather subtle averaged sense
that requires some careful counting.

For higher order bases in
\mathbb{Z}
versus
\mathbb{N}
, the key idea is to establish
a ‘separation of scales’ via a Diophantine approximation argument
that finds an arithmetic progression
P
approximating
some fixed
k
-basis
B
over the integers.
For elements of the iterated sumset
kB
at the scale of
P

we can apply a variant of the dyadic partitioning construction
to find a moderately efficient
k
-basis over the natural numbers,
losing a factor of
n
due to the exponential loss in Diophantine approximation.
We then reduce modulo the common difference of
P
,
which is chosen to reduce the number of elements from
n
to
n-1
,
so that we can conclude by induction, losing another factor of
n
.

Organisation of the paper.

We start in the next section by proving our results
comparing additive bases over the integers to those over the rationals.
In
Sections

3
and
4
we compare additive
k
-bases
over the natural numbers to those over the integers,

.
We conclude with some further remarks and open problems.

2
\mathbb{Z}
versus
\mathbb{Q}

Here we consider sets
A
of integers that have
k
-bases of size
n
over the rationals;
our task is to find small
k
-bases for
A
over the integers. We start with the upper bound,
which is a straightforward rounding argument.

Proof of
Theorem

1
.

Suppose
A\subset kB
for some set
B\subset\mathbb{Q}
with
|B|=n
. Let

C\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{\lfloor b\rfloor:b\in B\}\cup\{\lceil b\rceil:b\in B\}.

We claim that
A\subset kC
; this will imply that
\ell_{\mathbb{Z}}^{(k)}(A)\leq 2n
.

Indeed, let
a\in A
be arbitrary. Write it as
a=b_{1}+\dotsb+b_{k}
for some
b_{1},\dotsb,b_{k}\in B
.
Starting with
\lfloor b_{1}\rfloor+\dotsb+\lfloor b_{k}\rfloor

change the floor signs to the ceilings, one term at a time.
Since
\lfloor b_{1}\rfloor+\dotsb+\lfloor b_{k}\rfloor\leq a
,
each change increases the sum by
0
or
1
, and
\lceil b_{1}\rceil+\dotsb+\lceil b_{k}\rceil\geq a
,
one of the intermediate sums is actually equal to
a
. Hence
a\in kC
.
∎

Vector model.

For the remainder of this section we work towards the proof of the lower bound.
As indicated in the introduction, the key idea will be to translate the problem to a vector model.
This will be achieved by
Lemma

7
below.
We first require the following lemma on linear equations.
In the statement, we call a matrix
A
-by-
B
if it has
A

B

Lemma 6
.

Suppose
L_{1},\dotsc,L_{r}
are
A
-by-
B

M_{1},\dotsc,M_{r}
are
C
-by-
B

and
t_{1},\dotsc,t_{r}
are vectors in

for any

there exists an index

and vector

L_{i}x=M_{i}c+t_{i}
. Then there exists an index

such that the equation
L_{i}x=M_{i}c
has a solution in
\mathbb{Q}^{A}

for every
c\in\mathbb{Q}^{C}
.

In the conclusion of the lemma one cannot replace
\mathbb{Q}^{A}
by
\mathbb{Z}^{A}
. For example, let
A=B=1
,
C=2
,
t_{1}=t_{2}=t_{3}=0

L_{1}x=L_{2}x=L_{3}x=2x
,
M_{1}(c_{1},c_{2})=c_{1}

M_{2}(c_{1},c_{2})=c_{2}
,
M_{3}(c_{1},c_{2})=c_{1}+c_{2}

Here for any

we can solve one of the three equations
2x=M_{i}c
,
as at least one of

Proof.

For each
i\in[r]
we consider the affine subspace

S_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{c\in\mathbb{Q}^{C}:\exists x\in\mathbb{Q}^{A},\ L_{i}x=M_{i}c+t_{i}\}.

Assume for the sake of contradiction that all

For any

\lvert S_{i}\cap[N]^{C}\rvert\leq N^{C-1}

.
We deduce
\lvert\bigcup_{i\leq r}(S_{i}\cap[N]^{C})\rvert\leq rN^{C-1}
, which contradicts
the assumption that
\bigcup_{i}(S_{i}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{C})=\mathbb{Z}^{C}
if
N>r
.
∎

Lemma 7
.

Suppose
\ell_{\mathbb{Z}}^{(k)}(A)\leq m
for every
A\subset\mathbb{Z}
satisfying
\ell_{\mathbb{Q}}^{(k)}(A)\leq n
.
Let
C=\{e_{1},\dotsc,e_{n}\}
be the standard basis and
\mathbf{1}=(1,\dotsc,1)\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
.
Then there exist sets
B_{0},\dotsc,B_{k-1}\subset\mathbb{Q}^{n}
satisfying

\lvert B_{0}\rvert+\dotsb+\lvert B_{k-1}\rvert=m\ \text{ and }\ kC\subset\bigcup\{B_{i_{1}}+\dotsb+B_{i_{k}}+\Delta_{i}\mathbf{1}:i_{1}+\dots+i_{k}\equiv 1\text{ mod }k\},

where
\Delta_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\bigl\lfloor\frac{i_{1}+\dotsb+i_{k}}{k}\bigr\rfloor
is
an integer that depends only on

.

Proof.

There will be three steps in the proof:
(1) restricting attention to a special class of
k
-fold sumsets
A
in
\mathbb{Q}
,
(2) setting up simultaneous equations that describe possible ways to represent
A
as a
k
-fold sumsets in
\mathbb{Z}
,
(3) applying
Lemma

6
to deduce that there is a uniform way to represent such sets
A

and translating this into the conclusion of the lemma.

1. Restricting to sets
A
of a special form.

Since
kC+1=k(C+\frac{1}{k})
, every set of the form
kC+1
is a
k
-fold sumset in
\mathbb{Q}
.
So, by the assumption of the lemma, for every
n
-element

C\subset\mathbb{Z}
there exists a set
B\subset\mathbb{Z}
of size
m
such that

kC+1\subset kB
. We restrict to sets of the form
C=\{kx:x\in C^{\prime}\}

where
C^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{Z}
with
|C^{\prime}|=n
. Letting

\displaystyle B_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{b\in\mathbb{Z}:kb+i\in B\}\quad\text{for each }i=0,1,\dotsc,k-1,

\displaystyle\ \text{ we have}\ \lvert B_{0}\rvert+\dotsb+\lvert B_{k-1}\rvert=m,\ \text{ and }

(1)

\displaystyle kC^{\prime}\subset\bigcup_{i_{1}+\dotsb+i_{k}\equiv 1\pmod{k}}B_{i_{1}}+\dotsb+B_{i_{k}}+\Delta_{i}.

(2)

2. Setting up the equations.

Consider the problem of deciding if,
given an
n
-element
C^{\prime}
, there are sets
B_{0},\dotsc,B_{k-1}
satisfying (
1
)
and (
2
). There are
\binom{m+k-1}{k-1}
ways to choose the sizes of
B_{0},\dotsc,B_{k-1}

to satisfy (
1
). Treating the elements of
B_{0},\dotsb,B_{k-1}
as unknowns, for each of these ways,
there are a number of potential ways that elements of
kC^{\prime}
can be written as elements of the right hand size of (
2
).
For each of these ways, we obtain a system of equations of the form

c_{j_{1}}+\dotsb+c_{j_{k}}=b_{i_{1}}+\dotsb+b_{i_{k}}+\Delta_{i}

as
j_{1},\dotsc,j_{k}
range over
[n]
, and
c_{1},\dotsc,c_{n}
denote the elements of
C^{\prime}
.
Solvability of such a system is equivalent to being able to satisfy (
1
) and (

)
in a particular way.

3. Conclusion of the proof.

By the assumption, for any choice of
c_{1},\dotsc,c_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
, at least one of these linear systems has
a solution over
\mathbb{Z}
. From
Lemma

6
it then follows that one of these linear systems has
a solution
(b_{1},\dotsc,b_{m})\in\mathbb{Q}^{m}
for every
(c_{1},\dotsc,c_{n})\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
.
We denote this system by
Lx+\Delta=Mc
, where
L
is
m
-by-
N
,
M
is
n
-by-
N

and
\Delta\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
, with
N=|kC^{\prime}|

We let
(S_{0},\dots,S_{k-1})
be the partition of
\{1,\dots,m\}

where the unknowns

correspond to the choice of

.

To create the vector model, we will solve this system for

\mathbb{Q}^{n}

To do so, we consider the

We denote its rows by
b_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(b_{j}^{(1)},\dotsc,b_{j}^{(n)})\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}

The required sets

Linear algebra.

We are now nearly ready to prove the main result of this section.
As discussed in the introduction, the vector model established above
will allow us to bring into play methods from linear algebra.
To do so, we employ the following lemma, in which we call a linear space
a
coordinate subspace
if it is spanned by some set of standard basis vectors.

Lemma 8
.

Let
F
be a field and
V\subset F^{n}
a vector space of codimension
d
.
Then there exists a coordinate subspace
W
of dimension
d
such that

|W\cap(V+t)|\leq 1
for every
t\in F^{n}
.

Proof.

The subspace
V
is a zero set of a system of
d
linear equations.
By putting the equations in a row echelon form and permuting the coordinates,
we may assume that the
i
’th linear equation is of the form

x_{i}+c_{i,i+1}x_{i+1}+\dotsb+c_{i,n}x_{n}=0
.
Then the subspace
W
spanned by
e_{1},\dotsc,e_{d}
has the required property.
Indeed, if we had two distinct vectors in
W\cap(V+t)
for some
t\in F^{n}

then their difference would belong to
W\cap V
, but clearly
W\cap V=\{0\}
.
∎

We conclude this section by proving its main result.

Proof of
Theorem

2
.

We will show that there exists a set
A\subset\mathbb{Z}
satisfying

\ell_{\mathbb{Q}}^{(k)}(A)\leq n
and
\ell_{\mathbb{Z}}^{(k)}(A)\geq m\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}2n-k^{4}n^{1-1/k}

We assume for contradiction that no such
A
exists, thus obtaining the vector model

B_{0},B_{1},\dotsc,B_{k-1}\subset\mathbb{Q}^{n}
from
Lemma

7
.
For each
i=0,1,\dotsc,k-1
, let
V_{i}\subset\mathbb{Q}^{m}
be the span of
B_{i}
. By
Lemma

8
there is a coordinate subspace

W_{i}\subset\mathbb{Q}^{n}
of dimension at least
n-\lvert B_{i}\rvert
such that
\lvert V_{i}\cap(W_{i}+t)\rvert\leq 1
for all
t\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
.
By shrinking
W_{i}
, we may assume that
\dim W_{i}=\max\{n-\lvert B_{i}\rvert,0\}
.

Let
C_{i}
be the set of standard basis vectors that spans
W_{i}
.
Note that
\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\lvert C_{i}\rvert=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\dim W_{i}\geq kn-m
.
Let
C^{\prime}
be the set of standard basis vectors that are contained in at least
k-1
of the sets
C_{0},\dotsc,C_{k-1}
.
From
kn-m\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\lvert C_{i}\rvert\leq k\lvert C^{\prime}\rvert+(k-2)n
, and our choice of
m
, it follows
that
\lvert C^{\prime}\rvert\geq k^{3}n^{1-1/k}
. Hence, there exists an index
i_{0}\in\{0,1,\dotsc,k-1\}
such that

\lvert\bigcap_{i\neq i_{0}}C_{i}\rvert\geq k^{2}n^{1-1/k}.

Let
C^{\prime\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\bigcap_{i\neq i_{0}}C_{i}
.
The
k
-fold sumset
kC^{\prime\prime}
is of size

\lvert kC^{\prime\prime}\rvert=\binom{k^{2}n^{1-1/k}+k-1}{k}\geq k^{2k}n^{k-1}/k!.

(3)

Now consider
B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\bigcup_{i=0}^{k-1}B_{i}
and
write
D\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{\Delta_{i}\mathbf{1}:i_{1}+\dotsb+i_{k}\equiv 1\pmod{k}\}
.
Noting that in every solution to
i_{1}+\dotsb+i_{k}\equiv 1\pmod{k}

at least one of
i_{1},\dotsc,i_{k}
is not equal to
i_{0}
, we obtain

\displaystyle kC^{\prime\prime}\subset kC

\displaystyle\subset\bigcup_{i_{1}+\dotsb+i_{k}\equiv 1\pmod{k}}B_{i_{1}}+\dotsb+B_{i_{k}}+\Delta_{i}\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle\subset\bigcup_{i\neq i_{0}}B_{i}+(k-1)B+D.

Now recall that each standard basis vector in
C^{\prime\prime}
belongs to each
W_{i}
with
i\neq i_{0}
.
Since
B_{i}\subset V_{i}
and
\lvert W_{i}\cap(V_{i}+t)\rvert\leq 1
for every
t\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\lvert kC^{\prime\prime}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\neq i_{0}}\lvert kC^{\prime\prime}\cap\bigl(B_{i}+(k-1)B+D\bigr)\rvert\leq\sum_{i\neq i_{0}}\lvert(k-1)B+D\rvert

\displaystyle\leq(k-1)\binom{m+k-2}{k-1}k^{k-1}\leq(k-1)\frac{(2n)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}k^{k-1}.

As this inequality contradicts (
3
), we conclude that there does exist a set

A
satisfying
\ell_{\mathbb{Q}}^{(k)}(A)\leq n
and
\ell_{\mathbb{Z}}^{(k)}(A)\geq 2n-k^{4}n^{1-1/k}
.
∎

3
\mathbb{N}
versus
\mathbb{Z}
:
2
-bases

Here we consider sets
A
of natural numbers
that have
2
-bases of size
n
over the integers;
our task is to find small
2
-bases for
A
over the natural numbers.
We start with the upper bound, implementing the dyadic construction
mentioned in the introduction.

Proof of
Theorem

3
.

By assumption, we have
A\subseteq B+B
for some
B\subset\mathbb{Z}
with
|B|\leq n
.

We fix
x_{1}<x_{2}<\dots<x_{n}
in
\mathbb{N}
so that each element of
B

is
\pm x_{i}
for some
i
and let

B^{+}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(B\cap\mathbb{N})\cup\bigcup_{j=0}^{\lfloor\log_{2}n\rfloor}B_{j},

where each
B_{j}
contains all elements of the form
x_{r}-x_{s}

where
s=2^{j}\lfloor 2^{-j}r\rfloor
or
r=2^{j}\lceil 2^{-j}s\rceil
.

We claim that
A\subseteq B^{+}+B^{+}
. To see this, consider
a\in A

and write
a=b+b^{\prime}
with
b,b^{\prime}\in B
. If both
b,b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
then
we have the same representation
a=b+b^{\prime}
in
B^{+}+B^{+}
.
The other case is that
a=x_{r}-x_{t}
for some
1\leq t<r\leq n
.
We write
r
and
t
in binary and let
2^{j}
be the
most significant column in which they differ.
Then
s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}2^{j}\lfloor 2^{-j}r\rfloor=2^{j}\lceil 2^{-j}t\rceil
.
Now
a=x_{r}-x_{t}=(x_{r}-x_{s})+(x_{s}-x_{t})

represents
a
in
B^{+}+B^{+}
, as required.
Clearly
|B^{+}|\leq n+2n(1+\log_{2}n)
.
∎

Now we prove the lower bound, where roughly speaking we find that one needs
order
n
elements at each dyadic scale, in a suitably averaged sense.

Proof of
Theorem

4
.

We fix
0<x_{1}<x_{2}<\dots<x_{n}
in
\mathbb{N}
rapidly growing, say each
x_{r}=4^{r}
,
and let
A=\mathbb{N}\cap(C+C)
, where
C
consists of all
\pm x_{r}
with
1\leq r\leq n
.
Then
C
witnesses
\ell_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)\leq 2n
. Consider any
B\subset\mathbb{N}
with
A\subseteq B+B
.
We need a lower bound on
|B|
. Our bound will only use the fact that
B+B
contains

A^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{x_{r}-x_{t}:1\leq t<r\leq n\}
, so we can discard from
B
any elements
\geq x_{n}
.

We partition
B
into sets
B_{r}=B\cap[x_{r-1},x_{r})
for
1\leq r\leq n
, where
x_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}0
.
We further partition each
B_{r}
into sets
B_{r,s}=B_{r}\cap[x_{r}-x_{s},x_{r}-x_{s-1})
for
1\leq s\leq r
.
The key observations regarding this partition are that
if
a=x_{r}-x_{t}=b+b^{\prime}
for some
1\leq t<r\leq n
and
b,b^{\prime}\in B
with
b\geq b^{\prime}
then

i.

for some
1\leq s\leq r
we have
b\in B_{r,s}
and
b^{\prime}<x_{s}
, and

ii.

either
b^{\prime}\in B_{s}\cup B_{s-1}
or
a=x_{r}-x_{s-1}

is the only element of
A^{\prime}
of the form
b+b^{\prime\prime}
with
b^{\prime\prime}\in B
and
b^{\prime\prime}<x_{s-2}
,
in which case we call
a
‘unique’.

Indeed, we must have
b\in B_{r}
, as if
b\geq x_{r}
then
b+b^{\prime}>x_{r}-x_{t}
is too large
and if
b<x_{r-1}
then
b+b^{\prime}<2x_{r-1}<x_{r}-x_{t}
is too small.
Then
b\in B_{r,s}
for some
1\leq s\leq r
,
and
b^{\prime}<x_{s}
, as otherwise
b+b^{\prime}\geq x_{r}
is too large.
Furthermore, if
b^{\prime}<x_{s-2}
then
x_{r}-x_{s}<b+b^{\prime}<x_{r}-x_{s-1}+x_{s-2}<x_{r}-x_{s-2}
,
so
b+b^{\prime}=x_{r}-x_{s-1}
.

For
1\leq s\leq n
we define degrees
d_{s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}|B_{s}|+|B_{s-1}|+\sum_{r:r\geq s}|B_{r,s}|
,
noting that
\sum_{s}d_{s}\leq 3|B|
.

For each
0\leq j\leq\log_{2}n
we let
X_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{x_{s}:d_{s}\in[2^{j},2^{j+1})\}
,
so
\sum_{j}2^{j}|X_{j}|\leq\sum_{s}d_{s}\leq 3|B|
.

For each
0\leq i\leq\log_{2}n
we let
A_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{x_{r}-x_{t}:r-t\in[2^{i},2^{i+1})\}
.

For each
a=x_{r}-x_{t}\in A^{\prime}
we fix some representation
a=b_{a}+b^{\prime}_{a}
with
b_{a}\geq b^{\prime}_{a}
in
B+B

and define the scale
i
of
a

by
a\in A_{i}
and representation scale
j
of
b_{a}
by
x_{s}\in X_{j}
, where
b_{a}\in B_{r,s}
.
We note that
t\leq s\leq r
.

If
a
is not unique, we call
a
‘up’ if
j>i
or ‘down’ if
j\leq i
.
We define
n^{!}_{i},n^{+}_{i},n^{-}_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
so that
A_{i}

has
n^{!}_{i}2^{i}
unique,
n^{+}_{i}2^{i}
up and
n^{-}_{i}2^{i}
down elements,
noting that
n^{!}_{i}+n^{+}_{i}+n^{-}_{i}=2^{-i}|A_{i}|\geq n-2^{i+1}
.

Clearly
\sum_{i}n^{!}_{i}\leq\sum_{i}n^{!}_{i}2^{i}\leq|B|
,
as
a\mapsto b_{a}
is an injective map from unique elements to
B
.

To estimate the number of up elements, we fix any
j>i
and any
x_{s}\in X_{j}
,
then note that there are
\leq 2^{i+1}
choices of
r\in[s,t+2^{i+1})\subset[s,s+2^{i+1})

and then
\leq 2^{i+1}
choices of
t\in(r-2^{i+1},r]
. This gives

n^{+}_{i}2^{i}\leq\sum_{j:j>i}|X_{j}|\cdot 2^{i+1}\cdot 2^{i+1}.

Summing over
i
, we deduce

\sum_{i}n^{+}_{i}\leq 4\sum_{j}2^{j}|X_{j}|\leq 12|B|
.

For down elements, for any
j\leq i
let
N_{i,j}
count
elements
a
of scale
i
with representation scale
j
, i.e.
N_{i,j}=|\{a\in A_{i}:b_{a}\in B_{r,s}\text{ and }x_{s}\in X_{j}\}|
.
Thus
n^{-}_{i}2^{i}=\sum_{j:j\leq i}N_{i,j}
. On the other hand,
by definition of degree and
X_{j}
we have

\sum_{i:i\geq j}N_{i,j}\leq\sum_{x_{s}\in X_{s}}\lvert B_{s}\cup B_{s-1}\rvert\cdot\bigg\lvert\bigcup_{r:r\geq s}B_{r,s}\bigg\rvert\leq|X_{j}|\cdot 2^{j+1}\cdot 2^{j+1}.

Summing over
j
, we deduce

\sum_{i}n^{-}_{i}\leq\sum_{j\leq i}2^{-i}N_{i,j}\leq 4\sum_{j}2^{j}|X_{j}|\leq 12|B|
.

To conclude,
25|B|\geq\sum_{i}(n^{!}_{i}+n^{+}_{i}+n^{-}_{i})\geq\sum_{i}(n-2^{i+1})\geq n\log_{2}n-4n
.
∎

4
\mathbb{N}
versus
\mathbb{Z}
: higher order bases

Our task here is to consider a set
A
of natural numbers
with a
k
-basis of size
n
over the integers
and a small
k
-basis for
A
over the natural numbers.
In fact, it will be more convenient to consider a set
A
of non-negative reals
with a
k
-basis of size
n
over the reals
and find a small
k
-basis
B
over the set of non-negative reals.
This suffices, as if we thus obtain
B
from some
A\subset\mathbb{N}

then taking
\lfloor b\rfloor
and
\lceil b\rceil
for each
b\in B

gives a
k
-basis of
A
over
\mathbb{N}
, by the same rounding argument
as in the proof of
Theorem

1
.

Theorem

5
therefore follows from the following theorem.

Theorem 9
.

If
B\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with
|B|=n
then we can find a set
X\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with
|X|\leq 2n^{3}k\log(k)

such that
\left(k(B\cup-B)\right)\cap\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\subset kX
.

As discussed in the introduction, our approach is to find an arithmetic progression (AP) that approximates
B
,
effectively deal with all the big elements of
k(B\cup-B)
, quotient out by the step size of the AP
to reduce the number of elements, and then iterate.

We first find the appropriate AP.

Lemma 10
.

Consider reals
0\leq x_{1}<\dots<x_{n}=1
so that
x_{1}\leq 1-1/C
with
C\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then there exist
L\geq C^{-n-1}
and
y_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
so that, writing
x_{i}=y_{i}L+z_{i}
,
each
|z_{i}|\leq L/C
and
z_{1}=z_{n}
.

Proof.

Consider the map
q\colon\mathbb{R}\to(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{n};\lambda\mapsto([\lambda x_{i}])_{i=1}^{n}
,
where
[\lambda x_{i}]
is the fractional part of
\lambda x_{i}
.
Consider the subset
H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{z\in(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{n}:z_{1}=z_{n}\}
and its preimage
q^{-1}(H)\subset\mathbb{R}
.
Since
x_{n}/x_{1}\geq 1-1/C
, we find that
q^{-1}(H)=\alpha\mathbb{Z}
is an AP with step size
\alpha\leq C
.
By the pigeonhole principle there exist
i,j\leq\alpha C^{n}\leq C^{n+1}
so that

\|q((i-j)\alpha)\|_{\infty}=\|q(i\alpha)-q(j\alpha)\|_{\infty}\leq 1/C
.
Let
L\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{(i-j)\alpha}=\Omega(C^{-n-1})
and write
x_{i}=y_{i}L+z_{i}
with
y_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
|z_{i}|\leq L/C
.
∎

The following lemma establishes the ‘separation of scale’ mentioned in the introduction:
it shows that elements of
k(B\cup(-B))
which are small compared to the scale of the AP
are expressible in
k(B^{\prime}\cup(-B^{\prime}))
where
B^{\prime}
is on the scale of the error terms in the approximation.

Lemma 11
.

Let
B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
where
x_{i}=y_{i}L+z_{i}
, with
y\in\mathbb{Z}
,
|z_{i}|\leq L/C
and
C>2k
.
Let
B^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Then

\{x\in k(B\cup(-B)):|x|<L/2\}\subset k(B^{\prime}\cup(-B^{\prime})).

Proof.

Consider
\sum_{j=1}^{k}\epsilon_{j}x_{i_{j}}\in k(B\cup(-B))
where
\epsilon_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}
.
Note that
\left|\sum_{j=1}^{k}\epsilon_{j}z_{i_{j}}\right|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left|z_{i_{j}}\right|\leq kL/C<L/2
.
On the other hand, since
y_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, if
\left|\sum_{j=1}^{k}\epsilon_{j}y_{i_{j}}L\right|>0
,
then in fact
\left|\sum_{j=1}^{k}\epsilon_{j}y_{i_{j}}L\right|\geq L
, so that
\left|\sum_{j=1}^{k}\epsilon_{j}x_{i_{j}}\right|>L/2
.
Hence, if
\left|\sum_{j=1}^{k}\epsilon_{j}x_{i_{j}}\right|<L/2
, then
\left|\sum_{j=1}^{k}\epsilon_{j}y_{i_{j}}L\right|=0
,
so that
\sum_{j=1}^{k}\epsilon_{j}x_{i_{j}}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\epsilon_{j}z_{i_{j}}\in k(B^{\prime}\cup(-B^{\prime}))
.
∎

In the next lemma we efficiently cover the elements of
k(B\cup(-B))
on the scale of the AP.

Lemma 12
.

Let
B=\{x_{1}<\dots<x_{n}=1\}\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
L>0
.
Then there exists a set
X\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with
|X|\leq nk\log_{2}(3k/L)
such that

\{x\in k(B\cup(-B)):x\geq L/2\}\subset kX.

Proof.

Fix
\sum_{j=1}^{q}x_{i_{j}}-\sum_{j=q+1}^{k}x_{i_{j}}\in k(B\cup(-B))
with
\sum_{j=1}^{q}x_{i_{j}}-\sum_{j=q+1}^{k}x_{i_{j}}\geq L/2
.

For
0\leq m\leq\log(3/L+k)
we consider the differences

D_{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sum_{j=1}^{q}\lfloor 2^{m}x_{i_{j}}\rfloor-\sum_{j=q+1}^{k}\lceil 2^{m}x_{i_{j}}\rceil.

Since
x_{i}\in(0,1)
implies
\lceil x_{i}\rceil=1
and
\lfloor x_{i}\rfloor=0
, it follows that
D_{0}=1+q-k
or
D_{0}=q-k
depending on whether
n\in\{i_{1},\dotsc,i_{q}\}
or not.
In either case
D_{0}\leq 1
. Also, for any
m
we have

\lfloor 2^{m}x_{i_{j}}\rfloor\leq 2^{m}x_{i}\leq\lceil 2^{m}x_{i_{j}}\rceil\leq\lfloor 2^{m}x_{i_{j}}\rfloor+1,

\ \text{ so }\ \ D_{m}\geq 2^{m}\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{q}x_{i_{j}}-\sum_{j=q+1}^{k}x_{i_{j}}\Bigr)-k.

Hence, for
m=\log(3/L+k)
, we have
D_{m}\geq 2^{m}L/2-k>0
.

We claim that there exists an
m_{0}\in\{0,1,\dots,\log_{2}(3k/L)\}
such that
D_{m_{0}}\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
.
To see this, we may assume that
D_{0}<0
, for otherwise we are done, in view of
D_{0}\leq 1
.
Let
m_{0}
be minimal so that
D_{m_{0}}\geq 0
. Note that
m_{0}\geq 1
, so that
D_{m_{0}-1}<0
exists.
For any number
x
, we have
\lfloor 2^{m_{0}}x\rfloor\leq 2\lfloor 2^{m_{0}-1}x\rfloor+1

and
\lceil 2^{m_{0}}x\rceil\geq 2\lceil 2^{m_{0}-1}x\rceil-1
.
Hence,
D_{m_{0}}\leq 2D_{m_{0}-1}+k
. Since
D_{m_{0}-1}\leq-1
, the claim follows.

With these observations in place, we can construct
X
as follows. For
m\leq\log_{2}(3k/L)
let

X_{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\left\{x_{i}-\frac{\left\lfloor 2^{m}x_{i}\right\rfloor-p}{2^{m}}:1\leq i\leq n,0\leq p\leq k-1\right\}\cup\left\{\frac{\left\lceil 2^{m}x_{i}\right\rceil}{2^{m}}-x_{i}:i=1,\dots,n\right\},

and let
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\bigcup_{m=0}^{\log_{2}(3k/L)}X_{m}
. First note that indeed
|X|\leq nk\log_{2}(3k/L)
.
To prove that
kX
indeed covers all big differences,
consider any
\sum_{j=1}^{q}x_{i_{j}}-\sum_{j=q+1}^{k}x_{i_{j}}\in k(B\cup(-B))

with
\sum_{j=1}^{q}x_{i_{j}}-\sum_{j=q+1}^{k}x_{i_{j}}\geq L/2
.
Using the claim, find
m_{0}
so that
2^{m_{0}}D_{m_{0}}\in\{0,\dots,k-1\}
.
Note that
X_{m_{0}}\subset X
contains the elements

y_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}x_{i_{1}}-\frac{\left\lfloor 2^{m_{0}}x_{i_{1}}\right\rfloor-D_{m_{0}}}{2^{m_{0}}}&\text{if $j=1$}\\
x_{i_{j}}-\frac{\left\lfloor 2^{m_{0}}x_{i_{j}}\right\rfloor}{2^{m_{0}}}&\text{if $j=2,\dots q$}\\
\frac{\left\lceil 2^{m_{0}}x_{i_{j}}\right\rceil}{2^{m_{0}}}-x_{i_{j}}&\text{if $j=q+1,\dots k$},\end{cases}

so that
\sum_{j=1}^{k}y_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{q}x_{i_{j}}-\sum_{j=q+1}^{k}x_{i_{j}}
. The lemma follows.
∎

Having prepared the ingredients, we now come to the proof of the main result of this section.

Proof of
Theorem

9
.

Set
C=3k
.
We proceed by induction on
n
. Write
B_{n}=B=\{x_{1}<\dots<x_{n}\}
.
First note that multiplying all elements by the same factor doesn’t affect the problem,
so we may assume
x_{n}=1
. We distinguish two cases: either
x_{1}\geq 1-1/C
or not.

If
x_{1}\geq 1-1/C
, we let
L=1
and note that
x_{i}=y_{i}L+z_{i}
with
y_{i}=1
for all
i
and
|z_{i}|\leq L/C
. Hence, by
Lemma

12
there exists an
X^{n,1}\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with
|X^{n,1}|\leq nk\log_{2}(3k)\leq 2nk\log(k)
such that

\{x\in k(B\cup(-B)):x\geq 1/2\}\subset kX^{n,1}.

By
Lemma

11
, we find that if we let
B^{\prime}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{|z_{i}|:i=1,\dots,n\}
, then

\{x\in k(B\cup(-B)):x<1/2\}\subset k(B^{\prime}_{n}\cup-B^{\prime}_{n}),

so it remains to cover
k(B^{\prime}_{n}\cup-B^{\prime}_{n})
. We write
B_{n}^{\prime}=\{x^{\prime}_{1}<\dots<x^{\prime}_{n}\}

and renormalise so that
x^{\prime}_{n}=1
. Since
z_{n}=0
, we have
x^{\prime}_{1}=0<1-1/C
, so we reduce to the next case.

For notational convenience, we henceforth assume
x_{1}<1-1/C
(rather than
x_{1}^{\prime}<1-1/C
).
Apply
Lemma

10
to find
L\geq C^{-n-1}
so that we can write
x_{i}=y_{i}L+z_{i}

with
y_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, each
|z_{i}|\leq L/C
and
z_{1}=z_{n}
. By
Lemma

12
,
we can find
X^{n,2}
with
|X^{n,2}|\leq nk\log_{2}(3k/L)\leq nk\log_{2}(3kC^{n+1})\leq 2n^{2}k\log(k)
so that

\{x\in k(B_{n}\cup(-B_{n})):x\geq L/2\}\subset kX^{n,2}.

By
Lemma

11
, we find that if we let
B_{n-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{|z_{i}|:i=1,\dots,n\}
then

\{x\in k(B_{n}\cup(-B_{n})):x<L/2\}\subset k(B_{n-1}\cup-B_{n-1}).

Since
z_{1}=z_{n}
we have
|B_{n-1}|\leq n-1
.
Hence, by induction, there is
Y\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
of size
|Y|=2(n-1)^{3}k\log(k)
such that

k(B_{n-1}\cup-B_{n-1})\subset kY
.
Combining these sets, we obtain

k(B\cup-B)\subset k(Y\cup X^{n,1}\cup X^{n,2}).

Finally,
|Y\cup X^{n,1}\cup X^{n,2}|\leq 2(n-1)^{3}k\log(k)+2nk\log(k)+2n^{2}k\log(k)\leq 2n^{3}k\log(k)
.
∎

5
Concluding remarks and open problems

Bases over
\mathbb{Q}
versus
\mathbb{Z}
.

Here we believe that the upper bound of
2n
should be tight.
In the vector model, for the case
k=2
one needs to prove the following.

Conjecture 13
.

Suppose
B_{0},B_{1}\subset\mathbb{Q}^{n}
are such that
B_{0}+B_{1}

contains every
e_{i}+e_{j}
with
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
.
Then
|B_{0}|+|B_{1}|\geq 2n
.

An extension of
Conjecture

13
would be to show that
if
|B_{0}|=n-t
for some
t\geq 0
then
|B_{1}|\geq n+\tbinom{t+1}{2}
.
It is also natural to consider
Conjecture

13
over finite fields,
for which we expect the answer is essentially the same,
changing
2n
to
2n-2
over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
(when
e_{i}+e_{i}=0
).

Bases over
\mathbb{N}
versus
\mathbb{Z}
.

Is there is any polynomial gap for fixed
k
? We believe not.

Conjecture 14
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon>0
there is
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
so that
if
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}\cap kB
for some
B\subset\mathbb{Z}
with
|B|=n>n_{0}

then
A\subseteq kC
for some
C\subset\mathbb{N}
with
|C|\leq n^{1+\varepsilon}
.

Multiplication / higher dimensions.

The change of domain question can be formulated in any abelian group.
For example, we could consider
G=\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,
D=\mathbb{N}^{2}

and compare bases over
A\subset D
over
D
and over
G
.
The infinite-dimensional version of this question is even more natural,
as it is equivalent to taking
G=\mathbb{R}^{\times}_{>0}
(the positive reals with multiplication)
and comparing multiplicative bases of
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{>0}
over
G
and over
\mathbb{N}_{>0}
.
Here, we believe there might be a polynomial gap.

Question 15
.

Are there
\varepsilon>0
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
so that for any
n>n_{0}

there is
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}\cap BB
for some
B\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}
with
|B|=n

such that
A\not\subseteq CC
for any
C\subset\mathbb{N}
with
|C|<n^{1+\varepsilon}
?
