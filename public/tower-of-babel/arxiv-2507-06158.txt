1.
Introduction

Let
A
be an
n\times n
invertible matrix with integer entries and let
\mathcal{D}
be a finite subset of
\mathbbm{Z}^{n}
. The pair
(A,\mathcal{D})
is called a
digit system
(or
number system
) with base
A
and digit set
\mathcal{D}
.
If a given vector
x\in\mathbbm{Z}^{n}
admits an expansion of the form

x=d_{0}+Ad_{1}+\cdots+A^{k}d_{k}
, where
k
is a nonnegative integer and
d_{0},d_{1},\dots,d_{k}\in\mathcal{D}
, we say that
x
has a finite
expansion in the digit system
(A,\mathcal{D})
. If every
x\in\mathbbm{Z}^{n}
can be expressed uniquely in such a form, then
(A,\mathcal{D})
is said to have the
finiteness
and
uniqueness properties
. Such digit systems and their properties have been studied extensively in the literature, for instance in
[
9
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
20
]
, and
[
21
]
. Their properties can also be viewed geometrically which leads to a tiling theory for so-called
self-affine tiles
, see for instance
[
24
]
and
[
25
]
.

The concept of a number system with a matrix base can be extended to rational matrices. Let
A\in\mathbbm{Q}^{n\times n}
be invertible. Then the set of representable vectors is not just
\mathbbm{Z}^{n}
but

\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]=\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}(\mathbbm{Z}^{n}+A\mathbbm{Z}^{n}+\cdots+A^{k}\mathbbm{Z}^{n}).

That is,
\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]
consists of all vectors
x\in\mathbbm{Q}^{n}
of the form

x=x_{0}+Ax_{1}+\cdots+A^{\ell}x_{\ell}
, where
\ell
is a nonnegative integer and
x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell}\in\mathbbm{Z}^{n}
. The collection
\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]
can also be seen as the smallest nontrivial
A
-invariant
\mathbbm{Z}
-module containing
\mathbbm{Z}^{n}
. If
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]
is finite we call
(A,\mathcal{D})
again a
digit system
with base
A
and digit set
\mathcal{D}
. (Note that if
A
has integer entries, we have
\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]=\mathbbm{Z}^{n}
and everything reduces to the case discussed before.) In
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
, Jankauskas and Thuswaldner gave sufficient and necessary conditions to guarantee the existence of a finite set
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]
for which every
x\in\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]
can be expressed in the form

(1)

x=d_{0}+Ad_{1}+\cdots+A^{k}d_{k},

where
k
is a nonnegative integer and
d_{0},d_{1},\dots,d_{k}\in\mathcal{D}
. The pair
(A,\mathcal{D})
is then called a digit system having the
finiteness property
. If, in addition,
\mathcal{D}
is a complete residue system of
\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]/A\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]
, then the representation of
x
in (
2
) is unique if
k\in\mathbbm{N}
is chosen minimally. In this case,
(A,\mathcal{D})
is said to have the
uniqueness property
. The special case where
A
is a
1\times 1
matrix produces a digit system whose base is a rational number
p/q
, where
p
and
q
are coprime integers with
\left|p\right|>\left|q\right|
. Such a digit system and its automaticity properties have been studied extensively by Akiyama et al. in
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
, and
[
6
]
.

Assume that the
n\times n
matrix
A
is
expanding
,
i.e.
, that all eigenvalues of
A
must have modulus greater than 1, and that the digit set
\mathcal{D}
is a finite subset of
\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]
which is a complete residue system of
\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]/A\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]
. In this case one can show that the digit system
(A,\mathcal{D})
is close to the finiteness property in the sense that it admits a finite
attractor

\mathcal{A}
such that each
x\in\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]
can be written in the form

(2)

x=d_{0}+Ad_{1}+\cdots+A^{k}d_{k}+A^{k+1}p,

where
k
is a nonnegative integer,
d_{0},\dots,d_{k}\in\mathcal{D}
, and
p\in\mathcal{A}
. In this paper, we investigate digit systems with these properties. In particular, we concentrate on the case where
A
is a
2\times 2
expanding matrix and choose the digit set to be collinear. We introduce finite-state transducer automata which realize the addition of the vectors
\pm(1,0)^{\top}
and
\pm(0,1)^{\top}
to a given vector
x\in\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]
on the digit expansion (
2
) of
x
. These addition automata, which are of interest in their own right, can be used to determine whether
(A,\mathcal{D})
has the finiteness property and, more generally, to characterize the attractor of
(A,\mathcal{D})
. Indeed, we are able to provide these automata explicitly and use them to characterize the finiteness property and the attractors for this whole class of digit systems. The shape of our digit sets allows us to relate our matrix digit systems to other kinds of digit systems, namely to canonical number systems (CNS) and shift radix systems (SRS). Canonical number systems have a long history that goes back to Knuth
[
18
]
, and they were first studied systematically by Kátai and his coworkers in
[
16
]
for Gaussian integers,
[
14
]
for real quadratic number fields, and
[
15
]
for imaginary quadratic number fields. They were later studied in greater generality in
[
22
]
,
[
23
]
,
[
26
]
, and in the “nonmonic” case that is particularly interesting for us, in
[
30
]
. On the other hand, shift radix systems were invented in
[
1
]
and have been studied more extensively in
[
8
]
and
[
19
]
. They are known to contain nonmonic canonical number systems as special cases; see
[
8
]
.

2.
Arithmetics of Matrix Digit Systems

We first recall the theory of matrix digit systems as developed in
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
. Suppose
A\in\mathbbm{Q}^{n\times n}
is expanding. Then
\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]/A\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]
is a finite abelian group as was shown for instance in
[
12
]
. Let
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]
be a complete system of residues of
\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]/A\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]
.
To enable positional arithmetic computations, we also assume that
\mathcal{D}
contains the zero vector
0=(0,\dots,0)^{\top}
. The
digit function
is defined to be the mapping
\bm{\mathbf{d}}:\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]\to\mathcal{D}
which sends a given vector
x\in\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]
to the unique element
\bm{\mathbf{d}}(x)\in\mathcal{D}
satisfying

x\equiv\bm{\mathbf{d}}(x)\pmod{A\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]},

i.e.,
x-\bm{\mathbf{d}}(x)\in A\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]
. Thus,
\bm{\mathbf{d}}
sends the vector
x
to its “remainder” in
\mathcal{D}
. Define the dynamical system
\Phi:\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]\to\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]
associated to
\bm{\mathbf{d}}
by

\Phi(x)=A^{-1}\big(x-\bm{\mathbf{d}}(x)\big).

Recall that the
attractor

\mathcal{A}
of
\Phi
is the smallest subset of
\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]
such that
\Phi(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{A}
and for each
x\in\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]
, there exists
k\in\mathbbm{N}
such that
\Phi^{k}(x)\in\mathcal{A}
. Such a set
\mathcal{A}
is unique and is denoted by
\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}
.

It has been shown in
[
12
, Proposition 2.5]
that
\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}
is finite whenever
A
is expanding. In this case,
\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}
consists of all
periodic points
of
\Phi
, i.e., vectors
p\in\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]
such that
\Phi^{r}(p)=p
for some
r\in\mathbbm{Z}^{+}
. Moreover, each
x\in\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]
can be written uniquely in the form

(3)

x=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}A^{j}d_{j}+A^{n}p

for some minimal
n\in\mathbbm{N}
,
d_{0},\dots,d_{n-1}\in\mathcal{D}
, and
p\in\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}
. In fact,
n
is the smallest positive integer for which
\Phi^{n}(x)\in\mathcal{A}
,
d_{j}=(\bm{\mathbf{d}}\circ\Phi^{j})(x)
for all
j\in\left\{0,\dots,n-1\right\}
, and
p=\Phi^{n}(x)
.

Because
p
is a periodic point, there is a smallest positive integer
r
such that
\Phi^{r}(p)=p
. Hence there exist unique
b_{0},\dots,b_{r-1}\in\mathcal{D}
such that
p=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{r-1}A^{k}b_{k}+A^{r}p
. Iterating this equation for arbitrarily large
N
gives

p=\sum_{k=0}^{Nr-1}A^{k}b_{k\Mod r}+A^{Nr}p.

Hence, by substituting to
p
in (
3
),
x
may be written in the form

(4)

x=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}A^{j}d_{j}+A^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{Nr-1}A^{k}b_{k\Mod r}+A^{Nr+n}p=:(\sigma)_{A},

where
\sigma
is the infinite word
(b_{r-1}\cdots b_{0})^{\infty}d_{n-1}\cdots d_{0}
. This expansion is called the
A
-adic representation
of
x
. As a consequence of (
4
), each
x\in\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]
corresponds to a unique infinite word of the form
\sigma=(b_{r-1}\cdots b_{0})^{\infty}d_{n-1}\cdots d_{0}
over
\mathcal{D}
for which
(\sigma)_{A}=x
.
If
0\in\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}
, then a vector
x\in\mathbbm{Z}^{n}[A]
corresponding to the infinite word
0^{\infty}d_{n-1}\cdots d_{0}
can be represented by the finite word
d_{n-1}\cdots d_{0}
over
\mathcal{D}
, and we say that
x
has a finite expansion.
In particular, if
\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}=\left\{0\right\}
then the matrix digit system
(A,\mathcal{D})
has the finiteness property.

3.
Statement of the Main Results

For the remainder of this paper, we will focus on the case where
n=2
. Let us consider a
2\times 2
matrix of the form

A=A(\alpha,\beta)=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\beta\\
1&-\alpha\end{bmatrix},

where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbbm{Q}
. Observe that
A
is precisely the companion matrix of the polynomial
p(x)=x^{2}+\alpha x+\beta\in\mathbbm{Q}[x]
. This turns out to be a convenient choice for
A
as any other nonscalar matrix
B
with characteristic polynomial
p(x)
has
A
as its Frobenius normal form.

Throughout this paper, we assume that
p(x)
is irreducible over
\mathbbm{Q}
. Furthermore, we assume that
A
is expanding in order to ensure that the attractor of
\Phi
is finite. We also denote by
c
the (unique) positive integer such that
cp(x)=cx^{2}+ax+b
is a primitive polynomial with integer coefficients (here,
a=c\alpha
and
b=c\beta
). We also use
q(x)
to denote the polynomial
cp(x)
. Because
q(x)
is irreducible in
\mathbbm{Q}[x]
, it follows that
\left|\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]/A\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]\right|=\left|b\right|

[
28
, Proposition 2.2]
.

We begin our discussion with the following result. The set
\mathcal{D}
defined here will serve as our digit set for the rest of the paper.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose
A=A(\alpha,\beta)
is the companion matrix of the irreducible polynomial
p(x)=x^{2}+\alpha x+\beta\in\mathbbm{Q}[x]
. Then the set
\mathcal{D}=\left\{(0,0)^{\top},(1,0)^{\top},\dots,(\left|b\right|-1,0)^{\top}\right\}
is a complete residue system of
\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]/A\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]
.

Proof.

Let
v=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{m}A^{j}v_{j}\in\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]
(where each
v_{j}
is in
\mathbbm{Z}^{2}
). Since each
v_{j}
is an integral linear combination of the canonical basis vectors
\mathbf{e}_{1}
and
\mathbf{e}_{2}
of
\mathbbm{Q}^{2}
and
\mathbf{e}_{2}=A\mathbf{e}_{1}
, it follows that

v=\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}A^{j}(b_{j},0)^{\top}

for some
b_{j}\in\mathbbm{Z}
. Because
q(A)=cA^{2}+aA+bI=0_{2\times 2}
, we may take
b_{0}\in\left\{0,1,\dots,\left|b\right|-1\right\}
by adding or subtracting
q(A)\,\textbf{e}_{1}=0_{2\times 1}
to
v
. Thus

v+A\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]=(b_{0},0)^{\top}+A\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]\in\mathcal{D}+A\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A],

and so
\mathcal{D}
contains a complete residue system modulo
A\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]
. Since
\left|\mathcal{D}\right|=\left|b\right|=\left|\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]/A\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]\right|
, it follows that
\mathcal{D}
is a complete residue system of
\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]/A\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]
.
∎

The proof of Proposition
1
also applies to companion matrices of arbitrary size
n
. It similarly holds for when
A
is a
2\times 2
matrix of the form

A=\begin{bmatrix}r&s\\
\varepsilon&t\end{bmatrix}

where
r\in\mathbbm{Z}
,
\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}
, and
s,t\in\mathbbm{Q}
. This is because the vectors
\mathbf{e}_{1}
and
(r,\varepsilon)^{\top}=A\mathbf{e}_{1}
are the columns of a
2\times 2
integer matrix with determinant
\pm 1
, and thus also constitute an integral basis for the square lattice
\mathbbm{Z}^{2}
.

We now turn our attention to the digit system
(A,\mathcal{D})
, where
A=A(\alpha,\beta)
is the companion matrix of
p(x)=x^{2}+\alpha x+\beta
and
\mathcal{D}
is the digit set defined in Proposition
1
. Our goal is to determine a transducer automaton that performs the addition of
\pm(1,0)^{\top}
and
\pm(0,1)^{\top}
in the digit system
(A,\mathcal{D})
and to characterize the attractor
\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}
of
\Phi
.

To state our main result, we need to define a
finite letter-to-letter transducer
that realizes the addition of a vector
x\in\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]
by the canonical basis vectors
\mathbf{e}_{1}
and
\mathbf{e}_{2}
of
\mathbbm{Q}^{2}
. We recall below the definition of a finite letter-to-letter transducer.

Definition
.

A
(finite letter-to-letter) transducer
or
automaton
is an ordered 6-tuple
\mathcal{T}=(\mathbf{Q},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathbf{E},\mathbf{I},\mathbf{T})
, where:

•

\mathbf{Q}
is a finite nonempty set whose elements are called
states
;

•

\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are finite sets of
digits
, called the
input
and
output alphabets
, respectively;

•

\mathbf{E}\subseteq\mathbf{Q}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}\times\mathbf{Q}
is called the
transition relation
; and

•

\mathbf{I}
and
\mathbf{T}
are nonempty subsets of
\mathbf{Q}
, called the sets of
initial
and
terminal
states, respectively.

A transducer
\mathcal{T}
can be represented pictorially by means of a directed, edge-labeled graph known as a
transition diagram
. Here,
\mathbf{Q}
is the set of vertices and a directed edge is drawn from vertex
q_{1}
to vertex
q_{2}
with label
a\mid b
if and only if
(q_{1},a,b,q_{2})\in\mathbf{E}
. Such an edge is denoted by
q_{1}\xrightarrow{a\mid b}q_{2}
. A sequence of the form

\mathcal{P}:q_{0}\xrightarrow{a_{0}\mid b_{0}}q_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{1}\mid b_{1}}q_{2}\xrightarrow{a_{2}\mid b_{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{a_{n-1}\mid b_{n-1}}q_{n}\xrightarrow{a_{n}\mid b_{n}}\cdots

where
q_{i}\in\mathbf{Q}
,
(a_{i},b_{i})\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}
, and
(q_{i},a_{i},b_{i},q_{i+1})\in\mathbf{E}
for all
i\in\mathbbm{N}
, is called a
walk
in the transducer
\mathcal{T}
. The infinite word
\mathbf{a}=\cdots a_{n}a_{n-1}\cdots a_{1}a_{0}\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbbm{N}}
is called the
input
of
\mathcal{P}
, and the word
\mathbf{b}=\cdots b_{n}b_{n-1}\cdots b_{1}b_{0}\in\mathcal{B}^{\mathbbm{N}}
is called its corresponding
output
. The pair
\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{b}
is called the
label
of
\mathcal{P}
.

For more detailed material on automata theory and transducers, we refer the reader to
[
7
]
,
[
10
]
, and
[
29
]
.

Recall that if
\sigma
is an infinite word over
\mathcal{D}
of the form
(b_{r-1}\cdots b_{0})^{\infty}d_{n-1}\cdots d_{0}
, we denote the element of
\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]
whose digit representation is
\sigma
by
(\sigma)_{A}
. If
b_{0}=\cdots=b_{r-1}=0
and
d_{n-1}\neq 0
, then we identify
\sigma
with the finite word
d_{n-1}\cdots d_{0}
. We introduce the following operations:

\displaystyle(\sigma^{\pm P})_{A}

\displaystyle=(\sigma)_{A}\pm(1,0)^{\top},

\displaystyle(\sigma^{\pm Q})_{A}

\displaystyle=(\sigma)_{A}\pm A(c,0)^{\top}\pm(a-c,0)^{\top},

\displaystyle(\sigma^{\pm R})_{A}

\displaystyle=(\sigma)_{A}\mp A(c,0)^{\top}\mp(a,0)^{\top},

\displaystyle(\sigma^{\pm S})_{A}

\displaystyle=(\sigma)_{A}\pm(c,0)^{\top},

\displaystyle(\sigma^{\pm T})_{A}

\displaystyle=(\sigma)_{A}\pm A(c,0)^{\top}\pm(a+c,0)^{\top},

\displaystyle(\sigma^{\pm U})_{A}

\displaystyle=(\sigma)_{A}\pm(0,1)^{\top}=(\sigma)_{A}\pm A(1,0)^{\top}.

Using these symbols, we are now ready to define the
addition automaton
that will occur in Theorem
2
. We consider the automaton with set of states
\mathbf{Q}=\{\pm P,\pm Q,\allowbreak\pm R,\pm S,\pm T,\pm U\}
, with input and output alphabets
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{D}
, and with sets of initial and terminal states
\mathbf{I}=\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{Q}
. To define the transition relation
\mathbf{E}
between the states, we determine what becomes of the rightmost digit of
\sigma
whenever the above operations are applied to it. They turn out to be distinct, depending on the values of
\alpha
and
\beta
. Consequently, the transition relation
\mathbf{E}
varies across different cases and will be formally defined for each one.

We now state our main result.

Theorem 2
.

Let
A=A(\alpha,\beta)
be the companion matrix of the irreducible polynomial
p(x)=x^{2}+\alpha x+\beta\in\mathbbm{Q}[x]
,
c
be the unique positive integer for which the polynomial
q(x):=cp(x)
is a polynomial with coprime integer coefficients, and
a=c\alpha,b=c\beta
. Consider the digit system
(A,\mathcal{D})
where

\mathcal{D}=\left\{(0,0)^{\top},(1,0)^{\top},\dots,(\left|b\right|-1,0)^{\top}\right\},

and denote by
\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}
the attractor of the dynamical system
\Phi
induced by
(A,\mathcal{D})
. Then the attractor
\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}
and the addition automata are characterized as follows:

1.

If
0<\alpha\leq\beta-1
, then
\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}=\left\{0\right\}
and the associated addition automaton is given in Section
4
.

2.

If
0<-\alpha<\beta-1
and
\alpha<-1
, then
\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}=\left\{(0,0)^{\top},(a+c,c)^{\top},\dots,K(a+c,c)^{\top}\right\}
where
K
is the largest positive integer such that
K(a+b+c)\leq b-1
. The associated addition automaton is given in Section
5
.

3.

If
0<-\alpha<\beta-1
and
\alpha\geq-1
, then
\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}=\left\{0\right\}
and the associated addition automaton is given in Section
5
.

4.

If
0<-\alpha<-\beta-1
, then
\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}
contains
\left\{(0,0)^{\top},-(a,c)^{\top},-(c,0)^{\top}\right\}
. The associated addition automaton is given in Section
6
.

5.

If
0<\alpha<-\beta-1
, then
\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}
contains
\left\{(0,0)^{\top},-(a+c,c)^{\top},\cdots,-K(a+c,c)^{\top}\right\}
where
K
is the largest positive integer such that
K(-a-b-c)\leq-b-1
. The associated addition automaton is given in Section
7
.

We mention that these addition automata contain not only interesting arithmetic information of the underlying digit system
(A,\mathcal{D})
, but they are also strongly related to the
contact graphs
studied in
[
31
]
and, hence, also contain important information on geometric objects related to
(A,\mathcal{D})
. Indeed, they characterize the boundary of “fundamental domains” related to
(A,\mathcal{D})
. We do not pursue this here but intend to come back to this in future work.

A similar result as Theorem
2
has been established in
[
32
]
for expanding integer matrices of the form

M=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
\varepsilon&d\end{bmatrix},

where
a,b,d\in\mathbbm{Z}
and
\varepsilon\in\left\{1,-1\right\}
, with characteristic polynomial
\chi(x)=x^{2}+\alpha x+\beta\in\mathbbm{Z}[x]
. The attractor
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
of the dynamical system generated by
\Phi(z)=M^{-1}(z-\delta)
, where
\delta
is the unique element of
\left\{(0,0)^{\top},(1,0)^{\top},\dots,(\left|\beta\right|-1,0)^{\top}\right\}
with
\delta\equiv{z}\pmod{M}
, was determined by constructing a finite letter-to-letter right transducer that realizes the addition by the vectors
(1,0)^{\top}
and
(0,1)^{\top}
. The identity

(5)

\chi(M)=M^{2}+\alpha M+\beta I=0_{2\times 2},

which directs any possible carries in the addition by
(1,0)^{\top}
, features prominently in the construction of such a transducer. It turns out that the form of the attractor
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi}
depends solely on the values of
\alpha=-\tr(M)
and
\beta=\det(M)
.

D. Knuth introduces a similar automaton in
[
17
]
for the numeration system with base
-3/2
in his study of the space
\mathbbm{K}=\mathbbm{R}\times\mathbbm{Q}_{2}
, where
\mathbbm{Q}_{2}
denotes the field of
2
-adic rational numbers.

Our approach in finding the attractor
\mathcal{A}_{\Phi}
will be similar to that in
[
32
]
; this time, we make use of the identity

q(A)=cA^{2}+aA+bI=0_{2\times 2}

in order to direct any carries when we add by
(1,0)^{\top}
. However, in our more general situation the structure of the occurring transducers is much more involved and a complete construction of these automata seems to be out of reach.

4.
The case
0<\alpha\leq\beta-1

First, assume that
0<\alpha\leq\beta-1
. Then
b>0
and so
\left|\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]/A\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]\right|=b
. Furthermore,
0<a\leq b-c
and this implies that
b>c
and
b>a
.
Following the notation of
[
32
]
,
(\nu,0)^{\top}\in\mathcal{D}
is identified with
\nu
, and a horizontal bar over a term means that particular term is taken to be one digit.
Direct computation, together with the identity
q(A)=0
, yields the following relations for
\nu\in\left\{0,1,\dots,b-1\right\}
.

\displaystyle(\sigma\nu)^{P}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sigma\,\overline{\nu+1},&0\leq\nu<b-1\\
\sigma^{R}\,0,&\nu=b-1\end{cases}

\displaystyle(\sigma\nu)^{-P}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sigma\,\overline{\nu-1},&1\leq\nu<b\\
\sigma^{-R}\,\overline{b-1},&\nu=0\end{cases}

\displaystyle(\sigma\nu)^{\pm Q}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sigma^{\pm T}\,\overline{\nu\pm a\mp c\pm b},&0\leq\nu\pm a\mp c\pm b<b\\
\sigma^{\pm S}\,\overline{\nu\pm a\mp c},&0\leq\nu\pm a\mp c<b\\
\sigma^{\mp Q}\,\overline{\nu\pm a\mp c\mp b},&0\leq\nu\pm a\mp c\mp b<b\end{cases}

\displaystyle(\sigma\nu)^{\pm R}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sigma^{\pm Q}\,\overline{\nu\mp a\pm b},&0\leq\nu\mp a\pm b<b\\
\sigma^{\mp S}\,\overline{\nu\mp a},&0\leq\nu\mp a<b\end{cases}

\displaystyle(\sigma\nu)^{\pm S}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sigma\,\overline{\nu\pm c},&0\leq\nu\pm c<b\\
\sigma^{\pm R}\,\overline{\nu\pm c\mp b},&0\leq\nu\pm c\mp b<b\end{cases}

\displaystyle(\sigma\nu)^{\pm T}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sigma^{\pm S}\,\overline{\nu\pm a\pm c},&0\leq\nu\pm a\pm c<b\\
\sigma^{\mp Q}\,\overline{\nu\pm a\pm c\mp b},&0\leq\nu\pm a\pm c\mp b<b\end{cases}

In addition,
(\sigma\nu)^{\pm U}=\sigma^{\pm P}\nu
for each
\nu\in\left\{0,1,\dots,b-1\right\}
and this holds for any values of
\alpha
and
\beta
.
We show the derivation of the relation involving
\sigma^{S}
. If
0\leq\nu<b-c
, then

\big((\sigma\nu)^{S}\big)_{A}=A(\sigma)_{A}+(\nu,0)^{\top}+(c,0)^{\top}=A(\sigma)_{A}+(\nu+c,0)^{\top}.

By assumption,
c\leq\nu+c<b
, and so
(\sigma\nu)^{S}=\sigma\,\overline{\nu+c}
. If
b-c\leq\nu<b
, then
\nu+c\geq b
. Thus a carry occurs in the addition by
(c,0)^{\top}
which is captured by
(b,0)^{\top}=-A^{2}(c,0)^{\top}-A(a,0)^{\top}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\big((\sigma\nu)^{S}\big)_{A}

\displaystyle=A(\sigma)_{A}+(\nu+c,0)^{\top}

\displaystyle=A(\sigma)_{A}+(\nu+c-b,0)^{\top}-A^{2}(c,0)^{\top}-A(a,0)^{\top}

\displaystyle=A\big((\sigma)_{A}-A(c,0)^{\top}-(a,0)^{\top}\big)+(\nu+c-b,0)^{\top}

\displaystyle=A(\sigma^{R})_{A}+(\nu+c-b,0)^{\top}.

Because
0\leq\nu+c-b<c<b
, we have
\nu+c-b\in\mathcal{D}
. It follows that
(\sigma\nu)^{S}=\sigma^{R}\,\overline{\nu+c-b}
, thereby proving the relation. The other identities are proven similarly. The elements of the transition relation
\mathbf{E}
corresponding to the relation involving
(\sigma\nu)^{S}
are
S\xrightarrow{\nu\mid\nu+c}\bullet
, where the black dot denotes a terminal state, when
0\leq\nu+c<b
, and
S\xrightarrow{\nu\mid\nu+c-b}R
when
0\leq\nu+c-b<b
.

Observe that at most two of the three possibilities for
Q
and
-Q
hold, depending on the sign of
a-c
, which in turn depends on whether
\alpha>1
,
\alpha=1
, or
\alpha<1
. An example of the case when
\alpha>1
is given by the automaton in Figure

. This models the addition by
(1,0)^{\top}
in the digit system
(A,\mathcal{D})
when

. In all cases, a vertex labeled
\overline{u}
, where
u\in\left\{P,Q,R,S,T,U\right\}
, is the state corresponding to the operation
-u
.
Note that we have omitted the vertices labeled

and
\overline{T}
in Figure

.
These states play no role in the automaton, as no walk includes
T
or
\overline{T}
unless it starts at one of these states.

To get the string
\sigma^{u}
for

to the transducer with starting state labeled

from right to left, moving along the edges corresponding to their digits. Every edge label is of the form

Suppose

has a digit expansion represented by the finite string
\sigma
and is fed into the transducer starting at state

is finite.

If we end up in one of the other states, then we consider the remaining digits of

\sigma^{P}
has only finitely many nonzero entries.

is still finite.

(1,0)^{\top}

for any

and

.

\alpha<-1
.

, set

labeled

.
Applying the automaton to

.

\gamma^{\infty}u
or

over

.

, where

is the largest positive integer such that

, for some finite word

over
\mathcal{D}
, if the trail produced by
\sigma

•

, for

over
\mathcal{D}

\sigma

w
over
\mathcal{D}
, if the trail produced by
\sigma

T
.

8.
Relationship between matrix and polynomial digit systems

Our construction of the automata in the previous sections showed that the characteristic polynomial of
A=A(\alpha,\beta)
mattered more than the actual matrix
A
itself. Indeed, we used solely the matrix equation

q(A)=cA^{2}+aA+bI=0_{2\times 2}

when dealing with carries in the addition by
(1,0)^{\top}
. Hence, such computations hold for any matrix
B
whose Frobenius normal form is
A
(as
B
will then have the same characteristic polynomial as
A
) and for which the digit set
\mathcal{D}=\{(0,0)^{\top},(1,0)^{\top},\dots,(\left|b\right|-1,0)^{\top}\}
is also a complete set of coset representatives of
\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[B]/B\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[B]
. This allows us to work only with the polynomial
q(x)
instead of the matrix
A
.

We now turn to the question of relating the matrix digit system
(A,\mathcal{D})
with the so-called digit system induced by the ring
\mathbbm{Z}[x]/q\mathbbm{Z}[x]
, as introduced by Scheicher et al. in
[
30
]
.

We first recall the necessary concepts in
[
30
]
. While the results in
[
30
]
hold for any commutative ring
R
with unity, we focus our attention on the case where
R=\mathbbm{Z}
. Let
d\in\mathbbm{Z}^{+}
and

P=p_{d}x^{d}+\cdots+p_{1}x+p_{0}\in\mathbbm{Z}[x]

such that
p_{d}
and
p_{0}
are nonzero. Let
X=x+P\mathbbm{Z}[x]
be the image of
x
under the canonical epimorphism from
\mathbbm{Z}[x]
to
\mathbbm{Z}[x]/P\mathbbm{Z}[x]=:\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{N}
be a complete residue system of
\mathcal{R}/(X)
. Define the map
D_{\mathcal{N}}:\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{N}
where any
r\in\mathcal{R}
is mapped to the unique element
D_{\mathcal{N}}(r)
of
\mathcal{N}
satisfying

r\equiv D_{\mathcal{N}}(r)\pmod{X},

i.e.,
r-D_{\mathcal{N}}(r)\in(X)
. Let
T:\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{R}
be the associated dynamical system defined by

T(r)=\dfrac{r-\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{N}}(r)}{X}.

The triple
(\mathcal{R},X,\mathcal{N})
is called the
digit system over
R
defined by
P
and
\mathcal{N}
.

In all that follows, we assume that the polynomial
P
is primitive and identify any
n\in\mathbbm{Z}
with its image under the canonical epimorphism from
\mathbbm{Z}
to
\mathcal{R}
. Observe that any
r\in\mathcal{R}
can be written in the form

(6)

r=\sum_{i=0}^{k}r_{i}X^{i}

where
r_{0},\ldots,r_{k}\in\mathbbm{Z}
and
X=x+P\mathbbm{Z}[x]
. Furthermore,
P(X)=0
in
\mathbbm{Z}[x]/P\mathbbm{Z}[x]
. Hence, we may take
r_{0}\in\left\{0,1,\dots,\left|P(0)\right|-1\right\}
by adding or subtracting
P(X)=0
to
r
in (
6
). It follows that
\mathcal{N}=\left\{0,1,\dots,\left|P(0)\right|-1\right\}
contains a complete residue system of
\mathcal{R}/(X)
(and we will also see later that
\mathcal{N}
is in fact a complete residue system).

We begin by stating
[
30
, Lemma 4.1]
in our current setting. It is a classical result that allows us to represent
r
in a “canonical” form similar to (
6
) that is unique under certain restrictions.

Lemma 3
.

Let
P=p_{d}x^{d}+\cdots+p_{1}x+p_{0}\in\mathbbm{Z}[x]
be a primitive polynomial of positive degree
d
. For each
r\in\mathbbm{Z}[x]/P\mathbbm{Z}[x]
, there exist unique
r_{0},\dots,r_{k}\in\mathbbm{Z}
, with
k\in\mathbbm{N}
minimal and
r_{d},\dots,r_{k}\in\left\{0,1,\dots,\left|p_{d}\right|-1\right\}
whenever
k\geq d
, such that

r=\sum_{i=0}^{k}r_{i}X^{i}

where
X=x+P\mathbbm{Z}[x]
.

In a similar vein, Rossi et al. established an analogue of Lemma
3

for matrix digit systems in the proof of
[
28
, Proposition 2.2]
. We state the result formally below, assuming once more that
P\in\mathbbm{Z}[x]
is primitive of positive degree
d
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
C\in\mathbbm{Q}^{d\times d}
be the companion matrix of some
q\in\mathbbm{Q}[x]
such that
P=cq
for some
c\in\mathbbm{Z}
. Then every
v\in\mathbbm{Z}^{d}[C]
can be written uniquely in the form

v=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}b_{i}C^{i}\mathbf{e}_{1}

where
\mathbf{e}_{1}=(1,0,\ldots,0)^{\top}\in\mathbbm{Q}^{d}
,
\ell\in\mathbbm{N}
is minimal,
b_{0},\dots,b_{\ell}\in\mathbbm{Z}
and
b_{d},\dots,b_{\ell}\in\left\{0,1,\dots,\left|p_{d}\right|-1\right\}
if
d\leq\ell
.

Lemma
4
also lets us represent any vector
v\in\mathbbm{Z}^{d}[C]
in a unique “canonical” form with conditions similar to those of Lemma
3
. Using Lemmas
3
and

4
, Rossi et al. defined the mapping
h:\mathbbm{Z}^{d}[C]\to\mathbbm{Z}[x]/P\mathbbm{Z}[x]
where

h\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}b_{i}C^{i}\mathbf{e}_{1}\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}b_{i}X^{i}.

Lemma
4
guarantees that
h
is well defined, and Lemma
3
shows that
h
is bijective. In fact, it can be shown that
h
is a
\mathbbm{Z}
-module isomorphism, and so
\mathbbm{Z}^{d}[C]\cong\mathbbm{Z}[x]/P\mathbbm{Z}[x]
as
\mathbbm{Z}
-modules. Furthermore, the restriction of
h
to
C\mathbbm{Z}^{d}[C]
is an isomorphism from
C\mathbbm{Z}^{d}[C]
onto
(X)
, and hence
\mathbbm{Z}^{d}[C]/C\mathbbm{Z}^{d}[C]\cong\mathcal{R}/(X)
. Because
\left|\mathbbm{Z}^{d}[C]/C\mathbbm{Z}^{d}[C]\right|=\left|\mathcal{R}/(X)\right|=\left|P(0)\right|
, it follows that the previously defined subset
\mathcal{N}
of
\mathcal{R}
is indeed a complete residue system of
\mathcal{R}/(X)
.

Let us consider once more the companion matrix
A=A(\alpha,\beta)
of
p=x^{2}+\alpha x+\beta
and
c\in\mathbbm{Z}^{+}
such that
q=cp=cx^{2}+ax+b
is primitive with integer coefficients. By the preceding discussion, the
\mathbbm{Z}
-modules
\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]
and
\mathbbm{Z}[x]/q\mathbbm{Z}[x]
are isomorphic via the mapping
h:\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]\to\mathbbm{Z}[x]/q\mathbbm{Z}[x]
. We use
h
to establish a relationship between the digit systems
(A,\mathcal{D})
and
(\mathcal{R},X,\mathcal{N})
where
\mathcal{R}=\mathbbm{Z}[x]/q\mathbbm{Z}[x]
,

\mathcal{D}=\left\{(0,0)^{\top},(1,0)^{\top},\dots,(\left|b\right|-1,0)^{\top}\right\},\text{ and }\mathcal{N}=\left\{0,1,\dots,\left|b\right|-1\right\}.

We consider the digit functions
\bm{\mathbf{d}}:\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]\to\mathcal{D}
and
D_{\mathcal{N}}:\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{N}
, together with their associated dynamical systems
\Phi:\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]\to\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]
and
T:\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{R}
as defined previously. Let
v=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\ell}b_{i}A^{i}\mathbf{e}_{1}\in\mathbbm{Z}^{2}[A]
where
\ell\in\mathbbm{N}
is minimal,
b_{0},\dots,b_{\ell}\in\mathbbm{Z}
and
b_{2},\dots,b_{\ell}\in\left\{0,1,\dots,c-1\right\}
whenever
\ell\geq 2
. Then
h(v)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\ell}b_{i}X^{i}
. Furthermore,
\bm{\mathbf{d}}(v)=(b_{0}^{\prime},0)^{\top}
and
D_{\mathcal{N}}(h(v))=b_{0}^{\prime}
where
b_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}
such that
b_{0}^{\prime}\equiv b_{0}\pmod{b}
. Suppose
k\in\mathbbm{Z}
such that
b_{0}-b_{0}^{\prime}=kb
. Then

\displaystyle\Phi(v)

\displaystyle=A^{-1}[v-\bm{\mathbf{d}}(v)]=A^{-1}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i}A^{i}\mathbf{e}_{1}+(b_{0}-b_{0}^{\prime})\mathbf{e}_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=A^{-1}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i}A^{i}\mathbf{e}_{1}+kb\mathbf{e}_{1}\right]=A^{-1}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i}A^{i}\mathbf{e}_{1}-kcA^{2}\mathbf{e}_{1}-kaA\mathbf{e}_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=A^{-1}\left[\sum_{i=3}^{\ell}b_{i}A^{i}\mathbf{e}_{1}+(b_{2}-kc)A^{2}\mathbf{e}_{1}+(b_{1}-ka)A\mathbf{e}_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=3}^{\ell}b_{i}A^{i-1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+(b_{2}-kc)A\mathbf{e}_{1}+(b_{1}-ka)\mathbf{e}_{1},

where
b_{3},\dots,b_{\ell}\in\left\{0,1,\dots,c-1\right\}
. Thus,

(h\circ\Phi)(v)=\sum_{i=3}^{\ell}b_{i}X^{i-1}+(b_{2}-kc)X+(b_{1}-ka).

On the other hand,

\displaystyle T(h(v))

\displaystyle=\dfrac{h(v)-D_{\mathcal{N}}(h(v))}{X}=\dfrac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i}X^{i}+(b_{0}-b_{0}^{\prime})}{X}=\dfrac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i}X^{i}+kb}{X}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i}X^{i}-kcX^{2}-kaX}{X}=\dfrac{\sum\limits_{i=3}^{\ell}b_{i}X^{i}+(b_{2}-kc)X^{2}+(b_{1}-ka)X}{X}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=3}^{\ell}b_{i}X^{i-1}+(b_{2}-kc)X+(b_{1}-ka)=(h\circ\Phi)(v).

Thus, we have proven the following result.

Theorem 5
.

Let
A\in\mathbbm{Q}^{2\times 2}
be the companion matrix of the irreducible polynomial
p(x)=x^{2}+\alpha x+\beta
, and
c\in\mathbbm{Z}^{+}
such that
q(x)=cp(x)=cx^{2}+ax+b
is a primitive polynomial with integer coefficients. Suppose
h
,
\mathcal{D}
,
\mathcal{N}
,
\Phi
, and
T
are as defined previously. Then

T\circ h=h\circ\Phi.

Hence, in order to obtain the mapping
\Phi
, and subsequently the digits of a given vector
v
, one simply conjugates the dynamical system
T
by the isomorphism
h
. This implies that the digits in the matrix system
(A,\mathcal{D})
may be obtained from the system
(\mathcal{R},X,\mathcal{N})
via conjugation. Observe that Theorem
5
also holds true for a companion matrix of any size
d
with the same proof.

9.
Shift radix systems and special cases

The notion of shift radix systems was introduced formally in 2005 by Akiyama et al. in
[
1
]
, in which they established several of its properties and its connections to existing number systems. In particular, shift-radix systems are intimately related to the so-called beta-expansions introduced in 1957 by Renyi
[
27
]
. They have also been used to characterize when a given polynomial with integer coefficients is the base of what is called a canonical number system, of which
[
30
]
is a generalization (see
[
3
]
for more details).

Berthé et al. in
[
8
]
also associated certain collections of fractal tiles with shift radix systems and explored some of their basic properties. For a full survey on shift radix systems, their properties, relationships with other numeration systems, and their geometric aspects, we refer the reader to
[
19
]
.

We now use certain regions associated with shift-radix systems to address cases that were not covered in the proof of Theorem
2
. We begin by recalling the definition of a shift radix system.

Definition
.

Let
d
be a positive integer and
\bm{\mathbf{r}}=(r_{0},r_{1},\dots,r_{d-1})\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
. The
shift-radix system
associated to
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
is the mapping
\tau_{\bm{\mathbf{r}}}:\mathbbm{Z}^{d}\to\mathbbm{Z}^{d}
defined by

\tau_{\bm{\mathbf{r}}}(\bm{\mathbf{z}})=(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{d-1},-\left\lfloor\bm{\mathbf{rz}}\right\rfloor),

where
\bm{\mathbf{z}}=(z_{0},z_{1},\dots,z_{d-1})\in\mathbbm{Z}^{d}
,
\left\lfloor\cdot\right\rfloor
is the floor function, and
\bm{\mathbf{r}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}
is the usual inner product of
\bm{\mathbf{r}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{z}}
in
\mathbbm{R}^{d}
. The system
\tau_{\bm{\mathbf{r}}}
is said to have the
finiteness property
if for each
\bm{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbbm{Z}^{d}
, there exists
k\in\mathbbm{N}
such that
\tau_{\bm{\mathbf{r}}}^{k}(\bm{\mathbf{z}})=\bm{\mathbf{0}}
. The following sets are associated to the SRS
\tau_{\bm{\mathbf{r}}}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{d}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\bm{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}\mid\text{ for all }\bm{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbbm{Z}^{d},\text{ there exist }k,\ell\in\mathbbm{N}\text{ such that }\tau_{\bm{\mathbf{r}}}^{k}(\bm{\mathbf{z}})=\tau_{\bm{\mathbf{r}}}^{k+\ell}(\bm{\mathbf{z}})\right\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{d}^{(0)}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\bm{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}\mid\tau_{\bm{\mathbf{r}}}\text{ has the finiteness property}\right\}.

Observe that
\mathcal{D}_{d}
consists of all vectors
\bm{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
for which the sequence
\big(\tau_{k}(\bm{\mathbf{z}})\big)_{k=1}^{\infty}
is eventually periodic for all
\bm{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbbm{Z}^{d}
. Clearly,
\mathcal{D}_{d}^{(0)}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{d}
. In fact,
[
1
, Section 4]
shows that
\mathcal{D}_{d}^{(0)}
can be obtained by “cutting out” polyhedra from
\mathcal{D}_{d}
. Figure
7
shows an approximation of
\mathcal{D}_{2}^{(0)}
.

Let
