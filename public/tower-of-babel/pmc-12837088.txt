Engineering chitosan fibers with MSC-exosome cargo: a clinically translatable multifunctional dressing for regenerative therapy in infected wound management
Authors: Jing Gao, Rui Qiao, Chenyong Fu, Di Sun, Dan Jin, Qing Zhang, Zhe Li, Guanjing Lang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic wound healing is a complex clinical challenge, particularly due to microbial colonization and the deactivation of repair cells. This study presents an innovative strategy involving the combination of micron-scale chitosan fibers with exosomes, aiming to develop a new type of dressing with multiple functionalities, including dynamic exudate management, antimicrobial properties, angiogenesis promotion, and tissue repair. The goal is to offer a cost-effective and clinically translatable treatment solution for infected wounds.MethodsChitosan (CS) fibers were prepared using a wet-spinning technique and subsequently modified through N-succinylation (NCS), followed by needle-punching to construct CS/NCS blended nonwoven fabrics. The physicochemical properties, water absorption/retention, and mechanical behavior of the materials were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, and X-ray diffraction (XRD). Antibacterial and hemostatic performance were also evaluated. Mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (MSC-EXO) were loaded onto the modified chitosan fibers via electrostatic assembly, forming the CS/NCS-EXO composite dressing, which was further tested in a rat model for infected wound repair.ResultsCompared to pure CS fibers and NCS fibers, the CS/NCS material demonstrated superior mechanical properties and moisture retention capacity in a wet state. Antibacterial assays showed that the CS/NCS material exhibited significantly enhanced antimicrobial activity against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli. Hemostatic experiments revealed that the CS/NCS group significantly shortened bleeding time and reduced blood loss. In the infected skin defect repair experiment, the CS/NCS-EXO group significantly accelerated wound healing, demonstrating the most prominent tissue repair effect, accompanied by abundant angiogenesis as confirmed by immunohistochemical staining.ConclusionThis study successfully developed a chitosan fiber-based exosome composite dressing system, which effectively coordinates infection control and tissue regeneration through a triple mechanism of “structural water-locking, mechanical adaptation, and bioactive synergy.” This material provides a scalable solution for chronic wound management and shows promising clinical application prospects.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04863-9.

Introduction

The clinical management of chronic wound healing faces multiple pathophysiological challenges, with microbial colonization causing persistent infection and dysfunction of repair cells as key limiting factors [1, 2]. Although antibiotic therapy effectively controls acute infections, the overuse of antibiotics has led to the emergence of resistant strains, prompting the clinical need for alternative antimicrobial strategies [3, 4]. Modern wound healing theories emphasize that an ideal dressing should surpass the limitations of traditional antimicrobial properties and integrate dynamic moisture regulation, tissue regeneration promotion, and non-invasive care, thereby precisely adapting to the evolving microenvironment during healing [5–7].

In this context, the natural cationic polysaccharide chitosan has attracted attention due to its inherent antimicrobial, biocompatible, and hemostatic properties [8, 9]. However, traditional nano-sized chitosan materials are limited by complex preparation processes, high production costs, and difficulties in balancing liquid absorption and swelling with mechanical stability [10]. Wet-spinning microfiber technology was developed that significantly reduces manufacturing costs while preserving the basic functionality of the material [11]. However, these materials still have two major limitations: first, insufficient hydrophilic modification, which restricts exudate management capabilities; second, the lack of bioactive components to actively regulate repair cell behavior, which hinders their ability to meet the regenerative needs of complex wounds.

In recent years, mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes have shown unique advantages as acellular therapeutic carriers, as they contain active components such as VEGF and miRNA that can effectively promote angiogenesis and remodel the immune microenvironment [12–14]. However, the efficient local delivery of exosomes to the wound site remains a significant challenge: physical adsorption methods lead to rapid degradation of the active components, while chemical cross-linking methods may compromise the integrity of the exosome membrane [15–17]. This technical bottleneck severely limits the practical application of exosome therapy in wound management.

To address these challenges, this study proposes an innovative strategy of a “micron-scale chitosan fiber-exosome composite system” to synergistically manage exudates, deliver MSC-exosomes, and remodel the tissue regeneration microenvironment. The fibers are optimized through wet spinning to improve the fiber network topology, enhancing exudate adsorption and mechanical stability. Electrostatic assembly is used to load exosomes onto the modified chitosan fibers, creating a stable delivery system. Finally, the exosome’s angiogenic function is integrated into the system to promote the regenerative repair process. This composite system accelerates angiogenesis in infected wounds and breaks the “infection-delayed healing” cycle through its antimicrobial and repair-promoting dual mechanisms, offering a clinically translatable solution for treating infected wounds.

Materials and methods

Preparation of NCS fibers and needle-punched nonwoven fabric

One gram of chitosan fibers (1.67 dtex × 38 mm, deacetylation degree > 90%, molecular weight 100 kDa, provided by Wenzhou Jiayuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) was immersed in 25 mL of a 0.05 g/mL solution of succinic anhydride in anhydrous ethanol (provided by China National Pharmaceutical Group Corporation). The reaction was conducted at 60 °C for 24 h. After the reaction, the product was repeatedly washed with ethanol and vacuum dried for 8 h to yield NCS fibers.

The CS fibers and NCS fibers were mixed in a 1:1 ratio and subjected to a needle-punching process to form a nonwoven fabric with a weight of 120 g/m², referred to as CS/NCS. Pure chitosan nonwoven fabric (CS) was prepared by the same method. Additionally, pure N-succinylated chitosan nonwoven fabric (NCS) was prepared using the same needle-punching process.

Scanning electron microscopy

The morphology of the needle-punched nonwoven fabrics was observed using an ApreoS Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Prior to scanning, the samples were coated with a thin layer of gold.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

The chemical structure and composition of CS, NCS, and CS/NCS were characterized using a Vertex 70 Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) Spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) in the 4000–500 cm⁻¹ range.

X-ray diffraction analysis

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of the samples was conducted using a D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer (Cu, 40 kV, 40 mA) with a scan speed of 10°/min and a scanning range of 5°–70°.

Zeta potential analysis

The CS, NCS, and CS/NCS materials were milled to an appropriate particle size and then dispersed in a 0.5 wt% solution. The pH of the solution was adjusted using acetic acid or sodium hydroxide, followed by homogenization. The Zeta potential of the materials was measured using a Zeta potential analyzer (NANO ZSE, Malvern Instruments, UK).

Mechanical properties

CS, NCS, and CS/NCS samples were cut into 20 × 60 mm strips and tested for mechanical properties using a Universal Material Testing Machine (Instron 5982, USA). Half of the samples were tested in a dry state, while the other half were tested in a wet state.

Water contact angle

The hydrophilicity and hydrophobicity of the nonwoven fabrics were determined by water contact angle measurements using the sessile drop method. The contact angles were calculated using ImageJ software by measuring the angle formed between the water droplet and the fabric surface.

Water absorption test

CS, NCS, and CS/NCS samples were cut into 20 × 60 mm strips and weighed dry (W₀). The samples were immersed in deionized water in a 50 mL beaker. At various time points (5 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h), the samples were removed, allowed to drain until no visible liquid dripped off, and weighed again (W₁). Antibacterial test

The antibacterial activity of the nonwoven fabric dressings against Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Gram-negative Escherichia coli (E. coli) was evaluated using a shake flask test. Fifty microliters of bacterial inoculum were added to 5 mL of PBS solution in a centrifuge tube, which was incubated in a 37 °C water bath and shaken for 16–24 h to achieve a bacterial concentration of 10⁵–10⁶ CFU/mL. CS, NCS, and CS/NCS samples (0.03 g) were sterilized by autoclaving (121 °C, 0.12 MPa, 30 min). The sterilized samples were placed into the culture tubes, with non-material tubes serving as the control. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the bacterial solution was diluted 100 times, and 200 µL was plated on agar plates. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and the number of bacterial colonies was recorded.

Tail bleeding test in rats

All experimental procedures were conducted in strict compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee. Nine Adult Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (250–300 g) were randomly assigned to three groups using a computer-generated random number sequence to ensure unbiased allocation. All rats were fasted for 12 h and allowed to acclimatize at 25 °C for 30 min before being anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 3% pentobarbital sodium (30 mg/kg). The rats were then fixed in a rodent holder, and their tails were disinfected with 75% ethanol. The tail was cut at 2–3 cm from the tip, leaving a 1–1.5 mm diameter tail artery. After 30 s of natural bleeding, the first drop of blood was absorbed with filter paper. Subsequently, CS, NCS, and CS/NCS dressings were applied to the wound. The bleeding time (from the cut to the cessation of bleeding) was recorded using a stopwatch, and the amount of blood loss was quantified using the filter paper weight method. The experiment was conducted at 28–30 °C, and a lidocaine gel was applied to the wound site for pain relief. At least three biological replicates were used per group. An overdose of isoflurane was used to ensure the animal is unconscious and pain-free before euthanasia, then cervical dislocation was performed by trained personnel.

Extraction and identification of MSC exosomes

The bone MSC line originated from a male rat (Sprague Dawley, Cat. No.: #SCSP-402) and was purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Available from: https://www.cellbank.org.cn.). MSCs were cultured in 89% MEM α medium (Gibco), 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (10,000 U/mL, Gibco) at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. The culture medium was replaced every 3–4 days to maintain cell growth and proliferation. When MSCs reached 70%–80% confluence, the medium was replaced with serum-free DMEM, and cells were cultured for an additional 48 h to promote exosome secretion. The culture supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 1000×g for 10 min to remove cell debris. The supernatant was then subjected to ultracentrifugation at 100,000×g for 1 h at 4 °C to isolate the exosome pellet, which was resuspended in PBS. The resulting exosomes were further characterized and used for functional studies.

Exosome morphology was analyzed using a Hitachi HT7800 transmission electron microscope (TEM) (Hitachi, Japan), with exosome samples negatively stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid. Particle size distribution and yield were analyzed by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NanoSight NS300). Exosome surface markers were verified by Western blot using specific antibodies against CD9, CD63, CD81, and TSG101 (Abcam).

Fluorescence microscopy of NCS/CS-EXO

Samples were cut into 10 mm diameter disks and examined without staining, utilizing the inherent fluorescence of chitosan fibers (excitation wavelength: 488 nm; emission wavelength: 520 nm). Exosomes were fluorescently labeled with PKH-26 (MedChemExpress, China) following the manufacturer’s standardized protocol. Dual-channel images were captured using a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan), and fluorescence intensity ratios (green/red) were calculated using NIS-Elements software.

Exosome release

The release kinetics of exosomes from the scaffolds were quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay. Triplicate scaffold samples (120 mg per sample, loaded with 0.7 µg of EXOs) were immersed in 400 µL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and incubated under gentle agitation (100 rpm) at 37 °C. At predetermined time intervals, 10 µL aliquots of the release medium were collected and replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS to maintain sink conditions. The cumulative protein concentration of released exosomes was determined via BCA assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) using a pre-established calibration curve. Absorbance measurements were performed at 562 nm on a microplate reader, with PBS serving as the blank control.

HUVECs tube formation in vitro

To assess the stability and biological function of exosomes loaded within NCS/CS biomaterial, exosomes (100 µl undiluted stock) were first incubated with NCS/CS material (120 mg) for 3 days. Subsequently, the composite material was immersed in 1 ml PBS for 24 h to obtain the exosome-loaded elution medium. Meanwhile, 3rd-5th passage HUVECs were seeded at a density of 4 × 10⁵ cells/mL onto Matrigel-precoated 96-well plates. Cells were then resuspended in complete medium containing 2% FBS and supplemented with equal volumes of either PBS elution medium (BLANK group), NCS/CS elution medium (NCS/CS group), or NCS/CS-Exo elution medium (NCS/CS-EXO group), as well as a 10-fold diluted exosome stock (EXO group). After incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 6 h, tube formation was assessed. Representative images of tube-like structures were captured using an inverted microscope, and tube lumen count was quantified using Image J software.

Infection-induced full-thickness skin defect repair in rats

Twenty-seven adult Sprague-Dawley rats (250–300 g) were housed under standard laboratory conditions and randomly divided into three groups (n = 9 per group). To adhere to ethical guidelines, a self-controlled design was employed without including untreated blank controls. Following anesthesia induction, three full-thickness circular excision wounds (1.0 cm diameter) were created symmetrically along the dorsal spine of each rat. Each wound was inoculated with 200 µL of Staphylococcus aureus suspension and left uncovered for 24 h to establish infection. Subsequently, wounds were topically treated with one of three materials: CS, CS/NCS, or CS/NCS-EXO. Dressings were replaced every 48 h to maintain intervention efficacy. Wound healing progression was evaluated at days 0, 3, 7, and 14 post-intervention by calculating the wound closure rate using standardized photographic measurements. For histopathological and molecular analyses, three animals per group were humanely euthanized at each time point (days 3, 7, and 14) in accordance with animal welfare protocols. Harvested tissues underwent hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining for general morphology, Masson’s trichrome staining for collagen deposition, and CD31 immunohistochemistry to assess angiogenesis.

Secretory protein profiling in wound exudate

Postoperative day-3 wound dressings (~ 120 mg) were weighed and incubated in 1 mL sterile PBS to extract cytokines. The dressings were thoroughly mixed to facilitate release of cytokines into the buffer and briefly incubated at 4 °C before centrifugation at 5000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The clear supernatants were collected and subjected to quantitative analysis of IL-6, TNF-α, and VEGF-a by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) using commercial kits from reputable manufacturers (Elabscience, China) in accordance with the manufacturers’ protocols.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using Prism software (version 10.1.2) or ORIGIN software (version 2024). Experimental data were obtained from three independent replicates and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For inter-group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was initially performed to assess overall differences between groups. Where significant variance was detected by ANOVA (p < 0.05), subsequent pairwise comparisons were carried out using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test.

Results

Morphological characteristics of chitosan microfibers

To investigate the microstructure and surface morphology of CS, NCS, and CS/NCS composite needle-punched nonwoven fabrics, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed at different magnifications. As shown in Fig. 1a–c, the pure CS nonwoven fabric is composed of a large number of randomly oriented and interlocked fibers, forming a typical three-dimensional porous network. The fiber diameter is relatively uniform, and at higher magnification (Fig. 1c), the surface of the CS fibers is smooth and dense, without visible pores or grooves. After succinylation modification, the morphology of the NCS nonwoven fabric showed significant changes (Fig. 1d-f). While the overall three-dimensional porous network structure was maintained (Fig. 1f), the fiber surface was no longer smooth. At medium and high magnifications (Fig. 1e, f), distinct grooves or striations aligned along the fiber axis were clearly observed, giving the fiber surface a rough and uneven appearance. These grooves varied in depth and width. For the CS/NCS composite needle-punched nonwoven fabric (Fig. 1g-i), the SEM images clearly show the coexistence of fibers with two different morphologies. At low magnification (Fig. 1g), the fiber arrangement and pore structure were similar to those of the previous two materials, but the fiber shapes exhibited greater diversity. At medium and high magnifications (Fig. 1h, i), fibers with relatively smooth surfaces (like CS) and those with pronounced axial groove structures (similar to NCS) were observed. These fibers interwoven and entangled to form the composite material’s framework. The average fiber diameter for CS, NCS, and CS/NCS composite nonwoven fabrics did not show significant differences (Fig. 1j–l).

Physicochemical properties of chitosan microfibers

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR spectra of CS, NCS, and their composites are shown in Fig. 2a. The chitosan spectra exhibit characteristic peaks at 3345 cm⁻¹ (overlapping O-H stretching and N-H stretching), 2860 cm⁻¹ (C-H stretching), 1635 cm⁻¹ (C = O, amide I band), 1560 cm⁻¹ (N-H, amide II band), 1376 cm⁻¹ (C-N, amide III band), 1153 cm⁻¹ (C-O-C bridge), 1013 cm⁻¹ (C6-OH stretching), and 895 cm⁻¹ (β-(1,4)-glycosidic bond) [18]. Comparing the FTIR spectra of NCS with pure CS, a peak at 1381 cm⁻¹ was attributed to the symmetric stretching of COO⁻. The NCS spectrum shows stronger absorption at 1635 cm⁻¹ (amide I band) and 1560 cm⁻¹ (amide II band), indicating successful succinylation of chitosan at the N-position, forming the − NH − CO − group. Furthermore, a new peak at approximately 1717 cm⁻¹ suggests the reaction occurred at the O-position, resulting in the formation of − C−O − CO − groups [19]. The peak at 1384 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the − COO group in the NCS structure [20]. The 2918 cm⁻¹ peak (− CH₂) further supports the succinylation of − NH₂. The FTIR spectrum of the CS/NCS composite exhibited both the CS and NCS characteristic peaks, confirming the successful integration of both components’ chemical characteristics. Notably, the CS/NCS spectrum displayed a weaker peak at 1384 cm⁻¹, corresponding to the succinyl group, which correlates with the relatively lower content of succinylated chitosan in the composite material. Additionally, the peak at 1013 cm⁻¹ showed increased intensity, indicating the preservation of chitosan’s polysaccharide structure in the composite material.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

X-ray diffraction patterns (Fig. 2b) show that pure CS exhibits a characteristic broad diffraction peak around 20° (2θ). NCS also exhibits a broad peak near 20°, but with lower intensity compared to CS. This change is attributed to the introduction of succinyl groups, which disrupt the regular alignment of polymer chains and interfere with the formation of intermolecular hydrogen bonds. The succinyl groups increase the spacing between chitosan molecular chains, reducing its crystallinity. The XRD pattern of the CS/NCS composite shows similar features, with a broad peak at 20° whose intensity is between those of CS and NCS.

Mechanical properties

The mechanical properties of CS, NCS, and CS/NCS composite nonwoven fabrics in both dry and wet states were evaluated (Fig. 3a-c for dry state, Fig. 3d-f for wet state), which are critical for wound dressing operability and environmental adaptability. In the dry state, pure CS nonwoven fabric was the most flexible (with a high elongation at break of > 95%) but exhibited the lowest strength (~ 1.0 MPa). Succinylation significantly improved the strength (~ 1.35 MPa) and stiffness of NCS, but at the expense of flexibility (elongation at break ~ 48%). This improvement is consistent with the FTIR results (Fig. 2a), confirming that the succinyl group enhances intermolecular interactions, overcoming the plasticizing effect of the reduced crystallinity observed in the XRD analysis. The CS/NCS composite exhibited excellent synergy, combining high strength (~ 1.25 MPa) and good flexibility (elongation at break ~ 83%), outperforming the individual components. In the wet state, the strength of all samples significantly decreased due to water plasticization, which weakened intermolecular hydrogen bonds and increased segment mobility. Notably, although NCS had the highest dry-state strength, it exhibited the largest reduction in wet-state strength (~ 73%), likely due to enhanced hydration from the hydrophilic groups. Pure CS had the highest wet-state strength (~ 0.39 MPa), though its elongation decreased. Importantly, the CS/NCS composite demonstrated the best wet-state performance, with a significant increase in elongation at break (~ 110%), far exceeding both its dry-state value and the other wet-state samples. This suggests that unique interactions between CS and NCS components in the wet state facilitate excellent flexibility, making it particularly beneficial for wound dressings, as it better conforms to moist, irregular wound beds, adapts to body movement, reduces interference with new tissue, and maintains the integrity of the dressing.

Hydrophilicity: contact angle, water absorption, and water retention

To comprehensively evaluate the physical properties of CS, NCS, and CS/NCS composite needle-punched nonwoven fabrics as wound dressing materials, we assessed surface wettability, water absorption kinetics, and water retention capacity. Water contact angle (WCA) measurements were performed, recording the contact angle of water droplets on the sample surfaces at t = 0 s and t = 30 s. The results, shown in Fig. 4, indicate that all three nonwoven fabrics exhibited some hydrophobicity at t = 0 s, with contact angles greater than 90°. Specifically, the pure CS nonwoven fabric exhibited the highest initial contact angle, approximately 137.2° ± 5.6°, while the initial contact angles of NCS and CS/NCS nonwoven fabrics were 119.4° ± 3.7° and 120.3° ± 2.8°, respectively, both significantly lower than that of pure CS (p < 0.05). However, over time, the wettability behavior of the surfaces showed significant differences. After 30 s, the contact angle of pure CS decreased slightly to 134.6° ± 5.2°, indicating that its surface retained strong hydrophobicity, making it difficult for the water droplet to spread and penetrate. In contrast, the contact angle of NCS nonwoven fabric dropped sharply to 60.6° ± 3.2° within 30 s, demonstrating rapid wettability and significant hydrophilicity. The contact angle of CS/NCS composite fabric also decreased markedly after 30 s, reaching 79.3° ± 4.5°, showing an intermediate wettability rate and hydrophilicity between pure CS and pure NCS. Compared to the initial contact angle, the contact angles of NCS and CS/NCS decreased significantly after 30 s (p < 0.05), while the change for CS was minimal.

We tested the water absorption and water retention performance of the materials (Fig. 2c, d). Pure CS nonwoven fabric exhibited limited water absorption, with a 24-hour equilibrium water absorption rate of approximately 7.1% ± 1.3%. NCS nonwoven fabric demonstrated the highest water absorption potential, with a rapid absorption rate and a 24-hour equilibrium water absorption rate of about 40.6% ± 1.4%. CS/NCS composite nonwoven fabric exhibited intermediate absorption behavior, with a 24-hour equilibrium water absorption rate of about 27.9% ± 1.3%. During the evaporation process, pure CS nonwoven fabric lost water the fastest, with a water retention rate of only about 14.6% ± 2.3% after 24 h. NCS nonwoven fabric showed significantly improved water retention, maintaining about 35.0% ± 2.4% water after 24 h. Notably, CS/NCS composite nonwoven fabric exhibited the best water retention performance, with the slowest water loss, maintaining about 45.8% ± 2.3% water after 24 h.

Surface charge

To evaluate the surface charge characteristics of the materials, Zeta potential measurements were conducted on CS, NCS, and CS/NCS materials (Fig. 4c, d). The pure chitosan needle-punched nonwoven fabric (CS) exhibited the highest Zeta potential, reaching 83.9 ± 4.2 mV, indicating significant positive charge. After succinylation modification, the Zeta potential of NCS significantly decreased to 63.0 ± 2.8 mV (p < 0.01), while the Zeta potential of CS/NCS composite materials was 67.1 ± 3.9 mV, slightly higher than NCS but still significantly lower than CS (p < 0.01). Zeta potential distribution curves further confirmed this trend, with the peak value for CS in the 85–90 mV range, and for NCS and CS/NCS in the 65–70 mV and 70–75 mV ranges, respectively. This indicates that the succinylation modification significantly altered the surface charge characteristics of the materials. The abundant amino groups (-NH₂) on chitosan molecules are easily protonated to form positively charged -NH₃⁺ in neutral or acidic environments, resulting in a high positive charge density for CS. During succinylation, some amino groups are replaced by carboxyl groups (-COOH), which partially dissociate into negatively charged -COO⁻ in water, neutralizing some of the positive charges and causing a decrease in Zeta potential for NCS. The CS/NCS composite combines the properties of both components, with its surface charge being closer to that of NCS, suggesting that the NCS component is primarily located on the surface of the material.

Biological performance of chitosan microfibers

Antibacterial activity

Wound infection is one of the main factors delaying wound healing, so an ideal wound dressing should possess good antibacterial activity. Figure 5a, c shows the antibacterial effects of CS, NCS, and CS/NCS materials against Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Gram-negative Escherichia coli (E. coli). From the plate culture results, it is evident that, compared to the control group, the colony number in the CS group decreased, while the colony numbers in the NCS and CS/NCS groups significantly decreased, indicating stronger antibacterial activity of these two materials. The bactericidal rate quantification clearly demonstrated the differences in antibacterial performance among the three materials. For E. coli, the bacterial death rates for CS, NCS, and CS/NCS were 59.8% ± 3.2%, 98.3% ± 3.5%, and 98.7% ± 2.8%, respectively. For S. aureus, the bacterial death rates for the three materials were 60.2% ± 2.8%, 98.5% ± 2.6%, and 99.0% ± 2.3%. Statistical analysis revealed that NCS and CS/NCS exhibited significantly enhanced antibacterial activity compared to CS (p < 0.01), with no significant difference between NCS and CS/NCS (p > 0.05). These results suggest that succinylation significantly improved the antibacterial performance of chitosan, and the composite material CS/NCS retained this enhanced effect.

Hemostatic performance

Acute bleeding is a major clinical issue in skin trauma, and dressings must have strong hemostatic properties. Figure 5b, d, f show the hemostatic performance of CS, NCS, and CS/NCS materials in a rat tail cut model for simulating acute bleeding. Notably, the CS/NCS group showed the most impressive hemostatic performance, likely due to the combined effects of chemical modification and blending.

Loading and release of MSC-exosomes & HUVECs tube formation in vitro

In the exosome loading experiments with CS, CS/NCS, and CS/NCS-EXO materials, confocal microscopy images after electrostatic assembly showed that exosomes successfully attached to the surface of the materials (Fig. 6e). In the CS/NCS-EXO group, the amount of exosome binding remained relatively stable, indicating that the material modification enhanced exosome loading capacity. This result suggests that CS/NCS-EXO materials may offer potential advantages in exosome stability and drug delivery capabilities. The composite material demonstrated a biphasic exosome release profile, with an initial burst (40–50% within 24 h) followed by sustained release achieving ≥ 95% cumulative efficiency over 7 days (Figure S2).

The tube formation assay in HUVECs demonstrated that the NCS/CS-EXO extract significantly enhanced tube formation capacity compared to the BLANK and NCS/CS groups (without exosomes), while no statistically significant difference was observed between the NCS/CS-EXO and EXO groups. These results indicate that the dressing platform effectively delivers exosomes while preserving their intrinsic pro-angiogenic biological functionality (Figure S3).

By day 14, the CS group’s wound remained incompletely healed, while the CS/NCS and CS/NCS-EXO groups had significantly healed, with the CS/NCS-EXO group nearly restored to its original state. Healing area comparisons between groups revealed that the CS/NCS-EXO group consistently exhibited superior healing compared to the CS and CS/NCS groups, particularly at days 7 and 14.

Discussion

During skin injury or post-surgical wound healing, disruptions in the local microenvironment, infection risks, and underlying pathological factors often lead to complications such as hemorrhage, infection, and delayed healing, significantly affecting prognosis and increasing healthcare costs. Existing studies on functional dressings primarily aim to meet the multifaceted physiological requirements of wound healing, such as hemostasis, exudate management, promotion of debridement and epithelialization, while avoiding adhesive damage to granulation tissue and adapting to the dynamic changes in the healing microenvironment [21, 22]. Chitosan is widely used due to its antibacterial properties, biocompatibility, and hemostatic function. However, its clinical translation faces challenges such as complex preparation processes, high costs, insufficient hydrophilic modification, and a lack of active components that promote regeneration [23]. Meanwhile, the application of MSC-EXO in wound healing is limited by low delivery efficiency and instability, especially with local injection and intravenous administration, which face the problems of rapid clearance and repeated dosing. Although three-dimensional hydrogels show great potential as exosome delivery carriers, their high production costs and the potential to damage exosome membranes through chemical modification still restrict their widespread application [24].

In this study, chitosan fiber-based nonwoven fabrics were utilized as a platform for exosome delivery. Additionally, N-succinylation modification of chitosan was introduced to enhance hydrophilicity. Electrostatic interactions between the composite fibers and exosomes were leveraged to create a multifunctional wound reconstruction system that includes antibacterial, hemostatic, moisture control, and exosome sustained-release properties. This system not only significantly reduces material costs but also retains the antimicrobial and hemostatic properties of chitosan, providing an innovative solution for wound healing with promising clinical applications.

Using a wet spinning technique to prepare micron-sized chitosan fibers, combined with succinylation modification and needle-punched nonwoven processing, this study successfully overcame the limitations of traditional chitosan materials in terms of water absorption and retention. Chitosan itself is hydrophilic, but the wet-spun micron-sized chitosan fibers have a strongly hydrophobic surface, with a water contact angle near the superhydrophobic threshold. This results in the formation of a water barrier on the surface, limiting its exudate management capacity. However, through succinylation modification, N-succinyl chitosan (NCS) introduced hydrophilic carboxyl groups, significantly improving surface hydrophilicity, reducing the water contact angle, and enhancing water absorption and retention properties. Chitosan fibers, due to their surface structure and rigid framework, have limited water absorption. NCS, although capable of efficient water absorption by forming a gel-like state through rapid hydration, faces stability issues in water retention due to the loose structure of the gel network. By constructing the CS/NCS composite system, the rigid framework of chitosan provides stable three-dimensional structural support, while the dynamic hydrophilic groups of NCS form reversible hydration microchannels within the composite material. This synergistic effect not only overcomes the imbalance in water absorption and retention performance of individual components but also ensures efficient water absorption and prolonged water retention through capillary action and the confinement effect of the chitosan framework. Animal experiments further demonstrated that compared to the CS group, the CS/NCS group significantly enhanced epithelial protection during the wound healing process, primarily due to its superior water retention capacity. This effectively reduced adhesion to newly formed tissue during dressing changes and promoted the formation of new epithelium. The fiber microstructure introduced by the needle-punching process also optimized the water permeability and hydrogel properties of the fibers, overcoming the performance bottlenecks of individual materials.

Additionally, the CS/NCS composite nonwoven fabric exhibited remarkable wet-state extensibility. In its dry state, NCS enhanced the material’s rigidity (increased strength but reduced flexibility), which is likely related to the introduction of succinyl groups and the observed microstructural changes. However, the CS/NCS composite material achieved an effective balance between strength and flexibility in the dry state. More critically, the material’s performance in simulated physiological moist environments was tested. The plasticizing effect of water universally reduced the strength of all samples. However, the CS/NCS composite nonwoven fabric exhibited a particularly remarkable feature: in the wet state, it demonstrated exceptional flexibility (the elongation at break increased sharply to approximately 110%), far surpassing its dry-state value and the pure components. Although its wet-state strength decreased (~ 0.28 MPa), this outstanding wet-state flexibility is an ideal characteristic for advanced wound dressings. It allows the composite material to conform well to moist, irregular wound surfaces, adapt to bodily movements, reduce interference with newly formed tissues, and maintain the integrity of the dressing. This unique wet-state behavior may be attributed to enhanced lubrication effects at the CS/NCS interface, facilitating fiber slippage. Thus, the CS/NCS composite material cleverly overcomes the limitations of individual components through structural design, with its outstanding wet-state flexibility being one of the core advantages for its use as an advanced wound dressing. This makes it highly suitable for applications that require high conformability and skin adhesion.

In terms of antibacterial properties, the CS group exhibited some antimicrobial activity, whereas the NCS and CS/NCS groups showed more significant inhibitory effects on Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus. The enhanced hydrophilicity of NCS and CS/NCS improved their interaction with bacterial membranes, leading to a sustained release of antimicrobial agents, thereby boosting antibacterial efficacy. Furthermore, the CS/NCS composite material also demonstrated good hemostatic effects, which, combined with its hydrophilic and modified physicochemical properties, facilitated wound healing.

Finally, this study also demonstrated the advantages of NCS/CS composite materials in exosome delivery. Through electrostatic self-assembly, exosomes successfully interacted with the surface of NCS/CS composite materials, significantly enhancing the stability of the exosomes and avoiding the damage to exosome membranes commonly seen in traditional delivery systems. The hydrophilic modification of NCS improved the compatibility between exosomes and the carrier, promoting controlled release. This innovative delivery strategy showed good results in animal models, especially in promoting angiogenesis and skin regeneration, further confirming the potential of NCS/CS composite materials in wound healing.

Although this study highlighted the advantages of NCS/CS composite materials in wound healing, it does have limitations. The study is more clinically oriented, and while MSC-derived exosomes were used to improve the skin regeneration phenotype and angiogenesis in animal models, the underlying cellular mechanisms were not thoroughly validated, which is a gap in scientific research [25]. The animal model used E. coli-infected wound models, and further simulations involving additional bacterial strains are needed to create more comprehensive scenarios. While the exosome delivery system demonstrated good results, further research is needed to assess its long-term stability, concentration control, release efficiency, and sustained efficacy in complex wound environments. Additionally, the source and preparation costs of exosomes should also be considered. Therefore, future studies should focus on optimizing production processes, improving exosome delivery efficiency and stability, and evaluating its feasibility in clinical applications.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed a chitosan-based composite dressing incorporating MSC-derived exosomes, demonstrating superior performance in wound healing through synergistic effects of antibacterial, hemostatic, moisture retention, and exosome delivery capabilities. The novel CS/NCS-EXO system offers a promising, scalable solution for chronic wound management, with significant potential for clinical translation in enhancing tissue regeneration and infection control.
