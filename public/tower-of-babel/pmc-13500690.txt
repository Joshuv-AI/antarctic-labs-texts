Changes in myocardial elasticity derived from in-vivo cardiac MRI in a swine model of ischemic heart failure
Authors: Eli Lefkowitz, Jacob Ref, Fox Bravo, Aidan Gee, Pouria Mostafizi, Grace Gorman, Kenneth Fox, Jordan J. Lancaster, Tushar Acharya, Michael Moulton, Ryan Avery, Christopher Appleton, Steven Goldman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The progression of chronic heart failure after myocardial infarction (MI) is traditionally evaluated by serial changes in left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction (EF), a measure of systolic function. Our study evaluated how a combined wall stress and wall strain analysis of myocardial elasticity (secant modulus) offers a more complete assessment of myocardial changes that occur after MI. Using a clinically relevant ischemic swine model, we calculated secant modulus in-vivo from cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), to better understand changes in cardiac remodeling and to identify potential early detection markers. Male Yucatan mini swine (N = 15) underwent 90-min occlusion-reperfusion of the left anterior descending coronary artery to induce MI. Cardiac MRIs were obtained at baseline (before MI) and 1-month post-MI. Secant modulus was calculated in the anteroseptal (infarcted) and inferolateral (non-infarcted) regions, in the longitudinal and circumferential fiber directions. This method applied both strain and stress, the latter utilizing invasive hemodynamic LV pressure data acquired prior to MRIs. Anteroseptal and inferolateral longitudinal secant modulus increased from 28.01 ± 8.14 to 67.14 ± 15.08 kPa (p < 0.05) and from 13.88 ± 1.92 to 38.80 ± 7.29 kPa (p < 0.001), respectively. Anteroseptal and inferolateral circumferential secant modulus increased from 34.61 ± 10.33 to 119.17 ± 30.94 kPa (p < 0.05) and from 16.77 ± 1.32 to 58.76 ± 18.98 kPa (p < 0.001), respectively. The regional changes were accompanied with a decrease in EF from 60.6 ± 1.5 to 50.9 ± 3.3% (p < 0.01). Secant modulus has value in quantifying regional changes with LV dysfunction after ischemic insult; specifically, that regional abnormalities in elasticity occur in non-infarcted and infarcted areas. Although prior research has explored the ex-vivo characteristics of cardiac elasticity, there is limited research detailing in-vivo changes associated with diseases affecting myocardial fiber contractility. A better understanding of how the elasticity of the non-infarcted myocardium is adversely affected in-vivo may offer future opportunities for therapies preventing heart failure progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53804-w.

Introduction

Chronic heart failure (CHF) is a serious global health burden, with recent estimates indicating that as many as one out of four people will develop CHF within their lifetime [1]. The prevalence of heart failure is projected to increase substantially in coming years, attributed to an aging population and improved survival rates following cardiac events. Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) constitutes a common form of heart failure, with myocardial infarction (MI) being its leading cause.

The structural and functional changes that occur in the heart following MI are critical to our understanding of disease progression and development of novel therapeutic interventions. Echocardiography and ejection fraction (EF) have traditionally been used for evaluating cardiac function in patients with heart failure and MI [2]. Echocardiography provides real-time visualization with bedside capability, allowing clinicians to determine the area of ischemia by wall motion abnormalities. However, this approach is qualitative and does not provide quantitative information about regional changes in elasticity that occur in both infarcted and non-infarcted myocardium in the weeks following an acute ischemic event in the heart post-MI.

Advanced imaging techniques such as computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offer enhanced spatial resolution, which enables more accurate evaluation of myocardial function and left ventricular (LV) remodeling [3]. Cardiology has largely embraced mechanical concepts including wall strain and wall stress to quantify myocardial tissue properties. Wall strain measures deformation as a percentage change in length at the level of the myocardium [4]. Additionally, strain must be measured in 3 different directions, reflecting the anisotropy of the myocardium [5]. The longitudinal directions squeezes the base and the apex of the heart together, the circumferential directions provide a torsional contraction, and the radial directions contract inwards. Wall stress represents force per unit area on the myocardium and is often calculated using Laplace’s Law in clinical settings [6].

The composite of stress and strain analysis, expressed as the elastic modulus, helps to provide a better understanding of regional myocardial mechanics and intrinsic tissue elasticity. While Hooke’s law describes a linear relationship between stress and strain in ideal elastic materials, myocardial tissue is inelastic and exhibits nonlinear behavior over physiologic deformation ranges. Accordingly, we quantified stiffness as the slope of the stress–strain relationship between end-diastole and end-systole, where E is secant modulus, σ is the wall stress, and \varepsilon is the wall strain [7] (Fig. 1). Following MI, fibrosis and other pathological LV remodeling processes lead to increased myocardial stiffness, which adversely affects ventricular compliance and function [8]. Previous studies in mouse models have utilized the elastic modulus to evaluate changes in myocardial elasticity following therapeutic administration and demonstrate myocardial elastic stability in the experimental groups compared to controls [9].

In swine models, the elastic modulus has been assessed after MI using in-vivo magnetic resonance elastography, showing increased diastolic stiffness compared to controls (4.6 ± 0.7 kPa vs. 3.0 ± 0.6 kPa, p = 0.02). Ex-vivo tensile testing has corroborated these findings, with the infarcted LV demonstrating greater stiffness than controls (650 ± 80 kPa vs. 110 ± 20 kPa, p = 0.01) [10]. Notably, the variation in the elastic modulus across different measurement techniques suggests method-dependent results [11]. Myocardial stiffness is not constant; it varies regionally between infarcted and non-infarcted tissue, and throughout the cardiac cycle, emphasizing the need for methodologies to track elasticity changes between systole and diastole.

We propose that calculating secant modulus in-vivo using MRI-derived wall stress and wall strain measurements have the potential to quantify the extent of myocardial remodeling, and aid in the early detection of heart failure progression. We propose this methodology as an accessible and high-resolution approach for evaluating in-vivo cardiac elasticity in research and clinical settings.

Methods

Ethics approval

Research oversight and approval was conducted by University of Arizona’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. University Animal Care provided veterinary care, husbandry and surgical oversight within their facilities. All experiments and methods were performed in accordance with the National Institute of Health’s “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” and in compliance with Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) approved protocols at the University of Arizona Animal Care Program (number 17-259, approved 06/14/2017). The methods and results of this study are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Study model

Male Yucatan mini swine aged 9–12 months (N = 15) were purchased from Premier BioSource (Ramona, CA, USA). The swine were acclimated for 1 week prior to full socialization with the other swine and research staff. Swine experienced 12-h light/dark cycles, the room temperature was 70°F, and the relative humidity was 30–50%. Swine were monitored daily and provided enrichment. Enrichment involved cleaning the swine and running on treadmills. Swine were fed twice per day and housed separately. The study’s sample size was determined by balancing research feasibility and ethical considerations typical of large animal cardiac research. We selected a number of subjects that provide established precedent in similar large animal cardiac studies, while addressing the practical limitations of complex surgical procedures and available resources.

Anesthesia and euthanasia

Swine were sedated with ketamine (11–33 mg/kg, IM), midazolam (0.1–0.5 mg/kg, IM), or telazol (5–8 mg/kg, IM) [12]. General anesthesia maintained with isoflurane (1–3%). Analgesia was maintained during the procedure with buprenorphine (SR 0.12–0.27 mg/kg, SC), carprofen (2–4 mg/kg, IM;PO;SC), and bupivicaine (2 mg/kg, SC,II), and was continued post-operatively for 3–7 days as needed. Euthanasia was performed under anesthesia with 2 meq/kg potassium chloride (KCl) intravenously.

MI development

The left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery occlusion-reperfusion model has been previously detailed by our laboratory [12, 13]. Throughout the procedure, standard vital signs were continuously monitored. Anticoagulation with Heparin (100–200 USP/kg) was performed after percutaneous femoral access was established (8-11F introducer sheath). To prevent arrhythmia, Lidocaine (2–4 mg/kg) was administered as needed along with continuous infusion (20–60 mcg/kg/min), in addition to IV amiodarone for further rhythm management.

Using angiographic guidance, AL 0.75 or 1.0 guide catheters were used to place PTCA balloons (2.0–3.75 mm diameter × 12–15 mm length) distal to the first or second diagonal branch of the LAD. The injury was targeted towards the lower third of the LV as proximal injuries lead to higher occurrence of ventricular fibrillation. Contrast angiography was performed at 0, 45, and 90 min after LAD occlusion to confirm the balloon inflation pressure required for complete occlusion. After 90 min, the balloon was deflated, angiography was performed to confirm reperfusion. Access sites were closed by manual compression and all devices were removed. Cardiac MRIs were performed prior to the MI and 1-month post-MI (1 m pMI). This 1-month timepoint has been validated in previous swine MI models as representing established heart failure with significant ventricular dysfunction and remodeling [14, 15]. One swine died due to intractable ventricular fibrillation during the time of the MI, and one other swine died during anesthesia induction prior to cardiac catheterization. No antibiotics were used.

MRI image acquisition

Cardiac MRI studies were conducted with a 3-T MRI system (Magnetom Skyra, Siemens Healthineers). Localization initially utilized two-dimensional turbo flash (TFL2D) scout images, with a 400 mm × 400 mm field of view and 8 mm slice thickness for three orthogonal planes. Progressive refinement using two-chamber (2-CH), three-chamber (3-CH), and four-chamber (4-CH) positioning sequences established cardiac chamber orientation. Short axis (SAX) and long-axis views (2-CH, 3-CH, and 4-CH) were obtained with gated cine sequences retrospectively, with high spatial resolution (0.8 mm × 0.8 mm × 4 mm voxels) using RF spoiling, and with phase encoding in 4–7 segments based on heart rate. Parameters consisted of 45% phase oversampling, 23.53 ms repetition time (TR), 2.88 ms echo time (TE), and 15° flip angle. Three signal averages were taken at each slice position to mitigate artifact due to respiratory motion. Smart-signal averaging of gradient-recalled echo (GRE)-based array of pulse sequences was used for heart rate synchronization so breath-holding or ventilator gating techniques were not required.

Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) imaging was performed approximately 6 min after injecting 2.0 mL/s gadobenate dimeglumine contrast (0.2 mmol/kg, MultiHance, Bracco) (Fig. 3). Optimal myocardial nulling time was determined by an inversion recovery time (TI) scout sequence, ranging from 100 to 645 ms. LGE images were captured in the same orientation as the functional series. The following parameters were used: 1.35 mm × 1.35 mm × 4 mm voxels, a gated turbo flash sequence with nonselective inversion pulse, and 10° flip angle.Fig. 3LGE cardiac MRI images at baseline and one month after myocardial infarction. Infarct sizing and late gadolinium enhancement analysis in cardiac MRI

The American Heart Association (AHA) 17-segment model divides the LV into 17 regions from base to apex in order to calculate infarct percentage [16]. Delayed enhancement was evaluated in 1-month post-MI images using 8 to 12 slices, and the infarct size was calculated as a percentage of the LV. Endocardial and epicardial borders were segmented manually. During the analysis, the infarcted region as identified on LGE images was defined as the anteroseptal and anterior regions, and the non-infarcted region was defined as the inferolateral and inferior regions.

Cardiac wall strain imaging and analysis

At both timepoints, cardiac MRI images were contoured with the use of post-processing image analysis software (Circle Cardiovascular Imaging Inc., Calgary, AB, Canada). Endocardial and epicardial contours were created on the LV at systole and diastole slices of long-axis, SAX, 2-CH, 3-CH, and 4-CH cardiac MRIs feature tracking analysis, analogous to echocardiographic feature tracking, was performed using the software’s tissue tracking module. This technique tracks the movement of unique myocardial features through the cardiac cycle, allowing for the measurement of myocardial deformation without the need for tagged MRI sequences. The peak wall strain values were generated for each of the segments in the AHA 17-segment model, and the mean values of the infarcted region, representative of the LAD vascular territory, were calculated. The non-infarcted region was examined separately. The output of the analysis software provided peak Lagrangian wall strain as a percentage change in length from the diastolic to the systolic state. Secant modulus

Secant modulus was directly calculated as a function of wall stress and wall strain values from the cardiac MRI, where a Hookian constant modulus was assumed. The secant modulus was then averaged across all swine; circumferential and longitudinal fiber directions were analyzed separately.

Radial secant modulus was omitted in the analysis due to the inherent difficulty of measurement that produces a wide scatter in data. Radial strain is difficult to calculate due to the conical shape and the irregular wall thickness of the LV. Due to circumferential and longitudinal oriented forces, the radial direction is not always perpendicular to the mid-LV cavity. Additionally, longitudinal deformation creates coupling effects where systolic longitudinal shortening interacts with radial thickening that are mathematically difficult to separate, potentially introducing artifacts when imaging planes are not perfectly perpendicular to the true radial direction. The problems measuring radial strain have been noted by other investigators, with a much wider scatter in data than the circumferential and longitudinal fiber directions.

The greater degree of error that occurs with measuring radial wall stress and wall strain compound when calculating secant modulus. For all of the aforementioned reasons, the radial modulus would be an inconsistent and unreliable indicator of LV pathology, and instead we leverage measurements in the circumferential and longitudinal fiber directions.

Statistical analysis

Cardiac MRI data were analyzed using the Student’s paired t-test. When the Shapiro–Wilk Normality test failed, the Wilcoxon-Signed Rank test was performed. Statistical analysis was performed using SigmaPlot version 14.5 software. MRI image segmentation and AHA 17-segment infarct sizing were performed without blinding, as images were reviewed sequentially following baseline MRI and then following the post-MI MRI, and given that all swine underwent myocardial infarction there was only a single experimental group. Statistical analyses were handled by a separately and independently blinded analyst. The data analyst remained blinded throughout statistical analyses.

Data exclusion

Acquiring high-quality MRI data in swine presents significant technical challenges. Anesthetic dosing required achieving adequate sedation while preserving respiratory function; limiting subject movement was essential to optimal MRI data quality. One swine MRI in the longitudinal wall strain dataset was excluded due to tracking artifacts as mentioned above.

Discussion

This study investigated the use of in-vivo MRI-derived secant modulus to quantify regional myocardial elasticity changes before and 1-month after an acute anterior MI in a Yucatan mini swine model. We utilized high-resolution cardiac MRI to measure both wall stress and wall strain in infarcted and non-infarcted myocardial regions, providing an evaluation of myocardial mechanical properties beyond traditional global measurements such as LV EF [22].

Our findings demonstrate significant increases in secant modulus across both measured fiber directions (longitudinal and circumferential) at 1-month post-MI, with consistently higher values in infarcted regions compared to non-infarcted areas. These results are consistent with established swine models of post-MI heart failure, where significant ventricular dysfunction and remodeling occur by 4-weeks post-infarction [14, 15, 17]. Additionally, these results highlight the global nature of LV remodeling following an anterior MI and suggest that secant modulus may serve as a sensitive biomarker for quantifying these adverse changes in regional myocardial stiffness [23, 24].

Greater baseline wall stress in the anterior wall when compared with the lateral and posterior wall have been noted in prior studies [25]. This is potentially due to anterior wall fibers originating from the septum, which is a species-specific anatomical feature that contributes to the wall stress pattern in our swine model. The observed increase in secant modulus in both infarcted and non-infarcted regions shows the complex LV remodeling process following a cardiac ischemic injury. In the infarcted region, the dramatic decrease in elasticity suggests a replacement of contractile myocardium with stiff, interstitial tissue or fibrosis [26]. The moderate but significant increase in secant modulus in non-infarcted regions shows adverse remodeling with increased stiffness, presumably myocardial rather than fibrotic, also occurred in the inferior myocardium, an area vascularly isolated from the ischemic area. The pathophysiology of these remote effects remains unknown but might be due to the wrapping of myocardial layers, or increased wall stress from altered ventricular geometry and function [27, 28]. These regional changes in myocardial elasticity occurred alongside the expected, but the modest average 10% decline in global LV EF, from the anterior infarction [29, 30]. A key advantage of our approach is that it uses widely available and established cardiac MRI techniques without requiring additional specialized equipment or contrast agents [31]. This makes it applicable in any setting where cardiac MRI is already performed as part of routine care. The regional assessment of myocardial stiffness stands to complement existing measurements to provide a more complete evaluation of cardiac function and structure [32].

The secant modulus calculated in this study represents a net approximation spanning the systolic-to-diastolic transition and does not resolve instantaneous stiffness at specific phases of the cardiac cycle. Future studies using phase-resolved or diastolic-specific analyses would provide greater mechanistic insight. Additionally, myocardial stiffness is known to be influenced by age-related fibrosis and sex-specific differences in extracellular matrix composition and hormonal environment; the current study’s restriction to young adult male swine limits generalizability, and these variables should be explored in future work.

While the current study focuses on ischemic cardiomyopathy, the effective Young’s modulus approach may have broader applicability to conditions characterized by increased myocardial stiffness, such as HFpEF, cardiac amyloidosis, or hypertensive heart disease. However, direct extension to these conditions is speculative based on the current data and would require dedicated validation studies [33, 34].

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, our model represents a MI with a defined timeline of 1-month after the MI, whereas many clinical conditions involve chronic, progressive myocardial stiffening over a longer period [35]. Second, the use of Laplace’s law with a cylindrical model simplifies the complex cardiac geometry [36, 37]. While this cylindrical approximation offers computational efficiency, it may fail to adequately represent the heterogeneous stress patterns inherent to the left ventricle’s complex geometry, especially regarding an infarct heart with wall thinning. Tensile testing was not performed in conjunction with the imaging studies due to the method dependent results with secant modulus in other studies [11]. An additional limitation is the use of invasive methods to calculate LVESP and LVEDP with a conductance catheter which may limit translation into clinical practice. Few non-invasive methods that can be used to estimate LVEDP with echocardiography (E/e’ ratio) being the main method but should not be used in isolation to determine LVEDP [37]. Finally, the small sample size and use of only male swine may not take into account gender differences in cardiac response to an acute ischemic injury.

In conclusion, we have shown that in-vivo MRI-derived secant modulus can be used to evaluate changes in regional myocardial elasticity in the LV after an anterior MI, and that at one-month adverse changes in stiffness were present in an area remote from the ischemic injury. A better understanding of the cellular and mechanical mechanisms involved may have meaningful clinical applications in reducing post infarct heart failure. The calculation of the modulus may also have more immediate application in monitoring various cardiomyopathies, particularly in those that are characterized by increased myocardial stiffness with preserved EF [38, 39]. We believe future studies that explore the prognostic value of regional elasticity measurements in various forms of clinical cardiomyopathy are warranted.
