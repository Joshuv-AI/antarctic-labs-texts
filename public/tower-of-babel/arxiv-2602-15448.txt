Cosmological Averaging in Nonminimally Coupled Gravity
S. R. Pinto, P. P. Avelino

Abstract
We address the challenge, commonly referred to as the cosmological averaging problem, of relating the large-scale evolution of an inhomogeneous Universe to that predicted by a homogeneous matter distribution in theories of gravity with nonminimal matter–gravity couplings. To this end, we focus on the class of f ⁡ ( R , T ) f(R,T) models defined by f ⁡ ( R , T ) = R + F ⁡ ( T ) f(R,T)=R+F(T) , which provide a simple yet theoretically consistent realization of nonminimal matter–gravity interactions and can be reformulated as general relativity minimally coupled to a modified matter Lagrangian. Using nonstandard global monopole solutions as a toy model for realistic particles, we show that the spatial average of F F typically differs significantly from F F evaluated at the spatially averaged trace of T T , implying that homogeneous cosmological models generally fail to capture the correct large-scale dynamics of the Universe. We further show that dust in these theories generally exhibits a non-vanishing proper pressure. Our results underscore the necessity of properly accounting for spatial averaging when modeling cosmology in theories with nonminimal matter–gravity couplings.



I
Introduction

The Universe is highly inhomogeneous on small and intermediate scales, exhibiting nonlinear structures ranging from particles to planets and stars, and extending to galaxies, clusters, and cosmic filaments
Somerville and Davé 2015
. By contrast, standard cosmological models
Aghanim et al. 2020
are based on smooth, homogeneous, and isotropic solutions of Einstein’s equations, described by the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) metric
Robertson 1935
. Due to the nonlinear character of Einstein’s equations, it is not guaranteed that the large-scale evolution of an inhomogeneous Universe coincides with that of an exactly homogeneous FLRW spacetime. This observation underlies the cosmological averaging, or backreaction, problem, which arises when one attempts to relate the dynamics of an averaged geometry to the average of the local gravitational dynamics
Buchert 2000
;
Buchert 2001
;
Rasanen 2004
;
Kolb 2011
;
Ellis 2011
;
Clarkson et al. 2011
;
Buchert et al. 2020
. In this context, the resulting effective evolution equations generally include additional geometric terms that encode the impact of inhomogeneities. The physical significance and magnitude of such geometric backreaction effects remain actively debated (see, e.g.,
Baumann et al. 2012
;
Macpherson et al. 2019
).

A closely related aspect of the cosmological averaging problem concerns the coarse-graining of the matter sources entering the gravitational field equations. Even if the geometric backreaction effects are small and the spacetime is assumed to be well approximated by an FLRW geometry on all scales, averaging over fluid inhomogeneities generally yields an effective energy–momentum tensor that does not coincide with that of a homogeneous fluid obeying the same equation of state. Within general relativity, this issue is largely mitigated due to the minimal coupling between matter and geometry. Nevertheless, even in general relativity, it can become important in scenarios in which a single component drives both structure formation and cosmic acceleration, such as unified dark energy models
Avelino et al. 2004
;
Beca and Avelino 2007
;
Avelino et al. 2008
, where nonlinear inhomogeneities can substantially modify the effective equation of state.

These theories have received increasing attention, motivated by both phenomenological and theoretical considerations, and have been explored as alternatives to a cosmological constant to explain cosmic acceleration. A distinctive feature of these theories is that matter and geometry interact beyond the minimal coupling prescription of general relativity, leading to modified conservation laws and macroscopic dynamics of fluids and gravity that depend on the physics happening at microscopic scales

To address the cosmological averaging problem in this class of theories, we focus on
R+F(T)

This theory can be reformulated as general relativity minimally coupled to an effective matter Lagrangian, so that the gravitational sector retains its standard form while all nonminimal matter–geometry interactions are encoded in the matter sector. This makes
R+F(T)
gravity particularly well suited for isolating the effects of matter coarse-graining and tracing them directly to the underlying nonlinear matter coupling. Moreover, compared to more general nonminimally coupled models, this class of theories is known to evade several of the instabilities identified in the literature
Lacombe et al. 2024
(see, however,
Harko et al. 2025

II
f(R,T)
gravity

Here, we begin by reviewing the general framework of
f(R,T)
gravity
Harko et al. 2011
, which extends general relativity by allowing the gravitational action to depend explicitly on both the Ricci scalar
R\equiv g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu}
and the trace
T\equiv g^{\mu\nu}T_{\mu\nu}
of the energy–momentum tensor, providing a natural setting to explore the effects of nonminimal matter–gravity couplings.

The action for
f(R,T)
gravity is given by

S=\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\left[f(R,T)+\mathcal{L}_{\rm m}\right],

(1)

where
g
is the determinant of the metric
g_{\mu\nu}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\rm m}
is the Lagrangian of the matter fields. The corresponding equations of motion for the gravitational field are given by

2(R_{\mu\nu}-\Delta_{\mu\nu})f_{,R}-g_{\mu\nu}f=\mathcal{T}_{\mu\nu},

(2)

where a comma denotes a partial derivative.
R_{\mu\nu}
is the Ricci tensor,
\Delta_{\mu\nu}\equiv\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}-g_{\mu\nu}\Box
,
\Box\equiv\nabla_{\mu}\nabla^{\mu}
and,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}-2f_{,T}(T_{\mu\nu}+\mathbb{T}_{\mu\nu}),

(3)

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{\sqrt{-g}}\frac{\delta(\sqrt{-g}\mathcal{L}_{\rm m})}{\delta g^{\mu\nu}}=g_{\mu\nu}\mathcal{L}_{\rm m}-2\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}_{\rm m}}{\delta g^{\mu\nu}},

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle g^{\alpha\beta}\frac{\delta T_{\alpha\beta}}{\delta g^{\mu\nu}}=\frac{\delta T}{\delta g^{\mu\nu}}-T_{\mu\nu}\,.

(5)

Equation (
2
) can be rearranged as

G_{\mu\nu}\equiv R_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}R=\frac{1}{2}\mathscr{T}_{\mu\nu}\,,

(6)

where
G_{\mu\nu}
are the components of the Einstein tensor and

\mathscr{T}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{f_{,R}}\left[2\Delta_{\mu\nu}f_{,R}-g_{\mu\nu}(Rf_{,R}-f)+\mathcal{T}_{\mu\nu}\right]\,.

(7)

In
f(R,T)
gravity the standard energy–momentum tensor
T_{\mu\nu}
is typically not conserved. However, since the Einstein tensor is covariantly conserved, the tensor
\mathscr{T}_{\mu\nu}
must also satisfy

\nabla^{\mu}\mathscr{T}_{\mu\nu}=0\,.

(8)

However, the physical interpretation of Eq. (
8
), is not straightforward since
\mathscr{T}_{\mu\nu}
is nontrivially dependent on the Ricci scalar and its derivatives.

II.1
R+F(T)
gravity

For certain functional forms of
f(R,T)
, the physical meaning of the field equations becomes more transparent. A particularly illustrative example is the separable case,

f(R,T)=f_{1}(R)+f_{2}(T)\,.

(9)

In this scenario, the theory can be reformulated as an
f_{1}(R)
gravity model with an effective modification of the matter sector. More precisely, the
f_{2}(T)
term can be absorbed into the matter Lagrangian, yielding a modified matter Lagrangian

\mathscr{L}_{\rm m}=\ \mathcal{L}_{\rm m}+f_{2}(T)\,,

(10)

which in turn leads to a well-defined modified energy–momentum tensor. In this form, deviations from standard
f(R)
gravity are completely encoded in the modified matter dynamics induced by the explicit dependence on the trace
T
.

For simplicity, throughout this work we focus on the simpler class of
f(R,T)=R+F(T)
models, which can be recast as GR with a modified matter Lagrangian,

\mathscr{L}_{\rm m}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm m}+F\,.

(11)

For this class of models,
\mathscr{T}_{\mu\nu}
are the components of a modified energy-momentum tensor given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{T}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{\sqrt{-g}}\frac{\delta(\sqrt{-g}\mathscr{L}_{\rm m})}{\delta g^{\mu\nu}}

(12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}+Fg_{\mu\nu}-2F_{,T}\left(T_{\mu\nu}+\mathbb{T}_{\mu\nu}\right)\,.

Its trace is then

\mathscr{T}=T+4F-2F_{,T}\left(T+\mathbb{T}\right)\,.

(13)

R+F(T)
gravity is dynamically equivalent to GR with a modified matter Lagrangian, which might suggest that it offers little new physical insight
Fisher and Carlson 2019
. Nevertheless, this equivalence is particularly useful: it provides a familiar and well-controlled framework for exploring the phenomenology of
f(R,T)
gravity and, more generally, of theories featuring nonminimal couplings between geometry and matter fields. By working in a setting where the geometric sector remains standard while the matter sector is modified in a precise and tractable manner, one can isolate and analyze some of the physical consequences of such couplings without the additional complications associated to non-trivial modifications of the gravitational action itself.

II.2
Scalar-field matter source

To set the stage for the study of K-monopoles in the next section, here we briefly review a number of relevant results for a scalar-field matter source.

Consider a matter field described by a real scalar field multiplet
\phi^{a}
with Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}_{\rm m}(\phi^{a},X)
, where

X=-\frac{1}{2}\,\nabla_{\mu}\phi^{a}\nabla^{\mu}\phi^{a}\,.

(14)

The corresponding energy-momentum tensor is

T_{\mu\nu}=\mathcal{L}_{{\rm m},X}\,\nabla_{\mu}\phi^{a}\nabla_{\nu}\phi^{a}+\mathcal{L}_{\rm m}\,g_{\mu\nu}\,,

(15)

with trace

T\equiv{T^{\mu}}_{\mu}=-2X\,\mathcal{L}_{{\rm m},X}+4\,\mathcal{L}_{\rm m}\,.

(16)

Noting that

\displaystyle\frac{\delta X}{\delta g^{\mu\nu}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\,\nabla_{\mu}\phi^{a}\nabla_{\nu}\phi^{a}\,,

(17)

\displaystyle T_{,X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\,(X\,\mathcal{L}_{{\rm m},XX}-\mathcal{L}_{{\rm m},X})\,,

(18)

one immediately obtains

\frac{\delta T}{\delta g^{\mu\nu}}=\left(X\,\mathcal{L}_{{\rm m},XX}-\mathcal{L}_{{\rm m},X}\right)\nabla_{\mu}\phi^{a}\nabla_{\nu}\phi^{a}\,.

(19)

Using Eq. (
5
), one then finds

\mathbb{T}_{\mu\nu}=\left(X\,\mathcal{L}_{{\rm m},XX}-2\mathcal{L}_{{\rm m},X}\right)\nabla_{\mu}\phi^{a}\nabla_{\nu}\phi^{a}-\mathcal{L}_{\rm m}\,g_{\mu\nu}\,,

(20)

whose trace is equal to

\mathbb{T}=-2X^{2}\,\mathcal{L}_{{\rm m},XX}+4X\mathcal{L}_{{\rm m},X}-4\mathcal{L}_{\rm m}\,.

(21)

The equation of motion for the scalar field multiplet in
R+F(T)
gravity is given by:

\mathscr{L}_{{\rm m},\phi^{a}}+\mathscr{L}_{{\rm m},X}\Box\phi^{a}+\mathscr{L}_{{\rm m},XX}\nabla_{\mu}\phi^{a}\nabla^{\mu}X=0\,,

(22)

with
\mathscr{L}_{\rm m}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm m}+F(T)
.

III
Global K-monopoles in
R+F(T)
gravity

In this section, we consider K-monopoles as a particle toy model, using them to illustrate various properties of real particles in
R+F(T)
gravity, assuming that the spacetime metric is locally flat and can be approximated as Minkowskian inside the particle.

Consider a real scalar field multiplet
\{\phi^{1},\phi^{2},\phi^{3}\}
, in a
3+1
dimensional spacetime, described by the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}_{\rm m}=K(X)-V(\phi^{a})\,,

(23)

with

V(\phi^{a})=\frac{\lambda}{4}(\phi^{a}\phi^{a}-\eta^{2})^{2}\,,

(24)

Here,
\lambda>0
is a dimensionless real constant, and
\phi^{a}\phi^{a}=\eta^{2}
defines the vacuum manifold as a 2-sphere. For simplicity, we use units where
\eta=1
throughout the paper.

Derrick’s theorem states that scalar field theories with standard kinetic and potential terms do not admit nontrivial static solutions with finite energy in more than one spatial dimension
Hobart 1963
;
Derrick 1964
. Here, we circumvent this limitation by introducing a non-standard kinetic term
K(X)
, which enables the existence of static, finite-energy solutions. For the sake of definiteness, we shall assume that

K(X)=X|X|^{\alpha-1}\,,

(25)

with
\alpha\geq 1
. This particular choice for the kinetic term then implies that

\displaystyle X\mathcal{L}_{{\rm m},X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha K(X)\,,

(26)

\displaystyle X^{2}\mathcal{L}_{{\rm m},XX}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\alpha-1)\mathcal{L}_{{\rm m},X}\,.

(27)

To find maximally symmetric static solutions of global K-monopoles, we use the ansatz:

\phi^{a}=\frac{x^{a}}{r}f(r)\,,

(28)

where
r^{2}=x^{a}x^{a}
, and
f(r)
is a monotonically increasing radial profile satisfying
f(0)=0
and
f(\infty)=1
. Note that Eq. (
28
) implies
\phi^{a}\phi^{a}=f^{2}(r)
, so that the potential can be expressed as
V(\phi^{a})=V(f)=\lambda(f^{2}-\eta^{2})^{2}/4
. Consequently, the minima of
V
occur at
f=1
.

The field equation given in Eq. (
22
), for a static spherically symmetric K-monopole then becomes

\left[\frac{1+4F_{,T}}{\alpha|X|^{\alpha-1}}+4(4-2\alpha)F_{,TT}f^{\prime 2}\right]\frac{dV}{df}-\alpha(4-2\alpha)^{2}F_{,TT}|X|^{\alpha-1}X^{\prime}f^{\prime}=(1+F_{,T}(4-2\alpha))\left[\frac{\alpha-1}{X}X^{\prime}f^{\prime}+f^{\prime\prime}+\frac{2}{r}f^{\prime}-\frac{2}{r^{2}}f\right]\,,

(29)

where now

\displaystyle X\equiv X(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left(f^{\prime 2}+\frac{2}{r^{2}}f^{2}\right)\,,

(30)

\displaystyle X^{\prime}\equiv X^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-f^{\prime}f^{\prime\prime}-2\frac{ff^{\prime}r^{2}-rf^{2}}{r^{4}}\,

(31)

\displaystyle\frac{dV}{df}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda f(f^{2}-1)\,,

(32)

and a prime denotes differentiation with respect to
r
.

Equations. (
11
), (
12
), (
15
), and (
20
) imply that the proper energy density
\rho
and proper modified energy density
\varrho
of a static global monopole in a Minkowski spacetime are given by

\displaystyle\rho(r)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-{T^{0}}_{0}={\mathbb{T}^{0}}_{0}=-\mathcal{L}_{\rm m}\,,

(33)

\displaystyle\varrho(r)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-{\mathscr{T}^{0}}_{0}=-\mathscr{L}_{\rm m}=\rho-F\,.

(34)

On the other hand, using also Eqs. (
13
), (
16
), (
21
), and (
27
) one can show that the proper pressure,
p
, and proper modified pressure
\mathscr{P}
are given by

\displaystyle p(r)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{T{{}^{i}}_{i}}{3}=\frac{T-T{{}^{0}}_{0}}{3}=-\frac{2}{3}X\mathcal{L}_{{\rm m},X}+\mathcal{L}_{\rm m}\,,

(35)

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathscr{T}^{i}}_{i}}{3}=\frac{\mathscr{T}-{\mathscr{T}^{0}}_{0}}{3}

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p+F-2F_{,T}(\alpha-2)(\rho+p)\,.

The modified proper mass (or energy) of the monopole
\mathscr{M}
is then

\mathscr{M}=4\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\varrho r^{2}dr=m-4\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}F(T)r^{2}dr\,,

(37)

where
m
is the proper mass defined by

m=4\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho r^{2}dr\,.

(38)

To construct specific numerical solutions for
f
, we implement a shooting method in the near-core region, while adopting analytical approximations for
f
in the asymptotic (tail) region of the K-monopole, following a procedure similar to that described in
Pinto and Avelino 2025
. The existence of K-monopole solutions depends sensitively on both the functional form of
F(T)
and the parameter
\alpha
. In this work, we restrict our analysis to cases where such solutions exist and are well behaved, both the standard and modified proper densities remain positive everywhere in the spatial domain, and both the standard and modified proper masses are finite.

III.2
Matter on-shell Lagrangian

To assess whether the usual on-shell relation between the matter Lagrangian and the trace of the energy–momentum tensor is preserved at large distances from the monopole core, we compare their global spatial averages.

Taking into account that
\mathcal{L}_{\rm m}=-\rho
and
T=-\rho+3p
, one finds

\frac{\langle\mathcal{L}_{\rm m}\rangle_{r}}{\langle T\rangle_{r}}=\frac{\langle\rho\rangle_{r}}{-\langle\rho\rangle_{r}+3\langle p\rangle_{r}}=\frac{1}{1-3\widebar{w}_{r}}\,.

(48)

Since, in general,
\widebar{w}_{\infty}\equiv\langle p\rangle_{\infty}/\langle\rho\rangle_{\infty}\neq 0
, the global spatial averages of
\mathcal{L}_{\rm m}
and
T
do not, in general, coincide.

On the other hand,

\frac{\langle\mathscr{L}_{\rm m}\rangle_{r}}{\langle\mathscr{T}\rangle_{r}}=\frac{-\langle\varrho\rangle_{r}}{-\langle\varrho\rangle_{r}+3\langle\mathscr{P}\rangle_{r}}=\frac{1}{1-3\widebar{\mathcal{W}}_{r}}\,.

(49)

Given that
\widebar{\mathcal{W}}_{\infty}=0
, it follows that

\frac{\langle\mathscr{L}_{\rm m}\rangle_{\infty}}{\langle\mathscr{T}\rangle_{\infty}}=1\,.

(50)

Therefore, the equality
\mathscr{L}_{\rm m}=\mathscr{T}
holds on average within a K-monopole.

IV
Cosmological implications

On cosmological scales, the energy-momentum content of the Universe is typically modeled as a collection of perfect fluids. This description provides an effective characterization of the large-scale dynamics of the Universe by capturing the collective behavior of large particle ensembles. Assuming large-scale homogeneity and isotropy, and restricting to the spatially flat case, the spacetime geometry is described by the flat FLRW metric, with line element

ds^{2}=dt^{2}-d\vec{r}\cdot d\vec{r}=dt^{2}-a^{2}(t)\,d\vec{x}\cdot d\vec{x}\,,

(56)

where
t
denotes physical time,
a(t)
is the scale factor,
\vec{x}
are comoving Cartesian coordinates, and
d\vec{r}=a(t)\,d\vec{x}
.

For an ideal gas of particles, any macroscopic quantity

\widetilde{Q}(t,\vec{x})
is obtained from the underlying particle contributions
Q_{i}
as

\widetilde{Q}(t,\vec{x})=\frac{1}{V_{\rm c}}\int_{V_{\rm c}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Q_{i}(t,\vec{x})\,d^{3}x\,,

(57)

where
V_{\rm c}
is a small comoving volume centered at
\vec{x}
and containing
N
particles. A common but generally unjustified simplification is to replace
\widetilde{F}
with
F(\widetilde{T})
. According to Equation (
57
), macroscopic quantities must be defined as spatial averages of the corresponding microscopic expressions, implying that the correct quantity at the fluid-level is
\widetilde{F}
rather than
F(\widetilde{T})
. In the following, we will show that this distinction is particularly important if
F(T)
has a non-linear dependence on
T
(e.g., if
F(T)=\epsilon|T|^{\beta}
with
\beta\neq 1
), in which case
\widetilde{F}\neq F(\widetilde{T})
in general.

Consider a dust fluid of cold dark matter particles following the Hubble flow, with characteristic interparticle distance
\lambda\equiv{\widetilde{n}}^{-1/3}
, where
\widetilde{n}=\widetilde{\rho}/m=\widetilde{\varrho}/\mathscr{M}
is the particle number density. Assuming that
\lambda
is much larger than the particle radius
r_{*}
defined in the previous section, the macroscopic quantity
\widetilde{Q}
can be approximated as

\widetilde{Q}=\langle Q\rangle_{\lambda}\,.

(58)

Here, the right-hand side is computed by considering a single particle, as in the previous section.

For
F(T)=\epsilon|T|^{\beta}
, the ratio between the averaged quantities
\widetilde{F}
and
F(\widetilde{T})
is

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}\equiv\frac{\widetilde{F}}{F(\widetilde{T})}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\frac{4\pi\epsilon}{\lambda^{3}}\int_{0}^{\lambda}|T|^{\beta}r^{2}\,dr}{\epsilon\left(\frac{4\pi}{\lambda^{3}}\int_{0}^{\lambda}|T|r^{2}\,dr\right)^{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{4\pi}{\lambda^{3}}\right)^{1-\beta}\frac{\int_{0}^{r_{*}}|T_{*}|^{\beta}r^{2}\,dr}{\left(\int_{0}^{r_{*}}|T_{*}|r^{2}\,dr\right)^{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{4\pi r_{*}^{3}}{3\lambda^{3}}\right)^{1-\beta}=\left(\frac{\tilde{r}_{*}}{\lambda}\right)^{3(1-\beta)}\,,

(59)

where
r_{*}
is the particle radius defined in the previous section and
{\tilde{r}}_{*}=(4\pi/3)^{1/3}r_{*}
. Thus,
\mathfrak{f}
generally deviates from unity as
\lambda
increases relative to the particle radius (except when
\beta=1
).

For a dust fluid in a FLRW background, the
00
component of Eq. (
6
) becomes

3\left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{\varrho}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\widetilde{\rho}-\widetilde{F}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\widetilde{\rho}-\mathfrak{f}\,F(\widetilde{T})\Bigr)\,,

(60)

where
\widetilde{T}=-\widetilde{\rho}+3\widetilde{p}
. Importantly, for dust it is the modified proper pressure
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}
of the fluid, rather than its proper pressure
\widetilde{p}
, that is required to vanish. Consequently,
\widetilde{p}
is generally nonzero. This subtlety is often overlooked in the literature, where
\widetilde{p}
is typically assumed to vanish for dust in
f(R,T)
gravity (see, e.g.,
Alvarenga et al. 2013a
;
Alvarenga et al. 2013b
;
Zaregonbadi et al. 2016
;
Velten and Caramês 2017
;
Rudra and Giri 2021
;
Haghani et al. 2024
;
Solanke et al. 2023
).
Furthermore, the time-dependent factor
\mathfrak{f}
can deviate significantly from unity, a feature also neglected in previous studies
Alvarenga et al. 2013b
;
Zaregonbadi et al. 2016
;
Velten and Caramês 2017
;
Rudra and Giri 2021
;
Haghani et al. 2024
.

For the choice
F(T)=\epsilon|T|^{\beta}
, an exact expression for
\mathfrak{f}
can be obtained by noting that

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho}-\widetilde{\varrho}=n\left(m-\mathscr{M}\right)=\widetilde{\rho}\left(1-\frac{\mathscr{M}}{m}\right),

(61)

\displaystyle F(\widetilde{T})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon\,|-1+3\widebar{w}_{\infty}|^{\beta}\,\widetilde{\rho}^{\,\beta}.

(62)

As a result

\mathfrak{f}\equiv\frac{\widetilde{F}}{F(\widetilde{T})}=\epsilon^{-1}\left(1-\frac{\mathscr{M}}{m}\right)|-1+3\widebar{w}_{\infty}|^{-\beta}\widetilde{\rho}^{\,1-\beta}.

(63)

The evolution of
\widetilde{\varrho}=\mathscr{M}\,\tilde{n}
with the scale factor follows from Eq. (
8
), using the modified energy–momentum tensor defined in Eq. (
12
). One then obtains the modified continuity equation

\dot{\widetilde{\varrho}}+3H\left(\widetilde{\varrho}+\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}\right)=0.

(64)

Imposing
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}=0
, consistently with the modified von Laue condition, immediately yields

\widetilde{\varrho}=\mathscr{M}\,\tilde{n}\ \propto\ a^{-3},\qquad\widetilde{\rho}=m\,\tilde{n}\ \propto\ a^{-3},

(65)

where both
\mathscr{M}
and
m
are constants.

Hence, the standard evolution of a universe filled with dust is maintained in
R+F(T)
gravity, independently of the specific form of the function
F(T)
. This is a direct consequence of two key features. The first is a distinctive property of
R+F(T)
gravity, namely its equivalence to general relativity coupled to a modified matter Lagrangian. The second arises from the fact that the corresponding modified energy–momentum tensor satisfies the modified von Laue condition. The conclusion naturally extends to the case of moving particles, indicating that the evolution of the Universe during both the matter- and radiation-dominated eras remains effectively unchanged.

Notice that, although

\widetilde{\varrho}=\widetilde{\rho}-\widetilde{F},

(66)

and the effective pressure satisfies

\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}=\widetilde{p}+\widetilde{F}-2\widetilde{F}_{,T}\left(\widetilde{p}+\frac{1}{3}\widetilde{\mathbb{T}}^{i}_{\ i}\right)=0

(67)

one generally has

\widetilde{F}\neq F(\widetilde{T}),\qquad\widetilde{F}_{,T}\neq F_{,T}(\widetilde{T}),\qquad\widetilde{p}\neq 0.

(68)

This analysis emphasizes that consistent volume averaging, together with the use of the modified von Laue condition, is essential to recover the true cosmological evolution. Incorrectly identifying
\widetilde{F}
with
F(\widetilde{T})
or assuming
\tilde{p}=0
can produce spurious deviations from the standard dust-like behavior, potentially leading to misleading conclusions regarding the expansion history and the evolution of the energy density in this class of models.

Although the discussion above focuses on pressureless dust in
R+F(T)
gravity, its main qualitative conclusions hold more generally for other fluids and nonminimally coupled gravity theories. In particular, for
f(R,\mathcal{L}_{\rm m},T)
theories with nonlinear terms in
\mathcal{L}_{\rm m}
or
T
, or any theory where cosmological equations include nonlinear contributions of the averaged fluid energy density, improper averaging can similarly produce misleading cosmological predictions.

V
Conclusions

We investigated the cosmological averaging problem in theories of gravity with nonminimal couplings between matter and geometry, focusing on its impact on the large-scale evolution inferred from homogeneous cosmological models. We have shown that while averaging issues are present even in general relativity, they become particularly significant in theories with nonlinear matter–gravity interactions, where standard homogenization procedures need not preserve the correct macroscopic dynamics.

Using
R+F(T)
gravity as a well controlled minimal framework and K-monopoles as a particle proxy, we explored the challenges associated with cosmological averaging for a fluid composed of dust, clarifying several common misconceptions in the literature. In particular, we have shown that homogeneous cosmological models constructed under the assumption that
F(T)
can be applied directly to the global spatial average of
T
may fail to reproduce the correct large-scale dynamics of an inhomogeneous Universe. We have further demonstrated that, contrary to general relativity, dust is generally not pressureless in these theories.

Although our discussion has focused on the specific case of
R+F(T)
gravity, the underlying analysis is not restricted to this particular model. Rather, they apply more generally to theories of gravity featuring nonminimal couplings between matter and geometry, provided the standard assumption, widely adopted in a cosmological context, that spacetime can be well approximated by an FLRW metric on all relevant scales is maintained. Whether this assumption remains valid in such theories is ultimately a question for a more complete theoretical framework, particularly in light of the instabilities that have been reported in significant parts of the parameter space of nonminimally coupled gravity. Should these issues be resolved, the averaging problem investigated in this work would persist and must be addressed consistently.

In addition, we have deliberately not incorporated the stringent experimental constraints at redshift zero that severely restrict the functional form of
F(T)
. Our aim has not been to assess the phenomenological viability of these theories, but rather to use
R+F(T)
gravity as a controlled setting to investigate the robustness of homogenization procedures themselves, independently of the current experimental and observational limits.

Finally, an important ingredient that deserves further investigation is the role of geometrical backreaction. Although its effects within general relativity are commonly assumed to be small, this assumption does not necessarily extend to modified gravity theories. Indeed, even in the context of
f(R)
gravity, geometrical backreaction has already been shown to be relevant, in particular if a large fraction of dark matter turns out to be black holes
Cano 2024
. A consistent treatment of geometrical backreaction is therefore essential and should be incorporated in future studies of averaging in nonminimally coupled gravity theories.
