Decentralized Multi-product Pricing: Diagonal Dominance, Nash Equilibrium, and Price of Anarchy
Boxiao Chen † \text{Boxiao Chen}^{\dagger}, Jiashuo Jiang ‡ \text{Jiashuo Jiang}^{\ddagger}, Stefanus Jasin § \text{Stefanus Jasin}^{\lx@sectionsign}

Abstract
Decentralized decision making in multi–product firms can lead to efficiency losses when autonomous decision makers fail to internalize cross–product demand interactions. This paper quantifies the magnitude of such losses by analyzing the Price of Anarchy in a pricing game in which each decision maker independently sets prices to maximize its own product–level revenue. We model demand using a linear system that captures both substitution and complementarity effects across products. We first establish existence and uniqueness of a pure–strategy Nash equilibrium under economically standard diagonal dominance conditions. Our main contribution is the derivation of a tight worst–case lower bound on the ratio between decentralized revenue and the optimal centralized revenue. We show that this efficiency loss is governed by a single scalar parameter, denoted by μ \mu , which measures the aggregate strength of cross–price effects relative to own–price sensitivities. In particular, we prove that the revenue ratio is bounded below by 4 ​ ( 1 − μ ) / ( 2 − μ ) 2 4(1-\mu)/(2-\mu)^{2} , and we demonstrate the tightness of this bound by constructing a symmetric market topology in which the bound is exactly attained. We further refine the analysis by providing an instance–exact characterization of efficiency loss based on the spectral properties of the demand interaction matrix. Together, these results offer a quantitative framework for assessing the trade–off between centralized pricing and decentralized autonomy in multi–product firms.

1
Introduction

Pricing strategies form the backbone of modern revenue
management and play a central role in profitability across a wide range
of industries, including retail, e–commerce, logistics, and digital
services.
For firms that manage large portfolios of interconnected products, the
conceptual benchmark for decision making is a

centralized pricing scheme
.
Under this paradigm, a firm with perfect information and unlimited
computational capability jointly optimizes the prices of all
N

products, fully accounting for the network of substitution and
complementarity effects that link demand across the portfolio.
By internalizing these cross–product externalities—such as the fact
that discounting a flagship product may cannibalize sales of a premium
alternative or stimulate demand for a complementary accessory—the
centralized approach is guaranteed, in principle, to maximize total
revenue.

Despite its conceptual appeal, implementing centralized pricing is
often infeasible in practice.
The dimensionality of the joint optimization problem grows rapidly with
the number of products, the statistical task of estimating a complete
matrix of cross–elasticities is demanding, and organizational
constraints frequently prevent unified pricing decisions across
product lines or business units.
As a result, pricing decisions are commonly decentralized, either by
design or by necessity, with individual products or teams setting
prices based on local objectives and information.
Such decentralized decision making generally fails to internalize
cross–product effects and can therefore lead to outcomes that diverge
substantially from the centralized optimum.

In this paper, we investigate the performance of a

decentralized pricing scheme
relative to the centralized
benchmark.
We model decentralized pricing as a noncooperative game in which the
price of each product is chosen to maximize its own revenue, taking the
prices of all other products as given.
Our analysis focuses on the resulting Nash equilibrium and on
quantifying the efficiency loss relative to the centralized optimum—a
metric commonly referred to in the literature as the

Price of Anarchy
(PoA).

Our emphasis on decentralized pricing and its associated Nash
equilibrium is motivated by two salient features of modern commerce:

1.

Multi–agent competition.

Many contemporary marketplaces are inherently fragmented.
In settings such as online retail platforms or decentralized supply
chains, products are frequently sold by independent agents,
third–party sellers, or distinct business units within a larger
organization.
These agents act strategically to maximize the revenue of their own
products rather than the aggregate revenue of the platform or firm.
Moreover, each agent typically observes only its own sales and
pricing data, and has limited or no access to information about
cross–product demand interactions.
Such informational and incentive structures naturally lead to a
strategic environment in which prices are determined by a Nash
equilibrium.

2.

Algorithmic complexity and learning.

Even when a single firm controls all
N
products, fully centralized
pricing requires accurate knowledge of the entire demand system,
including the
N\times N
matrix of cross–price effects.
Estimating these interactions and solving the resulting joint
optimization problem is often computationally prohibitive or
statistically challenging, particularly in high–dimensional
settings with limited data.
As a result, firms commonly deploy simplified or modular learning
algorithms—such as independent bandit algorithms or product–level
demand learning modules—that effectively treat products as
independent or only weakly coupled.
Existing results (e.g.,
64
,
71
)
indicate that when such independent learning dynamics are applied in
environments with demand interactions, the induced prices converge
to the Nash equilibrium of the underlying pricing game rather than
to the centralized optimum.

Given these practical considerations, it is essential to understand the
magnitude of the revenue loss induced by decentralized pricing.
Although the Price of Anarchy has been studied extensively in other
settings—most notably in transportation networks (e.g.,

68
) and routing games (e.g.,

18
)—its application to multi–product pricing
problems has received comparatively little attention.
This gap motivates the central question of our study:

How much revenue is lost due to decentralized pricing, relative
to the centralized optimum, in a multi–product environment with
internally dependent demand?

To address this question, we consider the setting with a linear demand model
under economically standard structural assumptions that ensure
well–posedness.
Within this framework, we characterize both the centralized optimal
prices and the decentralized Nash equilibrium in closed form, and we
quantify the resulting efficiency loss using the Price of Anarchy
(PoA), defined as the ratio of revenue achieved under decentralization
to that under centralized optimization.
To the best of our knowledge, this paper provides the first
comprehensive analysis of the Price of Anarchy for linear demand
systems with cross–product effects.

1.1
Our Main Results and Contributions

We consider a linear demand model in which the demand for each
product depends linearly on the prices of all products in the
portfolio.
Within this framework, we rigorously analyze the ratio between the
aggregate revenue generated at the Nash equilibrium of the decentralized
pricing game and the maximum revenue achievable under joint
optimization by a centralized decision maker.
Our analysis yields the following main contributions.

Existence, uniqueness, and closed–form characterization.

We first establish the basic structural properties of the decentralized
pricing game.
Under economically standard assumptions—specifically, that the demand
sensitivity matrix is symmetric and strictly diagonally dominant—we
prove that the game admits a unique pure–strategy Nash equilibrium.
Beyond existence and uniqueness, we derive explicit closed–form
expressions for the equilibrium price vectors under both centralized
and decentralized pricing.
These characterizations allow us to explicitly isolate the distortion
introduced by decentralization.
In particular, we show that while the centralized planner sets prices
by inverting the full demand matrix and thus fully internalizes all
cross–product effects, decentralized agents effectively operate on a
modified matrix that downweights off–diagonal interactions, leading to
a systematic misalignment of incentives across products.

Worst–case efficiency bounds via diagonal dominance.

A central challenge in Price of Anarchy analysis is to obtain bounds
that are robust across a wide range of market structures.
We address this challenge by deriving a universal lower bound on the
Price of Anarchy that depends on a single scalar parameter

\mu\in[0,1)
.
This parameter quantifies the degree of diagonal dominance of the demand
matrix and can be interpreted as the aggregate strength of cross–price
effects—whether substitution or complementarity—relative to own–price
sensitivities.
We prove that for any market satisfying this dominance condition, the
ratio of decentralized to centralized revenue is bounded below as follows:

\text{PoA}\;\geq\;\frac{4(1-\mu)}{(2-\mu)^{2}}.

This bound reveals that efficiency loss is not arbitrary but is
structurally governed by the intensity of product interdependence.
When products are highly differentiated and cross–effects are weak
(
\mu\approx 0
), the bound approaches one, implying that decentralized
pricing is nearly optimal.
As products become closer substitutes or stronger complements
(
\mu\to 1
), the bound deteriorates, highlighting the increasing value
of coordination in highly coupled markets.

Tightness of the bound.

We further examine whether the derived bound is merely conservative or
whether it represents a fundamental limit.
We show that the bound is tight by constructing an explicit worst–case
market structure.
In particular, we analyze a fully symmetric, exchangeable model in
which each product interacts with every other product with equal
intensity.
In this setting, we show that the realized efficiency loss converges
exactly to the proposed lower bound.
This establishes that no sharper bound depending solely on the diagonal
dominance parameter
\mu
can be obtained, and that our result captures
the intrinsic limit of efficiency for this class of demand systems.

Exact spectral characterization of the Price of Anarchy.

Finally, we observe that the scalar parameter
\mu
reflects a
worst–case aggregation of cross–product effects and may therefore be
overly conservative in sparse or heterogeneous interaction networks.
To address this limitation, we develop an exact spectral
characterization of the Price of Anarchy.
We show that the efficiency loss is determined precisely by the
spectral properties of a normalized interaction matrix, and in
particular by the largest absolute value of its eigenvalues.
This characterization yields instance–specific efficiency guarantees
that explicitly account for the topology of the product interaction
network.
As a result, it can substantially improve upon the worst–case bound in
settings such as hub–and–spoke or other heterogeneous market
structures.

Taken together, our results provide a sharp and fully analytical
characterization of efficiency loss under decentralized pricing in
multi–product demand systems.
They bridge the pricing literature with broader work on efficiency loss
in games and decentralized optimization, and they offer concrete
insights into when decentralized pricing is nearly optimal and when
centralized coordination is essential.

1.2
Further Related Work

We now provide an overview of additional related literature.
Broadly speaking, our work is related to three streams of the literature:
(i) the literature on multi–product pricing problems,
(ii) the literature on Price of Anarchy,
and (iii) the literature on Nash equilibrium computation and convergence
analysis.

Multi-product pricing
.
Multi-product pricing is a fundamental problem in operations research
and operations management, especially in dynamic and data-driven
environments. It has been extensively studied within the framework
of Network Revenue Management (NRM). Since the early works
(e.g.,
29
,
63
,
30
), significant effort has been devoted to
improving regret guarantees, progressing from
O(\sqrt{T})
to
O(1)
.
For example,
58
shows that re-solving
the ex-ante relaxation once yields an improved regret bound of

o(\sqrt{T})
. Subsequently,
38
and

39
demonstrate that, under a
non-degeneracy condition, a resolving policy achieves
O(1)
regret.
More recently,
10
proposes an infrequent
re-solving policy and establishes an
O(1)
regret bound without
the non-degeneracy assumption. Using a different approach,

65
proves the same
O(1)
upper bound for
the NRM problem. A variety of extensions have been studied using
different methodologies (e.g.,
40
,
5
,
2
). Another formulation of multi-product pricing incorporates inventory
replenishment decisions (e.g.,
11
,
13
,
15
,
14
). Finally,
bandit-based formulations have also been widely adopted to study
multi-product pricing, leading to a range of approaches under
different settings (e.g.,
6
,
47
,
7
,
17
,
9
,
66
).

Despite this extensive literature, most existing work—including all
the studies cited above—focuses on centralized pricing, assuming a
single decision maker sets prices for all products. In contrast,
our paper studies the performance of a decentralized pricing scheme
in which each product optimizes its own price.

Price of Anarchy analysis
.
The concept of the Price of Anarchy (PoA), introduced by

48
, quantifies the efficiency loss arising
from selfish behavior in decentralized systems. Establishing
worst-case bounds on the PoA under various conditions has been a
central theme since the seminal work of
54
,
which showed that for networks with affine latency functions, the
PoA is bounded by
3/4
. These results were subsequently extended in
a series of works

59
,
19
,
20
,
56
,
21
. A comprehensive overview can
be found in the survey by
60
. The PoA has also been extensively studied in the context of supply
chain competition. Unlike routing games, where agents minimize
latency, supply chain agents (e.g., retailers and suppliers)
typically maximize profit, often giving rise to “tragedy of the
commons” phenomena.
55
quantified the efficiency
loss of decentralized supply chains operating under price-only
contracts, with further analysis in
25
.

36
studied the PoA of inventory replenishment
policies. Another stream of work applies PoA analysis to service
operations and queueing systems, where customers decide whether to
join a queue or which server to select based on individual incentives.
Various results have been obtained across different settings
(e.g.,
35
,
34
,
28
);
see also the early monograph by
33
. PoA has
further been studied in pricing and auction settings
(e.g.,
42
,
43
,
16
,
4
).

In contrast to this literature, we study a multi-product pricing
setting in which demand depends on the prices of all products. Our
technical approach is also novel: the analysis relies on a careful
matrix characterization of the resulting equilibrium, and shows
that the PoA can be bounded through spectral analysis of the
associated matrices.

Equilibrium computation and convergence
.
Our problem is related to the broad literature on learning in games,
ranging from query complexity in static environments to convergence
in repeated interactions. A major line of work studies the sample
complexity of computing Nash equilibria. In settings with deterministic
observations,
51
proposed an algorithm that
identifies all Nash equilibria by querying only a limited portion of
the game matrix; this result was further improved by

23
and
22
. Under noisy
observations,
70
employed a
lower-upper-confidence-bound (LUCB) approach

(
45
)
to identify pure-strategy Nash equilibria
with finite-sample guarantees, which were later sharpened by

52
. For mixed equilibria,
53

established high-probability bounds, while
41
derived
instance-dependent bounds in expectation using a novel LP-based
approach.

Complementary to these query-complexity results is the extensive
literature on computing Nash equilibria via regret minimization in
repeated games.
27
established the classical

O(1/\sqrt{T})
convergence rate. To overcome this slow rate, subsequent
works
(
24
,
57
,
62
,
69
,
12
)
developed techniques to
accelerate either average-iterate or last-iterate convergence. These
learning guarantees have since been extended beyond normal-form games
to richer settings, including convex–concave games

(
1
)
, strongly monotone games

(
3
,
44
)
, extensive-form games

(
26
,
49
)
, and multi-agent reinforcement
learning. Finally, learning-in-games has also been studied in pricing
contexts (e.g.,
4
,
31
,
67
,
50
,
8
), with a variety of
approaches offering convergence guarantees.

In contrast, our paper does not focus on designing learning algorithms
for games. Instead, we study the equilibrium outcome to which such
algorithms converge, and evaluate its performance relative to the
globally optimal solution of the pricing problem.

2
Problem Formulation and the Centralized Pricing Scheme

In this section, we discuss the model, the assumptions, and the centralized pricing scheme.

2.1
The Model

We consider a firm that sells
N
products by choosing prices to maximize the reward. To be specific, we denote by
p_{i}
the price chosen by the firm, for every product

i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.
Let

\bm{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{N})

denote the vector of prices. The realized demand for product
i
is given as

D_{i}(\bm{p})=F_{i}(\bm{p})+\epsilon_{i},

where
F_{i}(\bm{p})
is the expected demand as a function
of the entire price vector and
\epsilon_{i}
is a
zero–mean random shock.
The noise terms
\epsilon_{i}
are assumed to be
independent between products. In this paper, we assume that the expected demand function is linear in prices.

{assumption}

[Linear demand system]

For each product
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, the expected demand
function
F_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

F_{i}(\bm{p})=a_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}b_{ij}p_{j},

(1)

where the intercept
a_{i}
represents the baseline demand of product
i

when all prices are zero, and the slope
b_{ij}
is the

price sensitivity of the demand for product
i

with respect to the price of product
j
.
In particular,
b_{ii}
captures the own–price effect,
while
b_{ij}
for
j\neq i
captures cross–price effects.

Let
\bm{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N})^{\top}
denote the vector of intercepts, or
baseline demands, and let

\bm{B}=(b_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{N}
denote the

price sensitivity matrix
.
Define the vector–valued demand function

\bm{F}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
by

\bm{F}(\bm{p})=\bigl(F_{1}(\bm{p}),\ldots,F_{N}(\bm{p})\bigr)^{\top}.

Then, the expected demand formulation in (
1
) can be written compactly as

\bm{F}(\bm{p})=\bm{a}+\bm{B}\bm{p}.

The instantaneous revenue obtained at price vector

\bm{p}
is

R(\bm{p})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}F_{i}(\bm{p})
.
This revenue admits the
quadratic representation

R(\bm{p})=\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{p}+\bm{p}^{\top}\bm{B}\bm{p}.

Because
\epsilon_{i}
has zero mean and is
independent of
\bm{p}
,
the firm’s problem is to choose the price
\bm{p}
to maximize the expected total revenue
R(\bm{p})
. We study the design of pricing policies that maximize this total expected revenue.

2.2
Additional Assumptions

Motivated by economic and empirical considerations,
we impose several structural assumptions on the
price sensitivity matrix
\bm{B}
.
These assumptions ensure that the revenue maximization
problem
\max_{\bm{p}}R(\bm{p})
admits a unique optimizer
and that the resulting analysis is economically
meaningful and mathematically tractable.

We start with our first assumption below.

{assumption}

[Symmetry of the price sensitivity matrix]

The matrix
\bm{B}
is symmetric, that is,

b_{ij}=b_{ji}\text{ for all }i,j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}.

Assumption
2.2
states that cross–price
effects are symmetric: the effect of product
j
’s price
on the demand for product
i
equals the effect of
product
i
’s price on the demand for product
j
.
Equivalently,

\frac{\partial F_{i}(\bm{p})}{\partial p_{j}}=b_{ij}=b_{ji}=\frac{\partial F_{j}(\bm{p})}{\partial p_{i}}.

This symmetry condition arises naturally when demand is derived from consumer utility maximization. In particular, linear demand systems obtained from quadratic utility feature symmetric cross–price effects, since they correspond to the Hessian of the utility (or surplus) function (see for example
61
).
From a mathematical perspective, symmetry implies that

\bm{B}
has real eigenvalues and an orthogonal
eigenbasis, which will play a key role in our
subsequent spectral analysis.

We further impose the following assumption.

{assumption}

[Negative own–price effects]

For each product
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
,

b_{ii}<0.

Assumption
2.2
states that the demand
for each product is strictly decreasing in its own
price, holding all other prices fixed.
Equivalently,

\frac{\partial F_{i}(\bm{p})}{\partial p_{i}}=b_{ii}<0,

which is the standard monotonicity condition in
revenue management and demand modeling.

Before introducing further restrictions on
\bm{B}
,
it is useful to interpret the economic meaning of
cross–price effects.
For distinct products
i\neq j
, the sign of
b_{ij}

determines whether the two products are substitutes
or complements:

•

If
b_{ij}>0
, an increase in the price of product
j

raises the demand for product
i
, so the two products
are substitutes.

•

If
b_{ij}<0
, an increase in the price of product
j

reduces the demand for product
i
, so the two products
are complements.

•

If
b_{ij}=0
, the demand for product
i
is
independent of the price of product
j
.

Under Assumption
2.2
, the
substitute and complement relationships are symmetric
across product pairs, e.g., if
i
is a substitute for
j
, then
j
is also a substitute for
i
.

Our next condition bounds the magnitude of
cross–price effects relative to own–price effects.

{assumption}

[Strict diagonal dominance]

There exists a scalar
\mu\in[0,1)
such that, for each

i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, we have

\sum_{j\neq i}|b_{ij}|\leq\mu\,|b_{ii}|.

(2)

Assumption
2.2
requires that, for every
product
i
, the magnitude of its own–price effect

|b_{ii}|
strictly dominates the total magnitude of all
cross–price effects

\sum_{j\neq i}|b_{ij}|
.
The parameter
\mu\in[0,1)
quantifies the overall strength
of cross–product interactions.
When
\mu=0
, all cross–price effects vanish and products
are independent.
When
\mu
is close to
1
, cross–price effects are nearly
as strong as own–price effects, corresponding to a highly
interconnected market.

From a matrix perspective, condition
(
2
) implies that the matrix
-\bm{B}
is

strictly diagonally dominant
with positive diagonal
entries.
Recall that a matrix
\mathbf{M}=(m_{ij})
is said to be
strictly diagonally dominant if

|m_{ii}|>\sum_{j\neq i}|m_{ij}|\text{ for all }i.

Since
b_{ii}<0
by Assumption
2.2
, we
have

(-\bm{B})_{ii}=-b_{ii}=|b_{ii}|>0.

Moreover, because
\mu<1
, the inequality (
2
) implies

|b_{ii}|>\mu|b_{ii}|\geq\sum_{j\neq i}|b_{ij}|=\sum_{j\neq i}|(-\bm{B})_{ij}|,

which is exactly the strict diagonal dominance condition
for the matrix
-\bm{B}
.

Remark 2.1

(Role of
\mu
in the analysis)

The parameter
\mu
is the central quantity governing the
strength of cross–product interactions in the model.
All of our subsequent results—including the negative
definiteness of the demand matrix
\bm{B}
, the existence and
uniqueness of the Nash equilibrium, and the bounds on the
price of anarchy—are driven by the diagonal dominance
condition parameterized by
\mu
.
In particular, the price–of–anarchy bounds derived in
later sections will be stated explicitly as functions of

\mu
, making transparent how increasing interaction
strength degrades system–level performance.

We now state a fundamental implication of our structural
assumptions: the matrix
\bm{B}
is
negative definite, which in turn implies that the revenue
function
R(\bm{p})
is strongly concave.

Lemma 2.2

(Negative definiteness of
\bm{B}
)

Under Assumptions
2.2
,

2.2
, and
2.2
, the
matrix
\bm{B}
is symmetric and negative definite, and all
of its eigenvalues are strictly negative.

By Lemma
2.2
, the matrix
\bm{B}
is invertible
and its inverse
\bm{B}^{-1}
is also symmetric and negative
definite.
This property will be used repeatedly in the analysis
that follows.

2.3
The Centralized Pricing Scheme

We now discuss the centralized pricing scheme, which
chooses all prices jointly to maximize total revenue.
This provides a natural benchmark against which the
performance of alternative pricing schemes will be
evaluated.

The centralized pricing problem is the unconstrained
optimization problem

R^{*}=\max_{\bm{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}R(\bm{p}).

(3)

By Lemma
2.2
,
R(\bm{p})
is a
concave function, and standard optimization
methods can be used to achieve the optimal value
R^{*}
.
For this reason, we use
R^{*}
as the benchmark revenue
associated with centralized pricing.

The centralized optimizer and the associated maximum
revenue admit closed–form expressions.

Lemma 2.3

Under Assumptions
2.2
,

2.2
, and
2.2
, problem (
3
) has a
unique maximizer given by

\bm{p}^{*}=-\frac{1}{2}\bm{B}^{-1}\bm{a}.

The corresponding optimal revenue is

R(\bm{p}^{*})=-\frac{1}{4}\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{B}^{-1}\bm{a}.

Whenever
\bm{a}\neq\mathbf{0}
, the optimal revenue

R(\bm{p}^{*})
is strictly positive.
Indeed, since
\bm{B}^{-1}
is symmetric and negative
definite, we have

\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{B}^{-1}\bm{a}<0\quad\text{for all }\bm{a}\neq\mathbf{0}.

It follows that

R(\bm{p}^{*})=-\frac{1}{4}\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{B}^{-1}\bm{a}>0,

so the firm achieves strictly positive revenue whenever
baseline demand is nonzero.

3
A Decentralized Pricing Scheme

We now consider a decentralized scheme. Per our discussion in
Section

1
, we focus on the decentralized pricing scheme determined by the Nash equilibrium of the following pricing game.

Definition 3.1

(Decentralized pricing game)

The
decentralized pricing game
is a simultaneous–move game defined as follows.

•

Players.

There are
N
players, indexed by

i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, where player
i
controls the price
of product
i
.

•

Actions.

Each player
i
chooses a price
p_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
.

•

Payoffs.

Given a price vector

\bm{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{N})^{\top}
, the payoff of player
i
is
the revenue generated by product
i
,

u_{i}(\bm{p})=p_{i}F_{i}(\bm{p})=p_{i}\!\left(a_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}b_{ij}p_{j}\right).

In the pricing game defined in
Definition
3.1
, each player maximizes
only the revenue of its own product rather than the
firm’s total revenue.
As a result, the optimal price of any given product
depends on the prices chosen by all other products, and
these interdependencies create strategic tension across
players.
The Nash equilibrium concept formalizes a situation in
which no product can increase its own revenue by
unilaterally changing its price.

Definition 3.2

(Pure–strategy Nash equilibrium)

A price vector

\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}}=(p_{1}^{\mathrm{NE}},\ldots,p_{N}^{\mathrm{NE}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

is a
pure–strategy Nash equilibrium
of the
decentralized pricing game if, for every

i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and every alternative price

p_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
,

u_{i}(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}})\geq u_{i}(p_{i}^{\prime},\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}}_{-i}),

where

\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}}_{-i}=(p_{1}^{\mathrm{NE}},\ldots,p_{i-1}^{\mathrm{NE}},p_{i+1}^{\mathrm{NE}},\ldots,p_{N}^{\mathrm{NE}})^{\top}

denotes the vector of equilibrium prices of all players
other than
i
.
The notation
(p_{i}^{\prime},\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}}_{-i})
refers to the
price vector obtained by replacing the
i
th component
of
\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}}
with
p_{i}^{\prime}
while keeping all other
components fixed.

In other words, at a Nash equilibrium each player’s price
is a best response to the prices chosen by all other
players, and no player can increase its own revenue by
unilaterally deviating from its equilibrium price.

We now establish the existence of a Nash equilibrium for
the decentralized pricing game defined in
Definition
3.1
.
To this end, we first study the optimization problem
faced by a single player when the prices of all other
products are held fixed.

Fix a player
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and suppose that the
prices chosen by all other players are given by

\bm{p}_{-i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
.
Player
i
then chooses a price
p_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
to maximize
its own payoff, which we denote by

u_{i}(p_{i};\bm{p}_{-i})
to emphasize that
p_{i}
is the
decision variable and
\bm{p}_{-i}
is treated as fixed. It is easy to see that the payoff function of player
i
can be written as

u_{i}(p_{i};\bm{p}_{-i})=a_{i}p_{i}+b_{ii}p_{i}^{2}+p_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}b_{ij}p_{j},

(4)

which is a quadratic function of
p_{i}
.
Since we assume
b_{ii}<0
, the function

p_{i}\mapsto u_{i}(p_{i};\bm{p}_{-i})
is strictly concave in
p_{i}
and admits a
unique maximizer
p_{i}^{*}(\bm{p}_{-i})
, which is the

best response
of player
i
to the price vector

\bm{p}_{-i}
. The best response satisfies the first–order condition

\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial p_{i}}\bigl(p_{i}^{*}(\bm{p}_{-i});\bm{p}_{-i}\bigr)=0.

(5)

Solving (
5
) using
(
4
) yields

p_{i}^{*}(\bm{p}_{-i})=\frac{-a_{i}-\sum_{j\neq i}b_{ij}p_{j}}{2b_{ii}}.

(6)

Thus, the best response of each player is an affine
function of the prices chosen by the other players, a
direct consequence of the linear demand system.

At a Nash equilibrium, every player must be playing a
best response to the prices of all other players.
This yields a system of linear equations characterizing
equilibrium prices.

Proposition 3.3

(First–order conditions at a Nash equilibrium)

A price vector
\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a Nash
equilibrium of the decentralized pricing game if and
only if, for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
,

a_{i}+2b_{ii}p_{i}^{\mathrm{NE}}+\sum_{j\neq i}b_{ij}p_{j}^{\mathrm{NE}}=0.

(7)

It is convenient to write the system of first–order
conditions (
7
) in matrix form.

Definition 3.4

(Nash equilibrium matrix)

Define the matrix
\bm{A}^{\mathrm{NE}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
by

A^{\mathrm{NE}}_{ij}=\begin{cases}2b_{ii},&\text{if }i=j,\\[2.0pt]
b_{ij},&\text{if }i\neq j.\end{cases}

Equivalently,
\bm{A}^{\mathrm{NE}}
is obtained from
\bm{B}

by doubling its diagonal entries,

\bm{A}^{\mathrm{NE}}=\bm{B}+\mathrm{diag}(b_{11},\ldots,b_{NN}),

where
\mathrm{diag}(b_{11},\ldots,b_{NN})
denotes the
diagonal matrix with
(i,i)
–entry
b_{ii}
.
Since
\bm{B}
is symmetric by
Assumption
2.2
, the matrix

\bm{A}^{\mathrm{NE}}
is also symmetric.

Using Definition
3.4
, the system
(
7
) for
i=1,\ldots,N
can be written
compactly as

\bm{A}^{\mathrm{NE}}\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}}+\bm{a}=\mathbf{0},

or, equivalently,

\bm{A}^{\mathrm{NE}}\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}}=-\bm{a}.

(8)

To ensure that this linear system admits a unique
solution, we require
\bm{A}^{\mathrm{NE}}
to be
invertible, which is established next.

Lemma 3.5

Under Assumptions
2.2
,

2.2
, and
2.2
, the
matrix
\bm{A}^{\mathrm{NE}}
is symmetric and negative
definite; hence it is invertible.

We are now ready to state the main result of this section.

Theorem 3.6

(Existence, uniqueness, and characterization
of the Nash equilibrium)

Under Assumptions
2.2
,

2.2
, and
2.2
, the
decentralized pricing game admits a unique pure–strategy
Nash equilibrium

\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Moreover, this equilibrium is given by

\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}}=-(\bm{A}^{\mathrm{NE}})^{-1}\bm{a}.

Combining
Lemma

2.3
and

Theorem

3.6
, we obtain explicit
expressions for the centralized and decentralized price
vectors:

\bm{p}^{*}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{B}^{-1}\bm{a},\qquad\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}}=-(\bm{A}^{\mathrm{NE}})^{-1}\bm{a}.

Both price vectors depend linearly on the intercept
vector
\bm{a}
, but they are generated by different
matrices.
In general,
\bm{p}^{*}\neq\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}}
, reflecting the
inefficiency induced by decentralized pricing.
The next sections quantify this inefficiency through the
price of anarchy.

Remark 3.7

The existence and uniqueness of the Nash equilibrium established in

Theorem

3.6
is important not only from a static
equilibrium perspective, but also from an algorithmic one.
In practice, a decentralized pricing scheme is often implemented
through adaptive or learning-based procedures, in which each player
updates its price using only local payoff information.
A natural approach is for each player to employ a no–regret online
learning algorithm to maximize its own revenue.
Because each payoff function
u_{i}
is strictly concave in the player’s
own price, the resulting decentralized pricing game is a strongly
monotone game. It is therefore well known that when players independently apply
algorithms such as online gradient descent, the induced joint action
sequence converges to the unique Nash equilibrium, both in the
time–averaged sense and in the last–iterate sense (e.g.,

64
,
71
).
Thus, the Nash equilibrium characterized in

Theorem

3.6
naturally emerges as the convergence
point of decentralized learning dynamics.

4
Price of Anarchy

In this section, we compare the centralized optimal
revenue
R(\bm{p}^{*})
with the revenue attained at the
decentralized Nash equilibrium
R(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}})
.

4.1
Mathematical Expression of Price of Anarchy

We start by noting that
R(\bm{p}^{*})
and
R(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}})
can be written as quadratic forms in
the intercept vector
\bm{a}
.
This representation allows us to define and analyze the

price of anarchy
, which measures the worst–case
ratio between centralized and decentralized revenues
over all admissible instances.

Recall from Theorem
2.3
that the
centralized optimal prices and revenue are

\bm{p}^{*}=-\frac{1}{2}\bm{B}^{-1}\bm{a},\qquad R(\bm{p}^{*})=-\frac{1}{4}\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{B}^{-1}\bm{a}.

From Theorem
3.6
, the Nash
equilibrium prices are

\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}}=-(\bm{A}^{\mathrm{NE}})^{-1}\bm{a}.

The corresponding equilibrium revenue

R(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}})
admits an analogous quadratic
representation.
Whereas
R(\bm{p}^{*})
is proportional to

\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{B}^{-1}\bm{a}
, we will show that

R(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}})
can be written as

\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{K}\bm{a}
for a suitable matrix
\bm{K}
, which we
introduce next.

Definition 4.1

(Nash revenue matrix)

Define the matrix
\bm{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
by

\bm{K}:=4\!\left((\bm{A}^{\mathrm{NE}})^{-1}-(\bm{A}^{\mathrm{NE}})^{-1}\bm{B}(\bm{A}^{\mathrm{NE}})^{-1}\right).

Note that the normalization factor of
4
is chosen so that the
Nash equilibrium revenue admits the representation

R(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}})=-\frac{1}{4}\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{K}\bm{a},

which mirrors the centralized revenue formula

R(\bm{p}^{*})=-\frac{1}{4}\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{B}^{-1}\bm{a}.

This parallel form will simplify the subsequent
price–of–anarchy analysis.

We now formalize the notion of efficiency loss induced by
decentralized pricing.

Definition 4.2

(Price of anarchy)

For any nonzero intercept vector
\bm{a}\neq\mathbf{0}
, the

price of anarchy
is defined as

\mathrm{PoA}(\bm{a}):=\frac{R(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}})}{R(\bm{p}^{*})}.

The price of anarchy measures the fraction of the
centralized optimal revenue achieved at the Nash
equilibrium.
When
\mathrm{PoA}(\bm{a})=1
, decentralization is fully
efficient.
When
\mathrm{PoA}(\bm{a})<1
, decentralized pricing leads
to a revenue loss equal to
1-\mathrm{PoA}(\bm{a})
.
Since
\bm{p}^{*}
maximizes revenue, we always have

R(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}})\leq R(\bm{p}^{*})
, and therefore

\mathrm{PoA}(\bm{a})\leq 1
.

Using Theorems
3.6
and
2.3
, the price of anarchy can be
written as a ratio of two quadratic forms in the
intercept vector
\bm{a}
.

Proposition 4.3

(Price of anarchy expression)

For any nonzero
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we have
R(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}})=-\frac{1}{4}\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{K}\bm{a}
, which leads to

\mathrm{PoA}(\bm{a})=\frac{\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{K}\bm{a}}{\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{B}^{-1}\bm{a}}.

(9)

To facilitate our analysis of
\mathrm{PoA}(\bm{a})
, we next show that it can be written as a Rayleigh quotient of a symmetric positive definite matrix, and
its extreme values over all intercept vectors are given
by the eigenvalues of this matrix.

4.2
Spectral Representation of the Price of Anarchy

The matrices
\bm{B}^{-1}
and
\bm{K}
appearing in
(
9
) are associated with negative
definite matrices, since
\bm{B}
is negative definite.
For spectral analysis it is more convenient to work with
positive definite objects, which motivates the
following definitions.

Definition 4.4

(Positive definite revenue matrices)

Define
\tilde{\bm{L}},\tilde{\bm{K}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
by

\tilde{\bm{L}}:=-\bm{B}^{-1},\qquad\tilde{\bm{K}}:=-\bm{K}.

By Lemma
2.2
,
\tilde{\bm{L}}
is symmetric
and positive definite.
Using Proposition
4.3
, we can rewrite
the price of anarchy as

\mathrm{PoA}(\bm{a})=\frac{\bm{a}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{K}}\bm{a}}{\bm{a}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{L}}\bm{a}},\qquad\bm{a}\neq\mathbf{0}.

(10)

The key step is to convert the generalized Rayleigh
quotient in (
10
) into a standard one.
The expression in (
10
) involves two
matrices,
\tilde{\bm{K}}
and
\tilde{\bm{L}}
, and therefore
defines a
generalized Rayleigh quotient
.
To apply the classical spectral characterization, in
which extrema are given by eigenvalues of a single
matrix, the denominator must take the form

\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{x}
.

This is achieved by the change of variables

\mathbf{x}=\tilde{\bm{L}}^{1/2}\bm{a}
.
Under this transformation, the denominator becomes

\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{x}
, while the numerator is
mapped to
\mathbf{x}^{\top}\bm{M}\mathbf{x}
for a suitable
matrix
\bm{M}
.
This reduces the generalized eigenvalue problem to a
standard eigenvalue problem.

Definition 4.5

(Price–of–anarchy matrix)

Define the matrix
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
by

\bm{M}:=\tilde{\bm{L}}^{-1/2}\,\tilde{\bm{K}}\,\tilde{\bm{L}}^{-1/2},

where
\tilde{\bm{L}}^{-1/2}
denotes the unique symmetric
positive definite square root of
\tilde{\bm{L}}^{-1}
.

We briefly justify the existence of the matrix square
root
\tilde{\bm{L}}^{-1/2}
.
For any symmetric positive definite matrix

\mathbf{P}
, there exists a unique symmetric positive
definite matrix
\mathbf{P}^{1/2}
such that

\mathbf{P}^{1/2}\mathbf{P}^{1/2}=\mathbf{P}
.
This matrix is called the
symmetric positive
definite square root
of
\mathbf{P}
.

One way to construct
\mathbf{P}^{1/2}
is through the
eigen–decomposition of
\mathbf{P}
.
If

\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}
,
where
\mathbf{Q}
is orthogonal and

\mathbf{\Lambda}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N})
with
\lambda_{i}>0
, then

\mathbf{P}^{1/2}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{1/2}\mathbf{Q}^{\top},\qquad\mathbf{\Lambda}^{1/2}=\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{\lambda_{N}}).

Since
\tilde{\bm{L}}
is symmetric positive definite by
Lemma
2.2
, its inverse
\tilde{\bm{L}}^{-1}

is also symmetric positive definite.
Therefore the symmetric positive definite square root

\tilde{\bm{L}}^{-1/2}
exists, is unique, and is itself
symmetric.

Before stating our main theorem in this subsection, we introduce several
auxiliary matrices that simplify the spectral analysis.
The key observation is that both
\bm{A}^{\mathrm{NE}}
and

\bm{B}
are symmetric negative definite, so it is natural
to work with their positive definite negatives.

Definition 4.6

(Positive definite demand matrices)

Define

\bm{H}:=-\bm{B},\qquad\bm{G}:=-\bm{A}^{\mathrm{NE}}.

We next introduce an auxiliary matrix that will play a
central role in the spectral representation.

Definition 4.7

(Auxiliary matrix
\bm{Y}
)

Define
\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
by

\bm{Y}:=\bm{H}^{1/2}\bm{G}^{-1}\bm{H}^{1/2},

where
\bm{H}^{1/2}
denotes the symmetric positive definite
square root of
\bm{H}
.

The following lemma establishes the link between the
price–of–anarchy matrix
\bm{M}
and the auxiliary matrix

\bm{Y}
.

Lemma 4.8

(Eigenvalue characterization)

The matrix
\bm{M}
defined in Definition
4.5

is symmetric positive definite and can be written as

\bm{M}=4\,\bm{Y}(\bm{I}-\bm{Y}).

(11)

Moreover, if
\{\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{N}\}
are the
eigenvalues of
\bm{Y}
, then the eigenvalues of
\bm{M}
are

\{\,4\theta_{i}(1-\theta_{i})\,:\,i=1,\ldots,N\}
, where

\theta_{i}\in(0,1)
for all
i
.

We are now ready to state the main result of this
subsection.

Theorem 4.9

(Spectral representation of the price of anarchy)

Under Assumptions
2.2
,

2.2
, and
2.2
, for any
nonzero
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, define

\mathbf{x}:=\tilde{\bm{L}}^{1/2}\bm{a}
.
Then

\mathrm{PoA}(\bm{a})=\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\bm{M}\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}.

Moreover, the best–case and worst–case prices of anarchy
over all nonzero
\bm{a}
satisfy

\inf_{\bm{a}\neq\mathbf{0}}\,\mathrm{PoA}(\bm{a})=\lambda_{\min}(\bm{M}),\qquad\sup_{\bm{a}\neq\mathbf{0}}\,\mathrm{PoA}(\bm{a})=\lambda_{\max}(\bm{M}),

where
\lambda_{\min}(\bm{M})
and
\lambda_{\max}(\bm{M})

denote the smallest and largest eigenvalues of
\bm{M}
,
respectively.

By Lemma
4.8
, all eigenvalues of
\bm{M}
lie
in
(0,1]
, which implies

0<\mathrm{PoA}(\bm{a})\leq 1
for all
\bm{a}\neq\mathbf{0}
.
The upper bound reflects the fact that decentralized
pricing cannot outperform the centralized optimum.
The lower bound will be sharpened in the next subsection,
where we derive an explicit worst–case guarantee on the
efficiency of decentralization.

4.3
A Worst–case Bound

In this subsection, we derive an explicit lower bound for the price of anarchy in terms of the diagonal–dominance parameter
\mu
from
Assumption
2.2
.

Recall from Theorem
4.9
that, for a
fixed demand matrix
\bm{B}
,

\mathrm{PoA}_{\min}(\bm{B}):=\inf_{\bm{a}\neq\mathbf{0}}\,\mathrm{PoA}(\bm{a})=\lambda_{\min}(\bm{M}),

where
\bm{M}
is the symmetric positive definite matrix
defined in Definition
4.5
.
Our goal is to bound
\lambda_{\min}(\bm{M})
from below
using only the parameter
\mu
.

To control the spectrum of
\bm{M}
, we work through the
auxiliary matrix

\bm{Y}=\bm{H}^{1/2}\bm{G}^{-1}\bm{H}^{1/2}
.
This in turn requires a quantitative comparison between

\bm{H}^{-1}
and
\bm{G}^{-1}
.
The following lemma provides such a comparison.

Lemma 4.10

(Comparison of
\bm{H}^{-1}
and
\bm{G}^{-1}
)

Under Assumptions
2.2
,

2.2
, and
2.2
with
diagonal–dominance parameter
\mu\in[0,1)
, define

\alpha(\mu):=\frac{2-\mu}{1-\mu},\qquad\beta(\mu):=\frac{2+\mu}{1+\mu}.

(12)

Then, we have that

\beta(\mu)\,\bm{G}^{-1}\preceq\bm{H}^{-1}\preceq\alpha(\mu)\,\bm{G}^{-1},

where
\preceq
denotes the Loewner order on symmetric
matrices.

It is straightforward to verify that for

\mu\in[0,1)
,

\alpha(0)=\beta(0)=2,\qquad\alpha(\mu)\to+\infty\ \text{and}\ \beta(\mu)\to\frac{3}{2}\ \text{as }\mu\to 1^{-},

and that

1<\beta(\mu)\leq\alpha(\mu)\quad\text{for all }\mu\in[0,1).

As
\mu
increases, the gap between
\alpha(\mu)
and

\beta(\mu)
widens, reflecting the increasing strength
of cross–price interactions.

We now use the comparison between
\bm{H}^{-1}
and

\bm{G}^{-1}
to bound the spectrum of

\bm{Y}=\bm{H}^{1/2}\bm{G}^{-1}\bm{H}^{1/2}
.

Lemma 4.11

(Eigenvalue bounds for
\bm{Y}
)

Under Assumptions
2.2
,

2.2
, and
2.2
with
diagonal–dominance parameter
\mu\in[0,1)
, all
eigenvalues of
\bm{Y}
lie in the interval

\left[\frac{1-\mu}{2-\mu},\,\frac{1+\mu}{2+\mu}\right].

Recall from Lemma
4.8
that the eigenvalues
of
\bm{Y}
lie in
(0,1)
.
Lemma
4.11
provides a sharper
description in terms of
\mu
.
Indeed,

\frac{1-\mu}{2-\mu}>0,\qquad\frac{1+\mu}{2+\mu}<1\quad\text{for all }\mu\in[0,1),

so the bounds are consistent with positivity and
strictly less than one.
At
\mu=0
, the interval collapses to

\{1/2\}
, so all eigenvalues of
\bm{Y}
equal
1/2
.
As
\mu\to 1^{-}
, the interval converges to

[0,\,2/3]
.

We now state the main result of this subsection, which
provides an explicit lower bound on the price
of anarchy in terms of the diagonal–dominance parameter

\mu
.

Theorem 4.12

(Price of anarchy bound under diagonal dominance)

Suppose that Assumptions
2.2
,

2.2
, and
2.2
hold with
diagonal–dominance parameter
\mu\in[0,1)
. Then, the
worst–case price of anarchy satisfies

\mathrm{PoA}_{\min}(\bm{B}):=\inf_{\bm{a}\neq\mathbf{0}}\,\mathrm{PoA}(\bm{a})=\lambda_{\min}(\bm{M})\geq\frac{4(1-\mu)}{(2-\mu)^{2}}.

(13)

The bound in (
13
) has several natural
properties.
When
\mu=0
, it reduces to

4/2^{2}=1
, which implies

\mathrm{PoA}(\bm{a})=1
for all
\bm{a}\neq\mathbf{0}
.
This is consistent with the case of independent
products, where
b_{ij}=0
for
i\neq j
and decentralized
pricing is fully efficient. Define

f(\mu)=4(1-\mu)/(2-\mu)^{2}
. A direct calculation on the derivative of
f
with respect to
\mu
also shows that
f
is concave and is strictly (slowly) decreasing on
[0,1)
. Hence, when
\mu
is not too large, decentralized pricing achieves performance close to that of centralized pricing. Finally, as
\mu\to 1^{-}
, the bound converges to zero, indicating
that when cross–effects are comparable to own–price
effects, decentralized pricing can become arbitrarily
inefficient in the worst case.

Remark 4.13

(Numerical values of the bound)

The values
of the bound
4(1-\mu)/(2-\mu)^{2}
are plotted in
Figure

1
.
For instance, when
\mu
is at most 0.5, the Nash
equilibrium revenue is guaranteed to be at least

88.9\%
of the centralized optimum.

Figure 1:
The values of the bound
4(1-\mu)/(2-\mu)^{2}
for different
\mu
.

5
Tightness Analysis

In this section, we examine the tightness of the
price–of–anarchy bound established in
Theorem
4.12
.
We show that the bound is attained by an important and
highly symmetric demand system, which implies that the
lower bound is sharp.

Definition 5.1

(Fully symmetric exchangeable model)

The fully symmetric exchangeable model with
N
products
and interaction parameter
\rho\geq 0
is defined by

b_{ii}=-1\quad\text{for all }i,\qquad b_{ij}=\rho\quad\text{for all }i\neq j.

(14)

Equivalently,

\bm{B}=-\bm{I}+\rho(\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}-\bm{I})=-(1+\rho)\bm{I}+\rho\,\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top},

(15)

where
\mathbf{1}=(1,\ldots,1)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
denotes the
all–ones vector.

In this model, all products have identical own–price
sensitivities, normalized to
b_{ii}=-1
without loss of
generality, since any symmetric system with equal
own–price effects can be rescaled to this form.
All cross–price effects are identical and positive,

b_{ij}=\rho
for
i\neq j
, so the products are mutual
substitutes: increasing the price of any one product
raises the demand for every other product.

A direct calculation shows that, for the fully symmetric
exchangeable model with parameters
N
and
\rho
, the
diagonal–dominance parameter is

\mu=(N-1)\rho.

Hence, Assumption
2.2
holds if and only if

\rho<1/(N-1)
.
As the number of products
N
grows, this restriction
becomes more stringent, reflecting the fact that even
small pairwise interactions can accumulate across many
products and violate diagonal dominance.

The special structure of the symmetric model allows
closed–form spectral calculations.
In particular,
\bm{B}
is a rank–one perturbation of a
scaled identity matrix, which yields explicit formulas
for its eigenvalues and inverse.

Lemma 5.2

(Spectrum of the symmetric demand matrix)

In the fully symmetric exchangeable model, the matrix

\bm{B}
has eigenvalue

\lambda_{1}=-1+(N-1)\rho

with eigenvector
\mathbf{1}
, and eigenvalue

\lambda_{2}=-1-\rho

with multiplicity
N-1
, corresponding to any vector
orthogonal to
\mathbf{1}
.
Moreover,

\bm{B}^{-1}=-\frac{1}{1+\rho}\bm{I}-\frac{\rho}{(1+\rho)\bigl(1-(N-1)\rho\bigr)}\,\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}.

We now compute the centralized and Nash equilibrium
revenues for the symmetric model under a symmetric
intercept vector.
Because
\bm{B}
is invariant under any simultaneous
permutation of rows and columns, any solution of the
first–order conditions with a symmetric intercept
vector
\bm{a}=a\mathbf{1}
must itself be symmetric.
Consequently, both the centralized and Nash equilibrium
price vectors lie in the one–dimensional subspace
spanned by
\mathbf{1}
.

Lemma 5.3

(Centralized optimum in the symmetric model)

In the fully symmetric exchangeable model with intercept
vector
\bm{a}=a\mathbf{1}
and diagonal–dominance parameter

\mu=(N-1)\rho
, the centralized optimal prices are

\bm{p}^{*}=p^{*}\mathbf{1}
with

p^{*}=\frac{a}{2(1-\mu)},

and the corresponding optimal revenue is

R(\bm{p}^{*})=\frac{Na^{2}}{4(1-\mu)}.

We obtain analogous closed–form expressions for the
Nash equilibrium.

Lemma 5.4

(Nash equilibrium in the symmetric model)

In the fully symmetric exchangeable model with intercept
vector
\bm{a}=a\mathbf{1}
and diagonal–dominance parameter

\mu=(N-1)\rho
, the Nash equilibrium prices are

\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}}=p^{\mathrm{NE}}\mathbf{1}
with

p^{\mathrm{NE}}=\frac{a}{2-\mu},

and the corresponding Nash equilibrium revenue is

R(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}})=\frac{Na^{2}}{(2-\mu)^{2}}.

Combining Lemmas
5.3
and

5.4
yields the following result.

Theorem 5.5

(Tightness of the price–of–anarchy bound)

In the fully symmetric exchangeable model with intercept
vector
\bm{a}=a\mathbf{1}
and diagonal–dominance parameter

\mu=(N-1)\rho<1
, we have

\mathrm{PoA}(\bm{a})=\frac{R(\bm{p}^{\mathrm{NE}})}{R(\bm{p}^{*})}=\frac{4(1-\mu)}{(2-\mu)^{2}}.

This value coincides with the lower bound in
Theorem

4.12
, showing that the bound is
tight.

The fully symmetric exchangeable model therefore
represents a worst–case instance for decentralized
pricing.
Here all products are identical and interact
symmetrically, which maximizes the inefficiency induced
by uncoordinated price setting.
Moreover, the symmetric intercept vector

\bm{a}=a\mathbf{1}
aligns with the eigenvector of the
matrix
\bm{Y}
associated with its smallest eigenvalue

\theta_{\min}=(1-\mu)/(2-\mu)
, which minimizes the
function
4\theta(1-\theta)
and hence attains the
worst–case price of anarchy.

6
An Exact Characterization of PoA via Spectral Bound

In
Section

4
we showed that the worst–case
price of anarchy equals the smallest eigenvalue of

\bm{M}=4\bm{Y}(\bm{I}-\bm{Y})
,
where

\bm{Y}=\bm{H}^{1/2}\bm{G}^{-1}\bm{H}^{1/2},

and derived a universal lower bound in terms of the
diagonal–dominance parameter

\mu
. We further established in

Section

5
that this bound is tight
for the fully symmetric exchangeable model, in which all
products interact with equal strength.
When interaction strengths are heterogeneous, however,
this bound can be conservative.

The goal of this section is to provide an
exact
spectral characterization—rather than merely a tight lower bound—of the worst-case price of anarchy, fully capturing the structure of cross-product interactions. Our main result in this section, as stated in

Theorem

6.5
below, is

\mathrm{PoA}_{\min}=\frac{4\bigl(1-\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}\bigr)}{\bigl(2-\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}\bigr)^{2}}.

where
\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}
is a tight upper bound over the absolute values of the eigenvalues
of a suitably normalized interaction matrix.
This quantity generalizes the scalar parameter
\mu
and
captures heterogeneity in cross–price effects.
We begin by defining this normalized interaction matrix
and then prove the main result.

6.1
The Normalized Interaction Matrix

Let

d_{i}:=-b_{ii}=|b_{ii}|>0

denote the magnitude of the own–price effect for product

i
.
We normalize cross–price interactions by these
quantities.

Definition 6.1

(Normalized interaction matrix)

The
normalized interaction matrix

\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is defined by

(\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}})_{ij}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{b_{ij}}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}},&i\neq j,\\[6.0pt]
0,&i=j.\end{cases}

By construction,
\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}
is symmetric with
zero diagonal and possibly nonzero off–diagonal entries. For
i\neq j
, the entry

(M_{\mathrm{norm}})_{ij}=\frac{b_{ij}}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}

is a
dimensionless
measure of the strength of the cross–price interaction between products

i
and
j
, normalized by the geometric mean of their own–price sensitivities.
Thus,
|(M_{\mathrm{norm}})_{ij}|
is large when the cross effect
b_{ij}
is large relative to the two own effects,
and small when cross effects are weak compared to own effects.
The sign of
(M_{\mathrm{norm}})_{ij}
retains the economic direction of interaction (e.g., positive for
substitutes and negative for complements under the sign convention in Assumption 2).
Equivalently,
M_{\mathrm{norm}}=D^{-1/2}B_{\mathrm{off}}D^{-1/2}
rescales each product so that own–price
effects are comparable across products; the resulting matrix isolates
relative
cross–product coupling
and is invariant to heterogeneous magnitudes of
\{d_{i}\}
.
In the special case
d_{i}\equiv d
, we simply have
M_{\mathrm{norm}}=B_{\mathrm{off}}/d
.

Let

\bm{D}:=\mathrm{diag}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{N})

denote the diagonal matrix of own–price effect
magnitudes, and let
\bm{B}_{\mathrm{off}}
be the matrix
with entries
b_{ij}
for
i\neq j
and zeros on the
diagonal.
Then

\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}=\bm{D}^{-1/2}\,\bm{B}_{\mathrm{off}}\,\bm{D}^{-1/2}.

Because
\bm{B}
is symmetric
(
Section

2.2
), we know that the matrix

\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}
is a symmetric matrix with zero diagonal.

Definition 6.2

(Spectral interaction parameter)

The
spectral interaction parameter
is defined as

\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}:=\max\left\{\left|\lambda_{\max}(\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}})\right|,\left|\lambda_{\min}(\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}})\right|\right\},

where
\lambda_{\max}(\cdot)
denotes the largest
eigenvalue and
\lambda_{\min}(\cdot)
denotes the smallest eigenvalue.

It is clear to see that the spectral interaction parameter
\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}
defined in
Definition

6.2
gives a tight upper bound on the absolute values of all eigenvalues of the matrix
\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}})
.
The next lemma summarizes basic properties of

\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}
and
\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}
.

Lemma 6.3

(Properties of
\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}
)

Under Assumptions
2.2
,

2.2
, and
2.2
, the matrix

\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}
is symmetric, and all of its
eigenvalues lie in the interval

[-\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}},\,\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}]
.
Moreover, we have that

0\leq\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}<1\text{\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ and\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ }\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\mu_{i},

where

\mu_{i}:=\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{b_{ij}}{d_{i}}

is the local diagonal–dominance parameter for product

i
.

Note that the parameter
\mu
in
Theorem

4.12
satisfies
\mu=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\mu_{i}
.

6.2
Exact Formula for the Worse-case Price of Anarchy

We now show that the worst–case price of anarchy admits
an exact characterization in terms of the spectral
interaction parameter

\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}
defined through the normalized
interaction matrix
\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}
.
The key step is in deriving an explicit relationship between the
eigenvalues of

\bm{Y}=\bm{H}^{1/2}\bm{G}^{-1}\bm{H}^{1/2}

and those of
\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}
.

Lemma 6.4

(Spectrum of
\bm{Y}
via
\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}
)

Under Assumptions
2.2
,

2.2
, and
2.2
, let

\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N}
denote the eigenvalues of

\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}
(allowing multiplicity).
Then the eigenvalues of

\bm{Y}=\bm{H}^{1/2}\bm{G}^{-1}\bm{H}^{1/2}
are given by

\theta_{i}=\frac{1-\lambda_{i}}{2-\lambda_{i}},\qquad i=1,\ldots,N.

We are now ready to state the main result of this section.

Theorem 6.5

(Exact spectral formula for the price of anarchy)

Under Assumptions
2.2
,

2.2
, and
2.2
, the worst–case price of
anarchy satisfies

\mathrm{PoA}_{\min}=\frac{4\bigl(1-\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}\bigr)}{\bigl(2-\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}\bigr)^{2}},

(16)

where

\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}=\lambda_{\max}(\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}})
.
Moreover, the bound is attained for an intercept vector
of the form

\bm{a}^{*}=\bm{D}^{1/2}\bm{v}^{*},

where
\bm{v}^{*}
is an eigenvector of
\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}

associated with
\lambda_{\max}(\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}})
.

Recall that
Theorem

4.12
in

Section

4.3
established the universal bound

\mathrm{PoA}_{\min}\geq\frac{4(1-\mu)}{(2-\mu)^{2}}
.

Theorem

6.5
refines this result
by identifying the exact value of
\mathrm{PoA}_{\min}
.
Since

\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}\leq\max_{i}\mu_{i}=\mu

by
Lemma

6.3
and the function

f(\mu)=4(1-\mu)/(2-\mu)^{2}
is strictly decreasing on

[0,1)
, it follows that

\mathrm{PoA}_{\min}=f(\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}})\geq f\!\left(\max_{i}\mu_{i}\right),

which recovers and strengthens

Theorem

4.12
.
Moreover, the diagonal–dominance bound is tight if and
only if

\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}=\max_{i}\mu_{i}
, which happens under the worst-case scenario given in
Section

.

6.3
Examples

In this subsection, we illustrate the spectral characterization of the price
of anarchy through two representative examples.

Example 6.6

(Uniform local connectivity)

Suppose
\mu_{i}=\mu
for all
i
and that all own–price
effects are equal,
d_{i}=d
for all
i
.
This is exactly the fully symmetric exchangeable model
in
section

5
. Under these assumptions, each row sum of

\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}
is

\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{b_{ij}}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}=\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{b_{ij}}{d}=\mu_{i}=\mu.

Since
\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}
is nonnegative, symmetric,
and has constant row sums, the all–ones vector

\mathbf{1}
is an eigenvector with eigenvalue
\mu
.
By the Perron–Frobenius theorem (e.g.,

46
), this is the largest eigenvalue (also with largest absolute value),
so
\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}=\mu
.
The spectral formula therefore gives

\mathrm{PoA}_{\min}=\frac{4(1-\mu)}{(2-\mu)^{2}},

which recovers the tight bound from

Theorem

5.5
.

Example 6.7

(Star network)

Consider a star network with
N
products in which
product
0
(the center) interacts with all other
products, while the remaining products do not interact
among themselves.
Formally, let

b_{0j}=b_{j0}=\rho>0
for
j=1,\ldots,N-1
, and

b_{ij}=0
for
i,j\geq 1
with
i\neq j
.
Assume
d_{i}=|b_{ii}|=1
for all
i
.
Diagonal dominance requires
(N-1)\rho<1
.

The local interaction parameters are

\mu_{0}=(N-1)\rho,\qquad\mu_{j}=\rho\ \text{for }j\geq 1,

so
\max_{i}\mu_{i}=(N-1)\rho
.
The normalized interaction matrix has

(\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}})_{0j}=(\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}})_{j0}=\rho
for
j\geq 1
, and zeros elsewhere.
Its characteristic polynomial is

\lambda^{N-2}(\lambda^{2}-(N-1)\rho^{2})
, so its eigenvalues
are
\pm\rho\sqrt{N-1}
and
0
(with multiplicity

N-2
).
Hence we have that

\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}=\rho\sqrt{N-1}=\frac{\max_{i}\mu_{i}}{\sqrt{N-1}}.

The exact spectral formula (
16
) then
yields

\mathrm{PoA}_{\min}=\frac{4\bigl(1-\rho\sqrt{N-1}\bigr)}{\bigl(2-\rho\sqrt{N-1}\bigr)^{2}},

which is strictly larger than the row–sum bound

\frac{4\bigl(1-(N-1)\rho\bigr)}{\bigl(2-(N-1)\rho\bigr)^{2}}

based on
\max_{i}\mu_{i}
. For example, with
N=5
and
\rho=0.15
, we have

\max_{i}\mu_{i}=0.6
and

\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}=0.3
.
The row–sum bound gives

4(0.4)/(1.4)^{2}\approx 0.816
, whereas the spectral
formula yields

4(0.7)/(1.7)^{2}\approx 0.969
.
Thus the spectral parameter reveals that efficiency
loss can be much smaller than suggested by the scalar
diagonal–dominance bound.

7
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we study the efficiency loss induced by decentralized
pricing in multi–product firms.
Modeling pricing decisions as a noncooperative game under a general
linear demand system, we establish existence and uniqueness of a
pure–strategy Nash equilibrium under standard diagonal dominance
conditions.
Our main result is a tight worst–case lower bound on the Price of
Anarchy,

\frac{4(1-\mu)}{(2-\mu)^{2}},

where
\mu
captures the aggregate strength of cross–product
interactions.
This bound shows that decentralized pricing is nearly efficient when
products are weakly coupled, but can suffer substantial efficiency
losses as substitution or complementarity effects intensify.

We further sharpen this worst–case analysis by providing an exact
spectral characterization of the Price of Anarchy that accounts for
heterogeneous interaction structures.
Unlike the scalar bound, this characterization accurately reflects the
role of market topology, highlighting, for example, how hub–and–spoke
(star) networks can amplify inefficiencies relative to more balanced
interaction patterns.

From a managerial perspective, these results offer concrete guidance.
For firms with loosely connected products, the marginal revenue gain
from centralization is limited, supporting the use of autonomous
pricing teams or decentralized learning algorithms.
In contrast, when products are strongly coupled, uncoordinated pricing
decisions lead to substantial revenue leakage, justifying investment
in centralized optimization.
Future research may extend this framework to nonlinear demand models,
incorporate inventory or capacity constraints, or analyze finite–time
learning dynamics within this game–theoretic setting.

.
For the second sum (the cross terms), we will bound

using the diagonal dominance condition.
We use the inequality

for all

, which follows directly from
(a-b)^{2}\geq 0

For any

Summing over all pairs

\sum_{i\neq j}|H_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}|\leq\sum_{i\neq j}|H_{ij}|\cdot\frac{x_{i}^{2}+x_{j}^{2}}{2}.

We now reorganize the right-hand side by collecting terms involving each

For a fixed index

in the sum
\sum_{i\neq j}|H_{ij}|\cdot\frac{x_{i}^{2}+x_{j}^{2}}{2}
comes from two sources: terms where

is the first index:
\sum_{j\neq i}|H_{ij}|\cdot\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{2}
, contributing
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\neq i}|H_{ij}|
to the coefficient of
x_{i}^{2}
;
trms where
i
is the second index:
\sum_{k\neq i}|H_{ki}|\cdot\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{2}

is symmetric), we have

Therefore, the total coefficient of

As a result, we have that

\sum_{i\neq j}|H_{ij}|\cdot\frac{x_{i}^{2}+x_{j}^{2}}{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}|H_{ij}|\right)x_{i}^{2}.

Further from (

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\top}\bm{H}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i\neq j}H_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i\neq j}|H_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(d_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}|H_{ij}|\right)x_{i}^{2}.

From

\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{NE}}=(a\mathbf{1})^{\top}\left(\frac{a}{2-\mu}\mathbf{1}\right)=\frac{a^{2}}{2-\mu}(\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{1})=\frac{Na^{2}}{2-\mu}.

For the second term,
since
\mathbf{1}
is an eigenvector of
\mathbf{B}
with eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}=-1+(N-1)\rho=-1+\mu
, we have

\mathbf{B}\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{NE}}=\mathbf{B}\left(\frac{a}{2-\mu}\mathbf{1}\right)=\frac{a}{2-\mu}\cdot\lambda_{1}\cdot\mathbf{1}=\frac{a(\mu-1)}{2-\mu}\mathbf{1},

and

(\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{NE}})^{\top}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{NE}}=\left(\frac{a}{2-\mu}\mathbf{1}\right)^{\top}\cdot\frac{a(\mu-1)}{2-\mu}\mathbf{1}=\frac{a^{2}(\mu-1)}{(2-\mu)^{2}}(\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{1})=\frac{Na^{2}(\mu-1)}{(2-\mu)^{2}}.

Combining these terms, we have that

\displaystyle R(\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{NE}})

\displaystyle=\frac{Na^{2}}{2-\mu}+\frac{Na^{2}(\mu-1)}{(2-\mu)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{Na^{2}(2-\mu)+Na^{2}(\mu-1)}{(2-\mu)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{Na^{2}\left((2-\mu)+(\mu-1)\right)}{(2-\mu)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{Na^{2}}{(2-\mu)^{2}}.

Our proof is thus completed.

C.4
Proof of
Theorem

5.5

From
Lemma

5.3
and
Lemma

5.4
, we have that

R(\mathbf{p}^{*})=\frac{Na^{2}}{4(1-\mu)},\qquad R(\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{NE}})=\frac{Na^{2}}{(2-\mu)^{2}}.

Therefore, it holds that

\mathrm{PoA}(\mathbf{a})=\frac{R(\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{NE}})}{R(\mathbf{p}^{*})}=\frac{\frac{Na^{2}}{(2-\mu)^{2}}}{\frac{Na^{2}}{4(1-\mu)}}=\frac{Na^{2}}{(2-\mu)^{2}}\cdot\frac{4(1-\mu)}{Na^{2}}=\frac{4(1-\mu)}{(2-\mu)^{2}}.

This exactly equals the bound from
Theorem

4.12
.

D.1
Proof of
Lemma

6.3

Since
\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}
is real symmetric, all its eigenvalues are real. From the definition of the parameter
\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}
in
Definition

6.2
, we know that all eigenvalues lie in
[-\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}},\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}]
.

The non-negativity
\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}\geq 0
is immediate from definition.
We now prove the strict upper bound that
\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\mu_{i}
, where
\mu_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}b_{ij}/d_{i}
is the local diagonal-dominance parameter.
Since
Section

2.2
ensures
\mu_{i}<1
for all
i
, we immediately have
\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}<1
.

We now prove the key comparison between
\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}
and
\max_{i}\mu_{i}
.
Consider the similarity transformation
\bm{M}^{\prime}:=\bm{D}^{1/2}\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}\bm{D}^{-1/2}
, which has the same eigenvalues as
\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}
.
For
i\neq j
, it holds that

M^{\prime}_{ij}=\sqrt{d_{i}}\cdot\frac{b_{ij}}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{j}}}=\frac{b_{ij}}{d_{j}}.

The
j
-th column sum of
\bm{M}^{\prime}
is

\sum_{i\neq j}M^{\prime}_{ij}=\frac{1}{d_{j}}\sum_{i\neq j}b_{ij}=\frac{1}{d_{j}}\sum_{i\neq j}b_{ji}=\mu_{j},

where we used symmetry
b_{ij}=b_{ji}
and the definition
\mu_{j}=\sum_{i\neq j}b_{ji}/d_{j}
.
By Gershgorin’s theorem (see, e.g. Theorem 6.1.1 of
37
and the detailed argument in
1
) applied to the columns of
\bm{M}^{\prime}
(equivalently, the rows of
(\bm{M}^{\prime})^{\top}
), every eigenvalue
\lambda
of
\bm{M}^{\prime}
satisfies
|\lambda|\leq\mu_{j}
for some
j
.
Hence
\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}=\max\left\{|\lambda_{\max}(\bm{M}^{\prime})|,|\lambda_{\min}(\bm{M}^{\prime})|\right\}\leq\max_{j}\mu_{j}
. Our proof is thus completed.

Claim 6

(Factorization of

and

Under

2.2
,

2.2

Section

2.2

\displaystyle=\bm{D}^{1/2}(\bm{I}-\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}})\bm{D}^{1/2},

(36)

\displaystyle\bm{G}

(37)

Here
\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}=\bm{D}^{-1/2}\bm{B}_{\mathrm{off}}\bm{D}^{-1/2}

\bm{B}

This factorization holds regardless of the signs of the off-diagonal entries

We now prove

.
The proof proceeds by relating the eigenvalues of
\bm{Y}
to a generalized eigenvalue problem that reduces to an ordinary eigenvalue problem for
\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}

We claim that
\theta
is an eigenvalue of
\bm{Y}
if and only if
\theta
is a generalized eigenvalue of the pencil
(\bm{H},\bm{G})

\bm{x}

To see this, suppose

, which is nonzero since

is invertible.
Then we have

\displaystyle\bm{H}^{1/2}\bm{G}^{-1}\bm{H}^{1/2}\bm{z}

\displaystyle=\theta\bm{z}

\displaystyle\bm{G}^{-1}\bm{H}^{1/2}\bm{z}

\displaystyle=\theta\bm{H}^{-1/2}\bm{z}

\displaystyle\bm{G}^{-1}\bm{H}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\theta\bm{x}

\displaystyle\bm{H}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\theta\bm{G}\bm{x}.

Conversely, if
\bm{H}\bm{x}=\theta\bm{G}\bm{x}

\bm{z}:=\bm{H}^{1/2}\bm{x}
satisfies
\bm{Y}\bm{z}=\theta\bm{z}

Using the factorizations from
6

\bm{H}=\bm{D}^{1/2}(\bm{I}-\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}})\bm{D}^{1/2},\quad\bm{G}=\bm{D}^{1/2}(2\bm{I}-\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}})\bm{D}^{1/2}.

Left-multiplying by

(38)

This is the generalized eigenvalue problem for the matrix pencil

Since
\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}
is symmetric, let

Substituting

(1-\lambda)\bm{v}=\theta(2-\lambda)\bm{v}.

Since

\theta=\frac{1-\lambda}{2-\lambda}.

The eigenvectors of

D.3
Proof of
Theorem

6.5

From
Theorem

4.9
, we know that
\mathrm{PoA}_{\min}=\lambda_{\min}(\bm{M})=\min_{\theta\in\sigma(\bm{Y})}\left\{4\theta(1-\theta)\right\}
, where
\sigma(\bm{Y})
denotes the set of eigenvalues of the matrix
\bm{Y}
. Moreover,
by
Lemma

6.4
, for each eigenvalue
\theta_{i}
of the matrix
\bm{Y}
, we know that
\theta_{i}=\frac{1-\lambda_{i}}{2-\lambda_{i}}
where
\lambda_{i}
are eigenvalues of
\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}
. Note that

\displaystyle 4\theta_{i}(1-\theta_{i})=\

\displaystyle=4\cdot\frac{1-\lambda_{i}}{2-\lambda_{i}}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1-\lambda_{i}}{2-\lambda_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=4\cdot\frac{1-\lambda_{i}}{2-\lambda_{i}}\cdot\frac{(2-\lambda_{i})-(1-\lambda_{i})}{2-\lambda_{i}}

\displaystyle=4\cdot\frac{1-\lambda_{i}}{2-\lambda_{i}}\cdot\frac{1}{2-\lambda_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4(1-\lambda_{i})}{(2-\lambda_{i})^{2}}.

Therefore, we know that

\mathrm{PoA}_{\min}=\lambda_{\min}(\bm{M})=\min_{\theta\in\sigma(\bm{Y})}\left\{4\theta(1-\theta)\right\}=\min_{i=1,\dots,N}\left\{\frac{4(1-\lambda_{i})}{(2-\lambda_{i})^{2}}\right\}.

We now focus on the function

g(\lambda)=\frac{4(1-\lambda_{i})}{(2-\lambda_{i})^{2}}

for
\lambda\in[-\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}},\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}]
, where it follows from
Lemma

6.3
that all the eigenvalues of
\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}
lies in the interval
[-\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}},\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}]
.
By taking the derivative, we have that

g^{\prime}(\lambda)=-\frac{4\lambda}{(2-\lambda)^{3}}.

Since from
Lemma

6.3
, we know that
0<\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}<1
, then we know that the function
g(\lambda)
is increasing on the interval
[-\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}},0]
and decreasing on the interval
[0,\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}]
. Therefore, we have that

\mathrm{PoA}_{\min}\geq\min\left\{g(-\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}),g(\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}})\right\}.

It only remains to compare the value of
-g(\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}})
and
g(\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}})
. Since we know that
a:=\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}>0
, we have that

g(a)-g(-a)=-\frac{8a^{3}}{(4-a^{2})^{2}}\leq 0.

As a result, we have that

\mathrm{PoA}_{\min}\geq g(\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}})=\frac{4(1-\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}})}{(2-\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}})^{2}}.

The worst-case is achieved when
\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}
is indeed an eigenvalue of
\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}
(happens when
b_{ij}\geq 0
for all
i\neq j
) and the intercept vector
\bm{a}
corresponds (after transformation) to the eigenvector of
\bm{Y}
with eigenvalue
\theta_{\min}
.
From the proof of
Lemma

6.4
, this eigenvector in the
\bm{y}=\bm{D}^{1/2}\bm{x}
coordinates corresponds to an eigenvector
\bm{v}^{*}
of
\bm{M}_{\mathrm{norm}}
with eigenvalue
\mu_{\mathrm{spectral}}
.
Tracing back through the transformations,
\bm{a}^{*}=\bm{D}^{1/2}\bm{v}^{*}
achieves the minimum. Our proof is thus completed.
