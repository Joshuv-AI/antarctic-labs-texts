Network analysis of school connectedness and psychological crisis among university students
Authors: Ding Zhang, Nan Gao, Yao Wang, Xiaoqian Liu, Haoran Peng, Shihan Hu, Xuelan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveWith the rising incidence of psychological crisis among university students, the research focus has gradually shifted from a symptom-based to a protective-factor perspective, in which school connectedness is considered a potential protective factor. This study aimed to examine the network structure linking school connectedness and psychological crisis among university students, thereby providing empirical evidence for understanding the associations between these constructs.MethodsA total of 3,580 undergraduates from five universities in Henan Province, China, were surveyed using the School Connectedness Questionnaire and the Psychological Crisis Questionnaire. Network analysis was employed to reveal the associative patterns between the two constructs.Results(i) The network structure of school connectedness and psychological crisis was stable, with relatively strong internal connections within each cluster. In the cross-cluster network, a covariation was observed between “I can rely on my classmates when facing difficulties” (PS1) and “People around me hold prejudices against me” (CD4). (ii) Centrality analysis revealed that “Feeling lonely and helpless, as if on the verge of emotional collapse” (EBD8) and “Feeling incapable of doing anything well” (CD7) exhibited the highest expected influence. (iii) Bridge centrality results showed that “I can rely on my classmates when facing difficulties” (PS1) and “My classmates share happiness and sorrow with me” (PS3) had the strongest connections to the psychological crisis network, whereas “Feeling lonely and helpless, as if on the verge of emotional collapse” (EBD8) and “Having pessimistic or suicidal thoughts” (CD1) had the strongest connections to the school connectedness network. (iv) The global connectivity strength invariance test indicated that the global strength of the female network was significantly higher than that of the male network.ConclusionThis study employed psychological network analysis to systematically construct a school connectedness–psychological crisis network model among Chinese university students, revealing the complex associative structure between protective factors and crisis symptoms. Alleviating loneliness, correcting negative self-perceptions, and strengthening peer support may serve as three core targets for the prevention and intervention of psychological crisis among university students, potentially contributing to improved mental health and school adaptation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04661-9.

Introduction

The university period represents a critical transition from adolescence to adulthood. During this time, individuals face multiple pressures related to academics, interpersonal relationships, career development, and self-identity [1, 2]. The external environment for students has become increasingly complex and challenging, marked by social conflicts, family tensions, emotional confusion, academic and employment stress, interpersonal competition, and cultural clashes. This context can be associated with issues such as rebellious and impulsive tendencies, psychological fragility, low stress resilience, and a reliance on passive or even extreme coping strategies when encountering difficulties. A meta-analysis of literature from 2010 to 2020 by Yu and colleagues revealed an overall detection rate of 18.9% for mental health problems among Chinese university students. This includes specific rates of 23.5% for sleep problems, 20.8% for depression, 16.2% for self-harm, 13.7% for anxiety, 10.8% for suicidal ideation, 4.5% for somatization, and 2.7% for suicide attempts. Notably, the detection rates for anxiety, depression, sleep problems, and suicide attempts have shown significant increases over the past decade [3]. The National Mental Health Development Report of China (2021–2022) further indicates that 21.48% of university students are at risk for depression and 45.28% are at risk for anxiety [4]. As depression and anxiety are key indicators of psychological crisis, their high prevalence underscores the severity and complexity of mental health issues in this population. Therefore, an in-depth investigation into the factors associated with psychological crisis among university students is necessary to inform the development of effective intervention strategies.

Psychological crisis refers to a state of psychological imbalance that occurs when an individual confronts a sudden or major life adversity that exceeds their coping capabilities and habitual strategies, and is accompanied by disturbances and distress in cognition, emotion, physiology, and behavior [5]. Depressive symptoms represent the most typical and common clinical presentation of such crises. Findings from studies on depressive symptom networks have consistently shown that the connection between depressed mood and anhedonia is among the strongest within the network [6, 7]. Furthermore, stable and strong associations exist between affective symptoms and the typical symptoms of depressive episodes [8]. As research on student psychological crisis has deepened, investigators have increasingly recognized that the presence of early risk factors is associated with a higher likelihood of subsequent crisis events. Consequently, the perspective in psychological crisis early-warning and intervention research has gradually shifted from a symptom-focused to a protection-focused approach. Protective factors are variables that are associated with a lower probability of psychological crisis [9]. School connectedness, defined as a psychological state in which students perceive emotional bonds, care, and support from others (e.g., peers, teachers) within the school environment and develop a sense of belonging and identification with the school, has garnered widespread attention as one such protective factor [10]. This construct typically encompasses dimensions such as peer support, teacher support, and school belonging. Ecological Systems Theory posits that among the environmental factors related to human development, the school system constitutes a proximal microsystem in which individuals directly participate and engage in face-to-face interactions. This system is closely associated with the risk of negative mental health outcomes across the lifespan [11]. Research indicates that the accumulation of adverse school environmental factors is strongly linked to adolescent suicide and self-harm behaviors [12]. Within this context, peer relationships—a crucial interpersonal component of the school system—are significantly associated with adolescent problematic behaviors and mental health status [13]. A network analysis study of depression risk factors and symptoms among Pacific adolescents in New Zealand showed that school connectedness, the quality of relationships with mother/friends, and self-assessed weight were associated with both depression severity and specific symptoms [14]. This further confirms the robust association between school connectedness and mental health, suggesting that, regardless of cultural context, enhancing school connectedness may have universal significance for preventing adolescent depression and psychological crisis.

Grounded in Self-Determination Theory, the need for belonging is regarded as a fundamental psychological requirement. Fulfilling this need through school connectedness is positively associated with an individual’s sense of belonging and well-being [15, 16]. Evidence suggests that support from peers and teachers may be linked to weaker negative effects of stressful events [17]. Students with higher levels of school connectedness are more willing to engage in school activities, adhere to school norms, and demonstrate better psychosocial adaptation [18, 19]. Particularly within the collectivist cultural context of China, a sense of belonging and honor toward the collective may be crucial elements for university students’ school adjustment and mental health [20]. Therefore, a supportive and inclusive school environment may be an important correlate of student psychological crises. Nevertheless, the intricate interactions among different symptoms within a psychological crisis, as well as between these symptoms and various dimensions of school connectedness, remain to be fully elucidated.

Despite growing evidence linking school connectedness to mental health outcomes, two important gaps remain. First, most previous studies have examined school connectedness and psychological crisis as separate latent constructs using variable-centered approaches (e.g., regression, structural equation modeling) [21]. These methods assume that all items within a scale are interchangeable indicators of an underlying dimension, but they cannot reveal which specific aspects of school connectedness (e.g., peer support vs. teacher support vs. school belonging) are most strongly associated with particular crisis symptoms. Second, the pattern of direct associations between individual crisis symptoms (e.g., loneliness, negative self-evaluation) and specific dimensions of school connectedness has not been systematically mapped. Without this granular understanding, interventions may target broad constructs rather than the most critical nodes and pathways.

Network analysis offers a complementary framework that addresses these gaps [22]. Unlike latent variable models, network analysis conceptualizes psychological constructs as systems of interacting nodes (e.g., symptoms, protective factors). It allows researchers to (i) estimate the strength and pattern of pairwise associations among individual items, (ii) identify nodes with high centrality (i.e., those most strongly connected to other nodes within the same cluster), and (iii) identify bridge nodes (i.e., those that connect different clusters, such as school connectedness and psychological crisis). This level of specificity is essential for pinpointing potential targets for prevention and intervention. Prior work has shown that school connectedness has a stable network structure, with items such as “I am happy to be a member of this school” and “My teachers care about and support me” emerging as central nodes [23]. Similarly, psychological crisis symptoms (e.g., depression, anxiety) exhibit network structures in which symptoms reinforce each other [6, 7, 24]. However, no study to date has integrated these two networks to examine how specific school connectedness nodes are associated with specific psychological crisis nodes in a unified network.

Accordingly, this study employs psychological network analysis to systematically delineate the structural features of the networks of school connectedness and psychological crisis among Chinese university students. This approach offers a granular, item‑level perspective for understanding the relationship between protective factors and crisis symptoms, and provides hypothesis‑generating insights for school‑based psychological crisis intervention.

Methods

Participants

Using a convenience sampling approach, a total of 3,700 university students from five universities in Henan Province were recruited. A total of 3,580 valid questionnaires were collected, yielding a valid response rate of 96.8%. The sample comprised 1,280 male and 2,300 female students. In terms of academic year, 2,229 were freshmen, 1,262 were sophomores, and 89 were juniors. Regarding family background, 804 students were from urban areas and 2,776 were from rural areas. There were 704 only children and 2,876 non-only children. In addition, 326 students held student cadre positions, while 3,254 did not. The age of participants ranged from 16 to 24 years, with a mean age of 19.38 years (SD = 0.97).

Measures

School connectedness questionnaire

School connectedness was measured using the 10-item School Connectedness Scale developed by Yu et al. [25]. The scale encompasses three dimensions: teacher support, peer support, and school belonging. Items are rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Dimension scores are calculated as the mean of corresponding items, with higher total scores indicating stronger perceived teacher and peer support as well as a greater sense of school belonging. The scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity and is considered a reliable tool for assessing school connectedness [26–28]. In this study, the internal consistency of the scale was good (McDonald’s ω = 0.826). The internal consistency coefficients for each dimension reached acceptable levels: peer support (McDonald’s ω = 0.651), teacher support (McDonald’s ω = 0.654), and school belonging (McDonald’s ω = 0.754) [29]. The measurement model showed a good fit: χ²/df = 3.586, RMSEA = 0.057, CFI = 0.948, TLI = 0.919, SRMR = 0.039.

Psychological crisis questionnaire

Psychological crisis was assessed using the 25-item Psychological Crisis Assessment Questionnaire for University Students developed by Li et al. [5]. The questionnaire consists of three dimensions: cognitive dysfunction, emotional and behavioral dysregulation, and physiological discomfort. Responses are recorded on a five-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores reflecting more pronounced psychological crisis symptoms. The questionnaire has been validated with good reliability and validity, supporting its use as a reliable measure of psychological crisis among university students [30, 31]. In this study, the overall internal consistency of the scale was excellent (McDonald’s ω = 0.952). The internal consistency coefficients for the subscales were also high: physiological discomfort (McDonald’s ω = 0.857), emotional and behavioral dysregulation (McDonald’s ω = 0.908), and cognitive dysfunction (McDonald’s ω = 0.919). The measurement model showed a good fit: χ²/df = 3.597, RMSEA = 0.054, CFI = 0.919, TLI = 0.907, SRMR = 0.038.

Quality control

Quality control in this study encompassed the following aspects. First, during the administration process, paper-and-pencil tests were administered on a class-by-class basis. Trained test administrators read the instructions aloud on-site, ensuring that participants fully understood the purpose of the test, the testing methods, data usage, and confidentiality principles before proceeding with the unified administration. Second, in the data entry phase, questionnaires with missing key demographic variables, more than five missing items, or uniform responses were excluded. Data entry and processing were performed using EpiData to ensure accuracy throughout the entry process. To test for common method bias, we employed Harman’s single-factor test. An unrotated principal component analysis of all measurement items revealed six factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, with the first factor accounting for 27.67% of the total variance, which is below the critical threshold of 40%. This suggests that common method bias is not a serious concern in this study.

Network analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS (version 20.0) and R (version 4.5.1). The R code used to reproduce the network analysis is provided in the Supplementary Materials. First, we employed the EBICglasso method for network estimation and visualization. This approach integrates the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC) with the Graphical Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (GLASSO) algorithm to estimate a regularized partial correlation network. It effectively identifies core relationships among variables while controlling for spurious associations [32]. Through regularization, weaker estimated connections are automatically shrunk to zero, thereby highlighting key edges and enhancing the model’s interpretability and visual clarity. The network structure was visualized using the R package qgraph [33]. In the network graph, nodes labeled PS1–PS4 represent the four items measuring peer support (a dimension of school connectedness), TS1–TS3 represent the three items measuring teacher support, and SB1–SB3 represent the three items measuring school belonging. Nodes labeled PD1–PD7 represent the seven items measuring physiological discomfort (a dimension of psychological crisis), EBD1–EBD8 represent the eight items measuring emotional and behavioral dysregulation, and CD1–CD10 represent the ten items measuring cognitive dysfunction. To identify the core nodes with the greatest influence on the overall network structure, we quantified node centrality using indices such as expected influence, which measures the relative importance of nodes within the interconnected network. Furthermore, to explore the relationships between different clusters of psychological variables, we constructed a bridge network and calculated bridging centrality metrics to identify bridging nodes that connect different clusters [34]. In the bridge network, bridging centrality is used to assess a node’s influence on other node clusters within the network, including metrics such as bridge strength and bridge expected influence (BEI).

Second, to ensure the robustness of the network results, we used the R package bootnet (version 1.5) to evaluate network stability and accuracy. A nonparametric bootstrap with 3,000 resamples was performed to compute 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for edge weight accuracy. We then implemented a case-dropping bootstrap procedure to assess network stability by calculating the correlation stability (CS) coefficient. The CS coefficient indicates the maximum proportion of the sample that can be removed while still maintaining a correlation of at least 0.7 with the original centrality indices and edge weights within a 95% CI. A CS coefficient above 0.25 is considered acceptable, while a value above 0.5 indicates good stability [35].

Third, we used the Network Comparison Test (NCT) from the NetworkComparisonTest R package to assess gender differences in network characteristics [36]. Based on 3,000 permutation iterations, the test examined whether the two networks (male vs. female) differed significantly in: (a) global network strength (the absolute sum of all edge weights), (b) network structure (the maximum absolute difference in any edge weight), and (c) specific edges and node centrality.

Network structure

This study constructed a network to examine the associative patterns between school connectedness and psychological crisis in university students. The regularized network comprised 35 nodes and 595 possible edges, of which 133 edges had non-zero weights (average edge weight = 0.11). In Fig. 1, blue lines denote positive correlations, red lines denote negative correlations, and line thickness corresponds to the strength of association between nodes.

The network structure showed two stable and relatively dense clusters: a school connectedness cluster and a psychological crisis cluster. Within the psychological crisis cluster, the strongest connection was between “Feel gloomy and unable to be happy” (EBD5) and “Feel uninterested in everything and experience a sense of oppression” (EBD6), with an edge weight of 0.51. Within the school connectedness cluster, the strongest connection was between “I am happy to be a member of this school” (SB2) and “I am proud to belong to this school” (SB3), with an edge weight of 0.50. This pattern is consistent with strong internal coherence within each cluster after accounting for the shared variance of other items. In the cross-cluster network, edges linking school connectedness and psychological crisis generally had small weights. The edge with the strongest absolute weight connected “I can rely on my classmates when facing difficulties” (PS1) and “Others are prejudiced against me” (CD4), with a weight of -0.07, suggesting a covariation between these constructs.

Centrality analysis showed that “Feeling incapable of doing anything well” (CD7) and “Feeling lonely and helpless to the point of emotional overwhelm” (EBD8) had the highest expected influence in the network (each with a value of 1.50), suggesting that they were associated with many other nodes (Fig. 2).

To assess whether there are gender differences in the psychological crisis-school connectedness network, we conducted a network comparison analysis. First, the network structure invariance test showed no significant difference in the overall network structure between male and female groups (M = 0.114, p = 0.350). Second, the global connectivity strength invariance test revealed that the global strength of the female network (17.331) was significantly higher than that of the male network (16.224), and this difference was statistically significant (S = 1.107, p = 0.043). However, further edge invariance tests indicated that none of the specific connections between any two variables in the network differed significantly between males and females (all adjusted p-values > 0.05). Meanwhile, node centrality (including strength centrality and expected influence centrality) invariance tests also showed no statistically significant differences in centrality indices between genders for any node (all p > 0.139).

Discussion

The present study constructed a network of school connectedness and psychological crisis among university students. The results indicate that not all nodes within the network were equally important, and the strength of associations between nodes also varied markedly. Within the psychological crisis cluster, feeling depressed (EBD5) and losing interest (EBD6) exhibited the strongest association. These items correspond to the two core symptoms of the diagnostic criteria for depression (DSM-5 and ICD-11)—depressed mood and loss of interest, respectively. This finding aligns with previous network studies on depressive symptoms [6, 7] and is consistent with the view that symptoms are associated with each other in ways that may reinforce their mutual co-occurrence [23]. However, it should be noted that these two items share semantic overlap (both describe negative emotional states and loss of positive affect), which may have contributed to the strength of their estimated association. Therefore, this edge weight should be interpreted with caution. Within the psychological crisis symptom network, the strong connection between emotional symptoms may represent self-reinforcement within the cluster, forming a persistent negative cycle. Furthermore, within the school connectedness cluster, SB2 and SB3 showed the strongest association, which aligns with previous findings that school belonging occupies a core position within school connectedness [22]. Research has shown that a stronger sense of collective responsibility is associated with lower levels of depressive symptoms among university students [20]. In the context of Chinese collectivist culture, the sense of “honor” toward the collective may be a key element for university students’ school adaptation and mental health. In this network, the intra-domain edge weight can reach up to 0.51, while the absolute values of cross-domain edge weights are generally small. This distribution pattern is consistent with previous studies, in which cross-domain associations are typically weaker than intra-domain associations [14]. The results showed a weak but statistically stable cross-cluster connection between the peer support node (PS1: “I can rely on my classmates when facing difficulties”) and the perceived prejudice node (CD4: “People around me hold prejudices against me”). This suggests a potential association between the quality of peer relationships and psychological crises among university students. When individuals worry about being rejected or ridiculed, they may cease to actively seek peer support, and this is associated with a state of isolation. This vigilance and avoidance of interpersonal signals lies at the core of how perceived prejudice and unfairness are maintained and intensified [37]. The absence of peer support is associated with the activation of cognitive biases; conversely, reliable peer support is associated with lower levels of psychological distress. Research has shown that perceivable, stable peer support can effectively weaken negative cognitive patterns in adolescents and significantly alleviate their psychological distress [38]. When individuals experience greater peer support, such direct, positive interpersonal experiences may be associated with a reduction in the negative cascading effects triggered by cognitive biases, which may otherwise be associated with deteriorating overall mental health.

Node centrality analysis revealed that nodes CD7 (“Feeling incapable of doing anything well”) and EBD8 (“Feeling lonely and helpless to the point of emotional overwhelm”) ranked among the highest in the network for expected influence centrality, suggesting that they occupy relatively core positions within the overall psychological crisis-protective factor network. From the perspective of the emotion regulation process model [39], the negative self-efficacy evaluation reflected by CD7 is associated with reduced effective utilization of adaptive emotion regulation strategies such as cognitive reappraisal and attentional deployment, and this covaries with the loneliness and despair experiences characterized by EBD8. Conversely, persistent emotional pain reinforces the core belief of “I am incompetent,” forming a self-sustaining negative feedback loop. Together, they are identified as the cognitive-affective core symptoms of the psychological crisis network based on their network positions.

Furthermore, among the bridge nodes, EBD8 (feeling lonely and helpless to the point of emotional overwhelm) within the emotional and behavioral dysregulation symptoms and CD1 (having pessimistic or suicidal thoughts) within the cognitive dysfunction symptoms exhibited the highest bridge expected influence values. This suggests that school connectedness is associated with the psychological crisis network through pathways involving feelings of loneliness or negative cognitions. Higher levels of loneliness (EBD8) are associated with lower perceived peer support and a weaker sense of school belonging, which in turn are associated with reduced psychological protective resources. Research indicates that loneliness has a bidirectional association with problem behaviors and has been identified as a potential target in network studies [40]. Meanwhile, pessimistic or suicidal thoughts (CD1), as a profound cognitive distortion, directly attack an individual’s sense of meaning and future orientation, potentially disrupting the pathway through which school connectedness could exert positive influences. These two critical bridge nodes may be considered as potential key factors in the patterns of co-occurrence and persistence of psychological crises among university students, pending validation through longitudinal or experimental research.

A systematic comparison of the school connectedness-psychological crisis network between male and female university students revealed no significant difference in network structure between genders. However, it should be acknowledged that the sample was drawn exclusively from universities in Henan Province, China. These sampling characteristics may limit the generalizability of the gender comparison findings to students from other geographical regions or with different demographic backgrounds. This indicates that the pattern of associations between psychological crisis symptoms and school connectedness is essentially similar across genders, a finding consistent with previous network analyses of depressive symptoms [41]. Regarding network connectivity strength, the global strength of the female network was significantly higher, which differs somewhat from previous research [7]. This suggests that among females, the interconnections among various elements of psychological states (including both symptoms and resources) are tighter, and emotional or cognitive experiences show stronger associations within the network. This may be related to the fact that females generally report higher emotional sensitivity and interpersonal connectedness [42]. The absence of significant gender differences in network edges and node centrality further reinforces the reliability of the conclusion that the network structure shows no significant differences—meaning that the key interactive relationships and core hub nodes within the network are shared by both male and female university students. The reason may lie in the shared core developmental tasks faced by male and female students in higher education settings, such as establishing identity, building social support systems, and achieving academic autonomy, leading them to develop similar patterns of association between psychological experiences and school adaptation. Given the sampling limitations, these conclusions should be interpreted as specific to the present sample, and future research should test their replicability across broader, more diverse university populations.

The findings of this study provide preliminary, association-based clues for future intervention research. Given the cross-sectional nature of the data, the following interpretations should be viewed as exploratory and associative rather than prescriptive. First, when exploring factors related to the prevention and intervention of psychological crises among university students, feelings of loneliness and helplessness (EBD8) may be a node worthy of further investigation. This node exhibits both high centrality and high bridge expected influence, serving not only as a node with many connections within the network but also as a node associated with both school connectedness and the psychological crisis network. Second, cognitive dysfunction symptoms such as negative self-evaluation (CD7) and pessimistic or suicidal thoughts (CD1) might be considered as candidate indicators for high-risk screening of psychological crises and as potential foci for exploratory intervention studies. Third, nodes representing the peer support dimension in the network demonstrate high bridge centrality and are directly or indirectly associated with multiple crisis symptom nodes. This echoes the previous finding that peer support and teacher support alleviate suffering through indirect effects [13], reinforcing the notion that school-based relational supports operate via multiple pathways rather than direct effects alone. Based on this, one might hypothesize that enhancing universally accessible, high-quality peer support resources for university students may be associated with weaker negative cascading effects between cognitive biases and psychological crisis symptoms, although this hypothesis requires testing. Finally, considering the similarities and differences in network structure and connectivity strength between male and female students, it is worth exploring whether common symptom features (e.g., feelings of loneliness and helplessness, self-negation) and key protective factors (e.g., reliable peer support) show similar patterns of association with core psychological crisis symptoms across genders. At the implementation level, given the stronger global connectivity observed in the female network, future research could further examine whether more systematic and integrated support strategies are particularly relevant to the potentially more complex and sensitive emotional-interpersonal interaction patterns of female students. These suggestions are intended to generate hypotheses for future research, not to provide direct clinical recommendations based on the current cross-sectional findings.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any inference of causality or directional relationship between school connectedness and psychological crisis. While our network analysis reveals robust associations, it cannot determine whether lower school connectedness is associated with higher psychological crisis symptoms, whether psychological crisis symptoms are associated with subsequent declines in school connectedness, or whether both are influenced by unmeasured third variables. Longitudinal studies are needed to examine the temporal ordering of these associations. Second, the research sample is geographically concentrated in universities within Henan Province, China, and there is a significant imbalance in the ratio of rural to urban students (2,776 rural students vs. 804 urban students). This may limit the representativeness of the findings for students from different geographical regions and urban-rural backgrounds. Future research should sample across a broader geographical scope and more balanced demographic backgrounds to test the generalizability of the conclusions. Third, there is semantic overlap in the description of emotional experiences between EBD5 (“feeling depressed and unable to cheer up”) and EBD6 (“losing interest in everything and feeling oppressed”) in this study, which may have a certain impact on the estimated connection strength and should be interpreted with caution. Finally, the use of self-report questionnaires may introduce recall bias and social desirability bias. Future studies could incorporate objective indicators (e.g., cortisol levels, neuroimaging data) for multi-method exploration to more deeply reveal the underlying causal mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study employed psychological network analysis to systematically construct a school connectedness-psychological crisis network model among Chinese university students, revealing the complex associative structure between protective factors and crisis symptoms, and providing new empirical evidence for core symptom identification, cross-domain pathway analysis, and gender comparisons. (i) Depressed mood (EBD5) and loss of interest (EBD6) were observed as the strongest symptom connections within the network, forming a self-sustaining negative feedback loop within the crisis network. (ii) Negative self-evaluation (CD7) and feelings of loneliness and helplessness (EBD8) were the nodes with the highest centrality in the network, suggesting that these symptom nodes may be considered as core indicators for high-risk screening and potential foci for early intervention. (iii) EBD8 was identified as a core bridge node connecting the two systems of school connectedness and psychological crisis; this node’s associations are linked to students’ perceived peer support and sense of school belonging. Meanwhile, the negative association formed between reliable peer support (PS1) and perceived prejudice (CD4) suggests that peer support is associated with the negative cascading effects of cognitive biases. (iv) The network structure showed high similarity between genders, but the global connectivity strength of the female network was significantly higher. This indicates that although the patterns of association between psychological crisis symptoms and school connectedness are largely similar for male and female university students, symptoms in the female network may be more strongly interconnected, suggesting that more systematic and integrated support strategies may be considered when designing interventions.
