From prediction to action: developing a risk-stratified management tool for children with new-onset tic disorders
Authors: Yifang Qian, Qinyu Li, Hongjie Mao, Rong Chen, Jingrong Wang, Yingying Cai, Xiumei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMany children with provisional tic disorder (PTD) progress to chronic tics, but early risk stratification tools are lacking. In a prospective cohort of children with new-onset PTD, we developed a multivariable logistic regression model to predict 12-month progression to chronic tic disorders. The model was internally validated (bootstrap) and decision curve analysis was used to define risk thresholds. The final analytic cohort comprised 108 children. Three independent predictors of progression were identified: longer tic duration (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 1.18 per month, 95% CI: 1.01–1.38), higher parent tic questionnaire (PTQ) vocal score (aOR = 1.09 per point, 95% CI: 1.02–1.16), and presence of a comorbidity (aOR = 2.84, 95% CI: 1.01–8.02). A dual-threshold approach defined low- (< 30%), intermediate- (30–56%), and high-risk (≥ 56%) strata. The high-risk group had a 70.5% progression rate (vs. 27.3% in low-risk), a relative risk of 2.58, and a number needed to intervene of 2.3. The model was translated into a nomogram, online calculator, and risk-stratified management pathway. Introduction

Tic disorders (TD) are common neurodevelopmental conditions characterized by sudden, rapid, recurrent, non-rhythmic movements or vocalizations with onset in childhood1. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) differentiates between provisional tic disorders (PTD), chronic tic disorders (CTD), and Tourette syndrome (TS)2; however, defining these disorders as a spectrum (listed from least to most severe) has been proposed3,4. Epidemiological estimates indicate that PTD affects approximately 1.2% of school-aged children in China, with CTD and TS affecting 0.9% and 0.4%, respectively5. If PTD progresses to a chronic form, it can adversely affect long-term outcomes in multiple domains, such as educational attainment, social functioning, and quality of life6–9. Therefore, investigating the prognosis of PTD is essential for understanding the progression of the entire TD spectrum.

Traditionally, PTD was considered to have a favorable prognosis, with symptoms often remitting spontaneously within months10–15. However, this view is being re-examined. Recent prospective studies suggest that a substantial proportion of children—60% or more—continue to experience tics one year after onset, progressing to CTD or TS16–18. This evolving prognostic understanding presents a central clinical challenge. Parents are understandably concerned about whether tics will persist19,20, yet clinicians—primary care pediatricians, developmental‑behavioral pediatricians, child neurologists, or child psychiatrists—lack reliable, individualized tools to identify which children with newly diagnosed PTD will progress to chronic forms requiring long-term management21. A uniform “watchful waiting” approach may delay intervention for high-risk individuals, whereas proactively treating all children would lead to inefficient resource use and unnecessary burden for low-risk families. Consequently, a practical tool to predict prognosis at the PTD stage is important for enabling risk-stratified management and optimizing clinical decision-making.

Although several factors—such as tic severity17,18,22, illness duration18, tic complexity16,17, comorbidities [e.g., Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), anxiety, Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD)]17,18, adverse perinatal events22, and family history of TD16,22—have been linked to PTD prognosis, clinical prediction models specifically developed and prospectively validated in newly diagnosed PTD cohorts remain scarce. Moreover, existing models often focus on statistical performance without addressing the pivotal clinical question of actionable risk thresholds18,23,24. A further feasibility gap concerns assessment tools. While the Yale Global Tic Severity Scale (YGTSS) is considered a gold-standard assessment, its time-consuming nature limits utility in busy clinical settings25–27. In contrast, brief parent-reported instruments such as the Parent Tic Questionnaire (PTQ) are more feasible for widespread screening and routine follow-up28–30, yet systematic efforts to build and validate PTQ-based prediction models are lacking.

To address these gaps, this prospective cohort study aims to: (1) develop and validate a prediction model using readily available clinical indicators, including the PTQ, to quantify individualized risk of progression from PTD to CTD/TS; (2) employ decision curve analysis (DCA) to evaluate the model’s clinical net benefit and establish actionable risk thresholds for stratified management; and (3) translate the final model into an integrated decision-support system (nomogram, online calculator, and clinical management pathway) that can inform clinical decision-making based on predicted risk alongside functional impairment and comorbidities. By providing a coherent framework from risk assessment to clinical action, this study seeks to support a more efficient and proactive care model for PTD.

Methods

Study participants and setting

This single-center, prospective observational cohort study consecutively enrolled children with tic symptoms at the Department of Developmental and Behavioral Pediatrics, Fujian Children’s Hospital, from January to December 2023. The study protocol complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Children’s Hospital (Fujian Branch of Shanghai Children’s Medical Center) (No: 2022ETKLR10036). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) age 2–18 years; (2) meeting the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for PTD; and (3) attending a first physician-led consultation for tics. We defined a first consultation strictly as the initial visit with a pediatrician, developmental-behavioral pediatrician, child neurologist, or child psychiatrist. Visits to non-physician mental health professionals (e.g., psychologists) or evaluations for unrelated conditions were not considered prior tic-related consultations; children with such histories remained eligible. Only those with a previous physician-attended visit specifically for tics were excluded.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) a documented diagnosis of CTD or TS before the first tics consultation; (2) comorbid neurological disorders (e.g., epilepsy, cerebral palsy), malignancies, or systemic autoimmune diseases; and (3) completion of study questionnaires by a non-primary caregiver (defined as a guardian providing < 20 h of care per week). Loss to follow-up was defined as failure to complete the 12-month follow-up. Participants with either incomplete baseline data (for key predictors) or lost to follow-up were excluded from the primary prognostic analysis.

Assessment of predictor variables

Baseline assessments included all predictor variables. Tic severity was evaluated using both the PTQ and YGTSS. For model development, the PTQ (motor, vocal, and total scores from the validated Chinese version)29 was selected as the primary measure due to its clinical practicality. Additional predictors were categorized into three domains: (1) demographic and clinical characteristics: age, sex, adverse perinatal factors (e.g., gestational hypertension/diabetes, preterm or post-term birth, neonatal intensive care unit admission > 24 h), and developmental delay (e.g., walking after 18 months or delayed language milestones); (2) tic characteristics: tic duration at presentation and family history of tic disorders; (3) comorbidity and treatment factors: clinician-diagnosed ADHD, OCD, or emotional disorders (DSM-5 criteria), and use of tic-suppressing medication (e.g., aripiprazole, tiapride, or clonidine) between baseline and the 12-month follow-up. The YGTSS served for baseline characterization and comparison. Tic severity was categorized as “Moderate-to-Severe” for a YGTSS total score ≥ 25.

Outcome ascertainment

The primary outcome was determined at the 12-month follow-up visit (window: 12–14 months). A developmental and behavioral pediatrician, blinded to baseline data, conducted the follow-up evaluation and assigned diagnoses according to DSM-5 criteria. Participants were then stratified by outcome: the persistent group (those who progressed to meet diagnostic criteria for either CTD or TS) and the remission group (those whose tics had remitted).

Sample size estimation

The development of this prediction model was a planned secondary analysis conducted within an ongoing prospective tic disorders cohort. The sample size was calculated prospectively for this modeling objective based on the events-per-variable rule for prediction models, which requires 10 to 20 outcome events per candidate predictor. Based on prior literature, the estimated conservative progression rate from PTD to CTD or TS in a clinical cohort was 60%16–18. Assuming the final model would incorporate approximately 5 independent predictors and accounting for an estimated 15% loss to follow-up, the required sample size was calculated to be between 98 and 196 participants.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2). Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P-value < 0.05. Baseline characteristics are presented for the entire cohort and stratified by outcome (persistent vs. remission). Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the independent samples t-test; non-normally distributed variables are expressed as median (first and third quartiles) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables, expressed as frequency (percentage), were compared using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test.

Predictor selection followed a two-stage process. First, univariable logistic regression was applied to all candidate variables; those with P < 0.10 were retained for multivariable analysis. We assessed multicollinearity using variance inflation factors (VIF > 5 indicating substantial collinearity). The initial multivariable model was refined through stepwise backward elimination based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) to derive a parsimonious final model. The final model’s performance was internally validated via 1000 bootstrap samples to correct for optimism. We report the optimism-adjusted concordance index (C-index) and calibration slope. All plausible two-way interactions among final predictors were tested.

The clinical net benefit of the prediction model was evaluated across a range of probability thresholds using DCA31. The standardized net benefit (sNB) of the model-based strategy was compared against “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies. A Clinically useful risk threshold for clinical intervention was derived from the DCA. This threshold was used to stratify patients into risk categories, within which actual progression rates were compared. Key impact metrics, including relative risk (RR), absolute risk difference (ARD), and number needed to intervene (NNI), were calculated. To facilitate clinical application, the final model was presented as a nomogram, embedded in an online calculator, and translated into a structured decision pathway for risk-stratified management.

Results

Cohort characteristics and disease progression

During the study period, 133 children were screened. After applying exclusion criteria (n = 4) and accounting for loss to follow-up (n = 19) and missing data (n = 2), the final analytic cohort comprised 108 children, meeting the prespecified sample size requirement. At the 12-month follow-up, 55 children (50.9%) had progressed to CTD or TS (persistent group), while 53 (49.1%) were in remission.

The baseline characteristics of the cohort are presented (Table 1). The cohort was predominantly male (74.1%) with a median age of 6 years. Children in the persistent group had a significantly longer baseline tic duration (months) than those in remission [3 (1, 6) vs. 2 (1, 3), P = 0.017]. Vocal tic scores were also significantly higher in the persistent group, as measured by both the PTQ [6 (0, 12) vs. 3 (0, 7), P = 0.013] and the YGTSS [7 (0, 13) vs. 0 (0, 9), P = 0.005]. Accordingly, the total YGTSS score was higher in the persistent group [28 (20, 33) vs. 23 (19, 27), P = 0.033]. The prevalence of comorbidities (29.1% vs. 13.2%, P = 0.075) and medication use (58.2% vs. 37.7%, P = 0.053) was numerically higher in the persistent group, though these differences were not statistically significant. No significant between-group differences were observed for other demographic, clinical, or tic characteristics (all P > 0.05).

Development and performance of the prediction model

The vocal tic severity significantly differed between outcome groups (Table 1). In accordance with the pre-specified analytic plan and due to its superior clinical feasibility, the parent-reported PTQ vocal score was selected for model development over the clinician-administered YGTSS vocal score. This decision was further supported by a high correlation between the two measures (Spearman’s ρ = 0.880, P < 0.001) and a VIF of approximately 2.8, indicating that including both would introduce multicollinearity.

Univariable logistic regression of all candidate predictors identified four variables associated with progression (P < 0.10; see Supplementary Table S1): tic duration, PTQ vocal score, comorbidity, and medication use. No substantial multicollinearity was detected among these candidates (all VIFs < 1.1). Following multivariable logistic regression and stepwise backward selection based on the AIC, the final model retained three independent predictors (Table 2): tic duration in months [adjusted odds ratio (aOR) = 1.18 per month, 95% CI: 1.01–1.38; P = 0.041], PTQ vocal score (aOR = 1.09 per point, 95% CI: 1.02–1.16; P = 0.011), and comorbidity (aOR = 2.84, 95% CI: 1.01–8.02; P = 0.048).

At a threshold probability of 0.05, the model classified all patients as high-risk (intervention rate 100%, sensitivity 100.0%, specificity 0.0%, PPV 50.9%), yielding a sNB of 0.949. Because this threshold provides no discrimination among patients, we focused on thresholds with discriminative capacity (≥ 30%). Among these, the thresholds of 0.30 and 0.56 demonstrated distinct decision profiles. The threshold of 0.30 yielded a net benefit of 0.603 with a sensitivity of 89.1%, identifying 79.6% of the cohort for potential intervention. In contrast, the threshold of 0.56—corresponding to the maximum Youden’s index from the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis—provided higher specificity (75.5%) and positive predictive value (PPV) (70.5%), with an intervention proportion of 40.7%.

Given their complementary profiles in sensitivity and specificity, these two thresholds (0.30 and 0.56) were used to define a three-tier risk stratification system: low risk [predicted probability (P) < 30%, n = 22, 20.4%], intermediate risk (30% ≤ P < 56%, n = 42, 38.9%), and high risk (P ≥ 56%, n = 44, 40.7%). The predictive performance of this system is detailed (Table 4). The observed progression rate was 70.5% (95% CI: 55.8–81.8) in the high-risk group, which was significantly higher than the rate of 27.3% (95% CI: 13.2–48.2) in the low-risk group (RR = 2.58, 95% CI: 1.27–5.25; P = 0.002), corresponding to an ARD of 43.2% points. A significant dose-response trend across risk strata was confirmed (P for trend < 0.001). The NNI to identify one true progressor was 2.3 for the high-risk group, compared with 6.4 for the intermediate-risk group.

Development and application of a risk-stratified management pathway

Based on the final multivariable model, we developed a practical, risk-stratified management pathway for PTD (Fig. 3). The pathway commences with a comprehensive initial assessment, during which the three key predictors are collected, and initial psychoeducation is provided.

Proposed risk-stratified clinical management pathway for children with newly diagnosed PTD. The pathway commences with a comprehensive assessment of functional impairment and the collection of three core predictors. For children without significant impairment, the calculated progression probability stratifies them into low- (< 30%), intermediate- (30–56%), or high-risk (≥ 56%) groups, guiding respective management strategies centered on watchful waiting, structured monitoring, or proactive intervention. A key component is dynamic reassessment at follow‑up; new functional impairment or clinical “red flags” (e.g., acute exacerbation) trigger either risk recalculation or escalation to standard treatment. Arrows indicate the clinical decision flow.

For children without significant current functional impairment, the model-derived progression probability assigns one of three risk tiers, each linked to a distinct management strategy (Fig. 3). High-risk patients (P ≥ 56%) receive enhanced monitoring and proactive intervention. This includes intensified psychoeducation and school support, preparation for evidence-based behavioral therapies [e.g., habit reversal training (HRT), comprehensive behavioral intervention for tics (CBIT)], early management of comorbidities (e.g., cognitive-behavioral therapy for anxiety; stimulants for ADHD), and a lowered threshold for pharmacological treatment [e.g., antipsychotics (aripiprazole), alpha‑2 agonists (clonidine), and benzamides (tiapride)], often in collaboration with child neurology or child psychiatry. Intermediate-risk patients (30% ≤ P < 56%) are managed through structured education and regular monitoring with routine follow-up, along with clear “red flag” instructions to trigger reassessment. Low-risk patients (P < 30%) are managed with watchful waiting and low-intensity follow-up to reduce anxiety and avoid overtreatment. This pathway is designed to complement, rather than replace, clinical assessment of current functional status.

The pathway emphasizes ongoing dynamic assessment. At each follow-up, functional status is re-evaluated, and the emergence of any predefined clinical “red flags” triggers urgent reassessment, which may lead to risk probability recalculation or direct escalation to standard treatment. Red flags are defined in two categories: (A) acute tic changes—sudden dramatic increase in tic frequency/intensity, emergence of new complex motor or vocal tics, or self-injurious tics; and (B) significant functional deterioration, including academic decline (e.g., attention dispersion due to tic suppression, learning difficulties, declining grades), social deterioration (e.g., peer victimization, stigma, withdrawal), emotional/behavioral crisis (e.g., anxiety, self-injury, or suicidal ideation), sleep disturbance (e.g., severe insomnia or tic-related awakening with daytime drowsiness), or reduced quality of life (e.g., interference with family activities, loss of hobbies). This ensures the management plan remains responsive to the child’s evolving clinical status.

To operationalize the initial risk assessment, a nomogram was constructed, and an online risk calculator was deployed (Fig. 4; available at: qianyf.cn/RiskCalculator). By inputting the three readily available clinical variables, clinicians can instantly obtain an individualized probability of progression. This estimate directly informs the entry point into the corresponding branch of the PTD risk management pathway (Fig. 3), facilitating a seamless transition from risk prediction to stratified clinical management.

Discussion

Prospective studies indicate that tics persist in most children beyond one year after onset, often progressing to CTD or TS, which confers substantial long-term burden8,32,33. Current clinical decisions, however, rely heavily on the presence of established functional impairment34. The absence of tools for early risk stratification impedes a shift from reactive to proactive management. To address this gap, we developed and validated a clinical prediction model using a prospective cohort. To our knowledge, this model is the first to systematically employ the PTQ. It incorporates three readily assessable variables (tic duration, PTQ vocal score, and comorbidity) and provides an individualized estimate of the one-year risk of progression to CTD/TS. We further translated this model into a unified clinical decision-support framework that integrates risk prediction, stratification, and management guidance.

The three predictors in our model collectively reflect the broader neurodevelopmental impairments underlying chronic tic progression. First, longer tic duration emerged as an independent predictor, consistent with prospective evidence that duration > 3 months strongly predicts non-remission (OR = 4.20, 95% CI 1.20–14.73)18. Neurobiologically, prolonged duration may correspond to the consolidation of aberrant patterns within the cortico-basal ganglia-thalamic circuits and limbic structures (e.g., hippocampus). Previous studies indicate that larger baseline hippocampal volume predicts poorer tic outcomes24, and longer illness duration is associated with specific cognitive differences33. This evidence suggests that tic duration is not merely a passive measure of time but likely represents a critical window during which abnormal neural activity is repeatedly reinforced, leading to maladaptive changes in relevant brain regions. Ultimately, this persistent dysregulation of neural circuits may become stabilized, thereby reducing the likelihood of spontaneous symptom remission.

Second, the PTQ vocal score provided distinct predictive value. Supporting our results, Kim, S. et al. found that children with ≥ 3 distinct vocal tics in the first year had poorer long-term improvement (F = 8.16, P = 0.007)17. While baseline tic severity broadly links to prognosis17,18,22, we found motor tic scores lacked significance, suggesting vocal tics are a more specific marker of chronicity. This specificity may arise because vocal tics implicate more extensive neural networks subserving language, emotion, and cognitive control (e.g., frontal/temporal lobes)35,36, suggesting greater disruption within higher-order circuits. Indirectly supporting this pathophysiology, pre-treatment functional connectivity in specific regions has been shown to predict improvement in vocal tics, but not motor tics, following behavioral therapy37. This implies that the neural circuit states reflected by the PTQ vocal score may influence both disease trajectory and treatment responsiveness.

Finally, comorbid conditions (ADHD, OCD, or emotional disorders) constituted the strongest risk factor (OR = 2.84). This association likely stems from shared neuropathology, particularly within prefrontal-striatal circuits38,39. Comorbidities may signal that neurodevelopmental aberrations have extended beyond sensorimotor networks to those governing attention, executive function, and emotion. Furthermore, regions implicated in premonitory urges (e.g., anterior cingulate, insula) also subserve emotional regulation40. Co-occurring emotional disorders may thus reflect dysfunctional emotion-sensory-motor interactions, creating a vicious cycle wherein stress triggers tics and tic-related distress exacerbates emotional dysregulation. Concurrent compromise of top-down control from the prefrontal cortex41, alongside multi-neurotransmitter (e.g., dopaminergic, serotonergic) imbalances42, likely diminishes neural compensation and promotes chronicity.

A key feature of this study is its focus on practical utility, moving beyond the development of a statistically valid model (optimism-corrected C-index = 0.682) to address how it should be used clinically. Instead of a single statistical cut-off, we established two complementary risk thresholds (0.30 and 0.56) informed by distinct clinical goals. The lower threshold (0.30), set for high sensitivity, aims for screening to capture most potential progressors for enhanced monitoring. The higher threshold (0.56), characterized by a high positive predictive value, defines a core high-risk cohort with an observed progression rate of 70.5%, which supports targeted allocation of intensive assessment and preemptive intervention resources. This dual-threshold framework, inspired by risk stratification principles in chronic disease management43,44, allows clinical triage to balance breadth and precision.

The three-tier risk stratification system, utilizing dual thresholds of 0.30 and 0.56, was validated in our cohort. Data analysis supported its effectiveness in differentiating children with varying progression risks. Children in the high-risk group (predicted probability ≥ 0.56) had a 2.58 times greater risk of progression than the low-risk group (RR = 2.58). The NNI to identify one true progressor was only 2.3 in this group, indicating efficient resource utilization43,45. In contrast, the intermediate-risk group had an NNI of 6.4, suggesting that a strategy centered on monitoring and education may be more cost-effective under resource constraints. Notably, while a “treat all” strategy would yield a similar net benefit at very low thresholds (e.g., 0.05), it would result in unnecessary monitoring or intervention for nearly half of the cohort who will not progress. Our dual-threshold approach mitigates this by enabling risk-tailored management without substantially sacrificing net clinical benefit. Overall, this stratification system translates continuous predicted risk into discrete management categories, forming the foundation for developing differentiated clinical pathways.

To facilitate the clinical application of the model, we translated it into an integrated decision-support tool comprising a nomogram, an online risk calculator, and a structured management pathway. This tool enables rapid risk assessment and provides a standardized framework linking risk strata to recommended follow-up intensity, monitoring focus, and intervention preparedness. The pathway was designed by integrating key principles of TD management: (1) it uses risk prediction as one basis for initial triage, in parallel with an assessment of immediate functional impairment; (2) it embeds dynamic reassessment and a “red flag” warning system into the follow-up cycle, ensuring the management plan can be adjusted according to changes in the child’s condition; and (3) it prioritizes the screening and management of comorbidities, aligning with the modern understanding of tic disorders as part of a neurodevelopmental spectrum27,46–51. This is intended to shift from a reactive, impairment-triggered model toward proactive management focused on early identification and prevention.

From an implementation perspective, this tool is designed to benefit a range of providers who encounter children with new-onset tic disorders, including primary care pediatricians, developmental-behavioral pediatricians, child neurologists, and child psychiatrists. It may be particularly useful for non-specialist providers (e.g., general pediatricians or family physicians in community settings) who may have limited experience in tic prognosis. For such a non-specialist, the application can be illustrated with a concrete example: A 6-year-old boy presents with a 4-month history of simple motor and vocal tics (eye blinking and throat clearing), without major functional impairment. The provider completes the brief PTQ vocal score (e.g., 8), notes the tic duration (4 months), and identifies a pre-existing diagnosis of ADHD (comorbidity). Entering these three variables into the online calculator yields a predicted progression probability of 76.3% (high-risk). Guided by the pathway, the provider would schedule enhanced monitoring with a follow-up visit in, for instance, 2–4 weeks—though the exact interval should be individualized. The provider would also initiate structured psychoeducation, screen for co-occurring anxiety or other comorbidities, and discuss early preparation for behavioral therapy (e.g., HRT or CBIT), or seek a tele-consultation with a child neurologist for medication planning if functional impairment emerges. For a low-risk example (predicted probability 26.5% in a child with tic duration 1 month, PTQ vocal score 0, and no comorbidity), the same non-specialist would confidently adopt watchful waiting with a follow-up at 6 months, thereby reducing unnecessary referrals and family anxiety.

On a broader level, this tool offers support for different stakeholders. For healthcare systems, it provides a risk-informed framework for resource allocation. For clinicians, particularly non-specialists, it serves as an auxiliary tool for standardized assessment and triage. For patients and families, clear risk stratification and corresponding management plans facilitate risk communication and health education. We acknowledge that factors beyond chronicity risk—such as current tic-related functional impairment—may independently warrant early intervention, even in children with low predicted progression risk. Our pathway incorporates these considerations through its “red flag” system and the principle of dynamic reassessment. Crucially, the tool is designed to aid, not replace, clinical judgment. Its outputs are suggestions regarding follow-up intensity and management priorities, not specific treatment prescriptions. The final decision to intervene must still be made by the clinician, integrating individualized factors such as functional impairment, comorbidities, family preferences, and psychosocial context34,46–51.

Our study has limitations that warrant consideration. First, the strict “initial visit” enrollment criterion (first medical visit after onset) likely yields a lower observed progression rate than seen in referral populations. While this criterion prioritizes internal validity by establishing a clean inception cohort, the model is thus most directly applicable to newly presenting PTD cases. Second, as a single-center cohort with a modest sample size, the generalizability of our model and stratification framework requires validation in independent, multi-center populations across diverse settings. Such external validation should also explore model performance in subgroups such as children with pure motor tics (PTQ vocal score = 0). Third, the predictors are clinically based, derived from interviews and rating scales. Future models incorporating genetic markers, neuroimaging features, or detailed measures of environmental stress may account for residual outcome heterogeneity. Finally, whether early intervention can modify the risk associated with longer tic duration remains unknown and warrants prospective investigation.

In conclusion, this study advances the management of PTD by providing a clinically applicable prediction model and a preliminary decision-support framework centered on risk stratification. This framework bridges quantitative risk assessment with dynamic management recommendations, aiming to facilitate early identification, guide resources to higher-risk children, and promote proactive comorbidity intervention. External validation in broader populations and real-world studies assessing the tool’s impact on long-term patient outcomes and healthcare efficiency will be essential next steps toward its implementation in clinical practice.
