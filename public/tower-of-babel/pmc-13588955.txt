Cramps as the initial presentation of CANVAS/RFC1-related disorder
Authors: Joaquín Machado, Jocelyn Zwicker, Pierre R Bourque, Hanns Lochmüller, Jodi Warman-Chardon, Alberto Aleman, Fien Oelbrandt, Ari Breiner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiallelic mutations in the RFC1 gene are associated with CANVAS syndrome (cerebellar ataxia, neuropathy and vestibular areflexia). Peripheral nerve involvement in the form of sensory neuronopathy and small fiber autonomic axonopathy is a core feature of its presentation. Motor manifestations, in the other hand, remain poorly understood and incompletely characterized.We present two cases referred for evaluation of muscle cramps as the main clinical symptom, who were ultimately diagnosed with CANVAS. We also conducted a literature review on cramps and other motor manifestations as the predominant symptom in this condition.This study highlights the recognition of cramps as a possible isolated motor manifestation, expanding the phenotypic spectrum of RFC1-related disease and the need of a high index of suspicion to accurately identified the condition.Keywords: RFC-1, CANVAS, cramps, motor manifestations, motor neuropathy

Introduction

Biallelic intronic pentanucleotide repeat expansions in the gene encoding the replication factor complex subunit 1 (RFC1) on chromosome 4 were initially identified as the cause of late-onset ataxia and a specific clinical syndrome encompassing cerebellar ataxia, sensory neuropathy and vestibular areflexia (CANVAS).1–3 RFC1 is a complex pentameric polymerase accessory protein that plays a crucial role in DNA replication and repair. The repetitive locus is highly polymorphic, with considerable variability in both the repetitive motif and size in the general population. 4 CANVAS is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner, with the reference allele being (AAAAG)11, while biallelic AAGGG expansions (typically 800-1,000 repeats) represent the most common and widespread pathogenic configuration. 3 Although most patients are homozygous for pathogenic expansions, a small proportion are compound heterozygous, harboring a pathogenic repeat expansion on one allele and a truncating variant (nonsense or frameshift) on the other, supporting a conditional loss-of-function mechanism.5–7 Other less frequent pathogenic motifs include the specific Māori and Australian population AAAGG (10-25)AAGGG(n)AAAGG (4-6) configuration8,9; and ACAGG(n), reported in the Asian Pacific region 10 and Dutch patients. 11 Several publications have demonstrated that both the number of repeats and different repeat motifs of the expansion may influence the phenotype.

The phenotypic spectrum has expanded remarkably since the initial description of CANVAS, giving rise to the term “RFC1-related disorders” to highlight its multisystemic nature.12–14 Neuropathology studies have demonstrated neuronal loss in the dorsal root ganglion (DRG), 15 leading to axonal degeneration and secondary demyelination in the posterior columns and peripheral sensory nerves. 16 In contrast, motor manifestations in RFC1-related disease remain incompletely characterized, including the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, potential genotype–phenotype correlations, and the natural history of motor progression. We describe two patients with biallelic RFC1 expansions presenting with muscle cramps as the chief complaint, and review the clinical and electromyographic manifestations of motor involvement in CANVAS/RFC1-related disorders.

Patient 1 is a 77-year-old female who presented with progressive hand muscle cramps starting at age 65, consisting of spasms causing contortion of the fingers occurring many times daily. She subsequently developed mild numbness and paresthesia in her hands and feet, subtle balance impairment, and difficulty climbing stairs. She also reported xerophthalmia, xerostomia, and a 25-pound involuntary weight loss. She had a chronic unexplained cough since her fourth decade, which was investigated extensively, without an identified cause. There was no family history of neurological disease or consanguinity.

Clinical examination revealed gaze-evoked nystagmus, catch-up saccades on head impulse testing, absent lower extremity reflexes, patchy non-length-dependent loss of pinprick sensation, decreased vibration perception in all four limbs, proprioceptive errors at the great toes, and a wide-based gait with impaired tandem. Motor bulk, tone, and power were normal. No cramps were observed during examination.

Patient 2 was a 56-year-old male who described symptoms beginning in his mid-40s with episodic loss of balance. At age 52, he developed increasingly frequent leg cramps in the thighs and calves (his principal complaint) occurring mostly at night, lasting 15–20 seconds, and requiring stretching for relief. He also noted numbness in the thighs and feet, and mild hand clumsiness. He had a chronic unexplained cough since age 23. His daughter was diagnosed with essential tremor at age 17. There was no consanguinity.

Examination showed normal ocular motility and saccades, normal motor bulk, tone, and strength, absent Achilles reflexes, non-length-dependent decreased pinprick, elevated vibration detection thresholds, mild dysmetria, positive Romberg sign, and inability to tandem walk. No cramps or fasciculations were observed.

Investigations

In both patients, motor nerve conduction studies were normal, whereas sensory nerve action potentials (SNAPs) were absent or markedly reduced in multiple nerves bilaterally, consistent with a non-length-dependent sensory neuronopathy (Table 1). Needle electromyography (EMG) showed minor chronic neurogenic abnormalities, described in Table 1. Brain MRI showed no cerebellar atrophy.

Vitamin B6 levels were elevated in both patients, due to vitamin supplements (which were subsequently discontinued). Patient 1 reported a slight subjective improvement in her gait instability after stopping vitamin B6, although clinical examination did not change. Patient 2 had a HbA1C of 6.2%, compatible with pre-diabetes (6.0-6.4%). Antinuclear antibodies were weakly positive in both patients, at low titer (1:80). Anti-Tissue Transglutaminase IgA antibody was weakly positive in patient 1 (23 CU, normal range: <20 CU). Other normal/negative lab results have been summarized in Table 1. Hereditary sensory autonomic neuropathy testing of 91 genes (including genes DNMT1, KIF1B, KIF5A, RAB7A, SPTLC1) was performed for patient 2 only and was unremarkable.

Video head impulse testing (vHIT) in Patient 1 revealed covert and overt corrective saccades suggesting bilateral peripheral vestibular involvement.

Genetic testing through the University of Chicago laboratory confirmed biallelic pathogenic AAGGG repeat expansions in the RFC1 gene in both patients by flanking-PCR (F-PCR) and repeat-primed PCR (RP-PCR) followed by capillary electrophoresis. Exact repeat length quantification was not available, as F-PCR is unable to amplify large expansions beyond a certain threshold, and techniques such as Southern blot or long-read sequencing were not performed.

Discussion

In this study, we described two patients with a bi-allelic repeat AAGGG repeat expansion in RFC1 who presented specifically for evaluation of muscle cramps as an isolated motor complaint, without weakness, atrophy, or fasciculations. Clinical and electrophysiological findings of non-length-dependent sensory neuronopathy, together with long-standing unexplained chronic cough and evidence of vestibular (Patient 1) or cerebellar (Patient 2) involvement, led to the diagnostic consideration and genetic confirmation of CANVAS.

A striking clinical feature of our cases is the fact that cramps were the initial prominent symptom that prompted medical assessment, without additional motor manifestations such as weakness, atrophy, or fasciculations. It should be noted that while cramps are not consistently cited as part of the clinical spectrum of CANVAS, previous reports have documented their occurrence, with prevalence estimates ranging from 18% to 42%.13,17,18

Thus, motor symptoms may trigger diagnostic testing for RFC1, particularly when accompanied with chronic cough and sensory findings.

Most patients with pathogenic RFC1 expansions have shown evidence of sensory neuropathy/neuronopathy. In accordance with prior series,19–21 our 2 patients presented with classical findings on nerve conduction studies, including diminished or absent sensory responses. There were no alterations in motor conduction testing. Minor chronic denervation findings were observed on the needle EMG study. Early reports have emphasized the classical manifestations of CANVAS: vestibular and cerebellar deficits, sensory neuropathy and cough. Motor manifestations (including signs of upper and lower motor neuron involvement) have been described in variable proportions among different series.13,17,22 However, in most published cases no precise distinction has been made regarding the temporal evolution of motor symptoms. The different objective manifestations are reported together, which makes it difficult to determine whether this may be a distinct feature or rather a consequence of the natural progression of the disease leading to motor involvement. Electrophysiological studies further support the heterogeneity of motor involvement. Unlike the almost invariable finding of sensory nerve involvement, possible additional motor involvement is much more heterogeneous, with reported prevalence ranging from 4% to 45% across different series (see Table 2). It could be hypothesized that disease-related factors, such as the time of evolution and type of mutation, may be contributing to this variability. Additionally, we are not aware of any previously described cases in which cramps were an isolated motor clinical manifestation.

Among the largest series that highlighted motor involvement in RFC1 patients is the report by Huin et al. 17 They assessed a cohort of 50 patients with sensory neuropathy that were recruited from a database of ataxic patients. 44/50 (88%) were carriers of a biallelic pathological (AAGGG)n expansion in the RFC1 gene. Of the 38 with adequate clinical details, 24 (63.1%) were found to have some form of motor neuron involvement - isolated upper motor neuron (UMN) involvement in 28.9%, lower motor neuron (LMN) involvement in 18.4%, and mixed UMN/LMN findings in 15.7%. In this study, mixed motor and sensory neuronopathy was observed in 18.9% of cases. Cramps were reported in 7 patients (18.4%); all of these had chronic cough. Remarkably, unlike our patients, all 7 patients with cramps had additional objective motor findings, including spasticity/hyperreflexia, myokymia, or fasciculations. This contrasts to our cases, as they represent a subtle motor presentation that could easily be overlooked or attributed to benign causes.

Malaquias et al., 2023 24 evaluated clinical and electrophysiological findings in 67 patients carrying biallelic expansions of AAGGG repeats in the RFC1 gene. They identified signs of motor pathway involvement in a total of 12/67 patients (18%). In their cohort, 4/67 patients (6%) had lower motor neuron involvement including 1/67 (1.5%) fasciculations, 4/67 (6%) distal weakness, 1/67 (1.5%) atrophy and 2/67 (3%) with spasticity on clinical examination; and 8/67 (13%) had reduced compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitudes, of whom only 1 patient had abnormal examination findings (distal weakness). They also reported chronic denervation in 1 patient, and 1 patient with isolated fasciculations on needle EMG. All patients had alterations in sensory nerve conduction studies, with a non-length-dependent pattern in 83% and a length-dependent pattern in the remaining 17%. Cramps were not described in this cohort.

Patients with expansions in the ACAGG motif may have a higher frequency of motor involvement (fasciculations, atrophy, and weakness).10,25,26 However, given that there have been very few reported cases with this specific genetic variant, no conclusive statements can be made at this time.11,27 Scriba et al., 2020 7 described 3 patients with biallelic expansions of the repeat unit (ACAGG), which met the classic criteria of CANVAS and, in addition, had fasciculations, elevated serum creatine kinase levels, and evidence of denervation on EMG and muscle biopsy. One of the patients also reported severe leg cramps - approximately 20 years before the onset of the other symptoms. Our findings are best compared with those reported by Izumi et al., 2024, 26 who described a Japanese pedigree comprising three siblings with biallelic ACAGG expansions. In that series, painful muscle cramps were the initial cardinal symptom. However, during a follow up period exceeding 10 years, the siblings eventually developed signs of lower motor neuron involvement, including amyotrophy and active and chronic denervation on EMG, as well as spasticity and fasciculations in one individual. This progression contrasts with our patients, in whom cramps were the sole manifestation of motor involvement at the time of diagnosis. Although only longitudinal follow-up will clarify whether this represents an early stage of disease, our observations—particularly in patient 1, whose cramps had a comparatively later onset—suggest the possibility of a distinct phenotype. Furthermore, patients in the Japanese series exhibited a broad spectrum of RFC1-related manifestations, including cerebellar dysfunction or atrophy, sensory neuropathy, chronic cough, and dysautonomia. In addition, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis 28 reinforce the association of the ACAGG repeat motif with muscle atrophy, weakness, and fasciculations and that male sex increases the odds of atrophy and fasciculations. The pooled prevalence of painful muscle cramps was 16%, typically manifesting before ataxia and predominantly affecting the lower limbs.

As was true in our two patients, the onset of prominent and progressive muscle cramping may raise a suspicion of motor neuron disease (MND) and trigger referral to a neuromuscular specialist. Kermorvant H. et al., 2022 29 reported a similar patient eventually diagnosed with CANVAS who was initially referred for evaluation of possible MND in the context of progressive cramps together with, unlike our cases, prominent fasciculations. The examination and electromyographic study revealed findings of both sensory and motor neuronopathy with fasciculations and a diffuse chronic neurogenic pattern on needle EMG study. However, the patient also reported chronic cough, had evidence of cerebellar atrophy, and on follow up he developed nystagmus and ataxia. Autopsy studies in RFC1-related disease have documented degeneration of anterior horn cells, motor neurons of the hypoglossal nucleus 30 and have shown potential signs of synaptic dysfunction at the junction between the upper and lower motor neurons. 17 Such neuropathologic alterations could underlie dysarthria, dysphagia and other motor manifestations that could raise a suspicion of MND. In fact, Schaub et al., 2024 31 analyzed a cohort of 107 MND patients and identified 3/107 (∼3%) with biallelic pathogenic repeat expansions (AAGGG) in RFC1. 2 of 3 fulfilled Gold Coast criteria for amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) with upper and lower motor neuron signs; and the third one had a diagnosis of primary lateral sclerosis (PLS), including spasticity, increased reflexes and pseudobulbar palsy. Subsequent investigation following identification of the pathogenic gene also detected findings of chronic sensory neuropathy (3/3), cough (2/3), autonomic dysfunction (2/3), cerebellar ataxia (1/3), and vestibulopathy (2/3). The investigators raised the question of whether a “pure or predominant motor neuron disease might be another extreme phenotype of RFC1 spectrum disorder”. In reference to this, there is conflicting evidence as to whether there is a causal or spurious association between the RFC1 gene and the risk of developing a genetic form of ALS. 32 Certainly, this is an area which requires further exploration in the future.

It should be acknowledged that muscle cramps can be associated with variants in several other genes, with or without hyperCKemia. Although CK levels were normal in both patients and no additional pathogenic variants were identified in patient 2 (who underwent a 91-gene HSAN panel), a comprehensive genetic evaluation for cramp-associated genes was not performed, and the contribution of additional genetic modifiers cannot be entirely excluded.

Conclusion

Our cases highlight that cramps could represent a distinct early or isolated manifestation of motor impairment, expanding the phenotypic spectrum of RFC1-related disease. A high index of suspicion is therefore warranted to recognize this presentation, which may be associated with characteristic clinical or electrophysiological features and enable accurate diagnosis.
