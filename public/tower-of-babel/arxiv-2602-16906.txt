Inverse problems in electrolysers
Giovanni S. Alberti, Wadim Gerner, Matteo Santacesaria

Abstract
Аннотация We investigate the relationship between the electric potential within an electrolyser cell and its temperature and particle concentrations. Specifically, we identify the measurements required to uniquely reconstruct the potential ϕ \phi as a function of temperature and concentration.



Keywords
: Electrolyser, Inverse problems, System of elliptic PDEs, Calderon type problems

2020 MSC
: 35J57, 35R30, 35Q92, 70F17, 92E99

1
Introduction

1.1
Motivation

Hydrogen is utilised in many industrial processes
[
23
,
40
,
35
,
31
,
5
]
, including ammonium production which is used in fertilisers
[
44
]
and as such sustains the worlds growing need for food. Many common ways to produce hydrogen contain carbon dioxide as a by-product
[
50
]
which leads to environmental pollution. Promising alternatives are the proton exchange membrane (PEM) electrolysis and the anion exchange membrane (AEM) electrolysis. Both do not produce any carbon dioxide nor other harmful by-products, provided renewable energy is used to generate the electricity required for the reactions. The hydrogen produced in this way is also known as green hydrogen
[
45
]
. PEM water electrolyser technology goes back to the 1960s
[
20
,
19
,
18
,
42
]
and is well-developed. Its main disadvantage is that its membrane contains materials such as platinum, iridium or ruthenium as a catalyst which makes these devices expensive
[
29
]
. In contrast, AEM electrolysis requires, in comparison, catalysts which are less costly and may therefore provide a good alternative to PEM electrolysers
[
29
]
. However, the research of AEM electrolysers began more recently
[
51
,
32
,
34
]
and as such has not yet reached the same maturity as its PEM counter part. The underlying working principles pertaining PEM electrolysers and AEM electrolysers are the same. Electricity is injected into a device containing water, which in turn is eventually split into hydrogen and oxygen by means of electrochemical reactions. The hydrogen can then be extracted for industrial applications.

The goal of the present work is to study a simplified PEM/AEM-electrolyser model and to understand what type of measurements must be performed in order to uniquely determine the relationship between the electric potential
\phi
within the electrolyser and the temperature and the distinct ion concentrations within the device. Knowing this could help to improve AEM electrolyser designs in order to increase their lifetime.

1.2
The inverse problem

1.2.1
The equations and the unknowns

We use the following equations to model our electrolyser device:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}c_{i}-\operatorname{div}(D_{i}\nabla c_{i})=g_{i}\text{ for }1\leq i\leq M\text{, }\operatorname{div}(\epsilon\nabla\phi)=q\cdot c.

They arise from the macroscopic Maxwell equations, which describe the electric potential, and Fick’s laws, which describe the evolution of the ion concentrations. These equations are coupled via the free charges and currents which are generated by the charged ions. See
Appendix

A
for a detailed derivation of these equations, as well as
Appendix

B
for a possible extension of our model.

The symbols in the equations have the following meanings:

•

M
is the number of distinct particle species in our electrolyser;

•

c_{i}=c_{i}(t,x)
denotes the concentration of the
i
-th particle species and we set
c=(c_{1},\dots,c_{M})
;

•

T=T(t,x)
is the (scalar) temperature of the system;

•

D_{i}=D_{i}(t,c,T,x)
is the scalar diffusion coefficient of the
i
-th species which we assume to be bounded below and above by some positive constants;

•

g_{i}=g_{i}(t,c,T,x)
is the source-term corresponding to the
i
-th species production/annihilation;

•

\epsilon=\epsilon(x)
is the electric permeability which is assumed to be bounded below and above by some positive constant;

•

\phi=\phi(t,c,T,x)
is the electric potential;

•

q_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is the electric charge of the
i
-species and
q\cdot c
is the standard Euclidean inner product of
q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{M})
and
c=(c_{1},\dots,c_{M})
.

A priori, we do not have knowledge of the diffusion coefficients
D_{i}
nor of the precise structure of the sources
g_{i}
, nor of the potential
\phi
. As indicated in (
A10
) some additional structural assumptions on the electric potential may in principle be made. In turn using the Nernst equation, Butler-Volmer equation and Tafel equation some more specific expressions for the distinct electric potential contributions may be derived
[
33
,
36
,
43
]
. But in any case measurements will be required to determine the precise values of the parameters appearing in these kind of models. In the present work we do not make any structural assumptions on the electric potential
\phi
except that we assume that it can be expressed as a function of time, position, temperature and the particle-concentrations.

In our application we know the number of distinct particle species
M
and their corresponding charges
q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{M})
(in Coulomb). We assume here that
\epsilon=\epsilon(x)
is a known quantity. In a zero order approximation one could assume that it is constant throughout the domain
\Omega
occupied by the electrolyser or that
\epsilon
is a simple function with constant (but possibly distinct) values in certain distinguished areas of the domain, for instance the membrane, the anode, the cathode and the area containing the electrolyte.

The inverse problem we want to deal with is to reconstruct
D_{i}
,
g_{i}
and
\phi
as functions of
t,c,T,x
from certain measurements, see the coming
Section

1.2.2
for a discussion about the type of measurements we can perform. We notice that we have
M+1
equations in total and
2M+1
unknown functions (
M
functions
D_{i}
,
M
functions
g_{i}
and the potential
\phi
). It is therefore not clear to what extent all these quantities may be reconstructed; possibly, some additional structural assumptions on the sources may have to be made in order to be able to recover all these quantities uniquely. The main reconstruction result of the present work focuses on the special case
g_{i}=0
for all
i
, see
Section

1.2.3
, to gain first insights into this inverse problem, while the case of non-zero sources is left for future investigations.

1.2.2
The measurements

To be able to obtain information about the boundary values of
\phi
we can, as discussed in detail in
Section

A.2
, use a voltmeter to measure the voltage between any two points on the boundary. Current density-voltage profiles are also used in AEM electrolyser cells to evaluate their performance
[
33
]
. As we shall see, boundary measurements alone are not enough to determine the functional dependence of the potential
\phi
in the interior of the cell, cf.
2.3
. Therefore, we will consider a hybrid inverse problem where we assume that it is also possible for us to measure the temperature at interior points of the domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
occupied by the electrolyser cell. We point out that it is indeed possible to measure the temperature in the interior of electrolyser cells in practice
[
25
]
.

In addition to these measurements we still need to specify the boundary measurements and boundary conditions which we may perform and prescribe for
c
and
T
, respectively.

Let us start with the temperature. We are able to prescribe
T|_{\partial\Omega}
by controlling the temperature of the electrolyte at the boundary. In addition, for any given boundary point
x\in\partial\Omega
we can measure the temperature at nearby points that are displaced in the normal direction
\mathcal{N}(x)
, i.e. at
x+r\mathcal{N}(x)
for
0<r\ll 1
, where
r
is the distance of this point from
\partial\Omega
. For small
r
we can then take

\displaystyle\frac{T(x+r\mathcal{N}(x))-T(x)}{r}\approx\mathcal{N}(x)\cdot\nabla T(x)

and hence measure the normal trace of the temperature on
\partial\Omega
.

Regarding the concentrations, we may consider here the simplest electrolyser setup, cf.
[
34
]
, which contains
\mathrm{H_{2}O}
,
\mathrm{OH}^{-}
,
\operatorname{O}_{2}
,
\operatorname{H}_{2}
and
\operatorname{e}^{-}
:

\displaystyle\text{Anode: }2\text{OH}^{-}\rightarrow\text{H}_{2}\text{O}+\frac{\text{O}_{2}}{2}+2\text{e}^{-}

(1.1)

\displaystyle\text{Cathode: }2\text{H}_{2}\text{O}+2\text{e}^{-}\rightarrow\text{H}_{2}+2\text{OH}^{-}

(1.2)

\displaystyle\text{Overall reaction: }\text{H}_{2}\text{O}\rightarrow\text{H}_{2}+\frac{\text{O}_{2}}{2}

(1.3)

Regarding the boundary conditions that we may control let us first consider
\mathrm{H_{2}O}
. We can control the water inflow (at least on a part of
\partial\Omega
), which amounts to prescribing
\mathcal{N}\cdot(D_{\mathrm{H_{2}O}}\nabla c_{\mathrm{H_{2}O}})
(notice that in our simplified model we assume that the liquid water also follows Fick’s law). In addition, in an ideal situation, all the
\operatorname{OH}^{-}
that is produced at the cathode recombines at the anode, cf. (
1.2
),(
1.1
), and so no
\operatorname{OH}^{-}
escapes the system, i.e.
\mathcal{N}\cdot\left(D_{\operatorname{OH}^{-}}\nabla c_{\operatorname{OH}^{-}}\right)=0
. Concerning
\operatorname{O}_{2}
and
\operatorname{H}_{2}
we observe that they will be removed from the system through a pipe and so, by adjusting the strength at which they will be removed from the system, we may also prescribe (at a boundary portion) the values of
\mathcal{N}\cdot\left(D_{\operatorname{H}_{2}}\nabla c_{\operatorname{H_{2}}}\right)
and
\mathcal{N}\cdot\left(D_{\operatorname{O}_{2}}\nabla c_{\operatorname{O_{2}}}\right)
. Finally, regarding
\operatorname{e}^{-}
, the induced current that we can control corresponds to
\mathcal{N}\cdot\left(D_{\operatorname{e}^{-}}\nabla c_{\operatorname{e}^{-}}\right)
(where we assume here that the electrons also follow Fick’s law).

We conclude that in each case
\mathcal{N}\cdot(D_{i}\nabla c_{i})
can be prescribed (at a boundary portion) or is fixed and known a priori. We will assume here for simplicity that we have the freedom to prescribe
\mathcal{N}\cdot(D_{i}\nabla c_{i})
on all of
\partial\Omega
and for all
1\leq i\leq M
.

Further, near the entrance of the pipe/exit of the electrolyser at which the hydrogen
\operatorname{H}_{2}
and oxygen
\operatorname{O}_{2}
leave the electrolyser cell, we may measure the ion-concentrations so that we may measure the responses
c_{i}|_{P}
for some boundary portion
P\subset\partial\Omega
. To simplify the situation we assume throughout this work that
P=\partial\Omega
.

To summarise, we may prescribe and measure the following quantities

Prescribe:

\displaystyle\left(T,\mathcal{N}\cdot(D_{1}\nabla c_{1}),\dots,\mathcal{N}\cdot(D_{M}\nabla c_{M})\right)\text{ on }[0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega,

(1.4)

Measurements I:

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{N}\cdot\nabla T,c_{1},\dots,c_{M}\right)\text{ on }[0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega,

(1.5)

Measurements II:

\displaystyle\phi(t,c(t,x),T(t,x),x)-\phi(t,c(t,y),T(t,y),y)\text{ for all }x,y\in\partial\Omega,t\geq 0,

(1.6)

Measurements III:

\displaystyle T(t,x)\text{ for all }t\geq 0\text{, }x\in\Omega.

(1.7)

In practice, one has of course only a finite sample of measurements but we will assume here that we have access to infinitely many measurements.

We notice that in addition to prescribing the boundary values on
[0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega
we must also prescribe
T
and
\mathcal{N}\cdot\left(D_{i}\nabla c_{i}\right)
at the initial time
t=0
. One way to do this is to consider the situation in which an electrolyser cell is about to be operated for the first time. In that case
c_{\operatorname{OH}^{-}}=0
,
c_{\operatorname{e}^{-}}=0
,
c_{\operatorname{H}_{2}}=0
,
c_{\mathrm{H_{2}O}}=0
(if we assume that no water has yet been filled in the electrolyser). If the electrolyser is filled with air, then the
\operatorname{O}_{2}
concentration may be assumed to be constant throughout
\Omega
and given by the average
\operatorname{O}_{2}
concentration in the air. Similarly, the temperature will be in some equilibrium and may be in an idealised model assumed to be constant throughout
\Omega
at an initial time.

1.2.3
The static source-free case

In the present work we want to initiate the analysis of the inverse problem described in
Section

1.2.1
assuming that we can prescribe and measure the quantities of interest as described in (
1.4
)-(
1.7
). We want to assume here that our electrolyser cell reached an equilibrium state and therefore all quantities involved are time-independent and satisfy the corresponding static equations

\displaystyle-\operatorname{div}(D_{i}(c(x),T(x),x)\nabla c_{i}(x))=g_{i}(c(x),T(x),x)\text{ and }\operatorname{div}(\epsilon(x)\nabla(\phi(c(x),T(x),x))=q\cdot c(x).

(1.8)

Further, we will make at some points the (unphysical) assumption that
g_{i}=0
for all
i
. The situation in which
g_{i}\neq 0
is much more complex than its source-free counter part.
We hence focus at some instances on the following equations

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(D_{i}(c(x),T(x),x)\nabla c_{i}(x))=0\text{, }1\leq i\leq M\text{ and }\operatorname{div}(\epsilon(x)\nabla(\phi(c(x),T(x),x))=q\cdot c(x)

(1.9)

where
\epsilon=\epsilon(x)
is assumed to be known,
D_{i}=D_{i}(c,T,x)
and
\phi=\phi(c,T,x)
are unknown functions of the concentrations, the temperature and the position. Further
q\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is a known constant vector.

The inverse problem.

We consider the reconstruction of the functions
D_{i}
and
\phi
according to:

Prescribe:

\displaystyle\left(T|_{\partial\Omega},c_{1}|_{\partial\Omega},\dots,c_{M}|_{\partial\Omega}\right),

(1.10)

Measurements I:

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{N}\cdot\nabla T,\mathcal{N}\cdot(D_{1}\nabla c_{1}),\dots,\mathcal{N}\cdot(D_{M}\nabla c_{M})\right),

(1.11)

Measurements II:

\displaystyle\phi(c(x),T(x),x)-\phi(c(y),T(y),y)\text{ for all }x,y\in\partial\Omega,

(1.12)

Measurements III:

\displaystyle T(x)\text{ for }x\in\Omega.

(1.13)

Note that, compared to (
1.4
)-(
1.7
), we prescribe here the Dirichlet data and measure the Neumann data. This is due to the fact that from a mathematical perspective it is easier to deal with the quasi-linear forward problem for Dirichlet-boundary conditions rather than Neumann boundary conditions.
We shall see that using boundary measurements alone, (
1.11
) and (
1.12
), we are solely able to reconstruct the potential
\phi
within an ‘‘infinitesimal’’ neighbourhood of
\partial\Omega
, see
2.4
, and that it is not possible to reconstruct
\phi
at interior points unless one takes into account additional measurements, such as interior temperature measurements (
1.13
).

Main results

For any subset
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
, we denote the space of
\alpha
-Hölder continuous functions by
C^{0,\alpha}(U)
, with the convention
C^{0,0}(U)\equiv C^{0}(U)
. We denote the space of functions in
C^{0,\alpha}(U)
with finite
\alpha
-Hölder-norm by
C^{0,\alpha}_{b}(U)
.
If
U
is open, we set

\displaystyle\dot{C}^{1}_{b}(\overline{U}):=\left\{f\in C^{1}(U)\mid\sup_{x\in U}|\nabla f(x)|<\infty\text{ and }\nabla f\text{ extends continuously to }\overline{U}\right\}\text{ and }

\displaystyle\dot{C}_{b}^{1,1}(\overline{U}):=\left\{f\in\dot{C}^{1}_{b}(\overline{U})\mid\sup_{x,y\in U,x\neq y}\frac{|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)|}{|x-y|}<\infty\right\}.

Throughout the paper, we assume the following ellipticity conditions in
\Omega
:

\epsilon\geq\lambda,\hskip 20.00003ptD_{i}\geq\lambda\;\text{for every $i=1,\dots,M$,}\hskip 20.00003pt\partial_{s}\phi\geq\lambda

(2.1)

for some
\lambda>0
, where
\phi=\phi(p,s,x)
,
(p,s,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{M+1}\times\Omega
. In the following, we consider the quantity
\phi(c(x),T(x),x)
, so that we essentially demand that the potential is monotonically increasing as a function of the temperature (see Section
A.3
for some motivations on this condition).
Upon applying the chain rule in (
1.8
) to
\phi(c(x),T(x),x)
, it becomes clear that the assumptions in (
2.1
) make our system of equations elliptic.

2.1
The forward problem

Before we state the result we recall here that we consider the static equations

\displaystyle-\operatorname{div}(D_{i}(c(x),T(x),x)\nabla c_{i}(x))=g_{i}(c(x),T(x),x)\text{ and }\operatorname{div}(\epsilon(x)\nabla(\phi(c(x),T(x),x))=q\cdot c(x).

(2.2)

The following result guarantees the existence of weak solutions for the forward problem.

Theorem 2.1

(Existence of weak solutions)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded
C^{1}
-domain,
M\in\mathbb{N}
,
q\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
,
\phi\in\dot{C}_{b}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{M+1}\times\overline{\Omega})
,
D_{i}\in C^{0}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{M+1}\times\overline{\Omega})
,
g_{i}\in C^{0}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{M+1}\times\overline{\Omega})
for
1\leq i\leq M
,
\epsilon\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Suppose further that
(\epsilon,D_{i},\phi)
satisfy the ellipticity condition (
2.1
). Then for every
(\gamma,\tau)\in\left(W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\partial\Omega)\right)^{M+1}
there exists at least one weak solution
(c,T)\in\left(H^{1}(\Omega)\right)^{M+1}
of the following boundary value problem

\displaystyle-\operatorname{div}(D_{i}(c,T,x)\nabla c_{i})=g_{i}(c,T,x)\text{ and }\operatorname{div}(\epsilon\nabla(\phi(c,T,x)))=q\cdot c\text{ in }\Omega\text{, }(c,T)|_{\partial\Omega}=(\gamma,\tau)

(2.3)

where
c=(c_{1},\dots,c_{M})
,
1\leq i\leq M
.

The uniqueness of solutions is more subtle. We obtain the following partial result regarding the uniqueness of weak solution, see also
3.4
for an example of a related system of equations where uniqueness fails.

Theorem 2.2

(Uniqueness of source-free weak solutions with constant boundary conditions)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded domain with
C^{1}
-boundary and
M\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
D_{i}\in C^{0}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{M+1}\times\overline{\Omega})
for
1\leq i\leq M
,
q\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
,
\epsilon\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
\phi\in\dot{C}_{b}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{M+1}\times\overline{\Omega})
. Suppose further that
(\epsilon,D_{i},\phi)
satisfy the ellipticity condition (
2.1
). Then for every
(\gamma,\tau)\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\times W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\partial\Omega)\subset\left(W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\partial\Omega)\right)^{M+1}
there is a unique weak solution
(c,T)\in\left(H^{1}(\Omega)\right)^{M+1}
solving the boundary value problem

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}\left(D_{i}(c,T,x)\nabla c_{i}\right)=0\text{ and }\operatorname{div}(\epsilon\nabla(\phi(c,T,x)))=q\cdot c\text{ in }\Omega\text{, }(c,T)|_{\partial\Omega}=(\gamma,\tau).

(2.4)

In particular,
c=\gamma
throughout
\Omega
.

2.2
The inverse problem

2.2.1
Boundary measurements

Let us first discuss how to formally define boundary measurements.

If the sources
g_{i}
are square integrable we conclude from (
2.2
) that
\operatorname{div}(D_{i}\nabla c_{i})\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
D_{i}\nabla c_{i}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
so that we can make sense of the normal traces,
[
15
, I §2 Theorem 2.5]
,
\mathcal{N}\cdot\left(D_{i}\nabla c_{i}\right)\in W^{-\frac{1}{2},2}(\partial\Omega)
.
The temperature is of class
H^{1}(\Omega)
as a weak solution so that
\nabla T\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. It follows further from (
2.2
) by means of the chain rule that under the assumptions of the upcoming
2.3
we have
\operatorname{div}(\nabla T)\in L^{1}(\Omega)
. Consequently one can similarly make sense of the normal trace
\mathcal{N}\cdot\nabla T
as an element of
W^{-\frac{1}{p},p}(\partial\Omega)
, the topological dual space of
W^{1-\frac{1}{p},p}(\partial\Omega)
, for any
p>3
.

This allows us to consider the corresponding Cauchy-data of the solutions associated with (
2.2
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}:=\{(c|_{\partial\Omega},T|_{\partial\Omega},\mathcal{N}\cdot D_{1}(c,T,x)\nabla c_{1},\dots,\mathcal{N}\cdot(D_{M}(c,T,x)\nabla c_{M}),\mathcal{N}\cdot\nabla T)\mid(c,T)\text{ solves (\ref{S2E1})}\},

(2.5)

with
\mathcal{C}\subset\bigl(W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\partial\Omega)\bigr)^{M+1}\times\bigl(W^{-\frac{1}{2},2}(\partial\Omega)\bigr)^{M}\times W^{-\frac{1}{p},p}(\partial\Omega)
for any
p>3
.

The collection of Cauchy-data depends on
D_{i},g_{i},\phi,q
and
\epsilon
. Whenever of relevance, we will indicate such a dependence by writing for example
\mathcal{C}[\phi]
. It is not difficult to see that if
D_{i}\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
and
g_{i}\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
depend on position alone, then there exists a unique solution of (
2.2
) for any prescribed Dirichlet boundary conditions so that in this case we may view the Cauchy-data as a Dirichlet-to-Neumann map

\Lambda_{\operatorname{DN}}:\bigl(W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\partial\Omega)\bigr)^{M+1}\rightarrow\bigl(W^{-\frac{1}{2},2}(\partial\Omega)\bigr)^{M}\times W^{-\frac{1}{p},p}(\partial\Omega).

The following result shows that boundary measurements alone cannot reconstruct the electric potential.

Theorem 2.3

(Necessity of interior measurements)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded domain with connected
C^{1}
-boundary, let
M\in\mathbb{N}
and for
1\leq i\leq M
,
g_{i}\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
,
D_{i}\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
,
\epsilon\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
,
\phi,\Phi\in\dot{C}^{1,1}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{M+1}\times\overline{\Omega})
and
q\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
. Further, suppose that
(\epsilon,D_{i},\phi)
and
(\epsilon,D_{i},\Phi)
satisfy the ellipticity condition (
2.1
). Then the following two sets of properties are equivalent:

i)

(a)

For all
(\gamma,\tau)\in\left(W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\partial\Omega)\right)^{M+1}
we have

\phi(\gamma(x),\tau(x),x)-\phi(\gamma(y),\tau(y),y)=\Phi(\gamma(x),\tau(x),x)-\Phi(\gamma(y),\tau(y),y)\text{ for $\mathcal{H}^{2}$-a.e. $x,y\in\partial\Omega$;}

(b)

\Lambda_{\operatorname{DN}}[\epsilon,D_{i},g_{i},\phi]=\Lambda_{\operatorname{DN}}[\epsilon,D_{i},g_{i},\Phi]
.

ii)

(a)

\nabla_{x}(\phi(p,s,x)-\Phi(p,s,x))=0
for all
(p,s,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{M+1}\times\partial\Omega
where
\nabla_{x}
denotes the full Euclidean gradient with respect to
x
;

(b)

there is some
r\in\mathbb{R}
such that for all
(p,s,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{M+1}\times\partial\Omega
we have
\phi(p,s,x)-\Phi(p,s,x)=r
.

Remark 2.4
.

i)

2.3
tells us that boundary measurements alone are enough to reconstruct the values of the potential along
\partial\Omega
(up to a constant) and in addition, we can reconstruct the full gradient of the potential along
\partial\Omega
. This means that while we cannot reconstruct the potential
\phi
at any fixed interior point, the behaviour of
\phi
as we approach the boundary is determined through the gradient of
\phi
by the boundary measurements. In this sense boundary measurements can not only reconstruct the values of
\phi
along the boundary, but also in an ‘‘infinitesimal’’ neighbourhood around it.

ii)

If
\phi
is a potential,
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
is a bump function and
\widetilde{\phi}\in\dot{C}^{1,1}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{M+1}\times\overline{\Omega})
is any other function, then
\Phi:=\phi+\psi\cdot\widetilde{\phi}
will satisfy conditions (ii,a) and (ii,b) from which we can conclude that their Dirichlet-to-Neumann map coincide. This shows that in general no information about the behaviour of the potential at interior points can be obtained from boundary measurements alone.

2.2.2
Boundary and internal measurements

The goal of the present section is to show that when interior temperature measurements are taken into account, then the potential as well as the diffusion coefficients can be uniquely reconstructed, at least in the source-free situation.

In the following we will consider the static source-free problem

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(D_{i}(c,T,x)\nabla c_{i})=0\text{ and }\operatorname{div}(\epsilon\nabla(\phi(c,T,x))=q\cdot c\text{ in }\Omega.

(2.6)

The idea is to replace the temperature boundary measurements
\mathcal{N}\cdot\nabla T
by interior temperature measurements. The boundary measurements of interest are therefore captured by the following (reduced) Cauchy-data set

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\operatorname{red}}:=\{(c|_{\partial\Omega},T|_{\partial\Omega},\mathcal{N}\cdot D_{1}(c,T,x)\nabla c_{1},\dots,\mathcal{N}\cdot D_{M}(c,T,x)\nabla c_{M}\mid(c,T)\text{ solves (\ref{S2E3}}\},

(2.7)

which is a subset of
\left(W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\partial\Omega)\right)^{M+1}\times\left(W^{-\frac{1}{2},2}(\partial\Omega)\right)^{M}
. To indicate the dependence of
\mathcal{C}_{\operatorname{red}}
on the
D_{i}
and
\phi
we will also write
\mathcal{C}_{\operatorname{red}}[D,\phi]
where we use here the notation

.

The main result of this subsection is the following.

Theorem 2.5

(Unique reconstruction for the inverse problem with boundary and internal data)
.

Let

For all

Remark 2.6
.

It is well-known that the electrostatic (scalar) potential

is determined only up to constants, so that

is the best possible result in the sense that it shows that the diffusion coefficients are uniquely determined by our hybrid measurements and that the potential can be recovered up to a constant.

2.3
Comparison to other inverse problems in the mathematical literature

The problem of reconstructing the diffusion coefficients
D_{i}(c,T,x)
and the electric potential
\phi(c,T,x)
from boundary measurements and internal temperature data is at the intersection of three very active fields: the classical Calderón problem, inverse problems for quasilinear elliptic equations, and hybrid (coupled-physics) inverse problems.

The Classical Calderón Problem.

The linear prototype for the electrostatic sub-problem is Calderón’s problem
[
9
]
, which aims to recover a spatially varying conductivity from the Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DN) map. Global uniqueness in dimensions
n\geq 3
was established by Sylvester and Uhlmann using Complex Geometric Optics (CGO) solutions
[
48
]
. Subsequent research relaxed regularity assumptions
[
39
,
2
,
22
]
and addressed partial boundary data
[
27
]
. However, boundary-only measurements for elliptic equations inherently suffer from logarithmic stability
[
1
]
, necessitating internal data or strong prior assumptions for practical reconstructions.

Quasilinear and Nonlinear Elliptic PDEs.

Because
D_{i}
and
\phi
depend on the state variables
c
and
T
, the system governing the electrolyser is inherently quasilinear. The foundational uniqueness result for recovering solution-dependent coefficients from the DN map was established by Sun
[
47
]
, who introduced the method of linearisation at constant boundary data. This was later extended to anisotropic and gradient-dependent media
[
46
,
24
]
. Recently, the paradigm of higher-order linearisation has demonstrated that nonlinearities can actually facilitate uniqueness. Cârstea et al.
[
10
]
developed constructive methods for recovering quasilinear coefficients by exploiting the nonlinear interactions of CGO solutions, and these results have recently been expanded to partial data settings
[
28
]
.

Hybrid Inverse Problems and Coupled Systems.

To overcome the severe ill-posedness of boundary measurements, hybrid inverse problems utilise internal functionals (e.g., power or current densities). Seminal works by Bal and Uhlmann
[
3
]
demonstrated that internal data transforms the elliptic inverse problem into a well-posed transport problem, yielding Lipschitz stability. In our formulation, the internal temperature
T(x)
naturally serves as this internal data. Knowing the solution to one of the coupled equations everywhere inside the domain provides powerful constraints, conceptually linking our setup to inverse source and heat conduction problems with internal sensors
[
4
,
37
,
30
]
.

Finally, the primary reaction-diffusion-Poisson model considered here is very close to Poisson-Nernst-Planck (PNP) systems, a connection that becomes explicit when electric forces are incorporated into the particle flux (as discussed in Appendix B). Inverse problems in the coupled electrochemical context frequently aim to recover specific diffusion parameters or doping profiles from boundary data
[
8
,
6
,
7
]
. Our work synthesises these analytical domains: we leverage the analytical tools of nonlinear elliptic theory (via linearisation) while exploiting the structural constraints provided by hybrid internal measurements to overcome the fundamental non-uniqueness of the boundary-only problem, offering a robust framework for identifying solution-dependent parameters in coupled systems.

3.1.1
Existence of weak solutions

We start by examining a simplified system of equations. Once this is done we will prove that our original system of equations can be reduced to this simplified system.

Lemma 3.1

(Existence of weak solutions I)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

for all

and

for all

,

\displaystyle-\operatorname{div}(\nu_{i}(\eta,x)\nabla\eta_{i}(x))=G_{i}(\eta(x),x)\text{ in }\Omega,\hskip 20.00003pt\eta|_{\partial\Omega}=\gamma.

(3.1)

Доказательство
.

Let
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{N})\in\bigl(W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\partial\Omega)\bigr)^{N}
. We split the proof into several steps.

Step 1: The solution to (
3.1
) as a fixed point of a suitable operator
A
.
By standard linear elliptic theory, for every
v\in\left(L^{2}(\Omega)\right)^{N}
there exist unique weak solutions

,

, of the boundary value problem

\displaystyle-\operatorname{div}(\nu_{i}(v(x),x)\nabla w_{i}(x))=G_{i}(v(x),x)\text{ in }\Omega,\hskip 20.00003ptw_{i}|_{\partial\Omega}=\gamma_{i}.

(3.2)

We can therefore consider the solution operator

\displaystyle A:\left(\left(L^{2}(\Omega)\right)^{N},\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)\rightarrow\left(\left(H^{1}(\Omega)\right)^{N},\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right),

(3.3)

which maps
v\in\left(L^{2}(\Omega)\right)^{N}
to the unique solution
w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{N})
of (
3.2
). We observe that solutions of (
3.1
) correspond to fix points of

. We verify now that the operator

satisfies the conditions of Schaefer’s fixed point theorem, cf.
[
12
, Chapter 9.2.2 Theorem 4]

is continuous, compact and that the set
\left\{v\in\left(L^{2}(\Omega)\right)^{N}\mid v=\lambda A(v)\text{ for some }0\leq\lambda\leq 1\right\}
is bounded.

Step 2:
A
is continuous.
Let
v,\widetilde{v}\in\left(L^{2}(\Omega)\right)^{N}
with associated solutions
w=A(v),\widetilde{w}=A(\widetilde{v})
. Let us set
\overline{w}:=w-\widetilde{w}
(and denote the components by
w_{i},\widetilde{w}_{i}
, and
\overline{w}_{i}
, respectively). By (
3.2
), we have

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}&\nu_{i}(v,x)\nabla\overline{w}_{i}\cdot\nabla\overline{w}_{i}dx=\int_{\Omega}\nu_{i}(v,x)\nabla w_{i}\cdot\nabla\overline{w}_{i}dx-\int_{\Omega}\nu_{i}(v,x)\nabla\widetilde{w}_{i}\cdot\nabla\overline{w}_{i}dx\\
&=\int_{\Omega}\nu_{i}(v,x)\nabla w_{i}\cdot\nabla\overline{w}_{i}dx-\int_{\Omega}\nu_{i}(\widetilde{v},x)\nabla\widetilde{w}_{i}\cdot\nabla\overline{w}_{i}dx+\int_{\Omega}\left(\nu_{i}(\widetilde{v},x)-\nu_{i}(v,x)\right)\nabla\widetilde{w}_{i}\cdot\nabla\overline{w}_{i}dx\\
&=\int_{\Omega}(G_{i}(v,x)-G_{i}(\widetilde{v},x))\overline{w}_{i}dx+\int_{\Omega}(\nu_{i}(\widetilde{v},x)-\nu_{i}(v,x))\nabla\widetilde{w}_{i}\cdot\nabla\overline{w}_{i}dx,\end{split}

where we used that
\overline{w}_{i}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Using that
\nu_{*}\leq\nu_{i}
by assumption, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Poincaré’s inequality, we find

\displaystyle\nu_{*}\|\nabla\overline{w}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\|(\nu_{i}(\widetilde{v},\cdot)-\nu_{i}(v,\cdot))\nabla\widetilde{w}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{\|G_{i}(v,\cdot)-G_{i}(\widetilde{v},\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{D}(\Omega)}},

denotes the first Dirichlet-eigenvalue of

Let

\displaystyle\nu_{*}\|\nabla\widetilde{w}_{i}-\nabla w^{k}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\|(\nu_{i}(\widetilde{v},\cdot)-\nu_{i}(v_{k},\cdot))\nabla\widetilde{w}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{\|G_{i}(v_{k},\cdot)-G_{i}(\widetilde{v},\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{D}(\Omega)}}.

We will now show that every subsequence of
(v_{k})_{k}

(A(v_{k}))_{k}
to
A(\tilde{v})
. To this end, observe that every subsequence of
(v_{k})_{k}
has yet another subsequence (denoted in the same way) for which

\displaystyle\|\nabla\widetilde{w}_{i}-\nabla w^{k}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }k\rightarrow 0\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq N.

Then the Poincaré inequality yields
\|A(v_{k})-A(\widetilde{v})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\|\widetilde{w}-w_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\rightarrow 0
, which implies the continuity of
A
.

Step 3:
A
is compact.
Let

, and

\displaystyle\nu_{*}\|\nabla w_{i}-\nabla w^{0}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(\nu)\|\nabla w^{0}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\widetilde{C}(\Omega,G)(1+\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)})

\displaystyle\|w-w^{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq c(\Omega,\nu,G,\gamma)\left(1+\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)

(3.6)

and in turn
\|w\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\|w^{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|w-w^{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
. This proves that if

is

-bounded, then
(A(v_{k}))_{k}
is bounded in

Step 4: the set
\left\{v\in\left(L^{2}(\Omega)\right)^{N}\mid v=\lambda A(v)\text{ for some }0\leq\lambda\leq 1\right\}
is

-bounded.
Assume that

\displaystyle\nu_{*}\|\nabla w_{N}-\nabla w^{0}_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(\nu,\gamma,\Omega)+C_{1}(\Omega,G,\gamma)\left(1+\|\hat{v}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)

where

Step 5: (

The existence of a weak solution to (

∎

Remark 3.2

\phi(c(x),T(x),x)=\phi(c(x),h(c(x),\sigma(x),x),x)=\sigma(x)
and therefore

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(\epsilon\nabla(\phi(c,T,x)))=\operatorname{div}(\epsilon\nabla\sigma)=\operatorname{div}(\nu_{M+1}\nabla\sigma)=c\cdot q\text{ in }\Omega.

Lastly,
T|_{\partial\Omega}=h(c|_{\partial\Omega},\sigma|_{\partial\Omega},x)=h(\gamma,\phi(\gamma,\tau,x),x)=\tau
, so that
(c,T)
is a weak solution of (
2.3
), as desired.
∎

3.1.2
Uniqueness of weak solutions

In this subsection we focus on the source-free system (
2.6
) with constant Dirichlet-boundary conditions. The uniqueness of these solutions will be used during the second part of the proof of
2.5
.

Proof of
2.2
.

We recall that
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{M})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is a constant and observe first that we have the identity

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(D_{i}(\gamma(x),T(x),x)\nabla\gamma_{i})=0\text{ in $\Omega$},\hskip 20.00003ptT\in H^{1}(\Omega).

Let us then define
\phi_{0}(x):=\phi(\gamma,\tau(x),x)\in W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\partial\Omega)
, where
\tau\in W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\partial\Omega)
is the prescribed boundary condition for the temperature, recall (
2.4
). Further, let
L^{-1}_{\epsilon}
denote the solution operator corresponding to the linear elliptic PDE

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(\epsilon\nabla\eta)=v\text{ in }\Omega,\hskip 10.00002pt\eta|_{\partial\Omega}=\phi_{0},

(3.9)

so that
L^{-1}_{\epsilon}(v)=\eta
is the unique weak
H^{1}(\Omega)
solution of (
3.9
). Now, let
h
denote the inverse of
\phi
as in
3.3
. It is then easy to see that

\displaystyle(\gamma,\theta):=(\gamma,h(\gamma,L^{-1}_{\epsilon}(\gamma\cdot q)(x),x))

provides a solution to (
2.4
). Suppose now that
(c,T)
is any other solution to (
2.4
). Then, similarly as in the proof of
3.1
, we find

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}D_{i}(c,T,x)\nabla c_{i}\cdot\nabla c_{i}dx=\int_{\Omega}D_{i}(c,T,x)\nabla c_{i}\cdot\nabla(c_{i}-\gamma_{i})dx=0,

where we used that the
\gamma_{i}
are constant, that
c_{i}-\gamma_{i}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and that
\operatorname{div}(D_{i}(c,T,x)\nabla c_{i})=0
in the weak sense. Further,
0<\lambda\leq D_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq M
from which we conclude that
\|\nabla c_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0
and consequently
c_{i}=\gamma_{i}
in
\Omega
for all
1\leq i\leq M
and hence
c=\gamma
.

Further, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(\epsilon\nabla(\phi(c,T,x)))=q\cdot c=q\cdot\gamma\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and }\hskip 10.00002pt\phi(c,T,x)|_{\partial\Omega}=\phi_{0},

so that
\phi(c,T,x)=\phi(\gamma,T,x)
coincides with the unique weak solution to (
3.9
) with
v=q\cdot\gamma
. By uniqueness, we find that
\phi(\gamma,T,x)=L^{-1}_{\epsilon}(q\cdot\gamma)
, and consequently

T=h(\gamma,\phi(\gamma,T,x),x)=h(\gamma,L^{-1}_{\epsilon}(q\cdot\gamma)(x),x)=\theta.

So overall
(c,T)=(\gamma,\theta)
, and the solution is unique.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

There is no uniqueness in the general setting of
3.1
, as can already be seen in the scalar setting
N=1
. Suppose that
\partial\Omega\in C^{2}
. Let
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
be an eigenfunction of the Dirichlet Laplacian in
\Omega
, with eigenvalue
\lambda>0
. By elliptic regularity,
u\in H^{2}(\Omega)\subset C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
. We can then let
\rho\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a smooth bump function which is compactly supported and identical
1
on the image of
u
. We can then set
\nu_{1}\equiv 1
and
G_{1}(\eta,x):=\lambda\eta\rho(\eta)
. Then
G_{1}
is globally bounded and the boundary value problem

\displaystyle-\Delta\eta=G_{1}(\eta,x)=\lambda\eta(x)\rho(\eta(x))\text{, }\eta|_{\partial\Omega}=0

admits the solutions
\eta\equiv 0
and
\eta=u
. Thus, in the presence of sources additional constraints need to be imposed to guarantee uniqueness. The same example extends to the setting
N\geq 2
by selecting
\nu_{1}\equiv 1
,
G_{1}(\eta,x):=\lambda\eta_{1}\rho(\eta_{1})
and
\nu_{i}\equiv 1
,
G_{i}\equiv 0
for
2\leq i\leq N
and considering
\eta|_{\partial\Omega}=0
with the two distinct solutions
\eta=0
and
\eta=(u,0,\dots,0)
.

3.2
Boundary measurements

3.2.1
Voltage boundary measurements

We prove here that voltage boundary measurements
\phi(\gamma(x),\tau(x),x)-\phi(\gamma(y),\tau(y),y)
uniquely determine
\phi(p,s,x)
up to a constant along the boundary
\partial\Omega
. This is a key ingredient of the proof of
2.3
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded domain with connected
C^{1}
-boundary,
M\in\mathbb{N}
and
\Phi,\phi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{M+1}\times\overline{\Omega})
. The following two statements are equivalent:

i)

For every
(\gamma,\tau)\in\bigl(W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\partial\Omega)\bigr)^{M+1}
we have

\phi(\gamma(x),\tau(x),x)-\phi(\gamma(y),\tau(y),y)=\Phi(\gamma(x),\tau(x),x)-\Phi(\gamma(y),\tau(y),y)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for $\mathcal{H}^{2}$-a.e. $x,y\in\partial\Omega$;}

ii)

There exists some
r\in\mathbb{R}
such that for all
(p,s,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{M+1}\times\partial\Omega
we have
\phi(p,s,x)-\Phi(p,s,x)=r
.

Proof of
3.5
.

The implication
(ii)\Rightarrow(i)
is immediate. For the converse, we notice that the temperature
\tau
does not play any distinguished role in the context of
3.5
so that we set

\displaystyle N:=M+1\text{, }\rho:=(\gamma,\tau)\text{ and }z:=(p,s).

We need to show that the identity

\displaystyle\phi(\rho(x),x)-\phi(\rho(y),y)=\Phi(\rho(x),x)-\Phi(\rho(y),y)\text{ for all }\rho\in\left(W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\partial\Omega)\right)^{N}\text{ and }\mathcal{H}^{2}\text{-a.e. }x,y\in\partial\Omega

implies that
\phi(z,x)-\Phi(z,x)=r
for all
(z,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\partial\Omega
and some
r\in\mathbb{R}
independent of
(z,x)
.

We notice that if
\rho\in(C^{1}(\partial\Omega))^{N}
, then
\phi(\rho(\cdot),\cdot)\in C^{1}(\partial\Omega)
, and the identity

\displaystyle\phi(\rho(x),x)-\phi(\rho(y),y)=\Phi(\rho(x),x)-\Phi(\rho(y),y),\hskip 20.00003ptx,y\in\partial\Omega,

implies that
\nabla^{\partial\Omega}_{x}(\phi(\rho(x),x))=\nabla^{\partial\Omega}_{x}(\Phi(\rho(x),x))
for all
x\in\partial\Omega
, where
\nabla^{\partial\Omega}_{x}
denotes the tangential gradient. This yields

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\partial_{z_{i}}\phi)(\rho(x),x)\nabla^{\partial\Omega}_{x}\rho_{i}(x)+(\nabla^{\partial\Omega}_{x}\phi)(\rho(x),x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\partial_{z_{i}}\Phi)(\rho(x),x)\nabla^{\partial\Omega}_{x}\rho_{i}(x)+(\nabla^{\partial\Omega}_{x}\Phi)(\rho(x),x).

(3.10)

We now fix
z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and consider
\rho(x)\equiv z\in(C^{1}(\partial\Omega))^{N}
. We then find
(\nabla^{\partial\Omega}_{x}\rho_{i})\equiv 0
since each
\rho_{i}
is constant, and so (
3.10
) implies

\displaystyle(\nabla^{\partial\Omega}_{x}\phi)(z,x)=(\nabla^{\partial\Omega}_{x}\Phi)(z,x),\hskip 20.00003pt(z,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\partial\Omega.

(3.11)

Combining (
3.11
) with (
3.10
) then yields the identity

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\partial_{z_{i}}\phi)(\rho(x),x)\nabla^{\partial\Omega}_{x}\rho_{i}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\partial_{z_{i}}\Phi)(\rho(x),x)\nabla^{\partial\Omega}_{x}\rho_{i}(x),\hskip 20.00003pt\rho\in(C^{1}(\partial\Omega))^{N},\;x\in\partial\Omega.

(3.12)

Now fix
z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
and
1\leq j\leq N
. Pick
\rho_{i}(x)\equiv z_{i}
for
i\neq j
, which again implies
\nabla^{\partial\Omega}_{x}\rho_{i}(x)\equiv 0
for
i\neq j
. Then pick
\rho_{j}\in C^{1}(\partial\Omega)
such that
\rho_{j}(x_{0})=z_{j}
and
(\nabla^{\partial\Omega}_{x}\rho_{j})(x_{0})\neq 0
(which is clearly always possible by a local construction). Then (
3.12
) implies

\displaystyle(\partial_{z_{j}}\phi)(z,x_{0})(\nabla^{\partial\Omega}_{x}\rho_{j})(x_{0})=(\partial_{z_{j}}\Phi)(z,x_{0})(\nabla^{\partial\Omega}_{x}\rho_{j})(x_{0}),

and in turn we have
(\partial_{z_{j}}\phi)(z,x_{0})=(\partial_{z_{j}}\Phi)(z,x_{0})
. Since
z
,
x_{0}
and
j
were arbitrary, we deduce

\displaystyle(\partial_{z_{i}}\phi)(z,x)=(\partial_{z_{i}}\Phi)(z,x),\hskip 20.00003pt(z,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\partial\Omega\text{, }1\leq i\leq N.

(3.13)

Now (
3.11
) and (
3.13
) imply that the map
\phi-\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\partial\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
(z,x)\mapsto\phi(z,x)-\Phi(z,x)
has a vanishing gradient. Since by assumption
\partial\Omega
is connected, so is
\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\partial\Omega
and hence we conclude that
\phi(z,x)-\Phi(z,x)=r
for some
r\in\mathbb{R}
independent of
(z,x)
.
∎

3.2.2
Proof of
2.3

Proof of
2.3
.

According to
3.5
conditions (i,a) and (ii,b) are equivalent so that we may assume throughout that
\phi(p,s,x)-\Phi(p,s,x)=r
for some
r\in\mathbb{R}
and all
(p,s,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{M+1}\times\partial\Omega
.
We further notice that
\Lambda_{\operatorname{DN}}[\phi]
depends on
\phi
only through its gradient, recall (
2.2
), so that we may suppose that
r=0
throughout the upcoming considerations.

We are left with showing that, under the above assumptions, conditions (i,b) and (ii,a) are equivalent.

To this end, for fixed
(\gamma,\tau)\in\bigl(W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\partial\Omega)\bigr)^{M+1}
, set

\displaystyle\eta_{0}(x):=\phi(\gamma(x),\tau(x),x).

For given
v\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, denote by
L^{-1}_{\epsilon}(v)
the unique (
H^{1}(\Omega)
-)weak solution of the boundary value problem

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(\epsilon\nabla L^{-1}_{\epsilon}(v))=v,\hskip 10.00002ptL^{-1}_{\epsilon}(v)|_{\partial\Omega}=\eta_{0}.

(3.14)

We now let
(c,T)
and
(\overline{c},\overline{T})
denote the solutions to (
2.2
) with boundary data
(\gamma,\tau)
and with respect to the potentials
\phi
and
\Phi
, respectively. We set

\displaystyle\sigma(x):=\phi(c(x),T(x),x)\text{ and }\overline{\sigma}(x):=\Phi(\overline{c}(x),\overline{T}(x),x).

On the one hand, according to (
2.2
), we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(\epsilon\nabla\sigma)=c\cdot q\text{ and }\operatorname{div}(\epsilon\nabla\overline{\sigma})=\overline{c}\cdot q.

On the other hand, since
\phi
and
\Phi
coincide on the boundary,

\displaystyle\sigma|_{\partial\Omega}=\phi(\gamma(x),\tau(x),x)=\eta_{0}(x)=\Phi(\gamma(x),\tau(x),x)=\overline{\sigma}|_{\partial\Omega}.

We conclude that
\sigma=L^{-1}_{\epsilon}(c\cdot q)
and
\overline{\sigma}=L^{-1}_{\epsilon}(\overline{c}\cdot q)
.
Since by assumption the
D_{i}
and
g_{i}
depend on position alone, we see that
c_{i}
and
\overline{c}_{i}
satisfy the same linear elliptic PDE, (
2.2
), with the same boundary conditions. Thus,

throughout

.

This implies that the Dirichlet-to-Neumann maps
\Lambda_{\operatorname{DN}}[\phi]
and
\Lambda_{\operatorname{DN}}[\Phi]
coincide if and only if
\mathcal{N}\cdot\nabla T=\mathcal{N}\cdot\overline{T}
for all prescribed Dirichlet data. Further,

\displaystyle\sigma=L^{-1}_{\epsilon}(c\cdot q)=L^{-1}_{\epsilon}(\overline{c}\cdot q)=\overline{\sigma}.

We can apply the gradient to this identity, and we obtain for
x\in\Omega
:

\displaystyle(\partial_{s}\phi)(c(x),T(x),x)\nabla T(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{M}(\partial_{p_{i}}\phi)(c(x),T(x),x)\nabla c_{i}(x)+(\nabla_{x}\phi)(c(x),T(x),x)

\displaystyle=(\partial_{s}\Phi)(\overline{c}(x),\overline{T}(x),x)\nabla\overline{T}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{M}(\partial_{p_{i}}\Phi)(\overline{c}(x),\overline{T}(x),x)\nabla\overline{c}_{i}+(\nabla_{x}\Phi)(\overline{c}(x),\overline{T}(x),x).

Now, since
\phi(p,s,x)=\Phi(p,s,x)
for all
(p,s,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{M+1}\times\partial\Omega
, we find
(\partial_{s}\phi)(p,s,x)=(\partial_{s}\Phi)(p,s,x)
and
(\partial_{p_{i}}\phi)(p,s,x)=(\partial_{p_{i}}\Phi)(p,s,x)
for all
(p,s,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{M+1}\times\partial\Omega
.

Since
c(x)=\overline{c}(x)
in
\Omega
, the identities
\mathcal{N}\cdot(D_{i}(x)\nabla c_{i}(x))=\mathcal{N}\cdot(D_{i}(x)\nabla\overline{c}_{i})
are always satisfied. Recalling that
\partial_{s}\phi
and
\partial_{s}\Phi
, as well as
T
and
\overline{T}
, coincide on the boundary, we have for
x\in\partial\Omega

\displaystyle(\partial_{s}\phi)(\gamma(x),\tau(x),x)\mathcal{N}\cdot\nabla T(x)+\mathcal{N}(x)\cdot(\nabla_{x}\phi)(\gamma(x),\tau(x),x)

\displaystyle=(\partial_{s}\phi)(\gamma(x),\tau(x),x)\mathcal{N}\cdot\nabla\overline{T}(x)+\mathcal{N}(x)\cdot(\nabla_{x}\Phi)(\gamma(x),\tau(x),x).

We deduce that the condition
\mathcal{N}\cdot\nabla T=\mathcal{N}\cdot\nabla\overline{T}
is equivalent to
\mathcal{N}\cdot(\nabla_{x}\phi)(\gamma(x),\tau(x),x)=\mathcal{N}\cdot(\nabla_{x}\Phi)(\gamma(x),\tau(x),x)
for all
(\gamma,\tau)\in\bigl(W^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\partial\Omega)\bigr)^{M+1}
and all

, which in turn is equivalent to the identity
\mathcal{N}\cdot(\nabla_{x}\phi)(p,s,x)=\mathcal{N}\cdot(\nabla_{x}\Phi)(p,s,x)
for all
(p,s,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{M+1}\times\partial\Omega
.
Finally we observe that the identity
\phi(p,s,x)=\Phi(p,s,x)
for all
(p,s,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{M+1}\times\partial\Omega
implies that
(\nabla^{\partial\Omega}_{x}\phi)(p,s,x)=(\nabla^{\partial\Omega}_{x}\Phi)(p,s,x)

3.3
Interior measurements

3.3.1
A Linearisation lemma

Before we come to the proof of
2.5
, we will prove a linearisation lemma, which may be regarded as a generalisation of a result by Sun
[
47
, Lemma 2.10]
.

Lemma 3.6

(Linearisation lemma)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded domain with (possibly disconnected)
C^{1,\beta}
-boundary for some
0<\beta\leq 1
. Let
M,N\in\mathbb{N}
,
0<\alpha\leq 1
,
\nu_{*}\in(0,\infty)
and suppose that
\nu=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{M})\in C^{0,\alpha}_{b}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)
with
\nu_{*}\leq\nu_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq M
. Further, let
A\colon\left(L^{2}(\Omega)\right)^{M}\rightarrow\left(L^{2}(\Omega)\right)^{N}
be a continuous function with the following two properties:

i)

For every
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\subset\left(L^{2}(\Omega)\right)^{M}
there is some
0<\alpha_{\mu}\leq 1
such that
A(\mu)\in\left(C^{0,\alpha_{\mu}}(\overline{\Omega})\right)^{M}
,

ii)

For every
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
there is some
L_{\mu}\in(0,\infty)
such that
\|A(\mu+v)-A(\mu)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq L_{\mu}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
for all
v\in\left(L^{2}(\Omega)\right)^{M}
.

Take
f\in\left(C^{1,\beta}(\partial\Omega)\right)^{M}
and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
. For
t>0
, let
F^{t}\in\left(H^{1}(\Omega)\right)^{M}
and
u\in\left(H^{1}(\Omega)\right)^{M}
be weak solutions to the systems of equations

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}\left(\nu_{i}(A(F^{t})(x),x)\nabla F^{t}_{i}(x)\right)=0\text{, }1\leq i\leq M\text{, }F^{t}|_{\partial\Omega}=\mu+tf,

(3.15)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(\nu_{i}(A(\mu)(x),x)\nabla u_{i}(x))=0\text{, }1\leq i\leq M\text{, }u|_{\partial\Omega}=f.

(3.16)

Then, for every
1\leq i\leq M
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{N}\cdot\left(\nu_{i}(A(F^{t})(x),x)\nabla F^{t}_{i}\right)}{t}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}\cdot\left(\nu_{i}(A(\mu)(x),x)\nabla u_{i}\right)\text{ as }t\rightarrow 0\text{ in }W^{-\frac{1}{2},2}(\partial\Omega).

(3.17)

Remark 3.7
.

The elliptic equation (
3.16
) is a standard linear elliptic PDE for which uniqueness and existence results are available. The existence and uniqueness questions regarding (
3.15
) are more involved and we only point out that under the assumptions of
3.6
the existence of solutions can be proven in a similar fashion as in
3.1
. The statement of
3.6
is that no matter what solution
F^{t}
we select for the system (
3.15
), we will always observe the convergence (
3.17
) in the limit
t\rightarrow 0
.

Proof of
3.6
.

Strategy:
In view of (
3.17
), we need to establish the convergence of the normal traces of certain vector fields. Instead of working directly with the normal traces, we will show that the corresponding vector fields converge in
H(\Omega,\operatorname{div})
. Due to the continuity of the normal trace,
[
15
, I §2 Theorem 2.5]
, this will imply the convergence of the normal traces.

Step 1: Boundedness of
\kappa^{t}:=F^{t}-\mu
in
H^{1}
.
Observe that
\kappa^{t}_{i}
solves the following boundary value problem

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}\left(\tilde{\nu}_{i}\nabla\kappa^{t}_{i}\right)=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\kappa^{t}_{i}|_{\partial\Omega}=tf_{i},

(3.18)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\nu}_{i}(x):=\nu_{i}(A(\mu+\kappa^{t})(x),x).

According to
[
14
, Theorem 8.34]
and our assumptions on
A
and
\nu
there is a unique solution
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{M})\in\left(C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})\right)^{M}
to (
3.16
). We observe also that
\kappa^{t}_{i}-tu_{i}|_{\partial\Omega}=0
.
This allows us to compute

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\nu}_{i}\nabla\kappa^{t}_{i}\cdot\nabla\kappa^{t}_{i}dx=t\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\nu}_{i}\nabla\kappa^{t}_{i}\cdot\nabla u_{i}dx+\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\nu}_{i}\nabla\kappa^{t}_{i}\cdot\nabla(\kappa^{t}_{i}-tu_{i})dx=t\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\nu}_{i}\nabla\kappa^{t}_{i}\cdot\nabla u_{i}dx

(3.19)

where we integrated by parts and used (
3.18
) in the last step. Since the
\nu_{i}
are uniformly bounded below and above, we deduce that

\displaystyle\|\nabla\kappa^{t}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\lambda|t|\|\nabla u_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

(3.20)

where
\lambda>0
denotes a generic constant which may change its value from line to line and may depend on
\nu
and
\Omega
, but is always independent of
t
,
\kappa^{t}
,
f
,
\mu
and
A
. By means of Poincaré’s inequality we find

\displaystyle\|\kappa^{t}_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq|t|\|u_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\kappa^{t}_{i}-tu_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq|t|\|u_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lambda\|\nabla\kappa^{t}_{i}-t\nabla u_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\lambda|t|\|u_{i}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)},

where we used the triangle inequality and (
3.20
) in the last step. We hence conclude that

\displaystyle\|\kappa^{t}_{i}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\lambda|t|\|u_{i}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

(3.21)

Step 2:
L^{2}(\Omega)
-convergence of the vector fields.

Set
\nu_{i}^{\mu}(x):=\nu_{i}(A(\mu)(x),x)
. By (
3.19
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\nu}_{i}|\nabla\kappa^{t}_{i}-t\nabla u_{i}|^{2}dx=-t\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\nu}_{i}\nabla\kappa^{t}_{i}\cdot\nabla u_{i}dx+t^{2}\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\nu}_{i}|\nabla u_{i}|^{2}dx

\displaystyle=-t\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\nu}_{i}(\nabla\kappa^{t}_{i}-t\nabla u_{i})\cdot\nabla u_{i}dx=t\int_{\Omega}(\nu^{\mu}_{i}-\tilde{\nu}_{i})(\nabla\kappa^{t}_{i}-t\nabla u_{i})\cdot\nabla u_{i}dx

(3.22)

where we used (
3.16
) in the last step. By the Hölder continuity of
\nu

\displaystyle|\tilde{\nu}_{i}-\nu^{\mu}_{i}|=|\nu_{i}(A(\mu+\kappa^{t})(x),x)-\nu_{i}(A(\mu)(x),x)|\leq\lambda|A(\mu+\kappa^{t})(x)-A(\mu)(x)|^{\alpha}.

We combine this with (
3.22
) and obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\nu}_{i}|\nabla\kappa^{t}_{i}-t\nabla u_{i}|^{2}dx\leq\lambda|t|\|\nabla\kappa^{t}_{i}-t\nabla u_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\||A(\mu+\kappa^{t})-A(\mu)|^{\alpha}|\nabla u_{i}|\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda|t|\|\nabla\kappa^{t}_{i}-t\nabla u_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|A(\mu+\kappa^{t})-A(\mu)\|^{\alpha}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\nabla u_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

(3.23)

Recall that
u_{i}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
and thus
\|\nabla u_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}<\infty
. By properties of
A
and (
3.21
) we find

\displaystyle\|A(\mu+\kappa^{t})-A(\mu)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq L_{\mu}\|\kappa^{t}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\lambda L_{\mu}|t|\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

Then, by (
3.23
) we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\nu}_{i}|\nabla\kappa^{t}_{i}-t\nabla u_{i}|^{2}dx\leq\lambda L^{\alpha}_{\mu}\|u\|^{\alpha}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}|t|^{1+\alpha}\|\nabla u_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\kappa^{t}_{i}-t\nabla u_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Finally, using that
\tilde{\nu}_{i}
is uniformly bounded from below, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\frac{\kappa^{t}_{i}}{t}-\nabla u_{i}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq L^{\alpha}_{\mu}\lambda|t|^{\alpha}\|u\|^{\alpha}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\|\nabla u_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }t\rightarrow 0.

(3.24)

Since
\nabla F^{t}_{i}=\nabla\kappa^{t}_{i}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\tilde{\nu}_{i}\left(\frac{\nabla\kappa_{i}}{t}-\nabla u_{i}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|(\tilde{\nu}_{i}-\nu_{i}^{\mu})\nabla u_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(3.25)

Since

is uniformly bounded above, by (
3.24
) the first summand on the right hand side of (
3.25
) converges to zero as

. As for the second term, we can as before use the estimate

\displaystyle\|(\tilde{\nu}_{i}-\nu^{\mu}_{i})\nabla u_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\lambda\||A(\mu+\kappa^{t})-A(\mu)|^{\alpha}|\nabla u_{i}|\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\lambda L^{\alpha}_{\mu}\|\nabla u_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|u\|^{\alpha}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}|t|^{\alpha}

which converges to zero as

. Combining this with (

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\nu_{i}(A(F^{t})(x),x)\nabla F^{t}_{i}}{t}-\nu_{i}(A(\mu)(x),x)\nabla u_{i}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }t\rightarrow 0.

Step 3: Concluding the proof.
Since
\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{\nu_{i}(A(F^{t})(x),x)\nabla F^{t}_{i}}{t}-\nu_{i}(A(\mu)(x),x)\nabla u_{i}\right)=0
in view of (

Thus, the lemma follows by the continuity of the normal trace operator, cf.
[
15
, I §2 Theorem 2.5]
.
∎

A.1
Modelling the ion concentrations

We recall that electrolysis is based on the following reactions (
1.1
)-(
1.3
)

\displaystyle\text{Anode: }2\text{OH}^{-}\rightarrow\text{H}_{2}\text{O}+\frac{\text{O}_{2}}{2}+2\text{e}^{-}

\displaystyle\text{Cathode: }2\text{H}_{2}\text{O}+2\text{e}^{-}\rightarrow\text{H}_{2}+2\text{OH}^{-}

\displaystyle\text{Overall reaction: }\text{H}_{2}\text{O}\rightarrow\text{H}_{2}+\frac{\text{O}_{2}}{2}

In practice KOH may also be added in the case of an AEM electrolyser, but is in principle not required for an electrolyser to function
[
38
]
. We denote by
c_{i}
the concentration of the
i
-th particle species where
c_{i}(t,x)
is a function of time
t
and position
x
. If in addition
N_{i}
is the
i
-th species particle flux and
g_{i}
denotes the source of the
i
-th particle, then it follows from mass conversation, also known as Fick’s second law, that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}c_{i}+\operatorname{div}(N_{i})=g_{i}.

(A1)

Following Fick’s first law we suppose here that

\displaystyle N_{i}=-D_{i}\nabla c_{i}

where
D_{i}
is the diffusion coefficient of the
i
-th particle species. We further make the simplifying assumption that the diffusion tensor is scalar, i.e. the underlying medium is isotropic. Inserting this relationship into (
A1
) gives us the following parabolic diffusion equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}c_{i}-\operatorname{div}(D_{i}\nabla c_{i})=g_{i}.

(A2)

We emphasise that (
A2
) does not take into account electrostatic forces between the ions and that a more realistic model should incorporate such forces, since some of the particles within the device are charged and hence repel or attract each other. A more realistic model is presented in
Appendix

B
. However, its analysis is more challenging and left for future work, while the present manuscript focuses on the simplified equations (
A2
).

In general, the diffusion coefficients
D_{i}
, as well as the source terms are unknown to us and may all depend themselves on the temperature of the system as well as on the particle concentrations, i.e.

\displaystyle D_{i}=D_{i}(T,c,x,t)\text{, }g_{i}=g_{i}(T,c,x,t)

where
T
is the (scalar) temperature and
c=(c_{1},\dots,c_{M})
, where
M
is the number of distinct species. In turn,
c_{i}=c_{i}(t,x)
and
T=T(t,x)
are assumed to be functions of time and position themselves.

A.2
Modelling the electric potential

We start by considering the macroscopic Maxwell equations

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}D=\rho_{f}\text{, }\operatorname{curl}H=J_{f}+\partial_{t}D\text{, }\operatorname{div}B=0\text{, }\operatorname{curl}E=-\partial_{t}B

(A3)

where
B
is the magnetic field,
E
is the electric field,
H
is the magnetising field,
D
is the electric displacement field,
\rho_{f}
is the free charge density and
J_{f}
is the free current density. The relationship between the distinct fields is given by

\displaystyle H=\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}B-M\text{, }D=\epsilon_{0}E+P

where
P
is the polarisation field,
M
is the magnetisation field and
\mu_{0}
and
\epsilon_{0}
are the vacuum permeability and permittivity respectively. The
P
- and
M
-fields are in turn related to the bound charges and electric currents, but we make here the simplifying assumption that we are dealing with a linear medium so that we find the relationships, cf.
[
26
, Chapter 4.4 & 5.8]
,

\displaystyle M=\chi_{m}H\text{, }P=\chi_{e}\epsilon_{0}E

where
\chi_{m}
and
\chi_{e}
are the magnetic and electric susceptibility. We obtain

\displaystyle B=\mu H\text{, }D=\epsilon E

where
\mu=\mu_{0}(1+\chi_{m})
is the magnetic permeability and
\epsilon=\epsilon_{0}(1+\chi_{e})
is the electric permeability. Consequently (
A3
) becomes

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(\epsilon E)=\rho_{f}\text{, }\operatorname{curl}\left(\frac{B}{\mu}\right)=J_{f}+\partial_{t}(\epsilon E)\text{, }\operatorname{div}B=0\text{, }\operatorname{curl}E=-\partial_{t}B.

(A4)

In practice, an electrolyser cell will occupy some contractible (or even convex) region
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, so that the condition
\operatorname{div}B=0
becomes equivalent to the statement
B=\operatorname{curl}(A)
for some vector potential
A
. In turn

\displaystyle 0=\operatorname{curl}(E)+\partial_{t}B=\operatorname{curl}(E+\partial_{t}A).

Since
\Omega
is contractible this is equivalent to the statement
E=\nabla\phi-\partial_{t}A
and so (
A4
) becomes

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(\epsilon E)=\rho_{f}\text{, }\operatorname{curl}\left(\frac{B}{\mu}\right)=J_{f}+\partial_{t}(\epsilon E)\text{, }B=\operatorname{curl}A\text{, }E=\nabla\phi-\partial_{t}A.

(A5)

We notice that the vector potential
A
is unique up to the addition of gradient fields and that in turn
\phi
depends on the choice of
A
. Our goal now will be to fix a specific gauge in order to be able to associate a physical meaning with the potential
\phi
. If
E
is curl-free, or equivalently
B
is static, then we may pick
A
to be time-independent and obtain a scalar potential with
E=\nabla\phi
which has a physical meaning. Such a choice cannot be made for time-dependent fields. In order to associate a physical meaning to
\phi
in time-varying electromagnetic fields, we fix any two points
x,y\in\partial\Omega
. Using a voltmeter, we are able to assign the points
x,y
a corresponding measurement. What a voltmeter actually measures is the quantity
\int_{\gamma_{\operatorname{vm}}}E
which corresponds to the work, per charge, which is necessary to move a charge from point
x
to point
y
along the circuit path
\gamma_{\operatorname{vm}}
within the voltmeter. In general time-dependent fields, this quantity will be path dependent and hence the reading of the voltmeter will depend on the placement of the voltmeter, cf.
[
41
]
for a discussion about voltmeter measurements in time-varying electromagnetic fields.

However, if we make the simplifying assumption that the voltmeter is close to
\partial\Omega
and thus
\gamma_{\operatorname{vm}}
is well-approximated by a path
\gamma
on
\partial\Omega
which connects
x
and
y
, we find

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma_{\operatorname{vm}}}E\approx\int_{\gamma}E.

Since
\gamma
is tangent to
\partial\Omega
we see that if we can find a vector potential
A
such that
\partial_{t}A\perp\partial\Omega
, then

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma_{\operatorname{vm}}}E\approx\int_{\gamma}E=\int_{\gamma}\nabla\phi=\phi(y)-\phi(x).

Consequently our voltmeter measurements will correspond to potential differences on the boundary. Of course if
E
is a gradient field throughout
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, then this approximation becomes an identity.

Let us therefore examine when a potential
A
can be chosen such that
\partial_{t}A\perp\partial\Omega
. Since
E=\nabla\phi-\partial_{t}A
we see that we can find
\partial_{t}A\perp\partial\Omega
if and only if
E^{\parallel}
is a gradient field, where
E^{\parallel}
denotes the part of
E|_{\partial\Omega}
which is tangent to
\partial\Omega
. In our application
\partial\Omega
will be diffeomorphic to a sphere and so
E^{\parallel}
is a gradient field if and only if
\operatorname{curl}_{\partial\Omega}(E^{\parallel})=0
where
\operatorname{curl}_{\partial\Omega}
denotes the curl operator on
\partial\Omega
. We now make use of the fact that

\displaystyle\operatorname{curl}_{\partial\Omega}(E^{\parallel})=\mathcal{N}\cdot\operatorname{curl}(E)

where
\mathcal{N}
denotes the outward unit normal and
\operatorname{curl}
denotes the standard Euclidean curl. Since we have
E=\nabla\phi-\partial_{t}A
we get
\operatorname{curl}(E)=-\partial_{t}\operatorname{curl}(A)=-\partial_{t}B
. Hence
E^{\parallel}
is a gradient field if and only if
\mathcal{N}\cdot\partial_{t}B=0
or equivalently if and only if
\mathcal{N}\cdot B
is time independent. Therefore, as long as the normal part of the magnetic field
B
does not change in time we are able to identify a potential
\phi
with a physical significance.

To uniquely fix the temporal gauge
\partial_{t}A
we may prescribe its divergence as follows

\displaystyle\operatorname{curl}(\partial_{t}A)=\partial_{t}B\text{, }\operatorname{div}(\epsilon\partial_{t}A)=0\text{ in }\Omega\text{, }\partial_{t}A\perp\partial\Omega.

(A6)

It is easy to see that if we have two vector potentials
A_{1},A_{2}
satisfying (
A6
), then their difference must be a gradient field
\nabla f
which satisfies the elliptic equation

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(\epsilon\nabla\partial_{t}f)=0\text{ and }\nabla\partial_{t}f\perp\partial\Omega.

We notice that the condition
\nabla\partial_{t}f\perp\partial\Omega
means that
\partial_{t}f
is constant on
\partial\Omega
and since we are only interested in the gradient of
f
we may suppose that
\partial_{t}f|_{\partial\Omega}=0
. Therefore, if
\epsilon\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
is bounded away from zero, we find
\nabla\partial_{t}f=0
and consequently
\partial_{t}A_{1}=\partial_{t}A_{2}
as desired.

The existence of a solution is guaranteed as long as
\mathcal{N}\cdot B
is time-independent. As explained previously this condition implies that
E^{\parallel}
is a gradient field. Therefore, if
A
is any fixed vector potential with corresponding scalar potential
\phi
we find
\partial_{t}A^{\parallel}=\nabla_{\partial\Omega}\kappa
for some suitable function
\kappa
. We can then let
f
be a solution to the elliptic boundary value problem

\displaystyle f|_{\partial\Omega}=-\kappa\text{ and }\operatorname{div}(\epsilon\nabla f)=-\operatorname{div}(\epsilon\partial_{t}A)\text{ in }\Omega.

Then
\widetilde{A}:=A+\int_{0}^{t}\nabla fds
is a valid vector potential with

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\widetilde{A}=\partial_{t}A+\nabla f\text{, i.e.\ }(\partial_{t}\widetilde{A})^{\parallel}=(\partial_{t}A)^{\parallel}-\nabla_{\partial\Omega}\kappa=0\text{ and }\operatorname{div}(\epsilon\partial_{t}\widetilde{A})=0.

Imposing the gauge (
A6
) we obtain the following set of equations in terms of the electric potential
\phi
and the magnetic vector potential
A

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(\epsilon\nabla\phi)=\rho_{f}\text{, }\operatorname{curl}\left(\frac{\operatorname{curl}(A)}{\mu}\right)=J_{f}+\partial_{t}(\epsilon(\nabla\phi-\partial_{t}A))\text{, }\operatorname{div}(\epsilon\partial_{t}A)=0\text{, }\partial_{t}A\perp\partial\Omega.

(A7)

We notice that if
B
is static, i.e.
\partial_{t}B=0
, then the unique solution to (
A6
) is
\partial_{t}A=0
and hence we recover the identity
E=\nabla\phi
from classical electrostatics.

We further point out that it is a priori not clear to what extent the condition
\partial_{t}(\mathcal{N}\cdot B)=0
on
\partial\Omega
is a good approximation of our system under consideration and that experiments should be conducted to understand whether or not this condition is a good approximation of reality. In the present work we will investigate the static case
\partial_{t}B=0
for which this condition is trivially satisfied.

The quantity
\rho_{f}
in (
A7
) denotes the free charge density and in our application the free charges are precisely the distinct ions. In particular, if
c_{i}
is the concentration of the
i
-th species, then
c_{i}(t,x)
corresponds to the number of particles of species
i
within an infinitesimal volume around
x
(at time
t
). Therefore, the charge contribution within this infinitesimal volume of the
i
-th species will be given by
q_{i}c_{i}(t,x)
where
q_{i}
is the charge of the
i
-th species. The total charge per infinitesimal volume will then be the sum of the contributions of all distinct species, i.e.

\displaystyle\rho_{f}(t,x)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}q_{i}c_{i}(t,x)

where we recall that
M
is the number of distinct species. Similarly, the free current density
J_{f}
is the sum of the current densities of all species,
J_{f}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}J_{i}
, and, in turn, the current density of the
i
-th species corresponds to the expression
J_{i}=q_{i}N_{i}
where
N_{i}
is the particle flux. We therefore obtain the following additional formulas

\displaystyle\rho_{f}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}q_{i}c_{i}\text{ and }J_{f}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}q_{i}N_{i}=-\sum_{i=1}^{M}q_{i}D_{i}\nabla c_{i}

(A8)

where we used Fick’s first law in the last step. This couples (
A7
) and (
A2
).

A.3
Functional assumptions

We recall first that according to (
A2
), (
A7
) and (
A8
) we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}c_{i}-\operatorname{div}(D_{i}\nabla c_{i})=g_{i},\hskip 20.00003pt\operatorname{div}(\epsilon\nabla\phi)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}q_{i}c_{i}=q\cdot c,

(A9)

where
q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{M})
,
c=(c_{1},\dots,c_{M})
and
\cdot
denotes the standard Euclidean scalar product.

We observe that the
g_{i}
in (
A9
) represent the source terms, which are in turn related to the particle production/annihilation in the chemical processes involved in the electrolyser. These processes depend on the temperature as well as on the concentrations of the particles. We thus assume that the
g_{i}
are of the form
g_{i}=g_{i}(t,c,T,x)
. Similarly, the diffusion of the particles is influenced by the particle concentrations and the temperature of the system and thus we suppose that
D_{i}=D_{i}(t,c,T,x)
. From these assumptions and (
A9
) we see that

•

the potential
\phi
is coupled to the concentrations
c_{i}
;

•

and the concentrations are coupled to
T
through
g_{i}
and
D_{i}
.

Thus, we assume that there is a phenomenological relation between the potential
\phi
and the temperature and concentrations, i.e.
\phi=\phi(t,c,T,x)
. We make the additional assumption that the electric permeability is not affected much by the change in temperature and particle concentrations within the device and that it remains constant in time, i.e.
\epsilon=\epsilon(x)
. Note that in reality
\epsilon
depends on temperature, and so a more accurate model should include this dependence
[
17
, Chapter 4.4.1]
. Finally, we take the concentrations
c=(c_{1},\dots,c_{M})
and the temperature
T
to be our phenomenologically independent quantities in the sense that we suppose that
c=c(t,x)
and
T=T(t,x)
. The remaining quantities
g_{i}
,
D_{i}
, and
\phi
may be expressed as functions of
c
and
T
(as well as
t
and
x
).

We observe that from a physical perspective it is reasonable to assume that
0<D_{*}\leq D_{i}\leq D^{*}<\infty
for some constants
0<D_{*}<D^{*}<\infty
and for all
1\leq i\leq M
. This assumption essentially turns

\displaystyle\partial_{t}c_{i}-\operatorname{div}(D_{i}\nabla c_{i})=g_{i}

into a system of parabolic PDEs (or elliptic PDEs in the static case). However, we have an additional temperature dependence so that these continuity equations do not form a complete system of equations.

To ensure that we have a well-behaved system of equations we have to make some additional assumptions on the coefficients appearing in the remaining PDE

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(\epsilon\nabla\phi)=q\cdot c.

Just like in the case of the diffusion coefficients it is reasonable to suppose that

\epsilon\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and that
\epsilon\geq\epsilon_{*}>0
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
for some constant
\epsilon_{*}>0
. In addition, once we make our phenomenological structural assumption
\phi=\phi(t,c(t,x),T(t,x),x)
we may compute

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}(\phi(t,c(t,x),T(t,x),x))=(\partial_{T}\phi)(t,c(t,x),T(t,x),x)(\nabla T)(t,x)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{M}(\partial_{c_{i}}\phi)(t,c(t,x),T(t,x),x)(\nabla c_{i})(t,x)+(\nabla_{x}\phi)(t,c(t,x),T(t,x),x).

Consequently the prefactor of
\nabla T
in the equation
\operatorname{div}(\epsilon\nabla\phi)=q\cdot c
is
\epsilon(\partial_{T}\phi)
. So if we want to obtain a well-behaved equation for
T
we should demand that
0<a\leq(\partial_{T}\phi)\leq b<\infty
for suitable constants
0<a\leq b<\infty
. This assumption is motivated from a mathematical perspective. A physical interpretation of this assumption can be obtained as follows:

Imagine we are given two (identical) electrolyser devices which occupy two bounded regions
\Omega_{1}
,
\Omega_{2}
respectively where
\Omega_{2}=d+\Omega_{1}
for some suitable
d\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the distance between the electrolysers
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
is large enough that we may assume that their electromagnetic fields don’t influence each other. Since the potentials
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
on
\Omega_{1}
,
\Omega_{2}
are only unique up to constants we can fix some reference point
x_{1}\in\partial\Omega_{1}
, some reference temperature
T^{*}
and some reference concentration
c^{*}=(c^{*}_{1},\dots,c^{*}_{M})
and we normalise
\phi_{1}
on
\Omega_{1}
such that at the initial time
t=0
,
\phi_{1}(0,c^{*},T^{*},x_{1})=0
. Similarly, we set
x_{2}:=x_{1}+d
and use the normalisation
\phi_{2}(0,c^{*},T^{*},x_{2})=0
. We suppose now that we may prescribe the temperature profile
T_{0}(x)=T(0,x)
and the concentration profile
c_{0}(x)=c(0,x)
along the boundaries of our devices at the initial time
t=0
. We then fix some
T_{2}>T_{1}
and pick our profiles such that
T_{0}(x_{1})=T^{*}=T_{0}(x_{2})
,
c_{0}(x_{1})=c^{*}=c_{0}(x_{2})
and for some fixed
y_{1}\in\partial\Omega\setminus\{x_{1}\}
, letting
y_{2}:=d+y_{1}
, we prescribe
T_{0}(y_{1})=T_{1}
,
T_{0}(y_{2})=T_{2}
and ensure that
c_{0}(y_{1})=c_{0}(y_{2})
. Then our condition essentially demands that if we compare the voltage measurement between the points
x_{1}
and
y_{1}
at
t=0
with the voltage measurement between
x_{2}
and
y_{2}
at
t=0
we will find that the value between
x_{2}
and
y_{2}
is higher than the value between
x_{1}
and
y_{1}
.

Let us finally mention that it is customary in the chemistry literature to decompose the potential
\phi
in distinct contributions such as

\displaystyle\phi=\phi_{\operatorname{OCV}}+\phi_{\operatorname{act}}+\phi_{\operatorname{conc}}+\phi_{\operatorname{ohm}}

(A10)

where
\phi_{\operatorname{OCV}}
is the open circuit voltage,
\phi_{\operatorname{act}}
is the activation over-potential,
\phi_{\operatorname{conc}}
is the concentration over-potential and
\phi_{\operatorname{ohm}}
denotes the ohmic over-potential, cf.
[
33
]
. Sometimes additional terms are added, cf.
[
36
]
. See also
[
43
]
for a discussion about the interpretation of some of these potentials.

In our approach we deliberately do not perform any such decomposition but instead try to understand the type of measurements which are necessary in order to be able to reconstruct the dependence of the full potential
\phi
on quantities such as the temperature and concentrations. We further point out that, in view of the ideal gas law, the pressure of the system should be determined by the concentrations and the temperature of the system and therefore a pressure dependence is implicitly taken into account in our approach.

Приложение
B
An extended model including electric forces

While modelling the evolution of the ion concentrations, cf.
Section

A.1
, we neglected electric forces, which may, however, be of relevance in real life electrolyser devices. Therefore, a more realistic model should include these forces. Following Teorell’s work
[
49
]
, see also
[
16
]
for other types of diffusion models, one can make the following more realistic ansatz

\displaystyle N_{i}=-D_{i}\nabla c_{i}+\mu_{i}q_{i}c_{i}E

(B1)

where
N_{i}
denotes the particle flux of the
i
-th species,
D_{i}
is the diffusion coefficient of the
i
-th particle,
c_{i}
is the concentration of the
i
-th particle (in number of particles per volume),
\mu_{i}
is the mobility of the
i
-th species,
q_{i}
is the charge of the
i
-th species and
E
is the electric field.

Inserting this expression into Fick’s second law (
A1
) gives a modified continuity equation of the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}c_{i}+\operatorname{div}(\mu_{i}c_{i}q_{i}E-D_{i}\nabla c_{i})=g_{i}.

(B2)

In the present work, we focus on the simpler model (
A2
). Future work may explore the more realistic model (
B2
).
