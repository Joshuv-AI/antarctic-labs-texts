Criterion validity of brief, comprehensive, and latent measures of intrinsic capacity against frailty, disability, and mortality in older adults
Authors: Sunyoung Kim, Lan Luo, Sandra M. Shi, Chan Mi Park, Kevin T. Pritchard, Miji Kim, Chang Won Won, Dae Hyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntrinsic capacity (IC), the composite of physical and mental capacities, underpins the WHO Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) program. IC may be measured as a brief screen, a comprehensive assessment, or a latent factor score; whether the simpler measures retain the prognostic information of the complex ones remains untested within a single population.MethodsIn the nationwide Korean Frailty and Aging Cohort Study, 3011 community-dwelling adults aged 70–84 years (mean age, 76.0 [SD, 3.9] years; 52.5% women) were assessed for frailty and for activities of daily living (ADL) and instrumental ADL (IADL) disability and followed for mortality (median, 8.0 years). IC was measured by a Korean ICOPE screening score (K-ICOPE), a comprehensive clinical score (CIC), and a bifactor latent score (LIC); differences in discrimination were tested formally.ResultsDiscrimination for frailty rose with measurement intensity (area under the curve, 0.82, 0.86, 0.90; all pairwise P < .001) but not for ADL or IADL disability (all P ≥ .25). There were 500 deaths. Adjusted for sociodemographics and multimorbidity, each 1-SD higher IC was associated with lower mortality (hazard ratio 0.83 [95% CI, 0.75–0.91], 0.71 [0.64–0.78], 0.65 [0.58–0.73]), with closely similar discrimination (C statistic, 0.723, 0.731, 0.736; largest difference, 0.013 [0.005–0.021]). Discrimination from IC alone was lower and similar across measures (0.583, 0.604, 0.585).ConclusionsMeasurement intensity improved the characterization of frailty but added little to the ranking of long-term mortality risk. Because K-ICOPE was aligned by construction to the latent structure, these findings support the potential feasibility of brief ICOPE-style screening rather than validating the WHO instrument.

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines intrinsic capacity (IC) as the composite of an individual's physical and mental capacities across locomotor, vitality, cognitive, psychological, and sensory domains [1,2]. Unlike disease-based approaches, IC emphasizes functional reserve, and lower IC has been associated with frailty, disability, and mortality across diverse populations [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. IC decline is now coded in ICD-11 [8], and IC underpins the WHO Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) program, whose Step 1 screening is designed to detect possible declines for confirmation at Step 2 and to trigger preventive action, not to diagnose frailty [[9], [10], [11]].

IC has been operationalized in three broad ways: brief ICOPE-style screening based on self-reported items, comprehensive assessment with performance-based and clinician-administered instruments, and latent variable models that treat IC as a continuous multidimensional construct [2,12]. Latent and comprehensive measures have been validated against frailty, disability, and mortality in European and Asian cohorts [3,4,6,7,14,16], and ICOPE screening has been evaluated for implementation and for agreement with Step 2 assessment [11,13,15]. Three gaps remain. First, the three levels of measurement have not been applied to the same participants and compared against the same outcomes, so the prognostic cost of simplification is unknown [3,4,6,15,16]. Second, where more than one operationalization has been examined, differences in discrimination have generally been described rather than tested. Third, because the Fried phenotype shares performance indicators with IC, an apparent advantage of intensive measurement for frailty may reflect shared measurement rather than better capture of capacity, and that contribution has not been quantified.

IC, frailty, and disability are increasingly understood as distinct but related constructs along a continuum of healthy aging, in which declining capacity precedes and predicts the frailty state, which in turn precedes dependence [[17], [18], [19]]. Treating frailty and disability as clinically salient landmarks and mortality as the terminal endpoint, we asked, within one nationwide cohort, how much of the criterion validity of complex IC measurement is retained when IC is measured with a brief ICOPE-style screen. Criterion validity is used throughout in the statistical sense of comparative validity against reference outcomes, not as conceptual validation of one construct by another [17]. We hypothesized that discrimination for frailty and disability would improve with measurement intensity, because both outcomes are defined by thresholds on performance measures that the more intensive instruments capture directly, whereas the prognostic information for mortality would be largely preserved by the brief screen, because mortality is a distal endpoint carried chiefly by the general IC factor that all three operationalizations share [5,20].

Methods

Data source and study population

We used data from the Korean Frailty and Aging Cohort Study (KFACS), a nationwide multicenter cohort of community-dwelling older adults recruited at 10 sites across urban, suburban, and rural areas of South Korea between May 2016 and November 2017 [21]. Participants aged 70–84 years were enrolled using quota sampling stratified by 5-year age group and sex. Individuals with diagnosed dementia or major communication difficulties were excluded [21]. Participants who withdrew consent were excluded; the derivation of the analytic sample is reported in the Results and in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Complete-case analysis was primary for all association models, with multiple imputation as a sensitivity analysis. Hot-deck imputation of missing baseline values within strata of sex, age group, frailty status, and multimorbidity was retained for the descriptive tables, the measurement model, the reference centiles, and the estimates of discrimination for frailty and disability, for which preserving the empirical indicator distributions matters [22]. Because that imputation is conditioned on frailty, which is itself an outcome, the measurement model was also refitted in the participants with complete indicator data, without imputation of any kind (Supplementary Table S15).

The KFACS protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kyung Hee University Medical Center, and all participants provided written informed consent [21]. This secondary analysis followed the STROBE guidelines [23].

Measurement of intrinsic capacity

We operationalized IC at three levels of measurement (Supplementary Table S1) [3,14,24]. The latent IC score (LIC), used as the reference measure, was derived from a bifactor confirmatory factor model of 17 indicators covering the five ICOPE domains. The Korean ICOPE screening tool (K-ICOPE) was adapted from the WHO ICOPE screening tests using self-reported items available in KFACS while preserving alignment with the LIC structure (Supplementary Tables S1B and S2) [10]. K-ICOPE yields a summary score from 0–100, with higher scores indicating better IC. The comprehensive IC score (CIC) was constructed from validated domain-specific clinical instruments [10,[25], [26], [27], [28], [29]]. These were performance-based physical function measures for locomotion, nutritional assessment and handgrip strength for vitality, graded visual and hearing acuity for sensory function, the Mini-Mental State Examination for cognition, and the Geriatric Depression Scale for psychological function. Domain scores were transformed to a 0-to-100 scale and CIC was their unweighted mean; within the vitality and the sensory domain the two constituent instruments were weighted equally (Supplementary Table S1C) [30].

The three measures differ in measurement intensity, by which we mean the depth of data collection required to produce a score and the corresponding burden on patients, assessors, and health systems. K-ICOPE consists of brief self-reported items that can be completed in a few minutes at a routine visit and requires no equipment. CIC additionally requires performance-based and clinician-administered instruments, trained assessors, and dedicated equipment. LIC requires the full 17-indicator battery together with a fitted latent measurement model, and is therefore a research-grade rather than a point-of-care measure.

Measurement of outcomes and covariates

Frailty was assessed using the Fried phenotype (weakness, slowness, weight loss, exhaustion, and low physical activity) and classified as robust (0 criteria), prefrail (1–2), or frail (3 or more) [21,31]. Following the cohort protocol and the original Cardiovascular Health Study operationalization, the phenotype was applied to all community-dwelling participants, including the 337 (11.2%) with ADL dependence. Dependence in an activity of daily living does not invariably mark progression beyond frailty, since it may reflect long-standing or non-geriatric impairment in an otherwise physiologically robust individual. A sensitivity analysis restricted to ADL-independent participants is reported (Supplementary Table S13).

Exhaustion, handgrip strength, and gait speed enter both the Fried phenotype and the LIC. Handgrip strength is also a component of the CIC vitality domain and gait speed of its locomotor domain through the Short Physical Performance Battery, and all three are represented in K-ICOPE by the self-reported mobility and vitality items. Part of the frailty discrimination of all three measures therefore reflects shared indicators, and the frailty comparison is interpreted as comparative predictive validity in the presence of partially shared measurement.

Disability was defined as self-reported dependence in any activity of daily living (ADL) or instrumental activity of daily living (IADL) [32]. All-cause mortality was ascertained through biennial assessments and a 2025 update, using participant contact, household informants, and hospital records. For decedents, the date of death was taken from the informant-reported month when available and otherwise estimated as the midpoint between the last assessment at which the participant was known to be alive and the assessment at which death was reported. Follow-up time was measured from the date of the baseline assessment to the assigned date of death for decedents and to the date of the last completed assessment or contact for survivors, who were censored at that date; because vital status was ascertained at assessments rather than continuously, event times are assigned rather than observed dates.

Baseline covariates were age, sex, living arrangement, household income (≥3,000,000 vs <3,000,000 Korean won per month), education (>6 vs ≤6 years), physical activity, polypharmacy (≥5 medications), and multimorbidity (0, 1–2, or ≥3 chronic conditions). Physical activity was included in the mortality models but omitted from the frailty models, because low physical activity is itself a component of the Fried phenotype. The income threshold corresponds to approximately US$2600 at the 2016 average exchange rate of about 1160 Korean won per US dollar [21]. Health-related quality of life was assessed with the SF-12 [33].

Statistical analysis

The LIC was the general-factor score from a bifactor model of the 17 IC indicators, standardized to a mean of 0 and SD of 1. Sex- and age-specific reference centiles for all three measures were estimated using generalized additive models for location, scale and shape [[34], [35], [36], [37]]. Omega hierarchical and omega total were computed to quantify the variance attributable to the general IC factor. Convergent validity was assessed using Spearman rank correlations.

Associations with frailty and with ADL and IADL disability were examined using multivariable logistic regression, and associations with all-cause mortality using Cox regression. Each IC measure was modeled continuously, per 1-SD higher score. Models were unadjusted (Model 1), adjusted for sociodemographic characteristics, polypharmacy, and multimorbidity (Model 2), and, for mortality only, additionally adjusted for frailty and ADL and IADL disability (Model 3). Model 3 is presented as a mediator-adjusted secondary analysis, because frailty and disability plausibly lie on the causal pathway from low IC to death and share measurement with the exposure. Discrimination was quantified using the area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC) for the cross-sectional outcomes and Harrell's C statistic for mortality [38]. Because the AUC requires only the IC score and the outcome, discrimination for frailty and for disability was estimated in the full frozen analytic sample (N = 3011), whereas the regression models are complete-case on the covariates; the analytic N and number of events are reported separately for the association and the discrimination estimates (Supplementary Table S6a). Because that sample retains hot-deck-imputed indicator and frailty-component values, discrimination was also estimated in the participants in whom all five Fried components were observed (Supplementary Table S16), in the ADL-independent complete-case samples (Supplementary Table S13), and with the measurement model refitted without imputation of any kind (Supplementary Table S15). To keep the C statistics comparable, Models 1–3 were fitted in an identical complete-case sample.

Differences in discrimination were tested formally. Pairwise differences in AUC were assessed with the DeLong test for correlated curves. Pairwise differences in the C statistic were estimated by paired bootstrap with 2000 resamples.

In sensitivity analyses, overlap-free discrimination was re-estimated after removing the indicators shared with the Fried phenotype. These were gait speed, exhaustion, and handgrip strength for the LIC; the SPPB-based locomotor domain, the handgrip component of the vitality domain, and the MNA appetite and weight-loss items for the CIC; and the mobility and vitality domains for K-ICOPE. Because the bifactor model is not identified once gait speed is removed, the LIC exclusion series was fitted as a single general factor; the effect of shared measurement is therefore isolated by comparison with a matched single-factor model fitted to all 17 indicators, not with the primary bifactor score (Supplementary Table S5). Because the bifactor model was fitted and evaluated in the same sample, the frailty discrimination of the LIC was re-estimated by repeated stratified 10-fold cross-validation with 10 repetitions, refitting the measurement model in each training fold and scoring the held-out fold, and the DeLong comparisons with K-ICOPE and CIC were repeated using the cross-validated LIC score, which carries no in-sample optimism. A bootstrap optimism correction with the measurement model refitted in each of 1000 resamples is reported alongside it, together with the convergence rate and a comparison of converged and non-converged resamples (Supplementary Table S12). Association models were repeated under multiple imputation by chained equations, with m = 20 and with the outcomes and the Nelson-Aalen cumulative hazard included in the imputation model (Supplementary Table S11). They were also repeated separately by sex, restricted to ADL-independent participants (Supplementary Table S13), and using attained age as the time scale (Supplementary Table S10).

All analyses were generated from a single frozen analytic dataset with a fixed random seed, and programmatic assertions verified that every marginal total agreed across tables before results were reported. Analyses were performed using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Study population

Of 3014 participants, three withdrew consent, leaving 3011 in the analytic sample (Supplementary Figure S1). The study population had a mean age of 76.0 years (SD, 3.9) and 52.5% were women. Missing baseline values among the 3011 participants were as follows: at least one of the 17 latent IC indicators in 88 participants (2.9%), one or more frailty components in 107 (3.6%), household income in 212 (7.0%), and the SF-12 component scores in 43 (1.4%). The complete-case samples for the association models were 2732 for K-ICOPE, 2680 for CIC, and 2690 for LIC.

Derivation of IC measures

The bifactor model fitted the 17-indicator structure well (CFI, 0.982; TLI, 0.977; RMSEA, 0.049; SRMR, 0.048; Fig. 1). Omega hierarchical was 0.73 and omega total 0.87, supporting a substantive general IC factor with additional domain-specific variance. Frailty

Lower IC was associated with frailty across all three measures (Table 2). ADL and IADL disability

Lower IC was also associated with disability, but across a much narrower range (Table 2). ADL disability rose from 4.3% (11 of 255) above the 90th centile to 26.7% (81 of 303) at or below the 10th centile for K-ICOPE, from 3.7% (11 of 299) to 25.5% (77 of 302) for CIC, and from 3.7% (11 of 301) to 27.8% (84 of 302) for LIC. The corresponding gradients for IADL disability were 20.0% (51 of 255) to 39.6% (120 of 303), 19.4% (58 of 299) to 37.7% (114 of 302), and 23.6% (71 of 301) to 42.1% (127 of 302).

Each 1-SD higher IC was associated with lower odds of ADL disability (odds ratio 0.62 [95% CI, 0.54–0.70] for K-ICOPE, 0.65 [0.57–0.74] for CIC, and 0.62 [0.53–0.73] for LIC) and of IADL disability (0.68 [0.61–0.75], 0.67 [0.60–0.75], and 0.59 [0.51–0.67], respectively) (Supplementary Table S6a). Unlike frailty, for which the odds ratios separated sharply across the three measures, the disability associations were of similar magnitude whatever the measurement intensity.

In contrast to frailty, discrimination for disability was modest and did not differ across measures: ADL AUC 0.680 for K-ICOPE, 0.669 for CIC, and 0.677 for LIC; IADL AUC 0.590, 0.589, and 0.582 (N = 3011; 337 with ADL and 655 with IADL disability). None of the six pairwise DeLong comparisons approached significance (all P ≥ .25). Removing the shared indicators left disability discrimination essentially unchanged for all three measures (Supplementary Table S5).

In multivariable models including all three measures, frailty and ADL and IADL disability were the strongest independent correlates of IC (Supplementary Figure S4). Sex-stratified ADL and IADL dependence across LIC tertiles is shown in Supplementary Table S7. Within both sexes, dependence was lower at higher tertiles, whereas pooled IADL dependence was non-monotonic because the highest tertile was predominantly male and IADL dependence was more common among men at every level of IC.

Discussion

This study asked how much of the criterion validity of complex intrinsic capacity measurement is retained when IC is assessed with instruments simple enough to deploy at scale. In this nationwide cohort of community-dwelling Korean adults aged 70–84 years, K-ICOPE, CIC, and LIC were each associated with frailty, disability, and all-cause mortality, but their performance diverged sharply by outcome. Measurement intensity markedly improved discrimination for frailty, from 0.82 for the brief screen to 0.90 for the latent score, and every pairwise difference was significant, including when the latent score was cross-validated. Its effect on mortality discrimination was of a different order: the largest pairwise difference was 0.013 (95% CI, 0.005–0.021), statistically distinguishable from zero but three to six times smaller than the differences observed for frailty and far too small to change how an individual would be classified. Discrimination for disability was modest for all three measures and statistically indistinguishable between them, consistent with disability being shaped by environmental and social supports beyond intrinsic capacity. As ICOPE-based care is scaled across primary care and community settings, locally adapted screening tools are increasingly being validated [3,6,14,39]. Intrinsic capacity trajectories and composite frailty–intrinsic capacity indices have likewise been associated with incident disability in other Asian cohorts [40,41].

The unadjusted analyses sharpen this picture. Ranked on intrinsic capacity alone, the three measures performed almost identically for mortality (C statistic, 0.583, 0.604, and 0.585), and the comprehensive score, not the latent one, was nominally highest. Whatever the latent model adds is a better representation of the frailty phenotype, not a better ordering of long-term survival. How IC should be measured therefore follows from the clinical objective. Little long-term prognostic information was lost by simplification to a brief ICOPE-style screen, whereas comprehensive or latent assessment is needed to characterize frailty and functional reserve.

In the bifactor model, vitality, psychological, and locomotor function were central to the construct [20,42]. The two single-item sensory indicators loaded on the general factor alone and formed no separable sensory factor [16,43]. Vision and hearing contributed least as a domain (mean loading, 0.18), but the pattern is more specific than a sensory deficit. All three psychological indicators loaded substantially on the general factor (motivation, 0.64; negative affect, 0.53; positive affect, 0.52). That domain therefore had the highest mean loading (0.56), ahead of vitality (0.51), locomotor function (0.49), cognition (0.36), and sensory function (0.18). The cognitive indicators were heterogeneous: attention/calculation and visuospatial items loaded comparably with the locomotor indicators (0.52 and 0.51), whereas verbal memory and language loaded at 0.22 and 0.24, below the single-item vision indicator (0.28). Contributions to the general factor were weakest for hearing (0.08) and then, in a similar band, for verbal memory, language, and vision (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S14). The LIC therefore under-represents hearing in particular, and verbal cognitive function alongside vision, rather than sensory function as a whole. It is best read as a summary of intrinsic capacity weighted toward vitality, motivation, and mobility. Women had lower IC than men across the age range, supporting sex-specific centiles [6,44].

Within the continuum framing set out in the Introduction, the frailty and disability analyses reported here are comparative criterion validity against clinically salient landmarks, not conceptual validation of one construct by another. Because exhaustion, handgrip strength, and gait speed are shared between the Fried phenotype and all three IC measures, the LIC frailty AUC of 0.902 should be read as an upper bound.

The overlap analysis quantifies how much of that discrimination is borrowed. Against a matched single-factor baseline of 0.871, removing the three shared indicators reduced frailty discrimination to 0.821, a loss of 0.050. Disability discrimination was unaffected. The two effects should not be summed with the difference between the bifactor and single-factor specifications, since that difference reflects model form rather than shared measurement, and for the same reason the rank order of the three overlap-free scores is descriptive rather than a test of whether the ordering of the three measures survives the removal of shared indicators. Shared indicators therefore contributed materially to frailty prediction while having no discernible influence on disability prediction, where the association with IC is not an artifact of overlapping items.

The real-world context differs by setting. ICOPE screening is being adopted and evaluated within primary care and public health systems in a growing number of countries [13,45], whereas brief frailty instruments such as the FRAIL scale and the electronic frailty index are commonly used in hospital-based specialty settings. Our comparison speaks to the first setting. It asks what prognostic information the community-deployable screening tier retains relative to research-grade measurement, which is the operational question facing systems scaling ICOPE.

The bifactor structure of IC has been reproduced in cohorts across Europe and Asia [3,6,14,16,36], but the indicators that anchor the general factor are not fixed. Locomotor and cognitive measures carry the most weight in several Western cohorts, with mobility and delayed recall in SHARE [14] and gait speed and balance in ELSA [3], while cognitive recall dominates in CHARLS [6] and grip strength in a Hong Kong cohort [16]. In KFACS the individual indicators contributing most were exhaustion, from the vitality domain, and motivation, from the psychological domain, followed by handgrip strength and gait speed; at the domain level the psychological and vitality indicators carried the most weight. Which indicators weigh most reflects the measurement battery and the population rather than a fixed hierarchy. The strong vitality contribution in our cohort accords with the WHO account of vitality as the physiological substrate of IC [42].

The IC-mortality associations persisted after adjustment for sociodemographic factors and multimorbidity, and for the two more intensive measures after further adjustment for frailty and disability [4,6,7,46]. Because frailty and disability are plausible mediators and share indicators with the IC measures, the mediator-adjusted hazard ratios estimate the association not transmitted through, or shared with, frailty and disability, rather than the total association. After that adjustment, the association of the brief screen with mortality was no longer evident (0.90; 0.81–1.01), whereas the comprehensive and latent scores retained clear associations. This is the expected consequence of conditioning on variables that lie on the pathway and share measurement with the exposure, and it is why the case for brief screening rests on the total association and on rank discrimination, which the brief screen retains, rather than on this estimate. Comparable independent prognostic value has been reported in large Western cohorts modeling IC as a latent or comprehensive construct [3,7,14,36].

In health systems adopting ICOPE, a brief screen administered at routine primary-care appointments identifies older adults with possible declines in intrinsic capacity [10,11]. Its clinical value lies less in initiating further diagnostic testing than in shortening the path from detection to action. A positive screen should efficiently trigger domain-directed referral according to the domain affected: to audiology, optometry, physical and occupational therapy, speech-language pathology, nutrition, or mental health services. The aim is that declining capacity is remediated, compensated for, or adapted to before it progresses to frailty and dependence [18]. Within the WHO pathway, Step 2 assessment retains a role, but chiefly as the means of specifying which intervention is required rather than as a diagnostic end in itself.

Our findings add an empirical argument for that division of labor. The screening tier already carries almost all of the long-term prognostic information that far more intensive measurement provides, so there is little reason to use Step 2 for risk stratification. What Step 2 adds is a substantially better characterization of the frailty phenotype and of functional reserve. Step 1 determines who to look at; Step 2 determines what to do about it. Assessment intensity can therefore be matched to the clinical objective. Whether IC-guided stepped pathways of this kind delay functional decline and reduce hospitalizations should now be tested in pragmatic trials.

Strengths include nationwide recruitment, long-term mortality follow-up, a measurement model placing three IC operationalizations on a common footing, and formal testing of the differences in discrimination on which our conclusions rest. Several limitations remain.

First, KFACS used age-, sex-, and region-stratified quota sampling across 10 centers. Although nationwide, it was not post-stratified to census distributions and excluded the oldest-old (≥85 years), in whom intrinsic capacity and its prognostic associations may differ. The reference centiles should be regarded as cohort-based values for this age range rather than national norms [36,44].

Second, K-ICOPE was approximated from existing KFACS items rather than administered as the original WHO ICOPE instrument, and its cut points are specific to this operationalization. Because it was constructed to align with the latent IC structure, its convergence with LIC and its retention of prognostic information are partly built in by design. The reference centiles and screening cut points are population-specific and require re-derivation in each target setting before clinical use [10,15].

Third, sensory capacity was captured by single visual and auditory indicators, and hearing had both the weakest loading on the general factor and the highest proportion of missing data among the IC indicators. Vital status was ascertained only at biennial visits. Both features capture their domains incompletely. Non-differential misclassification would bias the IC-outcome associations toward the null, making these estimates conservative, but hearing loss is systematically associated with cognitive, psychological, and social factors, so the assumption of non-differential error cannot be taken for granted.

Fourth, the indicator set was constrained to the KFACS battery and is in that sense opportunistic. Social and environmental determinants, which in the WHO framework shape how capacity is expressed as functional ability, entered the analysis only as covariates rather than as part of the measurement model.

Finally, because the highest LIC tertile was predominantly male and IADL dependence was more prevalent among men, IADL disability did not decline monotonically across tertiles in the pooled sample, although it declined monotonically within each sex.

Conclusion

Operationalized as a brief screen, a comprehensive clinical assessment, or a latent construct, intrinsic capacity consistently identified older adults at increased risk of frailty, disability, and death. Measurement intensity improved the characterization of frailty substantially and significantly, but added almost nothing to the ranking of long-term mortality risk. Because K-ICOPE was constructed from cohort items aligned to the latent structure, these findings support the potential of brief ICOPE-style screening for population-scale risk stratification without establishing the scalability of the WHO instrument itself, which should be confirmed directly and with setting-specific cut points in the settings where ICOPE is deployed. As IC decline is incorporated into ICD-11 and ICOPE-based models of care, the practical implication is a division of labor: the simplest tier for population-scale case-finding, and comprehensive or latent measurement where frailty and functional reserve must be characterized in order to choose an intervention.
