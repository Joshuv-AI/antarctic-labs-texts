Perspectives of eating disorder clinicians on their professional well-being in the context of clinical care: a qualitative examination
Authors: Daria Hammond, Tanya Pardiwala, Sharon H. J. Hou, Sheila K. Marshall, Janet W. T. Mah, Jennifer S. Coelho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClinicians who provide clinical care for eating disorders (EDs) report stress, burnout, and system-level considerations that contribute to challenges with professional well-being. Yet, there are also positive factors that keep ED clinicians in their roles, though the factors that drive positive workplace well-being have been relatively under-explored in the literature. To elucidate both factors contributing to challenges and positive experiences in the workforce, we set out to explore the perceptions of eating disorder (ED) clinicians about engaging in care for individuals with EDs, and their experiences with their own well-being in the context of providing clinical care.MethodsA total of 27 clinicians working in ED services took part in an individual semi-structured interview. Clinicians were asked to share their experiences working with individuals who have EDs, their reflections on factors that contribute to workplace well-being, how they think about culture in their work, and for recommendations to improve clinician well-being and overall clinical care for EDs. Participants also completed online questionnaires assessing professional quality of life and vicarious resilience.ResultsUsing reflexive thematic analysis, five main themes were identified: (1) Lack of ED knowledge and understanding within the public and healthcare system; (2) Barriers and limitations to providing accessible, appropriate treatment; (3) The value of professional relationships, support, and collaboration; (4) The association between confidence and complexity of clinical presentations; and (5) The links between clinicians’ personal and professional well-being and experiences with provision of care.ConclusionsOur study highlights that when ED clinicians are able to provide care in a manner that is consistent with their professional desires and beliefs, they associate this with their own professional well-being. Conversely, acting in discordance with these values may occur alongside feelings of burnout and moral distress. The results underscore the importance of ED education and training within the broader healthcare context, and highlight clinicians’ lack of confidence in treating complex clinical cases, as well as a perceived reciprocal relationship between professional and personal well-being as factors that can contribute to well-being in the ED workforce.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14753-2.

Background

Eating disorders (EDs) can be long-standing and life threatening psychological and physical illnesses. As a result, there are ethical complexities for clinicians in providing care for EDs, particularly in the context of those that are severe and long-standing [1]. The complex clinical decision-making that is part of care for EDs may be associated with particularly high levels of burnout in clinicians, characterized as physical and emotional exhaustion with a gradual and cumulative onset [2]. Even prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, the vast majority of ED clinicians reported experiencing burnout [3]. While burnout was attributed to some of the clinical features associated with EDs, such as the long-standing duration of EDs in some individuals, Warren and colleagues [3] also found an additional influence from workplace and system-wide factors such as lack of organizational support and inadequate compensation. Burnout has been found to contribute significantly to employee turnover intention (i.e., plans to leave their position) among clinicians in the broader healthcare domain, and has also been negatively associated with quality of care and patient safety [4–6].

The COVID-19 pandemic seems to have exacerbated these concerns. With the challenges that arose during this pandemic, clinicians across healthcare fields have expressed increased turnover intention, citing factors such as higher psychological responses to stress and adverse working conditions [7]. Healthcare clinicians reported significantly higher levels of burnout compared to other occupational groups, including managers and teachers, following the pandemic [8]. The pandemic also led to surges in new presentations and hospitalizations for youth with restrictive EDs in Canada [9], which, coupled with existing limitations in the availability of specialized care for EDs, is associated with youth and caregivers experiencing challenges in accessing care [10]. Thus, while the provision of care for EDs has been reported by clinicians to be stressful and demanding for over a decade (e.g., 3), the surge in new presentations that occurred in the context of the pandemic may be contributing to stress, burnout, and poor professional well-being in ED clinicians.

Indeed, ED clinicians report stress, burnout, and perceptions of inadequate compensation and appreciation in their work, with approximately one-third of clinician respondents sharing a plan to leave their position within 1–2 years [10]. A recent review of clinician well-being further outlined the emotional toll experienced by ED clinicians [11], who commonly report high levels of compassion fatigue and emotional dissonance. Compassion fatigue has been defined as exhaustion arising from repeated exposure to another’s distress [12], while emotional dissonance is the suppression of emotions in order to remain neutral, or displaying emotions that do not align with one’s true feelings. ED clinicians cited factors such as workplace demands and job insecurity contributing to compassion fatigue and emotional dissonance [11].

Both compassion fatigue and emotional dissonance have been associated with turnover intention, as well as care outcomes and effectiveness among clinicians in the broader healthcare field [13–16]. Although the role of compassion fatigue and emotional dissonance on turnover intention has not (to the authors knowledge) been assessed in ED clinicians specifically, the established associations in healthcare clinicians are also likely relevant in an ED-specific context. Further, a recent meta-ethnography [17] suggested that ED clinicians feel they are limited in their capacity to help their clients, which is associated with how they experience and enjoy their profession, their working alliances with other healthcare providers, and perceptions of their patients.

Despite challenges of clinical care that have been noted by ED clinicians, ED clinicians generally have a positive attitude towards their work [11]. A model of work satisfaction drawn from another area of clinical care, clinicians providing care for refugees and asylum seekers, suggests that work satisfaction is associated with cultural awareness and clinician exposure to vicarious trauma and resilience [18]. This model may be helpful in conceptualizing factors that contribute to positive clinician experiences. In the model proposed by Puvimanasinghe and colleagues [18], clinicians experience benefits from seeing perseverance and growth in the clients they are working with (vicarious resilience), which is countered by their exposure to the painful and traumatic experiences of the clients (vicarious trauma). Given the long-standing and life-threatening nature of EDs, seeing individuals overcome adverse or traumatic experiences, and make progress towards recovery may evoke the unique, positive effects of vicarious resilience. ED services may also be associated with a risk of iatrogenic trauma [19].

To explore experiences related to vicarious trauma and vicarious resilience in ED clinicians, our team conducted a scoping review, with a goal of identifying potential protective factors that foster well-being. The significance of work, in which ED clinicians find meaning and satisfaction in their work, combined with exposure to resilience through hope and observation of change are factors that appear to support professional well-being [20]. However, our team identified that the focus of research is on risk and negative work experiences, rather than on resilience and well-being [20].

Taken together, factors such as stress, burnout, job satisfaction, and vicarious experiences of resilience and trauma can be understood through the lens of clinicians’ perceived ability to provide care in ways that are consistent with their personal and professional values, morals, and clinical judgement. Acting in discordance with these desires may be associated with feelings of stress, burnout, or vicarious trauma, whereas the delivery of care in alignment with these motivations may support professional well-being and experiences of vicarious resilience. The methods presented in this paper are part of a larger study where we explored clinician well-being and cultural considerations in ED healthcare professionals. Although we had initially conceptualized that cultural considerations and clinician well-being would be directly related, as in the model developed by Puvimanasinghe and colleagues [18], clinicians’ reflections about cultural considerations in ED care was distinct from their perspectives on professional well-being, and we focus on clinician perspectives about professional well-being in the current paper.

Drawing upon the findings of our scoping review [20], we sought to further explore the potential risk and protective factors for ED clinician well-being, with an emphasis on experiences related to vicarious resilience and vicarious trauma. The main objective of this study is to explore ED clinicians’ perceptions of professional well-being within the context of delivering care in ways that align with their beliefs, values, and clinical judgement, and how they view associations between the experiences of the individuals with EDs and their own professional well-being.

Methods

Procedure

Potential participants were invited to the study through member distribution lists (Eating Disorders Association of Canada) and provincial email distribution lists for ED professionals (e.g., British Columbia Eating Disorders Community of Practice, and mailing lists for staff of three provincial ED programs). We briefly advertised on a participant recruitment platform through a national organization, but due to detection of suspicious inquiries from individuals who did not appear to be healthcare professionals based on their responses to a screening discussion with the research team, we removed the advertisements for the study from this site. Study invitations informed participants of the study’s objective to understand factors associated with feelings of well-being in healthcare professionals who provide care for individuals with EDs, and how cultural considerations are associated with well-being, as part of the larger study (results related to perception of the role of cultural consideration in clinical care for EDs are presented separately; [21]).

Participants who were employed in a healthcare or clinical setting focused on EDs in Canada, proficient in English, and had access to a device with a working internet connection were eligible to participate. Interested and eligible individuals were invited to contact the research team, who then scheduled a meeting via telephone or virtual platform to review study information, including informed consent details. Written consent was obtained using the REDCap e-consent framework [22], an electronic data capture tool hosted at the BC Children’s Hospital Research Institute.

After obtaining consent, participants were invited to complete online questionnaires through REDCap, as outlined in the measures section. Questionnaires could be completed prior to or after the interview. Participants took part in a 45–60 min, one-on-one semi-structured interview via the video conferencing software Zoom. During the interview, participants were asked to share their experiences working with individuals who have EDs, their reflections on factors that contribute to workplace well-being, how they think about culture in their work, and for recommendations to improve clinician well-being and overall clinical care for EDs. The interview script is available in supplementary materials. Participants were offered a token of appreciation of $40 CAD in gift cards after completion of the interview and questionnaire.

The interviews were conducted by 4 members of the research team, with the majority of interviews (n = 24/27, 88.9%) led by co-authors DH and TP, and two additional team members providing coverage for interviews on days that these lead interviewers were unexpectedly not available. DH is a White, cisgender, and Canadian woman. TP is a first-generation South Asian immigrant and cisgender woman. Both interviewers have undergraduate training in psychology, and had previous work experience in ED settings at the time of leading the interviews. We incorporated reflexivity into our methodological approach to acknowledge and consider how our own backgrounds might shape our interpretations during the interviews, as well as during the analytical process. To mitigate potential perceived conflicts of interest, only research team members who were not involved in clinical care were involved in recruitment and interviews. Audio from the interview was recorded using a digital recording device and professionally transcribed verbatim. Transcripts were de-identified prior to analysis.

The study was approved by the University of British Columbia/Children’s and Women’s Hospital and Health Centre of British Columbia Research Ethics Board (protocol H23-00191).

Measures

Quantitative measures

Participants were invited to complete a questionnaire package, including measures of demographics, professional quality of life and vicarious resilience (as well as additional measures related to the broader research question about cultural considerations in ED care). These measures were intended to provide contextual information for our qualitative findings.

Professional Quality of Life Compassion satisfaction and fatigue, burnout, and secondary traumatic stress were measured using the Professional Quality of Life scale (ProQOL-5; [23], a 30-item measure in which participants were asked to indicate how often they agreed with each statement (e.g., ‘I feel connected to others’). Responses were made using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from ‘Never’ (1) to ‘Very Often’ (5). Items were independently grouped into compassion satisfaction, burnout, and secondary traumatic stress subscales, then summed to create a total score for each. Responses could range from 10 to 50, with a score of 22 or less being considered low, 23–41 being considered moderate, and 42 or more being considered high.

Vicarious Resilience Participants responded to the Vicarious Resilience Scale (VRS; [24], a 27-item scale designed to examine the unique and positive effects that might transform clinicians in response to witnessing trauma survivors’ recovery. The Vicarious Resilience Scale was developed to assess vicarious resilience working with survivors of severe traumas, such as torture, kidnapping, and interpersonal violence. However, the scope of the construct has expanded to assess helping professionals working with individuals who have survived a variety of difficult circumstances, such as those with anxiety, depression, and addiction [25]. Participants were asked to reflect on their experiences working with individuals who have survived traumas, and to indicate how their approach to their work and their own worldview might have changed following these events (e.g., ‘Better able to cope with uncertainties’). Responses were made using a 6-point Likert scale, ranging from ‘Did not experience this’ (0), to ‘Experienced this to a very great degree’ (5). Items were then summed to obtain an overall score, which could range from 0-135, with higher scores indicating higher levels of vicarious resilience.

Analyses

Quantitative measures

Descriptive data collected from the questionnaires, including demographics, professional quality of life and vicarious resilience, were examined using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 31; IBM Corp., 2023).

Qualitative analyses

We employed reflective thematic analysis and utilized Braun and Clark’s [26] recommended steps for qualitative research as a guiding approach. Transcripts were de-identified and first closely reviewed in NVIVO 1.7.2 by two members of the research team (DH and TP) to familiarize themselves with the data and develop a coding framework, which was further refined through team discussion. DH then independently coded 21 transcripts, and TP independently coded 6. To ensure consensus in the coding process, SH randomly selected one transcript to code, which was then reviewed by DH and TP. JSC also reviewed all transcripts to support the development of codes. The coded data were then combined into potential themes, which were finalized during a collaborative meeting with all co-authors. All team members examined and discussed the final themes, checking for bias. Braun and Clark’s method emphasizes interpretation and reflexivity as opposed to interrater reliability. Thus, coding procedures were not established to ensure interrater reliability or consistency, but rather to establish reflexive engagement, refine analytic interpretations, and examine how team members’ assumptions and perspectives shaped theme development.

Recruitment was open across Canada; however, the majority of participants were based in BC (n = 25; 92.6%), and a small number of participants from Alberta (n = 2; 7.4%). Participants came from a range of clinical backgrounds. Some worked in medical roles such as nursing and medicine (n = 10; 37.0%), while the rest worked in allied health roles such as counselling, psychology, social work, and dietetics (n = 17; 62.9%). Interview analysis

Five themes were derived from our reflective thematic analyses: (1) Lack of eating disorder knowledge and understanding within the public and healthcare system; (2) Barriers and limitations to providing accessible, appropriate treatment; (3) The value of professional relationships, support, and collaboration; (4) The association between confidence and complexity of clinical presentations; and (5) The links between clinicians’ personal and professional well-being and experiences with provision of care.

Theme 1: Lack of eating disorder knowledge and understanding within the public and healthcare system

A prevailing factor that clinicians felt impeded their ability to provide clinical care in the way they desired revolved around the limited knowledge and recognition about EDs both within and outside the workforce. More specifically, clinicians frequently noted that limited knowledge about EDs within practitioners working in the broad healthcare system (e.g., general practitioners, staff working in emergency settings, etc.) often hinders individuals from accessing appropriate care. Some clinicians felt that this delay led to more severe symptom presentations when individuals finally received proper care for their ED and highlighted how this ultimately reflected on their own work.We find that a lot of the [emergency doctors] don’t necessarily have a good understanding of eating disorders. And often, that client won’t be admitted and will be discharged from the [emergency room]. Sometimes they need to have multiple visits before they’re actually admitted. (HCP-11)

We find that a lot of the [emergency doctors] don’t necessarily have a good understanding of eating disorders. And often, that client won’t be admitted and will be discharged from the [emergency room]. Sometimes they need to have multiple visits before they’re actually admitted. (HCP-11)

Clinicians also highlighted that the lack of knowledge and education on part of other healthcare providers could often hinder their own clinical care provision and add an additional burden to their own work by invalidating the individual and their experiences.One of the challenges of working within the medical system is that medical care providers don’t necessarily have enough knowledge or education in how to treat eating disorders, which impacts how clients perceive treatment when they come to see us. In addition to the knowledge limitations within the healthcare system, clinicians also indicated that the families of individuals with EDs often have conflicting perspectives about EDs and the treatment process. For instance, families may differ on their outlook regarding the seriousness of a diagnosis, and clinicians commented that this can act as a barrier to treatment provision by influencing the individual’s own perspective of their ED as well as their engagement with the clinician and the program. Clinicians also felt that there could often be a disconnect between families and themselves regarding the speed or efficacy of treatment.Some families will say – why isn’t my daughter better? Why isn’t my son better? They’re not getting better. You need to do better at your job. (HCP-6)

Finally, a number of clinicians highlighted the association between patient beliefs about their own diagnoses and behaviours with societal messaging. In particular, they indicated that the ideals of diet culture and ‘clean eating’ can often skew the client’s perspective of what is healthy and normal, thus impeding the clinician’s attempt to elucidate the harmful nature of their ED behaviours.It’s a lot harder to explain to youth that this is not actually normal, this is not actually healthier, this is actually your ED brain talking. But yes, I see that there’s 45 Tik Tok videos that normalized this for you, and you think everybody does that. (HCP-4)

Theme 2: Barriers and limitations to providing accessible, appropriate treatment

Clinicians reported feeling challenged by the limitations within the healthcare system. They frequently described the challenges of providing care within tightly structured or highly standardized program guidelines. Clinicians discussed the frequent disconnect between program operations with the realistic needs of their clients, noting factors such as strict operating hours not aligning well with their clients’ daily lives and schedules. Many clinicians also expressed feelings of frustration or helplessness that they were unable to provide their client with tailored and individualized care, particularly when both the clinician and the individual with an ED recognized that the standardized treatment protocols within the program were not helpful.It’s not black and white. There’s just so much grey, and the grey is what gets missed. And I think that’s what patients see is, you’re not listening to me. You’re not hearing me. This is what is hard for me and how it’s hard for me. And oftentimes we’ll say, well, I hear you, but this is how we do things. (HCP-10)

It’s not black and white. There’s just so much grey, and the grey is what gets missed. And I think that’s what patients see is, you’re not listening to me. You’re not hearing me. This is what is hard for me and how it’s hard for me. And oftentimes we’ll say, well, I hear you, but this is how we do things. (HCP-10)

More specifically, many clinicians commented that the limited flexibility surrounding meal plans were a source of frustration for both themselves and the individuals with EDs who they are supporting. Clinicians highlighted examples such as their programs not supporting individuals who are vegan or vegetarian, families of lower incomes who might struggle to afford the recommended items, and lacking meal plan options for patients of diverse backgrounds.I’ve seen patients who just straight up said – I’m not going to follow this meal plan. It’s just too much for me. And yet, we’re not willing to work with them on that. And so, sure enough, they go home, and they don’t follow it, and then you end up seeing them three or four months down the road, and they’re almost sicker than when they first came to see us. But it’s hard, because of many of them – it’s their way to cope, right? We want to be supportive; we want to be trauma-informed… we don’t want to discharge them. (HCP-21)

The delivered treatment model was perceived by many clinicians to not align well with the current needs or goals of individuals with EDs. Clinicians suggested that factors such as treatment engagement and motivation to change may emerge more readily in when there are appropriate supports available in a timely manner. For example, difficulties can arise with individuals who are involuntarily admitted, or who are admitted into treatment programs that do not align with their current state of readiness, suggesting that this can ultimately impede the provision of care, and the perceived effectiveness of the clinical care available.We can only help so much as healthcare professionals. It kind of has to be a two-way street, for patients to be able to stay engaged and want the treatment to continue with it. (HCP-10)

Long wait times for accessing treatment also came up as a common challenge associated with challenges to the workforce, while conversely early intervention and acting quickly on an individual’s request for support can facilitate positive experiences for the workforce. Clinicians reported feeling frustrated and demoralized that an individual’s condition can deteriorate while awaiting treatment, ultimately presenting with more severe symptoms by the time of entering clinical care.I think the challenges are the wait times. I think when people are ready to receive help, you need to act on it quick because willingness to change kind of goes [quickly]. It’s such a long wait… [People] get sicker. (HCP-16)

Theme 3: The value of professional relationships, support, and collaboration

Social relationships, support, and collaboration were particularly valued by clinicians. They noted their appreciation for the involvement and support of program leadership, suggesting that this assists them in their capacity to care for their patients. Specific examples included upper management advocating for manageable caseloads and clinician well-being, attending team meetings, and having a strong understanding of the needs and complexities of the program.When we have a manager who’s really involved with our program and really supportive, it makes it so much easier for us to be able to support those really complex clients. (HCP-11)

When we have a manager who’s really involved with our program and really supportive, it makes it so much easier for us to be able to support those really complex clients. (HCP-11)

Clinicians also highlighted the importance of regular clinical supervision and formalized regular debriefing, emphasizing the need to include them in more programs. Clinicians who had access to these supports described feeling confident in handling complex clinical situations.

Additionally, clinicians also expressed their desire and appreciation for social relationships with their team members. They highlighted the importance of having colleagues with whom they could debrief or troubleshoot difficult situations, noting the positive impacts of these relationships on both their treatment provision and own professional well-being.Having someone to troubleshoot, having team members where you can talk through difficult pieces of certain cases, and having people you can debrief with, who understand what you’re facing and if you’re having a hard time, or if you just need to chat […] Having people who are involved on your team and who can help… is huge. I think it’s very uplifting and very supportive, and it’s one of the reasons I continued this work, even early on when I found it scary. (HCP-15)

I think it’s very uplifting and very supportive, and it’s one of the reasons I continued this work, even early on when I found it scary. (HCP-15)

Lastly, clinicians discussed the importance of close social relationships for the clients themselves. In particular, they suggested that individuals with EDs who have loved ones that are supportive and involved in treatment may have better outcomes than those who are more isolated. Clinicians highlighted their appreciation for supportive families, noting that certain aspects of clinical care were facilitated with their involvement, which in turn can help clinicians through seeing positive outcomes and hope.Having a family that is able to provide the care they need to fight the eating disorder… The resources of having the support, and that everyone’s aligning with them and not the eating disorder, from my experience, has been such a great strength in terms of them having a better outcome and being able to fight the eating disorder more quickly. Or at least get to a stage where they can actually live their life and go to school and all these things. It’s really hard to do it when they’re alone. (HCP-6)

Theme 4: The association between confidence and complexity of clinical presentations

Clinicians reported that they could often feel uncertain when treating individuals who present with more complex needs, and less confident in their ability to provide adequate treatment. For instance, clinicians highlighted the challenges and uncertainty that come with ED presentations with other co-occurring concerns such as anxiety, depression, obsessive-compulsive disorder, self-injurious behaviours, neurodivergence, or substance use difficulties.They’re experiencing disordered eating, but they’re also potentially experiencing new diagnoses of anxiety and depression… I think navigating both of those at the same time, and [knowing] how to support that [is tough], because, sometimes, inadvertently, you’re helping one but feeding the other. (HCP-4)

They’re experiencing disordered eating, but they’re also potentially experiencing new diagnoses of anxiety and depression… I think navigating both of those at the same time, and [knowing] how to support that [is tough], because, sometimes, inadvertently, you’re helping one but feeding the other. (HCP-4)

Clinicians also noted that a history of adverse experiences within a healthcare setting was often linked with patient engagement and trust. They noted that individuals could be fearful or distrusting of healthcare providers.I’ve noticed some of the time if they’ve had a really challenging experience in the hospital, they kind of associate our team with that experience. It makes total sense, but sometimes it’s difficult to engage them in our services based on those experiences and the connections that were made. (HCP-13)

Theme 5: The links between clinicians’ personal and professional well-being and experiences with provision of care

Clinicians described their motivations for entering the ED field. Some were drawn to the work by lived experiences with EDs, or the lived experience of a loved one, while others described entering the field incidentally, then developing a professional interest and passion for EDs over time. Regardless of their initial motivations, clinicians reported that a personal sense of passion and interest in EDs were key factors to why they remained in the field, despite its challenges.

I had my own history of disordered eating. It’s really what drew me towards working in this field. [Eating disorders] are such a passion for me… It’s also very taxing, so the passionate part keeps me in. (HCP-20)

Discussion

The current study examined ED clinicians’ experiences and perspectives on their provision of care, and the links with their sense of professional well-being. Five main themes were derived regarding the experiences of clinicians in providing ED care, including limitations in knowledge and understanding about EDs within and outside the healthcare system, barriers within the system of care, social relationships and collaboration with team members, clinician confidence in providing care for complex cases, and how care provision is linked to clinician experiences with personal and professional well-being.

The initial goals of this study included a plan to assess the experiences of clinicians working in EDs that were related to vicarious trauma and vicarious resilience. Though clinicians didn’t directly use these terms in the interview, there were elements of these experiences highlighted by clinicians, including references to “being disheartened by the patients’ challenges”, or “taking it home” and ruminating on difficult circumstances, as well as observations that the needs of individuals with EDs were not being met by available services. These insights suggest that clinicians are affected by hearing about the adverse experiences of clients with EDs. While there were no direct references to vicarious resilience in the interviews, clinicians described feeling inspired, grateful, or uplifted when witnessing individuals make progress towards recovery. These reports align with our quantitative findings, where participants reported moderate levels of vicarious resilience. Importantly, the protective factors that were identified by ED clinicians included the power of working on a team, and having opportunities for support and consultation.

Organizational factors were identified as additional key considerations for professional well-being. For example, some ED clinicians described feeling burnt out when managing a heavy caseload with limited resources and minimal support from their colleagues or upper management. Conversely, supportive managers who are engaged in team discussions, and responsive to concerns about caseload supported professional well-being. While providing clinical care to those with complex clinical presentations can be difficult, clinicians highlighted how the support of program leadership could facilitate this clinical work. Many clinicians also highlighted their appreciation for collaborative care teams, and educational support that contributed to confidence and knowledge base. The current literature suggests that clinicians working in the broader healthcare field generally lack confidence in their knowledge of, and ability to treat EDs [27, 28]. Further, clinicians have previously reported a need for more rigorous training on newer ED diagnoses (e.g., Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder), comorbidities, and navigating gender diversity with respect to EDs, as suggested by a recent scoping review [11]. A need for more regular clinical supervision has also been highlighted [11].

However, there is limited literature examining these topics among clinicians working specifically in the ED field. The current study expands upon the current literature by exploring how ED clinicians specifically also lack confidence in treating individuals with EDs, in particular the additional complexities that often present with ED diagnoses. The results demonstrate the potential benefit of expanded training and consultation opportunities for ED clinicians, and highlight the perceptions of clinicians that their confidence is associated with professional wellbeing.

Almost all clinicians discussed the difficulties of navigating strict and standardized guidelines within the system of care, often describing how they felt they were unable to help in certain complex situations, despite wanting to. Factors such as a lack of ability to provide individualized care, inflexible meal plans, and limited options for alternate supports for patients with co-occurring behaviours such as self-injury contributed to clinicians’ sense of frustration about the system of care. Many clinicians also highlighted the factors that they felt prevented individuals with EDs from getting help in a timely manner, such as symptoms being overlooked by healthcare practitioners with limited ED knowledge, or being faced with long waitlists. System barriers were perceived to contribute to more severe ED presentations upon connecting with ED-specific care, which in turn led to additional challenges for the clinicians supporting these individuals. We found that the sentiments expressed by clinicians when facing these limiting factors align well with the concept of moral distress – an emotion experienced when a series of obstacles makes it difficult to pursue the evident right course of action [29]. While previous literature has highlighted feelings of moral distress among ED clinicians as an influential element to professional well-being (e.g., [9]), our findings delve into factors that clinicians perceive might be associated with these feelings. Among clinicians, moral distress has been associated with decreased job satisfaction, decreased satisfaction with their own provision of care, lower levels of work engagement, and intention to resign [29].

The disconnect experienced by clinicians with regards to the approaches available in programs and the needs of individuals with EDs suggests the need to integrate strategies for inclusive care, and finding the right care approach at the right time for individuals with EDs. The need to enhance trust and create more flexible treatment options through co-production (engagement with partners with lived experience) are critical steps in creating inclusive mental healthcare environments [30]. Of note, Downs [30] recommends reflection on whether the system of care is overly rigid or challenging for the diverse needs of service users. In the current study, when clinicians felt they were able to deliver care that responded to the needs of individuals, they reported feeling happier and more satisfied in their position. In contrast, working with individuals who had experienced past harms and trauma engaging in the healthcare system was associated with difficulties for clinicians in engaging with individuals. Downs [31] presents the concept of the mental healthcare system environment being a risk for harm to staff as well as patients, and identifying the importance of mutual healing for both clinicians and patients. Clinicians’ reflections in the current study align with this conceptualization of the linked well-being of clinicians and individuals with EDs, in that professional well-being can be fostered by seeing clinical care benefit clients.

Clinicians acknowledged that while the nature of their job could be difficult and emotionally demanding, feeling that they were able to help individuals with EDs to the best of their ability ultimately made the job worthwhile. These findings support those of a recent review [11], which found that despite high workload and emotional demands, ED clinicians felt rewarded by their work and showed high levels of job satisfaction.

Our findings are in line with previous literature broadly linking ED clinician well-being to organizational factors such education and training adequacy, collaboration, and available resources [11, 32]. The current results add to existing literature in their exploration of moral distress, and other potential contributing factors to professional well-being, including the role of confidence in treating complex clinical cases and the reciprocal relationship between professional and personal well-being. Our findings suggest that ED workforce retention strategies may include expanded ED education and training, directly for clinicians, given their reflections about how adequate training and supervision supports their work with complex clinical scenarios. However, the results also suggest that ED education and training for those working in the broader healthcare context (e.g., general practitioners, staff working in emergency settings, etc.) can also be supportive of the ED workforce, as building knowledge and skills in the broader workforce can have positive effects by reduced the duration of an untreated ED and supporting informed, timely intervention [33].

Strengths and limitations

The current study included clinicians from a variety of disciplines and healthcare contexts. We engaged with clinicians practicing within Western Canada (British Columbia and Alberta), offering insight into the experiences of ED clinicians working in a public health care context with respect to their perspectives about factors that both facilitate and hinder their professional well-being. Clinicians worked with individuals across the lifespan, and presented with a range of educational background and experiences. The findings of the current study should be considered in the context of its limitations. Given that study advertisements were sent out through a variety of avenues, and clinicians needed to reach out to share their interest in participation, it is possible that selection bias could lead clinicians who had a better sense of professional well-being to be more likely to agree to participate. The qualitative and cross-sectional nature of the study, while offering insights to the lived experiences of participants, also limits our ability to make any causal or predictive claims while offering insights to people’s lived experiences. Additionally, 44% of clinicians in our sample reported 4 years or less experience working in the ED field. Our sample was also predominately women, with two-thirds of the sample identifying as White. Younger healthcare providers with less experience, as well as women, are more likely to report feelings of burnout and compassion fatigue [34, 35]. Further research to elucidate the predictors of workforce wellbeing in ED clinicians is needed. Barriers to under-represented groups working in the field of EDs have been identified by our team (see e.g., [21]). Clinicians with intersecting or under-represented identities may experience different pressures in the workforce, and as a result, future research focused on diverse clinicians is needed to inform workforce wellness strategies.

Conclusions

ED clinicians are drawn to working in the field for a variety of reasons, and are passionate about the work that they do. Providing care in a manner that is consistent with clinicians’ professional desires and beliefs is perceived to be positively associated with professional well-being. Conversely, when clinicians are supporting individuals with EDs who have experienced barriers to care, there can be challenges in providing collaborative clinical care that meets the needs of an individual or family, and clinicians share experiences that are consistent with moral distress. There was a clear association identified by clinicians about the experiences of the system of care for individuals with EDs, and the professional well-being and workplace experiences of clinicians. Strategies to reduce barriers in the system of care for EDs may have positive ripple effects on workforce well-being and retention. Future work to assess the connection between treatment outcomes for individuals with EDs and the clinicians who provide care can help inform workforce strategies to support professional well-being for those working in ED settings.
