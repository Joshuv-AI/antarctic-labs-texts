Low-Rank Plus Sparse Matrix Transfer Learning under Growing Representations and Ambient Dimensions
Jinhang Chai Xuyuan Liu Elynn Chen Yujun Yan

Abstract
Learning systems often expand their ambient features or latent representations over time, embedding earlier representations into larger spaces with limited new latent structure. We study transfer learning for structured matrix estimation under simultaneous growth of the ambient dimension and the intrinsic representation, where a well-estimated source task is embedded as a subspace of a higher-dimensional target task. We propose a general transfer framework in which the target parameter decomposes into an embedded source component, low-dimensional low-rank innovations, and sparse edits, and develop an anchored alternating projection estimator that preserves transferred subspaces while estimating only low-dimensional innovations and sparse modifications. We establish deterministic error bounds that separate target noise, representation growth, and source estimation error, yielding strictly improved rates when rank and sparsity increments are small. We demonstrate the generality of the framework by applying it to two canonical problems. For Markov transition matrix estimation from a single trajectory, we derive end-to-end theoretical guarantees under dependent noise. For structured covariance estimation under enlarged dimensions, we provide complementary theoretical analysis in the appendix and empirically validate consistent transfer gains.



Keywords:
Transfer learning; Low-rank plus sparse matrix denoising; Growing ambient dimension; Growing representation; Markov Transition Matrix Estimation; Structured Covariance Estimation.

1
Introduction

Modern learning systems rarely operate with a fixed representation. In practice, feature or state spaces expand over time as new sensors, modalities, or modeling components are introduced, while earlier representations are preserved as identifiable subspaces of later ones. Such representation growth arises in representation learning, reinforcement learning, and large-scale AI systems, where historical data are collected under simpler representations and new features are appended incrementally. From a statistical perspective, this evolution is highly asymmetric: source representations are often learned from abundant data, whereas newly expanded representations initially have limited samples, making retraining from scratch statistically inefficient.

This paper studies
transfer learning for structured matrix estimation under growing ambient dimensions and representations
. We consider a setting in which a well-estimated source task lives in a lower-dimensional ambient space, the target task lives in a strictly larger space, and the source representation is embedded as a known subspace of the target. The target parameter is modeled as the embedded source parameter plus low-dimensional low-rank innovations and sparse edits, reflecting the fact that representation expansion typically introduces only a small number of genuinely new latent directions.

Most existing transfer and multitask learning theory assumes a fixed ambient parameter space shared across tasks. Classical multitask learning posits shared low-dimensional representations learned jointly across tasks
3
;
4
, while domain adaptation studies distribution shift under a fixed hypothesis class
5
. These frameworks do not apply when the target task introduces new coordinates that were absent in the source task. On the other hand, the structured matrix estimation literature provides sharp guarantees for single-task estimation under fixed dimensions
7
;
10
;
11
, but offers no mechanism for reusing learned structure when the ambient dimension itself grows.

The central challenge is therefore to understand how previously learned structure can be reused when
both the ambient dimension and the intrinsic representation evolve
, and to quantify when such transfer yields improved statistical guarantees. We propose a general framework for
anchored transfer under representation expansion
, in which estimated source subspaces are embedded into the enlarged target space and only innovation components orthogonal to them are estimated. We develop an anchored alternating projection estimator and establish deterministic error bounds that decompose target estimation error into intrinsic target noise, representation growth, and source estimation error, yielding strictly improved rates compared to single-task estimation when rank and sparsity increments are small.

Technically, the analysis requires careful control of approximation and estimation errors arising from the optimality inequality. To eliminate the rotational ambiguity inherent in low-rank factorizations, we align the approximation point with the ground truth and develop an orthogonal error decomposition that isolates the effect of representation growth and enables sharp bounds.

We demonstrate the generality of our framework through two canonical applications. For Markov transition matrix estimation from a single trajectory, we derive end-to-end theoretical guarantees under entrywise dependent noise, where classical i.i.d. concentration fails. For structured covariance estimation under enlarged ambient dimensions, we provide complementary theoretical analysis in the appendix and empirically demonstrate consistent transfer gains.

\bullet

2
Problem Setup and Transfer Model

2.1
General Transfer Setting (
M
tasks)

We consider a sequence of related learning tasks indexed by
m=1,\dots,M
, where task
M
is the target of interest and tasks
1,\dots,M-1
serve as source tasks.
For each task
m
, we observe a noisy matrix

Y^{(m)}=\Theta^{(m)}+W^{(m)},\qquad\Theta^{(m)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{m}\times q_{m}},

(1)

where
\Theta^{(m)}
denotes the unknown task-specific parameter matrix and
W^{(m)}
is an additive noise matrix. The ambient dimensions
(p_{m},q_{m})
are allowed to vary across tasks.

Our goal is to estimate the target parameter
\Theta^{(M)}
by leveraging information from previously observed tasks
\{Y^{(m)}\}_{m<M}
, in addition to the target observation
Y^{(M)}
. We focus on transfer settings in which the tasks are related through their underlying structure, so that knowledge learned from earlier tasks can reduce the sample or estimation complexity of the target task.

Although the framework applies to general
M
, for clarity of exposition and theoretical analysis we specialize to the case
M=2
in the remainder of the paper. This two-task setting captures the essential challenges of transfer under evolving representations while keeping notation and proofs transparent; extensions to multiple sequential tasks follow naturally.

2.2
Ambient Feature Space Growth

A central feature of our setting is that the ambient feature space may expand across tasks. In particular, the dimension of the parameter matrix is allowed to increase from source to target, reflecting the introduction of new features, sensors, or modeling components. We focus on structured expansion scenarios in which existing features are preserved and embedded within a larger ambient space.

Formally, consider the two-task setting
M=2
. The source task parameter satisfies

\Theta^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times q_{1}},

while the target task parameter satisfies

\Theta^{(2)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times q_{2}},\qquad p_{2}\geq p_{1},\;q_{2}\geq q_{1}.

To compare objects across dimensions, we introduce an
embedding operator
:

B:\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times q_{1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times q_{2}},\quad B(A)=\begin{bmatrix}A&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},

which embeds a source matrix into the target ambient space by padding zeros outside a designated
p_{1}\times q_{1}
block.

This model captures ambient feature expansion in which new coordinates are appended rather than replacing existing ones. In these settings, retraining a high-dimensional model from scratch can be statistically inefficient when target-task data are limited, motivating transfer methods that explicitly reuse structure learned in the lower-dimensional ambient space of source tasks with more abundant data.

2.3
Representation Growth: Low-Rank and Sparse Transfer Structure

Within the expanded ambient feature space, we model how the
intrinsic representation
evolves across tasks. We assume that each task parameter admits a structured decomposition

\Theta^{(m)}=L^{(m)}+S^{(m)},

where
L^{(m)}
captures low-dimensional latent structure and
S^{(m)}
captures sparse, task-specific deviations.

Low-rank representation growth (anchored subspace expansion).

Let the source low-rank component admit the population factorization

L^{(1)}=U^{(1)}\Sigma^{(1)}(V^{(1)})^{\top},\;U^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times r_{1}},\;V^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{1}\times r_{1}}.

After embedding into the target ambient space, the source row and column subspaces become

B(U^{(1)})\subset\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times r_{1}},\qquad B(V^{(1)})\subset\mathbb{R}^{q_{2}\times r_{1}}.

We model the target low-rank component by
expanding these embedded subspaces with a small number of new directions
. Specifically, we assume that the column and row spaces of
L^{(2)}
can be written as

U^{(2)}=[\,B(U^{(1)})\;\;U_{\Delta}\,],\qquad V^{(2)}=[\,B(V^{(1)})\;\;V_{\Delta}\,],

where
U_{\Delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times\delta_{r,2}}
and
V_{\Delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{2}\times\delta_{r,2}}
have orthonormal columns and satisfy

U_{\Delta}^{\top}B(U^{(1)})=0,\qquad V_{\Delta}^{\top}B(V^{(1)})=0.

The target low-rank component is then parameterized as

L^{(2)}=U^{(2)}A^{(2)}(V^{(2)})^{\top},

for some coefficient matrix
A^{(2)}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{2}\times r_{2}}
, where
r_{2}=r_{1}+\delta_{r,2}
,
0<\delta_{r,2}<r_{1}
and
\delta_{r,2}\ll\delta_{p,2}
.

Under this construction, the embedded source subspaces are
explicitly preserved
, and any additional latent structure appears only through the orthogonal innovation subspaces spanned by
U_{\Delta}
and
V_{\Delta}
. We therefore define
representation growth
as this low-dimensional expansion of the row and column subspaces of the low-rank component relative to the embedded source representation, rather than merely an increase in matrix rank. The factor increment
\delta_{r,2}
is assumed to be small compared to the ambient feature expansion.

Remark 2.1

(Identifiability)
.

Given the embedding
B(\cdot)
, decomposing the target low-rank component into an embedded source subspace and an orthogonal innovation subspace is without loss of generality, up to standard low-rank indeterminacies. Without such an embedding, the source subspace would be unidentifiable in the enlarged ambient space.

Sparse structure transfer.

In addition to low-rank structure, we allow the sparse component to evolve via localized changes:

S^{(2)}=B\!\left(S^{(1)}\right)+\Delta^{(2)}_{S},\qquad\|\Delta^{(2)}_{S}\|_{0}\leq\delta_{r,2},

where
\delta_{r,2}\ll\|S^{(1)}\|_{0}
. This models sparse edits that affect only a small number of entries in the expanded ambient space.

Together, these assumptions describe a transfer regime in which the target task inherits most of its structure from the source task, with only low-dimensional representation innovations and sparse modifications introduced by the expanded feature set. This structure underlies both the anchored estimator in Section 3 and the improved statistical rates established in Section 4.

3
Estimation Method: Anchored Low-Rank Plus Sparse Transfer

3.1
Transfer Estimation Problem

We assume that the source task is estimated from abundant source data, yielding estimates
\widehat{L}^{(1)}
and
\widehat{S}^{(1)}
of the low-rank and sparse components. Let

\widehat{L}^{(1)}=\widehat{U}^{(1)}\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)}(\widehat{V}^{(1)})^{\top}

denote a rank-
r_{1}
factorization of the source low-rank estimate, and define the embedded source subspaces in the target ambient space as

\widetilde{U}^{(1)}:=B(\widehat{U}^{(1)})\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times r_{1}},\;\widetilde{V}^{(1)}:=B(\widehat{V}^{(1)})\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{2}\times r_{1}},

together with the embedded sparse anchor
S_{0}:=B(\widehat{S}^{(1)})
.

Given the target observation
Y^{(2)}
, our goal is to estimate
\Theta^{(2)}=L^{(2)}+S^{(2)}
under the transfer structure described in Section
2.3
. In particular, we seek a decomposition in which: (i) the column and row spaces of
L^{(2)}
contain the embedded source subspaces
\widetilde{U}^{(1)}
and
\widetilde{V}^{(1)}
, with at most
\delta_{r,2}
additional innovation directions; and
(ii) the sparse component
S^{(2)}
differs from the transferred anchor
S_{0}
in at most
\delta_{s,2}
entries.

Formally, given inputs
Y^{(2)}
, target hyperparameters
\delta_{r,2}
and
\delta_{s,2}
, and (optionally) incoherence parameter
\mu
, we aim to solve

\displaystyle\min_{U_{\Delta},V_{\Delta},A^{(2)},S}\ \frac{1}{2}\left\|Y^{(2)}-\Big(U^{(2)}A^{(2)}(V^{(2)})^{\top}+S\Big)\right\|_{F}^{2}

(2)

\displaystyle\quad\text{s.t.}\ \ U^{(2)}=[\widetilde{U}^{(1)}\ U_{\Delta}],\quad V^{(2)}=[\widetilde{V}^{(1)}\ V_{\Delta}],

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\|S-\hat{S}^{(1)}\|_{0}\leq\delta_{s,2},\quad U^{(2)}A^{(2)}(V^{(2)})^{\top}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mu}

This formulation makes explicit that only the
innovation subspaces

(U_{\Delta},V_{\Delta})
, the associated coefficients, and the
sparse edits
relative to
S_{0}
are estimated from the target data. The embedded source structure is treated as fixed, serving as an anchor that reduces the effective complexity of the target estimation problem.

3.2
Anchored Low-Rank and Sparse Projections

We solve (
2
) by alternating between two structured projections: an anchored low-rank projection, which preserves the embedded source subspaces while estimating low-dimensional innovations, and a sparse-edit projection, which enforces proximity to the transferred sparse anchor.

Anchored low-rank projection.
Given a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times q_{2}}
, we define the anchored low-rank projection

\mathsf{P}_{\text{anch-rank}}(M;\widetilde{U}^{(1)},\widetilde{V}^{(1)},\delta_{r,2},r_{1})

as the solution to
\min_{L}\ \|M-L\|_{F}^{2}
subject to the constraints that the column space of
L
contains
\mathrm{span}(\widetilde{U}^{(1)})
, the row space of
L
contains
\mathrm{span}(\widetilde{V}^{(1)})
, and
\mathrm{rank}(L)\leq r_{1}+\delta_{r,2}
.

Operationally, this projection preserves the components of
M
aligned with the embedded source subspaces and estimates only low-dimensional innovation directions orthogonal to them. Define the orthogonal projectors

P_{\widetilde{U}}:=\widetilde{U}^{(1)}(\widetilde{U}^{(1)})^{\top},\qquad P_{\widetilde{V}}:=\widetilde{V}^{(1)}(\widetilde{V}^{(1)})^{\top}.

(3)

The innovation component is obtained by projecting
M
onto the orthogonal complement,

(I-P_{\widetilde{U}})\,M\,(I-P_{\widetilde{V}}),

and extracting its leading
\delta_{r,2}
singular left and right directions, denoted by
U_{\Delta}
and
V_{\Delta}
.

The updated low-rank factors are then formed as

\widehat{U}^{(2)}=[\,\widetilde{U}^{(1)}\ \ U_{\Delta}\,]
and

\widehat{V}^{(2)}=[\,\widetilde{V}^{(1)}\ \ V_{\Delta}\,]
,
which explicitly enforce the subspace anchoring constraint. Given these factors, the coefficient matrix is updated in closed form as

\widehat{A}^{(2)}=(\widehat{U}^{(2)})^{\top}M\widehat{V}^{(2)}
and and the projection returns the low-rank estimate

\widehat{L}^{(2)}:=\widehat{U}^{(2)}\widehat{A}^{(2)}(\widehat{V}^{(2)})^{\top}
.

The anchored low-rank projection is summarized in Algorithm
2
in Appendix
A
.

Sparse-edit projection.

Given a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times q_{2}}
, we define the sparse-edit projection relative to the transferred anchor
S_{0}
as

\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{edit}}(M;S_{0},\delta_{s,2}):=S_{0}+\mathcal{H}_{\delta_{s,2}}(M-S_{0}),

where
\mathcal{H}_{k}(\cdot)
retains the
k
largest-magnitude entries and sets the rest to zero. This operator projects
M
onto the set of matrices that differ from
S_{0}
in at most
\delta_{s,2}
entries, corresponding to sparse edits relative to the source task.
The sparse-edit projection is summarized in Algorithm
3
in Appendix
A
.

Together, these two projections isolate the
low-rank subspace innovations
and
sparse modifications
introduced by the expanded feature space, while anchoring the estimation to structure learned from the source task. They form the basic building blocks of the transfer alternating projection algorithm presented next.

3.3
Transfer Alternating Projection Algorithm

We now present an algorithmic procedure to solve (
2
) bsed on the anchored low-rank and sparse-edit projections. The method follows an alternating projection scheme that iteratively refines the low-rank and sparse components while preserving the transferred structure from the source task.

Starting from the transferred sparse anchor
S_{0}=B(\widehat{S}^{(1)})
, the algorithm alternates between the following two steps:

Sparse-edit update.
Given the current low-rank estimate
L_{t}
, update the sparse component by projecting the residual onto the sparse-edit constraint set:

S_{t+1}=\mathsf{P}_{\mathrm{edit}}\bigl(Y^{(2)}-L_{t};\,S_{0},\delta_{s,2}\bigr).

This step estimates only sparse deviations relative to the transferred anchor
S_{0}
.

Anchored low-rank update.

Given the updated sparse estimate
S_{t+1}
, form the residual

M_{t+1}=Y^{(2)}-S_{t+1},

and apply the anchored low-rank projection to update

L_{t+1}=\mathsf{P}_{\text{anch-rank}}(M_{t+1};\widetilde{U}^{(1)},\widetilde{V}^{(1)},\delta_{r,2},r_{1}).

These two steps are repeated until convergence. The complete procedure is summarized in Algorithm

3.4
Computational Complexity and Practical Considerations

Each iteration of the transfer alternating projection algorithm consists of two computationally efficient operations: a sparse-edit projection and an anchored low-rank projection. The sparse-edit update requires identifying the
\delta_{s,2}
largest-magnitude entries of a matrix, which can be implemented in
O(p_{2}q_{2})
time via partial sorting or more efficiently using selection algorithms when
\delta_{s,2}
is small.

The anchored low-rank update requires computing a truncated singular value decomposition of rank
\delta_{r,2}
for the doubly projected matrix
(I-P_{\widetilde{U}})M(I-P_{\widetilde{V}})
. This step can be carried out in
O((p_{2}+q_{2})\delta_{r,2}^{2})
time using standard randomized or Lanczos-based SVD methods, and dominates the per-iteration cost when
\delta_{r,2}
is small relative to the ambient dimensions.

Importantly, the computational complexity depends on the
incremental representation growth

\delta_{r,2}
and the
sparse edit budget

\delta_{s,2}
, rather than on the full target rank
r_{2}
or sparsity
s_{2}
. This contrasts with non-transfer approaches that must estimate the entire low-rank and sparse structure from scratch in the enlarged ambient space.

In practice, the algorithm is initialized with
L_{0}=0
and
S_{0}=B(\widehat{S}^{(1)})
, and typically converges in a small number of iterations. A simple relative change criterion on successive iterates is sufficient for stopping, and the method is robust to moderate inaccuracies in the source estimates as quantified by the error decomposition in Section 4.

Each iteration requires one truncated SVD of rank
\delta_{r,2}
and one hard-thresholding operation of sparsity
\delta_{s,2}
; both are efficient when
\delta_{r,2}\ll\min\{p_{2},q_{2}\}
and
\delta_{s,2}\ll p_{2}q_{2}
.

4
Theoretical Guarantees (Non-Distributional)

This section establishes deterministic and distributional error bounds for the transfer estimator. We show that anchoring previously learned subspaces yields a sharp decomposition of the estimation error into intrinsic target noise, representation growth, and source estimation terms.

Assumptions and Notation.

We first collect the technical assumptions and notation used throughout the theoretical analysis.
We focus on the denoising setting (
1
) where
W^{(m)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{m}\times q_{m}}
is an arbitrary noise matrix.
All bounds below are deterministic and hold for any realization of
W^{(m)}
, with error measured in the operator norm
\|\cdot\|
and entry-wise max norm
\|\cdot\|_{\max}
.

Assumption 4.1

(Structural Regularity)
.

1.
(Low-rank incoherence.)
Let
L^{(2)}=U^{(2)}\Sigma^{(2)}V^{(2)\top}
be the SVD decomposition.
Assume
U^{(2)}
and
V^{(2)}
satisfy
\mu
-incoherence:

\|U^{(2)}\|_{2,\infty}\leq\mu\sqrt{r_{2}/p_{2}}
and

\|V^{(2)}\|_{2,\infty}\leq\mu\sqrt{r_{2}/q_{2}}
.
We denote this class by
L^{(2)}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
.

2.
(Sparse change.)

Assume

\|S^{(2)}-S^{(1)}\|_{0}=\delta_{s,2}\leq\dfrac{c\max\{p_{2},q_{2}\}}{\mu r_{2}^{3}}

for a small constant
c
.

Norms and notation.
For a matrix
A
, we use the following norms throughout:
\|A\|_{F}
: Frobenius norm;
\|A\|_{2}
: operator (spectral) norm;
\|A\|_{\max}:=\max_{i,j}|A_{ij}|
: entrywise max norm.
We use
a\lesssim b
to denote
a\leq Cb
for a universal constant
C>0
. All projection matrices
P_{\widetilde{U}},P_{\widetilde{V}}
refer to orthogonal projectors defined in (
3
) using the embedded estimated source subspaces
\widetilde{U}^{(1)},\widetilde{V}^{(1)}
.

Main Deterministic Error Bound.

We now state the main deterministic error bound for the transfer estimator obtained by Algorithm
1
. The result holds for arbitrary noise matrices and explicitly decomposes the estimation error into intrinsic target noise, representation growth, and source estimation effects.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(\widehat{L}^{(2)},\widehat{S}^{(2)})
be the output of Algorithm 1 applied to
Y^{(2)}
. Under Assumption
4.1
, the following bound holds deterministically:

\displaystyle\|\widehat{L}^{(2)}-L^{(2)}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\widehat{S}^{(2)}-S^{(2)}\|_{F}^{2}

(4)

\displaystyle\lesssim\underbrace{\left(r_{1}\|\widetilde{U}^{(1)\top}W^{(2)}\widetilde{V}^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}+\delta_{r,2}\|W^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}+\delta_{s,2}\|W^{(2)}\|_{\max}^{2}\right)}_{\text{target estimation error}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{(\|U^{(1)}\bar{P}_{U}-\widetilde{U}^{(1)}\|_{F}^{2}+\|V^{(1)}\bar{P}_{V}-\widetilde{V}^{(1)}\|_{F}^{2})\cdot\|A^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}}_{\text{subspace anchor error}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\|\widehat{S}^{(1)}-S^{(1)}\|_{F}^{2}}_{\text{sparse anchor error}},

where

\|W^{(2)}\|
is the operator (spectral) norm of the noise matrix, and
\|W^{(2)}\|_{\max}=\max_{i,j}|W^{(2)}_{ij}|
is the entrywise max norm.

The bound separates the total estimation error into three conceptually distinct components:

(1)
Intrinsic target estimation error.

This term depends only on the target noise
W^{(2)}
and the
incremental complexities

(\delta_{r,2},\delta_{s,2})
, together with the fixed anchored subspace dimension
r_{1}
. Importantly, the ambient dimension
(p_{2},q_{2})
enters only through the noise norms.

(2)
Subspace anchor error.

This term captures the effect of imperfect estimation of the source subspaces. The error scales with the subspace misalignment between
(U^{(1)},V^{(1)})
and their estimates, amplified by the operator norm of the target coefficient matrix
A^{(2)}
.

(3)
Sparse anchor error.

This term reflects errors in the transferred sparse structure and vanishes when the source sparse component is estimated exactly.

Crucially, the leading term depends on the
representation growth

\delta_{r,2}
and
sparse edit budget

\delta_{s,2}
, rather than the full target rank
r_{2}
or sparsity level
s_{2}
. This structural separation underlies the statistical advantage of transfer and enables strictly improved rates compared to non-transfer estimation, as shown next.

Remark 4.3

(Source estimation)
.

Algorithm
1
relies on estimated source quantities
(\widehat{L}^{(1)},\widehat{S}^{(1)})
, which can be obtained using standard structured matrix estimators, such as convex relaxation
(
12
)
or incoherence-constrained least squares
(
9
)
. When the source sparse component is absent (
S^{(1)}=0
), a simple truncated SVD suffices.

Errors in source estimation enter the bound in Theorem 4.2 through two channels: subspace misalignment of the embedded anchors and bias in the transferred sparse component. Importantly, when the source task is supported by substantially more data than the target task, these terms are of lower order than the intrinsic target estimation error and therefore do not affect the leading rate.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let the
r_{1}
-th eigenvalue of
L^{(1)}
be
\sigma_{r_{1}}
. When using the same incoherence-constrained least square estimator for the source task, the estimation bound boils down to

\displaystyle\|\widehat{L}^{(2)}-L^{(2)}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\widehat{S}^{(2)}-S^{(2)}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(r_{1}\|\widetilde{U}^{(1)\top}W^{(2)}\widetilde{V}^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}+\delta_{r,2}\|W^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}+\delta_{s,2}\|W^{(2)}\|_{\max}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(r_{1}\|W^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}+s_{1}\|W^{(1)}\|_{\max}^{2}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{\|A^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}}{\sigma_{r_{1}}^{2}}+1\right).

Transfer vs. Non-Transfer Estimation.

We now compare the performance of the proposed transfer estimator with a baseline approach that estimates the target task directly without leveraging source-task information.

Consider the standard low-rank plus sparse estimator applied directly to the target observation
Y^{(2)}
, without anchoring to the source task. Under Assumption
4.1
, existing near-optimal results for structured matrix estimation
(
9
)
imply that the non-transfer estimator
(\widehat{L}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{base}},\widehat{S}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{base}})
satisfies

\|\widehat{L}^{(2)}_{\text{base}}-L^{(2)}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\widehat{S}^{(2)}_{\text{base}}-S^{(2)}\|_{F}^{2}\;\lesssim\;r_{2}\|W^{(2)}\|^{2}+s_{2}\|W^{(2)}\|_{\max}^{2}.

where
r_{2}=r_{1}+\delta_{r,2}
and
s_{2}=\|S^{(2)}\|_{0}=s_{1}+\delta_{s,2}
.

Compare this single-task rate with the one in Corollary
4.4
, since

\bigl\|\widetilde{U}^{(1)\top}W^{(2)}\widetilde{V}^{(1)}\bigr\|_{2}\;\leq\;\|W^{(2)}\|_{2}

and
r_{2}=\delta_{r,2}+r_{1}\gg\delta_{r,2}
and
s_{2}=\delta_{s,2}+s_{1}\gg\delta_{s,2}
, our transferred estimator yields strictly smaller error.

5
Application to Markov Transition Matrix Estimation

We apply our subspace-anchored transfer framework to Markov transition matrix estimation from a single trajectory with expanding state spaces, casting the problem as denoising an empirical frequency matrix with dependent noise.
The key technical challenge is that the “noise” induced by a single Markov path is
entrywise dependent
, so classical i.i.d. concentration does not apply.

Let the source chain evolve on state space
[p_{1}]
and the target chain on
[p_{2}]
, where
p_{2}=p_{1}+\delta_{p,2}
. For each task
m\in\{1,2\}
, let
P^{(m)}
denote the transition matrix,
\pi^{(m)}
the stationary distribution, and

F^{(m)}:=\operatorname{Diag}(\pi^{(m)})P^{(m)},

the corresponding frequency matrix. We observe a single stationary trajectory of length
n_{m}
from each chain and form the empirical frequency matrices

\widehat{F}^{(m)}:=\frac{1}{n_{m}}\sum_{t=0}^{n_{m}-1}e_{X^{(m)}_{t}}e_{X^{(m)}_{t+1}}^{\top}.

By construction, the empirical frequencies satisfy

\widehat{F}^{(m)}=F^{(m)}+W^{(m)},

where
W^{(m)}
denotes the estimation noise induced by finite sampling. Since all entries of
\widehat{F}^{(m)}
are computed from a single Markov trajectory, the noise matrix
W^{(m)}
exhibits strong entrywise dependence, and classical i.i.d. concentration arguments do not apply. Nevertheless, this representation places Markov transition estimation exactly in the denoising setting of Section
2
, allowing deterministic guarantees to be applied once suitable bounds on
\|W^{(m)}\|
and
\|W^{(m)}\|_{\max}
are available.

We assume that the frequency matrices admit low-rank plus sparse decompositions
F^{(m)}=L^{(m)}+S^{(m)}
, and that
(F^{(1)},F^{(2)})
satisfy the transfer structure introduced in Section
2
. In particular, the target frequency matrix inherits the embedded source structure via the known embedding operator
B
, with low-rank subspace growth of at most
\delta_{r,2}
new directions and sparse edits affecting at most
\delta_{s,2}
entries.

This characterizes the scenario in which state-space expansion introduces only a small number of new latent directions and localized deviations, while preserving the dominant structure learned from the source chain.

Estimator: transfer denoising of
\widehat{F}
and plug-in
\widehat{P}
.

Given the empirical frequency matrices
\widehat{F}^{(m)}
for
m=1,2
, we estimate the target frequency matrix
F^{(2)}
by applying the transfer denoising procedure developed in Section
3
. Specifically, we treat
\widehat{F}^{(2)}
as the observation matrix in the denoising model and invoke Algorithm
1
, using the anchored subspaces and sparse anchor learned from the source task. The output of Algorithm
1
yields a denoised estimate

\widehat{F}_{\mathrm{tran}}^{(2)}\;=\;\widehat{L}^{(2)}+\widehat{S}^{(2)}.

To recover the transition matrix, we employ a standard plug-in estimator based on the denoised frequency matrix. We first estimate the stationary distribution by
\widehat{\pi}^{(2)}=\widehat{F}_{\mathrm{tran}}^{(2)}\mathbf{1}
and then define

\widehat{P}_{\mathrm{tran}}^{(2)}\;=\;\operatorname{Diag}(\widehat{\pi}^{(2)})^{-1}\widehat{F}_{\mathrm{tran}}^{(2)}.

(5)

When necessary, we project
\widehat{P}^{(2)}
onto the probability simplex row-wise to ensure stochasticity. The resulting estimator leverages structural information transferred from the source chain while adapting to the expanded state space using only target-task data.

Theoretical guarantee.

By combining Theorem
4.2
with recent concentration results for Markov frequency matrices from a single trajectory, we obtain an end-to-end statistical guarantee for the transfer estimator.

Corollary 5.1

(Transfer Markov transition estimation)
.

Let
\tau_{\star}
denote the
(1/4)
-mixing time of the target chain and define
\pi^{(2)}_{\min}=\min_{i}\pi^{(2)}_{i}
and
\pi^{(2)}_{\max}=\max_{i}\pi^{(2)}_{i}
. Under Assumption
C.1
in Appendix
C
, there exist constants
c,c_{0}>0
such that, with probability at least
1-n_{2}^{-c_{0}}
, the output
(\widehat{L}^{(2)},\widehat{S}^{(2)})
of Algorithm
1
satisfies

\displaystyle\|\widehat{F}^{(2)}_{\emph{tran}}-F^{(2)}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\pi_{\max}^{(2)}\tau_{\star}\log^{2}n_{2}}{n_{2}}\Big(r_{1}^{2}+\delta_{r,2}p_{2}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~+\delta_{s,2}\log p_{2}\Big)+\mathcal{E}_{\emph{src}},

where
E_{\mathrm{src}}
collects the source-anchor error terms from Theorem
4.2
.

Moreover, if the estimated stationary distribution
\widehat{\pi}^{(2)}=\widehat{F}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{tran}}\mathbf{1}
satisfies
\min_{i}\widehat{\pi}^{(2)}_{i}\geq\pi^{(2)}_{\min}/2
, then the plug-in transition estimator (
5
) obeys

\displaystyle\|\widehat{P}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{tran}}-P^{(2)}_{\mathrm{tran}}\|_{F}^{2}\;\lesssim\;(\pi^{(2)}_{\min})^{-2}\,\|\widehat{F}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{tran}}-F^{(2)}\|_{F}^{2},

\displaystyle\|\widehat{P}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{tran}}-P^{(2)}_{\mathrm{tran}}\|_{1}\;\lesssim\;(\pi^{(2)}_{\min})^{-1}\,\|\widehat{F}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{tran}}-F^{(2)}\|_{1}.

Remark 5.2

(Comparison to non-transfer and regime interpretation)
.

Corollary
5.1
shows that transfer replaces the ambient dependence on
(r_{2},s_{2})
by the incremental
complexities
(\delta_{r,2},\delta_{s,2})
, together with an anchored subspace term
r_{1}^{2}
arising from the inherited
“old-old” block. When
p_{2}\gg r_{1}
and the innovation is small, this term is typically dominated.
In contrast, directly estimating
F^{(2)}
(and hence
P^{(2)}
) without transfer at the same sample size
n_{2}

incurs a larger leading term,

\frac{\pi_{\max}^{(2)}\tau_{\star}\log^{2}n_{2}}{n_{2}}\big(r_{2}\,p_{2}+s_{2}\log p_{2}\big)\quad\text{(up to constants),}

which is strictly worse whenever
r_{2}\gg\delta_{r,2}
and
s_{2}\gg\delta_{s,2}
.

Remark 5.3

(Connection to feature-based reinforcement learning)
.

While our analysis is presented for discrete-state Markov chains, many reinforcement learning and control problems
parameterize
P
through feature maps and study the resulting conditional mean operators.
The same frequency-matrix-first viewpoint, combined with deterministic structured denoising and mixing-time concentration

9
, extends to such settings once the operator can be represented as a structured matrix
(e.g., after discretization or finite-dimensional embedding).
In particular,
8
studies transfer under shared feature spaces

\phi(\cdot)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
\psi(\cdot)\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
; our theory further allows the target representation to expand.

6
Empirical Application to Structured Covariance Estimation

We empirically validate the proposed transfer estimator on a two-task covariance estimation problem with enlarged ambient dimension, serving as a canonical instance of structured matrix estimation under representation expansion. For each task
m\in\{1,2\}
, we observe i.i.d. samples

\widehat{\Sigma}^{(m)}

theoretical analysis
for this setting is given in Appendix
D
.

In the source task, the ambient dimension is
p_{1}=10
with rank
r_{1}=3
, and the sample size is
n_{1}=500
, yielding an accurate estimate of the source structure. In the target task, the ambient dimension increases to
p_{2}=50
with rank
r_{2}=4
, corresponding to a small rank increment
\delta_{r,2}=1
. The target sample size varies over

n_{2}\in\{30,50,80,100,120,150,200,250,300\},

capturing regimes in which single-task estimation is statistically challenging. The target low-rank component is generated by embedding
L^{(1)}
into
\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times p_{2}}
and adding a rank-one innovation, consistent with the transfer model in Section
2
. The sparse component is generated analogously via embedded source sparsity plus a small number of diagonal edits.

We compare three methods:
(i)
Transfer AltProj
, Algorithm
1
applied to
\widehat{\Sigma}^{(2)}
using anchors learned from
\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)}
;
(ii) a
non-transfer structured estimator
applied directly to
\widehat{\Sigma}^{(2)}
; and
(iii)
single-task PCA
based on truncated SVD.
Additional experimental details are presented in Section
E
in the Appendix.

Performance is measured by Frobenius error of the estimated low-rank component and subspace estimation error via principal angles
\sin(\Theta)
-distance between
\widehat{U}^{(2)}
and
U^{(2)}
, averaged over
50
trials.

7
Conclusion

We proposed a transfer framework for structured matrix estimation under growing ambient dimensions and representations, together with an anchored estimator and deterministic error guarantees. Applications to Markov transition estimation and empirical results on structured covariance estimation demonstrate consistent transfer benefits under representation expansion.

Proof.

We sometimes omit the superscript
(2)

,
\widetilde{U}=(\widetilde{U}^{(1)},U_{\Delta})
and accordingly for
V
.
Likewise, we let
R_{\Delta}
and
\hat{R}_{\Delta}
such that
S=S^{(2)}=S^{(1)}+R_{\Delta}
,
\hat{S}=\hat{S}^{(1)}+\hat{R}_{\Delta}
, and
\widetilde{S}=\hat{S}^{(1)}+R_{\Delta}
.

Simple case

We first give a high-level proof sketch supposing the source is estimated at full precision. Therefore, we have
U^{(1)}=\widetilde{U}^{(1)},V^{(1)}=\widetilde{V}^{(1)}
, and
\hat{U}=\widetilde{U},\hat{V}=\widetilde{V}
. By the optimality condition of our optimization problem, it holds that

\displaystyle\|Y^{(2)}-\hat{U}\hat{A}\hat{V}^{\top}-\hat{S}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\|Y^{(2)}-\widetilde{U}\widetilde{A}\widetilde{V}^{\top}-\widetilde{S}\|_{F}^{2}

Note that
Y^{(2)}=UAV^{\top}+S+W
.
Expanding the above inequality, and using the separation lemma in
9
which states that

\langle L_{\Delta},S_{\Delta}\rangle\leq\frac{\|L_{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}+\|S_{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}}{2},

we obtain the following chain of inequalities.

\displaystyle\|L_{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}+\|S_{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim|\langle W,L_{\Delta}\rangle+\langle W,S_{\Delta}\rangle|

\displaystyle=|\langle W,U^{(1)}A_{\Delta,11}V^{(1)\top}+(UA_{12})_{\Delta}V^{(1)\top}+U^{(1)}(A_{21}V^{\top})_{\Delta}+(UA_{22}V^{\top})_{\Delta}\rangle+\langle W,S_{\Delta}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\|U^{(1)\top}WV^{(1)}\|_{2}\cdot\|A_{\Delta,11}\|_{*}+\|W\|_{2}\cdot\|(UA_{12})_{\Delta}V^{(1)\top}-U^{(1)}(A_{21}V^{\top})_{\Delta}\|_{*}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~+\|W\|_{2}\cdot\|(UA_{22}V^{\top})_{\Delta}\|_{*}+\|W\|_{\max}\cdot\|S_{\Delta}\|_{1}

where we denote
L_{\Delta}=\hat{U}\hat{A}\hat{V}^{\top}-\widetilde{U}\widetilde{A}\widetilde{V}^{\top}
and
S_{\Delta}=\hat{S}-\widetilde{S}=\hat{R}_{\Delta}-R_{\Delta}
. Further,
(UA_{12})_{\Delta}:=\hat{U}_{\Delta}\hat{A}_{12}-U_{\Delta}A_{12}
and similarly for
A_{\Delta,11}
,
(A_{21}V^{\top})_{\Delta}
and
(UA_{22}V^{\top})_{\Delta}
.

An important observation is that
L_{\Delta}
has an orthogonal decomposition. To specify, we explicitly write

\displaystyle L_{\Delta}=\underbrace{U^{(1)}A_{\Delta,11}V^{(1)\top}}_{L_{\Delta,1}}+\underbrace{(UA_{12})_{\Delta}V^{(1)\top}+U^{(1)}(A_{21}V^{\top})_{\Delta}}_{L_{\Delta,2}}+\underbrace{(UA_{22}V^{\top})_{\Delta}}_{L_{\Delta,3}}

We next prove those three sub-terms are orthogonal with each other. To see this,

\displaystyle\langle L_{\Delta,1},(UA_{12})_{\Delta}V^{(1)\top}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle U^{(1)}A_{\Delta,11}V^{(1)\top},(UA_{12})_{\Delta}V^{(1)\top}\rangle

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}(V^{(1)}A_{\Delta,11}^{\top}U^{(1)\top}(\hat{U}_{\Delta}\hat{A}_{12}-U_{\Delta}A_{12})V^{(1)\top})=0

where we used
U^{(1)\top}U_{\Delta}=U^{(1)\top}\hat{U}_{\Delta}=0
. Similarly we have
\langle L_{\Delta,1},U^{(1)}(A_{21}V^{\top})_{\Delta}\rangle=0
which implies
\langle L_{\Delta,1},L_{\Delta,2}\rangle=0
. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\langle L_{\Delta,1},L_{\Delta,3}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle U^{(1)}A_{\Delta,11}V^{(1)\top},(UA_{22}V^{\top})_{\Delta}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle U^{(1)}A_{\Delta,11}V^{(1)\top},\hat{U}_{\Delta}\hat{A}_{22}\hat{V}_{\Delta}^{\top}\rangle-\langle U^{(1)}A_{\Delta,11}V^{(1)\top},U_{\Delta}A_{22}V_{\Delta}^{\top}\rangle

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}(U^{(1)}A_{\Delta,11}V^{(1)\top}\hat{V}_{\Delta}\hat{A}_{22}^{\top}\hat{U}_{\Delta}^{\top})-\operatorname{Tr}(U^{(1)}A_{\Delta,11}V^{(1)\top}V_{\Delta}A_{22}^{\top}U_{\Delta}^{\top}\rangle=0

where again we use
V^{(1)\top}V_{\Delta}=V^{(1)\top}\hat{V}_{\Delta}=0
. Similarly, we can prove
\langle L_{\Delta,2},L_{\Delta,3}\rangle=0
.

It follows that

\displaystyle\|L_{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}=\|L_{\Delta,1}\|_{F}^{2}+\|L_{\Delta,2}\|_{F}^{2}+\|L_{\Delta,3}\|_{F}^{2}

(6)

We continue to rearrange the basic inequality. Note that the rank of
(UA_{12})_{\Delta}V^{(1)\top}-U^{(1)}(A_{21}V^{\top})_{\Delta}
is at most
2\delta_{r,2}
, using the translation inequality for nuclear and spectral norm
\|M\|_{*}\leq\sqrt{\operatorname{rank}(M)}\cdot\|M\|_{2}
and the orthogonal decomposition
6

\displaystyle\|L_{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}+\|S_{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|U^{(1)\top}WV^{(1)}\|_{2}\cdot\sqrt{r_{1}}\|A_{\Delta,11}\|_{2}+\|W\|_{2}\cdot\sqrt{2\delta_{r,2}}\|(UA_{12})_{\Delta}V^{(1)\top}-U^{(1)}(A_{21}V^{\top})_{\Delta}\|_{2}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~+\|W\|_{2}\cdot\sqrt{\delta_{r,2}}\|(UA_{22}V^{\top})_{\Delta}\|_{2}+\|W\|_{\max}\cdot\sqrt{\delta_{s,2}}\|S_{\Delta}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{r_{1}}\left(\frac{\|U^{(1)\top}WV^{(1)}\|_{2}}{\|W\|_{2}}+\sqrt{\frac{\delta_{r,2}}{r_{1}}}\right)\|W\|_{2}\cdot\left(\|L_{\Delta,1}\|_{F}+\|L_{\Delta,2}\|_{F}+\|L_{\Delta,3}\|_{F}\right)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+\|W\|_{\max}\cdot\sqrt{\delta_{s,2}}\|S_{\Delta}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\|L_{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}+\|S_{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}\leq r_{1}\|U^{(1)\top}WV^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}+\delta_{r,2}\|W\|_{2}^{2}+\delta_{s,2}\|W\|_{\max}^{2}

\|U^{(1)\top}WV^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|W\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~+\|UAV^{\top}-\widetilde{U}\widetilde{A}\widetilde{V}^{\top}\|_{F}^{2}+\|S^{(1)}-\widetilde{S}^{(1)}\|_{F}^{2}.

\displaystyle\|L_{\delta}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|UP_{U}\widetilde{A}(VP_{V})^{\top}-\widetilde{U}\widetilde{A}\widetilde{V}^{\top}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|UP_{U}\widetilde{A}(VP_{V})^{\top}-UP_{U}\widetilde{A}\widetilde{V}^{\top}\|_{F}^{2}+\|UP_{U}\widetilde{A}\widetilde{V}^{\top}-\widetilde{U}\widetilde{A}\widetilde{V}^{\top}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\widetilde{A}(VP_{V})^{\top}-\widetilde{A}\widetilde{V}^{\top}\|_{F}^{2}+\|UP_{U}\widetilde{A}-\widetilde{U}\widetilde{A}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim(\|\widetilde{V}-VP_{V}\|_{F}^{2}+\|UP_{U}-\widetilde{U}\|_{F}^{2})\cdot\|\widetilde{A}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\|V^{(1)}\bar{P}_{V}-\widetilde{V}^{(1)}\|_{F}^{2}+\|U^{(1)}\bar{P}_{U}-\widetilde{U}^{(1)}\|_{F}^{2})\cdot\|A\|_{2}^{2}

(8)

where in the second equality we use the definition of
\widetilde{A}
. The last equality uses that
\|\widetilde{A}\|_{2}=\|A\|_{2}
and that the lower-right subpart of
P_{U}
is identity matrix.

Subsequently, we can decompose the final estimation error as

\displaystyle\|\hat{L}-L\|_{F}^{2}+\|\hat{S}-S\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\hat{L}-\widetilde{L}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\widetilde{L}-L\|_{F}^{2}+\|\hat{S}-\widetilde{S}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\widetilde{S}-S\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|L_{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}+\|S_{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\widetilde{U}\widetilde{A}\widetilde{V}^{\top}-UAV^{\top}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\widetilde{S}^{(1)}-S^{(1)}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim(\|V^{(1)}\bar{P}_{V}-\widetilde{V}^{(1)}\|_{F}^{2}+\|U^{(1)}\bar{P}_{U}-\widetilde{U}^{(1)}\|_{F}^{2})\|A\|_{2}^{2}+\|S^{(1)}-\widetilde{S}^{(1)}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~+r_{1}\|\widetilde{U}^{(1)\top}W\widetilde{V}^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}+\delta_{r,2}\|W\|_{2}^{2}+\delta_{s,2}\|W\|_{\max}^{2}.

(9)

Combining (
B
)–(
B
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\|\hat{L}-L\|_{F}^{2}+\|\hat{S}-S\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\|V^{(1)}\bar{P}_{V}-\widetilde{V}^{(1)}\|_{F}^{2}+\|U^{(1)}\bar{P}_{U}-\widetilde{U}^{(1)}\|_{F}^{2}\right)\|A\|_{2}^{2}+\|S^{(1)}-\widetilde{S}^{(1)}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~+\|L_{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}+\|S_{\Delta}\|_{F}^{2}.

(10)

By Weyl’s inequality (see Theorem 2.9 in
[
11
]
) we have that

\displaystyle\|U^{(1)}\bar{P}_{U}-\widetilde{U}^{(1)}\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\min_{P\in\mathcal{O}_{r_{1},r_{1}}}\|U^{(1)}P-\widetilde{U}^{(1)}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{2}\|\hat{L}^{(1)}-L^{(1)}\|_{F}}{\sigma_{r_{1}}-\|\hat{L}^{(1)}-L^{(1)}\|_{2}}.

The same bound holds for
\|V^{(1)}\bar{P}_{V}-\widetilde{V}^{(1)}\|_{F}^{2}
.
The source estimation guarantee provides

\displaystyle\|\hat{L}^{(1)}-L^{(1)}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\hat{S}^{(1)}-S^{(1)}\|_{F}^{2}\leq r_{1}\|W^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}+s_{1}\|W^{(1)}\|_{\max}^{2}.

(11)

Note that the source SNR is typically large so that
\|W^{(1)}\|
is small. As long as
r_{1}\|W^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}+s_{1}\|W^{(1)}\|_{\max}^{2}\leq\sigma_{r_{1}}^{2}/4
it holds that
\|\hat{L}^{(1)}-L^{(1)}\|_{2}\leq\|\hat{L}^{(1)}-L^{(1)}\|_{F}\leq\sigma_{r_{1}}/2
it follows that

\displaystyle\|V^{(1)}\bar{P}_{V}-\widetilde{V}^{(1)}\|_{F}^{2}+\|U^{(1)}\bar{P}_{U}-\widetilde{U}^{(1)}\|_{F}^{2}\lesssim\frac{r_{1}\|W^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}+s_{1}\|W^{(1)}\|_{\max}^{2}}{\sigma_{r_{1}}^{2}}

Plugging into (
B
) we obtain

\displaystyle\|\hat{L}-L\|_{F}^{2}+\|\hat{S}-S\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{r_{1}\|W^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}+s_{1}\|W^{(1)}\|_{\max}^{2}}{\sigma_{r_{1}}^{2}}\|A\|_{2}^{2}+\|S^{(1)}-\widetilde{S}^{(1)}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~+r_{1}\|\widetilde{U}^{(1)\top}W\widetilde{V}^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}+\delta_{r,2}\|W\|_{2}^{2}+\delta_{s,2}\|W\|_{\max}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(r_{1}\|W^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}+s_{1}\|W^{(1)}\|_{\max}^{2}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{\|A\|_{2}^{2}}{\sigma_{r_{1}}^{2}}+1\right)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+r_{1}\|\widetilde{U}^{(1)\top}W\widetilde{V}^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}+\delta_{r,2}\|W\|_{2}^{2}+\delta_{s,2}\|W\|_{\max}^{2}.

where we again use (
11
) in the last inequality to bound the source sparsity estimation error.

As
W
is independent with
\widetilde{U}^{(1)}
and
\widetilde{U}^{(1)}
, likewise as the simple case,
\|\widetilde{U}^{(1)\top}W\widetilde{V}^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|W\|_{2}^{2}
for every realization of noise

And when

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{U}^{(1)\top}W\widetilde{V}^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}\asymp\frac{r_{1}}{\max\{p_{2},q_{2}\}}\mathbb{E}\|W\|_{2}^{2}.

Appendix D
Application to Statistical PCA with Enlarged Dimension and Low-rankness

We illustrate how the deterministic error bound in Theorem
4.2
translates into sharp statistical guarantees for principal component analysis under enlarged dimension and low-rankness. This setting provides a canonical example where transfer is natural: a source task is estimated from abundant data, while the target task has limited samples and an expanded ambient dimension.

D.1
Model and Sample Covariance Estimation

For each task
m\in\{1,2\}
, let
X^{(m)}_{1},\dots,X^{(m)}_{n_{m}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{m}}
be i.i.d. random vectors with mean zero and covariance

\Sigma^{(m)}=L^{(m)}+S^{(m)},

where
L^{(m)}
is a low-rank signal component and
S^{(m)}
is a sparse diagonal or entrywise perturbation. We observe the sample covariance

\widehat{\Sigma}^{(m)}=\frac{1}{n_{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{m}}X^{(m)}_{i}X^{(m)\top}_{i}=\Sigma^{(m)}+W^{(m)},

where
W^{(m)}:=\widehat{\Sigma}^{(m)}-\Sigma^{(m)}
denotes the sampling noise.

We consider an asymmetric regime where the source task has many samples (
n_{1}\gg n_{2}
) and the target task has limited samples, while the ambient dimension increases from
p_{1}
to
p_{2}
, the lowrankness grows from
r_{1}
to
r_{2}
, and the sparsity grows from
s_{1}
to
s_{2}
.

D.2
Transfer Structure under Representation Expansion

We adopt the same embedding operator
B(\cdot)
as in Section 2 to pad a
p_{1}\times p_{1}
matrix into a
p_{2}\times p_{2}
matrix. The target covariance is assumed to satisfy the transfer structure characterized in Section
3.1
with
\delta_{r,2}\ll r_{1}
and
\delta_{s,2}\ll s_{1}
. This models the addition of new variables whose covariance structure introduces only a small number of new latent directions.

We apply Algorithm
1
to the target observation

Y^{(2)}:=\widehat{\Sigma}^{(2)},

using anchors
(\widehat{L}^{(1)},\widehat{S}^{(1)})
learned from
\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)}
.

D.3
Statistical Error Rates via Theorem
4.2

Assume that the coordinates of
X^{(m)}
are sub-Gaussian with bounded fourth moments. Standard concentration results
[
22
]
imply

\mathbb{E}\|W^{(m)}\|_{2}^{2}\;\lesssim\;\frac{p_{m}}{n_{m}},\qquad\mathbb{E}\|W^{(m)}\|_{\max}^{2}\;\lesssim\;\frac{\log p_{m}}{n_{m}}.

Moreover,

\mathbb{E}\bigl\|\widetilde{U}^{(1)\top}W^{(2)}\widetilde{V}^{(1)}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}\;\lesssim\;\frac{r_{1}}{n_{2}}.

Substituting these bounds into Theorem
4.2
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\widehat{L}^{(2)}-L^{(2)}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\widehat{S}^{(2)}-S^{(2)}\|_{F}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\;\lesssim\;\frac{r_{1}^{2}}{n_{2}}+\frac{\delta_{r,2}p_{2}}{n_{2}}+\frac{\delta_{s,2}\log p_{2}}{n_{2}}+\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{src}},

where the source-dependent term satisfies

\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{src}}\;\lesssim\;\left(\frac{r_{1}p_{1}}{n_{1}}+\frac{s_{1}\log p_{1}}{n_{1}}\right)\left(\frac{\|A^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}}{\sigma_{r_{1}}^{2}}+1\right).

When
n_{1}\gg n_{2}
, the source contribution is lower order, and the leading rate depends only on the incremental complexities
(\delta_{r,2},\delta_{s,2})
.

Comparison to Non-Transfer PCA.
In the present setting, the target rank satisfies

r_{2}=r_{1}+\delta_{r,2}
,
where
r_{1}
is the intrinsic rank inherited from the source task. Importantly,
r_{1}
is constrained by the source sample size
n_{1}
, not by the target sample size
n_{2}
. In regimes where
n_{1}\gg n_{2}
, it is therefore natural for
r_{1}
to be as large as
O(n_{2})
, even when the target task itself has only a small number of samples.

Under this scaling, the non-transfer estimator applied directly to
\widehat{\Sigma}^{(2)}
incurs the error

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\widehat{L}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{base}}-L^{(2)}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\widehat{S}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{base}}-S^{(2)}\|_{F}^{2}\right]\;\lesssim\;\frac{r_{2}p_{2}}{n_{2}}+\frac{s_{2}\log p_{2}}{n_{2}}.

When
r_{1}\asymp n_{2}
, this bound does not vanish as
n_{2}
grows slowly relative to
p_{2}
, and single-task estimation fails to be consistent. In contrast, the transfer estimator depends on the
incremental rank

\delta_{r,2}
rather than the full rank
r_{2}
, yielding a convergent error rate whenever
\delta_{r,2}\ll r_{1}
and
\delta_{s,2}\ll s_{1}
, even in regimes where single-task PCA is statistically infeasible.
