Prolonged length of hospital stay and its associated factors among patients admitted with heart failure: a cross-sectional study in Dar Es Salaam, Tanzania
Authors: Yona Amon, Masunga K Iseselo, Emmanuel Sumari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProlonged hospital stay (LOS) is a major problem among patients admitted with heart failure (HF). A heightened cost of patients’ care, accompanied by a prolonged LOS, is associated with overutilization of the health facility resources, resulting in an increased health care budget in low-resource settings. Information on the factors influencing the LOS among patients admitted with HF in Tanzania is scarce. This study aimed to identify factors contributing to prolonged LOS among patients with HF.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted at the Jakaya Kikwete Cardiac Institute in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania, from May to June 2024. It included 327 participants recruited using simple random sampling. Data were collected through a self-administered questionnaire and analysed using SPSS version 27. Descriptive analysis was performed for the demographic and clinical characteristics to determine mean, standard deviation, frequency and percentages. Bivariate analysis was done to determine the association between the factors and the length of hospital stay. Furthermore, multivariable logistic regression for variables with p ≤ 0.2 was performed to rule out the confounding factors. A p-value < 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.ResultsAmong the 327 participants, 196 (59.9%) were female. The mean age of participants was 48.6 years with a standard deviation (SD) of ± 17.4 years. The mean length of stay was 11 days, with 167 (51.1%) staying 10 days or more (i.e. 3rd quartile in the population sample). Participants from rural areas were 2 times more likely to have longer hospital stays compared to those from urban areas (AOR = 2.12, 95% CI: 1.317–3.424, p = 0.002). Participants with hypertensive heart disease (HHD) as a comorbidity with HF were 2.8 times more likely to have prolonged LOS compared to heart muscle disorders (HMD) (AOR = 2.805, 95% CI: 2.08–5.09, p = 0.042). In addition, participants with underweight were 2 times more likely to have a prolonged length of hospital stay compared to those with normal weight (AOR = 2.051, 95% CI: 1.006–3.439, p = 0.007). Furthermore, participants with treatment complications were 2.2 times more likely to have a prolonged LOS compared to those with no complications (AOR = 2.237, 95% CI: 1.05–4.79, p = 0.038).ConclusionThe findings show prolonged hospital stays for patients with HF and associated factors that need to be addressed. This underscores the need to adhere to the guidelines of the underlying conditions and comorbidities during treatments. Also, a close follow-up of nutrition status and developing strategies for those patients from rural areas may shorten the hospital stay.Keywords: Prolonged hospital stay, Heart failure, Admitted patients

Introduction

Globally, prolonged length of hospital stay(LOS) related to heart failure (HF) presents a great burden at the individual, health facilities and the nations with an estimated 64 million people living with heart failure worldwide [1]. The LOS for a patient with HF is the total number of days an admitted patient remains in the hospital during a single admission event [2]. The LOS is estimated to range between 5 and 15 days in developed countries [3] while in low and middle-income countries it ranges between 3 and 23 days [4]. Information on LOS for patients with HF in the Sub-Saharan region is limited, but evidence in Ethiopia reported up to 14 days [5–7]. The study in the Kilimanjaro region showed that the magnitude of individuals with HF was 26.2% of the time [8], which was more than ten times higher than that reported in the Morogoro region (1.9%) [9]. Regardless of this variation in the prevalence of HF in Tanzania, the reported studies highlight the magnitude of HF and other factors, rather than prolonged LOS and its associated factors.

Prolonged LOS is associated with several factors. The prolonged LOS among patients with heart failure has been reported among the majority of patients with old age [10, 11]. Also, the presence of comorbidities in patients with heart failure, such as hypertensive heart disease, diabetes, pneumonia, anaemia, and kidney disease, increases the risk of complications and delays in recovery [12]. Moreover, factors such as NYHA, being female gender, low left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), cerebrovascular disease, community-acquired infection, arrhythmias and chronic alcohol consumption have been associated with prolonged LOS among patients admitted with HF [13, 14]. In addition, a high average of prolonged LOS is related to a lack of appropriate medical audit mechanisms, late presentation with complications, low resource availability, and poor economic conditions in African countries [15].

Prolonged LOS due to HF imposes a great economic burden related to costs of treatment and stress to the patients, community, countries at large and the increased utilisation of healthcare resources, which is associated with an increased healthcare budget in low-resource health settings [16]. Hence, the prolonged LOS is considered an economic problem in the healthcare system for patients with HF. For example, HF hospitalisation is associated with high costs of treatment, estimated between 3000 and 5000 dollars in developed countries and 1000 dollars in LMICs [17]. In other regions, prolonged LOS costs more than $24,000 per patient in one year and $126,819 throughout a lifetime to spend on medical expenses during hospital stays [18, 19]. In 2014, Tanzania’s estimated cost of caring for patients with HF was $19 million [20]. Instead of participating in other socio-economic and political activities that contribute to their prosperity, patients and their families spend a significant portion of their time in hospitals seeking care and treatment [21]. In addition, prolonged hospital stays in resource-limited settings expose patients to nosocomial infections, increasing the risk of complications and mortality ranging from 9% to 12.5% [22]. This is considered to be greater than the hospital death rate for HF patients admitted in developed countries (3.8–6.4%) [18].

In Tanzania, there is limited evidence on the length of hospital stays among patients admitted with HF. The available evidences show the burden of and magnitude, reasons for admissions, and treatment outcomes of patients with HF [23–25]. None of these reports on the length of hospital stay and its associated consequences in the Tanzanian context. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the LOS and its associated factors among patients admitted with HF in Dar es Salaam. Identifying these factors would foster interventions to reduce time spent in the hospital and the cost of care.

Methods

Study design and setting

This is a hospital-based cross-sectional study which was conducted at the Jakaya Kikwete Cardiac Institute (JKCI) in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania, from early May to late June 2024. The JKCI is a public National referral cardiac and teaching hospital that provides specialized cardiovascular diagnostics and treatment services for both inpatients and outpatients for both adults and children. These services cover cardiology, cardiovascular care, cardiac imaging, and cardiothoracic surgeries. Also, it offers specialized services like pediatric cardiology and vascular/endovascular treatments. The institute had 158 bed capacity and attended over 2000 outpatients and 100 inpatients every week, while admitting 700–800 patients with HF monthly [26].

Study population

This study included patients with HF admitted to the JKCI medical and surgical wards. We included both male and female inpatients aged 18 years and above with a confirmed diagnosis of HF by a cardiologist upon admission. In addition, participants who had more than three features of HF, such as jugular venous distension (JVD), abdominal swelling (ascites), upper and lower limbs oedema, coughing, and difficulty in breathing while lying in a flat position (orthopnea), reduced or preserved ejection fraction (HFrEF or HFpEF) and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND), were included in the study. We excluded patients who were seriously sick at the time of data collection. Furthermore, patients with missing information regarding the number of days spent in the hospital and those with reported LOS < 1 day were excluded.

Sampling procedure and sample size

A simple random sampling method was used to recruit the participants. In this method, a sampling frame was created, compiling a list of all eligible individuals admitted. Using a computer system generator, each participant was assigned a random number ranging from 1 to 700. This method ensures that every patient has the same probability of being selected for the study. The first author, who was an expert in cardiovascular sciences, was responsible for the organization and recruitment of the participants at the study site. The sample size was calculated according to Kish and Leslie’s formula, n = Z2 (p (100-p)/e2), where n is the desired sample size, Z is the standard normal distribution set as 1.96, which represents a 95% confidence interval. Furthermore, p- was the percentage of the target population thought to possess the trait under study, p = 26.2% adopted from the study done in Tanzania that reported the proportion of HF to be 26.2% [8], and e- was the acceptable margin of error or precision, which was 5%. Using this formula, a sample size estimated was 327.

Data collection tools and procedures

Data were collected using a Swahili-translated questionnaire, which was developed after a thorough review of previous studies done on a similar topic [8, 14, 21, 27]. The tool was then translated using a forward–back approach from English to Swahili, the language that the participants can easily understand. The questionnaire had four sections with a total of 27 questions. Section I had 6 questions on demographic information, Section II had 7 questions on individual characteristics, Section III had 8 questions on clinical characteristics, and Section IV had 6 questions on institutional characteristics. The researcher met the participants in their respective wards, both in medical and surgical wards, after the morning routines of care, informed them individually about the purpose of the study and their right to participation, insisting that it was voluntary and refusal would not affect their opportunity to treatment.

Participants who were eligible to participate were given the questionnaire to fill out, and the researcher assisted those who could not write. The researcher collected the filled questionnaires immediately after filling and filled some of the information, like echocardiographic and laboratory findings, in-hospital treatment, radiological findings, and prescribed drugs obtained from the medical chart. Body mass index (BMI) of the patients was assessed upon admission and categorised according to the World Health Organisation’s intervals: Underweight, Normal weight, Overweight or Obese and recorded in the patient’s chart for review. Once completed, the researcher checked if the data were complete. The researcher collected between 5 and 10 questionnaires each day, 5 days a week, from both medical and surgical wards until the desired sample was achieved.

Outcome measure

The outcome measure of this study was the LOS among patients hospitalised with HF. This was categorised into two categories: participants with less than 10 days were considered with short stay, while those with more than 10 days were considered staying longer. The cut-off point for prolonged LOS was considered as above 10 days (i.e. 3rd quartile in the population sample) of stay in the normal ward for admitted patients according to the National Health Insurance Fund (NHIF) of Tanzania [28]. Ten days is an administrative cut-off point between NHIF and health facilities in Tanzania for patients admitted with different disease conditions, including patients with HF. Furthermore, during those days, the facility would request approval from NHIF for an extended duration of stay for their patients in order to ensure legitimacy in reimbursement for claims. The independent variables were individual characteristics, including weight, height, age, gender, marital status, education level, employment status, place of residency, health insurance, and challenges during treatment. In addition, clinical characteristics such as types of medication, medication side effects, the New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification at admission, signs and symptoms, types of comorbidity, and comorbidities of HF.

Validity and reliability

To ensure the validity of this study, the questionnaire was developed following a review of published articles on a similar topic. Also, the tool was shared with the experts, including the supervisors, to cross-check the translation and if the questions answered the objectives; all inputs were used to improve the questionnaire. Furthermore, the tool was pre-tested on twelve participants with HF found at the study site but not on participants who were included in the main study to check for easy response and clarity of the questions, where no major comments were given [29]. Analysis for the pretest data was done to check for reliability, which revealed a reliability coefficient of 0.85, considered to be acceptable, consistent and relatively free from errors.

Missing data and data quality

To guarantee the quality of the data, the principal investigator utilised the correct data collection tools while ensuring that the confidentiality of the gathered data was preserved. The principal investigator, who had experience in cardiovascular sciences and clinical practices, performed the data collection. This enabled an assessment of patients’ conditions and record evaluations to meet the desired goals. In cases of any missing data during and after the collection process, the investigator examined bedside patient charts and conducted comprehensive reviews of the uncompleted questionnaires before entering the data.

Data management and analysis

After the data entry process was completed, analysis was performed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27. Descriptive analysis was performed to obtain the mean and standard deviation for continuous data, while frequencies and percentages were obtained for other demographic and clinical characteristics. Bivariate logistic regression was performed to determine the association between the participants’ demographic, clinical characteristics and the length of hospital stay; a p-value of < 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance. Further analysis: multivariate logistic regression analysis was done for the variables with a p-value ≤ 0.20 from bivariable logistic regression to control potential confounders. In addition, handling mediators was done in the designing phase of the study and during the analysis stage of the study variables. Furthermore, multicollinearity was assessed by considering independent variables that were strongly correlated and inflated each other’s variances. The variables deemed collinear were eliminated because the objective was to understand relationships.

Results

Socio-demographic and individual-level characteristics of the study participants

A total of 327 participants responded to the questionnaire, of which 196 (59.9%) were females. The mean age of participants was 48.6 ± 17.404 years. 271 (82.9%) of participants had an age between 15 and 60 years, and 208 (63.6%) were married. Furthermore, 310 (94.8%) participants had formal education171 (52.3%) were self-employed, and 171 (52.3%) were from rural areas. The mean body weight was 69.9 ± 16.172 kg, the mean height was 165 ± 165.4 cm, and 153 (46.8%) participants had a BMI above 24.9. The majority, 189 (57.8%) of the study participants had health insurance. The mean LOS was 11 ± 10.06 days, and 167 (51.1%)participants had prolonged LOS. Furthermore, the majority of participants, 199 (60.9%), had no challenges during their treatment. (Table 1).

Clinical and health facility factors of the participants

Of all participants, 117 (35.8%) experienced chest pain as a sign and symptom on admission. About two-thirds of participants, 145 (64.7%), had hypertension (HTN) as a comorbidity, while 117 (37%) had diuretics as part of treatment. In terms of comorbidity of HF, 101 (32.6%) had HHD, while in terms of types of HF, 197 (60.2%) had preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). Regarding the NYHA, more than half of the participants, 167 (51.1%), had class IV. Most of the participants, 290 (88.7%), had no medication side effects, while 315 (96.3%) denied experiencing treatment complications caused by caregivers (iatrogenic complications) (Table 2).

Participants from rural areas were less likely to have longer hospital stays compared to those from urban areas (COR, = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.34–0.83.34.83, p = 0.02). Discussion

This study determined the LOS and its associated factors among patients admitted with HF. It is revealed that more than half (51.1%) of the participants had a longer hospital stay of 11 days. Living in rural areas, payment category, HHD as a comorbidity condition for HF, were associated with the prolonged LOS. Also, treatment complications and underweight were associated with a prolonged LOS in the current study. This study was similar in terms of prolonged LOS to another study done in Ethiopia that found 12 days [14]. This similarity might be attributed to both Tanzania and Ethiopia populations found in Sub-Saharan Africa that may share the same commonalities, such as lifestyles and health-seeking behaviour in the late stage, as participants are admitted while seriously sick, hence increasing days of hospital stay [30]. Our findings are different from studies reported in Japan, with the mean length of stay being 19.5 (±12.5days) [31] and 15 days [32]. This can be attributed to both the pricing structure and the large number of beds [33].

Regarding payment categories, respondents without health insurance had an association with prolonged LOS. This can be attributed to the fact that the majority of medical services in specialised hospitals are provided at a high cost; hence, a lack of health insurance could increase out-of-pocket payments and result in a longer period of bill payment compared to those with insurance [34]. Another key finding was that the NHIF funding has resulted in better services for insured populations compared to the uninsured, hence prolonged LOS. Our study elucidates that the patient’s ability to pay can alter prolonged LOS. It is also worthwhile to note that our results were similar to a study in Ghana, which showed that lack of health insurance was associated with prolonged LOS [35]. The current findings differ from studies reported in the United States [36] and Jordan [37], which showed that uninsured patients had significantly shorter hospital stays (0.3 days) relative to insured patients. The disparities may arise from the variation in healthcare provision among populations, which has been shown to rely on insurance status. Furthermore, by considering health system differences among patients with HF, recent Central European studies have revealed that nutritional status, BMI and sex-related differences are important determinants of in-hospital outcomes in patients with HF [38, 39]. Hence, these positive outcomes may reduce prolonged LOS among patients admitted with HF. Therefore, lack of health insurance has an association with prolonged LOS.

In terms of causes of HF, participants with HHD had longer hospital stays compared to those who had other causes. This can be described by the fact that patients with HHD may have complications like cardiac arrhythmias and infections, alongside pre-existing conditions that can increase the prolonged LOS [40]. Also, the presence of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) among patients with HHD has been associated with prolonged hospital stay as reported by Surma et al [41]. This indicates that patients with HF should receive quality health services that prevent them from complications associated with prolonged hospital stays. The finding of the current study is similar to that reported in Ghana, in which HHD was associated with increased hospital stays [35]. This can be related to both Tanzania and Ghana being in Sub-Saharan Africa, hence they may share similar cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension) related to HF [42]. In contrast with our findings, the study in Poland reported that the chief cause of HF was ischemic heart disease (IHD) [43] rather than HHD. This can be explained by the fact that Poland is one of the developed countries compared to Tanzania, hence sharing IHD as the commonest cause of HF due to lifestyle differences, including dietary plans, treatment regimens, and economic activities, which substantiates the differences.

Regarding underweight as a factor associated with prolonged LOS, the underlying condition leading to malnutrition and muscle loss (cardiac cachexia) increases the risk of complications [44]. This is further supported by a systematic review, which reported that being underweight is a source of infections that lowers immunity and hence requires more hospitalisation [45]. These findings are similar to those reported in Ethiopia, which revealed that patients with HF who were malnourished and underweight had a prolonged LOS [14]. The similarities in low BMI of these two studies can be due to the same geographical location between Tanzania and Ethiopia, as both face food shortages that influence more participants to be malnourished or underweight.

We found that place of residency had an association with prolonged LOS, where participants from rural areas had longer hospital stays compared to those from urban areas. This is because population distribution is mostly found in rural areas where there are limited healthcare services compared to urban areas (rural-urban divide) [46]. In addition, those from rural areas may lack awareness of their health status, including regular check-ups of their health, due to the remoteness of health facilities compared to those in urban areas. A study in Ethiopia showed that more than one-half of the participants were from rural areas and, regardless, had no association with the prolonged LOS [14]. This similarity in place of residency is due to both Ethiopia and Tanzania being nearly similar in terms of population distribution to rural areas, and both are Sub-Saharan African countries, hence they may share commonalities. Differences in association between them might be due to the design, as that in Ethiopia was a prospective cohort study, while this was a cross-sectional one.

Limitations

This study has several limitations; firstly, the study was conducted in a single cardiac health facility. Although JKCI is the only cardiac health facility in Tanzania and receives referrals across the country, patients with HF admitted to other facilities may not be represented. Secondly, it focused on patients with a single disease criterion (HF) only; hence, the prolonged LOS for patients with the same condition can differ due to various complex factors, including individual patient characteristics, clinical factors, and organisational influences or variations in medical practices. Thirdly, factors identified as associated with prolonged LOS reflect those prevalent among patients who survived and remained in care, while risk factors among patients who died were not captured. Consequently, the associations reported may not fully represent the determinants of outcomes in all patients with HF. Furthermore, the study excluded very sick patients and those with extremely short LOS (<1 day, likely those who died soon after admission). This might have underestimated the impact of certain risk factors that are common among severely ill patients. BMI of the patients taken during admission could affect the validity of the study, which may be confounded by fluid overload and oedema.

Conclusions

In this study, the prevalence of prolonged LOS among patients with HF is 51.1%. Hypertensive heart disease, treatment complications, being underweight, uninsured and rural residence were the factors associated with prolonged LOS. Addressing these factors may decrease the number of days spent by patients in the hospital. Increasing physical activity, controlling comorbidities, community awareness of insurance and improving discharge planning may shorten hospital stays for patients with HF. Health facilities should develop strategies to reduce prolonged LOS, especially for patients from rural areas. A multicentre study of LOS with a larger sample size in a similar context is recommended.
