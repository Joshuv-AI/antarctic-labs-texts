Validity and reliability of the Organic method used for assessment of plumage damage in laying hens
Authors: John E. Brindle, Leslie Foldager, Fernanda M. Tahamtani, Mogens Krogh, Anja B. Riber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Severe feather pecking is a persistent welfare concern in laying hens, leading to feather loss and potential pain and injury within commercial flocks. Evaluating the extent of plumage damage provides a practical measure of the prevalence and severity of this behavior. In Denmark, the Organic method was developed as a standardized tool for assessing plumage condition during on-farm welfare inspections. The present study aimed to determine the validity and reliability of the Organic method and to identify potential refinements to improve its accuracy. The Organic method was compared with four established assessment approaches: the Bilčík, Bright, Tauson, and Welfare Quality® (WQ) methods. The Organic and Bright methods are non-intrusive, i.e., the hens are observed from a distance, whereas the other methods require handling of the hens. Laying hens from organic commercial farms were transported to the experimental facility at AU-Viborg. Five groups of 150 hens, aged 25, 38, 50, 61, and 73 weeks, were assessed by five observers, with the Organic method applied twice per hen. The study found the Organic method to be easy to implement, even for observers with no prior experience. A sample size of 100 hens was sufficient to provide a reliable estimate of flock-level feather pecking. Based on the results, clarification of the threshold between scores 1 and 2 to “5 cm at the largest extent” is recommended. Overall, the Organic method offers a valid and reliable approach for assessing plumage condition and supports practical welfare evaluation in laying hens.

Introduction

Severe feather pecking is a damaging behavior commonly observed in laying hens, regardless of the production system in which they are kept (Rodenburg et al., 2008). The behavior involves forceful pecking, pulling, and removal of feathers from conspecifics (Savory, 1995). Feather pecking is widely recognized as an indicator of compromised welfare, as it is an abnormal behavior developed in response to environmental, social, and management-related stressors that limit the birds’ ability to express species-specific behaviors (Rodenburg et al., 2013). In addition, feather removal is painful for the recipient bird (Gentle and Hunter, 1990). In more severe cases, feather loss can lead to skin damage, increasing the risk of cannibalism, which may become fatal (Savory and Mann, 1997). Such injuries also create entry points for pathogens, potentially resulting in secondary infections, prolonged suffering, and increased mortality (Fossum et al., 2009). In addition, severe feather pecking has been associated with heightened fearfulness (Vestergaard et al., 1993; Jones et al., 2005) and is therefore considered a manifestation of reduced animal welfare.

Feather pecking is often understood as a consequence of frustrated behavioral needs, particularly when birds are unable to perform highly motivated natural behaviors such as foraging or dustbathing (Blokhuis, 1989; Vestergaard and Lisborg, 1993). More broadly, animal welfare is well established as a multifaceted concept that extends beyond physical health to include the ability to express species-specific behaviors and maintain appropriate social interactions (Fraser et al., 1997; Farm Animal Welfare, 2009). Environmental constraints that limit these behavioral opportunities can lead to stress, frustration, and the emergence of abnormal behaviors such as feather pecking in laying hens (Neethirajan, 2025; Papageorgiou et al., 2023; Rose et al., 2024). Social dynamics within flocks can also influence access to resources and contribute to the development and spread of such damaging behaviors (Zeltner et al., 2000). Furthermore, inappropriate management, such as interruption in the circadian clock, may also trigger feather pecking behaviors in laying hens (Bessei et al., 2023).

Obtaining a reliable measurement of the level of severe feather pecking behavior in large commercial flocks is time-consuming, as performance of the behavior may be sporadic and vary with time of day (Kjaer, 2000). Despite these difficulties, plumage damage in laying hens has long been considered a practical and informative indicator of feather pecking activity within a flock (Bilčík and Keeling, 1999). Supporting this approach, Bilčík and Keeling (1999) reported a positive association between the number of severe feather pecks received and the extent of plumage damage. Additionally, several studies have shown that plumage damage increases with age (Nicol et al., 2006; Drake et al., 2010; Hinrichsen et al., 2016). Such deterioration may result from feather wear, cumulative damage over time, or an escalation in severe feather pecking behavior. Nevertheless, the majority of plumage damage has been attributed to feather pecking (Bilčík and Keeling, 1999).

A range of methods have been developed to assess plumage condition. These approaches differ in terms of the body regions evaluated and the level of detail within their scoring scales. Additionally, some methods require physical handling of individual hens, such as the Bilčík, Tauson, and Welfare Quality® methods (Bilčík and Keeling, 1999; Tauson et al., 2005; Welfare Quality®, 2009), whereas others rely on non-intrusive visual assessments conducted at a distance, such as the Bright method (Bright et al., 2006) and the Organic method (NaturErhvervsstyrelsen, 2017). Non-intrusive methods offer practical advantages, as they are less time-consuming and reduce stress associated with catching and handling birds (Kjaer et al., 2011). However, whole-body assessments conducted at a distance using the Organic method may make it difficult to detect small or less visible areas of plumage damage, particularly in younger hens. In contrast, the Bright method provides a more detailed non-intrusive assessment by scoring five body regions using five levels, potentially providing greater detail while avoiding the stress associated with catching and handling birds (Bright et al., 2006; Kjaer et al., 2011). The Welfare Quality® method requires handling but uses a relatively simple three-level scale across three body regions and provides a defined framework for classifying overall plumage condition (Welfare Quality®, 2009). The Tauson method also requires handling and provides a more detailed assessment of five body regions using four levels (Tauson et al., 2005). Finally, the Bilčík method provides the most detailed assessment of the five methods considered here, covering 11 body regions and six scoring levels, but requires handling and includes body areas that are less likely to be targeted by feather pecks, e.g., legs, head and underneck (Bilčík and Keeling, 1999).

Despite their widespread use, most assessment methods focus primarily on quantifying plumage damage without providing clear guidance on how to interpret the results. This lack of interpretative frameworks may complicate the evaluation of damage severity and its implications for animal welfare. Notable exceptions include the Tauson and Welfare Quality® methods, both of which classify plumage condition into defined categories such as poor, intermediate, or good (Tauson et al., 2005; Welfare Quality®, 2009).

In Denmark, plumage condition is routinely assessed during official animal welfare inspections of organic flocks of laying hens. To support this process, the Organic method was developed based on practical industry experience in collaboration with the Danish Agricultural Agency (NaturErhvervsstyrelsen, 2017). In contrast to many other methods, the Organic method provides explicit guidance for interpreting plumage condition scores in relation to both feather condition and animal welfare (NaturErhvervsstyrelsen, 2017). As the Organic method is based solely on practical experience, there is a need to assess whether this method is useful for measuring the extent of plumage damage due to feather pecking as a proxy for laying hen welfare on-farm and ensuring that inspection outcomes are fair and consistent for egg producers. To the best of our knowledge, the Organic method has not previously been evaluated in peer-reviewed scientific literature. Consequently, the evidence supporting its use for an on-farm assessment of laying hen welfare remains limited.

The aim of the present study was to evaluate the validity and reliability of the Organic method. Validity was assessed by comparing outcomes from the Organic method with those obtained using four established welfare assessment methods. Reliability was assessed by examining both inter-observer and intra-observer consistency. A secondary objective was to determine whether adjustments to the assessment protocol could improve its accuracy. Findings from this study further support the use of the Organic method as a practical tool for evaluating welfare in laying hens.

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional study is reported following the STROBE-VET guidelines (Sargeant et al., 2016) for Strengthening and Reporting Observational Studies in Epidemiology (Veterinary Extension).

Animals and housing

Laying hens from organic commercial farms were brought to the experimental facility at Aarhus University. In total, five different groups of hens at 25, 38, 50, 61, and 73 weeks of age were included in the study (Table 1). The five age groups were selected because it was expected that plumage condition would deteriorate with age (Nicol et al., 2006; Drake et al., 2010; Hinrichsen et al., 2016) and the different scoring protocols should, therefore, be tested in different levels of plumage damage. The groups originated from different flocks on five farms. The order of the groups was determined by the availability of flocks with the desired age (25-73 weeks) and with age intervals of approximately 12 weeks between October 31st and December 19th, 2018. Three groups consisted of white hybrid Lohmann LSL-Classic hens (aged 25, 50, and 73 weeks), while the remaining two groups consisted of brown hybrid Lohmann Brown-Lite hens (aged 38 and 61 weeks). All flocks were housed on-farm according to Danish requirements for organic egg production (Økologivejledningen, 2018). Group size was approximately 150 hens (min-max: 148–151; mean: 149.4). When assessing plumage condition in commercial flocks, a sample size of 100 hens per flock is typically recommended (Welfare Quality®, 2009). A group size of 150 hens was therefore selected to evaluate whether increasing the recommended sample size by up to 50% would influence the reliability of the results. Hens were collected from the farm during the early morning, before popholes were opened. The lights were switched off, and hens were randomly selected from different areas of the house and from various resources (nest boxes, litter, feed troughs, drinking nipples, perches/tiers). Each group was transported to Aarhus University in turn, ensuring that only one group was present at the experimental facilities at any given time.

At the experimental facilities, the hens were housed in two pens, measuring 4 m × 6 m each, resulting in a stocking density of 3.1 hens per m2. Nest boxes and perches were available, and the hens had ad libitum access to feed and water. Bedding was provided in the form of an approximately 3 cm deep layer of wood shavings. Each group of hens was housed for 3 days. At the end of the third day, the hens were euthanized using CO2.

Data collection

Five observers participated in the data collection. Prior to the data collection, all five observers were introduced to the five methods used for assessing the plumage condition. All observers were asked to read the description of the different assessment protocols. Any questions regarding how to use the methods were discussed in plenum, no other formal training was provided. Observers differed in their level of experience with plumage assessment. One observer was highly experienced (>10,000 hens assessed), three were moderately experienced (1,000–3,000 hens), and one was completely naïve (0 hens). We included observers at different levels of experience to mimic real-life conditions that would be experience when employing the Organic method on-farm.

Plumage Assessment Methods. In addition to the Organic method (NaturErhvervsstyrelsen, 2017), four other methods were applied: the Bilčík (Bilčík and Keeling, 1999), Bright (Bright et al., 2006), the modified Tauson (Tauson) (Riber and Hinrichsen, 2016), and Welfare Quality® (WQ) (Welfare Quality®, 2009) methods. The modified Tauson is based on the method described by Tauson et al. (2005), but expanded with a written description of the different scores. Consistency among the methods was evaluated based on the number of hens assigned to each plumage condition category (good, moderate, or poor), both for the whole body and, where possible, for individual body parts.

To evaluate the reliability of the Organic method, inter-observer reliability was calculated for all methods to enable comparison. Intra-observer reliability was assessed based on repeated applications of the Organic method. Additionally, we investigated whether the reliability of the Organic method was affected by the sample size. To facilitate comparison with another non-intrusive but more detailed approach, the Bright method was included. The remaining three methods were selected because they involve handling of the hens and represent a gradient of complexity: the WQ method being the simplest, the Tauson method intermediate, and the Bilčík method the most detailed. Furthermore, these handling-based methods are among the most commonly used approaches for assessing plumage damage in scientific literature. The methods differ in both the number and type of body parts assessed. In contrast, the Organic method assesses the body as a whole rather than evaluating individual body parts. An overview of the body parts included in the other methods is provided in the Appendices 1 - 5. One exception is the Tauson method, in which the back and rump are combined into a single body part (back+rump). Similarly, in the WQ method, both back and rump, as well as head and neck, are each treated as single body regions.

Practical Procedure. Upon arrival at the experimental farm at Aarhus University, hens were individually removed from the transport crates and fitted with uniquely numbered leg bands. Each hen was then assessed by the five observers, each using one of the three intrusive methods (Bilčík, Tauson, and WQ). After approximately half of the hens in a group (≈ 75 hens) had been assessed, they were released one at a time into a pen by an assistant. Afterward, the five observers assessed plumage condition with the two non-intrusive methods (Bright and Organic). This process was repeated for the remaining hens. On days 2 and 3, data collection in each pen began by dimming the lights substantially to facilitate catching and placing hens into transport crates before plumage damage assessment.

All observers assessed each hen once using the Bright, WQ, Tauson, and Bilčík methods, and twice using the Organic method, with the order of assessments varied. During assessment, the identity of the hen and the observer was recorded simultaneously with the plumage scores according to the method applied. Observers using the same method were positioned so that they had no visual access to each other’s data sheets. During data collection the two youngest groups of hens (25 and 38 weeks) were omitted from the second round of assessment using the Organic method (day 3). These hens were omitted due to the large majority being assigned a score of 0 during the first round.

Data handling. To facilitate comparison across the assessment methods, a standardized interpretation of plumage condition was established. The Organic method was used as the reference, in which plumage condition is categorized into three levels: poor, moderate, and good. The WQ and Tauson methods apply a similar classification, whereas the Bright and Bilčík methods do not provide a direct interpretation of their raw scores. All methods distinguish between the absence of bald patches and the presence of bald patches of varying size. However, they differ in the threshold used to separate moderate and poor plumage condition. Some methods define this threshold as a bald patch ≥ 5 cm in diameter (Bilčík, WQ, Organic), while others use an area-based threshold of ≥ 5 cm² (Bright, Tauson). Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 3.4.4; R Core Team (2023); Vienna, Austria) and Stata (version 11.2; StataCorp (2023); Texas, USA). To assess consistency between methods in the distribution of hens across plumage condition categories (good, moderate, and poor), two separate models were fitted for each body parameter (i.e., whole body and individual body parts): one including Tauson1 and one including Tauson2. Because the two categorization approaches derived from the Tauson protocol differ by definition, they were expected to yield different results and were therefore not included in the same model.

The proportional distribution of hens across plumage condition categories summed for the whole body and for each individual body part were analysed using multinomial logistic regressions with assessment method and age as explanatory variables. Initially, the interaction between method and age was also included. However, those models did not converge due to the large number of zeros in the data, and the interaction term was excluded from the models. The variable ‘observer’ was also included as a fixed factor to account for variation between observers. Furthermore, the correlation between repeated measures of the same bird was accounted for in the calculation of the covariance matrix by use of the Huber and White (sandwich) estimator of variance. For analyses of individual body parts, only methods that assessed the respective region were included. The Organic and WQ methods did not assess the neck or tail individually; the Organic, WQ, and Tauson methods did not assess the wings, back, or rump individually; and the Bright and Organic methods did not assess the belly individually. These methods were therefore excluded from the corresponding analyses.

Inter-observer reliability was analysed by calculating weighted Cohen’s kappa agreement coefficients following the formulas found in Fleiss et al. (2003) and with the aid of bootstrapping techniques similar to those suggested by Vanbelle and Albert (2008). The weighting scheme used was linear: 1 - |i - j|/(k - 1), for i and j among the original integer plumage scores (see Table 2) having steps of 1 between adjacent scores for all of the investigated methods, and k is the total number of possible scores for the specific method. With this formula, perfect agreement was weighted by 1, and this weight was gradually reduced for disagreements between adjacent scores until the weight for the highest level of disagreement (e.g., one observer giving the highest score and the other giving the lowest score), which was weighted by 0. The interpretation of the kappa values was based on the suggestion by Landis and Koch (1977). For each method (Bilčík, Bright, Organic, Tauson and WQ), weighted kappa was calculated between all pairs of observers for the original sample and for each of 999 bootstrap samples. Note that since this is done using the methods actual plumage scores, Tauson is not divided into Tauson1 and Tauson2 for this part of the analyses. For each of these 1000 bootstrap samples, the 10 pairwise kappa statistics (among observers) were averaged to obtain a single kappa coefficient per method. Bootstrapping was used to account for correlations due to the repeated use of the same bird for multiple methods and assessments by the same observers and to avoid distributional assumptions for this average kappa statistic. The overall difference in average weighted kappa coefficients among methods were tested by Hotelling’s T2 test as shown by Vanbelle and Albert (2008) using the hot.test function from the multiagree R package (https://github.com/svanbelle/multiagree). Differences in kappa values between methods were based on the 95% confidence intervals for the pairwise comparisons, which are part of the output from the hot.test function. This procedure was performed three times with different sample sizes: 100 scored hens, 125 scored hens and 150 scored hens.

Observer bias for the current observer and method was calculated as the difference between the average of pairwise weighted kappa coefficients including the current observer and the average of the pairwise comparisons among the other observers. Finally, intra-observer reliability for the Organic method was calculated using the same methods as for inter-observer reliability but substituting the pairs of observers by the scores of each observer the first and second time this method was performed for each hen. Comparisons among observers were conducted using the same approach as described above for comparisons among methods.

Results of the weighted kappa analysis of inter-observer reliability for each assessment method, based on whole-body and individual body-part assessments of 100, 125, and 150 hens, can be found in Appendices 7 and 8. Weighted kappa values for body parts assessed by only one method, and therefore not comparable across methods, are provided in Appendix 9. Overall, the level of agreement between observers for the sum of all body parts was high for all assessment methods and sample sizes (min-max kappa: 0.56-0.67). Nevertheless, differences between assessment methods were observed for all sample sizes (P < 0.001). At a sample size of 100 hens, the Organic method differed only from the other non-intrusive method (Bright), showing a lower level of agreement (κ = 0.62 vs. 0.67). At sample sizes of 125 and 150 hens, this difference was no longer statistically significant; kappa was unchanged for the Bright method but increased to 0.63 and 0.64 for the Organic method. Instead, a difference was observed between the Organic and WQ methods, with kappa for WQ decreasing slightly from κ = 0.57 at 100 and 125 hens to κ = 0.56 at 150 hens. Across all sample sizes, the WQ method consistently showed the lowest level of agreement between observers.

Regarding agreement across body parts, the rump, whether assessed alone or as part of back and rump, showed the highest inter-observer agreement (min-max κ: 0.61–0.73). For the rump, the Bilčík and Tauson methods had the lowest agreement (κ: 0.61-0.62), while the Bright and WQ methods showed the highest (κ: 0.69-0.73). For the back and neck, inter-observer agreement did not differ between the non-intrusive Bright method and the handling-based Bilčík method. For the Neck, the weighted kappa for Tauson was for all sample sizes significantly higher than Bilcik, while only for N = 150 significantly higher than Bright. For Bilcik kappa from 0.47 to 0.49, for Bright kappa from 0.45 to 0.50 (actually decreasing with large sample size), and for Tauson it was 0.53 to 0.54. In general, across body parts and methods, only slight changes in the inter-observer reliability were seen when increasing the sample size from 100 to 125 and further on to 150 hens. None of the changes was consistent within method or within body part.

Discussion

The results from this study highlight the differences and similarities between commonly used methods for the assessment of plumage condition in laying hens and the Organic method. As none of the assessment methods can be appointed as the gold standard, these differences and similarities are used to discuss the reliability and validity of the Organic method.

Agreement in outcome between assessment methods

All methods differed in how they classified hens into the categories good, moderate, and poor plumage condition. A key challenge arises from applying a uniform three-category classification to five distinct assessment protocols. In this study, the Bilčík and Bright methods appeared to be biased towards classifying plumage as either good or moderate. Even at 73 weeks of age, neither method classified any hens as having poor plumage condition. The observed bias may stem from the simplification involved in translating method-specific scores into the three categories, potentially leading to hens being assigned to a better category than their actual condition would suggest.

For example, in the Bilčík method, individual body parts can receive scores of 0–2 when no bald patches are present, corresponding to good condition. When summed across all 11 body parts, this can yield a whole-body score of up to 22, which still falls within the “good” category. However, identical total scores may reflect very different distributions of plumage damage across body parts. For instance, a hen could have four completely denuded body parts and intact plumage elsewhere, resulting in a whole-body score of 20. Therefore, that hen would be classified as having good plumage condition under the present criteria. In contrast, a score of 44 is required to classify a hen as having poor plumage condition using the Bilčík method. A threshold that is likely unrealistically high, as plumage would typically be considered poor well before such a score is reached. By comparison, the WQ method classifies whole-body plumage as poor if a single body part has a featherless area ≥ 5 cm in diameter. Consequently, the WQ method classified 76 out of 500 observations as poor by 25 weeks of age, and none as good by 73 weeks of age.

The three-category scoring aligned well with all methods, except when applied to individual body parts in the Tauson method, where score 3 falls between the categories of good and moderate. The descriptive text for this score states: “Complete or almost completely feathered, few feathers damaged. Featherless areas < 5 cm².” These statements appear contradictory, particularly in comparison with the Bright method, where a score associated with a bald patch < 5 cm² is described as “severe damage to feathers but localized naked area (< 5 cm²).” To address this ambiguity, two separate analyses were conducted: one (Tauson1) in which a score of 3 in the Tauson method was classified as a moderate plumage condition, and another (Tauson2) in which it was classified as good plumage condition. These findings suggest that the simplified three-category scoring used in this study is not well-suited for whole-body assessments when applied to the Bilčík and Bright methods. Accordingly, the Bilčík method may be more appropriate for comparisons at the level of individual body parts rather than whole-body assessments. In contrast, the Organic, Tauson, and WQ methods produced a more balanced distribution of hens across categories at the whole-body level. Across all ages, the Organic method yielded results intermediate between the Tauson and WQ methods in terms of the proportion of hens classified in the outermost categories (good and poor). Thus, among the five methods evaluated, the Organic method can be considered moderate in its classification while still making use of the full range of categories. More generally, the present findings highlight the challenges associated with comparing welfare assessment protocols that differ in scoring structure and level of detail. Similar concerns have been raised for other poultry welfare assessment protocols, where assessment methods must balance validity, consistency between assessors, and practical feasibility under commercial conditions (Webster et al., 2008).

Plumage condition across flock ages

On Danish organic laying hen farms, plumage condition assessed using the Organic method is used as an indicator of animal welfare during inspections. As hens age, a higher proportion of individuals with plumage damage is accepted before welfare is considered compromised in the flock (NaturErhvervsstyrelsen, 2017). This places an important requirement on the Organic method to provide an accurate and representative assessment of plumage condition across all ages. The age-related deterioration in plumage condition observed in the present study is consistent with previous reports from both experimental and commercial cage-free systems (Nicol et al., 2006; Drake et al., 2010; Hinrichsen et al., 2016; Sokołowicz et al., 2023). However, our results further suggest that assessment method can influence the perceived severity of plumage deterioration, particularly in younger flocks where minor localized bald patches may be present, which are difficult to detect using non-intrusive assessments. However, our data suggest that, unlike the WQ method, the Organic method may underestimate the presence of bald patches in younger hens (i.e., 25 and 38 weeks of age). At these ages, overall plumage condition is typically good, although small bald patches may occur. Such patches can easily go unnoticed when using a non-intrusive method, as surrounding feathers may partially or fully cover them, particularly when the feather condition is otherwise intact. The Tauson method, despite involving direct handling, also did not classify any hens as having poor whole-body plumage at 25 and 38 weeks. However, similar to the Bilčík and Bright methods, it appeared somewhat biased towards the good and moderate categories, possibly because localized damage in otherwise intact plumage may not be sufficient to result in a poor overall score.

The Organic method therefore appears better suited for assessing plumage condition in older hens, where damage may be more extensive and less likely to be obscured by surrounding feathers. It should be noted that the present study compared flocks of different ages rather than following the same flock over time. Consequently, age-related patterns should be interpreted as associations between flock age and plumage condition rather than direct evidence of within-flock deterioration over time.

Suitability of different body parts for plumage assessment

One aim of analysing differences between methods at the level of individual body parts was to determine whether some regions could be assessed more consistently across methods than others. The neck was the only body part for which no differences were found between methods. In this case, the Bilčík and Bright methods showed similar categorisation when analysed together with the Tauson1 method.

Several hens had bald patches in the lower belly region (closest to the breast) even at a young age, which may explain why the WQ method, unlike the other methods, identified hens with poor whole-body plumage at these ages. The WQ method, which involves handling, uses a simpler three-point scale and assesses only three body parts, including the belly. Importantly, if any one body part exhibits a large bald patch, the entire bird is classified as having poor plumage condition. In addition, the presence of a brood patch may further complicate the assessment of the belly. These factors raise questions about whether the belly plumage condition is a reliable indirect indicator of feather pecking. Furthermore, bald patches are difficult, if not impossible, to detect using non-intrusive methods like the Organic method as hens are observed from a distance and typically from above.

For the tail, the count data showed that at 73 weeks of age, the Bright method classified fewer hens as having poor plumage condition compared to the other methods. The difference between the Bright and Bilčík methods was unexpected, given that their descriptions of poor plumage are nearly identical. Our findings highlight the challenges of assessing plumage condition from a distance: when birds can be handled and feathers manipulated (e.g., by spreading the tail), damage is more easily detected.

Inter-observer reliability for the different methods

Using the Organic method, the five observers achieved substantial agreement across all sample sizes. Among whole-body assessments, only the Bright method had higher inter-observer agreement, and only at a sample size of 100. Both methods are non-intrusive; however, the more detailed scoring system of the Bright method may provide greater guidance to observers, thereby reducing disagreement. The WQ method showed lower agreement than the Organic method at sample sizes of 125 and 150. This may be explained by differences in how observers interpreted the belly region. In the present study, the naïve observer defined the belly more narrowly than the others. As the belly showed low inter-observer agreement across all methods that included it (WQ, Tauson, Bilčík), such inconsistencies can strongly influence whole-body agreement. For individual body parts, agreement was lowest for the primary wing feathers. As these receive less than 3% of feather pecks (Bilčík and Keeling, 1999), they are likely a poor indicator of feather pecking damage. In contrast, the highest agreement was found for the rump and back+rump. These regions are both highly visible in non-intrusive assessments and account for more than 30% of feather pecks (Bilčík and Keeling, 1999), supporting their inclusion in assessment protocols.

Reporting of inter-observer reliability in plumage assessment is limited and complicated by variation in metrics used (Tauson et al., 1984; Bright et al., 2006; Keppler, 2010; Kjaer et al., 2011). Kjaer et al. (2011) reported on the agreement for a handling-based method (κ = 0.61) and agreement for a non-intrusive method (κ = 0.59), both lower than the results found in the present study for the Organic method (κ = 0.67). Consistent with our findings, Kjaer et al. (2011) reported the highest agreement for body parts corresponding to back+rump, and lower agreement for regions including the belly. However, they found higher agreement for wings than in the present study, possibly because their assessment combined coverts and primary feathers, whereas we assessed primary feathers separately. Similar to our results, Bright et al. (2006) also reported lower agreement for primary wing feathers. Unlike Kjaer et al. (2011), who consistently found lower agreement for non-intrusive methods, our results showed comparable or higher agreement for non-intrusive approaches. This suggests that inter-observer reliability may depend not only on whether a method is intrusive, but also on the specific design of the assessment protocol.

Intra-observer reliability for the Organic method

The repeated assessments of each hen using the Organic method allowed estimation of intra-observer reliability (self-agreement) for each observer. The intra-observer measure reflects the consistency with which an observer assigns scores. As expected, intra-observer reliability was higher than inter-observer reliability, in line with previous findings (Keppler, 2010). Overall, the results suggest that the Organic method is easy to apply and that its scoring criteria are sufficiently clear to enable consistent assessments, even for a naïve observer with no prior experience in plumage scoring. This is particularly relevant because animal welfare assessments in commercial production systems are frequently conducted by auditors and other personnel who may have limited prior knowledge of poultry welfare (Webster at al., 2008).

The only exception was observer 3, who showed relatively low self-agreement despite being moderately experienced. Together with the consistently low agreement between this observer and others across all assessment methods (as indicated by the observer bias analysis), this suggests difficulty in interpreting the scoring parameters.

Does sample size matter?

When assessing plumage condition on-farm, only a sample of the flock can be evaluated due to time constraints (Kjaer, 2000; Bright et al., 2006; Marchewka et al., 2013). If this sample provides a reliable estimate of the flock’s overall plumage condition, it can be considered sufficient (Bright et al., 2006). One way to assess sample adequacy is to examine whether inter- and intra-observer reliability change with increasing sample size (Bright et al., 2006). If agreement remains stable, the smallest sample size can be regarded as sufficient (Bright et al., 2006). For the Organic method, only minor and non-significant changes in reliability were observed when increasing the sample size from 100 to 125 or 150 hens. Thus, the results indicate that a sample size of 100 is sufficient to obtain a reliable estimate of flock plumage condition, assuming the sample is representative of the flock. A similar conclusion was reached by Bright et al. (2006) for the non-intrusive Bright method, although they noted that in flocks with high within-flock variation, a larger sample size (at least 200 hens) may be required to reliably detect changes in plumage condition.

Practical application of the Organic method

During preparation for data collection, we encountered ambiguity when interpreting the Organic method scoring. Specifically, the threshold for distinguishing between scores 1 and 2 is defined as a bald patch “5 cm in diameter.” Observers reported that it was unclear whether this referred to a circular patch with a diameter of 5 cm in all directions or to the maximum extent of the patch. We adopted the latter interpretation, “5 cm at the largest extent”, which aligns with the criterion used in the WQ method. This represents a stricter definition than requiring a uniform 5 cm diameter. Despite this stricter threshold, the Organic method yielded a moderate classification of whole-body plumage relative to the other methods. Based on this, we recommend clarifying the protocol to explicitly define the threshold as either “5 cm in diameter at the largest extent” or simply “5 cm at the largest extent” to avoid ambiguity.

In the present study, the Organic method was applied under experimental conditions to allow repeated assessment of the same individuals. However, there is no clear reason to expect the method to perform differently under on-farm conditions. For routine on-farm welfare assessments, indicators must be feasible to collect under commercial conditions. Plumage condition is particularly relevant because it can provide a quick, non-invasive, and practical measure that can be assessed without specialized equipment (Jung et al., 2026). However, as with other practical welfare indicators, plumage condition provides an indirect measure of the underlying behavioral problem and therefore must be validated to ensure that it reliably reflects feather-pecking related welfare consequences (Browning, 2022).In practice, hens typically maintain a distance from humans that falls within the recommended observation range (< 2 m). In more fearful flocks, observers can remain stationary to allow birds to habituate and approach for improved visibility. On-farm application may be more practical, as observers can select alternative birds if visibility is obstructed or individuals move away.

Conclusion

The findings indicate that the Organic method provides a moderate assessment of whole-body plumage condition for all categories. The influence of age on validity appears minor, likely negligible, even in younger flocks, supporting its use across all ages. Among the body parts examined, only the neck showed consistent categorisation across assessment methods. In terms of reliability, the highest inter-observer agreement was found for the rump and back+rump, which is advantageous for the Organic method, as these areas, along with the neck, are visible from most angles during non-intrusive assessment. Both inter- and intra-observer reliability results suggest that the Organic method is easy to apply and that its scoring descriptions are generally sufficient (with minor clarifications needed) to ensure consistent assessments, even by inexperienced observers. Overall, the findings support that the Organic method, as used in animal welfare inspections at Danish organic farms, provides a valid and reliable estimate of flock plumage condition.
