Simultaneous Approximation of the Score Function and Its Derivatives by Deep Neural Networks
Konstantin Yakovlev, Nikita Puchkin

Abstract
We present a theory for simultaneous approximation of the score function and its derivatives, enabling the handling of data distributions with low-dimensional structure and unbounded support. Our approximation error bounds match those in the literature while relying on assumptions that relax the usual bounded support requirement. Crucially, our bounds are free from the curse of dimensionality. Moreover, we establish approximation guarantees for derivatives of any prescribed order, extending beyond the commonly considered first-order setting.



1
Introduction

Score estimation, the task of learning the gradient of the log density, has become a crucial part of generative diffusion models
(
Song and Ermon 2019
;
Song et al. 2021
)
.
These models achieve state-of-the-art performance in a wide range of domains including images, audio and video synthesis
(
Dhariwal and Nichol 2021
;
Kong et al. 2021
;
Ho et al. 2022
)
.
To sample from the desired distribution, one needs to have an accurate score function estimator along the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process.
In the context of diffusion models the score estimation is done through the minimization of
denoising score matching
loss function over the class of neural networks
(
Song et al. 2021
;
Vincent 2011
;
Oko et al. 2023
)
.
Another recipe for score estimation is
implicit score matching
proposed by
Hyvärinen 2005
.
The proposed objective includes not only the score function, but also its Jacobian trace.

A crucial research question is to determine the
iteration complexity
of the distribution estimation given inaccurate score function.
The convergence theory of diffusion models has received much attention in the recent years.
Some works
(
De Bortoli 2022
;
Chen et al. 2023b
;
Benton et al. 2024
;
Li and Yan 2024
)
study SDE-based samplers under the assumption that the score estimator is
L^{2}
-accurate.
However, several recent works
(
Yang et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024a
;
Li et al. 2024b
;
Li et al. 2024c
;
Huang et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025
)
demonstrate that ODE-based samplers can achieve faster convergence rate, provided that not only the score function but also its Jacobian matrix are well-approximated.
Notably,
Huang et al. 2025
further requires boundedness of the second derivatives of the estimated score.
A similar issue arises in the convergence analysis of Langevin dynamics under the inaccurate drift assumption
(
Huggins and Zou 2017
;
Majka et al. 2020
)
.
In these works, the approximate drift is assumed to be Lipschitz continuous, a condition that is satisfied if the drift has bounded derivatives on the entire space.

Recent works considering denoising score matching
(
Oko et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Tang and Yang 2024
;
Azangulov et al. 2024
;
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025b
)
primarily focus on quantification of the
L^{2}
-error leaving the study of higher-order derivatives out of the scope.
To our knowledge, only
Shen et al. 2023
addresses this issue providing rigorous guarantees for approximation of first-order derivatives.
However, the problem of approximating higher-order score derivatives remains open.
Furthermore,
Shen et al. 2023
assumes that the target distribution has a bounded support while generative diffusion models usually deal with data corrupted with Gaussian noise.

Recent advances in analysis of differentiable neural networks have demonstrated their ability to approximate not only functions but also their derivatives.
For example, Sobolev training
(
Czarnecki et al. 2017
)
and physics-informed neural networks
(
Raissi and Karniadakis 2018
;
Raissi et al. 2019
;
Mishra and Molinaro 2023
)
leverage derivative information to improve generalization ability of the resulting estimators.
Furthermore,
Gühring and Raslan 2021
;
De Ryck et al. 2021
;
Abdeljawad and Grohs 2022
;
Belomestny et al. 2023
establish that differentiable neural networks can universally approximate functions and their derivatives in a range of smoothness classes, including Sobolev and Hölder spaces.
Their findings suggest that neural networks are well-suited for tasks requiring higher-order smoothness, such as score estimation through implicit score matching.
The only drawback of these works is that they focus on full-dimensional settings and face the curse of dimensionality. In contrast, we are interested in scenarios where data lies near a low-dimensional manifold, which is typical for real-world data
(
Bengio et al. 2013
;
Pope et al. 2021
;
Brown et al. 2023
)
.
Although some works have explored approximation capabilities of neural networks with a rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation function under the assumption that the input data lies exactly on a low-dimensional manifold or has a small Minkowski dimension
(
Schmidt-Hieber 2019
;
Chen et al. 2019
;
Nakada and Imaizumi 2020
;
Jiao et al. 2023
)
, the problem of simultaneously approximating functions and their derivatives in a noisy setup, which is common in analysis of generative diffusion models, remains underexplored.

We also note that score estimation along with its high-order derivatives is crucial when estimating the density ridge
(
Genovese et al. 2014
;
Sasaki et al. 2018
)
.
Specifically, to estimate the density ridge, one must have a precise estimate of the score function and its derivatives up to order three in the neighbourhood of the ridge.
Moreover, a common data model for density ridge estimation assumes a hidden manifold structure: the target distribution is generated by first sampling a latent variable supported on an unknown low-dimensional manifold and then adding full-dimensional noise
(
Genovese et al. 2014
)
.

This naturally leads to the following research question:

Can a feedforward neural network simultaneously approximate the score function and all its derivatives up to any prescribed order, while operating in the presence of full-dimensional noise and avoiding the curse of dimensionality?

Contribution.
We present approximation theory for simultaneous approximation of the score function and its derivatives up to an arbitrarily large predefined order.
Our theory covers the case of data distributions obtained by first applying a smooth mapping to a uniformly distributed input on a low‑dimensional unit cube and then convolving the result with Gaussian noise.
Specifically, we consider distributions for which a random vector
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
is generated according to the model:

\displaystyle X_{0}=g^{*}(U)+\sigma Z,

where
U\sim\mathrm{Un}([0,1]^{d})
and
Z\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I_{D})
are independent.
Here, the unknown mapping
g^{*}:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
belongs to a certain smoothness class.
Notably, the effective dimension
d
may be significantly smaller than the ambient dimension
D
, which is a common feature of real-world data arising in tasks such as density ridge estimation
(
Genovese et al. 2014
)
and diffusion-based generative modeling
(
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025b
)
.
A key advantage of our approach is that it does not impose the restriction of bounded support on the target distribution, in contrast to prior work such as
Shen et al. 2023
.

Extending the approximation framework originally developed for rectified linear unit (ReLU) neural networks
(
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025b
)
, we consider Gaussian error linear units (GELU)
(
Hendrycks and Gimpel 2016
)
, an infinitely smooth alternative to ReLU.
We leverage the results from
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025a
, which showed that feedforward neural networks with GELU activation functions can approximate elementary operations including multiplication, exponential functions, and division.
We also carefully track the dependence on both the ambient dimension
D
and the noise scale
\sigma
in the derivation of the architecture of the neural network.
Note that capturing this dependence is crucial in high-dimensional settings.

Preliminaries and notations

Norms.
For a vector
v
we denote its Euclidean norm by
\|v\|
, the maximal absolute value of its entries by
\|v\|_{\infty}
, and the number of its non-zero entries by
\|v\|_{0}
.
Similarly,
\|A\|_{\infty}
and
\|A\|_{0}
denote the maximal absolute value of entries of
A
and the number of its non-zero entries, respectively.
Finally, for a function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}=\operatornamewithlimits{esssup}_{x\in\Omega}\|f(x)\|,\quad\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,w)}=\left\{\int\limits_{\Omega}\|f(x)\|^{2}w(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\right\}^{1/2}.

When the set
\Omega
is clear from context, we often write
\|f\|_{L^{2}(w)}
to avoid the abuse of notation.

Smoothness spaces.
To characterize regularity of functions in our analysis, we introduce the Sobolev space and the Hölder class.
Their formal definitions are given below.

Definition 2.1

(Sobolev space)
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{r}
be an open set.
Define the Sobolev space
W^{k,\infty}(\Omega)
with
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
as

\displaystyle W^{k,\infty}(\Omega)=\{f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega):\partial^{\mathbf{k}}f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\quad\text{for every }{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{r}\text{ with }|{\mathbf{k}}|\leqslant k\},

where
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
is the Lebesgue space.
We define the Sobolev seminorm on
W^{k,\infty}(\Omega)
as

\displaystyle|f|_{W^{k,\infty}(\Omega)}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{r},\;|{\mathbf{k}}|=k\end{subarray}}\|\partial^{\mathbf{k}}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

Based on the seminorm, introduce the Sobolev norm on
W^{k,\infty}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{W^{k,\infty}(\Omega)}=\max_{0\leqslant m\leqslant k}|f|_{W^{m,\infty}(\Omega)}.

Definition 2.2

(Hölder class)
.

For given
\beta>0
we let
{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}
to be the largest integer strictly less than
\beta
.
Let also the function space
C^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}(\Omega)
contain the functions
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
which have bounded and continuous derivatives up to order
{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}
, that is,

\displaystyle C^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}=\left\{f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}:\max_{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{r},\;|{\mathbf{k}}|\leqslant{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}\|\partial^{\mathbf{k}}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}<\infty\right\}.

The Hölder class consists of those functions in
C^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}(\Omega)
whose derivatives of order
{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}
are
(\beta-{\lfloor\beta\rfloor})
-Hölder-continuous.
Formally, we define

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}^{\beta}(\Omega,\mathbb{R},H)=\left\{f\in C^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}(\Omega):\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{r}\\
|{\mathbf{k}}|\leqslant{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}\end{subarray}}\|\partial^{\mathbf{k}}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leqslant H,\;\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{r}\\
|{\mathbf{k}}|={\lfloor\beta\rfloor}\end{subarray}}\sup_{x\not=y\in\Omega}\frac{|\partial^{\mathbf{k}}f(x)-\partial^{\mathbf{k}}f(y)|}{1\wedge\|x-y\|_{\infty}^{\beta-{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}}\leqslant H\right\}.

The Hölder class for vector-valued functions is defined component-wise.
That is, a mapping
h:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, belongs to
{\mathcal{H}}^{\beta}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{m},H)
if and only if each coordinate function
h_{i}
lies in
{\mathcal{H}}^{\beta}(\Omega,\mathbb{R},H)
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant m
.

In this paper we focus on feed-forward neural networks with the activation function

\displaystyle\mathrm{GELU}(x)=x\cdot\Phi(x),\quad\Phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{x}e^{-t^{2}/2}{\mathrm{d}}t.

The choice of the activation function is motivated by the fact that it is infinitely smooth, and all its derivatives are uniformly bounded (see Lemma
B.9

Next, for a vector

\displaystyle\mathrm{GELU}_{b}(x)=(\mathrm{GELU}(x_{1}-b_{1}),\dots,\mathrm{GELU}(x_{r}-b_{r})),\quad x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}.

For some
L\in\mathbb{N}
and a vector

, a neural network of depth
L
and architecture
W
is a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{W_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{W_{L}}
such that

\displaystyle f(x)=-b_{L}+A_{L}\circ\mathrm{GELU}_{b_{L-1}}\circ A_{L-1}\circ\mathrm{GELU}_{b_{L-2}}\circ\dots\circ A_{2}\circ\mathrm{GELU}_{b_{1}}\circ A_{1}\circ x,

(1)

where
A_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{W_{j}\times W_{j-1}}
is a weight matrix and

The maximum number of neurons of each layer is denoted as
\|W\|_{\infty}

We define the class of neural networks of the form (

3
Main results

We begin by formulating the main assumption of our work, which allows us to mitigate the curse of dimensionality.

Assumption 3.1
.

Let
g^{*}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\beta}([0,1]^{d},\mathbb{R}^{D},H)
, for some
H>0
and
d,D\in\mathbb{N}
, with
\|g^{*}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\leqslant 1
.
Let
\sigma>0
, and suppose that the data sample
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
is generated by the model:

\displaystyle X_{0}=g^{*}(U)+\sigma\,Z,

where
U\sim\mathrm{Un}([0,1]^{d})
and
Z\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I_{D})
are independent random elements.

Informally, Assumption
3.1
models distributions concentrated near a low-dimensional manifold, perturbed by isotropic Gaussian noise.
This is motivated by the common observation that real-world data often has a small intrinsic dimension despite residing in a high-dimensional space
(
Levina and Bickel 2004
;
Pope et al. 2021
)
.
This assumption is milder than those in related works, as it does not require the image of
g^{*}
to have a positive reach
(
Tang and Yang 2024
;
Azangulov et al. 2024
)
or its distribution to have a lower-bounded density with respect to the volume measure
(
Oko et al. 2023
;
Tang and Yang 2024
;
Azangulov et al. 2024
)
.
Similar relaxed assumptions have been successfully used to analyze the convergence rates of generative adversarial networks
(
Schreuder et al. 2021
;
Stéphanovitch et al. 2024
)
and diffusion models
(
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025b
)
, and they play a crucial role in alleviating the curse of dimensionality.

In view of Assumption
3.1
, the data distribution density
\mathsf{p}^{*}(y)
has an explicit form

\displaystyle\mathsf{p}^{*}(y)=(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{-D}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-g^{*}(u)\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}\,{\mathrm{d}}u,\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}.

(3)

Therefore, score function also can be explicitly written

\displaystyle s^{*}(y)=\nabla\log\mathsf{p}^{*}(y)=-\frac{y}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{f^{*}(y)}{\sigma^{2}},\quad f^{*}(y)=\frac{\int\limits_{[0,1]^{d}}g^{*}(u)\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-g^{*}(u)\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u}{\int\limits_{[0,1]^{d}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-g^{*}(u)\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u}.

(4)

The derived result motivates us to consider GELU neural networks with tailored architecture.
We now present the main results of the paper, focusing on the quantitative expressive power of the neural network class defined in (
2
) in approximating the true score function.

Theorem 3.2

(approximation of the true score function)
.

Grant Assumption
3.1
.
Also assume that
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
is sufficiently small in the sense that it satisfies

\varepsilon^{\beta}\leqslant\frac{{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}!}{Hd^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}\sqrt{D}}\left(1\wedge\frac{C_{1}\sigma^{2}}{\sqrt{D}m^{2}\left(\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log\left(mD\sigma^{-2}\right)\right)}\right)

and

(H\vee 1)^{2}P(d,\beta)^{2}Dm^{2}(\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log(mD\sigma^{-2}))\varepsilon\leqslant C_{2}\sigma^{2},

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are absolute positive constants.
Then for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a score function approximation
\bar{s}\in{\mathcal{S}}(L,W,S,B)
of the form

\displaystyle\bar{s}(y)=-\frac{y}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{\bar{f}(y)}{\sigma^{2}},\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{D},

which satisfies

\displaystyle(i)

\displaystyle\quad\max_{1\leqslant l\leqslant D}\max_{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D},\,|{\mathbf{k}}|\leqslant m}\|\partial^{\mathbf{k}}[\bar{s}_{l}-s^{*}_{l}]\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}\lesssim\sigma^{-4|{\mathbf{k}}|-8}e^{{\mathcal{O}}(|{\mathbf{k}}|\log|{\mathbf{k}}|)}D^{2}\varepsilon^{2\beta}\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon)\log^{2}\left(mD\sigma^{-2}\right),

\displaystyle(ii)

\displaystyle\quad\max_{1\leqslant l\leqslant D}|\bar{f}_{l}|_{W^{k,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}\leqslant\sigma^{-2k}\exp\left\{{\mathcal{O}}\Big(k^{2}\log\left(mD\log(1/\varepsilon)\log\left(\sigma^{-2}\right)\right)\Big)\right\},\quad\text{for all }0\leqslant k\leqslant m.

Moreover,
\bar{f}
has the following configuration:

\displaystyle L\lesssim\log\big(mD\sigma^{-2}\log(1/\varepsilon)\big),\quad\log B\lesssim m^{85}D^{8}\log^{26}(mD\sigma^{-2})\log^{21}(1/\varepsilon),

\displaystyle\|W\|_{\infty}\vee S\lesssim\varepsilon^{-d}D^{16+P(d,\beta)}m^{132+17P(d,\beta)}\sigma^{-48-4P(d,\beta)}\left(\log(mD\sigma^{-2})\log(1/\varepsilon)\right)^{38+4P(d,\beta)},

where
P(d,\beta)=\binom{d+{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}{d}
.

A full proof of Theorem
3.2
is given in Section
4
.
We now compare our result with that of
Shen et al. 2023
.
For this purpose, let us set
m=1
and assume that the precision parameter
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small.
Under these conditions, Theorem
3.2
guarantees the mean square error of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{2\beta})
for both the score function and all of its derivatives up to order
m
.
This is achieved by a neural network with
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-d})
non-zero parameters, thereby mitigating the curse of dimensionality.
This matches the dependence on
\varepsilon
obtained in
Shen et al. 2023
.

However, there are key differences in the assumptions of the two results.
First,
Shen et al. 2023
assumes that the data is supported in a neighbourhood of radius
\rho
of a sufficiently regular manifold and has bounded support,
while our Assumption
3.1
allows for unbounded data distributions with low-dimensional structure.
Note that the image of
g^{*}
is not necessarily a manifold.
Second, the analysis in
Shen et al. 2023
heavily relies on a general approximation result for functions from the space
C^{s}
for some
s\in\mathbb{N}
(see Definition
2.2
).
A key limitation of this approach is that it does not provide approximation guarantees for derivatives of order higher than one.
In contrast, by leveraging specific properties of the score function under Assumption
3.1
, we circumvent this restriction.
Since the score function is infinitely smooth in our setting (see
(
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025b
, Lemma 4.1)
), we establish approximation guarantees not only for the score itself but also for its derivatives of arbitrarily high order.
The most important limitation of
(
Shen et al. 2023
)
is that the neighborhood radius
\rho
is required to vanish with the approximation error
\varepsilon
.
This forces the data to lie arbitrarily close to the manifold, allowing their analysis to mimic the case of data supported exactly on it.
For this reason, the setup of
(
Shen et al. 2023
)
is even more restrictive than the ones of
(
Tang and Yang 2024
;
Azangulov et al. 2024
)
.
In contrast, or model allows the noise scale
\sigma
in Assumption
3.1
to be fixed, directly resolving this limitation and handling a wider class of data distributions.
This work thus establishes the first approximation error bounds for this broad class of distributions that avoid the curse of dimensionality.

We also emphasize that the result derived in Theorem
3.2
cannot be derived from general approximation theory for functions and their derivatives
(
Belomestny et al. 2023
;
De Ryck et al. 2021
)
.
Specifically, applying the bound on the Sobolev seminorms of
f^{*}
(see (
4
)) from Lemma
4.1
, and invoking Corollary 1 from
Belomestny et al. 2023
,
yields an approximation of each component of
s^{*}
and its derivatives up to order
m
with accuracy
\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(\varepsilon^{2\beta})
on
[0,1]^{D}
using a neural network with
\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}((mD\log(1/\varepsilon))^{D})
non-zero parameters.
Evidently, when the ambient dimension
D\gtrsim\log(1/\varepsilon)
, this parameter count exceeds that given in Theorem
3.2
.
Finally,
Belomestny et al. 2023
does not guarantee a finite Sobolev norm outside the unit cube, limiting its applicability to
L^{2}
-norm error quantification.

Let us elaborate on the intuition behind claim
(ii)
of Theorem
3.2
.
Its proof builds upon Lemma
4.1
, which establishes that, for each
1\leqslant l\leqslant D
and
1\leqslant k\leqslant m
, it holds that
|f^{*}_{l}|_{W^{k,\infty}}\leqslant\sigma^{-2k}\exp\{{\mathcal{O}}(k\log k)\}
.
Our bound, however, deteriorate faster with
k
as
\exp\{{\mathcal{O}}(k^{2}\log m)\}
.
This faster growth with respect to
k
is an artifact of our proof.
It originates from applying the general Sobolev norm bound for function composition (Lemma
B.11
).
The composition rule inherently accounts for all derivative combinations, introducing a combinatorial overhead that leads to the
k^{2}
dependence.
Crucially, this discrepancy does not limit the applicability of our main result.
Thanks to its mild requirement of controlling only a small number of derivatives (
m\leqslant 2
), Theorem
3.2
allows us to quantify the approximation error in the convergence analysis of diffusion models
(
Yang et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024a
;
Li et al. 2024b
;
Li et al. 2024c
;
Huang et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025
)
.

It is worth noting that Theorem
3.2
can be extended to the case where one aims to approximate the score function along the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck forward process
(
Oko et al. 2023
;
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025b
)
.
This extension is possible since our construction does not require approximating derivatives with respect to the time variable.
We emphasize that by setting
m=1
in Theorem
3.2
, we recover the approximation result of the true score function similar to that derived in
(
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025b
, Theorem 3.3)
.
However, in our case, the dependence on
\sigma
becomes worse both in terms of the number of non-zero parameters and the approximation accuracy.
We argue that this dependence could be made identical to that of
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025b
by adapting their argument if one does not need to approximate derivatives of the score function.

4
Proof of Theorem
3.2

The proof of our main approximation theorem requires a new technique, as standard approaches are insufficient under Assumption
3.1
.

A natural starting point is the method of
(
Shen et al. 2023
, Theorem 8)
.
This approach combines the Whitney extension theorem
(
Fefferman 2006
)
with dimensionality reduction via linear projections
(
Baraniuk and Wakin 2009
)
, which is applicable in the case of a sufficiently regular, low-dimensional manifold.
However, this path encounters two fundamental obstacles in our setting.
First, the image of
g^{*}
is not guaranteed to be a well-conditioned manifold and, therefore, the result of
Baraniuk and Wakin 2009
is inapplicable.
Second, even under a manifold assumption, we will inevitably encounter issues with limited differentiability, since the embedding into a lower-dimensional space only approximately preserves distances.

Therefore, we develop a new constructive approach.
Our proof is inspired by the technique of
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025b
but requires significant extension to operate in Sobolev norms of arbitrary order.
A key and novel difficulty is ensuring good behavior of the approximation at infinity, which was not addressed by prior constructions
(
Oko et al. 2023
;
Tang and Yang 2024
;
Azangulov et al. 2024
;
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025b
)
.
This difficulty is resolved by leveraging GELU network approximations of elementary functions from
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025a
.

We now present the complete proof of Theorem
3.2
, decomposing it into several steps to enhance readability.

Step 1: local polynomial approximation.
Following
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025b
, we introduce the score function
s^{\circ}
induced by a local polynomial approximation of
g^{*}
.
Formally, for each
{\mathbf{j}}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{d})\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{d}
with
N=\lceil 1/\varepsilon\rceil
we denote

\displaystyle u_{\mathbf{j}}=\frac{{\mathbf{j}}}{N},\quad{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathbf{j}}=\left[\frac{j_{1}-1}{N},\frac{j_{1}}{N}\right]\times\left[\frac{j_{2}-1}{N},\frac{j_{2}}{N}\right]\times\dots\times\left[\frac{j_{d}-1}{N},\frac{j_{d}}{N}\right].

Then the local polynomial approximation is defined for all
[0,1]^{d}
as

\displaystyle g^{\circ}(u)=\sum_{{\mathbf{j}}\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{d}}g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u),\quad g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)=\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d},\;|{\mathbf{k}}|\leqslant{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}\frac{\partial^{\mathbf{k}}g^{*}(u_{\mathbf{j}})(u-u_{\mathbf{j}})^{\mathbf{k}}}{{\mathbf{k}}!}\mathbbm{1}(u\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathbf{j}}).

(5)

We denote

\displaystyle s^{\circ}(y)=-\frac{y}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{f^{\circ}(y)}{\sigma^{2}},\quad f^{\circ}(y)=\frac{\int\limits_{[0,1]^{d}}g^{\circ}(u)\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-g^{\circ}(u)\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u}{\int\limits_{[0,1]^{d}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-g^{\circ}(u)\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u}.

(6)

It is not straightforward how to generalize the result of
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025b
(Proposition G.1) to the case when we need to evaluate the score derivatives.
We therefore propose a simpler approach based on the following construction.
For a parameter
R>0
, which will be determined later, we define

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}:\min_{u\in[0,1]^{d}}\|y-g^{*}(u)\|\leqslant R\right\}.

(7)

The next result quantifies the closeness of
f^{*}
and
f^{\circ}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Grant Assumption
3.1
.
Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a compact set as defined in (
7
), and consider
f^{\circ}
as described in (
6
).
Let also
\|g^{*}-g^{\circ}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\leqslant 1\wedge\sigma^{2}/(R+3)
.
Then for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
it holds that

\displaystyle(i)

\displaystyle\quad\max_{1\leqslant l\leqslant D}\left(|f^{\circ}_{l}|_{W^{k,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}\vee|f^{*}_{l}|_{W^{k,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}\right)\leqslant 4^{k+1}k!\cdot\sigma^{-2k},

\displaystyle(ii)

\displaystyle\quad\max_{1\leqslant l\leqslant D}|f^{\circ}_{l}-f^{*}_{l}|_{W^{k,\infty}({\mathcal{K}})}\leqslant\sigma^{-2k}(1+(R+3)/\sigma^{2})e^{3k+3}k!\cdot\|g^{\circ}-g^{*}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}.

We move the proof of Lemma
4.1
to Appendix
A.1
.
Now we note from (
6
) that for any
{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}
and
1\leqslant l\leqslant D

\displaystyle\|D^{{\mathbf{k}}}s^{\circ}_{l}-D^{{\mathbf{k}}}s^{*}_{l}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}=\sigma^{-4}\|D^{{\mathbf{k}}}f^{\circ}_{l}-D^{{\mathbf{k}}}f^{*}_{l}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant\sigma^{-4}|f^{\circ}_{l}-f^{*}_{l}|^{2}_{W^{|{\mathbf{k}}|,\infty}({\mathcal{K}})}+2\left(|f^{\circ}_{l}|^{2}_{W^{|{\mathbf{k}}|,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}+|f^{*}_{l}|^{2}_{W^{|{\mathbf{k}}|,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}\right)\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{D}\setminus{\mathcal{K}}}\mathsf{p}^{*}(y){\mathrm{d}}y.

(8)

Assuming that

\displaystyle\|g^{\circ}-g^{*}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\leqslant 1\wedge\sigma^{2}/(R+3),

(9)

which will be verified later in the proof, we apply Lemma
4.1
and obtain that

\displaystyle\|D^{{\mathbf{k}}}s^{\circ}_{l}-D^{{\mathbf{k}}}s^{*}_{l}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant\sigma^{-4|{\mathbf{k}}|}(|{\mathbf{k}}|!)^{2}\left[\sigma^{-4}(1+(R+3)/\sigma^{2})^{2}e^{6|{\mathbf{k}}|+6}\|g^{\circ}-g^{*}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}^{2}+4^{2|{\mathbf{k}}|+3}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{D}\setminus{\mathcal{K}}}\mathsf{p}^{*}(y){\mathrm{d}}y\right].

(10)

In the next steps, we derive the final bound and optimize the value of
R
.
Specifically, the remainder of the proof involves building a GELU network that is close to
s^{\circ}
.

Step 2: reduction to approximation of
s^{\circ}
on a compact set.
We seek an approximation
s
to
s^{\circ}
of the form

\displaystyle s(y)=-\frac{y}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{f(y)}{\sigma^{2}},\quad f\in\mathsf{NN}(L,W,S,B).

We also let

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}=[-R_{\infty},R_{\infty}]^{D},\quad R_{\infty}=5/2\vee\sup_{y\in{\mathcal{K}}}\|y\|_{\infty},

(11)

where
{\mathcal{K}}
is defined in (
7
).
Thus, using similar argument to (
4
) and the observation that
{\mathcal{K}}\subseteq{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\|D^{{\mathbf{k}}}s_{l}-D^{{\mathbf{k}}}s^{\circ}_{l}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}=\sigma^{-4}\|D^{{\mathbf{k}}}f_{l}-D^{{\mathbf{k}}}f^{\circ}_{l}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant\sigma^{-4}|f_{l}-f^{\circ}_{l}|^{2}_{W^{|{\mathbf{k}}|,\infty}({\mathcal{K}}_{\infty})}+2\left(|f_{l}|^{2}_{W^{|{\mathbf{k}}|,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}+8^{|{\mathbf{k}}|+1}(|{\mathbf{k}}|!)^{2}\sigma^{-4|{\mathbf{k}}|}\right)\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{D}\setminus{\mathcal{K}}}\mathsf{p}^{*}(y){\mathrm{d}}y,

(12)

where the last inequality uses Lemma
4.1
.
Therefore, the goal is to build an approximation of
f^{\circ}
on the set
{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}
with
|f_{l}|_{W^{|{\mathbf{k}}|,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}
close to
|f^{\circ}_{l}|_{W^{|{\mathbf{k}}|,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}
.
We note that
Yakovlev and Puchkin 2025b
used a non-differentiable clipping operation to guarantee that the approximation is bounded at infinity.
We avoid this obstacle by using a more intricate technique that uses a differentiable approximation of clipping operation.

Step 3: composition of simpler functions.
The following steps are devoted to approximation of
f^{\circ}
on a compact
{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}
defined in (
11
).
We first recall from (
6
) that the expression for
f^{\circ}(y)
is given by

\displaystyle f^{\circ}(y)=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\frac{\int\limits_{[0,1]^{d}}g^{\circ}(u)\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-g^{\circ}(u)\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u}{\int\limits_{[0,1]^{d}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-g^{\circ}(u)\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u}.

In view of (
5
), we deduce that
f^{\circ}
takes the following form:

\displaystyle f^{\circ}(y)=\frac{\sum_{{\mathbf{j}}\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{d}}\int\limits_{{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathbf{j}}}g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u}{\sum_{{\mathbf{j}}\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{d}}\int\limits_{{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathbf{j}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u}.

(13)

Note that for arbitrary
{\mathbf{j}}\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{d}
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
and
u\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathbf{j}}
we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\|y-g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|y-g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u_{\mathbf{j}})\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}+\frac{\|g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)-g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u_{\mathbf{j}})\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}-\frac{(y-g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u_{\mathbf{j}}))^{\top}(g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)-g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u_{\mathbf{j}}))}{\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle=V_{{\mathbf{j}},0}(y)+\frac{\|g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)-g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u_{\mathbf{j}})\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}+\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d},\;1\leqslant|{\mathbf{k}}|\leqslant{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}V_{{\mathbf{j}},{\mathbf{k}}}(y)\frac{(u-u_{\mathbf{j}})^{\mathbf{k}}}{{\mathbf{k}}!},

(14)

where for each
{\mathbf{j}}\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{d}
we introduced the functions

\displaystyle V_{{\mathbf{j}},0}(y)=\frac{\|y-g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u_{\mathbf{j}})\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}},\quad V_{{\mathbf{j}},{\mathbf{k}}}(y)=-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}(y-g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u_{\mathbf{j}}))^{\top}\partial^{\mathbf{k}}g^{*}(u_{\mathbf{j}}),\quad{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d},\;1\leqslant|{\mathbf{k}}|\leqslant{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}.

(15)

This observation suggests that the integrals

\displaystyle\int\limits_{{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathbf{j}}}g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u,\quad\int\limits_{{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathbf{j}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u

(16)

are compositions of
V_{{\mathbf{j}},{\mathbf{k}}}
and
V_{{\mathbf{j}},0}
with a function taking

\displaystyle 1+|\{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d}:1\leqslant|{\mathbf{k}}|\leqslant{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}\}|=|\{{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d+1}:|{\mathbf{k}}|={\lfloor\beta\rfloor}\}|=\binom{d+{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}{d}

arguments as input, which may be significantly smaller than the ambient dimension
D
.
For notation simplicity, we denote
P(d,\beta)=\binom{d+{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}{d}
.

Step 4: approximation of low-dimensional arguments.
We note from (
15
) that
V_{{\mathbf{j}},{\mathbf{k}}}(y)
can be implemented exactly using a single linear layer.
The approximation result for
V_{{\mathbf{j}},0}(y)
is formulated in the following Lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
be a compact set given by (
11
) and the function
V_{{\mathbf{j}},0}
be as defined in (
15
).
Then for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}\in(0,1)
there exists a GELU network
\widetilde{\varphi}_{{\mathbf{j}},0}\in\mathsf{NN}(L,W,S,B)
such that

\displaystyle(i)

\displaystyle\quad\|\widetilde{\varphi}_{{\mathbf{j}},0}-\exp\circ(-V_{{\mathbf{j}},0})\|_{W^{m,\infty}({\mathcal{K}}_{\infty})}\leqslant\varepsilon^{\prime},

\displaystyle(ii)

\displaystyle\quad\|\widetilde{\varphi}_{{\mathbf{j}},0}\|_{W^{m,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}\leqslant\exp\{{\mathcal{O}}(m^{4}\log(mDR\sigma^{-2}\log(1/\varepsilon^{\prime})))\}.

Furthermore,
\widetilde{\varphi}_{{\mathbf{j}},0}
has the following configuration:

\displaystyle L\asymp\log(mDR\sigma^{-2})+\log\log(1/\varepsilon^{\prime}),\quad\|W\|_{\infty}\vee S\lesssim D^{2}m^{14}(\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon^{\prime})+\log^{2}(mDR\sigma^{-2})),

\displaystyle\log B\lesssim m^{23}(\log^{3}(1/\varepsilon^{\prime})+\log^{3}(mDR\sigma^{-2})),

where the number of layers is the same for all
{\mathbf{j}}\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{d}
.

The proof of Lemma
4.2
is deferred to Appendix
A.2
.

Step 5: approximation of integrals.
Introduce a mapping
\mathscr{R}_{\mathbf{j}}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{P(d,\beta)}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}_{\mathbf{j}}(y)=\left((V_{{\mathbf{j}},{\mathbf{k}}}(y)\;:\;{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d},\;1\leqslant|{\mathbf{k}}|\leqslant{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}),\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)^{\top}.

(17)

In addition, we define a mapping
a_{\mathbf{j}}:{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathbf{j}}\to\mathbb{R}^{P(d,\beta)}
componentwise by

\displaystyle a_{\mathbf{j}}(u)=\left(((u-u_{\mathbf{j}})^{\mathbf{k}}/{\mathbf{k}}!\;:\;{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d},\;1\leqslant|{\mathbf{k}}|\leqslant{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}),\|g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)-g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u_{\mathbf{j}})\|^{2}\right).

(18)

Therefore, from (
4
) we deduce that for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
and
u\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathbf{j}}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{\|y-g^{\circ}(u)\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}=V_{{\mathbf{j}},0}(y)+\mathscr{R}_{\mathbf{j}}(y)^{\top}a_{\mathbf{j}}(u).

Hence, the integrals from (
16
) simplify to

\begin{split}\int\limits_{{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathbf{j}}}g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u&=\exp(-V_{{\mathbf{j}},0}(y))\Psi_{\mathbf{j}}[g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}](y),\\
\int\limits_{{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathbf{j}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\|y-g^{\circ}_{\mathbf{j}}(u)\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u&=\exp(-V_{{\mathbf{j}},0}(y))\Psi_{\mathbf{j}}[1](y),\end{split}

(19)

where for a function
\psi
that can yield vector or scalar outputs, we define

\displaystyle\Psi_{\mathbf{j}}[\psi](y)=\int\limits_{{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathbf{j}}}\psi(u)\exp\left\{-\mathscr{R}_{\mathbf{j}}(y)^{\top}a_{\mathbf{j}}(u)\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u,\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}.

(20)

For the notation simplicity, we denote
\Psi_{\mathbf{j}}[1]
to emphasize that
\psi
represents the constant function equal to one.
The following results offers approximation bounds for
\Psi_{\mathbf{j}}[\psi]
, where
\psi
is a bounded scalar function.
To complete the current step, it remains to approximate the integrals outlined in (
19
), which is addressed in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let the assumptions of Lemma
4.2
and let

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{\mathbf{j}}[\psi](y)=\exp(-V_{{\mathbf{j}},0}(y))\cdot\Psi_{\mathbf{j}}[\psi](y),\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{D},

where
\psi:{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathbf{j}}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\|\psi\|_{W^{0,\infty}({\mathcal{U}}_{\mathbf{j}})}\leqslant 2
.
Let also
\varepsilon
be sufficiently small so that

\displaystyle\|g^{\circ}-g^{*}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\leqslant 1,\quad\frac{5(H\vee 1)^{2}P(d,\beta)^{2}\sqrt{D}(R+3)\varepsilon}{2\sigma^{2}}\leqslant 1.

Then, for any
\varepsilon^{\prime}\in(0,1)
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a GELU network
\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{\mathbf{j}}\in\mathsf{NN}(L_{\Upsilon},W_{\Upsilon},S_{\Upsilon},B_{\Upsilon})
such that

\displaystyle(i)

\displaystyle\quad\|\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{\mathbf{j}}-\Upsilon_{\mathbf{j}}\|_{W^{m,\infty}({\mathcal{K}}_{\infty})}\leqslant\varepsilon^{d}\varepsilon^{\prime},

\displaystyle(ii)

\displaystyle\quad\|\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{\mathbf{j}}\|_{W^{m,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}\leqslant\exp\{{\mathcal{O}}(m^{5}\log(mD\sigma^{-2}\log(1/\varepsilon^{\prime})))\}\varepsilon^{d}.

Furthermore, the configuration of
\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{\mathbf{j}}
is as follows:

\displaystyle L_{\Upsilon}\asymp\log(mDR\sigma^{-2})+\log\log(1/\varepsilon^{\prime}),\quad\log B_{\Upsilon}\lesssim m^{35}\log^{3}(1/\varepsilon^{\prime})\log^{7}(mDR\sigma^{-2}),

\displaystyle\|W_{\Upsilon}\|_{\infty}\vee S_{\Upsilon}\lesssim D^{2}m^{21+7P(d,\beta)}(\log(1/\varepsilon^{\prime})+\log(mDR\sigma^{-2}))^{3+P(d,\beta)}.

where the number of layers does not depend on
{\mathbf{j}}\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{d}
and the function
\psi
.

The proof of Lemma
4.3
is deferred to Appendix
A.3
.
Since
\sup_{u\in[0,1]^{d}}\|g_{\mathbf{j}}(u)\|\leqslant 2
, then Lemma
4.3
implies that there exist GELU neural networks
\{P_{{\mathbf{j}},l}\}_{l=1}^{D}
and the network
Q_{\mathbf{j}}
that belong to the class
\mathsf{NN}(L_{\Upsilon},W_{\Upsilon},S_{\Upsilon},B_{\Upsilon})
satisfying

\displaystyle\|Q_{\mathbf{j}}-\Upsilon_{\mathbf{j}}[1]\|_{W^{m,\infty}({\mathcal{K}}_{\infty})}\vee\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant D}\|P_{{\mathbf{j}},l}-\Upsilon_{\mathbf{j}}[g^{\circ}_{{\mathbf{j}},l}]\|_{W^{m,\infty}({\mathcal{K}}_{\infty})}\leqslant\varepsilon^{d}\varepsilon^{\prime}.

Therefore, by Lemma
B.13
, the parallel stacking across
{\mathbf{j}}\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{d}
implies the existence of GELU networks
\{{\mathcal{P}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{D}
and
{\mathcal{Q}}
, such that

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathcal{Q}}-\sum_{{\mathbf{j}}\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{d}}\Upsilon_{\mathbf{j}}[1]\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}({\mathcal{K}}_{\infty})}\vee\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant D}\left\|{\mathcal{P}}_{l}-\sum_{{\mathbf{j}}\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{d}}\Upsilon_{\mathbf{j}}[g^{\circ}_{{\mathbf{j}},l}]\right\|_{W^{m,\infty}({\mathcal{K}}_{\infty})}\leqslant\varepsilon^{\prime}.

(21)

Furthermore, Lemmata
4.3
and
B.13
also suggest that

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{Q}}\|_{W^{m,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}\vee\max_{1\leqslant l\leqslant D}\|{\mathcal{P}}_{l}\|_{W^{m,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}\leqslant\exp\{{\mathcal{O}}(m^{5}\log(mDR\sigma^{-2}\log(1/\varepsilon^{\prime})))\}.

(22)

Finally, we conclude from Lemma
B.13
that
{\mathcal{Q}},{\mathcal{P}}_{1},\dots,{\mathcal{P}}_{D}\in\mathsf{NN}(\breve{L},\breve{W},\breve{S},\breve{B})
with

\displaystyle\breve{L}\asymp\log(mDR\sigma^{-2})+\log\log(1/\varepsilon^{\prime}),\quad\log\breve{B}\lesssim m^{35}\log^{3}(1/\varepsilon^{\prime})\log^{7}(mDR\sigma^{-2}),

\displaystyle\|\breve{W}\|_{\infty}\vee\breve{S}\lesssim\varepsilon^{-d}D^{2}m^{21+7P(d,\beta)}(\log(1/\varepsilon^{\prime})+\log(mDR\sigma^{-2}))^{3+P(d,\beta)}.

(23)

Step 5: deriving the final approximation.
The current step aims to perform division of
{\mathcal{P}}_{l}
and
{\mathcal{Q}}
for each
1\leqslant l\leqslant D
, as stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let the compact set
{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
be defined as in (
11
), and
f^{\circ}
be as given in (
13
).
Let also the GELU networks
{\mathcal{Q}}
and
\{{\mathcal{P}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{D}
be as defined above in (
21
).
Then, choosing

\displaystyle\log(1/\varepsilon^{\prime})\asymp m^{6}(R^{2}+1)\sigma^{-2}\log(mDR\sigma^{-2})\log(1/\varepsilon)

guarantees that there exists a GELU network
\bar{f}\in\mathsf{NN}(\bar{L},\bar{W},\bar{S},\bar{B})
such that
\bar{f}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
and

\displaystyle(i)

\displaystyle\quad\max_{1\leqslant l\leqslant D}\|\bar{f}_{l}-f^{\circ}_{l}\|_{W^{m,\infty}({\mathcal{K}}_{\infty})}\leqslant\varepsilon^{\beta},

\displaystyle(ii)

\displaystyle\quad\max_{1\leqslant l\leqslant D}|\bar{f}_{l}|_{W^{k,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}\leqslant\sigma^{-2k}\exp\{{\mathcal{O}}(k^{2}\log(mD\log(1/\varepsilon)\log(R\sigma^{-2})))\},\quad\text{for all }0\leqslant k\leqslant m.

Furthermore,
\bar{f}
has

\displaystyle\bar{L}\lesssim\log(mDR\sigma^{-2}\log(1/\varepsilon)),\quad\log\bar{B}\lesssim m^{53}R^{16}\sigma^{-16}\log^{10}(mDR\sigma^{-2})\log^{4}(1/\varepsilon),

\displaystyle\|\bar{W}\|_{\infty}\vee\bar{S}\lesssim\varepsilon^{-d}D^{4}m^{84+13P(d,\beta)}(R\sigma^{-2})^{24+2P(d,\beta)}(\log(1/\varepsilon)\log(mDR\sigma^{-2}))^{7+P(d,\beta)}.

We move the proof of Lemma
4.4
to Appendix
A.4
.
The next step is to derive the approximation accuracy of the final approximation given by
\bar{s}(y)=-\sigma^{-2}y+\sigma^{-2}\bar{f}(y)
, where
\bar{f}
is the neural network from Lemma
4.4
.
For any
{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}
with
|{\mathbf{k}}|\leqslant m
and
1\leqslant l\leqslant D
we obtain from (
4
), (
4
), Lemma
C.1
and the triangle inequality that

\displaystyle\|\partial^{\mathbf{k}}[\bar{s}_{l}-s^{*}_{l}]\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\sigma^{-4|{\mathbf{k}}|}(|{\mathbf{k}}|!)^{2}\sigma^{-4}D\varepsilon^{2\beta}(1+(R+3)\sigma^{-2})^{2}e^{6|{\mathbf{k}}|}+(\|\bar{f}\|^{2}_{W^{m,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}+8^{m}(m!)^{2}\sigma^{-4m})\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{D}\setminus{\mathcal{K}}}\mathsf{p}^{*}(y){\mathrm{d}}y.

Applying Lemma
4.4
we arrive at

\displaystyle\|\partial^{\mathbf{k}}[\bar{s}_{l}-s^{*}_{l}]\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}

(24)

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\sigma^{-4|{\mathbf{k}}|-8}\exp\{{\mathcal{O}}(|{\mathbf{k}}|\log|{\mathbf{k}}|)\}(1+R^{2})D\varepsilon^{2\beta}+\exp\{{\mathcal{O}}(m^{2}\log(mDR\sigma^{-2}\log(1/\varepsilon)))\}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{D}\setminus{\mathcal{K}}}\mathsf{p}^{*}(y){\mathrm{d}}y.

The next Lemma provides the tail probability upper bound for the data distribution.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be the compact set as defined in (
7
) with
R\geqslant\sigma\sqrt{D}
.
Then it holds that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{D}\setminus{\mathcal{K}}}\mathsf{p}^{*}(y){\mathrm{d}}y\leqslant\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{16}\left(-\frac{R^{2}-D\sigma^{2}}{D\sigma^{2}}\wedge\frac{\sqrt{R^{2}-D\sigma^{2}}}{\sigma}\right)\right\}.

The full proof of Lemma
4.5
is provided in Appendix
A.5
.
Hence, using Lemma
4.5
we deduce that taking

\displaystyle R=\sigma\sqrt{D}+16\sigma\left(\sqrt{D\log(\varepsilon_{0}^{-2\beta}/D)}\vee\log(\varepsilon_{0}^{-2\beta}/D)\right)

(25)

ensures that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{D}\setminus{\mathcal{K}}}\mathsf{p}^{*}(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y\leqslant\varepsilon_{0}^{2\beta},

where
\varepsilon_{0}
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\log(1/\varepsilon_{0})\asymp m^{2}\log(1/\varepsilon)+m^{2}\log(mD\sigma^{-2}).

(26)

Therefore, from (
24
) and the above definitions given in (
25
) and (
26
) we deduce that

\displaystyle\|\partial^{\mathbf{k}}[\bar{s}_{l}-s^{*}_{l}]\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sigma^{-4|{\mathbf{k}}|-8}\exp\{{\mathcal{O}}(|{\mathbf{k}}|\log|{\mathbf{k}}|)\}D^{2}\varepsilon^{2\beta}\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon)\log^{2}(mD\sigma^{-2})

\displaystyle\quad+\exp\{{\mathcal{O}}(m^{2}\log(mD\sigma^{-2}\log(1/\varepsilon)))\}\varepsilon_{0}^{2\beta}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sigma^{-4|{\mathbf{k}}|-8}\exp\{{\mathcal{O}}(|{\mathbf{k}}|\log|{\mathbf{k}}|)\}D^{2}\varepsilon^{2\beta}\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon)\log^{2}(mD\sigma^{-2}).

Hence, claim
(i)
holds true.
From (
25
) and (
26
) we deduce that

\displaystyle R\lesssim\sqrt{D}m^{2}(\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log(mD\sigma^{-2})),

which together with Lemmata
4.4
implies that
\bar{f}
belongs to the class
\mathsf{NN}(L,W,S,B)
with

Proof of Lemma

The proof follows the identical argument as provided in Lemma A.2 of

\displaystyle\|W\|_{\infty}\vee S\lesssim D^{2}(m^{3}\log(1/\varepsilon^{\prime})+m^{3}\log(mDR\sigma^{-2}))^{3+P(d,\beta)},

thereby completing the proof.

\square
