The Experience of “Feeling Cold” in Patients Undergoing In‐Centre Haemodialysis
Authors: Vivi de Fries Bjerrum, Johanne Weiers, Signe Heine Sørensen, Helle Meldgaard Kjær, Ingrid Villadsen Kristensen, Christina Egmose Frandsen, Jeanette Finderup
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe symptom of “feeling cold” in patients with kidney failure is underexplored in the literature and absent from standard symptom assessment tools. Virginia Henderson highlighted temperature regulation as a fundamental need, emphasising the importance of addressing this symptom in renal care. It has been observed that patients in in‐centre haemodialysis are relying on the usage of heating pads and blankets all year around.ObjectiveTo examine how patients undergoing haemodialysis experience the symptom of “feeling cold”.DesignA qualitative study with a phenomenological approach.ParticipantsTwenty‐six adult patients with kidney failure receiving in‐centre haemodialysis participated.ApproachIndividual semi‐structured interviews were conducted, with an interview guide based on Van Manen's four lifeworld existentials. Data were analysed using Malterud's systematic text condensation.FindingsParticipants reported diverse experiences of “feeling cold,” often describing it negatively. This symptom significantly affected their psychological, physical, and social well‐being. Some participants also noted experiences of “feeling hot,” indicating a complex interplay of temperature sensitivity.Conclusions“Feeling cold” emerged as a meaningful and distressing experience among patients with kidney failure. These findings enhance the understanding of patients' lived experiences and highlight the need for further investigation into the prevalence and severity of this phenomenon, including among patients with kidney failure not on dialysis. Addressing temperature sensitivity might improve symptom assessment and overall patient care in renal healthcare settings.Keywords: chronic kidney disease, haemodialysis, patient experience, qualitative research, symptoms

Introduction

Patients with kidney failure experience a significant symptom burden that increases alongside the development of chronic kidney disease (CKD), in addition, it can be challenging to manage and bring relief to the symptoms (Sharma et al. 2020). Some of the most common uraemic symptoms amongst patients with kidney failure are fatigue or lack of energy, feeling drowsy, pain and pruritus (Almutary et al. 2013). Symptoms are self‐reported subjective experiences that may cause suffering and prompt patients to seek effective management (Kalantar‐Zadeh et al. 2022, p. 186).

In clinical practice, the phenomenon of “feeling cold” has been observed among patients under‐going haemodialysis treatment. Patients often rely on heating pads and blankets and some wear winter jackets all year around. Many have described the sensation of feeling cold as a significant source of discomfort and frustration. The experience has also led to conflict with spouses, as differing temperature preferences can create an uncomfortable living environment. As nurses we observed this discomfort, but due to lack of evidence, we were unable to validate these experiences which may leave the patients being unsupported.

Literature Review

Existing literature addressing the symptom of feeling cold among patients with kidney failure is sparse. “Feeling cold” or “temperature sensitivity” is mentioned as a part of a list of symptoms or as part of a symptom cluster, but not individually explored (Heidenheim et al. 2003; Nixon et al. 2020; Paude 2014; Yong et al. 2009). Only one study quoting a patient saying “I'm always frozen, always… you just ask the family. It'll be burning hot outside in summer, but I have to have the heating on. I drive ‘em crazy with it.” (Pugh‐Clarke et al. 2017, p. 205). But the study has no further in‐depth investigation of how the phenomenon unfolds.

Likewise, “feeling cold” is not mentioned in assessment tools commonly used for this patient group, such as The dialysis Symptom Index, IPOS‐RENAL, Kidney Disease and Quality of Life (KDQOL‐SF) and Chronic Kidney Disease Symptom Burden Index (CKD‐SBI) (Almutary et al. 2016; Cicely Saunders Institute 2012; RAND, & University of Arizona 1995; Weisbord et al. 2004). A recent PhD thesis included “feeling cold” in developing a new assessment tool, but it is unknown how widespread the implication of this tool is yet. The thesis points towards, that up to 75% of patients with kidney failure may experience the symptom of feeling cold (Pugh‐Clarke 2025). Yet the phenomenon of feeling cold still highlights an unexplored area in the research.

Studies show that understanding and encouraging patients to ask questions builds resilience and enables patients to live well with kidney failure. Engaging patients in their kidney care and addressing symptoms helps improve their wellness. Living well with kidney disease and the symptoms that come along with it is the goal of all kidney foundations, patient groups, and professional societies (Kalantar‐Zadeh et al. 2021).

To be able to maintain one's body temperature is a basic need and has been identified in general nursing theory (Henderson 1991). The experience of “feeling cold” can be experienced by everyone, and is something everyone aims to prevent. When an individual is unable to maintain their body temperature, something has to be done. Therefore, it is essential to offer high‐quality fundamental care when this need arises. Understanding how the individual experiences their uraemic symptoms, which symptoms are experienced and how to bring relief to them is key (Kalantar‐Zadeh et al. 2022). For healthcare professionals to be able to provide care for this symptom, knowledge of the patient experience needs to be explored. Consequently, an overlooked dimension of this phenomenon comes into focus, underscoring the symptom of “feeling cold” as a symptom to cover in kidney care.

To our knowledge, no other studies have described the experience of “feeling cold” amongst patients undergoing in‐centre haemodialysis. The aim of the study is to explore how patients undergoing in‐centre haemodialysis experience the phenomenon of “feeling cold”.

Materials and Methods

The study was conducted with a qualitative design using a phenomenological approach (Rietmeijer and Veen 2022) based on Van Manen's four life worlds (Beck 2021). This approach was chosen to explore the phenomenon of “feeling cold” although not knowing if the phenomenon exists at all. Data were collected as semi‐structured interviews with patients receiving in‐centre haemodialysis treatment. This study has been reported according to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) (Tong et al. 2007).

Participants

The study recruited patients from a haemodialysis clinic, which is a satellite unit accommodating 34 patients and staffed by 21 nurses. Patients receive maintenance haemodialysis 3–4 times a week. A nephrologist is available twice a week, one time face‐to‐face and one time by phone. The interviews were conducted in November 2024, when the temperature was around 10°C outside. Participants were recruited through a nurse from the dialysis unit, who asked all Danish‐speaking patients if they would like to participate. The inclusion criteria were patients with kidney failure on maintenance haemodialysis and had provided informed consent. The exclusion criteria were patients who were too ill to participate in the interview and patients not able to speak Danish. Broad inclusion criteria were applied intentionally to capture diverse experiences of the phenomenon across age, dialysis vintage, and illness duration, consistent with phenomenological enquiry seeking variation in lived experience (Norlyk and Harder 2010).

Data Collection

Data were collected through semi‐structured interviews inspired by Kvale and Brinkmann (2014) and based on an interview guide. The interview guide was divided into two parts. The first part, collecting demographic data such as age, sex, duration of CKD and duration of haemodialysis. The second part, collecting data about the life world experiences of the participants around the phenomenon of “feeling cold”. This second part was inspired by Van Manen's four life worlds (Beck 2021); the lived body, the lived time, the lived space and the lived human relation. These four life worlds were translated into four specific research questions, which further were translated into interview questions supported by follow‐up questions. The interview guide translated into English is to be found as Supporting Information: Material (S1). A pilot interview was conducted with a person without a healthcare background to ensure the interview questions were clear and understandable. The interviews were conducted by the first three authors, when the participants received haemodialysis. Interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim by the interviewers, who had no prior experience conducting interviews but were closely supervised by the fourth and last authors.

Rather than applying the concept of data saturation, information power guided considerations of data sufficiency, whereby the specificity of the study aim, sample specificity, established theoretical framework, and richness of dialogue indicated that the dataset was sufficiently informative to support the analysis (Malterud et al. 2016).

Data Analysis

Data were analysed inductively using systematic text condensation (STC) as outlined by Malterud (2012). First, the transcribed interviews were carefully read to form an overall impression and preliminary themes emerged. Second, meaningful units were identified and categorised into coding groups, which were derived from the preliminary themes. Third, subgroups were formed, and the meaningful units were condensed in present and first person. Finally, in Step four, the final themes were established, and the data was reconceptualized using past tense and third person. NVIVO 15 was used to manage the data and distribute all meaningful units into the coding groups. This process was carried out by the first three authors with guidance by the fourth and last authors. Sub‐groups and final themes were discussed with the fourth and last authors to ensure they aligned with the conducted empirical data. The steps of the STC analysis process are illustrated in Figure 2.

Two new coding groups emerged during the process. The seven coding groups are then consolidated into four larger groups. In Step 3, sub‐groups are formed, all meaningful units are sorted, and a condensation is written based on these. Finally, Step 4 presents the final themes, which are based on the findings. The dotted line indicates that all meaningful units related to participants who experience “feeling hot” have been extracted and subsequently included in Theme 5.

Ethical Considerations

This study adheres to the ethical principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration (World Medical Association 2024). All participants provided informed consent, and were assured of their anonymity and that they could withdraw from the study at any time without facing any consequences. The study poses no risk or adverse events to the participants. The Danish Data Protection Agency approved data management for all the participating patients (approval: 1‐16‐02‐88‐25). All data were managed in accordance with the General Data Protection Regulation (The European Parliament 2016).

Findings

A total of 26 patients agreed to participate and were interviewed. One patient was not approached due to serious illness. The recruitment process is illustrated in Figure 1. The interviews lasted between 3.5 and 24 min and data consisted in total of 140 pages.

Recruitment process of participants.

The participants were between 30 and 86 years old. Of these, 19 were men and 7 were women. The average duration of being diagnosed with CKD was 10.98 years, ranging from 1 year to 49 years of CKD. While the average duration of being on haemodialysis was 3 years, this is ranging from 3 weeks up to 19 years.

Five main themes were identified through STC (Figure 2). Theme 1: “Feeling cold” changed after having kidney failure. Theme 2: “Feeling cold” is experienced as negative and unpredictable. Theme 3: “Feeling cold” affects the patient psychologically, physically and socially. Theme 4: Patients learning to live with the experience of “feeling cold”. Theme 5: Secondary finding: Co‐occurring experiences of “feeling hot”

“Feeling Cold” Changed After Having Kidney Failure

Participants experienced feeling cold after being diagnosed with kidney failure, a change they had not experienced prior to their diagnosis. This was, for example, expressed through cold hands and feet. Consequently, they had to wear more layers of clothing due to increased sensitivity to the cold. One of the participants had clearly felt the difference between being transplanted or not. Prior to the transplantation, he experienced feeling cold, but when he was transplanted, he experienced feeling hot.

I were not used to be very sensitive to the cold before I got my kidney failure. I've only genuinely felt cold maybe five times in my life; I think I can count it on both hands. After I had kidney failure, it's become more common for me to feel cold. So, I definitely notice that I've become more sensitive to the cold because my kidney failure, that's for sure.

(Participant 12)

Across participant accounts, a recurring pattern was that individuals described feeling colder after the onset of kidney failure.

“Feeling Cold” Is Experienced as Negative and Unpredictable

The participants experienced “feeling cold”, which had a unique expression for each individual. For some, it was an external sensation, with a participant experiencing the cold as if not wearing clothes, even though they were fully dressed.

(…) it actually feels like I'm not wearing any pants, like you know, the chilly feeling I get. Like, you get chilly when you sit around in your underwear, but I'm wearing pants and I still get the same cold feeling over my thighs.

(Participant 3)

For others, the cold was perceived as an internal feeling, described as freezing all the way to the soul or as if cold water was running through their veins.

Well, just in general, in the evening, and if you have to go out, then (puts arms over shoulders and shivers) (…) it's right inside, like in the soul.

(Participant 26)

Participants reported that feeling cold could be localised to specific body parts, such as one hand or foot, while the rest of the body remained unaffected. This unpredictability added to the distress, as some felt cold during dialysis treatments while others experienced the opposite. One participant described the debilitating effects: his fingers could become completely numb from the cold, and another mentioned chattering teeth. Many struggled to warm up again, which compounded the negative experience, even though most could do interventions to alleviate the cold.

Overall, participants kept their homes at a temperature of 24°C–25°C. Some needed to turn on the heating during the summer because they felt cold. Wearing just a t‐shirt in the summer felt too cold for them. The autumn and winter months were particularly challenging, as many participants reported heightened issues with feeling cold. Even while wearing sweaters next to a heated radiator, the struggle against the cold remained, often described as a gradual and progressive worsening of the symptom.

I'm cold all the time, yes. It's like I'm missing the generator that's supposed to produce some warmth.

(Participant 24)

Although several participants experienced the symptom of “feeling cold”, the experience, severity, and duration were individual and varied from person to person. Many participants reported feeling cold most of the time, regardless of whether they were on dialysis. The extent to which they felt cold varied throughout the year. The unpredictability of when they felt cold added to the burden, with some experiencing it primarily in autumn and winter, while others experienced it year‐round.

No, it's more like, I mean, I think it's more when it becomes autumn I feel cold. I don't feel it in the summer. Well, maybe I do sometimes anyway.

(Participant 22)

The timing of the cold sensation was also erratic; some felt it gradually from morning to afternoon, while others primarily experienced it in the evening. The sensation of cold could last anywhere from 10 min to 2 h, and some felt cold continuously. If participants experienced feeling cold through and through, they had difficulty warming up again. Some felt cold when they first initiated dialysis treatment but do not experience it in the same way any longer.

Participants often experienced temperature sensitivity in relation to haemodialysis. They felt cold while undergoing haemodialysis, but the way they experienced the cold during treatment was different from how they felt when they were at home.

No, but I can also feel cold at other times. But not in the same way, I don't think (…) it's like the cold comes from within when I'm on [haemo]dialysis.

(Participant 17)

Haemodialysis could affect the symptom, with the symptom in some cases being concentrated solely during the treatment. For some patients, “feeling cold” became a way of life, as it was connected to a treatment they could not live without. The symptom had a physical expression that varied over time. The experience of the symptom was unpredictable; it was difficult to say when or how it would occur. Participants experienced the symptom differently. However, there was a common pattern that participants described the symptom in a negative tone, often using words such as ‘annoying’ and ‘unbearable’ to describe it.

“Feeling Cold” Affects the Patient Psychologically, Physically and Socially

Several participants found it unbearable to be in situations where they felt cold. The coldness in their fingers bothered them and was uncomfortable. For many, feeling cold became a burden. Some participants found it difficult to ignore symptoms like teeth chattering and body shaking from the cold. These sensations took up significant mental space and affected their psyche. It was hard for them to focus on anything other than the experience of being cold. No matter how many layers they wore, they still couldn't warm up. This left them wondering why they were experiencing it. Especially during haemodialysis, it was difficult for them to think about anything other than the cold.

I think it's something that takes up a lot of space when you're cold. In your mind.

(Participant 12)

This quotation reveals an acknowledgement of the psychological impact of feeling cold. It implies that the sensation of coldness is not limited to the physical body but also occupies significant mental space, possibly overwhelming one's thoughts or focus. The experience of feeling cold seems to permeate the mind, becoming a dominant presence in one's consciousness. A psychological aspect emerged, with participants experiencing mental effects related to feeling cold. For several, it created frustration and confusion, as they struggled to understand why they had to endure this symptom.

Concerning their experience of feeling cold, some participants talked to their informal caregiver about it, while others kept it to themselves and dealt with the issue alone. In some cases, participants no longer received a response from their spouse when they mentioned feeling cold. However, their informal caregiver often noticed when they were cold, and for some, their spouse was considerate and showed understanding. The temperature in the home became a topic of discussion for several participants. For example, a participant preferred the home to be a few degrees warmer than his spouse did, as he felt cold more often. Participants also noticed differences in how they dressed at home compared to their informal caregiver. In some cases, these differences led to small disagreements, but they tried to compromise and adapt to each other. These differences did not, however, create major conflicts that affected their relationship. When participants received guests in their home, the temperature setting was sometimes considered too high. Likewise they had noticed that others turned the heat up when they came to visit them.

No, it's also other places (…) But, I mean, the places we go, it's cold there too. But, actually… it's the same places I go to a lot. So, I think they give it a little thought (…) And I can see that too. When we get there, the fireplace is on. And I don't think they usually do that otherwise. People do think a bit about the fact that I'm coming.

(Participant 22)

Not all participants mentioned to their informal caregiver that they had challenges with feeling cold. In most cases, however, it turned out that their informal caregiver took their needs into consideration if they mentioned it. Some participants felt their experience might be a burden to others, so it was easier not to bring it up. On the other hand, some participants experienced understanding from their informal caregiver regarding their situation, which they greatly appreciated.

Furthermore, feeling cold prevented some participants from doing activities they used to do. A participant could no longer wash work machines because his hand could not tolerate the cold water as it could before he developed kidney failure. Another participant could no longer swim as usual because the water felt too cold.

I just feel cold there. I think it's a shame because I would really like to go swimming, but, well, yeah. It's not that fun when you're just feeling cold.

(Participant 11)

The quotation highlights how physical discomfort, in this case feeling cold, can overshadow enjoying participation in activities they would otherwise find pleasurable. Many participants experienced being held back in their activities when they felt cold. They often felt like they did not want to do anything and preferred to stay home with the heat on.

I can't really do that, but I didn't want to do anything (because of feeling cold), I think I can say it like that. I just wanted to stay at home with the heat turned on (…) it affects my activities.

(Participant 16)

The participants felt that the symptom impacted several aspects of their daily life and prevented them from engaging in specific activities. For some, the symptom was seen as a burden, while others found it difficult to talk about with their informal caregiver but appreciated being acknowledged for their experiences.

Patients Learning to Live With the Experience of “Feeling Cold”

When participants felt cold, they used various methods to alleviate the discomfort, and what worked varied from person to person. Some found relief by putting on an extra sweater, wearing gloves, a hat, or a scarf outdoors, or by using thermal underwear or socks at night. Others mentioned that sitting with a heated blanket or pad, drinking hot beverages, turning on the stove, or taking a warm bath could also help.

Yeah, well, I have a heated blanket, so I can try that. But, you know, sometimes, if it's really deep inside, a warm bath is just the best.

(Participant 13)

The participants tried to distract themselves from the cold. This could be through activities like watching television, wrapping up in a blanket, or talking to a informal caregiver. However, they expressed that it was difficult to be distracted, when they were feeling too cold.

Well, it's hard to ignore it when your teeth are constantly chattering.

(Participant 12)

Some participants, however, had gotten used to being cold and had reached a point where they accepted the temporary discomfort and simply waited for it to subside. Others had come to terms with the symptom, no longer experiencing it as a burden. They believed that complaining about being cold didn't help and only prevented them from engaging with those around them. Some addressed the problem when it arose and had found solutions that worked for them.

But it's not like I'm sitting here complaining all the time. It's just become a habit, you know? That's just how it is.

(Participant 23)

How participants coped with and lived with the symptom was highly individual. It could involve physical actions to alleviate the discomfort in the moment, distracting themselves from what they found uncomfortable in the long term, or eventually fully accepting the symptom as a part of life. A pattern emerged where participants tried to manage the symptom independently rather than seeking help. Several participants had reached a point of acceptance, integrating the symptom into their lives. Through acceptance or other coping strategies, they had learned to live with it.

Secondary Finding: Co‐Occurring Experiences of “Feeling Hot”

While interviewing the participants about their experience of “feeling cold”, the symptom of “feeling hot” came to light. “Feeling hot” was a symptom that likewise had a negative impact on the participants. Some participants experienced both symptoms, while others either experienced feeling cold or feeling hot. Most often, the participants got hot flashes right after HD treatment. It was experienced as an internal heat that was difficult to get rid of and cool down from. They described that they sweated profusely, and it felt like they had been running a marathon. Other participants expressed that the experience of being drenched in their own sweat was frustrating and intense.

(…) Then I start to sweat profusely. I've never run a marathon, but I could imagine that it would feel something like that. You are drenched in sweat, it's literally running down my body.

(Participant 8)

Hot flashes often occurred at night after HD treatment. It was necessary for some to change their bedding and pyjamas multiple times. The participants experienced that the intensity of the hot flashes varied, and sometimes they did not occur after the end of treatment. It was difficult for the participants to predict when and to what degree they would feel hot. To them, it felt like the body needed to readjust to the right temperature after HD. For some, the symptoms improved after some time of having received treatment. After the hot flashes, a participant felt ill and had to go to bed, while another became too tired to drive his car and found his ability to concentrate impaired. Some participants dressed in a particular way to ensure their bodies could release the heat. The participants who experienced “feeling hot” found it necessary to be able to undress, open windows, or take cold showers. Often, the symptom was described as difficult to go through.

But when I have been through dialysis, then it also happens that I experience these “amazing” situations, where I sweat like crazy. So very much. I can easily change clothes, also underwear and so on and so on, three times in a night.

(Participant 15)

One participant, who suffered from hot flashes, wanted the house kept cooler than his girlfriend preferred. In winter, a participant experienced a delayed sensation of coldness compared to others. Hot flashes were experienced as worse in the summer but could also occur suddenly.

The symptom of “feeling hot” was described by the participants as a symptom that affected the same parameters as those who experienced “feeling cold”. Both affected quality of life and everyday life, and the symptom experience was comparable to “feeling cold”. Hot flashes emerged as a significant factor affecting sleep quality at night. Participants woke up multiple times during the night, resulting in poor sleep quality and, thereby, a reduced quality of life. “Feeling hot” likewise manifested differently from occurrence to occurrence and from person to person.

Discussion

The findings of this study reflects the multidimensional impact of the symptom of “feeling cold” among patients undergoing haemodialysis. This discussion explores the implications of these findings. The study's insight into the experiences of the symptom burden associated with CKD highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding. Especially those symptoms that are underrepresented in existing literature and clinical assessment tools, including the symptom of “feeling cold”.

The informants each have different ways of describing their experiences, yet they describe a common phenomenon of “feeling cold”. It is essential to understand how each person experiences the sensation of feeling cold in their own body. The relationship‐building aspect in the innermost dimension of Fundamentals of Care (FoC) encourages a curious approach to engaging with the individual to uncover how they personally experience the phenomenon (Kitson et al. 2013). If patients do not consider a permanent solution to their symptom as available, they may choose to remain silent rather than speak about it. The internal experience of feeling cold is difficult for others to observe, making the symptom invisible to everyone except the person experiencing it. Recognising what is not immediately apparent requires particular attentiveness and emphasises the need for dialogue and awareness to explore what is at stake for the individual and to detect changes that manifest only internally. Participants described “feeling cold” as a significant and distressing experience and which affects the patients in a long range of dimensions. The unpredictability and variability of the symptom suggests a complex phenomenon that demands further exploration.

The findings show that the informants primarily use negatively charged words to describe their symptom experience. According to Theory Of Unpleasant Symptoms (TOUS), this reflects how the informants consider the “quality” aspect of the symptom (Lenz et al. 1997), which is expressed through their choice of words. This, in turn, illustrates the degree to which the symptom is experienced.

For the informants, it can be challenging to categorise the symptom, as there is significant individual variation in how it manifests. TOUS describes four aspects of symptom experience, with “duration” and “intensity” being relevant here (Lenz et al. 1997). The perception of symptom intensity is closely linked to when patients feel cold and the severity of their experience. The informants do not know when, for how long, or to what degree they will experience the symptom. This uncertainty about how the symptom manifests can have a negative feedback effect on the symptom itself, as all aspects of TOUS interact with each other (Lenz et al. 1997).

As pointed out a psychological aspect emerges which can further influence their experience of feeling cold. TOUS refers to “distress” as the extent to which a patient is bothered by the symptom (Lenz et al. 1997). Experiencing symptom burden can be considered as a strain in daily life. The perception of the symptom may therefore reinforce the negative experience, as the sensation of cold becomes a dominant concern in the individual's consciousness. Feeling cold is not merely an isolated physical phenomenon but affects multiple aspects of the individual, manifesting here as a psychological impact. This as well is seen in patients with CKD experiencing other symptoms, for example pruritus (Menzaghi et al. 2023). These findings emphasise the importance of an approach to the patients' symptoms that considers the psychological and social context of their lives.

The sensation of feeling cold is not considered as a noteworthy symptom, even though it is expressed as a burden by the patients. One reason for not verbalising the sensation of feeling cold could be that patients do not consider their experience as relevant or important enough to involve others. The lack of dialogue may be a contributing factor to why this symptom has not been previously explored in depth.

Living with a chronic illness may involve multiple symptoms and challenges. This requires significant coping (Benkel et al. 2020). One possible interpretation is that acceptance of the symptom may reflect the challenges of managing multiple competing symptoms associated with chronic illness. By accepting the sensation of feeling cold, patients do not have to spend energy on trying to resolve this issue. This suggests that the informants may lack the capacity to explore the symptom further and identify its underlying cause.

A secondary finding emerged as participants repeatedly described experiences of feeling hot. Although not part of the original research aim, it's suggesting a related but distinct phenomenon warranting future investigation.

Strengths and Limitations

Interviews were conducted during haemodialysis treatment in shared clinical environments, where interruptions from staff and the presence of other patients may have influenced participants' willingness to disclose sensitive experiences. The interviews were conducted in the autumn, and it is unclear whether this has influenced the findings. If the interviews had instead been conducted in the summer, the findings might have been different. Patients with CKD have reduced cognition and may experience worsening memory (Brickman et al. 1996), which could have affected the findings. Interview duration varied considerably (3.5–24 min), potentially affecting the depth and richness of individual interviews. However, we strove for credibility, dependability, confirmability and transferability (Lincoln and Guba 1985), why methodological considerations are elaborated. Therefore the large sample size and total dataset of 140 transcript pages contributed to analytical breadth. One interview was significantly shorter than the rest (3.5 min.), reflecting a participant not experiencing the symptom. Some interviewers had prior clinical relationships with participants. While this may have facilitated rapport and openness during interviews, it may also have influenced participants' responses through social desirability or perceived expectations.

Implications for Clinical Practice

Temperature regulation is not included in assessment tools for patients with CKD, presenting challenges for nurses in identifying this symptom as it is not recognised among the typical symptoms. Incorporating temperature regulation into these assessment instruments could enhance both patient and nurse awareness. Increased nurse recognition of this symptom would foster patient acknowledgement, as many individuals tend to minimise how feeling cold affects them, viewing it merely as a common experience. By normalising this symptom in clinical settings, patients may feel more comfortable discussing it. Potential clinical strategies include individualised warming plans, provision of blankets or heating devices during treatment, consideration of dialysate temperature where clinically appropriate, and documentation of temperature‐related symptoms as part of routine symptom assessment.

Our findings show that patients appreciate being understood and acknowledged in relation to their symptoms. Therefore, our study is relevant in terms of validating patients' experiences of feeling cold. This could pave the way for further investigation of the symptom and potential relief measures.

When patients share their experiences with close family members, they report a heightened sense of recognition and appreciation, suggesting that relational dynamics play a pivotal role in symptom perception. This correlation indicates that the nurse's engagement, grounded in the FoC approach, profoundly impacts patients' symptom experiences.

Despite the omission of temperature regulation symptoms in many assessment tools, opportunities for discussion exist, particularly in sections for documenting additional symptoms. However, due to patients' reticence to address their experiences, feelings related to cold may remain unreported. Our research underscores the importance of validating these experiences, potentially guiding further exploration into the implications of temperature regulation within CKD care.

Conclusion

Based on the findings participants are describing an increased sensitivity to cold following the onset of kidney failure and haemodialysis treatment.

The findings suggest that “feeling cold” may represent an important but underexplored aspect of the symptom burden among some patients with kidney failure. Furthermore, the findings enhance the understanding of patients living with kidney failure. Despite variations in how participants experience the symptom, it is uniformly described in negative terms. Further research is needed to examine the prevalence, severity and potential mechanisms underlying temperature‐related symptoms.

During the study, an additional finding emerged. Several participants experienced feeling hot in conjunction with haemodialysis. Both symptoms affected quality of life and daily living.
