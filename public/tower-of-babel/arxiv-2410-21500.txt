Noncommutative Singularity Theory


Abstract
Résumé This is an expository article on the noncommutative singularity theory of power series in noncommuting variables, its motivation from deformation theory, and its applications to contractibility of curves and the classification of smooth 3-fold flops.



1
Introduction

It is a fact of life that there are just too many functions, and to gain structural insight, some restriction is necessary. Taking that as given, what comes next is harder, since it involves making choices.

To first approximation, making the choice of a class of functions amounts to making the choice of a research area : differential geometry is often considered to be the study of
C^{\infty}
functions, algebraic geometry thought to be the exclusive realm of polynomial functions, and symplectic topology to be, well, the land where symplectomorphisms roam free.

Whilst this viewpoint provides helpful motivation, it is simply flat-out false from any reasonable technical perspective. There are many situations where within an area ‘defined’ by a class of functions, to prove certain theorems requires an enlargement or extension to that class of functions. This may be frustrating. For example, if you begin with polynomials that look as nice as
u^{2}+v^{2}+x^{2}+y^{2}
, it does initially take some convincing that formal power series really are objects that humans should consider, and that analytic changes in coordinates really do make your life easier.
If a problem can be stated entirely within one class of functions, there is understandably a reluctance to extend that class without a clear and compelling reason to do so.

The purpose of this article is to introduce noncommutative singularity theory as an object in its own right, and to then explain why this extension from commutative to noncommutative polynomials (and power series) is needed to solve various contraction and classification problems within algebraic geometry.

With this in mind, and attempting to accomplish multiple goals, the structure of this article is as follows. §
2
outlines what noncommutative singularity theory is and states some of its initial results. This is all factual, but it doesn’t address the why : that is partially contained in §
3
, which outlines the original intuition from deformation theory, but in fact is mostly explained in §
4
and §
5
, which covers in detail the applications to birational geometry. Throughout §
4
and §
5
there is a particular focus on what the new noncommutative technology buys us, in the form of various theorems and geometric consequences. Two conjectures are briefly outlined in §
5.4
.

2
Noncommutative Singularity Theory

Polynomial functions are elements in the polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
for some
d\geq 1
. To set language, noncommutative (polynomial) functions will simply be elements of the free algebra
\mathbb{C}\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\rangle
.

2.1
Classical singularity theory

To classify even smooth points in algebraic geometry, one must work suitably locally.
For this article this means working with elements of the commutative power series ring
\mathbb{C}[\kern-2.5pt[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]\kern-2.5pt]
, although alternatives are available. The key point is that elements in the commutative power series ring are allowed to consist of infinite sums, and as such they can be represented by the form

f=\uplambda_{1}+\uplambda_{2}x+\uplambda_{3}y+\uplambda_{4}xy+\ldots

The ring
\mathbb{C}[\kern-2.5pt[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]\kern-2.5pt]
is sometimes alternatively referred to as the formal power series ring, with the word ‘formal’ acting as a reminder that its elements do not need to satisfy any radius of convergence conditions.

At first glance, it is not so clear that moving from polynomials (finite sums) to power series (infinite sums) makes life easier, given that there seem to be many more elements. But it does, since there are now
many
more symmetries, in the form of analytic changes in coordinates. Given this huge symmetry group, it is then possible to ask about classifying functions. This is the domain of singularity theory.

Aim 2.1

(Singularity Theory à la Arnold
[
1
]
).

Up to specified isomorphism, classify all
f\in\mathbb{C}[\kern-2.5pt[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]\kern-2.5pt]
that satisfy some fixed numerical criteria, and produce theory for when classification is not possible.

The following is perhaps the most fundamental class of examples. Although the condition below is a priori unmotivated, it is numerical, being the count of ideals satisfying a certain property.

Definition 2.2
.

An element
f\in\mathbb{C}[\kern-2.5pt[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]\kern-2.5pt]=\mathbb{C}[\kern-2.5pt[\mathsf{x}]\kern-2.5pt]
is said to be a simple singularity if

\#\{I\mid I\textnormal{ proper ideal of }\mathbb{C}[\kern-2.5pt[\mathsf{x}]\kern-2.5pt]\textnormal{ with }f\in I^{2}\}<\infty.

This is a non-standard definition of a simple singularity. Like many numerical definitions within singularity theory, it is only really justified
after
classification, when it becomes more clear that it is equivalent to every other characterisation of simple singularities in the literature.

Theorem 2.3

(e.g.
[
27
, §8]
)
.

If
f
is a simple singularity in the sense of
2.2
, then up to relabelling variables
z_{1},\ldots z_{d-2},x,y
, and up to isomorphism (analytic or formal changes of coordinates in the domain),
f
is one of

\begin{array}[]{lll}A_{n}&\quad z^{2}+x^{2}+y^{n+1}&n\geq 1\\[2.84526pt]
D_{n}&\quad z^{2}+x^{2}y+y^{n-1}&n\geq 4\\[2.84526pt]
E_{6}&\quad z^{2}+x^{3}+y^{4}&\\[2.84526pt]
E_{7}&\quad z^{2}+x^{3}+xy^{3}&\\[2.84526pt]
E_{8}&\quad z^{2}+x^{3}+y^{5}&\end{array}

where
z^{2}=z_{1}^{2}+\ldots+z_{d-2}^{2}
.

From the viewpoint of later sections, several remarks are in order. First, in
2.2
there is no mention of ADE, yet this still appears in the classification
2.3
. Second,
2.3
illustrates the very small number of simple singularities, namely just two (countable) infinite families and only three other cases. And third, with
2.3
in hand, working with simple singularities becomes much easier, since various problems may be reduced to checking statements against a short list of elegant forms.

2.2
Noncommutative singularity theory

To first approximation, the overall idea of noncommutative singularity theory is to replace the commutative object
\mathbb{C}[\kern-2.5pt[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]\kern-2.5pt]
with the formal
noncommutative
power series ring
\mathbb{C}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle
, then to play the same game as in §
2.1
. This is not quite true, since in the noncommutative setting there are many more ‘free objects’ given by quivers, and as in
2.8
below this makes the theory richer, and widens applications.

In the first case, the ring
\mathbb{C}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle
is similar to the free algebra in
d
variables, except now elements are possibly infinite sums

f=\uplambda_{1}+\uplambda_{2}x+\uplambda_{3}y+\uplambda_{4}xy+\uplambda_{5}yx+\ldots

Whilst for
d\geq 2
the ring
\mathbb{C}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle
is not noetherian, has exponential growth, and is pathologically horrible from many perspectives, as in the commutative case its automorphim group is huge, and so classifying elements satisfying certain numerical criteria still turns out to be possible. But, as in the classical case, to do this still requires choices.

Problem 2.4
.

When should
f,g\in\mathbb{C}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle
be viewed as being the same ? And what would be natural numerical criteria ?

To explain first the notion of equivalence, recall that every
f\in\mathbb{C}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle
can be differentiated with respect to any variable. The rule is simple : cycle then score off the leftmost variable. For example, in humane coordinates
x
and
y
, to differentiate
x^{3}y
with respect to
x
,

x^{3}y=xxxy\xrightarrow{\scriptstyle\textnormal{cycle}}\begin{array}[]{c}xxxy\\
xxyx\\
xyxx\\
yxxx\end{array}\xrightarrow{\scriptstyle\textnormal{score off}}\begin{array}[]{c}xxy\\
xyx\\
yxx\\
0\end{array}

where ‘score off’ either scores off the leftmost
x
, or returns zero if the monomial does not begin with
x
. By definition, it follows that
\updelta_{x}(x^{3}y)=x^{2}y+xyx+yx^{2}
. Reassuringly,
\updelta_{y}(y^{3})=3y^{2}
.

Definition 2.5
.

Given any
f
, the Jacobi algebra is

\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}f:=\frac{\mathbb{C}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle}{(\kern-2.5pt(\updelta_{z}f\mid z\mbox{ is a variable})\kern-2.5pt)},

where the double brackets
(\kern-2.5pt(\,)\kern-2.5pt)
denote the closure of the two-sided ideal.

Example 2.6
.

The element
f=x^{4}+xy^{2}\in\mathbb{C}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x,y\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle
has

\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}f=\frac{\mathbb{C}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x,y\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle}{(\kern-2.5pt(4x^{3}+y^{2},xy+yx)\kern-2.5pt)},

which as explained in
2.13
below is a nine-dimensional algebra.

Write
f\cong g
provided that
\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}f\cong\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}\,g
.

Aim 2.7
.

With
\mathbb{C}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle
fixed, and the notion of equivalence
\cong
now defined, the aim of singularity theory remains : classify all elements satisfying some numerical (or structural) criteria, and produce theory for when classification is not possible.

Remark 2.8
.

An alternative description of the noncommutative power series ring
\mathbb{C}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle
is the complete path algebra of the
d
-loop quiver. Other quivers are possible, and all of the constructions in this section and below naturally and easily generalise to that setting
[
11
]
. These other quivers are needed for multi-curve geometric applications ; see §
3.4
, §
4.1
and
5.3
later.

2.3
The singularity theory toolkit

This subsection briefly outlines some of the singularity theory toolkit, adapted into the noncommutative setting.

The first most fundamental problem is to establish the correct notion of dimension and the related numerical invariants. Using the Gelfand–Kirillov (GK) dimension, which is standard for noncommutative rings, is problematic since
\mathop{\mathrm{GKdim}}\nolimits\mathbb{C}[\kern-2.5pt[x]\kern-2.5pt]=\infty
. Fundamentally, GK dimension is not well adapted to complete rings. To overcome this, we return to the basic fact that
\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}f
is local, with Jacobson radical
\mathfrak{J}
say. This gives an intrinsic filtration

\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}f\supseteq\mathfrak{J}\supseteq\mathfrak{J}^{2}\supseteq\ldots.

Definition 2.9
.

The (Jacobson-radical)
\mathfrak{J}
-dimension,
\mathop{\mathrm{Jdim}}\nolimits\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}f
, is the growth rate of the steps in the chain above, namely

\inf\left\{r\in\mathbb{R}\mid\textnormal{for some $c\in\mathbb{R}$, }\dim\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}f/\mathfrak{J}^{n}\leq cn^{r}\text{ for every $n\in\mathbb{N}$}\right\}.

(2.1)

To calibrate, note that
\mathop{\mathrm{Jdim}}\nolimits\mathbb{C}[\kern-2.5pt[x]\kern-2.5pt]=1
, and that
\mathop{\mathrm{Jdim}}\nolimits\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}f=0
is equivalent to
\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}f
being a finite dimensional algebra.

Remark 2.10
.

There are several well-known results about GK dimension : for example, that it can take any real value
\geq 2
(or
0
,
1
or
\infty
) but no values in the ‘Bergman gap’ between
1
and
2
. We do not know analogous results for
\mathfrak{J}
-dimension, even for the restricted class of Jacobi algebras. In the context of this survey, however, it is clear that if the
\mathfrak{J}
-dimension  is
\leq 1
then it is either 0 or 1, just as for GK dimension.

Whilst the growth rate of the whole chain (
) gives the dimension, the numerical properties of its individual pieces also holds valuable information. In what follows, we will always assume that
f
contains no constant or linear terms, which we will abbreviate as
f\in\mathbb{C}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle_{\geq 2}
.

Definition 2.11
.

For
f\in\mathbb{C}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle_{\geq 2}
, and
m\geq 1
, the
m
th corank of
f
is defined to be

\mathcal{C}\mathrm{rk}_{m}(f)=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\frac{\mathfrak{J}^{m}}{\mathfrak{J}^{m+1}}\right),

where
\mathfrak{J}
is the Jacobson radical of
\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}f
.

The first of these invariants
\mathcal{C}\mathrm{rk}(f):=\mathcal{C}\mathrm{rk}_{1}(f)
is often referred to as simply the corank. The corank is determined by the linear conditions imposed by derivatives, and is therefore uniquely determined by the degree two part of
f
.

Theorem 2.12

(Splitting Lemma,
[
11
, 4.5]
)
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{C}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle_{\geq 2}
. Then
f\cong x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{r}^{2}+g
for some
g\in\mathbb{C}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x_{r+1},\ldots,x_{d}\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle_{\geq 3}
, where
d-r=\mathcal{C}\mathrm{rk}(f)
. In particular,

\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}f\cong\frac{\mathbb{C}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x_{r+1},\ldots,x_{d}\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle}{(\kern-2.5pt(\updelta_{x_{r+1}}g,\ldots,\updelta_{x_{d}}g)\kern-2.5pt)}.

The coranks are properties of the leading terms of elements of the closed Jacobian ideal of derivatives, and can be calculated using a standard basis. In the commutative context, the standard basis (also known as a local Gröbner basis) is well known. Loosely speaking, one follows the usual Buchberger approach of cancelling leading terms, but regarding the leading terms as those of low degree. The theory and key results all hold in the setting of noncommutative power series
[
17
]
. From a user persepective, there are two points to note : noncommutativity means leading terms may have nontrivial cancellation with themselves, and, being non-noetherian, the resulting basis may be infinite (although not of course in nice settings such as
\mathop{\mathrm{Jdim}}\nolimits\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}f=0
).

Example 2.13
.

Continuing
2.6
, consider
f=x^{4}+xy^{2}
. The leading term of a nonzero power series is defined to be the term of least degree, with lexicographic order used to break ties. So, for example, the leading term of
xy^{2}+yxy+y^{3}
is
xy^{2}
.

Setting
A=y^{2}+4x^{3}
and
B=xy+yx
for the two derivatives, the leading term
y^{2}
of
A
cancels itself in the expression
yA-Ay
to give

yA-Ay=4yx^{3}-4x^{3}y\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\equiv}\limits^{B}}4yx^{3}+4x^{2}yx\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\equiv}\limits^{B}}4yx^{3}-4xyx^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\equiv}\limits^{B}}4yx^{3}+4yx^{3}=8yx^{3}

where
\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\equiv}\limits^{B}}
denotes congruence modulo
B
. Thus
C=yx^{3}\in(A,B)
. Similarly
Ax^{3}-yC=4x^{6}\in(A,B)
. All other attempts to cancel leading terms reduce to zero, and so the (normalised) standard basis is

y^{2}+4x^{3},\ xy+yx,\ yx^{3},\ x^{6}.

The leading terms of these are
y^{2},xy,yx^{3},x^{6}
, and the set of monomials not divisible by any of these provides a basis of
\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}f
. In this case we see at once therefore that

1,\ x,\ y,\ x^{2},\ yx,x^{3},\ yx^{2},\ x^{4},\ x^{5}

is a basis of
\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}f
and so it has dimension 9, as claimed.
Morover, the degree
m
monomials in this monomial basis descend to a basis of
\mathfrak{J}^{m}/\mathfrak{J}^{m+1}
, which implies that

\mathcal{C}\mathrm{rk}_{1}(f)=\mathcal{C}\mathrm{rk}_{2}(f)=\mathcal{C}\mathrm{rk}_{3}(f)=2\textrm{\ \ and\ \ }\mathcal{C}\mathrm{rk}_{4}(f)=\mathcal{C}\mathrm{rk}_{5}(f)=1.

Together with
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x,y\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle/\mathfrak{J}=1
, these numbers add up to
9=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}f
.

Another key property of Jacobi algebras is that they are invariant under automorphisms, and so in particular under arbitrary analytic changes in coordinates.

Theorem 2.14

(
[
11
, 3.7]
)
.

If
F\in\mathrm{Aut}\,\mathbb{C}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle
then
\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}f\cong\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}\,F(f)
.

Other natural parts of the commutative theory, such as Saito’s famous result
[
24
]
on quasihomogeneous power series, also work in the noncommutative context, although not quite yet at the elementary entry level of this discussion ; see
[
19
]
.

Remark 2.15
.

Whilst any type of singularity theory is really ‘just’ the classification of Jacobi (or commutatively, Tjurina) algebras up to isomorphism, thinking in terms of functions allows us to access the toolkit of singularity theory. Even when the Jacobi algebras are finite dimensional, perhaps the main advantage of thinking in terms of functions is
2.14
, as this enables the construction of many isomorphisms which seem completely out of reach of more classical approaches to the classification of noncommutative finite dimensional algebras.

2.4
Proposal

The following proposal
2.16
, which we view as the noncommutative version of simple singularities, is our main reason for developing noncommutative singularity theory.

Proposal 2.16
.

Under the numerical criterion
\mathop{\mathrm{Jdim}}\nolimits\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}f\leq 1
, we propose that :

(1)

a classification is in fact possible.

(2)

the classification is ADE.

(3)

this algebraic classification
is
(and implies) the classification of flops and of crepant divisorial contractions to curves.

The proposal is still conjectural in its form stated above, as this is strongly related to Conjecture
5.6
below. The current state of the art, partially explained in §
5
, in part replaces the numerical condition
\mathop{\mathrm{Jdim}}\nolimits\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}f\leq 1
with a structural one, and solves that : for all geometric applications including the classification of
3
-fold flops, this is enough. However, from the viewpoint of future ease of use (numerical properties are easier to verify than structural ones), and also to make the analogy with simple singularities in
2.3
more compelling, verifying
2.16
in the generality stated remains the founding question of noncommutative singularity theory.

3
Deformation Theory

This section describes the links, via deformation theory, between noncommutative singularity theory on the one hand and both classification and contractibility problems in birational geometry on the other. Applications are given later, in §
4
and §
5
.

3.1
Deformation theory framework

One reason for deforming an object, say
M
, is the hope that doing so will attach an intrinsic invariant to
M
, and furthermore that this invariant will be useful. In good cases it is possible to extract simple data from the invariant, for example a number such as its dimension (if that even makes sense). Intrinsically attaching numbers to objects is hard, and the framework of deformation theory is generally a good way of doing this.

Alas, deformation theory involves choices. To ensure that the theory is intrinsic, it is natural to insist on the functorial approach. That is, given some object
M
, the first aim is to construct a functor

\mathcal{D}\mathrm{ef}_{M}\colon\mathrm{TestObjects}\to\mathrm{Sets}

which sends a test object
R
to a set
\{\mbox{deformations of }M\mbox{ over }R\}/\sim
. To make this rigorous, the following need to be defined, or rather declared :

(1)

The category
\mathrm{TestObjects}
.

(2)

The definition of ‘deformations of
M
over
R
’.

(3)

The equivalence relation
\sim

There is a fourth choice, suppressed throughout this article, since we will always assume that the target category is the category of sets.

3.2
Commutative deformation theory

Let
X
be a variety, and
\mathcal{F}\in\operatorname{\mathrm{Qcoh}}X
be a quasi-coherent sheaf. To deform
\mathcal{F}
via the classical Grothendieck formulation, consider the functor

\mathrm{c}\mathcal{D}\mathrm{ef}\colon\mathfrak{cart}\to\operatorname{\mathrm{Sets}}

where
\mathfrak{cart}
is the category of commutative local artinian
\mathbb{C}
-algebras, sending

(R,\mathfrak{m})\mapsto\left.\left\{(\mathcal{G},\upeta)\left|\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{G}\in\operatorname{\mathrm{Qcoh}}(X\times\operatorname{\mathrm{Spec}}R),\\
\mathcal{G}\mbox{ is flat over }\operatorname{\mathrm{Spec}}R,\\
\upeta\colon\mathcal{G}|_{X\times 0}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathcal{F}.\end{array}\right.\right\}\middle/\sim\right.

where
\sim
is the natural equivalence relation outlined in a greater generality below. The point is that, roughly speaking, a deformation of
\mathcal{F}
is a sheaf
\mathcal{G}
which is infinitesimally thicker than
\mathcal{F}
(namely, a sheaf on
X\times\operatorname{\mathrm{Spec}}R
), roughly of the same size (flatness), which recovers the original
\mathcal{F}
(restricted to the zero fibre). There are many choices of such isomorphisms on the zero fibre, so we carry those choices as part of the data.

3.3
Noncommutative deformation theory

It is very intuitive that more test objects will lead to more information. This subsection enlarges the category of test objects
\mathfrak{cart}
from §
3.2
by adding in (some) noncommutative artinian rings. The resulting deformation functor will be similar, but requires some reinterpretation.

For simplicity, consider the same setup as in §
3.2
, namely
X
is a variety, and
\mathcal{F}\in\operatorname{\mathrm{Qcoh}}X
. The noncommutative deformation functor is defined to be

\mathcal{D}\mathrm{ef}\colon\mathfrak{art}_{1}\to\operatorname{\mathrm{Sets}}

from finite dimensional augmented
\mathbb{C}
-algebras to sets, sending

(\Gamma,\mathfrak{n})\mapsto\left.\left\{(\mathcal{G},\upvartheta,\upeta)\left|\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{G}\in\operatorname{\mathrm{Qcoh}}X,\\

-\otimes_{\Gamma}\mathcal{G}\colon\operatorname{\mathrm{mod}}\Gamma\to\operatorname{\mathrm{Qcoh}}X\mbox{ is exact},\\
\upeta\colon(\Gamma/\mathfrak{n})\otimes_{\Gamma}\mathcal{G}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathcal{F}.\end{array}\right.\right\}\middle/\sim\right.

where, for any given
\Gamma
,

3.4
Deforming multiple objects

Perhaps the clearest way of observing the benefit of noncommutative deformation theory is when we try to deform more than one object, say
\mathcal{F}_{1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{2}
. Classically, the commutative solution is to take the direct sum
\mathcal{F}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{F}_{2}
, and apply the technology of §
3.2
. The commutative hull is then constructed as a factor of
\mathbb{C}[\kern-2.5pt[x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}]\kern-2.5pt]
, where
t=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathrm{Ext}^{1}_{X}(\mathcal{F}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{F}_{2},\mathcal{F}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{F}_{2})
.

But this clearly loses information, since Ext groups are directed : indeed, in general

\mathrm{Ext}^{1}_{X}(\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2})\ncong\mathrm{Ext}^{1}_{X}(\mathcal{F}_{2},\mathcal{F}_{1}).

Since the order matters in the Ext groups, forcing the commutativity in
\mathbb{C}[\kern-2.5pt[x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}]\kern-2.5pt]
means that often almost all information is lost.

The solution to deforming
n
objects is to realise that, since Exts are directed, the prorepresenting hull should naturally be constructed as a factor of a quiver with
n
vertices, where the number of arrows from vertex
i
to vertex
j
should be the integer
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathrm{Ext}^{1}_{X}(\mathcal{F}_{i},\mathcal{F}_{j})
. This naturally records the asymmetry in the Ext groups, and also gives a more natural and compelling description of what the tangent space should be.

To do this on a technical level requires the introduction of the category of test objects
\mathfrak{art}_{n}
, which are roughly speaking finite dimensional
\mathbb{C}
-algebras where all simples are one-dimensional ; this last condition rules out matrix algebras appearing. For many equivalent characterisations of the category
\mathfrak{art}_{n}
, we refer the reader to
[
16
]
.

To deform the collection
\mathcal{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{n}
, each
\mathcal{F}_{i}
is replaced by an injective resolution
\mathcal{F}_{i}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{I}_{i}
, and from that the endomorphism DG-algebra
\mathcal{E}\mathrm{nd}_{X}(\oplus\mathcal{I}_{i})
is constructed. The Maurer–Cartan formulation then gives a functor

\mathcal{D}\mathrm{ef}_{\mathcal{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{n}}\colon\mathfrak{art}_{n}\to\operatorname{\mathrm{Sets}}

(3.1)

called the multi-pointed noncommutative deformation functor. Full details can be found in e.g.
[
25
,
13
]
. As always there are choices ; it is also possible to consider

\mathcal{D}\mathrm{ef}_{\mathcal{F}_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathcal{F}_{n}}\colon\mathfrak{art}_{1}\to\operatorname{\mathrm{Sets}}

from §
3.3
. We describe in §
4
below the behaviour of these functors in the situation of curve contractions, and outline the reasons why from almost all geometric viewpoints (
) is the key.

In the left hand case ➀, the morphism is an isomorphic away from a point, whereas in the right hand case ➁ the morphism contracts a divisor to a curve, and is an isomorphism away from the one-dimensional locus on the base. Although the above pictures are drawn in the algebraic category, we will often implicitly pick a closed point on the base within the non-isomorphism locus, complete at that point, and thus assume that the base
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{con}}=\mathrm{Spec}\,\mathcal{R}
is complete local.

Above the chosen point
p
, the fibre (given reduced scheme structure) is a collection of curves
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}
, all isomorphic to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
. The number of curves depends on which closed point on the base is chosen.

Regardless, applying §

to the collection of structure sheaves
\mathcal{O}_{C_{1}},\ldots,\mathcal{O}_{C_{n}}\in\operatorname{\mathrm{Qcoh}}\mathcal{X}
gives rise to the noncommutative deformation functor

\mathcal{D}\mathrm{ef}_{C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}}\colon\mathfrak{art}_{n}\to\operatorname{\mathrm{Sets}}.

Theorem 4.1

(
[

,

)
.

There is an algebra

and a functorial isomorphism

where
\sim
is the action of inner automorphisms in

.

The algebra
\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{con}}
is called the contraction algebra. In fact, there is a second algebra
\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{def}}
called the noncommutative deformation algebra, but it is shown in

that the two are isomorphic ; from the viewpoint of §

We briefly note that there is a small technical issue in

4.2
Which deformation theory ?

Given the abundance of deformation functors in §
3
, it seems natural to wonder which solves the geometric problems of interest.

Around the chosen point
p
on the base, above which there are curves
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}
, we first ask which deformation functor detects the difference between sitation ➀ and ➁. In all cases, without assumptions on the singularities of
\mathcal{X}
, and without crepant assumptions, this is answered by the noncommutative deformation functor.

Theorem 4.2

(
[
13
]
)
.

Given a contraction
\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{con}}
as in (
), pick a point
p
on the base and consider the corresponding contraction algebra
\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{con}}
above that point. Then, locally around
p
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathop{\mathrm{Jdim}}\nolimits\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{con}}=0&\iff&\mbox{Situation ➀ (flopping)}.\\[5.69054pt]
\mathop{\mathrm{Jdim}}\nolimits\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{con}}=1&\iff&\mbox{Situation ➁ (div$\to$curve)}.\\
\end{array}

With extra smoothness assumptions,
\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{con}}
is a Jaocbi algebra (see §
4.3
), and so in addition to detecting the nature of the geometry locally around
p
, the above theorem strongly motivates the study of potentials
f
satisfying the numerical condition
\mathop{\mathrm{Jdim}}\nolimits\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}f\leq 1
.

The larger question is which deformation functor, if any, is sufficient to
classify
the curve neighbourhood, since this would be the strongest property of all. The following has become known as the Donovan–Wemyss conjecture, where for ease of exposition here we restrict to the case of a single curve. Classification requires some form of smoothness, and the rough slogan is that contraction algebras are always the classifying objects for smooth crepant contractible neighbourhoods.

Conjecture 4.3
.

(Donovan–Wemyss)
Let
\mathcal{X}_{1}\to\operatorname{\mathrm{Spec}}\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\mathcal{X}_{2}\to\operatorname{\mathrm{Spec}}\mathcal{R}_{2}
be as in (
), where both are crepant, both
\mathcal{X}_{i}
are smooth, and both
\mathcal{R}_{i}
are complete local cDV. Write
\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{con}}
and
\mathrm{B}_{\mathrm{con}}
for their corresponding contraction algebras, and in each case assume that there is only one curve. Then

\mathcal{R}_{1}\cong\mathcal{R}_{2}\iff\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{con}}\cong\mathrm{B}_{\mathrm{con}}.

In the setting of flops ➀, Conjecture
4.3
is now a theorem due to
[
12
,
3
,
18
,
20
]
, where after a series of reduction steps
[
20
]
apply their Derived Auslander–Iyama correspondence. The divisor-to-curve case of
4.3
remains open.

Regardless, it is useful to regard the word conjecture as a challenge to extend the scope of
4.3
to the maximum extent possible ; it may even be true that contractibility is not required, and that the noncommutative deformation algebra classifies smooth crepant curve neighbourhoods in full generality.

4.3
Link to noncommutative singularity theory

As already alluded to, when
\mathcal{X}
is smooth and the morphism is crepant, it is automatic that
\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{con}}
is the Jacobi algebra of a quiver with potential. The quiver can be described easily from the data of the configuration of the curves contracted and their normal bundles
[
26
, 2.15]
. Describing the potential is significantly more challenging.

This is precisely the domain of noncommutative singularity theory : with the combinatorial data of the quiver fixed, it asks what functions (=potentials) can arise. To calibrate, in the case of a single crepant curve, which is often the hardest case,
\mathrm{A}_{\mathrm{con}}
is a factor of
\mathbb{C}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x,y\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle
. The fact we should expect only two variables to show up should be viewed in analogy with simple singularities in
2.3
; for a full explanation, see
[
6
, A.13]
.

The following is a numerical consequence of the classification of flops via Jacobi algebras in the previous subsections.

Theorem 5.5
.

The GV invariants are known for all smooth irreducible
3
-fold flops.

Full details will again appear in

]
. To outline one particularly remarkable phenomenon,

\mathrm{E}_{7}

f\in\mathbb{C}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x,y\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle

\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}f
is isomorphic to the contraction algebra of some
\mathcal{X}\to\mathrm{Spec}\,\mathcal{R}
in
4.1
(either case ➀ or ➁).

Conjecture 5.6

(Geometric Realisation Conjecture)
.

Every
f
whose Jacobi algebra satisfies
\dim\mathcal{J}\mathrm{ac}f\leq 1
is geometric.

Tens of thousands of computer searches (with Magma
[
5
]
and Singular
[
10
]
) have failed to uncover counterexamples to
5.6
, but the methods of
[
6
,
8
]
do not yet seem strong enough to prove it. At this stage, it seems that a new ingredient in noncommutative singularity theory will be needed, if indeed
5.6
is true.

Now let
C\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}\subseteq X
, where
X
is a Calabi–Yau
3
-fold. Write
\Lambda_{\mathrm{def}}
for the noncommutative deformation algebra associated to
C
, obtained via noncommutative deformation theory. It is conjectured in
[
7
]
that in this 3-fold setting, numerical properties of
\Lambda_{\mathrm{def}}
are sufficient to detect whether or not
C
contracts.

Conjecture 5.7

(Artin Contractibility for
3
-folds)
.

In a suitably local neighbourhood of
C
in
X
, the following are equivalent.

(1)

C
contracts to a point and is an isomorphism elsewhere.

(2)

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\Lambda_{\mathrm{def}}<\infty
.

The statement of
5.7
is for a single curve, but one of the advantages of the noncommutative deformation theory technology is that it finally permits clean statements for multi-curve configurations ; for details see
[
7
]
.

It may be the case that the condition
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\hat{X},\mathcal{O}_{\hat{X}})=0
needs to be added to the assumptions in the conjecture, since this would then more closely match the classical Artin statement for surfaces. However, just as for
5.6
above, tens of thousands of computer searches suggest that the condition
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\Lambda_{\mathrm{def}}<\infty
is so restrictive that the rational neighbourhood assumption may in fact come for free.

To end, it seems fitting to note the limitations of our view, or, perhaps better, the enormous potential in what has not been covered.
The discussion here has considered what we might term noncommutative function germs, that is, elements of
\mathbb{C}\langle\kern-2.5pt\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\rangle\kern-2.5pt\rangle
or more generally elements of complete local path algebras on quivers. That is based largely on our admiraction and profitable exploitation of a few of the ideas of Arnold’s seminal paper
[
1
]
. Conventional commutative singularity theory is of course a much larger subject, exemplified by the vast literature, such as
[
2
,
22
,
9
]
to cite a few thousand pages. We have not mentioned notions of stability,
k
-determinacy, adjacency, versality, more general map germs, vector fields, or a host of other very natural notions. These may or may not have profitable extensions into the noncommutative realm. But for now we simply do not know, in part due to our technical limitations, but also since we do not yet know how we would be able to apply them.
